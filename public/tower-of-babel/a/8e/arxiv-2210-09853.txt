Rational curvature invariants for 2-complexes
Henry Wilton

Abstract
New invariants for 2-dimensional cell complexes are defined, which can be interpreted as curvature bounds. These invariants are proved to be rational and computable in a companion article. This document is a survey that collects theorems about these invariants, computes examples, and lays out a programme of conjectures.



1
Introduction

1.1
Historical background

In a 1911 paper, Max Dehn initiated the study of infinite discrete groups of symmetry: he posed the
word problem
, and solved it in the case of the fundamental group of a closed surface. Already, in Dehn’s seminal work, there is a tension between the combinatorial and geometric approaches: he gave a geometric solution to the word problem for surfaces in 1911
[
18
]
, then followed it with a combinatorial solution the following year
[
19
]
. This tension persists today.

The combinatorial approach rose to the fore in the immediate aftermath of Dehn’s work, and the field of
combinatorial group theory
was born. Combinatorial group theory can be characterised as the study of groups defined by presentations, i.e. sets of generators subject to relations. The paradigmatic example is the theory of groups with one relator, developed by Dehn’s student Wilhelm Magnus in the 1930s. Magnus solved the word problem in all one-relator groups using combinatorial methods
[
38
]
, and combinatorial group theory remained the dominant method of studying infinite discrete groups for half a century.

Dehn himself seems to have had reservations about the combinatorial method: he famously said ‘So you went about it blind!’ when he saw Magnus’ combinatorial proof
[
43
, p.126]
. Nevertheless, the geometric method took a back seat until the 1980s, when Mikhail Gromov, a differential geometer, repopularised Dehn’s work. Gromov observed that Dehn’s 1911 solution to the word problem, using the geometry of the hyperbolic plane, applies to a much larger class of group: the
(word-)hyperbolic groups
, which enjoy a coarse form of negative curvature
[
23
]
. So began the field of
geometric group theory
, which studies groups via their actions on metric spaces. Hyperbolic groups became the paradigmatic examples, and geometric group theory is now the dominant method of studying infinite discrete groups.

While many examples fit into both the combinatorial and geometric paradigms, no over-arching theory unifies the two. The geometric point of view is satisfying and offers very general arguments for certain classes of groups, but leaves other groups untouched. Baumslag–Solitar groups, as well as other small and natural examples, which are well handled by the combinatorial approach, are pathological from the geometric perspective.

Our purpose here is to propose a new approach that enjoys some of the advantages of both. Like Dehn, we are motivated by topology, and therefore we will work with 2-dimensional cell complexes rather than presentations. (There is no loss of generality, since a presentation naturally defines a presentation complex.) To each such complex
X
we will associate several numerical invariants, which synthesise several recent strands of research in group theory and low-dimensional topology. They encompass both one-relator groups and many natural geometric conditions, including small-cancellation conditions and some of Gromov’s metric curvature conditions. Despite being defined combinatorially, we argue that these invariants should be thought of geometrically, as curvature bounds.

1.2
Curvature invariants

We start with the following crude definition. The
average curvature
of a finite 2-complex
X
is defined to be

\kappa(X):=\frac{\chi(X)}{\mathrm{Area}(X)}

where
\chi(X)
is the Euler characteristic of
X
and
\mathrm{Area}(X)
is the number of 2-cells of
X
. This definition is motivated by the Gauss–Bonnet theorem, which tells us that the average Gaussian curvature of a closed Riemannian surface
S
is
2\pi\chi(S)/\mathrm{Area}(S)
.

The average curvature is not very different from the Euler characteristic, and so will not on its own contain much information about
X
or its fundamental group.
However, it is a familiar idea in topology and geometry that we may define interesting invariants of a space
X
by probing it with maps from other spaces
Y
. Following this idea, we define more refined invariants by extremising
\kappa
over certain classes of 2-complexes
Y
that map to
X
. (In fact, it turns out to be fruitful to allow
Y
to be a slightly more general object called a
branched
2-complex.

The fundamental result about our invariants is the
rationality theorem
, which tells us that the extrema in the above definitions are realised, and in so doing justifies the words ‘maximal’ and ‘minimal’ in the names of the curvatures. The proof realises
\rho_{\pm}(X)
and
\sigma_{\pm}(X)
as extrema of rational linear programming problems, along the lines of Calegari’s rationality theorem for stable commutator length in free groups
[
11
]
. In particular, as well as being rational, these invariants can be computed, at least in principle.

Theorem 1.1

(Rationality theorem)
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible 2-complex, then

\rho_{+}(X)=\max_{Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)}\kappa(Y)

and

\sigma_{+}(X)=\max_{Y\in\mathrm{Surf}(X)}\kappa(Y)\,.

Likewise,
\rho_{-}(X)
and
\sigma_{-}(X)
are the corresponding minima. In particular,
\rho_{\pm}(X),\sigma_{\pm}(X)\in\mathbb{Q}
. Furthermore, they can be computed algorithmically from
X
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
is long and technical, and is therefore in a self-contained paper
[
56
]
.

Theorem
1.1
provokes the question of which ranges of values the curvature invariants can take. The next theorem constrains them inside a certain three-dimensional simplex.

Theorem A

(Curvature inequalities)
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible 2-complex, then

-\infty<\rho_{-}(X)=\sigma_{-}(X)\leq\sigma_{+}(X)\leq\rho_{+}(X)\leq 1\,.

Of the various relationships contained in Theorem
A
, much the most striking and difficult to prove is that
\rho_{-}(X)=\sigma_{-}(X)
, which means that the minimal irreducible curvature is always realised by an essential map from a branched surface.

These results motivate a broad programme, which can be summarised in two problems that we call, for ease of reference, the
botany problem
and the

. Given the historical emphasis in geometric group theory on upper curvature bounds, the cases of
non-positive curvature
(i.e.
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 0
or
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
) and

(i.e.
\rho_{+}(X)<0
or
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
) are of particular interest.

The botany problem asks for examples of 2-complexes satisfying curvature bounds.

(Botany problem)

For natural classes of 2-complexes
X

\sigma_{+}(X)

\sigma_{+}(X)<0

(ii)

If

(iii)

If

Our second broad problem is the geography problem, which asks for constraints on 2-complexes that satisfy curvature bounds.

Problem 1.3

(Geography problem)
.

Prove theorems about 2-complexes
X
satisfying constraints on
\rho_{\pm}(X)
or
\sigma_{\pm}(X)
, especially under the hypotheses that either
\rho_{+}(X)
or
\sigma_{+}(X)
is negative or non-positive.

We offer a few contributions to the geography problem here. The first is an easy observation, which characterises
concise
2-complexes.

Definition 1.4
.

A 2-complex is called
concise
unless it has either two 2-cells with homotopic attaching maps or a single 2-cell whose boundary is a proper power.

Equivalently, for a group presentation
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\mid r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\rangle
, the associated presentation complex
X
is concise if the family of cyclic subgroups
\{\langle r_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle r_{n}\rangle\}
of the free group
F_{m}=\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\rangle
is malnormal. This definition is slightly stronger than the definition of a concise 2-complex used by Chiswell–Collins–Huebschmann
[
15
]
, but is convenient for our purposes.

Proposition C
.

For
X
a finite, irreducible 2-complex, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\rho_{+}(X)=1
;

(ii)

\sigma_{+}(X)=1
;

(iii)

X
is not concise.

The proof is given in §
12.1
. The proposition implies that there is no finite 2-complex
X
with
\sigma_{+}(X)<\rho_{+}(X)=1
. I expect this to be the only collection of curvature constraints compatible with Theorem
A
that are not realised by any finite, irreducible 2-complex.

The next contribution is more interesting, and illustrates the strength of these invariants. It is not difficult to see that any closed surface
X
has constant irreducible curvature – that is,
\rho_{+}(X)=\rho_{-}(X)
(see Example
7.5
). Remarkably, 2-complexes with constant irreducible curvature can be completely classified, and they are all closely related to surfaces. More precisely, they are
irrigid
, which roughly means that they can be obtained from surfaces with boundary by gluing their boundary circles together.

Theorem D
.

Let

It is doubtful that any other collection of curvature constraints leads to a complete characterisation beyond Proposition

2
Branching and area

A 2-dimensional complex
X
is the result of attaching 2-cells to a topological graph along their boundaries. This graph is the 1-skeleton
X_{(1)}
, and is constructed by attaching gluing edges from an edge set
E_{X}
to a vertex set
V_{X}
.
6
6

6

Several formalisms for graphs can be used. For instance,
X_{(1)}
can be taken to be a Serre graph, as in Stallings’ definitive paper
[
51
]
. Our discussion here does not rely on the details of the formalism chosen.
The 2-cells of a 2-complex
X
are called
faces
, and the set of faces is denoted by
F_{X}
. Formally, the faces can be modelled as circles mapping into
X_{(1)}
. After a homotopy, we may always assume that the attaching maps of the faces are immersions, at the cost of removing any face whose attaching maps is homotopically trivial in the 1-skeleton.

Example 2.1

(Presentation complex)
.

A group presentation
\mathcal{P}=\langle A\mid R\rangle
defines a presentation complex
X=X_{\mathcal{P}}
. The 1-skeleton
X_{(1)}
is a wedge of circles indexed by
A
. The faces correspond to the elements of
R
: each
r\in R
defines a loop in
X_{(1)}
, and this loop defines the attaching map of the corresponding face.

The maps we allow between 2-complexes are
combinatorial
, meaning that the send vertices to vertices, edges to edges and faces to faces, all homeomorphically.

This much is standard, but it turns out to be fruitful to extend the category of 2-complexes that we work with in a slightly non-standard way.

Definition 2.2
.

A
branched 2-complex

X
is a 2-complex equipped with a notion of
area
, namely a map

\mathrm{Area}:F_{X}\to\mathbb{Z}_{>0}\,.

The notation
\mathrm{Area}(X)
is also used to denote the sum of the areas of the faces of
X
, and we insist that
\mathrm{Area}(X)>0
.

We also allow a slightly wider selection of maps between branched 2-complexes. A
branched morphism
between branched 2-complexes
\phi:Y\to X
still sends vertices to vertices and edges to edges homeomorphically. It also send faces to faces, but on faces the maps are allowed a single ramification point in the centre; that is, on the faces, a map is modelled on the polynomial map

z\mapsto z^{m}

of the unit disc in the complex plane, for some positive integer
m
. In particular,
\phi
assigns to each face
f
of
Y
a
multiplicity

m_{\phi}(f)\in\mathbb{N}
, namely the ramification index at the centre of
f
. However, the branching of the map
\phi
is constrained to respect area in the following sense:

\mathrm{Area}(f)=m_{\phi}(f)\mathrm{Area}(\phi(f))

for each face
f\in F_{Y}
.

Each 2-complex has a canonical structure as a branched 2-complex, obtained by setting the area of each face equal to 1. We will sometimes call such a 2-complex
standard
, to emphasise that there is no branching. Note that a branched morphism between two standard 2-complexes is just a combinatorial map in the standard sense.

Branched coverings of surfaces provide a wealth of natural examples, as long as one is careful to place the branch points in the centres of faces.

Example 2.3

(Branched self-coverings of the 2-sphere)
.

For each
n\geq 1
, let
X_{n}
be the cellulation of the 2-sphere with two faces glued along an equatorial circle with
n
vertices and
n
edges. Let
X=X_{1}
, and make it into a standard 2-complex by giving both faces area 1. There is an
n
-sheeted map
\phi_{n}:X_{n}\to X
that wraps the equator of
X_{n}

n
times around the equator of
X
. If we set the area of each face of
X_{n}
equal to
n
, then the map
\phi_{n}
is a branched morphism.

Our goal is to understand a 2-complex
X
by analysing a large set of maps
Y\to X
, so it is of the utmost importance to constrain the kinds of maps we allow.

Definition 2.4

(Branched coverings and immersions)
.

A branched map
\phi:Y\to X
is a
branched immersion
if it is locally injective away from the midpoints of the 2-cells; equivalently, a branched immersion is a map that is injective on the links of vertices. If
X
and
Y
are standard then
\phi
is an
immersion
, and is locally injective everywhere.

If
\phi
is a bijection on the links of vertices, then
\phi
is a
branched covering
. An immersion which is a branched covering is exactly the usual topological notion of
covering map
.

Stallings famously explained how to study the subgroups of free groups by folding maps of graphs
[
51
, §5.4]
. Stallings’ folding procedure provides an algorithm to factor a map of finite graphs as a composition of a
\pi_{1}
-surjection and an immersion. Our set-up makes it easy to extend folding to 2-complexes: one just folds the 1-skeleta, and then identifies any 2-cells with the same image. The next lemma makes this precise.

Lemma 2.5

(Folding 2-complexes)
.

Every map of finite, connected, branched 2-complexes
\phi:Y\to X
factors as

Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\phi_{0}}}{{\to}}\bar{Y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bar{\phi}}}{{\to}}X

where
\phi_{0}
is a surjection on fundamental groups and
\bar{\phi}
is a branched immersion.

Cousins of this lemma have already been used extensively
[
32
,
33
,
34
,
35
]
. The reader is referred to
[
56
, Lemma 3.8]
for a proof.

It would be convenient if we could always work with branched immersions. However, sometimes it is necessary to
unfold
in order to understand the structure of a given complex, as the following simple example shows.

Example 2.6

(Folded surface)
.

Consider a compact surface
S
equipped with a simplicial triangulation (i.e. every edge and face has distinct vertices), and let
X
be the result of folding a single pair of adjacent edges of
S
at a common vertex. Then there are no branched immersions from closed surfaces to
X
.

In order to handle examples like this, we relax the definition of a branched immersion slightly to an
essential map
. In related contexts, this definition already played important roles in
[
55
]
and
[
35
]
. The idea is that folding an essential map
Y\to X
does not change any important information about
Y
.

Definition 2.7

(Essential map)
.

A map of branched 2-complexes
\phi:Y\to X
is
essential
if, after folding, the resulting map
\phi_{0}:Y\to\bar{Y}
satisfies the following conditions.

(i)

The induced map on the 1-skeleton
\phi_{0}:Y_{(1)}\to\bar{Y}_{(1)}
is a homotopy equivalence.

(ii)

When restricted to the interiors of faces,
\phi_{0}
is a homeomorphism. Equivalently,
\phi_{0}
preserves area.

If
\phi
is essential and, furthermore,
\bar{\phi}:\bar{Y}\to X
is an isomorphism, then
\phi
is called an
essential equivalence
.

Note that the fold map
S\to X
from Example
2.6
is essential, indeed an essential equivalence. This illustrates an important principle: given a 2-complex
X
, there may be an essential equivalence
Y\to X
such that the structure of
Y
is more apparent than the structure of
X
.

3
Group pairs

It is often convenient to think about 2-complexes in a more algebraic way, provided by the following definition. Throughout this section, the 2-complex
X
is assumed to be connected.

Definition 3.1

(Group pair)
.

A
group pair

(F,\mathcal{A})
consists of a finitely generated free group
F
together with a cofinite action of
F
on a set
\mathcal{A}
such that every stabiliser is infinite cyclic.

There is a natural theory of group pairs, which it is not hard to develop on its own terms. However, the point of this definition is that it captures some of the underlying information of a 2-complex, and so we will develop the theory in parallel with the corresponding properties of 2-complexes and the maps between them.

Definition 3.2

(Group pair of a 2-complex)
.

Every finite, connected 2-complex
X
has an
associated group pair

(F_{X},\mathcal{A}_{X})
, defined as follows. The free group
F_{X}
is the fundamental group of the 1-skeleton
X_{(1)}
. The boundaries of the faces define a finite set
\{\langle w_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle w_{n}\rangle\}
of infinite cyclic subgroups of
F_{X}
, each defined up to conjugacy. The set
\mathcal{A}_{X}
is then the disjoint union of the sets of left-cosets
F_{X}/\langle w_{i}\rangle
, with the natural action by left multiplication.

Remark 3.3
.

Usually
X
is assumed to be concise. This means that the set
\{\langle w_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle w_{n}\rangle\}
is
malnormal
– that is, any intersection
g\langle w_{i}\rangle g^{-1}\cap\langle w_{j}\rangle
is trivial unless
i=j
and
g\in\langle w_{i}\rangle
. In this case,
\mathcal{A}_{X}
can be identified with the disjoint union of the sets of conjugates of the
\langle w_{i}\rangle
, with the natural action by conjugation. We give this slightly more complicated definition in order to handle 2-complexes that are not concise.

A
morphism
of a group pairs
\phi:(F,\mathcal{A})\to(G,\mathcal{B})
consists of a homomorphism
\phi:F\to G
and a set map
\phi:\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
subject to the usual equivariance condition, i.e. that
\phi(g.a)=\phi(g)\phi(a)
. Any map of 2-complexes
\phi:Y\to X
gives rise to a natural map of the associated group pairs

\phi_{*}:(F_{Y},\mathcal{A}_{Y})\to(F_{X},\mathcal{A}_{X})

defined as follows. The group homomorphism
\phi_{*}:F_{Y}\to F_{X}
is the map induced on fundamental groups of 1-skeleta by
\phi
, but there is also an equivariant map
\phi_{*}:\mathcal{A}_{Y}\to\mathcal{A}_{X}
: if
\phi_{*}
sends the face corresponding to
\langle u_{i}\rangle
to the face corresponding to
\langle w_{j}\rangle
then, after choosing conjugacy representatives so that
\phi_{*}(u_{i})\in\langle w_{j}\rangle
, the map
\mathcal{A}_{Y}\to\mathcal{A}_{X}
is defined by

\phi_{*}(h\langle w_{i}\rangle)=\phi_{*}(h)\langle u_{j}\rangle\,.

A morphism of group pairs is said to be
injective
if both the map of free groups and the map of sets is injective. Perhaps surprisingly, this corresponds exactly to the notion of essential map of 2-complexes defined in the previous section. The following lemma
[
55
, Lemma 1.12]
characterises when
\phi
is essential in terms of
\phi_{*}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

A map of finite, connected 2-complexes
\phi:Y\to X
is essential if and only if the induced map of associated group pairs

\phi_{*}:(F_{Y},\mathcal{A}_{Y})\to(F_{X},\mathcal{A}_{X})\,,

is injective.

The lemma shows that, up to essential equivalence, essential maps to
X
from finite, connected complexes
Y
correspond naturally to subgroups
F_{Y}
of
F_{X}
and
F_{Y}
-invariant cofinite subsets
\mathcal{A}_{Y}
of
\mathcal{A}_{X}
.

Remark 3.5
.

It follows immediately from Lemma
3.4
that a composition of essential maps is essential.

Two-complexes that are essentially equivalent in the sense of §
2
have, in particular, isomorphic group pairs. More generally, 2-complexes with isomorphic group pairs are said to be
Nielsen equivalent
.

Definition 3.6
.

An isomorphism of group pairs
(F,\mathcal{A})
and
(H,\mathcal{B})
consists of a group isomorphism
\phi:F\to H
and a
\phi
-equivariant bijection
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
. If the associated group pairs of 2-complexes
X
and
Y
are isomorphic, then
X
and
Y
are said to be
Nielsen equivalent
.

In particular, an essential equivalence of connected 2-complexes
f:Y\to X
is a morphism of 2-complexes that induces an isomorphism between the associated group pairs.

For presentation complexes, Definition
3.6
coincides with the usual definition of Nielsen equivalence of group presentations:
\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\mid r_{1},\ldots,r_{n}\rangle
and
\langle a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{m}\mid r^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,r^{\prime}_{n}\rangle
are Nielsen equivalent if and only if there is an isomorphism of free groups

\alpha:\langle a_{1},\ldots a_{m}\rangle\to\langle a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots a^{\prime}_{m}\rangle

such that, up to permuting indices,
\alpha(r_{i})
is conjugate to
r^{\prime}_{i}
.

For branched 2-complexes, the area of the faces needs to be added to the data of the pair.

Definition 3.7

(Branched group pair)
.

A
branched group pair
consists of a group pair
(F,\mathcal{A})
together with an
F
-invariant map
\mathrm{Area}:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Two branched group pairs are said to be isomorphic if the underlying group pairs
(F,\mathcal{A})
and
(H,\mathcal{B})
are isomorphic and the associated bijection
\mathcal{A}\to\mathcal{B}
preserves area.

It is easy to see that a branched 2-complex determines a branched group pair, and so it makes sense to say that branched 2-complexes are Nielsen equivalent if their associated branched group pairs are isomorphic.

4
Irreducible complexes

Two-dimensional complexes are too complicated to classify up to arbitrary homotopy equivalence. However, sometimes they can be simplified, and the following definition, which also played central roles in
[
55
]
and
[
35
]
, encapsulates various cases in which this is possible. The material in this section is very similar to
[
35
, §3.1–2]
. Note that we do not assume that our 2-complexes are connected.

Definition 4.1

(Visibly reducible and unfoldable)
.

If a 2-complex
X
satisfies any of the following three conditions then it is said to be
visibly reducible
:

(i)

the one-skeleton of
X
has a vertex of valence 0 or 1;

(ii)

a vertex of
X
is locally separating;

(iii)

X
has a
free face
, i.e. an edge that is hit exactly once by one face.

There is one further simplification move that plays an important role.

(iv)

There is an essential equivalence
X^{\prime}\to X
that is not an isomorphism.

If
X
satisfies condition (iv) but none of conditions (i)-(iii) then it is said to be
unfoldable
. If
X
satisfies none of conditions (i)-(iv) then it is said to be
visibly irreducible
.

Remark 4.2
.

Each of conditions (i)-(iv) in Definition
4.1
can be recognised from the structure of the links of the vertices of
X
:

(i)

the link of some vertex has at most one vertex;

(ii)

the link of some vertex is disconnected;

(iii)

the link of some vertex contains a vertex of valence 1;

(iv)

the link of some vertex has a cut vertex.

In each case, there is a corresponding move that simplifies
X
.

In summary, every 2-complex is visibly reducible, or unfoldable, or visibly irreducible. Unfolding increases the number of vertices and edges while keeping the number of faces the same, and so it is not hard to see that a finite complex can only be unfolded finitely many times, at which point it will be either visibly reducible or visibly irreducible. Thus, the following definition applies to all 2-complexes.

Definition 4.3

(Irreducible 2-complex)
.

A finite 2-complex
X
is
irreducible
if there is an essential equivalence
X^{\prime}\to X
such that
X^{\prime}
is visibly irreducible. Otherwise,
X
is
reducible
.

Since it may be possible to unfold a 2-complex
X
in several different ways, it is
a priori
possible that
X
could, on the one hand, be essentially equivalent to a visibly irreducible 2-complex
X^{\prime}
and, on the other hand, also be essentially equivalent to a visibly reducible 2-complex
X^{\prime\prime}
. Using a famous lemma of Whitehead
[
53
]
, it transpires that irreducibility of a 2-complex can be characterised via algebraic properties of the associated group pair. We will use the following convenient formulation (cf.
[
35
, Proposition 3.8]
).

Lemma 4.4

(Whitehead’s lemma)
.

A finite 2-complex
X
is reducible if and only if the associated group pair
(F,\mathcal{A})
satisfies one of the following conditions:

(i)

F
is trivial;

(ii)

F
is cyclic and
\#\mathcal{A}=1
;

(iii)

there is a non-trivial free splitting
F=A*B
and each stabiliser of an element of
\mathcal{A}
is conjugate into either
A
or
B
.

In particular, if
X
is irreducible, then any unfolding sequence eventually terminates in a visibly irreducible 2-complex.

Irreducible 2-complexes are very common. Indeed, even if
X
is reducible, as long as
\pi_{1}(X)
is sufficiently complicated, a procedure of repeatedly unfolding and simplifying will eventually produce a canonical irreducible 2-complex that maps to
X
. The following lemma is proved in
[
35
, Lemma 3.10 and 3.14]
.

Lemma 4.5

(Irreducible cores)
.

Let
X
be a connected, finite, standard 2-complex. If
\pi_{1}X
is not free and doesn’t split non-trivially as a free product, then there is an immersion
\phi:\widehat{X}\to X
such that
\widehat{X}
is irreducible and
\phi
is an isomorphism of fundamental groups. Furthermore, any branched immersion from an irreducible branched complex
Y\to X
factors canonically through
\widehat{X}
.

The complex
\widehat{X}
is called the
irreducible core
of
X
. In fact, any finite complex
X
can be factored into irreducible 2-complexes and simple discs, where a
simple disc
is a 2-complex
D
with a single face and homeomorphic to a disc.

Lemma 4.6

(Unfolding a 2-complex)
.

Let
X
be any finite, connected, standard 2-complex. There is a finite, connected graph
G
, a finite set of connected, irreducible 2-complexes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
, and a finite set of discs
D_{1},\ldots,D_{n}
such that there is an essential equivalence

X^{\prime}=G\vee X_{1}\vee\ldots\vee X_{m}\vee D_{1}\vee\ldots\vee D_{n}\to X

for some choice of iterated wedge
X^{\prime}
of the factors.

The proof is similar to the proofs of Lemmas
4.4
and
4.5
, and left as an exercise to the reader. The 2-complex
X^{\prime}
is called an
unfolding
of
X
. We record one important consequence here.

Lemma 4.7

(Irreducible immersion)
.

Let
X
be a connected, finite, standard 2-complex. Unless
X
is homotopy equivalent to a graph, there is an immersion
Y\to X
with
Y
irreducible.

Proof.

Consider an unfolding
X^{\prime}
coming from Lemma
4.6
. If
m\geq 1
then we may take
Y
to be the result of folding the map
X_{1}\to X
. Otherwise,
X^{\prime}
is a obtained by wedging discs onto the graph
G
, and so is homotopy equivalent to a graph, which implies that
X
is too.
∎

Thus, as proposed in the introduction, we can study very general classes of 2-complexes
X
via maps from irreducible 2-complexes. The reader should note, however, that even the very weak hypothesis of Lemma
4.7
is not sharp, since there are irreducible 2-complexes that are homotopic to a point.

Example 4.8
.

Let
X
be the presentation complex associated to the presentation
\langle a,b\mid bab^{-1}a^{-2},b\rangle
. Since this is a balanced presentation of the trivial group, it follows from the Hurewicz and Whitehead theorems that
X
is contractible. But the link of the unique vertex of
X
is the complete graph on 4 generators, so
X
is irreducible by Remark
4.2
.

Example
4.8
is the first of an infinite family of examples of contractible, irreducible 2-complexes constructed by Miller and Schupp
[
41
]
.

5
Surfaces

A finite 2-complex
X
is said to be a
surface
if its realisation is a closed topological surface. Equivalently,
X
is a surface if and only if the link of every vertex is a circle. Since circles do not satisfy any of the conditions of Remark
4.2
, it follows that any surface is irreducible. Surfaces provide some of the most important examples of irreducible complexes, and they will play a special role.

Just as Whitehead’s lemma characterises the group pairs associated to irreducible complexes, we will also need a result that characterises the 2-complexes that are Nielsen equivalent to surfaces. This is provided by the following lemma; the proof follows a theorem of Culler
[
17
]
.

Lemma 5.1

(Culler’s lemma)
.

If
X
is a finite 2-complex which is Nielsen equivalent to a surface
S
then there is a (homeomorphic, but possibly combinatorially distinct) surface
S^{\prime}
and an essential equivalence
S^{\prime}\to X
.

Proof.

Let
N
be a closed tubular neighbourhood of the 1-skeleton of
S
in its realisation. Then
N
is homotopy equivalent to
S_{(1)}
and the boundary components of
N
correspond exactly to the faces of
S
. Because
X
and
S
are Nielsen equivalent, the fundamental groups of the 1-skeleta
S_{(1)}
and
X_{(1)}
are isomorphic, and because graphs are Eilenberg–Mac Lane spaces, this isomorphism is induced by a homotopy equivalence
\phi:N\to X_{(1)}
.

Since the group isomorphism comes from a Nielsen equivalence it also respects the faces, and
\phi_{*}
sends the conjugacy classes of the cyclic subgroups of
\pi_{1}(N)
corresponding to the boundary components of
N
bijectively to the conjugacy classes of the cyclic subgroups of
\pi_{1}(X_{(1)})
corresponding to the faces of
X
.

We now follow the proof of
[
17
, Theorem 1.4]
to endow the realisation of
S
with a new 2-complex structure, so that
\phi
becomes a morphism of 2-complexes. After a homotopy, we may take
\phi
to be transverse to the midpoints of edges of
X_{(1)}
. The preimages of these midpoints are then disjoint unions of properly embedded circles and arcs in
N
. Furthermore, the complementary regions map to neighbourhoods of vertices and so, since
\phi
is a homotopy equivalence, every complementary region is a topological disc.

In particular, each embedded circle in the preimage bounds a disc, and so can be removed by a homotopy of
\phi
which reduces the number of components of preimages. In the same way, we may also remove any arcs that are isotopic into the boundary of
N
.

After these modifications, the union of the preimages of the midpoints of edges of
X_{(1)}
is a collection of properly embedded arcs, which cut
N
into discs. The dual of this decomposition of
N
is a graph
S^{\prime}_{(1)}
, and
\phi
naturally induces a morphism of graphs
\phi^{\prime}
from
S^{\prime}_{(1)}
to
X_{(1)}
. Furthermore,
\phi^{\prime}
is a homotopy equivalence because
\phi
was. Finally, we view the natural maps from the boundary components of
N
to
S^{\prime}_{(1)}
as attaching maps to define a 2-complex
S^{\prime}
, and
\phi^{\prime}
extends to a morphism of 2-complexes
S^{\prime}\to X
. We have already seen that
\phi^{\prime}
is a homotopy equivalence of 1-skeleta, and the construction also guarantees that
\phi^{\prime}
sends faces to faces bijectively, so
\phi^{\prime}
is in fact an essential equivalence.
∎

While Lemma
4.7
implies that there exist essential maps from irreducible 2-complexes to a 2-complex
X
under very mild hypotheses on
X
, it is far from obvious that there should be a similar result guaranteeing the existence of essential maps from surfaces to general
X
. However, such a result exists, and follows by combining Culler’s lemma with
[
55
, Theorem F]
.

Theorem 5.2

(Essential maps from surfaces exist)
.

Every finite, irreducible complex
X
admits an essential branched map
S\to X
, where
S
is a surface.

Surfaces are in some sense the simplest irreducible 2-complexes. The next theorem offers one way of making this heuristic precise.

Theorem 5.3

(Surfaces are minimal among irreducible complexes)
.

A finite, irreducible 2-complex
X
is a surface if and only if it satisfies the following property: every essential map
\phi:Y\to X
, with
Y
finite and irreducible, is a finite-sheeted branched covering map.

Proof.

We start with the ‘only if’ direction. Suppose that
X
is a surface, so every link of a vertex in
X
is a circle. Consider an essential map
\phi:Y\to X
, where
Y
is irreducible. If
\bar{\phi}:\bar{Y}\to X
is the folded representative then
\bar{Y}
is also irreducible, by Whitehead’s lemma. Since the folded representative is a branched immersion, it induces injective maps on links; in particular, the link of each vertex of
\bar{Y}
embeds into a circle. But every proper, non-empty subgraph of a circle has a vertex of valence either zero or one, which would imply that
\bar{Y}
is visibly reducible, a contradiction. Therefore,
\bar{\phi}
is surjective on links and hence a branched covering map. Finally, since circles do not have cut vertices,
\bar{Y}
is not unfoldable, and hence
Y=\bar{Y}
.

To prove the ‘if’ direction, suppose that
X
satisfies the given property, so that any essential map
Y\to X
is a finite-sheeted branched covering map if
Y
is finite and irreducible. By Theorem
5.2
, there is an essential map
S\to X
with
S
of surface type. By the hypothesis the map
S\to X
is a branched covering map, so the induced maps on links are isomorphisms. Thus every link of
X
is also a circle, and so
X
is also a surface.
∎

6
Curvature invariants

We are now ready to define our notions of curvature. The first definitions adapt Euler characteristic to the context of branched complexes.

Definition 6.1

(Total and average curvature)
.

The
total curvature
of a finite branched 2-complex
X
is the quantity

\tau(X):=\mathrm{Area}(X)+\chi(X_{(1)})

where
\chi(X_{(1)})
is the usual Euler characteristic of the 1-skeleton
X_{(1)}
, namely
\#V_{X}-\#E_{X}
. The
average curvature
of
X
is

\kappa(X):=\frac{\tau(X)}{\mathrm{Area}(X)}=1+\frac{\chi(X_{(1)})}{\mathrm{Area}(X)}\,.

The total curvature is closely related to the Euler characteristic.

Remark 6.2
.

If
X
is standard then
\tau(X)
is the Euler characteristic
\chi(X)
. Furthermore, if
\phi:Y\to X
is a branched map then each face
f
has area
m_{\phi}(f)\geq 1
, and so
\tau(Y)\geq\chi(Y)
.

On their own, the total and average curvatures cannot determine much about
X
. The idea of our next invariants is to extremise these quantities over complexes mapping to
X
.

Definition 6.3

(Irreducible curvature)
.

Let
X
be a finite branched 2-complex, and let
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
denote the set of essential maps
Y\to X
, where
Y
is any finite, irreducible, branched 2-complex. The quantity

\rho_{+}(X):=\sup_{Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)}\kappa(Y)

is called the
maximal irreducible curvature
of
X
. Likewise, the
minimal irreducible curvature

\rho_{-}(X)
is defined to be the corresponding infimum. By convention,
\rho_{\pm}(X)=\mp\infty
if
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
is empty.

We saw in §
4
that
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
is usually non-empty; for instance, Lemma
4.7
says that
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
is non-empty unless
X
is homotopy-equivalent to a graph.

Definition
6.3
can be used to say that a 2-complex
X
has
non-positive
irreducible curvature (if
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 0
),
negative
irreducible curvature (if
\rho_{+}(X)<0
),
constant
irreducible curvature (
\rho_{+}(X)=\rho_{-}(X)
), and so on. Since geometric group theorists are traditionally most interested in upper bounds on curvature, we will be especially concerned with
\rho_{+}(X)
.

Instead of extremising over essential maps from the relatively large class of irreducible complexes, one may instead restrict attention to the smaller class of (closed) surfaces, and this leads to another pair of invariants in the same way.

Definition 6.4

(Surface curvature)
.

Let
X
be a finite branched 2-complex, and let
\mathrm{Surf}(X)
denote the set of essential maps
Y\to X
, where
Y
is a closed surface. The quantity

\sigma_{+}(X):=\sup_{Y\in\mathrm{Surf}(X)}\kappa(Y)

is called the
maximal surface curvature
of
X
. Likewise, the
minimal surface curvature

\sigma_{-}(X)
is defined to be the corresponding infimum. We again adopt the convention that
\sigma_{\pm}(X)=\mp\infty
if
\mathrm{Surf}(X)
is empty.

Since a composition of essential maps is essential, Lemma
4.7
and Theorem
5.2
together guarantees that
\mathrm{Surf}(X)
is non-empty unless
X
is homotopy equivalent to a graph.

As before, Definition
6.4
can be used to make sense of the ideas of a 2-complex
X
having
non-positive
surface curvature (if
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
),
negative
surface curvature (if
\rho_{+}(X)<0
) etc., and we will be especially concerned with
\sigma_{+}(X)
, because of geometric group theory’s traditional focus on upper curvature bounds.

7
Inequalities and first examples

We start with a few easy observations. Throughout this section we will assume that
X
is an irreducible branched complex. However, the irreducibility hypothesis can usually be relaxed to the weaker assumption that
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
is non-empty.

The first result records some easy inequalities between the invariants.

Lemma 7.1

(Trivial inequalities)
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible, branched 2-complex then

-\infty\leq\rho_{-}(X)\leq\sigma_{-}(X)\leq\sigma_{+}(X)\leq\rho_{+}(X)\leq 1\,.

Proof.

The first inequality, that
-\infty\leq\rho_{-}(X)
, is vacuous. Since surfaces are irreducible, there is a natural inclusion
\mathrm{Surf}(X)\subseteq\mathrm{Irred}(X)
, whence
\rho_{-}(X)\leq\sigma_{-}(X)
and
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq\rho_{+}(X)
. By Theorem
5.2
,
\mathrm{Surf}(X)\neq\varnothing
, so
\sigma_{-}(X)\leq\sigma_{+}(X)
. Finally, for any essential
Y\to X
, we have

\kappa(Y)=1+\frac{\chi(Y_{(1)})}{\mathrm{Area}(Y)}

by definition. If
Y
is irreducible then in particular the 1-skeleton
Y_{(1)}
is a core graph, so
\chi(Y_{(1)})\leq 0
and
\kappa(Y)\leq 1
. Taking the supremum over all
Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)
, the final inequality follows,
∎

Eventually, we will sharpen these inequalities to prove Theorem
A
. The next lemma records that the invariants are monotonic in the obvious way under essential maps.

Lemma 7.2

(Monotonicity)
.

If
\psi:X^{\prime}\to X
is an essential branched morphism of finite, irreducible, branched 2-complexes then:

(i)

\rho_{+}(X^{\prime})\leq\rho_{+}(X)
and
\rho_{-}(X^{\prime})\geq\rho_{-}(X)
;

(ii)

\sigma_{+}(X^{\prime})\leq\sigma_{+}(X)
and
\sigma_{-}(X^{\prime})\geq\sigma_{-}(X)
.

Proof.

Since a composition of essential maps is essential, there is a natural map
\psi_{*}:\mathrm{Irred}(X^{\prime})\to\mathrm{Irred}(X)
obtained by composing an essential map
Y\to X
with
\psi
. Since
\kappa(\psi_{*}(Y))=\kappa(Y)
, this proves item (i). Furthermore,
\psi_{*}(\mathrm{Surf}(X^{\prime}))\subseteq\mathrm{Surf}(X)
, so item (ii) also follows.
∎

When the essential map in question is a branched covering, we can say more. The next lemma encodes the observation that average curvature is constant under passing to branched coverings. Note that the restriction of any branched covering map
\phi:X^{\prime}\to X
to 1-skeleta is a covering map of graphs. If
X
is connected then this covering map has a degree, and we may define the
degree
of
\phi
to be the degree of the covering map of 1-skeleta. In particular,
\phi
is said to be
finite sheeted
if
\deg(\phi)
is finite.

The next lemma shows that the quantities
\tau(X)
and
\kappa(X)
interact well with finite-sheeted branched covering maps.

Lemma 7.3

(Finite-sheeted branched coverings)
.

If
\phi:X^{\prime}\to X
is a finite-sheeted branched covering map of finite, irreducible 2-complexes then
\tau(X^{\prime})=\deg(\phi)\tau(X)
and
\kappa(X^{\prime})=\kappa(X)
.

Proof.

The proof that

\tau(X^{\prime})=\deg(\phi)\tau(X)

is identical to the standard proof of the Riemann–Hurwitz theorem. Since
\mathrm{Area}(X^{\prime})=\deg(\phi)\mathrm{Area}(X)
trivially, it follows that
\kappa(X^{\prime})=\kappa(X)
.
∎

It follows that our curvature bounds
\rho_{\pm}
and
\sigma_{\pm}
are also invariant under passing to branched coverings.

Proposition 7.4

(Branched coverings and curvature bounds)
.

If
X^{\prime}\to X
is a finite-sheeted branched covering map of finite, irreducible 2-complexes then
\rho_{\pm}(X^{\prime})=\rho_{\pm}(X)
and
\sigma_{\pm}(X^{\prime})=\sigma_{\pm}(X)
.

Proof.

We prove the proposition for the irreducible curvatures; the proof for surface curvatures is identical. By Lemma
7.2
,
\rho_{-}(X)\leq\rho_{-}(X^{\prime})
and
\rho_{+}(X)\geq\rho_{+}(X^{\prime})
, so it suffices to prove the reverse inequalities.

Suppose now that
Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
Y
is visibly irreducible. Since branched immersions pull back along essential maps
[
35
, Lemma 3.13]
, there is a finite-sheeted branched covering
Y^{\prime}\to Y
and an essential map
Y^{\prime}\to X^{\prime}
. Since
Y^{\prime}
has the same links as the visibly irreducible complex
Y
, it is itself visibly irreducible, so
Y^{\prime}\in\mathrm{Irred}(X^{\prime})
. Now
\kappa(Y^{\prime})=\kappa(Y)
by Lemma
7.3
and so, taking suprema or infima as appropriate, the result follows.
∎

A little caution is needed when applying Proposition
7.4
. If
X
is standard then
X^{\prime}
is only standard if the map
X^{\prime}\to X
is an
unbranched
covering.

The results discussed so far make it easy to compute the curvatures of our first family of examples.

Example 7.5

(Surfaces)
.

Consider a closed surface
S
. By Theorem
5.3
,
Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(S)
if and only if there is a finite-sheeted branched covering
Y\to S
, so
\kappa(Y)=\kappa(S)
by Lemma
7.3
. Thus, by definition,

\rho_{\pm}(S)=\kappa(S)

and therefore
\sigma_{\pm}(S)=\kappa(S)
by Lemma
7.1
. In particular, if
S
is standard and has one face, then all curvatures equal the Euler characteristic
\chi(S)
.

In summary, surfaces have constant irreducible curvature. In fact, Theorem
D
tells us that the equation
\rho_{+}(S)=\rho_{-}(S)
is surprisingly close to a characterisation of surfaces.

8
Nielsen equivalence

The curvature invariants defined here are too sensitive to be invariant under homotopy equivalence. The irreducible, contractible 2-complex
X
of Example
4.8
provides an absurd example, since

\rho_{+}(X)\geq\kappa(X)=1/2

but
X
is homotopy equivalent to the 1-point complex
*
, which has
\rho_{+}(X)=-\infty
.

Thus,
\rho_{+}
is not invariant under homotopy equivalence, and indeed similar reasoning tells us that neither are
\rho_{-}
or
\sigma_{\pm}
. However, they are invariant under a stronger equivalence relation. Recall from §
3
that two 2-complexes are
Nielsen equivalent
if their associated group pairs are isomorphic. The purpose of this section is to prove that the quantities
\rho_{\pm}(X)
and
\sigma_{\pm}(X)
are invariant under Nielsen equivalence. That is, we need to show that
\rho_{\pm}(X)
and
\sigma_{\pm}(X)
only depend on the associated group pair
(F_{X},\mathcal{A}_{X})
.

Since taking fundamental groups only makes good sense for connected complexes, the first observation is that we may assume that every
Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)
is connected.

Remark 8.1
.

Let
Y
be a finite branched 2-complex of positive area. If
Y=Y_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup Y_{n}
, then
\tau(Y)
is the sum of the
\tau(Y_{i})
, so

\kappa(Y)=\sum_{i}t_{i}\kappa(Y_{i})

where
0\leq t_{i}=\mathrm{Area}(Y_{i})/\mathrm{Area}(Y)\leq 1
. In particular,

\min_{i}\kappa(Y_{i})\leq\kappa(Y)\leq\max_{i}\kappa(Y_{i})

and so, in the definitions of
\rho_{\pm}(X)
and
\sigma_{\pm}(X)
, we may restrict attention to connected
Y
in
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
or
\mathrm{Surf}(X)
.

By Lemma
3.4
, the branched group pairs of connected branched complexes in
\mathrm{Irred}(X)
correspond exactly to branched group pairs
(F_{Y},\mathcal{A}_{Y})
equipped with injective maps
\phi_{*}:(F_{Y},\mathcal{A}_{Y})\to(F_{X},\mathcal{A}_{X})
. The next step is therefore to compute
\kappa(Y)
from the group pair
(F_{Y},\mathcal{A}_{Y})
, which comes down to computing
\chi(Y_{(1)})
and
\mathrm{Area}(Y)
. The former is straightforward, since

\chi(Y_{(1)})=1-\mathrm{rk}F_{Y}

when
Y
is connected.

For the latter, we need to associate an area to each
b\in\mathcal{A}_{Y}
, which corresponds to some face
f
of
Y
. The image
a=\phi_{*}(b)\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
has stabiliser conjugate to some
\langle w_{j}\rangle
which in turn corresponds to the face
\phi(f)
of
X
. After conjugating, we may assume that the stabiliser of
b
is some
\langle u_{i}\rangle
and that
\phi_{*}(u_{i})\in\langle w_{j}\rangle
. The multiplicity of
f
can now be computed as the index

m_{\phi}(f)=|\langle w_{i}\rangle:\langle\phi_{*}(u_{j})\rangle|

so, by the compatibility between area and multiplicity, we may compute the area of

as

\mathrm{Area}(f)=|\langle w_{i}\rangle:\langle\phi_{*}(u_{j})\rangle|\mathrm{Area}(\phi(f))\,.

Since
\mathrm{Area}(Y)
is the sum of the areas of its faces, this shows that
\mathrm{Area}(Y)
can also be computed from the group pair.

Since Whitehead’s lemma (Lemma
4.4
) characterises those group pairs
(F_{Y},\mathcal{A}_{Y})
that correspond to
irreducible
complexes
Y
, it follows that
\rho_{\pm}(X)
are determined by the group pair
(F,\mathcal{A})
associated to
X
. Thus we have proved:

Proposition 8.2

(Nielsen invariance of irreducible curvatures)
.

If
X
and
X^{\prime}
are Nielsen equivalent 2-complexes, then
\rho_{\pm}(X)=\rho_{\pm}(X^{\prime})
.

An identical argument substituting Culler’s lemma for Whitehead’s lemma shows that
\sigma_{\pm}(X)
also only depends on the group pair, yielding the following:

Proposition 8.3

(Nielsen invariance of surface curvatures)
.

If two finite, irreducible 2-complexes
X
and
X^{\prime}
are Nielsen equivalent, then
\sigma_{\pm}(X)=\sigma_{\pm}(X^{\prime})
.

9
Irrigid complexes

This section is devoted to studying a class of 2-complexes that slightly generalise surfaces, and which played important roles in
[
54
]
and
[
55
]
. Here we christen them
irrigid complexes
. Two important theorems are proved: Theorem

, specifically the fact that
\rho_{-}=\sigma_{-}
, and Theorem

Definition 9.1
.

A
surface decomposition
of a group pair
(F,\mathcal{A})
is a graph of groups
\mathcal{G}
such that
\pi_{1}\mathcal{G}=F
of the following form. The edge groups are all infinite cyclic. The underlying graph is bipartite, with vertices of the following two types.

(i)

Surface vertices

v
have an associated compact surface with boundary
\Sigma_{v}
, and the associated vertex group is identified with
\pi_{1}(\Sigma_{v})
.

(ii)

Cyclic vertices
have infinite-cyclic vertex groups.

For a surface vertex
v
, the incident edge groups are identified isomorphically with the fundamental groups of the boundary components of
\Sigma_{v}
. Some subset of the cyclic vertices, called the
peripheral
vertices, is identified with the
F
-orbits of
\mathcal{A}
, and the vertex group of a peripheral cyclic vertex is the corresponding stabiliser (which is well-defined up to conjugacy).

A 2-complex
X
is called
irrigid
if the associated group pair of each connected component admits a surface decomposition.

It is immediately clear that if
X
is a surface then it is irrigid. Baumslag–Solitar groups provide a more interesting class of examples.

Example 9.2
.

Let
X
be the standard presentation complex of the Baumslag–Solitar group

BS(m,n)=\langle a,b\mid ba^{m}b^{-1}a^{-n}\rangle

and let

denote the relator
ba^{m}b^{-1}a^{-n}
. The free group

can be viewed as the fundamental group of the following space. Let
\Sigma
be a 3-holed sphere with boundary components
c_{0},c_{1},r
, and let
C
be a circle with fundamental group generated by a simple loop

. Then construct a graph of spaces
\mathcal{X}
by identifying
c_{0}
with
a^{n}
and
c_{1}
with
a^{-m}
. This gluing introduces a stable letter
b
in the fundamental group of
\mathcal{X}
, and the fundamental group of
\mathcal{X}
naturally has presentation

\langle a,b,c_{0},c_{1},r\mid c_{0}c_{1}r\,,\,c_{0}a^{-n}\,,c_{1}ba^{m}b^{-1}\rangle\,.

It follows that
\pi_{1}(\mathcal{X})
is free on the generators
a,b
, while
c_{0}=a^{n}
,
c_{1}=ba^{-m}b^{-1}
, and

. Therefore, the graph-of-groups decomposition for

implicit in the construction of

is a surface decomposition of

In general, an algorithm of Cashen can be used to determine whether or not a given pair is irrigid

13
]
.

Although irrigid complexes are more general than surfaces, they can in practice be replaced by branched maps from surfaces.

Lemma 9.3
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible, irrigid complex, then there is a branched surface
S
with
\kappa(S)=\kappa(X)
and an essential map

.

Sketch proof.

Working componentwise, we may assume that
X
is connected, so the associated group pair
(F,\mathcal{A})
has a surface decomposition
\mathcal{G}

\mathcal{G}
is an isomorphism by Marshall Hall’s theorem

30

For each cyclic vertex group of
\mathcal{G}

of

\mathcal{G}_{1}
and
\mathcal{G}_{2}

The resulting graph of groups

consists of surfaces glued along their boundary components and so, attaching 2-cells to each vertex group as they were attached in the two copies of
\mathcal{G}

(H,\mathcal{B})
of surface type. By construction, there is an induced map of group pairs
(H,\mathcal{B})\to(F,\mathcal{A})

7

This is where the fact that each edge group is distinct from its successor in the cyclic order is used.
so

It remains to compute

\kappa(X)=\kappa(X_{1}\sqcup X_{2})

The next lemma is a restatement of

Lemma 9.4
.

Let

With these results in hand, we can now easily prove two of the theorems from the introduction.

Proof of Theorem
A
.

In the light of Lemma

, it suffices to prove that

-\infty<\rho_{-}(X)=\sigma_{-}(X)\,.

The rationality theorem tells us that
\rho_{-}(X)
is realised by some

\rho_{-}(X)=\kappa(Y)>-\infty

such that

\sigma_{-}(X)\leq\sigma_{-}(Y)\leq\kappa(S)=\rho_{-}(X)

by Lemma

∎

Our second theorem is the classification of complexes with constant irreducible curvature. Precisely, it asserts that every such 2-complex is irrigid.

8

8

Proof of Theorem

If

\rho_{-}(X)\leq\kappa(Y)<\kappa(X)\leq\rho_{+}(X)

Since it plays an important role, it is also worth mentioning that the results stated here also give a proof that

10
Relations to other invariants

Although
\sigma_{\pm}
and
\rho_{\pm}
appear to be new invariants, they are related to various other invariants already in the literature. The purpose of this section is to explain some of these relationships.

10.1
Deficiency

The deficiency of a presentation is an alternative normalisation of the Euler characteristic, traditionally used in combinatorial group theory.

Definition 10.1

(Deficiency)
.

The
deficiency
of a finite group presentation

\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\mid r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\rangle

is the quantity
n-m
. Note that this is
\delta(X)=1-\chi(X)
, where
X
is the associated presentation complex. The
deficiency

\delta(G)
of a group
G
is the maximum deficiency of a presentation for that group.

Presentations with deficiencies bounded below have been a popular topic throughout the history of combinatorial group theory.
9
9

9

The Baumslag–Pride theorem, that groups with
\delta(G)\geq 2
are large, is perhaps the most notable result in this direction
[
2
]
.
One limitation is that no general theorems can be proved about the subgroups of groups with deficiency bounded below, since by taking free products any finitely presented group appears as a free factor in a presentation with arbitrarily large deficiency. However, upper bounds on irreducible curvature in turn impose a natural lower bound on the deficiencies of the freely indecomposable finitely presented subgroups of
G=\pi_{1}(X)
.

Remark 10.2
.

Let
X
be a finite, standard 2-complex. The inclusion of any non-free, freely indecomposable, finitely presented subgroup
H
of
G=\pi_{1}(X)
is represented by an immersion
Y\to X
, where
Y
is irreducible
[
33
, Lemma 4.2]
. If
\rho_{+}(X)=0
then
\tau(Y)\leq 0
for every such
Y
, and so

\delta(H)\geq\delta(Y)=1-\chi(Y)\geq 1-\tau(Y)\geq 1\,.

Likewise, if
\rho_{+}(X)<0
then
\tau(Y)<0
for every such
Y
, and so
\delta(H)\geq 2
.

This suggests that
\rho_{+}(X)
should be thought of as a a ‘stabilised’ version of deficiency. The 2-complexes with non-positive irreducible curvature provide good candidates for presentations of deficiency at least one about which one can hope to prove useful theorems. Likewise, the 2-complexes with negative irreducible curvature provide good candidates for presentations of deficiency at least two to study. A few such theorems are collected in §
12
below.

10.2
Primitivity rank

Primitivity rank is an invariant of words in free groups introduced by Puder
[
47
]
. A word
w
in a free group
F
is
primitive
if it is contained in a minimal generating set, and otherwise is said to be
imprimitive
.

Definition 10.3

(Primitivity rank)
.

Let
F
be a free group and
w\in F
a non-trivial word. The quantity

\pi(w):=\min\{\mathrm{rk}(H)\mid w\mathrm{\,imprimitive\,in\,}H\leq F\}

is the
primitivity rank
of
w
. By convention, if
w
is primitive in
F
(and hence in all its subgroups) then
\pi(w)=\infty
.

Puder and Parzanchevski related the primitivity rank to the behaviour of random word maps on symmetric groups
[
48
]
, while Louder and the author related it to the subgroup structure of 1-relator groups
[
34
]
10
10

10

See §
11.3
for further discussion of one-relator groups.
.The connection with one-relator groups stems from the observation that the primitivity rank is also related to an extremal value of the total curvature, when restricted to immersed 2-complexes of area 1.

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
F
be a free group, let
w\in F
be a non-trivial element, and let
X
be the natural presentation complex of the one-relator group
F/\llangle w\rrangle
. Then

2-\pi(w)=\max\{\tau(Y)\mid Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)\,,\,\mathrm{Area}(Y)=1\}

Proof.

If
H\leq F
realises the minimum in the definition of
\pi(w)
then the associated immersion of core graphs
Y_{(1)}\to X_{(1)}
carries a lift
\hat{w}
of
w
. Since
H
cannot split freely relative to
w
, the complex
Y
constructed by attaching a face along
\hat{w}
to
Y_{(1)}
is irreducible, by Whitehead’s lemma. Since

\tau(Y)=2-\mathrm{rk}H=2-\pi(w)

this establishes that the left hand side of the desired equation is no greater than the right hand side.

To prove the reverse inequality, it remains to show that any other
Z\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)
with
\mathrm{Area}(Z)=1
has
\tau(Z)\leq\tau(Y)
. Indeed, the 1-skeleton
Z_{(1)}
defines a subgroup
K
of
F
containing
w
imprimitively, again by Whitehead’s lemma. But
\tau(Z)>\tau(Y)
implies that
\mathrm{rk}(K)<\mathrm{rk}(H)
, because
\mathrm{Area}(Z)=\mathrm{Area}(Y)=1
, contradicting the hypothesis that
H
realises the minimum.
∎

The lemma quickly implies a relationship between the primitivity rank and the maximal irreducible curvature.

Lemma 10.5

(Primitivity rank and
\rho_{+}(X)
)
.

If
X
is the presentation complex of the one-relator group
F/\llangle w\rrangle
then

2-\pi(w)\leq\rho_{+}(X)\,.

Proof.

If
\mathrm{Area}(Y)=1
then
\kappa(Y)=\tau(Y)
, so Lemma
10.4
shows that
2-\pi(w)=\kappa(Y)
for some
Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)
. The result is then immediate.
∎

This result provides useful estimates of
\rho_{+}
because the primitivity rank can often be computed by hand for small examples; see, for instance,
[
34
, Example 1.4]
.
11
11

11

More generally, algorithms to compute
\pi(w)
are described in
[
48

and
[

, Lemma 6.4]
.

Lemma

suggests that the quantity
\rho_{+}(X)
can be thought of as stabilised version of primitivity rank, in analogy with stabilising commutator length to give stable commutator length.

12

for more on stable commutator length.
We therefore propose the following definition.

Definition 10.6
.

Let
F
be a free group and
w\in F
be a non-trivial element. The
stable primitivity rank
of
w
is defined to be

s\pi(w):=1-\rho_{+}(X)

where
X
is the natural presentation complex associated to the one-relator group
F/\llangle w\rrangle
.

13

13

The reader may reasonably wonder why
s\pi(w)
is defined to be
1-\rho_{+}(X)
, rather than

. This is because
s\pi(w)
should be thought of as a (suitably normalised) Euler characteristic of a graph, not of a 2-complex, so we should subtract the term coming from the face of

A much stronger improvement of Lemma

.

If

is the presentation complex of the one-relator group

10.3
Stable commutator length

Let
w
be a non-trivial element of a free group
F
as in the previous section, but this time we assume that
w
is in the commutator subgroup
[F,F]
. Recall the standard definitions of commutator length and its stable cousin.

Definition 10.8

(Commutator length and stable commutator length)
.

The
commutator length
of
w
,
\mathrm{cl}(w)
, is the minimal positive integer
n
such that
w
is a product of
n
commutators. The quantity

\mathrm{scl}(w):=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{\mathrm{cl}(w^{n})}{n}

is the
stable commutator length
of
w
. (In fact this makes sense for any group.)

There are two important results about stable commutator length in a free group
F
. The Duncan–Howie theorem asserts that
\mathrm{scl}(w)\geq 1/2
unless
w
is a proper power
[
20
]
. Calegari’s rationality theorem asserts that
\mathrm{scl}(w)\in\mathbb{Q}
, and furthermore there is an algorithm to compute it
[
11
]
. In fact, this algorithm is effective, and has been implemented
[
12
]
.

As usual, we may consider a standard presentation complex
X
for
F/\llangle{w}\rrangle
, with a single face
f
. The next lemma documents a relationship between the surface curvature of
X
and the stable commutator length.

Lemma 10.9

(Surface curvature and stable commutator length)
.

For
w\in[F,F]
and
X
the standard presentation complex of
F/\llangle{w}\rrangle
,

1-2\mathrm{scl}(w)\leq\sigma_{+}(X)\,.

The lemma will follow from the stronger Proposition
10.12
below. The point of Lemma
10.9
is that the quantity
1-2\mathrm{scl}(w)
can be thought of as an
oriented
version of the surface curvature for the complex
X
. In this oriented version the areas of the faces of surfaces mapping to
X
are counted with signs. Therefore, the oriented area is necessarily smaller than the unoriented area, which leads to the stated inequality.

From this point of view, the Duncan–Howie theorem asserts that the oriented surface curvature of a one-relator complex is always non-positive (cf. the stronger Theorem
11.18
below), while Calegari’s theorem asserts that the oriented surface curvature is rational and computable (cf. the rationality theorem
[
56
]
).

In fact, the above discussion can be generalised beyond the one-relator context, to an arbitrary oriented 2-complex.

Definition 10.10

(Orientation)
.

An
orientation
on a branched 2-complex
X
is a cellular 2-cycle

c=\sum_{f\in F(X)}c_{f}.f

such that
|c_{f}|=\mathrm{Area}(f)
for each face
f
. An
oriented 2-complex
is a 2-complex
X
equipped with an orientation
c
.

Since
c
is a 2-cycle it defines a non-trivial element of
H_{2}(X,\mathbb{R})
, and also a non-trivial element of the second relative homology of the 1-skeleton
X_{(1)}
relative to the faces. In particular,
\partial c
is a boundary in the graph
X_{(1)}
, and so we can define the
stable commutator length
of
\partial c
. We recall the definition below, and refer the reader to Calegari’s monograph
[
10
]
for an authoritative discussion.

Definition 10.11

(Stable commutator length)
.

Let
\mathrm{Admiss}(c)
be the set of continuous maps of compact, oriented surfaces
S\to X_{(1)}
(not necessarily connected, but without spherical components) such that

\partial S\mapsto\deg(S)\partial c\,,

where
\deg(S)
is a positive real number. Then

\mathrm{scl}(c):=\inf_{S\in\mathrm{Admiss}(c)}-\frac{\chi(S)}{2\deg(S)}\,.

In the case where
X
is the presentation complex of a one-relator group
F/\llangle w\rrangle
, this coincides with the usual definition of stable commutator length given above
[
10
, Proposition 2.10]
. We can now phrase a relationship between stable commutator length and surface curvatures for all oriented 2-complexes.

Proposition 10.12
.

Let
c
be an orientation on a branched 2-complex
X
. Then

\sigma_{-}(X)\leq 1-\frac{2\mathrm{scl}(c)}{\mathrm{Area}(X)}\leq\sigma_{+}(X)

Proof.

By Calegari’s rationality theorem, there is
S\in\mathrm{Admiss}(c)
such that

\mathrm{scl}(c)=-\frac{\chi(S)}{2\deg(S)}\,.

Calegari also shows that
S
can be taken to be
monotone
, meaning that the orientation on
\partial S
restricted from the orientation on
S
coincides with the orientation on
\partial S
pulled back from
c

[
10
, Proposition 2.13]
. By capping off the boundary components, the surface
S
defines a closed surface
\widehat{S}\to X
with
\mathrm{Area}(\widehat{S})=\deg(S)\mathrm{Area}(X)
. Note also that

\displaystyle\kappa(\widehat{S})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1+\frac{\chi(S)}{\mathrm{Area}(\widehat{S})}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2\deg(S)\mathrm{scl}(c)}{\deg(S)\mathrm{Area}(X)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{2\mathrm{scl}(c)}{\mathrm{Area}(X)}\,.

Such a surface
S
is called
extremal
and, by a lemma of Calegari
[
9
, Lemma 2.7]
,
\widehat{S}
is essential. In particular,
\widehat{S}\in\mathrm{Surf}(X)
, and so

\sigma_{-}(X)\leq\kappa(\widehat{S})\leq\sigma_{+}(X)

which gives the result.
∎

Lemma
10.9
follows immediately by specialising to the case of a single face of area 1. Again, the moral is that a suitable normalisation of stable commutator length can be thought of as an oriented version of the surface curvature.

Recall that the Duncan–Howie theorem tells us that every one-relator complex has non-positive oriented surface curvature. It remains an open question whether or not every 2-boundary
c
in a free group satisfies
\mathrm{scl}(c)\geq 1/2
. However, from our point of view, the natural threshold corresponding to non-positive oriented surface curvature for an oriented complex is

\mathrm{scl}(c)\geq\frac{\mathrm{Area}(X)}{2}\,,

and some oriented 2-complexes do indeed have positive oriented surface curvature: for instance, any cellulation of the 2-sphere provides an example.

Since stable commutator length can be computed in practice, Proposition
10.12
offers a practical way to obtain lower bounds on
\sigma_{+}
and upper bounds on
\sigma_{-}
.

Examples

So far, surfaces have provided the only classes of examples that we have considered. The goal of this section is to collect a wider variety of examples, which illustrate the various possibilities that can occur. In the first few sections, however, we collect techniques for computing the invariants.

11.1
Angle structures and
CAT(\kappa)
spaces

A more traditional way to impose curvature restrictions on a 2-complex
X
is to use an
angle structure
, namely a path metric on the link of each vertex. Often, this angle structure is induced from a path metric on the whole of
X
, where each edge of each link is assigned the angle of the corresponding corner. Gromov’s link condition implies that a Euclidean (respectively, hyperbolic) polygonal metric is locally
CAT(0)
(respectively, locally
CAT(-1)
) if and only if, in the induced angle structure, the link of every vertex has systole at least
2\pi

[
7
, Lemma II.5.6]
.

An angle structure also makes it possible to impose bounds on the average curvature, following ideas of Wise
[
58
]
. The next definition can be compared to
[
58
, Definition 2.2]
.

Definition 11.1
.

Consider a finite, irreducible 2-complex
X
, and suppose that
X
is equipped with an angle structure. Let
f
be a face of
X
which is an
n(f)
-gon, meaning that its boundary is a circle with
n(f)
edges, and with total interior angle
\alpha(f)
. The
curvature
of
f
is the quantity

c(f):=2\pi\mathrm{Area}(f)-\pi n(f)+\alpha(f)\,.

We say that the angle structure is
non-positively curved
(respectively,
negatively curved
) if:

(i)

the link of every vertex has systole at least
2\pi
; and

(ii)

c(f)\leq 0
for each face
f
(respectively,
c(f)<0
for each face
f
).

This definition of the curvature of a face becomes easier to interpret with some input from the Gauss–Bonnet theorem for geodesic polygons.

Lemma 11.2

(Riemannian Gauss–Bonnet)
.

Consider a face
f
with
\mathrm{Area}(f)=1
. If
f
is endowed with the angle structure of a geodesic polygon
P
in some Riemannian surface, then

c(f)=\int_{P}\kappa_{\mathrm{Gauss}}.dA

where
\kappa_{\mathrm{Gauss}}
is the Gauss curvature.

Proof.

The Gauss–Bonnet theorem for geodesic polygons asserts that

\int_{P}\kappa_{\mathrm{Gauss}}.dA=\alpha(f)-(n(f)-2)\pi

and the result follows immediately by the definition of
c(f)
.
∎

In particular, the angle structures coming from locally
CAT(0)
Euclidean structures are non-positively curved, and the angle structures coming from locally
CAT(-1)
hyperbolic structures are negatively curved. The use of this definition is in the following estimate, a combinatorial version of the Gauss–Bonnet theorem (cf.
[
1
, p. 107]
,
[
40
, Theorem 4.6]
,
[
58
, Theorem 2.3]
).

Lemma 11.3

(Combinatorial Gauss–Bonnet)
.

Let
S
be a finite branched surface equipped with an angle structure. If the total angle around every vertex is at least
2\pi
then

2\pi\tau(S)\leq\sum_{f\in F_{S}}c(f)\,.

In particular, if the angle structure is non-positively curved then
\tau(S)\leq 0
. If the angle structure is negatively curved then
\tau(S)<0
.

Proof.

Since the link of each vertex has total angle at least
2\pi
, the number of vertices of
X
is at most the sum of the interior angles across
X
divided by
2\pi
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in F_{X}}c(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in F_{X}}2\pi\mathrm{Area}(f)-\pi n(f)+\alpha(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\mathrm{Area}(X)-2\pi\#E_{X}+\sum_{f\in F_{X}}\alpha(f)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2\pi\mathrm{Area}(X)-2\pi\#E_{X}+2\pi\#V_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\pi\tau(X)

as required.
∎

Furthermore, the curvature of a face interacts well with branched maps.

Remark 11.4
.

Suppose that
\phi:Y\to X
is a branched map, and that
f
is a face of
Y
. If the image
\phi(f)
has
n
sides then
f
has
m_{\phi}(f)n
sides; likewise, if
\alpha
is the total interior angle of
\phi(f)
then
m_{\phi}(f)\alpha
is the total interior angle of
f
. Since area is also multiplicative, it follows that

c(f)=m_{\phi}(f)c(\phi(f))\\

by definition.

As a result, the curvature of a face provides an upper bound on the surface curvature of a standard 2-complex.

Proposition 11.5

(Face curvature bounds surface curvature)
.

Let
X
be a standard 2-complex equipped with an angle structure. If the link of every vertex has systole at least
2\pi
then

2\pi\sigma_{+}(X)\leq\max_{f\in F_{X}}c(f)\,.

Proof.

Consider an essential map from a surface
S\to X
. Since
S\to X
is essential, links of
S
map to immersed loops in links of
X
, and in particular are of length at least
2\pi
. Applying Lemma
11.3
and Remark
11.4
, we get

\displaystyle 2\pi\tau(S)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in F_{S}}c(f)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{f\in F_{S}}m_{\phi}(f)c(\phi(f))

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\mathrm{Area}(S)\max_{f\in F_{X}}c(f)

and the result follows.
∎

In particular, a locally
CAT(0)
Euclidean 2-complex has non-positive surface curvature.

Corollary 11.6

(Surface curvature of locally
CAT(0)
2-complexes)
.

If
X
is a finite 2-complex equipped with a non-positively curved angle structure (for instance, if
X
is a locally CAT(0) Euclidean complex), then
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
.

Likewise, locally
CAT(-1)
hyperbolic 2-complexes have negative surface curvature.

Corollary 11.7

(Surface curvature of locally
CAT(-1)
2-complexes)
.

If
X
is a finite 2-complex equipped with a negatively curved angle structure (for instance, if
X
is a locally CAT(-1) hyperbolic complex), then
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
.

The proposition has one more consequence which is very useful in practice, because it depends only on combinatorial information. By the
girth
of a graph we mean the combinatorial length of the shortest non-trivial loop.

Corollary 11.8

(Girths of links)
.

Let
X
be a standard 2-complex in which every face has
n
sides. Then

\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 1-\frac{n}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{\lambda}\right)

where
\lambda
is the smallest girth of the link of a vertex in
X
.

Proof.

Equip
X
with an angle structure in which every edge of every link has length
2\pi/\lambda
. Then the systole of every link is at least
2\pi
, and every face has curvature

c(f)=2\pi-\pi n+2\pi\frac{n}{\lambda}=2\pi\left(1-\frac{n}{2}\left(1-\frac{2}{\lambda}\right)\right)

and the result follows from Proposition
11.5
.
∎

Remark 11.9

(Wise’s sectional curvature)
.

In Wise’s terminology, the 2-complexes considered in this section are of non-positive or negative
planar sectional curvature
. In
[
58
, Definition 2.4]
, Wise defines a stronger notion of (non-planar)
sectional curvature
. In our terminology, his arguments show that a 2-complex
X
with an angle structure of non-positive sectional curvature has
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 0
, while an angle structure of negative sectional curvature would guarantee that
\rho_{+}(X)<0
.

11.2
Small-cancellation and random complexes

Small-cancellation conditions are a useful combinatorial method of imposing curvature-like constraints on group presentations; see the book of Lyndon and Schupp for an authoritative treatment
[
37
, Chapter V]
.

Informally, a
piece
of
X
is a path that appears in two different ways in the attaching maps of the 2-cells. Formally, it may be defined as follows. Let
S_{X}
be the disjoint union of the boundaries of the 2-cells of
X
, so
X
is characterised by the attaching map
S_{X}\to X_{(1)}
. A piece is then an off-diagonal component of the fibre product
S_{X}\times_{X_{(1)}}S_{X}
. A 2-complex satisfies the
C(p)

small-cancellation condition
if no face of
X
is a union of fewer than
p
pieces. It is well known that the
C(6)
condition can be thought of as a form of non-positive curvature, while the
C(7)
condition can be thought of as a form of negative curvature. These heuristics fit precisely into the framework of surface curvature.

Proposition 11.10

(
C(6)
complexes have non-positive surface curvature)
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible, standard,
C(6)
complex then
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove that

\tau(Y)\leq 0

whenever
\phi:Y\to X
is an essential map from a surface.

For a face
f
of
Y
, let
n(f)
be the number of vertices of
Y_{(1)}
adjacent to
f
of valence at least 3. Consider the natural graph structure on
Y_{(1)}
obtained by deleting the vertices of valence 2. The number of edges is then exactly

\sum_{f\in F_{Y}}n(f)/2

while the number of vertices is at most

\sum_{f\in F_{Y}}n(f)/3\,,

so

\tau(Y)\leq\sum_{f\in F_{Y}}\mathrm{Area}(f)-n(f)/2+n(f)/3=\sum_{f\in F_{Y}}\mathrm{Area}(f)-n(f)/6\,.

But the area of
f\in F_{Y}
is
m_{\phi}(f)
because
X
is standard, and
n(f)\geq 6m_{\phi}(f)
because
X
is
C(6)
, so

\tau(Y)\leq\sum_{f\in F_{Y}}m_{\phi}(1-6/6)=0

as required.
∎

In exactly the same way, we obtain a similar result for
C(7)
complexes.

Proposition 11.11

(
C(7)
complexes have negative surface curvature)
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible, standard
C(7)
complex then
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
.

Any
C(7)
presentation complex
X
with at least as many relators as generators now provides an example with
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
but
\rho_{+}(X)>0
. For an especially large class of examples, one can use random presentations in the few-relator model.

Example 11.12

(Random few-relator complexes)
.

Consider a randomly chosen presentation in the few-relator model with
n
generators and
m
relators of length
l
. The probability that this presentation is
C(7)
tends to 1 as
l\to\infty
, and so the standard presentation complex also satisfies
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
with high probability. On the other hand,
\chi(X)=1-n+m
and the probability that
X
is not visibly irreducible tends to 0 as
l\to\infty
. Therefore, with probability tending to 1,

\rho_{+}(X)\geq\frac{\tau(X)}{\mathrm{Area}(X)}=\frac{1-n+m}{m}=1+(1-n)/m>0

unless
m<n
.

This provides a rich class of examples of complexes with negative surface curvature but positive irreducible curvature.

Since the inequality used in Example
11.12
only provides a lower bound, it remains an interesting problem to estimate the irreducible curvatures of few-relator complexes with
m<n
. The following conjecture represents the most optimistic possibility.

Conjecture 11.13

(Irreducible curvature bounds of few-relator complexes)
.

If
X
is a random few-relator complex as in Example
11.12
, then the probability that
\rho_{+}(X)=\kappa(X)
tends to
1
as
l\to\infty
. In particular, with high probability,
\rho_{+}(X)<0
if
m<n-1
and
\rho_{+}(X)=0
if
m=n-1
.

The
density model
of random groups, in which
m
is proportional to
(2n-1)^{dl}
for some density
0<d<1
, is also much-studied, and the resulting group is infinite and hyperbolic with high probability if
d<1/2

[
24
]
. These presentation complexes are irreducible and have positive Euler characteristic by definition, so certainly
\rho_{+}(X)>0
. However, a random complex satisfies
C(7)
with high probability at density
d<1/12

[
44
]
, so such a complex
X
does have negative surface curvature, and it is likely that this estimate extends to densities less than
1/2
.

Conjecture 11.14

(Surface curvature of random complexes in the density model)
.

For a random presentation complex
X
in the density model at density
d<1/2
, the probability that
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
tends to
1
as
l\to\infty
.

11.3
One-relator groups

Groups with a single defining relation were much studied in the early years of combinatorial group theory. According to Chandler and Magnus, Dehn hypothesised a geometric solution to their word problem
[
14
, p. 114]
. However, Magnus’ eventual solution was combinatorial
[
38
]
, and indeed none of the curvature conditions most commonly used in geometric group theory – Gromov-hyperbolicity, automaticity,
CAT(0)
metrics etc – can apply to all one-relator groups, since they all imply a quadratic isoperimetric inequality.

In 2003, Wise introduced notions of
non-positive
and
negative immersions

[
57
,
58
]
as tools to attack certain open problems about one-relator groups, especially a famous question, due to Baumslag, asking whether or not one-relator groups are coherent
[
4
]
.
14
14

14

See
[
59
]
for a survey of recent work on coherence.
These ideas have been developed over the last decade in papers by Helfer–Wise
[
25
]
and Louder and the author
[
32
,
34
]
, culminating in resolutions of Baumslag’s question for one-relator groups with torsion
[
33
,
60
]
and 2-free one-relator groups
[
35
]
. Very recently, Baumslag’s question was completely resolved in the affirmative by Jaikin-Zapirain–Linton
[
29
]
.

These developments can be interpreted as the beginning of a geometric theory of one-relator groups. In this section, we will see that this new geometric theory of one-relator groups can also be interpreted using maximal irreducible curvature.

Consider the standard presentation complex
X
for the presentation

\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\mid w\rangle

of a one-relator group
G
. Recall that
G
is torsion-free if and only if the relator
w
is not a proper power
[
37
, Proposition 5.17]
. Note also that, as long as
n>1
, the standard presentation complex
X
is irreducible if and only if
w
is not conjugate into a proper free factor.

The following theorem, proved independently by Helfer–Wise and by Louder and the author, can be thought of as the first proof of an upper bound on irreducible curvature
[
25
,
32
]
.

Theorem 11.15

(Non-positive irreducible curvature for one-relator groups)
.

Let
X
be the standard presentation complex of a one-relator group
G
, and suppose that
X
is irreducible. If
G
is torsion-free then
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 0
.

The theorem gives an exact computation of
\rho_{+}
for two-generator, one-relator groups.

Corollary 11.16
.

Let
X
be the standard presentation complex of a two-generator, one-relator group. If
X
is irreducible and
G
is torsion-free then
\rho_{+}(X)=0
.

Proof.

Since the presentation has two generators,

\tau(X)=\chi(X)=2-2=0

so the identity map realises the upper bound from the theorem.
∎

Theorem
11.15
also implies that
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
by Lemma
7.1
. This result contravenes the standard heuristics of geometric group theory. For instance, it is usually posited that any reasonable notion of non-positive curvature for a group
G
should imply that
G
satisfies a quadratic isoperimetric inequality. However, there are famously Baumslag–Solitar groups with exponential Dehn function
[
6
, Exercise 7.2.11]
, and yet their presentation complexes are non-positively curved in our sense.

Example 11.17

(Baumslag–Solitar groups)
.

The standard presentation complex
X
of the
(m,n)
-Baumslag–Solitar group

BS(m,n)\cong\langle a,b\mid ba^{m}b^{-1}a^{-n}\rangle

has
\rho_{+}(X)=0
by Corollary
11.16
. Furthermore,
X
is irrigid by Example
9.2
, so Lemma
9.3
provides
S\in\mathrm{Surf}(X)
with
\kappa(S)=\kappa(X)=0
, whence
\sigma_{+}(X)=0
also.

One-relator groups include many examples that are even more pathological, such as the Baumslag–Gersten group
BG=\langle a,b\mid b^{a}a(b^{a})^{-1}a^{-2}\rangle

[
3
,
21
]
. From our perspective, all of these are non-positively curved as long as they are torsion-free. (The case with torsion is addressed in Theorem
11.19
below.)

The question of negative curvature for one-relator groups is considerably more delicate. In Theorem
12.8
below, we shall see that if
\rho_{+}(X)<0
and
G=\pi_{1}(X)
then every 2-generator subgroup of
G
is free. The results of Louder and the author
[
34
,
35
]
show that this is the only obstruction to negative irreducible curvature for one-relator groups.

Theorem 11.18

(Torsion-free one-relator groups with negative irreducible curvature)
.

If
X
is the standard presentation complex of a torsion-free one-relator group
G
and every two-generator, one-relator subgroup of
G
is free then
\rho_{+}(X)<0
.

In
[
34
]
, these one-relator groups are said to have
negative immersions
.

The above theorems apply to one-relator groups without torsion, but the case with torsion is also of great interest. The following account reframes the proof of coherence given in
[
33
]
(similar to Wise’s independent proof
[
60
]
) in the language of irreducible curvature.

Consider a one-relator presentation

G\cong\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\mid u^{k}\rangle

where
k>1
. The obvious presentation complex
X
fails to be concise, and so
\rho_{+}(X)=1
by Proposition
C
, showing that the hypothesis that
G
should be torsion-free in Theorem
11.15
cannot be removed. However, there is a natural modification of the ideas of this paper in which
X
is allowed to be an
orbi-complex
, and the face of
X
can be endowed with a cone point. In this modified framework,
G=\pi_{1}(X)
, where the single face of
X
is attached along the word
u
and has a cone point of order
k
. The standard assignment of areas should give the unique face of
X
an area of
1/k
.

In this modified framework, the techniques of the proof of Theorem
11.15
apply as before, except that the areas of faces are divided by
k
.
15
15

15

Since one-relator groups are virtually torsion-free, one may also pass to a finite-sheeted cover which is a genuine complex, as opposed to an orbi-complex, as in
[
33
, Theorem 2.2]
. This avoids the need to discuss orbi-complexes.
For any essential map from an irreducible complex
Y\to X
, the proof of Theorem
11.15
now gives

k\mathrm{Area}(Y)+\chi(Y_{(1)})\leq 0\,.

Since the left hand side can be rewritten as
(k-1)\mathrm{Area}(Y)+\tau(Y)
, it follows that

\kappa(Y)=\frac{\tau(Y)}{\mathrm{Area}(Y)}\leq 1-k\,.

In conclusion we have the following estimate on the irreducible curvature of the presentation orbi-complex of a one-relator group with torsion, which was implicit in both
[
33
]
and
[
60
]
.

Theorem 11.19

(Negative irreducible curvature for one-relator groups with torsion)
.

If
X
is the standard presentation orbi-complex for a one-relator group

G\cong\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\mid u^{k}\rangle

then
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 1-k
. In particular, if
k>1
then
\rho_{+}(X)<0
.

This is in line with the general expectation that one-relator groups with torsion are negatively curved. For instance, Newman’s spelling theorem asserts that all one-relator groups with torsion are hyperbolic
[
42
]
.

Finally, we make a remark about one-relator groups and Nielsen equivalence. Propositions
8.2
and
8.3
show that
\rho_{\pm}
and
\sigma_{\pm}
are always invariant under Nielsen equivalence of
presentations
. In the special case of one-relator groups, this can be extended to Nielsen equivalence of generating sets by a deep theorem of Magnus
[
37
, Proposition 5.8]
.

Theorem 11.20

(Magnus’ theorem)
.

Consider two one-relator presentations

\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\mid w\rangle~\textrm{and}~\langle a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{m}\mid w^{\prime}\rangle

for a group. If
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
and
\{a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{m}\}
are Nielsen equivalent as generating sets then the two presentations are also Nielsen equivalent.

This raises the possibility that our invariants could, at least in principle, be used to distinguish Nielsen equivalence classes of generating sets of one-relator groups.

Corollary 11.21

(Nielsen equivalence of generating sets for one-relator groups)
.

Consider two one-relator presentations

\langle a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\mid w\rangle~\textrm{and}~\langle a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{m}\mid w^{\prime}\rangle

for a group, with corresponding presentation complexes
X
and
X^{\prime}
respectively. If
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\}
and
\{a^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,a^{\prime}_{m}\}
are Nielsen equivalent as generating sets then
\rho_{\pm}(X)=\rho_{\pm}(X^{\prime})
and
\sigma_{\pm}(X)=\sigma_{\pm}(X^{\prime})
.

11.4
Geometric 2-complexes

The next definition allows us to apply these ideas to 3-manifolds.

Definition 11.22

(Geometric 2-complex)
.

A 2-complex
X
is called
geometric
if it is endowed with the following additional structure.

(i)

For each vertex
v
, the link
\mathrm{Lk}(v)
is equipped with a planar embedding, i.e. a cellular embedding into
S^{2}
. This determines how to canonically thicken the 1-skeleton
X_{(1)}
to a (possibly non-orientable) handlebody.

(ii)

For each face
f
, the corresponding curve on the surface of the handlebody is 2-sided.

In particular, a presentation is called
geometric
if its presentation complex is geometric.

By thickening the 1-skeleton to a handlebody and the faces to 2-handles, a geometric 2-complex
X
determines a 3-manifold with boundary
M=M(X)
that deformation retracts to
X
. Note that the structure of a geometric complex pulls back along an essential map.

If
X
is an irreducible geometric 2-complex then the boundary
\partial M
of the thickened 3-manifold is naturally endowed with the structure of a 2-complex, and the inclusion map induces an immersion
\partial M\to X
. The next fact is morally a consequence of Poincaré duality, which implies that
\chi(\partial M)=2\chi(M)
for any compact 3-manifold, although we give a direct proof using the spherical links of
X
(cf.
[
58
, Theorem 10.2]
).

Proposition 11.23

(Poincaré duality)
.

If
X
is a finite, irreducible, geometric, branched 2-complex then
\kappa(\partial M(X))=\kappa(X)
.

Proof.

Every face of
X
appears twice in
\partial M
, so
\mathrm{Area}(\partial M(X))=2\mathrm{Area}(X)
, and it suffices to prove that
\chi(\partial M_{(1)})=2\chi(X_{(1)})
. To compute
\chi(\partial M_{(1)})
, we introduce an auxiliary 2-complex
L
, the disjoint union of the cellular decompositions of the 2-spheres given by the links of the vertices of
X
.

Since
L
consists of one sphere for each vertex of
X
, we have
\chi(L)=2\#V_{X}
, while
2\#E_{X}=\#V_{L}
by definition. Therefore

\displaystyle 2\chi(X_{(1)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\#V_{X}-2\#E_{X}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\chi(L)-\#V_{L}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\#F_{L}-\#E_{L}\,.

But
F_{L}
naturally bijects with
V_{\partial M}
, while
E_{\partial M}
bijects with
E_{L}
, so
2\chi(X_{(1)})=\chi(\partial M_{(1)})
as required.
∎

This in turn implies that the irreducible curvature of a 3-manifold spine is always equal to the surface curvature.

Corollary 11.24

(Curvatures of geometric complexes)
.

If
X
is a geometric 2-complex then
\rho_{\pm}(X)=\sigma_{\pm}(X)
.

Proof.

For any
Y\in\mathrm{Irred}(X)
, the proposition implies that
\kappa(\partial M(Y))=\kappa(Y)
. Since
\partial M(Y)\to Y\to X
is an essential map from a surface, it follows that the range of values attained by maps from irreducible 2-complexes is equal to the range of values attained by maps from surfaces.
∎

Some 2-complexes are not geometric but nevertheless are
virtually
geometric, meaning that some finite-sheeted branched cover is geometric
[
22
, Theorem 21]
. Virtually geometric 2-complexes were classified by Cashen
[
13
, Theorem 5.9]
. Since Corollary
11.24
applies to branched 2-complexes as well as standard ones, it follows that virtually geometric 2-complexes also satisfy this conclusion.

Corollary 11.25

(Curvatures of virtually geometric 2-complexes)
.

If
X
is a virtually geometric 2-complex then
\rho_{\pm}(X)=\sigma_{\pm}(X)
.

Proof.

If
X^{\prime}\to X
is a geometric branched covering, then
\rho_{\pm}(X^{\prime})=\sigma_{\pm}(X^{\prime})
by Corollary
11.24
, so

\rho_{\pm}(X)=\rho_{\pm}(X^{\prime})=\sigma_{\pm}(X^{\prime})=\sigma_{\pm}(X)

by Proposition
7.4
.
∎

Conjecturally, Corollary
11.24
should explain many of the unusually good properties of 3-manifold groups, such as coherence
[
49
]
(cf. Conjecture
12.4
below). In order to apply it, however, one would need bounds on the surface curvatures of geometric 2-complexes. These bounds are the content of the next two conjectures. The first is a cousin of Papakyriakopoulos’ sphere theorem
[
46
]
.

Conjecture 11.26

(Non-positive curvature for geometric complexes)
.

Let
X
be a finite, irreducible, geometric 2-complex. If
\sigma_{+}(X)>0
then either
\partial M(X)
has a spherical component or
\pi_{1}(X)
splits freely.

The analogous conjecture for negative curvature can be thought of as a weak form of hyperbolisation for Haken 3-manifolds.

Conjecture 11.27

(Negative curvature for geometric complexes)
.

Let
X
be a finite, irreducible, geometric, branched 2-complex. If
\sigma_{+}(X)=0
then either
\partial M(X)
has a toroidal boundary component or
\pi_{1}(X)
has a
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
subgroup.

11.5
Graphs of free groups

Graphs of free groups have been a popular class of examples throughout the history of geometric group theory. They exhibit a rich range of behaviours and are very difficult to study in full generality. In the same spirit, I expect them to be an interesting test class for our rational curvature invariants. Nothing is currently known, so we will state some conjectures and questions.

Before doing so, however, we should note that there are various possible choices of 2-complex that one might associate to a given graph of free groups. Nielsen invariance provides some reassurance, but nevertheless the values of
\rho_{\pm}
and
\sigma_{\pm}
will depend on this choice. Making the correct choice of 2-complex will necessarily be part of resolving the conjectures and questions below.

Mapping tori of free group automorphisms and, more generally, endomorphisms, have proved an especially fruitful class of examples. The following conjecture posits that they should all be non-positively curved in our strongest sense.

Conjecture 11.28

(Irreducible curvatures of mapping tori of free group endormorphisms)
.

If
X
is a compact mapping torus of an injective endomorphism of a finitely generated free group then
\rho_{+}(X)=0
.

The lower bound
\rho_{+}(X)\geq 0
follows from the fact that
\chi(X)=0
. Wise proved that such

[

. Adapting his argument may be sufficient to prove Conjecture

The question of which of these examples have negative surface curvature is likely to be more subtle. The following conjecture is the most optimistic possible statement.

Conjecture 11.29

(Surface curvatures of mapping tori of free group endormorphisms)
.

If
X
is a compact mapping torus of an injective endomorphism of a finitely generated free group then
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
unless
\pi_{1}(X)
has a Baumslag–Solitar subgroup.

Caution is needed here! Example
11.37
below provides an example of a 2-complex
X
with
\sigma_{+}(X)=0
but

Graphs of free groups with cyclic edge groups have also been a popular source of examples. I expect the following conjecture to be quite easy to prove, but it will be a useful warm up for more challenging examples.

Conjecture 11.30

(Graphs of free groups with cyclic edge groups)
.

If
X
is a compact, connected, irreducible graph of graphs with circular edge spaces, then
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 0
. Furthermore,
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
unless
\pi_{1}(X)
has a Baumslag–Solitar subgroup.

In the case of general graphs of free groups, it is unclear what the correct classification should be. However, finding it may shed further light on this mysterious class.

Problem 11.31

(General graphs of free groups)
.

Determine when a compact, connected graph of graphs
X
has non-positive or negative surface or irreducible curvatures.

Non-positive irreducible curvature

Recall that a map of 2-complexes

Definition 12.1

(Non-positive immersions)

A finite, standard 2-complex

The ideas around this definition have been developed by Wise more recently in

It is not hard to see that the non-positive-immersions property is a slight weakening of our notion of non-positive irreducible curvature.

Lemma 12.2

(Non-positive irreducible curvature implies non-positive immersions)
.

Let
X
be a finite, standard 2-complex. If
\rho_{+}(X)\leq 0
then
X
has contracting non-positive immersions.

Proof.

For any immersion from a finite, connected complex
Y\to X
, consider an unfolding

Y^{\prime}=G\vee Y_{1}\vee\ldots\vee Y_{m}\vee D_{1}\vee\ldots\vee D_{n}

as in Lemma

and
Y^{\prime}
are homotopy equivalent, the Euler characteristic of

can be written as

\chi(Y)=\chi(G)+\sum_{i=1}^{m}(\chi(Y_{i})-1)\,.

The graph
G
is connected so
\chi(G)\leq 1
, and the hypothesis on
\rho_{+}(Y_{i})
implies that

\chi(Y_{i})=\tau(Y_{i})\leq 0\,,

so
\chi(Y)\leq 1
with equality only if

is a tree and

. Therefore,
\chi(Y)>0
implies that

is homotopy equivalent to a tree wedged with discs, and is in particular contractible, as required.
∎

It is an open question whether there is an example of a 2-complex

Indeed, the fact that non-positive irreducible curvature can be recognised algorithmically is one argument in favour of this point of view.

12.3

Negative irreducible curvature puts even stronger constraints on a 2-complex

A finite, standard 2-complex

Lemma 12.7

If

below, which relaxes the hypotheses to
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
. Italiano, Martelli and Migliorini recently gave the first example of a non-hyperbolic group of finite type without Baumslag–Solitar subgroups
[
28
]
, but no 2-dimensional examples are known. A recent theorem of Linton
[
31
]
proves that one-relator groups with negative immersions – i.e. with
\rho_{+}(X)<0
– are hyperbolic, confirming part of Conjecture
12.9
in the one-relator case.

12.4
Surface curvature bounds

As we have seen in the previous two sections, recent developments in the theory of one-relator groups can be seen as explorations of the consequences of upper bounds on irreducible curvature. Surface curvature has not yet been as thoroughly investigated, and so the constraints that surface curvature bounds impose on
X
are still conjectural. We list some of those conjectures here. We have already seen many examples of complexes with upper bounds on their surface curvature, and these conjectures are consistent with those examples. Throughout,
X
is a finite, standard, irreducible 2-complex.

Non-positive curvature is frequently used in geometric group theory to prove that a space is aspherical: this is the Cartan–Hadamard theorem for CAT(0) spaces
[
7
, Theorem II.4.1]
, a standard result about small-cancellation complexes
[
15
]
, and the Lyndon asphericity theorem for one-relator groups
[
36
,
16
]
. We have already seen that
X
is aspherical if it has non-positive irreducible curvature, but we conjecture that this result extends to the much wider context of non-positive surface curvature.

Conjecture 12.10

(Non-positive surface curvature implies aspherical)
.

If
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
then
X
is aspherical.

A suitable formulation of van Kampen’s lemma implies that any map from a 2-sphere to
X
is homotopic to a morphism of 2-complexes
S\to X
, where the realisation of
S
is a 2-sphere. The challenge of Conjecture
12.10
is to improve this morphism to an essential map.

Another frequent use of non-positive curvature in group theory is to the solve the word problem: this follows from the quadratic isoperimetric inequality for CAT(0) groups
[
7
, Proposition III.
\Gamma
.1.6]
, from Greendlinger’s lemma for small-cancellation groups
[
37
]
, and is a famous theorem of Magnus for one-relator groups
[
38
]
. Our second conjecture proposes that, in the case of 2-complexes, all of these can be deduced from a bound on surface curvature.

Conjecture 12.11

(Non-positive surface curvature implies solvable word problem)
.

If
\sigma_{+}(X)\leq 0
then the word problem is solvable in
\pi_{1}(X)
.

The last two conjectures suggest that non-positive surface curvature is similar to other notions of non-positive curvature commonly used in geometric group theory. However, as noted after Theorem
11.15
, there are 2-complexes of non-positive surface curvature with very large isoperimetric inequality, and in particular
\sigma_{+}(X)
does not imply any of the standard notions of non-positive curvature already used in geometric group theory.

However, it is likely that negative surface curvature is closely related to the dominant notion of negative curvature for groups, namely word-hyperbolicity.

Conjecture 12.12

(Negative surface curvature implies hyperbolic)
.

If
\sigma_{+}(X)<0
then
\pi_{1}(X)
is word-hyperbolic.

This conjecture posits a common generalisation of Greendlinger’s lemma for small-cancellation groups and the B.B. Newman spelling theorem for one-relator groups with torsion. It should also shed light on the cases of non-positively curved Euclidean 2-complexes and one-relator presentation complexes, where there is not yet a complete understanding of which groups are word-hyperbolic. However, Example
11.37
provides an example of a non-positively curved, Euclidean, one-relator 2-complex, for which hyperbolicity of the fundamental group is not a consequence of Conjecture
12.12
.

The conjectures to this point all concern upper curvature bounds. However, it is possible that lower bounds may also provide useful information.

Conjecture 12.13

(Positive surface curvature implies no surface subgroups)
.

If
\sigma_{-}(X)>0
then no subgroup of
\pi_{1}(X)
is isomorphic to the fundamental group of a closed surface
S
with
\chi(S)\leq 0
.

The challenge of Conjecture
12.13
is to prove that
any
finite 2-complex
X
with
\pi_{1}(X)
a surface group has
\rho_{-}(X)\leq 0
.

In §
9
, we saw that Baumslag–Solitar groups are closely related to surfaces, because both can be thought of as the fundamental groups of irrigid complexes. An even more intriguing possibility is that
\sigma_{-}(X)>0
also implies that
\pi_{1}(X)
has no Baumslag–Solitar subgroups. There are very few known examples of infinite, finitely presented groups without surface or Baumslag–Solitar subgroups.
