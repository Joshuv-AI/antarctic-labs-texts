Subtractive random forests with two choices
Francisco Calvillo, Luc Devroye, Gábor Lugosi

Abstract
Recommendation systems are pivotal in aiding users amid vast online content. Broutin, Devroye, Lugosi, and Oliveira proposed Subtractive Random Forests ( surf ), a model that emphasizes temporal user preferences. Expanding on surf , we introduce a model for a multi-choice recommendation system, enabling users to select from two independent suggestions based on past interactions. We evaluate its effectiveness and robustness across diverse scenarios, incorporating heavy-tailed distributions for time delays. By analyzing user topic evolution, we assess the system’s consistency. Our study offers insights into the performance and potential enhancements of multi-choice recommendation systems in practical settings.



Keywords:
subtractive random forests, multi-choice recommendation

1
Introduction

Today’s online platforms have a record number of items available for their users. In many cases, presenting them as a catalogue is no longer a good option and can make it difficult for the user to find what they are interested in.
Recommendation systems have become an essential tool to cope with the overload of information available on the web.

A well-known way to approach recommendation systems today is through
deep learning, and many of the most effective recommendation systems
use this principle, see
[
14
]
. However, the mechanism of
these systems is notable for its opacity. In the realm of online
sequential recommendation systems, Broutin, Devroye, Lugosi, and
Oliveira
[
6
]
present an approach, offering a
framework that considers the temporal aspect of user preferences by a
simple mechanism. They define a model recommending topics based on a
random time delay. The topic that is recommended at time
n
is the
same that was recommended at time
n-Z_{n}
, where
(Z_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a
sequence of i.i.d. random variables identically distributed on

\{1,2,\ldots\}
. This approach led them to study a family of random
forests called subtractive random forests (
surf
), allowing a
detailed structural study. However, one expects a recommendation
system to make several recommendations at a time, not just one.

To model this more realistic scenario, our paper proposes a two-choice
recommendation system inspired by the same idea. We envision a
scenario where users are presented with two independent
recommendations, drawn from the same temporal recommendation mechanism
and allowing users to select the most appealing option.
The resulting model has some intriguing mathematical properties, and the main goal of this paper
is to analyze the model in order to understand the long-term behavior of such recommendation systems.

While our model provides a mathematically tractable framework to study sequential recommendation dynamics, it is intentionally abstract and does not incorporate key aspects of modern recommender systems such as content information, user-specific features, or contextual data. This abstraction limits its direct applicability to real-world platforms, where personalization and diversity are central goals. However, we view this simplification as a necessary step: by first understanding the behavior of a minimal, analytically transparent model, we lay the groundwork for tackling more complex and realistic settings in which such additional factors can be systematically incorporated.

Beyond serving as a recommender system in its own right, our model can also be viewed as a theoretical tool to gain insight into more complex algorithms. In particular, it offers a simple framework to reason about phenomena such as the repeated occurrence of topics in classical recommender systems, where machine learning mechanisms are often treated as black boxes.
By studying this simplified yet transparent model, we can uncover structural patterns that would otherwise remain hidden, offering insight into the dynamics of more sophisticated recommendation methods.

1.1
A two-choice recommendation model

We assume that the initial pool of topics is infinite and represented by the set of non-positive integers
\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}
.
We now consider independent and identically distributed random variables
Z
,
W
,
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}

on the set of positive integers
\mathbb{N}
.
Define

q_{i}=\mathbb{P}(Z=i)\quad\text{and}\quad p_{i}=\mathbb{P}(Z\geq i),\quad i\geq 1~.

Each topic
i\leq 0
is assigned a preference value
U_{i}
within the range
[0,1]
, where we assume that
(U_{n})_{n\leq 0}
is a sequence of random variables independent of the sequences
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
.
In the context of our model, the term user refers to a representative individual interacting with the system, and the values
U_{i}
represent how appealing each topic is to this user.

Following
[
6
]
, we can define a sequential random colouring of the positive integers as follows. For each non-positive integer
i\leq 0
, define its colour
C_{i}=i
and its preference value
V_{i}=U_{i}
. We assign colours and preference values to the positive integers
n\in\mathbb{N}
by the recursion

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}C_{n}=C_{n-Z_{n}}\text{ and }V_{n}=V_{n-Z_{n}}&\text{ if }V_{n-Z_{n}}<V_{n-W_{n}}\\
C_{n}=C_{n-W_{n}}\text{ and }V_{n}=V_{n-W_{n}}&\text{ otherwise }~.\end{array}\right.

Thus, by identifying the colour
C_{n}
as the topic recommended at time
n\geq 1
, this definition means that at the time instant
n\geq 1
, the user receives two recommendations (the topic
C_{n-Z_{n}}
, and the topic
C_{n-W_{n}}
), and chooses the one with the lowest (best) preference value.

This process naturally defines a random directed graph whose vertex set is
\mathbb{Z}
by drawing an edge from any positive integer
n\geq 1
to any integer
m<n
if and only if
m=n-Z_{n}
or
m=n-W_{n}
. Vertices with negative index are called
leaves
, as is customary for final nodes in dags (i.e., directed acyclic graphs).
Let
T_{n}
denote the set of vertices that are reached from the vertex
n\geq 1
. This set can be defined recursively by saying that
T_{n}=\{n\}
for any
n\leq 0
, and

T_{n}=\{n\}\cup T_{n-Z_{n}}\cup T_{n-W_{n}}

for any
n\geq 1
. Define

\mathcal{L}_{n}=T_{n}\cap\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}

as the set of vertices with a non-positive index that is reached from
n
. In other words,
\mathcal{L}_{n}
is the set of leaves in the subgraph of vertices reached from
n
. Note that

C_{n}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{i\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}U_{i}~\text{ and }~V_{n}=\min_{i\in\mathcal{L}_{n}}U_{i}~.

One can assess the system’s long-term performance by analyzing the asymptotic behavior of the sequence
(V_{n})_{n\geq 1}
. If
V_{n}
approaches
\inf_{i\leq 0}U_{i}
as
n\to\infty
, then this means that after waiting a sufficient amount of time, the topics recommended to the user tend to align more closely with their preferences. This gives us a consistency criterion for our model.
We consider the three following configurations of the preference values:

(i)

U_{0}<U_{-1}<U_{-2}<U_{-3}\dots

(ii)

U_{0}>U_{-1}>U_{-2}>U_{-3}\dots

(iii)

(U_{n})_{n\leq 0}
i.i.d. and uniformly distributed in
[0,1].

These assumptions correspond to natural scenarios and allow us to study the long-term consistency of the recommendation system.
In the first case, topics have a monotone preference with the most recent (corresponding to
i=0
) being the most preferred one.
In the second case, older topics are preferred, while in the third case, topics have a random order of preference.
We say that the system is consistent if, in the long run, it offers near-optimal recommendations to the user in terms of their preference.
In Section
1.3
, we rigorously define various notions of consistency, corresponding to the scenarios above.
These definitions lead us to study the sequence
(V_{n})_{n\geq 1}
by studying the set of leaves
\mathcal{L}_{n}
.
We will pay particular attention to the three following random variables:

\bullet

R_{n}=\max\left\{i:i\in\mathcal{L}_{n}\right\}
(the rightmost leaf in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
)

\bullet

L_{n}=\min\left\{i:i\in\mathcal{L}_{n}\right\}
(the leftmost leaf in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
)

\bullet

\Lambda_{n}=|\mathcal{L}_{n}|
(the number of leaves reached from
n
).

Each random variable helps us understand the long-term behavior of
V_{n}
in the three initial configurations described above.
Ideally, one would hope that
V_{n}\to\inf_{i\leq 0}U_{i}
.
In configuration (i) this is equivalent to
R_{n}\to 0
,
in configuration (ii) it is equivalent to
L_{n}\to-\infty
, while in case (iii) to
\Lambda_{n}\to\infty
.
However, one cannot expect to have
R_{n}\to 0
in a general case.
To introduce a more reasonable consistency criterion for case (i), we require boundedness of the sequence
(R_{n})_{n\geq 1}~.

Let us define the subsets
T_{n}^{Z}=T_{n}^{W}=S_{n}=\{n\}
for any
n\leq 0
, and let

T_{n}^{Z}=\{n\}\cup T_{n-Z_{n}}^{Z}~,

T_{n}^{W}=\{n\}\cup T_{n-W_{n}}^{W}~,

\text{and }S_{n}=\{n\}\cup S_{n-\min(Z_{n},W_{n})}

for any
n\geq 1
, so that
T_{n}^{Z}\subset T_{n}
(resp.
T_{n}^{W}\subset T_{n}
) is the set of vertices that can be reached from
n
by following only the
Z
-edges (resp.
W
-edges), and
S_{n}\subset T_{n}
is the set of vertices that can be reached from
n
by following only the shortest edges.

Finally, for any
n\geq 1
, let
r_{n}=\mathbb{P}\left(0\in T_{n}^{Z}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(0\in T_{n}^{W}\right)
denote the probability that vertex
0
belongs to the
Z
-chain starting at vertex
n
. We set
r_{0}=1
so that
r_{n}
satisfies the recursion

r_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}r_{n-i}~.

Remark 1
.

Observe that the chains
T_{n}^{Z}
,
T_{n}^{W}
, and
S_{n}
can be naturally interpreted as a renewal process, where each step along the chain corresponds to a renewal event, and the edge lengths play the role of renewal intervals. Within this framework, the quantity
r_{n}
coincides with the renewal measure of the process corresponding to
T_{n}^{Z}
.

1.2
Related work

This paper builds on the work of Broutin, Devroye, Lugosi and Oliveira
[
6
]
, who studied a one-choice version of this model by examining the properties of a graph whose vertex set is
\mathbb{Z}
, and whose edges are
\{n,n-Z_{n}\}
for every
n\geq 1
, given some sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
of i.i.d. random variables distributed in
\{1,2,3,\dots\}
. This defines a random forest where each non-positive integer is the root of a tree. They also define a random coloring by setting
C_{i}=i
for all
i\leq 0
, and
C_{n}=C_{n-Z_{n}}
for all
n\geq 1
. Each tree in the forest corresponds to a colour (i.e., a topic). The graph that we consider in our paper is nothing else than the superposition of two independent copies of this forest, and the sets
T_{n}^{Z}
and
T_{n}^{W}
defined above are the chains that connect the vertex
n
to the root of its tree in each of these two copies.

One of the main results in
[
6
]
shows that the process has two
distinct behaviors: if
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
, then, almost surely, all trees
in the forest are finite, meaning that no topic will be recommended
infinitely often, while if
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
, after some random amount
of time, all vertices connect to the same tree almost surely, meaning
that all topics recommended to the user become the same.
In the two-choice
model however, there are three distinct regimes:
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
;
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
yet

\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
; and
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
.
We call these the light-, moderate-, and heavy-tailed regimes.

The random forest in
[
6
]
appears as a subgraph of a random graph model previously studied by Hammond and Sheffield in
[
10
]
. Given a sequence
(Z_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
of independent random variables, identically distributed in
\{1,2,3,\dots\}
, they consider a graph with vertex set
\mathbb{Z}
such that vertices
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
m<n
, are connected by an edge if and only if
m=n-Z_{n}
. One can obtain the random forest in
[
6
]
by removing all edges between any two vertices with a non-positive index. They show that the random graph has almost surely a unique component when the sum
\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}
converges, and the graph almost surely has infinitely many connected components when the sum diverges.

Other variants of the (single-choice) random subtractive process have been analyzed in related probabilistic models. Blath, González, Kurt, and Spano
[
4
]
studied long-range seed bank models, where the genealogical structure of populations is governed by random time jumps analogous to the subtractive mechanism. Chierichetti, Kumar, and Tomkins
[
8
]
examined sequence models whose asymptotic behavior shares similarities with renewal-type processes underlying our setting. Baccelli, Haji-Mirsadeghi, Khezeli, and Sodre
[
3
,
2
,
1
]
explored subtractive dynamics in the context of unimodular random graphs and population processes. Finally, Igelbrink and Wakolbinger
[
12
]
investigated reinforced urn schemes where coalescence probabilities play a role closely related to recurrence properties of subtractive chains.

1.3
Results

The goal of this paper is to understand the behavior of the two-choice
model by studying its consistency in each of the three configurations
(i),(ii), and (iii), for three types of tails, light, moderate, and
heavy:

1.

\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
(light tails)

2.

\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
(moderate tails)

3.

\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
(heavy tails)

We now introduce the main definitions of consistency.

Definition 1
.

We say that the system is consistent in configuration (ii) if
L_{n}\to-\infty
;
while it is consistent in configuration (iii) if
\Lambda_{n}\to\infty
.
We differentiate between strong and weak consistency based on whether
convergence occurs almost surely or in probability.
In the case of configuration (i), we say that the system is weakly consistent if
(R_{n})_{n\geq 1}

is a tight sequence. It is strongly consistent if the sequence
(R_{n})_{n\geq 1}

is almost surely bounded.

Remark 2
.

Pareto tails.

Consider a distribution with Pareto tails, that is,
q_{n}=\Theta(1/n^{1+\alpha})
, with
\alpha>0
. This implies that
p_{n}=\Theta(1/n^{\alpha})
. When
\alpha>1
, we see that
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
.
The more interesting situation is when
\alpha\in(0,1]
. For
\alpha\in(1/2,1]
, we have
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
, yet
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
.
Finally, for
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
, we have
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
.
For light-tailed
Z
, we recall from Broutin, Devroye, Lugosi, Oliveira
[
6
]
that
r_{n}\to 1/\mathbb{E}Z
. For moderate and heavy tails,
however, we have
r_{n}\to 0
. When
\alpha\in(0,1)
, then
Z
is in the domain
of attraction of the extremal stable law with parameter
\alpha
, which itself
has a tail distribution function that decays at the rate
1/n^{\alpha}
(see, e.g., Ibragimov and Linnik
[
11
]
, Zolotarev
[
15
]
or Malevich
[
13
]
). One can then deduce that
r_{n}
decays at the rate

1/n^{1-\alpha}
. In particular,
\sumop\displaylimits_{n}r_{n}^{2}<\infty
when
\alpha\in(0,1/2)
, which roughly corresponds to the case of heavy tails.

Next, we describe the main results of the paper.
For a summary, see the table at the end of this section.

Light tails

When
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
, time jumps are so short that
T_{n}
can’t reach distant leaves, thus forcing the set
\mathcal{L}_{n}
to be bounded.
This case does not require much work, since one can quickly determine consistency or inconsistency in each of the three configurations just by looking at the sequence
(L_{n})_{n\geq 1}
, which happens to be bounded almost surely.

Theorem 2
.

Let
L_{\infty}=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{n}
. If
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
, then
L_{\infty}
is finite almost surely.
Thus, the sequences
(R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are almost surely bounded.

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(L_{\infty}\leq-x\right)\leq 2\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(n-Z_{n}\leq-x\right)=2\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n+x}~.

Since
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
, we know that
\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}p_{n+x}
is finite and goes to
0
as
x\rightarrow+\infty
. Thus, by continuity of measure,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(L_{\infty}=-\infty\right)=\lim_{x\rightarrow+\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(L_{\infty}\leq-x\right)=0~.

Since
|L_{n}|\geq\Lambda_{n}
and
|L_{n}|\geq|R_{n}|
, this also implies that
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are almost surely bounded.

Moderate and heavy tails

When
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
, a different behavior emerges:

Theorem 3
.

Let
Z
,
W
,
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be i.i.d. random variables. Assume furthermore that
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty~.
Then, with probability one,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}<\infty~.

In other words, the system is not strongly consistent in the
heavy-tailed regime in configuration (iii).

Proof.

Note that if
\min(Z_{n},W_{n})\geq n
, then
\Lambda_{n}\in\{1,2\}
. Thus,
to prove Theorem
3
, it suffices to show
that with probability one,
\min(Z_{n},W_{n})\geq n
happens
infinitely often. This follows by the second Borel-Cantelli lemma since the events

(\{\min(Z_{n},W_{n})\geq n\})_{n\geq 0}

are independent and

\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}\mathbb{P}\left(\min(Z_{n},W_{n})\geq n\right)=\mathbb{E}\left(\min(Z,W)\right)=\infty~.

The behavior of the set
\mathcal{L}_{n}
for moderate and heavy tails is
better understood by examining its extreme points
L_{n}
and
R_{n}
.
The following two theorems are proved in Section
3
.

Theorem 4
.

If
q_{i}>0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
, then, with
probability one,

L_{n}\to-\infty~.

Thus, in configuration (ii) the system is strongly consistent in the
moderate and heavy-tailed regimes.

Strong (in-)consistency in configuration (i) is established in the
next result:

Theorem 5
.

Let
Z
,
W
,
(Z_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(W_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be i.i.d. random variables.

1.

If
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
, then
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|R_{n}|<\infty
almost surely.

2.

If
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
, then
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|R_{n}|=\infty
almost surely.

Given that the leftmost leaf in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
goes to
-\infty
and the rightmost leaf remains bounded in the moderate heavy-tail regime (i.e., when
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
), one can expect that the total number of leaves reached by the vertex
n
diverges under these two assumptions. Thus, strong consistency is observed in each of the three configurations (i), (ii), and (iii).

On the other hand, only configuration (i) leads to strong consistency
when the distribution of
Z
has a heavy-tail (i.e., when
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
). However, when
\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}<\infty
, the model remains weakly consistent in configuration (iii),
as established by the next two results. Theorems
6
and
7
are proved in Sections

4.1
and
4.2
, respectively.

Theorem 6
.

Assume that the distribution of
Z
is aperiodic, that is, the greatest common divisor of the support of
Z
is
1
. If
Z
exhibits a moderate-sized tail (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
), then

\Lambda_{n}\to\infty\text{ almost surely. }

Theorem 7
.

When
Z
has a heavy tail (i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
), and
\sumop\displaylimits_{n\geq 0}r_{n}^{2}<\infty~,
then

\Lambda_{n}\to\infty\textit{ in probability.}

We recall from Remark
2
that the summability of
r_{n}^{2}
is assured for nearly all
heavy-tailed
Z
.

Simulations

To complement our theoretical analysis, we conducted a series of simulations to examine the empirical behavior of the model. We considered discrete Pareto distributions for the random variables
Z
and
W
, with probabilities
q_{n}=c/n^{1+\alpha}
, so that varying
\alpha
produces light-tailed, moderate-tailed, and heavy-tailed regimes. Our analysis focused on the evolution of the preferential value
V_{n}
of the topic recommended at time
n
in configuration (iii), where topics are assigned independent uniform preference values.

The one-choice model

Some of our proofs use the observation that the graph contains several copies of the subtractive random forest studied in
[

reached by vertex

Proof.
[Proof of Theorem

] From Theorem

we know that with probability one, for all
k\leq 0
,
there are at most a finite number of integers
n\geq 1
such that

. Thus, the sequence
(\min T_{n}^{Z})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, taking

, cannot take the same value an

Proof.
[Proof of Theorem

Assume that

such that, with probability one,

In particular, for any

4
Number of leaves

4.1
Moderate tails

Consider the number
\Lambda_{n}
of leaves reached by vertex
n
, when the distribution of
Z
is such that
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
.
We first show divergence in probability,
implying weak consistency in configuration (iii).

Proposition 11
.

Assume that the distribution of
Z
is aperiodic. If
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
, then

\Lambda_{n}\to\infty\quad\text{in probability as }n\to\infty.

The proof uses the balance that arises in the moderate-tail regime. The distribution of delays is sufficiently heavy to ensure that each non-leaf vertex in
T_{n}
has a non-negligible chance of reaching a leaf, but light enough to guarantee that
T_{n}
contains a substantial number of non-leaf vertices. Consequently, the overall number of opportunities to generate new leaves grows significantly with
n
. This balance is illustrated in Figure
4
: only in the moderate tail regime (
\alpha=0.6
in the figure) do we simultaneously observe a sizable number of blue vertices (non-leaves) and a large population of red vertices (leaves).

To formalize this heuristic, we focus on the chain
S_{n}
, obtained by repeatedly following the shortest outgoing edge starting from vertex
n
. In the moderate-tailed regime, this chain typically contains many vertices, and each of these vertices has an additional opportunity to connect to a leaf through its longest outgoing edge. We therefore define

J_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{m\in S_{n}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})\geq m},

so that
J_{n}
counts the number of vertices in
S_{n}
that are connected to a leaf via their longest outgoing edge. Controlling the growth of
J_{n}
makes it possible to turn the heuristic balance into a rigorous statement.

Lemma 12
.

The expected number of vertices in the chain
S_{n}
that connect to a leaf through their longest outgoing edge grows to infinity with
n
:

\mathbb{E}[J_{n}]\;\to\;\infty\quad\text{as }n\to\infty~.

Proof.

Note that for
n\geq m\geq 1
, the events
\{m\in S_{n}\}
and
\{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})\geq m\}
are independent. Define
v_{m}:=\mathbb{P}(0\in S_{m})
for all
m\geq 1
and let
v_{0}=1
, so that

\mathbb{E}J_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}(m\in S_{n})\mathbb{P}(\max(Z_{m},W_{m})\geq m)=\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}v_{n-m}\mathbb{P}(\max(Z_{m},W_{m})\geq m)~.

The sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 0}
can be better understood by considering the arithmetic renewal process
M_{n}
with holding times given by the i.i.d. random variables
(\min(Z_{i},W_{i}))_{i\geq 1}
, and initialized with
M_{0}=0
. In other words,

M_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\min(Z_{i},W_{i})~.

By definition,
v_{n}
is the probability that
n
can be expressed as a sum of independent random variables
(\min(Z_{i},W_{i}))_{i\geq 0}
. In terms of the renewal process, this means that

v_{n}=\mathbb{P}(\exists k\geq 0:M_{k}=n)~.

Hence, the sequence
(v_{n})_{n\geq 0}
corresponds to the renewal measure of the aperiodic renewal process
(M_{n})_{n\geq 0}
. By the Erdős–Feller–Pollard renewal theorem
[
9
]
, it follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}v_{n}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)}>0~.

(4.1)

Thus, for some constant
c>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}J_{n}\geq c\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}p_{m}\to\infty\quad\text{as }n\to\infty~.

(4.2)

We use the second-moment method to show that
J_{n}\to\infty
in probability.

Lemma 13
.

Assume that
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
and
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
, and let
\mathbb{V}J_{n}
denote the variance of the random variable
J_{n}
. Then

\frac{\mathbb{V}J_{n}}{(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}}\to 0\quad\text{as }n\to\infty~.

(4.3)

In particular, we have:

\mathbb{P}\left(J_{n}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2}\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(J_{n}-\mathbb{E}J_{n}\leq-\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2}\right)\leq 4\frac{\mathbb{V}J_{n}}{(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}}\to 0~,

and therefore
J_{n}\to\infty
in probability as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.
[Proof of Lemma
13
]
To show (
4.3
), we prove that
\mathbb{E}[J_{n}^{2}]/(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}\to 1
as
n\to\infty
. We first define

p_{n}^{*}=\mathbb{P}(\max(Z,W)\geq n)=2p_{n}-p_{n}^{2}~.

Recall that
v_{n}=\mathbb{P}(0\in S_{n})
, as introduced earlier. Then, for
n\geq 1
,

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}v_{n-m}v_{n-k}+\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq m\leq n}(p_{m}^{*})^{2}v_{n-m}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}v_{n-m}v_{n-k}+O(1)

(4.4)

since
\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq m\leq n}(p_{m}^{*})^{2}v_{n-m}^{2}
is bounded by
4\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
.

Now, define for any
m\geq 1
the random variable
X_{m}=\max(Z_{m},W_{m})
and observe that for any
1\leq k<m\leq n
, the events
\left\{X_{k}\geq k\right\}
and
\left\{X_{m}\geq m,m\in S_{n},k\in S_{n}\right\}
are independent.
Thus, for any fixed
n\geq 1
, we may write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[J_{n}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}\mathbb{P}\left(m\in S_{n},k\in S_{n},X_{m}\geq m,X_{k}\geq k\right)+\mathbb{E}J_{n}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}\mathbb{P}\left(m\in S_{n},k\in S_{n},X_{m}\geq m\right)+\mathbb{E}J_{n}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\left[k\in S_{n},X_{m}\geq m\middle|m\in S_{n}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(m\in S_{n}\right)+\mathbb{E}J_{n}

If
m\in S_{n}
, the event
\{k\in S_{n},X_{m}\geq m\}
can only happen if one of the two random variables
Z_{m}
and
W_{m}
is greater or equal to
m
and the other one is smaller than
m-k
, for otherwise
k
could not belong to
S_{n}
when
1\leq k<m\leq n
. Thus,
using the independence of
Z_{m}
with respect to
W_{m}
,
\{k\in S_{n}\}
and
\{m\in S_{n}\}
, and using the fact that

p_{m}^{*}+p_{m}^{2}=2p_{m}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}

\displaystyle\left[J_{n}^{2}\right]

(4.5)

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}\times 2\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\left[Z_{m}\geq m,W_{m}\leq m-k,k\in S_{n}\middle|m\in S_{n}\right]\mathbb{P}\left(m\in S_{n}\right)+\mathbb{E}J_{n}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}(p_{m}^{*}+p_{m}^{2})v_{n-m}\mathbb{P}\mathopen{}\left[W_{m}\leq m-k,k\in S_{n}\middle|m\in S_{n}\right]+\mathbb{E}J_{n}

\displaystyle=4\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}(p_{m}^{*}+p_{m}^{2})v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{k-m}\mathbb{P}\left(k\in S_{m-i}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(W_{m}=i\right)+\mathbb{E}J_{n}

\displaystyle=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}(p_{m}^{*}+p_{m}^{2})v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}v_{m-k-i}q_{i}+\mathbb{E}J_{n}~.

(4.6)

Observe that

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}p_{m}^{2}v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}q_{i}v_{m-k-i}

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}p_{m}^{2}v_{n-m}

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}p_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{\infty}p_{m}^{2}\right)\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}^{*}\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\mathbb{E}J_{n}\right)

by using the inequality in (
4.2
), which leads to

\mathbb{E}[J_{n}^{2}]=2\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq k<m\leq n}p_{k}^{*}p_{m}^{*}v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}v_{m-k-i}q_{i}+O\left(\mathbb{E}J_{n}\right)~.

(4.7)

Fix some
\epsilon>0
. By (
4.1
), we know that there exists some constant
x>0
such that for all
m\geq x
, we have
|v_{m}-\lambda|<\epsilon
, where
\lambda=1/(\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W))
.

If
m\geq x
, see that

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}v_{m-i}

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-x}q_{i}(\lambda+\epsilon)+\sumop\displaylimits_{m-x<i\leq m}q_{i}

\displaystyle\leq(\lambda+\epsilon)+p_{m-x}~.

Thus, there exists some constant
y>x
such that for all
m\geq y
,

\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m}q_{i}v_{m-i}\leq\lambda+2\epsilon~.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k<m\leq n\\
\textit{s.t. }m-k<y\end{subarray}}p_{m}^{*}v_{n-m}\underbrace{p_{k}^{*}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}v_{m-k-i}q_{i}}_{\leq 1}

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{1\leq m\leq n}p_{m}^{*}v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=m-y}^{m}1

\displaystyle=(y+1)\mathbb{E}J_{n}=O\left(\mathbb{E}J_{n}\right)~,

and

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq k<m\leq n\\
\textit{s.t. }n-m<y\end{subarray}}p_{k}^{*}\underbrace{p_{m}^{*}v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}v_{m-k-i}q_{i}}_{\leq 1}

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{n-y\leq m\leq n}1\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}^{*}

\displaystyle\leq(y+1)\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{n}p_{k}^{*}=O\left(\mathbb{E}J_{n}\right)~.

Putting everything together, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[J_{n}^{2}]=\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n-y}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m-y}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}v_{n-k}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}q_{i}v_{m-k-i}+O\left(\mathbb{E}J_{n}\right)~.

(4.8)

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}=\sumop\displaylimits_{s=1}^{n-y}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m-y}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}v_{n-m}v_{n-k}+O\left(\mathbb{E}J_{n}\right)~.

(4.9)

Finally, observe that for any
n>y
we have:

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n-y}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m-y}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}\underbrace{v_{n-m}\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{m-k}q_{i}v_{m-k-i}}_{\leq(\lambda+2\epsilon)^{2}}\leq(\lambda+2\epsilon)^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n-y}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m-y}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}

(4.10)

and

\displaystyle\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n-y}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m-y}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}\underbrace{v_{n-m}v_{n-k}}_{\geq(\lambda-\epsilon)^{2}}\geq(\lambda-\epsilon)^{2}\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n-y}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{m-y}p_{m}^{*}p_{k}^{*}~.

(4.11)

From (
4.8
), (
4.9
), (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) it follows that

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[J_{n}^{2}]}{(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}}\leq\left(\frac{\lambda+2\epsilon}{\lambda-\epsilon}\right)^{2}~.

Since this is true for any
\epsilon>0
small enough, and
\mathbb{E}[J_{n}^{2}]\geq(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}
, we conclude that

\frac{\mathbb{E}[J_{n}^{2}]}{(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}}\to 1

as
n\to\infty
.

We now turn to the proof of Proposition
11
, which asserts that the number of leaves in the medium-tail scenario diverges in probability. We have already shown that
J_{n}\to\infty
in probability, where
J_{n}
counts the number of edges connecting vertices in the chain
S_{n}
to leaves via their longest outgoing edge. Proposition
11
follows from this result, but some care is needed:
J_{n}
counts edges, not distinct leaves, and multiple edges may connect to the same leaf. The key idea of the proof is to show that the number of edges of the type
\max(Z_{n},W_{n})
connecting to any single leaf cannot grow to infinity. This ensures that the divergence of
J_{n}
indeed implies that the number of distinct leaves connected to
T_{n}
also diverges to infinity in probability.

Proof.
[Proof of Proposition
11
]
Let us define

\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}=\left\{k\leq 0:\textit{there exists an }m\in S_{n}\textit{ such that }\max(Z_{m},W_{m})=m-k\right\}~.

In other words,
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}
is the subset of vertices in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
that are connected to some vertex in
S_{n}
with positive index through the longest edge (i.e., given by
\max(Z,W)
).

Given
n\geq 1
and
k\in\mathbb{Z}
, we introduce the
random variable

D_{n,k}=\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})=m-k}.

Note that for any fixed
x>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle\left(\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}\right|<x\right)

(4.12)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left[\max_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}}\sumop\displaylimits_{m\in S_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})=m-k}<\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2x}\right]\cap\left[\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}\right|<x\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{k\leq 0}\left[D_{n,k}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2x}\right]\right)~.

(4.13)

Since
J_{n}
can be written as

J_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}}\sumop\displaylimits_{m\in S_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})=m-k}~,

we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left[\max_{k\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}}\sumop\displaylimits_{m\in S_{n}}\mathbbm{1}_{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})=m-k}<\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2x}\right]\cap\left[\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}\right|<x\right]\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{P}\left(J_{n}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq 4\frac{\mathbb{V}J_{n}}{(\mathbb{E}J_{n})^{2}}\to 0~.

(4.14)

Moreover, for any
k\leq 0
and
y>0
, by Chernoff’s bound
[
7
]
,
[
5
]
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{n,k}>y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\left(\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{\max(Z_{m},W_{m})=m-k}>y\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(y-p_{1-k}^{*}-y\log\left(\frac{y}{p_{1-k}^{*}}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e}{y}\right)^{y}(p_{1-k}^{*})^{y}~.

Let
y_{n}=\mathbb{E}J_{n}/(2x)
and assume that
n
is large enough so we have
y_{n}\geq 2
and

\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{\infty}(p_{m}^{*})^{y_{n}}\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{\infty}(p_{m}^{*})^{2}<\infty\text{ (since }\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty\text{ )}~.

Thus, by the union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{k\leq 0}\left[D_{n,k}\geq\frac{\mathbb{E}J_{n}}{2x}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{k\leq 0}\left(\frac{e}{y_{n}}\right)^{y_{n}}(p_{1-k}^{*})^{y_{n}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\frac{e}{y_{n}}\right)^{y_{n}}\sumop\displaylimits_{k=1}^{\infty}(p_{k}^{*})^{2}\to 0~.

(4.15)

By putting (
4.13
), (
4.14
), and (
4.15
) together we have that
\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\mathcal{L}_{n}^{*}\right|<x\right)\to 0
as
n\to\infty
for every fixed
x>0
, which proves Proposition
11
.

Having established divergence in probability, we are now ready to prove almost sure divergence.

Proof.
[Proof of Theorem
6
]
For any
n\geq 1
, define

\mathbb{P}\left(Y_{n}\leq k\right)\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{m\geq n}\mathbb{P}\left(\min(Z_{m},W_{m})>m-k\right).

Since
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)<\infty
, the sum above is finite and goes to

as

goes to infinity. Hence,

in probability.

and apply Corollary

the set of integers so that

and the edges are

(Z_{k})_{k\geq n+1}
and

(W_{k})_{k\geq n+1}
. This gives us a random variable
N_{n}>n
,

N_{n}\in S_{k}
for all

. Let
I_{n}\leq n\}

be the root of this tree, and note that

Y_{n}\leq I_{n}\leq n<N_{n}~.

:

, one can choose

.

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}>2n\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(N_{n}\leq 2n,\Lambda_{I_{n}}\leq m\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(N_{0}>n\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\Lambda_{I_{n}}\leq m\right)~.

m\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\cap_{n\geq 1}\cup_{k\geq 2n}\left[\Lambda_{k}\leq m\right]\right)=0.

By the union bound,

\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{m\geq 1}\cap_{n\geq 1}\cup_{k\geq 2n}\left[\Lambda_{k}\leq m\right]\right)=0~,

which implies that
\Lambda_{n}\to\infty
almost surely.

4.2
Heavy tails

In this section, we study the heavy-tailed case, that is,
\mathbb{E}\min(Z,W)=\infty
. From Theorem
3
we already know that
\Lambda_{n}
cannot go to infinity with probability one.
Here we show that, in contrast to this,
\Lambda_{n}\to\infty
in probability when
\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}<\infty
.

Lemma 14
.

For any integers
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\geq\max(1,k)
,

\mathbb{P}\left(k\in T_{n}^{Z}\right)\leq r_{n-k}~.

Proof.

Clearly, we have
\mathbb{P}\left(k\in T_{n}^{Z}\right)=r_{n-k}
whenever
k\geq 0
. Moreover,
r_{n}
satisfies the recursion

r_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}q_{i}r_{n-i}~.

Thus, for any
k>0
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(-k\in T_{n}^{Z}\right)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{i}=i+k\right)\mathbb{P}\left(i\in T_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n}q_{k+i}r_{n-i}

\displaystyle=\sumop\displaylimits_{i=k+1}^{n+k}q_{i}r_{n+k-i}

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{n+k}q_{i}r_{n+k-i}=r_{n+k}~.

Proof.
[Proof of Theorem
7
]
Assume that
\sumop\displaylimits_{n=1}^{\infty}r_{n}^{2}<\infty
. Fix a constant
w>0
and let
I_{n}^{w}
denote the number of intervals of the form
(kw,(k+1)w]
, with
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, that are intersected by the chain
T_{n}^{Z}
. Note that, for any
x>0
, if
I_{n}^{w}<x
then
T_{n}^{Z}<wx
, thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n}^{w}<x\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(T_{n}^{Z}<wx\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(Z_{1}+\dots+Z_{\lfloor wx\rfloor}>n\right)\to 0~.

(4.16)

Let
\ell\geq 1
be an arbitrary integer and observe that, if the event
I_{n}^{w}\geq 2\ell
occurs, then one can define a random set of vertices
A_{n}
such that

1.

A_{n}
is independent of
(W_{k})_{k\geq 1}~,

2.

A_{n}\subset T_{n}^{Z}~,

3.

|A_{n}|=\ell~,

4.

\text{for all }k\neq m\in A_{n}~,~|k-m|\geq w~.

Conditionally on
I_{n}^{w}\geq 2\ell
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{k\neq m,k\in A_{n}}T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)

(4.17)

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,m\in A_{n}\\
\textit{s.t. }k\neq m\end{subarray}}\mathbbm{1}_{T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset}

\displaystyle\quad\leq\mathbb{E}\sumop\displaylimits_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,m\in A_{n}\\
\textit{s.t. }k\neq m\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\quad\leq{\ell\choose 2}\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}k,m\leq n\\
\textit{s.t. }|k-m|\geq w\end{subarray}}\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)

(4.18)

by using the first moment method and the independence of
A_{n}
with respect to
(W_{k})_{k\geq 1}
. Let
k<m
be a pair of integers such that
|k-m|\geq w
and observe that

\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{Z}\neq\emptyset\right)~.

Thus, the union bound, Lemma
14
and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality yield

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=-\infty}^{k}\mathbb{P}\left(i\in T_{k}^{W}\right)\mathbb{P}\left(i\in T_{m}^{Z}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sumop\displaylimits_{i=-\infty}^{k}r_{k-i}r_{m-i}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{\infty}r_{i}^{2}}\times\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=m-k}^{\infty}r_{i}^{2}}.

(4.19)

From (
4.18
) and (

), it follows that

\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{k\neq m,k\in A_{n}}T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)\leq{\ell\choose 2}\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=0}^{\infty}r_{i}^{2}}\times\sqrt{\sumop\displaylimits_{i=w}^{\infty}r_{i}^{2}}.

(4.20)

Fix
\epsilon>0
. From (
4.20
) we know that we can choose
w
big enough so we have for
n\geq 1
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\cup_{k\neq m,k\in A_{n}}T_{k}^{W}\cap T_{m}^{W}\neq\emptyset\right)\leq\epsilon.

Finally, observe that if
\Lambda_{n}<\ell

\mathbb{P}\left(\Lambda_{n}<\ell\right)\leq\mathbb{P}\left(I_{n}^{w}<2\ell\right)+\epsilon~.

From (

.

5
Generalization to
k
choices.

A natural generalization of the model allows for
k\geq 2
choices. Fix
k\geq 2
and consider
k
independent sequences of i.i.d. random variables
(Z_{i}^{(1)})_{i\geq 1},\dots,(Z_{i}^{(k)})_{i\geq 1}
. Given a sequence of preference values
(U_{i})_{i\leq 0}
, we define a sequential random coloring by setting
V_{n}=U_{n}
and
C_{n}=n
for all
n\leq 0
, and for
n>0
letting

C_{n}=C_{n-Z_{n}^{(j)}}\quad\text{and}\quad V_{n}=V_{n-Z_{n}^{(j)}}\quad\text{where}\quad j=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{1\leq i\leq k}V_{n-Z_{n}^{(i)}}

for
n\geq 1~.

The set of vertices reached from vertex
n
is now defined recursively as

T_{n}=\{n\}\cup\left(\bigcupop\displaylimits_{i=1}^{k}T_{n-Z_{n}^{(i)}}\right),\quad n\geq 1~,

with
T_{n}=\{n\}
for all
n\leq 0
. As in the two-choice model, we may then define
R_{n}
,
\Lambda_{n}
, and
L_{n}
accordingly. The graph associated with this model therefore, contains multiple embedded copies of the two-choice process. It follows from this observation that consistency in the two-choice model carries over to the
k
-choice model for any
k\geq 2
, and for each of the three configurations of the initial pool of topics considered in this paper. As a consequence, Theorem
4
extends automatically to the
k
-choice model:

Theorem 15
.

Assume that
q_{i}>0
for all
i\in\mathbb{N}
and
\mathbb{E}Z=\infty
. Then, in the
k
-choice model, with
probability one,

L_{n}\to-\infty~.

Thus, the system is strongly consistent in configuration (ii).

However, the increase in the number of available recommendations allows one to work under weaker assumptions on the tail of
Z
, and in this setting the results can be sharpened.
In particular, one can adapt the proof given in Section
4.1
by modifying the recursion defining
S_{n}
to

S_{n}=\{n\}\cup S_{\,n-\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z_{n}^{(i)}},

and by controlling the growth of

J_{n}=\sumop\displaylimits_{m=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{m\in S_{n}}\,\mathbbm{1}_{\max_{1\leq i\leq k}Z_{m}^{(i)}\geq m}~,

to establish the following result.

Theorem 16
.

Assume that the distribution of
Z
is aperiodic, and that

\mathbb{E}[Z]=\infty,\qquad\mathbb{E}\!\left[\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z^{(i)}\right]<\infty~,

where
Z^{(1)},\dots,Z^{(k)}
are independent copies of
Z
.
Then, in the
k
-choice model,

\Lambda_{n}\to\infty\quad\text{almost surely.}

The arguments used in the proofs of Theorems
2
,
3
and Theorem
5
extend naturally to the present setting, leading to the following three results.

Theorem 17
.

Let
L_{\infty}=\inf_{n\in\mathbb{N}}L_{n}
. If
\mathbb{E}Z<\infty
, then
L_{\infty}
is finite almost surely.
Thus, in the
k
-choice model, the sequences
(R_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}},(L_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are almost surely bounded.

Theorem 18
.

Let
Z^{(1)},\dots,Z^{(k)}
be independent copies of
Z
. Assume that

\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z^{(i)}=\infty~.

Then, in the
k
-choice model, with probability one,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\Lambda_{n}<\infty~.

Theorem 19
.

Let
Z^{(1)},\dots,Z^{(k)}
be independent copies of
Z
.

1.

If
\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z^{(i)}<\infty
, then
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|R_{n}|<\infty
almost surely in the
k
-choice model.

2.

If
\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z^{(i)}=\infty
, then
\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}|R_{n}|=\infty
almost surely in the
k
-choice model.

In summary, the consistency table from Section
1.4
remains unchanged in the
k
-choice model. The only difference is that the moderate-tail regime is now characterized by the conditions
\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z^{(i)}<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}Z^{(1)}=\infty
, while the heavy-tail regime corresponds to
\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq k}Z^{(i)}=\infty
.

6
Conclusion

We introduced and studied a simple mathematical model for
recommendation systems based on giving each user two random choices,
which include a mixture of past choices and untried options. In this
setup, we identify three regimes based on the tail behavior of the
sizes of the jumps in the past.

Although our model is primarily theoretical, its analysis offers qualitative insights that may inform the design of recommendation systems. In particular, it highlights how structural properties of the recommendation process can influence long-term behavior and diversity. Extending the model to incorporate personalization and adaptive feedback remains an interesting direction for future work.

6.1
Comparison with standard baselines

To situate our results within the broader landscape of recommendation methods, it is natural to consider connections to standard algorithmic baselines. Classical approaches, such as collaborative filtering, multi-armed bandits, and deep learning–based recommender systems, rely on adaptive strategies to cope with noisy or partially observed user preferences. While our model is highly stylized and focuses on the structural properties of the recommendation process rather than optimization or learning, comparing its behavior conceptually to these frameworks helps highlight both its scope and its limitations.

A natural point of comparison is with multi-armed bandit algorithms
[
16
,
17
]
, which also model sequential decision-making and recommendation dynamics. However, the two frameworks differ fundamentally: in our model, the “rewards” (preference values) are static and noise-free, with randomness arising solely from the distribution of edge lengths, while in bandit algorithms, the primary challenge is to manage uncertainty in the reward distributions through exploration and exploitation. Because of this distinction, standard bandit baselines are not directly applicable to our setting, and a meaningful comparison would require a reformulation of the bandit problem that departs significantly from its usual assumptions.

A closer comparison can be drawn with bandit algorithms in the noiseless setting, where the reward associated with each arm is fixed and known after a single pull. In that framework, the problem is essentially trivial: the optimal arm is identified immediately, and the learning phase vanishes. By contrast, our model remains nontrivial even in the absence of noise, since the randomness lies not in the evaluation of topics but in the structure of the recommendation process itself. The stochastic nature of the edge lengths forces the system to repeatedly revisit or discover new topics, giving rise to rich asymptotic behaviors such as strong or weak consistency.

6.2
Perspectives and open problems

One might naturally expect the
k
-choice model to exhibit distinct behavior in the
k+1
regimes defined by the tail conditions

\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq\ell}Z^{(i)}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\min_{1\leq i\leq\ell+1}Z^{(i)}<\infty,

for each
\ell\in\{0,\dots,k\}
(with the understanding that the left-hand condition is omitted when
\ell=0
and the right-hand condition is omitted when
\ell=k
).
Although our results extend straightforwardly to the
k
-choice setting, the current consistency analysis does not distinguish between these individual regimes. A deeper understanding of the distinctions between these regimes and their implications for recommendation outcomes would be of significant interest.

The present model remains highly abstract and does not directly reflect real-world applications. For instance, the assumption of static preference values for each topic may not capture the evolution of actual user behavior. A natural direction for future work is to develop dynamic generalizations of the model that account for time-varying preferences for each topic.
