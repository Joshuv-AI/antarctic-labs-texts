Negative binomial count splitting for single-cell RNA sequencing data
Anna Neufeld, Joshua Popp, Lucy L. Gao, Alexis Battle, Daniela Witten

Abstract
The analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data often involves fitting a latent variable model to learn a low-dimensional representation for the cells. Validating such a model poses a major challenge. If we could sequence the same set of cells twice, we could use one dataset to fit a latent variable model and the other to validate it. In reality, we cannot sequence the same set of cells twice. Poisson count splitting was recently proposed as a way to work backwards from a single observed Poisson data matrix to obtain independent Poisson training and test matrices that could have arisen from two independent sequencing experiments conducted on the same set of cells. However, the Poisson count splitting approach requires that the original data are exactly Poisson distributed: in the presence of any overdispersion, the resulting training and test datasets are not independent. In this paper, we introduce negative binomial count splitting , which extends Poisson count splitting to the more flexible negative binomial setting. Given an n × p n\times p dataset from a negative binomial distribution, we use Dirichlet-multinomial sampling to create two or more independent n × p n\times p negative binomial datasets. We show that this procedure outperforms Poisson count splitting in simulation, and apply it to validate clusters of kidney cells from a human fetal cell atlas.

In our toy example in Figure

In the setting of Example

Figure

Now, suppose that we were able to sequence the same set of cells twice to obtain two independent datasets
X^{\mathrm{train}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
and
X^{\mathrm{test}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
generated from
\mathbb{X}
in (
1
) (with the same true underlying
L
and
\beta
matrices). We could estimate
L
and/or
\beta
using only
X^{\mathrm{train}}
, and could then validate the results or conduct inference using
X^{\mathrm{test}}
. Thus, the challenges associated with Examples 1 and 2 displayed in Figure 1(c) and 1(d) would be entirely avoided. Similarly, we could estimate one set of clusters on
X^{\mathrm{train}}
and another set of clusters on
X^{\mathrm{test}}
and compare the two clusterings using a metric such as the adjusted Rand Index
[
Hubert and Arabie 1985
]
, entirely avoiding the challenge of Example 3.

In practice, we cannot sequence the same set of cells twice. Instead, we propose using our single dataset
X
to reverse engineer two datasets
X^{\mathrm{train}}
and
X^{\mathrm{test}}
that function like independent sequencing experiments performed on the same sets of cells. Our proposal is an extension of the ideas of
Batson et al. 2019
,
Sarkar and Stephens 2021
, and
Neufeld et al. 2022
, who perform this reverse engineering under the assumption that
X_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Poisson}(\Lambda_{ij})
. However, scRNA-seq data are typically overdispersed relative to the Poisson or the binomial distribution, and so the
Poisson count splitting
procedure developed in these earlier papers will fail to produce independent training and test sets.

In recent work,
Neufeld et al. 2023
and
Dharamshi et al. 2023
developed
data thinning
, a vast generalization of Poisson count splitting that enables us to split a random variable drawn from a number of well-known distributional families into two or more independent components. In this paper, we focus on the special case of data thinning for negative binomial random variables, which allows us to obtain independent matrices
X^{\mathrm{train}}
and
X^{\mathrm{test}}
under the assumption that the elements of
X
are independent draws from negative binomial distributions. We refer to this procedure as
negative binomial count splitting
. Critically,
X^{\mathrm{train}}
and
X^{\mathrm{test}}
are drawn from the same distribution as
X
, up to a parameter scaling.

Figure
1
(c) and Figure
1
(d) show that, in our toy example, negative binomial count splitting correctly determines that
K^{*}=1
and controls the Type 1 error rate. Figure
1
(f) shows that a modified version of “intradataset cross-validation” that makes use of negative binomial count splitting yields a confusion matrix that accurately reflects the absence of signal.

Negative binomial count splitting requires a negative binomial assumption to ensure independence between the training and test sets. However, once the data has been split, we are free to use any latent variable estimation method or inferential technique.

2
Background

2.1
A review of data thinning

Our goal is to decompose an scRNA-seq dataset
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
into independent datasets
X^{\mathrm{train}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
and
X^{\mathrm{test}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
drawn from the same model as
X
, up to a parameter scaling.
We first review
Poisson count splitting
, which accomplishes this goal if
\mathbb{X}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Poisson}(\Lambda_{ij})
.

Algorithm 1

(Poisson count splitting)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
. For a chosen
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}\in(0,1)
such that
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\epsilon_{m}=1
, draw
\left(\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(1)},\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(M)}\right)\mid\mathbb{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}\left(X_{ij},\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}\right)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
be a dataset with entries
X_{ij}
drawn from
\mathbb{X}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\poi(\mu_{ij})
. If we apply Algorithm
1
to each element
X_{ij}
of this data, then
(1)
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathrm{Poisson}(\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
j=1,\ldots,p
, and (2) the folds
\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}^{(M)}
are mutually independent.

Theorem
1
follows from the well-known binomial thinning property of the Poisson distribution (see
Durrett 2019
, Section 3.7.2). For any fold
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
, we define
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
. The mutual independence between folds given in Theorem
1
ensures that, for all
m\in\{1,\ldots,M\}
,
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
is independent of
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
. Thus, by treating
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
as a training set and
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
as a test set, we arrive at an alternative to cross-validation that uses Poisson count splitting, rather than sample splitting, to create training and test sets.

Sarkar and Stephens 2021
and
Neufeld et al. 2022
apply Poisson count splitting to overcome the challenges arising in Examples 1 and 2 of Section
1
, under the assumption that the scRNA-seq data follows a Poisson distribution. Unfortunately, the
Poisson assumption in Theorem
1
is necessary to achieve independence between the folds
\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}^{(M)}
. If the data instead follow a negative binomial distribution, then
\mathbf{X}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}^{(M)}
are correlated, and Poisson count splitting will fail to provide a valid approach for model evaluation or inference.

Neufeld et al. 2023
and
Dharamshi et al. 2023
expand upon Poisson count splitting to describe a general recipe for decomposing a single random variable
\mathbb{Y}\sim F_{\theta}
, for some distribution
F_{\theta}
indexed by an unknown parameter
\theta
, into independent pieces
\mathbb{Y}^{(m)}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}Q^{(m)}_{\theta}
, where
Q^{(m)}_{\theta}
is a (possibly different) distribution indexed by the same parameter
\theta
. They refer to this general framework as
data thinning
. In particular, the framework developed by
Neufeld et al. 2023
enables us to decompose a negative binomial random variable into
M\geq 2
independent negative binomial random variables, without knowledge of the mean parameter. While the property of the Poisson distribution that allows for Poisson count splitting is well-known, its negative binomial counterpart is less well-known. In fact, prior to
Neufeld et al. 2023
, thinning the negative binomial distribution appears to be unexplored outside of the time series literature of the 1990s
[
Joe 1996
]
.

2.2
Negative binomial models for scRNA-seq data

Throughout this paper, we let
\NB(\mu,b)
denote the negative binomial distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\mu+\frac{\mu^{2}}{b}
, for
\mu>0
and
b>0
. This parameterization is commonly used when modeling scRNA-seq data. Note that if
\mathbf{y}\mid\tau\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(\mu\tau)
and
\tau\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(b,b)
, then
\mathbf{y}\sim\NB(\mu,b)
. Because the variance
\mu+\frac{\mu^{2}}{b}
is always strictly larger than the mean
\mu
, the negative binomial model is overdispersed relative to the Poisson model. This motivates its use in the analysis of RNA sequencing data, where the data are non-negative integers with excess variance relative to the Poisson distribution
[
Choudhary and Satija 2022
,
Hafemeister and Satija 2019
,
Sarkar and Stephens 2021
,
Lopez et al. 2018
]
. We refer to the parameter
b
as the overdispersion parameter. As
b\rightarrow\infty
, the negative binomial distribution approaches the Poisson distribution. In the scRNA-seq literature, it is common to assume that each gene, but not each cell, has its own overdispersion parameter
[
Hafemeister and Satija 2019
,
Love et al. 2014
,
Lopez et al. 2018
]
. Thus, in what follows, we will assume that
\mathbb{X}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\mathrm{NB}\left(\mu_{ij},b_{j}\right)
.

3
Negative binomial count splitting

In this section, we consolidate some theoretical results from
Neufeld et al. 2022
and
Neufeld et al. 2023
to facilitate their immediate application to scRNA-seq data. Results in this section are proven in Appendix
B
.

3.1
Algorithm and main result

We now introduce negative binomial count splitting and state the key result.

Algorithm 2

(Negative binomial count splitting)
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
. For a chosen
M\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,
b_{j}^{\prime}\geq 0
for
j=1,\ldots,p
, and
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}\in(0,1)
such that
\sum_{m=1}^{M}\epsilon_{m}=1
, draw

\left(\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{(M)}\right)\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\mathrm{DirichletMultinomial}\left(X_{ij},\epsilon_{1}b_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}b_{j}^{\prime}\right)
.

The marginals of a Dirichlet-multinomial distribution are beta-binomial, i.e.
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\text{BetaBinomial}(X_{ij},\epsilon_{m}b_{j}^{\prime},(1-\epsilon_{m})b_{j}^{\prime})
. While binomial thinning has appeared in numerous papers as a way to construct training and test sets from count-valued data
[
Neufeld et al. 2022
,
Sarkar and Stephens 2021
,
Leiner et al. 2022
]
, to our knowledge beta-binomial thinning has only been used for this purpose by
Neufeld et al. 2023
. The beta-binomial thinning operator has appeared in the time series literature as far back as
McKenzie 1986
for constructing autoregressive processes with negative binomial marginals. The following result, which appeared for
M=2
in the context of autoregressive processes in
Joe 1996
, tells us what happens when
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{NB}\left(\mu_{ij},b_{j}\right)
and we apply Algorithm
2
with
b_{j}^{\prime}=b_{j}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
be a dataset such that the entries
X_{ij}
are realizations of
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\NB\left(\mu_{ij},b_{j}\right)
. If we apply Algorithm
2
to each element
X_{ij}
of this data using
b_{j}^{\prime}=b_{j}
,
then (1)
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\NB(\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij},\epsilon_{m}b_{j})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
,
j=1,\ldots,p
, and (2) the folds
X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(M)}
are mutually independent.

Drawing
(\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(M)})\mid\mathbb{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}
from a Dirichlet-multinomial distribution, as in Algorithm
2
, is the same as first drawing
\left(\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{M}\right)
from a
\mathrm{Dirichlet}(\epsilon_{1}b_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,\epsilon_{m}b_{j}^{\prime})
distribution and then letting
(\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(M)})\mid\mathbb{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}(X_{ij},\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_{M})
. Thus, to accommodate the overdispersion in
\mathbb{X}_{ij}
relative to the Poisson distribution, we add additional randomness to the sampling process by making the parameters
(\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M})
from Algorithm
1
random variables.

Theorem
2
implies that
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
is independent of
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}:=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
for
m=1,\ldots,M
. To address Examples
1
,
2
, and
3
from Section
1
, we will use
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
as a training set and
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
as a test set. The bottom line is that if we believe that
\mathbb{X}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\NB\left(\mu_{ij},b_{j}\right)
and we know the true values
b_{j}
, then a direct extension of Poisson count splitting is available.

3.2
The role of the parameter
b_{j}^{\prime}

Theorem
2
requires that we apply Algorithm
2
with the correct value of the overdispersion parameter; i.e. that
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\mu_{ij},b_{j})
and we choose
b_{j}^{\prime}=b_{j}
. In this section, we consider what happens when
b_{j}^{\prime}\neq b_{j}
.

When
b^{\prime}_{j}=\infty
, drawing
\left(\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{(M)}\right)\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\mathrm{DirichletMultinomial}\left(X_{ij},\epsilon_{1}b_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}b_{j}^{\prime}\right)
is equivalent to drawing
\left(\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{(1)},\ldots,\mathbf{X}_{ij}^{(M)}\right)\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\mathrm{Multinomial}\left(X_{ij},{\epsilon}_{1},\ldots,{\epsilon}_{M}\right)
, and so Algorithm
2
reduces to Algorithm
1
.

Theorem 3

(
Neufeld et al. 2022
)
.

If
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\NB\left(\mu_{ij},b_{j}\right)
and we apply Algorithm
2
with
b_{j}^{\prime}=\infty
to
\mathbf{X}_{ij}
, then (1)
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\overset{\mathrm{ind.}}{\sim}\NB(\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij},b_{j})
and (2)
\mathrm{Cor}(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij},\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij})=\frac{\sqrt{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})}}{\sqrt{\frac{b_{j}^{2}}{\mu_{ij}^{2}}+\frac{b_{j}}{\mu_{ij}}+\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})}}
, where
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}:=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
.

Theorem
3
says that while applying Poisson count splitting (or negative binomial count splitting with
b_{j}^{\prime}=\infty
) on data from a negative binomial distribution yields training and test sets that follow the same model as the full data up to a parameter scaling, these datasets are positively correlated. The positive correlation increases as the true value of
b_{j}
decreases, and decreases to
0
as
b_{j}\rightarrow\infty
. Moreover, we see from Theorem
3
that the overdispersion parameters (and thus the variances) of
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}
are too small relative to Theorem
2
. Thus, by failing to put enough noise into our sampling process, applying Poisson count splitting to negative binomial data results in training and test sets that are not as noisy as they should be, leading to positive correlation between them.

We now consider the more general case of finite
b_{j}^{\prime}
. The following result is included (under a different parameterization) in
Neufeld et al. 2023
.

Theorem 4
.

If
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\sim\NB(\mu_{ij},b_{j})
and we apply Algorithm
2
with parameter
b_{j}^{\prime}
:

1.

\E[\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}]=\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}
,

2.

\Var(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij})=\epsilon_{m}\Var(\mathbf{X}_{ij})+\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}-1\right)
,

3.

\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij},\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij})=\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(1-\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}\right)
, where
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}:=\mathbf{X}-\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
.

Unlike in Theorem
3
, the folds of data that result from applying Algorithm
2
with arbitrary values for
b_{j}^{\prime}
do not necessarily follow negative binomial distributions. The first statement of Theorem
4
says that, regardless of the value of
b^{\prime}_{j}
used, the expected value of
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
and
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
are scaled by
\eps_{m}
and
(1-\eps_{m})
compared to
\E[\mathbb{X}]
in (
1
). Thus, we can estimate the latent space using the training set
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
, and validate these estimates using the held out set
X^{(m)}
. The second statement of Theorem
4
tells us that using the wrong value for
b_{j}^{\prime}
affects the variance of
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
. The third statement says that if
b^{\prime}_{j}\neq b_{j}
, then the training and test sets are correlated, and the magnitude of the correlation grows with the magnitude of the discrepancy between
b_{j}
and
b_{j}^{\prime}
. The result is displayed and empirically confirmed in Figure
2
. In order to use
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
to validate a model fit to
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
or to do valid inference on latent variables fit to
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
, we need independence between
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
and
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
. Thus, the major takeaway from Theorem
4
is that, when the true
b_{j}
are unknown, it is important to estimate them well. In Section
4
, we use the well-known R package
sctransform

[
Hafemeister and Satija 2019
]
to estimate each
b_{j}
.

3.3
The role of the parameters
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}

In this section, we consider the case where we used the “correct” value of
b_{j}^{\prime}
and thus the results of Theorem
2
hold. In this setting, it is simple to show that, for a given fold
m
, the parameter
\epsilon_{m}
governs a tradeoff between the amount of information in the training set
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}
and in the test set
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}
. This result is summarized in the following theorem.

Theorem 5

(Information tradeoff as we vary
\eps
)
.

If
\mathbb{X}_{ij}\sim\NB(\mu_{ij},b_{j})
, then the Fisher information contained in a single datapoint
X_{ij}
for the parameter
\mu_{ij}
is
I_{\mu_{ij}}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})=\frac{b_{j}}{(b_{j}+\mu_{ij})\mu_{ij}}
. If we apply Algorithm
2
with
b^{\prime}_{j}=b_{j}
, then for
m=1,\ldots,M
, the Fisher information contained in
\mathbb{X}^{(m)}_{ij}
for the parameter
\mu_{ij}
is
\epsilon_{m}I_{\mu_{ij}}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})
, and the corresponding Fisher information contained in
\mathbb{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}
is
(1-\epsilon_{m})I_{\mu_{ij}}(\mathbf{X}_{ij})
.

We will see in Section
4
that the ideal choice of
\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}
depends on the application.

4
Simulation Study

In this section, we apply negative binomial count splitting to Example 1 and Example 2 from Section
1
, instantiated to the setting where there are
K^{*}
true discrete latent variables which represent cell types that we wish to estimate using
k
-means clustering. More specifically, we consider the following settings:

Example 1, instantiated to clustering:
We fit models with
K
clusters for
K=1,\ldots,10
, evaluate each model, then let the best value of
K
be our estimate of
K^{*}
.

Example 2, instantiated to clustering:
We cluster the data into
K=2
estimated cell types and test each gene for differential expression across the two estimated clusters.

After introducing our simulation setup in Section
4.1
, we show that negative binomial count splitting can be easily applied to Example 1 (Section
4.2
) and Example 2 (Section
4.3
).

4.1
Data generating mechanism

We generate datasets with
n
cells,
p
genes, and
K^{*}
true cell types. Each gene has a baseline expression level
\exp(\beta_{j0})
where
\beta_{j0}\overset{\mathrm{i.i.d.}}{\sim}N(0,1)
for
j=1,\ldots,p
.

For each dataset, we assign each cell to one of the
K^{*}
clusters with equal probability. The first column of the latent variable matrix
L\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times K^{*}}
contains ones, and the remaining columns are indicators for clusters
k=2,\ldots,K^{*}
. The matrix
\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times K^{*}}
stores
\beta_{10},\ldots,\beta_{p0}
in the first column. When
K^{*}>1
,
\beta_{j2}=\beta^{*}
for the first
5\%
of the genes, and the rest of the entries in the second column of
\beta
are
0
. If
K^{*}>2
, then
\beta_{j3}=\beta^{*}
for the next
5\%
of the genes, and all other entries are
0
. We continue filling in the
\beta
matrix in this manner until all
K^{*}
columns have been filled. We consider different values of
\beta^{*}
for different datasets, but within a dataset we always use the same value of
\beta^{*}
such that all
K^{*}
clusters are equally easy to detect. Finally, we let
\log\left(\Lambda\right)=L\beta^{\top}
.

We let the overdispersion parameter
b_{j}
for the
j
th gene be a function of the average expression
\bar{\Lambda}_{j}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Lambda_{ij}
for that gene
[
Choudhary and Satija 2022
,
Hafemeister and Satija 2019
,
Love et al. 2014
]
. More specifically, we set
b_{j}=\frac{\bar{\Lambda}_{j}}{\tau}
for either
\tau=1
(“mild overdispersion”) or
\tau=5
(“severe overdispersion”). We then let
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\sim\mathrm{NB}(\Lambda_{ij},b_{j})
, such that
\Var(\mathbb{X}_{ij})=\Lambda_{ij}\left(1+\frac{\Lambda_{ij}}{b_{j}}\right)\approx\Lambda_{ij}\left(1+\tau\right)
. We omit size factors from this simulation study, as they are not the focus of this paper.

4.2
Selecting the number of clusters

4.2.1
Methods

We now introduce the general algorithm used in this section.

Algorithm 3

(Estimating the number of clusters)
.

Start with datasets
X^{\mathrm{train}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
and
X^{\mathrm{test}}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
and parameter
\eps\in(0,1)
, where
\E\left[X^{\mathrm{test}}\right]=\frac{1-\eps}{\eps}\E[X^{\mathrm{train}}]
. For
K=1,\ldots,10
:

1.

Run k-means clustering to estimate
K
clusters on
\log(X^{\mathrm{train}}+1)
. This yields a cluster assignment
\hat{c}_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

2.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,p
, estimate
\E[\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{\mathrm{train}}]
with the sample mean of the training set points assigned to the same cluster:

\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{train}}_{ij}=\frac{1}{\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\mathbb{1}\{\hat{c}_{i^{\prime}}=\hat{c}_{i}\}}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}X^{\mathrm{train}}_{i^{\prime}j}\mathbb{1}\{\hat{c}_{i^{\prime}}=\hat{c}_{i}\}
.

3.

Estimate
\E[\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{\mathrm{test}}]
as follows:

\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{test}}_{ij}=\frac{1-\eps}{\eps}\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{train}}_{ij}
.

4.

Compute the within-cluster mean squared error on the test set as follows:

MSE(K)=\frac{1}{n\times p}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\log\left(X^{\mathrm{test}}_{ij}+1\right)-\log\left(\hat{\mu}^{\mathrm{test}}_{ij}+1\right)\right)^{2}.

(2)

We apply Algorithm
3
as follows.

Naive method:
Run Algorithm
3
with
X^{\mathrm{train}}=X^{\mathrm{test}}=X
and
\eps=0.5
.

Poisson count splitting (PCS):
Apply Algorithm
1
to
X
with
M=2
and some
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
to obtain
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
. Run Algorithm
3
with
X^{\mathrm{train}}=X^{(1)},X^{\mathrm{test}}=X^{(2)}
, and
\epsilon=\epsilon_{1}
.

Negative binomial count splitting, known
b
(NBCS-known):
Apply Algorithm
2
to
X
with
M=2
, some choice of
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
, and
(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{p}^{\prime})=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})
to obtain
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
. Run Algorithm
3
using
X^{\mathrm{train}}=X^{(1)},X^{\mathrm{test}}=X^{(2)}
, and
\epsilon=\epsilon_{1}
.

Negative binomial count splitting, estimated
b
(NBCS-estimated):
First, use the
R
package
sctransform

[
Hafemeister and Satija 2019
]
to obtain estimates
\hat{b}_{1},\ldots,\hat{b}_{p}
of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{p}
. Details are given in Appendix
C
. Then run Algorithm
2
on matrix
X
with
M=2
, some choice of
(\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2})
, and
(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{p}^{\prime})=(\hat{b}_{1},\ldots,\hat{b}_{p})
to obtain
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
. Then run Algorithm
3
using
X^{\mathrm{train}}=X^{(1)},X^{\mathrm{test}}=X^{(2)}
, and
\epsilon=\epsilon_{1}
.

We now extend both versions of NBCS to perform cross-validation.

Negative binomial cross-validation, known
b
(NBCV-known):
Obtain
(X^{(1)},\ldots,X^{(M)})
by running Algorithm
2
on
X
with
M=10
,
\epsilon_{m}=\frac{1}{M}
for
m=1,\ldots,M
, and
(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{p}^{\prime})=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{p})
. For
m=1,\ldots,M
, apply Algorithm
3
with

X^{\mathrm{train}}=X^{(-m)}
,
X^{\mathrm{test}}=X^{(m)}
, and
\eps=\frac{M-1}{M}
. For each value of
K
, record the total MSE summed across the
M
folds.

Negative binomial cross-validation, estimated
b
(NBCV-estimated):
First obtain estimates
\hat{b}_{1},\ldots,\hat{b}_{p}
of
b_{1},\ldots,b_{p}
using
sctransform
on the full data
X

2
with
(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{p}^{\prime})=(\hat{b}_{1},\ldots,\hat{b}_{p})
.

We already showed in Section
1
that the naive method fails to provide a viable solution to Examples 1 and 2; nonetheless we include it here for the sake of comparison. We do not consider sample splitting in this section, as we already saw in Section
1
that it fails to provide a viable solution to our problems of interest.

Testing for differential expression

4.3.1
Methods

5
Application to fetal cell atlas data

In this section, we apply negative binomial count splitting to solve the problems associated with Example
3
from Section
1
.
Cao et al. 2020
sequenced more than 4 million cells from 121 human fetal samples to create a fetal cell atlas, with a goal of organizing the cells from each of 15 different organs into cell types and cell subtypes. In one analysis, after preprocessing, they clustered the expression data from
the kidney cells into nine main cell types. They then subsetted the data to include only the cluster thought to correspond to metanephric kidney cells, and clustered that subset into 10 cell subtypes. As there is no ground truth available for the main cell types or the cell subtypes, it is difficult to validate their results.
Cao et al. 2020
use a procedure that they call
intradataset cross-validation
; see their Figure 2. We describe a simplified version of this procedure in Algorithm
5
.

Algorithm 5

(Intradataset cross-validation,
Cao et al. 2020
)
.

Input: a cell-by-gene expression matrix
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
.

1.

After preprocessing, cluster the dataset
X
to obtain estimated cell types
\hat{L}(X)^{\mathrm{cluster}}
.

2.

Divide the
n
cells into
5
folds and, for
m=1,\ldots,5
:

(a)

Let all cells in the
m
th fold be the test set; the remaining cells are the training set.

(b)

Train a classifier to predict
\hat{L}(X)^{\mathrm{cluster}}_{i}
using
X_{i}
, where
i
indexes the cells in the training set.

(c)

Use this trained classifier
to predict
\hat{L}^{\mathrm{cluster}}_{i^{\prime}}
using
X_{i^{\prime}}
, where
i^{\prime}
indexes the cells in the test set. Let
\hat{L}_{i^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{classifier}}
denote the prediction for the
i^{\prime}
th cell.

3.

For
i=1,\ldots,n
, the
i
th cell now has a value
\hat{L}(X)^{\mathrm{cluster}}_{i}
, obtained from clustering, and
\hat{L}^{\mathrm{classifier}}_{i}
, obtained via prediction when it belonged to the test set. Make a confusion matrix comparing
\hat{L}(X)^{\mathrm{cluster}}
and
\hat{L}^{\mathrm{classifier}}
. A diagonal confusion matrix is treated as evidence that the clusters are reproducible, since the cluster assignment for a given cell can be recovered by a classifier that was trained without knowledge of that cell’s cluster assignment. The adjusted Rand index (ARI) numerically summarizes the degree of agreement between
\hat{L}(X)^{\mathrm{cluster}}
and
\hat{L}^{\mathrm{classifier}}
.

In Section
1
(Figure
1
(e)), we showed using a toy dataset that this procedure is problematic. Because the entire dataset
X
is used in Step 1 to estimate the clusters
\hat{L}(X)^{\mathrm{cluster}}
, the test set in Step 2(c) has not truly been held out of the training process. Thus, despite the fact that the clusters estimated on the toy dataset are driven by random noise, the confusion matrix output by Algorithm
5
is close to diagonal and the ARI is close to
1
(Figure
1
(e)). Negative binomial count splitting provides a simple alternative (Figure
1
(f)), which we outline below.

Algorithm 6

(Intradataset cross-validation via count splitting)
.

Input: a cell-by-gene expression matrix
X\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n\times p}
.

1.

Obtain an estimate
\hat{b}_{j}
of the gene-specific overdispersion parameter
b_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
.

2.

Apply Algorithm
2
(negative binomial count splitting) with
M=2
,
\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=0.5
, and
(b_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,b_{p}^{\prime})=(\hat{b}_{1},\ldots,\hat{b}_{p})
to create two folds of data,
X^{(1)}
and
X^{(2)}
.

3.

After preprocessing, apply a clustering algorithm to
X^{(1)}
to obtain an estimated cluster
\hat{L}(X^{(1)})_{i}
for the
i
th cell for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

4.

After preprocessing, apply the same clustering algorithm to
X^{(2)}
to obtain an estimated cluster
\hat{L}(X^{(2)})_{i}
for the
i
th cell for
i=1,\ldots,n
.

5.

Make a confusion matrix comparing
\hat{L}(X^{(1)})
and
\hat{L}(X^{(2)})
. A diagonal confusion matrix (up to a permutation of the columns) is treated as evidence of cluster reproducibility, since the cells are reliably assigned to the same cluster on independent realizations of the data. The ARI numerically summarizes the degree of agreement between
\hat{L}(X^{(1)})
and
\hat{L}(X^{(2)})
.

We now compare Algorithm
5
and Algorithm
6
on data from the human fetal cell atlas. We consider two versions of Algorithm
6

\hat{b}_{j}=\infty

\hat{b}_{j}

\epsilon_{1}=\epsilon_{2}=0.5
such that
X^{(1)}
and

are identically distributed. Our goal is not to discover the optimal clusters in this dataset, but rather to compare Algorithm
5
and Algorithm
6
as strategies for validating clusters. As such, we do not attempt to reproduce the exact analysis from
Cao et al. 2020

To start, we apply Algorithm

and both versions of Algorithm

178,603

We next apply Algorithm

annotated as metanephric cells. The results are shown in panels (d), (e), and (f) of Figure

6
Discussion

In this paper, we introduced an algorithm to split a negative binomial random variable into two or more independent negative binomial random variables, and we applied it to the analysis of scRNA-seq data. This algorithm is also applicable in other settings; data are modeled as negative binomial in a wide variety of fields. Furthermore, while we were particularly motivated by unsupervised settings in which sample splitting is not an option, negative binomial count splitting can also be used in supervised settings such as inference after variable selection in negative binomial regression. For further discussion, see
Neufeld et al. 2023
and
Dharamshi et al. 2023
. An implementation of the techniques in this paper is available in the
R
package
countsplit
, and tutorials showing how this can be integrated with existing scRNA-seq software packages, are available at
anna-neufeld.github.io/countsplit.tutorials
.

and

B.3
Proof of Theorem
4

While parts of this theorem were proved in
[
24
]
, we prove this theorem in full directly here so that the notation matches that of this paper.

The first statement of Theorem
4
follows directly from the law of total expectation. Regardless of the value of
b^{\prime}_{j}
,

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}=X_{ij}\right]\right]=\E\left[\epsilon_{m}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right]=\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij},

\displaystyle\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}=X_{ij}\right]\right]=\E\left[(1-\epsilon_{m})\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right]=(1-\epsilon_{m})\mu_{ij}.

This is because the parameters
b_{j}^{\prime}
do not affect the expected values of the

\mathrm{DirichletMultinomial}\left(X_{ij},\epsilon_{1}b_{j}^{\prime},\ldots,\epsilon_{M}b_{j}^{\prime}\right)
distribution, only the variance.

The second statement of Theorem
4
uses the law of total variance. We start by deriving the marginal variance of
\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)
, as follows:

\displaystyle\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\right)\right]+\Var\left(\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\right]\right).

As
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbf{X}_{ij}=X_{ij}\sim\bb\left(X_{ij},\epsilon_{m}b_{j}^{\prime},(1-\epsilon_{m})b_{j}^{\prime}\right)
, we plug in the (known) mean and variance of the beta-binomial distribution.

\displaystyle\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\E\left[\frac{\mathbf{X}_{ij}\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})(b_{j}^{\prime}+\mathbf{X})}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\right]+\Var\left(\epsilon_{m}\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})b_{j}^{\prime}}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\E\left[\mathbf{X}\right]+\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\E\left[\mathbf{X}^{2}\right]+\epsilon_{m}^{2}\Var\left(\mathbf{X}_{ij}\right).

We next plug in the known mean and variance of
\mathbf{X}_{ij}\sim\NB(\mu_{ij},b_{j})
.

\displaystyle\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})b_{j}^{\prime}\mu_{ij}}{(b^{\prime}+1)}+\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\left(\mu_{ij}+\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}+\mu_{ij}^{2}\right)+\epsilon_{m}^{2}\left(\mu_{ij}+\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}+\frac{\epsilon_{m}^{2}\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}+\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\mu^{2}}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\left(\frac{1}{b_{j}}+1\right).

To compare the magnitude of this variance to

\epsilon_{m}\Var(\mathbf{X})
, we add and subtract
\epsilon_{m}\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}
.

\displaystyle\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=\left[\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}+\epsilon_{m}\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\right]-\left[\epsilon_{m}\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}-\epsilon_{m}^{2}\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\right]+\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\mu_{ij}^{2}}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\left(\frac{1}{b_{j}}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{m}\Var(\mathbf{X})-\left[\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\right]+\frac{\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\mu_{ij}^{2}}{(b_{j}^{\prime}+1)}\left(\frac{1}{b_{j}}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{m}\Var(\mathbf{X})+\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}-1\right),

as claimed in Theorem
4
. We omit the derivation of
\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}\right)
, as it is identical to the derivation above after noting that
\Var(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbb{X}_{ij}=X_{ij})=\Var(\mathbf{X}_{ij}-\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbb{X}_{ij}=X_{ij})=\Var(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\mathbb{X}_{ij}=X_{ij})
.

Finally, for the third statement of Theorem
4
, we use the fact that

2\times\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij},\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij})=\Var(\mathbb{X}_{ij})-\Var(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij})-\Var\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right).

We then plug in the known values of these variances, and
simplify, as follows:

\displaystyle 2\times\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij},\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij})

\displaystyle=\Var(\mathbb{X}_{ij})-\epsilon_{m}\Var(\mathbb{X}_{ij})-\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}-1\right)

\displaystyle\ \ \ \ \ -(1-\epsilon_{m})\Var(\mathbb{X}_{ij})-\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}-1\right)

\displaystyle=-2\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}-1\right).

Thus,
\mathrm{Cov}(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij},\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij})=\epsilon_{m}(1-\epsilon_{m})\frac{\mu_{ij}^{2}}{b_{j}}\left(1-\frac{b_{j}+1}{b_{j}^{\prime}+1}\right)
.

B.4
Proof of Theorem
5

We first derive the Fisher information contained in
X_{ij}
for the parameter
\mu_{ij}
:

\displaystyle I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbb{X}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=-\E\left[\frac{d^{2}}{d\mu_{ij}^{2}}\log\left(f\left(\mathbb{X}_{ij}\mid\mu_{ij},b_{j}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\E\left[\frac{d^{2}}{d\mu_{ij}^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{\Gamma(\mathbb{X}_{ij}+b_{j})}{\Gamma(b_{j})\mathbb{X}_{ij}!}\right)+\mathbb{X}_{ij}\log\left(\frac{\mu_{ij}}{\mu_{ij}+b_{j}}\right)+b_{j}\log\left(\frac{b_{j}}{\mu_{ij}+b_{j}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\E\left[\mathbb{X}_{ij}\left(\frac{-1}{\mu_{ij}^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})^{2}}\right)+b_{j}\left(\frac{1}{(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu_{ij}}-\frac{\mu_{ij}+b_{j}}{(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})^{2}}=\frac{b_{j}}{\mu_{ij}(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})}.

Now we derive the Fisher information contained in
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}
for the parameter
\mu_{ij}
:

\displaystyle I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)

\displaystyle=-\E\left[\frac{d^{2}}{d\mu_{ij}^{2}}\log\left(f\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\mid\eps\mu_{ij},\eps b_{j}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\E\left[\frac{d^{2}}{d\mu_{ij}^{2}}\left(\log\left(\frac{\Gamma(\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(m)}+\epsilon_{m}b_{j})}{\Gamma(\epsilon_{m}b_{j})(\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(m)})!}\right)+\mathbb{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\log\left(\frac{\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}}{\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}+\epsilon_{m}b_{j}}\right)+\epsilon_{m}b_{j}\log\left(\frac{\epsilon_{m}b_{j}}{\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}+\epsilon_{m}b_{j}}\right)\right)\right]

\displaystyle=-\E\left[\mathbb{X}_{ij}^{(m)}\left(\frac{-1}{\mu_{ij}^{2}}+\frac{1}{(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})^{2}}\right)+\epsilon_{m}b_{j}\left(\frac{1}{(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})^{2}}\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon_{m}}{\mu_{ij}}-\frac{\epsilon_{m}\mu_{ij}+\epsilon_{m}b_{j}}{(\mu_{ij}+b_{j})^{2}}=\epsilon_{m}I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbb{X}_{ij}\right).

Theorem
2
guarantees independence between
\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}
and
\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}
, and therefore
I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}\right)+I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(m)}_{ij}\right)=I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbb{X}_{ij}\right)
. It follows that
I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbf{X}^{(-m)}_{ij}\right)=(1-\epsilon_{m})I_{\mu_{ij}}\left(\mathbb{X}_{ij}\right)
.

Appendix C
Implementation details for Section
4

We use the
R
package
sctransform
in the following manner to estimate gene-specific overdispersion parameters in the simulations for Section
4
.

Briefly, for each gene
j=1,\ldots,p
,
sctransform
begins by fitting a negative binomial GLM with
\mathbb{X}_{j}
as the response, and the logged total number of unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) for the
n
cells as the covariate. (In our simulations, the total number of UMIs for a cell is the row sum for that cell.) This yields a maximum likelihood estimate
\hat{b}_{j}^{\mathrm{MLE}}
for each gene
j=1,\ldots,p
. These maximum likelihood estimates are known to be quite noisy for sparse negative binomial data. Furthermore, the “null model” that only includes the total number of UMIs as a covariate may be the correct model for
the majority of the genes, but will be incorrect for any genes that exhibit true differential expression across unknown latent variables. Thus, as a second step,
sctransform
fits a smooth kernel regression to estimate a relationship between the average expression of each gene and the gene-specific overdispersion. These smoothed estimates are used as the gene-specific overdispersion parameters.

We run the
vst()
function from the
sctransform
package in
R
with its default settings in Section
4
. We note that we simulated data in which the two main assumptions of
sctransform
are met: most genes are not differentially expressed, and there is a smooth relationship between the average expression of a gene and its parameter
b_{j}
. We use the same strategy in Section
5
, although in the real data setting we do not know for sure that the modeling assumptions of
sctransform
are met.
