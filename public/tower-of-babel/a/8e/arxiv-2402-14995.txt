Abstract
. If U U is a unitary operator on a separable complex Hilbert space ℋ \mathcal{H} , an application of the spectral theorem says there is a conjugation C C on ℋ \mathcal{H} (an antilinear, involutive, isometry on ℋ \mathcal{H} ) for which C ​ U ​ C = U ∗ . CUC=U^{*}. In this paper, we fix a unitary operator U U and describe all of the conjugations C C which satisfy this property. As a consequence of our results, we show that a subspace is hyperinvariant for U U if and only if it is invariant for any conjugation C C for which C ​ U ​ C = U ∗ CUC=U^{*} .

1.
Introduction

A version of the spectral theorem says that any unitary operator
U
on a separable complex Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is unitarily equivalent to a multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
on a Lebesgue space
L^{2}(\mu,X)

[
33
, p. 13]
. Here
X
is a compact Hausdorff space,
\mu
is a finite positive Borel measure on
X
, and
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mu,X)
is unimodular
\mu
-almost everywhere. If
J
is the mapping from
L^{2}(\mu,X)
to itself defined by
(Jf)(x)=\overline{f(x)}
,
x\in X
(the bar denotes complex conjugation), then
J
is an antilinear, isometric, and involutive map and is called a
conjugation
. Furthermore, the conjugation
J
induces the adjoint identity
JM_{\varphi}J=M_{\overline{\varphi}}=M_{\varphi}^{*}
. Via unitary equivalence, given any unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, this results in a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
for which
CUC=U^{*}
(see Lemma
2.3
). In the parlance of operator theory, one says that
U
is a
C
-symmetric operator

[
18
,
19
]
. As it turns out, there are many conjugations
C
on
\mathcal{H}
for which
CUC=U^{*}
. The goal of this paper is to describe them all.

Towards this goal, let

\mathscr{C}_{s}(U):=\{\mbox{$C$ is a conjugation on $\mathcal{H}$}:CUC=U^{*}\}.

As discussed in the previous paragraph,
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)\not=\varnothing
(see also Proposition
2.5
). On the other extreme,
\mathscr{C}_{s}(I)
, where
I
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
, is simply the set of
all
conjugations on
\mathcal{H}
. The subscript
s
, for “symmetric”, in the above definition of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
might seem superfluous. However, in a follow up paper to this one
[
28
]
, we will explore the set
\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
(notice the subscript
c
), the conjugations on
\mathcal{H}
that commute with
U
, i.e.,
CUC=U
.
This paper provides several characterizations of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
. One such characterization (Theorem
8.3
) involves the spectral theorem. When one is fortunate enough to have a concrete spectral decomposition of a unitary operator, for example when
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix, one can give a very tangible description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
. In particular, Theorem
4.2
says that if
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix
with spectral decomposition

U=W\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}I_{n_{1}}&&&\\
&\xi_{2}I_{n_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\xi_{d}I_{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix}W^{*},

where
W
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix,
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d}
(
1\leqslant d\leqslant n
) are the distinct eigenvalues of
U
,
I_{n_{j}}
is the
n_{j}\times n_{j}
identity matrix, and
n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{d}=n
, then any conjugation
C
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
for which
CUC=U^{*}
must take the form

(1.1)

C=W\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}JW^{*},

where
each
V_{j}
is an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
unitary matrix satisfying
V_{j}^{t}=V_{j}
(
t
denotes the transpose of a matrix), and
J
denotes the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

J[x_{1}\;x_{2}\cdots x_{n}]^{t}=[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\cdots\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}.

A version of (
1.1
) can be obtained in the infinite dimensional setting when the eigenvectors for
U
are complete in
\mathcal{H}
. In particular, we give a concrete description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
when
U
is the Fourier–Plancherel transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(Example
4.4
) and when
U
is the Hilbert transform on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
(Example
4.6
).

The main driver of nearly all the results in this paper is a measure-theoretic decomposition of any
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
given in Theorem
3.2
. This enables us to examine manageable pieces of
C
of
\mathcal{H}
on certain reducing subspaces of
U
.

Another description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
relies less on the spectral decomposition of a unitary operator
U
, which is often quite intangible in the general setting, and more on some special properties of
U
. For example, if
U
is a
bilateral shift
(see Definition
6.1
),
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
was described in
[
5
]
(see also Theorem
6.5
). In particular, when

is
the

(M_{\xi}f)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
on

(

is normalized Lebesgue measure on the unit circle

), then
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
if and only if
C=M_{u}J
, where
J
is the conjugation on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
given by
Jf=\overline{f}
and

is unimodular almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
(see Example

).

The unitary multiplication operator
M_{\psi}
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
, where
\psi
is an inner function (a bounded analytic function on
\mathbb{D}
whose radial boundary values on
\mathbb{T}
have modulus one

-almost everywhere
[
10
,
23
]
), turns out to be a bilateral shift
[
29
, Proposition 5.17]
and a concrete description of

is given in Theorem

. In fact (see Remark

), such

serve as models for all bilateral shifts and so, in a way, Theorem

, is a canonical model for
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
for any bilateral shift

.

As a byproduct of several results in this paper, we can connect conjugations with the hyperinvariant subspaces of a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
(those subspace which are invariant for any bounded operator on
\mathcal{H}
commuting with
U
). Certainly the invariant and hyperinvariant subspaces of unitary operators have been discussed before (for example
[
8
,
9
,
31
]
). Here we connect them with conjugations in the following way. For a (closed) subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent: (i)
\mathcal{M}
is hyperinvariant for
U
; (ii)
C\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every

. This result is obtained, along with several other equivalent conditions covered in this paper, via Theorems
3.2
,
3.11
, and
8.8
and is officially stated in Theorem

.

2.
Basic facts about conjugations and spectral measures

All Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
in this paper will be complex and separable.
Let
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of all bounded linear transformations on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of all
bounded antilinear transformations
on
\mathcal{H}
. By this we mean that

C\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
when

C({\bf x}+\alpha{\bf y})=C{\bf x}+\overline{\alpha}C{\bf y}
for all
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
(
C
is antilinear)
and
\sup\{\|C{\bf x}\|:\|{\bf x}\|=1\}
is finite (
C
is bounded).
We say that
C\in\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
is a
conjugation
if
C
satisfies the two additional conditions
\|C{\bf x}\|=\|{\bf x}\|
for all
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
(
C
is isometric) and
C^{2}=I
(
C
is involutive).
By the polarization identity, a conjugation also satisfies

(2.1)

\langle C{\bf x},C{\bf y}\rangle=\langle{\bf y},{\bf x}\rangle\;\mbox{for all ${\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}.$}

Conjugations play an important role in operator theory and were initially studied in
[
13
,
14
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. More recently, conjugations were explored in various settings and applications in
[
4
,

,

,

,

,

]
.

Example 2.2
.

Many types of conjugations were outlined in
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
. Below are a few basic ones that are relevant to this paper.

(a)

As discussed in the introduction, the mapping
Cf=\bar{f}
defines a conjugation on
L^{2}(\mu,X)
. In particular, the mapping
C[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots\;x_{n}]^{t}=[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\;\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}
defines a conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
. Throughout this paper we will use the notation
t
to represent the transpose of a matrix. In addition, vectors in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
will be viewed as column vectors since, for an
n\times n
matrix
A
of complex numbers, we will often consider the linear transformations on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by
{\bf x}\mapsto A{\bf x}
.

(b)

One can consider the conjugations

(Cf)(t)=\overline{f(t)}
and
(Cf)(t)=\overline{f(-t)}
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
These were used in
[
1
,
2
]
to study symmetric operators and their connections to physics.

(c)

If
u
is an inner function,
H^{2}
is the standard Hardy space, and
\mathcal{K}_{u}:=H^{2}\ominus uH^{2}
is the model space associated with
u
(considering everything as a subspace of
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
via radial boundary values), then
(Cf)(\xi)=u(\xi)\overline{\xi f(\xi)}
defines a conjugation on
\mathcal{K}_{u}

[
15
, Ch. 8]
.

This next lemma enables us to transfer a conjugation on one Hilbert space to a conjugation on another. The (easy) proof is left to the reader.

Lemma 2.3
.

Suppose
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{K}
are Hilbert spaces and
V:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{K}
is a unitary operator. If
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
then
VCV^{*}
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{K}
.

For a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}
and an
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we say that
A
is
C
-symmetric
if
CAC=A^{*}
. A multitude of operators from a variety of settings enjoy this property
[
17
,
18
,
21
,
22
]
. Here are a few examples that are relevant to this paper.

Example 2.4
.

(a)

If
\varphi\in L^{\infty}(\mu,X)
and
M_{\varphi}
denotes the multiplication operator
M_{\varphi}f=\varphi f
on
L^{2}(\mu,X)
, then the conjugation
C
from Example
2.2
(a) satisfies

CM_{\varphi}C=M_{\overline{\varphi}}=M_{\varphi}^{*}.

Thus,
M_{\varphi}
is
C
-symmetric.
For a normal operator
N\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})

N

on

for some finite positive Borel measure on some compact Hausdorff space

to see that

is a

-symmetric operator for some conjugation

\mathcal{H}
.

(b)

The conjugation

(Uf)(x)=f(x-1)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

-symmetric unitary operator.

(c)

The conjugation
(Cf)(t)=\overline{f(t)}
from Example
2.2
(b) makes the Fourier–Plancherel transform a

-symmetric unitary operator on

Though not used in this paper, but certainly in our follow up paper
[
28
]
, we recall the following result from
[

]
which shows that any unitary operator can be built from conjugations. We include a short proof (slightly different from the original) for the reader’s convenience.

Proposition 2.5
.

For each unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, there are conjugations
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
U=J_{1}J_{2}
. Moreover,
J_{1},J_{2}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
.

Proof.

For a given unitary
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, we can use the spectral theorem argument from Example
2.4
(a) (and also from the introduction) to produce a conjugation
J_{1}
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
J_{1}UJ_{1}=U^{*}
. Now observe that
J_{2}:=U^{*}J_{1}
is a conjugation,
J_{2}UJ_{2}=U^{*}
, and
J_{1}J_{2}=U
.
∎

The following result will be used from time to time in this paper.

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose
U,V,W
are unitary operators on
\mathcal{H}
such that
WUW^{*}=V
. Then
W\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)W^{*}=\mathscr{C}_{s}(V)
.

The following result from
[
20
, Lemma 3.2]
, which will be generalized below, gives an explicit description of all the conjugations on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proposition 2.7
.

A mapping
C
on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
is a conjugation if and only if
C=VJ
, where
V
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix with
V^{t}=V
and
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by
J[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots\;x_{n}]^{t}=[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\;\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}
, i.e.,

C[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots x_{n}]^{t}=V[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}.

Our discussion below needs a generalization of the previous result for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
to an abstract (separable) Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, but, of course, we need a substitute for
V^{t}
since the “transpose” of a linear operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
needs a proper definition (as opposed to the adjoint
V^{*}
which has a clear and well understood definition). For a fixed orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}=\{{\bf u}_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 1}
for
\mathcal{H}
and an
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
, we use the notation

(2.8)

[A]_{\mathscr{B}}=[a_{mn}]_{m,n\geqslant 1}=[\langle A{\bf u}_{n},{\bf u}_{m}\rangle]_{m,n\geqslant 1}

to denote the matrix representation of
A
with respect to
\mathscr{B}
, considered as a bounded operator on the sequence space

(2.9)

\ell^{2}_{+}:=\Big\{{\bf x}=[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots]^{t},x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}:\|{\bf x}\|_{\ell^{2}_{+}}=\Big(\sum_{j\geqslant 1}|x_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}<\infty\Big\}

by
{\bf x}\mapsto[A]_{\mathscr{B}}{\bf x}
.
For this fixed orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}
, we also define a conjugation
J_{\mathscr{B}}
on
\mathcal{H}
by

(2.10)

J_{\mathscr{B}}\Big(\sum_{j\geqslant 1}c_{j}{\bf u}_{j}\Big)=\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\overline{c_{j}}{\bf u}_{j}.

In other words,
J_{\mathscr{B}}
fixes every basis element
{\bf u}_{j}
and extends antilinearly to
\mathcal{H}
.
Here is a version of Proposition
2.7
for a general, possibly infinite dimensional, separable Hilbert space.

Proposition 2.11
.

For an orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}=\{{\bf u}_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 1}
for a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, the mapping
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
if and only if there is a unitary operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
such that
[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
and
C=V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
.

Proof.

Suppose
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
V=C\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
(since it is linear, isometric, and onto). Moreover, applying (
2.10
) and then (
2.1
), we see that

\displaystyle\langle V{\bf u}_{m},{\bf u}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C\!J_{\mathscr{B}}{\bf u}_{m},{\bf u}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C{\bf u}_{m},{\bf u}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C{\bf u}_{n},{\bf u}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle C\!J_{\mathscr{B}}{\bf u}_{n},{\bf u}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle V{\bf u}_{n},{\bf u}_{m}\rangle.

Thus
[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
and
C=V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
.

Conversely, if
V
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
with
[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
, define
C=V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
. Observe that
C
is antilinear and isometric. We just need to verify that
C^{2}=I
. Since
C
is antilinear, note that
C^{2}=V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
is a bounded
linear
operator on
\mathcal{H}
. Moreover, since
J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
is also a bounded linear operator on
\mathcal{H}
, we see that

(2.12)

[C^{2}]_{\mathscr{B}}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}[J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}]_{\mathscr{B}}.

Now observe that for all
m,n\geqslant 1
, again making use of (
2.1
) and (
2.10
),

\displaystyle\langle J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}{\bf u}_{m},{\bf u}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle J_{\mathscr{B}}V{\bf u}_{m},{\bf u}_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle J_{\mathscr{B}}{\bf u}_{n},V{\bf u}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle{\bf u}_{n},V{\bf u}_{m}\rangle

\displaystyle=\overline{\langle V{\bf u}_{m},{\bf u}_{n}\rangle}.

Thus, from our assumption that
[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
, we see that

(2.13)

[J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}]_{\mathscr{B}}=\overline{[V]_{\mathscr{B}}}=\overline{[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}}=[V^{*}]_{\mathscr{B}}.

From (
2.12
) it follows that
[C^{2}]_{\mathscr{B}}=[I]_{\mathscr{B}}
and so
C^{2}=I
. Thus,
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
.
∎

Remark 2.14
.

If
J
is
any
conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
, one can show there exists an orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}
for
\mathcal{H}
such that
J=J_{\mathscr{B}}

[
18
, Lemma 1]
. Thus, there is some freedom in the above analysis, if so desired, to choose a conjugation

first instead of the orthonormal basis

.

A version of the spectral theorem for unitary operators (see
[
7
, Ch. IX, Thm. 2.2]
or
[
33
, Ch. 1]
) says that if
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
, then there is a unique spectral measure
E(\cdot)
defined on the Borel subsets of
\sigma(U)
, the spectrum of
U
, such that

(2.15)

U=\int_{\sigma(U)}\xi dE(\xi).

Moreover, for any spectral measure
E(\cdot)
on
\mathbb{T}
(i.e.,
E(\cdot)
is a projection-valued function on the Borel subsets of
\mathbb{T}
such that
E(\mathbb{T})=I
and
E(\cdot)
is countably additive), there is a unique unitary operator
U
associated with
E(\cdot)
via (
2.15
).

For a spectral measure
E(\cdot)
and
{\bf x},{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}
, the function

\mu_{{\bf x},{\bf y}}(\cdot):=\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf y}\rangle

defines a finite complex Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
and, in particular, for each
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
,

(2.16)

\mu_{{\bf x}}:=\mu_{{\bf x},{\bf x}}

defines a positive finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
, sometimes called an
elementary measure
.

The following proposition from
[
24
]
describes the relationship between a unitary operator, its spectral measure, and a conjugation.

Proposition 2.17
.

For a conjugation
C
and a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
with associated spectral measure
E(\cdot)
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
;

(b)

CE(\Omega)C=E(\Omega)
for every Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\sigma(U)
.

3.
Decompositions of conjugations

As we will see in subsequent sections, the set
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
is quite large and so an important step in understanding it is to decompose each
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
into more manageable pieces. This decomposition will involve various types of invariant subspaces. Recall that a (closed) subspace
\mathcal{M}
of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is
invariant
for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
if
A\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
;
reducing
if
A\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
and
A^{*}\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
; and
hyperinvariant
if
T\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every
T\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
that commutes with
A
. We begin with the following lemma which follows from (
2.1
) and the fact that
C^{2}=I
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mathcal{M}
is a
C
-invariant subspace of
\mathcal{H}
, then
C\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{M}
and
C\mathcal{M}^{\perp}=\mathcal{M}^{\perp}
.

Below is a useful decomposition theorem for a conjugation. For the rest of this paper,
M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
will denote the set of all finite positive Borel measures on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}
. For
\mu,\sigma\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
use use the standard notation
\mu\ll\sigma
for
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to
\sigma
and
\mu\perp\sigma
for
\mu
is singular with respect to
\sigma
.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
,
E(\cdot)
be its associated spectral measure, and for each
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
, let
\mu_{{\bf x}}
denote the associated elementary measure from (
2.16
). For any
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, let

(3.3)

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}:=\{{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}:\mu_{{\bf x}}\ll\mu\}.

Then we have the following.

(a)

\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
is a reducing subspace for
U
.

(b)

If
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
, then

(i)

C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
and
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}
.

(ii)

C=C_{\mu}\oplus C_{\mu}^{\perp}
where
C_{\mu}:=C|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}
and
C_{\mu}^{\perp}:=C|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}}
.

(iii)

C_{\mu}(U|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}})C_{\mu}=U^{*}|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}}
and
C_{\mu}^{\perp}(U|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}})C_{\mu}^{\perp}=U^{*}|_{\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}}.

Proof.

The proof of (a) is routine and we omit it (see
[
25
, §65, §66]
for the details).

To prove (b), let
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
and
\Omega
be a Borel subset of
\sigma(U)
for which
\mu(\Omega)=0
.
By Proposition
2.17
,
C
commutes with
E(\Omega)
and thus, via (
2.1
),

\displaystyle\mu_{C{\bf x}}=\langle E(\Omega)C{\bf x},C{\bf x}\rangle=\langle CE(\Omega){\bf x},C{\bf x}\rangle=\langle{\bf x},E(\Omega){\bf x}\rangle=\mu_{{\bf x}}(\Omega)=0.

This proves that
C{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
. Since
C
is a conjugation, we see that
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
and
C\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}^{\perp}
(Lemma
3.1
). This proves (i) and (ii). Part (iii) follows from the facts that
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
is a reducing subspace for
U
and
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
.
∎

As a note here, other properties of the reducing subspace
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
were explored by Halmos in
[
25
, §65]
. Observe that if
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
and
\delta_{\alpha}
denotes the point mass at
\alpha

\mathcal{H}_{\delta_{\alpha}}=\ker(U-\alpha I).

Corollary 3.4
.

For a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, let
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
.
Suppose
\alpha\in\mathbb{T}
is an eigenvalue for
U
.
Then

C=C_{\delta_{\alpha}}\oplus C_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{\perp},
where
C_{\delta_{\alpha}}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U|_{\ker(U-\alpha I)})
and
C_{\delta_{\alpha}}^{\perp}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U|_{\ker(U-\alpha I)^{\perp}})
.

The following corollary will play an important role later on.

Corollary 3.5
.

If
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
with

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 1}\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{j}},\quad\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{j}}:=\ker(U-\xi_{j}I),

then

(3.6)

C=\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 1}C|_{\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{j}}}

and

(3.7)

C|_{\mathscr{E}_{\xi_{j}}}U|_{\mathscr{E}_{\xi_{j}}}C|_{\mathscr{E}_{\xi_{j}}}=U^{*}|_{\mathscr{E}_{\xi_{j}}}\;\mbox{for all $j\geqslant 1$}.

Remark 3.8
.

Apply Theorem
3.2
to the case when
\mu=m
(normalized Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
) and let
\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{ac}}:=\mathcal{H}_{m}
and
\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{sing}}:=\mathcal{H}_{m}^{\perp}
. The decomposition
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{ac}}\oplus\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{sing}}
was first explored in
[

]
and considered further in
[

,
29
,
30
]
. One can prove the following: Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and let

.
If

is the Lebesgue decomposition from above, then

and

.

A von Neumann–Wold type decomposition surveyed in
[

]
yields the following decomposition of a unitary operator

on

by reducing subspaces

,
\mathcal{L}
, and

such that

\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\operatorname{ac}}\bigoplus\mathcal{L}\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\mathbb{T}}\ker(U-\alpha I).

Here one proves the following:
Let

and

Though Theorem
3.2
seems relatively simple, it yields interesting results about hyperinvariant subspaces of unitary operators.
For a unitary operator, every hyperinvariant subspace is also reducing (but not the converse). Theorem
3.11
below connects hyperinvariant subspaces with conjugations. The culmination of this discussion will be given in Theorems
8.8
and
8.10
.

Theorem 3.10
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
with spectral measure
E(\cdot)
. Then every hyperinvariant subspace for
U
is invariant for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
.
Moreover, every hyperinvariant subspaces can be written as
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
, where
\Omega
is a Borel subset of
\sigma(U)
.

Proof.

From
[
33
, Proposition 6.9]
(see also
[
8
]
), every hyperinvariant subspace for
U
can be written as
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
for some Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\sigma(U)
. Since
C
commutes with
E(\cdot)
(Proposition
2.17
(b)),
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
is invariant for
C
.
∎

We now connect
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
with hyperinvariant subspaces. A first step in this direction is this following result – which might be contained somewhere in the literature but we include a proof for the reader’s convenience. We will see an extension of this result in Theorems
8.8
and
8.10
below.

Theorem 3.11
.

If
\mathcal{M}
is a hyperinvariant subspace of a unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
, then there is a
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
for which
\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
.

Proof.

From Theorem
3.10
, every hyperinvarient subspace
\mathcal{M}
of
U
, with associated spectral measure
E(\cdot)
, takes the form
\mathcal{M}=E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
for some Borel subset
\Omega\subseteq\sigma(U)
. Since
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
is also reducing, then
V:=U|_{E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}}
is a unitary operator whose spectral measure is
E(\cdot)E(\Omega)
.

Let
\mathcal{W}^{*}(V)
denote the von Neumann algebra generated by
V
(i.e., the smallest
\ast
-closed and strongly closed algebra containing
V
). Using
[
7
, Ch. IX, Cor. 7.9]
one can produce a
separating vector

{\bf e}\in\mathcal{H}
for
\mathcal{W}^{*}(V)
in that if
A\in\mathcal{W}^{*}(V)
satisfies
A{\bf e}={\bf 0}
, then
A\equiv 0
. By
[
7
, Ch. IX, Prop. 8.3]
, the corresponding elementary measure
\mu_{{\bf e}}
from (
2.16
) is a
scalar-valued spectral measure
for
V
in that
\mu_{{\bf e}}(\Delta)=0
if and only if
E(\Delta)E(\Omega)=E(\Delta\cap\Omega)=0
.

To complete the proof, we will show that
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{{\bf e}}}
. For the
\subseteq
containment, observe that if
{\bf x}\in E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
and
\Delta
is a Borel subset of
\Omega
, then

(3.12)

\mu_{{\bf x}}(\Delta)=\langle E(\Delta){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle=\|E(\Delta){\bf x}\|^{2}

and thus, since
\mu_{{\bf e}}
is a scalar valued spectral measure for
V=U|_{E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}}
, we see that if
\mu_{{\bf e}}(\Delta)=0
, then
E(\Delta)=0
. Thus, by (
3.12
),
\mu_{{\bf x}}\ll\mu_{{\bf e}}
and so
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{{\bf e}}}
. This verifies
E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{{\bf e}}}
.

For the
\supseteq
containment, let
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{{\bf e}}}
. Then
\mu_{{\bf y}}\ll\mu_{{\bf e}}
and so
\mu_{{\bf y}}(\Delta)=0
whenever
\mu_{{\bf e}}(\Delta)=0
. We want to show that
{\bf y}\in E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
. Since
\mu_{{\bf e}}
is a scalar valued spectral measure for
V
, we see that
\mu_{{\bf e}}(\sigma(U)\setminus\Omega)=0
. Using the assumption that
\mu_{{\bf y}}\ll\mu_{{\bf e}}
, we obtain

\mu_{{\bf y}}(\sigma(U)\setminus\Omega)=\|E(\sigma(U)\setminus\Omega){\bf y}\|^{2}=0.

Thus, since
\mathcal{H}=(E(\Omega)\mathcal{H})\oplus(E(\sigma(U)\setminus\Omega)\mathcal{H})
, it follows that
{\bf y}\in E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
. This verifies
\mathcal{H}_{\mu_{{\bf e}}}\subseteq E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
.
∎

We end this section with a discussion of when
\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{1}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{2}}
for
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. Observe that if
\nu
is a scalar spectral measure for
U
(i.e.,
\nu(\Delta)=0
if and only if
E(\Delta)=0
) then
\mathcal{H}_{\nu}=\mathcal{H}
. Next let

[{\bf x}]_{U,U^{*}}:=\bigvee\{U^{n}{\bf x}:n\in\mathbb{Z}\},

where
\bigvee
denotes the closed linear span,
denote the
\ast
-cyclic invariant subspace generated by
{\bf x}
.
For any
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
the condition
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
implies that
[{\bf x}]_{U,U^{*}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
(since
\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
is reducing).

Recall
[
25
, §48]
the standard Boolean operations
\wedge
and
\vee
for
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
defined on Borel subsets
\Omega
of
\mathbb{T}
by

(\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})(\Omega)=\mu_{1}(\Omega)+\mu_{2}(\Omega);

(\mu_{1}\wedge\mu_{2})(\Omega)=\inf\{\mu_{1}(\Omega\cap A)+\mu_{2}(\Omega\setminus A):\mbox{$A$ is a Borel set}\}.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mu
be any scalar spectral measure for
U
. For
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{1}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{2}};

(b)

\nu_{1}\wedge\mu\ll\nu_{2}\wedge\mu.

Proof.

Assume condition (b) and let
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{1}}
. Then
\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle\ll\nu_{1}
. Since
\mu
is a scalar spectral measure for
U
, we see that
\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle\ll\mu
. Hence
\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle\ll\nu_{1}\wedge\mu\ll\nu_{2}\wedge\mu
. Thus
\langle E(\cdot){\bf x},{\bf x}\rangle\ll\nu_{2}

and so
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{2}}
. Thus,
(b)\Longrightarrow(a)
.

For the proof of
(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
, let us assume that
\nu_{1}\wedge\mu
is not absolutely continuous with respect to
\nu_{2}\wedge\mu
. Then there is a nonzero measure
\nu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
such that
\nu\ll\nu_{1}\wedge\mu
and
\nu\perp\nu_{2}\wedge\mu
. Let
{\bf e}\in\mathcal{H}
be a vector such that
\mu_{{\bf e}}
is a scalar spectral measure (for example a separating vector for von Neumann algebra generated by
U
– from the proof of Theorem
3.11
). Since
\mu
and
\mu_{{\bf e}}
are mutually absolutely continuous as scalar spectral measures for
U
, it follows that
\nu\ll\mu_{{\bf e}}
. By
[
7
, Ch.IX, Lemma 8.6]
there is a nonzero
{\bf y}\in[{\bf x}]_{U,U^{*}}

such that
\mu_{{\bf y}}=\nu
. Therefore,
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{1}}
. On the other hand if
{\bf y}\in\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{2}}
then
\mu_{{\bf y}}=\nu\ll\nu_{2}\wedge\mu
, which is a contradiction.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. If
\nu_{1}\ll\nu_{2}
then
\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{1}}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{2}}
.

Corollary 3.15
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
\mu
be any scalar spectral measure for
U
. For
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{1}}=\mathcal{H}_{\nu_{2}}
;

(b)

\nu_{1}\wedge\mu
and
\nu_{2}\wedge\mu
are mutually absolutely continuous.

4.
A matrix description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)

Our first description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
will involve matrix a representation of
U
with respect to an orthonormal basis. Though this might seem a bit difficult to apply at first, we will see, through our decomposition theorem from the previous section, that it can often yield a tangible description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
. Recall our notation from §
2
, where, for a fixed orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}=\{{\bf u}_{j}\}_{j\geqslant 1}
for a (separable) Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and for
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
,
[A]_{\mathscr{B}}
denotes the matrix representation of
A
with respect to
\mathscr{B}
, and
J_{\mathscr{B}}
denotes the conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
for which
J_{\mathscr{B}}{\bf u}_{n}={\bf u}_{n}
for all
n\geqslant 1
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Suppose
U
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
. Then
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
if and only if there is a unitary operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}
satisfying

(a)

[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
;

(b)

[V]_{\mathscr{B}}[U]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[U]_{\mathscr{B}}[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
;

(c)

C=V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
.

Proof.

Proposition
2.11
says that
C
is a conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
if and only if
C=V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}
for some unitary
V
on
\mathcal{H}
with
[V]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[V]_{\mathscr{B}}
. Now, to fulfill the requirement that
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
, observe that

\displaystyle CU^{*}C=U

\displaystyle\iff(V\!J_{\mathscr{B}})U^{*}(V\!J_{\mathscr{B}})=U

\displaystyle\iff V(J_{\mathscr{B}}U^{*}\!J_{\mathscr{B}})(J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}})=U

\displaystyle\iff[V]_{\mathscr{B}}[J_{\mathscr{B}}U^{*}\!J_{\mathscr{B}}]_{\mathscr{B}}[J_{\mathscr{B}}V\!J_{\mathscr{B}}]_{\mathscr{B}}=[U]_{\mathscr{B}}

\displaystyle\iff[V]_{\mathscr{B}}[U]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}[V^{*}]_{\mathscr{B}}=[U]_{\mathscr{B}}.

Notice the use of (
2.13
) above. Thus,

CU^{*}C=U\iff[V]_{\mathscr{B}}[U]_{\mathscr{B}}^{t}=[U]_{\mathscr{B}}[V]_{\mathscr{B}},

which completes the proof.
∎

One can use the above discussion to describe
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
when
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix.

Theorem 4.2
.

Suppose
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix with spectral decomposition

U=W\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}I_{n_{1}}&&&\\
&\xi_{2}I_{n_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\xi_{d}I_{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix}W^{*},

where
W
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix,
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{d}\in\mathbb{T}
are the distinct eigenvalues of
U
,
I_{n_{j}}
is the
n_{j}\times n_{j}
identity matrix, and
n_{1}+n_{2}+\cdots+n_{d}=n
. Then
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
if and only if

(4.3)

C=W\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}JW^{*},

where, for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
,
V_{j}
is an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
unitary matrix which satisfies
V_{j}^{t}=V_{j}
, and
J
is the conjugation on
\mathbb{C}^{n}
defined by

J[x_{1}\;x_{2}\;\cdots\;x_{n}]^{t}=[\overline{x_{1}}\;\overline{x_{2}}\;\cdots\;\overline{x_{n}}]^{t}.

Proof.

Suppose that
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
.
For the unitary matrix

\widetilde{U}=\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}I_{n_{1}}&&&\\
&\xi_{2}I_{n_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\xi_{d}I_{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix},

Corollary
3.5
says that any
\widetilde{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\widetilde{U})
can be written as

\widetilde{C}=\widetilde{C}|_{\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{1}}}\oplus\widetilde{C}|_{\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{2}}}\oplus\cdots\oplus\widetilde{C}|_{\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{d}}},

where

\mathscr{E}_{j}=\ker(\widetilde{U}-\xi_{j}I)

(which is the span of appropriate standard basis vectors for
\mathbb{C}^{n}
)
and

\widetilde{C}_{j}:=\widetilde{C}|_{\mathcal{E}_{\xi}}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\widetilde{U}|_{\mathscr{E}_{\xi_{j}}})\;\mbox{for all $1\leqslant j\leqslant d$}.

Theorem
4.1
says that for each
1\leqslant j\leqslant d
there is an
n_{j}\times n_{j}
unitary matrix
V_{j}
such that such that
\widetilde{C}_{j}=V_{j}J|_{\mathscr{E}_{j}}
and
V_{j}^{t}=V_{j}
. Note that
J|_{\mathscr{E}_{j}}
fixes the appropriate standard basis vectors. Since
\widetilde{U}|_{\mathcal{E}_{\xi_{j}}}=\xi_{j}I_{n_{j}}
, condition (b) of Theorem
4.1
is automatic. Thus, every
\widetilde{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\widetilde{U})
takes the form

\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}J.

Now apply Lemma
2.3
and Proposition
2.6
to see that
C
takes the form in (
4.3
).

Conversely suppose that
C
is the conjugation from (
4.3
). Then, due to the fact that each of the unitary matrices
V_{j}
satisfy
V_{j}^{t}=V_{j}
and
JAJ=\overline{A}
for any matrix
A
,
CUC
is equal to

\displaystyle W\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}J\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}I_{n_{1}}&&&\\
&\xi_{2}I_{n_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\xi_{d}I_{n_{d}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}JW^{*}

\displaystyle=W\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}J\begin{bmatrix}\xi_{1}V_{1}&&&\\
&\xi_{2}V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\xi_{d}V_{d}\end{bmatrix}JW^{*}

\displaystyle=W\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{2}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\xi_{1}}\overline{V_{1}}&&&\\
&\overline{\xi_{2}}\overline{V_{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\overline{\xi_{d}}\overline{V_{d}}\end{bmatrix}W^{*}

\displaystyle=W\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\

&&\ddots&\\
&&&V_{d}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\xi_{1}}V_{1}^{*}&&&\\
&\overline{\xi_{2}}V_{2}^{*}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\

\displaystyle=W\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\xi_{1}}I_{n_{1}}&&&\\

&&\ddots&\\
&&&\overline{\xi_{d}}I_{d_{d}}\end{bmatrix}W^{*}=U^{*}.

Thus
C\in\mathscr{C}_{c}(U)
, which completes the proof.
∎

In certain circumstances, we can extend Theorem

to the infinite dimensional setting. We mention two interesting examples of unitary operators from harmonic analysis.

Example 4.4
.

Let

(\mathcal{F}f)(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(t)e^{-ixt}dt,

denote the classical Fourier–Plancherel transform on

(defined initially on
L^{1}(\mathbb{R})\cap L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
by the above integral and extended to be a unitary operator on

). It is known that

and
\mathcal{F}H_{n}=(-i)^{n}H_{n}
,
where

is the
n

H_{n}(x)=c_{n}e^{-x^{2}/2}h_{n}(x),\;\;n\geqslant 0,

c_{n}
is a constant for which
\|H_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=1
, and
h_{n}
is the
n
-th Hermite polynomial.
Moreover,
\mathscr{B}=\{H_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
forms an orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})

[
16
, Ch.11]
.
Thus, using our earlier notation from Corollary
3.5
,

(4.5)

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{E}_{1}\bigoplus\mathcal{E}_{-i}\bigoplus\mathcal{E}_{-1}\bigoplus\mathcal{E}_{i},

where

\mathscr{B}_{1}=\{H_{4k}\}_{k\geqslant 0},

\mathscr{B}_{-i}=\{H_{4k+1}\}_{k\geqslant 0},

\mathscr{B}_{-1}=\{H_{4k+2}\}_{k\geqslant 0},

\mathscr{B}_{i}=\{H_{4k+3}\}_{k\geqslant 0},
are orthonormal bases for
\mathcal{E}_{1},\mathcal{E}_{-i},\mathcal{E}_{-},\mathcal{E}_{-i}
respectively, we can write
\mathcal{F}
in block operator form with respect to the decomposition in (

) as

\mathcal{F}=\begin{bmatrix}I|_{\mathcal{E}_{1}}&&&\\
&-iI|_{\mathcal{E}_{-i}}&&\\
&&-I|_{\mathcal{E}_{-1}}&\\
&&&iI|_{\mathcal{E}_{i}}\end{bmatrix}.

Furthermore, any conjugation
C
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
for which
C\mathcal{F}C=\mathcal{F}^{*}
must take the (block) form

C=\begin{bmatrix}V_{1}&&&\\
&V_{-i}&&\\
&&V_{-1}&\\
&&&V_{i}\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}J_{1}&&&\\
&J_{-i}&&\\
&&J_{-1}&\\
&&&J_{i}\\
\end{bmatrix},

where, for each
k\in\{1,-i,-1,i\}
,
J_{k}
is the conjugation on
\mathcal{E}_{k}
which fixes every element of
\mathscr{B}_{k}
and
V_{k}
is a unitary operator on
\mathcal{E}_{k}
for which
[V_{k}]_{\mathscr{B}_{k}}^{t}=[V_{k}]_{\mathscr{B}_{k}}
.

Example 4.6
.

Suppose

(\mathscr{H}g)(x)=\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{PV}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{g(t)}{x-t}dt,

is the Hilbert transform on

. Then
\sigma(\mathscr{H})=\sigma_{p}(\mathscr{H})=\{i,-i\}
and, again using the notation from Corollary
3.5
,

L^{2}(\mathbb{R})=\mathcal{E}_{i}\bigoplus\mathcal{E}_{-i}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{E}_{i}
has orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}_{i}=\{f_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
, where

f_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi}}\frac{(x+i)^{n-1}}{(x-i)^{n}},

and
\mathcal{E}_{-i}
has orthonormal basis
\mathscr{B}_{-i}=\{g_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
, where

See
[

, Ch. 12]
for the details.
Moreover, by a similar discussion as in the previous example, any conjugation

where for each

,

.

5.
Natural conjugations on vector valued

This section provides a model for conjugations on vector valued Lebesgue spaces. It will be useful for our description of

6.
Conjugations and bilateral shifts

Many interesting, and naturally occurring, unitary operators are bilateral shifts. Examples include the Hilbert and Fourier transforms on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; the translation operator
(Uf)(x)=f(x-1)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; the dilation operator
(Uf)(x)=\sqrt{2}f(2x)
on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
; and the special class of multiplication operators
Uf=\psi f
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
(where
\psi
is an inner function) which will be the focus of the next section. The fact that the above operators are bilateral shifts were carefully explained in
[
29
]
. This section gives an initial description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
for this class of operators. Another description will be discussed in the next section. We begin with precisely what we mean by the term “bilateral shift”.

Definition 6.1
.

A unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}
is a
bilateral shift
if there is a subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
for which

(a)

U^{n}\mathcal{M}\perp\mathcal{M}
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
;

(b)

{\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{n}\mathcal{M}}
.

In the above, note that
U^{-1}=U^{*}
. The subspace
\mathcal{M}
is called an
associated wandering subspace
for the bilateral shift
U
. Of course there is
the
bilateral shift
M_{\xi}
on
L^{2}(m,\mathbb{T})
defined by
(M_{\xi}f)(\xi)=\xi f(\xi)
where the wandering subspace
\mathcal{M}
is the space of constant functions.

Though the wandering subspace
\mathcal{M}
in Definition
6.1
is not unique, its dimension is
[
26
]
. The term “bilateral shift ” comes from the fact that since

(6.2)

\mathcal{H}=\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\,U^{n}\mathcal{M},

every
{\bf x}\in\mathcal{H}
can be uniquely represented as

{\bf x}=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{n}{\bf x}_{n},\;\mbox{where ${\bf x}_{n}\in\mathcal{M}$ for all $n\in\mathbb{Z}$}.

This allows us to define a natural unitary operator

(6.3)

W:\mathcal{H}\to\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M}),\quad W\Big(\bigoplus_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}U^{n}{\bf x}_{n}\Big)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{n}\xi^{n}.

Moreover,

WUW^{*}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi},

where

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
is the bilateral shift from (
5.2
) defined on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M})
by

(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi),\quad\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}{\bf x}_{n}\xi^{n}\in\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\mathcal{M}).

Example 6.4
.

Examples of bilateral shifts come from a variety of places. These operators were explored in

(a)

If

on

and

Remark 7.1
.

For an inner function
\psi
, a known result (see for example

where
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}=H^{2}\ominus\psi H^{2}
is the model space associated with

\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
is a wandering subspace for

(recall Definition

is a finite Blaschke product with

[

\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}=L^{2}\oplus L^{2}\oplus\cdots\oplus L^{2}.

The norm of an element

\|\emph{\textbf{f}}\|=\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\|f_{j}\|^{2}_{L^{2}}\Big)^{1/2}.

When

is finite.
Furthermore, the operator
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
(called the

) is given by

\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}\Big)\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=[\xi f_{j}(\xi)]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}.

We also define

\ell^{2}_{N}:=\Big\{{\bf x}=[x_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N},x_{j}\in\mathbb{C}:\|{\bf x}\|_{\ell^{2}_{N}}=\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}|x_{j}|^{2}\Big)^{1/2}<\infty\Big\}.

When

is equal to

which was the space discussed earlier in (

Theorem 7.4
.

For an inner function
\psi

(a)

Every

has the unique decomposition

(7.5)

f=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(f_{j}\circ\psi),

where each
f_{j}
belongs to
L^{2}
and

\|f\|=(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\|f_{j}\|^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}
.

(b)

The operator

W:L^{2}\to\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2},\quad Wf=[f_{1}\;f_{2}\;f_{3}\;\ldots]^{t},

is unitary and

W^{*}[k_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(k_{j}\circ\psi).

(c)

WM_{\psi}W^{*}=\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
and
WM_{\overline{\psi}}W^{*}=\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\bar{\xi}}
.

(d)

If
A\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2})
satisfies
AM_{\psi}=M_{\psi}A
, then

Af=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}(f_{j}\circ\psi)\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{kj}\circ\psi),

where
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}_{N}))
.

(e)

If
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\mathcal{B}(\ell^{2}_{N}))
, then the operator
A
defined in (d) is unitarily equivalent to
M_{\Phi}
on
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
, i.e.,

[f_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\mapsto\Phi[f_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}.

Proof.

By (
7.2
),
every
f\in L^{2}
can be written (uniquely) as

(7.6)

f=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\psi^{j}\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}a_{jk}h_{k}.

Moreover, since
\psi^{j}K_{\psi}\perp\psi^{j^{\prime}}K_{\psi}
for all
j\not=j^{\prime}
and
\{h_{k}\}_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}
forms an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
, we have

(7.7)

\|f\|^{2}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}|a_{jk}|^{2}<\infty.

Rewrite the expression in (
7.6
) as

f=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{jk}\psi^{j}.

By (
7.7
) the function

f_{k}=\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}a_{jk}z^{j}
belongs to
L^{2}
and thus,

f=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(f_{k}\circ\psi).

Finally, by Parseval’s theorem and (
7.7
), note that

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}\|f_{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}|a_{jk}|^{2}=\|f\|^{2}.

This verifies statements (a) and (b). To verify statement (c), note that

\displaystyle WM_{\psi}W^{*}[k_{j}]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}

\displaystyle=WM_{\psi}\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(k_{j}\circ\psi)

\displaystyle=W\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot\psi\cdot(k_{j}\circ\psi)

\displaystyle=W\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot((\xi k_{j})\circ\psi)

\displaystyle=[\xi k_{j}(\xi)]^{t}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}.

This shows that
WM_{\psi}W^{*}=\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
. Similarly, one can verify the second equality in (c).

To prove (d) and (e), recall from
[
33
, Chapter III]
that the bounded operators on the space
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
that commute with
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
must take the form of multiplication by the matrix function
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
.
Putting this all together, we see that if
A\in\mathcal{B}(L^{2})
commutes with
M_{\psi}
, then
WAW^{*}
commutes with
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
and so
WAW^{*}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi},

equivalently
A=W^{*}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}W
.
This translates to an operator on
L^{2}
by

\displaystyle Af

\displaystyle=W^{*}\Phi Wf

\displaystyle=W^{*}[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}[f_{1}\;f_{2}\;\ldots]^{t}

\displaystyle=W^{*}\Big[\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{1j}f_{j}\;\;\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{2j}f_{j}\;\;\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{3j}f_{j}\;\;\ldots\Big]^{t}

\displaystyle=h_{1}\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{1j}f_{j}\Big)\circ\psi+h_{2}\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{2j}f_{j}\Big)\circ\psi+\ldots

\displaystyle=(f_{1}\circ\psi)\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{k1}\circ\psi)\Big)+(f_{2}\circ\psi)\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{k2}\circ\psi)\Big)+\cdots,

which completes the proof of (d) and (e).
∎

From our earlier discussion, we know that the standard conjugation
Jf=\bar{f}
on
L^{2}
induces the standard conjugation on
J
on
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
by

{\emph{\textbf{J}}}{\bf F}=[\bar{f}_{1}\;\bar{f}_{2}\;\ldots]=\overline{{\bf F}}

Here is our description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
when

is inner.

Theorem 7.8
.

Suppose that
\psi
is an inner function and
\{h_{j}\}_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}
is an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{K}_{\psi}
. Then
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
if and only if there is a
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
such that

(a)

\Phi^{*}\Phi=I
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
;

(b)

\Phi^{t}=\Phi
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
;

(c)

{\displaystyle Cf=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}(\bar{f}_{j}\circ\psi)\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{k,j}\circ\psi),}

for all
f\in L^{2}
with the decomposition from (
7.5
).

Proof.

Suppose
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
. Note that
\widetilde{C}:=WCW^{*}
defines a conjugation on
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
. Since
CM_{\psi}C=M_{\overline{\psi}}
, we can use
Theorem
7.4
(c) to see that

\widetilde{C}\Big(\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}\Big)\widetilde{C}=\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\bar{\xi}}.

Since
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}M_{\xi}
on
\bigoplus_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}L^{2}
is unitary equivalent to
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
on
\mathscr{L}^{2}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
(recall (
7.3
)), Theorem
6.5
yields (with the choice of conjugation
J
on
\ell^{2}_{N}
being
J{\bf x}=\overline{{\bf x}}
), a matrix-valued function
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
that is unitary valued
m
-almost everywhere, i.e.,
\Phi\Phi^{*}=\Phi^{*}\Phi=I
, and such that
\widetilde{C}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}}
. Moreover, since
\widetilde{C}^{2}=I
we see that

I=(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}})(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}})=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}(\emph{\textbf{J}}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}})=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\overline{\Phi}}

and so

\overline{\Phi}=\Phi^{*}
. Combine this with the previous identity to see that
\Phi^{t}=\Phi
. So far we have shown that if
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
then
C=W^{*}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}})W
where
\Phi
satisfies the conditions of (a) and (b). If
\Phi
satisfies the conditions (a) and (b) then a short argument will show that
\Phi
is unitary valued almost everywhere and the condition (b) will show that
J\Phi J=\Phi^{*}
almost everywhere. Proposition
5.6
now says that
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
and hence
W^{*}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}})W\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
. So we have shown that
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
if and only if
C=W^{*}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}})W
, where
\Phi
satisfies the conditions in (a) and (b).

It remains to verify the formula in (c). Observe that for all
f=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}h_{j}\cdot(f_{j}\circ\psi)\in L^{2}
,

\displaystyle Cf

\displaystyle=W^{*}\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}\emph{\textbf{J}}Wf

\displaystyle=W^{*}[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}[\bar{f}_{1}\;\bar{f}_{2}\;\ldots]^{t}

\displaystyle=W^{*}\Big[\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{1j}\bar{f}_{j}\;\;\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{2j}\bar{f}_{j}\;\;\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{3j}\bar{f}_{j}\;\;\ldots\Big]^{t}

\displaystyle=h_{1}\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{1j}\bar{f}_{j}\Big)\circ\psi+h_{2}\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant N}\varphi_{2j}\bar{f}_{j}\Big)\circ\psi+\ldots

\displaystyle=(\bar{f}_{1}\circ\psi)\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{k1}\circ\psi)\Big)+(\bar{f}_{2}\circ\psi)\cdot\Big(\sum_{1\leqslant k\leqslant N}h_{k}\cdot(\varphi_{k2}\circ\psi)\Big)+\cdots.

Conversely, suppose there is a
\Phi=[\varphi_{ij}]_{1\leqslant i,j\leqslant N}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(m,\ell^{2}_{N})
such that conditions (a), (b), and (c) hold. Theorem
7.4
(e) and the argument above shows that
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(M_{\psi})
.
∎

Remark 7.9
.

.

becomes

on

. Furthermore,

(the constant functions) and the expansion of an

from Theorem

says that every

must take the form

Example 7.11
.

When

above, one can check that the functions

,

Then, one can check that

says that every

\mathbb{T}

\varphi_{12}(\xi)=\varphi_{21}(\xi)

\begin{bmatrix}\overline{\varphi_{11}(\xi)}&\overline{\varphi_{21}(\xi)}\\
\overline{\varphi_{21}(\xi)}&\overline{\varphi_{22}(\xi)}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\varphi_{11}(\xi)&\varphi_{21}(\xi)\\

Conjugations via the spectral theorem

This section describes
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
using the multiplicity version of the spectral theorem. Important applications of this are Theorem
8.8
and Theorem

below which connect the invariant subspaces of

.

We begin with the following (multiplicity) version of the spectral theorem for unitary operators from
[
7
, Ch. IX, Theorem 10.20]
. The reader might need a refresher of the notation from §
5
.

Theorem 8.1

(Spectral Theorem)
.

For a unitary operator
U
on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, there are mutually singular measures

\mu_{\infty},\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
, along with Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{\infty},\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2},\ldots
each with corresponding
\operatorname{dim}\mathcal{H}_{k}=k
,
k=\infty,1,2,3,\dots
, and an isometric isomorphism

\mathcal{I}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{\infty},\mathcal{H}_{\infty})\oplus\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})\oplus\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{2},\mathcal{H}_{2})\oplus\cdots

such that

\mathcal{I}U\mathcal{I}^{*}
is equal to the unitary operator

(8.2)

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(\infty)}\oplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(1)}\oplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(2)}\oplus\cdots,

where for
i=\infty,1,2,3,\ldots
,

\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}:\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})\to\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i}),\quad\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi)=\xi\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi).

The main driver of the results of this section is the following.

Theorem 8.3
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with spectral representation as in Theorem
8.1
. For a conjugation
C
on
\mathcal{H}

(a)

,

(b)

For each

on

such that

(c)

For each

there are
\mathbf{C}_{j}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{j},\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{j}))
such that
\mathbf{C}_{j}(\xi)
is a conjugation for
\mu_{j}

on
\mathcal{H}_{i}

Proof.

(a)\Longrightarrow(d)
: Set

(8.4)

\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}:=\mathcal{I}U\mathcal{I}^{*}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(\infty)}\oplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(1)}\oplus\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(2)}\oplus\cdots,

and, for a conjugation
C

\mathcal{H}

which, by Lemma

Assuming that
CUC=U^{*}

\widetilde{\!\mathbf{C}}\,\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}\,\widetilde{\!\mathbf{C}}=\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\bar{\xi}}.

Now observe that

\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}(\widetilde{\!\mathbf{C}}\,\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}})=(\widetilde{\!\mathbf{C}}\,\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\bar{\xi}})\,\widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}}=(\widetilde{\!\mathbf{C}}\ \widetilde{\!\emph{\textbf{J}}})\widetilde{\emph{\textbf{M}}}_{\xi}.

This says that the operator

The spectral theorem applied to

(a)\Longrightarrow(d)
.

(d)\Longrightarrow(c)

(c)\implies(b)

we have

\displaystyle(\emph{\textbf{A}}_{\mathbf{C}_{i}}\emph{\textbf{M}}^{(i)}_{\xi}\emph{\textbf{f}}_{i})(\xi)

Theorem 8.6

Let

. Then following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathfrak{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
.

(b)

There are
\mathbf{C}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathscr{A}\!\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
such that
\mathbf{C}(\xi)
is a conjugation for
\mu
almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
and

\mathfrak{C}=\emph{\textbf{A}}_{\mathbf{C}}
.

(c)

For any conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
there is a
\mathbf{U}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
such that
\mathbf{U}(\xi)
is unitary and
J
–symmetric for
\mu
almost every
\xi\in\mathbb{T}
and
\mathfrak{C}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\mathbf{U}}\emph{\textbf{J}}
.

As an application of the above, we have the following connection between conjugations and hyperinvariant subspaces.

Proposition 8.7
.

Let
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
. If
\mathcal{K}\subseteq\mathscr{L}^{2}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
is an invariant subspace for every
\mathfrak{C}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
, then
\mathcal{K}
is hyperinvariant for
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
.

Proof.

For any fixed conjugation
J
on
\mathcal{H}
, (
5.4
) says that
\emph{\textbf{J}}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi})
and thus
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for
J
. By Theorem
8.6
,
\mathcal{K}
is also invariant for all of the conjugations
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{u}\emph{\textbf{J}}
, where
u\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is unimodular
\mu
-almost everywhere. Therefore,
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for every
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{u}=\emph{\textbf{M}}_{u}\emph{\textbf{J}}\emph{\textbf{J}}
, where
u\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
is unimodular. From here, one can argue that
\mathcal{K}
is also invariant for
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\chi_{\Omega}}
for any Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\sigma(U)
(indeed let
u=1
on
\Omega
and
-1
on
\mathbb{T}\setminus\Omega
and note that
\chi_{\Omega}=(1+u)/2
) and, consequently, for any
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{v}
where
v\in L^{\infty}(\mu)
.

Now fix any unitary
U_{0}
on
\mathcal{H}
and let
{\mathbf{U}}_{0}\equiv U_{0}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B(\mathcal{H})})
denote the operator-valued constant function. By our discussion in the introduction (see also Proposition
2.5
),
U_{0}
is
J_{0}
–symmetric for some conjugation
J_{0}
. Let
\emph{\textbf{J}}_{0}
be the conjugation on
\mathscr{L}(\mu,\mathcal{H})
given by
(\emph{\textbf{J}}_{0}\emph{\textbf{f}})(\xi)=J_{0}(\emph{\textbf{f}}(\xi))
. Therefore,
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for
\emph{\textbf{J}}_{0}
and for
\mathbf{U}_{0}\emph{\textbf{J}}_{0}
and thus for
{\mathbf{U}}_{0}
.
Similarly,
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for
\mathbf{U}_{0}^{*}
. It follows that
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for the von Neumann algebra containing all constant unitary valued functions in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Finally,
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for the von Neumann algebra generated by
\{\emph{\textbf{M}}_{v}:v\in L^{\infty}(\mu)\}
and the constant operator-valued functions from
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
. From here, one can fashion an argument that
\mathcal{K}
is invariant for every element of
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
. Since
\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
forms the class of operators
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\Phi}
,
\Phi\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu,\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}))
, that commute with
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
,
\mathcal{K}
is hyperinvariant for
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
.
∎

From here, we can state our main connection between conjugations and hyperinvariant subspaces.

Theorem 8.8
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
If
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
is an invariant subspace for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
, then
\mathcal{M}
is hyperinvariant for
U
.

Proof.

The notation from Theorem
8.3
proves that the invariance of
\mathcal{M}
for all
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
implies that

\mathcal{I}\mathcal{M}=\bigoplus\mathcal{K}_{i}
, where the subspace
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is invariant for all all
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\mathbf{U}_{i}}\emph{\textbf{J}}_{i}
, where
\mathbf{U}_{i}\in\mathscr{L}^{\infty}(\mu_{i},\mathcal{H}_{i})
is unitary valued
\mu
-almost everywhere. Now apply Proposition
8.7
to see that
\mathcal{K}_{i}
is hyperinvariant for each
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}^{(i)}
. Finally, using the description of the commutant of
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}

[
7
, p. 307, Theorem IX.10.20]
, we obtain that
\mathcal{I}\mathcal{M}
is hyperinvariant for
\emph{\textbf{M}}_{\xi}
. This shows that
\mathcal{M}
is a hyperinvariant subspace for
U
.
∎

The simple example bellow illustrates why requirement in Theorem
8.8
that
\mathcal{M}
is invariant for
every

C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
is an important one.

Example 8.9
.

Consider the diagonal unitary operator
U\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{C}^{2})
defined by
U=\text{diag}[\lambda,\lambda]
(where
\lambda\not=0
) with respect to the standard basis
\{{\bf e}_{1},{\bf e}_{2}\}
for
\mathbb{C}^{2}
. The only hyperinvariant subspaces for
U
are the trivial ones
\{{\bf 0}\}
and
\mathbb{C}^{2}
(since any
2\times 2
matrix commutes with
U
). Define two conjugations
C_{1}
,
C_{2}
on
\mathbb{C}^{2}
by

C_{1}({\bf e}_{1})={\bf e}_{1},\;C_{1}({\bf e}_{2})={\bf e}_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad C_{2}({\bf e}_{1})={\bf e}_{2},\;C_{2}({\bf e}_{2})={\bf e}_{1}

(and of course extend antilinearity to all of
\mathbb{C}^{2}
). One can check that
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
. Note that each
C_{i}
separately has more invariant subspaces than the trivial ones
\{{\bf 0}\}
and
\mathbb{C}^{2}
, but only the trivial subspaces are simultaneously invariant for both of them.

Our summary theorem connecting hyperinvariant subspaces and conjugations is the following.

Theorem 8.10
.

Let
U
be a unitary operator on a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with spectral measure
E(\cdot)
. For a subspace
\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
, the following are equivalent.

(a)

\mathcal{M}
is hyperinvariant;

(b)

\mathcal{M}=E(\Omega)\mathcal{H}
for some Borel set
\Omega\subseteq\sigma(U)
;

(c)

\mathcal{M}=\mathcal{H}_{\mu}
for some
\mu\in M_{+}(\mathbb{T})
;

(d)

C\mathcal{M}\subseteq\mathcal{M}
for every
C\in\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
.

Proof.

(a)\Longrightarrow(b)
is Theorem
3.10
.
(b)\Longrightarrow(c)
is Theorem
3.11
.
(c)\Longrightarrow(d)
is Theorem
3.2
.
(d)\Longrightarrow(a)
is Theorem
8.8
.
∎

8.3

(the description of
\mathscr{C}_{s}(U)
when
U
is an
n\times n
unitary matrix). The same is true for Examples
4.4
and
