Precise Learning Curves and Higher-Order Scaling Limits for Dot Product Kernel Regression
Lechao Xiao, Hong Hu, Theodor Misiakiewicz, Yue M. Lu, Jeffrey Pennington

Abstract
As modern machine learning models continue to advance the computational frontier, it has become increasingly important to develop precise estimates for expected performance improvements under different model and data scaling regimes. Currently, theoretical understanding of the learning curves that characterize how the prediction error depends on the number of samples is restricted to either large-sample asymptotics ( m → ∞ m\to\infty ) or, for certain simple data distributions, to the high-dimensional asymptotics in which the number of samples scales linearly with the dimension ( m ∝ d m\propto d ). There is a wide gulf between these two regimes, including all higher-order scaling relations m ∝ d r m\propto d^{r} , which are the subject of the present paper. We focus on the problem of kernel ridge regression for dot-product kernels and present precise formulas for the mean of the test error, bias, and variance, for data drawn uniformly from the sphere with isotropic random labels in the r r th-order asymptotic scaling regime m → ∞ m\to\infty with m / d r m/d^{r} held constant. We observe a peak in the learning curve whenever m ≈ d r / r ! m\approx d^{r}/r! for any integer r r , leading to multiple sample-wise descent and nontrivial behavior at multiple scales. Introduction

Modern machine learning has entered an era in which scaling is arguably the most critical ingredient to improve performance. Recent breakthroughs such as GPT-3
[
24
]
and PaLM
[
11

While extrapolation of empirical learning curves is widely used to make predictions about how a model might perform when extra resources become available, a rigorous theoretical understanding is lacking. A fundamental obstacle in developing a detailed theoretical model of such learning curves is that they depend on many moving parts, e.g. the data distribution, the network architecture, the training algorithm, among others. In addition, even in the simplest possible settings, the learning curves can exhibit non-trivial structure that naive scaling laws fail to model, e.g. the well-known double-descent phenomenon
[
7
,
3

In the past couple years, a large amount of effort from the community has improved our theoretical understanding of such phenomena and in some cases precise characterizations of learning curves have been obtained (see e.g.,
[

[

In contrast, many state-of-the-art models operate in a regime where the amount of data is much larger than the data dimensionality; for example, large text corpora can contain trillions of tokens, whereas the effective input dimensionality of language models is at most millions. Therefore, going beyond the linear scaling regime (
m\propto d
) to higher-order scaling regimes (
m\propto d^{r}
) is essential in improving our understanding of modern machine learning systems, and is the focus of the current paper.

Several works have investigated the behavior of the learning curves for nonlinear scalings in the dot-product kernel or random features setting, but they have done so only in the noncritical regime where

[

Structure of the Empirical Kernel and Marchenko-Pastur Distribution

The structure of the empirical kernel matrix
K({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})
plays a critical role in characterizing the sample-wise test error for the kernel ridge regressor associated to
K
. We assume the training set size scales polynomially, i.e.
m\sim d^{r}
for some positive integer
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
. Decompose this kernel into low-, critical- and high-frequency modes as follows,

\displaystyle K({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})=\sum_{k<r}\sigma_{k}^{2}Y_{k}({\bm{X}})Y_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\sigma_{r}^{2}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\sum_{k>r}\sigma_{k}^{2}Y_{k}({\bm{X}})Y_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}\,.

(3)

The low- and high-frequency parts have simple structures since
N(d,k)/m
either diverges to infinity or converges to zero with rate as least
d^{\pm 1}
, yielding concentration that results in significant simplification. To be precise,
for high-frequency modes
k>r
,
Y_{k}({\bm{X}})
is a “fat" matrix and
Y_{k}({\bm{X}})Y_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}/N(d,k)={\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}
where
\Delta_{d}
vanishes as
d\to\infty

[
32
]
. Thus, the high-frequency parts behave like a regularizer in the following sense,

\displaystyle\sum_{k>r}\sigma_{k}^{2}Y_{k}({\bm{X}})Y_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}=\sum_{k>r}\sigma_{k}^{2}N(d,k){\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}=\left(\sum_{k>r}\hat{h}_{k}^{2}\right){\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}\,.

(4)

On the other hand, when
k<r
,
Y_{k}({\bm{X}})
is a
m\times N(d,k)
“tall" matrix with
N(d,k)/m=O(d^{k}/m)=O(d^{-(r-k)})\to 0
. Similarly,
Mei et al. 2021
show that
Y_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{k}({\bm{X}})/m={\bm{I}}_{N(d,k)}+\Delta_{d}
, implying that
when restricted to the subspace spanned by low-frequency functions
\{Y_{kl}\}_{k<r}
, the regressor associated to the empirical kernel
K({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})
acts like a pure multiplicative scaling.

It remains to understand the critical-frequency mode
Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})
. It turns out that if
N(d,k)/m\to\alpha\in(0,\infty)
,
then the empirical spectral measure of the random matrix
Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})/m
converges to the Marchenko-Pastur distribution
\mu_{\alpha}

\displaystyle\mu_{\alpha}(t)=\left(1-\frac{1}{\alpha}\right)^{+}\delta_{0}(t)+\frac{\sqrt{(\alpha_{+}-t)(t-\alpha_{-})}}{2\pi\alpha t}\bm{1}_{[\alpha_{-},\alpha_{+}]}(t),\,\mathrm{where}\,\,\alpha_{\pm}=(1\pm\sqrt{\alpha})^{2}.

(5)

where

1

4
Generalization Error of Dot-Product Kernel Regression

In this section, we establish the
average
generalization error for the kernel regression in the asymptotic regime
N(d,r)/m\to\alpha
, for some
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
r\geq 1
fixed. We assume the label function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{S}_{d-1})
is given by

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{l\in[N(d,k)]}\hat{f}_{kl}Y_{kl}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k\geq 1}\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}^{\top}Y_{k}({\bm{x}})\,,

(10)

where
\hat{f}_{kl}
are the “Fourier" coefficients and
\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}=[f_{kl}]_{l\in N(d,k)}^{\top}
. We need to make a technical assumption that for
k^{\prime},k\geq r

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}=\bm{0},\,\quad\quad\mathbb{E}\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}^{\top}=\frac{\hat{F}_{k}^{2}}{N(d,k)}{\bm{I}}_{N(d,k)}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}\hat{\bm{f}}_{k^{\prime}}^{\top}=\bm{0}_{N(d,k)\times N(d,k^{\prime})}

(11)

i.e.
\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}
is centered with isotropic covariance and
\{\hat{\bm{f}}_{k}\}_{k\geq r}
are mutually uncorrelated.
Note that we allow
\hat{f}_{kl}
to be deterministic for
k<r
.
We let
{\bm{F}}=(\hat{F}_{k})_{\geq 1}
be a fixed sequence with

\sum_{k\geq 1}\hat{F}_{k}^{2}<\infty
, where
\hat{F}_{k}^{2}=\sum_{l\in[N(d,k)]}\hat{f}_{kl}^{2}
for
k<r
.
For convenience, set
\hat{F}_{>j}^{2}=\sum_{k>j}\hat{F}_{k}^{2}
(similarly for
\hat{F}^{2}_{\geq j}
,
\hat{F}^{2}_{\leq j}
, etc.) and use
{\bm{f}}
to denote the random vector
\{\hat{f}_{kl}\}_{kl}
.
Given training inputs
{\bm{X}}
and observed labels
{\bm{Y}}=f({\bm{X}})+{\bm{\epsilon}}
, where
\bm{\epsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{m})
is the noise, the prediction using kernel function
K
is given by

\displaystyle y({\bm{x}})=K({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}})(K({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})+\lambda{\bm{I}}_{m})^{-1}(f({\bm{X}})+\bm{\epsilon})\,.

(12)

Here
\lambda\geq 0
is the regularization. As such, the
mean
test error over the random labels is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}({\bm{X}};\lambda,{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{h}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{f}}}\mathrm{Err}({\bm{X}};\lambda,{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}})\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\mathrm{Err}({\bm{X}};\lambda,{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}})=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}},\bm{\epsilon}}|y({\bm{x}})-f({\bm{x}})|^{2}\,.

(13)

To state our results, we need to introduce two functions
\chi_{B}
and
\chi_{V}
which are related to the bias and variance in the generalization error,

\displaystyle\chi_{B}(\alpha,\xi)=\int(1+\xi t)^{-2}\mu_{\alpha}(t)dt\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\chi_{V}(\alpha,\xi)=\alpha\xi^{2}\int{t}(1+\xi t)^{-2}\mu_{\alpha}(t)dt\,.

(14)

Both
\chi_{B}
and
\chi_{V}
have closed-form representations; see Sec
C.5
.
Define the effective regularization associated to the
r
-th order scaling to be

\displaystyle\xi_{r}(\hat{\bm{h}},\lambda,\alpha)=\frac{\hat{h}_{r}^{2}}{\alpha(\lambda+\hat{h}_{>r}^{2})}

(15)

Finally,
we define the bias and variance associated to the
r
-th order scaling to be

\displaystyle\mathrm{B}_{r}(\alpha)=\mathrm{B}_{r}(\alpha;\lambda,{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{h}})=

\displaystyle\chi_{B}(\alpha,\xi_{r}(\hat{\bm{h}},\lambda,\alpha))\hat{F}_{r}^{2}+\hat{F}_{>r}^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle\mathrm{V}_{r}(\alpha)=\mathrm{V}_{r}(\alpha;\lambda,{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{h}})=

\displaystyle\chi_{V}\left(\alpha,\xi_{r}(\hat{\bm{h}},\lambda,\alpha)\right)\left(\hat{F}_{>r}^{2}+\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}\right)

(17)

The following is our main result, which characterizes the test error in the asymptotic regime
m\propto d^{r}
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
and
r\geq 1
be fixed. Assume
N(d,r)/m\to\alpha
as
d\to\infty
. Then the average test error is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}({\bm{X}};\lambda,{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{h}})=\mathrm{B}_{r}(\alpha;\lambda,{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{h}})+\mathrm{V}_{r}(\alpha;\lambda,{\bm{F}},\hat{\bm{h}})+\Delta_{d},

(18)

where
\Delta_{d}\to 0
in probability.

4.1
Interpretations

We provide some high-level interpretations of the bias term
\mathrm{B_{r}}
and variance term
\mathrm{V_{r}}
.

The Bias.

From Eq. (
16
),
the regressor learns all low-frequency modes (
k<r
) but none of the high-frequency modes (
k>r
) as the bias
\mathrm{B}_{r}
contains no low-frequency modes (i.e.
k<r
) but all high-frequency modes
\hat{F}_{>r}^{2}
. Importantly, the regressor is progressively learning the critical-frequency mode
Y_{r}
as the training size
m=\frac{1}{\alpha}N(d,r)
increases, i.e. from
\alpha=\infty
to
\alpha=0^{+}
since
\chi_{B}(\alpha,\xi_{r}(\hat{\bm{h}},\lambda,\alpha))\to 1
if
\alpha\to\infty
and
\chi_{B}(\alpha,\xi_{r}(\hat{\bm{h}},\lambda,\alpha))\to 0
if
\alpha\to 0^{+}
. See Fig.
3
for the illustration.

The Variance.

From Eq. (

\chi_{\mathrm{V}}
treats all high-frequency modes
\hat{F}_{>r}^{2}
the same as the noise term
\bm{\epsilon}
. Moreover,
\chi_{\mathrm{V}}\to 0
as
\alpha\to 0
or
\infty
and is peaked at
\alpha=1

\xi_{r}
and it diverges to infinity with rate
\xi_{r}^{\frac{1}{2}}
as
\xi_{r}\to\infty
. Indeed, when
\alpha=1
and
\xi^{-1}/2\leq t\leq\xi^{-1}
, we have
t(1+\xi t)^{-2}\mu_{\alpha}(t)\propto\xi^{-1/2}
which implies
\chi_{V}(1,\xi)\geq\xi^{2}\int t(1+\xi t)^{-2}\mu_{\alpha}(t)\bm{1}_{\xi^{-1}/2\leq t\leq\xi^{-1}}dt\propto\xi^{1/2}
.

Finally, Eq. (
18

\Delta_{d}
) when
m\approx N(d,r)\sim d^{r}
but also when
d^{r-1+\delta}\lesssim m\lesssim d^{r-\delta}
(i.e. when “
\alpha=\infty
") and when
d^{r+\delta}\lesssim m\lesssim m^{r+1-\delta}
(i.e. when “
\alpha=0^{+}
") for any
\delta\in(0,1/2)
. Indeed, in the non-critical scaling regime
d^{r-1+\delta}\lesssim m\lesssim d^{r-\delta}
,
\alpha=N(d,k)/m\to\infty
as
d\to\infty
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{B_{r}}(\alpha=\infty)+\mathrm{V_{r}}(\alpha=\infty)=\hat{F}_{\geq r}^{2}+0=\hat{F}_{\geq r}^{2}\,.

(19)

As such, the regressor learns all low-frequency modes but none of the critical- and high-frequency modes (
k\geq r
), which is consistent with the result in
[
17
,
32
]
. A similar argument also shows
\mathrm{B_{r}}(\alpha=0^{+})+\mathrm{V_{r}}(\alpha=0^{+})=\hat{F}_{>r}^{2}
, namely, the regressor also learns the
r
-frequency mode. This observation implies that we can glue together Eq. (
18
) for
r\geq 1
and remove all duplicate terms to generate a sample-wise learning curve (LC):

\displaystyle\mathrm{LC}(m;\lambda,{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}})=\sum_{r\geq 1}\left(\mathrm{B}_{r}\left(\frac{N(d,r)}{m};\lambda,{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}}\right)-(r-1)\hat{F}_{r}^{2}\right)+\mathrm{V}_{r}\left(\frac{N(d,\leq r)}{m};\lambda,{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}}\right)

(20)

where
N(d,\leq r)=\sum_{k=1}^{r}N(d,k)
.
The “
-(r-1)\hat{F}_{r}^{2}
" term in the above equation is due to the fact that
\hat{F}_{r}^{2}
is over-counted
(r-1)
many times (one in each
\mathrm{B}_{k}
for
k=1,...,(r-1)
.) It is worth mentioning that using
\alpha=N(d,\leq r)/{m}
rather than

\alpha={N(d,r)}/{m}
in the variance
\mathrm{V}_{r}
captures the finite-size correction more accurately. See Eq. (
206
).

Corollary 1
.

If, for
1\leq r\in{\mathbb{N}}
, (1)
N(d,r)/m\to\alpha
for some
\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, or (2)
d^{r-1+\delta}\lesssim m\lesssim d^{r-\delta}
for some
\delta\in(0,1/2)
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}({\bm{X}};{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}},\lambda)=\mathrm{LC}(m;\lambda,{\bm{f}},\hat{\bm{h}})+\Delta_{d}

(21)

where
\Delta_{d}\to 0
in probability as
d\to\infty
.

Recall that for each
r
, the variance term
\mathrm{V}_{r}
could diverge to infinity as
\xi_{r}\to\infty
at
\alpha=1
. Thus we might expect a peak in the learning curve for each
r
, yielding the multiple-descent phenomenon, as shown in Fig.
1
. However, such phenomena can disappear by making the heights of the peaks small via choosing
\xi_{r}
small. We will discuss this point in the experimental section.

4.2
Proof Sketch

The proof of this theorem is quite involved; see Sec.
C
. For simplicity, we assume the observed labels are noiseless, i.e.
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=0
.
An ingredient is to understand the structure of the operator

\displaystyle{\bm{T}}_{K}f({\bm{x}})=K({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}})(K({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})+\lambda{\bm{I}}_{m})^{-1}f({\bm{X}})\,.

(22)

The high-level strategy is as follows. We decompose the function into low-, critical- and high-frequency parts
f=f_{<r}+f_{r}+f_{>r}
. As such, the test error is roughly

\displaystyle\mathrm{Err}({\bm{X}})\approx\mathrm{Err}_{<r}({\bm{X}})+\mathrm{Err}_{r}({\bm{X}})+\mathrm{Err}_{>r}({\bm{X}})\,\,\mathrm{where}\,\,\mathrm{Err}_{r}({\bm{X}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{f}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}|{\bm{T}}_{K}f_{r}({\bm{x}})-f_{r}({\bm{x}})|^{2}\,,

and similarly for
\mathrm{Err}_{<r}({\bm{X}})
and
\mathrm{Err}_{>r}({\bm{X}})
. The next step is to estimate each part separately.

Low-frequencies.

Using the fact that the low-frequency parts of the kernel function
K
is almost an isometric operator on the column space of
Y_{<r}({\bm{X}})
, one can show that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}|T_{K}(f_{<r})({\bm{x}})-f_{<r}({\bm{x}})|^{2}=\Delta_{d}\to 0
in probability,
pointwisely
.

Critical-frequency.

Up to a vanishing term,
one can remove all non-critical frequencies in the kernel function
K
in
T_{K}
in the sense of making the following substitutions

\displaystyle K({\bm{x}},{\bm{X}})\to\sigma_{r}^{2}Y_{r}({\bm{x}})^{\top}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad K_{r}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}}^{\prime})\to\sigma_{r}^{2}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\hat{h}^{2}_{>r}{\bm{I}}_{m}\,.

(23)

Thus, with
\gamma=(\lambda+\hat{h}_{>r}^{2})
,
T_{K}f_{r}({\bm{x}})-f_{r}({\bm{x}})=Y_{r}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{r}({\bm{X}}){\bm{f}}_{r}+\Delta_{d}
, where

\displaystyle{\bm{M}}_{r}({\bm{X}})=\left({\bm{I}}_{N(d,r)}-\sigma_{r}^{2}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}(\sigma_{r}^{2}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\gamma{\bm{I}}_{m})^{-1}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})\right)\,.

(24)

Taking expectation with respect to
{\bm{x}}
(using orthogonality of
Y_{r}({\bm{x}})
) and then with respect to
{\bm{f}}_{r}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{f}}_{r}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}|Y_{r}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{M}}_{r}({\bm{X}}){\bm{f}}_{r}|^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{f}}_{r}}|{\bm{M}}_{r}({\bm{X}}){\bm{f}}_{r}|^{2}=\hat{F}_{r}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}({\bm{M}}_{r}^{2})/N(d,r)\,.

(25)

Applying the Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula and then Theorem
1
,

\displaystyle\hat{F}_{r}^{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left(Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})/m+m\sigma_{r}^{2}/\gamma{\bm{I}}_{N(d,r)}\right)^{-2}/N(d,r)\to\hat{F}_{r}^{2}\int\frac{\mu_{\alpha}(t)}{(t+\xi_{r})^{2}}dt\,.

High-frequencies.
The cross term
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{f}}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}{\bm{T}}_{K}f_{>r}({\bm{x}})f_{>r}({\bm{x}})=\Delta_{d}
and thus

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{f}},{\bm{x}}}|{\bm{T}}_{K}f_{>r}({\bm{x}})-f_{>r}({\bm{x}})|^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{f}},{\bm{x}}}|{\bm{T}}_{K}f_{>r}({\bm{x}})|^{2}+\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{f}},{\bm{x}}}|f_{>r}({\bm{x}})|^{2}+\Delta_{d}\,.

(26)

The second term is equal to
\hat{F}_{>}^{2}
. The calculation of the first term is similar to that of the critical frequency above (namely, we remove all high-/low-frequency components in
K
.)

5.2
Deep Convolutional Kernels

The eigenstructure of general CNN kernels are much more complicated as they depend on both the frequencies (i.e. the order of the polynomials) and the topologies of the networks
[
42
]
. To rigorously describe the eigenstructure, a heavy dose of notation must be introduced, which is beyond the scope of the paper. Nevertheless, the approach developed here is readily extended to cover general CNN kernels. We briefly describe the main ideas.

Following
[
42
]
,
we assume the input space is still
\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}={\mathbb{S}}_{d_{0}-1}^{p}
, where
p
is the number of patches. For simplicity, we assume the network has
L
convolutional layers and in each layer, the filter size and the stride are all equal to
d_{0}
. Thus the spatial dimension of the input is reduced to 1 after
L
convolutional layers. We then add a non-linearality and a dense layer to generate the logits. The kernel has the following form

\displaystyle K({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\sum_{{\bm{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{p}}\sum_{{\bm{l}}\in\prod_{i\in[p]}[N(d_{0},k_{i})]}\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}Y_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}({\bm{x}})Y_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}({\bm{x}}^{\prime}),\,\mathrm{where}\quad Y_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}({\bm{x}})=\prod_{i\in[p]}Y_{k_{i},l_{i}}({\bm{x}}_{i})\,.

(30)

Unlike dot-product kernels in which
{\bm{k}}
is a scalar and the eigenvalues depend only on
|{\bm{k}}|
(i.e. the frequencies),
\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}
depends on both
|{\bm{k}}|
and the spatial structure of the vector
{\bm{k}}
in a rather complicated manner. Nevertheless, as
d_{0}\to\infty
,
\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}\sim d_{0}^{-j_{{\bm{k}}}}=d^{-j_{\bm{k}}/L}
for some
L\leq j_{\bm{k}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We can then categorize the eigenvectors according to the decay order of
\sigma^{2}_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}
. Unlike the case of dot-product kernels or the one-hidden layer CNN kernels, in which eigenvectors with same-order eigenvalues are in the same eigenspace (i.e. the eigenvalues are the same), multiple-layer CNN kernels can have
multiple
eigenspaces with the same-order eigenvalues. Although this results in extra challenges (see below), our overall approach carries over. Consider the critical scaling regime
m\sim d^{r}
, for
r=j/L
for some
L\leq j\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Likewise, we can decompose the kernel into low-, critical- and high-frequency parts according to
j_{\bm{k}}<r
,
j_{\bm{k}}=r
and
j_{\bm{k}}>r
, resp. Following similar assumptions on the labels and eigenvalues, the bias and the variance can be essentially reduced to computing

\displaystyle\chi_{B}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{r}}\Tr{\bm{R}}_{r}^{2}({\bm{R}}_{r}+Y_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{r}({\bm{X}})/m)^{-2}

(31)

\displaystyle\chi_{V}=

\displaystyle\frac{N_{\leq r}}{m}\frac{1}{N_{\leq r}}\Tr({\bm{R}}_{\leq r}+Y_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})/m)^{-2}Y_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Y_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})/m

(32)

where
{\bm{R}}_{r}
and
{\bm{R}}_{\leq r}
are diagonal matrices whose entries are determined by the eigenvalues of the critical-frequency modes. In the dot-product kernels or one-hidden layer CNN kernels setting,
{\bm{R}}_{r}
/
{\bm{R}}_{\leq r}
is a scaled identity matrix and simple, closed-form expressions for the above traces straightforwardly follow from the Marcenko-Pastur distribution. However, for a general diagonal matrix
{\bm{R}}_{r}
with bounded limiting spectra,
{\bm{R}}_{r}
does not commute with
Y_{r}^{\top}({\bm{X}})Y_{r}({\bm{X}})
, and a more detailed random matrix analysis is needed. See the supplementary material for more details.

6
Experiments

We provide experiments to show that our learning curves (Eq. (
21
)) accurately capture empirical sample-wise learning curve even when the ambient dimensions remains small. Even though our theoretical results require averaging the test error over random labels (aka, mean test error), our experimental results suggest this is unnecessary, i.e. the learning curve Eq. (
21
) can capture the test error accurately for any given draw of label function.

Experimental setup.

We generate a polynomial kernel function
h(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{7}\hat{h}_{k}^{2}P_{k}(t)
, where
P_{k}
is the degree-
k
Legendre polynomial in
d
dimensions. The kernel function can be efficiently computed via
K({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=h({\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{x}}^{\prime})
. We choose the label function to be
f({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{7}\hat{F}_{k}y_{k}({\bm{x}})
, where
y_{k}({\bm{x}})=\sum_{j\in[d]}w_{k,j}\prod_{i=j}^{j+k-1}{x_{i}}
, and the coefficients
w_{k,j}
are randomly sampled from a Gaussian and then normalized so that
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}|y_{k}({\bm{x}})|^{2}=1
for each
k
. Therefore
\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}|f({\bm{x}})|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{7}\hat{F}_{k}^{2}
. For simplicity, we also set
\sigma_{\epsilon}^{2}=0
(i.e. noiseless) and
\lambda=0
(i.e. ridgeless). Note that when
m\lesssim d^{r}
, the regressor still contains “effective noise"
\hat{F}_{>r}^{2}
from un-learnable high-frequency modes and “effective regularization"
\hat{h}_{>r}^{2}
. Finally, in our experiments, we choose
\hat{F}_{k}^{2}=k^{-2}
and
\hat{h}_{k}^{2}=\mathrm{Gap}^{-(k-1)}
, where we will vary the value of the spectral gap:
\mathrm{Gap}=\hat{h}_{k}^{2}/\hat{h}_{k+1}^{2}
.
Under this setup, the predicted learning curve
\mathrm{LC}(m)=\mathrm{LC}(m;\mathrm{Gap})
depends only on the spectral gap of the kernel.

To simulate higher-order scaling (
r\geq 3
),
the dimension
d
has to be very small as we need to invert a sequence of matrices of size ranging from
m=1
to
m\propto d^{r}/r!
.
Due to the constraints in compute and memory, the largest
m
we can have is typically
m_{\mathrm{max}}\approx 25,000
for one single GPU and
d
in our experiments is typically around
d=24
. As such we are in a regime with strong finite-size corrections. Finally, all experiments are run in a single A-100 using Google Cloud Colab Notebooks.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we establish precise asymptotic formulas for the sample-wise learning curves in the kernel ridge regression setting for a family of dot-product kernels in the polynomial scaling regimes
m\propto d^{r}
for all
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
. We demonstrate that these formulas can capture empirical learning curves surprisingly well even in the regime where strong finite-size corrections would be expected. We rigorously prove that the learning curves can be non-monotonic near
m\propto d^{r}/r!
for each
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
. There are a couple limitations of our approach which could be improved in future work. The first one is the strong assumption on the distribution of the input data, namely, the uniform distribution on the spherical type of data. In addition, the learning curves are obtained only in the kernel regression setting and extending the results to the random feature setting (see, e.g.,
[
29
]
) and the feature learning setting
[
44
]
would be meaningful future directions.

\displaystyle 2N(d,r)^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\sum_{{\bm{i}},{\bm{j}},{\bm{i}}\cap{\bm{j}}\neq\emptyset}|B_{{\bm{i}}{\bm{i}}}B_{{\bm{j}}{\bm{j}}}(g_{\bm{i}}^{2}({\bm{x}})g_{\bm{j}}^{2}({\bm{x}})-1)|

\displaystyle\lesssim

For any

It remains to prove Eq. (

By symmetries,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}x_{t}^{2}d{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{d}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}\sum_{1\leq t\leq d}x_{t}^{2}d{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{d}\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}1d{\bm{x}}=\frac{1}{d}\,.

(73)

By symmetries again,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}\prod_{1\leq t\leq j-1}x_{t}^{2}d{\bm{x}}

(74)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}\prod_{1\leq t\leq j-1}x_{t}^{2}\left(\sum_{1\leq i\leq j-1}x_{i}^{2}+\sum_{j\leq i\leq d}x_{i}^{2}\right)d{\bm{x}}

\displaystyle(d-j+1)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}\prod_{1\leq t\leq j}x_{t}^{2}d{\bm{x}}+(j-1)\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}x_{1}^{2}\prod_{1\leq t\leq j-1}x_{t}^{2}d{\bm{x}}

We use hypercontractivities to bound the error term, namely, the second term. Recall that for any

\displaystyle\left(\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}|f({\bm{x}})|^{q}d{\bm{x}}\right)^{1/q}\leq(q-1)^{\deg(f)/2}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}|f({\bm{x}})|^{2}d{\bm{x}}\right)^{1/2}\,.

and

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}}\prod_{1\leq t\leq j}x_{t}^{2}d{\bm{x}}=

(82)

Finally, Eq. (

Appendix C
Generalization

We aim to obtain the asymptotic formulas for the test error in this section.
In the high-level, we decompose the empirical kernel
K({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})
into low-, critical- and high-frequency modes,
where we have concentration in the low- and high-frequency parts of the kernel. The test error associated to these two parts are easier to handle.
The critical-frequency part is more difficult in which random matrix behaviors emerge, namely, the Marchenko-Pastur distribution.
As such, our first step is to remove the contribution in the test error coming from the non-critical frequency parts. After that, the remaining is essentially equivalent to computing the trace of certain functional forms related to the Marchenko-Pastur distribution.

We consider a general setting that includes the dot-product kernels, the one-hidden-layer and the multiple-layer convolutional kernels (NNGP and NT kernels.)
In what follows, we use
\Delta_{d},\Delta_{d}^{\prime},\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime}
, etc. to represent quantities that converge to 0 in probability (the absolute value of a scalar, the norm of a vector, the operator norm of a matrix, etc.), whose exact form may change from line to line.

C.1
Setup

For
d\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, let
\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be the input space associated with a probability measure
\sigma^{(d)}
and a kernel function
K^{(d)}
. Assume the kernel function has the following eigen-structure

\displaystyle K^{(d)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}\sum_{l\in N_{kn}^{(d)}}\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}})\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})

(83)

in the sense
K^{(d)}
, as the integral operator from
L^{2}(\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)},\sigma^{(d)})
to itself,

\displaystyle K^{(d)}\phi_{knl}^{(d)}({\bm{x}})=\int K^{(d)}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{\prime})\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}^{\prime})\sigma^{(d)}(d{\bm{x}}^{\prime})=(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}).

(84)

Here
\{\phi^{(d)}_{knl}\}_{knl}
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)},\sigma^{(d)})
. We also assume
K^{(d)}
is a trace-class operator, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sum_{knl}\langle K^{(d)}\phi_{knl}^{(d)},\phi_{knl}^{(d)}\rangle=\sum_{k\geq 1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}N_{kn}^{(d)}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}<\infty\,.

(85)

In the above notations, we use the triplet
(k,n,l)
to index the eigenfunctions
\phi_{knl}^{(d)}
. The tuple
(k,n)
determines the eigenspace, whose eigenvalue is of the form “
(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}=C_{n}d^{-s_{k}}+\mathrm{Lower\,\,Order}
" and
l
lists all eigenfunctions in the
kn
-eigenspace.
We make the following assumptions.

Kernel Assumptions.

(1.)

Spectral Gap.
There are
\delta_{0}>0
and a sequence of strictly increasing positive real numbers
\{s_{k}\}
with
|s_{k}-s_{k-1}|\geq\delta_{0}
for all
k\geq 2
such that

\displaystyle(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}\sim d^{-s_{k}}\sim(N_{kn}^{(d)})^{-1}

(86)

Moreover,
\{E_{k}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
is independent from
d
which grows at most exponentially. We also assume that there is a sequence of real numbers
\{\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}\}_{kn}
with
\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}\neq 0
unless
k
is sufficiently large and

\displaystyle\sum_{k}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}<\infty\quad\mathrm{and}\quad(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}N_{kn}^{(d)}=\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}\quad\mathrm{as}\quad d\to\infty

(87)

(2.)

Hypercontractivity Inequalities.
For any
p\geq 2
there are constant
C_{p,k}
such that for any function
f
in the closure of
\mathrm{Span}\{\phi^{(d)}_{jnl}\}_{j\leq k}

\displaystyle\|f\|_{p}\leq C_{p,k}\|f\|_{2}

(88)

(3.)

Concentration of Quadratic Forms.

Let
\phi^{(d)}_{k}({\bm{x}})
denote the column vector consists of elements
\{\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}})\}_{l\in[N_{kn}{(d)}n\in[E_{k}]}
.
For every sequence of matrices
\{{\bm{A}}^{(d)}\}
with uniformly bounded operator norm,

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}N_{kn}^{(d)}\right)^{-2}\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{x}}}|\phi^{(d)}_{k}({\bm{x}})^{\top}{\bm{A}}^{(d)}\phi^{(d)}_{k}({\bm{x}})-\Tr{\bm{A}}^{(d)}|^{2}\to 0\quad\mathrm{as}\quad d\to\infty.

(89)

(4.)

Addition Theorem.
For
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
n\in[E_{k}]
and
{\bm{x}}\in\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)}

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in[N_{kn}^{(d)}]}\phi_{knl}^{(d)}({\bm{x}})^{2}=N_{kn}^{(d)}

(90)

Let us briefly explain the assumptions. The
Spectral Gap
assumption basically says, we can classify the eigenvectors into countably many categories indexed by
k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
. In the
k
-th category, it has exactly
E_{k}
many eigenspaces, each of them has dimensions
\sim d^{s_{k}}
and eigenvalues
d^{-s_{k}}
. It also implies the number of eigenfunctions with eigenvalues
\lesssim d^{-s_{k}}
is
\sim d^{s_{k}}
.
Assumptions (1.), (2.) and (4.) together are stronger than those in Theorem 6 in
Mei et al. 2021
(and slightly less technical), which allow us to apply kernel concentration from
Mei et al. 2021
. In particular, they imply concentration of the low- and high-frequency parts of the empirical kernel
K^{(d)}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})
. Finally,
Assumption (3) is designed to meet the requirements in Lemma
1
, which allows us to claim Marchenko-Pastur type behavior of the gram matrix induced by the feature map
\phi_{k}
. We provide a couple examples.

Example 1

(Dot-product Kernels)
.

When
\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)}={\mathbb{S}}_{d-1}
and
K^{(d)}
is the dot-product kernel, we have
E_{k}=1
,
s_{k}=k
,
N_{kn}=N(d,k)\sim d^{k}/k!
, and
\phi_{knl}=Y_{kl}
(note that
n=0
since
E_{k}=1
.) Note that by the Addition Theorem of spherical harmonics (Theorem 4.11 in
Frye and Efthimiou 2012
),

\displaystyle\sum_{l\in[N(d,k)]}Y_{kl}({\bm{x}})^{2}=N(d,k)

(91)

Example 2

(One-hidden-layer Convolutional Kernels)
.

Sightly more general setting is the one-layer convolutional kernel (NNGP or NT kernels). In this case,
\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)}={\mathbb{S}}_{d_{0}-1}^{p}
where
p
is the number of patches and the input dimension is
d=pd_{0}
. We can set either
p=O(1)
(i.e. independent of
d_{0}\to\infty
) or
p\sim d^{\alpha_{p}}
for some
\alpha_{p}>0
. This kernel is essentially the sum of
p
dot-product kernels. As such,
E_{k}=1
,
N_{kn}{(d)}=pN(d_{0},k)\sim pd_{0}^{k}/k!

and
(\sigma_{kn}^{u}pd)^{2}\sim(pd_{0}^{k})^{-1}
. If
p\sim d^{\alpha_{p}}
and
d_{0}\sim d^{\alpha_{d_{0}}}
with
\alpha_{d_{0}}+\alpha_{p}=1
, we have
s_{k}=\alpha_{p}+k\alpha_{d_{0}}
and
d^{-s_{k}}
is the decay rate of the
k
-th order spherical harmonics.

Example 3

(Multiple-layer Convolutional Kernels)
.

General convolutional kernels are much more complicated
[
42
]
. In this case,
\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)}={\mathbb{S}}_{d_{0}-1}^{p}
where
p
is the number of patches and the input dimension is
d=pd_{0}
.
We additionally assume,
p=k_{0}^{L-1}
for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
and the network has
L
convolutional layers with filter size and strides being the same in each layer (equal to
d_{0}
in the first layer and to
k_{0}
for the remaining
(L-1)
layers.)
The eigenstructures of such kernels are studied in
Xiao 2021
. The eigenfunctions are tensor products of spherical harmonics defined on copies of
{\mathbb{S}}_{d_{0}-1}
,

\displaystyle Y_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}({\bm{x}})=\prod_{i\in[p]}Y_{k_{i}l_{i}}({\bm{x}}_{i})

(92)

The eigenvalues are more complicated to compute as they depend on both the frequencies of
Y_{{\bm{k}},{\bm{l}}}
and the topologies of the networks. When
d_{0}\propto d^{\alpha_{d_{0}}}
and
k_{0}\propto d^{\alpha_{k_{0}}}
with
\alpha_{d_{0}}+(L-1)\alpha_{k_{0}}=1
and
\alpha_{k_{0}},\alpha_{d_{0}}>0
, the eigenvalue of
Y_{{\bm{k}}{\bm{l}}}
is
\propto d^{-({\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}})+{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}}))}
, as
d\to\infty
. Here
{\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}})\equiv|{\bm{k}}|\alpha_{d_{0}}
is the frequency index of
Y_{{\bm{k}}{\bm{l}}}
and
{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}})=J_{\bm{k}}\alpha_{k_{0}}
is the spatial index, where
J_{\bm{k}}
is the number of edges in the sub-tree connecting all interacting patches (i.e.
k_{i}\neq 0
) to the output; see
Xiao 2021
for more details.

As there can possibly exist
{\bm{k}}
and
{\bm{k}}^{\prime}
with
{\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}})+{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}})={\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})+{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}}^{\prime})
(i.e., same order of decay) but
({\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}}),{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}}))\neq({\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}}^{\prime}),{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}}^{\prime}))
(i.e. different space-frequency combination),
there can exist more than one eigenspaces whose eigenvalues decay to zero with the same rate
d^{-({\mathscr{F}}({\bm{k}})+{\mathscr{S}}({\bm{k}}))}
, but with different leading coefficients. This is the main reason why we need to allow
|E_{k}|>1
in Eq. (
83
).

Next we discuss the assumptions on the label function.
Let
{\bm{X}}
be the training set
with
m\sim d^{s_{r}}
many training samples for some
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
fixed. Then let the ground true label function to be

\displaystyle f({\bm{x}})=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}\sum_{l\in[N_{kn}^{(d)}]}\hat{f}_{knl}\phi_{knl}^{(d)}({\bm{x}}).

(93)

Let
N_{k}^{(d)}=\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}N_{kn}^{(d)}
. We assume, for
k\geq r
,
\hat{\bm{f}}_{kn}=\{\hat{f}_{knl}\}_{l\in[N_{kn}^{(d)}]}
is a random vector with

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\hat{\bm{f}}_{kn}=\bm{0}\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\mathbb{E}\hat{\bm{f}}_{kn}\hat{\bm{f}}_{kn}^{\top}=\frac{\hat{F}_{kn}^{2}}{N_{kn}^{(d)}}{\bm{I}}_{N_{kn}^{(d)}}.

(94)

and
\{\hat{\bm{f}}_{kn}\}_{n\in[E_{k}],k\geq r}
are mutually independent. One concrete example is

\displaystyle\hat{\bm{f}}_{kn}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\bm{0},\frac{\hat{F}_{kn}^{2}}{N_{kn}^{(d)}}{\bm{I}}_{N_{kn}^{(d)}}\right)\,.

(95)

For
k<r
, we assume the coefficients are deterministic with
\sum_{l}\hat{f}_{knl}^{2}=\hat{F}_{kn}^{2}
,
\sum_{n}\hat{F}_{kn}^{2}=\hat{F}_{k}^{2}
and

\displaystyle\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}\hat{F}_{k}^{2}<\infty

(96)

Our goal is to compute the average test error over the random labels defined above in the scaling limit
m\sim d^{s_{r}}
.

C.2
Structure of the Empirical Kernels

For convenience, denote

\displaystyle\phi^{(d)}_{\leq k}({\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=[\phi^{(d)}_{jnl}({\bm{x}})]_{l\in[N_{jn}^{(d)}],1\leq j\leq k,n\in[E_{j}],}^{\top}

(97)

\displaystyle N_{k}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}N_{kn}^{(d)}

(98)

\displaystyle N_{\leq k}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{1\leq j\leq k}N_{\leq j}^{(d)}

(99)

Let
{\bm{x}}_{i}^{\top}
be the
i
-th row of the training matrix
{\bm{X}}
.
Similarly,

\displaystyle Z_{k}({\bm{X}})=[\phi^{(d)}_{k}({\bm{x}}_{0}),\dots,\phi^{(d)}_{k}({\bm{x}}_{m-1})]^{\top}\quad\quad

\displaystyle Z_{\leq k}({\bm{X}})=[\phi^{(d)}_{\leq k}({\bm{x}}_{0}),\dots,\phi^{(d)}_{\leq k}({\bm{x}}_{m-1})]^{\top}

(100)

\displaystyle{\bm{\Lambda}}_{k}=\mathrm{diag}\left([(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}{\bm{I}}_{N_{kn}^{(d)}}]_{n\in[E_{k}]}\right)

\displaystyle{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\leq k}=\mathrm{diag}\left([(\sigma_{jn}^{(d)})^{2}{\bm{I}}_{N_{jn}^{(d)}}]_{n\in[E_{j}],1\leq j\leq k}\right)

(101)

Note that
Z_{k}({\bm{X}})
(
Z_{\leq k}({\bm{X}})
) is an
m\times N_{k}^{(d)}
(
m\times N_{\leq k}^{(d)}
) matrix and
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{k}
(
{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\leq k}
) is an
N_{k}^{(d)}\times N_{k}^{(d)}
(
N_{\leq k}^{(d)}\times N_{\leq k}^{(d)}
) diagonal matrix. The followings are defined similarly,

\displaystyle Z_{<k}({\bm{X}}),\quad Z_{kn}({\bm{X}}),\quad{\bm{\Lambda}}_{<k},\quad{\bm{\Lambda}}_{kn},\quad N_{<k}^{(d)},\quad N_{kn}^{(d)}\,.

(102)

Next, we decompose the train-train kernel into two parts: the
\leq r
frequency part and the
>r
frequency parts,

\displaystyle K^{(d)}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}Z_{k}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\Lambda}}_{k}Z_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}=Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\Lambda}}_{\leq}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\sum_{k\geq r+1}Z_{k}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\Lambda}}_{k}Z_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}

(103)

\displaystyle=Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}}){\bm{\Lambda}}_{\leq}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\sum_{k\geq r+1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})^{\top}

(104)

\displaystyle\equiv K_{\leq r}^{(d)}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})+K^{(d)}_{>r}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})

(105)

Assumptions (1.) (2.)
allow us to apply kernel concentration
[
17
,
32
]
, which implies that the low-frequency and high-frequency parts of the empirical kernels are concentrated. By saying concentration in the high-frequency part, we mean

Claim 1
.

Let

\displaystyle\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{kn}^{(d)}}Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{m}\,.

(106)

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}\|\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0

(107)

The proof of this claim essentially follows from the arguments and results in Theorem 6 of
[
32
]
; see Sec.
C.7
.
Thus

\displaystyle K^{(d)}_{>r}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq r+1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}N_{kn}^{(d)}(\displaystyle{\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{kn}^{(d)})

(108)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq r+1}\hat{h}_{k}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{m}+\sum_{k\geq r+1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}

(109)

Denote

\displaystyle\hat{h}_{>r}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq r+1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}N_{kn}^{(d)}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}\sim 1

(110)

\displaystyle\Delta_{>r}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq r+1}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}\,,

(111)

we can write

\displaystyle K^{(d)}_{>r}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})=\hat{h}_{>r}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{>r}^{(d)},\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{>r}^{(d)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0

(112)

By saying the low-frequency part of the kernel concentrates, we mean (Theorem 6
[
32
]
)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}Z_{<}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{<}({\bm{X}})={\bm{I}}_{N_{<}^{(d)}}+\Delta_{<r}^{(d)},\quad\mathrm{where}\quad\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{<r}^{(d)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0

(113)

Finally, Lemma
1
and
Assumption (3.)
imply that if
N_{r}^{(d)}/m\to\alpha\in(0,\infty)
, then the empirical measure of the critical part of the kernel matrix

\frac{1}{m}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})

, and the low-and-critical frequency parts

\frac{1}{m}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})

converge to the Marchenko-Pastur distribution
\mu_{\alpha}
weakly by Lemma
1
. In particular,
\|\frac{1}{m}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\frac{1}{m}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})\|_{\mathrm{op}}=O(1)
in probability as
d\to\infty
.

For convenience, we summarize the structure of the empirical kernel in the following.

Corollary 3
.

Assume
Assumptions (1.-4.)
. Let
r\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
and
\alpha>0
be fixed and
m=m^{(d)}
be such that
N_{r}^{(d)}/m\to\alpha\in(0,\infty)
as
d\to\infty
. Let
{\bm{X}}
, of shape
m\times d
, be the training set matrix whose rows are drawn, uniformly, iid from
\bm{{\mathcal{X}}}^{(d)}
. Then we have the following structure for the empirical kernel matrix

High-frequency Features:

\displaystyle K^{(d)}_{>r}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})=\hat{h}_{>r}^{2}{\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{>r}^{(d)},

(114)

Low-frequency Features:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}Z_{<}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{<}({\bm{X}})={\bm{I}}_{N_{<}^{(d)}}+\Delta_{<r}^{(d)}

(115)

Critical-frequency Features:

\displaystyle\text{the empirical measure of}\,\,\frac{1}{m}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})\to\mu_{\alpha}

(116)

Low-and-critical-frequency Features:

\displaystyle\text{the empirical measure of}\,\,\frac{1}{m}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})^{\top}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})\to\mu_{\alpha}

(117)

Let
0\leq\lambda=O(1)
be the regularization and
\gamma=\lambda+\hat{h}_{>r}^{2}
be the effective regularization. To ease the notations, denote

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{<}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}Z_{<r}({\bm{X}})\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}Z_{\leq r}({\bm{X}})\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{>}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}Z_{>r}({\bm{X}})\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{r}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{m}}Z_{r}({\bm{X}})

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{<}=\gamma^{-1}m{\bm{\Lambda}}_{<}\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}=\gamma^{-1}m{\bm{\Lambda}}_{\leq}\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{>}=\gamma^{-1}m{\bm{\Lambda}}_{>}\quad

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{r}=\gamma^{-1}m{\bm{\Lambda}}_{r}

Clearly, Corollary
3
and the assumption on the spectra imply that in probability as
d\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\|\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{<}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{<}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{r}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\lesssim 1

(118)

Then we can write
K^{(d)}
and
K_{\lambda}^{(d)}
as

\displaystyle K^{(d)}_{\lambda}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})\equiv K^{(d)}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})+\lambda{\bm{I}}_{m}=\gamma(\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}+{\bm{I}}_{m})+\Delta_{>r}^{(d)}\equiv{\bm{K}}+\Delta_{>r}^{(d)}

(119)

where

\displaystyle{\bm{K}}=\gamma(\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}+{\bm{I}}_{m})

(120)

Then by Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula

\displaystyle{\bm{K}}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\gamma^{-1}\left({\bm{I}}_{m}-\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\left(\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}^{-1}+\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\right)^{-1}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\right)=\gamma^{-1}\left({\bm{I}}_{m}-\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{D}}}^{-1}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\right)

(121)

where

\displaystyle\overline{{\bm{D}}}

\displaystyle=\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}^{-1}+\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\,.

(122)

The matrix
\overline{{\bm{D}}}
plays a critical role in the remaining analysis.
We have the following control regarding its eigenvalues, which says the eigenvalues of
\overline{{\bm{D}}}
are away from
0
and
\infty

Lemma 3
.

There are constants
0<\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}
independent of
d
such that, in probability as
d\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}{\bm{I}}\prec\overline{{\bm{D}}}\prec\lambda_{2}{\bm{I}}

(123)

We will prove the lemma later in Sec.
C.6
.

Note that

\displaystyle K^{(d)}_{\lambda}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})^{-1}=(K^{(d)}_{\lambda}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})^{-1}{\bm{K}}){\bm{K}}^{-1}=({\bm{I}}_{m}+{\bm{K}}^{-1}\Delta_{>r}^{(d)})^{-1}{\bm{K}}^{-1}=({\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime}){\bm{K}}^{-1}

(124)

where
\|\Delta_{d}^{\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0
in probability since
\|{\bm{K}}^{-1}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\leq\gamma^{-1}
and

\|\Delta_{>r}^{(d)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0
in probability.

Similarly, we can write

\displaystyle M({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})\equiv

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}K({\bm{X}},x)K({\bm{X}},x)^{\top}

(125)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq k\leq r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{4}Z_{k}({\bm{X}})Z_{k}({\bm{X}})^{\top}+\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{4}Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})^{\top}

(126)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle m^{-1}\gamma^{2}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}^{2}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}+m^{-1}\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime}

(127)

where

\displaystyle\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime}\equiv m\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{4}Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})^{\top}=m\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{4}N_{kn}^{(d)}({\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{kn}^{(d)})

(128)

We have
\|\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0
in probability as

, since

\displaystyle m\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{4}N_{kn}^{(d)}\sim

\displaystyle m\sum_{k>r}d^{-s_{k}}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}N_{kn}^{(d)}

(129)

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle d^{s_{r}}d^{-s_{r+1}}\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}(\sigma_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}N_{kn}^{(d)}

(130)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle d^{s_{r}}d^{-s_{r+1}}\sum_{k>r}\sum_{n\in[E_{k}]}\hat{h}_{kn}^{2}\lesssim d^{-\delta_{0}}\to 0

(131)

Finally, the above estimates imply

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}

\displaystyle\equiv K_{\gamma}^{(d)}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})^{-1}M({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})K_{\gamma}^{(d)}({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}})^{-1}

(132)

\displaystyle=({\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime}){\bm{K}}^{-1}M({\bm{X}},{\bm{X}}){\bm{K}}^{-1}({\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime})

(133)

\displaystyle=m^{-1}({\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime}){\bm{K}}^{-1}(\gamma^{2}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}^{2}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime}){\bm{K}}^{-1}({\bm{I}}_{m}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime})

(134)

\displaystyle=m^{-1}\left(\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\left(\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}^{-1}+\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\right)^{-2}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{T}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)

(135)

\displaystyle=m^{-1}\left(\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}\overline{{\bm{D}}}^{-2}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{T}+\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right)

(136)

with the error term
\|\Delta_{d}^{\prime\prime\prime}\|_{\mathrm{op}}\to 0
in probability.

C.3
Reduction I: Reducing the MSE to Traces

We will repeatedly use the following simple results.

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\bm{u}}
be a random vector with
\mathbb{E}{\bm{u}}=\bm{0}

(138)

Here

\displaystyle I_{2}

\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{d}\|_{op}\to 0
as
d\to\infty
in probability. Let
{\bm{u}}=[\beta_{<}{\bm{e}}_{<}^{\top},\beta_{r}{\bm{e}}_{r}^{\top}]^{\top}
be a unit vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{\leq r}^{(d)}}
, where
{\bm{e}}_{<}
and
{\bm{e}}_{r}
are unit vectors in
{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{<r}^{(d)}}
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{N_{r}^{(d)}}

. We want to show that for some
\lambda_{1}>0

\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\leq{\bm{u}}^{\top}D{\bm{u}}={\bm{u}}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}_{\leq}^{-1}{\bm{u}}+{\bm{u}}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}{\bm{u}}

(220)

. Thus there is a constant

such that

(221)

then
C\leq 2\alpha_{+}
in probability.
Thus by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}^{\top}D{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle\geq c\beta_{r}^{2}+\|\beta_{<}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{<}{\bm{e}}_{<}+\beta_{r}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{r}{\bm{e}}_{r}\|_{2}^{2}

(222)

\displaystyle\geq c\beta_{r}^{2}+(\|\beta_{<}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{<}{\bm{e}}_{<}\|_{2}-\|\beta_{r}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{r}{\bm{e}}_{r}\|_{2})^{2}

(223)

\displaystyle\geq c\beta_{r}^{2}+((1-\Delta_{d})|\beta_{<}|-C|\beta_{r}|)^{2}

(224)

where
\Delta_{d}\to 0
in probability.
If
C|\beta_{r}|<\frac{1}{2}|\beta_{<}|
, the above is greater than
(1/2-\Delta_{d})\beta_{<}^{2}+c\beta_{r}^{2}\gtrsim 1
; otherwise

C|\beta_{r}|\geq\frac{1}{2}|\beta_{<}|
and

\displaystyle{\bm{u}}^{\top}D{\bm{u}}\geq 2c\beta_{r}^{2}/2\geq(c\beta_{r}^{2}+c(\frac{1}{2C}\beta_{<})^{2})/2\geq c\min(1,\frac{1}{2C})^{2}/2\gtrsim 1

(225)

The other direction is easier as both
\overline{{\bm{\Lambda}}}^{-1}_{\leq}
and
\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}^{\top}\overline{{\bm{Z}}}_{\leq}
have operator norms bounded above.

1

The proof is split into two part: the ultra-high frequency parts

and the median-high-frequency part,

Controlling the Ultra-high-frequency.

Recall that

\displaystyle\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}\equiv\frac{1}{N_{kn}^{(d)}}Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})Z_{kn}({\bm{X}})^{\top}-{\bm{I}}_{m}\,.

(226)

By
Assumption (4.)
, the diagonals are zero and we have

\displaystyle\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}

\displaystyle=[Z_{kn}({\bm{x}}_{i})^{\top}Z_{kn}({\bm{x}}_{j})/N_{kn}^{(d)}]_{i,j\in[m],i\neq j}

Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}\|_{\mathrm{op}}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\|\Delta_{kn}^{(d)}\|_{\mathrm{F}}^{2}=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\sum_{i\neq j}|Z_{kn}({\bm{x}}_{i})^{\top}Z_{kn}({\bm{x}}_{j})/N_{kn}^{(d)}|^{2}

(228)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(N_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{l,l^{\prime}}\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}_{i})\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}_{j})\phi^{(d)}_{knl^{\prime}}({\bm{x}}_{i})\phi^{(d)}_{knl^{\prime}}({\bm{x}}_{j})

(229)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(N_{kn}^{(d)})^{2}}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i\neq j}\sum_{l}\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}_{i})^{2}\phi^{(d)}_{knl}({\bm{x}}_{j})^{2}

(230)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N_{kn}^{(d)}}m(m-1)\leq\frac{1}{N_{kn}^{(d)}}m^{2}

(231)

E_{k}

(233)

E.3
Natural Data vs. Spherical Data

We compare the spectrum of the NTKs of CIFAR10 associated with three architectures (FCN: fully-connected networks, CNN-VEC: convolutional networks without pooling, and CNN-GAP: convolutional networks with a global average pooling) against the one-layer convolutional kernels with spherical-type of data. Recall that the larger spectral gap between eigenspaces triggers the multiple-descent phenomenon. This phenomenon disappears, and the learning curve becomes monotonic when the spectral gap is small. Fortunately, for CIFAR10, the spectrum of the NTKs are continuous, and the learning curves are monotonic (power-law decay.) As such, there is still a gap between our results/assumptions and natural data.
