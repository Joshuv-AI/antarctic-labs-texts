Spanning tree enumeration via triangular rank-one perturbations of graph Laplacians
Christian Go, Khwa Zhong Xuan, Xinyu Luo, Matthew T. Stamps

Abstract
. We present new short proofs of known spanning tree enumeration formulae for threshold and Ferrers graphs by showing that the Laplacian matrices of such graphs admit triangular rank-one perturbations. We then characterize the set of graphs whose Laplacian matrices admit triangular rank-one perturbations as the class of special 2 2 -threshold graphs, introduced by Hung, Kloks, and Villaamil. Our work introduces (1) a new characterization of special 2 2 -threshold graphs that generalizes the characterization of threshold graphs in terms of isolated and dominating vertices, and (2) a spanning tree enumeration formula for special 2 2 -threshold graphs that reduces to the aforementioned formulae for threshold and Ferrers graphs. We consider both unweighted and weighted spanning tree enumeration.



1.
Introduction

The motivation for this article originated with a linear algebraic technique for spanning tree enumeration introduced by Klee and Stamps
[
14
]
. Kirchhoff’s celebrated Matrix-Tree theorem
[
12
]
asserts that the number of spanning trees
\tau(G)
in a graph
G
is encoded in any cofactor of its Laplacian matrix
L(G)
. While the theorem ensures that
\tau(G)
can be computed for a general graph
G
in polynomial time, more efficient enumeration formulae exist for commonly studied classes of graphs, including complete
[
1
,
4
,
5
,
24
]
, complete multipartite
[
3
,
10
,
15
,
22
,
23
]
, threshold
[
6
,
9
,
20
]
, and Ferrers
[
8
]
graphs. Klee and Stamps
[
14
]
demonstrated that it can be easier to work with a rank-one perturbation of
L(G)
rather than
L(G)
itself. They used their approach to give original straightforward proofs for each of the enumeration formulae listed above. In this article, we present further simplified proofs of the spanning tree enumeration formulae for threshold and Ferrers graphs by showing that the vertices of such graphs can always be ordered so the associated Laplacian matrices admit triangular rank-one perturbations, which raises the following question:

Question 1.1
.

Which graph Laplacians admit triangular rank-one perturbations?

We prove that the Laplacian matrix of a graph
G
admits a triangular rank-one perturbation if and only if
G
belongs to the class of
special
2
-threshold
graphs introduced by Hung, Kloks, and Villaamil
[
11
]
. Our proof relies on a new characterization of special
2
-threshold graphs that generalizes the well known characterization of threshold graphs in terms of isolated and dominating vertices. We also present a spanning tree enumeration formula for special
2
-threshold graphs that generalizes the aforementioned formulae for threshold and Ferrers graphs, revealing an unexpected connection between these seemingly unrelated classes of graphs. Our results extend naturally to weighted spanning tree enumerators, generalizing results of Martin and Reiner
[
19
]
and Ehrenborg and van Willigenburg
[
8
]
.

The remaining sections of this article are organized as follows: In Section
2
, we set up some basic definitions and notation from graph theory, survey a number of spanning tree enumeration formulae in the literature, and review several linear algebraic techniques for spanning tree enumeration, which we use to present new short proofs of the spanning tree enumeration formulae for threshold and Ferrers graphs. Section
3
is the main section of this article where we prove that a graph admits a triangular rank-one perturbation if and only if it is special
2
-threshold, and introduce a spanning tree enumeration formula for special
2
-threshold graphs that reduces to the known formulae for the special cases of threshold and Ferrers graphs. This is also where we generalize the characterization of threshold graphs involving isolated and dominating vertices to special
2
-threshold graphs. We conclude the article with weighted versions of our main results in Section
4
.

2.1.1.
Complete Graphs & Generalizations

The first class of graphs we consider in this article are the complete graphs. A graph is
complete
if every pair of distinct vertices forms an edge. A complete graph on
n

K_{n}
. The graph illustrated in Figure

K_{4}

is

if
V
can be partitioned into (disjoint) subsets
V_{1},\ldots,V_{k}
such that two vertices form an edge if and only if they belong to different subsets. Such a graph is customarily denoted by
K_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{k}}
where
n_{i}=|V_{i}|
for
1\leq i\leq k
. The graph illustrated in Figure

2.1.2.
Threshold Graphs & Generalizations

To introduce the next class of graphs, we must first define isolated and dominating vertices. A vertex is
isolated
in a graph
G
if it is not contained in any edges of
G
and
dominating
in
G
if it forms an edge with every other vertex in
G
. A graph
G
is
threshold
if every induced subgraph contains an isolated or dominating vertex. Threshold graphs were first introduced by Chvátal and Hammer in
[
7
]
, and they have many equivalent characterizations
[
18
]
.

One of the best known characterizations is that every threshold graph can be constructed from an initial vertex by repeatedly appending either an isolated vertex or a dominating vertex. To be more precise, given an ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of the vertices of a graph
G
, the
lower neighborhood
of the vertex
v_{i}
, denoted by
N^{<}(v_{i})
, is the set of all vertices adjacent to
v_{i}
with an index lower than
i,
that is,
N^{<}(v_{i})=N(v_{i})\cap\{v_{1},\dots,v_{i-1}\}.

Theorem 2.1

(
[
7
]
)
.

A graph
G
on
n
vertices is threshold if and only if its vertices can be ordered
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
such that

N^{<}(v_{i})=\emptyset\quad\text{or}\quad N^{<}(v_{i})=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}

for every
i\in[n]
.

We call such an ordering of the vertices of a threshold graph a
construction order
.

A connected bipartite graph is Ferrers if and only if it does not contain an induced matching, i.e., an induced subgraph isomorphic to

Proposition 2.5

Every Ferrers graph is special

Proof.

Let

2.2.
Spanning Tree Enumeration

There are numerous contexts (e.g., see
[
2
]
or
[
17
]
) in which it is valuable to know the number of spanning trees
\tau(G)
in a graph
G
. Calculating
\tau(G)
, however, is not as trivial as one might expect. For instance, there is no obvious systematic way to determine that the graph in Figure
1
(a) has
11
spanning trees without listing them out, as shown in Figure
3
. For certain classes of graphs, however, it is possible to determine the number of spanning trees from the number of vertices in a graph or the degrees of its vertices.

We proceed to survey several well known spanning tree enumeration formulae from throughout the literature beginning with Cayley’s Formula for the number of spanning trees in a complete graph. While commonly attributed to Cayley
[
5
]
, this formula was first proved by Borchardt
[
4
]
and has since seen numerous different proofs throughout the enumerative combinatorics literature. See the books by Aigner and Ziegler
[
1
]
and Stanley
[
24
]
for more details.

Theorem 2.6

(Cayley’s Formula)
.

The number of spanning trees in the complete graph on
n
vertices is

\tau(K_{n})=n^{n-2}.

Example 2.5
.

By Theorem
2.6
, the complete graph on
4
vertices has
\tau(K_{4})=4^{4-2}=16
spanning trees, which we illustrate in Figure
10
of the appendix.

Cayley’s Formula has been generalized to complete multipartite graphs with different proofs by Austin
[
3
]
, Klee and Stamps
[
14
]
, Lewis
[
15
]
, and Onodera
[
22
]
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
n_{1},\dots,n_{k}
be positive integers and let
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{k}.
The number of spanning trees in the complete multipartite graph
K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}}
is

\tau(K_{n_{1},\dots,n_{k}})=n^{k-2}\prod_{i=1}^{k}(n-n_{i})^{n_{i}-1}.

In the special case of complete bipartite graphs, the formula in Theorem
2.7
can be simplified with distinct proofs by Hartsfield and Werth
[
10
]
and Scoins
[
23
]
.

Theorem 2.8
.

The number of spanning trees in the complete bipartite graph
K_{m,n}
is

\tau(K_{m,n})=m^{n-1}n^{m-1}.

Example 2.6
.

By Theorem
2.8
, the complete bipartite graph
K_{2,3}
has
\tau(K_{2,3})=2^{3-1}\cdot 3^{2-1}=12
spanning trees, which we illustrate in Figure
11
of the appendix.

The following formula for the number of spanning trees in a threshold graph in terms of its vertex degrees has various proofs by Chestnut and Fishkind
[
6
]
, Hammer and Kelmans
[
9
]
, Klee and Stamps
[
14
]
, and Merris
[
20
]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a threshold graph with
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. Then

\tau(G)=\frac{\prod_{v_{i}\in D}\left(\deg(v_{i})+1\right)\prod_{v_{j}\in I}\deg(v_{j})}{n}

where
D,I\subseteq V
are the subsets of dominating and isolated vertices, respectively, in
G
.

Example 2.7
.

By Theorem
2.9
, the threshold graph

Ferrers graphs have a similarly beautiful formula, first shown by Ehrenborg and van Willigenburg
[

]
with a different proof by Klee and Stamps

.

Theorem 2.10
.

Let

be a Ferrers graph whose vertex set is partitioned as

and

\tau(G)=\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{m}\deg(r_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}\deg(c_{j})}{m\cdot n}.

2.3.
Linear Algebraic Techniques

It can be very convenient to encode the information of a graph in a matrix. The
Laplacian matrix
of a graph
G=(V,E)
with
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
is the
n\times n
matrix
L=L(G)
with entries given by

L(i,j)=\begin{cases}\deg(v_{i})\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if }i=j,\\
-1\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{if }\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E,\\
0\hskip 14.22636pt&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

The powerful and elegant Matrix-Tree Theorem of Kirchhoff
[
12
]
asserts that the number of spanning trees in a graph is given by any cofactor of its Laplacian matrix.

Theorem 2.11

(Matrix-Tree Theorem)
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with Laplacian matrix
L
. Then

\tau(G)=(-1)^{i+j}\det(L_{i,j})

for any
1\leq i,j\leq n
where
L_{i,j}
denotes the
(n-1)\times(n-1)
matrix obtained by deleting the
i
th row and
j
th column from
L
.

Example 2.9
.

The Laplacian matrix of the graph
G
in Figure

-1&0&0&2&-1&0\\

and the Matrix-Tree Theorem, applied with

, asserts that

Proof of Theorem
2.9
.

Let
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
be the ordering of vertices in
V
given by the construction order of
G
. We claim that the rank-one perturbation
L(G)+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
, where
\mathbf{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the indicator vector of the dominating vertices in
G
, that is,

a_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }v_{i}\in D,\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

and
\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the all-ones vector, is upper triangular. To see why, observe that for
1\leq j<i\leq n
,
(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T})(i,j)=1
if and only if
v_{i}
is a dominating vertex if and only if
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E
if and only if
L(i,j)=-1
.
Since
L(G)+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
is upper triangular, its determinant is simply the product of its diagonal entries, which are

\left(L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}\right)\left(i,i\right)=\begin{cases}\deg\left(v_{i}\right)+1&\text{if }v_{i}\in D,\\
\deg\left(v_{i}\right)&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Thus,

\det\left(L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}\right)=\deg(v_{1})\cdot\prod_{i\in D}\left(\deg(v_{i})+1\right)\cdot\prod_{i\in I}\deg(v_{i}).

Since
N(v_{1})=D
,
\deg(v_{1})=|D|=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
, and the desired expression for
\tau(G)
follows directly from Lemma
2.12
.
∎

This is the main section of our article where we prove that a graph Laplacian admits a triangular rank-one perturbation if and only if the graph is special

Theorem 3.1

-threshold.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

Starting with the reverse direction, let
G=(V,E)
be a special
2

3.2
, there exists a construction order
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
of

for some

. Then the rank-one perturbation
L(G)+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
where
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
are the indicator vectors of the subset

of
U

U

a_{i}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }v_{i}\in D,\\

0&\text{else},\end{cases}

is upper triangular since for

(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T})(i,j)=1
if and only if

It remains to prove Theorem
3.2
. Since every special
2
-threshold graph

\preceq
on

from threshold to special

-threshold graphs. Let

\{v,w\}\notin E
. Since there is no edge between any pair of vertices in
U^{c}
,
U^{c}
is an independent set in
G
.

For part (b), suppose
v,w\in U^{c}
such that
N(v)\neq\emptyset
and
N(w)\neq\emptyset
, and consider the induced subgraph
G[W]
where
W=\{v,w\}\cup N(v)\cup N(w)
. By part (a),
N(v)\cup N(w)\subseteq U
, which means
N(v)\cup N(w)=U\cap W
. Since every vertex in
N(v)\cup N(w)
is adjacent to
v
or
w
,
G[W]
does not have any isolated vertices. Moreover,
N_{W}(x)\neq(W\setminus\{x\})\cap U
for every
x\in N(v)\cup N(w)
. Therefore, since
G
is
U
-threshold, it must be the case that
N(v)=U\cap W=N(v)\cup N(w)
or
N(w)=U\cap W=N(v)\cup N(w)
, which means
N(v)\subseteq N(w)
or
N(w)\subseteq N(v)
.

For part (c), let
v,w\in U
such that
N_{U^{c}}(v)\neq\emptyset
and
N_{U^{c}}(w)\neq\emptyset
, and consider the induced subgraph
G[W]
with
W=\{v,w\}\cup(N_{U^{c}}(v)\cup N_{U^{c}}(w))\setminus(N_{U^{c}}(v)\cap N_{U^{c}}(w))
. Since every vertex in
W\setminus\{v,w\}
is adjacent to exactly one of
v
or
w
, it must be the case that one of
v
or
w
is an isolated or
U
-dominating vertex in
G[W]
, which implies that one of
v
or
w
is not adjacent to any vertices in
W\setminus\{v,w\}
. Therefore,
N_{U^{c}}(v)\subseteq N_{U^{c}}(w)
or
N_{U^{c}}(w)\subseteq N_{U^{c}}(v)
.

For part (d), let
v,w\in U
such that
v\prec w
in the induced threshold graph
G[U]
. By part (c), either
N_{U^{c}}(v)\subseteq N_{U^{c}}(w)
or
N_{U^{c}}(v)\supseteq N_{U^{c}}(w)
. Suppose for contradiction that
N_{U^{c}}(v)\subsetneq N_{U^{c}}(w)
and let
z\in N_{U^{c}}(w)\setminus N_{U^{c}}(v)
. There are two cases to consider based on whether or not
v
and
w
are adjacent: either (i)
\{v,w\}\in E
and there exists
u\in U
such that
\{u,v\}\notin E
and
\{u,w\}\in E
or (ii)
\{v,w\}\notin E
and there exists
u\in U
such that
\{u,v\}\in E
and
\{u,w\}\notin E
. For each case, it is straightforward to check that the induced subgraph
G[W]
on
W=\{v,w,z,u\}
fails to be
U
-threshold, which contradicts the assumptions that
G
is
U
-threshold and there exists
z\in N_{U^{c}}(w)\setminus N_{U^{c}}(v)
. It must, therefore, be the case that
N_{U^{c}}(v)\supseteq N_{U^{c}}(w)
.
∎

Proposition 3.5
.

If
G=(V,E)
is a
U
-threshold graph for some
U\subseteq V
, then the relation
\trianglelefteq
is a total order on
V/\sim
.

Proof.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
U
-threshold graph for some
U\subseteq V
. We will show that
\trianglelefteq
is reflexive, antisymmetric, transitive, and total on
V/\sim
.

•

(Reflexivity) Suppose
x,y\in V
such that
x\sim y
. Then
\deg_{U}(x)=\deg_{U}(y)
,
\deg_{U^{c}}(x)=\deg_{U^{c}}(y)
, and either
x,y\in U
or
x,y\in U^{c}
. Since
x\sim y
in
V
implies
x\sim y
in
U
for any
x,y\in U
, it follows that
x\trianglelefteq y
in
V
.

•

(Antisymmetry) Suppose
x,y\in V
such that
x\trianglelefteq y
and
x\trianglerighteq y
. Then
x,y\in U
or
x,y\in U^{c}
; otherwise,
\{x,y\}\in E
and
\{x,y\}\notin E
, which is impossible. If
x,y\in U^{c}
, then
\deg_{U}(x)=\deg_{U}(y)
by the definition of
\trianglelefteq
and
\deg_{U^{c}}(x)=\deg_{U^{c}}(y)=0
by Lemma
3.4
(a). Similarly, if
x,y\in U
, then
\deg_{U}(x)=\deg_{U}(y)
and
\deg_{U^{c}}(x)=\deg_{U^{c}}(y)
by the definitions of
\sim
on
U
and
\trianglelefteq
. In either case,
x\sim y
in
V
.

•

(Transitivity) Suppose
x,y,z\in V
such that
x\trianglelefteq y
and
y\trianglelefteq z
. We consider the eight possible combinations of memberships for
x
,
y
, and
z
in
U
and
U^{c}
:

–

If
x,y,z\in U
, then
x\preceq y\preceq z
and
\deg_{U^{c}}(x)\geq\deg_{U^{c}}(y)\geq\deg_{U^{c}}(z)
. Thus,
x\trianglelefteq z
by the transitivity of
\preceq
and
\geq
.

–

If
x,y\in U
and
z\in U^{c}
, then
\deg_{U^{c}}(x)\geq\deg_{U^{c}}(y)
and
\{y,z\}\in E
. By Lemma
3.4
(c),
N_{U^{c}}(x)\supseteq N_{U^{c}}(y)
. Therefore,
z\in N_{U^{c}}(y)\subseteq N_{U^{c}}(x)
, which means
\{x,z\}\in E
and
x\trianglelefteq z
.

–

If
x,z\in U
and
y\in U^{c}
, then
\{x,y\}\in E
and
\{y,z\}\notin E
. Since
y\in N_{U^{c}}(x)
and
y\notin N_{U^{c}}(z)
, and
N_{U^{c}}(x)
and
N_{U^{c}}(z)
are nested by Lemma
3.4
(c), it must be the case that
\deg_{U^{c}}(x)\geq\deg_{U^{c}}(z)
. Moreover,
x\preceq z
by Lemma
3.4
(d), hence
x\trianglelefteq z
.

–

If
y,z\in U
and
x\in U^{c}
, then
\{x,y\}\notin E
and
\deg_{U^{c}}(y)\geq\deg_{U^{c}}(z)
. By Lemma
3.4
(c),
N_{U^{c}}(y)\supseteq N_{U^{c}}(z)
. Therefore,
x\notin N_{U^{c}}(y)\supseteq N_{U^{c}}(z)
, which means
\{x,z\}\notin E
and
x\trianglelefteq z
.

–

If
x\in U
and
y,z\in U^{c}
, then
\{x,y\}\in E
and
\deg_{U}(y)\leq\deg_{U}(z)
. By Lemma
3.4
(a-b),
N(y)\subseteq N(z)
. Therefore,
x\in N(y)\subseteq N(z)
, which means
\{x,z\}\in E
and
x\trianglelefteq z
.

–

If
y\in U
and
x,z\in U^{c}
, then
\{x,y\}\notin E
and
\{y,z\}\in E
.
Since
y\notin N(x)
and
y\in N(z)
, and
N(x)
and
N(z)
are nested by Lemma
3.4
(a-b), it must be the case that
\deg_{U}(x)\subseteq\deg_{U}(z)
. Thus,
x\trianglelefteq z
.

–

If
z\in U
and
x,y\in U^{c}
, then
\deg_{U}(x)\leq\deg_{U}(y)
and
\{y,z\}\notin E
. By Lemma
3.4
(a-b),
N(x)\subseteq N(y)
. Therefore,
z\notin N(y)\supseteq N(x)
, which means
\{x,z\}\notin E
and
x\trianglelefteq z
.

–

If
x,y,z\in U^{c}
, then
\deg_{U}(x)\leq\deg_{U}(y)\leq\deg_{U}(z)
, and
x\trianglelefteq z
by the transitivity of
\leq
.

•

(Totality) Suppose
x,y\in V
. If exactly one of
x
and
y
belongs to
U
, then
x\trianglelefteq y
or
x\trianglerighteq y
since
\{x,y\}\in E
or
\{x,y\}\notin E
. If
x,y\in U^{c}
, then
x\trianglelefteq y
or
x\trianglerighteq y
by Lemma
3.4
(b). If
x,y\in U
, then
x\preceq y
or
x\succeq y
since
\preceq
is a total order on
U/\sim
, and
x\trianglelefteq y
or
x\trianglerighteq y
by Lemma
3.4
(c-d).

Therefore, the binary relation
\trianglelefteq
is a total order on
V/\sim
.
∎

We now have all the necessary tools to prove Theorem
3.2
.

Proof of Theorem
3.2
.

Starting with the forward direction, let
G=(V,E)
be a special
2
-threshold graph on
n
vertices. Then there exists
U\subseteq V
such that
G
is
U
-threshold (Remark
2.2
). We claim that any ordering of
V
obtained from
\trianglelefteq
on
V/\sim
with the elements of each equivalence class inserted consecutively satisfies the desired property. Let
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
be such an ordering and suppose
v_{k}\in V
such that
N^{<}(v_{k})\neq\emptyset
. It suffices to show there is no vertex
v_{j}
with
j<k
such that

(i)
v_{j}\in U^{c}
and
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E
or  (ii)
v_{j}\in U
and
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\notin E
.

For (i), Lemma
3.4
(a) implies that
v_{k}\in U
, which means
v_{j}\triangleright v_{k}
and the ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
could not have arisen as a refinement of
\trianglelefteq
. For (ii), our assumption that
N^{<}(v_{k})\neq\emptyset
and (i) imply that
v_{k}\in U^{c}
; otherwise,
v_{k}
would be a dominating vertex in
G[U]
, which would imply
\{v_{j},v_{k}\}\in E
. Since
v_{k}\in U^{c}
,
v_{j}\triangleright v_{k}
and the ordering
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
could not have arisen as a refinement of
\trianglelefteq
.

For the reverse direction, suppose a graph
G
has a construction order
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
for some
U\subseteq V
. Let
W\subseteq V
and consider the highest index vertex
v_{i}
in
W
. Then
W\subseteq\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i}\}
, which means
N_{W}(v_{i})=N^{<}(v_{i})\cap W.
Since
N^{<}(v_{i})=\emptyset
or
N^{<}(v_{i})=U\cap\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}
, it must be the case that
N_{W}(v_{i})=\emptyset
or
N_{W}(v_{i})=(W\setminus v_{i})\cap U
. Therefore,
v_{i}
is either isolated or
U
-dominating in
G[W]
, which means
G
is special
2
-threshold.
∎

We conclude this section with an extension of Proposition
2.5
that emerges as a straightforward corollary of Lemma
3.4
. While a threshold graph is a
U
-threshold graph where
U
is its entire vertex set, a Ferrers graph is a connected
U
-threshold graph where
U
is an independent set.

Corollary 3.6
.

A connected
U
-threshold graph is Ferrers if and only if
U
is independent.

Proof.

A Ferrers graph
G=(V,E)
with
V=R\sqcup C
is
U
-threshold with
U=C
by Proposition
2.5
. Therefore, it suffices to show that a connected
U
-threshold graph is Ferrers whenever
U
is independent.
Let
G=(V,E)
be a
U
-threshold graph such that
U
is an independent set. By Lemma
3.4
(a),
U^{c}
is also independent, so
G
is bipartite with
V=U\sqcup U^{c}
. By Lemma
3.4
(b), the neighborhoods of the vertices in
U^{c}
are nested. Finally, since
U
is independent, the neighborhoods of the vertices in
U
are equal to their restrictions to
U^{c}
, which means they are nested by Lemma
3.4
(c). Ordering the vertices in each part,
U
and
U^{c}
, by neighborhood containment, one can check that
G
is Ferrers.
∎

4.
Weighted Results

This is the final section of the article where we present weighted analogues to our main results for edge weighted graphs. An
edge weighting
of a graph
G=(V,E)
is a function
\omega:E\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that assigns a numerical
weight
to each edge
e\in E
. When the vertices of
e
are specified, i.e.,
e=\{v_{i},v_{j}\}
for some
v_{i},v_{j}\in V
, we abbreviate
\omega(\{v_{i},v_{j}\})
to
\omega_{i,j}
. For a given edge weighting
\omega
of
G
, the weighted analogues of degree, Laplacian matrix, and spanning tree enumerator are defined as follows:

•

The
weighted degree
of a vertex
v_{i}\in V
is given by

\deg(v_{i};\omega)=\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}\omega_{i,j}=\sum_{\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E}\omega_{i,j}.

•

The
weighted Laplacian matrix

L(G;\omega)
is the
|V|\times|V|
matrix whose entries are given by

L(G;\omega)(i,j)=\begin{cases}\deg(v_{i};\omega)&\text{ if }i=j,\\
-\omega_{i,j}&\text{ if }\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E,\\
0&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

•

The
weighted spanning tree enumerator
of
G
is given by

\tau(G;\omega)=\sum_{T\in\mathcal{T}(G)}\prod_{e\in E(T)}\omega(e),

where
\mathcal{T}(G)
denotes the set of all spanning trees in
G
.

It is straightforward to check that the unweighted analogues of these objects are recovered from the special case where all the weights are equal to
1
.

Weighted spanning tree enumeration formulae for various classes of graphs are well-established in the literature. For instance, the weighted version of Cayley’s Formula (Theorem
2.6
) is known as the Cayley-Prüfer Theorem
[
21
]
.

Theorem 4.1

(Cayley-Prüfer Theorem)
.

Let the edges of a complete graph on
n
vertices be weighted by
\omega_{i,j}=x_{i}x_{j}
for some indeterminates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
. Then

\tau(K_{n};\omega)=\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\right)\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\right)^{n-2}.

The following weighted version of Theorem
2.9
for threshold graphs is a special case of a result by Martin and Reiner
[
19
, Theorem 4, Equation (11)]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a threshold graph with
V=\{v_{1},\dots,v_{n}\}
and edge weighting
\omega_{i,j}=x_{i}x_{j}
for some indeterminates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}.
Then

\tau(G;\omega)=\frac{\displaystyle\left(\prod_{k=1}^{n}x_{k}\right)\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in D}\left(x_{i}+\sum_{v_{k}\in N(v_{i})}x_{k}\right)\right)\left(\prod_{v_{j}\in I}\sum_{v_{k}\in N(v_{j})}x_{k}\right)}{\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}},

where
D,I\subseteq V
are the subsets of dominating and isolated vertices in
G
, respectively.

Ehrenborg and van Willigenburg
[
8
]
proved a weighted version of Theorem
2.10
for Ferrers graphs. Their formula makes use of the notion of a conjugate partition: If
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m})
is an integer partition, then its
conjugate partition
is
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})
where
n=\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}
counts the number of parts
\lambda_{i}
with
\lambda_{i}\geq j
for
1\leq j\leq n
. For example, the conjugate partition of
(3,2,2,1)
is
(4,3,1)
. To visualize the relationship between a general partition
\lambda
and its conjugate
\lambda^{\prime}
, observe that
\lambda_{i}
counts the number of boxes in the
i
th row in the Ferrers diagram of
\lambda
whereas
\lambda_{j}^{\prime}
counts the number of boxes in the
j
th column. Alternatively, the Ferrers diagram of
\lambda^{\prime}
is obtained by reflecting the Ferrers diagram of
\lambda
about the diagonal line through its upper leftmost box.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a Ferrers graph with
V=R\sqcup C
,
|R|=m
,
|C|=n
, and edge weighting
\omega_{i,j}=x_{i}y_{j}
for some indeterminates
x_{1},\dots,x_{m},y_{1},\dots,y_{n}.
Moreover, let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\dots,\lambda_{m})
be the integer partition corresponding to the Ferrers diagram of
G
. Then

\tau(G;\omega)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{m}x_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{j=1}^{n}y_{j}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{i=2}^{m}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\lambda_{i}}y_{k}\right)\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{j=2}^{n}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{\lambda_{j}^{\prime}}x_{k}\right)\right)

where
\lambda^{\prime}=(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime},\dots,\lambda_{n}^{\prime})
is the conjugate partition of
\lambda
.

Klee and Stamps
[
13
]
presented straightforward proofs for the weighted versions of all the formulae in Section
2.2
using the following weighted analogue to Lemma
2.12
.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices with edge weighting
\omega
and weighted Laplacian matrix
L=L(G;\omega)
, and let
\mathbf{a}=\langle a_{i}\rangle_{i\in[n]}
and
\mathbf{b}=\langle b_{i}\rangle_{i\in[n]}
be column vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then

\det\left(L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}\right)=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}\right)\cdot\tau(G;\omega).

Next, we prove a weighted version of Theorem
3.1
with which we can apply Lemma
4.4
to prove a weighted spanning tree enumeration formula for special
2
-threshold graphs that generalizes Theorems
4.2
and
4.3
in the same way Theorem
3.3
generalizes Theorems
2.9
and
2.10
.

Theorem 4.5
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph on
n
vertices whose edges are weighted by
\omega_{i,j}=x_{i}x_{j}
for some indeterminates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}.
There exists an upper-triangular rank-one perturbation of the weighted Laplacian matrix of
G
if and only if
G
is special
2
-threshold.

Proof.

Starting with the reverse direction, let
G=(V,E)
be a special
2
-threshold graph on
n
vertices. By Theorem
3.2
, there exists a construction order
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
for some
U\subseteq V
. Let
L=L(G,\omega)
be the weighted Laplacian matrix of
G
with respect to that order, and let
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be defined by

a_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{if }v_{i}\in D,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}\qquad b_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{if }v_{i}\in U,\\
0&\text{else}.\end{cases}

Then
L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
is upper triangular since for
1\leq j<i\leq n
,
(\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T})(i,j)=x_{i}x_{j}
if and only if
v_{i}\in D
and
v_{j}\in U
if and only if
\{v_{i},v_{j}\}\in E
if and only if
L(i,j)=-x_{i}x_{j}
.

For the forward direction, suppose
G=(V,E)
is a graph with
V=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
and edge weights
\omega_{i,j}=x_{i}x_{j}
for some indeterminates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, such that
L=L(G,\omega)
admits an upper triangular rank-one perturbation, that is, there exists a rank-one matrix
M\in R^{n\times n}
such that
L+M
is upper-triangular. Since
M
is rank-one, it can be factored into an outer product
M=\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
of two column vectors
\mathbf{a}=\langle a_{i}\rangle
and
\mathbf{b}=\langle b_{i}\rangle
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Moreover, since
L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
is upper triangular and
L(i,j)\in\{-x_{i}x_{j},0\}
for
1\leq j<i\leq n
, it follows that
M(i,j)\in\{x_{i}x_{j},0\}
for
1\leq j<i\leq n
, and we may assume without loss of generality that
M\in\{0,x_{i}x_{j}\}^{n\times n}
and
a_{i},b_{i}\in\{0,x_{i}\}
for
1\leq i\leq n
. Let
D=\{v_{i}\in V\mid a_{i}=x_{i}\}
and
U=\{v_{i}\in V\mid b_{i}=x_{i}\}
. If
v_{i}\in V\setminus D
, then
a_{i}=0
, which means
L(i,j)=M(i,j)=0
for
1\leq j<i\leq n
, hence
N^{<}(v_{i})=\emptyset.
If
v_{i}\in D
, on the other hand, then
a_{i}=x_{i}
, which means

M(i,j)=\begin{cases}x_{i}x_{j}&\text{if }v_{j}\in U,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad L(G)(i,j)=\begin{cases}-x_{i}x_{j}&\text{if }v_{j}\in U,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

hence
N^{<}(v_{i})=U\cap\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{i-1}\}.
Therefore,
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
is a construction order with respect to
U
, and
G
is special
2
-threshold by Theorem
3.2
.
∎

With this, we can prove the following weighted version of Theorem
3.3
.

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
G=(V,E)
be a
U
-threshold graph with construction order
v_{1},\dots,v_{n}
and edge weighting
\omega_{i,j}=x_{i}x_{j}
for some indeterminates
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
. Then

\tau(G,\omega)=\frac{\displaystyle\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in V}x_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in D_{U}}\left(x_{i}+\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{j}\right)\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in V\setminus D_{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{j}\right)}{\displaystyle\left(\sum_{v_{i}\in D}x_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\sum_{v_{i}\in U}x_{i}\right)},

where
D\subseteq V
is the set of
U
-dominating vertices in the construction of
G,
and
D_{U}=D\cap U
.

Proof.

From the proof of Theorem
4.5
, the rank-one perturbation
L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
where

a_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{if }v_{i}\in D,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}\qquad b_{i}=\begin{cases}x_{i}&\text{if }v_{i}\in U,\\
0&\text{else},\end{cases}

is upper triangular. Note that
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}(i,i)=0
for
v_{i}\notin D_{U}
and
\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}(i,i)=x_{i}^{2}
for
v_{i}\in D_{U}.
Thus, the diagonal entries of
L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
are given by

\left(L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}\right)\left(i,i\right)=\begin{cases}\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{i}x_{j}+x_{i}^{2}&\text{if }v_{i}\in D_{U},\\
\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{i}x_{j}&\text{else.}\end{cases}

Since
L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}
is upper triangular,

\begin{split}\det\left(L+\mathbf{a}\mathbf{b}^{T}\right)&=\prod_{v_{i}\in D_{U}}\left(\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{i}x_{j}+x_{i}^{2}\right)\cdot\prod_{v_{i}\in V\setminus D_{U}}\left(\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{i}x_{j}\right)\\
&=\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in V}x_{i}\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in D_{U}}\left(x_{i}+\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{j}\right)\right)\cdot\left(\prod_{v_{i}\in V\setminus D_{U}}\sum_{v_{j}\in N(v_{i})}x_{j}\right)\end{split}

By construction, the entries of
\mathbf{a}
and
\mathbf{b}
sum up to

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=\sum_{v_{i}\in D}x_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{i=1}^{n}b_{i}=\sum_{v_{i}\in U}x_{i}.

The desired expression for
\tau(G,\omega)
follows directly from Lemma
4.4
.
∎

To see how Theorem
4.6
provides a common generalization of Theorem
4.2
and Theorem
4.3
, observe the following:

•

If
G=(V,E)
is a threshold graph on
n
vertices, then it is
U
-threshold with
U=V
by Remark
2.1
and
D=N(v_{1})
. Substituting
D_{U}=D
,
V\setminus D_{U}=\{v_{1}\}\cup I
, and

\sum\limits_{v_{i}\in D}x_{i}=\deg(v_{1},\omega)/x_{1}=\sum\limits_{v_{j}\in N(v_{1})}x_{j}

into the formula in Theorem
4.6
– and simplifying appropriately – yields the formula in Theorem
4.2
.

•

If
G=(V,E)
is a Ferrers graph with
V=R\sqcup C
, then it is
U
-threshold with
U=C
by Proposition
2.5
,
D=R
since every vertex in
R
is adjacent to
v_{1}=c_{1}
, and
D_{U}=U\cap D=R\cap C=\emptyset
. Since
N(r_{i})=\{c_{j}\ |\ 1\leq j\leq\lambda_{i}\}
and
N(c_{i})=\{r_{j}\ |\ 1\leq j\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\}
, it follows that

\sum_{c_{j}\in N(r_{i})}y_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda_{i}}y_{j}\quad\text{and}\sum_{r_{j}\in N(c_{i})}x_{j}=\sum_{j=1}^{\lambda^{\prime}_{i}}x_{j}

for every
r_{i}\in R
and
c_{i}\in C
. Moreover,

\sum_{c_{i}\in C}y_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}y_{j}\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{r_{i}\in R}x_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}

since
C=N(r_{1})
,
R=N(c_{1})
,
\lambda_{1}=m
, and
\lambda^{\prime}_{1}=n
. Substituting
D=R
,
U=C
, and the above identities into the formula in Theorem
4.6
– and simplifying appropriately – yields the formula in Theorem
4.3
.
