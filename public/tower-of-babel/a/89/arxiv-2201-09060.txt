Abstract
. We study orbit-finite systems of linear equations, in the setting of sets with atoms. Our principal contribution is a decision procedure for solvability of such systems. The procedure works for every field (and even commutative ring) under mild effectiveness assumptions, and reduces a given orbit-finite system to a number of finite ones: exponentially many in general, but polynomially many when the atom dimension of input systems is fixed. Towards obtaining the procedure we push further the theory of vector spaces generated by orbit-finite sets, and show that each such vector space admits an orbit-finite basis. This fundamental property is a key tool in our development, but should be also of wider interest.



1.
Introduction

Applications of linear algebra, and in particular of systems of linear equations,
are ubiquitous in computer science (see e.g.
(
Colcombet 2015
;
Suárez
et al. 1996
;
Cormen
et al. 2009
)
).
In this paper, motivated by recent and potential future applications to analysis of data-enriched
models
(
Hofman
et al. 2017
;
Hofman and Lasota 2018
;
Gupta
et al. 2019
;
Bojańczyk et al. 2021
)
,
we augment systems of linear equations with
atoms

(
Bojańczyk 2019
;
Pitts 2013
)
(also called data values)
thus shifting from finite to orbit-finite systems.
The infinite sets that we study are constructed using atoms
which can only be accessed in a very limited way, namely can only be tested for equality.

Fix a countably infinite set
\text{\sc Atoms}=\left\{1,2,3,\ldots\right\}
,
whose elements are called
atoms
, assuming that the only
operations on atoms are (dis)equality tests.
As an example, consider pairs of distinct atoms

C=\left\{\,{\alpha}{\beta}\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{2}\;\middle|\;{\alpha}\neq{\beta}\,\right\}

as unknowns (for succinctness, here and in the sequel we write ordered pairs
({\alpha},{\beta})
of atoms as
{\alpha}{\beta}
, and likewise
for triples),
and the infinite system of equations

\displaystyle{\alpha}{\beta}-2{\cdot}{\beta}{\gamma}+{\gamma}{\alpha}\ =\ 1\qquad({\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}\in\text{\sc Atoms},{\alpha}\neq{\beta}\neq{\gamma}\neq{\alpha}).

The system is finitely described by the above formula using only (dis)equalities between atoms,
and therefore is invariant under all permutations of atoms.
Furthermore, up to permutation of atoms the system consists of just one equation – it is one
orbit
;
in the sequel we consider orbit-finite systems (finite unions of orbits).
Each unknown
{\alpha}{\beta}\in C
is determined (
supported
) by 2 atoms (its
atom dimension
is 2)
while each equation by 3 atoms, therefore the atom dimension
of the whole example system is 3.
The example equations are finite, but need not to be so in general.
Our primary goal is to algorithmically test if such a system has a solution, that is
a rational assignment
\mathbf{x}:C\to\mathbb{Q}
, or maybe an integer assignment
\mathbf{x}:C\to\mathbb{Z}
, that satisfies all the equations,
i.e.,

\mathbf{x}({\alpha}{\beta})-2{\cdot}\mathbf{x}({\beta}{\gamma})+\mathbf{x}({\gamma}{\alpha})\ =\ 1

for every
{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{3}
such that
{\alpha}\neq{\beta}\neq{\gamma}\neq{\alpha}
.

We use the language of linear algebra.
For instance, a solution is a vector over
C
(belongs
to the vector space generated by
C
),
and the above system
may be presented as an infinite matrix plus the infinite right-hand side vector:

\displaystyle\qquad\ \ \ \,{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\begin{matrix}12\,&13\,&23\,&34\,&31\,&41\,&42\,&\ldots\end{matrix}}

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0.5,0.5,0.5}\begin{matrix}123\\
234\\
134\\
312\\
\vdots\end{matrix}}\quad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\begin{bmatrix}\ \ 1\ &\ 0\ &\!-2\ &\ 0\ &\ 1\ &\ 0\ &\ 0\ &\ldots\ \ \\

\ \ 0\ &\ 1\ &\ 0\ &\!-2\ &\ 0\ &\ 1\ &\ 0\ &\ldots\ \ \\
\ \!-2\ &\ 0\ &\ 1\ &\ 0\ &\ 1\ &\ 0\ &\ 0\ &\ldots\ \ \\
\ \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\ \ \end{bmatrix}}\qquad{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\begin{bmatrix}\ 1\ \\

The columns of the matrix are indexed by pairs
{\alpha}{\beta}\in C

{\alpha}{\beta}{\gamma}\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{3}
where
{\alpha}\neq{\beta}\neq{\gamma}\neq{\alpha}
.

Contribution.

As the main contribution, we provide an algorithm for solvability of orbit-finite systems of linear equations.
More formally, our algorithm accepts as input a system consisting of an orbit-finite matrix
\mathbf{A}

and a right-hand side vector
\mathbf{t}
,
both
finitely-supported
(i.e., definable using finitely many fixed atoms, hence finitely presentable).
The algorithm checks whether the given system admits a solution which is also finitely-supported (hence
also finitely presentable).

The coefficients in
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{t}
, as well as in the solutions, are assumed to come from an arbitrary fixed
commutative ring
(\mathbb{K},0,1,+,\cdot)

which is assumed to be
effective
:
its elements are finitely representable; equality is decidable for these representations;
ring operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication) are computable using the representations;
and solvability of finite systems over
\mathbb{K}
is decidable.
Examples abound: the rational field
\mathbb{Q}
; the integer ring
\mathbb{Z}
; finite commutative rings;
the field of algebraic numbers; the field of complex numbers.

In brief, the algorithm computes a number of finite systems of linear equations over
\mathbb{K}

and answers positively exactly when all these systems are solvable.
The number of finite systems and their sizes are exponential in general;
however, once the atom dimension of the input system is fixed, the algorithm computes only polynomially many
finite systems of polynomial size.
In particular, for fixed atom dimension we obtain polynomial time procedures
for solvability over
\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{Z}
.
On the way we also provide an algorithm for
finitary
solvability where one only seeks solutions which
assign zero to almost all unknowns.

On the mathematical level, we
push further the theory of orbit-finitely generated vector spaces initiated in
(
Bojańczyk et al. 2021
)
,
in order to obtain a key tool for our algorithmic considerations:
we show that each orbit-finitely generated vector space admits an orbit-finite basis.
We believe that this finding is of independent wider interest.

Numerous applications arise in

2.
Preliminaries on sets with atoms

Our definitions rely on basic notions and results of the theory of
sets with atoms

(
Bojańczyk 2019
)
,
also known as nominal sets
(
Pitts 2013
)
.
We only work with
equality atoms
which have no additional structure except for
the equality.

We fix a countably infinite set
\text{\sc Atoms}=\left\{1,2,3,\ldots\right\}
, whose elements we call
atoms
.
We reserve Greek letters
{\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma},\ldots
to range over atoms.
Informally speaking, a set with atoms is a set that can have atoms, or other sets with atoms,
as elements.
Formally, we define the universe of sets with atoms by a suitably adapted cumulative hierarchy of sets,
by transfinite induction: the only set of
rank
0 is the empty set; and for a cardinal
i
, a set of rank
i
may contain, as elements,
sets of rank smaller than
i
as well as atoms.
In particular, nonempty subsets
X\subseteq\text{\sc Atoms}
have rank 1.

The group
Aut
of all permutations of
Atoms
, called in this paper
atom automorphisms
,
acts on sets with atoms by consistently renaming all atoms in a given set.
Formally, by another transfinite induction, for
\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}
we define
\pi(X)=\left\{\,\pi(x)\;\middle|\;x\in X\,\right\}
.
Via standard set-theoretic encodings of pairs or finite sequences we obtain, in particular,
the pointwise action on pairs
\pi(x,y)=(\pi(x),\pi(y))
,
and likewise on finite sequences. Relations and functions from
X
to
Y
are considered as subsets of
X\times Y
.

We restrict to sets with atoms that only depend on finitely many atoms, in the following sense.
For
S\subseteq\text{\sc Atoms}
, let
\text{\sc Aut}_{S}=\left\{\,\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}\;\middle|\;\pi({\alpha})={\alpha}\text{ for every }{\alpha}\in S\,\right\}
be the set of atom automorphisms
that
fix

S
.
We call elements of
\text{\sc Aut}_{S}

S
-atom automorphisms
.
A
support
of
x
is any finite set
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
(we use the symbol
\subseteq_{\text{fin}}
for finite subsets)
such that for all

\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}_{S}
it holds
\pi(x)=x
.
In this case we also say that
x
is
S
-supported
.
As a special case, a function
f
is supported by
S
if

f(\pi(x))=\pi(f(x))
for every argument
x
and
\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}_{S}
.
An
S
-supported set is also
S^{\prime}
-supported, as long as
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
.
An element (or set)
x
is
finitely supported
if it has some finite support; in this case

x
has
the least support
, denoted
\text{sup}(x)
, called
the support
of
x

(cf.
(
Bojańczyk 2019
, Sect. 6)
).
Sets supported by
\emptyset
we call
equivariant
.

For instance, given
{\alpha},{\beta}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
, the support of the set
\text{\sc Atoms}\setminus\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}
is
\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}
;
in general, a set is
S
-supported if and only if it is invariant under all
S
-atom automorphisms.
The set
\text{\sc Atoms}^{2}
and the projection function
\pi_{1}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{2}\to\text{\sc Atoms}:({\alpha},{\beta})\mapsto{\alpha}

are both equivariant;
and the support of a tuple
({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{n})\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{n}
, encoded as a set in a standard way, is
the set of atoms
\left\{{\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{n}\right\}
appearing in it.

From now on, we shall only consider sets that are hereditarily finitely
supported, i.e., ones that have a finite support, whose every element has some finite support,
and so on.

Orbit-finite sets.

Let
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
. Two atoms or sets with atoms
x,y
are
in the same
S
-orbit
if
\pi(x)=y
for some
\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}_{S}
.
This equivalence relation splits all atoms and sets with atoms
into equivalence classes, which we call
S
-orbits
;

\emptyset
-orbits we call equivariant orbits.
By the very definition, every
S
-orbit
O
is
S
-supported:
\text{sup}(O)\subseteq S
and,
even if the inclusion is strict (which may happen only for singleton orbits),
O
is also a
\text{sup}(O)
-orbit.
When the set
S
is irrelevant, we simply speak of an
orbit
, meaning an
S
-orbit for some
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.

Every
S
-supported set is a union of (necessarily disjoint)
S
-orbits; the set is

orbit-finite
if this union is finite.
Orbit-finiteness is stable under orbit-refinement: if
S\subseteq S^{\prime}
, a finite union of
S
-orbits is also a finite union
of
S^{\prime}
-orbits (but the number of orbits may increase).
Examples of orbit-finite sets are:
Atoms
(1 orbit);

\text{\sc Atoms}-\left\{{\alpha}\right\}
for some
{\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
(1 orbit);

\text{\sc Atoms}^{2}
(2 orbits: diagonal and non-diagonal);

\text{\sc Atoms}^{3}
(5 orbits, corresponding to equality types of triples);
non-repeating
n
-tuples of atoms (1 orbit)

\text{\sc Atoms}^{(n)}=\left\{\,\!({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{n})\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{n}\!\;\middle|\;{\alpha}_{i}\neq{\alpha}_{j}\text{ for all }i\neq j\,\right\}\!;

n
-sets of atoms
{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose n}=\left\{\,X\subseteq\text{\sc Atoms}\;\middle|\;|X|=n\,\right\}
(1 orbit).

The set
\mathcal{P}_{\text{fin}}(\text{\sc Atoms})
of all finite subsets of atoms is orbit-infinite as cardinality is an invariant of each orbit.

Orbit representation.

For a positive integer
k>0
, denote by
\text{\sc S}_{k}
the symmetric group on
\left\{1,\ldots,k\right\}
.
Given a subgroup
G\leq\text{\sc S}_{k}
of the symmetric group
\text{\sc S}_{k}
,
we denote by
\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G

the set of non-repeating
k
-tuples of atom modulo coordinate permutations from the group
G
.
More formally, we define an equivalence in
\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
, where a tuple
a=({\alpha}_{1},\ldots,{\alpha}_{k})\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
is equivalent
to every tuple
a\circ\sigma:=({\alpha}_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,{\alpha}_{\sigma(k)})
, where
\sigma\in G
.
The equivalence classes are thus finite.
Then we define a canonical quotient

\pi_{G}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G

mapping a tuple
a\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
to its equivalence class.

Example 2.1.

Let
k=3
and
G\leq\text{\sc S}_{3}
be generated by the cyclic shift
\sigma
to the right:

\sigma\ =\

\begin{pmatrix}1&2&3\\
2&3&1\end{pmatrix}
.
The quotient
\pi_{G}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(3)}\to\text{\sc Atoms}^{(3)}/G
maps each triple
({\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma})
to

\left\{({\alpha},{\beta},{\gamma}),({\gamma},{\alpha},{\beta}),({\beta},{\gamma},{\alpha})\right\}
.

\triangleleft

Lemma 2.2 (
(
Bojańczyk 2019
)
, Thm. 6.3).

Every equivariant orbit is in equivariant bijection with

\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and some subgroup
G\leq\text{\sc S}_{k}

3.
Orbit-Finite Basis Theorem

Proviso.

Throughout the paper we fix a countable commutative ring
(\mathbb{K},0,1,+,\cdot)
with multiplicative unit
1
,
and assume that the ring is
effective
:
its elements are finitely representable and solvability of finite systems of linear equations is decidable.
As a direct consequence, equality is decidable for the element representations,
and the ring operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication) are
computable using the representations.
The most prominent examples are rationals
\mathbb{Q}
and integers
\mathbb{Z}

Vectors.

We are investigating vector spaces

4.
Solving linear equations

We note that the inner product of two vectors
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
, defined as

\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}\ =\ \sum_{b\in B}\mathbf{x}(b)\,\mathbf{y}(b),

is not always well-defined.
We consider the right-hand side sum as well-defined when there are only finitely many
b\in B

for which both
\mathbf{x}(b)
and
\mathbf{y}(b)
are non-zero
(equivalently, the intersection
\text{dom}(\mathbf{x})\cap\text{dom}(\mathbf{y})
is finite).
In particular, the inner product
\mathbf{x}\cdot\mathbf{y}
is always well-defined when one of
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
is finitary.

Remark 2.

Consider
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
. Since vectors are finitely supported and hence (c.f. Lemma
3.2
) contain only finitely many different numbers,

\text{dom}(\mathbf{x})\cap\text{dom}(\mathbf{y})
is finite exactly when
the right-hand side sum is
unconditionally convergent
,
i.e., convergent to the same value irrespectively of the order in which
the elements
b\in B
are enumerated
3
3

3

We are grateful to Szymon Toruńczyk for attracting our attention to unconditional convergence.
.

\triangleleft

Systems of linear equations.

Fix an orbit-finite set
C
(one can think of
C
as an indexing set of columns of a matrix).
By a linear equation over
C
we mean a pair
e=(\mathbf{a},t)
where

\mathbf{a}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
is a vector of left-hand side coefficients and
t\in\mathbb{K}
is a right-hand side target value.
A solution of
e
is any vector
\mathbf{x}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
such that the inner product

\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is well-defined and equals
t
.
We may consider constrained solutions, e.g.,

finitary
ones.

A system of linear equations is just an indexed set of equations over the same set
C
.
Formally, an orbit-finite system of linear equations (over
C
)
is any finitely-supported function
B\to_{\text{fs}}\text{\sc Lin}(C)\times\mathbb{K}

from some orbit-finite indexing set
B
(one may think of
B
as an indexing set of rows of a matrix).
By projecting to the first component we get a function
\mathbf{A}:B\to_{\text{fs}}\text{\sc Lin}(C)

which we call the
matrix
of the system;
by projecting to the second component (the target)
we get a finitely-supported function
\mathbf{t}:B\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}
, i.e., a vector in
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
,
which we call the
target
of the system.
The representation
B\to_{\text{fs}}\text{\sc Lin}(C)
of the matrix may be equivalently written as
a finitely-supported function
\mathbf{A}:B\times C\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}

(thus
\mathbf{A}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B\times C)
and hence it deserves boldface).

Systems of linear equations, when input to algorithms,
are assumed in the sequel to be given by a matrix-target pair
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{t})
:

\displaystyle\quad\ \ \ \,\begin{matrix}\ \cdots\quad&c&\quad\cdots\ \end{matrix}

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}\vdots\\
b\\
\vdots\end{matrix}\ \ \begin{bmatrix}\ &\vdots&\ \\
\ \cdots&\mathbf{A}(b,c)&\cdots\ \ \\
&\vdots&\ \end{bmatrix}\qquad\begin{bmatrix}\vdots\\
\mathbf{t}(b)\\
\vdots\ \end{bmatrix}

A solution of a system of equations is any vector
\mathbf{x}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
which is a solution of all equations in the system.
Note that
C
can be seen as the indexing set of unknowns of the system.

For
b\in B
we denote by
\mathbf{A}(b,\_)\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
the row vector indexed by
b
,
and symmetrically, for
c\in C
we denote by
\mathbf{A}(\_,c)\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
the column vector indexed by
c
.
One can also consider the
augmented
matrix
\mathbf{A}|\mathbf{t}:B\times(C\uplus\left\{*\right\})\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}
.

In all the examples below let
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
.

Example 4.1.

Let columns be indexed by
C=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(2)}
and rows by
B={\text{\sc Atoms}\choose 2}
.
Consider the system of equations containing,
for every
\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}\in B
, the equation

({\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}{\beta}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{{\beta}{\alpha}},1)
.
Using the formal-sum notation as in (
1
) it may be written as

({\alpha}{\beta}+{\beta}{\alpha},1)
or,
identifying column indexes
{\alpha}{\beta}\in C
with unknowns, as:

{\alpha}{\beta}+{\beta}{\alpha}\ =\ 1\qquad({\alpha},{\beta}\in\text{\sc Atoms},{\alpha}\neq{\beta}).

All the equations are thus finitary, and the target is
\mathbf{t}={\mathbf{1}}_{B}
.
The constant vector
\mathbf{x}={\mathbf{\frac{1}{2}}}:({\alpha},{\beta})\mapsto\frac{1}{2}
is a solution.
The system has no finitary solution, as such a solution is in contradiction with the infinitary target
\mathbf{t}={\mathbf{1}}_{B}
.
Furthermore, the system has no integer (infinitary) solution either, as any such solution
\mathbf{x}
would
necessarily satisfy, for every distinct atoms
{\alpha},{\beta}\in\text{\sc Atoms}\setminus\text{sup}(\mathbf{x})
, the equality
\mathbf{x}({\alpha}{\beta})=\mathbf{x}({\beta}{\alpha})
,
which is in contradiction
with
\mathbf{x}({\alpha}{\beta})+\mathbf{x}({\beta}{\alpha})=1
.

\triangleleft

Example 4.2.

Let
C=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(2)}
,
B=\text{\sc Atoms}
, and
consider the system of equations containing, for every
{\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
, the equation

({\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}\_},1)
.
As before, identifying column indexes
{\alpha}{\beta}\in C
with unknowns, the system may be written as:

\sum_{{\beta}\in\text{\sc Atoms}\setminus\left\{{\alpha}\right\}}\!{\alpha}{\beta}\ =\ 1\qquad({\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}).

All the equations are thus infinitary.
The system has an integer solution.
Take any two fixed atoms
{\gamma},{\delta}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
and consider the vector

\mathbf{x}\ =\ {\mathbf{1}}_{\_{\gamma}}+{\mathbf{1}}_{{\gamma}{\delta}}.

Indeed, for
{\alpha}\neq{\gamma}
we have

{\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}\_}\cdot\mathbf{x}={\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}{\gamma}}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}{\gamma}}=1
as required.
Furthermore, for
{\alpha}={\gamma}
we have

{\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}\_}\cdot\mathbf{x}={\mathbf{1}}_{{\gamma}{\delta}}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{{\gamma}{\delta}}=1
as required.
The system has no finitary solution (essentially for the same reason as in the previous example), and no equivariant one
(as the only equivariant vectors over
C
are constant ones
q\cdot{\mathbf{1}}
, and the inner product

{\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}\_}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}
is ill-defined for every
{\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
as long as
q\neq 0
).

\triangleleft

The two above examples show that the solvability problem is sensitive to additional restrictions on solutions:
the answer changes if solutions are additionally required to be
equivariant, finitary, or integer.
The next example shows that our implicit restriction to finitely-supported solutions also matters:

Example 4.3.

Let
C={\text{\sc Atoms}\choose 2}
,
B=\text{\sc Atoms}
, and
consider the system of equations containing, for every
{\alpha}\in B
, the equation

({\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{{\alpha},\_\right\}},1)
, where

\left\{{\alpha},\_\right\}\ =\ \left\{\,\left\{{\alpha},{\gamma}\right\}\;\middle|\;{\alpha}\neq{\gamma}\in\text{\sc Atoms}\,\right\}

is the set of all 2-sets containing
{\alpha}
.
We argue that the system has no (finitely supported) solution
(despite the apparent similarity to the system in Example
4.2
).
Towards contradiction suppose it has a solution
\mathbf{x}
, supported by some
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.
Thus it is constant on every
S
-orbit in
{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose 2}
.
An infinite
S
-orbit in
{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose 2}
is either the set
{\text{\sc Atoms}\setminus S\choose 2}

of all 2-sets disjoint from
S

or, for some fixed
{\alpha}\in S
, the set of all 2-sets with one element
{\alpha}
and the other element not in
S
:

\left\{\,\left\{{\alpha},{\gamma}\right\}\;\middle|\;{\gamma}\in\text{\sc Atoms}\setminus S\,\right\}

Therefore each infinite
S
-orbit in
{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose 2}
intersects infinitely with
\left\{{\alpha},\_\right\}
for some
{\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
.
In consequence,
\mathbf{x}
is necessarily
0
when restricted to any infinite
S
-orbit in
{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose 2}
as otherwise

{\mathbf{1}}_{\left\{{\alpha},\_\right\}}\cdot\mathbf{x}
would be ill-defined for some
{\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
.
Therefore
\mathbf{x}
is forcedly finitary, and the argument of the previous examples applies.

On the other hand the system would have an integer solution if we drop the implicit finite-support constraint.
For instance, taking any enumeration
\text{\sc Atoms}=\left\{{\alpha}_{0},{\alpha}_{1},{\alpha}_{2},\ldots\right\}
of atoms,
the function
\mathbf{x}:C\to\mathbb{K}
that maps each set

\left\{{\alpha}_{2n},{\alpha}_{2n+1}\right\}
to
1
, for
n=0,1,\ldots
, and all other sets to
0
, satisfies all equations.
Note that
\mathbf{x}
is not finitely supported, i.e., there is no finite
S\subseteq\text{\sc Atoms}
such that

\pi(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
for all
\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}_{S}
.

\triangleleft

Solvability of linear equations.

We investigate the following type of solvability problems:

Solv
(\mathbb{K})
:

Input::

an orbit-finite system of linear equations.

Question::

does it have a solution?

As our main result we prove:

Theorem 4.4.

Solv
(\mathbb{K})
is decidable for every fixed effective commutative ring
\mathbb{K}
.

The proof, occupying the whole Sections
6
and
7
,
is by a reduction to solvability of finite systems of linear equations, and
the transformation suffers from a singly-exponential blowup.
As an intermediate step
we also consider a variant of the problem where solutions are constrained to be finitary, called

Fin-Solv
(\mathbb{K})
.

Remark 3.

In case
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
, when coefficients in the input system are rational and we seek for rational solutions,
as a corollary of the proof we deduce that
the answer does not change if solutions are relaxed to real ones.

\triangleleft

Spans.

For a subset
P\subseteq\text{\sc Lin}(B)
, we define
\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)\subseteq\text{\sc Lin}(B)
as the set of
all linear combinations of vectors from
P
, forming a subspace of
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)\ =\ \ &\{\,q_{1}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{1}+\ldots+q_{k}\cdot\mathbf{p}_{k}\;|\;k\geq 0,\\
&\,\,\ q_{1},\ldots,q_{k}\in\mathbb{K},\ \mathbf{p}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{p}_{k}\in P\,\}.\end{aligned}

Given a matrix
\mathbf{A}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B\times C)
with rows
B
and columns
C
, we can define a partial operation of
multiplication of
\mathbf{A}
by a vector
\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
in an expected way:

(\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v})(b)=\mathbf{A}(b,\_)\cdot\mathbf{v}

for every
b\in B
.
The result
\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
is well-defined if
\mathbf{A}(b,\_)\cdot\mathbf{v}
is well-defined for all
b\in B
.
The multiplication
\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}
can be also seen as an orbit-finite linear combination of
column vectors
\mathbf{A}(\_,c)
, for
c\in C
, with coefficients given by
\mathbf{v}
.
This allows us to define the span of
\mathbf{A}
seen as a
C
-indexed orbit-finite set of
vectors
\mathbf{A}(\_,c)\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
:

(3)

\displaystyle\text{\sc Span}(\mathbf{A}):=\left\{\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C),\ \mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}\text{ well-defined}\,\right\}.

The solvability problem for a system of equations
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{t})
amounts thus to deciding
if
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Span}(\mathbf{A})
.
When
\mathbf{v}
is finitary, well-definedness is vacuous, and we may define:

\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\mathbf{A})\ :=\ \left\{\,\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(C)\,\right\}\ =\ \text{\sc Fin-Span}(P),

for
P=\left\{\,\mathbf{A}(\_,c)\;\middle|\;c\in C\,\right\}
the set of column vectors of
\mathbf{A}
.

We start by proving the Orbit-Finite Basis Theorem in Section

Then we prove decidability of

of classical finite systems of linear equations.
This step relies on a generalisation of
cogs
introduced in
(
Bojańczyk et al. 2021

Hofman and

)
.
Finally,
in Section

we reduce
Solv
(\mathbb{K})
to

(\mathbb{K})

thus completing the proof of Theorem
4.4
.
This part strongly relies again on the technology developed in Section

.

5.
Proof of the Orbit-Finite Basis Theorem

In this section we prove Theorem
3.3
,
i.e., provide a construction of an orbit-finite basis in
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
, where
B
is an arbitrary orbit-finite set.

Preliminaries.

The mapping
x\mapsto\text{sup}(x)
is equivariant:

Claim 1.

\text{sup}(\pi(x))=\pi(\text{sup}(x))
for every element
x
and
\pi\in\text{\sc Aut}
.

We rely on the following basic properties of orbits:

Claim 2.

Let
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.
Each equivariant orbit
O

contains at most
|S|!
many elements
x
with
\text{sup}(x)=S
.

Claim 3.

Every orbit is either a singleton or an infinite set.

Definition 5.1.

Let
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.
We define the
S
-atom dimension
of an
S
-orbit
O
, written

S\text{-dim}(O)
, as the size of
\text{sup}(x)
for some (every) element
x\in O
, but not counting elements of
S
:

S\text{-dim}(O)\ :=\ |\text{sup}(x)\setminus S|.

The choice of
x
is irrelevant due to Claim
1
.
When
S
is clear from the context we omit
S
and speak of
atom dimension
.

Reduction to single-orbit
B
.

We claim that we can assume, w.l.o.g., that
B
is a single orbit.
Indeed, let
T=\text{sup}(B)
and let
B=B_{1}\uplus\dots\uplus B_{n}
be the partition into
T
-orbits.
Then
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
is isomorphic to the Cartesian product
\text{\sc Lin}(B_{1})\times\dots\times\text{\sc Lin}(B_{n})
.
Denote by
\iota_{i}:\text{\sc Lin}(B_{i})\to\text{\sc Lin}(B)
the natural embedding that extends a vector
\mathbf{v}:B_{i}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}

by 0 for all
b\in B\setminus B_{i}
:

\iota_{i}(\mathbf{v})(b)\ :=\ \begin{cases}\mathbf{v}(b)&\text{ if }b\in B_{i}\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Supposing we have orbit-finite bases
\widehat{B_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{B_{n}}
of the vector spaces
\text{\sc Lin}(B_{1}),\ldots,\text{\sc Lin}(B_{n})
, respectively,
we get the basis
\widehat{B}
of
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
as the union of embeddings of
\widehat{B_{1}},\ldots,\widehat{B_{n}}
:

\iota_{1}(\widehat{B_{1}})\ \cup\ \ldots\ \cup\ \iota_{n}(\widehat{B_{n}}).

We thus assume w.l.o.g. that
B
is a single
T
-orbit.

As the support of a function is also a support (but not necessarily the support) of its domain, we note:

Claim 4.

T\subseteq\text{sup}(\mathbf{v})
for every vector
\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
.

Tight orbits.

A key role is played in the proof by the concept of

orbits.

Definition 5.2.

Let
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.
An
S
-orbit

is called

if
S\subseteq\text{sup}(x)
for every
x\in O
.

In particular, every singleton is a tight orbit.

Example 5.3.

Recall Example
3.4
.
In case of

, the tight orbits

are the following ones:

B\qquad{\alpha}\_\qquad\_{\beta}\qquad\left\{{\alpha}{\beta}\right\}

where
{\alpha},{\beta}
range over atoms and
{\alpha}\neq{\beta}
.
The set
B
is an equivariant orbit,
{\alpha}\_
is an
\{{\alpha}\}
-orbit,
\_{\beta}
is a
\{b\}
-orbit,
and
\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}
is an
\{{\alpha},{\beta}\}
-orbit.
Contrarily, for two fixed and distinct

, the
\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}
-orbit

\neq\!\!{\alpha}{\beta}\ =\ \left\{\,{\gamma}{\beta}\;\middle|\;{\gamma}\notin\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}\,\right\},

is not tight.

\triangleleft

W.l.o.g. we can assume that
B
is tight, i.e.,
T\subseteq\text{sup}(b)
for every
b\in B
.
Indeed, it is
sufficient to continue with

. Then
B^{\prime}
and

are related by a

-supported bijection.
For future use we state:

Claim 5.

Let
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.
Every
S
-orbit

is in an
S
-supported bijection with a tight
S
-orbit.

For every tight
S
-orbit

, the size of

is at most the size of the support of elements of

.
Furthermore, by Claim
2
, for every fixed

there are only finitely many
S
-orbits inside

.
In consequence we deduce that the set of all tight orbits in

is orbit-finite:

Claim 6.

The set
\left\{\,O\;\middle|\;O\subseteq B\text{ a tight orbit}\,\right\}
is orbit-finite.

In the sequel we order tight orbits in
B
with respect to inclusion.

Definition of the basis.

We define
\widehat{B}
as the set of characteristic vectors of all tight orbits

:

\widehat{B}\ :=\ \left\{\,{\mathbf{1}}_{O}\;\middle|\;O\subseteq B\text{ a tight orbit}\,\right\}.

Once
B
is fixed, the set
\widehat{B}
is orbit-finite due to Claim

.
Since every singleton is a tight orbit,
{\mathbf{1}}_{b}\in\widehat{B}
for every

;
informally speaking,
\widehat{B}
extends
B
.

Example 5.4.

Continuing Example
5.3
, where
B=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(2)}
,
the basis vectors are the following ones:

\displaystyle{\mathbf{1}}\qquad{\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}\_}\qquad{\mathbf{1}}_{\_{\beta}}\qquad{\mathbf{1}}_{{\alpha}{\beta}},

for any non-equal
{\alpha},{\beta}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
.

\triangleleft

It now remains to argue that
\widehat{B}

spans the whole space
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
,
and that it is linearly independent.

Spanning.

Given a subset
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
such that
T\subseteq S
, we distinguish the set of all tight
S^{\prime}
-orbits
for
T\subseteq S^{\prime}\subseteq S
:

\text{\sc TO}(T,S)\ :=\ \left\{\,O\;\middle|\;O\subseteq B\text{ a tight $S^{\prime}$-orbit},T\subseteq S^{\prime}\subseteq S\,\right\}.

For every fixed
S
the set
\text{\sc TO}(T,S)
is finite since, due to Claim
2
,

B
includes only finitely many
S^{\prime}
-orbits for every fixed
S^{\prime}\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
.

We prove that
\widehat{B}
spans the whole space, i.e., each vector is a linear combination of vectors from
\widehat{B}
.
To this aim we fix a finite subset
S\subseteq\text{\sc Atoms}
such that
T\subseteq S
and prove that
every
S
-supported vector
\mathbf{v}
is a linear combination of vectors from

\widehat{B}_{S}\ =\ \left\{\,{\mathbf{1}}_{O}\;\middle|\;O\in\text{\sc TO}(T,S)\,\right\}\ \subseteq\ \widehat{B}.

For every fixed
S
the set
\widehat{B}_{S}
is finite, as
\text{\sc TO}(T,S)
is so.

Lemma 5.5 (Spanning).

Let
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
such that
T\subseteq S
.
Each
S
-supported vector
\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
is a linear combination of vectors from
\widehat{B}_{S}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
and
S\subseteq_{\text{fin}}\text{\sc Atoms}
such that
\text{sup}(\mathbf{v})\subseteq S
.
By Lemma
3.2
(i),
\mathbf{v}

is constant when restricted to every
S
-orbit
O
; we may thus write
\mathbf{v}(O)
to denote this constant value.
We naturally define the
S
-orbit-domain
of
\mathbf{v}
as follows:

S\text{-orbit-dom}(\mathbf{v})\ :=\ \left\{\,O\;\middle|\;O\subseteq B\text{ an $S$-orbit},\ \mathbf{v}(O)\neq 0\,\right\}.

For two
S
-supported vectors
\mathbf{w},\mathbf{w}^{\prime}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
, we write

\mathbf{w}\prec\mathbf{w}^{\prime}
if
S\text{-orbit-dom}(\mathbf{w})
is obtained from
S\text{-orbit-dom}(\mathbf{w}^{\prime})
by removing one
S
-orbit
and replacing it by arbitrarily many
S
-orbits of strictly smaller
S
-atom dimension.

We define a representation of
\mathbf{v}
in basis
\widehat{B}

by structural induction with respect to the transitive closure of
\prec
.
Concerning the induction base, if

Otherwise, suppose the claim holds for all strictly smaller vectors

Take an

\displaystyle S^{\prime}:=\text{sup}(x)\cap S

Claim 7.

For every

Proof.

Recall that

Consider the subset

Consider the vector

Linear independence.

We rely on the following property of tight orbits (not true for arbitrary orbits):

Claim 8.

If orbits
O,O_{1},\ldots,O_{n}
are tight and

O\subseteq O_{1}\cup\ldots\cup O_{n}
then

O\subseteq O_{i}
for some
i=1,\ldots,n
.

Proof.

If
O
is a singleton then the claim holds vacuously.
Relying on Claim
3
we may thus assume that
O
is infinite.

Suppose
O\subseteq O_{1}\cup\ldots\cup O_{n}
for a tight
S
-orbit

and arbitrary tight orbits

O_{1},\ldots,O_{n}
.
Take any
x\in O
and let

.
Consider elements
\pi(x)\in O
for all
S
-atom automorphisms
\pi
, thus ranging over all elements of
the orbit

.
At least one of the orbits
O_{1},\ldots,O_{m}
, say the
S_{1}
-orbit

, necessarily contains
\pi(x)
and
\pi^{\prime}(x)
, for
some two

-atoms automorphisms
\pi,\pi^{\prime}
, such that the sets
\pi(R)
and
\pi^{\prime}(R)

are disjoint.
By tightness of

(and relying on Claim

) we get

and

which implies

for all

-atom automorphisms

.
∎

We now argue that the set
\widehat{B}
is linearly independent.
Towards contradiction, suppose that the zero vector is obtainable as a linear combination of basis vectors

(6)

\displaystyle q_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{O_{1}}\ +\ \ldots\ +\ q_{n}\cdot{\mathbf{1}}_{O_{n}}\quad=\quad{\mathbf{0}},

for some tight pairwise-different orbits

and

Take any inclusion-maximal orbit among

, say

.
We distinguish two cases.

1

If

.

2

Otherwise

Taking any

we derive a contradiction, as the value

while the value of the right-hand side is

.

6.
Decidability of finitary solvability

In this section we prove decidability of the finitary solvability problem.

Fin-Solv
(\mathbb{K})
:

Input::

an orbit-finite system of linear equations.

Question::

does it have a finitary solution?

Theorem 6.1.

Fin-Solv
(\mathbb{K})
is decidable for every fixed effective commutative ring
\mathbb{K}
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B\times C)
and
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
be the input.
We need to check if
\mathbf{t}\in

\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\mathbf{A})
, or equivalently

\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)
, where
P=\left\{\,\mathbf{A}(\_,c)\;\middle|\;c\in C\,\right\}
is an orbit-finite set of vectors from
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
.
As
P
can be computed from
\mathbf{A}
,
from now on we assume we are given
P
and
\mathbf{t}
.

Simplifying assumptions.

First, for simplicity of presentation
we assume that
P
(but not
\mathbf{t}
) is equivariant; hence also
B
is forcedly so.

We further assume w.l.o.g. that
all vectors are finitary:
P\subseteq\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(B)
and
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(B)
.
Indeed,
according to Remark
1

we may compute an orbit-finite basis
\widehat{B}
of
\text{\sc Lin}(B)
, and then compute
the representations
P^{\prime}\subseteq\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(\widehat{B})
and
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\in\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(\widehat{B})
of
P
and
\mathbf{t}
in this basis.
As
\widehat{B}
is a basis, the representation preserves solvability:
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)
if, and only if
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P^{\prime})
.

Finally, we assume w.l.o.g. that
B
is
straight
, by which we mean that
each of its orbits
is in equivariant bijection with
\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
By Lemma
2.2
, each (equivariant) orbit in
B
is in equivariant bijection with

\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
and some subgroup
G\leq\text{\sc S}_{k}
.
The vector space
\text{\sc Lin}(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G)
is, in turn, in equivariant bijection
with the subspace of all
G
-invariant
vectors in

\text{\sc Lin}(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)})
, i.e. vectors
v:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to\mathbb{K}

satisfying
v(a\circ\sigma)=v(a)
for every
a\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
and
\sigma\in G
.
This yields the embedding

\iota:\text{\sc Lin}(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G)\to\text{\sc Lin}(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)})

given by pre-composing with the canonical quotient

\pi_{G}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}/G
,

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\quad\mapsto\quad\iota(\mathbf{v})\ =\ \mathbf{v}\circ\pi_{G}.

The embedding
\iota
extends to
\text{\sc Lin}(B)\to\text{\sc Lin}(B^{\prime})
, where
B^{\prime}
is the disjoint union of straight orbits
corresponding to orbits of
B
.
The embedding is efficiently computable, and preserves linear combinations and finitariness.
By the latter property we may restrict
\iota
to finitary vectors, namely

\iota:\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(B)\to\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(B^{\prime})
.
Therefore, writing
P^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{t}^{\prime}
for
\iota(P)
and
\iota(\mathbf{t})
, respectively, we deduce that

\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)
if and only if
\mathbf{t^{\prime}}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P^{\prime})
.

Summing up, by an
instance
of the problem we mean a triple
(V,P,\mathbf{t})
consisting of
a vector space
V=\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(B)
generated by an equivariant straight orbit-finite set
B
,
an equivariant orbit-finite subset
P\subseteq V
, and a vector
\mathbf{t}\in V
.
The instance is
solvable
if
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)
.

Canonical form.

Recall that the atom dimension of the orbit
\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
is
k
.
Up to an equivariant bijection, we may present
B
as a disjoint union

B=B_{1}\uplus\ldots\uplus B_{n}
where
B_{i}=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(p_{i})}
for some
p_{i}\in\mathbb{N}
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Therefore the vector space
\text{\sc Fin-Lin}(B)
is equivariantly isomorphic to

(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(p_{1})}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K})\times\ldots\times(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(p_{n})}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K}).

For convenience we prefer to work with vector spaces in the following canonical form, where
all orbits
\text{\sc Atoms}^{(p)}
of the same atom dimension
p
are grouped together:

(8)

\displaystyle V\ =\ (\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{1})}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{1}})\times\ldots\times(\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{m})}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{m}}),

where
k_{1},\ldots,k_{m}
are pairwise different nonnegative integers, and
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}
are arbitrary positive integers.
A vector space
V
in canonical form (
8
) is thus the Cartesian product of
m

components
.
The definition of domain naturally extended to vectors of the form
\mathbf{v}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
as follows:

\text{dom}(\mathbf{v})=\left\{\,a\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{v}(a)\neq(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell}\,\right\}.

Definition 6.2.

By the
atom dimension
of a vector space
V
in canonical form (
8
)
we mean the maximum among atom dimensions
of orbits
\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{i})}
, i.e.,
\max(k_{1},\ldots,k_{m})
.

The component
{V_{i}=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{i})}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{i}}}
of largest atom dimension
we call the
main component
of
V
and denote as
\widetilde{V}
.
Assuming w.l.o.g.
i=1
(the main component is the first one) we may write

V\ =\ \widetilde{V}\times V^{\prime}

where
V^{\prime}
is the Cartesian product of all non-main components.
Thus every vector
\mathbf{v}\in V
decomposes as a pair

(9)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}=(\widetilde{\mathbf{v}},\mathbf{v}^{\prime})\in\widetilde{V}\times V^{\prime}.

Furthermore,

\widetilde{V}
embeds into
V
as the subspace

\widetilde{V}\times\left\{{\mathbf{0}}\right\}\times\ldots\times\left\{{\mathbf{0}}\right\}
, where
{\mathbf{0}}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{i})}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{i}}
maps
every tuple
a\in\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{i})}
to
(0,\ldots,0)\in\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{i}}
,
and likewise
V^{\prime}
embeds into
V
.
Using the embeddings implicitly, we may write

(10)

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\ =\ \widetilde{\mathbf{v}}+\mathbf{v}^{\prime}

in place of (
9
).

Summing up, instances
(V,P,\mathbf{t})
are assumed from now on to consist of
a vector space
V
in canonical form (
8
).

Locally solvable instances.

We distinguish
locally solvable
instances
(V,P,\mathbf{t})
, defined as follows.
Let
\widetilde{V}=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
be the main component (for succinctness of notation
we write
k,\ell
instead of
k_{1},\ell_{1}
).
We use the restriction operation: for
X\subseteq\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}
and
\mathbf{w}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
we define

{\mathbf{w}}{\restriction}_{X}(a)=\begin{cases}\mathbf{w}(a)&\text{ if }a\in X\\
{\mathbf{0}}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Given a
k
-set
A\in{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose k}
, we may consider the
A
-restriction

(\widetilde{V},P^{\prime},\mathbf{t}^{\prime})

of the instance, where

P^{\prime}=\left\{\,{\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}}{\restriction}_{A^{(k)}}\;\middle|\;\mathbf{v}\in P\,\right\}\qquad\mathbf{t}^{\prime}={\widetilde{\mathbf{t}}}{\restriction}_{A^{(k)}}.

Thus the
A
-restriction is essentially a finite system of at most
|A^{(k)}|=k!
equations.
Any restriction of a solvable instance is solvable too.
An instance is called
locally solvable
if each of its
A
-restrictions is solvable, for every

A\in{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose k}
.
Clearly, each solvable instance is locally solvable, but the opposite implication is not true in general
(one of the reasons is that local solvability only refers to the main component).

Claim 9.

Local solvability is decidable.

We later make use of the fact that for any two different (but not necessarily disjoint)
k
-sets

A,A^{\prime}\in{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose k}
, the sets

A^{(k)}
and
(A^{\prime})^{(k)}
are always disjoint.

Reduction of atom dimension.

The following lemma is the core of the proof of Theorem
6.1
:

Lemma 6.3.

Given a locally solvable instance
(V,P,\mathbf{t})
as above,
one may construct another instance
(\overline{V},\overline{P},\overline{\mathbf{t}})

where atom dimension of
\overline{V}
is strictly smaller
than that of
V
, and such that
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(P)
if and only if
\mathbf{\overline{t}}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\overline{P})
.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

Using the lemma we prove that the finitary spanning problem
reduces to solvability of finite systems of linear equations,
which implies decidability.
First, local solvability of an instance is a necessary condition for solvability, and is decidable by Claim
9
.
The algorithm thus checks if the input instance is locally solvable: if it is not so it answers negatively,
and if it is so the algorithm applies the construction of Lemma
6.3
to produce an instance
of strictly smaller atom dimension.
Continuing so iteratively, the algorithm finally
arrives at
V
of atom dimension equal to
0
, i.e., at a finitely dimensional vector space
V
.
In this case the set
P
, being an orbit-finite subset of
V
, is necessarily finite too, and the problem amounts to solving a finite system of linear equations.
∎

We thus concentrate from now on on proving Lemma
6.3
.

Cogs.

We rely on a generalisation of cogs in
(
Bojańczyk et al. 2021
)

and of simple hypergraphs in
(
Hofman and
Rózycki 2021
)
.
Let
A,S\in{\text{\sc Atoms}\choose k}
be two disjoint subsets of atoms of size
k
, and
let
\sigma:A\to S
be a bijection.
For every
I\subseteq A
, we define
an injective mapping

(11)

\displaystyle\sigma_{I}:A\to A\cup S\qquad\qquad\sigma_{I}({\alpha})=\begin{cases}{\alpha}&\text{ if }{\alpha}\notin I\\
\sigma({\alpha})&\text{ if }{\alpha}\in I.\end{cases}

Intuitively, the set
I
specifies those elements
{\alpha}\in A
that should be replaced by
\sigma({\alpha})
.
In particular,
\sigma_{\emptyset}
is the identity on
A
and
\sigma_{A}=\sigma
. Let
\mathbf{w}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
be a vector satisfying
\text{dom}(\mathbf{w})\subseteq A^{(k)}
.
In (
12
) below we implicitly extend
\sigma_{I}
, in an arbitrary way, to an atom automorphism
\text{\sc Atoms}\to\text{\sc Atoms}
.
A
cog of
\mathbf{w}
via
\sigma
is the vector
[\sigma](\mathbf{w}):\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
defined as:

(12)

\displaystyle[\sigma](\mathbf{w})\ =\ \sum_{I\subseteq A}(-1)^{|I|}\cdot\sigma_{I}(\mathbf{w}).

Thus the domain of
[\sigma](\mathbf{w})
is a finite set of size at most
k!\cdot 2^{k}
.

Example 6.4.

Let
k=2
,
\ell=1
,

, and

\mathbf{w}\ =\ {\alpha}{\beta}+2\cdot{\beta}{\alpha}\ \in\ \text{\sc Atoms}^{(2)}\to_{\text{fin}}\mathbb{K}\ .

Let
\sigma:\left\{{\alpha},{\beta}\right\}\to\left\{{\gamma},{\delta}\right\}
be defined by
\sigma({\alpha})={\gamma}
and
\sigma({\beta})={\delta}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle[\sigma](\mathbf{w})\ =\ \quad

\displaystyle{\alpha}{\beta}+2\cdot{\beta}{\alpha}\ \ -\ {\gamma}{\beta}-2\cdot{\beta}{\gamma}

\displaystyle\,+\,\,

\displaystyle{\gamma}{\delta}+2\cdot{\delta}{\gamma}\ \ \ -\ {\alpha}{\delta}-2\cdot{\delta}{\alpha}.

\triangleleft

Claim 10.

Let
\mathbf{w}:\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k)}\to\mathbb{K}^{\ell}
such that

.
Then
{\big([\sigma](\mathbf{w})\big)}{\restriction}_{A^{(k)}}=\mathbf{w}
.

Consider some locally solvable instance

We start by restricting the set

Let

.
For any

and

let

denote the subspace

Furthermore, let

denote the set of non-repeating

-tuples containing

element of
S
, and
define the subspace

of

:

(13)

\displaystyle V_{S}=({\text{\sc Atoms}^{k_{1}}_{\neg S}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{1}})\ \times\ldots\ \times({\text{\sc Atoms}^{k_{m}}_{\neg S}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{m}}).

Thus

Consider an instance

We observe that any finitary solution

of

, where

may be renamed, using a

We have thus argued that:

Claim 11.

For every

The instance

.

The instance
(\overline{V},\overline{P},\overline{\mathbf{t}})
.

Let
\widetilde{V}=\text{\sc Atoms}^{(k_{1})}\to_{\text{fs}}\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{1}}
be the main component of
V

(for succinctness of notation we write

,

For any

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \overline{V}\ =\ ({\text{\sc Atoms}^{k}_{S}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{K}^{\ell})\ \times\ ({\text{\sc Atoms}^{k_{2}}_{\neg S}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{2}})&\\
\ \times\ \ldots\ \times\ ({\text{\sc Atoms}^{k_{m}}_{\neg S}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathbb{K}^{\ell_{m}})&.\end{aligned}

We now proceed to defining

to
\widetilde{V}
), and in consequence

Claim 12.

For every

,

Using Claim

We define

(16)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{v}}=\left\{\,\mathbf{v}-\Delta^{\!\!\prec}\widetilde{\mathbf{v}}\;\middle|\;\prec\;\in\mathcal{O}\,\right\}

Claim 13.

For every
\mathbf{v}\in P^{\prime}

As an orbit-finite union of orbit-finite sets is always orbit-finite

\overline{P}

Refering to Remark

Correctness.

For every
\prec\;\in\mathcal{O}
, vector
\mathbf{w}\in\widetilde{(P^{\prime})}

7.
Solvability reduces to finitary solvability

In this section we reduce solvability to finitary solvability:

Theorem 7.1.

Solv
(\mathbb{K})
reduces to
Fin-Solv
(\mathbb{K})
.

Let
\mathbf{A}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B\times C)
and
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Lin}(B)
be the input system.
In terms of spans, the solvability problem amounts to deciding if
\mathbf{t}\in\text{\sc Span}(\mathbf{A})
.
We will prove the result by effectively constructing a matrix
\widetilde{\mathbf{A}}
with the same row-indexing set

B
as
\mathbf{A}
, such that
\text{\sc Span}(\mathbf{A})=\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})
.

Well-definedness and exactness.

Let

\mathbf{x}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
a vector.
We start by a characterisation of vectors
\mathbf{x}\in\text{\sc Lin}(C)
for which the product

\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is well-defined.
Recall that
\mathbf{y}(b)
is well-defined if and only if
there are only finitely many
c\in C
such that

\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0
and
\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0
.
Let
S=\text{sup}(\mathbf{A})\cup\text{sup}(\mathbf{x})
; in other words,
S
is the support of the pair
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x})
.
We say that the pair
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x})
is
exact
if for every
b\in B
and
c\in C

such that
\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0
and
\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0
it holds

(24)

\displaystyle\text{sup}(c)\subseteq\text{sup}(b)\cup S.

Lemma 7.2.

\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is well-defined if and only if
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x})
is exact.

Proof.

For the if direction, suppose
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x})
is exact, and consider an arbitrary fixed
b\in B
.
Let
T=\text{sup}(b)\cup S
.
By (
24
) the support of every
c
satisfying
\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0
and
\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0

is included in
T
.
By Claim
2
in Section
5
,
for every fixed set
T^{\prime}\subseteq T
, every orbit
O\subseteq C
contains at most
|T^{\prime}|!
elements
c\in O
such that
\text{sup}(c)=T^{\prime}
,
and since
C
is orbit-finite,
there are only finitely many
c\in C
satisfying
\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0
and
\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0
.
The product
\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is thus well-defined, as required.

For the opposite direction, suppose
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{x})
is not exact, i.e., for some
b\in B
and
c\in C
we have:

\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0,\qquad\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0,\qquad\text{sup}(c)\not\subseteq\text{sup}(b)\cup S.

According to the latter condition,
some atom
{\alpha}\in\text{\sc Atoms}
satisfies
{\alpha}\in\text{sup}(c)
and
{\alpha}\not\in T=\text{sup}(b)\cup S
.
Note that every
T
-atom automorphism preserves
b
and
\mathbf{A}
, and hence also preserves the row
vector
\mathbf{A}(b,\_)
.
Consider an infinite family of
T
-automorphisms
\pi
that map
{\alpha}
to different atoms
\pi({\alpha})\notin T
.
For every such
\pi
we have
\pi(c)\neq c
, but
\mathbf{A}(b,\pi(c))=\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0
.
Furthermore, every such
\pi
preserves
\mathbf{x}
, and hence we have

\mathbf{x}(\pi(c))=\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0
.
In consequence, there are infinitely many
c\in C
such that

\mathbf{A}(b,c)\neq 0
and
\mathbf{x}(c)\neq 0
, i.e., the product
\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{x}
is not well-defined on
b
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Note the injection

\text{\sc Span}(\mathbf{A})=\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})
.

Proof.

W.l.o.g. we assume that
\mathbf{A}
contains non-zero column vectors only
(otherwise, since

is a single orbit,

all

are zero vectors and the claim holds vacuously).
In one direction, consider any vector
\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})
, i.e.,

\mathbf{v}\ =\ q_{1}\cdot(\mathbf{A}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{1})+\ldots+q_{n}\cdot(\mathbf{A}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{n})

for

and
{\mathbf{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathbf{w}}_{n}\in\widetilde{C}
, which immediately

\mathbf{v}\ =\ \mathbf{A}\cdot(q_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{1}+\ldots+q_{n}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{n})\ \in\ \text{\sc Span}(\mathbf{A}).

We are going to prove that
\mathbf{v}\in\text{\sc Fin-Span}(\widetilde{\mathbf{A}})

Consider the representation of
\mathbf{x}

\mathbf{x}\ =\ q_{1}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{1}+\ldots+q_{\ell}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{\ell}.

7.3
we know that
\mathbf{A}\cdot{\mathbf{w}}_{i}
is well-defined
and hence
{\mathbf{w}}_{i}\in\widetilde{C}

for all

\displaystyle\mathbf{v}\ =\ \

Complexity.

We conclude with a rough estimation of complexity with respect to the number of orbits in
B
and
C
,
and the atom dimension of the input system
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{t})
defined as the largest atom dimension
of each of its orbits, plus the size of its support.

The blow-up of reduction of Theorem
7.1
is exponential in the atom dimension
of input, but polynomial in the number of orbits in
B
and
C
.
Likewise is the number and size of finite systems of equations that are produced in the procedure of
Theorem
6.1
.
Summing up, the combined algorithm for
Solv
(
\mathbb{K}
) produces exponentially many
finite systems of exponential size
(polynomially many finite systems of polynomial size, when atom dimension of input is fixed),
and answers positively exactly when all these systems are solvable.

In the two most significant special cases, namely
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Q}
or
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{Z}
, finite systems are solvable in
P
.
Therefore, the problems
Solv
(
\mathbb{Q}
) and
Solv
(
\mathbb{Z}
) are in
ExpTime
, and likewise
are
Fin-Solv
(
\mathbb{Q}
) and
Fin-Solv
(
\mathbb{Z}
).
When atom dimension of input is fixed, all these problems are in
P
.

8.
Final remarks

We have shown decidability of solvability of orbit-finite systems of linear equations over an arbitrary effective commutative ring.
We expect applicability of this general result in various corners; as a first example,
combining our result with the insight of
(
Hofman and
Rózycki 2021
)
leads to decidability of rechability
in integer-relaxation of data-enriched Petri nets.

We leave a lot of questions for further research—here we list the most important ones.
First, the immediate next step is to compute the whole solution sets represented, for instance, as a (coset of)
an orbit-finitely spanned vector subspace.
Second, an intriguing open question is whether solvability is still decidable if the finite-support restriction on solutions is dropped
(like in
(
Klin et al. 2015
)
)?
Furthermore, an important restriction on solutions is
nonnegativity
, as it allows to model systems of inequalities.
According to our preliminary results
Fin-Solv
(
\mathbb{Q}
),
Fin-Solv
(
\mathbb{Z}
) and
Solv
(
\mathbb{Q}
)
are decidable under the nonnegativity restriction,
but we don’t know the status of
Solv
(
\mathbb{Z}
).
Finally, in this paper we have exclusively considered equality atoms and are very curious about other richer structures.
For instance, concerning ordered atoms, the results of
(
Hofman and Lasota 2018
)
indicate a huge increase of complexity of
solvability, compared to equality atoms.
