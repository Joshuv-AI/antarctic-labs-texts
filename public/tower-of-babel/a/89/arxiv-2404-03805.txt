Blessing of dimension in Bayesian inference on covariance matrices
Shounak Chattopadhyay, Anru R. Zhang, David Dunson

Abstract
Bayesian factor analysis is routinely used for dimensionality reduction in modeling of high-dimensional covariance matrices. Factor analytic decompositions express the covariance as a sum of a low rank and diagonal matrix. In practice, Gibbs sampling algorithms are typically used for posterior computation, alternating between updating the latent factors, loadings, and residual variances. In this article, we exploit a blessing of dimensionality to develop a provably accurate pseudo-posterior for the covariance matrix that bypasses the need for Gibbs or other variants of Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling. Our proposed Factor Analysis with BLEssing of dimensionality (FABLE) approach relies on a first-stage singular value decomposition (SVD) to estimate the latent factors, and then defines a jointly conjugate prior for the loadings and residual variances. The accuracy of the resulting pseudo-posterior for the covariance improves with increasing dimensionality. We show that FABLE has excellent performance in high-dimensional covariance matrix estimation, including producing well calibrated credible intervals, both theoretically and through simulation experiments. We also demonstrate the strength of our approach in terms of accurate inference and computational efficiency by applying it to a gene expression data set.



Keywords:

Bayes, Factor analysis, High-dimensional, Large p,
Posterior approximation, Scalable, Singular value decomposition

1
Introduction

Inference on covariance matrices in high-dimensional data is a key focus in many application areas, motivating a rich literature on associated statistical methods. One thread of this literature avoids modeling of the data and instead focuses on high-dimensional covariance matrix estimators under various assumptions on the inherent low-dimensional structure in the data, including (but not limited to) banded covariance
(
Bickel and Levina, 2008
)
, low rank structure
(
Shikhaliev et al., 2019
)
, low rank with sparsity
(
Richard et al., 2012
)
, sparse covariance
(
Bien and Tibshirani, 2011
)
, and sparse inverse
precision matrix estimation
(
Zhang and Zou, 2014
)
. Our interest is instead in model-based Bayesian approaches, which have advantages in terms of naturally accommodating complexities in the data and quantifying uncertainty, while having disadvantages in terms of computational efficiency, particularly in implementing Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) algorithms to sample from the posterior distribution.

Our particular focus is on factor models, which express the covariance matrix in a low rank plus diagonal form, and represent a popular probabilistic model-based alternative to principal components analysis (PCA). Factor analysis
introduces a lower-dimensional latent factor
\eta_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
corresponding to each observation
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
with
k\ll p
, such that
y_{i}=\Lambda\eta_{i}+\epsilon_{i}
for a factor loadings matrix
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
and independent idiosyncratic errors
\epsilon_{i}\sim N_{p}(0,\Sigma)
. Following typical practice, we let the latent factors have independent Gaussian priors
\eta_{i}\sim N_{k}(0,\mathbb{I}_{k})
.
Marginalizing out the latent factors leads to
y_{i}\sim N_{p}(0,\Psi)
, with the covariance matrix
\Psi=\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}+\Sigma
. This provides a convenient decomposition of the covariance, which
dramatically reduces the number of free parameters required to model the
p\times p
covariance matrix from
p(p+1)/2
to
(p-(k-1)/2)k+p
.

There is a vibrant recent literature improving upon and expanding the scope of Bayesian factor analysis
(
Schiavon et al., 2022
;
De Vito et al., 2021
;
Frühwirth-Schnatter, 2023
;
Roy et al., 2021
;
Ma and Liu, 2022
;
Bolfarine et al., 2022
;
Xie et al., 2022
)
. Even with increasingly rich classes of priors and data types, the canonical approach for posterior computation remains Gibbs samplers that iterate between updating latent factors, factor loadings, residual variances, hyperparameters controlling the hierarchical prior, and other model parameters. This approach is simple to implement in broad model classes, but commonly faces problems with slow mixing, particularly as data dimensionality and complexity increase.
Issues with slow mixing and bottlenecks with large datasets have motivated alternative posterior sampling algorithms that apply Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) after marginalizing out the latent factors, exploiting sufficient statistics and parallel computation to facilitate implementation for large sample sizes
(
Chandra et al., 2023
;
Man and Culpepper, 2022
)
. Nonetheless, all current MCMC sampling algorithms for large covariance matrices face substantial computational hurdles as the number of dimensions increases.

The lack of sufficiently scalable MCMC algorithms has motivated a rich literature on developing more computationally efficient algorithms for Bayesian inference in factor models. Variational Bayes approaches
(
Hansen et al., 2023
;
Wang and Stephens, 2021
)
attempt to approximate the posterior but often the accuracy of the approximation is poor, with a tendency to dramatically underestimate posterior uncertainty. A concise review comparing variational approximations and posterior sampling approaches for factor analysis is provided in
Foo and Shim, 2021
. Maximum
a posteriori
(MAP) estimation under sparsity priors
(
Srivastava et al., 2017
)
and expectation-maximization (EM) algorithms
(
Ročková and George, 2016
;
Avalos-Pacheco et al., 2022
;
Zhao et al., 2016
)
provide computationally efficient point estimates of the covariance matrix.
Sabnis et al., 2016
develop a distributed computing framework aggregating local estimates of the covariance across different cores.

Srivastava et al., 2017
;
Ročková and George, 2016
, and
Sabnis et al., 2016
provide theoretical results regarding estimation accuracy and posterior concentration, without providing any guarantees on uncertainty quantification. A key open problem in the literature remains how to obtain an efficient posterior approximation for a high-dimensional covariance that has guarantees in terms of valid frequentist coverage of credible intervals.

Fast algorithms for Bayesian factor analysis capable of scaling efficiently to high-dimensional data typically sacrifice the ability to provide an accurate characterization of uncertainty. The focus of this article is on proposing a simple approach for overcoming this limitation, providing computationally efficient inference on a high-dimensional covariance matrix.
With the increasing dimension, we have more and more variables loading on the same small number of latent factors.
As a consequence, we obtain a blessing of dimensionality phenomenon allowing us to first pre-estimate the latent factors and then leverage on this point estimate to obtain a pseudo-posterior distribution of the factor loadings
\Lambda
and error variances
\Sigma
under conjugate priors on these parameters. This in turn induces a pseudo-posterior distribution for
the covariance matrix
\Psi=\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}+\Sigma
.
Our proposed Factor Analysis with BLEssing of dimensionality (FABLE) approach completely bypasses MCMC.
To ensure that the pseudo-posterior credible intervals for the entries of the covariance are asymptotically normal with guarantees on valid frequentist coverage, we develop a data-adaptive coverage correction (CC); we refer to the adjusted FABLE approach as CC-FABLE.

Fan et al., 2023
also pre-estimate latent factors, but in the context of regression with high-dimensional correlated covariates. In their approach, a first-stage principal components analysis is carried out for the high-dimensional covariates. The results are then used to obtain a modified covariate vector consisting of principal component scores and de-correlated versions of the original covariates. Using this new covariate vector in the second stage high-dimensional regression avoids multicollinearity. This focus, and the corresponding methodology, are substantially different from ours.
A blessing of dimensionality phenomenon is also explored in
Li et al., 2018
, where estimation of the covariance between a subset of the variables of interest is improved by incorporating auxiliary information from other variables.

In Section
2
, we develop our FABLE methodology with the CC-FABLE modification and provide an approach to choose key hyperparameter values. In Section
3
, we provide theoretical results on pseudo-posterior contraction rates and uncertainty quantification, leveraging on a blessing of dimensionality phenomenon. In Section
4
, we validate our approach with comparison to other state-of-the-art approaches from the viewpoint of estimation error, frequentist coverage, and computational runtime in different numerical experiments. In Section
5
, we present a relevant application of our approach to a large gene expression data set and highlight distinct advantages over competitors. In Section
6
, we provide a discussion on future research directions.

2
Proposed Methodology

2.1
Initial Approach

The observed data consist of
\mathbf{Y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, where
y_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. We consider a factor model for
y_{i}
given by

y_{i}=\Lambda\eta_{i}+\epsilon_{i},\qquad\epsilon_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{p}(0,\Sigma),

(1)

where we have omitted the intercept term, assuming the data have been centered prior to analysis. Here,
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
is an unknown matrix of factor loadings,
\eta_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{k}(0,\mathbb{I}_{k})
is the
i
th latent factor, and
\epsilon_{i}
is a zero-mean idiosyncratic error having diagonal covariance
\Sigma=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{p}^{2})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. It is typical to assume that the dimension of the latent factors
k
is much smaller than the dimension of the data
p
. Integrating out the latent factors leads to

y_{i}\mid\Lambda,\Sigma\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{p}(0,\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}+\Sigma).

(2)

The covariance matrix
\Psi=\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}+\Sigma
of the
y_{i}
s in (
2
) is decomposed as the sum of a low-rank and a diagonal matrix.
In this paper, our goal is to estimate
L=\Lambda\Lambda^{\top},\Sigma,
and

\Psi=\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}+\Sigma
. We first illustrate our methodology assuming
k
is known. We discuss an approach to estimate
k
in Section
2.3
.

Let
\mathbf{M}=[\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and
\Lambda=[\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{p}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k}
, with
\lambda_{j}^{\top}
denoting the
j
th row of
\Lambda
. We also denote the
j
th column of
\mathbf{Y}
as
y^{(j)}
, so that
\mathbf{Y}=[y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(p)}].
The latent factor model (
1
) can be alternatively expressed as

y^{(j)}=\mathbf{M}\lambda_{j}+\epsilon^{(j)},

(3)

where
\epsilon^{(j)}
is the
j
th column of the matrix
E=[\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}]^{\top}=[\epsilon^{(1)},\ldots,\epsilon^{(p)}],
with
\epsilon^{(j)}\overset{ind}{\sim}N_{n}(0_{n},\sigma_{j}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{n})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. Writing in matrix form, we obtain

\mathbf{Y}=\mathbf{M}\Lambda^{\top}+E.

(4)

Our strategy to obtain computationally efficient pseudo-posterior samples for
\Psi
is outlined as follows. We first estimate
\mathbf{M}
by a plug-in estimator
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}

based on the singular value decomposition (SVD) of
\mathbf{Y}
and then consider a surrogate model to (
3
) where
\mathbf{M}
is replaced by
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
. Conditional on the observed data,
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
is fixed. Thus, the surrogate model is equivalent to
p
parallel regression problems. We then let the regression coefficient and the error variance for the
j
th regression have conjugate normal-inverse gamma prior distributions for
j=1,\ldots,p
to obtain their pseudo-posterior samples across the
p
different regressions in parallel. Finally, we combine the pseudo-posterior samples of the regression coefficients and the error variances to obtain a pseudo-posterior sample of
\Psi
.

To motivate our choice for
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
, we start with the SVD of
\mathbf{Y}
, given by

\mathbf{Y}=UDV^{\top}+U_{\perp}D_{\perp}V_{\perp}^{\top},

(5)

where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k},D\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k},V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times k},U_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times(r-k)},D_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{(r-k)\times(r-k)},
and
V_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(r-k)}
, with
r=p\wedge n.
The columns of
U,U_{\perp},V,V_{\perp}
consist of orthonormal vectors, with
U^{\top}U_{\perp}=V^{\top}V_{\perp}=\mathbf{O}_{k\times(r-k)}
, where
\mathbf{O}_{p_{1}\times p_{2}}
denotes the
p_{1}\times p_{2}
matrix with all entries equal to
0
. Let
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Y}V/\sqrt{p}=UD/{\sqrt{p}}
and
\widehat{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
be an invertible matrix satisfying

\widehat{C}\widehat{C}^{\top}=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}=\dfrac{D^{2}}{np}.

(6)

An immediate choice of
\widehat{C}
satisfying (
6
) is given by
\widehat{C}=D/\sqrt{np}
. Lastly, we let

\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{A}(\widehat{C}^{\top})^{-1}.

(7)

For the choice of
\widehat{C}=D/\sqrt{np},
it is straightforward from (
7
) that
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\sqrt{n}U.
We will denote this particular choice of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\sqrt{n}U
as the
canonical choice
of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
, observing that other choices are also possible for different choices of
\widehat{C}
satisfying (
6
). We provide a heuristic leading to (
7
) at the end of this Section.

Given a particular choice of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
, we consider the following surrogate model:

y^{(j)}=\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}+\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(j)},\quad\widetilde{\epsilon}^{(j)}\overset{ind}{\sim}N_{n}(0,\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{n})

(8)

for
j=1,\ldots,p
. The model (
8
) could be interpreted as a version of (
3
) with
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
substituted for the original matrix of latent factors
\mathbf{M}
and new parameters
\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
,
\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}>0
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. Next, we endow
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2})_{j=1}^{p}
with independent normal-inverse gamma (NIG) priors
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2})\overset{iid}{\sim}\mbox{NIG}(0_{k},\tau^{2}\mathbb{I}_{k},\gamma_{0}/2,\gamma_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}/2)
. That is, we let

\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle\sim N_{k}(0_{k},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\,\tau^{2}\,\mathbb{I}_{k}),\quad\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\sim\mbox{IG}\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{0}}{2},\dfrac{\gamma_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}}{2}\right).

(9)

The global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
allows us to
a priori
shrink the factor loadings towards zero, regularizing
\widetilde{\Lambda}=[\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\lambda}_{p}]^{\top}
and favoring sparsity. We discuss a data-driven strategy to estimate
\tau^{2}
in Section
2.3
.

The surrogate model (
8
) and the conjugate prior specification (
9
) motivate the pseudo-posterior densities
\widetilde{\Pi}_{j}
of
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2})
for each
j=1,\ldots,p
, given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widetilde{\Pi}_{j}(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2})&=\mbox{NIG}\left(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\mid\mu_{j},\mathbf{K},\gamma_{n}/2,\gamma_{n}\delta_{j}^{2}/2\right)\\
&=N_{k}\left(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}\mid\mu_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\mathbf{K}\right)\mbox{IG}\left(\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\,\middle|\,\dfrac{\gamma_{n}}{2},\dfrac{\gamma_{n}\delta_{j}^{2}}{2}\right),\end{split}

(10)

with the updated hyperparameters given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mu_{j}&=\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\dfrac{\mathbb{I}_{k}}{\tau^{2}}\right)^{-1}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}y^{(j)},\\
\mathbf{K}&=\left(\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}+\dfrac{\mathbb{I}_{k}}{\tau^{2}}\right)^{-1},\\
\gamma_{n}&=\gamma_{0}+n,\\
\gamma_{n}\delta_{j}^{2}&=\gamma_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}+\left(y^{(j)\top}y^{(j)}-\mu_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mu_{j}\right).\end{split}

(11)

The pseudo-posteriors are simply obtained by treating
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
as a fixed design matrix for the
j
th surrogate regression and then applying Bayes’ rule. To obtain independent pseudo-posterior samples of
\Psi
, we first draw independent samples
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2})\overset{ind}{\sim}\widetilde{\Pi}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. We then let
\widetilde{\Lambda}=[\widetilde{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\lambda}_{p}]^{\top},\widetilde{L}=\widetilde{\Lambda}\widetilde{\Lambda}^{\top},\widetilde{\Sigma}=\mbox{diag}(\widetilde{\sigma}_{1}^{2},\ldots,\widetilde{\sigma}_{p}^{2}),
and
\widetilde{\Psi}=\widetilde{L}+\widetilde{\Sigma}
denote the pseudo-posterior samples of
\Lambda,L=\Lambda\Lambda^{\top}
,
\Sigma
, and
\Psi
, respectively.
Our proposed Factor Analysis with BLEssing of dimensionality (FABLE) approach obtains independent pseudo-posterior samples of the covariance matrix
\Psi
in an embarrassingly parallel fashion, bypassing the need to carry out MCMC.

A natural concern is whether different choices of
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
satisfying (
7
) would affect the distribution of
\widetilde{\Psi}
. The following result, with proof in Section
A
of the Supplementary Material, ensures that this is not the case.

Proposition 2.1
.

(i) For any
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
satisfying (
7
), we have
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=n\mathbb{I}_{k}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}=nUU^{\top}.

(ii) The pseudo-posterior distribution of
\widetilde{L},\widetilde{\Sigma},
and
\widetilde{\Psi}
obtained from the FABLE approach only depends on
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
through
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}
and
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}\widehat{\mathbf{M}}^{\top}
.

In our experience, the pseudo-posterior samples
\widetilde{\Psi}
perform well when estimating the true covariance matrix, both in terms of simulation studies and theoretical posterior contraction rates. However, we observed that the entry-wise pseudo-posterior credible intervals obtained from the samples
\widetilde{\Psi}
tend to underestimate the uncertainty associated with estimating the entry-wise elements of the true covariance matrix, from the perspective of frequentist coverage. In Section
2.2
, we build on the initial FABLE approach developed in this subsection and propose a coverage correction (CC) of FABLE called CC-FABLE. We provide provably accurate guarantees on posterior contraction rates and frequentist coverage of the CC-FABLE procedure in Section
3
.

Lastly, we describe the heuristic leading to (
7
).
From (
4
), the matrix
\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{Y}V/\sqrt{p}
satisfies

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{M}\dfrac{\Lambda^{\top}V}{\sqrt{p}}+\dfrac{EV}{\sqrt{p}}.

Let
C=V^{\top}\Lambda/\sqrt{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
. Based on the consistency of spectral estimates, we expect
E
to be approximately independent of
V
as both
n,p
grow. As a result, we expect
EV/\sqrt{p}\approx 0
for increasing
p
. This leads us to

\mathbf{A}\approx\mathbf{M}C^{\top}

(12)

or equivalently,
a_{i}\approx C\eta_{i}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
a_{i}
is the
i
th row of
\mathbf{A}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Since
\eta_{i}\sim N_{k}(0,\mathbb{I}_{k}),
the marginal density of
a_{i}
is approximately given by
a_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{k}(0_{k},CC^{\top})
, for
i=1,\ldots,n
. This motivates the following estimator
\widehat{C}\widehat{C}^{\top}
of
CC^{\top}
:

\widehat{C}\widehat{C}^{\top}=\dfrac{1}{n}\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{A}=\dfrac{1}{np}D^{2},

leading us to (
6
). Given any
\widehat{C}
satisfying (
6
), we use (
12
) to propose
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{A}(\widehat{C}^{\top})^{-1}
as an estimate of
\mathbf{M}.

In particular, the canonical choice
\widehat{\mathbf{M}}=\sqrt{n}U
is the same as the estimator obtained by carrying out principal components analysis (PCA) on the matrix
\mathbf{Y}\mathbf{Y}^{\top}

(
Fan et al., 2023
)
.
However, (
7
) permits other choices corresponding to different choices of
\widehat{C}
satisfying (
6
), providing a general framework for obtaining estimates of the latent factors.

2.2
Coverage Correction

Although the pseudo-posterior of
\widetilde{\Psi}
obtained from (
10
) performed well in terms of point estimation across simulation studies, we observed under-coverage of the entry-wise credible intervals, particularly when the signal-to-noise ratios
R_{j}=\|\lambda_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/\sigma_{j}^{2}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
are large. To improve coverage, we propose a simple coverage correction (CC) of the FABLE approach, which we denote by CC-FABLE. We provide a theoretical justification of this approach in Section
3
.
We found that the correction leads to improved performance in simulation studies, particularly with increasing
R_{j}
s.

To improve coverage, we include an additional factor
\rho^{2}
in the conditional pseudo-posterior variance when generating
\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
following (
10
). That is, we modify
\widetilde{\Pi}_{j}
in (
10
) to

{\Pi}_{j}^{C}\left(\lambda_{C,j},\sigma_{C,j}^{2}\right)=N_{k}\left(\lambda_{C,j}\middle|\mu_{j},\rho^{2}\sigma_{C,j}^{2}\mathbf{K}\right)\mbox{IG}\left(\sigma_{C,j}^{2}\middle|\dfrac{\gamma_{n}}{2},\dfrac{\gamma_{n}\delta_{j}^{2}}{2}\right),

(13)

for
j=1,\ldots,p
, where
\mathbf{K}=\{\mathbf{M}^{\top}\mathbf{M}+(\mathbb{I}_{k}/\tau^{2})\}^{-1}=\mathbb{I}_{k}/(n+\tau^{-2})
and
\gamma_{n},\mu_{j},\delta_{j}^{2}
are as in (
11
).
We then proceed as before to draw coverage-corrected pseudo-posterior samples
(\lambda_{C,j},\sigma_{C,j}^{2})\overset{ind}{\sim}\Pi_{j}^{C}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. Letting
\Lambda_{C}=[\lambda_{C,1},\ldots,\lambda_{C,p}]^{\top}
and
\Sigma_{C}=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{C,1}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{C,p}^{2})
, we construct a coverage-corrected (CC) pseudo-posterior sample of the covariance matrix as
\Psi_{C}=\Lambda_{C}\Lambda_{C}^{\top}+\Sigma_{C}.
Having computed the hyperparameters
\gamma_{n}
and
\delta_{j}^{2}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
as in (
11
), the pseudo-posterior mean
\widehat{\Psi}_{C}
of
\Psi_{C}
is available analytically, with its
uv
th entry for
1\leq u,v\leq p
given by

\displaystyle\begin{split}\widehat{\Psi}_{C,uv}&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\mu_{u}^{\top}\mu_{v},&\text{if $u\neq v,$}\\
\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}+\left(1+\dfrac{k\rho^{2}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right)\dfrac{\gamma_{n}\delta_{u}^{2}}{\gamma_{n}-2},&\text{if $u=v,$}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(14)

provided
\gamma_{n}>2
. For finite
k,\rho^{2},
and
\tau^{2}
, the pseudo-posterior mean
\widehat{\Psi}_{C}
can approximated as
\widehat{\Psi}_{C}=G_{0}G_{0}^{\top}+\Delta,
where
G_{0}=[\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{p}]^{\top}
and
\Delta=\mbox{diag}(\delta_{1}^{2},\ldots,\delta_{p}^{2})
, as
n
increases. From here on, we take
\widehat{\Psi}_{C}=G_{0}G_{0}^{\top}+\Delta
to be the pseudo-posterior mean of
\Psi
under CC-FABLE, unless mentioned otherwise.

We choose
\rho
to ensure that a summary statistic of the entrywise asymptotic coverages is
1-\alpha
, such as their average or minimum. Let
B
denote the
p\times p
symmetric matrix with entries
b_{uv}
, where

\displaystyle\begin{split}b_{uv}&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(1+\dfrac{\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mu_{v}\|_{2}^{2}+(\mu_{u}^{\top}\mu_{v})^{2}}{\mathcal{V}_{u}^{2}\|\mu_{v}\|^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{v}^{2}\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/2},&\text{if $u\neq v,$}\\
\left(1+\dfrac{\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\mathcal{V}_{u}^{2}}\right)^{1/2},&\text{if $u=v,$}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

(15)

with
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}=\|(\mathbb{I}_{n}-UU^{\top})y^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}/n
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. For example, we can ensure that all the entrywise asymptotic coverages are at least
1-\alpha
by letting
\rho=\sup_{u,v}b_{uv}
.
Another possibility is to let
\rho
be the root of a nonlinear equation, which ensures the average of the asymptotic entrywise coverages is
1-\alpha
; we discuss the associated nonlinear equation along with the rationale behind possible choices of
\rho
at the end of Section
3.3
. Based on our experiences with finite sample simulations, we recommend choosing
\rho
to the mean of
b_{uv}
for
1\leq u\leq v\leq p
as a computationally efficient strategy. This performed well with both median and average entrywise coverages close to
1-\alpha
. In what follows, we only consider the CC-FABLE version of our approach for theoretical justification, simulation results, and the real data application.

2.3
Hyperparameter Choice

Before obtaining pseudo-posterior samples of the covariance matrix, one needs to carefully choose two key hyperparameters. These are the number of factors
k
and the global variance of the factor loadings
\tau^{2}
. To estimate
k
, we use the approach described in
Chen and Li, 2022
. The authors construct an information criterion
\mbox{JIC}(k)
by adding the penalty
\nu(k,n,p)=k(n\vee p)\log(n\wedge p)
to the model deviance. The proposed
\mbox{JIC}(k)
criterion is similar in spirit to the Akaike information criterion (AIC) or the Bayesian information criterion (BIC)
(
Akaike, 1974
;
Schwarz, 1978
)
. Given an upper bound
\mathcal{K}_{0}
for the possible values of
k
, we estimate
\widehat{k}=\min_{1\leq k\leq\mathcal{K}_{0}}\mbox{JIC}(k).

To choose an upper bound
\mathcal{K}_{0}
for plausible values of
k
, we consider the singular values
s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\ldots\geq s_{n\wedge p}
of
\mathbf{Y}
and let
\mathcal{K}_{0}
be the smallest integer
\mathcal{K}
such that
\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{K}}s_{j}\right)/\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n\wedge p}s_{j}\right)\geq S_{0}
, where we typically let
S_{0}
be
0.5
or
0.75
. In our experience across simulation studies and applications to gene expression datasets, we found this choice of
\widehat{k}
to perform well.

We next proceed to estimate
\tau^{2}
, which can be interpreted as the global shrinkage parameter of the loadings. For this, we employ an empirical Bayes approach. Since
\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}\mid\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2},\tau^{2}\sim N_{k}(0,\tau^{2}\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}\mathbb{I}_{k})
for
j=1,\ldots,p

a priori
, we obtain an estimate of
\tau^{2}
by conditioning on
(\widetilde{\lambda}_{j},\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2})
and then maximizing the conditional likelihood, leading to

\widehat{\tau}_{0}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left({\|\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}}/{\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}}\right)/(kp).

Following from the pseudo-posterior updates (
10
) and (
11
), we simply estimate
k
by
\widehat{k}
as before,
\|\widetilde{\lambda}_{j}\|_{2}^{2}
by
\mathcal{L}_{j}^{2}=\|U^{\top}y^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}/n
, and
\widetilde{\sigma}_{j}^{2}
by
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}=\|(\mathbb{I}_{n}-UU^{\top})y^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}/n
for
j=1,\ldots,p
. We remark here that
U
is dependent on the estimate
\widehat{k}
.
This leads us to the plug-in estimate of
\tau^{2}
given by

\widehat{\tau}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{p\widehat{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\dfrac{\mathcal{L}_{j}^{2}}{\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}}.

(16)

The consistency of the point estimators
\mathcal{L}_{j}^{2}
and
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}
for
1\leq j\leq p
is proven in Section
E
, Lemma
E.4
of the Supplementary Material. Lastly, we observed that inference using the CC-FABLE procedure is not sensitive to
(\gamma_{0},\delta_{0}^{2})
, and thus we use the default values
\gamma_{0}=\delta_{0}^{2}=1
in practice.
Once the number of factors
k
and the common variance
\tau^{2}
have been estimated using
\widehat{k}
and
\widehat{\tau}^{2}
, respectively, we then proceed to obtain pseudo-posterior samples of
\Psi
using CC-FABLE as described before.

2.4
Final Algorithm

In this subsection, we provide an algorithm for implementing CC-FABLE on a given data set to obtain pseudo-posterior samples of the high-dimensional covariance matrix. We first tune the relevant hyperparameters
k
and
\tau^{2}
as in Section
2.3
and proceed to obtain coverage-corrected samples as in Section
2.2
. The relevant steps are described in Algorithm
1
.

Input:
The data matrix
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
, number of Monte Carlo (MC) samples
N_{0}
, inverse-gamma hyperparameters
(\gamma_{0},\sigma_{0}^{2}),
and the upper bound on the cumulative singular value proportion
{S}_{0}
. Let
r=n\wedge p
.

Step 1:
Compute the SVD of
\mathbf{Y}
as
\mathbf{Y}=U^{*}D^{*}V^{*\top}
with
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},D\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},V\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times r}
, and
D=\mbox{diag}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{r})
such that
s_{1}\geq s_{2}\geq\ldots\geq s_{r}\geq 0.

Step 2:

Let
\widehat{k}={\mbox{argmin}}\,\{\mbox{JIC}(k^{*})\,|\,k^{*}=1,\ldots,\mathcal{K}_{0}\},
where
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\mbox{argmin}_{1\leq\mathcal{K}\leq r}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathcal{K}}s_{j}\right)/\left(\sum_{j=1}^{r}s_{j}\right).

Step 3:
Let
U
consist of the first
\widehat{k}
columns of
U^{*}
. For
1\leq j\leq p
, let
y^{(j)}
denote the
j
th column of
\mathbf{Y}
, and obtain

\mathcal{L}_{j}^{2}=\|U^{\top}y^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}/n
and

\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}=\|(\mathbb{I}_{n}-UU^{\top})y^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}/n.

Step 4:
Estimate
\tau^{2}
by

\widehat{\tau}^{2}=\dfrac{1}{p\widehat{k}}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\dfrac{\mathcal{L}_{j}^{2}}{\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}}.

Step 5:
For
1\leq j\leq p
, let
\mu_{j}=\sqrt{n}U^{\top}y^{(j)}/\left(n+\widehat{\tau}^{-2}\right).

For
1\leq u,v\leq p
, let

\displaystyle\begin{split}b_{uv}&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\left(1+\dfrac{\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\mu_{v}\|_{2}^{2}+(\mu_{u}^{\top}\mu_{v})^{2}}{\mathcal{V}_{u}^{2}\|\mu_{v}\|^{2}+\mathcal{V}_{v}^{2}\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}}\right)^{1/2},&\text{if $u\neq v,$}\\
\left(1+\dfrac{\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}}{2\mathcal{V}_{u}^{2}}\right)^{1/2},&\text{if $u=v,$}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

and let
\rho=\overline{b}=\left(\sum_{1\leq u\leq v\leq p}b_{uv}\right)/\{p(p+1)/2\}
be the coverage-correction factor.

Step 6:
Let
\gamma_{n}=\gamma_{0}+n
. For
j=1,\ldots,p
, evaluate

\gamma_{n}\delta_{j}^{2}=\gamma_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}+y^{(j)\top}\left(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\dfrac{nUU^{\top}}{n+\widehat{\tau}^{-2}}\right)y^{(j)}.

Step 7:
For each
t=1,\ldots,N_{0}
, independently sample
({\lambda}_{C,j}^{(t)},{\sigma}_{C,j}^{(t)2})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
from

{\sigma}_{C,j}^{(t)2}\sim\mbox{IG}\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{n}}{2},\dfrac{\gamma_{n}\delta_{j}^{2}}{2}\right),\qquad{\lambda}_{C,j}^{(t)}\mid{\sigma}_{C,j}^{(t)2}\sim N_{k}\left(\mu_{j},\dfrac{\rho^{2}{\sigma}_{C,j}^{(t)2}}{n+\widehat{\tau}^{-2}}\mathbb{I}_{k}\right).

Form
{\Lambda}_{C}^{(t)}=[{\lambda}_{C,1}^{(t)},\ldots,{\lambda}_{C,p}^{(t)}]^{\top}
and
{\Sigma}_{C}^{(t)}=\mbox{diag}({\sigma}_{C,1}^{(t)2},\ldots,{\sigma}_{C,p}^{(t)2})
.

Step 8:

For each
t=1,\ldots,N_{0}
, let
L_{C}^{(t)}=\Lambda_{C}^{(t)}\Lambda_{C}^{(t)\top}
, and compute the
t
th coverage-corrected sample as

\Psi_{C}^{(t)}=L_{C}^{(t)}+\Sigma_{C}^{(t)}.

Output:
The
N_{0}
MC samples of the covariance matrix
\Psi_{C}^{(1)},\ldots,\Psi_{C}^{(N_{0})}
.

3
Theoretical Support

3.1
Setup and Assumptions

In this section, we provide theoretical guarantees of the proposed CC-FABLE procedure in estimating the high-dimensional covariance matrix. Most of the existing literature
(
Pati et al., 2014
;
Bhattacharya and Dunson, 2011
)
on obtaining provable guarantees for Bayesian factor models utilizes the machinery of Bayes’ rule in deriving posterior contraction rates. There is a clear need for further theoretical work on uncertainty quantification in such models.
Xie and Wu, 2023
consider a signal-plus-noise model and derive theoretical properties of a quasi-posterior based approach, with guarantees on asymptotic frequentist coverage of the quasi-posterior intervals. However, their model only considers a square signal matrix, with the underlying focus different from high-dimensional covariance estimation. In contrast, the proposed method carries out inference on the underlying covariance matrix, without directly relying on Bayes’ rule to obtain the pseudo-posterior samples, which makes theoretical analysis of the proposed approach challenging. We overcome this challenge by leveraging on a blessing of dimensionality phenomenon which requires both
n
and
p=p_{n}
to grow, providing results on both the pseudo-posterior contraction and uncertainty quantification of our approach. The blessing of dimensionality phenomenon allows accurate estimation of the latent factor subspace up to rotational ambiguity, which serves as an important component behind the theoretical results. The proofs of all the theorems can be found in Sections
B
-
D
of the Supplementary Material.

We assume the following data-generating model:

y_{i}=\Lambda_{0}\eta_{0i}+\epsilon_{i},

(17)

where
\epsilon_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{p}(0,\Sigma_{0})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
with
\Sigma_{0}=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{01}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{0p}^{2}),
and
\eta_{0i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{k}(0,\mathbb{I}_{k})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
. Here,
\Lambda_{0}=[\lambda_{01},\ldots,\lambda_{0p}]^{\top}
is the true factor loadings matrix while
\eta_{0i}
are the true latent factors; integrating them out provides us
y_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{p}(0,\Lambda_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}+\Sigma_{0})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
as the marginal distribution of the data. Let
\mathbf{Y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
and
M_{0}=[\eta_{01},\ldots,\eta_{0n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
be the data matrix and the true matrix of latent factors, respectively, so that the true data generating model may be written as

\mathbf{Y}=M_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}+E,

(18)

where
E=[\epsilon_{1},\ldots,\epsilon_{n}]^{\top}.
Our primary goal is the inference for the covariance matrix
\Psi_{0}=\Lambda_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}+\Sigma_{0}.

For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{1}\times n_{2}}

s_{1}(A)\geq\ldots\geq s_{n_{1}\wedge n_{2}}(A)

and

as

.
We assume the following conditions on the true data-generating model:

Assumption 3.1
.

We have
p_{n}\to\infty
and
(\log p_{n})/n=o(1)

as

.

Assumption 3.2
.

s_{k}(\Lambda_{0})\asymp\|\Lambda_{0}\|\asymp\sqrt{p_{n}}

,

, and
\underset{1\leq j\leq p_{n}}{\min}\|\lambda_{0j}\|_{2}\geq c_{1}

The hyperparameters

Such assumptions are standard in the existing literature on asymptotic theoretical properties of latent factor models. Assumption

Let

3.3
Uncertainty Quantification

We now consider uncertainty quantification of entrywise elements of the true covariance matrix
\Psi_{0}
. For
1\leq u,v\leq p,
let
{\Psi}_{C,uv}
and
\Psi_{0,uv}
denote the
uv
th element of
{\Psi}_{C}
and
\Psi_{0}
, respectively, where
\Psi_{C}
denotes the coverage-corrected pseudo-posterior sample obtained from the CC-FABLE discussed in Section
2.2
. For
1\leq u,v\leq p
, we have
\Psi_{0,uv}=\lambda_{0u}^{\top}\lambda_{0v}+\sigma_{0u}^{2}\mathbbm{1}(u=v)
, where
\mathbbm{1}
denotes the indicator function. In what follows, we discuss results for a fixed value of the coverage-correction factor
\rho>0
and then provide an approach to appropriately choose
\rho
.

We now let
T_{uv}=\mu_{u}^{\top}\mu_{v}+\delta_{u}^{2}\mathbbm{1}(u=v)
be an estimator of
\Psi_{0,uv}
. Then, we have the following result approximating the pseudo-posterior distribution of
{\Psi}_{C,uv}
by a suitable Gaussian distribution centered at
T_{uv}
as both
n,p_{n}
increase with
\rho>0
fixed.

Theorem 3.7
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
–
3.4
hold and let
\sqrt{n}/p_{n}=o(1)
.
For
1\leq u,v\leq p
, let

\displaystyle\begin{split}l_{0,uv}^{2}(\rho)&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\rho^{2}(\sigma_{0v}^{2}\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{0u}^{2}\|\lambda_{0v}\|_{2}^{2}),&\text{for $u\neq v$},\\
2\sigma_{0u}^{4}+4\rho^{2}\,\sigma_{0u}^{2}\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2},&\text{for $u=v$.}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Then, as
n\to\infty,

\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\,\left|{\Pi}_{C}\left\{\dfrac{\sqrt{n}({\Psi}_{C,uv}-T_{uv})}{\l_{0,uv}(\rho)}\leq x\right\}-\Phi(x)\right|\overset{P_{0}}{\to}0\quad\text{for all $1\leq u,v\leq p.$}

Theorem
3.7
allows us to approximate the asymptotic pseudo-posterior distribution of each element of the covariance matrix after suitable centering and scaling using a Gaussian distribution with mean
0
and variance
l_{0,uv}^{2}(\rho)
. The proof of Theorem
3.7
is in Section
C
of the Supplementary Material. We next state a result regarding the asymptotic law of the quantity
\sqrt{n}\,(T_{uv}-\Psi_{0,uv})/\mathcal{S}_{0,uv}
, demonstrate how this result can be used to obtain asymptotic entrywise frequentist coverage for
\Psi_{0,uv}
using CC-FABLE, and outline different choices of
\rho
. For general random variables
X_{n}
and
X
, we denote
X_{n}
converging in distribution to
X
by
X_{n}\implies X.

Theorem 3.8
.

Suppose the Assumptions of Theorem
3.7
hold. For
1\leq u,v\leq p
, let

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathcal{S}_{0,uv}^{2}&=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sigma_{0v}^{2}\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{0u}^{2}\|\lambda_{0v}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2}\|\lambda_{0v}\|_{2}^{2}+(\lambda_{0u}^{\top}\lambda_{0v})^{2},&\text{for $u\neq v$},\\
2(\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{0u}^{2})^{2},&\text{for $u=v$.}\end{array}\right.\end{split}

Then, as
n\to\infty
, one has

\sqrt{n}(T_{uv}-\Psi_{0,uv})/\mathcal{S}_{0,uv}\implies N(0,1)
for all
1\leq u,v\leq p.

The proof of Theorem
3.8
is available in Section
D
of the Supplementary Material. Under Theorem
3.7
, the
100(1-\alpha)\%
asymptotic pseudo-posterior credible interval of
{\Psi}_{C,uv}
is given by

\mathcal{C}_{uv}(\rho)=\left[T_{uv}\pm z_{1-(\alpha/2)}\dfrac{l_{0,uv}(\rho)}{\sqrt{n}}\right],

where
z_{1-(\alpha/2)}=\Phi^{-1}\left\{1-(\alpha/2)\right\}.

Thus, the probability of
\mathcal{C}_{uv}(\rho)
covering
\Psi_{0,uv}
under repeated sampling is given by

\displaystyle P_{0}\left\{\Psi_{0,uv}\in\mathcal{C}_{uv}(\rho)\right\}

\displaystyle=P_{0}\left\{\dfrac{\sqrt{n}\,\left|T_{uv}-\Psi_{0,uv}\right|}{\mathcal{S}_{0,uv}}\leq z_{1-(\alpha/2)}\dfrac{l_{0,uv}(\rho)}{\mathcal{S}_{0,uv}}\right\}

\displaystyle\to q_{uv}(\rho):=2\Phi\left\{z_{1-(\alpha/2)}\dfrac{l_{0,uv}(\rho)}{\mathcal{S}_{0,uv}}\right\}-1,

as
n\to\infty,
using Theorem
3.8
.

The form of
q_{uv}(\rho)
suggests a general data-adaptive framework to choose
\rho
. For example, we can ensure that all the entrywise asymptotic coverages are at least
1-\alpha
by letting
\rho=\sup_{u,v}b_{uv}
for
1\leq u\leq v\leq p
, with
b_{uv}
defined in Section
2.2
. To see why, first let
\hat{q}_{uv}(\rho)
be the consistent estimator of
q_{uv}(\rho)
obtained by substituting
\sigma_{0j}^{2}
and
\lambda_{0j}
with
\mathcal{V}_{j}^{2}
and
\mu_{j}
, respectively, for
j=1,\ldots,p
; these quantities are defined in Section
2
. Next, observe that
\hat{q}_{uv}(\rho)
is a non-decreasing function of
\rho>0
for all
1\leq u,v\leq p
. It is clear that
\hat{q}_{uv}(b_{uv})=1-\alpha
for all
1\leq u\leq v\leq p.
Thus,
\hat{q}_{uv}\left(\sup_{u^{\prime},v^{\prime}}b_{u^{\prime}v^{\prime}}\right)\geq\hat{q}_{uv}(b_{uv})=1-\alpha.
Alternatively, we can ensure the average of the entrywise asymptotic coverages is
1-\alpha
by numerically solving

\dfrac{2}{p(p+1)}\displaystyle\sum_{1\leq u\leq v\leq p}\hat{q}_{uv}(\rho)=1-\alpha

(19)

for
\rho
. We observed in simulation experiments that simply letting

\rho=\overline{b}=\left(\sum_{1\leq u\leq v\leq p}b_{uv}\right)/\left\{\dfrac{p(p+1)}{2}\right\}

is a close approximation to the root of (
19
). In practice, we thus recommend choosing
\rho=\overline{b}
for computational convenience, as this avoids solving the nonlinear equation. This worked well in the simulations we carried out, both from the perspective of median and average asymptotic entrywise coverages.
Since Lemma
E.5
in Section

satisfies
\|B\|_{\infty}=O_{P_{0}}(1)
, all the aforementioned estimates of

are bounded.

4
Simulation Results

4.1
Setup

In this Section, we compare the proposed CC-FABLE approach with competitors when judging their performance from the viewpoint of estimation error, uncertainty quantification (UQ), and computational efficiency. With
\Lambda_{0}=(\Lambda_{0,uv})_{1\leq u,v\leq p}
the true matrix of loadings and
\Sigma_{0}=\mbox{diag}(\sigma_{01}^{2},\ldots,\sigma_{0p}^{2})
the true matrix of idiosyncratic error variances, the data generating covariance matrix is given by
\Psi_{0}=\Lambda_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}+\Sigma_{0}
. For both the estimation error and UQ experiments, we proceed as follows. We vary the number of samples
n\in\{500,1000\}
, the number of dimensions
p\in\{1000,5000\}
, and let the true number of factors be
k=10.
For each possible
(n,p)\in\{500,1000\}\times\{1000,5000\}
, we generate
(\Lambda_{0},\Sigma_{0})
as

\displaystyle\Lambda_{0,jl}

\displaystyle\overset{ind}{\sim}0.5\,\widetilde{\delta}_{0}+0.5\,N(0,0.5^{2})

for all
1\leq j\leq p,1\leq l\leq k
, and

\displaystyle\sigma_{0j}^{2}

\displaystyle\overset{ind}{\sim}\mathcal{U}(0.5,5),

for all
1\leq j\leq p,

where
\widetilde{\delta}_{0}
denotes a point mass at
0
. For each combination
(n,p)
, we use the generated
\Psi_{0}=\Lambda_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}+\Sigma_{0}
and replicate the data generating process
R=100
times. Given an estimator
\widehat{\Psi}_{0}
of
\Psi_{0}
, we judge the estimator using the relative spectral error, defined as

\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{0},\widehat{\Psi}_{0})=\dfrac{\|\Psi_{0}-\widehat{\Psi}_{0}\|}{\|\Psi_{0}\|},

where
\|A\|=\sup_{\|x\|_{2}=1}\|Ax\|_{2}
. When using a Bayesian method, we consider
\widehat{\Psi}_{0}
to be the posterior mean. For a given
(n,p)
and a particular replicate, we obtain
\mathcal{L}(\Psi_{0},\widehat{\Psi}_{0})
given an estimator
\widehat{\Psi}_{0}
and then average this quantity over the replicates. For the UQ experiment, we simply consider the
95\%
entrywise credible/pseudo-credible intervals obtained and compute the frequentist coverage over the
R=100
replicates. To ease the computational burden, we consider the coverage of a randomly chosen
100\times 100
submatrix of
\Psi_{0}
, corresponding to the covariance of
100
randomly chosen variables held fixed across replicates, for each
(n,p).
Lastly, we present a runtime analysis of the different methods considered in the simulation studies. First, we compute the theoretical computational complexity of the proposed CC-FABLE method. Next, we carry out numerical experiments to compare the computational runtimes for the different approaches.

As competitors to the proposed CC-FABLE approach, we consider the multiplicative gamma shrinkage prior formulation
(
Bhattacharya and Dunson, 2011
)
and the approach in
Ročková and George, 2016
and denote them by MGSP and ROTATE, respectively, across all the simulations. The approach in
Bhattacharya and Dunson, 2011
implements posterior sampling with Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), yielding posterior samples of the covariance matrix, from which we extract the posterior mean and entrywise
95\%
credible intervals. In contrast, the approach of
Ročková and George, 2016
only provides a point estimate of the covariance matrix, using expectation-maximization (EM) algorithms to approximate the posterior mean corresponding to spike and slab prior distributions on the factor loadings. As a result, we do not consider ROTATE for our UQ experiments. For CC-FABLE, we consider
1000
Monte Carlo iterates; for estimation error experiments, we use the pseudo-posterior mean, which is analytically available. For MGSP, we consider
3000
MCMC iterates and discard the first
1000
as burn-in. As described earlier, CC-FABLE does not require MCMC for inference, instead providing direct Monte Carlo samples. To implement MGSP, we used the
infinitefactor
package
(
Poworoznek et al., 2021
)

4.4
Computational Efficiency

For the purposes of computing order complexity, we assume
k
is fixed and
n,p
vary. We first consider the order complexity when generating
N_{0}
pseudo-posterior samples of the covariance matrix. The first stage SVD of the
n\times p
matrix
\mathbf{Y}
has
\mathcal{O}(np\min(n,p))
complexity
(
Vasudevan and Ramakrishna, 2017
)
, which needs to be only computed once. Computing the
p
hyperparameters
\mu_{j}
and
\delta_{j}^{2}
for
j=1,\ldots,p
has
\mathcal{O}(np)
complexity, while generating each Monte Carlo sample of
({\Lambda}_{C},{\Sigma}_{C})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
has
\mathcal{O}(p)
complexity since
({\lambda}_{C,j},{\sigma}_{C,j}^{2})
are independently generated for
j=1,\ldots,p
. Lastly, obtaining
\Psi_{C}=\Lambda_{C}\Lambda_{C}^{\top}+\Sigma_{C}
has
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
complexity for each sample, leading to
\mathcal{O}(p^{2}N_{0})
complexity to obtain
N_{0}
samples. Thus, obtaining
N_{0}
pseudo-posterior samples of the covariance matrix after coverage-correction has
\mathcal{O}\{p^{2}N_{0}+pN_{0}+np\min(n,p)\}=\mathcal{O}\{p^{2}N_{0}+np\min(n,p)\}
complexity. Next, we consider the order complexity to obtain the pseudo-posterior mean of the covariance. As before, we would require computing the SVD with
\mathcal{O}\{np\min(n,p)\}
complexity, while computing the hyperparameters
(\mu_{j},\delta_{j}^{2})
for
j=1,\ldots,p
has
\mathcal{O}(np)
complexity. Obtaining the matrix
G=G_{0}G_{0}^{\top}
from
G_{0}
has
\mathcal{O}(p^{2})
complexity.
Thus, computing the pseudo-posterior mean of
\Psi
has asymptotic complexity
\mathcal{O}\{p^{2}+np\min(n,p)\}
.

The runtime for both sampling and pseudo-posterior mean computation may be substantially improved by considering computationally efficient implementations of the SVD step such as the randomized SVD
(
Halko et al., 2011
)
when either
n
or
p
is very large. Additionally, since the CC-FABLE algorithm employs embarrassingly parallel sampling, the computational runtime would be further improved by parallelization of the computations across cores.

In Figure
1
, we compare the three methods CC-FABLE, MGSP, and ROTATE in terms of obtaining (pseudo) posterior samples and point estimates of the covariance matrix. We consider the same data-generating scenario as in Section
4.1
. We fix
n=500
and vary
p
from
500
to
5000
in increments of
500
, obtaining runtime measurements across
R=20
independent replicates for each choice of
p
. As before, we consider
3000
MCMC iterations for MGSP and discard the first
1000
iterates as burn-in. The experiments were carried out on an M1 MacBook Pro with 32 GB of RAM (random-access memory). We compare CC-FABLE with MGSP when obtaining samples of the factor loadings and the error variances, and with ROTATE when obtaining a point estimate of the covariance matrix. In both cases, CC-FABLE is faster compared to its competitors. When obtaining samples, CC-FABLE is about
150-200
times faster than MGSP, with this ratio remaining fairly constant as
p
increases. When obtaining a point estimator of the covariance, CC-FABLE is at least twice as fast as ROTATE.

5
Application

In this Section, we apply our method to identify associations between expressed genes in a high-throughput sequencing immune cell dataset. Identifying gene expression associations in varying immune cell populations and among multiple lineages is critical to understanding the inflammatory response to infectious and chronic diseases
(
Ota et al., 2021
;
Saini et al., 2022
)
. This enables subsequent detection of corresponding cellular and molecular pathways underlying pathological conditions. For this application, we consider the GSE109125 dataset, which contains RNAseq data from 127 highly purified immune cell lineages including adaptive and innate lymphocytes, myeloid cells, mast cells, and neutrophils. The lineages along with the associated differentiation cascades were profiled using the ImmGen ULI pipeline
(
Yoshida et al., 2019
)
.

We first log-transformed raw data counts
R_{ij}
for the
i
th cell and
j
th gene as
y^{*}_{ij}=\log_{2}(R_{ij}+1)
. Next, we proceed to center the data for analysis before fitting the latent factor model. Finally, we use the
genefilter
package in
R

(
Gentleman et al., 2023
)
to filter relevant genes. In this case, we filtered genes according to their variance and considered the top
10\%
of genes with the highest variances. There were no cells with missing entries.
After carrying out appropriate pre-processing of the dataset, we obtain expression data
\mathbf{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
for
p=5300
genes measured on
n=205
cells.

We compare results from applying the three methods CC-FABLE, MGSP, and ROTATE on the data. The fitted log-likelihoods using the whole data
\mathbf{Y}
are
-490006.4
,
-503574
, and
-728959.9
for CC-FABLE, MGSP, and ROTATE, respectively. This suggests that CC-FABLE fits the data the best among the three methods. The estimated rank when using the

criterion as highlighted in Section
2.3
is
\widehat{k}=30
. As a posterior predictive check, we investigated the average coverage of entrywise predictive intervals across all the entries of
\mathbf{Y}
. When using CC-FABLE, the average predictive coverage is
0.97
. To further investigate model fit, we also looked into the proportion of variance explained for each gene using CC-FABLE. The average proportion of variance explained across variables when using CC-FABLE is
0.83
, indicating that the factor model is a good fit.

We now focus on estimating a
100\times 100
submatrix of the
5300\times 5300
covariance matrix, corresponding to the
100
genes with the highest variability. Suppose the indices of these genes are
\mathcal{I}_{0}\subset\{1,2,\ldots,p\}
. We assess estimation performance
using out-of-sample fit on a randomly chosen

50

In Figure

Both MGSP and ROTATE exhibit an essentially monotonically decreasing trend as extra genes are added, so including additional genes does not improve inferences on the genes of interest in using these methods. In sharp contrast, CC-FABLE has rapidly improved performance initially as extra genes are added, exhibiting a clear blessing of dimensionality. After a few hundred genes, the gain levels off, and eventually there is a modest decline, perhaps due to the need to add additional factors not related to the genes of interest when large numbers of additional genes are added.

The proposed method thus shows promising results in this high-dimensional gene expression data application and compares favourably to the two state-of-the-art gold standard approaches to Bayesian factor analysis. The results obtained from CC-FABLE come at a fraction of the computational budget compared to MGSP due to its embarrassingly parallel sampling scheme requiring no MCMC. 6
Discussion

In this paper, we developed a computationally scalable approach to fit high-dimensional Bayesian latent factor models with Gaussian data. Leveraging on a blessing of dimensions, the proposed FABLE approach completely bypasses the need to carry out MCMC to provide pseudo-posterior samples of the covariance matrix for inference. By avoiding MCMC altogether, our approach averts the pitfalls of MCMC in higher dimensions, such as slow mixing and unsatisfactory convergence. Due to its embarrassingly parallel nature, the FABLE approach leads to immense computational benefits over current approaches dependent on MCMC.
We developed a coverage-correction modification to FABLE called CC-FABLE with provable guarantees on asymptotic frequentist coverage of its pseudo-credible intervals. The CC-FABLE pseudo-posterior also enjoys desirable theoretical properties such as consistency and compares favorably to state-of-the-art approaches to Bayesian factor analysis from the viewpoint of estimation error, uncertainty quantification, and computational efficiency in our numerical experiments and gene expression data application.

This work naturally leads to several important directions for future research. The first direction is to model non-Gaussian data, such as binary or count data. Such data are routinely collected in ecological studies.
For example,
y_{ij}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,p
could indicate the presence/absence or abundance of species
j
in sample
i
. For presence/absence data, let
\mbox{P}(y_{ij}=1\mid\alpha_{0},\eta_{i},\lambda_{j})=\mbox{F}\left(\alpha_{0}+\eta_{i}^{\top}\lambda_{j}\right),
with
\eta_{i}\overset{iid}{\sim}N_{k}(0,\mathbb{I}_{k})
. Here,
F
is a link function, such as the probit or the logistic link. The observations
y_{ij}
are mutually independent conditional on the latent factors
\eta_{i}
, but integrating them out induces dependence between
y_{i1},\ldots,y_{ip}.
Bayesian inference in such models typically proceeds via a data augmentation Gibbs sampler by introducing auxiliary continuous variables
z_{ij}
and alternating between sampling
\eta_{i},\lambda_{j},
and
z_{ij}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
and
j=1,\ldots,p
given the data
\mathbf{Y}=(y_{ij})
using MCMC
(
Albert and Chib, 1993
;
Polson et al., 2013
)
. However, in our motivating ecological settings involving insects and fungi,
p

is on the order of
10,000-100,000
, rendering current MCMC-based Bayesian factor analytic approaches computationally infeasible. To extend FABLE to this setting, we can pre-estimate the latent factors via an SVD or an appropriate generalized linear model (GLM) variant, and then fit the resulting Bayesian GLMs in parallel for each species, potentially relying on Laplace approximations.

An additional important direction is to modify the FABLE inferential framework to
allow more intricate hierarchical modeling. For example in Bayesian factor analysis, it is often of interest to (1) include covariate effects, (2) use more elaborate priors on the loadings to favor sparsity and adaptive selection of the number of factors, (3) consider more flexible latent factor distributions, and/or (4) allow nonlinear measurement structure. In exploratory analyses, we have observed good performance for ad hoc FABLE modifications to include covariates and for simulated data having non-Gaussian latent factor distributions. Problem (2) can potentially be addressed in the second stage via MCMC algorithms implemented in parallel for the different sparse regressions after inferring the latent factors using a sparse SVD in a first stage. Problem (4) may necessitate non-linear dimensionality reduction in the first stage. Obtaining concrete implementations with theory support for problems (1)-(4) is of future interest.

The current work also hints at further investigation into the theoretical properties of Bayesian factor models. Although there are existing results focusing on posterior consistency with carefully chosen shrinkage priors on the factor loadings, these are primarily in the case where the idiosyncratic model errors are Gaussian. It would thus be interesting to explore how the blessing of dimensions influences both posterior consistency and uncertainty quantification in different settings, such as when the data are non-Gaussian.
Furthermore, there remains a clear need to extend existing theoretical results on Bayesian factor models to results on uncertainty quantification of the covariance matrix.

(20)

\sigma_{2}=\underset{j}{\max}\sqrt{\sum_{i}b_{ij}^{2}},

Proof.

Refer to Corollary 3.11 in

Lemma E.4
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
hold. For all
1\leq u,v\leq p,
we have as
n\to\infty
,

\dfrac{1}{n}y^{(u)\top}UU^{\top}y^{(v)}\overset{P_{0}}{\to}\lambda_{0u}^{\top}\lambda_{0v}

and

\dfrac{1}{n}\|(\mathbb{I}_{n}-UU^{\top})y^{(u)}\|_{2}^{2}\overset{P_{0}}{\to}\sigma_{0u}^{2}.

Proof.

We first note the identity

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{n}y^{(u)\top}UU^{\top}y^{(v)}-\lambda_{0u}^{\top}\lambda_{0v}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,\lambda_{0u}^{\top}\left(\dfrac{M_{0}^{\top}M_{0}}{n}-\mathbb{I}_{k}\right)\lambda_{0v}+\dfrac{1}{n}(\lambda_{0u}^{\top}M_{0}^{\top}\epsilon^{(v)}+\lambda_{0v}^{\top}M_{0}^{\top}\epsilon^{(u)})\,

\displaystyle+\dfrac{1}{n}\epsilon^{(u)\top}U_{0}U_{0}^{\top}\epsilon^{(v)}+\dfrac{1}{n}y^{(u)\top}(UU^{\top}-U_{0}U_{0}^{\top})y^{(v)}.

he first term is bounded by
\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}\|\lambda_{0v}\|_{2}\|(M_{0}^{\top}M_{0}/n)-\mathbb{I}_{k}\|\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n}
with probability at least
1-o(1)
, using Lemma
E.1
. The second term is handled by first observing that
\lambda_{0u}^{\top}M_{0}^{\top}\epsilon^{(v)}\mid M_{0}\sim N\left(0,\sigma_{0v}^{2}\lambda_{0u}^{\top}M_{0}^{\top}M_{0}\lambda_{0u}\right)
and then Lemma
E.1
to argue that
|\lambda_{0u}^{\top}M_{0}^{\top}\epsilon^{(v)}|\lesssim 1/\sqrt{n}
with probability at least
1-o(1).

The third term is seen to be
O_{P_{0}}(1/n)
and the fourth term is seen to be
O_{P_{0}}(n^{-1}+p_{n}^{-1})
using Proposition
3.5
along with
\|y^{(u)}\|_{2}\lesssim\sqrt{n}
, with probability at least
1-o(1)
. Therefore, one has the desired result for all
1\leq u,v\leq p
.

To derive the second result, we first observe that
\|y^{(u)}\|_{2}^{2}\sim(\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{0u}^{2})\,\chi_{n}^{2}
. An application of the above result along with the weak law of large numbers provides that this quantity converges in probability to
\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}+\sigma_{0u}^{2}-\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}^{2}=\sigma_{0u}^{2}
for any
1\leq u\leq p.

∎

Lemma E.5
.

Let
B=(b_{uv})_{1\leq u,v\leq p}
denote the matrix of coefficients as in Section
2.2
. Then,
\|B\|_{\infty}=O_{P_{0}}(1)
under Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
.

Proof.

First, consider
u,v
such that
u\neq v
.
Following from Assumption
3.2
, there exists
W_{1}>0
such that
\|\lambda_{0u}\|_{2}>c_{1}
for all
1\leq u\leq p
. Let
c_{2}=\min_{u}\sigma_{0u}^{2}>0
from Assumption
3.3
. Following from Lemma
E.4
, we have for sufficiently large

1-o(1)
: (1)
\|\mu_{u}\|_{2}^{2}>c_{1}/2

Lemma E.6
.

Suppose Assumptions
3.1
-
3.4
hold. For each
j=1,\ldots,p
, we have

\delta_{j}^{2}=\dfrac{Z_{j}}{n}+F_{j},

where
Z_{j}/\sigma_{0j}^{2}\sim\chi_{n-k}^{2}
and

\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|F_{j}|\lesssim\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{p_{n}}

with probability at least
1-o(1)
.

Proof.

Suppose the SVD of
X_{0}=M_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}=U_{0}D_{0}V_{0}^{\top}
. Let
P_{U_{0}}=U_{0}(U_{0}^{\top}U_{0})^{-1}U_{0}^{\top}=U_{0}U_{0}^{\top}
denote the projection matrix onto the column space of
U_{0}
. We can express
\delta_{j}^{2}
as

\delta_{j}^{2}=\dfrac{Z_{j}}{n}+F_{j},

where we have

\displaystyle Z_{j}

\displaystyle=y^{(j)\top}\left(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}}\right)y^{(j)},

\displaystyle F_{j}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\gamma_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}}{\gamma_{n}}+\dfrac{1}{n}y^{(j)\top}\left(P_{U_{0}}-\dfrac{\widehat{M}\widehat{M}^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right)y^{(j)}-\dfrac{\gamma_{0}}{n\gamma_{n}}y^{(j)\top}\left(\mathbb{I}_{n}-\dfrac{\widehat{M}\widehat{M}^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right)y^{(j)}.

Letting
\mathbf{Y}=M_{0}\Lambda_{0}^{\top}+E
where
E=[\epsilon^{(1)}\ldots\epsilon^{(p)}]
, we have
(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}})\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}})E
, implying
(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}})y^{(j)}=(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}})\epsilon^{(j)}\sim N_{n}(0,\sigma_{0j}^{2}(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}}))
. Since
\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}}
is idempotent (i.e.,
(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}})^{2}=\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}}
), we have

\dfrac{Z_{j}}{\sigma_{0j}^{2}}=\dfrac{\|\left(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}}\right)\,y^{(j)}\|_{2}^{2}}{\sigma_{0j}^{2}}\sim\chi_{\mbox{tr}(\mathbb{I}_{n}-P_{U_{0}})}^{2}\equiv\chi_{n-k}^{2}.

We now obtain the stated probabilistic upper bound on
|F_{j}|.
We observe

\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|F_{j}|\leq\dfrac{\gamma_{0}\delta_{0}^{2}}{\gamma_{n}}+\dfrac{1}{n}\left\|P_{U_{0}}-\dfrac{nUU^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right\|\max_{1\leq j\leq p}||y^{(j)}||_{2}^{2}+\dfrac{\gamma_{0}}{n\gamma_{n}}\left\|\mathbb{I}_{n}-\dfrac{nUU^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right\|\max_{1\leq j\leq p}||y^{(j)}||_{2}^{2},

as
\widehat{M}\widehat{M}^{\top}=nUU^{\top}
. Next, we have
\max_{1\leq j\leq p}||y^{(j)}||_{2}^{2}\lesssim n
with probability at least
1-o(1)
, following Lemma 1 in
Laurent and Massart, 2000
.
We start with

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle:=\left\|P_{U_{0}}-\dfrac{nUU^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|U_{0}U_{0}^{\top}-UU^{\top}\right\|+\left\|UU^{\top}-\dfrac{nUU^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\lesssim\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{p_{n}}+\dfrac{1}{n}\lesssim\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{p_{n}},

with probability at least
1-o(1)
. The second inequality is obtained using Proposition
3.5
. Next, we work with

\displaystyle G_{2}:=

\displaystyle\,\left\|\mathbb{I}_{n}-\dfrac{nUU^{\top}}{n+\tau^{-2}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,\|\mathbb{I}_{n}-UU^{\top}\|+\dfrac{\tau^{-2}}{n}\|UU^{\top}\|

\displaystyle\lesssim

\displaystyle\,1.

Combining the bounds for
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
,
we have with probability at least
1-o(1),

\max_{1\leq j\leq p}|F_{j}|\lesssim\dfrac{1}{n}+\dfrac{1}{p_{n}}.

∎

Lemma F.2
.

Suppose in the setup of Theorem
3.7
, we have
T_{n}=Z_{n}+Y_{n}
for random variables
T_{n},Z_{n},Y_{n}
such that the pseudo-posterior of
Y_{n}
concentrates around
0
as
n,p\to\infty
and the pseudo-posterior of
Z_{n}
is the
N(0,1)
density. Then as
n,p\to\infty,

\sup_{x}\,\left|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x)-\Phi(x)\right|\overset{P_{0}}{\to}0.

Proof.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. The concentration property of
Y_{n}
implies that
{\Pi}_{C}(|Y_{n}|>\epsilon)\overset{P_{0}}{\to}0
as
n,p\to\infty.

Fix
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Using the triangle inequality, one obtains

\displaystyle\left|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x)-\Phi(x)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x,|Y_{n}|\leq\epsilon)-\Phi(x)\right|+{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x,|Y_{n}|>\epsilon),

with the second term bounded by
{\Pi}_{C}(|Y_{n}|>\epsilon)\overset{P_{0}}{\to}0.

The first term may be decomposed as

\displaystyle\left|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x,|Y_{n}|\leq\epsilon)-\Phi(x)\right|

\displaystyle=S_{1}+S_{2},

where
S_{1}=|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x,|Y_{n}|\leq\epsilon)-\Phi(x)|\mathbbm{1}(Y_{n}>0)
and
S_{2}=|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x,|Y_{n}|\leq\epsilon)-\Phi(x)|\mathbbm{1}(Y_{n}\leq 0).
We first consider

\displaystyle S_{1}=

\displaystyle\left|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x,|Y_{n}|\leq\epsilon)-\Phi(x)\right|\mathbbm{1}(Y_{n}>0)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left|{\Pi}_{C}(Z_{n}\leq x-Y_{n},Y_{n}\leq\epsilon)-{\Pi}_{C}(Z_{n}\leq x,Y_{n}\leq\epsilon)\right|\mathbbm{1}(Y_{n}>0)+

\displaystyle{\Pi}_{C}(Z_{n}\leq x,Y_{n}>\epsilon)\mathbbm{1}(Y_{n}>0)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\,{\Pi}_{C}(x-Y_{n}\leq Z_{n}\leq x,Y_{n}\leq\epsilon)\mathbbm{1}(Y_{n}>0)+{\Pi}_{C}(|Y_{n}|>\epsilon),

where the inequality in the second line above is obtained by observing
\Phi(x)=\Pi_{C}(Z_{n}\leq x)=\Pi_{C}(Z_{n}\leq x,Y_{n}\leq\epsilon)+\Pi_{C}(Z_{n}\leq x,Y_{n}>\epsilon)
and then using the triangle inequality. The first term can be bounded by
\Phi(x)-\Phi(x-\epsilon)\leq L\epsilon
for a universal constant
L>0
, as
\Phi
is Lipschitz continuous. The second term goes to
0
in
P_{0}
-probability, and thus is less than
L\epsilon
with probability at least
1-o(1)
for sufficiently large
n
. Thus, for sufficiently large
n
,
S_{1}\leq 2L\epsilon
with probability at least
1-o(1)
. Similarly,
S_{2}\leq 2L\epsilon
with probability at least
1-o(1)
for sufficiently large
n
. Combining, we have

\sup_{x}\,\left|{\Pi}_{C}(T_{n}\leq x)-\Phi(x)\right|\leq 4L\epsilon

with probability at least
1-o(1),
for any fixed
\epsilon>0
. This shows the result. We remark here that the result may be easily extended to the case where
Z_{n}\implies N(0,1)
as
n\to\infty
analogously.
∎
