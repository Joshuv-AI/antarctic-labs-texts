A manifold learning approach to nonlinear model order reduction of quasi-static problems in solid mechanics
Lisa Scheunemann, Erik Faust

Abstract
The proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) – a popular projection-based model order reduction (MOR) method – may require significant model dimensionalities to successfully capture a nonlinear solution manifold resulting from a parameterised quasi-static solid-mechanical problem. The local basis method by Amsallem et al. [ 1 ] addresses this deficiency by introducing a locally, rather than globally, linear approximation of the solution manifold. However, this generally successful approach comes with some limitations, especially in the data-poor setting. In this proof-of-concept investigation, we instead propose a graph-based manifold learning approach to nonlinear projection-based MOR which uses a global, continuously nonlinear approximation of the solution manifold. Approximations of local tangents to the solution manifold, which are necessary for a Galerkin scheme, are computed in the online phase. As an example application for the resulting nonlinear MOR algorithms, we consider simple representative volume element computations. On this example, the manifold learning approach Pareto-dominates the POD and local basis method in terms of the error and runtime achieved using a range of model dimensionalities.

Engineers and scientists can use parametric model order reduction (p-MOR) methods to considerably accelerate simulation workflows in such applications (see e.g.

Crucially, the reductions in simulation time achieved by parametric reduced order models (ROMs) in an online application phase must offset the cost incurred in training these models in the offline stage

In this publication, we are primarily concerned with broadly applicable methods which already yield suitable speed-ups in online simulation time with comparatively little training data, and thus comparatively little computational offline cost. At the same time, we aim to retain relatively high levels of accuracy and to minimise the investment of human effort required for applications to new problems.
In particular, we propose an implementationally simple projection-based MOR framework which uses graph-based manifold learning techniques to obtain a general, globally nonlinear approximation of the solution manifold in which to search for solutions.
As motivated in the following, such a method improves upon some shortcomings of existing projection-based MOR methods in handling nonlinearities in the solution manifold, particularly in the data-poor context.

Furthermore, simplified physical models might still require a solution procedure which leads to non-negligible computational cost in the online phase, meaning that further reduction is necessary.

Rather than formulating an alternative, simplified model, some p-MOR methods instead aim to accelerate the solution procedure for a given model based on data gathered in the offline phase, often by introducing an approximation space for the primary unknown variables.
There are several p-MOR methods which utilise physics- and structure-

On the other hand, specialising algorithms to problems or extending them to novel fields of application is user-time-intensive; this investment may not be recouped in every application.
The decision as to which p-MOR method may be used in a given context should thus generally be informed by the trade-offs between relevant performance metrics
[
35

Accordingly, further developments in more general p-MOR methods, such as those explored in this investigation, usefully complement specialised strategies.

An appealing approach to reduce a quasi-static solid-mechanical problem in a general way is to approximate the set of all possible solutions based on as little training data as possible, and to confine the search for solutions to this approximation.
For a well-posed parameterised problem, each possible parameter combination uniquely defines a solution and the solution is a continuous function of these parameters
[
36
]
. Thus, the set of all possible solutions defines a solution manifold: a connected set of solutions which locally resembles an Euclidean space
[
37
]
. The dimensionality of the solution manifold is determined by the parameterisation; in the case of a quasi-static RVE problem with given geometry, this may involve the macroscopic strain and, potentially, history variables. Since there are generally far fewer parameters than unknown variables in such a problem, the space in which to search for solutions can potentially be reduced considerably. In addition to the robustness that comes with the retention of the underlying solution scheme
[
23
]
, such a projection-based MOR framework generalises well to a range of physical behaviours, with moderate theoretical and implementational effort.

The proper orthogonal decomposition (POD)

,

,

,

In conjunction with the POD, hyper-reduction methods such as those proposed in

If global, nonlinear trends in these solution manifolds can be exploited efficiently this way, a manifold learning-based NLMOR scheme prove a viable alternative to local basis methods and their alternatives in accelerating RVE computations based on limited data.

Parameterised quasi-static solid mechanical problems

In this work, we investigate nonlinear projection-based MOR methods to be applied to quasi-static, parametrised solid mechanical problems discretised via the FEM. As an example application, we consider computations on an RVE in the context of computational homogenisation.
For this class of problems, the local balance of linear momentum at a point
\vec{X}
in the reference configuration can be written as

\text{Div}\bm{P}(\vec{X})-\vec{b}(\vec{X})=\vec{0}\,,

(1)

where
\bm{P}
is the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress tensor,
\vec{b}
the bulk force per volume element in the reference configuration, and
Div
the divergence operator
[
71
, p.146]
. Boundary conditions, e.g. in the displacement
\vec{\underline{u}}(\vec{X})
and the nominal traction vector
\vec{t}(\vec{X})=\bm{P}(\vec{X})\vec{N}

\vec{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})=\vec{\underline{u}}^{*}\,,\vec{X}\in\partial\Omega^{D}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{P}(\vec{X})\vec{N}=\vec{t}^{*}\,,\vec{X}\in\partial\Omega^{N}

then determine a solution
\vec{\underline{u}}(\vec{X}),\vec{X}\in\Omega
on a domain
\Omega

with boundary
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega^{D}\cup\partial\Omega^{N}

[
71
, p.146]
. Here,
\vec{\underline{u}}^{*}
and
\vec{t}^{*}
are boundary values in the displacement and nominal traction, respectively, and
\vec{N}
denotes the outward normal at
\vec{X}\in\partial\Omega^{N}
. In the context of RVEs, periodic boundary conditions, which are described in Section
7.1
, further play a crucial role.

To model the material behaviour at a point
\vec{X}
, a constitutive law describing the stress-strain relationship
\bm{P}(\bm{F})
, e.g. in terms of the deformation gradient
\bm{F}
, is required
[
72
, p.40]
.
In this proof-of-concept investigation, we consider a simple compressible hyperelastic neo-Hooke material law, which postulates a reversible behaviour derived from a stored energy function

W=\frac{\mu}{2}(I_{c}-3)+\frac{\kappa}{4}(J^{2}-1-2\ln{J})\,,

(2)

such that
\bm{P}=\dfrac{\partial W}{\partial\bm{F}}

[
71
, p.207]
. Here,
I_{c}
is the first invariant of the right Cauchy Green strain tensor,
J=\text{det}(\bm{F})
is the volumetric strain, and
\kappa
and
\mu
are the bulk and shear moduli
[
71
, p.247,254]
.

Note that solutions
\vec{\underline{u}}(\vec{X})
of Eq. (
1
) on
\Omega
are minimisers of a potential
\Pi

[
71
, p.157,206]
, where

\Pi=\int_{\Omega}W(\vec{X})dV-\int_{\Omega}\vec{b}(\vec{X})dV-\int_{\partial\Omega^{N}}\vec{t}(\vec{X})dA\,.

(3)

Taking the first variation of this potential, which must vanish at a minimum of
\Pi

[
71
, p.382]
, or multiplying Eq. (
1
) by a kinematically admissible test function and integrating over
\Omega
yields the weak form of the balance of linear momentum
[
72
, p.82]

\int_{\Omega}\bm{P}(\bm{X}):\delta\bm{F}(\bm{X})dV-\int_{\Omega}\vec{b}(\bm{X})\cdot\delta\vec{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})dV-\int_{\partial\Omega}\vec{t}(\bm{X})\cdot\delta\vec{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})dA=0\,.

(4)

Here,
\delta\vec{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})
and
\delta\bm{F}(\bm{X})
denote the kinematically admissible variations in the displacement and the deformation gradient, respectively
[
72
, p.82]
.

The discretisation of Eq. (
4
) via a standard Galerkin FE formulation which uses the same Ansatz functions for
\vec{\underline{u}}(\vec{X)}
and
\delta\bm{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})
yields

\delta\vec{u}^{T}\vec{g}(\vec{u};\vec{p})=0\,,

(5)

and, since Eq. (
5
) must vanish for any kinematically admissible
\delta\bm{u}
,

\vec{g}(\vec{u};\vec{p})=\vec{0}\,.

(6)

Here,
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denotes the nodal displacement vector via which the continuous displacement
\bm{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})
is discretised,
\delta\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
the discrete counterpart of the variation
\delta\bm{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})
,
\bm{g}(\vec{u};\vec{p})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
the nodal residua, and
\vec{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta}
the parameters which determine a solution
[
72
, p.125]
. The parameter vector
\vec{p}
might for example contain values necessary to specify boundary conditions. This is the case in the example RVE computation outlined in Section
7
, which is parameterised in terms of the macroscopic displacement gradient
\bm{\bar{H}}=\bm{\bar{F}}-\bm{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}
.

The
D
-dimensional vector
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
in Eq. (
6
) contains the primary unknowns of the system.
A Newton-Raphson scheme can be used as a solver for these unknown variables, in which case Eq. (
6
) is linearised around the current iterate
\bm{u_{\text{cur}}}
to yield an equation system for the displacement increment
\Delta\vec{u}

[
72
, p.148]

\bm{K}(\bm{u_{\text{cur}}};\vec{p})\Delta{\vec{u}}=-\vec{g}(\bm{u_{\text{cur}}};\vec{p})\,.

(7)

The tangent appearing in the above defines a classical finite element stiffness matrix
\bm{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}

[
73
]

\bm{K}(\bm{u_{\text{cur}}};\vec{p})=\frac{\partial\vec{g}(\bm{u_{\text{cur}}};\vec{p})}{\partial{\vec{u}}}\,,

(8)

which is also assembled from nodal values on the element level
[
72
, p.127]
.

Dirichlet, Neumann, or periodic boundary conditions (BCs) necessitate modifications to the linear equation system in Eq. (
7
)
[
73
]
. In the following, we will assume these changes to have been made, such that Eq. (
7
) defines a linear system of
D
equations with
D
unknown independent variables. Following the solution of this linear system, the previous value of the discretised displacement vector may be updated with the increment
\Delta\bm{u}
such that

\bm{u_{\text{cur}}}\leftarrow\bm{u_{\text{cur}}}+\Delta\bm{u}\,.

(9)

Nonlinear projection-based model order reduction for quasi-static solid-mechanical problems

The number of unknowns
D
of quasi-static solid-mechanical problems discretised via the FEM (e.g. in the case of RVE computations) is often significant: in Section
7
we tackle a simple example with
D=17,151
independent degrees of freedom; values of
D>1,000,000
are readily encountered in real-world problems (see e.g.
[
22
,
74
,
75
]
).
The computational cost incurred in computations like these can be considerable: in the best case, the assembly of the linear system in Eq. (
7
) scales with
\mathcal{O}(D)
and its solution with
\mathcal{O}(D^{2})

[
76
]
.
Applications like
multiscale modelling often necessitate thousands of solutions to such problems. The resulting computational cost precludes treatment using full FE simulations with currently available computational resources.

To achieve practicable overall simulation times in such a multi-query setting, the computational cost of individual simulations must be reduced. This can be realised if, rather than the costly original problem, a reduced problem with
d\ll D
unknown degrees of freedom is considered. As motivated in the introduction, parametric projection-based MOR offers an attractive framework for performing such a reduction. Typically, projection-based MOR schemes define a reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
populated by reduced vectors
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with the aim of searching for solutions within this reduced space
[
20
,
21
,
40
]
. A reconstruction map
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
is required to define reconstructed (approximate) solutions

\bm{\bar{u}}=R(\bm{y})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
in the original solution space. In solid-mechanical applications of projection-based MOR, the reconstruction mapping
R
is commonly chosen to be linear, i.e.
\bm{\bar{u}}=\bm{\psi}\bm{y}

[
20
,
21
,
40
]
or piecewise linear
[
37
,
36
,
59
,
3
,
4
]
. The columns of the matrices
\bm{\psi}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
defining such a linear map, which might e.g. be obtained via the POD
[
39
]
, can then be interpreted as modes for the solutions
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, while the reduced vectors
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
contain the corresponding mode activity coefficients.

Alternatively, MOR can be viewed from a manifold learning perspective.
In the Introduction, we noted that the set of all possible solutions to a parameterised quasi-static solid mechanical problem defines a solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}=\{\vec{u}\mid\exists\bm{p}:\bm{g}(\vec{u};\vec{p})=\vec{0}\}
with
\vec{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta}
, which is a connected set of solutions
\vec{u}(\vec{p})
that locally resembles a Euclidean space
[
37
,
36
]
. For a well-posed parameterised problem, the solution manifold assumes the dimensionality
\delta
of the parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{\delta}
, meaning that
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
is much lower-dimensional than the solution space
\mathbb{R}^{D}

[
36
]
. The visualisation in Fig.
1
illustrates a solution manifold with
\delta=1
in a solution space with
D=3
. An appealing approach to reducing the original problem size
D
then might be to approximate a superset of the solution manifold using an approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
. Here,
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
is a
d
-dimensional manifold in the
D
-dimensional solution space, where
\delta<d\ll D
. An approximation space is also visualised in Fig
1
with
d=2
. In the framework of projection-based model order reduction,
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
can be defined via the reconstruction map
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The
solutions
\bm{\bar{u}}
making up the approximation space can be defined as the images
\bm{\bar{u}}=R(\bm{y})
of vectors in the reduced space
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that the
\bm{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
parameterise
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
. Then, a ROM can seek for solutions in the approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
, which is much lower dimensional than the solution space.
In the ideal case in which
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
is indeed a superset of the solution manifold, i.e.
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}\supset\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
, all solutions in
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
are also in
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
. In this Section, we briefly sketch a framework for NLMOR along these lines. Note that, for the dynamic case, a formal framework for NLMOR using continuous Ansatz approximation spaces has been proposed in
[
69
]
.

Figure 1
:
Illustration of a
\delta=1
dimensional solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
(black line) in a
D=3
dimensional solution space, approximated via a
d=2
dimensional approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\bar{u}}}
(surface with blue-red colour gradient). Snapshots are shown as black dots. Note that here,
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{\bar{u}}}\supset\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}

Practically, the reduced space and a reconstruction map
R
can be constructed in a data-based manner.
To this end, snapshot solutions
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},i\in 1,..,s
to the full FEM problem in Eq. (
6
) for
s
parameter samples
\vec{p}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta}
might be used, where
\vec{g}(\vec{u}_{i};\vec{p}_{i})=\vec{0}

[
14
]
.
To construct the reduced space, a projection-based MOR scheme based on manifold learning techniques might then find an embedding
\bm{y}_{i}=M(\vec{u}_{i})
for each
\vec{u}_{i}
with an embedding map
M:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
, e.g. via a criterion that preserves essential structural features.
The reconstruction
\vec{\bar{u}}_{i}=R(\vec{y}_{i})=M^{-1}(\vec{y}_{i})
might also be found, e.g. by minimising the reconstruction error
\|\bm{\bar{u}}_{i}-\bm{u}_{i}\|_{2}
over all

. Note that this reconstruction error generally does not vanish, such that
\bm{\bar{u}}\neq\bm{u}
. Specific schemes for finding suitable embeddings
\vec{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
are discussed in Sections
4.1
,
4.2
,
5.2
, and
5.3
. Note, however, that many powerful dimensionality reduction techniques which might be used to this end only define the embedding mapping

and reconstruction mapping
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
implicitly. Instead, these techniques only provide an embedding
\vec{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponding to previously gathered solution data
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, since it is highly nontrivial to find explicit mappings between points in
d
and
D
-dimensional spaces
\vec{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
in a general manner

]
.

It will prove useful in the following to locally approximate
R
via a linearisation, i.e. an approximation of a map to the tangent space
\mathcal{T}_{\vec{u}}\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
to
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
. At a point with current reduced coordinates
\vec{y}_{\text{cur}}
and current displacement
\bm{u_{\text{cur}}}
, such a linearisation might be expressed as

\vec{\bar{u}}-\bm{u_{\text{cur}}}\approx\bm{\varphi}(\vec{y}-\vec{y}_{\text{cur}})\,,

(10)

where
\vec{\bar{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
denotes the reconstructed, discretised displacement corresponding to
\vec{{y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The tangent
\bm{\varphi}
, which will turn out to be very useful in the following, is defined as

\bm{\varphi}=\frac{\partial R(\vec{y})}{\partial\vec{y}}\Bigr|_{\vec{y}_{\text{cur}}}\,.

(11)

If
R
is only given implicitly,
\bm{\varphi}
can of course not be calculated directly and must be estimated. In the framework of a Galerkin scheme (see e.g.
[
78
]
), the columns of
\bm{\varphi}
can be interpreted as discrete local Ansatz functions for the increments
\vec{\bar{u}}-\bm{u_{\text{cur}}}
, while
\vec{y}-\vec{y}_{\text{cur}}
are the corresponding coefficients.
Note that the familiar POD
[
39
]
, which is outlined in Section
4.1
, uses a
d
-dimensional linear subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{D}
as an approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
, such that
M:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
are globally linear by construction.
Alternative methods for the construction of a linearisation as in Eq. (
10
) will be discussed in Sections
4.2
and
5.5
.

In the framework of a Galerkin scheme, a projection-based MOR method can then search for solutions in the approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
, i.e. search for solutions to

\delta\bm{\bar{u}}^{T}\vec{g}(\vec{\bar{u}};\vec{p})=0\,,

where
\vec{\bar{u}}\in\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
and
\delta\bm{\bar{u}}
denotes the corresponding variation, which lies in the tangent space to the manifold at
\vec{\bar{u}}
, i.e.
\delta\bm{\bar{u}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\vec{u}}\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
. With
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
parameterised via
\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
through the reconstruction
R
, we can search for solutions in the much lower-dimensional reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and approximate the variation as
\delta\bm{\bar{u}}=\bm{\varphi}\delta\bm{y}
to yield

\delta\bm{y}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}\vec{g}(R(\vec{y});\vec{p})=0\,.

This must, again, vanish for any admissible
\delta\bm{y}
, which implies that the reduced residual

\vec{g}_{r}(\vec{y};\vec{p})=\bm{\varphi}^{T}\vec{g}(R(\vec{y});\vec{p})=\vec{0}\,,

(12)

which is now also
d
- rather than
D
-dimensional, must vanish.

In analogy to Eq. (
7
), we can search for solutions to Eq. (
12
) via a Newton scheme

\bm{K}_{r}(\vec{y}_{\text{cur}};\vec{p})\Delta\bm{y}=-\vec{g}_{r}(\vec{y}_{\text{cur}};\vec{p})\,,

(13)

where
\bm{K}_{r}
defines a reduced stiffness matrix

\bm{K}_{r}(\vec{y};\vec{p})=\frac{\partial\vec{g}_{r}(\vec{y};\vec{p})}{\partial\vec{y}}=\frac{\partial(\bm{\varphi}^{T}\vec{g}(R(\vec{y});\vec{p}))}{\partial\vec{y}}=\bm{\varphi}^{T}\frac{\partial\vec{g}(\vec{\bar{u}};\vec{p})}{\partial\vec{\bar{u}}}\frac{\partial\vec{\bar{u}}}{\partial\vec{y}}\,,

i.e.

\bm{K}_{r}(\vec{y};\vec{p})=\bm{\varphi}^{T}\bm{K}(R(\vec{y});\vec{p})\bm{\varphi}\,.

(14)

As in Eq. (
9
), the reduced variables
\bm{y}
may be incremented following the solution of Eq. (
13
) via

\bm{y_{\text{cur}}}\leftarrow\vec{y_{\text{cur}}}+\Delta\bm{y}\,.

(15)

4.1
Proper Orthogonal Decomposition: robust linear MOR

The proper orthogonal decomposition (or POD; also known as principal components analysis, Karhunen-Loeve decomposition, or singular value decomposition) is a simple, yet robust linear projection-based MOR method
[
79
]
. It works on the principle of projecting the snapshot data

\mathbb{R}^{D}

\delta

\delta

The POD produces the mapping to and from the low-dimensional linear subspace of dimension

[

with the snapshot matrix
\bm{U}

yields the columns of the mapping matrix

4.2
Local bases: nonlinearity via locality

(a)

Clustered snapshot vectors on solution manifold in high-dimensional solution space
\vec{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
.

(b)

Piecewise linear approximation of solution manifold using the LPOD based on clustered snapshot vectors
\vec{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times s}
.

Figure 3
:
Illustration of dimensionality reduction using the local basis method. Cluster centroids shown as larger spheres. Note that low-dimensional visualisations of manifold learning techniques obscure some of the nuances of dimensionality reduction.

As discussed in Section
4.1
, the POD may require model sizes
d_{\text{POD}}
significantly in excess of the dimensionality
\delta
of the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
, which limits the achievable reductions in simulation time.
The local basis method (LPOD) attempts to circumvent this obstacle by localising the POD and using an approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
which consists of multiple locally linear approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{j}
, each of which is valid in a cluster or subdomain
j
in solution
\mathbb{R}^{D}
or parameter space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
An illustration of this approach can be found in Fig.
3
. During the online solution procedure, the most appropriate of these local approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
for cluster
c
is selected for reduction in each iteration.
Originally, the local basis method was proposed in the context of a Gauss-Newton scheme and a scheme from
[
43
]
was used for hyper-reduction
[
1
]
, but the application to a Newton-Raphson scheme is straightforward (see e.g.
[
3
]
).

As the POD, the LPOD constructs its reduced spaces from a snapshot matrix
\bm{U}
of solutions to a full FE problem.
In the offline phase, a
k
-means clustering step is performed on this data via Lloyd’s algorithm
[
1
]
.
k
cluster centroids
\vec{u}_{j}^{c},j=1,...,k
are initialised as randomly selected snapshots. In each iteration of the clustering scheme, the cluster association
\vec{c}^{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{s}
for each snapshot is updated. Each snapshot is associated with the cluster to the centroid of which it is closest, i.e.

c_{i}^{u}=\text{argmin}_{j}d(\bm{u}_{i},\vec{u}_{j}^{c})\,,

where
d(\bm{u}_{i},\vec{u}_{j}^{c})
is an appropriate metric. Here, a classical L2 norm is used for simplicity, i.e.
{d(\bm{u}_{i},\vec{u}_{j}^{c})=\|\bm{u}_{i}-\vec{u}_{j}^{c}\|_{2}}
.
The centroid is then updated as the algorithmic mean of the snapshots now belonging to that cluster, i.e.

\vec{u}_{j}^{c}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}_{j}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\vec{u}_{i}\,,

where
\mathcal{C}_{j}=\{i:c_{i}^{u}=j\}

is the set of snapshots in the cluster and
|\mathcal{C}_{j}|
its cardinality (the number of snapshots in the cluster). The procedure is repeated until no further change arises in the cluster association update. To avoid degenerate clusters, we only accept the output of Lloyd’s algorithm if each cluster’s size exceeds
|\mathcal{C}|_{\text{core,min}}
.
Note that alternative clustering schemes utilising a physics- or problem-based distance metric are also possible, and often advantageous
[
37
,
36
,
59
]
.

After convergence has been achieved with Lloyd’s algorithm, the next
\text{ceil}(r|\mathcal{C}_{j}|)
nearest snapshots
\vec{u}_{i}
to cluster centroid
\vec{u}_{j}^{c}
are furthermore added to each cluster
j
to introduce an overlap. Here,
r>0
defines the relative amount by which the cardinality of each cluster is to be increased, and
ceil
rounds to the nearest larger whole number. This improves the stability of the solution scheme in basis switches, as in the ideal case, transition regions between clusters are captured by all adjacent local POD bases
[
1
]
.

We introduce a slight modification of this clustering scheme for robustness: it seems helpful, especially in the low-data context, to introduce a minimum cluster size
|\mathcal{C}|_{\text{min}}
to which clusters must be and a maximum cluster size
|\mathcal{C}|_{\text{max}}
beyond which they may not be enlarged. In the cluster enlargement step, a cluster is then enlarged until its cardinality reaches
\max\big(|\mathcal{C}|_{\text{min}},\min(|\mathcal{C}_{j}|+\text{ceil}(r|\mathcal{C}_{j}|),|\mathcal{C}|_{\text{max}})\big)
.

Following the clustering step, linear local approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
can be constructed for the snapshots
\vec{u}_{i},i\in\mathcal{C}_{j}
in each of the
j=[1,...,k]
clusters
[
1
]
. Firstly, the snapshots within a cluster are centered; in
[
1
]
, this is done using the initial condition
\bm{u}_{0}

for the snapshots in all clusters. To obtain approximations of tangents
\mathcal{T}_{\vec{u}}\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
to the solution manifold, we instead centre using the cluster centroids

\vec{u}_{i}^{s}=\vec{u}_{i}-\vec{u}_{j}^{c}\,,\quad i\in\mathcal{C}_{j},j=[1,...,k]\,,

which ensures that the cluster centroids are contained in the local approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
associated with that cluster (see Fig.
3
). Finally, a snapshot POD can be performed for each of the cluster snapshots matrices
\bm{U}_{j}^{c}
, where

\bm{U}_{j}^{c}=[\bm{u}_{i}^{s}]_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{j}}\,,

which yields the local POD bases
\bm{\psi}_{j}^{c}
, as described in Section
4.1
.
See Algorithm
4
in
C
for pseudocode outlining the offline computations required for the LPOD.

In the iterative online solution procedure, the cluster
c
with the closest centroid to the current solution
\vec{\bar{u}_{\text{cur}}}
is selected, i.e.

c=\text{argmin}_{j}d(\bm{\bar{u}_{\text{cur}}},\vec{u}_{j}^{c})\,.

The POD basis
\bm{\psi}_{c}^{c}
associated with this cluster is then chosen to define the local approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
.
In
[
1
]
, the local bases and reduced increments saved at previous time/load steps are exploited such that the distance computation
d(\cdot,\cdot)
does not scale with the dimensionality of the original problem
D
. Since performance optimisation and runtime comparisons are not within our scope currently, this is not done here; the distance computation is performed in the solution space.
Additionally, a basis realignment scheme is outlined in
[
56
]
to account for the point of transition from one cluster
a
to the next
b
in the affine subspace spanned by the reference initial condition
\vec{u}_{0}
and the basis
\bm{\psi}_{b}^{c}
.
Note that the cluster enlargement which produces overlapping clusters already goes some way toward ensuring that possible points of transition between subspaces are contained in the linear approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
associated with the neighbouring clusters.
Finally, a simple projection could ensure that after a transition from cluster
a
to cluster
b
, the displacement
\vec{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}
is contained in the linear approximation space spanned by the local basis
\bm{\psi}_{b}^{c}
around the cluster centroid
\vec{u}_{b}^{c}

\vec{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}\leftarrow\bm{\psi}_{b}^{c}{\bm{\psi}_{b}^{c}}^{T}(\vec{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}-\vec{u}_{b}^{c})+\vec{u}_{b}^{c}\,.

Such a projection does not, however, seem to be required for the examples considered in the scope of this investigation.

Once an appropriate linear approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
defined by basis
\bm{\psi}_{c}^{c}
has been identified, incremental solutions can be sought in this subspace as in the case of the POD, i.e.

\bm{K}_{r}\Delta\vec{y}=-\vec{g}_{r}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{K}_{r}=\bm{\psi}_{c}^{cT}\bm{K}\bm{\psi}_{c}^{c}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\vec{g}_{r}=\bm{\psi}_{c}^{cT}\vec{g}\,,

whereupon the current iterate can be incremented

\Delta{\vec{\bar{u}}}=\bm{\psi}_{c}^{c}\Delta\vec{y},\quad{\vec{\bar{u}_{\text{cur}}}}\leftarrow{\vec{\bar{u}_{\text{cur}}}}+\Delta{\vec{\bar{u}}}\,.

As in the case of the POD, the linearity of the approximation spaces
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}^{c}
obtained via the LPOD means that a tangent is available by construction and hyper-reduction schemes can be applied without undue effort. The desired scaling behaviour independent from the problem size
D
may be achieved
[
1
,

]
.

By identifying locally rather than globally linear structures in solution space, the LPOD achieves a closer approximation of the solution manifold than the POD. In the trade-off between accuracy and computational cost in the online phase, this has enabled researchers to obtain overall performance improvements
[
37
,
36
,
59
,
3
,
4
]
. As outlined in the introduction, however, this generally successful approach is subject to limitations, especially in the data-poor context: locally linear approximations might not parameterise the solution manifold as closely as possible, properly handling cluster transitions is challenging
[
56
]
, cluster transitions may yield instabilities or inaccuracies in the solution scheme
[
61
]
, and the quality of the obtained ROM scales with the quality of the underlying clusters (the assurance of which is intricate).

5
Projection-based MOR by manifold learning: global nonlinearity

As motivated in the introduction, we instead pursue a manifold learning approach to nonlinear MOR: graph-based manifold learning methods
[
77
,
81
,
82
]
are used to exploit global, nonlinear trends in the solution manifold based on the snapshot data. This may yield a closer, smoother approximation
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
of the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
than established methods, especially in the data-poor context, meaning that a manifold learning-based NLMOR scheme might outperform local basis methods and their alternatives in accelerating RVE computations based on limited data. Note that manifold learning methods do not yield a tangent
\bm{\varphi}
(see Eq. (
11
)) by construction; a linearisation needs to be constructed a posteriori
[
77
]
.

5.1
Manifold learning for dimensionality reduction

As discussed in Section
3
, a sensible, generalisable approach to nonlinear projection-based MOR is to aim to parameterise a region in solution space, i.e. the approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
, with reduced variables
\vec{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
to approximate a superset of the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
. Here, the dimension of the approximation
d
exceeds the dimensionality of the solution manifold
\delta
but is far smaller than the dimension of the original solution space, i.e.
\text{dim}(\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}})=\delta<\text{dim}(\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}})=d\ll D
. As the problem parameterisation
\bm{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{\delta}
determines the dimensionality of the solution manifold,
\delta
is often known a priori. When it is not, for example when plastic behaviour introduces a load path dependency,
\delta
may be estimated from the snapshot data
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times s}
, as outlined in
B
.

The dimensionality reduction problem underlying such a projection-based MOR scheme could be formulated as follows:
use snapshot data
\bm{U}
to find a nonlinear embedding map
M
from the solution space to a reduced space, i.e.
M:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The embedded coordinates in the reduced space
\vec{y}_{i}=M(\vec{u}_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
should be constructed so as to retain as much as possible of the information contained within the snapshots
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, i.e. capture as much as possible of the structure of the solution manifold.

An approach to tackle this nonlinear dimensionality reduction problem are graph-based manifold learning techniques (see e.g.
[
77
]
), which are popular in image and speech processing. Some researchers have also successfully applied these methods to surrogate modelling in fluid dynamics and solid statics
[
83
,
13
,
5
]
as well as to projection-based MOR in fluid dynamics
[
78
,
84
,
85
,
68
]
.
In this proof-of-concept investigation, we consider the Laplacian Eigenmaps (LEM)
[
81
]
and the Locally Linear Embedding (LLE)
[
82
,
77
]
as examples of graph-based manifold learning methods.

Conceptually, these methods find an embedding
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
for snapshot data
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times s}
in a reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which conserves certain structural information (e.g., local distances in the case of LEM
[
81
]
or local linearity in the case of LLE
[
82
]
).
Firstly, a graph
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{B}^{s\times s}
(where
\mathbb{B}
indicates the Boolean domain
\mathbb{B}\in\{0,1\}
) is created for the snapshot solution data
\bm{U}
, e.g. via a mutual or symmetric
k
-nearest neighbour approach. Here,
G_{ij}=1
indicates that snapshots
\vec{u}_{i}
and
\vec{u}_{j}
are connected via an edge, while
G_{ij}=0
indicates unconnected snapshots. The graph thus contains information about local connectivity within the data and acts as a data-based model of the structure of the manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}

[
81
]
. Based on the structural data contained in the graph adjacency matrix and some additional, method-specific data, an embedding
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
in the reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is then found.
Figure
4

As motivated in Section
3
, a reconstruction mapping
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
from the reduced space to the solution space as well as its derivative
\bm{\varphi}=\frac{\partial R(\vec{y})}{\partial\vec{y}}
, or at least approximations thereof are required for the online phase of a projection-based Galerkin MOR scheme.
The embedding
M
, reconstruction
R
, and derivative
\nicefrac{{\partial R(\vec{y})}}{{\partial\bm{y}}}

[
81
]
. Doing so in a general fashion is nontrivial, as the implicitly defined embedding mapping

M:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
is highly nonlinear in general, and must be so in order for the approximation space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
to closely approximate the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
.

\vec{u}_{j}
,
or

;

in the mutual

-nearest neighbour approach, an edge in the graph

is introduced whenever sample
\vec{u}_{i}
is among the

nearest neighbours of sample
\vec{u}_{j}

and
vice versa

As suggested in

Weights
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
are then assigned to each graph edge. This can for example be done via a Gaussian kernel
W_{ij}=E_{ij}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{t}\|\vec{u}_{i}-\vec{u}_{j}\|^{2}\right)

[

It can also be advantageous to weight the distance function with some variability metric of the underlying problem
[

. A Gaussian kernel eventually yields an embedding
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
which optimally preserves local distance information since minimising distance changes in the embedding
M:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponds to a search for Eigenfunctions of the manifold Laplace-Beltrami operator, the Green’s function of which is the Gaussian
[
81
]
.

Following
[
81
]
, a solution is found to the generalised Eigenvalue problem in terms of the graph Laplacian
\bm{L}

\bm{L}\vec{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\bm{D}\vec{v}_{i}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{L}=\bm{D}-\bm{W}\,,

with weight matrix
\bm{W}
and diagonal weight matrix
\bm{D}

D_{(ii)}=\sum_{j}W_{ij}=\sum_{i}W_{ij}\,.

D_{(ii)}
measures the sum total of the weights of the edges of the graph that are adjacent to sample
\vec{u}_{i}
, and can be seen as an importance measure of sample
\vec{u}_{i}

[
81
]

\bm{D}^{-1}\bm{L}\vec{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\vec{v}_{i}

may be solved instead
[
86
]

[

]

The lowest Eigenvalue vanishes (i.e.,
\lambda_{1}=0
), and a

) of the samples
\vec{u}_{i},i\in[1,..,s]
is defined by the next lowest Eigenvectors
\vec{v}_{i},i\in[2,..,d+1]

[
81

[
81
]
. The new coordinates
\vec{y}_{i}
in the reduced space corresponding to
\vec{u}_{i}
are given by row
i
of the matrix
[
86
]

\bm{Y}^{T}=[\vec{v}_{2},..,\vec{v}_{d+1}]\,,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\vec{y}_{i}=[v_{2,i},..,v_{d+1,i}]^{T}\,.

As noted in Section

, LEM does not, by construction, yield the reconstruction

In

[

.
Finally, Kernel regression is suggested in

Note furthermore that, as visualised in the examples in
[

This occurs because the LEM only considers the distances between data points which are connected by an edge of the underlying graph, i.e., points which are close, explicitly. Furthermore, only local distance information – rather than, for example, information on local angles and topology – are used.

5.3
Locally Linear Embedding

The Locally Linear Embedding (LLE) by Roweis et al.
[
82
]
is a manifold learning method which preserves local topology. The summary in this Section is based mainly on the description in
[
77
]
.

Firstly, LLE constructs a graph
\bm{G}\in\mathbb{B}^{s\times s}
via a
k
-nearest neighbour approach, as already outlined in Section
5.2
: an edge in the graph
G_{ij}=1
is introduced, whenever sample
\vec{u}_{i}
is among the
k
nearest neighbours of sample
\vec{u}_{j}
, either
and
vice versa (mutual
k
-nearest neighbours) or
or
vice versa (symmetric
k
-nearest neighbours), else
G_{ij}=0

[
77
]
.

Then, a weight matrix
\bm{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
for the graph edges is constructed based on the principle of locally linear reconstruction.
All weights
W_{ij}
for nodes
i
and
j
which are not connected are set to
0
.
The weights
W_{ij}
for nodes
i
and
j
which are connected by an edge in Graph
\bm{G}
are constructed on the principle that the displacement vector corresponding to node
i
in solution space
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
is optimally reconstructed via a linear combination of the displacement vectors
\vec{u}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},j\in N_{i}
of its neighbours
N_{i}=\{j\mid G_{ij}=1\}
with (reconstruction) weights
\bm{W}
, i.e. the minimisation problem

\min_{\bm{W}}\sum_{i}\|\vec{u}_{i}-\sum_{j\in N_{i}}W_{ij}\vec{u}_{j}\|^{2}

is solved
[
77
]
.

Practically, this can be implemented as follows
[
77
]
:
iterate through all nodes
i
and assemble the local covariance matrix of the distances between the point
\vec{u}_{i}
and its neighbours
\vec{u}_{j},j\in N_{i}
. The centered distances from
\bm{u}_{i}
to each of its neighbours
\vec{u}_{j}
are collected into a matrix
\Delta\bm{U}^{N_{i}}

and the local covariance matrix is computed as

\bm{G}_{i}=\Delta\bm{U}^{N_{i}T}\Delta\bm{U}^{N_{i}}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\Delta\bm{U}^{N_{i}}=[\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}]_{j\in N_{i}}\,.

Note that in the above, the indices
i
and
j
denote snapshot vector
i
and its neighbours
j\in N_{i}
. The entries in the weight matrix
\bm{W}
corresponding to the columns
j\in N_{i}
in row
i
can then be computed via the normalised solution of the (local) linear equation system

\bm{G}_{i}\vec{w}_{i}=\bm{\bar{1}}\,,

where
\bm{\bar{1}}
denotes a vector of ones and

W_{i,j\in N_{i}}=\vec{w}_{i}\,.

When
\bm{G}_{i}
is badly conditioned, the solution can be determined by modifying the diagonal of
\bm{G}_{i}
with a small factor
\Delta

\bm{G}_{i}=\bm{G}_{i}+\frac{\Delta^{2}}{N_{i}}\text{tr}(\bm{G}_{i})\bm{I}\,.

Finally, the embedding
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
is constructed such that the locally linear interpolation can be performed with minimum error using the same weight matrix
\bm{W}
in the reduced space
[
77
]
. In other words, the reduced vectors
\vec{y}_{i}
are determined as solutions to the minimisation problem

\min_{\bm{Y}}\sum_{i}\|\vec{y}_{i}-\sum_{j\in N_{i}}W_{ij}\vec{y}_{j}\|^{2}\,.

As in the case of the Laplacian Eigenmaps, the
d
entries of the
s
reduced vectors
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
can be computed as the
d
Eigenvectors corresponding to the second to
d+1
-th lowest Eigenvalues of a matrix
\bm{M\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}}
, in this case

\bm{M}=(\bm{I}-\bm{W})^{T}(\bm{I}-\bm{W})\,,

i.e.

\bm{M}\vec{v}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\vec{v}_{i}\,.

The new coordinates
\vec{y}_{i}
in the reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponding to
\vec{u}_{i}
are again given by row
i
of the matrix
[
86
]

\bm{Y}^{T}=[\vec{v}_{2},..,\vec{v}_{d+1}]\,,\quad\text{i.e.}\quad\vec{y}_{i}=[v_{2,i},..,v_{d+1,i}]^{T}\,.

Pseudocode for the LLE algorithm can be found in Algorithm
7
in
C
.

As outlined in Section
5.2
for the case of the LEM, LLE also provides no reconstruction
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
or tangent
\bm{\varphi}=\frac{\partial R(\vec{y})}{\partial\vec{y}}
by construction. An approximation of the reconstruction operation
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
can be defined intuitively by locally linear interpolation with reconstruction weights
[
89

In this work, we instead propose a nonlinear projection-based MOR scheme which directly utilises the embedding
\vec{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
obtained via manifold learning techniques to parameterise the approximation
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
of the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}

\mathbb{R}^{d}
, as the solution manifold can be approximated more closely, meaning that higher levels of accuracy can be reached with a given model size

Instead of linearising globally, we compute a locally linear approximation
\Delta\vec{\bar{u}}=\bm{\varphi}\Delta\vec{y}
(cf. Eq (
19
)) of the reconstruction mapping
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}

around a point
\vec{y}_{\text{cur}}
in the reduced space. The mapping matrix
\bm{\varphi}
approximates the tangent to the reconstruction
R:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
according to Eq. (
11

This is tantamount to mapping from the reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to an approximation of the tangent to the approximation space
\mathcal{T}_{\vec{u}}\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
at the corresponding point in the solution space
\vec{u}_{\text{cur}}
.

The linearisation around
{\vec{y}}_{\text{cur}}
is performed in a data-based manner in the online phase, based on the reduced
\vec{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\in N_{y}
and the original coordinates
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},i\in N_{y}
of the
N
snapshots
N_{y}
nearest to
{\vec{y}}_{\text{cur}}
. The search for the set of nearest neighbours
N_{y}
is performed in the reduced space; pseudocode can be found in Algorithm
9
in
C
. The linearisation scheme is visualised schematically in Fig.
6

We are thus looking for the optimal linear mapping

\Delta\vec{\bar{u}}=\bm{\varphi}\Delta\vec{y}

(19)

around
\bm{y}_{\text{cur}}
. Note that this linear mapping is defined with respect to the current value of the original and reduced degrees of freedom
\bm{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}
and
\bm{y}_{\text{cur}}
resulting from the previous Newton iteration, i.e.

\vec{\bar{u}}-\bm{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}=\bm{\varphi}(\vec{y}-\bm{y}_{\text{cur}})\,.

As the FEM problem is to be solved in the reduced space,
\bm{y}_{\text{cur}}
is generally known while
\bm{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}
is not.
Rearranging for
\vec{\bar{u}}
yields

\vec{\bar{u}}=\bm{\varphi}\vec{y}-\bm{\varphi}\bm{y}_{\text{cur}}+\bm{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}=\bm{\varphi}\vec{y}+\vec{u}^{0}

(20)

with (unknown) offset
\vec{u}^{0}=\bm{\bar{u}}_{\text{cur}}-\bm{\varphi}{\vec{y}}_{\text{cur}}
.
We now seek the
\bm{\varphi}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
and
\vec{u}^{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
with which Eq. (
20
) optimally maps between the reduced
\vec{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i\in N_{y}
and original
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},i\in N_{y}
coordinates of the
N
nearest neighbours
N_{y}
of
\bm{y}_{\text{cur}}
, in a least squares sense.

For convenience, we introduce local snapshot matrices
\bm{U}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times N}
and
\bm{Y}_{N}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
featuring the original and reduced coordinates of the identified neighbours
N_{y}

\bm{U}_{N}=[\bm{u}_{n}]_{n\in N_{y}},\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{Y}_{N}=[\bm{y}_{n}]_{n\in N_{y}}\,,

with which the least squares problem may be written as

\min_{\bm{\varphi}}f(\bm{\varphi},\vec{u}^{0})=\min_{\bm{\varphi},\vec{u}^{0}}\sum_{n}\sum_{i}(\sum_{j}\varphi_{ij}Y_{Njn}+u_{i}^{0}-U_{Nin})^{2}\,.

The necessary conditions are given by

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\varphi_{kl}}=0\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{k}^{0}}=0\,.

Differentiation yields

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\varphi_{kl}}=\sum_{n}2Y_{Nln}(\sum_{j}\varphi_{kj}Y_{Njn}+u_{k}^{0}-U_{Nkn})=0

and

\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{k}^{0}}=\sum_{n}2(\sum_{j}\varphi_{kj}Y_{Njn}+u_{k}^{0}-U_{Nkn})=0\,.

The necessary conditions thus define a linear equation system with
D\times d+D
equations and unknowns

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sum_{j}Y_{Nln}\varphi_{kj}Y_{Njn}+\sum_{n}Y_{Nln}u_{k}^{0}-\sum_{n}Y_{Nln}U_{Nkn}

\displaystyle=0\,,

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sum_{j}\varphi_{kj}Y_{Njn}+\sum_{n}u_{k}^{0}-\sum_{n}U_{Nkn}

\displaystyle=0\,,

or, in matrix notation

\displaystyle\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}+\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}\vec{u}^{0T}-\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}

\displaystyle=\bm{0}\,.

(21)

\displaystyle\bm{\varphi}\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}+N\vec{u}^{0}-\bm{U}_{N}\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}

\displaystyle=\vec{0}\,,

(22)

where
\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}
denotes a vector of ones with
N
entries.
Elimination of
\vec{u}^{0}
via Eq. (
22
)

\vec{u}^{0}=\frac{1}{N}(\bm{U}_{N}-\bm{\varphi}\bm{Y}_{N})\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}\,,

and substitution into Eq. (
21

\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}+\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}\frac{1}{N}\bm{\bar{1}}_{N}^{T}(\bm{U}_{N}^{T}-\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T})-\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}=\bm{0}\,,

i.e.

\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}+\bm{Y}_{N}\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}-\bm{Y}_{N}\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}-\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}=\bm{0}\,,

where
\bm{1}_{N}
is an
N\times N
matrix of ones.
By collecting terms, we obtain

(\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}-\bm{Y}_{N}\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T})\bm{\varphi}^{T}=\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}-\bm{Y}_{N}\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}

and

\bm{Y}_{N}(\bm{I}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N})\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}=\bm{Y}_{N}(\bm{I}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N})\bm{U}_{N}^{T}\,,

with the
N\times N
identity matrix
\bm{I}_{N}
.
With the symmetric
N\times N
weight matrix
\bm{W}_{N}

\bm{W}_{N}=\bm{I}_{N}-\frac{1}{N}\bm{1}_{N}\,,

this becomes

\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\bm{\varphi}^{T}=\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{U}_{N}^{T}\,.

We can now finally solve for
\bm{\varphi}

\bm{\varphi}\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}=\bm{U}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}\,,

to yield

\bm{\varphi}=\bm{U}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}(\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T})^{-1}\,.

(23)

Note that
\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}
must be full-rank and invertible. To ensure that this condition is met, we must simply ensure that the number of (non-degenerate) neighbouring points exceeds the dimensionality of the reduced space, i.e.

of the

With the local tangent matrix
\bm{\varphi}
, incremental solutions can be sought in the approximated tangent space of the approximation space

as outlined in Eqs. (

, the LPOD
[

is of rank

\Delta\vec{y}=\bm{R}_{\perp}^{-1}\Delta\vec{y}_{\perp}\,,

(28)

and the reduced and original solutions incremented via Eq. (

Two-stage projection: achieving problem size independence

The nonlinear projection-based MOR scheme proposed in Section
5.5
satisfies many of the requirements set forth in the introduction:
it reduces the search space in which solutions are sought to a relatively tight approximation
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{u}}}
of the solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
, i.e.
d\not\gg\delta
; as the manifold learning methods outlined in Sections
5.2
and
5.3
are not data-hungry, it may function with relatively few snapshots
s
; the scheme is sufficiently general to apply to a wide range of quasi-static parametric problems.
For a given desired level of accuracy, the model size
d_{\text{ManL}}
required by a manifold learning-based NLMOR scheme, meanwhile, may be considerably smaller than that required by the POD, i.e.
d_{\text{POD}}>d_{\text{ManL}}
. The solution of the linear system of equations in Eq. (
27
), which scales with
\mathcal{O}(d^{3})
in the worst case, may therefore be much accelerated.

However, some components of the algorithm are still tied to the dimension of the original problem
D
, such as the local linearisation and the QR decomposition, as well as the assembly, each of which scale with
\mathcal{O}(D)
.
In this Section, we propose a two-stage MOR scheme with which independence from the problem size
D
can be achieved. The approach is motivated by
[
77
, p.231]
: data on a nonlinear
\delta
-manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
in a
D
-dimensional space can often be linearly compressed nearly losslessly into a
\bar{d}
-dimensional intermediate space
\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
, from which a nonlinear embedding can be performed to a reduced
d
-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, where
D\ll\bar{d}<d
. Thus, we use the POD to perform a linear reduction step
\bar{M}:\mathbb{R}^{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
from the
D
-dimensional solution space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
to a
\bar{d}
-dimensional intermediate space
\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
and a nonlinear reduction
M:\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
(e.g., via the LEM) from the
\bar{d}
-dimensional intermediate space to the
d
-dimensional reduced space
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. This scheme is visualised schematically in Fig.
7
. The local linearisation and QR decomposition must then no longer be performed with respect to the original solution space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
.
Beyond the improved scaling behaviour, the graph building, manifold learning, and local linearisation steps may also be more robust if performed with respect to the lower-dimensional intermediate space. Of course, a hyper-reduction scheme is required to achieve the desired scaling behaviour for the assembly operation, but this is beyond the scope of this work.

Figure 7
:
Schematic of two-stage reduction process using POD and manifold learning technique such as Laplacian Eigenmaps.

The application of such a two-stage MOR scheme is straightforward in principle: in the offline phase, a linear reduction is performed on the snapshot data
\bm{U}
to the intermediate space
\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
via the POD, yielding a projection matrix
\bm{\psi}
(see Section
4.1
).

\bm{\psi}
defines a mapping between the solution and intermediate spaces as well as a new snapshot matrix
\bm{\bar{Y}}
in the intermediate space

\bm{\bar{y}}=\bm{\psi}^{T}\vec{u}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\bar{Y}}=\bm{\psi}^{T}\bm{U}\,.

Then, the linearly reduced solution manifold in the intermediate space
\mathcal{M}_{\bar{\bm{y}}}=\{\vec{\bar{y}}\mid\vec{\bar{y}}=\bm{\psi}^{T}\bm{u}\,,\bm{g}(\vec{u};\vec{p})=\vec{0},\}
is approximated with a graph; a graph adjacency matrix (and possibly weights) are computed as discussed in Section
5.2
, using the snapshot coordinates in the intermediate space
\bm{\bar{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times s}
as nodes.
A graph-based nonlinear MOR technique is used to obtain an array of
s
reduced
d
-dimensional snapshot vectors
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
for the snapshot coordinates in the intermediate space
\bm{\bar{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}\times s}
.

In each iteration in the online phase of such a two-stage nonlinear MOR scheme, a local linearisation can be performed as discussed in Section
5.5
, but with respect to the intermediate space
\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
. The local snapshot matrices are now defined as

\bm{\bar{Y}}=[\bm{\bar{y}}_{i}]_{i\in N_{y}}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{Y}_{N}=[\vec{y}_{i}]_{i\in N_{y}}\,,

and the local tangent
\bm{\varphi}
from Eq. (
11
) becomes

\bm{\varphi}=\bm{\bar{Y}}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T}(\bm{Y}_{N}\bm{W}_{N}\bm{Y}_{N}^{T})^{-1}\,.

As above, the mapping matrix can be orthonormalised via a reduced QR decomposition

\bm{\varphi}_{\perp},\bm{R}_{\perp}\leftarrow\text{QR}(\bm{\varphi})\,.

Incremental solutions can now be sought in the approximated tangent space to the solution manifold in the intermediate space
\mathcal{T}_{\bm{\bar{y}}}\mathcal{M}_{\bar{y}}
via a reduced Newton scheme. The approach is analogous to that articulated in Section
5.5
, but with respect to the intermediate space
\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}
, i.e. the nonlinear reduction is performed on the reduced equations obtained via the POD as outlined in Section
4.1
. This yields a doubly reduced Newton scheme

\bm{K}_{r}\Delta\vec{y}=-\vec{g}_{r}\,,\quad\text{where}\quad\bm{K}_{r}=\bm{\varphi}_{\perp}^{T}\bm{\psi}^{T}\bm{K}\bm{\psi}\bm{\varphi}_{\perp}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\vec{g}_{r}=\bm{\varphi}_{\perp}^{T}\bm{\psi}^{T}\vec{g}\,.

The increment in reduced space can be obtained as in Eq. (
28
), i.e.
\Delta\vec{y}=\bm{R}_{\perp}^{-1}\Delta\vec{y}_{\perp}
, and the linearised reconstruction of the increment in solution space
\mathbb{R}^{D}
becomes

\Delta{\vec{\bar{u}}}=\bm{\psi}\bm{\varphi}\Delta\vec{y}\,.

Note that the local linearisation and the QR decomposition now scale with
\mathcal{O}(\bar{d})
, while the solution of the linear system scales with
\mathcal{O}(d^{2})
.
Hyper-reduction is not the focus of this proof-of concept investigation, and was not implemented for the examples considered in Section
7
. See
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
for details about potential hyper-reduction schemes.

7
Numerical Example

7.1
RVE computations for the FE
2
method

In some applications, such as composite materials design
[
93
]
and biomechanical research
[
94
]
, it is desirable to model the mechanical behaviour of a macroscopic component as well as its microstructure.
Classical computational multiscale methods exploit a scale separation assumption to resolve macroscopic and microscopic behaviour separately; a microscale RVE is constructed to approximate the microstructure.

In the case of the FE
2
method
[
19
]
, the evaluation of a constitutive law on the macroscale is replaced by a volume averaging over the solution to the RVE problem. Other computational homogenisation schemes, such as the data-based methods proposed in
[
95
,
13
,
96
]
, first build a considerable database of RVE solution data and use this data to train surrogate models for the constitutive behaviour
\bm{\bar{P}}(\bm{\bar{H}})
exhibited by the RVE. In either case, a considerable number of computations on an RVE are required, which is why projection-based MOR methods such as those outlined in Sections
4.1
,
4.2
, and
5
are particularly attractive in this field.

This microscale RVE can be subjected to macroscale strain information using the macroscopic deformation gradient
\bm{\bar{F}}
or displacement gradient
\bm{\bar{H}}
via periodic boundary conditions: the displacement on the scale of the microstructure
\bm{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})
is assumed to consist of a linear contribution due to the prescribed displacement gradient
\bm{\bar{H}}
and a fluctuation contribution
\tilde{\bm{\underline{u}}}(\bm{X})
which is periodic on the unit cell, i.e.
[
19
]

\bm{\underline{u}}(\bm{X})=\bm{\bar{H}}\bm{X}+\tilde{\bm{\underline{u}}}(\bm{X})\,.

The boundary
\partial\Omega
of the RVE can be decomposed into two parts which are coupled due to periodicity. For the implementation, it is convenient to define an independent part consisting of points
\bm{X}_{-}\in\partial\Omega_{-}
and an associated dependent part consisting of points
\bm{X}_{-}\in\partial\Omega_{-}
on this boundary, where the behaviour of the dependent part is formulated in terms of the behaviour of the independent part as
[
19
]

\tilde{\bm{\underline{u}}}(\bm{X}_{+})=\tilde{\bm{\underline{u}}}(\bm{X}_{-})\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{t}(\bm{X}_{+})=-\bm{t}(\bm{X}_{-})\,.

Note that periodic boundary conditions satisfy Hill’s micro-macro heterogeneity condition
[
97
]
.

With this formulation, the nodal degrees of freedom discretising
\tilde{\bm{\underline{u}}}(\bm{X})
become the primary unknowns to the RVE problem to be solved for using the FEM and a Newton-Raphson scheme as in Eq. (
7
).
The homogenised stress and stiffness response of the RVE to the load can then be used to obtain information about the effective constitutive behaviour of the inhomogeneous material at the considered point on the macroscale, as characterised by the first Piola-Kirchhoff stress
\bm{\bar{P}}
and nominal constitutive modulus
\bm{\bar{\mathbb{A}}}

[
73
]
.

Performance optimisations are not an emphasis of this proof-of-concept investigation; we did not consider, for example, hyper-reduction techniques to accelerate the costly assembly of stiffness matrices and residua. Such measures are necessary for real-time online performance in projection-based MOR methods, but are beyond the scope of this investigation. Nevertheless, all methods were implemented in the same FE framework and the algorithms relevant for the online solution procedure differ only in the methodology used to obtain the projection matrices required for reducing the linear system of equations resulting from a Newton-Raphson scheme. Thus, runtime variations are mostly due to the complexity of the procedure used to obtain these projection matrices, as well as the number of iterations required to achieve convergence

1

. While the absolute runtime values are therefore not particularly meaningful, preliminary observations about relative performance trends may be drawn.

In Fig.
21
, the mean and maximal relative modeling error obtained for all model sizes
d
are laid out over the runtime required for simulations along all load paths to converge. The marker size in each case is chosen to be proportional to
d
. While the LEM and LLE behave as expected, with smaller models yielding lower runtimes, the variation in runtime exhibited by the LPOD does not simply scale with the model size, as discussed in more detail below. The POD yields lower runtimes for smaller models for
d=30
,
d=20

d=15
, but sees an uptick at
d=12
. This is because of the increased number of iterations required for the reduced Newton scheme to converge in many load steps, which may be due to the small, linear model not sufficently capturing the solution manifold.

The locally linearised, orthonormalised LLE Pareto-dominates the other methods in the trade-off between mean relative modeling error and runtime, and also appears the best choice overall in terms of the maximal relative modeling error obtainable in a range of runtimes. The LEM, though it obtains similarly low values in the modeling errors, requires considerably more time to do so – which is almost exclusively due to the higher number of iterations required for convergence. This might be a consequence of the reduced degree to which the LEM with
t\rightarrow\infty
retains global structural information in its embedding (see Fig.
14(a)
) which leads the Newton scheme to follow less straightforward a path along the approximation of the solution manifold.

Meanwhile, the LPOD is subject to a considerable variation in runtime which does not scale simply with changing model dimension. This appears to be due to the behaviour of the method around cluster transition zones: when a solution lies close to the transition zone between two or more local ROMs with this method, the search for solutions may proceed alternatingly in the reduced spaces defined by the one, and then the other ROM. Even when no modeling errors are created in the cluster transition itself, and even if repeated transitions do not require projections from one reduced space to the other, constraining the search for solutions alternatingly to one of two potentially quite different subspaces may lead to an undesirable zig-zagging behaviour. Perhaps additional modifications to the cluster transition methodology beyond these suggested in
[
56
]
could improve the performance of the LPOD in the context of quasi-static solid-mechanical problems to be solved with a Newton-Raphson scheme. For example, employing a basis enrichment procedure when an appropriate zig-zagging criterion is met may allay some of these issues.

Overall, the results of this exploratory timing investigation are encouraging. In terms of the level of accuracy achievable in a given amount of time, the LPOD yields an improvement over the POD, and the locally linearised LLE yields a further improvement yet. The LEM is less competitive on account of the number of iteration required for convergence in the Newton scheme.

9
Summary and Conclusions

In this proof-of-concept investigation, we propose a manifold learning approach to nonlinear projection-based MOR for parameterised quasi-static problems, which addresses some of the shortcomings of the POD and LPOD.
The POD may require significant model dimensionalities
d
to successfully capture a nonlinear solution manifold resulting from a quasi-static solid-mechanical problem. The LPOD from
[
1
]
has been generally successful in addressing this deficiency by making use of local, rather than global, POD bases. However, this approach comes with some drawbacks, especially in the data-poor case: cluster-wise linear approximations do not parameterise the solution manifold as closely as possible, cluster transitions are precarious and may yield instabilities and inefficiencies in the solution scheme, and the quality of the obtained ROM scales with the quality of the underlying clusters.
In contrast, we apply manifold learning techniques to obtain a continuous and nonlinear, rather than discontinuous and locally linear, approximation space. A locally linear mapping between the reduced and original solution spaces is computed in the online phase.

After the manifold learning approach is motivated in the Introduction, Section
2
covers some essentials of FE modelling for quasi-static solid-mechanical problems.
Section
3
then outlines our conceptual approach to nonlinear projection-based MOR. Section
4
recalls the POD and the LPOD, and Section
5
introduces our alternative approach. Two manifold learning methods are covered in Subsections
5.1
,
5.2
, and
5.3
, and the linearisation scheme in Subsection
5.5
. Section
6
finally lays out a two-stage MOR scheme with which a scaling behaviour independent from the original problem size
D
can be achieved. Numerical experiments on a simple artificial RVE are detailed in Section
7
, and Section
8
features an ad-hoc parameter study which characterises the qualitative influence of algorithmic parameters.

In the examples explored in Sections
7
and
8
, the locally linearised LEM and LLE yield more of a performance boost over the POD than the LPOD, when considering the level of accuracy achievable with a given model size. Conversely, the LEM and LLE achieve a set modeling error level with smaller model sizes, meaning that smaller and potentially more efficient models can be used to attain a target level of accuracy. The two-stage MOR scheme, in which the POD is used for a nearly lossless compression before the manifold leaning step, encouragingly yields almost identical results. This indicates that, in the investigated example, the local linearisation as well as the QR decomposition can be performed with respect to the lower-dimensional intermediate space rather the solution space, yielding an improved scaling behaviour with little penalty in terms of accuracy. Performance trends appear stable for independent random realisations of the training and testing load paths, a different amount of training data, a different underlying FE model fidelity, and a range of reasonable model parameters. While the investigations summarised in Section
7
were conducted with parameters motivated by simpler preliminary investigations, the parameter study outlined in Section
8
indicates that slight performance improvements can readily be realised via further parameter tuning. Via a judiciously chosen Gauss weight or the selection of a mutual
k
-nearest neighbour graph building algorithm, for example, the mean modeling error level achieved by the locally linearised LEM with
d=15
can be reduced by approximately
10\%
. At the same time, the performance of the locally linearised manifold learning algorithms does not vary too strongly when model parameters are varied within reasonable bounds, suggesting that the methods are sufficiently robust in the face of parameter changes.

No in-depth timing experiments were conducted, as performance optimisation and hyper-reduction were not an objective of this proof-of-concept investigation. However, preliminary runtime results suggest that the locally linearised, orthonormalised LLE is able to produce results with a given level of accuracy in a noticeably shorter time than the POD and the LPOD, or to achieve a higher accuracy in a given budget of computational time; i.e. the locally linearised LLE Pareto-dominates these existing methods on the considered example. Both the LPOD and the locally linearised LEM suffer from slow convergence in some instances. In the case of the LPOD, we noted that this is likely due to alternating iterations within distinct subspaces in cluster transition regions. In the case of the LEM, we hypothesised that this is due to the reduced degree to which the LEM retains global structural information, causing the Newton scheme to follow less straightforward a path along the approximation of the solution manifold.

Overall, the results of this proof-of-concept investigation are encouraging: the locally linearised LLE (as well as the LEM, providing convergence issues can be addressed) appears to be able to outperform the state-of-the-art LPOD in reducing a quasi-static RVE problem with a nonlinear solution manifold that proves more challenging for the POD.

10
Outlook

Given the encouraging results of this proof-of-concept investigation, further research on nonlinear projection based MOR via graph-based manifold learning techniques in quasi-static solid mechanics appears warranted.
Firstly, the implementation of a hyper-reduction scheme such as one of those proposed in
[
41
,
42
,
43
,
44
,
45
]
is necessary to achieve competitive online performance.

Secondly, the resulting nonlinear MOR framework ought to be applied to a range of problems, beyond the simple artificial RVE computations investigated here as proof-of-concept verification. Performance comparisons to alternative approaches such as the LPOD, autoencoder applications, and Ansatz manifold parameterisations for nonlinear projection-based MOR, but also alternative MOR techniques such as surrogate models are particularly intriguing in this context.
This would serve to highlight for which classes of problems projection-based MOR schemes based on manifold learning techniques can be applied profitably. It would also help diagnose open issues and identify suitable directions for further development.
The handling of spatial localisation phenomena such as those resulting from plasticity or damage in solids might be one such research direction.
In parallel, further developments of the LPOD, e.g. considering adaptivity and additional cluster transition safeguards, might improve its performance on quasi-static problems in solid mechanics.

If the further development outlined above proves successful, nonlinear projection-based MOR methods using manifold learning techniques may carve out a niche in applications to parametric problems where little data is available to train a ROM and little human-time available to craft a specialised model, but comparatively high levels of accuracy are required.
Such applications maybe found in fields ranging from multiscale modelling over inverse problems to optimisation.
Beyond use as a standalone MOR scheme, nonlinear projection-based MOR methods could be applied to manufacture additional training data for data-hungry surrogate models which excel in terms of online computation time, but may feature prohibitively expensive offline phases.

Appendix A
Derivation of global linearisation

A linear projection matrix
\bm{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
is sought to optimally map between the original snapshot matrix
\bm{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times s}
and the corresponding reduced coordinates
\bm{Y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times s}
in the least-squares sense. A rigorous derivation can be found in
[
85
]
; here, we provide a simple sketch using linear algebra.

The objective for the least-squares problem can be written in index notation as

\min_{\bm{\phi}}f(\bm{\phi})=\min_{\bm{\phi}}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}(U_{ij}-\sum_{k}\phi_{ik}Y_{kj})^{2}\,.

The necessary conditions for an optimum are given by

\frac{\partial f}{\partial\phi_{ab}}=0\,.

Differentiation yields

-\sum_{j}2Y_{bj}(U_{aj}-\sum_{k}\phi_{ak}Y_{kj})=0

and

\sum_{j}\sum_{k}Y_{bj}Y_{kj}\phi_{ak}=\sum_{j}Y_{bj}U_{aj}\,.

In matrix notation, this is equivalent to

\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{T}\bm{\phi}^{T}=\bm{Y}\bm{U}^{T}\,.

such that
\bm{\phi}
is given by

\bm{\phi}^{T}=(\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{T})^{-1}\bm{Y}\bm{U}^{T}\,,\quad\text{and}\quad\bm{\phi}=\bm{U}\bm{Y}^{T}(\bm{Y}\bm{Y}^{T})^{-1}\,.

Appendix B
Correlation dimension

The dimensionality
\delta
of a solution manifold
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
is not always known a priori. Luckily, it is possible to estimate
\delta
from the snapshot data
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},i=[1,..,s]
which lies on
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
via the correlation dimension originally utilised by
[
100
]
. For the sake of completeness, we provide a simple derivation here.

To this end, it is useful to consider the probability
P_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon)
that two arbitrary snapshots
i
and
j
lie at a distance of less than some length scale
\varepsilon
from each other, i.e.
[
77
, p.53]

P_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon)=P(\|\vec{u}_{i}-\vec{u}_{j}\|_{2}\leq\varepsilon)\,.

It is possible to estimate this probability from the snapshot data by computing the distances between all pairs of points
\vec{u}_{i}
and
\vec{u}_{j}
, and determining the proportion of distances smaller than
\varepsilon
, i.e.

\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon)=\frac{1}{s(s-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{s}\sum_{j=i+1}^{s}H(\varepsilon-\|\vec{u}_{i}-\vec{u}_{j}\|)\,,

where
H
is the Heaviside function
[
77
, p.54]
. Assuming the data to be sufficiently dense and homogeneously spaced, in the limit as
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
,
\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}
grows as a length for a one-dimensional manifold, as a surface for a two-dimensional manifold, and so on
[
77
, p.54]
, i.e.
\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon)=k\varepsilon^{\delta}
, or

\delta=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log(\frac{1}{k}\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon)}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon))-\log(k)}{\log(\varepsilon)}=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\frac{\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon)}

with the proportionality factor
k
disappearing in the limit. As both numerator and denominator tend to
-\infty
, we may apply l’Hospital

\delta=\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\nicefrac{{\frac{\partial\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon))}{\partial\varepsilon}}}{{\frac{\partial\log(\varepsilon)}{\partial\varepsilon}}}\,,

and use the definition of the derivative with
\varepsilon_{2}>\varepsilon_{1}

\delta=\lim_{\varepsilon_{1}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\varepsilon_{2}\rightarrow\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon_{2}))-\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon_{1}))}{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{\varepsilon_{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}{\log(\varepsilon_{2})-\log(\varepsilon_{1})}\,,

to yield

\delta=\lim_{\varepsilon_{1}\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\varepsilon_{2}\rightarrow\varepsilon_{1}}\frac{\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon_{2}))-\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon_{1}))}{\log(\varepsilon_{2})-\log(\varepsilon_{1})}\,.

As the limits cannot be realised exactly with finite data, the dimensionality
\delta
of
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
can instead be practically estimated via a scale-dependent correlation dimension
\delta_{\text{SD}}
defined over a range of length scales
\varepsilon
and via finite increments
\Delta\varepsilon
as

\delta_{\text{SD}}=\frac{\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon+\Delta\varepsilon))-\log(\tilde{P}_{\text{CD}}(\varepsilon))}{\log(\varepsilon+\Delta\varepsilon)-\log(\varepsilon)}\,.

Under the aforementioned assumptions of sufficiently dense, homogeneous data and at relatively low
\varepsilon
, this scale-dependent correlation dimension provides an estimate of
\delta
which only relies on the snapshot data
\vec{u}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},i=[1,..,s]
and does not require a priori knowledge about
\mathcal{M}_{\bm{u}}
.

Note that practically, the parameters
\varepsilon
and
\Delta\varepsilon
must be chosen such that a non-negligible number of points lie at a distance of less than
\varepsilon+\Delta\varepsilon
, but more than
\varepsilon
from each other. If
\Delta\varepsilon
is chosen to be excessively small,
\delta_{\text{SD}}
is prone to noise, while for excessively large
\Delta\varepsilon
, it becomes difficult to isolate the desired limit of
\delta_{\text{SD}}
. For this analysis, we varied
\varepsilon
between
\varepsilon_{\text{min}}=0
and
\varepsilon_{\text{max}}=\max_{i,j}(\|\vec{u}_{i}-\vec{u}_{j}\|_{2})
, and selected
\Delta\varepsilon=\frac{\varepsilon_{\text{max}}-\varepsilon_{\text{min}}}{100}
.
In Fig.
22
, the scale-dependent correlation dimension
\delta_{\text{CD}}
associated with the snapshot data for the example discussed in Section
7
is shown over this range of length scales
\varepsilon
. At low
\varepsilon
, we approximately recover the known a priori manifold dimensionality
\delta=6
.

Correlation dimension for the snapshot data
\bm{U}
associated with the example RVE problem investigated in Section
7
.

Algorithm 12

Solve a nonlinear mechanical problem with periodic boundary conditions via the FEM, using graph-based manifold learning methods for MOR, with orthonormalised local linearisation and intermediate POD reduction

Input:

\bm{X}_{\text{glob}},\bm{E}_{\text{nodes}},\bm{E}_{\text{mat}},\text{ELEM},\text{MAT},\text{SHAPE},\text{param},\text{BC info},\bm{U},\bm{Y},\bm{\bar{Y}},\bm{\psi}

//
Initialise global unreduced and reduced displacement fluctuation

\tilde{\bm{u}}\leftarrow\vec{0}
,

\vec{y}\leftarrow\vec{y}_{0}

Incremental time-stepping

while

t_{n}<=1

do

Modify current load via load multiplier

\bm{\bar{H}}_{n}=\bm{\bar{H}}(t_{n})

Iterative solution for nonlinear problem

\text{res}\leftarrow 10^{10}
,
\text{res}_{\text{old}}\leftarrow 10^{11}
;

\Delta\tilde{\bm{u}}_{\text{BC}}\leftarrow\bm{\psi}\bm{\varphi}\Delta\bm{y}
;

Sort into whole displacement fluctuation increment vector

\Delta\tilde{\vec{u}}\leftarrow 0
,

\Delta\tilde{\vec{u}}_{I_{\text{idep}}}\leftarrow\Delta\tilde{\vec{u}}_{\text{BC}}
;

Increment displacement fluctuation and reduced variables

\tilde{\vec{u}}\leftarrow\tilde{\vec{u}}+\Delta\tilde{\vec{u}}
,

Get residual for convergence check

;

\bm{r}_{\text{BC}}\leftarrow\text{BC}

;

;

end while

Increment time

t_{n}\leftarrow t_{n}+\Delta t
;
