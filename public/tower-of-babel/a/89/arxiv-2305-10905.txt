1.
Introduction

Let us consider the following class of nonlocal equations

(1.1)

-\Delta u+u=\left(\ln\frac{1}{|x|}\ast F(u)\right)f(u),\quad\text{ in }\,\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{R})
is the primitive function of the nonlinearity
f
vanishing at zero.
This two-dimensional problem has remained open for a long time because of the sign-changing nature of the Coulomb interaction, given by the convolution,
for which variational methods do not straightforward apply.

Indeed, on the one hand, equation (
1.1
) has a variational structure, in the sense that at least formally, it turns out to be the Euler-Lagrange equation related to the energy functional

(1.2)

I(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(|\nabla u|^{2}+u^{2}\bigg){\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ln(|x-y|)F(u(y))F(u(x)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y.

On the other hand, the second term in (
1.2
) is not well-defined on the natural Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. A few attempts to overcome this difficulty have been done, in particular for power-like nonlinearities, see
[
9
]
and references therein, where the finiteness of such logarithmic convolution term is required as additional condition, which settles the problem in a proper subspace of
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. However, it is well known how the smaller the space the more difficult is to get energy estimates in order to prove existence results. Recently, in
[
7
]
a different approach has been developed by means of a new wighted version of the Trudinger–Moser inequality, for which the problem is well defined in a log-weighted Sobolev space where variational methods can be applied up to cover the maximal possible nonlinear growth, which in dimension two it is of exponential type. See also
[
4
]
for further extensions in this direction.

Let us make the following assumptions on the nonlinearity
f
:

(
f_{1}
)

f(s)\geq 0
for all
s\geq 0
and
f(s)\equiv 0
for all
s\leq 0
.
f(s)\leq Cs^{p}e^{4\pi s^{2}}
as
s\rightarrow+\infty
for some
p>0
and
f(t)=o(t)
as
t\rightarrow 0^{+}
.

(
f_{2}
)

there exist
C>1>\tau>0
such that
\tau\leq\frac{F(s)f^{\prime}(s)}{f^{2}(s)}\leq C
for any
s>0
;

(
f_{3}
)

\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{F(s)f^{\prime}(s)}{f^{2}(s)}=1
or equivalently

\lim\limits_{s\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{d}{ds}\frac{F(s)}{f(s)}=0
;

(
f_{4}
)

there exist
\beta>0
and
0<\rho<1/4
such that

\lim\limits_{t\rightarrow+\infty}\frac{tF(t)}{e^{4\pi t^{2}}}\geq\beta>\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\sqrt{\ln 2}\cdot\pi^{\frac{3}{2}}}.

Notice that from (
f_{3}
), we deduce that there exist
\tau_{0}\in(0,1)
and

t_{0}>0
such that
\frac{F(t)f^{\prime}(t)}{f^{2}(t)}\geq\tau_{0}
for any
t\geq t_{0}
, which in turn implies that

\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{f^{\prime}(s)}{f(s)}{\rm d}s\geq\tau_{0}\int_{t_{0}}^{t}\frac{f(s)}{F(s)}{\rm d}s.

So, from (
f_{1}
) we have that there exist
M_{0}>0
and

t_{0}>0
such that

(1.3)

F(t)\leq M_{0}|f(t)|,\quad|t|\geq t_{0}.

Condition
(f_{1})
is the usual exponential growth assumption as prescribed by the Trudinger-Moser inequality, which gives the following

(1.4)

0\leq F(t)\leq C\cdot\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}t^{2},&t\leq\bar{t},\\
t^{p-1}e^{4\pi t^{2}},&t\geq\tilde{t}\end{array}\right.

for some
\tilde{t}>\bar{t}>0
. Assumptions (
f_{2}
) and (
f_{3}
) have been introduced in
[
7
]
and turn out to be the key ingredients in order to prove boundedness of Palais-Smale sequences. In fact, the difficulty here to obtain the boundedness of Palais-Smale sequences is due to the presence of a non-homogenous nonlinearity
f
for which the Ambrosetti-Rabinowitz condition fails to work. Assumption (
f_{4}
) is in the spirit of de Figueiredo-Miyagaki-Ruf condition
[
10
]
which in dimension two turns out to be the key ingredient in order to have compactness. (It is somehow the equivalent of the Brezis-Nirenberg condition for the mountain pass level
c<\frac{1}{N}S^{N/2}
in higher dimensions
N\geq 3
, where
S
is the optimal constant for the critical Sobolev embedding
H^{1}\hookrightarrow L^{2^{*}}
.)

An explicit example of function
F(t)
satisfying our set of assumptions is the following:

(1.5)

F(t)=\frac{t^{2}}{\ln\frac{1}{\ln(1+t)}}e^{4\pi t^{2}},\quad t\in(0,+\infty).

Let us point out that the result of
[
7
]
does not cover this kind of nonlinearities and in this respect, the approach developed here is an improvement which allows for more general nonlinearities.

Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose the nonlinearity
f
satisfies
(f_{1})
–
(f_{4})
. Then, problem (
1.1
) possesses a positive solution
u\in H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

We observe that as a consequence of
[
8
]
, the solution obtained in Theorem
1.1
is actually radially symmetric, up to translations, and strictly decreasing.

From the point of view of applications, equation (
1.1
) boasts a longstanding presence in quite different nonlinear contexts, from the early studies on Polarons by Fröhlich in the ’30s, to more recent applications to quantum gravity and plasma physics; see
[
16
]
and references therein. The mathematical success, iniziated by Lieb in the ’70s
[
13
]
(let us mention that in the last two years more than 200 papers have been devoted to this topic) is due to the richness of the framework with challenges which range form Functional Analysis, to local and nonlocal systems of PDEs and Potential Theory, see
[
4
,
8
]
. Let us emphasize that a major difficulty in studying the so-called limiting case (
1.1
), is the lack of a proper function space setting. The approach developed in
[
7
]
in dimension two and the extended to any dimension in
[
4
]
, consists of introducing a logarithmic weight on the mass part of the Sobolev norm, namely

(1.6)

\|u\|^{2}:=\|u\|_{H^{1}}^{2}+\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u|^{q}\log(1+|x|){\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{2}{q}},\quad q>2,

and completing smooth compactly supported functions with respect to this norm. This, together with a suitable version of the Trudinger-Moser inequatlity yields a weighted Sobolev space in which the energy functional is well-defined and of class
C^{1}
. This opens the way to variational methods which provide mountain pass type solutions to (
1.1
) which have finite energy in terms of (
1.6
). However, observe that the norm (
1.6
) is not invariant under translations whereas the
energy functional
I
does. Moreover, the quadratic part of the energy functional is never coercive in that context.
Because of the above reasons, the authors in
[
7
,
4
]
can consider nonlinearities
f
whose asymptotic behavior near the origin is given by
s^{q}
, with
q>1
, which plays an crucial role in proving the boundedness of PS sequences. A major purpose of this paper is to remove this restriction and allow to consider the wider range of super-linear nonlinearities, that is
f(t)=o(t)
, as
t\rightarrow 0
.

Here we exploit an asymptotic approximation procedure developed in
[
15
]
to set the problem (
1.1
) in the standard Sobolev space
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. (See also
[
1
]
for further penalization methods in the case of power-like nonlinearities.)
In order to overcome the difficulty of the sign-changing Newtonian kernel, we modify equation (
1.1
) as follows

(1.7)

-\Delta{u}+u-\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u)\bigg]f(u)=0,\quad\mbox{ in }\,\,\mathbb{R}^{2},

where
\alpha\in(0,1)
and

\lim\limits_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}G_{\alpha}(x):=\lim\limits_{\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}}\frac{|x|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}=-\ln|x|

for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
.
The corresponding energy functional to (
1.7
)
is well defined in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
for fixed
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
which enables us to use minimax methods to establish the existence of
positive solutions for (
1.7
).

By passing to the limit as
\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}
, a convergence argument within
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
allows us to prove that the limit solution turns out to be a positive solutions to the original problem (
1.1
). Here the main difficulty is the balance between the too loose sign-changing logarithm kernel and the exponential growth rate of a fairly general nonlinearity. Some uniform bounds with respect to the asymptotic approximation parameter
\alpha
turn out to be the key in order to get compactness and then existence of a finite energy (in the
H^{1}
sense) solution. We are confident that the methods developed here might be useful in other contexts.

2.
Preliminaries

For
1\leq s\leq+\infty
, we denote by
\|\cdot\|_{s}
the usual
norm of the Lebesgue space
L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as well as

H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):=\{u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\nabla u\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})\}

is the usual Sobolev space endowed with the norm

\|u\|:=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(|\nabla u|^{2}+u^{2}){\rm d}x\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. In the sequel, when it is not misleading, constants
C
may change value from line to line.

Let us next recall some basic facts starting with the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality, see for instance
[
2
]
, which will be frequently used throughout this paper.

Lemma 2.1
.

(Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality)
Let
s,r>1
and
\alpha\in(0,N)
with
1/s+\alpha/N+1/r=2
,
f\in L^{s}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
h\in L^{r}(\mathbb{R}^{N})
. There exists a sharp constant

C_{s,N,\alpha,r}
independent of
f,h
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}\ast f(x)\bigg]h(x){\rm d}x\leq C_{s,N,\alpha,r}\|f\|_{s}\|h\|_{r}.

If
t=s=\frac{2N}{2N-\alpha}
, then

C_{s,N,\alpha,r}=C_{N,\alpha}=\pi^{\alpha/2}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N}{2}-\frac{\alpha}{2})}{\Gamma(N-\frac{\alpha}{2})}\bigg\{\frac{\Gamma(\frac{N}{2})}{\Gamma(N)}\bigg\}^{-1+\frac{\alpha}{N}},

and if
N=2,\alpha\in(0,1]
, then
C_{2,\alpha}\leq 2\sqrt{\pi}
.

As afore mentioned, in dimension two the maximal degree of summability for functions with membership in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is of exponential type, for which we recall the first result available in the whole plane due to
[
5
]
:

(2.1)

\sup_{\|\nabla u\|_{2}\leq 1,\,\|u\|_{2}\leq M}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\left(e^{\alpha|u|^{2}}-1\right)\,{\rm d}x\leq C(\alpha)\|u\|_{2}<\infty,\text{ if }\alpha<4\pi.

(See also
[
6
]
for a wide context on this topic.)

Lemma 2.2
.

Assume that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
then there exists
C>0
independent of
n
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{F(u_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{\nu}}{\rm d}y\leq C

uniformly for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where
\nu\in(0,1)
.

Proof
By Hölder’s inequality, (
f_{1}
) and (
f_{2}
), one has

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u_{n}(y)){\rm d}y<+\infty

uniformly for
n
, since
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
(see
[
11
]
). It then follows from (
f_{1}
) and (
f_{2}
) that

(2.2)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{F(u_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{\nu}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{F(u_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{\nu}}{\rm d}y+\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{F(u_{n}(y))}{|x-y|^{\nu}}{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq C+\bigg(\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{2\nu}}{\rm d}y\bigg)^{1/2}\cdot\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F^{2}(u_{n}(y)){\rm d}y\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u_{n}|^{4}+(e^{9\pi|u_{n}|^{2}}-1){\rm d}y\bigg)^{1/2}

\displaystyle\leq C,

where the last inequality follows from the fact that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
H^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

\square

The proof of the following Lemma is standard and we omit it.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any
\alpha\in(0,1]
, there exists
C_{\beta}>0
such that

\frac{s^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}\leq C_{\beta}s^{-\beta}

holds for all
\beta\in(\alpha,+\infty)
and
s>0
.

3.
The asymptotic approximation method

3.1.
The modified equation

Recently in
[
8
]
, the authors used the method of moving plans to prove that
positive solutions of (
1.1
) are radially symmetric. Based on this fact, it is natural to restrict ourself to the space
H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
of radially symmetric functions.

Set
G_{\alpha}(x)=\frac{|x|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}
,
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and consider the following equation

(3.1)

-\Delta{u}+u=\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u)\bigg]f(u)

which has the associated energy functional

I_{\alpha}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(|\nabla u|^{2}+u^{2}\bigg){\rm d}x+\frac{1}{2\alpha}\bigg[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u){\rm d}x\bigg]^{2}-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u)\bigg]F(u){\rm d}x.

3.2.
Regularity of the modified energy functional

According to the definition of
G_{\alpha}
, by means of the Hardy-Littleword-Sobolev inequality, let us prove the following

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\alpha>0
, then
I_{\alpha}\in C^{1}(H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\mathbb{R})
and

I^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u)v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\nabla u\nabla v+uv\bigg){\rm d}x+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u){\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u)v{\rm d}x-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u)\bigg]f(u)v{\rm d}x

for any
u,v\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.

Proof
It is standard to check that for fixed
\alpha>0
, one has
I_{\alpha}\in C(H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}),\mathbb{R})
. By straightforward calculations, the Gâteaux derivative of
I_{\alpha}
at
u\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is given by

I^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u)v=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg(\nabla u\nabla v+uv\bigg){\rm d}x+\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u){\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u)v{\rm d}x-\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u)\bigg]f(u)v{\rm d}x,

for any
v\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
It remains to prove that
I^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u_{n})\rightarrow I^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u)
if
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Now on the one hand,
observe that uniformly in
v\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with
\|v\|\leq 1
,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u_{n}){\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u_{n})v{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u){\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u)v{\rm d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u_{n}){\rm d}x\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(u_{n})v-f(u)v|{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\big|F(u_{n})-F(u)\big|{\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u)v{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(u_{n})v-f(u)v|{\rm d}x+o_{n}(1)

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(u_{n})-f(u)|^{2}{\rm d}x\bigg)^{1/2}\cdot\|v\|+o_{n}(1)\|v\|.

On the other hand, by (
f_{1}
) and (
f_{2}
), one has for any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

|f(u_{n})|^{2}\leq C_{\varepsilon}\bigg(|u_{n}|^{2}+e^{(8\pi+\varepsilon)|u_{n}|^{2}}\bigg)=:g(u_{n}).

It is easy to check that
g(u_{n})\rightarrow g(u)
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
due to the fact that
u_{n}\rightarrow u
in
H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Hence, by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, we get
\|f(u_{n})\|_{2}^{2}\rightarrow\|f(u)\|_{2}^{2}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, from (
3.2
) one has that, uniformly
in
v\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with
\|v\|\leq 1
,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u_{n}){\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u_{n})v{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(u){\rm d}x\cdot\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u)v{\rm d}x\bigg|=o_{n}(1).

Moreover, by Lemma
2.2
-
i)
and the Hardy-Littleword-Sobolev inequality,
we have for any
v\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, with
\|v\|\leq 1
,

(3.4)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u_{n})\bigg]f(u_{n})v{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u)\bigg]f(u)v{\rm d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u_{n})\bigg]|f(u_{n})-f(u)|v{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*|F(u_{n})-F(u)|\bigg]f(u)v{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(u_{n})-f(u)|v{\rm d}x+\|F(u_{n})-F(u)\|_{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}\|f(u)v\|_{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq Co_{n}(1)\|v\|+C\|F(u_{n})-F(u)\|_{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}\|v\|.

Observe that by the mean value theorem, Hölder’s inequality, (
f_{1}
) and (
f_{2}
), there exists
\theta\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle\|F(u_{n})-F(u)\|^{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}_{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|f(u_{n}+\theta(u_{n}-u))(u_{n}-u)|^{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|(|u_{n}|+|u|)(u_{n}-u)|^{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}{\rm d}x+C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(|u_{n}|+|u|)^{\frac{4p}{4-\alpha}}|u_{n}-u|^{\frac{4}{4-\alpha}}e^{{\frac{16\pi}{4-\alpha}}(|u_{n}|+|u_{0}|)^{2}}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq o_{n}(1)+C\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}(|u_{n}|+|u|)^{\frac{4pt}{4-\alpha}}|(u_{n}-u)|^{\frac{4t}{4-\alpha}}{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{t}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{{\frac{16\pi t^{\prime}}{4-\alpha}}\pi(|u_{n}|+|u_{0}|)^{2}}{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq o_{n}(1)+Co_{n}(1)\cdot\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{{\frac{128\pi t^{\prime}}{4-\alpha}}\pi(|u_{n}-u_{0}|^{2}+|u_{0}|^{2})}{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}}

\displaystyle\leq Co_{n}(1),

where
\frac{1}{t}+\frac{1}{t^{\prime}}=1
. Then, from (
3.4
) we have

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u_{n})\bigg]f(u_{n})v{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[\frac{1}{|x|^{\alpha}}*F(u)\bigg]f(u)v{\rm d}x\bigg|=o_{n}(1)\|v\|,

which together with (
3.3
) completes the proof.

3.3.
Critical points of the modified energy functional

Lemma 3.2
.

Assume that
(f_{1})
–
(f_{4})
hold, then we have:

i)

there exist constants
\rho,\eta>0
such that
I_{\alpha}|_{S_{\rho}}\geq\eta>0
for all

u\in S_{\rho}=\{u\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}):\,\,\|u\|=\rho\};

ii)

there exists
e\in H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
with
\|e\|>\rho
such that

I_{\alpha}(e)<0
.

Proof.

We first assume that
u\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
\|u\|\leq\theta
for some
\theta\in(0,1)
. Obviously,
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla u|^{2}<1
.
Moreover, by (
f_{1}
)-(
f_{2}
) and (
1.4
), there is
C_{\theta}>0
such that

|F(u)|^{\frac{4}{3}}\leq C\bigg(|u|^{\frac{8}{3}}+|u|^{\frac{4}{3}(p+1)}e^{\frac{8\pi}{3}|u|^{2}}\bigg).

So by (
f_{1}
), the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality yields

(3.5)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|x-y|}F(u(y))F(u(x)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|F(u)|^{\frac{4}{3}}{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|u|^{\frac{8}{3}}+|u|^{\frac{4}{3}(p+1)}e^{\frac{8\pi}{3}|u|^{2}}{\rm d}x\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}

\displaystyle\leq C\bigg(\|u\|_{\frac{8}{3}}^{4}+\|u\|_{\frac{4\varsigma}{3}(p+1)}^{2(p+1)}\bigg),\quad\varsigma>1.

From (
3.5
) and Lemma
2.3
we deduce that

(3.6)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(u(y))F(u(x)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(u(y))F(u(x)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{F(u(y))F(u(x))}{|x-y|}{\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\|u\|^{2}-C\bigg(\|u\|_{\frac{8}{3}}^{4}+\|u\|_{\frac{4\varsigma}{3}(p+1)}^{2(p+1)}\bigg).

So, let
\|u\|=\rho>0
be small
enough, it is easy to check that there exists
\eta>0
such that

I_{\alpha}(u)\geq\eta
for any
\alpha\in(0,1)
: this completes the proof of
i)
.

For the proof of
ii)
, let us take
e_{0}\in H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies
e_{0}(x)\equiv 1
for
x\in B_{\frac{1}{8}}(0)
,
e_{0}(x)\equiv 0
for
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus{B_{\frac{1}{4}}(0)}
and
|\nabla e_{0}(x)|\leq C
. Set

(3.7)

\Psi(t):=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F\big(te_{0}\big){\rm d}x\bigg)^{2},

then by (
f_{2}
) we have
F(t)\leq(1-\tau)f(t)t
for
t\geq 0,\tau\in(0,1)
, and then

\frac{\Psi^{\prime}(t)}{\Psi(t)}\geq\frac{2}{(1-\tau)t}\quad\text{ for\,all }\,t>0.

Integrating this over
[1,s]
, we find

(3.8)

\Psi(s)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F\big(se_{0}\big){\rm d}x\bigg)^{2}\geq\Psi(1)s^{\frac{2}{1-\tau}}.

Note that

\frac{s^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}\geq\ln\frac{1}{s},\quad\text{for}\,s\in(0,1].

From (
3.7
) and (
3.8
) we have

(3.9)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(te_{0})

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|e_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F\big(te_{0}(y)\big)F\big(te_{0}(x)\big){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|e_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{|x-y|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F\big(te_{0}(y)\big)F\big(te_{0}(x)\big){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|e_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int\int_{|x-y|\leq\frac{1}{2}}\ln\frac{1}{|x-y|}F\big(te_{0}(y)\big)F\big(te_{0}(x)\big){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|e_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{\ln 2}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(te_{0}){\rm d}x\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|e_{0}\|^{2}-\frac{\ln 2}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(e_{0}){\rm d}x\bigg)^{2}t^{\frac{2}{1-\tau}},

which implies that there exists
t_{0}>0
large enough such that
I_{\alpha}(t_{0}e_{0})<0
.

\square

So
I_{\alpha}
has a mountain pass geometry, with mountain pass value

c_{\alpha}=\inf\limits_{\gamma\in\Gamma}\max\limits_{t\in[0,1]}I_{\alpha}(\gamma(t)),

where

\Gamma:=\{\gamma\in C([0,1],H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})):\,\gamma(0)=0,\gamma(1)=e\}.

The existence of a Cerami sequence for
I_{\alpha}
, namely
\{u_{n}\}\subset H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
such that

I_{\alpha}(u_{n})\rightarrow c_{\alpha},\quad\quad(1+\|u_{n}\|)I^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u_{n})\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{ as }n\rightarrow+\infty,

is given by the variant of the Mountain Pass Theorem in
[
12
]
.

Remark 3.3
.

Observe from Lemma
3.2
that there exist two constants
a,b>0
independent of
\alpha
such that
a<c_{\alpha}<b
.

3.4.
Level estimates for the modified energy

Let us now define Moser’s type functions
w_{n}(x)
supported in
B_{\rho}(0)
as follows:

\displaystyle w_{n}(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\sqrt{\ln n},&0\leq|x|\leq\rho/n,\\
\frac{\ln(\rho/|x|)}{\sqrt{\ln n}},&\rho/n\leq|x|\leq\rho,\\
0,&|x|\geq\rho,\end{array}\right.

where

is given in
(f_{4})
.
We have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\|w_{n}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\bigg(|\nabla w_{n}|^{2}+w_{n}^{2}\bigg){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=1+\rho^{2}(\frac{1}{4\ln n}-\frac{1}{4n^{2}\ln n}-\frac{1}{2n^{2}})

\displaystyle=:1+\rho^{2}\delta_{n}.

From

\frac{s^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}\geq\ln\frac{1}{s},\quad\text{ for }\,s,\alpha\in(0,1],

the following holds

(3.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(w_{n})\bigg]F(w_{n}){\rm d}x

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(w_{n}(x))F(w_{n}(y)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(w_{n}(x))F(w_{n}(y)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\int_{B_{\rho}(0)}\ln\frac{1}{|x-y|}F(w_{n}(x))F(w_{n}(y)){\rm d}x{\rm d}y\geq 0.

Lemma 3.4
.

The mountain pass level
c_{\alpha}
satisfies
\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in(0,1)}c_{\alpha}<\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Recalling (
f_{4}
), for

(3.12)

\varepsilon\in\bigg(0,\beta-\frac{1}{\rho^{2}\sqrt{\ln 2}\cdot\pi^{3/2}}\bigg),

there exists
t_{\varepsilon}>0
such that

(3.13)

\displaystyle tF(t)\geq(\beta-\varepsilon)e^{4\pi t^{2}},\quad\text{for}\,\,t\geq t_{\varepsilon}.

We divide the proof into three cases:

Case 1.
Let
t\in\left[0,\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}}\right]
, then by (
3.10
)-(
3.11
), we have for large
n
,

(3.14)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(tw_{n})

\displaystyle=\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|w_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(tw_{n}(y))F(tw_{n}(x)){\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle<\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|w_{n}\|^{2}<\frac{1}{2}.

Case 2.
Let
t\in\bigg(\sqrt{2},+\infty\bigg)
. According to the definition of
w_{n}
, we have for
large
n
,
tw_{n}(x)\geq t_{\varepsilon}
for
x\in B_{\rho/n}
.
From (
3.10
)-(
3.13
), we deduce that for large
n
,

(3.15)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(tw_{n})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|w_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ln\frac{1}{|x-y|}F(tw_{n}(y))F(tw_{n}(x)){\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|w_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{\ln 2}{2}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F(tw_{n}){\rm d}x\bigg)^{2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1+\delta_{n}\rho^{2}}{2}t^{2}-\frac{(\ln 2)\pi^{3}(\beta-\varepsilon)^{2}\rho^{4}}{n^{4}t^{2}\ln n}e^{4t^{2}\ln n}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle t^{2}\bigg(1-\frac{1}{t^{4}\ln n}n^{4(t^{2}-1)}\bigg).

Let

g(n,t):=\frac{1}{t^{4}\ln n}n^{4(t^{2}-1)},t\geq\sqrt{2},n\geq 2.

then there exists
n_{0}>0
such that

g(n,t)\geq 1
for
n\geq n_{0}
and
t\geq\sqrt{2}
.
Hence, when
t\in\bigg(\sqrt{2},+\infty\bigg)
and
n
is large enough,

(3.16)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(tw_{n})<\frac{1}{2}.

Case 3.
Let
t\in\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}},\sqrt{2}\right]
. According to the definition of
w_{n}
, we have for
sufficiently large
n
,
tw_{n}(x)\geq t_{\varepsilon}
for
x\in B_{\rho/n}
. From (
3.10
)–(
3.13
) we have

(3.17)

\displaystyle I_{\alpha}(tw_{n})\leq

\displaystyle\frac{t^{2}}{2}\|w_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ln\frac{1}{|x-y|}F(tw_{n}(y))F(tw_{n}(x)){\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\frac{1+\delta_{n}\rho^{2}}{2}t^{2}-\frac{(\ln 2)\pi^{3}(\beta-\varepsilon)^{2}\rho^{4}}{n^{4}t^{2}\ln n}e^{4t^{2}\ln n}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1+\delta_{n}\rho^{2}}{2}t^{2}-\frac{1}{2\ln n}n^{4(t^{2}-1)}:=\psi_{n}(t).

Then, there exists
t_{n}>0
such that
\displaystyle\psi_{n}(t_{n})=\max_{t>0}\psi_{n}(t)

(3.18)

t_{n}^{2}=\bigg[1+\frac{\ln(1+\rho^{2}\delta_{n})-\ln 4}{4\ln n}\bigg].

Clearly,

for large

. Then, by (
3.17
) and (
3.18
) we have

(3.19)

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(t)\leq

\displaystyle\psi_{n}(t_{n})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t_{n}^{2}\frac{1+\delta_{n}\rho^{2}}{2}-\frac{1}{8\ln n}(1+\delta_{n}\rho^{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1+\delta_{n}\rho^{2})\bigg[\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\ln(1+\rho^{2}\delta_{n})-\ln 4}{8\ln n}-\frac{1}{8\ln n}\bigg]

\displaystyle\leq

which, together with the definition of

Combining (

), (

) and (

),

. Indeed, for fixed

. Define

\square

3.5.
Compactness

In this Section we analyze the behavior of Cerami’s sequences. Let us begin with the following

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume
(f_{1})
–
(f_{4})
and let
\{u_{n}\}\subset H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
be an arbitrary Cerami sequence for
I_{\alpha}
at level
c_{\alpha}
.
Then,
\{u_{n}\}
stays bounded in
H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
as well as

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]F(u_{n}){\rm d}x\bigg|<C,\quad\quad\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]f(u_{n})u_{n}{\rm d}x\bigg|<C.

Proof.

Since
\{u_{n}\}\subset H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a Cerami sequence, as

we have

(3.21)

\frac{1}{2}\|u_{n}\|^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]F(u_{n}){\rm d}x\rightarrow c_{\alpha}

and for all

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla u_{n}\nabla v{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{n}v{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]f(u_{n})v{\rm d}x=o_{n}(1)\|v\|.

Now take

to get

(3.23)

\|u_{n}\|^{2}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]f(u_{n})u_{n}{\rm d}x=o_{n}(1).

In order to verify the boundedness of
\{u_{n}\}
, let us introduce a suitable test
function as follows

\displaystyle v_{n}:=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{F(u_{n})}{f(u_{n})},&u_{n}>0,\\
\\
(1-\tau)u_{n},&u_{n}\leq 0,\\
\end{array}\right.

with
\tau
as
(f_{2})
. We have
|v_{n}|\leq C|u_{n}|
since
F(t)\leq(1-\tau)f(t)t
and
f(t)=0
if and only if
t=0
. Thus
v_{n}
is well defined in
H^{1}_{r}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. Taking
v=v_{n}
in (
3.22
) and recalling that
f(t),F(t)=0
for
t\leq 0
we have

(3.24)

\displaystyle(1-\tau)

\displaystyle\int_{u_{n}\leq 0}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}{\rm d}x+\int_{u_{n}>0}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\bigg(1-\frac{F(u_{n})f^{\prime}(u_{n})}{f^{2}(u_{n})}\bigg){\rm d}x+(1-\tau)\int_{u_{n}\leq 0}u_{n}^{2}{\rm d}x

\displaystyle+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{n}\frac{F(u_{n})}{f(u_{n})}{\rm d}x-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(|x|)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]F(u_{n}){\rm d}x=o_{n}(1)\|v_{n}\|.

and by recalling (
3.21
) we also have

\displaystyle(1-\tau)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}

\displaystyle|\nabla u_{n}|{\rm d}x+(1-\tau)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{n}^{2}{\rm d}x+2c_{\alpha}-\|u_{n}\|^{2}\geq o_{n}(1)\|v_{n}\|,

which implies

(3.25)

\tau\|u_{n}\|^{2}\leq o_{n}(1)\|u_{n}\|+2c_{\alpha}.

As a consequence, we have proved that
\|u_{n}\|\leq C
for some
C>0
independent of
n
. Moreover, we immediately have from (
3.21
) and (

\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]F(u_{n}){\rm d}x\bigg|<C,\quad\quad\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\bigg[G_{\alpha}(x)\ast F(u_{n})\bigg]f(u_{n})u_{n}{\rm d}x\bigg|<C.

\square

Lemma 3.6
.

let
\{u_{n}\}
be a bounded Cerami sequence for
I_{\alpha}
at level
c_{\alpha}
.
Then, there exists
C>0
independent of

such that

Since
\{u_{n}\}
is bounded in

in

u_{n}\rightarrow u

and define

. Let us show that

(3.26)

\|v_{n}\|^{2}\leq 1.

From

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}|\nabla u_{n}|^{2}\bigg(1-\frac{F(u_{n})f^{\prime}(u_{n})}{f^{2}(u_{n})}\bigg){\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{n}\frac{F(u_{n})}{f(u_{n})}{\rm d}x+2c_{\alpha}-\|u_{n}\|^{2}=o_{n}(1),

and in turn

for
n
large enough. Next we give an

above,

u_{\alpha}(x)\leq M\exp\bigg(-\frac{1}{2}|x|\bigg)\quad\text{for}\,\,|x|\geq R.

Here
R,M
are independent of
\alpha
.

\square

Proof of Theorem 1.1.
We are now in the position to carry out the proof of our main result which we divide into two steps:

Step 1.
Let us show that
u_{0}\in H_{r}^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies
I^{\prime}(u_{0})=0
.

For any
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have

(4.7)

\displaystyle I^{\prime}_{\alpha}(u_{\alpha})\varphi=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\nabla u_{\alpha}\nabla\varphi{\rm d}x+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}u_{\alpha}\varphi{\rm d}x

\displaystyle-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(u_{\alpha}(y)){\rm d}yf(u_{\alpha})\varphi{\rm d}x.

Recalling (
3.41
), we have

(4.8)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u_{\alpha})\varphi{\rm d}x\rightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}f(u_{0})\varphi{\rm d}x,\quad\text{as}\,\,\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}.

Then it follows from Lemma
2.3
that for any fixed
\varphi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, we have for
\alpha\in(0,\alpha^{*})

(4.9)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}1_{|x-y|\leq 1}F(u_{\alpha}(y))f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\frac{4(\omega-1)}{3\omega}}}1_{|x-y|\leq 1}F(u_{\alpha}(y))f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x)\bigg|:=h_{\alpha}(x,y),

which, together with Lemma
4.1
and (
4.8
), yields

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}h_{\alpha}(x,y)-\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\frac{4(\omega-1)}{3\omega}}}1_{|x-y|\leq 1}F(u_{0}(y))f(u_{0}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}x{\rm d}y\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\frac{4(\omega-1)}{3\omega}}}1_{|x-y|\leq 1}\big(F(u_{\alpha}(y))-F(u_{0}(y))\big)f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}y{\rm d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle+\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\frac{1}{|x-y|^{\frac{4(\omega-1)}{3\omega}}}1_{|x-y|\leq 1}F(u_{0}(y)){\rm d}y\big(f(u_{\alpha}(x))-f(u_{0}(x))\big)\varphi(x){\rm d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle=:

\displaystyle I_{1}+o_{\alpha}(1).

Observe by Lemma
4.1
that there exists
C>0
independent of
\alpha
such that

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1_{|x-y|\leq 1}f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}y{\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}1_{\Omega}{\rm d}yf(u_{0}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}x+o_{\alpha}(1),

where
|\Omega|
is large enough. Thus, the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem implies
I_{1}\rightarrow 0
, as
\alpha\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
Furthermore, since
\{h_{\alpha}\}
has a strongly convergent subsequence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
,
we use the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem in (
4.9
) to get

(4.10)

\displaystyle\int\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(u_{\alpha}(y)){\rm d}yf(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\rightarrow-\int\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}\ln(|x-y|)F(u_{0}(y)){\rm d}yf(u_{0}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}x.

When
|x-y|\geq 1
, there exists
\tau=\tau(|x-y|)\in(0,1)
such that

(4.11)

0\geq G_{\alpha}(x-y)=\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}=-|x-y|^{-\tau\alpha}\ln|x-y|,

where
\tau
depends on
|x-y|
.
Since
\varphi
has a compact support, from Lemma
4.2
and
(f_{1})
we have

(4.12)

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}\cdot 1_{|x-y|\geq 1}F(u_{\alpha}(y))f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg||x-y|^{-\tau\alpha}\ln|x-y|\cdot 1_{|x-y|\geq 1}F(u_{\alpha}(y))f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|(|x|+|y|)F(u_{\alpha}(y))f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\bigg|C\bigg(F(u_{\alpha}(y))+M|y|e^{-\frac{1}{2}|y|}\bigg)f(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x)\bigg|

\displaystyle=:\bar{h}_{\alpha}(x,y).

Using Lemma
4.2
and Lemma
3.7
, we have that
\{\bar{h}_{\alpha}\}
has
a strongly convergent subsequence in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Combining (
4.11
) with (
4.12
), similarly to (
4.9
), by the Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem, one has

(4.13)

\displaystyle\int\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\frac{|x-y|^{-\alpha}-1}{\alpha}F(u_{\alpha}(y)){\rm d}yf(u_{\alpha}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\rightarrow-\int\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}\ln|x-y|F(u_{0}(y)){\rm d}yf(u_{0}(x))\varphi(x){\rm d}x.

Fatou’s lemma yields

(4.14)

\displaystyle\bigg|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\ln|x-y|F(u_{0}(y)){\rm d}yF(u_{0}(x)){\rm d}x\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\liminf\limits_{\alpha\rightarrow 0}\bigg(\int\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}G_{\alpha}(x-y)F(u_{\alpha}(y)){\rm d}yF(u_{\alpha}(x)){\rm d}x

\displaystyle-\int\int_{|x-y|\geq 1}G_{\alpha}(x-y)F(u_{\alpha}(y)){\rm d}yF(u_{\alpha}(x)){\rm d}x\bigg).

By the Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality, (

\displaystyle\int\int_{|x-y|\leq 1}G_{\alpha}(x-y)F(u_{\alpha}(y)){\rm d}yF(u_{\alpha}(x)){\rm d}x

\displaystyle\leq C_{\omega}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}F^{\omega}(u_{\alpha}){\rm d}x\bigg)^{2}\leq C
