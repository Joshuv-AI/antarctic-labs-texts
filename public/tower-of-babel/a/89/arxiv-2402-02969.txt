Towards Understanding the Word Sensitivity of Attention Layers: A Study via Random Features
Simone Bombari, Marco Mondelli

Abstract
Understanding the reasons behind the exceptional success of transformers requires a better analysis of why attention layers are suitable for NLP tasks. In particular, such tasks require predictive models to capture contextual meaning which often depends on one or few words, even if the sentence is long. Our work studies this key property, dubbed word sensitivity (WS), in the prototypical setting of random features. We show that attention layers enjoy high WS, namely, there exists a vector in the space of embeddings that largely perturbs the random attention features map. The argument critically exploits the role of the softmax \softmax in the attention layer, highlighting its benefit compared to other activations (e.g., ReLU). In contrast, the WS of standard random features is of order 1 / n 1/\sqrt{n} , n n being the number of words in the textual sample, and thus it decays with the length of the context. We then translate these results on the word sensitivity into generalization bounds: due to their low WS, random features provably cannot learn to distinguish between two sentences that differ only in a single word; in contrast, due to their high WS, random attention features have higher generalization capabilities. We validate our theoretical results with experimental evidence over the BERT-Base word embeddings of the imdb review dataset.

Deep learning theory has provided a quantitative description of phenomena routinely occuring in state-of-the-art models, such as double-descent

Recent work tackling this problem
characterizes the sample complexities required by simplified attention models
[

,
27
]
.
However, learning is limited to a specific set of targets, such as sparse functions
[

To formalize the problem, we represent the textual data with
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, where the
n
rows represent the word embeddings in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, we define the word sensitivity (WS) in (
3.4
), as a measure of how changing a row in
X
modifies the embedding of a given feature map. We focus our study on the prototypical setting of
random features
, i.e., where the weights of the layers are random. In particular, we consider
(i)
the
random features
(RF) map
[
52
]
, defined in (
3.1
), and
(ii)
the
random attention features
(RAF) map
[
27
]
, defined in (

). The former models a fully connected architecture, while the latter captures the structure typical of attention layers.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Theorem
1
shows that an RF map has
low
WS, specifically of order
1/\sqrt{n}
, where
n
is the context length. This means that changing a single word has a negligible effect on the output of the map. In fact, to have a significant effect, one needs to change a constant fraction of the words, see Remark
4.1
. Furthermore, increasing the depth of the architecture does not help with the word sensitivity, as shown by Theorem
2
.

•

Our main result, Theorem
3
, shows that a RAF map has
high
WS, specifically of constant order which does not depend on the length of the context. This means that changing even a single word can have a significant effect on the output of the map, regardless of the context length. The argument critically exploits the role of the
\softmax
in the attention layer, and numerical simulations show its advantages compared to other activations (e.g., ReLU).

•

Section
6
exploits the bounds on the word sensitivity to characterize the generalization error when the data changes meaning after modifying a single word in the context. In particular, we consider generalized linear models trained on RF and RAF embeddings, and we establish whether a fine-tuned or retrained model can learn to distinguish between two samples that differ only in one row. While the answer is provably negative for random features (Theorems
4
and
5
), random attention features are capable of generalizing.

Most of our technical contributions require no distributional assumptions on the data, and the generality of our findings is confirmed by numerical results on the BERT-Base embeddings of the imdb dataset
[
42
]
, and on the pre-trained BERT-Base model itself. Our code is publicly available at the GitHub repository
simone-bombari/attention-sensitivity
.

Sensitivity of neural networks.

We informally use the term sensitivity to express how a perturbation of the input changes the output of the model. Previous work explored various mathematical formulations of this concept (e.g., the input-output Lipschitz constant) in the context of both robustness
[

,
17
]

[

[

3
Preliminaries

We consider a sequence of
n
tokens
\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, with
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
for every
i
, where
d
denotes the token embedding dimension, and
n
the context length. These tokens altogether represent the textual sample
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
.
We denote by
\Flat(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, with
D=nd
, the flattened (or vectorized) version of
X
. Given a vector
x
,
\left\|x\right\|_{2}
is its Euclidean norm. Given a matrix
A
,
\left\|A\right\|_{2}:=\left\|\Flat(A)\right\|_{2}\equiv\left\|A\right\|_{F}
is its Frobenius norm. We indicate with
e_{i}
the
i
-th element of the canonical basis, and denote
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. All the complexity notations
\Omega(\cdot)
,
\omega(\cdot)
,
\mathcal{O}(\cdot)
,
o(\cdot)
and
\Theta(\cdot)
are understood for sufficiently large context length
n
, token embedding dimension
d
, number of neurons
k
, and number of input samples
N
. We indicate with
C,c>0
numerical constants, independent of
n,d,k,N
. Throughout the paper, we make the following assumption, which is easily achieved by pre-processing the raw data.

Assumption 1

(Normalization of token embedding)
.

For every token
x_{i}
, we assume
\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
.

Random Features (RF).

A fully connected layer with random weights is commonly referred to as a
random features
map
[
52
]
. The map acts from a vector of covariates to a feature space
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, where
k
denotes the number of neurons. Thus, we flatten the context
\Flat(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
before feeding it in the layer, and the RF map
\varphi_{\textup{RF}}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
takes the form

\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)=\phi(V\Flat(X)),

(3.1)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is a non-linearity applied component-wise and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times D}
is the random features matrix, with
V_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/D)
. This scaling of the variance of
V
ensures that the entries of
V\Flat(X)
have unit variance, as
\left\|\Flat X\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{D}
by Assumption
1
.
We later consider a similar model with several random layers, referred to as
deep random features
(DRF) model and recently considered by
[
15
,
55
]
.

Random Attention Features (RAF).

We consider a single-head sequence-to-sequence self-attention layer without biases
\varphi_{\textup{QKV}}(X)

[
64
]
, given by

\varphi_{\textup{QKV}}(X)=\softmax\left(\frac{XW_{Q}^{\top}W_{K}X^{\top}}{\sqrt{d^{\prime}}}\right)XW_{V}^{\top},

(3.2)

where the
\softmax
is applied row-wise and defined as
\softmax(s)_{i}=e^{s_{i}}/\sum_{j}e^{s_{j}}
;
W_{Q},W_{K},W_{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{d^{\prime}\times d}
are respectively the queries, keys and values weight matrices.
As in previous related work
[
27
]
, we simplify the above expression with the re-parameterization
W:=W_{Q}^{\top}W_{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, and by removing the values weight matrix. This is for convenience of presentation, and our results can be generalized to the case where queries, keys and values are random independent features, see Remark
5.1
at the end of Section
5
. Thus, we define the
random attention features
layer
\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
as

\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)=\softmax\left(\frac{XWX^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)X,

(3.3)

where
W_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
. We refer to the argument of the
\softmax
with the shorthand
S(X):=XWX^{\top}/\sqrt{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. The scaling of the variance of
W
ensures that the entries of
S(X)
have unit variance. Differently from
[
27
]
, we do not consider the biased initialization discussed in
[
63
]
, designed to make the diagonal elements of
W
positive in expectation. As remarked in
[
63
]
, this initialization is aimed at replicating the final attention scores of vision transformers, and its utility on language models is less discussed. Furthermore, we remark that our simplified model does not include any of the architectural tweaks introduced to allow transformer models to process longer contexts
[
10
,
8
]
, as it aims to capture the specific properties of a single attention layer.

Word sensitivity (WS).

The word sensitivity (WS) measures how the embedding of a given mapping
\varphi(X)
is impacted by a change in a single word. Given a sample
X=[x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, the
perturbed
sample
X^{i}(\Delta)
is obtained from
X
by setting its
i
-th row to
x_{i}+\Delta
and keeping the remaining rows the same. Here,
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denotes the perturbation of the
i
-th token, and its magnitude does not exceed the scaling of Assumption
1
, i.e.,
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
. Formally, given a mapping
\varphi
, we are interested in

\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(X)=\sup_{i\in[n],\,\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}}\frac{\left\|\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}}.\vskip 6.99997pt

(3.4)

In words,
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(X)
denotes the highest relative change of
\varphi(X)
, upon changing a single token in the input. Our goal is to study how
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(X)
behaves for the RF and RAF models, with respect to the context length
n
. Informally, if
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(X)=o(1)
for any
X
, the mapping
\varphi
has
low word sensitivity
. On the contrary, if
\mathcal{S}_{\varphi}(X)=\Omega(1)
,
\varphi
has
high word
sensitivity
.

We remark that, in language models, tokens are elements of a discrete vocabulary. However, working directly in the embedding space (as in definition (
3.4
)) is common in the theoretical literature
[
37
]
, and critical steps of practical algorithms for language models also take place in the embedding space
[
25
,
58
,
70
]
.

The definition of WS recalls similar notions of sensitivity in the context of adversarial robustness
[
24
,
17
,
67
,
13
]
, as well as the literature that designs adversarial prompting schemes for language models
[
25
,
30
,
70
]
.
However, in contrast with the definition in (
3.4
), these works consider a
trained
model and, therefore, the results implicitly depend on the training dataset. Our analysis of the WS dissects the impact of the feature map
\varphi
, and it does not involve any training process. The consequences on training (and generalization error) will be considered in Section
6
.

4
Low WS of random features

We start by showing that the word sensitivity for the RF map in (
3.1
) is
low
, i.e.,

\mathcal{S}_{{\textup{RF}}}=o(1)
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\varphi_{\textup{RF}}
be the random features map defined in (
3.1
), where
\phi
is Lipschitz and not identically
0
. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a generic input sample s.t. Assumption
1
holds, and assume
k=\Omega(D)
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(X)
denote the word sensitivity of
\varphi_{\textup{RF}}
defined in (
3.4
). Then, we have

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(X)=\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right)=o(1),\vskip 5.0pt

(4.1)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cD)
over
V
.

Theorem
1
shows that the RF model has a low word sensitivity, vanishing with the length of the context
n
. This means that, regardless of how any word is modified, the RF mapping is
not sensitive
to this modification, when the length of the context is large. The proof follows from an upper bound on the numerator of (
3.4
), due to the Lipschitz continuity of
\phi
, and a concentration result on the norm at the denominator. The details are deferred to Appendix
B
.

Remark 4.1
.

Theorem
1
is readily extended to the case where the number of words
m
that can be modified in the context is
o(n)
. In fact, to achieve a sensitivity of constant order, a constant fraction of rows in
X
must be changed, i.e.
m=\Theta(n)
. This extension of Theorem
1
is also proved in Appendix
B
, and it further illustrates that a fully connected architecture is unable to capture the change of few (namely,
m=o(n)
) semantically relevant words in a long sentence.

We numerically validate this result in Figure
2
(left), where we estimate the value of
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}
for different token embedding dimensions
d
, as the context length
n
increases. Clearly,
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}
decreases as the context length increases, regardless of the embedding dimension
d
. In fact, the curves corresponding to different values of
d
(in different colors) basically coincide.

Deep Random Features (DRF).

As an extension of Theorem
1
, we show that the
word sensitivity is low
also for
deep
random features. We consider the DRF map

\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}(X):=\phi(V_{L}\phi(V_{L-1}(...V_{2}\phi(V_{1}\Flat(X))...))),

(4.2)

where
\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the component-wise non-linearity,
k
is the number of neurons at each layer, and
V_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
are the random weights at layer
l
, with
[V_{1}]_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta/D)
and
[V_{l}]_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta/k)
for
l>1
.
We set
\beta
according to He’s initialization
[
33
]
, which ensures

\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta)}\left[\phi^{2}(\rho)\right]=1,

(4.3)

as done in
[
31
]
to avoid the problem of vanishing/exploding gradients in the analysis of a deep network.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}
be the deep random feature map defined in (
4.2
), where
\phi
is Lipschitz and
\beta
is chosen s.t. (
4.3
) holds. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a generic input sample s.t. Assumption
1
holds, and assume
k=\Theta(D)
,
L=o(\log k)
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{DRF}}(X)
denote the word sensitivity of
\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}
defined in (
3.4
). Then, we have

\mathcal{S}_{\textup{DRF}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{e^{CL}}{\sqrt{n}}\right),\vskip 6.99997pt

(4.4)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}d)
over
\{V_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.

Theorem
2
shows that, as in the shallow case, the word sensitivity decreases with the length of the context
n
. The proof requires showing that
\left\|\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}(X)\right\|_{2}
concentrates to
\sqrt{k}
, which is achieved via (
4.3
). The strategy to bound the term
\left\|\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}(X)\right\|_{2}
is similar to that of the shallow case. The
details are deferred to Appendix
C
.

The exponential dependence on
L
comes from our worst-case analysis of the Lipschitz constant of the model, and it does not fully exploit the independence between the

’s. Thus, we expect the actual dependence of the WS on

to be milder. This is confirmed by the numerical results of Figure

(right), where we numerically estimate
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{DRF}}
for different depths

as the context length

increases. For all values of

, the word sensitivity quickly decreases with

.

5
High WS of random attention features

In contrast with random features, we show that the
word sensitivity is high
for the RAF map in (
3.3
), i.e.,
\mathcal{S}_{{\textup{RAF}}}=\Omega(1)
.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}
be the random attention features map defined in (
3.3
). Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a generic input sample s.t. Assumption
1
holds, and assume
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
. Let
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RAF}}(X)
denote the word sensitivity of
\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}
defined in (
3.4
). Then, we have

\mathcal{S}_{{\textup{RAF}}}(X)=\Omega(1),\vskip 6.00006pt

(5.1)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}d)
over
W
.

Theorem
3
shows that the RAF model has a high word sensitivity, regardless of the length of the context
n
, as long as
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
. This requires a number of tokens
n
that grows slower than the embedding dimension
d
. Models such as BERT-Base or BERT-Large have an embedding dimension of 768 and 1024
[
22
]
, which allows our results to hold for fairly large context lengths.
In fact, we prove a stronger statement: for
any
index
i\in[n]
, there exists a perturbation
\Delta^{*}
(possibly dependent on
i
) s.t. the RAF map changes significantly when evaluated in
X^{i}(\Delta^{*})
. The proof of Theorem
3
is deferred to Appendix
D
, and a sketch follows.

Proof sketch.

The argument is not constructive, and the difficulty in finding a closed-form solution for the perturbation
\Delta^{*}
is due to the lack of assumptions on the sample
X
, which is entirely generic. We follow the steps below.

Step 1
: Find a direction
\delta^{*}
aligned with many words
x_{j}
’s.
Using the probabilistic method, we prove the existence of a vector
\delta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\left\|\delta^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
, s.t. its inner product with the tokens embeddings
x_{j}
’s is large for a constant fraction of the words in the context. In particular, Lemma
D.1
shows that
\left(x_{j}^{\top}\delta^{*}\right)^{2}=\Omega(d^{2}/n)
for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
j\in[n]
.

Step 2
: Exhibit two directions
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
both aligned with many words in the feature space
\{W^{\top}x_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.
Exploiting the properties of the Gaussian attention features
W
, we deduce the existence of two vectors
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
that
(i)
are far from each other, and
(ii)
ensure a constant fraction of the entries of
XW\Delta^{*}_{k}/\sqrt{d}
,
k\in\{1,2\}
, to be large. In particular, by exploiting our assumption
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
, Lemmas
D.2
and
D.3
show that
[XW\Delta^{*}_{k}/\sqrt{d}]_{j}=\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)
for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
j\in[n]
, with
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
.

Step 3
: Show that the attention concentrates towards the perturbed word.
Recall that
s(X):=\softmax(XWX^{\top}/\sqrt{d})
denotes the attention scores matrix. Then, Lemma
D.4
proves that the attention scores
[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{k}))]_{j:}^{\top}
are well approximated by the canonical basis vector
e_{i}
, for
k\in\{1,2\}
and an
\Omega(n)
number of rows
j\in[n]
. This intuitively means that a constant fraction of tokens
x_{j}

moves all their attention
towards the
i
-th modified token
x_{i}+\Delta^{*}_{k}
. This step critically exploits the
\softmax
function in the RAF map: if one entry in its argument is
\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right)
, then the attention scores concentrate as described above.

Step 4
: Conclude with at least one perturbation between
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
.
Finally, exploiting the fact that
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
, Lemma
D.5
proves that at least one of them gives a
\Delta^{*}
s.t.
\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)-\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}))\right\|_{F}=\Omega(\sqrt{dn})
. This, together with the upper bound
\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)\right\|_{F}=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{dn})
, concludes the argument. ∎

6
Generalization on context modification

The study of the word sensitivity is motivated by understanding the capabilities of a model to learn to distinguish two contexts
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
that only differ by a word. In fact, in practice, modifying a single row/word of
X
can lead to a significant change in the meaning, see Figure
1
. We formalize the problem in a supervised learning setting, and characterize whether a
generalized linear model
(GLM) induced by a feature map
\varphi
generalizes over a sample
(X^{i}(\Delta),y_{\Delta})
, after being trained on
(X,y)
. Crucially, the index
i\in[n]
and the perturbation
\Delta
are s.t. the label
y_{\Delta}
is different from
y
(e.g.,
y\in\{-1,+1\}
and
y_{\Delta}=-y
), namely, perturbing the
i
-th word changes the meaning of the context.

Supervised learning with generalized linear models (GLMs).

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
be a labelled training dataset, where
\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
contains the training data
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathcal{Y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}]^{\top}\in\{-1,1\}^{N}
the corresponding binary labels. The sample
(X,y)
does not belong to
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
, and it is introduced later in the training set. Let
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a feature map, and consider the GLM

f_{\varphi}(\cdot,\theta)=\varphi(\cdot)^{\top}\theta,\vskip 5.0pt

(6.1)

where
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are trainable parameters of the model. We define the feature matrix as
\Phi_{\varphi}:=[\varphi(X_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(X_{N})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
, and focus on the quadratic loss

\mathcal{L}(\theta):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(\varphi(X_{j})^{\top}\theta-y_{j}\right)^{2}.\vskip 5.0pt

(6.2)

Minimizing (
6.2
) with gradient descent gives (see equation (33) in
[
6
]
)

\theta^{*}=\theta_{0}+\Phi_{\varphi}^{+}(\mathcal{Y}-\Phi_{\varphi}\theta_{0}),\vskip 5.0pt

(6.3)

where
\theta^{*}
is the gradient descent solution,
\theta_{0}
is the initialization, and
\Phi^{+}_{\varphi}
is the Moore-Penrose inverse of
\Phi_{\varphi}
.

Our goal is to establish whether the additional training on the sample
(X,y)
allows the model
f_{\varphi}
to generalize on
(X^{i}(\Delta),y_{\Delta})
, with
y_{\Delta}=-y
. Thus, we do
not
focus on the case where
f_{\varphi}(X,\theta^{*})=y
and
f_{\varphi}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})=y_{\Delta}
, i.e.,
f_{\varphi}(\cdot,\theta^{*})
already generalizes well on the two new samples.
Instead, we look at how
f_{\varphi}(\cdot,\theta^{*})

extrapolates
the information contained in the pair
(X,y)
to the perturbed sample
X^{i}(\Delta)
. This motivates the following assumption.

Assumption 2
.

There exists a parameter
\gamma\in[0,2)
s.t.

\left|f_{\varphi}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})-f_{\varphi}(X,\theta^{*})\right|\leq\gamma.\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.4)

The parameter
\gamma
captures the degree over which the trained model
f_{\varphi}(\cdot,\theta^{*})
can distinguish between
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
. If
\gamma=0
,
f_{\varphi}(\cdot,\theta^{*})
does not recognize any difference between
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
; instead, if
f_{\varphi}(\cdot,\theta^{*})
correctly classifies the two samples
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
(i.e.,
f_{\varphi}(X,\theta^{*})=y
and
f_{\varphi}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})=y_{\Delta}=-y
), then
\gamma=2
, which is beyond the scope of our analysis.

We consider training on
(X,y)
in the following two ways.

(a) Fine-tuning.
First, we look at the model obtained after
fine-tuning
the solution
\theta^{*}
defined in (
6.3
) over the new sample
(X,y)
. This means that
\theta^{*}
is the initialization of a gradient descent algorithm trained on this single sample. As
\left(\varphi(X)^{\top}\right)^{+}=\varphi(X)/\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}
, the fine-tuned solution is given by

\theta^{*}_{f}=\theta^{*}+\frac{\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left(y-\varphi(X)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right).\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.5)

(b) Re-training.
Second, we re-train the model from scratch, after adding the pair
(X,y)
to the training set. The new training set is denoted by
(\mathcal{X}_{r},\mathcal{Y}_{r})
, where
\mathcal{X}_{r}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N},X)
contains the training data and
\mathcal{Y}_{r}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{N},y]
the binary labels. Thus, denoting by
\Phi_{\varphi,r}:=[\varphi(X_{1}),\ldots,\varphi(X_{N}),\varphi(X)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times p}
the new feature matrix, the re-trained solution
\theta^{*}_{r}
takes the form

\theta^{*}_{r}=\theta_{0}+\Phi_{\varphi,r}^{+}(\mathcal{Y}_{r}-\Phi_{\varphi,r}\theta_{0}).\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.6)

The quantity of interest is the
test error
on
(X^{i}(\Delta),y_{\Delta})
:

\textup{Err}_{\varphi}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta):=\left(f_{\varphi}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta)-y_{\Delta}\right)^{2},\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.7)

where
\theta\in\{\theta^{*}_{f},\theta^{*}_{r}\}
is the vector of parameters obtained either after fine-tuning or re-training.

6.1
Random features do not generalize

By exploiting the low word sensitivity of random features, we show that both the fine-tuned and retrained solutions generalize poorly.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\varphi_{\textup{RF}}
be the random features map defined in (
3.1
), with
\phi
Lipschitz and not identically
0
, and let
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{f})=\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot)^{\top}\theta^{*}_{f}
be the corresponding model fine-tuned on the sample
(X,y)
, where
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
satisfies Assumption
1
and
\theta^{*}_{f}
is given by (
6.5
). Assume
k=\Omega(D)
,
|f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*})|=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
, and that Assumption
2
holds with
\gamma\in[0,2)
. Let
\textup{Err}_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})
be the test error of
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{f})
on
(X^{i}(\Delta),y_{\Delta})
as defined in (
6.7
). Then, for any
\Delta
s.t.
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
and any
i\in[n]
, we have

\textup{Err}_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})>(2-\gamma)^{2}-\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right),\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.8)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cD)
over
V
.

Proof sketch.

The idea is to use (
6.5
) to obtain that

f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})=\frac{\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left(y-f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*})\right).

(6.9)

Next, we note that

\left|\frac{\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}-1\right|\leq\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(X).\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.10)

Theorem
1
gives that the RHS of (
6.10
) is small, which combined with (
6.9
) implies that
f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})
is close to

y+f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*}).\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.11)

By upper bounding
f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*})
via Assumption
2
, we obtain that (
6.11
) cannot be far from
y
. This implies that
f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})
cannot be close to the correct label
y_{\Delta}=-y
.
The complete proof is in Appendix
B
. ∎

Theorem 5
.

Let
\varphi_{\textup{RF}}
be the random features map defined in (
3.1
), with
\phi
Lipschitz and non-linear, and let
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{r})=\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot)^{\top}\theta^{*}_{r}
be the corresponding model re-trained on the dataset
(\mathcal{X}_{r},\mathcal{Y}_{r})
that contains the pair
(X,y)
, thus with

\theta^{*}_{r}
defined in (
6.6
).
Assume the training data to be sampled i.i.d. from a distribution
\mathcal{P}_{X}
s.t.
\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\mathcal{P}_{X}}[X]=0
, Assumption
1
holds, and the Lipschitz concentration property is satisfied. Let
N\log^{3}N=o(k)
,
N\log^{4}N=o(D^{2})
and
k=\Omega(D)
. Assume that
|f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*})|=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
and that Assumption
2
holds with
\gamma\in[0,2)
. Let
\textup{Err}_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})
be the test error of
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{r})
on
(X^{i}(\Delta),y_{\Delta})
defined in (
6.7
). Then, for any
\Delta
s.t.
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
and any
i\in[n]
, we have

\textup{Err}_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})>(2-\gamma)^{2}-\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right),\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.12)

with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-c\log^{2}N\right)
over
V,\mathcal{X}_{r}
.

Proof sketch.

The idea is to leverage the stability analysis in
[
14
]
, which gives

f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})=\mathcal{F}_{\textup{RF}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta))\left(f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*}_{r})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*})\right),\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.13)

where

\mathcal{F}_{{\textup{RF}}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta)):=\frac{\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}P_{\Phi}^{\perp}\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)}{\left\|P_{\Phi}^{\perp}\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.14)

is the
feature alignment
between
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
induced by
\varphi_{\textup{RF}}
and
P_{\Phi}
the projector over
\textrm{Span}\{\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X_{1}),\ldots,\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X_{N})\}
. After some manipulations, we have

\left|\mathcal{F}_{\textup{RF}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta))-1\right|\leq\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(X)\frac{\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right\|_{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K_{{\textup{RF}},r}\right)}},\vskip 3.00003pt

(6.15)

where
K_{\textup{RF},r}:=\Phi_{\textup{RF},r}\Phi^{\top}_{\textup{RF},r}
is the kernel of the model. A lower bound on its smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{\rm min}\left(K_{{\textup{RF}},r}\right)
follows from the fact that the kernel is well-conditioned (see Lemma
B.2
), which crucially relies on the assumptions on the data (i.i.d. and Lipschitz concentrated) and the scalings
N\log^{3}N=o(k)
,
N\log^{4}N=o(D^{2})
. As the word sensitivity
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{RF}}(X)
is upper bounded by Theorem
1
, from (
6.15
) we conclude that
\mathcal{F}_{\textup{RF}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta))
is close to
1
.

Since
K_{\textup{RF},r}
is invertible, the re-trained model
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{r})
interpolates the dataset
(\mathcal{X}_{r},\mathcal{Y}_{r})
, giving that
f(X,\theta^{*}_{r})=y
. Thus, as
\mathcal{F}_{\textup{RF}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta))\approx 1
,
f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})
is close to (
6.11
), and we conclude from the same argument used for Theorem
4
. The complete proof is in Appendix
B
. ∎

In a nutshell, by exploiting the low word sensitivity of random features, Theorems
4
-
5
show that, after either fine-tuning or re-training, the model does not learn to “separate” the predictions on the samples
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
. As a consequence, the test error is lower bounded by
(2-\gamma)^{2}
. In fact,
\gamma
is the distance between the predictions on
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
before fine-tuning/re-training (see (
6.4
)), and the ground-truth labels have distance
2
(
y_{\Delta},y\in\{-1,1\}
and
y_{\Delta}=-y
).

While Theorem
4
does not require distributional assumptions on the data, Theorem
5
considers i.i.d. training data, satisfying Lipschitz concentration. This property corresponds to having well-behaved tails, and it is common in the related theoretical literature
[
17
,
48
,
12
]
, see Appendix
A
for the formal definition and a discussion.

We remark that Assumption
2
requires the model
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*})
to give a similar output when evaluated on the two new samples
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
. Thus, we are asking if the model generalizes on
X^{i}(\Delta)

only
from the additional training on
X
. Now, one could design an adversarial
\Delta
s.t.
f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})
and
f(X,\theta^{*}_{r})
are different from each other (so that
f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})=y_{\Delta}
while
f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*}_{r})=y
), by exploiting the adversarial vulnerability of random features
[
24
,
23
,
13
]
. However, if we restrict the possible
\Delta
’s to those that satisfy Assumption
2
, Theorems
4
and
5
prove that such adversarial patch cannot be found. We finally note that, when the context length
n
is comparable or larger than the number of training samples
N
, the model becomes adversarially robust to any token modification and Assumption
2
automatically holds, see Appendix
E
for details.

6.2
Random attention features can generalize

Next, the behavior of random features is contrasted with that of random attention features. Let us consider the RAF model
f_{\textup{RAF}}(\cdot,\theta):=\Flat(\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(\cdot))^{\top}\theta
, where
\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(\cdot)
is defined in (
3.3
). Theorem
3
proves that the word sensitivity of
\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(\cdot)
is large. This suggests that the RAF model is capable of extrapolating the information contained in
(X,y)
to correctly classify the perturbed sample
X^{i}(\Delta)
. While proving a rigorous statement on a fine-tuned/re-trained RAF model remains challenging, we provide experimental evidence of this generalization capability.

Figure

(first row) shows that, after fine-tuning on
(X,y)
,

can be close to the perturbed label

, even if the model before fine-tuning was unable to distinguish between
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
. Specifically, the two central sub-plots consider the RAF model for two values of the context length

, even when the parameter
\gamma
in (

) is close to
0
, i.e.,
X
and

were indistinguishable before fine-tuning; in general, the test error
\textup{Err}_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})
is often smaller than the lower bound of

(dashed black line), which holds for random features.

The left sub-plot considers the RF model for

In a nutshell, our results show that, when a RAF model is not able to distinguish two points with opposite labels (that only differ in one word), fine-tuning or retraining on one of these points allows the loss on also the other point to decrease. In contrast, this is not the case for the RF model, where the loss on the second point can be lower bounded according to Theorems

and

6.3
Experimental details

The experiments of Figure
4
are performed on multiple independent trials, for different choices of the training data. We report in cross markers the results obtained by choosing
\Delta
after optimizing the test error in (
6.7
) via gradient descent. While this approach directly minimizes the metric of interest, it results in low test error only when
\gamma
is rather large, regardless of the model taken into account (RF, RAF, or ReLU-RAF). In contrast, optimizing a different loss controls the value of
\gamma
, while still achieving small error for RAF (and, to a smaller extent, ReLU-RAF). We report in circular markers the results obtained by minimizing the following two losses (respectively, for fine-tuning and re-training):

\ell_{\theta^{*}_{f}}(\Delta):=\left(\frac{\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}+1\right)^{2},\qquad\ell_{\theta^{*}_{r}}(\Delta):=\left(\mathcal{F}_{{\textup{RAF}}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta))+1\right)^{2}.

(6.16)

This choice is suggested by (

) and (
6.13
) which, after assuming for simplicity that
f_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})=f_{\textup{RAF}}(X,\theta^{*})=0
, can be re-written as

f_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})=\frac{\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\,y,\qquad f_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})=\mathcal{F}_{{\textup{RAF}}}(X,X^{i}(\Delta))\,y.

(6.17)

Achieving small error means that the LHS of (
6.17
) is close to
-y
, which corresponds to making the losses in (
6.16
) small. Further numerical results and comparisons between different optimization algorithms for finding
\Delta
are in Appendix

.

7
Conclusions

This work provides a formal characterization of the fundamental difference between fully connected and attention layers. To do so, we consider the prototypical setting of random features and study the
word sensitivity
, which captures how the output of a map changes after perturbing a single row/word of the input. On the one hand, the sensitivity of standard random features decreases with the context length and, in order to obtain to a significant change in the output of the map, a constant fraction of the words needs to be perturbed. On the other hand, the sensitivity of random attention features is large, regardless of the context length, thus indicating the suitability of attention layers for NLP tasks. These bounds on the word sensitivity translate into formal negative generalization results for random features, which are contrasted by positive empirical evidence of generalization for the attention layer.

Our analysis allows the
perturbations to be any (bounded) vector in the embedding space. Taking the tokenization process (and, hence, the discrete nature of the textual samples) into account offers an exciting avenue for future work.

A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, we denote by
[A]_{i:}
its
i
-th row, by
[A]_{:j}
its
j
-th column, and by
[A]_{ij}
its entry at position
(i,j)
.

We say that a random variable or vector respects the Lipschitz concentration property if there exists an absolute constant
c>0
such that, for every Lipschitz continuous function
\varphi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mathbb{E}|\varphi(X)|<+\infty
and for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\varphi(x)-\mathbb{E}_{X}[\varphi(x)]\right|>t\right)\leq 2e^{-ct^{2}/\left\|\varphi\right\|_{\mathrm{Lip}}^{2}}.

(A.1)

The family of Lipschitz concentrated distributions covers a number of important cases, e.g., standard Gaussian
[
65
]
, uniform on the sphere and on the unit (binary or continuous) hypercube
[
65
]
, or data obtained via a Generative Adversarial Network (GAN)
[
56
]
.

Appendix B

In this section, we provide the proofs for our results on the random features model. Thus, we will drop the sub-script “RF” in all the quantities of this section, for the sake of a cleaner notation. We consider a single textual data-point
X=[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}

\varphi(X)=\phi(V\Flat(X)),

(B.1)

where
V_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/D)
,
D=nd
and
\phi
is the activation function, applied component-wise to the pre-activations
V\Flat(X)
.

Further in the section, we will investigate the generalization capabilities of the RF model

on token modification. We use the notation
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y})
to indicate the original labelled training dataset, with
\mathcal{X}=(X_{1},\ldots,X_{N})
,
X_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
\mathcal{Y}=[y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}]^{\top}\in\{-1,+1\}^{N}
. We will use the short-hand
\Phi:=[\varphi(X_{1}),...\varphi(X_{N})]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times p}
for the feature matrix and
K:=\Phi\Phi^{\top}
for the kernel. According to (
6.3
), minimizing the quadratic loss over this dataset returns the parameters

\theta^{*}=\theta_{0}+\Phi^{+}(\mathcal{Y}-\Phi\theta_{0}).

(B.2)

We consider the test error on the modified sample

))

(B.3)

We will investigate this quantity for both the fine-tuned and the re-trained model. In particular, the new solution obtained after fine-tuning over the sample

\theta^{*}_{f}=\theta^{*}+\frac{\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left(y-\varphi(X)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right),

\theta^{*}_{r}=\theta_{0}+\Phi_{r}^{+}(\mathcal{Y}_{r}-\Phi_{r}\theta_{0}),

(B.5)

where we denote by
\Phi_{r}:=[\varphi(X_{1}),...\varphi(X_{N}),\varphi(X)]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times p}

.

The outline of this section is the following:

1.

We use Lemma

to upper bound the numerator of the word sensitivity

defined in (

.

2.

We prove Theorem

, where we lower bound

for the fine-tuned solution
\theta^{*}_{f}
as a function of
\gamma
.

3.

We prove Theorem
5
, where we lower bound

for the retrained solution
\theta^{*}_{r}
as a function of
\gamma
. This result requires additional assumptions and analysis:

•

We report in our notation Lemma 4.1 from
[
14
]
, which defines the

between the two samples
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
.

•

In Lemma

•

In Lemma

Lemma B.1
.

Let
\varphi(X)
be the random feature map defined in (
3.1
), with
\phi
Lipschitz and
k=\Omega(D)
. Let
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
be such that
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
. Then, for every
i\in[n]
, we have

\left\|\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}}\right),

(B.6)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cD)
over
V
.

Proof.

Let’s condition on the event

\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{D+k}{D}}\right),

(B.7)

which happens with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}D)
over
V
, by Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
65
]
. Thus, for every

, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\phi(V\Flat{(X^{i}(\Delta))})-\phi(V\Flat{(X)})\right\|_{2}

(B.8)

\displaystyle\leq M\left\|V(\Flat{(X^{i}(\Delta))}-\Flat{(X)})\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq M\left\lVert V\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{D+k}{D}}\sqrt{d}

\displaystyle=C_{1}\sqrt{\frac{D+k}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{2}\sqrt{\frac{k}{n}},

where the first inequality comes from the Lipschitz continuity of
\phi
, and the last step is a consequence of
k=\Omega(D)
. ∎

Proof of Remark
4.1
.

The only difference with respect to the argument for Theorem

Theorem
4

Let
f(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{f})
be the RF model fine-tuned on the sample
(X,y)
, where
\phi
in (
3.1
) is Lipschitz and not identically 0,
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
is a generic sample s.t. Assumption

\theta^{*}_{f}
is defined in (

). Assume

,
|f(X,\theta^{*})|=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)

2
holds with
\gamma\in[0,2)

\textup{Err}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})

(B.14)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-cD)
over
V
.

Proof.

We have

f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})=\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\theta^{*}_{f}=\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\theta^{*}+\frac{\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left(y-\varphi(X)^{\top}\theta^{*}\right),

(B.15)

where the second step is justified by (

As
\left|\varphi(X)^{\top}\theta^{*}-\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\theta^{*}\right|=\left|f(X,\theta^{*})-f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})\right|\leq\gamma
by Assumption
2

\displaystyle\left|f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})-y\right|

\displaystyle=\left|f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*})-f(X,\theta^{*})+\left(\frac{\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}-1\right)\left(y-f(X,\theta^{*})\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\gamma+\left|\frac{\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}-1\right|\left|y-f(X,\theta^{*})\right|.

By Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we have

\left|\frac{\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))^{\top}\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}-1\right|=\left|\frac{\left(\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi(X)\right)^{\top}\varphi(X)}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\right|\leq\frac{\left\|\varphi(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}}{\left\|\varphi(X)\right\|_{2}^{2}}\leq\mathcal{S}(X).

\mathcal{S}(X)=\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\left|y_{\Delta}-y\right|-\gamma-\mathcal{S}(X)\left|y-f(X,\theta^{*})\right|

\displaystyle=2-\gamma-S(X)\left|y-f(X,\theta^{*})\right|

\displaystyle=2-\gamma-\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right),

where the third step is a consequence of

, and the fourth step comes from

\textup{Err}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})=\left(f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{f})-y_{\Delta}\right)^{2}\geq\left(2-\gamma-\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right)\right)^{2}>(2-\gamma)^{2}-\mathcal{O}\left(1/\sqrt{n}\right).

(B.19)

∎

In this section, we provide the proofs for our results on the deep random features model. We consider a single textual data-point
X=[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
that satisfies Assumption
1
. We consider the deep random features model defined in (

\varphi_{\textup{DRF}}(X):=\phi(V_{L}\phi(V_{L-1}(...V_{2}\phi(V_{1}\Flat X)...))),

\phi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is the non-linearity applied component-wise at each layer, and
V_{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times D}
are the random weights at layer

[V_{1}]_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,\beta/D)
and

]

\mathbb{E}_{\rho\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\beta)}\left[\phi^{2}(\rho)\right]=1.

(C.2)

Thus, we require the activation function
\phi
to guarantee at least one value
\beta
for which the previous equation is respected. We will consider
\beta
to be a positive constant dependent only on the activation
\phi
.

Let’s introduce the shorthands

\displaystyle\varphi_{0}(X)

\displaystyle=\Flat(X),

(C.3)

\displaystyle\varphi_{l}(X)

\displaystyle=\phi\left(V_{l}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right),\qquad\textup{for }l\in[L].

The outline of this section is the following:

1.

In Lemma
C.1
, we prove that at every layer, the norm of the features
\left\|\varphi_{l}(X)\right\|_{2}
, with
l>1
, concentrates to
\sqrt{k}
. This is the step where He’s initialization is necessary.

2.

In Lemma
C.2
, we show that
\left\|\varphi_{l}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{l}(X)\right\|_{2}
can be upper bounded by a term that grows exponentially with the depth of the layer
l
.

3.

In Theorem
3
, we upper bound
\mathcal{S}_{\textup{DRF}}
, concluding the argument.

Lemma C.1
.

Let
\varphi_{l}(X)
be defined in (
C.3
), and let
\phi
be a Lipschitz function such that (
4.3
) admits at least one solution
\beta
. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
be a generic input sample such that Assumption
1
holds, and let

. Then, for every
l>0
, we have

\left|\left\|\varphi_{l}(X)\right\|_{2}-\sqrt{k}\right|\leq e^{Cl}\log D,

(C.4)

with probability at least
1-2L\exp(-c\log^{2}D)
over
\{V_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.

Proof.

By Assumption
1
the statement trivially holds for
l=0
, if we replace
\sqrt{k}
with
\sqrt{d}
. Let’s consider this the base case of an induction argument to prove the statement for
l\in[L]
.

Thus, using the notation
k_{0}=D
and
k_{l}=k
for
l\in[L]
, the inductive hypothesis becomes

\left|\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}-\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\right|\leq e^{C(l-1)}\log D,

(C.5)

with probability at least
1-2(l-1)\exp(-c\log^{2}D)
on
\{V_{m}\}_{m=1}^{l-1}
. Let’s condition on this high probability event and on
\left\lVert V_{l}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq C_{1}
until the end of the proof. By Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
65
]
and since
k=\Theta(D)
, there exists
C_{1}
large enough and independent from
l
, such that this holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}k)
over
V_{l}
. We therefore aim to prove the thesis for

e^{C}:=\mathop{\rm max}\nolimits\left(1,MC_{1}+1\right),

(C.6)

where we denote by
M
the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
. (
C.6
) explicitely shows that
C
is a natural constant dependent only on the activation function
\phi
.

We have

\varphi_{l}(X)=\phi(V_{l}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X))=\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}+V_{l}\left(\varphi_{l-1}(X)-\frac{\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right),

(C.7)

which gives

\left\|\varphi_{l}(X)-\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq MC_{1}\left|\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}-\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\right|\leq MC_{1}e^{C(l-1)}\log D,

(C.8)

where the first step is true since
\phi
is
M
-Lipschitz, and the last step is a direct consequence of the inductive hypothesis (
C.5
).

Let’s now consider the second term in the left hand side of (
C.8
). Let’s define the shorthand
\rho=V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. In the probability space of
V_{l}
,
\rho
is distributed as a Gaussian random vector, such that all its entries
\rho_{i}
are i.i.d. Gaussian with variance
\beta
. Thus, we have

\mathbb{E}_{V_{l}}\left[\left\|\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k_{l-1}}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}\left[\left\|\phi\left(\rho\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}\right]=k_{l}\mathbb{E}_{\rho_{1}}\left[\phi^{2}\left(\rho_{1}\right)\right]=k,

(C.9)

where the last step is a consequence of (
C.2
), and of
k_{l}=k
for
l\in[L]
.

As the
\rho_{i}
’s are independent and
\phi
is Lipschitz, the random variables
\left(\phi^{2}\left(\rho_{i}\right)-1\right)
are independent, mean-0, and sub-exponential, such that
\left\|\phi^{2}\left(\rho_{i}\right)-1\right\|_{\psi_{1}}\leq C_{2}
. Thus, by Bernstein inequality (cf. Theorem 2.8.1. in
[
65
]
), we have

\mathbb{P}_{\rho}\left(\left|\sum_{i=1}^{k}\left(\phi^{2}\left(\rho_{i}\right)-1\right)\right|\geq\sqrt{k}\log k\right)\leq 2\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k),

(C.10)

which gives

\left|\left\|\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}-k\right|\leq\sqrt{k}\log k,

(C.11)

with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k)
over
V_{l}
. We will condition on such high probability event until the end of the proof.

Thus, we have

\left\|\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{k+\sqrt{k}\log k}=\sqrt{k}\sqrt{1+\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{k}}}\leq\sqrt{k}\left(1+\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\sqrt{k}+\log k,

(C.12)

and

\left\|\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}\geq\sqrt{k-\sqrt{k}\log k}=\sqrt{k}\sqrt{1-\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{k}}}\geq\sqrt{k}\left(1-\frac{\log k}{\sqrt{k}}\right)=\sqrt{k}-\log k.

(C.13)

Putting together the last two equations gives

\left|\left\|\phi\left(V_{l}\frac{\sqrt{k}\,\varphi_{l-1}(X)}{\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}}\right)\right\|_{2}-\sqrt{k}\right|\leq\log k.

(C.14)

Applying (
C.8
), (
C.14
) and the triangle inequality gives

\left|\left\|\varphi_{l}\left(X\right)\right\|_{2}-\sqrt{k}\right|\leq MC_{1}e^{C(l-1)}\log k+\log k\leq\left(MC_{1}e^{C(l-1)}+e^{C(l-1)}\right)\log k\leq e^{Cl}\log k,

(C.15)

where the second and the third step are both consequences of (
C.6
). This inequality, performing a union bound on the high probability events we considered so far, holds with probability at least
1-2(l-1)\exp(-c\log^{2}k)-2\exp(-c_{1}k)-2\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}k)\geq 1-2l\exp(-c\log^{2}k)
over
\{V_{m}\}_{m=1}^{l}
, as soon as we consider
c=\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits(c_{1},c_{2})
.
∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let
\varphi_{l}(X)
be defined in (
C.3
), let
\phi
be a Lipschitz function and
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
a generic input sample such that Assumption
1
holds. Assume
k=\Theta(D)
. Then, for every
l\geq 0
, for every
i\in[n]
and for any
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
, we have

\left\|\varphi_{l}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{l}(X)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}e^{Cl},

(C.16)

with probability at least
1-2L\exp(-ck)
over
\{V_{l}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.

Proof.

Let’s prove the statement by induction over
l
. The base case
l=0
is a direct consequence of
\left\|\Flat(X^{i}(\Delta))-\Flat(X)\right\|_{2}=\left\|\Delta\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
, which makes the thesis true for any
C\geq 0
.

In the inductive step, the inductive hypothesis becomes

\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}e^{C(l-1)},

(C.17)

with probability at least
1-2(l-1)\exp(-ck)
on
\{V_{m}\}_{m=1}^{l-1}
. Let’s condition on this high probability event and on
\left\lVert V_{l}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq C_{1}
until the end of the proof. By Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
65
]
and since
k=\Theta(D)
, there exists
C_{1}
large enough and independent from
l
, such that this holds with probability at least
1-2\exp(-c_{1}k)
over
V_{l}
.

We aim to prove the thesis for
e^{C}=MC_{1}
, and
c=c_{1}
, where
M
is the Lipschitz constant of
\phi
. We have

\displaystyle\left\|\varphi_{l}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{l}(X)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\phi(V_{l}\varphi_{l-1}(X^{i}(\Delta)))-\phi(V_{l}\varphi_{l-1}(X))\right\|_{2}

(C.18)

\displaystyle\leq MC_{1}\left\|\varphi_{l-1}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{l-1}(X)\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leq MC_{1}\sqrt{d}e^{C(l-1)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{d}e^{Cl},

with probability at least
1-2(l-1)\exp(-ck)-2\exp(-c_{1}k)=1-2l\exp(-ck)
over
\{V_{m}\}_{m=1}^{l}
, which gives the thesis.
∎

Appendix D
Proofs for random attention features

In this section, we provide the proofs for our results on the random attention features model.

We consider a single textual data-point
X=[x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
that satisfies Assumptions
1
. We assume
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
. We consider the random attention features model defined in (
3.3
), i.e.,

\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)=\softmax\left(\frac{XWX^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}\right)X,

(D.1)

where
W_{i,j}\mathbin{\overset{}{\sim}}_{\rm i.i.d.}\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
sampled independently from
X
. We define the argument of the
\softmax
as
S(X)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
given by

S(X)=\frac{XWX^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}},

(D.2)

and its evaluation on the perturbed sample as

S(X^{i}(\Delta))=\frac{X^{i}(\Delta)WX^{i}(\Delta)^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}.

(D.3)

The attention scores are therefore defined as the row-wise
\softmax
of the previous term, i.e.,

[s(X)]_{j:}=\softmax\left([S(X)]_{j:}\right),

(D.4)

which allows us to rewrite (
3.3
) as

\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)=s(X)X.

(D.5)

Through this section, we will always consider this notation and assumptions, which will therefore not be repeated in the statement of the lemmas before our final result Theorem
3
.

The outline of this section is the following:

1.

In Lemma
D.1
, we prove the existence of a vector
\delta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that its inner product with the token embeddings
x_{i}
’s is large for a constant fraction of the words in the context.

2.

In Lemma
D.2
, we show that the result in the previous Lemma can be extended to two different vectors
\delta^{*}_{1}
and
\delta^{*}_{2}
, that are very different from each other, i.e.,
\left\|\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
. The reason why we would like this statement to hold on two vectors will be clear in Lemma
D.5
.

3.

In Lemma
D.3
, exploiting the properties of the Gaussian attention features
W
, we show that there exist two different vectors
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
, that are very different from each other, i.e.,
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
, such that a constant fraction of the entries of the vector
XW\Delta^{*}_{k}/\sqrt{d}
,
k\in\{1,2\}
, is large.

4.

In Lemma
D.4
, we prove that, for the two vectors
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
and an
\Omega(n)
number of rows
j\in[n]
, the attention scores
[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{k}))]_{j:}^{\top}

are well approximated by the canonical basis vector
e_{i}
for
k\in\{1,2\}
. This intuitively means that a constant fraction of tokens
x_{j}
puts all their attention towards the
i
-th modified token
x_{i}+\Delta^{*}_{k}
.

5.

In Lemma
D.5
, using an argument by contradiction, we prove that at least one between
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
is such that
\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)-\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}))\right\|_{F}=\Omega(\sqrt{dn})
.

6.

In Theorem
3
, we upper bound
\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)\right\|_{F}
, concluding the argument.

Lemma D.1
.

There exists a vector
\delta^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, such that

\left\|\delta^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},

(D.6)

and

\left(x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}\right)^{2}=\Omega\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\right),

(D.7)

for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

Let
X^{\top}=UDV^{\top}
the singular value decomposition of
X^{\top}
, where
U\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
D\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}
, and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and let
U_{r}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times r}
be the matrix obtained keeping only the first
r=\Rank(X)
columns of
U
.
Let
\delta_{\varepsilon}
be defined as follows

\delta_{\varepsilon}:=\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}U_{r}\varepsilon,

(D.8)

where we consider
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
uniformly distributed on the sphere
\sqrt{r}\,\mathbb{S}^{r-1}
of radius
\sqrt{r}
.
By definition we have
U_{r}^{\top}U_{r}=I
, which implies

\left\|U_{r}\varepsilon\right\|_{2}=\left\|\varepsilon\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{r}.

(D.9)

As
r\leq n
, we have that

\left\|\delta_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},

(D.10)

for every
\varepsilon
.
Let’s call
Z^{\varepsilon}_{i}
the random variable
x_{i}^{\top}\delta_{\varepsilon}
.

By contradiction, let’s assume the thesis to be false. Then, in particular, by (
D.10
), there is no
\varepsilon
such that
\delta_{\varepsilon}
respects the second part of the thesis. This implies that, for every
\varepsilon
, there are at least
\left\lceil n/2\right\rceil
values of
i
such that
\left(Z^{\varepsilon}_{i}\right)^{2}<0.01\,d^{2}/n
. Let’s define the indicators

\chi_{i}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\left(Z_{i}^{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}<0.01\,d^{2}/n,\\
0,&\text{if }\left(Z_{i}^{\varepsilon}\right)^{2}\geq 0.01\,d^{2}/n.\end{cases}

(D.11)

Thus, by contradiction, we have

\sum\chi_{i}\geq\left\lceil\frac{n}{2}\right\rceil\geq\frac{n}{2},

(D.12)

for every
\varepsilon
. Thus, by the probabilistic method,

\frac{n}{2}\leq\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\sum\chi_{i}\right]=\sum\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\chi_{i}\right]=n\,\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\left[\chi_{1}\right]=n\,\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left(Z^{\varepsilon}_{1}\right)^{2}\leq 0.01\,d^{2}/n\right),

(D.13)

where the second equality is true as the
Z^{\varepsilon}_{i}
are identically distributed, and the last step comes from the definition of the indicators in (
D.11
). This implies, for every
i
,

\mathbb{P}_{\varepsilon}\left(\left(Z^{\varepsilon}_{i}\right)^{2}\leq 0.01\,d^{2}/n\right)\geq 0.5.

(D.14)

Let
\rho\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
be a standard Gaussian vector and define
\rho_{r}=\left\|\rho\right\|_{2}/\sqrt{r}
. We have that
\mathbb{E}[\rho_{r}^{2}]=1
and
\textup{Var}(\rho_{r}^{2})=1/r
. Thus, for every
r
, by Chebyshev’s inequality, we can write

\mathbb{P}_{\rho_{r}}\left(\rho_{r}^{2}\leq 3\right)\geq 0.75\geq 0.5.

(D.15)

By rotational invariance of the Gaussian measure, we have that
\rho_{r}\varepsilon
is distributed as a standard Gaussian vector. In particular, we have

\rho_{r}Z_{i}^{\varepsilon}=\rho_{r}x_{i}^{\top}\delta_{\varepsilon}=\left(\sqrt{\frac{d}{n}}U_{r}^{\top}x_{i}\right)^{\top}\left(\rho_{r}\varepsilon\right).

(D.16)

As
U_{r}^{\top}x_{i}
is a fixed vector independent from
\varepsilon
and
\rho_{r}
, we have that
\rho_{r}Z_{i}^{\varepsilon}
is a Gaussian random variable (in the probability space of
\varepsilon
and
\rho_{r}
), with variance
d\left\|U_{r}^{\top}x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}/n=d^{2}/n
, as
x_{i}\in\textrm{Span}\{\Rows(U_{r}^{\top})\}
by construction.
Thus,
\sqrt{n}\rho_{r}Z^{\varepsilon}_{i}/d
is distributed as a standard Gaussian random variable
g_{i}
.

Therefore, putting together (
D.14
) and (
D.15
) gives

\mathbb{P}_{g_{i}}\left(g_{i}^{2}\leq 0.03\right)\geq 0.25.

(D.17)

However, we can upper-bound the left hand side of the previous equation exploiting the closed form of the pdf of a standard Gaussian random variable. This gives

\mathbb{P}_{g_{i}}\left(|g_{i}|\leq\sqrt{0.03}\right)<\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\cdot 2\cdot\sqrt{0.03}<0.14<0.25,

(D.18)

which leads to the desired contradiction.
∎

Lemma D.2
.

There exist two vectors
\delta^{*}_{1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\delta^{*}_{2}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\left\|\delta^{*}_{1}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|\delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d},

(D.19)

and
x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}_{1}>0
,
x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}_{2}>0
, and

x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}_{1}=\Omega\left(\sqrt{d}\log^{2}d\right),

(D.20)

x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}_{2}=\Omega\left(\sqrt{d}\log^{2}d\right),

(D.21)

for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

By Lemma
D.1
, we have that there exists a vector
\delta^{*}
, with
\left\|\delta^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
such that

\left(x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}\right)^{2}=\Omega\left(\frac{d^{2}}{n}\right),

(D.22)

for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
i\in[n]
. This also implies (up to considering
-\delta^{*}
instead of
\delta^{*}
) that

x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}=\Omega\left(\frac{d}{\sqrt{n}}\right)=\Omega\left(\sqrt{d}\log^{2}d\right),

(D.23)

where the last step is justified by
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
.

Let’s now define

\delta^{*}_{1}=\frac{\delta^{*}}{2}+\frac{v}{2},

(D.24)

and

\delta^{*}_{2}=\frac{\delta^{*}}{2}-\frac{v}{2},

(D.25)

where
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a generic fixed vector such that
\left\|v\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, and
\left\|Xv\right\|_{2}=0
, i.e.
v\in\textrm{Span}\{\Rows(X)\}^{\perp}
. This is again possible because of
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
. As
\left\|\delta^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
by Lemma
D.1
, the first part of the thesis follows from a straightforward application of the triangle inequality.

Since
v^{\top}x_{i}=0
for every
1\leq i\leq n
, we also have

x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}_{1}=x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}_{2}=x_{i}^{\top}\delta^{*}/2,

(D.26)

which readily gives the desired result.
∎

Lemma D.3
.

Let’s define
\sigma(\Delta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\sigma(\Delta):=\frac{XW\Delta}{\sqrt{d}},

(D.27)

where
\Delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}d)
over
W
, there exist
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
such that,

\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d}),

(D.28)

and

\sigma(\Delta^{*}_{1})_{i}=\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right).

(D.29)

\sigma(\Delta^{*}_{2})_{i}=\Omega\left(\log^{2}d\right).

(D.30)

for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
i\in[n]
.

Proof.

Let’s set

\Delta=CW^{\top}\delta,

(D.31)

where
\delta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is independent from
W
, and
C
is an absolute constant that will be fixed later in the proof. For every
i
, we can write

\delta=\frac{\delta^{\top}x_{i}}{\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}x_{i}+\delta_{i}^{\perp},

(D.32)

with
\delta_{i}^{\perp}
being orthogonal with
x_{i}
by construction. Let’s for now suppose
\left\|\delta_{i}^{\perp}\right\|_{2}\neq 0
. Let
a(\delta)_{i}:=\delta^{\top}x_{i}
.
We have

\displaystyle\sigma(\Delta)_{i}

\displaystyle\geq C\frac{a(\delta)_{i}}{\sqrt{d}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left\|W^{\top}x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-C\left|\frac{x_{i}^{\top}WW^{\top}\delta_{i}^{\perp}}{\sqrt{d}}\right|

(D.33)

\displaystyle=C\frac{a(\delta)_{i}}{\sqrt{d}\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}}\left\|W^{\top}x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}-C\frac{\left\|\delta_{i}^{\perp}\right\|_{2}}{d}\left|x_{i}^{\top}WW^{\top}\frac{\sqrt{d}\,\delta_{i}^{\perp}}{\left\|\delta_{i}^{\perp}\right\|_{2}}\right|.

In the probability space of
W
, as the entries of
W
are i.i.d. and Gaussian
\mathcal{N}(0,1/d)
, we have that
W^{\top}x_{i}
and
W^{\top}\frac{\sqrt{d}\,\delta_{i}^{\perp}}{\left\|\delta_{i}^{\perp}\right\|_{2}}
are two independent standard Gaussian vectors, namely,
\rho_{1}
and
\rho_{2}
. This implies

\left\|W^{\top}x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\Omega(d),\qquad\left|x_{i}^{\top}WW^{\top}\frac{\sqrt{d}\,\delta_{i}^{\perp}}{\left\|\delta_{i}^{\perp}\right\|_{2}}\right|=:\left|\rho_{1}^{\top}\rho_{2}\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{d}\log d\right),

(D.34)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log^{2}d)
, over the probability space of
W
. The first statement holds because of Theorem 3.1.1. of
[
65
]
, and the second one because, in the probability space of
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{1}^{\top}\rho_{2}
is a Gaussian random variable with variance
\left\|\rho_{2}\right\|_{2}^{2}
, which in turn is
\mathcal{O}\left(d\right)
with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}d)
over
\rho_{2}
. Using
\left\|x_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=d
, we get, for two positive absolute constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,

\sigma(\Delta)_{i}\geq C_{1}\frac{a(\delta)_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}-C_{2}\frac{\left\|\delta^{\perp}_{i}\right\|_{2}\log d}{\sqrt{d}}\geq C_{1}\frac{a(\delta)_{i}}{\sqrt{d}}-C_{2}\frac{\left\|\delta\right\|_{2}\log d}{\sqrt{d}}.

(D.35)

Notice that the previous equation would still hold even in the case
\left\|\delta_{i}^{\perp}\right\|_{2}=0
, which is therefore a case now included in our derivation.

By Lemma
D.2
, we can choose two vectors
\delta^{*}_{1}
and
\delta^{*}_{2}
, independently from
W
such that, for
k\in\{1,2\}
,
\left\|\delta^{*}_{k}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
and
a(\delta^{*}_{k})_{i}=\Omega(\sqrt{d}\log^{2}d)
for
\Omega(n)
indices
i\in[n]
. This gives, for another absolute positive constant

,

\sigma(\Delta^{*}_{1})_{i}\geq C_{3}\log^{2}d-C_{2}\log d=\Omega(\log^{2}d),

(D.36)

\sigma(\Delta^{*}_{2})_{i}\geq C_{3}\log^{2}d-C_{2}\log d=\Omega(\log^{2}d),

(D.37)

for
\Omega(n)
indices
i\in[n]
.

Let’s now verify that
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=C\left\|W^{\top}(\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2})\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
. As
\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2}
is independent on
W
, and
\left\|\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{d}
, we have that
W^{\top}(\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2})
is a standard Gaussian random vector, in the probability space of
W
. Thus, by Theorem 3.3.1 of
[
65
]
, we have

\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=C\left\|W^{\top}(\delta^{*}_{1}-\delta^{*}_{2})\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d}),

(D.38)

with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{3}d)
, on the probability space of
W
.

We are left to verify that, for
k\in\{1,2\}
,
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{k}\right\|_{2}=C\left\|W^{\top}\delta_{k}^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
. This is readily implied by Theorem 4.4.5 of
[
65
]
, which gives
\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\mathcal{O}\left(1\right)
with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{4}d)
. Taking the intersection between this high probability event and the ones in (
D.34
) and (
D.38
), and setting
C
small enough to have
C\left\lVert W\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\leq 1
, we get the thesis.
∎

Lemma D.4
.

For every
i\in[n]
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}d)
over
W
, there exist
\Delta^{*}_{1}
and
\Delta^{*}_{2}
such that,

\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d},\qquad\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d}),

(D.39)

and

\left\|[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{j:}^{\top}-e_{i}\right\|_{2}=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(D.40)

\left\|[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{2}))]_{j:}^{\top}-e_{i}\right\|_{2}=o\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\right),

(D.41)

for at least
\Omega(n)
indices
j\in[n]
, where

represents the
i
-th element of the canonical basis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

As we can write
X^{i}(\Delta)=X+e_{i}\Delta^{\top}
, we can rewrite (
D.3
) as

\sqrt{d}\,S(X^{i}(\Delta))=X^{i}(\Delta)WX^{i}(\Delta)^{\top}=XWX^{\top}+XW\Delta e_{i}^{\top}+e_{i}\Delta^{\top}WX+e_{i}\Delta^{\top}W\Delta e_{i}^{\top}.

(D.42)

Let’s look at the
j
-th row of
S(X^{i}(\Delta))
, with
j\neq i
. We have

[S(X^{i}(\Delta))]_{j:}=\frac{x_{j}^{\top}WX^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}+\frac{x_{j}^{\top}W\Delta e_{i}^{\top}}{\sqrt{d}}.

(D.43)

With probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{1}\log^{2}d)
over
W
, we have that all the
n
entries of the vector
x_{j}^{\top}WX^{\top}/\sqrt{d}
are smaller than
\log d
, as they are all standard Gaussian random variables. By Lemma
D.3
, we have that there exist
\Delta_{1}^{*}
and
\Delta_{2}^{*}
such that
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
,
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}
,
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=\Omega(\sqrt{d})
and

\frac{x_{j}^{\top}W\Delta^{*}_{k}}{\sqrt{d}}=\Omega(\log^{2}d),\qquad k\in\{1,2\},

(D.44)

for
\Omega(n)
indices
j\in[n]
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c_{2}\log^{2}d)
over
W
.
Let’s consider this set of indices until the end of the proof, where we have that, for
k\in\{1,2\}
,
[S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{k}))]_{ji}=\Omega(\log^{2}d)
.

After applying the
\softmax
function to the row in (
D.43
) as indicated in (
D.4
), we get, for every index
k\in[n]
and
k\neq i

\displaystyle{}_{jk}

\displaystyle=\softmax\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{j:}\right)_{k}

(D.45)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{jk}\right)}{\sum_{l}\exp\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{jl}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{\exp\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{ji}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{Cd^{\log d}}=o\left(\frac{1}{d}\right),

and

\displaystyle{}_{ji}

\displaystyle=\softmax\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{j:}\right)_{i}

(D.46)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\sum_{l\neq i}\exp\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{jl}\right)}{\sum_{l}\exp\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{jl}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{nd}{\exp\left([S(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{ji}\right)}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{nd}{Cd^{\log d}}=1-o\left(\frac{1}{d}\right),

where the last step is justified by the fact that
d/\log^{4}d=\Omega(n)
.

Thus, for the same reason, we have

\left\|[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{j:}^{\top}-e_{i}\right\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{k\neq i}[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{jk}^{2}+\left(1-[s(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))]_{ji}\right)^{2}=no\left(\frac{1}{d^{2}}\right)=o\left(\frac{1}{d}\right).

(D.47)

As (
D.47
) can be written also with respect to
\Delta^{*}_{2}
, the thesis readily follows.
∎

Lemma D.5
.

For every
i\in[n]
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-c\log^{2}d)
over
W
, there exist
\Delta^{*}
such that
\left\|\Delta^{*}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}

\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)-\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}))\right\|_{F}=\Omega(\sqrt{dn}).

(D.48)

Proof.

By (

), we have that,

\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))=s(X^{i}(\Delta))X^{i}(\Delta),

(D.49)

By Lemma
D.4
, we have that there exists
\Delta^{*}_{1}
, with
\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}\right\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}

\Omega(n)
indices

(D.50)

with probability at least

over
W
. Until the end of the proof, we will refer to

as a generic index for which the previous equation holds, and we will condition on the high probability event that the equations in the statement of Lemma

As we can write

. By Lemma

\Delta^{*}_{2}
such that

Then, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\Delta^{*}_{1}-\Delta^{*}_{2}\right\|_{2}=

\displaystyle\left\|\left(x_{i}+\Delta^{*}_{1}\right)-\left(x_{i}+\Delta^{*}_{2}\right)\right\|_{2}

(D.54)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\left(\left(x_{i}+\Delta^{*}_{1}\right)-\left[\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))\right]_{j:}\right)+\left(\left[\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{1}))\right]_{j:}-\left[\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)\right]_{j:}\right)

\displaystyle-\left(\left(x_{i}+\Delta^{*}_{2}\right)-\left[\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{2}))\right]_{j:}\right)-\left(\left[\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X^{i}(\Delta^{*}_{2}))\right]_{j:}-\left[\varphi_{\textup{RAF}}(X)\right]_{j:}\right)\|

Appendix E
Assumption
2
and adversarial robustness

Assumption
2
requires the perturbation
\Delta
to be such that the model
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*})
gives a similar output when evaluated on the two new samples
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
. This assumption is necessary to understand the different behaviour of the RF and the RAF model in our setting, as there in fact exists an adversarial patch
\Delta
such that, for example,
f(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})
and
f(X,\theta^{*}_{r})
are very different from each other (e.g.,
f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})=y_{\Delta}
while
f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*}_{r})=y
).

This conclusion derives from the adversarial vulnerability of the RF model, extensively studied in previous work
[
24
,
23
,
13
]
. We remark that this vulnerability depends on the scalings of the problem, i.e.,
n,d
and
N
. In fact, (using the re-trained solution as example) we can write (assuming
\theta_{0}=0
for simplicity)

\displaystyle\left|f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*}_{r})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\left(\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right)^{\top}\Phi_{\textup{RF},r}^{+}\mathcal{Y}_{r}\right|

(E.1)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right\|_{2}\left\lVert\Phi_{\textup{RF},r}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}\left\|Y_{r}\right\|_{2}.

If we bound the three terms on the RHS separately, we get:

•

\left\|\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta))-\varphi_{\textup{RF}}(X)\right\|_{2}=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{k/n}\right)
with probability at least
1-\exp(-cD)
over
V
, by Lemma
B.1
;

•

\left\lVert\Phi_{\textup{RF},r}^{+}\right\rVert_{\textup{op}}=\lambda_{\mathop{\rm min}\nolimits}^{-1/2}(K_{\textup{RF},r})=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{1/k}\right)
, with probability at least
1-\exp\left(-c\log^{2}N\right)
over
V
and
\mathcal{X}_{r}
, by Lemma
B.2
;

•

\left\|Y_{r}\right\|_{2}=\sqrt{N+1}
, as we are considering labels in
\{-1,1\}
.

Thus, we conclude that

\left|f_{\textup{RF}}(X^{i}(\Delta),\theta^{*}_{r})-f_{\textup{RF}}(X,\theta^{*}_{r})\right|=\mathcal{O}\left(\sqrt{\frac{N}{n}}\right),

(E.2)

with high probability. As a consequence, in the regime where
N=o(n)
,
f_{\textup{RF}}(\cdot,\theta^{*}_{r})
cannot distinguish between the samples
X
and
X^{i}(\Delta)
, without the additional need for Assumption
2
.
This result is also shown in our experiments, in the left subplots of Figure
4
, where the points approach smaller values of
\gamma
and consequently higher values of the error as
N
decreases, becoming comparable with
n
.
