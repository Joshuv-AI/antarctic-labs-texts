KORN AND POINCARÉ-KORN INEQUALITIES: A DIFFERENT PERSPECTIVE
Giovanni Di Fratta (1), Francesco Solombrino (1)

Abstract
. We present a concise point of view on the first and the second Korn’s inequality for general exponent p p and for a class of domains that includes Lipschitz domains. Our argument is conceptually very simple and, for p = 2 p=2 , uses only the classical Riesz representation theorem in Hilbert spaces. Moreover, the argument for the general exponent 1 < p < ∞ 1<p<\infty remains the same, the only change being invoking now the q q -Riesz representation theorem (with q q the harmonic conjugate of p p ). We also complement the analysis with elementary derivations of Poincaré-Korn inequalities in bounded and unbounded domains, which are essential tools in showing the coercivity of variational problems of elasticity but also propedeutic to the proof of the first Korn inequality. Mathematics Subject Classification. 35A23, 46N20, 46F10, 74B20. Key words. Korn inequality, Poincaré-Korn inequality, Riesz representation theorem, Weyl lemma.



1.
Introduction and motivation

Korn’s inequality is a fundamental result in mathematical analysis and a
cornerstone of linear elasticity theory. For an elastic body occupying a
regular region
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
, or more generally for a smooth bounded
domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
,
N\geqslant 1
, the inequality, in its more
classical form, states the existence, for every
1<p<\infty
, of a
positive constant
K_{\Omega,p}>0
, depending only on
\Omega
and
p
, such that

(1.1)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant K_{\Omega,p}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N}),

with
\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}
being the symmetric part of the Jacobian matrix

\nabla\boldsymbol{u}
,
|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|
and
|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|

the corresponding Frobenius norms (see sec.
2.1
).

In the language of the theory of elasticity, the symmetric part of the
gradient represents the strain experienced by an elastic body when subjected
to deformation. Inequality (
1.1
) allows controlling the

L^{p}
-norm of the gradient of the displacement field with the
L^{p}
-norm of
the
linearized
strain tensor. It is a fundamental tool for
establishing the existence, uniqueness, and stability of solutions to linear
and nonlinear elastic deformation problems. However, it has applications in
diverse areas of mathematics and physics. The family of open sets where the
inequality (
1.1
) holds, sometimes referred to as

Korn domains

[
1
]
, allows for a well-posed theory of
linear elasticity and, therefore, for reliable reduced models, for example,
for the study of elastic plates and
shells
[
11
,
12
]
.

The inequality (
1.1
) is usually referred to as the

second
Korn
inequality
[
10
,
50
,
55
]
to
distinguish it from its variant in the restricted class

C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
of vector fields with compact support
in
\Omega
, usually known as the
first
Korn inequality, which states
the existence of a positive constant
K_{\Omega,p}>0
, such that

(1.2)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant K_{\Omega,p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

The first Korn inequality, for
p=2
, immediately follows from the equality

(1.3)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+|\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}\quad\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Note that, when
p=2
, the constant in (
1.2
) can be chosen independently of
\Omega
(for example,
K_{\Omega,2}=2
). This turns out to be a general remark. Indeed, as long as one is not interested in optimal constants, a simple scaling argument on balls gives that the constant
K_{\Omega,p}
in equation (
1.2
) can always be taken to be independent of
\Omega
.

Despite their significance, available proofs of Korn inequalities are pretty
involved. Their proof strategies typically rely on approximation and
compactness arguments combined with Calderón-Zygmund estimates for singular
integral operators. As described in subsection
1.1
, over
the years, there has been a sporadic yet constant scientific production of
different points of view that often led to simplifications or extensions.

The primary objective of this paper is to present a concise point of view on
the first and the second Korn’s inequality, for general exponent
p
, and for
a class of domains that includes Lipschitz domains. Our proof is conceptually
very simple and, for
p=2
, gives a proof of the second Korn’s inequality
that uses only the classical Riesz representation theorem in Hilbert spaces.
Moreover, conceptually, the argument for the general exponent
p
remains the
same, the only change being invoking now the
q
-Riesz representation theorem
(with
q
the harmonic conjugate of
p
) instead of the classical
2
-Riesz
representation theorem.
We stress that the chief outcomes of the article are known,
the novelty being in the alternative take on the subject we propose.
Further details on the approach are given in section
2
.

2.
Contributions of the present work

The main results of the present work are concise proofs of first and second
Korn’s inequality, as well as Poincaré-Korn inequalities. To state our

\boldsymbol{\langle}\textrm{\text{{{div}}}}_{\operatorname{skw}}\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{\varphi}\boldsymbol{\rangle}:=-\boldsymbol{\langle}\boldsymbol{U},\nabla_{\operatorname{skw}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\rangle}
. Simple computations show that for
\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
there holds

(2.10)

\displaystyle-\Delta\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=-\nabla\text{div}\boldsymbol{u}-2\textrm{\text{{{div}}}}_{\operatorname{skw}}\nabla_{\operatorname{skw}}\boldsymbol{u},

(2.11)

\displaystyle-\Delta\boldsymbol{u}

\displaystyle=+\nabla\text{div}\boldsymbol{u}-2\textrm{\text{{{div}}}}_{\operatorname{sym}}\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u},

and very same relations hold if
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Note that if
N=3
, we have
-2\textrm{\text{{{div}}}}_{\operatorname{skw}}\nabla_{\operatorname{skw}}\boldsymbol{u}=\mathbf{curl}\mathbf{curl}\boldsymbol{u}
and (
2.10
) reduces to the
classical Helmholtz decomposition in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

2.2.
Main results

Our first result concerns Poincaré-Korn
inequalities in bounded and unbounded domains. These inequalities are
essential tools in showing the coercivity of variational problems of
elasticity. One of them (cf. (
2.13
)) is a propedeutic
tool for the proof of the first Korn’s inequality: it shows that the symmetric
gradient seminorm is actually a norm on
C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Theorem 1

(
Poincaré-Korn inequalities
)
.

Let
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
be an open set and
1\leqslant p<\infty
. The following Poincaré-Korn inequalities hold.

i.

There exists a constant
C_{p,N}>0
depending only on
p
and

N
, such that

(2.12)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant C_{p,N}^{p}\int_{\Omega}|x|^{p}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

In particular, if
\Omega
is also bounded, then there exists a constant

\kappa_{p,\Omega}>0
depending only on
p
and
\Omega
, such that

(2.13)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant\kappa_{p,\Omega}^{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

ii.

If
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, then there exist constants

\kappa_{p,\Omega},\kappa_{p,\partial\Omega}>0
, depending only on
p
and

\Omega
such that

(2.14)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant\kappa_{p,\Omega}^{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+\kappa_{p,\partial\Omega}\int_{\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\qquad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N}).

We give an elementary proof of Theorem
1
in
section
4
. Here, instead, we make some remarks. First,
we stress that in contrast to Korn’s inequality which does not hold for
p=1
, the Poincaré-Korn inequality holds even for
p=1
and

arbitrary
open sets. Second, we note that
ii.
implies
(
2.13
). Indeed, if
\Omega
is bounded but
not
Lipschitz, one
can extend by zero an element
\boldsymbol{u}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
to an element of
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})
where
B
is a ball containing
\Omega
, and evaluate (
2.14
)
on
\boldsymbol{u}_{0}
(and with
\Omega
replaced by
B
) to get back to
(
2.13
). We opted to present (
2.13
) as a particular
case of (
2.12
).
Finally, it will be apparent from
the proof that one can take

(2.15)

C_{p,N}:=(2+|p-2|+\sqrt{N})\cdot\frac{p}{p+N},\quad\kappa_{p,\Omega}:=(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)C_{p,N},\quad\kappa_{p,\partial\Omega}:=p\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+1}{p+N}}}\right)(\operatorname{diam}\Omega).

However, there is no pretense of optimality in the previous constants.

Our second contribution is the main objective of our paper and concerns a
concise point of view on the first and second Korn inequality. To state the
results in the generality we want, we need to introduce the proper functional
setting.

For every
1\leqslant p<\infty
, we denote by
E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
the vector space of
L^{p}
-vector-fields, whose symmetric gradient, in
the distributional sense, is still in
L^{p}
, i.e.,

(2.16)

E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\{\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}):\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}\in L^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})\}.

It is standard to show (see, e.g.,
[
61
]
) that
E^{1,p}(\Omega)
is a Banach space when endowed with the norm

(2.17)

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{E^{1,p}}:=(\|\boldsymbol{u}\|^{p}_{L^{p}}+\|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}\|^{p}_{L^{p}})^{1/p}=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}}}\right)^{1/p}.

Also, the Poincaré-Korn inequality (
2.13
) assures that on the
Banach subspace of
E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, given by

(2.18)

E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}):=\overline{C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})},

i.e., defined as the closure of
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
in

E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, the seminorm
\|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{p}}
is actually a norm equivalent to
(
2.17
). It is standard to show that, as happens for the space

W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, even
E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
has the extension-by-zero property. Namely, if
\boldsymbol{u}\in E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
then

(2.19)

\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}\in E^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N})\quad\text{and}\quad\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}=\widetilde{\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}},

where we denoted by
\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
and
\widetilde{\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}}
the extensions by zero of
\boldsymbol{u}
and
\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}
to the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Indeed, if
\boldsymbol{u}\in E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
(\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a
sequence in
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
such that

\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\to\boldsymbol{u}
in
E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, then for every
\boldsymbol{\Phi}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N\times N})
we have that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\langle}\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\rangle}_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot\textrm{\text{{{div}}}}_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}=-\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}\cdot\textrm{\text{{{div}}}}_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\;\;=\;\;\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{\varphi}_{n}:\boldsymbol{\Phi}

(2.20)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}\widetilde{\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}}:\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\boldsymbol{\langle}\widetilde{\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}},\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\rangle}_{\mathbb{R}^{N}}.

But the extension-by-zero property is not the only feature that the spaces

E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
share.
Indeed, as we show in Theorem
2
below, when
1<p<\infty
and
\Omega
is a bounded open set, then
E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})=W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and their associated norms are
equivalent. Also, one has
E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})=W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
when
1<p<\infty
and
\Omega
is what we call an
extension
domain
.

Definition 1
.

We say that a bounded open set
\Omega
is an extension
domain
(
for
E^{1,p}
)
when any element
\boldsymbol{u}\in E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
can be extended to an element of
E^{1,p}(\mathbb{R}^{N},\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

Remark 1
.

Generally speaking,
E^{1,p}
-extension domains can be
investigated by transposing the known techniques from
W^{1,p}
to
E^{1,p}
. For example, the technique in
[
54
]
, which suitably adapts the standard extension by reflection to the setting of symmetrized gradients, shows that Lipschitz domains are
E^{1,p}
-extension domains. Recently, it has been shown that
(\varepsilon,\delta)
-domains in the sense of Jones are
E^{1,p}
-extension domains. For that, we refer the reader to
[
3
, Thm 6.2]
which generalizes previous results contained in
[
35
]
. Finally, we observe that a notion of
E^{1,p}_{0}
-extension domain would not be
interesting because, as we have shown in (
2.20
), every bounded
open set would be of this type.

Theorem 2

(
first and second Korn’s inequality
)
.

For
1<p<\infty
, the following assertions hold:

i.

If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an extension domain, then
E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})=W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and there exists a constant

K_{\Omega,p}>0
such that

(2.21)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant K_{\Omega,p}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in W^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

ii.

If
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{N}
is an open set, then
E^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})=W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and there exists a constant

K_{\Omega,p}>0
such that

(2.22)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant K_{\Omega,p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\quad\forall\boldsymbol{u}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

Remark 2
.

As already remarked in the introduction, as long as one is not interested in optimal constants, a simple scaling argument on balls gives that the constant
K_{\Omega,p}
in equation (
2.22
) can always be taken to be independent of
\Omega
.
Also, as already recalled in section
1.1
,
Korn inequalities are no longer true when
p=1
and the space
W^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
E^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
are
different. The space
E^{1,1}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
is often denoted
by
\operatorname{LD}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and plays a pivotal role in the
mathematical theory of plasticity. We refer the reader to
[
61
, Chap.
II]
for some of the main properties of the space
\operatorname{LD}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
.

The concise proof of Theorem
2
is given at the beginning of
section

and relies on the following lemma, whose proof is
deferred to the end of section

.

Let

. If

Proof of Lemma

for
N=3

Let

\boldsymbol{u}\in E^{1,p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
and consider a ball

for every

\boldsymbol{\varphi}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})
. Therefore,
by combining this equality with (

\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{1,p}_{0}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})
,

, such that

-\Delta\boldsymbol{v}=-(\Delta\boldsymbol{u}+\nabla\text{div}\boldsymbol{u})\quad\text{in }\mathcal{D}^{\prime}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

In particular, applying the divergence operator to both sides of

is harmonic in

, and this, by Weyl’s

(3.5)

\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}/2)\in C^{\infty}(B).

\mathbf{curl}

\mathbf{curl}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})
is harmonic in

\mathbf{curl}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})\in C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N}).

But then

\displaystyle-\Delta(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{curl}\mathbf{curl}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v})-\nabla\text{div}(\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{v}/2)+\nabla\text{div}(\boldsymbol{v}/2)

\displaystyle\in C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})+C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})+\nabla\text{div}(W^{1,p}_{0}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N}))

(3.9)

\displaystyle\in C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})+C^{\infty}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})+W^{-1,q^{\prime}}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})

and, therefore, given that
-\Delta\boldsymbol{v}\in W^{-1,q^{\prime}}(B,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, we are back to (
3.8
), and from that point on,
the proof is the same as for
N=3
.
\square

4.
Proof of Theorem
1
: Poincaré-Korn
inequalities

Following the ideas of
[
25
]
, we first give elementary proofs
of the Poincaré-Korn inequalities, whose unique ingredient is the
divergence theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1
. For
every
\varepsilon>0
, we consider the vector field

\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
defined by

\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}:=(\boldsymbol{u},\varepsilon)
. Note that
|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|=(|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}+\varepsilon^{2})^{1/2}

and, therefore,
|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{\alpha}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
for every
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
. Here, with a convenient abuse
of notation, we used the same symbol to denote the Euclidean norms in
\mathbb{R}^{N+1}
and
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. It is easy to check that the following equalities hold

(4.1)

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(\nabla^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{u}x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{p}\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p}x)-\frac{N}{p}|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p},

(4.2)

\displaystyle|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u}\cdot(\nabla\boldsymbol{u}x)

\displaystyle=\text{div}((\boldsymbol{u}\cdot x)|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u})-(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot x)\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u})-|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2},

from which, summing term by term, we get

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{N}{p}+\frac{|\boldsymbol{u}|^{2}}{|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{2}}}}\right)|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p}

\displaystyle=-2|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-1}\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|}\cdot(\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}\,x)-(\boldsymbol{u}\cdot x)\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u})

(4.3)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\frac{1}{p}\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p}x)+\text{div}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|}\cdot x}}\right)\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|}}}\right).

The previous relation is the key ingredient in the proof of
Theorem
1
.

Proof of
Theorem
1
.
i.

Proof of (
2.12
).

If
\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{N})
, integrating on

\Omega
both sides (
4.3
), and then passing to the limit for

\varepsilon\to 0
, we get that

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+N}{p}}}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p-1}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}||x|+\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}||\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u})||x|,

and, we are left to evaluate the last term on the right-hand side of the
previous relation (
4.4
). For that, we observe that

(4.5)

\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u})=|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[(p-2)(\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u})\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|}\cdot\frac{\boldsymbol{u}}{|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|}+\text{div}\boldsymbol{u}}}\right]

and, therefore, given that
|\text{div}\boldsymbol{u}|=|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}:I|\leqslant\sqrt{N}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|
, we have the estimate

(4.6)

\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}||\text{div}(|\boldsymbol{u}_{\varepsilon}|^{p-2}\boldsymbol{u})||x|\leqslant(|p-2|+\sqrt{N})\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p-1}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|.

Estimating (
4.4
) with (
4.6
) we get that

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{N+p}{p}}}\right)\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant(2+|p-2|+\sqrt{N})\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p-1}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}||x|\,,

which establishes (
2.12
) when
p=1
. For
p>1
, Hölder
inequality gives the Poincaré-Korn inequality under the weighted form
(
2.12
).

Proof of (
2.13
).
It is sufficient to note that
the proof of (
2.12
) works verbatim if one replaces everywhere
x

with
x-x_{0}
,
x_{0}
being an arbitrary point of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. But then, if
\Omega

is bounded, one gets

(4.7)

\|\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}\leqslant C_{p,N}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\inf_{x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}}\sup_{x\in\Omega}|x-x_{0}|}}\right)\|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}\|_{L^{p}(\Omega)}

from which (
2.13
) follows as a special case.
\square

Proof of
Theorem
1
.
ii.

Proof of
(
2.14
).
If
\boldsymbol{u}\in C^{\infty}(\overline{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{N})
and
\Omega
is a bounded Lipschitz domain, then integrating on

\Omega
both sides of (
4.3
), passing to the limit for
\varepsilon\to 0
, and taking into account (
4.6
), we get that

(4.8)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant C_{p,N}\int_{x\in\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p-1}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}||x|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+1}{p+N}}}\right)\int_{\xi\in\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}|\xi|.

Again, the previous computation works verbatim if one replaces everywhere
x

with
x-x_{0}
, with
x_{0}
an arbitrary point of
\mathbb{R}^{N}
. Therefore, we have

(4.9)

\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)C_{p,N}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p-1}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+1}{p+N}}}\right)(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\,.

Now, if

applied to the first term on the right-hand side of (

), we
obtain that

(4.10)

\frac{1}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\leqslant\frac{(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)^{p}C_{p,N}^{p}}{p}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla_{\operatorname{sym}}\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}+\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{p+1}{p+N}}}\right)(\operatorname{diam}\Omega)\int_{\partial\Omega}|\boldsymbol{u}|^{p}\,,

from which (
2.14
) follows.
\square
