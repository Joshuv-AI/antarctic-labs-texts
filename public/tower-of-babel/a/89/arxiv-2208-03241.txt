Fine Grained Analysis of High Dimensional Random Walks
Roy Gotlib, Tali Kaufman

Abstract
One of the most important properties of high dimensional expanders is that high dimensional random walks converge rapidly. This property has proven to be extremely useful in a variety of fields in the theory of computer science from agreement testing to sampling, coding theory and more. In this paper we present a state of the art result in a line of works analyzing the convergence of high dimensional random walks [ KM17 , DK17 , KO18 , AL20 ] , by presenting a structured version of the result of [ AL20 ] . While previous works examined the expansion in the viewpoint of the worst possible eigenvalue, in this work we relate the expansion of a function to the entire spectrum of the random walk operator using the structure of the function; We call such a theorem a Fine Grained High Order Random Walk Theorem. In sufficiently structured cases the fine grained result that we present here can be much better than the worst case while in the worst case our result is equivalent to [ AL20 ] . In order to prove the Fine Grained High Order Random Walk Theorem we introduce a way to bootstrap the expansion of random walks on the vertices of a complex into a fine grained understanding of higher order random walks, provided that the expansion is good enough. In addition, our single bootstrapping theorem can simultaneously yield our Fine Grained High Order Random Walk Theorem as well as the well known Trickling down Theorem. Prior to this work, High order Random walks theorems and Tricking down Theorem have been obtained from different proof methods.

Comparison with other known decompositions

Similar decompositions of the high dimensional random walks were already known for two-sided high dimensional expanders
[

,
KO18
]

Unlike the case in two-sided high dimensional expanders, in one-sided high dimensional expanders the eigenspaces of the high dimensional random walks are not currently understood, even approximately.
We do, however, understand the expansion of key random walks on the
0
-dimensional cochains.
We follow this by showing that if this expansion is strong enough (i.e. these random walks converge fast enough) we can boost it to all levels and get a decomposition theorem without eigendecomposition.
In order to apply our bootstrapping method we have to show that the expansion of the aforementioned
0

Note that the decomposition achieved by our bootstrapping theorem differs from the decomposition known in two-sided expanders in one

Note that many results that use local spectral expanders, such as their usage in proving the existence of cosystolic expanders, agreement testing, locally testable codes and more only require one application of the walk operator and thus we believe that our result will prove to be influential with regards to these fields.

Proof Layout

As we mentioned before, we analyze the
non-lazy up-down random walk
- a high dimensional analogue of the non-lazy random walk in graphs.
We would like to get a decomposition of the non-lazy random walk operator.
In order to do that we decompose the space of cochains to spaces which we term level cochains.
Our proof is then comprised of two steps:
A bootstrapping Lemma that reduces the problem of decomposing the non-lazy up-down random walk operator to a simple recursive condition about the
0

We note that solving the recursive condition is considerably simpler than proving the decomposition directly as the
0

Link viewers

Local-to-global arguments are typically structured by taking a global cochain, decomposing it to local cochains (i.e. cochains on the links of the vertices of the complex) and then using a property of the cochains in the links in order to argue about the original cochain.
Two extremely useful ways of decomposing a global cochain to cochains in the links are
restriction
and
localization

These two methods share many properties, for example:

•

They preserve constant functions - Any global constant function is constant locally.

•

They are linear - The local view of sum of any two global cochains is the sum of the local views.

•

They interact well with the inner product - The expected value of the local inner products of two cochains is their inner product.

We start by identifying these properties and defining a general object that satisfy them called a

A link viewer

In the advantage step we try to bound the remainder we have left in the bootstrapping stage.

Lemma 2.2

Consider, now, the following:

\displaystyle\left<d{F},{G}\right>

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(k+1)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right)d{F}({\sigma}){G}({\sigma})}=\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(k+1)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right)\ex_{{\tau}\in X(k)}{\left[{F}({\tau})\middle|{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\right]}{G}({\sigma})}=

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in X(k)}{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right){F}({\tau})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\sigma}\in X(k+1)\\

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in X(k)}{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right){F}({\tau})\ex_{{\sigma}\in X(k+1)}{\left[{G}({\sigma})\middle|{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\right]}}

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
{F}
be an

Then:

d_{j-1}\cdots d_{i}{F}({\sigma})=\ex_{{\tau}\in X\left(i\right)}{\left[{F}({\tau})\middle|{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\right]}

Proof.

By induction, the case of

Induction step:

\displaystyle d_{j}\cdots d_{i}{F}({\sigma})

\displaystyle=\ex_{{\tau}\in X(j)}{\left[d_{j}\cdots d_{i}{F}({\tau})\middle|{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\right]}=\ex_{{\tau}\in X(j)}{\left[\ex_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in X\left(i\right)}{\left[{F}({\tau}^{\prime})\middle|{\tau}^{\prime}\subseteq{\tau}\right]}\middle|{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{j+1}}{\frac{1}{j+2}\sum_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in\binom{{\tau}}{i+1}}{\frac{1}{\binom{j+1}{i+1}}{F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}=\sum_{{\tau}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{j+1}}{\frac{1}{j+2}\sum_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in\binom{{\tau}}{i+1}}{\frac{(j-i)!(i+1)!}{(j+1)!}{F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{j+1}}{\sum_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in\binom{{\tau}}{i+1}}{\frac{(j-i)!(i+1)!}{(j+2)!}{F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}=\sum_{{\tau}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{i+1}}{(j-i+1)\frac{(j-i)!(i+1)!}{(j+2)!}{F}({\tau})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{i+1}}{\frac{(j-i+1)!(i+1)!}{(j+2)!}{F}({\tau})}=\sum_{{\tau}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{i+1}}{\frac{1}{\binom{j+2}{i+1}}{F}({\tau})}

\displaystyle=\ex_{{\tau}\in X\left(i\right)}{\left[{F}({\tau})\middle|{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\right]}

We turn our attention to showing that the adjoint operator of the signless differential localizes to the links in the following way:

.

be an

d^{*}_{i}\cdots d^{*}_{j-1}{F}({\sigma})=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(j-i-1)}{\left[{F}_{{\sigma}}({\tau})\right]}

Proof.

By induction, the case where

\displaystyle d^{*}_{i}\cdots d^{*}_{j}{F}({\sigma})

3
Up-Down and Down-Up Operators

In this section we will present two objects of interest - the up-down and down-up walks.
These are natural operators that result from considering a standard walk on the
k
-dimensional faces of a simplicial complex using the following two steps:
A
down step
where, given a
k
-dimensional face, the walk moves to a
(k-1)
-dimensional face that is contained in it with equal probability.
And an
up step
in which, given a
k
-dimensional face, the walk moves to a
(k+1)
-dimensional face with probability proportional to its weight.
Applying the up step followed by the down step yields the up-down walk while applying the down step followed by the up step yields the down-up walk.

Definition 3.1

(Up-down and down-up operators)
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex.
Then define the up-down random walk to be:

\left[M^{+}_{k}\right]_{{\sigma},{\tau}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{k+2}&{\sigma}={\tau}\\
\frac{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\setminus{\sigma}\right)}{k+2}&{\sigma}\cup{\tau}\in X(k+1)\\
0&\text{Otherwise}\end{cases}

And the down-up random walk to be:

\left[M^{-}_{k}\right]_{{\sigma},{\tau}}=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in\binom{{\sigma}}{k}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}^{\prime}}\left({\sigma}\setminus{\tau}^{\prime}\right)}&{\sigma}={\tau}\\
\frac{1}{k+1}\weightletter_{{\sigma}\cap{\tau}}\left({\tau}\setminus{\sigma}\right)&{\sigma}\cap{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
0&\text{Otherwise}\end{cases}

We would now like to present characterization the up-down random walk and the down-up random walk using the signless differential (this characterization had appeared in
[
KO18
, Corollary 3.7]
and is given here for completeness).
In order to do that, consider the following Lemma:

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex, it holds that:

M^{+}_{k}=d^{*}_{k}d_{k}\qquad\qquad M^{-}_{k}=d_{k-1}d^{*}_{k-1}

Proof.

Let
{F}\in C^{k}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
and
{\sigma}\in X(k)
, then:

\displaystyle d^{*}d{F}({\sigma})

\displaystyle=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\left[\left(d{F}\right)_{{\sigma}}({\tau})\right]}=\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right)\left(d{F}\right)_{{\sigma}}({\tau})}=\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right)d{F}({\tau}\cup{\sigma})}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right)\frac{1}{k+2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime}\subseteq{\sigma}\cup{\tau}\end{subarray}}{{F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+2}\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime}\subseteq{\sigma}\cup{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+2}\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\left(\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right){F}({\sigma})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime}\subseteq{\sigma}\cup{\tau}\\
{\tau}^{\prime}\neq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime})}\right)}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+2}\left(\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right){F}({\sigma})}+\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime}\subseteq{\sigma}\cup{\tau}\\
{\tau}^{\prime}\neq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+2}\left({F}({\sigma})+\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(0)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\sigma}\cup{\tau}^{\prime}={\sigma}\cup{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}^{\prime}\setminus{\sigma}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+2}\left({F}({\sigma})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\sigma}\cup{\tau}^{\prime}\in X(k+1)\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}^{\prime}\setminus{\sigma}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=M^{+}_{k}{F}

In addition:

\displaystyle dd^{*}{F}({\sigma})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{d^{*}{F}({\tau})}=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\ex_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in X_{{\tau}}(0)}{\left[{F}_{{\tau}}({\tau}^{\prime})\right]}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{{\tau}^{\prime}\in X_{{\tau}}(0)}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\tau}^{\prime}\right){F}_{{\tau}}({\tau}^{\prime})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\sigma}\right){F}_{{\tau}}({\sigma}\setminus{\tau})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime}\in X_{{\tau}}(0)\\
{\tau}\cup{\tau}^{\prime}\neq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\tau}^{\prime}\right){F}_{{\tau}}({\tau}^{\prime})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\neq{\sigma}\\
{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\cap{\sigma}={\tau}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\tau}^{\prime\prime}\setminus{\tau}\right){F}_{{\tau}}({\tau}^{\prime\prime}\setminus{\tau})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\cap{\sigma}={\tau}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\tau}^{\prime\prime}\setminus{\tau}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime\prime})}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k+1}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(k-1)\\
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}}\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\in X(k)\\
{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\cap{\sigma}\in X(k-1)\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\tau}^{\prime\prime}\cap{\sigma}}\left({\tau}^{\prime\prime}\setminus{\sigma}\right){F}({\tau}^{\prime\prime})}\right)

\displaystyle=M^{-}_{k}{F}

∎

4
The Non-Lazy Walk Operator

Our main object of study is going to be the non-lazy
k
-dimensional random walk operator.
This operator is a generalization of the non-lazy random walk operator in graphs to higher dimensions.
In graphs the non-lazy random walk operator moves between two vertices if they have an edge connecting them.
The higher dimensional version of this operator is going to be something very similar:
It is going to move between two
k
-faces if there is a
(k+1)
-face that contains both faces.

Definition 4.1

(The Non-Lazy
k
-dimensional Random Walk Operator)
.

Let
X
be a pure
d
-dimensional simplicial complex define the
k
-dimensional random walk operator to be the following operator:

\left[\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{k}\right]_{{\sigma},{\tau}}=\begin{cases}\frac{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\setminus{\sigma}\right)}{k+1}&{\sigma}\cup{\tau}\in X(k+1)\\
0&\text{Otherwise}\end{cases}

One can also think of the non-lazy random walk operator as the regular up-down operator with the lazy part removed.
Formally:

Observation 4.2
.

For every dimension
k
it holds that:

\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{k}=\frac{k+2}{k+1}M^{+}_{k}-\frac{1}{k+1}I

Of specific interest is the
0
-dimensional non-lazy up-down operator as it can be used to describe
every
one of the
0
-dimensional
i
-up-down operators.
This is because every one of these operators ultimately move between a vertex and its neighbours.
The more steps one takes up the complex the more mixed the result is.
By that we mean that the non-lazy walk operator has a larger effect on the result.
Quantitatively, this can be formulated via the following Lemma.

Lemma 4.3
.

\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{0}=\frac{i+1}{i}M^{+{i}}_{0}-\frac{1}{i}I

Proof.

Consider the up operator:

\displaystyle M^{+{i}}_{0}{F}({v})

\displaystyle=d^{*}_{0}\cdots d^{*}_{i-1}d_{i-1}\cdots d_{0}{F}({v})=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\left[\left(d_{i-1}\cdots d_{0}{F}\right)_{{v}}({\tau})\right]}=

\displaystyle=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\left[d_{i-1}\cdots d_{0}{F}({\tau}{v})\right]}=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\left[\ex_{{u}\in X\left(0\right)}{\left[{F}({u})\middle|{u}\subseteq{\tau}{v}\right]}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\weightletter_{{v}}\left({\tau}\right)\sum_{{u}\in{\tau}{v}}{\frac{1}{\binom{i+1}{1}}{F}({u})}}=\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\sum_{{u}\in{\tau}{v}}{\frac{\weightletter_{{v}}\left({\tau}\right)}{i+1}{F}({u})}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\frac{\weightletter_{{v}}\left({\tau}\right)}{i+1}{F}({v})}+\sum_{{\tau}\in X_{{v}}(i-1)}{\sum_{{u}\in{\tau}}{\frac{\weightletter_{{v}}\left({\tau}\right)}{i+1}{F}({u})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(i)\\
{v}\in{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in X(0)\\
{u}\neq{v},{u}\in{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\frac{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right)}{(i+1)^{2}\weightletter\left({v}\right)}{F}({u})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\sum_{{u}\in X(0)}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(i)\\
{u}\neq{v}\\
{u},{v}\in{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\frac{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right)}{(i+1)^{2}\weightletter\left({v}\right)}{F}({u})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in X(0)\\
{v}{u}\in X(1)\end{subarray}}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(i)\\
{u},{v}\in{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\frac{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right)}{(i+1)^{2}\weightletter\left({v}\right)}{F}({u})}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in X(0)\\
{v}{u}\in X(1)\end{subarray}}{\frac{1}{(i+1)^{2}\weightletter\left({v}\right)}{F}({u})\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in X(i)\\
{u},{v}\in{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in X(0)\\
{v}{u}\in X(1)\end{subarray}}{\frac{\binom{i+1}{2}\weightletter\left({v}{u}\right)}{(i+1)^{2}\weightletter\left({v}\right)}{F}({u})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in X(0)\\
{v}{u}\in X(1)\end{subarray}}{\frac{i\weightletter\left({v}{u}\right)}{2(i+1)\weightletter\left({v}\right)}{F}({u})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\frac{i}{i+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{u}\in X(0)\\
{v}{u}\in X(1)\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{v}}\left({u}\right){F}({u})}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{i+1}{F}({v})+\frac{i}{i+1}\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{0}{F}({v})

Therefore:

\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{0}=\frac{i+1}{i}M^{+{i}}_{0}-\frac{1}{i}I

∎

5
Analyzing the Non-Lazy Random Walk Operator

We are now ready to start analyzing the random walk operators.
In order to do so we are going to use a
local to global
argument.
In order to apply a local to global argument we must understand how to view a cochain through the links.
Specifically we will be interested in viewing methods that satisfy the following:

Definition 5.1

(Link viewer)
.

A
link viewer
is any transformation
\Lambda
that accepts a face
{\sigma}
and a cochain in
X
.
It then returns a cochain in
X_{{\sigma}}
.
In addition, a link viewer satisfies the following:

•

For every face
{\sigma}
it holds that
\Lambda_{{\sigma}}
is linear.

•

For every face
{\sigma}
it holds that
\Lambda_{{\sigma}}\mathbbm{1}=\mathbbm{1}
.

•

For every two cochains
{F},{G}
and any two faces of the same dimension
{\sigma}
,
{\tau}
it holds that:

\dim\left({F}\right)-\dim\left(\Lambda_{{\sigma}}{F}\right)=\dim\left({G}\right)-\dim\left(\Lambda_{{\tau}}{G}\right)

In addition, denote the dimensional difference for vertices by:

\Delta\left(\Lambda\right)=\dim\left({F}\right)-\dim\left(\Lambda_{{v}}{F}\right)

•

Viewing of a cochain in a link is only determined by the cochain and the face for which it is the link (and not the path taken to achieve said view).
Formally: For every
{\tau}\subseteq{\sigma}\in X
it holds that:

\Lambda_{{\sigma}}=\Lambda_{{\sigma}\setminus{\tau}}\Lambda_{{\tau}}

•

For every dimension
i
it holds that:

\left<{F},{G}\right>=\ex_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\left[\left<\Lambda_{{\sigma}}{F},\Lambda_{{\sigma}}{G}\right>\right]}

Of particular interest are link viewers that view the non-lazy random walk operator in “the right way”:

Definition 5.2
.

A link viewer respects the non-lazy up-down random walk if for every
k
:

\left<\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{k}{F},{F}\right>=\ex_{{v}\in X(0)}{\left[\left<\left(M^{\prime}\right)^{+}_{k-\Delta\left(\Lambda\right)}\Lambda_{{v}}{F},\Lambda_{{v}}{F}\right>\right]}

Definition 5.3

(
i
-level cochain)
.

A cochain
{F}
is an
i
-level cochain with respect to
\Lambda
if it holds that:

\forall{\sigma}\in X(i-1):\left<\Lambda_{{\sigma}}{F},\mathbbm{1}\right>=0

When the link viewer is clear from context we will simply refer to them as
i
-level cochains.
Denote the set of
i
-level
j
-dimensional cochains in
X
by
C_{\Lambda,i}^{j}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

For every dimension
j\leq i
:

C_{\Lambda,i}^{k}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)\subseteq C_{\Lambda,j}^{k}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)

Proof.

Let
{F}\in C_{\Lambda,i}^{k}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
and let
{\sigma}
be a
(j-1)
-dimensional face and note the following:

\displaystyle\left<\Lambda_{{\sigma}}{F},\mathbbm{1}\right>

\displaystyle=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(i-j)}{\left[\left<\Lambda_{{\tau}}\Lambda_{{\sigma}}{F},\Lambda_{{\tau}}\mathbbm{1}\right>\right]}=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(i-j)}{\left[\left<\Lambda_{{\tau}{\sigma}}{F},\mathbbm{1}\right>\right]}=0

Where the last equality is due to the fact that
{\sigma}\cup{\tau}
is an
(i-1)
-dimensional face.
∎

Of particular interest are
i
-level cochains that are orthogonal to the
(i+1)
-level cochains.
We will therefore define the following:

Definition 5.5

(Proper
i
-level cochain)
.

A cochain
{F}
is a proper
i
-level cochain with respect to
\Lambda
if it holds that:

{F}\in C_{\Lambda,i}^{j}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)\cap\left(C_{\Lambda,i+1}^{j}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)\right)^{\bot}

When the link viewer is clear from context we will simply refer to them as proper
i
-level cochains.
Denote the set of proper
i
-level
j
-dimensional cochains in
X
by
C_{\Lambda,\hat{i}}^{j}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
.

Consider the following key property of proper level cochains:

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
i<j
and let
{F}
be a proper
i
-level cochain and
{G}
be a proper

-level cochain then:

\left<{F},{G}\right>=0

Proof.

Due to Lemma
5.4
it holds that
{G}
is also a

by definition.
∎

Note that considering proper level cochains is one of the key ingredients of this paper.
In previous results (For example
[

]
) instead of considering proper

-level cochains the authors essentially considered cochains in

Using proper level cochains allows us to separate the different levels completely and achieve a proper decomposition.
Since analyzing the decomposition with only pure level cochains in mind is hard (as even if
{F}
is a level cochain the same cannot be said about

) we resort to first show the following technical theorem.

Theorem 5.7

(Bootstrapping Theorem, Technical Version)
.

Let
X
be a pure
d
-dimensional simplicial complex,
\Lambda
be a link viewer that respects the non-lazy up-down random walk and for every

6
Trickling Down

Before we present our random walk decomposition Theorem, let us start with a “warm up”:
An alternative proof for the trickling down theorem

We believe that the fact that the main tool presented here can be used to prove the trickling down theorem is of independent interest: it shows that there is a single, local to global argument at the heart of both claims.
In the trickling down theorem we are interested in the connection between the
0

0

It will, therefore, be natural to consider a link viewer that does not incur a decrease in dimension.
One such link viewer is the restriction link viewer defined as:

Definition 6.1

Given a simplicial complex

-dimensional non-lazy up-down on the links of the vertices.
We will show that applying Theorem
5.8

However, before applying Theorem
5.8

•

Theorem 7.9

(Random walk decomposition)

Let

d
-dimensional pure simplicial complex.
Also, assume that for every face

of dimension smaller than

2

\displaystyle\lambda_{\emptyset,1,k}\left\|{F}^{=0}\right\|^{2}+(1-\lambda_{\emptyset,1,k})\left(1-\frac{k}{k+1}\left(1-\gamma_{-1}\right)\right)\left\|{F}^{=0}\right\|^{2}

7.3.1

Minimal cochains appear naturally in a topological definition of expansion and were widely studied over

We will present a definition of an analogue of these cochains over the real numbers and show that minimal

We begin by noting that the topological definition of expansion uses

Definition 7.10

(Oriented complex)
.

We define of an oriented simplicial complex
X
as a simplicial complex that has an underlying orientation on the vertices.
We also define the set
X_{ord}
to be the set of all possible orientations of the faces of

Definition 7.11

(Cochain over oriented complex)
.

A cochain
{F}\in C^{i}\left(X_{ord};\mathbbm{R}\right)
is a function
{F}:X_{ord}\rightarrow\mathbbm{R}
such that for every permutation
\rho
and every face
{\sigma}\in X_{ord}
it holds that:

{F}({\sigma}_{\rho(0)},\cdots,{\sigma}_{\rho(i)})=\sign(\rho){F}({\sigma}_{0},\cdots,{\sigma}_{i})

For the rest of this section we will assume that the complex has some underlying orientation and that faces are given in that underlying distribution.
For that reason, unless otherwise stated, we will ignore the orientation of the complex.

Another key definition in topological notions of high dimensional expanders is the coboundary operator.
In the
0

\mathbbm{F}_{2}
the coboundary operator can be thought of as marking all the edges that leave a set of vertices.

Definition 7.12

(Coboundary operator)

Define the coboundary operator
\delta_{i}:C^{i}\left(X_{ord};\mathbbm{R}\right)\rightarrow C^{i+1}\left(X_{ord};\mathbbm{R}\right)

\delta_{i}{F}({\sigma}_{0},\cdots,{\sigma}_{i+1})=\sum_{j=0}^{i+1}{(-1)^{j}{F}({\sigma}_{0},\cdots,{\sigma}_{j-1},{\sigma}_{j+1},\cdots,{\sigma}_{i+1})}

Note that the dimension of the coboundary operator is always clear from context and thus we will often denote the coboundary operator simply by
\delta
.

Definition 7.13

(Coboundary)
.

We define the set of coboundaries to be:

B^{i}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)=\image(\delta_{i-1})

We are now ready to define a minimal cochain - a family of cochains that are all

Definition 7.14

(Minimal cochain)
.

A cochain
{F}\in C^{i}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
is minimal if:

{F}=\argmin_{{G}\in B^{i}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)}{\left\|{F}+{G}\right\|}

In order to show that that every minimal cochain is indeed a
k
-level cochain we will first consider a weaker notion of minimality called local-minimality.

Definition 7.15

(Locally minimal cochain)
.

A cochain
{F}\in C^{i}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
is
\Lambda^{\ell}
-
i
-locally minimal if for every
{\sigma}\in X(i)
it holds that
\Lambda^{\ell}_{{\sigma}}{F}
is minimal.

We will show that every
k
-cochain that is
(k-1)
-locally minimal is also a
k
-level cochain.
We will then follow this by showing that any minimal cochain is
(k-1)
-locally minimal and thus prove the claim.

Lemma 7.16
.

Let
X
be a simplicial complex and let
{F}\in C^{i}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
be a cochain.
It holds that
\left\|{F}-a\right\|^{2}<\left\|{F}\right\|^{2}
if one of the following conditions hold:

•

a\leq 0
and
a>2\ex_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\left[{F}({\sigma})\right]}
.

•

a\geq 0
and
a<2\ex_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\left[{F}({\sigma})\right]}

Proof.

Note that the following hold:

\left\|{F}-a\right\|^{2}<\left\|{F}\right\|^{2}\Leftrightarrow\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right)\left({F}({\sigma})-a\right)^{2}}<\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})^{2}}\Leftrightarrow\\
\Leftrightarrow\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})^{2}}-2a\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}+a^{2}<\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})^{2}}\Leftrightarrow\\
\Leftrightarrow a^{2}<2a\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}

If
a\geq 0
then:

a^{2}<2a\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}\Leftrightarrow a<2\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}

In addition, if
a\leq 0
then:

a^{2}<2a\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}\Leftrightarrow a>2\sum_{{\sigma}\in X(i)}{\weightletter\left({\sigma}\right){F}({\sigma})}

∎

Corollary 7.17
.

Let
{F}\in C^{0}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
be a cochain.
If
{F}
is minimal then
\ex_{{\sigma}\in X(0)}{\left[{F}({\sigma})\right]}=0

Proof.

Note that the
0

\ex_{{\sigma}\in X(0)}{\left[{F}({\sigma})\right]}\neq 0

satisfies the conditions of Lemma
7.16
and thus contradict the minimality of
{F}

7.3.2
Perfectly Balanced Cochains

We will now show a different family of cochains that are
k
-level cochains.
Specifically we define the notion of perfectly balanced cochains.
This will be a purely combinatorial definition and we will show that cochains that satisfy this combinatorial definition are
k
-level cochains (up to an additive constant).

Definition 7.21

(Perfectly balanced set of faces)
.

A set of faces
S\subseteq X(k)
is perfectly balanced over links of dimension
i
if:

\forall{\sigma}\in X(i):\sum_{{\tau}\in S}{\weightletter\left({\tau}\right)}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}{\tau}\in S\\
{\sigma}\subseteq{\tau}\end{subarray}}{\weightletter_{{\sigma}}\left({\tau}\setminus{\sigma}\right)}

Definition 7.22

(Perfectly balanced cochain)
.

A cochain
{F}\in C^{k}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
is perfectly balanced over faces of dimension
i
if there exists a perfectly balanced set of faces
S\subseteq X(k)
that is perfectly balanced over faces of dimension
i
such that:

{F}({\sigma})=\begin{cases}1&{\sigma}\in S\\
0&{\sigma}\notin S\end{cases}

Lemma 7.23
.

Let
{F}\in C^{k}\left(X;\mathbbm{R}\right)
be perfectly balanced over faces of dimension
i
then
{F}-\ex{\left[{F}\right]}
is an
i
-level cochain.

Proof.

The Lemma follows directly from the definition:

\forall{\sigma}\in X(i):\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(k-i-1)}{\left[\Lambda^{\ell}_{{\sigma}}\left({F}-\ex{\left[{F}\right]}\right)({\tau})\right]}=\\
=\ex_{{\tau}\in X_{{\sigma}}(k-i-1)}{\left[\Lambda^{\ell}_{{\sigma}}{F}({\tau})-\ex{\left[{F}\right]}\right]}=\ex_{{\tau}\in X(k)}{\left[{F}({\tau})\right]}-\ex{\left[{F}\right]}=0

∎
