An adaptive transfer learning perspective on classification in non-stationary environments
Henry W J Reeve

Abstract
We consider a semi-supervised classification problem with non-stationary label-shift in which we observe a labelled data set followed by a sequence of unlabelled covariate vectors in which the marginal probabilities of the class labels may change over time. Our objective is to predict the corresponding class-label for each covariate vector, without ever observing the ground-truth labels, beyond the initial labelled data set. Previous work has demonstrated the potential of sophisticated variants of online gradient descent to perform competitively with the optimal dynamic strategy (Bai et al. 2022). In this work we explore an alternative approach grounded in statistical methods for adaptive transfer learning. We demonstrate the merits of this alternative methodology by establishing a high-probability regret bound on the test error at any given individual test-time, which adapt automatically to the unknown dynamics of the marginal label probabilities. Further more, we give bounds on the average dynamic regret which match the average guarantees of the online learning perspective for any given time interval.



1
Introduction

A longstanding paradigm in theoretical statistics has been to focus on settings in which the practitioner has access to a sample consisting of observations drawn from a distribution
P
, and the practitioner’s objective is to estimate a parameter of the very same distribution
P
, where “parameter” here is to be construed broadly to include any property of the distribution, from a real-valued parameter such as a population mean, through to an infinite-dimensional parameter such as a Bayes optimal classifier. Whilst such settings, often provide a natural model, in many real-world problems we wish to leverage data from source distribution
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
which is related, but distinct from the target distribution
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
corresponding to the parameter of interest. Transfer learning refers to the wide array of methods developed for transferring knowledge from these related source data to a target domain
(
Zhuang et al.,, 2020
)
. Applications range from medical settings, where a practitioner may seek to combine information from new and historical data to estimate the efficacy of a new treatment or vaccine
(
Aloui et al.,, 2023
)
, through to natural language processing where practitioners frequently utilise vast corpora of unlabelled text data to pre-train language models, before fine-tuning for a specific task of interest
(
Devlin et al.,, 2019
)
.

In light of the impressive empirical successes of transfer learning, the topic has recently attracted a great deal of interest from the statistical and theoretical machine learning communities. There has been a strong focus on the canonical problem of binary classification where both the source
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
and the target
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
are distributions over a space
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
consisting tuples
(x,y)
, where
x\in\mathcal{X}
is a covariate-vector and
y\in\{0,1\}
is a binary label. The objective is to identify an optimal rule
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
for which
minimises the probability of an error
\phi(X^{\mathrm{tg}})\neq Y^{\mathrm{tg}}
for
(X^{\mathrm{tg}},Y^{\mathrm{tg}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
, whilst the large majority of the data is distributed according to
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
.

One important line of work on binary classification considers the covariate-shift setting
(
Sugiyama and Mueller,, 2005
;
Gretton et al.,, 2009
;
Candela et al.,, 2009
;
Sugiyama and Kawanabe,, 2012
;
Sugiyama et al.,, 2012
;
Kpotufe and Martinet,, 2018
;
Kpotufe and Martinet,, 2021
)
in which conditional probabilities of the labels given the feature vector coincide between the source and target distributions, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(Y^{{\mathrm{sc}}}|X^{{\mathrm{sc}}}=x)=\mathbb{P}(Y^{{\mathrm{tg}}}|X^{{\mathrm{tg}}}=x)
for
(X^{{\mathrm{sc}}},Y^{{\mathrm{sc}}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
and
(X^{{\mathrm{tg}}},Y^{{\mathrm{tg}}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
. Crucially, the Bayes optimal classification rule
\phi
is a function of this conditional probability
x\mapsto\mathbb{P}(Y^{{\mathrm{tg}}}|X^{{\mathrm{tg}}}=x)
. As such, the covariate shift setting allows for consistent classification with respect to the target
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
given only data from the source
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
, provided that the marginal distribution over the target covariates
X^{{\mathrm{tg}}}
is dominated by the corresponding source marginal over
X^{{\mathrm{sc}}}
. The minimax optimal rate for the covariate shift regime depends upon the local scaling behaviour of the ratio between the source and target marginals, characterised by the transfer exponent
(
Kpotufe and Martinet,, 2021
)
, in conjunction with other distributional properties and the sample sizes from both
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
and
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
. More broadly, consistent classification with respect to a target distribution
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
using only data from a distinct source distribution
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
is also possible provided that the conditional probability of a label for the two distributions are sufficiently similar that the Bayes optimal decision boundaries coincide
(
Menon et al.,, 2018
;
Cai and Wei,, 2020
;
Cannings et al.,, 2020
)
. Consistent classification with source data alone is also possible in certain label-noise settings provided that conditional probability of a label for the two distributions are related by an identifiable affine transformation

(
Blanchard et al.,, 2017
;
Reeve and Kabán,, 2019
;
Reeve and Kabán,, 2019
;
Scott,, 2018
;
Scott and Zhang,, 2019
)
.

Learning a classification rule who’s performance approaches the Bayes error inevitably necessitates distributional assumptions. An alternative approach is the Vapnik-Chervonenkis paradigm which instead places constraints on the family of potential classifiers

(
Vapnik and Chervonenkis,, 1974
)
. Interestingly, if we insist that
\phi
be chosen from a low capacity class, then even with the covariate-shift assumption, source data alone is no longer sufficient for effective learning
(
Ben-David et al., 2010b,
)
. Indeed, for certain families of classifier, learning an optimal model is essentially equivalent to learning the entire distribution
(
Hopkins et al.,, 2023
)
. As such, transfer learning within the Vapnik-Chervonenkis paradigm has focused on the role of the discrepancy measures between
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
and
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
which typically depend upon the collection of candidate models
(
Ben-David et al., 2010a,
;
Ben-David et al., 2010b,
;
Mansour et al.,, 2009
;
Mohri and Medina,, 2012
;
Yang et al.,, 2013
;
Cortes et al.,, 2019
)
. A generalised notion of transfer exponent extends to Vapnik-Chervonenkis paradigm by bounding the excess error on the target distribution in terms of the excess error on the source distribution
(
Hanneke and Kpotufe,, 2019
;
Hanneke et al.,, 2023
)
.

A very natural distributional relationship between the source
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
and target
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
distributions is the label-shift setting, where we assume that whilst the marginal probabilities of the class labels may differ
\mathbb{P}(Y^{{\mathrm{sc}}}=y)\neq\mathbb{P}(Y^{{\mathrm{tg}}}=y)
, the class-conditional distributions for the two distributions

\mathbb{P}(X^{{\mathrm{sc}}}=x|Y^{{\mathrm{sc}}}=y)=\mathbb{P}(X^{{\mathrm{tg}}}=x|Y^{{\mathrm{tg}}}=y)
coincide, for
(X^{\mathrm{sc}},Y^{\mathrm{sc}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
,
(X^{\mathrm{tg}},Y^{\mathrm{tg}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
and
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
(see Assumption
1
below for more details)
(
Saerens et al.,, 2002
;
Zhang et al.,, 2013
;
Du Plessis and Sugiyama,, 2014
;
Nguyen et al.,, 2016
;
Lipton et al.,, 2018
;
Azizzadenesheli et al.,, 2019
;
Garg et al.,, 2020
;
Maity et al., 2022b,
;
Wu et al.,, 2021
;
Bai et al.,, 2022
)
. The label-shift assumption is motivated by the multitude of settings in which a covariate-vector corresponding to an observation may be viewed as a causal consequence of the class membership represented by the label
(
Garg et al.,, 2020
)
. A central finding is that the label-shift regime allows for consistent classification with respect to the target distribution
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
in a semi-supervised setting where the practitioner is given a combination of labelled tuples from the source distribution
(X_{i}^{\mathrm{sc}},Y_{i}^{\mathrm{sc}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
, in conjunction with unlabelled covariates from the target distribution
X_{i}^{\mathrm{tg}}
, where
(X_{i}^{\mathrm{tg}},Y_{i}^{\mathrm{tg}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
, but
Y_{i}^{\mathrm{tg}}
is unobserved. Recent work
(
Maity et al., 2022b,
)
has identified the minimax optimal rates for non-parametric classification with label-shift, revealing the dependency upon distributional properties such as smoothness in the aforementioned semi-supervised setting, as well as demonstrating the impact of additional labelled data from the target distribution.

Transfer learning approaches to non-parametric classification have shown the utility of a (typically large) labelled sample from the source distribution
(X_{i}^{\mathrm{sc}},Y_{i}^{\mathrm{sc}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
, in conjunction with smaller labelled sample from the target distribution
(X_{i}^{\mathrm{tg}},Y_{i}^{\mathrm{tg}})\sim{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}

(
Cai and Wei,, 2020
;
Maity et al.,, 2024
)
. In particular, a relatively small amount of additional labelled data from the target distribution allows one to efficiently learn a classifier in settings where there is more complex relationship between the conditional probabilities of the label for the two distributions which may vary over the covariate domain
(
Reeve et al.,, 2021
)
. Statistical approaches to transfer learning have also consider problems beyond the classification setting including high-dimensional linear and generalised regression
(
Li et al., 2023b,
;
Tian and Feng,, 2023
;
Sun and Zhang,, 2023
)
, graphical models
(
Li et al., 2023a,
)
, non-parameteric regression
(
Liu et al.,, 2023
;
Cai and Pu,, 2024
)
, functional mean estimation
(
Cai et al., 2024b,
)
and contextual multi-armed bandits
(
Suk and Kpotufe,, 2021
;
Cai et al., 2024a,
)
.

A key theme in statistical approaches to supervised transfer learning is adaptivity
(
Kpotufe and Martinet,, 2021
;
Cai and Wei,, 2020
;
Reeve et al.,, 2021
)
, particularly with respect to the strength of the relationship between
{P_{\mathrm{sc}}}
and
{P_{\mathrm{tg}}}
. Indeed, let’s suppose we have both a labelled sample from the source and the target distribution. If the source distribution and the target distribution are sufficiently close, in an appropriate sense, then the optimal approach may be to simply run a standard supervised learning algorithm on the combined data set which pools observations from the two samples. On the other hand, if the source and target distributions are completely unrelated then the additional data from the source distribution is without value for the task at hand, and optimal approach may well be to apply a standard supervised learning algorithm on the target data alone. In practice though, we typically won’t have prior knowledge of the strength of the relationship between the source and target distributions. This necessitates careful adaptive approaches which typically build upon the groundbreaking approaches to adaptive estimation of Efroimovich, Pinsker, Goldenshluger and Lepski
(
Efroimovich and Pinsker,, 1984
;
Klemelä and Tsybakov,, 2001
;
Lepski and Spokoiny,, 1997
;
Lepski,, 2022
;
Lepskii,, 1992
;
Goldenshluger and Lepski,, 2013
)
.

Recently, there has been an effort to extend the statistical insights of transfer learning to non-stationary sequential settings
(
Maity et al., 2022a,
;
Wu et al.,, 2021
;
Bai et al.,, 2022
)
. In such settings, the observations arrive sequentially, with each successive observation sampled from time-dependent distribution. The challenge is to effectively utilise historical data to perform contemporary predictions, despite the distributional changes that occur over time. Of particular importance, is a non-stationary semi-supervised classification setting in which one has access to a fixed labelled data set from a source distribution, and then for subsequent rounds the learner observes a covariate-vector
X_{\ell}
to which they are required predict the corresponding class label
Y_{\ell}
, where at each stage the tuple
(X_{\ell},Y_{\ell})\sim P_{\ell}
is drawn from a distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\{0,1\}
. Hence, the learner must make sequential predictions without ever observing the labels
Y_{\ell}
, beyond the fixed labelled data set, and the distribution
P_{\ell}
may change over time. Clearly, distributional assumptions are necessary for any substantive performance guarantees, and a natural assumption is that of label-shift
(
Wu et al.,, 2021
;
Bai et al.,, 2022
)
. Wu et al. explored an approach to this non-stationary label-shift setting
(
Wu et al.,, 2021
)
grounded in the theory of online learning
(
Cesa-Bianchi and Lugosi,, 2006
)
. Notably, without further assumptions on the dynamics of the sequence of distributions, beyond the label-shift assumption, it is demonstrated that since it is possible to leverage the initial sample to obtain a noisy estimate of the contemporary risk, one can effectively apply online gradient descent to provide predictions which perform favourably by comparison with the optimal fixed classification rule. However, the optimal fixed classification rule could still be radically sub-optimal compared to a more dynamic strategy, in a non-stationary setting. This observation motivated Bai et al.
(
Bai et al.,, 2022
)
to introduce a methodology for classification with non-stationary label-shift which provably performs well in comparison with the optimal dynamic policy. The approach of Bai et al. leverages a sophisticated variant of online gradient descent where the learning rate is tuned adaptively via an innovative meta algorithm. Dynamic regret bounds are established which guarantee adaptation to the overall amount of non-stationarity, quantified by a total-variation metric which measures the cumulative changes in the class-label probabilities.

In this work we shall also consider a classification setting with non-stationary label-shift. However, rather than employ techniques from the online learning literature
(
Wu et al.,, 2021
;
Bai et al.,, 2022
)
we shall instead take the view that transfer learning in settings where the strength of the distributional relationship is unknown is, in essence, a problem of adaptive statistical estimation. As such, we shall employ a methodology which draws inspiration from the Lepski-technique for adaptive estimation
(
Lepski and Spokoiny,, 1997
)
and demonstrate the merits of such an approach. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We introduce a methodology for classification with non-stationary label-shift (Section
3
). Our method combines an approach to estimating a transformed density-ratio which doesn’t require that the class-conditional distributions are absolutely continuous (Section
3.1
). We then introduce a Legendre-polynomial based procedure for estimating the current marginal probabilities of the class labels (Section
A
). Notably our procedure is fully adaptive and does not require prior knowledge the distributional properties which characterise the degree of non-stationarity.

•

We provide a high-probability bound on the classification error for a an individual test-time (Theorem
1
). This bound exhibits a favourable dependency of the algorithm upon a range of distributional parameters. This includes both parameters which characterise the recent dynamics of the non-stationary label probabilities in terms of the smoothness level over the recent past (Assumption
2
), and more familiar distributional parameters which characterise the behaviour of the transformed density in terms of smoothness (Assumption
3
) and amount of mass in the vicinity of the optimal decision boundary (Assumption
4
).

•

We then leverage these insights to provide high-probability bounds on the average dynamic regret. For our first average regret bound (Theorem
2
) we leverage a flexible assumption which allows for sporadic jumps, between which the label probabilities vary smoothly with time (Assumption
5
). We also give a high-probability bound on the average dynamic regret which avoids making explicit assumptions on how the label-probabilities vary over time (Corollary
3
). Instead, we bound the average dynamic regret in terms of the of cumulative changes quantified through a total-variation metric (
4
). The algorithms for these bounds coincide, and bounds are attained without prior knowledge of quantities such as the total-variation of the label-probabilities.

Overall, we demonstrate the merits of the adaptive-estimation perspective for sequential non-stationary problems. On the one hand, this approach achieves average regret bounds on the dynamic regret in terms of the total variation of the label-probability changes (Corollary
3
), in the spirit of
(
Bai et al.,, 2022
)
. Moreover, the adaptive methodology also achieves bounds which hold with high-probability for any single test-time (Theorem
1
), which are not implied by bounds on the overall average.

In the next section we shall formally introduce the statistical setting for the remainder of the paper.

2
Statistical setting

We shall begin by considering a transfer learning setting for binary classification in which the learner has access to a fixed data set. Later we shall extend our approach to the sequential setting (Section
5
). Our data consists of three samples
\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{u}
. The sample
\mathcal{D}_{0}
consists
2n_{0}
observations
X^{0}_{0},\ldots,X^{0}_{2n_{0}-1}
drawn independently from a (Borel) distribution
\nu_{0}
on a metric space
\mathcal{X}
with metric
d_{\mathcal{X}}
. The sample
\mathcal{D}_{1}
consists of
2n_{1}
observations
X^{1}_{0},\ldots,X^{1}_{2n_{1}-1}
drawn independently from a distribution
\nu_{1}
on
\mathcal{X}
. It is convenient to assume the sample sizes
2n_{0}
,
2n_{1}
are even to facilitate the discussion of our methodology, which involves sample splitting. We also have a sample
\mathcal{D}_{u}
consisting of
n
observations
X_{0},\ldots,X_{n-1}
which are independent but not necessarily identically distributed. Indeed, we shall assume the existence of a partially observed sequence of independent, but not necessarily identically distributed, random pairs
((X_{\ell},Y_{\ell}))_{\ell=0}^{\infty}
, where for each
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}:=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
we have a covariate
X_{\ell}
, which takes values in
\mathcal{X}
, and a binary label
Y_{\ell}
, which takes values in
\{0,1\}
. The learner’s objective will be to accurately predict the binary label
Y_{{n}}
corresponding a test point
X_{{n}}
.
Later we shall consider a dynamic analogue of this setting in which we wish to classify test points over a series of rounds (Section
5
).

To state our original goal precisely, we let
\mathcal{F}_{n_{0},n_{1},{n}}
denote the set of all data–dependent classifiers of the form
\varphi\equiv\varphi_{{n}}:\mathcal{X}^{2n_{0}+2n_{1}+n+1}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
. Our objective is to choose
\varphi\in\mathcal{F}_{n_{0},n_{1},{n}}
to minimise the test error

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{n}}(\varphi)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\varphi(X_{{n}})\neq Y_{{n}}~|~\mathcal{D}_{0},\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{u}\bigr\},

where we will often suppress the dependence of both
\varphi
and
\mathcal{E}
on the data
\mathcal{D}_{0}
,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
,
\mathcal{D}_{u}
for the sake of notational convenience. We shall also apply the notation
\mathcal{E}_{{n}}(\phi)
to (data independent) classifiers
\phi:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
by viewing such maps as elements of
\mathcal{F}_{n_{0},n_{1},{n}}
which only depend upon their final argument.

Whilst we consider a non-stationary setting, we will require some persistent structure on the distribution of
(X_{\ell},Y_{\ell})
for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We shall adopt a generalisation of the popular
label-shift
assumption
Saerens et al., (2002)
;
Zhang et al., (2013)
;
Du Plessis and Sugiyama, (2014)
;
Nguyen et al., (2016)
;
Lipton et al., (2018)
;
Azizzadenesheli et al., (2019)
;
Garg et al., (2020)
;
Maity et al., 2022b ()
;
Wu et al., (2021)
;
Bai et al., (2022)
.

Assumption 1

(Label-shift)
.

There exists a pair of Borel probability distributions

\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
on
\mathcal{X}
such that for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
, the conditional distribution of
X_{\ell}
given
Y_{\ell}=y
is given by
\nu_{y}
.

Assumption

1
ensures that that the class-conditional distributions
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
are stationary. Hence, the joint distribution of each tuple
(X_{\ell},Y_{\ell})
is governed by
\nu_{1}
,
\nu_{0}
in conjunction with the non-stationary label-probability

\displaystyle\pi_{\ell}:=\mathbb{P}(Y_{\ell}=1).

(1)

In order to predict
Y_{{n}}
we shall require that
\pi_{{n}}
is in some sense “predictable” from
(\pi_{\ell})_{\ell=0}^{{n}-1}
. To make this notion precise we fix
\alpha_{\diamond}\in\mathbb{N}
and for each
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{\diamond}]
,
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
we let
\Sigma(\alpha,\lambda)
denote the Hölder class consisting of all
{p_{\alpha}}:=\max\{\lceil\alpha\rceil-1,0\}
-times differentiable functions
g:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that the
(p_{\alpha})
-th derivative
g^{({p_{\alpha}})}
satisfies

\displaystyle|g^{({p_{\alpha}})}(u_{0})-g^{({p_{\alpha}})}(u_{1})|\leq\lambda\,\left|u_{0}-u_{1}\right|^{\alpha-{p_{\alpha}}},

for all
u_{0}
,
u_{1}\in[0,1]
. Note that
g^{(0)}
refers to the function
g
itself. In particular,
\Sigma(0,\lambda)
denotes the class of all functions
g:[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that
\sup g-\inf g\leq\lambda
.

Assumption 2

(Temporal smoothness)
.

There exists
m\in[{n}]
,
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{\diamond}]
,
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
and
g_{\pi_{t}}\in\Sigma(\alpha,\lambda)
such that for all
\ell\in\{{n}-m,\ldots,{n}\}
,

\displaystyle\pi_{\ell}=g_{\pi_{t}}\bigg(\frac{n-\ell}{m}\bigg).

We shall also assume that the relationship between the class-conditional distributions varies smoothly over the metric space
\mathcal{X}
. To state our assumption we let
\nu_{w}:=(1-w)\nu_{0}+w\nu_{1}
for
w\in(0,1)
and let
\eta:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow[0,1]
denote the Radon–Nikodym derivative
(
Dudley,, 2018
, Section 5.5)

\displaystyle\eta(x):=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\nu_{1}}{d\nu_{1/2}}(x),

for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
, the existence of which is guaranteed by the Radon-Nikodym theorem. Similarly, we define
\overline{\eta}(A):=\nu_{1}(A)/\{2\nu_{1/2}(A)\}
for Borel sets
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. Given
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
r\in[0,\infty)
we let
\mathrm{B}_{r}(x):=\{\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{X}~:~d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\tilde{x})<r\}
and
\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{r}(x):=\{\tilde{x}\in\mathcal{X}~:~d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\tilde{x})\leq r\}
denote, respectively, the open closed metric balls centered at
x
and of radius
r
. We shall make the following assumption on
\eta
.

Assumption 3

(Spatial smoothness)
.

There exists
\beta\in(0,1]
,
\theta\in[0,\infty)
such that for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
and all
r>0
we have,

\displaystyle\bigl|\eta(x)-\overline{\eta}\bigl\{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{r}(x)\bigr\}\bigr|\leq\theta\,\nu_{1/2}\bigl\{\mathrm{B}_{r}(x)\bigr\}^{\beta}.

Assumption
3
is an analogue of the measure-theoretic notion smoothness of Chaudhuri and Dasgupta
Chaudhuri and Dasgupta, (2014)
and is relatively mild. For example, suppose that
\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the Lebesgue absolutely continuous component of
\nu_{0}+\nu_{1}
has a density bounded from below on its support
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{0}+\nu_{1})
, which is sufficiently well behaved (regular in the sense of
(
Audibert,, 2004
, (2.1))
). Suppose further that
\eta
is
\beta_{H}
-Hölder continuous with respect to the Euclidean norm. Then Assumption
3
holds with
\beta=\beta_{H}/d
. More broadly though, Assumption
3
does not require that
\mathrm{supp}(\nu_{0}+\nu_{1})
is bounded. Furthermore, Assumption
3
does not require that the individual class-conditional densities have a density bounded below, in contrast to
Maity et al., 2022b ()
.

Finally, we shall utilise a margin assumption in the spirit of
Mammen and Tsybakov, (1999)
. Let’s write
\mu_{{n}}
for the distribution of
X_{n}
, so that

\displaystyle\mu_{{n}}=\nu_{{\pi_{{n}}}}=(1-\pi_{{n}})\nu_{0}+\pi_{{n}}\nu_{1}.

(2)

Assumption 4

(Tysbakov margin)
.

There exists a function
\gamma:(0,\infty)\rightarrow(0,1]
which is non-decreasing with
\gamma(1)=1
, and for all
\zeta\in(0,1]
we have

\displaystyle\nu_{1/2}\bigl(\bigl\{x\in\mathcal{X}:0<|\eta(x)-1-\pi_{{n}}|<\zeta\bigr\}\bigr)\leq\gamma(\zeta)/\zeta.

Assumptions
4
is best understood in the context of the following lemma.

Lemma 1
.

Suppose
\varphi:\mathcal{X}^{2n_{0}+2n_{1}+{n}}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is a classifier. Then,

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{{n}}(\varphi)=\pi_{{n}}+2\int\varphi\,(1-\pi_{{n}}-\eta)\,d\nu_{1/2}.

Hence, letting
\varphi_{{n}}^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
be the classifier defined by
\varphi_{{n}}^{\star}(x):=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\eta(x)>1-\pi_{{n}}\right\}}
for
x\in\mathcal{X}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{n}(\varphi)=\mathcal{E}_{n}(\varphi_{{n}}^{\star})+2\int_{\{\varphi\neq\varphi_{{n}}^{\star}\}}|1-\pi_{{n}}-\eta|\,d\nu_{1/2}.

Hence,
\varphi_{{n}}^{\star}
is the Bayes optimal classifier at time
{n}
and Assumption
4
bounds the amount of mass in the vicinity of the Bayes optimal decision boundary. Lemma
1
will also serve as the starting point for our methodology.

An important special case of Assumption
4
is the polynomial setting where we may take a function of the form
\gamma_{\kappa,\psi}:(0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,1]
by
\gamma_{\kappa,\psi}(z):=1\wedge\left(\psi z^{\kappa}\right)
, in place of
\gamma
. Notice that Assumption
4
always holds with
\gamma=\gamma_{1,1}
. However, we can often choose
\gamma
so that
\lim_{\zeta\searrow 0}\gamma(\zeta)/\zeta=0
. For example, if
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\nu_{0}
and
\nu_{1}
have bounded Lebesgue density, and
\eta
is smooth with a uniform lower bound on
\|\nabla\eta\|
within a bounded neighbourhood of the
(d-1)
-dimensional manifold
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\>\,\eta(x)=1-\pi_{{n}}\}
, then for a sufficiently large constant
\psi\geq 1
, Assumption
4
will hold with
\gamma=\gamma_{2,\psi}
. As we shall see, in Theorem
1
, such situations will allow for sharper bounds on the excess classification error.

\displaystyle\overline{\log}(z):=\begin{cases}\log(z)&\text{ if }z>e\\

3
Methodology

Our starting point is Lemma
1
which verifies that the Bayes optimal classifier
\varphi_{{n}}^{\star}:\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
is given by

\varphi_{{n}}^{\star}(x):=\mathbbm{1}{\left\{\eta(x)+\pi_{{n}}>1\right\}},

for
x\in\mathcal{X}
. This motivates a plug-in strategy whereby we:

(1) Obtain an estimate
\hat{\eta}_{\delta}
for
\eta
;

(2) Obtain an estimate
\hat{\pi}_{{n}}
of
\pi_{{n}}
;

(3) Define
\varphi\equiv\varphi_{{n}}
by
\varphi(x):=\mathbbm{1}\left\{\hat{\eta}_{\delta}(x)>1-\hat{\pi}_{{n}}\right\}
.

We shall address (1) and (2) in subsections
3.1
and
3.2
, respectively.

3.1
Estimating the transformed density ratio

In this section we describe our methodology for estimating the transformed density ratio
\eta
. Estimating the density-ratio is an important topic in itself
(
Sugiyama et al.,, 2012
)
. However, by estimating the transformed density-ration
\eta
, rather than the density-ratio itself we require less stringent assumptions on the class-conditional distributions. In particular, the transformed density ratio
\eta
may be well-behaved even when the density ratio itself does not exist.

Our starting point is to utilise local confidence intervals for the class-conditional distributions with employ local Dvoretzky–Kiefer–Wolfowitz–Massart–type concentration inequalities
Reeve, (2024)
.

Given
q\in[0,1]
and
{\tilde{\varepsilon}}>0
we define

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{U}}_{\flat}(q,{\tilde{\varepsilon}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{8\bigl(\sqrt{{\tilde{\varepsilon}}\sigma^{2}(q)+{\tilde{\varepsilon}}^{2}}+\{1-2q\}{\tilde{\varepsilon}}\bigr)}{3(1+2{\tilde{\varepsilon}})},

Given
\tilde{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
we define
\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(q,\tilde{n},\delta):=\widehat{\mathrm{U}}_{\flat}\left(q,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\tilde{n})\right)\vee\left({1}/{\tilde{n}}\right)
. Next, for each
y\in\{0,1\}
we let
\hat{\nu}_{y}
be the estimate of
\nu_{y}
defined by

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{y}(A):=\frac{1}{n_{y}}\sum_{i=0}^{n_{y}-1}\mathbbm{1}_{\{X^{y}_{i}\in A\}}

for Borel sets
A\subseteq\mathcal{X}
. Further more, for
\omega\in\{-1,1\}
and
\delta\in(0,1)
we let

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{y,\omega,\delta}(A):=\begin{cases}\hat{\nu}_{y}(A)-\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(1-\hat{\nu}_{y}(A),n_{y},\delta)&\text{ if }\omega=-1\\
\hat{\nu}_{y}(A)+\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(\hat{\nu}_{y}(A),n_{y},\delta)&\text{ if }\omega=1.\end{cases}

Furthermore, for
\omega\in\{-1,1\}
we let

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}_{\omega,\delta}(A):=\biggl(\biggl(\frac{\hat{\nu}_{1,\omega,\delta}(A)}{\hat{\nu}_{0,-\omega,\delta}(A)+\hat{\nu}_{1,\omega,\delta}(A)}\biggr)\vee 0\biggr)\wedge 1,

whenever
\hat{\nu}_{0,-\omega,\delta}(A)+\hat{\nu}_{1,\omega,\delta}(A)>0
and let
\hat{\eta}_{\omega,\delta}(A):=(\omega+1)/2
otherwise. We also define,

\displaystyle\hat{\eta}^{\mathrm{mid}}_{\delta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\hat{\eta}_{-1,\delta}(A)+\hat{\eta}_{1,\delta}(A)\right\}

\displaystyle\hat{W}_{\delta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\hat{\eta}_{1,\delta}(A)-\hat{\eta}_{-1,\delta}(A)

\displaystyle\hat{I}_{\delta}(A)

\displaystyle:=\left[\hat{\eta}^{\mathrm{mid}}_{\delta}(A)-\hat{W}_{\delta}(A),\hat{\eta}^{\mathrm{mid}}_{\delta}(A)+\hat{W}_{\delta}(A)\right].

For each
x\in\mathcal{X}
we let

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{dist}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x):=\bigcup_{y\in\{0,1\}}\bigl\{d_{\mathcal{X}}(x,X_{\ell}^{y}):\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\cap[0,n_{y}-1]\bigr\}.

Next, we define
\widehat{\mathrm{rad}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\delta)
to be the maximal value of
r\in\widehat{\mathrm{dist}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)
such that

\displaystyle\bigcap_{\tilde{r}\in\widehat{\mathrm{dist}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x)\,:\,\tilde{r}\leq r}\hat{I}_{\delta}\bigl\{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\tilde{r}}(x)\bigr\}\neq\emptyset.

Finally, we define
\hat{\eta}_{\delta}(x):=\hat{\eta}^{\mathrm{mid}}_{\delta}\bigl\{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{\widehat{\mathrm{rad}}_{\mathcal{X}}(x,\delta)}(x)\bigr\}
as our estimate of
\eta(x)
.

In Lemma
6
we shall show that, provided Assumption
3
holds, for each
x\in\mathcal{X}
the following bound holds with high probability,

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\eta}_{\delta}(x)-\eta(x)\right|\leq 14\left(\left\{\theta^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\breve{n})\right\}^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\vee\sqrt{\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\breve{n})}\right),

where
\breve{n}:=n_{0}\wedge n_{1}
. Moreover, this point-wise bound will be shown to entail a corresponding high-probability simultaneously bound for a set of large measure (Proposition

For

\Delta_{\tau,\kappa,\delta}^{\pi,\alpha}(r,0)=\sqrt{{\mathrm{I}_{{\kappa}/{2}}\hskip-1.42262pt\left(r\right)\varepsilon_{{\delta}/{\tau}}(r)}}/{\mathbb{V}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})}.

Our bound for the setting with smoothly varying labels with jumps is as follows.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\mathbb{T}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a time interval with
\max\mathbb{T}=\tau\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose Assumption
1
holds, Assumption
3
holds with
\beta\in(0,1]
,
\theta\in[0,\infty)
, Assumption
5
holds with
\mathrm{T}=|\mathbb{T}|
,
\mathrm{J}\in[\mathrm{T}]
,
\alpha\in[0,\alpha_{\diamond}]
and
\lambda\in[0,\infty)
, and Assumption
6
holds with
\kappa\in[1,2+1/\alpha]
,
\psi\in[1,\infty)
. There exists a constant
\mathrm{C}_{+}\geq 1
, depending only on
\alpha_{\diamond}
and
\kappa
such that for all
\delta\in(0,\mathbb{V}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})/\mathrm{C}_{+}]
and
\breve{n}=\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}
with
\theta\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\breve{n})^{\beta}\leq\left\{\mathbb{V}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})/\mathrm{C}_{+}\right\}^{2\beta+1}
then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}}(\bm{\varphi})\leq\psi\,\mathrm{C}_{+}\bigl\{\Delta_{\delta}^{\eta}(\breve{n})\vee\Delta_{\tau,\kappa,\delta}^{\pi,\alpha}(\mathrm{T}/\mathrm{J},\lambda)\bigr\}^{\kappa}+\mathrm{C}_{+}\delta.

To interpret Theorem

let’s view

and

as a constant and take

and

. Then Theorem

yields a bound of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}}(\bm{\varphi})\right\}\leq\tilde{O}\left(\Delta_{\delta}^{\eta}(\breve{n})^{\kappa}+\left\{\lambda+\sqrt{\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{T}}}\right\}^{\frac{\kappa}{2\alpha+1}}\left\{\frac{\mathrm{J}}{\mathrm{T}}\right\}^{\frac{\kappa\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\right).

As an alternative to making assumptions on the behaviour of the sequence of label-probabilities
(\pi_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
we can instead introduce a metric on the overall level of change over a given time interval. Given
\alpha\in[1,\infty)
we let

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}(\pi):=\left(\sum_{\ell=\min\mathbb{T}}^{\max\mathbb{T}-1}|\pi_{\ell}-\pi_{\ell+1}|^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha},

(4)

where we suppress the dependence upon
\mathrm{T}
for notational convenience. When
\alpha=1

\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}(\pi)
corresponds precisely to the total variation measure explored by Bai et al.
Bai et al., (2022)
. For larger values of
\alpha

\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}(\pi)
places an increasing penalty on sequences with numerous small changes, rather than a small number of jumps. Note that for
\alpha>1
, the quantity
\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}(\pi)
does not correspond to a norm on the sequence
(\pi_{\ell}-\pi_{\ell+1})_{\ell}
since the function
(z_{\ell})_{\ell}\mapsto\left(\sum_{\ell}|z_{\ell}|^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\right)^{\alpha}
is not sub-additive.

Corollary 3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}\subseteq\mathbb{N}
be a time interval with
\max\mathbb{T}=\tau\in\mathbb{N}
. Suppose Assumption
1
holds, Assumption
3
holds with
\beta\in(0,1]
,
\theta\in[0,\infty)
and Assumption
6
holds with
\kappa\in[1,\infty)
and
\psi\in[1,\infty)
. There exists a constant
\mathrm{C}_{+}\geq 1
, depending only on
\kappa
, such that for all
\delta\in(0,\mathbb{V}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})/\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{v}}]
and
\breve{n}=\min\{n_{0},n_{1}\}
with
\theta\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\breve{n})^{\beta}\leq\left\{\mathbb{V}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})/\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{v}}\right\}^{2\beta+1}
then with probability at least
1-\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}}(\bm{\varphi})\leq\psi\,\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{v}}\bigl\{\Delta_{\delta}^{\eta}(\breve{n})\vee\Delta_{\tau,\kappa,\delta}^{\pi,\alpha}(\mathrm{T},\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}(\pi))\bigr\}^{\kappa}+\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{v}}\delta.

To interpret Corollary
3
let’s again view
\psi
and
\mathbb{V}(\nu_{0},\nu_{1})
as a constant and take
\alpha\in[1,2]
and
\delta=1/\breve{n}
. Then Corollary
3
yields a bound of the form

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\{\mathcal{R}_{\mathbb{T}}(\bm{\varphi})\right\}\leq\tilde{O}\left(\Delta_{\delta}^{\eta}(\breve{n})^{\kappa}+\left\{\mathbb{V}_{\alpha}(\pi)+\mathrm{T}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\}^{\frac{\kappa}{2\alpha+1}}\,\mathrm{T}^{-\frac{\kappa\alpha}{2\alpha+1}}\right).

This recovers the bound of
Bai et al., (2022)
for the important special case of

and
\kappa=1
.

The goal of this section is to give a high-level overview of the proof structure for the high-probability regret bounds in Theorems

6.1
The transformed density ratio estimator

We shall leverage our regulartiy assumption on the transformed regression function

Recall that
\breve{n}=n_{0}\wedge n_{1}

\displaystyle\left|\hat{\eta}_{\delta}(x)-\eta(x)\right|\leq 14\left(\left\{\theta^{\frac{1}{\beta}}\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\breve{n})\right\}^{\frac{\beta}{2\beta+1}}\vee\sqrt{\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\breve{n})}\right).

Proposition 4

Suppose Assumption

6.2

Next, we turn to our performance bounds for estimating the label probabilities. First we consider our estimator

Our bound on the estimation error of

Proposition 5

Suppose that Assumptions

and

yields the following corollary on estimating the label probabilities.

Suppose that we are in the setting of Proposition

7
Confidence intervals for the class-conditional distributions

Recall the definitions of
\widehat{\mathrm{U}}_{\flat}
,
\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\tilde{n})
and
\widehat{\mathrm{U}}
from Section
3.1
. Furthermore, given
p\in[0,1]
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}>0
we define

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}_{\flat}(p,\tilde{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle:=\frac{8\bigl(\sqrt{9\varepsilon\sigma^{2}(p)+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{2}}+\{1-2p\}\tilde{\varepsilon}\bigr)}{9+2\tilde{\varepsilon}}.

For
p\in[0,1]
,
\tilde{n}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta\in(0,1]
with
\log(\tilde{n})\geq e\delta
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{U}(p,\tilde{n},\delta)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{U}_{\flat}(p,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\tilde{n}))\vee(1/\tilde{n}),

\displaystyle\mathrm{CI}(p,\tilde{n},\delta)

\displaystyle:=\bigl[p-\mathrm{U}(1-p,\tilde{n},\delta),p+\mathrm{U}(p,\tilde{n},\delta)\bigr],

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}(q,\tilde{n},\delta)

\displaystyle:=\bigl[q-\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(1-q,\tilde{n},\delta),q+\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(q,\tilde{n},\delta)\bigr].

Given
x\in\mathcal{X}
we let
\mathcal{B}(x):=\bigl\{\overline{\mathrm{B}}_{r}(x)\bigr\}_{r\geq 0}
and for each
y\in\{0,1\}
we define events

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{B\in\mathcal{B}(x)}\biggl\{\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)\in\mathrm{CI}\bigl(\nu_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta\bigr)\biggr\},

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)

\displaystyle:=\bigcap_{B\in\mathcal{B}(x)}\biggl\{\nu_{y}(B)\in\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}\bigl(\hat{\nu}_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta\bigr)\biggr\}.

Lemma 2
.

Suppose that
\delta\in(0,1)
,
\tilde{n}\in\mathbb{N}
and
q,p\in[0,1]
satisfy
\log(\tilde{n})\geq e\delta
and
q-p\leq\mathrm{U}(p,\tilde{n},\delta)
. Then
q-p\leq\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(1-q,\tilde{n},\delta)
.

Proof.

Since
q-p\leq\mathrm{U}(p,\tilde{n},\underline{\delta})
we either have
q-p\leq 1/\tilde{n}
or
q-p>1/\tilde{n}
. In the former case the consequence
q-p\leq 1/\tilde{n}\leq\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(1-q,\tilde{n},\delta)
is immediate. Henceforth, we shall focus on the latter case. Since
1/\tilde{n}<q-p\leq\mathrm{U}(p,\tilde{n},\underline{\delta})=\max\{1/\tilde{n},\mathrm{U}_{\flat}(p,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\tilde{n}))\}
we have
q-p\leq\mathrm{U}_{\flat}(p,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\tilde{n}))
. By rearranging it follows that
q-p\leq\widehat{\mathrm{U}}_{\flat}(1-q,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(\tilde{n}))=\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(1-q,\tilde{n},\delta)
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Given
\delta\in(0,1)
,
y\in\{0,1\}
and
n_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
\log(n_{y})\geq e\delta
. Then
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)
for all
x\in\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)
holds for some
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
, and take
B\in\mathcal{B}(x)
so that
\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)\in\mathrm{CI}(\nu_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta)
. Hence, we have

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)-\nu_{y}(B)\leq\mathrm{U}(\nu_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta),

so that applying Lemma
2
with
p=\nu_{y}(B)
and
q=\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)
yields

\displaystyle\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)-\nu_{y}(B)\leq\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(1-\hat{\nu}_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta).

Similarly, by
\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)\in\mathrm{CI}(\nu_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta)
we also have

\displaystyle\bigl\{1-\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)\bigr\}-\bigl\{1-\nu_{y}(B)\bigr\}\leq\mathrm{U}(1-\nu_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta),

so that applying Lemma
2
with
p=1-\nu_{y}(B)
and
q=1-\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)
yields
\nu_{y}(B)-\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)\leq\widehat{\mathrm{U}}(\hat{\nu}_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta)
. Thus, we have
\nu_{y}(B)\in\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}(\hat{\nu}_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta)
. Since this holds for all
B\in\mathcal{B}(x)
we have
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)\subseteq\widehat{\mathcal{A}}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Suppose
y\in\{0,1\}
,
n_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
,
\delta\in(0,1]
satisfy
\log(n_{y})\geq e\delta
and let
\underline{\delta}_{y}:=4\lceil\log_{4/3}(n_{y})\rceil\{\delta/\log(n_{y})\}^{2}

\mathbb{P}\bigl\{\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{CI},y}(x,\delta)\bigr\}\geq 1-\underline{\delta}_{y}
.

Proof.

The definition of
\underline{\delta}_{y}
entails

\displaystyle\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(n_{y})

\displaystyle=\log(\log(n_{y})/\delta)/n_{y}=\log\bigl(4\lceil\log_{4/3}(n_{y})\rceil/\underline{\delta}_{y}\bigr)/(2n_{y}).

The result now follows from two applications of
(
Reeve,, 2024
, Corollary 3)
combined with a union bound.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Suppose
y\in\{0,1\}
,
\delta\in(0,1]
,
x\in\mathcal{X}
and
n_{y}\in\mathbb{N}
satisfy
\log(n_{y})\geq e\delta
. Then, for all

with

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\bigl\{\widehat{\mathrm{CI}}\bigl(\hat{\nu}_{y}(B),n_{y},\delta\bigr)\bigr\}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{16}{3}\sqrt{p\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(n_{y})}+20\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(n_{y}).

Proof.

Let’s suppose that the event

\displaystyle q

\displaystyle:=\min\{\hat{\nu}_{y}(B),1-\hat{\nu}_{y}(B)\}\leq p+\mathrm{U}_{\flat}(p,\varepsilon^{\mathrm{il}}_{\delta}(n_{y}))\vee\biggl(\frac{1}{n_{y}}\biggr)

∎

Lemma 9
.

Suppose that Assumptions

and
2
hold with

,

,

Proof of Lemma
9
.

By Assumption
2
there exists
g_{\pi_{t}}\in\Sigma(\alpha,\lambda)
such that
\pi_{\ell}=g_{\pi_{t}}({n-\ell+1}/{m})
for all
\ell\in\{{n}-m,\ldots,{n}\}
. Let’s write
h:[0,1]\rightarrow[0,1]
for the function defined by

\displaystyle h(u):=\nu_{0}(f)+\big\{\nu_{1}(f)-\nu_{0}(f)\big\}g_{\pi_{t}}(u),

for
u\in[0,1]
. Recall that

\overline{\lambda}_{f}:=\lambda\big|\nu_{1}(f)-\nu_{0}(f)\big|/\{p_{\alpha}!\,\sqrt{2\alpha+1}\}
. It follows that
h\in\Sigma(\alpha,\overline{\lambda}_{f}\{p_{\alpha}!\,\sqrt{2\alpha+1}\})
and so
h
is
p_{\alpha}
-times differentiable. Now suppose
p_{\alpha}>0
. Then, by Taylor’s theorem for each
u\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle h\left(u\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{p_{\alpha}-1}\frac{h^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\cdot u^{k}+\int_{0}^{u}\frac{h^{(p_{\alpha})}(\xi)}{(p_{\alpha}-1)!}\cdot\xi^{p_{\alpha}-1}d\xi.

Thus, for each
u\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\bigg|h\left(u\right)-\sum_{k=0}^{p_{\alpha}}\frac{h^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\cdot u^{k}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{0}^{u}\frac{h^{(p_{\alpha})}(\xi)}{(p_{\alpha}-1)!}\cdot\xi^{p_{\alpha}-1}d\xi-\frac{h^{(p_{\alpha})}(0)}{p_{\alpha}!}\cdot u^{p_{\alpha}}\bigg|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{({p_{\alpha}}-1)!}\int_{0}^{u}\big|h^{({p_{\alpha}})}(\xi)-h^{({p_{\alpha}})}(0)\big|\xi^{{p_{\alpha}}-1}d\xi

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\overline{\lambda}\,u^{\alpha-{p_{\alpha}}}}{({p_{\alpha}}-1)!}\int_{0}^{u}\xi^{{p_{\alpha}}-1}d\xi=\overline{\lambda}_{f}\sqrt{2\alpha+1}\ \,u^{\alpha}.

On the other hand, if
p_{\alpha}=0
, then for
u\in[0,1]
, the bound

\displaystyle\bigg|h\left(u\right)-\sum_{k=0}^{p_{\alpha}}\frac{h^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\cdot u^{k}\bigg|\leq\overline{\lambda}_{f}\sqrt{2\alpha+1}\ \,u^{\alpha},

follows immediately from
h\in\Sigma(\alpha,\overline{\lambda}_{f}\{p_{\alpha}!\,\sqrt{2\alpha+1}\})
.

The polynomials

). Hence, the collection of polynomials of the form

, and as such form a basis of the
({p_{\alpha}}+1)
-dimensional vector space consisting of polynomials of degree at most
p
. Thus, we may choose

such that
a_{j}=0
for all

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{p_{\alpha}}\frac{h^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\cdot u^{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{p_{\alpha}+1}a_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j-1}\left(\frac{mu}{q}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{p(q)+1}a_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j-1}\left(\frac{mu}{q}\right),

for all
u\in[0,1]
. Note also that for

\displaystyle h\bigg(\frac{i}{m}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\nu_{0}(f)+\big\{\nu_{1}(f)-\nu_{0}(f)\big\}g_{\pi_{t}}\bigg(\frac{i}{m}\bigg)=\nu_{0}(f)+\big\{\nu_{1}(f)-\nu_{0}(f)\big\}\pi_{t-i}=\mu_{t-i}(f).

Thus, with
M\equiv M(q)
we have

\displaystyle\big|\mu_{t-i}(f)-\left(M_{i,:}\right)a\big|

\displaystyle=\bigg|h\bigg(\frac{i}{m}\bigg)-\sum_{k=0}^{p(q)}a_{j}\mathcal{L}_{j-1}\bigg(\frac{i}{q}\bigg)\bigg|

\displaystyle=\bigg|h\bigg(\frac{i}{m}\bigg)-\sum_{k=0}^{p(q)}\frac{h^{(k)}(0)}{k!}\cdot\bigg(\frac{i}{m}\bigg)^{k}\bigg|\leq\overline{\lambda}_{f}\sqrt{2\alpha+1}\ \,u^{\alpha}\,\bigg(\frac{i}{m}\bigg)^{\alpha},

for all

and
\mu_{t}(f)=\left(M_{0,:}\right)a
. Note also that the choice of

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{q}w(i,q)M_{i,:}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{q}M_{0,:}\bigl({M}^{\top}{M}\bigr)^{+}{{M_{i,:}}}^{\top}M_{i,:}

\displaystyle\bigg|

Since

∎

Lemma 18
.

The polynomials
(\mathcal{L}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are orthonormal with respect to the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle:=\int_{0}^{1}f(z)g(z)dz
. Moreover, for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
z\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}_{k}(z)|\leq\sqrt{2k+1}\hskip 14.22636pt\text{ and }\hskip 14.22636pt|\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\prime}(z)|\leq 2k^{2}\sqrt{2k+1}.

Proof of Lemma
18
.

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}
, lets write
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}
for the standard Legendre polynomial of degree
k
, defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(z):=\frac{1}{2^{k}k!}\frac{d^{k}}{dz^{k}}\{z^{2}-1\}^{k},

for
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\equiv 1
. Note that for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{k}(z)=\sqrt{2k+1}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(2z-1),

(15)

for all
z\in[0,1]
. Hence, by
(
Kaplan,, 2003
, Theorem 17(i))
we have
\sup_{z\in[-1,1]}|\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}(z)|\leq 1
. Thus, by the Markov brother’s inequality
Shadrin, (2004)
we have
\sup_{z\in[-1,1]}|\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k}^{\prime}(z)|\leq k^{2}
. Consequently, we have
\sup_{z\in[0,1]}|\mathcal{L}_{k}(z)|\leq\sqrt{2k+1}
and
\sup_{z\in[0,1]}|\mathcal{L}_{k}^{\prime}(z)|\leq 2k^{2}\sqrt{2k+1}
. Moreover, by
(
Kaplan,, 2003
, Theorem 18)
we have

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k_{0}}(z)\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{k_{1}}(z)dz=\begin{cases}\frac{2}{2k_{0}+1}&\text{ if }k_{0}=k_{1}\\
0&\text{ if }k_{0}\neq k_{1}.\end{cases}

for any
k_{0},~k_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Hence, by (
15
) the polynomials
(\mathcal{L}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are orthonormal with respect to the inner product
\left\langle f,g\right\rangle:=\int_{0}^{1}f(z)g(z)dz
.
∎
