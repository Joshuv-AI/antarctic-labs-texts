The spin- 1 2 \frac{1}{2} Heisenberg ferromagnet on the pyrochlore lattice: A Green’s function study
T. Hutak ? {}^{\textsf{\footnotesize{\mbox{?}}}}, P. Müller ? {}^{\textsf{\footnotesize{\mbox{?}}}}, J. Richter ? , ?, T. Krokhmalskii ? {}^{\textsf{\footnotesize{\mbox{?}}}}, O. Derzhko ? , ?

Abstract
We consider the pyrochlore-lattice quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet and discuss the properties of this spin model at arbitrary temperatures. To this end, we use the Green’s function technique within the random-phase (or Tyablikov) approximation as well as the linear spin-wave theory and quantum Monte Carlo simulations. We compare our results to the ones obtained recently by other methods to corroborate our findings. Finally, we contrast our results with the ones for the simple-cubic-lattice case: both lattices are identical at the mean-field level. We demonstrate that thermal fluctuations are more efficient in the pyrochlore case (finite-temperature frustration effects). Our results may be of use for interpreting experimental data for ferromagnetic pyrochlore materials. Key words: quantum spin system, Heisenberg model, pyrochlore lattice, Green’s functions, random-phase approximation PACS: 75.10.-b, 75.10.Jm

1
Introduction

One of the standard three-dimensional models for a study of geometrically frustrated spin systems
is the pyrochlore Heisenberg antiferromagnet
[
15
,
16
]
.
It is known that the nearest-neighbor exchange interactions, because of the lattice geometry
(a three-dimensional network of corner-sharing tetrahedra),
cannot establish magnetic ordering even at zero temperature
T=0
.
Besides the purely theoretical interest in the spin-system physics of this celebrated model,
there are various real materials
which can be described with the help of the antiferromagnetic Heisenberg model on the pyrochlore lattice
[
17
,
18
]
.

Less attention has been paid to the pyrochlore-lattice Heisenberg
ferromagnet
.
Clearly, the set of the eigenvalues of the Heisenberg Hamiltonian (energy levels) does not depend on the sign of the exchange interaction.
However, the ordering of these energy levels is different for the antiferromagnetic and ferromagnetic signs of the exchange interaction.
As a result,
the low-energy levels for the pyrochlore-lattice Heisenberg antiferromagnet
are the high-energy levels for the pyrochlore-lattice Heisenberg ferromagnet
and they may manifest themselves only in finite-temperature thermodynamics,
when due to thermal fluctuations they can come into play.
Several systematic theoretical studies
[
19
,
20
]
demonstrated that this is really the case.
For example,
it was found
that the Curie temperature for the pyrochlore lattice is lower than for the simple-cubic lattice
[
21
,
19
,
20
]
,
although both lattices have the same number of nearest neighbors and thus are identical at the mean-field level.

From the experimental side,
there is a number of compounds,
the magnetic properties of which can be described using the pyrochlore-lattice quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet

[
22
,
23
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
]
.

In what follows,
we first introduce the model and present the corresponding linear spin-wave theory (section
2
).
In section
3
, we describe the Green’s function approach to the model
and in section
4
we discuss the low- and intermediate-temperature properties of the model making connections to previous studies.
In section

2
The model and the linear spin-wave theory

We begin with a brief description of the model.
The three-dimensional pyrochlore lattice can be viewed as four interpenetrating face-centered-cubic sublattices.
We set the edge length of the cubic cell of each face-centered-cubic sublattice to unity.
Furthermore,
we denote the origins of these face-centered-cubic sublattices as

{\bf{r}}_{1}=(0,0,0)
,

{\bf{r}}_{2}=(0,\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4})
,

{\bf{r}}_{3}=(\frac{1}{4},0,\frac{1}{4})
,
and

{\bf{r}}_{4}=(\frac{1}{4},\frac{1}{4},0)
.
For each face-centered-cubic sublattice, the position of its lattice sites is given by three integers
m_{1}
,
m_{2}
, and
m_{3}
,
i.e.,
by
{\bf{R}}_{m}=m_{1}{\bf{e}}_{1}+m_{2}{\bf{e}}_{2}+m_{3}{\bf{e}}_{3}

with

{\bf{e}}_{1}=(0,\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2})
,

{\bf{e}}_{2}=(\frac{1}{2},0,\frac{1}{2})
,
and

{\bf{e}}_{3}=(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2},0)
,
see
[
28
,
19
]
.
As a result,
the lattice sites of the
N
-site pyrochlore lattice are given by

{\bf{R}}_{m;\alpha}={\bf{R}}_{m}+{\bf{r}}_{\alpha}
,
where
m=1,\ldots,{\cal{N}}
,
{\cal{N}}=\frac{1}{4}N
and
\alpha=1,2,3,4
.

We consider the spin-
\frac{1}{2}
isotropic Heisenberg model.
Its Hamiltonian has the following form:

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=J\sum_{\langle m;\alpha,n;\beta\rangle}{\bf{s}}_{m;\alpha}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{n;\beta}

\displaystyle=J\sum_{m}\left({\bf{s}}_{m;1}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{m;2}+{\bf{s}}_{m;1}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{m;3}+{\bf{s}}_{m;1}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{m;4}+{\bf{s}}_{m;2}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{m;3}+{\bf{s}}_{m;2}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{m;4}+{\bf{s}}_{m;3}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{m;4}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+{\bf{s}}_{m;1}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{{m_{1}-1,m_{2},m_{3};2}}+{\bf{s}}_{m;1}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{{m_{1},m_{2}-1,m_{3};3}}+{\bf{s}}_{m;1}\cdot{\bf{s}}_{{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}-1;4}}\right.

\displaystyle\left.+{\bf{s}}_{m_{1}-1,m_{2},m_{3};2}\!\cdot\!{\bf{s}}_{{m_{1},m_{2}-1,m_{3};3}}\!+\!{\bf{s}}_{m_{1}-1,m_{2},m_{3};2}\!\cdot\!{\bf{s}}_{{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}-1;4}}\!+\!{\bf{s}}_{m_{1},m_{2}-1,m_{3};3}\!\cdot\!{\bf{s}}_{{m_{1},m_{2},m_{3}-1;4}}\right),

(2.1)

where the first sum in equation (
2.1
) runs over all nearest neighbors
m;\alpha
and
n;\beta
,

J=-|J|<0
is the ferromagnetic exchange interaction constant,
and
the sum in the second line in equation (
2.1
) runs over all
m=(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})
,
m_{a}=1,\ldots,{\cal{L}}_{a}
(
a=1,2,3
),
{\cal{L}}_{1}{\cal{L}}_{2}{\cal{L}}_{3}={\cal{N}}
.
The spin operators at different sites commute and satisfy the following commutation relations at the same site:

\displaystyle\left[s^{+},s^{-}\right]=2s^{z},\qquad\left[s^{\pm},s^{z}\right]=\mp s^{\pm},

(2.2)

where
s^{\pm}=s^{x}\pm{\rm{i}}s^{y}
.
We have assumed
\hbar=1
for brevity.

We begin with a brief description of the linear spin-wave theory which should be valid in the low-temperature limit.
Using the Holstein-Primakoff transformation

\displaystyle s^{+}=\sqrt{2s}\sqrt{1-\frac{a^{\dagger}a}{2s}}a\approx\sqrt{2s}a,\qquad s^{-}=\sqrt{2s}a^{\dagger}\sqrt{1-\frac{a^{\dagger}a}{2s}}\approx\sqrt{2s}a^{\dagger},\qquad s^{z}=s-a^{\dagger}a,

(2.3)

we introduce the bosonic operators
a_{m;\alpha}
(and
a^{\dagger}_{m;\alpha}
) with
\alpha=1,2,3,4
and
m=1,\ldots,{\cal{N}}
.
Furthermore,
we introduce the bosonic operators

\displaystyle a_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal{N}}}}\sum_{m}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\rm{i}}{\bf{q}}\cdot{\bf{R}}_{m}}a_{m;\alpha}\,,

(2.4)

where
{\bf{q}}\cdot{\bf{R}}_{m}=q_{1}m_{1}+q_{2}m_{2}+q_{3}m_{3}

and
q_{a}=2\piup z_{a}/{\cal{L}}_{a}
,
z_{a}=1,\ldots,{\cal{L}}_{a}
(
a=1,2,3
).
[Note that
q_{1}=\frac{1}{2}(q_{y}+q_{z})
,
q_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(q_{x}+q_{z})
, and
q_{3}=\frac{1}{2}(q_{x}+q_{y})
.]
Then, the spin Hamiltonian given in equation (
2.1
) can be approximately rewritten in the following form:

\displaystyle H=12{\cal{N}}s^{2}J-2sJ\sum_{\bf{q}}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}a^{\dagger}_{{\bf{q}};1}&a^{\dagger}_{{\bf{q}};2}&a^{\dagger}_{{\bf{q}};3}&a^{\dagger}_{{\bf{q}};4}\end{array}\right){\bf{F}}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}a_{{\bf{q}};1}\\
a_{{\bf{q}};2}\\
a_{{\bf{q}};3}\\
a_{{\bf{q}};4}\end{array}\right),

where

\displaystyle{\bf{F}}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}3&-\phi_{1}&-\phi_{2}&-\phi_{3}\\
-\phi^{*}_{1}&3&-\phi_{12}^{*}&-\phi_{13}^{*}\\
-\phi^{*}_{2}&-\phi_{12}&3&-\phi_{23}^{*}\\
-\phi^{*}_{3}&-\phi_{13}&-\phi_{23}&3\end{array}\right),

\displaystyle\phi_{a}={\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{{\rm{i}}q_{a}}{2}}\cos\frac{q_{a}}{2}\,,\qquad\phi_{ab}={\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{{\rm{i}}(q_{a}-q_{b})}{2}}\cos\frac{q_{a}-q_{b}}{2}.

(2.16)

After diagonalizing the bilinear Bose form in equation (
2
) we arrive at

\displaystyle H=12{\cal{N}}s^{2}J-2sJ\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\bf{q}}\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}\xi_{{\bf{q}};\gamma}^{\dagger}\xi_{{\bf{q}};\gamma}\,.

(2.17)

The energies of the linear spin waves (magnons)

-2sJ\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}=|J|\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}

are determined by the eigenvalues of the matrix
{\bf{F}}

\displaystyle\omega_{1;{\bf{q}}}=\omega_{2;{\bf{q}}}=4,\qquad\omega_{3;{\bf{q}}}=2+D_{{\bf{q}}}\,,\qquad\omega_{4;{\bf{q}}}=2-D_{{\bf{q}}}\,,

\displaystyle D^{2}_{{\bf{q}}}=1+\cos\frac{q_{x}}{2}\cos\frac{q_{y}}{2}+\cos\frac{q_{x}}{2}\cos\frac{q_{z}}{2}+\cos\frac{q_{y}}{2}\cos\frac{q_{z}}{2}.

(2.18)

The calculated spin-wave dispersion coincides with the result reported in
[
26
]

[see figure 1 (c) of that paper].

Now, from equation (
2.17
) we can obtain the internal (intrinsic) energy per site

\displaystyle\frac{\langle H\rangle}{N}=3s^{2}J-\frac{sJ}{2{\cal{N}}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\bf{q}}\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}n_{{\bf{q}};\gamma}

(2.19)

and the magnetization per site

\displaystyle\langle s^{z}\rangle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m;\alpha}\langle s^{z}_{m;\alpha}\rangle=s-\frac{1}{4{\cal{N}}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\bf{q}}n_{{\bf{q}};\gamma}\,.

(2.20)

Here,

n_{{\bf{q}};\gamma}=1/({\mathrm{e}}^{|J|\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}/T}-1)

denotes the Bose-Einstein distribution function
(we set
k_{\rm{B}}=1
for brevity)
and
s=\frac{1}{2}
.
Below we use the linear spin-wave theory predictions (
2.19
) and (
2.20
) for comparison with the Green’s function approach results.

Green’s function method. Random-phase approximation

Now, we turn to the Green’s function approach

,
4
,

,

,

,

.
We introduce the following operators

\displaystyle s_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal{N}}}}\sum_{m}{\mathrm{e}}^{\mp{\rm{i}}{\bf{q}}\cdot{\bf{R}}_{m}}s^{\pm}_{m;\alpha}

(3.1)

and the (retarded) Green’s function

\displaystyle G_{\alpha\beta}(t)\equiv\langle\langle s^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}|s^{-}_{{\bf{q}};\,\beta}\rangle\rangle_{t}=-{\rm{i}}\Theta(t)\langle[s^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}(t),s^{-}_{{\bf{q}};\,\beta}]\rangle,\qquad G_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm{d}}t{\rm{e}}^{{\rm{i}}\omega t}G_{\alpha\beta}(t),

(3.2)

see, e.g.,
[
29
]
.
The first-order equation of motion reads:

\displaystyle\omega\langle\langle s^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}|s^{-}_{{\bf{q}};\,\beta}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}=\frac{2}{{\cal{N}}}\sum_{m}\langle s_{m;\alpha}^{z}\rangle\delta_{\alpha\beta}+{\rm{i}}\langle\langle\dot{s}^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}|s^{-}_{{\bf{q}};\,\beta}\rangle\rangle_{\omega}\,,\qquad{\rm{i}}\dot{s}^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\cal{N}}}}\sum_{m}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\rm{i}}{\bf{q}}\cdot{\bf{R}}_{m}}\left[s^{+}_{m;\alpha},H\right].

(3.3)

Calculating the commutator in the right-hand side of the second equation in equation (
3.3
)
(see appendix)
and introducing the random-phase (or Tyablikov) approximation

s^{z}_{\text{A}}s^{\pm}_{\text{B}}\to\langle s^{z}\rangle s^{\pm}_{\text{B}}

[
29
]

(see appendix)
we obtain the closed-form equation for the Green’s function

\displaystyle\sum_{\gamma}\left(\omega\delta_{\alpha\gamma}+2J\langle s^{z}\rangle F_{\alpha\gamma}\right)G_{\gamma\beta}(\omega)=2\langle s^{z}\rangle\delta_{\alpha\beta}\,,

(3.4)

where

-2J\langle s^{z}\rangle{\bf{F}}
is the frequency matrix,

{\bf{G}}(\omega)
is the matrix of Green’s functions,
and

{\bf{M}}=2\langle s^{z}\rangle{\bf{1}}

see also equation (
A.4
).
The matrix
{\bf{F}}
appeared already in the linear spin-wave calculations,
see equation (
2

We can find the eigenvalues and the eigenvectors of the matrix
{\bf{F}}
,
which satisfy

\sum_{\beta}F_{\alpha\beta}\langle\beta|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle=\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}\langle\alpha|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle
.
The eigenvalues
\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}
,
\gamma=1,2,3,4
are given in equation (
2

The (orthonormal) eigenvectors
\langle\alpha|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle
,
\gamma=1,2,3,4
are too lengthy to be presented here.

Finally,
using the identity

{\bf{1}}=\sum_{\gamma}|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|
,
we can resolve equation (
3.4
) with respect to
{\bf{G}}
,

{\bf{G}}=2\langle s^{z}\rangle(\omega{\bf{1}}+2J\langle s^{z}\rangle{\bf{F}})^{-1}\sum_{\gamma}|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|=\sum_{\gamma}[2\langle s^{z}\rangle/(\omega+2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}})]|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|

to get the desired result

\displaystyle G_{\alpha\beta}(\omega)=2\langle s^{z}\rangle\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\frac{\langle\alpha|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|\beta\rangle}{\omega+2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}}.

(3.5)

Here,
\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|\beta\rangle
is the
\beta

\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|
.
Clearly, the magnetization
\langle s^{z}\rangle
which enters equation (
3.5

see equation (
3.7

As can be seen from equation (
3.5

the excitation energies are given by

-2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}=2|J|\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}

In the limit

Moreover, they coincide with the results of

For finite temperatures

More sophisticated effects of the temperature on the excitation energies

The rotation-invariant Green’s function approach
considers the equation of motion up to the second order,
which obviously involves Green’s functions of higher order than the initial ones.
Several products of three-spin operators are then simplified by a decoupling like

After all,
the correlation functions, the vertex parameter, and the condensation term which is related to magnetic long-range order
are determined self-consistently
[within the Tyablikov approximation,
one faces only one resulting equation for
\langle s^{z}\rangle
, see equation (

)].
For further details see
[

Since
D_{\bf{q}}\to 2-\frac{1}{16}|{\bf{q}}|^{2}

\omega_{4;{\bf{q}}}
is the acoustic branch of the spectrum,

The spin stiffness

;
it shows the same temperature behavior as
\langle s^{z}\rangle
vanishing as

Knowing the Green’s functions,
we calculate the equal-time correlation functions via the relation

\displaystyle\langle s^{-}_{{\bf{q}};\,\beta}s^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}\rangle=\frac{{\rm{i}}}{2\piup}\lim_{\epsilon\to+0}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{\rm{d}}\omega\frac{G_{\alpha\beta}(\omega+{\rm{i}}\epsilon)-G_{\alpha\beta}(\omega-{\rm{i}}\epsilon)}{{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{\omega}{T}}-1}=2\langle s^{z}\rangle\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\frac{\langle\alpha|\gamma{\bf{q}}\rangle\langle\gamma{\bf{q}}|\beta\rangle}{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}}{T}}-1}.

(3.6)

These correlation functions yield important thermodynamic characteristics.
In particular,
using the identity
s^{z}=\frac{1}{2}-s^{-}s^{+}
which leads to

\langle s^{z}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{m;\alpha}\langle s^{-}_{m;\alpha}s^{+}_{m;\alpha}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\alpha}\sum_{\bf{q}}\langle s^{-}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}s^{+}_{{\bf{q}};\alpha}\rangle
,
we arrive at the following formula for
\langle s^{z}\rangle
:

\displaystyle\langle s^{z}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\langle s^{z}\rangle}{2{\cal{N}}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\bf{q}}\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}}{T}}-1}.

(3.7)

In fact,

.

We can obtain the internal energy of the spin model at hand by averaging the Hamiltonian (
2.1
).
Since we have calculated only the Green’s function
\langle\langle s^{+}|s^{-}\rangle\rangle

\langle\langle s^{z}|s^{z}\rangle\rangle

\langle s^{z}s^{z}\rangle

.

\displaystyle\frac{\langle H\rangle}{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{3}{2}J\langle s^{z}\rangle-\frac{J\langle s^{z}\rangle^{2}}{2{\cal{N}}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\bf{q}}\frac{\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}}{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}}{T}}-1}+\frac{J\langle s^{z}\rangle}{4{\cal{N}}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha,\beta=1\\

\displaystyle\varphi_{12}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{21}^{*}=\phi_{1}\,,\qquad\varphi_{13}=\varphi_{31}^{*}=\phi_{2}\,,\qquad\varphi_{14}=\varphi_{41}^{*}=\phi_{3}\,,

.

\chi

\langle s^{z}\rangle=\chi h
, where

the Green’s function

\displaystyle\langle s^{z}\rangle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\langle s^{z}\rangle}{2{\cal{N}}}\sum_{\gamma=1}^{4}\sum_{\bf{q}}\frac{1}{{\mathrm{e}}^{-\frac{2J\langle s^{z}\rangle\omega_{\gamma;{\bf{q}}}-h}{T}}-1}\,,

(3.10)

cf. equation (

Following, e.g.,
[

]
, for

5
Summary

In the present paper
we have discussed the thermodynamic properties of the pyrochlore-lattice quantum Heisenberg ferromagnet.
Our main results
(Curie temperature,
temperature dependences of the magnetization and the specific heat,
dynamic structure factor)
have been obtained within the random-phase approximation.
We compare them to the linear spin-wave theory at low temperatures
as well as to the rotation-invariant Green’s function method
[
19
]
,
quantum Monte Carlo simulation, and high-temperature expansion
[
40
,
19
]
results.
Although the random-phase approximation goes beyond the mean-field treatment,
thus leading to different results for the pyrochlore-lattice and the simple-cubic-lattice case,
it takes into account not all fluctuations, and ferromagnetic ordering is slightly overestimated
in comparison with the second-order rotation-invariant Green’s function method results.
Nevertheless,
the random-phase approximation Green’s function method together with the high-temperature expansion
provide a simple route to describe quantum Heisenberg ferromagnets for all temperatures.
Of course, the quantum Monte Carlo simulations or the rotation-invariant Green’s function method are also applicable,
though these are more complicated techniques.

The Green’s function method which uses the Tyablikov approximation can be applied to some other spin models related to the pyrochlore lattice.
For example,
one can in a similar way consider the ferromagnet
in the presence of the breathing anisotropy or the second-nearest neighbor interactions.
However,
the most interesting problem within this context
is the study of the properties of the pyrochlore-lattice quantum Heisenberg antiferromagnet.
In zero magnetic field,
the partition functions of the Heisenberg ferro- and antiferromagnet are connected by the obvious relation:

Z_{\rm{FM}}(-T)=Z_{\rm{AFM}}(T)
.
This implies that the specific heat of the antiferromagnet at temperature
T
is given by the specific heat of the ferromagnet taken at temperature
-T
.
However, in the thermodynamic limit, because of spontaneous symmetry breaking, this relation is not valid.
Clearly,
the rotation-invariant Green’s function approach,
which implies
\langle s^{z}\rangle=0
,
seems to be an appropriate approximation for examining that case.
The work in this direction is in progress
[
47
]
.
