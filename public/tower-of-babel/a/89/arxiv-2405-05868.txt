Trustworthy Dimensionality Reduction A dissertation report presented for the completion of the degree of Master of Statistics (M. Stat.) from Indian Statistical Institute
Subhrajyoty Roy

Abstract
Different unsupervised models for dimensionality reduction like PCA, LLE, Shannon’s mapping, tSNE, UMAP, etc. work on different principles, hence, they are difficult to compare on the same ground. Although they are usually good for visualisation purposes, they can produce spurious patterns that are not present in the original data, losing its trustability (or credibility). On the other hand, information about some response variable (or knowledge of class labels) allows us to do supervised dimensionality reduction such as SIR, SAVE, etc. which work to reduce the data dimension without hampering its ability to explain the particular response at hand. Therefore, the reduced dataset cannot be used to further analyze its relationship with some other kind of responses, i.e., it loses its generalizability. To make a better dimensionality reduction algorithm with a better balance between these two, we shall formally describe the mathematical model used by dimensionality reduction algorithms and provide two indices to measure these intuitive concepts such as trustability and generalizability. Then, we propose Localized Skeletonization and Dimensionality Reduction (LSDR) algorithm which approximately achieves optimality in both these indices to some extent. The proposed algorithm has been compared with the state of the art algorithms such as tSNE and UMAP and is found to be better overall in preserving global structure while retaining useful local information as well. We also propose some of the possible extensions of LSDR which could make this algorithm universally applicable for various types of data similar to tSNE and UMAP.

Introduction

During the current age of big data, data sources have become easily accessible, leading to an abundance of available real-world data to solve any practical problems. This abundance is mostly related to the sheer increase in the number of features or variables about a particular sample, rather than the increase in the number of sample points. However, analysing these high-dimensional datasets in their entirety can pose various challenges. Because of the curse of dimensionality, the samples become sparse in high dimensions leading to the overfitting of many models. Also, most of these high-dimensional features are not linked to the response variable under study, and, one cannot use visualisation techniques to see the common patterns to obtain insight into the variables. Therefore, it is natural to have a reduced representation of these high dimensional datasets which should not compromise the subsequent analysis.
Dimensionality Reduction
is such a transformation of high-dimensional data into a meaningful representation of reduced dimensionality
[
51
]
. Ideally, this reduced dimension should be equal to the intrinsic dimension of the data, which is described as the minimum number of parameters required to account for the variability presented in the observed data
[
15
]
. For example, it is well known that only
(k-1)
variables are enough to describe the segregation between
k
well-separated classes of any dimension
[
15
]
.

There are various applications of dimensionality reduction in different fields of science. Compression of digital signals from audio, video and images
[
22
]
, analysis of fMRI scans, gene expression, protein-coding datasets in biostatistics
[
47
]
, finding word embeddings in Natural Language Processing
[
21
]
, stock market analysis in Finance
[
6
,
55
]
are some of the real-life use-cases for dimensionality reduction techniques.

Dimensionality reduction algorithms can broadly be classified into three groups based on their focus and approach.

1.

Metric preserving Algorithms
focus on reducing any high dimensional data by preserving local distances or increasing global variability. PCA, Kernel PCA, Maximum variance unfolding, Hessian Map, Multidimensional scaling, tSNE etc. are examples of such algorithms. Neural network models such as autoencoders try to minimize the reconstruction error which is also a metric in high dimensional space.

2.

Graphical Algorithms
focus on reducing the dimensionality by approximating the geodesic distance based on the creation of neighbourhood graphs. Isomap, UMAP, Locally linear embedding, Laplacian eigenmaps etc. are examples of such algorithms.

3.

Supervised Algorithms
focus on reducing the dimensionality of predictor variables so that the ability to explain the response variable is not reduced. Sliced Inverse Regression (SIR), Sliced Average Variance Estimation (SAVE), Mean Average Variance Estimation (MAVE) are some of the examples of this nature.

1.1
Mathematical Setup of Dimensionality Reduction

The mathematical setup of dimensionality reduction starts with an
n\times p
size dataset
\boldsymbol{X}
, where each row represents a sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
, for
i=1,2,\dots n
. For the sake of simplicity, one assumes that these datapoints
\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{n}
are independent and identically distributed observations from a distribution
F
. To make dimensionality reduction meaningful, it is also assumed that the support of this distribution
F
has an intrinsic dimension
d
(with
d<<p
), i.e. the datapoints
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
lie on or near a
d
-dimensional manifold in the
\mathbb{R}^{p}
space.

It is often easier to work if we restrict the attention of the Riemannian manifold only, but the general problem makes no assumption about the manifold and allows discontinuous clusters to be present. The goal is to obtain a low dimensional representation
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
corresponding to each
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
such that the geometry of the manifold is preserved in the low dimensional space as much as possible. Since the problem does not associate a metric to measure the distortion, it is an ill-posed problem. Almost all of the existing DR algorithms define objective functions (or a metric) which is a characterization of the dissimilarity between geometric properties of the high and low dimensional manifolds, and then it tries to minimize that metric to obtain the “best” lower dimensional representation. Since there is no universal characterization, most of these objective function building process is ad-hoc in nature. Later, in Section
2.1
, we shall provide a unified framework to define the problem more concretely, and also formally define what is meant by a dimensionality reduction algorithm.

1.2
Existing Dimensionality Reduction Algorithms

1.2.1
Metric Preserving Algorithms

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is the most popular metric-preserving DR algorithm. It aims to find the linear projections
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}=\boldsymbol{M}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
of the data
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
to preserve the highest variability. The objective becomes same as maximizing
\text{trace}\left(\boldsymbol{M}^{\intercal}\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\boldsymbol{M}\right)
. An analytical solution of this problem can be obtained by finding the normalized eigenvectors associated with the
d
largest eigenvalues of the empirical variance-covariance matrix
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
of the zero mean data (i.e. the centered data). Although it has been used in various applications
[
48
,
37
,
20
,
23
]
, it suffers from many problems.

1.

PCA restricts its attention to only linear functions of the variables. However, if the underlying manifold is nonlinear, PCA cannot recover such structure. Localized PCA
[
24
,
38
]
and Kernel PCA
[
42
,
41
]
are two extensions that considers functions in an RKHS (see Appendix
A.1
) instead of only linear functions to counter this problem.

2.

It is not necessarily true that the information contained in the dataset must be explained by variance (not by other moments). An instance of this problem is described in detail in Example
1
.

Example 1
.

Since PCA, Kernel PCA or probabilistic PCA uses the variance in the data as a measure of information content, it can fail when most of the information presented in the data is along the direction with minimum variance. For example, if the dataset contains two long linear clusters as shown in Figure
1
, PCA fails to detect the shape or the structure of the manifold. A desirable dimensionality reduction algorithm should be able to detect these clusters, and create a one-dimensional mapping where the clusters are mapped to some disjoint intervals in
\mathbb{R}
.

(b)

:

3.

The third problem is the trustability issues. Since PCA takes the approach to maximize the variances, and the solution is given by the principal eigenvectors of the covariance matrix. These principal eigenvectors have an asymptotic normal distribution irrespective of the distribution of the original
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}

[
30
]
, hence the top few principal components tend to look like Gaussian distributed random variables. A simulated experiment has been performed in Example
2
to visualize this issue. Although all the principal components taken together returns only a rotated version of the original dataset, which makes PCA very desirable, its lower dimensional embeddings tend to distort the distribution of the data, which is undesirable. Any statistical inference based on the PCA reduced form could then be misleading in nature.

Example 2
.

As noted earlier, PCA has trustability issues as the principal eigenvectors are asymptotically normally distributed
[
30
]
, hence the low dimensional representation usually tend to look like normally distributed. A simulation was performed to demonstrate this effect.

1.

In first simulation,
1000
datapoints are generated from
N(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}_{5})
. From the data, the first two principal component directions were estimated, and then the projection onto them is obtained. As shown in Figure
2
, the algorithm outputs a 2-dimensional Gaussian data as best embedding of the original
5
-dimensional data.

2.

In the second simulation,
1000
datapoints are generated from the unit
5
-dimensional hypercube, where each of the variables are i.i.d. uniform
(0,1)
. Again, the first two principal components are extracted and plotted in Figure
3
. The output again looks similar to a Gaussian distribution.

Figure 2
:

:
First two principal components as extracted by uniformly distributed noise data

Therefore, despite the original data being very different, the reduction outputted by PCA is similar, resulting in similar inference about the data (whenever the analysis first performs PCA as dimensionality reduction and analyzes the reduced data).

1.2.2
Graphical Algorithms

Another approach to dimensionality reduction is by approximating the geodesic distance through the curved manifold. These algorithms start by creating a neighbourhood graph
G
where each datapoint
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
is connected to its
k
-nearest neighbours. The length of the shortest path between two points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
through the graph
G
serves as an approximation of the geodesic distance between
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
. Isomap, a popular graph-based DR algorithm
[
29
,
35
]
uses multidimensional scaling to find low dimensional points to approximate these distances through
G
. Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP)
[
31
]
, one of the most recent DR algorithms, creates the
k
-neighbourhood graph
G
and computes the simplicial sets
[
13
]
generated by that neighbourhood graph. Then the high and low dimensional simplicial sets are matched against each other.

These graph-based algorithms mainly suffer from two problems. The first one being the choice of the parameter
k
, the optimal number of neighbours, which if not properly chosen, may lead to artificial holes. For the purpose of demonstration, we generated
100
datapoints uniformly from the unit square and obtained the neighbourhood graphs for
k=1,3
and
10
. As shown in Figure
4
, for
k=1
, the neighbourhood graph is highly disconnected, and even for
k=10
, there remains an artificial hole in the dataset. There is no consensus about the choice of
k
in a data-dependant way, since there is no established theory about what metric should one look at.

Figure 4
:
Neighbourhood graphs with
k=1,3,10
, for
100
points generated uniformly in the unit square

On the other hand, another problem occurs when there are tightly clustered points having small intra-cluster distances and having high inter-cluster distances. In such a case, the neighbourhood graph often becomes disconnected. Therefore, the preservation of distances based on the neighbourhood graph leads to distortion of the global structure of the dataset. UMAP, the most recent graph-based DR algorithm, suffers from a similar problem
[
50
,
49
,
36
]
. The algorithm assumes that the data is uniformly distributed on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
, and hence, away from any boundaries, any ball of fixed volume should contain approximately the same number of points of
\boldsymbol{X}
regardless of where on the manifold it is centered. However, if there are more than one clusters present in the data, the neighbourhood graph
G
becomes disconnected, i.e. for two clusters present in the data, the neighbourhood graph can be split into two disjoint neighbourhood graph
G=G_{1}\cup G_{2}
, where
G_{1},G_{2}
are neighbourhood graphs for each of the clusters. In that case, the ordering or the position of the clusters have very little effect, since the disconnected graphs can be rearranged without any constraint. For this reason, UMAP fails to convey the ordering of the clusters (present in the original data), into the reduced dimensionality, as shown in Figure
5
.

A few other graph-based algorithms such as Locally Linear Embedding (LLE)
[
39
]
, Laplacian eigenmaps
[
1
]
etc., all suffer from similar problems described above.

1.2.3
Supervised Dimensional Reduction Algorithms

Sufficient Dimensionality Reduction Framework describes the supervised framework for dimensionality reduction. Any kind of dimensionality reduction analysis is generally followed by clustering, classification, regression analysis using the reduced set of variables. If such classification labels or regression variables are known a-priori, one may use this information on response variable in formulation of the dimensionality reduction problem, so that the reduced set of variables is more meaningful. It starts with a framework given as
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\mid\sigma\left(\left\{\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}),\dots\phi_{d}(X)\right\}\right)
, where
\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{d}
are unknown functions to be estimated. Specifically for linear embedding functions, one needs to estimate
\beta_{1},\dots\beta_{d}
such that
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}\perp\!\!\!\perp\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\mid(\beta_{1}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}},\beta_{2}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}},\dots\beta_{d}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})
. Bing Li
[
27
]
provides a useful discussion on the numerous methods that has been developed to solve this problem. Popular methods under sufficient dimensionality reduction framework includes,

1.

Sliced Inverse Regression
[
28
,
53
]
. Here, the idea is that the difference between the conditional expectations
\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}=y_{1})-\mathbb{E}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}=y_{2})
contains only the effect due the principal SDR directions.

2.

Sliced Average Variance Estimation (SAVE)
[
11
]
. The basic idea is that
\text{Var}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}=y)
contains only the variation due to the directions orthogonal to SDR.

3.

Generalized SIR and Generalized SAVE attempts to solve the general problem by restricting the
\sigma
-field
\sigma\left(\left\{\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}),\dots\phi_{d}(X)\right\}\right)
to a RKHS instead.

The main problem with the SDR approach is that it performs poorly if the response variable is categorical. For example, if there are
c
levels in the categorical variable
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}
, then only
(c-1)
SDR directions can be obtained. Also, since the reduction is performed only with a particular response in mind, the reduced set of variables cannot be reused in other problems. An example of how SIR may result in loss of information has been given as follows.

Example 3
.

We apply dimensionality reduction for Wisconsin breast cancer data based on two algorithms, tSNE and SIR. As shown in Figure
6
, they output a very different shape of the reduced data. While SIR only tries to best preserves the classification of benign and malignant cells, tSNE does it unsupervisedly, and outputs a one dimensional curvilinear manifold of the data. It was seen that the horizontal axis outputted by tSNE roughly aligns to the size of the tumour, with a strongly negative correlation of
-0.7013
. The tSNE output also says that it is much easier to classify the malignant and benign cells if the tumour is large, while it is much harder to segregate if it is small. SDR techniques will generally fail to consider this as it is optimized only to discriminate between the two classes.

1.3
Existing Metrics

As described in Section
1.2
, despite the existence of a large number of dimensionality reduction algorithms, most of these suffer from some kind of trustability issues. Also, there is little consensus about how to evaluate the performance of a dimensionality reduction algorithm since there exists a diverse range of frameworks under which the existing algorithms are derived and they use different metrics for defining their corresponding loss functions. Let us define
A_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
(and
B_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})
) as the set of indices
j
such that the
j
-th datapoint is a
k
nearest neighbour of the
i
-th datapoint in high (and low) and
r_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
(and
\widetilde{r}_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})
) denote the corresponding ranks according to the distances from
i
-th datapoint in high (and low) dimensional space, with the nearest neighbour having rank
1
. Almost all of the existing metrics to evaluate dimensionality reduction work on the idea of preserving local structures, as follows

1.

Separability indices
[
18
,
34
]
such as
\text{TSI}(\boldsymbol{X},\boldsymbol{Y})=1-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\dfrac{1}{k}\sum_{j\in A_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}\boldsymbol{1}_{j\not\in B_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})},

2.

“Trustworthiness” and “continuity” measures proposed by Kaski et al.
[
25
]
, given by

Trustworthiness

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{2}{nk(2n-3k-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in B_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})\backslash A_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})}(r_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{y}_{j})-k)

Continuity

\displaystyle=1-\dfrac{2}{nk(2n-3k-1)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j\in A_{k}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\backslash B_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}_{i})}(\widetilde{r}_{k,i}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})-k)

These two quantities are created based on the intuitive idea that to measure trustworthiness of the output of an algorithm, if the low dimensional visualization shows that two points are close together, then they should also remain close together in the original dataset. The continuity is the other side of the coin, points in a vicinity in the original space should be projected close together, so that the visualization of a new test datapoint can be obtained based on the proximity calculations.

1.4
Contributions of this study

Despite having a large number of different metrics for evaluating different dimensionality reduction algorithms, there is no consensus about a single metric. Also, most of these metrics, using some form of
k
-nearest neighbours, only focus on retaining the local structure, then they are only meaningful when the reduced data is used for clustering and classification using nearest neighbour-based algorithms. Our contribution would be to formally define a dimensionality reduction framework, under which we will provide a precise definition for an algorithm to qualify as a dimensionality reduction algorithm such that almost all of the existing dimensionality reduction algorithms will obey the definition. Then, we will consider two metrics under that general setup based on two important properties of the ideal dimensionality reduction algorithm, named as “Trustability index” and “Consistency index”.

While these two metrics will allow us to compare performances of different algorithms, we shall try to explore how these metrics can be reduced and what are the essential components of an algorithm that naturally correlates with these metrics. These, we shall try to build a dimensionality reduction algorithm by integrating those components which should guarantee better performances than the existing algorithms.

For brevity of presentation, the proofs of all the theorems are given in the Appendix
B
.

2
A Generalized Framework

2.1
The Framework for Dimensionality Reduction

We start with a generic setup where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
is a
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued random variable where
p
is large. The support of the distribution of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
is approximately a lower dimensional manifold. Denoting this intrinsic dimension as
d
, there should exist
\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{d}
functions from
\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that each of them represents a coordinate system along the manifold. When one takes account of all these coordinates, the part left out must be a random variation that the coordinate system cannot take an account for. With this interpretation, we define the following.

Definition 1
.

A
d
-dimensional reduction for the
\mathbb{R}^{p}
valued random variable
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
is a specification of the functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dots\phi_{d}
with each
\phi_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for
i=1,2,\dots d
and
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that,

1.

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}=f\left(\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}),\dots\phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\right)+\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})
almost surely.

2.

\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dots\phi_{d}
are measurable (may be continuous and smooth) functions. Each of these embeds the original data into the lower dimensional space, hence are called embedding functions.

3.

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
is a measurable reconstruction function that approximately retrieves
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
.

4.

\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})
is the error in reconstruction, which is independent of the
\sigma
-field generated by
\{\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}),\dots\phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\}
.

5.

\mathbb{E}(\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}))=0
.

Following are some of the examples to show that the above definition is a very general one.

Example 4
.

The first and trivial example of a dimensional reduction is the set of coordinate functions (or any permutation of that). Formally, a
p
-dimensional reduction for any
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued random variable
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
is given by
\phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{x})=x_{\pi(i)}
, where
x_{i}
is the
i
-th coordinate of
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\pi:\{1,2,\dots p\}\rightarrow\{1,2,\dots p\}
is a permutation function. Clearly,
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=0
and
f(x)=(x_{\pi^{-1}(1)},\dots x_{\pi^{-1}(p)})
.

Example 5
.

Let,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
has a multivariate Gaussian distribution. Then, starting with any orthonormal basis
B=\{\boldsymbol{v}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{v}_{d},\dots\boldsymbol{v}_{p}\}
(for
d<p
), one may decompose,
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\boldsymbol{v}_{i}+\sum_{i=(d+1)}^{p}\psi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
. Here,
\phi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=\boldsymbol{v}_{i}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
are the embedding functions and
f(x_{1},\dots x_{d})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}x_{i}\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is the reconstruction function. Due to multivariate normality of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
and orthogonality of
B
, the error
\sum_{i=(d+1)}^{p}\psi_{i}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\boldsymbol{v}_{i}
is independent of the
\sigma
-field generated by the embedding functions.

Note that, the same example also holds if
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
follows a mixture of Gaussian distributions. Any linear dimensionality reduction technique that computes the embedding functions with respect to some orthogonal basis (including PCA) falls under this category.

Theorem 1
.

If there exists two measurable functions
f
and
g
such that

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}=f(\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}),\dots\phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}))+\epsilon_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=g(\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}),\dots\phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}))+\epsilon_{2}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})

i.e. both can be regarded as reconstruction function corresponding to the reduction
(\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{d})
, then both reconstructions are same almost surely.

The implication of this result is that to determine such a dimensional reduction, it is enough to specify the embedding functions
\phi_{1},\phi_{2},\dots\phi_{d}
only, since in view of Theorem
1
, any reconstruction function will output the same decoded version almost surely.

2.2
Dimensionality reduction algorithms

Now, turning to the real world datasets, we do not have the random variable
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
, but instead we have i.i.d. data
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n}
, each being
p
-dimensional. Therefore, any dimensionality reduction algorithm basically defines a loss function (convex or nonconvex)
[
51
]
which is a function of the original datapoints, the embedding outputs
\mathsf{y}_{ij}=\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})
for
j=1,2,\dots d
and
i=1,2,\dots n
, the reconstruction function
f
, and the errors
\epsilon
. However, in view of Theorem
1
, the specification of
f
is not necessary when
\mathsf{y}_{ij}
is known, and also when the reconstruction and the errors are known, specification of the original dataset is not needed. Therefore, one may consider a loss function as
L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}):i=1,\dots n;j=1,\dots d\},\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1}),\dots\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n})\right)
for
n
observations. However, such specification requires different loss functions to be specified for each choice of the intrinsic dimension
d
(which is not known a-priori), as the loss function above is a function from
\mathbb{R}^{nd}\times\mathbb{R}^{np}
to
\mathbb{R}
. This requires special analysis for each of these loss functions, hence we extend this notion and consider only a single loss function
L:\mathbb{R}^{2np}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
given by

L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}):i=1,\dots n;j=1,\dots p\},\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1}),\dots\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n})\right)

while we perform the minimization of this loss subject to the constraints that
\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=0
for all
j=(d+1),\dots p
and for all
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
, in order to estimate the embedding functions.

Definition 2
.

A dimensionality reduction algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is a function from
\{1,2,\dots p\}\times\mathbb{R}^{np}
to a restricted class
\mathcal{C}
of functions (say smooth, linear, piecewise linear etc.) along with a specification of the loss function
L:\mathbb{R}^{2np}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
. This means, the algorithm takes the first argument as the number of embedding functions (i.e the lower dimension
d
) and the second argument as the
n
datapoints each of dimension
p
. The algorithm then chooses the best possible reduction by minimizing the associated loss function for any
d=1,2,\dots p
,

(\widehat{\phi}_{1},\dots\widehat{\phi}_{d})=\arg\min_{\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{d}\in\mathcal{C}}L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}):i=1,\dots n;j=1,\dots p\},\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1}),\dots\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n})\right),\\
\text{subject to the constraints }\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=0\ \forall j=(d+1),\dots p,\ \forall\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

(1)

The embedded lower dimensional representation is denoted by
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})
, where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}
is a
n\times d
matrix, whose rows are
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{i}=(\widehat{\phi}_{1}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}),\dots\widehat{\phi}_{d}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i}))
.

The loss function should not be any arbitrary one, there are some obvious restrictions.

1.

L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)=L\left(\{a_{j}+\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
, i.e. a translation to the embedding functions preserves the shape and the structure of the data, hence the loss function should not change. Note that, the translation amount may be different in different coordinates.

2.

L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)=L\left(\{(T\circ\phi)_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
, where
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
is an orthonormal transformation. Since, orthonormal transformations simply rotate the original data, the structure and shape of the data is preserved.

3.

L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)=L\left(\{a\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)
. If all the axis are scaled by the same amount, the loss function should not change. If the scaling factors are different, the shape of the function may distort.

4.

For any other transformation that distorts the shape of the data, the loss function should output something different.

5.

L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)\geq 0
and is equal to
0
if and only if
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})=0
for all
i=1,\dots n
.

Note that, the rotational invariance imply permutation invariance, hence the optimization problem given in Eq. (
1

\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=0

functions, while the optimization is done on the rest.

2.5
Trustability and Consistency index

A dimensionality reduction algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
is characterized by the loss function
L(\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{p},\epsilon)
according to Definition
2
, hence one may measure the trustability of the algorithm based on the loss function itself.

Consider the following situation: starting with any algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
and a
p
-dimensional dataset
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{n\times p}
, we ask the algorithm to reduce the data into a
p
-dimensional output
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}_{n\times p}
. Clearly, the original data itself becomes the “best” possible reduction achieving
0
loss as
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})=0
for all
i=1,\dots n
. Since the algorithm minimizes the loss function given in Eq. (
1
), which is invariant under translation, uniform scaling and rotation, we should have
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}\approx\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}+\lambda\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
for some mean vector
\boldsymbol{\mu}
, real number
\lambda
and orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
. Therefore, the quantity
\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\lambda,\boldsymbol{P}}\|\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}\|_{2}^{2}
could be regarded a measure of how trustworthy the algorithm is in preserving shape and structure of the original dataset. The above minimization problem can be exactly solved by the Procrustes problem (see Appendix
A.3
), which results in the construction of a
Trustability Index
for any dimensionality reduction algorithm.

Definition 3
.

The
Trustability Index
of any dimensionality reduction algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for any dataset
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{n\times p}
is given by,

\text{TI}(\mathcal{A}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}):=\sum\text{sing}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{YY}}})-\left(\sum\text{sing}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{XX}}})\right)^{-1}\left(\sum\text{sing}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{XY}}}\right)^{2}

where
\sum\text{sing}(\boldsymbol{A})
denotes the sum of the singular values of the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
, and

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{XX}}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}),\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{XY}}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}),\ \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{YY}}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}})^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}})

where
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}=\mathcal{A}_{n}(p,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})
and
\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}}
are the
n\times p
matrices whose each row is
n^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
and
n^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}
respectively.

Remark 1
.

The more trustable an algorithm is, the less is the value of the index. This means, the most trustworthy algorithm attains the index value equal to
0
.

Example 6
.

The algorithm that has a
0
-
1
loss function, outputs a random subset of size
d
of the available
p
variables. Thus, with
d=p
, it outputs a permutation of columns of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
, hence attains TI equal to 0. Similarly, for Principal Component Analysis, since
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Y}}
is simply a orthogonal transformation of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
, it is also most trustable algorithm.

Naturally, the above algorithms are not reasonably well if
d<p
. Therefore, along with Trustability Index, we need another index which is competing in nature. The problem in the aforementioned algorithms is that they have a large variability in its output under different transformations of the original data.

Theorem 4
.

Let,
\boldsymbol{z}=T(\boldsymbol{x})
be some invertible transformation
T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
. For a dimensionality reduction algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
, with loss function
L
and restricted class of functions
\mathcal{C}
, if

1.

For any
\phi\in\mathcal{C}
, both
\phi\circ T
and
\phi\circ T^{-1}
belong to
\mathcal{C}
.

2.

\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})=\epsilon(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}_{i})=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{i}
for all samples
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n}
and transformed samples
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}_{i}=T(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})
.

Then,

\min_{\phi_{1},\dots\phi_{d}\in\mathcal{C}}L\left(\{\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)=\min_{\psi_{1},\dots\psi_{d}\in\mathcal{C}}L\left(\{\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}_{i})\}_{i,j=1}^{n,p},\{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{\epsilon}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right)

where both the above minimization are performed subject to the conditions that
\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=\psi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}})=0
for all
j=(d+1),\dots p
.

Theorem
4
opens up the possibility that even if the underlying intrinsic manifold is changed, as long as the errors in reconstruction remain the same, the loss also remains the same. For example, in Figure
7
, we demonstrate two different 2D datasets, both of which have an underlying one-dimensional manifold. Clearly, the underlying one-dimensional manifolds can be transformed into each other employing a simple one-to-one transformation, hence a desirable algorithm should output the same one-dimensional coordinate representation for both these datasets. In Theorem
4
, the assumption that the errors remain the same even after the transformation is crucial. Therefore, even if
T(\boldsymbol{x})=\lambda\boldsymbol{x}
for any
\lambda>0
is a one-to-one transformation, it cannot be readily applied to the data as it may shrink (or inflate) both the errors and the extent of the low dimensional manifold simultaneously. We, on the other hand, want to apply this transformation only to the underlying manifold and keeping the reconstruction error fixed. Therefore, we may employ the following scheme.

1.

From

2.

T:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}

\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}=(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}_{n})

T((\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{\mathcal{A},d})_{i})=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{z}}_{i}

.

3.

Create new dataset
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}=\boldsymbol{\mathsf{Z}}+(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{\mathcal{A},d})
.

4.

By means of Theorem
4
, the output of the algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
applied on this new dataset i.e.
\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})

Again, one can use Procrustes’s problem (see Appendix

Definition 4

(Consistency Index)
.

The
Consistency Index
for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for any dataset
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
at intrinsic dimension
d
, is given by,

\text{CI}(\mathcal{A}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}},d)=\max_{T\text{ is invertible}}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\lambda,\boldsymbol{P}}\|\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{P}\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})\|_{2}^{2}

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}=T(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{\mathcal{A},d})+(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{\mathcal{A},d})
. Similar to the trustability index, lower values of consistency index implies that algorithm is more consistent in its outputs.

Example 7
.

Let us consider another simple dimensionality reduction algorithm, which always outputs the same constant, i.e.
\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})=\text{constant}
for any
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
. Then, it is possible to achieve the highest consistency in output (i.e. the consistency index is equal to
0
), however, the algorithm is not at all trustable, since at
d=p
, the algorithm does not output a rotated, uniformly scaled and translated version of the original dataset.

In its generality, it is not possible to compute the consistency Index for an algorithm, since the maximization happens over all invertible functions
T
. Rather, we shall look at the maximization subject to the constraint that the invertible function
T
lie in some RKHS
\mathcal{H}
containing
\mathbb{R}^{p}
-valued functions. Note that, it is not true that all functions of the RKHS is invertible (for example, if
f\in\mathcal{H}
, then
(-f)\in\mathcal{H}
and hence
f+(-f)\equiv 0\in\mathcal{H}
, which is non-invertible). Also, we would like the invertible functions to be such that it does not scale the dataset too small or too large. Therefore, we restrict our attention to the subclass of functions (i.e. a subset of the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
generated by a matrix-valued kernel
k
),

\mathcal{H}^{\ast}=\left\{f:f\in\mathcal{H},f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{ij}k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\boldsymbol{e}_{j},0\leq\beta_{ij}\leq 1,\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\beta_{ij}=1,\text{ and }f\text{ is invertible}\right\}.

Even then, since the index itself depends on the particular algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
we choose, unless we have an understanding of how the algorithm behaves, it is hard to evaluate the maximum value analytically. Since
\mathcal{H}^{\ast}
is an infinite set, it is also not computationally possible to maximize by searching over the whole space
\mathcal{H}^{\ast}
. Under certain simple conditions, this maximization over all
T\in\mathcal{H}^{\ast}
can be reduced to maximization over only a finite set of functions, which make this problem computationally tractable. The following theorem proves this fact.

Theorem 5
.

If
S(T)=\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\lambda,\boldsymbol{P}}\|\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{P}\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})\|_{2}^{2}
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}
is as given in Definition
4
, is a convex function in
T
, then
\max_{T\in\mathcal{H}^{\ast}}S(T)=\max_{T\in\partial\mathcal{H}^{\ast}}S(T)
, where

\partial\mathcal{H}^{\ast}=\left\{k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\boldsymbol{e}_{j}:i=1,\dots n;\ j=1,\dots p\right\}.

Since,
\partial\mathcal{H}^{\ast}
is now a finite set, therefore, we can use it to compute the consistency Index for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
approximately. Therefore, we have the following approximate version of consistency Index, called Tractable Consistency Index.

Definition 5
.

Tractable Consistency Index
for any algorithm
\mathcal{A}_{n}
for any dataset
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}
at intrinsic dimension
d
is given by,

\text{TCI}(\mathcal{A}_{n},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}},d)=\max_{T\in\partial\mathcal{H}^{\ast}}\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\lambda,\boldsymbol{P}}\|\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}})-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{P}\mathcal{A}_{n}(d,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}})\|_{2}^{2}

where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}}=T(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{\mathcal{A},d})+(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}-\boldsymbol{\mathsf{X}}_{\mathcal{A},d})
, and
\partial\mathcal{H}^{\ast}=\left\{k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i})\boldsymbol{e}_{j}:i=1,\dots n;\ j=1,\dots p\right\}
where
k(\cdot,\cdot)
is a positive definite matrix-valued kernel function from
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
to
\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\mathbb{R}^{p}
.

3
Localized Skeletonization and Dimensionality Reduction (LSDR)

3.1
Manifold Approximation Graph

As discussed by Maaten and Hinton
[
50
]
, one cannot hope to preserve all pairwise distances in the lower dimensional space, since the same
\epsilon
-ball in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
can hold more equidistant points than in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d<p
). This phenomenon is popularly referred to as the “Crowding problem”. Therefore, the authors argue that only preserving the local distances can be achieved. A visualisation of this argument is given in Figure
8(b)
, where the two red points shown have very different distances in Euclidean sense and in the distance through the manifold sense. As we notice, this distance through the manifold can be measured by connecting several smaller localized distances, where a point is connected to its local neighbourhood and only these small distances are summed up to calculate the larger distances. Hence, if one can obtain a graph
G
connecting these datapoints by edges whenever two points are in close proximity, then the corresponding graph would be an approximation of the underlying manifold. Consequently, the distance between any two points through the manifold can be computed by the length of the shortest path between those two points through the graph. Delaunay tessellation and Minimum Cost Spanning Tree (MCST) are two integral components of creation of such approximation graphs.

Delaunay triangulation (also known as a Delone triangulation, in short DT) for a given set
P
of discrete points in a general position on a plane, is a triangulation
DT(P)
such that no point in
P
is inside the circumcircle of any triangle in
DT(P)
. It can be shown that this triangulation avoids narrow triangles and maximizes the sum of the minimum angles of all triangles
[
9
]
, hence provide a good triangular approximation of the underlying mesh. Delaunay
[
10
]
extended the idea of triangulation into tessellation which can be obtained for any set of points
P
lying in general position in any dimensional Euclidean vector space. Figure
8(a)
shows one such example of DT for points distributed on a circle on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. One could simply use DT as the required graph
G
, but there are some longer edges that connect points lying on the opposite parts of the circle as DT outputs a graph with only triangles. However, if Delaunay triangulation is modified to have polygonal tessellation as well, then such tessellation would allow us to create the required graph.

Let,
P=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}
be a set of points lying in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and its Delaunay triangulation is denoted as
DT(P)=(G_{DT}(P),E_{DT}(P))
. Consider a point
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and let
e_{1},\dots e_{k}\in E_{DT}(P)
be the associated edges with one endpoint equal to
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
. Then, considering a multivariate normal distribution centered at
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
with variance
\sigma I_{p}
, one can show that
\dfrac{\|e_{j}\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}
has a
\chi^{2}_{p}
distribution. Similarly,
\dfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\|e_{j}\|^{2}\sim\Gamma(kp/2,1/2)
distribution. Therefore, one can show that

\dfrac{\|e_{j}\|^{2}}{\sum_{j^{\prime}=1}^{k}\|e_{j^{\prime}}\|^{2}}\sim\text{Beta}\left(\dfrac{p}{2},\dfrac{(k-1)p}{2}\right)

Hence, one can choose some
\alpha\approx 0.95
and use the above statistic and compare it with the threshold
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{p}{2},\frac{(k-1)p}{2},\alpha\right)
which denotes the
\alpha
-th quantile of
\text{Beta}\left(\frac{p}{2},\frac{(k-1)p}{2}\right)
distribution, and remove the particular edge
e_{j}
if the statistic exceeds this threshold. This is similar to a statistical hypothesis testing setup where the null hypothesis assumes that the points in the locality of
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
which are joined by DT follows a multivariate normal distribution. When the null hypothesis is false, the high value of the test statistic would indicate the presence of a longer edge
e_{j}
in DT, hence the removal of this edge make the graph
DT(P)
a better approximation to the manifold.

The edge removal process will only keep the small edges in
DT(P)
. However, if there are two or more clusters present in the data, then some global structure or characteristic need to be retained (Otherwise, similar problems as described in Section
1.2
and Figure
5
may occur). This global structure can only be preserved through some large edges, which may be deleted in the aforementioned edge removal process. In this case, the Euclidean minimum cost spanning tree (MCST) provides us with useful insights. Euclidean MCST is a subgraph of the Delaunay tessellation
DT(P)
such that it is a spanning tree with the sum of its edge lengths equal to the minimum possible value. The Euclidean MCST for the same circular data is shown in Figure
8(c)
. The following result provides a theoretical justification of why MCST is a reasonable choice to preserve global structure between several clusters.

Theorem 6
.

Let us assume we have two groups of points given by,

\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{\mu}_{1}\boldsymbol{1}_{C_{1}}+\boldsymbol{\mu}_{2}\boldsymbol{1}_{C_{2}}+\epsilon

such that
|C_{1}|=m,|C_{2}|=n
and the errors
\epsilon
are independently distributed. Assume that for any
\delta>0
,

P(\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|_{2}>\delta)<1

for any
\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
lying in the same class. Then, the probability that the euclidean MCST of these set of points has two edges connecting different classes tends to zero as
m,n\rightarrow\infty
, namely the MCST would have two subtrees, each of them being a spanning tree for each of the class, and there will be only one edge that connects two classes.

Remark 2
.

The condition required for Theorem
6
is satisfied if
\epsilon
has a continuous distribution with support containing
0
. It simply requires that the points within the same cluster can be probabilistically arbitrarily close, which if not met, indicates that the cluster itself can be broken into more than one sub-clusters. Also, Theorem
6
holds for more than two clusters as well, where between any two clusters there is at most a single edge of MCST.

Theorem
6
shows that the edges which are part of the MCST are extremely crucial to approximate the underlying manifold of a point set
P
. Therefore, we start with the Delaunay triangulation
DT(P)
of the point set
P
, then perform the edge removal process by comparing the edge length based test statistic with the cut-off from beta distribution, and only remove the edge it is not a part of the MCST. This whole procedure yields us a graph
G_{\alpha}(P)
for the point set. An example of such
G_{\alpha}(P)
is shown in Figure
8(d)
. We call such a graph as
\alpha
-approximation of a manifold generated by the point set
P
.

3.2
Skeletonization

The next step in our algorithm is to obtain a skeletal representation of the manifold from its graph approximation. This skeletal representation would allow one to obtain points lying on the underlying low-dimensional manifold (i.e. without any errors). In mathematical terms, a skeletal representation of a point set
P=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\boldsymbol{x}_{2},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}
is given by a subset of points
Sk(P)=\{\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{1}},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{k}}\}
such that
\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{j}}=f(\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{j}}),\dots,\phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{j}}))
i.e.
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x}_{i_{j}})=0
for all
j=1,2,\dots k
(see Definition
1
). Let us now consider a single point
\boldsymbol{x}=f(\phi_{1}(\boldsymbol{x}),\dots\phi_{d}(\boldsymbol{x}))+\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x})
where
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Let
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a vector such that
\boldsymbol{v}^{\intercal}\nabla\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=0
for all
j=1,2,\dots d
, i.e.
v
is locally orthogonal to each of the embedding functions. Applying Taylor’s theorem, we then get
\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{v})\approx\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})+\boldsymbol{v}^{\intercal}\nabla\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})=\phi_{j}(\boldsymbol{x})
, i.e. the embedding remains approximately constant on the line
\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}
where
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a parameter. Observe that, in this case,
\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v})\approx\epsilon(\boldsymbol{x})+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}
, which shows that the error is locally linear on that orthogonal line. This realization then allows us to define boundaries of the manifold in mathematical terms, a point
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}
is a boundary point if either
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}=\displaystyle\max_{\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}\in P}(\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v})
or
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}=\displaystyle\min_{\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v}\in P}(\boldsymbol{x}+\lambda\boldsymbol{v})
for some
\boldsymbol{x}\in Sk(P)
. The set of all boundary points are denoted as
B(P)
.

Now consider a point
\boldsymbol{x}\in Sk(P)
and let
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}\in B(P)
be a corresponding boundary point. Assume that
\boldsymbol{x}_{b}
is a part of a
(p-1)
-dimensional hyperplane
\mathcal{P}
constituted of some edges in the graph
G_{\alpha}(P)
obtained from Delaunay tessellation. If this hyperplane is part of two
(p+1)
-simplices
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, they must lie in the opposite side of
\mathcal{P}
, therefore, the ray parallel to the vector
(\boldsymbol{x}_{b}-\boldsymbol{x})
starting from
\boldsymbol{x}
must intersect
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
both. Clearly, for one of this intersection point, the distance from the intersection to
\boldsymbol{x}
must be larger than
\|\boldsymbol{x}_{b}-\boldsymbol{x}\|
which contradicts the extremality of the boundary point. Therefore, we have the following result.

Theorem 7
.

Any boundary point of the manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
must lie on a
(p-1)
dimensional hyperplane which is a part of atmost one
(p+1)
-simplex.

Due to Theorem
7
, it is possible to obtain the boundary points by simply verifying the condition for each vertex in
G_{\alpha}(P)
. Figure
9(a)
shows the boundary of
G_{\alpha}(P)
computed in this way for a point set arranged in a spiral. However, the significance of this procedure is that it enables us to detect the boundary without any reference to the skeletal representation, hence one can obtain skeletal representation from the boundary itself. To see this, note that the skeletal points are given by those where the error is equal to
0
, and the boundary points are essentially where the error attains its maximum value in magnitude. Since the error is locally linear as we have shown, one can move away from the boundary inside the point cloud which should necessarily decrease the error. Therefore, the point that is the most distance away from the boundaries should have the smallest error in magnitude, which may serve our purpose as a skeletal point.

and its boundary

in the form of a one dimensional manifold in a 2D plane, its approximation graph

In order to put these ideas into a working algorithm, for every point
\boldsymbol{x}\in P
, we compute its distance from the boundary as

d_{G}(\boldsymbol{x},B(P))=\min_{\boldsymbol{x}_{b}\in B(P)}d_{G}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{b})

where
d_{G}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{j})
is the sum of the length of edges in the shortest path between
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
through the graph
G_{\alpha}(P)
. Then, we iterate over all points
\boldsymbol{x}\in P
again and mark them as a skeletal point if

d_{G}(\boldsymbol{x},B(P))=\max_{\boldsymbol{w}\in NN_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})}d_{G}(\boldsymbol{w},B(P))

where
NN_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the set of
k
-nearest neighbours of
\boldsymbol{x}
. When
k
is sufficiently small, the above criterion assures that the identified skeletal points lie in the more inner region than their neighbours. This idea of the skeleton as the most distant points from its boundary dates back to Blum, 1967
[
3
]
and is later proved to have many optimality criterion
[
44
]
. Such a skeletal representation is shown in Figure
9(b)
where the skeletal points are identified with a larger size and different colour. As seen from Figure
9(b)
, the skeleton points lie approximately in the middle of the one-dimensional spiral manifold.

3.3
Multidimensional Scaling and Kernel embedding

Multidimensional scaling (MDS) attempts to find a low dimensional representation
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\}
with each
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that the usual Euclidean distance between
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{y}_{j}
approximately equals
q_{ij}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
, for pre-specified choice of
q_{ij}

[
4
]
. Therefore, for pre-specified values of the distances, it attempts to find points in a
d
-dimensional space that preserves the distances as much as possible, by minimizing the stress function

\text{Stress}_{D}(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{y}_{n})=\left(\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq n}(q_{ij}-\|\boldsymbol{y}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}_{j}\|)^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

This variation is known as the metric MDS. Since the distances are invariant under translation and rotational transformation of
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
’s, the solution to metric MDS is not unique. However, one can show that a condition similar to the desired values of trustability index mentioned in Section
2.5
can be achieved by metric MDS.

Theorem 8
.

Let us assume for a dataset
\{\boldsymbol{x}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{x}_{n}\}
, the metric MDS is applied on
q_{ij}=\|\boldsymbol{x}_{i}-\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\|
to obtain an embedding
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
. Then,
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\boldsymbol{v}+\boldsymbol{P}\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
for all
i=1,2,\dots n
for some
\boldsymbol{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
and some orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
.

Theorem
8
shows the connection between metric MDS and the trustability index. In other words, it says that as long as the original distances can be recovered in some proposed DR algorithm, a metric MDS step then ensures that the DR algorithm remains optimal with respect to the trustability index. However, the distances which are needed to pass into the metric MDS step is the Euclidean distance. In contrast, if we pass an approximation to the geodesic distance in the metric MDS setup, then one can show under certain reasonable assumptions that the corresponding DR algorithm will be optimal with respect to the consistency index defined earlier.

Theorem 9
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a smooth co-ordinate function of a Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
such that a unit-velocity curve in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
induces a constant velocity curve in
\mathcal{M}
. Let,
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a local diffeomorphism such that
\phi\circ f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{p}
be the coordinate function of another Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M}^{\prime},g^{\prime})
with

g(v,w)=cg^{\prime}(\phi^{\ast}v,\phi^{\ast}w),\qquad\forall v,w\in\mathcal{M}

where
\phi^{\ast}
is the push-forward and
c
is some positive real constant. Then, for any
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n}\}
with each
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the
d
-dimensional output
\{\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{y}_{n}\}
of metric MDS of
\{f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\dots f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})\}
and the
d
-dimensional output
\{\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{z}_{n}\}
of metric MDS of
\{\phi\circ f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{1}),\dots\phi\circ f(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{n})\}
are related by

\boldsymbol{z}_{i}=\boldsymbol{v}+\lambda\boldsymbol{Py}_{i},\qquad i=1,2,\dots n

for some fixed
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and some orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
.

From the skeletal points obtained in Section
3.2
, one may assume that these points lie on the low dimensional manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. The assumptions made in Theorem
9
enables one to approximate the geodesic distance on this manifold by summing up local Euclidean distances through the approximation graph
G_{\alpha}
. Therefore, if one can use the length of the shortest paths between two skeletal points on
G_{\alpha}
as an approximation to the geodesic distance, then Theorem
9
ensures that the output of the metric MDS of these distances would ensure a dimension reduction for the skeletal points and that DR would be optimal with respect to the consistency index. This is precisely what we will do to find the low dimensional embedding of the skeletal points.

However, the metric MDS would be unable to properly find low dimensional embedding for the non-skeletal points, since they do not lie exactly on the manifold. Therefore, given that the skeletal points sufficiently cover the entire structure of the manifold, one may use the technique mentioned in Section
2.3
to obtain a low-dimensional embedding of non-skeletal points. While metric MDS preserves the global structure as much as possible by considering only the skeletal points, the local structure is recovered by the kernel embedding technique, hence the whole procedure is expected to balance the local and global structures found in the high dimensional data nicely.

end

5
Conclusions and Future Aspects

Analysis of the Trustability index and the Tractable consistency index of various algorithms provide insights into how these algorithms behave in general. In essence, the output of tSNE or UMAP may be used in general for visualisation purposes. However, if the post-reduction analysis requires classification or clustering on the reduced data, then Laplecian Eigenmaps provide a good choice of balance. Also, since the graphical algorithms are generally more trustable, one should use graph-based approximation to the manifold to preserve the global structure, and this primarily gave the intuition of LSDR.

Turning to our proposed algorithm, LSDR outperforms both tSNE and UMAP in terms of its preservation of global structures. Use of metric MDS with the manifold approximation graph
G_{\alpha}
provides us with near-optimality properties with respect to both the trustability and consistency indices (see Theorem
8
and
9
). LSDR preserves the global structure by using skeletal representation, while the local structure is provided by the kernel embedding based usage. In a sense, LSDR is similar to SVM, where the skeletal points act as support points, and any point on the space can be represented in low dimension using the representations of these support points. One of the major problem in the trustability of tSNE and UMAP is that they are iterative in nature, therefore, the final embedding depends a lot on the initialization of the points. In contrast, our proposed LSDR is not an iterative algorithm, hence overcomes this difficulty, but retaining the same level of performance for these setups. The choice of hyperparameters is one of the deciding factors of the performance of LSDR. For example, if
k
is chosen very large, then there will naturally be fewer skeletal points, and consequently, these points would not be sufficient to describe the manifold’s skeleton properly. Therefore, a choice of smaller
k
(about 3-5) and a higher bandwidth for the kernel is required to ensure that all the points in the dataset are sufficiently represented.

On the other hand, there are some obvious limitations. The most expensive part in LSDR is the Delaunay tessellation which has a worst-case complexity of
O(n^{p/2})
. Therefore, if
p
is large, it is not computationally feasible in its current form. One could then proceed by making chunks of the whole dataset with respect to close proximities and then perform Delaunay tessellation (this would reduce effective
n
). Another choice could be to use Gabriel graph or Beta skeleton
[
44
,
8
]
which are mathematically proven to be a good approximation to the underlying manifold. However, their implementations were not readily available in
R
, hence with the limited scope of time and space, we could not explore it further. Another problem occurs if there is no error in the data, i.e. the data lies in an exact
d
-dimensional manifold in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
. In this case, LSDR would detect every point as a point on the boundary
B(P)
, and theoretically, every point would become a skeletal point. While in a realistic scenario, there are always errors, such a case might occur if
n<p
. In this case, an initial PCA (or metric MDS) step is needed to be performed to reduce the dimension while preserving the pairwise distances. Since in a
d
-dimensional plane, only
(d+1)
distances are needed to be known to define the position of a point, and since there are
(n-1)
pairwise distances available, it is possible to embed all these
n
points in
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
without changing their pairwise distances. After this initial adjustment, our proposed method LSDR can be used. Finally, LSDR requires its input to become numerical quantities, hence it cannot reduce the dimensionality of the points if only a similarity metric is given. For example, LSDR cannot be used for textual data. However, this problem can again be solved by using an initial metric MDS step to obtain numerical representations in
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
by preserving the similarities (or distances).

A.1
Reproducible Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS)

Reproducible Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) is a Hilbert space of functions which is generated by a basis of kernel functions. If
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a kernel function, then the RKHS generated by its is the set of functions

\mathcal{H}=\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,x_{i}):x_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\text{ and }\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}k(x_{i},x_{i})<\infty\right\},

is a Hilbert space equipped with the inner product

\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,x_{i}),\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\beta_{j}k(\cdot,y_{j})\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}k(x_{i},y_{j})

Clearly,
\mathcal{H}
is a set of functions
f
that maps elements of
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
. For any choice of positive definite kernel function, the existence of such RKHS is guaranteed by Moore–Aronszajn theorem
[
2
]
. To evaluate any function
f\in\mathcal{H}
at any
x\in\mathcal{X}
, one can simply compute
\langle f,k(\cdot,x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
since,

\langle f,k(\cdot,x)\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(\cdot,x_{i}),k(\cdot,x)\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k(x,x_{i})=f(x).

This means, the evaluation functional
T_{x}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that maps a function
f\rightarrow f(x)
is a linear functional. This turns out to be a defining property of RKHS in the sense that, any Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
on which evaluation functional is a bounded linear functional is generated by some positive definite kernel
k

[
2
,
27
]
.

Example 8
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be all functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}
such that the support of the Fourier transform of
f
is contained in
[-a,a]
. Then,

k(x,y)=\dfrac{\sin(a(y-x))}{a(y-x)}

and
\langle f,g\rangle=\displaystyle\int fg
, defines a RKHS.

Example 9
.

Another popular example of RKHS is the class of functions
f:\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
for any
p
, generated by the radial basis kernel

k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})=\exp\left(-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|^{2}\right),\qquad\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

Example 10
.

Finally, an example of non-continuous kernel is the Bregman kernel defined by,

k(x,y)=\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }x=y\\
0&\text{ otherwise }\end{cases}

This kernel could be a good choice if there are categorical variables in the dataset. This kernel reproduces a RKHS
\mathcal{H}
containing all square integrable real-valued functions.

While the RKHS is introduced in a fixed variable setup, it can generalized to stochastic setup easily by replacing
x_{i}
’s by
\mathcal{X}
-valued random variables
\mathsf{x}_{i}:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{X}
. Now, if one considers the linear functional
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
that maps
f\in\mathcal{H}
to
\mathbb{E}(f(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega))
, and assumes that
\mathbb{E}(k(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega),\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega))<\infty
, then it follows that
\mathcal{E}
is a bounded linear functional. Riesz representation theorem
[
46
]
guarantees that there is a unique member
f_{0}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\langle f,f_{0}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{E}(f)
, which one may define as
\mathbb{E}(k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega))
.

Analogously, if
\mathbb{E}(k(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega),\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega))<\infty
, one may define the variance operator of
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
in
\mathcal{H}
as,

\Gamma_{xx}=\mathbb{E}(k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega))\otimes k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega)))-\mathbb{E}(k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega)))\otimes\mathbb{E}(k(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega)))

where
\otimes
is the symbol for tensor product (see Li
[
27
]
for details). One can further generalize this concept to define covariance operator between functions lying in two different RKHS. Let,
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and
\mathcal{H}_{y}
be two RKHS generated by two different kernels
k_{x}
and
k_{y}
. Then, the covariance operator is similarly given by

\Gamma_{xy}=\mathbb{E}(k_{x}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega))\otimes k_{y}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}(\omega)))-\mathbb{E}(k_{x}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}(\omega)))\otimes\mathbb{E}(k_{y}(\cdot,\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}(\omega))).

This means, for any
f(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}})\in\mathcal{H}_{x}
and
g(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}})\in\mathcal{H}_{y}
,
\text{Var}(f(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}))=\langle f,\Gamma_{xx}f\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}
and
\text{Cov}(f(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}},g(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}})=\langle f,\Gamma_{xy}g\rangle_{\mathcal{H}_{x}}
. In particular, according to Theorem 12.1 of Li
[
27
]
, when one has i.i.d. sample
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{n}
for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{1},\dots\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{n}
for
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}
, these operators are estimated as

\widehat{\Gamma}_{xx}=\dfrac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{G}_{x},\ \widehat{\Gamma}_{xy}=\dfrac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{G}_{x}\boldsymbol{G}_{y},\ \widehat{\Gamma}_{yy}=\dfrac{1}{n}\boldsymbol{G}_{y}

where the matrix
G_{x}
and
G_{y}
have entries given by,

\displaystyle(G_{x})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=k_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j})-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}k_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j})-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j}k_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j})+\dfrac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}k_{x}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}_{j})

(3)

\displaystyle(G_{y})_{i,j}

\displaystyle=k_{y}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{j})-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i}k_{y}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{j})-\dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{j}k_{y}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{j})+\dfrac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i}\sum_{j}k_{y}(\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{i},\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}_{j}).

(4)

Definition 6

(Universal Kernels)
.

A continuous kernel
k(\cdot,\cdot)
on a compact metric space
(\mathcal{X},d)
is called
universal
if and only if the RKHS
\mathcal{H}
induced by the kernel is dense in
C(\mathcal{X})
, the space of continuous functions on
\mathcal{X}
, with respect to the infinity norm
\|f-g\|_{\infty}
.

Remark 3
.

Existence of universal kernels allows one to approximate any continuous function by some elements of RKHS. Steinwart
[
45
]
showed that the radial kernels
k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\exp(-\lambda\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\|^{2})
is an example of universal kernel.

The following theorem provides a easy condition to assure independence of two random variables, by using zero-covariance structure in RKHS reproduced by universal kernels.

Theorem 10

(Gretton et al.
[
19
]
)
.

If
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and
\mathcal{H}_{y}
are RKHS generated from universal kernels
k_{x},k_{y}
on the compact metric spaces
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
, then
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathsf{y}}
are independent with respect to the joint measure
P_{x,y}
on
(\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y},\Lambda_{x}\times\Lambda_{y})
(where
\Lambda_{x}
and
\Lambda_{y}
are Borel
\sigma
-algebras on
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
) if and only if the covariance operator
\Gamma_{xy}=0
.

Remark 4
.

The theorem can be generalized to non-compact spaces also, without the choice of universal kernel. However, the RKHS should then contain a sufficiently rich class of functions to represent all higher order moments. The theorem for a more lenient assumption about kernels has been proven by Fukumizu
[
14
]
via a notion of characteristic kernels.

A.2
Vector Valued RKHS

As defined in the previous section, RKHS is created to structure the space of functions from
\mathcal{X}
to
\mathbb{R}
. A generalization to vector valued function (in general operator valued function) is possible
[
5
]
. Let,
\mathcal{X}
be the domain space, and
\mathcal{W}
be the range which is also a Hilbert space. Let,
L(\mathcal{W})
denotes the space of all bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{W}
.

Definition 7
.

A function
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\rightarrow L(\mathcal{W})
is said to be an operator-valued positive definite kernel if

1.

For each pair
(x,y)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}
,
k(x,y)
is a self-adjoint operator in
L(\mathcal{W})
, i.e. for any
w_{1},w_{2}\in\mathcal{W}
,
\langle k(x,y)w_{1},w_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\langle w_{1},k(x,y)w_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

2.

For any
x_{1},\dots x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}
and any
w_{1},\dots w_{n}\in\mathcal{W}
,

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}\langle w_{i},k(x_{i},x_{j})w_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}\geq 0

Similar to defining the RKHS, we consider the basis functions given by
k_{x}w(\cdot)=k(\cdot,x)w
defined by
k_{x}w(y)=k(x,y)w
for any
x\in\mathcal{X},w\in\mathcal{W}
. The RKHS
\mathcal{H}
, given by the closure of
\mathcal{H}_{0}
, where

\mathcal{H}_{0}=\text{span}\left\{k_{x}w(\cdot):x\in\mathcal{X},w\in\mathcal{W}\right\}

equipped with the inner product,

\left\langle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}k_{x_{i}}w_{i},\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\beta_{i}k_{y_{i}}z_{i}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\alpha_{i}\beta_{j}\langle w_{i},k(x_{i},y_{j})z_{j}\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}

The reproducing property here is that
\langle f(x),w\rangle_{\mathcal{W}}=\langle f,k_{x}w\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
for any
f\in\mathcal{H}
. In particular, if
\mathcal{W}=\mathbb{R}^{p}
the
p
-dimensional Euclidean vector space, then we can choose
w_{i}
’s as the basis of the vector space, hence, the above infinite summands in expressing the functions in the RKHS can be reduced to a finite sum over choices of
w_{i}
, as we can simply take
w_{i}=\boldsymbol{e}_{i}
, for
i=1,2,\dots p
, where
e_{i}
is the
i
-th Euclidean basis vector. Therefore, the functions in the RKHS can be expressed as,

f(\cdot)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{p}\alpha_{ij}k(x_{i},\cdot)\boldsymbol{e}_{j}

A.3
Procrustes Analysis

Procrustes Analysis refers to a form of statistical analysis which is used to analyze the shape of the distribution of datasets. The name “Procrustes” refers to a bandit in greek mythology, who used to cut or stretch the limbs of his victims to fit them into a bed.

There are many variations of Procrustes problem
[
17
]
. The simplest variation asks one to find the optimal rotation matrix
\boldsymbol{P}
such that the matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
can be transformed into
\boldsymbol{B}
using the orthogonal transformation
\boldsymbol{T}
. That means, the problem asks to find
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}=\arg\min_{\boldsymbol{P}}\|\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{PB}\|_{2}
, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the usual Frobenius norm subject to
\boldsymbol{P}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{P}=\boldsymbol{I}
. Here, we consider a general problem instead
[
43
]
, that allows translation, uniform scaling and optimal rotation to approximately transform a matrix
\boldsymbol{A}_{n\times p}
to
\boldsymbol{B}_{n\times p}
. Therefore, we consider the generalized problem,

\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu},\lambda,\boldsymbol{P}}\|\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{PB}\|_{2}^{2}

where
\boldsymbol{\mu}
is a
(p\times 1)
column vector,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is a scaling factor and
\boldsymbol{P}
is an orthogonal matrix. Hence, we consider a Lagrangian
L=\|\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{PB}\|_{2}^{2}+\eta\|\boldsymbol{P}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{P}-\boldsymbol{I}\|_{2}^{2}
, where
\eta
is the Lagrangian tuning parameter. Now, we compute the derivatives of
L
with respect to
\boldsymbol{\mu},\lambda
and
\boldsymbol{P}
as follows.

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol{\mu}}

\displaystyle=-2((\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{PB})^{\intercal}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\intercal})\boldsymbol{1}_{n}

(5)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\lambda}

\displaystyle=2\text{trace}\left(\boldsymbol{PB}((\boldsymbol{A}-\lambda\boldsymbol{PB})^{\intercal}-\boldsymbol{\mu}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\intercal})\right)

(6)

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial L}{\partial\boldsymbol{P}}

\displaystyle=-2\lambda\left(A-\lambda\boldsymbol{PB}-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}\right)\boldsymbol{B}^{\intercal}+2\eta\boldsymbol{P}(\boldsymbol{P}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{P}-\boldsymbol{I})

(7)

Setting these derivatives given in Eq. (
5
)-(
7
) equal to zero to obtain the normal equations, we obtain that,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mu}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{n}\left(\boldsymbol{A}-\widehat{\lambda}\boldsymbol{\widehat{P}B}\right)^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}

(8)

\displaystyle\widehat{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{\text{trace}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}\boldsymbol{BA}^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{I}-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\intercal})\right)}{\text{trace}\left(\boldsymbol{BB}^{\intercal}(\boldsymbol{I}-n^{-1}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{1}_{n}^{\intercal})\right)}

(9)

and
\boldsymbol{\widehat{P}}
is the closest orthogonal matrix to
\dfrac{1}{\widehat{\lambda}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}^{\intercal}
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}
are demean versions of
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
respectively. Thus, the optimal rotation matrix is given by
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}=\boldsymbol{UV}^{\intercal}
such that
\dfrac{1}{\widehat{\lambda}}\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}^{\intercal}
has a singular value decomposition (SVD)
\boldsymbol{U\Sigma V}^{\intercal}
. Clearly, since this solution is independent of
\lambda
, one may obtain
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}
by simply performing SVD of
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}\tilde{B}}^{\intercal}
. The optimal value of
\widehat{\lambda}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}
can then be retrieved by means of Eq. (
8
) and Eq. (
9
).

Therefore, the following steps calculates the optimal transformations from
\boldsymbol{A}
to
\boldsymbol{B}
.

1.

For each column in
\boldsymbol{A}
, compute its average and subtract it from the corresponding entries. Similarly, demand the columns of
\boldsymbol{B}
. Denote this demeaned version as
\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}
and
\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}
respectively.

2.

Let
\boldsymbol{M}=\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}
, and let its SVD is denoted as
\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{U\Sigma V}^{\intercal}
. Optimal rotation is
\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}=\boldsymbol{UV}^{\intercal}
.

3.

Finally, the optimal scaling factor is simply,

\widehat{\lambda}=\dfrac{\text{trace}\left(\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\intercal}\right)}{\text{trace}\left(\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}\right)}

Note that, in particular,
R=\|\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{1}_{n}\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\intercal}-\lambda\boldsymbol{PB}\|_{2}^{2}=\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}-\lambda\boldsymbol{P\tilde{B}}\|_{2}^{2}
is simply a quadratic function in
\lambda
, whose minimum is given by

\displaystyle\min\|\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}-\lambda\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}})-\dfrac{\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}\widehat{\boldsymbol{P}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}})^{2}}{\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}})}

\displaystyle=\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}})-\dfrac{\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{V})^{2}}{\text{trace}(B^{\intercal}B)}

\displaystyle=\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}})-\dfrac{\text{trace}(\boldsymbol{U\Sigma V}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{U}^{\intercal}\boldsymbol{V})^{2}}{\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}})}

\displaystyle=\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}})-\dfrac{\text{trace}(\boldsymbol{\Sigma})^{2}}{\text{trace}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}})}

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
is the diagonal matrix consisting of the singular values of the matrix
\tilde{\boldsymbol{A}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{B}}^{\intercal}
.

Let us compute the conditional probability

Appendix C
More Simulated Examples

Several simulated examples, their embeddings using tSNE, UMAP and LSDR, under different hyperparameters such as perplexities, n-neighbours, kernel bandwidths etc. are described in Figure
14
-
24
. These examples are taken from the analysis of Google PAIR engineers
[
7
,
54
]
, where an extensive comparison of tSNE and UMAP is already available. These are some of the datasets where tSNE and UMAP are found to have better performances, and yet in these datasets, LSDR achieves a similar level of performances compared to them. In particular, in linked circle data (see Figure
18
) and in trefoil knot data (see Figure
24
), both tSNE and UMAP only sees the local structure, and hence outputs two unlinked circles and a large circle respectively. However, there does not exist a continuous transformation that can map those two linked circles into two unlinked circles, and similarly, the trefoil knot, being a non-trivial knot, is not isomorphic to a single large circle. These notions can only be achieved if we look at a global scale, and LSDR takes care of that.

On the other hand, in the two long linear cluster data (see Figure
18
), it is possible to separate the two clusters by a single line, hence even a one-dimensional embedding should be able to perform a reduction of higher quality. However, tSNE and UMAP does not necessarily show this importance in their first reduced component. In other words, it is not necessarily true that the first reduced variable obtained by tSNE or UMAP is necessarily better than its second reduced component. But, LSDR turns out to be capable of using such structure in its first reduced output only, while the secondary component (or reduced variable) provides an orthogonal direction to look at. This phenomenon can be explained by the fact that the metric MDS step in LSDR (which provides the low dimensional embeddings for the skeletal points) actually perform an eigendecomposition, which creates several orthogonal eigen-directions in the order of the most explainable variation of distances through the manifold approximation graph
G_{\alpha}
.
