Observables from the Spinning Eikonal
Andres Luna, Nathan Moynihan, Donal O’Connell, Alasdair Ross

Abstract
We study the classical dynamics of spinning particles using scattering amplitudes and eikonal exponentiation. We show that observables are determined by a simple algorithm. A wealth of complexity arises in perturbation theory as positions, momenta and spins must be iteratively corrected at each order. Even though we restrict ourselves to one-loop computations at quadratic order in spin, nevertheless we encounter and resolve a number of subtle effects. Finally, we clarify the links between our work and various other eikonal approaches to spinning observables.

One of these factors is the exponent of an eikonal function which we denote as
\chi

2

4

Finally, in section
5

We defer the presentation of results up to quadratic order in spin in the 2PM approximation order to appendix
A

B

2
Translation Operators and Eikonal exponentiation

We begin with the assumption that eikonal exponentiation for spinning particles is of the form
Cristofoli:2021jas

\displaystyle\exp\bigg(

\displaystyle{\displaystyle i\chi(\Pi(p_{1},p_{2})\cdot x,p_{1},p_{2})/\hbar}\bigg)^{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}

(1)

\displaystyle=\delta^{a_{1}^{\prime}}_{a_{1}}\delta^{a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{2}}+i\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\,\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot q)\,e^{-iq\cdot x/\hbar}\,\mathcal{A}_{4}(p_{1},p_{2},q)^{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}\,,

where
\mathcal{A}_{4}(p_{1},p_{2},q)
is the all-order
2\rightarrow 2
amplitude with momentum transfer
q
, and we will ignore the quantum remainder. The eikonal phase
\chi
is a function of the momentum that determines the scattering plane, e.g. the plane orthogonal to
p_{1},p_{2}
above. We note especially that
\chi
depends on
x
via its projection onto this plane
\Pi(p_{1},p_{2})\cdot x
.

Let’s now consider a two-particle initial state with spin

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\phi_{b}\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\xi_{1}^{a_{1}}\xi_{2}^{a_{2}}a^{\dagger}\left(p_{1}\right)_{a_{1}}a^{\dagger}\left(p_{2}\right)_{a_{2}}|0\rangle

(2)

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\phi_{b}\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\xi_{1}^{a_{1}}\xi_{2}^{a_{2}}\left|p_{1},a_{1};p_{2},a_{2}\right\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\int\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\phi_{b}\left(p_{1},p_{2}\right)\left|p_{1},\xi_{1};p_{2},\xi_{2}\right\rangle\,,

where
a_{1}
and
a_{2}
are spin (little group) indices and the
\xi_{i}
are vectors in the little group space which pick a specific orientation for the spin expectation value.
We evolve this state with the
S
-matrix in the usual way to give a final state

\displaystyle S\ket{\Psi}=\ket{\Psi}+\int

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\right)\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\,\phi_{b}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}-q,p_{2}^{\prime}+q\right)\ket{p_{1}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime};p_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}

(3)

\displaystyle\times\hat{\delta}\left(2p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot q-q^{2}\right)\hat{\delta}\left(2p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot q+q^{2}\right)i\mathcal{A}_{4}(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime},q)_{a_{1}a_{2}}^{a^{\prime}_{1}a^{\prime}_{2}}\,\xi_{1}^{a_{1}}\xi_{2}^{a_{2}}\,.

We wish to write this final state in terms of the eikonal given in eq. (
1
), and we can do so by noting that the shifted delta functions in the state above can be written in terms of the
unshifted
functions via a translation operator, i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot q-q^{2})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot q+q^{2})=e^{q\cdot Y}\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot q)e^{-q\cdot Y},

(4)

where we have defined

\displaystyle Y_{\mu}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{2}^{\prime\mu}}-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial p_{1}^{\prime\mu}}.

(5)

Adopting the notation
\widetilde{X}=e^{q\cdot Y}Xe^{-q\cdot Y}
, and suppressing the little group indices for the moment, we can include an amplitude to find

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot q-q^{2})\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot q+q^{2})\mathcal{A}_{4}

\displaystyle=e^{q\cdot Y}\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot q)e^{-q\cdot Y}\mathcal{A}_{4}

(6)

\displaystyle=\hat{\delta}(2\tilde{p}_{1}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2\tilde{p}_{2}\cdot q)\left[\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{4}+e^{q\cdot Y}[e^{-q\cdot Y},\mathcal{A}_{4}]\right]\,.

In these expressions, the differential operator
e^{\pm q\cdot Y}
acts on all quantities to its right.
Notice that we have rewritten the four-point amplitude
\mathcal{A}_{4}
in terms of a translated amplitude
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{4}
at the expense of picking up a commutator term.

We recognise the first term on the right of equation (
6
) as the inverse Fourier transform of the eikonal in eq. (
1
), i.e.

\displaystyle i\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}\cdot q)

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}\cdot q)\mathcal{A}_{4}(s,q^{2})^{a_{1}^{\prime}a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}

(7)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar^{4}}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}x\,e^{iq\cdot x/\hbar}\left\{\exp\bigg({i\chi(\Pi(p_{1},p_{2})\cdot x,p_{1},p_{2})/\hbar}\bigg)^{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}-\delta^{a_{1}^{\prime}}_{a_{1}}\delta^{a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{2}}\right\}\,,

and so we conclude that the final state can be written

\displaystyle S\ket{\Psi}=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\right)\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\,\phi_{b}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}-q,p_{2}^{\prime}+q\right)\ket{p_{1}^{\prime};p_{2}^{\prime}}

(8)

\displaystyle\times\left(e^{q\cdot Y}\int\mathrm{d}^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}e^{i\chi(\Pi(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})\cdot x,p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime})}e^{-q\cdot Y}+e^{q\cdot Y}\hat{\delta}(2p_{1}^{\prime}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2p_{2}^{\prime}\cdot q)[e^{-q\cdot Y},\mathcal{A}_{4}]\right),\,

or, restoring the little group indices and expressing everything in terms of shifted functions,

\displaystyle S\ket{\Psi}=

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\right)\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\,\phi_{b}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}-q,p_{2}^{\prime}+q\right)\ket{p_{1}^{\prime},a_{1}^{\prime};p_{2}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}

(9)

\displaystyle\times\left(\int\mathrm{d}^{4}xe^{iq\cdot x}\exp\bigg({i\chi(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2})}\bigg)^{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}+\hat{\delta}(2\tilde{p}_{1}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2\tilde{p}_{2}\cdot q)\left[\mathcal{A}_{4}-\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{4}\right]^{a_{1}^{\prime},a_{2}^{\prime}}_{a_{1},a_{2}}\right)\xi^{a_{1}}\xi^{a_{2}}.\,

For the final state to be solely described by the eikonal, it’s essential that the second term becomes negligible in the classical limit. This is achieved if the amplitudes are shift-invariant up to
\mathcal{O}(\hbar)
corrections — so we expect that the amplitudes obey the property

\displaystyle\hat{\delta}(2\tilde{p}_{1}\cdot q)\hat{\delta}(2\tilde{p}_{2}\cdot q)e^{-q\cdot Y}[e^{q\cdot Y},\mathcal{A}(q)]=\mathcal{O}(\hbar).

(10)

One can easily check this property for the usual Mandelstam
s
and
t
invariants:
Y^{\mu}s^{n}=Y^{\mu}t^{n}=0
, which implies that an amplitude which is a pure polynomial in
s,t
is unaffected by the shift. However, the mass term is an exception, since for example

\displaystyle\tilde{m}_{1}^{2}=m_{1}^{2}+\tilde{p}_{1}\cdot q+\mathcal{O}(\hbar).

(11)

Thankfully, this additional term is taken care of by the delta functions and we conclude that the amplitude with external scalars is indeed invariant at all orders. Including massive, spinning external particles complicates the situation, introducing terms of the form
\epsilon\cdot p
and
\epsilon\cdot\epsilon
, where
\epsilon
is a polarization tensor (or spinor) for the massive spin-
s
particle in the initial or final state.

For the moment, we will confine ourselves to studying linear in spin amplitudes at one loop order, which means we need to show that
e^{-q\cdot Y}[e^{q\cdot Y},\mathcal{A}(q)]=\mathcal{O}(\hbar)
at
\mathcal{O}(s_{i})
. In the classical limit, we can exchange all polarizations for spin-vectors
s_{i}^{\mu}
, and so we conclude that the scattering amplitude is a Lorentz scalar function of the vectors
\{s_{1},p_{1},p_{2},q\}
. Since we have established that Mandelstam variables and mass terms are shift-invariant (up to terms killed by the delta function), we only need to consider the possible terms that arise involving spin. At linear order in spin, using the vectors at our disposal, we can only construct the scalar
\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2},q,s_{1})
. This is clearly invariant under the explicit shifts of the momentum
p_{i}\rightarrow p_{i}\pm q/2
, however we also need to check that it is invariant under the shift of the spin. Since
q
is of order
\hbar
, we can express the shift as a boost

\displaystyle s_{1}(p_{1}+\frac{1}{2}q)=s_{1}(p_{1})+\frac{1}{2m_{1}^{2}}p_{1}^{\mu}(q\cdot s_{1}),

(12)

such that the spin contribution also vanishes. Higher orders terms in this expansion are quantum corrections.

We have shown that
\varepsilon(p_{1},p_{2},q,s_{1})
is invariant under the shift, and therefore so is the amplitude:
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{4}-\mathcal{A}_{4}=0
.

With this in mind, let’s consider the change in an observable
\mathbb{O}
given by

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}=\braket{\Psi|S^{\dagger}\hat{\mathbb{O}}S|\Psi}-\braket{\Psi|\hat{\mathbb{O}}|\Psi}=\braket{\Psi|S^{\dagger}[\hat{\mathbb{O}},S]|\Psi}\,.

(13)

In the limit of large classical spin and converting all commutators into Poisson brackets, we can express this final state in terms of purely scalar functions as
Cristofoli:2021jas

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar^{4}}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi\left(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\right)\int\hat{\mathrm{d}}^{4}q\mathrm{~d}^{4}x\phi_{b}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}-q,p_{2}^{\prime}+q\right)e^{iq\cdot x/\hbar}

(14)

\displaystyle\times\left(\left\{\mathbb{O}_{1}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}\right),e^{i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\right\}_{\text{B.H. }}+\mathbb{O}_{1}(q)e^{i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\right)\left|p_{1}^{\prime},\xi_{1};p_{2}^{\prime},\xi_{2}\right\rangle,

where we have adopted the notation

\displaystyle\left\{\mathbb{O}_{1}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}\right),e^{i\chi\left(x_{\perp},s;\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\right\}_{\text{B.H. }}\equiv e^{i\chi/\hbar}\left(-\{\mathbb{O},\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{\mathbb{O},\chi\}\}+\cdots\right).

(15)

Since all our functions are now scalar (the expectation values of the operators), we can perform the
q,x
integrals by stationary phase, finding the following conditions on
q
and
x

\displaystyle Q^{\mu}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mu}}\chi(\Pi(p_{1}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}Q,p_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}Q)\cdot x,p_{1}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}Q,p_{2}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}Q,\tilde{s}_{1},\tilde{s}_{2})

(16)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mu}}\chi(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2},\tilde{s}_{1},\tilde{s}_{2}),

and

\displaystyle X^{\mu}=b^{\mu}-\frac{\partial}{\partial Q_{\mu}}\chi(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2},\tilde{s}_{1},\tilde{s}_{2}).

(17)

Notice that
all
functions that appear in the eikonal that are dependent on
p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}
must be evaluated on the shifts determined by the translation.

The final state can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\ket{\Psi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\hbar^{4}}\int\mathrm{d}\Phi_{b}\left(p_{1}^{\prime},p_{2}^{\prime}\right)\phi\left(p_{1}^{\prime}-Q,p_{2}^{\prime}+Q\right)e^{iQ\cdot X/\hbar}

(18)

\displaystyle\times\left(\left\{\mathbb{O}_{1}\left(p_{1}^{\prime}\right),e^{i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\right\}_{\text{B.H. }}+\mathbb{O}_{1}(Q)e^{i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\right)\left|p_{1}^{\prime},\xi_{1};p_{2}^{\prime},\xi_{2}\right\rangle,

where now we note that

\displaystyle\tilde{p}_{1}=p_{1}^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}Q=p_{1}+\frac{1}{2}Q,~~~~~\tilde{p}_{2}=p_{2}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}Q=p_{2}-\frac{1}{2}Q.

(19)

We can of course evaluate
\bra{\Psi}S^{\dagger}
by stationary phase too, finding

\displaystyle\bra{\Psi}S^{\dagger}=\int d\Phi(p_{1},p_{2})\phi_{b}(p_{1}-Q,p_{2}+Q)e^{-iQ\cdot X/\hbar}e^{-i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\bra{p_{1},\xi_{1};p_{2},\xi_{2}},

(20)

such that

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{1}

\displaystyle=\braket{\Psi|S^{\dagger}[\mathbb{O}_{1},S]\Psi}

(21)

\displaystyle=\biggl\langle\!\!\!\biggl\langle e^{-i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\left\{\mathbb{O}_{1}(p_{1}),e^{i\chi\left(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2};\langle\tilde{w}\rangle\right)/\hbar}\right\}_{B.H.}+\mathbb{O}_{1}(Q)\biggr\rangle\!\!\!\biggr\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathbb{O}_{1}(Q)-\{\mathbb{O}_{1}(p_{1}),\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{\mathbb{O}_{1}(p_{1}),\chi\}\}+\cdots\,.

We emphasise that after stationary phase, tilde variables (such as
\tilde{p}_{1}
etc) involve the classical shift
Q
rather than the quantum shift
q
.
If we consider the impulse, taking
\mathbb{O}_{1}=\mathbb{P}^{\mu}_{1}
, then only the first term is non-zero and we find the expected result

\displaystyle\Delta p_{1}^{\mu}=Q^{\mu}=-\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{\mu}}\chi(x_{\perp},\tilde{p}_{1},\tilde{p}_{2}).

(22)

The
angular
impulse, or spin kick, is determined by choosing
\mathbb{O}_{1}=\mathbb{W}_{1}^{\mu}
, which now does give rise to an expansions in Poisson brackets involving the spin vector given by

\displaystyle s_{ij}^{\mu}(p)\hat{\delta}(p-p^{\prime})=\frac{1}{m}\braket{p,a_{i}|\mathbb{W}^{\mu}|p^{\prime},a_{j}},

(23)

such that

\displaystyle\Delta s_{1}^{\mu}=s_{1}^{\mu}\left(Q\right)-\left\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\chi,\left\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\right\}\right\}+\cdots,

(24)

where the spin vector without indices is understood as being an expectation value.
We can think of this expression for the spin-kick in terms of a boost and a little group rotation, covariantly expressed as

\displaystyle W^{\mu}_{~\nu}(p,n)=\exp\left(\frac{\theta}{m}\varepsilon^{\mu}_{~\nu}(p,n)\right).

(25)

To see that this generates the terms above, we note that, at leading order, we have

\displaystyle\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\}=\frac{1}{m_{1}}\varepsilon^{\mu}\left(\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s_{1}},p_{1},s_{1}\right),~~~~~\frac{1}{2}\{\chi,\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\}\}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{1}{m_{1}^{2}}\varepsilon^{\mu\nu}\left(\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s_{1}},p_{1}\right)\varepsilon_{\nu}\left(\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s_{1}},p_{1},s_{1}\right),

(26)

and similarly at higher order in
\chi
. This tells us that the infinite sum of Poisson brackets is nothing more than a little group rotation around the axis
n^{\mu}=\frac{1}{\theta}\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s_{\mu}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle s_{1}^{\mu}-\left\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\chi,\left\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\right\}\right\}+\cdots=W^{\mu}_{~\nu}\left(p_{1},\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s_{1}}\right)s_{1}^{\nu}

(27)

We can represent the final spin as the composition of a boost and rotation acting on the initial spin

\displaystyle s_{1}^{\mu}(p_{1})+\Delta s_{1}^{\mu}

\displaystyle=W^{\mu}_{~\nu}K^{\nu}_{~\rho}s_{1}^{\rho}(p_{1})

(28)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{1}{m_{1}}\varepsilon^{\mu}_{~\nu}\left(\frac{\partial\chi}{\partial s},p_{1}\right)\right)\exp\left(\frac{2}{m_{1}^{2}}Q^{[\nu}p_{1\rho]}\right)s_{1}^{\rho}(p_{1})

\displaystyle=s_{1}^{\mu}-(s_{1}(p_{1})\cdot Q)p_{1}^{\mu}-\left\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\right\}-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\chi,\left\{s_{1}^{\mu},\chi\right\}\right\}+\cdots

where on the last line we have expressed the “direct” term at first order in
Q
as

\displaystyle s_{1}^{\mu}(Q)=\frac{1}{m_{1}^{2}}\left((Q\cdot p_{1})s_{1}^{\mu}(p_{1})-(s_{1}(p_{1})\cdot Q)p_{1}^{\mu}\right),

(29)

and dropped the higher order in

\mathbb{O}
stands for either the momentum
p

56

\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(2),\mu}_{i,\textrm{iter}}=\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}_{i}^{(1),\mu}}{\partial u_{i}^{\nu}}\frac{\Delta u_{i}^{(1),\nu}}{2}+\sum_{i}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}_{i}^{(1),\mu}}{\partial a_{i}^{\nu}}\frac{\Delta a_{i}^{(1),\nu}}{2}+\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}_{i}^{(1),\mu}}{\partial b^{\nu}}\frac{\Delta b^{(1),\nu}}{2}.

4
Relation to other approaches

By application of this prescription, we have derived covariant observables from the eikonal phase, namely the classical impulse and the spin kick, up to the second post-Minkowskian approximation, and up to quadratic order in the spin of the particles. Results at these order were first obtained in covariant form in Ref.
Liu:2021zxr
, by means of the spinning generalisation of the (worldline-based) post-Minkowskian effective field theory (PMEFT) of Ref.
Kalin:2020mvi
. The comparison between both results is straightforward, up to expressing them in a basis of vectors. We have verified they perfectly agree with each other.

However, there are other existing derivations of these quantities from the Eikonal in the literature, with supposedly different formulations, e.g. those coming from a canonical approach
Bern:2020buy
and those derived from worldline techniques
Jakobsen:2021zvh
. It is interesting to compare these approaches, and so we do so in this section.

4.1
Relation to covariant (worldline) methods

Let us comment on the relation to another derivation of these observables from an eikonal phase, which was obtained in Ref.
Jakobsen:2021zvh
using a so-called worldline quantum field theory (WQFT). First, we verified that both eikonal phases are a perfect match. Notably, their results for observables are given in terms of the spin tensor, which is related to the (Pauli-Lubanski) spin vector by means of

\displaystyle S^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{m}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}p_{\sigma}S_{\rho},\qquad S^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2m}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}p_{\nu}S_{\sigma\rho}.

(78)

In consequence, we find that to compare their spin-tensor change
\Delta S^{\mu\nu}
with our spin kick
\Delta S^{\mu}
we need to evaluate

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{m}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}(\Delta p_{\sigma}S_{\rho}+p_{\sigma}\Delta S_{\rho}+\Delta p_{\sigma}\Delta S_{\rho}),

(79)

or alternatively

\displaystyle\Delta S^{\mu}=\frac{1}{2m}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\sigma\rho}(\Delta p_{\nu}S_{\sigma\rho}+p_{\nu}\Delta S_{\sigma\rho}+\Delta p_{\nu}\Delta S_{\sigma\rho}).

(80)

While our results agree perfectly, it is worth commenting on some similarities and slight differences in our approaches.
First, they obtain their result through the relation

\displaystyle\Delta\tilde{S}^{\mu\nu}=\frac{4}{m}\tilde{S}^{\rho[\mu}\frac{\partial\tilde{\chi}}{\partial\tilde{S}_{\nu]}^{\ \ \rho}},

(81)

where the tilde once again denotes shifted variables. Indeed, these are naturally chosen by the time-symmetric propagators in their worldline.
Furthermore, they introduce a shift in their eikonal phase of the form

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{i}^{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{S}_{i}^{\mu\nu}+2(u_{i}\cdot S_{i})^{[\mu]}u_{i}^{\nu]}

(82)

\displaystyle\tilde{b}^{\mu}

\displaystyle\rightarrow\tilde{b}^{\mu}+\xi_{2}\tilde{u}_{2}^{\mu}-\xi_{1}\tilde{u}_{1}^{\mu}+\tilde{S}_{2}^{\mu\nu}u_{2,\nu}-\tilde{S}_{1}^{\mu\nu}u_{1,\nu},

(83)

where the
\xi_{i}
depend in general on
b\cdot u_{i}
and
S_{i}^{\mu\nu}u_{i,\nu}
. While such shifts vanish on-shell, they do produce contributions in the directions of
\tilde{u}_{i}
upon taking derivatives of the eikonal phase with respect to
\tilde{b}
and
\tilde{S}^{\mu\nu}
in a similar fashion to eq. (
56
). This shift was demanded to preserve symmetry in their worldline. In order to ensure we rightly reproduce longitudinal terms in the impulse, we also perform the following shift in impact parameter in the Eikonal phase

\tilde{b}^{\mu}\rightarrow\tilde{b}^{\mu}+\xi_{2}\tilde{u}_{2}^{\mu}-\xi_{1}\tilde{u}_{1}^{\mu},

(84)

where

\xi_{1}=\frac{\tilde{b}\cdot(\gamma\tilde{u}_{2}-\tilde{u}_{1})}{\gamma^{2}-1},\qquad\xi_{2}=\frac{\tilde{b}\cdot(\tilde{u}_{2}-\gamma\tilde{u}_{1})}{\gamma^{2}-1}.

(85)

However, we have found that the spin shift in eqs. (
82
)-(
83
) is unsubstantial for the spin vector, while required for the spin-tensor.

4.2
Relation to canonical methods (the
D_{SL}
formula)

Concurently with the worldline result of Ref.
Liu:2021zxr
, results at the 2PM and quadratic in spin orders were obtained using scattering amplitudes in Ref.
Kosmopoulos:2021zoq
, building on the formalism introduced in Ref.
Bern:2020buy
.
The comparison between amplitudes-based results and worldline approaches is obscured by the fact of them being written in terms of different variables, namely canonical or covariant. Such a comparison is now well understood and fairly explored (see, for example, discussions in Ref.
Jakobsen:2022zsx
).

Instead, we will use our newly-built intuition to provide some insight into the peculiar form of the so-called
D_{SL}
formulas, which directly obtain impulses and spin kicks from the eikonal phase. These were reverse-engineered by first obtaining the observables through
\mathcal{O}(G^{2})
using Hamilton’s equations. Let us try to shed some light onto their structure. We start with their usual form

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}^{i}\,

\displaystyle=-\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\,\chi\}-\frac{1}{2}\,\{\chi,\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\,\chi\}\}-D_{SL}\left(\chi,\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\,\chi\}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\,\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\,D_{SL}\left(\chi,\chi\right)\}\,,

(86)

where
\mathbb{O}
can stand for either the transverse momentum
p_{\perp}
or the spin
S
.
Here, we’ll show that the iteration piece

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2}\,\{\chi_{1},\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\chi_{1}\}\}-D_{SL}\left(\chi_{1},\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\chi_{1}\}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\,\{\mathbb{O}^{i},D_{SL}\left(\chi_{1},\chi_{1}\right)\}\,.

(87)

can be instead written as

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial L^{j}}\Delta L^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial p^{j}}\Delta p^{(1),j}\,,

(88)

in complete analogy with equation (
75
), thus replacing the mysterious form of the
D_{SL}
formula with the more natural evaluation in shifted variables.

To prove this, let us start by reviewing these formulas. The different brackets appearing in eq. (
87
) are given by

\displaystyle\{p^{i}_{\perp},f\}\equiv-\frac{\partial f}{\partial b^{i}}\,,\qquad\{S^{i},f\}\equiv\epsilon^{ijk}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S^{j}}S^{k}\,,\qquad D_{SL}\left(f,g\right)\equiv-\,\epsilon^{ijk}S^{k}\,\frac{\partial f}{\partial S^{i}}\,\underline{\frac{\partial g}{\partial L^{j}}}\,.

(89)

The functions
f
and
g
in general depend on
S^{i}
,
p^{i}
and
b^{i}
(though
p^{i}
is taken as inert in the brackets). For this reason, the
L
derivative in the
D_{SL}
bracket is interpreted as

\displaystyle\underline{\frac{\partial}{\partial L^{j}}}\equiv\frac{\partial}{\partial L^{j}}-\frac{p^{j}p^{k}}{p^{2}}\frac{\partial}{\partial L^{k}}

(90)

Here, we write the formula considering only one spinning particle, but the generalisation to two spinning particles is straightforward.
We start by writing separately the first two orders in the post-Minkowskian approximation

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}

\displaystyle=-\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\chi_{1}\},

(91)

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(2),i}

\displaystyle=-\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\chi_{2}\}+\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}

(92)

where
\chi_{n}
is the nPM Eikonal phase, and we focus on the iteration term, which is defined in eq. (
87
).
Reinserting back eq. (
91
) into the iteration, we note we can write

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,\{\chi_{1},\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}\}+D_{SL}\big(\chi_{1},\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}\big)+\frac{1}{2}\,\{\mathbb{O}^{i},D_{SL}\left(\chi_{1},\chi_{1}\right)\}\,.

(93)

Now, from the definition of the Poisson bracket, we know

\displaystyle\{\chi_{1},\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}\}=\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\{\chi_{1},S^{j}\}=\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j},

(94)

where in the last equality we have used eq. (
86
) for spin at 1PM

\displaystyle\Delta S^{(1),i}=-\{S^{i},\chi_{1}\}.

(95)

It is also useful to analyse the expression for the
D_{SL}
bracket, when one of its arguments is the eikonal phase. This is

\displaystyle D_{SL}(\chi_{1},g)

\displaystyle=-\epsilon^{ijk}S^{k}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\partial S^{i}}\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}

(96)

\displaystyle=\{S^{j},\chi_{1}\}\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}=-\Delta S^{(1),j}\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}=\Delta L^{(1),j}\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}},

(97)

where we have used the conservation of total angular momentum

\displaystyle\Delta J^{i}=\Delta S^{i}+\Delta L^{i}=0.

(98)

Then, the iteration can be written as

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\Delta L^{j}+\frac{1}{2}\,\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\}\,.

(99)

Now, since the Poisson bracket acts as a differential on its arguments, we find it satisfies the Leibniz-like condition

\displaystyle\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\}

\displaystyle=\Delta L^{(1),j}\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\}+\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}

(100)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\Delta L^{(1),j}+\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}.

(101)

where, in the first term of the second line we have used eq. (
91
), along with the fact that the action of the Poisson bracket commutes with the derivative
{\partial}/{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}
. Inserting this back we have

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\Delta L^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\,.

(102)

Recalling eq. (
90
), the iteration becomes

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial L^{j}}\Delta L^{j}

\displaystyle-\frac{p^{j}p^{k}}{2p^{2}}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial L^{k}}\Delta L^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\,.

(103)

Finally, we have verified that up to the orders in spin of our interest, the second line in eq. (
103
) satisfies

\displaystyle-\frac{p^{j}p^{k}}{2p^{2}}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial L^{k}}\Delta L^{j}-\frac{1}{2}\{\mathbb{O}^{i},\Delta L^{(1),j}\}\frac{\partial\chi_{1}}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial p^{j}}\Delta p^{(1),j}.

(104)

This allows to write the iteration as

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial L^{j}}\Delta L^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial p^{j}}\Delta p^{(1),j}\,,

which had already been presented in eq. (
88
). We have thus shown that the iteration terms on the
D_{SL}
formula simply encode the evaluation in shifted variables of lower order impulses.

This analysis readily extends to the more generic case considered in Ref.
Bern:2023ity
, where a relaxation of the spin supplementary condition results in extra degrees of freedom, which are encoded in the boost vector
K^{i}
. In that case, the operator
\mathbb{O}
can stand for
K
as well, and we need to generalise the algebra as

\displaystyle\{S_{i},S_{j}\}=\epsilon_{ijk}S_{k}\,,\quad\{S_{i},K_{j}\}=\epsilon_{ijk}K_{k}\,,\quad\{K_{i},K_{j}\}=-\epsilon_{ijk}S_{k}\,,

(105)

while promoting the
D_{SL}
operator to

\displaystyle D_{L}(f,g)\equiv-\epsilon_{ijk}\bigg(S_{k}\frac{\partial f}{\partial S_{i}}+K_{k}\frac{\partial f}{\partial K_{i}}\bigg)\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}\,.

(106)

The crucial point is that due to the generalisation of the algebra, we now have

\displaystyle\{\chi_{1},\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}\}=\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\{\chi_{1},S^{j}\}=\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial K^{j}}\Delta K^{(1),j}.

(107)

Furthermore, we can again consider

\displaystyle D_{L}(\chi_{1},g)

\displaystyle=\{S^{j},\chi_{1}\}\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}}=\Delta L^{(1),j}\frac{\partial g}{\underline{\partial L^{j}}},

(108)

where the two derivatives in eq. (
106
) correspond to the two brackets in the algebra involving
S^{i}
. The rest of the derivation follows in analogy, and results in

\displaystyle\Delta\mathbb{O}_{\textrm{iter}}^{(2),i}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial S^{j}}\Delta S^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial K^{j}}\Delta K^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial L^{j}}\Delta L^{(1),j}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial\Delta\mathbb{O}^{(1),i}}{\partial p^{j}}\Delta p^{(1),j}\,,

again showing the iterations simply come from the evaluation in shifted variables.
We have verified the validity of this formula to second order in the coupling and linear order in either
S^{i}
or
K^{i}
, for the impulse, spin kick and boost kick, as in Ref.
Bern:2023ity
.

5
Conclusions

In this paper, we studied the classical dynamics of spinning particles using scattering amplitudes and eikonal resummation.
A key assumption of our work was
that the loop expansion of scattering amplitudes can be rewritten as an exponential.
This eikonal exponentiation is by now well understood for scalar particles
(see
DiVecchia:2023frv
for a comprehensive review).
However the spinning case is more complicated, since the relevant amplitudes are matrices in spin space.
Consequently, the exponential form of the amplitudes is also a matrix.
It would be particularly useful to deepen our understanding of eikonal exponentation for spinning amplitudes.

Having assumed eikonal exponentiation, our path to determining observables followed standard methods.
We chose an initial state in the domain of validity of the classical theory.
Time evolution was conveniently captured using the
S
matrix.
Physical observables were determined as expectations of appropriate operators n the final state.
This is very much in the spirit of KMOC
Kosower:2018adc
;
Maybee:2019jus
and other work on observables and the eikonal
DiVecchia:2021bdo
;
Cristofoli:2021jas
;
DiVecchia:2022nna
;
DiVecchia:2022piu
;
Gatica:2023iws
;
Georgoudis:2023eke
.
As emphasised in
Cristofoli:2021jas
;
Georgoudis:2023eke
, care is required in the use of eikonal methods to determine the final state in a scattering event because the delta-function support of the eikonal differs from the actual on-shell condition for final states.
In this paper, we introduced a systematic way of accounting for this mismatch involving a translation operator in momentum space.
This translation shifts momenta in the eikonal such that the correct, physical, on-shell condition is satisfied.

The translation led directly to an interesting structure of the eikonal function for spinning particles.
A particularly important point is that, after translation and at the order we studied, the classical eikonal organises itself around the “half-way” point of the scattering event.
That is, the translated eikonal depends on
p+\Delta p/2
: the momentum plus half the total impulse,
rather than on a particle’s momentum
p
alone.
Similarly the classical eikonal function depends on the “half-way” spin rather than initial or final spins.

A beautiful aspect of the eikonal approach to classical dynamics is that the actual values of observables follow from stationary phase conditions.
One of these stationary phase equations picks a specific spatial point
x
, which is the impact parameter
b
at lowest order.
In the case of spin, we found the determination of
x
to be particularly subtle.
It was very important to retain the matrix structure of the amplitude in this step.
In many respects, our approach is similar to the worldline approach of reference
Jakobsen:2021zvh
.
However, in the worldline approach the position was fixed by demanding conservation of angular momentum.
We note that the position appearing in the eikonal in our approach is in general different to the position relevant for conservation of angular momentum.
The two points happen to agree at the accuracy we required in this paper, but in general we expect these points to differ at higher orders.

Our approach correctly reproduced known expressions for the impulse and spin kick at one loop, and to quadratic order in spin.
These observables are known to have a complicated structure, but in our approach this complexity arose from an algorithm which is very simple.
Observables become complicated because as one continues to higher orders, one has to recall that lower order expressions for the observable involved positions, momenta and spins which must all be corrected to go to the next order in accuracy.
This iterative structure generates complex expressions from simple rules.
Clearly it will be interesting to study these ideas at higher orders (both in terms of loops and spins).
We expect the same basic ideas to apply, though considerable care will be required to account for effects which we were able to neglect.

In the context of the spin kick, we imposed the constraint of equation (
71
).
Constraints have also arisen in other approaches to spin, notably reference
Bern:2020buy
.
Clarifying the origin of these constraints would be very useful for further connecting scattering amplitudes with classical physics.
Constraints are very well understood classically; evidently they must also emerge from quantum field theory but how this happens remains unclear.

The literature already contains a number of papers examining amplitudes, observables and classical physics using eikonal methods.
We showed how our approach is consistent with the canonical approach of Ref.
Bern:2020buy
and the worldline formalism of Ref.
Jakobsen:2021zvh
.
Assuming that our assumption of eikonal exponentiation is valid, our approach opens the way towards an all-order understanding of spinning observables in the contect of the eikonal.

It is perhaps worth observing that the KMOC formalism
Kosower:2018adc
;
Maybee:2019jus
already provides an amplitudes-based method of computing classical observables for spinning particles which is expected to hold to all orders in perturbation theory.
However eikonal exponentiation may provide a way to determine observables at large scattering angle; the KMOC formalism (as currently understood) is intrinsically perturbative in the scattering angle.

Much work remains to be performed before we can really say that we understand how spin, amplitudes, and classical physics work together in detail.
We completely neglected radiation, for example.
It would be very interesting to understand how radiation and spin fit together in a more general eikonal picture.
As in the case of scalar particles, radiation requires incorporating the state of the radiation field, presumably as a coherent-state factor along the lines of references
Ciafaloni:2018uwe
;
Cristofoli:2021jas
;
DiVecchia:2022nna
;
DiVecchia:2022piu
.
A more general eikonal along these lines could be very useful for understanding spin effects in gravitational waveforms which have very recently been studied using KMOC
Kosower:2018adc
;
Cristofoli:2021vyo
in references
DeAngelis:2023lvf
;
Brandhuber:2023hhl
;
Aoude:2023dui
.
We look forward to working on this topic in the near future.
