Conditional well-posedness and data-driven method for identifying the dynamic source in a coupled diffusion system from one single boundary measurement
Chunlong Sun, Mengmeng Zhang, Zhidong Zhang

Abstract
This work considers the inverse dynamic source problem arising from the time-domain fluorescence diffuse optical tomography (FDOT). We recover the dynamic distributions of fluorophores in biological tissue by the one single boundary measurement in finite time domain. We build the uniqueness theorem of this inverse problem. After that, we introduce a weighted norm and establish the conditional stability of Lipschitz type for the inverse problem by this weighted norm. The numerical inversions are considered under the framework of the deep neural networks (DNNs). We establish the generalization error estimates rigorously derived from Lipschitz conditional stability of inverse problem. Finally, we propose the reconstruction algorithms and give several numerical examples illustrating the performance of the proposed inversion schemes. Keywords: inverse dynamic source problem, uniqueness, conditional stability, deep neural networks, generalization error estimates, numerical inversion. AMS Subject Classifications: 35R30, 65M32.

Introduction.

1.1
Background and mathematical model.

Fluorescence diffuse optical tomography (FDOT) is one type of diffuse optical tomography that uses fluorescence light from fluorophores in biological tissue, which is rapidly gaining acceptance as an important diagnostic and monitoring tool of symptoms in medical applications
[
2
,
3
]
. The fluorescence contrast agents allow tracking non-invasively and quantitatively specific molecular events or provide some clinically important information
in vivo
. For FDOT, two processes are coupled, namely,
excitation
and
emission
(
fluorescence
), which can be described by a coupled diffusion system as follows.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the background medium with its boundary
\partial\Omega
. Let
u_{e},u_{m}
be the photon density of excitation light and emission light, respectively. Furthermore, we consider the time-dependent fluorophores which means that the absorption coefficient
\mu_{f}
depends on both
x
and
t
. Then we consider the following initial boundary value problems for
u_{e},\,u_{m}
with
T<\infty
:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)u_{e}(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u_{e}&=0,&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times\{0\},\\
\mathcal{B}u_{e}&=g(x,t),&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1.1)

and

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)u_{m}(x,t)&=\mu_{f}(x,t)u_{e}(x,t)=:S[\mu_{f},u_{e}],&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
u_{m}&=0,&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times\{0\},\\
\mathcal{B}u_{m}&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1.2)

where
\mu_{f}
denotes the dynamic distributions of fluorophores inside
\Omega
. The elliptic operator
\mathcal{A}
and boundary condition
\mathcal{B}
are defined as

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{A}\psi&=-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(x)\nabla\psi)+\mu_{a}(x)\psi,\ \psi\in H^{2}(\Omega),\\
\mathcal{B}\psi&=\Big(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}+\beta\psi\Big)\Big|_{\partial\Omega},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1.3)

where
\beta>0
is the Robin coefficient;
\kappa(x)
is the diffusion coefficient;
\mu_{a}
is the absorption coefficient of background medium. The time-domain FDOT is a method to achieve imaging of
\mu_{f}
from the boundary data given by

\varphi(x,t)=\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big|_{\Gamma\times(0,T)},

(1.4)

where
\Gamma
is a nonempty open subset of boundary
\partial\Omega
.

In the aspect of theoretical analysis, the uniqueness of recovering
\mu_{f}(x,t)
requires the whole information of
u_{m}
on
\Omega\times(0,T)
, but it is impractical in applications. However, although the boundary measurement as in (
1.4
) is practical, it is not sufficient to uniquely reconstruct
\mu_{f}(x,t)
and its uniqueness is almost open. Actually, even for recovering the stationary fluorophores (i.e.,
\mu_{f}(x,t)\equiv\mu_{f}(x)
) from (
1.4
), the literature on the uniqueness is relatively rare. An identifiability result of absorption coefficient
\mu_{f}(x)
is established in
[
23
]
, but it requires strong prior assumptions on
\mu_{f}(x)
. The authors prove that for
\Omega=\mathbb{R}_{+}^{3}
,
x=(\tilde{x},x_{3})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}^{1}
, by supposing
\mu_{f}(x)
has the variable separable form
\mu_{f}(x)=p(\tilde{x})q(x_{3})
with known vertical information
q(x_{3})
, the horizontal information
p(\tilde{x})
can be uniquely determined from the boundary measurement (
1.4
) with
\Gamma=\partial\Omega
. Assuming that the support of
\mu_{f}(x)
is at a point, called a point target, the minimal number of observation detectors to determine the point target location as well as its local stability are investigated in
[
19
]
.

In this work, we study the inverse problem of recovering the dynamic source
\mu_{f}(x,t)
from boundary data (
1.4
). Suppose that
S[\mu_{f},u_{e}](x,t;x_{s})
in (
1.2
) possesses the following semi-discrete formulation:

S[\mu_{f},u_{e}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(x)\chi_{{}_{t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})}},

(1.5)

where
\chi
is the indicator function, the time mesh
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
is given and the spatial components
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
are undetermined. For the unknown
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we consider to recover them in
L^{2}(\Omega)
. Since the excitation
u_{e}
is known, after solving
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, the values of
\{\mu_{f}(\cdot,t_{k})\}_{k=0}^{K-1}
can be recovered as
\mu_{f}(\cdot,t_{k})={p_{k+1}(\cdot)}/{u_{e}(\cdot,t_{k})},\ k=0\cdots,K-1

We conclude the inverse problem of time-domain FDOT as follows:

\text{recovering $\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}$ in (\ref{ua-time}) from the one single boundary measurement (\ref{data})}.

2
Preliminaries.

2.1
Eigensystem of operator
\mathcal{A}
.

For the operator
\mathcal{A}
on
H^{2}(\Omega)
with Robin boundary condition, we denote the eigensystem by
\{\lambda_{n},\varphi_{n}(x)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
. Then the following properties will be valid:

•

0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots
and
\lambda_{n}\to\infty
as
n\to\infty
;

•

\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset H^{2}(\Omega)
is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}(\Omega)
.

Furthermore, if
\varphi_{n}
is an eigenfunction of
\mathcal{A}
corresponding to
\lambda_{n}
, so is
\overline{\varphi_{n}}
, where
\overline{\varphi_{n}}
is the complex conjugate of
\varphi_{n}
. Hence we have that the set
\{\varphi_{n}(x)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
coincides with
\{\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
.
The trace theorem yields that
\{\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}|_{\partial\Omega}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.
Also, we denote
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}
as the weighted inner product in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
.

The next lemmas concern the vanishing property and the density of
\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}
on
\partial\Omega
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
\Gamma
is a nonempty open subset of
\partial\Omega
, then for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}
can not vanish on
\Gamma
.

Proof.

See
[
22
, Lemma 2.1]
.
∎

Lemma 2.2
.

The set
\text{Span}\{\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}|_{\partial\Omega}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
is dense in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
.

Proof.

Not hard to see that
H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
is dense in
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
under the norm
\|\cdot\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}
. So it is sufficient to show
\tilde{\psi}\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
vanishes almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega
if
\langle\tilde{\psi},\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

We set
\psi
be the weak solution of the system below:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{A}\psi(x)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\psi&=\tilde{\psi},&&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

We have
\psi\in H^{2}(\Omega)
from the regularity
\tilde{\psi}\in H^{3/2}(\partial\Omega)
, sequentially the Green’s identity can be used. For
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{A}\psi,\varphi_{n}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\psi,\mathcal{A}\varphi_{n}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi,\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}-\langle\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\varphi_{n}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi+\beta\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=\langle\tilde{\psi},\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}.

From
\mathcal{A}\psi=0
on
\Omega
and the fact
\langle\tilde{\psi},\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=0
, we have

\langle\psi,\mathcal{A}\varphi_{n}\rangle_{\Omega}=\lambda_{n}\langle\psi,\varphi_{n}\rangle_{\Omega}=0.

So we have proved that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\langle\psi,\varphi_{n}\rangle_{\Omega}=0
. Recalling the completeness of
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
, it holds that
\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
.
From the definition of weak derivative and Sobolev space, we have
\|\psi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=0
. By the continuity of the trace operator, it gives that

\|\psi\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|\psi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=0,\quad\Big\|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega)}\leq C\|\psi\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}=0.

This means that
\tilde{\psi}=0
almost everywhere on
\partial\Omega
and the proof is complete.
∎

2.2
The auxiliary functions
\{\xi_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
and the coefficients
\{c_{z,n}\}
.

From the above lemma, we are allowed to construct the orthonormal basis
\{\tilde{\xi}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\kappa,\partial\Omega)
. Firstly we set
\tilde{\xi}_{1}=\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}|_{\partial\Omega}/\|\frac{\partial\varphi_{1}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\|_{L^{2}(\kappa,\partial\Omega)},
and assume that the orthonormal set
\{\tilde{\xi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{l-1}
has been built for
l=2,3,\cdots
. Then we set
n_{l}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
be the smallest number such that
\frac{\partial\varphi_{n_{l}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}|_{\partial\Omega}\notin\text{Span}\{\tilde{\xi}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{l-1}
, and pick
\tilde{\xi}_{l}\in\text{Span}\{\frac{\partial\varphi_{n_{l}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}|_{\partial\Omega},\ \tilde{\xi}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{\xi}_{l-1}\}
satisfying

\langle\tilde{\xi}_{l},\tilde{\xi}_{j}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=0\ \text{for}\ j=1,\cdots,l-1,\ \text{and}\ \|\tilde{\xi}_{l}\|_{L^{2}(\kappa,\partial\Omega)}=1.

The density of
\text{Span}\{\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}|_{\partial\Omega}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
in
L^{2}(\kappa,\partial\Omega)
yields that
\{\tilde{\xi}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
is an orthonormal basis in
L^{2}(\kappa,\partial\Omega)
. Also, we have

\tilde{\xi}_{l}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
for each
l\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Next, for
l\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we define
\xi_{l}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
be the weak solution of the system:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{A}\xi_{l}(x)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\xi_{l}&=\tilde{\xi}_{l},&&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(2.1)

Fixing
z\in\partial\Omega
, we define the series

\psi_{z}^{N}\in H^{1}(\Omega)
as

\psi_{z}^{N}(x)=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\overline{\xi_{l}(x)},\ x\in\Omega.

(2.2)

The finite summation
\psi_{z}^{N}
is constructed following the role of Dirac delta function, which reflects the information of the targeted function on a specific point via integration.
With the following lemma, we can give the coefficients
\{c_{z,n}\}
.

Lemma 2.3
.

For each
z\in\partial\Omega
and
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,
\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}
exists and we denote the limit by

.

Proof.

From Green’s identities we have

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\mathcal{A}\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n}^{-1}\Big(\langle\mathcal{A}\psi_{z}^{N},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\frac{\partial\overline{\varphi_{n}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}+\langle\frac{\partial\psi_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{n}^{-1}\langle\beta\frac{\partial\psi_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}+\psi_{z}^{N},\frac{\partial\overline{\varphi_{n}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{n}^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\langle\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\tilde{\xi}_{l}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=:c_{z,n}^{N},

where the system (
2.1
) and the boundary condition of
\varphi_{n}
are used. From the definition of
\{\tilde{\xi}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
, we have
\langle\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\tilde{\xi}_{l}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=0
for large
l
. Hence the value of
c_{z,n}^{N}
will not change if
N
is sufficiently large. This gives that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}
exists and the proof is complete.
∎

\{u_{z}^{N}\}

Quadrature rules
[
31
]
.
We also need to state the quadrature rules applied for approximately computing the integrals in the loss function. Consider a mapping
h:\mathbb{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\bar{d}}\mapsto\mathbb{Y}\subset\mathbb{R}^{m}
. To approximate the integral

\displaystyle\bar{h}:=\int_{\mathbb{D}}h(y)dy

we need the quadrature points

\displaystyle\bar{h}_{N}:=\sum_{i=1}^{N}w_{i}h\left(y_{i}\right).

We further assume that the quadrature error is

\displaystyle\left|\bar{h}-\bar{h}_{N}\right|\leqslant C_{q}\left(\|h\|_{\mathbb{Y}},\bar{d}\right)N^{-\alpha}

(2.5)

for some
\alpha>0
, which is well-known for several classical quadrature formulas.

The uniqueness and conditional stability.

3.1
Representation of the forward operator.

To investigate the inverse problem, we firstly give the representation formula of the forward operator, which maps the unknown source to the boundary flux data. With a small
\epsilon>0
we give the following equation

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})u(x,t)&=p(x),&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,\epsilon),\\
u(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,\epsilon)\cup\Omega\times\{0\},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(3.1)

and prove the next lemma for equation (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

We define
w_{z}^{N}=u_{z}^{N}+\psi_{z}^{N}
, where
u_{z}^{N}
and
\psi_{z}^{N}
satisfy (
2.3
) and (
2.2
) respectively. Then for the solution

of equation (

-\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(z,t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}p(x)w_{z}^{N}(x,t)\ dx.

Proof.

From the definition of
\psi_{z}^{N}
, we see that

(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})\psi_{z}^{N}=0.
This result and (
2.3
) show that

satisfies the equation

(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})w_{z}^{N}=0,\ (x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),

with the boundary condition

and the initial condition
w_{z}^{N}(x,0)=0
. Therefore, Green’s identities give that for each
v\in H^{1}(\Omega)
with the boundary condition
\mathcal{B}v=0
,

\int_{\Omega}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+q)w_{z}^{N}(x,t)v(x)+\kappa\nabla w_{z}^{N}(x,t)\cdot\nabla v(x)\ dx-\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa v(x)\frac{\partial w_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,t)\ dx=0,\ \ t\in(0,T).

From equation (
3.1
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}p(x)w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})u(x,\tau)\ w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau.

Green’s identities and the vanishing initial conditions of
u
and
w_{z}^{N}
give that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+q)u(x,\tau)\ w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+q)w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ u(x,\tau)\ dx\ d\tau,

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u(x,\tau))\ w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\kappa(x)\nabla u(x,\tau)\cdot\nabla w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,\tau)w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau\ dx\ d\tau.

For the boundary condition of
w_{z}^{N}(x)
, we have that

\mathcal{B}w_{z}^{N}=\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\overline{\tilde{\xi}_{l}(x)},\quad x\in\partial\Omega.

Then it holds that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,\tau)w_{z}^{N}(x)\ dx\ d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\ \big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\cdot,\tau),\tilde{\xi}_{l}(\cdot)\big\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}\ d\tau-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\beta\kappa(x)\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,\tau)\frac{\partial w_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\ \big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\cdot,\tau),\tilde{\xi}_{l}(\cdot)\big\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}\ d\tau+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa(x)u(x,\tau)\frac{\partial w_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau,

where the boundary condition
\mathcal{B}u=0
is used.
Hence we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}p(x)w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+q)w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\ u(x,\tau)+\kappa(x)\nabla w_{z}^{N}(x,t-\tau)\cdot\nabla u(x,\tau)\ dx\ d\tau

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\partial\Omega}\kappa(x)u(x,\tau)\frac{\partial w_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(x,t-\tau)\ dx\ d\tau-\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\ \big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\cdot,\tau),\tilde{\xi}_{l}(\cdot)\big\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}\ d\tau

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\ \big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\cdot,\tau),\tilde{\xi}_{l}(\cdot)\big\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}\ d\tau.

For the above equality, differentiating at time
t
gives that for
t\in(0,\epsilon)
,

-\sum_{l=1}^{N}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\ \big\langle\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\cdot,t),\tilde{\xi}_{l}(\cdot)\big\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=\int_{\Omega}p(x)w_{z}^{N}(x,t)\ dx.

From Assumption (
2.4
)
(c)
we have for a.e.
t\in(0,\epsilon)
,

-\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(z,t)=\lim_{N\to\infty}\int_{\Omega}p(x)w_{z}^{N}(x,t)\ dx.

The proof is complete.
∎

With the coefficients
\{c_{z,n}\}
defined in Lemma (
2.3
), we can deduce the next corollary straightforwardly.

Corollary 3.2
.

For a.e.
z\in\partial\Omega
and
t\in(0,\epsilon)
, it holds that

-\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(z,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}p_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}\tau}),

where
u
is the solution of equation (
3.1
) and
p_{n}:=\langle p(\cdot),\varphi_{n}(\cdot)\rangle_{\Omega}
.

Proof.

In this proof, let us the limit
\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle p(\cdot),\overline{w_{z}^{N}(\cdot,t)}\rangle_{\Omega}
.
Fixing
N\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, from the definition of
\psi_{z}^{N}
we have

\psi_{z}^{N}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
. So the Fourier expansion of
\psi_{z}^{N}
can be given as
\psi_{z}^{N}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}^{N}\overline{\varphi_{n}}
, where
c_{z,n}^{N}
is defined in the proof of Lemma (
2.3
). Moreover, from
w_{z}^{N}=u_{z}^{N}+\psi_{z}^{N}
and equation (
2.3
), we have

w_{z}^{N}(x,t)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}^{N}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t})\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}.

The above result together with the regularity
\psi_{z}^{N}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
yields that
w_{z}^{N}(\cdot,t)\in L^{2}(\Omega)
for
t\in[0,\infty)
. Also recall that
p\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, then

\langle p(\cdot),\overline{w_{z}^{N}(\cdot,t)}\rangle_{\Omega}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}^{N}p_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t}).

The condition in Assumption (
2.4
) implies that the Dominated Convergence Theorem can be applied to the above series. So we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}^{N}p_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\lim_{N\to\infty}c_{z,n}^{N}p_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t}).

From (
2.3
), we have

\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle p(\cdot),\overline{w_{z}^{N}(\cdot,t)}\rangle_{\Omega}=\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}^{N}p_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}p_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t}).

The proof is complete.
∎

The next two lemmas will be used in the proof of the uniqueness.

Lemma 3.3
.

Recall that
\{\lambda_{j}\}_{j=1}^{\infty}
is the set of distinct eigenvalues with increasing order, and the coefficients
\{c_{z,n}\}
are defined in Lemma (
2.3
).
For any nonempty open subset
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
, if

\sum_{\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}}c_{z,n}\eta_{n}=0\ \text{for}\ j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}\ \text{and\ a.e.}\ z\in\Gamma,

then
\{\eta_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}=\{0\}
.

Proof.

Fixing
j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, from the proof of Lemma (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle c_{z,n}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}^{-1}\lim_{N\to\infty}\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\mathcal{A}\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j}^{-1}\lim_{N\to\infty}\Big(\langle\mathcal{A}\psi_{z}^{N},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\Omega}-\langle\psi_{z}^{N},\frac{\partial\overline{\varphi_{n}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}+\langle\frac{\partial\psi_{z}^{N}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\overline{\varphi_{n}}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{j}^{-1}\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\langle\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\tilde{\xi}_{l}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}.

With the definition of the orthonormal basis
\{\tilde{\xi}_{l}\}_{l=1}^{\infty}
, the above series is actually finite. This gives that for a.e.
z\in\Gamma
,

\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\tilde{\xi}_{l}(z)\langle\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\tilde{\xi}_{l}\rangle_{\kappa,\partial\Omega}=\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(z),

which leads to
\sum_{\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}}\eta_{n}\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(z)=0
for a.e.
z\in\Gamma
. Assume that
\{\eta_{n}:\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}\}\neq\{0\}
, then the linear independence of the set
\{\varphi_{n}(x):\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}\}
yields that
\sum_{\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}}\eta_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)
is not vanishing on
\Omega
. However, we see that
\sum_{\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}}\eta_{n}\varphi_{n}(x)
is an eigenfunction corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda_{j}
. These and Lemma (
2.1
) give that
\sum_{\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}}\eta_{n}\frac{\partial\varphi_{n}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}
can not vanish almost everywhere on
\Gamma
, which is a contradiction. Hence, we prove that
\{\eta_{n}:\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}\}=\{0\}
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, namely,
\eta_{n}=0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. The proof is complete.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

Set
\Gamma\subset\partial\Omega
be a nonempty open subset and let
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}\eta_{n}
be absolute convergent for a.e.
z\in\Gamma
. Then the result

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}\eta_{n}(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t})=0\ \text{for}\ t\in(0,\epsilon)\ \text{and\ a.e.}\ z\in\Gamma

implies that
\eta_{n}=0
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

Proof.

From
[
34
, Lemma 3.5]
we have that
\sum_{\lambda_{n}=\lambda_{j}}c_{z,n}\eta_{n}=0
for each
j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and a.e.
z\in\Gamma
. Then Lemma (
3.3
) gives the desired result and completes the proof.
∎

The next proposition concerns with the uniqueness result of equation (
3.1
).

Proposition 3.5
.

For the PDE (
3.1
), the data
\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big|_{\Gamma\times(0,\epsilon)}
can uniquely determine
p(x)
.

Proof.

Given
p(x)
and
\tilde{p}(x)
, we set
u
and
\tilde{u}
be the corresponding solutions of equation (
3.1
) with
p
and
\tilde{p}
respectively. If
\frac{\partial u}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big|_{\Gamma\times(0,\epsilon)}=\frac{\partial\tilde{u}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big|_{\Gamma\times(0,\epsilon)},
we need to show that
\|p-\tilde{p}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=0
.

From Corollary (
3.2
), we have

\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{z,n}(p_{n}-\tilde{p}_{n})(1-e^{-\lambda_{n}t})=0,\quad t\in(0,\epsilon).

Then by Lemma (
3.4
), we conclude that
p_{n}=\tilde{p}_{n}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. This leads to the desired result and completes the proof.
∎

Now we can state the uniqueness theorem of the inverse problem (
1.6
).

Theorem 1
.

For equation (
1.2
), the data (
1.4
) can uniquely determine the source (
1.5
).

More precisely, given two sets of unknown
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
and
\{\tilde{p}_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we denote the corresponding solutions of equation (
1.2
) by
u_{m}
and
\tilde{u}_{m}
respectively. Providing

\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big|_{\Gamma\times(0,T)}=\frac{\partial\tilde{u}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big|_{\Gamma\times(0,T)},

it holds that

p_{k}=\tilde{p}_{k}\ \text{in}\ L^{2}(\Omega)\ \text{for}\ k=1,\cdots,K.

Proof.

From equation
(\ref{emission-um})
, if we restrict
t^{*}\in(0,t_{1})
, then we can give the models of
u(x,t^{*})
and
\tilde{u}(x,t^{*})
as follows:

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})u(x,t^{*})&=p_{1}(x),&&(x,t^{*})\in\Omega\times(0,t_{1}),\\
u(x,t^{*})&=0,&&(x,t^{*})\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{1})\cup\Omega\times\{0\},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

and

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})\tilde{u}(x,t^{*})&=\tilde{p}_{1}(x),&&(x,t^{*})\in\Omega\times(0,t_{1}),\\
\tilde{u}(x,t^{*})&=0,&&(x,t^{*})\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{1})\cup\Omega\times\{0\}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

From Proposition (
3.5
), we have that
p_{1}=\tilde{p}_{1}
in the sense of
L^{2}(\Omega)
, which also gives that
u(x,t_{1})=\tilde{u}(x,t_{1})
.

After that, by setting
t^{*}=t-t_{1}
and
t^{*}\in(0,t_{2}-t_{1})
, we have

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})u(x,t^{*})&=p_{2}(x),&&(x,t^{*})\in\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1}),\\
u(x,0)&=u(x,t_{1}),&&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x,t^{*})&=0,&&(x,t^{*})\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1}),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

and

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})\tilde{u}(x,t^{*})&=\tilde{p}_{1}(x),&&(x,t^{*})\in\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1}),\\
\tilde{u}(x,0)&=u(x,t_{1}),&&x\in\Omega,\\
u(x,t^{*})&=0,&&(x,t^{*})\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1}).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

Next, if we set
w=u-u(x,t_{1})
and
\tilde{w}=\tilde{u}-u(x,t_{1})
, we have that

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})w(x,t^{*})&=p_{2}(x)-\mathcal{A}u(x,t_{1}),&&(x,t^{*})\in\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1}),\\
w(x,t^{*})&=0,&&(x,t^{*})\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1})\cup\Omega\times\{0\},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

and

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})\tilde{w}(x,t^{*})&=\tilde{p}_{2}(x)-\mathcal{A}u(x,t_{1}),&&(x,t^{*})\in\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1}),\\
\tilde{w}(x,t^{*})&=0,&&(x,t^{*})\in\partial\Omega\times(0,t_{2}-t_{1})\cup\Omega\times\{0\}.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

From Proposition (
3.5
), we have that
p_{2}=\tilde{p}_{2}
and
u(x,t_{2})=\tilde{u}(x,t_{2})
.

Continuing this argument, we can achieve the desired result and complete the proof.
∎

3.2
Conditional stability of Lipschitz type

We next consider the Lipschitz stability for our inverse problem of recovering
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
in (
1.5
) from the boundary data (
1.4
). Similar as done in
[
40
,
39
]
, the crucial point is to construct an integral identity connecting the inversion input data with the unknown functions.

We introduce the adjoint problem of (
1.2
) as

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (-c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})\phi(x,t)&=0,\qquad\qquad x\in\Omega,\ t\in(0,T),\\
\phi(x,T)&=0,\qquad\qquad x\in\Omega,\\
{\mathcal{B}}{\phi}&=\begin{cases}\omega(x,t),&x\in\Gamma,\ t\in(0,T),\\
0,&x\in\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma,\ t\in(0,T).\end{cases}\\
\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(3.2)

Then the following lemma gives a variational identity.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\Gamma
be a nonempty open subset of boundary
\partial\Omega
. For the given time mesh
\{t_{k}\}_{k=0}^{K}
with
t_{K}=T
, we denote
u_{m}
and
\tilde{u}_{m}
as the solution of equation (
1.2
) with

S[\mu_{f},u_{e}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(x)\chi_{{}_{t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})}}\ \text{and}\ \tilde{S}[\tilde{\mu}_{f},u_{e}]=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\tilde{p}_{k}(x)\chi_{{}_{t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})}}

respectively. Then there holds that

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\int_{\Omega}(p_{k}-\tilde{p}_{k})(x)\ \phi[\omega](x,t)\ dx\ dt=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\omega(x,t)\big(\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial\tilde{u}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big)\ dx\ dt,

(3.3)

where
\phi[\omega]
is the solution to (
3.2
) for
\omega\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))
.

Proof.

We see that
U_{m}:=u_{m}-\tilde{u}_{m}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)U_{m}(x,t)&=\sum_{k=1}^{K}(p_{k}-\tilde{p}_{k})(x)\chi_{{}_{t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})}},&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
U_{m}(x,0)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}U_{m}&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(3.4)

Multiplying
\phi(x,t):=\phi[\omega](x,t)
on the two sides of equation (
3.4
) and integrating them in
\Omega\times(0,T)
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})U_{m}(x,t)\phi(x,t)\ dx\ dt=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\int_{\Omega}(p_{k}-\tilde{p}_{k})(x)\phi[\omega](x,t)\ dx\ dt.

(3.5)

By
\phi(x,T)=0
,
U_{m}(x,0)=0
and
\{\phi,U_{m}\}\subset L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\ (c^{-1}\partial_{t}U_{m})\ \phi\ dx\ dt

\displaystyle={\int_{\Omega}(U_{m}\phi)|_{t=0}^{t=T}\ dx}-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}U_{m}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}\phi)\ dx\ dt

(3.6)

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}U_{m}(c^{-1}\partial_{t}\phi)\ dx\ dt.

The Green formula also gives that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{A}U_{m})\ \phi\ dx\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(\mathcal{A}{\phi})\ U_{m}\ dx\ dt

(3.7)

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}\big(\phi\frac{\partial U_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-U_{m}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big)\ dx\ dt.

Combining (
3.5
)-(
3.7
) together and using (
3.2
), we finally have

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\int_{\Omega}(p_{k}-\tilde{p}_{k})(x)\phi[\omega](x,t)\ dx\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}\big(\phi\frac{\partial U_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-U_{m}\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big)\ dx\ dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\partial\Omega}(\mathcal{B}\phi)\frac{\partial U_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\ dx\ dt.

Henceforth, utilizing the boundary condition

\mathcal{B}\phi=\begin{cases}\omega(x,t),&x\in\Gamma,\ t\in(0,T),\\
0,&x\in\partial\Omega\setminus\Gamma,\ t\in(0,T),\end{cases}\\

we deduce the desired result and complete the proof.
∎

For the set of functions
\{p_{k}(x)\}_{k=1}^{K}
, we use the related vector form as

\vec{p}:=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{K})
and define the operator
L:(L^{2}(\Omega))^{K}\to L^{2}(\Omega\times(0,T))
as

L\vec{p}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}p_{k}(x)\chi_{{}_{t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})}}.

Next we give a bilinear functional with respect to
L\vec{p}
and
\omega(x,t)
by

\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p},\omega)=\int_{\Omega\times(0,T)}(L\vec{p})(x,t)\ \phi[\omega](x,t)\ dx\ dt,

(3.8)

where
\phi[\omega](x,t)
satisfies (
3.2
). The corresponding norm
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is introduced in the following lemma.

Lemma 3.7
.

With (
3.8
), we define the function
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}:l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K})\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}:=\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p},\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}},

(3.9)

where

W:=\{\psi:\psi\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T)),\ \psi\not\equiv 0\}.

Then
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is a norm on
l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K})
.

Proof.

Firstly, let us prove the well-definedness of
\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}
. With Holder inequality, we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p},\omega)|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}\int_{\Omega}\big|p_{k}(x)\ \phi[\omega](x,t)\big|\ dx\ dt

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|p_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\left\|\phi[\omega]\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega\times(t_{k-1},t_{k}))}\leq C\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\Gamma)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|p_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(t_{k-1},t_{k};\Gamma)}\left\|p_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(0,T;\Gamma)}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|p_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

which gives

\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p},\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}}\leq C\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left\|p_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}=C\|\vec{p}\|_{l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K})},\ \omega\in W.

So we have

\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq C\|\vec{p}\|_{l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K})}<\infty
.

Next we prove that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is a norm on
l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K})
. Firstly, it is obviously that

\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\geq 0\ \text{and}\ \|c\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}=|c|\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\ \text{for}\ c\in\mathbb{R}\ \text{and}\ \vec{p}\in l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K}).

Secondly, from the definition of
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
, we can prove that

\displaystyle\|\vec{p_{1}}+\vec{p_{2}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p}_{1}+L\vec{p}_{2},\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}}\leq\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p}_{1},\omega)|+|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p}_{2},\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p}_{1},\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}}+\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p}_{2},\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}}=\|\vec{p}_{1}\|_{\mathcal{L}}+\|\vec{p}_{2}\|_{\mathcal{L}},

which is the triangle inequality.
At last, we need to show that
\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}=0
leads to
\vec{p}=0
.
Given
\|\vec{p}\|_{\mathcal{L}}=0
, we have that
|\mathcal{L}(L\vec{p},\omega)|=0
for each
\omega\in W
. This together with Lemma (
3.6
) yields that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\omega(x,t)\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\ dx\ dt=0,\quad\forall\omega\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T)),

which leads to
\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=0
in
L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))
. Then by Theorem (
1
), we deduce that
p_{k}=0
in
L^{2}(\Omega)
for
k=1,\cdots,K
, i.e.,
\vec{p}=0
.
Now we have proved that
\|\cdot\|_{\mathcal{L}}
is a norm on
l^{1}((L^{2}(\Omega))^{K})
and the proof is complete.
∎

Now we can establish the conditional stability of Lipschitz type for the inverse problem by the weighted norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{L}}
.

Theorem 2
.

We set
\Gamma
to be a nonempty open subset of boundary
\partial\Omega
, and denote the solutions of (
1.2
) with
\vec{p}=(p_{1},\cdots,p_{K})
and
\vec{\tilde{p}}=(\tilde{p}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{p}_{K})
by
u_{m}
and
\tilde{u}_{m}
respectively. Then the next stability result holds

\displaystyle\|\vec{p}-\vec{\tilde{p}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq\Big\|\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial{\tilde{u}}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}.

Proof.

By (
3.3
) and the definition ((
3.9
)), we have

\displaystyle\|\vec{p}-\vec{\tilde{p}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\frac{|\mathcal{L}(L(\vec{p}-\vec{\tilde{p}}),\omega)|}{\left\|\omega\right\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}}

\displaystyle=\mathop{\sup}\limits_{\omega\in{W}}\|\omega\|^{-1}_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}\Big|\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Gamma}\omega(x,t)\big(\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial\tilde{u}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\big)\ dx\ dt\Big|.

With Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, we immediately deduce that

\|\vec{p}-\vec{\tilde{p}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq\Big\|\frac{\partial u_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial{\tilde{u}}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}.

The proof is complete.
∎

4
Generalization error estimates.

In this section, we introduce the proposed reconstruction scheme with its associated error estimates. This lays the foundations of our approach that we subsequently test numerically. We decompose this inverse problem (
1.6
) into solving a forward problem to get
u_{e}
and an inverse problem for recovering
(\mu_{f},u_{m})
. More precisely, we solve the forward problem (
1.1
) with the known boundary input to obtain
u_{e}(x,t)
, and then reconstruct
\mu_{f}
and
u_{m}
from the observation data (
1.4
) with the obtained solution
u_{e}
.

4.1
Loss functions.

We assume that the activation function is of
C^{2}
regularity. This includes activation such as
\sigma=\tanh
.
Then, for the neural network
u_{e,\theta_{e}}
,
u_{m,\theta_{m}}
defined by (
2.4
) in terms of
\sigma
, we set
u_{e,\theta_{e}},u_{m,\theta_{m}}\in C^{l}(\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T])
for

l=0,1,2
. For the network parameters

\theta_{e},\theta_{m}\in\Theta:=\{\{(W_{k},b_{k})\}_{k=1}^{K}:W_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}\times d_{k-1}},b_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{k}}\}
,
the set of all possible trainable parameters,
u_{e,\theta_{e}}(x,t)
,
u_{m,\theta_{m}}(x,t)
up to second order derivatives are bounded in
\overline{\Omega}\times[0,T]
for any specified
\theta_{e},\theta_{m}
.

For given boundary input, it is necessary to solve the forward problem
(
1.1
) first. We denote the approximate solution of (
1.1
) is
u_{e,\theta_{e}}
, which is a deep neural networks function with the networks parameters
\theta_{e}
. Next the following residuals for the forward problem (
1.1
) are introduced.

•

Interior PDE residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{e}}(x,t):=\left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)u_{e,\theta_{e}}(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

•

Spatial boundary residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{e}}(x,t):=\mathcal{B}u_{e,\theta_{e}}(x,t)-\mathcal{B}u_{e}(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

•

Temporal boundary (initial status) residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{e}}(x):=u_{e,\theta_{e}}(x,0),\quad x\in\Omega.

We minimize the following loss function

\displaystyle J_{1}(\theta_{e})=\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{e}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{e}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{e}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}

(4.1)

to search for the optimal parameters
\theta^{*}_{e}
.
The obtained
u_{e}^{*}(x,t):=u_{e,\theta_{e}^{*}}(x,t)
can be regarded as approximate solution of the forward problem (
1.1
).

Next, we introduce a form loss function of data-driven solution of inverse problems
that ensures reconstruction accuracy owing to the conditional stability of the inverse problem.
For this purpose, we need to define suitable residuals measuring the errors of the governed system and the input data.
Define the following residuals for the emission system.

•

Interior PDE residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m},\theta_{f},\theta_{e}^{*}}(x,t):=(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})u_{m,\theta_{m}}(x,t)-\mu_{f,\theta_{f}}(x,t)u_{e,\theta_{e}^{*}}(x,t;x_{s}),\quad(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T).

•

Spatial boundary residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}}(x,t):=\mathcal{B}u_{m,\theta_{m}}(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T).

•

Temporal boundary (initial status) residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}}(x):=u_{m,\theta_{m}}(x,0),\quad x\in\Omega.

•

Data residual

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}}(x,t):=\frac{\partial u_{m,\theta_{m}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\varphi^{\delta}(x,t),\quad(x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T).

Thus, a loss function minimization scheme seeks to minimize these residuals comprehensively with some weights balancing different residuals. We define a new loss function involving the norm of the derivatives for some residuals, namely

\displaystyle J_{2}(\theta_{f},\theta_{m})=

\displaystyle\lambda\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m},\theta_{f},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

(4.2)

\displaystyle+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}}\|_{H^{1}(0,T;{H^{1}(\partial\Omega)})},

where
\lambda
is a hyper-parameter to balance the residuals between the knowledge of PDE and the measurement data.

To evaluate the integrals in (
4.1
) and (
4.2
) numerically, we introduce the training sets

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{d}:=\left\{(x_{n},t_{n}):(x_{n},t_{n})\in\Gamma\times(0,T],\quad n=1,2,\cdots,N_{d}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{int}:=\left\{(\widetilde{x}_{n},\widetilde{t}_{n}):(\widetilde{x}_{n},\widetilde{t}_{n})\in\Omega_{T},\quad n=1,2,\cdots,N_{int}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{tb}:=\left\{(\overline{x}_{n},0):\overline{x}_{n}\in\Omega,\quad n=1,2,\cdots,N_{tb}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{sb}:=\left\{(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n}):(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n})\in\partial\Omega_{T},\quad n=1,2,\cdots,N_{sb}\right\}.

Applying these sets and the numerical quadrature rules
[
31
]
, we can consider the following two empirical loss function

\displaystyle J_{1}^{N}(\theta_{e})=\sum_{n=1}^{\bar{N}_{int}}\bar{\omega}_{n}^{int}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{e}}(\widetilde{x}_{n},\widetilde{t}_{n})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\bar{N}_{tb}}\bar{\omega}_{n}^{tb}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{e}}(\overline{x}_{n})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\bar{N}_{sb}}\bar{\omega}_{n}^{sb}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{e}}(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n})\Big|^{2},

(4.3)

and

\displaystyle J_{2}^{N}(\theta_{f},\theta_{m})=

\displaystyle\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{N_{d}}\omega_{n}^{d}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}}(x_{n},t_{n})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{int}}\omega_{n}^{int}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{f},\theta_{m},\theta_{e}^{*}}(\widetilde{x}_{n},\widetilde{t}_{n})\Big|^{2}

(4.4)

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{tb}}\omega_{n}^{tb,0}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}}(\overline{x}_{n})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{tb}}\omega_{n}^{tb,1}\Big|\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}}(\overline{x}_{n})\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{sb}}\omega_{n}^{sb,0}\Big|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta}(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{sb}}\omega_{n}^{sb,1}\Big|\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n})\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{sb}}\omega_{n}^{sb,2}\Big|\frac{\partial\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta}}{\partial t}(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n})\Big|^{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{N_{sb}}\omega_{n}^{sb,3}\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta}}{\partial t\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}(\widehat{x}_{n},\widehat{t}_{n})\Big|^{2},

where the coefficients

\bar{\omega}^{int}_{n},\;\bar{\omega}^{tb}_{n},\;\bar{\omega}^{sb}_{n},\ \omega^{d}_{n},\;\omega^{int}_{n},\;\omega^{tb,k}_{n},\;\omega^{sb,j}_{n}
with
k=0,1
and
j=0,1,2,3
are the quadrature weights. Applying the quadrature rules (
2.5
), it is easy to see that the error for the loss function is

\displaystyle|J_{1}(\theta_{e})-J_{1}^{N}(\theta_{e})|

\displaystyle\leq C\max\{\bar{N}_{int}^{-\bar{\alpha}_{int}},\bar{N}_{tb}^{-\bar{\alpha}_{tb}},\bar{N}_{sb}^{-\bar{\alpha}_{sb}}\},

(4.5)

\displaystyle|J_{2}(\theta_{f},\theta_{m})-J_{2}^{N}(\theta_{f},\theta_{m})|

\displaystyle\leq C\max_{k=0,1,j=0,1,2,3}\{N_{d}^{-\alpha_{d}},N_{int}^{-\alpha_{int}},N_{tb}^{-\alpha_{tb,k}},N_{sb}^{-\alpha_{sb,j}}\},

where
C
depends on the continuous norm
\|\cdot\|_{C(\Omega)}
of the integrands.
Therefore, the underlying solutions and neural networks have to be sufficiently regular such that the residuals can be approximated to high accuracy by the quadrature rules.

4.2
The estimates of generalization error.

We give the regularity estimate of the PDEs with non-homogenous boundary condition.
The following
L^{p}
estimate on elliptic system can be found in reference
[
1
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
w(x)\in H^{2}(\Omega)
solve

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} -\Delta w&=f(x),&&x\in\Omega,\\
\frac{\partial w}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}+\beta w&=b(x),&&x\in\partial\Omega,\end{aligned}\end{cases}

for
0<\beta_{0}\leq\beta(x)\in C(\partial\Omega)
. Then we have

\left\|w\right\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\tilde{b}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)})\leq C(\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|b\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)}),

where
\tilde{b}(x)\in H^{1}(\Omega)
is the extension of
b(x)\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Consider the following PDEs with non-homogenous boundary condition

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)V(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
V(x,0)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}V(x,t)&=B(x,t),&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(4.6)

if the boundary condition
B\in H^{1}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})
, then there exists a unique solution
V\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))
with the estimate

\displaystyle\|V\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\|B\|_{H^{1}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})}.

Proof.

For any fixed
t\in[0,T]
, define
\Lambda_{b}(x,t)
from

\displaystyle\begin{cases}-\mathcal{A}\Lambda_{b}(x,t)=0,&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\Lambda_{b}(x,t)=B(x,t),&x\in\partial\Omega.\end{cases}

(4.7)

By the regularity estimate on elliptic problem and Lemma (
4.1
), there exists a unique solution
\Lambda_{b}(\cdot,t)\in H^{2}(\Omega)
satisfying
\|\Lambda_{b}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C_{t}\|\tilde{B}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}
. Since
\beta(x)
is independent of
t
, we also have for
k=0,1,2
that

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}^{k}\Lambda_{b}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\partial_{t}^{k}\tilde{B}(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\leq C\|\partial_{t}^{k}B(\cdot,t)\|_{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)},\quad t\in[0,T].

Let
AC[0,T]
be the space of absolutely continuous functions. For
B(x,\cdot)\in AC[0,T]
, it follows
\Lambda_{b}(x,\cdot)\in AC[0,T]
by (
4.7
). So we can decompose
V=V_{c}+\Lambda_{b}
, where
V_{c}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} (c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A})V_{c}(x,t)&=-c^{-1}\partial_{t}\Lambda_{b},&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\mathcal{B}V_{c}(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times[0,T],\\
V_{c}(x,0)&=-\Lambda_{b}(x,0)=0,&&x\in\Omega.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

From the regularity estimates in
[
12
]
, there holds

\displaystyle\|V_{c}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\|\partial_{t}\Lambda_{b}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C\|\partial_{t}B\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})}.

Sequentially, we have

\displaystyle\|V\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq\|\Lambda_{b}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\|V_{c}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C(\|B\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})}+\|\partial_{t}B\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})})

\displaystyle\leq C\|B\|_{H^{1}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})}.

The proof is complete.
∎

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \mathcal{E}_{G,u_{e}}:=&\left\|u_{e}^{*}-u_{e,ex}\right\|_{C\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)},\\
\mathcal{E}_{G,\vec{p}}:=&\left\|{\vec{p}_{*}-\vec{p}_{ex}}\right\|_{\mathcal{L}},\\
\mathcal{E}_{G,u_{m}}:=&\left\|u_{m}^{*}-u_{m,ex}\right\|_{C\left([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega)\right)},\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(4.8)

where

\hat{u}_{m}:=u_{m}^{*}-u_{m,ex}
with

u_{m}^{*}=u_{m,\theta_{m}^{*}}=u_{m,\theta_{m}^{*}}[\mu_{f,\theta_{f}^{*}},u_{e,\theta_{e}^{*}}]=u^{*}_{m}[\mu^{*}_{f},u^{*}_{e}],

we have the equation

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)\hat{u}_{m}(x,t)&=\mu^{*}_{f}u^{*}_{e}-\mu_{f,ex}u_{e,ex}+\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m}^{*},\theta_{f}^{*},\theta_{e}^{*}},&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\hat{u}_{m}(x,0)&=\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x),&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\hat{u}_{m}(x,t)&=\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x,t),&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

and the observation data

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=\frac{\partial u_{m,\theta_{m}^{*}}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial u_{m,ex}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x,t)+\varphi^{\delta}-\varphi,\quad(x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T].

We make the decomposition
\hat{u}_{m}:=\hat{u}_{m,1}+\hat{u}_{m,2}
, where
\hat{u}_{m,1}
,
\hat{u}_{m,2}
satisfy

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)\hat{u}_{m,1}(x,t)&=\mu^{*}_{f}u^{*}_{e}-\mu_{f,ex}u_{e,ex},&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\hat{u}_{m,1}(x,0)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\hat{u}_{m,1}(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,1}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,2}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}=\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x,t)+(\varphi^{\delta}-\varphi)-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,2}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\quad(x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T],

and

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)\hat{u}_{m,2}(x,t)&=\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m}^{*},\theta_{f}^{*},\theta_{e}^{*}},&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\hat{u}_{m,2}(x,0)&=\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x),&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\hat{u}_{m,2}(x,t)&=\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x,t),&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

respectively.

Next we split
\hat{u}_{m,1}=\hat{u}_{m,11}+\hat{u}_{m,12}
, where
\hat{u}_{m,11}
,
\hat{u}_{m,12}
satisfy

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)\hat{u}_{m,11}(x,t)&=(\mu^{*}_{f}-\mu_{f,ex})u_{e,ex},&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\hat{u}_{m,11}(x,0)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\hat{u}_{m,11}(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(4.11)

with

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,11}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,1}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,12}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x,t)+(\varphi^{\delta}-\varphi)-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,2}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,12}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}},\;(x,t)\in\Gamma\times(0,T],

and

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} \left(c^{-1}\partial_{t}+\mathcal{A}\right)\hat{u}_{m,12}(x,t)&=\mu^{*}_{f}(u^{*}_{e}-u_{e,ex}),&&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
\hat{u}_{m,12}(x,0)&=0,&&x\in\Omega,\\
\mathcal{B}\hat{u}_{m,12}(x,t)&=0,&&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T).\end{aligned}\end{cases}

For the inverse problem (
4.11
)-(
4.12
),
according to conditional stability result, i.e, Theorem (
2
), we get

\displaystyle\|{\vec{p}_{*}-\vec{p}_{ex}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq

\displaystyle C\Big\|\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,11}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C\Big\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}(x,t)+(\varphi^{\delta}-\varphi)-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,2}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}-\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,12}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\|\varphi^{\delta}-\varphi\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\Big\|\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,2}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\Big\|\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,12}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\Big\|\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,2}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))}+\Big\|\frac{\partial\hat{u}_{m,12}}{\partial{\overrightarrow{\bf n}}}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\partial\Omega\times(0,T))}+\delta.

(4.13)

Using trace theorem and Sobolev embedding theorem, we further get

\displaystyle\|{\vec{p}_{*}-\vec{p}_{ex}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\|\hat{u}_{m,2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3/2}(\Omega))}+\|\hat{u}_{m,12}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{3/2}(\Omega))}+\delta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\|\hat{u}_{m,2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\hat{u}_{m,12}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}+\delta.

(4.14)

According to the estimates in
[
12
]
, the second term of the inequality (
4.14
) satisfies

\displaystyle\|\hat{u}_{m,2}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m}^{*},\theta_{f}^{*},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\ \quad+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})}+\|\partial_{t}\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;{H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)})}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m}^{*},\theta_{f}^{*},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{H^{1}(0,T;{H^{1}(\partial\Omega)})}\Big).

(4.15)

Using (
4.10
), the third term in (
4.14
) satisfies

\displaystyle\|\hat{u}_{m,12}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{2}(\Omega))}\leq

\displaystyle C\|\mu^{*}_{f}(u^{*}_{e}-u_{e,ex})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\|(u^{*}_{e}-u_{e,ex})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-3/2}(\partial\Omega))}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}\Big).

(4.16)

Combining (
4.13
)-(
4.16
) together and using the Sobolev embedding theorem, we get the generalization error estimate for
\vec{p}

\displaystyle\|{\vec{p}_{*}-\vec{p}_{ex}}\|_{\mathcal{L}}\leq

\displaystyle C\Big(\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\partial\Omega))}+\delta

\displaystyle\quad+\|\mathcal{R}_{d,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(\Gamma\times(0,T))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{{int},\theta_{m}^{*},\theta_{f}^{*},\theta_{e}^{*}}\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}+\|\mathcal{R}_{tb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\quad+\|\mathcal{R}_{sb,\theta_{m}^{*}}\|_{H^{1}(0,T;{H^{1}(\partial\Omega)})}\Big).

(4.17)

The formulations in (
4.9
) are generated by applying (
4.10
) and (
4.17
), with the quadrature rules (
2.5
) respectively. The proof is complete.
∎

The above estimates include the difference between the underlying solution and the approximation solution produced by data-driven method of the inverse problem, which represent the approximation of the problem in a finite dimensional space, and essentially reflect the stability due to both the model itself and the reconstruction scheme. Therefore, based on the result of Theorem (
3
), we can conduct the reconstruction algorithms both for excitation process to solve the direct problem and emission process to solve the inverse problem.

6
Conclusion and remark

In this work, the inverse dynamic source problem by the one single boundary measurement in finite time domain is considered, which is arising from the time-domain fluorescence diffuse optical tomography (FDOT). We establish the uniqueness theorem and the conditional stability of Lipschitz type for the inverse problem by a defined weighted norm. A deep neural network-based reconstruction scheme with a new loss function has been proposed to solve the inverse problem. Generalization error estimates based on the Lipschitz conditional stability of inverse problems have been established, which provide a measure of the stability and accuracy of the proposed method in solving the inverse problem. Some numerical experiments have been conducted, which indicate the effectiveness of the approach in this work.
