Theory of Topological Nernst and Thermoelectric Transport in Chiral Magnets
Zachariah Addison, Lauren Keyes, Mohit Randeria

Abstract
We calculate the thermoelectric transport of spin-orbit coupled conduction electrons in the presence of topological spin textures. We show, within a controlled, semiclassical approach that includes all phase space Berry curvatures, that the Nernst effect has two contributions in addition to the usual effect proportional to a magnetic field. These are an anomalous contribution governed by the momentum-space Berry curvature and proportional to net magnetization, and a topological contribution determined by the real-space Berry curvature and proportional to the topological charge density, which is non-zero in skyrmion phases. We derive a generalized Mott relation expressing the thermoelectric tensor as the chemical potential derivative of the conductivity tensor and show how the Sondheimer cancellation in the Nernst effect is evaded in chiral magnets.

Mott Relation.
Temperature gradients couple to the distribution function via the real-space gradient operator

\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}=\bm{r}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}[T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}+\hat{\bm{m}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{\hat{m}}]

in the Boltzmann equation. In contrast, electric field perturbations only enter the Boltzmann equation through the
semiclassical equations of motion:
\dot{\bm{r}}\rightarrow\dot{\bm{r}}_{E}
and
\dot{\bm{k}}\rightarrow\dot{\bm{k}}_{E}
(see Appendix
D

\dot{\bm{r}}_{E}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}+\dot{\bm{k}}_{E}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}
takes the form
\dot{\bm{r}}\cdot{\bm{E}}\partial_{\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}}
. This allows a simple relationship between the different field dependent perturbations to the distribution function to be established.

The solution to the Boltzmann equation requires inverting the operator

Model Calculations.
Given an arbitrary band structure, one can use equations (
32
) and (
42
) to compute the thermoelectric conductivity and Nernst signals. As illustrative examples, we calculate these transport signals in 2D for a system with a parabolic dispersion and for a tight binding model.

Consider parabolic bands with arbitrary SOC in 2D. To calculate
{A}(\mu)
, we use
\varepsilon_{\pm}(\bm{k})=\varepsilon_{0}+a^{2}k^{2}\pm J
and Eq. (
32
) to find that
{A}(\mu)=2\pi^{2}/3\,(\Theta[\mu-(\varepsilon_{0}-J)]-\Theta[\mu-(\varepsilon_{0}+J)])
(see dotted lines in Fig.
2
(a)). The non-analytic structure in
{A}(\mu)
occurs at the band edges. For the Nernst signal
{N}(\mu)=-4\pi^{3}tJ/3\mu^{2}\,\Theta(\mu-(\varepsilon_{0}+J))
and is nonzero only when electron states in both bands are occupied (see Fig.
2
(b)).

Fig.
2
a shows
{A}(\mu)
and Fig.
2
b shows

\varepsilon(\bm{k})=-2t(\cos(k_{x}a)+\cos(k_{y}a))
. The quadratic band approximation with
\varepsilon_{0}=-4t
is marked by the dashed red lines. The non-analytic jumps in

and

are due to the non-analyticity of the density of states as the chemical potential crosses the band edge.

Near the Van Hove singularities,

and

are sharply enhanced as is commonly recognized in signatures of thermoelectric transport

;

Discussion.
Through a controlled semiclassical analysis in the regime where
\lambda<J\ll E_{F}
,
a\ll l\ll L_{s}
, we have shown that the thermoelectric conductivity is composed of the sum of an anomalous contribution, proportional to the average magnetization, and a topological contribution, proportional to the topological charge density. In addition, we have shown that a Mott relation holds even in the presence of a nonzero topological charge density, thus justifying a commonly held assumption
Shiomi et al. 2013
;
Hirschberger et al. 2020
;
Kolincio et al. 2021
;
Mizuta and Ishii 2016
;
Gayles et al. 2018
;
Xia et al. 2019
. As a consequence of the Mott relation, the thermoelectric conductivity and Nernst signal are enhanced at points in the band structure in which there is a rapidly changing density of states, such as near van Hove singularities.

We estimate the order of magnitude of the Nernst signal by approximating Eq. (
42
) with 3D parabolic bands and find
N^{T}\sim(k_{B}/e)(k_{F}\ell)(k_{B}T/E_{F})(n_{\text{top}}a^{2}){N}(\mu)
. In a skyrmion material with
a/L_{s}=1/100
,
n_{\text{top}}a^{2}\approx 10^{-3}
.
We use
k_{B}T/E_{F}\approx 10^{-4}
, and
10<k_{F}\ell<100
. For
(tJ/\mu^{2})\approx 1/3
, we find
{N}(\mu)\approx 100
.
We thus estimate
N^{T}
in the range (
10^{-3}
–
10^{-2})\,(k_{B}/e)
which is equal to 86 - 860 nV/K, similar to what has been
measured in experiments; see e.g.,
Hirschberger et al. 2020
.

We have considered the regime
a\ll\ell\ll L_{s}
in the analysis above. The question of how to solve the Boltzmann equation when
a\ll L_{s}\ll\ell
, the analog of the “strong field limit”, is an open question. We have focused here on the intrinsic part of the anomalous thermoelectric response arising from k-space Berry curvature, known to be the dominant contribution to the anomalous Hall response in many materials. The question of how extrinsic effects like skew and side-jump scattering impact the thermoelectric response has not been explored. These are all important questions for future research.

Appendix B
Thermal Perturbations and the Boltzmann Equation

To calculate the Nernst effect we must calculate the charge current to first order in spatial derivatives of the temperature. Contributions proportional to the scattering time
\tau
derive from temperature gradient induced corrections to the electronic distribution function that can be determined by solving the Boltzmann equation.

\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\bm{\xi})=\partial_{t}f(\bm{\xi})+\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}f(\bm{\xi})+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}f(\bm{\xi})

(120)

Note that there is no explicit time-dependence in
\bm{H}
such that
\partial_{t}f(\bm{\xi})=0
. For simplicity in what follows we will suppress the band index. Here we solve this equation under the relaxation time approximation

\dfrac{d}{dt}f(\bm{\xi})\approx-\dfrac{f(\bm{\xi})-f^{0}(\bm{\xi})}{\tau}

(121)

with relaxation time
\tau
. Here
f^{0}(\bm{\xi})
is the equilibrium distribution function

f^{0}(\bm{\xi})=\dfrac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon(\bm{\xi})-\mu)/k_{B}T}+1}

(122)

In the presence of temperature gradients we have

-\dfrac{f(\bm{\xi})-f^{0}(\bm{\xi})}{\tau}=\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot(\bm{\nabla}_{r}\hat{\bm{m}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{m}f(\bm{\xi})+\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}f(\bm{\xi}))+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}f(\bm{\xi})

(123)

where we have used

\bm{\nabla}_{r}f(\bm{\xi})=\bm{\nabla}_{r}\hat{\bm{m}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{m}f(\bm{\xi})+\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}f(\bm{\xi})

(124)

with

\bm{\nabla}_{m}=\bigg(\dfrac{\partial}{\partial m_{r_{x}}},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial m_{r_{y}}},\dfrac{\partial}{\partial m_{r_{z}}}\bigg)

(125)

Without the statistical drive induced by the temperature gradient the distribution function is at equilibrium and the Boltzmann equation dictates

0=\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}f^{0}(\bm{\xi})+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}f^{0}(\bm{\xi})

(126)

B.1
First Order in Temperature Gradients

To determine the distribution function to first order in temperature gradients we may make a power series expansion of
f(\bm{\xi})

f(\bm{\xi})\approx f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})]+g(\bm{\xi})

(177)

where

is first order in temperature gradients (i.e.
g(\bm{\xi})\propto\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})

\displaystyle\dfrac{-g(\bm{\xi})}{\tau}=\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot(\bm{\nabla}_{r}\hat{\bm{m}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{m}g(\bm{\xi})+\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})])+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}g^{T}(\bm{\xi})

\displaystyle\bigg(\tau(\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}\hat{\bm{m}}(\bm{r})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{m}+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k})+1\bigg)g(\bm{\xi})=-\tau\dot{\bm{r}}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})]

(178)

This is in the form

\bigg(1+\mathds{P}\bigg)g(\bm{\xi})=-\tau\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi}))]

(179)

g(\bm{\xi})=-\tau\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}(\mathds{P})^{n}\bigg(\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})]\bigg)

(180)

Note here we consider a constant temperature gradients (i.e.
\bm{\nabla}_{r}T(\bm{r})=\text{constant}
) such that
\mathds{P}
acts only acts on
\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})
and
\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})]

\bm{\nabla}_{r}
. With this simplification we have

\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})

B.2
Scaling of Terms in Perturbed Distribution Function

We have:

\tau(\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k})\approx\tau\bigg(\dfrac{\bm{\nabla}_{k}\mathcal{E}(\bm{\xi})}{\hbar}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}-\dfrac{\bm{\nabla}_{r}\mathcal{E}(\bm{\xi})}{\hbar}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}\bigg)

(183)

Where we have used
\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})\approx\mathcal{E}(\bm{\xi})
. These terms multiply
\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})]
in
g(\bm{\xi})
and will thus contribute where
\partial_{T}f^{0}[\widetilde{\mathcal{E}}(\bm{\xi})]
is large, which is in a range of

around the Fermi energy. Therefore we have

\displaystyle\tau\bigg(\dfrac{\bm{\nabla}_{k}\mathcal{E}(\bm{\xi})}{\hbar}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}-\dfrac{\bm{\nabla}_{r}\mathcal{E}(\bm{\xi})}{\hbar}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k}\bigg)\sim\tau\bigg(\bm{v}_{F}\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}+\dfrac{\lambda}{\hbar L_{s}}\partial_{k}\bigg)\sim\dfrac{\ell}{L_{s}}

where we note that
J\sim\mathcal{E}_{F}
and at most
\partial_{r_{i}}\sim 1/L_{s}
and
\partial_{k_{i}}\sim a
. This statement relies heavily on the condition
\lambda\ll J
and the action of the operator on
\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})
(e.g. the first term in Eq. (
184
) actually scales as
(\ell/L_{s})(\lambda/\mathcal{E}_{F})
). This shows that at leading order
\tau(\dot{\bm{r}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{r}+\dot{\bm{k}}(\bm{\xi})\cdot\bm{\nabla}_{k})
scales as
\ell/L_{s}
, which in the regime of interest is much less than one. We thus only need to consider the first few
n
in the sum of Eq. (
182
).

For quadratic dispersion, the conductivities

{A}(\mu)
have opposite sign. This leads to a complete cancellation in
{A}(\mu)
when electronic states of both bands are filled.
For the Nernst signal

\mu

\mu
