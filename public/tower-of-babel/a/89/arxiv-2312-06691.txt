Abstract.

A conjecture of S. Ramírez states that every
indecomposable non-degenerate involutive set-theoretic solution to the Yang-Baxter
equation with dihedral permutation group of order
2n
has cardinality
2n
. The
conjecture is verified for odd
n
and disproved for even
n
. The proof for odd

n
is obtained from the more general result that the permutation group of a finite
solution never acts as a Frobenius group.

1
Introduction

After Drinfeld’s suggestion
[
5
]
to study solutions
r
to the set-theoretic
Yang-Baxter equation

(r\times 1_{X})(1_{X}\times r)(r\times 1_{X})=(1_{X}\times r)(r\times 1_{X})(1_{X}\times r),

(1)

solutions were found on symplectic manifolds
[
24
]
, and then in connection with
Artin-Schelter regular rings
[
8
,
9
]
and algebras of I-type
[
23
]
.
A solution
r(x,y)=({}^{x}y,x^{y})
of Eq. (
1
) is said to be
non-degenerate

[
6
]
if its component maps
y\mapsto{}^{x}y
and
x\mapsto x^{y}
are bijective.
If
X
does not admit a non-trivial partition
X=Y\sqcup Z
with induced solutions on

Y
and
Z
, the solution
(X,r)
is said to be
indecomposable
. A systematic
study of non-degenerate
involutive
solutions
r=r^{2}
was initiated by Etingof
et al.
[
6
]
who associate a
structure group

G_{X}
to any solution of
Eq. (
1
).
For a finite non-degenerate involutive solution on
X
, a finite factor group
G(X)

of
G_{X}
, acting on
X

by permutations, has become an important invariant of the solution. For example,
such a solution
(X,r)
is indecomposable if and only if the
permutation group

G(X)
acts transitively on
X
.

Recently, Ramírez
[
14
]
conjectured that a dihedral permutation group

G(X)
of an indecomposable non-degenerate involutive solution of Eq. (
1
) must
be of
cardinality
|X|
. If true, Ramírez’ conjecture would provide a highly
remarkable, exceptional family of involutive Yang-Baxter groups
[
4
]
and
corresponding solutions to Eq. (
1
). For example, such solutions would be
their own universal covering
[
21
]
(see Section 3 for details).

In this paper, we verify the conjecture for dihedral groups
D_{2n}
with odd
n
,
and provide a simple counter-example for even
n
. Dihedral groups

D_{2n}
with odd
n
are Frobenius groups. Ramírez
[
14
]
proved that
the conjecture is “almost true” in the sense that a possible counter-example
must satisfy
|X|=n
whenever
G(X)=D_{2n}
. We show that in the latter case,

D_{2n}
acts as a Frobenius group on
X
. To exclude this possibility, we prove
that more generally, the permutation group of a finite non-degenerate involutive
solution
(X,r)
of Eq. (
1
) never acts as a Frobenius group on
X
.

The occasion to write this paper arose from a speculation on a question of
Ramírez and Vendramin
[
15
]
which asks whether a finite
non-degenerate involutive solution
(X,r)
is decomposable if for some
x\in X
,
the permutation
\sigma(x)
contains a cycle of length coprime to
|X|
.
Castelli
[
2
]
verified this for
multipermutation
solutions (see

[
6
]
or Section 2), while the first
author obtained a positive answer if
G(X)
is nilpotent
[
13
]
. If the
decomposibility
question
[
15
]
is true in general, this would give an alternative proof of
our positive result for dihedral groups
D_{2n}
with
n
odd.

2
Cycle sets and braces

In this section we recall some facts about cycle sets and braces as far as needed
below. For a finite non-degenerate involutive solution
r(x,y)=({}^{x}y,x^{y})
and

x\in X
, let
\sigma(x)\colon X\rightarrow X
be the self-map of
X
given by

\sigma(x)^{-1}(y)=y^{x}
. The binary operation
x\cdot y:=\sigma(x)(y)
then satisfies

(x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot z)=(y\cdot x)\cdot(y\cdot z)

(2)

for all
x,y,z\in X
. A set with a binary operation
(X;\cdot)
satisfying
Eq. (
2
) where the maps
\sigma(x)\colon X\rightarrow X
with
\sigma(x)(y)=x\cdot y

are bijective is said to be a
cycle set

[
17
]
. With
x^{y}:=\sigma(y)^{-1}(x)

as above, every cycle set
X
determines a solution to Eq. (
1
):

r(x,y)=({}^{x}y,x^{y})

(3)

with

{}^{x}y:=x^{y}\cdot y.

(4)

By
[
17
]
, Proposition 1 and Theorem 2, this gives a one-to-one correspondence
between finite cycle sets and finite
non-degenerate involutive set-theoretic solutions to the Yang-Baxter equation.
Being given by a single operation, cycle sets are much easier to handle than solutions

r\colon X\times X\rightarrow X\times X
to Eq. (
1
). The correspondence extends to
infinite solutions if a non-degeneracy condition for cycle sets is assumed, which states
the square map
T\colon X\rightarrow X
with
T(x):=x\cdot x
is bijective. For finite cycle
sets, it is important to know that this map is always bijective. The somewhat asymmetric
equations (
3
) and (
4
) can be put into a more appealing form:

r(y\cdot x,y)=(x\cdot y,x).

By
[
17
]
, Proposition 5, the operation of a cycle set
X
extends in a unique
fashion to the free abelian monoid
\mathbb{N}^{(X)}
, so that the following equations hold
for
a,b,c\in\mathbb{N}^{(X)}
:

\displaystyle a\cdot(b+c)

\displaystyle=(a\cdot b)+(a\cdot c)

(5)

\displaystyle(a+b)\cdot c

\displaystyle=(a\cdot b)\>\cdot\>(a\cdot c).

(6)

If
X
is non-degenerate (e. g., finite), the unique extension further extends to
the free abelian group
\mathbb{Z}^{(X)}
(
[
17
]
, Proposition 6). An abelian group

A
with a binary operation
(A;\cdot)
satisfying Eqs. (
5
) and (
6
) is
said to be a
linear cycle set

[
17
]
or a
brace

[
18
]
. By
Eq. (
6
), every brace is a cycle set. Conversely, every
non-degenerate cycle set
X
embeds into a brace
A_{X}
with additive group

\mathbb{Z}^{(X)}
.

There are more operations on a brace
A
. For example, there is a second
group operation

a\circ b:=a^{b}+b

(7)

from which the cycle set operation can be recovered. Guarnieri and Vendramin
[
10
]

showed that a set
(A,+,\circ)
with two group operations,
(A;+)
being commutative,
is a brace if and only if the single equation

(a+b)\circ c=(a\circ c)-c+(b\circ c)

holds in
A
. (This led to the concept of
skew-brace
where the commutativity of

(A;+)
is dropped.) The group
(A;\circ)
of a brace
A
acts from the left via

\sigma(a)
on the additive group by Eq. (
5
) and

(a\circ b)\cdot c=a\cdot(b\cdot c),

(8)

so that
A
becomes a left
A^{\circ}
-module. Similarly,
A
is a right
A^{\circ}
-module
under the exponential action:

\displaystyle(a+b)^{c}

\displaystyle=a^{c}+b^{c}

\displaystyle a^{b\circ c}

\displaystyle=(a^{b})^{c}.

The operation (
7
) generalizes Jacobson’s circle operation

a\circ b:=ab+a+b

(9)

which makes the Jacobson radical of any (associative unital) ring
into a brace. Following Jacobson’s terminology for radical rings
[
12
]
, the
group
A^{\circ}:=(A;\circ)
of a brace
A
is called the
adjoint group
, and
the inverse of an element
a\in A
is denoted by
a^{\prime}
. Note that the neutral element
of the adjoint group is 0, as in the adjoint group of a radical ring.

The ring operation
ab
(juxtaposition) in Eq. (
9
) is useful also for
braces. For example, a
right ideal

[
18
]
of a brace
A
is defined to be
an additive subgroup
I
which is closed with respect to right multiplication:
a\in I
and
b\in A
implies
ab\in I
. If also
ba\in I
, then
I
is called an
ideal

[
18
]
. In terms of the above operations, an additive subgroup
I
of
A
is a

right ideal
if
I
is an
A^{\circ}
-submodule, and an
ideal
if, in addition,

I
is a
normal subgroup of
A^{\circ}
. In contrast to ring theory, left ideals play no significant
part in the theory of braces. For an ideal
I
of a brace
A
, the residue classes

a+I
form a brace
A/I
, and there is a canonical brace morphism
A\twoheadrightarrow A/I
, like in
ring theory.

An important ideal of any brace
A
is the
socle

\mbox{Soc}(A):=\{a\in A\>|\>\forall\,b\in A\colon a\cdot b=b\}.

(10)

For a non-degenerate cycle set
X
, the
permutation group

G(X)
, generated by the

\sigma(x)
with
x\in X
, is the adjoint group
of
A(X):=A_{X}/\mbox{Soc}(A_{X})
. Indeed, Eq. (
10
) shows that the kernel of the brace
morphism
A_{X}\twoheadrightarrow A_{X}/\mbox{Soc}(A_{X})
consists of the elements for which the adjoint
action on
X
is trivial.

In general, the cycle set morphism

\sigma\colon X\rightarrow A(X)

(11)

is not injective. Its image
\sigma X
is called the
retraction
of
X
. A
non-degenerate cycle set
X
is said to be
irretractable
if
\sigma
is injective,
otherwise
retractable
. If
some iterate
\sigma^{n}X
is a singleton, the solution to Eq. (
1
) associated with

X
is said to be a
multipermutation
solution
[
6
]
.

The brace
A_{X}
is functorial in
X
:

Proposition 1

.

Let
f\colon X\rightarrow Y
be a morphism of non-degenerate cycle sets. There is
a unique brace morphism
A_{f}\colon A_{X}\rightarrow A_{Y}
such that the following diagram
commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{X}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{Y}.}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A_{f}}

Proof.
As the additive group of
A_{X}
is free abelian,
f
extends uniquely to an additive
group homomorphism
A_{f}
so that the diagram commutes. For simplicity, let us
write
f
instead of
A_{f}
in the following argument. By Eq. (
8
) and induction,
the sub-cycle set
X
of
A_{X}
is invariant under the adjoint action of
G_{X}
. For

a\in A_{X}
and
x,y\in X
, it follows that Eq. (
6
) gives

\displaystyle f\bigl((a+x)\cdot y\bigr)=f\bigl((a\cdot x)\cdot(a\cdot y)\bigr)=f(a\cdot x)\cdot f(a\cdot y)

\displaystyle f(a+x)\cdot f(y)=\bigl(f(a)+f(x)\bigr)\cdot f(y)=\bigl((f(a)\cdot f(x)\bigr)\cdot\bigl((f(a)\cdot f(y)\bigr).

Thus, if
f(a\cdot z)=f(a)\cdot f(z)
holds for all
z\in X
, then
f\bigl((a+x)\cdot y\bigr)=f(a+x)\cdot f(y)
. Conversely, assume that
f\bigl((a+x)\cdot y\bigr)=f(a+x)\cdot f(y)
holds for all
y\in X
, this implies that

f(a\cdot x)\cdot f(a\cdot x)=\bigl((f(a)\cdot f(x)\bigr)\cdot\bigl((f(a)\cdot f(x)\bigr)
. Since
X
is non-degenerate, it follows that
f(a\cdot x)=f(a)\cdot f(x)
.

So the equation
f(a\cdot x)=f(a)\cdot f(x)
(with
a\in A_{X}
and
x\in X
) remains
valid if an element of
X
is added to or subtracted from
a
. As the equation holds
for
a\in X
, it does hold in general. Now Eq. (
5
) shows that
x
in the equation
can be replaced by any element of
A_{X}
. Thus
A_{f}
is a morphism of linear
cycle sets, hence a morphism of braces.
\Box

Factoring out the socle, one should expect that
A(X)
is functorial as well.
However, the following example shows that this is not the case if
f
is not
surjective:

Example 1.
Let
X
be an irretractable cycle set with an element

x=x\cdot x
. Assume that
\sigma(y)\neq 1_{X}
for all
y\in X
. A 4-element
cycle set
X
of that type is given in Example 2 of the next section. Regard
X
as
a sub-cycle set of
A_{X}
. The sub-cycle set
Y:=X\sqcup\{0\}
of
A_{X}
is still
irretractable, and
Z:=\{x,0\}
is a retractable sub-cycle set of
Y
. As the composed
cycle set morphism
Z\hookrightarrow Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\sigma}}{{\longrightarrow}}A(Y)
is injective, it cannot
factor through
\sigma\colon Z\rightarrow A(Z)
. Thus
A_{X}
cannot be replaced by
A(X)
in
the commutative diagram of Proposition
1
.

For surjective cycle set morphisms, we have the following corollary:

Corollary.

Let
f\colon X\twoheadrightarrow Y
be a surjective morphism of
non-degenerate cycle sets. There is a unique brace morphism
A(f)\colon A(X)\twoheadrightarrow A(Y)

such that the following diagram commutes:
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces X}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces Y}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(X)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(Y).}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces f}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\sigma}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A(f)}

Proof.
We only have to show that
A_{f}\colon A_{X}\rightarrow A_{Y}
maps
\mbox{Soc}(A_{X})
into

\mbox{Soc}(A_{Y})
. Thus, let
a\in\mbox{Soc}(A_{X})
be given. For all
x\in X
, we have

A_{f}(a)\cdot A_{f}(x)=A_{f}(a\cdot x)=A_{f}(x)
. Since
f
is surjective and
Y=A_{f}(X)

generates
A_{Y}
, this implies that
A_{f}(a)\in\mbox{Soc}(A_{Y})
. Whence
A_{f}

induces a brace morphism
A(f)\colon A(X)\rightarrow A(Y)
. Since
\sigma Y
generates

A(Y)
, the brace morphism
A(f)
is surjective.
\Box

For a finite brace
A
, let
\pi(A)
be the set of primes dividing the order of
A
. The
primary components
A_{p}
of the additive group are right ideals, and we have a
decomposition

A=\bigoplus_{p\in\pi(A)}A_{p}.

(12)

The following result, which sheds some light upon the Yang-Baxter equation, is part of

[
19
]
, Proposition 2. For completeness, we include a proof.

Proposition 2

.

Let
A=I\oplus J
be a brace with right ideals
I
and
J
. Up to isomorphism, the
brace
A
is uniquely determined by the sub-braces
I
and
J
. For
a\in I
and
b\in J
, the mutual adjoint actions between
I
and
J
are determined by

b\circ a={}^{b}a\circ b^{a},

(13)

where as in Eq.
(\ref{4})
,
{}^{b}a:=b^{a}\cdot a
.

Proof.
If
I
and
J
are given, the brace structure of
A
is determined by the mutual
adjoint actions between
I
and
J
. Thus, it is enough to verify Eq. (
13
). Note
that
{}^{b}a\in I
and
b^{a}\in J
. By Eq. (
7
), we have
b\circ a=b^{a}+a=a+b^{a}=(b^{a}\cdot a)^{b^{a}}+b^{a}=(b^{a}\cdot a)\circ b^{a}={}^{b}a\circ b^{a}
.
\Box

Recall that a sub-cycle set
X
of a brace
A
is said to be a

cycle base

[
18
]
if
X
is
A^{\circ}
-invariant and generates the additive
group of
A
. For example,
the retraction
\sigma X
of a non-degenerate cycle set
X
is a cycle base in
A(X)
.

Corollary.

Let
A=I\oplus J
be a brace with an ideal
I
and a
right ideal
J
. For
a\in I
and
b\in J
, the equations
a\cdot b=b
and
b\cdot a=b\circ a\circ b^{\prime}
hold.

Proof.
Replacing
b
by
a\cdot b
in Eq. (
13
) yields
(a\cdot b)\circ a=(b\cdot a)\circ b
. Thus
(a\cdot b)\circ b^{\prime}=(b\cdot a)\circ b\circ a^{\prime}\circ b^{\prime}
. Since
I^{\circ}

is normal in
A^{\circ}
, this implies that
(a\cdot b)\circ b^{\prime}\in I\cap J=0
. Whence

a\cdot b=b
, and thus
b\cdot a=b\circ a\circ b^{\prime}
.
\Box

The following observation is trivial, but fundamental.

Proposition 3

.

Let
X
be a non-degenerate cycle set. The map
(\ref{11})
is equivariant with
respect to the adjoint group
G(X)
.

Proof.
Every element of
G(X)
is a finite product
g_{1}\circ\cdots\circ g_{n}
with
g_{i}=\sigma(x_{i})
or
g_{i}=\sigma(x_{i})^{\prime}
for some
x_{i}\in X
. By induction, we can assume
that
n=1
. Thus, in the first case, we have to verify that
\sigma\bigl(\sigma(x)(y)\bigr)=\sigma(x)\cdot\sigma(y)
. This follows since (
11
) is
a cycle set morphism. Hence
\sigma(y)=\sigma(x)^{\prime}\cdot\sigma\bigl(\sigma(x)(y)\bigr)
.
Replacing
y
by
\sigma(x)^{\prime}(y)
, we get
\sigma\bigl(\sigma(x)^{\prime}(y)\bigr)=\sigma(x)^{\prime}\cdot\sigma(y)
.
\Box

3
Frobenius action and Ramirez’ conjecture

Let
G
be a transitive permutation group, acting on a finite set
X
. Assume that
the elements
g\neq 1
in
G
leave at most one
x\in X
fixed. If some
g\neq 1

has a fixed point in
X
, then
G
is called a
Frobenius group
. Frobenius’
theorem
[
7
]
states that the elements which leave no element
of
X
fixed together with 1 form a normal subgroup
K
of
G
, which is now called
the
Frobenius kernel
. Thus
G=K\rtimes G_{x}
for any stabilizer
G_{x}
of some

x\in X
. Therefore
G_{x}
is said to be a
Frobenius complement
of the action.
To this date, a proof of Frobenius’ theorem has not been found without character
theory.

A simple calculation (
[
11
]
, V.8.3) shows that
|H|
divides
|K|-1
. In
particular, the orders of
H
and
K
are relatively prime. Another remarkable fact
about Frobenius groups is that the Frobenius kernel and the set of Frobenius
complements are unique (
[
11
]
, V.8.17).

In this section, we show for a finite cycle set
X
, that the action of the
permutation group
G(X)
on
X
cannot be a Frobenius action. Note that by

[
6
]
, Theorem 2.15,
G(X)
is solvable. If the Frobenius complements are
solvable, Shaw
[
22
]
found a character-free proof of Frobenius’ theorem
using the transfer map.

Theorem 1

.

Let
X
be a finite cycle set. Then the permutation group
G(X)
does not act as a
Frobenius group on
X
.

Proof.
Suppose that
G(X)
acts as a Frobenius group on
X
. Thus
G(X)
is the adjoint
group of a brace
A(X)
. Let
K
be the Frobenius kernel of the action. So the order
of
K
is relatively prime to each Frobenius complement. Hence Eq. (
12
) gives
a decomposition
A(X)=I\oplus H
into right ideals with
|I|=|K|
. By Hall’s
theorem (
[
16
]
, 9.1.7),
K
is conjugate to
I
. Since

K
is a normal subgroup of
G(X)
, it follows that
K=I
, and thus
K
is a brace
ideal of
A(X)
. Thus
A(X)=K\rtimes H
is a semidirect product of braces, and

H
is a Frobenius complement.

Let
x\in X
be the fixed point of
H
. By Proposition
3
, the map (
11
)
is
G(X)
-equivariant. Thus
\sigma(x)=k+h
with
k\in K
and
h\in H
, and

h\cdot(k+h)=h\cdot\sigma(x)=\sigma(h(x))=\sigma(x)=k+h
. In particular,

h\cdot k=k
, that is,
h\circ k\circ h^{\prime}=k
by the corollary of Proposition
2
.
So
h
and
k
commute. For the action of
k
on
X
,
this implies that
k(x)=k(h(x))=h(k(x))
. So
h
fixes
x
and
k(x)
, which
gives
k(x)=x
. Whence
k=0
.

Now the Frobenius complement
H
fixes
x
. Hence
H
fixes
\sigma(x)=h
. On the other
hand, the corollary of Proposition
2
, applied to the semidirect product
A(X)=K\rtimes H
of
braces, implies that
K^{\circ}
acts trivially on
H
. So
G(X)
fixes
\sigma(x)
.
Since
\sigma X
is a cycle base of
A(X)
, it follows that
\sigma(x)
generates

A(X)
. Whence
A(X)\subset H
, a contradiction.
\Box

Now let us turn to Ramírez’ conjecture
[
14
]
. By the corollary of
Proposition
1
, a surjective morphism
f\colon Y\twoheadrightarrow X
of non-degenerate
indecomposable cycle sets induces a surjective brace morphism
A(f)\colon A(Y)\twoheadrightarrow A(X)
. If
A(f)
is bijective, the morphism
f
is said to be a
covering

[
21
]
of
X
. Choose a fixed
x\in X
. By
[
21
]
, Corollary 4.6, there is a
one-to-one correspondence, up to isomorphism, between the coverings of
X
and the
subgroups of the
fundamental group

\pi_{1}(X,x):=\{a\in G(X)\>|\>a(x)=x\}
of

X
. Like in topology, there is a
universal covering

p\colon\widetilde{X}\twoheadrightarrow X
such that
\widetilde{X}
has trivial fundamental group, that is
G(\widetilde{X})

acts simply transitively on
\widetilde{X}
. Note that by the definition of a
covering,
G(\widetilde{X})
is isomorphic to
G(X)
. So there is a bijection between

\widetilde{X}
and
G(X)
, which endows
G(X)
with a second cycle set structure
coming from identification with
\widetilde{X}
.

Ramírez’ conjecture states that a finite indecomposable cycle set with dihedral
permutation group

D_{2n}
has trivial fundamental group, i. e. it coincides with its universal covering.
The following example restricts the range of validity of the conjecture to odd
n
.

Example 2.
Let
X=\{1,2,3,4\}
be the cycle set given by the permutations

\sigma(1)=(2,4),\quad\sigma(2)=(1,2,3,4),\quad\sigma(3)=(1,4,3,2),\quad\sigma(4)=(1,3).

This cycle set occurs as Example 14 in
[
3
]
. Its adjoint group is dihedral of
order 8. The brace
A(X)
is listed as
B_{7}
in
[
20
]
, and is given explicitly in
Example 2 of
[
18
]
.

Thus, it remains to consider the odd case. It is well known that dihedral groups
D_{2n}

with odd
n
are Frobenius groups. By lack of a reference, we give a short proof.

Proposition 4

.

For odd
n\geqslant 3
, the dihedral group
D_{2n}
is a Frobenius group.

Proof.
D_{2n}
is a Coxeter group, generated by two reflections
b,c
, with relations

b^{2}=c^{2}=1
and
(bc)^{n}=1
. Alternatively,
D_{2n}
is generated by the rotation
a:=bc

and the reflection
b
, so that the relations become
a^{n}=1
and
bab=a^{-1}
. (Indeed,

bab=b(bc)b=cb=(bc)^{-1}
.) There is a cyclic subgroup
\langle a\rangle
, and the
remaining
n
elements are reflections. If
n
is odd, each subgroup generated by a
reflection is a Sylow subgroup. All these 2-element subgroups are conjugate by Sylow’s
theorem. Since
n
is odd, each of them coincides with its normalizer. Thus
D_{2n}
is
a Frobenius group with Frobenius kernel
\langle a\rangle
and Frobenius complement

\langle b\rangle
.
\Box

For dihedral groups
D_{2n}
with odd
n
, Ramírez’ conjecture can now be verified.

Theorem 2

.

Let
D_{2n}
be a dihedral group with odd
n\geqslant 3
. Every indecomposable cycle set
X

with permutation group
D_{2n}
is of cardinality
2n
.

Proof.
Since
X
is indecomposable,
G(X)
acts transitively on
X
. Let
H
be the stabilizer
of some
x\in X
. So the other elements of
X
are fixed by some conjugate of
H
. Since
G(X)

acts faithfully on
X
, the intersection of all conjugates of
H
is trivial. Thus
H

does not contain a rotation of
D_{2n}
. As the product of two reflections is a rotation,

H
contains at most one reflection, which yields
|H|\leqslant 2
. If
|H|=2
, the
action of
G(X)
on
X
is equivalent to the action by conjugation on its reflections.
Indeed,
gHg^{-1}\mapsto g(x)
with
g\in G(X)
is a
G(X)
-equivariant isomorphism
between the
G(X)
-set of reflections in
G(X)
(under conjugation) and the
G(X)
-set
X
.
By Proposition
4
,
G(X)
is a Frobenius group, which is impossible by Theorem
1
.
Whence
|H|=1
and
|X|=2n
.
\Box

Remark.
Ramírez and Vendramin (
[
15
]
, Question 3.16) asked
whether a finite cycle set
X
must be decomposable if some
\sigma(x)
with
x\in X

contains a cycle
of length coprime to
|X|
. If true, we could get a simpler proof of Theorem
1
:
Since
\sigma X
is a cycle base of
A(X)
, it cannot be contained in the Frobenius
kernel. Hence it meets a Frobenius complement
H
, that is, a stabilizer of some
x\in X
.
As the order of
H
is coprime to
|X|
, the cycle set
X
would be decomposable, a
contradiction.
