Solving order 3 difference equations
Heba Bou KaedBey, Mark van Hoeij, Man Cheung Tsui

Abstract
. We classify order 3 3 linear difference operators over ℂ ⁡ ( x ) \mathbb{C}(x) that are solvable in terms of lower order difference operators. To prove this result, we introduce the notion of absolute irreducibility for difference modules, and classify (for arbitrary order) modules that are irreducible but not absolutely irreducible.

2.2.
Difference Galois theory

Let

(

We now define representation-theoretic operations on difference modules.

Let

5.1.1.
Motivation of the Paper

Can one always decide if a recurrence of order 3 or 4 is 2-solvable or not?
This leads to the following questions:

(1)

Which cases can occur?

(2)

How to solve those cases?

(3)

How to

Remark 5.8

(Remarks on questions (1), (2), and (3))

(1)

Let

with

as in

5.6
. Then

is irreducible and

is reducible. This raises the following question.

Suppose that

is not absolutely irreducible, in other words, there exists

is reducible. Which values of

(2)

For order

, see
[
2
]
. Order

is work in progress; we need to develop an algorithm for each potential case, one of which was illustrated in

.

(3)

This is the most difficult question. The only way to answer it in the differential case was through Galois theory. The main goal in this paper is to tackle this problem for the difference case. We complete this task for order 3 in
Section

7

The three tasks are quite different in nature. If we limit ourselves to say orders 3 and 4, then task (1), to plausibly list all possible cases, is the easiest task.
Task (2) is to design an algorithm for each case.
The main goal in this paper is to develop theory needed for task (3).

6.
Semisimplicity and absolute irreducibility

We now develop some representation theory in the context of difference modules to decompose irreducible modules into absolutely irreducible modules.

As with differential modules

Therefore the class of semisimple

We now show that restriction and induction preserve semisimplicity as well.
See
[

Proposition 6.1

Let

-module and

-module, with

Proof.

(It would be sufficient to only consider

-submodule.

is irreducible, the

-submodule

. But this writes

as a sum of irreducible

-submodules

We next show

of

In this appendix, we refine the decomposition in

Theorem B.1

(Mackey’s formula)
.

Let

,

, and
l=\lcm(s,t)
. Given a
D_{s}
-module

-module isomorphism

Proof.

Consider the isomorphisms

D\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d-1}D_{d}\otimes\Phi^{i}\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d-1}D_{t}\otimes_{D_{l}}(\Phi^{i}\otimes D_{s})

where the second isomorphism sends

to
\Phi^{jt}\otimes\Phi^{i}\otimes\Phi^{ks}

The first and third term are isomorphic as

Therefore

\begin{split}N\mathord{\uparrow}^{1}_{s}\mathord{\downarrow}^{1}_{t}&\cong\left(D\otimes_{D_{s}}N\right)\mathord{\downarrow}^{1}_{t}\cong\bigoplus_{i=0}^{d-1}D_{t}\otimes_{D_{l}}\left(\Phi^{i}\otimes D_{s}\right)\otimes_{D_{s}}N\\

.

as well.

To see that
\tilde{N}\coloneqq(\Phi^{i}\otimes N)\mathord{\downarrow}^{s}_{l}\mathord{\uparrow}^{t}_{l}
is irreducible, let
P
be a nonzero irreducible

-submodule of
\tilde{N}
. We must show that
P=\tilde{N}
. To do this, we first consider the restriction
P\mathord{\downarrow}^{t}_{l}
, which is a
D_{l}
-submodule of the restriction
\tilde{N}\mathord{\downarrow}^{t}_{l}
. Using (
5
) of
Remark

5.2
we can decompose
\tilde{N}\mathord{\downarrow}^{t}_{l}
as

(10)

\tilde{N}\mathord{\downarrow}^{t}_{l}=(\Phi^{i}\otimes N)\mathord{\downarrow}^{s}_{l}\,\mathord{\uparrow}^{t}_{l}\,\mathord{\downarrow}^{t}_{l}=(\Phi^{i}\otimes N)\mathord{\downarrow}^{s}_{l}\oplus(\Phi^{i+t}\otimes N)\mathord{\downarrow}^{s}_{l}\\
\oplus(\Phi^{i+2t}\otimes N)\mathord{\downarrow}^{s}_{l}\oplus\cdots\oplus(\Phi^{i+(l-t)}\otimes N)\mathord{\downarrow}^{s}_{l}

Since we just proved that each such direct summand in (
10
) is absolutely irreducible (hence irreducible), at least one of these direct summands must lie within

. But

is just

viewed as a

-module, so

acts transitively on the direct summands of (

contains all the direct summands of (
10
). In other words,

, and so
\tilde{N}
is irreducible.
∎

Appendix C
More results on absolute irreducibility

The following theorem clarifies why our definition of absolute irreducibility agrees with other common usages of the term.

Theorem C.1
.

Assume
(F,\phi)=(C(x),x\mapsto x+1)
.
For
L\in D
and
G=\Gal(L)
, the following are equivalent.

(1)

V(L)
is an irreducible
H
-module for every algebraic subgroup
H
of
G
with
[G:H]
finite.

(2)

V(L)
is an irreducible
G^{\circ}
-module, where
G^{\circ}
is the connected component of the identity in
G
.

(3)

L
is absolutely irreducible.

Proof.

We may assume that
L
is irreducible, otherwise (
1
)–(
3
) are all false.
(By
Proposition

2.1
,

V(L)
is a reducible
G
-module iff

L
is reducible over
F
.)

(
1
)
\Leftrightarrow
(
2
):
Clearly (
2
) is the special case
H=G^{\circ}
of (
1
).
Conversely, let
H
be a finite-index, closed subgroup of
G
. By
[
20
, Proposition 2.2.1]
,
H
contains
G^{\circ}
.
So if
V(L)
is irreducible as a
G^{\circ}
-module, then it is also irreducible as an
H
-module.

(

)
\Rightarrow
(
3
):
We will prove the contrapositive of (
1
)
\Rightarrow
(
3
).
Suppose that
L
is not absolutely irreducible.
By
Theorem

6.4
,
M_{L}\cong N\mathord{\uparrow}^{1}_{s}
for some
s>1
.
By the discussion after
Remark

5.3
,
L
is gauge equivalent to some

.

Since replacing

by

does not alter the truth of the implication (

)
\Rightarrow
(
3
), we can assume that

is in

.
So now

\Phi^{s}
, and we can write

for some orthogonal idempotents

within the Picard-Vessiot ring

(
