Equivalences of stable categories of Gorenstein local rings
Tony J. Puthenpurakal

Abstract
. In this paper we give bountiful examples of Gorenstein local rings A A and B B such that there is a triangle equivalence between the stable categories CM ( A A ), CM ( B B ).



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 13D09, 13D15 ; Secondary 13C14, 13C60

1.
introduction

Let
R
be a commutative Artin ring and let
\Lambda
be a not-necessarily commutative Artin
R
-algebra. Let
\operatorname{\underline{mod}}(\Lambda)
denote the stable category of
\Gamma
.
The study of equivalences of stable categories of Artin
R
-algebras has a rich history; see
[
2
, Chapter X]
. Auslander discovered that many concepts in representation theory of Artin algebras have
natural analogues in the study of maximal Cohen-Macaulay ( = MCM) modules of a commutative Cohen-Macaulay local ring
A
. See
[
15
]
for a nice exposition of these ideas.
By following Auslander’s idea we investigate equivalences of the stable
category of MCM modules over commutative Cohen-Macaulay local rings. If
A
is Gorenstein local then,
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
, the stable category of MCM
A
-modules has a triangulated structure, see
[
4
, 4.4.1]
. So as a first
iteration in this program, we may investigate triangle equivalences of stable categories of MCM modules over commutative Gorenstein local rings.

There is paucity of examples of Gorenstein local rings
A,B
such that
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
is triangle equivalent to
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
. There are two well-known examples of triangle equivalences. First is
Knörrer periodicity
[
9
]
for hypersurfaces. Another gives an equivalence
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(\widehat{A})
where
A
is a excellent, henselian, Gorenstien isolated singularity, see
[
8
, A.6]
. In this paper we give bountiful examples of Gorenstein local rings
A
and
B
such that there is a triangle equivalence between the stable categories
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A),\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)
.

The Example:

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Gorenstein local ring of dimension
d\geq 1
. Assume
A
is essentially of finite type over a field
K
and that
A
is an isolated singularity. Also assume that the completion of
A
is a domain. Further assume that the Grothendieck group of the completion of
A
is a finitely generated abelian group.

Let
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
be the set of isomorphism classes of pointed etale neighborhoods of
A
, see
3.1
,
3.2
.
It is well-known that
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
is a directed set and
A^{h}
, the henselization of
A
,
is

A^{h}=\lim_{R\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)}R.

By Remark
3.3
, for any
B\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
there exists atleast one infinite chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
starting at
B
.

1. Let
A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow A_{n}\rightarrow\cdots
be a chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. Then we show that there exists
n_{0}
such that

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n+1})\quad\text{for all $n\geq n_{0}$}.

2. There exists a chain
B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow B_{n}\rightarrow\cdots
in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)

such that

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})\quad\text{for all $n\geq 1$}.

Remark 1.1
.

In (2) note that
B_{n}
is NOT henselian, see
6.1
. Nevertheless

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
is Krull-Schmidt for all
n\geq 1
.

Technique used to prove the result:

Under the hypotheses of the example it can be shown that
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is a finitely generated abelian group, see
3.7
.
Let
R,S\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
and let
R\xrightarrow{\psi}S
be a homomorphism in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. We show there is a commutative diagram

\textstyle{G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(S))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{S}}
\textstyle{G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(R))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{R}}
\scriptstyle{G(\psi)}

\textstyle{G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))}

Here
G(\psi),f_{R},f_{S}
are injective by
3.4
and
3.8
. We show that if
G(\psi)
is an isomorphism then
\psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(R)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(S)
is an equivalence, see
3.6
.

(1) We note that if
A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow A_{n}\rightarrow\cdots
be a chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)

then we have an ascending chain of subgroups of
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))

\operatorname{image}f_{A_{1}}\subseteq\operatorname{image}f_{A_{2}}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\operatorname{image}f_{A_{n}}\subseteq\cdots

As
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is a finitely generated abelian group it follows that there exists
m
such that
\operatorname{image}f_{A_{n}}=\operatorname{image}f_{A_{m}}
for all
n\geq m
. It follows that

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n+1})\quad\text{for all $n\geq m$}.

(2) As
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is a finitely generated abelian group we first show that there exists
B\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
such that the natural map
f_{B}\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
is surjective.
As it is injective, see
3.8
it follows that
f_{B}
is an isomorphism. So again by
3.8
we get
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
.

We choose an infinite chain

B_{1}\rightarrow B_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow B_{n}\rightarrow\cdots
in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
starting at
B=B_{1}
. It follows from the commutative diagram above that
f_{B_{n}}\colon G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B_{n}))\rightarrow G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is an isomorphism for all
n\geq 1
. So by
3.8
we get
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
for all
n\geq 1
.

Remark 1.2
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Gorenstein local ring, essentially of finite type over a field
K
and that
A
is an isolated singularity. Then by
[
7
, 1.5]
the natural map
G(A)\rightarrow G(A^{h})
is injective. Assume
\widehat{A}
is also a domain (automatic if
\dim A\geq 2
).
The entire argument as above goes through if we just assume the quotient group
G(\widehat{A})/G(A)
is a finitely generated abelian group. Regrettably we do not have a nice class of rings with this property (i.e., with
G(\widehat{A})
infinitely generated abelian group and
G(\widehat{A})/G(A)
finitely generated abelian group).

Here is an overview of the contents of this paper. In section two we prove some general results on Grothendieck groups of triangulated categories. In section three we discuss some preliminary facts on pointed étale neighbourhoods of a local ring. In section four we discuss our example. In the next section we give many examples of local rings with
G(\widehat{A})
finitely generated.
In the appendix we discuss a fact which is crucial for us.

2.
Some generalities on Grothendieck groups of triangulated categories

Throughout all triangulated categories considered will be skeletally small.
Let
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{D}
be triangulated categories.

2.1
.

An triangulated functor
F\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
is called an
equivalence up to direct summand’s

if it is fully faithful and any object
X\in\mathcal{D}
is isomorphic to a direct summand of
F(Y)
for
some
Y\in\mathcal{C}
.

2.2
.

We say
\mathcal{C}
has
weak cancellation
, if for
U,V,W\in\mathcal{C}
we have

U\oplus V\cong U\oplus W\implies V\cong W.

2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be a triangulated category. The Grothendieck group
G(\mathcal{C})
is the quotient group of the free abelian group
on the set of isomorphism classes of objects of
\mathcal{C}
by the Euler relations:
[V]=[U]+[W]
whenever there is an exact triangle in
\mathcal{C}

U\rightarrow V\rightarrow W\rightarrow U[1].

As
[U[1]]=-[U]
in
G(\mathcal{C})
, it follows that any element of
G(\mathcal{C})
is of the form
[V]
for some
V\in\mathcal{C}
.

We first show:

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\phi\colon\mathcal{C}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}
be a triangulated functor which is an equivalence upto direct summands. Then the natural map
G(\phi)\colon G(\mathcal{C})\rightarrow G(\mathcal{D})
is injective.

Proof.

Suppose
G(\phi)([U])=[\phi(U)]=0
in
G(\mathcal{D})
. So by
[
13
, 2.4]
there exists
X,Y,Z\in\mathcal{D}
and triangles

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\rightarrow Y\oplus\phi(U)\rightarrow Z\rightarrow X[1],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\rightarrow Y\rightarrow Z\rightarrow X[1].

There exists
X_{1},Z_{1}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
X\oplus X_{1}=\phi(M)
and
Z\oplus Z_{1}=\phi(N)
. We add the triangles
X_{1}\xrightarrow{1}X_{1}\rightarrow 0\rightarrow X_{1}\rightarrow 0\rightarrow X_{1}[1]
and
0\rightarrow Z_{1}\xrightarrow{1}Z_{1}\rightarrow 0
to the above triangles.

The modified second triangle is

\phi(M)\rightarrow Y^{\prime}\rightarrow\phi(N)\rightarrow\phi(M)[1].

It follows that
Y^{\prime}=\phi(L)
for some
L\in\mathcal{C}
.
The modified first triangle becomes

\phi(M)\rightarrow\phi(L)\oplus\phi(U)\rightarrow\phi(N)\rightarrow\phi(M)[1].

Note
\phi(L)\oplus\phi(U)=\phi(L\oplus U)
. We now use the fact that if
W_{1},W_{2}\in\mathcal{C}
then
W_{1}\cong W_{2}
if and only if
\phi(W_{1})\cong\phi(W_{2})
. As
\phi
is fully faithful we get triangles in
\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\rightarrow L\oplus U\rightarrow N\rightarrow M[1],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\rightarrow L\rightarrow N\rightarrow M[1].

It follows that
[U]=0
in
G(\mathcal{C})
.
∎

A natural question is when is
Y\in\mathcal{D}
is isomorphic to
\phi(X)
for some
X\in\mathcal{C}
. A necessary condition is of course that
[Y]\in\operatorname{image}G(\phi)
. Some what surprisingly the converse also holds if
\mathcal{D}
satisfies weak cancellation.

Theorem 2.5
.

(with hypotheses as in Theorem
2.4
). Also assume
\mathcal{D}
satisfies weak cancellation.
If
[V]\in\operatorname{image}G(\phi)
then there exists
W\in\mathcal{C}
with
\phi(W)\cong V
.

We need the following:

Lemma 2.6
.

(with hypotheses as in Theorem
2.4
). Let
U,V\in\mathcal{C}
. If
\phi(U)
is a direct summand of
\phi(V)
then
U
is a direct summand of
V
.

Proof.

We have a diagram

\textstyle{\phi(U)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{h}
\textstyle{\phi(V)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f}
\scriptstyle{g}

\textstyle{\phi(V)}

with
f^{2}=f
and
g\circ h=1_{\phi(U)}
. As
\phi
is fully faithful there exists
f_{1}\colon V\rightarrow V
,
g_{1}\colon V\rightarrow U
and
h_{1}\colon U\rightarrow V
with
\phi(f_{1})=f
,
\phi(g_{1})=g
and
\phi(h_{1})=h
. Again as
\phi
is fully faithful we get

h_{1}\circ g_{1}=f_{1}
,
f_{1}^{2}=f_{1}
and
g_{1}\circ h_{1}=1_{U}
. The result follows.
∎

We now give

Proof of Theorem
2.5
.

Say
[V]=[\phi(U)]
in
G(\mathcal{D})
. By
[
13
, 2.4]
we have triangles in
\mathcal{D}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\rightarrow Y\oplus\phi(U)\rightarrow Z\rightarrow X[1],\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle X

\displaystyle\rightarrow Y\oplus V\rightarrow Z\rightarrow X[1].

There exists
X_{1},Z_{1}\in\mathcal{D}
such that
X\oplus X_{1}=\phi(M)
and
Z\oplus Z_{1}=\phi(N)
. We add the triangles
X_{1}\xrightarrow{1}X_{1}\rightarrow 0\rightarrow X_{1}\rightarrow 0\rightarrow X_{1}[1]
and
0\rightarrow Z_{1}\xrightarrow{1}Z_{1}\rightarrow 0
to the above triangles.
Set
Y^{\prime}=Y\oplus X_{1}\oplus Z_{1}
.

The (modified) first triangle yields

Y^{\prime}\oplus\phi(U)\cong\phi(E)
for some
E\in\mathcal{C}
. By
2.6
we have that
U
is a direct summand of
E
. Say
E=U\oplus U_{1}
. So
Y^{\prime}\oplus\phi(U)\cong\phi(U)\oplus\phi(U_{1})
. As
\mathcal{D}
satisfies weak cancellation we get
Y^{\prime}\cong\phi(U_{1})
.

The (modified) second triangle yields

\phi(U_{1})\oplus V\cong\phi(L)
for some
L\in\mathcal{C}
. By
2.6
we have that
U_{1}
is a direct summand of
L
. Say
L=U_{1}\oplus U_{2}
. So we obtain

\phi(U_{1})\oplus V=\phi(U_{1})\oplus\phi(U_{2}).

As
\mathcal{D}
satisfies weak cancellation we get
V\cong\phi(U_{2}).
The result follows.
∎

The following consequence of Theorem
2.5
is significant.

Corollary 2.7
.

(with hypotheses as in Theorem
2.5
). If
G(\phi)
is an isomorphism then
\phi
is an equivalence.

Proof.

By our assumption
\phi
is fully faithful. By Theorem
2.5
it follows that
\phi
is dense. So
\phi
is an equivalence.
∎

Another surprising consequence of Theorem
2.5
is the following:

Corollary 2.8
.

(with hypotheses as in Theorem
2.5
). Let
Y\in\mathcal{D}
. Then there exists
X\in\mathcal{C}
with
\phi(X)=Y\oplus Y[1]
.

Proof.

Notice in
G(\mathcal{D})
we have

[Y\oplus Y[1]]=[Y]+[Y[1]]=[Y]-[Y]=0.

The result follows from Theorem
2.5
.
∎

3.
pointed étale neighborhoods

In this section we recall definition of pointed étale extensions and discuss a few of its properties.

3.1
.

A local homomorphism
\psi\colon(A,\mathfrak{m})\rightarrow(B,\mathfrak{n})
of
local rings is unramified provided
B
is essentially of finite type over
A

(that is,
B
is a localization of some finitely generated
A
-algebra) and
the following properties hold.

(i)
\mathfrak{m}B=\mathfrak{n}
, and

(ii)
B/\mathfrak{m}B
is a finite separable field extension of
A/\mathfrak{m}
.

If, in addition,
\psi
is flat, then we say
\psi
is étale. (We say also that
B

is an unramified, respectively, étale extension of
A
.) Finally, a pointed
étale neighborhood of
A
is an étale extension
(A,\mathfrak{m})\rightarrow(B,\mathfrak{n})
inducing
an isomorphism on residue fields.

3.2
.

The isomorphism classes
of pointed étale neighborhoods of a local ring
(A,\mathfrak{m})
form a direct
system (see
[
6
, Chapter 3]
for details). This implies that if
A\rightarrow B

and
A\rightarrow C
are pointed étale neighborhoods then there is at most
one homomorphism
B\rightarrow C
making the obvious diagram commute.
Let
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
be the set of isomorphism classes of pointed étale neighborhoods of
A
.
Then
A^{h}
, the henselization of
A
,
is

A^{h}=\lim_{R\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)}R.

Remark 3.3
.

Assume
A
is Cohen-Macaulay and essentially of finite type over a field
K
. Also assume
\widehat{A}
is a domain.
By
6.1
it follows that
A^{h}
is NOT of essentially finite type over
K
. Thus
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
contains infinitely many terms. So there exists atleast one infinite chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. Note we may assume this infinite chain starts at
A

Let
B\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. Then
B^{h}=A^{h}
and we may assume
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(B)\subseteq\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. By argument as before there exists an infinite chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(B)
(and so in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
) starting at
B
.

3.4
.

Let
A
be an excellent Gorenstein isolated singularity. Let
B,C\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
and let
\psi\colon B\rightarrow C
be a morphism in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. Note
B,C
are also an excellent Gorenstein isolated singularity. Note
\psi
is flat, see
[
6
, Chapter 1, 2.7]
, and so induces a functor

\psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(C)
which is clearly fully faithful. Furthermore as
C
is étale over
B
we get that every MCM
C
-module
M
is a direct summand of
N\otimes_{B}C
where
N
is a MCM

B
-module, see
[
11
, 10.5, 10.7]
. By Theorem
2.4
the natural map

G(\psi)\colon G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B))\rightarrow G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(C))
is injective.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Gorenstein local ring. Then
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
has weak cancellation (see
2.2
)

Proof.

Suppose
M,N,L
are MCM
A
-modules such that
M\oplus N\cong M\oplus L
in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
.
Then as
A
-modules
M\oplus N\oplus A^{r}\cong M\oplus L\oplus A^{s}
for some
r,s
. By
[
11
, 1.16]
it follows that
N\oplus A^{r}\cong L\oplus A^{s}
as
A
-modules. So
N\cong L
in
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A)
.
∎

3.6
.

(with hypotheses as in
3.4
) If
G(\psi)\colon G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B))\rightarrow G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(C))
is an isomorphism then by
2.7
and

3.5
,
we get that
\psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(C)
is an equivalence.

3.7
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be an excellent Gorenstein isolated singularity. Then
A^{h}
is also an excellent Gorenstein isolated singularity, see
[
11
, 10.7]
. In particular we have an equivalence of categories
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(\widehat{A})
, by
[
8
, A.6]
. If
G(\widehat{A})
is finitely generated abelian group then
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(\widehat{A}))=G(A)/([A])
is finitely generated abelian group. It follows that
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is finitely generated abelian group.

3.8
.

Let
A
be an excellent Gorenstein isolated singularity. Let
B\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. Then the map
f\colon B\rightarrow A^{h}
is flat. It is readily verified that
f\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
is fully faithful (use
B^{h}=A^{h}
). Also as
f\colon B\rightarrow A^{h}
is a direct limit of étale-extensions every MCM
A^{h}
-module
M
is a direct summand of
N\otimes_{B}A^{h}
where
N
is a MCM

B
-module, see
[
11
, 10.5, 10.7]
. By Theorem
2.4
the natural map

f_{B}\colon G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B))\rightarrow G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is injective. If
f_{B}
is an isomorphism then by
2.7
and

3.5
,
we get that
f\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
is an equivalence.

4.
The example

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Gorenstein local ring of dimension
d\geq 1
. Assume
A
is essentially of finite type over a field
K
and that
A
is an isolated singularity. Also assume that the completion of
A
is a domain. Further assume that the Grothendieck group of the completion of
A
is a finitely generated abelian group.

Let
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
be the set of isomorphism classes of pointed etale neighborhoods of
A
.
It is well-known that
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
is a directed set and
A^{h}
, the henselization of
A
,
is

A^{h}=\lim_{R\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)}R.

By
3.7
it follows that
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is a finitely generated abelian group.

1. Let
A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow A_{n}\rightarrow\cdots
be a chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
.

Claim:

There exists
n_{0}
such that

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n+1})\quad\text{for all $n\geq n_{0}$}.

Proof of Claim:

Let
R,S\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
and let
R\xrightarrow{\psi}S
be a homomorphism in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
. Note as
\psi,f_{R}\colon R\rightarrow A^{h}
and
f_{S}\colon S\rightarrow A^{h}
are flat we get a commutative diagram

\textstyle{G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(S))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{S}}
\textstyle{G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(R))\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{f_{R}}
\scriptstyle{G(\psi)}

\textstyle{G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))}

Here
G(\psi),f_{R},f_{S}
are injective by
3.4
and
3.8
. By
3.6
it follows that if
G(\psi)
is an isomorphism then
\psi\colon\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(R)\rightarrow\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(S)
is an equivalence.
We note that if
A_{1}\rightarrow A_{2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow A_{n}\rightarrow\cdots
is a chain in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)

then we have an ascending chain of subgroups of
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))

\operatorname{image}f_{A_{1}}\subseteq\operatorname{image}f_{A_{2}}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\operatorname{image}f_{A_{n}}\subseteq\cdots

As
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is a finitely generated abelian group it follows that there exists
m
such that
\operatorname{image}f_{A_{n}}=\operatorname{image}f_{A_{m}}
for all
n\geq m
.
Thus the map

G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n}))\rightarrow G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n+1}))
is an isomorphism for all
n\geq m
. It follows that

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A_{n+1})\quad\text{for all $n\geq m$}.

2. We have
G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is a finitely generated abelian group, we may assume
[N_{1}],\ldots,[N_{s}]
generate it as an abelian group. By proof of
[
11
, Theorem 10.7]
we may assume there exists
A_{i}\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
and finitely generated
A_{i}
module
N_{i}
such that
N_{i}=A^{h}\otimes_{A_{i}}M_{i}
. As
\theta_{i}\colon A_{i}\rightarrow A_{h}
is flat (with zero dimensional fiber) it follows from
[
12
, 23.3]
that
N_{i}
is a MCM
A_{i}
-module. As

\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
is directed there exists
B\in\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
such that there exists maps
\gamma_{i}\colon A_{i}\rightarrow B
in
\mathfrak{E}_{P}(A)
for all
i
. Set
E_{i}=B\otimes_{A_{i}}N_{i}
. Then as
\gamma_{i}
is flat (with zero dimensional fiber),
E_{i}
is MCM
B
-module. Furthermore
E_{i}\otimes_{B}A^{h}\cong N_{i}
. It follows that
f_{B}\colon G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B))\rightarrow G(\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h}))
is surjective. As it is injective, see
3.8
it follows that

is an isomorphism. So again by

we get
\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B)\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})
.

Now let

be an infinite chain in

, see

. By the commutative diagram above we get

is an isomorphism for all

\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(B_{n})\cong\operatorname{\underline{CM}}(A^{h})

In this section we give many examples of Gorenstein rings with

5.1
.

Let

5.2
.

Let

5.3

Let

In this section we prove the following result

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Cohen-Macaulay local ring of dimension

. Assume
A
is of essentially of finite type over a field

. Also assume
\widehat{A}
, the completion of

Then the henselization

of

We believe Theorem
6.1
is already known. However we are unable to find a reference. As this result is crucial for us we give a proof.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
(A,\mathfrak{m})
be a Cohen-Macaulay local ring of dimension
d\geq 1
. Assume
A
is of essentially of finite type over a field
K
. Also assume that
A
is a domain. Then
A=T_{P}
for some affine domain
T
over
K
and
P
a prime in
T
.

Proof.

By assumption
A=W^{-1}R
where
R=K[X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}]/I
and
W
is a multiplicatively closed ideal in
R
. The maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
of
A
is of the form
W^{-1}P
where
P
is a prime in
R
. It follows that
A=R_{P}
. Let
P_{1}
be a minimal prime of
I
such that
0pt(P/P_{1})=\dim A
. We have a a surjective map

R\rightarrow T=R/P_{1}
which induces a surjection
\epsilon\colon A\rightarrow T_{P}
. As
\dim A=\dim T_{P}
and
A
is a domain it follows that
\epsilon
is an isomorphism. The result follows.
∎

We now give

Proof of Theorem

.

in
