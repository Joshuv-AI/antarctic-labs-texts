Symmetry-induced decoherence-free subspaces
Jonathan Dubois, Ulf Saalmann, Jan Michael Rost

Abstract
Preservation of coherence is a fundamental yet subtle phenomenon in open systems. We uncover its relation to symmetries respected by the system Hamiltonian and its coupling to the environment. We discriminate between local and global classes of decoherence-free subspaces for many-body systems through the introduction of “ghost variables”. The latter are orthogonal to the symmetry and the coupling to the environment does not depend on them. Constructing them is facilitated in classical phase space and can be transferred to quantum mechanics through the equivalent role that Poisson and Lie algebras play for symmetries in classical and quantum mechanics, respectively. Examples are given for an interacting spin system.



Introduction:

A physical system interacting with an environment
Gardiner1983
;
Risken1984
;
Gotze1992
;
Breuer2002
relaxes to an equilibrium or a non-equilibrium steady state on time scales much longer than the relaxation time
Qian2006
. In the steady state the system no longer evolves in time due to its coupling to the environment and
all
associated physical observables reach a constant value. This characteristic is inherent to classical and quantum open systems.

Quantum mechanically, relaxation is accompanied by decoherence which remains a major obstacle in putting physical devices to work for quantum computation
Grover1997
;
Ladd2010
;
nich10
.
Therefore, decoherence-free subspaces
Lidar1998
;
Albert2016
;
Tarnowski2021
(DFS), which are protected against decoherence effects
Habib1998
, play a crucial role in realizing quantum computing
Grover1997
;
Mohseni2003
;
Friesen2017
.
Understanding and formulating the general conditions underlying DFS is the prerequisite to tame decoherence and to establish open systems which exhibit non-stationary long-time dynamics (NLD), that is, remain out of equilibrium despite their interaction with an environment
Buca2019
;
Tindall2020
;
Tarnowski2021
.

In closed systems, it is well known that symmetries, i.e., constants of motion, are crucial to characterize their dynamics. Here, we will show that this is also the case for open systems: Symmetries can be used to formulate simple conditions for DFS in terms of the dependence of the system and the coupling to the environment on these symmetries. Moreover, these conditions can be identified and formulated in an intuitive way via classical dynamics since exact symmetries hold quantum mechanically as well as classically through their equivalent formulation in terms of commutators and Poisson brackets, respectively.
The classical phase-space perspective will also allow us to introduce naturally new classes of DFS where the coupling to the environment depends only on so called “ghost variables”, orthogonal to the symmetries.
Finally, we will present the quantized conditions for the DFS in terms of eigenstates of the symmetry operator.

Theory of symmetry-induced DFS in quantum mechanics:

Finally, we are in a position to briefly sketch the derivation of the quantized condition (
3
) for DFS. We have a set of commuting operators,
\{\mdmathbb{Z}_{i}\}
, its conjugate
\mdmathbb{J}
and and the ghost operators. Together, they span the Hilbert space for the open system dynamics.

The Hamilton operator
\mdmathbb{H}
(and similarly all Lindblad operators
\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha}
) with the symmetry
\mdmathbb{J}
can be written as a direct sum of irreducible (square) matrices

\mdmathbb{H}=\bigoplus_{n}\mdmathbb{H}_{n},\qquad\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha}=\bigoplus_{n}\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha n},

(9)

where
\mdmathbb{X}_{n}
is associated with the eigenspace of

\mdmathbb{J}
.
They are defined as
\mdmathbb{X}_{n}\,{=}\,{\rm tr}_{n}\mdmathbb{P}_{n}\mdmathbb{X}\mdmathbb{P}_{n}
where
\mdmathbb{P}_{n}\,{=}\,\prod_{j(J_{\!j}\neq J_{n})}(\mdmathbb{J}{-}J_{\!j}\mdmathbb{1})/(J_{n}{-}J_{\!j})
are projectors

of
\mdmathbb{J}

to the irreducible one of
\mdmathbb{J}_{n}

Corresponding to the multiplicity

This completes our classical, semiclassical and quantum treatment of symmetry-induced DFS. It clearly reveals that the notion of non-stationary (oscillating) behavior and its equivalence to real eigenvalues of the Lindbladian applies equally to classical, semiclassical and quantum systems which establishes DFS for classical and semiclassical systems. For the latter, while coherent oscillations in classical systems can still occur in the presence of dissipation (through the second term in (
5
), see
Keeling2010
;
Bhaseen2012
;
Munoz2019
for examples), diffusion can inhibit long-time oscillatory non-stationary motion (through the third term in (
5
) being of order
\mathord{\mathchar 126h}
). The eigenoperator of the adjoint Lindbladian corresponding to the real nonzero eigenvalues can be constructed classically, see Eq. (
4
), or quantum mechanically (
\mdmathbb{A}_{nm}
) from a symmetry. Playing the role of a beating operator,
A
or
\mdmathbb{A}_{mn}
connect different elements of the constant of motion
J
or
\mdmathbb{J}
in form of its layers in phase space or its irreducible representations in Hilbert space, respectively. Elements
\mdmathbb{J}_{n}
of the symmetry itself can be expressed in terms of the beating operator as
\mdmathbb{J}_{n}\,{=}\,\mdmathbb{A}_{mn}^{\dagger}\mdmathbb{A}_{mn}
.
This irreducible representation also plays a crucial role in the context of breaking the ergodicity thermalization hypothesis in Hamiltonian systems
Sala2020
, which is related to NLD in the absence of dissipation.
For DFS, the Lindblad operators must be degenerate with respect to at least two different irreducible subspaces
n\neq m
of the symmetry, while the hamiltonian must not be degenerate, see (
3
). This gives a clear picture how DFS emerge.

Guided by this insight, we have identified a new class of couplings to the environment leading to DFS, namely when the Lindblad operators do only depend on ghost operators which span the Hilbert space but commute with the symmetry
\mdmathbb{J}
, as explicitly demonstrated with the Heisenberg XXZ spin model.
In terms of the eigenoperator, DFS exist if
[\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha},\mdmathbb{A}_{nm}]\,{=}\,[\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger},\mdmathbb{A}_{nm}]\,{=}\,0

. Note, however, that the latter condition is not necessary since for the adjoint Lindbladian to have real eigenvalues, it follows from (

\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha}^{\dagger}[\mdmathbb{A},\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha}]{+}[\mdmathbb{L}^{\dagger}_{\alpha},\mdmathbb{A}]\mdmathbb{L}_{\alpha}\,{=}\,0
must vanish. How this extends the classes of possible interactions with the environment leading to DFS even further beyond those depending only on ghost operators will be a subject of further research
in_preparation
.

II
Heisenberg spin model

We provide details of the classical construction of ghost variables in order to define environments that allow for decoherence-free subspaces for the Heisenberg model presented in the text.

Classical spin algebra

We consider a chain of
N
spins with in the classical limit is described by the spin variables
\mathbf{S}_{i}=S^{x}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{x}+S^{y}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{y}+S^{z}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{z}
with
i=1,...,N
and the spin algebra

\{S^{\alpha}_{i},S^{\beta}_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}S_{i}

(S13)

with
\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}
the Levi-Civita symbol. The corresponding non-canonical Poisson bracket is given by

\{F,G\}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{S}_{i}\cdot\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial\mathbf{S}_{i}}\times\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial\mathbf{S}_{i}}.

(S14a)

Equivalently, we can describe the dynamics with the beating variables
S^{\pm}_{i}=S^{x}_{i}\pm{\rm i}S^{y}_{i}
, for which the Poisson bracket reads

\displaystyle\{F,G\}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg({-}2{\rm i}S^{z}_{i}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{+}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{-}_{i}}-\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{-}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{+}_{i}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\qquad+{\rm i}\,S^{+}_{i}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{+}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{z}_{i}}-\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{z}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{+}_{i}}\bigg)-{\rm i}\,S^{-}_{i}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{-}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{z}_{i}}-\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{z}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{-}_{i}}\bigg)\bigg),

(S14b)

with the fundamental spin algebra
\{S^{z}_{i},S^{\pm}_{i}\}=\mp{\rm i}S^{\pm}_{i}
and
\{S^{+}_{i},S^{-}_{i}\}=-2{\rm i}S^{z}_{i}
.
Note that in both cases, the classical Poisson bracket is non-canonical and is related to the quantum Lie bracket by
[\mdmathbb{F},\mdmathbb{G}]\equiv{\rm i}\mathord{\mathchar 126h}\{F,G\}
.

Cylindrical coordinates and conserved quantity

In cylindrical coordinates
\{S_{i}^{z},\theta_{i}\}
it is
\mathbf{S}_{i}=\sqrt{S_{i}{}^{2}{-}(S^{z}_{i})^{2}}\,(\mathbf{e}_{x}\cos\theta_{i}+\mathbf{e}_{y}\sin\theta_{i})+S^{z}_{i}\mathbf{e}_{z}
or equivalently

S^{\pm}_{i}=\sqrt{S_{i}{}^{2}{-}(S_{i}^{z})^{2}}\,\exp(\pm{\rm i}\theta)
with
S_{i}{}^{2}=|\mathbf{S}_{i}|^{2}
a Casimir invariant.
Therewith the Hamiltonian (in the semiclassical limit) reads

H(\theta_{i},S^{z}_{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(\omega_{i}S^{z}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left(2\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{[S_{i}^{2}-(S^{z}_{i})^{2}][S_{j}^{2}-(S^{z}_{j})^{2}]}\cos\left(\theta_{i}-\theta_{j}\right)+\Delta_{ij}S^{z}_{i}S^{z}_{j}\right)\bigg),

(S15)

and the Poisson bracket becomes

\{F,G\}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigg(\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial\!\theta_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial S^{z}_{i}}-\dfrac{\partial F}{\partial S^{z}_{i}}\dfrac{\partial G}{\partial\!\theta_{i}}\bigg).

(S16)

From the Hamiltonian (
S15
), it is clear that

J=\dfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S^{z}_{i},

(S17)

is a conserved quantity due to the invariance under a rotation around
\mathbf{e}_{z}
.
The variable canonically conjugate to
J
such that
\{\theta,J\}=1
is given by

\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i},

(S18)

corresponding to the total angle.
There is some freedom for choosing the set of canonically-conjugate variables from there and therefore the
ghost variables
. Indeed, note that one can
transform one set of ghost variables into another one by canonical transformations. The most natural starting point are the Jacobi coordinates for the celestial many-body problem
\Delta\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}
. It is clear that
\{\Delta\theta_{i},J\}=\{\Delta\theta_{i},\theta\}=0
for
i=1,...,N-1
. The goal is now to find the set of variables
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i}
canonically conjugate to
\Delta\theta_{i}
, i.e.,
\{\Delta\theta_{i},\widetilde{S}^{z}_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}
with
\delta_{ij}
the Kronecker delta.

Ghost variables using canonical transformations

For doing so, we use the
F_{2}
generating function
Goldstein1980
. We recall that for a set of canonically conjugate variables
(\boldsymbol{\theta},\mathbf{S}^{z})
the new set of canonically conjugate variables
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{z})
can be found by using transformations, such that
F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{z})
, such that

\mathbf{S}^{z}=\dfrac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\theta}},\qquad\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\dfrac{\partial F_{2}}{\partial\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{z}}.

(S19)

Here, the old variables are given by
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{N})
and
\mathbf{S}^{z}=(S^{z}_{1},...,S^{z}_{N})
. The new variables are
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(\Delta\theta_{1},...,\Delta\theta_{N{-}1},\theta)
and
\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{z}=(\widetilde{S}^{z}_{1},...,\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}1},J)
. Given the form of the new variables
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
which we have imposed, the generating function is given by

F_{2}(\boldsymbol{\theta},\widetilde{\mathbf{S}}^{z})=J\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{N{-}1}\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i}(\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}).

(S20)

Indeed, from (
S19
) and (
S20
), we obtain that
\widetilde{\theta}_{0}\equiv\theta=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\theta_{i}
and
\widetilde{\theta}_{i}\equiv\Delta\theta_{i}=\theta_{i+1}-\theta_{i}
for
i=1,...,N{-}1
. The expression of the old momenta with respect to the new ones are found using (
S19
) as

S^{z}_{1}=J-\widetilde{S}^{z}_{1},\qquad S^{z}_{i}=J+\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i{-}1}-\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i},\qquad S^{z}_{N}=J+\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}1},

(S21)

for
i=2,...,N{-}1
. We can easily check that (
S17
) is fulfilled. In order to invert this transformation and obtain the expression of the ghost variables with respect to
S^{z}_{i}
, a convenient way is to write it in a matrix form

\begin{bmatrix}S^{z}_{1}\\
S^{z}_{2}\\
S^{z}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
S^{z}_{N{-}2}\\
S^{z}_{N{-}1}\\
S^{z}_{N}\\
\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-1&0&0&\ldots&0&0&1\\
1&-1&0&\ldots&0&0&1\\
0&1&-1&\ldots&0&0&1\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&-1&0&1\\
0&0&0&\ldots&1&-1&1\\
0&0&0&\ldots&0&1&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{S}^{z}_{1}\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}3}\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}2}\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}1}\\
J\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(S22)

The inverse transformation is given by

\begin{bmatrix}\widetilde{S}^{z}_{1}\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{2}\\
\vdots\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}3}\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}2}\\
\widetilde{S}^{z}_{N{-}1}\\
J\\
\end{bmatrix}=\dfrac{1}{N}\begin{bmatrix}-N{+}1&1&1&\ldots&1&1&1\\
-N{+}2&-N{+}2&2&\ldots&2&2&2\\
-N{+}3&-N{+}3&-N{+}3&\ldots&3&3&3\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ldots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
-2&-2&-2&\ldots&-2&N{-}2&N{-}2\\
-1&-1&-1&\ldots&-1&-1&N{-}1\\
1&1&1&\ldots&1&1&1\\
\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}S^{z}_{1}\\
S^{z}_{2}\\
S^{z}_{3}\\
\vdots\\
S^{z}_{N{-}2}\\
S^{z}_{N{-}1}\\
S^{z}_{N}\\
\end{bmatrix}.

(S23)

Therefore, the ghost variables are given by the Jacobi momentum coordinates

\widetilde{S}^{z}_{k}=\dfrac{k}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}S^{z}_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}S^{z}_{i},\qquad\Delta\theta_{k}=\theta_{k+1}-\theta_{k},\qquad k=1,...,N{-}1

(S24)

as given in the main text. One can easily check that the transformation is canonical with
\{\theta,J\}=1
,
\{\Delta\theta_{i},\widetilde{S}^{z}_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}
and
\{\theta,\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i}\}=\{J,\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i}\}=\{\theta,\Delta\theta_{i}\}=\{J,\Delta\theta_{i}\}=\{\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i},\widetilde{S}^{z}_{j}\}=\{\Delta\theta_{i},\Delta\theta_{j}\}=0
for all
i
and
j
.
In the same way, we find the change of coordinates for the angles and relative angles.

In this new set of variables, the Hamiltonian
H(J,\Delta\theta_{i},\widetilde{S}_{i}^{z})
replacing the one in Eq. (
S15
) is independent of the angle
\theta

Beating variables and transformation back to Cartesian spin algebra

As mentioned in the main text, from canonical variables associated to a conserved quantity
(\theta,J)
, one can construct beating observables
A=f(J)\exp({-}{\rm i}\theta)
and

One can always perform the inverse change of coordinates from (

In the previous paragraph, we have described the canonical transformation from

\displaystyle=J,

(S26a)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\widetilde{S}_{i}{}^{2}-(\widetilde{S}^{z}_{i})^{2}}\exp(\pm{\rm i}\Delta\theta_{i}),

\displaystyle i

\displaystyle=1,...,N{-}1,

Note that

From (
S27a
), we upgrade the beating and ghost variables to their quantum mechanical counterparts, the beating and ghost operators. To do so, we replace the variables by their associated operators (which are matrices) in the expressions (

\mdmathbb{S}_{i}^{z}

For the Heisenberg spin model, we find a simplified version of a beating operator
