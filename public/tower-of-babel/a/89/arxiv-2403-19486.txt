Distributionally robust monopoly pricing: Switching from low to high prices in volatile markets
Tim S.G. van Eck, Pieter Kleer, Johan S.H. van Leeuwaarden

Abstract
Traditional monopoly pricing assumes sellers have full information about consumer valuations. We consider monopoly pricing under limited information, where a seller only knows the mean, variance and support of the valuation distribution. The objective is to maximize expected revenue by selecting the optimal fixed price. We adopt a distributionally robust framework, where the seller considers all valuation distributions that comply with the limited information. We formulate a maximin problem which seeks to maximize expected revenue for the worst-case valuation distribution. The minimization problem that identifies the worst-case valuation distribution is solved using primal-dual methods, and in turn leads to an explicitly solvable maximization problem. This yields a closed-form optimal pricing policy and a new fundamental principle prescribing when to use low and high robust prices. We show that the optimal policy switches from low to high prices when variance becomes sufficiently large, yielding significant performance gains compared with existing robust prices that generally decay with market uncertainty. This presents guidelines for when the seller should switch from targeting mass markets to niche markets. Similar guidelines are obtained for delay-prone services with rational utility-maximizing customers, underlining the universality and wide applicability of the low-high pricing principle.



1
Introduction

Optimizing revenue in contemporary markets represents a pivotal challenge, extensively explored within theoretical pricing models spanning disciplines such as operations research, computer science, and economics. Prevailing in the theoretical pricing literature is the assumption that sellers possess complete knowledge of how the market responds to price variations. This assumption relies on a full understanding of the demand function, serving as a prior in Bayesian analysis, and significantly influencing the derived optimal price. However, the theoretical advantage of assuming full knowledge is compromised by the scarcity or unreliability of market data. A principle known as Wilson’s doctrine
Wilson 1987
calls for less detailed yet parsimonious models, and the development of more resilient pricing theories, capable of accommodating imprecise market details
Carroll 2019
.

Robust pricing theories seek strategies that perform well across diverse markets, reducing sensitivity to detailed market knowledge. The interplay between a seller’s pricing strategy and market knowledge then becomes crucial. A complete lack of knowledge may result in an overly pessimistic worst-case scenario, rendering a robust price excessively conservative and impractical. A more realistic approach is to assume partial knowledge. This paper addresses this scenario in the context of a monopolist selling a single type of good to a diverse, independent consumer base.

The monopolist aims to determine the revenue-maximizing price by balancing aggressive (low) pricing for a larger market share against higher pricing for a smaller market share. Each consumer’s valuation of the good is an independent draw from a given valuation distribution with the purchase occurring when this valuation exceeds the price. The expected revenue is defined as the product of the price times the probability that the valuation exceeds the price.
While the optimal price is easily determined when the valuation distribution is known
Riley and Zeckhauser 1983
,
Myerson 1981
, the challenge arises when only partial market knowledge is available.

This study focuses on the case where only the mean, variance, and an upper limit of the valuation distribution are known. Mean-variance information proves suitable for robust decision-making, capturing both the anticipated future scenario (mean) and the risk associated with less likely scenarios (variance). This choice is motivated by the often scarce or non-existent data faced by sellers, where mean, variance, and upper bounds can be based on expert opinions—intuitive statistics that are easier to estimate than the entire valuation distribution; see e.g.
Scarf 1958
,
Azar and Micali 2012
.

The partially-informed seller needs to decide on the price in the face of uncertain market demand, much in the same way as an investor who only knows the risk and return of assets. To do so, the seller employs a maximin decision framework. The maximin price, or robust price, is determined by first establishing the worst-case valuation distribution that minimizes expected revenue for all prices and then maximizing expected revenue by setting the optimal price. This involves solving two optimization problems—a semi-infinite linear program and finding the global optimum of a bimodal function that depends delicately on the price and information parameters. The comprehensive nature of this approach reveals the intricate interplay between partial knowledge and robust pricing, surpassing earlier studies
Azar and Micali 2012
,
Kos and Messner 2015
,
Suzdaltsev 2018
,
Chen et al. 2022a
,
Chen et al. 2023b
and highlighting a novel robust pricing principle emerging only when knowledge includes both the upper bound and variance.

Consequently, when there is sufficiently low dispersion, indicative of relatively uniform consumer valuations, sellers are inclined to cater to “mass markets” through competitive pricing strategies. Conversely, as dispersion increases, the average consumer’s willingness to pay diminishes. The heightened heterogeneity among consumers prompts firms to adopt a strategy of restricting sales to a more specialized “niche market” characterized by higher prices. We add a theory aligned with the above intuition, showing that for a rich class of potential consumer valuations, a robust seller indeed switches from low pricing with large market share to high pricing and small market share in response to increased consumer valuation dispersion.

To understand the main pricing principle in this paper, now assume that the seller only knows the mean, standard deviation and support, and chooses to fit to these summary statistics a Beta distribution. Since the monopolist now only pretends to know the valuation distribution, the optimal price found from assuming a Beta distribution is only an approximation, as the ground truth valuation is likely not to be Beta distributed. In fact, the Beta distribution is only one of infinitely many distributions with the same mean, standard deviation and support, and only serves to calculate the expected revenue. This paper presents an alternative pricing strategy, where the monopolist does not commit to a specific valuation distribution, and instead first determines the worst-case valuation distribution that minimizes demand, and only then solves for the revenue-maximizing price. We prove that this robust maximin price corresponds to the black solid line in Figure

Related literature

Our study adds to the stream of research on robust pricing decisions under limited information, where sellers must commit to fixed prices without possessing complete knowledge of market valuation and the corresponding demand function.
Our approach focuses on mean-variance (or standard deviation) information, a widely studied information set in applications such as investment theory
Natarajan and Teo 2017
, option pricing
De Schepper and Heijnen 2007
, insurances
Roos et al. 2022
, service operation management
van Eekelen et al. 2022
,
Taylor 2018
and inventory management
Scarf 1958
,
Gallego 1992
. In the management science and operations literature, this information setting is commonly referred to as ‘distribution-free’, highlighting that the decision-making under uncertainty does not require detailed or full distributional knowledge.

Distributionally robust optimization.

Such distribution-free analysis combined with optimization falls in the more general domain of distributionally robust optimization (DRO), a mathematical framework that addresses uncertainty in optimization problems by considering an ambiguity set of possible probability distributions for the involved random variables, rather than relying on a single assumed distribution. The ambiguity sets considered in this paper are based on moment information, as in the pioneering work of
Scarf 1958
on robust inventory management, and later generalized to a powerful method with many applications
Delage and Ye 2010
,
Wiesemann et al. 2014
. Applied to monopoly pricing, this moment-based DRO approach helps create more robust and conservative pricing strategies that perform well under a range of scenarios. Instead of making strong assumptions about the specific valuation distribution, DRO considers a set of possible distributions, forming a certain ambiguity set, and resulting in pricing strategies that are robust across different scenarios, without relying on a single, possibly incorrect, assumed distribution.
This aligns well with Occam’s razor
Chen et al. 2023a
and Wilson’s doctrine
Carroll 2019
, two principles of parsimony that aim for explanatory models able to capture the essence without making unnecessary assumptions or requiring overly strong parametric assumptions, such as particular features of probability distributions.

Regularity assumptions.

The lion’s share of the pricing literature assumes strong regularity conditions on the valuation distribution that guarantee existence and uniqueness of an optimal price. In particular, assuming that the valuation has a monotone hazard rate, or some closely related condition, can guarantee that the revenue function is concave and has a unique optimizer
Ziya et al. 2004
,
Schweizer and Szech 2019
.
Such regularity assumptions greatly simplify the mathematical framework, but true valuation distributions are more often irregular than not
Morganti 2022
,
Roughgarden and Schrijvers 2016
.

Loertscher and Muir 2022
write: “The standard assumption, which is almost universally maintained in economics, is that expected revenue is concave. The typical justification for this assumption, other than it being standard, is that it is deemed an analytic simplification that permits one to focus on the key economic insights without cluttering the analysis with case distinctions and multiplicity of local maxima. We have never seen it justified on the basis of empirical evidence, and we will not impose it.” See
Celis et al. 2014
for empirical evidence that the value distribution need not be regular. See also the literature overview in
Loertscher and Muir 2022
for more references on non-concave revenue and a proof that whenever two or more separate markets merge, the revenue is no longer concave. Even in robust settings, regularity conditions are often maintained for mathematical tractability, see e.g.
Maskin and Riley 1984
,
Eren and Maglaras 2010
,
Allouah et al. 2022
.
For robust pricing as in this paper, one should let go of regularity completely, as the worst-case distribution that plays a pivotal role in the maximin analysis typically fails this assumption. In fact, if you would add to the minimization problem the condition that the worst-case valuation needs to be regular, we would lose the key take-aways and novel economic insights of this paper.

Pricing in service operations.

Monopoly pricing in the context of delay-sensitive service systems has been extensively examined. A well-established body of literature, as detailed by
Hassin and Haviv 2003
, delves into mathematical models that capture customer decision-making regarding joining delay-sensitive services based on individual utility considerations. The pioneering work by
Naor 1969
laid the groundwork for this branch of operations management. Naor’s seminal model views the system as an M/M/1 queue and models customer utility as linearly dependent on the price, value of service, and the anticipated waiting time. Naor revealed that rational individuals have a tendency to overcrowd the system and suggested the introduction of a fixed fee for service that optimizes social welfare. The determination of this socially optimal fee involves solving first-order conditions, akin to classical monopoly pricing. Expanding upon the foundational work by Naor, numerous studies investigated pricing and control considerations for a variety of extended queueing scenarios, with often the focus on determining the price that maximizes revenue or welfare; see
e.g.
Dewan and Mendelson 1990
,
Chen and Frank 2004
,
Ha 1998
,
Taylor 2018
,
Baron et al. 2023
,
Chen et al. 2022b
.
Stidham 1992
made a significant contribution by introducing the concept of random valuation for individual customers and assuming that the arrival rate of customers with a valuation surpassing a specified threshold. In this framework, valuation is often referred to as willingness-to-pay, and as in classical monopoly pricing, is a random variable with some given distribution.
Cachon and Feldman 2011
,
Haviv and Randhawa 2014
build on Stidham’s framework and assume that a rational customer joins when its random valuation exceeds the sum of the price and the expected delay costs, and determined optimal prices when the valuation distribution is assumed to be known. We will perform a distribution-free analysis of this rational queueing model and show that again the optimal pricing strategy switches from low to high pricing when the standard deviation of the valuation crosses a certain threshold.

Robust pricing with three prices

We proceed with the most general setting covered in this study, where instead of knowing an upper bound or exact value, the seller knows that the standard deviation is contained in an interval
[\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\bar{\sigma}]
. The width of this interval thus reflects how confident the seller is about the actual value
\sigma
, or equivalently, how accurate the seller can estimate
\sigma

From Lemma

we see that
the revenue maximization problem becomes

\sup_{p}\tilde{R}(p)

\displaystyle\tilde{R}(p)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\bar{\sigma},\beta))=\begin{cases}R_{1}(p)=\frac{p(\mu-p)^{2}}{(\mu-p)^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2}},\quad&p\in(0,\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}],\\
R_{2}(p)=\frac{p(\mu-p)}{\beta-p},\quad&p\in[\bar{\upsilon}_{1},\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}],\\

(24)

Theorem 5.1

(Three prices theorem)
.

When the seller knows
\mu
,
\beta
, and that

, the optimal robust price
p^{*}=\arg\sup_{p}\tilde{R}(p)
is contained in the set

Proof.

When
\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}=\bar{\sigma}
, we know from Proposition
4.2
that

\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}<\bar{\sigma}

4.1
and
4.2

respectively. What remains is to exclude the potential boundary optimal prices
\bar{\upsilon}_{1}
and
\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}
.

We will now show that
\bar{\upsilon}_{1}
cannot be the unique optimal price. Since
\bar{\upsilon}_{1}\in(0,\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1})

\displaystyle R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\bar{\sigma},\beta))

\displaystyle\geq R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\bar{\sigma},\beta))=\max\{R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\bar{\sigma})),R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\beta))\}.

(25)

In addition, it holds that

\displaystyle R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\bar{\sigma},\beta))

\displaystyle=R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\bar{\sigma}))\mathds{1}\{p\in(0,\bar{\upsilon}_{1}]\}+R(p,\mathcal{P}(\mu,\beta))\mathds{1}\{p\in(\bar{\upsilon}_{1},\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1})\},

5.2
Effect of bundling

The existing literature considering mean-variance information described a pricing policy with only one robust price
Azar and Micali 2012
,
Chen et al. 2022a
, the low price in our pricing policy. We also include support information, and find two and at most three price functions. Our new pricing policy that adjusts the price to market volatility is transparent and simple with as main strength a high price responding to high uncertainty, leading to high revenue. If the goal is to use only one, instead of two or three prices, the seller could opt for bundling.
The seller then sets a price for a bundle of goods, so that customers buy either the bundle or nothing. A big theme in the pricing literature centers around the question whether bundling is better than separate sales, and in general there is no clear or straightforward answer to that question
Bakos and Brynjolfsson 1999
,
Hart and Nisan 2017
.
We will use the result for the single product to assess this bundle product pricing. Assuming valuations are i.i.d.,
the revenue of selling a bundle of
m
goods with valuations
X_{1},\ldots,X_{m}
for a bundle price
p_{(m)}
has expected revenue

\displaystyle p_{(m)}\cdot\mathbb{P}\Big(\sum_{i=1}^{m}X_{i}\geq p_{(m)}\Big).

(35)

Turning to the limited information setting,
the single-product information about the mean, variance and maximal valuation translates into information for the bundle by multiplying all three characteristics with the number of goods
m
.
Notice that a bundle of
m
i.i.d. goods, each with mean
\mu
, standard deviation
\sigma\in[\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\bar{\sigma}]
and maximal valuation
\beta
can be viewed as selling a single good with as valuation the average of all
m
valuations, so with mean
\mu
, maximal value
\beta
and

\sigma\in[\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}}{\sqrt{m}},\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{m}}]
. As a result, for any
p<\mu
and large enough
m
, the tail bound (
6
) becomes

\displaystyle\inf_{\mathbb{P}\in\mathcal{P}\left(\mu,\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}}{\sqrt{m}},\frac{\bar{\sigma}}{\sqrt{m}},\beta\right)}\mathbb{P}(X\geq p)=\frac{(\mu-p)^{2}}{(\mu-p)^{2}+\tfrac{\bar{\sigma}^{2}}{m}}

(36)

and the expected worst-case revenue is maximized by the low price
p^{*}_{l}(\mu,\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{m}},\beta)
, yielding an optimal price and revenue that approach
\mu
when
m
tends to infinity. This result also follows from
Chen et al. 2022a
,
Giannakopoulos et al. 2023

7
Conclusions and outlook

Transitioning from low to high pricing in response to increased market volatility has not been previously documented in the existing literature on robust pricing. When next to the mean, the seller is only aware of the standard deviation, this leads to relatively lower prices that decrease with risk. Similarly, relying exclusively on valuation bounds results in risk-insensitive pricing.
The distinctive switch from low to high pricing occurs only when the seller possesses, in addition to the mean and valuation bounds, sufficiently precise information about the standard deviation. This specific combination of factors contributing to the phenomenon might explain why it has been overseen so far.
Another reason might be the dominance of low pricing strategies in prior research, coupled with a focus on the intuition that prices should decrease with the level of risk or volatility.

As illustrated in the example of Beta-distributed valuations discussed in the introduction, instances of large or extreme volatility translate into worst-case scenarios, akin to all-or-nothing scenarios. When these scenarios become sufficiently likely, the seller shifts strategies from low pricing and aggressive market penetration to high prices tailored exclusively for those users with the highest valuations.
This strategic shift emphasizes the imperative to tailor pricing strategies to accommodate different levels of market uncertainty, particularly in instances of substantial market volatility. We anticipate that the transition to higher pricing due to increased risk, as elucidated in this study via a basic yet parsimonious pricing model, holds broader applicability in more generalized or detailed models and real-world settings. We have demonstrated this already for the setting of delay-prone services.

Exploring the occurrence of low-high pricing in robust settings with partial demand information captured in terms of ambiguity sets is worthwhile to investigate in more detail. Ambiguity sets not explored in this paper include those conditioned on the first three or more moments, or distance-based ambiguity sets centered around a reference distribution, with distance measured in terms of metrics like
\phi
-divergence, Kullback-Leibler distance and Wasserstein distance.
While necessitating a distinct mathematical approach, we anticipate that these alternative ambiguity sets may also yield instances of low-high pricing, particularly for sufficiently high levels of ambiguity.

B.1
Proof of Lemma
4.2

We first set
\mu=1
without loss of generality. Recall
\hat{R}(p)
from (
18
) and define
\hat{R}(p;\mu,\sigma,\beta)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\hat{R}(p)
. In addition, consider the scaled parameters
\nu=\frac{\sigma}{\mu}
,
\tau=\frac{\beta}{\mu}
, and
\psi=\frac{p}{\mu}
. From (
18
) it follows that

\displaystyle\hat{R}(p;\mu,\sigma,\beta)=\hat{R}(\psi\mu;\mu,\nu\mu,\tau\mu)=\mu\cdot\hat{R}(\psi;1,\nu,\tau).

(80)

Hence, using the substitution
p=\psi\mu
, we have

\displaystyle\arg\sup_{p}\hat{R}(p;\mu,\sigma,\beta)=\mu\arg\sup_{\psi}\hat{R}(\psi;1,\nu,\tau).

(81)

We will now proceed with
\mu=1
. Consider that for
\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}
to be the unique global optimum, the following ordering needs to hold:

p^{*}_{h}(\sigma,\beta)<\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}<p^{*}_{l}(\sigma).

(82)

We will show that this ordering never occurs by considering two cases that exhaust all possibilities. The first case considers
\sigma\geq 1
. We claim the following:

\displaystyle p^{*}_{h}(\sigma,\beta)\geq 1\geq\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}.

(83)

The second inequality is trivial. To see why the first inequality holds true, note that

\displaystyle\beta-\sqrt{\beta(\beta-(1+\sigma^{2}))}\geq 1

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta-1\geq\sqrt{\beta^{2}-\beta-\beta\sigma^{2}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\beta^{2}-2\beta+1\geq\beta^{2}-\beta-\beta\sigma^{2}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow 1+\beta\sigma^{2}\geq\beta.

(84)

This is true since both
\sigma^{2}\geq 1
and
\beta\geq 1
.

The second case considers
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Denote

\beta_{l}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{2}\left(2+\sigma^{2}+A^{\frac{1}{3}}+\frac{\sigma^{4}}{A^{\frac{1}{3}}}\right)\text{with }A\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sigma^{4}(\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}+1)^{2},

(85)

as the unique value of
\beta
for which
p^{*}_{l}(\sigma)=\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}
,
and

\beta_{h}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{2-2^{\frac{4}{3}}\sigma^{4}B^{-\frac{1}{3}}-2^{\frac{2}{3}}B^{\frac{1}{3}}}{2(1-\sigma^{2})}\text{with }B\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-2\sigma^{8},

(86)

as the unique value for which
p^{*}_{h}(\sigma,\beta)=\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}
. For (
82
) to hold, it has to be the case that
\beta_{h}<\beta_{l}
. Hence, we will show that the opposite, namely
\beta_{h}\geq\beta_{l}
is always true. It is sufficient to demonstrate that
\beta_{h}\geq(1-\sigma^{2})\beta_{h}\geq\beta_{l}
. This results in the following inequality:

2\sigma^{\frac{4}{3}}+2\sigma^{\frac{8}{3}}\geq\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{\frac{4}{3}}(\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}+1)^{\frac{2}{3}}+\frac{\sigma^{\frac{8}{3}}}{(\sqrt{1+\sigma^{2}}+1)^{\frac{2}{3}}}.

(87)

Dividing by
\sigma^{\frac{4}{3}}
and substituting
c
for
\sigma^{\frac{2}{3}}
results in

2+2c^{2}\geq c+(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{\frac{2}{3}}+\frac{c^{2}}{(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{\frac{2}{3}}},

(88)

which is the same as

2+2c^{2}-c\geq(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{-\frac{2}{3}}\left[(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{\frac{4}{3}}+c^{2}\right].

(89)

Since
\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1\geq 2
for every
c\in(0,1)
, we have

\frac{2}{3}\geq 2^{-\frac{2}{3}}\geq(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{-\frac{2}{3}}.

(90)

Hence, combining (
89
) with (
90
) results in

2+2c^{2}-c\geq\frac{2}{3}\left[(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{\frac{4}{3}}+c^{2}\right],

(91)

which is equivalent to

3+2c^{2}-\frac{3}{2}c\geq(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{\frac{4}{3}}.

(92)

Denote the left-hand side as
r(c)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=3+2c^{2}-\frac{3}{2}c
and the right-hand side as
t(c)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=(\sqrt{1+c^{3}}+1)^{\frac{4}{3}}
. Note that the quadratic function
r(c)
has one local minimum at
c^{*}=\frac{3}{8}
and that
t(c)
is strictly increasing in
c
. By inspection, one can verify that (
92
) is true for both
c=0
and
c=c^{*}
. Since
r(c)
is decreasing on the interval
(0,c^{*}]
, and
t(c)
increasing, it follows that (
92
) is true for all
c\in(0,c^{*}]
. Both functions are increasing on the interval
(c^{*},1]
. We will argue that it suffices to check (
92
) for a finite number of points to conclude that it holds for all
c\in(c^{*},1)
.

Assume that for a given
\epsilon>0
and
d,d+\epsilon\in(c^{*},1]
, we have
r(d)>t(d+\epsilon)
. Then (
92
) holds for any
c\in[d,d+\epsilon]
, as

\displaystyle r(c)\geq r(d)\geq t(d+\epsilon)\geq t(c),

(93)

since
r
and
t
are both continuous and increasing. Therefore, we can make a discretization of the interval
(c^{*},1]
formed by
K
points
c^{*}=c_{0}<c_{1}<...<c_{K}=1
with
c_{k}=c_{0}+\epsilon*k
, for all
k\in\{1,...,K\}
. As it turns out,
K=6
is the smallest number for which this method works and has corresponding
\epsilon=\frac{1-c^{*}}{6}=\frac{5}{48}
. Table
2
shows the function evaluations that together prove (
92
).

This proves that (
82
) never occurs when
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Hence,
\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{1}
is never the unique global optimum.

B.2
Proof of Corollary
5.2

With shorthand notation
r\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mu}{\sigma}
and
r_{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\sqrt{1+(\frac{\mu}{\sigma})^{2}}>r
, observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{l}(\mu,\bar{\sigma})}{\partial r}=\dfrac{\left(\sqrt[3]{r+r_{+}}+\sqrt[3]{r-r_{+}}\right)^{2}\left(\left(r+r_{+}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}-\left(r-r_{+}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\right)}{2r^{2}r_{+}\left(r-r_{+}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\left(r+r_{+}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}}\geq 0,

(94)

as
r>0
and

\displaystyle\left(r+r_{+}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}-\left(r-r_{+}\right)^{\frac{2}{3}}\geq 0.

(95)

Hence,
\rho_{l}(\mu,\bar{\sigma})
increases in
\mu
and decreases in
\bar{\sigma}
.
Next, denote
s\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\beta}{\mu}
and observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{m}(\mu,\beta)}{\partial s}=2\left(1-\dfrac{2s-1}{2\sqrt{\left(s-1\right)s}}\right)\leq 0.

(96)

Therefore,
\rho_{m}(\mu,\beta)
increases in
\mu
and decreases in
\beta
.
With
q\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{\mu^{2}+\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}^{2}}{\mu\beta}
observe that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho_{h}(\mu,\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\beta)}{\partial q}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-q}}-1\geq 0,

(97)

since
q=\frac{\underaccent{\bar}{\upsilon}_{2}}{\beta}<1
. This shows that
\rho_{h}(\mu,\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\beta)
increases in
\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}
and decreases in
\beta
.
Next, recall that
p^{*}_{l}(\bar{\sigma})
is the unique real interior solution of
R_{1}(p)
, so solving

\displaystyle(p^{*}_{l}(\bar{\sigma}))^{3}-3\mu(p^{*}_{l}(\bar{\sigma}))^{2}+3(\mu^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2})p^{*}_{l}(\bar{\sigma})=\mu(\mu^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2}).

(98)

To simplify the analysis, we change variables by substituting
\mu_{2}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mu^{2}+\bar{\sigma}^{2}
(the maximal second moment), and write (
98
) as

\displaystyle(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu))^{3}-3\mu(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu))^{2}+3\mu_{2}p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)=\mu\mu_{2}.

(99)

By keeping
p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)
implicit while isolating
\mu
and
\mu_{2}
in (
99
), we can write (
98
) as

\displaystyle L_{1}(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu),\mu_{2})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu))^{3}+3p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)\mu_{2}}{3(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu))^{2}+\mu_{2}}=\mu,

(100)

or

\displaystyle L_{2}(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu),\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{-(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu))^{2}(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)-3\mu)}{3p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)-\mu}=\mu_{2}.

(101)

Differentiating both sides of (
100
) with respect to
\mu
yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L_{1}(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu),\mu_{2})}{\partial p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)}\cdot\frac{\partial p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)}{\partial\mu}=\frac{3((p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)))^{2}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{(3p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)^{2}+\mu_{2})^{2}}\cdot\frac{\partial p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)}{\partial\mu}=1,

(102)

showing that
p^{*}_{l}(\bar{\sigma})
is (strictly) increasing in
\mu
. Differentiating both sides of (
101
) with respect to
\mu_{2}
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\partial L_{2}(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu),\mu)}{\partial p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)}\cdot\frac{\partial p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)}{\partial\mu_{2}}=\frac{-6p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)(p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)-\mu)^{2}}{(3p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)-\mu)^{2}}\cdot\frac{\partial p^{*}_{l}(\mu_{2}-\mu)}{\partial\mu_{2}}=1,

(103)

showing that
p^{*}_{l}(\bar{\sigma})
is (strictly) decreasing in
\bar{\sigma}
. To see why
p^{*}_{m}(\beta)
is increasing in
\mu
and decreasing in
\beta
, we examine
\rho_{m}(\mu,\beta)={2p^{*}_{m}(\beta)}/{\mu}-1
, which is clearly increasing in
p^{*}_{m}(\beta)
. Hence, the same monotonicity properties directly apply to
p^{*}_{m}(\beta)
. Finally,
p^{*}_{h}(\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\beta)
increases in
\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}
and decreases in
\beta
, due to
p^{*}_{h}(\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma},\beta)=p^{*}_{m}(\mu+{\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}^{2}}/{\mu},\beta)
and monotonicity of both
\mu+{\underaccent{\bar}{\sigma}^{2}}/{\mu}
and
p^{*}_{m}(\beta)
.
