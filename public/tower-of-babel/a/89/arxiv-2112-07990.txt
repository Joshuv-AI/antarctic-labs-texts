Supervised learning of analysis-sparsity priors with automatic differentiation
Hashem Ghanem, Joseph Salmon, Nicolas Keriven, Samuel Vaiter

Abstract
Sparsity priors are commonly used in denoising and image reconstruction. For analysis-type priors, a dictionary defines a representation of signals that is likely to be sparse. In most situations, this dictionary is not known, and is to be recovered from pairs of ground-truth signals and measurements, by minimizing the reconstruction error. This defines a hierarchical optimization problem, which can be cast as a bi-level optimization. Yet, this problem is unsolvable, as reconstructions and their derivative w.r.t. the dictionary have no closed-form expression. However, reconstructions can be iteratively computed using the Forward-Backward splitting (FB) algorithm. In this paper, we approximate reconstructions by the output of the aforementioned FB algorithm. Then, we leverage automatic differentiation to evaluate the gradient of this output w.r.t. the dictionary, which we learn with projected gradient descent. Experiments show that our algorithm successfully learns the 1D Total Variation (TV) dictionary from piecewise constant signals. For the same case study, we propose to constrain our search to dictionaries of 0-centered columns, which removes undesired local minima and improves numerical stability.



I
Introduction

Denoising is a widely-tackled problem that emerges in many fields,
where the goal is to restore signals from noisy observations. This includes that the model of the imaging system is
a priori
known:
\boldsymbol{y=w+\varepsilon}
, where
\boldsymbol{w},\boldsymbol{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
are the true and the measured signals, respectively, and
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is additive noise.
In addition, a prior hypothesis on the nature of the signal, or an embedding of it, might be available, like sparsity
[
10
]
. Such extra knowledge can be incorporated in the optimization process to get better reconstructions (
e.g.,
a higher Signal to Noise Ratio (SNR)).

Sparsity priors exist in two forms
[
6
]
:

i)
synthesis (traditional) sparsity where
\boldsymbol{w}=\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{u}
,
\boldsymbol{D}
is a linear operator, and
\boldsymbol{u}
is sparse;

ii)
analysis sparsity where
\boldsymbol{v}=\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is sparse. This paper is interested in the latter.
As a convex surrogate, this prior is enforced on
\boldsymbol{w}
by adding the term
\|\boldsymbol{D^{\top}w}\|_{1}
to the (generally quadratic) loss function
[
9
]
, where
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is the
\ell_{1}
norm. Putting all together, the problem consists in finding:

\boldsymbol{\hat{w}}=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{w}}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\lambda\|\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}

for some regularization amplitude parameter
\lambda\geq 0
.
The linear operator
\boldsymbol{D}
is either user-defined, or learnt directly from data.

The main problem we tackle in this work is to extract both
\boldsymbol{D}
and
\lambda
from data with supervised learning.
Having that
\lambda\|\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}=\|(\lambda\boldsymbol{D})^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}
, this problem is equivalent to extracting the product
\lambda\boldsymbol{D}
as one object, thus we stop notating
\lambda
explicitly from now on and keep
\boldsymbol{D}
.
To avoid trivial solutions and undesired local minima, it is common to restrict the search space to an admissible set
\mathcal{C}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
, where dictionaries have a specific property. In
[
12
]
for instance,
\boldsymbol{D}
is forced to be orthogonal,
i.e.,
\mathcal{C}=\{\boldsymbol{D};\boldsymbol{DD}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{I}_{p}\}
. In
[
11
]
,
\boldsymbol{D}
is constrained to be a convolution dictionary.
However, it is still challenging to find an admissible set that performs well in all applications of this problem
[
17
]
.
In this work, we don’t commit to find such universal set.
Formally, we suppose we have a dataset
(\boldsymbol{y}_{l},\boldsymbol{w}_{l})_{l=1}^{L}
, that includes
L
pairs of measurements and their associated ground truth signals.
Knowing such pairs, our task is to find the operator
\boldsymbol{D}
that minimizes the mean squared error between reconstructions and ground truth signals:

\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}}\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{D})=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\big\|\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}\left(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y}_{l}\right)-\boldsymbol{w}_{l}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\iota_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{D})

(1a)

s.t.

\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{w}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}\frac{1}{2}\|\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{w}\|_{2}^{2}+\|\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}\kern 5.0pt,

(1b)

where
\mathcal{C}
is an admissible set of dictionaries, and
\iota_{\mathcal{C}}
is the indicator function:
\iota_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{D})=0
if
\boldsymbol{D}\in\mathcal{C}
and
+\infty
otherwise.

One can recast
Equation

1
as a
bilevel optimization problem
: in the outer problem, we learn the model
\boldsymbol{D}
as in
Eq.

1a
while in the inner problem, we denoise measurements following
Eq.

1b
.
We already know that the inner part can be solved applying the Forward-Backward splitting (FB) algorithm on the dual problem
[
5
]
.
However, due to the
\ell_{1}
norm, neither the solution nor its gradient
w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{D}
have closed-form expressions.
Thus, the minimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
cannot be derived analytically nor obtained with gradient-based methods.

To illustrate how our algorithm can recover a dictionary
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
, we consider the well-known problem of
1D piecewise constant signals reconstruction
as a case-study
[
5
]
, where this prior indicates that
(w_{2}-w_{1},\dots,w_{1}-w_{p})^{\top}
is sparse.
The estimator is often written as an instance of
Eq.

1b
, with
\boldsymbol{D}=\boldsymbol{D}_{TV}
is the dictionary associated to the 1D Total Variation (TV) regularization: for all
i\in\{1,\dots,p\};\boldsymbol{D}_{i,i}=-1,\boldsymbol{D}_{i+1,i}=1
, and 0 otherwise, up to rescaling.

Contribution
We approximately recover the analysis-sparsity operator
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
by:

i)
replacing the true minimizer
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})
by the output of the FB algorithm applied on the dual problem;

ii)
deploying
automatic differentiation
, a technique capable of evaluating the gradient of an algorithm
w.r.t.
input variables, to solve
Eq.

1a
with projected gradient descent.
We empirically show that our method recovers the TV dictionary
\boldsymbol{D}_{TV}
from piecewise constant signals.
Finally, for the same case study, we reduce the admissible set
\mathcal{C}
to dictionaries with columns summing up to zero, and empirically prove that this increases stability and extracts the TV operator with higher quality than previous methods.

Related work
The bilevel problem was first posed in
[
11
]
, where the authors smoothed the
\ell_{1}
norm so that the derivative of
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})

w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{D}
has a closed-form expression.
Then, they applied gradient descent to find a local minimizer
\boldsymbol{D}
.
However, the sought for sparsity is degraded with this methodology.
In
[
14
]
, a relaxation regime of
\ell_{1}
with the smooth
\ell_{2}
norm is adopted, with a relaxation parameter easy to assign.
Recently in
[
10
]
, a formula of
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})
is obtained under some conditions. This formula includes inverting a large matrix, which is computed iteratively while Automatic Differentiation (AD) is used to evaluate gradients.
In
[
16
]
, and without a proof of convergence, they restrict
\boldsymbol{D}
to have unit columns norm, that also satisfy
\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}=\boldsymbol{I}
, in order to avoid trivial solutions (
e.g.,
repeated columns).
The work in
[
4
]
solves the 2D piecewise signals reconstruction problem using AD, constrained by learning
convolution-type
dictionaries of kernels with small support, which improves the quality of standard 2D TV. Such strong constraints are not considered in our work, however we will see that simple column-centering suffices to learn a high-quality dictionary in the 1D version of this problem.

II
Automatic Differentiation (AD)

To compute gradients, one often manually writes down its analytical expression.
Yet, this can only be performed for functions with closed-form expression, which is not the case in
Eq.

1b
.
An alternative is symbolic differentiation, automatically performed by computer tools like Mathematica
[
7
]
when dealing with syntax tree expressions.
A third approach consists in approximating gradients using numerical differentiation. It is easy to implement though exposed to round-off errors
[
8
]
and expensive in case of a high number of variables (here
p\times m
).

Automatic Differentiation (AD), that is of interest in this work, mitigates the previous drawbacks.
AD manipulates computation flow in a computer program, with all numerical computations reduced to compositions of elementary operations, for which derivative rules are known
[
15
]
.
In such computer programs, a value must be assigned to each input variable, thus, any operation in the code will result in a variable with a numerical value.
During the execution of the program, AD consists in
[
1
]
: 1) keeping a trace to all intermediate variables that are dependent on the ones we want to differentiate for; 2) once an operation is performed on a dependent variable, say
v_{i}
, to evaluate another
v_{j}
, directly computing the derivative value
dv_{j}/dv_{i}
; 3) accumulating derivatives in step 2 through the chain rule.
This gives the derivative value of the whole composition
w.r.t.
a chosen variable.
AD can trace numerical computations in recursion functions and in control-flow (
if, while, for
) statements. Thus, AD efficiently differentiates not only closed-form formulas, but also implemented algorithms.
This makes AD suitable for gradient-based optimization.
AD can be implemented in two ways:
forward
mode and
reverse
mode.
In our work, we adopt the reverse mode
1
1

1

this is the mode implemented in the PyTorch framework
.
Let us assume having
y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{m})
as a function of the variable
x=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
, with
v
as an intermediate variable.
The backward scheme takes in input the vector
\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}=(\bar{y}_{1},\dots,\bar{y}_{m})
, and accumulates gradients as follows: first
\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}=\boldsymbol{J}_{y}^{\top}(v)\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}
, then
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}=\boldsymbol{J}_{v}^{\top}(x)\bar{\boldsymbol{v}}
, where
\left(\boldsymbol{J}_{y}(v)\right)_{i,j}=dy_{i}/dv_{j}
. The output
\bar{\boldsymbol{x}}
is nothing but the transpose Jacobian-vector product
\boldsymbol{J}^{\top}_{y}(x)\bar{\boldsymbol{y}}
.

III
Proposed algorithm

We solve the
dual
problem of
Eq.

1b
with Forward-Backward splitting (FB).
Using Automatic Differentiation, we obtain gradients of the previous FB algorithm
w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{D}
.
These gradients are used to learn a local minimum
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
using gradient descent, while projecting
\boldsymbol{D}
on the admissible set
\mathcal{C}
at every iteration.

III-A

Deriving the dual problem of
Eq.

1b

The term
\|\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}
in
Eq.

1b
is neither differentiable
w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{w}
, nor has a simple proximal operator that can be efficiently performed.
Hence, we cannot apply the gradient descent or the FB algorithm directly to evaluate
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})
.
However, the latter is possible if we tackle this optimization from the dual perspective
[
13
]
.
In fact, one can prove that
Eq.

1b
is equivalent to its dual problem
[
5
]
:

\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})=\mathop{\mathrm{arg\,min}}_{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}~\frac{1}{2}\big\|\boldsymbol{Dz}-\boldsymbol{y}\big\|_{2}^{2}~+~\iota_{B_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{z})\kern 5.0pt,

(2)

where
B_{\infty}=\{\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{m};~|z_{i}|\leq 1,\ \forall i\in[m]\}
is the unit ball of the
\ell_{\infty}
norm.
The target recovery
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}
is then given by:

\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})=\boldsymbol{y}-\boldsymbol{D}~\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y}).

(3)

III-B

Solving the dual problem with the FB algorithm

Fortunately, the term
\iota_{B_{\infty}}(\boldsymbol{z})
has a simple proximal function given by
\Pi_{B_{\infty}}
: the orthogonal projection on the ball
B_{\infty}
. So indeed, we solve
Eq.

2
with an accelerated FB algorithm, namely the Fast Iterative Shrinkage-Thresholding Algorithm (FISTA)
[
2
]
.
At each iteration we update
\boldsymbol{z}
as follows:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{z}_{i+1}&=\prox_{\iota_{B_{\infty}}}\big(\boldsymbol{q}_{i}-\eta_{1}\nabla(\frac{1}{2}\big\|\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{q}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y}\big\|_{2}^{2})\big)\\
&=\Pi_{B_{\infty}}\big(\boldsymbol{q}_{i}-\eta_{1}\boldsymbol{D}^{\top}(\boldsymbol{D}\boldsymbol{q}_{i}-\boldsymbol{y})\big)\end{split}

(4)

s.t.
\boldsymbol{q}_{1}=\boldsymbol{z}_{0}
is the initialization of
\boldsymbol{z}
,
\eta_{1}
is the step size, and:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\boldsymbol{q}_{i+1}&=\boldsymbol{z}_{i}+\frac{t_{i}-1}{t_{i+1}}(\boldsymbol{z}_{i}-\boldsymbol{z}_{i-1})\\
t_{i+1}&=\frac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{1+4t_{i}^{2}})\kern 5.0pt;t_{1}=1.\end{split}

(5)

To conclude with the inner part: having the matrix
\boldsymbol{D}
and the vector
\boldsymbol{y}
as
input
, one can compute
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})
with a sufficient number of updates as in
Eq.

4
, then get
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})

in output
using
Eq.

3
.

III-C

Outer loop design to learn the dictionary

Let us first notice that when the admissible set
\mathcal{C}=\mathbb{R}^{p\times m}
, the solution
\hat{\boldsymbol{D}}
is
not unique
as
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is invariant to the coefficients order in a vector,
e.g.,
\|(u_{1},u_{2})^{\top}\|_{1}=\|(u_{2},u_{1})^{\top}\|_{1}
. Thus, permuting columns in
\boldsymbol{D}
will lead to the same
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})
in
Eq.

1b
, which means the same cost
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{D})
. In this paper, we consider any solution that is a minimizer of
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{D})
. We also assume that the second dimension of
\boldsymbol{D}
is given, while optimizing for it might be the subject of a future work.

Towards our goal, we use AD to get the gradient of the mean squared error (MSE) term in
\mathcal{E}(\boldsymbol{D})
, by tracing the FB algorithm that solves
Eq.

1b
. In fact, to reduce computational cost, we do not compute the full MSE but sample a batch of training signals at each iteration (see Alg.
1
). We denote this term by
\mathcal{E}_{\rm MSE}(\boldsymbol{D})
. Since proving the convergence of AD’s Jacobian to the variational one is complicated in such non-smooth setting, we replace the true reconstruction
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D},\boldsymbol{y})
with the output produced by the FB algorithm, and empirically show that it is a good proxy. For simplicity, we keep the same notation for this output.
We randomly initialize
\boldsymbol{D}_{0}
, then we start each iteration
t
by setting
PyTorch AD framework
to record operations on
\boldsymbol{D}_{t}
.
We can then compute the output
\hat{\boldsymbol{w}}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t},\boldsymbol{y})
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\rm MSE}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t})
.
Now, we use the PyTorch AD to get the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\rm MSE}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t})
, and we update the estimated dictionary as

{\boldsymbol{D}}_{t+1}=\boldsymbol{D}_{t}-\eta_{2}\nabla\mathcal{E}_{\rm MSE}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t})\kern 5.0pt,

(6)

where
\eta_{2}>0
is a step size. Lastly, we project
\boldsymbol{D}_{t}
on the admissible set
\mathcal{C}
by computing
\Pi_{\mathcal{C}}(\boldsymbol{D}_{t})
.

V
Conclusion and future work

To alleviate the difficulty of deriving gradients to learn analysis-sparsity dictionaries, that are optimized through a bilevel problem, we proposed to make use of automatic differentiation, a technique shown to have high proficiency in machine learning and deep learning.
In the absence of a theoretical link between the automatic differentiation and the analytical one in our setting, our experiments on the piecewise constant signals reconstruction problem showed a proof-of-concept, and a promising methodology that can be applied in other problems setting.
For the same case study, we also incorporated a simple column-wise centering projection, which significantly increased the stability of the algorithm and the quality of the learned dictionary.

Further investigations can be done to generalize our algorithm to 2D signals, particularly the 2D piecewise constant signals reconstruction, while trying to combine other constraints. Moreover, it would be interesting to study the problem again when the dimensions of the dictionary are not given, for instance to learn graph incidence matrices.
