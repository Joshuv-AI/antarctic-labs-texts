A Quantum Unique Games Conjecture
Hamoon Mousavi, Taro Spirig

Abstract
After the NP-hardness of computational problems such as 3 3 SAT and MaxCut was established, a natural next step was to explore whether these problems remain hard to approximate. While the quantum extensions of some of these problems are known to be hard—indeed undecidable—their inapproximability remains largely unresolved. In this work, we introduce definitions for the quantum extensions of Label-Cover and Unique-Label-Cover. We show that these problems play a similarly crucial role in studying the inapproximability of quantum constraint satisfaction problems as they do in the classical setting.

The Label-Cover problem

, it is NP-hard to approximate Label-Cover within any additive constant. This result, combined with gap-preserving reductions from Label-Cover, has led to optimal inapproximability results for several other key problems, including

An instance of Label-Cover consists of a bipartite graph with left vertex set
U
and right vertex set
V
, a large label set (also called an alphabet)
\mathcal{L}
, and maps
\pi_{u,v}:\mathcal{L}\to\mathcal{L}
associated with each edge
(u,v)
. These maps are usually called projections. A
labelling

a:U\cup V\to\mathcal{L}
is said to satisfy an edge
(u,v)
if it respects the associated projection map, meaning if
\pi_{u,v}(a(u))=a(v)
. The
value of the instance
is the maximum fraction of satisfied edges over all possible labelings.

In quantum information theory, the quantum extension of Label-Cover, known as
entangled projection games

[
13
]
, is a special case of
nonlocal games

[
14
,
15
]
.
1
1

1

We primarily adopt the CSP terminology, while referencing certain naming conventions from the nonlocal games literature for the convenience of readers familiar with that field. See Section
1.2
for a discussion of our terminology.
The primary distinction lies in the definition of labeling. A
quantum labeling
generalizes probabilistic labeling. In classical probabilistic labeling, each vertex is assigned a probability distribution over labels. In quantum labeling, each edge is assigned a distribution over pairs of labels—one for each vertex incident on the edge.

More formally, a quantum labeling assigns quantum measurements to each vertex, with the measurement outcomes corresponding to labels. When both vertices of an edge are measured, the resulting probability distribution determines the likelihood that the edge is satisfied. The
quantum value
of the instance is the maximum expected fraction of satisfied edges over all possible quantum labelings. A formal definition is given in Section
3.1
.

In the quantum setting, the role of the PCP theorem is played by the
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}=\mathrm{RE}
theorem of Ji et al.
[
16
]
: it is
\mathrm{RE}
-hard to approximate the quantum value of Label-Cover within any additive constant.
2
2

2

Recall that
\mathrm{RE}
is the class of recursively enumerable languages. Stating that a problem is
\mathrm{RE}
-hard is another way of saying that it is undecidable.
Now, as with the classical case, one might expect to derive optimal inapproximability results for other quantum constraint satisfaction problems. O’Donnell and Yuen
[
17
]
, for instance, extended Håstad’s results to the optimal inapproximability of quantum
3
XOR. So far, the classical and quantum theories of inapproximability have followed parallel trajectories.

In the classical development, after the initial success of Håstad, researchers discovered some of the limitations of Label-Cover. To address these, Khot
[
18
]
introduced a modification to the Label-Cover problem, imposing a uniqueness condition on the projection maps. This modified problem is known as the Unique-Label-Cover and it remains an open question whether it is hard to approximate. However, assuming that Unique-Label-Cover is hard to approximate, researchers showed optimal inapproximability results for a variety of other problems, including MaxCut
[
19
]
. This assumption is known as the Unique Games Conjecture (UGC)
[
18
]
.

A striking divergence arises between the classical and quantum theories of inapproximability with the introduction of Unique-Label-Cover. Kempe et al.
[
20
]
showed that the quantum value of Unique-Label-Cover is efficiently approximable. This implies that a
direct
quantum analogue of the UGC does not hold. As a result, none of the classical consequences of the UGC can be straightforwardly extended to the quantum domain. This presents a significant challenge to the development of a comprehensive quantum theory for hardness of approximation.

For instance, understanding the complexity of the anti-ferromagnetic Heisenberg model—a quantum generalization of MaxCut and a problem of great significance in physics and quantum information—remains an open question. While this problem is known to be
\mathrm{QMA}
-hard, the complexity of approximating it is still unresolved.
3
3

3

Recall that
\mathrm{QMA}
is the quantum analogue of
\mathrm{NP}
.
Hwang et al.
[
21
]
made significant progress on this question, but their results rely on the classical UGC, which limits them to showing
\mathrm{NP}
-hardness rather than
\mathrm{QMA}
-hardness.

Finding the right candidate for a quantum extension of UGC is key for advancing the theory of inapproximability in the quantum setting. This work proposes such a candidate.

We begin by noting that Kempe et al.’s algorithm for approximating the quantum value of Unique-Label-Cover applies only to some of many possible quantum values that are defined in the literature on nonlocal games. Examples of quantum values include, but are not limited to, the tensor-product, commuting-operator, synchronous, and oracularizable-synchronous values. These arise from different models for quantum entanglement. Label-Cover remains hard across all these models
[
22
,
23
,
16
]
, whereas Unique-Label-Cover is efficiently approximable in only some of them
[
20
]
.

We choose a model where Unique-Label-Cover might still be hard to approximate. This choice forms the basis of our
quantum Unique Games Conjecture
(qUGC). We give an informal overview in Section
1.3
. Formal definitions can be found in Section
3.2
.

We then show how classical reductions from Unique-Label-Cover can be “quantized” to prove inapproximability results for quantum CSPs. We illustrate this approach with
2
-Lin
[

]
and MaxCut
[
19
]
, and we believe the same tools can be applied to Raghavendra’s seminal work on CSPs

We conclude this section with a few remarks.

Relationship between UGC and qUGC.
The cases of

Alternative Approaches to UGC.
Some of the variants of quantum UGC proposed in this paper may be easier to prove hardness for than the classical UGC itself. Proving hardness for any of these variants could strengthen confidence in the UGC. Conversely, some of our UGC variants may be easier to disprove, and doing so could offer insights into disproving UGC. Figure

Universality.
Label-Cover is exhibiting a remarkable

From another perspective, however, the constraints encountered in the search for a quantum analogue of the UGC could serve as a guiding principle in identifying the most appropriate model for quantum value from a computational standpoint.

This paper builds on our previous work on noncommutative CSPs
[
25

We are also equally interested in whether a broader perspective on CSPs might uncover unifying principles that could provide new insights into classical CSPs. Our remarks on alternative approaches to UGC and universality are examples of the insights we are pursuing.

Noncommutative Value.
A

assigns an observable to each vertex, i.e.

\displaystyle\text{ maximize:}

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{1-\operatorname{tr}(X_{i}X_{j})}{2}

(1.2)

\displaystyle\text{subject to:}

\displaystyle X_{i}\in\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H}),

where

\omega_{\mathrm{nc}}(G)\geq\omega_{c}(G)

The noncommutative value has been the focus of our previous work on CSPs
[

Quantum Value.
A
quantum partition
lies somewhere in between a classical and noncommutative partition: A noncommutative partition
X
is
quantum

X_{i}
and
X_{j}

(i,j)
is an edge. In quantum mechanics, commuting observables can be simultaneously measured, emphasizing the physical motivation for this additional commutation relation.

The
quantum value
, denoted
\omega_{q}(G)
, is therefore obtained from (
1.2
) by imposing the extra commutation relations:

\displaystyle\text{ maximize:}

\displaystyle\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\frac{1-\operatorname{tr}(X_{i}X_{j})}{2}

(1.3)

\displaystyle\text{subject to:}

\displaystyle X_{i}\in\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H}),

\displaystyle X_{i}X_{j}=X_{j}X_{i}\text{ for all }(i,j)\in E.

Since every classical assignment is a quantum assignment and every quantum assignment is a noncommutative assignment, we obtain the chain of inequalities
\omega_{\mathrm{nc}}(G)\geq\omega_{q}(G)\geq\omega_{c}(G)
.

Using the same approach outlined here, we can define the classical and quantum values for all CSPs (refer to Section

Remark.
It is important to note that the commutativity requirement in a quantum assignment does not imply that all observables in the assignment commute with each other, nor does it mean that all observables can be diagonalized in the same basis. Commutativity is not a transitive property: every operator commutes with the identity operator, but not every pair of operators commute with each other. For example, in MaxCut, if two vertices are at a distance of two or more, their observables in a quantum assignment need not commute.

1.3
Quantum Unique Games Conjecture

Before moving on to our conjecture, we first briefly review decision problems and reductions between them. Readers familiar with the terminology can skip the next section.

1.3.1
Reductions

In the context of hardness of approximation, it is often easier to work with a version of a computational problem known as the
decision problem with a promise
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be any computational problem for which
\omega_{c},\omega_{q},\omega_{\mathrm{nc}}
are defined. For example
\mathcal{P}
could be MaxCut, Label-Cover, or Unique-Label-Cover. For every
s,t\in\{c,q,\mathrm{nc}\}
and
\zeta,\delta>0
, the
decision problem

\mathcal{P}_{s,t}(1-\zeta,\delta)
is defined as follows: given an instance
\phi\in\mathcal{P}
that is promised to be either

•

\omega_{s}(\phi)\geq 1-\zeta
, called a “yes” instance, or

•

\omega_{t}(\phi)<\delta
, called a “no” instance,

decide whether the instance is a “yes” or “no” instance. When
s=t
, we simply write
\mathcal{P}_{s}(1-\zeta,\delta)
to denote
\mathcal{P}_{s,s}(1-\zeta,\delta)
.

A
reduction
from
\mathcal{P}_{s,t}(\gamma,\delta)
to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{s^{\prime},t^{\prime}}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
is any efficient map
\mathcal{P}\to\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
that is

•

complete: it maps “yes” instances to “yes” instances, and

•

sound: it maps “no” instances to “no” instances.

As a simple example, for every choice of
\zeta,\delta>0
, the decision problem
\mathcal{P}_{q,\mathrm{nc}}(1-\zeta,\delta)
reduces to
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{nc},\mathrm{nc}}(1-\zeta,\delta)
via the identity map. Soundness is trivial, and completeness holds because
\omega_{\mathrm{nc}}(\phi)\geq\omega_{q}(\phi)
for all instances
\phi
. This is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{q,\mathrm{nc}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{nc},\mathrm{nc}}
(we often drop the completeness and soundness parameters in our informal discussions in this introduction). There are several other trivial reductions between decision versions of
\mathcal{P}
, and they are illustrated in Figure

1.5
Proof Ideas

In the conventional
\mathrm{NP}
proof system for Label-Cover, the certificate is simply the list of labels for the vertices. This is what we called
classical assignment
in Section
1.2
. The verifier’s task in this proof system is to check that the assigned labels satisfy the edge constraints.

Håstad’s gap-preserving reduction from Label-Cover (used, for instance, in proving the hardness of
3
SAT and
3
XOR) is best viewed as the construction of a more efficient version of this
\mathrm{NP}
proof system, known as a probabilistically checkable proof (PCP). The PCP certificate for Label-Cover is an encoding of the classical assignment in binary using an error-correcting code, commonly referred to as the Long-Code, pioneered in
[
43
]
. In addition to verifying that the labels in the encoding satisfy the edge constraints, the PCP verifier must also check that the proof consists of valid codewords. The soundness of this PCP relies on Fourier analysis. This was one of the key insights of Håstad’s celebrated work.

In our case, we are reducing Unique-Label-Cover to
2
-Lin (Section
4
) and MaxCut (Section
5
), but the core principles remain the same. Our reductions are based on the classical reductions by Khot
[
18
]
and KKMO
[
19
]
, respectively. There are, however, several key differences, which we discuss in the rest of this section. For concreteness, we focus on the reduction from Unique-Label-Cover to MaxCut.

Completeness.
This step requires a generalization of the Long-Code to encode quantum assignments. This is a straightforward generalization of the Long-Code to the operator setting and it is due to O’Donnell and Yuen
[
17
]
. The main challenge in proving completeness is ensuring that the encoded quantum assignment satisfies the commutation requirements of a quantum MaxCut assignment.

Soundness.
The proof of soundness, as is customary, is done contrapositively. We begin with a quantum assignment for the MaxCut instance that achieves a large value, and from this, we construct a quantum assignment that has a sufficiently large value in the Unique-Label-Cover instance.

The classical proof constructs a probabilistic labeling based on the Fourier coefficients of the MaxCut assignment. Given a MaxCut assignment
f
, the labeling samples a subset of labels
S
with probability proportional to the squared Fourier coefficients,
\widehat{f}(S)^{2}
. It then probabilistically selects a label
a\in S
for each vertex. In the quantum setting, these squared Fourier weights are positive semidefinite operators. Defining
P^{a}\coloneqq\sum_{S:a\in S}\frac{1}{|S|}\widehat{f}(S)^{2}
for each label
a
results in a
POVM measurement
for each vertex in the Unique-Label-Cover instance (see Section
2.2
for the definition of POVMs). This is also due to
[
17
]
.

These measurements satisfy the commutation relations required for a quantum assignment. However, they are not projective measurements, as required by definition of quantum assignment. Naimark’s dilation theorem (see, for example, Watrous
[
44
]
) is typically used in such cases to convert POVMs into projective measurements. However, due to the commutation requirements (and the use of tracial states), Naimark’s theorem is not applicable. Therefore, we need to projectivize these POVMs while preserving the commutation relations, which we accomplish using the projectivization lemma proved in Section
6
.

Classically, the proof of soundness relies on a deep result from Boolean Fourier analysis, known as the Majority-Is-Stablest
[
45
]
, which relates Boolean functions to their Fourier weights. If the operators in our assignments were simultaneously diagonalizable, we could apply this theorem directly, as in the classical proof. However, the inherent noncommutativity of quantum assignments prevents us from doing so, forcing us to take a new approach.

To explain the problem and our resolution at a high level, recall that our goal is to construct a quantum labeling for an instance of Unique-Label-Cover. Let
u
be a vertex from the left-vertex set, and let
N_{u}
denote its neighbors in the right-vertex set. In the operator setting, while each neighbor’s operator individually commutes with
u
’s, the operators assigned to the neighbors may not commute with each other. This noncommutativity is a fundamental obstacle.

We first encountered this issue earlier, when attempting to view quantum assignments as extensions of probabilistic assignments. In a classical probabilistic assignment, each vertex
u
is assigned a single probability measure
P_{u}
over the set of labels. In contrast, a quantum assignment associates two probability measures,
P_{u,e}
and
P_{v,e}
, with each edge
e=(u,v)
. The crux of the issue is that different edges incident on
u
—say,
e
and
e^{\prime}
—can impose vastly different probability measures on
u
, which may conflict with each other.

This same phenomenon arises in the proof of soundness, except it manifests not in the probability measures, but in the Fourier coefficients of the quantum assignment. Classical soundness proofs organize around sets of neighbors, but this approach is too coarse to be applicable to the operator setting. The key to resolving this issue is to structure the proof around individual edges; this allows us to manage the noncommutative nature of the operator assignments without the sort of conflicts we mentioned earlier.

1.6
Future Directions

There are many open problems concerning the quantum value of CSPs and the conjectures we have proposed regarding Unique-Label-Cover:

1.

Quantizing Classical Reductions:
The works of O’Donnell and Yuen
[
17
]
, as well as this paper, establish straightforward translations of some of the most well-known classical inapproximability results to the quantum setting. Could this indicate a more general phenomenon? More precisely, can any classical gap-preserving reduction from Label-Cover (or its variants like Unique-Label-Cover or Smooth-Label-Cover) be extended to a reduction for the quantum value?

2.

UGC versus qUGC:
What is the relationship between the classical UGC and the quantum UGC? If UGC is proven via a reduction from Label-Cover, can this reduction be “quantized” to prove qUGC? A positive answer to the previous question would also resolve this one affirmatively.

3.

Variants of qUGC:
In the classical setting, several formulations of UGC have been shown to be equivalent (see Section 3 in
[
46
]
and references therein). Could similar equivalences hold for the quantum variants discussed in this paper? See Section
3.2
for further discussion of these qUGC variants.

4.

Noncommutative CSPs:
This work focuses primarily on the quantum value of CSPs. Could variants of qUGC help resolve hardness of approximation questions for the noncommutative value? The noncommutative value, which also plays an important role in quantum information, exhibits some intriguing behavior. As shown in Figure
7
, unlike the classical or quantum value, the noncommutative value of MaxCut can be computed in polynomial time. However, it is known that the noncommutative value of Max-
3
-Cut is undecidable
[
47
,
16
]
.
[
25
]
provides a
0.864
-approximation algorithm for the noncommutative value of Max-
3
-Cut. We suspect that a variant of qUGC could prove useful in establishing the optimality of this algorithm.

5.

Weaker Version of
\mathbf{MIP^{*}=RE}
:
Is there a direct way to prove the hardness of approximating the quantum value of Label-Cover without relying on
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}=\mathrm{RE}
? One potential approach is to quantize Dinur’s proof of the classical PCP theorem
[
8
,
9
,
10
,
48
]
. The commutation relations in
\mathrm{LC}_{q}
could make this quantization feasible. Could this weaker result also have some of the implications of
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}=\mathrm{RE}
such as the resolution of the Connes Embedding Problem?

6.

Integrality Gap Instances for MaxCut:
What is the smallest ratio between the classical and quantum values across all MaxCut instances? Assuming quantum UGC, we show this ratio is the Goemans-Williamson constant
\alpha_{\mathrm{gw}}\approx 0.878
(Theorem
35
). Can we construct an instance attaining this ratio?

7.

Label-Cover for QMA:
Approximating the classical value of Label-Cover is
\mathrm{NP}
-complete (PCP theorem). Similarly, approximating the noncommutative and quantum values of Label-Cover is
\mathrm{RE}
-complete (
\mathrm{MIP}^{*}=\mathrm{RE}
theorem). What version of Label-Cover or Unique-Label-Cover naturally captures
\mathrm{QMA}
? Could further constraints on the variations of Label-Cover and Unique-Label-Cover explored in this paper lead to suitable candidates? A positive resolution to this problem would answer the Quantum Games PCP Conjecture
[
49
]
.

8.

Hardness of Approximation in Hamiltonian Complexity:
In quantum information theory, quantum MaxCut often refers to the anti-ferromagnetic Heisenberg model, a special case of the local Hamiltonian problem. Although
[
21
]
attempts to prove UGC hardness for the anti-ferromagnetic Heisenberg model, reductions from UGC only establish
\mathrm{NP}
-hardness. Could variations of qUGC yield stronger results?

While the inapproximability of the anti-ferromagnetic Heisenberg model is one of our motivations for proposing the quantum UGC, our current methods do not yet apply to the inapproximability of local Hamiltonian problems.

9.

Gadget Reductions:
In classical complexity theory, gadgets
[
50
,
12
]
are widely used to demonstrate hardness of approximation. Can this method be “quantized”?
[
47
,
51
]
are examples of gadget reductions in the quantum setting.

2.1
Notations

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we let
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
. When
x
is a function from
[n]
, we may treat it as a vector and write
x_{i}\coloneqq x(i)
. For
\pi\in S_{n}
, a permutation, we let
x\circ\pi
be the vector for which
(x\circ\pi)_{i}=x_{\pi(i)}
, for all
i
.

Hilbert spaces in this work are always assumed to be finite dimensional vector spaces
\mathbb{C}^{d}
for some
d\in\mathbb{N}
. We denote the set of operators on the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
. The unitary group on
\mathcal{H}
is denoted by
\mathcal{U}(\mathcal{H})
. Given a Hilbert space, we use
\operatorname{tr}
to denote its dimension-normalized trace. Given two operators
A
and
B
, their commutator is denoted by
[A,B]\coloneqq AB-BA
.

2.2
Measurements

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
, an
m
-outcome POVM
P
acting on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is a set of positive semidefinite (PSD) operators
\{P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}\}
in
\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
that sum to the identity operator. POVM stands for
positive operator-valued measure
, which captures the most general notion of measurement in quantum mechanics. We need two definitions concerning POVMs.

Definition 1

(Simultaneous measurability)
.

A set of POVMs
\{P_{i}^{a}\}_{a\in[m]}
, for
i\in[n]
, is said to be simultaneously measurable, or commuting for short, if
[P_{i}^{a},P_{j}^{b}]=P_{i}^{a}P_{j}^{b}-P_{j}^{b}P_{i}^{a}=0
for all
a,b\in[m]
and
i\neq j
.

Definition 2

(Self-commuting measurements)
.

A POVM is said to be self-commuting if all of its operators commute.

Throughout this paper, all POVMs are self-commuting. We denote the set of
m
-outcome self-commuting POVMs on
\mathcal{H}
by
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
. Operators of simultaneously measurable self-commuting POVMs can all be diagonalized in the same basis.

PVMs, short for projective-valued measures, are a special case of POVMs, and indeed a special case of self-commuting POVMs. A POVM
\Pi=\{\Pi^{1},\ldots,\Pi^{m}\}
is said to be a PVM if each
\Pi^{a}
is a projection operator, i.e.
(\Pi^{a})^{*}=\Pi^{a}
and
(\Pi^{a})^{2}=\Pi^{a}
. We let
\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
denote the set of
m
-outcome PVMs on
\mathcal{H}
. Almost all POVMs appearing in this paper are PVMs with the exception of a few places where self-commuting POVMs appear.

A unitary
X
that is also Hermitian is called an
observable
. The set of all observables is denoted by
\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
. Observables and binary-outcome PVMs are in one-to-one correspondence: for every observable
X
, operators
\Pi^{+1}\coloneqq(I+X)/2
and
\Pi^{-1}\coloneqq(I-X)/2
form a PVM. A set of binary-outcome PVMs are simultaneously measurable if and only if their corresponding observables commute.

Indexing measurement operators.
As suggested, the binary set
\{-1,+1\}
(the set of eigenvalues of observables) is used to index operators in a binary-outcome PVM. When
m\geq 3
, the set
[m]
is used to index operators in an
m
-outcome PVM. This choice helps to make our equations easier to parse. See also Section
3.3
.

2.3
Computational Problems

For us, a
computational problem
is a pair
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
where
\mathcal{P}
is the set of instances and
\omega:\mathcal{P}\to[0,1]
is the function to be computed. We often write
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}
for
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
. In this paper we call
\omega
the
value
(function) and when it is clear from the context we write
\mathcal{P}
to refer to the computational problem itself.

We now define the
decision version
of a computational problem.

Definition 3
.

For every
0<\delta<\gamma\leq 1
, the decision problem of
(\mathcal{P},\omega)
, denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(\gamma,\delta)
, is the problem: given an instance
\phi\in\mathcal{P}
with the promise that either

•

\omega(\phi)\geq\gamma
, called a “yes” instance, or

•

\omega(\phi)<\delta
, called a “no” instance,

decide whether the instance is a yes or no instance. We refer to
\gamma
as the completeness parameter and
\delta
as the soundness parameter.

If distinct values
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
are used for yes and no instances, that is if

•

\omega_{1}(\phi)\geq\gamma
, is a yes instance, and

•

\omega_{2}(\phi)<\delta
, is a no instance,

then the corresponding decision problem is denoted
\mathcal{P}_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}(\gamma,\delta)
. Thus
\mathcal{P}_{\omega}(\gamma,\delta)
is
\mathcal{P}_{\omega,\omega}(\gamma,\delta)
.

Definition 4
.

A reduction from
\mathcal{P}_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}(\gamma,\delta)
to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}_{1},\omega^{\prime}_{2}}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
is a deterministic polynomial time map
r
that sends every instance in
\mathcal{P}
to an instance in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
. Furthermore
r
needs to be complete and sound. Completeness is the property that a yes instance is sent to a yes instance. The soundness is the property that a no instance is sent to a no instance. When a reduction exists we may write
\mathcal{P}_{\omega_{1},\omega_{2}}(\gamma,\delta)\longrightarrow\mathcal{P}^{\prime}_{\omega^{\prime}_{1},\omega^{\prime}_{2}}(\gamma^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
.

2.4
Fourier Analysis

We quickly review the basic definitions and notations from Fourier analysis on hypercube. For a proper introduction, refer to the textbook
[
52
]
. Let
g:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a function and let
\{\pm 1\}^{m}
be equipped with the uniform probability measure. For every
S\subseteq[m]
, the
characteristic function

\chi_{S}:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\rightarrow\{\pm 1\}
is given by
\chi_{S}(x)=\prod_{a\in S}x_{a}
. This set forms an orthonormal basis, that is for every
S,T\subseteq[m]

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\chi_{S}(x)\chi_{T}(x)\right]=\delta_{S,T},

where
\delta_{S,T}
is the Kronecker delta. For all
S\subseteq[m]
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, the Fourier coefficient of
g
at
S
is

\displaystyle\widehat{g}(S)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\chi_{S}(x)g(x)\right].

The Fourier inversion formula is

\displaystyle g(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}\chi_{S}(x)\widehat{g}(S).

Definition 5
.

For
a\in[m]
, the influence of the
a
’th index on
g
, denoted
\mathrm{Inf}_{a}(g)
, is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Inf}_{a}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathrm{Var}_{x_{a}}\left[g(x)\right]\right]=\sum_{S:a\in S}\widehat{g}(S)^{2}.

The
k
-degree influence of the index
a
on
g
is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Inf}_{a}^{\leq k}(g)=\sum_{S:a\in S,\lvert S\rvert\leq k}\widehat{g}(S)^{2}.

Definition 6
.

Let
-1<\rho\leq 0
. The noise stability of
g
at
\rho
is

S_{\rho}(g)=\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\left[g(x)g(x\mu)\right],

where
x
is sampled from the uniform distribution, and
\mu
is sampled independently such that
\mu_{a}=1
with probability
\frac{1+\rho}{2}
and
\mu_{a}=-1
with probability
\frac{1-\rho}{2}
.

We can now state two Fourier analytic theorems used in our proofs.

Theorem 7

(Bourgain’s Junta Theorem
[
53
]
)
.

Let
g:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to\{\pm 1\}
be any Boolean function and
k>0
an integer. Then for every
\frac{1}{2}<t<1
, there exists a constant
c_{t}>0
such that

\displaystyle\textit{if}\quad\sum_{S:\lvert S\rvert>k}\widehat{g}(S)^{2}<c_{t}k^{-t}\quad\textit{then}\quad\sum_{S:\lvert\widehat{g}(S)\rvert\leq\frac{1}{10}4^{-k^{2}}}\widehat{g}(S)^{2}<\frac{1}{100}.

Theorem 8

(Majority is Stablest (MIS)
[
45
]
)
.

For every
\rho\in(-1,0]
and
\varepsilon>0
, there is a small enough
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon,\rho)>0
and a large enough
k=k(\varepsilon,\rho)
such that if
g:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\rightarrow[-1,1]
is any function with
\mathrm{Inf}_{a}^{\leq k}(g)\leq\delta,

for all
a\in[m]
, then

S_{\rho}(g)\geq 1-\frac{2}{\pi}\arccos{\rho}-\varepsilon.

Even though the Fourier analysis of functions on the Boolean hypercube does not depend on the range, we frequently encounter functions
\alpha:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
so it is useful to address a few minor issues that arise. Let
\alpha:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
where
\mathcal{H}
is a finite-dimensional Hilbert space. For all
S\subseteq[m]
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, the Fourier transform and its inverse are defined exactly as before

\displaystyle\widehat{\alpha}(S)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\chi_{S}(x)\alpha(x)\right]\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(x)=\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}\chi_{S}(x)\widehat{\alpha}(S).

Moreover, the Parseval identity also holds as in the scalar case

\sum_{S\subseteq[m]}\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2}=I.

It is clear that since
\alpha(x)
are observables, the operators
\widehat{\alpha}(S)
are Hermitian, and thus
\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2}
are PSD. Consequently, the set of squared Fourier coefficients
\{\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2}\}_{S\subseteq[m]}
defines a POVM.

We say
\alpha:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\cal{H})
is odd if for every
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
,
\alpha(x)=-\alpha(-x)
. A simple calculation shows that
\widehat{\alpha}(\emptyset)=0
whenever
\alpha
is odd.

Lemma 9
.

Let
\alpha:\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
be a function defined on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Consider the operators

\displaystyle P^{a}=\sum_{S:a\in S}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert}\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2},

for all
a\in[m]
. Then the set
P=\{P^{a}\}_{a\in[m]}\cup\{Q\}
where
Q=I-\sum_{a}P^{a}
is a POVM. If in addition
\alpha
is odd, then
Q=0
. If observables
\alpha(x)
commute for all
x
, then
P
is a self-commuting POVM.

Proof.

By Parseval, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{a}P^{a}=\sum_{a}\sum_{S:a\in S}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert}\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2}=\sum_{S:S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2}\leq I,

Therefore
Q=I-\sum_{a}P^{a}
is a PSD matrix, and we have a POVM. When
\alpha
is odd, we have
\widehat{\alpha}(\emptyset)=0
and therefore
Q=I-\sum_{S:S\neq\emptyset}\widehat{\alpha}(S)^{2}=0
by Parseval’s identity. Since Fourier coefficients are linear combinations of
\alpha(x)
’s, the self-commutation of the POVM follows trivially.
∎

3.1
CSPs, Assignments, and Values

All computational problems in this paper are examples of constraint satisfaction problems (CSPs). We refer to
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
as the
k
-ary CSP over an alphabet of size
m
. We now define an instance of
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
.

Definition 10
.

For
k,m\in\mathbb{N}
, an instance in
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
consists of

•

a pair
(V,E)
, where
V
is a set of variables and
E
is a multiset of
k

V
representing constraints,

•

a set of
predicates

f_{e}:[m]^{k}\to\{0,1\}
for every
e\in E
, and

•

a probability distribution
p
on
E
. We refer to
p_{e}\coloneqq p(e)
as the weight of the constraint
e
.

We denote this instance with the tuple
(V,E,f,p)
. The set
[m]
is referred to as the alphabet or the set of labels. We let
p_{e}=0
whenever
e\notin E
. We always let
n\coloneqq|V|
.

Remark.
Note that
E
is a multiset. This means that the same
k
-tuple of
V
, say
e
, may appear multiple times in
E
each time with a different predicate. When
k=2
, the pair
(V,E)
can be viewed as a directed graph (possibly with parallel edges since
E
is a multiset).

The arity
k
is always fixed, but in the context of Label-Cover, as we will soon see, it is customary to let the alphabet size
m
vary. We denote by
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}
the union of
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
for all
m
. We assume a natural representation of instances is fixed. The size of the instance is the length of such a representation, and it is clearly a
\mathrm{poly}(n,m)
.

We next define the
classical and quantum values
of instances in
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
.

Definition 11

(Classical assignment and classical value)
.

Given an instance
\phi
in
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
, a (classical) assignment is a function
a:V\to[m]
.

The value of this assignment on the constraint
e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
is
f_{e}(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{k}})
.

The value of this assignment on the instance, denoted
\omega(\phi,a)
, is the weighted sum

\sum_{e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in E}p_{e}f_{e}(a_{i_{1}},\ldots,a_{i_{k}}).

Finally, the (classical) value of the instance, denoted
\omega_{c}(\phi)
, is the maximum of
\omega(\phi,a)
over all classical assignments.

Definition 12

(Quantum assignment and quantum value)
.

A quantum assignment consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a function
\Pi:V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
such that for every constraint
e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in E
the PVMs
\Pi_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Pi_{i_{k}}
are simultaneously measurable.

The value of this assignment on the constraint
e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
is

\sum_{a\in[m]^{k}}f_{e}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots\Pi_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}\right).

The value of the assignment on the instance, denoted
\omega(\phi,\Pi)
, is the weighted sum

\sum_{e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in E}p_{e}\sum_{a\in[m]^{k}}f_{e}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots\Pi_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}\right).

Finally, the quantum value of the instance, denoted
\omega_{q}(\phi)
, is the supremum of
\omega(\phi,\Pi)
over all quantum assignments.

Note that the quantum value is well-defined since the traces
\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots\Pi_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}\right)
are real numbers for every constraint
e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
due to the commutation relations imposed in the definition of the quantum assignment. When
\mathcal{H}
is one-dimensional, a quantum assignment reduces to a classical assignment, implying the inequality
\omega_{q}(\phi)\geq\omega_{c}(\phi)
.

We now define the
noncommutative value
of an instance in
{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
. The key difference between noncommutative and quantum assignments is that the simultaneous measurability constraint is no longer required.

Definition 13

(Noncommutative assignment and noncommutative value)
.

Given an instance
\phi
in
{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
, a noncommutative assignment consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a function
\Pi:V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
.

The value of the assignment on the constraint
e=(i,j)
is

\sum_{a,b\in[m]}f_{e}(a,b)\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{i}^{a}\Pi_{j}^{b}\right)

The value of the assignment on the instance, denoted
\omega(\phi,\Pi)
, is the weighted sum

\sum_{e=(i,j)\in E}p_{e}\sum_{a,b\in[m]}f_{e}(a,b)\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{i}^{a}\Pi_{j}^{b}\right).

Finally, the noncommutative value of the instance
\omega_{\mathrm{nc}}(\phi)
is the supremum of
\omega(\phi,\Pi)
over all noncommutative assignments.

Remark.
While the classical and quantum values are defined for all instances, the noncommutative value is defined only for instances in
2
-CSPs. This is because, for
k\geq 3
, the traces
\operatorname{tr}\left(\Pi_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots\Pi_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}\right)
are not necessarily real numbers when
\Pi_{i_{1}},\ldots,\Pi_{i_{k}}
are not simultaneously measurable.

Every classical assignment is a quantum assignment and every quantum assignment is a noncommutative assignment. Consequently, we have the chain of inequalities
\omega_{\mathrm{nc}}(\phi)\geq\omega_{q}(\phi)\geq\omega_{c}(\phi)
.

We conclude this section by noting that infinitely many types of quantum assignments and values can be defined.

A Spectrum of Quantum Values.
So far, we have introduced three types of assignments for constraint satisfaction problems, based on the degree of noncommutativity allowed between measurement operators. At one extreme, classical assignments allow only fully commuting measurements, while at the other, noncommutative assignments impose no commutation relations. Quantum assignments lie in between these two extremes. More generally, we can conceive of a spectrum of quantum assignments, parameterized by the
degree of noncommutativity
allowed between measurement operators:

•

\omega_{\mathrm{sq}}

6

6

•

The

\mathbb{Z}_{2}

{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}\coloneq{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}(2)

{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}

Definition 24
.

An instance in
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
is a tuple
(V,E,r,p)
where

•

V
is some set and
E
is a multiset of
k
-tuples of

,

•

r_{e}\in\{0,1\}
is a parity bit for every
e\in E
,

•

and
p
is a probability distribution on
E
.

To view this as an instance of
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(2)
, as in Definition
10
, we define predicates
f_{e}:\{0,1\}^{k}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
for every edge
e\in E
such that for every
a\in\{0,1\}^{k}
,
f_{e}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})=1
if and only if
\sum_{i\in[k]}a_{i}=r_{e}
, where the arithmetic is over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

The special case
{3}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
is sometimes referred to as
3\mathrm{XOR}
. The set of all instances in
{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
where all the parity bits are

is known as MaxCut. Since the parity bits are fixed for MaxCut, we denote its instances by triples
(V,E,p)
.

Example
.

Consider the following weighted linear system over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}

\displaystyle x_{1}+x_{2}=0,

\displaystyle x_{1}+x_{2}=0,

\displaystyle x_{1}+x_{2}=1,

\displaystyle x_{3}+x_{4}=1,

with weights
1/2,1/4,1/8,1/8
. An instance
\psi=(V,E,r,p)
of

that represents this system is given by

, where

, with parity bits

and edge weights
p_{e_{1}}=1/2,p_{e_{2}}=1/4,p_{e_{3}}=1/8,p_{e_{4}}=1/8
. Note that there is some flexibility when converting a linear system to an instance of
{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
; for instance, one could let
e_{4}=(4,3)
instead of
e_{4}=(3,4)
.

For binary-alphabet CSPs, it is often more convenient to use the multiplicative group of
\{\pm 1\}
rather than
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
as the set of labels.

Example
.

The linear system from the previous example can also be written multiplicatively as

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{2}=1,

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{2}=1,

\displaystyle x_{1}x_{2}=-1,

From here on, we assume that

With this change, it becomes more natural to write assignments to
{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
instances in multiplicative form as well. The multiplicative analogue of binary-outcome PVMs are observables. Recall from section

Definition 25

(Quantum assignment and quantum value for
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}

.

A quantum assignment to a
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}

(V,E,r,p)
consists of a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
and a function
\alpha:V\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})

\alpha_{i_{1}},\ldots,\alpha_{i_{k}}

The value of the assignment
\alpha

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}r_{e}\operatorname{tr}(\alpha_{i_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{i_{k}}).

The value of the assignment
\alpha

\omega(\phi,\alpha)

\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in E}p_{e}\,r_{e}\operatorname{tr}(\alpha_{i_{1}}\cdots\alpha_{i_{k}}).

Finally, the quantum value of the instance is the supremum of
\omega(\phi,\alpha)

In this section, we present a proof of the following theorem:

Theorem 27
.

\frac{1}{2}<t<1
and sufficiently small

,

-hard.

Our proof is an extension of Khot’s proof to the operator setting (according to Khot

Theorem

follows directly as a corollary of the next theorem:

Theorem 28
.

For every
\frac{1}{2}<t<1
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\zeta,\delta>0
such that

\mathrm{ULC}_{q}(1-\zeta,\delta)\longrightarrow{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}_{q}(1-O(\varepsilon),1-O(\varepsilon^{t})).

To prove Theorem
28
, we will first describe the reduction and then establish its completeness and soundness in the following sections.

Reduction.

Fix
\frac{1}{2}<t<1
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\zeta,\delta>0
be constants to be determined later.

Given an instance
\phi=((U,V),E,\pi,p)
in
\mathrm{ULC}
, we now describe how to construct an instance
\psi=(\overline{V},\overline{E},\overline{r},\overline{p})
in
{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
. Let
m
be the alphabet size of
\phi
.

Distribution.
We first define a probability distribution. Let
\Pr:U\times V\times\{\pm 1\}^{m}\times\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to[0,1]
be given by

\Pr(u,v,x,\mu)=\frac{p_{u,v}}{2^{m}}\varepsilon^{|\mu|}(1-\varepsilon)^{m-|\mu|}

where
|\mu|
, the Hamming weight, denotes the number of
-1
’s in
\mu
. This clearly defines a valid distribution. Indeed
\Pr(u,v,x,\mu)
is the probability of independently sampling

•

(u,v)
from the distribution
p
,

•

x
from the uniform distribution on
\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, and

•

\mu
from the noise distribution
\varepsilon^{|\mu|}(1-\varepsilon)^{m-|\mu|}
.

We also use
\Pr
to denote the marginal distributions when no ambiguity arises. For example, for every
u\in U,x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, we write
\Pr(u,x,\mu)
to mean

\sum_{v\in V}\Pr(u,v,x,\mu).

Similarly, we have

\Pr(u)=\sum_{v,x,\mu}\Pr(u,v,x,\mu)=\sum_{v:(u,v)\in E}p_{u,v}

for every
u\in U
. Lastly, whenever we write
\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}
(or with any other subset of the indices
u,v,x,\mu
), the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution
\Pr
.

Formula for the Value of
\boldsymbol{\phi.}
For example, given a quantum assignment
\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
to
\phi
, its value is given by the following expectation

\omega(\phi,\Pi)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}p_{u,v}\sum_{a\in[m]}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(a)}\Big)=\mathbb{E}_{u,v}\left[\sum_{a\in[m]}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(a)}\Big)\right].

(4.1)

Construction of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
.
We now construct
\psi=(\overline{V},\overline{E},\overline{r},\overline{p})
. The vertex set is defined as
\overline{V}=(U\cup V)\times\{\pm 1\}^{m}
. We define three types of constraints:

•

\overline{E}_{1}
consists of
((u,x),(u,x\mu))
for all
u\in U
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
,

•

\overline{E}_{2}
consists of
((v,x),(v,x\mu))
for all
v\in U
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, and

•

\overline{E}_{3}
consists of
((u,x),(v,(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu))
for all
(u,v)\in E
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
,

and set
\overline{E}=\overline{E}_{1}\cup\overline{E}_{2}\cup\overline{E}_{3}.
All parity bits
\overline{r}_{e}
are set to
1
. For every
u,v,x,\mu
, the edge weights are defined as follows:

\displaystyle\overline{p}_{(u,x),(u,x\mu)}=\frac{1}{4}\Pr(u,x,\mu),\quad\overline{p}_{(v,x),(v,x\mu)}=\frac{1}{4}\Pr(v,x,\mu),\quad\overline{p}_{(u,x),(v,(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)}=\frac{1}{2}\Pr(u,v,x,\mu).

It should be clear that this defines a probability distribution on
\overline{E}
, and this completes the construction of
\psi
.

There is a minor issue with this construction, which will be addressed at the end of this section using a technique known as
folding
. For now, as we did with the instance
\phi
, we will derive a formula for the quantum value of
\psi
.

Formula for the Value of
\boldsymbol{\psi.}
Let
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
be a quantum assignment to
\psi
. We use the shorthand notation
\alpha_{u}(x)\coloneqq\alpha((u,x))
for all
(u,x)\in\overline{V}
. Using the general formula from Definition
25
and the fact that
\overline{r}_{e}=1
we obtain

\omega(\psi,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e=(w_{1},w_{2})\in\overline{E}}\overline{p}_{e}\operatorname{tr}(\alpha(w_{1})\alpha(w_{2})).

Breaking down the sum according to the edge types and expanding the edge weights, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum_{e=(w_{1},w_{2})\in\overline{E}}\overline{p}_{e}\operatorname{tr}(\alpha(w_{1})\alpha(w_{2}))

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{u,x,\mu}\Pr(u,x,\mu)\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4}\sum_{v,x,\mu}\Pr(v,x,\mu)\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{v}(x)\alpha_{v}(x\mu)\Big)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{u,v,x,\mu}\Pr(u,v,x,\mu)\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)

which can be rewritten as:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)+\frac{1}{4}\alpha_{v}(x)\alpha_{v}(x\mu)+\frac{1}{2}\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)\right].

Therefore, the value of the assignment is

\omega(\psi,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{8}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)+\alpha_{v}(x)\alpha_{v}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)\right].

(4.2)

4.1.1
Folding

As it stands, there is an issue with our construction of
\psi
. Since all the parity bits in
\psi
are set to
1
, the instance becomes trivial: even the constant classical assignment achieves the maximum classical value of
1
. However, this is not a problem because we are actually interested in the
folded value
of
\psi
(as opposed to its ordinary value):

Definition 29

(Folded assignments and values)
.

A (classical, quantum, or noncommutative) assignment
\alpha
to the instance
\psi
(constructed above) is folded if
\alpha_{u}(-x)=-\alpha_{u}(x)
for all
u\in U\cup V
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
.

The folded (classical, quantum, or noncommutative) value of the instance is the maximum value over all folded (classical, quantum, or noncommutative) assignments.

For example, the constant assignment is not folded. The
folding trick
, which we discuss next, shows how to construct an instance
\psi^{\prime}
from
\psi
such that the folded value of
\psi
is the same as the ordinary value of
\psi^{\prime}
. This is a standard trick from the PCP literature
[
11
]
, and the following lemma serves as the basis for its use in this paper. The proof is provided in Section
7
.

Lemma 30

(Folding Trick)
.

Given an instance
\psi
as constructed above, there exists another efficiently constructed instance
\psi^{\prime}\in{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
such that assignments to
\psi^{\prime}
one-to-one correspond with folded assignments to
\psi
. Furthermore, this correspondence preserves the value. Consequently, the folded value of
\psi
is the same as the ordinary value of
\psi^{\prime}
.

Therefore, for the remainder of the proof of Theorem
28
, we can assume, without loss of generality, that valid assignments to
\psi
are folded.

4.2
Completeness

Let
\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
be a quantum assignment to
\phi
such that
\omega(\phi,\Pi)\geq 1-\zeta
. We construct a quantum assignment
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\cal{H})
to
\psi
such that
\omega(\psi,\alpha)\geq 1-\varepsilon-\zeta/4
. Choosing
\zeta
sufficiently small, we can ensure
\omega(\psi,\alpha)\geq 1-2\varepsilon
. This will complete the proof of completeness.

For every
u\in U\cup V
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, define

\displaystyle\alpha_{u}(x)=\sum_{a\in[m]}x_{a}\Pi_{u}^{a}.

These operators are Hermitian

\alpha_{u}(x)^{*}=\sum_{a\in[m]}x_{a}^{*}(\Pi_{u}^{a})^{*}=\sum_{a\in[m]}x_{a}\Pi_{u}^{a}=\alpha_{u}(x)

and unitary

\alpha_{u}(x)^{*}\alpha_{u}(x)=\sum_{a,b\in[m]}x_{a}x_{b}\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{u}^{b}=\sum_{a\in[m]}\Pi_{u}^{a}=I.

Also functions
\alpha_{u}
are odd
\alpha_{u}(-x)=-\alpha_{u}(x)
.

Furthermore, for every
u\in U\cup V
and
x,y\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
we have:

\displaystyle\left[\alpha_{u}(x),\alpha_{u}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}x_{a}y_{b}\left[\Pi_{u}^{a},\Pi_{u}^{b}\right]=0,

as operators in a PVM commute. Finally for every
(u,v)\in E
and
x,y\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
we have:

\displaystyle\left[\alpha_{u}(x),\alpha_{v}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}x_{a}y_{b}\left[\Pi_{u}^{a},\Pi_{v}^{b}\right]=0,

as
\Pi_{u}
and
\Pi_{v}
are simultaneously measurable when
(u,v)\in E
. Therefore
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\cal{H})
is a folded quantum assignment to
\psi
.

We now calculate
\omega(\psi,\alpha)
using Equation (
4.2
). First note

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u,x,\mu}\left[\sum_{a,b}x_{a}x_{b}\mu_{b}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{u}^{b}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=(1-2\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{u}\left[\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Big)\right]=1-2\varepsilon,

where in the second line we used
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[x_{a}x_{b}\right]=\delta_{a,b}
and
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mu_{b}\right]=1-2\varepsilon
. Similarly note

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\sum_{a,b}x_{a}x_{\pi_{v,u}(b)}\mu_{b}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{v}^{b}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=(1-2\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{u,v}\left[\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(a)}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\geq(1-2\varepsilon)(1-\zeta),

where the inequality follows from (
4.1
) and the assumption
\omega(\phi,\Pi)\geq 1-\zeta
. Putting everything together in (
4.2
), we obtain

\displaystyle\omega(\psi,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{4}\Big((1-2\varepsilon)+(1-2\varepsilon)(1-\zeta)\Big)\geq 1-\varepsilon-\frac{\zeta}{4},

as desired.

4.3
Soundness

Let
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\cal{H})
be a folded quantum assignment to
\psi
such that
\omega(\psi,\alpha)\geq 1-\frac{1}{32}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}
, where
b_{t}>0
is a constant to be determined later. Let
d
denote the dimension of
\cal{H}
. We construct a quantum assignment
\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
to
\phi
such that
\omega(\phi,\Pi)\geq\Omega(\varepsilon 4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}})
. This will complete the proof of soundness, as we can choose
\delta
smaller than
\Omega(\varepsilon 4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}})
.

Good Edges.
By the assumption on the value of
\alpha
and (
4.2
), we have

\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)+\alpha_{v}(x)\alpha_{v}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)\right]\geq 4-\frac{1}{4}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

Since
\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\alpha_{v}(x)\alpha_{v}(x\mu)\right]\leq 1
, it follows that

\mathbb{E}_{u,v,x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)\right]\geq 3-\frac{1}{4}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

Therefore, by a simple averaging argument, with probability at least half over the edges
(u,v)\in E
(of the Unique-Label-Cover instance
\phi
), we have

\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{u}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{u}(x)\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\Big)\right]\geq 3-\frac{1}{2}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

(4.3)

We refer to edges satisfying (
4.3
) as good.

Good Classical Assignments.
Fix a good edge
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
. By the commutation relations (of quantum assignment), operators
\alpha_{\mathbf{u}}(\{\pm 1\}^{m})
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{v}}(\{\pm 1\}^{m})
are diagonalizable in the same basis. Fix such a basis and write the operators in this basis. For
j\in[d]
, let
\alpha_{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{u},j}(x)
denote the
j
th diagonal entry of
\alpha_{\mathbf{u}}(x)
, with similar notation for
\alpha_{\mathbf{e},\mathbf{v},j}
. For simplicity, we refer to them as
\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{v},j}
when the choice of
\mathbf{e}=(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
is clear from the context.

Note that the range of
\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{v},j}
is
\{\pm 1\}
. Thus, we are effectively extracting classical assignments from the quantum assignment.

By the definition of the dimension-normalized trace, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{\mathbf{u}}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{u}}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{\mathbf{u}}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{v}}((x\circ\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}})\mu)\Big)=\mathbb{E}_{j}\Big[\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{v},j}((x\circ\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}})\mu)\Big]

where
\mathbb{E}_{j}
is to be taken with respect to the uniform distribution on
[d]
. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{j}\Big[\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\Big[\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{v},j}((x\circ\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}})\mu)\Big]\Big]\geq 3-\frac{1}{2}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

Again, by a simple averaging argument, for at least half of the indices
j\in[d]
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\Big[\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x\mu)+2\alpha_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{v},j}((x\circ\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}})\mu)\Big]\geq 3-b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

(4.4)

We call such indices good.

Two Inequalities.
Fix a good index
\mathbf{j}
. Since both
\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(x\mu)
and
\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}((x\circ\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}})\mu)
are bounded above by
1
, from (
4.4
), we obtain the following two inequalities:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\Big[\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(x\mu)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq 1-b_{t}\varepsilon^{t},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\Big[\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(x)\alpha_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}((x\circ\pi_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{u}})\mu)\Big]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

For ease of notation, let
\sigma=\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}
,
\beta=\alpha_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}
, and
\gamma=\alpha_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}
. Rewriting the inequalities with these shorthand notations gives:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\big[\beta(x)\beta(x\mu)\big]

\displaystyle\geq 1-b_{t}\varepsilon^{t},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\big[\beta(x)\gamma((x\circ\sigma^{-1})\mu)\big]

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

The next part of the proof derives the consequences of these two inequalities using Fourier analysis, following the template established by Khot and Håstad.

First, let us express these inequalities in terms of the Fourier expansions:

\displaystyle\sum_{S}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}

\displaystyle\geq 1-b_{t}\varepsilon^{t},

(4.5)

\displaystyle\sum_{S}\widehat{\beta}(S)\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}

\displaystyle\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}.

(4.6)

These Fourier calculations are standard.

Consequences of the First Inequality.
We now derive the consequences of (
4.5
). Define two sets
\mathcal{S}_{1},\mathcal{S}_{2}
of subsets of
\{\pm 1\}^{m}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{1}=\Big\{S\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{m}:|S|<\varepsilon^{-1}\Big\},\quad\mathcal{S}_{2}=\Big\{S\subseteq\{\pm 1\}^{m}:|\widehat{\beta}(S)|>\frac{1}{10}4^{-\varepsilon^{-2}}\Big\}.

Using (
4.5
), we can write

\displaystyle 1-b_{t}\varepsilon^{t}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}

\displaystyle=\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}+\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}+e^{-2}\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2},

where in the last step we used the inequality
(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}\leq e^{-2\varepsilon|S|}
. Applying Parseval’s identity, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}<\frac{b_{t}}{1-e^{-2}}\varepsilon^{t}.

(4.7)

We now choose
b_{t}=c_{t}(1-e^{-2})
, where
c_{t}
is the constant in Bourgain’s theorem. We apply Bourgain’s theorem (Theorem
7
) to (
4.7
), with the parameter
k
in the statement of the theorem set to
\varepsilon^{-1}
. The conclusion is that

\displaystyle\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}<\frac{1}{100}.

(4.8)

Consequence of the Second Inequality.
We now focus on (
4.6
) and aim to show that the contribution of
S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}
to the left hand side is significant. Specifically, we want to prove

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\beta}(S)\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}\Big|>\frac{1}{4}.

(4.9)

First, note that by Cauchy-Schwarz, Parseval’s identity, and (
4.7
), we have

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))(1-2\varepsilon)^{\lvert S\rvert}\Bigr\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{2\lvert S\rvert}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{1}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}<c_{t}\varepsilon^{t},

(4.10)

and similarly, using (
4.8
), we find

\displaystyle\Bigl\lvert\sum_{S\notin\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\beta}(S)\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))(1-2\varepsilon)^{\lvert S\rvert}\Bigr\rvert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{100}.

(4.11)

Thus, by combining (
4.6
),(
4.10
), and (
4.11
), we conclude that (
4.9
) holds for sufficiently small
\varepsilon
.

For our application, we need a slightly refined version of (
4.9
). Since
\beta
and
\gamma
are odd functions, we can rewrite (
4.9
) as

\displaystyle\Big|\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\beta}(S)\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}\Big|>\frac{1}{4}.

Squaring both sides and then applying Cauchy-Schwarz yields

\displaystyle\frac{1}{16}

\displaystyle<\Big|\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\beta}(S)\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))(1-2\varepsilon)^{|S|}\Big|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{2|S|}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))^{2}(1-2\varepsilon)^{2|S|}

where in the last step we used Parseval’s identity. Since
\frac{1}{4\varepsilon|S|}\geq e^{-4\varepsilon|S|}\geq(1-2\varepsilon)^{2|S|}
, we can rewrite the above as

\displaystyle\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\frac{1}{|S|}\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))^{2}>\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(4.12)

By definition, for all
S\in\mathcal{S}_{2}
, we have
|\widehat{\beta}(S)|^{2}>\frac{1}{100}4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}}
. Combining this with (
4.12
), we get that

\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\frac{1}{|S|}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))^{2}

can never be smaller than
\frac{\varepsilon}{400}4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}}
. Thus, we have shown

\displaystyle\sum_{\emptyset\neq S\in\mathcal{S}_{1}\cap\mathcal{S}_{2}}\frac{1}{|S|}\widehat{\beta}(S)^{2}\widehat{\gamma}(\sigma(S))^{2}>\frac{\varepsilon}{400}4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}}.

(4.13)

Quantum Assignment to
\boldsymbol{\phi}
.
We now construct the quantum assignment
\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
to
\phi
and analyse its value. For every
u\in U\cup V
and
a\in[m]
, define the operators

P_{u}^{a}=\sum_{S:a\in S}\frac{1}{|S|}\widehat{\alpha}_{u}(S)^{2},

and let
P_{u}=\{P_{u}^{a}\}_{a\in[m]}.
We may also use the notation
\sum_{S\ni a}
to mean
\sum_{S:a\in S}
.

By Lemma
9
, the
P_{u}
are self-commuting POVMs. Furthermore,
P_{u}
and
P_{v}
are simultaneously measurable when
(u,v)\in E
. So by the Projectivization Lemma in Section
6
, there exists a PVM assignment
\Pi
attaining the same value as
P
. So we just need to calculate the value of
P
.

Value of the Assignment
\boldsymbol{P}
.
Substituting into (
4.1
), the value of this assignment is

\displaystyle\omega(\phi,P)=\mathbb{E}_{u,v}\left[\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\Big(P_{u}^{a}P_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(a)}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{u,v}\left[\sum_{a}\sum_{S\ni a}\,\sum_{T\ni\pi_{u,v}(a)}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\widehat{\alpha}_{u}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{v}(T)^{2}\Big)\right].

Our first goal is to eliminate the expectation. At the beginning of this section, we established that with probability at least half, an edge
(u,v)\in E
is a good edge. Among all good edges, let
(\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v})
be the one that minimizes the expression inside the expectation:

\sum_{a}\sum_{S\ni a}\,\sum_{T\ni\pi_{u,v}(a)}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\widehat{\alpha}_{u}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{v}(T)^{2}\Big).

Thus it follows that

\displaystyle\omega(\phi,P)\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{a}\sum_{S\ni a}\,\sum_{T\ni\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(a)}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{u}}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{v}}(T)^{2}\Big).

Next, we aim to eliminate the trace. With probability at least half, an index
j\in[d]
is good. Among all good indices, let
\mathbf{j}
be the one that minimizes

\sum_{a}\sum_{S\ni a}\,\sum_{T\ni\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(a)}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{u},j}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{v},j}(T)^{2}.

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\omega(\phi,P)\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{a}\sum_{S\ni a}\,\sum_{T\ni\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(a)}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert\lvert T\rvert}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}(T)^{2}.

Ignoring the terms where
T\neq\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(S)
, we have

\displaystyle\omega(\phi,P)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{4}\sum_{a}\sum_{S\ni a}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert^{2}}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}(\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(S))^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}(\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(S))^{2}.

Finally, using (
4.13
), for every good edge and index, we know that

\sum_{S\neq\emptyset}\frac{1}{\lvert S\rvert}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{j}}(S)^{2}\widehat{\alpha}_{\mathbf{v},\mathbf{j}}(\pi_{\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}}(S))^{2}\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{400}4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}}.

Therefore we conclude that
\omega(\phi,P)\geq\frac{\varepsilon}{1600}4^{-2\varepsilon^{-2}}
, as desired.

5.1
Statements

In this section, we present a proof of the following theorem:

Theorem 31
.

Assuming wqUGC, for every
-1<\rho<0
and
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathrm{MaxCut}_{q}(\frac{1-\rho}{2}-\varepsilon,\frac{\arccos\rho}{\pi}+\varepsilon)
is
\mathrm{RE}
-hard.

This is comparable to Khot et al.
[
19
]
:

Theorem 32
.

Assuming UGC, for every
-1<\rho<0
and
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathrm{MaxCut}_{c}(\frac{1-\rho}{2}-\varepsilon,\frac{\arccos\rho}{\pi}+\varepsilon)
is
\mathrm{NP}
-hard.

Our proof is an extension of Khot et al.’s proof to the operator setting.

Theorem
31
follows directly as a corollary of the next theorem:

Theorem 33
.

For every
-1<\rho<0
and
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\zeta,\delta>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{ULC}_{\mathrm{wq}}(1-\zeta,\delta)\longrightarrow\mathrm{MaxCut}_{q}\left(\frac{1-\rho}{2}-\varepsilon,\frac{\arccos\rho}{\pi}+\varepsilon\right).

To prove Theorem
33
, we will first describe the reduction and then establish its completeness and soundness in the following sections.

Reduction.

Fix
-1<\rho<0
and
\varepsilon>0
. Let
\zeta,\delta>0
be constants to be determined later. Given an instance
\phi=((U,V),E,\pi,p)
in
\mathrm{ULC}
, we now describe how to construct an instance
\psi=(\overline{V},\overline{E},\overline{p})
in
\mathrm{MaxCut}
. Let
m
be the alphabet size of
\phi
.

Distribution.
We first define a probability distribution. Let
\Pr:U\times V\times V\times\{\pm 1\}^{m}\times\{\pm 1\}^{m}\to[0,1]
be given by

\Pr(u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu)=\frac{p_{u,v}p_{u,v^{\prime}}}{2^{m}p_{u}}\left(\frac{1-\rho}{2}\right)^{|\mu|}\left(\frac{1+\rho}{2}\right)^{m-|\mu|}

where
p_{u}
denotes the marginal distribution
p_{u}=\sum_{v:(u,v)\in E}p_{u,v}
. This clearly defines a valid distribution. Indeed
\Pr(u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu)
is the probability of sampling

•

u
from the marginal distribution
p_{u}
,

•

v
and
v^{\prime}
from the conditional distributions
\frac{p_{u,v}}{p_{u}}
and
\frac{p_{u,v^{\prime}}}{p_{u}}
, respectively,

•

x
from the uniform distribution on
\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, and

•

\mu
from the noise distribution
\left(\frac{1-\rho}{2}\right)^{|\mu|}\left(\frac{1+\rho}{2}\right)^{m-|\mu|}
.

We also use
\Pr
to denote the marginal distributions when no ambiguity arises. For example, for every
(u,v)\in E
, we write
\Pr(u,v)
to mean

\sum_{v^{\prime},x,\mu}\Pr(u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu)

which is
p_{u,v}
. Similarly, the conditional probability
\Pr(v\lvert u)
is

\frac{1}{p_{u}}\sum_{v^{\prime},x,\mu}\Pr(u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu)=\frac{p_{u,v}}{p_{u}}.

Lastly, whenever we write
\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}
(or with any other subset of the indices
u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu
), the expectation is taken with respect to the distribution
\Pr
. For example, given a weak-quantum assignment
\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
to
\phi
, its value is given by the following expectation

\omega(\phi,\Pi)=\sum_{(u,v)\in E}p_{u,v}\sum_{a\in[m]}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(a)}\Big)=\mathbb{E}_{u,v}\left[\sum_{a\in[m]}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{a}\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(a)}\Big)\right].

(5.1)

Construction of
\boldsymbol{\psi}
.
We now construct
\psi=(\overline{V},\overline{E},\overline{p})
. The vertex set is defined as
\overline{V}=V\times\{\pm 1\}^{m}
. Let
N_{u}\subseteq V
denote the set of neighbors of vertex
u\in U
in the graph
((U,V),E)
. The constraints
\overline{E}
consists of
((v,x\circ\pi_{v,u}),(v^{\prime},(x\circ\pi_{v^{\prime},u})\mu))
for all
u\in U
,
v,v^{\prime}\in N_{u}
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
. The corresponding edge weight is given by
\Pr(u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu).

Formula for the Value of
\boldsymbol{\psi.}
Let
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\cal{H})
be a quantum assignment to
\psi
. Using the general formula from Definition
25
and the fact that for
\mathrm{MaxCut}
the parity bits are all equal to
-1
we obtain

\omega(\psi,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{e=(w_{1},w_{2})\in\overline{E}}\overline{p}_{e}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha(w_{1})\alpha(w_{2})\Big).

Writing down the edges and edge weights explicitly, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{e=(w_{1},w_{2})\in\overline{E}}\overline{p}_{e}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha(w_{1})\alpha(w_{2})\Big)=\sum_{u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}\Pr(u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu)\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{v}(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\alpha_{v^{\prime}}((x\circ\pi_{v^{\prime},u})\mu)\Big)

which can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{v}(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\alpha_{v^{\prime}}((x\circ\pi_{v^{\prime},u})\mu)\Big)\right].

Therefore, the value of the assignment is

\displaystyle\omega(\psi,\alpha)=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{v}(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\alpha_{v^{\prime}}((x\circ\pi_{v^{\prime},u})\mu)\Big)\right].

(5.2)

5.2
Completeness

We first need a technical lemma.

Lemma 34
.

Let
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\Pi_{3}
be
m
-outcome PVMs. Then, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in[m]}\operatorname{tr}\big(\Pi_{1}^{a}\Pi_{2}^{a}\big)\geq 2\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{1}^{a}\Pi_{3}^{a}+\Pi_{2}^{a}\Pi_{3}^{a}\Big)-3.

Proof.

We calculate

\displaystyle 2-2\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\big(\Pi_{1}^{a}\Pi_{2}^{a}\big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{1}^{a}+\Pi_{2}^{a}-\Pi_{1}^{a}\Pi_{2}^{a}-\Pi_{2}^{a}\Pi_{1}^{a}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a}\Big\|\Pi_{1}^{a}-\Pi_{2}^{a}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}\Big(\Big\|\Pi_{1}^{a}-\Pi_{3}^{a}\Big\|_{2}+\Big\|\Pi_{3}^{a}-\Pi_{2}^{a}\Big\|_{2}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{a}2\Big\|\Pi_{1}^{a}-\Pi_{3}^{a}\Big\|_{2}^{2}+2\Big\|\Pi_{3}^{a}-\Pi_{2}^{a}\Big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=8-4\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\big(\Pi_{1}^{a}\Pi_{3}^{a}\big)-4\sum_{a}\operatorname{tr}\big(\Pi_{2}^{a}\Pi_{3}^{a}\big).

∎

Let
\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
be a weak-quantum assignment to
\phi
such that
\omega(\phi,\Pi)\geq 1-\zeta
. We construct a quantum assignment
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
to
\psi
such that
\omega(\psi,\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho)(1-4\zeta)
. Choosing
\zeta
such that
4\zeta<\varepsilon
, we can ensure
\omega(\psi,\alpha)\geq\frac{1-\rho}{2}-\varepsilon
. This will complete the proof of completeness.

For every
v\in V
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, define

\displaystyle\alpha_{v}(x)=\sum_{a\in[m]}x_{a}\Pi_{v}^{a}.

These operators are Hermitian and unitary.

Furthermore, for every vertex
u\in U
, vertices
v,v^{\prime}\in N_{u}
and
x,y\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
we have

\displaystyle\left[\alpha_{v}(x),\alpha_{v^{\prime}}(y)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b}x_{a}y_{b}\left[\Pi_{v}^{a},\Pi_{v^{\prime}}^{b}\right]=0,

since
\Pi
is a weak-quantum assignment, and thus measurements
\Pi_{v}
and
\Pi_{v^{\prime}}
are simultaneously measurable when
v,v^{\prime}\in V
share a neighbor in
U
. We conclude that
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
is a quantum assignment to
\psi
.

We now calculate
\omega(\psi,\alpha)
using Equation (
5.2
). We have

\displaystyle\omega(\psi,\alpha)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{v}(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\alpha_{v^{\prime}}((x\circ\pi_{v^{\prime},u})\mu)\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}\left[\sum_{a,b}x_{\pi_{v,u}(a)}x_{\pi_{v^{\prime},u}(b)}\mu_{b}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{v}^{a}\Pi_{v^{\prime}}^{b}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{c}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(c)}\Pi_{v^{\prime}}^{\pi_{u,v^{\prime}}(c)}\Big)\right],

where in the third line we used
\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[x_{a}x_{b}\right]=\delta_{a,b}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mu}\left[\mu_{b}\right]=\rho
and we redefined the running index of the sum by
c=\pi_{v,u}(a)=\pi_{v^{\prime},u}(b)
. Using Lemma
34
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime}}\left[\sum_{c}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(c)}\Pi_{v^{\prime}}^{\pi_{u,v^{\prime}}(c)}\Big)\right]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{u,v,v^{\prime}}\left[2\sum_{c}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{c}\Pi_{v}^{\pi_{u,v}(c)}\Big)+2\sum_{c}\operatorname{tr}\Big(\Pi_{u}^{c}\Pi_{v^{\prime}}^{\pi_{u,v^{\prime}}(c)}\Big)-3\right]

\displaystyle\geq 4(1-\zeta)-3=1-4\zeta,

where we used the assumption
\omega(\phi,\Pi)\geq 1-\zeta
.
Putting everything together, we obtain

\displaystyle\omega(\psi,\alpha)\geq\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\rho}{2}(1-4\zeta)\geq\frac{1}{2}(1-\rho)(1-4\zeta),

as desired.

5.3
Soundness

Let
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})

\psi

\Pi:U\cup V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
to

such that

Good Vertices in
\boldsymbol{U}
.
By the assumption on the value of

Therefore, by a simple averaging argument, with probability at least
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
over the vertices

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{v,v^{\prime},x,\mu}\left[\operatorname{tr}\Big(\alpha_{v}(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\alpha_{v^{\prime}}((x\circ\pi_{v^{\prime},u})\mu)\Big)\right]\leq 1-\frac{2}{\pi}\arccos{\rho}-\varepsilon.

(5.3)

Good Classical Assignments.
Due to the commutation relations in the quantum assignment

where the expectation is with respect to the uniform distribution over

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{j}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x,\mu}\left[\beta_{\mathbf{u},j}(x)\beta_{\mathbf{u},j}(x\mu)\right]\right]\leq 1-\frac{2}{\pi}\arccos{\rho}-\varepsilon.

5.4
Integrality Gap

In Section
1.4
and Figure
9
, we suggested that, assuming wqUGC, there are instances
G
of MaxCut for which
\omega_{c}(G)\approx 0.878\,\omega_{\mathrm{sdp}}(G)
and
\omega_{q}(G)\approx\omega_{\mathrm{sdp}}(G)
. This is a direct consequence of the following theorem:

Theorem 35
.

Assuming wqUGC, there exists an instance
G
of MaxCut for which
\omega_{c}(G)=\alpha_{\mathrm{gw}}\,\omega_{q}(G)
, where
\alpha_{\mathrm{gw}}=\frac{2}{\pi}\min_{0\leq\theta\leq\pi}\frac{\theta}{1-\cos(\theta)}
is the Goemans-Williamson constant.

Proof.

First consider the chain of inequalities
\alpha_{\mathrm{gw}}\,\omega_{q}(G)\leq\omega_{c}(G)\leq\omega_{q}(G)
derived from (
1.4
). The following three straightforward facts show that the inequality on the left,
\alpha_{\mathrm{gw}}\,\omega_{q}(G)\leq\omega_{c}(G)
, must be tight:

1.

the classical value can be exactly computed in
\mathrm{NP}
,

2.

6
Projectivization Lemma

A (weak-quantum, quantum, or noncommutative)
POVM assignment
is defined similarly to a (weak-quantum, quantum, or noncommutative) assignment, except that we use self-commuting POVMs instead of PVMs in the definitions. In this section, we aim to show that the choice between PVMs and self-commuting POVMs does not affect the value. Specifically, we prove that if a POVM assignment achieves a value
\lambda
, then there also exists a PVM assignment that achieves a value of at least
\lambda
.

Extremal POVMs.
The set of POVMs is convex and compact, so by the Krein-Milman theorem, every POVM can be expressed as a convex combination of extremal POVMs. According to Holevo
[
57
]
, PVMs are extremal POVMs. However it is important to note that not all extremal POVMs are PVMs. For more details, see Watrous
[
44
]
.

The set of self-commuting POVMs is also convex and compact, with PVMs remaining extremal in this smaller subset. In fact, PVMs are the only extremal points in the set of self-commuting POVMs. Thus, we can express any self-commuting POVM as convex combinations of some PVMs. However, to achieve the goal of this section, we must proceed carefully: the POVMs in a POVM assignment satisfy certain commutation relations, which we must account for.

Objective Function.
Let
P=\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\}\subset\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
be a quantum POVM assignment to a
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
instance
\phi=([n],E,f,p)
. Recall that
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}
denotes the set of self-commuting POVMs. The value of this assignment is given by

\omega(\phi,P)=\sum_{e=(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in E}p_{e}\sum_{a\in[m]^{k}}f_{e}(a_{1},\ldots,a_{k})\operatorname{tr}\left(P_{i_{1}}^{a_{1}}\cdots P_{i_{k}}^{a_{k}}\right).

Let us write
\omega_{\phi}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
to represent
\omega(\phi,P)
. We view
\omega_{\phi}
as a function
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
. On a quantum assignment, due to the commutation relations, the image is guaranteed to lie in the interval
[0,1]
. However, since the set of quantum assignments is not convex, we instead consider the domain to be the convex and compact set
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}^{n}
.

This function behaves well coordinate-wise: for every
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n},Q\in\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
,
\lambda\in[0,1]
, and
i\in[n]
, we have the property

\displaystyle\omega_{\phi}(P_{1},\ldots,\lambda P_{i}+(1-\lambda)Q,\ldots,P_{n})=\lambda\omega_{\phi}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{i},\ldots,P_{n})+(1-\lambda)\omega_{\phi}(P_{1},\ldots,Q,\ldots,P_{n}).

(6.1)

This property holds because each POVM
P_{i}
contributes linearly to
\omega_{\phi}(P_{1},\ldots,P_{n})
. Specifically, at most one element
P_{i}^{a}\in P_{i}
, for some
a\in[m]
, appears in each term of the summation.

Given (
6.1
) and the convexity of the domain, the function is coordinate-wise both convex and concave. However, strictly speaking, convexity is defined for real-valued functions, so we will not use this terminology here.

Lemma 36
.

Let
\phi=(V,E,f,p)
be a
{k}\textit{-}\mathrm{CSP}(m)
and let
P:V\to\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
be a quantum POVM assignment to
\phi
. There exists a quantum (PVM) assignment
\Pi:V\to\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
to
\phi
for which the value is at least the value of
P
.

Note that
\Pi
is defined on the same Hilbert space as
P
. By Property (
6.1
) of the objective function, Lemma
36
is a direct corollary of the following lemma.

Lemma 37
.

For every
P\in\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
there exists an integer
N
and
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\ldots,\Pi_{N}\in\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{H})
such that
P
is in the convex hull of
\Pi_{i}
’s. Furthermore, if
M\in\mathcal{M}(\mathcal{H})
is any matrix that commutes with
P
, it also commutes with every
\Pi_{i}
.

Proof.

Let
P=\{P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}\}
. Let
\mathcal{A}
be the commutative algebra generated by
P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}
. Let
\Delta_{1},\ldots,\Delta_{N}
be a complete set of minimal projections in
\mathcal{A}
: that is
\Delta_{i}
are minimal projections and
\Delta_{i}\Delta_{j}=0
if
i\neq j
and
\Delta_{1}+\cdots+\Delta_{N}=I
. Since
\mathcal{A}
is a finite-dimensional commutative algebra, it is straightforward to show that such a complete set of minimal projections always exists.

Let
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
denote the set of
m
-outcome POVMs on
\mathcal{A}
. For example
P\in\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
. Similarly let
\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
denote the set of
m
-outcome PVMs on
\mathcal{A}
. By Holevo’s theorem, elements of
\mathrm{PVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
are extremal in the larger set of all POVMs, and therefore, they must also be extremal in the smaller set
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
. Using a perturbative argument, we show that these are the only extremal measurements in
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
.

Suppose
Q=\{Q^{1},\ldots,Q^{m}\}
is extremal in
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\mathcal{A})
. For each
a\in[m]
we can express
Q^{a}=\sum_{i}\alpha_{a,i}\Delta_{i}
for some
\alpha_{a,i}\in[0,1]
. If
Q
is not a PVM, then there must exist some
a\in[m]
and
i\in[N]
such that
\alpha_{a,i}\in(0,1)
. Then there must also exist a
b\in[m]\setminus\{a\}
for which
\alpha_{b,i}\in(0,1)
. For simplicity, assume
a=1
and
b=2
. For sufficiently small
\varepsilon>0
, consider two POVMs

Q^{1}+\varepsilon\Delta_{i},Q^{2}-\varepsilon\Delta_{i},Q^{3},\ldots,Q^{m}

and

Q^{1}-\varepsilon\Delta_{i},Q^{2}+\varepsilon\Delta_{i},Q^{3},\ldots,Q^{m}.

Clearly they are in
\mathcal{A}
and
Q
is a convex combination of them. This is a contradiction.

Since
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
is convex and compact, given
P\in\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
, by the Krein-Milman theorem, there exist extremal
\Pi_{1},\ldots,\Pi_{N}\in\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
such that
P
is in their convex hull. Since extremal measurements in
\mathrm{POVM}_{m}(\cal{H})
are PVMs this completes the proof of the first part.

The second part follows from the fact that every element in
\mathcal{A}
is a polynomial in commuting operators
P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}
. So if
M
is a matrix commuting with
P^{1},\ldots,P^{m}
, it commutes with every element in
\mathcal{A}
including
\Pi_{i}
’s.
∎

7
Folding Lemma

Lemma
.

Given an instance
\psi
as constructed in the proof of Theorem
28
, there exists another efficiently constructed instance
\psi^{\prime}\in{2}\textit{-}\mathrm{Lin}
such that assignments to
\psi^{\prime}
one-to-one correspond to folded assignments to
\psi
with the same value. Consequently, the folded value of
\psi
is the same as the ordinary value of
\psi^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
\xi_{m}
be a subset of
\{\pm 1\}^{m}
such that for every
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
exactly one of
x
or
-x
is contained in
\xi_{m}
. For every
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, let
\kappa(x)=1
if
x\in\xi_{m}
and
\kappa(x)=-1
otherwise. Finally let
\theta(x)=\kappa(x)x
. Clearly
\theta(x)\in\xi_{m}
for all
x
.

We now define the instance
\psi^{\prime}=(\overline{V}^{\prime},\overline{E}^{\prime},\overline{r}^{\prime},\overline{p}^{\prime})
. Let
\overline{V}^{\prime}=(U\cup V)\times\xi_{m}
. The edges
e\in\overline{E}^{\prime}
, their parity bits
\overline{r}^{\prime}_{e}\in\{\pm 1\}
, and their weights
\overline{p}^{\prime}_{e}
are defined as follows:

1.

For every
u\in U\cup V
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, let
((u,\theta(x)),(u,\theta(x\mu)))
be an edge in
\overline{E}^{\prime}
. Set its parity bit to
\kappa(x)\kappa(x\mu)
. Set its weight to be the same as the weight of
((u,x),(u,x\mu))
in
\psi
.
8
8

8

Note that
\overline{E}^{\prime}
is allowed to be a multiset (see Definition
10
). For example, both
((u,\theta(x)),(u,\theta(x\mu)))
and
((u,\theta(-x)),(u,\theta(-x\mu)))
are edges in
\overline{E}^{\prime}
, each with their own parity bits and weights.

2.

For every edge
(u,v)\in E
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
, let
((u,\theta(x)),(v,\theta((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)))
be an edge in
\overline{E}^{\prime}
. Set its parity bit to
\kappa(x)\kappa((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)
. Set its weight to be the same as the weight of
((u,x),(v,(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu))
in
\psi
.

Let
\alpha^{\prime}:\overline{V}^{\prime}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
be an arbitrary (classical, quantum, or noncommutative) assignment to
\psi^{\prime}
. Define the assignment
\alpha:\overline{V}\to\mathrm{Obs}(\mathcal{H})
to
\psi
such that
\alpha(u,x)=\kappa(x)\alpha^{\prime}(u,\theta(x))
for all
u\in U\cup V
and
x\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
. It holds that

\alpha(u,-x)=\kappa(-x)\alpha^{\prime}(u,\theta(-x))=-\kappa(x)\alpha^{\prime}(u,\theta(x))=-\alpha(u,x)

and thus
\alpha
is folded. Also given any folded assignment
\alpha
to
\psi
, the assignment
\alpha^{\prime}
is uniquely determined using the identity
\alpha^{\prime}(u,\theta(x))=\kappa(x)\alpha(u,x)
. This is well-defined since
\alpha^{\prime}(u,\theta(-x))=\kappa(-x)\alpha(u,-x)=\kappa(x)\alpha(u,x)=\alpha^{\prime}(u,\theta(x))
. This completes the proof of one-to-one correspondence.

Next we show that
\alpha
satisfies an edge in
\overline{E}
if and only if
\alpha^{\prime}
satisfies the corresponding edge in
\overline{E}^{\prime}
. We do this for one of the two types of edges in the constructions of
\psi
and
\psi^{\prime}
. The other case can be proved similarly.

Fix an arbitrary edge
(u,v)\in E
and
x,\mu\in\{\pm 1\}^{m}
. By the construction of
\psi
, assignment
\alpha
satisfies the edge
((u,x),(v,(x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu))
if and only if
\alpha_{u}(x)=\alpha_{v}((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)
. By the definition of
\alpha
, this is equivalent to
\kappa(x)\alpha^{\prime}_{u}(\theta(x))=\kappa((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu)\alpha^{\prime}_{v}(\theta((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu))
. Finally by the construction of
\psi^{\prime}
, this is equivalent to saying that
\alpha^{\prime}
satisfies the edge
((u,\theta(x)),(v,\theta((x\circ\pi_{v,u})\mu))).

∎
