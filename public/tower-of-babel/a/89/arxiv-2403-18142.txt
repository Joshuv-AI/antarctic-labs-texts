HERTA: A High-Efficiency and Rigorous Training Algorithm for Unfolded Graph Neural Networks
Yongyi Yang, Jiaming Yang, Wei Hu, Michał Dereziński

Abstract
As a variant of Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), Unfolded GNNs offer enhanced interpretability and flexibility over traditional designs. Nevertheless, they still suffer from scalability challenges when it comes to the training cost. Although many methods have been proposed to address the scalability issues, they mostly focus on per-iteration efficiency, without worst-case convergence guarantees. Moreover, those methods typically add components to or modify the original model, thus possibly breaking the interpretability of Unfolded GNNs. In this paper, we propose HERTA: a High-Efficiency and Rigorous Training Algorithm for Unfolded GNNs that accelerates the whole training process, achieving a nearly-linear time worst-case training guarantee. Crucially, HERTA converges to the optimum of the original model, thus preserving the interpretability of Unfolded GNNs. Additionally, as a byproduct of HERTA, we propose a new spectral sparsification method applicable to normalized and regularized graph Laplacians that ensures tighter bounds for our algorithm than existing spectral sparsifiers do. Experiments on real-world datasets verify the superiority of HERTA as well as its adaptability to various loss functions and optimizers.

Introduction

Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) have become a powerful modern tool for handling graph data because of their strong representational capabilities and ability to explore the relationships between data points
(
Kipf & Welling 2016
;
Battaglia et al. 2018
;
Xu et al. 2018
)
. Like many deep learning models, GNNs are generally designed by heuristics and experience, which makes analyzing and understanding them a difficult task.
Unfolded GNNs

(
Yang et al. 2021a
)
are a type of GNNs that are rigorously derived from an optimization problem, which makes their inductive bias and training dynamics easier to interpret.

Despite their enhanced interpretability, training unfolded models can be extremely expensive, especially when the graph is large and (relatively) dense. This issue can come from two aspects:

1)

Slow iterations
: in every iteration the whole graph must be fed into the model, and the interactive nature of graphs prevents trivially utilizing techniques like mini-batch training;

2)

Slow convergence
: the training convergence rate is related to the connectivity of the graph and the well-conditionedness of the node features, and the model converges slowly when the data is ill-conditioned.

Many methods have been proposed to address the high cost of training unfolded models
(
Chen et al. 2018b
;
Chiang et al. 2019
;
Zeng et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Fey et al. 2021
;
Jiang et al. 2023
)
, primarily by using graph sampling to enable mini-batch training schemes that reduce the per-iteration cost (Issue
1
).
However, these approaches typically require distorting the underlying optimization objective explicitly or implicitly, thus diminishing the rigorous and interpretable nature of Unfolded GNNs. Moreover, existing works have mostly focused on per-iteration efficiency, while the convergence rate of the optimization in training Unfolded GNNs (Issue
2
) remains un-addressed, leading to methods that are not robust to ill-conditioned data.

We propose HERTA: a High-Efficiency and Rigorous Training Algorithm for Unfolded GNNs, which is an algorithmic framework designed to address both Issues
1
and
2
, while at the same time preserving the rigorous and interpretable nature of Unfolded GNNs.
Unlike many existing methods that require changing the GNN model or defining a new objective, HERTA converges to the optimum of the original training problem, and thus preserves the interpretability of the unfolded models. Moreover, HERTA uses a specialized preconditioner to ensure fast linear convergence to the optimum, requiring only a logarithmic number of passes over the data. We show empirically that HERTA can be used to accelerate training for a variety of GNN objectives, as an extension of popular optimization techniques.

A key novelty of our algorithm lies in the construction of a preconditioner for the GNN objective, which accelerates the convergence rate. To construct this preconditioner, we design a spectral sparsification method for approximating the squared inverse of a normalized and regularized Laplacian matrix, by relying on an extension of the notion of effective resistance to overcome the limitations of existing graph sparsification tools. Below, we present an informal statement of our main result.

Theorem 1.1

(Informal version of
Theorem

5.1
)
.

HERTA solves the
\lambda
-regularized Unfolded GNN objective (
4.2

n

In practice, the node feature dimensionality

Related Work

Unfolded Graph Neural Networks.
Unlike conventional GNN models, which are designed mainly by heuristics, the forward layers of Unfolded GNNs are derived by explicitly optimizing a graph-regularized target function
(
Zhang et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2021a
;
Ma et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Yang et al. 2021b
;
Zhu et al. 2021
;
Ahn et al. 2022
)
. This optimization nature of Unfolded GNNs allows developing models in a more interpretable and controllable way. The presence of an explicit optimization problem allows designers to inject desired properties into the model and better understand it. This approach has been used to overcome known GNN issues such as over-smoothing
(
Oono & Suzuki 2019
;
Liu et al. 2020
;
Yang et al. 2020
)
and sensitivity towards spurious edges
(
Zügner et al. 2018
;
Zhu et al. 2020
)
.

Efficient Graph Neural Network Training.
In order to address the scalability issue of GNNs, many techniques have been proposed. There are two major directions in the exploration of the solutions of this issue. The first direction is to adopt sampling methods. These methods include node-wise sampling
(
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
, layer-wise sampling
(
Chen et al. 2018a
)
and subgraph-wise sampling
(
Chiang et al. 2019
;
Jiang et al. 2023
)
. The second direction is to utilize embeddings from past iterations to enhance current iterations and to obtain more accurate representation with mini-batches and fewer forward layers
(
Fey et al. 2021
;
Ding et al. 2021
;
Xue et al. 2023
)
.

As we indicated in
Section

1
, all of the above
methods aim at addressing Issue
1
(
Slow Iterations
), and none of them achieves a worst-case convergence guarantee without affecting the underlying GNN model. In contrast, HERTA addresses both Issue
1
and also Issue
2
(
Slow Convergence
), and possesses a theoretical guarantee on the training convergence rate while preserving the original target model.

Matrix Sketching and Subsampling.

Our techniques and analysis are closely related to matrix sketching and subsampling, which are primarily studied in the area of Randomized Numerical Linear Algebra (RandNLA,
Woodruff et al. 2014
;
Drineas & Mahoney 2016
;
Martinsson & Tropp 2020
;
Murray et al. 2023
). Given a large matrix, by sketching or sampling we compress it to a much smaller matrix with certain properties preserved, thus accelerating the solution by conducting expensive computations on the smaller matrix. This type of methods lead to improved randomized algorithms for tasks including low rank approximation
(
Halko et al. 2011
;
Cohen et al. 2017a
;
Clarkson & Woodruff 2017
)
, linear system solving
(
Peng & Vempala 2021
;
Dereziński & Yang 2023
)
, least squares regression
(
Rokhlin & Tygert 2008
;
Meng et al. 2014
)
and so on
(
Cohen et al. 2021
)
. Our usage of these methods includes constructing spectral sparsifiers of graphs, obtained by edge subsampling
(
Spielman & Srivastava 2008
;
Spielman & Teng 2011
;
Koutis et al. 2016
)
, which are central in designing fast solvers for Laplacian linear systems
(
Vishnoi et al. 2013b
;
Kelner et al. 2013
;
Peng & Spielman 2014
)
.

Preliminaries

For a vector
{\bm{x}}
, we denote its

\|{\bm{x}}\|
. We use

to denote diagonalization of

, we use

. We use
\mathrm{nnz}(\bm{M})
to denote the number of non-zero entries of
\bm{M}
. For two positive semidefinite (PSD) matrices
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
, if there exists
\epsilon\in(0,1)
such that

\displaystyle(1-\epsilon)\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\preceq{\bm{\Sigma}}\preceq(1+\epsilon)\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}},

(3.1)

then we say
{\bm{\Sigma}}\approx_{\epsilon}\widetilde{{\bm{\Sigma}}}
, where
\preceq
refers to Loewner order.

We use
{\bm{1}}
to denote a vector with all entries equal to
1
, and the dimension of which is determined by the context if not specified. We use
{\bm{\delta}}_{u}
to denote a vector with
u
-th entry equal to
1
and all other entries equal to
0
. We also use
\delta_{u,v}
to represent the
v
-th entry of
{\bm{\delta}}_{u}
, i.e.:

\delta_{u,v}=\begin{cases}1&u=v\\
0&u\neq v\end{cases}.

For a function
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
that is bounded from bottom, a point
{\bm{w}}_{0}
is called a solution of
\ell
with
\epsilon
error rate if it satisfies
\ell({\bm{w}}_{0})\leq(1+\epsilon)\ell^{*}
where
\ell^{*}=\inf_{{\bm{w}}}\ell({\bm{w}})
.

We adopt big-O notations in the time complexity analysis. Moreover, we use the notation
\tilde{O}(\cdot)
to hide polynomial logarithmic factors of the problem size. As an example,
O(\log(n))
can be expressed as
\tilde{O}(1)
.

For a matrix
{\bm{\Pi}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
, we call it a Gaussian sketch if its entries are i.i.d.

{\bm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

Problem Setting

Throughout this paper, we consider a graph
\mathcal{G}
with
n
nodes and
m
edges. We denote its adjacency matrix by
{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, degree matrix by
{\bm{D}}=\mathop{\mathrm{diag}}\left({\bm{A}}{\bm{1}}\right)\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
, and Laplace matrix (or Laplacian) by
{\bm{L}}={\bm{D}}-{\bm{A}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
. We also use the normalized adjacency
\hat{\bm{A}}={\bm{D}}^{-1/2}{\bm{A}}{\bm{D}}^{-1/2}
and normalized Laplacian
\hat{\bm{L}}={\bm{I}}-\hat{\bm{A}}
. Notice that we assume there’s a self-loop for each node to avoid
0
-degree nodes, which is commonly ensured in GNN implementations
(
Kipf & Welling 2016
;
Hamilton et al. 2017
)
, and therefore
{\bm{D}}^{-1/2}
always exists. We also use
{\bm{B}}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
to represent the incidence matrix of graph
\mathcal{G}
, that is, if the
i
-th edge in
\mathcal{G}
is
(u_{i},v_{i})
, then the
i
-th row of
{\bm{B}}
is
{\bm{\delta}}_{u_{i}}-{\bm{\delta}}_{v_{i}}
(the order of
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
is arbitrary). It is not hard to see that
{\bm{L}}={\bm{B}}^{\top}{\bm{B}}
.

We also assume that for each node
u
in the graph, there is a feature vector
{\bm{x}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a label vector
1
1

1

We allow the label to be a vector to make the concept more general. When labels are one-hot vectors, the task is a classification task, and when labels are scalars, it is a regression task.

{\bm{y}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{c}
attached with it. Let
{\bm{X}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
and
{\bm{Y}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
be the stack of
{\bm{x}}_{i}
-s and
{\bm{y}}_{i}
-s. We assume
n\geq\max\{d,c\}
and
{\bm{X}}
and
{\bm{Y}}
are both column full rank.

The Unfolded GNN we consider in this paper is based on TWIRLS
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
, which
is defined by optimizing the following objective (called the “energy function”):

E({\bm{Z}};{\bm{W}}):=\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\left({\bm{Z}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{L}}{\bm{Z}}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{Z}}-f({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}})\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}.

(4.1)

Here
{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}
is the optimization variable that can be viewed as the hidden state of the model,
{\bm{W}}
is the learnable parameter of the model, and
\lambda>0
is a hyper-parameter to balance the graph structure information and node feature information. Note that
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{c}
is a function of
\bm{X}
and is parameterized by matrix
{\bm{W}}
.

For the model training, given a loss function
\mathcal{\ell}:\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}\times\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}\to\mathbb{R}
, the training of TWIRLS can be represented by the following bi-level optimization problem

\displaystyle{\bm{W}}^{*}\in\arg\min_{{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times c}}\ell\left[{\bm{Z}}^{*}({\bm{W}});{\bm{Y}}\right]

(4.2)

\displaystyle\text{s.t.}\quad{\bm{Z}}^{*}({\bm{W}})\in\arg\min_{{\bm{Z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times c}}E({\bm{Z}};{\bm{W}}),

(4.3)

where the solution of inner problem
eq.

4.3
(i.e.
{\bm{Z}}^{*}({\bm{W}})
) is viewed as the model output. The outer problem
eq.

4.2
is viewed as the problem of training.

In
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
, the forward process of TWIRLS is obtained by unfolding the gradient descent algorithm for minimizing

\displaystyle{\bm{Z}}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle={\bm{Z}}^{(t)}-\alpha\nabla E\left({\bm{Z}}^{(t)}\right)

(4.4)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha\lambda-\alpha){\bm{Z}}^{(t)}+\alpha\hat{\bm{A}}{\bm{Z}}^{(t)}+\alpha f\left({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}}\right),

(4.5)

where
\alpha>0
is the step size of gradient descent. Notice that we fix
{\bm{W}}
and view

{\bm{Z}}
. The model output is
{\bm{Z}}^{(T)}

T

\alpha

is large, it is clear that
{\bm{Z}}^{(T)}
is an approximation of
{\bm{Z}}^{*}

In this paper, we always assume

. Therefore, we directly consider the explicit optimal solution of

4.1

\displaystyle{\bm{Z}}^{*}({\bm{W}})=\arg\min_{{\bm{Z}}}E({\bm{Z}})=\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-1}f({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}}).

(4.6)

In
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
, the implementation of

is arbitrary as long as it can be trained (i.e. computable, smooth, etc.). In this paper we consider a simple implementation of

which is a linear function, namely
f({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}})={\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}
where
{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times c}
. Notice that this is also the practical setting used in

)
for many datasets. For loss function we choose the mean squared error (MSE) loss defined as
\ell({\bm{Z}},{\bm{Y}}):=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{Z}}-{\bm{Y}}\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}

5
Algorithm and Analysis

In this section we present our main algorithm HERTA, give the convergence result and analyze its time complexity. We also introduce our key techniques and give a proof sketch for the main result. To start we introduce the following notion.

Definition 5.1

(Effective Laplacian dimension)
.

For normalized Laplacian
\hat{\bm{L}}
and regularization term
\lambda>0
, define

\displaystyle n_{\lambda}:=\mathrm{Tr}\left[\hat{\bm{L}}\left(\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}\right)^{-1}\right]

(5.1)

as the effective Laplacian dimension of the graph.

Denote
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
as the eigenvalues of
\hat{\bm{L}}
. Then the effective Laplacian dimension can be written as
n_{\lambda}=\sum_{i}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\lambda^{-1}}
. We can see that
n_{\lambda}
is roughly the number of eigenvalues of
\hat{\bm{L}}
which are of order
\Omega(\lambda^{-1})
, i.e., the number of “large eigenvalues” mentioned in
Theorem

1.1
. It is not hard to see that
n_{\lambda}\leq n
and
n_{\lambda}\to n
as
\lambda\to\infty
.

Intuitively,
n_{\lambda}
represents the number of eigen-directions for which the Laplacian regularizer
\frac{\lambda}{2}\mathrm{Tr}({\bm{Z}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{L}}{\bm{Z}})
is large, thus having a significant impact on the energy function (
4.1
). For the remaining eigen-directions which are less significant, the effect of the regularizer is minimal. This can also be seen from the loss function (
4.9
): when we apply matrix
({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}})^{-1}
to a vector, the effect of the Laplacian is dominated by the
{\bm{I}}
term if this vector is aligned with a “small” Laplacian eigen-direction (i.e., with an eigenvalue significantly smaller than
\lambda^{-1}
), and thus the objective is close to the usual unregularized least squares loss.

Based on the above discussion, it stands to reason that the larger the effective Laplacian dimension
n_{\lambda}
of the graph, the more work we must do during the training to preserve the graph structure of the GNN. With the above discussion, we formally give our main result for HERTA.

Theorem 5.1

(Main result)
.

For any
\epsilon>0
, with a proper step size
\eta
, number of iterations
T
and constant
K>0
,
Algorithm

1
finds a solution
\hat{\bm{W}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times c}
in time

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left(\left(m+nd\right)c\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}+n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d+d^{3}\right)

(5.2)

such that with probability
1-\frac{1}{n}
,

\displaystyle\ell\left[{\bm{Z}}^{*}({\bm{\hat{W}}});{\bm{Y}}\right]\leq(1+\epsilon)\cdot\ell^{*},

(5.3)

where
\ell^{*}:=\min_{{\bm{W}}}\ell\left[{\bm{Z}}^{*}\left({\bm{W}};{\bm{Y}}\right)\right]
.

Notice that if we assume
n_{\lambda}=O(n/\lambda^{2})
, then
\tilde{O}(n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d)=\tilde{O}(nd)
. By further assuming the number of classes

, we recover the bound given by

1.1

5.1
Analysis of TWIRLS Training

In this subsection, we introduce the framework that we use to analyze the complexity of TWIRLS training. We also provide a complexity analysis of the implementation used in
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
.

As noted in
Section

4
, the whole optimization problem can be viewed as a bi-level optimization problem, where the inner-problem
eq.

4.3
approximates the linear system solver
\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-1}({\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{i})
, and the outer problem
eq.

4.2
uses the linear system solution to approximate the optimal solution of the training loss
eq.

4.7
. When we discuss the solution of outer problems, we treat the solver of the inner problem (linear solver) as a black box. We formally define a linear solver as follows.

Definition 5.2

(Linear solver)
.

For a positive definite matrix
{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and a real number
\epsilon>0
, we call
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
a linear solver for
{\bm{H}}
with
\epsilon
error rate if it satisfies

\displaystyle\forall{\bm{u}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},~\left\|f({\bm{u}})-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}\right\|_{{\bm{H}}}\leq\epsilon\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}\right\|_{{\bm{H}}}.

(5.4)

In
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
, the outer problem is also solved by standard gradient descent. The gradient of
\ell_{i}\left({\bm{w}}_{i}\right)
is

\displaystyle\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}}_{i})={\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-2}\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{i}\right).

(5.5)

In actual implementation, the matrix inverse is calculated by linear solvers. Suppose that we have a linear solver for
({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}})
denoted by
\mathcal{S}
, then by embedding it into
eq.

5.5
we can obtain the gradient approximation as

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}}_{i})}:={\bm{X}}^{\top}\mathcal{S}\left[\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{i}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{i}\right)\right].

(5.6)

The following convergence result can be derived for this approximate gradient descent. See Appendix for the proof.

Lemma 5.1

(Convergence)
.

If we minimize
eq.

4.9
using gradient descent with the gradient approximation defined in
eq.

5.6
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a linear solver of
{\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}
with
\mu
error rate satisfying
\mu\leq\min\left\{\frac{\epsilon^{1/2}}{50\kappa({\bm{X}})\lambda^{2}},1\right\}
, then, to obtain an
\epsilon\in(0,1)
error rate the outer problem
eq.

4.9
, the number of iterations needed is

\displaystyle T=O\left(\kappa\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right),

(5.7)

where
\kappa:=\kappa({\bm{X}}^{\top}(\bm{I}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}})^{-2}{\bm{X}})
.

Based on
Lemma

5.1
, in
Appendix

B
we provide a detailed analysis of the time complexity of the implementation used by
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
. From this analysis, one can figure out two bottlenecks of the computational complexity for solving TWIRLS: 1) The number of iterations needed for the inner loop is dependent on
\lambda
; 2) The number of iterations needed for the outer loop is dependent on the condition number of the outer problem. Our acceleration algorithm is based on the following two observations, which correspond to the aforementioned bottlenecks:

1.

The data matrix in the inner loop is a graph Laplacian plus identity. It is possible to solve the linear system defined by this matrix faster by exploiting its structure.

2.

A good enough preconditioner can be constructed for the outer iteration using techniques from RandNLA.

5.2
Key Techniques

In this subsection, we introduce the mathematical tools that we will use in the proof of our main result.

SDD Solvers.

If a symmetric matrix
{\bm{H}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
satisfies

\displaystyle\forall 1\leq k\leq n,~~{\bm{H}}_{k,k}\geq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|{\bm{H}}_{k,i}\right|,

(5.8)

it is called a symmetric diagonal dominated (SDD) matrix. It has been shown that the linear system defined by sparse SDD matrices can be solved in an efficient way, via a SDD solver
(
Kelner et al. 2013
;
Peng & Spielman 2014
)
.

Since
{\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}
is indeed a SDD matrix in our problem, we can apply off-the-shelf SDD solvers. In the following, we use
\mathsf{SDDSolver}_{\epsilon}({\bm{H}},{\bm{u}})
to denote the SDD solver that calculates
{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}
with error rate less or equal to
\epsilon
2
2

2

Principally,
\mathsf{SDDSolver}({\bm{H}};\cdot)
is a vector function, but for convenience we sometimes also apply it to matrices, in which case we mean applying it column-wisely. We also use the same convention for other vector functions.
. From
Lemma

A.1
, the time complexity of calculating
\mathsf{SDDSolver}_{\epsilon}({\bm{H}};{\bm{u}})
is
\tilde{O}\left[\mathrm{nnz}({\bm{H}})\right]
. See
Section

A.2
for details.

Fast Matrix Multiplication.

For a matrix
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
where
n>d
, it is known that calculating
{\bm{Q}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}
takes
O(nd^{2})
time if implemented by brute force. In HERTA, we manage to achieve a faster calculation of
{\bm{Q}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}
through Subsampled Randomized Hadamard Transformation (SRHT)
(
Tropp 2011
;
Woodruff et al. 2014
)
. The key idea of SRHT is to first apply a randomized Hadamard transformation to the matrix to make the “information” evenly distributed in each row, then uniformly sample the rows of
{\bm{Q}}
to reduce the dimension. In the following, we use
\mathsf{Hadamard}
to represent the Hadamard transformation, see
Section

A.3
for the definition of
\mathsf{Hadamard}
as well as a detailed introduction.

5.3
Regularized Spectral Sparsifier

It has been shown that for any connected graph with
n
nodes, (no matter how dense it is) there is always a sparsified graph with
\tilde{O}\left(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
edges whose Laplacian is an
\epsilon
-spectral approximation of the original graph Laplacian
(
Spielman & Teng 2011
)
. Although the existence is guaranteed, it is generally hard to construct such a sparsified graph. An algorithm that finds such a sparsified graph
is called a spectral sparsifier.

While there has been many existing explorations on constructing spectral sparsifier of a given graph with high probability and tolerable time complexity
(
Spielman & Srivastava 2008
;
Spielman & Teng 2011
;
Koutis et al. 2016
)
, the setting considered in this paper is different from the standard one: instead of graph Laplacian
\bm{L}
, the matrix we concern is the normalized and regularized Laplacian
\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}
.

At first glance, a spectral sparsifier also works for our problem since
\tilde{{\bm{L}}}\approx_{\epsilon}\hat{\bm{L}}
implies
\tilde{{\bm{L}}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}\approx_{\epsilon}\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}
. However, it turns out that it is possible to make the bound even tighter if we take into account the regularization term
\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}
here. A similar argument is also made in
(
Calandriello et al. 2018
)
. We note that the setting explored in
(
Calandriello et al. 2018
)
is also different from ours: we consider the normalized Laplacian of a graph, which is not a Laplacian of any graph (even allowing weighted edges).

To this end, we propose a new spectral sparsifier that works for
\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}
which is used in our problem. This spectral sparsifier reduces the number of edges to
O\left(\frac{n_{\lambda}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log n\right)
, which, as we discussed before, is smaller than
O\left(\frac{n}{\epsilon^{2}}\log n\right)
obtained by standard spectral sparsifier.

Lemma 5.2

(Regularized spectral sparsifier)
.

Let
\hat{\bm{L}}
be a (normalized) graph Laplacian with
m
edges and
n
nodes. There exists a constant
C
such that for any
\epsilon>0
and
\lambda>0
,
Algorithm

2
outputs
a (normalized) graph Laplacian
\tilde{\bm{L}}
with
n
nodes and
O\left(\frac{n_{\lambda}}{\epsilon^{2}}\log n\right)
edges in time
\tilde{O}(m)
, such that

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}\approx_{\epsilon}\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}{\bm{I}}

(5.9)

holds with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{2n}
.

The basic idea behind
Lemma

5.2
is to use ridge leverage score sampling for the normalized incidence matrix
{\bm{B}}{\bm{D}}^{-1/2}

(
Cohen et al. 2017a
)
, which reduces the problem to estimating a regularized version of the effective resistance. We modify the existing methods of estimating effective resistance
(
Vishnoi et al. 2013a
)
to make it work for the normalized and regularized Laplacian.

5.4
Main Algorithm

Now we are ready to present our main algorithm HERTA, see
Algorithm

1
. As mentioned before, HERTA is composed of two major components: constructing a preconditioner matrix
{\bm{P}}
and applying it to the optimization problem. Below we introduce each part.

Constructing the Preconditioner.

For the outer problem which can be ill-conditioned, we first precondition it using a preconditioner
{\bm{P}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
which is a constant level spectral approximation of the Hessian, i.e.
{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-2}{\bm{X}}
. We claim that the matrix
{\bm{P}}
constructed inside
Algorithm

1
indeed satisfies this property.

Lemma 5.3

(Preconditioner)
.

Let
{\bm{P}}
be the matrix constructed in
Algorithm

1
. With probability at least
1-\frac{1}{n}
,

\displaystyle{\bm{P}}\approx_{\frac{1}{2}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-2}{\bm{X}}.

(5.10)

The training loss comparison between HERTA and standard optimizers on MSE loss with
\lambda=1
. Dataset used from left to right: ogbn-arxiv, citeseer, pubmed.

Solving the Outer Problem.

After obtaining the preconditioner
{\bm{P}}
which approximates the Hessian by a constant level, we use it to precondition the outer problem and provably reduce the condition number to a constant. With the new problem being well-conditioned, iterative methods (like gradient descent) take much less steps to converge.

Lemma 5.4

(Well-conditioned Hessian)
.

Let
\ell^{\prime}
be such that
\ell^{\prime}({\bm{w}}_{i})=\ell({\bm{P}}^{-1/2}{\bm{w}}_{i})
. Suppose for some constant
c_{0}\in(0,1)
we have
{\bm{P}}\approx_{c_{0}}{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-2}{\bm{X}}
, then we can bound the condition number of Hessian of
\ell^{\prime}
as

\displaystyle\kappa\left(\nabla^{2}\ell^{\prime}\right)\leq(1+c_{0})^{2}.

(5.11)

Moreover, if
{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
is a solution of
\ell^{\prime}
with
\epsilon
error rate, then
{\bm{P}}^{-1/2}{\bm{w}}^{\prime}
is a solution of
\ell
with
\epsilon
error rate.

Notice that, the problem
\ell^{\prime}
defined in
Lemma

5.4
can be viewed as the original problem
\ell
with
{\bm{X}}
being replaced by
{\bm{X}}{\bm{P}}^{-1/2}
, therefore we can use
Lemma

5.1
and obtain the convergence rate of HERTA. With the convergence result and analysis of the running time for each step, we are able to prove
Theorem

5.1
. See
Section

C.7
for the full proof.

Remark on the Unavoidable
\lambda^{2}
in Runtime.
Notice that
\lambda^{2}
appears in the time bound given in
Theorem

5.1
. From the proof in
Section

C.5
we can see that this term originates from the squared inverse of
{\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}
. In
Section

D.2
, we show that in the worst case even if we approximate a matrix to a very high precision, the approximation rate of the corresponding squared version can still be worse by a factor of the condition number of the matrix, which in our case leads to the unavoidable
\lambda^{2}
in the runtime.

Remark on Graph Sparsifying in Each Iteration.
Note that in
Algorithm

1
, we use the complete Laplacian
\hat{\bm{L}}
for gradient iterations (i.e., the for-loop), and the graph sampling only occurs when constructing the preconditioner
{\bm{P}}
. We note that sampling
\hat{\bm{L}}
in the for-loop can lead to extra loss in the gradient estimation, which forces us to sparsify the graph to a very high precision to ensure an accurate enough gradient for convergence. This could result in a suboptimal running time. Moreover, as discussed in
Section

1
, the current running time bound for HERTA is optimal up to logarithmic factors, which means that there is little to gain from performing extra sampling in for-loops. See
Section

D.3
for a more detailed and quantitative discussion.

Remark on the Surprising Effectiveness of HERTA on Cross Entropy Loss.

In
Section

6.2

The results show that the gradient under CE loss can be viewed as the gradient under MSE loss with one term being normalized by softmax, and the Hessian under CE loss can be viewed as a rescaled version of the Hessian under MSE loss. 7
Conclusions

In this paper we present HERTA: a High-Efficiency and Rigorous Training Algorithm that solves the problem of training Unfolded GNNs on a graph within a time essentially of the same magnitude as the time it takes to load the input data. As a component of HERTA, we also propose a new spectral sparsifier that works for normalized and regularized graph Laplacian matrices.

Experimental results on real world datasets show the effectiveness of HERTA. Moreover, it is shown that HERTA works for various loss functions and optimizers, despite being derived from a specific loss function and optimizer. This shows the universality of HERTA and verifies that it is ready to use in practice.

7.1
Future Directions

In this subsection, we briefly discuss some potential future directions to explore with HERTA.

Extending to more Complex Models.
In this paper, we stick to a rather straightforward implementation of TWIRLS.
Although it is verified in
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
that this implementation is already strong enough in many cases, under more general and subtle scenarios we might want to use more complex implementations of TWIRLS or other Unfolded GNN models. Therefore, it is critical to extend HERTA to those more complex settings. We discuss possible directions for implementing HERTA for more complex
f
as well as incorporating attention mechanisms in
Section

D.4
.

More Detailed Analysis of CE Loss and other Losses.
Although in this paper we provided an analysis explaining why HERTA can work on CE loss, finding a theoretically rigorous bound for CE loss as well as other loss functions is another important direction for future work.

Appendix A
Introduction to the Mathematical Tools

In the main paper, due to space limitations, our introduction of certain mathematical tools and concepts remains brief. In this section, we provide a more comprehensive introduction to the mathematical tools utilized in the main paper.

A.1
Subsampling

Subsampling is used in multiple places in our Algorithm. When we say subsampling
s
rows of a matrix
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
with probability
\{p_{k}\}_{k=1}^{n}
and obtain a new matrix
\tilde{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times d}
, it means each row of
\tilde{\bm{M}}
is an i.i.d. random vector which follows the same distribution of a random vector
{\bm{\xi}}
that satisfies the following property:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left\{{\bm{\xi}}=\sqrt{\frac{1}{sp_{k}}}{\bm{m}}_{k}\right\}=p_{k},

(A.1)

where
{\bm{m}}_{k}
is the
k
-th row of matrix
{\bm{M}}
. It is clear that constructing such a subsampled matrix
\tilde{\bm{M}}
takes time
O(sd)
. Moreover, if
p_{k}=\frac{1}{n}
, we say the subsampling is with uniform probability.

The operator of subsampling
s
rows of a
n
-row matrix is clearly a linear transformation. Therefore it can be represented by a random matrix
{\bm{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
. Notice that when we apply
{\bm{S}}
to a matrix (i.e.
{\bm{S}}\cdot{\bm{M}}
) we don’t actually need to construct such a matrix
{\bm{S}}
and calculate matrix product, since we only carry out sampling.

A.2
SDD Solvers

As mentioned in the main paper, for a sparse SDD matrix
{\bm{M}}
, it is possible to fast approximate its linear solver. The following
Lemma

A.1
rigorously states the bound we can obtain for SDD solvers.

Lemma A.1

(
Peng & Spielman 2014
)
.

For any SDD matrix
{\bm{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and any real number
\epsilon>0
, there exists an algorithm to construct an operator
\mathsf{SDDSolver}_{\epsilon}\left({\bm{M}};\cdot\right)
, such that
\mathsf{SDDSolver}_{\epsilon}\left({\bm{M}};\cdot\right)
is a linear solver for
{\bm{M}}
with
\epsilon
error rate and for any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, calculating
\mathsf{SDDSolver}_{\epsilon}({\bm{M}};{\bm{x}})
takes time

\displaystyle O\left(m\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\mathop{\mathrm{poly}}\log(m)\mathop{\mathrm{poly}}\log\left(\kappa({\bm{M}})\right)\right),

(A.2)

where
m=\mathrm{nnz}({\bm{M}})
.

A.3
Fast Matrix Multiplication

As mentioned in the main paper, we use SRHT to achieve fast matrix multiplication. Here we further explain this process. We refer interested readers to
(
Tropp 2011
)
for more details.
For a positive integer number
n
that is a power of
2
, the Hadamard transformation of size
n
is a linear transformation recursively defined as follows:

\displaystyle{\bm{H}}_{n}:=\begin{bmatrix}{\bm{H}}_{n/2}&{\bm{H}}_{n/2}\\
{\bm{H}}_{n/2}&-{\bm{H}}_{n/2}\end{bmatrix}

(A.3)

and
{\bm{H}}_{1}=1
. For a vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define

\displaystyle\mathsf{Hadamard}({\bm{x}}):=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}{\bm{H}}_{n}{\bm{x}}.

(A.4)

Notice that when
n
is not a power of
2
, we pad the vector
{\bm{x}}
with
0
-s beforehand, therefore the requirement of
n
being a power of
2
is ignored in the actual usage. From its recursive nature, it is not hard to see that applying a Hadamard transformation to each column of an
n\times d
matrix only takes
O(d\log n)
time.

Let
{\bm{R}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
be a diagonal matrix whose diagonal entries are i.i.d. Rademacher variables, and
{\bm{S}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times n}
be a subsampling matrix with uniform probability and
s=O\left(\frac{d}{\beta^{2}}\log n\right)
. For a matrix
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
, its SRHT with
\beta
error rate is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{SRHT}_{\beta}({\bm{Q}}):={\bm{S}}\mathsf{Hadamard}\left({\bm{R}}{\bm{Q}}\right),

(A.5)

which is exactly the matrix
\tilde{\bm{Q}}
we use in
Algorithm

1
. A fast matrix multiplication result can be achieved using SRHT.

Lemma A.2

(
Tropp 2011
)
.

For matrix
{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}
where
n\geq d
and
\mathrm{rank}(\bm{Q})=d
, let
\tilde{\bm{Q}}=\mathsf{SRHT}_{\beta}({\bm{Q}})
where
\beta\in(0,1/4)
, then we have

\displaystyle\tilde{\bm{Q}}^{\top}\tilde{\bm{Q}}\approx_{\beta}{\bm{Q}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}

(A.6)

with probability at least
1-\frac{1}{2n}
.

Appendix B

(

)

In this section we analyze the time complexity of the implementation used in
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
, which uses gradient descent to solve both inner and outer problem.

Inner Problem Analysis.

We first analyze the time complexity of the inner problem solver used in
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
. Recall that for the inner problem
eq.

4.3
, we need to find an approximation of the linear solver of
{\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}
. In
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
, it is implemented by a standard gradient descent. Here we consider approximately solving the following least square problem by gradient descent:

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}=\arg\min_{{\bm{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right){\bm{v}}-{\bm{u}}\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(B.1)

The gradient step with step size
\mu
is:

\displaystyle{\bm{v}}^{(t+1)}=(1-\mu)\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{2}{\bm{v}}^{(t)}+\eta\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right){\bm{u}}.

(B.2)

Theorem B.1

(Inner Analysis)
.

If we update
{\bm{v}}^{(t)}
through
eq.

B.2
with initialization
{\bm{v}}^{(0)}={\bm{0}}
and a proper
\eta
, then after
T=O\left(\lambda^{2}\log\frac{{\lambda}}{\epsilon^{2}}\right)
iterations we can get

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{v}}^{(T)}-\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-1}{\bm{u}}\right\|_{({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}})}\leq\epsilon\left\|\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-1}{\bm{u}}\right\|_{({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}})}

(B.3)

for any
\epsilon\in(0,1)
.

Proof.

Let
{\bm{H}}={\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}
. Notice that the strongly convexity and Lipschitz constant of Problem
eq.

B.1
is
\mathscr{C}=1
and
L\leq 9\lambda^{2}
respectively.
From
Lemma

C.1
, take
\eta=\frac{1}{L}
and
{\bm{v}}^{(0)}={\bm{0}}
, we have

\displaystyle\|{\bm{H}}{\bm{v}}^{(T)}-{\bm{u}}\|^{2}\leq(1-9\lambda^{2})^{T}\|{\bm{u}}\|^{2}

(B.4)

for any
T\in\mathbb{N}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\frac{\|{\bm{v}}^{(T)}-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}}}{\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{u}}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\|{\bm{H}}{\bm{v}}^{(T)}-{\bm{u}}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}^{-1}}}{\|{\bm{u}}\|^{2}_{{\bm{H}}^{-1}}}

(B.5)

\displaystyle\leq 3\lambda\frac{\|{\bm{H}}{\bm{v}}^{(T)}-{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}}{\|{\bm{u}}\|_{2}^{2}}

(B.6)

\displaystyle\leq 3\lambda(1-9\lambda^{2})^{{T}}.

(B.7)

To obtain an error rate
\epsilon
, the smallest number of iterations
T
needed is

\displaystyle T=\left(\log\frac{1}{1-(9\lambda^{2})^{-1}}\right)^{-1}\log\frac{{3\lambda}}{\epsilon^{2}}+1=O\left[\left(\log\frac{1}{1-\lambda^{-2}}\right)^{-1}\log\frac{\kappa}{\epsilon^{2}}\right]=O\left(\lambda^{2}\log\frac{\lambda}{\epsilon^{2}}\right).

(B.8)

∎

Overall Running Time.

As we proved above in
Theorem

B.1
, the number of iterations needed for solving the inner problem to error rate
\beta
with the implementation of
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
is
\tilde{O}\left(\lambda^{2}\log\frac{1}{\beta}\right)
, which is related to the hyper-parameter
\lambda
. For each inner iteration (i.e.
eq.

B.2
), we need to compute
({\bm{I}}+\lambda{\bm{L}})({\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}-\hat{\bm{y}})
. Since this is a sparse matrix multiplication, the complexity of this step is
O(m+nd)
. Putting things together, we have the time complexity of calling inner problem solver is
\tilde{O}\left((m+nd)\lambda^{2}\log\frac{1}{\beta}\right)
.

From
Lemma

5.1
, the number of outer iterations needed is
\tilde{O}\left(\kappa_{o}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)
, where
\kappa_{o}
is the condition number of the outer problem. Moreover,
Lemma

5.1
also indicates that we require
\beta\leq\frac{\epsilon^{1/2}}{25\kappa({\bm{X}})\lambda}
, so solving inner problem takes
\tilde{O}(\lambda^{2}\log 1/\beta)=\tilde{O}(\lambda^{2}\log 1/\epsilon)
. In each outer iteration, we need to call inner problem solver constant times, and as well
as performing constant matrix vector multiplications whose complexity is
O(nd)
. Therefore the overall running time of solving the training problem is
\tilde{O}\left[\kappa_{o}\left(\lambda^{2}(m+nd)+nd\right)\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}\right]
.

Proof of Theoretical Results

We first note that since

are in the range

C.1
Descent Lemma

In this subsection, we introduce a descent lemma that we will use to analyze the convergence rate. This is a standard result in convex optimization, and we refer interested readers to
(
Bottou et al. 2018
)
for more details.

Lemma C.1
.

If
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is
L
-Lipschitz smooth and
c
-strongly convex, and we have a sequence of points
\left\{{\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}^{(t+1)}={\bm{w}}^{(t)}-\eta{\bm{g}}^{(t)},

(C.1)

where
\left\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}-\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\gamma\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}
and
\gamma<1
,
\eta=\frac{1-\gamma}{(1+\gamma)^{2}L}
, then we have

\displaystyle\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(T)}\right)-\ell^{*}\leq\left[1-\kappa^{-1}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\right)^{2}\right]^{T}\left[\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(0)}\right)-\ell^{*}\right],

(C.2)

where
\ell^{*}=\inf_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell({\bm{w}})
and
\kappa=\frac{L}{c}
.

Proof.

From the condition
\left\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}-\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq\gamma\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}
, we have that

\displaystyle(1-\gamma)\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}\|_{2}\leq\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}\leq(1+\gamma)\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}\|_{2}

(C.3)

and

\displaystyle-2\left<g^{(t)},\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right>

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}-\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}\right\|_{2}^{2}-\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.4)

\displaystyle\leq\left[(\gamma^{2}-1)-(1-\gamma)^{2}\right]\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.5)

\displaystyle=2(\gamma-1)\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.6)

From Lipschitz smoothness, we have

\displaystyle\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t+1)}\right)-\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)

\displaystyle\leq-\eta\left<{\bm{g}}^{(t)},\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right>+\frac{1}{2}L\eta^{2}\|{\bm{g}}^{(t)}\|_{2}^{2}

(C.7)

\displaystyle\leq\left(\eta(\gamma-1)+\frac{1}{2}L\eta^{2}(1+\gamma)^{2}\right)\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}.

(C.8)

It’s not hard to show that the optimal
\eta
for
eq.

C.8
is
\eta=\frac{1-\gamma}{L(1+\gamma)^{2}}
. Substituting
\eta=\frac{1-\gamma}{L(1+\gamma)^{2}}
to
eq.

C.8
and use convexity we can get

\displaystyle\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t+1)}\right)-\ell^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left[\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)-\ell^{*}\right]-\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}}{2L(1+\gamma)^{2}}\left\|\nabla\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.9)

\displaystyle\leq\left[\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)-\ell^{*}\right]-\frac{c(1-\gamma)^{2}}{L(1+\gamma)^{2}}\left[\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(t)}\right)-\ell^{*}\right]

(C.10)

By induction we have

\displaystyle\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(T)}\right)-\ell^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left[1-\kappa^{-1}\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\right)^{2}\right]^{T}\left[\ell\left({\bm{w}}^{(0)}\right)-\ell^{*}\right].

(C.11)

∎

C.2
Bound of Loss Value by Gradient

In this subsection, we derive an inequality that allows us to bound the value of loss function by the norm of gradient.

Lemma C.2
.

If
\ell:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a
\mathscr{C}
-strongly convex and smooth function, and
{\bm{w}}^{*}=\arg\min_{{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\ell({\bm{w}})
is a global optimal, then for any
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, we have

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{w}})\leq\max\left\{(1+\epsilon)\ell({\bm{w}}^{*}),\frac{1}{\epsilon\mathscr{C}}\|\nabla\ell({\bm{w}})\|^{2}\right\}.

(C.12)

Proof.

Using the inequality (4.12) from
(
Bottou et al. 2018
)
, we have

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{w}})\leq\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}\|\nabla\ell({\bm{w}})\|^{2}+\ell({\bm{w}}^{*}).

(C.13)

If
\ell({\bm{w}})\geq(1+\epsilon)\ell({\bm{w}}^{*})
, we have

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{w}})\leq\frac{1}{2\mathscr{C}}\|\nabla\ell({\bm{w}})\|^{2}+\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\ell({\bm{w}}).

(C.14)

Shifting the terms in
eq.

C.14
gives
\ell({\bm{w}})\leq\frac{1+\epsilon}{2\mathscr{C}\epsilon}\|\nabla\ell({\bm{w}})\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon\mathscr{C}}\|\nabla\ell({\bm{w}})\|^{2}
, which proves the claim.

∎

C.3
Proof of
Lemma

5.1

Given a point
{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we first consider the error between the estimated gradient
\widetilde{\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})}
and the true gradient
\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})
. Below we denote
{\bm{H}}={\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}
and
{\bm{z}}={\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}-\hat{\bm{y}}_{i}
. It’s not hard to notice that
\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right\|^{2}
and
\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}
. Moreover, we denote
\ell^{*}_{i}=\inf_{{\bm{w}}}\ell^{*}_{i}
as the optimal value of
\ell_{i}
.

we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})}-\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\right\|=

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}+\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{S}({\bm{z}}))-\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)\right]\right\|

(C.15)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right]\right\|+\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{S}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}})\right]\right\|.

(C.16)

As shown, the gradient error can be decomposed into two terms, namely
\left\|\widetilde{\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})}-\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\right\|=E_{1}+E_{2}
, where
E_{1}=\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right]\right\|
and
E_{2}=\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{S}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}})\right]\right\|
. Below we analyze each part separately.

Notice that, as we assumed
{\bm{X}}
is full-rank,
\ell_{i}
is a strongly convex function with parameter
\mathscr{C}=\sigma_{\min}\left({\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{X}}\right)\geq\frac{\sigma_{\min}({\bm{X}})^{2}}{9\lambda^{2}}
. Let
\mathcal{D}=\left\{\left.{\bm{w}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\right|\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\leq(1+\epsilon)\ell_{i}^{*}\right\}
. If
{\bm{w}}\in\mathcal{D}
, then
{\bm{w}}
is already a good enough solution. Below we assume
{\bm{w}}\not\in\mathcal{D}
, in which case by
Lemma

C.2
, we have
\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon\mathscr{C}}\|\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\|^{2}
, which means

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{2}{\mathscr{C}\epsilon}\left\|{\bm{X}}^{-1}{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|^{2}\leq\frac{18\lambda^{2}}{\sigma_{\min}({\bm{X}})^{2}\epsilon}.

(C.17)

For
E_{1}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{1}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right]\right\|_{2}

(C.18)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}({\bm{X}})\left\|\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}

(C.19)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}({{\bm{X}}})\left\|\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{{\bm{H}}}

(C.20)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}({{\bm{X}}})\mu\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{{\bm{H}}}

(C.21)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}({{\bm{X}}})\mu\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}

(C.22)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{18}{\epsilon}}\kappa({{\bm{X}}})\lambda\mu\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}

(C.23)

\displaystyle\leq 5\epsilon^{-1/2}\kappa({{\bm{X}}})\lambda\mu\|\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\|_{2}.

(C.24)

For
E_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle E_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{S}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}})\right]\right\|_{2}

(C.25)

\displaystyle\leq{\sigma_{\max}({\bm{X}})}(1+\mu)\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}\left(\mathcal{S}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\right)\|_{{\bm{H}}}

(C.26)

\displaystyle\leq{\sigma_{\max}({\bm{X}})}(1+\mu)\|\mathcal{S}({\bm{z}})-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\|_{{\bm{H}}}

(C.27)

\displaystyle\leq{\sigma_{\max}({\bm{X}})}(1+\mu)\mu\|{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{z}}\|_{{\bm{H}}}

(C.28)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{18}{\epsilon}}\kappa({\bm{X}})(1+\mu)\mu\lambda\sqrt{3\lambda}\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\|_{2}

(C.29)

\displaystyle\leq 20\epsilon^{-1/2}\kappa({\bm{X}})\mu\lambda^{2}\|\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}}_{i})\|_{2}.

(C.30)

Combine the bound of
E_{1}
and
E_{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}}_{i})}-\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}}_{i})\right\|\leq E_{1}+E_{2}\leq 25\epsilon^{-1/2}\kappa({\bm{X}})\mu\lambda^{2}\|\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}}_{i})\|_{2}.

(C.31)

Now, consider the optimization. Let
\left\{{\bm{w}}_{i}^{(t)}\right\}_{t=1}^{T}
be a sequence such that

\displaystyle{\bm{w}}_{i}^{(t+1)}={\bm{w}}^{(t)}-\eta\widetilde{\nabla\ell_{i}\left({\bm{w}}_{i}^{(t)}\right)}.

(C.32)

Let
\kappa
be the condition number of
{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{X}}
and let
\ell_{i}^{*}
be the optimal value of
\ell_{i}
. Let
\gamma=25\epsilon^{-1/2}\kappa({\bm{X}})\lambda^{2}\mu\leq\frac{1}{2}
. From
Lemma

C.1
, we have with a proper value of
\eta
,

\displaystyle\ell_{i}\left({\bm{w}}_{i}^{(T)}\right)-\ell^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{1+\gamma}\right)^{2}\kappa^{-1}\right)^{T}\left[\ell_{i}\left({\bm{w}}_{i}^{(0)}\right)-\ell_{i}^{*}\right]

(C.33)

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-(9\kappa)^{-1}\right)^{T}\left[\ell_{i}\left({\bm{w}}_{i}^{(0)}\right)-\ell_{i}^{*}\right].

(C.34)

Therefore, the number of iterations
T
required to achieve
\epsilon
error rate is

\displaystyle T=O\left(\left(\log\frac{1}{1-(9\kappa)^{-1}}\right)^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)=O\left(\kappa\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right).

(C.35)

Proof of

The basic idea of

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle~\hat{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\top}(\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{L}}(\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}_{i}+\lambda^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}_{i}^{\top}(\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{I})^{-2}\hat{\bm{b}}_{i}

\displaystyle~\|\hat{\bm{B}}(\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}_{i}\|^{2}+\lambda^{-1}\|(\hat{\bm{L}}+\lambda^{-1}\bm{I})^{-1}\hat{\bm{b}}_{i}\|^{2}.

We first prove a lemma which is related to the approximation rate of squared matrices.

Lemma C.5
.

Suppose that
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
are two
n\times n
PD matrices, and
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\approx_{\frac{\beta}{\kappa}}{\bm{\Sigma}}
, where
\kappa
is the condition number of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and
\beta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{8}\right)
, then we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}\approx_{8\beta}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}.

(C.46)

Proof.

Let
\epsilon=\frac{\beta}{\kappa}
.
The condition
{\bm{\Sigma}}\approx_{\epsilon}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
implies that
\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right\|\leq\epsilon\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}\right\|
. We have

\displaystyle\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right){\bm{x}}\right\|

\displaystyle=\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right){\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|

(C.47)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right){\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|\times\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|

(C.48)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)\right\|\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|\times\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|

(C.49)

\displaystyle\leq\epsilon\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}\right\|\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}^{-1}\right\|\times\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|

(C.50)

\displaystyle=\epsilon\kappa\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|

(C.51)

\displaystyle=\beta\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|.

(C.52)

For any
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, by triangle inequality we have

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|-\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right){\bm{x}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|\leq\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|+\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right){\bm{x}}\right\|.

(C.53)

Subtracting the inequality
\left\|\left({\bm{\Sigma}}-\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right){\bm{x}}\right\|\leq\beta\left\|{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\right\|
we derived before into
eq.

C.53
and squaring all sides, we have

\displaystyle(1-\beta)^{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}{\bm{x}}\leq{\bm{x}}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}{\bm{x}}\leq(1+\beta)^{2}{\bm{x}}^{\top}{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}{\bm{x}}.

(C.54)

Since
0<\beta<\frac{1}{8}
, the claim is proved.
∎

Let
{\bm{T}}={\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-2}{\bm{X}}
be the true Hessian. Let
\tilde{\bm{T}}={\bm{X}}^{\top}\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\tilde{\bm{L}}\right)^{-2}{\bm{X}}

\tilde{\bm{L}}

\tilde{\bm{T}}\approx_{8\beta}{\bm{T}}
.

Next, we show that

\tilde{\bm{H}}={\bm{I}}+\lambda\tilde{\bm{L}}

\mathcal{S}

where
{\bm{q}}_{j}

j

{\bm{Q}}
and

respectively. From

\displaystyle\left\|{\bm{Q}}{\bm{z}}-\tilde{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{X}}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}\left(\mathcal{S}({\bm{x}}_{j})-\tilde{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.55)

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{S}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right)-\tilde{\bm{H}}\left(\sum_{j=1}^{d}z_{j}{\bm{x}}_{j}\right)\right\|_{2}^{2}

(C.56)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\beta^{2}}{3\lambda}\left\|\tilde{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{X}}{\bm{z}}\right\|_{\tilde{\bm{H}}}^{2}

(C.57)

\displaystyle\leq\beta^{2}\|\tilde{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{X}}{\bm{z}}\|_{2}^{2}.

(C.58)

Therefore we have

\displaystyle(1-\beta)\|\tilde{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{X}}{\bm{z}}\|_{2}\leq\|{\bm{Q}}{\bm{z}}\|\leq(1+\beta)\|\tilde{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{X}}{\bm{z}}\|_{2},

Proof of

5.1

As we noted in the main paper,
Algorithm

1
is composed by two components: constructing the preconditioner
{\bm{P}}
and applying it to the optimization.

Constructing the Preconditioner.

From
Lemma

5.2
, the first step that applies spectral sparsifier to get
\tilde{\bm{L}}
requires
\tilde{O}(m)
time and the number of non-zero entries in
\tilde{\bm{L}}
is
\tilde{O}\left(n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}\right)
. By
Lemma

A.1
, running the SDD solver with error rate
\frac{\beta}{3\lambda}
in the second step requires
\tilde{O}\left({n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}}\right)
time. Notice that since
{\bm{X}}
is an
n\times d
matrix, we actually need to run SDD solver for
d
times, and this introduces another
d
factor in the time complexity.

As we noted in
Section

A.3
, applying the Hadamard transformation to
{\bm{R}}{\bm{Q}}
requires
\tilde{O}(d)
time, and applying the subsampling requires
\tilde{O}(n)
time. Since
\tilde{\bm{Q}}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times d}
, calculating
{\bm{P}}=\tilde{\bm{Q}}^{\top}{\bm{Q}}
requires
O(d^{2}s)
time. Since

, this step takes
\tilde{O}(d^{3})
time. We use brute force to calculate
{\bm{P}}^{\prime}={\bm{P}}^{-1/2}
, and this takes
O(d^{3})
time.

As a summary, constructing the preconditioner
{\bm{P}}^{\prime}
takes
\tilde{O}(n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d+nd+d^{3})
time. Notice that we only perform this step once during the whole algorithm.

Performing the Iterations.

Next we consider the time required for each iteration. We calculate
{\bm{u}}^{(t)}
from right to left and it takes

time. Next, we need to perform two SDD solvers with error rate
\mu
. From

, it takes

. Calculating

and

To conclude, performing each iteration requires

5.1
and

5.3
, with a proper step size, the number of iterations needed for solving outer problem is

.

Combining the analysis above together, to overall complexity is

\displaystyle\tilde{O}\left[n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d+nd+d^{3}+\left(m\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)+nd\right)\log\left(\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)\right]=\tilde{O}\left(n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d+d^{3}+(m+nd)\left(\log 1/\epsilon\right)^{2}\right),

D.1
An Analysis of CE Loss v.s. MSE Loss

Although HERTA is derived from MSE loss, the experiment result shows it also works on CE loss. In this subsection we provide an analysis showing the similarity of the gradient and Hessian of TWIRLS on CE loss and MSE loss, to offer a intuitive explanation why a method that is derived from MSE loss can work on CE loss.

In this section, for a node
u\in\{1,2,\cdots,n\}
, we use
{\bm{y}}^{(u)}\in\mathbb{R}^{c}
to represent the
u
-th row of
{\bm{Y}}
(notice we use super-script here to distinguish from
{\bm{y}}_{i}
used before),
{\bm{h}}_{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to represent the
u
-th row of
\left({\bm{I}}+\lambda\hat{\bm{L}}\right)^{-1}
. For
p\in\{1,2,\cdots d\}
, we use
{\bm{x}}_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
to denote the
p
-th column of
{\bm{X}}
. For
i\in\{1,2,\cdots c\}
, we use
y^{(u)}_{i}
to denote the
i
-th entry of
{\bm{y}}^{(u)}
(or in other words the
(u,i)
-th entry of
{\bm{Y}}
), and
w_{p,i}
to denote the
(p,i)
-th entry of
{\bm{W}}
.

The MSE loss of TWIRLS defined in
eq.

4.7
can be decomposed into summation of sub-losses of each node, i.e.

\displaystyle\ell({\bm{W}})=\sum_{u=1}^{n}\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}-{\bm{y}}^{(u)}\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}=\sum_{u=1}^{n}\ell^{(u)}\left({\bm{W}}\right),

(D.1)

where
\ell^{(u)}({\bm{W}})=\frac{1}{2}\left\|{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}-{\bm{y}}^{(u)}\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{2}
. For a specific class
i\in\{1,2,\cdots,c\}
,
we have the gradient of
\ell^{(u)}
w.r.t.
{\bm{w}}_{i}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell^{(u)}}{\partial{\bm{w}}_{i}}={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}{\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{i}-{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}\times{{y}}^{(u)}_{i}.

(D.2)

It is not hard to see from

D.2
that the Hessian of
\ell^{(u)}
with respect to

is

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{w}}_{i}}\ell^{(u)}\left({\bm{W}}\right)={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}{\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}.

(D.3)

For classification tasks, the target
{\bm{y}}^{(u)}
-s are one-hot vectors representing the class of the node. Notice when we calculate cross entropy loss, we use
\mathsf{softmax}
to normalize it before feeding it into the loss function. In the following for a vector

whose
i
-th entry is

\displaystyle\mathsf{softmax}({\bm{v}})_{i}=\frac{\exp(v_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp(v_{j})}.

(D.4)

Suppose the
u
-th node belongs to class
k

u
-th node is defined as

\displaystyle\mathsf{CE}^{(u)}({\bm{W}})

\displaystyle=-\log\mathsf{softmax}\left({\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}\right)_{k}

(D.5)

\displaystyle=-{\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{k}+\log\sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp\left({\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{j}\right),

(D.6)

The gradient of

\displaystyle=-{\bm{\delta}}_{i,k}\times{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}_{u}}+\frac{{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}\exp\left({\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{i}\right)}{\sum_{j=1}^{c}\exp\left({\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{w}}_{j}\right)}

(D.7)

\displaystyle={\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}\mathrm{softmax}\left({\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}\right)_{i}-{\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}\times{{y}}_{i}^{(u)}.

By taking another partial differentiation, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}\mathsf{CE}^{(u)}}{\partial w_{p,i}\partial w_{q,i}}

(D.9)

\displaystyle=\left({\bm{x}}_{p}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}\right)\left({\bm{x}}_{q}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}\right)\left[s_{i}-s_{i}^{2}\right],

(D.10)

where

. Rewriting

D.10

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}_{{\bm{w}}_{i}}\mathsf{CE}^{(u)}({\bm{W}})=(s_{i}-s_{i}^{2}){\bm{X}}^{\top}{\bm{h}}_{u}{\bm{h}}_{u}^{\top}{\bm{X}}.

(D.11)

Comparing

D.2

D.8

A More General Construction.

For a PD matrix
{\bm{\Sigma}}
and its spectral approximation
\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}
, we call
\frac{\psi\left({\bm{\Sigma}}^{2},\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}^{2}\right)}{\psi\left({\bm{\Sigma}},\widetilde{\bm{\Sigma}}\right)}
the Squared Error Rate. As noted above, in the worst case the squared error rate can be as large as the condition number of
{\bm{\Sigma}}
. However, the construction above is limited to
2\times 2
matrices. Now we construct a more general worst case of the squared error rate and perform a loose analysis. Although not rigorously proved, the construction and the analysis suggest the origination of large squared error rates: it approaches the upper bound (condition number) when the eigenspace of the two matrices are very well aligned but not exactly the same.

Let
{\bm{A}}
be an ill-conditioned matrix with all eigenvalues very large except one eigenvalue equals to
1
, and the eigenvalues of
{\bm{B}}
are all closed to the eigenvalues of
{\bm{A}}
. Specifically, Let the SVD of
{\bm{A}}
be
{\bm{A}}={\bm{U}}\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\lambda}}){\bm{U}}^{\top}
, where
{\bm{\lambda}}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}&\lambda_{2}&\cdots\lambda_{n}\end{bmatrix}
, and suppose
\lambda_{n}=1
and
\lambda_{k}\gg 1,\forall k\leq n-1
. For simplicity we just let
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
have the same eigenvalues. Suppose the SVD of
{\bm{B}}
is
{\bm{B}}={\bm{V}}\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\lambda}}){\bm{V}}^{\top}
and let
{\bm{\Lambda}}=\mathrm{diag}({\bm{\lambda}})
. We denote the one sided error rate of
{\bm{A}}
and
{\bm{B}}
by
\epsilon
, i.e.
\epsilon=\left\|{\bm{A}}^{-1/2}{\bm{B}}{\bm{A}}^{-1/2}\right\|
. We have

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}{\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{V}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{V}}^{\top}{\bm{U}}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}\right\|

(D.15)

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}\right\|,

(D.16)

where
{\bm{W}}={\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{V}}
.

Since
\lambda_{n}=1
and for all
k\leq n-1
,
\lambda_{k}\gg 1
, we have

\displaystyle{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-\frac{1}{2}}=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_{1}^{-1/2}&&&\\
&\lambda_{2}^{-\frac{1}{2}}&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&\lambda_{n}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\end{bmatrix}\approx\begin{bmatrix}0&&&\\
&0&&\\
&&\ddots&\\
&&&1\end{bmatrix},

(D.17)

and therefore

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}{\bm{W}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{W}}^{\top}{\bm{\Lambda}}^{-1/2}\right\|\approx({\bm{W}}{\bm{\Lambda}}{\bm{W}}^{\top})_{n,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bm{W}}_{k,n}^{2}\lambda_{k},

(D.18)

{\bm{A}}_{i,j}
represents the
(i,j)

{\bm{A}}

For simplicity, we assume all
\lambda_{k}(k\leq n-1)
are approximate equal, i.e.
\lambda_{1}\approx\lambda_{2}\cdots\approx\lambda_{n-1}=\gamma
. Then we have

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}{\bm{W}}_{k,n}^{2}\lambda_{k}

(D.19)

\displaystyle\approx\gamma\left[\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\bm{W}}_{k,n}^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}{\bm{W}}_{n,n}^{2}\right]

(D.20)

\displaystyle\approx\gamma\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}{\bm{W}}_{k,n}^{2}

(D.21)

\displaystyle=\gamma\left(1-{\bm{W}}_{n,n}^{2}\right).

(D.22)

Recall
{\bm{W}}={\bm{U}}^{\top}{\bm{V}}
, we have
{\bm{W}}_{n,n}={\bm{u}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{n}
, where
{\bm{u}}_{n}
and
{\bm{v}}_{n}
are the
n
-th column of
{\bm{U}}
and
{\bm{V}}
respectively. Thus we have

\epsilon\approx\gamma\left[1-\left({\bm{u}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{n}\right)^{2}\right].

(D.23)

Now we can see the spectral approximation error
\epsilon

\gamma

\left[1-\left({\bm{u}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]

When
\gamma

\left[1-\left({\bm{u}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{n}\right)^{2}\right]

{\bm{B}}

{\bm{A}}
. For example if
(1-{\bm{u}}_{n}^{\top}{\bm{v}}_{n})\approx\gamma^{-1}
, we can get a spectral approximation error
\epsilon\approx 1
. However, after we square the matrices, the eigenvalues will also get squared, but eigenvectors remains unchanged. That will enlarge the spectral approximation error by a factor of
\gamma
, i.e.
\left\|{\bm{A}}^{-1}{\bm{B}}^{2}{\bm{A}}^{-1}\right\|\approx\gamma^{2}\gamma^{-1}=\gamma
, which becomes very large. In this case the squared error rate is
\gamma
, the condition number of
{\bm{A}}
.

D.3
Applying Graph Sparsification in Each Iteration

As mentioned in the main paper, unlike most existing work, in our algorithm we don’t sparsify the graph in each training iteration. The proof of
Lemma

5.1
(see
Section

C.3
) suggests the reason why performing graph sparsification in each iteration can lead to suboptimal running time. In this subsection we illustrate this claim in detail. The intuition is that, in order to ensure convergence of the training, we require a small error rate in the gradient estimation, which is of the order
\epsilon^{1/2}
as we have showed in
Section

C.3
. It is acceptable for the SDD solver because the running time of the SDD solver only logarithmly depends on the error rate. However, if we sparsify the graph, to obtain an
\epsilon^{1/2}
error rate we will need to sample
O(n_{\lambda}/\epsilon)
edges, which grows linearly with
1/\epsilon
, and can be large especially when
\epsilon
is very small. Below is a more detailed analysis.

Consider in the for-loop of
Algorithm

1
we replace the
\hat{\bm{L}}
by a sparsified version
{\bm{L}}^{\prime}=\mathsf{Sparsify}_{\omega}\left(\hat{\bm{L}}\right)
, where
\omega\in(0,1)
. Let
{\bm{H}}^{\prime}={\bm{I}}+\lambda{\bm{L}}^{\prime}
. From
Lemma

5.2
, we have
{\bm{H}}^{\prime}\approx_{\omega}{\bm{H}}
. Consider
E_{1}
defined in
Section

C.3
, it now becomes

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left\|{\bm{X}}^{\top}\left[\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{\prime-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right]\right\|

(D.24)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\left\|\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{\prime-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|.

(D.25)

Here we can not proceed by using the fact that
\mathcal{S}
is a linear solver, because now
\mathcal{S}
is not a linear solver for
{\bm{H}}
but for
{\bm{H}}^{\prime}
, thus we will have to split the error term again:

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\prime}\leq\sigma_{\max}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\left\|\mathcal{S}\left({\bm{H}}^{\prime-1}{\bm{z}}\right)-{\bm{H}}^{\prime-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|+\sigma_{\max}({\bm{X}})\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}+{\bm{H}}^{\prime-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|.

(D.26)

Of the two terms on the right-hand side of
eq.

D.26
, the first one can be bounded with a similar method used in
Section

C.3
, and the second term is bounded by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\max}({\bm{X}})\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}-{\bm{H}}^{\prime-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\left\|I-{\bm{H}}^{-1}{\bm{H}}^{\prime-2}{\bm{H}}^{-1}\right\|\|{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\|

(D.27)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma_{\max}\left({\bm{X}}\right)\omega\left\|{\bm{H}}^{-2}{\bm{z}}\right\|

(D.28)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\frac{8}{\epsilon}}\kappa({\bm{X}})\lambda\omega\left\|\nabla\ell_{i}\left({\bm{w}}\right)\right\|.

(D.29)

Therefore, in order to obtain
E_{1}^{\prime}\leq\|\nabla\ell_{i}({\bm{w}})\|
, we at least require
\omega\leq\frac{\epsilon^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{8}\kappa({\bm{X}})\lambda}=O\left(\epsilon^{-1/2}\right)
. From
Lemma

5.2
, the number of edges in the sparsified graph
\omega
error rate is
O(n_{\lambda}/\epsilon)
. A similar analysis can be also applied to
E_{2}
, and by repeating the proof in
Section

C.3
, we obtain an overall running time bound

\displaystyle O(m+n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d+d^{3}+n_{\lambda}\epsilon^{-1}\log 1/\epsilon),

(D.30)

which, although eliminates the
m\left(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right)^{2}
term, introduces an extra
n_{\lambda}\epsilon^{-1}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}
term, and is usually worse than the original bound we derived in
Theorem

5.1
, especially when requiring a relatively small
\epsilon
.

That being said, although not very likely, when
\epsilon
and / or
\lambda
are large, it is possible that directly sparsifying the graph in each iteration is beneficial. Considering this, we can adopt a mixed strategy: when
n_{\lambda}\epsilon^{-1}\leq m\log(1/\epsilon)
, we sparsify the graph in each iteration, elsewise we don’t. This leads to the following overall running time bound:

\displaystyle O\left[m+n_{\lambda}\lambda^{2}d+d^{3}+\min\left\{m\log\frac{1}{\epsilon},n_{\lambda}\epsilon^{-1}\right\}\log\frac{1}{\epsilon}\right],

(D.31)

which is slightly better than the one we presented in
Theorem

5.1
.

D.4
Possible Directions for Extending HERTA to More Complex Models

The assumption that
f({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}})={\bm{X}}{\bm{W}}
provides us with a convenience that the Hessian of this model is a constant matrix. Therefore in
Algorithm

1
we only need to calculate the preconditioner
{\bm{P}}
for one time. However, if
f({\bm{X}};{\bm{W}})
is implemented by a non-linear network, then the Hessian will change by the time and might be hard to calculate, which will be a key challenge to using a more complex
f
. We note that this can be possibly addressed by constructing a linear approximation of
f
using its Jaccobian. In each iteration, we can use the Jaccobian to replace the
{\bm{X}}
used in current version of HERTA, and recalculate
{\bm{P}}
at each iteration. Since the convergence is fast, we only need to recalculate the Jaccobian for a small number of iterations, so should not bring massive change to the running time of the algorithm.

The attention mechanism of TWIRLS in
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
is achieved by adding a concave penalty function to each summand of the the
\mathrm{Tr}\left({\bm{Z}}^{\top}\hat{\bm{L}}{\bm{Z}}\right)
term in
eq.

4.1
. For specific penalty functions, it might still possible to find a inner problem solver, as long as the problem stay convex. We note that this depends on concrete implementation of the penalty term used. Investigating how to fast solve the inner problem under various penalty functions should also be an important problem for future study.

Appendix E
Implementation Details

In the experiments, the datasets are loaded and processed using the DGL package
(
Wang et al. 2019
)
. We use the original inner problem solver in
(
Yang et al. 2021b
)
since it is not computation bottleneck. It can be in principle replaced by any implementation of SDD solvers.

For the calculating the gradient of the preconditioned model, we presented a calculation method in
Algorithm

1
which maintains the lowest computational complexity. In preliminary experiments, we tested this calculation method with using the autograd module in pytorch
4
4

4

https://pytorch.org/
and verified that they have the same output, and similar computational efficiency on real world datasets (again in practice this step is not a bottleneck). Therefore, we simply use pytorch autograd module to compute gradients in experiments.

Using the pytorch autograd module also enables us to apply HERTA on various loss functions and optimizers: we only need to perform the preconditioning and indicate the loss function. The gradient and optimization algorithm with be automatically realized by pytorch.

In order to ensure a fair comparison and prevent confounding factors, we don’t using any common training regularization techniques like weight decay or dropout. For each setting, we repeat the experiment with learning rates in
\{0.001,0.01,0.1,1,10\}
and choose the trial which the training loss does not explode and with the lowest final training loss to report in the main paper.
