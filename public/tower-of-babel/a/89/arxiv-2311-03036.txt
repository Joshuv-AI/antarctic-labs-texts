On regularized polynomial functional regression
Markus Holzleitner, Sergei V. Pereverzyev

Abstract
This article offers a comprehensive treatment of polynomial functional regression, culminating in the establishment of a novel finite sample bound. This bound encompasses various aspects, including general smoothness conditions, capacity conditions, and regularization techniques. In doing so, it extends and generalizes several findings from the context of linear functional regression as well. We also provide numerical evidence that using higher order polynomial terms can lead to an improved performance.



1
Introduction

Functional data are frequently used and cover a wide range of applications, e.g. in engineering, econometrics, health-care, medical images, just to name a few. These data are usually given in the form of functions, time series, shapes, or more general objects. Specific examples include traffic flow data, weather data, functional magnetic imaging resonance data and many more. However, their intrinsic infinite dimensionality poses major challenges in the development and analysis of efficient learning algorithms. While the term "functional data analysis" was coined by
[
22
,
23
]
, it is nowadays a very popular research area, and for comprehensive overview articles on the current state of the field, in terms of methods, theory and applications, we refer e.g. to
[
24
,
31
,
11
,
25
]
.

In this paper we focus on functional regression with scalar response, and the arguably most well studied method in this context assumes a linear relation between inputs and outputs, so that the responses are described by a linear functional of the (functional) inputs and some additional noise term.
Two main approaches currently exist in the literature to tackle this problem. One is functional principal component analysis
[
4
,
9
,
33
]
, which assumes that the slope can be described by a basis consisting of the leading functional principal components of the explanatory variable.

The second one assumes that the slope lies in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). This approach allows for using techniques from standard works on kernel regression (see e.g.
[
5
,
8
,
15
,
13
]
) and is therefore very popular, see e.g.
[
30
,
29
,
35
]
and references therein. Also more sophisticated results have recently been developed in this setting, that allow e.g. for dealing with online learning
[
6
,
7
]
and distributed learning
[
29
,
30
,
14
]
. It is also worth mentioning several works in the more general context of linear function on function regression (where the responses are also functions), see e.g.
[
28
,
19
,
2
]
.
The goal is always to estimate the slope and intercept of the underlying linear model, based on a regularized linear predictor constructed from training data, and provide associated non-asymptotic convergence guarantees.
All these results have in common, that they lack generality in the sense that they are not simultaneously studying the interplay of smoothness, capacity conditions and general regularization scheme, with one notable exception being
[
12
]
in the related setting of least squares regression over Hilbert spaces.
Just like in standard polynomial regression, where the linear model is embedded into a polynomial version, it is also natural to consider such scenario in the functional setting, in order to get better predictors. However, this field of research is still sparsely investigated, and we are only aware of
[
32
]
, who propose an extension with quadratic functional regression as the most prominent example. Note also the work by
[
10
]
, who consider a hypothesis test to find out, whether a linear model is sufficient.
To the best of our knowledge, and similar to the linear case, a systematic and general treatment of functional polynomial regression is still missing. The main aim of our work is to fill this gap and thus our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

Introduce polynomial functional regression in a rather general setting and derive finite sample bounds. This allows for inclusion of general smoothness conditions, capacity conditions and regularization techniques as done e.g. in the work by
[
8
,
13
,
15
,
20
]
for kernel regression in the standard supervised setting. Our work does not even assume an RKHS setting, and, also in this regard, is therefore more general than most of the previous works dealing with the linear case.

•

Provide a systematic way on how the resulting predictor model can be evaluated explicitly in case of (one parameter) Tikhonov regularization. Surprisingly, to compute the required coefficients, we found out that it is sufficient to only evaluate the linear case.

•

Provide a numerical toy example that shows the possible advantage of using higher order functional regression.

Our work will now be structured as follows: the next section introduces the required notions and proves our main result, a finite sample bound for polynomial regression. Then we will discuss an algorithmic realization of regularized polynomial functional regression in the case of Tikhonov regularization and finally, introduce a toy example demonstrating the advantage of using higher order polynomials.

2
Setting and main result

2.1
Overall setting and assumptions

Let
\mathbb{I}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and consider the associated space
L^{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
consisting of square integrable functions with respect to the Lebesgue measure
\mu
, so that

\displaystyle\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I})}=\int_{\mathbb{I}}|u(t)|^{2}d\mu(t).

Moreover, let
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
be a space of random variables
Y=Y(\omega)
defined on a probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
,
\omega\in\Omega
, with bounded second moments,
so that

\displaystyle\left\lVert Y\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})}:=\mathbb{E}|Y|^{2}=\int_{\Omega}|Y(\omega)|^{2}d\mathbb{P}(\omega).

Consider also the tensor product
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})\otimes L^{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
,
which is nothing but a collection of random variables
X(\omega,s)
indexed by points
s\in\mathbb{I}
and having bounded second moments in the following sense:

\displaystyle\left\lVert X\right\rVert_{\mathbb{P},\mu}^{2}:=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert X(\omega,\cdot)\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I})}.

The inner products in the considered Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}
will always be denoted by
\left\langle.,.\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
, and the space is indicated by a subscript.

Functional data consist of random i.i.d. samples of functions
X_{1}(s),...,X_{N}(s)
, that can be seen as a realization of a stochastic process
X(\omega,s)\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})\otimes L^{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
. Now let us discuss the setting of polynomial functional regression (PFR): Let
Y\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
be a scalar response, and
X\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})\otimes L^{2}\left(\mathbb{I}\right)
a corresponding functional predictor. We make the following assumption on
X
(as imposed in a similar way e.g. in
[
35
,
30
]
):

Assumption 1
.

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in\Omega}\left\lVert X(\omega,\cdot)\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{I})}\leq\kappa.

In PFR one aims at minimizing the expected prediction risk:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}(U_{p}(X))=\mathbb{E}\left(|Y(\omega)-U_{p}(X(\omega,\cdot))|^{2}\right)\to\min,

(1)

where
U_{p}(X(\omega,\cdot))
is a polynomial regression of order
p
:

\displaystyle U_{p}(X(\omega,\cdot))=u_{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{p}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u_{l}(s_{1},...,s_{l})\prod_{j=1}^{l}X(\omega,s_{j})d\mu(s_{j}).

Here
u_{l}\in L^{2}_{l}
, where

\displaystyle L^{2}_{l}=\underbrace{L^{2}(\mathbb{I})\otimes\cdots\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{I})}_{l\text{ -times}}.

To proceed and formalize the setting further, consider the operator

\displaystyle A_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})

assigning to any
u_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
the corresponding constant random variable. Moreover, consider
A_{l}:L^{2}_{l}\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, such that

\displaystyle(A_{l}u)(\omega)=\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u_{l}(s_{1},...,s_{l})\prod_{j=1}^{l}X(\omega,s_{j})d\mu(s_{j}).

(2)

Let, also,
\mathbb{L}^{2}=\bigoplus_{l=0}^{p}L^{2}_{l}
be a direct sum of spaces
L^{2}_{l}
consisting of finite sequences
u=(u_{0},...,u_{p})
,
u_{0}\in L^{2}_{0}=\mathbb{R}
,
u_{l}\in L^{2}_{l}
,
l=1,2,...,p
, equipped with the norm
\left\lVert u\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{l=0}^{p}\left\lVert u_{l}\right\rVert_{L^{2}_{l}}^{2}
, and consider the bounded linear operator (which is also a Hilbert-Schmidt one, as will be seen from Lemma
1
)
\mathbb{A}:\mathbb{L}^{2}\to L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})
, given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}u=(A_{0},A_{1},...,A_{p})\circ(u_{0},u_{1},...,u_{p})=\sum_{l=0}^{p}A_{l}u_{l}.

(3)

Observe that

\displaystyle A_{l}^{*}:L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})\to L^{2}_{l},(A_{l}^{*}Z)(s_{1},...s_{l})=\int_{\Omega}Z(\omega)\prod_{i=1}^{l}X(\omega,s_{i})d\mathbb{P}(\omega),

because

\displaystyle\left\langle A_{l}^{*}Z,u\right\rangle_{L^{2}_{l}}=\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}\int_{\Omega}Z(\omega)\prod_{i=1}^{l}X(\omega,s_{i})d\mathbb{P}(\omega)u(s_{1},...,s_{l})\prod_{i=1}^{l}d\mu(s_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}Z(\omega)\left(\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u(s_{1},...,s_{l})\prod_{i=1}^{l}X(\omega,s_{i})d\mu(s_{i})\right)d\mathbb{P}(\omega)=\left\langle Z,A_{l}u\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})},

and therefore,
\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}
is a
(p+1)\times(p+1)
matrix of the operators

\displaystyle\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}=\left\{A_{k}^{*}A_{l}:L^{2}_{l}\to L^{2}_{k},k,l=0,1,...,p\right\},

where
A_{0}^{*}A_{0}u_{0}=u_{0}
and

\displaystyle A_{0}^{*}A_{l}u

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u(s_{1},...,s_{l})\prod_{i=1}^{l}X(\omega,s_{i})d\mu(s_{i})d\mathbb{P}(\omega),

\displaystyle A_{k}^{*}A_{l}u(s_{1},...,s_{k})

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\prod_{j=1}^{k}X(\omega,s_{j})\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u(\tilde{s}_{1},...,\tilde{s}_{l})\prod_{i=1}^{l}X(\omega,\tilde{s}_{i})d\mu(\tilde{s}_{i})d\mathbb{P}(\omega),

\displaystyle k,l

\displaystyle=1,...,p.

Equipped with this notation, we have that

U_{p}(X(\omega,\cdot))=\mathbb{A}u
, such that (
1
) is reduced to the least square solution of the equation
\mathbb{A}u=Y
, because
\mathcal{E}(U_{p}(X))=\left\lVert Y-\mathbb{A}u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})}
. Let us also use the following standard assumption:

Assumption 2
.

The projection
\mathcal{P}Y
of
Y
on the closure of the range of
\mathbb{A}
is such that
\mathcal{P}Y\in\text{Range}(\mathbb{A})
.

It is well known (see e.g.
[
16
]
[Proposition 2.1.]), that under Assumption
2
the minimizer
u=u^{+}=(u_{0}^{+},...,u_{p}^{+})
of (
1
) solves
\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}u=\mathbb{A}^{*}Y
.

For the sake of analysis let us also adopt the following response noise model:

Assumption 3
.

\displaystyle Y=\mathbb{A}u^{+}+\varepsilon,

(4)

where a noise variable
\varepsilon:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is independent from
X
,
\mathbb{E}(\varepsilon)=0
, and for some
\sigma>0
it should satisfy either the condition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\varepsilon(\omega)|^{2})\leq\sigma^{2},

(5)

or obey, for any integer
\tilde{m}\geq 2
and some
M>0
, a slightly stronger moment condition, which is also standard in the literature, see e.g.
[
29
]
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(|\varepsilon(\omega)|^{\tilde{m}})\leq\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\tilde{m}!M^{\tilde{m}-2}.

(6)

However, the involved operators are inaccessible, because we do not know
\mathbb{P}
. Thus, we want to approximate them by using training data
(Y_{i},X_{i}(\cdot))
,
i=1,...,N
, consisting of
N
independent samples
of the response and the functional predictor
(Y(\omega),X(\omega,\cdot))
, so that

\displaystyle Y_{i}=\mathbb{A}_{i}u^{+}+\varepsilon_{i},

where
\mathbb{A}_{i}
is defined in the same way as
\mathbb{A}
by the replacement of
X(\omega,\cdot)

in the formulas (
2
) and (
3
) with
X_{i}(\cdot)
, and
\varepsilon_{i}
is a sample from the noise variable introduced in Assumption
3
.

Moreover,
u^{+}
does not depend continuously on the initial datum, such that we need to employ a regularization.

Let us start by considering Tikhonov regularization, i.e. for
\lambda>0
we want to find the minimizer
u_{\lambda}
of the regularized PFR

\displaystyle\left\lVert Y-\mathbb{A}u\right\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})}+\lambda\left\lVert u\right\rVert^{2}_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}\to\text{min},

(7)

which solves the equation
\lambda u+\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}u=\mathbb{A}^{*}Y
and can be approximated by the solution
u_{\lambda}^{N}
of

\displaystyle\lambda u+[\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}u=[\mathbb{A}^{*}Y]_{N},

(8)

These approximations are given by
[\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}=\left\{[A_{k}^{*}A_{l}]_{N}:L^{2}_{l}\to L^{2}_{k},k,l=0,1,...,p\right\}

so that:

\displaystyle[A_{0}^{*}A_{0}]_{N}u

\displaystyle=u

\displaystyle[A_{0}^{*}A_{l}]_{N}u

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u(s_{1},...,s_{l})\prod_{j=1}^{l}X_{i}(s_{j})d\mu(s_{j}),

\displaystyle[A_{k}^{*}A_{l}]_{N}u(s_{1},...,s_{k})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\prod_{j=1}^{k}X_{i}(s_{j})\int_{\mathbb{I}^{l}}u(\tilde{s}_{1},...,\tilde{s}_{l})\prod_{m=1}^{l}X_{i}(\tilde{s}_{m})d\mu(\tilde{s_{m}}),

\displaystyle k,l

\displaystyle=1,...,p.

(9)

and
[\mathbb{A}^{*}Y]_{N}=([A_{0}^{*}Y]_{N},...,[A_{p}^{*}Y]_{N})\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, so that

\displaystyle[A_{0}^{*}Y]_{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}

\displaystyle[A_{l}^{*}Y]_{N}(s_{1},...,s_{l})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i}\prod_{j=1}^{l}X_{i}(s_{j}),

\displaystyle l

\displaystyle=1,...,p.

(10)

In Section
3
we will use the above approximations for computing Tikhonov and interated Tikhonov regularizations in the context of PFR.

We will also use the fact that for any
u\in\mathbb{L}^{2}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{A}u\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})}=\left\lVert\sqrt{\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}}u\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

(11)

which follows immediately from the polar decomposition of the operator
\mathbb{A}
.

2.2
Operator norms and related auxiliary estimates

Let us move on by collecting several estimates related to the norms of the previously discussed operators.
To this end let us also introduce the notion of effective dimension:

Definition 1
.

For a compact and self-adjoint operator
T
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with countable sequence of nonnegative eigenvalues the associated effective dimension is defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(T,\lambda)=\text{Tr}((T+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-1}T)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{j}}{\lambda+\sigma_{j}},

see
[
5
]
. We will use the abbreviation
\mathcal{N}(\lambda)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A},\lambda)
.

The proof of the following lemma is a simple exercise.

.

\text{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{2})

\mathcal{H}_{1}

\text{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{1},\mathcal{H}_{1})=\text{HS}(\mathcal{H}_{1}).

Under Assumption
1

\displaystyle\left\lVert[A_{l}]_{N}\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(L^{2}_{l},\mathbb{R}))}=\left\lVert[A_{l}^{*}]_{N}\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(\mathbb{R},L^{2}_{l})}

\displaystyle\left\lVert A_{k}^{*}A_{l}\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(L^{2}_{l},L^{2}_{k})},\left\lVert[A_{k}^{*}A_{l}]_{N}\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(L^{2}_{l},L^{2}_{k})}

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{A}\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(\mathbb{L}^{2},L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P}))}

For any

\displaystyle k,l

\displaystyle=1,...,p,\omega\in\Omega.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Consider the random variable
\xi(\omega)=(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{A}(\omega)
taking values in
\text{HS}(\mathbb{L}^{2})
(we will give a bound on the HS-norm below). Again in the spirit of Lemma
6
, the operators
\xi_{i}=(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{A}^{i}
,
i=1,...,N

can be seen as independent observations of
\xi(\omega)
. Also recall from Lemma
2
that
\mathbb{E}(\mathcal{A}(\omega))=\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}
and that
\left\lVert\mathcal{A}(\omega)\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq\tilde{\kappa}^{2}
.

Then we observe
\mathbb{E}(\xi(\omega))=(\lambda\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A})^{-1/2}\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}
. Moreover it is evident from the spectral calculus for self-adjoint operators that

\displaystyle\left\lVert\xi(\omega)\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(\mathbb{L}^{2})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\lVert\mathcal{A}(\omega)\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(\mathbb{L}^{2})}\leq\frac{\tilde{\kappa}^{2}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}.

Let now
\left\{\varphi_{m}\right\}_{m}
be an orthonormal basis in
\mathbb{L}^{2}
that contains the eigenvectors of
\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}
corresponding to eigenvalues
\left\{\sigma_{m}\right\}_{m}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\lVert\xi(\omega)\right\rVert_{\text{HS}(\mathbb{L}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\mathcal{A}(\omega)\varphi_{m}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}^{2}

2.3
Statement and proof of main result

In Subsection
2.1
we have already mentioned the necessity to employ a regularization in the context of PFR to find an approximation for the solution of (
4
) and started with considering Tikhonov regularization. In this section we want to formulate and prove our main result for even more general regularization scheme covering Tikhonov regularization as a particular case. That scheme is attributed to A. B. Bakushinskii (1967) and we introduce it here as follows (see, for example,
[
15
,
18
]
).

Definition 2
.

A one-parametric family of functions
\{g_{\lambda}\}
is called a regularization scheme for a bounded self-adjoint operator
B
in a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, if there are constants
\gamma_{0},\gamma_{-1/2}
and
\gamma_{-1}
such that for any
\lambda
meeting the inequalities
0<\lambda\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}
the following bounds hold:

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}}\sqrt{\sigma}\left|g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\frac{\gamma_{-1/2}}{\sqrt{\lambda}}

(23)

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}}\left|g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\frac{\gamma_{-1}}{\lambda}

(24)

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}}\left|1-\sigma g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\gamma_{0}

(25)

The qualification of the regularization scheme
\left\{g_{\lambda}\right\}
is the maximum value
q

such that for some positive constant
\gamma_{q}
it holds

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}}\left|r_{\lambda}(\sigma)\sigma^{q}\right|\leq\gamma_{q}\lambda^{q},

(26)

where

\displaystyle r_{\lambda}(\sigma)=1-\sigma g_{\lambda}(\sigma)

is the so-called residual function.

Let us remark that one can also immediately deduce

\displaystyle\sup_{0<\sigma\leq\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}}\left|\sigma g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\right|\leq\gamma_{0}+1

(27)

from (
2
).
Note that Tikhonov regularization (
7
), (
8
) fulfils Definition
2
with
g_{\lambda}(\sigma)=(\sigma-\lambda)^{-1}
,
and
q=1
,
while iterated Tikhonov regularization with
\tilde{q}
iterations corresponds to
g_{\lambda}(\sigma)=g_{\lambda,\tilde{q}}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{\sigma}(1-(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+\sigma})^{\tilde{q}})
and
q=\tilde{q}
.

Recall that under Assumption
2
the minimizer
u=u^{+}=(u_{0}^{+},...,u_{p}^{+})
of (
1
) solves
\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}u=\mathbb{A}^{*}Y
. Then in view of Definition
2
a regularized approximate solution of the later equation can be written in the form

\displaystyle u^{N}_{\lambda}=g_{\lambda}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N})[\mathbb{A}^{*}Y]_{N}

In order to account for the smoothness of the target vector function
u^{+}
, we will use the concept of general source conditions indexed by operator monotone functions in the context of learning (see, e.g.,
[
17
,
1
]
or
[
16
]
[Section 2.8.2.] for an extended discussion), and thus need to introduce several notions:

Definition 3
.

A continuous non-decreasing function
\varphi:[0,a]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is called index function if
\varphi(0)=0
.
An index function is called operator monotone if for any bounded non-negative self-adjoint operators
A,B
on a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
with

\left\lVert A\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}},\left\lVert B\right\rVert_{{\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}}}\leq a
, we have that
A\prec B
implies
\varphi(A)\prec\varphi(B)
; here the symbol
\prec
accounts for the corresponding inequalities of the associated quadratic forms, i.e., the relation
A\prec B
means, for example, that for any
x
from
\mathcal{H}
it holds
\left\langle Ax,x\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\left\langle Bx,x\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}
.

Definition 4
.

We say that an index function
\varphi:[0,a]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is covered by the qualification
q
if the function
\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{q}/\varphi(\sigma)
is non-decreasing on
[0,a]
.

The following well known result tells us that operator monotone index functions are covered by qualification
1

Lemma 8

(see e.g. Lemma 3 in
[
17
]
or Lemma 3.4.
[
15
]
)
.

For each operator monotone index function
\varphi
there is a constant

such that, whenever
0<s\leq t\leq\tilde{\kappa}^{2}
, we have

\frac{\varphi(t)}{t}\leq C\frac{\varphi(s)}{s}
.

Now we can state the smoothness assumption as follows:

Assumption 4
.

For some operator monotone index function
\varphi:[0,\tilde{\kappa}^{2}]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
we have that

\displaystyle u^{+}=\varphi(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A})v^{+}

for some

Remark 1

Note that when

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{A}(u^{N}_{\lambda}-\bar{u}^{N}_{\lambda})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})}\leq\Xi(\gamma_{0}+\gamma_{-1}+1)\frac{(M+\sigma)\log(2/\delta)}{\tilde{\kappa}}S(N,\lambda).

(32)

Proof.

We use the following decomposition:

\displaystyle\left\lVert u^{N}_{\lambda}-\bar{u}^{N}_{\lambda}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}=\left\lVert g_{\lambda}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N})([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}u^{+}-[A^{*}Y]_{N})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{\frac{1}{2}}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}}\underbrace{\left\lVert([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{\frac{1}{2}}g_{\lambda}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}}}_{(IV)}

\displaystyle\cdot\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}u^{+}-[A^{*}Y]_{N})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

and

\displaystyle\left\lVert\mathbb{A}(u^{N}_{\lambda}-\bar{u}^{N}_{\lambda})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P})}=\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A})^{\frac{1}{2}}g_{\lambda}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N})([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}u^{+}-[A^{*}Y]_{N})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{\frac{1}{2}}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\lVert([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}+\lambda\mathbb{I})g_{\lambda}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}}

\displaystyle\cdot\left\lVert([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}\to\mathbb{L}^{2}}\left\lVert(\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}+\lambda\mathbb{I})^{-\frac{1}{2}}([\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}u^{+}-[A^{*}Y]_{N})\right\rVert_{\mathbb{L}^{2}},

so that we can deduce (
31
) and (

) by the spectral calculus, (

)–(

To deal with (IV) efficiently, similar to (III), we use the spectral calculus, Hölder’s inequality, and (

)–(

) to obtain:

\displaystyle(IV)\leq\sup_{0\leq\sigma\leq\tilde{\kappa}^{2}}(\sigma+\lambda)^{\frac{1}{2}}g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\leq\sup_{0\leq\sigma\leq\tilde{\kappa}^{2}}(\sigma^{\frac{1}{2}}+\lambda^{\frac{1}{2}})g_{\lambda}(\sigma)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\lambda}}(\gamma_{-1/2}+\gamma_{-1}).

∎

Below we also employ the following statement:

Lemma 12

There exists a
\lambda^{*}

Proof.

3
Computations for Tikhonov regularization and its iterations

In this chapter, we, at first, describe how to find the regularized approximation
u_{\lambda}^{N}
(
8
)
constructed in the form
u_{\lambda}^{N}=(u_{\lambda,0}^{N},u_{\lambda,1}^{N},...,u_{\lambda,p}^{N})\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, so that

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,0}^{N}

\displaystyle=b_{0}\in\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,l}^{N}(s_{1},...,s_{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{l,i}\prod_{j=1}^{l}X_{i}(s_{j})\in L^{2}_{l},\quad l=1,...,p.

Inserting this form into (
8
) and equating the corresponding coefficients now result in the following system of linear equations for
b_{0}
and for
b_{k,i}
with
k=1,...,p
and
i=1,...,N
:

\displaystyle(\lambda+1)b_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{s=1}^{N}b_{l,s}c_{i,s}^{l}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i},

(39)

\displaystyle\lambda b_{k,i}+\frac{1}{N}b_{0}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{s=1}^{N}b_{l,s}c_{i,s}^{l}=\frac{1}{N}Y_{i},

(40)

where
c_{i,s}=\int_{\mathbb{I}}X_{i}(\tilde{s})X_{s}(\tilde{s})d\mu(\tilde{s})
.

Next we observe that the form of the equations for the coefficients
b_{0},b_{i,k}
allows for a reduction to a system of only
(N+1)
equations. Indeed, if we sum up the equations (
40
) for all
i=1,...N
and then subtract the resulting sum from (
39
),
we obtain

\displaystyle b_{0}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{k,i},

Moreover, (
40
) allows for the conclusion that
b_{k,i}=b_{1,i}
for any
k=1,...,p
, because (
40
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\lambda b_{k,i}=\frac{1}{N}Y_{i}-\frac{1}{N}b_{0}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{s=1}^{N}b_{l,s}c_{i,s}^{l},

where the right-hand side does not depend on
k
.

Thus, the coefficients of polynomial functional regression regularized by the Tikhonov method with a single regularization parameter are completely defined by its first (linear) term.

Recall that the Tikhonov regularization scheme has the qualification
q=1
, while Lemma
11
and Theorem
1
suggest to use of a regularization with the qualification
q\geq\frac{3}{2}
.
Therefore, we consider the iterated Tikhonov regularization with
q\geq 2
being the number of iteration steps:

\displaystyle\lambda u_{\lambda,q}^{N}+[\mathbb{A}^{*}\mathbb{A}]_{N}u_{\lambda,q}^{N}

\displaystyle=\lambda u_{\lambda,q-1}^{N}+[\mathbb{A}^{*}Y]_{N},

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,0}^{N}

\displaystyle=0,

where
u_{\lambda,q}^{N}=(u_{\lambda,0,q}^{N},u_{\lambda,1,q}^{N},...,u_{\lambda,p,q}^{N})\in\mathbb{L}^{2}
, and

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,0,q}^{N}

\displaystyle=b_{0,q}\in\mathbb{R},

\displaystyle u_{\lambda,l,q}^{N}(t,s_{1},...,s_{l})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{N}b_{l,i,q}\prod_{j=1}^{l}X_{i}(s_{j})\in L^{2}_{l},\quad l=1,...,p.

To construct
u_{\lambda,q}^{N}
one needs to solve the following system of linear equations for coefficients
b_{0,q},b_{l,i,q}
:

\displaystyle(\lambda+1)b_{0,q}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{s=1}^{N}b_{l,s,q}c_{i,s}^{l}

\displaystyle=\lambda b_{0,q-1}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}Y_{i},

\displaystyle\lambda b_{k,i,q}+\frac{1}{N}b_{0,q}+\frac{1}{N}\sum_{l=1}^{p}\sum_{s=1}^{N}b_{l,s,q}c_{i,s}^{l}

\displaystyle=\lambda b_{k,i,q-1}+\frac{1}{N}Y_{i},

\displaystyle k=1,...,p,\quad i=1,...,N.

Here, by the same reasoning as above, we also see that this reduces to a system of
(N+1)
equations, from which only the coefficients
b_{0,q},b_{1,i,q}
of the components,
u_{\lambda,0,q}^{N},u_{\lambda,1,q}^{N}
need to be found.

Overall, we can observe that a single-parameter regularization in the context of PFR puts emphasis on the linear part of that regression. This can be seen as a limitation. One possibility to overcome it is to use a multiple-parameter regularization scheme, which we plan to discuss in a forthcoming work. But even a multiple-parameter regularization will still reduce functional regression to a linear problem. In order to go beyond the linearity, one can employ deep neural networks, which have been discussed recently in
[
34
,
26
]
. Thus there are many ways to explore in functional data analysis.
