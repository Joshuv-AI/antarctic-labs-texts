Heisenberg-limited Hamiltonian learning for interacting bosons
Haoya Li, Yu Tong, Hongkang Ni, Tuvia Gefen, Lexing Ying

Abstract
We develop a protocol for learning a class of interacting bosonic Hamiltonians from dynamics with Heisenberg-limited scaling. For Hamiltonians with an underlying bounded-degree graph structure, we can learn all parameters with root mean squared error ϵ \epsilon using 𝒪 ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) \mathcal{O}(1/\epsilon) total evolution time, which is independent of the system size, in a way that is robust against state-preparation and measurement error. In the protocol, we only use bosonic coherent states, beam splitters, phase shifters, and homodyne measurements, which are easy to implement on many experimental platforms. A key technique we develop is to apply random unitaries to enforce symmetry in the effective Hamiltonian, which may be of independent interest.

The natural question is, can we achieve the Heisenberg limit for many-body quantum systems? When applying the existing quantum-enhanced protocols to the many-body setting, one quickly encounters difficulties. When many entangled probes are used, one needs many copies of the quantum system with the same parameters that can evolve simultaneously without interacting with each other. It is often unclear how one can create these copies, except for certain scenarios, such as when many probes undergo evolution under the same field strength. For long coherent time-evolution, the many-body nature of the quantum systems becomes problematic as subsystems undergo open-system dynamics, and phenomena such as thermalization prevent local observables from having enough sensitivity to achieve the Heisenberg limit. One can consider performing entangled measurements across all parts of the many-body system. Still, the difficulty in simulating the system makes finding a good measurement strategy extremely difficult.

2.1
Learning an anharmonic oscillator

We first consider the simple case in which

H_{\mathrm{AHO}}=\omega n+\frac{\xi}{2}n(n-1),

(3)

where
n=b^{\dagger}b
. We want to estimate the coefficients
\omega
and
\xi
with root mean squared error (RMSE) at most
\epsilon
.

This is a quantum sensing problem with two parameters to be estimated. In quantum sensing, one usually calculates the quantum Cramér-Rao bound (QCRB) that provides a lower bound on the MSE of unbiased estimators. Because the two parameters correspond to Hamiltonian terms that commute with each other, the QCRB scales inverse quadratically with time, allowing us to achieve the Heisenberg-limited scaling. This bound, however, is valid only for local estimation where the prior distribution of the estimators is already concentrated around the exact value. Here we provide an estimation protocol that achieves this scaling without any prior knowledge of the parameters.

Our protocol builds upon a robust frequency estimation algorithm similar to the robust phase estimation algorithm proposed in
[
38
]
as well as the alternative version in
[
60
]
. In the robust phase estimation algorithm, we assume that through performing certain experiments that we will specify when introducing our protocol, we have access to a random variable
Z_{\delta}(t)
from measurement results, such that
|Z_{\delta}(t)-e^{-i\omega t}|\leq 1
with probability at least
1-\delta
, and generating such a random variable requires evolution time
\mathcal{O}(t\log(\delta^{-1}))
. With multiple samples of this variable for different values of
t
and
\delta
, we can generate an estimate of
\omega
with RMSE at most
\epsilon
using
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
total evolution time. The algorithm proceeds by iteratively obtaining estimates with increasing accuracy through longer time evolution until the target precision is achieved. A detailed description of the algorithm and proof of its correctness can be found in Section
A
.

We initialize the system in a coherent state
\ket{\alpha}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{k}(\alpha^{k}/\sqrt{k!})\ket{k}
, and let the system evolve under the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{AHO}}
. In the end we perform homodyne measurements with quadrature operators
X=(b+b^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
and
P=i(b^{\dagger}-b)/\sqrt{2}
in separate experiments. With these measurement results we will be able to estimate
\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}=\braket{\alpha|e^{iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}be^{-iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}|\alpha}
, which can be exactly computed to be

\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}=\alpha e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}e^{-i\omega t}e^{|\alpha|^{2}e^{-i\xi t}}.

(4)

We perform this calculation in Section
B
.

Using (
4
), we can extract the values of
\omega
and
\xi
from
\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}
. For
\omega
, note that
\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}/\alpha=e^{-i\omega t}+\mathcal{O}(|\alpha|^{2})
, and therefore we can choose
|\alpha|
to be below a small constant so that an estimate for
\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}/\alpha
will be close to
e^{-i\omega t}
within some small constant distance, which enables us to apply the robust frequency estimation algorithm to estimate
\omega
with RMSE at most
\epsilon
using total evolution time
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
.

For
\xi
, we can extract its value by constructing a periodically oscillating signal through

e^{-i\xi t}=\frac{1}{|\alpha_{1}|^{2}-|\alpha_{2}|^{2}}\log\left(\frac{\alpha_{2}\braket{b}_{\alpha_{1},t}}{\alpha_{1}\braket{b}_{\alpha_{2},t}}\right)+1.

(5)

This enables us to estimate
\xi
using the robust frequency estimation algorithm. Note that, once again,
\braket{b}_{\alpha_{1},t}
and
\braket{b}_{\alpha_{2},t}
only need to be estimated to constant precision, rather than
\epsilon
precision which would result in an
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-2})
scaling that would destroy the Heisenberg-limited scaling.

In the above procedure, we need to estimate the expectation of
X
and
P
operators, which are unbounded operators that can infinitely amplify any error in the quantum state. Fortunately, we found that we can replace them with operators
X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}
and
P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}
, where
\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}=\int_{|x|\leq M}\ket{x}\bra{x}\mathrm{d}x
and
\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}
is similarly defined. This means truncating the eigenvalues of these operators at a threshold
M=\mathcal{O}(1)
. In practice, we can simply discard any
X
and
P
samples that are above the threshold
M
to implement the measurement associated with these truncated operators. This fact, together with the error tolerance in the robust frequency estimation algorithm, enables us to tolerate a constant amount of error from SPAM and time evolution.
The combined error from all sources should be below a small constant, which is sufficient for achieving arbitrarily high precision.

2.2
Learning two coupled anharmonic oscillators

Next, we consider a system consisting of two coupled anharmonic oscillators, where the Hamiltonian is of the following form:

H=\omega_{1}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}+\omega_{2}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{12}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{21}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}+\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}n_{1}(n_{1}-1)+\frac{\xi_{2}}{2}n_{2}(n_{2}-1)

(6)

The goal is to learn all the coefficients
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
,
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}
, and
h_{12}
(
h_{21}=h^{*}_{12}
).

We first focus on learning the single-mode coefficients
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
,
\xi_{1}
, and
\xi_{2}
. To do this, we will insert random unitaries during time evolution to decouple the bosonic modes from each other. In other words, the time evolution operator undergoes the following transformation

e^{-iHt}\mapsto\prod_{j=1}^{r}U_{j}^{\dagger}e^{-iH\tau}U_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}e^{-iU_{j}^{\dagger}HU_{j}\tau},

(7)

where the
U_{j}
,
j=1,2,\cdots,r
, are the random beam splitters or phase shifters that we insert,
r=t/\tau
, and the product goes from right to left. Each
U_{j}
is independently drawn from a distribution that we denote by
\mathcal{D}
. In the limit of
\tau\to 0
, the dynamics can be described by an effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}U^{\dagger}HU.

(8)

This can be seen by considering the Taylor expansion of the time-evolved state in a small time step:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[e^{-iU^{\dagger}HU\tau}\rho e^{iU^{\dagger}HU\tau}]

\displaystyle=\rho-i\tau\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[[U^{\dagger}HU,\rho]]+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})

(9)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[U^{\dagger}HU]\tau}\rho e^{i\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[U^{\dagger}HU]\tau}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}).

Note that the above is not a rigorous proof, because the
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
residue is an unbounded operator. We provide a rigorous bound of how far the actual dynamics deviate from the limiting effective dynamics with finite
\tau>0
in Section
E
. The above procedure introduces additional randomness to our protocol, but it does not introduce any sample complexity overhead, because we only need the final quantum states to be close in terms of the trace distance.

To learn all the single mode coefficients, we let the unitary
U
drawn from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
be

U=e^{-i\theta n_{1}},\quad\theta\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi]).

(10)

Here
\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])
is the uniform distribution over
[0,2\pi]
.
We can then compute the effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta n_{1}}He^{-i\theta n_{1}}\mathrm{d}\theta=\omega_{1}n_{1}+\omega_{2}n_{2}+\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}n_{1}(n_{1}-1)+\frac{\xi_{2}}{2}n_{2}(n_{2}-1).

(11)

In other words, the coupling term
h_{12}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{21}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}
is cancelled in the process, due to the equality
e^{i\theta n_{1}}b_{1}e^{-i\theta n_{1}}=e^{-i\theta}b_{1}
.
We can interpret this procedure as enforcing a particle number conservation on the first bosonic mode. In the effective Hamiltonian, the two bosonic modes are no longer coupled together, and therefore we can apply the learning algorithm described in Section
2.1
to learn the parameters of the two modes separately. For a more detailed description of the protocol see Section
C.1
.

Next, we will learn the coupling coefficient
h_{12}
. We will use the following unitaries

U_{x}(\theta)=e^{i\theta(b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1})},\quad U_{y}(\theta)=e^{\theta(b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}-b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1})}.

(12)

Our protocol is based on the observation that under a single-particle basis rotation,
h_{12}
can be estimated from the new single-mode coefficients. More precisely, we let
\tilde{b}_{1}=U_{y}(\pi/4)b_{1}U_{y}^{\dagger}(\pi/4)
,
\tilde{b}_{2}=U_{y}(\pi/4)b_{2}U_{y}^{\dagger}(\pi/4)
, and the new bosonic modes will be related to the old ones through

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{b}_{1}\\
\tilde{b}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\pi/4)&\sin(\pi/4)\\
-\sin(\pi/4)&\cos(\pi/4)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(13)

We will then rewrite the Hamiltonian (
6
) in terms of
\tilde{b}_{1}
and
\tilde{b}_{2}
. The quadratic part of
H
can be written as

\tilde{\omega}_{1}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}+\tilde{\omega}_{2}\tilde{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}+\tilde{h}_{12}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}+\tilde{h}_{21}\tilde{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}
, where

\tilde{\omega}_{1}=\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{2}+\operatorname{Re}h_{12}.

(14)

Therefore,
\operatorname{Re}h_{12}
can be estimated if we can learn
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
.
The quartic part becomes more complicated, but the procedure we describe next will yield an effective Hamiltonian of a simpler form.

In our protocol for learning
\operatorname{Re}h_{12}
, we will let the random unitaries
U_{j}
in (
7
) be

U_{j}=U_{x}(-\theta/2),\quad\theta\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi]),

(15)

where
\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])
denotes the uniform distribution on
[0,2\pi]
. Note that
e^{-i\theta\tilde{n}_{1}}=e^{-i(\theta/2)(n_{1}+n_{2})}U_{x}(-\theta/2)
where
\tilde{n}_{1}=\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}
, and because the total particle number
n_{1}+n_{2}
is conserved, the random unitary
U_{x}(-\theta/2)
is equivalent to
e^{-i\theta\tilde{n}_{1}}
up to a global phase. This random unitary, as in (
11
), results in an effective Hamiltonian in which
\tilde{n}_{1}
is conserved. The effective Hamiltonian can be written as the following

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\tilde{\omega}_{1}\tilde{n}_{1}+\tilde{\omega}_{2}\tilde{n}_{2}+\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{11}}{2}\tilde{n}_{1}(\tilde{n}_{1}-1)+\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{22}}{2}\tilde{n}_{2}(\tilde{n}_{2}-1)+\tilde{\xi}_{12}\tilde{n}_{1}\tilde{n}_{2}.

(16)

In this effective Hamiltonian, the two bosonic modes
\tilde{b}_{1}
and
\tilde{b}_{2}
are still coupled through the term
\tilde{\xi}_{12}\tilde{n}_{1}\tilde{n}_{2}
. However, because the particle numbers on both modes are conserved, we can simply initialize the system with no particle on the mode
\tilde{b}_{2}
, and the coupling term will have no effect. More specifically, the initial state we use is
U_{y}(\pi/4)\ket{\alpha}\ket{0}
, which is an
\alpha
-eigenstate for
\tilde{b}_{1}
and a
0
-eigenstate for
\tilde{b}_{2}
. The effective Hamiltonian can then be further reduced to

H_{\mathrm{effective}}^{\prime}=\tilde{\omega}_{1}\tilde{n}_{1}+\frac{\tilde{\xi}_{11}}{2}\tilde{n}_{1}(\tilde{n}_{1}-1).

(17)

This enables us to learn
\tilde{\omega}_{1}
using the single-mode protocol in Section
2.1
, which then gives us
\operatorname{Re}h_{12}
through (
14
). When performing homodyne measurement in the end, we also need to apply
U_{y}(-\pi/4)
to rotate back to the original single-particle basis. We write down the quantum state we get right before measurement to summarize the whole procedure:

U_{y}\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(U_{x}\left(\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right)e^{-iH\tau}U_{x}\left(-\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right)\right)U_{y}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\ket{\alpha}\ket{0},

(18)

where all
\theta_{j}
are independently drawn from the uniform distribution over
[0,2\pi]
.

The above procedure yields
\operatorname{Re}h_{12}
. For
\operatorname{Im}h_{12}
, we only need to switch the roles of
U_{x}(\theta)
and
U_{y}(\theta)
and go through the same procedure. For a more detailed discussion, see Section
C.2
.

2.3
Learning an
N
-mode system

So far, we have concerned ourselves with learning small systems with one or two modes, but the protocol we develop can be easily generalized to
N
-mode systems. This section will focus on
N
bosonic modes arranged on a 1D chain. For the more general situation with a bounded degree graph, e.g.,
D
-dimensional square lattice, Kagome lattice, etc., see Section
D
. The Hamiltonian is described by (
1
), where the bosonic modes are labeled
1,2,\cdots,N
, and
i
and
j
are adjacent only when
j=i\pm 1
.

For this
N
-mode system, we consider a divide-and-conquer approach. We will apply random unitaries so that in the effective dynamics, the system is divided into clusters of one or two modes, each of which does not interact with the rest of the system. In this way, we can learn the parameters associated with each cluster independently and in parallel using our protocol in Section
2.2
.

More specifically, we apply random unitaries in the same way as described in (
7
). The random unitary
U_{j}
is first chosen to be

U_{j}=\prod_{k=1}^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor}e^{-i\theta_{3k}n_{3k}},

(19)

where the random variables
\theta_{3k}
are independently drawn from
\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])
, the uniform distribution over
[0,2\pi]
. Randomly applying the unitaries from this distribution enforces particle number conservation on sites with indices that are integer multiples of
3
. Therefore, any Hamiltonian term
b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}
that involves sites
3,6,9,\cdots
are canceled. The effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\omega_{1}n_{1}+\omega_{2}n_{2}+h_{12}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{21}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}

(20)

\displaystyle+\omega_{4}n_{4}+\omega_{5}n_{5}+h_{45}b_{4}^{\dagger}b_{5}+h_{54}b_{5}^{\dagger}b_{4}

\displaystyle+\cdots

\displaystyle+\sum_{i}\frac{\xi_{i}}{2}n_{i}(n_{i}-1),

where we did not include the terms
\omega_{3}n_{3}
,
\omega_{6}n_{6}
, etc., because they only contribute a global phase.
In this Hamiltonian, the two modes
1
and
2
form a cluster: they only interact with each other but not with the rest of the system. The same is true for modes 5 and 6, 7 and 8, etc. We can then apply the two-mode protocol in Section
2.2
to learn all coefficients associated with modes 1, 2, 5, 6, … Note that coefficients associated with different clusters can be learned in parallel in the same experiment.

Other coefficients remain to learn, such as
\omega_{3}
,
h_{23}
, and
h_{34}
. We can adopt the same strategy but choose the random unitary
U_{j}=\prod_{k=0}^{\lfloor N/3\rfloor-1}e^{-i\theta_{3k+1}n_{3k+1}}
so that modes 2 and 3, 5 and 6, etc. now form clusters. Similarly, we can let modes 3 and 4, 6 and 7, etc., form clusters. In this way, we can learn all the coefficients in the Hamiltonian using three different clustering schemes. The total evolution time required for carrying out all experiments will only be three times the cost of a two-mode protocol because different clusters can be learned in parallel.

More generally, we consider a system whose interaction can be described by a bounded-degree graph. We can design similar clustering schemes based on an appropriate coloring of its link graph, i.e., the graph whose vertices are the edges of the original graph. The overhead introduced will be quadratic in the degree of the original graph and independent of the system size
N
. This is discussed in more detail in Section
D
.

3
Discussion

In this work, we propose a protocol to learn a class of interacting bosonic Hamiltonians with Heisenberg-limited scaling. Our protocol uses only elements of linear optics that that can be implemented on various experimental platforms. Besides achieving the Heisenberg-limited scaling, our protocol can also tolerate a constant amount of
SPAM noise thanks to the robust frequency estimation subroutine discussed in Section
2.1
. As a part of the protocol, we also propose a method to enforce symmetry on the effective Hamiltonian governing the system’s evolution as discussed in more detail in Section
4.1
.

To our knowledge, our work is the first to propose a method that learns interacting bosonic Hamiltonians with Heisenberg-limited scaling in a scalable way. However, many open problems remain to be solved in this research direction. In this work, we only consider the particle-number preserving Hamiltonian in (
1
), but realistic Hamiltonians may contain terms that do not preserve the particle number, such as the coupling term in the Jaynes–Cummings model
[

]
and capacitive and inductive couplings between superconducting circuits
[
41
]
. Also, higher-order anharmonic effects beyond the fourth order may be non-negligible in certain quantum systems.

In our protocol, we need to apply random unitaries with a frequency that depends on the target precision. For higher precision, the speed of applying these unitaries will also need to be faster, which may be a problem for experimental implementation. A possible solution is to use some form of continuous control as considered in
[
62
,
63
,
64
,
65
,
66
,
40
]
. Moreover, since our protocol requires letting the system evolve coherently for
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
times to reach
\epsilon

It would be therefore interesting to explore whether noise suppression techniques such as dynamical decoupling
[
67
,
68
,
69
,
70
]
and quantum error correction
[

,
72
,
73
,
74
,
75
,
76
]
can mitigate this limitation and whether they can be incorporated into our protocol in a useful and scalable way.

Random Clifford unitaries played a crucial role in the classical shadow formalism
[

,
77
]
as well as Hamiltonian learning
[
39
]
. Similarly, one may wonder whether the random gaussian unitaries used in this work can be useful for other quantum information tasks for bosonic systems, such as classical shadow tomography for continuous-variable systems

,

,

]
.

4.1
Enforcing symmetry using random unitaries

This section will describe how to enforce symmetry using random unitaries. This strategy is similar in spirit to the symmetry protection strategies in
[
54
,
55
]
, but is easier to scale to an
N
-mode system in the current setting.

Let us first consider the general case where we have a compact Lie group
G
that describes the symmetry we want in the quantum system. Our quantum system is evolving under a Hamiltonian
H
that does not necessarily satisfy this symmetry, i.e., there may exist
g\in G
such that
gHg^{-1}\neq H
(here we equate an element of the Lie group with its matrix representation). We want to have the system evolve under an effective Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{effective}}
that satisfies the symmetry, i.e.,

gH_{\mathrm{effective}}g^{-1}=H_{\mathrm{effective}}.

(21)

We achieve this by inserting random unitaries in the same way as in (
7
), which gives us an effective Hamiltonian according to (
8
). The distribution from which we draw the random unitaries is the Haar measure on
G
, which we denote by
\mu
. The effective Hamiltonian can be computed as

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\int gHg^{-1}\mu(\mathrm{d}g).

(22)

When the Hamiltonian
H
is unbounded, the above equality may only hold in a weak sense.
We can verify that this effective Hamiltonian satisfies the desired symmetry because

g^{\prime}H_{\mathrm{effective}}g^{\prime-1}=\int g^{\prime}gH(g^{\prime}g)^{-1}\mu(\mathrm{d}g)=\int g^{\prime}gH(g^{\prime}g)^{-1}\mu(\mathrm{d}(g^{\prime}g))=H_{\mathrm{effective}}.

(23)

Here we have used the property of the Haar measure that
\mu(\mathrm{d}(g^{\prime}g))=\mu(\mathrm{d}g)
.

It may not be easy to randomly apply elements from the symmetry group
G
. Still, in our learning protocol, we will only enforce symmetries that are either U(1) or
\mathrm{U}(1)\times\mathrm{U}(1)\times\cdots\times\mathrm{U}(1)=U(1)^{\times N}
, where sampling can easily be done for each U(1) group separately.

Appendix A
Robust frequency estimation

Our main tool to achieve the Heisenberg limit is an algorithm to estimate the frequency from a complex-valued signal with Heisenberg-limited scaling. This algorithm resembles the robust phase estimation algorithm in
[
38
]
but is different in that we can deal with cases where the frequency we want is encoded in the expectation value rather than the probability.

More precisely, we assume access to a signal
Z_{\delta}(t)
that is close to
e^{-i\omega t}
, where
|\omega|<W
, by a constant amount of error in both the phase and the amplitude with probability
1-\delta
, where
\delta
can be tuned. It is also reasonable to assume that for smaller
\delta
, generating the corresponding
Z_{\delta}(t)
will be more costly, i.e., requiring longer evolution time. Our algorithm then uses
Z_{\delta}(t)
for different values of
\delta
and
t
to refine the estimation of
\omega
iteratively. In each iteration, we use the result from the previous iteration to get an estimate
\theta_{j}
satisfying

\omega/\widetilde{W}\in\left(\theta_{j}-\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j}},\theta_{j}+\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j}}\right),\penalty\ \mod 2\pi,

(24)

where
\widetilde{W}=3W/\pi

is a normalization factor. A detailed algorithm description can be found in Algorithm
1
, adapted from
[
60
]
.

Algorithm 1

Robust frequency estimation

Input:

\epsilon
: target accuracy,
\delta
: upper bound of the failure probability,
W
: upper bound for
\omega
.

Let
\widetilde{W}=\frac{3W}{\pi}
and
J=\max\{1,\lceil\log_{2}(4\pi\widetilde{W}/(3\epsilon))\rceil
}.

Set
\theta_{-1}=0
.

for

j=0,1,\ldots,J-1

do

Set
t_{j}=2^{j}/\widetilde{W}
.

Calculate
\delta_{j}=\frac{27\epsilon^{2}}{\pi^{2}\widetilde{W}^{2}}\frac{2^{3j-6}}{(2^{J}-1)}
.

Define a candidate set
S_{j}=\left\{\frac{2k\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}\right\}_{k=0,\ldots,2^{j}-1}
.

\theta_{j}=\argmin_{\theta\in S_{j}}\left|\theta-\theta_{j-1}\right|_{2\pi}
.

end

for

Add a proper integer multiple of
2\pi
to
\theta_{J-1}
such that
|\theta_{J-1}|\leq\pi
.

Output:

\widetilde{W}\theta_{J-1}
as an approximation to
\omega
.

In the following theorem, we analyze the performance of the above algorithm for a fixed set of values for
\delta
in each iteration. We will then optimize these values to achieve the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{-1})
scaling in Corollary
2
.

Theorem 1
.

Suppose that
|\omega|<W
is known in advance and that we have access to a signal
Z_{\delta}(t)
such that

•

|Z_{\delta}(t)|=1
,

•

|Z_{\delta}(t)-e^{-i(\omega t+f(t))}|\leq\eta
with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
\sup_{t}|f(t)|\leq C_{f}<{\pi/3}
,

•

2\arcsin\frac{\eta}{2}+C_{f}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}
,

•

generating
Z_{\delta}(t)
requires evolution time
C_{Z}t(\log(\delta^{-1})+1)
,

then we can produce an estimate
\hat{\omega}
such that

\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\omega}-\omega|^{2}]\leq\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}E_{j}^{2}\delta_{j}+\epsilon^{2}/4,

(25)

with total evolution time at most

\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}2^{j}(\log(\delta_{j}^{-1})+1),

(26)

where

E_{0}=2\pi,\quad E_{j}=4\pi\widetilde{W}/(3\cdot 2^{j})\ \forall j\geq 1,\quad J=\lceil\log_{2}(4\pi\widetilde{W}/(3\epsilon))\rceil,\quad\widetilde{W}=3W/\pi,

(27)

and each
\delta_{j}\in(0,1]
is arbitrarily chosen.

Proof.

Denote
\omega/\widetilde{W}=\frac{\omega\pi}{3W}
by
\tilde{\omega}
, then
|\tilde{\omega}|<\pi/3
. We proceed by choosing a sequence of
t_{j}
,
j=0,1,\ldots,J-1
and refining the estimation of
\tilde{\omega}
progressively. In each iteration we generate a signal
Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})
for arbitrarily chosen
\delta_{j}
.

First, let
t_{0}=1/\widetilde{W}
, then with probability at least
1-\delta_{0}
,

|Z_{\delta_{0}}(t_{0})-e^{-i(\tilde{\omega}+f(t_{0}))}|\leq\eta,

which yields

|\tilde{\omega}-(-\arg Z_{\delta_{0}}(t_{0}))|\leq 2\arcsin\frac{\eta}{2}+C_{f}<\pi/3\mod 2\pi.

Thus
\tilde{\omega}\in(-\arg Z_{\delta_{0}}(t_{0})-\pi/3+2k\pi,-\arg Z_{\delta_{0}}(t_{0})+\pi/3+2k\pi)
for some integer
k
. Let
\theta_{-1}=0
and
S_{0}=\{-\arg Z_{\delta_{0}}(t_{0})\}
, then by choosing
\theta_{0}=\argmin_{\theta\in S_{0}}|\theta-\theta_{-1}|_{2\pi}=-\arg Z_{\delta_{0}}(t_{0})
, we obtain

\tilde{\omega}\in(\theta_{0}-\frac{\pi}{3},\theta_{0}+\frac{\pi}{3}),\penalty\ \mod 2\pi.

Here
\left|\theta\right|_{2\pi}
, is defined to be the minimum distance to
0
modulo
2\pi
, i.e.,
\left|\theta\right|_{2\pi}=\pi-|(\theta\mod 2\pi)-\pi|
.

At step
j
, we set
t_{j}=2t_{j-1}
,
S_{j}=\left\{\frac{2k\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}\right\}_{k=0,\ldots,2^{j}-1}
, and
\theta_{j}=\argmin_{\theta\in S_{j}}\left|\theta-\theta_{j-1}\right|_{2\pi}
.
Now we are ready to prove that if

|Z_{\delta_{j^{\prime}}}(t_{j^{\prime}})-e^{-i(\tilde{\omega}2^{j^{\prime}}+f(t_{j^{\prime}}))}|\leq\eta,

(28)

for all
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j
, then

\tilde{\omega}\in(\theta_{j}-\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j}},\theta_{j}+\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j}}),\penalty\ \mod 2\pi,

(29)

for all
j
by induction. The case
j=0
is already proved. Suppose (
29
) holds for
j-1
.
Because of
28
, we have

|\tilde{\omega}2^{j}-(-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j}))|\leq 2\arcsin\frac{\eta}{2}+C_{f}<\pi/3\mod 2\pi.

Thus

\tilde{\omega}\in I_{k}:=\left(\frac{2k\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})-\pi/3}{2^{j}},\frac{2k\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})+\pi/3}{2^{j}}\right)\mod 2\pi,

for some
k=0,1,\ldots 2^{j}-1
. Notice that
\dist(I_{k},I_{k^{\prime}})\geq\frac{\pi}{2^{j-1}}-\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-1}}=\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-2}}
for any
k\not=k^{\prime}
, and that the length of the previous estimation
(\theta_{j-1}-\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-1}},\theta_{j-1}+\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-1}})
is exactly
\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-2}}
, we can ensure that only one
k^{*}
satisfies
I_{k}\cap(\theta_{j-1}-\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-1}},\theta_{j-1}+\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-1}})\not=\varnothing,\mod 2\pi
. Moreover, the corresponding
k^{*}
satisfies

\frac{2k^{*}\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}=\argmin_{\theta\in S_{j}}|\theta-\theta_{j-1}|_{2\pi}
, since

\left|\frac{2k^{*}\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}-\theta_{j-1}\right|_{2\pi}\leq\left|\frac{2k^{*}\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}-\tilde{\omega}\right|_{2\pi}+\left|\tilde{\omega}-\theta_{j-1}\right|_{2\pi}<\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j}}+\frac{\pi}{3\cdot 2^{j-1}}=\frac{\pi}{2^{j}},

and

\left|\frac{2k\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}-\theta_{j-1}\right|_{2\pi}\geq\frac{\pi}{2^{j-1}}-\left|\frac{2k^{*}\pi-\arg Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})}{2^{j}}-\theta_{j-1}\right|_{2\pi}>\frac{\pi}{2^{j-1}}-\frac{\pi}{2^{j}}=\frac{\pi}{2^{j}}

for any
k\not=k^{*}
. Now we have proved (
29
).

In the end, notice that (
29
) has an ambiguity of modulus
2\pi
, we add a proper integer multiple of
2\pi
to
\theta_{J-1}
such that
|\theta_{J-1}|\leq\pi
. We then choose this adjusted
\theta_{J-1}
as our estimate for
\tilde{\omega}
, and our estimate for
\omega
is
\widetilde{W}\theta_{J-1}=:\hat{\omega}
. From the above analysis we can see that if (
28
) holds for
0\leq j^{\prime}\leq j-1
, which means that all the iterations from
0
to
j-1
are successful, then by (
29
),
\tilde{\omega}
is contained in
(\theta_{j-1}-\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}),\theta_{j-1}+\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}))+2k\pi
for some integer
k
, and our estimate
\theta_{J-1}
is contained in
(\theta_{j-1}-\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}),\theta_{j-1}+\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}))+2k^{\prime}\pi
for some integer
k^{\prime}
. Since
|\tilde{\omega}|<\pi/3
, we have
((\theta_{j-1}-\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}),\theta_{j-1}+\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}))+2k\pi)\subset(-\pi,\pi)
, and then
((\theta_{j-1}-\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}),\theta_{j-1}+\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j-1}))+2k^{\prime}\pi)\cap[-\pi,\pi]=\varnothing
if
k^{\prime}\not=k
. Hence we must have
k=k^{\prime}
since
|\theta_{J-1}|\leq\pi
. Therefore the error in the normalized
\tilde{\omega}
is at most
E_{j}/\widetilde{W}=4\pi/(3\cdot 2^{j})
, for
j=1,2,\cdots,J-1
. If the very first iteration fails, the error is at most
E_{0}/\widetilde{W}=2\pi
. If all the iterations are successful, then by (
29
) and the argument above,
|\theta_{J-1}-\tilde{\omega}|\leq\pi/(3\cdot 2^{J-1})\leq\epsilon/(2\widetilde{W})
. From these observations, we will compute the expected error.

We define the random variable
j_{\mathrm{fail}}
to be the first iteration that fails, i.e.,

|Z_{\delta_{j^{\prime}}}(t_{j^{\prime}})-e^{-i(\tilde{\omega}2^{j^{\prime}}+f(t_{j^{\prime}}))}|\leq\eta,\ \forall j^{\prime}<j_{\mathrm{fail}},\quad|Z_{\delta_{j_{\mathrm{fail}}}}(t_{j_{\mathrm{fail}}})-e^{-i(\tilde{\omega}2^{j_{\mathrm{fail}}}+f(t_{j_{\mathrm{fail}}}))}|>\eta.

(30)

If such a
j_{\mathrm{fail}}
cannot be found, i.e., all iterations are successful, then we let
j_{\mathrm{fail}}=J
.
From the above analysis, conditional on
j_{\mathrm{fail}}=j<J
, the error will be at most
E_{j}/\widetilde{W}
. In other words
\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\omega}-\theta_{J-1}|^{2}|j_{\mathrm{fail}}=j]\leq E_{j}^{2}/\widetilde{W}^{2}
. If
j=J
, then the error is at most
\epsilon/(2\widetilde{W})
. Also, we have

\Pr[j_{\mathrm{fail}}=j]=(1-\delta_{0})(1-\delta_{1})\cdots(1-\delta_{j-1})\delta_{j}\leq\delta_{j}.

(31)

Therefore the expected square error is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\omega-\hat{\omega}|^{2}]

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W}^{2}\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\omega}-\theta_{J-1}|^{2}]

(32)

\displaystyle=\widetilde{W}^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{J}\mathbb{E}[|\tilde{\omega}-\theta_{J-1}|^{2}|j_{\mathrm{fail}}=j]\Pr[j_{\mathrm{fail}}=j]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}E_{j}^{2}\delta_{j}+\epsilon^{2}/4.

This proves (
25
). Generation of each
Z_{\delta_{j}}(t_{j})
requires an evolution time of
C_{Z}t_{j}(\log(\delta_{j}^{-1})+1)
, and hence we have total evolution time (
26
) by adding them up.
∎

In the theorem above, we have left a great deal of flexibility in choosing
\delta_{j}
. Below, we will try to answer that if we want the MSE to satisfy
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\omega}-\omega|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
, how we should choose the
\delta_{j}
to minimize the total evolution time required. We first state our result:

Corollary 2
.

Suppose that
|\omega|<W
is known in advance and that we have access to a signal
Z_{\delta}(t)
such that

•

|Z_{\delta}(t)|=1
,

•

|Z_{\delta}(t)-e^{-i(\omega t+f(t))}|\leq\eta
with probability at least
1-\delta
, where
\sup_{t}|f(t)|\leq C_{f}<{\pi/3}
,

•

2\arcsin\frac{\eta}{2}+C_{f}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}
,

•

generating
Z_{\delta}(t)
requires evolution time
C_{Z}t(\log(\delta^{-1})+1)
,

then we can produce an estimate
\hat{\omega}
such that
\mathbb{E}[|\hat{\omega}-\omega|^{2}]\leq\epsilon^{2}
, with total evolution time at most
\mathcal{O}(C_{Z}\epsilon^{-1})
.

Proof.

By (
25
) and (
26
), we essentially need to solve the following optimization problem to get the optimal
\{\delta_{j}\}
:

\displaystyle\underset{\{\delta_{j}\}}{\text{minimize}}\ \sum_{j=0}^{J-1}2^{j}\log(\delta_{j}^{-1})

(33)

\displaystyle\text{subject to}\ \sum_{j=0}^{J-1}E_{j}^{2}\delta_{j}\leq\frac{3}{4}\epsilon^{2}.

This optimization problem can be easily solved using the concavity of the logarithmic function, and the optimal
\delta_{j}
is

\delta_{j}=\frac{3\epsilon^{2}}{4E_{j}^{2}}\frac{2^{j}}{(2^{J}-1)}.

(34)

Using this choice of
\delta_{j}
, we can then compute the total evolution time required through
26
.

\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}2^{j}(\log(\delta_{j}^{-1})+1)=\underbrace{\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}2^{j}\log(\delta_{j}^{-1})}_{\mathrm{(I)}}+\underbrace{\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}(2^{J}-1)}_{\mathrm{(II)}}.

(35)

For term
\mathrm{(II)}
, we have

\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}(2^{J}-1)<\pi C_{Z}\frac{8\pi}{3\epsilon}

(36)

by our choice of
J
given in
27
.
For
\mathrm{(I)}
, using our expression for
\delta_{j}
and the expression for
E_{j}
in (
27
), we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{(I)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\sum_{j=0}^{J-1}2^{j}\log\left(\frac{4E_{j}^{2}}{3\epsilon^{2}}\frac{2^{J}-1}{2^{j}}\right)

(37)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\log\left(\frac{16\pi^{2}\widetilde{W}^{2}}{3\epsilon^{2}}(2^{J}-1)\right)+\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}2^{j}\log\left(\frac{64\pi^{2}\widetilde{W}^{2}}{27\epsilon^{2}}\frac{2^{J}-1}{2^{j}}\frac{1}{4^{j}}\right)

\displaystyle<\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\log\left(\frac{64\pi^{3}\widetilde{W}^{3}}{9\epsilon^{3}}\right)+\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\sum_{j=1}^{J-1}2^{j}\log\left(\frac{4}{3}8^{J-j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\log\left(\frac{64\pi^{3}\widetilde{W}^{3}}{9\epsilon^{3}}\right)+\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\log\left(\frac{4}{3}\right)(2^{J}-2)+\frac{\pi C_{Z}}{\widetilde{W}}\log(8)(2^{J+2}-2J-2)

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)+\mathcal{O}(C_{Z}\epsilon^{-1}).

In the last line, we have used the fact that
\epsilon\leq W
(as otherwise we can simply estimate
\omega
by
0
) to bound the first term on the second-to-last line. Combining (
35
), (
36
), and (
37
), we can see that the total evolution time of the entire procedure is
\mathcal{O}(C_{Z}\epsilon^{-1})
.
∎

Appendix B
Learning an anharmonic oscillator

The basic building block of our algorithm is a method to learn a single anharmonic oscillator of the form

H_{\mathrm{AHO}}=\omega b^{\dagger}b+\frac{\xi}{2}n(n-1),

(38)

where
n=b^{\dagger}b
.
We will then outline the experiments we run to learn the coefficients
\omega
and
\xi
from this Hamiltonian. We first start with a coherent state

\ket{\alpha}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k}.

(39)

We then let the system evolve under the Hamiltonian
H_{\mathrm{AHO}}
for time
t
, and obtain the quantum state

e^{-iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}\ket{\alpha}=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k}}{\sqrt{k!}}e^{-i\omega kt-i\frac{\xi}{2}k(k-1)t}\ket{k}.

(40)

In the end, we perform POVM measurement in the eigenbasis of either
X=(b+b^{\dagger})/\sqrt{2}
or
P=i(b^{\dagger}-b)/\sqrt{2}
, and by taking average we obtain
\braket{X}_{\alpha,t}
and
\braket{P}_{\alpha,t}
, where
\braket{\cdot}_{\alpha,t}
means taking expectation with respect to the state
e^{-iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}\ket{\alpha}
. With these, we can then obtain the expectation value of
b
through

\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\braket{X}_{\alpha,t}+i\braket{P}_{\alpha,t}\right).

(41)

The expectation values
\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}
for a certain set of
\alpha
and
t
will enable us to estimate
\omega
and
\xi
, and we will demonstrate this below. First we can compute
be^{-iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}\ket{\alpha}
to be

\displaystyle be^{-iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}\ket{\alpha}

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k}e^{-i\omega kt}e^{-i\frac{\xi}{2}k(k-1)t}}{\sqrt{(k-1)!}}\ket{k-1}

(42)

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{k+1}e^{-i\omega(k+1)t}e^{-i\frac{\xi}{2}k(k+1)t}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k}.

This yields a closed-form expression for
\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}
:

\displaystyle\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}\sum_{k}\frac{\alpha|\alpha|^{2k}e^{-i\omega t}e^{-i\xi kt}}{k!}

(43)

\displaystyle=\alpha e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}e^{-i\omega t}e^{|\alpha|^{2}e^{-i\xi t}}.

Now we are ready to estimate
\omega
and
\xi
with the help of Corollary
2
. To estimate
\omega
, we define

Z(t)=\frac{\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}}{\left|\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|}=e^{-i(\omega t+|\alpha|^{2}\sin(\xi t))},

then
Z(t)=e^{-i(\omega t+f(t))}
, where
f(t)=|\alpha|^{2}\sin(\xi t)
. Therefore,
\sup_{t}|f(t)|\leq|\alpha|^{2}
. The exact value of
Z(t)
is, however, inaccessible in practice, and we need to find an approximation
Z_{\delta}(t)
such that
|Z_{\delta}(t)-Z(t)|\leq\eta
with probability at least
\delta
if we want to utilize Corollary
2
. In the following, we decompose the approximation error into three parts and analyze them separately.

Truncation error.

The first part of the approximation error comes from the truncation of the observables. In our protocol, we truncate the observables up to a threshold
M
, which means rather than estimating the
\braket{X}_{\alpha,t}
and
\braket{P}_{\alpha,t}
we estimate
\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}
and
\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}
. Here
\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}
and
\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}
are defined to be

\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}=\int_{-M}^{M}\ket{x}\bra{x}\mathrm{d}x,\quad\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}=\int_{-M}^{M}\ket{p}\bra{p}\mathrm{d}p.

This is necessary for the robustness of our protocol. With the original unbounded observables
X
and
P
, any small error in the quantum state can potentially be infinitely magnified in the expectation value. The use of bounded observables will ensure that this does not happen.

In the following, we will ensure that the error introduced by this truncation is acceptable for our protocol.
From Chebyshev’s inequality, one has

\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq M)\leq\frac{\braket{X^{2}}_{\alpha,t}}{M^{2}}\leq\frac{2\braket{b^{\dagger}b}_{\alpha,t}+1}{M^{2}}=\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{M^{2}},

(44)

where we have used the fact that
\braket{b^{\dagger}b}_{\alpha,t}=|\alpha|^{2}
. Then, by Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,

\displaystyle\left|\braket{X}_{\alpha,t}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|

\displaystyle=|\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M\}}}_{\alpha,t}|\leq\sqrt{\braket{X^{2}}_{\alpha,t}}\sqrt{\braket{\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|>M\}}^{2}}_{\alpha,t}}

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\braket{b^{\dagger}b}_{\alpha,t}+1}\sqrt{\mathbb{P}(|X|\geq M)}\leq\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{{M}}.

Similarly, one has

\mathbb{P}(|P|\geq M)\leq\frac{\braket{P^{2}}_{\alpha,t}}{M^{2}}\leq\frac{2\braket{b^{\dagger}b}_{\alpha,t}+1}{M^{2}}=\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{M^{2}},

(46)

and

\displaystyle\left|\braket{P}_{\alpha,t}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|\leq\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{{M}}.

(47)

Combining the error bounds for
X
and
P
truncations, we will have an error bound for the truncated
b
operator.
Let

Z_{M}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}+i\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right),

(48)

then

\displaystyle\left|Z_{M}(t)-\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\left|\operatorname{Re}Z_{M}(t)-\operatorname{Re}\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|^{2}+\left|\operatorname{Im}Z_{M}(t)-\operatorname{Im}\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|^{2}}

(49)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\braket{X}_{\alpha,t}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|^{2}+\left|\braket{P}_{\alpha,t}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{{M}}.

Simulation error.

In practice, the final state we obtained is different from the ideal state
e^{-iH_{\mathrm{AHO}}t}\ket{\alpha}
.
This is because in the multi-mode situation,
H_{\mathrm{AHO}}
is the Hamiltonian of the effective dynamics, which differs from the actual dynamics by a small error. In this sense, we only
simulate
the effective dynamics, thus calling this error
the simulation error
.
We denote the expectation with respect to the real final state obtained by
\braket{\cdot}_{\alpha,t,r}
, where
r
stands for the parameters used in the simulation. More precisely, as will be explained in Section
C.1
, and in particular (
76
),
r
is the number of random unitaries that we insert during the time evolution. In Section
E
, we will show that for any given
\eta_{0}>0
, there exists a choice of
r
such that

\left|\braket{O}_{\alpha,t,r}-\braket{O}_{\alpha,t}\right|\leq\left\|O\right\|\eta_{0},

(50)

for any bounded observable
O
. In particular, for any given
\eta_{0}>0
, there is a choice of
r
such that

\left|\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|\leq M\eta_{0},\penalty\ \left|\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|\leq M\eta_{0}.

(51)

Define

Z_{M,r}(t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}+i\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right),

(52)

then

\displaystyle\left|Z_{M,r}(t)-Z_{M}(t)\right|

(53)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|^{2}+\left|\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t}\right|^{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq M\eta_{0}.

Statistical error.
In practice, homodyne measurement generates samples corresponding to the quadrature operator
X
. By discarding the samples with norm larger than
M
, we obtain samples
\hat{x}_{1},\hat{x}_{2},\cdots,\hat{x}_{L}
corresponding to
X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}
. We then approximate
\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}
through the average
\bar{x}=(\hat{x}_{1}+\hat{x}_{2}+\cdots+\hat{x}_{L})/L
. Similarly, we can generate
\hat{p}_{1},\hat{p}_{2},\cdots,\hat{p}_{L}
corresponding to
P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}
, and use
\bar{p}=(\hat{p}_{1}+\hat{p}_{2}+\cdots+\hat{p}_{L})/L
to approximate
\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}
. It is clear that
\hat{x}
and
\hat{p}
are unbiased estimates for
\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}
and
\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}
. Define

\bar{Z}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\bar{x}+i\bar{p}),

(54)

then

\displaystyle\left|\bar{Z}-Z_{M,r}(t)\right|=\sqrt{\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\bar{x}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right|^{2}+\left|\bar{p}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right|^{2}\right)}

(55)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\left|\bar{x}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right|,\left|\bar{p}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right|\right\}.

Thus by the union bound and Hoeffding’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\bar{Z}-Z_{M,r}(t)|\geq{\eta_{1}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\bar{x}-\braket{X\mathbf{1}_{\{|X|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right|\geq\eta_{1}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\bar{p}-\braket{P\mathbf{1}_{\{|P|\leq M\}}}_{\alpha,t,r}\right|\geq\eta_{1}\right)

(56)

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{-\frac{L\eta_{1}^{2}}{2M^{2}}}+2e^{-\frac{L\eta_{1}^{2}}{2M^{2}}}=4e^{-\frac{L\eta_{1}^{2}}{2M^{2}}}.

Putting the three types of error together, we have

\displaystyle\left|\bar{Z}-\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\bar{Z}-Z_{M,r}(t)\right|+\left|Z_{M,r}(t)-Z_{M}(t)\right|+\left|Z_{M}(t)-\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|

(57)

\displaystyle\leq\eta_{1}+M\eta_{0}+\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{{M}},

with probability at least
1-4e^{-\frac{L\eta_{1}^{2}}{2M^{2}}}
. Define

Z_{\delta}(t)=\frac{\bar{Z}}{\left|\bar{Z}\right|},

(58)

then

\displaystyle\left|Z_{\delta}(t)-Z(t)\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{\bar{Z}}{\left|\bar{Z}\right|}-\frac{\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}}{\left|\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|}\right|\leq\frac{2\left|\bar{Z}-\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|}{\left|\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|}

(59)

\displaystyle=\frac{2\left|\bar{Z}-\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|}{|\alpha|e^{|\alpha|^{2}(\cos(\xi t)-1)}}\leq 2|\alpha|^{-1}e^{2|\alpha|^{2}}\left|\bar{Z}-\braket{b}_{\alpha,t}\right|.

Hence,

\left|Z_{\delta}(t)-Z(t)\right|\leq 2|\alpha|^{-1}e^{2|\alpha|^{2}}\left(\eta_{1}+M\eta_{0}+\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{{M}}\right),

(60)

with probability at least
1-4e^{-\frac{L\eta_{1}^{2}}{2M^{2}}}
. In order for the condition
2\arcsin\frac{\eta}{2}+C_{f}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}
in Theorem
1
to hold, we need

2\arcsin\left(|\alpha|^{-1}e^{2|\alpha|^{2}}\left(\eta_{1}+M\eta_{0}+\frac{2|\alpha|^{2}+1}{{M}}\right)\right)+|\alpha|^{2}\leq\frac{\pi}{3}.

(61)

In order for
1-4e^{-\frac{L\eta_{1}^{2}}{2M^{2}}}\geq 1-\delta
to hold, we need

L\geq\frac{2M^{2}}{\eta_{1}^{2}}\log\frac{4}{\delta}.

(62)

In conclusion, we have constructed a signal
Z_{\delta}(t)
to estimate the parameter
\omega
that satisfies the conditions required by Corollary
2
.

Appendix C
Learning two coupled anharmonic oscillators

In this section, we consider a system consisting of two coupled anharmonic oscillators, and the Hamiltonian is of the following form:

H=\omega_{1}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}+\omega_{2}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{12}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{21}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}+\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}n_{1}(n_{1}-1)+\frac{\xi_{2}}{2}n_{2}(n_{2}-1)

(75)

The goal is to learn all the coefficients
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
,
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}
, and
h_{12}
(
h_{21}=h^{*}_{12}
).

C.1
Single-mode coefficients

We first focus on learning the single-mode coefficients
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
,
\xi_{1}
, and
\xi_{2}
. To do this, we will insert random unitaries during time evolution to decouple the bosonic modes from each other. In other words, the time evolution operator undergoes the following transformation

e^{-iHt}\mapsto\prod_{j=1}^{r}U_{j}^{\dagger}e^{-iH\tau}U_{j}=\prod_{j=1}^{r}e^{-iU_{j}^{\dagger}HU_{j}\tau},

(76)

where the
U_{j}
,
j=1,2,\cdots,r
, are the random linear optics unitaries that we insert,
r=t/\tau
, and the product goes from right to left. Each
U_{j}
is independently drawn from a distribution that we denote by
\mathcal{D}
. In the limit of
\tau\to 0
, the dynamics can be described by an effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}U^{\dagger}HU.

(77)

This can be seen by considering the Taylor expansion of the time-evolved state in a small time step:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[e^{-iU^{\dagger}HU\tau}\rho e^{iU^{\dagger}HU\tau}]

\displaystyle=\rho-i\tau\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[[U^{\dagger}HU,\rho]]+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})

(78)

\displaystyle=e^{-i\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[U^{\dagger}HU]\tau}\rho e^{i\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}[U^{\dagger}HU]\tau}+\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2}).

The above is not a rigorous proof because the
\mathcal{O}(\tau^{2})
residue is an unbounded operator. We will provide a rigorous bound of how far the actual dynamics deviate from the limiting effective dynamics with finite
\tau>0
in Section
E
.

To learn all the single mode coefficients, we let the unitary
U
drawn from the distribution
\mathcal{D}
be

U=e^{-i\theta b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}},\quad\theta\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi]).

(79)

Here
\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi])
is the uniform distribution over
[0,2\pi]
.
We can then compute the effective Hamiltonian

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}}He^{-i\theta b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}}\mathrm{d}\theta=\omega_{1}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}+\omega_{2}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{2}+\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}n_{1}(n_{1}-1)+\frac{\xi_{2}}{2}n_{2}(n_{2}-1).

(80)

In other words, the coupling term
h_{12}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+h_{21}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1}
is cancelled in the process, due to the equality

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}}b_{1}e^{-i\theta b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}b_{1}\mathrm{d}\theta=0.

(81)

We can interpret this procedure as enforcing a particle number conservation on the first bosonic mode.

The effective Hamiltonian has the desirable feature that the two bosonic modes are no longer coupled together. Therefore we can apply the learning algorithm described in Section
B
to learn the parameters of the two modes separately.

C.2
The coupling coefficient

Next, we consider learning the coupling coefficient
h_{12}
. We observe that the coupling term can be transformed into a local one under a single-particle basis transformation. This is done through the following two operators

U_{x}(\theta)=e^{i\theta(b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}+b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1})},\quad U_{y}(\theta)=e^{\theta(b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{2}-b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{1})},

(82)

which correspond to Pauli-
X

Y

\begin{pmatrix}U_{x}(\theta)b_{1}U_{x}^{\dagger}(\theta)\\
U_{x}(\theta)b_{2}U_{x}^{\dagger}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)&i\sin(\theta)\\
i\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix},\ \begin{pmatrix}U_{y}(\theta)b_{1}U_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta)\\
U_{y}(\theta)b_{2}U_{y}^{\dagger}(\theta)\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\theta)&\sin(\theta)\\
-\sin(\theta)&\cos(\theta)\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

(83)

We first perform the Pauli-
Y
rotation and define

\tilde{b}_{1}=U_{y}(\pi/4)b_{1}U_{y}^{\dagger}(\pi/4),\quad\tilde{b}_{2}=U_{y}(\pi/4)b_{2}U_{y}^{\dagger}(\pi/4).

(84)

Through (
83

\begin{pmatrix}b_{1}\\
b_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{b}_{1}\\
\tilde{b}_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(85)

We will then rewrite the Hamiltonian (
75
) in terms of
\tilde{b}_{1}
and
\tilde{b}_{2}
. The quadratic part of
H
can be written as

\tilde{\omega}_{1}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}+\tilde{\omega}_{2}\tilde{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}+\tilde{h}_{12}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}+\tilde{h}_{21}\tilde{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1},

(86)

where

\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\omega}_{1}&\tilde{h}_{12}\\
\tilde{h}_{21}&\tilde{\omega}_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\
-1&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\omega_{1}&h_{12}\\
h_{21}&\omega_{2}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\
1&1\end{pmatrix}.

(87)

In particular, we have

\tilde{\omega}_{1}=\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{2}+\operatorname{Re}h_{12}.

(88)

For the quartic part, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}n_{1}(n_{1}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi_{1}}{2}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}^{\dagger}b_{1}b_{1}=\sum_{ijkl=1}^{2}\xi^{(1)}_{ijkl}\tilde{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\tilde{b}^{\dagger}_{j}\tilde{b}_{k}\tilde{b}_{l},

(89)

\displaystyle\frac{\xi_{2}}{2}n_{2}(n_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=\frac{\xi_{2}}{2}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{2}^{\dagger}b_{2}b_{2}=\sum_{ijkl=1}^{2}\xi^{(2)}_{ijkl}\tilde{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\tilde{b}^{\dagger}_{j}\tilde{b}_{k}\tilde{b}_{l}.

In particular

\xi^{(1)}_{1111}=\frac{\xi_{1}}{4},\quad\xi^{(2)}_{1111}=\frac{\xi_{2}}{4}.

(90)

Combining (

\tilde{b}_{1}

\tilde{b}_{2}

H=\tilde{\omega}_{1}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}+\tilde{\omega}_{2}\tilde{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}+\tilde{h}_{12}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}+\tilde{h}_{21}\tilde{b}_{2}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}+\sum_{ijkl=1}^{2}(\xi^{(1)}_{ijkl}+\xi^{(2)}_{ijkl})\tilde{b}^{\dagger}_{i}\tilde{b}^{\dagger}_{j}\tilde{b}_{k}\tilde{b}_{l}.

(91)

The above expression is much more complicated than the original expression in (
75

U=e^{-i\theta\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}},\quad\theta\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi]).

(92)

With the same derivation as in (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}e^{-i\theta\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}\mathrm{d}\theta=0,

(93)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{i\theta\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}\tilde{b}_{2}e^{-i\theta\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}}\mathrm{d}\theta=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}e^{2i\theta}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{2}\tilde{b}_{2}\mathrm{d}\theta=0.

\omega_{1}

\displaystyle\tilde{b}_{1}\ket{\Phi(0)}=\tilde{b}_{1}U_{y}(\pi/4)\ket{\alpha}\ket{0}

Note that this Hamiltonian only acts non-trivially on the bosonic mode

Let us now briefly summarize the whole procedure. We start from a state
\ket{\alpha}\ket{0}
, apply
U_{y}(\pi/4)
, let the system evolve for time

, while applying random

, and in the end apply

U_{y}(-\pi/4)\prod_{j=1}^{r}(e^{i\theta_{j}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}}e^{-iH\tau}e^{-i\theta_{j}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}})U_{y}(\pi/4)\ket{\alpha}\ket{0},

(98)

for randomly sampled
\theta_{j}
,
j=1,2,\cdots,r
.
Note that
e^{-i\theta_{j}\tilde{b}_{1}^{\dagger}\tilde{b}_{1}}=e^{-i(\theta_{j}/2)(n_{1}+n_{2})}U_{x}(-\theta_{j}/2)

(99)

Consequently we can replace all

with

. The quantum state we get in the end is, therefore

(100)

Note that the adjacent

and
U_{x}(\theta_{j-1}/2)
can be merged into
U_{x}(-(\theta_{j}-\theta_{j-1})/2)
, so that we only need to apply one

, which through (

. For

\tilde{b}_{1}=U_{x}(\pi/4)b_{1}U_{x}^{\dagger}(\pi/4),\quad\tilde{b}_{2}=U_{x}(\pi/4)b_{2}U_{x}^{\dagger}(\pi/4),

(101)

and then (

\tilde{\omega}_{1}=\frac{\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}}{2}+\operatorname{Im}h_{12}.

(102)

We can then change the whole procedure accordingly to estimate

U_{x}\left(-\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\prod_{j=1}^{r}\left(U_{y}\left(-\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right)e^{-iH\tau}U_{y}\left(\frac{\theta_{j}}{2}\right)\right)U_{x}\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\ket{\alpha}\ket{0}.

(103)

Appendix D
Using a divide-and-conquer approach to learn an
N
-mode system

In this section, we consider the general case, where the Hamiltonian is of the form:

H=\sum_{\braket{i,j}}h_{ij}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}+\sum_{i}\omega_{i}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i}+\frac{\xi_{i}}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i}(n_{i}-1).

(104)

We will use a divide-and-conquer approach to learn the coefficients in this Hamiltonian. Specifically, we will insert random unitaries during time evolution to decouple the system into clusters containing one or two modes that do not interact with each other and learn the coefficients in each cluster in parallel.

We assume that the bosonic modes are arranged on a graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
, where
\mathcal{V}
is the set containing all vertices, each of which corresponds to a bosonic mode, and
\mathcal{E}
contains all edges.
\sum_{\braket{i,j}}
means summation over all vertices linked by an edge.

We consider decoupling the system with the help of a graph
\mathcal{L}=(\mathcal{E},\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}})
that is the link graph of
\mathcal{G}
. The set
\mathcal{E}
is the set of all edges in
\mathcal{G}
, and
\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{L}}
is the set of edges of
\mathcal{L}
, which we will now define. For any two edges
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{E}
, we have
(e,e^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}
if and only if they share a vertex in
\mathcal{V}
.

Next, we color the graph
\mathcal{L}
with the following rule: any two vertices in
\mathcal{E}
must be colored differently if they are at most distance
2
from each other. The number of colors needed for this coloring is at most
\chi=\operatorname{deg}(\mathcal{L})^{2}+1
, and such a coloring can be easily found by a greedy algorithm: we can simply color a vertex by any color that its neighbors or next-neighbors have not used, and such a color is always available because there are at most
\chi-1
neighbors and next-neighbors. For a graph
\mathcal{G}
with degree
D
,
\operatorname{deg}(\mathcal{L})\leq 2(D-1)
, and therefore
\chi\leq 4(D-1)^{2}+1
. This coloring yields a decomposition of the edges

\mathcal{E}=\bigsqcup_{c=1}^{\chi}\mathcal{E}_{c},

(105)

where
\mathcal{E}_{c}
is the set of edges with color
c
.

For each color
c=1,2,\cdots,\chi
, we then learn all the coefficients associated with this color. We denote by
\mathcal{V}_{c}
all the vertices (bosonic modes) that are contained in an edge in
\mathcal{E}_{c}
. During time evolution, we apply random unitaries of the form

U=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}e^{-i\theta_{i}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i}},\quad\theta_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}([0,2\pi]).

(106)

Here
\theta_{i}
,
i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}
, are independent random variables. Following the derivation in (
78
), we can see that the effective Hamiltonian is

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\right)e^{-i\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\theta_{i}n_{i}}He^{i\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\theta_{i}n_{i}}.

(107)

We can then examine the effect of this transformation on each term. For a term
b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}
,
k\neq l
, if
k
is in
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}
but
l
is not, then

\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\right)e^{-i\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\theta_{i}n_{i}}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}e^{i\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\theta_{i}n_{i}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\omega_{k}e^{i\omega_{k}}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}=0

(108)

The same is true if
l
is in
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}
but
k
is not. When both
k,l\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}
, then

\displaystyle\prod_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\theta_{i}\right)e^{-i\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\theta_{i}n_{i}}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}e^{i\sum_{i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}}\theta_{i}n_{i}}

(109)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\omega_{k}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\omega_{l}e^{i(\omega_{k}-\omega_{l})}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}=0.

In other words, for any coupling term
b^{\dagger}_{k}b_{l}
, the above procedure will cancel it out if either
k
or
l
is in
\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}
. All other terms are preserved because they commute with
n_{i}
for any
i\in\mathcal{V}\setminus\mathcal{V}_{c}
. The only possible
b^{\dagger}_{k}b_{l}
terms left are those with
k,l\in\mathcal{V}_{c}
.
This also means that
(k,l)\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
because of the following argument: first by definition of
\mathcal{V}_{c}
there must exists
k^{\prime}
and
l^{\prime}
such that
(k,k^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
and
(l,l^{\prime})\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
. We must have
(k,l)\in\mathcal{E}
, as otherwise, this coupling term would not exist at all. This means that unless
k^{\prime}=l^{\prime}
, the two edges
(k,k^{\prime})
and
(l,l^{\prime})
as vertices in
\mathcal{L}
are next-neighbors, which is not allowed in our coloring. Therefore
k^{\prime}=l^{\prime}
and we have
(k,l)\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
.
Consequently, the effective Hamiltonian is

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\sum_{(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{c}}h_{ij}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}+\sum_{i}\omega_{i}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{i}+\frac{\xi_{i}}{2}\sum_{i}n_{i}(n_{i}-1).

(110)

Next, we will show that the above Hamiltonian is decoupled into clusters of sizes at most
2
. We will do this by showing that any bosonic mode
i
interacts with at most one other bosonic mode in the above Hamiltonian. This can be proved by contradiction: if
i
interacts with both
j
and
k
in the above Hamiltonian, then
(i,j)\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
and
(i,k)\in\mathcal{E}_{c}
, which makes
(i,j)
and
(i,k)
neighbors as vertices in
\mathcal{L}
, and this is forbidden in our coloring.

With the decoupled Hamiltonian in (
110
), we can then learn the coefficients in each one- or two-mode cluster independently and in parallel using the algorithms described in Sections
B
and
C
. Looping over all colors
c\in\{1,2,\cdots,\chi\}
, we will obtain all the coefficients in the Hamiltonian.

Appendix E
Deviation from the effective dynamics

In this section, we consider the error introduced by simulating the effective dynamics with the insertion of random unitaries, as mentioned in Section
C.1
. Suppose
\mathcal{D}
is a distribution over the set of unitaries, and the initial state of the system is represented by the density matrix
\rho(0)
. The actual final state obtained after the insertion of
r
random unitaries is

\mathbb{E}_{U_{j}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left(\prod_{1\leq j\leq r}^{\leftarrow}U_{j}^{\dagger}e^{-i\tau H}U_{j}\right)\rho(0)\left(\prod_{1\leq j\leq r}^{\rightarrow}U_{j}^{\dagger}e^{i\tau H}U_{j}\right),

(111)

where each
U_{j}
is inserted after time
\tau=\frac{t}{r}
. On the other hand, the desired final state, which facilitates the subsequent steps of the learning process, is

e^{-itH_{\mathrm{effective}}}\rho(0)e^{itH_{\mathrm{effective}}},

(112)

where
H_{\mathrm{effective}}
is the effective Hamiltonian:

H_{\mathrm{effective}}=\mathbb{E}_{U\sim\mathcal{D}}U^{\dagger}HU.

In this section, we provide an analysis of the difference between the two dynamics for a certain class of Hamiltonians and thereby complete the analysis of approximation errors investigated in Section
B
. For the sake of the Hamiltonians studied in this paper, we consider the Hamiltonians of the following form:

H=\sum_{\braket{i,j}}h_{ij}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}+\sum_{i}\omega_{i}n_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\braket{jklm}}\xi_{jklm}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}b_{m},

(113)

where in the last term we denote by
\braket{jklm}
the index quadruples such that
\{j,k,l,m\}
form a connected subgraph in the underlying graph
\mathcal{G}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
of bosonic modes. We begin with a lemma describing the action of these Hamiltonians on the product of coherent states.

Lemma 6
.

Let

H=\sum_{\braket{i,j}}h_{ij}b_{i}^{\dagger}b_{j}+\sum_{i}\omega_{i}n_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\braket{jklm}}\xi_{jklm}b_{j}^{\dagger}b_{k}^{\dagger}b_{l}b_{m},

(114)

and

\phi=\bigotimes_{i\in\mathcal{V}}\left(e^{-|\alpha_{i}|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{k}e^{-i\zeta_{i,k}}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k}_{i}\right),

(115)

where
\alpha_{i}
is a complex number of magnitude
O(1)
, and
\zeta_{i,k}\in\mathbb{R}
can be any real number. Then

\|H\phi\|=O(N\max\{|\xi_{jklm}|,|\omega_{i}|,|h_{i,j}|\}),

(116)

and

\|H^{2}\phi\|=O(N^{2}(\max\{|\xi_{jklm}|,|\omega_{i}|,|h_{i,j}|\})^{2}),

(117)

where
N=|\mathcal{V}|+|\mathcal{E}|

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
H/\max\{|\xi_{jklm}|,|\omega_{i}|,|h_{i,j}|\}
. Therefore we assume
\max\{|\xi_{jklm}|,|\omega_{i}|,|h_{i,j}|\}=1
without loss of generality. Notice that
H
is the sum of
O(N)
terms, and each term takes the form
b^{\dagger}_{p}b_{q}
or
b^{\dagger}_{p}b_{q}b^{\dagger}_{r}b_{s}
, where
p
,
q
,
r
,
s
may be repeated. We will prove that each term acting on
\ket{\phi}
yields a state whose norm is
O(1)
. We first demonstrate this for
\left\|b^{\dagger}_{p}b_{q}b^{\dagger}_{r}b_{s}\phi\right\|
. Simple calculation shows

\displaystyle b^{\dagger}_{p}b_{q}b^{\dagger}_{r}b_{s}\phi

(118)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bigotimes_{i\notin\{p,q,r,s\}}\left(e^{-|\alpha_{i}|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{i}^{k}e^{-i\zeta_{i,k}}}{\sqrt{k!}}\ket{k}_{i}\right)\otimes\bigotimes_{j\in\{p,q,r,s\}}\left(e^{-|\alpha_{j}|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{k}e^{-i\zeta_{j,k}}}{\sqrt{k!}}\sqrt{P_{j}(k)}\ket{k+\sigma_{j}}_{j}\right),

where
P_{j}
’s are polynomials with
\sum_{j\in\{p,q,r,s\}}\deg P_{j}=4
, and
\sigma_{j}
is an integer determined by the numbers of
b^{\dagger}_{j}
and
b_{j}
in
b^{\dagger}_{p}b_{q}b^{\dagger}_{r}b_{s}
. For example, if
p=q=r=1
,
s=2
, then
P_{1}(k)=(k+1)^{3}
,
\sigma_{1}=1
,
P_{2}(k)=k
,
\sigma_{2}=-1
. Straight calculations can show that

\displaystyle\left\|e^{-|\alpha_{j}|^{2}/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha_{j}^{k}e^{-i\zeta_{j,k}}}{\sqrt{k!}}\sqrt{P_{j}(k)}\ket{k+\sigma_{j}}_{j}\right\|^{2}
