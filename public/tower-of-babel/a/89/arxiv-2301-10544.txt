Stray field computation by inverted finite elements: a new method in micromagnetic simulations
Tahar Z. BOULMEZAOUD 1,2,3, Keltoum KALICHE 1,3

Abstract
. In this paper, we propose a new method for computing the stray-field and the corresponding energy for a given magnetization configuration. Our approach is based on the use of inverted finite elements and does not need any truncation. After analyzing the problem in an appropriate functional framework, we describe the method and we prove its convergence. We then display some computational results which demonstrate its efficiency and confirm its full potential.

1.
Introduction

In micromagnetics, the structure of a magnetic body
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
if often described by the magnetization
{\boldsymbol{M}}
, which is a vector field defined over
\Omega
and minimizing the Landau-Lifschitz functional. In terms of dimensionless variables, the latter energy can be written into the form

{\mathscr{E}}({\boldsymbol{M}})=\alpha\int_{\Omega}|\nabla{\boldsymbol{M}}|^{2}+\int_{\Omega}\varphi({\boldsymbol{M}})dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{H}}_{e}.{\boldsymbol{M}}dx,

where
\varphi\geqslant 0
is a function describing the orientation of the magnetization,
\alpha>0
is a real parameter,

{\boldsymbol{H}}_{e}
the external magnetic field and
u
is magnetostatic potential. The latter quantity is related to the stray-field (or the magnetic induction)
{\boldsymbol{h}}
by

(1)

{\boldsymbol{h}}=-\nabla u.

The existence of the scalar potential
u
comes from Maxwell’s equation

(2)

{\boldsymbol{\rm curl}\,}{\boldsymbol{h}}={\boldsymbol{0}}\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

Moreover, the stray-field
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and the magnetization
{\boldsymbol{M}}
are related by the equation

(3)

\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{h}}+{\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega})=0\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{3},

where
\chi_{\Omega}
stands for characteristic (or indicator) function of
\Omega
. Rewritting (
3
) in terms of
u
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}
gives the well known equation

(4)

\Delta u=\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega})\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

This equation can also be written into the form

(5)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcll}\Delta u&=&\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
\Delta u&=&0&\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega},\\
\displaystyle{[u]}&=&0&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\\
\displaystyle{\left[\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\right]}&=&-{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}&\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\end{array}\right.

where
{\boldsymbol{n}}
is the exterior normal on
\partial\Omega
.
In addition, the magnetization
{\boldsymbol{M}}
is subject to the Heisenberg-Weiss condition

(6)

|{\boldsymbol{M}}|\mbox{ is constant in }\Omega.

It is well known that calculating the stray-field
{\boldsymbol{h}}
and the corresponding energy

(7)

{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|{\boldsymbol{h}}|^{2}dx,

from the magnetization
{\boldsymbol{M}}
is one of the most important steps in studying micromagnetic configurations of a body
\Omega
. We may observe that a consequence of identity (
4
) is that the stray-field energy also writes

(8)

{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{h}}dx.

(see also the weak formulation of (
4
) hereafter).

In the existing litterrature,
one can find mainly two categories of methods. In the first category the calculation of
u
and
{\boldsymbol{h}}
is often based on solving the elliptic partial differential equation
(
4
). In that case, the computational domain is often truncated and approximation is done in a sufficiently large bounded region (see, e. g.,
[
3
]
,
[
18
]
,
[
10
,
15
]
,
[
6
]
and
[
21
]
,
[
12
]
and
[
13
]
). In the second category of methods, the approach consists to evaluate
u
using the integral formula (see, e. g.,
[
9
]
)

(9)

u({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\Omega}\frac{({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{y}}).{\boldsymbol{M}}({\boldsymbol{y}})}{|{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{3}}d{\boldsymbol{y}}.

Among methods using formula (
9
), one can mention methods based on the Fast Fourier transfor and fast Multipole methods (see, e. g.,
[
4
]
,
[
17
]
),
H
-matrix techniques (
[
20
]
) or direct integration methods (see, e. g.,
[
9
]
,
[
16
]
).

In this paper, the focus is on computing the stray-field
{\boldsymbol{h}}
from the magnetization
{\boldsymbol{M}}
by a novel approach based on the use of inverted finite element method (IFEM). IFEM was first introduced by Boulmezaoud in
[
5
]
for solving elliptic problem in unbounded domains without any truncation. In the context of equation (
4
) considered here, the domain of computation
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is considered in its entirety. The deployment of IFEM is based on a weak formulation of (
4
) in an appropriate weighted space.

The paper is organized as follows. In section
2
, we employ some weighted function spaces to study equation (
4
), completed with asymptotic conditions when
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\rightarrow+\infty
. In particular, we give some details about the behavior at large distances and about the smoothness of the solution and of its derivatives. Section
3
is devoted to an outline of IFEM. After giving the general lines of the method, we prove its convergence in the context of
equation (
4
). In the last section, we give some numerical results obtained with a 3D code.

2.
Preliminaries. Well posedness of the problem

In the sequel,
\Omega
denotes an open and connected subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
having a lipschitzian boundary, not necessarily bounded (althought in physical applications
\Omega
is often a bounded domain). Let
{\boldsymbol{M}}
be a vector field defined over
\Omega
. From a strictl mathematical point, unless otherwise indicated, we only assume that

(10)

\int_{\Omega}|{\boldsymbol{M}}|^{2}dx<\infty.

Assumption (
10
) is obviously valid when
\Omega
is bounded (or has a finite volume) and when
|{\boldsymbol{M}}|
is satisfying the Heisenberg-Weiss constraint (
6
). In that case

\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}=\int_{\Omega}|{\boldsymbol{M}}|^{2}dx=|{\boldsymbol{M}}|^{2}|\Omega|<+\infty.

We now come back to equation (
4
). Without going into the technicalities of Poisson equation, it can be seen that existence and uniqueness of solutions to (
4
)
depend on the required behavior at large distances, that is when
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\rightarrow+\infty
. To illustrate this, one may observe that polynomial growth of solutions at large distances should be excluded, otherwise uniqueness may be lost since harmonic polynomials can be added to any solution of (
4
) (see, e. g.,
[
11
]
,
[
2
]
). Fortunately, in the current context,
u
must fulfill the physical constraint

(11)

\ \int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx<\infty,

which means that
{\boldsymbol{h}}=-\nabla u
has a finite energy. In view of Hardy’s inequality (see, e. g.,
[
2
]
), it is natural to require that

(12)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{|u|^{2}}{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}+1}dx<\infty.

For this reason, we need to introduce some weighted function spaces. For all integers
\ell\geqslant 0
and
m\geqslant 0
,

W^{m}_{\ell}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
stands for the space of all the functions satisfying

\forall|\lambda|\leqslant m,\;(1+|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2})^{(\ell+|\lambda|-m)/2}D^{\lambda}v\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}).

This space is endowed with the norm

(13)

\|v\|_{W^{m}_{\ell}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}=\left(\sum_{|\lambda|\leqslant m}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}+1)^{|\lambda|+\ell-m}|D^{\lambda}v|^{2}dx\right)^{1/2}.

In other words, elements of
W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
are those functions satisfying (
11
) and (
12
). We may observe that
non vanishing polynomial functions do not belong to
W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.
Before continuing with problem (
4
), let us recall that for any function
v\in W^{1}_{\alpha}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
,
\alpha\in{\mathbb{R}}\backslash\{-1/2\}
, one has (see, e. g.,
[
1
]
)

(14)

\lim_{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\rightarrow+\infty}|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{\alpha+1/2}\|u(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|,.)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=0,

where
{\mathbb{S}}^{2}
is the unit sphere of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and

(15)

\|u(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|,.)\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=\int_{{\mathbb{S}}^{2}}|u(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|,\sigma)|^{2}d\sigma.

Equation (
4
), completed with asymptotic conditions (
11
) and (
12
), can be written into the variational form: find
u\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
such that

(16)

\forall v\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\nabla u.\nabla vdx=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{M}}.\nabla vdx.

We have the following result

Proposition 2.1

(Well posedness)
.

Suppose that assumption (
10
) holds true. Then, (
16
), and consequently (
4
), has one and only one solution in
W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. Moreover, the following estimates hold

(17)

\displaystyle\|(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}+1)^{-1/2}u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle 2\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)},

(18)

\displaystyle\;\|\nabla u\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

We should note immediately that the decay of
u
at large distances is faster than in estimates (
17
) and (
18
). Actually,
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
and
(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}+1)^{1/2}\nabla u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}
as it will be stated in Proposition
2.2
hereafter.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
–
Let us first recall the classical Hardy inequality

(19)

\forall v\in{\mathscr{D}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{|u|^{2}}{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}}dx\leqslant 4\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{|\nabla u|^{2}}dx.

Thus,

(20)

\forall v\in{\mathscr{D}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\frac{|u|^{2}}{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}+1}dx\leqslant 4\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{|\nabla u|^{2}}dx.

By density of
{\mathscr{D}}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
in
W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
(see
[
14
]
), the last inequality remains valid
for
v\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. It follows that the bilinear form on the left hand side of
(
16
) is coercive. The linear form on the right hand side of (
16
) satisfies

\left|\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{M}}.\nabla vdx\right|\leqslant\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)^{3}}.\|\nabla v\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}.

Existence and uniqueness follow from Lax-Milgram theorem. Moreover, taking
v=u
in
(
16
) gives estimate (
18
). Combining with inequality
(
20
) gives (
17
).

\blacksquare

Since the right hand side of (
16
) is in a divergence form, we also get the following result

Proposition 2.2

(Asymptotic behavior)
.

Let
u\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
be solution of (
4
). Then,

(1)

u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
,

(2)

(1+|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2})^{1/2}\nabla u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}
.

(3)

\lim_{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\rightarrow+\infty}|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{3/2}\|u(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|,.)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}=0
where
\|u(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|,.)\|_{L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})}
is defined by (
15
).

Proof of Proposition (
2.2
)
–
Let us prove that
u\in W^{1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. This is a direct consequence
of the following lemma which is a particular case of
a more general result proven in
[
2
]
(Theorem 2.16):

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
m\geqslant 0
be an integer. Then, the Laplace operator

\Delta:W^{m+1}_{m+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\mapsto W^{m-1}_{m+1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\perp{\mathbb{R}}

is an isomorphism.

Here
W^{-1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
stands for the dual space of
W^{1}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.
It may be noted at this point that
constant functions belong to
W^{1}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. By
W^{-1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\perp{\mathbb{R}}
we mean the space of functions

f\in W^{-1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
satisfying

(21)

\langle f,1\rangle_{W^{-1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}),W^{1}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})}=0.

In the context of equation (
4
), the right hand side is
f=\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega})
. Thus,

f\in W^{-1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
(since, obviously,
{\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega}\in W^{0}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}
) and (
21
)
is automatically fulfilled. We conclude that
u\in W^{1}_{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, thanks to Lemma
2.3
. This ends the proof of the two first assertions.
The third assertion follows from property (
14
).

\blacksquare

Remark –

One can also prove that
u\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
by means of the Fourier transform.

Proposition 2.4

(Regularity)
.

Assume that
\Omega
is a bounded open set of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
with a
{\mathscr{C}}^{1,1}
boundary and that

(22)

{\boldsymbol{M}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)^{3},\;\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mbox{ and }{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega).

Let
u\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
be solution of (
4
). Then,

(1)

u_{|\Omega}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
,

(2)

u_{|{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}}\in W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})
, that is

\forall 1\leqslant i,j\leqslant 3,\;(|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{2}+1)\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}).

(c)

If
{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}=0
on
\partial\Omega
, then
u\in W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

Proof of Proposition (
2.4
).
–
Let
u_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
such that

u_{0}=0\mbox{ and }\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n}={\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega.

Set

U=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}u-u_{0}&\mbox{ in }\Omega,\\
u&\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}.\end{array}\right.

Since
[U]=0
on
\partial\Omega
, we easily deduce that
U\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. Moreover, we have

[\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}]=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,

Thus,

\Delta U=(\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}-\Delta u_{0})\chi_{\Omega}\in W^{0}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}).

The right hand side of this equation satisfies

\langle\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}-\Delta u_{0},1\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{M}}-\nabla u_{0})dx=\int_{\Omega}({\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}-\frac{\partial u_{0}}{\partial n})d\sigma=0.

In view of Lemma
2.3
, we deduce that
U\in W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. By restrincting to
\Omega
and to
{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}}
we get
u_{|\Omega}=U_{|\Omega}+u_{0}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
and
u_{|{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}}=U_{|{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}}\in W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})
. Suppose now that

{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}=0
on
\partial\Omega
. Then,

\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega})\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. Since
\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega})=0
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
, we also deduce that

\mathrm{div}\,({\boldsymbol{M}}\chi_{\Omega})\in W^{0}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. In view of Lemma
2.3
, we deduce that
u\in W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
. This ends the proof of Proposition
2.4
.

\blacksquare

) the potential
u

Suppose that

{\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}}

, while

Since
\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
,
\widetilde{\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}}\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}){\hookrightarrow}W^{0}_{-1}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

and

3.
Inverted finite elements method

Inverted finite elements method was developed by Boulmezaoud
[
5
]
. We will tailor it here
for solving problem (
4
). The starting point consists to partition the whole space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
into two subdomains

(26)

{{\mathbb{R}}}^{3}={\overline{\Omega}_{0}}\cup{\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}}.

Here
\Omega_{0}
is bounded region while
\Omega_{\infty}
is an unbouded one. We should note immediately that the bounded
\Omega_{0}
is not intented to be large. In particular, we do not rule out the possibility that
\Omega_{0}=\emptyset
and
\Omega_{\infty}={\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. However, the following constraint is imposed to
\Omega_{\infty}
(or, indirectly, to
\Omega_{0}={\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega_{\infty}}
):
\Omega_{\infty}
is the non-overlapping union of a finite number of infinite tetrahedra, that is

(27)

{\overline{\Omega}_{\infty}}=T_{1}\cup T_{2}\cup\dots\cup T_{M},

with
T_{1}
,..,
T_{M}
,
M\geqslant 1
, are
M
infinite tetrahedra satisfying the assumptions

•

T_{1}
,..,
T_{M}
have a common fictitious vertex. Subsequently, we assume that this common fictitious vertex is the origin.

•

the intersection of two arbitrary infinite tetrahedra
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
,
1\leqslant i<j\leqslant M
, is either the empty set, a whole edge (a half-line) or a whole unbounded face.

The concept of infinite tetrahedron and, more generally, of infinite simplices, was introduced in
[
5
]
. For the sake of clarity, we recall here this concept in 3D configurations. Given four non-coplanar points
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{3}
of the euclidian affine space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, define the infinite tetrahedon
T
whose vertices are
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{3}
as the set of all the points
{\boldsymbol{x}}
which take the form

{\boldsymbol{x}}=\lambda_{0}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}+\lambda_{1}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}+\lambda_{2}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}+\lambda_{3}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{3},\;\sum_{i=0}^{3}\lambda_{i}=1,

with
\lambda_{0}\leqslant 0
,
\lambda_{i}\geqslant 0
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant 3
. It is usual to call
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
the fictitious vertex of
T
, while

{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{3}
are called the real vertices. It is worth noting that
T
is closed and convex. The tetrahedron
S_{T}
, associated to
T
, is the convex hull of the points
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{3}
. The
altitude vector
of
T
is
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{T}=\pi_{T}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}-{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
, where
\pi_{T}{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
is the orthogonal projection of
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0}
on the affine plane containing
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{1}
,
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{2}
and
{\boldsymbol{a}}_{3}

and separating
T
and
S_{T}
. Notice that

(28)

\forall{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T\cap S_{T},\;{\boldsymbol{h}}_{T}.({\boldsymbol{x}}-{\boldsymbol{a}}_{0})=|{\boldsymbol{h}}_{T}|^{2}.

\{{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\;|\;|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|+|x_{3}|<R\}
and

as the union of

infinite tetrahedra (see Figure

{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
in which
\Omega_{0}
is the octahedron
\{{\boldsymbol{x}}=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\;|\;|x_{1}|+|x_{2}|+|x_{3}|<R\}
. Here
\Omega_{\infty}
is the union of 8 infinite tetrahedra.

Subsequently, we denote by
S_{i}
,
1\leqslant i\leqslant M
, the tetrahedron associated to
T_{i}
and by
{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}
the altitude vector corresponding of
T_{i}
. We have

\overline{\Omega_{0}}\cap\overline{\Omega_{\infty}}=\cup_{i=1}^{M}(T_{i}\cap S_{i}).

Set

(29)

r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}.{\boldsymbol{x}}}{|{\boldsymbol{h}}_{i}|^{2}}\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in S_{i}\cup T_{i}.

Since
{\boldsymbol{0}}
is the fictitious vertex of each
T_{i}
and in view of (
28
) it can easily be proved that

r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\geqslant 1\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{i},\;r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\leqslant 1\mbox{ for }\in S_{i},\mbox{ and }r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=1\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{i}\cap S_{i}.

In terms of the local barycentric coordinates in
S_{i}
,
(\lambda^{(i)}_{0},\lambda^{(i)}_{1},\lambda^{(i)}_{2},\lambda^{(i)}_{3})
, we can write

r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=1-\lambda^{(i)}_{0}({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{k=1}^{3}\lambda^{(i)}_{k}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{i}\cup S_{i}.

The following continuity property holds true: if
T_{i}
and
T_{j}
are neighbors, then

(30)

r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})=r_{j}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mbox{ for all }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{i}\cap T_{j}.

The local polygonal inversion associated to

T
is defined as

(31)

\begin{array}[]{rrll}\phi_{i}\;\;:&\;(S_{i}\cup T_{i})\backslash\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}&\longrightarrow&(S_{i}\cup T_{i})\backslash\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\},\\
&{\boldsymbol{x}}&\mapsto&\displaystyle{\frac{{\boldsymbol{x}}}{r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})^{2}}.}\end{array}

Obviously
\phi_{i}
is a bijection between
S_{i}
and
T_{i}
. It is also an involution which preserves

T_{i}\cap S_{i}
, that is
\phi_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{x}}
for
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{i}\cap S_{i}
.

Define now the
global polygonal inversion

\phi
from
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\{0\}
into itself
and the
global polygonal radius

r(.)
as follows

r({\boldsymbol{x}})=r_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mbox{ and }\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})=\phi_{i}({\boldsymbol{x}})\mbox{ for all }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in T_{i}\cup S_{i},1\leqslant i\leqslant M.

In virtue of property (
30
),
r
and
\phi
are well defined and
continuous
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. Moreover,

\phi
maps
\Omega_{\infty}
into
\Omega_{0}
and conversely. We have

(32)

\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})={\boldsymbol{x}}\mbox{ for all }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\overline{\Omega_{0}}\cap\overline{\Omega_{\infty}}.

Moreover, the exists two constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
such that

c|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\leqslant r({\boldsymbol{x}})\leqslant c_{2}|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\mbox{ for all }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3},

In other words,
r({\boldsymbol{x}})\sim|{\boldsymbol{x}}|
and
|\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})|\sim{|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{-1}}\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
. In the sequel,

\Omega_{\star}
stands for the image of
\Omega_{\infty}
by the inversion
\phi
. From a strictly mathematical
point of view
\Omega_{\star}=\Omega_{0}\backslash\{{\boldsymbol{0}}\}
. However, since
\Omega_{0}
and
\Omega_{\star}
will be meshed differently. We will therefore deal with them separately.

We now consider a family of pairs of
triangulations
({{\mathcal{T}}_{h}},{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star})_{h}
where

•

({{\mathcal{T}}_{h}})_{h}
are regular triangulations of
\Omega_{0}
satisfying the usual conformity assumptions (see
[
5
]
or
[
7
]
). In particular, elements of
{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}
are supposed shape regular in the usual sense: there exists a constant
c_{0}>0
not depending on
h
such that

(33)

\max_{K\in{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}}\frac{h_{K}}{\rho_{K}}\leqslant c_{0}.

Here
h_{K}
and
\rho_{K}
are respectively the diameter of
K
and the diameter of the sphere inscribed inside of the tetrahedron
K
.

•

({\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star})_{h}
are regular triangulations of
\Omega_{\star}
which satisfies, besides (
33
), the following additional requirement:

–

for each
K_{\star}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}
, there exists
i\leqslant M
such that
K_{\star}\subset S_{i}
(in other words,
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}
is a conforming union of triangulations of the subdomains
S_{1}
,..,
S_{M}
).

–

the triangulations
({\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star})_{h}
are
\mu
-graded, where
\mu\in(0,1]
is a fixed parameter. That means that there exists three constants
c^{\star}_{1}>0
,
c^{\star}_{2}>0
and
c^{\star}_{3}>0
, not depending on
h
, such that

(34)

\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star\star}}\frac{h_{K}}{d^{1-\mu}_{K}}\leqslant c^{\star}_{1}h,\;

(35)

\max_{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}\backslash{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star\star}}h_{K}\leqslant c^{\star}_{2}h^{1/\mu},\;

(36)

\min_{K_{\star}\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star\star}}d_{K_{\star}}\geqslant c^{\star}_{3}h^{1/\mu},

where
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star\star}=\{K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}\;|\;{\boldsymbol{0}}\not\in K\}
(elements not touching the origin),
d_{K}=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{x}}\in K}|{{\boldsymbol{x}}}|
for all
K\in{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}
, and

h=h({{\mathcal{T}}_{h}},{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star})=\max_{K\in{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}\cup{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}}h_{K},

Conditions (
34
), (
35
) and (
36
) mean that tetrahedra of

{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}
which are adjacent to
\partial\Omega_{\star}\cap\partial\Omega_{\infty}
have a size of order
h
, while those touching the fictitious vertex
{\boldsymbol{0}}
have a size of order
h^{1/\mu}
. Construction of graded meshes is detailed in
[
5
]
.

•

{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}
and
{\mathcal{T}}_{h}^{\star}
have the same vertices, edges and faces on the common boundary
\Omega_{0}\cap\Omega_{\infty}=\partial\Omega_{0}=\partial\Omega_{\star}\backslash\{0\}
.

In the sequel, given a function
{\boldsymbol{v}}
defined over
\Omega_{\infty}
,
{\hat{v}}
stands for
the function
defined on
\Omega_{\star}
as follows

(37)

{\hat{v}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star})=\frac{1}{r({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star})^{\gamma}}v(\phi({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star})),\;\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star}\in\Omega_{\star}.

with
\gamma>0
a parameter. Conversely, we have

(38)

v({\boldsymbol{x}})=\frac{1}{r({\boldsymbol{x}})^{\gamma}\ }{\hat{v}}(\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})),\;\mbox{ for }{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega_{\infty}.

Now, let
k\geqslant 1
be a fixed integer and consider the finite dimensional space

W_{h}=\{v\in{\mathscr{C}}^{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})\;|\;\forall K\in\mathcal{T}_{h},v_{|K}\in({\mathbb{P}}_{k})^{3};\;\;\forall K^{*}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}^{*},{\hat{v}}_{|K^{*}}\in({\mathbb{P}}_{k})^{3},\,{\hat{v}}({\boldsymbol{0}})=0\}.

We may observe that functions of
W_{h}
are piecewise polynomial in the FEM region
\Omega_{0}
, but not in the IFEM
region
\Omega_{\infty}
. The last observation is due to distorsion resulting from the composition with the inversion
and the multiplicative factor involved in formula (
37
).

Another observation concerns the behavior at large distances of functions belonging to
W_{h}
. Let
{v}\in W_{h}
. Then,
v\in H^{1}_{loc}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}
. Moreover, since
{\hat{v}}({\boldsymbol{0}})=0
, we have

|{\hat{v}}({\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star})|\leqslant C_{0}|{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star}|,\mbox{ for all }{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star}\in\Omega_{\star},

for some constant
C_{0}
, not depending on
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{\star}
. It follows that for all
{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\Omega_{\infty}
, we have

|v({\boldsymbol{x}})|=|r({\boldsymbol{x}})|^{-\gamma}|{\hat{v}}(\phi({\boldsymbol{x}}))|\leqslant C|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{-\gamma}|\phi({\boldsymbol{x}})|\leqslant C|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{-\gamma-1}.

In similar way, we prove that

|\nabla v({\boldsymbol{x}})|\leqslant C|{\boldsymbol{x}}|^{-\gamma-2}.

We deduce this

(39)

\gamma>-\frac{1}{2}\Longrightarrow W_{h}{\hookrightarrow}W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}.

This justifies the following assumption on
\gamma
:

(40)

\gamma>-\frac{1}{2}.

The discrete problem writes: find
u_{h}\in W_{h}
such that

(41)

\forall w_{h}\in W_{h},\;\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}\nabla u_{h}.\nabla w_{h}dx=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{M}}.\nabla w_{h}dx.

The corresponding stray-field energy is given by

(42)

{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u_{h}|^{2}dx=\int_{\Omega}{\boldsymbol{M}}.\nabla u_{h}dx.

We have

Proposition 3.1
.

The discrete problem (
41
) has one and only one solution
u_{h}\in W_{h}
and
{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)
. If in addition,
\gamma>0
and
u\in W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, then

(43)

\displaystyle\|u-u_{h}\|_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{1}h^{\tau k}\|u\|_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}},

(44)

\displaystyle 0\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{2}h^{\tau k}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}},

where
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
are two constants not depending on
h
,
{\boldsymbol{M}}
and
u
, and

(45)

\tau=\min(\frac{\gamma}{\mu k},1).

Proposition
3.1
states in particular that if
u\in W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
and if the mesh of
\Omega_{\star}
is graded enough (
\mu\leqslant\frac{\eta}{k}
), then the error is similar to that held in the finite element method in bounded domains, that is

(46)

\|u-u_{h}\|_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}\leqslant Ch^{k}\|u\|_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}.

We also have

(47)

0\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})\leqslant C_{2}h^{k}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|u\|_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}.

When smoothness of
u
is only local, we have this

Proposition 3.2
.

Assume
u_{|{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}}\in W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})
and
u_{|\Omega}\in H^{k+1}(\Omega)
. Assume also that
\Omega\subset\Omega_{0}
and that
{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}_{|\Omega}
is a triangulation of
\Omega
. Then,

(48)

\|u-u_{h}\|_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}\leqslant C_{1}(h^{\tau k}\|u\|_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}(\Omega_{\infty})}+h^{k}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Omega)}+h^{k}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Omega_{0}\backslash\overline{\Omega})}),

(49)

0\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})\leqslant C_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}(h^{\tau k}\|u\|_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}(\Omega_{\infty})}+h^{k}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Omega)}+h^{k}\|u\|_{H^{k+1}(\Omega_{0}\backslash\overline{\Omega})}).

with
\tau
given by (
45
).

From Proposition
2.4
, we know that if
\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}\in L^{2}(\Omega)
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}\in H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)
, then
u_{|{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega}}\in W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})
and
u_{|\Omega}\in H^{2}(\Omega)
. With
k=1
(P1 like elements) and
\gamma=1
, we get the error estimate

(50)

\|u-u_{h}\|_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}\leqslant Ch(\|u\|_{W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})}+\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}),

(51)

0\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})\leqslant C_{2}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}h(\|u\|_{W^{2}_{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{\Omega})}+\|u\|_{H^{2}(\Omega)}),

for any
\mu\in(0,1]
. This estimate is similar to the usual finite element error for elliptic problems in
bounded domain.

Proof of Propositions
3.1
and
3.2
–
Observe first that
u
is also solution of the minimization problem

\min_{v\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}F(v),\;\mbox{ with }F(v)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla v|^{2}-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{M}}.\nabla vdx,

and, by virtue of (
16
), we have

F(u)=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx=-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}({\boldsymbol{M}}),

Similarly, the approximate solution
u_{h}
is solution of

\min_{{\boldsymbol{v}}_{h}\in W_{h}}F(v_{h}),

and, in view of (
41
), we have

F(u_{h})=-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u_{h}|^{2}dx=-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h}),

Since
W_{h}\subset W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}
, we deduce that
F(u_{h})\geqslant F(u)
. Thus,
{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)
.

Now, Céa’s lemma gives

\|u-u_{h}\|_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}\leqslant C_{1}\inf_{w_{h}\in W_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}.

for some constant
C_{1}
not depending on
u
nor on
h
. In
[
5
]
, the following estimate is proven:

\inf_{w_{h}\in W_{h}}\|u-w_{h}\|^{2}_{W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})^{3}}\leqslant C(h^{2\tau k}\|u\|^{2}_{W^{k+1}_{k+\gamma}(\Omega_{\infty})}+h^{2k}\sum_{K\in{{\mathcal{T}}_{h}}}\|u\|^{2}_{H^{k+1}(K)}).

We easily get estimate (
43
) and (
48
). In addition, we have

0\leqslant{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx-\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}|\nabla u_{h}|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{3}}{\boldsymbol{M}}.(\nabla u-\nabla u_{h})dx.

Thus,

{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)-{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u_{h})\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\|{\boldsymbol{M}}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.\|\nabla u-\nabla u_{h}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}.

Combining with (
43
) gives (
44
). Estimates (
48
) and (
49
) are obtained by the same argument.

\blacksquare

Appendix A
Solving the problem in the case of an inhomogeneously magnetized ball (numerical example 2)

The resolution of the system in the case of a ball
B_{r_{0}}
and
{\boldsymbol{M}}
given by (
55
) can be done by means of a decomposition on spherical harmonics
(Y_{\ell}^{m})_{\ell\geqslant 0,-\ell\leqslant m\leqslant\ell}
(which is orthonormal with respect to the inner product in
L^{2}({\mathbb{S}}^{2})
). We have outside the ball
B_{r_{0}}
:

\Delta u=0\mbox{ in }{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{B}_{r_{0}}.

Developping
u
on the basis of spherical harmonics gives (see, e. g.,
[
19
]
):

u({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}A_{\ell}^{m}\left(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}}\right)^{-\ell-1}Y_{\ell}^{m}(\varphi,\theta).

where
(A_{\ell}^{m})_{\ell\geqslant 0,-\ell\leqslant m\leqslant\ell}
is a sequence of complex coefficients. On the other hand, we have in the interior of the ball

\Delta u=\mathrm{div}\,{\boldsymbol{M}}=2\frac{\cos\theta}{r}=\frac{\alpha}{r}Y_{1}^{0}(\varphi,\theta)\mbox{ in }\overline{B}_{r_{0}},\;\;\mbox{ with }\alpha=4\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{3}}.

Writing

u({\boldsymbol{x}})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{+\infty}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}u_{\ell}^{m}(r)Y_{\ell}^{m}(\varphi,\theta),\mbox{ in }\overline{B}_{r_{0}},

gives:

\frac{1}{r^{2}}\frac{d}{dr}(r^{2}\frac{du_{\ell}^{m}}{dr})(r)-\frac{\ell(\ell+1)}{r^{2}}u_{\ell}^{m}(r)=\frac{\alpha}{r}\delta_{\ell,1}\delta_{m,0}\mbox{ for all }\ell\geqslant 0\mbox{ and }-\ell\leqslant m\leqslant\ell,

where
\delta_{i,j}
,
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
j\in{\mathbb{N}}
, denotes the usual Kronecker delta. The solutions of this equations are of the form

u_{\ell}^{m}(r)=B_{\ell}^{m}\left(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}}\right)^{\ell}+C_{\ell}^{m}\left(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}}\right)^{-\ell-1}+\frac{\alpha}{3}r\ln(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}})\delta_{\ell,1}\delta_{m,0},

where
B_{\ell}^{m}
and
C_{\ell}^{m}
are constants. Since

u\in W^{1}_{0}({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
we deduce that
u_{B_{r_{0}}}\in H^{1}(B_{r_{0}})
. Necessarily

C_{\ell}^{m}=0
for all
\ell\geqslant 0
and
|m|\leqslant\ell
. Since
[u]_{\partial\Omega}=0
, we deduce that

B_{\ell}^{m}=A_{\ell}^{m}
for all
\ell\geqslant 0
and
|m|\leqslant\ell
. In addition,

\left[\frac{\partial u}{\partial r}\right]_{\partial\Omega}=-{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{n}}=-{\boldsymbol{M}}.{\boldsymbol{e}}_{r}=0.

Thus, for all
\ell\geqslant 0
and
|m|\leqslant\ell
we have

\ell\frac{B^{m}_{\ell}}{{r_{0}}}+\frac{\alpha}{3}\delta_{\ell,1}\delta_{m,0}=-\frac{\ell+1}{{r_{0}}}A^{m}_{\ell}.

Thus,
A_{l}^{m}=B_{l}^{m}=0
for
(\ell,m)\neq(1,0)
and

A_{1}^{0}=B_{1}^{0}=-\frac{\alpha{r_{0}}}{9}.

Thus, if
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\geqslant{r_{0}}
then

u({\boldsymbol{x}})=A_{1}^{0}\left(\frac{{r_{0}}}{r}\right)^{2}Y_{1}^{0}=-\frac{{r_{0}}^{3}}{9r^{2}}\alpha Y_{1}^{0}=-\frac{2{r_{0}}^{3}\cos\theta}{9r^{2}}=-\frac{2{r_{0}}^{3}z}{9r^{3}}.

and

\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\backslash\overline{B}_{r_{0}})}=\frac{32\pi}{243}{r_{0}}^{3}.

If
|{\boldsymbol{x}}|\leqslant{r_{0}}
then

u({\boldsymbol{x}})=(B_{1}^{0}\frac{r}{{r_{0}}}+\frac{\alpha}{3}r\ln(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}}))Y_{1}^{0}=(-\frac{r}{9}+\frac{r}{3}\ln(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}}))\alpha Y_{1}^{0}=\frac{2r\cos\theta}{9}(-1+3\ln(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}})).

Thus,

u=\frac{2z}{9}(-1+3\ln(\frac{r}{{r_{0}}})),

and

\|\nabla u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\overline{B}_{r_{0}})}=\frac{64\pi}{243}{r_{0}}^{3}.

Thus, the energy of the corresponding stray-field is

{\mathscr{E}}_{sf}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{{r_{0}}^{3}}|\nabla u|^{2}dx=\frac{16}{81}\pi{r_{0}}^{3}.
