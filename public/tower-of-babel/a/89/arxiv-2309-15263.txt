Singularities of the solution to a Monge–Ampère equation on the boundary of the 3-simplex
Mattias Jonsson, Nicholas McCleerey, Neil Patram, Benjamin W. Scott

Abstract
. We show that the metric defined by the solution to the tropical Monge-Ampère equation, as defined by Hultgren, Mazzon, and the first two authors, on the boundary of the 3-simplex is asymptotic to the Gross-Wilson metric on S 2 S^{2} near each of the 6 singular points. We deduce in addition that the solution is not C 1 , 1 C^{1,1} across the singular points. Compared to previous works, our starting point is the real Monge-Ampère equation, as opposed to the complex structure.



1.
Introduction

Motivated by the Strominger-Yau-Zaslow conjecture
[
SYZ96
]
in mirror symmetry, in
[
GW00
]
, Gross and Wilson study the “large complex-structure limit” of generic K3 surfaces. We briefly recall their results. A generic K3 is an elliptic fibration over
\mathbb{CP}^{1}
with 24 isolated singularities; after a hyperkähler rotation and a renormalization, the large complex-structure limit can be reduced to the limit when the volume of the fibres tends to zero.

When the fibres are sufficiently small, Gross-Wilson glue model metrics (due to Ooguri-Vafa) near the singular fibres onto an explicit semi-flat metric, producing a nearly Ricci-flat metric on the K3. The metric is a good approximation for the genuine Ricci-flat metrics produced by the Calabi-Yau theorem, and so it is shown that the two converge to the same affine metric on
S^{2}\setminus\{\text{24 points}\}
in the Gromov-Hausdorff sense; the metric is moreover a “Monge-Ampère metric” in the sense that its local convex potentials solve a real Monge-Ampère equation.

This motivates the idea of first constructing a Monge-Ampère metric on the base, with some number of isolated singularities, and then using that to study the degenerating complex structure. In dimension 2, the first examples of such metrics were constructed in
[
GSVY90
]
; subsequently, Loftin
[
Lof05
]
constructed many such metrics on
S^{2}
and showed they were asymptotic to the metric of Gross-Wilson near the singular points. One important property that he is unable to show however is the integrality of the induced singular affine structure.

On the other hand, if one assumes the existence of such a Monge-Ampère metric (in some form), then Yang Li has recently shown in a series of breakthrough works
[
Li22
,
Li20
]
how to relate it to the degenerating complex metrics, thereby proving a version of the SYZ conjecture on certain degenerating families.

It is thus an important question to study these singular Monge-Ampère metrics in greater detail. This is a highly non-trivial question, see e.g.
[
Li20
,
Li22
]
for a discussion of the difficulties. In certain cases however, progress can be made – we focus in particular on the recent paper
[
HJMM22
]
, where the problem is solved satisfactorily by assuming a high degree of symmetry on the base. In that paper, by realizing
S^{2}
as the boundary of a symmetric
3
-simplex,
\partial\Delta
, one obtains a natural singular affine structure; let
\lambda
be the corresponding Lebesgue measure (coming from pulling back the usual Lebesgue measure in charts). A notion of “
c
-convexity” for continuous functions on
\partial\Delta
is then defined, and it is shown there exists an (essentially) unique
c
-convex solution to the “tropical Monge-Ampère equation:”

(1.1)

\nu_{\psi}=\lambda,

where
\nu_{\psi}
is the “tropical Monge-Ampère measure,” see
[
HJMM22
]
. The Hessian metric,
g
, derived from
\psi
is then shown to be the required Monge-Ampère metric; it has singularities at exactly 6 points, corresponding to the midpoints of the edges of
\partial\Delta
.

The metric
g
can be plugged into the machinery of
[
Li20
]
for certain degenerating families of K3’s, and so it is natural to relate it to the metrics of Gross-Wilson and Loftin. This is the task we take up in this paper:

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
g
is the Monge-Ampère metric on
S^{2}
minus 6 points constructed in
[
HJMM22
]
. Then, near any singular point, there exists a complex coordinate
z
such that
g(z)
is asymptotic to the Gross-Wilson metric
[
Lof05
, Section 3]
, i.e.:

g(z)=(-\log|z|+O(1))|dz|^{2}.

The main difficulty in showing Theorem
1.1
is in producing the complex coordinate;
[
GW00
,
Lof05
]
work exclusively in complex coordinates, which is convenient for describing the asymptotics of the metric, but provides very little explicit information about the affine structure and convex potential. Meanwhile,
[
HJMM22
]
starts by fixing the affine structure (so that it is automatically integral, for instance); this comes at the cost of locking the complex structure behind a non-explicit coordinate change.

We show that, using a local convex potential
\varphi
for
g
, it is possible to compute the requisite coordinate transformation in terms of the derivatives of
\varphi
. A technical difficulty is that
\varphi
cannot be chosen to be smooth in a neighborhood of the singular point; this is a natural phenomenon, but makes it necessary to chose the coordinate carefully, so that it is continuous. This is accomplished by using symmetries. We then show that the identity function is a “special” holomorphic coordinate for the special Kähler structure induced by
g
. This utilizes computations from
[
CH19
,
Hay15
]
, and ultimately shows the conformal factor is harmonic, from whence Theorem
1.1
follows.

In fact, our method seems to work more generally for Monge-Ampère metrics coming from optimal transport problems where there is a joint
\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}
symmetry on the domain and range. While we do not pursue this further, it seems an interesting problem to look for more general transport problems where similar results hold; such problems may be relevant to the metrics contructed in
[
AH23
,
Li23
]
, for instance.

Finally, the asymptotics we deduce using our conformal transformation can be used to show that the potential of the metric in
[
HJMM22
]
is not
C^{1,1}
across the singular points:

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\psi
be the
c
-convex solution to (
1.1
) on
\partial\Delta
. Then
\psi
is not
C^{1,1}
across any of the 6 singular points.

By the main result of
[
SY20
]
,
\psi
is
C^{1,\alpha}
for any
0<\alpha<1
, so Theorem
1.2
is optimal.

We conclude the introduction with a brief outline of the rest of the paper. In Section 2, we recall the setup of
[
HJMM22
]
, and use it to derive a local optimal transport problem, whose solution can be used to recover the
c
-convex function
\psi
. In Section 3, we investigate the symmetries of the optimal transport problem, and use them to show that certain functions, constructed from the derivatives of the convex potential
\varphi
, are continuous. Finally, in Section 4, we construct our isothermal coordinates, and prove Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Proposition 3.1
.

The potential
\varphi
is smooth on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}
. Moreover:

\varphi=\varphi\circ R=\varphi\circ A\text{ on }\Omega\setminus\{y=x\},

and
\partial\varphi(\Omega^{\pm\pm})=\Theta^{\pm\pm}.
In addition, it is strictly convex on this domain, in the sense that
D^{2}(\varphi)>0
at all points in the interior.

Proof.

The proposition follows from the discussion in Section 2, but here we give a proof using only optimal transport theory.

We work with
\psi:=\varphi^{\vee}
, the Legendre transform of
\varphi
. The map
\partial\psi
is the optimal map sending
\Theta
to
\Omega
with
\Omega
convex; thus, by
[
Caf92
]
,
\psi
is
C^{2}
and strictly convex in
\Theta
, so
\partial\psi
is a local homeomorphism whose image is
\Omega
, in an a.e. sense. This regularity of
\psi
, along with some standard results of convex analysis
[
Roc70
]
, imply
\psi^{\vee}=\varphi
and therefore
\partial\varphi\circ\partial\psi=\mathrm{Id}_{2}
. It will therefore suffice to show
\partial\psi(\Theta^{\pm\pm})=\Omega^{\pm\pm},
and then conclude

\partial\varphi(\Omega^{\pm\pm})=\partial\varphi\circ\partial\psi(\Theta^{\pm\pm})=\Theta^{\pm\pm}.

The necessary fact is the
cyclical monotonicity
of the transport map
\partial\psi.
That is, for points
v_{1},\cdots,v_{n+1}\in\Theta,
where
v_{n+1}=v_{1},
we have

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\langle v_{k+1},\partial\psi(v_{k+1})-\partial\psi(v_{k})\rangle\geq 0.

For
n=2,
this reduces to

\langle v-w,\partial\psi(v)-\partial\psi(w)\rangle\geq 0

for any
v,w\in\Theta.

Since the symmetry
R^{T}
is linear and
R^{T}(\Theta)=\Theta
, the function
\psi\circ R^{T}
is another convex function on
\Theta.
Additionally, by the chain rule

\partial(\psi\circ R^{T})(\Theta)=R\circ\partial\psi\circ R^{T}(\Theta)=R\circ\partial\psi(\Theta)=R(\Omega)=\Omega.

Therefore by the uniqueness part of Brenier’s Theorem, we have
\psi=\psi\circ R
. Now take a point
(x,y)\in\Theta.
Cyclical monotonicity tells us

\langle(x,y)-R^{T}(x,y),\partial\psi(x,y)-\partial(\psi\circ R^{T})(R^{T}(x,y))\rangle\geq 0.

Then by the chain rule we have

\langle(x-y,y-x),(I-R)\partial\psi(x,y)\rangle\geq 0.

Then the above inequality tells us

(x-y)(\psi_{x}-\psi_{y})+(y-x)(-\psi_{x}+\psi_{y})=2(x-y)(\psi_{x}-\psi_{y})\geq 0.

Since
x<y
on
\Theta^{+}
and
y<x
on
\Theta^{-}
, we find that
\psi_{x}\leq\psi_{y}
on
\Theta^{+}
and
\psi_{y}\leq\psi_{x}
on
\Theta^{+}.
This fact, coupled with the fact that
\partial\psi
is an open map preserving Lebesgue measure, implies
\partial\psi(\Theta^{\pm})=\Omega^{\pm}
. In fact, the uniqueness part of Brenier’s Theorem implies that
\partial\psi
is also the optimal map in the transport problems between
\Theta^{\pm}
and
\Omega^{\pm}.

We now demonstrate the further decomposition
\partial\psi(\Theta^{\pm\pm})=\Omega^{\pm\pm}.
We restrict our focus to
\Theta^{-};
the other half follows similarly. Since
A^{T}
is linear on
\Theta^{-}
and
\partial\psi
is optimal between
\Theta^{-}
and
\Omega^{-},
applying the same argument as before gives
\psi=\psi\circ A^{T}
. Let
(x,y)\in\Omega^{-}.
Then by cyclical monotonicity

\langle(x,y)-A^{T}(x,y),\partial\psi(x,y)-\partial(\psi\circ A^{T})(A^{T}(x,y))\rangle\geq 0.

Simplifying gives

\langle(3x+3y,-x-y),(I-A)\partial\psi(x,y)\rangle\geq 0.

Thus,

(3x+3y)(3\psi_{x}-\psi_{y})+(-x-y)(3\psi_{x}-\psi_{y})=2(x+y)(3\psi_{x}-\psi_{y})\geq 0.

Since
x<-y
on
\Theta^{-+}
and
y<-x
on
\Theta^{--}
, we see that
3\psi_{x}\leq\psi_{y}
on
\Theta^{-+}
and
\psi_{y}\leq 3\psi_{y}
on
\Theta^{--}
. As before we find
\partial\psi(\Theta^{-\pm})=\Omega^{-\pm}
and
\partial\psi
is optimal between
\Theta^{-\pm}
and
\Omega^{-\pm}
. Analogous results hold for the other half.

We have succeeded in demonstrating
\partial\varphi(\Omega^{\pm\pm})=\Theta^{\pm\pm}
and now turn our focus towards establishing symmetry and regularity of
\varphi
on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}.
Since
\psi
is invariant under the involutive transformations
R^{T}
and
A^{T}
we find that
\varphi
is invariant under the transformations
R
and
A
. Since
\Theta^{+}
and
\Omega^{+}
are convex regions and
\partial\varphi(\Omega^{+})=\Theta^{+}
, by
[
Caf92
]

\varphi
is also strictly convex and smooth on
\Omega^{+}
; similar statements hold for
\varphi
on
\Omega^{-}
. Since
\partial\varphi(\Omega^{+-}\cup\Omega^{--})=\Theta^{+-}\cup\Theta^{--}
and these regions are convex,
[
Caf92
]
again gives smoothness and strict convexity of
\varphi
on
\Omega^{+-}\cup\Omega^{--}
. Thus we have demonstrated that
\varphi
is smooth and strictly convex on an open cover of
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}
and therefore conclude.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Define:

v:=\varphi_{x}+\varphi_{y}\quad\text{ and }\quad\rho:=v_{x}+v_{y}\quad\text{ on }\ \ \Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}.

Then
v=v\circ R=-v\circ A
and
\rho=\rho\circ R=\rho\circ A
on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\}
, and hence
v
and
\rho
admit continuous extensions to
\Omega
and
\Omega^{*}
, respectively, which we still denote by
v
and
\rho
. Moreover,
\rho
is strictly positive on
\Omega^{*}
.

Proof.

Working on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\}
, we have that:

\partial\varphi=\partial(\varphi\circ A)=A^{T}(\partial\varphi)\circ A.

We may now sum the columns of
A^{T}
to conclude that:

\varphi_{x}+\varphi_{y}=-\varphi_{x}(A)-\varphi_{y}(A),

i.e.
v=-v\circ A
. Similarly, we see that
v=v\circ R
, since the columns of
R^{T}
sum to
1
. The exact same process works to show
\rho=\rho\circ R=\rho\circ A
.

The symmetries
v=-v\circ A
and
\rho=\rho\circ A
now imply that
v
and
\rho
extend continuously to
\Omega^{*}.
In addition,
[
Caf96
, Theorem C3]
and
[
SY20
]
dictate that
\varphi
is
C^{1,\alpha}
on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}
for some
\alpha>0.
Hence
v
extends continuously to
\Omega.

We are left to check
\rho>0
. By the symmetry
\rho=\rho\circ A,
we can restrict ourselves to the set where
\varphi
is smooth. The Monge-Ampère equation
\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}-\varphi_{xy}^{2}=1
and the fact that the square root function is strictly increasing together imply

\varphi_{xy}\geq-\sqrt{\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}-1}>-\sqrt{\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}}.

Thus,

\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy}>\varphi_{xx}-2\sqrt{\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}}+\varphi_{yy}=(\sqrt{\varphi_{xx}}-\sqrt{\varphi_{yy}})^{2}\geq 0.\qed

4.
Isothermal Coordinates and the Special Kähler Structure

We now find isothermal coordinates for the Hessian metric
g:=\nabla d\varphi
on
\Omega\setminus\{x=y\geq 0\}
, in terms of the potential. By Corollary
3.2
, both the coordinates and the conformal factor extend continuously, but not smoothly, across the slit. However, the key point is that both fail to be smooth in exactly the same way – namely, by the transformation
A
. This will allow us to show in Proposition
4.2
that the conformal factor actually extends smoothly in the isothermal coordinates.

We can then define a special Kähler structure (SKS) on the punctured disk in these isothermal coordinates. The isothermals we choose are moreover seen to be “special holomorphic” for this SKS, and so by
[
CH19
, Proposition 10.2.9]
, we see that the conformal factor is harmonic. It follows that the SKS is of logarithmic type with order 1; as previously mentioned, this means it is of the same type as the Gross-Wilson metric
[
GW00
,
Lof05
]

Proposition 4.1
.

Define:

u(x,y):=x-y.

Then the coordinate change
f(x,y)=(u(x,y),v(x,y))
is a homeomorphism on
\Omega
, smooth on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\},
mapping the subdomains
\Omega^{\pm\pm}
into the four quadrants of the plane and fixing the origin. Moreover, the
(u,v)
-coordinates are isothermal for
g
on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}
; specifically, we have:

g=\rho^{-1}|du^{2}+dv^{2}|\text{ on }\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}.

Proof.

By writing down the
g
-Laplacian in
(x,y)
-coordinates, it is easy to check that (many) linear combinations of
x,y,\varphi_{x},
and
\varphi_{y}
define isothermal coordinates for
g
on the set where it is smooth. Here however, we will prove the proposition by direct computations.

By Corollary
3.2
, we see that
f
is continuous on
\Omega
and smooth on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}.
To demonstrate the injectivity of
f
it will suffice to prove it along lines of the form
x-y=c
. We have:

\tfrac{d}{dx}v(x,x-c)=\varphi_{xx}(x,x-c)+2\varphi_{xy}(x,x-c)+\varphi_{yy}(x,x-c),

on
(\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\})\cap\{x-y=c\}
, so
v
is strictly increasing along these lines. We can extend this to the exceptional line
\{x=y\}
by the symmetry
v=-v\circ A
(which additionally implies
v(0,0)=0
). Thus,
f
is a continuous injection and therefore a homeomorphism.

To see that
f
maps the subdomains
\Omega^{\pm\pm}
to the four quadrants of the plane, we observe first that
u<0
on
\Omega^{+}
and
u>0
on
\Omega^{-}.
Then by Proposition
3.1
, along with the definitions of
\Theta^{\pm\pm},
we see that
v<0
on
\Omega^{+-}\cup\Omega^{--}
and
v>0
on
\Omega^{++}\cup\Omega^{-+}.

The proof that
(u,v)
-coordinates are isothermal is a computation. We have:

\displaystyle du^{2}+dv^{2}

\displaystyle=(dx-dy)^{2}+((\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{xy})dx+(\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy})dy)^{2}

\displaystyle=(1+\varphi_{xx}^{2}+2\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{xy}^{2})dx^{2}

\displaystyle+(-1+\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}+\varphi_{xy}^{2}+\varphi_{xy}\varphi_{yy})(dxdy+dydx)

\displaystyle+(1+\varphi_{xy}^{2}+2\varphi_{xy}\varphi_{yy}+\varphi_{yy}^{2})dy^{2}.

Using the Monge-Ampère equation
\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}-\varphi_{xy}^{2}=1
to eliminate the constant terms then gives:

\displaystyle du^{2}+dv^{2}

\displaystyle=\varphi_{xx}(\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy})dx^{2}

\displaystyle+\varphi_{xy}(\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy})(dxdy+dydx)

\displaystyle+\varphi_{yy}(\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy})dy^{2}

\displaystyle=(\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy})g.\qed

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
\widetilde{\Omega}=f(\Omega)
and
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}=\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. Then
\widetilde{\Omega}
is invariant under the linear transformations:

G=\begin{pmatrix}-1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}\quad\quad H=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{pmatrix}.

Moreover,
\rho
is invariant under these transformations, and hence
g
admits a smooth extension

g:=\rho^{-1}|du^{2}+dv^{2}|

to all of
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}
, which is Kähler with respect to the standard
(u,v)
-complex structure.

Proof.

The invariance of
\widetilde{\Omega}
under the transformations
G
and
H
follows from the symmetries
u=-u\circ R=u\circ A
and
v=v\circ R=-v\circ A
. Indeed,
f\circ R(x,y)=G\circ f(x,y)
and
f\circ A(x,y)=H\circ f(x,y),
so

\displaystyle G(\widetilde{\Omega})=G\circ f(\Omega)=f\circ R(\Omega)=f(\Omega)=\widetilde{\Omega}

\displaystyle H(\widetilde{\Omega})=H\circ f(\Omega)=f\circ A(\Omega)=f(\Omega)=\widetilde{\Omega}

.

Additionally, Proposition
4.1
implies that
f
is smooth on
\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\}
and that
f(\Omega\setminus\{y=x\geq 0\})=\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0,v\geq 0\}
. It follows that
\rho
is smooth on
\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0,v\geq 0\}
. The symmetry
\rho=\rho\circ A
in
(x,y)
-coordinates becomes the symmetry
\rho=\rho\circ H
in
(u,v)
-coordinates, and since
H
is linear, we find that
\rho
is actually smooth on
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}
; it follows that
g
admits the desired smooth extension to all of
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}
.

Finally, since
f
is isothermal for
g
, we have that
g
is compatible with the standard complex structure on
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}
; it is thus trivially Kähler.
∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\nabla
denote the pull-back of the flat connection on
\Omega^{*}
by
f
. Then
(g,\nabla)
is a special Kähler structure on
\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0,v\geq 0\}
.

Proof.

For the duration of the proof we restrict ourselves to
\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0,v\geq 0\}
. We need to show that
\nabla
is a symplectic, torsion-free, flat connection satisfying the identity

(4.1)

(\nabla_{X}J)Y=(\nabla_{Y}J)X,

where
J
is the standard complex structure on
\widetilde{\Omega}
. That
\nabla
is torsion-free and flat is immediate from the definition. To check that
\nabla
is symplectic, we calculate the Kähler form in
(x,y)
-coordinates:

\omega=g(J\cdot,\cdot)=\frac{1}{\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy}}(-dvdu+dudv)=\frac{2\ du\wedge dv}{\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy}}.

Then:

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=\frac{2(u_{x}\ dx+u_{y}\ dy)\wedge(v_{x}\ dx+v_{y}\ dy)}{\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy}}=\frac{2(u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x})dx\wedge dy}{\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy}}=2\ dx\wedge dy.

Thus,
\omega
is parallel with respect to
\nabla.

Finally, we check (
4.1
). Write
(x,y)=(x^{1},x^{2})
, and
J=J^{j}_{k}\,dx^{k}\otimes\frac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}}

for the complex structure, using index notation. Then

\displaystyle\nabla J

\displaystyle=\partial_{\ell}J^{j}_{k}\ dx^{\ell}\otimes dx^{k}\otimes\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x^{j}},

so (
4.1
) becomes
\partial_{\ell}J^{j}_{k}=\partial_{j}J^{\ell}_{k}
, where we have been writing
\partial_{\ell}f:=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x^{\ell}}
.
To verify this, we compute the complex structure in
(x,y)
-coordinates:

\displaystyle J

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{\partial}{\partial u}\otimes dv+\tfrac{\partial}{\partial v}\otimes du

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x}}(v_{y}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}-v_{x}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y})\otimes(v_{x}\ dx+v_{y}\ dy)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{u_{x}v_{y}-u_{y}v_{x}}(-u_{y}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}+u_{x}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y})\otimes(u_{x}\ dx+u_{y}\ dy)

\displaystyle=\rho^{-1}[(-v_{x}v_{y}-u_{x}u_{y})(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\otimes dx)+(-v_{y}^{2}-u_{y}^{2})(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\otimes dy)

\displaystyle+(v_{x}^{2}+u_{x}^{2})(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\otimes dx)+(v_{x}v_{y}+u_{x}u_{y})(\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\otimes dy)].

Exactly as before, we may use the Monge-Ampère equation to compute that:

\displaystyle u_{x}^{2}+v_{x}^{2}=1+(\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{xy})^{2}=\varphi_{xx}\,\rho

\displaystyle u_{y}^{2}+v_{y}^{2}=1+(\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy})^{2}=\varphi_{yy}\,\rho

\displaystyle u_{x}u_{y}+v_{x}v_{y}=\varphi_{xy}\,\rho,

so we conclude that:

\displaystyle J=-\varphi_{xy}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\otimes dx+-\varphi_{yy}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\otimes dy+\varphi_{xx}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\otimes dx+\varphi_{xy}\ \tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\otimes dy.

Since
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}(-\varphi_{xy})=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}(-\varphi_{yy})
and
\tfrac{\partial}{\partial y}\varphi_{xx}=\tfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\varphi_{xy}
, we find (
4.1
) is satisfied. Thus,
\nabla
induces a special Kähler structure on
\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0,v\geq 0\}.

∎

The crucial observation is that the SKS
(g,\nabla)
always admits a special holomorphic coordinate; with our specific choice of isothermals, it will actually be the identity function. As shown in
[
CH19
, Corollary 10.2.10]
, the existence of such a coordinate places severe restrictions on the possible singularities of the SKS.

Proposition 4.4
.

The function:

Recall that a holomorphic coordinate in said to be

Theorem 4.5
.

The conformal factor
\rho^{-1}
is harmonic on
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}
, and hence:

\rho^{-1}=-C\log\lvert z\rvert+h(z),

for some
C\geq 0
and some smooth harmonic function

on
\widetilde{\Omega}
.

Proof.

We follow
[
CH19
, Proposition 10.2.9]
. Write:

\alpha=\begin{bmatrix}\alpha^{1}_{1}&\alpha^{1}_{2}\\
\alpha_{1}^{2}&\alpha_{2}^{2}\end{bmatrix}

for the connection
1
-form of
\nabla
in
(u,v)
-coordinates on
\widetilde{\Omega}\setminus\{u=0,v\geq 0\}
. The coordinate

is flat with respect to

, so we have:

0=\nabla(du)=-\alpha_{1}^{1}\otimes du-\alpha_{2}^{1}\otimes dv;

it follows that
\alpha_{1}^{1}=\alpha_{2}^{1}=0
. The symplectic form is
\omega:=\rho^{-1}idu\wedge dv
; since

also, we get:

0=\nabla\omega=-\rho^{-1}\alpha_{2}^{2}-\rho^{-2}d\rho\implies\alpha_{2}^{2}=-d\log\rho.

By the proof of

, we have
\alpha_{1}^{2}=*\alpha_{2}^{2}
, where

is the Hodge operator with respect to the standard metric

. Then, the fact that
\nabla

d^{*}\alpha_{2}^{2}=-|\alpha_{2}^{2}|^{2}.

We then have that:

\Delta\log\rho=d^{*}d\log\rho=\lvert\alpha_{2}^{2}\rvert^{2}=\lvert d\rho\rvert^{2}.

It follows that:

\Delta\rho^{-1}=\Delta e^{-\log\rho}=-\rho^{-1}\Delta\log\rho+\rho^{-1}\lvert d\log\rho\rvert^{2}=0.

Knowing

is smooth on

lets us conclude

on all of
\widetilde{\Omega}^{*}.

A well-known result for harmonic functions (e.g.
[

, Theorem 3.9]
), now shows
\rho^{-1}=C\log|z|+h(z)
.
∎

Corollary 4.6
.

In the classification of
[
CH20
]
, the SKS
(g,\nabla)
is of logarithmic type with order 1.

Proof.

The proof consists of ruling out the possibility

. To do this, we show that in such a case the connection
\nabla,
a priori a flat connection on

We begin by augmenting our flat atlas with conformal charts around each singular point, as we have done so far around the point

. To do this, we define

r_{i,j}^{-1}(n):=(x-y,(\psi_{i,j})_{x}+(\psi_{i,j})_{y}),

where

on the neighborhood of the point

enclosed by
n_{i},

n_{j},

n_{ijk},

where

To see that this is homeomorphism to an open set of the plane, we repeat the proof of Proposition

. Note that around each singular point

we have two conformal charts

and

We claim that
\{p_{i,j},r_{i,j}\}
is a smooth atlas for

Inspecting the aforementioned regions over which

First, the transition function from

. Then to prove compatibility of

As demonstrated in the proof of Proposition

, if

is a flat connection on

enabling us to take a nonzero vector and complete it to a nonvanishing vector field on

via parallel transport. This is a contradiction, and thus we conclude

∎

We can now show that the

-norm of

Theorem 4.7
.

In
(x,y)
-coordinates, the second derivatives of
\varphi
blow up at the origin.

Proof.

We shall show that

2\rho^{-1}\leq\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy};

since
\rho^{-1}
blows up as
(x,y)\rightarrow(0,0)
, by Corollary
4.6
, the result follows.

We have
\varphi_{xx}+2\varphi_{xy}+\varphi_{yy}=\rho,
so:

\varphi_{xy}^{2}=\frac{\varphi_{xx}^{2}+2\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}+\varphi_{yy}^{2}}{4}-\frac{\rho}{2}(\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy})+\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}.

Substituting into the Monge-Ampère equation gives:

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}-\varphi_{xy}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{4}\varphi_{xx}^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\varphi_{xx}\varphi_{yy}-\tfrac{1}{4}\varphi_{yy}^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}(\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy})-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}

\displaystyle=-\tfrac{1}{4}(\varphi_{xx}-\varphi_{yy})^{2}+\frac{\rho}{2}(\varphi_{xx}+\varphi_{yy})-\frac{\rho^{2}}{4}.

Since
\rho>0
, we conclude. As a remark, a further analysis of the above equation actually shows that each of
\varphi_{xx},\varphi_{yy},
and
-\varphi_{xy}
are bounded below by
(1-\varepsilon)\rho^{-1}
as
(x,y)\rightarrow 0
, where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary.
∎
