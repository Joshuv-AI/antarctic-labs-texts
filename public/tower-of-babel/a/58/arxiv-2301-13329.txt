Multi-Structural Games and Beyond
Marco Carmosino ORCID:0009-0007-1118-1352, Ronald Fagin ORCID:0000-0002-7374-0347, Neil Immerman ORCID:0000-0001-6609-5952, Phokion G. Kolaitis ORCID:0000-0002-8407-8563, Jonathan Lenchner ORCID:0000-0002-9427-8470, Rik Sengupta ORCID:0000-0002-9238-5408

Abstract
. Multi-structural (MS) games are combinatorial games that capture the number of quantifiers of first-order sentences. On the face of their definition, MS games differ from Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé (EF) games in two ways: first, MS games are played on two sets of structures, while EF games are played on a pair of structures; second, in MS games, Duplicator can make any number of copies of structures. In the first part of this paper, we perform a finer analysis of MS games and develop a closer comparison of MS games with EF games. In particular, we point out that the use of sets of structures is of the essence and that when MS games are played on pairs of structures, they capture Boolean combinations of first-order sentences with a fixed number of quantifiers. After this, we focus on another important difference between MS games and EF games, namely, the necessity for Spoiler to play on top of a previous move in order to win some MS games. Via an analysis of the types realized during MS games, we delineate the expressive power of the variant of MS games in which Spoiler never plays on top of a previous move. In the second part we focus on simultaneously capturing number of quantifiers and number of variables in first-order logic. We show that natural variants of the MS game do not achieve this. We then introduce a new game, the quantifier-variable tree game, and show that it simultaneously captures the number of quantifiers and number of variables. We conclude by generalizing this game to a family of games, the syntactic games , that simultaneously capture reasonable syntactic measures and the number of variables.

1.
Introduction

Combinatorial games are an effective tool to analyze the expressive power of logics on sets of structures. The prototypical example of such combinatorial games are the Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé (EF) games
[
Ehr61
,
Fra54
]
, played by two players, called Spoiler and Duplicator; EF games capture the quantifier rank of first-order sentences and have been used to analyze the expressive power of first-order logic (FO). Specifically, for every
r\in\mathbb{N}
, two structures
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
satisfy the same FO-sentences of quantifier rank
r
if and only if Duplicator wins the
r
-round EF game on
({\bf A},{\bf B})
.
Since this holds true for finite and infinite structures alike, EF games can be used in finite model theory, unlike other tools from mathematical logic that fail in the finite realm, such as the compactness theorem. Variants of EF games in which the players start by playing unary relations were used to analyze the expressive power of monadic
NP
, which is the collection of problems in
NP
that are definable by sentences of existential monadic second-order logic
[
Fag75
,
dR84
,
Sch94
,
FSV95
]
. A
different variant of EF games are the pebble games; they capture the number of variables and were used to analyze the expressive power of finite-variable first-order logic and infinitary logics

[
Bar77
,
Imm82
,
KV92
]
.

Multi-structural (MS) games were introduced in
[
Imm81
]
, re-discovered recently in
[
FLRV21
]
, and investigated further in
[
FLVW22
]
and
[
CFI+24
]
. MS games capture the number of quantifiers of FO-sentences, which is another important parameter in understanding the expressive power of FO. If one is interested in showing that a property
P
of finite structures, such as connectivity or acyclicity, is not expressible by
any
fixed FO-sentence
\psi
, then both EF games and MS games work equally well. The original motivation of introducing MS games, however, came from the potential use of MS games to obtain lower bounds when a sequence
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
of FO-sentences is considered so that a property
P
is expressed by
\psi_{n}
on structures with at most
n
elements. Let
h(n)
be a function from the natural numbers to the natural numbers. In
[
Imm81
]
,
\mathrm{QN}[h(n)]
is defined to be the class of all properties
P
for which there is a

uniform
sequence
\{\psi_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, of FO-sentences such that
\psi_{n}
expresses
P
on structures with at most
n
elements,
\psi_{n}
has
O(h(n))
quantifiers, and
h(n)
is constructible in
\mathrm{DSPACE}[h(n)]
, where
\mathrm{DSPACE}
stands for deterministic space.
From results in
[
Imm81
]
, it follows that on the class of all ordered finite structures,

\mathrm{NL}\subseteq\mathrm{QN}[\log(n)].

Thus, proving that some
NP
-complete or some
P
-complete problem requires
\omega(\log(n))
quantifiers would separate
\mathrm{NL}
from
NP
or from
P
, and this could be achieved by playing MS games on ordered finite structures. In contrast, as pointed out in
[
Imm81
]
, FO-sentences of quantifier rank
O(\log(n))
capture isomorphism on ordered finite structures, and hence EF games cannot be used to prove lower bounds for sequences of FO-sentences with
\omega(\log(n))
quantifiers. Even though the early optimism of using combinatorial games to separate complexity classes has yet to materialize, it is still worth exploring MS games further with the aim of developing a deeper understanding of their features and potential uses.

On the face of their definitions, EF games and MS games differ in the following ways. First, EF games are played on a pair
({\bf A},{\bf B})
of structures, while MS games are played on a pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
of
sets
of structures; second, in each round of the MS game, Duplicator can make any number of copies of structures on the side that she has to play; and, third, in the EF game, Duplicator must maintain a partial isomorphism between
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
, while in the MS game, she must maintain a partial isomorphism between some structure from
\mathcal{A}
and some structure from
\mathcal{B}
. Spoiler wins the
r
-round EF game on
({\bf A},{\bf B})
iff there is a FO-sentence of quantifier rank
r
that is true on
{\bf A}
and false on
{\bf B}
, while Spoiler wins the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
iff there is a FO-sentence with
r
quantifiers that is
separating
for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, i.e., it is true on every structure in
\mathcal{A}
and false on every structure in
\mathcal{B}
.

In this paper, we carry out a finer analysis of MS games and shed additional light on how EF games compare with MS games. In particular, when applying MS games to obtain inexpressibility results, one can use infinite sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
of structures. We show that the use of infinite sets does not yield stronger inexpressibility results; however, the sets used cannot always be assumed to be singletons (i.e., we cannot always start with a pair of structures). We also show that
r
-round MS games restricted to singleton sets capture Boolean combinations of FO-sentences with
r
quantifiers. Finally, we add insight into Duplicator’s ability to make copies of structures during the game: if enough copies of each structure are made before the first round, then Duplicator does not need to make any additional copies during gameplay.

In
[
FLRV21
]
and
[
FLVW22
]
, it was pointed out that, unlike the EF game, Spoiler may get an advantage in the MS game by playing “on top,” i.e., by placing a pebble on top of an existing pebble or a constant. Here, we first give a self-contained proof of the fact that, in general, Spoiler may not win an MS game without sometimes playing on top. We then explore the expressive power of the variant of the MS game in which Spoiler never plays on top. By analyzing the
types
(i.e., the combinations of atomic and negated atomic formulas satisfied by the pebbles played and the constants), we characterize when Spoiler wins the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
without playing on top in terms of properties of separating sentences for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

We next study combinatorial games that simultaneously capture number of quantifiers and number of variables. It is well known that EF games can be adapted to simultaneously capture quantifier rank and number of variables by limiting the number of pebbles used. In contrast, we show that natural variants of MS games obtained by limiting the number of pebbles fail to simultaneously capture number of quantifiers and number of variables. For this reason, we introduce a new game, the
quantifier-variable tree
game (QVT game). The QVT game is inspired by the Adler-Immerman game
[
AI01
]
, which was introduced to study formula size. Variants of the Adler-Immerman game were subsequently investigated in
[
GS05
]
to study the succinctness of logics, and in
[
HV15
]
to obtain lower bounds for formula size in propositional logic and in FO. Our main result about the QVT game asserts that Spoiler wins the
r
-round,
k
-pebble QVT game on a pair
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
of sets of structures if and only if there is a separating sentence for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
r
quantifiers and
k
variables.

Finally, we generalize the QVT game to a class of two-player games called
syntactic games
, that simultaneously capture some other measure of first-order formulas and the number of variables. We focus on
compositional
syntactic measures, i.e., measures on first-order formulas defined inductively for formulas in terms of its value on their subformulas. Quantifier count, quantifier rank, and formula size are all examples of such measures. Our syntactic games provide a unified game-theoretic setting in which to study these measures. A similar game for formulas of the infinitary logic
\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}
was studied in
[
VW13
]
.

In conclusion, our results yield new insights into MS games, their variants, and generalizations. The next step in this investigation would be to use these games to
determine the optimal value of a complexity measure and number of variables to express various combinatorial properties as a function of the size of the relevant structures.

2.
Preliminaries

Throughout this paper, we consider a relational schema
\tau
consisting of finitely many relation and constant symbols. We denote the set of relation symbols in
\tau
by
\mathsf{Pred}(\tau)
. We write
\tau
-structures in boldface, e.g.,
{\bf A}
. We denote the universe (i.e., set of elements) of
{\bf A}
by
A
, and sets of
\tau
-structures using calligraphic typeface, e.g.,
\mathcal{A}
.

2.1.
Pebbled structures

We have a set
\mathcal{C}
of
pebble colors
, with arbitrarily many
pebbles
of each color available. A
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
is
pebbled
if zero or more elements from
A
have one or more pebbles on them, so that
at most
one pebble of each color is present in
{\bf A}
. If pebbles of color
1,\ldots,t
are placed on elements
a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\in A
, we refer to this pebbled structure as
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
. For readability, when the context is obvious, we refer to
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
as the
t
-pebbled (or simply pebbled) structure
{\bf A}
. Note that the unpebbled structure
{\bf A}
corresponds to the pebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~\rangle
, with an empty set of pebbles. When
t=0
, the notation
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
refers to the unpebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~\rangle
.

{defi}

Consider two pebbled structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\rangle
, and let
f:A\to B
be the map such that:

•

for
1\leq i\leq t
, we have that
f
maps
a_{i}\mapsto b_{i}
.

•

for each constant symbol
c
in
\tau
, we have that
f
maps
c^{\bf A}\mapsto c^{\bf B}
.

We say the pair
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\rangle
of pebbled structures
matches
(and call it a
matching pair
) iff the map
f
defined above is an isomorphism between the substructures of
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
induced by its domain and range.

Of course, note that the pebbled structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\rangle
may form a matching pair even if
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
are not isomorphic. In fact, they may even form a matching pair without any pebbles placed on them.

2.2.
The multi-structural game

Fix
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and two nonempty sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
of
\tau
-structures. We define the
r
-round multi-structural game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
as follows. We refer to
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
as the
left
and
right
sides, respectively. Informally, in each round of the game, one player (Spoiler) places a pebble on every structure on either the left side or the right side. Then the other player (Duplicator) places a pebble of the same color on every structure on the other side. Duplicator (but not Spoiler) may copy structures to make different placements on distinct copies of the same structure. Duplicator tries to ensure that, at the end of every round, there is some matching pair (Definition
2.1
); Spoiler tries to eliminate all matching pairs in
r
or fewer rounds.

We now give a formal description of the game. We view all structures henceforth as pebbled structures. The game proceeds over up to
r
rounds, by building a sequence of
configurations

(\mathcal{A}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{0}),(\mathcal{A}_{1},\mathcal{B}_{1}),\ldots
, where
\mathcal{A}_{0}=\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}_{0}=\mathcal{B}
. Initially, if there are no matching pairs, then Spoiler wins the
0
-round game. Otherwise, inductively, in each round
t=1,\ldots,r
, with configuration
(\mathcal{A}_{t-1},\mathcal{B}_{t-1})
, Spoiler selects one of the following moves:

•

\mathsf{Play\text{-}Left}
:

(a)

For each pebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1}\rangle\in\mathcal{A}_{t-1}
, Spoiler places a pebble colored
t
on an element
a_{t}\in A
, creating the pebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
. Call the resulting set
\mathcal{A}_{t}
.

(b)

For each pebbled structure
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t-1}\rangle\in\mathcal{B}_{t-1}
, Duplicator
may
make any number of copies of this pebbled structure, and then for each such copy,
must
place a pebble colored
t
on an element
b_{t}\in B
, creating the pebbled structure
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\rangle
. Call the resulting set
\mathcal{B}_{t}
.

(c)

At the end of this round, if no
t
-pebbled structure in
\mathcal{A}_{t}
forms a matching pair with a
t
-pebbled structure in
\mathcal{B}_{t}
, then Spoiler wins the game. Otherwise, if
t<r
, play continues.

•

\mathsf{Play\text{-}Right}
: This move is dual to
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Left}
; Spoiler places pebbles colored
t
on the pebbled structures on the right side, and Duplicator responds on the left side.

At the end of round
r
, Duplicator wins the game if there is still some
r
-pebbled structure on the left and some
r
-pebbled structure on the right forming a matching pair.

After
t
rounds of the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
have been played, we have the collection
\mathcal{A}_{t}
of pebbled structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
on the left, and the collection
\mathcal{B}_{t}
of pebbled structures
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\rangle
on the right, where
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
range over
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
respectively, and
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
are the elements pebbled by the players on
{\bf A}
and
{\bf B}
in round
i
, for
1\leq i\leq t
. A
strategy
for Spoiler is a function that takes as input such a configuration
(\mathcal{A}_{t},\mathcal{B}_{t})
, and provides him with the next move in the game. Specifically, given such a configuration, a strategy tells Spoiler whether to play
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Left}
or
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Right}
; if it tells Spoiler to play
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Left}
, then for each pebbled structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle\in\mathcal{A}_{t}
, the strategy provides an element
a_{t+1}\in A
for Spoiler to play the pebble colored
t+1
on. Similarly, if it tells Spoiler to play
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Right}
, then for each pebbled structure
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t}\rangle\in\mathcal{B}_{t}
, the strategy provides an element
b_{t+1}\in B
for Spoiler to play the pebble colored
t+1
on. A
winning
strategy for Spoiler in the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a strategy such that Spoiler always wins the game if he follows it.

We say that Duplicator follows the
oblivious
strategy if in each round, for every pebbled structure on the side she plays on, she makes as many copies as there are elements in the universe of the structure, and then plays a pebble on a different element in each copy. It is obvious that if Duplicator wins the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, then Duplicator can win by following the oblivious strategy. From now on, unless we say otherwise, we will assume that Duplicator follows the oblivious strategy in the MS game.

We next state an important definition.

{defi}

Given two sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
of
\tau
-structures, a
separating sentence for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
is a FO-sentence

\psi
such that every
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{A}
has
{\bf A}\models\psi
, and every
{\bf B}\in\mathcal{B}
has
{\bf B}\models\lnot\psi
.

Clearly,
\psi
is a separating sentence for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
if and only if
\lnot\psi
is a separating sentence for
(\mathcal{B},\mathcal{A})
.

-quantifier separating sentence for

.

2.3.
Strategies

A perusal of the proof of Theorem
2.2
in
[
Imm81
]
or in
[
FLRV21
]
reveals that if
\psi
is a separating sentence for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
r
quantifiers, then Spoiler has a winning strategy for the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
where he “follows”
\psi
. To explain in more precise terms what “following”
\psi
means, assume that
\psi
is of the form
Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta
, where
\theta
is quantifier-free. If
Q_{t}
is
\exists
, then Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Left}
, while if
Q_{t}
is
\forall
, then Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Play\text{-}Right}
. Assume by induction that at the start of round
t\geq 1
, the configuration has the property that for every
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1}\rangle
on the left and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{t-1}\rangle
on the right in this configuration, we have:

\displaystyle{\bf A}

\displaystyle\models Q_{t}x_{t}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta(a_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1}/x_{t-1})

\displaystyle{\bf B}

\displaystyle\models\lnot Q_{t}x_{t}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta(b_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,b_{t-1}/x_{t-1}).

If
Q_{t}
is
\exists
, then there is some
a_{t}\in A
such that

{\bf A}\models Q_{t+1}x_{t+1}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta(a_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,a_{t}/x_{t})
; in this case, Spoiler picks such an element
a_{t}
and plays the pebble on it. If
Q_{t}
is
\forall
, then
{\bf B}\models\exists x_{t}\lnot Q_{t+1}x_{t+1}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta(b_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,b_{t-1}/x_{t-1})
, and so there is an element
b_{t}\in B
such that
{\bf B}\models\lnot Q_{t+1}x_{t+1}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta(b_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,b_{t}/x_{t})
; in this case, Spoiler picks such an element
b_{t}
and plays the pebble on it. Since in each of these cases, more than one witness to the existential quantifier may exist, there may exist different strategies for Spoiler obtained by “following”
\psi
; however, each of these strategies is a winning strategy for Spoiler. We say that a strategy for Spoiler is
obtained
from
\psi
if it is one of the strategies for Spoiler obtained by following
\psi
in this way.

The preceding discussion is summarized in the first part of the next result; the second part of the result can be extracted from the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Theorem 1
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
, and let
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
be two sets of
\tau
-structures. Then, the following are true.

(a)

If
\psi\equiv Q_{1}x_{1}\ldots Q_{r}x_{r}\theta
is a separating sentence for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
r
quantifiers, then Spoiler can win the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
using a strategy obtained from
\psi
. Moreover, for every such strategy and for all pebbled structures
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{r}\rangle
on the left and
\langle{\bf B}~|~b_{1},\ldots,b_{r}\rangle
on the right in the final configuration arising by using this strategy, we have
{\bf A}\models\theta(a_{1}/x_{1},\dots,a_{r}/x_{r})
and
{\bf B}\models\lnot\theta(b_{1}/x_{1},\ldots,b_{r}/x_{r})
.

(b)

If Spoiler has a winning strategy
\mathcal{S}
for the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, then there is a separating sentence
\psi
for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
r
quantifiers such that the strategy
\mathcal{S}
is one of the strategies for Spoiler obtained from
\psi
.

2.4.
Types

The following concepts will recur throughout this paper. An
atomic
formula is an expression of one of the following forms: an equality
x_{i}=x_{j}
between two distinct variables; an equality
c_{i}=x_{j}
between a constant symbol and a variable; an equality
c_{i}=c_{j}
between two distinct constant symbols; an expression
R(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
, where
R
is an
m
-ary relation symbol in the given schema, for some
m\geq 1
, and
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
are not-necessarily-distinct variables or constant symbols. A
type
over an
r
-tuple of distinct variables,
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
, is a conjunction of atomic and negated atomic formulas such that for each atomic formula with variables from
x_{1},\ldots,x_{r}
, exactly one of the atomic formula and its negation appears as a conjunct. We will often denote such a type by
t(x_{1},\ldots,x_{r})

3.
Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé Games vs. Multi-Structural Games

Ehrenfeucht-Fraïssé games capture indistinguishibility with respect to quantifier rank, while multi-structural games capture indistinguishibility with respect to number of quantifiers. Each of these two families of games yields a method for establishing lower bounds in the corresponding fragment of first-order logic. Here, we compare the two methods and uncover differences between them.

A

In summary, we now have the following rather complete picture about the similarities and the differences between EF games and MS games:

(a)

r
-round EF games played on a pair of structures capture expressibility by a FO-sentence of quantifier rank
r
.

(b)

r
-round MS games played on sets of structures capture expressibility by a FO-sentence with
r

The sets of structures can be taken to be finite, but not singletons.

(c)

r
-round MS games played on a pair of structures (i.e., on singletons) capture expressibility by a Boolean combination of FO-sentences, each with
r
quantifiers.

The difference between (a) and (c) is because FO-sentences of quantifier rank
r
are closed under disjunctions and conjunctions, whereas FO-sentences with
r
quantifiers are not.

We also have the following trade-off between the EF Game Method and the MS Game Method in establishing
inexpressibility results about a property
P
: for EF games, we can work with pairs of structures, but we need to find them and to show that they have the desired properties in Method
2
; in contrast, for MS games we can collect all the structures that satisfy property
P
on one side and all the structures that do not satisfy
P
on the other, but then it may be harder to show that Duplicator wins the MS game on these sets.

4.
Play-on-Top Moves in MS Games

In
[
FLRV21
]
, the authors draw attention to a surprising difference between the EF game and the MS game. Specifically, in the EF game, Spoiler gains no advantage by ever placing a pebble on top of an existing pebble or a constant. As it turns out, there are instances of the MS game where Spoiler wins, but only if he may sometimes play “on top”, i.e., he places a pebble on a constant or on an existing pebble. •

\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}

6.
The Syntactic Game

Consider a node

in the game tree
\mathcal{T}
for a given QVT game. The counter

\mathcal{L}_{\omega_{1},\omega}

[

. In this section, we will derive games precisely capturing many familiar complexity measures, including: quantifier count, quantifier rank, and formula size. The main idea in all of these games would be to have specific types of
pebble moves
corresponding to the inductive ways to build up FO formulas; Spoiler will use these moves to explicitly simulate a separating formula, and will try to do this and close the game tree without running out of a “budget” on the syntactic measure, which we keep track of by means of a counter. In these moves, the number of pebble colors captures the number of variables. For each logical connective occurring in the language in question, there is a move and a corresponding cost of using that operator. For example, to count the quantifier number, we add
1
for each quantifier used, as in the QVT game. Thus, these games capture the descriptive cost function under consideration. They are the natural tool for proving descriptive lower bounds; sometimes they are the best choice for proving upper bounds as well.

6.1.
Compositional syntactic measures

Recall that
\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
is the set of well-formed FO formulas over the schema
\tau
, that use only the variables
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
. Note that
\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
is defined inductively, where each formula is the
application of a logical symbol to some subformulas. A
compositional syntactic measure
uses such an inductive definition to measure formula complexity.

Recall that
\mathsf{Pred}(\tau)
is the set of predicate symbols of
\tau
, assumed to be finite.

{defi}

A
compositional syntactic measure
is a function
f:\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)\to\mathbb{N}
defined in terms of helper
functions
h_{\lnot},h_{\exists},h_{\forall}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
,
h_{\lor},h_{\land}:\mathbb{N}^{2}\to\mathbb{N}
,
h_{\mathsf{atomic}}:\mathsf{Pred}(\tau)\to\mathbb{N}
, as follows:

•

if
\varphi
is atomic, say
\varphi=P(t_{1},\ldots,t_{r})
for some
P\in\mathsf{Pred}(\tau)
, and terms
t_{1},\ldots,t_{r}
, where
r
is the arity of
P
, then
f(\varphi)=h_{\mathsf{atomic}}(P)
.

•

if
\varphi=\neg\psi
, then
f(\varphi)=h_{\lnot}(f(\psi))
.

•

if
\varphi=(\psi\lor\gamma)
, then
f(\varphi)=h_{\lor}(f(\psi),f(\gamma))
.

•

if
\varphi=(\psi\land\gamma)
, then
f(\varphi)=h_{\land}(f(\psi),f(\gamma))
.

•

if
\varphi=\exists x(\psi)
, then
f(\varphi)=h_{\exists}(f(\psi))
.

•

if
\varphi=\forall x(\psi)
, then
f(\varphi)=h_{\forall}(f(\psi))
.

We call such a measure “compositional” because the value of
f
is determined by the value of
f
on its subformulas. Compositional syntactic measures are a large and natural class of syntactic measures, which includes quantifier rank, quantifier number, formula size, and number of atomic formulas. However, we remark that the number of distinct variables is
not
a compositional measure, as the value of this measure on, say, a disjunction depends not only on the values on the subformulas, but actually on the subformulas themselves. We also note that in Definition
6.1
, we treat equality as an ordinary binary predicate.

We could have stated Definition
6.1
purely in terms of well-formed formulas over
\tau
, instead of the slice
\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
. However, we chose our convention to evoke the idea of fixing
k
variables at the outset, which will be relevant in the rest of this section.

The following examples show that quantifier number, quantifier rank, and formula size are indeed compositional syntactic measures.

{exa}

The
quantifier count

f_{q}
satisfies:

•

if
\varphi
is atomic,

f_{q}(\varphi)=0
. In other words,
h_{\mathsf{atomic}}(\varphi)=0
.

•

if
\varphi=\neg\psi
,

f_{q}(\varphi)=f_{q}(\psi)
. In other words,
h_{\lnot}(n)=n
.

•

if
\varphi=(\psi\land\gamma)
or
\varphi=(\psi\lor\gamma)
,
f_{q}(\varphi)=f_{q}(\psi)+f_{q}(\gamma)
. In other words,
h_{\lor}(m,n)=h_{\land}(m,n)=m+n
.

•

if
\varphi=\exists x\psi
or
\varphi=\forall x\psi
,
f_{q}(\varphi)=1+f_{q}(\psi)
. In other words,
h_{\exists}(n)=h_{\forall}(n)=n+1
.

{exa}

The
quantifier rank

f_{r}
satisfies:

•

if
\varphi
is atomic,

f_{r}(\varphi)=0
. In other words,
h_{\mathsf{atomic}}(\varphi)=0
.

•

if
\varphi=\neg\psi
,

f_{r}(\varphi)=f_{r}(\psi)
. In other words,
h_{\lnot}(n)=n
.

•

if
\varphi=(\psi\land\gamma)
or
\varphi=(\psi\lor\gamma)
,
f_{r}(\varphi)=\max(f_{r}(\psi),f_{r}(\gamma))
. In other words,
h_{\lor}(m,n)=h_{\land}(m,n)=\max(m,n)
.

•

if
\varphi=\exists x\psi
or
\varphi=\forall x\psi
,
f_{r}(\varphi)=1+f_{r}(\psi)
. In other words,
h_{\exists}(n)=h_{\forall}(n)=n+1
.

{exa}

The
formula size

f_{s}
satisfies:

•

if
\varphi
is atomic,

f_{r}(\varphi)=1
. In other words,
h_{\mathsf{atomic}}(\varphi)=1
.

•

if
\varphi=\neg\psi
,

f_{r}(\varphi)=1+f_{r}(\psi)
. In other words,
h_{\lnot}(n)=n+1
.

•

if
\varphi=(\psi\land\gamma)
or
\varphi=(\psi\lor\gamma)
,
f_{r}(\varphi)=1+f_{r}(\psi)+f_{r}(\gamma)
. In other words,
h_{\lor}(m,n)=h_{\land}(m,n)=1+m+n
.

•

if
\varphi=\exists x\psi
or
\varphi=\forall x\psi
,
f_{r}(\varphi)=1+f_{r}(\psi)
. In other words,
h_{\exists}(n)=h_{\forall}(n)=n+1
.

We are now ready to define the syntactic game.

6.2.
The syntactic game

Fix a
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
, and let
\alpha_{\bf A}
be a
partial assignment
, i.e., a function that maps a set of variables to elements in
A
. We call the ordered pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})
a
structure-assignment pair
. Note that if we identify pebble colors with variables, a pebbled
\tau
-structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
has a one-to-one correspondence with the pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})
, where
\alpha_{\bf A}
maps the variable corresponding to a pebble color to the element in
A
that it is placed on. In particular, a
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
has a one-to-one correspondence with the pair
({\bf A},\varnothing)
, where
\varnothing
is the empty assignment.

Just as a
\tau
-structure
{\bf A}
satisfies a sentence over
\tau
, we can extend this notion to a structure-assignment pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})
satisfying a
formula
, which is allowed to have free variables as long as all such variables are in the domain
2
2

2

Note that throughout this section, the term
domain
will always refer to the domain of the assignment functions, and never to the universe of
\tau
-structures.
of
\alpha_{\bf A}
. Formally, let
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})
be a structure-assignment pair, and
\psi
be a formula such that
\mathsf{Free}(\psi)=\{x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}}\}\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\alpha_{\bf A})
. Suppose
\alpha_{\bf A}(x_{i_{j}})=a_{i_{j}}\in A
for all
1\leq j\leq m
. We say that
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})

satisfies

\psi
(or
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\psi
) if
{\bf A}\models\psi(x_{i_{1}}/a_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{m}}/a_{i_{m}})
.

Of course, for every structure-assignment pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})
and formula
\psi
with
\mathsf{Free}(\psi)\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\alpha_{\bf A})
, exactly one of
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\psi
and
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\lnot\psi
is true.

For the rest of this paper, by convention, we will denote sets of structure-assignment pairs with script typeface
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}
, and so on. Note that the
\tau
-structures among the pairs in any such
\mathscr{A}
can in general be different. We now state a definition for convenience.

{defi}

Two sets
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
of structure-assignment pairs are called
domain-consistent
if there is a set
W
of variables such that for every structure-assignment pair
(\mathbf{X},\alpha_{\mathbf{X}})\in\mathscr{A}\cup\mathscr{B}
, we have that
\mathbf{dom}(\alpha_{\mathbf{X}})=W
. We denote this common domain
W
by
\mathsf{dom}(\mathscr{A})
(or, equivalently,
\mathsf{dom}(\mathscr{B})
).

In other words, every partial assignment across all pairs in the two sets have the same domain. In particular, every structure-assignment pair within the set
\mathscr{A}
(or, equivalently,
\mathscr{B}
) also has the same domain for its partial assignment.

We can now formalize what it means for a formula with free variables to be a separating formula, thereby generalizing Definition
2.2
.

{defi}

Let
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
be domain-consistent sets of structure-assignment pairs with common domain
\mathsf{dom}(\mathscr{A})
. A
separating formula for
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is a FO-formula
\psi
with
\mathsf{Free}(\psi)\subseteq\mathsf{dom}(\mathscr{A})
, such that every
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}
satisfies
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\varphi
, and every
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}
satisfies
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\lnot\varphi
.

Note that this does generalize Definition
2.2
, using the correspondence between
{\bf A}
and
({\bf A},\varnothing)
. A separating sentence is just a separating formula with no free variables.

We are now ready to define the syntactic game. Fix two domain-consistent sets
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
of structure-assignment pairs. For a compositional syntactic measure
f
, define the
(r,k)
-syntactic game on
f
, denoted
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f)
, on
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
as follows.

Two players, Spoiler and
Duplicator, play by growing a
game tree

\mathcal{T}
, starting from a single root node
X_{\textrm{root}}
. As in the QVT game, we have a set
\mathcal{C}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
of pebble colors, with arbitrarily many pebbles available of each color. Once again, the color
x_{i}
will correspond to the variable
x_{i}
. The leaf nodes in
\mathcal{T}
are open or closed, with the root
X_{\textrm{root}}
considered to be an open leaf at the start of the game. As before, we define
\mathcal{T}
to be
closed
if all its leaves are closed. The notion of an internal vertex being closed also carries over from Section
5
. The nodes
X\in V(\mathcal{T})
will correspond to a tuple
\langle({\text{left}},{\text{right}})\,\big|\,c\rangle
as before. We define a configuration of the game as before, and maintain the same invariant as in the QVT game. Recall that a pebbled
\tau
-structure
\langle{\bf A}~|~a_{1},\ldots,a_{t}\rangle
now has a one-to-one correspondence with the pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})
, where
\alpha_{\bf A}
is the assignment that maps a variable corresponding to a pebble color to the element in
A
it is placed on. To avoid notational clutter, we will view
\mathscr{A}
or
\mathscr{B}
as either a set of pebbled
\tau
-structures or as a set of structure-assignment pairs interchangeably, where the precise one will be clear from context.

At the root node
X_{\textrm{root}}\in V(\mathcal{T})
,

•

the left side consists of
\mathscr{A}
, viewed as pebbled
\tau
-structures (with the pebbling inherited from the assignment functions).

•

the right side consists of
\mathscr{B}
, viewed as pebbled
\tau
-structures (with the pebbling inherited from the assignment functions).

•

the counter
r^{\prime}
is set to
r
.

The root node, therefore, is denoted
X_{\textrm{root}}=\langle({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r\rangle
. Note that since
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
are domain-consistent, every pebbled
\tau
-structure at the root has the same set of pebbles on it. We will maintain this invariant throughout every node of
\mathcal{T}
(i.e., every configuration will correspond to a pair of domain-consistent sets
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
). As before, the root is considered an open leaf node at the start.

Once again, Spoiler on his turn can perform any of the following moves on an open leaf node
X=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
, as long as the precondition for that move is met:

•

\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
: precondition:
h^{-1}_{\exists}(r^{\prime})\neq\varnothing

(a)

Spoiler chooses a pebble color
x_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
. If all structures in
X
contain the pebble color
x_{i}
, Spoiler removes all of these pebbles.

(b)

For each pebbled structure
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
, Spoiler places a pebble colored
x_{i}
on an element in the universe of
{\bf A}
. Call this new set of pebbled structures
\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime}
.

(c)

For each pebbled structures
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
, Duplicator
may
make any number of copies of
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})
, then
must
place a pebble colored
x_{i}
on an element in the universe of
{\bf B}
and an element in the universe of each copy. Call this new set of pebbled structures
\mathscr{B}^{\prime\prime}
.

(d)

Spoiler chooses some
r^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
with
h_{\exists}(r^{\prime\prime})=r^{\prime}
. Note that this is guaranteed to exist by the precondition.

(e)

Spoiler makes a new open leaf
X^{\prime}=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime\prime}\rangle
in
\mathcal{T}
, with parent
X
. Note that
X
is no longer a leaf in
\mathcal{T}
.

•

\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Right}
: precondition:
h^{-1}_{\forall}(r^{\prime})\neq\varnothing

This move is dual to
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
; Spoiler plays on
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
, Duplicator responds on
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
, and Spoiler chooses some
r^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
with
h_{\forall}(r^{\prime\prime})=r^{\prime}
.

•

\mathsf{Split\text{-}Left}
: precondition:
h^{-1}_{\lor}(r^{\prime})\neq\varnothing

(a)

Spoiler partitions
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
so that
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}=\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{1}\cup\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{2}
.

(b)

Spoiler chooses
r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
h_{\lor}(r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2})=r^{\prime}
. Note that these are guaranteed to exist by the precondition.

(c)

Spoiler makes two new open leaf nodes
X_{1}=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{1}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}_{1}\rangle
and
X_{2}=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{2}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}_{2}\rangle
in
\mathcal{T}
. Both new nodes have parent
X
.

•

\mathsf{Split\text{-}Right}
: precondition:
h^{-1}_{\land}(r^{\prime})\neq\varnothing

This move is dual to
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Left}
; Spoiler partitions
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
and chooses
r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2}\in\mathbb{N}

such that
h_{\land}(r^{\prime}_{1},r^{\prime}_{2})=r^{\prime}
.

•

\mathsf{Swap}
: precondition:
h^{-1}_{\lnot}(r^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
.

(a)

Spoiler chooses
r^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
h_{\lnot}(r^{\prime\prime})=r^{\prime}
.

(b)

Spoiler makes a new open leaf node
X^{\prime}=\langle({\mathscr{B}^{\prime}},{\mathscr{A}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime\prime}\rangle
in
\mathcal{T}
, with parent
X
.

•

\mathsf{Close}
: If there is an atomic formula
\varphi
over
\tau
that is separating (Definition
6.2
) for
(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime})
, such that
h_{\mathsf{atomic}}(\varphi)=r^{\prime}
, Spoiler can mark
X

closed
.

We say that
\mathcal{T}
is
closed
if there are no open leaf nodes. Spoiler wins the game if he closes
\mathcal{T}
, and Duplicator wins otherwise.

If
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
are sets of
\tau
-structures, we will abuse notation slightly and talk about the syntactic game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
, where these will be viewed as sets of structure-assignment pairs with the empty assignment throughout.

We are now ready to characterize the expressive power of the syntactic game. The key to the proof is the observation that a closed game tree
\mathcal{T}
is isomorphic to the parse tree of a formula separating

(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
.

Theorem 18

(Syntactic Game Fundamental Theorem)
.

Fix a compositional syntactic measure
f
, and domain-consistent sets
\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}
of structure-assignment pairs, with
\mathsf{dom}(\mathscr{A})\subseteq\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}\}
. Spoiler has a winning strategy for the game
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f)
on
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
if and only if there is a formula
\varphi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
with
f(\varphi)=r
, that is
separating for
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
.

Proof 6.1
.

(\Longrightarrow:)
Suppose Spoiler closes
\mathcal{T}
even with optimal play from Duplicator. We use induction on the closed game tree
\mathcal{T}
to prove that there is a separating formula. If
\mathcal{T}
consists of a single node, then Spoiler closed the root node for his first move, and this corresponds to an atomic formula with the specified counter value by definition of the
\mathsf{Close}
move. Otherwise, consider a closed tree
\mathcal{T}
, and consider Spoiler’s first move on the root. Inductively, each of the subtrees corresponds to a separating formula. We consider each possible first move by Spoiler. Let the root node be
X_{\textrm{root}}=\langle({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r\rangle
.

•

Suppose Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Swap}
, creating
X=\langle({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{A}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
. By induction,
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A})
is separable by a formula
\psi
such that
f(\psi)=r^{\prime}
. Consider the formula
\varphi=\lnot\psi
, and observe that
\varphi
is a separating formula for
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
. Furthermore,
f(\varphi)=h_{\lnot}(f(\psi))=h_{\lnot}(r^{\prime})=r
.

•

Suppose Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Left}
, creating
X_{1}=\langle({\mathscr{A}_{1}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r_{1}\rangle
and
X_{2}=\langle({\mathscr{A}_{2}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r_{2}\rangle
. By induction,
(\mathscr{A}_{1},\mathscr{B})
is separable by a formula
\varphi_{1}
with
f(\varphi_{1})=r_{1}
, and
(\mathscr{A}_{2},\mathscr{B})
is separable by a formula
\varphi_{2}
with
f(\varphi_{2})=r_{2}
. Then,
\varphi=\varphi_{1}\lor\varphi_{2}
is a separating formula for
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
, and
f(\varphi)=h_{\lor}(r_{1},r_{2})=r
.

•

If Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Right}
, the analysis is similar.

•

Suppose Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
using the pebble color
x_{i}
, creating
X=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
. By induction,
(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime})
has a separating formula
\psi
with
f(\psi)=r^{\prime}
. Let
\varphi=\exists x_{i}\psi
, and consider one particular structure-assignment pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{{\bf A}})\in\mathcal{A}
. We know this pair became a pair
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{{\bf A}})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
, such that
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{{\bf A}})\models\psi
. Furthermore, the only difference between the two pairs is the mapping of the domain element
x_{i}
. Regardless of whether or not
x_{i}\in\mathsf{dom}(\alpha_{{\bf A}})
, we have
({\bf A},\alpha_{{\bf A}})\models\exists x_{i}\psi
, just by reverting the mapping of the element
x_{i}
(or arbitrarily mapping it, if it was not in the domain). On the other hand, if some
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}
satisfies
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\exists x_{i}\psi
, then Duplicator can respond to Spoiler’s
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
move by (re-)mapping the variable
x_{i}
to a witness for
\psi
, and the resulting pair
({\bf B},\alpha^{\prime}_{{\bf B}})\in\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
would satisfy
({\bf B},\alpha^{\prime}_{{\bf B}})\models\psi
. This is a contradiction, so every
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}
satisfies
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\lnot\exists x_{i}\psi\equiv\lnot\varphi
. Therefore,
\varphi
is a separating formula for
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
. Of course,
f(\varphi)=f(\exists x_{i}\psi)=h_{\exists}(f(\psi))=h_{\exists}(r^{\prime})=r
.

•

If Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Right}
, the analysis is similar.

(\Longleftarrow:)
We use induction on the separating formula. Suppose there is a separating formula
\varphi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
for
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
with
f(\varphi)=r
. We will show inductively that Spoiler has a winning strategy in the game
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f)
on
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
.

If
\varphi
is atomic, Spoiler can just use
\varphi
to close the root node

X_{\textrm{root}}=\langle({\mathscr{A}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r\rangle
. Otherwise, inductively, we have the following cases:

•

If
\varphi=\lnot\psi
, then Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Swap}
, creating child
X_{1}=\langle({\mathscr{B}},{\mathscr{A}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
where
f(\psi)=r^{\prime}
and
r=h_{\lnot}(r^{\prime})
. Note that the precondition is met, so Spoiler can play this move. Note that
\psi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
separates
(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A})
, so by induction, Spoiler wins the remaining game.

•

If
\varphi=\psi\lor\gamma
, then every
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}
satisfies
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\psi\lor\gamma
, whereas every
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}
satisfies
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\lnot(\psi\lor\gamma)\equiv\lnot\psi\land\lnot\gamma
. Let
\mathscr{A}_{1}
be the subset of
\mathscr{A}
satisfying
\psi
, and let
\mathscr{A}_{2}=\mathscr{A}-\mathscr{A}_{1}
. Let
f(\psi)=r_{1}
and
f(\gamma)=r_{2}
, so that
r=h_{\lor}(r_{1},r_{2})
. Now, Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Left}
with nodes
\langle({\mathscr{A}_{1}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r_{1}\rangle
and
\langle({\mathscr{A}_{2}},{\mathscr{B}})\,\big|\,r_{2}\rangle
. Note that the precondition is met, so Spoiler can play this move. The formula
\psi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
separates
(\mathscr{A}_{1},\mathscr{B})
and satisfies
f(\psi)=r_{1}
. Similarly, the formula
\gamma\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
separates
(\mathscr{A}_{2},\mathscr{B})
and satisfies
f(\gamma)=r_{2}
. By induction, Spoiler wins the remaining games.

•

If
\varphi=\psi\land\gamma
, Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Right}
analogously.

•

Suppose
\varphi=\exists x_{i}\psi
. If
f(\psi)=r^{\prime}
, then note that
r=h_{\exists}(r^{\prime})
. Note that the precondition is met, so Spoiler can play
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
. By assumption, every
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}
has
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\exists x_{i}\psi
. So for some
a\in A
,
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A})\models\psi
, where
\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A}
is identical to
\alpha_{\bf A}
except that
x_{i}
has been (re)mapped to the element
a
. Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
, placing pebble
x_{i}
on the witness
a
on each
{\bf A}
. Note that this creates exactly
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A})
. On the other hand, every
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}
satisfies
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\lnot\exists x_{i}\psi\equiv\forall x_{i}\lnot\psi
, and so every
b\in B
is a witness for
\lnot\psi
, and so even after making copies, Duplicator can never play on a witness for
\psi
in any
{\bf B}
. So for each pair
({\bf B},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf B})
created by Duplicator, we have
({\bf B},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf B})\models\lnot\psi
. At the end, therefore, we have the new tree node
X^{\prime}=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
, where
\psi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
is a separating formula for
(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime})
with

, and so by induction, Spoiler wins the remaining game.

•

If
\varphi=\forall x_{i}\psi
, Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Right}
analogously.

This proves both directions of the theorem.

We now complete this subsection with a result showing that a familiar tool for analyzing these games carries over to this general framework as well. The following proposition shows that Duplicator’s oblivious strategy is optimal in the syntactic game for
every
compositional syntactic measure.

Proposition 19
.

Let
f
be a compositional syntactic measure. If Duplicator has a winning strategy in the
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f)
on
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
, then the oblivious strategy is winning.

Proof 6.2
.

It suffices to consider the case where Spoiler plays
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
on some node
X=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
of
\mathcal{T}
, creating the new node
X^{\prime}=\langle({\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime}},{\mathscr{B}^{\prime\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime\prime}\rangle
. Note that
h_{\exists}(r^{\prime\prime})=r^{\prime}
. The
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Right}
move will be analogous, and Duplicator has no agency elsewhere.

Suppose there is no separating formula
\varphi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
for
(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime})
with
f(\varphi)=r^{\prime}
. It suffices to show that regardless of Spoiler’s move, as long as Duplicator responds obliviously, there is no separating formula
\psi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
for
(\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime\prime})
with
f(\psi)=r^{\prime\prime}
.

Suppose WLOG Spoiler plays the pebble color
x_{i}
on his move. Consider all pairs
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime}
, and take any arbitrary formula
\psi\in\mathrm{FO}^{k}(\tau)
with
f(\psi)=r^{\prime\prime}
that is true for
all
these pairs. In other words, for every pair
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime}
, we have
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A})\models\psi
. An arbitrary pair
({\bf A},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime}
arose from a pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
, with Spoiler changing the assignment
\alpha_{\bf A}
by just (re)assigning the variable
x_{i}
. Furthermore, every pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
gave rise to a new pair in this way.

We claim that this means the formula
\sigma=\exists x_{i}\psi
is true of all pairs
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
. This follows by simply reversing the assignment of the variable
x_{i}
in each of the pairs. It follows that for each pair
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\in\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
, we have
({\bf A},\alpha_{\bf A})\models\sigma
. Note that
f(\sigma)=f(\exists x_{i}\psi)=h_{\exists}(f(\psi))=h_{\exists}(r^{\prime\prime})=r^{\prime}
. By our assumption, therefore, there must be some pair
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
satisfying
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\sigma
. In other words,
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\exists x_{i}\psi
.

Now, when Duplicator responds obliviously, she (re)assigns the variable
x_{i}
in all possible ways in each of the pairs in
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
. In particular, therefore, in one of the copies of the pair
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})
defined above, she plays on a witness
b\in B
for
\psi
, creating the pair
({\bf B},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}^{\prime\prime}
. But then,
({\bf B},\alpha^{\prime}_{\bf B})\models\psi
, and so
\psi
cannot be a separating formula for
(\mathscr{A}^{\prime\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime\prime})
.

Since
\psi
was arbitrary, we are done.

Of course, Proposition
17
follows immediately as a corollary.

It is worth remarking that the proof of Proposition
19
also captures why it is critical for Duplicator to use her copying ability. Duplicator needs a pair
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\in\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
to be a witness for the formula
\psi
. However, the same pair can be a witnessing structure for two different such formulas. In particular, it could be the case that
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\exists x_{i}\psi_{1}
and
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})\models\exists x_{i}\psi_{2}
, but the elements in
B
that witness
\psi_{1}
and
\psi_{2}
are
different
. Making copies of the pair
({\bf B},\alpha_{\bf B})
in order to have two new assignments of
x_{i}
, enabling different elements to act as witnesses simultaneously in the same move, achieves this.

6.3.
Properties of some measures

Having defined the syntactic game, it is natural to ask for the properties of specific syntactic measures that can enable us to apply existing tools of analyzing similar games, in order to have the results carry over to this realm.

Consider the quantifier rank syntactic measure, denoted by
f_{r}
in Example
6.1
. Consider the game
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f_{r})
, and observe that it is very similar to the QVT game. The only difference is that in the
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Left}
and
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Right}
moves on a tree node
X=\langle({\mathcal{A}^{\prime}},{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
, Spoiler labels the new nodes in
\mathcal{T}
with two values whose maximum is
r^{\prime}
, instead of two values that sum to
r^{\prime}
.

The following proposition considers when we could look at games on each of the singleton pairs, rather than entire classes of
\tau
-structures.

Proposition 20
.

Consider the syntactic game for any compositional syntactic measure
f
satisfying:

(a)

there are finitely many equivalence classes of the equivalence relation “satisfy the same
FO
-sentences with
f
-measure
r
and
k
variables”,

(b)

each such class is definable by a sentence with the parameters defining the class,

(c)

the set of sentences with these parameters is closed under disjunction.

Then, for all nonempty sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{B}
of
\tau
-structures, and for all
r,k\in\mathbb{N}
, the following statements are equivalent:

(a)

Spoiler wins the
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f)
on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
.

(b)

For every
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{A}
and every
{\bf B}\in\mathcal{B}
, there is a separating
k
-variable sentence
\varphi
for
(\{{\bf A}\},\{{\bf B}\})
with
f(\varphi)=r
.

Proof 6.3
.

(\Longrightarrow:)
Take a separating
k
-variable sentence
\varphi
for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
with
f(\varphi)=r
. This is separating for every pair
(\{{\bf A}\},\{{\bf B}\})
with
{\bf A}\in\mathcal{A}
and
{\bf B}\in\mathcal{B}
.

(\Longleftarrow:)
By property 1, there are finitely many equivalence classes of the equivalence relation “satisfy the same
FO
-sentences with measure
r
and
k
variables”, and by property 2, each such class is definable by a
FO
-sentence with these parameters. Let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{m}
be the list of all such sentences arising from structures in
\mathcal{A}
, and consider the sentence
\psi=\theta_{1}\lor\ldots\lor\theta_{m}
. By property 3,
\psi
is a
k
-variable formula with
f(\psi)=r
. We claim that
\psi
is a separating sentence for
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
. Indeed, clearly, every structure in
\mathcal{A}
satisfies one of the
\theta_{j}
’s. We claim that no structure
{\bf B}\in\mathcal{B}
does. Otherwise, take
{\bf B}\in\mathcal{B}
satisfying
\theta_{j}
for some

{\bf B}
satisfies the same sentences with measure

and
k

{\bf A}\in\mathcal{A}
. But then
(\{{\bf A}\},\{{\bf B}\})
is inseparable, which is a contradiction.

immediately helps us relate the syntactic game on the quantifier rank measure

We next remark on the formula size measure, denoted by
f_{s}
in Example
6.1
. Consider the
\mathcal{SG}(r,k;f_{s})
game, and observe that it would be very similar to the QVT game, with the following differences on a node
X=\langle({\mathcal{A}^{\prime}},{\mathcal{B}^{\prime}})\,\big|\,r^{\prime}\rangle
:

•

For a
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Left}
,
\mathsf{Pebble\text{-}Right}
, or
\mathsf{Swap}
move, Spoiler would decrement the value of the counter
r^{\prime}
to
r^{\prime}-1
.

•

For a
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Left}
or
\mathsf{Split\text{-}Right}

r^{\prime}
, Spoiler would choose
r^{\prime}_{1}
and
r^{\prime}_{2}
such that

.

•

Spoiler would only close nodes with counter value of

Note that this last game is very similar to the ones in

]
and
[

]
. In fact, as in
[

]
, we could have changed the rules of the syntactic game and allowed Duplicator to choose one of the branches from a split move to continue along as well, without affecting the eventual winner. But then we would lose the property that a closed game tree is isomorphic to a parse tree of a separating sentence.

Finally, we remark briefly on the measure
f_{t}
that is always zero. This is clearly a valid compositional syntactic measure. It should be clear that
\mathcal{SG}(0,k;f_{t})
is equivalent to the
k
-pebble game
[

]
. Formally, we claim that Spoiler wins the

-pebble game on

if and only if he wins
\mathcal{SG}(0,k;f_{t})
on

. Indeed, if Spoiler has a winning strategy on the
k
-pebble game, he can make the same move at the root node of

, and then once Duplicator responds, he can split the resulting node into singletons and win inductively on each of them. Conversely, if Duplicator wins the
k
-pebble game, she just plays the same moves on the syntactic game without making any copies, proving the result.

7.
Open Problems & Future Directions

Our results suggest several open problems about multi-structural games and their
variants. To begin with, we saw that the hereditary MS game with
repebbling (Game
15
) does not simultaneously capture the
number of quantifiers and the number of variables. What is the
fragment of FO captured by this game? Considered from a slightly
different perspective, how is the “hereditary” property reflected by
FO semantics?

We also investigated the variant of the MS game in which Spoiler wins
without ever playing on top. In some cases, Spoiler wins the MS game
on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
without playing on top on the left side (but may play
on top on the right), while in other cases, this is reversed. Is it
true that Spoiler wins the
r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
without
playing on top if and only if Spoiler wins both the
r
-round MS game
on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
without playing on top on the left side, and the

r
-round MS game on
(\mathcal{A},\mathcal{B})
without playing on top on the right
side?

We investigated compositional syntactic measures and their associated
games for first-order logic (introduced for the case of infinitary formulas by
[
VW13
]
). These definitions allowed us to relate structural properties
of such complexity measures to properties of game strategies. What
other relationships are possible? Can we unify and better understand
logical inexpressibility results by studying syntactic measures?

Beyond these and other related problems, the main challenge is to use
syntactic games to prove lower bounds on any reasonable complexity
measure, first on unordered structures and then, hopefully, on ordered
structures — the latter will be very difficult, because of
connections to longstanding open problems in computational complexity
(see Section
1
).
A few exceptions notwithstanding (e.g.,
[
Sch94
,
Ruh99
,
Sch01
]
), there are relatively few inexpressibility results obtained using EF games on ordered structures or on structures with built-in arithmetic predicates. Can we go beyond these applications of the EF games? What about other syntactic measures on ordered structures, such as simultaneous bounds on number of quantifiers and number of variables?
