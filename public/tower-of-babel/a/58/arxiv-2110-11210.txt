Minimax Problems with Coupled Linear Constraints: Computational Complexity and Duality
Ioannis Tsaknakis, Mingyi Hong, Shuzhong Zhang, Ioannis Tsaknakis, Mingyi Hong, Shuzhong Zhang

Abstract
In this work we study a special minimax problem where there are linear constraints that couple both the minimization and maximization decision variables. The problem is a generalization of the traditional saddle point problem (which does not have the coupling constraint), and it finds applications in wireless communication, game theory, transportation, just to name a few. We show that the considered problem is challenging, in the sense that it violates the classical max-min inequality, and that it is NP-hard even under very strong assumptions (e.g., when the objective is strongly convex-strongly concave). We then develop a duality theory for it, and analyze conditions under which the duality gap becomes zero. Finally, we study a class of stationary solutions defined based on the dual problem, and evaluate their practical performance in an application on adversarial attacks on network flow problems.



keywords

min-max problem, coupled constraints, duality theory, computational complexity, max-min inequality.

In the above expression,

Problems (

For example, the original problem (

However, we will show that problem (

On the other hand, compared with (

1.1
Representative Applications

1.1.1
Adversarial attacks in resource allocation problems

Consider a setting where a player (the ‘user’) aims to optimally allocate a resource of fixed total amount
c_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
, across
n
different tasks. The goal is to maximize certain utility which is a function of the allocation.
Also, there is an adversary that tries to reduce the utility of the user, by designing
an allocation strategy across the
n
tasks that ‘forces’ the user (which utilizes the remaining resource) to select an allocation that lowers its utility. As a concrete example, let us consider a transmission rate maximization problem under the presence of a jammer, which arises in wireless communications.

Specifically, we consider a problem where a single user (i.e., a transmitter-receiver pair) transmits messages over
n
channels. The goal of this user it to allocate power (the ‘resource’) across the
n
channels (the ‘tasks’) such that its transmission rate (the ‘utility’) is maximized. Suppose that there is a jammer (the ‘adversary’) in the system, whose goal is to minimize the user’s rate (e.g.,
[
17
]
).
In this problem we assume that the channel is Gaussian, and denote with
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}
the power allocations of the user and the jammer, respectively. Then, the user’s system rate is given by
[
17
,
30
]

R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(1+\frac{g^{u}_{i}x_{i}}{\sigma^{2}+g^{j}_{i}y_{i}}\right),

where
g^{u}_{i}
,
g^{j}_{i}
are the gains of the user and the jammer on channel
i
, respectively, and
\sigma^{2}
is the noise power.
Note that both players’ power allocations are subject to constraints
\mathcal{X}=\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{x}\geq{\bf{0}},\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\leq\bar{x}\}
and
\mathcal{Y}=\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\mathbf{y}\geq{\bf{0}},\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}\leq\bar{y}\}
, where
\bar{x},\bar{y}
are the total power budgets for the user and the adversary, respectively.
Moreover, in certain communication systems such as cognitive radio network
[
52
]
, it is required that the total transmission power on each channel is upper bounded by the so-called
interference temperature
(denoted by a constant
c>0
), in order to limit the
total
interference caused to other (perhaps more important) co-channel users in the system.
Consequently, the problem of how to
optimally
attack the user in such a multi-channel wireless system can be formulated as the following linearly constrained minimax problem, which is a special case of (
mM-I
):

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}R(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

1.1.2
Adversarial attacks in network flow problems

Consider a
flow network
represented by a directed graph
G=(V,E)
, where
V
is the set of vertices and
E
is the set of edges. Let
x_{e}
and
p_{e}
denote the flow and capacity on each edge
e\in E
, respectively; let
\mathbf{x}:=\{x_{e}\}_{e\in E}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
and
\mathbf{p}:=\{p_{e}\}_{e\in E}\in\mathbb{R}^{|E|}
denote the vectors of edge flows and the edge capacities, respectively.
Let
q_{e}(x_{e})
denote the cost of moving one unit of flow across edge
e
,
and
s
and
t
the source and sink node, respectively.
Suppose
F
is a set that collects all the edges used by
\mathbf{x}
to deliver a total of
r_{t}
units of flow from
s
to
t

q_{tot}(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{e\in F}q_{e}(x_{e})x_{e}

A minimum cost network flow problem can be defined as finding the paths with the minimum cost from source to sink, so that we can successfully transport certain amount of flow
[
8
]

\mathbf{y}:=\{y_{e}\}_{e\in E}
denote the set of flows controlled by the adversary, and
b>0
be its total budget. Then, the problem of adversary can be formulated as follows:

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf{0}\leq\bf{y}\leq\bf{p}}}{{\sum_{(i,j)\in E}y_{ij}=b}}}

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\bf{0}\leq\bf{x}\leq\bf{p}}}{{\sum\limits_{(i,t)\in E}x_{it}=r_{t}}}}\sum_{(i,j)\in E}\overline{q}_{ij}(x_{ij},y_{ij})x_{ij}

(2)

s.t.

\displaystyle\bf{x}+\bf{y}\leq\bf{p}

\displaystyle\hskip-8.53581pt\sum_{(i,j)\in E}x_{ij}-\sum\limits_{(j,k)\in E}x_{jk}=0,\;\forall j\in V\setminus\{s,t\},

where
\overline{q}_{ij}(x_{ij},y_{ij})
is related to the cost of routing both
x_{ij}
and
y_{ij}
onto the network; a simple choice is
\overline{q}_{ij}(x_{ij},y_{ij})=q_{ij}(x_{ij}+y_{ij})
.
We note that such a kind of attack falls under the scope of network interdiction problems
[
45
]
and there are several works in literature that study variants of this application
[
15
,
43
,
44
]
. We note that problem (
2
) can be used to model attacks for real systems such as
communication networks
[
15
]
or power networks
[
43
]
, in which the adversary aims to maximize the cost of the network owner by injecting spurious traffic or disabling network components.

1.2
Contributions

In this work, we study the minimax problem with coupled linear constraints (
mM-I
). Our main contributions are listed below:

\bullet
We
first identify relationships between (
mM-I
) and a number of its variants, and show that even when
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is strongly-convex in
\mathbf{x}
and strongly-concave in
\mathbf{y}
, the well-known max-min inequality does not hold true. Additionally, we show that (
mM-I
) and its variants are NP-hard in general.

\bullet

We develop a duality theory under the assumption that the objective
f
is strongly concave w.r.t
\mathbf{y}
. Specifically, we define three different dual problems, identify the conditions under which strong duality holds, and establish the equivalence of their solutions to that of the original primal problem.

\bullet
Based on our developed dual problems, we identify a new stationary solution concept, and develop a first-order algorithm to evaluate the practical performance of such a solution concept on an adversarial attack problem for network flows.

We emphasize that in order to evaluate the quality of the proposed stationary solution concept, we need to develop an efficient numerical algorithm. The algorithm itself is new (to our knowledge), but its analysis is relatively standard, and we do not have the space to include the details here; the algorithmic development is indeed not the focus of this paper.

1.3
Related Works

The class of problems (
mM-I
) is closely related to problems in optimization and game theory. Below, we review the related literature.

Minimax problems.
These problems have been extensively studied
under different assumptions for the objective
f
. For instance, there are works for (strongly)-convex (strongly)-concave
[
33
,
34
,
50
]
, non-convex (strongly)-concave
[
26
,
27
,
30
,
37
]
, and non-convex non-concave
[
28
,
37
,
51
]
problems. We refer the interested readers to a recent survey about detailed developments
[
42
]
. Specifically, in
[
26
]
the ‘classical’ Gradient Descent-Ascent (GDA) algorithm is analyzed, and convergence is established to stationary solutions, under the assumption that the objective is non-convex (strongly)-concave. Moreover, in
[
34
]
two extensions of GDA are studied, namely the Optimistic Gradient Descent-Ascent (OGDA), and the Extragradient (EG) algorithm, albeit in a convex-concave setting. In another extension, the inner-level problem is solved using multiple steps of a first-order algorithm; the convergence of this scheme is analyzed in
[
37
]
assuming that the objective is non-convex in
\mathbf{x}
, and satisfies either the PL condition or is concave in
\mathbf{y}
.

The above works focus on problems
without
any constraints that couple the inner and outer variables. In these problems, the optimality conditions that are typically used are: a) the stationarity conditions of objective
f
w.r.t
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, and b) the stationarity condition of the upper-level objective
\rho(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, i.e.,
0\in\partial\rho(\mathbf{x})
. However, these conditions were defined for problems with no coupled constraints, and thus they cannot be applied in our case. Therefore, it is no longer clear how algorithms such as OGDA can be used.
Consequently, the analysis of (
mM-I
) requires different approaches.

There are a couple of very recent works that study min-max problems with coupled constraints similar to (
mM-I
). In
[
9
]
the authors extend the notion of local minimax points (e.g.
[
23
]
) for min-max problems with coupled constraints, and derive the respective necessary and sufficient optimality conditions. However, no duality theory or algorithms are developed.
Moreover, in
[
18
]
the authors study a min-max problem where the objective is convex-concave, the coupled constraints are of the form
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq 0
, for some function
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
that is concave in
\mathbf{y}
, and the solution concept they consider is the same as in this work. Most importantly they also assume that the inner function
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\geq\bf{0}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is convex, which makes the problem tractable. On the contrary, we make no such assumption, and develop our theory and algorithm for the case where the problem is still difficult (i.e., NP-hard).

Generalized Nash equilibrium games.
Our problem formulation is also related to the
generalized Nash equilibrium (GNE)
game
[
12
,
11
,
14
]
, which is an extension of Nash games where the action space of each player depends on the actions of the other players. Specifically, a two player GNE game can be formulated as follows
[
12
]
:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}(\mathbf{y})}f_{1}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),\;\;\min\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}(\mathbf{x})}f_{2}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),

(3)

where
f_{1}
,
f_{2}
are the utilities of two players.
Problem (
mM-I
) is related to GNE games in the zero-sum case where
f_{1}=-f_{2}
. However, in GNE games the goal is to attain a Nash equilibrium, and not a minimax/maximin point. In other words, unlike problems (
mM-I
) the two problems do not have an order. In addition, note that for GNE games, the equilibirum solutions do not always exist, and certain (strong) assumptions are required; for instance the utilities
f_{1},f_{2}
need to be quasi-convex
[
22
]
.

One technique that was developed for GNEs
[
47
]
utilizes the Nikaido-Isoda function
[
36
]
to reformulate the GNE-finding problem to a constraint optimization task. However, the main results in this case have been obtained under the assumption that the utilities are convex.
Also, a number of penalty approaches
[
39
,
13
]
have been proposed, nonetheless these are considered difficult to solve in practice. Another approach reduces the problem of finding the KKT points of a GNE game to a variational inequality problem
[
2
]
.
Overall, the above approaches cannot be applied in problem (
mM-I
) because they were developed for problems of the form (
3
), where each player has its own utility function, and the order of play does not matter.

Stackelberg games.
Another class of game-theoretic problems that are closely related to problem (
mM-I
) are
Stackelberg games

[
48
,
38
,
49
]
, in which there are two players, the leader and the follower. The leader can anticipate the follower’s actions. In this case, the order in which the two players act matters.
Then a (general-sum) Stackelberg game can be formulated as the following bi-level program:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\text{ s.t. }\mathbf{y}\in\arg\min\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Therefore, these games are in some sense more general than problem (
mM-I
), which can only model zero-sum Stackelberg games.
On the other hand, typically in Stackelberg games the action space of the follower (resp. the leader) is independent of the action of the leader (resp. the follower); see, e.g.,
[
54
, sec. 2.6.1]
. As a result, problem (
mM-I
) extends the zero-sum Stackelberg games by taking into consideration the interactions between the players’ actions.

Bi-level optimization.
Finally, we would like to discuss the relationship between problem (
mM-I
) and bi-level optimization problems of the following form:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\quad\text{ s.t. }\mathbf{y}\in\arg\min\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}(\mathbf{x})}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Bi-level optimization was formally introduced in
[
5
]
, and it is also related to the broader class of problems of Mathematical Programming with Equilibrium Constraints
[
31
]
. A generic instance of bi-level problems includes all minimax problems as special cases, with or without coupled constraint; see
[
7
,
10
,
29
]
for a number of survey papers. However, bi-level optimization problems are in general very challenging to solve. More precisely
[
29
]
: 1) even linear bi-level problems are NP-hard, 2) the lower-level problem might have multiple solutions, 3) the feasible region defined by the lower-level problem can be a non-convex set, 4) they are non-smooth in general.

Several techniques have been developed in the literature for solving these problems. One such technique uses the value function
V(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}(\mathbf{x})}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
to express the lower-level problem as an inequality constraint, and transforms the bi-level problem into a single-level optimization task
[
53
,
25
]
. However, not only this reformulated problem does not satisfy any of the known constraint qualifications (CQ), but also the non-smoothness of
V(\mathbf{x})
makes the problem difficult.
Alternative CQs such as calmness conditions have been proposed,
which are trivially satisfied in minimax problems
[
53
]
. Based on the calmness condition, a penalty method has been developed in
[
25
]
, but it only applies to bi-level problems in which the constraints set of the lower-level problem is independent of the variable of the upper one.
Overall, this generic theory does not provide much understanding about the structure of our problem.

There is a recent line of work in (stochastic) single and two-time scale gradient descent methods
[
16
,
21
,
24
,
6
]
. Under the assumption that the lower-level problem is strongly-convex (and thus admits a unique solution) and unconstrained, the authors attempt to minimize the objective
\phi(\mathbf{x})=F(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x}))
. In this case the implicit function theorem provides access to the gradient
\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x})
.
Unfortunately, in our case the constraints on the lower-level problem prevent us from using these type of methods.

All the above approaches were developed for general bi-level problems. As a result, they were designed to be suitable for a wide range of problems. Moreover, these approaches are either not applicable to our problem, or even if they are, they are not very attractive (e.g., not very efficient, do not have iteration complexity guarantees, difficult to analyze due to non-smoothness). On the other hand, problem (
mM-I
) has a special minimax structure, that is the lower-level objective
f
is the negative of the upper level one
F
, and the coupling constraints are linear in the decision variables. Therefore, in contrast to the general methods presented here, our goal is to leverage the special structure of problem (
mM-I
) to design methods tailored for it.

2
Minimax problems with coupled linear constraints

In this section, we derive a few unique properties about problem (
mM-I
), and outline the challenges in designing efficient algorithms for it.
To begin with our analysis, let us first introduce a number of variants of problem of (
mM-I
), and understand their relations.

Definition 1
.

We define the following problems:

A.

Inner-Level Coupling (I)

1.

miniMax with Inner-Level Coupling (mM-I)

\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

(mM-I)

2.

Maximin with Inner-Level Coupling (Mm-I)

\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right).

(Mm-I)

B.

Outer-Level Coupling (O)

1.

miniMax with Outer-Level coupling (mM-O)

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\leq\bf{c}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right),\;\;\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\in\arg\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

2.

Maximin with Outer-Level coupling (Mm-O)

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{y})+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right),\;\;\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{y})\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

We note that for the two outer-level coupled problems, the constraint set of the outer problem depends on the solutions
\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
of the inner one. Since
\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
is typically a non-linear function, technically such a constraint is not a linear one any more. Further, at this point these problems are under-specified, as it is not clear which of the solutions of
\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})
and
\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{y})
(e.g. one, some, or all) are expected to satisfy the constraints
A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\leq\bf{c}
and
A\mathbf{x}^{*}(\mathbf{y})+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}
, respectively.
Note that these two outer-level coupling problems are mainly introduced to better understand the two inner-level coupling problems. They have their own special structures, therefore require a separate treatment (which is beyond the scope of the current paper).

For problem (

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\phi(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{\rm where}\quad\phi(\mathbf{x}):=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

(4a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}^{*}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}).

(4b)

For problem (
Mm-I

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{*}\in\arg\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y})

(5a)

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\arg\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}^{*}\leq\mathbf{c}}\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{x}),\quad\mbox{\rm where}\quad\tilde{\phi}(\mathbf{x}):=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

\underline{f},\overline{f}
such that:

\displaystyle\underline{f}\leq f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq\overline{f},\;\forall~\mathbf{x}\in X,\;\mathbf{y}\in Y.

(6)

Next, we
characterize the relationships between the four problems in Definition
1
. As a convention, we will denote the
optimal
objective value of a problem
P
as
v\left(P\right)
.

Proposition 4
(Relations between problems).

For the values of the problems (
mM-I
), (
Mm-I
), (
B.1.
) and (
B.2.
), the following relationships hold:

•

v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_minimax}})\leq v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:out_minimax}})
;

•

v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_maximin}})\geq v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:out_maximin}})
;

•

v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:out_minimax}})\geq v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:out_maximin}})
.

Moreover, there is no definitive relation between the rest of the pairs, namely the pairs

\left\{v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_minimax}}),v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_maximin})}\right\}
,

\left\{v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_minimax}}),v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:out_maximin})}\right\}
,

\left\{v\mbox{\rm(\ref{eq:out_minimax}}),v(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_maximin}})\right\}
. That is,
any relation of
<
,
=
, or
>
can hold between them.

Proof.
See Appendix
A.1
.

The most remarkable fact concerns the relation between (
mM-I
) and (
B.2.
) [or between (
B.1.
) and (
Mm-I
)].
Note that after exchanging the min and max operators, (
B.2.
) becomes (
mM-I
).
Proposition
4
shows that that there is no definite relation between (
mM-I
) and (
B.2.
).
On the contrary,
in standard minimax problems (
1
), the max-min inequality
[
35
, Theorem 1.3.1]
holds, that is

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)\leq\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right),

(7)

for any function
f
and non-empty, closed sets
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
. In addition, if
f
is convex in
\mathbf{x}
and concave in
\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}
are compact, then the values of these problems are equal.

The results shown in proposition (
4
) indicate that the four problems we studied above should be harder than the standard minimax problems. Indeed, as we show below, problems (
mM-I
)-(
B.1.
) are all NP-hard in general, even when the objective function
f
is strongly-convex strongly-concave, and the sets
\mathcal{X}
and
\mathcal{Y}
are compact.

Proposition 5
.

Consider the (
mM-I
) problem with a strongly-convex strongly-concave objective
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. This problem is NP-hard.

Proof.

Consider the problem

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{1}}\left(\max\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle-\mathbf{1}\leq\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{1}}}{{\mathbf{x}-2\mathbf{y}=\bf{0}}}}\|\mathbf{x}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{x}^{T}Q\mathbf{y}-4\|\mathbf{y}\|^{2}+\mathbf{d}^{T}\mathbf{x}\right).

(8)

where
Q\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is a symmetric matrix with
Q\preceq 0
,
\mathbf{d}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
\mathbf{1}
is a vector of ones. We can easily verify that problem (
8
) is of the form (
mM-I
), and satisfies Assumption
2
.
Then, using the coupled equality constraint
\mathbf{x}-2\mathbf{y}={\bf{0}}
(which can be also expressed with two inequality constraints, i.e.,
\mathbf{x}-2\mathbf{y}\leq{\bf{0}}
and
-\mathbf{x}+2\mathbf{y}\leq{\bf{0}}
), problem (
8
) can be written as

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{0}\leq\mathbf{x}\leq\mathbf{1}}\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{x}^{T}Q\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{d}^{T}\mathbf{x}.

Since
Q\preceq 0
the above is a difficult constrained concave minimization problem. To be more precise, it is established in
[
40
]
that this box-constrained non-convex quadratic problem is NP-hard. The proof is now completed.

Proposition 6
.

Consider the (
B.1.
) problem with a strongly-convex concave objective
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
. This problem is NP-hard.

Proof.

Consider the problem

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq 1,\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}^{*}(\mathbf{x})=0}\left(\max\limits_{\|\mathbf{y}\|\leq 1}\left\{M\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}+\sum\limits_{i,j}^{n}c_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}+\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right\}\right),

(9)

where
c_{i,j}
are constants, and
M>0
is selected such that the function
g(\mathbf{x})=M\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}+\sum\limits_{i,j}^{n}c_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}
is strongly convex in a compact domain (which is a superset of the set
\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq 1
). Therefore, the objective of problem (
9
) is strongly convex in
\mathbf{x}
, and concave (linear) in
\mathbf{y}
.
Then, (
9
) can be equivalently written as

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\|\mathbf{x}\|\leq 1,\mathbf{x}-\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\|\mathbf{x}\|}=0}\left\{M\mathbf{x}^{T}\mathbf{x}+\sum\limits_{i,j}^{n}c_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}+\|\mathbf{x}\|\right\}\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1}\left\{\sum\limits_{i,j}^{n}c_{ij}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{2}\mathbf{x}_{j}^{2}\right\}+M+1.

(10)

It is a known fact that the problem in the RHS of equation (
10
) is in general NP-hard, which
establishes the NP-hardness of problem (
9
).
The proof is now completed.

We would like to stress here that the classical min-max problem of the form (
1
), where the objective
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is differentiable, strongly convex in
\mathbf{x}
, and strongly concave in
\mathbf{y}
, is not NP-hard.
To see this, one needs only to observe that for any
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
, the direction
(\nabla_{x}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{T},-\nabla_{y}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{T})^{T}
defines a separating hyperplane regarding any stationary solution. In particular, for any given stationary solution
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
we have

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{x}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}),-\nabla_{y}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{*})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\nabla_{x}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{T}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{*})-(\nabla_{y}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)^{T}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{y}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-[f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y})-f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0,

where the first inequality in the above derivation is due to the gradient inequality of the convex-concave property, and the second inequality is due to the stationary property:

f(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y})\leq f(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})\leq f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{*}),

for all
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
.
Using the ellipsoid method (see e.g.
[
20
]
), this implies that finding a stationary solution can be done in polynomial-time.
In terms of finding stationary solutions effectively in practice without resorting to the ellipsoid method, there are several works in the literature in which convergence is shown for the above problem to approximate global solutions (i.e., saddle points) in a finite number of iterations for any given precision; see for instance
[
34
,
50
]
.
Therefore, it is the introduction of the linear coupling between the inner and the outer variables in the constraints (of one of the two problems) that renders the problem intractable, even under strong assumptions. To illustrate this point more clearly consider the following example.

Example 7
.

Consider the following simple linearly constrained min-max problem

\displaystyle\min\limits_{x\in[0,1]}\left(\max\limits_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle y\in[-1,1]}}{{x-2y=0}}}f(x,y):=x^{2}-\frac{1}{2}xy-4y^{2}\right).

Notice that the objective
f(x,y)
is strongly convex in
x
and strongly concave in
y
. Then, it is easy to see that inner function takes the form

\phi(x)=\max\limits_{y\in[-1,1]:x-2y=0}x^{2}-\frac{1}{2}xy-4y^{2}=x^{2}-\frac{1}{2}x\frac{1}{2}x-4\frac{1}{4}x^{2}=-\frac{1}{4}x^{2}.

Clearly,
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is non-convex; in fact it is strongly concave. Therefore we see that while the objective
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is a simple strongly convex strongly concave function, the resulting value function
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is non-convex.

Despite the fact that the above results show that the linearly constrained minimax problems are NP-hard in general, it is still desirable to design efficient algorithms for them (for computing certain stationary solutions).
As we have noted in Sec.
1.3
, standard algorithms for minimax problems do not apply, and generic methods for bi-level optimization can be inefficient.
Our idea is to explore the structure of these problems from their dual perspective, and identify equivalent problems that are much easier to optimize. Since our focus is mainly given to the inner-level coupled problems, in the next section we develop a duality theory for problem (
mM-I
).

3
Duality Theory for (
mM-I
)

To begin with, let us define the dual problems.

Definition 8
(Dual Problems).

Consider the Lagrangian of problem (
mM-I
):

\displaystyle L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}(A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}),

(11)

where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf 0}
is the vector of Lagrangian multipliers.
Then, define the following three dual problems:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right),

(D1)

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right),

(D2)

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0},\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).

(D3)

Note that due to the special structure of our problem, we can potentially have a few more possible ‘dual’ problems. However, our analysis will focus on three of them listed above, since they will be instrumental in our subsequent analysis and development of stationary solution concept. Below, we provide the weak duality theorem of problem (
mM-I
).

Theorem 9
(Weak duality).

Under Assumption
2
, we have:

\displaystyle v\left(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_minimax}}\right)\leq v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D1}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D2}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D3}}\right).

Proof.

First, notice that we can exchange the order of the min operators:

\hskip-2.84526pt\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}},\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).\hskip-5.69054pt

So

v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D1}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D2}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D3}}\right)
.
Moreover, using the max-min inequality we have that

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle\leq\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\;\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

Minimizing the problems on both sides over the set
\mathcal{X}
, we can obtain

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).

(12)

Moreover, consider an arbitrary
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and observe that if there exists an index
i
such that
\left[A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\right]_{i}>0
, then we have:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})+\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}\left\{-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\left(A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\right)\right\}=-\infty.

(13)

On the other hand, if it holds that
A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{c}
, then:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})>\underline{f},\quad\forall\;\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}.

(14)

As a result, (
13
) and (
14
) imply that:

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)

\displaystyle=\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq{\bf{c}}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)=\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq{\bf{c}}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Minimizing both sides of the above equality over
\mathcal{X}
we get:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq{\bf{c}}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)=\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).

(15)

Finally, combining (
12
) and (
15
), we conclude that:

\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq{\bf{c}}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)\leq\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf{0}}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).

The proof is now completed.

Next, we develop the strong duality of problem (
mM-I
). That is, we identify the conditions under which strong duality holds, and establish the equivalence of the solutions of the primal problem with those of the dual problems. To begin with, we impose the following assumptions:

Assumption 10
.

Assume that the following holds:

1.

f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is strongly concave in
\mathbf{y}
, for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, with modulus
\mu_{y}
.

2.

For every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
there exists
\mathbf{y}\in{\rm relint}(\mathcal{Y})
such that
(A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c})\leq{\bf 0}
.

Based on the above assumptions, we have the following duality theorem.

Theorem 11
(Duality Theorem).

Under Assumptions
2
and
10
, strong duality holds, that is, we have the following relations:

\displaystyle v\left(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_minimax}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D1}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D2}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D3}}\right).

Also, for the solutions of the dual problems we have that:

1.

There exists a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}\geq{\bf 0}
such that
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
is a solution of (
D2
) if an only if
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})
is a solution of (
mM-I
).

That is, the tuple
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
satisfies the following condition: 1)
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})
is a solution (i.e., a minimax point) of the inner min-max problem with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}
, i.e.,
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})\in\arg\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast});
2)
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}
is a global minimizer of the function
G(\boldsymbol{\lambda}):=\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
,
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}\in\arg\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf 0}}G(\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

2.

There exists a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}\geq{\bf 0}
such that
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
is a solution of (
D3
) if an only if
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})
is a solution of (
mM-I
).

That is:

\left((\mathbf{x}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}),\mathbf{y}^{*}\right)\in\arg\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0},\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

Proof.

We divide the proof into two parts.

Objective equivalence.
We already established in Theorem
9
that

v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D1}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D2}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D3}}\right)
.
Note that strong duality holds for problem
\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, for any fixed
\mathbf{x}
, as a consequence of Assumption
10
(see, e.g.,
[
4
, Prop. 5.3.1]
). Then,

\displaystyle\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\;\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\right)=\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)=\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right).

That is, we have
v\left(\mbox{\rm\ref{eq:inn_minimax}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D1}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D2}}\right)=v\left({\rm\ref{eq:D3}}\right)
.

Solution equivalence.
Next we show the second part of the claim.

First, we show that the solutions of (
mM-I
) and (
D1
) are equivalent. Consider an arbitrary
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, and let us define the following problems

\displaystyle(A):\quad g_{A}(\mathbf{x}):=\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle(B):\quad g_{B}(\mathbf{x}):=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle(C):\quad g_{C}(\mathbf{x}):=\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}).

Let us denote
\left(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{A}\right)
as a solution of (A), and with
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})
a solution of (B). We will show below that if
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})
is a solution of (B), then
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B}
is a solution of (C). Conversely, we will show that if
\mathbf{y}^{*}
is a solution of (C), then there exists a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
such that
(\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
is a solution of (B).

To begin with,
using
[
4
, Prop. 5.3.1]
, we see that Assumption
10
implies that strong duality holds for problem (C).
From
[
4
, Prop. 5.3.2]
we know that when strong duality holds,
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})
satisfies the following conditions:
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B}\geq\bf{0}
,
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A}-\mathbf{c}\leq\bf{0}
, and

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A}=\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B}).

(16)

Moreover, observe that
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})
is a solution of (B), and thus

\displaystyle\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B}=\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})=\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})-(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})^{T}(A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c})\right\}.

(17)

Then, combining (
16
) and (
17
), and considering the strong concavity of
L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})
w.r.t
\mathbf{y}
, we can infer that
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A}=\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B}
. Therefore,
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B}
is a solution of problem (A), and as a result a solution of (C).

Conversely, we will show that if
\mathbf{y}^{*}
is a solution of (C) then there exists a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}
such that
(\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
is a solution of (B). Indeed, under strong duality, for the unique (due to strong concavity) solution
\mathbf{y}^{*}
of (C) there exist Lagrange multipliers
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}\geq{\bf 0}
such that
(\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
is a saddle point of the Lagrangian
L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

[
4
, Prop. 5.3.2]
. We know that the set of saddle points of
L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is the intersection of the set of minimax points
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{A},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{A})
with the set of maximin points
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{B},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{B})
, and thus it is implied that
(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})
is a solution of (B).

We have shown for some arbitrary
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
that
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}}
is a solution of (C) if and only
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}})
is a solution of (B), for some
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}}\geq{\bf{0}}
; here we used the notation
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}}
to make the dependence to that arbitrary
\mathbf{x}
clear.
Finally, note that the strong duality holds for problem (C) for any fixed
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
. This implies that the value of the problems (A), (B), and (C) is the same at every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
, that is

g_{C}(\mathbf{x})=g_{A}(\mathbf{x})=g_{B}(\mathbf{x}),\;\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}.

Therefore,
g_{C},g_{A},g_{B}
have the same set of global minima
\mathcal{X}^{*}
. Then, at any given
\mathbf{x}^{*}\in\mathcal{X}^{*}
,
\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}
is a solution of (C) if and only if
(\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}})
is a solution of (B), for some
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}}\geq{\bf{0}}
.
In conclusion, a point
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}})
is a solution of (
mM-I
) if and only if
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*}_{\mathbf{x}^{*}})
is a solution of (
D1
).
The claim is proved.

Next, we show that the solutions of (
mM-I
) and (
D2
)/(
D3
) are equivalent. Note that the solution sets of the problems (
D1
), (
D2
) and (
D3
) are equivalent, since the order of the min operators can be exchanged. That is, a solution
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
of (
D2
) is also a solution of (
D1
), and vice versa; the similar result holds true between (
D3
) and (
D1
).
In addition, using the (solution) equivalence between problems (
mM-I
) and (
D1
) established above, we can conclude that
a point
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*},\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{*})
is a solution of (
D2
) if an only if
(\mathbf{x}^{*},\mathbf{y}^{*})
is a solution of (
mM-I
). The same result also holds for (
D3
).
The proof is now completed.

In the above proof, note that despite Assumption
10
, which ensures strong duality, the solution sets of the problems (A) and (B) (denoted with
S_{A},S_{B}
, respectively) are not the same. Nonetheless, the original problem (
mM-I
) and the dual one (
D2
) are equivalent (in the sense mentioned in Theorem
11
). This is due to the fact that
S_{B}\subseteq S_{A}
, which implies that every solution of
B
is a saddle point (i.e., the points in the set
S_{saddle}=S_{B}\cap S_{A}
) of the Lagrangian
L
and vice versa.
Below we provide a relevant example.

Example 12
.

Consider
a problem of the form (
mM-I
), where the inner task takes the form

\max_{y\in[0,2],y\leq 1}-y^{2}+2y
;
the exact form of the outer task is irrelevant for the context of this example.
Then, the primal (A) and dual (B) problems (of the inner task) are

p^{\ast}=\max_{y\in[0,2]}\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\left\{-y^{2}+2y-\lambda(y-1)\right\}

d^{\ast}=\min_{\lambda\geq 0}\max_{y\in[0,2]}\left\{-y^{2}+2y-\lambda(y-1)\right\}.

Note that Assumption
10
is satisfied in this example, and thus
p^{\ast}=d^{\ast}
(i.e., strong duality). However, it can be easily shown that the solutions set of the primal problem is
S_{A}=\{(1,\lambda):\lambda\geq 0\}
, while the respective set of the dual problem is different and consists only of one point, namely
S_{B}=\{(1,0)\}\subseteq S_{A}
. As a result, there is a unique saddle point, i.e.,
S_{saddle}=S_{B}\cap S_{A}=S_{B}=\{(1,0)\}
.

4
First-order stationarity conditions

In Sec.
2
we established that finding the globally optimal solutions of problem (
mM-I
) is NP-hard in general. It is then useful to identify some high-quality solution concepts that can be computed efficiently. In this section, we propose to leverage the duality theory developed in the previous section to identify such a class of first-order stationary solutions. More specifically, we will define first-order stationary solutions based on the dual problem (
D2
). Note that one can directly work with the problem
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y},A\mathbf{x}+B\mathbf{y}\leq\bf{c}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, but this is challenging since
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is neither smooth nor convex.

Before we proceed let us make a few additional assumptions:

Assumption 13
.

We impose the following assumptions

1.

The function
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable w.r.t. both
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
.

2.

The function
f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
is strongly convex in
\mathbf{x}
, for every
\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
, with modulus
\mu_{x}
.

3.

The function
f
has Lipschitz continuous gradients, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})\right\|\leq L_{x}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}\\
\mathbf{y}_{1}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{2}\\
\mathbf{y}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|,\forall\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}.

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-\nabla_{\mathbf{y}}f(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})\right\|\leq L_{y}\left\|\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}\\
\mathbf{y}_{1}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{2}\\
\mathbf{y}_{2}\end{bmatrix}\right\|,\forall\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X},\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}.

Next, let us define the following quantities:

\displaystyle H(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

(18a)

\displaystyle G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

(18b)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{y}}(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\arg\max\limits_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\;\;\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}):=\arg\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}H(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda}).

(18c)

Using these new notations, problem (
D2
) can be decomposed into the following two problems:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}H(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\min_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}\left(\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\;L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)\quad\mbox{(Inner-Level Problem)}

(19)

\displaystyle\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\min\limits_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq\bf{0}}\left(\min\limits_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}}H(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)\quad\quad\;\mbox{(Outer-Level Problem)}.

(20)

Note that the assumption that
f
is strongly-convex in
\mathbf{x}
provides us with a number of useful properties which facilitate algorithm design.
Specifically, it implies that the function
G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
defined above, which is the objective of the dual problem (

), is differentiable. However, notice that
G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is not necessarily convex and therefore any reasonable notion of stationary conditions for (D2) will involve the gradient and the stationary points of
G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
. Therefore, below we derive the formula of the gradient of

Suppose that Assumptions

10

\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=-A\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)-B\overline{\mathbf{y}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right),\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)+\mathbf{c},

where
\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right),\overline{\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)
are defined in (

Now we are ready to provide definitions of (exact and approximate) stationary solutions, based on the dual problem (
D2
).

Definition 15
(Stationary Solutions).

A solution
\left(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)
is an exact stationary solution of (
D2
) if the following holds:

•

(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\mathbf{y}})=\left(\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right),\overline{\mathbf{y}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{x}},\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right)
;

•

\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is a stationary point of
G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, that is

\left\langle\nabla G\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right),\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq 0
.

We will later show that the stationarity conditions for
G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
imply the complementarity condition
\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}^{T}\nabla G\left(\overline{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)=0
with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\geq{\bf 0}
. Next, we provide the definition of the approximate stationary solutions.

Definition 16
(
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate Stationary Solution).

Let us define the following quantities:

\displaystyle Q(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\text{\rm proj}_{\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}-\alpha\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\right)\right),

\displaystyle d(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})

\displaystyle:=\|\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})-\mathbf{x}\|^{2}+\|\overline{\mathbf{y}}(\overline{\mathbf{x}}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\boldsymbol{\lambda})-\mathbf{y}\|^{2},

where
\alpha>0
is a constant independent of
\epsilon
and
\delta
.
Then, a point
\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\in\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}\times\mathbb{R}^{k}_{+}
is called an
(\epsilon,\delta)
-approximate stationary solution if it holds that

\displaystyle\left\|Q\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right\|\leq\epsilon,\;\;d\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\leq\delta^{2}.

In words, at an exact stationary solution of (
D2
), the inner-level minimax problem is solved to global optimality, while the outer-level one reaches an exact stationary solution.
However, finding such points in practice is unrealistic. The notion of approximate stationary solutions is then introduced as a relaxation. At an approximate stationary solution
\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{\mathbf{y}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)
the inner-level problem is solved inexactly, in the sense that the distance of
(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}},\widetilde{\mathbf{y}})
from the exact solution (of the inner-level problem (
19
) with
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
) is small. In addition, the function
Q(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is the stationarity gap of the outer-level problem at
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
.

Next, we analyze the implications of a point being an (exact or approximate) stationary solution. In particular,
we will show that at an exact stationary solution, the coupled linear constraints as well as the complementary slackness condition will be satisfied.
Further, at an approximate stationary solution, the choice of

So far, we have defined a suitable (dual) reformulation of the original problem (
mM-I

First, we obtain an approximate solution of the inner-level problem (
19
), by using any reasonable iterative subroutine that can solve a strongly-convex strongly-concave minimax problem. In Algorithm

Such an algorithm can be, for instance, the gradient descent-(multi-step) ascent
[

or the extragradient method
[

]
.
Afterwards, one iteration of gradient descent is performed on the outer problem (

In the subsequent discussion, we refer to

First of all, under the Assumptions

6
Concluding Remarks

In this paper we study a minimax problem, where the constraints of the inner maximization problem depends linearly on the variable of the outer one. This special structure makes the problem NP-hard, even when the objective is strongly-convex strongly-concave. We then develop the duality theory of the problem, and establish conditions under which strong duality holds. These results allow us to introduce duality-based reformulations of the original problem, and propose a set of first-order stationarity conditions.
Moreover, we demonstrate that the proposed formulation can model adversarial attacks on network flow problems.
In the future, we plan to study more applications of this formulation, as well as to related problems where the linear constraints are in the outer problem.

It is possible to extend the main results (e.g., the duality theory, the definition and properties of stationary points) to problems with general, not necessarily linear, constraints of the form
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq 0
, where
g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
. This is possible with addition of the following assumptions (below we provide only the key assumptions):

1.

g_{i}(\cdot,\mathbf{y})
is concave
\forall\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}
and
g_{i}(\mathbf{x},\cdot)
is convex
\forall\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X},\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
.

2.

For every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathcal{X}
there exists
\mathbf{y}\in{\rm relint}(\mathcal{Y})
such that
g(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})<{\bf 0}
.

3.

|g_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{1})-g_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y}_{2})|\leq L\left[\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\|+\|\mathbf{y}_{1}-\mathbf{y}_{2}\|\right],\;\forall~\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{X},~\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2}\in\mathcal{Y}
, for some
L>0
,
\forall i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}

Lemma 20
(Danskin’s Theorem).

Consider the problem
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
, where
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
is differentiable and strongly concave in
\mathbf{y}
, and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a non-empty compact set. Also, consider the set
\mathcal{Y}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})=\arg\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
which is a singleton for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then,
\phi(\mathbf{x})
is differentiable and it holds that
\nabla\phi(\mathbf{x})=\nabla_{x}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x}))
, where
\mathbf{y}^{\ast}(\mathbf{x})=\arg\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}f(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})
.

Proof of Lemma
14
.

To begin with, we will compute the gradient of function
H(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}L(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
, defined in (
18a
).
Indeed, notice that
\mathcal{Y}
is a compact set,
L
is differentiable and has a unique maximum due to its strong-concavity in
\mathbf{y}

[
3
]
we can infer that

is differentiable with:

\displaystyle\nabla H(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\nabla L\left(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right),\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)=\begin{bmatrix}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}f(\mathbf{x},\overline{\mathbf{y}}\left(\mathbf{x},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right))-A^{T}\boldsymbol{\lambda}\\

where

Next we will show that

\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{=}}\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{f(\rho\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\rho)\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y})-\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\left(A\left(\rho\mathbf{x}_{1}+(1-\rho)\mathbf{x}_{2}\right)+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\right)\right\}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(c)}}{{\leq}}\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{\rho f(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{y})-\rho\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\left(A\mathbf{x}_{1}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\right)\right\}-\frac{\mu_{x}}{2}\rho(1-\rho)\|\mathbf{x}_{1}-\mathbf{x}_{2}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\max_{\mathbf{y}\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{(1-\rho)f(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{y})-(1-\rho)\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{T}\left(A\mathbf{x}_{2}+B\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{c}\right)\right\}

11

w.r.t.
\mathbf{x}
(with modulus

We can bound the first term in the above inequality as follows:

\displaystyle\max\left\{0,\left[A\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}-\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right)+B\left(\widetilde{\mathbf{y}}-\overline{\mathbf{y}}\left(\overline{\mathbf{x}}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right),\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right)\right]_{i}\right\}
