Validated integration of semilinear parabolic PDEs
Jan Bouwe van den Berg Maxime Breden Ray Sheombarsing

Abstract
Integrating evolutionary partial differential equations (PDEs) is an essential ingredient for studying the dynamics of the solutions. Indeed, simulations are at the core of scientific computing, but their mathematical reliability is often difficult to quantify, especially when one is interested in the output of a given simulation, rather than in the asymptotic regime where the discretization parameter tends to zero. In this paper we present a computer-assisted proof methodology to perform rigorous time integration for scalar semilinear parabolic PDEs with periodic boundary conditions. We formulate an equivalent zero-finding problem based on a variation of constants formula in Fourier space. Using Chebyshev interpolation and domain decomposition, we then finish the proof with a Newton–Kantorovich type argument. The final output of this procedure is a proof of existence of an orbit, together with guaranteed error bounds between this orbit and a numerically computed approximation. We illustrate the versatility of the approach with results for the Fisher equation, the Swift–Hohenberg equation, the Ohta–Kawasaki equation and the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation. We expect that this rigorous integrator can form the basis for studying boundary value problems for connecting orbits in partial differential equations.

Introduction

During the last decades, computer-assisted proofs have become an increasingly effective tool in
the study of nonlinear ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and dynamical systems in general.
The rapid progress of the development of computer hardware has made it possible to put numerical
simulations on a rigorous footing through the construction of theorems whose
hypotheses can be verified with the aid of a computer. Today, there exists a large variety of rigorous
numerical methods for studying invariant objects in systems of ODEs, such as equilibria, periodic orbits,

More precisely, in this paper we develop a rigorous computational method for validating solutions of initial value problems for scalar semilinear parabolic equations, with periodic boundary conditions, of the form

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}=(-1)^{R+1}\dfrac{\partial^{2R}u}{\partial x^{2R}}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\dfrac{\partial^{j}g^{(j)}(u)}{\partial x^{j}},&t\in(0,2\tau],\ x\in[0,2\pi],\\[8.61108pt]

are given polynomial functions for

To sketch the context and be able to compare with the existing literature, we start with a more conceptional discussion rather than diving into details immediately.
Conceptually, one can think of (
1
) as being simply an equation of the form

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}=H(u),

(2)

and we assume to have an approximate solution
\bar{u}
at our disposal. Our strategy for validating this approximate solution starts by using a well chosen splitting of the righthand side as

\displaystyle H(u)=\mathcal{L}u+G(u),

(3)

where
\mathcal{L}
is a linear operator which may or may not depend on time.
When
\mathcal{L}
is time
independent
, as it essentially is in the current work (although it is taken piecewise constant in time at a later stage),
we can rewrite (
2
) as a fixed point problem, using the semigroup associated to
\mathcal{L}
and the fixed point operator
\tilde{T}
defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(u)(t)=e^{t\mathcal{L}}f+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}G(u(s))\mbox{d}s,\quad t\in[0,2\tau].

(4)

Our validation procedure for proving the existence of an orbit near
\bar{u}
will be based on the Banach fixed-point theorem. Therefore, we would like
\tilde{T}
to be a contraction on a neighborhood of
\bar{u}
, preferably for long integration times
2\tau
.

Remark 1.2
.

We rely heavily on the structure of equation (
1
), and more precisely on the fact that the highest order term
(-1)^{R+1}\dfrac{\partial^{2R}}{\partial x^{2R}}
is dissipative. Indeed, this ensures that the associated semi-group is contracting on the higher order Fourier modes. Yet, we want our approach to be applicable for equations showcasing interesting dynamics, hence we must also be able to handle the appearance of some unstable modes, which means we allow the semigroup generated by
\mathcal{L}
to not be contracting for (finitely many) low order modes.

We note that, of course, using the splitting
H(u)=\mathcal{L}u+G(u)
, one could always artificially choose
\mathcal{L}
so that the associated semigroup is contracting, but that does not mean that
\tilde{T}
itself will be contracting
for larger integration times, since this is also influenced by
G(u)
.
For instance, the scalar ODE
u^{\prime}=u
can be split as
u^{\prime}=-ku+(k+1)u
, with
k>0
, which gives rise to

\displaystyle\tilde{T}(u)(t)=e^{-tk}f+(k+1)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-(t-s)k}u(s)\mbox{d}s,

which is not contracting in
C^{0}
, except for short times.

The pivotal choice of
\mathcal{L}
that we make in this work is meant to help us getting a contracting operator
\tilde{T}
, even when the semigroup generated by
\mathcal{L}
is not contracting for low order modes. To that end, we take for
\mathcal{L}
an approximation of
DH(\bar{u})
, the Fréchet derivative of
H
at the approximate solution, in order to make
DG(\bar{u})=\mathcal{L}-DH(\bar{u})
small. However, by imposing that
\mathcal{L}
does not depend on time, in general we cannot hope to get
\mathcal{L}
arbitrarily close to
DH(\bar{u})
. Therefore, we further reformulate the problem by first turning it into a zero finding problem

\displaystyle F(u)(t)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,e^{t\mathcal{L}}f+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)\mathcal{L}}G(u(s))\mbox{d}s-u(t)=0,\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,2\tau],

which itself is then turned into another fixed point problem, given by a Newton-like operator

\displaystyle T:u\mapsto u-AF(u),

(5)

where
A
is a suitable approximate inverse of
DF(\bar{u})
. Notice that, for
A=I
, we get
T=\tilde{T}
. Since, as mentioned previously, we already expect
\tilde{T}
to be contracting for sufficiently high order Fourier modes, we only need
A
to act nontrivially on finitely many modes. Once a suitable approximate inverse
A
is defined, we then want to prove that
T
is a contraction in a neighborhood of
\bar{u}
, and this is what most of this paper is devoted to.

Before we proceed, let us again emphasize that the issue of whether
T
is contracting or not, and the issue of whether or not we are actually able to prove it (with computer assistance), both depend greatly on three main (related) ingredients:

•

the choice of
\mathcal{L}
,

•

the choice of
A
,

•

the choice of the Banach space in which we try to prove the contraction.

Remark 1.3
.

In all of the approaches discussed above, as well as in the current work, when trying to validate solutions for larger and larger time intervals, it proves more efficient (and it is often mandatory in order to obtain a proof) to split the interval
[0,2\tau]
into shorter pieces. Of course, one has to glue these subproblems together, and to do so there are two alternatives, which we call here
time stepping
(also sometimes referred to as
concatenation scheme
) and
domain decomposition
.

The idea of time stepping is to start with some initial condition and to rigorously integrate the associated initial value problem on a small time
interval. If the proof is successful, we compute a rigorous enclosure for the endpoint of the orbit and try to integrate the
enclosure
forward in
time. This process is repeated as long as necessary (and possible). In order for long time integration to be feasible, the size of the
enclosures should be “managed” properly. In particular, they should not grow too fast.
This is where the dissipativity of the system can prove very helpful, if correctly leveraged.

An alternative to time stepping is domain decomposition. The idea of this approach is to still split the time domain into smaller subintervals, but then to validated everything at once, via a single fixed point operator coupling all pieces together. This solution can be computationally more expensive, but has the advantage of being directly adaptable to solving boundary value problems in time, which is not as straightforward for the time stepping approach. This time domain decomposition strategy already proved very powerful in the context of validated integration of ODEs
[
46
]
.

In this work, we mainly focus on the domain decomposition approach, which will be presented in detail in Section
6
, but time stepping is also covered in Appendix
A
.

Remark 1.4
.

Up to now, while reviewing the literature concerning rigorous integration of PDEs, we stayed at a rather general level of exposition, and in particular have not yet mentioned discretization choices. We purposely postponed this discussion until now, in order to first better emphasize the conceptual similarities between these different techniques, but of course the way the approximate solutions are represented (and the often related choices of function spaces that are made) are crucial when actually having to derive the precise estimates needed for the validation.

Regarding the spatial variable, Fourier series and associated sequences spaces are used in
[
3
]
, whereas results using finite elements and Sobolev spaces can be found in
[
23
,
28
]
. Regarding the time variable, most of the existing works make use of approximations based on low order 1D finite elements, the notable exception being
[
14
]
, where a setup allowing for a spectral approach in both space and time is introduced.

In this work, we make use of a Fourier decomposition in space. Roughly speaking, dissipativity then tells us that the long time behavior of the dynamical system should mostly be governed by only a finite number of Fourier modes, see Section
3
. This is why it makes sense to study (
1
) via a Fourier expansion in space in the first place, on both paper and in the computer, and it influences the way we define
\mathcal{L}
. Specifically, for the high frequency Fourier modes, we take a time and space average of
DH(\bar{u})
as a starting point for defining
\mathcal{L}
, which yields a diagonal operator in Fourier space. We then also incorporate a time independent approximation of
DH(\bar{u})
projected onto finitely many (low frequency) Fourier modes. Regarding the time variable, we approximate the solution using polynomial interpolation at Chebyshev nodes, which allows us to easily obtain high order approximations when needed, e.g. for accuracy purposes.

•

A new and deliberate choice of the operator
\mathcal{L}
used for the Duhamel reformulation (
4
), specifically: time independent but spatially dependent. This greatly improves the applicability of the validation procedure compared to more naive choices like
\mathcal{L}=(-1)^{R+1}\dfrac{\partial^{2R}}{\partial x^{2R}}

\mathcal{L}
as in (

\mathcal{L}

•

The incorporation of the domain decomposition technique, which will facilitate a future extension to boundary value problems in time, and which comes with an interesting twist in the way the approximate inverse

used for the Newton-Kantorovich reformulation (

) is defined (see Section

•

In the context of domain decomposition, the operator

The Newton-Kantorovich contraction theorem is then applied to a function space with a (time-dependent) weighted norm, where the weight are piecewise constant in time, see (

•

The usage of Chebyshev interpolation in time, whose order can be adapted to the problem at hand.

General estimates and associated code (available at

) in terms of the Fourier coefficients is posed on a space of continuous functions from
[-1,1]
into a space of geometrically decaying sequences

\displaystyle\ell^{1}_{\nu}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\biggl\{a\in\mathbb{C}^{\mathbb{Z}}:\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|a_{n}\right|\nu^{|n|}<\infty\biggr\},

endowed with the norm

\left\|a\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left|a_{n}\right|\nu^{|n|}
,
where
\nu\geq 1
is some decay rate to be chosen later. We define

\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,C\left(\left[-1,1\right],\ell^{1}_{\nu}\right)
to be the space of continuous functions from
[-1,1]
into

\ell^{1}_{\nu}
.

Recall that the Fourier coefficients of the product of two Fourier expansions is given by
the discrete convolution: when
u
and
v
are
2\pi
-periodic functions given by

u(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{u}_{n}e^{inx}

and
v(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{v}_{n}e^{inx}
,
then

\displaystyle\left(uv\right)(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\hat{u}\ast\hat{v}\right)_{n}e^{inx},\qquad\text{where}\qquad\left(\hat{u}\ast\hat{v}\right)_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{u}_{m}\hat{v}_{n-m},\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

and these formal computations are justified as soon as
u
and
v
are smooth enough, say Lipschitz-continuous, which will be the case in this work.

Remark 2.1
.

In order to simplify the notation, in the rest of this paper we will use the same symbol to denote a function and its (discrete) Fourier transform, that is we write

u(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}u_{n}e^{inx}.

It should be clear from the context whether
u
denotes the function
x\mapsto u(x)
or the sequence of Fourier coefficients
u=\left(u_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
.

We also recall that the discrete convolution gives
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
a Banach algebra structure.

Lemma 2.2
.

For all
u,v\in\ell^{1}_{\nu}
,
u\ast v\in\ell^{1}_{\nu}
and

\left\|u\ast v\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}\leq\left\|u\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}\left\|v\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}
.

2.2
Chebyshev interpolation

In this section we recall the basics of Chebyshev interpolation. The reader is referred to

[
11
,
34
,
39
]
for a comprehensive introduction into the theory of Chebyshev approximation.

Definition 2.3

(Chebyshev points)
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. The
K
-th order Chebyshev points
\left(t^{K}_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{K}

are defined by
t^{K}_{k}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\cos(\frac{\pi k}{K})
.

Remark 2.4
.

We shall omit the superscript
K
from the notation whenever it can be easily inferred
from the context. We have ordered the Chebyshev points from
1
to
-1
, i.e.,

t_{0}=1
and
t_{K}=-1
. In the literature the points
\left(t_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{K}
are often referred to as the Chebyshev points of the second kind.

We will refer to the
K
-th order polynomial which interpolates a continuous function
f:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}

at the Chebyshev points
\left(t_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{K}
as the
K
-th order Chebyshev interpolant of
f
.
Furthermore, we shall denote the operator which sends a continuous function
f
to its
K
-th order Chebyshev
interpolant by
P_{K}:C([-1,1],\mathbb{C})\rightarrow C([-1,1],\mathbb{C})
.
We note that
f-P_{K}(f)
vanishes at
t=\pm 1
for any
K
and any
f
, because we have chosen a family of Chebyshev nodes containing the endpoints of the interval
[-1,1]
. We denote the space
C([-1,1],\mathbb{C})
with the
\sup
-norm by
C^{0}
throughout.
Our approximate solutions and our analysis makes use of Chebyshev interpolation in time, and we therefore need to control the associated interpolation errors.

Definition 2.5

(
C^{l}
interpolation error)
.

For
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and
l\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
we denote by
\sigma_{K,l}
a real constant such that, for all
f\in C^{l+1}([-1,1],\mathbb{C})
,

\displaystyle\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\sigma_{K,l}\left\|f^{(l+1)}\right\|_{C^{0}}.

In practice, we need an explicit value for this constant. One can easily find explicit values in the literature, at least for real-valued functions, but not necessarily optimal ones. In Appendix
B
, we recall some known results about this constant, and provide almost optimal values for
\sigma_{K,0}
and
K
small, which is a crucial case for this work.

Another case which we will encounter frequently is when the function to be interpolated is analytic in an open set of the complex plane containing the segment
[-1,1]
. In such a situation, the following error estimate applies.

Definition 2.6
.

For
\rho\geq 1
, we denote by
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}\subset\mathbb{C}
the open ellipse with foci
\pm 1
such that the length of the semi-major and of the semi-minor axes sum up to
\rho
, or equivalently, the set of all complex numbers
z
such that
|z-1|+|z+1|<\rho+\rho^{-1}
. The set
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
is sometimes referred to as a
Bernstein ellipse
. Notice that
\rho=1
is a degenerate case:
\mathcal{E}_{1}=(-1,1)
.

Theorem 2.7
.

Let
\rho>1
. Suppose
f:\left[-1,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
can be analytically extended to
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
, and is bounded on
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\frac{4\rho^{-K}}{\rho-1}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|f(z)\right|,

for any
K\in\mathbb{N}
.

The proof can be found in
[
39
, Theorem
8.2
]
.

In practice, we compute and represent interpolation polynomials using the Chebyshev basis
\left(T_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Definition 2.8
.

The Chebyshev polynomials
T_{k}:\left[-1,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are defined by
the relation
T_{k}\left(\cos\theta\right)=\cos\left(k\theta\right)
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and

\theta\in\left[0,\pi\right]
.

Remark 2.9
.

In the literature the polynomials
\left(T_{k}\right)_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
are often referred to as the Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind. The
K
-th order Chebyshev points
\left(t^{K}_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{K}
are the points in
\left[-1,1\right]
at which
T_{K}
attains its extrema. Furthermore, note that
T_{k}\left(t^{K}_{j}\right)=\cos(\frac{\pi jk}{K})
for
k,j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.

The polynomials
\left(T_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{K}

constitute a basis for the space of
K
-th order polynomials
\mathbb{P}_{K}
. Hence, any polynomial
P
of degree at most
K
, and in particular any interpolation polynomial
P_{K}(f)
, can be uniquely written as

\displaystyle P=P_{0}+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{k}T_{k},

(11)

for some Chebyshev coefficients
P_{0},\ldots,P_{K}\in\mathbb{C}
.

For any given polynomial written in the Chebyshev basis, we can easily control both its
C^{0}
norm and its supremum on a Bernstein ellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
, in terms of its Chebyshev coefficients.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
and
P=P_{0}+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}P_{k}T_{k}
, then

\displaystyle\left\|P\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\left|P_{0}\right|+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|P_{k}\right|,

(12)

and

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|P(z)\right|\leq\left|P_{0}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|P_{k}\right|\left(\rho^{k}+\rho^{-k}\right).

(13)

Proof.

Any element
z
of
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
can be written
z=\frac{\omega+\omega^{-1}}{2}
for some
\omega\in\mathbb{C}
such that
1\leq|\omega|\leq\rho
, and we have the identity

T_{k}\bigl(\frac{\omega+\omega^{-1}}{2}\bigr)=\frac{\omega^{k}+\omega^{-k}}{2}
,
which holds because both sides are analytic and coincide for
\omega
on the unit circle. Hence

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|T_{k}(z)\right|=\frac{\rho^{k}+\rho^{-k}}{2}
,
which yields (
13
).
∎

Remark 2.11
.

Note that (
11
) is, up to the coordinate transformation
x=\cos\theta
, a Fourier cosine series.
This is the motivation for using the factor
2
in front of the coefficients
(P_{k})_{k=1}^{K}
.
In particular, with this convention the Chebyshev coefficients of the product of two Chebyshev expansions can be computed directly (i.e., without a rescaling factor) via the discrete convolution.

2.3
A fixed point Theorem

Let
(X^{m},\|\cdot\|_{X^{m}})_{m=1}^{M}
be Banach spaces,
X=\Pi_{m=1}^{M}X^{m}
the product space, and
\pi^{m}:X\to X^{m}
the projections onto the components. Let
r_{*}=(r_{*}^{m})_{m=1}^{M}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{M}
and
\bar{x}\in X
. For any
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{M}
, we define
\bbox(\bar{x},r)=\{x\in X:\left\|\pi^{m}(x-\bar{x})\right\|_{X^{m}}\leq r^{m}\text{ for }1\leq m\leq M\}
. We consider a map
T\in C^{1}(\bbox(\bar{x},r),X)
. For
r,r_{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{M}
we say that
r\leq r_{*}
if
r^{m}\leq r_{*}^{m}
for all
1\leq m\leq M
. Finally, we denote partial Fréchet derivatives by
D_{i}
.

The following statement, based on the Banach fixed point Theorem, provides explicit conditions under which
T
has a unique fixed point in
\bbox(\bar{x},r)
(for some explicit
r
). Many similar versions of this theorem have been used in the last decades for computer-assisted proofs, see e.g.
[
3
,
17
,
21
,
28
,
30
,
44
,
50
]
. The one we use here, with possibly different radii in each component, originates from
[
40
]
.

Theorem 2.12
.

Assume that
Y^{m}\geq 0
,
Z^{m}_{i}\geq 0
,
W^{m}_{ij}\geq 0
for
1\leq i,j,m\leq M
satisfy

\displaystyle\left\|\pi^{m}(T(\bar{x})-\bar{x})\right\|_{X^{m}}

\displaystyle\leq Y^{m},

(14)

\displaystyle\left\|\pi^{m}D_{i}T(\bar{x})\right\|_{B(X^{i},X^{m})}

\displaystyle\leq Z^{m}_{i},

(15)

\displaystyle\left\|\pi^{m}(D_{i}T(x)-D_{i}T(\bar{x}))\right\|_{B(X^{i},X^{m})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}\left\|\pi^{j}(x-\bar{x})\right\|_{X^{j}}

\displaystyle\text{for all }x\in\bbox(\bar{x},r_{*}).

(16)

If
r,\eta\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{M}
with
r\leq r_{*}
satisfy

\displaystyle Y^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z^{m}_{i}r^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}r^{i}r^{j}

\displaystyle\leq r^{m}

(17)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z^{m}_{i}\eta^{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}\eta^{i}r^{j}

\displaystyle<\eta^{m}

(18)

for
1\leq m\leq M
,
then
T
has a unique fixed point in
\bbox(\bar{x},r)
.

Proof.

Writing

\displaystyle T(x)-\bar{x}=T(\bar{x})-\bar{x}+DT(\bar{x})(x-\bar{x})+\int_{0}^{1}\left[DT\left(\bar{x}+s(x-\bar{x})\right)-DT\left(\bar{x}\right)\right](x-\bar{x})\,\mbox{d}s,

the inequalities (
17
) imply that
T
maps
\bbox(\bar{x},r)
into itself. Then, writing

\displaystyle DT(x)=DT(\bar{x})+\left[DT\left(x\right)-DT\left(\bar{x}\right)\right],

and considering the weighted maximum norm

\left\|x\right\|_{X}=\max_{1\leq m\leq M}\frac{\left\|\pi^{m}x\right\|_{X^{m}}}{\eta^{m}},

(19)

we get, for all
x
in
\bbox(\bar{x},r_{*})
,

\displaystyle\left\|DT(x)\right\|_{B(X,X)}\leq\max_{1\leq m\leq M}\frac{1}{\eta^{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\biggl(Z^{m}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}r^{j}\biggr)\eta^{i},

and (
18
) yields that
T
is a contraction on

\bbox(\bar{x},r)\subset X
(which is a closed set for
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{X}
).
∎

Remark 2.13
.

If
M=1
, we recover a more
classical
form of this statement, since
\eta
can be factored out in (
18
) and therefore plays no role. In that case,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{X}=\left\|\cdot\right\|_{X^{1}}
, and the set
\bbox(\bar{x},r)
is simply a ball for this norm.

There are various ways one could get similar but arguably simpler versions of Theorem
2.12
. For example, by imposing
\eta=r
, i.e. replacing the conditions (
17
) and (
18
) by

\displaystyle Y^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z^{m}_{i}r^{i}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}r^{i}r^{j}

\displaystyle\leq r^{m},

(20)

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z^{m}_{i}r^{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}r^{i}r^{j}

\displaystyle<r^{m},

(21)

for
1\leq m\leq M
, or by simply asking that

\displaystyle Y^{m}+\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z^{m}_{i}r^{i}+\sum_{i,j=1}^{M}W^{m}_{ij}r^{i}r^{j}

\displaystyle\leq r^{m},

(22)

for
1\leq m\leq M
, which then implies (
20
) and (
21
). The downside of these simplifications is that they lead to conditions on the constants
Y^{m}
,
Z^{m}_{i}
and
W^{m}_{i,j}
that are more stringent than (
17
)-(
18
).

For many computer-assisted proofs, most of the effort goes into actually getting as sharp as possible constants
Y^{m}
,
Z^{m}_{i}
and
W^{m}_{i,j}
satisfying (
14
)-(
16
), and this work is no exception. Once these constants are obtained, we feel that it is somewhat wasteful to use simpler but more stringent forms of Theorem
2.12
. When the proof is not “tight”, it is likely that (
17
)-(
18
), (
20
)-(
21
) and (
22
) can all be satisfied. However, for the examples presented in Theorem
7.1
, Theorem
7.3
and Theorem
7.6
, for which we did successfully verify (
17
)-(
18
), there is no
r
such that either (
22
) or even (
20
)-(
21
) holds, which means the proof would have failed if we had tried to use these simplified version of Theorem
2.12
.

In the sequel, we use this theorem with
M=1
in the case where the time interval
[0,2\tau]
is not split, and with
M>1
when using domain decomposition. In Section
3
, we introduce in full detail the setup in which we apply this theorem to validate solutions of (
1
). The remainder of the paper is then devoted to the derivation of (computable) bounds
Y^{m}
,
Z^{m}_{i}
and
W^{m}_{ij}
satisfying (
14
), (
15
) and (
16
) for this problem.

3
Functional analytic setup

In this section we construct a fixed point map whose fixed points correspond to solutions of (
1
). Here we consider a
single
time domain. The generalization to domain decomposition is presented in Section
6
.
First, we
recast (
1
) into an infinite set of coupled ODEs on the sequence space
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
by using a Fourier transformation
in the spatial variable. We then reformulate the system of ODEs as an equivalent zero finding problem on
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
by using the variation of
constants formula. Next, we perform a finite dimensional reduction
by approximating a finite number of (time varying) Fourier modes with the aid of Chebyshev interpolation. This reduction is
used to set up a Newton-like map
T
based at an approximate (numerically computed) zero, which is the one on which Theorem
2.12
will be applied.

3.1
An equivalent zero-finding problem

In this section we set up a zero finding problem for (
1
) by using the variation of constants formula.
First, rewrite (
1
) as

\displaystyle\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}-\mathcal{L}u

\displaystyle=(-1)^{R+1}\dfrac{\partial^{2R}u}{\partial x^{2R}}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\dfrac{\partial^{j}g^{(j)}(u)}{\partial x^{j}}-\mathcal{L}u,

(23)

where
\mathcal{L}
is the linear operator which is going to be the generator of the semigroup used in the Duhamel formula. The choice of
\mathcal{L}
is actually crucial in our approach: we want
\mathcal{L}
to make the linearization of the right-hand side of (
23
) around the approximation solution as a small as possible, but we also want to keep
\mathcal{L}
relatively simple, so that we can easily get explicit estimates on the associated semigroup. The precise definition of
\mathcal{L}
is given in Section
3.4
.

Next, let
\left(u_{n}\left(t\right)\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
and
\left(f_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
denote the Fourier coefficients of
u\left(t,\cdot\right)
and
f
, respectively, that is

\displaystyle u(t,x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}u_{n}(t)e^{inx},\quad f(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}f_{n}e^{inx},

for all
t\in[0,2\tau]
and
x\in\left[0,2\pi\right]
. Slightly abusing the notations, we also use
(g^{(j)}_{n}(u)\left(t\right))_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
to denote the Fourier coefficients of
g^{(j)}(u)\left(t,\cdot\right)
, i.e.

\displaystyle g^{(j)}(u)(t,x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}g^{(j)}_{n}(u)(t)e^{inx},

for all
j=0,\ldots,2R-1
.

Since the approximation of the solutions will be made using Chebyshev interpolation in time, we rescale the time domain
[0,2\tau]
to
[-1,1]
, on which the theory of Chebyshev approximations is developed (see Section
2.2
).
Substitution of the above Fourier expansions into (
23
) yields an infinite dimensional system of ODEs on
[-1,1]
for
the Fourier coefficients
\left(u_{n}\right)_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
:

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\dfrac{du}{dt}(t)-\tau\mathcal{L}u(t)=\tau\biggl((-1)^{R+1}\mathcal{D}^{2R}u(t)+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\mathcal{D}^{j}g^{(j)}(u(t))-\mathcal{L}u(t)\biggr),&t\in[-1,1],\\[8.61108pt]
u\left(-1\right)=f,\end{cases}

(24)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the Fourier transform of
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}
:

\left(\mathcal{D}u\right)_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,inu_{n},

and where we abuse notation and denote the Fourier transform of
\mathcal{L}
still by
\mathcal{L}
.

Finally, integration of (
24
) with the aid of variation of constants yields the following map.

Definition 3.1
.

The zero finding map
F:\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
for (
1
) is defined by

\displaystyle F\left(u\right)\left(t\right)

\displaystyle\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,e^{\tau(t+1)\mathcal{L}}f+\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}}\gamma(u(s))\mbox{d}s-u(t),

where, for any
v\in\ell^{1}_{\nu}
,

\gamma(v)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,(-1)^{R+1}\mathcal{D}^{2R}v+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\mathcal{D}^{j}g^{(j)}(v)-\mathcal{L}v.

(25)

Remark 3.2
.

While we have not defined
\mathcal{L}
precisely yet (this will be done in Section
3.4
), we are assuming that
\mathcal{L}
generates a
C^{0}
semi-group, with smoothing properties so that
F
is well defined, even if

\gamma
does not necessarily map
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
to itself.

3.2
Finite dimensional reduction

In this section we introduce a finite dimensional reduction of
F
.
To accomplish this we will need to truncate the phase space

\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
and to discretize time.

Definition 3.3

(Truncation of phase space)
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
be a truncation parameter.
The projection
\Pi_{N}:\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
is defined by

\displaystyle\left(\Pi_{N}(u)\right)_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\begin{cases}u_{n},&0\leq|n|\leq N,\\
0,&|n|>N.\end{cases}

Furthermore, we set
\Pi_{\infty}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,I-\Pi_{N}
, where
I
is the identity on
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
. We also introduce the subspaces
\mathcal{X}^{N}_{\nu}=\Pi_{N}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right)
and
\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}=\Pi_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right)
, for which we have

\mathcal{X}_{\nu}=\mathcal{X}^{N}_{\nu}\oplus\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}.

Remark 3.4
.

Henceforth we shall identify
\Pi_{N}\left(u\right)
with the vector of functions

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}u_{-N}\\
\vdots\\
u_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in C\left(\left[-1,1\right],\mathbb{C}^{2N+1}\right).

Definition 3.5

(Time discretization)
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}
.
The Fourier-Chebyshev projection
\Pi_{KN}:\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\rightarrow C\left(\left[-1,1\right],\mathbb{C}^{2N+1}\right)
is defined by

\displaystyle\Pi_{KN}(u)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\begin{bmatrix}P_{K}(u_{-N})\\
\vdots\\
P_{K}(u_{N})\end{bmatrix},

where
P_{K}:C\left([-1,1],\mathbb{C}\right)\rightarrow C\left([-1,1],\mathbb{C}\right)
is the operator which sends a continuous function to its Chebyshev interpolant
(see Section
2.2
). We sometimes abuse notation by applying
P_{K}
to a vector of functions, meaning we apply
P_{K}
to each component, e.g.
\Pi_{KN}(u)=P_{K}\Pi_{N}(u)
. Furthermore, we set
\Pi_{\infty N}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Pi_{N}-\Pi_{KN}
. We note that
\Pi_{\infty N}(u)
vanishes at
t=\pm 1
.

The truncation of phase space and discretization of time give rise to the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{\nu}=\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\oplus\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}\oplus\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu},

(26)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Pi_{KN}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right),\quad\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Pi_{\infty N}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right),\quad\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Pi_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right).

We equip
\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}
with the norm

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Biggl\|\biggl[\left|u_{n0}\right|+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|u_{nk}\right|\biggr]_{n=-N}^{N}\Biggr\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}=\sum_{|n|\leq N}\biggl(\left|u_{n0}\right|+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|u_{nk}\right|\biggr)\nu^{|n|}.

(27)

There are several reasons for choosing this particular
norm over the more obvious supremum norm.
First of all, the norm in (
27
) is numerically easy to compute, whereas the
computation of a supremum norm is relatively complicated. Furthermore, with this norm it is easy to compute
operator norms which amounts to computing weighted
\ell^{1}
-norms of finite dimensional matrices.
This is to be contrasted with the use of a supremum norm, where the analysis of linear operators is much more complicated.
Lastly, it follows from (
12
) that the norm in (
27
) is stronger than the supremum norm, i.e.,

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}\left\|u(t)\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}\leq\left\|\|u\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}\leq\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}},\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu},

thereby allowing one to relate the two norms in a straightforward manner. Here and throughout the paper, when we apply the
C^{0}
-norm to an element of
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
, it should be understood as applying the
C^{0}
-norm to each Fourier mode.

The subspaces
\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}

are both endowed with the
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}\left(C^{0}\right)}=\left\|\left\|\cdot\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}
norm, i.e.,

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}\left(C^{0}\right)}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\left\|u_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|}.

Furthermore, the full space
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
is equipped with the norm

\displaystyle\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left\|\Pi_{KN}u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}+\epsilon_{\infty N}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}+\epsilon_{\infty}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty}u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}

(28)

where
\epsilon_{\infty N},\epsilon_{\infty}>0
are weights whose purpose is to provide some control over the truncation errors in phase space and the interpolation errors in time. The choice of these weights is different for each example in Section
7
(the specific values can be found in the code). Over the whole space, the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}
still controls the
C^{0}
norm:

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\vartheta_{\epsilon}=\max(1,\epsilon_{\infty N},\epsilon_{\infty})
. Then,

\left\|u\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}\left(C^{0}\right)}\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}\left\|u\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}},\quad\text{for all }u\in\mathcal{X}_{\nu}.

Finally, we define a finite dimensional reduction of
F
.

Definition 3.7

(Finite dimensional reduction of
F
)
.

The finite dimensional reduction
F_{KN}:\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}
of
F

is defined by

F_{KN}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Pi_{KN}\circ F|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}
.

3.3
A posteriori analysis

In this section we construct a Newton-like map for
F
by using the finite dimensional
reduction
F_{KN}
. To this end, suppose we have computed the following:

(i)

An approximate zero
\bar{u}
in
\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\simeq\mathbb{C}^{(2N+1)\left(K+1\right)}
of
F_{KN}
.

(ii)

The derivative
DF_{KN}\left(\bar{u}\right)
.

(iii)

An approximate
injective
inverse
A_{KN}
of
DF_{KN}\left(\bar{u}\right)
.

Remark 3.8
.

One can check that
A_{KN}
is injective by verifying that the bound in (
37
) is strictly smaller than
1
. In fact, our computer-assisted proof can only be successful when this
inequality is satisfied, cf. (
18
). Therefore, if the computer-assisted
proof is successful, we may a posteriori conclude that
A_{KN}
is injective without any further ado.

We will use the finite dimensional data to construct an approximate inverse of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
.
We anticipate that

\displaystyle\Pi_{\infty N}DF\left(\bar{u}\right)\approx-\Pi_{\infty N}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\Pi_{\infty}DF\left(\bar{u}\right)\approx-\Pi_{\infty}

in a small neighborhood of
\bar{u}
provided
K
and
N
are sufficiently large. These observations motivate the following
definitions:

Definition 3.9

(Approximation of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
)
.

The approximate derivative
\widehat{DF}:\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
of
F
at
\bar{u}
is defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{DF}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,DF_{KN}\left(\bar{u}\right)\oplus\left(-\Pi_{\infty N}\right)\oplus\left(-\Pi_{\infty}\right).

Definition 3.10

(Approximate inverse of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
)
.

The approximate inverse
A:\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
is defined by

\displaystyle A\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,A_{KN}\oplus\left(-\Pi_{\infty N}\right)\oplus\left(-\Pi_{\infty}\right).

Next, we define a Newton-like operator
T
for
F
based at
\bar{u}
:

Definition 3.11

(Newton-like operator for
F
)
.

The Newton-like operator
T:\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
for
F
based at
\bar{u}
is defined by

\displaystyle T\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,I-AF.

The idea is to seek fixed points of
T
in a small neighborhood of
\bar{u}
. To be more precise, let
B_{r,\epsilon}(0)

denote the
closed
ball of radius
r>0
centered at
0
in
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle B_{r,\epsilon}(0)=\left\{h\in\mathcal{X}_{\nu}:\left\|\Pi_{KN}\left(h\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}+\epsilon_{\infty N}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}\left(h\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}+\epsilon_{\infty}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\left(h\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}\leq r\right\}.

(29)

We shall prove the existence of a fixed point
u^{*}
of
T
in
B_{r,\epsilon}\left(\bar{u}\right)=\bar{u}+B_{r,\epsilon}(0)
, where
r>0
is an unknown radius to be determined, by using Theorem
2.12
, with
M=1
and
X=\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
.

Remark 3.12
.

This fixed point
u^{*}
of
T
corresponds to a zero of
F
, since
A
is injective. We have thus found the solution of the initial value problem (
1
).
In view of Lemma
3.6
, the error bound on
the distance between the solution
u^{*}
and its approximation
\bar{u}
is controlled by

\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[0,2\tau],x\in[0,2\pi]\end{subarray}}|u^{*}(t,x)-\bar{u}(t,x)|\leq\|u^{*}-\bar{u}\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}r.

More precisely, the solution obtained via the computer assisted argument is a mild solution, continuous in time with values in
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
, and it is well known that parabolic problems of the form (
1
) have a unique mild solution which is in fact a classical solution
[
25
,
31
]
. However, the maximal existence time
t_{\text{max}}
of this unique solution could in principle be smaller than
2\tau
. Our proof additionally shows the maximal existence time
t_{\text{max}}
is in fact larger than
2\tau
, and provides more detailed information on the regularity of the solution (for
\nu>1
), namely that is it analytic in the space variable on a strip of width
\ln\nu
.

3.4
Construction of
\mathcal{L}

For reasons already outlined in the introduction, and that will be made more concrete later on, we want
\mathcal{L}
to be a time
independent

approximation of

\displaystyle h\mapsto(-1)^{(R+1)}\mathcal{D}^{2R}h+\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\mathcal{D}^{j}\left(\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u}(s))\ast h\right),\quad h\in\ell^{1}_{\nu},

(30)

where
\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}
simply denotes the derivative of the polynomial function
g^{(j)}
.
Although we choose
\mathcal{L}
to be uniform in
s
, it does approximate the linear operator (
30
),
hence we have that, with
\gamma
defined in (
25
),

s\mapsto D\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\approx 0.

To construct
\mathcal{L}
, we start by introducing vectors
\bar{v}^{(j)}\in\Pi^{N}\left(\ell^{1}_{\nu}\right)
,
j=0,\ldots,2R-1
, which in practice should be (constant in time) approximations of
s\mapsto\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})(s)
. Then, we define
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
on
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}=\Pi_{N}\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\oplus\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\Pi_{N}u\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,(-1)^{R+1}\mathcal{D}^{2R}\Pi_{N}u+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\mathcal{D}^{j}\Pi_{N}\bigl(\bar{v}^{(j)}\ast\Pi_{N}u\bigr)\\
&\left(\tilde{\mathcal{L}}\Pi_{\infty}u\right)_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\biggl(-n^{2R}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}(in)^{j}\bar{v}^{(j)}_{0}\biggr)u_{n}\qquad\text{for all }|n|>N.\end{aligned}\right.

Notice that
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
leaves
\mathcal{X}^{N}_{\nu}
invariant, and that it does not depend on time, therefore its restriction to
\mathcal{X}^{N}_{\nu}
can be represented as a finite dimensional matrix, that we denote by
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}
.

Finally, we consider a diagonal matrix
\Lambda_{N}=\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{-N},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\right)
and an invertible matrix
Q
such that

\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}\approx Q\Lambda_{N}Q^{-1},

and define

\mathcal{L}_{N}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,Q\Lambda_{N}Q^{-1},

and
\mathcal{L}
on
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}=\Pi_{N}\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\oplus\Pi_{\infty}\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
by

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\mathcal{L}\Pi_{N}u\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\mathcal{L}_{N}\Pi_{N}u\\
&\left(\mathcal{L}\Pi_{\infty}u\right)_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\biggl(-n^{2R}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}(in)^{j}\bar{v}^{(j)}_{0}\biggr)u_{n}\quad\text{for all }|n|>N.\end{aligned}\right.

For further use, we denote, for all
|n|>N
,

\lambda_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,-n^{2R}+\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}(in)^{j}\bar{v}^{(j)}_{0},

(31)

and

\mathcal{R}_{N}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\mathcal{L}_{N}-\tilde{\mathcal{L}}_{N}.

Notice that with this notation we can write

\Pi_{N}D\gamma(\bar{u})u=\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\mathcal{D}^{j}\Pi_{N}\left[\left(\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})-\bar{v}^{(j)}\right)\ast\Pi_{N}u+\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})\ast\Pi_{\infty}u\right]-\mathcal{R}_{N}\Pi_{N}u.

(32)

Now that
A
and
\mathcal{L}
have been explicitly constructed, we are ready to derive the estimates needed to prove that
T
has a locally unique fixed point around
\bar{u}
.

4
General estimates

We compile here a list of lemmas which will be used extensively in the computation of the
Y
,
Z
and
W
bounds needed for Theorem
2.12
. These are essentially technical estimates, the reader more interested in an overview of the proof is encouraged to jump directly to Section
5
, and to only refer to this section when needed.

4.1
An
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
dual estimate

Lemma 4.1
.

For a given
(2N+1)\times(2N+1)
matrix
B
with complex entries, define
\Upsilon(B)\in\mathbb{C}^{2N+1}
by

\Upsilon_{n}(B)=\max_{|m|\leq N}\frac{|B_{n,m}|}{\nu^{|m|}},\quad|n|\leq N.

Then, for any
v\in\Pi_{N}\ell^{1}_{\nu}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2N+1}
,

\displaystyle\left|Bv\right|\leq\Upsilon(B)\left\|v\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}},

where the absolute values apply component-wise.

4.2
Controlling convolution products

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
a,b,h\in\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
. Define, for all
n
in
\mathbb{Z}
,

\Phi^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\max\biggl\{\epsilon_{\infty N}\max_{|n-m|\leq N}\frac{\left\|a_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}},\ \epsilon_{\infty}\sup_{|n-m|>N}\frac{\left\|b_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}}\biggr\},

\check{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\max\biggl\{\max(1,\epsilon_{\infty N})\max_{|n-m|\leq N}\frac{\left\|a_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}},\ \epsilon_{\infty}\sup_{|n-m|>N}\frac{\left\|b_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}}\biggr\},

and

\displaystyle\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}(a,b)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\max\biggl\{

\displaystyle\max(1,\epsilon_{\infty N})\max_{|m|\leq N}\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left\|a_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|-|m|},

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\epsilon_{\infty}\sup_{|m|>N}\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left\|b_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|-|m|}\biggr\}.

Then, for all
n
in
\mathbb{Z}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(a\ast\Pi_{\infty N}h+b\ast\Pi_{\infty}h\right)_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\Phi^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)\left\|h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}},

\displaystyle\left\|\left(a\ast\Pi_{N}h+b\ast\Pi_{\infty}h\right)_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\check{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)\left\|h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}},

and

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{N}\left[a\ast\Pi_{N}h+b\ast\Pi_{\infty}h\right]\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}(a,b)\left\|h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}.

Proof.

Let
h^{\infty N}=\Pi_{\infty N}h
and
h^{\infty}=\Pi_{\infty}h
. For all
s\in[-1,1]
, by using (
28
),

\displaystyle\left|\left(a\ast h^{\infty N}+b\ast h^{\infty}\right)_{n}(s)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\left|a_{m}(s)h^{\infty N}_{n-m}(s)\right|+\left|b_{m}(s)h^{\infty}_{n-m}(s)\right|\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|n-m|\leq N}\frac{\left\|a_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}}\left\|h^{\infty N}_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n-m|}

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{|n-m|>N}\frac{\left\|b_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}}\left\|h^{\infty}_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n-m|}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{|n-m|\leq N}\frac{\left\|a_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}}\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}+\sup_{|n-m|>N}\frac{\left\|b_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\nu^{|n-m|}}\left\|\Pi_{\infty}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)\left\|h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}.

The second estimate is obtained in the same way, using additionally that

\displaystyle\sum_{|m|\leq N}\left\|h_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|m|}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Pi_{KN}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}+\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\leq\max(1,\epsilon_{\infty N})\left(\left\|\Pi_{KN}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}+\epsilon_{\infty N}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}\right).

For the final estimate, introducing
h^{N}=\Pi_{N}h
, we proceed in a similar fashion:

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{N}\left[a\ast h^{N}+b\ast h^{\infty}\right]\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|n|\leq N}\sum_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\left(\left\|a_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\left\|h^{N}_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}+\left\|b_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\left\|h^{\infty}_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right)\nu^{|n|}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{|m|\leq N}\left\|h_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|m|}\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left\|a_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|-|m|}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum_{|m|>N}\left\|h_{m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|m|}\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left\|b_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|-|m|}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\left\|\Pi_{KN}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}+\epsilon_{\infty N}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\times\max(1,\epsilon_{\infty N})\max_{|m|\leq N}\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left\|a_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|-|m|}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad+\epsilon_{\infty}^{-1}\left\|\Pi_{\infty}h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}\,\epsilon_{\infty}\sup_{|m|>N}\sum_{|n|\leq N}\left\|b_{n-m}\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{|n|-|m|}.\qed

Remark 4.3
.

As soon as
b
only has a finite number of non-zero Fourier modes, the suprema in
\Phi^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)
,
\check{\Phi}^{\epsilon}_{n}(a,b)
and
\tilde{\Phi}^{\epsilon}(a,b)
become maxima that can be computed.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
r\geq 0
and
p
be a polynomial, written in the canonical basis

p(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{N_{p}}p_{k}x^{k}.

If we denote by
|p|
the polynomial

|p|(x)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\sum_{k=0}^{N_{p}}|p_{k}|x^{k},

then for all
\|v\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\leq 1
and
\|h\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\leq 1
we have

\left\|p\left(\bar{u}+rv\right)\ast v\ast h\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}^{2}\,|p|\left(\left\|\bar{u}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}+\vartheta_{\epsilon}r\right).

Proof.

We use the Banach algebra properties of
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
and
\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})
to get

\displaystyle\left\|p\left(\bar{u}+rv\right)\ast v\ast h\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|p\left(\bar{u}+rv\right)\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\|v\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\|h\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}

\displaystyle\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{p}}|p_{k}|\left\|\bigl(\left\|\bar{u}\right\|_{C^{0}}+r\left\|v\right\|_{C^{0}}\bigr)^{k}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{p}}|p_{k}|\left\|\bigl(\left\|\bar{u}\right\|_{C^{0}}+r\left\|v\right\|_{C^{0}}\bigr)\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}^{k}

\displaystyle\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}^{2}\sum_{k=0}^{N_{p}}|p_{k}|\bigl(\left\|\bar{u}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}+\vartheta_{\epsilon}r\bigr)^{k}.\qed

4.3
Interpolation errors

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\tau>0
and
\rho>1
,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{\tau(t+1)\lambda}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\rho^{-K}}{\rho-1}\exp\left(\tau\Bigl(\Re(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda)^{2}(\rho+\rho^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda)^{2}(\rho-\rho^{-1})^{2}}\Bigr)\right),

where
\Re(\lambda)
and
\Im(\lambda)
denote the real part and the imaginary part of
\lambda
, respectively.

Proof.

From Theorem
2.7
we get that

\displaystyle\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{\tau(t+1)\lambda}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{4\rho^{-K}}{\rho-1}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}e^{\tau\Re\left(\lambda(z+1)\right)}.

Any
z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
can be written
z=\frac{1}{2}\left(re^{i\theta}+r^{-1}e^{-i\theta}\right)
, for some
(r,\theta)\in[1,\rho]\times[0,2\pi]
, and we then have

\displaystyle\Re\left(\lambda\left(z+1\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\Re(\lambda)\left(1+\frac{1}{2}(r+r^{-1})\cos\theta\right)-\Im(\lambda)\frac{1}{2}(r-r^{-1})\sin\theta

\displaystyle=\Re(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda)^{2}(r+r^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda)^{2}(r-r^{-1})^{2}}\cos(\theta-\theta_{r,\lambda}),

for some
\theta_{r,\lambda}\in[0,2\pi]
, hence

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\Re\left(\lambda(z+1)\right)=\Re(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda)^{2}(\rho+\rho^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda)^{2}(\rho-\rho^{-1})^{2}}.\hfill\qed

Remark 4.6
.

For a given
\lambda
, the quality of the above estimate depends on the choice of
\rho
, therefore in practice we approximately optimize over
\rho
.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
\varphi_{N}\in C^{0}\left([-1,1],\mathbb{C}^{2N+1}\right)
, seen as an element of
\Pi_{N}\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau>0
,
\sigma_{K,0}>0
satisfying Definition
2.5
, and consider the
(2N+1)\times(2N+1)
diagonal matrix

D_{N}(t)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\Re(\Lambda_{N})^{-1}\left(e^{\tau(1+t)\Re(\Lambda_{N})}-I_{2N+1}\right)=\tau\int_{-1}^{t}\left|e^{\tau(t-s)\Lambda_{N}}\right|\mbox{d}s,

(33)

where
\Re
and
|\cdot|
applied to matrices must be understood component-wise. Let
\mathcal{D}_{N}=\Pi_{N}\mathcal{D}\Pi_{N}
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|(I-P_{K})\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\tau\sigma_{K,0}\left(\left\||\mathcal{D}_{N}^{j}|+|Q||\Lambda_{N}|D_{N}(1)|Q^{-1}||\mathcal{D}_{N}^{j}|\right\|_{B(\ell^{1}_{\nu},\ell^{1}_{\nu})}\right)\left\|\varphi_{N}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}\left(C^{0}\right)}.

Proof.

Using Definition
2.5
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|(I-P_{K})\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\left\|\left\|(I-P_{K})\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\tau\sigma_{K,0}\left\|\left\|\frac{d}{dt}\left(t\mapsto\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\tau\sigma_{K,0}\left\|\left\|\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(t)+\tau\mathcal{L}_{N}\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(s)\mbox{d}s\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad=\tau\sigma_{K,0}\left\|\left\|\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(t)+\tau Q\Lambda_{N}\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\Lambda_{N}}Q^{-1}\mathcal{D}^{j}\varphi_{N}(s)\mbox{d}s\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\tau\sigma_{K,0}\left\||\mathcal{D}_{N}^{j}|\left\|\varphi_{N}\right\|_{C^{0}}+|Q||\Lambda_{N}|D_{N}(1)|Q^{-1}||\mathcal{D}_{N}^{j}|\left\|\varphi_{N}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}.\qed

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\varphi:[-1,1]\to\mathbb{C}
be an infinitely differentiable function,
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
\tau>0
,
q\in\mathbb{N}
and

\tilde{C}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\sigma_{K,q}\left(\left|\tau\lambda\right|^{q+1}\frac{e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda)}-1}{\Re(\lambda)}\left\|\varphi\right\|_{C^{0}}+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{q}\left|\tau\lambda\right|^{i}\left\|\varphi^{(q-i)}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right),

where
\varphi^{(i)}
is the
i
-th derivative of
\varphi
and
\sigma_{K,q}
satisfies Definition
2.5
. Then

\displaystyle\left\|(I-P_{K})\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\lambda}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\tilde{C}.

Assume further that
\varphi
can be analytically extended onto
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
for some
\rho>1
, and let

\displaystyle\zeta(x)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left\{\begin{aligned} &\dfrac{e^{x}-1}{x}\qquad&x\neq 0,\\
&1\qquad&x=0,\end{aligned}\right.

and

\displaystyle\check{C}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\frac{2\tau(\rho+\rho^{-1}+2)}{\rho^{K}(\rho-1)}\zeta\left(\tau\left(\Re(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda)^{2}(\rho+\rho^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda)^{2}(\rho-\rho^{-1})^{2}}\right)\right)\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|\varphi(z)\right|.

Then

\displaystyle\left\|(I-P_{K})\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\lambda}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\check{C}.

Proof.

The first estimate is a direct consequence of the interpolation error estimate described in Definition
2.5
, and of the fact that

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{d^{q+1}}{dt^{q+1}}\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\lambda}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\leq\left(\left|\tau\lambda\right|^{q+1}\frac{e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda)}-1}{\Re(\lambda)}\left\|\varphi\right\|_{C^{0}}+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{q}\left|\tau\lambda\right|^{i}\left\|\varphi^{(q-i)}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right).

The second estimate follows from Theorem
2.7
. More precisely, for any
z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
we consider
\gamma(\tilde{s})=-1+\tilde{s}(z+1)
,
\tilde{s}\in[0,1]
, so that
\gamma
parametrizes the segment
[-1,z]
, and we estimate

\displaystyle\tau\left|\int_{\gamma}e^{\tau(z-y)\lambda}\varphi(y)\mbox{d}y\right|

\displaystyle\leq\tau\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}|\varphi(z)|\right)\left(\frac{\rho+\rho^{-1}}{2}+1\right)\int_{0}^{1}\left|e^{\tau(z+1)(1-s)\lambda}\right|\text{d}s

\displaystyle=\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}|\varphi(z)|\right)\left(\frac{\rho+\rho^{-1}}{2}+1\right)\left(\frac{e^{\tau\Re((z+1)\lambda)}-1}{\Re((z+1)\lambda)}\right).

Since the map
\zeta
is increasing on
\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\tau\left|\int_{\gamma}e^{\tau(z-y)\lambda}\varphi(y)\mbox{d}y\right|\leq\left(\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}|\varphi(z)|\right)\left(\frac{\rho+\rho^{-1}}{2}+1\right)\tau\zeta\left(\tau\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\Re((z+1)\lambda)\right),

where, as already established in the proof of Lemma
4.5
,

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\Re((z+1)\lambda)=\Re(\lambda)+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda)^{2}(\rho+\rho^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda)^{2}(\rho-\rho^{-1})^{2}}.

From Theorem 2.7, we get

\displaystyle\left\|(I-P_{K})\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\lambda}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\frac{4}{\rho^{K}(\rho-1)}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\tau\left|\int_{\gamma}e^{\tau(z-y)\lambda}\varphi(y)\mbox{d}y\right|,

which finishes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Assume we need to control an interpolation error of the form
(I-P_{K})g
, where the function
g
is known, and one of the above lemmas is applicable. Rather than directly using the appropriate lemma, one may get a sharper estimate by first splitting the error as follows

\displaystyle\left\|(I-P_{K})g\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\left\|(P_{K_{0}}-P_{K})g\right\|_{C^{0}}+\left\|(I-P_{K_{0}})g\right\|_{C^{0}},

where
K_{0}
is larger than
K
. The rational behind this splitting is that, by taking
K_{0}
large enough,
P_{K_{0}}g
becomes an extremely accurate approximation of
g
, and the first term
\left\|(P_{K_{0}}-P_{K})g\right\|_{C^{0}}
is essentially the interpolation error one is interested in. The main difference is now that
(P_{K_{0}}-P_{K})g
is merely a polynomial, hence its
C^{0}
norm can very easily be estimated via (
12
), and we only have to use one of the above lemmas to control the second term
\left\|(I-P_{K_{0}})g\right\|_{C^{0}}
, which is expected to be much smaller since
K_{0}
is larger than
K
.

4.4
Truncation errors in Fourier space

Lemma 4.10

(Truncation-error)
.

Define the linear operators
K^{(j)}_{\infty}:\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}
,
j=0,\ldots,2R-1
, by

\displaystyle\left[K^{(j)}_{\infty}\left(u\right)\left(t\right)\right]_{n}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\begin{cases}0,&0\leq|n|\leq N,\\[8.61108pt]
\displaystyle\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau\lambda_{n}\left(t-s\right)}(in)^{j}u_{n}\left(s\right)\mbox{d}s,&|n|>N.\end{cases}

(34)

Then
K^{(j)}_{\infty}
is bounded and

\displaystyle\left\|K^{(j)}_{\infty}\right\|_{\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu},\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}\right)}\leq\chi^{(j)}_{N}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\sup_{n>N}n^{j}\frac{1-e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}.

Proof.

Let
u\in\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}
. Then, using that
\Re(\lambda_{n})=\Re(\lambda_{-n})
for all
|n|>N
,

\displaystyle\left\|K^{(j)}_{\infty}\left(u\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\leq\tau\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\left(\left\|u_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}+\left\|u_{-n}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right)n^{j}\left\|t\mapsto\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})\left(t-s\right)}\mbox{d}s\right\|_{C^{0}}\nu^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\left(\left\|u_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}+\left\|u_{-n}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right)n^{j}\frac{1-e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}\nu^{n}

\displaystyle\leq\chi^{(j)}_{N}\left\|u\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}.\qed

Remark 4.11
.

Note that

\chi_{N}^{(j)}\sim N^{-2R+j}
as
N\to\infty
, by (
31
). An easy way to get an explicit value for
\chi_{N}^{(j)}
is given in Appendix
D
.

Bounds

In this section, we derive computable bounds
Y
,
Z
and
W
satisfying assumptions (
14
)-(
16
) of Theorem
2.12
, for the operator
T
, the space
X=\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
and the approximate solution
\bar{u}
introduced in Section
3
. Since we use here the case
M=1
, we drop the indices
i,j
and the exponent
m
from
Y
,
Z
and

.

5.1
Y-bound

In this subsection, we deal with the residual bound
Y
satisfying assumption (
14
) of Theorem
2.12
. First, observe that

\displaystyle T(\bar{u})-\bar{u}

\displaystyle=-AF(\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\left(-A_{KN}F_{KN}(\bar{u})\right)\oplus\Pi_{\infty N}F(\bar{u})\oplus\Pi_{\infty}F(\bar{u}).

We are going to estimate each of the three terms separately, i.e. we derive bounds
Y_{KN}
,
Y_{\infty N}
and
Y_{\infty}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|A_{KN}F_{KN}(\bar{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}\leq Y_{KN},\quad\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}\leq Y_{\infty N},\quad\left\|\Pi_{\infty}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}\leq Y_{\infty},

so that

\displaystyle Y\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,Y_{KN}+\epsilon_{\infty N}^{-1}Y_{\infty N}+\epsilon_{\infty}^{-1}Y_{\infty},

satisfies (
14
).

5.1.1
Finite dimensional projection

A bound for the finite dimensional part can be obtained by simply evaluating

\displaystyle Y_{KN}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left\|A_{KN}F_{KN}(\bar{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}.

In order to make this bound computable, we need to be able to rigorously enclose each entry of
F_{KN}(\bar{u})
, that is, to get enclosure for the vectors

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})(t_{k})=e^{\tau(t_{k}+1)\mathcal{L}}\Pi_{N}f+\tau\Pi_{N}\left(\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\mathcal{L}}\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\mbox{d}s\right)-\bar{u}\left(t_{k}\right),

for all
0\leq k\leq K
.

Remark 5.1
.

There are at least two convenient ways of representing (each component of) an element
\bar{u}\in\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}
, namely via its values at the Chebyshev nodes, or via its coefficients in the Chebyshev basis. The Chebyshev coefficients representation simplifies all the norm computations, whereas the representation with the values at the nodes is the one we naturally end up with when computing the projection of
\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})
onto
\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}
. In practice, we therefore chose to implement
F_{KN}
as going from Chebyshev coefficients to values at Chebyshev nodes.

Recalling that
\mathcal{L}
leaves both
\mathcal{X}^{N}_{\nu}
and
\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}
invariant, we have

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})(t_{k})=e^{\tau(t_{k}+1)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\Pi_{N}f+\tau\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\Pi_{N}\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\mbox{d}s-\bar{u}\left(t_{k}\right).

Therefore, we need to be able to rigorously enclose integrals of the form

\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s,

where
\varphi(s)\in\mathbb{C}^{2N-1}
and each component of
\varphi
is a polynomial in
s
. Here we use the fact that

\mathcal{L}_{N}=Q\Lambda_{N}Q^{-1},

where
\Lambda_{N}=\text{diag}\left(\lambda_{-N},\ldots,\lambda_{N}\right)
, to write

\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s=Q\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\Lambda_{N}}Q^{-1}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s,

where
Q^{-1}\varphi
is another vector of polynomials, and thus the whole estimate boils down to rigorously enclosing integrals of the form

\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\lambda_{n}}\varphi(s)\mbox{d}s,

where
\varphi
is a scalar polynomial. We explain in detail in Appendix
C
how this can be done.

Remark 5.2
.

We expect to get
Y_{KN}
small provided
\bar{u}
is an accurate approximate solution of the (truncated) problem. In practice, if the obtained value of
Y_{KN}
is not small enough for (
17
) to hold, we should try to increase
K
(or to split the time interval, see Section
6
).

5.1.2
Interpolation error estimates

We now turn our attention to the computation of the
Y_{\infty N}
bound. We need to estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}\left(T(\bar{u})-\bar{u}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle=\left\|\left\|\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{KN}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}},

i.e., to control the interpolation error for
\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})
. Since
\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})
is a quantity that we can compute explicitly, we apply the idea presented in Remark
4.9
, i.e. we consider
K_{0}\geq K
and estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{KN}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leq\left\|\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}+\left\|\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{KN}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}.

We then estimate
\left\|\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{KN}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}
using (
12
), and
\left\|\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}
by combining Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.8
to obtain the following statement.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
K_{0}\geq K
,

\tilde{f}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,Q^{-1}\Pi_{N}f,\quad\tilde{c}^{Y}(s)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,Q^{-1}\Pi_{N}\gamma(\bar{u}(s)).

For each
n\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
|n|\leq N
, consider also
0\leq q_{n}\leq K_{0}
,
\rho_{n},\check{\rho}_{n}>1
,

\displaystyle\Omega_{n}=\frac{4\rho_{n}^{-K_{0}}}{\rho_{n}-1}\exp\left(\tau\left(\Re(\lambda_{n})+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda_{n})^{2}(\rho_{n}+\rho_{n}^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda_{n})^{2}(\rho_{n}-\rho_{n}^{-1})^{2}}\right)\right)|\tilde{f}_{n}|,

\tilde{C}_{n}=\sigma_{K_{0},q_{n}}\left(\left|\tau\lambda_{n}\right|^{q_{n}+1}\frac{e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}-1}{\Re(\lambda_{n})}\left\|\tilde{c}^{Y}_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}+\tau\sum_{i=0}^{q_{n}}\left|\tau\lambda_{n}\right|^{i}\left\|\left(\tilde{c}^{Y}_{n}\right)^{(q_{n}-i)}\right\|_{C^{0}}\right),

and

\displaystyle\check{C}_{n}=

\displaystyle\quad\frac{2\tau(\check{\rho}_{n}+\check{\rho}_{n}^{-1}+2)}{\check{\rho}_{n}^{K_{0}}(\check{\rho}_{n}-1)}\exp\left(\tau\left(\Re(\lambda_{n})+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\Re(\lambda_{n})^{2}(\check{\rho}_{n}+\check{\rho}_{n}^{-1})^{2}+\Im(\lambda_{n})^{2}(\check{\rho}_{n}-\check{\rho}_{n}^{-1})^{2}}\right)\right)\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\check{\rho}_{n}}}\left|\tilde{c}^{Y}_{n}\right|.

Then

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\left\||Q|\left(\Omega+\min\left(\tilde{C},\check{C}\right)\right)\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}},

where
\Omega=\left(\Omega_{n}\right)_{-N\leq n\leq N}
,
\tilde{C}=\left(\tilde{C}_{n}\right)_{-N\leq n\leq N}
,
\check{C}=\left(\check{C}_{n}\right)_{-N\leq n\leq N}
, and
\min\left(\tilde{C},\check{C}\right)
has to be understood component-wise.

Proof.

First note that

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})

\displaystyle=\left(I-P_{K_{0}}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{\tau(t+1)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\Pi_{N}f+\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\Pi_{N}\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\mbox{d}s\right)

\displaystyle=Q\left(I-P_{K_{0}}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{\tau(t+1)\Lambda_{N}}\right)\tilde{f}+Q\left(I-P_{K_{0}}\right)\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\Lambda_{N}}\tilde{c}^{Y}(s)\mbox{d}s\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\Pi_{N}F(\bar{u})-\Pi_{K_{0}N}F(\bar{u})\right|

\displaystyle\leq|Q|\left(\left|\left(I-P_{K_{0}}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{\tau(t+1)\Lambda_{N}}\right)\right|\left|\tilde{f}\right|+\left|\left(I-P_{K_{0}}\right)\left(t\mapsto\tau\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau(t-s)\Lambda_{N}}\tilde{c}^{Y}(s)\mbox{d}s\right)\right|\right),

(35)

where the absolute values have to be understood component-wise. We then apply, component-wise, Lemma
4.5
and Lemma
4.8
to the first and the second term respectively.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

For each component, it is not clear a priori which choice of
q_{n}
leads to the smallest
\tilde{C}_{n}
, so in practice we just compute
\tilde{C}_{n}
for all
q_{n}
between
1
and
K_{0}
and then keep the smallest result. Similarly, we approximately optimize for each
\rho_{n}
and
\check{\rho}_{n}
. Finally, in practice we replace the
C^{0}
norms and the supremum over Bernstein ellipses by the easy-to-evaluate upper-bounds provided in Lemma
2.10
.

5.1.3
Truncation in phase space

Next, we compute a bound for the residual associated to the truncation in phase space. First note that

\Pi_{\infty}\left(T(\bar{u})-\bar{u}\right)=\Pi_{\infty}F(\bar{u}).

Therefore, for each
|n|>N
we have

\displaystyle F_{n}(\bar{u})(t)=e^{\tau\lambda_{n}(t+1)}f_{n}+\tau\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\lambda_{n}\tau\left(t-s\right)}\gamma_{n}(\bar{u}(s))\mbox{d}s,

which we estimate by

\displaystyle\left\|F_{n}\left(\bar{u}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})^{+}}|f_{n}|+\left\|\gamma_{n}(\bar{u})\right\|_{C^{0}}\frac{e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}-1}{\Re(\lambda_{n})},

where
\Re(\lambda_{n})^{+}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\max(\Re(\lambda_{n}),0)
. We then get the
Y_{\infty}
bound by taking the
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
norm of the right-hand side. Notice that
\gamma(\bar{u})
only has finitely many non-zero coefficients, hence this
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
norm amounts to a finite computation. Assuming we have a bound on the
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
norm of the tail of

, this contributes to the
\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}
bound on the tail of
F
after estimating
e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})^{+}}
uniformly for
|n|>N
.

5.2
Z-bounds

In this subsection, we now focus on the bound
Z
satisfying assumption (

) of Theorem
2.12
.
In order to estimate the norm of

, we take an arbitrary

in
B_{1,\epsilon}\left(0\right)
(see (

,

and

such that

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{KN}DT(\bar{u})h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}\leq Z_{KN},\quad\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}DT(\bar{u})h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu}}\leq Z_{\infty N},\quad\left\|\Pi_{\infty}DT(\bar{u})h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}\leq Z_{\infty},

so that

\displaystyle Z\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,Z_{KN}+\epsilon_{\infty N}^{-1}Z_{\infty N}+\epsilon_{\infty}^{-1}Z_{\infty},

(36)

satisfies (

, to rewrite

as

\displaystyle DT\left(\bar{u}\right)h

\displaystyle=\left(I-A\widehat{DF}\right)-A\left(DF\left(\bar{u}\right)-\widehat{DF}\right).

Remark 5.5
.

From (

) (with
M=1
), we see that we must have

in order to apply Theorem
2.12
. When deriving the estimates
Z_{KN}
,
Z_{\infty N}
and
Z_{\infty}
, we are going to see how the choices we made when defining

are instrumental in achieving that goal. A careful choice of the weights
\epsilon_{\infty N}
and
\epsilon_{\infty}
is also helpful in order to make

small. However, unlike what (

,
Z_{\infty N}
and
Z_{\infty}
themselves (as well as the forthcoming bound

5.2.1
Finite dimensional projection

By definition of
A
and
\widehat{DF}
,

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{KN}\left(I-A\widehat{DF}\right)h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|I_{KN}-A_{KN}DF_{KN}\left(\bar{u}\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{B}\left(\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu},\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\right)},

(37)

which is just a finite computation. In order to obtain the bound
Z_{KN}
, the main task is therefore to enclose

\Pi_{KN}\left(DF(\bar{u})-\widehat{DF}\right)h=\Pi_{KN}DF(\bar{u})\left(\Pi_{\infty N}+\Pi_{\infty}\right)(h),

for all
h\in B_{1,\epsilon}(0)
. We then have to multiply the result by
|A_{KN}|
in order to get a bound on

\left\|\Pi_{KN}A\left(DF\left(\bar{u}\right)-\widehat{DF}\right)h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}}.

We compute

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}\left[DF(\bar{u})\left(\Pi_{\infty N}+\Pi_{\infty}\right)(h)\right](t_{k})

\displaystyle=\tau\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\mathcal{L}_{N}}\Pi_{N}D\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\left(\Pi_{\infty N}+\Pi_{\infty}\right)(h)(s)\mbox{d}s

\displaystyle=\tau Q\int_{-1}^{t_{k}}e^{\tau(t_{k}-s)\Lambda_{N}}Q^{-1}\Pi_{N}D\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\left(\Pi_{\infty N}+\Pi_{\infty}\right)(h)(s)\mbox{d}s,

where, according to (
32

\displaystyle\Pi_{N}D\gamma(\bar{u}(s))\left(\Pi_{\infty N}+\Pi_{\infty}\right)(h)(s)=

Remark 5.6
.

A careful inspection of this bound reveals why we can hope it to be small, so that

6
Domain decomposition

In this section, we introduce a generalization of the setup introduced in Section
3
, which is more efficient for handling longer integration times. Indeed, as one would expect, most of the estimates derived in Section
5
get worse when the integration time
\tau
increases. This happens in very explicit ways, as the
Y_{\infty N}
,
Z_{\infty N}
and
W_{\infty N}
estimates all contain a multiplicative factor
\tau
, but also in slightly more hidden but no less impactful manners, for instance in the
Z_{KN}
and
Z_{\infty N}
estimates, which depend on how well the functions
\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})
are approximated by the time-independent
\bar{v}^{(j)}
.

A natural option to compensate for these growing factors when
\tau
increases would be to also increase the degree
K
of Chebyshev interpolation used. Indeed, several of the above-mentioned bounds are proportional to the interpolation error constant
\sigma_{K,0}
, namely
Z_{\infty N}
and
W_{\infty N}
, whereas
Y_{\infty N}
behaves even more favorably when
K
increases, as
C^{l}
or even analytic interpolation error estimates can be used there (as opposed to the
C^{1}
interpolation error estimate corresponding to
\sigma_{K,0}
). However, it should already be noted that increasing
K
does not improve the factors related to the difference between
\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})
and
\bar{v}^{(j)}
in the
Z
estimates.

Another possibility is to split the time domain into multiple smaller subdomains, and to use several “copies” of the zero finding problem introduced previously, which are only weakly coupled via “matching conditions” at the boundary between successive subintervals. If we consider
M
subintervals, assumed for the moment to have equal length to simplify the discussion, this essentially means replacing
\tau
by
\frac{\tau}{M}
in the estimates mentioned above, and several estimates then scale like
\frac{\tau}{M}\sigma_{K,0}
. Since
\sigma_{K,0}
behaves roughly like
\frac{\ln(K)}{K}
(see Theorem
B.2
), we already see that increasing
M
is slightly more efficient than increasing
K
for these estimates (see
[
5
]
for a more throughout discussion on this specific aspect). Besides, for the critical estimate
Z
, increasing
M
instead of
K
has another substantial benefit related to our set-up with the time independent
\mathcal{L}
. Indeed, we are now allowed to consider a different
\mathcal{L}
on each (now smaller) subdomain.
In other words, we can approximate
\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})
by
\bar{v}^{(j)}
that is piece-wise constant in time rather than constant over the whole time interval, resulting in a much smaller difference
\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime}(\bar{u})-\bar{v}^{(j)}
, and therefore in a smaller
Z
estimate. For the sake of simplifying the discussion, we completely ignored the impact of the matching conditions on the estimates here, but this will be discussed in detail in Section
6.3
.

A key aspect that we wish to emphasize is that, thanks to these matching conditions, we still validate the entire solution at once. This means the domain decomposition setup can then be used to solve boundary value problems in time, for instance between local unstable and stable manifolds of objects of interest. This will be the subject of a future work.

An alternative approach for long time integration is time stepping: we also decompose the time domain, but then validate the solution on each subdomain successively, while rigorously propagating the errors/enclosures obtained at each step. While this direction is not the main focus of our work, the approach we developed can also handle time-stepping, which we describe in more detail in Appendix
A
.

In this section, we describe the modifications required for the domain decomposition approach. One key aspect is that the approximate inverse
A
should be modified judiciously. Once this is done, most of the computations presented in Section
5
can be re-used, and we therefore mainly describe the new estimates that have to be incorporated.

G:\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right)^{M}\rightarrow\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}\right)^{M}
defined by

G^{(m)}\left(u\right)\left(t\right)\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left\{\begin{aligned} &-u^{(m)}\left(t\right)&\qquad m=1,\\
&e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\mathcal{L}^{(m)}}u^{(m-1)}(1)-u^{(m)}\left(t\right)&\qquad m=2,\ldots,M.\end{aligned}\right.

Notice that
G
is invertible, and that

\displaystyle\left(G^{-1}(u)\right)^{(m)}(t)=

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left\{\begin{aligned} &-u^{(m)}\left(t\right)&\qquad m=1,\\
&-u^{(m)}(t)-e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\mathcal{L}^{(m)}}\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}\left(\prod_{j=l+1}^{m-1}e^{2\tau_{j}\mathcal{L}^{(j)}}\right)u^{(l)}(1)&\qquad m=2,\ldots,M.\end{aligned}\right.

with the convention
\prod_{j=l+1}^{m-1}e^{2\tau_{j}\mathcal{L}^{(j)}}=I
when
l=m-1
.

We extend the projections

\Pi_{KN},\Pi_{\infty N}
and
\Pi_{\infty}
from the single domain
to the multiple domains setting in the natural way. This gives rise to the subspaces

\displaystyle(\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu})^{M}=\Pi_{KN}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}\right),\quad(\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu})^{M}=\Pi_{\infty N}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}\right),\quad(\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu})^{M}=\Pi_{\infty}\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}\right)

and to the decomposition

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}=(\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu})^{M}\oplus(\mathcal{X}^{\infty N}_{\nu})^{M}\oplus(\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu})^{M}.

In order to introduce an appropriate approximate inverse, it will be convenient to use a block-decomposition of linear operators acting on
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}
, based on the above decomposition.

Definition 6.2

(Approximation of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
)
.

Given
\bar{u}
in
\left(\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\right)^{M}
, we introduce an approximate derivative
\widehat{DF}:\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}
of
F
at
\bar{u}
defined by

\displaystyle\widehat{DF}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}DF^{M}_{KN}\left(\bar{u}\right)&0&0\\
\hline\cr\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{KN}&-I^{M}_{\infty N}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\Pi_{\infty}G\Pi_{\infty}\end{array}\right).

Remark 6.3
.

Note that
\Pi_{KN}G\Pi_{\infty N}=0
and
\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{\infty N}=-I^{M}_{\infty N}
, so that this approximate derivative
\widehat{DF}
is simply obtained by always keeping the “important” part of
DF
, namely
G
, and by neglecting the others terms except on the finite dimensional subspace
\left(\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\right)^{M}
.

It is tempting be somewhat lazy, go one step further and also neglect the
\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{KN}
term, so that
\widehat{DF}
and then its inverse
A
become block-diagonal, which makes subsequent estimates easier. However, in practice we want the approximate derivative, and especially the approximate inverse of the derivative, to become more and more accurate when
N
and
K
increase, so that, at least in principle, we always get a contraction for
T
by taking the finite dimensional projection large enough. If the
\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{KN}
term is not included, the consequence is that quantities like

\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

for all
|n|\leq N
would have to be estimated in the new
Z^{1}_{\infty N}
bound, and would have to be small enough for us to prove that
T
is a contraction. Because these exponentials get stiffer and stiffer when
N
increases, this would introduce a very undesirable dependency between
K
and
N
, somewhat reminiscent of the CFL condition, where
K
(the time discretization parameter) has to be large enough with respect to
N
(the space discretization parameter).

Including the
\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{KN}
term in
\widehat{DF}
, and adapting
A
accordingly, allows us to alleviate this limitation.

Definition 6.4

(Approximate inverse of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
)
.

Given
\bar{u}
in
\left(\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\right)^{M}
, we introduce an approximate inverse
A:\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}\rightarrow\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}
of
DF\left(\bar{u}\right)
, defined by

\displaystyle A\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left(\begin{array}[]{c|c|c}A_{KN}&0&0\\
\hline\cr\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{KN}A_{KN}&-I^{M}_{\infty N}&0\\
\hline\cr 0&0&\Pi_{\infty}G^{-1}\Pi_{\infty}\end{array}\right),

where
A_{KN}
is a numerically computed approximate inverse of
DF^{M}_{KN}(\bar{u})
.

Up to the fact that
A_{KN}
is not the exact inverse of
DF^{M}_{KN}(\bar{u})
,
A
is the inverse of
\widehat{DF}
.
We can now again consider a Newton-like operator
T=I-AF
, this time mapping the product space
\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}
into itself, and try to validate a posteriori a numerically computed approximate solution
\bar{u}
by using Theorem
2.12
.

On each copy of
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
in
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}
, we use the norm defined in (
28
), possibly with different weights
\epsilon_{\infty N}
and
\epsilon_{\infty}
, which are thus denoted by
\epsilon_{\infty N}^{(m)}
and
\epsilon_{\infty}^{(m)}
. For any
u\in\mathcal{X}^{M}_{\nu}
and
r=(r^{(1)},\ldots,r^{(m)})\in\mathbb{R}^{M}_{>0}
, we define

\displaystyle B^{M}_{r,\epsilon}(u)=\prod_{m=1}^{M}B_{r^{(m)},\epsilon^{(m)}}\left(u^{(m)}\right),

which will play the role of
\bbox(u,r)
when applying Theorem
2.12
.

6.2
Estimates on
G
and
G^{-1}

We collect here basic estimates on
G
and
G^{-1}
that will be used several times in the next subsection, where we describe the extra terms appearing in the bounds
Y
,
Z
and
W
when domain decomposition is used.

Lemma 6.5
.

Let
\bar{w}\in\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{KN}\right)^{M}
. Then,

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}G^{(1)}\left(\bar{w}\right)\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}=0

and, for
m=2,\ldots,M
,

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}G^{(m)}\left(\bar{w}\right)\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\left\||Q|\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\Lambda_{N}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}|Q^{-1}|\ \left|\bar{w}^{(m-1)}(1)\right|\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}.

Proof.

By definition of
G
, we have

\Pi_{\infty N}G^{(m)}\left(\bar{w}\right)=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0&\qquad m=1,\\
&\Pi_{\infty N}\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\mathcal{L}^{(m)}}\bar{w}^{(m-1)}(1)\right)&\qquad m=2,\ldots,M.\end{aligned}\right.

For all
m=2,\ldots,M
, we then estimate

\displaystyle\left\|\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\mathcal{L}_{N}^{(m)}}\bar{w}^{(m-1)}(1)\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\|\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\mathcal{L}_{N}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\left|\bar{w}^{(m-1)}(1)\right|\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\left\||Q|\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\Lambda_{N}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}|Q^{-1}|\ \left|\bar{w}^{(m-1)}(1)\right|\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}}.

∎

While these interpolation errors for stiff exponentials were exactly the terms we were worried about when we discussed the definition of
\widehat{DF}
and
A
in Remark
6.3
, they are less worrisome here because they get multiplied by
\left|\bar{w}^{(m-1)}(1)\right|
, which are not specified here but which will always be small when we apply Lemma
6.5
in Section
6.3
repeatedly.

Remark 6.6
.

In order to make this estimate computable, we have to get estimates on

\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

for all
|n|\leq N
. This is accomplished using Lemma
4.5
together with Remark
4.9
. Similarly to what we did in Section
5.1.2
, we estimate this error by splitting it into an almost exact part, using an interpolation polynomial of high degree
K_{0}
, and an interpolation error estimate:

\displaystyle\left\|\left(I-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\left\|\left(P_{K_{0}}-P_{K}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}+\left\|\left(I-P_{K_{0}}\right)\left(t\mapsto e^{(t+1)\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}},

where the first term can be easily estimated via (
12
), and for the second one we use again Lemma
4.5
,
for some well chosen
\rho>1
(in practice a different
\rho
is taken for each
m
and
n
).

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
u\in\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}
. For
m=1,\ldots,M
and
|n|>N
,

\displaystyle\left\|\left(G^{-1}(u)\right)^{(m)}_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\left\|u_{n}^{(m)}\right\|_{C^{0}}+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}\exp\left(2\tau_{m}\Re\left(\lambda^{(m)}_{n}\right)^{+}+2\sum_{j=l+1}^{m-1}\tau_{j}\Re\left(\lambda_{n}^{(j)}\right)\right)\left|u^{(l)}_{n}(1)\right|,

where we again use the convention that empty sums are equal to
0
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}\left(G^{-1}(u)\right)^{(m)}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\left\|\Pi_{\infty}u^{(m)}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}+\sum_{l=1}^{m-1}e^{\mu_{m,l}}\left\|\Pi_{\infty}u^{(l)}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})},

where

\mu_{m,l}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\sup_{n>N}\left(2\tau_{m}\Re\left(\lambda_{n}^{(m)}\right)^{+}+2\sum_{j=l+1}^{m-1}\tau_{j}\Re\left(\lambda_{n}^{(j)}\right)\right).

Proof.

The proof of Lemma
6.7
follows directly from the definition of
G^{-1}
and the triangle inequality.
∎

6.3
How to adapt the bounds

In this subsection, we derive computable bounds
Y
,
Z
and
W
satisfying assumptions (
14
)-(
16
) of Theorem
2.12
, for the operator
T
, the space
X=\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{M}
and an approximate solution
\bar{u}
in
\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{KN}\right)^{M}
. The dependencies in
F
on the various subdomains are mostly uncoupled, except for the matching conditions at the boundaries, which means many of the estimations derived in Section
5
can be re-used. Throughout this section we will comment sparsely on the bookkeeping involved to obtain the “component” bounds
Y^{(m)}
,
Z_{i}^{(m)}
and
W^{(m)}_{ij}
, which are needed for using Theorem
2.12
with
M>1
.

As was done in Section
5
, each bound
Y^{(m)}
,
Z_{i}^{(m)}
and
W^{(m)}_{ij}
will be split into three parts corresponding to the decomposition (
26
) of
\mathcal{X}_{\nu}
, i.e.

\displaystyle Y^{(m)}:=Y_{KN}^{(m)}+\left(\epsilon^{(m)}_{\infty N}\right)^{-1}Y^{(m)}_{\infty N}+\left(\epsilon^{(m)}_{\infty}\right)^{-1}Y^{(m)}_{\infty},\quad\text{for all }1\leq m\leq M,

and similarly for
Z^{(m)}_{i}
and
W^{(m)}_{ij}
.

Remark 6.8
.

Similarly to what we did in Section
5
, it will be convenient to consider arbitrary
h
and
v
in
B^{M}_{1,\epsilon}(0)
in order to derive the bounds
Z^{(m)}_{i}
and
W^{(m)}_{ij}
. That is we are going to look for
Z^{(m)}_{i}
such that

\displaystyle\left\|\pi^{(m)}DT(\bar{u})h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{M}Z^{(m)}_{i}\left\|h^{(i)}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}},

and for
W^{(m)}_{ij}
such that, writing an arbitrary
u\in B^{M}_{r_{*},\epsilon}(\bar{u})
as
\bar{u}+r_{*}v
,

\displaystyle\left\|\pi^{(m)}\left(DT(\bar{u}+r_{*}v)-DT(\bar{u})\right)h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\leq r_{*}\sum_{i=1}^{M}\sum_{j=1}^{M}W^{(m)}_{ij}\left\|h^{(i)}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\left\|v^{(j)}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}.

6.3.1
Modifications for
Y_{KN}

Each entry of
F_{KN}(\bar{u})
can still be computed (or more precisely enclosed), and so the generalization is straightforward: we simply consider

\displaystyle Y^{(m)}_{KN}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\left\|\pi^{(m)}\left(A_{KN}F_{KN}(\bar{u})\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}},\quad m=1,\ldots,M,

with the
F
and
A_{KN}
of Section
6.1
.

6.3.2
Modifications for
Y_{\infty N}

On each subdomain
1\leq m\leq M
, we have to estimate

\left\|\Pi_{\infty N}F^{(m)}(\bar{u})\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}
,
for which we can reproduce verbatim the analysis of Section
5.1.2
. However, we also get an additional term

\Pi_{\infty N}G\Pi_{KN}A_{KN}F_{KN}(\bar{u})

to estimate. We therefore consider
\bar{w}=A_{KN}F_{KN}(\bar{u})\in\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{KN}\right)^{M}
, and use Lemma
6.5
together with Remark
6.6
to get an upper bound for this quantity.

6.3.3
Modifications for
Y_{\infty}

We have to bound

\pi^{(m)}\Pi_{\infty}\left(T(\bar{u})-\bar{u}\right)=\pi^{(m)}G^{-1}\left(\Pi_{\infty}F(\bar{u})\right),

and to that aim we use the first part of Lemma
6.7
, together with

\displaystyle\left|F^{(m)}_{n}\left(\bar{u}\right)(1)\right|=\left|e^{2\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\bar{u}^{(m-1)}_{n}(1)+\tau_{m}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{(1-s)\tau_{m}\lambda_{n}^{(m)}}\gamma_{n}^{(m)}(\bar{u}(s))\mbox{d}s\right|,

(45)

and

\displaystyle\left\|F^{(m)}_{n}\left(\bar{u}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{2\tau_{m}\Re\left(\lambda_{n}^{(m)}\right)^{+}}\left|\bar{u}^{(m-1)}_{n}(1)\right|+\left\|\gamma_{n}^{(m)}\left(\bar{u}\right)\right\|_{C^{0}}\frac{e^{2\tau_{m}\Re(\lambda_{n}^{(m)})}-1}{\Re(\lambda_{n}^{(m)})},

(46)

for each
m=1,\ldots,M
and
n>N
. Notice that, except maybe for
m=1
,
\bar{u}^{(m-1)}_{n}=0
because we only consider
|n|>N
, and that there are only finitely many
n
for which
\gamma_{n}^{(m)}\left(\bar{u}\right)
, and hence
F^{(m)}_{n}\left(\bar{u}\right)
, are non-zero. Therefore we can compute the associated
\ell^{1}_{\nu}
norm, which gives us the bounds
Y^{(m)}_{\infty}
for
1\leq m\leq M
.

6.3.4
Modifications for
Z_{KN}

Up to using the new definition of
A
and
\widehat{DF}
from Section
6.1
, there is no significant change to the
\Pi_{KN}(I-A\widehat{DF})
part of that estimate, which remains a finite computation.
In view of (
15
) we just need to extract the different components
(Z_{KN})^{(m)}_{i}
, which is essentially a bookkeeping task. Let
A_{KN}^{(m,m^{\prime})}
be the block of the matrix
A_{KN}
linking the
m^{\prime}
-th to the
m
-th domain, and similarly for
DF^{M}_{KN}(\bar{u})^{(m,m^{\prime})}
, then
we need to compute

\|I_{KN}-\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}A_{KN}^{(m,m^{\prime})}DF^{M}_{KN}(\bar{u})^{(m^{\prime},i)}\|_{B(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{KN},\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{KN})}
as the first part of the bound
(Z_{KN})^{(m)}_{i}
.

Regarding the
\Pi_{KN}\left(DF(\bar{u})-\widehat{DF}\right)h
part, we can reproduce verbatim the analysis of Section
5.2.1
on each subdomain separately. There are no new terms coming from the matching conditions, since

\Pi_{\infty N}h^{(m)}(1)=0

for
1\leq m\leq M
.
When determining the contribution of this term to the bound
(Z_{KN})^{(m)}_{i}
,
the only coupling between the domains is through left-multiplication by
A_{KN}^{M}
.

6.3.5
Modifications for
Z_{\infty N}

Regarding the
\Pi_{\infty N}\left(I-A\widehat{DF}\right)
part of that estimate, we again get an extra term of the form
\Pi_{\infty N}G\left(\bar{w}\right)
, this time with

\displaystyle\bar{w}=\left(I^{M}_{KN}-A_{KN}^{M}DF^{M}_{KN}(\bar{u})\right)h_{KN},

for
h\in B^{M}_{1,\epsilon}(0)
. We can then easily estimate each
|\bar{w}^{(m)}(1)|
,
and subsequently use Lemma
6.5
.
The decomposition into components
(Z_{\infty N})^{(m)}_{i}
is a bookkeeping exercise as already outlined in Section
6.3.4
.

Regarding the
\Pi_{\infty N}A\left(DF\left(\bar{u}\right)-\widehat{DF}\right)
part of that estimate,
we also pick up another term of the form
\Pi_{\infty N}G\left(\bar{w}\right)
, this time with

\bar{w}=A_{KN}\Pi_{KN}\left(DF(\bar{u})-\widehat{DF}\right)h,

for
h\in B^{M}_{1,\epsilon}(0)
. The
\Pi_{KN}\left(DF(\bar{u})-\widehat{DF}\right)h
part was already estimated in (
5.2.1
), since all the extra terms related to the coupling appearing in
DF(\bar{u})
are also included in
\widehat{DF}
and therefore cancel out. We then simply have to multiply this by
|A_{KN}|
, evaluate the value at 1, use Lemma
6.5
, and do diligent bookkeeping to
decompose the contributions to
(Z_{\infty N})^{(m)}_{i}
.

6.3.6
Modifications for
Z_{\infty}

We have to estimate, for all
m=1,\ldots,M
,

\left\|\pi^{(m)}\Pi_{\infty}A\left(DF\left(\bar{u}\right)-\widehat{DF}\right)h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}=\left\|\pi^{(m)}G^{-1}\left(\Pi_{\infty}w\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}

where

\displaystyle w=DF(\bar{u})(h)-G(h),

for
h\in B^{M}_{1,\epsilon}(0)
. For all
|n|>N
, and
1\leq m\leq M
, with the notation
\tilde{g}^{(m,j)}
inherited from Section
5.2.3
,

\displaystyle w^{(m)}_{n}(t)

\displaystyle=\tau_{m}\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\tau_{m}(t-s)\lambda^{(m)}_{n}}\left(\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}(in)^{j}\left(\tilde{g}^{(m,j)}(s)\ast h^{(m)}(s)\right)_{n}\right)\mbox{d}s,

hence

\displaystyle\left\|\Pi_{\infty}w^{(m)}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}\leq\vartheta_{\epsilon}\left\|h^{(m)}\right\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\chi_{N}^{(m,j)}\left\|\tilde{g}^{(m,j)}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})},\qquad\text{for }m=1,\ldots,M,

which can be combined with the second part of Lemma
6.7
to obtain the
(Z_{\infty})^{(m)}_{i}
estimates.

6.3.7
Modifications for
W_{KN}

As was the case for the
Y
and the

bounds, there is nothing to change in the finite part of the
W
bound, we can just re-use, component-wise, the bounds from Section
5.3.1
.

6.3.8
Modifications for
W_{\infty N}

We get a final extra term of the form
\Pi_{\infty N}G\left(\bar{w}\right)
, this time with

\bar{w}=A_{KN}\Pi_{KN}\left(DF(\bar{u}+r_{*}v)-DF(\bar{u})\right)h,

for

. The
\Pi_{KN}\left(DF(\bar{u}+r_{*}v)-DF(\bar{u})\right)h

).
Starting back from there, and also generalizing the notation
\Xi^{(j)}
from (

) to multiple domains:

\Xi^{(m,j)}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\begin{pmatrix}|Q^{(m)}|\,D^{(m)}_{N}(t_{0})\,|(Q^{(m)})^{-1}|\ |\mathcal{D}^{j}|\\
\vdots\\
|Q^{(m)}|\,D^{(m)}_{N}(t_{K})\,|(Q^{(m)})^{-1}|\ |\mathcal{D}^{j}|\end{pmatrix},

we get, for all

,

\displaystyle\left|\Pi_{KN}\left(DF^{(m)}(\bar{u}+r_{*}v)-DF^{(m)}(\bar{u})\right)h\right|\leq

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad r_{*}\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\Xi^{(m,j)}\,\Pi_{N}\left(\left|\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime\prime}\right|\left(\|\bar{u}^{(m)}\|_{C^{0}}+r_{*}\|v^{(m)}\|_{C^{0}}\right)\ast\|v^{(m)}\|_{C^{0}}\ast\|h^{(m)}\|_{C^{0}}\right).

We then define the “evaluation at

” operator
E_{1}:\mathcal{X}^{KN}_{\nu}\to\mathbb{C}^{2N+1}
by

, and use Lemmas
3.6
,
4.1
and

to obtain the
(W_{\infty N})^{(m)}_{ij}
estimates.

\displaystyle|\bar{w}^{(m)}(1)|\leq

\displaystyle\quad r_{*}\vartheta_{\epsilon}^{2}\sum_{m^{\prime}=1}^{M}\|v^{(m^{\prime})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\|h^{(m^{\prime})}\|_{\mathcal{X}_{\nu}}\sum_{j=0}^{2R-1}\Upsilon\left(|E_{1}A^{(m,m^{\prime})}_{KN}|\,\Xi^{(m^{\prime},j)}\right)\,\left|\left(g^{(j)}\right)^{\prime\prime}\right|\left(\left\|\bar{u}^{(m^{\prime})}\right\|_{\ell^{1}_{\nu}(C^{0})}+\vartheta_{\epsilon}r^{*}\right),

(47)

where
A^{(m,m^{\prime})}_{KN}
is the block of the matrix

linking the
m^{\prime}
-th to the
m
-th domain. Finally, we apply Lemma

(W_{\infty N})^{(m)}_{ij}

Modifications for

We have to estimate, for all

\left\|\pi^{(m)}\Pi_{\infty}A\left(DF\left(\bar{u}+r_{*}v\right)-DF\left(\bar{u}\right)\right)h\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}}=\left\|\pi^{(m)}G^{-1}\left(\Pi_{\infty}z\right)\right\|_{\mathcal{X}^{\infty}_{\nu}},

7
Applications

In this section, we present some applications of the validation procedure introduced in this paper, and discuss the obtained results. First, using the domain decomposition approach, we showcase the broad applicability of our method in Section
7.1
, by using it to rigorously integrate several PDEs of the form (
1
) having different properties and different dynamics. In Section
7.2
, we highlight the influence of one of the most significant aspects of this work, namely the solution-adapted choice of the operator
\mathcal{L}
made in Section
3.4
, by reproducing some of the proofs from Section
7.1
but now with a trivial choice of
\mathcal{L}
, and by comparing the results. In Section
7.3
, we discuss the merits of time stepping as an alternative to domain decomposition.

7.1
Different examples of equations

We start with what is arguably the simplest nonlinear parabolic equation.

Theorem 7.1

7.3
Using time stepping

For a given equation of the form (
1
), when we try to use the approach presented in this paper with domain decomposition, in order to validate a solution for a larger and larger integration time
\tau
, we have to keep increasing the number
M
of subdomains used, or alternatively to keep increasing the number
K
of Chebyshev nodes used in each subdomain.

Remark 7.8
.

In practice, as soon as the
Y
bound is no longer a limiting factor in getting a successful proof, it is usually more efficient to increase
M
than to increase
K
. This was already noticed in the context of ODEs
[
5
]
, and is even more apparent in the current work, because increasing
M
(i.e. taking more and thus smaller subdomains) means our operator
\mathcal{L}
better approximates
DH(\bar{u})
(with the notation of the introduction).

However,
M
cannot be taken arbitrarily large, because at some point the dimension of the subspace
\left(\mathcal{X}_{\nu}^{KN}\right)^{M}
becomes too large for a computer to handle, memory-wise. This limitation is inherent to the domain-decomposition approach, where one has to deal with the entire orbit at once.

On the other hand, with time stepping one can validate smaller portions of an the orbit one after the other, at the price of having to propagate the error bounds from one portion to the next. We can adapt our methodology to such a time stepping strategy. The details of controlling the propagating error bounds are given in Appendix
A
. One setting in which these errors are relatively easy to manage is when the solution converges to an isolated equilibrium. Indeed, in that case we expect the semiflow to be contracting, and the errors to shrink between the beginning and the end of one step. Therefore, in principle one should be able to rigorously integrate the solution for as long as desired (see Remark
A.1
). Of course there is still a limitation related to computing power, but it is now predominantly related to speed (i.e., to the time taken by the computer to validate each step), rather than memory.

Below is an example of a validated solution of Fisher’s equation (
48
), for which we used time stepping in order to go much further in time than in Section
7.1
.

Appendix A
Time stepping

When performing long time integration, an alternative for domain decomposition is to perform time stepping, i.e., to use the data at the final time of a step as the initial data for the next step. This may lead to a build-up of errors,
hence we need sharp enclosures for the endpoint of the orbit at each time step. We already have the one given by the validation radius, which is valid for all
t\in[-1,1]
, but we can also use the fact that we only need a bound for
t=1
to get possibly better estimates.
Indeed, suppose we have successfully proved the existence of a fixed
point
u^{\ast}\in\bar{u}+B_{r,\epsilon}(0)
of
T
for some
r>0
, then in the case of a single domain (
M=1
) we have

\displaystyle u^{\ast}(1)

\displaystyle=e^{2\tau\mathcal{L}}f+\tau\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}(1-s)}\gamma\left(u^{\ast}(s)\right)\mbox{d}s

(52)

\displaystyle=e^{2\tau\mathcal{L}}f+\tau\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}(1-s)}\gamma\left(\bar{u}(s)\right)\mbox{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\tau r\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}(1-s)}D\gamma\left(\bar{u}\left(s\right)\right)h(s)\mbox{d}s

\displaystyle\quad+\tau r^{2}\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\tau\mathcal{L}(1-s)}\int_{0}^{1}\left(1-\tilde{s}\right)D^{2}\gamma\left(\bar{u}\left(s\right)+\tilde{s}rh\left(s\right)\right)\left[h(s),h(s)\right]\mbox{d}\tilde{s}\ \mbox{d}s,

for some
h\in B_{1,\epsilon}(0)
. We now see how we can derive an explicit enclosure of
u^{\ast}(1)
, based on this formula, studying separately the finite dimensional projection given by
\Pi_{N}
, and the higher order modes.

Remark A.1
.

In some situations the error bound at
t=1
may be tighter than the one at
t=-1
(the initial data
f
). Observe that
in the above expression, the only term which depends on the initial data is
e^{2\tau\mathcal{L}}f
. Suppose we started with an enclosure for
f
, for instance because
f
is in fact the output of a previous time step, and assume that the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
are all negative, for example because the dynamics is close to a stable stationary state. We then get an enclosure for
e^{2\tau\mathcal{L}}f
which is narrower than the one of
f
. Provided the error bounds we get for the remaining terms in (
52
) are small enough, it is thus possible that the total error bound we get for
u^{*}(1)
is smaller than the one we started with for
f
, meaning that in such a situation errors do not grow from one time-step to the next. For the solution depicted in Figure
6
, we are seemingly getting very close to the homogeneous equilibrium, which is attractive, hence in that situation the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{L}
should all be negative, and we can in principle integrate forever.

If we were to deal with an orbit which converges to a nonhomogeneous equilibrium, as is seemingly the case in Figure
1
for instance, there is one additional difficulty in rigorously integrating “forever” using time stepping, namely that the equilibrium is no longer isolated due to the spatial translation invariance of the problem. This then means that there will be a
zero
eigenvalue in the linearized operator at the equilibrium, and that we cannot hope to shrink the errors in all directions. This issue is somewhat specific to our choice of periodic boundary conditions, and would for instance disappear with Dirichlet or Neumann boundary conditions.

Appendix B
Interpolation error estimates

In this section, we provide explicit formulas for the constants
\sigma_{K,l}
satisfying Definition
2.5
. Let us first show that, although most of the interpolation error estimates of this kind that can be found in the literature are stated for real-valued functions, they do also hold for complex valued-functions.

Lemma B.1
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
l\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
and
\sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,l}
a real constant such that, for all
f\in C^{l+1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})
,

\displaystyle\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,l}\left\|f^{(l+1)}\right\|_{C^{0}}.

(53)

Then (
53
) also holds for any
f\in C^{l+1}([-1,1],\mathbb{C})
, i.e., one can take
\sigma_{K,l}=\sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,l}
in Definition
2.5
.

Proof.

Let
f\in C^{l+1}([-1,1],\mathbb{C})
and fix any
x\in[-1,1]
. Denoting by
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
the argument of the complex number
f(x)-P_{K}(f)(x)
, we get

\displaystyle\left|f(x)-P_{K}(f)(x)\right|

\displaystyle=e^{-i\theta}\left(f(x)-P_{K}(f)(x)\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left(e^{-i\theta}(f(x)-P_{K}(f)(x))\right)

\displaystyle=\Re\left(e^{-i\theta}f\right)(x)-P_{K}(\Re\left(e^{-i\theta}f\right))(x)

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,l}\left\|\Re\left(e^{-i\theta}f\right)^{(l+1)}\right\|_{C^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\sigma^{\mathbb{R}}_{K,l}\left\|f^{(l+1)}\right\|_{C^{0}},

where the imaginary terms that should appear in the second line must vanish since we are computing a real quantity.
∎

Theorem B.2
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
l\in\{0,1,\ldots K\}
, and
\Lambda_{K}
be the Lebesgue constant associated to interpolation at the
K+1
Chebyshev nodes (see, e.g.,
[
39
]
, and
[
18
]
for explicit values and tight bounds of
\Lambda_{K}
). Then,

\displaystyle\sigma_{K,l}

\displaystyle\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\begin{cases}\min\left\{\left(1+\Lambda_{K}\right)\left(\frac{\pi}{2}\right)^{l+1}\frac{(K-l)!}{(K+1)!}\ ,\ \frac{1}{l+1}\sum_{q=0}^{\left[\frac{l}{2}\right]}\frac{1}{4^{q}\,(l-2q)!\,(q!)^{2}}\right\},&0\leq l\leq K-1,\\[8.61108pt]
\dfrac{1}{2^{K-1}(K+1)!}&l=K.\end{cases}

(54)

satisfies Definition
2.5
.

For real-valued functions, the case
l=K
is classical, as is the first part of the estimate for
l\leq K-1
, which follows from Jackson’s theorem (see e.g.
[
11
]
). Lemma
B.1
ensures these results carry over to complex-valued functions. However, the obtained estimates are far from sharp for small values of
K
. An alternative estimate, namely the second part of the minimum for
l\leq K-1
, was derived in
[
5
]
. In the important case
l=0
, this estimate gives
1
and is thus smaller than
\left(1+\Lambda_{K}\right)\frac{\pi}{2}\frac{K!}{(K+1)!}
for
K\leq 3
, but still relatively far from being sharp.

In the sequel, we introduce an optimization problem which is equivalent to finding the optimal value of
\sigma_{K,0}
satisfying Definition
2.5
. According to Lemma
B.1
, we can restrict our attention to real-valued functions. Using interval arithmetic, we are then able to find a rigorous (and relatively tight) enclosure of the solution of the minimization problem, thereby providing an almost optimal value for
\sigma_{K,0}
.

Definition B.3
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and
y\in\mathbb{R}^{K+1}
. Let
P_{K}(y)
denotes the polynomial of degree at most
K
interpolating
y
at the Chebyshev nodes, i.e. such that
P_{K}(y)(t_{k})=y_{k}
for
k=0\dots K
. Whenever
y
is such that

\displaystyle|y_{k}|\leq|t_{k}|\text{ for }k=0\dots K,\quad\text{and}\quad\left|y_{k+1}-y_{k}\right|\leq\left|t_{k+1}-t_{k}\right|\text{ for }k=0\dots K-1,

(55)

we also introduce, for all
t\in[-1,1]
,

\displaystyle\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)=\min\bigl\{y_{k^{*}}+t-t_{k^{*}},\,y_{{k^{*}}+1}-t+t_{{k^{*}}+1},\,|t|\bigr\},

and

\displaystyle\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)=\max\bigl\{y_{k^{*}}-t+t_{k^{*}},\,y_{{k^{*}}+1}+t-t_{{k^{*}}+1},\,-|t|\bigr\},

where
{k^{*}}\in\{0,1,\ldots,K-1\}
is such that
t\in[t_{k^{*}},t_{{k^{*}}+1}]
.

Notice that (
55
) ensures that
\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)
and
\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)
are well defined: for
t=t_{k}
we get
\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)=\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)=y_{k}
regardless of whether we consider
k^{*}=k
or
k^{*}=k-1
. Notice also that
\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)
(resp.
\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)
) is the largest (resp. the smallest) piece-wise linear function
\varphi
such that
\varphi(t_{k})=y_{k}
for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
,
\varphi(0)=0
, and
|\varphi^{\prime}(t)|\leq 1
for all
t\in[-1,1]\setminus\{0,t_{0},\ldots,t_{K}\}
.

Proposition B.4
.

Let
K\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, and

\displaystyle\Omega_{K}=\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{K+1},\ |y_{k}|\leq|t_{k}|\text{ for }k=0\dots K,\ |y_{k+1}-y_{k}|\leq|t_{k+1}-t_{k}|\text{ for }k=0\dots K-1\right\}.

Then,

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f^{\prime}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}}=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[-1,1]\\
y\in\Omega_{K}\end{subarray}}\max\left(\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)-P_{K}(y)(t),\,P_{K}(y)(t)-\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)\right).

(56)

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume
\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1
and
f(0)=0
, so that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f^{\prime}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}}=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f(0)=0,\ \left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}.

For any
f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})
such that
f(0)=0
and
\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1
, consider the vector
y\in\mathbb{R}^{K+1}
defined by
y_{k}=f(t_{k})
for
k=0\dots K
, which belongs to
\Omega_{K}
. Since
\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1
, we get

\displaystyle\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)\leq f\leq\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y),

and
P_{K}(f)=P_{K}(y)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\leq\max_{t\in[-1,1]}\max\left(\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)-P_{K}(y)(t),\,P_{K}(y)(t)-\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)\right),

and hence

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f^{\prime}\neq 0\end{subarray}}\frac{\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}}{\left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}}\leq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[-1,1]\\
y\in\Omega_{K}\end{subarray}}\max\left(\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)-P_{K}(y)(t),\,P_{K}(y)(t)-\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)\right).

Conversely, for any
y\in\Omega_{K}
, the function
f=\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)
satisfies
f(0)=0
and
\left|f^{\prime}(t)\right|\leq 1
for each
t
where
f^{\prime}
is defined. Considering a sequence of
C^{1}
functions
f_{n}
such that
f_{n}(0)=0
,
\left\|f^{\prime}_{n}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1
, and
\left\|f_{n}-f\right\|_{C^{0}}\underset{n\to\infty}{\rightarrow}0
, we get that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f(0)=0,\ \left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\geq\max_{t\in[-1,1]}\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)-P_{K}(y)(t).

Similarly,

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f(0)=0,\ \left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\geq\max_{t\in[-1,1]}P_{K}(y)(t)-\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t),

and therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in C^{1}([-1,1],\mathbb{R})\\
f(0)=0,\ \left\|f^{\prime}\right\|_{C^{0}}=1\end{subarray}}\left\|f-P_{K}(f)\right\|_{C^{0}}\geq\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[-1,1]\\
y\in\Omega_{K}\end{subarray}}\max\left(\overline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)-P_{K}(y)(t),\,P_{K}(y)(t)-\underline{\varphi}_{K}(y)(t)\right),

which concludes the proof.
∎

Proposition
B.4
gives us a tractable way of obtaining the optimal constant
\sigma_{K,0}
, the r.h.s. of (
56
) being an optimization problem over a compact set of
\mathbb{R}^{K+2}

K=2

Theorem B.5

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\in[-1,1]\\

Remark B.7
.

We only provide enclosures up to
K=6
because larger values of
K
did not prove useful in this work: in order to get a smaller contraction rate (i.e.
Z
bound), increasing the number of subdomains is more efficient than increasing
K
. This was already observed in
[
5
]
when using interpolation in time for ODEs, and becomes even more important in this work, as increasing the number of subdomains also improves our piece-wise constant in time approximation
\mathcal{L}
of

(with the notations of the introduction), which in turns improves the
Z
bound. The only reason to increase
K
is to get a smaller defect (the
Y
bound).

However, it should also be noted that the computational cost of the algorithm we used to obtain rigorous enclosures of

increases dramatically with

, and that this approach does not seem suitable without algorithmic improvements for much larger values of

.

Remark B.8
.

Since the constant

plays a crucial role in our validation estimates, as it appears in the

bound, see (

), a possible way to improve upon the setup presented in this paper would be, in the interpolation in time, to replace the Chebyshev nodes by interpolation nodes chosen to minimize
\sigma_{K,0}
, but we did not explore this option in this work.

Appendix C
Quadrature for the exponential integrals

In this section we explain how to compute rigorous enclosures for

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{t}e^{\lambda\left(t-s\right)}\psi(s)\ \mbox{d}s

\displaystyle=\frac{t+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(t+1\right)\left(1-s\right)\right)\psi\left(\frac{t+1}{2}\left(s+1\right)-1\right)\mbox{d}s,

(57)

where
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
,
t\in(-1,1]
and
\psi:[-1,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is a polynomial function, written in the Chebyshev basis

\displaystyle\psi=\psi_{0}+2\sum_{k=1}^{K}\psi_{k}T_{k},

(58)

for some
K\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
.
These enclosures are needed to
rigorously evaluate the map
F_{KN}
on the computer. The idea is to approximate (
57
)
by using a quadrature rule based at the Chebyshev points and to compute a bound for the associated error.

Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature.

Let
(t^{K_{1}}_{k})_{k=0}^{K_{1}}
denote the Chebyshev points of order
K_{1}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 2}

(see Definition
2.3
). The parameter
K_{1}
is the order of the quadrature
rule and need not be equal to
K
. If
f:\left[-1,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is sufficiently smooth and
K_{1}

is sufficiently large, then

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f(s)\ \mbox{d}s\approx\int_{-1}^{1}f_{K_{1}}(s)\ \mbox{d}s,

where
f_{K_{1}}
is the Chebyshev interpolant of
f
of order
K_{1}
. In particular, note that

\displaystyle f_{K_{1}}=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{1}}f\left(t^{K_{1}}_{k}\right)\phi_{k},

where
(\phi_{k})_{k=0}^{K_{1}}
are the
Lagrange polynomials
associated to

(t^{K_{1}}_{k})_{k=0}^{K_{1}}
, i.e.,
\phi_{k}(t^{K_{1}}_{l})=\delta_{kl}

for
0\leq k,l\leq K_{1}
. Hence

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f_{K_{1}}(s)\ \mbox{d}s=\sum_{k=0}^{K_{1}}f(t_{k}^{K_{1}})w_{k},\quad w_{k}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\int_{-1}^{1}\phi_{k}(s)\ \mbox{d}s,\quad 0\leq k\leq K_{1}.

(59)

Remark C.1
.

The weights
\left(w_{k}\right)_{k=0}^{K_{1}}
are independent of the objective function
f
and are commonly referred
to as the Chebyshev quadrature weights. These weights can be efficiently computed by using the Discrete Fourier
Transform as explained in
[
48
]
.

In conclusion, the integral of
f
can be approximated by

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}f(s)\ \mbox{d}s\approx\sum_{k=0}^{K_{1}}w_{k}f(t_{k}^{K_{1}}).

This particular quadrature rule is referred to as
Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature
. Furthermore,
the latter approximation is exact whenever
f
is a polynomial of at most order
K_{1}
.
The reader is referred to
[
39
,
48
]
for a more detailed treatment
of Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature.

The next theorem, presented in
[
39
]
, provides a bound for the error
associated to Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature:

Theorem C.2
.

Suppose
f:\left[-1,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
can be analytically extended to the open ellipse
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
,
for some
\rho>1
. If
f
is bounded on
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
, and
K_{1}
is even then

\displaystyle\left|\int_{-1}^{1}f(s)\ \mbox{d}s-\sum_{k=0}^{K_{1}}w_{k}f(t_{k}^{K_{1}})\right|\leq\frac{64}{15}\frac{\rho^{-K_{1}}}{\rho^{2}-1}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|f(z)\right|.

Proof.

See
[
39
, Theorem
19.3
]
. The statement there is only made for real valued functions, but the proof readily extends to complex valued functions.
∎

As mentioned before, the strategy is to use Clenshaw-Curtis quadrature to approximate (
57
) and to
bound the associated error with the aid of the Theorem
C.2
. To use this theorem, however, we
need one final estimate to bound the integrand in (
57
) on
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
, and this is where it proves convenient to have
\psi
written in the Chebyshev basis:

Lemma C.3
.

Let
\rho\geq 1
,
t\in[-1,1]
,
\psi
as in (
58
), and write
\lambda=\alpha+i\beta
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
. Then

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|\frac{t+1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(t+1\right)\left(1-z\right)\right)\psi\left(\frac{t+1}{2}\left(z+1\right)-1\right)\right|

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\frac{t+1}{2}\exp\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}(\rho+\rho^{-1})^{2}+\beta^{2}(\rho-\rho^{-1})^{2}}\right)\left(\left|\psi_{0}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\psi_{k}\right|\left(\rho^{k}+\rho^{-k}\right)\right).

Proof.

Since
\frac{t+1}{2}\left(\mathcal{E}_{\rho}+1\right)-1\subset\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
, it follows from Lemma
2.10
that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|\psi\left(\frac{t+1}{2}\left(z+1\right)-1\right)\right|\leq\left|\psi_{0}\right|+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\left|\psi_{k}\right|\left(\rho^{k}+\rho^{-k}\right).

Besides, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\left|\frac{t+1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(t+1\right)\left(1-z\right)\right)\right|=\frac{t+1}{2}\exp\left(\frac{t+1}{2}\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\Re\left(\lambda\left(1-z\right)\right)\right).

It only remains to be proven that

\sup_{z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}}\Re\left((\alpha+i\beta)\left(1-z\right)\right)=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}(\rho+\rho^{-1})^{2}+\beta^{2}(\rho-\rho^{-1})^{2}}.

(60)

Any
z\in\mathcal{E}_{\rho}
can be written
z=\frac{1}{2}\left(re^{i\theta}+r^{-1}e^{-i\theta}\right)
, for some
(r,\theta)\in[1,\rho]\times[0,2\pi]
. We then have

\displaystyle\Re\left((\alpha+i\beta)\left(1-z\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha\left(1-\frac{1}{2}(r+r^{-1})\cos\theta\right)+\beta\frac{1}{2}(r-r^{-1})\sin\theta

\displaystyle=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{\alpha^{2}(r+r^{-1})^{2}+\beta^{2}(r-r^{-1})^{2}}\cos(\theta-\theta_{r}),

for some
\theta_{r}\in[0,2\pi]
, which yields (
60
).
∎

Appendix D
Computing the
\chi_{N}
bounds

We provide here an explicit procedure for computing the bounds
\chi_{N}^{(j)}
,
j=0,\ldots,2R-1
, introduced in Lemma
4.10
and used in the
Z_{\infty}
and
W_{\infty}
bounds.

It will be useful to consider

\alpha_{l}=(-1)^{l}\left(\bar{v}^{(2l)}\right)_{0},\qquad l=0,\ldots,R-1,

and the polynomial

P(X)=-X^{R}+\sum_{l=0}^{R-1}\alpha_{l}X^{l},

so that

\Re(\lambda_{n})=P(n^{2}).

1.

Define

N^{(j)}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\max\left(\sqrt{\sum_{l=0}^{R-1}\frac{|2l-j|}{2R-j}|\alpha_{l}|},N+1\right),

for which (since
N\geq 0
)

\left(N^{(j)}\right)^{2}\geq\max\left(\sum_{l=0}^{R-1}\frac{|2l-j|}{2R-j}|\alpha_{l}|,1\right).

We have

\displaystyle-jP(X)+2XP^{\prime}(X)=(j-2R)X^{R}+\sum_{l=0}^{R-1}(2l-j)\alpha_{j}X^{l},

hence, for
n\geq N^{(j)}
,

\displaystyle-jP(n^{2})+2n^{2}P^{\prime}(n^{2})\leq 0,

and therefore the map
n\mapsto\frac{n^{j}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}
is decreasing. Since this map goes to
0
when
n
goes to infinity, it means it is also positive for
n\geq N^{(j)}
, in which case
\Re(\lambda_{n})
must be negative, therefore

\displaystyle\sup_{n\geq N^{(j)}}n^{j}\frac{1-e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}\leq\sup_{n\geq N^{(j)}}\frac{n^{j}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}\leq\frac{\left(N^{(j)}\right)^{j}}{-\Re\left(\lambda_{N^{(j)}}\right)}.

2.

Compute

\displaystyle\tilde{\chi}_{N}^{(j)}\,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\tiny{\raisebox{0.0pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{def}}}}}{{=}}\,\max_{N<n\leq N^{(j)}}n^{j}\frac{1-e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}.

•

If

\displaystyle\frac{\left(N^{(j)}\right)^{j}}{-\Re\left(\lambda_{N^{(j)}}\right)}\leq\tilde{\chi}_{N}^{(j)},

then
\chi_{N}^{(j)}=\tilde{\chi}_{N}^{(j)}
and we are done.

•

Otherwise, compute
\tilde{N}^{(j)}>N^{(j)}
so that

\displaystyle\frac{\left(\tilde{N}^{(j)}\right)^{j}}{-\Re\left(\lambda_{\tilde{N}^{(j)}}\right)}\leq\tilde{\chi}_{N}^{(j)},

and then we get

\displaystyle\chi_{N}^{(j)}=\max_{N<n\leq\tilde{N}^{(j)}}n^{j}\frac{1-e^{2\tau\Re(\lambda_{n})}}{-\Re(\lambda_{n})}.
