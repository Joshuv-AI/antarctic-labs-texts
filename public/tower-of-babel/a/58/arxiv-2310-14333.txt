Analysis of Iterative Methods for the Linear Boltzmann Transport Equation
Paul Houston, Matthew E. Hubbard, Thomas J. Radley

Abstract
. In this article we consider the iterative solution of the linear system of equations arising from the discretisation of the poly-energetic linear Boltzmann transport equation using a discontinuous Galerkin finite element approximation in space, angle, and energy. In particular, we develop preconditioned Richardson iterations which may be understood as generalisations of source iteration in the mono-energetic setting, and derive computable a posteriori bounds for the solver error incurred due to inexact linear algebra, measured in a relevant problem-specific norm. We prove that the convergence of the resulting schemes and a posteriori solver error estimates are independent of the discretisation parameters. We also discuss how the poly-energetic Richardson iteration may be employed as a preconditioner for the generalised minimal residual (GMRES) method. Furthermore, we show that standard implementations of GMRES based on minimising the Euclidean norm of the residual vector can be utilized to yield computable a posteriori solver error estimates at each iteration, through judicious selections of left- and right-preconditioners for the original linear system. The effectiveness of poly-energetic source iteration and preconditioned GMRES, as well as their respective a posteriori solver error estimates, is demonstrated through numerical examples arising in the modelling of photon transport. Keywords: discontinuous Galerkin methods; linear Boltzmann transport equation; iterative solution; algebraic error; error estimates. Mathematics Subject Classification (2020): 65N15, 65N22, 65N30.



1.
Introduction

The linear Boltzmann transport equation models the scattering, absorption and streaming of radiative particles through a medium and is widely used in many applications including radiotherapy treatment planning
[
1
]
, the design of nuclear reactors
[
2
]
and climate sciences
[
3
]
. We consider the stationary form of the equation whose solution, referred to as the
angular flux
, models the (mean) equilibrium distribution of the particle species under consideration; this is dependent on up to six independent variables.

The high dimensionality of the underlying problem has led to the development of a variety of different discretisation approaches for the spatial, angular and energetic components of the problem. For energy, the multigroup method has historically been the predominant discretisation scheme
[
2
]
, whereby a finite number of non-overlapping energy groups are employed and the energetic dependence of the angular flux is approximated by a piecewise-constant (or known) function. Extensions to higher-order approximations in energy include the generalised multigroup method
[
4
,
5
]
and the discontinuous Galerkin method outlined in our recent article
[
6
]
. Importantly, for problems in which particles always lose energy during a scattering event, either by local energy deposition or by production of secondary particles, multigroup-based energetic discretisations permit an ordered solver strategy for the angular flux in each energy group in order of decreasing energy. In terms of the angular discretisation, popular approaches include spherical harmonics methods
[
7
,
2
,
8
]
, discrete ordinates methods
[
2
,
8
]
, and mesh-based approximations constructed using, for example, continuous finite elements
[
9
]
, discontinuous finite elements
[
10
,
6
,
11
]
or wavelets
[
12
]
. Spatially, typically finite element, finite volume, finite difference, and characteristic-based methods are employed.

When the stationary linear Boltzmann transport equation is approximated using a discrete ordinates or mesh-based method in angle and a multigroup method in energy, the resulting linear system is typically very large and thus one must resort to iterative solution methods. Assuming that the physics of the problem is such that the approximate angular flux can be computed sequentially in order of descending energy groups, one needs only to solve a set of discretised mono-energetic problems. The literature for this is vast; see
[
13
]
, for example, and the references therein. Among the most common iterative methods for mono-energetic problems is
source iteration
, which exploits the fact that the system matrix can be decoupled as the sum of a scattering term and an easily-invertible transport operator. The convergence rate of source iteration is characterised by the
scattering ratio

c\in[0,1)
which may be determined from problem data. For highly-scattering media where
c\approx 1
, the convergence of source iteration can be very slow.

To address this issue, a number of acceleration techniques have been proposed. Among the most common technique is diffusion-synthetic acceleration (DSA)
[
14
,
15
]
, in which each source iteration is interspersed with a diffusion problem whose solution is added as a correction to the current angular flux iterate. While DSA is provably and rapidly convergent independently of
c
for some discretised model problems, it is known that the choice of discretisation of the diffusion problem must be consistent with that of the transport problem
[
15
]
. Furthermore, the DSA preconditioner may require special modification to deal with highly heterogeneous media
[
16
]
.

In recent years, purely algebraic techniques based on Krylov subspace methods have been studied. The generalised minimal residual method (GMRES)
[
17
]
and the biconjugate gradient stabilised method (BI-CGSTAB)
[
18
]
have both been employed in radiation transport applications. In
[
19
]
, applications of GMRES employing preconditioners based on incomplete LU factorisations and transport sweeps were shown to be competitive against DSA. In
[
20
]
, GMRES and BI-CGSTAB are employed with Jacobi and block Jacobi preconditioning. In
[
21
]
, it was shown that GMRES, employing a preconditioner based on a partially-consistent DSA scheme (which may not otherwise be convergent as a stationary iterative method), can be more effective than fully-consistent DSA schemes in multidimensional geometries with highly scattering regions.

The original GMRES algorithm in
[
17
]
seeks to successively minimise the
\ell_{2}
-norm of a residual vector associated with a (finite-dimensional) system of linear equations, which is returned alongside the approximate solution upon termination of the algorithm. Generalisations of the GMRES method to Hilbert space settings have been made in
[
22
,
23
]
, and in the finite-dimensional case the authors of
[
24
]
present a modification of GMRES in which the vector norm used in the residual minimisation condition is induced by a positive-definite matrix. This last point is significant since, in finite element applications, the vector solution of the underlying system of equations is commonly the set of coefficients in the expansion of a finite element basis - in this setting, the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the residual vector may not be a useful measure of the error between the true finite element solution and its approximation obtained by inexact solution of the discrete equations
[
25
]
. The problem of obtaining meaningful measures of functional error incurred by premature termination of an iterative linear solver is especially important in the context of adaptive finite element methods; see
[
26
]
for a survey of the interplay between discretisation and algebraic error estimation.

In this article, we consider the numerical solution of the system of linear equations arising from a full space-angle-energy discretisation of the linear Boltzmann transport equation employing the discontinuous Galerkin finite element method (DGFEM) outlined in our previous article
[
6
]
. Here, we generalise the source iteration method to the poly-energetic setting, including a poly-energetic analogue to the mono-energetic scattering ratio, and employ the resulting scheme as a preconditioner within a GMRES method. We also discuss a simple modification of mono-energetic source iteration that exhibits faster convergence rates at no extra cost. The convergence of the poly-energetic and generalised mono-energetic source iteration schemes is proven and computable
a posteriori
solver error estimates are derived, which are guaranteed to bound an associated problem-dependent norm of the functional error in a mesh-independent manner. The practical implementation of these error bounds is also discussed; in particular, we show how residual-based
a posteriori
solver error estimates may be incorporated into standard implementations of GMRES in such a way that the resulting method sequentially minimises the error estimate at each iteration. Numerical experiments illustrating the practical performance of each solver are presented: we observe that, using realistic photon scattering models, the convergence rates of poly-energetic source iteration and preconditioned GMRES, as well as their respective
a posteriori
solver error estimates, are highly dependent on the energy range. In particular, GMRES is particularly effective in the low-energy photon scattering regime where the convergence of source iteration stagnates.

The outline of this paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we introduce the stationary linear Boltzmann transport equation. Then, in Section
3
, we summarise its high-order space-angle-energy DGFEM discretisation as presented in
[
6
]
. In Section
4
, we introduce and discuss a number of iterative methods for the solution of the resulting linear system of equations and provide convergence proofs and
a posteriori
solver error bounds. Section
5
discusses the implementation of the proposed iterative methods and their corresponding error estimates. Section
6
presents numerical experiments which demonstrate the performance of the proposed iterative methods for both mono-energetic and poly-energetic problems. Finally, in Section
7
we summarise the work presented in this article and highlight some areas of future work.

2.
Model Problem

For
d=2,3
, let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote an open bounded Lipschitz spatial domain and
\mathbb{S}=\{\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:|\bm{\mu}|_{2}=1\}
denote the surface of the
d
-dimensional unit sphere, where
|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the
\ell_{2}
-norm. Given
0\leq E_{\min}<E_{\max}<\infty
, let
\mathbb{E}=\left[E_{\min},E_{\max}\right]
and
\mathcal{D}=\Omega\times\mathbb{S}\times\mathbb{E}
. The linear Boltzmann transport problem is given by: find
u:\mathcal{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfying

(1)

\displaystyle\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u+(\alpha+\beta)u

\displaystyle=S[u]+f\textnormal{ in }\mathcal{D},

(2)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=g_{D}\textnormal{ on }\Gamma_{in}.

Here,
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
denotes the spatial gradient operator,
\alpha
and
\beta
denote given data terms (described below),
f
is a forcing term,
g_{D}
is a specified (inlet) boundary condition, and
\Gamma_{in}=\{(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu},E)\in\overline{\mathcal{D}}:\mathbf{x}\in\partial\Omega\textnormal{ and }\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}(\mathbf{x})<0\}
denotes the inflow boundary of
\mathcal{D}
, where
\mathbf{n}
is the outward unit normal vector to
\Omega
on
\partial\Omega
. The operator
scattering operator

S[u]
is defined by

(3)

S[u](\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\rightarrow E)u(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime},E^{\prime})\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\,\textnormal{d}E^{\prime},

where
\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\rightarrow E)
is a non-negative scattering kernel satisfying
\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\rightarrow E)=0
for
E^{\prime}<E
.
The
macroscopic scattering cross-section

\beta
is given by

(4)

\beta(\mathbf{x},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime},E\rightarrow E^{\prime})\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\,\textnormal{d}E^{\prime}.

Here,
\alpha
, referred to as the
macroscopic absorption cross-section
, is assumed to be non-negative and independent of the angular variable; i.e.,
\alpha(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu},E)\equiv\alpha(\mathbf{x},E)
. We remark that such assumptions are valid for isotropic media. Finally, we assume that there exists a constant
\alpha_{0}>0
such that

(5)

\overline{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},E):=\alpha(\mathbf{x},E)+\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}\left(\beta(\mathbf{x},E)-\gamma(\mathbf{x},E)\right)\geq\alpha_{0},

where

(6)

\gamma(\mathbf{x},E)=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\rightarrow E)\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\,\textnormal{d}E^{\prime}.

We remark that, in applications to photon transport, one typically has
\gamma(\mathbf{x},E)\leq\beta(\mathbf{x},E)

[
27
]
, and so (
5
) holds on a finite energy domain
0<E_{min}<E_{max}<\infty
even when
\alpha=0
.

3.
Discretisation

Following
[
6
]
, we discretise the Boltzmann transport problem (
1
)-(
2
) using a DGFEM approach; we recall the key constructions below.

3.1.
Spatial discretisation

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
denote a subdivision of the spatial domain
\Omega
into open disjoint elements
\kappa_{\Omega}
such that
\overline{\Omega}=\bigcup_{\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\overline{\kappa_{\Omega}}
. Given
p\geq 0
, the spatial finite element space is defined by

(7)

\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}=\left\{v\in L_{2}(\Omega):v|_{\kappa_{\Omega}}\in\mathbb{P}_{p}(\kappa_{\Omega})\textnormal{ for all }\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\right\},

where
\mathbb{P}_{p}(\kappa_{\Omega})
denotes the space of polynomials of total degree
p
on
\kappa_{\Omega}
.

For a given element
\kappa_{\Omega}
, the union of its
(d-1)
-dimensional open faces is denoted by
\partial\kappa_{\Omega}
. For a given direction
\bm{\mu}\in\mathbb{S}
, the inflow and outflow boundaries of
\kappa_{\Omega}
are defined, respectively, by

\displaystyle\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\kappa_{\Omega}:\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\kappa_{\Omega}}(\mathbf{x})<0\right\},\quad\partial_{+}\kappa_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\partial\kappa_{\Omega}:\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\kappa_{\Omega}}(\mathbf{x})\geq 0\right\},

where
\mathbf{n}_{\kappa_{\Omega}}(\mathbf{x})
denotes the outward unit vector to
\kappa_{\Omega}
at
\mathbf{x}\in\partial\kappa_{\Omega}
.

Given a face
F\subset\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega
,
\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
, we define the upwind jump of a (sufficiently smooth) function
v
across F by

\lfloor v\rfloor=v^{+}-v^{-},

where
v^{\pm}
denotes the interior, respectively, exterior trace of
v
on
F
, relative to
\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
.

3.2.
Angular discretisation

We shall employ the widely-used cube-sphere discretisation of the angular domain. To this end, let
\mathbb{S}_{h}
denote the closed polygonal/polyhedral surface of the
d
-dimensional cube in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, respectively. We write
\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}=\{\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}\}
to denote a surface mesh consisting of tensor product elements
\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}
, i.e., so that
\mathbb{S}_{h}=\bigcup_{\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}}\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}
.
Letting
\phi_{\mathbb{S}}:\mathbb{S}_{h}\rightarrow\mathbb{S}
denote the smooth invertible mapping defined for any
\tilde{\bm{\mu}}\in\mathbb{S}_{h}
by
\phi_{\mathbb{S}}(\tilde{\bm{\mu}})=|\tilde{\bm{\mu}}|_{2}^{-1}\tilde{\bm{\mu}}
, we define a mesh
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}
consisting of curved surface elements on
\mathbb{S}
by

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}=\{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}=\phi_{\mathbb{S}}(\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}):\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}\in\tilde{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}}\}
. Then, given
q\geq 0
, the angular finite element space is defined by

(8)

\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}=\left\{v\in L_{2}(\mathbb{S}):v|_{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}=\tilde{v}\circ\phi_{\mathbb{S}}^{-1}(\kappa_{\mathbb{S}})\textnormal{ with }\tilde{v}\in\mathbb{Q}_{q}(\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}})\textnormal{ for all }\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}\right\},

where
\mathbb{Q}_{q}(\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}})
denotes the space of polynomials of degree at most
q
in each of the
(d-1)
coordinate directions parametrising
\tilde{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}
.

3.3.
Energetic discretisation

For
N_{\mathbb{E}}\geq 1
, let
E_{\max}:=E_{0}>E_{1}>\dots>E_{N_{\mathbb{E}}}=:E_{\min}
denote a partition of the energy domain
\mathbb{E}
into
N_{\mathbb{E}}

energy groups
. The interval
\kappa_{g}=(E_{g},E_{g-1})
,
1\leq g\leq N_{\mathbb{E}}
, is referred to as the
g^{th}
energy group, and we define by
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}=\left\{\kappa_{g}\right\}_{g=1}^{N_{\mathbb{E}}}
to be the energetic mesh. Given
r\geq 0
, the energetic finite element space is defined by

(9)

\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{E}}=\left\{v\in L_{2}(\mathbb{E}):v|_{\kappa_{g}}\in\mathbb{P}_{r}(\kappa_{g})\textnormal{ for all }\kappa_{g}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}\right\}.

3.4.
Discontinuous Galerkin method

Using the definitions of
\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}
,
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}
outlined in the previous sections, we define the full space-angle-energy mesh
\mathcal{T}
by

(10)

\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}=\left\{\kappa=\kappa_{\Omega}\times\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}\times\kappa_{g}:\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega},\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}},\kappa_{g}\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{E}}\right\}.

We combine the separate finite element spaces to define the space-angle-energy finite element space

\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{E}}.

We also define the broken graph space
\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
by

(11)

\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})=\left\{v\in L_{2}(\mathcal{D}):\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v|_{\kappa}\in L_{2}(\kappa)\textnormal{ for all }\kappa\in\mathcal{T}\right\}.

The DGFEM scheme reads as follows: find
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
such that

(12)

a(u_{h},v_{h})=s(u_{h},v_{h})+\ell(v_{h})

for all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
, where
a:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\times\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
s:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\times\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and
\ell:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
are defined, respectively, for all
w,v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
, by

\displaystyle a(w,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\Bigg[\sum_{\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\int_{\kappa_{\Omega}}(-\bm{\mu}w\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v+(\alpha+\beta)wv)\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\quad\quad+\int_{\partial_{+}\kappa_{\Omega}}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}|w^{+}v^{+}\,\textnormal{d}s-\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}|w^{-}v^{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\Bigg]\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E,

\displaystyle s(w,v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}S[w]v\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E,

\displaystyle\ell(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\left[\sum_{\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\int_{\kappa_{\Omega}}fv\ \,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}+\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}|g_{D}v^{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\right]\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E.

4.
Analysis of Iterative Solvers

In this section we study the convergence of iterative solvers for the DGFEM scheme (
12
). Following
[
6
]
, we introduce the DGFEM-energy norm
|||\cdot|||_{DG}:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined for all
v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
by

\displaystyle|||v|||_{DG}^{2}

\displaystyle=||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}v||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\sum_{\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}\left(||v^{+}-v^{-}||_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega}^{2}+||v^{+}||_{\partial\kappa_{\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\right)\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E,

where the (semi)norm
||v||_{\omega}^{2}=\int_{\omega}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}_{\kappa_{\Omega}}|v^{2}\,\textnormal{d}s
for
\omega\subset\partial\kappa_{\Omega}
. We recall the following theorem from
[
6
]
.

Theorem 1
.

The DGFEM bilinear form
a(\cdot,\cdot)-s(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive with respect to the DGFEM-energy norm in the sense that

(13)

|||v_{h}|||_{DG}^{2}\leq a(v_{h},v_{h})-s(v_{h},v_{h})

for all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
.

Remark 2
.

The above coercivity result trivially holds for all
v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
.

For the purposes of simplifying the presentation of the forthcoming linear solvers, we endow
\mathbb{V}
with an inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{V}\times\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
and define the linear operators
A,S:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
, for all
w_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
, respectively, by

(14)

\displaystyle\langle Aw_{h},v_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=a(w_{h},v_{h})\textnormal{ for all }v_{h}\in\mathbb{V},

(15)

\displaystyle\langle Sw_{h},v_{h}\rangle

\displaystyle=s(w_{h},v_{h})\textnormal{ for all }v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}.

Furthermore, by defining
F\in\mathbb{V}
by

(16)

\langle F,v_{h}\rangle=\ell(v_{h})\textnormal{ for all }v_{h}\in\mathbb{V},

the DGFEM (
12
) can be equivalently defined: find
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
such that

(17)

(A-S)u_{h}=F.

For the analysis of eigenvalues of linear operators on
\mathbb{V}
, we introduce a finite element space
\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{C}}
of complex-valued discontinuous piecewise-polynomial functions, which may be understood as the linear span of functions in
\mathbb{V}
over the field
\mathbb{C}
. In such cases, the definitions of the bilinear forms
a
and
s
, as well as the linear operators
A
and
S
, are extended in a trivial way, and now
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{C}}\times\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{C}}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
denotes a sesquilinear form which is linear in the first argument and antilinear in the second argument.

4.1.
Source iteration

Source iteration seeks to solve the following sequence of DGFEM problems: given
u_{h}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{V}
, find
\{u_{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{V}
such that

(18)

Au_{h}^{(n+1)}=Su_{h}^{(n)}+F,

for
n\geq 0
. We briefly remark that (
18
) is well-posed and that
a(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive with respect to the norm
|||\cdot|||_{a}:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined for all
v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
by

(19)

\displaystyle|||v|||_{a}^{2}

\displaystyle=||\sqrt{\alpha+\beta}v||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}

(20)

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\left(\sum_{\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}||v^{+}-v^{-}||_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega}^{2}+||v^{+}||_{\partial\kappa_{\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\right)\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E

in the sense that

(21)

|||v|||_{a}^{2}=a(v,v)\qquad\forall v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T}).

Theorem 3
.

Let
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
denote the exact solution to the DGFEM problem (
12
) and
\{u_{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{V}
denote the sequence of approximate solutions to
u_{h}
defined by (
18
). Then we have that

(22)

|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{a}\leq\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}\ |||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)}|||_{a}

for all
n\geq 0
, where

\displaystyle q_{\beta}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,E\in\mathbb{E}}\ \frac{\beta(\mathbf{x},E)}{\alpha(\mathbf{x},E)+\beta(\mathbf{x},E)},\qquad q_{\gamma}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,E\in\mathbb{E}}\ \frac{\gamma(\mathbf{x},E)}{\alpha(\mathbf{x},E)+\beta(\mathbf{x},E)}.

Thus, we have the following
a posteriori
solver error bound:

(23)

|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{DG}\leq\sqrt{r_{\gamma}}\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n)}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

for all
n\geq 0
, where

r_{\gamma}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,E\in\mathbb{E}}\ \frac{\gamma(\mathbf{x},E)}{\overline{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},E)}.

Proof.

Starting from the coercivity result (
21
), we have, using (
18
), that

\displaystyle|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{a}^{2}

\displaystyle=a(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=s(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}).

Invoking the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we obtain

\displaystyle s(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle\quad\quad=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}S[u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)}](u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})\ \,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\ ||\sqrt{\gamma}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\quad\quad\leq\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}\ |||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)}|||_{a}\ |||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{a}.

The first part of the theorem now follows directly. To derive the
a posteriori
error bound, we note that, by using Theorem
1
, we have

\displaystyle|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{DG}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})-s(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=s(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle\leq||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n)}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\ ||\sqrt{\gamma}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{r_{\gamma}}\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n)}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\ |||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{DG},

as required.
∎

Corollary 4
.

If
\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}<1
, the sequence of approximate solutions
\{u_{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{V}
generated by (
18
) satisfies
u_{h}^{(n)}\rightarrow u_{h}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Motivated by the operator form (
18
), we note that source iteration can be understood as a preconditioned Richardson iteration of the form

(24)

u_{h}^{(n+1)}=u_{h}^{(n)}+P^{-1}\left(F-(A-S)u_{h}^{(n)}\right),

where the preconditioner
P^{-1}=A^{-1}
. The contraction factor

(25)

c=\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}=\sqrt{\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,E\in\mathbb{E}}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x},E)}{\alpha(\mathbf{x},E)+\beta(\mathbf{x},E)}\cdot\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega,E\in\mathbb{E}}\frac{\gamma(\mathbf{x},E)}{\alpha(\mathbf{x},E)+\beta(\mathbf{x},E)}}

may be understood as a poly-energetic analogue of the mono-energetic scattering ratio
c_{mono}

[
13
]
:

(26)

c_{mono}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\alpha(\mathbf{x})+\beta(\mathbf{x})}.

We also note that the spectral radius
\rho(G)
of the source iteration operator
G=A^{-1}S
satisfies
\rho(G)\leq c
, as suggested by Theorem
3
. To see this, let
(\lambda,w_{h})\in(\mathbb{C},\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{C}})
be an eigenvalue-eigenvector pair of
G
, so that we have

A^{-1}Sw_{h}=\lambda w_{h}.

It can be shown that a characterisation of
\lambda
is given by

\lambda=s(w_{h},w_{h}^{*})/a(w_{h},w_{h}^{*}).

The boundedness of
|\lambda|
follows by straightforward considerations.

In the mono-energetic case that, it can be shown that, provided the bilinear form
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric positive-semidefinite, the spectrum of
A^{-1}S
lies in a disc
D(a,r)
of radius
r=\frac{c}{2}=\frac{c_{mono}}{2}
centred at
r=\frac{c}{2}=\frac{c_{mono}}{2}

[
27
]
. This is because, by the characterisation above, any
\lambda\in\sigma(A^{-1}S)
can be written in the following form for some
w_{h}=x_{h}+iy_{h}
with
x_{h},y_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
:

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{s(w_{h},w_{h}^{*})}{a(w_{h},w_{h}^{*})}=\frac{s(x_{h},x_{h})+s(y_{h},y_{h})}{a(x_{h},x_{h})+a(y_{h},y_{h})+i\left[a(y_{h},x_{h})-a(x_{h},y_{h})\right]},

where the symmetry of
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is used to simplify the numerator and all bilinear forms are now real-valued. The bounds on
\sigma(A^{-1}S)
in the complex plane are then shown after some simple considerations
[
27
]
.

4.1.1.
Generalised source iteration for mono-energetic problems

In the case where the energetic dependence of the test and trial functions is suppressed, we consider the following modification of (
18
): given
u_{h}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{V}
and
0<\omega<1
, find
\{u_{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{V}
such that

(27)

(A-\omega M)u_{h}^{(n+1)}=(S-\omega M)u_{h}^{(n)}+F,

for
n\geq 0
, where the operator
M:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{V}
is defined for all
w_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
by

(28)

\langle Mw_{h},v_{h}\rangle=m(w_{h},v_{h})\textnormal{ for all }v_{h}\in\mathbb{V},

and

(29)

m(w,v)=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})v(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}.

In view of developing a convergence theory analogous to that of Theorem
3
, we introduce the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Assuming that
\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime})
admits the form

\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\theta_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})P_{\ell}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime}),

where
P_{\ell}
denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree
\ell

[
8
]
, we have that

(30)

|s(w,v)-\omega m(w,v)|\leq r(\theta,\omega)m(w,w)^{\frac{1}{2}}m(v,v)^{\frac{1}{2}}

for all
w,v\in L_{2}(\Omega\times\mathbb{S})
, where

r(\theta,\omega)=\sup\left\{\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\left|\frac{\theta_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})}{\beta(\mathbf{x})}-\omega\right|:0\leq\ell<\infty\right\}.

Proof.

It is convenient to prove the more general inequality

|s(w,v^{*})-\omega m(w,v^{*})|\leq r(\theta,\omega)m(w,w^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}m(v,v^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}

in the case where
w
and
v
are possibly complex-valued and
d=3
; the lemma is then proven by selecting
w
and
v
to be real-valued. Here, we use the notation
v^{*}
to denote the complex conjugate of a complex-valued function
v\in L_{2}(\Omega\times\mathbb{S})
. The proof generalises trivially to the case
d=2
.

Let
w
and
v
admit the following spherical harmonics decompositions:

\displaystyle w(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}w_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})Y_{\ell,m}(\bm{\mu}),

\displaystyle v(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}v_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})Y_{\ell,m}(\bm{\mu}),

where
\{Y_{\ell,m}:0\leq\ell<\infty,-\ell\leq m\leq\ell\}
denotes the set of spherical harmonics functions satisfying the following orthogonality property on
L_{2}(\mathbb{S})
:

\int_{\mathbb{S}}Y_{\ell,m}(\bm{\mu})Y_{\ell^{\prime},m^{\prime}}^{*}(\bm{\mu})\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}=\frac{4\pi}{2\ell+1}\delta_{\ell,\ell^{\prime}}\delta_{m,m^{\prime}}

and
\delta_{ij}
denotes the Kronecker delta.

Substituting these expressions for
w
and
v
into
m(w,v^{*})
yields

\displaystyle m(w,v^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{x})w(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})v^{*}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{x})w_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})v_{\ell,m}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}.

Substituting the expressions for
w
,
v
and
\theta
(defined above) into
s(w,v^{*})
, and noting that the Legendre polynomials
P_{\ell}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime})
satisfy

P_{\ell}(\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime})=\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}Y_{\ell,m}^{*}(\bm{\mu}^{\prime})Y_{\ell,m}(\bm{\mu}),

we have

\displaystyle s(w,v^{*})

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}\cdot\bm{\mu}^{\prime})w(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime})v^{*}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}

\displaystyle=\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}\frac{2r+1}{4\pi}\sum_{s=-r}^{r}\int_{\Omega}\theta_{r}(\mathbf{x})w_{r,s}(\mathbf{x})v_{r,s}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}.

Hence, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|s(w,v^{*})-\omega m(w,v^{*})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\int_{\Omega}\left(\theta_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})-\omega\beta(\mathbf{x})\right)w_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})v_{\ell,m}^{*}(\mathbf{x})\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\int_{\Omega}\left|\theta_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})-\omega\beta(\mathbf{x})\right|\left|w_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})\right|\left|v_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})\right|\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\sup_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\left|\frac{\theta_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})-\omega\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\beta(\mathbf{x})}\right|}_{=:r(\theta,\omega)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\int_{\Omega}\beta(\mathbf{x})\left|w_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})\right|\left|v_{\ell,m}(\mathbf{x})\right|\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle\leq r(\theta,\omega)\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\|\sqrt{\beta}\,w_{\ell,m}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}\|\sqrt{\beta}\,v_{\ell,m}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq r(\theta,\omega)\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\|\sqrt{\beta}\,w_{\ell,m}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{2\ell+1}{4\pi}\sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell}\|\sqrt{\beta}\,v_{\ell,m}\|_{L_{2}(\Omega)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}

\displaystyle=r(\theta,\omega)m(w,w^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}m(v,v^{*})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

∎

Remark 6
.

We point out that
\beta=\theta_{0}
. Moreover, if the differential scattering cross-section satisfies Mercer’s condition
[
28
]
(so that
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric and positive-semidefinite), we have that
r(\theta,\omega)=\max\{\omega,1-\omega\}
.

Before we analyse the convergence of the iteration (
27
), we briefly remark that it is well-posed and that, for
0\leq\omega<1
,
a(\cdot,\cdot)-\omega m(\cdot,\cdot)
is coercive with respect to the norm
|||v|||_{a,\omega}:\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined for all
v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
by:

\displaystyle|||v|||_{a,\omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=||\sqrt{\alpha+(1-\omega)\beta}v||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\left(\sum_{\kappa_{\Omega}\in\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}}||v^{+}-v^{-}||_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega}^{2}+||v^{+}||_{\partial\kappa_{\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}^{2}\right)\ \,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}

in the sense that

|||v|||_{a,\omega}^{2}=a(v,v)-\omega m(v,v)

for all
v\in\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{T})
. Note that
|||\cdot|||_{a}=|||\cdot|||_{a,0}
.

Theorem 7
.

Let
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
denote the exact solution to the DGFEM problem (
12
) and
\{u_{h}^{(n)}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}\subset\mathbb{V}
denote the sequence of approximations to
u_{h}
defined by (
27
). Then we have

(31)

|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{a,\omega}\leq r(\theta,\omega)q_{\beta,\omega}|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)}|||_{a,\omega}

for all
n\geq 0
, where

q_{\beta,\omega}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\alpha(\mathbf{x})+(1-\omega)\beta(\mathbf{x})}.

Thus, we have the following
a posteriori
solver error estimate:

(32)

|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{DG}\leq r(\theta,\omega)\sqrt{r_{\beta}}||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

for all
n\geq 0
, where

(33)

r_{\beta}=\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\alpha(\mathbf{x})}.

Proof.

The proof closely follows that of Theorem
3
, albeit in the mono-energetic case, whereby we exploit the coercivity of
a(\cdot,\cdot)-\omega m(\cdot,\cdot)
with respect to
|||\cdot|||_{a,\omega}
. Using Lemma
5
and (
27
), we have

\displaystyle|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{a,\omega}^{2}

\displaystyle=a(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})-\omega m(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=s(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})-\omega m(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle\leq r(\theta,\omega)\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq r(\theta,\omega)\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\alpha(\mathbf{x})+(1-\omega)\beta(\mathbf{x})}\ |||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n)}|||_{a,\omega}\ |||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{a,\omega},

as required. To show the
a posteriori
error bound, we have

\displaystyle|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{DG}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})-s(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle=s(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})-\omega m(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)},u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})

\displaystyle\leq r(\theta,\omega)\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq r(\theta,\omega)\sqrt{\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\overline{\alpha}(\mathbf{x})}}\ ||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\ ||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}||_{DG}.

Noting that the integrals over
\mathbb{E}
appearing in (
4
) and (
6
) are removed in the mono-energetic case, and that
\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\mu},E^{\prime}\rightarrow E)=\theta(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu}^{\prime}\cdot\bm{\mu})
, we have that
\beta=\gamma
and so (
5
) reduces to
\overline{\alpha}=\alpha
. The second part of the theorem is proven on rearrangement.
∎

Corollary 8
.

If
r(\theta,\omega)q_{\beta,\omega}<1
, the sequence of approximate solutions
\{u_{h}^{(n)}\}\subset\mathbb{V}
generated by (
27
) satisfies
u_{h}^{(n)}\rightarrow u_{h}
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

As in the case of source iteration, we note that generalised source iteration can be understood as a preconditioned Richardson iteration of the form given in (
24
) where now the preconditioner
P^{-1}=(A-\omega M)^{-1}
. Likewise, we also note that the spectral radius
\rho(G_{\omega})
of the generalised source iteration operator
G_{\omega}=(A-\omega M)^{-1}(S-\omega M)
satisfies

where

c_{mono,\omega}=r(\theta,\omega)\cdot\sup_{\mathbf{x}\in\Omega}\frac{\beta(\mathbf{x})}{\alpha(\mathbf{x})+(1-\omega)\beta(\mathbf{x})}=\frac{r(\theta,\omega)c_{mono}}{1-\omega c_{mono}},

as suggested by Theorem
7
. The proof follows identically as in the case of standard (poly-energetic) source iteration.

Under the assumptions on

in Remark
6
, it can be shown that the spectrum of

lies in the union of two discs
D(a_{1},r_{1})\cup D(a_{2},r_{2})
of radii
r_{1}=\frac{(1-\omega)c_{mono}}{2(1-\omega c_{mono})}
,
r_{2}=\frac{\omega c_{mono}}{2(1-\omega c_{mono})}
and centred at
a_{1}=\frac{(1-\omega)c_{mono}}{2(1-\omega c_{mono})}
,
a_{2}=\frac{-\omega c_{mono}}{2(1-\omega c_{mono})}
, respectively, cf.
[

]
.

4.2.
GMRES

The generalised minimal residual (GMRES) method
[
17
]
is one of the most popular Krylov subspace methods for large, nonsymmetric sparse systems of linear equations and has been proposed for neutron transport applications in
[
19
]
. Given the problem

(34)

Ax=b,

where
x
and
b
are elements of a (possibly infinite-dimensional) complex separable Hilbert space
X
endowed with an inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
,
A:X\rightarrow X
denotes a bounded and invertible linear operator, and
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0}
denotes the initial residual induced by an initial guess
x_{0}\in X
, GMRES constructs a sequence of Krylov subspaces
\{K_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
, where

K_{n}=\textnormal{span}\{r_{0},Ar_{0},\dots,A^{n-1}r_{0}\}.

At iteration
n
, the
n^{th}
iterate
x_{n}\in X
is chosen to satisfy

(35)

x_{n}={\arg\min}_{y\in x_{0}+K_{n}}||b-Ay||_{X}

and GMRES computes the residual error
||r_{n}||_{X}=||b-Ax_{n}||_{X}
as a byproduct. Typically,
X
is usually taken to be
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{C}^{n}
and the inner product
(\cdot,\cdot)_{X}
is taken to be the Euclidean inner product; generalisations to other Hilbert spaces have been considered, cf.,
[
22
,
23
]
.

When
X=\mathbb{R}^{n}
endowed with the usual inner product and
A=Q\Lambda Q^{-1}
represents a diagonalisable matrix with
\Lambda=\textnormal{diag}\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}\}
a diagonal matrix of eigenvalues and
Q
a matrix of corresponding eigenvectors, the residual error at the
k^{th}
iteration of GMRES satisfies
[
29
]

\frac{||r_{k}||_{2}}{||r_{0}||_{2}}\leq||Q||_{2}||Q^{-1}||_{2}\min_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k},p(0)=1}\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|p(\lambda_{i})|,

where
\mathbb{P}_{k}
denotes the set of polynomials of degree
k
with leading coefficient equal to 1. In particular, if
\sigma(A)
is contained in the disc
D(a,r)
centred at
a\in\mathbb{C}
with radius
r>0
, then we have

\displaystyle\min_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k},p(0)=1}\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|p(\lambda_{i})|

\displaystyle\leq\min_{p\in\mathbb{P}_{k},p(0)=1}\max_{\lambda\in D(a,r)}|p(\lambda)|

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\lambda\in D(a,r)}\left|\left(1-\frac{z}{a}\right)^{k}\right|=\left(\frac{r}{|a|}\right)^{k}.

While we do not give a formal proof of convergence of GMRES applied to the operator equation (
17
), we point out that pre-multiplying the equation by
A^{-1}
yields the following (equivalent) problem for
u_{h}
:

(36)

(I-A^{-1}S)u_{h}=A^{-1}F.

By earlier remarks, we recall that
\sigma(I-A^{-1}S)\subset D(a,r)
with
a=1
and
r=\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}
; moreover, if
s(\cdot,\cdot)
is symmetric positive-semidefinite, we have that
\sigma(I-A^{-1}S)\subset D(a,r)
with
a=1-\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}
and
r=\frac{1}{2}\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}
. Thus, GMRES applied to the preconditioned operator equation (
36
) yields

\frac{||r_{k}||_{2}}{||r_{0}||_{2}}\leq||Q||_{2}||Q^{-1}||_{2}\left(q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}\right)^{\frac{k}{2}}

in the former case and

\frac{||r_{k}||_{2}}{||r_{0}||_{2}}\leq||Q||_{2}||Q^{-1}||_{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}}{2-\sqrt{q_{\beta}q_{\gamma}}}\right)^{k}

in the latter case.

As remarked in
[
25
]
, the
\ell_{2}
-norm of the residual
r_{k}
is not always the best norm in which to measure the true solver error in finite element applications. In Section
5
, we discuss how the operator equation (
34
) may be modified in order for standard implementations of GMRES to solve the minimisation problem (
35
) in the case where
X=(\mathbb{V},||\cdot||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D};\overline{\alpha})})
denotes a finite element space equipped with a weighted
L_{2}
-norm
||\cdot||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D};\overline{\alpha})}
.

4.3.
Residual-based
a posteriori
solver error estimation

For any
w_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
, we introduce the
residual functional

R[w_{h}]:\mathbb{V}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined for all
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
by

(37)

R[w_{h}](v_{h})=\ell(v_{h})-\left[a(w_{h},v_{h})-s(w_{h},v_{h})\right].

We remark that (
37
), as well as the theorem below, hold in both the poly-energetic or mono-energetic settings with appropriate modifications to the finite element space
\mathbb{V}
.

Theorem 9
.

Let
\hat{u}_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
denote an approximation to the exact solution
u_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
of (
12
). Then the following
a posteriori
error estimate holds:

(38)

|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||_{DG}\leq\sqrt{\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{T}}\eta_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{h})^{2}}=||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})},

where
\eta_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{h})=||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})||_{L_{2}(\kappa)}
and
r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})\in\mathbb{V}
denotes the unique solution to the variational problem

(39)

(\overline{\alpha}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h}),v_{h})_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}=R[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h})\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||_{DG}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq a(u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h},u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})-s(u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h},u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})

\displaystyle=\ell(u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})-\left[a(\hat{u}_{h},u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})-s(\hat{u}_{h},u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})\right]

\displaystyle=R[\hat{u}_{h}](u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})=(\overline{\alpha}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h}),u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h})_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{T}}||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})||_{L_{2}(\kappa)}^{2}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||_{DG},

as required.
∎

Remark 10
.

The computation of the local solver error estimates
\eta_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{h})
requires the solution
r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})
of (
39
). This can be achieved via local problems on each
\kappa\in\mathcal{T}
as follows. Let
\mathbb{V}_{\kappa}\subset\mathbb{V}
denote the finite element subspace of piecewise-polynomial functions supported on
\kappa=\kappa_{\Omega}\times\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}\times\kappa_{g}
. The local problem for
r_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{h}):=r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})|_{\kappa}
reads as follows: find
r_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{h})\in\mathbb{V}_{\kappa}
such that

(\overline{\alpha}r_{\kappa}(\hat{u}_{h}),v_{h})_{L_{2}(\kappa)}=R_{\kappa}[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h})\quad\forall v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}_{\kappa},

where we note that
R[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h})
(with
v_{h}\in\mathbb{V}
) can be written in the form

R[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h})=\sum_{\kappa\in\mathcal{T}}R_{\kappa}[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h}),

with each
R_{\kappa}[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h})
given by

\displaystyle R_{\kappa}[\hat{u}_{h}](v_{h})

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa_{g}}\int_{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}\int_{\kappa_{\Omega}}\left(f+S[\hat{u}_{h}]-\bm{\mu}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\hat{u}_{h}-(\alpha+\beta)\hat{u}_{h}\right)v_{h}\,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\kappa_{g}}\int_{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\setminus\partial\Omega}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}|(\hat{u}_{h}^{-}-\hat{u}_{h}^{+})v_{h}^{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E

\displaystyle\quad\quad+\int_{\kappa_{g}}\int_{\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}}\int_{\partial_{-}\kappa_{\Omega}\cap\partial\Omega}|\bm{\mu}\cdot\mathbf{n}|(g_{D}-\hat{u}_{h}^{+})v_{h}^{+}\,\textnormal{d}s\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E.

5.
Implementation

We give a brief overview of the practical implementations of the source iteration, generalised source iteration and GMRES
a posteriori
solver error estimates. The starting point for all three methods is a matrix representation of the DGFEM scheme (
12
). Denoting by
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset\mathbb{V}
,
N=\dim\mathbb{V}
, a basis of the space-angle (or space-angle-energy) finite element space, we introduce the matrices
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{S},\mathbf{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
with entries

(\mathbf{A})_{ij}=a(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})
,
(\mathbf{S})_{ij}=s(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})
,
(\mathbf{M})_{ij}=m(\phi_{j},\phi_{i})
, respectively, and the vector
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
, where
(\mathbf{F})_{i}=\ell(\phi_{i})
. Thereby, (
12
) can be recast as the linear equation

(40)

\mathbf{Au}=\mathbf{Su}+\mathbf{F}

for the vector of coefficients
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
in the expansion of
u_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{u})_{i}\phi_{i}
.

Since the dimension of the finite element space
N
may be very large, it is infeasible to store
\mathbf{A}
,
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{M}
in full. We instead opt for matrix-free actions of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{S}
on vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, cf.
[
6
]
, in the case of source iteration, or matrix-free actions of
(\mathbf{A}-\omega\mathbf{M})^{-1}
and
\mathbf{S}-\omega\mathbf{M}
on vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
in the case of generalised source iteration. Since
\mathbf{M}
is a block-diagonal matrix with each block corresponding to a space-angle element, we remark that the action
(\mathbf{A}-\omega\mathbf{M})^{-1}
(respectively,
\mathbf{S}-\omega\mathbf{M}
) on a vector is not more computationally challenging than that of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
(respectively,
\mathbf{S}
).

5.1.
Source iteration and generalised source iteration

The source iteration scheme, as well as its generalised variant in the mono-energetic case, can be expressed in the form

(41)

\mathbf{u}_{n+1}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{Ku}_{n}+\mathbf{F}),

where
\mathbf{u}_{n}
(respectively,
\mathbf{u}_{n+1}
) denotes the expansion of
u_{h}^{(n)}
(respectively,
u_{h}^{(n+1)}
) in the basis of
\mathbb{V}
. The matrices
\mathbf{P},\mathbf{K}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
are given, respectively, by
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{S}
in the source iteration case and by
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{A}-\omega\mathbf{M}
and
\mathbf{K}=\mathbf{S}-\omega\mathbf{M}
in the generalised source iteration case. It can be shown that, using the initial condition
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\bm{0}
, the
n^{th}
iterate can be expressed as

(42)

\mathbf{u}_{n}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\mathbf{G}^{k}\right)\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{F},

where
\mathbf{G}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{K}
.
It is convenient to recast this iteration as

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{u}_{n}+\mathbf{r}_{n},

\displaystyle\mathbf{r}_{n+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{Gr}_{n},

with initial conditions
\mathbf{u}_{0}=\bm{0}
and
\mathbf{r}_{0}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{F}
, in which case it is equivalent to (
41
). It can be shown that this iteration generates the same iterates (
42
) as well as a sequence of preconditioned residuals
\mathbf{r}_{n}=\mathbf{P}^{-1}(\mathbf{F}-(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{S})\mathbf{u}_{n})
.

Since the
a posteriori
error estimates of Theorems
3
and
7
are of the form

|||u_{h}-u_{h}^{(n+1)}|||_{DG}\leq c||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n)}-u_{h}^{(n+1)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})},

we need only compute the constant
c
for the given problem and solver and assemble the
a posteriori
error estimate element-wise. Furthermore, since

u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf{r}_{n})_{i}\phi_{i},

we may write

||\sqrt{\beta}(u_{h}^{(n+1)}-u_{h}^{(n)})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}=\sqrt{\mathbf{r}_{n}\cdot\mathbf{Mr}_{n}}.

We briefly discuss the cost per iteration of the method (
41
). The matrices
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{K}
may be expressed as block-matrices
\mathbf{P}=(\mathbf{P}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{M}
and
\mathbf{K}=(\mathbf{K}_{ij})_{i,j=1}^{M}
, where each
\mathbf{P}_{ij},\mathbf{K}_{ij}\in\mathbb{R}^{\dim\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}\times\dim\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}}
and
M=\dim\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}
in the mono-energetic setting or
M=\dim(\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{E}})
in the poly-energetic setting. One typically has
\mathbf{P}_{ij}=\bm{0}
for
i\neq j
. In the classical multigroup discrete ordinates treatment of the linear Boltzmann transport equation, and in the implementation given in
[
6
]
, the on-diagonal matrix blocks
\mathbf{P}_{ii}
are themselves block-diagonal, with each block corresponding to the system matrix arising from a DGFEM discretisation of a transport problem. For this reason, the action of
\mathbf{P}^{-1}
is assumed to be computationally inexpensive. On the other hand, the matrix
\mathbf{K}
is block-dense, owing to the coupling between angle-energy element pairs via (
15
). Thus, the action of
\mathbf{K}
typically dominates the computational time taken to perform a single iteration of (
41
).

5.2.
GMRES

We employ a standard implementation of GMRES which accepts (the action of) a matrix
\mathbf{A}
, a right-hand side
\mathbf{b}
and desired tolerance
TOL
. We denote this method by
gmres
(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{b},
TOL
)
, which returns an approximate solution
\hat{\mathbf{x}}
to the matrix equation
\mathbf{Ax}=\mathbf{b}
as well as the corresponding residual norm
||\mathbf{r}||_{2}=||\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{A}\hat{\mathbf{x}}||_{2}\leq
TOL
. For simplicity of presentation, we do not consider using GMRES with restarts, but stress that the implementation of the method remains unchanged in this setting.

By a judicious selection of
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{b}
, we can restructure the original matrix equation (
40
) in such a way that
gmres
returns the
a posteriori
error estimate in Theorem
9
. Moreover, we can do so using any preconditioner
\mathbf{P}\approx\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{S}
that we have available. Let
\hat{\mathbf{M}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
denote the matrix with entries

(\hat{\mathbf{M}})_{ij}=\int_{\mathbb{E}}\int_{\mathbb{S}}\int_{\Omega}\overline{\alpha}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu},E)\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu},E)\phi_{j}(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu},E)\ \,\textnormal{d}\mathbf{x}\,\textnormal{d}\bm{\mu}\,\textnormal{d}E.

Moreover, let
\mathbf{L}
denote the lower-triangular matrix arising in the Cholesky decomposition of
\hat{\mathbf{M}}=\mathbf{LL}^{*}
. By executing

(43)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=\mbox{\tt gmres}(\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{S})\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{L},\mathbf{L}^{-1}\mathbf{F},\mbox{\tt TOL}),

(44)

\displaystyle\hat{\mathbf{u}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{L}\hat{\mathbf{z}},

we can ensure that
|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||_{DG}\leq

TOL
. To see this, note that the first equation yields an auxiliary solution
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
satisfying

||\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{F}-(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{S})\mathbf{P}^{-1}\mathbf{L}\hat{\mathbf{z}})||_{2}\leq\textnormal{\tt TOL},

and the second equation tells us that the left-hand-side is equal to
||\mathbf{L}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{r}}||_{2}=\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{r}}}
, where
\hat{\mathbf{r}}=\mathbf{F}-(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{S})\hat{\mathbf{u}}
denotes a residual vector. Finally, we relate the residual vector
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
to the residual function
r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})
appearing in the statement of Theorem
9
through the latter’s expansion in the basis of
\mathbb{V}
:

r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(\mathbf{r})_{j}\phi_{j}.

Using this expansion of
r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})
and setting
v_{h}=\phi_{i}
for
1\leq i\leq N
in (
39
) yields the following equation:

\hat{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{r}=\hat{\mathbf{r}}.

Thus,

\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf{r}}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{M}}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{r}}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{r}^{*}\hat{\mathbf{M}}\mathbf{r}}=||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})},

which is precisely the
a posteriori
error estimator of Theorem
9
.

For simplicity, we consider the choice of preconditioner
\mathbf{P}=\mathbf{A}
; i.e. the transport component of the original system matrix in (
40
). This allows us to write the preconditioned system matrix passed to
gmres
as
\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{SA}^{-1})\mathbf{L}
. In addition to the computation of the actions of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{S}
on a vector as outlined before for source iteration, the GMRES scheme (
43
)-(
44
) additionally requires the computation of the Cholesky decomposition of a (possibly very large) matrix
\hat{\mathbf{M}}
, which in principle could be very computationally costly. However, if one employs an orthogonal basis
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
with respect to the
\overline{\alpha}
-weighted
L_{2}(\mathcal{D})
-inner product, both
\hat{\mathbf{M}}
and
\mathbf{L}
become diagonal matrices, the latter of which has diagonal entries
(\mathbf{L})_{ii}=||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}\phi_{i}||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}
. For this reason, the actions of
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
at each GMRES iteration are assumed to be slightly faster than that of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
and significantly faster than that of
\mathbf{S}
. Thus, the cost of evaluating the action of the matrix
\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{A}-\mathbf{S})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{L}^{-1}(\mathbf{I}-\mathbf{SA}^{-1})\mathbf{L}
on a vector is comparable to a single step of source iteration, which performs the action of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{S}
on a vector.

Finally, we consider the cost of orthogonalisation in the GMRES algorithm. It is expected that the cost of orthogonalising one vector against another is comparable to that of applying
\mathbf{L}
or
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
to a vector. However, the number of orthogonalisations performed at iteration
n
of GMRES is equal to
n
. Thus, provided that the dimension of the largest Krylov subspace generated is not too large, we still expect that the action of
\mathbf{S}
at each iteration is the most computationally-expensive operation in the scheme. We conclude that a single step of GMRES is thus comparable in terms of computational cost to a single step of source iteration in the sense that the additional cost of vector orthogonalisation, as well as the actions of
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
, are small compared to those of
\mathbf{A}^{-1}
and
\mathbf{S}
which are required by both algorithms.

We briefly remark on the importance of the factor
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
in the
a posteriori
error estimator
||\mathbf{L}^{-1}\hat{\mathbf{r}}||_{2}=||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}
. As noted earlier, equations (
43
)-(
44
), generate a discrete solution
\hat{\mathbf{u}}
and thus a corresponding finite element function
\hat{u}_{h}
for which
|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||\leq

TOL
. Observing that
\mathbf{L}=\textnormal{diag}(||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}\phi_{i}||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})})
, it can be shown that

(45)

\min_{i}||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}\phi_{i}||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}\leq\frac{||\hat{\mathbf{r}}||_{2}}{||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}r_{h}(\hat{u}_{h})||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}}\leq\max_{i}||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}\phi_{i}||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})},

Thus, if (
43
)-(
44
) are executed without including
\mathbf{L}
and
\mathbf{L}^{-1}
(or equivalently if one sets
\mathbf{L}=\mathbf{I}
) and the
gmres
call in equation (
43
) returns an approximation
\hat{\mathbf{z}}
such that
||\hat{\mathbf{r}}||_{2}\leq

TOL
, then the true DGFEM-energy-norm error actually satisfies

|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||_{DG}\leq\frac{\mbox{\tt TOL}}{\min_{i}||\sqrt{\overline{\alpha}}\phi_{i}||_{L_{2}(\mathcal{D})}}.

Furthermore, if the diagonal elements of
\mathbf{L}
vary in magnitude - for instance, if
\mathcal{T}
is highly non-uniform or if
\overline{\alpha}
is highly anisotropic - the inequalities (
45
) may not be sharp. Thus, the significance of the matrix
\mathbf{L}
is to weight the components of the residual vector
\hat{\mathbf{r}}
in such a way that
|||u_{h}-\hat{u}_{h}|||_{DG}
is bounded above by the statement in Theorem
9
.

6.
Numerical Results

6.1.
Mono-energetic benchmark

We consider the application of mono-energetic source iteration, generalised source iteration and right-preconditioned GMRES for the numerical solution of the DGFEM problem to a benchmark posed in two spatial dimensions and one angular dimension. The focus of this benchmark is to investigate the behaviour of all three methods with respect to changes in discretisation parameters such as mesh granularity and polynomial degree of the approximation, as well as changes in problem parameters such as the domain size and optical thickness, which we take as the product
(\alpha+\beta)h
between the spatial mesh size parameter and the macroscopic total cross-section.

We employ spatial domains
(0,L)^{2}
(in dimensionless units) with
L\in\{\frac{1}{10},1,10\}
and macroscopic total cross-sections
\sigma=\alpha+\beta
with
\sigma\in\{\frac{1}{10},1,10\}
. The macroscopic absorption and scattering cross-sections are taken to be the globally-constant functions
\alpha=(1-c)\sigma
and
\beta=c\sigma
, respectively, where
c\in\{\frac{1}{10},\frac{3}{10},\frac{5}{10},\frac{7}{10},\frac{9}{10}\}
denotes the scattering ratio
c=\frac{\beta}{\alpha+\beta}
. The differential scattering cross-section is taken to be
\theta=\frac{\beta}{|\mathbb{S}|}
, which models isotropic scattering. For each test problem, the forcing data
f
and the boundary data
g_{D}
are chosen such that the analytical solution to (
1
) is given by

u(\mathbf{x},\bm{\mu})=\textnormal{exp}\left(-(\mathbf{x}\cdot\bm{\mu})^{2}\right).

A sequence of space-angle meshes
\mathcal{T}=\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}\times\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}
is employed, consisting of
|\mathcal{T}_{\Omega}|\in\{4,16,64,256\}
spatial elements and
|\mathcal{T}_{\mathbb{S}}|\in\{8,16,32,64\}
curved angular elements (constructed as in Section
3
). Each space-angle element
\kappa\in\kappa_{\Omega}\times\kappa_{\mathbb{S}}\in\mathcal{T}
is endowed with a finite element space
\mathbb{P}_{p}(\kappa_{\Omega})\times\mathbb{Q}_{q}(\kappa_{\mathbb{S}})
employing a uniform polynomial degree
p=q\in\{0,1,2\}
. The total number of degrees of freedom of the finite element space
\mathbb{V}=\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}\otimes\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}
varies from
|\mathbb{V}|=32
for the coarsest discretisation to
|\mathbb{V}|=294912
for the finest discretisation.

The
a posteriori
error estimates of Theorems
3
and
7
are applied for the standard and generalised versions of source iterations, whereas the residual-based
a posteriori
error estimate of Theorem
9
is applied for the right-preconditioned GMRES method. In the case of generalised source iteration, the optimal relaxation parameter
\omega=\frac{1}{2}
, which minimises the contraction factor in the statement of Theorem
7
, is employed.

(0,20)^{2}
(in units of cm) and the energetic domain is taken to be
(10,1000)
(in units of keV). The data terms
\alpha
and
\theta
are chosen to mimic the Compton scattering of photons through water. This is achieved by setting

The number of iterations taken by poly-energetic source iteration and GMRES to resolve the solution on a single group to within that group’s solver tolerance are roughly comparable for most energy groups, but on the lowest energy group the poly-energetic source iteration requires many more iterations than GMRES to achieve the same group solver tolerance.

Finally, we comment on the storage requirements of poly-energetic source iteration and GMRES. While both methods return a solution vector of length
\dim\mathbb{V}=\dim\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}\times\dim\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}\times\dim\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{E}}
, these vectors may be assembled incrementally starting from the highest energy group. Thus, for each energy group
1\leq g\leq N_{\mathbb{E}}

M=\dim\mathbb{V}_{\Omega}\times\dim\mathbb{V}_{\mathbb{S}}\times\dim\mathbb{P}_{p}(\kappa_{g})
corresponding to a block of the full solution vector. The number of vectors of length
M
required to perform the implementation of poly-energetic source iteration as outlined in Section
5
remains constant as the number of iterations increases, whereas the storage requirements for GMRES grow linearly with the dimension of the Krylov subspace generated at each iteration. While
M
may be moderately large for practical applications, Table
2
suggests that, even for the lowest energy group, only a small number of Krylov vectors of length
M
may be required to achieve a modest global solver tolerance.

7.
Conclusions

In this article we have investigated the application of iterative linear solvers for the system of equations arising from the DGFEM discretisation of the linear Boltzmann transport equation. In particular, we presented a generalisation of source iteration and modified source iteration for the poly-energetic and, respectively, mono-energetic settings and showed that the resulting iterative solvers are robustly convergent with respect to the discretisation parameters employed in the DGFEM scheme. Furthermore, we introduced a GMRES solver based on employing source iteration as a preconditioner. For all three approaches computable
a posteriori
error bounds on the solver error were deduced. In the case of GMRES, this bound was established through a judicious choice of the left- and right-preconditioners for the original system.

The practical performance of the proposed iterative solvers was studied through a series of numerical experiments. Here we observed that GMRES outperformed source iteration (and modified source iteration for mono-energetic problems) when considering the number of iterations required to attain convergence. This was particularly evident for poly-energetic problems at lower energy groups. In terms of computational costs, we recall that one step of source iteration is comparable to one iteration of GMRES and hence the latter approach is highly advantageous in these lower energy regimes.

As part of our future work, we plan to consider the exploitation of DSA approaches, though their extension to poly-energetic problems requires additional investigation, as well as the use of alternative preconditioning strategies, cf., for example,
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. Furthermore, the linear solver
a posteriori
error bounds developed here will be implemented within an adaptive mesh refinement framework whereby both solver and discretisation error are simultaneously controlled.
