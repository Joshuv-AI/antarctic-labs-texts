Lemma 3.4

[

\epsilon\in\{\pm 1\}
. Then as
|\rho(G)|\geqslant 8
, we have
|\pi(q-\epsilon)|\geqslant 5
, and so
\pi(q-\epsilon)\subseteq V_{i}
and
V_{j}\subseteq\{2\}\cup\pi(q+\epsilon)
, where
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
. It is a contradiction. Hence
|\pi(q\pm 1)|\geqslant 3
, as we claimed. If
\pi(|G:M|)\neq\emptyset
, then using Lemma
2.11
, there exists
k\in\{1,2\}
such that
\pi(q^{2}-1)\cup\pi(|G:M|)\subseteq V_{k}
. Thus as
\rho(G)=\pi(S)
and
|\rho(G)|\geqslant 8
, we have a contradiction. Therefore
\pi(|G:M|)=\emptyset
. Using Lemma
3.3
, for some
i\in\{1,2\}
,
V_{i}=\{2\}\cup\pi(q+\epsilon)
and
V_{j}=\pi(q-\epsilon)
. Let
{a_{i}}_{s}\in V_{i}
, for some
s\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Then for some
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
,
{a_{i}}_{s}{a_{j}}_{s}\,|\,\chi(1)
. Let
0\in\mathrm{Irr}(R(G))
be a constituent of
\chi_{R(G)}
. Also let
I:=I_{G}(0)
and
N:=\frac{I}{R(G)}
. Now we consider Dickson’s list on the structure of
N
. First assume that
N=S
. Then as the Schur multiplier of
S
is trivial,
0
is extendible to
G
. Thus
\mathrm{cd}(G|0)=\{0(1)\,m\,|\,m\in\mathrm{cd}(S)\}
. Therefore by the structure of
F(n)
,
0(1)=1
and
\mathrm{cd}(G|0)=\mathrm{cd}(S)
. This is a contradiction as
\chi(1)\in\mathrm{cd}(G|0)
. Hence
N\lneq S
and one of the following cases holds:

Case 1)

N
is either an elementary abelian
2
-group, contained in a dihedral group, or a Frobenius group whose kernel is an elementary abelian
2
-group. If
N
is a Frobenius group and
0
is extendible to
I
, then using Lemma
2.12
,
\Delta(G)[\pi(S)\setminus\{2\}]
is a complete graph with at least three vertices. It is a contradiction. Then using Clifford’s theorem and Lemma
2.12
,
{a_{i}}_{s}{a_{j}}_{s}y|\chi(1)
, for some
y\in\pi(S)\setminus\{{a_{i}}_{s},{a_{j}}_{s}\}
which is a contradiction with the structure of
F(n)
.

Case 2)

N\cong A_{5}
. Then as
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(5)
is the Schur representation group of
A_{5}
, using Clifford’s theorem, for some
m\in\mathrm{cd}(G|0)
,
m
is divisible by
0(1)\frac{q(q^{2}-1)}{20}
. Hence there exists
y\in(\pi(q-\epsilon)\setminus\{5\})\cap V_{j}
such that
\Delta(G)[\{y\}\cup\pi(q+\epsilon)]
is a complete graph with at least three vertices. It is a contradiction with the structure of
F(n)
.

Case 3)

N\cong\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(2^{m})
, where
m\neq f
is a positive divisor of
f
and
2^{m}\geqslant 8
. Then using Clifford’s theorem, there exists
l:=0(1)2^{f-m}(2^{f}+1)\frac{2^{f}-1}{2^{m}-1}\in\mathrm{cd}(G|0)
. Now as
m|f
, using Zsigmondy’s theorem there exists
y\in\pi\Big(\frac{2^{f}-1}{2^{m}-1}\Big)
such that
\Delta(G)[2y(2^{f}+1)]
is a complete graph with at least three vertices. This is again a contradiction and completes the proof.
∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
A

We begin this section with the following observation.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
G
be a finite group with disconnected regular character-graph. Then
\Delta(G)
is isomorphic to either
\overline{K_{3}}
or
\overline{K_{2}}
.

Proof.

If
|\rho(G)|\leqslant 2
, then
\Delta(G)\cong\overline{K_{2}}
and we are done. Now assume that
|\rho(G)|\geqslant 3
. Using Lemma
2.8
,
\Delta(G)\cong\overline{K_{3}}
or
\Delta(G)
has precisely two complete connected components. If the first case occurs, we have nothing to prove. Suppose the later case holds. Then as
\Delta(G)
is regular, by
[
21
, Theorem
6.3
]
,
G
is a solvable group. Hence using
[
26
, Theorem
3
]
,
\Delta(G)\cong\overline{K_{2}}
, which is a contradiction. This completes the proof.
∎

Now we are ready to prove our main theorem.

Proof of Theorem A.

Let
G
be a finite group with
k
-regular character-graph
\Delta(G)
, for some integer
k\geqslant 2
. Also let the eigenvalues of
\Delta(G)
be in the interval
[-2,\infty)
. As
k\geqslant 2
, using Lemma
4.1
, we assume that
\Delta(G)
is connected. Then Lemma
2.8
implies that
\overline{\Delta(G)}
is bipartite. If
\Delta(G)
is an exceptional graph, then by Lemma
2.7
,
\overline{\Delta(G)}
is non-bipartite which is a contradiction. Hence using
[
5
]
and Lemma
2.5
,
\Delta(G)
is either a cocktail party graph or a line graph. If
\Delta(G)
is a cocktail party graph, then we are done. Thus we can assume that
\Delta(G)=L(\Gamma)
, for some graph
\Gamma
.
Hence as
\Delta(G)
is connected, the graph
\Gamma
is connected. Also Lemma
2.3
implies that
\Gamma
has no subgraph isomorphic to
C_{5}
and
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)\leqslant 2
. Now by Lemma
2.4
, one of the following cases occurs:

Case 1)
For some positive integer
r
, the graph
\Gamma
is
r
-regular. If
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)=1
, then as
k\geqslant 2
,
\Delta(G)=L(\Gamma)\cong K_{3}
. Suppose
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)=2
and
m:=|V(\Gamma)|
. Also let
X:=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4}\}
be the set of vertices of a maximum matching in
\Gamma
with edges
x_{1}x_{2}
and
x_{3}x_{4}
. Then
4r=\sum_{i=1}^{4}deg(x_{i})\geqslant\sum_{v\in V(\Gamma)\setminus X}\,deg(v)=(m-4)r
. Thus
4\leqslant m\leqslant 8
. Note that
\Gamma\setminus X
is an empty graph and hence
2\leqslant r\leqslant 4
. If
r=2
, then using the structure of
\Gamma
, we deduce that
\Gamma\cong C_{4}\cong\Delta(G)
. Thus we can assume that
r=3
or
4
. Now one of the following cases occurs:

1.

m=8
. Then
|V(\Gamma)\setminus X|=4
. Note that every vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)\setminus X
is adjacent to
r
vertices in
X
. Thus there exists a vertex
x_{i}\in X
, for some
1\leqslant i\leqslant 4
such that
deg(x_{i})\geqslant r
and it is a contradiction.

2.

m=7
. Then
r=4
and
|V(\Gamma)\setminus X|=3
. Thus we obtain
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)=3
which is again a contradiction.

3.

m=6
. Then
|V(\Gamma)\setminus X|=2
. Hence as every vertex
v\in V(\Gamma)\setminus X
is adjacent to
r
vertices in
X
, in all cases,
\Gamma
has a subgraph isomorphic to
C_{5}
which is impossible.

4.

m=5
. Then
r=4
and
\Gamma\cong K_{5}
. It is a contradiction.

5.

m=4
. Then
r=3
and
\Delta(G)=L(\Gamma)\cong L(K_{4})\cong\mathrm{CP}(3)
. This completes the proof in this case.

Case 2)
The graph
\Gamma
is a semi-regular bipartite graph with parameters
(n_{1},n_{2},r_{1},r_{2})
where
n_{1}r_{1}=n_{2}r_{2}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
1\leqslant n_{1}\leqslant n_{2}
. Let
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)=1
. Then it is clear that
n_{1}=1
and
\Gamma\cong K_{1,n_{2}}
. Thus as
k\geqslant 2
,
\Delta(G)=L(\Gamma)\cong K_{n_{2}}

n_{2}\geqslant 3
. Hence we can assume that
\alpha^{{}^{\prime}}(\Gamma)=2

n_{1}=2

n_{2}\geqslant 3

\Delta(G)=L(\Gamma)\cong L(K_{2,n_{2}})\cong F(n_{2})

\Delta(G)=L(\Gamma)\cong\mathrm{CP}(2)

\Gamma
