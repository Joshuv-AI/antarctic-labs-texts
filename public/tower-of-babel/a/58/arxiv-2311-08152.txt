Towards Reasoning in Large Language Models via Multi-Agent Peer Review Collaboration
Zhenran Xu, Senbao Shi, Baotian Hu, Jindi Yu, Dongfang Li, Min Zhang, Yuxiang Wu

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities in general natural language processing tasks but often fall short in complex reasoning tasks. Recent studies have explored human-like problem-solving strategies, such as self-correct , to push further the boundary of single-model reasoning ability. In this work, we let a single model “step outside the box” by engaging multiple models to correct each other. We introduce a multi-agent collaboration strategy that emulates the academic peer review process. Each agent independently constructs its own solution, provides reviews on the solutions of others, and assigns confidence levels to its reviews. Upon receiving peer reviews, agents revise their initial solutions. Extensive experiments on three different types of reasoning tasks show that our collaboration approach delivers superior accuracy across all ten datasets compared to existing methods. Further study underscores the effectiveness of integrating confidence in reviews, demonstrates the superiority of feedback exchange over mere solution sharing, and highlights the role of capability and diversity in fostering successful collaboration. Code is available at https://github.com/HITsz-TMG/Multi-agent-peer-review .

To this end, we propose a

The contributions of this work are threefold:

•

By letting a single agent “think outside the box”, we propose a multi-agent collaboration method that emulates the peer review process – “creation, review, and revision”.

•

Our collaboration method consistently outperforms previous strong baselines across three reasoning tasks and ten datasets.

•

We demonstrate the efficacy of integrating confidence and sharing feedback, suggesting a viable path forward the optimization of LLM-based multi-agent systems.

we propose a human-mimicking multi-agent collaboration process,

Methodology

Overview.
In this work, we introduce a multi-agent collaboration strategy that mirrors the academic peer review process.
The overall process is illustrated in Figure
2
.
The process consists of three stages – “creation, review, and revision”.
In stage 1, for a given question, each agent first independently submits its own solution.
In stage 2, each agent performs a systematic review of its peers’ solutions.
In stage 3, upon receiving the reviews, each agent refines its initial solution and re-submits the improved version.
An example of these stages is provided in Section
4.6
.
We next elaborate these stages in detail.

Stage 1: Create.

The peer review process starts with each agent submitting its own solution.
Specifically, when presented with a question
q
,
A group of
n
agents, denoted as
{A_{1},A_{2},...,A_{n}}
, perform chain-of-thought reasoning.
Each agent generates its own reasoning chains and corresponding answers
a_{i}
.
Take Figure
2
as an example,
the initial answers of three agents are all incorrect.

Stage 2: Review.

We next feed each agent
A_{i}
with the solution of its peers (i.e.,
A_{j}
, where
j\neq i
), one at a time, to write reviews
r_{ij}
.
Note that
r_{ij}
denotes the review
from
agent
A_{i}

to
agent
A_{j}
.
In current review forms,
reviewers do more than just provide overall assessments; they also indicate their certainty about their evaluations.
Mirroring such real-life review practices,
the agents not only examine the reasoning process step by step but also attach a confidence score to quantify the reliability of their feedback.
To achieve this, we concatenate each peer solution with the following instruction:

Please examine this agent’s reasoning process step by step and offer feedback on its reasoning. You can rate your confidence in your feedback on a scale from 1-10, where 10 indicates the highest level of confidence.

In the example shown in Figure
2
,
one agent accurately determines the initial height but forgets to multiply the ratio twice. Conversely, another agent multiplies the ratio twice but gets the initial height wrong.
Although their initial answers are wrong,
they can provide effective feedback confidently to each other, based on the difference between their solutions and those of their peers.

Stage 3: Revise.

We finally feed each agent
A_{i}
with the reviews from its peers (i.e.,
r_{ji}
), all at once, to refine the initial solutions.
The agent considers both the peer solutions in dialogue history and the received peer reviews.
To achieve this, we concatenate the reviews with the following instruction:

Using other agents’ solutions and feedbacks as additional information, can you provide your answer to the problem?

In the example illustrated in Figure
2
,
out of three agents, two successfully integrate accurate feedback into their final responses. This results in both agents converging on the same correct solution after revision.

Unlike prior studies such as
Du et al. 2023
and
Sun et al. 2023
, we do not explicitly ask the agents to reach a consensus at the end of the review.
The final prediction is determined through a majority vote among the
n
participating agents.
This approach ensures the impartiality and balance of our peer review process.

Results

Main results.

Table

Our peer review strategy surpasses all single-agent and multi-agent baselines across all datasets,
especially significant in more challenging benchmarks (+1.40% on GSM8K, +3.80% on SVAMP, +2.75% on AQuA over the prior state-of-the-art).

Table

Our collaboration method consistently outperforms all prior baselines across all datasets.
The effectiveness of self-correction notably decreases in non-math reasoning tasks,

However, when we introduce feedback from other agents, there is a significant enhancement in performance, rising from 66.13% to 73.20%. This demonstrates the advantage of integrating external feedback over relying solely on internal feedback mechanisms.

Ablation study.

For the ablation of
confidence
in reviews,
we alter the prompt of Stage 2 described in Section
3
.
Specifically, we remove the prompt of eliciting confidence, simplifying it to:

“Please examine this agent’s reasoning process step by step and offer feedback on its reasoning”
.
Table
2
shows the effectiveness of expressing uncertainty and integrating confidence in feedback on mathematical reasoning datasets.
An example of confidence ablation is shown in Appendix
B
.
As stated in the prompt of Stage 3, the updated answer uses other agents’ solutions and feedback as additional information.
In the absence of confidence scores, the updated answer tends to be misled by ineffective feedback and remains incorrect.
In contrast, the integration of confidence score enables the model to selectively aggregate valuable information.
By focusing more on solutions with higher confidence levels rather than on less reliable feedback, the confidence scores guide the model towards the correct answer.

For commonsense reasoning in Table
3
and symbolic reasoning in Table
4
,
incorporating confidence can result in unchanged or even reduced performance.
This decrease may stem from the tendency of LLMs to exhibit overconfidence and significant miscalibration when verbalizing their confidence in non-math datasets
Xiong et al. 2023b
.
We will further discuss the relationship between the level of confidence and the correctness of feedback in Section
4.5
.

For the ablation of
solutions
,
we leave out the the dialogue history of Stage 2 during the answer revision process in Stage 3,
so that the peer solutions cannot be seen.
We also change the prompt of Stage 3 into
“Using other agents’ feedbacks as additional information, can you provide your answer to the problem? ”
.
From Table
2
,
3
and
4
, omitting solutions resulted in decreased performance in 9 out of 10 datasets,
indicating that solutions contribute valuable complementary information to the feedback received.
In addition,

Ours (w/o solution)
, which involves exchanging only peer feedback,
outperforms
Multi-agent Debate
, which exchanges only peer solutions,
in 8 out of 10 datasets.
This demonstrates that peer feedback is more effective than peer solutions in multi-agent collaboration.

In the non-math dataset Penguins,

We study how the number of agents and review rounds affects performance.
From Figure

Interplay between confidence scores and feedback correctness.

We manually annotate the correctness of 600 feedback each from the GSM8K and Penguins datasets.
We present the relationship between confidence scores and feedback correctness in Figure

We have the following observations:

(2) The second row of Figure

Performance variability with diverse LLMs.

We explore what kind of LLMs can collaborate well and improve each other through the peer review process.
We set the agent number at 2, and use GSM8K for the analysis.
We focus on two key factors: the capability gap and diversity between LLMs.
The capability gap refers to the difference in the accuracy of initial responses provided by two LLMs.
The diversity is measured by
INCON

Xiong et al. 2023a
. Specifically, suppose there are
n
LLMs
L=\{l_{1},\cdots,l_{n}\}
, and a dataset with
m
samples
X=\{x_{1},\cdots,x_{m}\}
. We define
p^{i}_{j}
as the prediction of
l_{i}
on
x_{j}
. Then the
INCON
of
L
on
X
can be defined as:

\texttt{INCON}=\sum_{k=1}^{m}\frac{\Phi(p^{1}_{k},\cdots,p^{n}_{k})}{m},

(1)

\Phi
is a sign function, it will be assigned a value of 1 if there are any two variables in
\Phi
that are not equal, otherwise,
\Phi
takes a value of 0.

The collaboration results are presented in Table
5
.
We have the following conclusions:
(1) More powerful models provide more effective feedback.
The
0301
version of
gpt-3.5-turbo
contributes to a 2.4% accuracy improvement in the

(2) LLMs with smaller capability gap can collaborate better.
By comparing the first two rows,

(3) LLMs with higher diversity can collaborate better.
With a similar capability gap, the collaboration between

5
Conclusion

In this work, we let a single model think outside the box by engaging multiple models to collaborate on complex reasoning.
We introduce a multi-agent collaboration strategy that emulates the academic peer review process,
following an iterative cycle of creation, review, and revision.
Extensive experiments show that our approach delivers superior accuracy across all ten datasets and three tasks compared to existing methods.
Further analysis shows the effectiveness of confidence integration and
demonstrates the superiority of feedback exchange over mere solution sharing.

Limitations

Despite the effectiveness of multi-agent methods,
it is essential to consider the associated costs, particularly in terms of token consumption.
The balance between effectiveness and efficiency emerges as a critical area for exploration.
In addition, the underlying factors for LLM collaboration are more than just capability and answer diversity.
The factors also include role and tool specification of agents.
Further research may focus on identifying which types of agents can collaborate more effectively together, thereby providing deeper insights into the optimization of multi-agent systems in practical applications.
