Covering Simple Orthogonal Polygons with Rectangles
Aniket Basu Roy

Abstract
We study the problem of Covering Orthogonal Polygons with Rectangles. For polynomial-time algorithms, the best-known approximation factor is O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\sqrt{\log n}) when the input polygon may have holes [Kumar and Ramesh, STOC ’99, SICOMP ’03], and there is a 2 2 -factor approximation algorithm known when the polygon is hole-free [Franzblau, SIDMA ’89]. Arguably, an easier problem is the Boundary Cover problem where we are interested in covering only the boundary of the polygon in contrast to the original problem where we are interested in covering the interior of the polygon, hence it is also referred as the Interior Cover problem. For the Boundary Cover problem, a 4 4 -factor approximation algorithm is known to exist and it is APX -hard when the polygon has holes [Berman and DasGupta, Algorithmica ’94]. In this work, we investigate how effective is local search algorithm for the above covering problems on simple polygons. We prove that a simple local search algorithm yields a PTAS for the Boundary Cover problem when the polygon is simple. Our proof relies on the existence of planar supports on appropriate hypergraphs defined on the Boundary Cover problem instance. On the other hand, we construct instances where support graphs for the Interior Cover problem have arbitrarily large bicliques, thus implying that the same local search technique cannot yield a PTAS for this problem. We also show large locality gap for its dual problem, namely the Maximum Antirectangle problem.

Introduction

Polygon Covering is a classical problem in Computational Geometry.
In this work we are interested in orthogonal polygons, i.e., all its sides are horizontal or vertical line segments, and we are required to cover the polygon with a minimum number of orthogonal rectangles such that the union of the rectangles is same as the polygon. This problem has a long history and
it has been studied under the guise of various names.
To the best of our knowledge, the earliest reference to this problem is an unpublished work by Masek
[
29
]
which is mentioned by Garey and Johnson in their book
Computers and Intractability

[
18
]
.
Here, the problem is referred to as the Rectilinear Picture Compression problem
and claimed to be
NP
-hard.
Later Culberson and Reckhow
[
13
]
proved it to be
NP
-hard for simple polygons, i.e., polygons that do not have holes.

Orthogonal Polygon Covering problem has been studied also from the perspective of polynomial time approximation algorithms where the state-of-the-art approximation ratio is
O(\sqrt{\log n})
by Kumar and Ramesh
[
27
]
.
Also, the question has been posed for simple polygons. Franzblau gave a
(2+h)
-approximation algorithm which implies a
2
-factor approximation for simple polygons
[
16
]
, where
h
is the number of holes.
Whereas in 1981, Chaiken, Kleitman, Saks, and Shearer
[
9
]
gave a polynomial time exact algorithm when the polygon is both vertically and horizontally convex. This restriction was relaxed to just vertically (resp. horizontally) convex polygons to attain the same result by Franzblau and Kleitman
[
17
]
.

Chaiklen et al.
[
9
]
also studied the problem of covering the boundary of the polygon (which they referred as
Edge Cover
problem) and the corners of the polygon with minimum number of rectangles, and studied the respective duality gaps.
Culberson and Reckhow
[
13
]
proved that even the boundary cover problem is
NP
-hard for hole-free polygons. Berman and DasGupta
[
7
]
showed that the problem is
APX
-hard when we allow holes in the polygon for the interior cover as well as the boundary cover problem.

Although several articles have been published over the years, especially from the perspective of applications
[
11
,
25
]
, theoretical improvements have stagnated in the last two decades.

In the current work, we are interested in giving better approximation algorithms for the boundary cover problem for simple orthogonal polygons.
To our knowledge, the best known approximation factor is
4
by the same algorithm that works for the boundary cover problem for arbitrary polygons
[
7
]
.

1.1
Our Contribution

Our main contribution is to show the existence of planar support for appropriate hypergraphs defined on the boundary cover problem instance as stated below.

Theorem 1

(Planar Support for Boundary Cover)
.

Given a simple orthogonal polygon
P
and a set of containment-maximal rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
with respect to
P
, there exists planar support graph for
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
.

In order to prove this theorem, we observe many novel properties of containment-maximal rectangles, which could be of independent interest and useful elsewhere.
As a corollary of this theorem, it immediately follows that local search yields a Polynomial Time Approximation Scheme (PTAS) for the boundary cover problem and the corner cover problem by applying Lemma
7
, which is a framework, independently devised by Chan and Har-Peled
[
10
]
and Mustafa and Ray
[
30
]
. Please refer to Subsection
2.1
for more on this framework.

Corollary 2
.

Local Search yields a PTAS for the Boundary Cover problem for simple orthogonal polygons.

Corollary 3
.

Local Search yields a PTAS for the Corner Cover problem for simple orthogonal polygons.

On the negative side, we construct interior cover problem instances whose minimal support graphs contain arbitrarily large bicliques thus implying that a PTAS cannot be attained using the above local search framework (Theorem
19
).
We also give a negative result for the dual problem of finding maximum sized antirectangle (also known as the maximum hidden set problem) by showing the existence of instances such that the dual intersection graphs have arbitrarily large bicliques, thus implying local search can be arbitrarily smaller than the optimum solution (Corollary
21
).
Please refer to Section
2
for the precise definitions of the above problems.

1.1.1
High-level Idea and Organization

There are broadly two parts to our proof of the existence of the planar supports for hypergraphs defined on the boundary cover instances. If we think of the existence of planar supports as the desired property then we prove that this property is
hereditary
, i.e., if a family satisfies the property then so does any of its sub-families (Lemma
16
in Section
4
).
We also prove that the
complete
families of containment-maximal rectangles have this property (Lemma
18
in Section
5
). By complete we mean the set of all possible containment-maximal rectangles with respect to
P
.

In Section
4
, we use the notion of Left-Right Coloring to prove the hereditary property. The Left-Right Coloring has been used for testing planarity of graphs in the literature
[
14
]
. The central idea in proving the hereditary property is that if a family has a star graph as its support then the family without the rectangle corresponding to the center of the star, still has a planar support. See Figure
1
for an example and Section
3
for the technical details.

We prove the planar support property for complete family of rectangles by induction on the number of vertices of the horizontal R-tree. Given an orthogonal polygon
P
, we prove that if the statement is true for a complete family with respect to a
smaller
polygon, then it is also true for the complete family with respect to
P
. The formal statement can be found in Section
5
and the full proof can be found in Section
B
in the appendices.

1.2
Related Work

1.2.1
Visibility Graphs

Given an orthogonal polygon
P
, define a graph
G=(V,E)
where
V
is finite point set in
P
, and
(p,q)\in E(G)
if there exists an axis-parallel rectangle
R\subseteq P
such that
p,q\in R
.

Observe that a polygon covering corresponds to a clique cover in
G
if
V
is sufficiently dense in
P
. A natural lower bound is the size of an independent set in
G
, which is also called an antirectangle. The minimum clique cover size is denoted by
\theta
and the maximum antirectangle (independent set) size is denoted by
\alpha
.

As mentioned by Chaiken et al.
[
9
]
,
Chvátal asked whether
\theta\stackrel{{\scriptstyle?}}{{=}}\alpha
, which was disproved by Szemerédi
by constructing a polygon with
\theta=8
and
\alpha=7
. Chung managed to attain the same bound using a simple polygon. Erdős asked whether there exists a constant
c>1
such that
\theta\leq c\cdot\alpha
. The only known upper bound is
\theta=O(\alpha\log\alpha)
due to Franzblau
[
16
]
.

1.2.2
Rectangle Rule List Minimization

Applegate et al.
[
1
]
studied the
Rectangle Rule List Minimization problem, which is to find the
shortest list of rules needed to create a given pattern. Given a rectangular grid colored
c_{0}
, a pair
(R,c)
is called a
rectangle rule
where
R
is a rectangle and
c
is a color from a color palette. A sequence of rectangle rules
{\mathcal{R}}
,
(R_{1},c_{1}),\dots,(R_{n},c_{n})
is called a Rectangle Rule List, and
P_{\mathcal{R}}
is the colored pattern such a sequence produces. The objective is to find a minimum length sequence
{\mathcal{R}}
that creates a target colored pattern. When the color palette has a single color, it is the Orthogonal Polygon Covering problem.

1.2.3
Covering Boolean Matrices with Combinatorial Rectangles

As orthogonal polygons can be drawn in a bounded grid, one can map every grid cell to
1
if it is contained within the input polygon and
0
otherwise.
In Communication Complexity, the following problem is of considerable interest.

Given an
n\times n
Boolean matrix
M=A\times B
, where
A,B\in\{0,1\}^{n}
, a combinatorial rectangle
R
is defined as
R=K\times L\subseteq[n]\times[n]
. A
1
-rectangle is a combinatorial rectangle
R
such that all its entries are
1
. Finding the minimum number of
1
-rectangles such that all the
1
s in
M
are covered is called the rectangle covering problem.
Note that in this context one is free to permute the rows and columns unlike our case.
It has the following implication with the non-deterministic complexity of
M
.

Proposition 4

(Proposition 5.2 in
[
33
]
)
.

Let
f:X\times Y\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and
M(f)
be the corresponding Boolean matrix. If there is a cover of
1
-entries of
M(f)
by
t

1
-rectangles, then there is a non-deterministic protocol that verifies whether
f(x,y)=1
with cost
\log_{2}t
.

This problem has also been referred as the Boolean Basis problem and related to the Polygon Covering problem by Lubiw
[
28
]
. Yet another way to view the problem is to cover the edges of the bipartite graph, whose adjacency matrix is
M
, with minimum number of bicliques.

2
Preliminaries

All polygons are assumed to be orthogonal, i.e., their sides are parallel to either the vertical or horizontal axes. A polygon is said to be simple if it has no holes, i.e., it is simply connected. Given a simple orthogonal polygon
P
, we denote
\partial P
to be the boundary of
P
. All rectangles are assumed to be axis-parallel.
A rectangle
R\subseteq P
is called containment-maximal with respect to a polygon
P
if
R
is not contained in a rectangle
R^{\prime}\subseteq P
of larger area. Henceforth, we shall use the word “maximal” to denote containment-maximal for the sake of brevity. Observe, as a consequence of maximality, all four sides of
R
have non-empty intersection with
\partial P
.
We denote
{\mathcal{R}}^{K}(P)
to be the set of all maximal rectangles with respect to
P
, also referred as the complete family of rectangles with respect to
P
.

Definition 5

(Blocker of a Maximal Rectangle)
.

Given an orthogonal polygon
P
and a maximal rectangle
R\subseteq P
, any point
p\in\partial P
is called a blocker of
R
if
p\in R
and
p
is not a corner of
R
. If
p
intersects the top (resp. bottom, left, right) side of
R
then
p
is called a top (resp. bottom, left, right) blocker of
R
.

For the sake of brevity, we shall sometimes call a side
s
of
P
to be a blocker of
R
if
s\cap R\neq\emptyset
.

Given a family of maximal rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
contained in
P
,
for every point
p\in\partial P
we define a hyperedge
{\mathcal{R}}_{p}:=\{R\in{\mathcal{R}}\mid p\in R\}
.
We shall work with this hypergraph
({\mathcal{R}},\{{\mathcal{R}}_{p}\}_{p\in\partial P})
where the vertex set is
{\mathcal{R}}
and the hyperedges are
{\mathcal{R}}_{p}
for every
p\in\partial P
.
For the sake of brevity, we shall denote
({\mathcal{R}},\{{\mathcal{R}}_{p}\}_{p\in\partial P})
as
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
. Similarly, one can define
({\mathcal{R}},P)
.

Definition 6

(Support graph of a hypergraph)
.

Given a hypergraph
H=(V,{\mathcal{F}})
where
V
is the vertex set and
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{V}
is a family of subsets of
V
, we say
G=(V,E)
is a support graph of
H
if for every
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
, the subgraph induced on F,
G[F]
is connected.

See Figure
1
for an example of a family of maximal rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
with respect to polygon
P
on the left, and a planar support graph for the hypergraph
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
to its right. Observe, that the intersection graph of the rectangles is always a valid support graph, which may not be planar.

Interior Cover problem

Input:
An orthogonal polygon
P
in the plane.

Question:
A minimum sized set of rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
such that

\bigcup_{R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}}R_{i}=P.

Boundary Cover problem

Input:
An orthogonal polygon
P
in the plane.

Question:
A minimum sized set of rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
such that

\partial P\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}\\
R_{i}\subseteq P\end{subarray}}R_{i}.

Corner Cover problem

Input:
An orthogonal polygon
P
in the plane.

Question:
A minimum sized set of rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
such that

\textsf{corner}P\subseteq\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}R_{i}\in{\mathcal{R}}\\
R_{i}\subseteq P\end{subarray}}R_{i}

where
\textsf{corner}P
denotes the set of corners of
P
.

Maximum Antirectangle problem

Input:
An orthogonal polygon
P
in the plane.

Question:
A maximum sized set of points
Q\subset P
such that for every
p,q\in Q
, there does not exist a rectangle
R\subseteq P
where
p,q\in R
.

2.1
Local Search Framework

Local search has been a very useful heuristic for decades, and has been proved to give good approximation ratios, particularly for clustering and geometric optimization algorithm
[
2
,
19
]
. In this work, we will consider the particular local search framework introduced by Mustafa and Ray for covering problems in
[
30
]
. Almost the same framework works for packing problems, which was introduced around the same time by Chan and Har-Peled in
[
10
]
. Over the years, this framework has been used for a variety of packing and covering problems
[
3
,
4
,
5
,
6
,
21
,
26
,
31
,
32
]
. As a consequence of this framework, it is sufficient to show the existence of a sparse support graph for a hypergraph: in order to prove that local search yields a PTAS for the corresponding covering problem. Here by sparse, we mean the graph has strongly sublinear separators, which is a necessary condition for planarity.

The study of planar supports predates the local search framework in question. It has been studied in the context of hypergraph drawing. See
[
8
,
23
,
24
]
, and Section 2 in
[
32
]
for a short survey.
Durocher and Fraser
[
15
]
were arguably the first to make the connection and use the word “support” in the context of local search for covering problems.

Below, we state an abstraction of the result of Mustafa and Ray
[
30
]
.

Lemma 7

(Local Search Lemma
[
30
]
)
.

Given a hypergraph
H=(V,{\mathcal{F}})
where
V
is the vertex set and
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq 2^{V}
is a family of subsets of
V
, we want to compute the minimum sized
V^{\prime}\subseteq V
such that for every
F\in{\mathcal{F}}
,
V^{\prime}\cap F\neq\emptyset
. If there exists a planar support of
H
, then the local search algorithm computes a
(1+\epsilon)
-approximate solution in time
n^{O(1/\epsilon^{2})}
, for
\epsilon>0
.

Lastly, we state the local search algorithm for covering the boundary of the input simple polygon
P
, where
k
is the local search parameter which is set to
c/\epsilon^{2}
for some appropriate constant
c
.

Algorithm 1

Local Search Algorithm to cover the boundary of
P
.

1:

A simple polygon
P\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

2:

{\mathcal{R}}=\{R_{1},\dots,R_{m}\}
such that
\partial P\subseteq\bigcup{\mathcal{R}}
.

3:

S\leftarrow{\mathcal{R}}^{K}(P)

4:

S^{c}\leftarrow{\mathcal{R}}^{K}(P)\setminus S

5:

while

\exists X\subseteq S,\exists Y\subseteq S^{c}
such that
|X|\leq k
,
|Y|<|X|
, and
(S\setminus X)\cup Y
covers
\partial P

do

6:

S\leftarrow(S\setminus X)\cup Y

7:

S^{c}\leftarrow{\mathcal{R}}^{K}(P)\setminus S

8:

end

while

9:

return

S

2.2
Intersection Patterns

Given a simple orthogonal polygon
P
, if two maximal rectangles
R
and
R^{\prime}
with respect to
P
have a non-empty intersection then they intersect in one of the following ways:

1.

Corner Intersection.

R
has exactly one corner of
R^{\prime}
in its interior and vice versa.

2.

Piercing Intersection.
The two vertical (resp. horizontal) sides of
R
intersect the two horizontal (resp. vertical) sides of
R^{\prime}
.

Furthermore, if
R
and
R^{\prime}
have a piercing intersection and one of the sides of
R
is contained in one of the sides of
R^{\prime}
then we say that the two rectangles are
aligned
to each other. See Figure
2
.

Figure 2
:
The left pair has a corner intersection. The middle and the right have a piercing intersection; in which the right pair are also aligned to each other.

Definition 8
.

Given a simple orthogonal polygon
P
and a set of maximal rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
with respect to
P
, define a partial order
({\mathcal{R}},\prec)
. If for every
R,R^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{R}}
such that
R
and
R^{\prime}
have a piercing intersection and the two vertical sides of
R
intersect the two horizontal sides of
R^{\prime}
then we say
R\prec R^{\prime}
.

More informally, we say
R\prec R^{\prime}
if they have a piercing intersection and the width of
R
is less than that of
R^{\prime}
.

Lastly, we would like to emphasize that we shall deal with intersections at the boundary of the polygon, i.e.,
R\cap R^{\prime}\cap\partial P\neq\emptyset
which may not be true even when
R\cap R^{\prime}\neq\emptyset
.

2.3
Left-Right Coloring

In this section we recall a few definitions and results from a series of work by de Fraysseix, Ossona de Mendez, and Rosenstiehl
[
14
]
.

Definition 9

(DFS-orientation)
.

Given an undirected graph
G=(V,E)
, its DFS-orientation is a directed graph
G^{\prime}=(V,T\uplus B)
where
T
is the set of the tree edges obtained by depth-first traversal of
G
starting from a fixed vertex in
V
, and
B=E\setminus T
is the set of cotree edges.

Thus,
T
defines a partial order on
V
and the root of
T
is the minimum vertex.
Given a tree edge
e=(u,v)
, return edges of
e
are the cotree edges
e^{\prime}=(v^{\prime},u^{\prime})
such that
v^{\prime}
is a descendant of
v
or
v=v^{\prime}
, and
u^{\prime}
is an ancestor of
u
that is different from
u
.
Define a mapping
low:E\rightarrow V
, where for every
e\in E
,
low(e)
is the minimum vertex that can be reached by a directed path starting with
e
and containing at most one cotree edge.

Definition 10

(Left-Right Coloring)
.

Let
G^{\prime}=(V,T\uplus B)
be a DFS-oriented graph. A bipartition of its co-tree edges
B=L\uplus R
into two classes, referred to as left and right, is called left-right coloring,
if for every
v\in V
and outgoing edges
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
from
v
, the following two conditions are satisfied.

•

All return edges of
e_{i}
ending strictly higher than
low(e_{j})
belong to one class, and

•

All return edges of
e_{j}
ending strictly higher than
low(e_{i})
belong to the other class.

Lemma 11
.

[
14
]

A finite graph
G
is planar if and only if there exists a Left-Right coloring of the cotree edges with respect to a depth-first-tree
T
on
G
.

3
Family of Rectangles with a Kernel

The main results of this section are Lemmata
14
and
15
, which in turn are used to prove Lemma
16
in Section
4
.
(Please see Section
A
for missing details.)
In the following, we define a kernel of a rectangle family.
Informally, Lemma
14
says that if a rectangle family has a star graph as its support, then the rectangle corresponding to the center of the star belongs to the kernel. Lemma
15
states that a family of rectangles without its kernel still has a planar support.

Definition 12

(Kernel of a Family)
.

Given a family of maximal rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
with respect to a simple polygon
P
, the kernel of
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
is defined as follows.

ker({\mathcal{R}}):=\displaystyle\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\in\partial P\\
|{\mathcal{R}}_{p}|\geq 2\end{subarray}}{\mathcal{R}}_{p}

where
{\mathcal{R}}_{p}:=\{R\in{\mathcal{R}}\mid p\in R\}
.

A family
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
with non-empty kernel
ker({\mathcal{R}})
is called
proper
if for every
R\in{\mathcal{R}}
there exists
p\in\partial P
such that
ker({\mathcal{R}})\cup\{R\}\subseteq{\mathcal{R}}_{p}
. See Figure
3
for an example of a family that is not proper.

Figure 3
:
(\{R_{i},R_{j},R_{k},R_{\ell},R_{c}\},\partial P)
has a non-empty kernel
\{R_{c}\}
but it is not proper. Whereas,
(\{R_{i},R_{j},R_{c}\},\partial P)
is proper and has the same kernel. Note that
\partial P
is drawn with red lines.

Observation 13
.

Every proper family
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
with a non-empty kernel has a support that is a star graph with the center vertex corresponding to a kernel rectangle.

In the following, we show that the converse is also true.

Lemma 14
.

Given a family of maximal rectangles
{\mathcal{R}}
with respect to a simple orthogonal polygon
P
. If there exists a minimal support graph
G
of
({\mathcal{R}},\partial P)
such that
G
is a star graph then the rectangle corresponding to the center of
G
belongs to
ker({\mathcal{R}})
and

Fix some

and let

be the vertex in

corresponding to

Consider a DFS tree

We replace the edge

For every

7
Conclusion

It is easy to see that
\theta_{c}\leq\theta_{b}\leq\theta
, where
\theta,\theta_{b},\theta_{c}
are the sizes of the minimum interior, boundary, and corner covers, respectively.
What is not known are the upper bounds. In particular, what is the smallest value of
\beta
such that
\theta\leq\beta\cdot\theta_{b}
? Thus, our PTAS for the boundary cover problem for simple polygons would imply a
(\beta+\epsilon)
-approximation for the corresponding interior cover problem.
Heinrich-Litan and Lübbecke
[
22
]
, presented a number of computational results on the Polygon Covering problem. Notably, their experiments suggest
\theta\leq 2\theta_{b}
. An open question is to prove
\beta
to be less than
2
, which would improve the
2
-approximation algorithm of Franzblau
[
16
]
. In Figure
7
, we present a lower bound instance that implies
\beta\geq 3/2
.

Figure 7
:
The boundary of the polygon is drawn with thick black lines. Here the rectangles in the minimum boundary cover are
6
thin vertical rectangles and
6
wide horizontal rectangles. Additionally for the minimum interior cover, we have
5
blue squares. Thus,
\theta_{b}=12
and
\theta=17
. This polygon can be generalized such that for every
k\geq 2
,
\theta_{b}=2k+2
and
\theta=3k+2
. Thus,
\beta\geq 3/2
.

By far, the most challenging open question is whether there exists an
O(1)
-approximation algorithm running in polynomial time for the
Interior Cover
problem for orthogonal polygons possibly with holes?
Can Erdős’ question (see Subsection
1.2.1
) be answered in affirmative? Does there exist a primal-dual algorithm that could do so by returning interior cover and antirectangle solutions whose sizes are within a constant factor of each other?

Possibly, an easier task would be to settle the status of the
Corner Cover
problem for simple polygons. To the best of our knowledge we neither know a polynomial time exact algorithm or a proof of its
NP
-hardness.
