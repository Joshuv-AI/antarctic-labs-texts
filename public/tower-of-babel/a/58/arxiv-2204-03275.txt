Abstract
. A system of drift-diffusion equations for the electron, hole, and oxygene vacancy densities in a semiconductor, coupled to the Poisson equation for the electric potential, is analyzed in a bounded domain with mixed Dirichlet–Neumann boundary conditions. This system describes the dynamics of charge carriers in a memristor device. Memristors can be seen as nonlinear resistors with memory, mimicking the conductance response of biological synapses. In the fast-relaxation limit, the system reduces to a drift-diffusion system for the oxygene vacancy density and electric potential, which is often used in neuromorphic applications. The following results are proved: the global existence of weak solutions to the full system in any space dimension; the uniform-in-time boundedness of the solutions to the full system and the fast-relaxation limit in two space dimensions; the global existence and weak-strong uniqueness analysis of the reduced system. Numerical experiments in one space dimension illustrate the behavior of the solutions and reproduce hysteresis effects in the current-voltage characteristics.

1.
Introduction

The evolution of the microelectronics industry was influenced for more than
50 years by Moore’s law that predicts a doubling of the number of transistors on
a microchip about every two years. As this observation is going to cease to
apply because of physical scaling limitations, novel technologies or computing
approaches are needed. Neuromorphic computing seems to be a promising avenue.
It is a concept developed by C. Mead in the late 1980s to implement aspects of
(biological) neuronal networks as analog or digital copies on electric circuits.

A promising device as technology enabler of neuromorphic computing is the memristor,
which was postulated in
[
6
]
. We understand a memristor as a nonlinear
resistor with memory showing a resistive switching behavior. For a historical debate
of the memristor definition, we refer to
[
25
]
. Artificial neurons and
synapses can be built by using, e.g., ferroelectric materials, phase-change
materials, or memristive materials
[
16
]
. The oxide-based memristor consists
of a thin titanium dioxide film between two metal electrodes
[
21
]
. The oxygen
vacancies act as charge carriers. When an electric field is applied, the oxygen
vacancies drift and change the boundary between the low- and high-resistance layers.
In this way, memristors are able to mimic the conductance response of synapses.
Advantages of these devices are the low power consumption,
short switching time, and nano-size, allowing for high-density circuit architectures.

Memristor devices can be described by compact models,
relating the charge and flux and using the memristor Ohm law
[
21
]
.
In this paper, we are interested
in the internal physical processes of an oxide-based memristor,
and we focus on diffusive models like those in
[
11
,
23
]
.
They consist of drift-diffusion equations for the electron, hole, and oxygen
vacancy densities and the Poisson equation for the electric potential.

Since the electron-lattice relaxation is much faster than the oxygen vacancy drift,
it is sufficient to determine the electron and hole densities from
the stationary equations, while the oxygen vacancy density still satisfies the
transient equation. In this paper, we make this limit rigorous.
More precisely, we prove the global existence of weak solutions to the
full transient model in any space dimension
and the fast-relaxation limit in two space dimensions.
Furthermore, we analyze the limiting model (existence, weak-strong uniqueness)
and present some finite-volume simulations in one space dimension.
Up to our knowledge, this is the first mathematical analysis of a charge transport
model for memristors.

1.1.
Model equations and mathematical difficulties

The scaled equations for the electron density
n
, hole density
p
,
oxygen vacancy density
D
(or charged mobile
n
-type dopant density),
and electric potential
V
are given by

(1)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}n=\operatorname{div}J_{n},\quad J_{n}=\nabla n-n\nabla V,

(2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon\partial_{t}p=-\operatorname{div}J_{p},\quad J_{p}=-(\nabla p+p\nabla V),

(3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D=-\operatorname{div}J_{D},\quad J_{D}=-(\nabla D+D\nabla V),

(4)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\Delta V=n-p-D+A(x)\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\ t>0,

where
\varepsilon>0
is a small parameter describing the speed of relaxation to
the steady state,
\lambda>0
is the (scaled) Debye length,

J_{n}
,
J_{p}
, and
J_{D}
are the current densities of the electrons, holes, and
oxygen vacancies, respectively,
and
A(x)
is the given immobile
p
-type dopant (acceptor) density.
Following
[
23
]
, we neglect recombination-generation terms.
We use initial and physically motivated mixed Dirichlet–Neumann boundary conditions:

(5)

\displaystyle n(\cdot,0)=n^{I},\quad p(\cdot,0)=p^{I},\quad D(\cdot,0)=D^{I}

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,

(6)

\displaystyle n=\overline{n},\quad p=\overline{p},\quad V=\overline{V}

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{D},\ t>0,

(7)

\displaystyle J_{n}\cdot\nu=J_{p}\cdot\nu=\nabla V\cdot\nu=0

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{N},\ t>0,

(8)

\displaystyle J_{D}\cdot\nu=0

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{on }\partial\Omega,\ t>0.

This means that we prescribe the electron and hole densities as well as the
applied voltage on the Ohmic contacts
\Gamma_{D}
, while
\Gamma_{N}

models the union of insulating boundary segments.
The boundary is assumed to be not transparent to the oxygen vacancies, so
we assume no-flux conditions for
D
. This gives one of the mathematical
difficulties of the model, since we cannot perform certain partial integrations
as for
n
and
p
.

Another difficulty comes from the fact that we consider three species instead
two. Indeed, the quadratic drift terms can be estimated in the two-species system
for
(n,p,V)

by exploiting a monotonicity property. Assuming for simplicity that
\overline{n}=\overline{p}=0
,
using
n
and
p
as test functions in the
weak formulations of (
1
) and (
2
), respectively,
and adding both equations, we find from (
4
) that

(9)

\displaystyle\frac{\varepsilon}{2}\frac{d}{dt}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}(n^{2}+p^{2})dx+\int_{\Omega}(|\nabla n|^{2}+|\nabla p|^{2})dx=\int_{\Omega}n\nabla V\cdot\nabla ndx-\int_{\Omega}p\nabla V\cdot\nabla pdx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\nabla(n^{2}-p^{2})\cdot\nabla Vdx=-\frac{1}{2\lambda^{2}}\int_{\Omega}(n^{2}-p^{2})(n-p-D+A)dx

\displaystyle\leq-C(\lambda,D,A)\int_{\Omega}(n^{2}+p^{2})dx,

since
(n^{2}-p^{2})(n-p)\geq 0
and
D
is fixed in the two-species model.
This computation reduces the cubic term to
a quadratic one, which can be treated by Gronwall’s lemma. This idea cannot
be applied to the three-species model.

1.2.
State of the art and strategy of our proofs

These difficulties explain why there are only few analytical results in the
literature on
n
-species drift-diffusion equations with
n>2
.
They have been derived in
[
26
]
from a kinetic
Vlasov–Poisson–Fokker–Planck system in the diffusion limit.
In
[
1
]
, a three-species system similar to ours is considered,
in the context of corrosion models, but only a stability analysis of a
finite-volume scheme has been performed. The authors of
[
24
]

analyze a four-species system, but their model includes drift terms only
in the equations for the electrons and holes, which enables the authors to use the
monotonicity property explained above. General existence results for an
n
-species
model have been proved in
[
14
]
for an abstract drift operator
imposing suitable smoothing conditions.
Estimates in Lebesgue and Hölder spaces for
n
-species systems
have been derived in
[
5
]
without an existence analysis.
More general models involving positive semidefinite, nondiagonal
mobility matrices can be found in, e.g.,
[
7
]
.
A global existence analysis for
n
-species models was performed in

[
3
,
9
,
10
]
(and the large-time asymptotics in
[
8
]
)
assuming at most two space dimensions. This restriction can be understood as follows.

Instead of integrating by parts as in (
9
), the idea is to use
an elliptic estimate for
V
. Because of the mixed boundary conditions, we cannot
expect full elliptic regularity for the Poisson equation, but
there exists
r_{0}>2
such that
[
12
]

(10)

\|\nabla V\|_{L^{r_{0}}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(1+\|n-p-D+A\|_{L^{2r_{0}/(r_{0}+2)}(\Omega)}\big);

see Lemma
20
in the Appendix for the precise statement.
Using
\log n-\log\overline{n}
as a test function in the weak formulation of (
1
),
we can derive a uniform estimate for
n\log n
in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
;
see (
12
) below.
Then the Hölder inequality with
r_{0}^{\prime}=2r_{0}/(r_{0}-2)
and a generalized
Gagliardo–Nirenberg inequality (see Lemma
19
below) lead to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle n\nabla V\cdot\nabla ndx\leq\|n\|_{L^{r_{0}^{\prime}}(\Omega)}\|\nabla V\|_{L^{r_{0}}(\Omega)}\|\nabla n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq C\|n\|_{L^{r^{\prime}_{0}}(\Omega)}\big(1+\|n-p-D+A\|_{L^{2r_{0}/(r_{0}+2)}(\Omega)}\big)\|\nabla n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\|\nabla n\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{1+2d/(d+2)}+C(n,p,D,\delta),

where
\delta>0
, and
C(n,p,D,\delta)>0
depends on the
L^{1}\log L^{1}
norms of

n
,
p
, and
D
. The first term on the left-hand side can be absorbed, for
sufficiently small
\delta>0
, by the gradient term coming from the diffusion part
if the exponent is not larger than two, and this is the case if and only if
d\leq 2
.

Our strategy is different. As in
[
9
]
, the key estimate comes from the
free energy functional

(11)

\displaystyle H[n,p,D,V]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg\{n\bigg(\log\frac{n}{\overline{n}}-1\bigg)+p\bigg(\log\frac{p}{\overline{p}}-1\bigg)+D(\log D-1+\overline{V})\bigg\}dx

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V-\overline{V})|^{2}dx.

The first integral models the thermodynamic entropy, while the second integral
corresponds to the electric energy. We prove in Theorem
1
that

(12)

\frac{dH}{dt}+\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{n}{2\varepsilon}|\nabla(\log n-V)|^{2}+\frac{p}{2\varepsilon}|\nabla(\log p+V)|^{2}+\frac{D}{2}|\nabla(\log D+V)|^{2}\bigg)dx\leq C(\overline{n},\overline{p},\overline{V}).

While the authors of
[
9
]
have used this free energy inequality
as the starting point to derive iteratively
L^{\infty}
estimates in two
space dimensions, we use
another argument that allows us to obtain a global existence result in any
space dimension.

More precisely, we prove that (
12
) implies an
L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}(\Omega))

bound for
\sqrt{n_{k}}
(as well as
\sqrt{p_{k}}
and
\sqrt{D_{k}})
,
where
(n_{k},p_{k},D_{k},V_{k})
is a solution to an approximate problem with
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.
This bound is not sufficient to deduce strong compactness.
By a cutoff argument, we show that
n_{k}
(as well as
p_{k}
and
D_{k}
) are bounded
in
L^{r}(0,T;W^{1,r}(\Omega_{\delta}))
, where
\Omega_{\delta}=\{x\in\Omega:\operatorname{dist}(x,\partial\Omega)>\delta\}
and
r>1
. By the Aubin–Lions lemma, we
conclude strong
L^{s}(\Omega_{\delta}\times(0,T))
convergence of
n_{k}
for
s<r
, and
by the Theorem of de la Vallée–Poussin, weak
L^{1}(\Omega\times(0,T))
convergence
of
n_{k}
. Then we deduce from a Cantor diagonal argument the strong

L^{1}(\Omega\times(0,T))
convergence of
n_{k}
(as well as
p_{k}
and
D_{k}
).
This is the key argument to prove the global existence of weak solutions to
(
1
)–(
8
) in any space dimension. Our
strategy extends the results of
[
3
,
9
,
10
]
.

The second main result of this paper is the fast-relaxation limit
\varepsilon\to 0

for the solutions
(n_{\varepsilon},p_{\varepsilon},D_{\varepsilon},V_{\varepsilon})
to
(
1
)–(
8
). We expect that
J_{n}=J_{p}=0
holds in the limit,
leading to
n_{\varepsilon}=c_{n}e^{V_{\varepsilon}}
and
p_{\varepsilon}=c_{p}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}
, where

c_{n}
,
c_{p}>0
are constants determined by the Dirichlet boundary data.
The limit
\varepsilon\to 0
was already
performed in a two-species drift-diffusion system
[
19
]
,
exploiting a uniform lower positive bound for
n_{\varepsilon}
and
p_{\varepsilon}
.
Unfortunately, this argument cannot be used for our three-species system,
and we need another idea.

The starting point is again the free energy inequality (
12
), showing that

\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}\nabla(\log n_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon})\to 0,\quad\sqrt{p_{\varepsilon}}\nabla(\log p_{\varepsilon}+V_{\varepsilon})\to 0

strongly in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, where
Q_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
.
Since equation (
3
) for
D_{\varepsilon}
does not contain
\varepsilon
, we obtain

D_{\varepsilon}\to D_{0}
strongly in
L^{1}(Q_{T})
from the Aubin–Lions lemma.
As in
[
19
]
, the key step is to prove the strong convergence of

\nabla V_{\varepsilon}
, but in contrast to that work, we are lacking some estimates.
We formulate the Poisson equation for
V_{\varepsilon}
as

\lambda^{2}\Delta V_{\varepsilon}=c_{n}e^{V_{\varepsilon}}-c_{p}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}-D_{\varepsilon}+A(x)+E_{\varepsilon},

where
E_{\varepsilon}
is an error term.
Similar as in
[
19
]
, we exploit the monotonicity of
V_{\varepsilon}\mapsto c_{n}e^{V_{\varepsilon}}-c_{p}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}
to prove that
(\nabla V_{\varepsilon})
is a Cauchy sequence
and hence convergent in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
. The novelty is the proof of

E_{\varepsilon}\to 0
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Here, we need an
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
bound for
V_{\varepsilon}
,
and this is possible (only) in two space dimensions, according to
[
12
]
:

\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(1+\|(n_{\varepsilon}-p_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon}+A)\log|n_{\varepsilon}-p_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon}+A|\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\big)\leq C.

We infer that
\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\to\nabla V_{0}
strongly in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
,
and
V_{0}
solves the limiting Poisson equation

\lambda^{2}\Delta V_{0}=c_{n}e^{V_{0}}-c_{p}e^{-V_{0}}-D_{0}+A(x)
.
Note that, in contrast to
[
19
]
,
we need the restriction to two space dimensions.

1.3.
Main results

We impose the following assumptions.

(A1)

Domain:
\Omega\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(
d\geq 1
) is a bounded domain with Lipschitz
boundary
\partial\Omega=\Gamma_{D}\cup\Gamma_{N}
,
\operatorname{meas}(\Gamma_{D})>0
,

\Gamma_{N}

\partial\Omega
.

(A2)

Data:
T>0
,
\varepsilon>0
,
\lambda>0
,

.

(A3)

Boundary data:
\overline{n}
,
\overline{p}
,
\overline{V}\in W^{1,\infty}(\Omega)
satisfy

\overline{n}
,
\overline{p}>0
in
\Omega
.

(A4)

Initial data:
n^{I}
,
p^{I}
,
D^{I}\in L^{2}(\Omega)

satisfy
n^{I}
,
p^{I}
,
D^{I}\geq 0
in
\Omega
.

We set
Q_{T}=\Omega\times(0,T)
,

H_{D}^{1}(\Omega)=\{u\in H^{1}(\Omega):u=0
on
\Gamma_{D}\}
,
and we introduce the initial electric potential
V^{I}-\overline{V}\in H_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
as the
unique solution to

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\Delta V^{I}=n^{I}-p^{I}-D^{I}+A\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,

\displaystyle V^{I}=\overline{V}\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{D},\quad\nabla V^{I}\cdot\nu=0\quad\mbox{on }\Gamma_{N}.

The boundary data in Assumption (A3) are supposed to be time-independent to
simplify the computations.
In two space dimensions, it is sufficient to assume in Assumption (A4) that

n^{I}\log n^{I}
,
p^{I}\log p^{I}
,
D^{I}\log D^{I}\in L^{1}(\Omega)
since

implies that
n^{I}-p^{I}-D^{I}+A\in H_{D}^{1}(\Omega)^{\prime}
.
The regularity conditions in Assumptions (A3) and (A4) are imposed for simplicity;
they can be slightly weakened.

Our first main result is the global existence of weak solutions in any
space dimension.

Theorem 1

(Global existence)
.

Let Assumptions (A1)–(A4) hold. Then there exists a weak solution
(n,p,D,V)

to (
1
)–(
8
) satisfying

\displaystyle n\log n,\,p\log p,\,D\log D\in L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega)),

\displaystyle\sqrt{n},\,\sqrt{p},\,\sqrt{D}\in L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}(\Omega)),\quad J_{n},\,J_{p},\,J_{D}\in L^{1}(Q_{T}),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}n,\,\partial_{t}p,\in L^{1}(0,T;X^{\prime}),\quad\partial_{t}D\in L^{1}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{\prime}),\quad V\in L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)),

where
X:=H^{s}(\Omega)\cap H_{D}^{1}(\Omega)
and
s>1+d/2
.
This solution satisfies the free energy inequality

(13)

\displaystyle H[(n,p,D,V)(t)]

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{n}{2\varepsilon}|\nabla(\log n-V)|^{2}+\frac{p}{2\varepsilon}|\nabla(\log p+V)|^{2}

\displaystyle{}+\frac{D}{2}|\nabla(\log D+V)|^{2}\bigg)dxds\leq H^{I}+C(H^{I},\Lambda_{\varepsilon},T),

where the initial free energy
H^{I}[n,p,D,V]
is defined in (
11
),

H^{I}:=H[n^{I},p^{I},D^{I},V^{I}]
,

(14)

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}:=\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\big(\|\nabla(\log\overline{n}-\overline{V})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{2}+\|\nabla(\log\overline{p}+\overline{V})\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}^{2}\big),

and it holds that
C(H^{I},\Lambda_{\varepsilon},T)=0
if
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}=0
.

The property
\Lambda_{\varepsilon}=0
means that the boundary data are in thermal
equilibrium. In this case, the free energy is a nonincreasing function of time.
The entropy production in (
13
) is understood in the sense

n|\nabla(\log n-V)|^{2}=|2\nabla\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n}\nabla V|^{2}
, i.e.. we have

2\nabla\sqrt{n}-\sqrt{n}\nabla V\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
.

We approximate (
1
)–(
4
) by truncating the drift term and
proving the existence of a solution
(n_{k},p_{k},D_{k},V_{k})
to the approximate
problem. Estimates uniform in the truncation parameter
k
are obtained from
an approximate free energy inequality, similar to (
12
). As explained
before, we also derive uniform estimates in the domain
\Omega_{\delta}
,
which are needed to conclude the strong
L^{1}(Q_{T})
convergence of the approximate
solution. Because of low regularity, the difficulty is to identify the weak
limit of a truncated version of
2\nabla\sqrt{n_{k}}-\sqrt{n_{k}}\nabla V_{k}
. This is
done by combining the free energy estimates and the Aubin–Lions lemma,
applied in the domain
\Omega_{\delta}\times(0,T)
.

Similarly, as in
[
9
]
, we can prove, in the two-dimensional case,
that the weak solution from

is bounded uniformly in time.

Theorem 2

(Uniform
L^{\infty}
bounds)
.

Let (A1)–(A4) hold, let
d\leq 2
, and let

n^{I}
,
p^{I}
,
D^{I}\in L^{\infty}(\Omega)
.
Furthermore, let
(n,p,D,V)
be the weak solution to
(
1
)–(
8
) constructed in Theorem

.
Then there exists a constant
C(\varepsilon)>0
depending on
\varepsilon

such that for all

,

\|n(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|p(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}+\|D(t)\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C(\varepsilon).

The theorem is proved by an Alikakos-type iteration method.
The restriction to two space dimensions comes from the regularity
(
10
) for the electric potential. The rough idea of the proof is
to choose
n_{k}^{q-1}
in the weak formulation of (
1
) (and similarly
for
p_{k}
and
D_{k}
) and to derive an estimate in
L^{q}(Q_{T})
, which is uniform
in
k
and

. Then the limit

,

gives the desired
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})

bound. Since
n_{k}^{q-1}
is generally not an

function for

,

\varepsilon=0
in (
1
)–(

(15)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{0}=\operatorname{div}(\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0}),

,

, and the electron and hole densities

Theorem 3

(Existence and weak-strong uniqueness for the limit problem)

Let Assumptions (A1)–(A4) hold and let
\overline{n}\geq c>0
,
\overline{p}\geq c>0
in
\Omega
.
Then there exists a bounded weak solution

(D_{0},V_{0})
to (
15
)–(
18
). Moreover,
if
(D,V)
is a weak solution and

(D_{0},V_{0})
a bounded strong solution to (

)–(

)
satisfying

\inf_{Q_{T}}D_{0}>0,\ D_{0},\nabla\log D_{0},V_{0},\nabla V_{0}\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T}),\ \partial_{t}D_{0},\partial_{t}V_{0}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega)),

,

in
\Omega\times(0,T)
.

For the proof of the existence of a weak solution to
(
15
)–(
18
), we use the techniques of the proof of
Theorem
1
. Let
(D_{0,k},V_{0,k})
be a solution to a

, which is not sufficient to perform
the limit

in the nonlinear Poisson equation.
We need the strong convergence of

. Our idea is to derive

, which follows
from the free energy inequality for the reduced model or directly from
the nonlinear Poisson equation. Second, we
prove that
(V_{0,k})
is a Cauchy sequence. This is done by taking a particular
nonlinear test function in the Poisson equation, satisfied by the difference

V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}
, which leads to

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{2+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2}}{2(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{5/4}}|\nabla(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})|^{2}dxdt

The weak-strong uniqueness property is based on an estimation of the

\displaystyle H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}+c_{n}e^{V_{0}}f_{0}(V-V_{0})+c_{p}e^{-V_{0}}f_{0}(V_{0}-V)\bigg)dx

Lemma 5

The free energy inequality (

collected in the following lemma.

Lemma 8

(Global estimates for the approximate problem)
.

Let
(n_{k},p_{k},D_{k},V_{k})
be a weak solution to the approximate problem
(

)–(

\displaystyle\|g_{k}(n_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|g_{k}(p_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|g_{k}(D_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(32)

(33)

\displaystyle\big\|\sqrt{T_{k}(n_{k})}\nabla V_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\big\|\sqrt{T_{k}(p_{k})}\nabla V_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle{}+\big\|\sqrt{T_{k}(D_{k})}\nabla V_{k}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\|h_{k}(n_{k})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}(\Omega))}+\|h_{k}(p_{k})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}(\Omega))}+\|h_{k}(D_{k})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;W^{1,1}(\Omega))}

Proof.

),
and (

in the Appendix shows that

\big\|\sqrt{T_{k}(n_{k})}\big\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))}\leq C+C\|g_{k}(n_{k})\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}^{1/2}\leq C.

bound for

from the free energy

which proves (

Next, by the bound on the entropy production from (

\displaystyle\|\nabla h_{k}(n_{k})\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle=\big\|\nabla h_{k}(n_{k})-\sqrt{T_{k}(n_{k})}\nabla V_{k}\big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\big\|\sqrt{T_{k}(n_{k})}\nabla V_{k}\big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}\leq C.

Finally, we deduce from the proof of Lemma

in the Appendix that

such that (

and

The estimates of the previous lemma are not sufficient to show that the

Lemma 9

(Local estimates for the approximate problem)
.

Let
(n_{k},p_{k},D_{k},V_{k})
be a weak solution to the approximate problem
(
19
)–(
26
) and let

.
Then there exists a constant

depending on
\delta
but not on

or
\varepsilon
, such that

(36)

\displaystyle\|n_{k}\|_{L^{r}(0,T;W^{1,r}(\Omega_{\delta}))}+\|p_{k}\|_{L^{r}(0,T;W^{1,r}(\Omega_{\delta}))}+\|D_{k}\|_{L^{r}(0,T;W^{1,r}(\Omega_{\delta}))}

\displaystyle\leq C(\delta),

(37)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{t}n_{k}\|_{L^{r}(0,T;W^{-1,r}(\Omega_{\delta}))}+\|\partial_{t}p_{k}\|_{L^{r}(0,T;W^{-1,r}(\Omega_{\delta}))}+\|\partial_{t}D_{k}\|_{L^{r}(0,T;W^{1,r^{\prime}}(\Omega_{\delta})^{\prime})}

\displaystyle\leq C(\delta).

We define the cutoff function
\xi_{\delta}\in C_{0}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
such that

in

\xi_{\delta}=1
in
\Omega_{\delta}
,

\xi_{\delta}=0
in

\nabla h_{k}(n_{k})=T_{k}(n_{k})^{-1/2}\nabla n_{k}
imply that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\big(|\nabla h_{k}(n_{k})|^{2}+T_{k}(n_{k})|\nabla V_{k}|^{2}\big)\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt=\int_{0}^{T}\big|\nabla h_{k}(n_{k})-\sqrt{T_{k}(n_{k})}\nabla V_{k}\big|^{2}\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle{}+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla n_{k}\cdot\nabla V_{k}\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt\leq C+2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\nabla n_{k}\cdot\nabla V_{k}\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt.

and

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\nabla(n_{k}-p_{k}-D_{k})\cdot\nabla V_{k}\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\lambda^{2}}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(n_{k}-p_{k}-D_{k})(n_{k}-p_{k}-D_{k}+A)\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}-2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}(n_{k}-p_{k}-D_{k})\xi_{\delta}\nabla V_{k}\cdot\nabla\xi_{\delta}dxdt

\nabla V_{k}

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(T_{k}(n_{k})+T_{k}(p_{k})+T_{k}(D_{k})\big)|\nabla V_{k}|^{2}\xi_{\delta}^{2}dxdt

and

Next, we use the Gagliardo–Nirenberg

Thanks to estimates (

by the weak

)

and

. Indeed, we infer from

Now, the limit

where the constant

depends on the

.

Proof.

The free energy inequality implies that

since

∎

The following lemma provides

Lemma 12
.

Let

and

,

,

.
Then there exists

, depending on the
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))

,
p_{k}\log p_{k}
,
D_{k}\log D_{k}
and on

,

(and possibly on

\|n_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}+\|p_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}+\|D_{k}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\leq C(\varepsilon,k).

Proof.

Let
q\geq 2
and
\|D^{I}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}<M<L
.
We set
[z]_{L}=\min\{L,z\}
,
z_{+}=\max\{0,z\}
,
and
\phi_{L}(z)=([z]_{L}-M)_{+}
for

. Then

\int_{0}^{z}\phi_{L}(s)^{q-1}ds\geq\frac{1}{q}\phi_{L}(z)^{q}\quad\mbox{for }z\geq 0.

Because of the truncation,
\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q-1}
is an admissible test function
in the weak formulation of (
21
). Observing that the
definition of

shows that

\int_{0}^{t}\langle\partial_{t}D_{k},\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q-1}\rangle ds\geq\frac{1}{q}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{L}(D_{k}(t))^{q}dx,

we obtain from (

), Hölder’s inequality, and
T_{k}(D_{k})\leq k

(44)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\phi_{L}(D_{k}(t))^{q}dx+\frac{4}{q^{2}}(q-1)\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}|^{2}dxds

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}T_{k}(D_{k})\nabla V_{k}\cdot\nabla\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q-1}dxds

\displaystyle\leq Ck\int_{0}^{t}\|\nabla V_{k}\|_{L^{r_{0}}(\Omega)}\|\nabla\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2-1}\|_{L^{r^{\prime}_{0}}(\Omega)}ds,

By definition of the

By Lemma

\displaystyle\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2-1}\|_{L^{r_{0}^{\prime}}(\Omega)}

\displaystyle=\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{s}(\Omega)}^{1-2/q}

\displaystyle\leq C\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{(1-1/s)(1-2/q)}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{(1/s)(1-2/q)}

\displaystyle\leq C+C\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{1-1/s}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{1/s}.

Inserting these estimates into (
44

for an arbitrary
\delta>0
, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\phi_{L}

\displaystyle(D_{k}(t))\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+C\int_{0}^{t}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}ds

\displaystyle\leq Ckq+C(k)q\int_{0}^{t}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds+Ckq\int_{0}^{t}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2-1/s}\|\phi_{L}(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}^{1/s}ds

It remains to choose a sufficiently small

last term on the right-hand side and to apply Lemma

\|\phi_{L}(D_{k}(t))\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C,\quad t\in(0,T),

where

does not depend on

and consequently,

in
Q_{T}
.
The
L^{\infty}
bounds for

and

,
respectively.
∎

We proceed with the proof of Theorem

, which is
technically similar to the previous proof.
Let
q\geq 2
and
M>\|D^{I}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}
.
We set

for

\displaystyle\|e^{t}

\displaystyle\phi(D_{k}(t))\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\|\phi(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}ds-C\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\|\phi(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds

recalling that

\displaystyle\|e^{t}

\displaystyle\phi(D_{k}(t))\|_{L^{q}(\Omega)}^{q}+C\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\|\phi(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}ds-C\int_{0}^{t}e^{s}\|\phi(D_{k})^{q/2}\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}ds

Choosing

where

and

(but depending on

).

in
\Omega
,

. The

4.
Proof of Theorem
3

We start with the proof of some estimates.

4.1.
A priori estimates

The free energy of the limit problem is defined as

\displaystyle H_{0}[D_{0},V_{0}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(D_{0}\log\frac{D_{0}}{\overline{D}}-D_{0}+\overline{n}f_{0}(V_{0}-\overline{V})+\overline{p}f_{0}(\overline{V}-V_{0})+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}|\nabla(V_{0}-\overline{V})|^{2}\bigg)dx,

where
\overline{D}:=\exp(-\overline{V})
and the function
f_{0}
is given by

f_{0}(s)=(s-1)e^{s}+1
for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
.

Lemma 13

(Free energy inequality for the limit problem)
.

Let
(D_{0},V_{0})
be a smooth solution to (
15
)–(
18
).
Then there exists a constant
C>0
, only depending on
H_{0}[D^{I},V^{I}]
and
T
,
such that

H_{0}[D_{0}(t),V_{0}(t)]+\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{t}\int_{\Omega}D_{0}|\nabla(\log D_{0}+V_{0})|^{2}dxds\leq C,\quad 0<t<T.

Proof.

We calculate the time derivative of the free energy, using the definitions

n_{0}=\overline{n}\exp(V_{0}-\overline{V})
,
p_{0}=\overline{p}\exp(\overline{V}-V_{0})
:

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{0}}{dt}[D_{0},V_{0}]

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\partial_{t}D_{0}\log\frac{D_{0}}{\overline{D}}+(V_{0}-\overline{V})\partial_{t}\big(\overline{n}e^{V_{0}-\overline{V}}-\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}-V_{0}}\big)+\lambda^{2}\nabla(V_{0}-\overline{V})\cdot\nabla\partial_{t}V_{0}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\partial_{t}D_{0}\log\frac{D_{0}}{\overline{D}}+(V_{0}-\overline{V})\partial_{t}\big(\overline{n}e^{V_{0}-\overline{V}}-\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}-V_{0}}\big)

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}-(V_{0}-\overline{V})\partial_{t}(n_{0}-p_{0}-D_{0}+A(x))\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\log\frac{D_{0}}{\overline{D}}+V_{0}-\overline{V}\bigg)\partial_{t}D_{0}dx.

Inserting the equation for
D_{0}
and integrating by parts gives

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{0}}{dt}[D_{0},V_{0}]

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}D_{0}\nabla(\log D_{0}+V_{0})\cdot\nabla\big((\log D_{0}+V_{0})-(\log\overline{D}+\overline{V})\big)dx

\displaystyle\leq-\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}D_{0}|\nabla(\log D_{0}+V_{0})|^{2}dx+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}D_{0}|\nabla(\log\overline{D}+\overline{V})|^{2}dx.

The last term can be estimated from above by
CH_{0}[D_{0},V_{0}]
. Then Gronwall’s
lemma completes the proof.
∎

The free energy inequality yields the following uniform bounds:

\displaystyle\|D_{0}\log D_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\|V_{0}\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))}

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\|V_{0}\exp|V_{0}|\|_{L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))}+\big\|2\nabla\sqrt{D_{0}}+\sqrt{D_{0}}\nabla V_{0}\big\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}

\displaystyle\leq C,

since
2\nabla\sqrt{D_{0}}+\sqrt{D_{0}}\nabla V_{0}=\sqrt{D_{0}}\nabla(\log D_{0}+V_{0})

is uniformly bounded in
L^{2}(Q_{T})
.

4.2.
Approximate problem

Recalling
T_{k}(s)=\max\{0,\min\{k,s\}\}
for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
we introduce the approximate problem

(45)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}D_{0,k}=\operatorname{div}(\nabla D_{0,k}+T_{k}(D_{0,k})\nabla V_{0,k}),

(46)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\Delta V_{0,k}=c_{n}e^{V_{0,k}}-c_{p}e^{-V_{0,k}}-T_{k}(D_{0,k})+A(x)\quad\mbox{in }\Omega,\ t>0,

(47)

\displaystyle V_{0,k}=\overline{V}\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{D},\quad\nabla V_{0,k}\cdot\nu=0\mbox{ on }\Gamma_{N},

(48)

\displaystyle(\nabla D_{0,k}+T_{k}(D_{0,k})\nabla V_{0,k})\cdot\nu=0\mbox{ on }\partial\Omega,\ t>0,\quad D_{0,k}(0)=D^{I}\mbox{ in }\Omega.

The existence of weak solutions to this problem can be proved similarly
as in Section
2
. The only difference is the derivation of
an estimate for
V_{k}
in the Lax–Milgram argument because of the nonlinear
Poisson equation. As the nonlinearity is monotone, the

L^{2}
norm of
\nabla V_{k}
can be bounded in terms of the
L^{2}
norm of
D_{0,k}
,
like in the proof of Lemma
5
.

To pass to the limit
k\to\infty
, we need additional estimates.
First, the free energy inequality gives the following bound,
which can be also directly
proved from the Poisson equation using the test function
V_{0,k}-\overline{V}
:

(49)

\|V_{0,k}\exp(|V_{0,k}|)\|_{L^{1}(Q_{T})}\leq C.

Second, we introduce the following function and its convex conjugate:

(50)

g(s)=\exp(s^{2}/4),\quad g^{*}(t)=\sup_{s>0}(st-g(s))\quad\mbox{for }s,t>0.

Lemma 14
.

Let
\Phi(u):=u\sqrt{\log u}
for
u\geq 1
.
Then there exists
C>0
such that
\Phi(g^{*}(t))\leq Ct\log t
as
t\to\infty
.

Proof.

It holds that
g^{*}(t)=s_{t}t-\exp(s_{t}^{2}/4)
, where
s_{t}>0
is uniquely determined by

s_{t}\exp(s_{t}^{2}/4)=2t
for
t>0
. Thus,
for sufficiently large
t>0
, there exists
C>0

such that
s_{t}\leq C\sqrt{\log t}
, which implies that

g^{*}(t)\leq s_{t}t\leq Ct\sqrt{\log t}
for “large” values of
t
.
By definition of
\Phi
, we have

\Phi(g^{*}(t))\leq Ct\sqrt{\log t}\big[\log\big(Ct\sqrt{\log t}\big)\big]^{1/2}\quad\mbox{as }t\to\infty,

and consequently,

\frac{\Phi(g^{*}(t))}{t\log t}\leq\bigg(\frac{\log(Ct\sqrt{\log t})}{\log t}\bigg)^{1/2}\leq C\quad\mbox{as }t\to\infty,

which proves the lemma.
∎

Lemma
14
and the
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{1}(\Omega))
bound for
D_{0,k}\log D_{0,k}

from Lemma
13
show that

(51)

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Phi(g^{*}(D_{0,k}))dxdt\leq C+C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}D_{0,k}\log D_{0,k}dxdt\leq C.

Since
\Phi(u)/u\to\infty
as
u\to\infty
, we conclude from the
De la Vallée–Poussin lemma that
g^{*}(D_{0,k})
is uniformly integrable
in
Q_{T}
.

4.3.
Limit
k\to\infty

The weak convergence of the potential, proved in Section
2.4
, does not
allow us to conclude the convergence
\exp(V_{0,k})\to\exp(V_{0})
as
k\to\infty
.
By exploiting the
L^{1}(Q_{T})
bound for
V_{0,k}\exp(|V_{0,k}|)

and the monotonicity of the nonlinear terms in the Poisson equation,
we are able to prove the strong convergence of
(V_{0,k})
.

Lemma 15
.

It holds that
V_{0,k}\to V_{0}
strongly in
L^{p}(Q_{T})
for any
1\leq p<\infty
.

Proof.

We take the difference of the Poisson equation (
46
), satisfied by

V_{0,k}
and
V_{0,\ell}
for some
k
,
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

-\lambda^{2}\Delta(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})+c_{n}(e^{V_{0,k}}-e^{V_{0,\ell}})+c_{p}(-e^{-V_{0,k}}+e^{-V_{0,\ell}})=T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell}).

Then we choose the test function
(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{-1/4}(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})
in the weak formulation of the previous equation:

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{2+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2}}{2(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{5/4}}|\nabla(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+c_{n}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(e^{V_{0,k}}-e^{V_{0,\ell}})}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}dxdt

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+c_{p}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(-e^{-V_{0,k}}+e^{-V_{0,\ell}})}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}dxdt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}(T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell}))dxdt.

Using

\displaystyle c_{n}(

\displaystyle V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(e^{V_{0,k}}-e^{V_{0,\ell}})+c_{p}(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(-e^{-V_{0,k}}+e^{-V_{0,\ell}})

\displaystyle\geq\min\{c_{n},c_{p}\}(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})\big((e^{V_{0,k}}-e^{V_{0,\ell}})+(-e^{-V_{0,k}}+e^{-V_{0,\ell}})\big)

\displaystyle=2\min\{c_{n},c_{p}\}(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(\sinh(V_{0,k})-\sinh(V_{0,\ell})),

we find that

(52)

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}\int_{0}^{T}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}\frac{2+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2}}{2(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{5/4}}|\nabla(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})|^{2}dxdt

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+2\min\{c_{n},c_{p}\}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(\sinh(V_{0,k})-\sinh(V_{0,\ell}))}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}dxdt

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}|T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt.

We claim that the right-hand side converges to zero if

,
\ell\to\infty
.
For this, we decompose the right-hand side for some

into two parts:

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|\leq L\}}\frac{|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}|T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt,

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle:=\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|>L\}}\frac{|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}|T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt.

The integral

converges to zero as

,
\ell\to\infty

(53)

\displaystyle J_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|\leq L\}}|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}||T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt

\displaystyle\leq L\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}|T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt,

and the strong convergence

strongly in

implies that

(D_{0,k})
and also
(T_{k}(D_{0,k}))
is a Cauchy sequence.
The difficult part is the limit

We infer from the Fenchel–Young inequality that

\displaystyle J_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|>L\}}\big(1+|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|^{1/2}\big)\big(T_{k}(D_{0,k})+T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})\big)dxdt

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|>L\}}g(1+|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|^{1/2})dxdt

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|>L\}}g^{*}(T_{k}(D_{0,k}))dxdt+\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|>L\}}g^{*}(T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell}))dxdt

\displaystyle=:J_{21}+J_{22}+J_{23},

where

and

are defined in (

\displaystyle J_{21}

\displaystyle\leq 2\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\{|V_{0,k}|+|V_{0,\ell}|>L\}}\exp\bigg(\frac{1}{2}(1+|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|)\bigg)dxdt

\displaystyle\leq 2e^{1/2}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\max\{|V_{0,k}|,|V_{0,\ell}|\}>L/2}e^{\max\{|V_{0,k}|,|V_{0,\ell}|\}}dxdt

taking into account estimate (

Since
(V_{0,k})
is bounded in

, there exists

We have already shown that (

Thus, for any

.

J_{1}
, we obtain

\limsup_{k,\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}|T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt\leq 4\eta

for all
L>L_{\eta}
. Since
\eta>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\lim_{k,\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{|V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}|}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}|T_{k}(D_{0,k})-T_{\ell}(D_{0,\ell})|dxdt=0.

We perform the limit
k
,
\ell\to\infty
in (
52
), which shows that

\lim_{k,\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})(\sinh(V_{0,k})-\sinh(V_{0,\ell}))}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}dxdt=0.

We deduce from the trigonometric addition formula

\sinh(a)-\sinh(b)=2\sinh\frac{a-b}{2}\cosh\frac{a+b}{2}\quad\mbox{for }a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}

that

\lim_{k,\ell\to\infty}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\frac{\sinh((V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})/2)}{V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell}}\,\frac{\cosh((V_{0,k}+V_{0,\ell})/2)}{(1+(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2})^{1/4}}(V_{0,k}-V_{0,\ell})^{2}dxdt=0.

Taking into account that
\cosh(a)\geq 1
for
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
and that for every
0<p<\infty
,
there exists
c_{p}>0
such that
\sinh(a)/a\geq c_{p}|a|^{p}
for
a\in{\mathbb{R}}
, we conclude
that
(V_{0,k})
is a Cauchy sequence in
L^{p}(Q_{T})
for every
p<\infty

and consequently,
(V_{0,k})
is convergent in that space.
∎

We proceed with the proof of Theorem
3
.
Estimate (
49
) shows that

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\Psi(e^{\pm V_{0,k}})dxdt\leq C,

where
\Psi(u)=u\log u
for
u\geq 0
. By the De la Vallée–Poussin theorem,

(\exp(\pm V_{0,k}))
is uniformly integrable. The strong convergence
V_{0,k}\to V_{0}

in
L^{p}(Q_{T})
implies, up to a subsequence, that

\exp(\pm V_{0,k})\to\exp(\pm V_{0})
a.e. in
Q_{T}
. Thus,

\exp(\pm V_{0,k})\to\exp(\pm V_{0})\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}).

The proof in Section
2.4
shows that
D_{0,k}\to D_{0}
strongly
in
L^{1}(Q_{T})
,
\nabla V_{0,k}\rightharpoonup\nabla V_{0}
and

\nabla D_{0,k}+T_{k}(D_{0,k})\nabla V_{0,k}\rightharpoonup\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0}

weakly in
L^{1}(Q_{T})
.
These convergence allows us to perform the limit
k\to\infty
in
(
45
)–(
48
), showing that
(D_{0},V_{0})
is a weak solution
to (
15
)–(
18
).

4.4.
Weak-strong uniqueness for the limit problem

We continue by proving the weak-strong uniqueness property. Let

(D_{0},V_{0})
be a bounded strong solution and
(D,V)
be a weak solution
to (
15
)–(
18
) satisfying the assumptions of Theorem
3
.
The proof is based on the relative free energy

\displaystyle H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})]=H_{1}[D|D_{0}]+H_{2}[V|V_{0}],\quad\mbox{where}

\displaystyle H_{1}[D|D_{0}]=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(D\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}-D+D_{0}\bigg)dx,

\displaystyle H_{2}[V|V_{0}]=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}+c_{n}e^{V_{0}}f_{0}(V-V_{0})+c_{p}e^{-V_{0}}f_{0}(V_{0}-V)\bigg)dx,

recalling that
f_{0}(s)=(s-1)e^{s}+1
for
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The proof is divided into several
steps.

Step 1: Estimates for the potential
V-V_{0}
.

We wish to derive a bound for
V-V_{0}
in terms of the relative
free energy
H_{1}[D|D_{0}]
.
To this end, we use the test function
V-V_{0}
in the weak formulation of the
difference of the equations satisfied by
V
and
V_{0}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}\big(c_{n}(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}})-c_{p}(e^{-V}-e^{-V_{0}})\big)(V-V_{0})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(V-V_{0})(D-D_{0})dx.

Since
V_{0}
is bounded by assumption, we can estimate the second integral
on the left-hand side according to

\displaystyle\big(c_{n}

\displaystyle(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}})-c_{p}(e^{-V}-e^{-V_{0}})\big)(V-V_{0})

\displaystyle=c_{n}e^{V_{0}}(e^{V-V_{0}}-1)(V-V_{0})+c_{p}e^{-V_{0}}(e^{V_{0}-V}-1)(V_{0}-V)

\displaystyle\geq c|V-V_{0}|(e^{|V-V_{0}|}-1),

where
c>0
depends on the
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
norm of
V_{0}
. We infer that

(54)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|V-V_{0}|(e^{|V-V_{0}|}-1)dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}|V-V_{0}||D-D_{0}|dx.

Let
g_{\xi}(s)=\xi s(e^{s}-1)
for
\xi>0
,
s\geq 0
and
g_{\xi}^{*}
be its convex
conjugate. We deduce from the Fenchel–Young inequality and Lemma
18

in the Appendix that

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle|V-V_{0}||D-D_{0}|dx\leq\int_{\Omega}g_{\xi}(|V_{0}-V|)dx+\int_{\Omega}g_{\xi}^{*}(|D-D_{0}|)dx

\displaystyle\leq\xi\int_{\Omega}|V-V_{0}|(e^{|V-V_{0}|}-1)dx+\xi\int_{\Omega}\frac{(\log(1+|D-D_{0}|/\xi))^{2}}{1+\log(1+|D-D_{0}|/\xi)}\bigg(1+\frac{|D-D_{0}|}{\xi}\bigg)dx.

For
0<\xi<1
, the first term on the right-hand side can be absorbed by the
left-hand side of (
54
), leading to

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|V-V_{0}|(e^{|V-V_{0}|}-1)dx

\displaystyle\leq C\xi\int_{\Omega}\frac{(\log(1+|D-D_{0}|/\xi))^{2}}{1+\log(1+|D-D_{0}|/\xi)}\bigg(1+\frac{|D-D_{0}|}{\xi}\bigg)dx.

We claim that the right-hand side can be controlled by
H_{1}[D|D_{0}]
. In fact,
we claim that for
0<\gamma_{0}\leq D_{0}\leq\gamma_{1}
and
D\geq 0
,

(55)

\frac{(\log(1+|D-D_{0}|/\xi))^{2}}{1+\log(1+|D-D_{0}|/\xi)}\bigg(1+\frac{|D-D_{0}|}{\xi}\bigg)\leq C(\xi,\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1})\bigg(D\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}-D+D_{0}\bigg).

This can be seen by analyzing the behavior of both sides of (
55
)
for
D\to 0
,
D\to D_{0}
, and
D\to\infty
. For
D\to 0
, the left-hand side
of (
55
) remains bounded, while the right-hand side is uniformly positive.
For
D\to\infty
, both sides diverge like
D\log D
. Finally, for
D\to D_{0}
,
a Taylor expansion shows that both sides tend to zero quadratically in
|D-D_{0}|
.
We conclude that

(56)

\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}|V-V_{0}|(e^{|V-V_{0}|}-1)dx\leq CH_{1}[D|D_{0}].

Step 2: Estimate for
(dH_{1}/dt)[D|D_{0}]
.
We differentiate
H_{1}[D|D_{0}]
with
respect to time:

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{1}}{dt}[D|D_{0}]

\displaystyle=\bigg\langle\partial_{t}D,\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg\rangle+\bigg\langle\partial_{t}D_{0},1-\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg\rangle

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\cdot(\nabla D+D\nabla V)dx-\int_{\Omega}\nabla\bigg(1-\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg)\cdot(\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0})dx,

Elementary computations lead to

(57)

\frac{dH_{1}}{dt}[D|D_{0}]=-\int_{\Omega}D\bigg|\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg|^{2}dx-\int_{\Omega}D\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\cdot\nabla(V-V_{0})dx.

To reformulate the last integral,
we use
V-V_{0}
as a test function in the weak formulation
of the difference of the equations satisfied by
D
and
D_{0}
, respectively:

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}

\displaystyle(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\nabla(D-D_{0})dx+\int_{\Omega}D|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})\nabla V_{0}\cdot\nabla(V-V_{0})dx.

Rewriting the second term on the left-hand side,

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\nabla(D-D_{0})dx=\int_{\Omega}\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\bigg(\bigg(\frac{D}{D_{0}}-1\bigg)\nabla D_{0}+D\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}\cdot\nabla(V-V_{0})dx+\int_{\Omega}D\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}dx,

we find that

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}

\displaystyle(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}D|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx-\int_{\Omega}D\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}dx.

We add this expression to (
57
):

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{1}}{dt}

\displaystyle[D|D_{0}]+\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}D\bigg|\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg|^{2}dx+\int_{\Omega}D|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx

\displaystyle{}+2\int_{\Omega}D\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}dx=-\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx.

The last three terms of the left-hand side can be written as a square, leading to

(58)

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{1}}{dt}

\displaystyle[D|D_{0}]+\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle+\int_{\Omega}D\bigg|\nabla\bigg(\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}+V-V_{0}\bigg)\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx

We estimate the right-hand side by decomposing the integral in two terms,

I_{\pm}:=-\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})_{\pm}\nabla(V-V_{0})\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx,

recalling that
z_{+}=\max\{0,z\}
and
z_{-}=\min\{0,z\}
.
In the integral
I_{+}
, we consider “large” values of
D
, i.e.
D>c:=\inf_{Q_{T}}D_{0}>0
,
while “small” values of
D
, i.e.
0\leq D\leq c
, are taken into account in
I_{-}
.

First, using Young’s inequality and the assumptions

\nabla\log D_{0}
,
\nabla V_{0}\in L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
:

(59)

\displaystyle I_{+}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})_{+}\nabla\bigg(\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}+V-V_{0}\bigg)\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})_{+}\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}D\bigg|\nabla\bigg(\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}+V-V_{0}\bigg)\bigg|^{2}dx+C(D_{0},V_{0})\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{D}(D-D_{0})_{+}^{2}dx

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})_{+}\nabla\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}\cdot\bigg(\frac{\nabla D_{0}}{D_{0}}+\nabla V_{0}\bigg)dx.

Observe that the positive part avoids the singularity since

(D-D_{0})_{+}^{2}/D=0
if
D\leq c
. Taylor’s formula yields

D\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}-D+D_{0}=D_{0}\bigg(\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg(\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}-1\bigg)+1\bigg)=\frac{D_{0}}{2\xi}\bigg(\frac{D}{D_{0}}-1\bigg)^{2}\geq\frac{D_{0}^{2}}{2D}\bigg(\frac{D}{D_{0}}-1\bigg)^{2}

for some
1\leq\xi\leq D/D_{0}
. Then the second integral on the right-hand side of
(
59
) becomes

\int_{\Omega}\frac{1}{D}(D-D_{0})_{+}^{2}dx=\int_{\{D>D_{0}\}}\frac{D_{0}^{2}}{D}\bigg(\frac{D}{D_{0}}-1\bigg)^{2}dx\leq 2H_{1}[D|D_{0}].

The last integral in (
59
) is formulated as

\displaystyle I_{1}

\displaystyle:=\int_{\Omega}\bigg(1-\frac{D_{0}}{D}\bigg)_{+}\nabla\frac{D}{D_{0}}\cdot(\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0})dx

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\nabla F\bigg(\frac{D}{D_{0}}\bigg)\cdot(\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0})dx,

where
F(s)=(s-1-\log s)\mathrm{1}_{\{s>1\}}\geq 0
for
s>0
.
The no-flux boundary conditions allow us to integrate by parts:

I_{1}=-\big\langle\operatorname{div}(\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0}),F(D/D_{0})\big\rangle=-\langle\partial_{t}D_{0},F(D/D_{0})\rangle.

By our assumption
\partial_{t}D_{0}\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))
and the property

F(s)\leq s(\log s-1)+1
for
s\geq 0
, we conclude that there exists

\gamma_{1}\in L^{1}(0,T)
such that

I_{1}\leq\gamma_{1}(t)\int_{\Omega}F(D/D_{0})dx\leq\gamma_{1}(t)H_{1}[D|D_{0}].

Therefore, setting
\gamma_{2}(t)=\gamma_{1}(t)+2C(D_{0},V_{0})
,
we deduce from (
59
) that

(60)

I_{+}\leq\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}D\bigg|\nabla\bigg(\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}+V-V_{0}\bigg)\bigg|^{2}dx+\gamma_{2}(t)H_{1}[D,D_{0}].

Now, we estimate
I_{-}
. By our assumptions on
D_{0}
and
V_{0}
, we compute

\displaystyle I_{-}

\displaystyle\leq C(D_{0},V_{0})\int_{\Omega}|(D-D_{0})_{-}||\nabla(V-V_{0})|dx

\displaystyle\leq C(D_{0},V_{0})\int_{\Omega}(D-D_{0})_{-}^{2}dx+C(D_{0},V_{0})\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx.

The first integral can be bounded from above by
CH_{1}[D|D_{0}]
since

D\log(D/D_{0})-D+D_{0}\geq(D-D_{0})^{2}/(2D_{0})
for
D<D_{0}
.
The second integral is estimated from above by
CH_{2}[V,V_{0}]
. We conclude that

(61)

I_{-}\leq C(D_{0},V_{0})\big(H_{1}[D|D_{0}]+H_{2}[V|V_{0}]\big).

Inserting estimates (
60
) for
I_{+}
and (
61
) for
I_{-}
into
(
58
), we obtain for some
\gamma_{3}\in L^{1}(0,T)
,

(62)

\displaystyle\frac{dH_{1}}{dt}

\displaystyle[D|D_{0}]+\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle+\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}D\bigg|\nabla\bigg(\log\frac{D}{D_{0}}+V-V_{0}\bigg)\bigg|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\leq\gamma_{3}(t)H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})].

Step 3: Estimate of
\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle
.

The difference
V-V_{0}
satisfies the Poisson equation

D-D_{0}=-\lambda^{2}\Delta(V-V_{0})+c_{n}(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}})-c_{p}(e^{-V}-e^{-V_{0}}).

Thus, replacing
D-D_{0}
in
\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle

by the right-hand side and integrating by parts in the term
involving
\Delta(V-V_{0})
leads to

(63)

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{2}}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V-V_{0})|^{2}dx

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}+c_{n}\langle\partial_{t}(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}}),V-V_{0}\rangle-c_{p}\langle\partial_{t}(e^{-V}-e^{-V_{0}}),V-V_{0}\rangle.

The second term on the right-hand side can be reformulated according to

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}

\displaystyle(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}}),V-V_{0}\rangle=\langle\partial_{t}f_{0}(V-V_{0}),e^{V_{0}}\rangle+\langle\partial_{t}(e^{V_{0}}),(V-V_{0})(e^{V-V_{0}}-1)\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e^{V_{0}}f_{0}(V-V_{0})dx+\big\langle\partial_{t}(e^{V_{0}}),(V-V_{0})(e^{V-V_{0}}-1)-f_{0}(V-V_{0})\big\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e^{V_{0}}f_{0}(V-V_{0})dx+\big\langle\partial_{t}(e^{V_{0}}),e^{V-V_{0}}-(V-V_{0})-1\big\rangle.

Our assumption on
V_{0}
implies that
\partial_{t}(e^{V_{0}})\in L^{1}(0,T;L^{\infty}(\Omega))

such that

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}

\displaystyle(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}}),V-V_{0}\rangle\geq\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e^{V_{0}}f_{0}(V-V_{0})dx-\gamma_{4}(t)\int_{\Omega}(e^{V-V_{0}}-(V-V_{0})-1)dx

for some nonnegative function
\gamma_{4}(t)
. It holds that

e^{s}-s-1\leq C|s|(e^{|s|}-1)\quad\mbox{for }s\in{\mathbb{R}},

since both sides behave like
s^{2}
as
s\to 0
and for
|s|\to\infty
,
the right-hand side tends to infinity faster than the left-hand side.
Therefore, we deduce from (
56
) that

\langle\partial_{t}(e^{V}-e^{V_{0}}),V-V_{0}\rangle\geq\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e^{V_{0}}f_{0}(V-V_{0})dx-(\gamma_{4}(t)+C)H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})].

In a similar way, it follows that

-\langle\partial_{t}(e^{-V}-e^{-V_{0}}),V-V_{0}\rangle\geq\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega}e^{-V_{0}}f_{0}(V_{0}-V)dx-(\gamma_{4}(t)+C)H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})].

Inserting these inequalities into (
63
) and taking into account the
definition
H_{2}
shows that, for some
\gamma_{5}\in L^{1}(0,T)
,

\langle\partial_{t}(D-D_{0}),V-V_{0}\rangle\geq\frac{dH_{2}}{dt}[V|V_{0}]-\gamma_{5}(t)H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})].

Step 4: Conclusion.
We infer from the previous inequality and (
62
)
that

\frac{dH}{dt}[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})]\leq(\gamma_{3}(t)+\gamma_{5}(t))H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})]

for
0<t<T
. As
\gamma_{3}+\gamma_{5}\in L^{1}(0,T)
and
H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})]=0
at
t=0
,
Gronwall’s lemma implies that
H[(D,V)|(D_{0},V_{0})](t)=0
for
0<t<T
, which
gives
D=D_{0}
and
V=V_{0}
in
Q_{T}
and finishes the proof.

5.
Proof of Theorem
4

Since there is no factor
\varepsilon
in the equation for
D_{\varepsilon}
, we can proceed
as in the existence proof and show, using the Aubin–Lions lemma, that
up to a subsequence,

(64)

D_{\varepsilon}\to D_{0}\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T})\mbox{ as }\varepsilon\to 0.

The assumption on the boundary data
\overline{n}
and
\overline{p}
implies that

\Lambda_{\varepsilon}=0
(see (
14
)). Therefore, by the free energy inequality
(
13
),

\int_{0}^{T}\int_{\Omega}\big(n_{\varepsilon}|\nabla(\log n_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon})|^{2}+p_{\varepsilon}|\nabla(\log p_{\varepsilon}+V_{\varepsilon})|^{2}\big)dxdt\leq H^{I}\varepsilon.

It follows that

2\nabla\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}\nabla(\log n_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon})\to 0\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{2}(Q_{T}).

Furthermore, since
\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}

is uniformly bounded in
L^{\infty}(0,T;L^{2}(\Omega))
, we find that

\nabla n_{\varepsilon}-n_{\varepsilon}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}\big(2\nabla\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{n_{\varepsilon}}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\big)\to 0\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}).

By estimate (
72
), which holds in two space dimensions,

\|V_{\varepsilon}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega)}\leq C\big(1+\|(n_{\varepsilon}-p_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon}+A(x))\log|n_{\varepsilon}-p_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon}+A(x)|\|_{L^{1}(\Omega)}\big).

In view of inequality (
13
),
this gives a uniform
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
bound for
V_{\varepsilon}
. We infer that

\nabla(n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}})=e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}(\nabla n_{\varepsilon}-n_{\varepsilon}\nabla V_{\varepsilon})\to 0\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}).

By Poincaré’s inequality, since
\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}}=c_{n}=\mbox{const.}
,

(65)

\|n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}-\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}}\|_{L^{1}(Q_{T})}\leq C\|\nabla(n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}})\|_{L^{1}(Q_{T})}+C\|\nabla(\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}})\|_{L^{1}(Q_{T})}\to 0.

Similarly, it follows that

(66)

p_{\varepsilon}e^{V_{\varepsilon}}-\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}}\to 0\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}).

Next, we reformulate the Poisson equation as

\lambda^{2}\Delta V_{\varepsilon}=e^{V_{\varepsilon}}(n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}})-e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}(p_{\varepsilon}e^{V_{\varepsilon}})-D_{\varepsilon}+A=e^{V_{\varepsilon}}(\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}})-e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}(\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}})-D_{\varepsilon}+A+E_{\varepsilon},

where

E_{\varepsilon}:=e^{V_{\varepsilon}}(n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}-\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}})-e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}(p_{\varepsilon}e^{V_{\varepsilon}}-\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}})

is an error term. Then
V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
for some
\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
solves

\lambda^{2}\Delta(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})=\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}}(e^{V_{\varepsilon}}-e^{V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}})-\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}}(e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}-e^{-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}})-(D_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})+E_{\varepsilon}-E_{\varepsilon^{\prime}},

and choosing the test function
V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
in the weak formulation, we have

\displaystyle\lambda^{2}

\displaystyle\int_{\Omega}|\nabla(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})|^{2}dx=-\int_{\Omega}\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}}(e^{V_{\varepsilon}}-e^{V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}})(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})dx

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}-\int_{\Omega}\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}}(-e^{-V_{\varepsilon}}+e^{-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}})(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})dx+\int_{\Omega}(D_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})dx

\displaystyle\phantom{xx}{}-\int_{\Omega}(E_{\varepsilon}-E_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})dx

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Omega}(D_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})dx-\int_{\Omega}(E_{\varepsilon}-E_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})dx,

because of the monotonicity of
z\mapsto e^{z}
and
z\mapsto-e^{-z}
.
The strong convergences (
65
) and (
66
)
as well as the uniform
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
bound of
V_{\varepsilon}
imply that

E_{\varepsilon}\to 0
strongly in
L^{1}(Q_{T})
as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Therefore, in view
of (
64
),

\lambda^{2}\|\nabla(V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\leq\|V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{L^{\infty}(Q_{T})}\big(\|D_{\varepsilon}-D_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{L^{1}(Q_{T})}+\|E_{\varepsilon}-E_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\|_{L^{1}(Q_{T})}\big)\to 0

as
\varepsilon
,
\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
. Taking into account the Poincaré inequality, we
obtain
V_{\varepsilon}-V_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}\to 0
strongly in
L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
as

\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\to 0
. This means that
(V_{\varepsilon})
is a Cauchy sequence in

L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
and consequently, there exists a function

V_{0}\in L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega))
such that

(67)

V_{\varepsilon}\to V_{0}\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{2}(0,T;H^{1}(\Omega)).

Thus, up to a subsequence,
V_{\varepsilon}\to V_{0}
and
\exp(V_{\varepsilon})\to\exp(V_{0})

a.e. in
Q_{T}
. Because of the uniform bound for
(V_{\varepsilon})
in
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
,
this shows that
\exp(V_{\varepsilon})\rightharpoonup\exp(V_{0})
weakly* in
L^{\infty}(Q_{T})
.
Then we infer from (
65
) that

\displaystyle n_{\varepsilon}=e^{V_{\varepsilon}}(n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}})\to e^{V_{0}}(\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}})=\overline{n}e^{V_{0}-\overline{V}}=:n_{0}

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{a.e. in }Q_{T},

\displaystyle n_{\varepsilon}=e^{V_{\varepsilon}}(n_{\varepsilon}e^{-V_{\varepsilon}})\rightharpoonup e^{V_{0}}(\overline{n}e^{-\overline{V}})=n_{0}

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{weakly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}).

By the Dunford–Pettis theorem,
(n_{\varepsilon})
is uniformly integrable. Thus, the
a.e. convergence of
(n_{\varepsilon})
implies that

n_{\varepsilon}\to n_{0}\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T})

and by similar arguments,

p_{\varepsilon}\to p_{0}:=\overline{p}e^{\overline{V}-V_{0}}\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}).

Finally, we perform the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
in (
1
)–(
4
) and
prove that
(n_{0},p_{0},D_{0},V_{0})
satisfies the limit problem. The only delicate
limit is in the flux term in the equation for
D_{\varepsilon}
. The proof is similar
as in Section
2.4
. Indeed, the bound on the entropy production for

D_{\varepsilon}
in (
13
) shows that, up to a subsequence,

2\nabla\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}+\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\chi\quad\mbox{weakly in }L^{2}(Q_{T})

for some
\chi\in L^{2}(Q_{T})
.
We deduce from (
64
) and (
67
) that

\displaystyle\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\to\sqrt{D_{0}}\nabla V_{0}\quad\mbox{strongly in }L^{1}(Q_{T}),

\displaystyle\big\|\nabla\big(\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{D_{0}}\big)\big\|_{L^{2}(0,T;H^{-1}(\Omega))}\leq\big\|\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}-\sqrt{D_{0}}\big\|_{L^{2}(Q_{T})}\to 0.

Thus, we can identify
\chi=2\nabla\sqrt{D_{0}}+\sqrt{D_{0}}\nabla V_{0}
. This relation,
together with (
64
), yields

\nabla D_{\varepsilon}+D_{\varepsilon}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}=\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}\big(2\nabla\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}+\sqrt{D_{\varepsilon}}\nabla V_{\varepsilon}\big)\rightharpoonup\sqrt{D_{0}}\chi=\nabla D_{0}+D_{0}\nabla V_{0}

weakly in
L^{1}(Q_{T})
.
This implies, as at the end of Section
2.4
, that

\partial_{t}D_{\varepsilon}\rightharpoonup\partial_{t}D_{0}
weakly in
L^{1}(0,T;H^{s}(\Omega)^{\prime})

for
s>1+d/2
. The proof is finished.

6.1.
Numerical scheme

We assume that
\Omega=(0,L)
for
some
L>0
and we impose Dirichlet boundary conditions for
n
,
p
, and
V
.
For the numerical discretization, we formulate the reduced model
in terms of the quasi-Fermi potentials

\phi_{n}=-\log n_{0}+V_{0},\quad\phi_{p}=\log p_{0}+V_{0},\quad\phi_{D}=\log D_{0}+V_{0}.

The reduced system reads as

(68)

\displaystyle\partial_{x}J_{n,0}=\partial_{x}J_{p,0}=0,\quad\partial_{t}D_{0}+\partial_{x}J_{D,0}=0,\quad\lambda^{2}\partial_{xx}V_{0}=n_{0}-p_{0}-D_{0}+A(x),

(69)

\displaystyle J_{n,0}=-e^{V_{0}-\phi_{n}}\partial_{x}\phi_{n},\quad J_{p,0}=-e^{\phi_{p}-V_{0}}\partial_{x}\phi_{p},\quad J_{D,0}=-e^{\phi_{D}-V_{0}}\partial_{x}\phi_{D},

in
(0,L)
,
t>0
, with the initial and boundary conditions

\displaystyle\phi_{n}(0,t)=\phi_{p}(0,t)=U_{0},\quad\phi_{n}(L,t)=\phi_{p}(L,t)=U_{L},

\displaystyle\partial_{x}\phi_{D}(0,t)=\partial_{x}\phi_{D}(L,t)=0,

\displaystyle V_{0}(0,t)=V_{\rm bi}+U_{0},\quad V_{0}(L,t)=V_{\rm bi}+U_{L}\quad\mbox{for }t>0,

\displaystyle D_{0}(x,0)=D^{I}(x)\quad\mbox{for }x\in(0,L).

Here,
U_{0}
and
U_{L}
are two (possibly time-dependent) applied potentials
at the electrodes, and
V_{\rm bi}
is the built-in potential, which is the
potential that corresponds to the thermal-equilibrium densities
[
17
]
:

V_{\rm bi}=\log\bigg(\frac{1}{2}\big(D_{e}-A+\sqrt{(D_{e}-A)^{2}+4}\big)\bigg),

where
D_{e}
is the dopant concentration at the electrodes. Moreover, the
initial data for the electrons and holes are given by
n^{I}=\exp(V^{I})
and

p^{I}=\exp(-V^{I})
, where
V^{I}
is the solution to the Poisson equation with
the above boundary conditions.

The scaled Debye length is given by
\lambda^{2}=\varepsilon_{s}U_{T}/(qL^{2}n_{i})
,
where the meaning and the values of the physical parameters are as follows:

•

semiconductor permittivity of silicon:
\varepsilon_{s}=8.85\cdot 10^{-13}
As/Vcm;

•

thermal voltage at 300 K:
U_{T}=0.026
V;

•

elementary charge:
q=1.6\cdot 10^{-19}
As;

•

device length:
L=50
nm;

•

(reference) intrinsic density:
n_{i}=2\cdot 10^{19}
cm
-3
;

•

reference current density
J_{0}=400
Acm
-2
.

These values are similar to those in
[
23
]
, and they lead to

\lambda^{2}=2.86\cdot 10^{-4}
. Furthermore, we choose as in
[
23
]

constant scaled doping concentrations,
D^{I}=2.5
,
A=0.25
, and
D_{e}=25
.

Equations (
68
)–(
69
) are discretized by the finite-volume
method. More precisely, the continuity equations are approximated by a
Scharfetter–Gummel scheme introduced in
[
22
]
. For instance,
discretizing
(0,L)
by
x_{1}=0<x_{2}<\cdots<x_{N}=L
, the continuity equation
for the electrons becomes
J_{n,k+1/2}-J_{n,k-1/2}=0
on the control volume

\omega_{k}=((x_{k-1}+x_{k})/2,(x_{k}+x_{k+1})/2)
, where

J_{n,k+1/2}=\frac{1}{x_{k+1}-x_{k}}\big(B(V_{k}-V_{k+1})e^{V_{k}-\phi_{n,k}}-B(V_{k+1}-V_{k})e^{V_{k+1}-\phi_{n,k+1}}\big),

B(s)=s/(e^{s}-1)
is the Bernoulli function,
J_{k+1/2}
approximates
J_{n}
in

\omega_{k}
, and
(\phi_{n,k},V_{k})
approximates
(\phi_{n},V)(x_{k})
.
The continuity equation
for
D
is discretized by the implicit Euler method. At each time step,
we use Newton’s method to solve the discrete nonlinear system of
4N
variables,
using the solution from the previous time step as the initial guess.

Appendix A
Auxiliary lemmas

Introduce the functions
T_{k}(s)=\min\{k,s\}
for
s\geq 0
,
k\geq 1
and

g_{k}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\int_{1}^{y}\frac{dz}{T_{k}(z)}dy,\quad h_{k}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dz}{\sqrt{T_{k}(z)}},\quad s\geq 0.

Lemma 16
.

It holds that
g_{k}(s)=s(\log s-1)
for
0\leq s<k
and

g_{k}(s)\geq k(\log k-1)+(s-k)^{2}/(2k)
for
s\geq k
.

Proof.

We estimate for
0\leq s\leq k
,

g_{k}(s)=-\int_{0}^{s}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{dz}{T_{k}(z)}dy=-\int_{0}^{s}\int_{y}^{1}\frac{dz}{z}dy=s(\log s-1);

and for
s\geq k
,

g_{k}(s)=\int_{0}^{k}\int_{1}^{y}\frac{dz}{T_{k}(z)}dy+\int_{k}^{s}\int_{1}^{y}\frac{dz}{T_{k}(z)}dy\geq k(\log k-1)+\frac{1}{2k}(s-k)^{2},

ending the proof.
∎

Lemma 17
.

There exists
C>0
such that for all
k>1
,

\sqrt{T_{k}(s)}\leq C\big(1+\sqrt{|g_{k}(s)|}\big),\quad\sqrt{T_{k}(s)}\leq Ch_{k}(s)\quad\mbox{for }s\geq 0.

Proof.

We first prove the inequality
h_{k}(s)^{2}\leq C(1+|g_{k}(s)|)

and then
\sqrt{T_{k}(s)}\leq h_{k}(s)/2
for
s\geq 0
.
Combining both inequalities shows the lemma. The second inequality follows from

\displaystyle h_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{y}}=2\sqrt{s}=2\sqrt{T_{k}(s)}\quad\mbox{for }0<s<k,

\displaystyle h_{k}(s)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{k}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{y}}+\int_{k}^{s}\frac{dy}{\sqrt{k}}\geq 2\sqrt{k}=2\sqrt{T_{k}(s)}\quad\mbox{for }s\geq k.

If
0<s<k
, we have shown in Lemma
16
that

g_{k}(s)=s(\log s-1)
, and then
h_{k}(s)^{2}\leq C(1+|g_{k}(s)|)
is equivalent to

4s\leq C(1+s|\log s-1|)
for
0<s<k
, and this inequality is true for a suitable

C>0
. If
s\geq k
, again by Lemma
16
,
h_{k}(s)^{2}\leq C(1+|g_{k}(s)|)

follows from

\bigg(2\sqrt{k}+\frac{s-k}{\sqrt{k}}\bigg)^{2}\leq C\bigg(k(\log k-1)+\frac{(s-k)^{2}}{2k}\bigg),

and this inequality is valid for a suitably chosen
C>0
independent of
k
.
∎

Let
\xi>0
and define
g_{\xi}(x)=\xi x(e^{x}-1)
for
x\geq 0
and its convex conjugate

g_{\xi}^{*}(y)=\sup_{x>0}(xy-g_{\xi}(x))
for
y\geq 0
. The following lemma
provides an upper bound for
g_{\xi}^{*}
.

Lemma 18
.

The convex conjugate function of
g_{\xi}
can be estimated as

g_{\xi}^{*}(y)\leq\xi\frac{(\log(1+y/\xi))^{2}}{1+\log(1+y/\xi)}\bigg(1+\frac{y}{\xi}\bigg)\quad\mbox{for }y\geq 0.

Proof.

For given
y\geq 0
, let
\overline{x}(y)\geq 0
be the unique solution to

y=g^{\prime}_{\xi}(\overline{x}(y))=\xi(1+\overline{x}(y))e^{\overline{x}(y)}-\xi
. Then

g_{\xi}^{*}(y)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\overline{x}(y)y-g_{\xi}(\overline{x}(y))&\quad\mbox{for }y>g_{\xi}^{\prime}(0)=0,\\
0&\quad\mbox{for }y=g_{\xi}^{\prime}(0)=0.\end{array}\right.

Furthermore, it follows from the definition of
\overline{x}(y)

that
y/\xi\geq e^{\overline{x}(y)}-1
and hence,

\overline{x}(y)\leq\log(1+y/\xi)
. Therefore, since

(1+\overline{x}(y))e^{\overline{x}(y)}=1+y/\xi
by the definition of

\overline{x}(y)
, we have for
y\geq 0
,

\displaystyle g_{\xi}^{*}(y)

\displaystyle=\overline{x}(y)y-\xi\overline{x}(y)(e^{\overline{x}(y)}-1)=\xi\overline{x}(y)\bigg(1+\frac{y}{\xi}-e^{\overline{x}(y)}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\xi\overline{x}(y)\bigg(1+\frac{y}{\xi}-\frac{1+y/\xi}{1+\overline{x}(y)}\bigg)=\frac{\overline{x}(y)^{2}}{1+\overline{x}(y)}(y+\xi)\leq\frac{(\log(1+y/\xi))^{2}}{1+\log(1+y/\xi)}(y+\xi),

which shows the lemma.
∎
