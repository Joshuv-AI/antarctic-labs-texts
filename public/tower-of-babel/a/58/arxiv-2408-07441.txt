The physical mechanism of the streaming instability
Nathan Magnan, Tobias Heinemann, Henrik N. Latter

Abstract
The main hurdle of planet formation theory is the metre-scale barrier. One of the most promising ways to overcome it is via the streaming instability (SI). Unfortunately, the mechanism responsible for the onset of this instability remains mysterious. It has recently been shown that the SI is a Resonant Drag Instability (RDI) involving inertial waves. We build on this insight and clarify the physical picture of how the SI develops, while bolstering this picture with transparent mathematics. Like all RDIs, the SI is built on a feedback loop: in the ‘forward action’, an inertial wave concentrates dust into clumps; in the ‘backward reaction’, those drifting dust clumps excite an inertial wave. Each process breaks into two mechanisms, a fast one and a slow one. At resonance, each forward mechanism can couple with a backward mechanism to close a feedback loop. Unfortunately, the fast-fast loop is stable, so the SI uses the fast-slow and slow-fast loops. Despite this last layer of complexity, we hope that our explanation will help understand how the SI works, in which conditions it can grow, how it manifests itself, and how it saturates.

Introduction

A major problem in planet formation theory is the metre-scale barrier: we do not understand how pebbles assemble into planetesimals. Collisions between pebbles lead to bouncing and fragmentation rather than sticking
(
Weidenschilling, 1984
)
, and for small planetesimals, accretion become slower than the radial drift toward the star
(
Weidenschilling, 1977
)
.

One promising way to bridge this gap involves a mechanism called the
SI
(
SI
). This hydrodynamic instability afflicts gas and dust mixtures in proto-planetary discs (
Youdin & Goodman 2005
, hereafter
28
). The interesting thing is that it saturates by forming dust filaments and clumps
(
Johansen & Youdin, 2007
)
. The clumps can be dense enough to collapse gravitationally and form planetesimals
(
Johansen et al., 2007
)
. That being said, there is some debate about whether pre-existing turbulence
(
Umurhan et al., 2020
;
Chen & Lin, 2020
;
Schäfer et al., 2020
;
Schäfer & Johansen, 2022
;
Xu & Bai, 2022
;
Lim et al., 2024
)
, or a distribution of particle sizes
(
Krapp et al., 2019
;
McNally et al., 2021
;
Zhu & Yang, 2021
;
Yang & Zhu, 2021
)
quench the instability.

Here we address another difficulty, namely that the physics of the
SI
is still unclear. This is problematic because it is hard to ask the right questions about a phenomenon we do not understand. It also makes it challenging to interpret numerical simulations. This has already motivated several attempts to try and explain the instability
(
Youdin & Johansen, 2007
;
Jacquet et al., 2011
;
Lin & Youdin, 2017
)
.

Squire & Hopkins recently made progress on that front. They discovered in
Squire & Hopkins 2018a
(hereafter
20
) that the
SI
is part of a larger class of instabilities called
RDI
. Those arise when the dust drifts relative to the gas and the drift velocity matches the phase velocity of one of the gas waves. Then in
Squire & Hopkins 2020
(hereafter
22
) they provided a physical picture for the
SI
. The present paper is an effort to support and refine this picture with rigorous mathematics. Far from complicating things, we find that the algebra actually brings clarity. We hope that the steps of the derivation will guide our reader’s intuition, thus making it less laborious to understand the
SI
.

In a previous paper, we explained the simplest of all
RDI
, the one built on sound waves (
Magnan et al. 2024
, hereafter
14
). We developed a mathematical framework to study the instability’s eigenvectors. It showed that the
RDI
is based on a positive feedback loop between two processes. In the first one, the converging flows of a sound wave concentrate dust. In the second one, a dust density wave excites or amplifies sound waves. A similar loop is at the heart of every
RDI
, what varies is the gas wave and how it concentrates dust.

In this second paper, we apply the same framework to explain the more complicated but astrophysically relevant
SI
. It is an
RDI
built on inertial waves. Those are incompressive, so it is unclear how they can concentrate dust (a fundamentally compressive deed). Resolving this paradox is one of our key objectives. We suspect that our solution applies to all inertial-wave
RDI
, but we focus on the particular case of the
SI
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. We start in §
2
with a summary of the physics of the
SI
, which we hope will help the reader form an intuition for what is happening before we dive into the mathematics. Then in §
3
we present the physical system and its governing equations, and in §
4
we explain how the instability develops. Finally, we conclude in §
5
.

2
Outline of the mechanism

In this first section, we give a quick summary of how the streaming instability develops. We do not use any algebra or calculus yet. Our goal here is rather to give the reader an intuition for how perturbations grow. This will help clarify and motivate the mathematics of §
4
.

2.1
The setup: inertial waves, radial drift and resonance

Before we begin, let us define our setup. We consider a small patch of a
PPD
(
PPD
) modelled using the shearing box
(
Goldreich & Lynden-Bell, 1965
;
Hawley et al., 1995
;
Latter & Papaloizou, 2017
)
. This box is filled with incompressible gas, plus a small amount of dust modelled as a pressure-less fluid. The two components act upon each other via Stokes or Epstein drag.

The disc’s stable radial angular momentum stratification allows the gas to support inertial waves. These are transverse waves restored by the Coriolis force (Fig.
2
). Because of the
PPD
’s shear, only axisymmetric inertial waves can propagate without being damped. Their frequencies are
{\pm\hat{k}_{z}\kappa}
, with
\mathbf{k}
the wave-vector and
\kappa
the disc’s local epicyclic frequency. For more details on inertial waves, see
Balbus (2003)
.

In a
PPD
, pressure generally decreases with radius. This radial pressure gradient indirectly causes the dust to drift towards the star
(
Weidenschilling, 1977
)
. Precisely, the pressure gradient supports the gas radially, making it sub-Keplerian. Conversely, the dust ignores pressure, so it remains Keplerian and experiences a headwind. Drag induces angular momentum exchanges between dust and gas, so the dust loses momentum. Consequently, it drifts inwards (and azimuthaly).

For any drift velocity
\mathbf{v}
, there exists an inertial wave that propagates at just the right phase velocity
\mathbf{v}_{\varphi}
to keep up with the dust. Specifically, we demand that
{\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k}=\mathbf{v}_{\varphi}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k}}
where
\mathbf{k}
is the wave’s vector. Figure
2
shows how this condition ensures that a dust particle sitting on a wave crest remains on that crest. For reasons that will become clear later, we shall say that the dust is ‘in resonance’ with the inertial wave. The goal of the present paper is to study this resonance.

2.2
Forcing of a dust density perturbation by an inertial wave

First consider the dust as passive tracers (
i.e.
test particles). The gas behaves as in isolation, so inertial waves propagate freely. The resonant inertial wave can elicit a strong response from the dust via two mechanisms.

The strongest one relies on the well-known propensity of pressure bumps to trap dust particles. It is sketched out in Fig.
3
, in the reference frame that moves with the resonant inertial wave. This wave induces variations in the gas’ azimuthal speed
\mathbf{u}_{g,y}
(in blue). This means that a dust particle located on a wave crest in
\mathbf{u}_{g,y}
feels less headwind than usual, while a particle located on a wave trough feels more headwind than usual. Consequently, the dust’s rate of angular momentum loss and its radial drift speed are perturbed. The latter is equivalent to radial dust velocity perturbations (in brown), which compress the dust in pressure maxima.

Crucially, since we are at resonance, the dust’s radial drift lets particles remain on pressure crests. So, the dust density increases indefinitely as more and more dust arrives. This results in an algebraic instability we call the ‘forward action’.

The second mechanism, sketched in Fig.
4
, is weaker. The inertial wave induces gas motions in the radial-vertical plane. These winds try to entrain the dust, but orbital mechanics tell us that a particle pushed outwards does not experience any long-term gain in radius, it only oscillates. Indeed, a radial push does not provide any angular momentum, so the particle cannot stay on an outer orbit. Of course, in a
PPD
, there is gas in near-Keplerian rotation, so the displaced dust particle can receive angular momentum and follow the radial wind. But still, the orbital mechanics effect described above makes the dust’s radial motions slower than the gas’. Now since the dust moves vertically as quickly as the gas, and since the gas’ radial-vertical motions are fine-tuned to be perpendicular to
\mathbf{k}
, the dust must have a small component of motion parallel to
\mathbf{k}
. This results in dust convergence towards pressure nodes. For more details on this ‘imperfect radial entrainment’ process, see §
A
.

2.3
Forcing of an inertial wave by a dust density perturbation

To each action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. So the dust must also exert a drag force on the gas, we call it the back-reaction. Through this force, the gas can react strongly to a dust density perturbation – provided we are at resonance.

To see how this works, consider a dust density wave,
i.e.
a sinusoidal perturbation of the dust density advected by the radial drift. Note that the dust’s velocity is unperturbed, and that the wave’s vector
\mathbf{k}
may well have a vertical component. In regions where there is more dust than at equilibrium, the back-reaction is stronger than at equilibrium. Figure
5
shows that the radial component of this perturbed back-reaction has the right spatial profile to accelerate the radial flows of an inertial wave, thereby amplifying that wave.

2.4
Combining these two processes in a feedback loop

So far, we have seen two isolated processes. In the forward action, an inertial wave creates or amplifies dust density waves. In the backward reaction, a dust density wave creates or amplifies inertial waves. We can easily imagine the two processes connect in a feedback loop where the inertial wave concentrates dust, and the ensuing dust density wave strengthens the inertial wave, which can then concentrate dust faster,
etc
. This leads to an exponential instability, the
SI
.

There is, however, one last layer of complexity. The forward action breaks up into the fast pressure-bump mechanism and the slow radial wind mechanism. Similarly, the backward reaction breaks into the fast radial mechanism and the slow azimuthal mechanism. Therefore, we could imagine four feedback loops: fast-fast, fast-slow, slow-fast, and slow-slow.

It turns out that the fast-fast feedback loop is stable. Indeed, the pressure-bump mechanism concentrates dust in pressure maxima (Fig.
3
), whereas the radial backward mechanism amplifies the inertial wave whose pressure is anti-correlated with dust density (Fig.
5
). Because of this phase opposition, the two fast mechanisms work against each other.

As a result, ​​ the ​​
SI
​​​ relies ​​​ on ​​​ the ​​​ fast-slow ​​​ and ​​​ slow-fast ​​​ loops.
Those two loops are of equal strength, and cooperate well enough that the instability grows faster than either loop.

3
Presentation of the physical system

From this point onward, we shall detail and justify all the claims of §
2
. We hope that mathematics will help some readers understand the physical picture described in this paper.

Let us start with the setup (§
2.1
). Our physical system is equivalent to that studied by
28
, and similar to but simpler than that studied by
20
.

3.1
Governing equations

We consider a gas and dust mixture, which we model as a two-fluid system. The gas is assumed to be incompressible, and is described by its density
{\rho_{g}}
, its speed
{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{g}}
, and its pressure
{P}
. The dust is assumed to be pressure-less, and is described by its density
{\rho_{d}}
and velocity
{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}}
. The two fluids are coupled by a linear drag force from the gas onto the dust, and its back-reaction from the dust onto the gas.

Typically, when studying a small-scale instability, one accounts for the fact that they are in a
PPD
by using the shearing box
(
Goldreich & Lynden-Bell, 1965
;
Hawley et al., 1995
;
Latter & Papaloizou, 2017
)
. This local model follows a reference point as it orbits with radius
r_{0}
and frequency
\Omega_{0}
around the star. The local fluid dynamics are represented by Cartesian axes
{(x,\,y,\,z)}
pointing respectively in the radial, azimuthal, and vertical directions.

However, to study the
SI
, we must add a non-standard ingredient to our box. Indeed, the
SI
is powered by the radial drift of dust particles towards the star, which is itself powered by the disc’s global pressure gradient. So, we need a radial background pressure gradient
{\partial_{r}\overline{P}}
in our shearing box.

This introduces three complications. Firstly, the gas is pressure-supported, so its orbital frequency
\Omega_{\text{g}}
is slightly sub-Keplerian. Specifically,
{r\Omega_{\text{g}}^{2}=r\Omega_{\text{K}}^{2}+\rho_{g}^{-1}\partial_{r}\overline{P}}
, where
\Omega_{\text{K}}
is the Keplerian orbital frequency. Secondly, the dust orbits faster than the gas. This means that we need to decide which species our shearing box follows. We choose to follow the gas, so
{\Omega_{0}=\Omega_{\text{g},0}}
. It also means that the two fluids have different shear rates,
S_{\text{g}}
and
S_{\text{d}}
. Note that
S_{\text{g}}
and
S_{\text{d}}
are defined
in the absence of aerodynamical friction
. Finally, if we go back to the global cylindrical coordinates, we see that the radial geometric term in
{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\nabla$}}\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}}
​is non-zero. Consequently, it must also appear in our Cartesian box’s dust momentum equation.

The most convenient way to account for these complications is to replace the tidal potential
{\phi_{t}}
– which only accounts for gravitational and centrifugal forces – by two ‘effective’ potentials
{\phi_{e}^{g}}
and
{\phi_{e}^{d}}
, one for each species. Specifically:

\bullet

For the gas:
Since the shearing box follows the gas and since the gas is sub-Keplerian, the tidal potential takes the non-standard form

{\phi_{t}(\mathbf{x})\,=\,(\Omega_{\text{K},0}^{2}-\Omega_{\text{g},0}^{2})\,r_{0}\,x-\Omega_{\text{g},0}S_{\text{g}}\,x^{2}}
. We group
{\phi_{t}}
with the background pressure
\overline{P}
so that the gas potential recovers its standard form,

\phi_{e}^{g}=\phi_{t}+\frac{\partial_{r}\overline{P}|_{r_{0}}}{\rho_{g}}\,x=-\,\Omega_{\text{g},0}S_{\text{g}}\,x^{2}

\bullet

For the dust:
The dust’s effective potential, however, groups the sub-Keplerian tidal potential with the geometric term:

\phi_{e}^{d}=2\Omega_{\text{g},0}(\Omega_{\text{K},0}-\Omega_{\text{g},0})\,r_{0}\,x-\Omega_{\text{g},0}S_{\text{d}}\,x^{2}.

The two terms have the same size when

However, the exact values of the shear rates are non-essential, so we set
{S_{\text{d}}\!=\!S_{\text{g}}\!=\!\frac{3}{2}\Omega_{\text{g},0}\!=S}

4
Detailed physical picture of the SI

This section is the core of the paper, where we develop and justify the picture given in §
2
.

We start in §
4.1
by considering the dispersion relation, which shows an instability when the gas and dust are at resonance. The ensuing subsections explain the mechanism of this instability. First in §
4.2
we explain the forward action, by which an inertial wave concentrates dust. Then in §
4.3
we uncover the backward reaction, by which a dust density perturbation amplifies inertial waves. Finally in §
4.4
we demonstrate how these two processes feed into each other at resonance, creating an unstable feedback loop – the SI.

4.1
Analysis of the dispersion relation

The goal of this first subsection is to extract as much information as possible from the dispersion relation. This will allow us to estimate the growth rate of the
RDI
that is built on inertial waves, and to show that this instability and
SI
are one and the same.

But first, let us establish the dispersion relation. To do this, we look for axisymmetric modal solutions to Eqs. (
5
),
i.e.
solutions that can be decomposed into full Fourier modes of the form
{f(t,\mathbf{x})=\widehat{f}\,\mathrm{e}^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\omega t)}}
with
{k_{y}=0}
. Such solutions exist if
{\omega}
and
{k}
satisfy

[\omega^{2}-(\hat{k}_{z}\kappa)^{2}]\,[\omega-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{k}]\,[\omega-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{k}+\frac{i}{\tau}]\,[(\omega-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{k}+\frac{i}{\tau})^{2}-\kappa^{2}]\\
+\mu\,P(\omega,\mu)=0,

(6)

where

is a polynomial in
\omega

We want to understand in detail the physics of this instability. The dispersion relation gave us the growth rate, and highlighted the role of a resonance between two waves. But we still need to understand how those two waves interact, and why it leads to an instability.

4.2
Forward action: accumulation of dust by an inertial wave

RDI
arise from a feedback loop between two growth mechanisms. The first one, which we call the forward action, allows a gas wave to concentrate dust.

For the acoustic
RDI
, this process is straightforward: the perturbed velocities of a sound wave are convergent, and drag forces the dust to follow the gas, so dust concentrates (
14
). For the
SI
things are less clear, because inertial waves are incompressive: we need to understand how they can concentrate dust. We can best isolate this effect in the regime of test particles,
{\mu=0}
.

4.2.1
There is an algebraic instability of dust forced by an inertial wave

In this limit, the gas is unaffected by the dust. So, the inertial wave should always exist, even at resonance. Simply, it will exert a drag force on the dust, which will react in a non-modal manner. Therefore, let us assume a modal inertial wave
{f_{g}(t,\mathbf{x})=\widehat{f_{g}}\,\mathrm{e}^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\hat{k}_{z}\kappa\,t)}}
for the gas, and a non-modal answer
{f_{d}(t,\mathbf{x})=\widetilde{f_{d}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}}
from the dust. Here
\widetilde{f_{d}}(t)
is a time-dependent amplitude to be determined.

The dust equations (
5c
,
5d
) become

\displaystyle\left[{\rm d}_{t}+i(\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k})\right]\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}=-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d},

(11a)

\displaystyle\left[{\rm d}_{t}+i(\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k})\mathbf{I}+\mathbf{S}\right]\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}=\mathbf{C}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}-\frac{1}{\tau}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\,\hat{k}_{z}\kappa\,t}).

(11b)

They can be combined to form the fourth-order
ODE
(
ODE
)

\mathcal{L}_{-1}\,\mathcal{L}_{0}\,\mathcal{L}_{+1}\left[{\rm d}_{t}+i(\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k})\right]\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}=\mathbf{b}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\,\hat{k}_{z}\kappa\,t},

(12)

where the

are operators linked to the damped dust modes of §

\mathbf{b}
is a vector whose exact expression is of no interest here. What matters is that
\mathbf{b}

\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}

This form of the

{\hat{k}_{z}\kappa}
collides with one of the dust’s natural frequencies
{\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k}}
. Consequently, the particular solution to Eq. (

{t\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\,\hat{k}_{z}\kappa\,t}}

.

4.2.2
It is the dust density perturbation that grows

To understand this algebraic instability, let us investigate what grows (and how fast) by solving Eqs. (
11

Things are clearer in the reference frame that moves at the wave’s phase speed
{\mathbf{v}_{\varphi}\!=\!(\hat{k}_{z}\kappa/k)\,\hat{\mathbf{k}}}
. This adds an advection term and induces a Doppler shift, so Eqs. (

\displaystyle[{\rm d}_{t}+i(\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k}-\hat{k}_{z}\kappa)]\,\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}=-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d},

\displaystyle[{\rm d}_{t}+i(\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k}-\hat{k}_{z}\kappa)+\mathbf{S}]\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}=\mathbf{C}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}-\frac{1}{\tau}(\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}-\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}).

At resonance, this simplifies to

\displaystyle{\rm d}_{t}\,\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}=-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d},

(13a)

\displaystyle[{\rm d}_{t}+\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{C}+\frac{1}{\tau}]\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}=\frac{\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}}{\tau}.

(13b)

Note the structure of this system:
\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}
is controlled by
\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{d}
, which is itself controlled by
\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}
. This means we can solve Eq. (
13b
) first.

Its general solution is the sum of a homogeneous solution and a particular solution. The homogeneous solution is a linear combination of the eigenmodes of the dust velocity operator
{\left[{\rm d}_{t}+\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{C}+1/\tau\right]}
. Its eigenfrequencies all have a negative real part, so the homogeneous solution quickly decays to zero. After a few dust stopping times
\tau
, all that is left is the particular solution, which is a constant. We denote it
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}
to emphasise that it can be interpreted as a terminal velocity.

Therefore, over long timescales, Eq. (
13a
) simplifies to

{\rm d}_{t}\,\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}=-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}},

(14)

which reveals that the dust density perturbation grows linearly in time. Since it is the only dust feature to grow, we conclude that the forward action amplifies dust density waves.

Equation (
14
) also gives the ‘algebraic growth rate’ of the forward action,

\gamma_{1}=\frac{-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}}{\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}(t=0)}.

(15)

4.2.3
Dust density perturbations grow via two mechanisms

{i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}}
translate to
{\mbox{\boldmath{$\nabla$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}}
out of Fourier space. This tells us that the dust density grows because the secular dust motions are convergent. This is surprising, because drag makes dust follow the gas, and the gas motions are incompressive (and thus non-convergent).

To understand this, we need to calculate
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}
. It is defined by the equation
{[\mathbf{I}+\tau(\mathbf{S}-\mathbf{C})]\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}=\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the identity matrix. This already shows that it is the shear
\mathbf{S}
and the Coriolis force
\mathbf{C}
that allow the dust velocity to differ from the gas velocity. Then by solving the equation, we find the radial and vertical components of
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}
,

\displaystyle u_{d,x}^{\text{ter.}}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{g,x}+\,\,\frac{2\,\text{St}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}\,\hat{u}_{g,y}\,-\frac{\text{St}^{2}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}\hat{u}_{g,x},

(16a)

\displaystyle u_{d,z}^{\text{ter.}}

\displaystyle=\hat{u}_{g,z},

(16b)

revealing that
\mathbf{S}
and
\mathbf{C}
cause a difference in
radial
velocities.

This difference breaks the fine-tuned balance between
\hat{u}_{g,x}
and
\hat{u}_{g,z}
that made
\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{k}
. This allows the dust motions to be compressive:

\displaystyle-i\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}

\displaystyle=-i\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widehat{u}$}}_{g}-\frac{2i\,\text{St}\,k_{x}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}\hat{u}_{g,y}+\frac{i\,\text{St}^{2}k_{x}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}\hat{u}_{g,x},

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\tau\,k^{2}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}-i\frac{\tau\,\text{St}\,\hat{k}_{z}\,k^{2}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}\bigg)\,\widehat{h}.

(17)

So, we need to understand the physical mechanisms responsible for the two rightmost terms in Eq. (
16a
).

The term proportional to
\hat{u}_{g,y}
relies on a well-known mechanism. Remember the origin of the dust’s radial drift: the gas is slightly sub-Keplerian and the dust Keplerian, so the dust feels a headwind, loses angular momentum, and moves inward. Well here, the inertial wave perturbs the gas’ azimuthal velocity, so in some places the dust feels more (less) headwind than usual, loses more (less) angular momentum, and moves inward faster (slower), resulting in radial dust velocity perturbations. This explanation is supported by the similarity between the term of interest and Eq. (
4
). Fig.
3
shows that dust accumulates in pressure peaks of the inertial wave, and leaves pressure troughs. Indeed, the mechanism we just described is simply dust trapping by pressure bumps.

The other term’s mechanism is more complex, so we leave its detailed description to §
A
. To keep things here brief: the inertial wave perturbs the gas’ radial velocity, creating radial winds that entrain the dust. However, an isolated particle does not change angular momentum when pushed radially, so
it cannot migrate radially, it just oscillates around its initial
orbit ​​​ (§
A.1
). ​​​ Of course, ​​​ our ​​​ dust ​​​ particles ​​​ are ​​​ not ​​​ isolated, ​​​ they
interact with the gas via drag. Specifically, after a particle is pushed radially, it can exchange angular momentum with the background near-Keplerian gas flow. This allows dust particles to migrate radially and follow the gas’ radial flows (§
A.2
). But still, this angular momentum exchange process is not perfect, it cannot entirely erase the dust’s radial stiffness, so the dust moves slower than the gas in the radial direction (§
A.3
). Fig.
4
shows that this imperfect radial entrainment causes dust compression. Once again, our explanation is supported by the similarity between the term of interest and Eq. (
38
).

For small particles, the pressure-bump mechanism is much stronger than the radial wind mechanism, as it scales with
St
rather than
\text{St}^{2}
. Consequently, the dust density perturbation grows almost exactly in phase with the pressure perturbation.

4.2.4
Growth happens at resonance because the dust needs to not drift away from where it accumulates

Now that we understand the dust concentration mechanism, it is natural to wonder why it is only strong near resonance.

A clue comes from rewriting Eq. (
14
) outside resonance. It becomes

[{\rm d}_{t}+i(\mathbf{v}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mathbf{k}-\hat{k}_{z}\kappa)]\,\widetilde{\varrho}_{d}=-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}},

(18)

which only exhibits an algebraic instability at perfect resonance. Otherwise, the advective term is non-zero, meaning that dust particles drift relative to the inertial wave. Precisely, what matters is that they drift out of waveplanes.

​​​The role of resonance is to suppress this drift, so that particles cannot leave the plane they have been brought to by the mechanisms of §
4.2.3
. This allows the dust density to increase forever in pressure bumps as more and more particles arrive.

One may be concerned that this algebraic instability exists solely because we idealised the physics. Any detuning, however slight, kills the algebraic instability. So let us stress that the algebraic instability is not the
SI
. The algebraic instability is just a fragile manifestation of one of the two robust processes behind the
SI
: the process by which an inertial wave can cause large dust density perturbations.

Indeed, close to resonance, dust only drifts slowly, so the residence time of dust in pressure bumps is finite but long. This implies that a long transient regime of growth and a large final dust density is still possible at low detuning.

4.3
Backward reaction: forcing of an inertial wave by the dust

The preceding subsection focused on the first part of the
SI
, in which an inertial wave causes a large dust density perturbation. Let us now see how a dust density perturbation can excite inertial waves. To observe this effect, we need the dust to have a significant effect on the gas. So we leave the regime of test particles and assume
{\mu>0}
.

4.3.1
There is another algebraic instability

In any real system, the dust would be affected by the gas. But we can best isolate the process of interest in an idealised setup, in which the dust is decoupled from the gas. So let us assume a dust density wave
{f_{d}(t,\mathbf{x})=\widehat{f_{d}}\,\mathrm{e}^{i(\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{k}\,t)}}
that is not impacted by any gas perturbation. Remember that a dust density wave is a perturbation in dust density only, so the dust velocity remains unperturbed.

2

Because there is no restoring force, some may prefer to see this perturbation as an advected dust pattern rather than a wave.

This dust density perturbation modifies the back-reaction force of the dust on the gas. Following our experience of
14

{f_{g}(t,\mathbf{x})=\widetilde{f_{g}}(t)\,\mathrm{e}^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}}
.

We want to emphasise the effect of the dust density wave on the gas, so let us assume that the amplitude of the dust mode is much larger than that of the gas perturbation. This may only be true initially, if the gas perturbations gain amplitude over time. But while it is true, we can safely neglect the drag terms proportional to gas density or velocity in Eq. (
5b

These assumptions reduce the gas equations (

\displaystyle i\,\mathbf{k}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\cdot$}}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{g}=0,

\displaystyle[{\rm d}_{t}+\mathbf{S}]\,\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{g}=-i\,\mathbf{k}\,\widetilde{h}+\mathbf{C}\mbox{\boldmath{$\widetilde{u}$}}_{g}+\frac{\mu}{\tau}\,\mathbf{v}\,\widehat{\varrho}_{d}\,\mathrm{e}^{-i\,\mathbf{v}\cdot\mathbf{k}\,t}.

The rightmost term summarises how the dust density perturbation affects the back-reaction. At equilibrium, the dust moving with velocity
\mathbf{v}
pushed the gas in front of it. Now that there is a perturbation, in regions where there is more dust than at equilibrium, the gas feels a stronger push than at equilibrium. Conversely, in regions where there is less dust than usual, the gas feels a weaker push. We will see in §

With a bit of calculus, one can show that Eqs. (

At resonance, the forcing frequency

\tilde{u}_{g,x}
grows linearly in time. So do
\tilde{u}_{g,y}

\tilde{u}_{g,z}
and

4.3.2
It is an inertial wave that grows

To find out what grows and why, let us solve Eqs. (

.

At resonance, we find

\!\!\!\begin{pmatrix}\widetilde{h}(t)\vphantom{\displaystyle\frac{1}{1}}\\

\!\tilde{u}_{g,z}(t)\vphantom{\displaystyle\frac{1}{1}}\!\!\end{pmatrix}\!=\mathcal{A}\!\begin{pmatrix}\displaystyle\frac{\Omega}{k}\hat{k}_{x}\vphantom{\displaystyle\frac{1}{1}}\!\!\\

Eq. (

4.3.3
It grows because the back-reaction force has the right pattern to accelerate the flows of an inertial wave

Equation (
21
) indicates that if
{v_{x}<0}
but
{v_{y}=0}
, then the pressure perturbation of the growing inertial wave is in phase opposition with the dust density perturbation. We draw this situation in Fig.
5
. The perturbed back-reaction drives radial gas flows, which appear to always be in the same direction as the inertial wave’s radial velocity perturbations. So, the gas accelerates and the inertial wave gets stronger.

Conversely, if
{v_{x}=0}
and
{v_{y}>0}
, then Eq. (
21
) indicates that the growing inertial wave has its pressure pattern phase-shifted by
{\varphi=\pi/2}
with respect to the dust density pattern. We draw this situation in Fig.
6
. The same mechanism as above applies, except that this time the back-reaction accelerates the inertial wave’s azimuthal flows.

In reality, Eqs. (
3
) tells us that we have both
{v_{x}<0}
and
{v_{y}>0}
, so the radial and azimuthal mechanisms co-exist. They negotiate to select an inertial wave whose phase shift is a compromise between
\pi/2
and
\pi
, and allow it to grow. Now since
{v_{y}\ll|v_{x}|}
, the radial mechanism is stronger, and the growing inertial wave is almost exactly in phase opposition with the dust density perturbation.

4.3.4
Growth happens at resonance because it needs the back-reaction force to always amplify the inertial wave

Now that we understand the growth mechanism, it is natural to ask why it is only active near resonance.

Essentially, a non-resonant wave sees its phase shift evolve over time. For instance, at a given instant, it may have a phase shift of
{\varphi=\pi}
, in which case we are in the situation of Fig.
5
, and the wave gains amplitude. But a little while later, we have
{\varphi=0}
and the wave loses amplitude. These two situations happen equally often, hence why non-resonant waves do not grow. Only the resonant waves can maintain
{\varphi}
constant, remain at all times in the situation of Fig.
5
, and grow indefinitely.

That being said, the same point as in §
4.2.4
stands: the backward reaction is fragile, but the process by which dust density waves amplify inertial waves is robust. This process may be transient outside resonance, but that is all
RDI
need.

4.4.7
A point on phase-shifts

Remember that the forward action and the backward reaction both break into two mechanisms, one slow and one fast. The first term in the first parenthesis of Eq. (
32
) represents the slow forward mechanism, the second term in the same parenthesis represents the slow forward action, the first term in the second parenthesis is the fast backward reaction,
etc.

The feedback loop between the two fast mechanisms is stable (it only causes a frequency correction). Indeed, multiplying the two fast terms in the first line of Eq. (
32
) gives a positive real number. The fast-fast loop fails because the pressure-bump mechanism concentrates dust in pressure maxima (Fig.
3
), whereas the radial backward reaction favours the inertial wave whose pressure maxima are in phase with dust density minima (Fig.
5
). Therefore, the two mechanisms work against each other, they cannot cooperate.

As a result, it is the fast-slow and slow-fast feedback loops that drive the instability. Those loops can attain instability because the phase disagreement between their forward and backward mechanisms is lower, suggesting that the mechanisms are more amenable to cooperation.

For instance, let us focus on the fast-slow loop. The pressure-bump mechanism favours a dust density in phase with pressure (Fig.
3
), whereas the azimuthal backward reaction favours the inertial wave whose pressure pattern is dephased by
{\pi/2}
with respect to the dust density perturbation (Fig.
6
). Consequently, the two mechanisms can compromise by selecting the mode whose pressure is dephased by
{\pi/4}
with respect to the dust density. On this middle ground, both mechanisms cooperate.

To give this concept mathematical backing, let us artificially suppress the slow forward and fast backward mechanisms in Eqs. (
29
) and (
32
). First, the simplified Eq. (
32
) indicates that
\omega_{1}
has an argument of
3\pi/4
. Then, the simplified Eq. (
29
) indicates that
{\widehat{h}_{1}\propto\mathrm{e}^{i\pi/4}\,\widehat{\varrho}_{d,0}}
.

Finally, we should acknowledge that we are not the first to realise that there are several possible feedback loops.
Zhuravlev (2022)
gives a brief description of the fast-fast and fast-slow loops at the end of subsection 3.4, and
Squire & Hopkins (2020)
note in section 4 that the fast-fast loop is stable. However, their explanation focuses on the slow-fast loop. This is unfortunate, because the fast-slow loop is the only one that can dominate (in the low-
\hat{k}_{z}
branch, where
{\omega_{0}\ll\Omega}
).

5
Discussion and conclusion

This paper is the second of a pair investigating
RDI
. Those instabilities arise in mixtures of gas and dust, when the dust drifts at the right velocity to resonate with a gas wave. Our first paper studied the
RDI
that involves sound waves (
14
). The simplicity of this acoustic
RDI
helped us gain an intuition for how all
RDI
develop.

The present paper focuses on an inertial-wave
RDI
known as the
SI
. This instability grows in
PPD
by tapping the free energy contained in the solids’ radial drift (
28
). Interestingly, the
SI
saturates by forming dust filaments and clumps
(
Johansen & Youdin, 2007
)
. If the clumps are dense enough, they can collapse gravitationally and form planetesimals
(
Johansen et al., 2007
)
. This makes the
SI
a promising way to overcome the metre-scale barrier of planet formation.

Unfortunately, the
SI
is still poorly understood. This is not surprising, the coupled dynamics of gas and dust in a rotating disc are particularly complex. But it makes it hard to figure out how the instability saturates and to evaluate its robustness, thereby slowing down the community’s efforts to assess the value of the
SI
as a planetesimal formation theory.

Recognising this, several authors have already tried to explain the
SI
. The earliest attempt was the ‘peloton’ idea from
Youdin & Johansen (2007)
. This mechanism may explain why dust clumps in the later stages of the instability, but it is non-linear so it cannot explain the onset of the instability. Interestingly, our work allows us to track down the issue: the peloton idea relies on the assumption that the main effect of a dust overdensity is to reduce the dust’s drift velocity
\mathbf{v}
, whereas we show in §
4.4.5
that it is actually to increase the back-reaction force of the dust on the gas.
Jacquet et al. (2011)
were the first to make a connection between the
SI
and the well-known phenomenon of dust trapping in pressure maxima, and they also established the necessity of a Coriolis force to maintain those pressure bumps. However, their explanation is 2D whereas the
SI
requires three dimensions.
Lin & Youdin (2017)
find a formal analogy between the dynamics of dusty gas and the thermodynamics of ideal gas, and use it to frame the
SI
as a clockwise path in the
{(P,V)}
plane. While this is true, the analogy induces a layer of abstraction that impedes intuition. Finally,
22
propose an explanation founded on the powerful resonant drag paradigm. Our explanation ends up being similar to theirs, but delving into the mathematics allows us to clarify the picture and add details.

Like
22
, we posit that the
SI
is due to a feedback loop. The ‘forward action’ corresponds to the concentration of drifting dust by an inertial wave. It relies on two mechanisms. The fast one involves the tendency of a wave’s pressure maxima to trap dust, the slow one is due to the dust’s imperfect radial entrainment in the gas’ radial winds (§
A
). The ‘backward reaction’ corresponds to the amplification of an inertial wave by a sinusoidal dust-density pattern. Essentially, variations in dust density perturb the drag force of the dust on the gas. Because this perturbed back-reaction force has a sinusoidal pattern, it excites an inertial wave in the gas. Since the dust’s drift is mostly radial, the back-reaction force is mostly radial. But the dust’s drift also has a small azimuthal component, so one can divide the backward reaction into two mechanisms as well: fast radial and slow azimuthal. It turns out that the fast-fast feedback loop is stable, so the
SI
uses the fast-slow and slow-fast loops.
3
3

3

We think that another
RDI
called the settling instability (
20
) works in much the same way, except that the background dust-to-gas drift
\mathbf{v}
is vertical. This makes things much simpler, because the phase-shift issue that stabilises the fast-fast loop when
\mathbf{v}
is horizontal disappears. Therefore, to understand the onset of the settling instability, one only needs to understand one forward action, one backward reaction, and one feedback loop.

We also explain the role of resonance. In the forward action, resonance ensures that dust cannot drift away from the pressure bump it has been brought to. In the backward reaction, it ensures that the inertial wave is always strengthened by the excess back-reaction from the dust density wave, and never weakened by phase mixing.

Our explanation is backed by rigorous mathematics. We formalise the feedback loop idea with a clear asymptotic theory that breaks up and clearly isolates the different components of the two loops, because they manifest at different orders. §
4.4
offers a new way to compute the growth rate of
RDI
. Our procedure is slower than the linear algebra of Squire & Hopkins, but more transparent. This makes it easier to understand the physics behind any given
RDI
.

There are many physical effects that might be important in
PPD
and yet are neglected in the present study. We did not include self-gravity because we do not think it plays a key role at the linear stage (unless the disk is very massive). We only considered resonant modes because we learned in
14
that
RDI
keep a high growth rate even when there are several percent of detuning. We did not include gas viscosity nor dust diffusion, mainly because their stabilising effect has already been well characterised
(
Umurhan et al., 2020
;
Chen & Lin, 2020
)
. Similarly, we assumed that all the dust particles have the same size even though
Krapp et al. (2019)
and
McNally et al. (2021)
showed that the
SI
dwindles when the particle size distribution is broad. Finally and in no particular order, we did not include collisions between particles, we did not include any magneto-hydro-dynamic effect, we did not account for the dependence of the stopping time on the gas’ velocity, and we did not account for any background turbulence.

We think that our asymptotic procedure will be a useful tool, helping to predict the impact of the aforementioned physical effects on the
SI
. For instance, we used it in
14
to study the effect of detuning and a varying stopping time.

Appendix A
Dynamics of a dust grain pushed radially

To understand the slow forward mechanism, we need to figure out how dust grains respond to a radial gas flow. We shall do this progressively: first we will see how a particle responds to an instantaneous radial impulsion (§
A.1
), then we will add drag (§
A.2
), and finally we will switch from an impulsion to a constant wind (§
A.3
).

A.1
Instantaneous radial impulsion without drag

Let us start simple with a solid particle that is insensitive to drag, for instance because it is too big. We work in the (Keplerian) shearing box, so the particle moves according to

\displaystyle\ddot{x}

\displaystyle=+2\Omega\,\dot{y}+3\Omega^{2}\,x,

\displaystyle\ddot{y}

\displaystyle=-2\Omega\,\dot{x},

where
{x}
is the particle’s radial position,
{y}
its azimuthal position, and
{3\Omega^{2}x}
the force deriving from the tidal potential
\phi_{t}
. If we replace the azimuthal velocity
\dot{y}
by the deviation to Keplerian velocity
{\delta\dot{y}=\dot{y}+\frac{3}{2}\Omega x}
, the system simplifies to:

\displaystyle\ddot{x}

\displaystyle=+2\Omega\,\delta\dot{y},

(33a)

\displaystyle\delta\ddot{y}

\displaystyle=-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Omega\,\dot{x}.

(33b)

Assume that the particles starts at the origin with a small radial velocity. The initial condition are thus
{\mathbf{x}(0)=\mathbf{0}}
and
{\dot{\mathbf{x}}(0)=u_{0,x}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{x}}
, leading to

\displaystyle x(t)

\displaystyle=\,\,\,\,\textstyle\frac{u_{0,x}}{\Omega}\,\,\sin{(\Omega t)},

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=2\,\textstyle\frac{u_{0,x}}{\Omega}\left[\cos{(\Omega t)}-1\right].

Those equations indicate that the particle describes elliptic epicycles in the shearing box. Interestingly,
{\delta\dot{y}=-\frac{1}{2}\Omega\,x}
, so the particle is sub-Keplerian when
{x>0}
and super-Keplerian when
{x<0}
.

Since there is no drag, this can all be reframed as a classical orbital mechanics question: “A particle initially on a circular orbit receives an instantaneous radial push. How does it respond?". The answer is that the particle switches to the elliptical orbit that has the same specific angular momentum as the initial orbit. This process is depicted in Fig.
8
.

Now remember that angular momentum is proportional to azimuthal velocity. Therefore, whenever the particle is outside of its initial orbit, its azimuthal velocity is lower than that of the local circular orbit. Conversely, whenever the particle is inside of its initial orbit, its azimuthal velocity is higher than that of the local circular orbit.

A.2
Instantaneous radial impulsion with drag

Let us now perform the same calculation in the presence of drag. The findings of §
A.1
allow us to guess what will happen.

Drag will damp any radial motion, so the particle will eventually settle on a circular orbit. It is not obvious which circle it will choose, because the particle feels a tailwind and gains angular momentum at apoapsis, but feels a headwind and loses angular momentum at periapsis. Fortunately, a theorem on the sign of alternating series allows us to affirm that the final radius will be larger than
r_{0}

Indeed, once we include the drag exerted by Keplerian gas on the dust, the equations of motion (

\displaystyle=+2\Omega\,\delta\dot{y}-\frac{\dot{x}}{\tau},

\displaystyle\delta\ddot{y}

\displaystyle=-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Omega\,\dot{x}-\displaystyle\frac{\delta\dot{y}}{\tau}.

(34b)

Using the same initial conditions as in §
A.1
, we get

\displaystyle\!x(t)

\displaystyle=\!\frac{\tau\,u_{0,x}}{1\!+\!\text{St}^{2}}\!\left\{\left[1\!-\!\cos{(\Omega t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right]\!+\!\text{St}\sin{(\Omega t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right\},

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\frac{\tau\,u_{0,x}}{1\!+\!\text{St}^{2}}\!\bigg\{-\frac{3}{2}\Omega t\!+\!\frac{\text{St}(1\!-\!2\text{St}^{2})}{1\!+\!\text{St}^{2}}\left[1\!-\!\cos{(\Omega t)}\,\mathrm{e}^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}\right]\\

\displaystyle x(t\gg\tau)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\tau\,u_{0,x}}{1+\text{St}^{2}},

\displaystyle y(t\gg\tau)

meaning that the dust particle eventually settles on a circular orbit. The drag-induced circularisation of eccentric orbits was first calculated by
Adachi et al. (1976)

As expected, the difference between the initial and final radii,
{\Delta r=\frac{\tau\,u_{0,x}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}}

Indeed, small particles circularise almost immediately, so they do not have time to exchange much angular momentum with the gas. Conversely, big particles take several orbits to circularise, so they suffer from compensations between angular momentum gain at apoapsis and angular momentum loss at periapsis.

A.3
Constant and uniform radial wind

Let us increase the complexity again by replacing the impulsion with a constant radial gas flow
{\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{g}=u_{g,x}\,\mbox{\boldmath{$e$}}_{x}}
.

After a few dust stopping times, the dust should reach a constant radial velocity. To estimate this velocity, we note that in §
A.2
, the particle moved by roughly
\Delta r
in a time
\tau

{\frac{\Delta r}{\tau}=\frac{u_{0,x}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}}
.

Formally, if the gas’ azimuthal velocity is Keplerian, the radial wind transforms the equations of motion (
34
) to

\displaystyle\ddot{x}

\displaystyle=+2\Omega\,\delta\dot{y}-\frac{\dot{x}-u_{g,x}}{\tau},

(37a)

\displaystyle\delta\ddot{y}

\displaystyle=-\textstyle\frac{1}{2}\Omega\,\dot{x}-\displaystyle\frac{\delta\dot{y}}{\tau}.

(37b)

Those equations are identical to those that define
\mbox{\boldmath{$u$}}_{d}^{\text{ter}}
in §
4.2.3

Whatever the initial conditions, after a few dust stopping times, we get
{\dot{x}(t\gg\tau)\approx\frac{u_{g,x}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}}
, exactly as expected. From there, we deduce

\dot{x}(t\gg\tau)=u_{g,x}-\frac{\text{St}^{2}}{1+\text{St}^{2}}u_{g,x},

(38)

which is identical to Eq. (
16a

A.4
Key takeaways

In §
A.1
we learned that pushing a dust particle radially does not automatically change its long-term orbital radius. For instance, imagine an outward radial impulsion. Because it is radial, the particle does not gain any angular momentum. So it moves outward, but finds itself having a lower azimuthal velocity than the circular orbit at its new location. Consequently, it experiences a weak centrifugal force that cannot compensate gravity: the particle must fall back down.

Then in §
A.2
we saw that if a dust particle is surrounded by gas in Keplerian rotation, then a radial push will lead to a long-term radial displacement. This is a consequence of drag, which allows the particle to exchange angular momentum with the gas. In our example, the dust particle becomes sub-Keplerian when it moves outward. Therefore, it feels a tailwind and gains angular momentum. Ultimately, this is what allows it to settle on a higher circular orbit.

Finally in §
A.3
we found that dust can be entrained by a radial gas wind, but cannot keep up with that wind. This is due to the effect from §
A.1
: the weak centrifugal force does not compensate gravity, so there is a net body force pointing towards the star. This inward force partially cancels out the outward drag force from the wind, hence why the dust moves slower than the gas.
