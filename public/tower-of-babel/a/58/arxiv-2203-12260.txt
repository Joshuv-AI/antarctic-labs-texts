Reproduction of initial distributions from the first hitting time distribution for birth-and-death processes This research was supported by RIMS and by ISM.
Kosuke Yamato, and, Kouji Yano

Abstract
For birth-and-death processes, we show that every initial distribution is reproduced from the first hitting time distribution. The reproduction is done by applying to the distribution function a differential operator defined through the eigenfunction of the generator. Using the spectral theory for generalized second-order differential operators, we study asymmetric random walks.



1
Introduction

Birth-and-death processes are continuous-time Markov chains on
S=\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
which only jump to neighboring points.
Let us consider a birth-and-death process
X
with positive birth and death rates on
S\setminus\{0\}
and stopped at
0
. Let
\nu
be its initial distribution and
T_{0}
be its first hitting time of
0
.
Our aim is to reproduce the initial distribution
\nu
from the first hitting time distribution
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}[T_{0}\in dt]
,
where
\mathbb{P}_{\nu}
denotes the underlying probability measure of
X
under the initial distribution
\nu
.
We show that the reproduction of the initial distribution can be done via a differential operator obtained from the eigenfunctions of the generator.

A key tool is the spectral theory.
As we will see, there always exist the first hitting time densities
f_{i}(t)\ (i\geq 1,t>0)
, that is, there are functions such that
\mathbb{P}_{i}[T_{0}\in dt]=f_{i}(t)dt
. In addition, the density
f_{i}
has a spectral representation:

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)=\pi_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\rho(d\theta),

(1.1)

where
\pi
is the speed measure,
\rho
is the spectral measure and
\psi_{-\theta}
is a Dirichlet
(-\theta)
-eigenfunction of the generator, whose precise definitions will be given in Section
2
.
As is well-known (e.g., Karlin and McGregor
[
6
]
), the transition probability has the spectral representation:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}[X_{t}=j]=\pi_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\psi_{-\theta}(j)\rho(d\theta).

(1.2)

Comparing the representations in (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) and changing the order of the integration and the differentiation formally, we see the following result.
We will introduce a matrix
C
in Proposition
3.3
whose columns are generalized
0
-eigenfunctions of the generator
Q
in the sense that
C_{0}=0
and
C_{j-1}=QC_{j}
for
j\geq 1
.
We write
\partial_{t}=\frac{d}{dt}
and define
\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)
by the differential polynomial

\displaystyle\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)=\sum_{k=1}^{j}C(j,k)\partial_{t}^{k-1}\quad(j\geq 1).

(1.3)

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
S\setminus\{0\}
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu}[X_{t}=j]=\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)f_{\nu}(t)\quad(j\geq 1),

(1.4)

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu}[X_{t}=j]:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu\{i\}\mathbb{P}_{i}[X_{t}=j]\quad\text{and}\quad f_{\nu}(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu\{i\}f_{i}(t).

(1.5)

By taking
t\to 0
in (
1.4
), we obtain the reproduction of the initial distribution:

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
\nu
be a probability measure on
S\setminus\{0\}
. Then it holds

\displaystyle\nu\{j\}=\lim_{t\to+0}\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)f_{\nu}(t)\quad(j\geq 1).

(1.6)

To obtain the reproduction formula in the explicit form, we need to compute the spectral measure of the generator.
For this purpose, we look at birth-and-death processes as
generalized diffusions
,
and apply the spectral theory for the generalized second-order ordinary differential operators.
Using Doob’s
h
-transform, we compute the matrix
C
for asymmetric random walks.

In appendix, we study reproduction of the initial distributions for one-dimensional diffusions. We will show that under the existence of the Laplace transform of the spectral measure reproduction is possible for initial distributions with square integrable densities with respect to the speed measure.
The main tool is the spectral theory, especially the generalized Fourier transform and the spectral representation of the first hitting time densities.

Background of the study

In
[
13
]
, we have studied quasi-stationary distributions of one-dimensional diffusions.
Let
X
be a
\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{ds}
-diffusion on
S:=[0,b)\ (0<b\leq\infty)
stopped at
0
.
Let us denote the set of probability distributions on
S
by
\mathcal{P}(S)
or
\mathcal{P}S
.
For a set of initial distributions
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{P}(S\setminus\{0\})
, we say that the
first hitting uniqueness
holds on
\mathcal{P}
if the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}\ni\mu\mapsto\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T_{0}\in dt]\quad\text{is injective},

(1.7)

where
T_{x}
denotes the first hitting time of
x
for
x\in S
.
Recall a probability distribution
\nu
is called a
quasi-stationary distribution
of
X
when the following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{\nu}[X_{t}\in dx\mid T_{0}>t]=\nu(dx)\quad(t>0).

(1.8)

Define

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{exp}}:=\{\mu\in\mathcal{P}(0,b)\mid\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T_{0}\in dt]=\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}dt\quad\text{for some }\lambda>0\}.

(1.9)

One of the main results in
[
13
]
was the following:

Theorem 1.3

(
[
13
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
X
be a
\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{ds}
-diffusion on
[0,b)\ (0<b\leq\infty)

stopped at
0
satisfying

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}[T_{0}<\infty]=1\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{x}[T_{y}<\infty]>0\quad(x\in(0,b),y\in[0,b)).

(1.10)

Set

\displaystyle\mu_{t}(dx):=\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[X_{t}\in dx\mid T_{0}>t].

(1.11)

Assume the first hitting uniqueness holds on
\mathcal{P}_{\mathrm{exp}}
and

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}_{\nu}}[T_{0}\in dt]=\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}dt\quad\text{for some}\ \lambda>0\ \text{and some}\ {\nu}\in\mathcal{P}(0,b).

(1.12)

Then for
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(0,b)
and
\lambda>0
, the following are equivalent:

(i)

\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T_{0}>t+s]}{\mathbb{P}_{\mu}[T_{0}>t]}=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda s}\ (s>0)
.

(ii)

\mathbb{P}_{\mu_{t}}[T_{0}\in ds]\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}\lambda\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda s}ds
.

(iii)

\mu_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{}{\nu}
.

Here the convergence of probability distributions is in the sense of the weak convergence.
From Theorem
1.3
, we can see that if there is a quasi-stationary distribution
\nu_{\lambda}
with
\mathbb{P}_{\nu_{\lambda}}[T_{0}>t]=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}
, the convergence
\mu_{t}\xrightarrow[t\to\infty]{w}\nu_{\lambda}
is reduced to the tail behavior of
T_{0}
.

Rogers
[
10
]
has studied the first hitting uniqueness on
\mathcal{P}(0,b)
for one-dimensional diffusions and gave a sufficient condition for it by a condition on the resolvent density.
His condition was, however, too strong; he gave in the paper an example of a diffusion satisfying the first hitting uniqueness but not his condition.

Reproduction of initial distributions for a set
\mathcal{P}\subset\mathcal{P}(S)
obviously implies the first hitting uniqueness on
\mathcal{P}
. More precisely, we can see that the reproduction formula (
1.6
) provides the inverse of the map (
1.7
).

2
Birth-and-death processes

Let us consider a
birth-and-death process
on
S=\{0,1,2,\cdots\}
such that
0
is a trap, which is a continuous-time Markov chain with neighboring jumps such that
0
is a trap.
We denote the birth rates by
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
and the death rates by
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}[X_{\tau}=i+1]=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}},\quad\mathbb{P}_{i}[X_{\tau}=i-1]=\frac{\mu_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{P}_{i}[\tau>t]=\mathrm{e}^{-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})}

(2.1)

where
\tau=\inf\{t>0\mid X_{\tau}\neq X_{0}\}
denotes the first exit time from the initial state.
We set
\mu_{0}=\lambda_{0}=0
according to the assumption that
0
is a trap.
We assume the birth and death rates are positive on
S\setminus\{0\}
:
\lambda_{i}>0,\ \mu_{i}>0\ (i\geq 1)
.
Its
Q
-matrix

\displaystyle Q=(Q(i,j))_{i,j\geq 0}=\begin{pmatrix}Q(0,0)&Q(0,1)&\cdots\\
Q(1,0)&Q(1,1)&\cdots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots\end{pmatrix}

(2.2)

is given by

\displaystyle Q(i,i-1)=\mu_{i}\quad(i\geq 1)\quad\text{and}\quad Q(i,i+1)=\lambda_{i},\quad Q(i,i)=-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})\quad(i\geq 0).

(2.3)

We denote the transition probability by
P_{t}
, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{i}[X_{t}=j]=P_{t}(i,j)\quad(t\geq 0,\ i,j\geq 0).

(2.4)

For this process, the transition probability
P_{t}(i,j)
satisfies the forward and backward equations:
\partial_{t}P_{t}=P_{t}Q=QP_{t}
, that is,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{t}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{j-1}P_{t}(i,j-1)-(\lambda_{j}+\mu_{j})P_{t}(i,j)+\mu_{j+1}P_{t}(i,j+1)\quad(i,j\geq 0),

(2.5)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{t}(i,j)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}P_{t}(i-1,j)-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})P_{t}(i,j)+\lambda_{i}P_{t}(i+1,j)\quad(i,j\geq 0),

(2.6)

where we understand
\lambda_{-1}P_{t}(i,-1)=0
(see e.g., Anderson
[
1
, Chapter 2, Theorem 2.2]
).

Define the
speed measure

\pi=(\pi_{i})_{i\in S\setminus\{0\}}
on
S\setminus\{0\}
by

\displaystyle\pi_{1}=1,\quad\pi_{i}=\frac{\lambda_{1}\cdots\lambda_{i-1}}{\mu_{2}\cdots\mu_{i}}\quad(i\geq 2).

(2.7)

Note that
\pi
is characterized with
\pi_{1}=1
and the following balancing condition:

\displaystyle\pi_{i+1}\mu_{i+1}=\pi_{i}\lambda_{i}\quad(i\geq 1).

(2.8)

Define the operator
Q
by

\displaystyle Qf(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}Q(i,j)f(j)=\mu_{i}f(i-1)-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})f(i)+\lambda_{i}f(i+1)\quad(i\geq 0)

(2.9)

for a function
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
, where we understand
\mu_{0}f(-1)=0
.
Then
Q
is symmetric w.r.t.
\pi
in the sense that for functions
f,g:S\to\mathbb{R}
which are finitely supported and satisfy
f(0)=g(0)=0
the following holds:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(Qf)(i)g(i)\pi_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(i)(Qg)(i)\pi_{i}.

(2.10)

Indeed, by (
2.8
) it holds

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(Qf)(i)g(i)\pi_{i}

(2.11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\mu_{i}f(i-1)-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})f(i)+\lambda_{i}f(i+1))g(i)\pi_{i}

(2.12)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}f(i-1)g(i)\pi_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})f(i)g(i)\pi_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}f(i+1)g(i)\pi_{i}

(2.13)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}f(i)g(i+1)\pi_{i}-\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})f(i)g(i)\pi_{i}+\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\mu_{i}f(i)g(i-1)\pi_{i}

(2.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(i)(Qg)(i)\pi_{i}.

(2.15)

Generalized
0
-eigenfunctions

Let us recall the spectral representation of the transition probability
P_{t}
studied in Karlin and McGregor
[
6
]
.
We introduce the scale function of
Q
:

Proposition 3.1
.

The scale function of
Q
defined by

\displaystyle s(i):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0&(i=0),\\
&\frac{1}{\mu_{1}}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\frac{1}{\pi_{j}\lambda_{j}}&(i\geq 1)\end{aligned}\right.

(3.1)

satisfies
Qu=0
and
u(0)=0
.
Conversely, if
u
is a function satisfying
Qu=0
and
u(0)=0
, then
u
is a constant multiple of
s
.

Proof.

From the definition of
s
, it holds

\displaystyle Qs(i)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}s(i-1)-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})s(i)+\lambda_{i}s(i+1)

(3.2)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\mu_{i}}{\pi_{i-1}\lambda_{i-1}}+\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\pi_{i}\lambda_{i}}=0.

(3.3)

Let
u
be another function satisfying
u(0)=0
and

\displaystyle Qu(i)=\mu_{i}u(i-1)-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})u(i)+\lambda_{i}u(i+1)=0\quad(i\geq 1).

(3.4)

Set
c=u(1)
. If
c=0
, from (
3.4
), it inductively holds
u(i)=0\ (i\geq 0)
.
If
c\neq 0
, set
v=s-(1/c)u
. Then
v
satisfies
Qv=0
and
v(0)=v(1)=0
.
Thus it follows that
v(i)=0\ (i\geq 0)
, and we obtain
u=cs
.
∎

We introduce two difference operators
D_{\pi}
and
D_{s}
whose composition is the
Q
-matrix.

Proposition 3.2
.

Define

\displaystyle D_{\pi}f(i):=\frac{f(i)-f(i-1)}{\pi_{i}}\quad(i\geq 1)\quad\text{and}\quad D_{s}f(i):=\frac{f(i+1)-f(i)}{s(i+1)-s(i)}\quad(i\geq 0).

(3.5)

Then it holds
Qf(i)=D_{\pi}D_{s}f(i)\ (i\geq 1)
for every positive measurable function
f:S\to\mathbb{R}
.

Proof.

It holds from (
2.8
)

\displaystyle D_{\pi}D_{s}f(i)

\displaystyle=D_{\pi}(\pi_{i}\lambda_{i}(f(i+1)-f(i))

(3.6)

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi_{i}\lambda_{i}(f(i+1)-f(i))-\pi_{i-1}\lambda_{i-1}(f(i)-f(i-1))}{\pi_{i}}

(3.7)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{i}(f(i+1)-f(i))-\mu_{i}(f(i)-f(i-1))

(3.8)

\displaystyle=Qf(i).

(3.9)

∎

We introduce a matrix
C
which we will use in Proposition
4.1
to represent an eigenfunction of
Q
.

Proposition 3.3
.

There exists a unique matrix

\displaystyle C=(C(i,j))_{i,j\geq 0}=(C_{0},C_{1},\cdots)\quad\text{with}\quad C_{j}=(C(i,j))_{i\geq 0}=\begin{pmatrix}C(0,j)\\
C(1,j)\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}\quad(j\geq 0)

(3.10)

which satisfies the relation

\displaystyle QC_{j}=C_{j-1}\quad(j\geq 1)

(3.11)

with

\displaystyle C_{0}=0,\quad C_{1}(i)=s(i)\quad(i\geq 1)\quad\text{and}\quad C(i,j)=0\quad(i<j),

(3.12)

where
s
is the scale function given in (
3.1
).

Remark 3.4
.

Note that
C_{j}
is a generalized
0
-eigenfunction of rank
j
in the sense that

Q^{j}C_{j}=0
and
Q^{j-1}C_{j}=s\neq 0
.

Proof of Proposition
3.3
.

The matrix
C
must satisfy

\displaystyle C(i,j-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}Q(i,k)C(k,j)

(3.13)

\displaystyle=\mu_{i}C(i-1,j)-(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})C(i,j)+\lambda_{i}C(i+1,j)\quad(i\geq 1,j\geq 0).

(3.14)

Let us determine
C_{j}
recursively.
For
j=2,3,4,\cdots
, being fixed, suppose
C_{j-1}
has been determined.
For
i\geq j-1
, we have

\displaystyle C(i+1,j)=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}}(C(i,j-1)-\mu_{i}C(i-1,j)+(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})C(i,j)).

(3.15)

Substituting
i=j-1,j
in (
3.15
), we see that
C(j,j)
and
C(j+1,j)
are determined as

\displaystyle C(j,j)

\displaystyle=\frac{C(j-1,j-1)}{\lambda_{j-1}},

(3.16)

\displaystyle C(j+1,j)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}}(C(j,j-1)+(\lambda_{j}+\mu_{i})C(j,j)),

(3.17)

respectively.
By (
3.15
), the entries
C(i,j)
for
i\geq j
are determined recursively.
∎

4
\theta
-eigenfunctions and spectral representations

The eigenfunctions for
Q
can be obtained as the generating functions corresponding to the matrix
C
.

Proposition 4.1
.

For
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, define

\displaystyle\psi_{\theta}(i)=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}C(i,j)\theta^{j-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{i}C(i,j)\theta^{j-1}.

(4.1)

Then
u=\psi_{\theta}
is the unique solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle Qu=\theta u\quad\text{with}\ u(0)=0,\ D_{s}u(0)=1.

(4.2)

Proof.

It is obvious that
\psi_{\theta}(0)=0
.
It holds

\displaystyle D_{s}\psi_{\theta}(0)=\mu_{1}(\psi_{\theta}(1)-\psi_{\theta}(0))=\mu_{1}C(1,1)=1.

(4.3)

Note that for

\displaystyle\psi_{\theta}=(\psi_{\theta}(i))_{i\geq 0}=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{\theta}(0)\\
\psi_{\theta}(1)\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad r_{\theta}:=(r_{\theta}(i))_{i\geq 0}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\\
\theta\\
\theta^{2}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},

(4.4)

it holds

\displaystyle\psi_{\theta}=Cr_{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j}\theta^{j-1}.

(4.5)

Thus from (
3.11
) we have

\displaystyle Q\psi_{\theta}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}QC_{j}\theta^{j-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j-1}\theta^{j-1}=\theta\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}C_{j}\theta^{j-1}=\theta\psi_{\theta},

(4.6)

where we note that
C_{0}=0
.
Hence the function
\psi_{\theta}
is the solution of (
4.2
).

We check the uniqueness. Suppose
u=f
satisfies the equation (
4.2
).
Then
v:=f-\psi_{\theta}
satisfies
Qv=\theta v
with
v(0)=0
and
D_{s}v(0)=0
, which implies
v(1)=0
.
It follows from the definition of
Q
that
v(i)=0\ (i\geq 2)
.
Thus it follows
f=\psi_{\theta}
.
∎

From the spectral theory (see e.g., Karlin and McGregor
[
6
, p.501]
), there exists a measure
\rho
on
[0,\infty)
, which we call the spectral measure of
Q
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(i)^{2}\pi_{i}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(\theta)^{2}\rho(d\theta),

(4.7)

for every finitely supported function
f
with
f(0)=0
,
where
\widehat{f}
is the
generalized Fourier transform
of
f
:

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(i)\psi_{-\theta}(i)\pi_{i}.

(4.8)

The map
f\mapsto\widehat{f}
can be extended to the unitary map between
L^{2}(\pi)
and
L^{2}(\rho)
and the functions
\{\psi_{-\theta}(i)\}_{i\geq 1}
comprise an orthogonal basis of
L^{2}(\rho)
and satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{ij}}{\pi_{j}}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\psi_{-\theta}(j)\rho(d\theta)\quad(i,j\geq 1),

(4.9)

where
\delta_{ij}
is the Kronecker delta.
We now have the spectral representation of the transition probability
P_{t}
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi_{j}}P_{t}(i,j)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\psi_{-\theta}(j)\rho(d\theta)\quad(i,j\geq 1,t\geq 0).

(4.10)

From (
4.10
), we show the spectral representation of the first hitting time densities at
0
as follows:

Proposition 4.2
.

For
i\geq 1
, it holds

\displaystyle(f_{i}(t):=)\ \partial_{t}\mathbb{P}_{i}[T_{0}\leq t]=\pi_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\rho(d\theta).

(4.11)

Proof.

Note that
\mathbb{P}_{i}[T_{0}\leq t]=\mathbb{P}_{i}[X_{t}=0]
.
From (
2.5
) with
\lambda_{0}=\mu_{0}=0
, (
4.10
) and
\psi_{-\theta}(1)=C(1,1)=s(1)=1/\mu_{1}
, we have for
i\geq 1

\displaystyle\partial_{t}P_{t}(i,0)

\displaystyle=\mu_{1}P_{t}(i,1)

(4.12)

\displaystyle=\pi_{i}\mu_{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\psi_{-\theta}(1)\rho(d\theta)

(4.13)

\displaystyle=\pi_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\rho(d\theta).

(4.14)

∎

5
Proof of the reproduction formula

We prove Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Let us first consider the case where
\nu
is a point mass.
From Proposition
4.1
and
4.2
, we can see for
i\geq 1
that

\displaystyle\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)f_{i}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j}C(j,k){\partial_{t}}^{k-1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\rho(d\theta)

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{j}C(j,k)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}(-\theta)^{k-1}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\rho(d\theta)

(5.2)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\theta t}\psi_{-\theta}(i)\psi_{-\theta}(j)\rho(d\theta).

(5.3)

Thus (
1.3
) holds when
\nu
is a point mass.

Let us consider the general case.
Suppose we may find some constants
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
such that

\displaystyle\max_{i\geq 1}|\partial^{k}_{t}f_{i}(t)|\leq\alpha_{k}\quad(t>0).

(5.4)

Then we have

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu\{i\}|\partial^{k}_{t}f_{i}(t)|\leq\alpha_{k},

(5.5)

and we can change the order of
the differentiation w.r.t.
t
and the integration by
\nu
:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu\{i\}\partial^{k}_{t}f_{i}(t)=\partial^{k}_{t}\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu\{i\}f_{\nu}(t).

(5.6)

Then it follows that

\displaystyle\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)f_{\nu}(t)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\nu\{i\}\psi_{\partial_{t}}(j)f_{i}(t)=\mathbb{P}_{\nu}[X_{t}=j].

(5.7)

Here the last equality follows from (
1.4
) for a point mass.

Let us find
such a sequence
\{\alpha_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
as (
5.4
) is satisfied.
We construct it recursively.
From (
4.12
), it holds

\displaystyle f_{i}(t)=\mu_{1}P_{t}(i,1)\leq\mu_{1},

(5.8)

and we may take
\alpha_{0}:=\mu_{1}
.
Let
k\geq 0
and assume we have constants
\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{k}
satisfying (
5.4
).
Then from (
1.4
) in the case when
\nu
is a point mass, we have

\displaystyle C(k+2,k+2){\partial_{t}}^{k+1}f_{i}(t)=P_{t}(i,k+2)-\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}C(k+2,l){\partial_{t}}^{l-1}f_{i}(t)

(5.9)

for every
i\geq 1
.
Thus it follows

\displaystyle C(k+2,k+2)|{\partial_{t}}^{k+1}f_{i}(t)|\leq 1+\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}C(k+2,l)\alpha_{l-1}.

(5.10)

From (
3.16
) it holds
C(k+2,k+2)>0
and hence it holds

\displaystyle|{\partial_{t}}^{k+1}f_{i}(t)|\leq\frac{1+\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}C(k+2,l)\alpha_{l-1}}{C(k+2,k+2)},

(5.11)

and we may take

\displaystyle\alpha_{k+1}:=\frac{1+\sum_{l=1}^{k+1}C(k+2,l)\alpha_{l-1}}{C(k+2,k+2)}.

(5.12)

The proof is complete.
∎

6
Looking at birth-and-death processes as generalized diffusions

Generalized diffusions are the processes which unify birth-and-death processes and one-dimensional diffusions.
Here we recall some studies of generalized diffusions and consider the application to birth-and-death processes.
A main reference for the subject is Kotani and Watanabe
[
7
]
.

We say that
w:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty]
is a string when it is non-decreasing and right-continuous. Set
\ell(w):=\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}\mid w(x)=\infty\}
.
For a string
w
, we define the measure
dw
on
[0,\ell(w))
by
dw(a,b]=w(b)-w(a)\ (0\leq a<b)
and
dw\{0\}=w(0)
.

Let
w
be a string with and let
B
be a standard Brownian motion and let
L(t,x)
be the jointly continuous local time of
B
, that is, for every non-negative measurable function
f
it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t}f(B_{s})ds=2\int_{\mathbb{R}}f(x)L(t,x)dx.

(6.1)

Define

\displaystyle A(t):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{\ell(w)}L(t,x)dw(x)&(t<T_{0}\wedge T_{b}),\\
&\infty&(t\geq T_{0}\wedge T_{b}).\end{aligned}\right.

(6.2)

Then the process
X(t):=B(A^{-1}(t))
is a strong Markov process on
I
stopped at the boundaries. We call
X
the
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d}{dx}
-generalized diffusion
and
dw
the
speed measure
of
X
.
Note that if the
dw
has a full support in
(0,b)
, the process
X
is a diffusion, and if
dw
is supported on
\mathbb{N}
, the process
X
is a birth-and-death process.

It is known that the transition density of
X
has the spectral representation. There exists a jointly continuous function
p(t,x,y)

and a Radon measure
\rho_{w}
on
(0,\infty)
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}[X_{t}\in dy]=p(t,x,y)dw(y)\quad(t>0,x,y\in(0,b)),

(6.3)

and

\displaystyle p(t,x,y)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\psi_{-\lambda}(x)\psi_{-\lambda}(y)\rho_{w}(d\lambda)\quad(t>0,x,y\in(0,b)),

(6.4)

(see e.g., McKean
[
4
]
).
We call the measure
\rho_{w}
the spectral measure of
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d}{dx}
.

6.1
Strings and spectral measures

For a string
w
, its dual string
w^{-1}
defined by

\displaystyle w^{-1}(x):=\inf\{y>0\mid w(y)>x\}\quad(x\geq 0)

(6.5)

is also a string.

We denote by
\rho_{w}
(resp.
\sigma_{w}
) the spectral measure of
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d}{dx}
with Dirichlet (resp. Neumann) boundary condition at
0
, and if the boundary
\ell(w)
is regular, we assume the Dirichlet boundary condition at
\ell(w)
.
Then it is known that the following relation holds:

\displaystyle\rho_{w}(d\lambda)=\lambda\sigma_{w^{-1}}(d\lambda)\quad\text{on}\ [0,\infty)

(6.6)

(see Yano
[
15
, Theorem 2.2]
).
Thus we can obtain the spectral measure from the one corresponding to the dual string.
This relation is useful because
\sigma_{w}
can be sometimes obtained by the spectral theory, which we will explain below.

Let
w
be a string.
For
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
, define
u=\varphi_{\lambda}
as the unique solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle u(x)=1+\lambda\int_{0}^{x}dy\int_{0-}^{y}u(z)dw(z)\quad(x\geq 0),

(6.7)

and define
u=\psi_{\lambda}
as the unique solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle u(x)=x+\lambda\int_{0}^{x}dy\int_{0}^{y}u(z)dw(z)\quad(x\geq 0).

(6.8)

Define the spectral characteristic function
h
by

\displaystyle h(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{\ell(w)}\frac{dx}{\varphi_{\lambda}(x)^{2}}\quad(\lambda>0).

(6.9)

Note that the following equality holds:

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\ell(w)}\frac{dx}{\varphi_{\lambda}(x)^{2}}=\lim_{x\to\ell(w)}\frac{\psi_{\lambda}(x)}{\varphi_{\lambda}(x)}.

(6.10)

Indeed, since the functions
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
are
\lambda
-eigenfunctions for
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}
, it holds

\displaystyle d\left(\left(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\psi_{\lambda}\right)\varphi_{\lambda}-\left(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\varphi_{\lambda}\right)\psi_{\lambda}\right)=0,

(6.11)

and it is clear that
\left.\left(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\psi_{\lambda}\right)\varphi_{\lambda}-\left(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\varphi_{\lambda}\right)\psi_{\lambda}\right|_{x=0}=1
.
Thus
\left(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\psi_{\lambda}\right)\varphi_{\lambda}-\left(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\varphi_{\lambda}\right)\psi_{\lambda}=1
.
Thus it follows

\displaystyle d\left(\frac{\psi_{\lambda}}{\varphi_{\lambda}}\right)=\frac{(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\psi_{\lambda})\varphi_{\lambda}-(\frac{d^{+}}{dx}\varphi_{\lambda})\psi_{\lambda}}{\varphi_{\lambda}^{2}}dx=\frac{dx}{\varphi_{\lambda}^{2}},

(6.12)

and (
6.10
) holds.
By the spectral theory for generalized second-order differential operators, it is known that the function
h
is represented by

\displaystyle h(\lambda)=c+\int_{0-}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{w}(d\xi)}{\lambda+\xi}\quad(\lambda>0)

(6.13)

for
c=\inf\mathop{\rm Supp}dw
. Here we note that
\sigma_{w}\{0\}=1/w(\infty)
(see
[
7
, p.239]
).
Thus we can obtain
\sigma_{w}
by the Stieltjes inversion formula:

\displaystyle\sigma_{w}(I)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\lim_{\varepsilon\to+0}\int_{I}\mathop{\rm Im}h(-\lambda+i\varepsilon)d\lambda\quad\text{for }I\subset[0,\infty)\text{ with }\sigma_{w}(\partial I)=0.

(6.14)

6.2
Spectral measures of birth-and-death processes

Let us consider a birth-and-death process
X
whose
Q
-matrix is given by (
2.3
). To apply the theory of generalized diffusions, we need a string which defines the generalized diffusion equivalent to
X
.
For the scale function
s
defined in (
3.1
), we denote its linear interpolation by the same symbol:

\displaystyle s(x):=(i+1-x)s(i)+(x-i)s(i+1)\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 0\quad\text{such that}\quad i\leq x<i+1.

(6.15)

Define a string
m
on
(0,\infty)
by

\displaystyle m(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\pi_{i}1\{i\leq x\}\quad(x>0),

(6.16)

where
\pi_{i}
is given in (
2.7
).
Then

\displaystyle w:=m\circ s^{-1}

(6.17)

defines a string and the generalized diffusion
\widetilde{X}
corresponding to
w

has its state space
\{s(i)\}_{i\geq 0}
.

We check that the generalized diffusion
s^{-1}(\widetilde{X})
is a realization of
X
.
Recalling that the generalized diffusions are obtained by the time change of a Brownian motion by
A^{-1}
for
A
in (
6.2
), we can see

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{s(i)}[\widetilde{X}_{\tau}=s(i+1)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1/(\pi_{i-1}\lambda_{i-1})}{1/(\pi_{i}\lambda_{i})+1/(\pi_{i-1}\lambda_{i-1})}=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}}\quad(i\geq 1),

(6.18)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{s(i)}[\widetilde{X}_{\tau}=s(i-1)]

\displaystyle=1-\mathbb{P}_{s(i)}[\widetilde{X}_{\tau}=s(i+1)]=\frac{\mu_{i}}{\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i}}\quad(i\geq 1),

(6.19)

where
\tau:=\inf\{t>0\mid\widetilde{X}_{t}\neq\widetilde{X}_{0}\}
denotes the time of the first jump (see e.g.,
[
5
, Theorem 23.7]
and
[
9
, Proposition II.3.8]
).
Since
\tau
is exponentially distributed, we can specify the distribution by the mean. Recall the following well-known formula (see e.g.,
[
5
, Lemma 23.10]
):

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\int_{0}^{T_{a}\wedge T_{b}}f(\widetilde{X}_{t})dt\right]=\int_{\mathop{\rm Supp}dw\cap(a,b)}\frac{(x\wedge y-a)(b-x\vee y)}{b-a}f(y)dw(y)

(6.20)

\displaystyle(a,b,x\in\mathop{\rm Supp}dw,a<x<b),

where
f
is non-negative measurable function.
Applying this formula for
x=s(i),\ a=s(i-1),\ b=s(i+1)
and
f=1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{s(i)}\tau

\displaystyle=\frac{(s(i)-s(i-1))(s(i+1)-s(i))}{s(i+1)-s(i-1)}\pi_{i}=(\lambda_{i}+\mu_{i})^{-1}\quad(i\geq 1).

(6.21)

Thus from (
6.18
), (
6.19
) and (
6.21
), we see that the process
\widetilde{X}
is equivalent to
X
.

From (
6.6
) and (
6.13
), we can obtain the spectral measure
\rho_{w}
of
\widetilde{X}
(or
X
) through the eigenfunctions of
\frac{d}{dw^{-1}}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}
.
Since the eigenfunctions of
\frac{d}{dw^{-1}}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}
can be computed from those of
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}
, we have a formula to compute
\rho_{w}
from the eigenfunctions of
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}
. Here we go into the details.

It is not difficult to see that the dual string
w^{-1}
of
w
is equal to
s\circ m^{-1}
and for
x\geq 0
we have

\displaystyle w^{-1}(x)=s(i)\quad\text{for}\quad i\geq 1\quad\text{such that}\quad m(i-1)\leq x<m(i).

(6.22)

Let
\varphi^{(-)}_{\lambda}
and
\psi^{(-)}_{\lambda}
be functions given in (
6.7
) and
(
6.8
)
for
w=w^{-1}
, respectively.
Then as we have seen in the previous section, it holds

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\ell(w^{-1})}\frac{\psi^{(-)}_{\lambda}(x)}{\varphi^{(-)}_{\lambda}(x)}

\displaystyle=\int_{0-}^{\infty}\frac{\sigma_{w^{-1}}(d\xi)}{\lambda+\xi}

(6.23)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\lambda\ell(w)}+\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho_{w}(d\xi)}{\xi(\lambda+\xi)}\quad(\lambda>0),

(6.24)

where we used the obvious fact
w^{-1}(\infty)=\ell(w)
.
Note that
\inf\mathop{\rm Supp}dw^{-1}=0
since
w^{-1}(0)=s(1)>0
.
We consider the eigenfunctions of
\frac{d}{dx}\frac{d}{dw}
, which we denote by
u=\varphi^{d}_{\lambda}
,
v=\psi^{d}_{\lambda}\ (\lambda\in\mathbb{C})
, defined as the unique solutions of the following equations, respectively:

\displaystyle u(x)=1+\lambda\int_{0}^{x}dw(y)\int_{0}^{y}u(z)dz\quad(x\geq 0)

(6.25)

and

\displaystyle v(x)=w(x)+\lambda\int_{0}^{x}dw(y)\int_{0}^{y}v(z)dz.\quad(x\geq 0).

(6.26)

It may not be difficult to check that

\displaystyle\varphi^{(-)}_{\lambda}(w(x))=\varphi^{d}_{\lambda}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\psi^{(-)}_{\lambda}(w(x))=\psi^{d}_{\lambda}(x)\quad(x\geq 0).

(6.27)

We may also easily see that

\displaystyle\varphi^{d}_{\lambda}(x)=\psi^{+}_{\lambda}(x)\quad\text{and}\quad\psi^{d}_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{1}{\lambda}\varphi^{+}_{\lambda}(x)\quad(x\geq 0),

(6.28)

where
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
are the solutions of (
6.7
) and (
6.8
) for
w
in (
6.17
), respectively.

Eventually, it follows for
\lambda>0

\displaystyle\lim_{x\to\ell(w)}\frac{\varphi^{+}_{\lambda}(x)}{\psi^{+}_{\lambda}(x)}=\lim_{x\to\ell(w)}\frac{\lambda\psi^{d}_{\lambda}(x)}{\varphi^{d}_{\lambda}(x)}=\lim_{x\to\ell(w)}\frac{\lambda\psi^{(-)}_{\lambda}(w(x))}{\varphi^{(-)}_{\lambda}(w(x))}=\frac{1}{\ell(w)}+\lambda\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\rho_{w}(d\xi)}{\xi(\lambda+\xi)}.

(6.29)

Thus, we obtain the Stieltjes transform of
\xi^{-1}\rho_{w}(d\xi)
through the eigenfunctions
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
. By the Stieltjes inversion formula, we can compute
\rho_{w}
in some cases. See Section
7
for such examples.

6.3
Doob’s
h
-transform

Here we recall some basic properties of
h
-transform for generalized diffusions (see e.g., Takemura and Tomisaki
[
12
]
for details).
Since we are interested in the first hitting time of
0
, we restrict our attention to the case of generalized diffusions corresponding to a string
w
with
\ell(w)=\infty
.

Let
w
be a string and let
\gamma\geq 0
.
Suppose
k_{\gamma}
be a positive
\gamma
-eigenfunction for
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}
, that is,
k_{\gamma}(x)>0\ (x>0)
and

u=k_{\gamma}
is the unique solution of the following equation:

\displaystyle u(x)=a+b(x-c)+\gamma\int_{c}^{x}dy\int_{c-}^{y}u(z)dw(z)\quad(x>0)

(6.30)

for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
and
c>0
.
Here we note that for
\gamma\geq 0
the function

\displaystyle g_{\gamma}(x):=\psi_{\gamma}(x)\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{dy}{\psi_{\gamma}(y)^{2}}\quad(x\geq 0)

(6.31)

is a unique non-increasing solution of (
6.30
) satisfying

\displaystyle u(0)=1,\quad\lim_{x\to\infty}\frac{d^{+}}{dx}u(x)=0

(6.32)

(see e.g., Itô
[
3
]
for details).

The
h
-transform
\mathcal{L}^{[k_{\gamma}]}
of
\mathcal{L}=\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d}{dx}
by
k_{\gamma}
is defined by

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{[k_{\gamma}]}:=\frac{1}{k_{\gamma}}\left(\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d}{dx}-\gamma\right)k_{\gamma},

(6.33)

and we can easily check that

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}^{[k_{\gamma}]}=\frac{d}{dw^{[k_{\gamma}]}}\frac{d}{ds^{[k_{\gamma}]}}

(6.34)

for

\displaystyle dw^{[k_{\gamma}]}=k_{\gamma}^{2}dw,\quad ds^{[k_{\gamma}]}=k_{\gamma}^{-2}dx.

(6.35)

Note that when
k_{\gamma}(0)>0
, the boundary classification for the boundary
0
is not changed by the transform. As for the boundary
\infty
, it is entrance or natural depending on

\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty}k_{\gamma}(x)^{-2}dx\int_{x}^{\infty}k_{\gamma}(x)^{2}dw(x)

(6.36)

is finite or not.

Let us consider the case of
k_{\gamma}(0)=1
. When we denote the functions
\psi_{\lambda}
and
g_{\lambda}
for
\mathcal{L}^{[k_{\gamma}]}\ (\gamma\geq 0)
by
\psi_{\lambda}^{[k_{\gamma}]}
and
g_{\lambda}^{[k_{\gamma}]}
,
it holds

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}^{[k_{\gamma}]}=\frac{\psi_{\lambda+\gamma}}{k_{\gamma}},\quad g_{\lambda}^{[k_{\gamma}]}=\frac{g_{\lambda+\gamma}}{k_{\gamma}}\quad(\lambda\in\mathbb{C}).

(6.37)

Moreover, for the spectral measure
\rho_{w^{[k_{\gamma}]}}
of
\mathcal{L}^{[k_{\gamma}]}
is given by

\displaystyle\rho_{w^{[k_{\gamma}]}}(d\lambda)=\rho_{w}(d(\lambda-\gamma))\quad\text{on}\ (\gamma,\infty).

(6.38)

Thus the
h
-transformed transition probability subject to the absorbing boundary at
0
and the first hitting time densities are

\displaystyle P^{[k_{\gamma}]}_{t}(x,dy)=\mathrm{e^{-\gamma t}}\frac{k_{\gamma}(y)}{k_{\gamma}(x)}P_{t}(x,dy)\quad\text{and}\quad f^{[k_{\gamma}]}_{x}(t)=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\gamma t}}{k_{\gamma}(x)}f_{x}(t).

(6.39)

6.4
h
-transform for birth-and-death processes

Let
w
be a string such that
dw
is supported on a discrete countable set
\{a_{i}\}_{i\geq 1}\quad(0<a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots)
;
dw(x)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\pi_{i}\delta_{a_{i}}\quad(\pi_{i}>0)
.
As we have seen in Section
6.2
, the generalized diffusion corresponding to
w
is equivalent to a birth-and-death process.

For
\gamma\geq 0
, let
k_{\gamma}
be a
\gamma
-eigenfunction for
\frac{d}{dw}\frac{d}{dx}
:

\displaystyle k_{\gamma}(x)=a+bx+\gamma\int_{0}^{x}dy\int_{0}^{y}k_{\gamma}(z)dw(z)\quad(x>0)

(6.40)

for some constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
.
Note that since the boundary
0
for birth-and-death processes is always regular, all the
\gamma
-eigenfunction may be represented of the form (
6.40
).

Set
a_{0}:=0
. The function
k_{\gamma}
is linear on each interval
[a_{i},a_{i+1}]\ (i=0,1,2,\cdots)
. Indeed, for
i=0,1,2,\cdots,
and
\delta\in[0,(a_{i+1}-a_{i}))
, since
dw
is supported on
\{a_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
, it holds

\displaystyle k_{\gamma}(a_{i}+\delta)-k_{\gamma}(a_{i})

\displaystyle=b\delta+\gamma\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i}+\delta}dy\int_{0}^{y}k_{\gamma}(z)dw(z)

(6.41)

\displaystyle=b\delta+\gamma\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i}+\delta}dy\int_{0}^{a_{i}}k_{\gamma}(z)dw(z)

(6.42)

\displaystyle=b\delta+\gamma\delta\int_{0}^{a_{i}}k_{\gamma}(z)dw(z)

(6.43)

\displaystyle=\left(b+\gamma\int_{0}^{a_{i}}k_{\gamma}(z)dw(z)\right)\delta.

(6.44)

Suppose
k_{\gamma}(x)>0
for all
x\geq 0
.
For the scale function
s^{[k_{\gamma}]}
defined by (
6.35
), we have

\displaystyle s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i+1})-s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i})

\displaystyle=\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}k_{\gamma}(x)^{-2}dx

(6.45)

\displaystyle=\int_{a_{i}}^{a_{i+1}}\frac{(a_{i+1}-a_{i})^{2}}{(x(k_{\gamma}(a_{i+1})-k_{\gamma}(a_{i}))+a_{i+1}k_{\gamma}(a_{i})-a_{i}k_{\gamma}(a_{i+1}))^{2}}dx

(6.46)

\displaystyle=\frac{a_{i+1}-a_{i}}{k_{\gamma}(a_{i})k_{\gamma}(a_{i+1})}.

(6.47)

For the birth-and-death process corresponding to the
h
-transformed generalized diffusion, we show the birth and death rates
\{\lambda_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]}\}
and
\{\mu^{[k_{\gamma}]}_{i}\}
.
By the same argument in Section
6.2
, we see that

\displaystyle\frac{\lambda_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]}}{\mu_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]}}=\frac{s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i})-s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i-1})}{s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i+1})-s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i})}=\frac{a_{i}-a_{i-1}}{a_{i+1}-a_{i}}\cdot\frac{k_{\gamma}(a_{i+1})}{k_{\gamma}(a_{i-1})}

(6.48)

and

\displaystyle(\lambda_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]}+\mu_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]})^{-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i})-s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i-1}))(s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i+1})-s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i}))}{s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i+1})-s^{[k_{\gamma}]}(a_{i-1})}k_{\gamma}(a_{i})^{2}\pi_{i}.

(6.49)

Solving this, we obtain

\displaystyle\lambda_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]}=\frac{1}{\pi_{i}(a_{i+1}-a_{i})}\cdot\frac{k_{\gamma}(a_{i+1})}{k_{\gamma}(a_{i})}\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{i}^{[k_{\gamma}]}=\frac{1}{\pi_{i}(a_{i}-a_{i-1})}\cdot\frac{k_{\gamma}(a_{i-1})}{k_{\gamma}(a_{i})}.

(6.50)

We will apply this formula in Section
7
.

We can see how the matrix
C
is changed under an
h
-transform.
Using (
6.37
), we can compute the
\theta
-eigenfunction
\psi_{\theta}^{[k_{\gamma}]}
as

\displaystyle\psi^{[k_{\gamma}]}_{\theta}(i)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi_{\theta+\gamma}(i)}{k_{\gamma}(i)}

(6.51)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\gamma}(i)}\sum_{l=1}^{i}C(i,l)(\theta+\gamma)^{l-1}

(6.52)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\gamma}(i)}\sum_{l=1}^{i}C(i,l)\sum_{j=1}^{l}\binom{l-1}{j-1}\theta^{j-1}\gamma^{l-j}

(6.53)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{\gamma}(i)}\sum_{j=1}^{i}\theta^{j-1}\sum_{l=j}^{i}\binom{l-1}{j-1}C(i,l)\gamma^{l-j}.

(6.54)

Thus we now determine the matrix
C
corresponding to the
h
-transformed birth-and-death process, which we denote by
C^{[k_{\gamma}]}
:

\displaystyle C^{[k_{\gamma}]}(i,j)=\frac{1}{k_{\gamma}(i)}\sum_{l=j}^{i}\binom{l-1}{j-1}C(i,l)\gamma^{l-j}.

(6.55)

7
Examples

Here we give examples of birth-and-death processes and compute the corresponding matrices
C
.

7.1
Symmetric random walk

Let us consider the case
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}=\kappa>0
for every
i\geq 1
.
In this case,

\displaystyle\pi_{i}=1\quad(i\geq 1),\quad s(i)=i/\kappa\quad(i\geq 0)

(7.1)

and

\displaystyle Qf(i)=\kappa(f(i+1)-2f(i)+f(i-1))\quad(i\geq 1).

(7.2)

Solving the recurrence relation
Qf=\theta f
for
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
, we have the following linearly independent solutions
k_{\pm}
:

\displaystyle k_{\pm}(i):=\alpha^{\kappa}_{\pm}(\theta)^{i}\quad\text{for}\quad\alpha^{\kappa}_{\pm}(\theta)=\left(1+\frac{\theta}{2\kappa}\right)\pm\sqrt{\left(1+\frac{\theta}{2\kappa}\right)^{2}-1}\quad(i\geq 0),

(7.3)

where we note that
\alpha^{\kappa}_{\pm}(\theta)
are the solutions of the quadratic equation:
\kappa(\alpha^{2}-2\alpha+1)=\theta\alpha
.
We note that
\alpha^{\kappa}_{+}(\theta)\alpha^{\kappa}_{-}(\theta)=1
.
Thus, it follows

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta}(i)=\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\theta}{2\sqrt{\theta^{2}+4\kappa\theta}}\right)\alpha^{\kappa}_{+}(\theta)^{i}+\left(\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\theta}{2\sqrt{\theta^{2}+4\kappa\theta}}\right)\alpha^{\kappa}_{-}(\theta)^{i}

s

By the Stieltjes inversion, we have

\displaystyle\rho_{w}(d\theta)=\frac{\sqrt{\theta(4\kappa-\theta)}}{2\pi}1\{0<\theta<4\kappa\}d\theta.

(7.8)

For
\theta\in(0,4\kappa)

\displaystyle\psi_{-\theta}(i)=\frac{\sin(\beta(\theta)i)}{\kappa\sin(\beta(\theta))}=\frac{1}{\kappa}U_{i-1}(\cos(\beta(\theta))),

[

When we write

\displaystyle U_{k}(\theta)=\sum_{l=0}^{k}u(k,l)\theta^{l},

(7.11)

it holds

\displaystyle\psi_{\theta}(i)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}U_{i-1}\left(1+\frac{\theta}{2\kappa}\right)

(7.12)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{l=1}^{i}u(i-1,l-1)(1+\theta/2\kappa)^{l-1}

(7.13)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\kappa}\sum_{l=1}^{i}u(i-1,l-1)\sum_{j=1}^{l}\binom{l-1}{j-1}\left(\frac{\theta}{2\kappa}\right)^{j-1}

(7.14)

(7.15)

Thus it follows

\displaystyle C(i,j)=\frac{1}{2^{j-1}\kappa^{j}}\sum_{l=j}^{i}\binom{l-1}{j-1}u(i-1,l-1).

(7.16)

\displaystyle u(i,j)=\begin{cases}(-1)^{n-k}\binom{n+k}{n-k}2^{2k}&(i=2n,\ j=2k,\ 0\leq k\leq n),\\

Appendix A
Reproduction of initial distributions for one-dimensional diffusions

A.1
Initial distributions with square-integrable densities

Let
X
be a
\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{ds}
-diffusion on
[0,b)
stopped at
0
.
We assume the boundary
0
is accessible, that is, regular or exit.
Define the function
u=\psi_{\lambda}
as the unique solution of the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{ds}u=\lambda u,\quad u(0)=0,\quad\frac{d}{ds}u(0)=1\quad(0<x<b,\lambda\in\mathbb{R}).

(A.1)

From the spectral theory (see e.g., Coddington and Levinson
[
2
]
), it is known that there exists a measure
\sigma
on
[0,\infty)
and for every function
f:(0,b)\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{b}f(x)^{2}dm(x)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad f(x)=0\quad(x\geq R)\quad\text{for some }R\in(0,b),

(A.2)

it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{b}f(x)^{2}dm(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\widehat{f}(\lambda)^{2}\sigma(d\lambda),

(A.3)

where
\widehat{f}
is the generalized Fourier transform:

\displaystyle\widehat{f}(\lambda):=\int_{0}^{b}f(x)\psi_{-\lambda}(x)dm(x).

(A.4)

We call
\sigma
the spectral measure of the
\frac{d}{dm}\frac{d}{ds}
.
The transform has its inverse. For a function
g:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}g(\lambda)^{2}\sigma(d\lambda)<\infty\quad\text{and}\quad g(\lambda)=0\quad(\lambda\geq R)\quad\text{for some }R>0,

(A.5)

we define

\displaystyle\check{g}(x):=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(\lambda)\psi_{-\lambda}(x)\sigma(d\lambda).

(A.6)

Then it holds

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{b}\check{g}(x)^{2}dm(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}g(\lambda)^{2}\sigma(d\lambda).

(A.7)

From (
A.3
), these transforms are naturally extended to the unitary transforms between
L^{2}(dm)
and
L^{2}(\sigma)
. We denote the extended ones by the same symbol
\widehat{\ }
and
\check{\ }
.

In this section, we assume the existence of the Laplace transform of the spectral measure:

\displaystyle\mathrm{(S)}\quad\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\rho(d\lambda)<\infty\quad(t>0).

(A.8)

A sufficient condition for
\mathrm{(S)}
is the following:

Proposition A.1

(
[
14
]
)
.

Suppose
|s(0)|<\infty
and

\displaystyle m(x,c]\leq C(s(x)-s(0))^{-\delta}\quad(0<x<c)

(A.9)

for some
C>0
,
0<c<b
and
0<\delta<1
. Then the condition
\mathrm{(S)}
holds.

Under the condition
\mathrm{(S)}
, we may have the spectral representation of the first hitting time densities.

Proposition A.2

(
[
15
, Proposition 2.1]
)
.

Assume
\mathrm{(S)}
holds.
Then for any
0<x<b
the distribution of
T_{0}
under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
has a density
f_{x}(t)
on
(0,\infty)
w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure, that is, the following hold:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}[T_{0}\in dt]=f_{x}(t)dt\quad(0<x<b,\ t>0).

(A.10)

The first hitting time densities have a spectral representation:

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\psi_{-\lambda}(x)\rho(d\lambda)\quad(0<x<b,\ t>0).

(A.11)

and have another representation:

\displaystyle f_{x}(t)=\left.\frac{d}{ds(y)}p(t,x,y)\right|_{y=0}\quad(0<x<b,\ t>0).

(A.12)

Let

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{2}:=\{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(0,b)\mid\nu=\eta dm\quad\text{for some }\eta\in L^{2}(dm)\}.

(A.13)

Applying the spectral representation, we can reproduce the initial distribution on
\mathcal{P}_{2}
.
Before we state the theorem, we recall the relation of the Laplace transform of the signed measures and the completely monotone functions.
Let
\mathcal{M}
be a set of signed measures on
(0,\infty)
with the Laplace transforms, that is, for every
\mu\in\mathcal{M}
it holds

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\mu(t):=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}|\mu|(d\lambda)<\infty\quad(t>0),

(A.14)

where
|\mu|
denotes the total variation measure of
\mu
.
We say that a function
f:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a completely monotone when

(-1)^{k}f^{(k)}(t)\geq 0\ (k\geq 0,t>0)
.
We denote by
\mathrm{CM}
the set of the difference of two completely monotone functions:

\displaystyle\mathrm{CM}:=\{f-g\mid f,g:\ \text{completely monotone}\}.

(A.15)

By the classical Bernstein theorem (see e.g.,
[
11
, Theorem 1.4]
), the Laplace transform
\mathcal{L}
is a bijection from
\mathcal{M}
to
\mathrm{CM}
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}^{-1}:\mathrm{CM}\to\mathcal{M}
the inverse transform of
\mathcal{L}
.

Theorem A.3
.

Assume the condition
\mathrm{(S)}
holds.
Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
. Then
f_{\nu}\in\mathrm{CM}
and it holds

\displaystyle\frac{d\nu}{dm}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi_{-\lambda}(x)(\mathcal{L}^{-1}f_{\nu})(d\lambda)\quad m\text{-a.e.}

(A.16)

Therefore we can reproduce the initial distribution.

Proof.

Let
\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{2}
with
\nu=\eta dm
for
\eta\in L^{2}(dm)
.
Set
\mathbf{e}_{t}(\lambda):=\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\ (t>0,\lambda>0)
.
Note that by
\mathrm{(S)}
, the function
\mathbf{e}_{t}(\lambda)\in L^{2}(\rho)
.
Then from Proposition
A.2
and
\mathrm{(S)}
, it holds
f_{x}(t)=\check{\mathbf{e}_{t}}(x)
.
Since the generalized Fourier transform is unitary, the first hitting time density
f_{\nu}
under
\nu
satisfies

\displaystyle f_{\nu}(t):

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{b}f_{x}(t)\nu(dx)

(A.17)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{b}\check{\mathbf{e}_{t}}(x)\eta(x)dm(x)

(A.18)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda t}\widehat{\eta}(\lambda)\rho(d\lambda).

(A.19)

Thus, it holds

\displaystyle(\mathcal{L}^{-1}f_{\nu})(d\lambda)=\widehat{\eta}(\lambda)\sigma(d\lambda).

(A.20)

By taking the inverse of the generalized Fourier transform, we obtain (
A.16
).
∎
