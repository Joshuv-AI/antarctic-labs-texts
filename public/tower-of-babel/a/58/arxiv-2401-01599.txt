Generalization Error Curves for Analytic Spectral Algorithms under Power-law Decay
Yicheng Li, Weiye Gan, Zuoqiang Shi, Qian Lin

Abstract
The generalization error curve of certain kernel regression method aims at determining the exact order of generalization error with various source condition, noise level and choice of the regularization parameter rather than the minimax rate. In this work, under mild assumptions, we rigorously provide a full characterization of the generalization error curves of the kernel gradient descent method (and a large class of analytic spectral algorithms) in kernel regression. Consequently, we could sharpen the near inconsistency of kernel interpolation and clarify the saturation effects of kernel regression algorithms with higher qualification, etc. Thanks to the neural tangent kernel theory, these results greatly improve our understanding of the generalization behavior of training the wide neural networks. A novel technical contribution, the analytic functional argument, might be of independent interest.

Introduction

The neural tangent kernel (NTK)
theory
(
Jacot et al. 2018
)
, which shows
that the gradient kernel regression approximates the over-parametrized neural network trained by gradient descent well

(
Jacot et al. 2018
;
Allen-Zhu et al. 2019
;
Lee et al. 2019
)
,
brings us a natural surrogate to understand the generalization behavior of the neural networks in certain circumstances.
This surrogate has led to recent renaissance of the study of kernel methods.
For example, one would ask whether overfitting could harm the generalization
(
Bartlett et al. 2020
)
,
how the smoothness of the underlying regression function would affect the generalization error
(
Li et al. 2023
)
,
or if one can determine the lower bound of the generalization error at a specific function?
All these problems can be answered by the
generalization error curve

which aims at determining the exact generalization error of a certain kernel regression method with respect to
the kernel, the regression function, the noise level and the choice of the regularization parameter.
It is clear that such a generalization error curve would provide a comprehensive picture of the generalization ability of the corresponding kernel regression method

(
Bordelon et al. 2020
;
Cui et al. 2021
;
Li et al. 2023
)
.

Although there have been extensive works on the generalization errors of kernel regression,
most of them focused on the optimal rate of convergence under the minimax framework.
For example,
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
showed with a proper choice of the regularization parameter,
kernel ridge regression (KRR) can achieve the minimax optimal rate of convergence.
Being a special case, KRR falls into a large class of kernel methods often referred to as
spectral algorithms

(
Rosasco et al. 2005
;
Gerfo et al. 2008
)
.
For general spectral algorithms, subsequent works (e.g.,
Blanchard and Mücke 2018
;
Lin et al. 2018
) proved similar optimality results.
We refer the reader to Subsection
1.1
for more details.
However, these works are not sufficient to answer the aforementioned problems motivated from the studies in neural networks, since they only considered the method-dependent upper bound and the method-independent minimax lower bound of the generalization error.
In addition, most of them focused mainly on the rate of convergence and ignored the constant factors. Therefore, these traditional results are not enough to provide a comprehensive picture of the generalization error of kernel methods.

Going beyond the traditional results, several recent works attempt to describe the generalization error curve of kernel ridge regression (KRR).
With some heuristic arguments,

Bordelon et al. 2020
;
Cui et al. 2021
derived the generalization error curve of KRR under certain restrictive assumptions.
Under mild assumptions,
Li et al. 2023
first rigorously characterized the generalization error curve of KRR in terms of asymptotic convergence rate.
They show an exact bias-variance trade-off U-shaped curve for the generalization error of KRR with respect to the choice of regularization parameter.
Since the neural networks are often trained by the gradient descent,
it is of great interest to further study the generalization error curves of the gradient descent kernel regression.
To the best of our knowledge, few attention is made on this aspect.

In this paper, we study the generalization error curves of a large class of analytic spectral algorithms, including the kernel gradient method.
To be precise, let
\rho
be a probability distribution on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
and
f^{*}
be the unknown regression function
(
Andreas Christmann 2008
)
,
namely the conditional expectation
f^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(y|x)
.
Given
n
i.i.d. samples
\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, let
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
be the estimator given by a spectral algorithm with regularization parameter
\lambda=\lambda(n)>0
.
Then, our result shows that for
\lambda
in a reasonable range,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}~\Big|~X\right]=(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))\left(\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathcal{N}_{2,\varphi}(\lambda)\right),

where the conditional expectation is taken with respect to the training sample
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
,

\sigma^{2}
is the variance of the noise,

\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})
and
\mathcal{N}_{2,\varphi}(\lambda)
are two deterministic quantities (see (
28
) and (
29
)) corresponding to
the bias and the variance respectively, and
\varphi
is the filter function defining the spectral algorithm (see (
19
)).
Moreover, if
\lambda
does not lie in the reasonable range, we also show that the generalization error is lower bounded by nearly a constant.
The assumptions made in this paper are also mild and are satisfied for many RKHSs and spectral algorithms.
We refer to Section
3
for a complete statement for our main result.

With the exact
1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
form, our result characterizes
exactly and completely
the generalization error for a large class of analytic spectral algorithms.
In particular, it shows a solid U-shaped bias-variance trade-off curve that the bias decreases while the variance increases as the regularization strength
\lambda
decreases,
where the optimal point corresponds to the minimax optimal rate of convergence.
The result also shows that when the regularization is too weak, the overfitting method can not generalize, emphasizing the necessity of regularization.
Moreover, our result also expose a high-order saturation effect of some specific spectral algorithms.
Our result would greatly improve the understanding of the generalization behavior of spectral algorithms and the wide neural networks.

Last but not the least, the novel application of the “analytic functional argument” in deriving sharp estimations for spectral algorithms might be of independent interest and worth of further investigations.

1.1
Related works

Optimality of kernel methods

There are a large number of works studying the optimal rates of kernel ridge regression and also spectral algorithms.
The classical work
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)
proved the minimax optimality of KRR when the regression lies in the RKHS;
subsequent works
(
Steinwart et al. 2009
;
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)

further extend the result to the misspecified cases when the regression function does not lie in the RKHS.

Zhang and Yu 2005
and
Yao et al. 2007
considered the kernel gradient method and proved consistency and fast rates of convergence respectively.
The general spectral algorithms are first introduced
Bauer et al. 2007

and then studied extensive in the follow-up works
(
Gerfo et al. 2008
;
Rosasco et al. 2005
)
,
but the eigenvalue decay (see Assumption
1
) of the kernel was not considered so the rates are not optimal.
When certain restrictive conditions,
Caponnetto 2006
proved the minimax optimality of the spectral algorithms.
More recently, a sequence of works further extend the result to more general cases (e.g.,
Blanchard and Mücke 2018
;
Lin et al. 2018
).
In addition, the very recent work
(
Zhang et al. 2023
)
showed the optimality for the misspecified cases even when the regression function is unbounded.
We also refer to Table 1 in
Zhang et al. 2023
for a summary of the results.
However, as discussed above, these results only focused on the upper bounds and are not enough to provide the exact generalization error curve.

Recent advances in kernel ridge regression

Focusing particularly on KRR, a recent line of work provide further results on its generalization.
Some works
(
Rakhlin and Zhai 2018
;
Buchholz 2022
;
Beaglehole et al. 2022
;
Li et al. 2023
)

studied kernel ridgeless regression, which is the limiting case of KRR as the regularization goes to zero, and proved that it can not generalize.
Using a restrictive Gaussian design assumption and also some non-rigorous arguments,

Bordelon et al. 2020
;
Cui et al. 2021
derived the generalization error curve of KRR
and
Mallinar et al. 2022
studied further the interpolation regime.
For rigorous results,
Li et al. 2023
proved the generalization error curve with asymptotic rates in the form of
\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-r})
,
but the hidden constant factors are not tracked.

Kernel regression in the high-dimensional limit

There is also a line of works studying the generalization of kernel regression in the high-dimensional limit when the dimension of the input space
d
diverges with
n
.
For example, in the high-dimensional setting,
Liang and Rakhlin 2020
showed that kernel interpolation can generalize;

Ghorbani et al. 2020
;
Ghosh et al. 2021
;
Liu et al. 2021
;
Lu et al. 2023
studied the generalization of kernel ridge regression and kernel gradient method.
However, we would like to emphasize that their results could be substantially different from ours since the setting is different.
Moreover, the high dimensionality in their setting actually makes the problem easier since the kernel can now be linearizing and
the well-established random matrix theory can be applied, which is not the case in our setting.

2.1
Reproducing kernel Hilbert space

Let a compact metric space
\mathcal{X}
be the space of input and
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq\mathbb{R}
be the space of output.
Let
\rho
be the unknown probability measure supported on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
and
\mu
be the marginal probability measure of
\rho
on
\mathcal{X}
.
Denote by
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)
the space of (complex-valued) square integrable functions on
\mathcal{X}
.
Assume that
\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\rho}(y^{2})<\infty
, let the conditional expectation

\displaystyle f^{*}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\rho}(y\mid x)=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}y\differential\rho(y|x)\in L^{2}

(1)

be the
regression function
.
We fix a continuous positive definite

1

We consider complex-valued spaces here since the analytic functional argument later will be based on the complex analysis.
Now,
k
is conjugate symmetric
k(x,y)=\overline{k(y,x)}
and positive definite in the sense that

\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}\overline{c_{i}}c_{j}k(x_{i},x_{j})\geq 0
for any
n\geq 1
,
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\in\mathcal{X}

kernel
k:\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{C}
over
\mathcal{X}

and let
\mathcal{H}
be the (complex) separable reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) associated with
k
.
Note that we adopt the convention that inner product is linear in the second component and conjugate linear in the first component,
that is,
\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\overline{f(x)}g(x)\differential x
and
\left\langle{k(x,\cdot),f}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x)
for
f\in\mathcal{H}
.
Since
\mathcal{X}
is compact and
k
is continuous, we have
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}k(x,x)\leq\kappa^{2}<\infty
.
Consequently, we have the natural inclusion
S_{k}:\mathcal{H}\to L^{2}
which is Hilbert-Schmidt
(
Andreas Christmann 2008
;
Steinwart and Scovel 2012
)
.
Denote by
S_{k}^{*}:L^{2}\to\mathcal{H}
the adjoint operator of
S_{k}
.
Then,
T=S_{k}S_{k}^{*}:L^{2}\to L^{2}
defines a integral operator

\displaystyle(Tf)(x)=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\overline{k(x,x^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime})\differential\mu(x^{\prime}).

(2)

Moreover, it is well known
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Steinwart and Scovel 2012
)
that
T
is a self-adjoint, positive and trace class operator that the trace norm
\norm{T}_{1}\leq\kappa^{2}
.
Focusing only on the infinite dimensional case that
T
is not of finite rank, we have the spectral decomposition

\displaystyle T=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}P_{V_{m}},

(3)

where
(\mu_{m})_{m\geq 1}
is the decreasing sequence of the
distinct
positive eigenvalues of
T
and
P_{V_{m}}
is the projection onto the eigenspace
V_{m}
associated with
\mu_{m}
.
Denote by
d_{m}=\dim V_{m}
the multiplicity of
\mu_{m}
.
Let us further choose an orthonormal basis
\{e_{m,l}\}_{l=1}^{d_{m}}
for each
V_{m}
, where each
e_{m,l}
is the continuous representative among the
\mu
-equivalent class.
Then,
\{e_{m,l}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\overline{\Ran S_{k}}=(\Ker S_{k}^{*})^{\perp}\subseteq L^{2}
and

\{\mu_{m}^{1/2}e_{m,l}\}
forms an orthonormal basis of
\overline{\Ran S_{k}^{*}}=(\Ker S_{k})^{\perp}=\mathcal{H}
,
where we notice that
S_{k}
is injective since the support of
\mu
is
\mathcal{X}
.
Finally, the Mercer’s theorem
(
Andreas Christmann 2008
;
Steinwart and Scovel 2012
)
yields that

\displaystyle k(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\overline{e_{m,l}(x)}e_{m,l}(x^{\prime}),

(4)

where the summation converges absolutely and uniformly.
To be in line with the previous literature, we denote by
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
the eigenvalues of
T
counting multiplicities
and, with a little abuse of notation, denote by
e_{j}
the corresponding eigenfunction.
Then, we introduce the following assumption on the eigenvalues of
T
, which is commonly considered in the previous literature

(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
)
.

Assumption 1

(Eigenvalue decay)
.

There are some
\beta>1
and constants
c_{\mathrm{eig}},C_{\mathrm{eig}}>0
such that

\displaystyle c_{\mathrm{eig}}j^{-\beta}\leq\lambda_{j}\leq C_{\mathrm{eig}}j^{-\beta},\quad j\geq 1,

(5)

or equivalently,

\displaystyle\#\{i:\lambda_{i}\geq\lambda\}=\sum_{m:\mu_{m}\geq\lambda}d_{m}=\Theta(\lambda^{-1/\beta}),\quad\mbox{as}\quad\lambda\to 0.

(6)

where
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
are the eigenvalues (counting multiplicities) of the integral operator
T

and
\{\mu_{m}\}_{m\geq 1}
are the distinct ones defined in (
3
).

Remark 2.1
.

The equivalence of (
5
) and (
6
) is elementary and a proof can be found in Proposition
A.1
.
(
6
) allows us to deal with some less explicit cases.
For example, if we have
\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{k}\asymp m^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\geq 1
,
then
\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{-\beta}
is just equivalent to
\mu_{m}\asymp m^{-\gamma\beta}
.

The eigenvalue decay rate in Assumption
1
is a common assumption in the previous literature
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
;
Li et al. 2023
)
.
As we aim to derive the exact generalization error, the lower bound part in (
5
) is also necessary.
Assumption
1
is satisfied by many commonly used kernels, such as the Laplacian kernel, Matérn kernels and neural tangent kernels etc.
This assumption on the eigenvalues characterizes the smoothness of the functions in the RKHS and larger
\beta
implies better smoothness.
We also remark that since
T
is trace-class,
(\lambda_{j})_{j\geq 1}
is summable so the requirement
\beta>1
is necessary.
Assumption
1
is also closely connected to the effective dimension or capacity condition of the RKHS in the previous literature
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)
.
Later, with a spectral algorithm
\varphi
, we will introduce generalized
\varphi
-effective dimension that characterizes the variance of the method,
see (
29
).

Interpolation spaces

We need to further introduce the interpolation spaces
(
Steinwart and Scovel 2012
)
to state our results.
For
p\geq 0
, we define the fractional power
T^{p}:L^{2}\to L^{2}
by

\displaystyle T^{p}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}^{p}P_{V_{m}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}^{p}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\left\langle{e_{m,l},\cdot}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}e_{m,l}.

(7)

Then, we can introduce the interpolation space
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
by

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}]^{s}=\Ran T^{s/2}=\left\{f=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}^{s/2}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}a_{m,l}e_{m,l}~\Big|~\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\absolutevalue{a_{m,l}}^{2}<\infty\right\}\subseteq L^{2}.

(8)

For
f,g\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
with coefficients
(a_{m,l})
and
(b_{m,l})
respectively, we define the inner product in
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
by

\displaystyle\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{[\mathcal{H}]^{s}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\overline{a_{m,l}}b_{m,l}=\left\langle{T^{-\frac{s}{2}}f,T^{-\frac{s}{2}}g}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}.

(9)

Then, it is easy to see that
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
is a separable Hilbert space with an orthogonal basis
\{\mu_{m}^{s/2}e_{m,l}:~m\geq 1,~1\leq l\leq d_{m}\}
.
In particular, we have
[\mathcal{H}]^{0}\subseteq L^{2}
and also
[\mathcal{H}]^{1}=\mathcal{H}
.
We also have natural inclusions
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}\subseteq[\mathcal{H}]^{t}
for
s\geq t
and the inclusion is compact if
s>t
.
Moreover, the restriction of
T
(and also
T^{p}
) on
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
is also a bounded operator with the same spectra,
so we will still denote it by
T
(and also
T^{p}
) for simplicity.

Regular RKHS

To derive the tightest possible learning rate, we need to characterize the regularity of the functions in the RKHS to the greatest extent.
Since
V_{m}
is a finite dimensional space of
L^{2}
and also
\mathcal{H}
,
it is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space with respect to
\left\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}
and its reproducing kernel
k_{m}
is determined uniquely by

\displaystyle\left\langle{k_{m}(x,\cdot),f}\right\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{\mathcal{X}}\overline{k_{m}(x,x^{\prime})}f(x^{\prime})\differential\mu(x^{\prime})=f(x).

(10)

Choosing an orthonormal basis
\{e_{m,l}\}_{l=1}^{d_{m}}
, we have explicitly

\displaystyle k_{m}(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\overline{e_{m,l}(x)}e_{m,l}(x^{\prime}),

(11)

which is invariant of the choice of the basis.
It is also easy to see that

\displaystyle k(x,x^{\prime})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\mu_{m}k_{m}(x,x^{\prime}).

In this paper, we introduce the following condition of regular RKHS:

Assumption 2

(Regular RKHS)
.

There is some constant
M>0
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}k_{m}(x,x)=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\absolutevalue{e_{m,l}(x)}^{2}\leq M\sum_{m=1}^{N}d_{m},\quad\forall N\geq 1,

(12)

In this case, we call this RKHS (together with the underlying distribution
\mu
) is regular.

It is obvious that if the eigenfunctions are uniformly bounded, that is,
\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\absolutevalue{e_{m,l}(x)}^{2}\leq M
, then the RKHS is regular.
Moreover, there are some more cases that the RKHS is regular,
so we believe that it is a rather general assumption.

Example 2.1

(Shift-invariant periodic kernels)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{T}^{d}=[-\pi,\pi)^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional torus and
\mu
be the uniform measure on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Consider a shift-invariant kernel satisfying
k(x,y)=h(x-y)
,
where
h
is a function defined on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
.
Then, it is easy to show that the Fourier basis
\{\phi_{\bm{m}}=e^{i\left\langle{\bm{m},x}\right\rangle},~m\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\}

gives an orthonormal set of eigenfunctions of
T
.
Consequently, it is regular since the basis is uniformly bounded.
Moreover, if the corresponding eigenvalues satisfy
\lambda_{\bm{m}}\asymp(1+\norm{\bm{m}}_{2}^{2})^{-\alpha}
,
then the corresponding RKHS is
\mathcal{H}\cong H^{\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
, the Sobolev space on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
and also
[\mathcal{H}]^{s}\cong H^{s\alpha}(\mathbb{T}^{d})
.

Example 2.2

(Dot-product kernel on the sphere)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{S}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional sphere and
\mu
be the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
Consider a dot-product kernel satisfying
k(x,y)=h(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle)
, where
h
is a function on
[-1,1]
.
Then, the Funk-Hecke formula
(
Dai and Xu 2013
)

shows that the spherical harmonics
\{Y_{m,l}~m\geq 1,~1\leq l\leq d_{m}\}
consists of an orthonormal set of eigenfunctions of
T
,
where
\{Y_{m,l}~1\leq l\leq d_{m}\}
are order-
m
homogeneous harmonic polynomials and
d_{m}=\binom{m+d}{m}-\binom{m-2+d}{m-2}\asymp m^{d-1}
.
Using the theory of spherical harmonics, we can show that this RKHS is regular, see Subsection
B.1.1
.

Example 2.3

(Dot-product kernel on the ball)
.

Now, let us consider the
d
-dimensional unit ball
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{B}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\norm{x}\leq 1\}

with
\mu
be proportional to the classical weight
p(x)=(1-\norm{x}^{2})^{-1/2}
.
Consider still a dot-product kernel
k
.
Then, an analog of the Funk-Hecke formula on the ball
Dai and Xu 2013
shows that the space
V_{m}^{d}
of orthogonal polynomials of degree exactly
m

is an eigenspace associated with the same eigenvalue of
T

and
\dim V_{m}^{d}=\binom{m+d-1}{m}\asymp m^{d-1}
.
Similar to the spherical case, we can show that this RKHS is regular, see Subsection
B.1.2
.

In previous literature
(
Steinwart et al. 2009
;
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
,
the following
L^{\infty}
-embedding property is introduced to characterize the regularity of the RKHS.
We say
\mathcal{H}
has an embedding property of order
\alpha\in(0,1]

if
[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}
can be continuously embedded into
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu)
, that is,
the operator norm

M_{\alpha}\coloneqq\norm{[\mathcal{H}]^\alpha\hookrightarrow L^{\infty}(\mathcal{X},\mu)}<\infty.

(13)

Then, we define the embedding index by
\alpha_{0}=\inf\left\{\alpha:M_{\alpha}<\infty\right\}
.
It is obvious that
\alpha\leq 1
because

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\absolutevalue{f(x)}=\sup_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\langle{k(x,\cdot),f}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\norm{k(x,\cdot)}_{\mathcal{H}}\norm{f}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\kappa\norm{f}_{\mathcal{H}},

and it is also shown in
Fischer and Steinwart 2020
that
\alpha_{0}\geq\beta
.
To obtain sharp concentrations, previous works
(
Zhang et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2023
)
assume that
\alpha_{0}=\beta
.
Here we show that this embedding index condition is satisfied by the regular RKHS.

Proposition 2.2
.

Under Assumption
1
and Assumption
2
, the embedding index is
\alpha_{0}=1/\beta
.

Moreover, we remark that the embedding index condition only makes sense for the eigenvalues with power-law decay,
while the regular RKHS condition can also be considered for more general decays.
In fact, the regular RKHS condition essentially considers the eigenfunctions rather than the eigenvalues.

2.2
Spectral algorithm

Let
Z=\{(x_{i},y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}\subseteq\mathcal{X}\times\mathcal{Y}
be a set of training sample drawn i.i.d. from
\rho
.
We also denote by
X=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
the collection of sample inputs.
To introduce the spectral algorithm, we first introduce some auxiliary notations.
Denote by
k_{x}=k(x,\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}
.
Let
K_{x}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathcal{H}
be given by
K_{x}y=yk_{x}
, whose adjoint
K_{x}^{*}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}
is given by
K_{x}^{*}f=\left\langle{k_{x},f}\right\rangle_{\mathcal{H}}=f(x)
.
Moreover, we denote by
T_{x}=K_{x}K_{x}^{*}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
and

\displaystyle T_{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}T_{x_{i}}.

(14)

Here we notice that since
k(x,x)\leq\kappa^{2}
, we have
\norm{T_X}\leq\kappa^{2}
.
We also define the sample basis function

\displaystyle\hat{g}_{Z}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}K_{x_{i}}y_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}y_{i}k(x_{i},\cdot)\in\mathcal{H}.

(15)

Then, a spectral algorithm is obtained by applying to
\hat{g}_{Z}
a “regularized inverse” of
T_{X}
via a filter function
(
Bauer et al. 2007
)
.
We introduce first the following definition of filter functions.

Definition 2.3

(Filter functions)
.

Let
\left\{\varphi_{\lambda}:[0,\kappa^{2}]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}\mid\lambda\in(0,1)\right\}
be a family of functions
indexed with regularization parameter
\lambda
and define the remainder function

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}(z)\coloneqq 1-z\varphi_{\lambda}(z).

(16)

We say that
\left\{\varphi_{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in(0,1)\right\}
(or simply
\varphi_{\lambda}(z)
) is a filter function if:

(i)

For any fixed
\lambda
,
\psi_{\lambda}(z)\geq 0
is decreasing with respect to
z\in[0,\kappa^{2}]
.
For any fixed
z
,
\psi_{\lambda}(z)
decreases as
\lambda
decreases.

(ii)

There is some constant
E
such that

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}(z+\lambda)\varphi_{\lambda}(z)\leq E,\quad\forall\lambda\in(0,1).

(17)

(iii)

The
qualification
of this filter function is
\tau_{\max}\in[1,\infty]
such that
\forall 0\leq\tau\leq\tau_{\max}
(and also
\tau<\infty
),

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in[0,\kappa^{2}]}z^{\tau}\psi_{\lambda}(z)\leq F_{\tau}\lambda^{\tau},\quad\forall\lambda\in(0,1),

(18)

where
F_{\tau}
is a constant depending only on
\tau
.

(iv)

In addition, if
\tau_{\max}
is finite, then there is some constant
\underline{F}
that

\displaystyle\psi_{\lambda}(z)\geq\underline{F}\lambda^{\tau_{\max}},\quad\forall z\in[0,\kappa^{2}],~\lambda\in(0,1).

Now, given a filter function
\varphi_{\lambda}(z)
, a spectral algorithm is defined by

\displaystyle\hat{f}_{\lambda}=\varphi_{\lambda}(T_{X})\hat{g}_{Z}\quad\in\mathcal{H}.

(19)

We postpone concrete examples of spectral algorithms to the end of this subsection.

Remark 2.4
.

We remark that the property (i) and (iv) are not essential for the definition of filter functions in the literature
(
Bauer et al. 2007
;
Gerfo et al. 2008
;
Rosasco et al. 2005
)
,
but we introduce them to avoid some unnecessary technicalities in the proof.
In particular, property (i) says the general scheme of a spectral filter
and property (iv) determines exactly the qualification of this filter function.
We also remark that the regularization parameter
\lambda
will depend on the sample size
n
and
\lambda(n)
goes to zero as
n
tends to infinity,
so the upper bound of
\lambda\in(0,1)
is not essential and can be smaller if necessary.

To evaluate the performance of the spectral algorithm
\hat{f}_{\lambda}
, we consider the generalization error (or excess risk)
(
Andreas Christmann 2008
)
defined by

\displaystyle\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\left(\hat{f}(x)-f^{*}(x)\right)^{2}.

(20)

Moreover, it is more convenient to consider its conditional expectation with respect to
X
, namely
\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}\mid X\right]
,
which is still a random variable depending on
X
.
If the noise
\epsilon=y-f^{*}(x)
is independent of
x
, this conditional expectation is just taking the expectation over the noise.
For our main result, we will derive a precise estimation of this quantity in a high-probability form.

However, to derive the precise generalization error curve, the above definition of filter functions is not sufficient for our purpose.
The key novelty of our techniques is that we develop a special argument based on analytical functional calculus.
To this end, we introduce the following assumption on the analytic filter function.
As far as we know, we are the first to consider such properties of the filter function.

Assumption 3

(Analytic filter function)
.

Let

\displaystyle D_{\lambda}

\displaystyle=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\real z\in[-\lambda/2,\kappa^{2}],~\absolutevalue{\Im z}\leq\real z+\lambda/2\right\}

\displaystyle\quad\cup\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\absolutevalue{z - \kappa^2}\leq\kappa^{2}+\lambda/2,~\real z\geq\kappa^{2}\right\}.

The filter function
\varphi_{\lambda}(z)
can be extended to be an analytic function on some domain containing
D_{\lambda}

and the following conditions holds for all
\lambda\in(0,1)
:

(C1)

\absolutevalue{(z+\lambda)\reg(z)}\leq\tilde{E},~\forall z\in D_{\lambda}
;

(C2)

\absolutevalue{(z+\lambda)\rem(z)}\leq\tilde{F}\lambda,~\forall z\in D_{\lambda}
;

where
\tilde{E},\tilde{F}
are positive constants.

Remark 2.5
.

The domain
D_{\lambda}
is essential and is related to the analytic functional argument in the proof,
see also Figure
2
on page
2
for an illustration.
The two conditions (C1) and (C2) can be seen as the complex extension of
(
17
) and (
18
) respectively,
so one can expect that they also hold for the filter functions.
Indeed, we will show in the following many commonly used filter functions satisfy Assumption
3
.
The proof is postponed to Subsection
B.2
.

Figure 1:
An illustration of the filter functions
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
.

Example 2.4

(Kernel ridge regression)
.

The filter function of kernel ridge regression (KRR) is well known to be

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{KR}}(z)=\frac{1}{z+\lambda},\quad\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{KR}}(z)=\frac{\lambda}{z+\lambda}.

(21)

Both
\varphi_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\lambda}
are analytic on
\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\real z>-\lambda\right\}\supset D_{\lambda}
.
This filter function is of qualification only
\tau_{\max}=1
.

Example 2.5

(Iterated ridge regression)
.

To overcome the limited qualification of KRR,
Rosasco et al. 2005
introduce following iterated ridge (or iterated Tikhonov) method.
Let
p\geq 1
be fixed.
We define

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{IT,p}}(z)=\frac{1}{z}\left[1-\frac{\lambda^{p}}{(z+\lambda)^{p}}\right],\quad\psi^{\mathrm{IT,p}}_{\lambda}(z)=\frac{\lambda^{p}}{(z+\lambda)^{p}}.

(22)

It is easy to show that
z=0
is a removable singular point of
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{IT,p}}(z)
and both

\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{IT,p}}(z)
and
\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{IT,p}}(z)
are analytic on
\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\real z>-\lambda\right\}\supset D_{\lambda}
.
The merit of this filter function is that it has qualification
\tau_{\max}=p
.

To understand the name “iterated”, let us consider the particular case
p\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\varphi^{\mathrm{IT,p}}_{\lambda}(z)=\sum_{r=1}^{p}\lambda^{r-1}(z+\lambda)^{-r},

and the method is obtained by iterating the ridge method
p
times:

\displaystyle(T_{X}+\lambda)h_{i}=\hat{g}_{Z}+\lambda h_{i-1},\quad i=1,\dots,p.

Example 2.6

(Gradient flow)
.

The gradient flow method
(
Yao et al. 2007
)
is another popular regularization method.
Let us consider the empirical loss

\displaystyle L(f)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(f(x_{i})-y_{i})^{2}=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(K_{x_{i}}^{*}f-y_{i})^{2},\quad f\in\mathcal{H}.

Then, with the initial value
f_{0}=0
,
\dot{f}_{t}=-\nabla_{f}L(f_{t})
defines a gradient flow in
\mathcal{H}
.
The gradient flow equation, which can be solved in closed-form, gives the filter function

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)=\frac{1-e^{-tz}}{z},\quad\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)=e^{-tz},\quad t=\lambda^{-1}.

(23)

It is also easy to show that
z=0
is a removable singular point of
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)
,
so both
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)
and
\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)
are analytic on the whole complex plane.
Moreover, elementary inequalities can show that the gradient flow method has qualification
\tau_{\max}=\infty
with diverging
F_{\tau}=(\tau/e)^{\tau}
.

Example 2.7

(Gradient descent)
.

The gradient descent method is the discrete version of gradient flow.
Let
\eta>0
be a fixed step size.
Then, iterating gradient descent with respect to the empirical loss
t
steps yields the filter function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)&=\eta\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}(1-\eta z)^{k}=\frac{1-(1-\eta z)^{t}}{z},\quad\lambda=(\eta t)^{-1},\\
\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)&=(1-\eta z)^{t}.\end{aligned}

(24)

Moreover, when
\eta
is small enough, say
\eta<1/(2\kappa^{2})
, we have
\real(1-\eta z)>0
for
z\in D_{\lambda}
,
so we can take the single-valued branch of
(1-\eta z)^{t}
even when
t
is not an integer.
Therefore, we can extend the definition of the filter function so that
\lambda
can be arbitrary and
t=(\eta\lambda)^{-1}
.
It is also easy to show that
z=0
is a removable singular point of
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)
.
Consequently,
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)
and
\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)
are analytic on
D_{\lambda}
.
Similar to the gradient flow method, the gradient descent method is also of qualification
\tau_{\max}=\infty
with
F_{\tau}=(\tau/e)^{\tau}
.

We denote by
\#A
the cardinality of a set
A
.
We use the big-O notations
O(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot)
,
o(\cdot)
.

O_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
,
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
,
\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
and

.

(\xi_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of non-negative random variables and
(a_{n})_{n\geq 1}
a sequence of positive numbers.
We say
\xi_{n}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
if for any
\delta>0
, there exists
N_{\delta}
and
M_{\delta}

when
n\geq N_{\delta}
,
\mathbb{P}\left\{\absolutevalue{\xi_n}\leq M_{\delta}a_{n}\right\}\geq 1-\delta
.
The notation
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
is defined similarly
and
\xi_{n}=\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
iff
\xi_{n}=O_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
and
\xi_{n}=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
both hold.
Moreover, we say
\xi_{n}=o_{\mathbb{P}}(a_{n})
if
\xi_{n}/a_{n}
converges in probability to 0.
Also, we sometimes write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
if

.

3
Main results

3.1
More assumptions

Before stating our main theorem, we introduce two assumptions.
The first assumption concerns about the noise.
This assumption is quite standard and is satisfied if the noise is independent of the input
x
and has a bounded variance.

Assumption 4

(Noise)
.

We assume

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\rho}\left[\left(y-f^{*}(x)\right)^{2}\;\Big|\;x\right]=\sigma^{2}>0,\quad\mu\text{-a.e. }x\in\mathcal{X},

(25)

The second assumption is about the regression function
f^{*}
.
Recall the definition of interpolation spaces and that
\{e_{m,l}:~m\geq 1,~1\leq l\leq d_{m}\}
consists of an orthogonal set in
L^{2}
.
Then, we first assume that the regression function admits the following expansion in the sense of
L^{2}
-norm:

\displaystyle f^{*}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}f_{m,l}e_{m,l}.

(26)

Here we note that
\bar{f}_{m}^{2}\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\absolutevalue{f_{m,l}}^{2}=\norm{P_{V_m} \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}
is invariant of the choice of
\{e_{m,l}\}_{l=1}^{d_{m}}

which is an orthogonal basis of
V_{m}
.
Then, we assume that the regression function satisfies the following source condition.

Assumption 5

(Source)
.

There exists some
s>0
such that
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{t}
for any
t<s
,

f^{*}\neq 0
, and if
s<2\tau_{\max}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{m:\mu_{m}<\lambda}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\absolutevalue{f_{m,l}}^{2}=\sum_{m:\mu_{m}<\lambda}\bar{f}_{m}^{2}=\Omega(\lambda^{s}).

(27)

This assumption assumes that the regression function can be approximately described by a power-law decay with smoothness index
s
,
but it does not require that the coefficient of
f^{*}
decays exactly in a power-law manner,
which allows a wider range of regression functions to be considered.
We note that since we have to establish the exact generalization error curve, the lower bound is also necessary,
which is presented in a tail sum manner.
The following shows some examples of regression functions satisfying Assumption
5
,
whose proofs are deferred to Subsection
B.3
.

Example 3.1

(Exact power-law
f^{*}
)
.

Let Assumption
1
hold and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{k}\asymp m^{\gamma}
for some
\gamma\geq 1
.
Suppose
f^{*}
satisfy that
\bar{f}_{m}\asymp m^{-(\gamma p+1)/2}
for some
p>1
.
Then
f^{*}
satisfies Assumption
5
for
s=\frac{p}{\beta}
.
In particular, with a little abuse of notation, if we rearrange
(f_{m,l})
as

\displaystyle(f_{j})_{j\geq 1}=(f_{1,1},\dots,f_{1,d_{1}},f_{2,1},\dots,f_{m,1},\dots,f_{m,d_{m}},\dots).

Then, this example includes the case
\absolutevalue{f_j }\asymp\lambda_{j}^{\frac{s}{2}}j^{-\frac{1}{2}}\asymp j^{-\frac{s\beta+1}{2}}
considered in

Li et al. 2023
;
Cui et al. 2021
.

We can also consider the following case that there are some gaps in the coefficients of
f^{*}
.

Example 3.2
.

In this example, we assume Assumption
1
holds
and consider the
(f_{j})_{j\geq 1}
introduced in the previous example.
Suppose that for some
q\geq 1,p>1
,

\begin{cases}\absolutevalue{f_{j(l)}}\asymp l^{-(p+1)/2},&l=1,2,\dots,\\
f_{j}=0,&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

where
j(l)\asymp l^{q}
.
Then,
f^{*}
satisfies Assumption
5
for
s=p/(q\beta)
.

3.2
Main theorem

Let us first introduce the two deterministic quantities that characterize the bias and the variance respectively.
We define the main bias term by

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\norm{\rem(T) \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\psi_{\lambda}(\mu_{m})^{2}\sum_{l=1}^{d_{m}}\absolutevalue{f_{m,l}}^{2}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\psi_{\lambda}(\mu_{m})^{2}\bar{f}_{m}^{2}.

(28)

For the variance term, we extend the definition of effective dimension
(
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
)
to introduce the
\varphi
-effective dimension of order
p\geq 1
by

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}_{p,\varphi}(\lambda)\coloneqq\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\left[\lambda_{j}\varphi_{\lambda}(\lambda_{j})\right]^{p}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}d_{m}\left[\mu_{m}\varphi_{\lambda}(\mu_{m})\right]^{p}.

(29)

In particular,
\varphi^{\mathrm{KR}}
-effective dimension of order
p=1
is just the ordinary effective dimension considered in the previous work (see (
36
) in the proof).

Theorem 3.1
.

Under Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
and
5
,
for any
\lambda=\lambda(n)\to 0
, we have

•

If
\lambda=\Omega(n^{-\theta})
for some
\theta<\beta
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}~\Big|~X\right]=(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))\left(\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathcal{N}_{2,\varphi}(\lambda)\right),

(30)

where
\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})
and
\mathcal{N}_{2,\varphi}(\lambda)
are two deterministic quantities defined in

(
28
)
and
(
29
)
respectively.

•

If
\lambda=O(n^{-\beta})
, then

\displaystyle\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}~\Big|~X\right]=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}\left((\ln n)^{-4}\sigma^{2}\right),

(31)

where we notice that the hidden constant in
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(\cdot)
depends on
\varepsilon
.

3.3
Discussion

In this subsection, we discuss our main result in the following aspect:

Minimax optimal rate

Theorem
3.1
naturally recovers the minimax optimal rates of spectral algorithms that have been derived in previous works
(see
Lin et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2023
and also the references therein).
Let us suppose further that
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
as in the standard literature and
s\leq 2\tau
.
Then, for the bias term, Lemma
4.4
shows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})=\norm{\rem(T) \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}=O(\lambda^{s}).

For the variance term, Proposition
A.2
shows that

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathcal{N}_{2,\varphi}(\lambda)\asymp\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\lambda^{-1/\beta}.

Consequently, choosing
\lambda\asymp n^{-\theta}
with
\theta=\frac{1}{s\beta+1}
(as in the previous literature) yields the optimal rate

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}~\Big|~X\right]=O_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{-\frac{s\beta}{s\beta+1}}\right).

Exact generalization error curve

Our result provides a complete picture of the generalization error of spectral algorithms,
showing the effect of the choice of the regularization parameter, the source condition of the regression function, the noise level and also the choice of the filter function.
In terms of regularization, as the regularization strength
\lambda
decreases, the bias decreases while the variance increases,
showing that the
bias-variance trade-off
also exists for spectral algorithms and the learning curve is U-shaped as our traditional belief.
It also suggests that a proper choice of
\lambda
is necessary to achieve the optimal rate.

The main strength of our result is that it provides the exact
1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
form of the generalization error for a specific spectral algorithm when
\lambda
lies in the reasonable range.
In comparison, the previous works (for example,
Caponnetto and De Vito 2007
;
Lin et al. 2018
;
Zhang et al. 2023
)
on minimax optimal rates can only provide upper bounds.
While their results can also partially reflect the bias-variance trade-off, it is only in the
upper bound manner
.
More precisely, they demonstrate that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Error}=\mathrm{Bias}(\lambda)+\mathrm{Var}(\lambda),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\mathrm{Bias}(\lambda)\lesssim B(\lambda)\quad\mathrm{Var}(\lambda)\lesssim V(\lambda),

and then choose the optimal
\lambda
by balancing
B(\lambda)
and
V(\lambda)
.
They further check that, for such a choice of
\lambda
, the upper bound
B(\lambda)+V(\lambda)
can match the minimax lower bound.
However, as
B(\lambda)
and
V(\lambda)
are only upper bounds, there is no guarantee that they reflect the exact bias-variance trade-off.
Moreover, the standard approach in the recent literature on spectral algorithms is to apply the so-called approximation-estimation decomposition (see, e.g., the beginning of the Proof of Theorem 4.2 in
Lin et al. 2018
) to analyze the generalization error,
which directly loses the exact bias-variance trade-off.

In contrast, our results establish the precise approximation of
\mathrm{Bias}(\lambda)
and
\mathrm{Var}(\lambda)
for any
\lambda\propto n^{-\theta},\theta\in(0,\beta)
in the
1+o_{P}(1)
form,
providing the exact bias-variance trade-off even without losing constant factors.
While the recent work
(
Li et al. 2023
)
proved rigorously the learning curves of KRR, but only asymptotic rates (in the form of
\Theta_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-r})
) are provided,
so our result is also a refinement of their result even in the KRR case.
For general spectral algorithms,
as far as we know, we are the first to provide such
1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
form of exact generalization error curves.

The implication of the
1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1)
form of exact generalization error is that it allows us to consider the constant factor in the generalization error.
It could reflect precisely how the magnitude of the regression function and the noise affect the generalization error.
Moreover, from the oracle viewpoint, minimizing the sum of the two terms in (
30
)
would result in the best choice of
\lambda=\lambda(n)
and the best generalization error, going beyond merely the asymptotic rate.

Interpolating regime

We refer to the case of weak regularization, namely
\lambda=\lambda(n)=O(n^{-\beta})
, as the
interpolating regime
.
Theorem
3.1
shows that in this regime, the generalization error is of order
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}((\ln n)^{-4})
,
which is nearly a constant, so estimator does not generalize at all.

The performance of kernel methods in the interpolating regime is also considered in the previous literature.
Under restricted settings, several works

(
Rakhlin and Zhai 2018
;
Buchholz 2022
;
Beaglehole et al. 2022
)

shown the inconsistency of the kernel minimum-norm interpolator, which is the
\lambda\to 0^{+}
limit of spectral algorithms.
Being the most relevant with this paper,

Li et al. 2023
showed that for general RKHSs associated with a Hölder continuous kernel satisfying the embedding index condition,
the generalization error of KRR in the interpolating regime is
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}(n^{-\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
.
In comparison, we remove the condition of Hölder continuity and
also provide an improved lower bound
\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}((\ln n)^{-4})
for spectral algorithms in the interpolating regime
by refine the analysis using the condition of regular RKHS.
We believe that this improved lower bound further confirms kernel methods do not generalize in the interpolating regime,
highlighting the necessity of the regularization.

Saturation effect of higher order

The saturation effect refers to the phenomenon that, for a certain spectral algorithm with limited qualification,
it cannot achieve the minimax optimal rate of convergence when the smoothness of the regression function exceeds its qualification.
Since the traditional literature only provides the upper bound of the generalization error, they cannot prove the saturation effect.
The recent work
Li et al. 2023
rigorously proved the saturation effect for KRR, whose qualification is
\tau_{\max}=1
,
but there is still result for other spectral algorithms with higher (but limited) qualification,
such as the iterated ridge regularization (see Example
2.5
).

Thanks to the exact generalization error curve, we can prove the saturation effect for spectral algorithms with higher qualification.
Let us consider a spectral algorithm such that it has limited qualification
\tau_{\max}<\infty
, which is the case of the iterated ridge regularization.
Then, for
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
with
s\geq 2\tau_{\max}
and
f^{*}\neq 0
it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\psi_{\lambda}(\mu_{m})^{2}\bar{f}_{m}^{2}\geq\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\underline{F}\lambda^{2\tau}\bar{f}_{m}^{2}=\underline{F}\norm{\fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}\lambda^{2\tau}.

Consequently, with the upper bound in Lemma
4.4
,
we conclude that
\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})\asymp\lambda^{2\tau}
.
Then, when
\lambda
is not too small, the main theorem gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}~\Big|~X\right]

\displaystyle=(1+o_{\mathbb{P}}(1))\left(\mathcal{R}_{\varphi}^{2}(\lambda;f^{*})+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\mathcal{N}_{2,\varphi}(\lambda)\right)

\displaystyle=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}\left(\lambda^{2\tau}+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}\lambda^{-1/\beta}\right)=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{-\frac{2\tau\beta}{2\tau\beta+1}}\right).

To cover the case when
\lambda
can possibly be too small, we can consider
\bar{\lambda}=\max(\lambda,n^{-\theta})
for some
\theta<\beta

and apply the monotonicity of the variance term (Lemma
4.15
) as in
Li et al. 2023
.
We formulate it as a corollary.

Corollary 3.2

(Saturation effect)
.

Suppose Assumptions
1
,
2
,
3
,
4
hold.
Let
\varphi_{\lambda}
be a filter function with qualification
\tau=\tau_{\max}<\infty
.
Then, for any non-zero
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{s}
for
s\geq 2\tau
, for any choice of
\lambda=\lambda(n)\to 0
,
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{\hat{f}_\lambda- \fstar}_{L^{2}}^{2}~\Big|~X\right]=\Omega_{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{-\frac{2\tau\beta}{2\tau\beta+1}}\right).

(32)

Moreover, the lower bound is attained when
\lambda\asymp n^{-\theta}
for
\theta=\frac{\beta}{2\tau\beta+1}
.

The analytic functional argument

As one of our technical contributions, we develop an
analytic functional argument
based on analytic functional calculus in the context of spectral algorithms,
which enables us to derive the exact generalization error curve.
While functional calculus has a long history in operator perturbation theory
(
Hsing and Eubank 2015
)
,
as far as we know, we are the first to apply it to analyze spectral algorithms.
First, we would like to illustrate the difficulties here and why the existing techniques are not applicable.
The traditional literature on the optimal rate focused only on the upper bound and their approaches, which are based on the approximation-estimation decomposition
(for example, Eq. (88) in
Zhang et al. 2023
), are not applicable for the lower bound.
Moreover, it is in general more difficult to provide the lower bound than the upper bound since the former requires the error term to be infinitesimal,
For the simple case of KRR, the rigorous work
(
Li et al. 2023
)
determined the asymptotic rate of convergence,
but the proof method must rely on the
resolvent identity
of KRR, that is,

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{KR}}(A)-\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{KR}}(B)=(A+\lambda)^{-1}-(B+\lambda)^{-1}=(A+\lambda)^{-1}(B-A)(B+\lambda)^{-1},

and also
\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{KR}}(A)=\lambda(A+\lambda)^{-1}
.
This identity is crucial for concentrating the random terms (see (
35
)) to the non-random counterpart appearing
on the right hand side of (

where we will encounter quantities like
\varphi_{\lambda}(T)-\varphi_{\lambda}(T_{X})
and

However, for general spectral algorithms, it is impossible to derive similar identity.
Moreover, the effect of
\lambda
must also be taken into consideration.

Our “analytic functional argument” overcomes these difficulties using the analytic functional calculus that

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}(A)-\varphi_{\lambda}(B)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}\varphi_{\lambda}(z)\left[R_{A}(z)-R_{B}(z)\right]\differential z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}R_{B}(z)(A-B)R_{A}(z)\varphi_{\lambda}(z)\differential z,

where

is the resolvent of

and
\Gamma
is a contour.
Then, the terms in the integral resemble that of KRR (but note that now
z
a complex number now).
Surprisingly, with carefully chosen contour

depending on
\lambda

this crucial formula allows us to apply the concentration results obtained for the resolvent and derive very sharp estimations,
leading to the exact generalization error curve.
We believe that this novel technique is of independent interest and can be applied to other problems.

4.4
Analytic functional calculus

The analytic functional argument based is one of our main novelty in this paper.
Let us first recall some basic facts about analytic functional calculus.
We refer to for example,
Simon 2015
for mathematical details.

Definition 4.9
.

Let
A
be a linear operator on a Banach space
X
.
The
resolvent set

\rho(A)
is given by

\displaystyle\rho(A)\coloneqq\left\{\lambda\in\mathbb{C}\mid A-\lambda~\text{is invertible}\right\},

and we denote
R_{A}(\lambda)\coloneqq(A-\lambda)^{-1}
.
The spectrum of
A
is defined by

\displaystyle\sigma(A)\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\backslash\rho(A).

A simple but key ingredient in the analytic functional calculus is the following
resolvent identity
:

\displaystyle R_{A}(\lambda)-R_{B}(\lambda)=R_{A}(\lambda)(B-A)R_{B}(\lambda)=R_{B}(\lambda)(B-A)R_{A}(\lambda).

(49)

The resolvent allows us to define the value of
f(A)
in analog to the form of Cauchy integral formula,
where
A
is an operator and
f
is an analytic function.
This is often referred to as the analytic functional calculus, see e.g.,
Simon 2015
.

Proposition 4.10

(Analytic Functional Calculus)
.

Let
A
be an operator on a Hilbert space
H
and
f
be an analytic function defined on
D_{f}\subset\mathbb{C}
.
Let
\Gamma
be a contour contained in
D_{f}
surrounding
\sigma(A)
.
Then,

\displaystyle f(A)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)(z-A)^{-1}\differential z=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)R_{A}(z)\differential z,

(50)

and it is independent of the choice of
\Gamma
.

Remark 4.11
.

For a self-adjoint compact operator
A
, we have spectral decomposition

\displaystyle A=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}\left\langle{e_{i},\cdot}\right\rangle e_{i},

and
f(A)
is often defined by

\displaystyle f(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}f(\lambda_{i})\left\langle{e_{i},\cdot}\right\rangle e_{i}.

(51)

In fact, this definition is consistent to the one in Proposition
4.10
.
We remark that (
51
) is also valid for continuous
f
and
an extension to self-adjoint (not necessarily compact) operators is also possible by the spectral theorem
(
Simon 2015
, Section 5)
.

Now, let
\Gamma
be a contour contained in
D_{f}
surrounding both
\sigma(A)
and
\sigma(B)
.
Using (
49
), we get

\displaystyle f(A)-f(B)=-\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}f(z)\left[R_{A}(z)-R_{B}(z)\right]\differential z=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_{\Gamma}R_{B}(z)(A-B)R_{A}(z)f(z)\differential z.

(52)

We will use the following spectral mapping theorem to bound some operator norms in the proof,
see
Simon 2015
.

Proposition 4.12

(Spectral Mapping Theorem)
.

Let
A
be a bounded self-adjoint operator and
f
be a continuous function on
\sigma(A)
.
Then

\displaystyle\sigma(f(A))=\left\{f(\lambda)\mid\lambda\in\sigma(A)\right\}.

(53)

Proposition 4.13
.

When (

there is an absolute constant

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \norm{T_{\lambda}^{\hf}(T-z)^{-1} T_{\lambda}^{\hf}}&\leq C\\

(55)

Proof.

Using the spectral mapping theorem, for a self-adjoint operator
A
with
\sigma(A)\subseteq[0,\kappa^{2}]
we have

\displaystyle\norm{A_{\lambda}^{\hf} (A-z)^{-1}A_{\lambda}^{\hf}}=\sup_{t\in\sigma(A)}\absolutevalue{\frac{t+\lambda}{t-z}}.

Now, when

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\sigma(A)}\absolutevalue{\frac{t+\lambda}{t-z}}^{2}\leq\sup_{t\geq 0}\absolutevalue{\frac{t+\lambda}{t-z}}^{2}=\begin{cases}\frac{4\lambda^{2}}{\lambda^{2}+4\lambda x+8x^{2}},&-\frac{1}{2}\lambda\leq x\leq-\frac{1}{2(2+\sqrt{2})}\lambda,\\
\frac{5\lambda^{2}+12\lambda x+8x^{2}}{(\lambda+2x)^{2}},&x\geq\frac{1}{2(2+\sqrt{2})}\lambda,\\
\end{cases}

Tedious calculations show that the right hand side achieve the maximum of

at

, so

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\sigma(A)}\absolutevalue{\frac{t+\lambda}{t-z}}^{2}\leq 8,\quad z\in\Gamma_{\lambda,1}\cup\Gamma_{\lambda,2}.

, so

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in\sigma(A)}\absolutevalue{\frac{t+\lambda}{t-z}}\leq\sup_{t\in\sigma(A)}\absolutevalue{\frac{t+\lambda}{\kappa^2}}\leq\frac{\lambda+\kappa^{2}}{\kappa^{2}}\leq 2.

\displaystyle\norm{A_{\lambda}^{\hf} (A-z)^{-1}A_{\lambda}^{\hf}}\leq C.

For the second one, we notice that

\displaystyle\norm{T_{\lambda}^{\hf}(T_X - z)^{-1} T_{\lambda}^{\hf}}

\displaystyle=\norm{T_{\lambda}^{\hf} T_{X\lambda}^{-\hf} \cdot T_{X\lambda}^{\hf} (T_X - z)^{-1} T_{X\lambda}^{\hf} \cdot T_{X\lambda}^{-\hf}T_{\lambda}^{\hf}}

\displaystyle\leq\norm{T_{\lambda}^{\hf} T_{X\lambda}^{-\hf}}\cdot\norm{T_{X\lambda}^{\hf} (T_X - z)^{-1} T_{X\lambda}^{\hf}}\cdot\norm{T_{X\lambda}^{-\hf}T_{\lambda}^{\hf}}

The following theorem greatly improves the result in

Theorem 4.14

Under Assumptions

,
2
and

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{\norm{\reg(T_X)k_{x_i}}^2_{L^2} - \norm{\reg(T)k_{x_i}}^2_{L^2}}+\absolutevalue{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \norm{\reg(T)k_{x_i}}^2_{L^2} - \int_{\caX} \norm{\reg(T) k_x}_{L^2}^2 \dd\mu(x)}

\displaystyle\norm{\reg(T) k_x}_{L^{2}}^{2}

Lemma 4.15
.

The variance term
\mathbf{Var}(\lambda)
increases as
\lambda
decreases, i.e., for any
\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}
,
we have
\mathbf{Var}(\lambda_{1})\geq\mathbf{Var}(\lambda_{2})
.

Proof.

Let us define the kernel matrix
K=\frac{1}{n}\big(k(x_{i},x_{j})\big)_{n\times n}
.
Then, it is easy to show that the representation matrix of
T_{X}
on the set
\{k_{x_{i}}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is given by
K

(see, for example,
Li et al. 2023
).
Consequently, denoting a column vector
\mathbb{K}(X,\cdot)=(k_{x_{1}},\dots,k_{x_{n}})^{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}(T_{X})\mathbb{K}(X,\cdot)=\varphi_{\lambda}(K)\mathbb{K}(X,\cdot),

where the action of
\varphi_{\lambda}(T_{X})
on the left-hand side is element-wise.
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Var}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\norm{\reg(T_X)k_{x_i}}^{2}_{L^{2}}=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\absolutevalue{(\reg(T_X)k_{x_i})(x)}^{2}\differential\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\absolutevalue{(\reg(T_X)k_{x_i})(x)}^{2}\differential\mu(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{(\reg(T_X)\K(X,\cdot))(x)}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}\differential\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{(\reg(K) \K(X,\cdot))(x)}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}\differential\mu(x)=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\norm{\reg(K) \K(X,x)}_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}\differential\mu(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n^{2}}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left[\mathbb{K}(X,x)\right]^{H}\varphi_{\lambda}^{2}(K)\mathbb{K}(X,x)\differential\mu(x),

where
\left[\mathbb{K}(X,x)\right]^{H}
is the conjugate transpose of
\mathbb{K}(X,x)
.
Moreover, the property (i) of the filter function implies that
\varphi_{\lambda}(z)
increases as
\lambda
decreases.
Therefore, we get

\varphi_{\lambda_{1}}^{2}(K)\succeq\varphi_{\lambda_{2}}^{2}(K)
and the result follows.
∎

5
Conclusion

In this paper,
we rigorously illustrated a full characterization of the generalization error curves for a large class of analytic spectral algorithms,
providing an exact and complete picture of generalization errors of these kernel methods.
The result shows the interplay between the kernel, the regression function, the noise level and also the choice of the regularization parameter.
In particular, the result shows a solid U-shaped bias-variance trade-off curve with respect to the regularization parameter.
As applications, the result recovers the minimax optimal rates, shows the poor generalization in the interpolating regime,
and also exposes a high-order saturation effect.
These results would help us greatly improve our understanding of the generalization of spectral algorithms.

It is also of interest to extend ask if similar characterization holds for other non-analytic spectral algorithms.
One particular algorithm is the spectral cut-off method (also known as the truncated singular values decomposition)
(
Bauer et al. 2007
)
,
whose filter function is even not continuous:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{cut}}(z)=\begin{cases}z^{-1},&z\geq\lambda,\\
0,&z<\lambda.\end{cases},\mbox{\quad and\quad}\psi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{cut}}(z)=\mathbf{1}\left\{z<\lambda\right\}.

(72)

The difficulty here is that it is hard to approximate it by analytic ones while keeping the desired properties.
We think more analysis tools are needed to handle this case, so we would like to leave it as a future direction.

Finally, this work focuses on the eigenvalues of power-law decay and also a power-law like source condition.
It would also be interesting to extend the result to other situations, such as the eigenvalues of exponential decay and also the source condition of exponential decay.
We believe that the techniques developed in this work would be helpful in this direction.

Proposition A.1
.

Let
(a_{j})_{j\geq 1}
be a sequence of positive numbers descending to zero.
Then,

\displaystyle a_{j}=\Theta(j^{-\beta}),\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad\#\left\{j:a_{j}\geq\lambda\right\}=\Theta(\lambda^{-1/\beta})\quad\mbox{as}\quad\lambda\to 0.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\max\left\{j:a_{j}\geq\lambda\right\}=\min\left\{j:a_{j+1}<\lambda\right\}\leq\min\left\{j:C(j+1)^{-\beta}<\lambda\right\}=O(\lambda^{-1/\beta}).

(

On the other hand,

\displaystyle a_{j}\leq\inf\left\{\lambda:N(\lambda)<j\right\}\leq\inf\left\{\lambda:C\lambda^{-1/\beta}<j\right\}=O(j^{-\beta}).

B.1.1
Dot-product kernel on the sphere

Let
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{S}^{d}
be the
d
-dimensional sphere and
\mu
be the uniform measure on
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.
Then, classical results
(
Dai and Xu 2013
)
shows that
the eigen-decomposition of the spherical Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}
gives an orthogonal direct sum decomposition

\displaystyle L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\bigoplus_{m=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d}),

where
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
consists of the restriction of
n
-degree homogeneous harmonic polynomials with
d+1
variables on
\mathbb{S}^{d}

and
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is an eigenspace of
\Delta_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}
associated with eigenvalue
-m(m+d-1)
.
Moreover, the dimension of
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is given by

\displaystyle a_{m}\coloneqq\dim\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\binom{m+d}{m}-\binom{m-2+d}{m-2}\asymp m^{d-1},

and also
\sum_{k\leq m}a_{k}=\binom{m+d}{m}+\binom{m-1+d}{m-1}\asymp m^{d}
.

Moreover, the reproducing kernel
Z_{m}(x,y)
of
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
is well-defined and unique,
which can be given explicitly by

\displaystyle Z_{m}(x,y)=\sum_{l=1}^{a_{m}}Y_{m,l}(x)Y_{m,l}(y),

where
\{Y_{m,l}\}_{m=1}^{a_{m}}
is an arbitrary orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
.
Let us denote by
C_{m}^{\lambda}
the Gegenbauer polynomial, which is often defined by the following power series

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}C_{m}^{\lambda}(t)\alpha^{m}=\frac{1}{(1-2t\alpha+\alpha^{2})^{\lambda}}.

(84)

Then, when
d\geq 2
, we have

\displaystyle Z_{m}(x,y)=\frac{n+\lambda}{\lambda}C_{m}^{\lambda}(u),\quad u=\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle,\;\lambda=\frac{d-1}{2}.

(85)

Also,
Dai and Xu 2013
shows that

\displaystyle Z_{m}(x,x)=\frac{n+\lambda}{\lambda}C_{m}^{\lambda}(1)=\dim\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d}).

(86)

Furthermore, we have the following Funk-Hecke formula
(
Dai and Xu 2013
, Theorem 1.2.9)
.

Proposition B.1

(Funk-Hecke formula)
.

Let

and

be an integrable function such that
\int_{-1}^{1}\absolutevalue{h(t)}(1-t^{2})^{(d-2)/2}\differential t
is finite.
Then for every
Y_{m}\in\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d}}h(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle)Y_{m}(y)\differential\mu(y)=\mu_{m}(h)Y_{m}(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{S}^{d},

(87)

where
\mu_{m}(h)
is a constant defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{m}(h)=\omega_{d}\int_{-1}^{1}h(t)\frac{C_{m}^{\lambda}(t)}{C_{m}^{\lambda}(1)}(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-2}{2}}\differential t,

and
\omega_{d}
is the surface area of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d}
.

Comparing (
2
) and (
87
), we conclude that
\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})

is an eigenspace of
T
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{m}=\mu_{m}(h)
.
Consequently, we get
Z_{m}=k_{m}
and hence according to (
86
), we have

\displaystyle k_{m}(x,x)=Z_{m}(x,x)=\dim\mathcal{H}^{d}_{m}(\mathbb{S}^{d})=\dim V_{m},

so Assumption
2
holds for
M=1
.

B.1.2
Dot-product kernel on the ball

The case of the ball is similar to the case of the sphere.
We refer to
Dai and Xu 2013
for more details.
Let us consider the
d
-dimensional unit ball
\mathcal{X}=\mathbb{B}^{d}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}:\norm{x}\leq 1\}

with
\mu
be proportional to the classical weight
W(x)=(1-\norm{x}^{2})^{-1/2}
.
Let us denote by
V_{m}^{d}
the space of orthogonal polynomials of degree exactly
n

with respect to the inner product

\displaystyle\left\langle{f,g}\right\rangle_{W}=c_{W}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}f(x)g(x)W(x)\differential x,

where
c_{W}
is a normalization constant.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\dim V_{m}^{d}=\binom{m+d-1}{m}.

Moreover, we have the following analog of the Funk–Hecke formula
(
Dai and Xu 2013
, Theorem 11.1.9)
:

Proposition B.2

(Funk-Hecke formula)
.

Let
\lambda=\frac{d-1}{2}
and
h
be an integrable function such that
\int_{-1}^{1}\absolutevalue{h(t)}(1-t^{2})^{\lambda-1/2}\differential t
is finite.
Then for every
P_{m}\in V^{d}_{m}
,

\displaystyle c_{W}\int_{\mathbb{B}^{d}}h(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle)P_{m}(y)W(y)\differential y=\mu_{m}(f)P_{m}(x),\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{B}^{d},

(88)

where
\mu_{m}(h)
is a constant defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{m}(h)=c_{\lambda}\int_{-1}^{1}h(t)\frac{C_{m}^{\lambda}(t)}{C_{m}^{\lambda}(1)}(1-t^{2})^{\lambda-\frac{1}{2}}\differential t,

and
c_{\lambda}
is a constant that
\mu_{0}(1)=1
.

Consequently, for any inner product kernel
k(x,y)=h(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle)
,

V^{d}_{m}
consists of an eigenspace of
T
corresponding to the eigenvalue
\mu_{m}=\mu_{m}(h)
.

Moreover,
Dai and Xu 2013
shows that the reproducing kernel
k_{m}
for
V^{d}_{m}
is

\displaystyle k_{m}(x,y)=\frac{n+\lambda}{2\lambda}\left[C_{n}^{\lambda}(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle+x_{d+1}y_{d+1})+C_{n}^{\lambda}(\left\langle{x,y}\right\rangle-x_{d+1}y_{d+1})\right],

where
x_{d+1}=\sqrt{1-\norm{x}^{2}},y_{d+1}=\sqrt{1-\norm{y}^{2}}
.
Consequently,

\displaystyle k_{m}(x,x)=\frac{n+\lambda}{2\lambda}\left(C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)+C_{n}^{\lambda}(2\norm{x}^{2}-1)\right)\leq\frac{n+\lambda}{\lambda}C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)=\frac{n+\lambda}{\lambda}\frac{\Gamma(2\lambda+n)}{\Gamma(2\lambda)n!}

where we use
Dai and Xu 2013
for
C_{n}^{\lambda}(1)
.
Expanding the expression with
\lambda=(d-1)/2
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{n+\lambda}{\lambda}\frac{\Gamma(2\lambda+n)}{\Gamma(2\lambda)n!}/\dim V^{d}_{n}=\frac{2n+d-1}{n+d-1}\leq 2,

so Assumption
2
also holds with
M=2
.

B.2
Analytic filter functions

To further analyze the properties of filter functions in the complex plane, let us first recall some results in complex analysis.

Proposition B.3

(Maximum modulus principle)
.

Let
f
be an analytic function on an open set
\Omega
and
K\subset\Omega
be a compact set.
Then

\displaystyle\sup_{z\in K}\absolutevalue{f(z)}=\sup_{z\in\partial K}\absolutevalue{f(z)}.

Proposition B.4
.

The filter functions of KRR (Example
2.4
), iterated ridge (Example
2.5
) and gradient flow (Example
2.6
) satisfy Assumption
3
.

Proof.

In the case of KRR, this conclusion is trivial.

(
I
) Conditions (C1), iterated ridge and gradient flow: Define
H=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\real(z)\geq-\frac{\lambda}{2}\}
.
Note that the filter functions of gradient flow and iterated ridge are both in the form of

\varphi_{\lambda}(z)=g(u)/(\lambda u)
for some analytic function
g(u)
on
H
, where
u=z/\lambda
. Specifically, for iterated ridge,
g(u)=1-\frac{1}{(u+1)^{p}}
. For gradient flow,
g(u)=1-e^{-u}
. Note that
\frac{g(u)}{u}
can be extended to a analytic function on
H
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\absolutevalue{(z+\lambda)\reg(z)}=\absolutevalue{(u+1) g(u)/u}\eqqcolon G(u)>0

can also be considered as an analytic function on
H
.
In the case of iterated ridge,
\limsup_{\absolutevalue{u}\rightarrow\infty}G(u)=\limsup_{\absolutevalue{u}\rightarrow\infty}g(u)=1
is finite. As a result,
\sup_{u\in H}G(u)<\infty
which means that condition (C1) holds. The filter function of gradient flow also satisfies this condition owing to
\limsup_{\absolutevalue{u}\rightarrow\infty}g(u)\leq\sup_{u\in H}g(u)\leq 1+\sqrt{e}<\infty
.

(
II
) Condition (C2), iterated ridge and gradient flow: In the case of iterated ridge,

\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}\rem^{\mr{IT},p}(z)}=\left(\frac{\absolutevalue{\lambda}}{\absolutevalue{z+\lambda}}\right)^{p-1}\leq 2^{p-1}

for all
z
satisfying
\real(z)\geq-\frac{\lambda}{2}
.
As for gradient flow, when
\real(z)\leq 0
and
z\in D_{\lambda}
,

\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}\rem^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)}=\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}}e^{\real(-\frac{z}{\lambda})}\leq\frac{5}{2}\sqrt{e}.

If
\real(z)>0
and
z\in D_{\lambda}
,

\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}\rem^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)}\leq 2(1+\frac{\real(z)}{\lambda})e^{\real(-\frac{z}{\lambda})}.

Note that function
f(x)\coloneqq(1+x)e^{-x}
monotonly decreases on
[0,+\infty)
. Hence,
\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}\rem^{\mathrm{GF}}(z)}\leq 2
.
∎

Proposition B.5
.

The filter function of gradient descent (Example
2.7
) satisfyies Assumption
3
.

Proof.

For condition (C1), Note that
(z+\lambda)\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)
can be extended to be an analytic function on
D_{\lambda}
.
According to Proposition
B.3
, we only need to prove that
\sup_{z\in\Gamma_{\lambda}}(z+\lambda)\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}(z)
is controled by a constant independent of
\lambda
. Actually, for all
z\in\Gamma_{\lambda}
,
\frac{\absolutevalue{z+\lambda}}{\absolutevalue{z}}\leq 1+\frac{\absolutevalue{\lambda}}{\absolutevalue{z}}\leq 3
and
\absolutevalue{1-\eta z}^{\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}\leq\absolutevalue{1+\hf\eta\lambda}^{\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}\leq\sqrt{e}
. Hence, condition (C1) also holds for
\varphi_{\lambda}^{\mathrm{GD}}
.

As for condition (C2), we need to demonstrate that

\sup_{z\in D_{\lambda},\lambda\in(0,\varepsilon)}\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}\rem^{\mr{GD}}(z)}=\sup_{z\in D_{\lambda},\lambda\in(0,\varepsilon)}\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}(1-\eta z)^\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}<\infty

for some constant
\varepsilon>0
.
In fact, when
\absolutevalue{1-\eta z}\geq 1
,

\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}(1-\eta z)^\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\absolutevalue{1+\hf\eta\lambda}^{\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}\leq\frac{3}{2}\sqrt{e}

for all
z\in D_{\lambda}
. When
\absolutevalue{1-\eta z}<1
,

\absolutevalue{\frac{z+\lambda}{\lambda}(1-\eta z)^\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}\leq 1+\absolutevalue{\frac{z}{\lambda}(1-\eta z)^\frac{1}{\eta\lambda}}\leq 1+\absolutevalue{z(1-\eta z)^\frac{1}{\eta}}\leq\frac{3}{2}+2\kappa^{2}

for all
z\in D_{\lambda}
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
.
∎

B.3
Source condition on the regression function

Proof of Example
3.1
.

With Assumption
1
and
\sum_{k=1}^{m}d_{k}\asymp m^{\gamma}
, we have
\mu_{m}\asymp m^{-\gamma\beta}
. Select
s=\frac{p}{\beta}
. Then, for all

Hence,
f^{*}\in[\mathcal{H}]^{t}
for all

.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{m:\mu_{m}\leq\lambda}\bar{f}_{m}^{2}\geq c\sum_{m\geq C\lambda^{-(\gamma\beta)^{-1}}}m^{-\gamma p-1}=\Omega(\lambda^{\frac{p}{\beta}})=\Omega(\lambda^{s}).

.
∎

Proof of Example

.

.

where we recall that

,

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\sum_{j:\lambda_{j}\leq\lambda}\absolutevalue{f_j}^{2}=\sum_{l:\lambda_{j(l)}\leq\lambda}\absolutevalue{f_{j(l)}}^{2}\geq c\sum_{l\geq C\lambda^{-1/(q\beta)}}l^{-(p+1)}=\Omega(\lambda^{\frac{p}{q\beta}})=\Omega(\lambda^{s}).

B.4
Proof of Lemma
4.20

The proof follows the same idea as the proof of Lemma A.5 and Lemma A.10 in

,
but we establish the results for spectral algorithms and also improve the estimates by Lemma

using the regular RKHS property.
The proof will be divided into two cases:

and

.
For the first case, the regression function

is

-bounded so that we can directly apply the Bernstein inequality.
For the second case, we have to apply a truncation technique.

Proof for the case

.

By the inclusion relation of
[\mathcal{H}]^{t}
, we can replace
t
with

so now we have

.
We will use the Bernstein inequality in Lemma
A.7
.
Let us denote

\displaystyle\xi(x)=T_{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}(K_{x}f^{*}(x)-T_{x}f^{*}_{\lambda}).

(89)

Then, we have

\displaystyle T_{\lambda}^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left[\left(\tilde{g}_{X}-T_{X}f_{\lambda}^{*}\right)-\left(g-Tf_{\lambda}^{*}\right)\right]=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\xi(x_{i})-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\xi(x).

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\norm{\xi(x)}_{\mathcal{H}}^{m}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\norm{ T_\lambda^{-\hf} K_{x}(\fstar(x) - \flam(x)) }_{\mathcal{H}}^{m}

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\norm{T_\lambda^{-\hf} k_x}_{\mathcal{H}}^{m}\cdot\mathbb{E}\big(\absolutevalue{\fstar(x) - \flam(x)}^{m}~\big|~x\big)\right].

(90)

The first term in (

) is bounded through (
37

\displaystyle\norm{T_\lambda^{-\hf} k_x}_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}\leq M\mathcal{N}_{1}(\lambda)\leq C\lambda^{-1/\beta}.

For the second term, since

, using the embedding property Proposition
2.2
and also Lemma
4.4
,
for
\alpha\in(1/\beta,t)
, we have

\displaystyle\norm{\flam- \fstar}_{L^{\infty}}\leq M_{\alpha}\norm{\flam-\fstar}_{[\mathcal{H}]^{\alpha}}\leq M_{\alpha}F_{(t-\alpha)/2}\norm{\fstar}_{[\mathcal{H}]^{t}}\lambda^{\frac{t-\alpha}{2}}\leq CM_{\alpha}R\lambda^{\frac{t-\alpha}{2}}

Moreover, Lemma
4.4
also implies

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\absolutevalue{\flam(x) - \fstar(x)}^{2}=\norm{\flam(x) - \fstar(x)}_{L^{2}}^{2}\leq F_{t/2}^{2}\norm{\fstar}_{[\mathcal{H}]^{t}}^{2}\lambda^{t}\leq CR^{2}\lambda^{t}.

Plugging in these estimations in (

), we get

)

\displaystyle\leq(C\lambda^{-1/(2\beta)})^{m}\cdot\norm{\fstar- \fstar}_{L^{\infty}}^{m-2}\cdot\mathbb{E}\absolutevalue{\flam(x) - \fstar(x)}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}m!\left(CR^{2}\lambda^{t-1/\beta}\right)\left(CM_{\alpha}R\lambda^{\frac{t-\alpha-1/\beta}{2}}\right)^{m-2}

Consequently, applying Lemma

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}=CR^{2}\lambda^{t-1/\beta},\qquad L=CM_{\alpha}R\lambda^{\frac{t-\alpha-1/\beta}{2}}.

yields

\displaystyle\norm{T_\lambda^{-\hf} \left[\left(\gtl-T_X \flam\right)-\left(g^* - T \flam\right)\right]}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq CR\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-1/\beta}}{n}}\left(1+M_{\alpha}\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-\alpha}}{n}}\right)\lambda^{t/2}.

Since

Proof for the case

.

For this case, we have to apply a truncation technique.
To bound the extra error terms caused by truncation, we have to use the following proposition about the

embedding of the RKHS

(

.

Proposition B.6
.

Under Assumption
2
, for any
0<s\leq\alpha_{0}
and
\alpha>\alpha_{0}
, we have embedding

\displaystyle[\mathcal{H}]^{s}\hookrightarrow L^{q}(\mathcal{X},\differential\mu),\quad q=\frac{2\alpha}{\alpha-s}.

(91)

Now, let us consider

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \norm{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \xi(x_i)-\mathbb{E} \xi(x)}_{\mathcal{H}}&\leq\norm{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \bar{\xi}(x_i) - \E\bar{\xi}(x)}_{\mathcal{H}}+\norm{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \xi(x_i) \bm{1}_{\Omega_B^\complement}(x_i)}_{\mathcal{H}}\\

(92)

\displaystyle\norm{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \bar{\xi}(x_i) - \E\bar{\xi}(x)}_{\mathcal{H}}\leq CR\sqrt{\frac{\lambda^{-1/\beta}}{n}}\left(1+\frac{M_{\alpha}\lambda^{-\alpha/2}+B\lambda^{-t/2}}{\sqrt{n}}\right)\lambda^{t/2},
