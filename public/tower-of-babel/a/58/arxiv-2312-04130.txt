The wave equation on lattices and Oscillatory Integrals
Cheng Bi, Jiawei Cheng, Bobo Hua

Abstract
. In this paper, we establish sharp dispersive estimates for the linear wave equation on the lattice ℤ d {\mathbb{Z}}^{d} with dimension d = 4 d=4 . Combining the singularity theory with results in uniform estimates of oscillatory integrals, we prove that the optimal time decay rate of the fundamental solution is of order | t | − 3 2 ​ log ⁡ | t | |t|^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log|t| , which is the first extension of P. Schultz’s results [ 38 ] in d = 2 , 3 d=2,3 to the higher dimension. Moreover, we notice that the Newton polyhedron can be used not only to interpret the decay rates for d = 2 , 3 , 4 d=2,3,4 , but also to study the most degenerate case for all odd d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . Furthermore, we prove l p → l q l^{p}\rightarrow l^{q} estimates as well as Strichartz estimates and give applications to nonlinear wave equations.



1.
Introduction

Discrete analogs of partial differential equations on graphs have been extensively studied in recent years. Most works focus on elliptic and parabolic equations, see e.g.
[
4
,
18
]
. References related to wave equations include
[
14
,
30
,
31
]
.
In this article, we
consider the following nonlinear wave equation on the
d
-dimensional lattice
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
,

(1)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{2}u(x,t)-\Delta u(x,t)=F(u(x,t)),\\
&u(x,0)=g(x),\quad\partial_{t}u(x,0)=f(x),\end{aligned}\right.

where
x=(x_{1},\cdots,x_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
. The discrete Laplacian
\Delta
is defined by

\Delta u(x,t):=\sum_{y\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},\,\mathrm{d}(x,y)=1}\Big(u(y,t)-u(x,t)\Big),\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \mathrm{d}(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{d}|x_{j}-y_{j}|.

There are a number of physical applications where this equation appears in a natural way, mainly to describe the behaviour of wave propagation. One of the best known of them is the description of the vibrations of atoms inside crystals. A fundamental model is the monotonic chains, in which each atom vibrates as a simple harmonic oscillator and only feels the force of its nearest neighbours, see
[
11
,
13
,
45
]
.

As is shown in
[
19
]
, there exists a nontrivial Tychnoff-type solution to (
1
) with
F,g,f\equiv 0
. In particular, this solution does not belong to
l^{2}
, hence it is not a “physical” solution. Therefore, in what follows we restrict attention to the so-called semigroup solution, see (
13
) below.

Obtaining dispersive inequalities for linear dispersive equations usually serves as the first step in the study of nonlinear problems. Generally, it amounts to establishing a decay estimate for the
l^{\infty}
norm of the solution in terms of time and the
l^{1}
norm of the initial data.
On Euclidean space
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it is well-known that the decay rate of the solution to wave equation is of order
|t|^{-\frac{d-1}{2}}
, while for Schrödinger equation, it decays like
|t|^{-\frac{d}{2}}
, see e.g.
[
16
,
29
,
41
]
. These results have been extended to more general framework such as Heisenberg groups and H-type groups, see for instance H. Bahouri et al.
[
2
,
3
]
and M. Del Hierro
[
10
]
.

From now on we focus on (
1
). Let
F,g\equiv 0
and
\mathbb{T}^{d}:=[-\pi,\pi]^{d}
be the torus, we get the following Green’s function by the discrete Fourier transform (see Section
2
).

(2)

G(x,t):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\;\frac{\sin(t\,\omega(\xi))}{\omega(\xi)}\,d\xi,\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \omega(\xi)=\left({\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2-2\cos\xi_{j})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
(x,t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
and
x\cdot\xi=\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}\xi_{j}
is the usual inner product.

To establish the dispersive estimate, we look for possible value of
\beta<0
such that

(3)

|G(x,t)|\leq C(1+|t|)^{\beta},\quad\forall\,(x,t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}},

where the constant
C
only depends on
d
(or
C=C(d)
, in short). The symbols
C,c
will be used throughout to denote positive constants, which may vary from one line to the next. Analogous results as (
3
) have been studied for other dispersive equations with Cauchy data such as the discrete Schrödinger equation (DS, in short)

i\partial_{t}u(x,t)+\Delta u(x,t)=0

and the discrete Klein-Gordon equation (DKG)

(4)

\partial_{t}^{2}u(x,t)-\Delta u(x,t)+m_{*}^{2}\,u(x,t)=0,

where
m_{*}>0
is the mass parameter. These equations are closely related to our model, that is, the discrete wave equation (DW), which is the vanishing mass limit (
m_{*}\rightarrow 0
) of the DKG.

For the DS, due to the special form of its Green’s function, one can separate variables to reduce the problem to the case
d=1
, and obtain the sharp decay rate of
|t|^{-\frac{d}{3}}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, see
[
39
, Theorem 3]
.
Unfortunately, the DKG and the DW fail to have the separation-of-variables property, which leads to more complicated analysis as dimension increases. For the DKG, A. Stefanov and P. G. Keverekidis
[
39
, Theorem 5]
established a sharp decay estimate of
|t|^{-\frac{1}{3}}
on
\mathbb{Z}
. Later, V. Borovyk and M. Goldberg
[
6
, Corollary 2.5]
computed a decay of
|t|^{-\frac{3}{4}}
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
.
Recently, J.-C. Cuenin and I. A. Ikromov
[
9
, Theorem 1]
extended the results to dimensions 2, 3 and 4.

However, we shall see that the situation in the DW is harder than that of the DKG.
Progress in the DW mainly comes from P. Schultz, who settled the cases
d=2
and 3 in
[
38
]
with decay rates
|t|^{-3/4}
and
|t|^{-7/6}
, respectively. He analyzed (
2
) as well as a related oscillatory integral,

(5)

I(v,t):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{it\phi(v,\xi)}\frac{1}{\omega(\xi)}\,d\xi,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\phi(v,\xi):=v\cdot\xi-\omega(\xi),

where
(v,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
. Notice that the phase and amplitude are not smooth at the origin, which brings some difficulties. In light of (
3
) and the relation
G(x,t)=-\mbox{Im}\,I(x/t,t)
(see (
14
) below), in many occasions it is more convenient to establish inequalities of the following type,

(6)

|I(v,t)|\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{\beta},\quad\forall\,(v,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}.

We first look for critical points of
\phi(v,\cdot)
, which suggests a partition in the velocity space of
v
into the following regions,

(7)

\displaystyle\mbox{the exterior}\;(|v|>1),\;\;\mbox{the vicinity}\;(|v|\approx 1)\;\;\mbox{and the interior}\;(|v|<1)

of the light cone, see also Section
3.1
. The first two regions are handled by
[
38
]
in all dimensions
d\geq 2
. However, the situation becomes complicated inside the light cone, in which the degenerate critical points appear. By this we mean Hess
{}_{\xi}\phi(v_{0},\xi_{0})
, i.e. the Hessian of
\phi(v_{0},\cdot)
at
\xi=\xi_{0}
, is singular, where
v_{0}
is some velocity in the light cone and the wave number
\xi_{0}
is a critical point of
\phi(v_{0},\cdot)
.
In this case, the stationary phase method breaks down and we have to consider each dimension one-by-one.

In this article, we mainly consider the degenerate cases inside the light cone. This problem can be viewed as the stability of oscillatory integral under phase (linear) permutations. More precisely, for any
v_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, since

(8)

\phi(v,\xi)=(v-v_{0})\cdot\xi+\phi(v_{0},\xi),

we first get the decay rate of (
5
) with
v=v_{0}
, then we prove that the estimate holds uniformly for
v
in some neighborhood of
v_{0}
. Finally, we get the uniform estimate in
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by a finite covering on the velocity space.

We notice that decay rates in
d=2,3,4
can be well interpreted by the Newton polyhedron of
\phi
, see Section
4.2
. Moreover, when

d=3,4
the decay is governed by a single velocity

v_{0}=\left(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2d}},\,\cdots,\,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2d}}\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

at which the most degenerate case appears, that is, the rank of Hess
{}_{\xi}\phi(v_{0},\xi_{0})
attains the minimum
1
, see Lemma
3.1
, (
23
) and
Table 1
. This fact may be useful in predicting decay rates in higher dimensions, and we obtain the decay rates for all odd
d\geq 3
in such cases, see Theorem
1.5
below.

The uniform estimate of oscillatory integral is noteworthy in itself. Roughly speaking, we consider the following integral

(9)

J(t,S+P,\,\psi):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{it(S(x)+P(x))}\psi(x)\,dx,

with proper phase
S
, amplitude
\psi
and perturbation
P
. A natural question is whether the decay of
J(t,S,\,\psi)
as
t\rightarrow\infty
could extend to
J(t,S+P,\,\psi)
when
P
is “small” enough. Unidimensional uniform estimate goes back to I. M. Vinogradov
[
43
]
and J. G. Van der Corput
[
8
]
. When
d=2
, the adapted coordinate system is important. A. N. Varchenko
[
42
]
proved the existence of such coordinate system for analytic functions (without multiple
components) in his pioneering work studying the connection between oscillatory integral and Newton polyhedra. Based on this, V. N. Karpushkin
[
27
]
gave an affirmative answer to the question above in the real-analytic setting. Later, Ikromov and D. Müller
[
24
]
extended the results to smooth phase of finite type when
P
is linear. Besides, it is also effective to analyze the singularities, see for instance Arnold et al.
[
1
]
and J. J. Duistermaat
[
12
]
.

In higher dimensions, however, examples in
[
42
]
give an negative answer to the question above and show that the adapted coordinates may not exist. Moreover, even for
P=0
, it is still extremely difficult to determine the precise asymptotic behaviour of (
9
) in many cases. Many results in such cases are motivated by Newton polyhedra, see e.g.
[
42
]
, D. H. Phong and E. M. Stein
[
35
]
and M.Greenblatt
[
17
]
. For more details about the Newton polyhedra, we refer to Section
4
.

Since the seminal paper of Schultz
[
38
]
on dispersive estimates for the DW on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with
d=2,3
, whether these results can be extended to higher dimensional lattices has been left as a major open problem. In this article, we extend the results of
[
38
]
to
d=4
, serving as the first step towards the above open problem. Besides, the most degenerate case in high dimensions and the Strichartz estimates are also studied for the first time. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists
C>0
such that

|G(x,t)|\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log(2+|t|),\quad\forall\,(x,t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}\times{\mathbb{R}}.

Remark 1.2
.

The estimate is sharp in the sense that there exist some vector
v_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
and a constant
C>0
such that (cf.
[
28
]
)

|I(v_{0},t)|\geqslant C\,t^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log t,\quad\mbox{as}\quad t\rightarrow+\infty.

Our proof strategy is as follows. First, for all
d\geq 2
we give the characterization of the degenerate critical points of
\phi
. Then we give a decomposition of the phase in the most degenerate case, which is also useful in establishing the dispersive inequality for all odd
d\geq 3
. Finally, when
d=4
, using proper coordinate changes, we reduce the phase to simple polynomials by considering several cases separately. Then we finish by combining the singularity theory with some results on stability of the oscillatory integral, which firstly appeared in
[
26
]
.

In the terminology of Arnold
[
1
]
, the stable singularities which will appear in the proof are
A_{k}
(
k\geq 1
) and
D_{4}^{-}
. Besides, the polynomial
\xi_{1}\xi_{2}\xi_{3}
plays an important role in the most degenerate case. Moreover, we point out that the pattern of our proof may be useful in the study of dispersive equations on general graphs.

Theorem
1.1
directly leads to the
l^{p}\rightarrow l^{q}
estimates.
In the sequel, for any
a>0
, the notation “
a^{-}
” means that one can choose any
\epsilon>0
to replace
a
with
a-\epsilon
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
d=4
and
u
be the solution to (
1
) with
F,g\equiv 0
. If
1\leqslant p<q\leqslant+\infty
with
1/p-1/q\geqslant 1/2
, then there exists
C=C(p,q)
such that

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{l^{q}}\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{-\beta_{p,q}}\|f\|_{l^{p}},\ \ \forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \beta_{p,q}=3^{-}\left(\tfrac{1}{p}-\tfrac{1}{q}-\tfrac{1}{2}\right).

Furthermore, if
(1/p,1/q)
lies on the segment with vertices
(3/4,1/2)
and
(1,0)
, then there exists
C=C(p,q)
such that

\|u(t,\cdot)\|_{l^{q}}\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{-\zeta_{q}}\|f\|_{l^{p}},\ \ \forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}},\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \zeta_{q}=\left(\tfrac{3}{2}\right)^{-}\left(1-\tfrac{2}{q}\right).

This result can be used to prove the global existence of the solution for nonlinear equations with power type nonlinearity, see Section
5.3
.

Remark that the DKG is considered in
[
9
]
for
d=2,3,4
with
a proof relying also on the analysis of singularities. The analog of (
5
) is

(10)

\widetilde{I}(v,t)=\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{it(v\cdot\xi-\widetilde{\omega}(\xi))}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\omega}(\xi)}\,d\xi,\quad\mbox{with}\quad\widetilde{\omega}(\xi)=\left({m_{*}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}(2-2\cos\xi_{j})}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
m_{*}
is as in (
4
). The situation of the DW is more complicated than that of the DKG, which is essentially because that the phase and amplitude in (
10
) are more regular compared with (
5
).
For the same reason, in the following Strichartz estimates we need to overcome more difficulties caused by the operator
\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\Delta}}
, while the corresponding operator for the DKG, i.e.
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol{1}-\Delta}}
, is easier to deal with, see
[
9
]
and Section
5.2
. In what follows, for any
1\leq p\leq+\infty
, let
p^{\prime}
be the conjugate index of
p
.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
d=4
and
u
be the solution to (
1
). If indices
q,r,\widetilde{q},\widetilde{r}
satisfy

(11)

q,r,\tilde{q},\tilde{r}\geqslant 2,\quad\frac{1}{q}<\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{\tilde{q}}<\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{\tilde{r}}\right),

then there exists
C=C(q,r,\widetilde{q},\widetilde{r})
such that

\|u\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}\leqslant C\left(\|g\|_{l^{2}}+\|f\|_{l^{\frac{4}{3}}}+\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}l^{\frac{4\tilde{r}^{\prime}}{4+\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}}\right)

This result is proved by a combination of Theorem
1.1
, the
l^{p}
boundedness of
\frac{1}{\sqrt{-\Delta}}
and the result in M. Keel and T. Tao
[
29
]
.
The analog in the case
d=3
can be proved similarly, see Theorem
5.8
. Moreover, there is another Strichartz estimate following directly from Theorem
1.3
, see Theorem
5.2
and Remark
5.7
.

Most of our results focus on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}
. However, in higher dimensions, we have the following estimates by analyzing a special class of Newton polyhedra.

Theorem 1.5
.

For any odd
d\geq 3
, there exists
C=C(d)>0
such that

(12)

|I(v_{0},t)|\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{2d+1}{6}},\quad\forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where
v_{0}=(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2d}},\cdots,\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2d}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

As we mentioned before, when
d=3,4
, the decay rates are determined by the most degenerate case. We believe that this claim also holds for
d\geq 5
and (
12
) holds uniformly in
v_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, which is better than
[
9
, Remark 1(b)]
. They conjectured an estimate of order
|t|^{-\frac{2d+1}{6}}\log^{d-4}|t|
for
d\geq 5
.

This paper is organized as follows. In Section
2.1
we recall basic concepts about the DW, then we give complete proofs for results on uniform estimates of the ocsillatory integrals in Section
2.2
. Section
3
is devoted to proving Theorem
1.1
. In Section
4.1
we recall some facts of Newton polyhedra, and we give application to the DW in Section
4.2
, Theorem
1.5
is proved in Section
4.3
. In Section
5
, we prove Theorem
1.3
and Theorem
1.4
, then we give applications to the nonlinear equations.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Basics on
l^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
and the DW

Let
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
be the standard integer lattice graph in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For
p\in[1,\infty]
,
l^{p}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
is the
l^{p}
-space of functions on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with respect to the counting measure, which is a Banach space with the norm

||h||_{l^{p}}:=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left(\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|h(x)|^{p}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}},\ p\in[1,\infty),\\
&\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|h(x)|,\ p=\infty.\end{aligned}\right.

We shall also use
|h|_{p}
to denote the
l^{p}
norm of
h
for notational convenience. For
1\leqslant q,r<\infty
, the mixed space-time Lebesgue spaces
L^{q}_{t}l^{r}
are Banach spaces endowed with the norms

||F||_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}:=\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\left(\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|F(x,t)|^{r}\right)^{\frac{q}{r}}dt\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

with natural modifications for the case
q=\infty
or
r=\infty
. Moreover, for proper functions
h_{1},h_{2}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{d}
we define the convolution product as

h_{1}*h_{2}(x):=\sum_{y\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}h_{1}(x-y)h_{2}(y),\quad\forall\,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}.

The
l^{p}
spaces are analogous to the
L^{p}
spaces of functions defined on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Many results of the
L^{p}
spaces extend to the lattice such as the Hölder inequality, Young’s inequality for convolution and Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem. One major difference is that the
l^{p}
spaces are nested:

l^{p}\subset l^{q}
,
\forall\,1\leqslant p\leqslant q\leqslant\infty
.

The discrete Fourier transform of a proper function
h
is given by

\mathcal{F}(h)(\xi)=\hat{h}(\xi):=\sum_{x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}e^{-i\xi\cdot x}h(x),\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d},

while the inverse transform is

\mathcal{F}^{-1}(h)(x)=\check{h}(x):=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}h(\xi)d\xi,\quad\forall\,x\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}.

See e.g.
[
44
]
for more facts about discrete Fourier analysis. We may use the same notation to denote the Fourier transform and the inverse transform of a distrbution on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Applying the Fourier transform to both sides of (
1
), we get

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}^{2}\hat{u}(\xi,t)+\omega(\xi)^{2}\,\hat{u}(\xi,t)=0,\\
&\hat{u}(\xi,0)=\hat{g}(\xi),~~\partial_{t}\hat{u}(\xi,0)=\hat{f}(\xi),\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d}.\end{aligned}\right.

The solution to this ordinary differential equation is

\hat{u}(\xi,t)=\cos(t\omega)\hat{g}(\xi)+\frac{\sin(t\omega)}{\omega}\hat{f}(\xi),\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d},

which gives that

u(x,t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{i\xi\cdot x}\left(\cos(t\omega)\hat{g}(\xi)+\frac{\sin(t\omega)}{\omega}\hat{f}(\xi)\right)d\xi,\quad\forall\,(x,t)\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}.

In the notion of operator theory, for any
f,g\in l^{2}
and
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

(13)

u(t)=\cos(t\sqrt{-\Delta})g+\frac{\sin(t\sqrt{-\Delta})}{\sqrt{-\Delta}}f.

From now on we only consider the solution for zero initial position and a given initial velocity, i.e.
g\equiv 0
, unless otherwise stated. The other case can be treated similarly, see Section
5.2
. Then we get
u=f*G
with the Green’s function
G(x,t)
defined in (
2
). Moreover, for
x=vt
we have

(14)

G(x,t)=-\mbox{Im}\,I(v,t)

by the fact that
\omega(\xi)=\omega(-\xi)
and
I(v,t)=I(-v,t)
(cf. (
5
)).

2.2.
Results on uniform estimates.

We recall some notions and results which were initiated from
[
26
]
.
In the sequel, let
B_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\xi,r)
(resp.
B_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(\xi,r)
) be the usual open ball in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(resp.
{\mathbb{C}}^{d}
) with center
\xi
and radius
r
, while
\overline{B}_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\xi,r)
(resp.
\overline{B}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(\xi,r)
) denotes its closure.

Definition 2.1
.

For any
r,s>0
, the space
\mathcal{H}_{r}(s)
is defined as

\mathcal{H}_{r}(s):=\left\{P:\ \begin{aligned} &P\ \mbox{is holomorphic on}\ B_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(0,r)\ \mbox{and continuous}\\
&\mbox{on}\ \overline{B}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(0,r),\ \mbox{and}\ |P({w})|<s,\ \forall\,{w}\in\overline{B}_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(0,r)\end{aligned}\ \right\}.

Definition 2.2
.

Let
h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be real-analytic at 0. We write

M(h)\curlyeqprec(\beta,p)\ \ \mbox{for some}\ (\beta,p)\in(-\infty,0]\times{\mathbb{N}},

if for any
r>0
sufficiently small, there exist
\epsilon>0
,
C>0
and a neighbourhood
A\subset B_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(0,r)
of the origin such that

(15)

|J(t,h+P,\psi)|\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{\beta}\log^{p}(|t|+2)\|\psi\|_{C^{N}(A)},\ \forall\,(t,\psi,P)\in{\mathbb{R}}\times C_{0}^{\infty}(A)\times\mathcal{H}_{r}(\epsilon),

where
J
is as in (
9
),
N=N(h)\in{\mathbb{N}}
and

\|\psi\|_{C^{N}(A)}=\sup\big\{|\partial^{\gamma}\psi(\xi)|:\xi\in A,\,\gamma\in{\mathbb{N}}^{d},|\gamma|\leq N\big\}.

Some conventions are in order. Let
h,h_{1},h_{2}
be proper functions,
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and
(\beta_{j},p_{j})\in(-\infty,0]\times{\mathbb{N}}
for
j=1,2
. Then

(1)

we write
M(h,\xi)\curlyeqprec(\beta_{1},p_{1})
, if

M(\boldsymbol{\tau}_{\xi}h)\curlyeqprec(\beta_{1},p_{1}),\quad\mbox{where}\ \ \boldsymbol{\tau}_{\xi}h(y)=h(y+\xi),\ \ \forall\,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d};

(2)

we write
M(h_{2})\curlyeqprec M(h_{1})+(\beta_{1},p_{1})
, if

M(h_{1})\curlyeqprec(\beta_{1},p_{1})\quad\mbox{implies that}\ \ M(h_{2})\curlyeqprec(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2},p_{1}+p_{2});

(3)

we write
M(h_{2})\curlyeqprec M(h_{1})
, if
M(h_{2})\curlyeqprec M(h_{1})+(0,0)
;

(4)

we write
M(h)+(\beta_{1},p_{1})\curlyeqprec(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2},p_{1}+p_{2})
, if
M(h)\curlyeqprec(\beta_{2},p_{2})
.

Let
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},\cdots,\alpha_{d})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
be a weight with
\alpha_{j}>0
for all
j
.
For any
c>0
, the associated one-parameter family of dilations is defined as

\delta_{c}^{\alpha}(\xi):=(c^{\alpha_{1}}\xi_{1},\cdots,c^{\alpha_{d}}\xi_{d}),\quad\forall\,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Definition 2.3
.

A polynomial
h
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is called
\alpha
-homogeneous of degree
\varrho\geq 0
, if

h\circ\delta_{c}^{\alpha}(\xi)=c^{\varrho}h(\xi),\quad\forall\,(\xi,c)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times(0,+\infty).

Let
\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,d}
be the set of
\alpha
-homogeneous polynomials of degree
1
, and
H_{\alpha,d}
be the set of functions real-analytic at 0 with the associated Taylor’s series having the form
\sum_{\gamma\cdot\alpha>1}a_{\gamma}\xi^{\gamma}
, i.e. each monomial is
\alpha
-homogeneous of degree greater than 1.

The following useful lemmas first appeared in
[

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
h:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be real analytic at

\nabla h(0)\neq 0

M(h)\curlyeqprec(-n,0),\quad\forall\,n\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Lemma

can be proved directly through integrating by parts.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
h\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,d}
and
P\in H_{\alpha,d}
, then

M(h+P)\curlyeqprec M(h).

Proof.

The idea is from
[
26
, Lemma 1]
. Firstly, for any
c>0
, we define

\mathbf{P}(c,\alpha):=\left\{{w}=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{d})\in{\mathbb{C}}^{d}:|w_{j}|<\frac{c^{\alpha_{j}}}{2}\right\}.

Assume that
M(h)\curlyeqprec(\beta,p)
, then for any
r>0
there exist

\epsilon_{r}>0
,
C_{r}>0
and
A_{r}
as in Definition
2.2
such that
\mathbf{P}(c_{0},\alpha)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\subset A_{r}
for some
c_{0}=c_{0}({r})>0
.

By the Cauchy inequality (cf.
[
20
, Theorem 2.2.7]
), there exists
P_{0}>0
such that

P(z)=\sum_{\alpha\cdot\beta>1}a_{\beta}z^{\beta}\ \ \mbox{on}\ \ B_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(0,r),\ \ \mbox{with}\ \ |a_{\beta}|\leq\frac{P_{0}}{r^{|\beta|}}\ \ \mbox{for all}\ \beta.

Since
\alpha_{j}>0
for all

, there exists
\sigma>0
such that
\alpha\cdot\beta-1\geq\sigma|\beta|
for all
\beta

\alpha\cdot\beta>1
. Thus we can find
s=s(\epsilon_{r})>0
such that
\mathbf{P}(s,\alpha)\subset B_{{\mathbb{C}}^{d}}(0,r)
and then

\left|\frac{c_{0}}{s}\,P\circ\delta_{sc_{0}^{-1}}^{\alpha}(\xi)\right|\leq P_{0}\sum_{\alpha\cdot\beta>1}\left(\frac{s}{c_{0}}\right)^{\alpha\cdot\beta-1}\leq P_{0}\sum_{|\beta|\geq 2}\left(\frac{s}{c_{0}}\right)^{\sigma|\beta|}\leq\frac{\epsilon_{r}}{2},\ \ \forall\,\xi\in\mathbf{P}(c_{0},\alpha).

By the same token, there exists
\widetilde{\epsilon}>0
such that

c_{0}s^{-1}\Upsilon\circ\delta_{sc_{0}^{-1}}^{\alpha}\in\mathcal{H}_{r}(\epsilon_{r}/2),\quad\forall\,\Upsilon\in\mathcal{H}_{r}(\widetilde{\epsilon}).

Let
\widetilde{A}=\mathbf{P}(s,\alpha)\cap{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, since
h\in\mathcal{E}_{\alpha,d}
, for any
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\widetilde{A})
we have

\mathbf{I}:=J(t,h+P+\Upsilon,\psi)=\left(\frac{s}{c_{0}}\right)^{|\alpha|}J\left(tsc_{0}^{-1},h+c_{0}s^{-1}\left(P_{\delta}+\Upsilon_{\delta}\right),\psi_{\delta}\right)

by a change of coordinates, where we use notation
\varphi_{\delta}=\varphi\circ\delta_{sc_{0}^{-1}}^{\alpha}
with
\varphi=P,

\Upsilon
or
\psi
. Since supp

and
M(h)\curlyeqprec(\beta,p)
, we get

\displaystyle|\,\mathbf{I}\,|

\displaystyle\leq C_{r}\left(\frac{s}{c_{0}}\right)^{|\alpha|}\left(1+|tsc_{0}^{-1}|\right)^{\beta}\log^{p}(2+|tsc_{0}^{-1}|)\,\|\psi_{1}\|_{C^{N}}\leq\widetilde{C}(1+|t|)^{\beta}\log^{p}(2+|t|)\|\psi\|_{C^{N}},

where
\widetilde{C}=C_{r}\left(sc_{0}^{-1}\right)^{|\alpha|+\beta}

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

The following arguments work for all dimensions

, we shall focus on the case

in Section

.

3.1.
Classification of the critical points

In view of (
14
), we first give a detailed analysis of (
5
). We choose a nonnegative function
\eta\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with support in
(-2\pi,2\pi)^{d}
, which is non-vanishing on some neighborhood of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
such that

\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\eta(\xi+2\pi x)=1,\quad\forall\,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Let
\omega
be as in (
2
), since it is periodic, we have

(17)

I(v,t)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{it\phi(v,\xi)}\,\frac{\eta(\xi+2\pi x)}{\omega(\xi)}d\xi=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}e^{it\phi(v,\xi)}\frac{\eta(\xi)}{\omega(\xi)}d\xi.

Noting that both the phase and amplitude in (
17
) have singularity at 0, a direct calculation gives

(18)

\nabla\omega(\xi)=\frac{1}{\omega(\xi)}\big(\sin\xi_{1},\,\cdots,\,\sin\xi_{d}\big),\quad\forall\,\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{d})\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\backslash\{0\}.

For any
v\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we define

\mathcal{C}_{v}:=\Big\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\backslash\{0\}:\nabla_{\xi}\phi(v,\xi)=0\Big\},

where
\nabla_{\xi}\phi(v,\xi)
is the gradient of
\phi
in
\xi
.
We also define

(19)

\mathbf{V}(\xi):=\left({\sum_{j=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\xi_{j}}\right)\left({\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(2-2\cos\xi_{j}\right)}\right)^{-1},\quad\forall\,\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\backslash\{0\}.

If
\mathcal{C}_{v}\neq\emptyset
, by (
18
) and the expression of
\phi
, for any
\xi\in\mathcal{C}_{v}
, it holds that
|v|^{2}=\mathbf{V}(\xi)<1
.
Therefore,
\mathcal{C}_{v}=\emptyset
for any
v\in B_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(0,1)^{c}
. On the other hand,
[
37
, Proposition 5.6]
gives that
\mathcal{C}_{v}\neq\emptyset
for any
v\in B_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(0,1)
. So if we define

\Sigma_{k}:=\Big\{\xi\in\mathbb{T}^{d}\backslash\{0\}:\mbox{corank\,Hess}\,\omega(\xi)=k\Big\}\,\bigcap\left(\bigcup_{v\in B_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(0,1)}\,\mathcal{C}_{v}\right)\quad\mbox{for}\ \ k=0,\cdots,d,

then by the fact

\mbox{Hess}_{\xi}\phi(v,\xi)=-\mbox{Hess}\,\omega(\xi),\quad\forall\,(v,\xi)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

the set of degenerate critical points and the corresponding velocity set can be formulated as

(20)

\Sigma:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{d}\Sigma_{k}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\Omega:=\bigcup_{k=1}^{d}\Omega_{k},\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \Omega_{k}=\nabla\omega(\Sigma_{k}),\ \ k=0,\cdots,d.

Here
\nabla\omega(U)
(resp.
(\nabla\omega)^{-1}(U)
) is the image (resp. preimage) of
U
under the map
\nabla\omega
, and for simplicity we do not specify the dependence of these notations on
d
.

Now we give the characterization of
\Sigma
and some useful properties of the velocity sets. Without loss of generality, only the first quadrant
[0,\pi]^{d}
is considered by symmetry.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
d\geq 2
, then

(i)

\Sigma_{d}=\emptyset
, and
\Sigma_{1}=\Sigma_{1}^{\prime}\cup\Sigma_{1}^{\prime\prime}
, where
\Sigma_{1}^{\prime\prime}
is the set of
\xi
with exactly two components equal to
\frac{\pi}{2}
, while

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}^{\prime}=\left\{\xi\in[0,\pi]^{d}\backslash\{0\}:\sum_{j=1}^{d}\left(\cos\xi_{j}+\sec\xi_{j}\right)=2d,\ \xi_{j}\neq\frac{\pi}{2}\ \mbox{for all}\ j\right\}.

(ii)

If
d\geq 3
, for any
2\leq j\leq d-1
,
\Sigma_{j}
consists of
\xi
with exactly (
j+1
) components equal to
\frac{\pi}{2}
.

Proof.

For any
(\xi,\lambda)\in(\mathbb{T}^{d}\backslash\{0\})\times{\mathbb{R}}
, a direct computation yields

\mathcal{D}(\lambda,\xi)=\frac{1}{\omega(\xi)^{3}}\left(\omega(\xi)^{3}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\left(\frac{\cos\xi_{j}}{\omega(\xi)}-\lambda\right)-\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sin^{2}\xi_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}\left(\frac{\cos\xi_{j}}{\omega(\xi)}-\lambda\right)\right),

where
\mathcal{D}(\lambda,\xi)=\mbox{det}(\mbox{Hess}\,\omega(\xi)-\lambda\mathbb{I}_{d})
and
\mathbb{I}_{d}
is the identity matrix.

First, if
\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{d})
with

k
components equal to
\frac{\pi}{2}
for some
1\leq k\leq d
, we assume that
\xi_{j}=\frac{\pi}{2}
for
1\leq j\leq k
without loss of generality. If
k=d
, it is clear that

\mathcal{D}(\lambda,\xi)=(-1)^{d}\lambda^{d-1}(\lambda+\omega(\xi)^{-3}\,d).

If
k<d
, then
\xi_{l}\neq\frac{\pi}{2}
for
k+1\leq l\leq d
and
\mathcal{D}(\lambda,\xi)=\lambda^{k-1}\mathcal{D}_{1}(\lambda,\xi)
, where

\mathcal{D}_{1}(\lambda,\xi)=\frac{(-1)^{k}}{\omega(\xi)^{d-k}}\left(\lambda\omega(\xi)^{2}+\frac{k}{\omega(\xi)}-\lambda\sum_{j=k+1}^{d}\frac{\sin^{2}\xi_{j}}{\cos\xi_{j}-\lambda\omega(\xi)}\right)\prod_{j=k+1}^{d}\left(\cos\xi_{j}-\lambda\omega(\xi)\right),

which makes sense near
\lambda=0
, and
\mathcal{D}_{1}(0,\xi)\neq 0
.

Next, if
\xi=(\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{d})
with
\xi_{j}\neq\frac{\pi}{2}
for all
j
, then we get
\mathcal{D}(0,\xi)=0
if and only if the equation
\omega(\xi)^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{\sin^{2}\xi_{j}}{\cos\xi_{j}}
holds. In this case we have

\mathcal{D}(\lambda,\xi)=\lambda\,\mathcal{D}_{2}(\lambda,\xi),\quad\mbox{with}\ \ \mathcal{D}_{2}(\lambda,\xi)=-\frac{1}{\omega(\xi)^{d+1}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\frac{\sin^{2}\xi_{i}}{\cos\xi_{i}}\prod_{j\neq i}\left(\cos\xi_{j}-\lambda\omega(\xi)\right).

Then it is clear that
\mathcal{D}_{2}(0,\xi)\neq 0
. Collecting all these facts, we complete the proof.

4.
Newton Polyhedra

4.1.
Basic concepts and results

We recall some basics of Newton polyhedra, see also
[
1
,
24
,
42
]
. For the concepts in convex analysis and polytopes, we refer to
[
5
,
7
]
.

Let
S
be a function on
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
and real-analytic at 0, we shall always assume that

(30)

S(0)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\nabla S(0)=0.

Consider the associated Taylor series at 0,

(31)

S(\xi)=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{d}}s_{\gamma}\,\xi^{\gamma}.

The set

\mathcal{T}(S):=\{\gamma\in\mathbb{N}^{d}:s_{\gamma}\neq 0\}
is called the Taylor support. The
Newton polyhedron

\mathcal{N}(S)
is the convex hull of the set

\bigcup_{\gamma\,\in\,\mathcal{T}(S)}\left(\gamma+\mathbb{R}^{d}_{+}\right),\quad\mbox{where}\ \ {\mathbb{R}}^{d}_{+}=\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\xi_{j}\geq 0,\,j=1,\cdots,d\}.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be a face of
\mathcal{N}(S)
, we call

S_{\mathcal{P}}(\xi):=\sum_{\gamma\in\mathcal{P}}s_{\gamma}\,\xi^{\gamma}

the
\mathcal{P}
-part of the series in (
31
). Moreover, we say
S
is
\mathbb{R}
-nondegenerate if for any compact face
\mathcal{P}
,
\nabla S_{\mathcal{P}}
is nonvanishing on
(\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\})^{d}
, that is,

(32)

\bigcap_{j=1}^{d}\big\{\xi:{\partial_{j}S_{\mathcal{P}}}(\xi)=0\big\}\ \subset\ \,\bigcup_{j=1}^{d}\big\{\xi:\xi_{j}=0\big\}.

If
\mathcal{T}(S)\neq\emptyset
, the Newton distance
d_{S}
and the center
\boldsymbol{d}_{S}
are defined as

d_{S}=\inf\{\varrho>0:(\varrho,\varrho,\cdots,\varrho)\in\mathcal{N}(S)\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\boldsymbol{d}_{S}:=(d_{S},d_{S},\cdots,d_{S})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

The principle face
\pi_{S}
of
\mathcal{N}(S)
is the face of minimal dimension containing

\boldsymbol{d}_{S}
and
k_{S}:=d-\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\pi_{S})
, where
\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(U)
is the affine dimension of
U
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. The
\pi_{S}
-part, denoted by
S_{\pi}
, is called the principle part of
S
.

Since
d_{S}
depends on the choice of the coordinate systems, the height of
S
is given by

h_{S}:=\sup\{d_{S,\xi}\},

where the supremum is taken over all local analytic coordinate systems
\xi
which preserves the origin, and
d_{S,\xi}
is the Newton distance in coordinates
\xi
. A given coordinate system
\xi_{a}
is said to be adapted to
S
if
d_{S,\xi_{a}}=h_{S}
.

In our setting, the following result, derived by
[
42
, Proposition 0.7, 0.8]
, can be used to recognize whether a given coordinate system is adapted.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
d=2
, if
\boldsymbol{d}_{S}
lies on a compact face
\Gamma
of
\mathcal{N}(S)
with
\Gamma\subset\{\xi:a_{1}\xi_{1}+\xi_{2}=a_{2}\}
for some
a_{1},a_{2}\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
then the coordinate system is adapted if
S_{\Gamma}(\cdot\,,1)
does not have a real root of multiplicity larger than
\frac{a_{2}}{1+a_{1}}
.

Now let
S
be as in (
30
) and
J(t,S,\psi)
be as in (
9
),
where
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with support near the origin. Then the following asymptotic expansion holds (cf. e.g.
[
1
, pp. 181]
),

(33)

J(t,S,\psi)\approx\sum_{\tau}\sum_{\rho=0}^{d-1}c_{\tau,\rho,\psi}\,t^{\tau}\log^{\rho}t,\quad\mbox{as}\ \ t\rightarrow+\infty,

where
\tau
runs through finitely many arithmetic progressions not depending on
\psi
, which consists of negative rational numbers.

Let
(\tau_{S},\rho_{S})
be the maximum over all pairs
(\tau,\rho)
in (
33
) under the
lexicographic ordering such that for any neighborhood
U
of the origin, there exists
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}(U)
for which
c_{\tau_{S},\rho_{S},\psi}\neq 0
. We call
\tau_{S}
the oscillation index of
S
at 0 and
\rho_{S}
its multiplicity.

The following useful result is a consequence of the main theorem in Varchenko
[
42
]
,
see also Gilula
[
15
, Theorem 2.3]
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
S
be
\mathbb{R}
-nondegenerate and (
30
) hold, then
\tau_{S}\leq-{d_{S}}^{-1}
and
\rho_{S}\leq k_{S}-1
, that is, for any
\psi\in C_{0}^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
with support near the origin, there exists
C>0
such that

|J(t,S,\psi)|\leqslant C(1+|t|)^{-\frac{1}{d_{S}}}\log^{k_{S}-1}(2+|t|),\ \ \forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Moreover, if
d_{S}>1
, then
\tau_{S}=-d_{S}^{-1}
.

If
d=2
,
[
27
, Theorem 2.1]
and
[
42
, Theorem 0.6]
give the following stronger result:

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
S:\mathbb{R}^{2}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be as in (
30
), then there exist coordinate systems that are adapted to
S
.
Moreover,
M(S)\curlyeqprec(\tau_{S},\rho_{S})
and
\tau_{S}=-h_{S}^{-1}.

Firstly, note that the quadratic term
z_{d}^{2}
and the terms with the even index only appear once in (
35
).
For any compact face
\Gamma
on
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})
, the
\Gamma
-part
\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}
containing the term
z_{2j-1}z_{2j}^{2}
for some
1\leq j\leq\mathbf{k}
or the term
z_{d}^{2}
must be
{\mathbb{R}}
-nondegenerate, since
\partial_{2j}\mathbf{S}_{\Gamma}(z)=2z_{2j-1}z_{2j}
and (
32
) is fulfilled.

Then we reduce the problem to consider the left terms in
Y
, which is the polynomial
\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}
(recall (
28
)) up to a renumbering of the coordinates. Now we show that
\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}
is
{\mathbb{R}}
-nondegenerate.

For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{k}
, we define the
(k-1)
-simplex

\Theta_{k-1}^{\gamma}:=\left\{w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{k}_{+}:w\cdot\gamma=1\right\}.

Notice that
\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}
is related to the compact fact
\Theta_{\mathbf{k}-1}^{\gamma_{1}}
, where
\gamma_{1}=(\tfrac{1}{3},\cdots,\tfrac{1}{3})
. We begin with
\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}
itself. Since
\nabla\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}(w)=0
is equivalent to the equation

4\left(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}w_{j}\right)^{2}=w_{1}^{2}=w_{2}^{2}=\cdots=w_{\mathbf{k}}^{2},

which has only zero solution by a direct computation, then it suffices to consider other compact faces with dimension less than
\mathbf{k}-1
.
We claim that for any such face
\mathcal{P}
, the
\mathcal{P}
-part
(\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}})_{\mathcal{P}}
has similar expression as
\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}
.

Indeed, by the representation theorem (cf. e.g.
[
5
, Page 68]
), we have

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}})=\Theta_{\mathbf{k}-1}^{\gamma_{1}}+{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{\mathbf{k}}.

By
[
7
, Theorem 12.1]
, we know that any vertex of
\mathcal{P}
is also a vertex of
\Theta_{\mathbf{k}-1}^{\gamma_{1}}
, the vertices of
which are

\{3{e_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}
. Here
e_{j}
(
1\leq j\leq\mathbf{k}
) denotes the standard coordinate vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{\mathbf{k}}
.
Therefore, without loss of generality we can assume that the vertices of
\mathcal{P}
is
\{3{e_{j}}\}_{j=1}^{n}
for some
1\leq n<\mathbf{k}
. Then it is clear that
(\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}})_{\mathcal{P}}=\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{n}
, and by induction we know
\mathbf{Q}_{4,1}^{\mathbf{k}}
is
{\mathbb{R}}
-nondegenerate, so is
\mathbf{S}
.

Now we prove that for every compact face
\mathcal{W}
of
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\phi})
, it holds that
\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathcal{W}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{W}}
. By (
34
) and a direct computation, it suffices to prove that no vertex of
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\phi})
comes from the terms

T_{1}=z_{d}(\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}z_{2j-1})^{2}

and

\displaystyle T_{2N+1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}\Big((z_{2j-1}-z_{2j})^{2N+1}+(z_{2j-1}+z_{2j})^{2N+1}\Big)=\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}\sum_{m=1}^{N+1}c_{m,N}\,z_{2j-1}^{2m-1}\,z_{2j}^{2(N-m+1)}

for any
N\geq 2
, where we also used the expression of
R\circ\Psi
as in the proof of Lemma
3.6
.
One easily sees that
\mathcal{T}(T_{2N+1})\subset\mbox{ri}\left(\mathcal{N}(Y)\right)
, i.e. the relative interior of
\mathcal{N}(Y)
.
As for
T_{1}
,
we consider an unbounded face of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}_{\mathbf{S}}

\displaystyle:=\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}:\xi_{2j}=0,\,1\leq j\leq\mathbf{k}\}\cap\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})

\displaystyle=\left\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}:\frac{\xi_{d}}{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}\frac{\xi_{2j-1}}{3}\geq 1,\,\xi_{2i}=0,\,1\leq i\leq\mathbf{k}\right\}.

Then it is clear that
\mathcal{T}(T_{1})\subset\mbox{ri}(\mathcal{U}_{\mathbf{S}})
.
Therefore, the term
R\circ\Psi
is negligible, which means that
\widetilde{\phi}_{\mathcal{W}}=\mathbf{S}_{\mathcal{W}}
for any compact face
\mathcal{W}
of
\mathcal{N}(\widetilde{\phi})
, so

\widetilde{\phi}
is
{\mathbb{R}}
-nondegenerate.

Step 3.

Now we prove
d_{\mathbf{S}}=\frac{6}{2d+1}
and
k_{\mathbf{S}}=1
. Let

A_{2j}=\boldsymbol{e_{2j-1}}+2\boldsymbol{e_{2j}},\quad A_{2j-1}=3\boldsymbol{e_{2j-1}}\quad\mbox{for}\ \ 1\leq j\leq\mathbf{k},\quad\mbox{and}\ \ A_{d}=2\boldsymbol{e_{d}}.

Then
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}\subset\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{S})
, and for any
\lambda>0
, we have

\frac{\lambda}{2}\left(A_{d}+\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}A_{2j}\right)+\frac{\lambda}{6}\,\sum_{j=1}^{\mathbf{k}}A_{2j-1}=\left(\lambda,\cdots,\lambda\right)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Let
\lambda_{0}=\frac{6}{2d+1}
, then
(\mathbf{k}+1)\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2}+\mathbf{k}\frac{\lambda_{0}}{6}=1
and
\boldsymbol{\lambda_{0}}:=(\lambda_{0},\cdots,\lambda_{0})
is in the convex combination of
\{A_{k}\}_{k=1}^{d}
with strictly positive coefficients. In view of the convexity of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})
and the definition, we know
d_{\mathbf{S}}\leq\lambda_{0}
. However, since
\mathcal{T}(\mathbf{S})\subset\Theta_{d-1}^{\boldsymbol{w_{d}}}
,
we know
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})\subset\Theta_{d-1}^{\boldsymbol{w_{d}}}+{\mathbb{R}}_{+}^{d}
. Noting that

\Big\{(\varrho,\cdots,\varrho)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\varrho>0\Big\}\,\cap\,\Theta_{d-1}^{\boldsymbol{w_{d}}}=(\lambda_{0},\cdots,\lambda_{0})=:\boldsymbol{\lambda_{0}},

we get
d_{\mathbf{S}}\geq\lambda_{0}
, and thus
d_{\mathbf{S}}=\lambda_{0}=\frac{6}{2d+1}
.

Now we prove that
k_{\mathbf{S}}=1
. Notice that
\mathcal{A}=\Theta_{d-1}^{\boldsymbol{w_{d}}}\cap\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})
is a compact face of
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{S})
, while
\{A_{j}\}_{j=1}^{d}\subset\mathcal{A}
is linearly independent in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, so it is also affinely independent. Thus
\dim_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}(\mathcal{A})=d-1
, then by
[
7
, Theorem 3.5, Exercises 3.1]
, we know

\boldsymbol{\lambda_{0}}\in\mbox{ri}\,(\mathcal{A})
, which shows that
k_{\mathbf{S}}=1
.

In conclusion, we finish the proof of Theorem
1.5
by Theorem
4.2
.

From the proof, for odd

5.
Space-time Estimates and nonlinear equation

In this section, as a convention, for a function
h=h(x,t)
on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}\times{\mathbb{R}}
, we write
h=h(t)
for simplicity. Moerover, for two positive functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, we write
h_{1}\lesssim h_{2}
if there exists
C>0
independent of
t\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
h_{1}\leq Ch_{2}
, other dependence of the constants may be ignored.

5.1.
l^{p}\rightarrow l^{q}
estimates

In this part we give the proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

By Theorem
1.1
and the Plancherel theorem, we have

|G(t)|_{{\infty}}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-(\frac{3}{2})^{-}}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\ |G(t)|_{2}\lesssim 1.

Therefore, by interpolation we deduce that for any
2\leqslant k\leqslant\infty
, we have

(36)

|G(t)|_{k}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\zeta_{k}}.

By Young’s inequality, for
r\geq 2
satisfying

1+\frac{1}{q}=\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{p}
, it holds that

\displaystyle|u(t)|_{q}=|G(t)*f|_{q}\leqslant|G(t)|_{r}|f|_{p}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\zeta_{r}}|f|_{p}=(1+|t|)^{-\beta_{p,q}}|f|_{p}.

For the second assertion of Theorem
1.3
, we use the Sobolev embedding (cf. e.g.
[
22
, Theorem 3.6]
),
for any
g\in l^{2}
, we have

(37)

\displaystyle|G(t)*g|_{4}

\displaystyle\lesssim|\nabla(G(t)*g)|_{2}=\Big|\mathcal{F}\left(\nabla(G(t)*g)\right)\Big|_{2}=\Big|\omega\cdot\mathcal{F}({G(t)*g})\Big|_{2}

\displaystyle=\Big|\omega\cdot\widehat{G}(t)\cdot\widehat{g}\Big|_{2}=\Big|\omega\cdot\frac{\sin t\omega}{\omega}\cdot\widehat{g}\Big|_{2}=\Big|\sin t\omega\cdot\widehat{g}\Big|_{2}\leqslant|\widehat{g}|_{2}=|g|_{2}.

Moreover, by (
14
) it holds that

G(x-y,t)=\overline{G(y-x,t)}=G(y-x,t).

Hence
\langle G(t)*f,g\rangle=\langle f,G(t)*g\rangle.

Combining this with (
37
), we obtain

\displaystyle|G(t)*f|_{2}=\sup_{g\in l^{2},|g|_{2}=1}{|\langle G(t)*f,g\rangle|}\leq\sup_{g\in l^{2},|g|_{2}=1}{|f|_{\frac{4}{3}}\,|G(t)*g|_{4}}\lesssim|f|_{\frac{4}{3}}.

By Young’s inequality, we also have

|G(t)*f|_{\infty}\leq(1+|t|)^{-(\frac{3}{2})^{-}}|f|_{1}.

Thus for
2<q<\infty
, the Riesz-Thorin interpolation theorem yields

|u(t)|_{q}=|G(t)*f|_{q}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\zeta_{q}}|f|_{p},

which completes the proof.

∎

Remark 5.1
.

In Theorem
1.3
, we only sharpen the estimate
on a specific segment, one may conclude similar assertions on other segments using the same method.

Corollary 5.2
.

Let
u
be the solution to (
1
) on
{\mathbb{Z}}^{4}
with
g,F\equiv 0
, if

1\leqslant p<r\leqslant\infty,\quad 0<q<\infty\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{p}>\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{3q},

then there exists
C=C(p,q,r)
such that

\|u\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}\leqslant C|f|_{p}.

Furthermore, if
(\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{r})
lies on the segment with vertices
(\frac{3}{4},\frac{1}{2})
and
(1,0)
, then the same conclusion holds for
(p,q,r)
satisfying

\frac{1}{r}+\frac{1}{3q}<\frac{1}{2}

or
(p,q,r)=(\frac{4}{3},\infty,2)
.

Proof.

By Theorem
1.3
, for any
1\leqslant p<r\leqslant\infty
and
q>\beta_{p,r}^{-1}
, we have

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}^{q}

\displaystyle=\|G(t)*f\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}^{q}\lesssim\,|f|_{p}^{q}\,\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\ (1+|t|)^{-q\beta_{p,r}}\,dt\lesssim|f|_{p}^{q}.

In addition, if
\frac{1}{r}=(-2)\frac{1}{p}+2
and
1\leqslant p\leqslant\frac{4}{3}
, the index
\beta_{p,r}
can be replaced by
\zeta_{r}
. Then we finish the proof.

∎

Corollary

is the first version of Strichartz estimates. In next section, we prove another version, i.e. Theorem
1.4
, by a different method.

5.2.
Strichartz estimates

Before proving Theorem
1.4
, we first give some preparations. The following theorem is a direct consequence of
[
29
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
d\geq 2
and a family of operators
\{U(t)\}_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}
satisfy

|U(t)h|_{2}\lesssim|h|_{2},\quad\forall\,(h,t)\in l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}},

and the truncated decay estimate for some
\sigma>0
,

|U(t)(U(s))^{*}h|_{\infty}\lesssim(1+|t-s|)^{-\sigma}|h|_{1},\quad\forall\,(h,t,s)\in l^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\times{\mathbb{R}}^{2},

where
(U(t))^{*}
denotes the adjoint of
U(t)
. If

q,r\geqslant 2,\quad(q,r,\sigma)\neq(2,\infty,1)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\frac{1}{q}\leqslant\sigma\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{r}\right),

while
(\tilde{q},\tilde{r})
satisfies the same conditions, then

\|U(t)F_{1}\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}\lesssim|F_{1}|_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left\|\int_{s<t}U(t)(U(s))^{*}F_{2}(s)\,ds\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}\lesssim\|F_{2}\|_{L^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}_{t}l^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}

hold for all
(F_{1},F_{2})\in l^{2}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\times L_{t}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}l^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}
.

The following Lemma
5.4
can be found in
[
32
,
33
]
, we give an alternative proof here.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
d\geq 2
, then

(1+|x|)^{-d-\nu}\lesssim\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\,\omega(\xi)^{\nu}\,d\xi\right|\lesssim(1+|x|)^{-d-\nu}

holds for all
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
with
|x|
large enough, where
\nu=\pm 1
.

Proof.

We only prove the case
\nu=-1
, the other case is similar. For any
s\in{\mathbb{R}}
,

\mathbf{u}_{s}(\xi):=|\xi|^{s},\quad\forall\,\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

By the distribution theory, if
s\in(-d,0)
, there exists
C=C(d,s)
such that

(38)

\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}_{s})=C\,\mathbf{u}_{-d-s}.

Since
\omega^{-1}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}^{d}})
for
d\geq 2
, we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\omega(\xi)^{-1}\,d\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\omega(\xi)^{-1}\eta(\xi)\,d\xi=\mathcal{F}{\left(\omega^{-1}\eta\right)}(x),\ \ \forall\,x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d},

where
\eta
is as in (
17
). Now we split

\mathcal{F}{\left(\omega^{-1}\eta\right)}=\mathcal{F}\left((\omega^{-1}\eta\,\mathbf{u}_{1}-1)\mathbf{u}_{-1}\right)+\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}_{-1})=:\mathcal{F}(g_{1})+\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}_{-1}).

Inserting a smooth cut-off
\psi
with
\psi=1
near the origin, we get

\mathcal{F}{\left(\omega^{-1}\eta\right)}=\mathcal{F}(g_{1}\psi)+\mathcal{F}(g_{1}(1-\psi))+\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{u}_{-1})=:\mathbf{O}_{1}+\mathbf{O}_{2}+\mathbf{O}_{3}.

By (
38
) with
w=-1
, we get that
\mathbf{O}_{3}
behaves like
\mathbf{u}_{-d+1}
. Moreover, for any
\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{d}
, a direct computation and an induction yield

\partial^{\beta}(g_{1}\psi)=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u}_{1-|\beta|}).
When
|\beta|=d
, we get
\partial^{\beta}(g_{1}\psi)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
\mathbf{O}_{1}=\mathcal{O}(\mathbf{u}_{-d})
.

As for
\mathbf{O}_{2}
, a similar argument shows that

\partial^{N}(g_{1}(1-\psi))\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
large enough. Therefore, we finish the proof.

∎

In the sequel, for proper functions
h_{1}
and
h_{2}
, the opertator
h_{1}(D)
is defined as

(39)

h_{1}(D)h_{2}:=\mathcal{F}^{-1}(h_{1}(\omega))*h_{2},\quad\mbox{with}\ \,\omega\,\ \mbox{in \eqref{equ-gree}}.

Note that the operator
D
coincides with
\sqrt{-\Delta}
in operator theory, see (
13
).

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
b>\frac{d}{d-1}
, then
\frac{1}{D}:l^{a}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\rightarrow l^{b}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
is bounded for any
1\leq a\leq\frac{bd}{b+d}
.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.4
with
\nu=-1
, we get

(40)

\bigg|\frac{1}{D}\,f\bigg|_{b}\lesssim\big|(1+|\cdot|)^{-d+1}*|f|\big|_{b}=\sup_{h\in l^{b^{\prime}},|h|_{b^{\prime}}=1}\langle(1+|\cdot|)^{-d+1}*|f|,h\rangle.

For any
b>\frac{d}{d-1}
, the discrete Hardy-Littlewood-Sobolev inequality (cf. e.g.
[
23
, Section 1]
) and Hölder inequality yield

\displaystyle\left\langle(1+|\cdot|)^{-d+1}*|f|,h\right\rangle

\displaystyle\lesssim|f|_{\frac{bd}{b+d}}+|f|_{b}\lesssim|f|_{\frac{bd}{b+d}},

where in the last “
\lesssim
” we used the fact that
l^{\frac{bd}{b+d}}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})\subset l^{b}({\mathbb{Z}}^{d})
since
\frac{bd}{b+d}<b
.
Inserting this into (
40
), we finish the proof.

∎

Now we give the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

By Duhamel’s formula,

(41)

u(x,t)=\cos(tD)g(x)+\frac{\sin(tD)}{D}f(x)+\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin(t-s)D}{D}F(x,s)ds.

Let the truncated operators
U_{\pm}(t):=\chi_{[0,\infty)}(t)\,e^{\pm itD}
in Theorem
5.3
, where
\chi_{[0,\infty)}(t)=1
if
t\geq 0
, and
\chi_{[0,\infty)}(t)=0
if
t<0
. One can check that the following assertions hold:

\displaystyle\mbox{(a)}\ |U_{\pm}(t)f_{1}|_{2}\leqslant|f_{1}|_{2},\quad\mbox{(b)}\ (U_{+}(t))^{*}=U_{-}(t),

\displaystyle\mbox{(c)}\ e^{itD}e^{isD}f_{1}=e^{i(t+s)D}f_{1},\quad\forall\,(t,s)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}.

By (b), (c) and Young’s inequality, we deduce that

\displaystyle|U_{+}(t)(U_{+}(s))^{*}f_{2}|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant|e^{itD}e^{-isD}f_{2}|_{\infty}=|\mathcal{F}(e^{i(t-s)\omega})*f_{2}|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leqslant|\mathcal{F}(e^{i(t-s)\omega})|_{\infty}|f_{2}|_{1}\lesssim(1+|t-s|)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log(2+|t-s|)|f_{2}|_{1},

which is the truncated decay estimate. In the last “
\lesssim
” we used the fact that

(42)

|\mathcal{F}(e^{it\omega})|_{\infty}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\log(2+|t|),\quad\forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

The proof of (
42
) is similar to that of Theorem
1.1
, see also the remark in
[
38
, Page 692]
.
Now Theorem
5.3
implies

(43)

\|e^{itD}f\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}\lesssim|f|_{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left\|\int_{0}^{t}e^{i(t-s)D}F(x,s)ds\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}\lesssim\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}l^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}},

for any
t,s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, where
(q,r)
and
(\tilde{q},\tilde{r})
satisfy the conditions in Theorem
5.3
with
\sigma=(\frac{3}{2})^{-}
.

By (
41
), (
43
) and Euler’s formula, we have

(44)

\displaystyle\|u\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|\cos(tD)g\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}+\left\|\frac{\sin(tD)}{D}f\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}+\;\left\|\int_{0}^{t}\frac{\sin(t-s)D}{D}F(x,s)ds\right\|_{L^{q}_{t}l^{r}}

\displaystyle\lesssim|g|_{2}+\left|\frac{1}{D}f\right|_{2}+\left\|\frac{1}{D}F\right\|_{L_{t}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}l^{\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}\lesssim|g|_{2}+|f|_{{\frac{4}{3}}}+\|F\|_{L_{t}^{\tilde{q}^{\prime}}l^{\frac{4\tilde{r}^{\prime}}{4+\tilde{r}^{\prime}}}},

where in the last “
\lesssim
” we used Lemma
5.5

5.3.
Nonlinear equations

We use space-time estimates to obtain global well-posedness for small initial data.

Theorem 5.9
.

Let
k\geq 4
,
F(s)=|s|^{k-1}s
,
\forall\,s\in{\mathbb{R}}
and
g=0
in (
1
).
If
f\in l^{1}({\mathbb{Z}}^{4})
with
|f|_{1}
sufficiently small, then for any
2\leq p\leq\infty
, the global solution to (
1
) exists in
l^{p}
. We denote it by
u_{k}
, then it holds that

(45)

|u_{k}(t)|_{p}\lesssim(1+|t|)^{-\zeta_{p}},\quad\forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}}.

Proof.

We use the contraction mapping theorem. Let
\zeta_{k}
be as in Theorem
1.3
. We consider the metric space

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{h:\|h\|_{\mathcal{M}}=\sup_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}}(1+|t|)^{\zeta_{k}}|h(\cdot,t)|_{k}\leqslant 2C_{0}|f|_{1}\right\},

with
C_{0}=C_{0}(k,|f|_{1})
to be determined later. We also define
\Lambda
on
\mathcal{M}
,

\Lambda h:=\Lambda h(t)=G(t)*f+\int_{0}^{t}G(t-s)*F(h(s))\,ds,\quad\forall\,h\in\mathcal{M}.

If
h\in\mathcal{M}
, we prove that
\Lambda h\in\mathcal{M}
. By (
36
) and the fact that
|F(u(s))|\leq|u(s)|_{k}^{k}
,
\forall\,s\in{\mathbb{R}}
, it holds that

|\Lambda h(t)|_{k}\leqslant(1+|t|)^{-\zeta_{k}}|f|_{1}+\left|\int_{0}^{t}(1+|t-s|)^{-\zeta_{k}}|h(s)|^{k}_{k}\,ds\right|,\quad\forall\,t\in{\mathbb{R}},

where we also used Theorem
1.3
with
\beta_{1,k}=\zeta_{k}
. Note that
1+|t|\leqslant(1+|t-s|)(1+|s|)
for all
(s,t)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
, we obtain

(1+|t|)^{\zeta_{k}}|\Lambda h(t)|_{k}\leqslant|f|_{1}+\|h\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\,(1+|s|)^{\zeta_{k}-k\zeta_{k}}\,ds=:|f|_{1}+\|h\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}\,\mathbf{U}_{k}.

Since
k\geq 4
, then
\zeta_{k}-k\zeta_{k}<-1
and
\mathbf{U}_{k}<\infty
.
Thus for
C_{0}=C_{0}(k)
large enough,

\|\Lambda h\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leqslant|f|_{1}+\mathbf{U}_{k}\|h\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}\leqslant C_{0}(|f|_{1}+\|h\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}).

If
|f|_{1}
is sufficiently small such that
(2C_{0})^{k}|f|_{1}^{k}\leqslant|f|_{1}
, we have

\|\Lambda h\|_{\mathcal{M}}\leqslant C_{0}(|f|_{1}+\|h\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k})\leqslant 2C_{0}|f|_{1},

which shows that
\Lambda u\in\mathcal{M}
.

Moreover, one can show that
\Lambda
is a contraction. Indeed, there exists
C=C(k)
such that

\displaystyle\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\leqslant C\mathbf{U}_{k}(\|u_{1}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k-1}+\|u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{M}}^{k-1})\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{M}}

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C\mathbf{U}_{k}(2C_{0}|f|_{1})^{k-1}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{M}}<\frac{1}{2}\|u_{1}-u_{2}\|_{\mathcal{M}},\quad\forall\,u_{1},u_{2}\in\mathcal{M},

as long as
|f|_{1}
is sufficiently small. Therefore,
\Lambda
admits a fixed point in
\mathcal{M}
, which is a solution to (
1
).

By the same token, we can prove (
45
) for general
p\geq 2
, which finishes the proof of Theorem
5.9
.
