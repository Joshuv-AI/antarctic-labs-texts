On The Enumeration and Asymptotic Analysis of Fibonacci Compositions
Joshua M. Siktar

Abstract




Abstract

We study Fibonacci compositions, which are compositions of natural numbers that only use Fibonacci numbers, in two different contexts. We first prove inequalities comparing the number of Fibonacci compositions to regular compositions where summands have a maximum possible value. Then, we consider asymptotic properties of Fibonacci compositions, comparing them to compositions whose terms come from positive linear recurrence sequences. Finally, we consider analogues of these results where we do not allow the use of a certain number of consecutive Fibonacci numbers starting from
\displaystyle F_{2}=1
.

1.
Introduction and basic definitions

The goal of this paper is to unify and extend the theory on compositions of Fibonacci numbers, employing both the more general theory of compositions and the theory of positive linear recurrence sequences. To state our objectives more precisely, we must first define the notions of composition and partition.

Definition 1.1

(Compositions and Partitions)
.

A composition of an integer
\displaystyle n
is an ordered representation of positive integers
\displaystyle\{n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}\}
for which

(1.1)

n\ =\ n_{1}+n_{2}+\dots+n_{k}.

On the other hand, a partition of
\displaystyle n
is a collection of non-increasing positive integers
\displaystyle m_{1}\geq m_{2}\geq\dots\geq m_{j}
for which

(1.2)

n\ =\ m_{1}+m_{2}+\dots+m_{j}.

From these definitions one can easily observe that the number of partitions of any positive integer
\displaystyle n
is in general less than the number of its compositions. Indeed, any partition of
\displaystyle n
is a composition of
\displaystyle n
, and any reordering of its summands gives rise to another composition of
\displaystyle n
. Also, both compositions and partitions can repeat summands. While there are well-known asymptotic results on the enumeration of partitions (see
[
1
,
5
,

,

]
), in this paper we focus on compositions. In the literature, compositions are studied in two settings that are clearly equivalent: the direct enumeration of solutions to (
1.1
), and the placement of balls into bins that are arranged in a line. Both enumerative and asymptotic problems in this area have been studied extensively; see
[

and the references therein.

Definition 1.2

(Fibonacci Numbers)
.

We denote the Fibonacci numbers as the sequence where
\displaystyle F_{0}=0
,
\displaystyle F_{1}=1
, and

(1.3)

F_{k}\ =\ F_{k-1}+F_{k-2}

for all
\displaystyle k\geq 2
. We also define a Fibonacci composition as a composition of a natural number obtained using only Fibonacci numbers as summands.

We also rely on Zeckendorf decompositions to motivate the questions we study, so we define those next.

Definition 1.3

(Zeckendorf decomposition)
.

A Zeckendorf decomposition is a partition of a natural number into a sum of positive, non-adjacent Fibonacci numbers, where the Fibonacci numbers are taken as in Definition
1.2

It is well known that every positive integer has a unique Zeckendorf decomposition; see

One of the other major objects studied in this paper is the positive linear recurrence sequence, which we proceed to define carefully.

Definition 1.4

(PLRS)
.

A positive linear recurrence sequence (PLRS) of order
\displaystyle L
is a sequence of positive integers
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
with the following properties:

(1)

There are non-negative integers
\displaystyle L,c_{1},\dots,c_{L}

(1.4)

H_{i+1}\ =\ c_{1}H_{i}+c_{2}H_{i-1}+\dots+c_{L}H_{i+1-L}

for all
\displaystyle i\geq L,
where
\displaystyle L,c_{1},c_{L}>0

(2)

We have
\displaystyle H_{1}=1
, and the following decomposition holds for
\displaystyle 1\leq i<L
:

(1.5)

H_{i+1}\ =\ c_{1}H_{i}+c_{2}H_{i-1}+\dots+c_{i}H_{1}+1.

2.
Combinatorics of Fibonacci Compositions

The combinatorial analysis in this section will be split into two parts. First we consider the special case where all Fibonacci numbers can be used in composition; the second subsection discusses the more general case where we exclude the first
\displaystyle m-1
Fibonacci numbers from being used. The estimates in this section are not necessarily the tightest possible, but seeking further refinements using the same techniques does not add much insight. To perform this analysis, we introduce some notation for this section.

Notation 2.1

(Composition variables)
.

For
\displaystyle m,n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we denote:

(1)

\displaystyle X_{n}
as the random variable for the number of summands in a random Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
;

(2)

\displaystyle X_{n,m}
as the random variable for the number of summands in a random Fibonacci composition that does not use any summands in the list
\displaystyle\{F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
;

(3)

\displaystyle S_{n}
as the total number of summands amongst all Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
;

(4)

\displaystyle\iota(n)
as the index of the smallest Fibonacci number not less than
\displaystyle n
, i.e.,
\displaystyle\iota(n):=\min\{k\in\mathbb{N}^{+},F_{k}\geq n\}
.

2.1.
Compositions without missing summands

We first define the sets that will be analyzed in this subsection .

Notation 2.2
.

For
\displaystyle n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we denote

(2.1)

\overline{G}_{n}\ :=\ \Big\{\{a_{i}\}^{j}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+},\sum^{j}_{i=2}a_{i}=n,a_{i}=F_{i}\ \text{for some}\ i\Big\}

as the set of Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
, and

(2.2)

\overline{I}_{n,[k]}\ :=\ \Big\{\{a_{i}\}^{j}_{i=1}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+},\sum^{j}_{i=1}a_{i}=n,a_{i}\leq k\Big\}

as the set of compositions of
\displaystyle n
where each summand is at most
\displaystyle k
. Naturally we also denote
\displaystyle G_{n}:=|\overline{G}_{n}|
and
\displaystyle I_{n,[k]}:=|\overline{I}_{n,[k]}|

Later in this section we will bound the size of a collection of Fibonacci compositions from above by the size of a collection of compositions where each summand has a maximum possible value. While it is trivial that
\displaystyle G_{n}\leq I_{n,[n]}
, we use this as inspiration to find a tighter bound of the same nature.

Theorem 2.3
.

For
\displaystyle n\geq 6
, we have the inequality

(2.3)

G_{n}\ \leq\ I_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}.

Proof.

The proof will use, in a non-trivial way, that
\displaystyle\iota(n)\geq 6
if and only if
\displaystyle n\geq 6
. Our strategy is to construct a bijection between
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n}
and a subset of
\displaystyle\overline{I}_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}
, which will verify (
2.3
). We define the set

(2.4)

\mathcal{G}_{1}\ :=\ \{\{a_{i}\}^{j}_{i=1}\in\overline{G}_{n},a_{i}\leq\iota(n)-1\ \forall i\},

and define

(2.5)

\mathcal{G}_{2}\ :=\ \overline{G}_{n}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1}.

Precisely, the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2}
is the collection of all Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
using at least one Fibonacci number taking value at least
\displaystyle\iota(n)
. With that in mind, we define our map
\displaystyle T
on
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n}
as follows:

(1)

If
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
, then
\displaystyle T(\ell):=\ell
.

(2)

If
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
, then in place of every summand
\displaystyle a_{i}\geq 8
, insert the summands
\displaystyle\{4,1,1,\dots,1,4\}
, where exactly
\displaystyle a_{i}-8
consecutive
\displaystyle 1
s are used.

Notice that
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{1}\subseteq\overline{I}_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}
, and for compositions belonging to
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2}
, we have taken every Fibonacci summand with value at least
\displaystyle 8
, and replaced it by a collection of
\displaystyle 4
s and
\displaystyle 1
s. Since
\displaystyle n\geq 6
, necessarily
\displaystyle\iota(n)\geq 6
, so
\displaystyle 4
is a legal summand for compositions in
\displaystyle\overline{I}_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}
, and we conclude that
\displaystyle T(\overline{G}_{n})\subset\overline{I}_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}
.

Now we define the map
\displaystyle T^{-1}
on the set of compositions
\displaystyle T(\overline{G}_{n})
as follows:

(1)

If
\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
then define
\displaystyle T^{-1}(\kappa):=\kappa
.

(2)

If
\displaystyle\kappa\in T(\overline{G}_{n})\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1}
, then read the composition from left to right. Between the first
\displaystyle 4
and the second
\displaystyle 4
, add those
\displaystyle 4
s, and all summands in between them; replace all of those summands by their sum. Repeat this process until there are no more
\displaystyle 4
s in the composition.

Due to the construction of the set
\displaystyle T(\overline{G}_{n})
, any composition in the set will contain an even number of
\displaystyle 4s
, so the procedure we describe is well-defined. In particular, since compositions in
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n}
only contain Fibonacci numbers, the only
\displaystyle 4
s that appear are from splitting a larger summand into smaller ones.

Finally, to show that
\displaystyle T
is a bijection, the only non-trivial step is demonstrating that
\displaystyle T^{-1}(T(\ell))=\ell
whenever
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
. In applying
\displaystyle T
to
\displaystyle\ell
, we look for summands that are at least
\displaystyle 8
, and replace each of them with a
\displaystyle 4
, then some number of
\displaystyle 1
s, and then another
\displaystyle 4
. All other summands are left unchanged (including
\displaystyle 5
s, since
\displaystyle 5<\iota(n)
). Reversing this process is straightforward, as we just replace the aforementioned string of
\displaystyle 4
s and
\displaystyle 1
s by their sum. The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.4
.

We chose the parameters for this inequality very carefully;
\displaystyle 4
is the smallest natural number that is not a Fibonacci number, and
\displaystyle 5
is the only Fibonacci number larger than
\displaystyle 4
that can not be written as a sum of two
\displaystyle 4
s and some [possibly empty] set of other natural numbers; thus we insisted
\displaystyle\iota(n)
be large enough to ensure that
\displaystyle 5
was a legal summand.

In addition to this analysis, we can quickly calculate a summation formula for
\displaystyle I_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}
using Theorem 8 from
[
6
]
. Keeping the notation from that paper, let
\displaystyle R_{n,\ell,k}
denote the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
with
\displaystyle\ell
summands, each summand taking value at most
\displaystyle k
. Then we have the formula

(2.6)

R_{n,\ell,k}\ =\ \sum^{\ell}_{t=0}(-1)^{t}{{\ell}\choose{t}}{{n-t(k+1)+\ell-1}\choose{\ell-1}}.

We may set
\displaystyle k:=\iota(n)-1
, and then the inequality
\displaystyle\Bigl\lfloor{\frac{n}{\iota(n)-1}\Bigr\rfloor}\leq\ell\leq n
indicates how many summands we can have. Summing (
2.6
) over all suitable values of
\displaystyle\ell
, we conclude that

(2.7)

I_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}\ =\ \sum^{n}_{\ell=\lfloor{\frac{n}{\iota(n)-1}\rfloor}}\sum^{\ell}_{t=0}(-1)^{t}{{\ell}\choose{t}}{{n-t(k+1)+\ell-1}\choose{\ell-1}}.

We can use this enumeration to analyze events whose underlying probability space is all Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
, and then we can compute a lower bound on the expected value of this random variable.

Theorem 2.5
.

For
\displaystyle n\geq 6
, we have the lower bound

(2.8)

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\ \geq\ \frac{nF_{n}}{2I_{n,[\iota(n)-1]}}.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle X_{n}
is a non-negative random variable, we may apply Markov’s inequality (see for instance
[
20
, Theorem 18.21]
) to obtain the inequality

(2.9)

\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}\left[X_{n}\geq\frac{n}{2}\right].

Moreover, we observe that if a given Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
only has
\displaystyle 1
s and
\displaystyle 2
s as summands, then it has at least
\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}
summands, but the converse is not necessarily true. As an example, a Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle 8
is
\displaystyle 8=3+1+1+1+1+1
, which has more than
\displaystyle 4
summands but includes a
\displaystyle 3
as one of those summands.

It follows that

(2.10)

\mathbb{P}\left[X_{n}\geq\frac{n}{2}\right]\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}[Z_{[1,2]}],

where
\displaystyle Z_{[1,2]}
is the event where a Fibonacci composition only has
\displaystyle 1
s and
\displaystyle 2
s as summands. It is well-known that the number of ways to compose
\displaystyle n
using only
\displaystyle 1
s and
\displaystyle 2
s is equal to
\displaystyle F_{n}
, so

(2.11)

\mathbb{P}\left[X_{n}\geq\frac{n}{2}\right]\ \geq\ \frac{F_{n}}{G_{n}},

and combining (
2.9
) and (
2.11
) gives us

(2.12)

\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]\ \geq\ \frac{nF_{n}}{2G_{n}}.

Finally, thanks to the inequality (
2.3
) we find the lower bound (
2.8
).
∎

By taking an alternative route starting from (
2.12
), we get another inequality of interest in its own right.

Corollary 2.6
.

For all
\displaystyle n\geq 6
, we have

(2.13)

S_{n}\ \geq\ \frac{nF_{n}}{2}.

Proof.

Write
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]
as
\displaystyle\frac{S_{n}}{G_{n}}
. We may interpret
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X_{n}]
in this way because we are determining the average number of summands in a Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
.
∎

2.2.
Compositions with missing summands

Now we seek results analogous to those in the previous subsection, except now we do not allow the use of the first
\displaystyle m-1
Fibonacci numbers as summands. We need to ensure that such Fibonacci compositions can even exist for all numbers sufficiently large, no matter how many of the first Fibonacci numbers we do not allow. It turns out we can even find an exact threshold beyond which this behavior always takes place.

Lemma 2.7
.

For all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, there exists
\displaystyle N(m)\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that for all
\displaystyle n\geq N
, there exists a sequence of coefficients
\displaystyle\{\beta_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=m}\subset\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\displaystyle n=\sum^{\infty}_{j=m}\beta_{j}F_{j}
. Furthermore, we can choose
\displaystyle N(m):=F_{m}F_{m+1}
.

Proof.

Considering Definition
1.3
of the Fibonacci numbers, we will show that for all
\displaystyle n\geq F_{m}F_{m+1}
, there exist
\displaystyle\theta_{m},\theta_{m+1}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
\displaystyle n=\theta_{m}F_{m}+\theta_{m+1}F_{m+1}
.

Clearly, if
\displaystyle n=N(m)=F_{m}F_{m+1}
, we can set
\displaystyle\theta_{m}:=F_{m+1}
and
\displaystyle\theta_{m+1}:=0
. From here, we will proceed via induction, using
\displaystyle n=N(m)
as the base case. Suppose a suitable composition holds for some
\displaystyle n\geq N(m)
, and we want to find one for
\displaystyle n+1
. The strategy will be to add
\displaystyle 1
to the existing composition in such a way that the coefficients do not become negative. Since any two consecutive Fibonacci numbers are relatively prime, we may write the integer
\displaystyle 1
as a linear combination of any two consecutive Fibonacci numbers. In fact, there exist coefficients
\displaystyle\eta_{m},\eta_{m+1},\tau_{m},\tau_{m+1}
such that

(2.14)

\displaystyle\eta_{m}F_{m}+\eta_{m+1}F_{m+1}

\displaystyle=\ 1

\displaystyle\tau_{m}F_{m}+\tau_{m+1}F_{m+1}

\displaystyle=\ 1,

where without loss of generality, we assume
\displaystyle\eta_{m}\geq 0
,
\displaystyle\eta_{m+1}\leq 0
,
\displaystyle\tau_{m}\leq 0
, and
\displaystyle\tau_{m+1}\geq 0
.

We will want to add one of these equations to the existing composition
\displaystyle n=\theta_{m}F_{m}+\theta_{m+1}F_{m+1}
; which one we choose depends on the values of the coefficients. The following two Fibonacci identities, known as the
Cassini identities
, are well-known for
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and can be proven by induction:

(2.15)

F_{m}^{2}-F_{m-1}F_{m+1}\ =\ (-1)^{m+1};

(2.16)

F_{m-1}F_{m}-F_{m-2}F_{m+1}\ =\ (-1)^{m}.

These identities tell us if
\displaystyle m
is even, we can set
\displaystyle\eta_{m}:=-F_{m}
,
\displaystyle\eta_{m+1}:=F_{m-1}
,
\displaystyle\tau_{m}:=F_{m-1}
, and
\displaystyle\tau_{m+1}:=-F_{m-2}
; likewise, if
\displaystyle m
is odd, then we can set
\displaystyle\eta_{m}:=F_{m}
,
\displaystyle\eta_{m+1}:=-F_{m-1}
,
\displaystyle\tau_{m}:=-F_{m-1}
, and
\displaystyle\tau_{m+1}:=F_{m-2}
. Then, if either of the following pairs of statements is true, we have found a valid composition for
\displaystyle n+1
:

(1)

\displaystyle\theta_{m}+\eta_{m}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\theta_{m+1}+\eta_{m+1}\geq 0
;

(2)

\displaystyle\theta_{m}+\tau_{m}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\theta_{m+1}+\tau_{m+1}\geq 0
.

Assume first that
\displaystyle m
is even, and then
\displaystyle\theta_{m+1}+\eta_{m+1}\geq 0
and
\displaystyle\theta_{m}+\tau_{m}\geq 0
are both immediate. We assume for sake of contradiction that
\displaystyle\theta_{m+1}<-\tau_{m+1}
and that
\displaystyle\theta_{m}<-\eta_{m}
. In this case, we may use the specific values of
\displaystyle\eta_{m+1}
and
\displaystyle\tau_{m}
to obtain the inequality chain

(2.17)

N\ \leq\ \theta_{m}F_{m}+\theta_{m+1}F_{m+1}\ <\ -\eta_{m}F_{m}-\tau_{m+1}F_{m+1}\ =\ F_{m}^{2}+F_{m-2}F_{m+1}\ =\ F_{m}F_{m+1}-1\ <\ N,

which is a contradiction. The case where
\displaystyle m
is odd is similar.
∎

Remark 2.8
.

From this construction it is easy to see how many such compositions are possible using just
\displaystyle F_{m}
and
\displaystyle F_{m+1}
when
\displaystyle n\geq F_{m}F_{m+1}
. If
\displaystyle n=\ell F_{m}F_{m+1}
for some
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, then we can construct a composition of
\displaystyle n
using just
\displaystyle F_{m}
and
\displaystyle F_{m+1}
as summands in
\displaystyle\ell+1
ways. If
\displaystyle\ell F_{m}F_{m+1}<n<(\ell+1)F_{m}F_{m+1}
, then we can write such a composition in exactly
\displaystyle\ell
ways.

Now, we provide a [more delicate] variant of Theorem
2.3
that holds when we consider Fibonacci compositions without the summands
\displaystyle\{F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
; the challenge is identifying what number can fill the role of the
\displaystyle 4
s in the proof of Theorem
2.3
. The notation used is as follows:

(1)

\displaystyle G_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}
refers to the number of Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
that do not use
\displaystyle\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
as summands;

(2)

\displaystyle I_{n,\Big\{m,m+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\Big\}}
refers to the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
that only use
\displaystyle\Big\{m,m+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\Big\}
as possible summands.

Theorem 2.9
.

We have the inequality

(2.18)

G_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}\ \leq\ I_{n,\Big\{m,m+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\Big\}}

whenever
\displaystyle m\geq 4
, and either of the following sets of conditions are met:

(1)

\displaystyle m
is not a Fibonacci number and
\displaystyle\frac{5}{8}n\geq 3m+1
;

(2)

\displaystyle m+1
is not a Fibonacci number and
\displaystyle\frac{5}{8}n\geq 3m+3
.

Proof.

Allowing summands in our compositions up to
\displaystyle\frac{5}{8}n
ensures that for any
\displaystyle m\geq 4
, we have
\displaystyle\frac{F_{m+1}}{F_{m}}\geq\frac{8}{5}
. Moreover, between
\displaystyle\frac{5}{8}n
and
\displaystyle n
there is at most one Fibonacci number, and we denote it as
\displaystyle F_{k}
. Furthermore, since
\displaystyle F_{k}>\frac{n}{2}
, it can only appear in a composition of
\displaystyle n
once. This simplifies the forthcoming analysis.

Denote
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}
as the set of Fibonacci compositions without the summands
\displaystyle\{F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
, and
\displaystyle\overline{I}_{n,\Big\{m,m+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\Big\}}
as the set of compositions of
\displaystyle n
that only use
\displaystyle\Big\{m,m+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\Big\}
as possible summands. Notice that as long as
\displaystyle n\geq F_{m}F_{m+1}
, Lemma
2.7
assures us that the set
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}
is nonempty; if this set is empty, then the theorem trivially holds.

Just as in the proof of Theorem
2.3
, we will split the set
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}
into two sub-collections. The first of these is

(2.19)

\mathcal{G}_{1}\ :=\ \Bigg\{\{a_{i}\}^{\ell}_{i=1}\in\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}},a_{i}\leq\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\Bigg\}.

To accompany (
2.19
), we also define

(2.20)

\mathcal{G}_{2}\ :=\ \overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}\setminus\mathcal{G}_{1}.

Precisely, the set
\displaystyle\mathcal{G}_{2}
is the collection of all Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
using
\displaystyle F_{k}
as a summand, without using any of
\displaystyle\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
as summands. With that in mind, we define our map
\displaystyle T
on
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}
as follows:

(1)

If
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
, then define
\displaystyle T(\ell):=\ell
.

(2)

If
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
and
\displaystyle m
is not a Fibonacci number, then in place of
\displaystyle F_{k}
, insert the summands
\displaystyle\{m,m+1,m+1,\dots,m+1,r,m\}
. We use
\displaystyle j_{1}
copies of
\displaystyle m+1
, with
\displaystyle j_{1}
solving the equation
\displaystyle F_{k}-2m=j_{1}(m+1)+r_{1}
, where
\displaystyle r_{1}\in\{m+2,m+3,\dots,2m+1\}
.

(3)

If
\displaystyle\ell\in\mathcal{G}_{2}
and
\displaystyle m
is a Fibonacci number, then in place of
\displaystyle F_{k}
, insert the summands
\displaystyle\{m+1,m+2,m+2,\dots,m+2,r,m+1\}
. We use
\displaystyle j_{2}
copies of
\displaystyle m+2
, with
\displaystyle j_{2}
solving the equation
\displaystyle F_{k}-2(m+1)=j_{2}(m+2)+r_{2}
, where
\displaystyle r_{2}\in\{m+3,m+4,\dots,2m+3\}

Some remarks are needed here. First notice that since
\displaystyle m\geq 4
,
\displaystyle m
and
\displaystyle m+1
can not both be Fibonacci numbers. Moreover, the bounds relating
\displaystyle n
and
\displaystyle m
assure the existence solutions
\displaystyle(j_{1},r_{1})
and
\displaystyle(j_{2},r_{2})
to the equations
\displaystyle F_{k}-2m=j_{1}(m+1)+r_{1}
(if
\displaystyle m
is not a Fibonacci number) and
\displaystyle F_{k}-2(m+1)=j_{2}(m+2)+r_{2}
(if
\displaystyle m
is a Fibonacci number) respectively. Indeed, in the case where
\displaystyle m
is not a Fibonacci number, the bounds
\displaystyle\frac{5}{8}n\geq 3m+1
and
\displaystyle F_{k}>\frac{5}{8}n
tell us we may write
\displaystyle F_{k}
as a sum of two
\displaystyle m
s, some
\displaystyle j_{1}
copies of
\displaystyle m+1
, and a “leftover" quantity
\displaystyle r_{1}
that is definitely not equal to
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle 2
,
\displaystyle 3
, or
\displaystyle 5
. The case where
\displaystyle m
is a Fibonacci number is similar.
Finally, it is clear from the definition of
\displaystyle T
that

(2.21)

T(\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}})\ \subset\ \overline{I}_{n,\{m,m+1,\dots,\lfloor{\frac{5n}{8}\rfloor}\}}.

Now we can define the map
\displaystyle T^{-1}
on the set of compositions
\displaystyle T(\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}})
as follows:

(1)

If
\displaystyle\kappa\in\mathcal{G}_{1}
then define
\displaystyle T^{-1}(\kappa):=\kappa
.

(2)

If
\displaystyle\kappa\in T(\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}})
and
\displaystyle m
is not a Fibonacci number, then read the composition from left to right. Add all the terms between two consecutive appearances of
\displaystyle m
, including the two
\displaystyle m
s in the sum. Repeat this procedure until there are no more summands equal to
\displaystyle m
in the composition.

(3)

If
\displaystyle\kappa\in T(\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}})
and
\displaystyle m
is a Fibonacci number, then do the same steps as in the previous bullet point, but looking for
\displaystyle m+1
instead of
\displaystyle m
.

Due to the construction of the set
\displaystyle T(\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}})
, any composition in that set will contain an even number of
\displaystyle m
s (or
\displaystyle m+1
s, when
\displaystyle m
is a Fibonacci number), so the procedure we described is well-defined. The fact that
\displaystyle T
is a bijection between
\displaystyle\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}
and
\displaystyle T(\overline{G}_{n\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}})
follows from the same reasoning used in the proof of Theorem
2.3
.
∎

Remark 2.10
.

These bounds are far from being tight. Still, we can control the number of Fibonacci compositions from above by some number of standard compositions, which are a better studied object, even when we do not allow the use of the first few Fibonacci numbers. This bound could be refined by removing or adjusting some of the simplifying assumptions, but it is not worth complicating the main ideas of the proof.

Now we provide an analogue of Theorem
2.5
, where we exclude
\displaystyle\{F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
as possible summands.

Theorem 2.11
.

We have the lower bounds

(2.22)

\mathbb{E}[X_{n,m}]\ \geq\ \frac{n}{F_{m+1}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{F_{m}F_{m+1}}\right\rfloor\frac{1}{G_{n,\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}}

and

(2.23)

\mathbb{E}[X_{n,m}]\ \geq\ \frac{n}{F_{m+1}}\left\lfloor\frac{n}{F_{m}F_{m+1}}\right\rfloor\frac{1}{I_{n,\{m,m+1,\dots,\left\lfloor\frac{5n}{8}\right\rfloor\}}}

whenever
\displaystyle m\geq 2
and
\displaystyle n\geq F_{m}F_{m+1}
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle X_{n,m}
is a non-negative random variable, we may apply Markov’s Inequality to obtain the inequality

(2.24)

\frac{F_{m+1}}{n}\mathbb{E}[X_{n,m}]\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}\left[X_{n,m}\geq\frac{n}{F_{m+1}}\right].

If a given Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
only has
\displaystyle F_{m}
and
\displaystyle F_{m+1}
as summands (which is guaranteed to exist when
\displaystyle n\geq F_{m}F_{m+1}
by Lemma
2.7
), then it has at least
\displaystyle\frac{n}{F_{m+1}}
summands, but the converse is not necessarily true. It follows that

(2.25)

\mathbb{P}\left[X_{n,m}\geq\frac{n}{F_{m+1}}\right]\ \geq\ \mathbb{P}[Z_{n,[F_{m},F_{m+1}]}],

where
\displaystyle Z_{n,[F_{m},F_{m+1}]}
denotes the event where a Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
only has
\displaystyle F_{m}
and
\displaystyle F_{m+1}
as summands. Due to Remark
2.8
, we have that

(2.26)

\mathbb{P}[Z_{n,[F_{m},F_{m+1}]}]\ \geq\left\lfloor\frac{n}{F_{m}F_{m+1}}\right\rfloor\frac{1}{G_{n,\setminus\{F_{1},\dots,F_{m-1}\}}}.

Combining the inequalities (
2.24
), (
2.25
), and (
2.26
) immediately yields (
2.22
). We may also utilize (
2.18
) to deduce (
2.23
).
∎

3.
Asymptotic Analysis of Compositions

We now complement our estimates pertaining to Fibonacci compositions of a fixed
\displaystyle n
with an asymptotic analysis of these compositions as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
. Central to this asymptotic theory is the behavior of certain power series on the interval
\displaystyle(0,1)
, so our first subsection reviews this theory, while the second introduces and proves some of our main asymptotic results.

3.1.
Power Series on
\displaystyle(0,1)

We begin this subsection by introducing notation for our power series.

Notation 3.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
be a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers, and for each
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we define the power series

(3.1)

T_{B_{m}}(x)\ :=\ \sum^{\infty}_{i=m}x^{B_{i}}.

We also let
\displaystyle t_{B_{m}}(n)
denote the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
using summands from
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
, excluding the numbers
\displaystyle\{B_{1},B_{2},\dots,B_{m-1}\}
.

Notice that we need to assume
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
is strictly increasing to assure that no powers are repeated in the summation; moreover, we indicate that the sum represents the generating function for choosing summands for a composition from a given increasing sequence of positive integers. It is then easily seen that the power series (
3.1
) has a radius of convergence at least
\displaystyle 1
by comparison to the standard geometric series; we will henceforth restrict such power series to the interval
\displaystyle(0,1)
, and consider real-valued roots of the power series on that interval.

We can freely take derivatives of
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}
in the interval
\displaystyle(0,1)
, via term-by-term differentiation. Seeing that
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
\displaystyle x\in(0,1)
, and
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(0)=-1
, each equation of the form

(3.2)

T_{B_{m}}(x)\ =\ 1

has a unique root (where we’ve also used the blowup of
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}
at
\displaystyle x=1
). Denote this sequence of roots as
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{B_{m}}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
. We may define (
3.1
) for any increasing sequence of natural numbers, including the Fibonacci numbers, though we will replace
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}
with
\displaystyle R_{m}
when referring to this special case.

Notation 3.2
.

We define the power series

(3.3)

R_{m}(x)\ :=\ \sum^{\infty}_{i=m}x^{F_{i}},

and let
\displaystyle r_{m}(n)
denote the number of Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
that do not use any of the summands
\displaystyle\{F_{1},F_{2},\dots,F_{m-1}\}
. Each equation of the form

(3.4)

R_{m}(x)\ =\ 1

will have a unique root, and we denote this sequence of roots as
\displaystyle\{\alpha_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
.

With these constructions in place, we have the following proposition, which may be regarded as a routine exercise in real analysis. For sake of completeness we provide a short proof.

Proposition 3.3
.

The sequence
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{B_{m}}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
converges to
\displaystyle 1
as
\displaystyle m\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

By the Monotone Convergence Theorem for real numbers, if we show that the bounded sequence is strictly increasing, then it has a limit. To prove that the sequence is strictly increasing, notice that each function
\displaystyle R_{m}(x)
is increasing on
\displaystyle(0,1)
, and
\displaystyle f_{m+1}(x)
only differs from
\displaystyle R_{m}(x)
in that it has one less power of
\displaystyle x
in its expansion. Since
\displaystyle x^{B_{i}}
is an increasing monomial on
\displaystyle(0,1)
for each
\displaystyle i\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, necessarily
\displaystyle\gamma_{B_{m+1}}>\gamma_{B_{m}}
. Thus the sequence
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{B_{m}}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
has a limit, which we will call
\displaystyle\gamma_{B}
. Our construction of the sequence assures that
\displaystyle\gamma_{B}\leq 1
, so we assume for sake of contradiction that
\displaystyle\gamma_{B}<1
.

Since
\displaystyle f_{m}
is increasing on
\displaystyle(0,1)
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{B}\geq\gamma_{B_{m}}
for each
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
,

(3.5)

0\ <\ -1+\sum^{\infty}_{i=m}\gamma_{B}^{B_{i}}\ <\ \infty,

from which it follows that

(3.6)

\sum^{\infty}_{i=m}\gamma_{B}^{B_{i}}\ >\ 1

for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
. However, the series
\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=m}x^{B_{i}}
absolutely converges for all
\displaystyle x\in(0,1)
by comparison with the geometric series
\displaystyle\sum^{\infty}_{i=1}x^{i}
. That is,
\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\sum^{\infty}_{i=m}\gamma_{B}^{B_{i}}=0
, posing a contradiction.
∎

The sequence of roots
\displaystyle\{\gamma_{B_{m}}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
appears in the results of the next section, where we start proving asymptotic results for compositions that are only comprised of positive integers belonging to a given sequence. Different terms from these sequences of roots will appear depending on how many terms from the beginning of
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
we preclude from use in compositions.

3.2.
Asymptotic Results

Now we consider some results on the asymptotics of compositions for a given increasing sequence of positive integers. The starting point of this discussion is
[
21
, Theorem 7]
, proven in 2003, but we provide some generalizations and a new direction for what can be done with this type of result. Working with general increasing sequences of positive integers gives us the flexibility to later apply our theory to positive linear recurrence sequences.

Now we state the aforementioned
[
21
, Theorem 7]
on the asymptotics of Fibonacci compositions.

Theorem 3.4
.

Let
\displaystyle r_{2}(n)
denote the number of Fibonacci compositions of
\displaystyle n
(with any number of summands), and let
\displaystyle R_{2}(x):=\sum^{\infty}_{i=2}x^{F_{i}}
. Then, as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

(3.7)

r_{2}(n)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{R_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})}\alpha_{2}^{-n-1},

where
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}
is the unique positive root of
\displaystyle R_{2}(x)=1
, namely
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\approx 0.5276126
, so that
\displaystyle r_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})\approx 3.3749752
. In addition, if
\displaystyle\overline{r_{2}}(n)
denotes the mean number of summands in a random Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
, then as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

(3.8)

\overline{r_{2}}(n)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{\alpha_{2}R_{2}^{\prime}(\alpha_{2})}n\ \approx\ 0.561583n.

Using the same method, and replacing
\displaystyle R_{2}(x)
with
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(x)
, we obtain the following new theorem.

Theorem 3.5
.

As
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
we have the asymptotic behavior

(3.9)

{t_{B}}_{m}(n)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{{T_{B}}_{m}^{\prime}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}\gamma_{B_{m}}^{-n-1},

where
\displaystyle\gamma_{B_{m}}
is the unique positive root of the equation
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(x)=1
. In addition, if
\displaystyle\overline{t_{B}}_{m}(n)
denotes the mean number of summands in a random Fibonacci decomposition of
\displaystyle n
, then as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
, we have

(3.10)

\overline{{t_{B}}_{m}}(n)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{\gamma_{B_{m}}{T_{B}}_{m}^{\prime}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}n.

Proof.

We recall that the power series (
3.1
) is analytic in
\displaystyle(0,1)
. Calculating its first derivative yields

(3.11)

T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(x)\ =\ \sum^{\infty}_{i=m}B_{i}x^{B_{i}-1}.

If we restrict
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}
to the real interval
\displaystyle[0,1)
, we see that

(3.12)

\lim_{s\rightarrow 1^{+}}T_{B_{m}}(s)\ =\ +\infty,

so the Intermediate Value Theorem assures there exists some
\displaystyle\gamma_{B_{m}}\in(0,1)
for which
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(\gamma_{B_{m}})=1
. Moreover,
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
\displaystyle x\in(0,1)
due to formula (
3.11
), so this choice of
\displaystyle\gamma_{H}
is unique. The function

(3.13)

U_{H_{m}}(x)\ :=\ \frac{1}{1-T_{B_{m}}(x)}

has a pole at
\displaystyle x=\gamma_{B_{m}}
. Denote
\displaystyle g_{k}(n)
as the number of ways to write
\displaystyle n
as a composition of exactly
\displaystyle k
summands from the list
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=m}
. Then we recall that the local expansion of
\displaystyle F_{k}(x):=\sum^{\infty}_{i=0}g_{k}(i)x^{i}
around this pole (denoted
\displaystyle\alpha
) can be given as

(3.14)

F_{k}(x)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{\alpha T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}\cdot\frac{1}{1-\frac{x}{\gamma_{B_{m}}}},

and then

(3.15)

g_{k}(n)\ =\ [x^{n}]U_{H_{m}}(x)\ \sim\ \frac{1}{\gamma_{B_{m}}T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}\gamma_{B_{m}}^{-n}\ =\ \frac{1}{T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}\gamma_{B_{m}}^{-n-1}

as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
, which proves (
3.9
).

It remains to prove (
3.10
). To do so, we begin by defining
\displaystyle u
as the number of summands in a given composition of
\displaystyle n
, which has the associated bi-variate generating function

(3.16)

H_{m}(x,u)\ =\ \frac{1}{1-uT_{B_{m}}(x)}.

Since
\displaystyle\overline{t_{B_{m}}}(n)
can be thought of the first [discrete] moment of
\displaystyle t_{B_{m}}(n)
, we can use the approach of differentiating identities to calculate
\displaystyle\overline{t_{B_{m}}}(n)
:

(3.17)

\overline{t_{B_{m}}}(n)\ =\ \frac{1}{t_{B_{m}}(n)}[x^{n}]\frac{\partial}{\partial u}H_{m}(x,u)\ =\ \frac{1}{t_{B_{m}}(n)}\cdot\frac{T_{B_{m}}(x)}{(1-T_{B_{m}}(x))^{2}}.

With this in mind, we define

(3.18)

j_{B_{m}}(x)\ :=\ \frac{T_{B_{m}}(x)}{(1-T_{B_{m}}(x))^{2}},

and observe that
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}
has a pole of order
\displaystyle 2
at
\displaystyle x=\gamma_{B_{m}}
. Thus the first term in the local expansion of
\displaystyle j_{B_{m}}(x)
around
\displaystyle x=\gamma_{B_{m}}
is of order

(3.19)

j_{B_{m}}(x)\ \sim\ \left(\frac{1}{T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(\alpha)}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{(x-\gamma_{B_{m}})^{2}}.

Extracting the coefficients from (
3.19
) gives the asymptotic expansion

(3.20)

[x^{n}]j_{B_{m}}(x)\ \sim\ \left(\frac{n}{\gamma_{B_{m}}^{2}(T_{B_{m}}^{\prime}(\gamma_{B_{m}}))^{2}}\right)\gamma_{B_{m}}^{-n}

as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty
. Plugging this into (
3.17
) gives the formula (
3.10
).
∎

The case of
\displaystyle m:=3
for the Fibonacci numbers in Theorem
3.5
is of special interest since the frequency of
\displaystyle 1
s in compositions of Fibonacci numbers was studied in
[
21

. In other words, it gives us a sense of how much flexibility we lose in building Fibonacci compositions when we can no longer use
\displaystyle 1
as a summand, when compared to Theorem
3.4
.

Theorem 3.6

(Theorem 8 in
[
21
]
)
.

The proportion of summands equal to
\displaystyle 1
in a random Fibonacci composition of
\displaystyle n
tends to
\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\approx 0.5276125
as
\displaystyle n\rightarrow\infty

The related application of Theorem
3.5
is as follows.

Example 3.7
.

Let

4.
Comparing Fibonacci Compositions to General PLRS Compositions

We look to compare the Fibonacci numbers to other positive linear recurrence sequences.

4.1.
Relative Asymptotics of PLRS Compositions

Since every PLRS is a strictly increasing sequence of positive integers, Proposition
3.3
will apply to all such sequences. The central factor of understanding the relative asymptotics of compositions coming from Fibonacci numbers compared to some other PLRS is the asymptotic growth rate of such a sequence.

As a motivation for the forthcoming results, we showcase the relationship between the Fibonacci numbers and the Golden Ratio in an asymptotic framework. We may write

(4.1)

\phi\ =\ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{m+1}}{F_{m}}\ =\ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{m}+F_{m-1}}{F_{m}}\ =\ 1+\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{F_{m-1}}{F_{m}}\ =\ 1+\frac{1}{\phi}.

This is an equation that can be solved for
\displaystyle\phi
to obtain the classical value of the Golden Ratio,
\displaystyle\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}
. Notice this is the only positive, real root of (
4.1
). This procedure of generating a polynomial where a root is the sequence’s growth rate can be generalized to all PLRS, and we will need this tool in the present section. Consider a generic PLRS of order
\displaystyle L
with coefficients
\displaystyle\{c_{1},\dots,c_{L}\}
. Since
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{L}>0
, repeating (
4.1
) in this general case gives

(4.2)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\beta_{H}\ =:\ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H_{m+1}}{H_{m}}\ =\ \lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum^{L}_{i=1}c_{i}H_{m+1-i}}{H_{m}}\ =\\
&c_{1}+\sum^{L}_{i=2}\frac{c_{i}H_{m+1-i}}{H_{m}}\ =\ \sum^{L}_{i=1}\frac{c_{i}}{\beta_{H}^{i-1}},\end{aligned}

and we can multiply the resulting equation by
\displaystyle\beta_{H}^{L-1}
to obtain the polynomial

(4.3)

\beta_{H}^{L}-\sum^{L}_{i=1}c_{i}\beta_{H}^{L-i}\ =\ 0.

By Descartes’ Rule of Signs, the polynomial (
4.3
) will have exactly one positive root. Moreover, since
\displaystyle c_{L}>0
, we can not have
\displaystyle 0
as a root of (
4.3
).

Definition 4.1

(Asymptotic Growth Rate)
.

For a given PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
, we define
\displaystyle\beta_{H}:=\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H_{m+1}}{H_{m}}
as the asymptotic growth rate of
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
.

The derivation of (
4.2
) and (
4.3
) assures that
\displaystyle\beta_{H}
is uniquely defined and strictly positive, for any PLRS.

Definition 4.2

(Growth Polynomial)
.

For a given PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
, we call the left-hand side of (
4.3
) the growth polynomial associated with
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
.

Another simple and very useful property is the following: if
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
and
\displaystyle\{\widetilde{H_{m}}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
are two PLRS of the same order
\displaystyle L
with coefficients
\displaystyle\{c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{L}\}
and
\displaystyle\{\widetilde{c_{1}},\widetilde{c_{2}},\dots,\widetilde{c_{L}}\}
where
\displaystyle\widetilde{c_{i}}\geq c_{i}
for all
\displaystyle i\in\{1,2,\dots,L\}
, then
\displaystyle\widetilde{H}_{j}\geq H_{j}
for all
\displaystyle j\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
; crucial to this property taking place is that all PLRS have initial term
\displaystyle 1
. We will use this in the proof of Proposition
4.4
, which relates compositions using only Fibonacci numbers to compositions using only numbers belonging to some other PLRS. First, however, we prove that if sufficiently many initial values of a PLRS are given, then the coefficients can be uniquely determined. This result will be needed for some of the technical lemmas in this section.

Proposition 4.3

(Uniqueness from Initial Values)
.

For any given
\displaystyle L+1
initial values of a PLRS (including
\displaystyle H_{1}=1
), there is at most one PLRS of length
\displaystyle L
that satisfies those values.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle H_{1}=1
by definition, the PLRS recurrence gives a system of
\displaystyle L
equations used to determine
\displaystyle H_{2},H_{3},\dots,H_{L+1}
. The resulting system will be lower triangular (if the equations are written starting with
\displaystyle H_{2}=c_{1}H_{1}+1
and ending with
\displaystyle H_{L+1}=c_{1}H_{L}+\dots+c_{L}H_{1}
). In fact, the main diagonal will not have any zeros since all entries of a PLRS are strictly positive, so the coefficient matrix is invertible. However, the solution may have negative or fractional coefficients, so there is no guarantee that the solution corresponds to a PLRS.
∎

Now, we will fix
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, and this number indicates how many of the first numbers in the sequences are not allowed to be summands in compositions. Recall from the statement of Theorem
3.5
that
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(n)
denotes the number of compositions of
\displaystyle n
using the terms
\displaystyle\{H_{m},H_{m+1},H_{m+2},\dots\}
from any increasing sequence
\displaystyle\{B_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}\subset\mathbb{N}^{+}
. Meanwhile,
\displaystyle r_{m}(n)
denotes this same quantity specifically for the Fibonacci numbers. Our next proposition tells us when the number of Fibonacci compositions asymptotically grows faster than compositions with summands from some other PLRS, and vice-versa.

Proposition 4.4

(Fibonacci vs. PLRS Rates)
.

Let
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
be a PLRS according to Definition
1.4
, with asymptotic growth rate
\displaystyle\beta_{H}
. If
\displaystyle m
is sufficiently large, then the limit
\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t_{H_{m}}(n)}{r_{m}(n)}
has the following properties:

(1)

It equals
\displaystyle 0
if
\displaystyle\beta_{H}>\phi
;

(2)

It equals
\displaystyle+\infty
if
\displaystyle\beta_{H}<\phi
.

Proof.

Fix
\displaystyle m\geq 1
. If we apply Theorem
3.5
for both the PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
and the Fibonacci numbers, we see that

(4.4)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{t_{H_{m}}(n)}{r_{m}(n)}\ =\ \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\frac{1}{T^{\prime}_{H_{m}}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}\gamma^{-n-1}_{H_{m}}}{\frac{1}{R^{\prime}_{m}(\alpha_{m})}\alpha_{m}^{-n-1}}\ =\ \frac{R_{m}^{\prime}(\alpha_{m})}{T^{\prime}_{H_{m}}(\gamma_{B_{m}})}\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\frac{\alpha_{m}}{\gamma_{B_{m}}}\right)^{n+1}.

In other words, the relative behavior of
\displaystyle\alpha_{m}
and
\displaystyle\gamma_{B_{m}}
dictate the asymptotic behavior of this quotient. This relative behavior is in turn determined by whether
\displaystyle\beta_{H}<\phi
,
\displaystyle\beta_{H}>\phi
, or
\displaystyle\beta_{H}=\phi
.

If
\displaystyle\beta_{H}>\phi
, then the powers appearing in the power series
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(x)
(except possibly for the first few) are larger than those appearing in the power series
\displaystyle R_{m}(x)
. Then
\displaystyle T_{B_{m}}(x)<R_{m}(x)
for
\displaystyle x\in(0,1)
, and both power series are increasing on this interval, so
\displaystyle\gamma_{B_{m}}>\alpha_{m}
. The case of
\displaystyle\beta_{H}<\phi
is similar.
∎

There are two limitations of this result. The first is that some PLRS are such that the first few values of the sequence are smaller than the Fibonacci numbers, but then
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
becomes larger; an example of this is the PLRS of order
\displaystyle 6
with coefficients
\displaystyle c_{1}=1
,
\displaystyle c_{2}=c_{3}=c_{4}=c_{5}=0
, and
\displaystyle c_{6}=20
. This is the reason why we need
\displaystyle m
to be sufficiently large, and leave the issue of small
\displaystyle m
open for PLRS where this behavior takes place. On the other hand, if either of the following is true, we may set
\displaystyle m=1
in Proposition
4.4
:

(1)

\displaystyle H_{m}\geq F_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
;

(2)

\displaystyle H_{m}\leq F_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.

The second caveat is that we need information about the value of
\displaystyle\beta_{H}
to determine which case of Proposition
4.4
a given PLRS falls under. For a given PLRS, let
\displaystyle m_{0}
denote the smallest index for which
\displaystyle F_{m_{0}}\neq H_{m_{0}}
. We will now study some particular PLRS for which we can obtain the needed information and apply Proposition
4.4
readily.

Moreover, it is worth noting that if
\displaystyle\beta_{H}=\phi
then we need information on which sequence has larger terms at the beginning of the sequence; otherwise the method used to analyze the other cases is inconclusive. Thus, one may ask if this is a case that ever occurs. The next proposition and example are intended to address this question.

Proposition 4.5
.

The only PLRS of order
\displaystyle 2
with asymptotic growth rate equal to
\displaystyle\phi
is the Fibonacci numbers
\displaystyle\{F_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
. Furthermore, there is no PLRS of order
\displaystyle 3
with asymptotic growth rate equal to
\displaystyle\phi
.

Proof.

First we consider the case of PLRS of order
\displaystyle 2
. Recall that the asymptotic growth rate of a PLRS is the unique positive root of the sequence’s growth polynomial. Based on Definition
4.2
, the coefficients of a growth polynomial are all rational. If the PLRS is of order
\displaystyle 2
, this means the other root of the growth polynomial must be
\displaystyle\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}
. Furthermore, since every growth polynomial has leading coefficient
\displaystyle 1
, the polynomial in question must be
\displaystyle x^{2}-x-1
, which is the growth polynomial for
\displaystyle\{F_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
.

Now we consider the case of a PLRS of order
\displaystyle 3
. Assume for sake of contradiction that there exists a PLRS of order
\displaystyle 3
, where the positive root of the associated growth polynomial is
\displaystyle\phi
. Just as in the case of an order
\displaystyle 2
PLRS, we must have
\displaystyle\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}
as another root of the polynomial. Now, there will be one more root of this growth polynomial, which we will call
\displaystyle a
. Since the coefficients of a growth polynomial are all real-valued, and the other roots are real-valued,
\displaystyle a\in\mathbb{R}
. In particular, we cannot have a complex conjugate pair of roots because a cubic polynomial has at most three distinct roots, and two of them are determined to be real. Furthermore,
\displaystyle\phi
is the only positive root, and if the coefficients of the PLRS are
\displaystyle c_{1}
,
\displaystyle c_{2}
, and
\displaystyle c_{3}
, then
\displaystyle c_{3}\neq 0
, so
\displaystyle 0
cannot be a root of the growth polynomial. Thus we have
\displaystyle a<0
, and we observe that our growth polynomial must take the following form:

(4.5)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} (x^{2}-x-1)(x-a)\ &=\ 0\Rightarrow\\
x^{3}+(-a-1)x^{2}+(a-1)x+a\ &=\ 0.\end{aligned}

In general for a PLRS, the first and last coefficients must be negative integers, and all other coefficients must be non-positive integers (recall Definition
1.4
). If
\displaystyle-a-1<0
, then
\displaystyle a
is a non-negative integer; on the other hand, formula (
4.3
) implies that
\displaystyle 0<c_{3}=-a
, which means that
\displaystyle a
must be a strictly negative integer. We have reached a contradiction, thus no PLRS of order
\displaystyle 3
satisfying our conditions can exist.
∎

Meanwhile, in spite of Proposition
4.5
, there are other PLRS besides the Fibonacci numbers that yield an asymptotic growth rate of
\displaystyle\phi
.

Example 4.6
.

The simplest example of a PLRS of order greater than
\displaystyle 2
with asymptotic growth rate of
\displaystyle\phi
is the PLRS of order
\displaystyle 4
with
\displaystyle c_{1}=1
,
\displaystyle c_{2}=0
,
\displaystyle c_{3}=1
, and
\displaystyle c_{4}=1
. This PLRS has the growth polynomial
\displaystyle\beta_{H}^{4}-\beta_{H}^{3}-\beta_{H}-1
. Its roots are
\displaystyle\phi
,
\displaystyle\frac{1-\sqrt{5}}{2}
,
\displaystyle i
, and
\displaystyle-i
.

Remark 4.7
.

There are numerous results giving lower and upper bounds on the modulus of roots of a polynomial, based on the polynomial’s coefficients. Such estimates appear in sources such as
[
8
,
35
]
, but any such bound can give a sufficient condition for the growth rate of a particular PLRS being smaller or greater than
\displaystyle\phi
. In particular, many results are only written as an upper bound. However, if
\displaystyle a
is an upper bound for the modulus of any root of the equation

(4.6)

-\sum^{L}_{i=1}c_{i}x^{i}+1\ =\ 0,

then
\displaystyle\frac{1}{a}
is a lower bound for the modulus of any root of the equation

(4.7)

x^{L}-\sum^{L}_{i=1}c_{i}x^{L-i}\ =\ 0,

because these two polynomials have roots which are multiplicative inverses. In other words, if
\displaystyle r_{1},\dots,r_{L}
are the roots of (
4.6
), then the roots of (
4.7
) are
\displaystyle\frac{1}{r_{1}},\dots,\frac{1}{r_{L}}
. Notice also that (
4.7
) is just (
4.3
) with a different name for the variable.

4.2.
Case
\displaystyle m_{0}\geq L+2

Treating the previous subsection as the backbone of our asymptotic analysis, specifically Proposition
4.4
, we consider separately, in the present and next two subsections, the cases
\displaystyle m_{0}\geq L+2
;
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
; and
\displaystyle m_{0}\leq L
. Each case yields different scenarios for the possible relationships between the Fibonacci sequence
\displaystyle\{F_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
and an otherwise arbitrary PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{m}\}^{\infty}_{m=1}
.

This identity is needed to fully analyze the
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+2
case when
\displaystyle L
is odd (the case of
\displaystyle L
being even is more straightforward).

Lemma 4.8
.

If
\displaystyle L\geq 3
is odd and
\displaystyle k\geq L+1
, we have

(4.8)

2F_{k-L+2}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}\ =\ F_{k}+F_{k-L}.

Proof.

We will proceed by induction on
\displaystyle L
; the base case is to prove that

(4.9)

2F_{k-1}\ =\ F_{k}+F_{k-3}

for all
\displaystyle k\geq 4
, but this follows immediately from the Fibonacci recurrence. Now we may assume for some arbitrary
\displaystyle L\geq 3
odd that (
4.8
) holds for all
\displaystyle k\geq L+1
, and to complete the induction we must show that

(4.10)

2F_{k-L}+\sum^{\frac{L+1}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i1}\ =\ F_{k}+F_{k-L+2}

holds for all
\displaystyle k\geq L+3
. If we rearrange the terms in (
4.8
) and (
4.10
), we observe that it suffices to prove that

(4.11)

2F_{k-L}+\sum^{\frac{L+1}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}-F_{k-L-2}\ =\ 2F_{k-L+2}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}-F_{k-L}.

Subtracting away terms that appear on both sides of (
4.11
) gives

(4.12)

3F_{k-L}-F_{k-L-2}\ =\ F_{k-L+2},

which is an immediate consequence of the Fibonacci recurrence. This completes the induction.
∎

Lemma 4.9
.

If
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+2
, then the following behaviors hold:

•

If
\displaystyle L
is even then
\displaystyle F_{m+1}=H_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}

•

If
\displaystyle L
is odd then
\displaystyle F_{m+1}<H_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\geq m_{0}
.

Proof.

Consider first the case where
\displaystyle L
is even. Then
\displaystyle F_{m+1}=H_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\{1,2,\dots,L+1\}
. One can easily check that a possible set of coefficients for the PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
is
\displaystyle c_{i}=1
for all odd
\displaystyle i
, and
\displaystyle c_{L}=1
(with all other coefficients being zero). Due to Proposition
4.3
, this is the only possible set of coefficients for the PLRS. Now we can check that
\displaystyle F_{m+1}=H_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
via strong induction. Our construction of the sequence
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
automatically assures that
\displaystyle L+1
base cases hold. Let
\displaystyle k\geq L+1
and assume that
\displaystyle F_{m+1}=H_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\leq k
, and we will show that
\displaystyle F_{k+2}=H_{k+1}
. This is demonstrated by the coefficients of
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
and the recurrence for the Fibonacci numbers:

(4.13)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} H_{k+1}\ &=\ H_{k-L+1}+\sum^{\frac{L}{2}-1}_{i=0}H_{k-2i}\ =\ F_{k-L+2}+\sum^{\frac{L}{2}-1}_{i=0}F_{k-2i+1}\\
\ &=\ F_{k-L+4}+\sum^{\frac{L}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}\ =\ \dots\ =\ F_{k+2},\end{aligned}

completing the proof when
\displaystyle L
is even.

Now consider the case where
\displaystyle L
is odd. We will need a slightly weaker version of Lemma
4.8
; precisely, we will use the inequality

(4.14)

2F_{k-L+2}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}\ \geq\ F_{k}.

By construction,
\displaystyle H_{m}=F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\{1,2,\dots,L+1\}
. One can easily check that a possible set of coefficients for the PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
is
\displaystyle c_{i}=1
for all odd
\displaystyle i
except
\displaystyle L
, and
\displaystyle c_{L}=2
(with all other coefficients being zero). Again invoking Proposition
4.3
, this is the only possible set of coefficients for the PLRS.

Let
\displaystyle k\geq L+2
and assume that
\displaystyle H_{m}>F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\{L+2,L+3,\dots,k\}
. Then to complete the proof, we must show that
\displaystyle H_{k+1}>F_{k+2}
. Due to our choice of coefficients, we have the recurrence

(4.15)

H_{k+1}\ =\ 2H_{k-L+1}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}-1}_{i=0}H_{k-2i}.

Due to the inductive hypothesis, we have that

(4.16)

H_{k+1}\ >\ 2F_{k-L+2}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}-1}_{i=0}F_{k-2i+1},

and then using the Fibonacci recurrence and (
4.14
), we proceed as follows:

(4.17)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} H_{k+1}\ &>\ 2F_{k-L+2}+F_{k}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}-1}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}\\
\ &>\ F_{k+1}+F_{k}\ =\ F_{k+2}.\end{aligned}

This completes the proof when
\displaystyle L
is odd.
∎

As a corollary of the proof of Lemma
4.9
, we see that if
\displaystyle m_{0}\geq L+2
, then either
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+2
(if
\displaystyle L
is odd) or
\displaystyle H_{m}=F_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
(if
\displaystyle L
is even). In addition, this lemma shows that there are PLRS of order other than
\displaystyle 2
that generate the Fibonacci numbers, an observation that coincides with the result of Example
4.6
.

We may now turn our attention to the scenarios where
\displaystyle m_{0}\leq L+1
.

4.3.
The
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
case

The purpose of this lemma is to understand a specific PLRS that serves as an “edge case" for analyzing asymptotic growth when
\displaystyle L
is odd.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
\displaystyle k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
and let
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
be the PLRS of order
\displaystyle 2k+1
with
\displaystyle c_{i}=1
for all odd
\displaystyle i\leq 2k-1
,
\displaystyle c_{i}=0
for all even
\displaystyle i\leq 2k
, and
\displaystyle c_{2k+1}\geq 3
. Then this sequence has the following properties in comparison to the Fibonacci numbers:

•

\displaystyle H_{j}=F_{j+1}
for all
\displaystyle j\leq 2k+1
;

•

\displaystyle H_{j}>F_{j+1}
for all
\displaystyle j>2k+1
.

Proof.

The first claim of this proof can be easily checked by using the definition of a PLRS and the given coefficients for
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
. The second claim will follow from a strong induction argument. The base case is where
\displaystyle j=2k+2
, so we show that
\displaystyle H_{2k+2}-F_{2k+3}>0
. We use the definition of the recurrence and the equalities
\displaystyle H_{j}=F_{j+1}
for all
\displaystyle 1\leq j\leq 2k+1
to calculate

(4.18)

H_{2k+2}-F_{2k+3}\ =\ \sum^{k}_{i=1}H_{2i+1}+3H_{1}-F_{2k+3}\ \geq\ \sum^{k-1}_{i=1}H_{2i+1}+3-F_{2k+1}\ =\ \dots{\\
}\ =\ H_{3}+3-F_{5}\ =\ 3+3-5\ =\ 1\ >\ 0.

Now we may proceed to the inductive step. Supposing that
\displaystyle H_{j}>F_{j+1}
for all
\displaystyle 2k+2\leq j\leq m
, we want to show that
\displaystyle H_{m+1}>F_{m+2}
. Once again, we may use the recurrence defining
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
, along with the inductive hypothesis, as follows:

(4.19)

H_{m+1}-F_{m+2}\ =\ \sum^{k}_{i=1}H_{m-2k+2i}+c_{2k+1}H_{m-2k}-F_{m+1}-F_{m}\ >\ \sum^{k-1}_{i=1}H_{m-2k+2i}+3H_{m-2k}-F_{m}\ \\
\geq\ \sum^{k-2}_{i=1}H_{m-2k+2i}+3H_{m-2k}-F_{m-2}\ \geq\ \dots\ \geq\ 3H_{m-2k}-F_{m-2k+1}-F_{m-2k}\ \geq\ H_{m-2k}\ >\ 0,

which completes the proof.
∎

Now we are able to fully dissect the case
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
, when
\displaystyle L
is either odd or even.

Lemma 4.11
.

If
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
, then the following behaviors take place:

•

If
\displaystyle L
is even and we choose
\displaystyle c_{L}\geq 2
, then
\displaystyle H_{m}>F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m\geq m_{0}
.

•

If
\displaystyle L
is odd and we choose
\displaystyle c_{L}=1
, then
\displaystyle H_{m}<F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m\geq m_{0}

•

If
\displaystyle L
is odd and we choose
\displaystyle c_{L}\geq 3
then
\displaystyle H_{m}>F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m\geq m_{0}

Proof.

First, consider what happens if
\displaystyle L
is even. From the condition
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
, we must have that
\displaystyle H_{k}=F_{k+1}
for all
\displaystyle k\leq L
. By using the definition of the recurrence for the PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
, it becomes clear that we must have
\displaystyle c_{i}=1
for all
\displaystyle i\leq L-1
that are odd, and
\displaystyle c_{i}=0
for all
\displaystyle i\leq L-1
that are even. Since the PLRS has order
\displaystyle L
, we must have
\displaystyle c_{L}\geq 1
. If
\displaystyle c_{L}=1
, then
\displaystyle H_{m}=F_{m+1}
, which contradicts the assumption that
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
. Thus we must have that
\displaystyle c_{L}\geq 2
. Considering the event where
\displaystyle c_{L}=2
, we can prove that
\displaystyle H_{m}>F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m\geq m_{0}=L+1
using strong induction. One can easily check the base case, that
\displaystyle H_{m_{0}}>F_{m_{0}+1}
. Now, assume that
\displaystyle H_{j}>F_{j+1}
holds for all
\displaystyle L+1\leq j\leq k
, and we want to show that
\displaystyle H_{k+1}>F_{k+2}
. This proof follows from utilizing the recurrence defining
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
, and the fact that
\displaystyle H_{j}\geq F_{j+1}
for all
\displaystyle j\leq k
:

(4.20)

H_{k+1}-F_{k+2}\ =\ \sum^{\frac{L}{2}}_{i=1}H_{k-L+2i}+2H_{k-L+1}-F_{k+1}-F_{k+2}\\
>\ \sum^{\frac{L}{2}-1}_{i=1}H_{k-L+2i}+2H_{k-L+1}-F_{k-1}-F_{k-2}\ \geq\ \dots\ \ \geq\ 2H_{k-L+1}-F_{k-L+2}-F_{k-L+1}\ \geq\ 0,

which completes the proof in this case. Among other things, this shows that if
\displaystyle L
is even and
\displaystyle m_{0}=L+1
, the PLRS
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
will have asymptotic growth at least equal to the one of the Fibonacci numbers.

Now we consider what happens if
\displaystyle L
is odd. Similarly to the proof of Lemma
4.9
, the values of most of the coefficients will be fixed since we need
\displaystyle F_{m}=H_{m}
for all
\displaystyle m\leq L
. We require that
\displaystyle c_{i}=1
for all odd
\displaystyle i
less than
\displaystyle L
, and
\displaystyle c_{i}=0
for all even
\displaystyle i
; the value of
\displaystyle c_{L}
must be positive since the PLRS has order
\displaystyle L
, but its value is flexible. If
\displaystyle c_{L}=2
then we end up in the scenario described in Lemma
4.9
, so now we consider other possible values of
\displaystyle c_{L}
. If
\displaystyle c_{L}=1
then the recurrence is

(4.21)

H_{k}\ =\ \sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}}_{i=0}H_{k-2i-1},

and one can easily check that
\displaystyle H_{m_{0}}<F_{m_{0}+1}
. We can then prove that
\displaystyle H_{m}<F_{m+1}
for all
\displaystyle m>m_{0}
via strong induction. To do so, we assume that
\displaystyle H_{j}<F_{j+1}
holds for some
\displaystyle L+1\leq j\leq k
, and prove that
\displaystyle H_{k+1}<F_{k+2}
. We apply the recursion (
4.21
) replacing
\displaystyle k
with
\displaystyle k+1
, and then use the inductive hypothesis to obtain

(4.22)

H_{k+1}\ =\ \sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}}_{i=0}H_{k-2i}\ <\ F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}}_{i=1}H_{k-2i}\ \leq\ F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}.

How we proceed from here changes slightly depending on the parity of
\displaystyle k
. If
\displaystyle k
is even then we can complete the induction proof via the following argument:

(4.23)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}\ \leq\ F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{k-2}{2}}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}+F_{2}\\
\ &=\ F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{k-4}{2}}_{i=2}F_{k-2i+1}+F_{2}\ =\ \dots\ =\ F_{k+1}+F_{k}\ =\ F_{k+2}.\end{aligned}

Meanwhile, if
\displaystyle k
is odd, then
\displaystyle k\geq L+1
implies that
\displaystyle k\geq L+2
, and we instead proceed this way:

(4.24)

\displaystyle\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{L-1}{2}}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}\ <\ F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{k-3}{2}}_{i=1}F_{k-2i+1}+F_{3}\\
\ &=\ F_{k+1}+\sum^{\frac{k-3}{2}}_{i=2}F_{k-2i+1}+F_{5}\ =\ \dots\ =\ F_{k+1}+F_{k}\ =\ F_{k+2}.\end{aligned}

On the other hand, if
\displaystyle c_{L}\geq 3
, Lemma
4.10
identifies the specific behavior that this sequence takes: namely, we will have
\displaystyle H_{k}=F_{k+1}
for all
\displaystyle k\leq L+1
, and then
\displaystyle H_{k}>F_{k+1}
for all
\displaystyle k\geq L+2
, which is exactly what we wanted to show.
∎

4.4.
The
\displaystyle m_{0}\leq L
case

The case
\displaystyle m_{0}\leq L
introduces substantial difficulties not present in the other cases, and we are mostly leaving this case open in this paper. The main issue is that Proposition
4.3
no longer applies. Indeed, there is no longer enough information to uniquely determine all coefficients of the PLRS. It turns out the coefficients that are not uniquely determined by the first few values of the PLRS can have their sizes manipulated to change the sequence’s growth rate. For instance, if
\displaystyle\{H_{j}\}^{\infty}_{j=1}
is a PLRS of order
\displaystyle L
for which the coefficients
\displaystyle c_{1},c_{2},\dots,c_{L-1}
are fixed, then there exists a possible value of
\displaystyle c_{L}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
for which
\displaystyle\lim_{m\rightarrow\infty}\frac{H_{m+1}}{H_{m}}>\phi
. This follows from the fact that the constant term of (
4.3
) is
\displaystyle-c_{L}
. As
\displaystyle c_{L}
becomes larger, the unique positive root of the growth polynomial can be made arbitrarily large.

5.
Concluding remarks

We conclude this paper by discussing some potential directions of further, future investigations. The first direction is to consider asymptotics of compositions where only a sub-sequence of the Fibonacci numbers is allowed, for instance only the even-indexed Fibonacci numbers. This could serve as a more combinatorial companion to the recent paper
[
15
]
, which provides conditions on which decompositions of natural numbers into sub-sequences of Fibonacci numbers exist and are unique. Such a generalization is not necessarily encompassed by our theory of PLRS, because the first term may no longer be equal to
\displaystyle 1
.

Another possibility would be to extend our PLRS theory to recurrences where the coefficients need not to be integers. This would give us a richer class of sequences to compare to each other in our asymptotic framework. Notably, the theory in section
4
does not specifically use the property that PLRS are integer sequences, but only that certain coefficients of the recurrence defining the PLRS were positive. The trade-off, however, is that we would no longer be studying integer composition problems.

Finally, Section
4
of this paper left partially open the characterization of finding which PLRS sequences have greater asymptotic growth than the Fibonacci numbers, and which have smaller such growth. While some results exist to bound the modulus of roots of growth polynomials from above and below, we have not identified a complete characterization of which growth polynomials fall into which category.
