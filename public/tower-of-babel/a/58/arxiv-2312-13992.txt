Bayesian nonparametric boundary detection for income areal data
Matteo Gianella, Mario Beraha, Alessandra Guglielmi

Abstract
Recent discussions on the future of metropolitan cities underscore the pivotal role of (social) equity, driven by demographic and economic trends. More equal policies can foster and contribute to a city’s economic success and social stability. In this work, we focus on identifying metropolitan areas with distinct economic and social levels in the greater Los Angeles area, one of the most diverse yet unequal areas in the United States. Utilising American Community Survey data, we propose a Bayesian model for boundary detection based on areal income distributions. The model identifies areas with significant income disparities, offering actionable insights for policymakers to address social and economic inequalities. We have multiple observations (i.e., personal income of survey respondents) for each area, and our approach, formalised as a Bayesian structural learning framework, models areal densities through mixtures of finite mixtures. We address boundary detection by identifying boundaries for which the associated geographically contiguous areal densities are estimated as being very different without resorting to dissimilarity metrics or covariates. Efficient posterior computation is facilitated by a transdimensional Markov Chain Monte Carlo sampler. The methodology is validated via extensive simulations and applied to the income data in the greater Los Angeles area. We identify several boundaries in the income distributions, which can be explained ex-post in terms of the percentage of the population without health insurance, though not in terms of the total number of crimes, showing the usefulness of such an analysis to policymakers.

Introduction

For decades, Southern California has stood as an area, drawing people from across the Globe. Los Angeles (LA), by far the most populous city in California, has been a trailblazer in the national demographic shift: while the United States (US) is anticipated to be a people-of-colour majority country by 2044, the Los Angeles area crossed that threshold in the 1980s
(
PolicyLink, 2022
, see)
. However, since the early 1990s recession, LA has been a hurdle for California’s economic growth, failing to match the state’s advancements in education, health, and employment. This divergence has fuelled rising inequality, with LA emerging as a primary driver of statewide economic disparities. Particularly alarming are the poverty and working poverty rates, which have been higher in the LA area compared to the national averages since 1990 and showing an increasing trend opposite to the national trend
(
Matsunaga, 2008
)
. As of 2021, nearly 17% of LA residents live below the poverty line, with a working poverty rate of 7.7%, in contrast to the national level of 5.6%
(
United States Census Bureau, 2021
, data from)
. Economic inequality is strongly related to racial inequality, as people of colour are disproportionally affected. For instance, the 2017 Report on Equity Profile of the LA region
(
PolicyLink and PERE, 2017
)
revealed that almost a quarter of LA county’s African Americans (24.5%) and Latinos (23.7%) live below the poverty level, compared to 10.6% of white people. The latest Census Bureau data corroborates these disparities. The recent COVID-19 pandemic has honed the inequality gap in such an uneven context. Los Angeles county alone has registered one third of state cases, with approximately 40% of total deaths
(
Ong et al., 2020
;
Chang et al., 2022
;
Prager et al., 2022
)
.

Demographic and economic analyses have found that inequality hinders economic growth and prosperity while fostering economic and racial inclusion encourages greater economic mobility and growth
(
Berg et al., 2018
;
Ncube et al., 2021
)
. Additionally, criminological studies, such as
Hipp, 2007
, highlight that a more significant economic and racial inequality in neighbourhoods is associated with higher crime rates.
Promoting interactions between different neighbours, thus reducing social inequality, may lead to a significant reduction in crimes like burglary, vehicle theft, and robbery
(
Bellair, 1997
)
. All this underscores the interconnectedness of economic and social factors, emphasising the need for inclusive policies to promote regional prosperity. Urban policies must target the most fragile areas of the city, where inequality usually strikes the hardest.

To this end, in this paper, we consider the problem of identifying metropolitan areas with strikingly different economic and social levels.
Working on data from the Public Use Microdata Sample (PUMS) of the American Community Survey (ACS)
(
United States Census Bureau, 2023
, see)
, we aim to identify boundaries in LA that delineate areas with significant differences in income distributions. Specifically, we focus on the greater Los Angeles area (i.e., Los Angeles, Ventura, and Orange counties) and consider the personal income of survey respondents as a proxy for their economic status. The ACS data, collected annually, encompass information on incomes, jobs, and education. However, a limited number of geographic summaries are available in the PUMS dataset for confidentiality reasons. In particular, the finest unit of geography contained there is the so-called Public Use Microdata Area (PUMA), which are spatial non-overlapping areas that partition each state into contiguous geographic units containing roughly 100,000 people. PUMAs, having been created for demographic reporting, do not represent a useful partition for urban planners and policymakers, as they may not adequately represent the true spatial heterogeneity within larger metropolitan regions. This limitation is especially pertinent for densely populated areas, where multiple small PUMAs may coexist, and for less urbanised regions, where a single PUMA might cover a vast geographical area. In contrast, policymakers would need to detect larger zones where the social indicators are homogeneous while identifying possible “danger areas” as the borders between two areas with significantly different economic and social indicators.

We frame the problem as one of
boundary detection
. Boundary analysis methods are routinely used to identify borders (or zones) that distinguish different spatial regions. The typical main assumption underneath such methods is a strong geographical correlation or dependence in the variable of interest across nearby areas. If, for some neighbouring areas, such correlation is not observed in the data, this is used as evidence of the presence of a
boundary
. This procedure has been proposed, for instance, to highlight different patterns in disease mapping
(
Lee and Mitchell, 2012
;
Li et al., 2015
;
Gao et al., 2023
;
Aiello and Banerjee, 2023
, see, e.g.,)
or in environmental applications
(
Qu et al., 2021
)
. Typically, for boundary detection problems, only one observation is available for each areal unit, together with several covariates used to assess similarities and differences among areas.

In our context, instead, we have multiple observations (i.e., personal income of survey respondents) for each area, and we address boundary detection by identifying boundaries that separate areal units with significantly different area-specific densities of incomes from their neighbours. We do not have extra information, i.e. covariates, on each area in the spatial domain we consider, and hence our model does not use dissimilarity metrics between areas. Instead, we provide a Bayesian model to analyse the income data of the ACS and detect differences among the densities of the annual income between neighbouring PUMAs. In particular, area-specific income densities are assumed as random parameters. Unlike existing methods, our model detects a
boundary
if two neighbouring areal units show negligible spatial dependence between the corresponding income densities, thus producing borders when the annual income distributions are estimated as different. Intuitively, the estimated boundary underlines where the income gap, as represented by a random density, is more marked. This information holds potential for policymakers aiming to devise interventions that address social and economic inequalities. With our approach, it is not necessary to include areal covariates or a dissimilarity matrix in the model.

We ground the boundary detection problem in a Bayesian structural learning framework
(
Lauritzen, 1996
)
, assuming a prior distribution for the adjacency graph that represents the spatial structure. In each PUMA, we model the distribution of the income via a finite mixture of Gaussian distributions with a random number of components, a well-established method for approximating any density

(
Ghosal and Van der Vaart, 2017
, Section 2.3.3)
. To induce dependence across neighbouring areas, we build on the spatial mixture model introduced in
Beraha et al., 2021
by assuming a logistic multivariate CAR prior for the weights of the mixture, and this prior incorporates the random adjacency graph. In particular, this allows us to address boundary detection by identifying boundaries for which the associated geographically contiguous areal densities are estimated as being very different without resorting to dissimilarity metrics or covariates, as previously mentioned. A key difference is that, while
Beraha et al., 2021
fixes the number of components
H
to a “large” value and proposes a sparse prior for the mixture weights, following the classical procedure to deal with overfitted mixtures, we treat
H
as a random variable. This approach is necessary because the non-identifiability of overfitted mixtures
(
Rousseau and Mengersen, 2011
, see)
severely affects the ability to identify boundaries, as it will be explained later in greater detail. To compute the posterior distribution, we design a suitable transdimensional Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampler akin to
Green, 1995
. This approach ensures robust boundary detection when the number of mixture components is unknown.

As mentioned, available boundary detection approaches typically assume one observation for each areal unit, together with dissimilarity metrics. Hence, we compare our model against existing boundary detection models and algorithms using data summaries from the California census income dataset. In some cases, we also incorporate dissimilarity metrics if needed. Some competitor models or heuristic techniques are outperformed by our approach, as they fail to distinctly separate spatially contiguous areas based on the associated summary statistics. Two models have detected boundaries that partially overlap with those identified by our approach. Summing up, we show that competitor models struggle to detect boundaries between spatially contiguous areas with different densities but similar summary statistics. However, the boundaries detected by models incorporating dissimilarity metrics are driven more by these differences than by disagreements in the summary statistics.

2
A model for boundary detection

Consider observations
\boldsymbol{y}=\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{y}_{I}\right)
, where
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}=\left(y_{i,1},\dots,y_{i,N_{i}}\right)
for
i=1,2,\dots,I
and
j=1,2,\dots,N_{i}
, where
y_{i,j}
is the observation of individual
j
in area
i
. Each vector
\boldsymbol{y}_{i}
is associated with an area
i
, and
N_{i}
is the number of observations in such an area, which might differ through areas. In the application discussed in
Section

5
, we will use PUMAs as the areal unit. With notation
i\sim k
, we mean that areas
i
and
k
are geographically contiguous areas, meaning that they share at least a border. We represent the spatial dependence across areas through a random
I\times I
binary matrix (or adjacency graph)
G
. This graph is defined as follows:
(a)

G_{i,i}=0
for every
i=1,\dots,I
;
(b)

G_{i,k}=0
if the corresponding areas are not geographically contiguous (i.e., if
i\not\sim k
);
(c)

G_{i,k}
is a binary random variable, i.e.,
G_{i,k}\in\{0,1\}
, for all edges
(i,k)
such that
i\sim k
. We define the set
\{(i,k):i\sim k\}
as the set of
admissible edges

\Eadj
. Then, if two geographically contiguous areas
i
and
k
are such that
G_{i,k}=1
, they are called
neighbouring areas
and the associated graph edge
(i,k)
is a
neighbouring edge
. Similarly, if
G_{i,k}=0
for two geographically contiguous ares
i
and
k
, they are called
boundary areas
and the associated edge
(i,k)
is a
boundary edge
.

Given these definitions, let us describe how boundary detection is performed. As part of the Bayesian model, we assume
G
random with some (marginal) prior. All the inference on the boundary detection problem is based on the (marginal) posterior distribution of
G
, obtained via the MCMC algorithm we introduce below and detail in
Section

A.1
of the SM. Then, it is straightforward to estimate
\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y})
for any
(i,k)\in\Eadj
. An
estimated boundary edge
is an edge
(i,k)\in\Eadj
such that
\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y})<\gamma
, i.e., for which the posterior probability
\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y})
that there is an edge between areas
i
and
k
is smaller than a threshold
\gamma\in(0,1/2]
. Conversely, an
estimated neighbouring edge
is an edge
(i,k)\in\Eadj
such that
\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y})\geq\gamma
. The estimated
boundary graph

\hat{G}^{(b)}
collects all the estimated boundary edges, while the estimated
neighbouring graph

\hat{G}^{(n)}
collects all estimated neighbouring edges. Strictly speaking,
\hat{G}^{(b)}
and
\hat{G}^{(n)}
are disjoint collections of estimated edges such that
\hat{G}^{(b)}\cup\hat{G}^{(n)}=\Eadj
. In this model, we assume that an isolated areal unit
i
(e.g., an island) does not have admissible edges, i.e., there exists no area
k
such that
i\sim k
. As a consequence, no boundaries can be found between an island
i
and other areas. Of course, in different applications, assuming that the set of admissible edges of an island
i
is non-empty might be relevant.

Since we are interested in detecting differences in income distribution across geographically contiguous areas, the first building block is a model for spatially-dependent density estimation. To this end, we model the density in each area via a Gaussian mixture model, given their well-known ability to approximate any density (under mild conditions; see
Ghosal and Van der Vaart, 2017
, Section 2.3.3). We assume

y_{i,j}\mid\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)},\boldsymbol{\tau},H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize ind}}}{{\sim}}f_{i}(\cdot;H):=\sum_{h=1}^{H}w_{i,h}\,\mathcal{N}\left(\,\cdot\mid\tau_{h}\right),\text{ for }j=1,\dots,N_{i},\ i=1,\dots,I,

(2.1)

where
\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)}=\left(w_{i,1},\dots,w_{i,H}\right)
is a vector in the
H
-dimensional simplex
S^{H}
, i.e.,
w_{i,h}\geq 0
for all
h
and
\sum_{h}w_{i,h}=1
, and
\mathcal{N}\left(\cdot\mid\tau_{h}\right)
denotes the the Gaussian density with parameters
\tau_{h}=(\mu_{h},\sigma^{2}_{h})
, being
\mu_{h}
the mean and
\sigma^{2}_{h}
the variance. Observe that the parameters
\tau_{h}=1,\ldots,H
, in (
2.1
) are common across different areas: this assumption is often made when modelling related densities since it allows the adoption of a more parsimonious model but preserves the flexibility we need for density estimation
(
Quintana et al., 2022
, see, e.g.,)
. Moreover, note that data within each area
i
,
y_{i,j},j=1,\dots,N_{i}
, are assumed conditionally independent and identically distributed from
f_{i}
.

We take the Bayesian approach and complete the likelihood (
2.1
) with a joint prior for parameters
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{H})
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)}
’s and
H
. As it is standard in Bayesian nonparametric models, we assume

\tau_{h}=(\mu_{h},\sigma_{h}^{2})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize iid}}}{{\sim}}P_{0},\ h=1,\ldots,H,

(2.2)

where
P_{0}
is the normal-inversegamma density, i.e.,
P_{0}(\dd\mu_{h},\dd\sigma_{h}^{2})={\mathcal{N}}(\dd\mu_{h};\mu_{0};\sigma_{h}^{2}/\lambda)\times\invGamma\-(\dd\sigma_{h}^{2};c,d)
. The conditional prior of
\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)}
, given
H
and the adjacency graph
G
, assumes the strong spatial correlation across the observations we expect to see in the data. To incorporate this prior knowledge into the model, given that the
\tau_{h}
’s are shared, we induce spatial dependence through the prior on the weights by assuming a logistic multivariate conditionally autoregressive (CAR) prior
(
Beraha et al., 2021
)
as the marginal joint prior for
(\boldsymbol{w}_{(1)},\dots,\boldsymbol{w}_{(I)})
. That is, we let:

\displaystyle w_{i,h}

\displaystyle=\frac{\operatorname{e}^{\widetilde{w}_{i,h}}}{1+\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\operatorname{e}^{\widetilde{w}_{i,h}}}\quad h=1,\dots,(H-1),

\displaystyle w_{i,H}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1+\sum_{h=1}^{H-1}\operatorname{e}^{\widetilde{w}_{i,h}}}.

(2.3)

and assume a multivariate CAR distribution with parameters
\rho
,
\sigma^{2}
and the graph
G
for
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(1)},\-\dots,\-\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(I)})
:

vec(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(1)},\dots,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(I)})\mid\sigma^{2},G,H\sim\mathcal{N}_{I(H-1)}\left(\boldsymbol{0},\left(\left(F-\rho G\right)\otimes\dfrac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{I}_{H-1}\right)^{-1}\right),

(2.4)

where
\mathbf{I}_{H-1}
is the identity matrix of dimension
(H-1)
and
F=diag(\rho\sum_{k}G_{1,k}+(1-\rho)
,
\dots,\rho\sum_{k}G_{I,k}+(1-\rho))
,
\rho
and
\sigma^{2}
positive. Note that (
2.4
) is defined in terms of a multivariate CAR model, generalising the univariate CAR model in
Leroux et al., 2000
. See
Beraha et al., 2021
for further details and properties. Parameter
\rho
describes the global level of spatial correlation between the areas, where
\rho=0
represents independence and values of
\rho
close to 1 stand for strong spatial association. Then, the random graph
G
represents the local correlation structure between the areas. In the rest of the paper, we fix
\rho
to a value close to 1 to encourage spatial association. Note that fixing
\rho
to a large value is common practice in CAR models for boundary detection (when
G
is random) since the spatial correlation structure can be determined locally by
G
rather than globally by
\rho

(
Lee and Mitchell, 2012
;
Lee, 2013
, see, e.g.,)
. We will assume
G
random (see below) since
G
is the relevant parameter for boundary detection, as underlined at the beginning of this section. However, also assuming
\rho
random clearly introduces non-identifiability in (
2.4
). The marginal prior we assume for
\sigma^{2}
is standard, i.e.,

\sigma^{2}\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(\alpha/2,\beta/2),\ \alpha,\beta>0,

(2.5)

where the prior mean of
\sigma^{2}
is
\beta/(\alpha-2)
when
\alpha>2
. For the graph
G
we assume:

\begin{split}G_{i,k}\mid p&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize iid}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{Be}(p)\quad\text{ for all }(i,k)\in\Eadj;\\
p&\sim\operatorname{Beta}\left(a,b\right),\ a,b>0.\end{split}

(2.6)

From the above equation, it is clear that parameter
p
identifies the (random) prior probability of edge inclusion, i.e.,
p=\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=1)
, for each admissible edge. Then, according to our definition of boundary edge,
1-p=\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=0)
,
(i,k)\in\Eadj
, represents the prior probability of having a boundary edge between geographically contiguous areas. The Beta marginal prior is a default choice when modelling the probability of success.

Summing up so far, the random parameters of our model are
\boldsymbol{\tau}
,
\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)}
,
H
,
G
and
\sigma^{2}
. We have multiple observations in each area, assumed i.i.d. according to the mixture model (
2.1
). Our model addresses boundary detection by identifying boundaries
(i,k)
for which the associated vector of mixture weights
\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{(k)}
are estimated as being very different using the logistic multivariate CAR prior (
2.4
) containing
G
. Since the area-specific densities differ only through the mixture weights, we identify as boundaries those edges which separate areal units with significantly different area-specific densities from their neighbours.

The final building block of our model is the marginal prior for the number of components
H
. Indeed, as already mentioned in the Introduction, fixing
H
leads to undesired consequences for boundary detection. This deals with the well-known non-identifiability of mixture models
(
Rousseau and Mengersen, 2011
)
. In particular, within model (
2.1
), if
\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)}\approx\boldsymbol{w}_{(j)}
, then
f_{i}\approx f_{j}
, but the reverse is not true if
H
is too large.

To understand the issue, we provide two examples. Consider, first, two areas and suppose that data, in both areas, are distributed according to a mixture of
H_{0}
Gaussian kernels. If, under our model, we fix
H=H_{0}
, the model is identifiable and no issue is expected. On the other hand, if
H>H_{0}
, for a common set of atoms
\boldsymbol{\tau}
we can have multiple and different configurations of weights
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{2}
that approximate well the data-generating process. In particular, assume that the true density is

f_{0}\left(\cdot\right)=\sum_{h=1}^{3}w_{0,h}\,\mathcal{N}\left(\cdot\mid\mu_{0,h},\sigma_{0}^{2}\right),

with
\boldsymbol{w}_{0}=(0.3,0.5,0.2)
,
\boldsymbol{\mu}_{0}=\left(-2,0,2\right)
and
\sigma_{0}^{2}=0.5
.
If we set
H=6
and choose a common set of atoms
\boldsymbol{\mu}=(-2.1,-1.9,-0.1,0.1,1.9,2.2)

in the density

\widetilde{f}\left(\boldsymbol{w}\right)=\sum_{h=1}^{H}w_{h}\,\mathcal{N}\left(\,\cdot\mid\mu_{h},\sigma_{0}^{2}\right),

which has the same structure as the likelihood in (
2.1
), there exist at least two configurations,
\boldsymbol{w}_{1}=(0.15,0.15,0.25,0.25,0.1,0.1)
and
\boldsymbol{w}_{2}=(0.29,0.01,0.01,0.49,0.199,0.001)
, for which both
\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})
and
\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{2})
approximate
f_{0}
well enough; see
Figure

2.1
. As a result, if two geographically contiguous areas share the same density, model (
2.1
)-(
2.6
) would not be able to detect such similarity based solely on the mixture weights. The same argument also holds for geographically contiguous areas with different densities. This issue might result in poor boundary detection since the full-conditional of the graph
G
depends on the weights; see
Section

A.1
in the Supplementary Material.

Example of non-identifiability with overfitted mixtures. The black dashed line is
f_{0}
. The blue and orange lines are the mixtures
\widetilde{f}(\boldsymbol{w}_{1})
and

of six Gaussian kernels.

The issue described above is not purely hypothetical, but, on the contrary, it is often encountered in practice. Let us consider a simulated dataset with
I=36
of areas in a unit-squared domain. Depending on the area, we simulate
100
i.i.d. data points either
(i)
from a Student’s
t
distribution with
6
degrees of freedom, centred in
4
and with standard deviation equal to
1.5
(see the blue areas in
Figure

3(a)
); or
(ii)
from a Skew Normal distribution with location
\xi=4
, scale
\omega=1.3
and shape
\alpha=-3
parameters. This implies that, if we define
\delta=\alpha/\sqrt{1+\alpha^{2}}
, the mean of the distribution is
\xi+\omega\delta\sqrt{2/\pi}\approx 3.016
and the variance is
\omega^{2}(1-2\delta^{2}/\pi)\approx 0.722
(see the orange areas in
Figure

3(a)
). We apply model (
2.1
)-(
2.6
) and run the MCMC sampler for a total of 10,000 iterations, discarding the first half as burn-in. We compute
\mathbb{P}\left(G_{i,j}=1\mid\boldsymbol{y}\right)
for every admissible edge when we fix the number of components to a reasonably small value (
H=3
) and to a larger number, i.e.,
H=10
. As the cut-off threshold for boundary detection, we choose
\gamma=0.5
, i.e., compute the posterior median (boundary) graph. We observe that, when
H=3
, we achieve perfect boundary detection: the true boundary and the posterior median boundary graphs coincide. This is not true when
H=10
, as probabilities
\mathbb{P}(G_{i,j}=1\mid\mathbf{y})
are all between
0.9992
and
1
, implying that the boundary graph is empty for every reasonable value of
\gamma
(
\leq 0.5
). This indicates that boundary detection cannot be achieved when the number of components is fixed too large. We will complete this simulated case study in
Section

4.2
.

These two examples show that fixing
H
might be problematic for boundary detection. Parameter
H
must be learned from the data, and we assume as its marginal prior a shifted Poisson distribution, and we write:

H-1\sim Poi(\Lambda)

(2.7)

for
\Lambda>0
. Notice that, if
H=1
a.s., though the model is well defined, there is only one mixture weight in each area, which equals
1
. In this case, no boundaries will be found, since all estimated densities will be identical.

To conclude the section, we introduce, for each observation
y_{i,j}
, the corresponding latent cluster allocation variable
s_{i,j}
for
j=1,\dots,N_{i}
and
i=1,\dots,I
. Each latent variable indicates the component of the mixture to which the corresponding observation is allocated. This representation will be useful to derive a Gibbs sampler for our model. We rewrite (
2.1
) as:

\displaystyle y_{i,j}\mid s_{i,j}=h,\tau_{h},H

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\left(\,\cdot\mid\tau_{h}\right)\quad j=1,\dots,N_{i}\text{ and }i=1,\dots,I,

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(s_{i,j}=h\mid\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)},H\right)

\displaystyle=w_{i,h}\quad h=1,\dots,H.

(2.9)

A component in the mixture is said to be empty if no observations have been allocated to such a component. In the following, we will denote any allocated component as a cluster, and the number of clusters is the number of allocated components. From (
2.8
)-(
2.9
), it is clear that the allocated and empty components, as well as the number of clusters, are random variables, with marginal prior distributions induced by our model. We include them in the state space of the MCMC and compute their marginal posterior through the Gibbs sampler.

3
Posterior computation

From the conditional distribution of the data in (
2.8
)-(
2.9
) and the prior (
2.2
)-(
2.7
), we implement a transdimensional MCMC algorithm to obtain the joint posterior distribution of the number of components
H
, the vector of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}=(\boldsymbol{W},\boldsymbol{\tau},\boldsymbol{s}_{1},\dots,\boldsymbol{s}_{I})
, whose dimension depends on
H
, and the remaining global parameters
(\sigma^{2},G)
. Here,
\boldsymbol{W}
is the
I\times H
matrix whose
i,h
-th element is the mixture weight
w_{i,h}
, while
\boldsymbol{s}_{i}=\left(s_{i,1},\dots,s_{i,N_{i}}\right)
, for

i
and introduced in (
2.8
)-(
2.9
). Note that the support of each
s_{i,j}
is
\{1,\dots,H\}
. A transdimensional sampling algorithm (as the reversible jump MCMC) usually consists of two major steps:
(i)
a
between models move
which consists in the joint update of
H
and the corresponding parameter vector, and
(ii)
a
within model move
which implements, conditionally to
H
, a sampling scheme to update the vector of parameters
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}
,
\sigma^{2}
and

. We give more details on both moves here below.

3.1
Between-models move

The reversible jump MCMC sampler
(
Green, 1995
)
provides a general framework for transdimensional simulation schemes. It can be viewed as an extension of the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm. As it happens in standard Metropolis-Hastings, given the current state of the chain
(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H})
, the next state
(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}})
is sampled from a proposal distribution
q[(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}),(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}})]
and accepted with probability

\alpha\left[\left(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\right),\left(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}}\right)\right]=\min\left\{1,\frac{\pi\left(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}}\mid\boldsymbol{y}\right)\,q\left[(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}}),\left(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\right)\right]}{\pi\left(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\mid\boldsymbol{y}\right)\,q\left[\left(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\right),(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}})\right]}\right\}.

Usually, the proposal distribution is defined in two steps. If
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{H}}
and
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{H^{\prime}}}
, with
n_{H^{\prime}}>n_{H}
and
d=n_{H^{\prime}}-n_{H}
, first a random vector
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sampled from a distribution
q_{d}(\boldsymbol{u})
and then
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}}
is defined as
g_{H\rightarrow H^{\prime}}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H},\boldsymbol{u})
for a suitable mapping function
g_{H\rightarrow H^{\prime}}
that maps
\mathbb{R}^{n_{H}}
into
\mathbb{R}^{n_{H}^{\prime}}
. Since both the proposal distribution
q_{d}(\boldsymbol{u})
and the mapping function
g_{H\rightarrow H^{\prime}}
are arbitrary, the definition of an efficient between-models move might be a difficult task.

The approach we follow is based on
Norets, 2021
, where the author defines optimal auxiliary priors and proposals for generic nested models indexed by an integer
H
in
\{1,2,\dots\}
with unknown parameter
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}
and prior of the form
\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\mid H\right)\pi\left(H\right)
. Since the models are nested, the unknown parameters are nested as well, i.e., if
H^{\prime}>H
, the first
H
elements of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}}
correspond to vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}
. Given the current state
(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H})
, consider moving to
(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}})
with
H^{\prime}=H+1
. We denote the parameter vector associated with the extra component by
\boldsymbol{\theta}
. The joint distribution for
\left(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}},H\right)
is given by:

f\left(\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}},H\right)=\widetilde{\pi}_{H}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H},\boldsymbol{y}\right)\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\mid H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\right)\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}\mid H)\pi(H),

where
\widetilde{\pi}_{H}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H},\boldsymbol{y}\right)
needs to be defined. We choose such prior as the conditional posterior distribution of the extra component, i.e.,

\displaystyle\widetilde{\pi}_{H}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H},\boldsymbol{y}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y},H+1,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}),

\displaystyle\propto\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\mid H+1,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H+1}\right)\pi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H+1}\mid H+1\right).

This choice guarantees optimal conditions in terms of overall chain mixing and minimisation of the estimated variance, as shown in
Norets, 2021
. Note that mixture models are not generally considered as nested models. This is typically due to the usual assumption of a Dirichlet prior for the weights. Instead, our logisticMCAR prior lends itself naturally to the nested model framework much like the mixture of experts models analysed in
Norets, 2021
. Moreover, the proposal that adds one mixture component while leaving the others unchanged is along the same spirit of modern MCMC algorithms for Bayesian (nonparametric) mixture models. Think, for instance, of Algorithm 8 in
Neal, 2000
, the birth-death sampler in
Stephens, 2000
, the slice sampler in
Kalli et al., 2011
, and the conditional sampler in
Argiento and De Iorio, 2022
. The main difference is that with our approach, the new component is selected by taking the data into account, while in the other papers mentioned, the new components are proposed from the prior distribution, making our approach potentially more efficient.

Nonetheless, the optimal posterior
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y},H+1,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H})
is not known in closed form, so we use its Laplace approximation. Such approximation is justified by the Bernstein–von Mises theorem and the asymptotic behaviour of the (conditional) posterior distribution we consider: see, e.g.,
Walker, 1969
and the references therein. Note that sampling the extra component
\boldsymbol{\theta}
from its (approximated) conditional posterior distribution
\pi(\boldsymbol{\theta}\mid\boldsymbol{y},H+1,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H})
avoids the artificial construction of proposal distributions and mapping functions. Moreover, since we have marginalised w.r.t. the latent cluster allocation variables, the dimension of the proposal distribution is sensibly reduced. This mitigates the well known problem of poor mixing for multidimensional Metropolis-Hastings simulation schemes
(
Robert, 2014
, see, e.g.,)
.

3.2
Within-model move

The update of the parameter vector
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}
,
\sigma^{2}
and
G
, given
H
, is rather standard. The within-model move is obtained repeatedly sampling parameters as follows:

1.

For any
i=1,\dots,I
and
j=1,\dots,N_{i}
, independently update the cluster allocation variables from its full conditional distribution, using a Gibbs sampler update;

2.

Independently update the atoms
\boldsymbol{\tau}
of the mixture from its full conditional distribution, via a Gibbs sampler update;

3.

For each
i=1,\dots,I
and
h=1,\dots,H-1
, sample the transformed weights
\widetilde{w}_{i,h}
via an augmented Gibbs sampler update. This update is made introducing a latent Polya-Gamma random variable
\omega_{i,h}
and sampling sequentially from the full conditional
\pi(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{-(i,h)},\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\sigma^{2},\omega_{i,h};\rho)
, now available in closed form. We denote with
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{-(i,h)}
the matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}
without its
(i,h)
-th element. Details of this step can be found in
Beraha et al., 2021
, Section 5.

4.

Sample
\sigma^{2}
from its full conditional distribution;

5.

For any admissible edge
\left(i,j\right)
such that
i\sim j
, sample the corresponding graph edge
G_{i,j}
from its full conditional. This allows for the update of multiple graph edges in a single MCMC iteration, unlike other types of sampling strategies: see, for instance, the BDMCMC algorithm for Gaussian graphical models in
Mohammadi and Wit, 2015
.

5.3
Understanding the estimated boundary

In this paper, we provide a Bayesian model to analyse i.i.d. individual log-income data from areal units, with the aim of detecting boundaries among the units themselves. The key random parameters are the area-specific densities. Our model detects a
boundary
if two geographically contiguous areal units show negligible spatial dependence between the corresponding income densities, thus producing borders when the annual income distributions are estimated as different. Intuitively, the estimated boundary underlines where the income gap, as represented by a random density, is more marked. We do not include extra information in the model, such as dissimilarity metrics based on area-specific covariates.

However, if we focus only on the LA county PUMAs, extra information for each area can be collected. In particular, we consider the total number of crimes and the percentage of the population without health insurance. From the literature, it is known that lower income and social inequity are related to crimes
(
Hipp, 2007
)
and that income and healthcare are strongly associated
(
Braveman et al., 2010
)
. Monthly crime statistics in the LA county are openly available on the official Los Angeles County Sheriff’s Department site. We consider all the crimes recorded during 2020 (the same year as the income data). The proportion of the population without health insurance in LA is available on the Los Angeles County open data portal
(
County of Los Angeles, 2023
)
. In this section, we investigate if areas separated by a boundary by our model present relevant differences also in these two extra variables. We consider the estimated boundaries detected by our model between PUMAs of the LA county.

Figure

5.5
(left panel) shows the heatmap of the total number of crimes grouped by PUMA, with the boundaries detected by our model in red. There is no relevant difference in this variable’s values in PUMAs estimated as boundary areas, pointing out that the number of crimes might not be associated with economic inequality, unlike expected; see
Hipp, 2007
and references therein.
Figure

5.5
(right panel) shows the percentage of people without medical insurance for every PUMA in LA County, together with the boundaries detected by our model. From the figure, we see that the areas with a higher percentage of citizens without medical insurance are located in the centre and south of LA, as expected. Moreover, the estimated boundaries separate areas where this variable is different. Of course, this is expected since access to health insurance is highly correlated to a high income. Summing up, we found that the estimated boundary areas can be
explained
in terms of the percentage of population without health insurance. However, the total number of crimes does not seem to
explain
the estimated boundary areas we found.

6
Discussion

This paper focuses on income data within the Los Angeles Bay Area, comprising three counties in California. The city of Los Angeles has not kept pace with California advancements in education, health, and employment, with persistent uneven wage growth being a major driver of economic inequality. Racial disparities are a key factor, with African Americans and Latinos in LA County disproportionately living below the poverty line compared to white residents. The boundary detection model introduced in this paper proves valuable in identifying areas with significant disparities in population income. For policymakers, urban planners, and politicians committed to fostering more equitable policies, such information is instrumental in strategically planning interventions to mitigate social and economic inequalities.

Our study demonstrates the model’s ability to detect markedly different population
densities between geographically contiguous areas, validated through simulation and real-case scenarios, without the intervention of extra information such as other area-specific covariates. Specifically, our analysis reveals a clear division in the city of Los Angeles, with a wealthier bay area, encompassing affluent neighbourhoods like Beverly Hills, and a less affluent region covering downtown LA and the southern part of the city. This division aligns with findings from various studies and reports by sociologists, economists, and policymakers, affirming the robustness of the inference our model provides. We uncover results that affirm existing knowledge regarding economic and social disparities in major urban areas from the perspective of urban sociology. Notably, our approach adds value by validating established findings across various disciplines using Bayesian methods, thereby contributing to a more robust empirical foundation. Our method detects boundaries based on the estimated densities of the multiple observations per area, unlike other existing methods which assume uni- or multi-dimensional areal summary statistics.

As mentioned in the Introduction, there exists a vast literature on CAR priors or their generalisations for boundary detection, all defined in the case of a single response per area and often in the presence of dissimilarity metrics between area-specific covariates. The logistic Multivariate CAR prior in (
2.4
) adapts and extends CAR priors to the context of multiple observations per area, while prior (
2.6
) controls boundary detection without any kind of covariates. Alternative distributions to CAR priors in case of areal data include the DAGAR priors, both univariate
(
Datta et al., 2019
)
and multivariate. Recently,
Aiello and Banerjee, 2023
have extended this prior to boundary detection in the case of multivariate areal data. DAGAR priors might provide a computational advantage over CAR priors, since the undirected graph
G
is replaced by a directed acyclic graph (DAG), which makes posterior inference faster. However, the definition of this DAG depends on an arbitrary topological ordering of the areas. In the case of irregular grids, as the one we consider here, there are no theoretical guarantees that posterior inference is invariant w.r.t. the ordering of the nodes. Moreover, the adoption of CAR priors lead us to an efficient sampling MCMC scheme for all parameters involved without introducing any approximation in
G
, while a DAGAR-like prior in our context would have required a much more complex update for the graphical structure. We proved that our model detects boundaries when the associated estimated areal densities for the variable of interest (the log-income) are different, even when summary statistics are similar. Simpler Bayesian models applied to area-specific empirical quantiles can only detect boundaries driven by those summary statistics themselves or driven by dissimilarities based on extra covariates which might not be available in general.

Unlike previously proposed approaches to Bayesian boundary detection, our model is able to detect boundaries even without the aid of area-specific covariates since the difference between the area-specific densities of the data drives our inference. However, additional information from covariates can be easily incorporated into the model. For instance, integrating area-specific covariates can inform the boundary detection process by replacing (
2.6
) with a probit or logit regression model

\mathbb{P}(G_{i,k}=1)=g(\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})),

where
\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})
is a vector-valued dissimilarity function based on covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
,
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
of areas
i
and
k
(e.g.,
\Phi(\boldsymbol{x}_{i},\boldsymbol{x}_{k})_{\ell}=\lvert x_{i,\ell}-x_{k,\ell}\rvert
) and
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is a (random) parameter; see, for instance,
Lee and Mitchell, 2012
. Individual and area-specific covariates can be easily incorporated into the model by modifying the mixture kernel as follows

y_{i,j}\mid\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\sigma},H\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize iid}}}{{\sim}}\sum_{h=1}^{H}w_{i,h}\mathcal{N}(\cdot\mid\beta_{h}^{T}x_{i,j},\sigma^{2}_{h}).

This mixture model can be considered as a finite dimensional version of the “single-weight” dependent Dirichlet process mixture
(
Quintana et al., 2022
)
. This adaptability underscores the versatility of our model in accommodating diverse data sources for robust boundary detection.

Posterior inference requires reversible jump MCMC moves, whose computational challenges are well-known. The high dimensionality of the proposal distribution implies that the mixing of the chain for
H
is not extremely good. However, we propose an algorithm that does not get stuck in local modes of
H
, and, despite the initial value, we always obtain the same posterior distribution. Moreover, by combining the
optimal proposal
of
Norets, 2021
and an efficient
C++
code implementation, available via the
R
package
SPMIX
, we were able to scale inference up to a dataset with almost
80,000
observations while keeping a reasonable runtime. Scaling our approach to massive datasets with millions of observations or high dimensional parameters seems nontrivial. In particular, we believe that adapting ideas from
Miller and Harrison, 2018
and
Argiento and De Iorio, 2022
can lead to the development of other spatially-dependent priors for which posterior inference is more efficient (in terms of chain mixing) and less demanding (but still likely unfeasible for millions of data). Another alternative is to resort to Consensus Monte Carlo algorithms. The key idea of these algorithms is to split the data into subsets (usually called shards), perform posterior inference through MCMC in each subset in parallel and then combine the posterior inferences. This is an approximate method that avoids full posterior simulation with the full dataset; since the MCMC algorithms in each shard use only a portion of the whole dataset, they show a better mixing. The resulting algorithm scales much better w.r.t. the size of the dataset, since the most challenging parts can be executed in parallel and with no communication between cores. However, there is little or no work for Consensus Monte Carlo algorithms in the context of spatial data in the literature so far.

between-models move
which consists in the joint update of

which implements, conditionally to

\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H}

and

within-model move

The state of the MCMC, given the number of components

\{s_{i,j}\}_{i,j}
for
i=1,\dots,I
and
j=1,\dots,N_{i}
, the common mixture atoms
\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{H})

(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(1)},\dots,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(I)})
(where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i)}=\alr(\boldsymbol{w}_{(i)})
for

, the graph sparsity parameter

•

Independently update the components of the common mixture atom vector from

\pi\left(\tau_{h}\mid rest\right)\propto P_{0}\left(\tau_{h}\right)\textstyle\prod_{i,j\,:\,s_{i,j}=h}\,\mathcal{N}\left(y_{i,j}\mid\tau_{h}\right),\quad h=1,\dots,H.\\

•

For
i=1,\dots,I
,
j=1,\dots,N_{i}
, independently update the cluster allocation variables from

\pi\left(s_{i,j}=h\mid rest\right)\propto\invalr\left(w_{i,h}\right)\,\mathcal{N}\left(y_{i,j}\mid\tau_{h}\right),\quad h=1,\dots,H.\\

•

For each
i=1,\dots,I
and each
h=1,\dots,H
independently sample the transformed weight
\widetilde{w}_{i,h}
via the augmented Gibbs sampler. The augmentation technique goes as follows: we start from the full conditional for
\widetilde{w}_{i,h}
,

\pi(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{-(i,h)},rest)\propto\pi(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{-(i,h)},\rho,\sigma^{2})\times\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i),-h}),

(A.1)

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i),-h}
denotes the vector
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i)}
once component
h
has been removed. Now:

\pi(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{-(i,h)},\rho,\sigma^{2})\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu^{*}_{i,h},\Sigma^{*}_{i,h}\right),

where

\displaystyle\mu^{*}_{i,h}

\displaystyle=\mu_{i,h}+\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h,-h}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{-h,-h}^{-1}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i,-h}-\boldsymbol{\mu}_{i,-h}),

\displaystyle\Sigma^{*}_{i,h}

\displaystyle=(\rho\textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{I}G_{i,k}+1-\rho)^{-1}(\Sigma_{h,h}-\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{h,-h}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{-h,-h}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{-h,h}).

The second factor in the right-hand side of (
A.1
)
can be written as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i),-h})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\operatorname{e}^{\eta_{i,h}})^{N_{i,h}}}{(1+\operatorname{e}^{\eta_{i,h}})^{N_{i}}}

(A.2)

\displaystyle=2^{-N_{i}}\operatorname{e}^{(N_{i,h}-N_{i}/2)\eta_{i,h}}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\operatorname{e}^{-\omega\eta_{i,h}^{2}/2}\pi(\omega)\mathrm{d}\omega,

where
\eta_{i,h}=\widetilde{w}_{i,h}-\log\sum_{h^{\prime}\neq h}\operatorname{e}^{\widetilde{w}_{i,h^{\prime}}}
,
N_{i}
is the number of observations in area
i
and
N_{i,h}
is the observations in area
i
assigned to component
h
. The second equivalence in (
A.2
) comes from
Polson et al., 2013
and
\pi(\omega)
is the density of a Polya-Gamma distribution of parameters
(N_{i},0)
. Please, refer to the same paper for details about the definition and properties of Polya-Gamma random variables. Hence, introducing an auxiliary Polya-Gamma random variable
\omega_{i,h}\sim\operatorname{Polya-Gamma}(N_{i},0)
for each
i
and
h
, we can disintegrate the above measure w.r.t.
\omega_{i,h}
and obtain closed-form expressions for the full conditionals of
\widetilde{w}_{i,h}
and
\omega_{i,h}
. Finally, the augmented Gibbs sampler step consists of the following steps:

i.

sample the auxiliary Polya-Gamma random variable
\omega_{i,h}
from

\pi\left(\omega_{i,h}\mid\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i)}\right)=\operatorname{Polya-Gamma}\left(N_{i},\widetilde{w}_{i,h}-\log\textstyle\sum_{h^{\prime}\neq h}\operatorname{e}^{\widetilde{w}_{i,h^{\prime}}}\right)

for each
i,h
;

ii.

sample the transformed weight
\widetilde{w}_{i,h}
from the augmented full conditional distribution

\pi(\widetilde{w}_{i,h}\mid\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}_{-(i,h)},\boldsymbol{s}_{i},\sigma^{2},\omega_{i,h})=\mathcal{N}(\hat{\mu}_{i,h},\hat{\Sigma}_{i,h}),

where the parameters of the Gaussian random variables are the following:

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{i,h}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{\mu^{*}_{i,h}}{\Sigma^{*}_{i,h}}+N_{i,h}-\frac{N_{i}}{2}+\omega_{i,h}\log{\textstyle\sum}_{h^{\prime}\neq h}\operatorname{e}^{\widetilde{w}_{i,h^{\prime}}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{1}{\Sigma_{i,h}^{*}}+\omega_{i,h}\bigg)^{-1},

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}_{i,h}

\displaystyle=\bigg(\frac{1}{\Sigma^{*}_{i,h}}+\omega_{i,h}\bigg)^{-1};

•

for any
(i,k)\in\Eadj
, independently sample edge
G_{i,k}
from

\displaystyle\pi\left(G_{i,k}=1\mid rest\right)

\displaystyle\propto\exp\left\{\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)+\frac{\rho}{2\sigma^{2}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i)}^{\prime}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(k)}\right\},

\displaystyle\pi\left(G_{i,k}=0\mid rest\right)

\displaystyle\propto 1;

(A.3)

•

Sample the graph sparsity parameter
p
from

\pi\left(p\mid rest\right)=\textstyle\operatorname{Beta}\left(a+\sum_{(i,k)\in\Eadj}G_{i,k},b+\lvert\Eadj\rvert-\sum_{(i,k)\in\Eadj}G_{i,k}\right);

•

sample the common variance
\sigma^{2}
from

\pi\left(\sigma^{2}\mid rest\right)=\operatorname{Inv-Gamma}\left(\alpha_{p}/2,\beta_{p}/2\right),

where the posterior parameters are given by

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}

\displaystyle=\alpha+I(H-1),

\displaystyle\beta_{p}

\displaystyle=\beta+{\textstyle\sum}_{i,k=1}^{I}(F-\rho G)_{i,k}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i)}^{\prime}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(k)}.

Between-models move

The general idea behind the between-models move has already been presented in
Section

3
of the paper. Here, we specify the details in the case of our model. Specifically, the conditional posterior distribution becomes the joint law
\pi(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1}\mid\boldsymbol{y},H+1,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{\tau})
. Such distribution, in an implicit form, is proportional to

\prod_{i=1}^{I}\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}_{i}\mid\invalr\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{(i)},\widetilde{w}_{i,H+1}\right),\left(\boldsymbol{\tau},\tau_{H+1}\right),H+1\right)\prod_{h=1}^{H+1}\pi\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(h)}\mid\sigma^{2},G,H+1\right)\pi\left(\tau_{h}\mid H+1\right).

Its Laplace approximation is a multivariate Gaussian distribution with parameters

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}

\displaystyle=\argmax_{\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1}\right)}\log\pi(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1}\mid\boldsymbol{y},H+1,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{\tau});

(A.4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{V}^{*}

\displaystyle=-\mathbf{H}^{-1}\left(\log\pi(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1}\mid\boldsymbol{y},H+1,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{\tau})\right)\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*}\right),

(A.5)

where
\mathbf{H}\left(f\right)\left(\cdot\right)
denotes the Hessian of function
f
evaluated in
\cdot
.

The parameter vector
(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1})
, associated to the component we aim to propose is sampled from
\widetilde{\pi}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{V}^{*})\approx\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\right)
. While in the original reversible jump MCMC by
Green, 1995
, the proposed state is generated by the composition of a random proposal distribution and a deterministic mapping function, in this work, we rely on a direct sample of the state of the new component.

We now focus on the computation of the reversible jump acceptance ratio with current state
(H,\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H})
and proposed state
(H^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{H^{\prime}})
, with
H^{\prime}
equal to either
H+1
or
H-1
. At each iteration, we either decide to increase or reduce the problem dimension with equal probability. To simplify notation, let
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{+}=(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)})
,
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+}=\left(\boldsymbol{\tau},\tau_{H+1}\right)
. Moreover, when we write
\alr\left(\boldsymbol{A}\right)
and
\boldsymbol{A}
is a matrix, we mean that the additive log ratio transformation is applied to each row of matrix
\boldsymbol{A}
. Then, in case
H^{\prime}=H+1
, the move is accepted with probability
\min\left(1,A_{H,H+1}\right)
, where

\displaystyle A_{H,H+1}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\invalr(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{+}),\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+},H+1\right)\pi\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{+},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{+}\mid\sigma^{2},G,H+1\right)\pi\left(H+1\right)}{\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\invalr(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}),\boldsymbol{\tau},H\right)\pi\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{\tau}\mid\sigma^{2},G,H\right)\pi\left(H\right)}

(A.6)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\frac{1}{\widetilde{\pi}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H+1)},\tau_{H+1}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\right)}.

On the other hand, in case
H^{\prime}=H-1
, a random component
r
between
1
and
H-1
is selected as a candidate for the removal. Then, we define
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-}
as the matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}
after column
r
has been removed and, similarly, we define the vector
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{-}
. Then, the move is accepted with probability
\min\left(1,A_{H,H-1}\right)
, where

\displaystyle A_{H,H-1}=\,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\invalr(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-}),\boldsymbol{\tau}^{-},H-1\right)\pi\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}^{-},\boldsymbol{\tau}^{-}\mid\sigma^{2},G,H-1\right)\pi\left(H-1\right)}{\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{y}\mid\invalr(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}}),\boldsymbol{\tau},H\right)\pi\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{W}},\boldsymbol{\tau}\mid\sigma^{2},G,H\right)\pi\left(H\right)}

(A.7)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\widetilde{\pi}\left(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}^{(H)},\tau_{H}\mid\boldsymbol{\mu}^{*},\boldsymbol{V}^{*}\right).

A.4
Comparison with competitor models

In this section, we compare our model (called
SPMIX
in the following) with other models for boundary detection available in the literature. To the best of our knowledge, existing models and algorithms perform boundary detection only in the case of a single response per area, while our model achieves the same goal in the case of multiple responses in each geographical unit. In order to compare
SPMIX
with other methods, we need to compute summary statistics of the data in each area. As in the manuscript, we denote by
y_{i,j}
observation
j
in area
i
, for
j=1,2,\dots,N_{i}
and
i=1,\ldots,I
. As summary statistics of the data, we consider the five-dimensional vector of empirical quantiles for area
i

(q_{i,\alpha_{1}},\ldots,q_{i,\alpha_{5}})\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
, where
q_{i,\alpha}
is the empirical quantile of order
\alpha
for
y_{i,1},\ldots,y_{i,N_{i}}
. We assume
(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{5})=(0.05,0.25,0.5,0.75,0.95)
. Moreover, let
\boldsymbol{q}_{\alpha}=(q_{1,\alpha},\ldots q_{I,\alpha})
be the vector of
\alpha
-quantiles for all the areas, for any
\alpha
. In the following, we use the notation

\boldsymbol{1}_{p}
for the unit vector in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
, and
\boldsymbol{I}_{p}
is the
p\times p
identity matrix.

A.4.1
Naive MCAR model for boundary detection

We consider here an alternative MCAR model for boundary detection, which was suggested by a referee. The model for boundary detection based on the empirical quantiles assumes

\boldsymbol{q}_{\alpha}\mid\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\alpha},\tau^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\alpha},\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{I}\right)\qquad\alpha\in\{0.05,0.25,0.50.75,0.95\},

(A.8)

where
\boldsymbol{\psi}_{\alpha}
is the
I
-dimensional vector of the spatial random effect associated to the
\alpha
-quantiles. We jointly model the vector of spatial random effects
vec(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.05},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.25},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.5},\-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.75},\-\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.95})
, conditionally on the graph
G
and the local variance
\sigma^{2}
, as follows:

vec\left(\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.05},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.25},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.5},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.75},\boldsymbol{\psi}_{0.95}\right)\mid\sigma^{2},G\sim\operatorname{MCAR}\left(\boldsymbol{1}_{5}\otimes\widetilde{\boldsymbol{m}},\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{5}\otimes(F-\rho G)^{-1}\right).

(A.9)

Note that we assume the variance
\sigma^{2}
to be constant over the areas since, in our examples, the variability associated to each vector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\alpha}
is quite similar. We complete the model as follows:

\begin{split}G_{i,k}\mid p&\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize iid}}}{{\sim}}\operatorname{Be}(p)\ \text{for all }(i,k)\in\Eadj\\
p&\sim\operatorname{Beta}\left(a,b\right),\ a,b>0\\
\sigma^{2}&\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(\alpha_{s},\beta_{s}),\ \alpha_{s},\beta_{s}>0\\
\tau^{2}&\sim\operatorname{InvGamma}(\alpha_{t},\beta_{t}),\ \alpha_{t},\beta_{t}>0.\end{split}

(A.10)

In particular, note that the prior for the graph
G
matches the one under our spatial mixture model.

Observe that model (
A.8
)-(
A.9
) is not adequate to model a vector of quantiles, as it disregards the fact that, in each area,
(q_{i,0.05},\ldots,q_{i,0.95})
is a nondecreasing sequence. However, such a model can perform boundary detection based on the differences between the vector of empirical quantiles in geographically contiguous areas, as the full conditional of
G_{i,k}
(
(i,k)\in\Eadj
) is

\pi\left(G_{i,k}=1\mid rest\right)\propto\exp\left\{\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right)-\frac{\rho}{2\sigma^{2}}\|\Psi_{i}-\Psi_{k}\|^{2}\right\},\ \pi\left(G_{i,k}=0\mid rest\right)\propto 1,

where
\Psi_{i}=(\psi_{i,0.05},\ldots,\psi_{i,0.95})
. That is, the probability of detecting an edge is directly proportional to the
L_{2}
distance between the random effects associated with the quantiles in each area. In particular, such a distance can also be regarded as a discretization of the Wasserstein distance between random effects, therefore justifying the use of model (
A.8
)-(
A.10
) for boundary detection. For notational convenience, we refer to (
A.8
)-(
A.10
) as the
naive MCAR
in the rest of this section.

The hyperparameters of the
naive MCAR
model are chosen as follows. We set
\boldsymbol{\widetilde{m}}=m_{0}\boldsymbol{1}_{I}
, where
m_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
is equal to the empirical mean of
vec(\boldsymbol{q}_{0.05},\dots,\boldsymbol{q}_{0.95})
. For
\sigma^{2}
and
\tau^{2}
, we fix
\alpha_{s}=\beta_{s}=\alpha_{t}=\beta_{t}=2
, which is a common choice in the literature. The prior for
G
matches the one for our spatial mixture model, namely
p\sim\mathrm{Beta}(2,I)
,
I
being the number of areas; moreover, the global spatial association parameter
\rho
is set equal to
0.95
. The MCMC algorithm for posterior computation for the naive MCAR has been implemented in JAGS
(
Plummer et al., 2003
)
via the
rjags
package, storing a total of
1,000

A.4.2
SKATER algorithm for regionalisation

SKATER
(
Assunção et al., 2006
, Spatial ‘K’luster Analysis by Tree Edge Removal,)
is a regionalisation method based on graph partitioning.
Regionalisation
refers to the process of grouping a set of areal units (e.g., census tracts, municipalities, or districts) into a smaller number of contiguous regions that are internally homogeneous based on specific attributes. This procedure typically ensures that the resulting regions are spatially contiguous (i.e., they share boundaries and form a connected area). This definition makes it clear that regionalisation is more closely related to clustering than boundary detection. However, one could reasonably interpret the borders between different regions as boundaries. Although this interpretation slightly alters the traditional definition of a boundary, it allows us to compare the posterior inference from our model with the output from the SKATER algorithm. This algorithm first defines a minimum spanning tree (MST) from the adjacency graph that encodes the spatial structure of the data. Then, regionalisation is achieved by optimally pruning the MST.

We apply the SKATER algorithm as implemented in the
R
package
spdep

(
Bivand, 2022
)
using as observation in each area
i
the vector of empirical quantiles
(q_{i,0.05},q_{i,0.25},q_{i,0.5}
,
q_{i,0.75},q_{i,0.95})\in\mathbb{R}^{5}
. While constructing the minimum spanning tree, SKATER requires the computation of the adjacency graph, which is typically undirected and can be either weighted or unweighted. The
weight
or
cost
associated with each edge is usually determined by covariate-based dissimilarity metrics between geographically contiguous regions, with a higher cost reflecting a greater dissimilarity. For a fair comparison with our model that does not make use of covariates, we exclude dissimilarity metrics in the adjacency graph computation. In
Figure

A.7
, we display the boundaries detected by SKATER, defined as borders between areas assigned to different regions, for various edge-pruning specifications. It is important to note that pruning
K
edges results in the partition of the areal units into
K+1
regions.

(a)

K=5

(b)

K=10

Figure A.7:
Heatmap of the empirical means of the California Census data in each area, with estimated boundaries (by SKATER) marked in red (left); associated boxplots (right) between
l_{1}
distance of the data in neighbouring and boundary couples of areas. Panels (a) and (b) refer to
K=5,10
pruned edges, respectively.

There are similarities between the boundaries detected by our model and those identified by the SKATER algorithm. Both approaches tend to distinguish the central part of Los Angeles from the wealthier Bay Area, with some boundaries also observed in Orange County. However, a key limitation of the SKATER algorithm is that the number of tree pruning must be specified manually and cannot be learned from the data. Consequently, any value between
0
and the total number of areas can yield different results. The algorithm also lets the user specify the minimum and maximum number of areas to be estimated in each region, which makes the inference outcome even more sensitive to the input parameters. Moreover, the minimum spanning tree required by the algorithm is usually computed using a greedy algorithm that finds a local, but generally sub-optimal, solution using a node as a starting point. This entails that, according to the starting node, the MST can be different and since pruning happens only between branches of the MST, the regions identified by SKATER can differ.

To compare our model with the boundaries detected using the SKATER algorithm, we aim to replicate the global comparison described in
Section

5.2
of the manuscript, where we have computed the
L_{1}
distances between posterior densities for the set of neighbouring areas and the set of boundary areas. Since the output of the SKATER algorithm simply consists of a list of labels assigning each area to the corresponding region, in this case, we can only compute distances between the input data for the set of neighbouring areas and the set of boundary areas. We use the
l_{1}
distance between vectors, i.e., given two vectors
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{q}
,
d_{l_{1}}(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x^{\prime}})=\sum_{l=1}^{q}\lvert x_{l}-x^{\prime}_{l}\rvert
.
Figure

A.7
shows the boxplots of the
l_{1}
distances in the set of neighbouring and boundary areas when the number of tree prunings
K
is equal to
5
and
10
. In both cases, the boxes of the two boxplots span over the same values, suggesting that the algorithm has found no significant difference between the neighbouring and boundary areas; compare, instead, with
Figures

4(b)
and
4(a)
in the manuscript, where the
L_{1}
distances between our estimated densities in the set of neighbouring areas are smaller than those between boundary areas.

A.4.3
CARBayes model for boundary detection

The
CARBayes
package
(
Lee, 2013
)
is a well-known
R
package for modelling areal data using conditional autoregressive (CAR) priors within a Bayesian framework via MCMC simulation. It supports a broad range of models for areal data, handling both univariate and multivariate response variables that follow binomial, Gaussian, multinomial, Poisson, or zero-inflated Poisson distributions. We use
CARBayes
to fit the following model:

\displaystyle y_{i}\mid\psi_{i},\tau^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\psi_{i},\tau^{2})\qquad i=1,\dots,I;

\displaystyle\tau^{2}

\displaystyle\sim\invGamma(a,b);

where
y_{i}
represents the observed data in area
i
. The model can be applied only to cases with one datapoint per each area. The spatial random effect is denoted as
\boldsymbol{\psi}=(\psi_{1},\dots,\psi_{I})
, and it is modelled using the class of CAR priors.

For comparison, we consider the boundary detection model proposed by
Lee and Mitchell, 2012
, which uses the CAR prior from
Leroux et al., 2000
, defined as:

\psi_{i}\mid\psi_{-i},G,\rho,m,\sigma^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\frac{\rho\sum_{k}G_{i,k}\psi_{k}+(1-\rho)m}{\rho\sum_{k}G_{i,k}+(1-\rho)},\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\rho\sum_{k}G_{i,k}+(1-\rho)}\right),

and achieves boundary detection using covariate-based dissimilarity metrics. The model detects
boundaries
between areas with distinct characteristics. The model assumes that each admissible edge
G_{i,k}
is a binary random variable, modelled as
G_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
according to:

\displaystyle G_{i,k}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1&\text{if }\exp\left(-\sum_{l=1}^{q}z_{l,i,k}\,\alpha_{l}\right)\geq 0.5\text{ and }i\sim k\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases};

\displaystyle\alpha_{l}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mbox{\scriptsize ind}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{U}(0,M_{i})\qquad l=1,\dots,q,

In the above equation,
z_{l,i,k}
represents the
l
-dissimilarity metric between areas
i
and
k
,
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\dots,\alpha_{q})
is the regression parameter vector that controls the influence of the dissimilarity metrics, and
q
is the number of dissimilarities used. Very often, dissimilarities
z_{l,i,k}
,
l=1,\ldots,q
, between areas
i
and
k
are assumed as the difference in the values of a vector of
q
areal covariates
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
. The right endpoint
M_{i}>0
of the support of the marginal prior of
\alpha_{i}
is fixed depending on the values of the
z_{l,i,k}
. This model is implemented in the
CARBayes
package via the
S.CARdissimilarity()
function. In the rest of the section, we refer to this specific model as the
CARBayes
model.

The comparison between
SPMIX
and
CARBayes
is not straightforward. Our model does not use covariates nor dissimilarity metrics and is designed for multiple observations per area, while the
CARBayes
model requires a single, univariate response variable per area and depends on dissimilarity metrics. To address this, we assume the response variable
y_{i}
as the empirical median
q_{i,0.5}
in each area, and we augment our dataset with a list of dissimilarity metrics
\{\boldsymbol{Z}^{l}\}_{l=1}^{q}
, where each
\boldsymbol{Z}^{l}
is a
I\times I
dissimilarity matrix, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{Z}^{l}=[z^{l}_{i,k}]_{i,k}
. We specify two different sets of dissimilarity metrics, using the exogenous variables introduced in
Section

5.3
of the paper, to interpret the boundaries estimated by
SPMIX
. Focusing solely on LA County, we calculate, for each PUMA:
(a)
the number of recorded crimes in 2020, and define the dissimilarity metric
\boldsymbol{Z}^{a}
by setting
z^{a}_{i,k}
equal to the difference, in absolute value, of the number of crimes between areas
i
and
k
, for all
(i,k)\in\Eadj
;
(b)
the percentage of people without health insurance in 2020, and define the dissimilarity metric
\boldsymbol{Z}^{b}
by setting
z^{b}_{i,k}
equal to the difference, in absolute value, of the percentage of uninsured people between areas
i
and
k
, for all admissible edges
(i,k)
.
