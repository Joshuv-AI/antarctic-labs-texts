Model implementations of axion dark matter from kinetic misalignment
Cem Eröncel, Ryosuke Sato, Géraldine Servant, Philip Sørensen

Abstract
The axion kinetic misalignment mechanism (KMM) opens the possibility of explaining dark matter for almost any axion mass and decay constant that are not accessible by the standard misalignment mechanism, in particular at low values of the axion decay constant (i.e. large coupling). This is a new opportunity for most axion experiments which could be sensitive to dark matter and probe new regimes of axion cosmology. We scrutinise UV completions that lead to the KMM mechanism. These mainly rely on the early dynamics of the axion partner, the radial mode of the complex scalar field, from which the axion inherits kinetic energy. The damping of the radial-mode energy density is then a necessary ingredient. We study in detail thermal damping from interactions in the plasma. A minimal and rather natural implementation consists of a KSVZ-type model with a nearly-quadratic potential for the radial mode extended by U(1)-breaking higher-dimensional operators. Furthermore, we study Higgs portal interactions as an alternative damping mechanism and improve upon previously proposed implementations based on quartic potentials. These implementations can lead to the QCD axion being dark matter and in the reach of IAXO, while MADMAX, IAXO and ALPS II can be sensitive to a generic Axion-Like-Particle (ALP) as dark matter. Such models typically feature a kination era. We also show that ALP dark matter from KMM points to a particular realization of inflation.

2
Axion dark matter from kinetic misalignment

The dynamics required to start the rotation of the axion relies on radial dynamics of the full complex scalar field
P
.
We refer to the complex field
P
as the Peccei-Quinn (PQ)
[
27
]
field although our analysis is not restricted to the QCD axion but applies to all possible ALPs.

P
is decomposed in terms of the angular mode
\theta
(the axion) and the radial mode
\phi
as

\displaystyle P=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\phi e^{i\theta}.

(1)

Rotation of the field then corresponds to
\dot{\theta}\neq 0
, and it is conveniently measured by the PQ charge density

\displaystyle n_{\rm PQ}=\dot{\theta}\phi^{2},

(2)

which is the conserved charge related to the ALP shift symmetry:
\theta\to\theta+\delta
and which serves as the analogue of angular momentum of the field.

We will consider potentials for
P
which give the radial mode a ground state vacuum expectation value (VEV) of

\expectationvalue{\phi}=f_{a}.

(3)

The dimensionful axion field is given by
\theta f_{a}
and we will denote its mass by
m_{a}
. We will show how the rotation arises, but for now, let us consider the evolution after the onset of the rotation.

2.1
Relic abundance

As long as shift-symmetry-breaking effects are absent,
n_{\rm PQ}
is conserved in comoving space, i.e.
n_{\rm PQ}\propto a^{-3}
where
a
is the scale factor of the universe. It is therefore convenient to normalize the PQ charge to the entropy density,
s
, and use the yield parameter

\displaystyle Y=\frac{n_{\rm PQ}}{s},

(4)

which is constant in time as long as PQ charge and entropy are conserved.

We consider the scenario of a QCD axion or an ALP with a similar temperature-dependent potential. To fit the lattice result for QCD
[
28
]
we parametrize this temperature-dependent mass as

\displaystyle m_{a}^{2}(T)\approx m_{a}^{2}\times\begin{cases}(T/T_{c})^{-\gamma}&\text{if }T>T_{c}\\
1&\text{if }T<T_{c}\end{cases},\quad\mbox{where}\quad\begin{gathered}T_{c}=2.12\times\Lambda_{b,0},\\
\Lambda_{b,0}=\sqrt{f_{a}m_{a}},\\
\gamma=8.16,\end{gathered}

which sets the amplitude of a cosine potential for the axion:

\displaystyle V_{a}(T)\approx m_{a}(T)^{2}f_{a}^{2}\left[1-\cos(\theta)\right].

(8)

In the notation used here,
m_{a}
always refers to the zero-temperature value observed today and
m_{a}(T)
is used whenever the temperature-dependent value is referred to. As
m_{a}(T)
does not play a role in the initial kick dynamics and only enters near the time of fragmentation, modifying the fit of
m_{a}(T)
will at most affect constraints related to
T_{*}
defined below.
In most of our analysis,
f_{a}
and
m_{a}
are free parameters and characterise both the QCD axion and a generic ALP. We apply the same formula Eq. (
2.1
) to the generic ALP scenario for simplicity.

The relic axion number density is
n_{a}\approx 2n_{\rm PQ}

[
1
,
15
]
, such that the present-day axion relic energy density is

\displaystyle\rho_{a}\approx 2m_{a}n_{\rm PQ}.

(9)

In
[
15
]
we show that the relation between
n_{a}
and
n_{\rm PQ}
arises from the approximately conserved quantity known as the
action variable
. The relevant quantity to calculate is therefore
n_{\rm PQ}
as parametrized by the yield
Y=n_{\rm PQ}/s
. Demanding that the axion relic density matches the observed DM relic density is therefore equivalent to demanding
2Y=Y_{\rm DM}
, where the observed DM yield
Y_{\rm DM}
is

\displaystyle Y_{\rm DM}\approx\frac{3}{4}\frac{g_{*}(T_{\rm eq})}{g_{*s}(T_{\rm eq})}\frac{T_{\rm eq}}{m_{a}}\approx 0.64\frac{T_{\rm eq}}{m_{a}}.

(10)

where
T_{\rm eq}
is the temperature of the universe at matter-radiation equality.

The axion field keeps overshooting the periodic barriers of the axion potential as long as its kinetic energy exceeds the barrier height. The rotation is slowed down by Hubble friction while the potential barriers grow larger according to Eq. (
2.1
). Eventually, at temperature
T_{*}

In

\displaystyle T_{*}\approx(2.12)^{\frac{\gamma}{2+\gamma}}\left(2\times 10^{8}\right)^{\frac{2}{6+\gamma}}\left(\frac{g_{*}}{72}\right)^{-\frac{2}{6+\gamma}}\left(m_{a}f_{a}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{6+\gamma}}\left(\frac{h^{2}\Omega_{\phi,0}}{h^{2}\Omega_{\rm DM}}\right)^{-\frac{2}{6+\gamma}},

(11)

where
[

15
]

\displaystyle\left(\frac{h^{2}\Omega_{\phi,0}}{h^{2}\Omega_{\rm DM}}\right)\approx\quantity(\frac{m_a}{5\times 10^{-3}\,\rm{eV}})\quantity(\frac{Y}{40}),

(12)

One main point of this paper will be to extract predictions for

for any
(m_{a},f_{a})
as a function of the UV parameters of the theory that control the size of the kick, such as the mass of the radial mode
m_{\phi}

Axion fragmentation

A main result of

3
Ingredients for a KMM implementation

3.1
Starting the rotation

The Affleck-Dine-inspired mechanism which we explore in this work (and which is also reviewed in details in
[
22
]
) relies on two key features:

PQ-violating operators:

These are needed to give a kick to the axion from the motion of the radial mode.
They arise as higher-dimensional operators and can be motivated in SUSY models
[
20
]
. They are of the form

\displaystyle\frac{A}{n}\frac{P^{n}}{M^{n-3}}+\frac{A}{n}\frac{P^{*n}}{M^{n-3}}=\frac{2A}{n2^{n/2}}\cos(n\theta+\delta_\theta)\frac{\phi^{n}}{M^{n-3}},

(13)

where,
M
is a suppression scale which we typically set to the Planck scale
m_{\rm Pl}
, and
\delta_{\theta}
is a phase in the potential, which in general is not aligned with the minimum at
\theta=0
. As will be discussed further in Section
6
, SUSY motivates
A\sim{\cal O}(m_{\phi})
. The key idea is that even though this potential plays a major role initially, it rapidly becomes negligible as
\phi
decreases from a large VEV to
f_{a}
.

Large VEV driver:

To probe such higher-dimensional operators, the VEV of the PQ field has to be driven to a large initial value. A large initial VEV for
\phi
is an essential component of the constructions we consider and will be justified in Sec.
6
. It has so far been realistically justified only in the context where the
P
field acquires a VEV before or during inflation. This means that the axions we are considering fall into the so-called category of
“pre-inflationary”
axions.

A model with the above two components can start a rotation of the PQ field. The resulting
\dot{\theta}
right after the kick can be found by solving the equation of motion, which predicts
[
20
,
6
]

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{\rm kick}\simeq 2^{1-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{A\phi_{\rm kick}^{n-2}\sin(n\thetakick)}{M^{n-3}m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}|_{\phi=\phi_{\rm kick}}},

(14)

where
\theta_{\rm kick}
is the initial misalignment angle. Likewise,
\phi_{\rm kick}
is the amplitude of the radial mode at the time of the kick and
\dot{\theta}_{\rm kick}
is the angular velocity produced by the kick. The potential for the radial mode is denoted by
V
while the potential for the angular mode is denoted
V_{a}
.
We are working with large field values, so the usual mass defined by
V^{\prime\prime}
is not the relevant parameter as this is defined at the potential minimum. Instead, we define the effective radial mode mass

\displaystyle m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}\equiv\sqrt{\frac{V^{\prime}}{\phi}},

(15)

as this is the parameter that enters the equation of motion and thus the relevant parameter far from the minimum
[
7
]
.
This result is independent of the mechanism which sets the large VEV.

It is useful to parametrize the angular velocity inherited from the kick with the parameter
\epsilon

[
1
]
, which gives the angular velocity at the apoapsis of the field orbit as a fraction of the velocity required to maintain a circular orbit. If we consider the equation of motion (EOM) for the radial mode,

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+V^{\prime}=\dot{\theta}^{2}\phi,

(16)

we see that the radial potential competes with the centrifugal term
\dot{\theta}^{2}\phi
.
This is analogues to the usual Kepler problem: The centrifugal term is exactly balanced against the potential when
\dot{\theta}=\sqrt{\phi^{-1}V^{\prime}}
, which would then correspond to a perfectly circular orbit. Generically, when a rotation is started with any other value than
\dot{\theta}=\sqrt{\phi^{-1}V^{\prime}}
, the radial mode will oscillate about the equilibrium value
\phi\sim V^{\prime}/\dot{\theta}^{2}
which corresponds to an elliptic orbit. Because of Hubble friction, radial oscillations about
\phi\sim V^{\prime}/\dot{\theta}^{2}
decrease in amplitude and the equilibrium point decreases towards
f_{a}
as
\dot{\theta}
is depleted.
The link to the potential parameters will be given in the next sections.
We observe that
\epsilon=\dot{\theta}_{\rm ap}/m_{\phi}
is a useful parametrization, where
\dot{\theta}_{\rm ap}
is the angular velocity at the apoapsis of an orbit. This is equivalent to normalising the PQ charge to
n_{\phi}=\rho_{\phi,\rm pot}/m_{\phi}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\epsilon=\frac{n_{\rm PQ}/2}{n_{\phi}}=\frac{n_{\rm PQ}/2}{\rho_{\phi,\rm pot}/m_{\phi}}=\frac{\dot{\theta}_{\rm ap}}{m_{\phi}},

(17)

where
\rho_{\phi,\rm pot}=m_{\phi}^{2}\phi^{2}/2
is the potential energy density and we assumed that the radial potential is a pure quadratic potential. Since the centrifugal term in the
\phi
-EOM is balanced with the potential exactly for
\epsilon=1
, this value corresponds to a perfectly circular orbit.
\epsilon=0
corresponds to purely radial oscillations. Since
\epsilon
is defined at the apoapsis of the orbit, and
\epsilon>1
would imply that the measured point is not the apoapsis, we by construction have
0\leq\epsilon\leq 1
. Co et al.
[
1
,
6
,
7
]
note that an insufficiently circular orbit can lead to a parametric resonance in the radial mode. Throughout this work, we assume that
\epsilon
is large enough that no such resonance takes place.

3.2
Evolution after the kick and the need for radial motion damping

After the kick, the radial mode is not in its ground state. It has a large VEV,
\phi_{\rm kick}\gg f_{a}
, and unless the kick is perfectly circular,
\phi
will be oscillating, meaning that
P
will be in an elliptic orbit.
The energy density of radial oscillations behaves as cold dark matter. It will therefore dominate over the angular mode energy density. To realize axion dark matter from kinetic misalignment, these oscillations must be damped. We could in principle also consider the case where the radial mode plays the role of dark matter instead, but this is another project which we leave for future investigation. In this paper we focus on the case where the axion is dark matter. Defining
\rho_{r}
as the radiation energy density from the Standard Model (SM) thermal bath, if damping takes place when
\rho_{\phi}>\rho_{r}
, then it significantly impacts the SM plasma. We denote the temperature at which
\rho_{\phi}=\rho_{r}
as
T_{\rm dom}
and we denote the damping temperature as

Evolution of the radial and angular modes:

After the kick and the onset of the elliptic orbit of the field, the equilibrium value
\expectationvalue{\phi}
, about which
\phi
oscillates, continuously decreases. This relaxation can be characterized by PQ charge conservation. Once radial oscillations are damped, the generally elliptic orbit with
0\leq\epsilon\leq 1
is relaxed to a circular orbit with
\epsilon=1
, such that
\dot{\theta}\approx m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}
after damping. Furthermore, it was shown in
[
22
]
that even before damping we have
\expectationvalue{\dtheta}\approx m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}
.
Furthermore, PQ charge conservation implies
\expectationvalue{\nPQ}\propto a^{-3}
, such that
m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}\phi^{2}\propto a^{-3}
, which ensures that

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\phi}\propto m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}^{-1/2}a^{-3/2},

(18)

which is the same evolution as that of a homogeneously oscillating scalar field with constant mass, having
\rho\propto a^{-3}
. If the radial mass is time-dependent as a consequence of either the radial-mode dynamics or the thermal effects, this impacts the relaxation of the radial mode. Let us denote
a_{\rm kin}
the value of the scale factor of the universe when the energy density of the axion is dominated by its kinetic energy. This corresponds to the time when
\phi
reaches the minimum at
f_{a}
.
Expressing the possible time-dependence of the radial mass as
m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}\propto a^{-c_{m_{\phi}}}
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{\rm kick}}{f_{a}}\approx\left(\frac{a_{\rm kin}}{a_{\rm kick}}\right)^{(3-c_{m_{\phi}})/2}.

(19)

The analyses of axion fragmentation and kinetic misalignment
[
15
,
1
,
6
,
7
]
are only applicable for
T_{\rm kin}>T_{*}
. If this condition is violated, then a fragmentation or kinetic misalignment analysis must be carried out in the presence of radial dynamics. Such an analysis is beyond the scope of this work, and we display the parameter space where this assumption is violated even though there is in principle no reason why fragmentation could not take place before the relaxation of the radial mode.

After
\phi
reaches
f_{a}
, the angular mode does not follow the equilibrium value
\dot{\theta}\approx m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}
. Applying again PQ charge conservation,
n_{\rm PQ}=\dot{\theta}\phi^{2}\propto a^{-3}
we observe that once
\expectationvalue{\phi}=f_{a}
the angular mode redshifts as
\dot{\theta}\propto a^{-3}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\dtheta}\propto a^{-c_{\theta}}\quad\mbox{where}\quad c_{\theta}=\begin{cases}c_{m_{\phi}}&\text{for }T>T_{\rm kin},\\
3&\text{for }T<T_{\rm kin}.\\
\end{cases}

(20)

As suggested by the subscript,
T_{\rm kin}
marks the onset of kination-like scaling of the energy density of
\rho_{a}\propto\dot{\theta}^{2}\propto a^{-6}
such that a period of kination

4
Phenomenological constraints

Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN):

T_{*}
is bounded by BBN. This limit arises because the energy density of the rotating axion scales as
\rho_{a}\propto a^{-6}
until trapping, so that the energy density in the angular mode will compete with the radiation density at BBN if fragmentation takes place too late. Usually, additional energy density in light fields at BBN is parametrized and constrained through the change in the effective number of neutrinos,
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
. However, since the energy density in the rotating axion does not scale like radiation,
N_{\rm eff}
is not an appropriate parametrization. Instead, we apply the numerical code
AlterBBN 2.2

[
146
]
, which constrains a dark component scaling as
a^{-6}
to less than
\rho_{a}/\rho_{r}<0.19
at
T=1
MeV. To be compatible with BBN,
T_{*}
then has to satisfy

\displaystyle T_{*}\gtrsim 20\text{ keV}.

(21)

Discussion of this constraint can be found in
[
15
]
.

Furthermore, BBN also constrains damping of radial motion. We do not perform a detailed analysis of how the entropy injection associated with damping interferes with the observed
4
He production, and we instead simply require damping to take place before BBN. Therefore, in addition to Eq. (
21
), we also demand that

\displaystyle T_{\rm damp}\gtrsim\text{ MeV}.

(22)

In regimes in which the energy in the radial oscillations is small compared to the energy in radiation, this constraint may be too strong, as it is conceivable that dilution would be small enough not to disturb BBN. Nevertheless, to be conservative we impose this constraint regardless of how significant the entropy injection is.

Structure formation:

To satisfy the bounds on hot relics and from structure formation, we impose that at radiation-matter equality the axions produced from fragmentation must have a velocity
v_{a}(T_{\rm eq})\lesssim 10^{-3}
.
The derivation of the precise constraint from structure formation requires knowledge of the spectrum of fluctuations produced by fragmentation. These spectra were calculated in
[
15
,
16
]
, which showed that the structure formation constraint is subdominant to the BBN constraint discussed above.
Ref.
[
23
]
considered a similar structure formation constraint, somewhat stronger than the one implied by our condition. Since either condition is at most comparable with the BBN constraint we do not devote further attention to the matter.

Perturbativity:

For the physics to remain perturbative, we impose that all dimensionless couplings in the Lagrangian be less than
4\pi
.
This applies in particular to the self-interactions.
This condition typically excludes large radial mode masses, and therefore large axion masses, see for instance the relation (
60
) between the two.

Homogeneity:

Primordial density fluctuations source fluctuations in the axion field (as mentioned earlier, we work with
pre-inflationary axions
). At early times, these fluctuations are massless and their energy density redshifts as
a^{-4}
. However, if the axion homogeneous mode goes through a period of
a^{-6}
kination-like redshifting, the fluctuations grow in comparison and may come to dominate over the homogeneous mode. As we here seek to provide model implementations that lead to a rotating, homogenous axion field we treat this condition as a constraint. Nevertheless, such an inhomogeneous state may lead to dark matter with interesting phenomenology, which we leave for future work
[
147
]
.

The homogeneity condition was studied in detail in our model-independent paper
[
15
]
.
For an order-of-magnitude estimate, assume that the energy density of the fluctuations before kination-like scaling can be described by
\rho_{\rm fluc}\sim\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}\rho_{a}
, where
\rho_{a}
and
\rho_{\rm fluc}
are the energy densities of the homogeneous axion mode and the fluctuations respectively and
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
is the amplitude of the primordial power spectrum. After the onset of kination-like scaling at
a_{\rm kin}
the energy densities redshift as
\rho_{\rm fluc}\propto a^{-4}
and
\rho_{a}\propto a^{-6}
. Therefore, if the evolution is not interrupted, the fluctuations will eventually dominate at a time
a_{\rm NL}
, where
NL
signifies the onset of non-linear dynamics. These assumptions imply that

\displaystyle\frac{a_{\rm kin}}{a_{\rm NL}}\sim\sqrt{\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})}.

(23)

This rough estimate reproduces the result in
[
15
]
up to an
\mathcal{O}(1)
factor. Here
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
is evaluated at the comoving momentum scale
k_{\rm kin}
, which is set by
k_{\rm kin}=a_{\rm kin}H(T_{\rm kin})
.

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
is on the other hand constrained by Planck
[
148
]
to
A_{s}\approx 2.1\times 10^{-9}
at a pivot scale of 0.05 Mpc
-1
. It is usually assumed to be a flat spectrum although it is only tightly constrained around this pivot scale. We expect
k_{\rm kin}
to be many orders of magnitude larger than the pivot scale, such that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
might differ significantly from
A_{s}
. If it does not differ significantly, then the growth of fluctuations leads to a loss of homogeneity. For instance, if the amplitude of curvature fluctuations of the modes which enter the horizon at
a_{\rm kin}
is
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})=10^{-10}
then to avoid domination by axion fluctuations the period of
a^{-6}
evolution has to be shorter than

\displaystyle\frac{a_{*}}{a_{\rm kin}}\lesssim 10^{5}.

(24)

Conversely, if we leave
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
as a free parameter, then the maximally permissible amplitude of fluctuations at the onset of
a^{-6}
fluctuations is

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})\lesssim\left(\frac{a_{\rm kin}}{a_{*}}\right)^{2}.

(25)

If
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
has a flat spectrum, the above constraint translates into
(a_{\rm kin}/a_{*})^{2}>A_{s}=2.1\times 10^{-9}
. We can avoid this constraint by assuming a non-trivial spectrum for
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
.
It will be interesting to identify inflationary models that naturally avoid this constraint.
A suppressed spectrum of primordial fluctuations also changes the efficiency of fragmentation which would shift the fragmentation lines reported in Fig.
1
. However, this dependence is logarithmic
[
15
]
and we therefore do not take it into account here.
We sketch the needed ingredients on the inflationary model to lead to the desired suppression of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
while satisfying Planck constraints in Section
12
. The details will be presented in a separate paper.

Thermal
\phi
relics:

An interaction that is efficient enough to damp the radial oscillations will also bring the radial mode into thermal equilibrium. This is potentially dangerous for BBN and CMB. The thermal relic will decouple from the plasma if the interaction freezes out at a temperature
T_{\rm fo}>m_{\phi}
.
After decoupling from the plasma, the thermal relic will have an energy density of

\displaystyle\rho_{\phi,\rm thermal}(T_{\rm fo})\sim\frac{\rho_{r}}{g_{*}}\Big|_{T_{\rm fo}}.

(26)

It will be further diluted by reheating in the SM sector, i.e. by changes in
g_{*}
and
g_{*s}
. Therefore, as long as the relic remains hot, it will not be problematic unless it freezes out below
\sim 200
MeV. However, if the relic becomes cold, it will grow relative to the radiation density and can become problematic.

At sufficiently large
m_{\phi}
, such constraints are absent because the thermal relic decays into axions as soon as it is no longer relativistic, see Appendix
B
.
At sufficiently low
m_{\phi}
the constraints are absent because the relic remains relativistic until after
T_{\rm eq}
such that it both underproduces dark matter and does not disturb the CMB. The intermediate range is excluded by
N_{\rm eff}
constraints from either BBN or CMB. Therefore,
\phi
must satisfy

\displaystyle m_{\phi}<\mathcal{O}(1)\times T_{\rm eq}\quad\mbox{or}\quad\mathcal{O}(1)\times\frac{f_{a}^{2}}{m_{\rm Pl}}<m_{\phi},

(27)

where the values of the prefactors depend on the number of relativistic degrees of freedom. These constraints are discussed in more detail in Appendix
B
.

Fifth-force searches:

The radial mode couples to photons through
F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}
rather than
F_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}
. We express the scalar radial mode-photon interaction and pseudo-scalar axion-photon interactions as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\phi\gamma}=\frac{1}{4}g_{\phi\gamma\gamma}\phi F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}\ ,\ \ \ \mathcal{L}_{\theta\gamma}=\frac{1}{4}g_{\theta\gamma\gamma}aF_{\mu\nu}\tilde{F}^{\mu\nu}.

(28)

where
a=\theta f_{a}
is the axion field.
We generally expect
g_{\phi\gamma\gamma}
to be of the order of the axion-photon coupling
g_{\theta\gamma\gamma}
. Scalar couplings
are subject to much stronger constraints than those from pseudoscalar interactions. Although such constraints are not relevant for theories in which
m_{\phi}\sim f_{a}
, they are significant for theories with light radial modes such as those considered in this work. For a recent review of constraints on light scalar fields, see e.g.
[
149
]
.

Long-range forces
[
150
]
arise from scalar-mediated interactions through couplings like (
28
).
This leads to apparent violations of the equivalence-principle (EP) and deviations from the inverse-square law, which can be tested in fifth-force searches such as Eöt-Wash
[
151
]
and lunar ranging
[
152
]
.
Searches for long-range forces are sensitive to electromagnetically-coupled scalar fields even if such fields are not DM. If the scalar were to be dark matter, which is not the case studied here, it would also be constrained from atomic clocks through the oscillating electromagnetic perturbations
[
153
]
.

The most relevant constraints are summarized by Hess et al.
[
153
]
from torsion balance data
[
154
,
151
,
155
]
as well as data from the MICROSCOPE space mission
[
156
]
. Assuming a radial mode-photon coupling that is comparable in strength to the axion-photon coupling, such data constrain values of
f_{a}
beyond the range considered here. Therefore, constraints from EP violation in our context appear as a lower bound on
m_{\phi}
:

\displaystyle m_{\phi}\gtrsim 10^{-5}\text{ eV}.

(29)

The constraint is driven by the Eöt-Wash experiments on Cu and Pb test bodies
[
154
]
which constrains
g_{\phi\gamma\gamma}<10^{-20}
GeV
-1
, so if we do not consider
f_{a}>10^{13}
GeV the
\phi
-photon coupling can deviate from a KSVZ-like coupling by up to four orders of magnitude before exact coupling dependence has to be taken into account in Eq. (
29

It is possible to use data from ALP searches to constrain radial mode-photon interactions. This was studied by Flambaum et al.
[
157

, who derived scalar-photon constraints from CAST and ADMX. However, scalar-photon constraints from EP violation dominate those from ALP searches by many orders of magnitude
[
157
]
.

Axion and radial mode mass hierarchy:

In addition to being spontaneously broken, the PQ symmetry has a small explicit breaking leading to the small axion mass
m_{a}\ll f_{a}
.
When
m_{\phi}\sim f_{a}
one thus naturally expects
m_{a}\ll m_{\phi}
.
In the case where the radial mode is also light, one may wonder if it is possible to have the opposite hierarchy, i.e.,
m_{a}\gg m_{\phi}
.
We can address this question with an effective potential for the axion and the radial mode. By integrating out all of the other particles, this potential is of the form

\displaystyle V_{\rm eff}(P)=V_{\rm PQinv}(\phi)+V_{\rm PQviol}(\phi,\theta).

(30)

V_{\rm PQinv}
is invariant under the PQ symmetry, but
V_{\rm PQviol}
is not.
To realize
m_{\phi}\ll m_{a}
, we need to satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQinv}}{\partial\phi^{2}}+\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQviol}}{\partial\phi^{2}}\ll\frac{1}{\phi^{2}}\frac{\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQviol}}{\partial\theta^{2}}

(31)

at the potential minimum.
This requires some fine-tuning.
First, it is natural to have

\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQinv}/\partial\phi^{2}\gg\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQviol}/\partial\phi^{2},~(1/\phi^{2})\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQviol}/\partial\theta^{2}

since the PQ-violating terms are subdominant.
Also, it is natural to have

\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQviol}/\partial\phi^{2}\sim(1/\phi^{2})\partial^{2}V_{\rm PQviol}/\partial\theta^{2}
.
In this paper, we do not pursue the possibility of having
m_{\phi}\ll m_{a}

and focus on the region with
m_{a}<m_{\phi}
.

Impact of CP-violating term on the axion quality:

In this work, we are studying both QCD axions and general ALPs. The QCD axion is particularly sensitive to the higher-dimensional PQ-violating operator Eq. (
13
). In particular, this term should not introduce
\theta_{\rm QCD}\gtrsim 10^{-10}

[
158
,
159
]
, where
\theta_{\rm QCD}=0
is the CP-conserving minimum of the QCD potential. To estimate this shift, we can take
\delta_{\theta}=\pi/2
to maximally misalign the higher-dimensional potential from the QCD potential. In this case, in the late-time potential, where
\phi=f_{a}
, is

\displaystyle V_{\rm today}\approx-2^{1-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{Af_{a}^{n}}{M^{n-3}}\theta+\frac{1}{2}m_{a}^{2}f_{a}^{2}\theta^{2}+\mathcal{O}\left(\theta^{3}\right).

(32)

The CP-violating VEV induced by this potential is then

\displaystyle\theta_{\rm CPV}\approx 2^{1-\frac{n}{2}}\frac{AM^{3}}{m_{a}^{2}f_{a}^{2}}\left(\frac{f_{a}}{M}\right)^{n}.

(33)

For a QCD axion to successfully solve the strong CP problem, we require
\theta_{\rm CPV}\ll 10^{-10}
. We generally display this condition by shading the otherwise yellow QCD line of
[m_{a},f_{a}]
parameter space in blue in the range where it cannot be satisfied.

\phi
-to-axion decay:

The radial and angular modes are coupled. At the very least, the Lagrangian contains the kinetic term

\displaystyle|\partial_{\mu}P|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}\varphi)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{\mu}a)^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{\varphi^{2}}{\langle\phi\rangle^{2}}(\partial_{\mu}a)^{2}+\frac{\varphi}{\langle\phi\rangle}(\partial_{\mu}a)^{2},

(34)

where
P=\frac{\langle\phi\rangle+\varphi(x)}{2}e^{ia(x)/\langle\phi\rangle}
and
a=\theta f_{a}
is the axion field. The latter of these terms induces an
\varphi\to aa
decay with the rate

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varphi a}\sim\frac{1}{64\pi}\frac{m_{\phi}^{3}}{\langle\phi\rangle^{2}}.

(35)

Although the radial mode also experiences EM couplings, the corresponding rate is always less efficient because EM decay is suppressed by a factor
(f_{a}/f_{a\gamma})^{2}\approx 5\times 10^{-6}
relative to Eq. (
35
), where
f_{a\gamma}=g_{\theta\gamma\gamma}^{-1}
is the dimension-full axion-photon coupling which we assume to be KSVZ-like.

If the radial mode decayed into axions, it would produce a hot axion relic which could dominate the plasma. To avoid this, we impose the following constraint:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varphi a}<H\quad\mbox{while}\quad\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}>\rho_{r}\qquad\text{(hot axions dominate the plasma)},

(36)

where
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
is the energy in radial oscillations. Furthermore, even if the hot axion relic does not dominate the plasma, it may still lead to DM overproduction once it has cooled. Solving for the cold DM relic generated by radial-to-axion decay, we impose the following constraint

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\varphi a}<H\quad\mbox{while}\quad\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}\frac{2m_{a}}{m_{\phi}}\frac{g_{*}(T_{\rm eq})}{g_{*}(T)}\left(\frac{T_{\rm eq}}{T}\right)^{3}>\rho_{r,\rm eq}\qquad\text{(DM overproduction)},

(37)

where the subscript
eq
refers to quantities measured at radiation-matter equality.
These constraints are avoided if the radial oscillations are damped before
\phi
-to-axion decay becomes efficient. We therefore impose them only in the model implementation where the damping mechanism is specified.

We are now ready to move to the model implementations of KMM. The early cosmology of the models under consideration have been scrutinised also in
[
22
]
with a different purpose, to determine the parameter region leading to kination eras in the early universe.
Moreover, while the KMM has been pioneered in
[
1
]
, our motivation is to provide a synthesis and a status of the mechanism when putting together all requirements, by providing a systematic scan of the parameter space, in particular mapping out observable quantities of interest in the
(m_{a},f_{a})
plane, such as the value of the radial-mode mass, the damping temperature, and the needed suppression of the primordial spectrum of fluctuations.

5
Shape of the Peccei-Quinn potential

The shape of the radial-mode potential is crucial in determining whether or not KMM can take place.
A generic mexican-hat potential with quartic coupling has the form

\displaystyle V\supset\lambda\left(\absolutevalue{P}^{2}-\frac{f_{a}^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}.

(38)

As justified below, such generic potential leads to
KMM only at the cost of fine-tuning
\lambda
to small values.
We will therefore instead mainly focus on nearly-quadratic potentials a la Affleck-Dine which can be motivated in SUSY constructions where the PQ symmetry is spontaneously broken by radiative corrections. These were originally proposed in Ref.
[
160
]
, where soft SUSY-breaking generates a nearly-quadratic potential of the form

\displaystyle V_{\rm late}=m_{\phi}^{2}|P|^{2}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln(\frac{2|P|^2}{f_a^2})-\frac{1}{2}\right)+\frac{1}{4}m_{\phi}^{2}f_{a}^{2}.

(39)

Note that
(dV_{\rm late}/d\phi)|_{\phi=f_{a}}=0
and
(d^{2}V_{\rm late}/d\phi^{2})|_{\phi=f_{a}}=m_{\phi}^{2}
are satisfied and that we throughout this work assume
N_{\rm DW}=1
for simplicity, where
N_{\rm DW}
is the domain wall number.
The radial mass
m_{\phi}
may then be naturally light as it is protected by SUSY. In this framework,
PQ is spontaneously broken by the logarithmic running of the potential, which gives the radial mode a VEV at
\phi=f_{a}
once the radial mode is fully relaxed. Such models were first studied in the context of kinetic misalignment in
[
1
]
.
Note that the mass of the radial mode, which around the minimum is
m_{\phi}
, receives a logarithmic correction at high field values. However, this logarithmic correction is at most
\mathcal{O}(1)
. We therefore neglect the correction at large field amplitudes where we then take
V\approx m_{\phi}^{2}|P|^{2}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\phi}^{2}\phi^{2}
. Thus, the fundamental parameter
m_{\phi}
here agrees with
m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}\equiv\sqrt{\phi^{-1}V^{\prime}}
as defined in Eq. (
15
), such that there is no distinction between the effective mass at large field values and the fundamental parameter.

In the following sections
6

8

10
, we will investigate how radial dynamics in such a model can provide the initial conditions for fragmentation and kinetic misalignment. And in Section
11
, we will present analogous results for the quartic potential. In the next sub-section, we summarise the issues associated with the generic quartic potential

5.1
Hubble-induced mass term versus de Sitter fluctuations

As discussed in Section
3.1
, a crucial ingredient of the KMM mechanism is an initial field value for the radial mode that is much larger than the value at the minimum
f_{a}
of the PQ potential.
There are two main ways to realise this. The first way, which we discuss in more detail later, is a large negative Hubble-induced mass term for the radial mode,
-c_{H}H^{2}|P|^{2}
, which generates a minimum at large field values for a large initial Hubble expansion rate value
H
. The second way is a very light radial mode, which via de Sitter fluctuations during inflation drives the field to the value
[
6
]

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm kick}\sim\sqrt[4]{\frac{3}{8\pi^{2}}}\frac{H_{I}}{\sqrt[4]{\lambda}}.

(40)

This second possibility, applied to the quartic potential
38
, has been considered for KMM in Ref.
[
1
,
6
,
7
]
.
However, we find that this scenario faces challenges.
Note that our notation in Eq. (
38
) differs from the notation used in Co et al
in that
\lambda=\lambda^{2}_{\rm{Co\ et\ al}}
.
An important point is that the effective radial depends on the field value

\displaystyle m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}^{2}=\frac{1}{\phi}V_{\rm late}^{\prime}\approx\lambda\phi^{2}.

(41)

This is a key difference for the phenomenology of kinetic misalignement with respect to the models with a nearly-quadratic potential
for which the effective radial mass
m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}
is constant in
\phi
up to logarithmic effects.
The main issue with this scenario is the formation of domain walls at the time of trapping, which can be circumvented if
[
6
]

\displaystyle\frac{H_{I}}{f_{a}}\lesssim\mathcal{O}(0.1).

(42)

Since we are considering pre-inflationary scenario, we do not expect cosmic strings to be present, and the domain walls would therefore be stable and pathological.
Co et al.
[
6
]
propose to resolve this domain wall problem by invoking parametric resonance to non-thermally restore PQ-symmetry, generate cosmic strings and thereby render the string-wall network unstable. However, there is no such analysis to date. We have looked in detail at this case and we do not find any viable KMM implementations relying on de Sitter fluctuations with a quartic potential. In the rest of this section, we explain the difficulties.
To understand this problem, consider the yield produced in this class of models,

\displaystyle Y_{\rm kick}\approx\frac{n_{PQ}}{s}\Big|_{\rm kick}\approx\epsilon\frac{\sqrt{\lambda}\phi_{\rm kick}^{3}}{\frac{2\pi^{2}}{45}g_{*s}T_{\rm kick}^{3}},

(43)

which in terms of model parameters corresponds to

\displaystyle Y_{\rm kick}\approx 0.2\times\frac{\epsilon}{\lambda^{5/8}}\left(\frac{H_{I}}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{3/2},

(44)

where the prefactor has a mild dependence on
g_{*}(T_{\rm kick})
.
To realize solutions corresponding to sub-eV axions masses it is therefore necessary to consider either large values of the Hubble scale during inflation
H_{I}
or low values of
\lambda
. Large values of
H_{I}
are in tension with Eq. (
42
) and imply domain wall (DW) constraints. Therefore, setting aside concerns of naturalness for the moment, we are led to consider low values of
\lambda
. Specifically, we can consider values of
\lambda
which go below the range constrained by thermal
\phi
relics, Eq. (
123
), which corresponds to

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda}<\mathcal{O}(1)\frac{T_{\rm eq}}{f_{a}}.

(45)

However, this range of
\lambda
implies a low radial mode mass. This low radial mode mass cannot compete with the thermal mass
m_{\phi,\rm th}
generated by the interactions introduced for the thermal damping that will be discussed in sections

\epsilon

\displaystyle\xi\lesssim 2\sqrt{\frac{H_{I}}{m_{\rm Pl}}}\lambda^{1/8}.

(46)

A similar condition applies for Yukawa interactions. Such low values of the coupling does not permit damping. For instance, even with the most efficient Higgs portal interaction, Eq. (
100
),

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi}\approx 2\frac{\xi^{4}\phi^{2}}{\pi^{2}\alpha_{2}T},

(47)

taken at the most competitive conditions,
\phi=f_{a}
and

, efficient damping as defined by

requires

\displaystyle H_{I}\gtrsim 3\times 10^{12}\text{ GeV}\times\left(\frac{10^{8}\text{ GeV}}{f_{a}}\right),

(48)

which is in contradiction with the simple domain wall constraint Eq. (

\phi
relics, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathcal{O}(1)\times\frac{f_{a}}{m_{\rm Pl}}<\sqrt{\lambda},

(49)

then the large values

can only correspond to scenarios of eV-scale axions with values of

that are also constrained by the domain wall problem.

We solved numerically the Boltzmann equations and we found no solutions without DW problems for neither Yukawa-type interactions nor with the Higgs portal interaction. If a solution to the DW problem is assumed, then some parameter space becomes viable.
We therefore discard implementations where the large initial field value is due to de Sitter fluctuations.

Initial conditions in a nearly-quadratic potential

We rely on the potential used in

and later exploited for KMM in

\displaystyle V_{\rm early}=(m_{\phi}^{2}-c_{H}H^{2})|P|^{2}+\frac{A+c_{A}H}{n}\frac{P^{n}}{M^{n-3}}+h.c.+\frac{|P|^{2n-2}}{M^{2n-6}},

(50)

where we neglected the logarithmic correction to the late-time mass
m_{\phi}
,
H
is the Hubble parameter,
M
is a suppression scale which we typically take to be
m_{\rm Pl}\approx 2.4\times 10^{18}
GeV,
A
is a dimension-full constant which we typically take to be
\mathcal{O}(1)\times m_{\phi}
and
c_{A}
is an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant. The negative Hubble-induced mass can be naturally generated in models where
P
couples to fields which dominate the energy density of the universe
[
19
,
161
,
162
,
163
,
164
]
.
If at early times
m_{\phi}\ll H
, then this negative mass contribution drives up the VEV. To stabilize against this negative potential contribution, one can introduce a higher-dimensional term in the superpotential
[
19
]
of the form

\displaystyle W=\frac{1}{n}\frac{P^{n}}{M^{n-3}}.

(51)

This superpotential sources a scalar potential corresponding to the last term in Eq. (
50
). The A-term, i.e. the middle term of Eq. (
50
), is generated from
W
by soft SUSY breaking. In addition to the discussion in
[
19
]
, a summary of the generation of these terms can be found in
[
22
]
.

At early times when
H\gg m_{\phi}
, i.e. before the kick, the
H^{2}
term then drives
\phi
to a large VEV stabilized by the
|P|^{2n-2}
term. The VEV at the minimum is

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm early}=\left(\frac{2^{n-2}}{n-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n-4}}\left(HM^{n-3}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-2}}.

(52)

Around this VEV, both the radial and angular modes acquire large masses,

\displaystyle m_{\phi,\rm early}=\sqrt{2(n-1)}H\quad\mbox{and}\quad m_{a,\rm early}=\frac{\sqrt{2|c_{A}|n}}{\sqrt[4]{n-1}}H,

(53)

such that both fields are heavy during inflation and can track the minimum until
m_{\phi}\propto 3H
, where the late-time mass becomes important. This large early mass naturally suppresses inflationary fluctuations and precludes the commonly associated domain wall and isocurvature problems. We assume that the higher-dimensional operators become irrelevant once
m_{\phi}>3H
so that we can take the potential to be
V_{\rm late}
, given by Eq. (
39
), after this transition. This implies that the PQ field starts evolving in
V_{\rm late}
with an initial radial amplitude of
\phi_{\rm kick}=\phi_{\rm early}(H_{\rm kick})
, where
H_{\rm kick}
is the Hubble parameter measured at the time of the transition.

During the transition, the PQ violating terms
P^{n}+P^{*n}
can drive rotation. This is possible because the angular mode transitions from a minimum set by the
c_{A}H
term into a minimum set by the
A
term. The angular minima of these two contributions are determined by the complex phase of
c_{A}
and
A
, respectively. If the minima of these two contributions are not accidentally aligned, the field experiences a torque as the minimum shifts
[
20
]
and Eq. (
14
) applies with
\theta_{\rm kick}\neq 0
. With
A=\mathcal{O}(1)\times m_{\phi}
this naturally yields
\epsilon=\mathcal{O}(1)
such that

\displaystyle\dot{\theta}_{\rm kick}=\mathcal{O}(1)\times m_{\phi},

(54)

where
m_{\phi}
is the potential parameter appearing in Eq. (
50
) and not the much larger early-time mass given by (
53
).
This kick is delivered when
m_{\phi}\approx 3H
, which implies a kick temperature of

\displaystyle T_{\rm kick}\approx\sqrt[4]{\frac{10}{g_{*}(T_{\rm kick})\pi^{2}}}\sqrt{m_{\phi}m_{\rm Pl}}.

(55)

We assume that soon after the kick the PQ-violating potential becomes irrelevant so that we can disregard the higher-dimensional terms after the first rotation. This is confirmed by the numerical evaluation of the equations of motion
[
19
]
.

In the nearly-quadratic model, the radial mass
m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}
is approximately constant in time. Therefore, applying Eq. (
19
) we see that
\phi
reaches the minimum at
f_{a}
at
a_{\rm kin}
given by

\displaystyle\frac{\phi_{\rm kick}}{f_{a}}\approx\left(\frac{a_{\rm kin}}{a_{\rm kick}}\right)^{3/2}.

(56)

If no significant entropy injection takes place, then this corresponds to a temperature of

\displaystyle T_{\rm kin}\approx T_{\rm kick}\left(\frac{g_{*s}(T_{\rm kick})}{g_{*s}(T_{\rm kin})}\right)^{1/3}\left(\frac{f_{a}}{\phi_{\rm kick}}\right)^{2/3}.

(57)

After this time,
\dot{\theta}
transitions to a kination-like scaling as described in Section
3.2
.

Estimate of the yield:

After the kick is complete and the PQ-violating terms have become subdominant, the comoving PQ-charge is conserved,
n_{\rm PQ}\propto a^{-3}
. The resulting yield is constant in the absence of entropy injection:

\displaystyle Y_{\rm kick}=\frac{n_{PQ}}{s}=\epsilon\frac{m_{\phi}\phi_{\rm kick}^{2}}{\frac{2\pi^{2}}{45}g_{*s}T_{\rm kick}^{3}}\approx 0.8\times\epsilon\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{\frac{2n-6}{n-2}}\left(\frac{m_{\rm Pl}}{m_{\phi}}\right)^{\frac{n-6}{2n-4}},

(58)

where the prefactor has a mild dependence on
n
,
g_{*}(T_{\rm kick})
, and on
g_{*s}(T_{\rm kick})
.

Dark matter solution in the absence of yield dilution:

If no entropy injection takes place, the yield Eq. (
58
) eventually sources axion dark matter. The observed DM abundance,
Y_{\rm DM}\approx 2Y_{\rm kick}
is achieved for the value
m_{\phi}=m_{\phi,\rm DM,not\ diluted}
given by

\displaystyle m_{\phi,\rm DM,not\ diluted}\approx\

\displaystyle\xi_{n}\times g_{*}(T_{\rm kick})^{\frac{3(n-2)}{2(n-6)}}\left(\frac{g_{*s}(T_{\rm eq})}{g_{*s}(T_{\rm kick})g_{*}(T_{\rm eq})}\right)^{\frac{2(n-2)}{n-6}}\times m_{\rm Pl}\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{\frac{4(n-3)}{n-6}}\left(\frac{m_{a}\epsilon}{T_{\rm eq}}\right)^{\frac{2(n-2)}{n-6}}

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad\xi_{n}=(n-1)^{-\frac{2}{n-6}}\pi^{\frac{2-n}{n-6}}2^{\frac{9(n-2)}{2(n-6)}}3^{\frac{2(n-4)}{n-6}}5^{\frac{n-2}{2(n-6)}}.

(59)

For a few representative choices of
n
,

\displaystyle m_{\phi,\rm DM,not\ diluted}\approx\begin{cases}70\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{16}\left(\frac{m_{a}}{10^{-2}\text{ eV}}\right)^{10}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{0.9}\right)^{10}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad n=7,\\
2\times 10^{12}\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{7}\left(\frac{m_{a}}{10^{-2}\text{ eV}}\right)^{4}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{0.9}\right)^{4}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad n=10,\\
5\times 10^{13}\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{\frac{40}{7}}\left(\frac{m_{a}}{10^{-2}\text{ eV}}\right)^{\frac{22}{7}}\left(\frac{\epsilon}{0.9}\right)^{\frac{22}{7}}&\quad\mbox{if}\quad n=13.\end{cases}

(60)

The yield has a somewhat shallow dependence on the radial mass, i.e.

Y_{\rm kick}\propto m_{\phi}^{-l}

(61)

where

0.1, 0.25 or 0.3 for
n=

m_{\phi}
to compensate, especially for lower values of

9
Thermal damping via Yukawa interactions in nearly-quadratic potential

A well-motivated damping of radial oscillations is through a Yukawa interaction of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\rm int}=\sqrt{2}yP\bar{\chi}\left(\frac{1-\gamma^{5}}{2}\right)\chi+h.c.,

(76)

where
\chi
is a fermion that is efficiently coupled to the SM thermal bath
[
168
]
.
Such a Yukawa coupling is an integral part of KSVZ-type axion models. In this framework, the
\chi
-fermions are KSVZ fermions which are coupled to the SM via SU(3)
QCD
, and damping would be a natural consequence of the KSVZ construction.
This construction deviates from a conventional KSVZ model as we must assume
y\ll 1
for reasons discussed later in this section. Nevertheless, we take this as our benchmark case and assume the
\chi
fermions to be charged under SU(3)
QCD
.
Schematically, we have

\displaystyle\phi\hskip 8.53581pt\xleftrightarrow[\text{Yukawa}]{y}\hskip 8.53581pt\chi\hskip 8.53581pt\xleftrightarrow[\text{Gauge}]{g_{s}}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{SM plasma with gluons }g.

(77)

The
\chi
fermion mass receives a contribution from the
\phi
-interaction and at high temperature from the SM gauge interaction. We neglect any other sources such that the effective
\chi
mass is

\displaystyle m_{\chi,\rm eff}^{2}\approx m_{\chi,\phi}^{2}+m_{\chi,T}^{2}\approx y^{2}\phi^{2}+g_{s}^{2}T^{2}.

(78)

We define
\alpha_{s}\equiv g_{s}^{2}/4\pi
. We assume that the Yukawa coupling is weak such that

y<\alpha_{s}
.

9.1
Thermal effects and damping rates

Although the above structure appears rather simple, thermal effects lead to a very rich dynamics. For a detailed study of such thermal effects we refer to Mukaida et al.
[
168
,
169
,
170
]
. They are also crucial for an analysis of kination as discussed extensively and reviewed in
[
22
]
.
In particular, a Yukawa coupling of the type considered here generates thermal contributions to the radial potential. If
T>y\phi
,
\chi
particles in the thermal plasma give a thermal correction
\approx y^{2}T^{2}
to
m_{\phi}^{2}
. On the other hand, if
T<y\phi
, although
\chi
is absent from the thermal bath, it affects the effective gauge coupling via logarithmic running effect. This provides a thermal correction to the potential energy as
\delta V\approx\alpha_{s}^{2}T^{4}\log(y^2 \phi^2 / T^2)

[
171
,
168
]
. The second derivative of this term can be interpreted as a thermal mass correction to
\phi
. The full effective mass
m_{\phi,\rm eff}
is thus of the form
[
171
,
168
]

\displaystyle\begin{gathered}m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\approx\begin{cases}m_{\phi,0}^{2}+m_{\phi,\rm th}^{2}&\text{if}\quad T>y\phi,\\
m_{\phi,0}^{2}+m_{\phi,\rm ln}^{2}&\text{if}\quad T<y\phi,\end{cases}\\
\text{where}\quad m_{\phi,\rm th}^{2}\approx y^{2}T^{2}\quad\mbox{and}\quad m_{\phi,\rm ln}^{2}\approx\alpha_{s}^{2}\frac{T^{4}}{\phi^{2}}.\end{gathered}

Here
m_{\phi,0}
is the zero-temperature mass considered in the previous section,
m_{\phi,\rm th}
is the thermal mass and
m_{\phi,\rm ln}
is the thermal-log mass. The thermal-log mass replaces the ordinary thermal mass when the
\chi
-fermions are Boltzmann suppressed, i.e. for
T<y\phi
.
If either thermal potential is non-negligible, then it can significantly alter the dynamics of the kick. Furthermore, when the Yukawa interaction damps the radial oscillations, this energy is transferred to the SM plasma. This can further modify the parameter space if the yield dilution is significant.

The Yukawa coupling introduced in Eq. (
76
) gives rise to thermal damping through a variety of processes. The most relevant rates are the following:

- Perturbative decay:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi\bar{\chi}\chi}\approx\frac{y^{2}m_{\phi,0}}{8\pi}\quad\mbox{if}\quad m_{\phi,0}<gT\quad\mbox{and}\quad m_{\phi,0}<y\phi,

(81)

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\phi gg}\approx\alpha_{s}^{2}\frac{m_{\phi,0}^{3}}{\phi^{2}}\quad\mbox{if}\quad m_{\phi,0}<gT\quad\mbox{and}\quad m_{\phi,0}>y\phi

(82)

\displaystyle\quad\mbox{where}\quad b=\frac{T(r)}{16\pi^{2}}\frac{(12\pi)^{2}}{\ln\alpha_{s}^{-1}},

- Scattering processes with
\chi
-fermions:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\chi-\text{scat.}}\approx\begin{cases}y^{2}\alpha_{s}T&\text{if}\quad y\phi<\alpha_{s}T,\\
y^{4}\frac{\phi^{2}}{\alpha_{s}T}&\text{if}\quad\alpha_{s}T<y\phi<T,\end{cases}

(83)

- Loop-induced scattering processes with gluons
g
:

\displaystyle\Gamma_{\text{gluon scat.}}\approx\frac{b\alpha_{s}^{2}T^{3}}{\phi^{2}}.

(84)

These damping rates enter as a friction term in the equation of motion

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+\Gamma_{\rm tot}\dot{\phi}+m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi=\dot{\theta}^{2}\phi,

(85)

where the last term is the centrifugal term connecting the radial dynamics to the angular dynamics and
\Gamma_{\rm tot}
is the sum of all damping rates. A summary of all relevant damping rates as well as references can be found in Appendix
C
.

9.2
Boltzmann equations

The energy lost through damping is transferred to the radiation bath. Since several of the damping rates depend on
\phi
and/or
T
, the dynamics of this system can be non-trivial. The PQ fields may dominate the energy density and thus control
H
. Therefore, in Appendix
E
, we derive a set of Boltzmann equations to be solved numerically. The system of equations is

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=-(3H+\Gamma_{\rm tot}+c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi},

(86)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm circ}

\displaystyle=-(3H+c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm circ},

(87)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{r}

\displaystyle=-4H\rho_{r}+\Gamma_{\rm tot}\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi},

(88)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{a}

\displaystyle=-c_{\rho_{a}}H\rho_{a},

(89)

where
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
is the kinetic energy of the radial oscillations,
\rho_{\rm circ}
is the potential energy associated with the circular orbit about which the radial mode is oscillating and
\rho_{r}
is the energy density of the SM plasma. We model a possible temperature dependence of the radial mass with a parameter
c_{m_{\phi}}
defined as
m_{\phi}\propto a^{-c_{m_{\phi}}}
, such that
c_{m_{\phi}}=1
if thermal effects dominate the potential and
c_{m_{\phi}}=0
otherwise. Similarly, we parametrize the axion redshift with
\rho_{a}\propto a^{-c_{\rho_{a}}}
where
c_{\rho_{a}}=3
when
\phi\gg f_{a}
and
c_{\rho_{a}}=6
when
\phi\sim f_{a}
. The initial conditions for the system, as well as the
\chi
-fermion relativistic/non-relativistic transitions, are also discussed in Appendix
E
.

Consequences of thermal effects

Thermal potential and the absence of low-

solutions:

One interesting consequence of the thermal potential is that it precludes the low

(90)

This suppression disfavors

Intermediate relativistic phases:

In solutions with an intermediate relativistic phase, fermions are initially non-relativistic because of a large

In particular, if

Because

Constraints from
\chi

Since the
\chi

\displaystyle m_{\chi,\rm today}\approx yf_{a}\gtrsim\text{1 TeV}.

(91)

Thermal relics from
\chi

a)

To visualize the impact of the most important constraints, we show the parameter space that becomes viable if a given constraint is

In particular,

[m_{a},f_{a}]

By numerically solving the Boltzmann equations (

[f_{a},y,M,n,m_{\phi,0}]
and identify solutions that correspond to the correct DM relic for a given

.
We highlight the following:

Dominant damping rates:

We find that the process most commonly responsible for damping is scattering with relativistic
\chi
-fermions as given by Eq. (
83
). Damping tends to take place at temperatures near either the end or the beginning of the phase of relativistic
\chi
-fermions. Damping near the beginning of a relativistic phase is common because of the lifting of the
\chi
-fermion Boltzmann suppression rapidly increases the damping rate. Damping near the end of a relativistic phase is common because the
\Gamma_{\chi-\text{scat.}}\propto T^{-1}
behavior rapidly out-competes
H\propto T^{2}
in the short regime,
\alpha_{s}T<y\phi<T
, in which the damping rate is relevant. Damping near the end of relativistic phase can be problematic if the damping does not complete before Boltzmann suppression closes the process down.
See Appendix
E
for explicit examples of these scenarios.

Aside from
\chi
-scattering,
\phi
-to-axion decay can also play an important role in the damping of radial oscillations. As discussed in Section
4
, this mechanism is constrained by the danger of producing a hot axion relic in excess of constraints on dark radiation. This limits
\phi
-to-axion decay as a stand-alone damping mechanism.
Interestingly, we find that when
\phi
-to-axion decay is considered in conjunction with
\chi
-scattering then the decay can account for the remaining energy density which is left behind after
\chi
-scattering has damped most, but not all, of energy out of radial oscillations. The synergy in such two-step damping lies in the fact that after
\chi
-scattering has reduced the energy in radial oscillations to a fraction of
\rho_{r}

\phi
-to-axion decay can take place without the resulting hot axion being a significant part of the overall energy density.
An explicit example of such two-step damping can be found in Appendix
E
.

Thermal damping via Higgs portal in a nearly-quadratic potential

We now work out an alternative damping mechanism relying on a Higgs interaction of the form

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\supset\xi^{2}(\phi^{2}-f_{a}^{2})\left(H^{\dagger}H-\frac{v^{2}}{2}\right),

(96)

where
H
is the SM Higgs doublet with a VEV
\expectationvalue{H^\dagger H}=v_{\rm EW}^{2}/2
where
v_{\rm EW}\approx 246
GeV. Such an interaction was also considered for the quartic model by Co et al.
[
6
,
7
]
and the thermal effects were studied by Mukaida et al.
[
168
,
169
,
170
]
. We refer in particular to
[
170
]
.

The interaction term is tuned to cancel a contribution to the Higgs mass arising from the late-time VEV of
\expectationvalue{\phi}\sim f_{a}
, so as to avoid generating a Higgs mass larger than the observed Higgs mass
\xi f_{a}\gg m_{\rm H,0}
. This is the usual Higgs hierarchy problem which we do not discuss further here. Assuming the cancellation, the effective Higgs mass is

\displaystyle m_{\rm H,eff}^{2}\approx m_{\rm H,0}^{2}+\frac{1}{6}y_{t}^{2}T^{2}+\xi^{2}(\phi^{2}-f_{a}^{2}),

(97)

where
y_{t}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
is the Higgs-top Yukawa coupling, which we assume to be the dominant source of the Higgs thermal potential.

10.1
Thermal effects and damping rates

As was the case with the Yukawa interaction, the Higgs portal coupling Eq. (
96
) gives rise to a thermal potential for
\phi
. If
T^{2}>m_{\rm H,0}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\phi^{2}-f_{a}^{2})
, the SM Higgs boson in the thermal plasma gives thermal corrections
\approx\xi^{2}T^{2}
to
m_{\phi}^{2}
. On the other hand, if
T^{2}<m_{\rm H,0}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\phi^{2}-f_{a}^{2})
, although the SM Higgs boson is absent in the thermal bath, it affects the effective gauge coupling via a logarithmic running effect and the potential energy obtains a thermal correction
\delta V\approx\alpha_{2}^{2}T^{4}\log[(m_{\rm H,0}^{2}+\xi^{2}(\phi^{2}-f_{a}^{2}))/T^{2}]

[
171
,

For thermal damping though Eq. (

The main differences with the Yukawa case include limits arising from radial mode-Higgs mixing and the Higgs mass contribution.
As SM Higgs interactions are known to the percent-level

\displaystyle\vartheta_{\phi H}<0.1

If no such cancellation is assumed, then the observed Higgs mass imposes a strict upper bound of

\displaystyle\xi\ll\frac{m_{\rm H,0}}{f_{a}}.

(105)

While some parameter space remains viable even when asuming this bound, it implies fine-tuned very low coupling constants

KMM in a quartic potential with a Hubble-induced mass term

As an alternative to the implementations described above, we now investigate how higher-dimensional terms can drive a large VEV in a model with a quartic potential. The potential under consideration is

\displaystyle V=\frac{\lambda}{4}\left(\phi^{2}-f_{a}^{2}\right)^{2}-c_{H}H^{2}\phi^{2}+\frac{A+c_{A}H}{n}\frac{P^{n}}{M^{n-3}}+h.c.+\frac{|P|^{2n-2}}{M^{2n-6}},

(106)

which is analogous to Eq. (
50
). As with the nearly-quadratic potential, the Hubble-induced terms generate an early large mass at the VEV

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm early}=\left(\frac{2^{n-2}}{n-1}\right)^{\frac{1}{2n-4}}\left(HM^{n-3}\right)^{\frac{1}{n-2}}.

(107)

This time-dependent VEV is the same as in the nearly-quadratic model since the VEV is set purely by the higher dimensional terms. It makes both the angular and radial modes heavy during inflation which resolves the isocurvature and domain wall problems associated with models using instead de Sitter fluctuations to drive the large initial value of the field.
Although the early time-dependent minimum given by Eq. (
107
) is the same as in models with nearly-quadratic potentials, the VEV at the time of the kick differs. This is because the kick takes place at
m_{\phi}\sim\sqrt{\lambda}\phi\sim 3H
which implies

\displaystyle T_{\rm kick}\approx 0.3\times\sqrt{Mm_{\rm Pl}}\lambda^{\frac{n-2}{4n-12}},

(108)

\displaystyle\phi_{\rm kick}\approx 1.\times M\lambda^{\frac{1}{2n-6}}

(109)

where the exact value of the prefactors depends on
n
and
g_{*}(T_{\rm kick})
. Such a kick implies a yield of

\displaystyle Y_{\rm kick}\approx 0.8\times\epsilon\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{3/2}\lambda^{\frac{n-6}{4n-12}}.

(110)

The observed DM yield is achieved for

\displaystyle\sqrt{\lambda}\sim\mathcal{O}(1)\times\left(\frac{M}{m_{\rm Pl}}\right)^{\frac{3(n-3)}{n-6}}\left(\frac{\epsilon m_{a}}{T_{\rm eq}}\right)^{\frac{2(n-3)}{n-6}},

(111)

which determines the late-time radial mass
m_{\phi-\rm late}\approx\sqrt{\lambda}f_{a}
. With this solution, we can repeat the analysis discussed in Section
7
and describe the viable parameter space under the simplifying assumption that damping takes place early enough so that entropy injection can be ignored. This assumption leads to the parameter space described by Fig.
15
. Similar to the models with nearly-quadratic models, we observe two main families of solutions divided into lower and higher axion/
\phi
masses by the thermal
\phi
relic constraint. As with models with nearly-quadratic, we expect the lower-mass window to be closed once thermal potentials are considered.

CP violation and the
A

As in the previous models, the amplitude of the CP-violating potential responsible for driving the kick is controlled by the parameter
A

\displaystyle A\sim\mathcal{O}(1)\times\left(\frac{m_{a}}{T_{\rm eq}}\right)^{\frac{2(n-2)}{n-6}}m_{\rm Pl},

(112)

which means that

An important characteristic of the quartic potential is that since

\displaystyle\Gamma_{Sa}\sim\frac{1}{64\pi}\frac{m_{\phi,V^{\prime}}^{3}}{\phi^{2}}.

where the exact value of the

In most inflation models, the curvature power spectrum is approximately flat for the modes that exit the horizon during inflation. A suppression only occurs for the modes that exit the horizon during the last few efolds of inflation. Naively, we might think that by using an inflation model where
k_{\rm kin}
exits the horizon at the last few efolds, we can achieve the suppression that we want. However, this is problematic. The reason is that we have assumed that the kick happens after reheating. Therefore, the mode
k_{\rm kick}\equiv a_{\rm kick}H_{\rm kick}
which re-enters the horizon at the time of kick should leave the horizon before the end of inflation. By denoting
N_{\rm kick}
as the efold time at which
k_{\rm kick}
exits the horizon during inflation, we can write

N_{\rm kin}-N_{\rm kick}=\ln\quantity(\frac{a(N_{\rm kick})}{a(N_{\rm kin})})\sim\ln\quantity(\frac{k_{\rm kick}}{k_{\rm kin}})=\ln\quantity(\frac{a_{\rm kick}H_{\rm kick}}{a_{\rm kin}H_{\rm kin}}).

(117)

We now show that there is a lower bound on this value. For simplicity, let us assume that the reheating is instantaneous and the kick happens right after reheating. The universe between the kick and the kination-like scaling is either dominated by radiation all the time, or there is an early matter dominated era that starts at
a_{\rm dom}
and ends at
a_{\rm kin}
. Let
N_{\rm EMD}=\ln(a_{\rm kin}/a_{\rm dom})
denote the number of efolds of such an era if it exists. Then, by using the fact
H\propto a^{-2}
and
H\propto a^{-3/2}
during radiation and matter domination respectively, we can show that

N_{\rm kin}-N_{\rm kick}\sim\ln\quantity(\frac{a_{\rm kin}}{a_{\rm kick}})-\frac{1}{2}N_{\rm EMD}.

(118)

In the extreme case, when the matter domination lasts from the kick to the start of the kination, then
N_{\rm EMD}=\ln\quantity(a_{\rm kin}/a_{\rm kick})
, so we have
N_{\rm kin}-N_{\rm kick}\sim\ln\quantity(a_{\rm kin}/a_{\rm kick})/2
, i.e. the number of required efolds is reduced by half.

Summary and conclusion

We have re-examined Affleck-Dine-like implementations of the axion dark matter kinetic misalignment mechanism (KMM). By evaluating the models proposed by Co et al in
[
1
,
6
,
7
]
, we have determined precisely which
[m_{a},f_{a}]
parameter space can pass all the tests for a successful UV realization.
A successful implementation consists in a nearly-quadratic potential for the Peccei-Quinn scalar field
P
, Eq. (
39
) with an explicit PQ breaking term, Eq. (
13
).
We also analysed models with quartic potentials in Sec.
11
and found that these could be improved by driving a large radial VEV with a Hubble induced mass term Eq. (
107
) instead of de Sitter fluctuations Eq. (
40
).
The viable
[m_{a},f_{a}]
parameter space crucially depends on the axion partner, the radial mode of the PQ field.
To investigate the viable dark matter parameter space, we carried out both a damping-mechanism-independent analysis and analyses of models in which the radial oscillations are damped either through a KSVZ-like Yukawa interaction Eq. (
76
) or through a Higgs portal interaction Eq. (
96
).

In the damping-channel-independent analysis in Sec.
7
and Sec.
8
,
we have mapped out the best-case parameter space that can be realized by any model based on the nearly-quadratic potential.
The results are shown in
Fig.
2
,
3
,
5
, and
6
. The axion mass
m_{a}
and the radial mode mass
m_{\phi}
are related by Eq. (
60
). Generic features of these figures are the large-
m_{a}
regime which is constrained by the perturbativity condition, Eq. (
63
), and the small-
m_{a}
regime that is constrained by equivalence-principle constraints on the radial mode, Eq. (
29
). In addition, the intermediate
m_{a}
region is constrained by bounds on the thermal radial mode relic, Eq. (
27
). As a result, the available parameter space is separated into two disconnected regions; one is the low
m_{a}
region with
m_{\phi}<
eV and another is the high
m_{a}
region with
f_{a}^{2}/m_{\rm Pl}<m_{\phi}
.
As discussed in Sec.
3.2
, our analysis includes both the simplest scenario in which damping is assumed to take place through an unspecified mechanism early enough to avoid any entropy injection and the more general scenario in which damping takes place at specified temperature
T_{\rm damp}
, which potentially gives rise to significant injection of entropy to the SM plasma. The results for the simplest scenario, early damping, is summarised in Figs.

and

n=7,10,
and 13. Interestingly, the homogeneity condition presented in Sec.

, turns out to severely restrict the high-mass regime unless the power spectrum of primordial density fluctuations
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}
is much smaller on the relevant scale

than the value suggested by Planck on CMB scales. Such suppressed values of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
are observationally unconstrained and in our analysis we treat the condition as a constraint on the implementation of inflation which we assume is met.
To generalize the damping-independent analysis to arbitrary damping temperatures, we include entropy injections and evaluate how this shifts DM solutions and constraints. Possible thermal histories are classified in Fig.
4
. Generically, entropy injection shifts solutions to larger
m_{a}
and can thus open up parameter space that would otherwise not have been viable. This analysis is done in Sec.

In Sec.
9
, we investigated a KSVZ-like Yukawa coupling as an implementation of a mechanism to damp radial oscillations. The results are shown in Figs.
7
-
11
.
We describe the thermal effect on the potential from this interaction and details on the damping rates. The thermal potential excludes the low-mass regime identified above because of the competition between the
T=0
light radial masses and the thermal effects, but solutions for the high-mass regime remain. To describe the non-linear dynamics of this system, we cast the equations of motion into a system of Boltzmann equations which we integrate numerically.
The damping temperatures that are realized with this damping mechanism are reported in Fig.
7
.
As reported in Fig.
9
, we find that it is necessary to assume that
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
is smaller than the Planck reference value by a factor of about
10^{-3}
or
10^{-4}
. Assuming this, a parameter space with
10^{-6}~{\rm eV}\lesssim m_{a}\lesssim 10^{-1}~{\rm eV}
can be viable.
In a large range of parameter space, the QCD axion as well as a general ALP can be dark matter and in the reach of experiments such as ALPS-II, MADMAX, BRASS, BREAD and IAXO.
The parameter space also overlaps with the regime in which there may be an observable signal from gravitational lensing of axion miniclusters.
As shown in Fig.

Unfortunately, this GW amplification appears in tension with the need for the inflationary fluctuations,

\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
without suppressing the primordial GW background on the same scales
k_{\rm kin}

The parameter space that can be made viable with this implementation is summarized for

In Sec.
11
, we investigated models based on a quartic potentials. These are modified from the setup outlined in
[
1
,
7
,
8
]
by using a Hubble-induced mass instead of de Sitter fluctuations to drive a large initial radial VEV. The main motivation for this change is to alleviate the isocurvature and domain wall issues which challenge KMM scenarios with light PQ fields during inflation. In our implementation, such problems are avoided because both PQ components are heavy during inflation compared to the Hubble parameter.
In this way, the required tuning in the quartic coupling
\lambda
is strongly reduced from
\lambda<10^{-22}
, set by isocurvature, to about
\lambda\lesssim 10^{-6}
. Nevertheless, much lower values of
\lambda
are required to reach lower
m_{a}
, see Appendix
F
.
Because of these low values of
\lambda
, models with quartic potentials are less natural than SUSY-motivated models based on nearly-quadratic potentials. The case for quartic potentials is further weakened by the need to set the amplitude of the CP-violating potential by hand, see Eq. (
112
), for the kick to yield a roughly circular rotation with
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{O}(1)
. Such problem is not present in models based on nearly-quadratic potentials.
If these assumptions are permitted, then the models studied in Sec.
11
give rise to similar axion parameter space as in the nearly-quadratic scenario. The results for early damping is given in Fig.
15
and for thermal damping in Fig.
17
. Interestingly, this model features a regime in which neither a KSVZ-like Yukawa coupling nor a Higgs portal interaction is required for damping the radial mode, see Fig.
16
, as the decay of the radial mode into axions is enough to damp radial motion. Models based on quartic potentials require a similar amount of
\mathcal{P}_{\mathcal{R}}(k_{\rm kin})
suppression as required for nearly-quadratic potentials, see Fig.
17
.

In Appendix
F
, we collect a large number of additional figures scanning the full parameter space in both nearly-quadratic and quartic models (radial mode mass, Yukawa coupling, Higgs-portal coupling and Higgs quartic coupling) for completeness.

While all these scenarios only weakly depend on the reheat temperature of the universe, they are not independent of the inflation physics. We showed that
the primordial power spectrum has to be suppressed by typically 4 to 7 orders of magnitude at high wavenumbers. As discussed in Section
12
,
this motivates a class of two-step inflationary models that are discussed in
[
176
]
.

To close this summary, let us comment on possible scenarios which are not covered in this paper. We have assumed that the radial mode is relaxed to the potential minimum
\phi\simeq f_{a}
when the axion fragmentation starts. However, in principle, fragmentation could happen even if the radial mode is still rolling or oscillating. Such a scenario requires a detailed analysis of the dynamics of the radial and angular modes together. Another unexplored possibility is a scenario which does not satisfy the homogeneity condition Eq. (
25
). This scenario also has a rich phenomenology and will be presented in a future work
[
147
]
. Finally, we expect that the discussions in this paper remain relevant in stringy UV completions, as the axion kinetic energy cannot be dealt with independently, and damping the motion of other scalar moduli arising from the string compactififcation also needs to be addressed.

Appendix B
Thermal relics

In this work, we study the process in which oscillations of the radial mode are damped. Generically, this is achieved by introducing an interaction which efficiently connects the radial mode to the thermal plasma. While this solves the issue of excessive energy stored in radial oscillations, it also introduces a thermal population of
\phi
particles. In this appendix, we discuss the resulting constraints.

If the radial mode remains in thermal contact with the plasma indefinitely, and
m_{\phi}\gg T_{\rm BBN}
, then this population will become Boltzmann suppressed without posing a conflict with
N_{\rm eff}
. However, some damping interactions considered in this work are only efficient at high temperatures. Therefore, a thermal
\phi
relic may freeze out at some temperature
T_{\rm fo}\gg T_{\rm BBN}
.
At
T_{\rm fo}
, the thermal relic makes up a fraction
\rho_{\phi,\rm thermal}/\rho_{r}\sim 1/g_{*}
. Except for reheating, this ratio will remain constant until the thermal
\phi
relic becomes non-relativistic at some
T_{\rm nr}
. Because
T_{\rm nr}
refers to the temperature of the reheated SM plasma, it differs slightly from
T\sim m_{\phi}
and is

\displaystyle T_{\rm nr}\approx\left(\frac{g_{*s}(T_{\rm fo})}{g_{*s}(T_{\rm nr})}\right)^{1/3}m_{\phi}.

(121)

Below this temperature, the thermal
\phi
behaves as cold dark matter. If
m_{\phi}
is light enough for the relic to be stable against decay, then, at radiation-matter equality we find

\displaystyle\frac{\rho_{\phi,\rm thermal}}{\rho_{\rm DM}}\Big\rvert_{T_{\rm eq}}\approx\frac{1}{g_{*}(T_{\rm eq})}\frac{g_{*s}(T_{\rm eq})}{g_{*s}(T_{\rm fo})}\frac{m_{\phi}}{T_{\rm eq}},

(122)

where we made use of
\rho_{\rm r}(T_{\rm eq})\approx\rho_{\rm DM}(T_{\rm eq})
. To avoid overproduction of DM we arrive at

\displaystyle\text{Low mass window:}\quad m_{\phi}<\mathcal{O}(1)\times T_{\rm eq}.

(123)

This condition defines the low-mass regime of solutions observed e.g. in Fig.
2
. If instead
m_{\phi}
is large then the relic will decay directly into axions. The decay rate
\Gamma_{\varphi a}
is given by Eq. (
35
), which becomes efficient at a temperature
T_{\rm\phi a}
defined by
\Gamma_{\varphi a}\sim H(T_{\rm\phi a})
. The decay product is a hot population of axions which constitutes dark radiation subject to constraints from
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
.
Assuming that the thermal relic evolves as cold DM between
T_{\rm nr}
and
T_{\rm\phi a}
and as dark radiation at all other times, one can derive

\displaystyle\Delta N_{\rm eff}(T)\approx 2\frac{f_{a}}{\sqrt{m_{\rm Pl}m_{\phi}}}\frac{g_{*s}^{4/3}(T)}{g_{*s}(T_{\rm fo})g_{*}^{1/12}(T_{\rm\phi a})}.

(124)

N_{\rm eff}
is constrained directly at BBN to
\Delta N_{\rm eff}\lesssim 0.4

[
165
]
and at CMB to
\Delta N_{\rm eff}\lesssim 0.3

[
166
]
. For simplicity, we demand that
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
does not exceed 0.3 at either
T_{\rm BBN}
or at around the time of CMB, i.e.
T\sim
eV
\sim T_{\rm eq}
. At large enough
m_{\phi}
,
\phi
-to-axion decay terminates cold DM evolution early enough so that the mass range

\displaystyle\text{High mass window:}\quad\mathcal{O}(1)\times\frac{f_{a}^{2}}{m_{\rm Pl}}<m_{\phi}

(125)

is compatible with
\Delta N_{\rm eff}
constraints. In summary, if the damping interaction does not maintain
\phi
in thermal equilibrium with the plasma until
T\sim m_{\phi}
, then a constraint from the thermal
\phi
relic must be considered. This relic can be avoided through either Eq. (
123
), by making
m_{\phi}
light enough for the relic to underproduce DM, or through Eq. (
125
), by allowing
\phi
to decay into a hot but subdominant axion relic.

Appendix D
Restrictions on the reheating temperature

In this work, we generally allow for any reheating temperature between
T_{\rm kick}
and the energy scale of inflation
E_{I}
. However, there is a regime in which the choice of reheating temperatures is restricted by the consistency of the scenario, which we explore in this appendix. This discussion is specific to the nearly-quadratic models.

Because thermal effects respect the PQ symmetry and only enhance the radial potential and not the angular potential,
\epsilon
and
Y
are suppressed in scenarios where the radial mode is dominated by the thermal mass contributions at the time of the kick. We therefore favour solutions where
m_{\phi,0}
dominates at the time of the kick. Such
m_{\phi,0}
dominated scenarios can be realized in the regime where the
\chi
-fermions are Boltzmann suppressed, and the thermal potential is reduced from
m_{\phi,\rm th}
to the much smaller
m_{\phi,\rm ln}
at the time of the kick. However, this scenario is only consistent if there is no earlier time in which an early phase of relativistic
\chi
-fermions contribute a thermal mass large enough to start a kick.

To see how a would-be
m_{\phi,0}
-dominated kick can be spoiled by an earlier phase of relativistic
\chi
-fermions, note that prior to the kick
\phi
will track the early potential minimum given by Eq. (
52
), which evolves as

\displaystyle\phi\propto T^{\frac{2}{n-2}}\quad\text{before }T_{\rm kick}.

(137)

Thus,
\phi
redshifts slower than
T
for any choice of
n>4
. Therefore, any era in which
y\phi>T
will always be preceeded by an era in which
y\phi<T
if
T_{\rm reheat}
is arbitrarily high (see first plot of Fig.
22
). In such an earlier phase of relativistic
\chi
-fermions,
\phi
acquires the usual thermal mass
m_{\phi,\rm th}
, which can be much larger than
m_{\phi,\rm ln}
and which can therefore lead to an early kick. For some choices of parameters, this phenomenon makes solutions in which the kick is given in the non-relativistic phase, i.e. with
y\phi_{\rm kick}>T_{\rm kick}
, inconsistent unless reheating after inflation takes place in the window between the end of any would-be early relativistic phase with
m_{\phi,\rm th}>3H
and
T_{\rm kick}
itself. A specific example of how different choices
T_{\rm reheat}
can choose between two different families of solutions is illustrated in Fig.
22
. An exemplary map of the
[T_{\rm reheat},y]
parameter space is given in Fig.
23
, where the blue region corresponds to parameter space in which the kick takes place with
m_{\phi,\rm th}
and the triangular region around
y\sim 10^{-5}
corresponds to region in which an early
m_{\phi,\rm th}
kick can be avoided by postulating a sufficiently low
T_{\rm reheat}
.

Appendix E
Boltzmann equations and solutions of thermal damping

In this appendix, we derive the Boltzmann equations, which describe thermal damping. We then describe the numerical scheme we use to solve these systems of equations. We first consider the nearly-quadratic potential and then generalize to the quartic potential. Finally, in Subsection
E.2
we present examples of solutions.

E.1
Derivation

We first consider the case of the nearly-quadratic potential. We will, as usual, neglect the logarithmic correction and restrict ourselves to the regime in which higher-dimensional terms are suppressed such that the potential is

\displaystyle V\sim\frac{1}{2}m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi^{2},

(138)

where we have included the thermal correction in
m_{\phi,\rm eff}
.
The equations of motion for the radial and angular degrees of freedom of the PQ field are (see also Appendix G of
[
22
]
for an extensive discussion)

\displaystyle\ddot{\phi}+3H\dot{\phi}+\Gamma\dot{\phi}+m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi=\dot{\theta}^{2}\phi,

(139)

\displaystyle\ddot{\theta}+3H\dot{\theta}=-2\frac{\dot{\theta}\dot{\phi}}{\phi},

(140)

where
\Gamma
is the total dissipation rate. The total energy density of the field is

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm tot}=\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}+\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}+\rho_{\rm pot}=\frac{1}{2}\dot{\phi}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\phi^{2}\dot{\theta}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi^{2}.

(141)

To express the EOM in terms of Boltzmann equations, we consider the derivative of the energy density. If we allow for a time-dependence in the mass term from, e.g., thermal corrections, then we find

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm tot}=\dot{\phi}\ddot{\phi}+\phi\dot{\phi}\dot{\theta}^{2}+\phi^{2}\dot{\theta}\ddot{\theta}+m_{\phi,\rm eff}\dot{m}_{\phi,\rm eff}\phi^{2}+m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi\dot{\phi}.

(142)

We then apply the EOM and reduce:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm tot}=-(3H+\Gamma)\dot{\phi}^{2}-3H\dot{\theta}^{2}\phi^{2}+m_{\phi,\rm eff}\dot{m}_{\phi,\rm eff}\phi^{2}.

(143)

It is convenient to parameterize the time-dependence of
m_{\phi,\rm eff}
in terms of the parameter
c_{m_{\phi}}
,

\displaystyle m_{\phi,\rm eff}\propto a^{-c_{m_{\phi}}}.

(144)

In terms of this parameter
c_{m_{\phi}}
the last term of Eq. (
143
) becomes

\displaystyle m_{\phi,\rm eff}\dot{m}_{\phi,\rm eff}\phi^{2}=-c_{m_{\phi}}Hm_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi^{2},

(145)

so that
\dot{\rho}_{\rm tot}
can be written as

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm tot}=-(6H+2\Gamma)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-6H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}-2c_{m_{\phi}}H\rho_{\rm pot}.

(146)

Virial theorem:

To understand the relation between the potential and kinetic energy of the system, we apply the virial theorem, which for a monomial potential
\propto\phi^{n}
states that

\displaystyle\frac{n}{2}\expectationvalue{V}=\expectationvalue{T}\quad\mbox{where}\quad n=2\text{ for a quadratic potential},

(147)

where
V
is the total potential energy, and
T
is the total kinetic energy. We then have

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\rho_{\rm pot}}=\expectationvalue{\rho_{\rm kin}^\theta+\rho_{\rm kin}^\phi}.

(148)

We henceforth drop the brackets and understand all energy densities as time-averaged quantities. Combining Eq. (
148
) with Eq. (
141
) we conclude that

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm tot}=2\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}+2\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta},

(149)

so that

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm tot}=2\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\phi}+2\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\theta}=-(6H+2\Gamma+2c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-(6H+2c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}.

(150)

If we consider
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
and
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}
to be independent we can separate this equation into two Boltzmann equations:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\phi}=-(3H+\Gamma+c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi},

(151)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\theta}=-(3H+c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}.

(152)

Let us denote the energy of the circular orbit corresponding to a given angular velocity
\dot{\theta}
as
\rho_{\rm circ}
. Since for a circular orbit we have
\dot{\theta}=m_{\phi,\rm eff}
it follows that
\rho_{\rm circ}=\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
. We can therefore express the Boltzmann equations as a Boltzmann equation for the circular orbit and a Boltzmann equation for the oscillations about this orbit:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\phi}=-(3H+\Gamma+c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi},

(153)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm circ}=-(3H+c_{m_{\phi}}H)\rho_{\rm circ}.

(154)

With a total energy density of
\rho_{\rm tot}=2\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}+2\rho_{\rm circ}
. By conservation of energy this can be linked to the energy density of the plasma:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{r}=-4H\rho_{r}+\Gamma\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}.

(155)

The set of equations Eqs. (
153
-
155
) are then the equations we will solve numerically to describe the thermal damping of the PQ field. To complete the numerical setup, we also need to determine the initial conditions and specify how to describe the transition between phases of relativistic/non-relativistic fermions, where we do not have a description of the mass change of the form
m\propto a^{-c_{m}}
.

Initial conditions:

After the kick, we assume that the energy density is composed of angular kinetic and potential energy, i.e.

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm ini}=\frac{1}{2}m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi_{\rm kick}^{2}+\frac{1}{2}\dot{\theta}_{\rm kick}^{2}\phi_{\rm kick}^{2}=\frac{1+\epsilon^{2}}{2}m_{\phi,\rm eff}^{2}\phi_{\rm kick}^{2}.

(156)

After the kick this energy is distributed into
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
,
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}
and
\rho_{\rm pot}
. To identify the distribution, note that in
[
22
]
it was found that after the kick

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}=\epsilon\rho_{\rm pot},

(157)

so that in combination with the virial theorem, this implies

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm ini},

(158)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-\epsilon}{2}\rho_{\rm ini}

(159)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{\epsilon}{2}\rho_{\rm ini}.

(160)

These equations then specify the initial conditions for the Boltzmann equations.

Relativistic/non-relativistic fermion transition:

The parameter
k
takes into account continuous and gradual changes in the mass as with the temperature-dependence of a thermal mass. However, we generally approximate the transition between phases with relativistic and non-relativistic fermions as instantaneous because it is non-trivial to express in greater detail. Therefore we cannot encode the change in radial mass across such a transition in the parameter
c_{m_{\phi}}
as in Eq. (
144
). To take the transition into account in a physical way, we will instead assume that the transition is gradual enough to be adiabatic such that
\phi\propto m^{-1/2}
applies. Across a transition where the mass changes from
m\to m^{*}
this implies that
\phi
and an energy density
\rho\propto m^{2}\phi^{2}
is modified as

\displaystyle\phi

\displaystyle\underset{m\to m^{*}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\left(\frac{m}{m_{*}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\phi,

(161)

\displaystyle\rho

\displaystyle\underset{m\to m^{*}}{\xrightarrow{\hskip 28.45274pt}}\left(\frac{m_{*}}{m}\right)\rho.

(162)

We use this solution to account for a change in
m_{\phi,\rm eff}
across a relativistic/non-relativistic fermion transition. We assume that
\rho_{\rm pot}
,
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
and
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}
are all shifted by the same factor and that the energy required for this change is transferred to or from the radiation bath.

In the numerical Boltzmann solver, we then apply this transformation to
\phi
and the energy densities whenever the fermions transition between their relativistic and non-relativistic phases.

Quartic potential

For a quartic potential,

\displaystyle V\sim\frac{1}{4}\lambda\phi^{4},

(163)

we can follow the same steps as above to arrive again at

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm tot}=-6H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-2\Gamma\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-6H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}.

(164)

The virial theorem for
\phi^{n}
with
n=4
now states that
2\expectationvalue{V}=\expectationvalue{T}
, so that

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pot}=\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}+\frac{1}{2}\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}.

(165)

The combined Boltzmann equation is then

\displaystyle\frac{3}{2}\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\phi}+\frac{3}{2}\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\theta}=-6H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-2\Gamma\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-6H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}.

(166)

If we again assume that
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}
and
\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}
are independent, then we arrive at the following two Boltzmann equations:

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=-4H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}-\frac{4}{3}\Gamma\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi},

(167)

\displaystyle\dot{\rho}_{\rm kin}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=-4H\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}.

(168)

If a thermal potential becomes dominant, then the evolution is instead determined by
151
and
152
as in the quadratic case. The initial conditions are modified to

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm pot}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}\rho_{\rm ini},

(169)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm kin}^{\phi}

\displaystyle=(1-\epsilon)\frac{2}{3}\rho_{\rm ini}

(170)

\displaystyle\rho_{\rm kin}^{\theta}

\displaystyle=\epsilon\frac{2}{3}\rho_{\rm ini}.

(171)
