Sample Complexity Bounds for Estimating Probability Divergences under Invariances
Behrooz Tahmasebi, Stefanie Jegelka

Abstract
Group-invariant probability distributions appear in many data-generative models in machine learning, such as graphs, point clouds, and images. In practice, one often needs to estimate divergences between such distributions. In this work, we study how the inherent invariances, with respect to any smooth action of a Lie group on a manifold, improve sample complexity when estimating the 1-Wasserstein distance, the Sobolev Integral Probability Metrics (Sobolev IPMs), the Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD), and also the complexity of the density estimation problem (in the L 2 L^{2} and L ∞ L^{\infty} distance). Our results indicate a two-fold gain: (1) reducing the sample complexity by a multiplicative factor corresponding to the group size (for finite groups) or the normalized volume of the quotient space (for groups of positive dimension); (2) improving the exponent in the convergence rate (for groups of positive dimension). These results are completely new for groups of positive dimension and extend recent bounds for finite group actions.

4.1
1-Wasserstein Distance

For any probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, assume that we are given samples
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mu
. Let
\hat{\mu}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{i}}

\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure supported on

However, for
G
-invariant
\mu

\hat{\mu}
is not necessarily
G
-invariant.
To address this issue, we introduce and use a modified empirical measure, where the estimation is restricted to only the non-trivial parts of the Fourier transform of the measure (see the proof of Theorem
1
), as for measures that exhibit invariance, parts of the spectrum that correspond to non-invariant functions are zero. Thus, the modified empirical measure is always
G
-invariant. The first result of this paper is the following theorem on the convergence of the 1-Wasserstein distance for arbitrary (Borel) probability measures using the modified empirical measure.

Theorem 1

(Convergence rate of the 1-Wasserstein distance under invariances)
.

For any
G
-invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, as a function of

, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})]\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d}},

(4)

where
\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)
is the volume of the quotient space
\mathcal{M}/G
, and
d:=\dim(\mathcal{M}/G)\geq 3
. Also, the constant can only depend on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
. Consequently, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|W_{1}(\tilde{\mu},\tilde{\nu})-W_{1}(\mu,\nu)|]\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d}},

(5)

for any
G
-invariant probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
.

In proving this theorem, there are some complications to overcome. For instance, even though we used the notation
\dim(\mathcal{M}/G)
and
\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)
, we notice that the quotient space
\mathcal{M}/G
is
not
necessarily a manifold. Also, it may exhibit boundary, even though

is assumed to be boundaryless;
this can make the Fourier approach inapplicable to this problem

Tahmasebi and Jegelka 2023
)
. To address this issue, we define

(or

) as the dimension (or the volume) of the

of the quotient space. The principal part, denoted as
\mathcal{M}_{0}/G
, is a connected dense subset of
\mathcal{M}/G
, such that it has a manifold structure inherited from
\mathcal{M}
. Since it is a manifold, one can define its dimension/volume in a natural way. It is guaranteed that the principal part exists, and is unique, under the assumptions of this paper. Besides the principal part,
\mathcal{M}/G
is only a disjoint union of

many other manifolds, all of lower dimension than the principal part.
Note that

is defined with respect to the dimension of the quotient space

To compare the convergence rate with the general case (i.e., not necessarily

This shows that the sample complexity of estimating the 1-Wasserstein distance is improved under invariances; (1) the new exponent is

Proof sketch for Theorem

2
.
To define the estimator
\tilde{\mu}
, we need to review some facts about manifolds. The set of square-integrable
G
-invariant functions on a connected, compact, smooth manifold
\mathcal{M}
has an orthonormal basis
\phi_{\ell}^{\text{inv}}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
,
\ell=0,1,\ldots
that consists of the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathcal{M}
(see Appendix
A
for more details). For example, for the circle
\mathcal{M}=\mathbb{S}^{1}
, these functions correspond to the sinusoidal waves that are invariant under the group action. Given
n
samples
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots,X_{n}
, the Borel measure
\tilde{\mu}
is defined using its Radon-Nikodym derivative with respect to the uniform probability measure on
(\mathcal{M},g)
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\mu}}{dx}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{\ell=0}^{T-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi_{\ell}^{\text{inv}}(X_{i})\phi_{\ell}^{\text{inv}},

(9)

where
T
is a fixed positive integer (to be set). For any
T
,
\tilde{\mu}
is a Borel measure, but generally, it can be a signed measure. We can then take the closest probability measure to
\tilde{\mu}
in the 1-Wasserstein distance as the final estimation for
\mu
. With a slight abuse of notation, we denote the final output of the algorithm by
\tilde{\mu}
again.

The parameter
T
indicates when the sum is terminated. Note that estimating the higher-order coefficients in the Fourier basis requires many samples, and so if the Fourier coefficients decay quickly, one can neglect them and truncate the sum at a finite
T
with a small error. Indeed, choosing a higher number
T
of eigenfunctions reduces the bias of the estimator, but it also increases the variance due to the randomness of sampling and the difficulty of estimating the higher-order terms.
Therefore, optimizing
T
to balance the bias and variance terms, according to the problem’s parameters, allows us to achieve the best algorithm of this type (in terms of the convergence rate). Note that the decay of the Fourier coefficients is provided using the Sobolev space assumption (see Appendix
C
more details). We follow this approach to prove Theorem
2
.

4.3
Sobolev Integral Probability Metrics (Sobolev IPMs)

Sobolev Integral Probability Metrics (IPMs) are a family of integral probability metrics
(
Müller 1997
;
Sriperumbudur et al. 2012
)
that use the Sobolev space
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
as the set of test functions to define a divergence between probability measures.

Definition 2

(Sobolev IPMs)
.

For any
\alpha\geq 0
, the Sobolev IPM with parameter
\alpha
is defined as

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[f(x)]\Big\},

for any (Borel) probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
.

Here, we study the sample complexity of estimating Sobolev IPMs under invariances, where we assume that we have samples from a smooth probability measure
\mu
; more precisely,
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
for some
s\geq 0
.
Note that for
\alpha>d/2
, the Sobolev IPMs are special cases of the Maximum Mean Discrepancies (MMDs) with the Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
. Since we cover the convergence rates of MMDs in the next section, we focus on the non-MMD regime where
\alpha\leq d/2
.

Theorem 3

(Convergence rate of Sobolev IPMs under invariances)
.

Consider a
G
-invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
with
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
for some
s\geq 0
.

•

If
\alpha<d/2
, then there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, as a function of
n
i.i.d. samples
X_{1},X_{2}\ldots,X_{n}\sim\mu
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})]\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{s+\alpha}{2s+d}}\penalty\ \Big\|\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{d-2\alpha}{2s+d}},

where the constant only depends on the manifold and
\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)
is the volume of the quotient space
\mathcal{M}/G
with
d:=\dim(\mathcal{M}/G)
.

•

If
\alpha=d/2
, then there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu})]\lesssim\sqrt{\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\log(n)}{n}}.

We note that the same two-fold gain is observed in the convergence of the Sobolev IPMs when
\alpha\leq d/2
.

The convergence results behave differently in the three regimes. First, if
\alpha<d/2
, it extends the 1-Wasserstein distance convergence rate for smooth distributions to the Sobolev IPMs. Second, if
\alpha>d/2
, as we will see in the next section, the rate saturates at
O(n^{-1/2})
.
Finally, in the third convergence regime where
\alpha=d/2
, we get a convergence rate of the order
O(\sqrt{\log(n)/n})
.

Remark 1
.

While Theorem
3
holds when (at least)
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, we can extend it to all (Borel) probability measures (for which the rate corresponds to
s=0
), by the same approach as the proof of Theorem
1
.

Proof sketch for Theorem

3
. We follow the same approach as Theorem
2
. Here, the coefficients of the test functions
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
in the Laplace-Beltrami basis can be upper bounded using the parameter
\alpha
(by the definition of Sobolev spaces). Then, we use an appropriate frequency
T
, as a function of
\alpha
, to simultaneously minimize the bias and variance terms.

4.4
Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD)

The Maximum Mean Discrepancy (MMD) is an integral probability metric associated with a Positive Definite Symmetric (PDS) kernel, where the set of test functions is a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Space (RKHS)
(
Berlinet and Thomas-Agnan 2011
)
.

Definition 3
.

For any PDS kernel
K:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
with an RKHS denoted by
\mathcal{H}
, define

\displaystyle D_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\Big\{\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[f(x)]-\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[f(x)]\Big\},

for any (Borel) probability measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
.

Note that when
\alpha>d/2
, the Sobolev IPMs are MMDs, since in that case,
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
is an RKHS (Sobolev Inequality). For simplicity, we assume that the PDS kernel
K
is diagonalizable in the Laplace-Beltrami basis:

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\xi_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(x)\phi_{\ell}(y),

(10)

for some
\xi_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
. This holds, for example, for the dot-product kernels on the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
as well as all Sobolev spaces
\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
with
\alpha>d/2
.

To study the gain of invariances for estimating MMDs, we need to define a quantity that measures how many eigenfunctions can take non-zero coefficients when restricted to the
G
-invariant functions. This weighted quantity must also depend on the kernel decomposition in the Laplace-Beltrami operator.
To this end, define

\displaystyle\tr(K;G):=\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}^{\infty}m(\lambda;G)|\xi_{\ell}(\lambda)|,

(11)

where the sum is over all the eigenvalues of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on the manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
, and
m(\lambda;G)
denotes the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\lambda
when restricted to invariant eigenfunctions (see Appendix
A
for more details).

The following theorem related the quantity
\tr(K;G)
to the sample complexity gain of invariances for the MMD estimation problem.

Theorem 4
.

Consider a
G
-invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
with
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\in\mathcal{H}
. Then, there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\mathcal{H}}(\mu,\hat{\mu})]\leq\sqrt{\frac{\tr(K;G)}{n}}.

(12)

This allows us to immediately conclude the following result for the Sobolev IPMs with
\alpha>d/2
(i.e., the MMD regime).

Corollary 1

(Gain of invariances for the Sobolev IPMs in the MMD regime)
.

For any
\alpha>d/2
, there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu})]\leq\sqrt{\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G)}{n}},

where
\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G)
is the zeta function associated with the Lie group action
G
on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G):=\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}m(\lambda;G)\lambda^{-\alpha}.

(13)

Note that if
\alpha>d/2
(i.e., the MMD regime), the exponent is always
\frac{1}{2}
and it does not depend on the group of invariances.
Moreover, the gain of invariances in this case can intuitively be understood as having
effectively

\displaystyle n\times\frac{\mathcal{Z}(\alpha)}{\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G)}

(14)

samples instead of
n
samples, where
\mathcal{Z}(\alpha)
is the original zeta function of the manifold (achieved via the trivial group
G=\{\text{id}_{G}\}
). Therefore, the quantity
\mathcal{Z}(\alpha)/\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G)
represents the sample complexity gain of invariances in the kernel regime.

We provide an example to see how the behavior of the quantity
\mathcal{Z}(\alpha)/\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G)
can depend on more delicate information about the group action (i.e., more than the dimension of the group and the volume of the quotient space).

Example 2
.

Consider the unit circle
\mathbb{S}^{1}
with the round metric. Note that if we parameterize functions defined on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
as functions of the angle
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
, then the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator on
\mathbb{S}^{1}
are the sinusoidal functions:

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(\theta)=\exp(ik\theta)\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda=k^{2};\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}.

(15)

This allows us to compute the zeta function of the unit circle as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}^{\infty}m(\lambda)\lambda^{-\alpha}=2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-2\alpha}=2\zeta(2\alpha),

(16)

where
\zeta
denotes the Riemann zeta function.

Now consider the finite group of rotations around the center:
G:=\Big\{\frac{2\pi j}{|G|}:j\in\mathbb{Z}\Big\}.

An eigenfunction
\phi_{k}
is invariant with respect to the action of
G
if and only if we have

\displaystyle\phi_{k}(\theta)=\phi_{k}\Big(\theta+\frac{2\pi j}{|G|}\Big),

(17)

for any
\theta\in[0,2\pi)
and
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. This is equivalent to having
k
divide
|G|
. Thus, we get

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}(\alpha;G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}^{\infty}m(\lambda;G)\lambda^{-\alpha}=2|G|^{-2\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}k^{-2\alpha}

(18)

\displaystyle=2|G|^{-2\alpha}\zeta(2\alpha).

(19)

This shows that the gain of invariances in sample complexity, in this case, is to have effectively

\displaystyle n\times|G|^{2\alpha}

(20)

many samples instead of
n
samples. Note that
\alpha>d/2
in the MMD regime. Therefore, this example shows how the multiplicative gain can be improved from
|G|
to
|G|^{2\alpha}
in the MMD regime, which is in contrast to
the problem of estimating Sobolev IPMs in the non-MMD regime (Theorem
3
).

Example 3
.

Let
\mathbb{T}^{d}=[0,1]^{d}
denote the
d
-dimensional flat torus, and consider the isometric action of the group
G=\mathbb{T}^{\dim(G)}
with the circular shift on the first
\dim(G)
coordinates of
\mathbb{T}^{d}
; for any
\tau\in G
and
x\in\mathbb{T}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\tau x:=

\displaystyle\big(\tau_{1}+x_{1},\tau_{2}+x_{2},\ldots,\tau_{\dim(G)}+x_{\dim(G)},

(21)

\displaystyle x_{\dim(G)+1},\ldots,x_{d}\big),

(22)

where the coordinates are summed modulo one.
Consider the heat kernel on
\mathbb{T}^{d}
, defined as:

\displaystyle K_{\beta}(x,y)=\sum_{0\neq v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}}\exp(-\beta\|v\|^{2}_{2}+2\pi i\langle v,x+y\rangle).

Note that

\displaystyle\tr(K_{\beta};G)

\displaystyle=\sum_{0\neq v\in\mathbb{Z}^{d-\dim(G)}}\exp(-\beta\|v\|^{2}_{2})

(23)

\displaystyle=\Theta_{\beta}^{(d-\dim(G))}-1,

(24)

where

\displaystyle\Theta_{\beta}:=\sum_{v\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp(-\beta v^{2})>1.

(25)

This shows that the gain of having invariances with respect to the group
G
for estimating the MMD with the heat kernel is to have effectively

\displaystyle n\times\frac{\tr(K;\{\id_{G}\})}{\tr(K;G)}

\displaystyle=n\times\frac{\Theta_{\beta}^{d}-1}{\Theta_{\beta}^{d-\dim(G)}-1}

\displaystyle\approx n\times\Theta_{\beta}^{\dim(G)},

many samples, instead of
n
. This shows that the gain of invariances can exponentially depend on the Lie group dimension
\dim(G)
while estimating MMDs.

Proof sketch for Theorem

4
. The spectrum of the test functions
f\in\mathcal{H}
, in the Laplace-Beltrami basis, decays quickly in the MMD case, and it can be shown that we can take
T\to\infty
, and use a similar approach to Theorem
3
to prove the convergence result.

4.5
Density Estimation in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})

In this section, we present convergence results for estimating probability density functions
\frac{d\mu}{dx}
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
distance while having
n
i.i.d. samples from
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
.

Theorem 5

(Convergence rate for density estimation in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
distance under invariances)
.

Consider a
G
-invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
with
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
. Then, there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{d\tilde{\mu}}{dx}-

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\Big]\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{s}{2s+d}}\penalty\ \Big\|\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{d}{2s+d}},

where the constant only depends on the manifold and
\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)
denotes the volume of the quotient space
\mathcal{M}/G
with
d:=\dim(\mathcal{M}/G)\geq 3
.

We observe that the density estimation problem in the
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
distance exploits the same two-fold gain as previous cases.

4.6
Density Estimation in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})

In this part, we study the gain of group invariances for the density estimation problem in the
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
distance.

Theorem 6

(Convergence rate for density estimation in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
under invariances)
.

Consider a
G
-invariant probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
with
\frac{d\mu}{dx}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
for some
s>d/2
. Then, there exists an estimator
\tilde{\mu}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{d\tilde{\mu}}{dx}-

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\Big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\Big(\frac{(\vol(\mathcal{M}/G))^{\frac{2s}{s-d/2}}}{n}\Big)^{\frac{s-d/2}{2s+d}}\penalty\ \Big\|\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{d}{s+d/2}},

where the constant only depends on the manifold.

Note that here we only get a convergence result if
s>d/2
; otherwise, the density
\frac{d\mu}{dx}
is not necessarily bounded, since
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
contains unbounded functions in that case (Sobolev inequality).

Remark 2
.

Note that in the density estimation in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
distance, for finite groups, the effective number of samples is
n\times|G|
, as intuitively expected (for all
s,d
). However, for the
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
distance, better rates are achievable according to Theorem
6
. For instance, let us take
d=4
and
s=6
; then, for finite groups, the convergence rate becomes

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\Big\|\frac{d\tilde{\mu}}{dx}-

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})}\Big]\lesssim\Big(\frac{1}{n|G|^{3}}\Big)^{1/4}.

(26)

This shows that the effective number of samples can potentially be more than the linear gain, i.e., in this example, we have an effective number of samples
n\times|G|^{3}
.

We also notice that in this paper we used a specific simple algorithm to achieve the bound for the density estimation in
L^{\infty}(\mathcal{M})
, and it can be improved when there is no invariances
(
Uppal et al. 2019
)
. We leave the problem of proving tighter bounds for this case under invariances to future works.

6
Conclusion

In this paper, we studied the sample complexity gain of group invariances for estimating various divergence measures on manifolds, given a number of i.i.d. samples. We extended the recent theoretical results for finite group actions on submanifolds (of full dimension) of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
to arbitrary manifolds (including spheres, tori, hyperbolic spaces, etc.), and arbitrary groups (including groups of positive dimension). The gain of invariances in sample complexity is two-fold: improving the exponent and multiplying the number of samples by a factor depending on the group of invariances.
We also studied faster convergence rates for smooth distributions and showed a similar gain in that case.

Appendix A
Preliminaries for Proofs

This section reviews some essential preliminaries on group actions on manifolds
(
Tahmasebi and Jegelka 2023
)
. We consider a compact connected boundaryless smooth Riemannian manifold
(\mathcal{M},g)
with dimension
\dim(\mathcal{M})
. The Riemannian metric
g
allows us to define the volume element
d\vol_{g}(x)
on
\mathcal{M}
, and without loss of generality, we assume that it is normalized such that
\vol(\mathcal{M})=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\vol_{g}(x)=1
. The volume element is also denoted by
dx
whenever the choice of the Riemannian metric is evident from the context.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator
\Delta_{g}
is the unique continuous operator satisfying the integration by parts formula:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{M}}\langle\nabla_{g}\phi(x),\nabla_{g}\psi(x)\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}d\vol\nolimits_{g}(x)=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\Delta_{g}\phi(x)\psi(x)d\vol\nolimits_{g}(x),

(27)

for any smooth function
\phi,\psi:\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
. One can see that the Euclidean Laplacian
\Delta=\sum_{i}\partial_{i}^{2}
satisfies this definition by Green’s identities.

The Laplace-Beltrami operator eigenfunctions, denoted by
\phi_{\ell}
,
\ell=0,1,\ldots
, are the the sequence of functions satisfying the equation
\Delta_{g}\phi+\lambda_{\ell}\phi=0
on the manifold
\mathcal{M}
, where
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots
denote the eigenvalues of
(-\Delta_{g})
. Let us assume that
\|\phi_{\ell}\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi^{2}_{\ell}(x)d\vol_{g}(x)=1
, i.e., the eigenfunctions are normalized in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
. One important property of the Laplace-Beltrami eigenfunctions it that they constitute a basis for the function space
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

It can be shown that the sequence
\lambda_{\ell}\to\infty
as
\ell\to\infty
, thus it does not accumulate around any finite number. Moreover, according to the celebrated Weyl’s law, the density of eigenvalues is given by the following asymptotic formula:

\displaystyle N(\lambda):=\#\{\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda\}=\frac{\omega_{\dim(\mathcal{M})}}{(2\pi)^{\dim(\mathcal{M})}}\vol(\mathcal{M})\lambda^{\dim(\mathcal{M})/2}+O(\lambda^{(\dim(\mathcal{M})-1)/2}),

(28)

where
\omega_{\dim(\mathcal{M})}
is the volume of the unit
\dim(\mathcal{M})
-dimensional ball in the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{\dim(\mathcal{M})}
(with the usual metric). Even more, one can show that Weyl’s law holds locally around each point on the manifold:

\displaystyle N_{x}(\lambda):=\sum_{\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda}\phi^{2}_{\ell}(x)=\frac{\omega_{\dim(\mathcal{M})}}{(2\pi)^{\dim(\mathcal{M})}}\vol(\mathcal{M})\lambda^{\dim(\mathcal{M})/2}+O(\lambda^{(\dim(\mathcal{M})-1)/2}),

(29)

where the error term is uniformly bounded for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
. Note that integrating the above formula on the manifolds gives the original version of Weyl’s law.

A Lie group
G
acts on
\mathcal{M}
isometrically if for all
\tau\in G
, the corresponding map
x\mapsto\tau x
on the manifold is an isometry.
A function
f:\mathcal{M}\to\mathcal{M}
is called
G-
invariant, if and only if one has
f(x)=f(\tau x)
, for all
\tau\in G
, in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

For any function
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, one has

f=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\langle f,\phi_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\phi_{\ell}
. However, for any
G
-invariant function, one can show that many coefficients in the above series are a priory known to be zero. Indeed, for any eigenspace of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, such as
V_{\lambda}
, with eigenvalue
\lambda
, there exists a subspace
V_{\lambda,G}\subseteq V_{\lambda}
such that all
G
-invariant functions
f\in V_{\lambda}
can be written as a linear combination of functions in
V_{\lambda,G}

(
Tahmasebi and Jegelka 2023
)
. Without loss of generality, we assume that the eigenfunctions
\phi_{\ell}
are chosen such that for any
\lambda
there exists a subset of
\{\phi_{\ell}:\ell=0,1,\ldots\}
that forms an orthonormal basis for
V_{\lambda,G}
. Let
\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)
indicate whether
\phi_{\ell}\in V_{\lambda,G}
(with one) or not (with zero).
Let
N_{x}(\lambda;G):=\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda}\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)\phi_{\ell}^{2}(x)
.

We are interested in asymptotic estimation formulae for
N_{x}(\lambda;G)
to study the gain of invariances. Note that if
G=\{\text{id}_{G}\}
, then Weyl’s law provides such estimation. The following theorem shows how to extend it to non-trivial groups.

Theorem 7

(Local Weyl’s law for
G
-invariant functions,
(
Tahmasebi and Jegelka 2023
)
)
.

For any Lie group
G
acting smoothly on a connected compact boundaryless manifold
\mathcal{M}
, one has for all
x\in\mathcal{M}
,

\displaystyle N_{x}(\lambda;G)=\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda}\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)\phi^{2}_{\ell}(x)=\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\lambda^{d/2}+O(\lambda^{(d-1)/2}),

(30)

where
d:=\dim(\mathcal{M}/G)
and
\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)
denote the dimension and the volume of the principal part of the quotient space, respectively. Note that one always has
N_{x}(\lambda;G)\leq N_{x}(\lambda)
.

A Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}\subseteq L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
is called a Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces (RKHS) if and only if for any
x\in\mathcal{M}
and
f\in\mathcal{H}
, one has
|f(x)|\leq C_{x}\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}}
for some constant
C_{x}<\infty
. Associated with
\mathcal{H}
, there exists a PDS kernel
K:\mathcal{M}\times\mathcal{M}\to\mathbb{R}
, and according to Mercer’s theorem, it can be diagonalized in some basis in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
. In this paper, for the sake of simplicity, we consider kernels that can be diagonalized in the Laplace-Beltrami basis as follows:

\displaystyle K(x,y)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\xi_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}(x)\phi_{\ell}(y),

(31)

for some coefficients
\xi_{\ell}\in\mathbb{R}
. This includes dot-product kernels over spheres. We further assume that the coefficients, for each
\ell
, only depend on
\lambda_{\ell}
, thus we can denote them as
\xi(\lambda)
, too. A set of sufficient conditions for kernels on manifolds to satisfy such types of diagonalization is given in
(
Tahmasebi and Jegelka 2023
)
.

We also use the following definition of Sobolev spaces
\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
for any
s\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M}):=\Big\{f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M}):\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}:=\langle f,\phi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{s}\langle f,\phi_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}<\infty\Big\}.

(32)

Sobolev spaces are indeed Hilbert spaces under the following inner product:

\displaystyle\langle f_{1},f_{2}\rangle_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}:=\langle f_{1},\phi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\langle f_{2},\phi_{0}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{s}\langle f_{1},\phi_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}\langle f_{2},\phi_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}.

(33)

Note that Sobolev spaces are Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces if and only if
s>d/2

(
Tahmasebi and Jegelka 2023
)
. For any
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta<\infty
, one has
\mathcal{H}^{\beta}(\mathcal{M})\subseteq\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\subseteq L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
. Moreover,
H^{0}(\mathcal{M})=L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
.

Appendix B
Proof of Theorem
1

See
1

Proof.

Let
\phi_{\ell}
,
\ell=0,1,\ldots
, denote the eigenfunctions of the Laplace-Beltrami operator, corresponding to the eigenvalues
0=\lambda_{0}<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots
. Each eigenspace is denoted by
V_{\lambda_{\ell}}
, and its subspace of
G
-invariant eigenfunctions is denoted by
V_{\lambda_{\ell},G}
(see Appendix
A
for more details).
For any probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, define

\mu_{\ell}=\int_{\mathcal{M}}\phi_{\ell}d\mu
for each
\ell=0,1,\ldots
, and note that
\mu_{0}=1
.
Let
dx=\frac{1}{\vol(\mathcal{M})}d\vol_{g}(x)
denote the uniform measure on the manifold and define the inner-product in
L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
with respect to the measure
d\vol_{g}(x)
. If a probability measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the uniform measure, then
\frac{d\mu}{dx}
denotes its Radon-Nikodym derivative.

Lemma 1
.

Consider two Borel measures
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
with
\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\mu=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\nu=1
.
Also, assume that
\frac{d\mu}{dx},\frac{d\nu}{dx}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
with
\vol(\mathcal{M})=1
.
Then,

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq D_{1}(\mu,\nu),

(34)

where
D_{1}(\mu,\nu)
is the Sobolev IPM with parameter
\alpha=1
.

Proof.

Note that
\Lip(\mathcal{M})\subset L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
. By Rademacher’s theorem
(
Evans and Gariepy 2015
)
, any
f\in\Lip(\mathcal{M})
is differentiable almost everywhere, and thus
|\nabla_{g}f(x)|_{g}\leq 1
for almost every
x\in\mathcal{M}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{g}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\mathcal{M}}|\nabla_{g}f(x)|_{g}^{2}d\text{vol}_{g}(x)\leq\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\text{vol}_{g}(x)=1.

(35)

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
, one can write
f=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
with
f_{\ell}:=\langle f,\phi_{\ell}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{f\in\Lip(\mathcal{M})}\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\mu-\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\nu\Big\}

(36)

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|\nabla_{g}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}\leq 1\end{subarray}}\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\mu-\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\nu\Big\}.

(37)

Now note that since
\mu_{0}=\nu_{0}
, one can restrict the above optimization problem to functions with
f_{0}=0
. For those functions, we have
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{M})}=\|\nabla_{g}f\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}=1
, and thus we have

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\nu)\leq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{1}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\mu-\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\nu\Big\}=D_{1}(\mu,\nu).

(38)

∎

To prove Theorem
1
, we use Lemma
1
. First, note that it is impossible to immediately apply Lemma
1
since the unknown measure
\mu
is not necessarily absolutely continuous with respect to the uniform measure on the manifold. Nevertheless, let us define the sequence

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)\phi_{\ell}(X_{i}),

(39)

for each
\ell=0,1,\ldots
.

Given the probability measure
\mu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, define the twisted measure
\mu_{\star}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d\mu_{\star}}{dx}=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\exp(-\sigma\lambda_{\ell})\mu_{\ell}\phi_{\ell},

(40)

where
\sigma
is a fixed positive constant (to be set later). Note that for a fixed
\sigma
, the new measure
\mu_{\star}
is absolutely continuous with respect to the uniform measure. Indeed, its density is even smooth (i.e., having smooth derivatives with arbitrary orders), since the tail of the above summation goes to zero exponentially fast as
\ell\to\infty
. Also, note that
\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\mu_{\star}=1
, and with a bit more consideration, we can observe that
\mu_{\star}
is a probability measure. Let us also define
\tilde{\mu}_{\star}
similarly, where
\mu_{\ell}
is replaced with its empirical estimation as above. Thus, the measure
\tilde{\mu}_{\star}
is computable from the given samples.

There is also another interpretation for the probability measure
\mu_{\star}
. Let
X\sim\mu
denote a sample from
\mu
, and consider the heat diffusion corresponding to the Brownian motion on the manifold, started from
X
and stopped at time
t=\sigma
. The Brownian motion is assumed to be independent of the sample
X
, and it can be shown that the law of the process at time
t=\sigma
is the probability measure
\mu_{\star}
. Therefore, this gives a natural coupling between the two measures
\mu
and
\mu_{\star}
.

Hence, we can write

\displaystyle W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{\star})

\displaystyle\leq W_{1}(\mu,\mu_{\star})+W_{1}(\mu_{\star},\tilde{\mu}_{\star})

(41)

\displaystyle\leq C\sqrt{\sigma}+W_{1}(\mu_{\star},\tilde{\mu}_{\star}),

(42)

where we use the fact that for any probability measure
\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})
, one has
W_{1}(\nu,\nu_{\star})\leq W_{2}(\nu,\nu_{\star})\leq C\sqrt{\sigma}
for some constant
C
depending on the manifold. The last inequality is due to the fact that the law of the Brownian motion on the manifold at time
t=\sigma
behaves (locally) similarly to the Gaussian distribution on Euclidean spaces.

Let us now bound
\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{\star},\tilde{\mu}_{\star})]
. Since the two measures
\mu_{\star},\tilde{\mu}_{\star}
are absolutely continuous with respect to the uniform measure on the manifold, we can use Lemma
1
and Lemma
2
; thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{\star},\tilde{\mu}_{\star})]\leq\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda_{\ell})\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-\tilde{\mu}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda_{\ell}}\Big]}.

(43)

Let us define

\displaystyle R(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda}(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}\Big],

(44)

and note that we can rewrite Equation (
43
) as follows:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu_{\star},\tilde{\mu}_{\star})]\leq\sqrt{\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)\frac{dR(\lambda)}{\lambda}},

(45)

where the above formula must be understood as a Riemann-Stieltjes integral.

Using integration by parts and Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)\frac{dR(\lambda)}{\lambda}=\underbrace{\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)\frac{R(\lambda)}{\lambda}\Big|_{0^{+}}^{\infty}}_{=0}+\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}R(\lambda)\frac{2\sigma\lambda+1}{\lambda^{2}}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)d\lambda.

(46)

Now we use Lemma
3
and Theorem
7
to find an upper bound on the above integral:

\displaystyle\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}R(\lambda)\frac{2\sigma\lambda+1}{\lambda^{2}}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)d\lambda

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\lambda^{d/2}\frac{2\sigma\lambda+1}{\lambda^{2}}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)d\lambda

(47)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}(2\sigma\lambda^{d/2-1}+\lambda^{d/2-2})\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)d\lambda.

(48)

By a change of variables in the above integral, we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{0^{+}}^{\infty}R(\lambda)\frac{2\sigma\lambda+1}{\lambda^{2}}\exp(-2\sigma\lambda)d\lambda

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\sigma^{1-d/2}.

(49)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{\star})]\lesssim\sqrt{\sigma}+\sqrt{\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}}\sigma^{(2-d)/4}.

(50)

To minimize the above upper bound, we consider the function
p(\sigma)=\sqrt{\sigma}+\sqrt{\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}}\sigma^{(2-d)/4}
for
\sigma\in(0,\infty)
that achieves its minimum at

\displaystyle\sigma=\sigma_{\text{opt}}=\Big((1-d/2)^{2}\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{2/d},

(51)

and thus we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[W_{1}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}_{\star})]\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{1/d},

(52)

which completes the proof.
∎

Appendix D
Proof of Theorem
3

See
3

Proof.

We first need to prove the following upper bound on the Sobolev IPMs.

Lemma 2
.

For Borel measures
\mu,\nu
with
\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\mu=\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\mu=1
, such that
\frac{d\mu}{dx},\frac{d\nu}{dx}\in L^{2}(\mathcal{M})
and assuming
\vol(\mathcal{M})=1
, one has

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}^{2}(\mu,\nu)=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-\nu_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}},

(54)

for any
\alpha\geq 0
, where
\mu_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[\phi_{\ell}(x)]
and
\nu_{\ell}=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\nu}[\phi_{\ell}(x)]
for any
\ell
.

Proof.

Any function
f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})
can be written as
f=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}f_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
with
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})}^{2}=f_{0}^{2}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{\alpha}f_{\ell}^{2}<\infty
. From the definition,

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\mu-\int_{\mathcal{M}}fd\nu\Big\}

(55)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\Big\{\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\frac{d\mu}{dx}dx-\int_{\mathcal{M}}f\frac{d\nu}{dx}dx\Big\}

(56)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\Big\langle f,\frac{d\mu}{dx}-\frac{d\nu}{dx}\Big\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathcal{M})}

(57)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}f_{\ell}(\mu_{\ell}-\nu_{\ell})

(58)

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{\alpha/2}f_{\ell}\times\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-\nu_{\ell})}{\lambda_{\ell}^{\alpha/2}}

(59)

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{=}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\\
\|f\|_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})\leq 1}\end{subarray}}\sqrt{\underbrace{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{\ell}^{\alpha}f_{\ell}^{2}}_{\leq 1}}\times\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-\nu_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda_{\ell}^{\alpha}}}

(60)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-\nu_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda_{\ell}^{\alpha}}},

(61)

where (a) is due to the fact that
\mu_{0}=\nu_{0}=1
, and (b) follows from the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Given i.i.d. samples from a probability measure
\mu
with
\|\frac{d\mu}{dx}\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}<\infty
, we propose the following estimator. First, fix a parameter
T
(to be set later) and let
\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell;T)=\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)\mathbbm{1}\{\ell<T\}
for any
\ell=0,1,\ldots
. Then, define the sequence

\displaystyle\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell;T)\phi_{\ell}(X_{i}),

(62)

for each
\ell
and let
\frac{d\tilde{\mu}}{dx}:=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}\phi_{\ell}
. The measure
\tilde{\mu}
is thus well-defined, and
\int_{\mathcal{M}}d\tilde{\mu}=1
, but it is not necessarily a probability measure since it can be a signed measure. However, due to the truncation of the sum at
T
, we have
\frac{d\tilde{\mu}}{dx}\in\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})
. Define a new probability measure
\mu^{\star}
as

\displaystyle\mu^{\star}=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{M})}D_{\alpha}(\nu,\tilde{\mu}).

(63)

This shows that by the triangle inequality

\displaystyle D_{\alpha}(\mu,\mu^{\star})\leq D_{\alpha}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})+D_{\alpha}(\tilde{\mu},\mu^{\star})\leq 2D_{\alpha}(\mu,\tilde{\mu}),

(64)

where in above, we used the definition of the probability measure
\mu^{\star}
.

We claim that the estimated probability measure achieves the convergence rate claimed in the theorem. To this end, we need to prove upper bounds on
\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})]
. According to Lemma
2
, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,\mu^{\star})]

\displaystyle\leq 2\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})]

(65)

\displaystyle\leq 2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}[D^{2}_{\alpha}(\mu,\tilde{\mu})]}

(66)

\displaystyle=2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]}.

(67)

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]+\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell\geq T}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]

(68)

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]}_{\text{(I)}}+\underbrace{\sum_{\ell\geq T}\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}}_{\text{(II)}},

(69)

since
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}=0
for
\ell>T
.
∎

To upper bound the second term, we write

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell\geq T}\frac{\mu_{\ell}^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell>T}\mu_{\ell}^{2}\lambda^{s}_{\ell}\lambda^{-(s+\alpha)}_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{T}^{-(s+\alpha)}\sum_{\ell\geq T}\mu_{\ell}^{2}\lambda^{s}_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{T}^{-(s+\alpha)}\Big\|\frac{d{\mu}}{dx}\Big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}.

(70)

To bound the first term, we need to use the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

One has

\displaystyle R(\lambda):=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda}(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}\Big]\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\Big[N_{x}(\lambda;G)\Big],

(71)

where
N_{x}(\lambda;G)
is defined in Theorem
7
.

Proof.

Note that the coefficients
\tilde{\mu}_{\ell}
are empirical estimates of
\mu_{\ell}
given
n
i.i.d. samples; thus

\displaystyle R(\lambda_{T})=\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{T}}(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\Big\{\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{T}}\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)\Big(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[\phi^{2}_{\ell}(x)]-(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}[\phi_{\ell}(x)])^{2}\Big)\Big\}

(72)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\Big[\sum_{\ell:\lambda_{\ell}\leq\lambda_{T}}\mathbbm{1}_{G}(\ell)\phi^{2}_{\ell}(x)\Big]

(73)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mu}\Big[N_{x}(\lambda_{T};G)\Big].

(74)

∎

We use Lemma
3
to complete the proof. First, note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]\leq\int_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}\frac{dR(\lambda)}{\lambda^{\alpha}},

(75)

where
R(\lambda)
is defined in Lemma
3
and
dR(\lambda)
denotes the Stieltjes measure corresponding to
R(\lambda)
. By integration by parts, one has

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]\leq\int_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}\frac{dR(\lambda)}{\lambda^{\alpha}}=\frac{R(\lambda)}{\lambda^{\alpha}}\Big|_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}+\alpha\int_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}\frac{R(\lambda)}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}}d\lambda.

(76)

Now assume
\alpha<d/2
. By Theorem
7
and Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R(\lambda_{T})}{\lambda_{T}^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}\frac{R(\lambda)}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}}d\lambda

(77)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{n}\Big\{\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\lambda_{T}^{d/2-\alpha}+O(\lambda_{T}^{(d-1)/2-\alpha})

(78)

\displaystyle\quad+\alpha\int_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}\Big\{\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\lambda_{T}^{d/2-\alpha-1}+O(\lambda_{T}^{(d-1)/2-\alpha-1})\Big\}d\lambda\Big\}

(79)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\Big(1+\frac{\alpha}{d/2-\alpha}\Big)\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\lambda_{T}^{d/2-\alpha},

(80)

where in
\lesssim
the constant is absolute. Plugging in this estimation into Equation (
69
) results in

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\frac{d/2}{d/2-\alpha}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\lambda_{T}^{d/2-\alpha}+\lambda_{T}^{-(s+\alpha)}\Big\|\frac{d{\mu}}{dx}\Big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}.

(81)

We can choose the parameter
\lambda_{T}\in(0,\infty)
to minimize the above upper bound.

Note that the function
p(\lambda)=c_{a}\lambda^{-a}+c_{b}\lambda^{b}
with
a,b,c_{a},c_{b}>0
is minimized for
\lambda\in(0,\infty)
when

\displaystyle\lambda=\lambda_{\text{opt}}:=\Big(\frac{ac_{a}}{bc_{b}}\Big)^{1/(a+b)}.

(82)

Taking
a=s+\alpha
,
b=d/2-\alpha
, and

\displaystyle c_{a}=\Big\|\frac{d{\mu}}{dx}\Big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})},\quad c_{b}=\frac{1}{n}\frac{d/2}{d/2-\alpha}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G),

(83)

suggests to take

\displaystyle\lambda_{T}=\Big\{\Big((s+\alpha)\|d{\mu}/dx\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}\Big)\Big/\Big(\frac{d}{2n}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\Big)\Big\}^{1/(s+d/2)}.

(84)

Therefore, we have the following upper bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]

(85)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{n}\frac{d/2}{d/2-\alpha}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\Big\{\Big((s+\alpha)\|d{\mu}/dx\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}\Big)\Big/\Big(\frac{d}{2n}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\Big)\Big\}^{(d/2-\alpha)/(s+d/2)}

(86)

\displaystyle+\Big\|\frac{d{\mu}}{dx}\Big\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}\Big\{\Big((s+\alpha)\|d{\mu}/dx\|^{2}_{\mathcal{H}^{\alpha}(\mathcal{M})}\Big)\Big/\Big(\frac{d}{2n}\frac{\omega_{d}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)\Big)\Big\}^{-(s+\alpha)/(s+d/2)},

(87)

which shows that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{s+\alpha}{s+d/2}}\penalty\ \Big\|\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{d-2\alpha}{s+d/2}}.

(88)

This completes the proof for
\alpha<d/2
, since

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[D_{\alpha}(\mu,\mu^{\star})]\leq 2\sqrt{\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]}\lesssim\Big(\frac{\vol(\mathcal{M}/G)}{n}\Big)^{\frac{s+\alpha}{2s+d}}\penalty\ \Big\|\frac{d\mu}{dx}\Big\|_{\mathcal{H}^{s}(\mathcal{M})}^{\frac{d-2\alpha}{2s+d}}.

(89)

Now, we study the case where
\alpha=d/2
. By Theorem
7
and Lemma
3
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big[\sum_{\ell=1}^{T-1}\frac{(\mu_{\ell}-{\tilde{\mu}}_{\ell})^{2}}{\lambda^{\alpha}_{\ell}}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{R(\lambda_{T})}{\lambda_{T}^{\alpha}}+\alpha\int_{0^{+}}^{\lambda_{T}}\frac{R(\lambda)}{\lambda^{\alpha+1}}d\lambda

(90)
