Chiral central charge from a single bulk wave function
Isaac H. Kim, Bowen Shi, Kohtaro Kato, Victor V. Albert

Abstract
A ( 2 + 1 ) (2+1) -dimensional gapped quantum many-body system can have a topologically protected energy current at its edge. The magnitude of this current is determined entirely by the temperature and the chiral central charge, a quantity associated with the effective field theory of the edge. We derive a formula for the chiral central charge that, akin to the topological entanglement entropy, is completely determined by the many-body ground state wave function in the bulk. According to our formula, nonzero chiral central charge gives rise to a topological obstruction that prevents the ground state wave function from being real-valued in any local product basis.



Topological order
Wen 1990
;
Kitaev 2003
is an active topic of investigation today, simultaneously admitting connections to deep mathematics and the promise of intrinsically protected quantum devices. Well-known properties of topologically ordered systems in
(2+1)
dimensions are anyonic excitations in the bulk
Leinaas and Myrheim 1977
;
Wilczek 1982
;
Arovas et al. 1984
and robust gapless modes on the edge of the system
Thouless et al. 1982
;
Wen 1991
;
Kane and Fisher 1997
, both observed in fractional quantum Hall systems experimentally
Nakamura et al. 2020
;
Klitzing et al. 1980
;
Tsui et al. 1982
.

A remarkable observation is that the features characterizing the low-energy excitations of a system can be extracted from its many-body ground-state wave function. Early examples of this observation include topological entanglement entropy
Kitaev and Preskill 2006
;
Levin and Wen 2006
and the entanglement spectrum
Li and Haldane 2008
. Later work has gone on to extract other related properties,
e.g.,
topological
S
- and
T
-matrices of the anyonic excitations
Zhang et al. 2012
;
Cincio and Vidal 2013
.

These properties, however, do not exhaustively constitute the data that defines the phase of the underlying system. A missing piece of information is the
chiral central charge
(CCC), denoted as
c_{-}
here. A nonzero
c_{-}
implies the presence of gapless edge excitations, so a natural place in which the CCC appears is the physical edge of the system. At a temperature
T
that is low compared to the bulk excitation gap, the energy current
I
along the edge is
Kane and Fisher 1997
;
Senthil et al. 1999
;
Read and Green 2000
;
Cappelli et al. 2002
;
Kitaev 2006

I=\frac{\pi}{12}c_{-}T^{2}.

(1)

A prominent physical system with nonzero CCC is the two-dimensional electron gas in a magnetic field
Klitzing et al. 1980
;
Thouless et al. 1982
;
Tsui et al. 1982
. Besides admitting the well-known quantized electrical response protected by
U(1)
charge conservation symmetry, this system admits a quantized
thermal
response
Kane and Fisher 1997
as well, which manifests as a unidirectional edge energy current. This current corresponds to a nonzero
c_{-}
and exists independently of the
U(1)
symmetry
Read and Green 2000
;
Senthil et al. 1999
;
Cappelli et al. 2002
. More generally, the chiral central charge is an integer when the edge admits a chiral Luttinger liquid description
Wen 1991
, but can be a rational number in, for instance,
p+ip
superconductors and systems with non-Abelian anyons
Read and Green 2000
;
Moore and Read 1991
.

While the CCC describes a physical property of the edge, it is also related to certain properties of the bulk. For instance, the energy current at the edge can be related to a
2
-current in the bulk, which can be computed from a microscopic Hamiltonian
Kitaev 2006
;
Kapustin and Spodyneiko 2020
. In effective field theory approaches, CCC appears in the gravitational Chern-Simons term of the bulk action, which is responsible for the framing anomaly of the underlying system
Gromov et al. 2015
. This lets us relate CCC to the topological Berry phase under adiabatic variation of the metric
Bradlyn and Read 2015a
and the Hall viscosity on a sphere
Abanov and Gromov 2014
;
Klevtsov and Wiegmann 2015
;
Bradlyn and Read 2015a
;
Golan et al. 2019
. Moreover, given a set of ground state wave functions on a torus, CCC can be computed (up to a fixed integer) using topological
S
- and
T
-matrices
Zhang et al. 2012
or momentum polarization
Tu et al. 2013
. Alternatively, CCC can be inferred from the entanglement spectrum of the bulk reduced density matrix on a disk
Li and Haldane 2008
. However, a succinct closed-form formula that relates CCC to the ground state entanglement of a single wave function — akin to the topological entanglement entropy
Kitaev and Preskill 2006
;
Levin and Wen 2006
— has been missing.

In this Letter, we introduce a new formula that reveals a connection between CCC and the entanglement structure of the bulk. Our formula is based on the modular commutator — a new quantity expressed in terms of reduced density matrices of a many-body wave function. We argue that the CCC can be expressed in terms of the modular commutator for ground states of gapped Hamiltonians,
with or without symmetries. We numerically confirm our formula up to a small error attributable to finite-size effects in our companion paper
Kim et al. 2021
.

Summary of results—
For a general tripartite state
\rho_{ABC}
on a finite dimensional Hilbert space, the
modular commutator
is

J(A,B,C)_{\rho}:=i\text{Tr}\left(\rho_{ABC}[K_{AB},K_{BC}]\right),

(2)

where
K_{A}=-\ln\rho_{A}
is the modular Hamiltonian
Haag 2012
;
Casini 2008
associated with the reduced density matrix
\rho_{A}
on subsystem
A
. We can readily see that
J
is real (because
[K_{AB},K_{BC}]
is an antihermitian operator) and odd under complex conjugation (with respect to any product-state basis over the local degrees of freedom). The latter operation corresponds to time reversal in our physical context, meaning that it can flip the direction of a system’s edge current. Thus, the fact that
J
is odd under time reversal is a necessary property for it to encode information about the CCC.

Plugging in a many-body ground state
\sigma=|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|
satisfying the area law with a constant subcorrection term
Kitaev and Preskill 2006
;
Levin and Wen 2006
, we relate the modular commutator (
2
) to the edge energy current (
1
), obtaining our main result

J(A,B,C)_{\sigma}=\frac{\pi}{3}c_{-}

(3)

for subsystems
A
,
B
, and

depicted in Fig.

Markov-chain states—
An important fact about the modular commutator is that it vanishes if the underlying state is a quantum Markov chain
Petz 2003
. Specifically, consider a tripartite state
\rho_{XYZ}
. This state is a quantum Markov chain if its conditional mutual information — defined as
I(X:Y|Z)_{\rho}:=S(\rho_{XY})+S(\rho_{YZ})-S(\rho_{Y})-S(\rho_{XYZ})
, where
S(\rho):=-\text{Tr}(\rho\ln\rho)
is the von Neumann entropy of
\rho
— is zero. It turns out that

I(X:Z|Y)_{\rho}=0\implies J(X,Y,Z)_{\rho}=0~,

(4)

which can be proved via the important relation
Petz 2003
1

K_{XYZ}\,\rho_{XYZ}=(K_{XY}+K_{YZ}-K_{Y})\rho_{XYZ}

(5)

Applying this relation, the modular commutator becomes
J(X,Y,Z)=i\text{Tr}(\rho_{XYZ}[K_{XY},K_{XYZ}+K_{Y}])

Modular current—
Because
K_{\mathfrak{D}}
is local, we can define its “energy current,” which we refer to as the modular current.
We rewrite the modular Hamiltonian (
7
) in terms of
\widetilde{K}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{v}
, which collects all terms whose support contains site
v
, multiplied by the appropriate fraction so as to satisfy
K_{\mathfrak{D}}=\sum_{v\in\mathfrak{D}}\widetilde{K}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{v}
:

\widetilde{K}_{v}^{\mathfrak{D}}=\begin{cases}{\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{3}}\sum_{f:v\in f}K_{f}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{e:v\in e}K_{e}+K_{v}},&v\in\mathfrak{D}_{\text{int}}\\
{\displaystyle{\textstyle\frac{1}{3}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}f:v\in f,\\
f\in F(\mathfrak{D})\end{subarray}}K_{f}-{\textstyle\frac{1}{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}e:v\in e,\\
e\in E(\mathfrak{D})\setminus E(\mathfrak{D}_{\partial})\end{subarray}}K_{e}},&v\in\mathfrak{D}_{\partial}\end{cases}~.

(9)

The modular current from site
u
to
v
is then simply

f^{\mathfrak{D}}_{uv}:=i\langle[\widetilde{K}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{u},\widetilde{K}^{\mathfrak{D}}_{v}]\rangle~,

(10)

quantifying the non-commutativity of the modular Hamiltonian terms.

The modular current has properties analogous to that of the energy current of some local Hamiltonian at finite temperature
Kitaev 2006
. For instance,
f^{\mathfrak{D}}_{uv}
vanishes for any pair of points
u,v\in{\mathfrak{D}}
which are sufficiently far apart. Moreover, the current is conserved:

\sum_{v\in\mathfrak{D}}f^{\mathfrak{D}}_{uv}=0.

(11)

Lastly, one can explicitly show that the bulk modular current vanishes:
f^{\mathfrak{D}}_{uv}=0
for
u,v\in\mathfrak{D}_{\text{int}}
; see the Supplemental Material for details.

Since the modular current vanishes in the bulk of the disk, nontrivial current flows only along the edge. We define the
edge modular current

I_{\sigma}
to be a sum of
f_{uv}^{\mathfrak{D}}
over
u\in{L}
and
v\in{R}
, where
{L},{R}\subset\mathfrak{D}
lie on opposite sides of a cut perpendicular to the boundary. This current is insensitive to the choice of regions
{L},{R}
, as long as they are sufficiently large. Since there is no current deep enough in the bulk, nonzero contributions only come from sites that are at most two sites away from the edge. Moreover, because the current flows between nearby sites and is conserved, it suffices to consider only a few sites along the edge. For the cut depicted in Fig.
4

\{a,b,c\}\subset{L}
and
\{x,y,z,w\}\subset{R}

I_{\sigma}=\sum_{u\in{L}}\sum_{v\in{R}}f_{uv}^{\mathfrak{D}}=\frac{1}{4}J~.

(12)

Chiral central charge—
We now relate
I_{\sigma}
to
c_{-}
by viewing
K_{\mathfrak{D}}
as a physical Hamiltonian describing the same phase as that of the state
\sigma
.
First,
\sigma_{\mathfrak{D}}=e^{-K_{\mathfrak{D}}}
can be viewed as a thermal state of the local Hamiltonian
K_{\mathfrak{D}}
with the temperature
T=1
. Second,
\sigma_{\mathfrak{D}}
obeys an entanglement area law in
\mathfrak{D}
, and is indistinguishable from the ground state
\sigma
over the same region, an indication of
low
temperature for a system with a bulk energy gap. Third, the temperature is
high enough
so that the edge correlation length is small compared to the length of the disk. This is the right temperature range for which we can apply Eq. (
1
)
Kitaev 2006
.

Now, we can make a nontrivial but a reasonable physical assumption; if two local Hamiltonians with a bulk energy gap have the same bulk reduced density matrix, then their energy currents at the boundary at a temperature low compared to the bulk gap both obey Eq. (
1
), independent of other microscopic details. This expression is determined completely by the temperature and the CCC of the edge theory
Kitaev 2006
and cannot change unless the bulk undergoes a quantum phase transition. As is observed in the previous paragraph, the “temperature”
T=1
lies in the right temperature range. Using Eq. (
1
) and identifying the energy current to the edge modular current (
12
), we arrive at our main result:

J=\frac{\pi}{3}c_{-}.

(13)

Discussion—
In this Letter, we propose a new formula for the chiral central charge (CCC). The formula is based on the modular commutator
J
, a new quantity that is expressed in terms of reduced density matrices and that is odd under time reversal (i.e., complex conjugation with respect to a local basis). Compared to other similar works in this direction
Zhang et al. 2012
;
Tu et al. 2013
;
Bradlyn and Read 2015b
, the main advantage of our formula is that it can be directly computed from a single ground state wave function. Together with the prior work that established a connection between ground state entanglement and the anyonic data
Kitaev and Preskill 2006
;
Levin and Wen 2006
;
Li and Haldane 2008
;
Zhang et al. 2012
;
Shi et al. 2020
;
Shi 2020
, our result strengthens the idea that
all
the universal properties of
(2+1)
-dimensional gapped quantum phases may be encoded in a single ground state. Our formula is thus a useful addition to the existing toolkit for diagnosing topological properties of quantum many-body systems.

Aside from drawing a deep connection between ground state entanglement and the CCC, our result (
13
) also has an intriguing implication for the
intrinsic sign problem

Hastings 2016
. Specifically, if our formula for the CCC is correct for all gapped systems in (2+1) dimensions, then a system with nonzero CCC cannot have a parent Hamiltonian which is sign-problem free.
This is because a sign-problem free Hamiltonian necessarily admits a ground state with nonnegative coefficients in a local product basis, for which the CCC would be zero because
J
is odd under complex conjugation. Thus, a rigorous proof of our formula will lead to a microscopic proof that such systems have an intrinsic sign problem; no local Hamiltonian free of the sign problem can have ground states with nonzero
c_{-}
. This corroborates recent arguments pointing to a similar conclusion
Ringel and Kovrizhin 2017
;
Golan et al. 2020
;
Golan 2021
.

A curious fact is that the modular commutator did not possess any ultraviolet-divergent contributions in our calculation. Whether this is a general property of modular commutator or something specific to gapped phases in
(2+1)
dimensions is unclear. Perhaps field-theoretic calculations of our result can shed light on this problem.

For future work, it will be interesting to find analogues of
J
that are suitable for resolving symmetry-protected topological invariants that can be obtained from the ground state wave function,
e.g.,

Refs.

;
Turner et al. 2011
;
Haegeman et al. 2012
;
Marvian 2017
;
Shiozaki et al. 2017
;
Shapourian et al. 2017
;
Tasaki 2018
;
Shiozaki et al. 2018
;

;

;

Appendix A
Fermionic Markov chain

For fermions, the global Hilbert space does not have a tensor product structure. Thus, one must define a proper notion of partial trace more precisely. We provide a self-contained overview of this subject, following the treatment of Ref.
Araki and Moriya 2003
;
Nachtergaele et al. 2017
. We focus on fermionic systems on a finite lattice, which removes the subtleties that may appear in infinite systems.

The fermionic algebra is defined in terms of the canonical anticommutation relation:

\{a_{j}^{\dagger},a_{k}\}=\delta_{j,k},\quad\{a_{j},a_{k}\}=\{a_{j}^{\dagger},a^{\dagger}_{k}\}=0,

(14)

where
a_{j}^{\dagger}
and
a_{j}
are the fermionic creation and annihilation operators, respectively, and
\Lambda\ni j
is the set of fermion modes. The algebra generated by these operators is denoted as
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
. More generally, we denote a subalgebra generated by
\{a_{j}^{\dagger},a_{j}:j\in A\subset\Lambda\}
as
\mathcal{A}_{A}
.

To define a proper notion of partial trace, it will be helpful to consider a slight modification of the partial trace for qubits that admits a generalization in a straightforward way. Let
\Lambda^{\prime}
be a set of qubits. A partial trace over a subset
A^{\prime}\subset\Lambda^{\prime}
can be formally defined as follows:

\displaystyle\rho_{\Lambda^{\prime}}

\displaystyle\to\frac{1}{4^{|A^{\prime}|}}\sum_{P_{A^{\prime}}}P_{A^{\prime}}\rho P_{A^{\prime}}^{\dagger}

(15)

\displaystyle=\frac{I_{A^{\prime}}}{2^{|A^{\prime}|}}\otimes\rho_{\bar{A^{\prime}}}

where the summation is taken over the
4^{|A^{\prime}|}
Pauli strings acting on
A^{\prime}
(i.e., a product of single site Pauli operators and the identity operator
\{I_{j},X_{j},Y_{j},Z_{j}\}
with
j\in A^{\prime}
) and
I_{A^{\prime}}
is the identity matrix acting on
A^{\prime}
.
\bar{A^{\prime}}=\Lambda^{\prime}\setminus A^{\prime}
is the complement of
A^{\prime}
.

There is a simple analog of Eq. (
15
) for fermions, as explained in Ref.
Nachtergaele et al. 2017
. Density matrices of fermionic systems are operators
\rho_{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{+}
, such that
\rho_{\Lambda}
is positive. Here
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{+}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is the subalgebra formed by terms written as linear combination of even powers of the creation and annihilation operators.

Consider the following unitary operators:

\displaystyle u_{j}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=I

(16)

\displaystyle u_{j}^{(1)}

\displaystyle=a_{j}^{\dagger}+a_{j}

\displaystyle u_{j}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=a_{j}^{\dagger}-a_{j}

\displaystyle u_{j}^{(3)}

\displaystyle=I-2a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}.

The fermionic analog of Eq. (
15
) is

\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}(\rho_{\Lambda})\equiv\frac{1}{4^{|A|}}\sum_{\alpha\in\text{Ind}_{A}}u(\alpha)^{\dagger}\rho_{\Lambda}u(\alpha),

(17)

where
\text{Ind}_{A}=\{0,1,2,3\}^{|A|}
and
u(\alpha)=u_{j_{1}}^{(\alpha_{1})}\ldots u_{j_{n}}^{(\alpha_{n})}
for
A=\{j_{1},\ldots,j_{n}\}
. Let us note that the ordering of
u_{j_{n}}^{(\alpha_{n})}
is immaterial because they either commute or anticommute with each other.

It is straightforward to verify that
\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}
satisfies the following intuitive properties of partial trace:

1.

\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}(O)=O
for
O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda\setminus A}^{+}
.

2.

\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}\circ\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{B}=\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{B}\circ\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}=\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{AB}
, for
A\cap B=\emptyset
.

3.

\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}(O)\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda\setminus A}^{+}
for for
O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{+}
.

Thus, it is sensible to define the following notion of reduced density matrix for fermions:

\widetilde{\rho}_{A}=\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{\bar{A}}(\rho).

(18)

Armed with this knowledge, one can prove a necessary and sufficient condition for saturation of strong subadditivity for fermions.

A.0.1
Relating different reduced density matrices

Now we relate
\widetilde{\rho}_{A}
defined in Eq. (
18
) to a more familiar notion of reduced density matrix. This is done by associating a linear functional (trace) which maps operators in the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
(or
\mathcal{A}_{A}
) to complex numbers.

This is most convenient if we introduce the Majorana operators
\{c_{j},d_{j}\}_{j\in\Lambda}
such that
c_{j}=u_{j}^{(1)}
and
d_{j}=iu_{j}^{(2)}
, and notice that the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is generated
\{c_{j},d_{j}\}_{j\in\Lambda}
. In other words, every element in
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is a linear combination of monomials of the form

\prod_{j\in\Lambda}(c_{j}^{s_{j}}d_{j}^{t_{j}})
,
where
s_{j},t_{j}\in\{0,1\}
.

We formally define the trace operation (for operators in
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
) according to

{\text{Tr}}\,\prod_{j\in\Lambda}(c_{j}^{s_{j}}d_{j}^{t_{j}})\equiv 2^{|\Lambda|}\prod_{j\in\Lambda}(\delta_{s_{j},0}\cdot\delta_{t_{j},0}).

(19)

(Because the trace is a linear operation, the properties on the monomials above completely determine the trace operation.)

Now we look back at the operator
\widetilde{\rho}_{A}
defined in Eq. (
18
). Because
\widetilde{\rho}_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{A}^{+}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
, we have implicitly chosen the trace defined on
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
, according to Eq. (
19
). However, since
\mathcal{A}_{A}^{+}\subset\mathcal{A}_{A}
, we can choose a different normalization of the trace, defined according to:

{\text{Tr}}^{(A)}\,\prod_{j\in A}(c_{j}^{s_{j}}d_{j}^{t_{j}})\equiv 2^{|A|}\prod_{j\in A}(\delta_{s_{j},0}\cdot\delta_{t_{j},0}).

(20)

To distinguish the two traces, we introduced an upper index
(A)
.
Intuitively, this different normalization is possible because the Hilbert space associated with
{\Lambda}
decomposes into
2^{|\Lambda|-|A|}
identical copies of irreducible representation of the algebra
\mathcal{A}_{A}
.

Let
\rho_{A}\in\mathcal{A}^{+}_{A}
be a density matrix in terms of the trace defined in Eq. (
20
), obtained by rescaling
\widetilde{\rho}_{A}
, then the normalization
{\text{Tr}}^{(A)}\rho_{A}=1
(instead of
{\text{Tr}}\rho_{A}=1
) determines that

\widetilde{\rho}_{A}=\rho_{A}/2^{|\Lambda\setminus A|}.

(21)

In particular, this implies that

\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{A}=\ln\rho_{A}-I(|\Lambda|-|A|)\ln 2.

(22)

Thus,

\displaystyle\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}+\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{BC}-\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{B}-\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{ABC}

(23)

\displaystyle=\ln\rho_{AB}+\ln\rho_{BC}-\ln\rho_{B}-\ln\rho_{ABC},

and

{\text{Tr}}(\widetilde{\rho}_{A}\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{A})={\text{Tr}}^{(A)}({\rho}_{A}\ln{\rho}_{A})-(|\Lambda|-|A|)\ln 2.

(24)

The latter condition implies that
I(A:C|B)_{\rho}=I(A:C|B)_{\widetilde{\rho}}
.

A.0.2
Explicit basis

The discussion above took an algebraic approach, and the analysis did not need to specify any basis of the Hilbert space. For practical application it is often useful to think of the standard fermionic occupation number basis, with basis vectors
\{|n_{1},\cdots,n_{\Lambda}\rangle\}
, defined according to

a_{1}^{\dagger n_{1}}\cdots a_{|\Lambda|}^{\dagger n_{|\Lambda|}}|0,\cdots,0\rangle=|n_{1},\cdots,n_{|\Lambda|}\rangle,

(25)

where
n_{j}\in\{0,1\}
. Note that the ordering in the product of creation operators matters, and here we have chosen an ordering that increases the site labels.

Now the trace operation looks more standard, in the sense that the previous defining property of “
Tr
” in Eq. (
19
) now becomes the consequence of the formula

{\text{Tr}}(O)=\sum_{I}\langle I|O|I\rangle,\quad\forall O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}.

(26)

Here
\{|I\rangle\}
is a basis set written in terms of the fermionic occupation numbers,
i.e.,

|I\rangle=|n_{1}^{(I)},\ldots,n_{|\Lambda|}^{(I)}\rangle
where
n_{k}^{(I)}\in\{0,1\}
. (A similar analysis applies to
{\text{Tr}}^{(A)}
.)

As an explicit example, let
\Lambda=AB
, where
A
and
B
each contains one site. For this system, the total Hilbert space is
4
dimensional. The algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is a 16 dimensional vector space and
\mathcal{A}_{A}
is 4 dimensional. Consider density matrix
\rho_{AB}=|0,0\rangle\langle 0,0|
in the fermionic occupation number basis. Then one can easily verify:

\displaystyle\rho_{AB}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-ic_{1}d_{1}-ic_{2}d_{2}-c_{1}d_{1}c_{2}d_{2}}{4}

(27)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\rho}_{A}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-ic_{1}d_{1}}{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{|0,0\rangle\langle 0,0|+|0,1\rangle\langle 0,1|}{2}

and

\rho_{A}=\frac{1-ic_{1}d_{1}}{2}=|0\rangle\langle 0|.

(28)

A.0.3
Saturation of strong subadditivity

Lieb and Ruskai proved the strong subadditivity of entropy
Lieb and Ruskai 1973
:

I(A:C|B)_{\rho}\geq 0.

(29)

Moreover, Petz showed that, provided that
\rho_{ABC}
is positive definite, the following equation holds
Petz 2003
:

I(A:C|B)_{\rho}=0\quad\Rightarrow\quad K_{ABC}=K_{AB}+K_{BC}-K_{B}.

(30)

The main purpose of this section is to prove the statements analogous to Eq. (
29
) and (
30
) for fermions.

The results in this section is well-known but the following proof is new to the best of our knowledge. The key ingredient is Lieb’s three-operator generalization of the Golden-Thompson inequality
Lieb 1973
:

\text{Tr}(e^{\ln X-\ln Y+\ln Z})\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}(X(Y+tI)^{-1}Z(Y+tI)^{-1})dt,

(31)

where
I
here is the identity and
X,Y,Z
are positive definite operators.

To complete the proof, we first note a useful condition written in terms of
relative entropy between a pair of density matrices
D(\rho\|\lambda)=\text{Tr}(\rho(\ln\rho-\ln\lambda))
:

I(A:C|B)_{\rho}=D(\widetilde{\rho}_{ABC}\|\widetilde{\sigma}_{ABC})-\ln(\text{Tr}(\eta_{ABC})),

(32)

where
\widetilde{\sigma}_{ABC}=\eta_{ABC}/\text{Tr}(\eta_{ABC})
, is the normalized version of
\eta_{ABC}\equiv\exp(\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}+\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{BC}-\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{B})
. By Eq. (
31
),

\displaystyle\text{Tr}\left(\exp(\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}-\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{BC})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI}\widetilde{\rho}_{BC}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}\circ\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{C}\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI}\widetilde{\rho}_{BC}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI}\right)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI}\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{C}(\widetilde{\rho}_{BC})\frac{1}{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI}\right)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}\frac{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}}{(\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI)^{2}}\right)\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}\left(\widetilde{\text{Tr}}_{A}\left(\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}\right)\frac{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}}{(\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI)^{2}}\right)dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\text{Tr}\frac{\widetilde{\rho}_{B}^{2}}{(\widetilde{\rho}_{B}+tI)^{2}}dt

\displaystyle=1.

(In this calculation we have used the fact that
u(\alpha)
commutes with elements in
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{+}
.)

Therefore, the second term on the right hand side of Eq. (
32
) is nonnegative. The first term is nonnegative by the property of the relative entropy. Therefore, if the left hand side of Eq. (
32
) is zero, both terms on the right hand side must be zero. The first term is zero if and only if the two arguments are equal. Therefore, we conclude that

\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{ABC}=\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{AB}+\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{BC}-\ln\widetilde{\rho}_{B}.

(34)

Using Eq. (
23
), we conclude that

\ln\rho_{ABC}=\ln\rho_{AB}+\ln\rho_{BC}-\ln\rho_{B}.

(35)

In summary, the fermionic version of Eq. (
30
) and the strong subadditivity hold.
