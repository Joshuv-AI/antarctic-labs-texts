Quantum reversal: a general theory of coherent quantum absorbers
Mankei Tsang

Abstract
The fascinating concept of coherent quantum absorber—which can absorb any photon emitted by another system while maintaining entanglement with that system—has found diverse implications in open quantum system theory and quantum metrology. This work generalizes the concept by proposing the so-called reversal conditions for the two systems, in which a “reverser” coherently reverses any effect of the other system on a field. The reversal conditions are rigorously boiled down to concise formulas involving the Petz recovery map and Kraus operators, thereby generalizing as well as streamlining the existing treatments of coherent absorbers.

Introduction

A coherent quantum absorber, as conceived by Stannigel
et
al.

[
1
]
, is a system that absorbs any light emitted
by another system while maintaining entanglement with that system. The
idea has since found surprising implications, such as a technique for
finding the steady states of open quantum systems

[

,
2
,
3
]
and a method for constructing
efficient continuous measurements for quantum parameter estimation

[
4
,
5
]
. Remarkably, Yang, Huelga, and Plenio

[

,
8
,

—which has seen

systems in recent years

—can be
regarded as a special case of coherent absorbers. This relation
extends the potential impact of the absorber concept to the areas of
magnetometry, optomechanics, and gravitational-wave detectors.

While the absorber concept is fascinating and promising, many special
assumptions and a bewildering array of theoretical tools have been
invoked to study it, such as quantum Markov semigroups

[
13
]
, cascaded quantum networks
[
1
]
,
matrix-product states
[

]
, and quantum detailed balance

[

,

. The goal of this work is to
tease out the essential ideas and make the absorber notion more
rigorous as well as generalizable to other scenarios, far beyond the
narrow setting of photon emission and absorption considered in prior
works.

This generality calls for a different name for the generalized
absorber conditions proposed here—I call them the reversal
conditions. Whereas previous works

[
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
]
assume a

[

interacting with the second. Although Ref.
[
4
]
offers a
similar generalization, an appeal of the reversal conditions here is
that they are boiled down to concise but equivalent formulas in
terms of judiciously chosen concepts in open quantum system theory.
Out go the quantum Markov semigroups, the matrix-product states, and
many other extraneous concepts; in come antiunitary operators

[
15
,
3
]
, the Petz recovery map

[
16
,
17
,
18
]
, and Kraus operators
[
19
]
as the
more fundamental ingredients of the reversal conditions.

,

[

2
Reversal condition

2.1
Model

Consider the model depicted by Fig.
1
. Two
systems, denoted as A and B, are initially in a pure state

\ket{\psi}_{AB}
in Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. A
temporal mode of a traveling field with initial state
\ket{\chi}_{E}

in Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{E}
interacts with system A according to a
unitary operator
U
on
\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
. The field mode
then evolves according to an intermediate unitary operator
U_{E}
on

\mathcal{H}_{E}
, before interacting with system B according to a unitary
operator
V
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
. Note that the system on

\mathcal{H}_{E}
is called a temporal field mode only to provide a more
physical picture of the model; it can be an arbitrary system in
reality. All Hilbert spaces are assumed to be finite-dimensional in
the proofs for simplicity.

Figure 1:
A quantum circuit illustrating the
interactions between system A, system B, and the field mode.

Let
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
be the set of operators on Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
.
An operator on operators is called a map in this work, also called a
superoperator in the literature. Given
\ket{\chi}_{E}
and
U
, a
channel for system A can be modeled by a completely positive,
trace-preserving (CPTP) map
\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})

given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\rho_{A}

\displaystyle\equiv\tr_{E}\left[U(\rho_{A}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}\bra{\chi})U^{\dagger}\right],

(2.1)

where
\rho_{A}
is an arbitrary density operator on
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,

\tr_{xy\dots}
is the partial trace with respect to

\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}\otimes\dots
, and subscripts
A
,
B
, and
E

are used throughout this work to clarify the subspace to which each
expression belongs. Suppose that a density operator

\sigma\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
is a steady state of
\mathcal{F}
, viz.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\sigma

\displaystyle=\sigma.

(2.2)

The spectral form of
\sigma
is assumed to be

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}p_{n}\ket{n}_{A}\bra{n},

(2.3)

where
\{p_{n}:n=0,\dots,d_{A}-1\}
are the eigenvalues of
\sigma
,

\{\ket{n}_{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}:n=0,\dots,d_{A}-1\}
is an orthonormal
basis of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
,
d_{x}
is the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
, and each

\ket{n}_{A}
is an eigenvector of
\sigma
with eigenvalue
p_{n}
.

\sigma
is assumed to be full-rank, viz.,
p_{n}>0
for all
n
.

Following Roberts
et al.

[
3
]
, assume that

d_{B}=d_{A}
(hereafter abbreviated as
d
) and
\ket{\psi}_{AB}

is a purification of the steady state
\sigma
, given by

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\sqrt{p_{n}}\ket{n}_{A}\otimes\ket{\tilde n}_{B},

(2.4)

where

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde n}_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv W\theta\ket{n}_{A},

(2.5)

W:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{B}
is a unitary operator, and

\theta:\mathcal{H}_{A}\to\mathcal{H}_{A}
is an antiunitary operator.
The concept of antiunitary operators is reviewed in
Appendix
A
.

Remark 2.1
.

An antiunitary operator can always be decomposed as
u\vartheta
,
where
u
is a unitary operator and
\vartheta
is a conjugation
(antiunitary and
\vartheta^{2}=I_{A}
, where
I_{xy\dots}
is the
identity operator on
\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}\otimes\dots
). As
W

is left unspecified throughout this work,
u
may be absorbed into
the definition of
W
, and
\theta
in Eq. (
2.5
) can be
taken as a conjugation without loss of generality.

2.2
Definition of the reversal condition

Definition 2.1

(Reversal condition)
.

Assuming the model in Sec.
2.1
, the whole system is said
to obey the reversal condition if there exists a unitary
U_{E}
on

\mathcal{H}_{E}
such that

\displaystyle(I_{A}\otimes V)(I_{AB}\otimes U_{E})(U\otimes I_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E},

(2.6)

where
\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\in\mathcal{H}_{E}
is the final state of the field
mode.

Physically, the reversal condition means that system B, as a
“reverser,” can undo the entanglement between system A and the field
mode, with the help of an intermediate
U_{E}
. An equivalent picture is
to reverse the arrow of time depicted in Fig.
1

and rewrite Eq. (
2.6
) as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}

\displaystyle=(U^{\dagger}\otimes I_{B})(I_{AB}\otimes U_{E}^{\dagger})(I_{A}\otimes V^{\dagger})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}.

(2.7)

Under this reversed arrow of time,

\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}
is the initial state,

\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}
is the final state, and system B
is a reverser
in advance
that interacts with the field mode
first and prevents the entanglement between system A and the field
mode. Either way, system A is always assumed to be given throughout
this work, while system B is to be designed as the reverser.

The allowance of an intermediate
U_{E}
is a key property that makes
the reversal condition different from all previously proposed absorber
conditions
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
,
4
]
, which all
assume
U_{E}\propto I_{E}
. The reason for introducing
U_{E}
is to make
the reversal condition general enough to be boiled down to a simple
formula in terms of CPTP maps, as shown in Theorem
2.1

later.

The original absorber condition proposed by Stannigel
et al.

[
1
]
assumes that both
\ket{\chi}_{E}
and

\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}
are the vacuum state of a bosonic mode. System B
must then absorb any emission by system A—hence the name
absorber. Refs.
[
1
,
2
,
3
,
5
]
also
assume that the system-field interactions are infinitesimally weak so
that a continuous-time Markov model becomes valid; see
Sec.
5.2
later for details. Def.
2.1
,
on the other hand, makes no such assumptions and allows the field mode
to have arbitrary dynamics and arbitrary interaction with system A
before interacting with system B. As the condition in
Def.
2.1
is much more general and no longer restricted
to the absorber setting, it is appropriate to give it a different
name.

It can be shown that, given the model in Sec.
2.1
, a
V

that satisfies the reversal condition always exists

[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
; see also Prop.
4.1
later.

After the interactions with the field mode, systems A and B are
assumed, as in previously proposed absorber conditions, to return to
the initial state
\ket{\psi}_{AB}
. In other words,

\ket{\psi}_{AB}
is their steady state after sequential interactions
with multiple field modes, each with the same initial state

\ket{\chi}_{E}
. If systems A and B are not initially in this steady
state, they may still converge to it after many rounds of
interactions, such that the assumption of
\ket{\psi}_{AB}
as the
initial state becomes valid. The precise condition for the
steady-state convergence is, however, outside the scope of this work.

To proceed further, it is vital to define a CPTP map

\mathcal{G}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{B})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\rho_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv\tr_{E}\left[V^{\dagger}(\rho_{B}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\bra{\tilde\chi})V\right].

(2.8)

Notice the placements of
V^{\dagger}
and
V
. Under the arrow of time
depicted in Fig.
1
,
\mathcal{G}
is not a physical
channel, but it is a physical channel for system B if the arrow of
time is reversed, so that
V^{\dagger}
is the Schrödinger-picture
unitary and
\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}
is the ancilla input state. This
definition allows one to express the reversal condition in terms of
the
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
maps, as shown by Theorem
2.1

later.

2.3
A precise formula for the reversal condition

I now work towards Theorem
2.1
by presenting a series
of lemmas and definitions.

Lemma 2.1
.

The reversal condition is satisfied if and only if the
\mathcal{F}

and
\mathcal{G}
maps defined by Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.8
) obey

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{G})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi},

(2.9)

where
\mathcal{I}_{xy\dots}
is the identity map on

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}\otimes\dots)
.

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_1}

\displaystyle\equiv(U\otimes I_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E},

(2.10)

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_2}

\displaystyle\equiv(I_{A}\otimes V^{\dagger})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E},

(2.11)

so that

\displaystyle(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tr_{E}\left(\ket{\phi_1}\bra{\phi_1}\right),

\displaystyle(\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{G})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}

\displaystyle=\tr_{E}\left(\ket{\phi_2}\bra{\phi_2}\right).

(2.12)

To prove the “only if” part, note that Eq. (
2.6
) implies

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_2}

\displaystyle=(I_{AB}\otimes U_{E})\ket{\phi_1},

(2.13)

which implies Eq. (
2.9
) via Eqs. (
2.12
). To prove the “if”
part, note that Eq. (
2.9
) implies

\displaystyle\tr_{E}\left(\ket{\phi_1}\bra{\phi_1}\right)

\displaystyle=\tr_{E}\left(\ket{\phi_2}\bra{\phi_2}\right)

(2.14)

via Eqs. (
2.12
), meaning that
\ket{\phi_1}
and
\ket{\phi_2}

are purifications of the same density operator on

\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. Then there exists a unitary
U_{E}
on

\mathcal{H}_{E}
such that
[
19

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_2}

\displaystyle=(I_{AB}\otimes U_{E})\ket{\phi_1},

(2.15)

and Eq. (
2.6
) in Def.
2.1
is satisfied.
∎

To proceed further, it is necessary to establish some linear algebra
first.

Definition 2.2

(Hilbert-Schmidt inner product and adjoint)
.

The Hilbert-Schmidt inner product between two operators
X
and
Y

on the same Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
is defined as

\displaystyle\langle Y,X\rangle

\displaystyle\equiv\tr\left(Y^{\dagger}X\right).

(2.16)

The Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{HS}}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})

of a map
\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}Y,X\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Y,\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{HS}}X\rangle\quad\forall X,Y.

(2.17)

Definition 2.3

(Connes inner product
[
34
]
and adjoint)
.

The Connes inner product between operators
X
and
Y
with respect to
a full-rank density operator
\rho
, all on the same Hilbert space

\mathcal{H}
, is defined as

\displaystyle\langle Y,X\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle\equiv\langle Y,\mathcal{E}_{\rho}X\rangle=\tr\left(Y^{\dagger}\mathcal{E}_{\rho}X\right),

(2.18)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{\rho}X

\displaystyle\equiv\rho^{1/2}X\rho^{1/2}

(2.19)

is self-adjoint (
\mathcal{E}_{\rho}=\mathcal{E}_{\rho}^{\mathrm{HS}}
) and positive-definite
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product.

Let
\tau\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
be another full-rank density operator.
The adjoint
\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{Con}}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
of a map

\mathcal{M}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
with respect to the Connes
inner product is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{M}Y,X\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\langle Y,\mathcal{M}^{\mathrm{Con}}X\rangle_{\tau}\quad\forall X\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}),Y\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime}).

(2.20)

Connes introduced this inner product in the context of von Neumann
algebra
[
34
, Eq. (1)]
. The definition here in terms of the
density operator can be found, for example, in
Ref.
[
35
, Eq. (8.17)]
. It is also called the KMS inner product
in the literature for unknown reasons
[
36
]
. The
\mathrm{Con}

adjoint is instrumental in the works of Accardi and Cecchini

[
20
]
and Petz
[
16
,
17
]
.

Definition 2.4

(Petz recovery map
[
16
,
17
,
18
]
)
.

Given an initial state
\rho\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
, a CPTP map

\mathcal{F}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}^{\prime})
, and
\tau=\mathcal{F}\rho
for the

\mathrm{Con}
adjoint in Def.
2.3
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}\ \mathrm{Con}\ \mathrm{HS}}

(2.21)

is called the Petz recovery map. Explicitly,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\rho}\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{F}\rho}^{-1}.

(2.22)

Recall Remark
2.1
stating that
\theta
can always be
assumed to be a conjugation here. It follows that the map defined as

\displaystyle\Theta X

\displaystyle\equiv\theta^{-1}X\theta=\theta X\theta

(2.23)

is also a conjugation with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner
product. Define also the unitary map

\mathcal{W}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}X

\displaystyle\equiv WXW^{\dagger},

(2.24)

and the adjoint map
\mathcal{J}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}X

\displaystyle\equiv X^{\dagger}.

(2.25)

These maps will be essential in what follows; their properties are

.

Three more lemmas will be needed.

Lemma 2.2
.

Given Eq. (
2.5
),

\displaystyle\bra{\tilde n}_{B}X\ket{\tilde m}_{B}

\displaystyle=\bra{m}_{A}\mathcal{J}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}X\ket{n}_{A}.

(2.26)

Proof.

To deal with the antiunitary opreator in Eq. (
2.5
), I
switch temporarily from the braket notation to the proper

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
and write

as

. The unitarity of

\displaystyle\bra{\tilde n}_{B}X\ket{\tilde m}_{B}

\displaystyle=\langle W\theta n_{A},XW\theta m_{A}\rangle=\langle W^{\dagger}X^{\dagger}W\theta n_{A},\theta m_{A}\rangle=\langle m_{A},\theta W^{\dagger}X^{\dagger}W\theta n_{A}\rangle

(2.27)

\displaystyle=\bra{m}_{A}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{J}X\ket{n}_{A}.

(2.28)

Since
\mathcal{J}
commutes with any unitary or conjugation map by virtue

, the lemma follows.

\mathcal{F}\sigma=\sigma

]

Lemma 2.3

(Ref.
[
26

)
.

For any

,

\displaystyle\tr\left[(X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle=\langle Y,X\rangle_{\sigma}.

(2.29)

Proof.

\displaystyle\tr\left[(X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\sqrt{p_{n}p_{m}}\bra{n}_{A}X\ket{m}_{A}\bra{\tilde n}_{B}\mathcal{W}\Theta Y\ket{\tilde m}_{B}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{psi})})

(2.30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n,m}\sqrt{p_{n}p_{m}}\bra{n}_{A}X\ket{m}_{A}\bra{m}_{A}\mathcal{J}Y\ket{n}_{A}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Lemma~\ref{lem_ntilde}})

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\sigma^{1/2}X\sigma^{1/2}Y^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle=\langle Y,X\rangle_{\sigma}.

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Def.~\ref{def_connes}})

(2.33)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{M}_{2}\mathcal{M}_{1})^{*}

All the preceding preparations culminate in the following theorem,

\mathcal{F}

Theorem 2.1
.

The reversal condition is satisfied if and only if the
\mathcal{F}

and
\mathcal{G}
maps defined by Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.8
) obey

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1},

(2.35)

where
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}
is the Petz recovery map in Def.
2.4

with respect to the steady state
\sigma=\mathcal{F}\sigma
.

Proof.

Consider the left-hand side of Eq. (
2.9
) and write, using
Lemma
2.3
,

\displaystyle\tr\left[(X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle=\tr\left[(\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}}X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]=\langle Y,\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}}X\rangle_{\sigma}.

(2.36)

The right-hand side of Eq. (
2.9
) similarly gives

\displaystyle\tr\left[(X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)(\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{G})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle=\langle\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{W}\Theta Y,X\rangle_{\sigma}.

(2.37)

To prove the “only if” part, observe that the reversal condition
implies the equality of Eqs. (
2.36
) and (
2.37
) for any
X,Y

by Lemma
2.1
. By the definition of the
\mathrm{Con}
adjoint in
Def.
2.3
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}\ \mathrm{Con}}

\displaystyle=\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{W}\Theta.

(2.38)

Taking the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint, using the chain rule in
Lemma
2.4
, noting that
\mathcal{W}^{\mathrm{HS}}=\mathcal{W}^{-1}
and

\Theta^{\mathrm{HS}}=\Theta
, and applying the definition of the Petz map in
Def.
2.4
, one obtains

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}\ \mathrm{Con}\ \mathrm{HS}}=\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}\Theta,

(2.39)

which leads to Eq. (
2.35
).

To prove the “if” part, retrace the preceding steps backwards to go
from Eq. (
2.35
) to

\displaystyle\tr\left[(X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle=\tr\left[(X\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta Y)(\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{G})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

(2.40)

for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
. Now express an arbitrary

Z\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
as

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}Z_{jk}a_{j}\otimes b_{k}

(2.41)

in terms of a matrix
Z_{jk}
, a basis
\{a_{j}\}
of

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
, and a basis
\{b_{k}\}
of

\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
. Plug
X=a_{j}
and
Y=\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}b_{k}
into
Eq. (
2.40
) and take the sum
\sum_{j,k}Z_{jk}(\dots)
to obtain

\displaystyle\tr\left[Z(\mathcal{F}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]

\displaystyle=\tr\left[Z(\mathcal{I}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{G})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right]\quad\forall Z\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}),

(2.42)

which is equivalent to Eq. (
2.9
) and therefore implies the
reversal condition by Lemma
2.1
.
∎

Eq. (
2.35
) in Theorem
2.1
is a necessary
and sufficient condition for system B to be a reverser—any reverser
must obey Eq. (
2.35
), and any system B that obeys it is a
reverser.

Before closing this section, I give a noteworthy corollary.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{W}\Theta\sigma=\tr_{A}\left(\ket{\psi}_{AB}\bra{\psi}\right).

(2.43)

Under the reversal condition,
\tilde{\sigma}
is a steady
state of
\mathcal{G}
, viz.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\sigma}.

(2.44)

Proof.

By the recovery property of
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}

[
17
,
18
]
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\sigma=\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\mathcal{F}\sigma=\sigma.

(2.45)

Then use Eqs. (
2.35
), (
2.43
), and
(
2.45
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{W}\Theta\sigma=\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\sigma=\mathcal{W}\Theta\sigma=\tilde{\sigma}.

(2.46)

∎

3
Detailed balance

There exist many quantum generalizations of the detailed balance
condition. As discovered by Roberts
et al.

[
3
]
,
the one most relevant to the absorber theory is the so-called
SQDB-
\theta
condition proposed by Fagnola and Umanità
[
14
]
, where SQDB stands for standard quantum detailed
balance. It turns out that, if system A satisfies the SQDB-
\theta

condition, the reversal condition given by Theorem
2.1

for the whole system can be simplified significantly. Here I define a
discrete-time version of the SQDB-
\theta
condition for mathematical
simplicity.

Definition 3.1

(Discrete-time SQDB-
\theta
condition)
.

A CPTP map
\mathcal{F}
and its steady state
\sigma
are said to satisfy
the SQDB-
\theta
condition if

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}Y,X\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma},\quad\forall X,Y\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),\quad\forall n=0,1,2,\dots

(3.1)

Physically,
\mathcal{F}^{n}
is the channel for system A after it interacts
with
n
temporal field modes sequentially, each with the same initial
state
\ket{\chi}_{E}
. With further assumptions about
\mathcal{F}
, a
continuous-time limit of
\mathcal{F}^{n}
can be taken to give a quantum
Markov semigroup, which is discussed in more detail in
Sec.
5.2
.

The following theorem originates from Ref.
[
14
]
; I provide
a proof for completeness.

Theorem 3.1

(Ref.
[
14
]
)
.

The SQDB-
\theta
condition in Def.
3.1
implies each of the
following two conditions:

\displaystyle\sigma

\displaystyle=\Theta\sigma,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\Theta.

(3.3)

Conversely, the two conditions together imply the SQDB-
\theta

condition.

To prove this theorem, I need the following lemma first.

Lemma 3.1
.

For any
X,Y\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
,

\displaystyle\langle\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle Y,X\rangle_{\Theta\sigma},

(3.4)

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle Y,\Theta\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X\rangle_{\Theta\sigma}.

(3.5)

Proof.

To prove Eq. (
3.4
), use Def.
2.3
and
Prop.
A.2
to write

\displaystyle\langle\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle\Theta X,\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}\Theta Y\rangle=\langle\Theta\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}\Theta Y,X\rangle=\langle\mathcal{E}_{\Theta\sigma}Y,X\rangle=\langle Y,X\rangle_{\Theta\sigma}.

(3.6)

To obtain Eq. (
3.5
), replace
X
in Eq. (
3.4
) by

\Theta\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

First prove the forward direction. To derive
Eq. (
3.2
), combine Eq. (
3.1
) for
n=0
and
Eq. (
3.4
) to obtain

\displaystyle\langle Y,X\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle Y,X\rangle_{\Theta\sigma}.

(3.7)

Then plug
Y=I
to obtain

\displaystyle\langle I,X\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\tr\left(\sigma X\right)=\langle I,X\rangle_{\Theta\sigma}=\tr\left[(\Theta\sigma)X\right],

(3.8)

which holds for any
X
, leading to
\Theta\sigma=\sigma
.

For
n>0
, write

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}Y,X\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{DB})})

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\langle Y,\Theta\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X\rangle_{\sigma},

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{DBn}) and $\Theta\sigma=\sigma$})

(3.10)

which implies, by the definition of
\mathrm{Con}
in Def.
2.3
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}\ \mathrm{Con}}

\displaystyle=\Theta\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta,

(3.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}\ \mathrm{Con}\ \mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{Petz}}=\Theta\mathcal{F}^{n}\Theta,

(3.12)

giving Eq. (
3.3
) for
n=1
.

Now prove the converse. Eq. (
3.2
) can be plugged into
Eq. (
3.4
) to give Eq. (
3.1
) for
n=0
. To derive
Eq. (
3.1
) for
n>0
, take the
n
th power of
Eq. (
3.3
) to obtain Eq. (
3.12
) by the chain rule in
Lemma
2.4
. Eq. (
3.12
) is equivalent to
Eq. (
3.11
), which can be plugged into the right-hand side of
Eq. (
3.5
). Plug also
\Theta\sigma=\sigma
and use the
definition of
\mathrm{Con}
to obtain

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\langle Y,\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}\ \mathrm{Con}}X\rangle_{\sigma}=\langle\mathcal{F}^{n\ \mathrm{HS}}Y,X\rangle_{\sigma},

(3.13)

which is Eq. (
3.1
) for
n>0
.
∎

The remarkable simplification of the Petz map under the SQDB-
\theta

condition simplifies the reversal condition as well.

Corollary 3.1
.

If system A satisfies Eq. (
3.3
), the reversal condition is
satisfied if and only if

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}\mathcal{W}^{-1}.

(3.14)

Proof.

Plug Eq. (
3.3
) into Eq. (
2.35
).
∎

It is interesting to observe that system B mirrors properties of
system A under the reversal condition, as shown by
Corollary
2.1
and the corollary below.

Corollary 3.2
.

If system A satisfies the SQDB-
\theta
condition and the whole
system satisfies the reversal condition, then
\mathcal{G}
and its steady
state
\tilde{\sigma}
also satisfy a SQDB-
\tilde{\theta}
condition given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\sigma},

(3.15)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\tilde{\Theta},

(3.16)

where the conjugation
\tilde{\theta}
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, the conjugation

\tilde{\Theta}
on
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{B})
, and
\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{Petz}}
are respectively
defined by

\displaystyle\tilde{\theta}

\displaystyle\equiv W\theta W^{\dagger},

(3.17)

\displaystyle\tilde{\Theta}X

\displaystyle\equiv\tilde{\theta}X\tilde{\theta}=\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}X,

(3.18)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{Petz}}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{E}_{\mathcal{G}\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}=\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}.

(3.19)

Proof.

Use Eqs. (
3.18
), (
2.43
), and (
3.2
)
to write

\displaystyle\tilde{\Theta}\tilde{\sigma}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{W}\Theta\sigma=\mathcal{W}\sigma=\mathcal{W}\Theta\sigma=\tilde{\sigma}.

(3.20)

One can also show

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{Petz}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\sigma}}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{E}_{\tilde{\sigma}}^{-1}=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{E}_{\Theta\sigma}\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{E}_{\Theta\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{W}^{-1}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{sigma2}) and Prop.~\ref{prop_maps}})

(3.21)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{G}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{W}^{-1}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{sigma_invar})})

(3.22)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{HS}}\mathcal{E}_{\sigma}^{-1}\mathcal{W}^{-1}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{G_DB})})

(3.23)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\mathcal{W}^{-1}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{petz_exp}) and $\mathcal{F}\sigma=\sigma$})

(3.24)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}\mathcal{G}\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{G_reversal})})

(3.25)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\Theta}\mathcal{G}\tilde{\Theta},

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{Theta2})})

(3.26)

which leads to Eq. (
3.16
).
∎

4
Special reversal condition

A shortcoming of the reversal condition is that it is too vague about
the system-B dynamics
V
and the intermediate
U_{E}
needed to make
system B a reverser. Only certain properties of
V
are specified
through the relations between the
\mathcal{F}
and
\mathcal{G}
maps, while

U_{E}
is left completely unspecified, making the experimental design
of the reverser difficult. To overcome the shortcoming, I now focus
on a special reversal condition, where
U_{E}
is assumed to be the
identity and the
V
that makes system B a reverser can be
characterized more explicitly.

Definition 4.1

(Special reversal condition)
.

The whole system is said to satisfy the special reversal condition if
Def.
2.1
is satisfied with
U_{E}=I_{E}
, viz.,

\displaystyle(I_{A}\otimes V)(U\otimes I_{B})\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}

\displaystyle=\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}.

(4.1)

This definition, apart from allowing
\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}
to be
distinct from
\ket{\chi}_{E}
, is identical to the absorber condition
assumed by Godley and Guta in their Lemma 4.1
[
4
]
. They
have also proved that there always exists a
V
that satisfies the
condition; I include their result here for completeness.

Proposition 4.1

(Ref.
[
4
, Lemma 4.1]
)
.

A unitary
V
on
\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
that satisfies
Eq. (
4.1
) always exists.

Proof.

Recall the
\ket{\phi_1}
defined by Eq. (
2.10
). Since

\displaystyle\tr_{BE}\left(\ket{\phi_1}\bra{\phi_1}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{F}\sigma=\sigma,

(4.2)

\ket{\phi_1}
is a purification of
\sigma
.
\ket{\psi}_{AB}
is
also a purification of
\sigma
, so the two must be related by an
isometry
R:\mathcal{H}_{B}\to\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
that satisfies

R^{\dagger}R=I_{B}
as follows
[
19
]
:

\displaystyle\ket{\phi_1}

\displaystyle=I_{A}\otimes R\ket{\psi}_{AB}.

(4.3)

Now Eq. (
4.1
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle I_{A}\otimes V\ket{\phi_1}

\displaystyle=I_{A}\otimes VR\ket{\psi}_{AB}=\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E},

(4.4)

which can be satisfied if

\displaystyle VR

\displaystyle=\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E},

(4.5)

where
\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}:\mathcal{H}_{B}\to\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}

is a partial ket operator defined by

\displaystyle\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\ket{\xi}_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv\ket{\xi}_{B}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\quad\forall\ket{\xi}_{B}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}.

(4.6)

Eq. (
4.5
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle VR\ket{\tilde n}_{B}

\displaystyle=\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\ket{\tilde n}_{B}=\ket{\tilde n}_{B}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E},\quad n=0,\dots,d-1.

(4.7)

Observe that
\{R\ket{\tilde n}_{B}\}
are an orthonormal set with
d

elements in
\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
, and

\{\ket{\tilde n}_{B}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\}
are also an
orthonormal set with
d
elements in
\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
. It
follows that a unitary
V
that maps the former set to the latter set,
thereby satisfying Eqs. (
4.7
), (
4.5
), (
4.4
), and thus
(
4.1
), always exists.
∎

Since the dimension of
\mathcal{H}_{B}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
is
d\times d_{E}
, the
requirement on
V
given by Eq. (
4.7
) does not uniquely specify
it. Theorem
4.1
, to be shown later in this section, is
a more concrete result, demonstrating that the special reversal
condition can be expressed precisely in terms of Kraus operators.

I now prepare for Theorem
4.1
by defining the Kraus
operators needed there and presenting a lemma.

Definition 4.2

(Kraus operators)
.

Define

\displaystyle f_{j}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{j}_{E}U\ket{\chi}_{E}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A}),

(4.8)

\displaystyle g_{j}

\displaystyle\equiv\bra{j}_{E}V^{\dagger}\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{B})

(4.9)

with respect to the same overcomplete system

\{\ket{j}_{E}\in\mathcal{H}_{E}:j=0,\dots,D_{E}-1\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{E}
that
satisfies
[
19
, Definition 2.20]

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\ket{j}_{E}\bra{j}

\displaystyle=I_{E}.

(4.10)

\{f_{j}\}
and
\{g_{j}\}
are Kraus operators for the
\mathcal{F}
and

\mathcal{G}
maps defined by Eqs. (
2.1
) and (
2.8
), respectively,
viz.,

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\rho_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}f_{j}\rho_{A}f_{j}^{\dagger},

(4.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}\rho_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}g_{j}\rho_{B}g_{j}^{\dagger}.

(4.12)

Note that the overcomplete system
\{\ket{j}_{E}\}
need not be
orthonormal or even linearly independent—it needs only to satisfy
Eq. (
4.10
). Note also that

\ket{\xi}_{E}:\mathcal{H}_{x}\to\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}
and its adjoint

\bra{\xi}_{E}:\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{E}\to\mathcal{H}_{x}
in Eqs. (
4.8
)
and (
4.9
), where
\mathcal{H}_{x}
depends on the context, should be
regarded as partial ket and bra operators, as reviewed in
Appendix
B
.

Lemma 4.1

(Ref.
[
3
, Appendix D]
)
.

\displaystyle X\otimes I_{B}\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle=I_{A}\otimes Y\ket{\psi}_{AB}

(4.13)

if and only if

\displaystyle Y

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}X,

(4.14)

where
\mathcal{Q}:\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})\to\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
is a linear
map defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}

\displaystyle\equiv\Theta\mathcal{J}\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2},

(4.15)

and the so-called modular map
\Delta_{\sigma}
is defined as

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}X

\displaystyle\equiv\sigma X\sigma^{-1},

(4.16)

such that

\displaystyle\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2}X

\displaystyle=\sigma^{-1/2}X\sigma^{1/2}.

(4.17)

Proof.

To prove the “only if” part, start from Eq. (
4.13
)
and write

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\sqrt{p_{n}}X\ket{n}_{A}\otimes\ket{\tilde n}_{B}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m}\sqrt{p_{m}}\ket{m}_{A}\otimes Y\ket{\tilde m}_{B},

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{psi})})

(4.18)

\displaystyle\sqrt{p_{n}}\bra{m}_{A}X\ket{n}_{A}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{m}}\bra{\tilde n}_{B}Y\ket{\tilde m}_{B}=\sqrt{p_{m}}\bra{m}_{A}\mathcal{J}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}Y\ket{n}_{A},

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Lemma~\ref{lem_ntilde}})

(4.19)

\displaystyle X\sigma^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{1/2}\mathcal{J}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}Y,

(4.20)

which leads to Eq. (
4.14
). To prove the “if” part, it suffices
to show that Eq. (
4.14
) leads to

\displaystyle\bra{n}_{A}\otimes\bra{\tilde m}_{B}X\otimes I_{B}\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\bra{n}_{A}\otimes\bra{\tilde m}_{B}I_{A}\otimes Y\ket{\psi}_{AB}\quad\forall n,m.

(4.21)

Consider the right-hand side first:

\displaystyle\quad\bra{n}_{A}\otimes\bra{\tilde m}_{B}I_{A}\otimes Y\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\bra{n}_{A}\otimes\bra{\tilde m}_{B}I_{A}\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{J}\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2}X\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eqs.~(\ref{YWQX}) and (\ref{Q})})

(4.22)

\displaystyle=\bra{n}_{A}\otimes\bra{\tilde m}_{B}I_{A}\otimes\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{J}\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2}X\sum_{l}\sqrt{p_{l}}\ket{l}_{A}\otimes\ket*{\tilde l}_{B}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{psi})})

(4.23)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{n}}\bra{\tilde m}_{B}\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{J}\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2}X\ket{\tilde n}_{B}

(4.24)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{n}}\bra{n}_{A}\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2}X\ket{m}_{A}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Lemma~\ref{lem_ntilde}})

(4.25)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{n}}\bra{n}_{A}\sigma^{-1/2}X\sigma^{1/2}\ket{m}_{A}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{mod})})

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{m}}\bra{n}_{A}X\ket{m}_{A}.

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{sigma})})

(4.27)

By similar steps, the left-hand side of Eq. (
4.21
) is
given by

\displaystyle\bra{n}_{A}\otimes\bra{\tilde m}_{B}X\otimes I_{B}\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{p_{m}}\bra{n}_{A}X\ket{m}_{A},

(4.28)

which is equal to Eq. (
4.27
), and the desired equality given
by Eq. (
4.21
) is proved.
∎

Theorem 4.1
.

The special reversal condition is satisfied if and only if the Kraus
operators
\{f_{j}\}
and
\{g_{j}\}
in Def.
4.2
are related
by

\displaystyle g_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}f_{j},

(4.29)

where
\mathcal{Q}
is the linear map defined by Eq. (
4.15
). If
Eq. (
4.29
) holds for Kraus operators defined with respect to one
overcomplete system
\{\ket{j}_{E}\}
as per Def.
4.2
, then
it holds for Kraus operators in terms of any overcomplete system of

\mathcal{H}_{E}
.

Proof.

To prove the “only if” part, rewrite Eq. (
4.1
) as

\displaystyle U\otimes I_{B}\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\chi}_{E}

\displaystyle=I_{A}\otimes V^{\dagger}\ket{\psi}_{AB}\otimes\ket{\tilde\chi}_{E}

(4.30)

and apply
\bra{j}_{E}
on both sides to obtain

\displaystyle f_{j}\otimes I_{B}\ket{\psi}_{AB}

\displaystyle=I_{A}\otimes g_{j}\ket{\psi}_{AB},

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Def.~\ref{def_kraus}})

(4.31)

which implies Eq. (
4.29
) by Lemma
4.1
. To prove the “if”
part, note that Eq. (
4.29
) also implies Eq. (
4.31
) by
Lemma
4.1
. Then apply
\sum_{j}\ket{j}_{E}
on both sides of
Eq. (
4.31
) to obtain Eq. (
4.30
), which is equivalent to
the special reversal condition.

To prove the invariance of the condition to the assumed overcomplete
system, let
\{f_{j}^{\prime}\}
and
\{g_{j}^{\prime}\}
be the Kraus operators defined
with respect to another overcomplete system

\{\ket{j'}_{E}:j=0,\dots,D_{E}^{\prime}-1\}
. The new system is related to the
old system by

\displaystyle\bra{j'}_{E}=\bra{j'}_{E}\sum_{k}\ket{k}_{E}\bra{k}=\sum_{k}c_{jk}\bra{k}_{E},

(4.32)

where
c_{jk}=\innerproduct{j'}{k}
, and the Kraus operators are
similarly related by

\displaystyle f_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{jk}f_{k},

\displaystyle g_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{jk}g_{k}.

(4.33)

Now observe that the map
\mathcal{Q}
defined by Eq. (
4.15
)
is a linear map, since
\Delta_{\sigma}^{-1/2}

is linear while
\Theta
and
\mathcal{J}
are
antilinear. It follows that
\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}
is also linear,
and Eq. (
4.29
) implies

\displaystyle g_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{jk}g_{k}=\sum_{k}c_{jk}\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}f_{k}=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}\sum_{k}c_{jk}f_{k}=\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}f_{j}^{\prime},

(4.34)

which is Eq. (
4.29
) in terms of the new Kraus operators.
∎

Eq. (
4.29
) in Theorem
4.1
is a necessary and
sufficient condition for system B to be a special reverser—any
special reverser must obey Eq. (
4.29
), and any system B that obeys
it is a special reverser.

Notice that
\{\mathcal{Q}f_{j}\}
are a set of Kraus operators for

\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\Theta
. It follows that
\{\mathcal{W}\mathcal{Q}f_{j}\}
are
a set of Kraus operators for

\mathcal{W}\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\Theta\mathcal{W}^{-1}
, the right-hand side of

) in

. Eq. (
2.35
) in

\mathcal{G}
and

[

The SQDB-
\theta
condition discussed in Sec.

can also
simplify the special reversal condition. I need the following lemma
first.

Lemma 4.2
.

If Eq. (
3.3
) is satisfied, there exists a partially
isometric matrix
c\in\mathbb{C}^{D_{E}\times D_{E}}
such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}f_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}c_{jk}f_{k}.

(4.35)

Furthermore, if the set
\{f_{j}\}
is linearly independent, then

\{Qf_{j}\}
is also linearly independent and
c
is
unitary. Conversely, if
\{f_{j}\}
satisfies Eq. (
4.35
) with
a unitary
c
, then Eq. (
3.3
) holds.

Proof.

Eq. (
3.3
) implies the existence of a partially isometric

c
that satisfies Eq. (
4.35
) by
Ref.
[
19
, Exercise 6.15]
. Now assume that
\{f_{j}\}
is
linearly independent. The linear independence of
\{\mathcal{Q}f_{j}\}
can
be proved by contradiction: suppose that
\{\mathcal{Q}f_{j}\}
is
linearly dependent. Then there exists a nonzero

such that
\sum_{j}a_{j}\mathcal{Q}f_{j}=0
. As the
\mathcal{Q}
defined by

\sum_{j}a_{j}f_{j}=0

\{f_{j}\}
. By Ref.
[

, Exercises 6.14 and
6.15]
,

is unitary if
\{f_{j}\}
and
\{\mathcal{Q}f_{j}\}
are

The converse part is proved as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\rho_{A}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}f_{j}\rho_{A}f_{j}^{\dagger}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{f2})})

(4.36)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l}c_{jk}c_{jl}^{*}(\mathcal{Q}f_{k})\rho_{A}(\mathcal{Q}f_{l})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle(\textrm{by Eq.~(\ref{DB_kraus})})

(4.37)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}(\mathcal{Q}f_{k})\rho_{A}(\mathcal{Q}f_{k})^{\dagger}

\displaystyle(\textrm{isometry of $c$})

(4.38)

\displaystyle=\Theta\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{Petz}}\Theta\rho_{A}.

(4.39)

∎

The matrix

\displaystyle g_{j}

Random unitary channel

Appendix A
Antiunitary operators and maps

For clarity, the appendices use the proper notation of an inner
product
\langle\phi,\psi\rangle
between two elements
\phi
and
\psi
in a
Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. The inner product is assumed to be antilinear
with respect to the first argument and linear with respect to the
second argument.

An operator
X:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is said to be antilinear if

\displaystyle X(\alpha\psi+\beta\phi)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{*}X\psi+\beta^{*}X\phi\quad\forall\psi,\phi\in\mathcal{H},\forall\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}.

(A.1)

Denote the set of antilinear operators on
\mathcal{H}
as

\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{H})
. The adjoint
X^{\dagger}
of an antilinear
operator
X\in\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{H})
is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\phi,X\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi,X^{\dagger}\phi\rangle\quad\forall\phi,\psi\in\mathcal{H}.

(A.2)

X^{\dagger}
is also antilinear and
X^{\dagger\dagger}=X
. The
inverse
X^{-1}
of an antilinear operator
X
is defined by

\displaystyle X^{-1}X=XX^{-1}=I,

(A.3)

where
I
is the identity operator on
\mathcal{H}
.

X^{-1}
, if it exists, is antilinear.

An operator
T\in\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(\mathcal{H})
is said to be antiunitary if
it obeys

\displaystyle\langle T\phi,T\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi,\phi\rangle\quad\forall\phi,\psi.

(A.4)

If
T^{2}=I
in addition, then
T
is called a conjugation. I collect
some basic facts about antiunitary operators in the following
proposition; see, for example, Ref.
[
15
]
.

Proposition A.1
.

An antiunitary operator
T
has the following properties.

1.

T^{\dagger}=T^{-1}
, which is antiunitary.

2.

Given an orthonormal set
\{e_{j}\}
in
\mathcal{H}
,
\{Te_{j}\}
is another
orthonormal set.

3.

T
can always be decomposed as

\displaystyle T

\displaystyle=u\theta,

(A.5)

where
u
is unitary and
\theta
is a conjugation. For a
conjugation, there exists a unique orthonormal basis
\{e_{j}\}
of

\mathcal{H}
such that

\displaystyle\theta e_{j}

\displaystyle=e_{j}.

(A.6)

It follows that

\displaystyle\theta^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=\theta^{-1}=\theta,

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}

\displaystyle=T^{-1}=\theta u^{\dagger}.

(A.7)

The inverse
\mathcal{A}^{-1}
and the Hilbert-Schmidt adjoint
\mathcal{A}^{\mathrm{HS}}

of an antilinear map
\mathcal{A}
are defined in the same manner as those
of an antilinear operator. I collect some basic facts about unitary
and antiunitary maps in the following proposition.

Proposition A.2
.

Define the
\mathcal{J}
,
\Theta
, and
\mathcal{U}
maps on
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}X

\displaystyle\equiv X^{\dagger},

\displaystyle\Theta X

\displaystyle\equiv\theta X\theta,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}X

\displaystyle\equiv UXU^{\dagger},

(A.8)

where
U
is an arbitrary unitary operator on
\mathcal{H}
and
\theta
is
an arbitrary conjugation on
\mathcal{H}
.

1.

Commutation with
\mathcal{J}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\Theta

\displaystyle=\Theta\mathcal{J},

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}\mathcal{U}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}\mathcal{J}.

(A.9)

2.

Chain rules: for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(XY)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{J}Y)(\mathcal{J}X),

\displaystyle\Theta(XY)

\displaystyle=(\Theta X)(\Theta Y),

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(XY)

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{U}X)(\mathcal{U}Y).

(A.10)

3.

In terms of the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product,
\mathcal{U}
is
unitary, while
\mathcal{J}
and
\Theta
are antiunitary, viz.,
for any
X,Y\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{J}X,\mathcal{J}Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Y,X\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\Theta X,\Theta Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle Y,X\rangle,

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{U}X,\mathcal{U}Y\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle X,Y\rangle.

(A.11)

It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{J}^{-1}=\mathcal{J},

\displaystyle\Theta^{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\Theta^{-1}=\Theta,

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}^{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{U}^{-1}.

(A.12)

4.

A map in product form

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{A,B}X

\displaystyle\equiv AXB

(A.13)

obeys

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{A,B}^{\mathrm{HS}}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{J}A,\mathcal{J}B},

\displaystyle\Theta\mathcal{M}_{A,B}\Theta^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\Theta A,\Theta B},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}\mathcal{M}_{A,B}\mathcal{U}^{-1}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{M}_{\mathcal{U}A,\mathcal{U}B}.

(A.14)

5.

Let
\{e_{j}\}
be the eigenbasis of
\theta
satisfying
Eq. (
A.6
) and let the matrix representation of

X\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H})
in this basis be

\displaystyle X_{jk}

\displaystyle\equiv\left\langle e_{j},Xe_{k}\right\rangle.

(A.15)

Then

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{j},\mathcal{J}Xe_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=X_{kj}^{*},

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{j},\Theta Xe_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=X_{jk}^{*},

\displaystyle\left\langle e_{j},\Theta\mathcal{J}Xe_{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=X_{kj},

(A.16)

where
*
denotes the entry-wise complex conjugate.

6.

Let
X
be a normal operator and
s\in\mathbb{C}
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(X^{s})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{J}X)^{s^{*}},

\displaystyle\Theta(X^{s})

\displaystyle=(\Theta X)^{s^{*}},

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(X^{s})

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{U}X)^{s}.

(A.17)

Appendix B
Partial ket and bra operators

Consider two Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and
\mathcal{H}_{y}
and their tensor
product
\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
. I continue to use the proper notation
for the inner product and use the bra and ket notations only to denote
the operators defined in the following. A partial ket operator

\ket{\xi}_{y}:\mathcal{H}_{x}\to\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
, where

\xi\in\mathcal{H}_{y}
, is defined by

\displaystyle\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi

\displaystyle\equiv\psi\otimes\xi\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{x}.

(B.1)

Its adjoint, denoted as
\bra{\xi}_{y}:\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}\to\mathcal{H}_{x}
,
is defined by

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\bra{\xi}_{y}\phi,\psi\rangle\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{x},\phi\in\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}.

(B.2)

I collect some basic facts about the partial ket and bra operators
in the following proposition.

Proposition B.1
.

Let
\xi
and
\eta
be arbitrary elements of
\mathcal{H}_{y}
.

1.

\bra{\eta}_{y}\ket{\xi}_{y}=\langle\eta,\xi\rangle I_{x}
.

2.

Suppose that
\langle\xi,\xi\rangle=1
. The operator

\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}:\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}\to\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
is a projection of
\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
onto the
subspace

\displaystyle\ket{\xi}_{y}\mathcal{H}_{x}\equiv\left\{\psi\otimes\xi:\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{x}\right\}.

(B.3)

3.

If
\{\xi_{j}\}
is an overcomplete system of
\mathcal{H}_{y}
such that

\sum_{j}\ket{\xi_j}\bra{\xi_j}=I_{y}
, viz.,

\displaystyle\psi

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\xi_{j}\langle\xi_{j},\psi\rangle\quad\forall\psi\in\mathcal{H}_{y},

(B.4)

then

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\ket{\xi_j}_{y}\bra{\xi_j}_{y}

\displaystyle=I_{x}\otimes I_{y}.

(B.5)

4.

For any
X\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{x})
,

\displaystyle\ket{\xi}_{y}X

\displaystyle=(X\otimes I_{y})\ket{\xi}_{y},

\displaystyle X\bra{\xi}_{y}

\displaystyle=\bra{\xi}_{y}(X\otimes I_{y}).

(B.6)

5.

For any
X\in\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y})
and any
overcomplete system
\{\xi_{j}\}
of
\mathcal{H}_{y}
,

\displaystyle\tr_{y}X=\sum_{j}\bra{\xi_j}_{y}X\ket{\xi_j}_{y}.

(B.7)

Proof.

Let
\psi,\phi
be arbitrary elements of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
and let

\{a_{j}\}
,
\{b_{k}\}
, and
\{a_{j}\otimes b_{k}\}
be orthonormal bases
of
\mathcal{H}_{x}
,
\mathcal{H}_{y}
, and
\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
, respectively.

1.

\displaystyle\langle\phi,\bra{\eta}_{y}\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\phi\otimes\eta,\psi\otimes\xi\rangle=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle\langle\eta,\xi\rangle.

(B.8)

2.

\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}
is obviously self-adjoint. It is also
idempotent because

\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}=\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}
. It follows that
\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}
is a
projection operator. Now let

\mathcal{H}_{\xi}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
be the range of

\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}
. Let
\Psi
be an arbitrary element of

\mathcal{H}_{x}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{y}
and observe that

\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}\Psi\in\ket{\xi}_{y}\mathcal{H}_{x}
, which
implies
\mathcal{H}_{\xi}\subseteq\ket{\xi}_{y}\mathcal{H}_{x}
. Conversely, write
an arbitrary element of
\ket{\xi}_{y}\mathcal{H}_{x}
as
\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi
. Then

\displaystyle\ket{\xi}_{y}\bra{\xi}_{y}\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi

\displaystyle=\ket{\xi}_{y}\langle\xi,\xi\rangle\psi=\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi,

(B.9)

implying that
\ket{\xi}_{y}\mathcal{H}_{x}\subseteq\mathcal{H}_{\xi}
. Hence

\mathcal{H}_{\xi}=\ket{\xi}_{y}\mathcal{H}_{x}
.

3.

Write
\psi=\sum_{k,l}\psi_{kl}(a_{k}\otimes b_{l})
and

\phi=\sum_{m,n}\phi_{mn}(a_{m}\otimes b_{n})
. Then

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\langle\phi,\ket{\xi_j}_{y}\bra{\xi_j}_{y}\psi\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m,n,k,l}\phi_{mn}^{*}\psi_{kl}\langle a_{m}\otimes b_{n},\ket{\xi_j}_{y}\bra{\xi_j}_{y}a_{k}\otimes b_{l}\rangle

(B.10)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m,n,k,l}\phi_{mn}^{*}\psi_{kl}\langle b_{n},\xi_{j}\rangle\langle\xi_{j},b_{l}\rangle\langle a_{m},a_{k}\rangle

(B.11)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n,k,l}\phi_{mn}^{*}\psi_{kl}\langle b_{n},b_{l}\rangle\langle a_{m},a_{k}\rangle

(B.12)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\phi_{mn}^{*}\psi_{mn}=\langle\phi,\psi\rangle.

(B.13)

4.

\displaystyle(X\otimes I_{y})\ket{\xi}_{y}\psi

\displaystyle=(X\otimes I_{y})(\psi\otimes\xi)=(X\psi\otimes\xi)=\ket{\xi}_{y}X\psi.

(B.14)

5.

Let
Y
be an arbitrary element of
\mathcal{O}(\mathcal{H}_{x})
.

\displaystyle\sum_{j}\tr\left(\bra{\xi_j}_{y}X\ket{\xi_j}_{y}Y\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\langle a_{k},\bra{\xi_j}_{y}X\ket{\xi_j}_{y}Ya_{k}\rangle

(B.15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\langle a_{k}\otimes\xi_{j},X(Ya_{k}\otimes\xi_{j})\rangle

(B.16)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k}\langle a_{k}\otimes\xi_{j},X(Y\otimes I_{y})(a_{k}\otimes\xi_{j})\rangle

(B.17)

\displaystyle=\tr\left[X(Y\otimes I_{y})\right]=\tr\left[(\tr_{y}X)Y\right].

(B.18)

∎
