Coupled cluster theory for nonadiabatic dynamics: nuclear gradients and nonadiabatic couplings in similarity constrained coupled cluster theory
Eirik F. Kjønstad, Sara Angelico, Henrik Koch

Abstract
Coupled cluster theory is one of the most accurate electronic structure methods for predicting ground and excited state chemistry. However, the presence of numerical artifacts at electronic degeneracies, such as complex energies, has made it difficult to apply it in nonadiabatic dynamics simulations. While it has already been shown that such numerical artifacts can be fully removed by using similarity constrained coupled cluster (SCC) theory [ J. Phys. Chem. Lett. 2017, 8 , 19, 4801–4807], simulating dynamics requires efficient implementations of gradients and nonadiabatic couplings. Here, we present an implementation of nuclear gradients and nonadiabatic derivative couplings at the similarity constrained coupled cluster singles and doubles (SCCSD) level of theory, thereby making possible nonadiabatic dynamics simulations using a coupled cluster theory that provides a correct description of conical intersections between excited states. We present a few numerical examples that show good agreement with literature values and discuss some limitations of the method.

1
Introduction

Upon photoexcitation to an excited electronic state, molecular systems normally undergo relaxation through one or more conical intersections. When a system approaches such electronic degeneracies, the dynamics of the nuclei changes: the nuclear motion must then be treated in terms of a nuclear wavepacket and the coupling between the electrons and the nuclei (nonadiabatic coupling) becomes large, leading to a breakdown of the Born-Oppenheimer approximation. As a result, non-radiative transfer of nuclear population between electronic states becomes possible, something that typically occurs within tens or hundreds of femtoseconds after excitation by light.
(
Domcke et al. 2004
)

It is challenging to simulate such a process, however, as it requires an accurate treatment not only of the nuclear wavepacket but also of the electronic states involved. The choice of the electronic structure method often qualitatively alters the predicted dynamics, emphasizing the importance of a balanced and accurate treatment of the electronic states.
(
Mai and González 2020
)
Depending on the system of interest, it can be important to effectively capture static correlation (for example, by using complete active space methods
(
Roos et al. 1980
)
) or dynamical correlation (for example, by using perturbation theory
(
Andersson et al. 1990
)
or density functional theory
(
Huix-Rotllant et al. 2020
;
Li Manni et al. 2014
;
Hennefarth et al. 2023
)
). In the latter category, coupled cluster theory
(
Koch and Jørgensen 1990
;
Stanton and Bartlett 1993
)
is often particularly accurate,
(
Loos et al. 2020
)
making it a promising candidate for simulating photochemical processes.

A major theoretical issue has hindered this, however.
Coupled cluster methods are known to produce numerical artifacts at same-symmetry
intersections, where the potential energy surfaces become distorted and the energies can become complex-valued. This fact led some authors
(
Hättig 2005
;
Köhn and Tajti 2007
)
to question whether the method could be used to simulate excited state dynamics. As was recently shown by the authors and collaborators, however, the numerical artifacts of the method can be removed by constraining the electronic states to be orthogonal, which furthermore implies a correct intersection dimensionality (two directions lift the degeneracy) and a proper first-order behavior as the degeneracy is lifted (the intersections are conical).
(
Kjønstad et al. 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
These developments indicated that the modified coupled cluster method, known as similarity constrained coupled cluster (SCC)
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
theory, would be applicable in excited state nonadiabatic dynamics simulations.

Large-scale simulations require efficient implementations of the nuclear energy gradients and the nonadiabatic derivative coupling elements. In the case of coupled cluster energy gradients, both derivation techniques and efficient implementations are well-established and widespread.
(
Handy and Schaefer III 1984
;
Helgaker and Jørgensen 1988
;
Koch et al. 1990
;
Stanton and Gauss 1994
)
Less attention has been given to the nonadiabatic couplings. There have been two recent implementations at the coupled cluster singles and doubles level of theory (CCSD), although these arrive at the coupling elements through two different routes: one evaluates the couplings in terms of summed-state gradients
(
Tajti and Szalay 2009
;
Faraji et al. 2018
)
while the other (implemented by the authors) directly evaluates them by using standard Lagrangian/Z-vector techniques.
(
Christiansen 1999
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2023
)
There is some uncertainty about whether these are equivalent.
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2023
)

In this paper, we derive and implement analytical derivative couplings and energy gradients for the similarity constrained coupled cluster singles and doubles (SCCSD)
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
method, building on our recent CCSD implementations of analytical derivative coupling elements
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2023
)
and nuclear energy gradients.
(
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
)
Here we will focus on
implementation aspects.
In a recent study on thymine, we applied the implementation to perform the first nonadiabatic dynamics simulations with CCSD and SCCSD.
(
Kjønstad et al. 2024
)

2
Theory

2.1
Coupled cluster theory

In coupled cluster theory,
the ground state wave function is written as
Coester 1958
;
Bartlett and Musiał 2007

\displaystyle|\psi_{0}\rangle=\exp(\mathscr{T})|\mathrm{HF}\rangle,

(1)

where
\mathscr{T}
is called the cluster operator and the reference wave function
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
is usually taken to be the Hartree-Fock state. By applying the exponential operator, the mean-field wave function
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
is transformed into a correlated wave function
|\psi_{0}\rangle
that more accurately represents the electronic ground state.

In practice, the cluster operator
incorporates excitations only up to a given rank. For instance, it may include single and double excitations with respect to
|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
, in which case we obtain the coupled cluster with singles and doubles (CCSD)
(
Purvis III and Bartlett 1982
)
method. More generally, the cluster operator is written

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}=\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}}t_{\mu}\tau_{\mu}=T_{1}+T_{2}+\ldots+T_{n},

(2)

where
\mathscr{E}_{e}
denotes the included excitations, the subscript
k
in
T_{k}
denotes the excitation rank, and the sum truncates at a given excitation order
n
. For example,
\mathscr{T}=T_{1}+T_{2}
in CCSD. Each excitation operator
\tau_{\mu}
is weighted by an amplitude
t_{\mu}
, and this amplitude indicates the weight in
|\psi_{0}\rangle
of the associated configuration
|\mu\rangle=\tau_{\mu}|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
.

These cluster amplitudes are determined by projecting the Schrödinger equation onto the excitation subspace
\mathscr{E}_{e}
. We start from the Schrödinger equation for
|\psi_{0}\rangle
,

\displaystyle H|\psi_{0}\rangle=E_{0}|\psi_{0}\rangle,

(3)

where
H
denotes the electronic Hamiltonian,
|\psi_{0}\rangle
is given in Eq. (
1
), and
E_{0}
is the ground state energy. Next, we project the Schrödinger equation onto

\displaystyle\begin{split}\{\langle\mathrm{HF}|\exp(-\mathscr{T}),\langle\mu|\exp(-\mathscr{T})\},\end{split}

(4)

where
\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}
. This projection procedure gives a set of equations that determine
E_{0}
and
t_{\mu}
:

\displaystyle E_{0}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{HF}|\bar{H}|\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(5)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\langle\mu|\bar{H}|\mathrm{HF}\rangle=\Omega_{\mu},

(6)

where

\displaystyle\bar{H}=\exp(-\mathscr{T})H\exp(\mathscr{T}).

(7)

If we want to make the orbital dependence explicit, we can also write
(
Hald et al. 2003
)

\displaystyle\bar{H}=\exp(-\mathscr{T})\exp(\kappa)H\exp(-\kappa)\exp(\mathscr{T}),

(8)

where the orbital rotation operator is given by

\displaystyle\kappa=\sum_{ai}\kappa_{ai}(E_{ai}-E_{ia})=\sum_{ai}\kappa_{ai}E_{ai}^{-}.

(9)

Here,
E_{ai}
is the singlet excitation operator from the occupied orbital
i
to the virtual orbital
a
.
In calculations at a specific geometry,
\kappa=0
is normally understood to correspond to the Hartree-Fock orbitals, and we write
\bar{H}
as in Eq. (
7
). When we evaluate nuclear gradients, we need the latter form, Eq. (
8
), as
\kappa
will be different from zero when we make displacements away from the geometry where we wish to evaluate the nuclear gradient.

A peculiar feature of coupled cluster theory, which stems from the non-Hermiticity of
\bar{H}
, is the introduction of left (or bra) states that are different from the right (or ket) states. In particular,
|\psi_{0}\rangle^{\dagger}\neq\langle\psi_{0}|
. The left ground state does not have an exponential form, like in Eq. (
1
), but is instead expressed as

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}|=\Bigl(\langle\mathrm{HF}|+\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}}\bar{t}_{\mu}\langle\mu|\Bigr)\exp(-\mathscr{T}).

(10)

Nonetheless, like
|\psi_{0}\rangle
, it is also determined by projecting the Schrödinger equation. Starting from

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{0}|H=\langle\psi_{0}|E_{0},

(11)

we project onto

\displaystyle\{\exp(\mathscr{T})|\mathrm{HF}\rangle,\exp(\mathscr{T})|\mu\rangle\},

(12)

where
\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}
.
This results in an equation for
\bar{t}_{\mu}
:
(
Helgaker et al. 2013
)

\displaystyle 0=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},\tau_{\mu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\sum_{\nu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}}\bar{t}_{\nu}\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},\tau_{\mu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle.

(13)

The excited states can be described with two alternative but closely related approaches, the linear response
(
Koch and Jørgensen 1990
)
and equation of motion theories.
(
Stanton and Bartlett 1993
)
In the equation of motion approach, which we adopt here, the excited states are expressed as

\displaystyle|\psi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp(\mathscr{T})\Bigl(R_{0}^{n}|\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}}R_{\mu}^{n}|\mu\rangle\Bigr)

(14)

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n}|

\displaystyle=\Bigl(L_{0}^{n}\langle\mathrm{HF}|+\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}}L_{\mu}^{n}\langle\mu|\Bigr)\exp(-\mathscr{T}),

(15)

and these states are determined by the same projection procedure used
for the ground state. It will be convenient to define
|0\rangle=|\mathrm{HF}\rangle
so that
\mu=0
corresponds to the reference state and
\tau_{0}=1
. Then, if we let
\mathscr{E}
denote the set containing both
\mu=0
and
\mu\in\mathscr{E}_{e}
, we can write

\displaystyle|\psi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp(\mathscr{T})\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}R_{\mu}^{n}|\mu\rangle\Bigr)

(16)

\displaystyle\langle\psi_{n}|

\displaystyle=\Bigl(\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}L_{\mu}^{n}\langle\mu|\Bigr)\exp(-\mathscr{T}).

(17)

When we repeat the ground state projection procedure for the excited states, we find that the excited state amplitudes satisfy the eigenvalue equations

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{n}

\displaystyle=E_{n}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{n}

(18)

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}

\displaystyle=E_{n}\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}},

(19)

where

\displaystyle\bar{\mathcal{H}}_{\mu\nu}=\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nu\rangle,\quad\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{E},

(20)

i.e.,

\displaystyle\bar{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}=\begin{pmatrix}E_{0}&\bm{\eta}^{T}\\
\bm{0}&\bar{\bm{H}}\end{pmatrix},\quad\eta_{\nu}=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nu\rangle,\quad\bar{H}_{\mu\nu}=\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nu\rangle,\quad\mu,\nu\in\mathscr{E}_{e},

(21)

and

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}R_{n}^{0}\\
\bm{R}_{n}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}=\begin{pmatrix}L_{n}^{0}\;\bm{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}.

(22)

Here,
E_{n}
, with
n=1,2,\ldots
, denotes the excited state energy. Also the ground state wave functions satisfy the same eigenvalue equations, with

\displaystyle\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\
\bm{0}\end{pmatrix},\quad\bm{\mathcal{L}}_{0}^{\mathrm{T}}=\begin{pmatrix}1\;\bar{\bm{t}}^{\mathrm{T}}\end{pmatrix}.

(23)

Once
\langle\psi_{n}|
and
|\psi_{n}\rangle
have been determined,
we can evaluate the energy gradient
as

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{n}=\nabla\langle\psi_{n}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptH\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{n}\rangle

(24)

and the nonadiabatic derivative coupling vector as

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{mn}=\langle\psi_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nabla\psi_{n}\rangle.

(25)

These two quantities,
\bm{g}_{n}
and
\bm{d}_{mn}
, are of special interest in molecular dynamics. The gradient
\bm{g}_{n}
provides the force acting on the nuclei, while the derivative coupling
\bm{d}_{mn}
is responsible for nonadiabatic transitions between electronic states.

The derivative coupling vector diverges at conical intersections, where the electronic states are degenerate. This is easily seen in the exact limit, where we have

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{mn}=\langle\psi_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nabla\psi_{n}\rangle=\frac{\langle\psi_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt(\nabla H)\psi_{n}\rangle}{E_{m}-E_{n}}.

(26)

A proper description of such intersections is therefore essential if a method is to be applied in photochemical applications. Somewhat surprisingly, it turns out
(
Kjønstad et al. 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
that these intersections can only be described correctly if the method guarantees some sort of orthogonality between the excited states, something which is
not
true in standard coupled cluster theory.

2.2
Similarity constrained coupled cluster theory

In similarity constrained coupled cluster theory, which was designed for applications to nonadiabatic dynamics,
(
Kjønstad et al. 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
a set of electronic states are constrained to be orthogonal. Here, a subset of states
\mathscr{K}
are required to satisfy

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{kl}=\langle\psi_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathscr{P}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{l}\rangle=0,\quad k\neq l\in\mathscr{K},

(27)

where
\mathscr{P}
is a projection operator and so
\mathscr{O}_{kl}
is an approximation of
\langle\psi_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{l}\rangle
.
To enforce the orthogonality of the states, the cluster operator is expressed as

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}=\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}t_{\mu}\tau_{\mu}+\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\zeta_{kl}X_{kl},

(28)

where each
X_{kl}
is an excitation operator whose rank is higher than the
\mu
included in
\mathscr{E}
, and
\zeta_{kl}
enforces
\mathscr{O}_{kl}=0
.
Both
\mathscr{P}
and
X_{kl}
can be chosen in several different ways, leading to different variants of the theory.
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
Here we will assume that they only depend on the right excited states, so that

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}=\mathscr{P}(\{\mathcal{R}_{n}\}),\quad X_{kl}=X_{kl}(\mathcal{R}_{k},\mathcal{R}_{l}),

(29)

as this is required for the method to scale correctly with the size of the system (that is, to be size-extensive/size-intensive).
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
The similarity constrained coupled cluster method can be considered an extension of the standard coupled cluster method, since all the standard equations are kept unchanged. The only changes to the method are the additional
orthogonality conditions

\mathscr{O}_{kl}=0
and the additional excitation operators in
\mathscr{T}
.
Note, however, that the orthogonality conditions couple the ground state to the excited states in
\mathscr{K}
, implying that the ground and excited states must be determined simultaneously.

2.3
Nuclear gradients and derivative coupling elements

2.3.1
Lagrangians

As noted already, the nuclear gradients and derivative couplings are the two main components needed to simulate nonadiabatic excited state dynamics. We therefore need to know how to evaluate these elements efficiently in our approximate electronic structure methods. The generally adopted approach is to apply the Langragian method, also called the Z-vector method, which allows us to effectively evaluate gradients (for example, of the energy) by imposing a set of constraints on the parameters of the electronic structure method.
(
Handy and Schaefer III 1984
;
Helgaker and Jørgensen 1988
)

Its main advantage here is that it removes the need to explicitly evaluate the derivatives of these parameters, for example,
\mathrm{d}t_{\mu}/\mathrm{d}x
, where
x
is a nuclear coordinate. These derivatives are instead accounted for through the constraints and the associated Lagrangian multipliers.
If we assume that we want to evaluate the gradient of
E
with respect to
\bm{x}
, where
E
depends on a set of parameters
\bm{\lambda}
that can be determined by solving a set of equations
\bm{e}=0
, then the Lagrangian takes the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{L}(\bm{\lambda},\bar{\bm{\lambda}},\bm{x})=E(\bm{\lambda},\bm{x})+\sum_{p}\bar{\lambda}_{p}e_{p}(\bm{\lambda},\bm{x}),

(30)

where
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
contains the so-called Lagrangian multipliers.
The constraints (
\bm{e}=0
) are imposed by making the Lagrangian stationary with respect to the multipliers
\bar{\bm{\lambda}}
, while the multipliers are determined by making the Lagrangian stationary with respect to the parameters
\bm{\lambda}
. In other words, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\lambda_{p}}=0,\quad\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{p}}=e_{p}=0,

(31)

which implies that

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}E}{\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\mathrm{d}\mathscr{L}}{\mathrm{d}x}=\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial x}+\sum_{p}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\lambda_{p}}\frac{\partial\lambda_{p}}{\partial x}+\sum_{p}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{p}}\frac{\partial\bar{\lambda}_{p}}{\partial x}=\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}}{\partial x}.

(32)

The
total
derivative of
E
is replaced by the
partial
derivative of
\mathscr{L}
, and the latter contains no derivative of the parameters with respect to
\bm{x}
.

Our constraints are simply the equations that determine the parameters; for example, one set of constraints are the ground state equations
\Omega_{\mu}=0
, since these equations determine the amplitudes
t_{\mu}
. Each set of constraints is given a set of Lagrangian multipliers; for example, the ground state equations
\Omega_{\mu}=0
are associated with a set of multipliers
\bar{\zeta}_{\mu}
. Written out explicitly,
the nuclear gradient
\bm{g}_{n}
, where
n\in\mathscr{K}
, can be evaluated from the Lagrangian

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{L}_{n}&=E_{n}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(\bar{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}-E_{k})\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\\
&+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{E}_{k}(1-\langle\Lambda_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle)+\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{kl}\mathscr{O}_{kl}+\sum_{\mu}\bar{\zeta}_{\mu}\Omega_{\mu}+\sum_{ai}\bar{\kappa}_{ai}F_{ai},\end{split}

(33)

where
F
denotes the Fock matrix,
a
and
i
denote a virtual and an occupied orbital, respectively, and the energy of the
k
th state is expressed as

\displaystyle E_{k}=\langle\Lambda_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle.

(34)

Here,
\langle\Lambda_{k}|
is some (for now unspecified) vector that is normalized with respect to
|R_{k}\rangle
, that is,
\langle\Lambda_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle=1
.
Note that Eq. (
34
) will then be equal to the energy as a result of the eigenvalue equation in Eq. (
18
).
The first term in
\mathscr{L}_{n}
is the energy, and the rest are the Lagrangian constraints. The first and second constraints ensure that the states in
\mathscr{K}
are
eigenstates and that
E_{k}
is the energy of the
k
th state, while the third ensures that the states in
\mathscr{K}
are orthogonal (that is,
\mathscr{O}_{kl}=0
for
k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}
). The final two constraints are the ground state coupled cluster equations and the Hartree-Fock equations, which respectively determine the cluster amplitudes
t_{\mu}
and the molecular orbitals.

The Lagrangian for the derivative couplings is identical to that for the nuclear gradient (because the constraints
\bm{e}=0
are the same), except for the first term. The coupling
\bm{d}_{mn}
between
m,n\in\mathscr{K}
can be evaluated from

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{L}_{mn}&=O_{mn}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\bm{\mathcal{L}}}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}(\bar{\bm{\mathcal{H}}}-E_{k})\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{k}\\
&+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{E}_{k}(1-\langle\Lambda_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle)+\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{kl}\mathscr{O}_{kl}+\sum_{\mu}\bar{\zeta}_{\mu}\Omega_{\mu}+\sum_{ai}\bar{\kappa}_{ai}F_{ai},\end{split}

(35)

where, as proposed by Hohenstein,
(
Hohenstein 2016
)
we
define the bra-frozen overlap

\displaystyle O_{mn}=\langle\psi_{m}(\bm{x}_{0})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{n}(\bm{x})\rangle.

(36)

Here,
O_{mn}
introduces a parametric dependence on some reference geometry
\bm{x}_{0}
. This means that this Lagrangian can be used to evaluate the coupling exactly at
\bm{x}_{0}
and not at any other value of
\bm{x}
. However, since
\bm{x}_{0}
can be chosen freely, this implies no loss of generality.

2.3.2
Expressions for the gradient and couplings

Once the multipliers are known, we can evaluate the gradients and couplings as

\displaystyle\bm{g}_{n}

\displaystyle=\nabla E_{n}=\frac{\mathrm{d}E_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}=\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial\bm{x}}

(37)

and

\displaystyle\bm{d}_{mn}

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nabla\psi_{n}\rangle=\Big\langle\psi_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\mathrm{d}\psi_{n}}{\mathrm{d}\bm{x}}\Big\rangle=\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{mn}}{\partial\bm{x}}.

(38)

Let us first write out the gradient expression in detail. Note that when we take the partial derivative of the Lagrangian, we can treat the parameters as constants as their dependence on the nuclear coordinates is included in the multiplier terms. In addition, the dependence of the creation and annihilation operators on
\bm{x}
can be ignored, see Ref.
17
. Thus, the only explicit nuclear dependence resides in the Hamiltonian integrals.
We will denote the nuclear derivatives
\{\partial H/\partial x_{q}|_{0}\}
as
H^{(1)}
and let

\displaystyle\bar{H}^{(1)}=\exp(-\mathscr{T})\exp(\kappa)H^{(1)}\exp(-\kappa)\exp(\mathscr{T}),

(39)

where
\kappa=0
corresponds to the Hartree-Fock orbitals at
\bm{x}_{0}
.
Here, the Hamiltonian
H
is expressed in terms of a set of orthonormal molecular orbitals (OMOs) that are related to the so-called unmodified MOs or UMOs (defined as the MOs obtained from the MO coefficients at
\bm{x}_{0}
and the AOs at
\bm{x}
) through an orbital connection matrix. In the case of the symmetric connection, which we adopt here, we have

\displaystyle H^{(1)}=H^{[1]}-\frac{1}{2}\{S^{[1]},H\},

(40)

where
H^{[1]}
and
S^{[1]}
refer to the derivative of the UMO Hamiltonian and overlap, and
\{A,B\}
denotes the operator obtained by one-index transformations of
A
and
B
.
The latter term in Eq. (
40
) leads to the “reorthonormalization terms” in the gradient. We refer to the literature for more details on orbital connections.
(
Olsen et al. 1995
)

Since the derivative only acts on the Hamiltonian, it is convenient to introduce some further notation. In particular, if we let

\displaystyle\begin{split}\langle K_{k}|&=(\delta_{kn}-\langle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle)\langle\Lambda_{k}|+\langle\bar{L}_{k}|,\end{split}

(41)

the Lagrangian becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathscr{L}_{n}&=\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}(\langle K_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle+\bar{L}_{0}^{k}\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle)\\
&\quad+\langle\bar{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\sum_{ai}\bar{\kappa}_{ai}F_{ai}\\
&\quad+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{E}_{k}(1-\langle\Lambda_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle)+\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{kl}\mathscr{O}_{kl}\end{split}

(42)

and the nuclear gradient becomes

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{g}_{n}&=\mathscr{L}_{n}^{(1)}=\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}(\langle K_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle+\bar{L}_{0}^{k}\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle)\\
&\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad+\langle\bar{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\sum_{ai}\bar{\kappa}_{ai}F_{ai}^{(1)}.\end{split}

(43)

Note that, when we apply the derivative, the last two terms in
\mathscr{L}_{n}
vanish because there is no
H
-dependence in these terms and so the partial derivatives are zero. The expression for
\bm{g}_{n}
found here is similar to the standard coupled cluster case,
(
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
)
except that there are additional excited state terms which appear due to the coupling of states in
\mathscr{K}
that arises due to the orthogonality conditions (in this case, the
\langle K_{k}|
and
\bar{L}^{k}_{0}
terms with
k\neq n
).

The expression for the derivative coupling is similar. In particular, we find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{d}_{mn}=\mathscr{L}_{mn}^{(1)}&=O_{mn}^{(1)}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}(\langle K_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle+\bar{L}_{0}^{k}\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle)\\
&\quad\quad+\langle\bar{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\sum_{ai}\bar{\kappa}_{ai}F_{ai}^{(1)},\end{split}

(44)

where we instead have

\displaystyle\langle K_{k}|=-\langle\bar{\mathcal{L}}_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle\langle\Lambda_{k}|+\langle\bar{L}_{k}|.

(45)

Both
\bm{g}_{n}
and
\bm{d}_{mn}
consist of several terms that are transition elements of the gradient Hamiltonian
\bar{H}^{(1)}
. These are conveniently calculated in terms of density matrices; for general
\langle\mathcal{L}|
and
|\mathcal{R}\rangle
, we have

\displaystyle\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{(1)}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle=\sum_{pq}h_{pq}^{(1)}d_{pq}(\bm{\mathcal{L}},\bm{\mathcal{R}})+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}g_{pqrs}^{(1)}d_{pqrs}(\bm{\mathcal{L}},\bm{\mathcal{R}})+\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle h_{\mathrm{nuc}}^{(1)},

(46)

where

\displaystyle d_{pq}(\bm{\mathcal{L}},\bm{\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\exp(-\mathscr{T})E_{pq}\exp(\mathscr{T})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle

(47)

\displaystyle d_{pqrs}(\bm{\mathcal{L}},\bm{\mathcal{R}})

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\exp(-\mathscr{T})e_{pqrs}\exp(\mathscr{T})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle,\quad e_{pqrs}=E_{pq}E_{rs}-\delta_{qr}E_{ps},

(48)

and where we have used that the Hamiltonian can be expressed as

\displaystyle H=\sum_{pq}h_{pq}E_{pq}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pqrs}g_{pqrs}e_{pqrs}+h_{\mathrm{nuc}}.

(49)

In the above equations,
h_{pq}
and
g_{pqrs}
denote the one- and two-electron Hamiltonian integrals, respectively, and
h_{\mathrm{nuc}}
denotes the nuclear repulsion energy. The indices
p,q,r,
and
s
denote general molecular orbitals.

For both the gradient and the coupling elements, we evaluate the reorthonormalization terms by substituting
H^{(1)}
in Eq. (
40
) into
\bm{g}_{n}
and
\bm{d}_{mn}
and collecting the contributions that stem from
\{S^{[1]},H\}
. The end-result can be written as a contraction of
S^{[1]}
with a generalized Fock matrix. These terms are identical in CCSD and SCCSD (albeit with modified density matrices) and we refer to the literature for the detailed expressions.
(
Hald et al. 2003
;
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
)

As in other electronic structure methods,
\bm{d}_{mn}
can be subdivided into two terms, one that can induce transitions by translational motion (by violating the sum rule
(
Fatehi et al. 2011
)
) and one that does not induce such transitions. The term in
\bm{d}_{mn}
that violates the sum rule is
O_{mn}^{(1)}
, which can be expressed as

\displaystyle O_{mn}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{pq}d_{pq}(\mathcal{L}_{m},\mathcal{R}_{n})(W^{(1)}_{pq}-W^{(1)}_{qp}),

(50)

where

\displaystyle W^{(1)}_{pq}=\sum_{\alpha\beta}C_{\alpha p}C_{\beta q}\langle\chi_{\alpha}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\chi_{\beta}^{(1)}\rangle.

(51)

Here,
C_{\alpha p},C_{\beta q}
denote the molecular orbital coefficients and
\chi_{\alpha},\chi_{\beta}
denote atomic orbitals.
For a derivation of this expression for
O_{mn}^{(1)}
, see, e.g., Ref.
23
.

2.3.3
Stationarity equations: nuclear gradient

Evaluating the gradient and couplings is only possible after we have determined the Lagrangian multipliers. Let us begin with the gradient Lagrangian
\mathscr{L}_{n}
. The multipliers are determined from the stationarity equations, which involve derivatives with respect to the orbital rotation parameters,
\kappa_{ai}
, the cluster amplitudes,
t_{\mu}
, and the excited state amplitudes,
R_{\mu}^{k}
and
\zeta_{kl}
.

It turns out that the stationarity equations have a simpler form if the lambda states,
\langle\Lambda_{k}|
, at
\bm{x}_{0}
, are chosen to be equal to the left excited state vectors, that is, if

\displaystyle\langle\Lambda_{k}|=\langle L_{k}|,

(52)

and we will assume this in the remainder of the text.
Let us start by considering stationarity with respect to
\langle\Lambda_{m}|
, which will allow us to determine the multipliers
\bar{E}_{m}
. In fact, since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial\Lambda_{\mu}^{m}}\Big|_{0}=0=R_{\mu}^{m}\Big(E_{m}(\delta_{mn}-\langle\mathcal{\bar{L}}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{m}\rangle)-\bar{E}_{m}\Big),

(53)

we can ensure stationarity by setting

\displaystyle\bar{E}_{m}=E_{m}(\delta_{mn}-\langle\mathcal{\bar{L}}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{m}\rangle).

(54)

In addition, it turns out that the multipliers relating to the Hartree-Fock contribution to
the excited states, that is,
\bar{L}^{k}_{0}
, can be expressed in terms of the orthogonality multipliers
\bar{\gamma}_{kl}
. In particular, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial R_{0}^{m}}\Big|_{0}=0=-\omega_{m}\bar{L}_{0}^{m}+\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{kl}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{kl}}{\partial R_{0}^{m}},

(55)

where
\omega_{m}=E_{m}-E_{0}
, and, therefore,

\displaystyle\bar{L}_{0}^{m}=\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{kl}\Bigl(\frac{1}{\omega_{m}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{kl}}{\partial R_{0}^{m}}\Bigr).

(56)

The remaining multipliers (
\bm{\bar{\zeta}},\bm{\bar{L}}_{k},\bar{\gamma}_{kl},\bm{\bar{\kappa}}
) are non-redundant and are determined by
a linear system of equations that can be solved with standard techniques, for example, the Davidson algorithm.
(
Davidson 1975
)
But we can make one more simplification to this set of equations. The orbital multipliers (
\bm{\bar{\kappa}}
) depend on the ground and excited state multipliers (
\bm{\bar{\zeta}},\bm{\bar{L}}_{k},\bar{\gamma}_{kl}
), but not the other way around. Hence, we can determine the ground and excited state multipliers without first knowing the orbital multipliers.

Let us therefore first consider the response equations for the ground and excited state multipliers (
\bm{\bar{\zeta}},\bm{\bar{L}}_{k},\bar{\gamma}_{kl}
). For the cluster amplitude stationarity, we find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial t_{\mu}}\Big|_{0}&=0=\sum_{\nu}L^{n}_{\nu}\bar{F}(\bm{R}_{n})_{\nu\mu}+\sum_{\nu}\bar{\zeta}_{\nu}A_{\nu\mu}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\sum_{\nu\tau}\bar{L}^{k}_{\nu}P^{k}_{\nu\tau}\bar{F}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\tau\mu}\\
&+\sum_{l\neq j\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{lj}\Big\{\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{lj}}{\partial t_{\mu}}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{lj}}{\partial R^{k}_{0}}\Big(R^{k}_{0}\big(\eta_{\mu}-\sum_{\nu}L^{k}_{\nu}\bar{F}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\nu\mu}\big)+\sum_{\nu}R^{k}_{\nu}F^{0}_{\nu\mu}\Big)\Big\},\end{split}

(57)

where we have defined the various
\bm{t}
-derivative terms

\displaystyle A_{\nu\mu}=\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},\tau_{\mu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle=\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mu\rangle-E_{0}

(58)

\displaystyle\bar{F}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\nu\mu}=\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},\tau_{\mu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle

(59)

\displaystyle\eta_{\mu}=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\bar{H},\tau_{\mu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(60)

\displaystyle F^{0}_{\nu\mu}=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[[\bar{H},\tau_{\nu}],\tau_{\mu}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle,

(61)

as well as a projection matrix that removes components along the
k
th excited state:

\displaystyle P^{k}_{\nu\tau}=\delta_{\nu\tau}-R^{k}_{\nu}L^{k}_{\tau}.

(62)

In matrix notation, the stationarity equation can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{0}&=\bm{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\bm{F}}(\bm{R}_{n})+\bar{\bm{\zeta}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{A}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\bm{L}}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{P}_{k}\bar{\bm{F}}(\bm{R}_{k})\\
&+\sum_{l\neq j\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{lj}\Big\{\nabla_{t}\mathscr{O}_{lj}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{lj}}{\partial R^{k}_{0}}\Big(R^{k}_{0}(\bm{\eta}^{\mathrm{T}}-\bm{L}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bar{\bm{F}}(\bm{R}_{k}))+\bm{R}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{F}_{0}\Big)\Big\}.\end{split}

(63)

Note that Eq. (
63
) consists of four terms, the first being a constant term, since it does not depend on any of the multipliers, while the remaining three terms are linear in
\bm{\bar{\zeta}},\bm{\bar{L}}_{k}
, and
\bar{\gamma}_{kl}
, respectively. This will also be the case for the two next stationarity conditions.

For the excited state amplitude stationarity, we find that

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial R^{m}_{\mu}}\Big|_{0}=0=\sum_{\nu}L^{n}_{\nu}Y^{m}(\bm{R}_{n})_{\nu\mu}+\sum_{\nu}\bar{\zeta}_{\nu}Y^{m}_{\nu\mu}\\
&+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\sum_{\nu}\bar{L}^{k}_{\nu}\Big\{\delta_{km}(A_{\nu\mu}-\omega_{m}\delta_{\nu\mu})+\sum_{\tau}P^{k}_{\nu\tau}Y^{m}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\tau\mu}\Big\}\\
&+\sum_{l\neq j\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{lj}\Big\{\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{lj}}{\partial R^{m}_{\mu}}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{lj}}{\partial R^{k}_{0}}\Big(\delta_{km}\eta_{\mu}+R^{k}_{0}\big(Y^{m,0}_{\mu}-\sum_{\nu}L^{k}_{\nu}Y^{m}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\nu\mu}\big)+Y^{m,0}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\mu}\Big)\Big\},\end{split}

(64)

where we have defined the various
\bm{R}_{m}
-derivative terms

\displaystyle Y^{m}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\nu\mu}=\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY^{m}_{\mu}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle,\quad Y^{m}_{\mu}=\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial R^{m}_{\mu}},

(65)

\displaystyle Y^{m}_{\nu\mu}=\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY^{m}_{\mu}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(66)

\displaystyle Y^{m,0}_{\mu}=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(67)

\displaystyle Y^{m,0}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\mu}=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle.

(68)

In matrix notation, this stationarity equation can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{0}&=\bm{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{m}(\bm{R}_{n})+\bar{\bm{\zeta}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{m}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\bm{L}}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bigl\{\delta_{km}(\bm{A}-\omega_{m})+\bm{P}_{k}\bm{Y}_{m}(\bm{R}_{k})\bigr\}\\
&+\sum_{l\neq j\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{lj}\Big\{\nabla_{R_{m}}\mathscr{O}_{lj}+\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\frac{1}{\omega_{k}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{lj}}{\partial R^{k}_{0}}\Big(\delta_{km}\bm{\eta}^{\mathrm{T}}+R^{k}_{0}\big(\bm{Y}_{m,0}^{\mathrm{T}}-\bm{L}_{k}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Y}_{m}(\bm{R}_{k})\big)+\bm{Y}_{m,0}(\bm{R}_{k})^{\mathrm{T}}\Big)\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(69)

Finally, for
\zeta_{kl}
, we find

\displaystyle\begin{split}\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial\zeta_{kl}}\Big|_{0}&=0=\sum_{\mu\nu}L^{n}_{\mu}Z^{kl}_{\mu\nu}R^{n}_{\nu}+\sum_{\mu}\bar{\zeta}_{\mu}z^{kl}_{\mu}+\sum_{j\in\mathscr{K}}\sum_{\mu\nu\tau}\bar{L}^{j}_{\mu}P^{j}_{\mu\tau}Z^{kl}_{\tau\nu}R^{j}_{\nu}\\
&+\sum_{i\neq p\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{ip}\Big\{\sum_{j\in\mathscr{K}}\frac{1}{\omega_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{ip}}{\partial R^{j}_{0}}\Big(R_{0}^{j}\big(z_{kl}-\sum_{\mu\nu}L^{j}_{\mu}Z^{kl}_{\mu\nu}R^{j}_{\nu}\big)+\tilde{z}^{kl}_{\mu}R_{\mu}^{j}\Big)\Big\},\end{split}

(70)

where

\displaystyle Z^{kl}_{\mu\nu}=\Big\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta_{kl}}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\nu\Big\rangle,\quad z^{kl}_{\mu}=\Big\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta_{kl}}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\Big\rangle,\quad\tilde{z}^{kl}_{\nu}=\Big\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta_{kl}}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\nu\Big\rangle,\quad z_{kl}=\Big\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta_{kl}}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\Big\rangle

(71)

In matrix notation, this stationarity equation can be written

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bm{0}&=\bm{L}_{n}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Z}_{kl}\bm{R}_{n}+\bar{\bm{\zeta}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{z}_{kl}+\sum_{j\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\bm{L}}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{P}_{j}\bm{Z}_{kl}\bm{R}_{j}\\
&\quad\quad\quad+\sum_{i\neq p\in\mathscr{K}}\bar{\gamma}_{ip}\Big\{\sum_{j\in\mathscr{K}}\frac{1}{\omega_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{ip}}{\partial R^{j}_{0}}\Big(R^{j}_{0}(z_{kl}-\bm{L}_{j}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{Z}_{kl}\bm{R}_{j})+\tilde{\bm{z}}_{kl}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{R}_{j}\Big)\Bigr\}.\end{split}

(72)

This concludes our presentation of the ground and excited state response equations. To summarize, Eqs. (
63
), (
69
), and (
72
) form a coupled set of linear equations that can be solved using standard techniques. The next step is to determine the orbital multipliers.

In the case of orbital stationarity, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{L}_{n}}{\partial\kappa_{ai}}\Big|_{0}=0=\eta^{\kappa}_{ai}+\sum_{bj}\bar{\kappa}_{bj}A_{bjai}^{\mathrm{HF}},

(73)

where

\displaystyle A_{bjai}^{\mathrm{HF}}=\frac{\partial F_{bj}}{\partial\kappa_{ai}}\Big|_{0},

(74)

and where

\displaystyle\eta^{\kappa}_{ai}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\in\mathscr{K}}\Bigl(\langle K_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{\kappa}_{ai}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle+\bar{L}^{k}_{0}\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{\kappa}_{ai}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle\Bigr)+\langle\bar{\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}^{\kappa}_{ai}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle,

(75)

with

\displaystyle\bar{H}^{\kappa}_{ai}

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\kappa_{ai}}\Big|_{0}=\exp(-\mathscr{T})[E_{ai}^{-},H]\exp(\mathscr{T}).

(76)

Expressions for
\bm{A}^{\mathrm{HF}}
can be found elsewhere.
(
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
)
The terms in
\bm{\eta}_{\kappa}
can be expressed in terms of densities. In fact, for general
\langle\mathcal{L}|
and
|\mathcal{R}\rangle
, we have
(
Hald et al. 2003
)

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\bar{H}_{ai}^{\kappa}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle=-\mathscr{P}_{ai}^{-}\Bigl(\sum_{t}h_{at}\bigl(d_{ti}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})+d_{it}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})\bigr)\\
&\quad\quad+\sum_{trs}g_{atrs}\bigl(d_{itrs}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})+d_{tirs}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})\bigr)\Bigr),\end{split}

(77)

where

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}_{ai}^{-}X_{ai}=X_{ai}-X_{ia}.

(78)

2.3.4
Stationarity equations: derivative coupling

Looking at the gradient and coupling Lagrangians,
\mathscr{L}_{n}
and
\mathscr{L}_{mn}
, we see that they only differ in
the first term, which is equal to
E_{n}
in
\mathscr{L}_{n}
and
O_{mn}
in
\mathscr{L}_{mn}
; see Eqs. (
33
) and (
35
).
As a result, the differences in the stationarity equations only stem from this first term. The other terms are identical.
Let us start by writing out the bra-frozen overlap:

\displaystyle\begin{split}O_{mn}&=\langle\psi_{m}(\bm{x}_{0})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{n}(\bm{x})\rangle\\
&=\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\exp(-\mathscr{T})\exp(\kappa)\big|_{0}\exp(-\kappa)\exp(\mathscr{T})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\rangle.\end{split}

(79)

In the case of the ground state amplitudes, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial t_{\mu}}\Big|_{0}=\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\tau_{\mu}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\rangle=L^{m}_{\mu}R_{0}^{n}+\langle L_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\tau_{\mu}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{n}\rangle

(80)

Similarly, for the excited state amplitudes, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial R^{k}_{0}}\Big|_{0}=\delta_{kn}\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle=0,

(81)

and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial R^{k}_{\mu}}\Big|_{0}

\displaystyle=\delta_{kn}L^{m}_{\mu}+\sum_{l\neq j\in\mathscr{K}}\zeta_{lj}\Big\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial X_{lj}}{\partial R^{k}_{\mu}}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\Big\rangle

(82)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial\zeta_{lj}}\Big|_{0}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptX_{lj}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\rangle.

(83)

Finally, for the orbital rotation parameters, we find that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial\kappa_{ai}}\Big|_{0}=-\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\exp(-\mathscr{T})E_{ai}^{-}\exp(\mathscr{T})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\rangle=-\mathscr{P}_{ai}^{-}d_{ai}(\mathcal{L}_{m},\mathcal{R}_{n}).

(84)

More details about the stationarity equations for the coupling are given in Supporting Information S1.

As the stationarity equations for the coupling are identical to the ones for the gradient, except for the constant term, the same algorithm can be used to solve for the multipliers. The only required modifications consist in
appropriately modifying the constant term, which arises from partial derivatives either of
E_{n}
(for the gradient) or
O_{mn}
(for the coupling), as well as appropriately choosing the definition of
\langle K_{k}|
, see Eqs. (
41
) and (
45
). Furthermore, once the multipliers have been determined, the nuclear derivatives in the coupling can also be evaluated by the same implementation as the gradient, with the exception of
O_{mn}^{(1)}
.

2.4
Similarity constrained coupled cluster singles and doubles method

The expressions derived so far apply to any level of theory for the similarity constrained coupled cluster method. In the remainder of the paper, we will focus on the specifics at the singles and doubles level of theory.

2.4.1
Method

For the similarity constrained coupled cluster singles and doubles method, we define the cluster operator as

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}=T_{1}+T_{2}+\sum_{k\neq l\in\mathscr{K}}\zeta_{kl}X_{3}^{kl},

(85)

where

\displaystyle X_{3}^{kl}=\mathscr{P}_{kl}^{-}R^{k}_{1}R^{l}_{2}=R_{1}^{k}R_{2}^{l}-R_{1}^{l}R_{2}^{k}

(86)

and where we have defined the one- and two-electron state excitation operators

\displaystyle R^{k}_{1}=\sum_{\mu_{1}}R^{k}_{\mu_{1}}\tau_{\mu_{1}},\quad R^{k}_{2}=\sum_{\mu_{2}}R^{k}_{\mu_{2}}\tau_{\mu_{2}}.

(87)

Together with a choice of projection operator
\mathscr{P}
in the orthogonality relations, see Eq. (
27
), this defines the similarity constrained singles and doubles (SCCSD) method.
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)

Several choices of
\mathscr{P}
are possible. The most obvious choice is the one we will refer to as the natural projection, where we simply project onto the excitation subspace
\mathscr{E}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}=\sum_{\mu\in\mathscr{E}}|\mu\rangle\langle\mu|.

(88)

In the case of SCCSD, this operator will project onto the subspace defined by the Hartree-Fock reference as well as all single and double excitations out of this reference. While this choice of
\mathscr{P}
yields the correct untruncated limit, where
\mathscr{P}=\mathbb{I}
, it also gives rise to non-zero changes in the excitation energies of non-interacting subsystems, i.e., it is not fully size-intensive.
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
We use the shorthand SCCSD(
\mathscr{E}
) for this choice of
\mathscr{P}
.

Another choice is what we will refer to as the state projection, where we project onto the subspace of states
\mathscr{K}
,

\displaystyle\mathscr{P}=\sum_{k,l\in\mathscr{K}}|\mathcal{R}_{k}\rangle\langle\mathcal{R}_{k}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{l}\rangle^{-1}\langle\mathcal{R}_{l}|.

(89)

In this case, we preserve size-intensivity but at the cost of projecting onto a smaller subspace. The correct limit is still formally satisfied, however, since
\mathscr{P}=\mathbb{I}
once all states are included in
\mathscr{K}
. We use the shorthand SCCSD(
\mathscr{K}
) for this second choice of
\mathscr{P}
.

As we will show below, the choice of projection appears to often have a small impact on the obtained gradients and derivative coupling elements. For a description of other possible projection operators, see Supporting Information S2.

2.4.2
Implementation

Here we discuss the main aspects of the implementation. For a more detailed description, including programmable expressions, we refer the reader to Supporting Information S3–S5. The following discussion is meant to provide a general overview of how the gradients and coupling elements can be implemented, starting from an existing CCSD implementation.

Our implementation is restricted to two states, and so we will assume that
\mathscr{K}=\{a,b\}
and suppress the subindices “
ab
”, writing
\zeta
instead of
\zeta_{ab}
. The cluster operator can then be expressed as

\displaystyle\mathscr{T}=T_{1}+T_{2}+\zeta X_{3},\quad X_{3}=\mathscr{P}_{ab}^{-}R^{a}_{1}R^{b}_{2}.

(90)

To relate the CCSD and SCCSD implementations, it is particularly useful to note that

\displaystyle\begin{split}\bar{H}&=\exp(-\mathscr{T})H_{\kappa}\exp(\mathscr{T})\\
&=\exp(-\zeta X_{3})\bar{H}_{T}\exp(\zeta X_{3})\\
&=\bar{H}_{T}+\zeta[\hat{H},X_{3}]+\ldots\end{split}

(91)

where

\displaystyle H_{\kappa}

\displaystyle=\exp(\kappa)H\exp(-\kappa)

(92)

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{T}

\displaystyle=\exp(-T)H_{\kappa}\exp(T)

(93)

\displaystyle\hat{H}

\displaystyle=\exp(-T_{1})H_{\kappa}\exp(T_{1}).

(94)

Here,
\bar{H}_{T}
is the similarity transformed Hamiltonian at the CCSD level of theory, and
\hat{H}
is the so-called
T_{1}
-transformed Hamiltonian. The higher-order commutators do not contribute to any equations because of their high excitation rank. The SCCSD corrections arise solely from
\zeta[\hat{H},X_{3}]
.

These corrections have already been implemented for the ground and excited state equations, and we refer the reader to the original SCCSD paper, where we also provide expressions for the orthogonality conditions.
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
)
Here, we will only consider the changes that are required for the nuclear energy gradient and the derivative couplings, and these can be subdivided into two categories: the density matrices and the response vectors.

In the case of the density matrices, we need to evaluate the corrections

\displaystyle\Delta d_{pq}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle=\zeta\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[E_{pq},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle

(95)

\displaystyle\Delta d_{pqrs}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle=\zeta\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[e_{pqrs},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle,

(96)

where we
can again apply rank considerations. Since
[E_{pq},X_{3}]
is at least a double excitation, we
only have one non-zero block:

\displaystyle d_{ia}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})=\zeta\langle L_{2}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[E_{ia},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle R_{0}.

(97)

The same reasoning for the two-electron density leads to three distinct non-zero blocks:

\displaystyle\Delta d_{ijka}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle=\zeta\langle L_{2}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[e_{ijka},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle R_{0}

(98)

\displaystyle\Delta d_{bcka}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle=\zeta\langle L_{2}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[e_{bcka},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle R_{0}

(99)

\displaystyle\Delta d_{iajb}(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{R})

\displaystyle=\zeta\big(\langle L_{1}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[e_{iajb},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle R_{0}+\langle L_{2}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[e_{iajb},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{1}\rangle\big).

(100)

Expressions for these density terms are given in Supporting Information S3.

The corrections to the response vectors arise because of the
R_{k}
and
\zeta
-dependence of
X_{3}
, as well as the orthogonality conditions. For example, in the case of the
R_{k}
-dependence, we need to consider the
Y^{k}_{\mu}
operator, see Eq. (
65
), which for SCCSD becomes

\displaystyle Y^{k}_{\mu}=\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial R^{k}_{\mu}}=\zeta\Bigl[\hat{H},\frac{\partial X_{3}}{\partial R^{k}_{\mu}}\Bigr]+\ldots,

(101)

where we again find that the higher-order commutators do not contribute to any of the equations. By inserting the leading term of
Y^{k}_{\mu}
, and focusing on matrix transformations (that is, the action of matrices on vectors), we find that

\displaystyle\sigma^{m}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\mu}=\sum_{\nu}L_{\nu}Y^{m}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\nu\mu}

\displaystyle=\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle=\langle L_{2}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR^{k}_{1}\rangle,

(102)

where we have used rank-considerations to simplify:
[\hat{H},\partial X_{3}/\partial R^{k}_{\mu}]
consists of single excitations and higher as the derivative of
X_{3}
is a triple excitation operator and
\hat{H}
is a two-electron operator. Similarly, we find that

\displaystyle\sigma^{m}_{\mu}=\sum_{\nu}L_{\nu}Y^{m}_{\nu\mu}=\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle=\langle L_{1}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle+\langle L_{2}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle

(103)

and

\displaystyle Y_{\mu}^{m,0}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle=0

(104)

\displaystyle Y^{m,0}(\bm{R}_{k})_{\mu}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY_{\mu}^{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR_{k}\rangle=0.

(105)

Note that the
\bm{R}_{k}
-dependence in the the bra-frozen overlap derivative similarly vanishes due to rank considerations, in particular,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial R^{k}_{\mu}}\Big|_{0}

\displaystyle=\delta_{kn}L^{m}_{\mu}+\zeta\Big\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial X_{3}}{\partial R^{k}_{\mu}}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\Big\rangle=\delta_{kn}L^{m}_{\mu}.

(106)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial O_{mn}}{\partial\zeta}\Big|_{0}

\displaystyle=\langle\mathcal{L}_{m}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptX_{3}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}_{n}\rangle=0

(107)

For more details on the
\bm{R}_{k}
-derivative terms, see Supporting Information S4.

Considering the
\zeta
-dependence of
X_{3}
, we find that we can reuse
Y^{k}_{\mu}
terms:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\mu\nu}L_{\mu}Z_{\mu\nu}R_{\nu}&=\Big\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006ptR\Big\rangle\\
&=\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\hat{H},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR\rangle\\
&=\zeta^{-1}\sum_{\nu}\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY^{k}_{\nu}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR\rangle R^{k}_{\nu}\\
&=\zeta^{-1}\sum_{\nu}\sigma^{k}(\bm{R})_{\nu}R_{\nu}^{k},\end{split}

(108)

and, similarly,

\displaystyle\begin{split}\sum_{\nu}L_{\nu}z_{\nu}&=\Big\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\Big\rangle\\
&=\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\hat{H},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle\\
&=\zeta^{-1}\sum_{\nu}\langle L\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptY^{k}_{\nu}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathrm{HF}\rangle R^{k}_{\nu}\\
&=\zeta^{-1}\sum_{\nu}\sigma^{k}_{\nu}R_{\nu}^{k}.\end{split}

(109)

Finally, the reference-to-excited components vanish due to rank-considerations:

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu}\tilde{z}_{\nu}R_{\nu}=\Big\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006pt\frac{\partial\bar{H}}{\partial\zeta}\hskip 1.00006pt\Big|\hskip 1.00006ptR\Big\rangle=\langle\mathrm{HF}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt[\hat{H},X_{3}]\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006ptR\rangle=0.

(110)

The orthogonalities also contribute to the response vectors, as we need to evaluate the derivatives of these conditions with respect to
T
,
R_{k}
, and
R^{k}_{0}
. For this purpose, it is convenient to write out the orthogonality relation, where we take SCCSD(
\mathscr{E}
) as an example:

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{ab}=\langle\psi_{a}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathscr{P}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\psi_{b}\rangle=\sum_{\mu\nu\tau\in\mathscr{E}}\mathcal{R}^{a}_{\mu}\langle\mu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\exp(T^{\dagger})\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\nu\rangle\langle\nu\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\exp(T)\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\tau\rangle\mathcal{R}^{b}_{\tau}=\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{b}.

(111)

Note that we use
T
here instead of
\mathscr{T}
; since there are no non-zero contributions to
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}
that arise from the triple excitation in
\mathscr{T}
.
Now,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{ab}}{\partial\mathcal{R}^{b}_{\mu}}=(\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{a}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathcal{Q}})_{\mu},\quad\frac{\partial\mathscr{O}_{ab}}{\partial\mathcal{R}^{a}_{\mu}}=(\bm{\mathcal{Q}}^{\mathrm{T}}\bm{\mathcal{Q}}\bm{\mathcal{R}}_{b})_{\mu},

(112)

while the
T
derivatives act on
\bm{\mathcal{Q}}
and require us to consider terms like

\displaystyle\sum_{\nu\tau\in\mathscr{E}}\mathcal{L}_{\nu}\frac{\partial\mathcal{Q}_{\nu\tau}}{\partial t_{\mu}}\mathcal{R}_{\tau}=\langle\mathcal{L}\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\tau_{\mu}\exp(T)\hskip 1.00006pt|\hskip 1.00006pt\mathcal{R}\rangle,

(113)

where
\langle\mathcal{L}|
and
|\mathcal{R}\rangle
denote general vectors.
The same terms arise in the case of SCCSD(
\mathscr{K}
). Further details about the evaluation of these
T
derivative terms are given in Supporting Information S5.

3
Results and discussion

The gradients and derivative coupling elements have been implemented in a development version of the
e^{T}
program.
(
Folkestad et al. 2020
)
For comparisons of numerical and analytical gradients, see Supporting Information S6. Here, we will illustrate our implementation by locating minimum energy conical intersections (MECIs) for formaldimine and thymine and evaluating derivative coupling elements for lithium hydride.

3.1
Minimum energy conical intersections

We apply the algorithm by Bearpark et al.
(
Bearpark et al. 1994
)
to locate the MECIs. This algorithm constructs a gradient
\bm{G}
that is zero when two conditions are fulfilled: (a) the energy difference between the two intersecting states vanishes, and (b) the energy gradient along the intersection seam is zero. In particular,

\displaystyle\bm{G}=\mathcal{P}_{gh^{\perp}}\nabla E_{2}+2(E_{2}-E_{1})\frac{\bm{g}}{||\bm{g}||},

(114)

where

\displaystyle\bm{g}=\nabla(E_{2}-E_{1})=\bm{g}_{2}-\bm{g}_{1}

(115)

and
\mathcal{P}_{gh^{\perp}}
projects onto the complement of the
\bm{g}
-
\bm{h}
plane, where

\displaystyle\bm{h}=(E_{2}-E_{1})\bm{d}_{12}.

(116)

Note that
\bm{g}
and
\bm{h}
define the directions along which the degeneracy is lifted.
In Eq. (
114
), the second term in
\bm{G}
vanishes when the energy difference between the states vanishes. The first term minimizes the energy of the upper surface along the seam by projecting out the components that lift the degeneracy (i.e., by projecting out the
\bm{g}
-
\bm{h}
plane). The gradient
\bm{G}
is used in combination with an existing Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Fanno (BFGS) algorithm.
(
Folkestad et al. 2020
)

While these results show good agreement with literature,
(
Taylor et al. 2023
)
we also find that the system illustrates some limitations of the method. In particular, because the orthogonality equation is non-linear in
\zeta
, it may have more than one solution. For formaldimine, considering a linear interpolation between the Franck-Condon geometry and the distorted MECI, we see that the solution that is well-behaved in the Franck-Condon region breaks down and is replaced by a different well-behaved solution at the MECI (see Supporting Information S8A).
This indicates that the SCCSD method must in some cases be considered a correction that should only be applied close to the intersection. This is not always the case, however, as the method has been successfully applied globally in dynamics simulations.
(
Kjønstad et al. 2024
)

Careful considerations are needed when applying SCCSD locally in such simulations.

Dynamics simulations require smooth potential energy surfaces and derivative coupling elements, as this is necessary to avoid integration and energy conservation problems. Some care is therefore required when applying a trajectory-based dynamics method where SCCSD is switched on (instantly or, possibly, gradually) in the vicinity of conical intersections. In particular, an adaptive CCSD/SCCSD algorithm presupposes that the difference between CCSD and SCCSD is sufficiently small, and this is not guaranteed in general. However, it appears to be a reasonable assumption in many cases. In preliminary calculations on various systems (here and in other works
(
Kjønstad and Koch 2017
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2019
;
Kjønstad et al. 2024
)
), we often find corrections on the order of
10^{-3}
eV away from the defective intersections, with the onset of non-linearity in CCSD (coinciding with larger differences between CCSD and SCCSD) occurring only when the energy difference between the states is below
0.01
–
0.05
eV. Moreover, in the context of dynamics simulations, corrections of this magnitude resulted in practically identical time evolution with CCSD and SCCSD.
(
Kjønstad et al. 2024
)

Nevertheless, a more detailed study is needed to reach general conclusions about such a CCSD/SCCSD algorithm.

4
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented an implementation of analytical nuclear gradients and derivative coupling elements
for the similarity constrained coupled cluster singles and doubles method (SCCSD), building upon recent implementations for CCSD.
(
Schnack-Petersen et al. 2022
;
Kjønstad and Koch 2023
)
We have provided a few numerical examples, showing, for example, good agreement with literature values for a minimum energy conical intersection in protonated formaldimine, a simple model system for the chromophore in rhodopsin. However, we have also shown that the SCCSD method can in some cases have multiple solutions, suggesting that the method must in some cases (though not all, see Ref.
25
)
be considered a local correction.

Nevertheless, our implementation has made possible the first nonadiabatic dynamics simulations using both CCSD and SCCSD, as demonstrated in a separate nonadiabatic dynamics study on the ultrafast
\pi\pi^{\ast}
/
n\pi^{\ast}
photorelaxation in thymine.
(
Kjønstad et al. 2024
)
Given the accurate treatment of dynamical correlation in coupled cluster theory, we expect that its application in nonadiabatic dynamics simulations will provide valuable insights about the photochemistry of a variety of interesting systems.
