Abstract
. In the context of the Cauchy problem for the Korteweg-de Vries equation we extend the inverse scattering transform to initial data that behave at plus infinity like a sum of Wigner-von Neumann type potentials with small coupling constants. Our arguments are based on the theory of Hankel operators.

2.
Hankel Operators, basic definitions and important facts

Our approach is based upon techniques of the Hankel operator. Since the Hankel
operator is not a conventional tool in inverse problems for the reader’s
convenience we give a brief introduction to the theory of Hankel operators and
statements of what will be used.

Recall (see e.g.
[
14
]
) that a function
f
analytic in
\mathbb{C}^{\pm}
is in the Hardy space
H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\right)
if

\|f\|_{2}^{2}:=\sup_{y>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|f(x\pm\mathrm{i}y)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}x<\infty\text{.}

We set
H^{2}=H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{+}\right)
. It is a fundamental
fact that
f\left(z\right)\in H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\right)
has
non-tangential boundary values
f\left(x\pm\mathrm{i}0\right)
for almost
every (a.e.)
x\in\mathbb{R}
and
H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\right)

are Hilbert spaces with the inner product induced from
L^{2}
:

\langle f,g\rangle_{H_{\pm}^{2}}=\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}f\left(x\right)\bar{g}\left(x\right)\mathrm{d}x\text{.}

It is well-known that
L^{2}=H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{+}\right)\oplus H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{-}\right)\text{,}
the orthogonal (Riesz)
projection
\mathbb{P}_{\pm}
onto
H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\right)

being given by

(\mathbb{P}_{\pm}f)(x)=\pm\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{f(s)\mathrm{d}s}{s-(x\pm\mathrm{i}0)}.

(2.1)

A Hankel operator is an infinitely dimensional analog of a Hankel matrix, a
matrix whose
(j,k)
entry depends only on
j+k
. In the context of
integral operators the Hankel operator is usually defined as an integral
operator on
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
whose kernel depends on the sum of the
arguments

(\mathbb{H}f)(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}h(x+y)f(y)\mathrm{d}y,\;f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+}),\;x\geq 0,

(2.2)

and it is this form that Hankel operators typically appear in the inverse
scattering formalism. We however consider Hankel operators on
H^{2}
(c.f.

[
21
,
32
]
).

Let
(\mathbb{J}f)(x)=f(-x)
be the reflection operator in
L^{2}
. It
is clearly an isometry with the obvious property

\mathbb{J}\mathbb{P}_{\mp}=\mathbb{P}_{\pm}\mathbb{J}

(2.3)

Definition 2.1

(Hankel operator)
.

Let
\varphi\in L^{\infty}
. The operator

\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
defined by

\mathbb{H}(\varphi)f=\mathbb{J}\mathbb{P}_{-}(\varphi f),\;f\in H^{2},

(2.4)

is called the Hankel operator with the symbol
\varphi
.

It immediately follows from the definition and (
2.3
) that

\|\mathbb{H}(\varphi)\|\leq\|\varphi\|_{\infty}
and if

\mathbb{J}\varphi=\overline{\varphi}
then
\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
is
selfadjoint on
H^{2}
. A more subtle statement (Hartman’s theorem) says that

\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
is compact iff
\varphi=\varphi_{1}+\varphi_{2}
where

\varphi_{1}
is a function continuous on the closed real line and

\varphi_{2}
is analytic and uniformly bounded in the upper half plane.
However if
\varphi
is piecewise continuous with jump discontinuities then

\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
is no longer compact. The following deep theorem

[
34
]
plays a crucial role in our consideration.

Theorem 2.2

(Power, 1978)
.

Let
\mathbb{J}\varphi=\bar{\varphi}
(i.e.

\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
is selfadjoint),
\varphi
decay at
\pm\infty
. Suppose
that
\varphi
is piecewise continuous away from some points
\left\{\pm\omega_{j}\right\}
(may be infinitely many) and

\alpha_{j}:=\frac{1}{2}\left|\varphi\left(\omega_{j}+0\right)-\varphi\left(\omega_{j}-0\right)\right|

(assuming that the limits exist and are different). Then for the essential
spectrum of
\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
we have

\sigma_{ess}\left(\mathbb{H}(\varphi)\right)=\cup_{j}\left[-\alpha_{j},\alpha_{j}\right]\text{.}

The actual statement of Theorem
2.2
given in
[
34
]
is
more general.

Note that the Hankel operator
\mathbb{H}
defined by (
2.2
) is
unitary equivalent to
\mathbb{H}(\varphi)
with the symbol
\varphi
equal
to the Fourier transform of
h
. We emphasize though that the form
(
2.2
) does not prove to be convenient for our purposes and also
h

is in general not a function but a distribution. In integral form
(
2.2
) Hankel operators appeared naturally already in the classical
papers of Faddeev and Marchenko
[
29
]
on inverse scattering. However,
the well-developed theory of this class of operators (and even the name
itself) was not used at that time. In the KdV context, the Fredholm
determinant of
\mathbb{I}+\mathbb{H}
appears to be studied for the first
time in
[
33
]
. In recent
[
6
,
9
]
some ideas of

[
33
]
were extended far beyond the KdV case.

In the conclusion of this section we mention that the formulation of
Faddeev–Marchenko inverse scattering theory in terms of Hankel operators
defined as in Definition
2.1
(adjusted to the unit circle) and the
techniques stemming from this theory appeared only in the present century in

[
17

39
]
. However, the inverse scattering problem
was studied therein only for Jacobi operators and not in the context of

is unitary equivalent to the sum of orthogonal Hankel
operators and each WvN resonance produces a term in the KdV solution. This

is studied in

in the context of singular (i.e. with a local

Note that positons do not interact.

Similarly, Theorem

general and physically relevant case of a decaying ”periodic” structure
modelled by

\left(\gamma_{j}\right)\in l^{1}

If
\gamma=1/2

). More specifically

Theorem 5.1
.

Let a reflection coefficient

in (

such

V_{\pm}-I\in H^{2}\left(\mathbb{C}^{\pm}\right)\text{,}\end{array}

(5.3)

has a unique solution iff

\sup_{j}\frac{1}{2}\left|R\left(\omega_{j}+0\right)-R\left(\omega_{j}-0\right)\right|<1.
