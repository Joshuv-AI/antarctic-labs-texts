Abstract
. This paper presents the results of a needs analysis survey for RE ( RE ). The need for reverse engineers in digital forensics, continues to grow as malware analysis becomes more complicated. The survey was created to investigate tools used in the cybersecurity industry, the methods for teaching RE and educational resources related to RE . Ninety-three (n=93) people responded to our 58 question survey. Participants did not respond to all survey questions as they were optional. The data showed that the majority of 24 / 71 \nicefrac{{24}}{{71}} (33.8%) responses either strongly agreed and 22 / 71 \nicefrac{{22}}{{71}} (30.99%) of responses somewhat agreed that there is a shortage in RE resources. Furthermore, a majority of 17 / 72 \nicefrac{{17}}{{72}} (23.61%) responses indicated that they strongly disagree and that 27 / 72 \nicefrac{{27}}{{72}} (37.5%) somewhat disagree to the statement that graduates are leaving college with adequate RE knowledge. When asked if there is a shortage of adequate RE candidates, the majority of 33 / 71 \nicefrac{{33}}{{71}} (46.48%) responses strongly agreed and 20 / 71 \nicefrac{{20}}{{71}} (28.17%) somewhat agreed. In order to determine if this was a result of the tools at their disposal, a series of questions in regards to the two most popular RE tools were also asked. In response to a question about the efficiency of the IDA ( IDA ) GUI ( GUI ), majority of 16 / 50 \nicefrac{{16}}{{50}} (32%) respondents strongly agreed and 27 / 50 \nicefrac{{27}}{{50}} (54%) somewhat agreed. Similarly, in response to the same question, but in regards to the Ghidra GUI , majority of 14 / 32 \nicefrac{{14}}{{32}} (43.75%) respondents somewhat agreed. The results show that a significant number of respondents were self-taught and weren’t educated via a university, college, or employer in RE . It was concluded that RE is still a growing field, with few experts and a lack of proficiency, but not necessarily impeded by a lack of adequate tools. Our paper provides an overview of the current state of the RE community to be used for the future development of the RE domain.

1.
Introduction

The origins of
RE
can be traced back to hardware, where physical objects were deconstructed in order to determine design logic as well as functionalities. While hardware
RE
is still prevalent today, there is a significant shift in focus to
RE
software, especially within the security community. A formal definition of software
RE
was presented by
(
Chikofsky & Cross 1990
)
in 1990, as “…the process of analyzing a subject system to identify the system’s components and their relationships and create a representation of the system in another form or at a higher level of abstraction.” Over the years, software
RE
has gained significant momentum, particularly in the areas of program analysis and re-engineering and transformation
(
Conti et al. 2008
;
Canfora & Di Penta 2007
)
. Various
RE
toolkits were created that support a number of different architectures, assist with design recovery, and allow visualization of extracted data.

Although these advancements are significant, with the increasing complexity of cyberattacks and proliferation of highly stealthy and obfuscated malware, the need for software
RE
in the digital forensics and incident response continues to grow. Today,
RE
plays a critical role in the deep understanding and attempted mitigation of malicious software
(
Richard III 2009
)
, which
(
Luciano et al. 2018
)
stated is imperative to digital forensics. Major companies, such as Target, Equifax, and Apple, have been compromised and experienced the leakage of user data for millions of users. Additionally, cities in the United States have had to pay a significant amount of money to regain access to their systems after sophisticated ransomware attacks, as seen in Florida towns
(
Norwood 2019
)
. The Cybersecurity and Infrastructure Security Agency
CISA
(
CISA
) director Christopher Krebs has gone on to state that ransomware attacks are a national crisis
(
Seals 2020
)
. Finally, the United States government’s response to combating cyber attacks further demonstrates this urgency. The U.S. Fiscal Year Budget for 2020 has allocated 18.4 billion dollars for cybersecurity purposes to a number of agencies within the federal government
(
Executive Office of the President et al. 2019
;
Cybsecurity Funding 2019
)
.

While it is important to highlight technical advancements in
RE
and to continue to build powerful tools for
RE
, it is also critical that we try to understand outstanding issues that plague this field so that we can focus our energy on effective solutions.
(
Selfridge et al. 1993
)
points out some fundamental problems of
RE
, such as using example programs, understanding economic impact, and inter-researcher communication. It’s also clear that there are significant barriers to entry for students. These include the availability of appropriate training, the need to use complex or expensive software packages, and the lack of prerequisite knowledge. The latter is a serious issue, as the skills and knowledge most helpful in
RE
, including advanced systems programming, operating systems internals, assembler language, compilers, and more, are increasingly marginalized in academic programs
(
Richard III 2009
)
.

In this paper, we present the results of a detailed needs survey for
RE
. Our survey seeks to explore the general need for
RE
in the cybersecurity and digital forensics communities and how
RE
is taught in universities and learned via on-the-job training. Similar needs analysis surveys have been conducted in the literature for other fields of cybersecurity, such as cyber forensics
(
Rogers & Seigfried 2004
;
Harichandran et al. 2016
)
. We undertook this effort because it is crucial for the growth of
RE
as a whole to reflect on the current standing of the field and its challenges. Our contributions are as follows:

•

We present the primary account of a needs analysis survey for
RE
. Our 58 question survey gained the feedback from (n=93) respondents, for the analysis of
RE
needs.

•

We present primary data on how people learn, teach and work in
RE
while also explaining what challenges they face during that process.

•

Our analysis provides insight for advancing the
RE
in education and tools.

The paper is divided into several sections. First, we discuss background information on
RE
in Section
2
. In Section
3
, we share the methodology and survey design. The results are presented in Section
4
. Section
5
describes the limitations of our work, and Section
6
shares related work. Lastly, Section
7

2.
Background

In recent years, software
RE
has played a pivotal role in cybersecurity and digital forensics
(
Akbanov et al. 2018
;
Burji et al. 2010
;
Rahimian et al. 2013
)
.
There are basically two major static analysis techniques for software
RE
- disassembly and decompilation. Disassemblers are tools that generate low-level assembly code from a compiled binary for further scrutiny. On the other hand, decompilation is the process of translating an executable into high-level source code. These RE techniques are widely used in various computing fields ranging from software development to malware and vulnerability analysis. In software development,
RE
provides a mechanism for recovering and recreating a higher-level abstraction of legacy software for maintenance and patching even where the original source code is not available. In addition, these
RE
techniques can be applied for the recovery of source code, architecture and design discovery
(
Canfora & Di Penta 2007
)
, and the integrity validation of cryptographic algorithms
(
Eilam 2011
)
. In the malware analysis domain,
RE
is applied to identify the malicious payload, obfuscation, and other functionalities.

2.1.
Current Tools

A variety of tools are used to reverse engineer applications. Generally, these tools can be separated into two different categories: static analysis and dynamic analysis. Static analysis tools examine the application without executing it while dynamic analysis tools examine the application during execution. These categories are not mutually exclusive–some tools provide both static and dynamic analysis functionality. While open source tool development for
RE
is growing, reverse engineers typically use a mixture of open and closed source tools.

2.1.1.
Static Analysis Tools

IDA
by Hex Rays is a disassembler and decompiler that is interactive, programmable and extensible.
IDA
is hosted on Windows, Linux, or Mac OS X
(
Interactive Disassembler n.d.
)
. It was created by Ilfak Guilfanov, who later founded Hex Rays. Ghidra is a suite of tools developed for software
RE
created by the
NSA
(
NSA
)’s Research Directorate in support of the cybersecurity mission
(
Ghidra n.d.
)
. It was publicly released by the
NSA
on March 2019, with the source code being released on Github
(
National Security Agency 2020
)
. Radare2 is an open source
RE
tool suite that initially provided only static analysis capabilities, but now also provides an integrated low-level debugger
(
Radare Portable Reversing Framework n.d.
)
.

2.1.2.
Dynamic Analysis Tools

There are a variety of publicly available tools that can be used to perform dynamic analysis of an executable. The application will typically execute in a sandbox or virtual environment (e.g., Virtualbox, VMWare Workstation, Microsoft Hyper-V) to avoid contamination of the analysis machine as the user monitors application behavior. There are also web-based tools that allow users to upload files to be scanned in order to determine if they are malicious. Some examples are Virustotal and Hybrid Analysis. x64dbg is an open-source binary debugger for Windows, aimed at malware analysis and
RE
of executables for which source code is not available
(
x64dbg n.d.
)
. Immunity Debugger builds on a solid
UI
(
UI
) with function graphing, the industry’s first heap analysis tool built specifically for heap creation, and a large and well supported Python
API
(
API
) for easy extensibility
(
Immunity Debugger n.d.
)
.

The
WinDbg
(
WinDbg
) can be used to debug kernel-mode and user-mode code, analyze crash dumps, and examine the
CPU
(
CPU
) registers while the code executes
(
Download Debugging Tools for Windows 2020
)
. OllyDbg is a debugger that operates at the assembler level on Windows and focuses on binary code analysis. It is free and open source, which makes it a very popular debugging solution
(
OllyDbg n.d.
)
. Cheat Engine is a tool created for use primarily with single player video games and allows a user to scan for and change values of variables that control game behavior. It also includes a debugger, disassembler, assembler, and system inspection tools which can be of use to someone performing analysis of other types of applications
(
Cheat Engine n.d.
)
.

3.
Methodology

Given the different flavors of
RE
tools and the different ways in which practitioners use the tools for analysis and training, it is clear that there is no harmonized approach to
RE
within the security and forensics communities. Thus, to understand the driving factors and motivation for adopting specific tools or collection of tools by practitioners and educators, we conduct a need analysis survey. The survey was created and taken on Qualtrics. We began by obtaining a category two exemption from the
IRB
(
IRB
) at the BLINDED FOR REVIEW
(this meant that the survey did not record participant identification information or behavior, and posed no risk or harm to subjects not encountered in every day life). The recorded responses from the survey were then analyzed using various exploratory analysis methods.

3.1.
Survey Design & Data Analysis

The survey was mainly geared towards individuals who were professionals or educators in the cybersecurity and digital forensics fields. It consisted of 58 questions with the following composition:

•

10 Likert Scale

•

21 Multiple Choice

•

15 Multiple Choice (Checkbox)

•

9 Free Response

•

1 Slider

The survey questions covered the following: demographics, teaching
RE
, learning
RE
,
RE
Tools and platforms,
IDA
versus Ghidra, potential for web based
RE
platforms, and legal challenges in adopting and usage of
RE
. Collected responses were analyzed using exploratory data analysis and visualization techniques.

5.
Limitations

Our target audience included students, professionals, and educators that have
RE
experience. When trying to find participants, it was found that this area of cybersecurity isn’t as popular and the understanding is still relatively new. This resulted in fewer number of participants, even though overall, we received an acceptable sample size. Throughout the survey each question was optional allowing participants to skip and leave questions blank. It became clear that some questions were favored over others which resulted in inconsistent response counts for each question. There were also three participants that dropped out after the definitions portion and seven participates that stopped after the first question. This could be the result of loss of interest from the length of the survey with fifty-eight questions.

7.
Discussion and Conclusions

The results from our work illustrates that this domain is at its infancy with few experts and a lack of proficiency. The results also illustrate a lack of diversity amongst
RE
professionals. Among the participants that had an understanding of
RE
, a focus in cybersecurity or
RE
, could explain why most participants have either a masters or doctorate degree. Further development of courses at a basic level for undergraduate students and activities for high school students could prove to be beneficial. An early introduction to
RE
to students during middle school and high school may open up new interests that could turn into future academic focuses. The data suggests that computer science is the most popular field of study, perhaps due to it being the oldest major, or due to the advanced knowledge of programming needed when performing
RE
. Being self-taught turned out to be how most learned
RE
. This could also be why many respondents enjoy
RE
as a hobby just as much when working at their job.

Furthermore, another important conclusion made from the results of this survey is that the number of tools individuals use in
RE
are limited: the two most popular being
IDA
/
IDA
Pro and Ghidra, which were followed by
GDB
and Objdump as the next most popular. This popularity could be the result of the age of these tools, although Ghidra was only made public recently, which may hint that the maturity and ease of use of the tools may be a more important factor in their adoption in
RE
. One interesting item to note is that more participant claimed to use
IDA
/
IDA
Pro, than actually considering themselves knowledgeable in
IDA
/
IDA
Pro. In fact the trend as seen in Figure
4
is that the popular tools for
RE
are generally used more than they are understood which may indicate an underlying issue in
RE
educational approaches. This could be the result of the lack of schools with
RE
courses, resulting in the only option being to teach yourself and find other resources through either books or online resources. It may be helpful to add lectures or a playlist of video tutorials to already existing
RE
courses on the usage and functionality of the more popular tools.

Additionally, on the front of
IDA
versus Ghidra, it is important to note, as shown in Figure
2
, that
IDA
ranks higher than Ghidra across the board, in some features more than others. The feature preferred in
IDA
over Ghidra the most was variable renaming, this was the only feature that did not have minimal differences in the preference between the two
RE
tools which makes it clear that there should probably be work done to improve variable renaming in Ghidra. We also found it interesting that exactly half of respondents indicated their use of the free edition of
IDA
where as the other half used the commercial edition. This may represent an even split in the demand for
RE
tools in a commercial setting v.s. everyday, or minimal functionality usage. Although it should also be noted that when asked about if
IDA
/
IDA
Pro was advantageous over Ghidra based upon their feature sets, a slim majority indicated no.

The results showed that most who have taught
RE
prefer to use hands on activities and labs. This is a step in the positive direction as it tells us that instructors may have found an optimal teaching approach. The lack of a student’s prerequisite knowledge may be a result of
RE
classes being an elective, rather than a requirement.
RE
is a more advanced class that requires knowledge in multiple areas. The steep learning curve may discourage new students away, which could explain the lack of experts found in the results.
RE
is a field in the workforce that is very much needed for incident response teams.

The researchers acknowledge that a follow-up survey or additions to the current survey would be beneficial to gain more insight on specifics. Those that indicated they disagreed with certain question statements, could have also been asked to provide an explanation. With all surveys, there needs to be an understanding of what is wrong and what solutions must be made. There were also questions that participants could select tools to indicate their usage or efficacy. Future work could allow participants to rank the chosen tools. Lastly, a longitudinal study that includes both surveys and interviews with experts may shed light on how the domain and its educational capacity is changing over time. This is imperative as
RE
is becoming a cornerstone for improving the cybersecurity and incidence response stature of public and private organizations.
