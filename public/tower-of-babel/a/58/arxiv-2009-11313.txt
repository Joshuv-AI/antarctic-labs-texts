Framework for resource quantification in infinite-dimensional general probabilistic theories
Ludovico Lami, Bartosz Regula, Ryuji Takagi, Giovanni Ferrari

Abstract
Resource theories provide a general framework for the characterization of properties of physical systems in quantum mechanics and beyond. Here, we introduce methods for the quantification of resources in general probabilistic theories (GPTs), focusing in particular on the technical issues associated with infinite-dimensional state spaces. We define a universal resource quantifier based on the robustness measure, and show it to admit a direct operational meaning: in any GPT, it quantifies the advantage that a given resource state enables in channel discrimination tasks over all resourceless states. We show that the robustness acts as a faithful and strongly monotonic measure in any resource theory described by a convex and closed set of free states, and can be computed through a convex conic optimization problem. Specializing to continuous-variable quantum mechanics, we obtain additional bounds and relations, allowing an efficient computation of the measure and comparison with other monotones. We demonstrate applications of the robustness to several resources of physical relevance: optical nonclassicality, entanglement, genuine non-Gaussianity, and coherence. In particular, we establish exact expressions for various classes of states, including Fock states and squeezed states in the resource theory of nonclassicality and general pure states in the resource theory of entanglement, as well as tight bounds applicable in general cases.



I
Introduction

The success of quantum mechanics in fields such as computation, communication, and information processing owes to the fact that certain properties of quantum systems, dubbed
resources
, can be exploited to enable significant advantages over purely classical methods in practical tasks. A central aim in the investigation of such properties is their
quantification
, that is, the development of ways to measure and compare the resources contained in quantum systems
Chitambar and Gour 2019
. This can be done in two seemingly different ways. On the one hand, we can identify tasks of particular importance and ask: how useful is the given state in performing this task?
Horodecki and Oppenheim 2012
;
Brandão and Gour 2015
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Takagi et al. 2019
On the other hand, we can approach resource quantification from a very general, abstract perspective, and establish resource measures which apply to broad classes of resources
del Rio et al. 2015
;
Coecke et al. 2016
;
Regula 2018
;
Kostecki 2019
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Gonda and Spekkens 2019
. The latter approach is attractive as it can reveal common features connecting very different physical phenomena and is immediately applicable in a variety of settings, but it often lacks a direct relation with the operational significance of resources. The recent years have seen remarkable progress in connecting the two approaches, showing that resource measures which directly quantify practical advantages can be defined in broad classes of resource theories
Brandão and Gour 2015
;
Anshu et al. 2018
;
Takagi et al. 2019
;
Uola et al. 2019
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Sparaciari et al. 2020
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Regula et al. 2020
;
Kuroiwa and Yamasaki 2020
;
Seddon et al. 2021
;
Ducuara and Skrzypczyk 2020
;
Uola et al. 2020a
.

In the search for a universal axiomatic characterization of resources, it is then appealing to ask whether these general quantitative methods can apply even in physical theories beyond quantum mechanics
del Rio et al. 2015
;
Coecke et al. 2016
. The framework of
general probabilistic theories
(GPTs)
Ludwig 1985
;
Hartkämper and Neumann 1974
;
Davies and Lewis 1970
;
Lami 2018
provides a formalism which encompasses both quantum and classical probability theory among a myriad of more general theories. Its generality makes it an ideal candidate to study, for instance, hypothetical non-orthodox modifications of quantum mechanics
Barnum et al. 2014
;
van Dam 2013
, or extensions of it that include novel physical phenomena — the archetypal example being gravity
Mielnik 1974
;
Galley et al. 2020
.
Fortunately, resources can be studied also at this level of generality
Chiribella and Scandolo 2015
;
Chiribella and Scandolo 2017
;
Kostecki 2019
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Lami et al. 2018a
;
Aubrun et al. 2019a
;
Aubrun et al. 2019b
. In particular, Ref.
Takagi and Regula 2019
extended the connection between resource quantification and advantages in operational tasks also to resource theories in GPTs.

A major issue with the aforementioned approaches is that they typically apply only to finite-dimensional state spaces, or they place significant restrictions on the resources in consideration. For instance, previous works on resources in infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics are limited to the restricted Gaussian framework
Lami et al. 2018b
;
Lami et al. 2020
or make strong technical assumptions such as compactness of the relevant sets
Kuroiwa and Yamasaki 2020
. This prevents us from being able to use them in the description of some of the most fundamental physical settings, such as quantum optical systems or GPTs in general Banach spaces. An extension of the known results is non-trivial: the previously studied methods explicitly make use of the simplified topological structure of finite-dimensional spaces, and the optimization problems which define the relevant resource measures have not been characterized in the considerably more complex infinite-dimensional setting.

In this work, we develop a general method of quantifying resources in infinite dimensions, applicable both to quantum mechanics and broader GPTs, and directly connected with the performance advantage in practical tasks. The framework is based on a measure called the
robustness
, which found use in a variety of finite-dimensional settings in quantum mechanics
Vidal and Tarrach 1999
;
Harrow and Nielsen 2003
;
Brandão and Plenio 2008
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010a
;
Napoli et al. 2016
;
Regula 2018
;
Anshu et al. 2018
;
Takagi et al. 2019
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Regula et al. 2020
;
Seddon et al. 2021
and in GPTs
Takagi and Regula 2019
, but hitherto has not been considered in infinite-dimensional spaces. In particular, we introduce a variant of the robustness applicable to infinite-dimensional theories and show that it directly quantifies the maximal advantage that a given state provides in a family of channel discrimination tasks. This extends from the finite-dimensional cases a deep connection between resource quantification and the fundamental operational tasks of discrimination. The results apply to any resource theory described by a convex and closed set of states in any GPT, ensuring immediate applicability to the vast majority of physical settings of interest. Our methods rely on a novel extension of the optimization methods which underlie the robustness measure to arbitrary Banach spaces, explicitly considering in detail the issues which arise in such a generalization, and obtaining a characterization of properties such as strong duality. We further use our results to show that the robustness satisfies all of the requirements commonly desired from a valid resource measure
Vidal 2000
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
, including faithfulness and strong monotonicity.

Specializing to continuous-variable quantum mechanics, we introduce methods to simplify the evaluation of the robustness in several cases, and show that the measure can be computed analytically for some of the most relevant examples of states in resource theories of nonclassicality, entanglement, coherence, and genuine non-Gaussianity. We in particular establish exact expressions or tight bounds for: Fock states, squeezed states, and cat states in the resource theory of nonclassicality, as well as all pure states and an important mixed state based on the Hilbert operator in the resource theories of entanglement and coherence. We compare the robustness to a related resource measure often employed in finite dimensions, the standard robustness
Vidal and Tarrach 1999
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
, and show that the latter is not a well-behaved monotone in important continuous-variable resource theories such as entanglement, nonclassicality, and coherence. Specifically, we provide examples of states where our robustness measure is finite and well-behaved but the standard robustness diverges to infinity, and show in particular that this behavior affects most physically accessible states in the resource theory of nonclassicality.

All of our findings provide evidence for the suitability of our infinite-dimensional robustness measure as a universal resource quantifier in general resource theories, satisfying desirable properties and computable in many cases.

This work also serves as the companion to the paper
Regula et al. 2021
, which deals with resource quantification in continuous-variable quantum mechanics. Here we provide a derivation and extended discussion of the results stated in
Regula et al. 2021
, along with several additional developments specific to quantum theory. The general framework in GPTs can be thought of as a generalization of the concepts introduced in
Regula et al. 2021
.

The organization of the paper is as follows. Section
II
contains a comprehensive introduction to all of the relevant concepts, including GPTs, resource theories, and resource quantification. In Section
III
, we discuss the problems concerning defining a robustness measure in infinite dimensions, and establish a characterization of the relevant conic optimization problems. We show in Section
IV
that our proposed variant of the robustness indeed satisfies all of the requirements for a valid resource measure in any convex resource theory. Section
V
establishes the robustness as the figure of merit in channel discrimination tasks. In Section
VI
we discuss simplifications which occur when the GPT is chosen to be infinite-dimensional quantum mechanics, establishing several bounds and expression for the robustness, as well as characterizing strong duality. Finally, in Section
VII
we explicitly apply the robustness to the resource theories of nonclassicality (Sec.
VII.1
), entanglement (Sec.
VII.2
), genuine non-Gaussianity (Sec.
VII.3
), and coherence (Sec.
VII.4
), obtaining a multitude of resource-specific results.

Preliminaries

We first introduce some basic notation that will be used throughout the paper.

Given a real topological vector space
\mathcal{V}
, we will use
\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
to denote its continuous dual space, that is, the space of continuous linear functionals
F\colon\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
. We write
\left\langle F,x\right\rangle=F(x)
for any
x\in\mathcal{V},F\in\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
.

A set
\mathcal{S}
is called convex if
tx+(1-t)y\in\mathcal{S}
for any
x,y\in\mathcal{S}
and
t\in[0,1]
, and it is called a cone if
x\in\mathcal{S}\Rightarrow\lambda x\in\mathcal{S}
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We will use
\conv(\mathcal{S})=\left\{\left.\sum_{i=1}^{n}c_{i}x_{i}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;x_{i}\in\mathcal{S},\;c_{i}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\sum_{i}c_{i}=1\right\}
to denote the convex hull of
\mathcal{S}
, that is, the smallest convex set which contains
\mathcal{S}
; analogously,
\cone(\mathcal{S})=\left\{\left.\lambda x\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;x\in\mathcal{S},\;\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}\right\}
will denote the conic hull of
\mathcal{S}
, and
\cl(\mathcal{S})
the closure of
\mathcal{S}
, that is, the smallest closed set which contains
\mathcal{S}
. For two sets
\mathcal{S},\mathcal{R}
, we use
\mathcal{S}+\mathcal{R}=\left\{\left.s+r\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;s\in\mathcal{S},\;r\in\mathcal{R}\right\}
to denote their Minkowski sum.
Given a set
\mathcal{S}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, its dual cone
\mathcal{S}\textup{*}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
is given by
\left\{\left.Y\in\mathcal{V}\textup{*}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\langle Y,x\right\rangle\geq 0\;\forall x\in\mathcal{S}\right\}
.

As is standard in optimization theory, we will take
\inf\emptyset=\infty
and
\sup\emptyset=-\infty
. We will allow algebraic operations involving infinity whenever it leads to no ambiguities, with
c+\infty=\infty
and
c\infty=\operatorname{sgn}(c)\infty
for any
c\in\mathbb{R}
. We use
\log
to denote the logarithm to the base 2 and
\ln
for the natural logarithm.

II.1
General probabilistic theories

We provide a brief introduction to the formalism of GPTs based on standard references
Ludwig 1983
;
Ludwig 1985
;
Davies and Lewis 1970
; see also
(
Lami 2018
, Ch. 1-2)
for a modern rigorous introduction to the concepts. GPTs can be thought of a generalization of the formalism of quantum mechanics, aiming to recover a characterization of the probabilistic and statistical aspects of the manipulation of physical systems in an axiomatic manner.
While some literature considers only the technically more elementary finite-dimensional case, here we set out to describe the GPT machinery in full generality. The universality of the formalism described below rests upon a result by Ludwig
(
Ludwig 1985
, Ch. IV, Thm. 3.7)
, who deduced it from first principles — we expand on that below.

Our setting will be a
base norm Banach space
, namely, a type of ordered Banach space in which the order and the norm structures are intimately related with each other. To understand this notion better, let us discuss how a base norm space can be constructed. The basic object of interest is an ordered vector space
\mathcal{V}
, equipped with the
positive cone

\mathcal{C}
, which induces a partial order on
\mathcal{V}
according to
x\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}y\iff y-x\in\mathcal{C}
.
For
(\mathcal{V},\preceq_{\mathcal{C}})
to be an ordered vector space, we need
\mathcal{C}
to be a convex cone (i.e.,
\lambda\mathcal{C}+\mu\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
for all
\lambda,\mu\geq 0
) that is also
pointed
(i.e.,
\mathcal{C}\cup(-\mathcal{C})=\{0\}
). In what follows, we will also assume that
\mathcal{C}
is spanning (i.e.,
\mathcal{C}-\mathcal{C}=\mathcal{V}
).

The (algebraic) dual
\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
of an ordered vector space
\mathcal{V}
can itself be turned into an ordered vector space by the choice of the dual cone
\mathcal{C}\textup{*}
as the positive cone of
\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
. To define a GPT we also need to single out a strictly positive functional
U\in\mathcal{C}\textup{*}
called the
unit effect
. Here, strict positivity means that
\left\langle U,x\right\rangle\geq 0
for all
x\in\mathcal{C}
, with equality if and only if
x=0
. The order unit can be used to construct the
state space

\Omega\coloneqq\{x\in\mathcal{C}\,|\,\braket{U,x}=1\}
as well as the function
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Omega}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\inf\left\{\left.\lambda_{+}\!+\!\lambda_{-}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;x=\lambda_{+}\omega_{+}\!-\!\lambda_{-}\omega_{-},\;\lambda_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;\omega_{\pm}\in\Omega\right\}

(1)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\left\langle U,x_{+}+x_{-}\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;x=x_{+}-x_{-},\;x_{\pm}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}.

In the above setting, it is not difficult to verify that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Omega}
is always at least a seminorm. For
\mathcal{V}
to be a Banach space,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Omega}
needs to be a norm which makes
\mathcal{V}
complete
1
1

1

Namely, such that all Cauchy sequences converge. Here, a sequence
(x_{n})_{n}
of elements of
\mathcal{V}
is said to be
Cauchy
if for all
\epsilon>0
one can find an integer
N
such that
\left\|x_{n}-x_{m}\right\|_{\Omega}\leq\epsilon
for all
n,m\geq N
, and to converge if there exists
x\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|x_{n}-x\right\|_{\Omega}=0
.
, in which case we refer to
\mathcal{V}
as a base norm Banach space with base
\Omega
. We can then without loss of generality assume that
\mathcal{C}
and
\Omega
are both closed sets with respect to the topology induced by the base norm
Ellis 1966
.

Hereafter, we will always assume that
\mathcal{V}
is a base norm Banach space, and we will use the topology induced by the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Omega}
unless stated otherwise.

In order to axiomatically define the concept of a measurement, we need to consider linear functionals
E:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
whose values correspond to the probability of obtaining a certain measurement outcome.
Under the so-called no restriction hypothesis
Ludwig 1985
;
Barrett 2007
;
Chiribella et al. 2010
, which we will take as an axiom, the set of physically implementable measurement functionals is then formed by all possible continuous linear functionals
E:\Omega\to[0,1]
, which is precisely the set of functionals
E
such that
0\preceq E\preceq U
. Any such functional is called an
effect
, and a collection of effects
\{E_{i}\}
such that
\sum_{i}E_{i}=U
will be called a
measurement
, since its outcomes sum up to a valid probability distribution.

With the above notation, the base norm can be recast into the following dual form:

\displaystyle\left\|x\right\|_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\left\langle E,x\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;-U\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}E\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}U\right\}.

(2)

The dual norm on
\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
is known as the
order unit norm

\displaystyle\left\|Y\right\|^{\circ}_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\left\langle Y,x\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\|x\right\|_{\Omega}\leq 1\right\}

(3)

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\lvert\left\langle Y,\omega\right\rangle\rvert\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\Omega\right\}.

Accordingly, we will refer to
\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
as an order unit Banach space.

Before we move on, let us devote a moment to look at quantum theory from this new perspective. Given a Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, we can consider the base norm Banach space of all trace class operators on
\mathcal{H}
, ordered by the cone of positive semidefinite operators. Choosing the unit effect to be simply the trace, we see that the corresponding state space is formed by all density operators, i.e., positive semidefinite operators with trace
1
. The base norm turns out to coincide with the trace norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
, and the dual space with the order unit space of all bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
, equipped with the operator norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\infty}
. It can be noticed that measurements in the GPT sense generalize the concept of positive operator-valued measure (POVM) elements from quantum mechanics, and that a quantum measurement is simply identified with any valid POVM.

The final component of any GPT are the physical transformations, or channels, between states. This issue is typically more difficult to approach in an axiomatic way
Edwards 1971
;
Edwards 1972
, making it dependent on the particular setting under consideration. However, we will not need to assume anything about the set of physical transformations save for two of the weakest assumptions: first, that the identity transformation is physical (i.e., doing nothing is allowed), and second, that any physical transformation
\Lambda:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
always maps a state
\omega\in\Omega
into a valid state
\Lambda(\omega)\in\Omega^{\prime}
in the output state
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. These general assumptions guarantee that our results apply to any physical GPT in consideration.

To consider probabilistic state transformations, we will employ the general notion of an
instrument

Davies and Lewis 1970
. This corresponds to a collection of transformations
\{\Lambda_{i}\}
which are unnormalized channels, in the sense that each
\Lambda_{i}
maps a state
\omega
to another state
\omega_{i}^{\prime}
with some probability
p_{i}
, and thus the post-selected output of the transformation can be written as
\Lambda_{i}(\omega)=p_{i}\omega_{i}^{\prime}
. To ensure that the overall transformation is physical, we will consider an instrument to be any set
\{\Lambda_{i}\}_{i}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
such that
\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
for all
\omega\in\Omega
, and
\sum_{i}\Lambda_{i}
is a normalization-preserving transformation, i.e.,
\left\langle U^{\prime},\sum_{i}\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\right\rangle=\left\langle U,\omega\right\rangle
. This allows us to treat
\left\langle U^{\prime},\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\right\rangle
as the probability of the
i^{\text{th}}
transformation occurring.

As already anticipated, the GPT formalism, which may at first glance look as an ad-hoc generalization of quantum mechanics to a broader setting, can be actually deduced from first principles. Namely, the so-called Ludwig embedding theorem
(
Ludwig 1985
, Ch. IV, Thm. 3.7)
guarantees that any physical theory that obeys some basic requirements is equivalent to a GPT. Here, a physical theory is intended as a map that associates with every preparation procedure
\omega
of a fixed physical system and every measurement setting and outcome, together described as
E
, a number
\mu(E,\omega)\in[0,1]
that represents the probability of obtaining the outcome
E
when measuring the system prepared according to the procedure
\omega
. Among the axioms required for the Ludwig embedding theorem to apply, the only less intuitive one is the no-restriction hypothesis
(
Lami 2018
, Ch. 1)
.

II.2
Resource theories

A resource theory is concerned with the description of the manipulation of physical systems under a set of constraints that single out a specific property of states as a “resource” which — within the given setting — is expensive to generate or preserve. One then defines the set of free states
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Omega
as the states which do not possess a given resource, and the free operations
\mathcal{O}
as the channels which can be implemented without using any resource. Any state
\omega\notin\mathcal{F}
and any channel
\Lambda\notin\mathcal{O}
is considered resourceful, and is not allowed to be used for free within the physical constraints of the theory. In its general formulation, the formalism can describe virtually any aspect of manipulating physical states in any desired setting. However, making meaningful statements about general resources and their practical applications can be difficult, since different resources are built upon very different physical constraints, which means that they can have very different sets of free states and admit a variety of different classes of free operations. Our aim will therefore be to remain as general as possible in our investigation, only making very weak assumptions about resources in consideration, so that the results immediately apply to broad classes of resources.

We thus begin with two basic assumptions about the set
\mathcal{F}
: that it is closed with respect to the topology induced by the base norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\Omega}
, and that it is convex.

The assumption of closedness is a very natural one in any GPT. It can be understood as a consequence of the fact that the base norm distance
\left\|\omega-\sigma\right\|_{\Omega}
quantifies the distinguishability of two different states
Ludwig 1983
;
Kimura et al. 2010
;
Holevo 1973
;
Helstrom 1976
. That is, if a sequence of states
\{\omega_{n}\}_{n}
satisfies
\left\|\omega_{n}-\omega\right\|_{\Omega}\to 0
, the states in the sequence become indistinguishable from
\omega
by any measurement. As a consequence, performing any experiment on states in the sequence should yield results consistent with the results one would obtain by performing the experiment on
\omega
directly. In this sense, we can assume that a resource cannot be generated by simply taking a sequence of resourceless states, as this would contradict their indistinguishability.

Convexity, as we have mentioned, is a ubiquitous feature of GPTs. It relies only on the fundamental assumption that, having access to a preparation procedure which outputs a state
\sigma_{1}
with probability
p
and another state
\sigma_{2}
with probability
1-p
, we are allowed to erase the information about which of the states was prepared, resulting in the probabilistic mixture
p\sigma_{1}+(1-p)\sigma_{2}
. In most physical contexts, it is then intuitive to expect that simply mixing two states without any resource cannot result in a resourceful state.

While the vast majority of physically relevant resources are convex by definition, in some contexts the free states can form a non-convex set — the most common example being the resource theory based on the set of Gaussian states
Genoni and Paris 2010
. However, when considered in an operational setting, it is often easy to prepare mixtures of such states
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
;
Albarelli et al. 2018
, meaning that such convex combinations can also be considered free as they are not able to provide practical advantages when manipulated by free operations. Motivated by this, we will thus take convexity as a basic axiom of the considered resources, and we will call any theory satisfying this property a
convex resource theory
for clarity.

As for the free operations, we only make the weakest possible assumption: that no free operation can generate any resource from a resourceless state. That is, any free channel
\Lambda
must satisfy
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow\Lambda(\sigma)\in\mathcal{F}
. This class of maps is known as the maximal free operations
Chitambar and Gour 2019
, as any physical class of free operations should be a subset of
\mathcal{O}
. In a very similar way, we deem a probabilistic instrument
\{\Lambda_{i}\}
to be free whenever
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow\Lambda_{i}(\sigma)\in\cone(\mathcal{F})
for all
i
, that is, each
\Lambda_{i}
preserves the set of free states up to normalization.

II.3
Resource monotones

There are many, in general inequivalent, ways to quantify and compare the resource content of a state
Chitambar and Gour 2019
;
Regula 2018
. An axiomatic characterization of the conditions necessary for a function
M:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
to constitute a valid resource measure has been considered in various works
Vidal 2000
;
Horodecki 2001
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
. For simplicity, we will assume that the measure is suitably normalized and shifted so that its minimal value is 1; another common option is to consider 0 as the baseline value. The minimal requirements can then be formulated as follows.

(1a)

Minimality on the set of free states. That is,
\omega\in\mathcal{F}\Rightarrow M(\omega)=1
.

This is a natural requirement, since free states have less resources than resourceful ones by definition. However, a problem with this definition is that it does not ensure that
only
free states minimize the given measure. Therefore, a stronger constraint is often imposed:

(1b)

Faithfulness. That is,
M(\omega)=1\iff\omega\in\mathcal{F}
.

The condition (1b) is sometimes eschewed in favor of ease of evaluation, as non-faithful monotones can be easier to compute than faithful ones. However, it can be seen that faithfulness is required for any monotone which precisely characterizes a given resource, since any such measure needs to be able to distinguish a free state from a non-free state.

The next condition concerns the behavior of the measure under free operations.

(2a)

Monotonicity under free operations. That is,
M(\Lambda(\omega))\leq M(\omega)
for any
\Lambda\in\mathcal{O}
.

This is another natural requirement, since a free operation cannot generate any resources by definition. However, this condition does not capture the fact that state transformations can be performed probabilistically, where — with some nonzero probability — it might actually increase the resource
Vidal 2000
. To account for this, a stronger requirement is often imposed.

(2b)

Strong monotonicity under free operations. That is,
\sum_{i}p_{i}M\left(\frac{\Lambda_{i}(\omega)}{p_{i}}\right)\leq M(\omega)
for any free probabilistic protocol
\{\Lambda_{i}\}
where
p_{i}=\left\langle U,\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\right\rangle
.

We remark that, within quantum mechanics, a weaker notion of strong monotonicity based on the Kraus operators of a channel is often employed
Vidal 2000
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
.

Additionally, in all convex resource theories, a natural property that we previously discussed is that probabilistically mixing states should not increase the resource. This leads to the next condition.

(3)

Convexity. That is,
M\left(\sum_{i}c_{i}\omega_{i}\right)\leq\sum_{i}c_{i}M(\omega_{i})
for any convex combination
\sum_{i}c_{i}\omega_{i}
.

Our final requirement concerns the continuity properties of the monotones. Although various notions of continuity are sometimes used in the description of finite-dimensional resource measures
Horodecki 2001
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
, such requirements are often too strong in infinite dimensions
Eisert et al. 2002a
, and even in finite dimensions many useful resource monotones are not continuous
Brandão and Plenio 2010a
. However, a fundamental property of a resource that we discussed is its closedness. It is then natural to require that a resource cannot be increased by simply taking a limit of less resourceful states. This property is reflected by the lower semicontinuity of the function.

(4)

Lower semicontinuity. That is,
\displaystyle M(\omega)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}M(\omega_{n})
for any sequence such that
\{\omega_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
.

Having established these basic criteria, we can introduce a general definition of a bona fide resource measure.

Definition 1
.

A
resource measure
(or resource monotone) is a function
M:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
which satisfies the conditions of faithfulness (1b), strong monotonicity (2b), convexity (3), and lower semicontinuity (4).

Although these axiomatic requirements are often employed in the characterization of resources, they can be insufficient to ensure that a given function is
useful
as a quantifier. Take, for instance, the indicator function

\displaystyle M(\omega)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\omega\in\mathcal{F}\\
\infty&\omega\notin\mathcal{F}.\end{cases}

(4)

Such a function satisfies all of the criteria (1)-(4), but clearly is useless in benchmarking and comparing the resource content of different states. This issue becomes even more troublesome in infinite dimensions, where the existence of infinitely resourceful states with respect to certain tasks is a physical possibility
Eisert et al. 2002a
;
Keyl et al. 2003
. We have to keep in mind that the amount of resource a given state contains depends on the operational task under examination. Therefore, it is perfectly possible that certain monotones yield a finite value when evaluated on a state, while others diverge. However, from a pragmatic standpoint, the primary function of a resource measure should be to enable a quantitative comparison of the resource contents of different states. Therefore, a monotone which assigns an infinite value to a large fraction of quantum states can often be regarded as less useful, and it is of interest to establish monotones which remain finite even when others might diverge. Such a requirement appears to be difficult to formalize in a fully general way, but we will consider it explicitly in several relevant cases.

II.4
Robustness measures

Having established the basic properties which can be expected from a measure of a resource, we now consider a general type of quantifier in any resource theory: the so-called robustness measures. Defined first for quantum entanglement
Vidal and Tarrach 1999
and later generalized to other finite-dimensional resources in quantum theory
Brandão and Gour 2015
and GPTs
Takagi and Regula 2019
, the measures are defined through the basic concept of convex combinations of states. Intuitively, to quantify how robust the resources contained in a state
\omega
are to noise, one can ask: how much noise in the form of statistical mixing with some suitable noisy state
\tau
, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\,\omega+\frac{\lambda}{1+\lambda}\,\tau,

(5)

can
\omega
withstand before it becomes a free state? Two common types of robustness are then defined by optimizing over the noise states
\tau
. The
(generalized) robustness
can be defined for any state
\omega
as

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\,1+\lambda\,\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\left|\;\frac{\omega+\lambda\tau}{1+\lambda}\in\mathcal{F},\;\tau\in\Omega\right.\right\}.

(6)

Note that our definition differs by a constant term 1 from the commonly used terminology
Vidal and Tarrach 1999
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
which defines robustness as
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)-1
(or, generally,
R_{\mathcal{F}}(x)-\left\langle U,x\right\rangle
). This inconsequential change in notation is employed because it will make the measure more straightforward to apply also to unnormalized elements of
\mathcal{V}
.

The other common type of robustness is the
standard robustness
(also known as
free robustness
), for which the noise states are also free:

\displaystyle R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\,1+\lambda\,\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\left|\;\frac{\omega+\lambda\sigma}{1+\lambda}\in\mathcal{F},\;\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\right.\right\}.

(7)

Both of the robustness measures find a plethora of applications in finite-dimensional resource theories, including quantum resource distillation and dilution
Brandão and Plenio 2008
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010a
;
Brandão and Datta 2011
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Regula et al. 2020
, simulation of quantum circuits
Pashayan et al. 2015
;
Howard and Campbell 2017
;
Seddon et al. 2021
, as well as channel discrimination problems in quantum mechanics
Piani et al. 2016
;
Takagi et al. 2019
and broader GPTs
Takagi and Regula 2019
.

However, such results do not immediately generalize to infinite-dimensional resource theories, even within quantum mechanics. The first problem being that, in general, the definitions in Eq. (
6
)–(
7
) do not even guarantee lower semicontinuity, so the measures defined in this way can fail to satisfy condition (4) that we considered in the previous section. Additionally, the methods required to characterize robustness monotones and their operational applications — mathematical tools like convex duality, various bounds and relations with other monotones, resource-theoretic properties — were explicitly developed only in finite-dimensional theories. Their extension to infinite-dimensional spaces is non-trivial due to the considerably richer and more complex structure of infinite-dimensional theories.

Crucially, we will see that the definition of the generalized robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
can indeed be suitably adapted to general resource theories in infinite-dimensional GPTs, extending most of the useful properties of the measure familiar from finite-dimensional spaces. The case of the standard robustness
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
becomes more troublesome. Even in finite dimensions, it is known that there exist resource theories for which the standard robustness is not well-behaved and takes an infinite value for most resourceful states
Napoli et al. 2016
;
Liu et al. 2019
;
Regula et al. 2020
, which prevents it from being a useful quantifier. In the infinite-dimensional case, the measure suffers from this problem for the theory of nonclassicality, and diverges on several interesting states for entanglement theory as well. Since these are perhaps the two most important continuous-variable resources in quantum mechanics, this suggests that
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
may not be well suited to be a universal resource quantifier. Although we will also study the properties of
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
when relevant, our focus from now on will be mainly on
R_{\mathcal{F}}
.

III
Defining robustness measures in infinite dimensions

In our investigation, it will be useful to consider the optimization problems
R_{\mathcal{F}}
,
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
in the form of inequalities with respect to convex cones. To this end, we notice that by defining
\tau^{\prime}=\lambda\tau\in\mathcal{C}
in Eq. (
6
), we can write

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.1+\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega+\tau^{\prime}=(1+\lambda)\sigma,\;\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C},\;\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\right\}

(8)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}\sigma^{\prime},\;\sigma^{\prime}\in\cone(\mathcal{F})\right\}.

In a similar way, we write

\displaystyle R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\inf\left\{\left.\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\preceq_{\mathcal{F}}\sigma^{\prime},\;\sigma^{\prime}\in\cone(\mathcal{F})\right\}

(9)

where we use
A\preceq_{\mathcal{F}}B\iff B-A\in\cone(\mathcal{F})
to denote inequality with respect to the conic hull of
\mathcal{F}
. We will take the above to be the definitions of
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\tau)
and
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\tau)
when
\tau\in\mathcal{V}
is not a state.

In this section, we will use the tools of convex optimization to investigate the properties of these quantities and to define variants of the measures which are more suited to infinite-dimensional spaces. Before progressing with the study of the robustnesses explicitly, we will consider general optimization problems of this type.

III.1
Convex duality in Banach spaces

Let
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
be two closed convex cones. Define the
primal problem

P
as the optimization problem whose optimal value is given by

\displaystyle P(\omega)\coloneqq\inf_{\sigma}\left\{\left.\left\langle U,\sigma\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\sigma-\omega\in\mathcal{K}_{0},\,\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{1}\right\}.

(10)

The generalized robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
is obtained when
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\mathcal{C}
,
\mathcal{K}_{1}=\cone(\mathcal{F})
, and the standard robustness
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
when
\mathcal{K}_{0}=\mathcal{K}_{1}=\cone(\mathcal{F})
. We then define the corresponding
dual problem

D
as

\displaystyle D(\omega)\coloneqq\sup_{W}\left\{\left.\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;W\in\mathcal{K}_{0}\textup{*},\,U-W\in\mathcal{K}_{1}\textup{*}\right\}.

(11)

Notice that the optimal values are homogeneous functions of
\omega
(i.e.,
P(k\omega)=kP(\omega)
). The primal problem is called feasible if there exists a choice of
\sigma
which satisfies the constraints in Eq. (
10
), and infeasible otherwise; the dual is always feasible since one can choose
W=U
.

A commonly used property of such optimization problems, particularly in finite-dimensional spaces, is the strong duality: under certain conditions, it can be guaranteed that
P(\omega)=D(\omega)

Rockafellar 1970
;
Boyd and Vandenberghe 2004
. However, this is generally much more difficult to ensure in infinite-dimensional spaces
Ponstein 2004
;
Borwein and Lewis 2006
, motivating an alternative approach.

We will say that the primal problem
P
is
subfeasible

Duffin 1956
with subfeasible value
\lambda
if there exists sequences
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{1},\{\tau_{n}\}_{n}\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
such that
\{\sigma_{n}-\tau_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
in the base norm topology, and
\{\left\langle U,\sigma_{n}\right\rangle\}_{n}\to\lambda
in the standard topology on
\mathbb{R}
. Intuitively, this means that the choices of
\{\sigma_{n},\tau_{n}\}
— although not necessarily feasible for the primal problem
P
— approach feasibility. The
optimal subfeasible value
is then defined as

P^{\prime}(\omega)\coloneqq\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\lambda\text{ subfeasible for }P(\omega)\right\}
.

We now establish a general form of duality between the optimal primal subfeasible value and the optimal dual value. The result is based on general properties of linear and conic optimization, and many formulations of this principle can be found in the literature
Duffin 1956
;
Kretschmer 1961
;
Yamasaki 1968
;
Anderson 1983
;
Shapiro 2001
. We include a self-contained proof in Appendix
A
.

Proposition 2
.

For any
\omega\in\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\omega)=D(\omega).

(12)

Furthermore, the primal problem is subfeasible if and only if there exists a dual solution
W
which achieves the optimal value, with
\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle=D(\omega)=P^{\prime}(\omega)
.

In addition to the above duality result, it is of course of interest to ask: under what conditions is full strong duality achieved, that is, when do the values of the primal and dual problems satisfy
P(\omega)=D(\omega)
? There are several ways to establish sufficient conditions for such a property based on so-called generalized interior conditions
Borwein and Goebel 2003
;
Boţ et al. 2008
;
Ponstein 2004
, which require the existence of points in the interior (or various generalized notions thereof) of the cones
\mathcal{K}_{0}
or
\mathcal{K}_{1}
. Such properties are very non-trivial requirements in infinite-dimensional spaces, and they are out of scope of this work.

To derive a simpler condition, often possible to verify in practice, we establish several equivalent formulations of the optimization problems
P,P^{\prime}
, clarifying the definitions and the convergence requirement of the subfeasible solutions. Since
\mathcal{K}_{0},\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{C}
, each of the cones admits a bounded, closed, convex base
\mathcal{B}_{0},\mathcal{B}_{1}\subseteq\Omega
— in the case of the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
, the cones are
\mathcal{C}
with the base
\Omega
and
\cone(\mathcal{F})
with the base
\mathcal{F}
. We then have the following.

Lemma 3
.

For any
\omega\in\Omega
it holds that

\displaystyle P(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega=\lambda\sigma-(\lambda-1)\tau,\;\tau\in\mathcal{B}_{0},\;\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\right\}

(13)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\Big\{\lambda\;\Big|\;\exists\{\xi_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega\colon\xi_{n}=\lambda\sigma_{n}-(\lambda-1)\tau_{n},

\displaystyle\hphantom{\inf\Big\lx@text@lbrace \lambda\;\Big|\;}\tau_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{0},\;\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}\Big\}.

(14)

In particular, it suffices to look at sequences of normalized elements
\xi_{n}\in\mathcal{V}
such that
\left\langle U,\xi_{n}\right\rangle=1
when considering subfeasibility.

Alternatively, we can write

\displaystyle P(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\lambda(\mathcal{B}_{1}-\mathcal{K}_{0})\right\}

(15)

\displaystyle P^{\prime}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\lambda\cl(\mathcal{B}_{1}-\mathcal{K}_{0})\right\}.

The proof follows straightforwardly by manipulating the definitions of the optimization problems, and we include it in Appendix
A
for completeness.

Stemming from the above characterization, we obtain a sufficient condition for strong duality as follows.

Proposition 4
.

If either
\conv\!\left(\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup(-\mathcal{B}_{0})\right)
or
\mathcal{B}_{1}-\mathcal{K}_{0}
is a closed set, then
P(\omega)=P^{\prime}(\omega)=D(\omega)
holds for all
\omega\in\Omega
.

In the case of the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
, the condition requires that
\conv(\mathcal{F}\cup(-\Omega))
be closed.

We will later see that the condition can be used to show strong duality in several relevant cases. In particular, we will use it in Sec.
VI.1
to establish the strong duality of the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
in many continuous-variable quantum resources.

III.2
Lower semicontinuous robustness

As mentioned before, the definition of the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
is not guaranteed to satisfy lower semicontinuity (l.s.c.) — a property which reflects the fact that, if a state can be approximated by a sequence of states
\{\omega_{n}\}
with
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega_{n})\leq\lambda
, then we should also have
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\leq\lambda
. Mathematically, this corresponds to the fact that each set
\left\{\left.\omega\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\leq\lambda\right\}
should be closed.
We thus define the
l.s.c. robustness

\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
as the l.s.c. hull (also known as closure) of
R_{\mathcal{F}}
, that is, the largest function which is lower semicontinuous and upper bounded by
R_{\mathcal{F}}
(see e.g.
Ponstein 2004
). Specifically, we have the following.

Definition 5
.

The
lower semicontinuous robustness

\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
is defined as

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\liminf_{\xi\to\omega}\,R_{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\,\inf_{\left\|\xi-\omega\right\|_{\Omega}\leq\varepsilon}R_{\mathcal{F}}(\xi).

We will often refer to
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
simply as the robustness, since we shall see that it is the most natural extension of
R_{\mathcal{F}}
applicable to general infinite-dimensional resource theories and satisfying all desirable properties.

Recall that the value of the robustness can be identified with the optimal value of the optimization problem

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\inf\left\{\left.\left\langle U,\sigma\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}\sigma,\;\sigma\in\cone(\mathcal{F})\right\}.

(17)

Consider then the so-called epigraph of
R_{\mathcal{F}}
, defined as the set

\displaystyle\operatorname{epi}R_{\mathcal{F}}\coloneqq

\displaystyle\left\{\left.(\omega,\lambda)\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\leq\lambda\right\}

(18)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{\left.(\sigma-\tau,\,\left\langle U,\sigma\right\rangle)\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\sigma\in\cone(\mathcal{F}),\;\tau\in\mathcal{C}\right\}

in the space
\mathcal{V}\times\mathbb{R}
. The l.s.c. robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
can be understood as the function whose epigraph corresponds to the closure of
\operatorname{epi}R_{\mathcal{F}}

(
Barbu and Precupanu 2012
, 2.1.3)
.
It is then not difficult to show that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
is precisely the optimal subfeasible value of the optimization problem (
17
) as we have considered in the previous section.
This argument allows us to use the duality relation in Prop.
2
to identify the value of the l.s.c. robustness with the optimal value of the dual problem. We thus obtain several equivalent definitions of this measure, which we summarize in the following.

Corollary 6
.

Let
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Omega
be any closed and a convex set. For any state
\omega
it holds that

\displaystyle

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\,\inf_{\left\|\xi-\omega\right\|_{\Omega}\leq\varepsilon}R_{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)

(19)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\{\xi_{n}\}_{n}\in\mathcal{V}}\left\{\left.1+\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\frac{\xi_{n}+\lambda\tau_{n}}{1+\lambda}\in\mathcal{F},\;\tau_{n}\in\Omega,\;\{\xi_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega\right\}

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\cl(\lambda\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{C})\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;W\in\mathcal{C}\textup{*},\;\left\langle W,\sigma\right\rangle\leq 1\;\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\right\}

and the supremum in the last line is achieved whenever
{\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)<\infty}
.

In other words, the function
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
can be understood in three different ways: (1) as the tightest lower semicontinuous approximation to
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
; (2) as the least value of
R_{\mathcal{F}}
achievable by sequences of points converging to
\omega
; (3) as the optimization over dual witnesses, which generalizes an equivalent definition of finite-dimensional robustness
Brandão 2005
;
Regula 2018
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
.

We will show in the following section that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies requirements (1)-(4) from Sec.
II.3
to be considered a bona fide resource measure. We will thus regard
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
as the proper definition of the robustness in infinite-dimensional GPTs. Within quantum mechanics, we will later show that the functions
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
R_{\mathcal{F}}
are actually equal in many of the practically relevant cases.

One might wonder why extra care with the definition of the robustness is required here, but the distinction between
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
R_{\mathcal{F}}
is not necessary in finite-dimensional theories. This is a consequence of a very strong property of finite-dimensional spaces: all closed sets of states in such theories are compact. Compactness significantly simplifies the considered optimization problems, and in particular we have the following condition for equality of the two definitions.

Proposition 7
.

If
\mathcal{F}
is compact, then
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
for all
\omega
.

Proof.

Recalling our characterization in Lemma
3
and Prop.
4
, we have that

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\lambda\mathcal{F}-(\lambda-1)\Omega\right\},

(20)

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\cl(\lambda\mathcal{F}-(\lambda-1)\Omega)\right\}.

We will then show that
\omega\in\lambda\mathcal{F}-(\lambda-1)\Omega\iff\omega\in\cl(\lambda\mathcal{F}-(\lambda-1)\Omega)
for all
\lambda
. To this end, consider a convergent sequence
\{\lambda\sigma_{n}-(\lambda-1)\tau_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
with
\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{F},\tau_{n}\in\Omega
. Compactness of
\mathcal{F}
gives that there exists a subsequence
\{\sigma_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}
which converges to some
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
. Noting that
\{\lambda\sigma_{n_{s}}-(\lambda-1)\tau_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}
is a subsequence of a convergent sequence and therefore must converge to the same limit
\omega
, we have that the sequence

\displaystyle\{\lambda\sigma_{n_{s}}-(\lambda-1)\tau_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}-\{\lambda\sigma_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}

\displaystyle=-(\lambda-1)\{\tau_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}

(21)

is a sum of two convergent sequences and thus is convergent itself, hence
\{\tau_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}\to\tau\in\Omega
by the closedness of
\Omega
. Altogether, this gives

\displaystyle\{\lambda\sigma_{n_{s}}-(\lambda-1)\tau_{n_{s}}\}_{n_{s}}\to\lambda\sigma-(\lambda-1)\tau

(22)

and so
\omega=\lambda\sigma-(\lambda-1)\tau\in\lambda\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{C}
.
∎

We remark that a lower semicontinuous variant of
R^{s}
can be defined in full analogy with the results above:

\displaystyle\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\,\inf_{\left\|\xi-\omega\right\|_{\Omega}\leq\varepsilon}R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\xi)

(23)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\{\xi_{n}\}_{n}\in\mathcal{V}}\left\{\left.1+\lambda\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\frac{\xi_{n}+\lambda\delta_{n}}{1+\lambda}\in\mathcal{F},\;\delta_{n}\in\mathcal{F},\;\{\xi_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;0\leq\left\langle W,\sigma\right\rangle\leq 1\;\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\right\}.

An analogous compactness condition clearly suffices for the equality
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
as well.

IV
Robustness as a resource monotone

In order to fully motivate the use of
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
as a resource quantifier, we now characterize its properties in the axiomatic framework of resource monotones discussed in Sec.
II.3
.

For completeness, we describe the properties of both the usual definition of the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
as well as the lower semicontinuous robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
.

Lemma 8
.

For any closed and convex set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Omega
, the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
is:

(i)

convex,

(ii)

faithful, i.e., any
\omega\in\Omega
satisfies
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\geq 1
and
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=1\iff\omega\in\mathcal{F}
,

(iii)

monotonic on average under free operations, i.e., for any probabilistic protocol
\{\Phi_{i}\}_{i}
consisting of subchannels which map
\omega
to
\Phi_{i}(\omega)
with corresponding probability
p_{i}=\Tr\Phi_{i}(\omega)
and each
\Phi_{i}
satisfying
\Phi_{i}[\mathcal{F}]\subseteq\cone(\mathcal{F})
, we have

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\geq\sum_{i}p_{i}R_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\Phi_{i}(\omega)}{p_{i}}\right).

(24)

Proof.

Point (i) is obvious from convexity of
\mathcal{F}
. To show faithfulness in point (ii), write

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=\inf\left\{\left.\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\preceq_{\mathcal{C}}\sigma^{\prime},\;\sigma^{\prime}\in\cone(\mathcal{F})\right\}.

(25)

Clearly, if
\omega\in\mathcal{F}
, then
\omega
itself is a feasible solution and thus
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\leq 1
; the fact that
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\geq\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\geq 1
follows by taking
U
as a feasible solution in the dual (
19
). Conversely,
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=1
means that there exists a sequence
\{\sigma^{\prime}_{k}\}_{k}\in\cone(\mathcal{F})
satisfying
\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\omega\succeq_{\mathcal{C}}0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}\right\rangle=1.

(26)

But since
\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\omega\right\rangle=\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}\right\rangle-1
, we have that

\displaystyle 0=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\omega\right\rangle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\omega\right\|_{\Omega}

(27)

where the last equality follows since
\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\omega\in\mathcal{C}
and
\Omega
is a base for
\mathcal{C}

Hartkämper and Neumann 1974
(cf.
(
Lami 2018
, 1.37)
). We conclude that
\omega\in\cone(\mathcal{F})
, which can only be the case when
\omega\in\mathcal{F}
.

For (iii), consider any
\sigma^{\prime}\in\cone(\mathcal{F})
such that
\omega\preceq\sigma^{\prime}
. For any
\Phi_{i}
as in the Theorem, we have that

\displaystyle\Phi_{i}(\omega)\preceq\Phi_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\in\cone(\mathcal{F})

(28)

due to the fact that each
\Phi_{i}
preserves the state cone
\mathcal{C}
. Then,

\displaystyle R_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\Phi_{i}(\omega)}{p_{i}}\right)\leq\frac{\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\right\rangle}{p_{i}}

(29)

which gives

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}R_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\Phi_{i}(\omega)}{p_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}p_{i}\left(\frac{\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\right\rangle}{p_{i}}\right)

(30)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle,

where the last equality follows from the fact that
\sum_{i}\Phi_{i}
must be normalization-preserving for the whole protocol to constitute a valid physical transformation. Since this lower bound on
\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle
holds for any feasible
\sigma^{\prime}
, it must also hold for the the greatest lower bound
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
, which concludes the proof.
∎

We proceed to show that the l.s.c. robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
also satisfies the resource-theoretic requirements, in addition to the desirable property of lower semicontinuity.

Theorem 9
.

For any closed and convex set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Omega
, the l.s.c. robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
is:

(i)

convex,

(ii)

lower semicontinuous, i.e.,
\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\leq\liminf_{n\to\infty}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega_{n})
for any sequence
\{\omega_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
,

(iii)

faithful,

(iv)

monotonic on average under free operations.

Proof.

(i) Convexity follows from the convexity of
R_{\mathcal{F}}
, or can be straightforwardly shown using the dual formulation of
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
. Lower semicontinuity (ii) follows by definition.

To see that faithfulness (iii) holds, notice that the existence of a sequence
\{\sigma_{n}-\tau_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
with
\sigma_{n}\in\mathcal{F},\tau_{n}\in\mathcal{C}
and
\left\langle U,\sigma_{n}\right\rangle\to\left\langle U,\omega\right\rangle
means that
0=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\langle U,\tau_{n}\right\rangle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left\|\tau_{n}\right\|_{\Omega}
, so
\{\tau_{n}\}_{n}\to 0
. This gives that, in fact,
\{\sigma_{n}\}_{n}=\{\sigma_{n}-\tau_{n}\}_{n}+\{\tau_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
. We thus have that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)=1\Rightarrow\omega\in\mathcal{F}
, and the other direction follows directly from the faithfulness of
R_{\mathcal{F}}
.

To establish the strong monotonicity (iv) of
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, take any feasible
\lambda
such that
\omega\in\cl(\lambda\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{C})
, which means that there exists a sequence
\{\lambda\sigma_{k}-\tau_{k}\}_{k}\to\omega
with
\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{F},\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
. Now, since
\Phi_{i}
preserves the state cone and necessarily satisfies
\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(x)\right\rangle\leq\left\langle U,x\right\rangle
due to the normalization of
\sum_{i}\Phi_{i}
, each
\Phi_{i}
cannot increase the base norm. This can be seen by taking any feasible
a_{\pm}
in
\left\|x\right\|_{\Omega}=\inf\left\{\left.\left\langle U,a_{+}\right\rangle+\left\langle U,a_{-}\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;x=a_{+}-a_{-},\,a_{\pm}\in\mathcal{C}\right\}
and noticing that

\displaystyle\left\|\Phi_{i}(x)\right\|_{\Omega}\leq\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(a_{+})\right\rangle+\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(a_{-})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\left\langle U,a_{+}\right\rangle+\left\langle U,a_{i}\right\rangle.

(31)

Then

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\omega-\lambda\sigma_{k}+\tau_{k}\right\|_{\Omega}

(32)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\Phi_{i}(\omega)-\lambda\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})+\Phi_{i}(\tau_{k})\right\|_{\Omega}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\left\|\Phi_{i}(\omega)-\lambda\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})\right\rangle\frac{\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})}{\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})\right\rangle}+\Phi_{i}(\tau_{k})\right\|_{\Omega}

\displaystyle\geq 0

which means that, for each
\Phi_{i}(\omega)
there exists a sequence
\{\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\tau^{\prime}_{k}\}_{k}
converging to
\Phi_{i}(\omega)
with
\lambda^{\prime}_{k}=\lambda\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})\right\rangle
.
We then get

\displaystyle\sum_{i}p_{i}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\frac{\Phi_{i}(\omega)}{p_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}\left(\Phi_{i}(\omega)\right)

(33)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\liminf_{k\to\infty}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\tau^{\prime}_{k})

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}\liminf_{k\to\infty}\lambda\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\liminf_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i}\lambda\left\langle U,\Phi_{i}(\sigma_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda

where the first inequality is due to the lower semicontinuity of
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, the second inequality follows since
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\tau^{\prime}_{k})\leq R_{\mathcal{F}}(\lambda^{\prime}_{k}\sigma^{\prime}_{k}-\tau^{\prime}_{k})\leq\lambda^{\prime}_{k}
, the third inequality is a straightforward consequence of the definition of
\liminf
, and the last equality follows since each
\sigma_{k}
is a normalized state and
\sum_{i}\Phi_{i}
preserves normalization.
∎

We remark that the (strictly weaker) notion of strong monotonicity under selective measurements, often considered in quantum theory
Vidal 2000
;
Chitambar and Gour 2019
, can be recovered as a special case of our result by identifying each subchannel
\Phi_{i}
with a single Kraus operator
K_{i}\cdot K_{i}^{\dagger}
.

V
Robustness as the advantage in discrimination tasks

The task of state discrimination forms the foundation of the operational aspects of quantum theory
Holevo 1973
;
Helstrom 1976
;
Kitaev 1997
;
Chefles 2000
;
Childs et al. 2000
;
Acín 2001
;
Bae and Kwek 2015
;
Jenčová 2014
;
Watrous 2018
as well as GPTs
Ludwig 1985
;
Hartkämper and Neumann 1974
;
Kimura et al. 2010
;
Bae et al. 2016
;
Lami et al. 2018a
. The setup of the problem is as follows: after a state is randomly chosen from an ensemble
\{p_{i},\omega_{i}\}
, where
\omega_{i}\in\Omega
and each
p_{i}
denotes the corresponding probability, the goal is to correctly guess which of the states has been selected by performing a measurement
\{M_{i}\}
on the output state. As the figure of merit, we consider the expected probability of successfully distinguishing the states,

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\{p_{i},\omega_{i}\},\{M_{i}\})=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left\langle M_{i},\omega_{i}\right\rangle.

(34)

To characterize the optimal guessing probability, the quantity
p_{\mathrm{succ}}
can then be maximized over all choices of
\{M_{i}\}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{M}_{n}
denotes the set of all
n
-effect measurements on the given space. Importantly, state discrimination reveals a fundamental relation between the base norm of the given GPT and state distinguishability — for a fixed ensemble of two states, the base norm distance quantifies the maximal probability of successfully discriminating the states
Ludwig 1983
;
Kimura et al. 2010
:

\displaystyle\sup_{\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}\left(\{p_{i},\omega_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2},\mathbb{M}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\big(\!\left\|p_{1}\omega_{1}-p_{2}\omega_{2}\right\|_{\Omega}+1\big).

(35)

However, the discrimination of more than two states is much less well understood, and there are no general quantitative results which describe it as concisely.

Channel discrimination is a related task, where the goal is to distinguish between channels sampled from an ensemble
\{p_{i},\Lambda_{i}\}
with
\Lambda_{i}:\mathcal{V}\to\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
. Here, one can use a fixed state
\omega
as a probe system, which is sent through the randomly chosen channel and afterwards measured with a measurement
\{M_{i}\}
. Hence, the average probability of success for a given channel ensemble, input state, and output measurement is given by

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{succ}}\left(\{p_{i},\Lambda_{i}\},\{M_{i}\},\omega\right)=\sum_{i}p_{i}\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\right\rangle.

(36)

We remark that this can be regarded as a state discrimination of the ensemble
\{p_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\}
.
In addition to the fundamental applications of state discrimination tasks, infinite-dimensional channel discrimination finds use in applications such as quantum illumination
Lloyd 2008
;
Tan et al. 2008
and sensing
Pirandola et al. 2018
.

It is therefore important to characterize the advantages achievable in channel discrimination tasks in various constrained settings motivated by different physical considerations. The resource-theoretic framework lends itself well to such investigations, and we will thus ask a general and broadly applicable question:
how much
better can we discriminate channels by using a resourceful probe state
\omega\notin\mathcal{F}
, compared to a restricted setting in which we are limited to using only the free states
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
?

We will consider the discrimination of channels between two GPTs in spaces
\mathcal{V}
and
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}
, each endowed with a positive cone
\mathcal{C},\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, respectively, and a relevant closed and convex set of free states
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\Omega
,
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}\subseteq\Omega^{\prime}
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{n}
denote the set of
n
-effect measurements
\{M_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\subset\mathcal{V}^{\prime}\textup{*}
, and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
denote the set of all
n
-element channel ensembles of the form
\{p_{i},\Lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with
\sum_{i}p_{i}=1
. Given any choice of an ensemble
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
and an output measurement
\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
, the quantity that we focus on is the advantage provided by a given state over resourceless states. We quantify this through the ratio

\displaystyle\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma)}.

(37)

Note here that, in order to single out the advantage provided by the state
\omega
, we require that the same measurement
\mathbb{M}
be used in both cases.
We then obtain a characterization of the l.s.c. robustness as follows.

Theorem 10
.

For any
\omega\in\Omega
and any closed and convex set
\mathcal{F}
it holds that

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\\
n\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma)}=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega),

(38)

where the maximization is over all possible channel discrimination tasks. If
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)<\infty
, there exists a choice of
\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
,
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
which achieves the outermost supremum on the left-hand side.

Proof.

Consider any sequence
\{\lambda\sigma_{k}-\tau_{k}\}_{k}\to\omega
such that
\sigma_{k}\in\mathcal{F},\tau_{k}\in\mathcal{C}
. Then, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, any
\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}
, and any
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
, it holds that

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\right\rangle

(39)

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\lambda\sigma_{k}-\tau_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\limsup_{k\to\infty}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\lambda\sigma_{k})\right\rangle

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\sigma^{\prime})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\lambda\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma^{\prime})

where in the second line we used the continuity of
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\cdot)
for fixed
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathbb{M}
, and in the third line we used that
\omega\in\mathcal{C}\Rightarrow\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and each
M_{i}\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\textup{*}
, hence
\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\lambda\sigma_{k}-\tau_{k})\right\rangle\leq\left\langle M_{i},\Lambda_{i}(\lambda\sigma_{k})\right\rangle\,\forall k
. Since this holds for any feasible
\lambda
, we have

\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)

(40)

\displaystyle\leq\inf\left\{\left.\lambda\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma^{\prime})\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\omega\in\cl(\lambda\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{C})\right\}

\displaystyle=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)\,\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma^{\prime})

by definition of the robustness. This gives an upper bound on
\displaystyle\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma^{\prime})}
for any
n
,
\mathbb{M}
, and
\mathcal{A}
, which means that the least upper bound obeys

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\\
n\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma^{\prime})}\leq\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega).

(41)

For the other inequality, let
W\in\mathcal{V}\textup{*}
be any feasible dual solution such that
W\in\mathcal{C}\textup{*}
and
\left\langle W,\sigma\right\rangle\leq 1\;\forall\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
. Consider then the measurement
\mathbb{M}^{\prime}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
defined as
\{W/\left\|W\right\|_{\Omega}^{\circ},\,U-W/\left\|W\right\|_{\Omega}^{\circ}\}
and the channel ensemble
\mathcal{A}^{\prime}
given by the probability distribution
\{1,0\}
and channels
\{\mathrm{id},\Lambda^{\prime}\}
with
\Lambda^{\prime}
arbitrary. This gives

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}\\
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\\
n\in\mathbb{N}\end{subarray}}\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathbb{M}^{\prime},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A}^{\prime},\mathbb{M}^{\prime},\sigma)}

(42)

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{1}{\left\|W\right\|_{\Omega}^{\circ}}\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle}{\frac{1}{\left\|W\right\|_{\Omega}^{\circ}}\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle W,\sigma\right\rangle}

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle.

Optimizing over all feasible
W
, we have that the supremum on the left-hand side must equal
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
. Furthermore, from Prop.
2
we know that when
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)<\infty
, there exists an optimal choice of
W
such that
\left\langle W,\omega\right\rangle=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
, which allows us to construct the measurement
\mathbb{M}^{\prime}
achieving this value.
∎

The result establishes the l.s.c. generalized robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
as a precise quantifier of the advantage provided by a given state over all resourceless states
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
. This gives the measure a direct operational application, elevating
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
from a monotone defined through geometric considerations to a physically meaningful quantity, and revealing a general connection between discrimination tasks and resource quantifiers based on conic optimization problems. This not only extends the previous relations of this type found in general finite-dimensional resource theories of states
Takagi et al. 2019
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
, but adds to an increasing list of discrimination settings in which robustness-based quantifiers play a vital role
Piani and Watrous 2015
;
Piani et al. 2016
;
Takagi and Regula 2019
;
Uola et al. 2019
;
Uola et al. 2020b
;
Yuan et al. 2020
;
Skrzypczyk and Linden 2019
;
Oszmaniec and Biswas 2019
.

Several extensions of the result in Thm.
10
can be considered. In some settings in quantum mechanics, entanglement can be employed to increase the probability of success in discrimination tasks — by only sending part of an entangled probe system
\omega
through the channel, the correlations at the output can be exploited to distinguish channels more effectively
Kitaev 1997
. In order to single out the resource theory corresponding to the set
\mathcal{F}
as the source of all advantages, we have not considered possible trade-offs between entanglement and other resources here.

Furthermore, the advantage provided by a given state
\omega
could change if one allows different measurements to be used in the discrimination of
\{\Lambda_{i}(\omega)\}
and in the discrimination of
\{\Lambda_{i}(\sigma)\}
. Again, we do not consider this modified setting here explicitly in order to maintain full generality of our results.
To illustrate why allowing different measurements in (
38
) could lead to significantly different behavior, consider a state
\omega
for which
2<\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)<\infty
. By Theorem
10
, there exist
\mathcal{A}\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
and
\mathbb{M}\in\mathcal{M}_{2}
satisfying that

\frac{p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma)}>2.

(43)

Since
p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\omega)\leq 1
always holds, it follows that
\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}p_{\mathrm{succ}}(\mathcal{A},\mathbb{M},\sigma)<1/2
. But a success probability of
1/2
can always be achieved with a maximum likelihood guess. What happens is that the guessing strategy
\mathbb{M}
is so poor — when applied to free states — that even random guessing outperforms it. In this case, it would therefore be more natural to replace the
\mathbb{M}
in the denominator on the left-hand side of (
38
) with a new variable
\mathbb{M}^{\prime}
. Rather than compromising the applicability of Theorem
10
, this argument shows that it expresses the best of itself when the channel ensemble has more than
1/\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
elements. Luckily enough, this is often the case in applications — the interested Reader can find an example showcasing this at the end of Section
VII.1.3
.

Interestingly, for some finite-dimensional resources, the robustness measure
R_{\mathcal{F}}
was shown to remain the figure of merit — that is, the maximal advantage provided by a given state — in both of the extended settings that we described above. This holds in particular for discrete-variable quantum entanglement
Takagi et al. 2019
;
Bae et al. 2019
. It would be an interesting question to investigate cases where this can be established also in the infinite-dimensional regime.

VI
Quantum mechanics

We will now fix a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
be the Banach space of trace-class operators on
\mathcal{H}
, and
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
its continuous dual, the Banach space of bounded linear operators on
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\subseteq\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
be the subset of density operators. Here the cone
\mathcal{C}
corresponds to the cone of positive operators in
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, while
\mathcal{C}\textup{*}
is the cone of positive operators in
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
; we will use
\succeq
to denote inequality with respect to either of these. The base norm is given by the trace norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{1}
. We use the notation
\left\langle A,B\right\rangle
for the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\Tr(A^{\dagger}B)
. Given a state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
, we will write
\psi
for
\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}
.

This section complements the dedicated paper
Regula et al. 2021
, in which we focus on the case of continuous-variable quantum resources. In particular, the discussion below constitutes a technical companion to Ref.
Regula et al. 2021
, providing a detailed derivation and several extensions of the results mentioned there. Since all of our results for general GTPs immediately apply to quantum mechanics as a special case, the results of previous sections already serve as proofs of some of the results of
Regula et al. 2021
. Specifically, Thm. 1 in
Regula et al. 2021
is Thm.
10
here; Thm. 2 in
Regula et al. 2021
is Thm.
9
here; Thm. 3 in
Regula et al. 2021
is a consequence of our characterization in Sec.
III
and in particular the duality result in Prop.
2
. The other results will be established below.

VI.1
Strong duality

Returning to our discussion in Sec.
III.1
, it is of interest to ask when strong duality holds, that is, when the two optimization problems
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
and
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\omega)
have the same optimal value. This would allow one to simplify the application of the robustness, as the optimization over sequences
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n}\to\omega
in the definition of
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
(see Cor.
6
) would no longer be necessary.

First, we remark that insight from finite-dimensional spaces does not easily generalize here. In finite-dimensional theories, the existence of a single state
\sigma\in\sint(\mathcal{C})\cap\mathcal{F}
is a necessary and sufficient condition to ensure that the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
remains finite for all states
Datta 2009
, and indeed also ensures the strong duality of the measure
Takagi and Regula 2019
. In infinite-dimensional spaces, the conditions become more complicated, and one can easily see that an analogous requirement cannot be fulfilled.

Lemma 11
.

If
\mathcal{H}
is infinite dimensional, there is no state
\sigma
for which it holds that
\forall\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\;\exists\,\lambda\geq 0\text{ s.t. }\rho\preceq\lambda\sigma
.

Proof.

This is a consequence of the fact that a choice of
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
such that
\rho\preceq\lambda\sigma
exists if and only if
\ran(\sqrt{\rho})\subseteq\ran(\sqrt{\sigma})

Douglas 1966
, but since each
\sqrt{\sigma}
is a compact operator, its range cannot be the whole space
\mathcal{H}

(
Conway 1985
, p. 177)
.

To see this more explicitly, assume such a state
\sigma
exists. Clearly, its support must be the whole
\mathcal{H}
. Let
\sigma=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|
be its spectral decomposition, with
p_{n}>0
for all
n
and
\{\ket{e_{n}}\}_{n}
an orthonormal set. Set
\ket{\psi}\coloneqq\sum_{n}\sqrt{p_{n}}\ket{e_{n}}
, and for
N\in\mathbb{N}
consider the truncated states
\ket{\phi_{N}}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}^{-1}\right)^{-1/2}\sum_{n=0}^{N}p_{n}^{-1/2}\ket{e_{n}}
. If
|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\preceq\lambda\sigma
were to hold for some real
\lambda
, then evaluating this on
\ket{\phi_{N}}
would yield
N\leq\lambda
. Since this would need to hold for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we arrive at a contradiction.
∎

As a consequence, one might expect the robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
to be infinite for some states — indeed, the existence of infinitely resourceful states is often a natural property for infinite-dimensional systems
Eisert et al. 2002a
;
Keyl et al. 2003
, so it is not a surprising fact.

The non-existence of interior points of
\mathcal{C}
motivates us to study alternative approaches to strong duality. In order to establish a useful sufficient condition for this property, recall that the space
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
of trace-class operators can be regarded as the Banach dual space
(
Megginson 1998
, Definition 1.10.1)
of
\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})
, the space of compact operators on
\mathcal{H}
. Regarding the spaces as the dual pair
\sigma(\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H}),\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}))
, trace-class operators can then be endowed with the weak* topology induced by the seminorms of the form
\left|\left\langle\cdot,K\right\rangle\right|
for all
K\in\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{H})

(
Megginson 1998
, Definition 2.6.1)
. This gives us the following condition.

Theorem 12
.

If the conic hull
\cone(\mathcal{F})
is closed in the weak* topology, then strong duality holds and we have
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)
and
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)=\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)
for all
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
.

Proof.

We will show that
\mathcal{B}\coloneqq\conv(\mathcal{F}\cup(-\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})))
is closed in the trace norm topology and invoke Prop.
4
to prove that strong duality for
R_{\mathcal{F}}
is satisfied. To this end, define the subnormalized sets
\mathcal{F}_{\leq}\coloneqq\left\{\left.\lambda\sigma\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\lambda\in[0,1],\;\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\right\}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\leq}\coloneqq\left\{\left.\lambda\rho\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\lambda\in[0,1],\;\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})\right\}
. Recall that the unit ball
\mathcal{U}=\left\{\left.X\in\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\|X\right\|_{1}\leq 1\right\}
is weak*-compact by the Banach-Alaoglu theorem
(
Megginson 1998
, Theorem 2.6.18)
. Noting that
\cone(\mathcal{F})
is weak*-closed by assumption and
\mathcal{C}=\cone(\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}))
is readily verified to be weak*-closed, we have that
\mathcal{F}_{\leq}=\cone(\mathcal{F})\cap\mathcal{U}
and
\mathcal{D}_{\leq}=\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{U}
are both intersections of a weak*-compact and a weak*-closed set, meaning that they are both weak*-compact. But then
\mathcal{B}=\conv(\mathcal{F}_{\leq}\cup(-\mathcal{D}_{\leq}))
is the convex hull of a union of two convex, weak*-compact sets, and is thus weak*-compact itself, which in particular implies that it is weak*-closed. As the weak* topology is coarser than the norm topology on
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})
, we have that
\mathcal{B}
is norm-closed as desired.

An analogous statement holds for
\conv(\mathcal{F}\cup(-\mathcal{F}))
, showing that also
R^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
satisfies strong duality.
∎

Using the above result and our previously established conditions, we will see that most resources of practical relevance do indeed satisfy strong duality. Examples are:

(i)

Nonclassicality theory (see Sec.
VII.1
). Here, the cone
\cone(\mathcal{F})
was recently shown to be weak*-closed in Ref.
Ferrari et al. 2020
, so we immediately get strong duality using Thm.
12
.

(ii)

Entanglement theory. We will explicitly show this using Thm.
12
in Sec.
VII.2
.

(iii)

Coherence theory. We will explicitly show this using Thm.
12
in Sec.
VII.4
.

(iv)

Athermality (thermodynamics). Here, the set
\mathcal{F}
consists of a single state (the Gibbs state), which is clearly compact in any of the considered topologies, with strong duality following by Prop.
7
or Thm.
12
.

(v)

Energy-constrained resource theories. Specifically, for a general closed and convex set
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, one can define the restriction
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}=\left\{\left.\sigma\in\mathcal{F}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\langle H,\sigma\right\rangle\leq E\right\}
where
H
is an unbounded positive self-adjoint operator with a discrete spectrum of finite multiplicity. Any such set
\mathcal{F}^{\prime}
is in fact compact
Holevo 2003
, hence strong duality for
R_{\mathcal{F}^{\prime}}
is ensured by Prop.
7
.

The constraints on the energy of the system as in (v) are a common way to avoid discontinuities in infinite dimensions
Wehrl 1978
;
Eisert et al. 2002a
;
Holevo 2003
;
Winter 2016
. The assumptions on the allowed Hamiltonian are standard — any Hamiltonian obeying the so-called Gibbs hypothesis
Winter 2016
, i.e., such that
\Tr e^{-\beta H}<\infty
for all
\beta>0
, will indeed be an unbounded operator with a discrete spectrum of finite multiplicity.

A complete characterization of resources for which
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)
is an interesting question which we leave for future work.

VI.2
General bounds for the robustness

We will now present a couple of handy results that will allow us to compute the robustness of pure states.

Lemma 13
.

Let
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
and an arbitrary set of free states
\mathcal{F}
, it holds that

R_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)=\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi},

(44)

where
\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi}=\left\|\sigma^{-1/2}\ket{\psi}\right\|^{2}
, and the infimum is restricted to those free states
\sigma
such that
\ket{\psi}\in\mathrm{dom}\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\right)=\ran\left(\sigma^{1/2}\right)
, with
\mathrm{dom}
denoting the domain.

Proof.

We employ the characterization in the second line of (
8
). For a fixed state
\sigma
, it is easy to see that
\min\left\{\lambda:|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\leq\lambda\sigma\right\}=\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi}
. In fact, on the one hand, by the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for every vector
\ket{x}
we have that

\displaystyle\left|\braket{x|\psi}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\braket{x|\sigma^{1/2}\sigma^{-1/2}|\psi}\right|^{2}

(45)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sigma^{1/2}\ket{x}\right\|^{2}\left\|\sigma^{-1/2}\ket{\psi}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi}\braket{x|\sigma|x}

\displaystyle=\braket{x|\left(\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi}\sigma\right)|x},

implying that
|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\leq\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi}\sigma
. On the other hand, diagonalize
\sigma
as
\sigma=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}p_{n}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|
, where
p_{n}>0
for all
n
without loss of generality, and set
\ket{\phi_{N}}\coloneqq\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}^{-1}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|\right)\ket{\psi}
. Let
\lambda
be such that
|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\leq\lambda\sigma
. Taking the overlap of both sides with the vector
\ket{\phi_{N}}
yields

\displaystyle\braket{\psi|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}^{-1}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|\right)|\psi}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\braket{\phi_{N}|\psi}\right|^{2}

(46)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda\braket{\phi_{N}|\sigma|\phi_{N}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\braket{\psi|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}^{-1}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|\right)|\psi}.

Note that since
\ket{\psi}\in\mathrm{dom}\left(\sigma^{-1/2}\right)
we must have that
\braket{e_{n}|\psi}\neq 0
for some
n
, in turn implying that
\braket{\psi|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}^{-1}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|\right)|\psi}>0
. By simplifying we obtain that
\lambda\geq\braket{\psi|\left(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}^{-1}|{e_{n}}\rangle\!\langle{e_{n}}|\right)|\psi}=\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}p_{n}^{-1}\left|\braket{\psi|e_{n}}\right|^{2}
. Taking the limit
N\to\infty
yields
\lambda\geq\braket{\psi|\sigma^{-1}|\psi}
, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma 14
.

For any states
\rho,\omega\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)\geq\frac{\left\langle\rho,\omega\right\rangle}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle\sigma,\omega\right\rangle}.

(47)

In particular,

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\geq\frac{1}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\lvert\braket{\psi|\sigma|\psi}\rvert^{2}}

(48)

for any pure state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}
.

Proof.

The first claim is easily proved by noting that any
\frac{\omega}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle\sigma,\omega\right\rangle}
is a feasible dual solution in (
19
). The second claim follows by taking
\omega=|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|
.
∎

An interesting property of the pure-state bound in Eq. (
48
) is that it can relate the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
to the minimal relative entropy distance from the set of free states. Indeed, the robustness always provides an upper bound to the minimal relative entropy as
Datta 2009
;
Berta et al. 2015

\displaystyle\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}D(\rho\|\sigma)\leq\log R_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho),

(49)

where
D(\rho\|\sigma)=\left\langle\rho,\log\rho-\log\sigma\right\rangle
is the quantum (Umegaki) relative entropy. In certain cases, this bound can be tight.

Corollary 15
.

Whenever
R_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)
equals
\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\lvert\braket{\psi|\sigma|\psi}\rvert^{2}}
,
it holds that
\displaystyle\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}D(\psi\|\sigma)=\log R_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)
.

Proof.

The result follows since the condition is equivalent to

\displaystyle\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\log\frac{1}{\left\langle\psi,\sigma\right\rangle}\eqqcolon\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}D_{\min}(\psi\|\sigma)

\displaystyle=\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}D_{\max}(\psi\|\sigma)

(50)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\log R_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi),

and
D_{\min}(\psi\|\cdot)
(Rényi relative entropy of order 0) and
D_{\max}(\psi\|\cdot)
(sandwiched Rényi relative entropy of order
\infty
) provide, respectively, a lower and upper bound to the Umegaki relative entropy
Datta 2009
.∎

In finite-dimensional spaces, the condition of the Corollary is always satisfied for maximally resourceful states in any convex resource theory
Liu et al. 2019
;
Regula et al. 2020
, and can also hold for broader classes of states obeying certain symmetries
Bravyi et al. 2019
;
Seddon et al. 2021
. Although it is unclear if states of this kind can always be found in infinite-dimensional theories, we will find such examples for relevant resources.

VI.3
Robustness and seminorms

Many resources of practical relevance have a structure defined by free
pure
states and convex combinations thereof. Examples include the resource theory of entanglement, nonclassicality, non-Gaussianity, and coherence. Although the results we considered above apply to more general theories, it will be useful to study additional properties that this class of resources enjoys.

We then consider a norm-closed set
{f}\subseteq\mathcal{H}
of free pure states in the underlying Hilbert space, and define the set of free states
\mathcal{F}_{f}
to consist of convex combinations of such free states:

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{f}\coloneqq\cl\conv\left\{\left.\ket{v}\!\bra{v}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{v}\in{f}\right\}.

(51)

Here, the closure can be taken either in the weak topology or the norm topology, as the two coincide for convex sets. This definition is equivalent to
Holevo et al. 2005

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{f}=\left\{\left.\int_{{f}}\ket{v}\!\bra{v}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\ket{v}\!\bra{v})\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\mu\in P({f})\right\}.

(52)

where
P({f})
is the set of Borel probability measures supported on the closed set
\left\{\left.\ket{v}\!\bra{v}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{v}\in{f}\right\}
.

Remark 16
.

Taking the closure is, in general, necessary. In particular, when
{f}
denotes the set of product states on the tensor product of two infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces, Holevo, Shirokov, and Werner
Holevo et al. 2005
showed the existence of states
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}_{f}
which cannot be written as a convex combination
\sum_{k=1}^{r}p_{k}\ket{v_{k}}\!\bra{v_{k}}
with
\sum_{k=1}^{r}p_{k}=1
and
\ket{v_{k}}\in{f}
, even when one allows infinite discrete combinations with
r=\infty
.

We will assume without loss of generality that
{f}
is balanced, i.e.,
\ket{v}\in{f}\Rightarrow\lambda\ket{v}\in{f}
for any
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
s.t.
|\lambda|=1
. This condition can be easily fulfilled for any non-balanced set
{f}^{\prime}
by defining
{f}=\left\{\left.\lambda\ket{v}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;|\lambda|=1,\;\ket{v}\in{f}^{\prime}\right\}
, since the corresponding sets
\mathcal{F}_{f}
are the same.

Given any such set, define the seminorm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{f}^{\circ}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
by

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{x}\right\|_{f}^{\circ}\coloneqq\sup\left\{\left.\left|\braket{x|v}\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{v}\in{f}\right\},

(53)

and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{f}\colon\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
by

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{y}\right\|_{f}\coloneqq\sup\left\{\left.\left|\braket{y|x}\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\|\ket{x}\right\|_{f}^{\circ}\leq 1\right\}.

(54)

Arveson
Arveson 2009
characterized this function and explicitly showed that it is convex, absolutely homogeneous, and lower semicontinuous. In fact, it corresponds to the gauge function (Minkowski functional) of the set
\cl\conv{f}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{y}\right\|_{f}=\inf\left\{\left.\mu>0\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{y}\in\mu\cl\conv{f}\right\}.

(55)

However, although we follow
Arveson 2009
in using norm-like notation for both functions,
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{f}
defines a valid norm on
\mathcal{H}
only if
\cl\conv{f}
has nonempty interior, which is generally not the case. Indeed, we will later see that
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{f}
can be infinite in some cases.

Ref.
Arveson 2009
then extends
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{f}^{\circ}
to a seminorm on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
:

\displaystyle\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}^{\circ}\coloneqq\sup\left\{\left.\left|\braket{v|Z|u}\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{v},\ket{u}\in{f}\right\}.

(56)

With a slight modification of what was done in
Arveson 2009
, we define a function
\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{R}_{+}\cup\{\infty\}
as

\displaystyle\left\|X\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}\coloneqq\sup\left\{\left.\left|\left\langle Z,X\right\rangle\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}^{\circ}\leq 1\right\},

(57)

where the optimization is over operators
Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
. The optimization can be restricted to self-adjoint
Z
whenever
X
is self-adjoint.

We will now relate this function with the robustness.

Proposition 17
.

For any
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
, it holds that

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)\leq\left\|\rho\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}.

(58)

For any rank-one state
\psi=\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}
, it further holds that

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)=\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}=\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}^{2}.

(59)

Proof.

We begin by noticing that, for any positive self-adjoint operator
Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
and pure states
\ket{u},\ket{v}
, the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality for the inner product on
\mathcal{H}
gives

\displaystyle\left|\braket{u|Z|v}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\sqrt{Z}\ket{u},\sqrt{Z}\ket{v}\right\rangle\right|^{2}

(60)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\sqrt{Z}\ket{u}\right\|^{2}\left\|\sqrt{Z}\ket{v}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\braket{u|Z|u}\braket{v|Z|v}

where
\sqrt{Z}
is the positive square root of
Z
. This implies that, for any
Z\succeq 0
, we get

\displaystyle\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}^{\circ}=\sup\left\{\left.\left|\braket{v|Z|v}\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{v}\in{f}\right\}

(61)

since optimizing over
\ket{u},\ket{v}\in{f}
cannot achieve a higher value of
\left|\braket{u|Z|v}\right|
. This gives

\displaystyle\left\|\rho\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\left|\left\langle Z,\rho\right\rangle\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}\leq 1\right\}

(62)

\displaystyle\geq\sup\left\{\left.\left|\left\langle Z,\rho\right\rangle\right|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;Z\succeq 0,\,\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}}^{\circ}\leq 1\right\}

\displaystyle=\sup\left\{\left.\left\langle Z,\rho\right\rangle\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;Z\succeq 0,\;\braket{v|Z|v}\leq 1\;\forall\ket{v}\in{f}\right\}

\displaystyle=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\rho)

where the last equality follows from the dual formulation of the robustness (see Cor.
6
).

Using an argument based on
(
Arveson 2009
, 7.2)
, we can then relate
\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}
with
\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}^{2}
for any pure state. Notice that for any
\ket{\psi}
we have
\ket{\psi}/\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}\in\cl\conv{f}
, so for all
Z\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
it holds that

\displaystyle\left|\frac{\bra{\psi}}{\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}}Z\frac{\ket{\psi}}{\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}}\right|\leq\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}^{\circ}.

(63)

This then implies that

\displaystyle\left\|\psi\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}=\sup_{\left\|Z\right\|_{\mathcal{F}_{f}}^{\circ}\leq 1}\lvert\braket{\psi|Z|\psi}\rvert\leq\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|^{2}_{f}.

(64)

Together with Eq. (
62
) we have thus shown that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\leq\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{{f}}^{2}
, and so it remains to show the other inequality. To this end, consider any feasible
\ket{x}\in\mathcal{H}
such that
\left\|\ket{x}\right\|_{f}^{\circ}\leq 1
. The operator
W=\ket{x}\!\bra{x}
is then clearly positive, and

\displaystyle\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{F}}\left\langle W,\sigma\right\rangle=\sup_{\ket{v}\in{f}}\braket{v|W|v}=(\left\|\ket{x}\right\|_{f}^{\circ})^{2}\leq 1

(65)

which means that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\geq\left\langle W,\psi\right\rangle=\left|\braket{\psi|x}\right|^{2}
. Optimizing over all feasible
\ket{x}
gives
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}(\psi)\geq\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}^{2}
.
∎

The Theorem extends a general relation from finite-dimensional spaces
Regula 2018
, which includes several well-known correspondences: the generalized robustness of entanglement was previously shown to equal the squared sum of Schmidt coefficients of a pure state
Vidal and Tarrach 1999
;
Steiner 2003
;
Harrow and Nielsen 2003
(which is indeed the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{f}
in this case
Rudolph 2001
), and the generalized robustness of coherence was shown to equal the
\ell^{1}
norm for pure states
Piani et al. 2016
.

A useful way to view the result of the Theorem is as follows: for any rank-one state
\psi
, it suffices to optimize over rank-one witnesses in the dual formulation of Eq. (
19
).

VII
Examples and applications

We now consider explicit applications of our results in the characterization of several important quantum resources: optical nonclassicality, entanglement, coherence, and genuine non-Gaussianity.

VII.1
Nonclassicality

Coherent states
Schrödinger 1926
;
Klauder 1960
;
Glauber 1963
;
Sudarshan 1963
and their probabilistic mixtures are widely recognized as the most classical among all quantum states of a quantum harmonic oscillator, and hence defined as classical states. From a resource theory perspective, the identification of this particular set of states is motivated by the fact that they can be easily produced and manipulated with standard techniques in quantum optical settings. Moreover, states which are nonclassical according to this distinction can be exploited to obtain operational advantages in applications such as linear optical quantum computation
Knill et al. 2001
, quantum metrology
Yadin et al. 2018
;
Kwon et al. 2019
and entanglement generation
Aharonov et al. 1966
;
Kim et al. 2002
;
Wang 2002
;
Asbóth et al. 2005
.

Formally, we consider the quantum theory of a single harmonic oscillator. The corresponding Hilbert space is then
\mathcal{H}_{1}\coloneqq L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
, i.e., the space of square-integrable function on the real line. The annihilation and creation operators, denoted with
a,a^{\dagger}
, respectively, satisfy the commutation relations
[a,a^{\dagger}]=I
. Fock states are defined by
\ket{n}\coloneqq(a^{\dagger})^{n}\ket{0}/\sqrt{n!}
, where
\ket{0}
is the vacuum state. For a complex number
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}
, the corresponding coherent state is given by
\ket{\alpha}\coloneqq e^{-|\alpha|^{2}/2}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{\alpha^{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\ket{n}=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\ket{0}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}\coloneqq e^{\alpha a^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}a}
is a displacement operator. We deem free all the so-called classical states
Bach and Lüxmann-Ellinghaus 1986
;
Yadin et al. 2018
. Mathematically, we set
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{C}\coloneqq\cl\conv\left\{|{\alpha}\rangle\!\langle{\alpha}|:\alpha\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
; this can equivalently be understood as the set of states whose Glauber–Sudarshan
P
representation yields a valid probability distribution
Glauber 1963
;
Sudarshan 1963
;
Bach and Lüxmann-Ellinghaus 1986
. Among the simplest and most useful classical state is undoubtedly the thermal state with mean photon number
N\in[0,\infty)
, defined by

\tau_{N}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N+1}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{N}{N+1}\right)^{n}|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|.

(66)

Notable examples of nonclassical states, instead, include the Fock states themselves, as well as the so-called squeezed states, obtained by letting a squeezing operations act on the vacuum, i.e.,

\displaystyle S(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\exp\left[\frac{r}{2}\left((a^{\dagger})^{2}-a^{2}\right)\right],

(67)

\displaystyle\ket{\zeta_{r}}

\displaystyle\coloneqq S(r)\ket{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\cosh(r)}}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sqrt{\binom{2n}{n}}\tanh(r)^{n}\ket{2n},

(68)

where we assume that
r\geq 0

(
Barnett and Radmore 2002
, Eq. (3.7.2) and (3.7.5))
.

Recalling from the result in Sec.
VI.1
that strong duality holds in this resource theory, we have
R_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)
as well as
R^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)=\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)
for all states.

VII.1.1
Infinite standard robustness

We begin by establishing that the standard robustness
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}
is in fact infinite for most physically accessible states in this resource theory.

Lemma 18
.

Any
m
-mode state
\rho
with finite standard robustness of nonclassicality
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)<\infty
has a bounded normal-ordered characteristic function
\chi^{\rho}_{1}:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
, where
\chi_{1}^{\rho}(\alpha)\coloneqq e^{|\alpha|^{2}/2}\Tr[\rho\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}]
. Specifically,

\left\|\chi_{1}^{\rho}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\leq 2\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)-1\,,

(69)

where
\left\|\chi_{1}^{\rho}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\left|\chi_{1}^{\rho}(\alpha)\right|
.

Proof.

If
r\coloneqq R^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)=\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)<\infty
, for every
\epsilon>0
there exists a free state
\sigma\in\mathcal{F}
such that
\omega=\frac{\rho+(r+\epsilon-1)\sigma}{r+\epsilon}\in\mathcal{F}
. Both
\sigma
and
\omega
have positive
P
-functions, and hence their Fourier transform, i.e.
\chi_{1}^{\sigma}(\alpha)
and
\chi_{1}^{\omega}(\alpha)
, are positive-definite
Bochner 1933
;
Richter and Vogel 2002
;
Bohmann and Agudelo 2020
. In this context, a function
f:\mathbb{C}\to\mathbb{C}
is called positive definite if the matrix
\left(f(\alpha_{\mu}-\alpha_{\nu})\right)_{\mu,\nu=1,\ldots,N}
is positive semi-definite for all collections
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{N}\in\mathbb{C}
. This in turn implies
|f(\alpha)|\leq|f(0)|
, and in particular that
f
is bounded. The relation which defines the characteristic functions is linear, and hence

\chi^{\omega}_{1}(\alpha)=\frac{\chi^{\rho}_{1}(\alpha)+(r+\epsilon-1)\chi^{\sigma}_{1}(\alpha)}{r+\epsilon}\,.

(70)

Hence, also
\chi^{\rho}_{1}(\alpha)
has to be bounded, and moreover

\displaystyle\left\|\chi_{1}^{\rho}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

\displaystyle\leq(r+\epsilon)\left\|\chi_{1}^{\omega}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}+(r+\epsilon-1)\left\|\chi_{1}^{\sigma}\right\|_{L^{\infty}}

(71)

\displaystyle=(r+\epsilon)\left|\chi_{1}^{\omega}(0)\right|+(r+\epsilon-1)\left|\chi_{1}^{\sigma}(0)\right|

\displaystyle=2(r+\epsilon)-1\,,

where in the last step we used that
\chi_{1}^{\tau}(0)=\Tr[\tau\mathcal{D}_{0}]=\Tr[\tau]=1
for all density operators
\tau
. Since
\epsilon>0
was arbitrary, we deduce (
69
).
∎

Examples of states with unbounded
\chi_{1}
functions (and hence infinite standard robustness of nonclassicality) comprise Fock, squeezed and cat states, but also some physically achievable approximations thereof. Consider for instance a squeezed thermal state

\rho_{N,r}\coloneqq S(r)\tau_{N}S^{\dagger}(r)\,,

(72)

where
S(r)
is given by (
67
) and
\tau_{N}
by (
66
). A state such as that in (
72
) is a reasonable physical approximation to a pure squeezed state, one that can actually be achieved in a laboratory. It is well known that
\rho_{N,r}
is nonclassical if and only if
e^{2r}>2N+1
. It is also straightforward to show that its normal-ordered characteristic function evaluates to
\chi_{1}^{\rho_{N,r}}(\alpha)=e^{|\alpha|^{2}/2}e^{-\frac{1}{2}(2N+1)\left(e^{-2r}\alpha_{R}^{2}+e^{2r}\alpha_{I}^{2}\right)}
, where
\alpha_{R}\coloneqq\Re\alpha
and
\alpha_{I}\coloneqq\Im\alpha
. Therefore,
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{N,r})=+\infty
as soon as
\rho_{N,r}
is nonclassical. It is not difficult to generalize this statement to all Gaussian states, so that
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}
becomes trivial (namely, either
0
or
+\infty
) on this whole set of states.

But there is more: the next Proposition proves that a large class of pure (possibly non-Gaussian) states also have unbounded
\chi_{1}
function.

Proposition 19
.

Let
\ket{\psi}
be a nonclassical pure state having a vanishing overlap with a finite (possibly empty) set of coherent states. Then,
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\psi)=\infty
.

Proof.

Let us write
\ket{\psi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}\ket{n}
. The function
f(\alpha)=e^{|\alpha|^{2}/2}\braket{\psi|\alpha}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{c^{*}_{n}}{\sqrt{n!}}\alpha^{n}
is a complex entire function of order at most 2 (otherwise,
|\braket{\psi|\alpha}|
would diverge). If
N<\infty
is the number of zeros of
f(\alpha)
, its Hadamard factorization
Krantz 1999
becomes
f(\alpha)=e^{a\alpha^{2}+b\alpha}P_{N}(\alpha)
, where
P_{N}(\alpha)
is a polynomial of degree
N
and
|a|<\frac{1}{2}
in order for
|\braket{\psi|\alpha}|
to be bounded. The Husimi Q-function is then

\displaystyle Q^{\psi}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}|f(\alpha)|^{2}

(73)

\displaystyle=e^{-|\alpha|^{2}}e^{2\Re[a\alpha^{2}+b\alpha]}|P_{N}(\alpha)|^{2}

\displaystyle=e^{-\mathbf{r}(\alpha)^{T}A\mathbf{r}(\alpha)+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\mathbf{r}(\alpha)}|P_{N}(\alpha)|^{2}\,,

with

\mathbf{r}(\alpha)=\begin{pmatrix}\Re\alpha\\
\Im\alpha\end{pmatrix},\quad A=\begin{pmatrix}1-2\Re a&2\Im a\\
2\Im a&1+2\Re a\end{pmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{\beta}=\begin{pmatrix}2\Re b\\
-2\Im b\end{pmatrix}\,.

It is easy to see that the matrix
A
has eigenvalues
1\pm 2|a|
. The Fourier transform of a function in the form (
73
) has again the same structure, but with
A^{-1}
in place of
A
. Now, let us suppose for the moment that
a>0
. In this case,
A
has an eigenvalue strictly bigger than 1, and hence
A^{-1}
has one strictly smaller than 1. Thus,
\chi_{1}^{\psi}=e^{|\alpha|^{2}}\chi_{-1}^{\psi}
, where
\chi_{-1}^{\psi}
is the Fourier transform of
Q^{\psi}
, is necessarily unbounded and, by virtue of Lemma
18
, we conclude that
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}(\psi)=\infty
. If instead
a=0
, we have
A=A^{-1}
. At this point, we have to make a subsequent distinction: if
P_{N}
is the trivial polynomial,
\psi
is a coherent state and hence is obviously classical; if
P_{N}
is not trivial we end up once again with a divergent
\chi_{1}^{\psi}
(in this case the divergence is polynomial instead of exponential).
∎

Among the states which fulfil the hypothesis of the last result there are e.g. any finite superposition of Fock states and any nonclassical Gaussian state. It is worth noticing that the celebrated cat state
\ket{\alpha_{+}}\propto\ket{\alpha}+\ket{-\alpha}
is a pure state with vanishing overlap with infinitely many coherent states, but with unbounded
\chi_{1}
and hence infinite standard robustness of nonclassicality.

We remark that the number of coherent states for which
\braket{\psi|\alpha}
vanishes has been considered in
Chabaud et al. 2020
as the so-called stellar rank, related to the degree of non-Gaussianity of a state.

Let us briefly clarify an apparent similarity between the standard robustness of nonclassicality and the notion of Glauber–Sudarshan P representation
Glauber 1963
;
Sudarshan 1963
. Specifically, recalling that any state
\sigma\in\mathcal{C}
can be written as
\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha}\!\bra{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\alpha)
for some Borel probability measure
\mu
on the set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\coloneqq\{\ket{\alpha}\!\bra{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}

Bach and Lüxmann-Ellinghaus 1986
;
Holevo et al. 2005
, we can write the standard robustness
R^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}
as the least coefficient
\lambda
such that

\displaystyle\rho=\lambda\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha}\!\bra{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{+}(\alpha)-(\lambda-1)\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha}\!\bra{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\mu_{-}(\alpha)

(74)

for some two probability measures
\mu_{+},\mu_{-}
. Notice that any two measures
\mu_{\pm}
give rise to a signed measure (i.e., one allowing negative values)
\mu^{\prime}=\mu_{+}-\mu_{-}
; conversely, one can use the Hahn–Jordan decomposition theorem to write any signed measure on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
as the difference of two non-negative measures
\mu_{\pm}

(
Rudin 1966
, Section 6.6)
. This shows that the standard robustness (or, specifically,
R^{s}_{\mathcal{C}}-1
) admits a natural interpretation as the
negativity
of the resource (cf.
Tan et al. 2020
), in the sense that it quantifies the minimal negative part of a signed measure
\mu^{\prime}
such that

\displaystyle\rho=\int_{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}\ket{\alpha}\!\bra{\alpha}\,\mathrm{d}\mu^{\prime}(\alpha).

(75)

An important difference between this expression and the Glauber–Sudarshan P representation is that the latter is based on quasiprobability distributions over the set
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, which are a strictly more general concept than signed Borel measures.
In particular, we can see from Prop.
19
that there exist states which cannot be written as in (
75
) for any signed measure
\mu
, even though it is known that any state admits a representation in terms of a quasiprobability distribution on
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
in the form of the P representation
Glauber 1963
;
Sudarshan 1963
.

VII.1.2
Fock states

Proposition 20
.

For any
n>0
,

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\ket{n}\!\bra{n})=e^{n}\frac{n!}{n^{n}}.

(76)

Proof.

Define

\displaystyle\gamma_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\,\lvert\braket{\alpha|n}\rvert^{2}

(77)

\displaystyle=\sup_{a\geq 0}\,e^{-a}\frac{a^{n}}{n!}

\displaystyle=e^{-n}\frac{n^{n}}{n!}.

Lemma
14
then immediately gives

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\ket{n}\!\bra{n})\geq\gamma_{n}^{-1}.

(78)

Inspired by the methods used to compute the nonclassical trace distance of Fock states
Hillery 1987
;
Nair 2017
, consider now the phase-randomized coherent state

\displaystyle\sigma_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\ket{\sqrt{n}e^{i\theta}}\!\bra{\sqrt{n}e^{i\theta}}\mathrm{d}\theta

(79)

\displaystyle=e^{-n}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{n^{k}}{k!}\ket{k}\!\bra{k}.

Using Lemma
13
, we get

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\ket{n}\!\bra{n})

\displaystyle\leq\braket{n|\sigma_{n}^{-1}|n}

(80)

\displaystyle=e^{n}\frac{n!}{n^{n}}

\displaystyle=\gamma_{n}^{-1}.

The upper and lower bounds thus coincide, and we have
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\ket{n}\!\bra{n})=\gamma_{n}^{-1}
for any
n>0
.
∎

We note that the bound from Lemma
14
that we employed in the proof can be rephrased in terms of a geometric measure of nonclassicality based on the Husimi Q function, which was previously studied in several works
Wünsche et al. 2001
;
Malbouisson and Baseia 2003
;
Nair 2017
;
Marian and Marian 2020
. In particular, we have
\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho)\geq\frac{\left\langle\rho,\omega\right\rangle}{\pi Q_{\max}(\omega)}
for any state
\rho,\omega
, where
Q_{\max}(\omega)=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}Q_{\omega}(\alpha)
with
Q_{\omega}(\alpha)=\frac{1}{\pi}\braket{\alpha|\omega|\alpha}
being the Husimi Q-function.

We also have from Cor.
15
that the above immediately implies

\displaystyle\inf_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}D(\ket{n}\!\bra{n}\|\sigma)=\log\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\ket{n}\!\bra{n})=-\log\gamma_{n}.

(81)

This expression for the relative entropy of nonclassicality has been independently shown in
Ferrari et al. 2020
.

We can furthermore compute the robustness for the noisy Fock state

\displaystyle\rho_{n,t}\coloneqq t\ket{n}\!\bra{n}+(1-t)\sigma_{n}.

(82)

On the one hand, convexity of
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}
gives

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{n,t})\leq t\gamma_{n}^{-1}+(1-t),

(83)

and on the other hand using the feasible dual solution
W=\frac{\ket{n}\!\bra{n}}{\gamma_{n}}
gives

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{n,t})

\displaystyle\geq\left\langle W,\rho_{n,t}\right\rangle

(84)

\displaystyle=t\gamma_{n}^{-1}+(1-t)\frac{\braket{n|\sigma_{n}|n}}{\gamma_{n}}

\displaystyle=t\gamma_{n}^{-1}+(1-t).

In a similar way, we can bound the robustness of the mixed state

\displaystyle\rho_{n,q}\coloneqq q\ket{n}\!\bra{n}+(1-q)\ket{0}\!\bra{0}

(85)

as

\displaystyle q\gamma_{n}^{-1}\leq\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{n,q})\leq q\gamma_{n}^{-1}+(1-q).

(86)

VII.1.3
Squeezed states

Proposition 21
.

The robustness of nonclassicality of the squeezed states (

\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\zeta_{r})=e^{r}

For any squeezed state

\sigma_{s}\coloneqq S(s)\tau_{N(s)}S^{\dagger}(s),\qquad N(s)\coloneqq\frac{e^{2s}-1}{2},

where

and

2
2

2

The quantum covariance matrix of an

where

\displaystyle g(r,s)\coloneqq

\displaystyle\ \braket{\zeta_{r}|\sigma_{s}^{-1}|\zeta_{r}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \braket{0|S^{\dagger}(r)S(s)\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}S^{\dagger}(s)S(r)|0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ \braket{\zeta_{r-s}|\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}|\zeta_{r-s}}

Before we move on, we wish to remark that squeezed states can be used to demonstrate a particularly simple application of Theorem
10
to the theory of nonclassicality. Consider in fact a channel ensemble composed of several displacement operators, whose action is to translate the input state by a different amount along the same line in phase space; this can be understood as “classical message encoders” that encode a message on the input states in the form of a displacement parameter. Using a probe whose initial state is highly squeezed along the same direction allows for almost perfect discrimination of this ensemble. On the contrary, if the initial state is restricted to be classical, even the best measurement at the output will still yield a significant error. The robustness of the squeezed state, which we just computed in Proposition

VII.1.6
Multi-mode nonclassicality

The Hilbert space modelling
m
harmonic oscillators (or modes) is
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
, which can be written as a tensor product of the single-mode spaces
\mathcal{H}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\cdots\otimes L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
.
The theory of multi-mode nonclassicality admits a remarkably simple structure, in the sense that any
m
-mode coherent state is of the form
\ket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}=\ket{\alpha_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes\ket{\alpha_{m}}
with
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m})\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
. This leads to the property that the robustness of nonclassicality is, in fact, multiplicative for products of single-mode states.

Proposition 24
.

Let
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
be a collection of single-mode quantum states
\rho_{i}\in\mathcal{D}(L^{2}(\mathbb{R}))
. Then

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bigotimes_{i=1}^{m}\rho_{i}\right)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{i}).

(113)

Proof.

We will use
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to denote the set of classical states of the given mode. Let
\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{C}_{i}
be feasible states such that
\rho_{i}\preceq\lambda_{i}\sigma_{i}
. Then,
\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\preceq\left(\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}\right)\bigotimes_{i}\sigma_{i}
— this can be seen for
m=2
from the fact that

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\preceq(\lambda_{1}\sigma_{1}-\rho_{1})\otimes\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}+\rho_{1}\otimes(\lambda_{2}\sigma_{2}-\rho_{2})

(114)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}(\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2})-\rho_{1}\otimes\rho_{2}

where we used that the tensor product of positive operators is positive, and an extension to arbitrary
m
is immediate. Since
\bigotimes_{i}\sigma_{i}\in\mathcal{C}
, this shows in particular that feasible solutions for
R_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{i})=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{i})
can be used to construct a feasible solution for
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\right)
, leading to
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\right)\leq\prod_{i}\lambda_{i}
. As this holds for any feasible choice of
\lambda_{i}
, it must also hold for their infimum, which implies the desired relation
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\right)\leq\prod_{i}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{i})
.

Let
W_{i}
be feasible dual solutions for
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{i})
, with each
W_{i}\succeq 0
and
\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}_{i}}\left\langle W_{i},\sigma\right\rangle\leq 1
. Then
\bigotimes_{i}W_{i}\succeq 0
and

\displaystyle\sup_{\sigma\in\mathcal{C}}\left\langle\bigotimes_{i}W_{i},\sigma\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\mathbb{C}^{m}}\left\langle\bigotimes_{i}W_{i},\ket{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\!\bra{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\right\rangle

(115)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}}\left\langle\bigotimes_{i}W_{i},\bigotimes_{i}\ket{\alpha_{i}}\!\bra{\alpha_{i}}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{C}}\prod_{i}\braket{\alpha_{i}|W_{i}|\alpha_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq 1,

where in the first line we used that the supremum of a continuous linear functional over a set
\mathcal{S}
is equal to its supremum over
\cl\conv\mathcal{S}
, and in the second line we used that every
m
-mode coherent state is a product of single-mode coherent states. This means that
\bigotimes_{i}W_{i}
is a feasible witness for
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\right)
. Therefore
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}\left(\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\right)\geq\left\langle\bigotimes_{i}W_{i},\bigotimes_{i}\rho_{i}\right\rangle=\prod_{i}\left\langle W_{i},\rho_{i}\right\rangle
, and since this holds for any feasible
W_{i}
, it must also hold for their least upper bounds
\prod_{i}\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}(\rho_{i})
.
∎

One can notice that the submultiplicativity of the robustness
R_{\mathcal{F}}
in fact holds in any resource theory such that
\sigma_{1}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}),\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{2})\Rightarrow\sigma_{1}\otimes\sigma_{2}\in\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{H}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{2})
, but the full multiplicativity relies on the particular structure of pure states in the theory of nonclassicality.

VII.2
Entanglement

The foremost example of a quantum resource theory is entanglement theory
Werner 1989
;
Bennett et al. 1996a
;
Bennett et al. 1996b
;
Horodecki et al. 2009
(cf.
(
Chitambar and Gour 2019
, Sec. IV.A)
). In spite of its historical importance, comparatively little is known on the properties of entanglement in infinite-dimensional systems
Eisert et al. 2002a
;
Holevo et al. 2005
;
Shirokov 2010
;
Shirokov 2018
;
Ferrari et al. 2020
. Throughout this section, we will specialize our results to this case, and establish the corresponding robustness as a valid entanglement measure in the infinite-dimensional setting.

We consider a tensor product of two separable Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}=\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
. The set of free pure states is defined to consist of product vectors:

{f}=\left\{\left.\ket{\phi}\otimes\ket{\tau}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{\phi}\in\mathcal{H}_{A},\;\ket{\tau}\in\mathcal{H}_{B},\,\braket{\phi|\phi}=1=\braket{\tau|\tau}\right\}.

The closure of all convex combinations of such states defines the set of separable states
\mathcal{S}=\cl\conv\left\{\left.\ket{\psi}\!\bra{\psi}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{\psi}\in{f}\right\}
.

VII.2.1
Strong duality

We start our investigation by showing that Thm.
12
applies, thus implying that
R_{\mathcal{S}}=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
holds in this case as well.

Lemma 25
.

The cone
\cone(\mathcal{S})
of separable operators is closed in the weak* topology. Thus, for all states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})
it holds that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)=R_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
and
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)=R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
.

Proof.

Let
\{\ket{n}_{A}\}_{n}
and
\{\ket{m}_{B}\}_{m}
be orthonormal bases of the local Hilbert spaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
. For
N\in\mathbb{N}
, consider the subspaces
\mathcal{H}_{A}^{N}\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\{\ket{0}_{A},\ldots,\ket{N}_{A}\}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}^{N}\coloneqq\mathrm{span}\{\ket{0}_{B},\ldots,\ket{N}_{B}\}
, with corresponding projectors
P_{A}^{N}
and
P_{B}^{N}
. Define the mappings
\mathcal{P}_{N}:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\to\mathcal{T}\left(\mathcal{H}_{A}^{N}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}^{N}\right)
given by

\mathcal{P}_{N}(\cdot)\coloneqq P_{A}^{N}\otimes P_{B}^{N}(\cdot)P_{A}^{N}\otimes P_{B}^{N},

(116)

and construct the sets

\displaystyle{f}^{N}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\{\left.\ket{\phi}\!\otimes\!\ket{\tau}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{\phi}\!\in\!\mathcal{H}_{A}^{N},\;\ket{\tau}\!\in\!\mathcal{H}_{B}^{N},\,\braket{\phi|\phi}=\!1\!=\braket{\tau|\tau}\right\},

(117)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}^{N}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\conv\left(\left\{\left.|{\psi}\rangle\!\langle{\psi}|\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\ket{\psi}\in{f}^{N}\right\}\right).

(118)

Now, it is not difficult to verify that

\cone(\mathcal{S})=\bigcap_{N\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{P}_{N}^{-1}\left(\cone(\mathcal{S}_{N})\right).

(119)

In fact, on the one hand any
X\in\cone(\mathcal{S})
clearly satisfies that
\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)\in\cone(\mathcal{S}_{N})
. On the other, let
X
be a non-zero trace-class operator such that
\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)\in\cone(\mathcal{S}_{N})\subseteq\cone(\mathcal{S})
for all
N\in\mathbb{N}
. First, it is elementary to see that
X\geq 0
is in fact positive semidefinite. Second, it holds that
\lim_{N\to\infty}\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)]=\Tr[X]
, because
X
is trace-class. Incidentally, since
X\geq 0
and
X\neq 0
, we have that
\Tr X>0
. Therefore, we can apply the gentle measurement lemma
(
Winter 1999
, Lemma 9)
and conclude that
X_{N}\coloneqq\frac{\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)}{\Tr[\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)]}\in\mathcal{S}_{N}\subset\mathcal{S}
satisfies that

\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac{\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)}{\Tr\left[\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)\right]}=\frac{X}{\Tr[X]},

(120)

where the limit is with respect to the trace norm topology. Since
\mathcal{S}
is closed, we deduce that
\frac{X}{\Tr[X]}\in\mathcal{S}
and hence that
X\in\cone(\mathcal{S})
, as claimed.

Having established (
119
), let us see why this ensures that
\cone(\mathcal{S})
is weak*-closed. Note that the range of the map
\mathcal{P}_{N}
is finite-dimensional and the entries of
\mathcal{P}_{N}(X)
are weak*-continuous functions of
X
for a fixed
N
, so that the map
\mathcal{P}_{N}
is itself weak*-continuous. Since
\cone(\mathcal{S}_{N})
is closed, it follows that
\mathcal{P}_{N}^{-1}\left(\cone(\mathcal{S}_{N})\right)
is weak*-closed as well. Being an intersection of weak*-closed sets,
\cone(\mathcal{S})
is itself weak*-closed. Thus, an application of Thm.
12
concludes the proof.
∎

VII.2.2
Pure states

Having established the equality between the robustness and its lower semicontinuous version for the case of entanglement, we turn to the problem of computing the resulting function for pure states. Recall that, for any pure state
\ket{\psi}
, there exist orthonormal bases
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{H}_{A},\{\ket{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\in\mathcal{H}_{B}
such that
\ket{\psi}
can be written as

\displaystyle\ket{\psi}=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\ket{ii}

(121)

where
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a monotonically non-increasing, square-summable sequence in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
referred to as the Schmidt coefficients of
\ket{\psi}
. When at least one of
\mathcal{H}_{A}
and
\mathcal{H}_{B}
is finite-dimensional, there are at most
d=\min\{\dim\mathcal{H}_{A},\dim\mathcal{H}_{B}\}
non-zero Schmidt coefficients, and we have
Rudolph 2001

\displaystyle\left\|\ket{\psi}\right\|_{f}=\sum_{i=0}^{d}\mu_{i}.

(122)

We will extend the above also to the infinite-dimensional case, leading to a computable expression for the robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)
of any pure state.

The case of entanglement theory is peculiar because for pure states this same expression yields also the standard robustness
R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}
. While this has been established by Vidal and Tarrach
Vidal and Tarrach 1999
for the finite-dimensional case, we now extend their result to our general infinite-dimensional setting.

Proposition 26
.

Consider any pure state
\ket{\psi}\in\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B}
and let
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be the sequence of its Schmidt coefficients. Then

\displaystyle\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)=\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\right)^{2}.

(123)

In particular, a pure state has
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)<\infty
if and only if the sum
\sum_{i}\mu_{i}
converges.

Proof.

Set
\mu\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}
. Since the inequality
R_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\leq R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)
holds by construction, we need to show that
R_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\geq\mu^{2}
and
R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\leq\mu^{2}
.
To prove the latter inequality, define the family of vectors
\ket{w_{\xi}}\coloneqq\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\xi^{i}\ket{ii}
, where
\xi\in(0,1)
. Note that
\{\xi^{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{R})
, and consider the positive bounded operator
W_{\xi}=\ket{w_{\xi}}\!\bra{w_{\xi}}
. The fact that
\left\|W_{\xi}\right\|_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\leq 1
can be shown from a result by Shimony
Shimony 1995
, but we will prove this explicitly for completeness. Since any product state can be written as
\ket{v}=\ket{v_{A}}\otimes\ket{v_{B}}\coloneqq(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}\ket{k})\otimes(\sum_{l=0}^{\infty}b_{l}\ket{l})
with
\left\|\ket{v_{A}}\right\|=\left\|\ket{v_{B}}\right\|=1
using the Schmidt bases of
\ket{\psi}
, for any
\ket{v}\in{f}
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle W_{\xi},v\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\langle v|W_{\xi}|v\right\rangle

(124)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j=0}^{\infty}\xi^{i}\xi^{j}a_{i}a_{j}^{*}b_{i}b_{j}^{*}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\xi^{i}a_{i}b_{i}\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\xi^{i}a_{i}b_{i}\right)^{*}

\displaystyle=\left|\left\langle\Delta(W_{\xi}),|{v_{B}}\rangle\!\langle{v_{A}^{*}}|\right\rangle\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Delta(W_{\xi})\right\|^{2}_{\infty}\left\|\ket{v_{A}}\right\|^{2}\left\|\ket{v_{B}}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 1

where we used
\Delta(W_{\xi})
to denote the positive operator
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\xi^{i}\ket{i}\!\bra{i}
, and in the last line we used that
\xi^{i}\leq 1\;\forall i
. This shows that
\left\|W_{\xi}\right\|_{\mathcal{S}}^{\circ}\leq 1
and hence we can take
W_{\xi}
as a feasible solution for the robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
, giving
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\geq\left\langle W_{\xi},\psi\right\rangle
for any
\xi\in(0,1)
. This then gives

\displaystyle\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)

\displaystyle\geq\lim_{\xi\to 1^{-}}\left\langle W_{\xi},\psi\right\rangle

(125)

\displaystyle=\left(\lim_{\xi\to 1^{-}}\textbf{}\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\xi^{i}\mu_{i}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mu_{i}\right)^{2},

where in the third line we used Abel’s theorem, which extends also to the case when the series of
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
diverges to infinity.
We conclude that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\geq\mu^{2}
, which in particular implies that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)=\infty
when
\{\mu_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}\notin\ell^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

It remains to show that
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)=R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\leq\mu^{2}
, where we shall now assume that
\mu<\infty
. To this end, for
\theta\in[0,2\pi]
, define

\ket{\psi_{\theta}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mu^{1/2}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}e^{i\,2^{j}\theta}\mu_{j}^{1/2}\ket{j}

(126)

in either
\mathcal{H}_{A}
or
\mathcal{H}_{B}
, where the basis employed here is the Schmidt basis of
\ket{\psi}
(Eq. (
121
)). The state

\omega\coloneqq\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,\psi_{\theta}\otimes\psi_{\theta}^{\intercal}

(127)

is separable by construction. For indices
j,k,\ell,m\in\mathbb{N}
, we compute

\displaystyle\braket{jk|\omega|\ell m}

(128)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\sqrt{\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\mu_{m}}\,e^{i\,2^{j}\theta}e^{-i\,2^{k}\theta}e^{-i\,2^{\ell}\theta}e^{i\,2^{m}\theta}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\mu^{2}}\sqrt{\mu_{j}\mu_{k}\mu_{\ell}\mu_{m}}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{d\theta}{2\pi}\,\exp\left(i\left[2^{j}+2^{m}-2^{k}-2^{\ell}\right]\theta\right).

Since the binary representation of any integer is unique,
2^{j}+2^{m}\neq 2^{k}+2^{\ell}
unless either
j=k
and
\ell=m
or
j=\ell
and
k=m
. Therefore,

\braket{jk|\omega|\ell m}=\frac{\mu_{j}\mu_{m}}{\mu^{2}}\left(\delta_{j,k}\delta_{\ell,m}+\delta_{j,\ell}\delta_{k,m}-\delta_{j,k,\ell,m}\right),

(129)

where
\delta_{j,k,\ell,m}=1
if
j=k=\ell=m
, and
\delta_{j,k,\ell,m}=0
otherwise.
The last state we need to define is

\sigma\coloneqq\frac{1}{\mu^{2}-1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j,m=0,1,\ldots\\
j\neq m\end{subarray}}\mu_{j}\mu_{m}|{jm}\rangle\!\langle{jm}|,

(130)

which is also separable by construction. Now, consider that

\displaystyle\psi+\left(\mu^{2}\!-\!1\right)\sigma

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,m}\mu_{j}\mu_{m}|{jj}\rangle\!\langle{mm}|+\sum_{j\neq m}\mu_{j}\mu_{m}|{jm}\rangle\!\langle{jm}|

(131)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j,k,l,m}\mu_{j}\mu_{m}\left(\delta_{j,k}\delta_{\ell,m}+\delta_{j,\ell}\delta_{k,m}-\delta_{j,k,\ell,m}\right)

\displaystyle\times|{jk}\rangle\!\langle{lm}|

\displaystyle=\mu^{2}\omega.

Therefore, thanks to (
7
) we immediately see that
R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)\leq\mu^{2}
, concluding the proof.
∎

We note that
\ell^{1}
is dense in
\ell^{2}
; more generally, it can be shown that the set of states with
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)<\infty
is dense in
\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
with the trace norm topology.

VII.2.3
Comparison with standard robustness

In finite-dimensional quantum mechanics, the two robustnesses
R_{\mathcal{S}}
and
R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}
appear naturally in the quantification of the optimal rates for entanglement distillation and dilution (respectively) under asymptotically non-entangling operations
Brandão and Plenio 2008
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010a
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010b
. Since these two measures reflect complementary tasks, comparing them directly can yield useful insights into some peculiar features of infinite-dimensional entanglement theory. As we have seen, they turn out to coincide for all pure states. This prompts the natural question of whether there exist states that satisfy
R_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)<R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)
. We will now construct an extreme example of this behavior. To this end, let us start by recalling the definition of negativity, an entanglement measure given by
Vidal and Werner 2002

N(\rho)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\rho^{\Gamma}\right\|_{1}-1\right),

(132)

where
\Gamma
denotes partial transposition with respect to either of the subsystems
Peres 1996
. For a pure state
\ket{\psi}
as in (
121
), one finds that
2N(\psi)+1=R_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)=R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\psi)
coincides with the two robustnesses, and its value is given by Prop.
26
. In general, using the fact that separable states have a positive partial transpose, it is not difficult to show that

R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)\geq N(\rho)+1

(133)

for all states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})

Vidal and Werner 2002
.

Our example is based on a famous operator first constructed and studied by Hilbert
Weyl 1908
. Let
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
be the Hilbert space of square-summable complex sequences
(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
(with index starting from
1
), and denote by
\{\ket{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{+}}
its canonical basis. Construct the Hilbert operator
H_{-1}
on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
with matrix representation

(H_{-1})_{n,m}\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\text{if $n=m$,}\\
\frac{1}{n-m}&\text{if $n\neq m$.}\end{array}\right.

(134)

It has been shown by Hilbert himself in his lectures
Weyl 1908
that
H_{-1}
is bounded. Schur
Schur 1911
later proved that in fact

\left\|H_{-1}\right\|_{\infty}=\pi.

(135)

For an excellent account of this and related topics, see the book by Hardy, Littlewood and Pólya
(
Hardy et al. 1952
, Sec. 8.12)
, that by Steele
(
Steele 2004
, Ch. 10)
, and the set of lecture notes by Jameson
Jameson 2018
.

Proposition 27
.

There exist a density operator
\omega
over
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})
such that the corresponding maximally correlated state

\rho[\omega]\coloneqq\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}\omega_{n,m}|{n,n}\rangle\!\langle{m,m}|

(136)

on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes 2}
satisfies that

R_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)\leq 2\,,\qquad R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho)=N(\rho)=+\infty\,.

(137)

Proof.

Note that
H_{-1}^{\intercal}=-H_{-1}
, so that
iH_{-1}
is self-adjoint. We proceed by setting

\displaystyle\omega_{\pm}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{c}\,D\left(\mathbbm{1}\pm\frac{i}{\pi}H_{-1}\right)D,

(138)

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{n}\,|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|,

(139)

\displaystyle d_{n}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}\ln(n+1)},

(140)

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{n}^{2}<\infty.

(141)

We now verify that the state
\rho[\omega_{+}]
constructed as in (
136
) with
\omega_{+}
given by (
138
) satisfies all desired properties. First, thanks to (
135
) we have that
\omega_{\pm}\geq 0
. By definition of
c
, we also see that
\Tr\omega_{\pm}=1
, so that
\omega_{\pm}
are both valid density operator on
\mathcal{H}
. The associated states
\rho_{\pm}\coloneqq\rho[\omega_{\pm}]
then satisfy that

\displaystyle R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho_{\pm})

\displaystyle\geq N(\rho_{\pm})+1

(142)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\rho_{\pm}^{\Gamma}\right\|_{1}+1\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\|\omega_{\pm}\right\|_{\ell^{1}}+1\right),

where
\|X\|_{\ell^{1}}\coloneqq\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}|X_{n,m}|
. Note that

\displaystyle\left\|\omega_{\pm}\right\|_{\ell^{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c}\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}d_{n}d_{m}\left(\delta_{n,m}+\frac{1}{\pi}\left|(H_{-1})_{n,m}\right|\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi c}\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}d_{n}d_{m}\left|(H_{-1})_{n,m}\right|+1

\displaystyle=+\infty\,,

where the last equality is proved in
(
Hardy et al. 1952
, Sec. 8.12, p. 214)
. Therefore,

R^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho_{\pm})=N(\rho_{\pm})=+\infty\,.

(143)

It remains to prove that
R_{\mathcal{S}}(\rho_{\pm})\leq 2
. This follows immediately by observing that

\rho_{\pm}\leq\rho_{+}+\rho_{-}=\frac{2}{c}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}d_{n}^{2}\,|{nn}\rangle\!\langle{nn}|\,,

(144)

and the latter operator is clearly separable. This concludes the proof.
∎

The above example shows that our measure
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{S}}
and the standard robustness
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{S}}
behave in general very differently, with the latter sometimes assigning an infinite value to states that have a finite amount of entanglement according to the former. From an operational standpoint, this could hint at the fact that there exist infinite-dimensional states with finite distillable entanglement but infinite entanglement cost. This intuition relies on the already mentioned fact that the generalized robustness is intimately connected with a type of entanglement distillation, while the standard robustness appears to play an analogous role for entanglement dilution
Brandão and Plenio 2008
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010a
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010b
. To make this connection rigorous, however, one would need to generalize the argument in
Brandão and Plenio 2008
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010a
;
Brandão and Plenio 2010b
, which as stated works in finite-dimensional systems only, to infinite-dimensional ones. We leave this for future works.

VII.3
Non-Gaussianity

Non-Gaussianity is another type of quantum resource characterizing nonclassical features of quantum states defined in continuous-variable systems.
In particular, it is known that non-Gaussian states are required for a number of quantum information processing tasks
Giedke and Ignacio Cirac 2002
;
Eisert et al. 2002b
;
Niset et al. 2009
;
Lloyd and Braunstein 1999
;
Lami et al. 2018b
, motivating its rigorous quantification in a resource-theoretic formulation that considers the set of Gaussian states free
Genoni et al. 2008
;
Genoni and Paris 2010
;
Marian and Marian 2013
.
On the other hand, another operational standpoint where free resources should be what can be easily accessible in experiments leads to a resource theory of genuine non-Gaussianity
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
;
Albarelli et al. 2018
, which includes classical postprocessing and feedforwarded operations in its free operations and entails the closed convex hull of the set of Gaussian states as its free states. (See Ref.
Yamasaki et al. 2020
for a recent application of this framework to non-Gaussian state transformations.)
Genuine non-Gaussian states provide advantages in protocols such as continuous-variable universal quantum computation
Mari and Eisert 2012
;
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
, secure quantum communication
Lee et al. 2019
, and channel discrimination
Takagi et al. 2019
.
The robustness measure established here can then be used to provide a quantitative account of the operational power of genuine non-Gaussianity.
Here, we provide formulas for the robustness of genuine non-Gaussianity for Fock states and single-photon-added/subtracted states.

Let
\ket{\alpha,\xi}:=\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}S(\xi)\ket{0}

with
\alpha=|\alpha|e^{i\phi}
,
\xi=|\xi|e^{i\theta}
denote a single-mode Gaussian state where
S(\xi)=\exp\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\xi(a^{\dagger})^{2}-\xi^{*}a^{2}\right)\right]
is a (rotated) squeezing operator, which reduces to (
67
) when
\phi=0
and
|\xi|=r
.
We take the set of single-mode states without genuine non-Gaussianity as our set of free states, i.e.,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{G}:=\cl\conv\left\{\left.\ket{\alpha,\xi}\!\bra{\alpha,\xi}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\alpha,\xi\in\mathbb{C}\right\}
, as in
Takagi and Zhuang 2018
.

Although it is not clear whether the two expressions of robustness,
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{G}}
and
R_{\mathcal{G}}

VII.4
Quantum coherence

The operational aspects of the principle of superposition have recently been formalized in a resource-theoretic framework as the resource theory of coherence
Baumgratz et al. 2014
;
Streltsov et al. 2017
, with an extension to the infinite-dimensional case considered explicitly in
Zhang et al. 2016
. Due to superposition being a basis-dependent concept, the study of coherence begins by identifying a countable orthonormal basis for the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, denoted by
\{\ket{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
, as the set of free pure states. We stress here the difference from the theory of nonclassicality (Sec.
VII.1
), where the classical states
\{\ket{\alpha}\}_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}
do not form a mutually orthogonal set. The mixed free states — called incoherent states — are then all states diagonal in the given basis,
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\cl\conv\{\ket{i}\!\bra{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. For any operator with matrix representation
X=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}X_{i,j}|{i}\rangle\!\langle{j}|
, the
\ell^{1}
norm is defined as
\left\|X\right\|_{\ell^{1}}=\sum_{i,j=1}^{\infty}|X_{i,j}|
, which can be understood as the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\mathcal{I}}
for this resource theory (see Sec.
VI.3
). It is already known that the standard robustness
R^{s}_{\mathcal{I}}
does not provide a meaningful quantifier of this resource, but the generalized robustness
R_{\mathcal{I}}
has been successfully employed in finite dimensions
Napoli et al. 2016
.

The characterization of the robustness of coherence in infinite dimensions is very similar to our previous findings in the resource theories of entanglement and nonclassicality.
We start by noticing that also in this case the cone of free states is closed with respect to the weak* topology, which makes it possible to apply Thm.
12
and conclude that the lower semicontinuous robustness coincides with its simplified version.

Lemma 31
.

The cone
\cone(\mathcal{I})
is closed in the weak* topology. Thus, for all states
\rho\in\mathcal{D}(\mathcal{H})
it holds that
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{I}}(\rho)=R_{\mathcal{I}}(\rho)
.

Proof.

For
i,j\in\mathbb{N}
, define the functionals
\varphi_{i,j}:\mathcal{T}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{C}
given by
\varphi_{i,j}(X)\coloneqq\braket{i|X|j}
. Note that
\varphi_{i,j}
is weak*-continuous, essentially because the operator
|{j}\rangle\!\langle{i}|
is of finite rank and thus compact. Clearly, denoting by
\mathcal{C}
the cone of positive semidefinite operators on
\mathcal{H}
, it holds that

\cone(\mathcal{I})=\mathcal{C}\cap\bigcap_{i\neq j}\varphi_{i,j}^{-1}(0).

(157)

Since
\mathcal{C}
is weak*-closed, and so are the sets
\varphi_{i,j}^{-1}(0)
because the functionals
\varphi_{i,j}
are weak*-continuous, also
\cone(\mathcal{I})
must be weak*-closed. An application of Thm.
12
completes the proof.
∎

VIII
Discussion

We introduced a general method of quantifying convex resources in infinite-dimensional probabilistic theories through the robustness measure
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
, which provides a non-trivial extension of a finite-dimensional quantifier. We showed that such a measure not only satisfies the properties desired from a bona fide resource monotone — faithfulness, strong monotonicity, lower semicontinuity — but it also admits a direct operational interpretation as a figure of merit in a class of channel discrimination tasks. By studying the conic optimization problems underlying the robustness, we showed that the measure enjoys a useful dual formulation and can always be computed by measuring a single, suitably chosen effect.

We investigated the robustness further in the case of continuous-variable quantum mechanics, establishing a number of results which include more robust strong duality relations as well as lower and upper bounds which aid the quantification of quantum resources. We used our results to compute the robustness exactly for a variety of states in the resource theories of nonclassicality, entanglement, coherence, and genuine non-Gaussianity. By comparing the robustness
\underline{R}_{\mathcal{F}}
with the related standard robustness
\underline{R}^{s}_{\mathcal{F}}
, we showed that the former can remain a useful resource quantifier even in cases when the latter diverges to infinity.

Our contribution here is twofold. First, we established a unified view of the quantification of infinite-dimensional resources and their practical applications in discrimination tasks, laying the foundations for a systematic operational investigation of general resource theories in infinite-dimensional GPTs. Second, together with Ref.
Regula et al. 2021
, we provided readily applicable methods for benchmarking important continuous-variable quantum resources which underlie practical technological applications. We expect the robustness to find use as a meaningful and accessible tool in the study of resources in quantum mechanics and beyond.

Interesting follow-up developments would be to further improve on the characterization of the robustness in settings of interest, in particular through comparison with other common resource measures as well as evaluation of the robustness for larger classes of states. Another question is to consider whether the quantification of the robustness simplifies in restricted settings, such as for Gaussian states or energy-constrained sets of states. Open questions also remain in the characterization of strong duality for the robustness in infinite-dimensional spaces.

, so each of the terms must converge to
0
. We have thus shown the existence of a sequence of the form in (
172
); since any such sequence is also a valid subfeasible sequence, the two statements of the problem are equivalent.

The characterization in Eq. (
173
) is immediate by writing any
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
as
\lambda\sigma
with
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\lambda=\left\langle U,\sigma^{\prime}\right\rangle
.
∎

Remark 37
.

We note an alternative formulation which can be easier to characterize. The set
\mathcal{B}_{1}-\mathcal{K}_{0}
is in fact equivalent to
\mathcal{B}_{1\leq}-\mathcal{K}_{0}
where
\mathcal{B}_{1\leq}
denotes subnormalized states in the cone
\mathcal{K}_{1}
, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}_{1\leq}\coloneqq\left\{\left.\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{1}\;\rule{0.0pt}{9.5pt}\right|\;\left\langle U,\sigma\right\rangle\leq 1\right\}
. Indeed, the inclusion
\subseteq
is obvious; for
\supseteq
, consider that any
x\in\mathcal{B}_{1\leq}-\mathcal{K}_{0}
can be written as
x=\mu\sigma-\tau
for some
\mu\in[0,1]
,
\sigma\in\mathcal{B}_{1}
,
\tau\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
. Since
\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{1}
and
\mathcal{K}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{K}_{0}
, we can then define
\tau^{\prime}=\tau+(1-\mu)\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_{0}
and write
x=\sigma-\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{1}-\mathcal{K}_{0}
.

Proposition
22
.

Let
\ket{\zeta_{r}}_{\pm}
be single-photon-added/subtracted squeezed vacuum states. Then,

e^{-r+1}\cosh^{2}(r)\leq\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}({\zeta_{r}}_{\pm})\leq\frac{4e^{2r}}{3\sqrt{3}\sinh(r)}

(176)

for all
r\geq 0
.

A tighter lower bound can be obtained for
r\leq\ln\sqrt{2}\approx 0.35
as

\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}({\zeta_{r}}_{\pm})\geq e\cosh(r)^{-3},

(177)

and for
r\geq\ln\sqrt{2}
as

\underline{R}_{\mathcal{C}}({\zeta_{r}}_{\pm})\geq\frac{4e^{1+2r}}{27\sinh(r)}.

(178)

Proof.

The lower bounds all follow from (
47
) with suitable choices of
\omega=\ket{\zeta_{q}}_{+}\!\bra{\zeta_{q}}
. Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\left|\braket{\alpha|\zeta_{q}}_{+}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\frac{|\braket{\alpha|a^{\dagger}|\zeta_{q}}|^{2}}{\cosh^{2}(q)}

(179)

\displaystyle=\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\frac{1}{\cosh^{3}(q)}\,|\alpha|^{2}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}+\tanh(q)\Re(\alpha^{2})}

\displaystyle=\sup_{|\alpha|\in[0,\infty)}\frac{1}{\cosh^{3}(q)}\,|\alpha|^{2}e^{-|\alpha|^{2}+\tanh(q)|\alpha|^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{q-1}}{\cosh^{2}(q)}

where in the last equation we used that the supremum is achieved at
|\alpha|^{2}=(1-\tanh(q))^{-1}
and
noting that
\cosh^{3}(q)(1-\tanh(q))=\cosh^{2}(q)e^{-q}
.
We also have

\displaystyle\left|{}_{+}\braket{\zeta_{q}|\zeta_{r}}_{+}\right|^{2}=\frac{\left|\bra{\zeta_{q}}aa^{\dagger}\ket{\zeta_{r}}\right|^{2}}{\cosh^{2}(q)\cosh^{2}(r)}=\frac{1}{\cosh^{3}(r-q)}

(180)

where we used

\displaystyle\left|\bra{\zeta_{q}}aa^{\dagger}\ket{\zeta_{r}}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh(r)\cosh(q)}\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2n+1}{4^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}\left(\tanh(r)\tanh(q)\right)^{n}\right)^{2}

(181)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh(r)\cosh(q)(1-\tanh(r)\tanh(q))^{3}}=\frac{\cosh^{2}(r)\cosh^{2}(q)}{\cosh^{3}(r-q)}.

This gives

\displaystyle\frac{\left|{}_{+}\braket{\zeta_{q}|\zeta_{r}}_{+}\right|^{2}}{\sup_{\alpha\in\mathbb{C}}\left|\braket{\alpha|\zeta_{q}}_{+}\right|^{2}}=\frac{e^{-q+1}\cosh^{2}(q)}{\cosh^{3}(r-q)}

(182)

The general lower bound in Eq. (
176
) follows by choosing
q=r
. For
r\leq\ln\sqrt{2}
, we get a tighter bound with the choice of
q=0
, and for
r\geq\ln\sqrt{2}
we can choose
\displaystyle q=\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(2e^{2r}-3\right)
.

To obtain an upper bound, let us consider the ansatz in (
91
).

\displaystyle g_{\rm PA}(r,s)\coloneqq

\displaystyle{}_{+}\braket{\zeta_{r}|\sigma_{s}^{-1}|\zeta_{r}}_{+}

(183)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(r)}\braket{0|S^{\dagger}(r)aS(s)\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}S^{\dagger}(s)a^{\dagger}S(r)|0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(r)}\braket{\zeta_{r-s}|S^{\dagger}(s)aS(s)\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}S^{\dagger}(s)a^{\dagger}S(s)|\zeta_{r-s}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(r)}\left(\cosh(s){}_{+}\bra{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}+\sinh(s){}_{-}\bra{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}\right)\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}\left(\cosh(s)\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{+}+\sinh(s)\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{-}\right),

where
\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{+}=a^{\dagger}\ket{\zeta_{r-s}}
,
\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{-}=a\ket{\zeta_{r-s}}
are unnormalized single-photon added and subtracted states.
Since
\ket{\zeta_{r-s}}_{+}=\ket{\zeta_{r-s}}_{-}
and thus
\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{-}=\tanh(r-s)\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle g_{\rm PA}(r,s)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\cosh(s)+\sinh(s)\tanh(r-s)}{\cosh(r)}\right)^{2}{}_{+}\braket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}|\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}|\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{+}

(184)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}(r-s)}{}_{+}\braket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}|\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}|\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{+}

Using
\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{e^{-s}}{\cosh(s)}\tanh^{n}(s)\right)^{-1}|{n}\rangle\!\langle{n}|
, we get

\displaystyle{}_{+}\bra{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}\tau_{N(s)}^{-1}\ket{\tilde{\zeta}_{r-s}}_{+}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{s}\cosh(s)}{\cosh(r-s)\tanh(r-s)}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2n+1}{4^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}\left(\frac{\tanh(r-s)}{\tanh(s)}\right)^{2n+1}

(185)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{s}\cosh(s)}{\cosh(r-s)\tanh(r-s)}\frac{\tanh(r-s)}{\tanh(s)}\frac{1}{\left(1-\left(\frac{\tanh(r-s)}{\tanh(s)}\right)^{2}\right)^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{s}\cosh(s)}{\cosh(r-s)}\frac{\tanh^{2}(s)}{\left(\tanh^{2}(s)-\tanh^{2}(r-s)\right)^{3/2}}

where in the second equality we used
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{2n+1}{4^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}x^{2n+1}=x\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{4^{n}}\binom{2n}{n}x^{2n+1}\right)^{\prime}=x\left(x/\sqrt{1-x^{2}}\right)^{\prime}=x/(1-x^{2})^{3/2}
for
|x|<1
.
Then, we get

\displaystyle g_{\rm PA}(r,s)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{s}\cosh(s)}{\cosh^{3}(r-s)}\frac{\tanh^{2}(s)}{\left(\tanh^{2}(s)-\tanh^{2}(r-s)\right)^{3/2}}

(186)

\displaystyle=\frac{\cosh(s)\sinh^{2}(s)}{(1-\tanh(s))\left(\sinh(r)\sinh(2s-r)\right)^{3/2}}

g_{\rm PA}(r,s)
achieves its minimum at
s=s_{0}(r):=\frac{1}{4}\ln\left(4e^{2r}-3\right)
and it can be checked (after tedious calculation) that
g_{\rm PA}(r,s_{0}(r))=\frac{4e^{2r}}{3\sqrt{3}\sinh(r)}
, which gives the upper bound.
∎
