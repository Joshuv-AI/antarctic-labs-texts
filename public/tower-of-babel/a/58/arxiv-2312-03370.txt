Minimal slopes and bubbling for complex Hessian equations Thanks: Work supported in part by the Infosys Young Investigator award, SERB MATRICS grant MTR/2022/000260, PhD scholarship from the Indian Institute of Science, and the National Science Foundation grant DMS-2203607
Ved Datar, Ramesh Mete, Jian Song

Abstract
. The existence of smooth solutions to a broad class of complex Hessian equations is related to nonlinear Nakai type criterias on intersection numbers on Kähler manifolds. Such a Nakai criteria can be interpreted as a slope stability condition analogous to the slope stability for Hermitian vector bundles over Kähler manifolds. In the present work, we initiate a program to find canonical solutions to such equations in the unstable case when the Nakai criteria fails. Conjecturally such solutions should arise as limits of natural parabolic flows and should be minimizers of the corresponding moment-map energy functionals. We implement our approach for the J J -equation and the deformed Hermitian Yang-Mills equation on surfaces and some examples with symmetry. We prove that there always exist unique canonical solutions to these two equations on Kähler surfaces in the unstable cases. Such canonical solutions with singularities are also shown to be the limits of the corresponding J-flow and the cotangent flow on certain projective bundles. We further present the bubbling phenomena for the J J -equation by constructing minimizing sequences of the moment-map energy functionals, whose Gromov-Hausdorff limits are singular algebraic spaces.

Introduction

1.1.
Overview

Throughout the paper
(X^{n},\omega)
will denote a compact Kähler manifold (without boundary) of dimension
n
. We will denote the Kähler class of
\omega
by
\beta\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
, and
\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
will denote be another class. We let
A=\omega^{-1}\chi
for some smooth real
(1,1)
form
\chi
, and consider equations for the form

F(A)=f(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n})=h,

where
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
are eigenvalues of
A
. Under some additional conditions on
h
and
f
(for instance if

is a constant and
f
is the sum of reciprocals of the entries), existence of smooth solutions is equivalent to the positivity of certain intersection numbers - this is the so-called stable case (cf.
[
46
,
18
,
43
,
44
,
38
,

,
14
,
41
,
9
,
17
,
37
,

,

]
and references therein). The main theme of the present article is to explore existence of solutions in the unstable case. Such a solution will of course necessarily be singular. In the present work, we focus our attention on two protypical and important examples - the
J
-equation and the deformed Hermitian-Yang-Mills (dHYM) equations - to illustrate this point of view.

To set the stage, we first consider the more classical case of the complex Monge-Ampère equations. It follows from Yau’s resolution of the Calabi conjecture
[
46
]
that if
\alpha
is a Kähler class, then there exists a unique
\chi\in\alpha
solving

\begin{cases}\chi^{n}=e^{f}\omega^{n}\\
\chi>0,\end{cases},

where
f\in C^{\infty}(X)
satisfies the normalization
\int_{X}e^{f}\omega^{n}=\alpha^{n}
. On the other hand, by Demailly and Paun’s generalization of the classical Nakai-Moishezon criteria
[
18
]
, a necessary and sufficient condition for
\alpha
to be Kähler, and hence for the existence of a solution to the above Monge-Ampère equation, is that for any analytic set
Z^{m}\subset X
of pure dimension
m
, and any
k=0,1,\cdots,m
we have

\int_{Z}\alpha^{k}\wedge\beta^{m-k}>0.

Note that to check that
\alpha
is a Kähler class requires the construction of a smooth, real, positive
(1,1)
form in the class
\alpha
, and is a
pointwise
condition. On the other hand, the above criteria is completely numerical, and is in practice much easier to verify. A natural question to ask is whether the complex Monge-Ampere equation can be solved in a class that is not Kähler or even numerically effective (nef), or equivalently when some of the intersection numbers above are negative. The optimal result in this direction was obtained in
[
6
]
. Namely, if
\alpha
is a big class, and
\Omega
is a Radon measure that does not charge pluri-polar sets, then there exists a positive current
T\in\alpha
such that

\langle T^{n}\rangle=c\Omega.

Here
\langle\cdot\rangle
denotes the non-pluripolar product introduced by Bedford-Taylor
[
2
]
, and
c
is the ratio of the volume
\mathrm{vol}(\alpha)
of the big class
\alpha
and the total mass
\Omega(X)
. In particular we can take
\Omega=\omega^{n}
, and think of
c=\mathrm{vol}(\alpha)/\beta^{n}
as the (Monge-Ampère)
slope
of the pair
(\alpha,\beta)
. It is well known (cf.
[
26
,
5
,
3
,
4
]
) that there is an intersection theoretic characterization of the slope in this context, namely,

c=\sup\frac{(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)^{n}}{\pi^{*}\beta^{n}},

where the supremum is taken over all modifications
\pi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
such that
\pi^{*}\alpha-D
is big and nef. At least for surfaces, it follows from the Zariski decomposition that the slope is in general larger than the topological slope
\alpha^{2}/\beta^{2}
.

Motivated by the analysis of complex Monge-Ampère equations in big classes, one can ask for similar weak solutions for other complex Hessian-type equations using the non-pluripolar product (cf.
[
20
]
for viscosity type weak solutions for some classes of complex Hessian equations). In the present work we develop this circle of ideas for two prototypical equations, namely the
J
-equation and the deformed Hermitian-Yang-Mills (dHYM) equations. We show that for surfaces and certain projective bundles, there is an analogous connection between weak solutions and minimal slopes. We also propose several conjectures in higher dimensions which we will explore further in future work.

1.2.
The
J
-equation

Suppose now that
\alpha
is a Kähler class, and
\beta
, as before, is the Kähler class of a fixed Kähler form
\omega
. We say that a smooth Kähler form
\chi\in\alpha
satisfies the
J
equation if

(1.1)

n\frac{\chi^{n-1}\wedge\omega}{\chi^{n}}=\mu,

where
\mu>0
is a constant. The
J
-equation was introduced as a moment map equation in
[
21
]
and in the context of finding lower bounds of Mabuchi energy in
[
10
]
. If equation (
1.1
) admits a smooth solution, then the constant
\mu
is given by the topological intersection number defined below as the
J
-slope.

Definition 1.1
.

The
J
-slope of the triple
(X^{n},\alpha,\beta)
is defined by

(1.2)

\mu=\mu(X,\alpha,\beta)=n\frac{\alpha^{n-1}\cdot\beta}{\alpha^{n}}.

If
\beta
is a very ample divisor and
D
a general divisor in it’s linear system, one can write the
J
-slope
\mu
as

\mu=n\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{D}(\alpha|_{D})}{\mathrm{vol}_{X}(\alpha)}.

It is shown in
[
38
,
9
,
17
,
37
]
that equation (
1.1
) admits a smooth solution if and only if

\mu(Z,\alpha|_{Z},\beta|_{Z})<\mu(X,\alpha,\beta),

for any proper analytic subvariety
Z
of
X
. In analogy with slope stability for hermitian bundles over Kähler manifolds, we then have the following natural notion of
J
-stability.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that the triple
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is
J
-slope semi-stable (resp. stable) if

\mu(Z,\alpha|_{Z},\beta|_{Z})\leq\mu(X,\alpha,\beta),~\text{ (resp. $<$)}

for any proper analytic subvariety
Z
of
X
. We say it is
J
-slope unstable if it is not
J
-slope semi-stable.

In this paper, we will investigate the case when the
J
-stability
fails
and look for canonical solutions for the
J
-equation with singularities. In analogy with the notion of volume, we will first have to modify the notion of the
J
-slope.

Definition 1.3
.

We define the minimal
J
-slope for the triple
(X^{n},\alpha,\beta)
by

(1.3)

\zeta_{min}=\inf_{\pi,D}\left\{n\frac{(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)^{n-1}\cdot\pi^{*}\beta}{(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)^{n}}\right\}

where the infimum is taken over all modifications
\pi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
and effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisors
D
for which
\pi^{*}\alpha-D
is big and nef.

The inverse of the minimal
J
-slope is a natural analogue for the volume of a big class on a Kähler manifold in the context of the
J
-equation. Our first result is to solve the
J
-equation for Kähler surfaces, regardless of
J
-stability.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
(X,\alpha,\beta)
be a compact Kähler surface with two Kähler classes. Let
\omega
be a smooth Kähler metric in
\beta
. Then there exists a unique Kähler current
\mathcal{T}\in\alpha
solving the
J
-equation

(1.4)

2\langle\mathcal{T}\wedge\omega\rangle=\zeta_{min}\langle\mathcal{T}^{2}\rangle,

where
\zeta_{min}
is the minimal
J
-slope for
(X,\alpha,\beta)
in Definition
1.3
, and
\langle~,~\rangle
is the non-pluripolar product for positive currents. Furthermore,

(1)

There exists an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor
D
on
X
such that

\zeta_{min}=2\frac{(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta}{(\alpha-D)^{2}}.

In particular, the infimum is achieved without taking modifications.

(2)

(X,\alpha,\beta)
is
J
-slope unstable, if and only if
\zeta_{min}<\mu.

Analogous to the solution of the the complex Monge-Ampere equations in big classes and their relation with volume, we conjecture the following extension of Theorem

Conjecture 1.5
.

Let

1.3.
The deformed Hermitian Yang-Mills equation

Suppose now that
\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
is a class that is not necessarily positive. The deformed Hermitian-Yang-Mills (dHYM) equation, introduced in
[
32
]
as a mirror to the special Lagrangian equation, seeks a smooth closed real
(1,1)
form
\chi
in
\alpha
such that

\mathrm{Re}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{n}=(\cot\vartheta)\mathrm{Im}(\chi+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{n},

where
\vartheta\in(0,\pi)
is a topological constant defined below. The dHYM also arises as a moment map equation - the reader can refer to
[
15
]
for more details. By integrating both sides of the equation, we see that

is a topological invariant. Analogous to the case of the
J
-equation, we have the following definitions.

Definition 1.8
.

Let
X
be a Kähler manifold of complex dimension

and let
\alpha,\beta\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. Suppose
\beta
is big and semi-positive. We define the global dHYM-angle

\vartheta=\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\pi)

for the triple

by

\\
Re(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{n}=(\cot\vartheta)\\
Im(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{n},

whenever
Im(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{n}\neq 0
.

(1)

(X,\alpha,\beta)
is said to be dHYM-stable if for any Kähler class

,

\\
Re(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{k}\cdot\gamma^{n-k}\cdot X\geq(\cot\vartheta)\\
Im(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{k}\cdot\gamma^{n-k}\cdot X

and for any proper
m
-dimensional subvariety
Z
of
X
and
1\leq k\leq m
,

\\
Re(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{k}\cdot\gamma^{m-k}\cdot Z>(\cot\vartheta)\\
Im(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{k}\cdot\gamma^{m-k}\cdot Z.

(2)

(X,\alpha,\beta)
is said to be dHYM-semi-stable if for any Kähler class
\gamma
, any
m
-dimensional subvariety
Z
of
X
and

,

\\
Re(\alpha+\sqrt{-1}\beta)^{k}\cdot\gamma^{m-k}\cdot Z\geq(\cot\vartheta)\\

(3)

(X,\alpha,\beta)

Definition 1.9
.

Let
X

The cotangent of the minimal angle

such that

\cot\theta_{min}=\frac{(\alpha-D)^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta},~~(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta>0.

In particular, one does not have to take modifications in the definition of
\cot\theta_{min}
. Moreover we have that

\cot\theta_{min}\geq\cot\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta)

with equality if and only if
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is
dHYM
-semistable.

Note that though
\mathcal{T}
may not be a positive current, but since the current
\Theta:=\mathcal{T}-(\cot\theta_{min})\omega
is positive, the non-pluripolar product can be interpreted using multi-linearity. Explicitly, we set

\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle=\langle\Theta^{2}\rangle+2(\cot\theta_{min}+\sqrt{-1})\langle\Theta\wedge\omega\rangle+\left(\cot\theta_{min}+\sqrt{-1}\right)^{2}\omega^{2}.

The condition
\alpha\cdot\beta>0
is not strictly needed. For, if the intersection number were negative, one could simply work with
-\alpha
. We would also like to remark that if
\alpha
is pseudoeffective, then
\alpha\cdot\beta
is always nonnegative. The condition

\alpha\cdot\beta>0

holds if and only if the numerical dimension of
\alpha
is greater than or equal to
1
. The conjectural picture in higher dimensions is much more subtle for the dHYM equations than for the
J
-equation, essentially due to the lack of positivity of the class
\alpha
. We begin with the following definition.

Definition 1.11
.

Let
X
be a Kähler manifold of complex dimension
n>2
and let
\alpha,\beta\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. Suppose
\beta
is big and semi-positive. We define the semi-stable admissible set by

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{(\pi,X^{\prime},D):\pi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X~\textnormal{is~a~modification},~D~\textnormal{is~effective},~(X^{\prime},\pi^{*}\alpha-D,\pi^{*}\beta)~\textnormal{is~semi-stable}\right\}

and the minimal angle
\theta_{min}=\theta_{min}(X,\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\pi)
by

\theta_{min}=\inf\left\{\vartheta(X^{\prime},\pi^{*}\alpha-D,\pi^{*}\beta):~(\pi,X^{\prime},D)\in\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)\right\}

if
\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)
is not empty.

Conjecture 1.12
.

Let
X
be a Kähler manifold of complex dimension
n
and let
\alpha,\beta\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. Suppose
\beta
is Kähler,
\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)
is not empty and
\theta_{min}>0
. Then for any Kähler form
\omega\in\beta
, there exists a unique closed current
\mathcal{T}\in\alpha
such that

\text{Re}\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{n}\rangle=(\cot\theta_{min})\text{Im}\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{n}\rangle.

We shall see in Section
2.2
that even when
n=2
, the condition
\alpha\cdot\beta>0
ensures that
\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)
is non-empty and the two definitions of minimal angles agrees with each other.

Next, we study a parabolic version of the dHYM equation. There are in fact several flows in literature whose critical points satisfy the dHYM equation (cf.
[
32
,
42
]
). In the final section we focus on the cotangent flow introduced by Fu, Yau and Zhang in
[
25
]
, and prove a convergence result for the flow on
\mathrm{Bl}_{x_{0}}\mathbb{P}^{2}
with Calabi ansatz. We prove that the flow converges to the current
\mathcal{T}
constructed in the theorem above in a suitable weak sense.

Theorem 1.13
.

Let
X=\mathrm{Bl}_{x_{0}}\mathbb{P}^{2}
,
\omega
a fixed Kähler metric satisfying the Calabi ansatz in the class
\beta=b[H]-[E]
and
\alpha=p[H]-q[E]
a real
(1,1)
class with a representative
\chi\in\alpha
. We assume that
\alpha\cdot\beta>0
. Let
\chi_{0}:=\chi+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\underline{\varphi}\in\alpha
satisfying Calabi ansatz with
\Lambda_{\omega}\chi_{0}>0
, and let
\chi_{t}:=\chi_{\varphi_{t}}
be the cotangent flow emanating from
\chi_{0}
. Let

c_{0}=\cot\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta).

Then there exists a choice of
\chi_{0}
(to be specified later) such that the following convergence behaviour holds:

(1)

If
q>c_{0}
, then
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is dHYM-stable and
\chi_{t}
converges smoothly to a solution
\chi_{\infty}
of the dHYM equation.

(2)

If
q=c_{0}
, then
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is dHYM-semi-stable but not stable. In this case
\chi_{t}\rightarrow\chi_{\infty}
as currents, where
\chi_{\infty}
has bounded local potentials and satisfies the dHYM equation

\text{Re}(\chi_{\infty}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}=c_{0}\text{Im}(\chi_{\infty}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2},

where the wedge product is taken in the sense of Bedford-Taylor.

(3)

If
q<c_{0}
, then
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is dHYM-unstable. In this case also
\chi_{t}\rightarrow\chi_{\infty}
as currents. We can write
\chi_{\infty}=\chi_{\infty}^{\prime}+(\zeta-q)[E]
, where
\chi_{\infty}^{\prime}
has bounded local potentials and

\zeta:=\sup\Big\{\frac{(\alpha_{t}^{\prime})^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2\alpha_{t}^{\prime}\cdot\beta}~|~\alpha_{t}^{\prime}=\alpha-tE,~\alpha_{t}^{\prime}\cdot\beta>0\Big\}.

Moreover
\zeta=\cot\theta_{min}
, and
\chi_{\infty}^{\prime}
solves

\text{Re}(\chi_{\infty}^{\prime}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}=(\cot\theta_{min})\text{Im}(\chi_{\infty}^{\prime}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}

on
X\setminus E
. Globally,
\chi_{\infty}
solves

\langle\chi_{\infty},\chi_{\infty}\rangle-\omega^{2}=2\zeta\langle\chi_{\infty},\omega\rangle,

and so
\chi_{\infty}=\mathcal{T}
that we construct in Theorem
1.10
.

In fact, by adapting the arguments in the proof of Theorem
1.6
, it is possible to prove that the cotangent flow has a unique limit for
any

\chi_{0}
satisfying the Calabi ansatz and
\Lambda_{\omega}\chi_{0}>0
. The fact that
\zeta=\cot\theta_{min}
follows from the fact that both are (unique) solutions of a certain quadratic equation involving the volume function as we shall see in Sections
2
and
4
below. The interested reader should also refer to the recent preprint
[
8
]
for some fascinating results on singularity formation for the line-bundle curvature flow with Calabi ansatz on
\mathrm{Bl}_{x_{0}}\mathbb{P}^{n}
in the non-super critical and semi-stable cases. We expect that similar results should also hold for the co-tangent flow.

2.
Singular solutions to complex Hessian equations on Kähler surfaces

2.1.
The
J
-equation on Kähler surfaces

The aim of this section is to prove Theorem
1.4
. The starting point of the proof is the following elementary observation.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\alpha
be a big and nef class, and

t\geq 2\frac{\alpha\cdot\beta}{\alpha^{2}}.

Then the class
\gamma:=\alpha-t^{-1}\beta
is big.

Proof.

Note that
\alpha^{2}>0
. For any
\varepsilon>0
we set
\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\alpha+\varepsilon\beta
. By Lamari’s criteria
[
34
]
,
\gamma
is big if
\gamma^{2}>0
and
\gamma\cdot\alpha_{\varepsilon}>0
for some
\varepsilon>0
, since
\alpha_{\varepsilon}
itself is Kähler. But these two conditions are easily verified. Indeed we have

\displaystyle\gamma^{2}=\alpha^{2}+\frac{1}{t^{2}}\beta^{2}-\frac{2}{t}\alpha\cdot\beta\geq\frac{1}{t^{2}}\beta^{2}>0

\displaystyle\gamma\cdot\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\alpha^{2}-\frac{1}{t}\alpha\cdot\beta+\varepsilon\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{t}\beta\Big)\cdot\beta\geq\frac{1}{4}\alpha^{2}>0,

if we choose
\varepsilon<<1
so that

\varepsilon\left|\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{t}\beta\Big)\cdot\beta\right|<\frac{\alpha^{2}}{4}.

∎

We now let

\displaystyle\mu_{0}

\displaystyle=\inf\{t>0~|~\gamma_{t}:=\alpha-\frac{1}{t}\beta\text{ is big}\}

Since
\beta
is Kähler, clearly
\mu_{0}>0.
On the other hand, by the lemma above we have
\mu_{0}<\mu
.

Lemma 2.2
.

There exists a unique
\xi\in(\mu_{0},\mu]
such that

\mathrm{vol}\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{\xi}\beta\Big)=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\beta^{2}.

Moreover,
t\geq\xi
if and only if

\mathrm{vol}\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{t}\beta\Big)\geq\frac{1}{t^{2}}\beta^{2}.

Proof.

For
s\in\Big[\frac{1}{\mu},\frac{1}{\mu_{0}}\Big)
, consider the function

f(s)=\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-s\beta)}{s^{2}\beta^{2}}.

The function is clearly a continuous and strictly decreasing function in
s
. The continuity of
f(s)
of course uses the continuity of the volume function on
H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
. We also have that
f(\mu_{0}^{-1})=0
and

f(\frac{1}{\mu})=\mu^{2}\frac{\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-\frac{1}{\mu}\beta)}{\beta^{2}}\geq\mu^{2}\frac{(\alpha-\frac{1}{\mu}\beta)^{2}}{\beta^{2}}=1,

and so by the intermediate value theorem there must be a unique
s_{0}
such that
f(s_{0})=1
. We then take
\xi=1/s_{0}
.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

There exists a unique Kähler current
\mathcal{T}\in\alpha
such that

2\langle\mathcal{T},\omega\rangle=\xi\langle\mathcal{T}^{2}\rangle,~~\mathcal{T}\geq\frac{1}{\xi}\omega.

Proof.

By the above lemma and Theorem 4.1 in
[
6
]
, there exists a unique positive current
\Theta\in\alpha-\frac{1}{\xi}\beta
such that

\langle\Theta,\Theta\rangle=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\omega^{2}.

Then
\mathcal{T}:=\Theta+\frac{1}{\xi}\omega
is the required current.
∎

Proposition 2.4
.

We have the following characterization of
\xi
:

\xi=2\inf_{D}\frac{(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta}{(\alpha-D)^{2}},

where the infimum is taken over all effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisors
D
on
X
such that
\alpha-D
is big and nef. Moreover,
\xi<\mu
if and only if
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is unstable.

Proof.

We call the right hand side
\zeta
. We first prove that
\zeta\leq\xi
. To see this, let

\alpha-\frac{1}{\xi}\beta=Z+N

be the Zariski decomposition, where
Z
is the nef part and
N
is the negative and effective part. Then
\alpha^{\prime}:=\alpha-N
is Kähler, and so

\zeta\leq 2\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta}{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}=\xi.

To see the ratio above is equal to
\xi
we simply note that

\Big(\alpha^{\prime}-\frac{1}{\xi}\beta\Big)^{2}=Z^{2}=\mathrm{vol}\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{\xi}\beta\Big)=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\beta^{2}.

On the other hand, suppose
\zeta<\xi
. Let
t\in(\max(\zeta,\mu_{0}),\xi)
. Then there exists an effective divisor
D
on
X
such that
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-D
is big and nef, and

t>2\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta}{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}.

By Lemma
2.1

\alpha^{\prime}-t^{-1}\beta
is a big class. We also have that
\alpha-\alpha^{\prime}=D
is pseudoeffective, so

\mathrm{vol}\Big(\alpha-\frac{1}{t}\beta\Big)\geq\mathrm{vol}\Big(\alpha^{\prime}-\frac{1}{t}\Big)\geq\Big(\alpha^{\prime}-\frac{1}{t}\beta\Big)^{2}>\frac{1}{t^{2}}\beta^{2},

and so
t>\xi
by Lemma
2.2
, and we have a contradiction.
∎

To complete the proof of the theorem
1.4
we have the following:

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
\pi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
be a modification and
D
an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor such that
\pi^{*}\alpha-D
is big and nef. Then

2\frac{(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)\cdot\pi^{*}\beta}{(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)^{2}}\geq\xi,

and hence in particular that
\zeta_{min}=\xi
.

Proof.

We let
\alpha^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\alpha-D
,
\beta^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\beta
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}-\xi^{-1}\beta^{\prime}
. We argue by contradiction. So suppose

2\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta^{\prime}}{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}<\xi.

This is equivalent to

(\gamma^{\prime})^{2}>\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}(\beta^{\prime})^{2}=\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\beta^{2}.

Once again by Lemma
2.1
,
\gamma^{\prime}
is big. Now if we denote
\gamma=\alpha-\xi^{-1}\beta
, then
\pi^{*}\gamma-\gamma^{\prime}=D
is effective and so

\mathrm{vol}(\gamma)\geq\mathrm{vol}(\pi^{*}\gamma)\geq\mathrm{vol}(\gamma^{\prime})\geq(\gamma^{\prime})^{2}>\frac{1}{\xi^{2}}\beta^{2},

which is a contradiction since
\mathrm{vol}(\gamma)=\xi^{-2}\beta^{2}
by Lemma
2.2
.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

A nice consequence of the theorem
1.4
, and it’s proof, is that the minimal slope is achieved. That is, there exists an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor
D
on
X
such that
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-D
is big and nef and

\zeta_{min}=2\frac{\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta}{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}.

As in the proof above this immediately follows from the Zariski decomposition

\alpha-\frac{1}{\xi}\beta=Z+D.

Moreover, since
Z
is big and nef, we can write

\zeta_{min}=\min\left\{\frac{2\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta}{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}}~|~\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-D\text{ big and nef},~D\text{ effective $\mathbb{R}$-divisor and }(X,\alpha^{\prime},\beta)\text{ is $J$-slope semi-stable}\right\}.

That is, the minimum can be taken over the semi-stable pairs.

Remark 2.7
.

When
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is unstable, we also note that the divisor
D
is a destabilising divisor ie.
\mu_{D}>\mu_{X}
. This follows easily be rearranging the following basic estimate and using the fact that
D^{2}<0
:

2\frac{(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta}{(\alpha-D)^{2}}=\zeta_{min}<\mu=2\frac{\alpha\cdot\beta}{\alpha^{2}}.

2.2.
The deformed Hermitian-Yang-Mills equations on surfaces

We now move on to analysing the deformed Hermitian Yang Mills equations. We once again first specialize to the surface case. Without loss of generality, we may assume that

(2.1)

\alpha\cdot\beta>0.

For, if
\alpha\cdot\beta
were negative, we could simply simply replace
\alpha
by
-\alpha
and continue with the analysis.
We let

\zeta_{H}:=\sup_{D}\frac{(\alpha-D)^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta},

where the supremum is taken over all
\mathbb{R}
-effective divisors
D
on
X
such that

(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta>0.

The main theorem we wish to prove in this section is the following.

Theorem 2.8
.

There exists a unique closed current
\mathcal{T}\in\alpha
such that

\Re\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle=\zeta_{H}\cdot\Im\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle,~~\mathcal{T}\geq\zeta_{H}\omega.

Here
\langle,\rangle
denotes the non-pluripolar product, and the equality is that of measures. Moreover the global slope is achieved. That is, there exists an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor
D
on
X
such that

\zeta_{H}=\frac{(\alpha-D)^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta}.

We will prove shortly that
\zeta_{H}=\cot\theta_{min}
, and this will in particular complete the proof of Theorem
1.10
. We first need the following strengthening of Lemma
2.1
.

Lemma 2.9
.

Let
\alpha\in H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
and
\beta
be a Kähler class such that
\alpha\cdot\beta>0
. Let

t\leq\frac{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2\alpha\cdot\beta}.

Then
\alpha-t\beta
is a big class. In particular, with
c_{0}=\cot\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2\alpha\cdot\beta}
as above,
\alpha-c_{0}\beta
is a big class.

Proof.

By Lamari’s criteria
[
34
]
, a class
\gamma
on
X
is big if
\gamma^{2}>0
and
\gamma\cdot\beta>0
. We let
\gamma=\alpha-t\beta
. Then

\gamma^{2}=\alpha^{2}-2t\alpha\cdot\beta+t^{2}\beta^{2}\geq(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}>0.

If
t<0
, then

\gamma\cdot\beta=\alpha\cdot\beta-t\beta^{2}>\alpha\cdot\beta>0.

On the other hand if
t\geq 0
, then
\alpha^{2}\geq\beta^{2}
. In particular,
\alpha
itself is big again by Lamari
[
34
]
. Let
\alpha=Z+N
be the Zariski decomposition with
Z
big and nef, and
N^{2}<0
. We let
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-N
and
\alpha_{\varepsilon}=\alpha^{\prime}+\varepsilon\beta
which is Kähler for any
\varepsilon>0
. Note that

0<(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}=\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\cdot N+N^{2}<\alpha^{2}-2\alpha\cdot N,

and so
\alpha^{2}>2\alpha\cdot N
. We then have

\displaystyle\gamma\cdot\alpha_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}-t\alpha\cdot\beta-\alpha\cdot N+t\beta\cdot E+\varepsilon\beta\cdot(\alpha-t\beta)

\displaystyle\geq\alpha^{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2}-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{2}+\varepsilon\beta\cdot(\alpha-t\beta)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta^{2}}{2}+\varepsilon\beta\cdot(\alpha-t\beta)>0,

for
\varepsilon<<1
. Note that we used the fact that
\beta\cdot N\geq 0
in the second line. Once again by Lamari
[
34
]
, it follows that
\gamma
is big.

∎

We now let

t_{0}=\sup\{t\in\mathbb{R}~|~\alpha-t\beta\text{ is a big class}\}.

By the above lemma we have that
c_{0}<t_{0}
.

Lemma 2.10
.

There exists a unique
\xi\in[c_{0},t_{0})
such that

(2.2)

\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-\xi\beta)=(1+\xi^{2})\beta^{2}.

We also have that
\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-t\beta)<(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}
if
t\in(\xi,t_{0})
. Moreover, if
Z+N
is the Zariski decomposition of the big class
\alpha-\xi\beta
, then

(Z+\xi\beta)\cdot\beta>0.

Proof.

Consider the function

f(t)=\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-t\beta)-(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}.

By Deng’s result on the derivative of the volume function
[
19
]
(see also
[
45
]
), we have that

f^{\prime}(t)=-2\langle\alpha-t\beta\rangle\cdot\beta-2t\beta^{2}.

We claim that
f^{\prime}(t)
is increasing on
(-\infty,t_{0})
, i.e.
f
is convex. To see this, let
t_{1}<t_{2}
. Then by the super-additivity of the positive intersection product, we have

\langle\alpha-t_{1}\beta\rangle\cdot\beta\geq\langle\alpha-t_{2}\beta\rangle\cdot\beta+(t_{2}-t_{1})\beta^{2},

and so

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(t_{1})

\displaystyle\leq-2\langle\alpha-t_{2}\beta\rangle\cdot\beta-2(t_{2}-t_{1})\beta^{2}-2t_{1}\beta^{2}=f^{\prime}(t_{2}).

We also have that
f(t_{0})=-(1+t_{0}^{2})\beta^{2}<0
and

f(c_{0})=\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-c_{0}\beta)-(1+c_{0}^{2})\beta^{2}\geq(\alpha-c_{0}\beta)^{2}-(1+c_{0}^{2})\beta^{2}=0.

By the intermediate value theorem there is at least one zero between
[c_{0},t_{0})
and by convexity the zero must be unique. Moreover,
f(t)
has to be negative for all
t>\xi
. Finally, note that
f^{\prime}(\xi)<0
, and so

\displaystyle 0>f^{\prime}(\xi)=-2(\langle\alpha-\xi\beta\rangle\cdot\beta+\xi\beta^{2})=-2(Z+\xi\beta)\cdot\beta.

Here we used the fact that for if
\gamma=Z+N
is a Zariski decomposition, then
\langle\gamma\rangle\cdot\beta=Z\cdot\beta.

∎

Corollary 2.11
.

There exists a unique closed current
\mathcal{T}\in\alpha
such that

\mathrm{Re}\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle=\xi\cdot\mathrm{Im}\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle,~~~\mathcal{T}\geq\xi\omega.

Proof.

By Lemma
2.10
and Theorem
4.1

[
6
]
, there exists a unique closed positive current
\Theta\in\alpha-\xi\beta
such that

\langle\Theta^{2}\rangle=(1+\xi^{2})\omega^{2}.

Then
\mathcal{T}:=\Theta+\xi\omega
is the desired unique closed current in
\alpha
. Indeed,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle-\xi\cdot\mathrm{Im}\langle(\mathcal{T}+\sqrt{-1}\omega)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Re}\langle(\Theta+(\xi+\sqrt{-1})\omega)^{2}\rangle-\xi\cdot\mathrm{Im}\langle(\Theta+(\xi+\sqrt{-1})\omega)^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\Theta^{2}\rangle+2\xi\langle\Theta\wedge\omega\rangle+(\xi^{2}-1)\omega^{2}-2\xi\langle\Theta\wedge\omega\rangle-2\xi^{2}\omega^{2}

\displaystyle=0.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.8
.

We only need to prove that
\xi=\zeta_{H}
. We first argue that
\xi\leq\zeta_{H}
. To see this, let

\alpha-\xi\beta=Z+N

be the Zariski decomposition of the big class
\alpha-\xi\beta
, where
Z
is big and nef, and
N=\sum a_{i}D_{i}
is an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor with
(D_{i}\cdot D_{j})
negative definite. Note that by the choice of
\xi
, we have

(2.3)

Z^{2}=(1+\xi^{2})\beta^{2}.

Moreover, by the second part in Lemma
2.10
,

(\alpha-N)\cdot\beta=(Z+\xi\beta)\cdot\beta>0.

So
N
can be considered as an admissible divisor in the definition of
\zeta_{H}
. So if we set
\alpha^{\prime}:=\alpha-N
, we have

\zeta_{H}\geq\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta}=\frac{(Z+\xi\beta)^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2(Z+\xi\beta)\cdot\beta}=\xi,

where we used equation
(
2.3
)
.

Next, suppose we have a strict inequality
\xi<\zeta_{H}
. Then there exists a
\xi<t<\zeta_{H}
. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
t<t_{0}
ie.
\alpha-t\beta
is big. Since
t<\zeta_{H}
, there exists an effective divisor
D
such that
(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta>0
and

t<\frac{(\alpha-D)^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta}.

For simplicity, we denote
\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-D
. By Lemma
2.9
applied to the classes
\alpha^{\prime}
and
\beta
, we see that
\alpha^{\prime}-t\beta
is big. Next, since
t>\xi
and
(\alpha-t\beta)-(\alpha^{\prime}-t\beta)
is pseudoeffective, we also must have that

\mathrm{vol}(\alpha^{\prime}-t\beta)-(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}\leq\mathrm{vol}(\alpha-t\beta)-(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}<0.

But then,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle>\mathrm{vol}(\alpha^{\prime}-t\beta)-(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}

\displaystyle\geq(\alpha^{\prime}-t\beta)^{2}-(1+t^{2})\beta^{2}

\displaystyle=(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}-\beta^{2}-2t\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta>0,

a contradiction. Therefore, we must have
\xi=\zeta_{H}
.
∎

We note the following corollary of the main theorem.

Corollary 2.12
.

There exists an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor
D
on the compact Kähler surface
X
such that
(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta>0
and

\zeta_{H}=\frac{(\alpha-D)^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2(\alpha-D)\cdot\beta}.

We finally prove the following.

Proposition 2.13
.

\zeta_{H}=\cot\theta_{min}
. In particular, conjecture
1.12
holds in dimension two under the assumption that
\alpha\cdot\beta>0
.

Proof.

We first observe that if
\alpha\cdot\beta>0
then the semi-stable admissible set
\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)
is non-empty. In fact, with the divisor
D
from the above corollary, we see that the triple
(X,\alpha-D,\beta)
is dHYM-semi-stable. Recall that
\theta_{min}\in(0,\pi)
is defined by

\cot\theta_{min}=\sup\left\{\frac{(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)^{2}-\pi^{*}\beta^{2}}{2(\pi^{*}\alpha-D)\cdot\pi^{*}\beta}:~(\pi,X^{\prime},D)\in\mathcal{V}(X,\alpha,\beta)\right\}.

We now claim that

\cot\theta_{min}=\zeta_{H}.

Clearly
\cot\theta_{min}\geq\zeta_{H}
. Suppose we have a strict inequality. Then there exists a modification
\pi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
and an effective
\mathbb{R}
-divisor
D
on
X^{\prime}
such that

(2.4)

\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{2}-(\beta^{\prime})^{2}}{2\alpha^{\prime}\cdot\beta^{\prime}}>\zeta_{H},

where we let
\alpha^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\alpha-D
and
\beta^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\beta
. Let
\gamma=\alpha-\zeta_{H}\beta
and
\gamma^{\prime}=\alpha^{\prime}-\zeta_{H}\beta^{\prime}
. Then
(
2.4
)
is equivalent to the inequality

(\gamma^{\prime})^{2}>(1+\zeta_{H}^{2})\beta^{2}.

But then, since
\pi^{*}\gamma=\gamma^{\prime}+D
,

\mathrm{vol}(\gamma)\geq\mathrm{vol}(\pi^{*}\gamma)\geq\mathrm{vol}(\gamma^{\prime})\geq(\gamma^{\prime})^{2}>(1+\zeta_{H}^{2})\beta^{2},

a contradiction.
∎

2.3.
Some more conjectures and speculations for the
J
-equation

Taking inspiration from the surface case we make the following conjecture.

Conjecture 2.14
.

The pair
(X,\alpha,\beta)
is semi-stable if and only if
\zeta_{min}=\mu
.

We can prove one direction of the above conjecture, at least when
\alpha
and
\beta
lie in the rational Neron-Severi group
NS_{\mathbb{Q}}
.

Proposition 2.15
.

Let
\alpha
and
\beta
be the first Chern classes of ample
\mathbb{Q}
-line bundles
A
and
B
respectively. If
(\alpha,\beta)
is unstable, then
\zeta_{min}<\mu
.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, for instance by taking a sufficiently large power, we may assume that
A
and
B
are ample line bundles. By hypothesis, there exists a sub-variety
Z^{m}\subset X
such that

m\int_{Z}\alpha^{m-1}\cdot\beta>n\mu\int_{Z}\alpha^{m}.

Without loss of generality, for instance once again by raising
B
to a large power, we may assume that the linear system
|B|
contains a smooth divisor
D
. Let
\mathcal{I}_{Z}
denote the ideal sheaf of
Z
. We will use the following well known formulae to compute the intersection numbers:

\displaystyle\alpha^{n}=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\dim H^{0}(X,A^{k})}{k^{n}/n!}

\displaystyle\alpha^{n-1}\cdot\beta=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\dim H^{0}(D,(A\Big|_{D})^{k})}{k^{n-1}/(n-1)!}

\displaystyle\dim H^{0}(X,A^{k}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{Z}^{kx})=a_{0}(x)k^{n}+O(k^{n-1}),

where
x\in\mathbb{Q}
and

a_{0}(x)=\frac{\alpha^{n}}{n!}-\frac{x^{n-m}}{(n-m)!}\int_{Z}\frac{\alpha^{m}}{m!}+O(x^{n-p+1}),

for
x<<1
. Now, let
\pi:X^{\prime}=\mathrm{Bl}_{Z}X
and
\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\alpha-\varepsilon E
, where
E
is the exceptional divisor. For the rest of the argument we assume that
m<n-1
. If
m=n-1
then
X^{\prime}=X
and the argument is simpler. For any rational
\varepsilon
and
\varepsilon<<1
,
A_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}=\pi^{*}A\otimes(-\varepsilon E)
is an ample
\mathbb{Q}
line bundle. Recalling that
H^{0}(X^{\prime},(A_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{k})=H^{0}(X,A^{k}\otimes\mathcal{I}_{Z}^{k\varepsilon})
and using the above formulae we have

\displaystyle(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{n}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\dim H^{0}(X,A^{k}\otimes I_{Z}^{\varepsilon})}{k^{n}/n!}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{n}-{n\choose m}\varepsilon^{n-m}\int_{Z}\alpha^{m}+O(\varepsilon^{n-m+1}).

Next, if
D
is sufficiently general then
Z\cap D
is a divisor in
Z
and once again applying the same formulae, this time to
Z\cap D
and replacing
m,n
by
m-1
and
n-1
respectively, we see that

\displaystyle(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{n-1}\cdot\beta^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\dim H^{0}(D,(A\Big|_{D})^{k}\otimes I_{Z\cap D}^{k\varepsilon})}{k^{n-1}/(n-1)!}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{n-1}\cdot\beta-{n-1\choose m-1}\varepsilon^{n-m}\int_{Z\cap D}\alpha^{m-1}+O(\varepsilon^{n-m+1})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{n-1}\cdot\beta-{n-1\choose m-1}\varepsilon^{n-m}\int_{Z}\alpha^{m-1}\cdot\beta+O(\varepsilon^{n-m+1}).

We then have that

\displaystyle\zeta_{min}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{n-1}\cdot\beta^{\prime}}{(\alpha_{\varepsilon}^{\prime})^{n}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha^{n-1}\cdot\beta-{n-1\choose m-1}\varepsilon^{n-m}\int_{Z}\alpha^{m-1}\cdot\beta+O(\varepsilon^{n-m+1})}{\alpha^{n}-{n\choose m}\varepsilon^{n-m}\int_{Z}\alpha^{m}+O(\varepsilon^{n-m+1})}

\displaystyle=\mu-{n-1\choose m-1}\varepsilon^{n-m}\Big(\int_{Z}\alpha^{m-1}\cdot\beta-\mu\frac{n}{m}\int_{Z}\alpha^{m}\Big)+O(\varepsilon^{n-m+1})

\displaystyle<\mu

if
\varepsilon<<1
.

∎

Next, again taking inspiration from the surface case, we also expect that

\zeta_{min}=\inf\frac{(\alpha^{\prime})^{n-1}\cdot\beta^{\prime}}{(\alpha^{\prime})^{n}},

where the infimum is now taken over all modifications
\pi:X^{\prime}\rightarrow X
and all
semi-stable
classes
(\alpha^{\prime},\beta^{\prime})
of the form
\beta^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\beta
and
\alpha^{\prime}=\pi^{*}\alpha-D
, where
D
is an effective divisor such that
\alpha^{\prime}
is big and nef. Note that for
\varepsilon<<1
,
\alpha-\varepsilon\beta
is Kähler, and hence is in particular pseudoeffective. Moreover
(\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha-(\alpha-\varepsilon\beta),\beta)
is obviously stable, and hence the class of the divisors considered above is non-empty.

Heuristically, suppose the infimum in the definition of the minimal slope is achieved, then arguing as in the proof of the proposition above one can justify this expectation. Of course, whether the minimal slope is achieved or not seems to be a difficult question in general, and should be intimately connected to the resolution of Conjecture
1.5
.

3.
The
J
-flow with symmetry on projective bundles

We study the convergence of the
J
flow on projective bundles with Calabi ansatz in this section and in particular prove Theorems
1.6
and
1.7
. The convergence results were first established for the projective bundles
X_{m,n}=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}\oplus\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}}(-1))
on
\mathbb{P}^{n}
in
[
24
]
. As we shall see below, these results extend with no effort to more general projective bundles. Our novelty, in line with the theme of the present work, is to introduce a version of the minimal slope for projective bundles, and to relate the convergence of the flow and the limiting current to this slope. We also take this opportunity to settle some technical difficulties in
[
24
]
and fill in some details. The reader can refer to Remark
3.7
for a detailed comparison. In the final sub-section, we discuss bubbling phenomenon for the
J
-equation.

3.1.
The set-up and some preliminary observations

We will briefly review the Calabi ansatz
[
7
]
following the moment map method of Hwang-Singer
[
30
]
(cf. also
[
40
]
for more details). Let
X=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{O}_{M}\oplus L^{\oplus(m+1)})\rightarrow M
be a projective bundle, where
\pi:L\rightarrow M
is a negative line bundle on a compact Kähler manifold
(M^{n},\omega_{M})
such that
\omega_{M}\in c_{1}(L^{-1})
. We also denote the natural projection map from
X
to
M
by
\pi:X\rightarrow M
. There is natural “divisor at infinity” given by
D_{\infty}:=\mathbb{P}(L^{\oplus(m+1)})
. We let
P_{0}
be the projectivization of the zero section
(1,0,\cdots,0)\in\mathcal{O}\oplus L^{\oplus(m+1)}
. Note that
P_{0}
is of dimension
n
. For the sake of notation we set
E=\mathcal{O}\oplus L^{\oplus(m+1)}
, and
r=m+2
to be the rank of
E
. We denote the class of
D_{\infty}
by
\eta
. Then note that
\eta=c_{1}(\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}(E)}(1))
. In particular, the restriction of
\eta
to each fiber is simply
\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{r-1}}(1)
. We also think of classes in
H^{*}(M)
as classes in
H^{*}(X)
via the pullback
\pi^{*}
, and in fact this gives an injection.

Next, we outline the construction of Kähler metrics with Calabi ansatz. We fix a reference Kähler form
\omega_{M}\in c_{1}(L^{-1})
and choose a Hermitian metric
h
on
L
such that

\omega_{M}=-Ric(h)=\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2\pi}\partial\overline{\partial}\log h.

Denote by the
\pi:X\rightarrow M
the natural projection map, and consider Kähler metrics
\omega
on
X
of the form

\omega=\pi^{*}\omega_{M}+\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2\pi}\partial\overline{\partial}u(\rho),

where

\rho=\log|w|^{2}h(z),

z=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n})
are coordinates on the base
M
and
w=(w_{1},\cdots,w_{m+1})
are fiber coordinates on
L^{\oplus(m+1)}
. Working at a point
(z_{0},w_{0})
, and a triviliazation in which
d\log h(z_{0})=0
, one can compute that

\omega=(1+u^{\prime}(\rho))\pi^{*}\omega_{M}+\frac{he^{-\rho}}{2\pi}\Big(u^{\prime}\delta_{ij}+he^{-\rho}(u^{\prime\prime}-u^{\prime})\bar{w}_{i}w_{j}\Big)\sqrt{-1}dw_{i}\wedge d\bar{w}_{j}.

Note that
\rho=\infty
corresponds to the divisor
D_{\infty}
, while
\rho=-\infty
corresponds to
P_{0}
. It was observed by Calabi
[
7
]
that
\omega
extends to a Kähler metric on
X
if and only if the following conditions hold:

•

u^{\prime}>0
and
u^{\prime\prime}>0
.

•

U_{0}=u(\ln r)
is extendible to a smooth function across
r=0
with
U_{0}^{\prime}(0)>0
.

•

U_{\infty}(r)=u(-\ln r)+b\ln r
is extendible to a smooth function across
r=0
for some
b>0
and
U_{\infty}^{\prime}(0)>0
.

From the last two properties one can easily infer that
u^{\prime}
is a bounded function with

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow-\infty}u^{\prime}(\rho)=0,~\lim_{\rho\rightarrow\infty}u^{\prime}(\rho)=b>0,

and the Kähler class of
\omega
equals
\beta=[\omega_{M}]+b\eta.
A local model for such a function is given by

u_{0}(\rho)=b\log(1+e^{\rho}).

We fix one such
\omega
for the rest of this section. To solve the
J
-equation we seek Kähler metrics
\chi\in\alpha:=[\omega_{M}]+a\eta
such that

\chi=\pi^{*}\omega_{M}+\frac{\sqrt{-1}}{2\pi}\partial\overline{\partial}v(\rho),

where once again
v
is monotonically increasing, strictly convex and satisfies the above asymptotics when
\rho\rightarrow\pm\infty
. We call Kähler metrics satisfying the above conditions as metrics satisfying the Calabi ansatz.

Let
\omega
and
\chi
be Kähler metrics in the classes
\alpha
and
\beta
respectively satisfying the Calabi ansatz. Then as in
[
24
]
, we can define a function
\psi:[0,a]\rightarrow[0,b]
by
\psi(v^{\prime}(\rho))=u^{\prime}(\rho)
. It is then easy to see that the eigenvalues of
\omega
with respect to
\chi
are given by

\frac{1+\psi}{1+x}\text{ (with multiplicity $n$)},~\frac{\psi}{x}\text{ (with multiplicity $m$)},\text{ and }\psi^{\prime}\text{ (with multiplicity one)}.

Then solving the
J
-equation is equivalent to solving the following first order linear ODE for a monotonically increasing function:

(3.1)

\begin{cases}\psi^{\prime}(x)+\psi(x)\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)=\mu-\frac{n}{1+x}\\
\psi(0)=0,~\psi(a)=b.\end{cases}

More generally for
s\in[0,a)
we consider the following family of first order ODEs:

(3.2)

\begin{cases}\psi_{s}^{\prime}(x)+\psi_{s}(x)\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)=\mu_{s}-\frac{n}{1+x}\\
\psi_{s}(s)=0,~\psi_{s}(a)=b.\end{cases}

It is easily seen that a general solution to the ODE is given by

\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)=\frac{\mu_{s}I_{m,n,s}(x)-nI_{m,n-1,s}(x)+A_{s}}{(1+x)^{n}x^{m}},

where

I_{m,n,s}(x)=\int_{s}^{x}(1+t)^{n}t^{m}\,dt.

The boundary conditions force
A_{s}=0
, and for
s\in[0,a)
,

\mu_{s}=\frac{(1+a)^{n}a^{m}b+nI_{m,n-1,s}(a)}{I_{m,n,s}(a)}.

Note that
\mu_{s}\rightarrow\infty
as
s\rightarrow a
from the left. We first begin with a geometric characterization of
\mu_{s}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
\tilde{\pi}:\tilde{X}=\mathrm{Bl}_{P_{0}}X\rightarrow X
be the blow down map with exceptional divisor
E
, and
\tilde{\alpha}_{s}=\tilde{\pi}^{*}\alpha-sE
. Then

\mu_{s}=(m+n+1)\frac{(\tilde{\alpha}_{s})^{m+n}\cdot\tilde{\pi}^{*}\beta}{(\tilde{\alpha}_{s})^{m+n+1}}.

In particular,

(1)

\mu_{0}=\mu(X,\alpha,\beta)
, and

(2)

if
\mu\leq n
, then
\lambda
is the unique real number in
[0,a)
(with
\lambda=0
when
\mu=n
) such that

\zeta_{inv}=(m+n+1)\frac{(\tilde{\alpha}_{\lambda})^{m+n}\cdot\tilde{\pi}^{*}\beta}{(\tilde{\alpha}_{\lambda})^{m+n+1}}.

The proof involves standard intersection theoretic calculations, and we relegate it do the appendix. Next, we have the following elementary observation on the monotonicity of the solutions to the above ODE. As a consequence we obtain an existence result for the
J
equation on projective bundles.

Lemma 3.2
.

The following are equivalent.

(1)

\mu_{s}(1+s)\geq n
.

(2)

The solution
\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)
to
(
3.2
)
satisfies
\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)>0
for all
x\in(s,a]
.

(3)

The solution
\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)
to
(
3.2
)
satisfies
\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in(s,a]
. We also have that
\lim_{x\rightarrow s^{+}}\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{\prime}(x)
exists, and the limit is strictly positive if and only if
\mu_{s}(1+s)>n.

Proof.

Suppose
(1)
holds. From the expression for the general solution we have the identity

(1+x)^{n}x^{m}\psi(x)=\int_{s}^{x}(1+t)^{n-1}t^{m}\Big(\mu_{s}(1+t)-n\Big)\,dt.

From this it clearly follows that
\psi(x)>0
on
(s,a]
. Conversely it is easy to see that
(2)\implies(1)
. Next, suppose
(2)
holds. Then differentiating the above integral formula, and using the product rule we have

\displaystyle(1+x)^{n-1}x^{m}\Big(\mu_{s}(1+x)-n\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big[(1+x)^{n}x^{m}\psi(x)\Big]^{\prime}

\displaystyle=n(1+x)^{n-1}x^{m}\psi(x)+m(1+x)^{n}x^{m-1}\psi(x)+(1+x)^{n}x^{m}\psi^{\prime}(x).

Since
\psi(s)=0
, we then have that
\lim_{x\rightarrow s^{+}}\psi^{\prime}(x)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
\mu_{s}(1+s)=n.
Next, differentiating the defining equation for
\psi
we see that

\psi^{\prime\prime}(x)+\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)\psi^{\prime}(x)=\Big(\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}+\frac{m}{x^{2}}\Big)\psi(x)+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}>0.

From a standard ODE comparison principle, this implies that
\psi^{\prime}(x)>0
for
x\in(s,a]
.

Conversely suppose there is a solution to
(
3.2
)
with
\psi^{\prime}(x)>0
for all
x\in(s,a]
, then
\psi(x)>0
for all
x\in(s,a]
, and hence
(2)
and
(1)
hold. ∎

Corollary 3.3
.

With
(X,\alpha,\beta)
as above, the following are equivalent.

(1)

\mu>n.

(2)

There exists a smooth solution
\chi\in\alpha
to the
J
-equation

\Lambda_{\chi}\omega=\mu.

(3)

(X,\alpha,\beta)
is
J
-slope stable.

Next, we try to analyse the case when
\mu_{s}(1+s)=n
. To this effect, we let

\lambda:=\inf\{s\in[0,a)~|~\text{ there exists a solution to $\hyperref@@ii[eq:ode-j-equation-s]{\textup{{(\ignorespaces\ref*{eq:ode-j-equation-s}\/)}}}$ such that $\psi(x)>0$ on $(s,a]$}\}.

Then we have the following corollary.

Corollary 3.4
.

With
\lambda
defined as above and
\mu\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle\lambda=\inf\{s\in[0,a)~|~\mu_{s}(1+s)>n\}.

Furthermore,
\lambda
is the
unique
solution to the equation

\mu_{\lambda}(1+\lambda)=n

on the interval
[0,a)
with
\lambda=0
if and only if
\mu=n
. In fact
\mu_{s}
is a convex function for
s\in[0,a)
and
\lambda
is it’s unique minimizer.

Proof.

We only need to prove the last two statements. Consider the polynomial

p(s)=\Big(\mu_{s}(1+s)-n\Big)I_{m,n,s}(a).

This is degree
m+n+1
polynomial satisfying

\displaystyle p(0)=(\mu-n)I_{m,n}(a)<0

\displaystyle p(a)=(1+a)^{n+1}a^{m}b>0.

So there exists at least one zero in
(0,a)
. We claim that
p(s)
is increasing on
(0,a)
, thereby proving the uniqueness of the root. First by differentiating the equation

(1+a)^{n}a^{m}b=\mu_{s}I_{m,n,s}(a)-nI_{m,n-1,s}(a)

with respect to
s
, we observe that

\frac{d\mu_{s}}{ds}=\frac{\Big(\mu_{s}(1+s)-n\Big)(1+s)^{n-1}s^{m}}{I_{m,n,s}(a)}.

But then,

\displaystyle p^{\prime}(s)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{d\mu_{s}}{ds}(1+s)+\mu_{s}\Big)I_{m,n,s}(a)-\Big(\mu_{s}(1+s)-n\Big)(1+s)^{n}s^{m}

\displaystyle=\mu_{s}I_{m,n,s}(a)>0,

and hence
p
is strictly increasing. Note that
s=\lambda
is a unique critical point of
\mu_{s}
. Convexity of
\mu_{s}
follows from the observation that

\mu_{s}(1+s)-n=\frac{p(s)}{I_{m,n,s}(a)}

is increasing in
s
and hence so is
\frac{d\mu_{s}}{ds}
. It then follows that
s=\lambda
is the unique minimizer of
\mu_{s}
.
∎

Next, we have the following important consequence.

Corollary 3.5
.

Let
\mu\leq n
. Then for all
s\in(0,a)
with
s\neq\lambda
and all
x\in(0,a)
we have

\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)<\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x).

Proof.

For each fixed
x\in(0,a)
, we compute

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}[x^{m}(1+x)^{n}\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)]

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mu_{s}}{ds}I_{m,n,s}(x)-s^{m}(1+s)^{n}\mu_{s}+ns^{m}(1+s)^{n-1}

\displaystyle=\Big(\mu_{s}(1+s)-n\Big)(1+s)^{n-1}s^{m}\left(\frac{I_{m,n,s}(x)}{I_{m,n,s}(a)}-1\right),

which is negative when
s>\lambda
and positive when
s<\lambda
. Here we used the fact that
\mu_{s}(1+s)>n
when
s>\lambda
and
\mu_{s}(1+s)<n
when
s<\lambda
. Hence, the required conclusion follows immediately.
∎

Next, we write down the
J
-flow in terms of the moment interval function
\psi
. Let
\chi_{\varphi(t)}=\pi^{*}\omega_{M}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v(\rho,t)
, and
\psi(x,t):[0,a]\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,b]
such that

\psi(v^{\prime}(\rho,t),t)=u^{\prime}(\rho).

Then
\chi_{\varphi(t)}
solving the
J
flow is equivalent to
\psi
solving the following parabolic equation:

(3.3)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=Q(\psi)\Big(\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x^{2}}+\Big(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x}\Big)\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}-\Big(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)\psi-\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big),

where
Q:[0,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is given by

Q(y)=u^{\prime\prime}([u^{\prime}]^{-1}(y)).

We denote the initial function by
\psi_{0}:=\psi(\cdot,0)
.
From now on, we denote the spacial derivatives of
\psi
by
\psi^{\prime}
and the time derivative by
\dot{\psi}
. We begin the with following basic observation.

Lemma 3.6
.

There exists a solution to
(
3.3
)
for all time
t>0
. Moreover, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
t
such that for all
(x,t)\in[0,a]\times[0,\infty)
,

0<\psi^{\prime}(x,t)<C.

Proof.

It is well known
[
11
]
that the
J
flow on
any
Kähler manifold exists for all time. Differentiating the defining equation for
\psi
, we see that at a point
x=v^{\prime}(\rho)
,

\psi^{\prime}(x,t)\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial\rho^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial\rho^{2}}.

The positivity of
\psi^{\prime}
then follows from the convexity (in the space variable) of
v(\rho,t)
and
u(\rho)
. Next, it follows from the maximum principle (cf.
[
11
]
) that there exists a constant
C
such that

\Lambda_{\chi_{\varphi(t)}}\omega<C.

The upper bound then follows from the fact that

\Lambda_{\chi_{\varphi(t)}}\omega=\psi^{\prime}(x,t)+\psi(x,t)\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+x}.

Alternately, the bounds can also be proved directly by differentiating
(
3.3
)
and an application of maximum principle.
∎

[

]
.

Remark 3.7

(Comparison with the work of Fang-Lai

.

is proved in

3.2.
Comparison and monotonicity along the flow

If

. It turns out that the behaviour of the flow depends on the sign
\mu-n-m-1
. For the sake of completeness we will state all our results on the behaviour of the flow (ie. monotonicity, comparison principle etc.) for both the cases, but we will provide proofs only for the case when
\mu<n+m+1
since this subsumes the semi-stable and unstable cases. As in
[
24
]
, we first prove (weak) convergence starting with a very specific initial Kähler metric
\chi_{0}
so that

(3.4)

\psi_{0}(x)=\frac{b}{a}x.

We start with the following elementary observation about the initial function.

Lemma 3.8
.

If
\mu\leq n
, then
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}
is convex. In particular, for all
x\in[\lambda,a]
,

\psi_{0}(x)\geq\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x).

Proof.

The second part follows easily from the convexity and the fact that the inequality is satisfied at the end points. To prove the convexity we re-write

\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x)=T_{1}+T_{2},

where

\displaystyle T_{1}(x)=\frac{\mu_{\lambda}I_{m,n}(x)-nI_{m,n-1}(x)}{x^{m}(1+x)^{n}},

\displaystyle T_{2}(x)=\frac{nI_{m,n-1}(\lambda)-\mu_{\lambda}I_{m,n}(\lambda)}{x^{m}(1+x)^{n}}.

We claim that
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
are both convex. First note that

\displaystyle nI_{m,n-1}(\lambda)-\mu_{\lambda}I_{m,n}(\lambda)=\int_{0}^{\lambda}t^{m}(1+t)^{n-1}\Big(n-\mu_{\lambda}(1+t)\Big)\,dt\geq 0,

since
\mu_{\lambda}(1+\lambda)=n
. On the other hand
x^{-m}(1+x)^{-n}
is clearly convex, and so
T_{2}
is convex. For the convexity of
T_{1}
, we further re-write it as

T_{1}=x+(\mu_{\lambda}-n-m-1)\frac{I_{m,n}(x)}{x^{m}(1+x)^{n}},

using the elementary identity

(3.5)

(n+m+1)I_{m,n}(x)=nI_{m,n-1}(x)+x^{m+1}(1+x)^{n}.

We now claim that

q_{m,n}(x):=\frac{I_{m,n}(x)}{x^{m}(1+x)^{n}}

is concave on
(0,\infty)
. The convexity of
T_{1}
then follows immediately since
\mu_{\lambda}<n<n+m+1
. The proof of the claim is elementary albeit tedious. We provide details of only the key calculations. The motivated reader can easily fill the gaps. By the binomial expansion we have

q_{m,n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{m+1+k}\frac{x^{k+1}}{(1+x)^{n}}.

Differentiating twice we get

(1+x)^{n+2}q^{\prime\prime}_{m,n}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{n}{k}\frac{1}{m+1+k}\Big((k+1-n)(k-n)x^{k+1}+2(k-n)(k+1)x^{k}+k(k+1)x^{k-1}\Big).

Grouping the terms carefully we have

\displaystyle(1+x)^{n+2}q^{\prime\prime}_{m,n}(x)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k-1}\frac{(k-n)(k-1-n)x^{k}}{m+k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k}\frac{2(k-n)(k+1)x^{k}}{m+1+k}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\binom{n}{k+1}\frac{(k+1)(k+2)x^{k}}{m+2+k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\Bigg(\binom{n}{k-1}\frac{(k-n)(k-1-n)}{m+k}+\binom{n}{k}\frac{2(k-n)(k+1)}{m+1+k}+\binom{n}{k+1}\frac{(k+1)(k+2)}{m+2+k}\Bigg)x^{k}

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\Big(\frac{-2n}{m+1}+\frac{2n}{m+2}\Big)

\displaystyle=-2mn\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\binom{n-1}{k}\frac{x^{k}}{(m+k)(m+1+k)(m+2+k)}-\frac{2n}{(m+1)(m+2)}

\displaystyle<0.

Therefore,
q_{m,n}
is a concave function on
(0,\infty)
.
∎

Next, we show that the inequality above is preserved throughout the flow.

Lemma 3.9
.

Let
\mu\leq n
, and let
\psi(x,t)
solve the
J
flow
(
3.3
)
with
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
given by
(
3.4
)
. Then for all
(x,t)\in[0,a]\times[0,\infty)
we have

\psi(x,t)\geq\tilde{\psi}(x),

where

\tilde{\psi}(x)=\begin{cases}0,~x\in[0,\lambda]\\
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x),~x\in[\lambda,a].\end{cases}

Proof.

It is enough to show that
\psi(x,t)\geq\tilde{\psi}(x)
on
[\lambda,a]
. Consider the function
H:[\lambda,a]\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

H(x,t)=\psi(x,t)-\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x).

Then
H
satisfies the following evolution equation:

(3.6)

\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}=Q(\psi)\Bigg(H^{\prime\prime}+\Big(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x}\Big)H^{\prime}-\Big(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)H\Bigg).

Note that we used the fact that
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}
solves the second order ODE

\displaystyle\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}^{\prime\prime}+\Big(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x}\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}^{\prime}-\Big(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}=\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}.

We argue by contradiction. Suppose there exists
T>0
such that

H^{*}:=H(x^{*},t^{*}):=\inf_{(x,t)\in[0,a]\times[0,T]}H(x,t)<0.

Since

H(\lambda,t),~H(a,t),~H(x,0)\geq 0,

we have that
(x^{*},t)\in(\lambda,a)\times(0,T]
. We then get a contradiction by applying the maximum principle. Indeed,

\displaystyle 0\geq\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}(x^{*},t^{*})\geq-Q(\psi(x^{*},t^{*}))\Big(\frac{m}{{x^{*}}^{2}}+\frac{n}{{(1+x^{*})}^{2}}\Big)H^{*}>0.

∎

Lemma 3.10
.

For all
x\in[0,a]
,

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=0}\psi(\cdot,t)\leq 0.

Proof.

First note that by the identity
(
3.5
)
at
x=a
and the fact that
\mu\leq n
, we have

0\geq\Big(\mu-(n+m+1)\Big)I_{m,n}(a)=a^{m}(1+a)^{n}(b-a),

and so
b<a
.
Next, using the
J
flow equation we compute

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=0}\psi(\cdot,t)

\displaystyle=Q(\psi_{0})\Big(\psi_{0}^{\prime\prime}+\Big(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x}\Big)\psi_{0}^{\prime}-\Big(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)\psi_{0}-\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=Q(\psi_{0})\Big(\frac{b}{a}\Big(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x}\Big)-\frac{bx}{a}\Big(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)-\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)

\displaystyle=Q(\psi_{0})\frac{(b-a)n}{a(1+x)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq 0.

∎

Next, we prove that the monotonicity is preserved along the flow.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\mu\leq n
, and let
\psi(x,t)
solve the
J
flow
(
3.3
)
with
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
given by
(
3.4
)
. Then for all
(x,t)\in[0,a]\times[0,\infty)
we have

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\leq 0.

Proof.

Let
H(x,t)=\dot{\psi}(x,t)
. Then
H
satisfies the following evolution equation

\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}=\frac{Q^{\prime}(\psi)\dot{\psi}}{Q(\psi)}H+Q(\psi)\Big(H^{\prime\prime}+(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x})H^{\prime}-(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}})H\Big).

We once again argue by contradiction. Suppose there exists a
T>0
such that

\sup_{[0,a]\times[0,T]}H(x,t)>0.

By Calabi ansatz, it is not difficult to see that for all
t\geq 0
, there exists a uniform constant
C_{T}
such that

\Big|\frac{\psi^{\prime}(x,t)}{x}-\frac{\psi(x,t)}{x^{2}}\Big|\leq C_{T}.

We then set

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=1+\sup_{[0,a]\times[0,T]}\Big|\frac{Q^{\prime}(\psi)\dot{\psi}(x,t)}{Q(\psi)}\Big|

\displaystyle=1+\sup_{[0,a]\times[0,T]}\Big|Q^{\prime}(\psi)\left(\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x^{2}}+\Big(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x}\Big)\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}-\Big(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\Big)\psi-\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}}\right)\Big|.

Now consider the function
F(x,t)=e^{-Bt}H(x,t).
It satisfies the following evolution equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\Big(-B+\frac{Q^{\prime}(\psi)\dot{\psi}}{Q(\psi)}\Big)F+Q(\psi)\Big(F^{\prime\prime}+(\frac{m}{x}+\frac{n}{1+x})F^{\prime}-(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}})F\Big).

Let

F^{*}=F(x^{*},t^{*}):=\sup_{[0,a]\times[0,T]}F(x,t)>0.

As before, clearly
(x^{*},t^{*})\in(0,a)\times(0,T]
and so by maximum principle,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\leq\Big(-B+\frac{Q^{\prime}(\psi)\dot{\psi}}{Q(\psi)}\Big)F^{*}-Q(\psi)(\frac{m}{x^{2}}+\frac{n}{(1+x)^{2}})F^{*}

\displaystyle\leq-F^{*}<0,

a contradiction.
∎

3.3.
Proof of convergence

We first prove a weaker version of the theorem
1.6
for the special initial metric corresponding to
\psi_{0}
constructed in the previous section. At the level of
\psi
we have the following basic result.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mu\leq n
, and let
\psi(x,t)
solve the
J
flow
(
3.3
)
with
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)
given by
(
3.4
)
. Then
\psi(x,t)
converges in
C^{1}
to

\psi_{\infty}(x)=\begin{cases}0,~x\in[0,\lambda]\\
\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x),~x\in[\lambda,a].\end{cases}

Proof.

From the
C^{1}
-estimate in Lemma
3.6
and the monotonicity it follows that

\psi(x,t)\rightarrow\psi_{\infty}(x)

uniformly as
t\rightarrow\infty
, where
\psi_{\infty}
is an increasing and continuous function on
[0,a]
. Since
\psi(x,t)
is decreasing in
t
, it follows that
\psi_{\infty}(x)<b
whenever
x<a
. Moreover, by Lemma
3.9
, we also have that
\psi_{\infty}(x)\geq\tilde{\psi}(x)
for all
x
. We now consider the point-wise slope

\sigma[\psi]:=\psi^{\prime}+\psi\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+x},

which is defined for any differentiable monotonically increasing function
\psi:[0,a]\rightarrow[0,b]
and set
\sigma(x,t):=\sigma[\psi(x,t)].
Note that this is simply
\Lambda_{\chi_{t}}\omega
. The equation for
\psi
then takes the form

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=Q(\psi)\cdot\sigma^{\prime}(x,t).

In particular for each
t
,
\sigma(x,t)
is decreasing on
[0,a]
. The proof can be broken down into several steps.

•

We define

\displaystyle\lambda^{\prime}:=\sup\{x~|~\psi_{\infty}(x)=0\}

We claim that there exists a sequence of times
t_{j}\rightarrow\infty
such that for any compact set
K\subset(\lambda^{\prime},a)
,

\sigma(x,t_{j})\rightarrow\sigma_{\infty}

uniformly on
K
for some constant
\sigma_{\infty}
. To see this, fix any
0<\delta<<1
and
T>0
and observe that

\int_{T}^{\infty}\int_{\lambda^{\prime}+\delta}^{a-\delta}\Big|\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\Big|\,dx\,dt=\int_{\lambda^{\prime}+\delta}^{a-\delta}\Big(\psi(x,T)-\psi_{\infty}(x)\Big)\,dx\leq C,

for some constant
C
(independent of
\delta
). Now on
[\lambda^{\prime}+\delta,a-\delta]
,
\varepsilon<\psi_{\infty}<b-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(\delta)>0
. In particular there is a constant
c=c(\delta)>0
such that
Q(\psi(x,t))>c
for all
x\in[\lambda^{\prime}+\delta,a-\delta]
and
t>T
. Then

\int_{T}^{\infty}\Big(\sigma(\lambda^{\prime}+\delta,t)-\sigma(a-\delta,t)\Big)\,dt=\int_{T}^{\infty}\int_{\lambda^{\prime}-\delta}^{a-\delta}(-\sigma^{\prime}(x,t))\,dt\leq C=C(\delta),

and so there exists a sequence of times
t_{j}\rightarrow\infty
such that

\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\Big(\sigma(\lambda^{\prime}+\delta,t_{j})-\sigma(a-\delta,t_{j})\Big)=0.

Passing to a further sub-sequence we may assume that
\sigma(a-\delta,t_{j})\rightarrow\sigma_{\infty}
. But then by monotonicity,

\sigma(x,t_{j})\rightarrow\sigma_{\infty}

uniformly on
[\lambda^{\prime}+\delta,a-\delta].
Apriori
\sigma_{\infty}
may depend on
\delta
, but since the above convergence must hold for all
\delta
small enough, a simple diagonalization argument proves that
\sigma_{\infty}
is indeed independent of
\delta
and the uniform convergence holds for any compact subset
K\subset(\lambda^{\prime},a)
.

•

We next claim that
\psi_{\infty}
is differentiable on
(\lambda^{\prime},a)
and satisfies

\sigma[\psi_{\infty}]=\sigma_{\infty}.

To see this, let
x_{0},x\in(\lambda^{\prime},a)
and
t_{j}
be the sequence in the above claim. Then

\displaystyle\sigma_{\infty}(x-x_{0})

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\sigma(y,t_{j})\,dy

\displaystyle=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\Big(\psi^{\prime}(y,t_{j})+\psi(y,t_{j})\Big(\frac{n}{1+y}+\frac{m}{y}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+y}\Big)\,dy

\displaystyle=\psi_{\infty}(x)-\psi_{\infty}(x_{0})+\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\Big(\psi_{\infty}(y)\Big(\frac{n}{1+y}+\frac{m}{y}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+y}\Big)\,dy.

Rearranging we have the integral formula

\psi_{\infty}(x)=\psi_{\infty}(x_{0})+\sigma_{\infty}(x-x_{0})-\int_{x_{0}}^{x}\Big(\psi_{\infty}(y)\Big(\frac{n}{1+y}+\frac{m}{y}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+y}\Big)\,dy,

from which the conclusion follows.

•

Next, we claim that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda
. Consequently,
\sigma_{\infty}=\mu_{\lambda}
and
\psi_{\infty}\equiv\tilde{\psi}
. Since
\psi_{\infty}
solves a
J
-equation type ODE on
[\lambda^{\prime},a]
with boundary conditions
\psi_{\infty}(\lambda^{\prime})=0
and
\psi_{\infty}(a)=b
, it forces
\psi_{\infty}\equiv\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda^{\prime}}
on
[\lambda^{\prime},a]
. Furthermore since
\psi_{\infty}
is monotonically increasing, by Lemma
3.2
, we must have
\mu_{\lambda^{\prime}}(1+\lambda^{\prime})\geq n
, and hence by Corollary
3.4
(or rather it’s proof) since
\mu_{s}(1+s)
is increasing in
s
, we must have that
\lambda^{\prime}\geq\lambda
. On the other hand, by Lemma
3.9
and Corollary
3.5
, we have

\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda^{\prime}}(x)=\psi_{\infty}(x)\geq\tilde{\psi}(x)

on
[\lambda^{\prime},a]
which is in contradiction to Corollary
3.5
if
\lambda^{\prime}>\lambda
. Hence we must have that
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda
. The second part of the claim follows immediately. To summarize,
\psi(x,t)
converges uniformly to
\psi_{\infty}
on compact subsets of
(\lambda,a)
and
\psi_{\infty}
satisfies the following equation on
(\lambda,a)
:

\psi_{\infty}^{\prime}(x)+(n-1)\frac{\psi_{\infty}(x)}{x}=\zeta_{inv}.

In particular,

\psi_{\infty}(x)=\begin{cases}\tilde{\psi}_{\lambda}(x),~x\geq\lambda\\
0,~x\in[0,\lambda].\end{cases}

•

For any compact set
K\subset(\lambda,a)
we claim that
\psi^{\prime}(x,t)
converges uniformly to
\psi_{\infty}^{\prime}(x)
, and hence there exists a constant
C=C(K)
such that

C^{-1}\leq\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\leq C

for all
x\in K
and
t\geq 0
.
This follows easily from the observation

\psi^{\prime}(x,t)=\sigma(x,t)-\psi(x,t)\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+x}\xrightarrow{\text{u.c}}\sigma_{\infty}-\psi_{\infty}(x)\Big(\frac{n}{1+x}+\frac{m}{x}\Big)+\frac{n}{1+x}=\psi_{\infty}^{\prime}(x).

∎

At this point, one can easily obtain higher order estimates, and smooth convergence (away from
P_{0}
) along the flow for the
special initial data
. Instead, we will now obtain higher order estimates and convergence along the flow starting with an
arbitrary initial metric
satisfying the Calabi ansatz, thereby completing the proof of Theorem

. To this end we consider the following quantities:

•

Let
f:[0,b]\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow[0,a]

Equivalently,
f(\cdot,t)=[\psi(\cdot,t)]^{-1}
.

•

Analogously we define

•

The normalized local potential by

•

We similarly define

•

Finally we let

0,~\rho\geq\rho_{0}>0,\end{cases}

Lemma 3.13
.

If we let
\hat{\phi}_{\infty}:=\phi_{\infty}-\eta

\hat{\phi}_{\infty}
is uniformly bounded on
(-\infty,\infty)

Proof.

Note that

is a convex function with

\tilde{v}_{0}(\mathbb{R})=[\lambda,a]

.

∎

.

There exists a constant

Proof of Theorem
1.6

We let

be the solution to the

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\phi(\rho,t)=\mu-\Lambda_{\chi_{\phi}}\omega.

A simple application of maximum principle shows that

||\varphi-\phi||_{C^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times[0,\infty))}\leq C.

Recall that

\hat{\varphi}(\rho,t)=\varphi(\rho,t)-\phi(0,t).

\frac{\partial\hat{\varphi}}{\partial t}=\mu-\Lambda_{\chi_{0}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\hat{\varphi}}\omega-\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}(0,t)=\sigma_{\infty}+\delta(t)-\Lambda_{\chi_{0}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\hat{\varphi}}\omega,

\theta=\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\tilde{\pi}^{*}V_{\infty}\in\alpha^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}

F=\hat{\varphi}-(V_{\infty}-v_{0}).

Then
F
is smooth on
X\setminus P_{0}

\infty
along
E
. The key estimate that we need is the following.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
\chi_{t}=\chi_{0}+\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}\varphi
. There exist constants

such that

\Lambda_{\omega}\chi_{t}(\cdot)\leq Ce^{AF(\cdot,t)}

Proof.

We adapt the arguments in
[
38
]
. The evolution equation for
\log\Lambda_{\omega}\chi_{t}

4.1.
A review of the cotangent flow

Unlike for the

[

(

\begin{cases}\frac{d}{dt}\varphi=\cot\theta_{\varphi}-\cot\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta)\\
\varphi(0)=\underline{\varphi}.\end{cases}

From a PDE point of view it is important to assume that

since this condition is preserved along the flow and makes sure the right hand side is convex. The following theorem is proved in

Let
(X^{n},\omega)
be a compact Kähler manifold and
\chi

(1,1)
form in

\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta)\in(0,\pi)

satisfy the condition

above, the flow
(

⋆

t

)
has a unique solution on

.

In dimension two one can easily verify that an initial function satisfying condition
(
B
)
can
always
be found, no matter what class
\alpha
is chosen so long as
\alpha\cdot\beta\neq 0
.

.

Let
X
be a compact Kähler surface with a Kähler class

and a real

such that

such that

is a positive constant. In particular,
\underline{\varphi}

Moreover, if

(

Without loss of generality we may assume that

Then by hypothesis,

Let

Then

Now let

4.2.
The set-up and some preliminary observations

The dHYM equations on

with Calabi ansatz were first studied

where a complete solution to the existence problem is provided when the pair

is stable. In this section we restrict our attention to the case when

and study the convergence of the cotangent flow. In particular, we prove that the cotangent flow converges to the solutions constructed in Section

as basis element for
H^{1,1}(X,\mathbb{R})
, and we also use the suggestive notation
H=D_{\infty}
for the divisor or hyperplane at infinity. By scaling we normalize so that
\beta=b[H]-[E]
, and let
\alpha=p[H]-q[E]
an arbitrary real class. Thinking of
\mathrm{Bl}_{x_{0}}\mathbb{P}^{2}
as a compactification of
\mathbb{C}^{2}\setminus\{0\}
with the usual coordinates
(z_{1},z_{2})
we can write any invariant metric
\omega\in\beta
as

\omega=\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}u(\rho),

where
\rho=\log(|z_{1}|^{2}+|z_{2}|^{2}).
The condition that
\omega
is a (smooth) Kähler metric is that
u^{\prime},u^{\prime\prime}>0
and that
u
satisfies certain asymptotics as
\rho\rightarrow\pm\infty
. The precise nature of the asymptotics will not be important to us. The condition that
\omega\in\beta
is then equivalent to
u^{\prime}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow(1,b)
with

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow-\infty}u^{\prime}(\rho)=1,~\lim_{\rho\rightarrow\infty}u^{\prime}(\rho)=b.

Similarly we can express any smooth real
(1,1)
form
\chi\in\alpha
as

\chi=\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v(\rho),

and the corresponding boundary asymptotics for
v^{\prime}(\rho)
are

\lim_{\rho\rightarrow-\infty}v^{\prime}(\rho)=q,~\lim_{\rho\rightarrow\infty}v^{\prime}(\rho)=p.

Since
\alpha=p[H]-q[E]
and
\beta=b[H]-[E]
we compute that

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}=p^{2}-q^{2},\ \ \beta^{2}=b^{2}-1\ \ \text{and}\ \ \alpha\cdot\beta=bp-q,

and so

(4.1)

\displaystyle c_{0}=\cot\vartheta(X,\alpha,\beta)=\frac{p^{2}-q^{2}-b^{2}+1}{2(bp-q)}.

We let
\psi:[1,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
such that

v^{\prime}(\rho)=\psi(u^{\prime}(\rho)).

It is then easy to see that the eigenvalues of
\omega^{-1}\chi
are given by
\psi^{\prime}
and
\psi/x
(each with multiplicity one). In particular

\theta_{\chi}(x)=\mathrm{arccot}\psi^{\prime}(x)+\mathrm{arccot}\frac{\psi(x)}{x}.

The dHYM equation is then equivalent to
\theta_{\chi}^{\prime}\equiv 0
or equivalently

(4.2)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\psi^{2}-x^{2})^{\prime}=2c_{0}(x\psi)^{\prime}\\
\psi(1)=q,~\psi(b)=p.\end{cases}

As for the
J
-equation we also consider the following family of auxiliary equations for

:

(4.3)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\psi^{2}-x^{2})^{\prime}=2\tilde{c}_{s}(x\psi)^{\prime}\\
\psi(1)=s,~\psi(b)=p.\end{cases}

The auxiliary equations are precisely the dHYM equations for the perturbed classes
\tilde{\alpha}_{s}=\alpha-(s-q)[E]=p[H]-s[E]
.
As in
[
31
]
, the above equation integrates to

(4.4)

\psi^{2}-2\tilde{c}_{s}x\psi-x^{2}=\tilde{A}_{s},

where

\displaystyle\tilde{c}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{p^{2}-s^{2}-b^{2}+1}{2(pb-s)}=\frac{\tilde{\alpha}_{s}^{2}-\beta^{2}}{2\tilde{\alpha}_{s}\cdot\beta}

(4.5)

\displaystyle\tilde{A}_{s}

\displaystyle=s^{2}-2\tilde{c}_{s}s-1=p^{2}-2bp\tilde{c}_{s}-b^{2}.

We begin with the following elementary numerical inequalities.

Lemma 4.4
.

Since
s<pb
, we have

\tilde{c}_{s}<\min\left(\frac{p}{b},\frac{p-s}{b-1},bp\right).

In particular, we have

(1)

(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}-\tilde{c}_{s}\beta)
is a big class, and it is Kähler (resp. nef) if and only if
s>\tilde{c}_{s}
(resp.
\geq
).

(2)

The triple
(X,\tilde{\alpha}_{s},\beta)
is dHYM stable (resp. semi-stable) if and only if
s>\tilde{c}_{s}
(resp.
\geq
).

Proof.

Since
s<bp
, we see that
\tilde{\alpha}_{s}\cdot\beta>0

2.9

(\tilde{\alpha}_{s},\beta)
we see that
(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}-\tilde{c}_{s}\beta)
is a big class. In particular, we get

p-\tilde{c}_{s}b>0,~~~p-\tilde{c}_{s}b>s-\tilde{c}_{s}.

Alternatively, using

one can also obtain these inequalities by straightforward computation:

\displaystyle(p-\tilde{c}_{s}b)-(s-\tilde{c}_{s})

\displaystyle=\frac{(b+1)\Big((p-s)^{2}+(b-1)^{2}\Big)}{2(pb-s)}>0,

\displaystyle p-\tilde{c}_{s}b

\displaystyle=\frac{b(p^{2}+s^{2})-2ps+b(b^{2}-1)}{2(pb-s)}>\frac{(p-s)^{2}+b(b^{2}-1)}{2(pb-s)}>0,

\displaystyle bp-\tilde{c}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{(p^{2}+1)(b^{2}-1)+(bp-s)^{2}}{2(bp-s)}>0.

Note that

\tilde{\alpha}_{s}-\tilde{c}_{s}\beta=(p-\tilde{c}_{s}b)[H]-(s-\tilde{c}_{s})[E].

Hence
(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}-\tilde{c}_{s}\beta)
is a Kähler class if and only if
s-\tilde{c}_{s}>0
. Finally note that on a Kähler surface dHYM-stability of
(X,\tilde{\alpha}_{s},\beta)
is equivalent to the Kähler-ness of
(\tilde{\alpha}_{s}-\tilde{c}_{s}\beta)
.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

The following are equivalent.

(1)

s\geq\tilde{c}_{s}
.

(2)

There exists a unique solution
\tilde{\psi}_{s}
to
(
4.4
)
which is continuous on
[1,b]
and differentiable on
(1,b)
. Explicitly, we have

(4.6)

\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)=\tilde{c}_{s}x+\sqrt{\tilde{A}_{s}+(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})x^{2}}.

Moreover the solution also satisfies
(x\tilde{\psi}_{s})^{\prime}>0
.

Proof.

Solving the quadratic equation we see that any solution to
(
4.3
)
has to be of the form

\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)=\tilde{c}_{s}x\pm\sqrt{\tilde{A}_{s}+(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})x^{2}}.

We denote the positive and negative branches by
\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{+}
and
\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{-}
respectively and claim that the two branches do not intersect on
(1,b]
. To see this consider the quadratic

p(x)=\tilde{A}_{s}+(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})x^{2}.

It is enough to show that there is no zero in
(1,b]
. To see this, note that

p(1)=(s-\tilde{c}_{s})^{2}\geq 0,~p(b)=(p-\tilde{c}_{s}b)^{2}>0.

If there is a zero in
(1,b]
, then there exists a point
x_{0}\in(1,b)
such that

. But this a contradiction since

p^{\prime}(x_{0})=2x_{0}(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})>0.

In particular, if there is a continuous solution to
(
4.4

\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{+}
or
\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{-}.
Since

p=\tilde{\psi}_{s}(b)=\tilde{c}_{s}b+|p-\tilde{c}_{s}b|,

and since

, this forces
\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)=\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{+}(x)
. But then

s=\tilde{\psi}_{s}(1)=\tilde{c}_{s}+|s-\tilde{c}_{s}|,

and this forces

. Conversely, suppose

. Then by the same argument as above
\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)=\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{+}(x)
is the unique solution to
(
4.4
)
.

∎

Recall that
\xi\in[c_{0},t_{0})
is the unique real number solving
(
2.2
)
, and here
t_{0}=\min\left(\frac{p}{b},\frac{p-q}{b-1}\right)
,

. We have the following formulae for the volume of big (and Kähler) classes on

blowup at one point:

(4.7)

\mathrm{vol}\Big(x[H]-y[E]\Big)=\begin{cases}x^{n}-y^{n}~\text{ when }x>y>0;\\
x^{n}~\text{ when }x>0,~y\leq 0;\\
0,~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

If

, the class

is also Kähler and using
(
4.7
)
we easily see that

. While if
\xi\geq q
, the class
\alpha-\xi\beta
is big but not Kähler and again using
(
4.7
)
we easily see that

(4.8)

\xi=bp-\sqrt{(p^{2}+1)(b^{2}-1)},

since
\xi<\frac{p}{b}<bp+\sqrt{(p^{2}+1)(b^{2}-1)}
. Also note that for the case

the equation
(
2.2
)
can be re-written as

.

Lemma 4.6
.

As a function of
s
,
\tilde{c}_{s}
is strictly concave function on
(-\infty,bp)
and it has unique global maximum at
s=\xi
. In particular,

\xi=\sup\Big\{\tilde{c}_{s}~|~-\infty<s<bp\Big\}=\inf\Big\{s\in(-\infty,bp)~|~s\geq\tilde{c}_{s}\Big\}.

Moreover, the condition
q\leq c_{0}
is equivalent to
\xi\geq q
.

Proof.

Differentiating we get

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{c}_{s}}{ds}=\frac{(bp-s)^{2}-(p^{2}+1)(b^{2}-1)}{2(bp-s)^{2}},~~~\frac{d^{2}\tilde{c}_{s}}{ds^{2}}=-(p^{2}+1)(b^{2}-1)(bp-s)^{-3}<0.

Thus the function
\tilde{c}_{s}
is a strictly concave function on
(-\infty,bp)
, and
s=\xi
is the unique critical point in this interval and so it is the global maxima.
Let
f(s)=\tilde{c}_{s}-s
. Then the above derivative formula shows that
f^{\prime}(s)<0
and hence
f
is strictly decreasing. In particular,
f(s)>f(\xi)=0
for all
s<\xi
and the characterization about
\xi
are immediate. Since
c_{0}=\tilde{c}_{q}
, we have
q\leq c_{0}
if and only if
f(q)\geq 0
, which is equivalent to
\xi\geq q
(with equality holds in one if equality holds in other).
∎

We end the section by a comparison theorem for the family of solutions
\tilde{\psi}_{s}
. This will be needed in the following sections on convergence of the cotangent flow.

Lemma 4.7
.

For all
\xi\leq s_{1}<s_{2}<pb
, and all
x\in[1,b]
,

\tilde{\psi}_{s_{2}}(x)\geq\tilde{\psi}_{s_{1}}(x)

with equality if and only if
x=b
.

Proof.

Let
s\in[\xi,bp)
, then we have
s\geq\tilde{c}_{s}
and so the solution
\tilde{\psi}_{s}
is given by
(
4.6
)
. Suppose
x\in(1,b)
. Now differentiating
\tilde{\psi}_{s}
with respect to
s
we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d\tilde{\psi}_{s}}{ds}=\frac{d\tilde{c}_{s}}{ds}\left(x-\frac{bp-\tilde{c}_{s}x^{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{A}_{s}+(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})x^{2}}}\right).

From Lemma
4.4
we first observe that
bp-\tilde{c}_{s}x^{2}>0
for all

and for all
s<bp
. To see this note that if
\tilde{c}_{s}\leq 0
, then
bp-\tilde{c}_{s}x^{2}\geq bp-\tilde{c}_{s}>0
. On the other hand if
\tilde{c}_{s}>0
, then

bp-\tilde{c}_{s}x^{2}\geq bp-\tilde{c}_{s}b^{2}=b^{2}\left(\frac{p}{b}-\tilde{c}_{s}\right)>0.

Next we show that
x\in(1,b)
, we have

g(x):=x-\frac{bp-\tilde{c}_{s}x^{2}}{\sqrt{\tilde{A}_{s}+(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})x^{2}}}<0.

Suppose there exists some

\displaystyle x^{2}\Big(\tilde{A}_{s}+(1+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2})x^{2}\Big)\geq b^{2}p^{2}-2bp\tilde{c}_{s}x^{2}+\tilde{c}_{s}^{2}x^{4}.

Since

, it is easy to see that the above inequality gives

, which is a contradiction. Hence
\frac{d\tilde{\psi}_{s}}{ds}>0
for all

∎

4.3.
Comparison and monotonicity along the flow

For the rest of this section, we assume that we are not in the stable case. That is,
q\leq c_{0}
. Let
\chi_{\varphi_{t}}=\sqrt{-1}\partial\overline{\partial}v(\rho,t)
be a path of real
(1,1)
forms in
\alpha
, and let
\psi:[1,b]\times[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be given by

\psi(u^{\prime}(\rho),t)=v^{\prime}(\rho,t),

where the prime (at least for the time being) denotes the
\rho
-derivative. Changing variables
x=u^{\prime}(\rho)
and letting
Q(x):=u^{\prime\prime}(u^{\prime-1}(x))
,
\varphi_{t}
satisfies the cotangent flow if and only if
\psi
satisfies the following equation:

(4.9)

\begin{cases}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=Q(x)\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\cot\theta_{t}\\
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x),\end{cases}

for some initial data
\psi_{0}
, where

\theta_{t}(x):=\theta(x,t):=\mathrm{arccot}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}(x,t)+\mathrm{arccot}\frac{\psi(x,t)}{x}.

For convenience, we now change our notation and use primes will denote the
x
-derivative. Note that
Q(x)\geq 0
with equality if and only if
x\in\{1,b\}.
Expanding out the right hand side, we obtain

(4.10)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=\frac{Q(x)\csc^{2}(\theta)}{(x^{2}+\psi^{2})(1+(\psi^{\prime})^{2})}\Big((x^{2}+\psi^{2})\psi^{\prime\prime}+(1+(\psi^{\prime})^{2})(x\psi^{\prime}-\psi)\Big),

and the equation is easily seen to be strictly parabolic away from the boundary points
\{1,b\}
. We choose our initial data to be
admissible
, that is it satisfies

\psi_{0}^{\prime}+\frac{\psi_{0}}{x}>0,

and the boundary conditions
\psi_{0}(1)=q
and
\psi_{0}(b)=p
.

We first prove our main convergence result for a very special choice of initial metric
\chi_{0}
such that
\Lambda_{\omega}\chi_{0}
is a positive constant, that is
\psi_{0}:[1,b]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
satisfies

\begin{cases}\psi_{0}^{\prime}(x)+\frac{\psi_{0}(x)}{x}=2\mu>0,\\
\psi_{0}(1)=q,~\psi_{0}(b)=p.\end{cases}

We then must have

(4.11)

\psi_{0}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{x}+\mu x,

where

(4.12)

\mu=\frac{bp-q}{b^{2}-1},~~~\lambda=\frac{b(bq-p)}{b^{2}-1}.

Our first observation is that the graph of the initial function is below that of
\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}
, and that the flow is increasing at
t=0
.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
q\leq c_{0}
. For all
x\in[1,b]
we have

(1)

\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}(x)\geq\psi_{0}(x)
with equality if and only if
x=b
.

(2)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}\Big|_{t=0}\geq 0
.

Proof.

(1)

Let’s assume
x\in[1,b)
since at
x=b
we have
\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}(b)=\psi_{0}(b)=p
. For any
s\in\mathbb{R}
define a function
\psi^{(s)}:[1,b]\to\mathbb{R}
by

\psi^{(s)}(x)=\frac{\lambda^{(s)}}{x}+\mu^{(s)}x,

where

\mu^{(s)}=\frac{bp-s}{b^{2}-1},~~\lambda^{(s)}=\frac{b(bs-p)}{b^{2}-1}.

When
s=\xi
,

\displaystyle\mu^{(\xi)}-\xi

\displaystyle=\frac{bp-\xi}{b^{2}-1}-\xi=\frac{b(p-b\xi)}{b^{2}-1}>0,

\displaystyle\lambda^{(\xi)}

\displaystyle=\frac{b(b\xi-p)}{b^{2}-1}=-(\mu^{(\xi)}-\xi).

Note that we can re-write
\psi^{(s)}
as follows:

\displaystyle\psi^{(s)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{b(bs-p)}{(b^{2}-1)x}+\frac{(bp-s)x}{b^{2}-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{s}{(b^{2}-1)x}(b^{2}-x^{2})+\frac{bp}{(b^{2}-1)x}(x^{2}-1).

Since
x\in[1,b)
, the above expression implies
\psi^{(s)}(x)<\psi^{(\xi)}(x)
for all
x\in[1,b)
when
s<\xi
.

Now we show that
\psi^{(\xi)}(x)<\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}(x)
for all
x\in[1,b)
. We compute

\displaystyle\psi^{(\xi)}(x)-\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}(x)

\displaystyle=(\mu^{(\xi)}-\xi)x+\frac{\lambda^{(\xi)}}{x}-\sqrt{(1+\xi^{2})(x^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\mu^{(\xi)}-\xi)(x^{2}-1)}{x}-\sqrt{(1+\xi^{2})(x^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{b(p-b\xi)(x^{2}-1)}{(b^{2}-1)x}-\sqrt{(1+\xi^{2})(x^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{b(x^{2}-1)\sqrt{1+\xi^{2}}}{x\sqrt{b^{2}-1}}-\sqrt{(1+\xi^{2})(x^{2}-1)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{(1+\xi^{2})(x^{2}-1)}}{x\sqrt{b^{2}-1}}\Big(b\sqrt{x^{2}-1}-x\sqrt{b^{2}-1}\Big)<0,

since
x\in[1,b)
. In second line of the above computation, we used
\lambda^{(\xi)}=-(\mu^{(\xi)}-\xi)
. In the fourth line, we used
(p-b\xi)^{2}=(1+\xi^{2})(b^{2}-1)
which is the equation
\xi
satisfies. To complete the proof we note that
\psi^{(s)}\equiv\psi_{0}
when
s=q
, and that
\xi\geq q
.

(2)

At
t=0
,
\theta_{0}(x)=\mathrm{arccot}\psi_{0}^{\prime}+\mathrm{arccot}\frac{\psi_{0}}{x}
and then

\displaystyle\cot\theta_{0}=\frac{\psi_{0}^{\prime}\cdot\frac{\psi_{0}}{x}-1}{\psi_{0}^{\prime}+\frac{\psi_{0}}{x}}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\left(\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}}+\mu\right)\left(-\frac{\lambda}{x^{2}}+\mu\right)-1\right)=\frac{1}{2\mu}\left(\mu^{2}-1-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{x^{4}}\right).

So differentiating with respect to
x
we obtain

(\cot\theta_{0})^{\prime}(x,0)=\frac{2\lambda^{2}}{\mu x^{5}}\geq 0,

and hence

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(x,0)\geq 0.

∎

Next, we prove that the two properties above propagate along the flow using the maximum principle.

Lemma 4.9
.

Let
q\leq c_{0}
. For all
t\in[0,\infty)
and
x\in[1,b]
,

\psi(x,t)\leq\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}(x).

Proof.

In view of Lemma
4.7
,
it is enough to show that
\psi(x,t)\leq\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x)
for all
s>\xi
. We let

H_{s}(x,t):=\psi(x,t)-\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x).

The evolution equation for
H_{s}
is given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H_{s}}{\partial t}=\frac{Q\csc^{2}(\theta)}{(x^{2}+\psi^{2})(1+\psi^{\prime 2})}\Bigg((x^{2}+\psi^{2})H^{\prime\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle+(1+\psi^{\prime 2})(xH^{\prime}_{s}-H_{s})+(H_{s}^{2}+2H_{s}\tilde{\psi}_{s})\tilde{\psi}^{\prime\prime}_{s}

\displaystyle+\big((H^{\prime}_{s})^{2}+2H^{\prime}_{s}\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{s}\big)(x\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{s}-\tilde{\psi}_{s})\Bigg),

where we used the fact that
\tilde{\psi}_{s}
satisfies the following second order ODE:

(4.13)

(x^{2}+\tilde{\psi}_{s}^{2})\tilde{\psi}^{\prime\prime}_{s}(x)+(1+(\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{s})^{2})(x\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{s}-\tilde{\psi}_{s})=0,

on
(1,b]\times[0,\infty)
. At the spatial and time boundaries we observe that,

H_{s}(x,0)<0,~\forall x\in(1,b),~H_{s}(1,t)<0,~\forall t\in[0,\infty),~H_{s}(b,t)=0,~\forall t\in[0,\infty),

where the inequalities follows from Lemma
4.8
and Lemma
4.7
.

Arguing by contradiction, suppose there exists a
T>0
such that

H^{*}:=\underset{[1,b]\times[0,T]}{\sup}H_{s}(x,t)>0.

Note that

a(x,t):=\frac{Q\csc^{2}\theta}{(x^{2}+\psi^{2})(1+\psi^{\prime 2})}

is uniformly bounded above as the angle
\theta(x,t)
lies in a compact set of
(0,\pi)
and
Q=u^{\prime\prime}\circ u^{\prime-1}
.
We also have that
||\tilde{\psi}_{s}||_{C^{2}[1,b]}
is finite for
s>\xi
(Note that this fails at
s=\xi
). We define a constant
A=A(T)>0
by

A:=1+\lVert\tilde{\psi}_{s}\rVert_{C^{2}[1,b]}+\underset{[1,b]\times[0,T]}{\sup}a(x,t).

We let
B=1+A^{2}H^{*}+2A^{3}
and consider the function

F(x,t):=e^{-Bt}H_{s}(x,t).

First we see that

F^{*}:=F(x^{*},t^{*})=\underset{[1,b]\times[0,T]}{\sup}F(x,t)>0

for some
(x^{*},t^{*})\in[1,b]\times[0,T]
. Next, the spacial and time boundary conditions implies that
x^{*}\in(1,b)
and
t^{*}\neq 0
. It implies that

\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}(x^{*},t^{*})\geq 0,~~F^{\prime}(x^{*},t^{*})=0,~~F^{\prime\prime}(x^{*},t^{*})\leq 0.

Then by the maximum principle,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}(x^{*},t^{*})

\displaystyle=-BF(x^{*},t^{*})+a(x^{*},t^{*})\left((x^{2}+\psi^{2})F^{\prime\prime}+(1+\psi^{\prime 2})(xF^{\prime}-F)+(FH_{s}+2F\tilde{\psi}_{s})\tilde{\psi}^{\prime\prime}_{s}\right)(x^{*},t^{*})

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+a(x^{*},t^{*})(F^{\prime 2}H^{\prime}+2F^{\prime}\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{s})(x\tilde{\psi}^{\prime}_{s}-\tilde{\psi}_{s})(x^{*},t^{*})

\displaystyle\leq-BF^{*}+a(x^{*},t^{*})\Big(H^{*}+2\tilde{\psi}_{s}(x^{*})\Big)\tilde{\psi}^{\prime\prime}_{s}(x^{*})F^{*}

\displaystyle\leq-BF^{*}+(A^{2}H^{*}+2A^{3})F^{*}

\displaystyle=-F^{*},

which is a contradiction. Hence, we must have

\underset{[1,b]\times[0,\infty)}{\sup}H_{s}(x,t)\leq 0,

and so we get the desired result.
∎

Next, we prove that the two properties above propagate along the flow using the maximum principle.

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
q\leq c_{0}
. For all
t\in[0,\infty)
and all
x\in[1,b]
,

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(x,t)\geq 0.

Proof.

At the boundary points, since
Q(1)=Q(b)=0
, we have

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(1,t)=\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}(b,t)=0

for all
t
. The evolution equation for
\psi
is given by

(4.14)

\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial t}=Q(x)\csc^{2}\left(\mathrm{arccot}\frac{\psi}{x}+\mathrm{arccot}\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}\right)\left(\frac{\frac{\partial^{2}\psi}{\partial x^{2}}}{1+(\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x})^{2}}+\frac{\frac{1}{x}{\frac{\partial\psi}{\partial x}}-\frac{\psi}{x^{2}}}{1+(\frac{\psi}{x})^{2}}\right).

Taking one time derivative we see that
\dot{\psi}
satisfies the following equation:

\frac{\partial\dot{\psi}}{\partial t}=L(\dot{\psi})+a\dot{\psi},

where
L(f):={\bf A}(x,t)f^{\prime\prime}+{\bf B}(x,t)f^{\prime}
,

\begin{cases}\mathbf{A}(x,t)&:=\frac{Q(x)\csc^{2}(\theta)}{1+\psi^{\prime 2}},\\
\mathbf{B}(x,t)&:=Q(x)\csc^{2}(\theta)\Bigg(\frac{-2x\psi^{\prime\prime}}{(x\psi^{\prime}+\psi)(1+\psi^{\prime 2})}+\frac{2(\psi\psi^{\prime}-x)(x\psi^{\prime}-\psi)}{(x\psi^{\prime}+\psi)(1+\psi^{\prime 2})(x^{2}+\psi^{2})}+\frac{x}{x^{2}+\psi^{2}}\Bigg),\\
a(x,t)&:=Q(x)\csc^{2}(\theta)\Bigg(-\frac{4x\psi^{\prime}}{(x\psi^{\prime}+\psi)(x^{2}+\psi^{2})}+\frac{2x\psi^{\prime\prime}(\psi\psi^{\prime}-x)}{(x\psi^{\prime}+\psi)(1+\psi^{\prime 2})(x^{2}+\psi^{2})}+\frac{1}{x^{2}+\psi^{2}}\Bigg).\end{cases}

Arguing by contradiction, suppose there exists a
T>0
such that

\inf_{[1,b]\times[0,T]}\dot{\psi}(x,t)<0.

Let
C=C(T)>0
such that

C=1+\sup_{[1,b]\times[0,T]}|a(x,t)|.

Note that the supremum is finite since
\theta
is strictly between
(0,\pi)
and so
\csc^{2}\theta
is bounded above and below, and
x\psi^{\prime}+\psi
is bounded below. We now let

F(x,t)=e^{-Ct}\dot{\psi}(x,t),

and let
(x^{*},t^{*})\in[1,b]\times[0,T]
such that

F^{*}:=F(x^{*},t^{*}):=\inf_{[1,b]\times[0,T]}e^{-Ct}\dot{\psi}(x,t)<0.

Then by the boundary conditions,
x^{*}\in(1,b)
and
t^{*}\neq 0
. By the maximum principle,

\displaystyle 0\geq\frac{\partial F}{\partial t}(x^{*},t^{*})

\displaystyle=-CF^{*}+L(F)(x^{*},t^{*})+a(x^{*},t^{*})F^{*}

\displaystyle\geq(a(x^{*},t^{*})-C)F^{*}.

Now
a(x^{*},t^{*})-C\leq-1
and
F^{*}<0
and so we have a contradiction.
∎

4.4.
Proof of Theorem

We are now in a position to prove Theorem

. As in Section
3

.

Proposition 4.11
.

Let
q\leq c_{0}
, and let

)
with
\psi(x,0)=\psi_{0}(x)

\psi_{\infty}(x)\leq\tilde{\psi}_{\xi}(x)

\psi_{\infty}(b-)=p

The proof now has several steps.

•

Let
\theta(x,t)
be as before. By the choice of our initial condition, and since condition
(B)

for all

Lemma A.4

For positive integers

\sum_{j=1}^{s+1}(-1)^{j-1}{s+1\choose j}{s+q-j\choose q-j}={q+s\choose q}.

Proof.

The right hand side is simply the number of unordered integer solutions to

x_{0}+x_{1}+\cdots+x_{s}=q,~x_{i}\geq 0~\forall i.

We now count this in a different way. Let

E_{i}=\{(x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{s})~|~x_{0}+x_{1}+\cdots+x_{s}=q,~x_{i}\geq 1\}.

Since

For any

and hence equals
