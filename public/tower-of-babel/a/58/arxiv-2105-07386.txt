Assessing the G 0 ​ W 0 ​ Γ 0 ( 1 ) G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\, approach: Beyond G 0 ​ W 0 G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}} with Hedin’s full second-order self-energy contribution
Yanyong Wang, Patrick Rinke, Xinguo Ren

Abstract
We present a self-energy approach for quasiparticle energy calculations that goes beyond Hedin’s G ​ W GW approximation by adding the full second-order self-energy (FSOS- W W ) contribution. The FSOS- W W diagram involves two screened Coulomb interaction ( W W ) lines and adding the FSOS- W W to the G ​ W GW self-energy can be interpreted as first-order vertex correction to G ​ W GW ( G ​ W ​ Γ ( 1 ) GW\Gamma^{(1)} ). Our FSOS- W W implementation is based on the resolution-of-identity technique and exhibits better than O ⁡ ( N 5 ) O(N^{5}) scaling with system size for small to medium-sized molecules. We then present one-shot G ​ W ​ Γ ( 1 ) GW\Gamma^{(1)} ( G 0 ​ W 0 ​ Γ 0 ( 1 ) G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\, ) benchmarks for the G ​ W GW 100 test set and a set of 24 acceptor molecules. For semilocal or hybrid density functional theory starting points, G 0 ​ W 0 ​ Γ 0 ( 1 ) G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\, systematically outperforms G 0 ​ W 0 G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}} for the first vertical ionization potentials (vIPs) and electron affinities (vEAs) of both test sets. Finally, we demonstrate that a static FSOS- W W self-energy significantly underestimates the quasiparticle energies.



I
Introduction

The one-electron properties of interacting many-electron systems can be conveniently described
by the Green’s function – also called the electron propagator – of the system. In particular, the one-electron excitation energies, i.e., the energy change due to addition or removal of one electron, are given rigorously by the poles of the interacting Green’s function
G

Fetter and Walecka 1971
;
Martin et al. 2016
. Through Dyson’s equation, the target interacting Green’s function can be linked to an easily computable non-interacting reference Green’s function
G_{0}
via the self-energy
\Sigma
. Thus, the question of calculating the interacting Green’s function
G
of a many-electron system boils down to finding computationally affordable yet sufficiently accurate approximations for the self-energy
\Sigma

In Hedin’s formulation of the interacting many-body problem
Hedin 1965
, he casts the many-body Schrödinger’s equation into a set of five inter-dependent differential-integral equations. Hedin’s equations offer an elegant framework for deriving and computing self-energy approximations. The most prominent example is the
GW
approximation to the self-energy
Hedin 1965
, in which
\Sigma
is given by the product of
G
and the screened Coulomb interaction
W
.
GW
emerges naturally from Hedin’s equations as first-order approximation for the electron self-energy. Since its first applications to real materials in the 1980s
Hybertsen and Louie 1986
;
Godby et al. 1986

GW
approximation has become the method of choice for band structure calculations of solids

More recently, it has also gained attention in chemistry and applications to molecules and clusters are on the rise

;

In this article, we present a new formulation and implementation of a second-order screened vertex. Our work is motivated by the
GW
plus second-order screened-exchange (SOSEX) approach some of the present authors proposed a few years ago
Ren et al. 2015
. In
GW
+SOSEX, the SOSEX self-energy is added to the
GW

Grüneis et al. 2009
;
Jansen et al. 2010
;
Paier et al. 2012
;

1

1
(b), respectively.
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}
+SOSEX outperforms
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}
for the absolute energy positions of both the highest occupied MO (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied MO (LUMO), as well as the energy ordering of molecular states.

While the SOSEX self-energy can be cast into a vertex correction, this vertex cannot be derived from Hedin’s equations due to the presence of both the bare and the screened Coulomb interaction in the same diagrams. Instead, the expansion of the electron self-energy in Hedin’s equations in powers of
W
yields at second order diagram that is similar to the direct SOSEX term, but now contains only screened Coulomb interaction lines, as shown in Fig.
1

1

W

W
in the present work. Adding the FSOS-
W
correction to the
GW

\Gamma

, henceforth denoted
\Gamma^{(1)}

GW

\Gamma
notation for vertex corrections is overused and imprecise. Our
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
approach, for example, is completely different from what is called
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}

\Gamma_{0}
in Ref.
Ma et al. 2019a

that is included in both

\Sigma

In the past, the
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
self-energy scheme has been implemented in the linearized augmented plane-wave (LAPW) framework and applied to simple solids

;
Kutepov 2017
It was found that vertex corrections in both

and
\Sigma

W(\omega)

approach has also been investigated for the three-dimensional and two-dimensional electron gases, as well as mono-layer and bilayer graphene
Uimonen et al. 2015
;
Stefanucci et al. 2014
;
Pavlyukh et al. 2020b
. There it was found that the spectral function is not guaranteed to be positive. A positive definite variant was then proposed
Uimonen et al. 2015
;
Stefanucci et al. 2014
;
Pavlyukh et al. 2020b

Finally, we note that a correlation energy contribution
corresponding to FSOS-
W
has been employed as an addition to the RPA correlation energy and applied to
molecules
Eshuis et al. 2012

GW\Gamma^{(1)}
scheme can be defined,
we focus in this work on the one-shot
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
scheme and check its performance for molecular systems,

The obtained results will be compared to those obtained using the
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}

II
Methodology

II.1
Theoretical formulation

Within the Hedin’s formulation, the self-energy of an interacting many-electron system is given by

\displaystyle\Sigma(1,2)

\displaystyle=i\int d3d4\,G(1,3)W(4,1)\Gamma(3,2,4),

(1)

where numerals denote composite space, spin and time variables, e.g.,
1=(\mathbf{r}_{1},\,\sigma_{1},\,t_{1})
. In
Eq.
1
the vertex function
\Gamma
encodes all the many-body complexity that
goes beyond the
GW
approximation. Consequently, through Eq.
1
,
any approximation for
\Gamma
translates into a corresponding approximation for the self-energy.

The 3-point vertex function is formally given by

\displaystyle\Gamma(1,2,3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\delta(1,2)\delta(1,3)+

(2)

\displaystyle\int d6d7\frac{\delta\Sigma(1,2)}{\delta G(4,5)}G(4,6)G(7,5)\Gamma(6,7,3)\,.

Starting with the identity approximation for the vertex,
\Gamma(6,7,3)\approx\delta(1,2)\delta(1,3)
, produces the
GW
approximation. At the next iteration, we need to evaluate the functional derivative of the
GW
self-energy

\frac{\delta\Sigma(1,2)}{\delta G(4,5)}=iW(1,2)\delta(1,4)\delta(2,5)+G(1,2)\frac{\delta W(1,2)}{\delta G(4,5)}.

(3)

Inserting this functional derivative into the vertex function in Eq.
2
gives

\Gamma(1,2,3)=\delta(1,2)\delta(1,3)+iW(1,2)G(1,3)G(3,2)+\ldots

(4)

where we omitted terms of order
O(W^{2})
and higher. At first order in
W
the vertex is thus

\Gamma^{(1)}(1,2,3)=\delta(1,2)\delta(1,3)+iW(1,2)G(1,3)G(3,2).\qquad\,

(5)

and inserting it into the self-energy expression in Eq.
1
we obtain the
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
self-energy

\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize$GW\Gamma^{(1)}$\,}}(1,2)=\Sigma^{GW}(1,2)+\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize FSOS-}W}(1,2)\,.

(6)

\Sigma^{GW}
is the
GW
self-energy, and

\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize FSOS-}W}(1,2)=-\int d3d4\,G(1,3)W(4,1)W(3,2)G(3,4)G(4,2)

(7)

is the FSOS-
W
self-energy. It should be emphasized that a perturbative expansion of the self-energy in terms of
W
produces only
one diagram at first order (
GW
), and one at the 2nd order (FSOS-
W
), but six at the 3nd order. The self-energy diagrams up to the
first three orders were already been
presented in the seminal
GW
paper by Hedin in 1965
Hedin 1965
, and included in Appendix
A

of the present work for completeness.

Equation
7
is
de facto
the mathematical expression behind the diagrammatic
representation of Fig.
1
(c). In contrast, as discussed in Ref.
Ren et al. 2015
,
the mathematical expression for the SOSEX self-energy, corresponding to Fig.
1
(b), is given by

\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize SOSEX}}(1,2)=-\int\text{d}3\text{d}4\,G(1,3)v(4,1)W(3,2)G(3,4)G(4,2).

(8)

As mentioned in the introduction, we are only interested in one-shot
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
, i.e., in using only the noninteracting reference Green’s function
G_{0}
and the corresponding screened Coulomb interaction
W_{0}
without iterating eq.
7
to self-consistency via Dyson’s equation. The FSOS-
W
self-energy of Eq.
7
calculated using
G_{0}
and
W_{0}
will be denoted FSOS-
W_{0}
.

Starting from the reference Kohn-Sham density functional theory (KS-DFT) or Hartree-Fock (HF) orbitals
\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r})

and orbital energies
\varepsilon_{n}
, the non-interacting Green’s function on the imaginary frequency axis is given by

G_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};i\omega)=\sum_{n}\frac{\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r})\psi^{*}_{n}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{i\omega+\varepsilon_{\text{F}}-\varepsilon_{n}},

(9)

where
\varepsilon_{\text{F}}
is the Fermi level. The screened Coulomb interaction
W_{0}
can be conveniently represented
within the products of the single-particle molecular orbitals (MOs), resulting in the two-electron screened Coulomb repulsion integrals,

\displaystyle\langle pq|W_{0}(i\omega)|rs\rangle

\displaystyle\kern 5.0pt=\int\text{d}\mathbf{r}\text{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\psi^{*}_{p}(\mathbf{r})\psi_{r}(\mathbf{r})W_{0}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r}^{\prime};i\omega)\psi^{*}_{q}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\psi_{s}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}).

(10)

where
p,q,r,s
denote arbitrary (both occupied and virtual) MOs.
Using Eqs.
7
,
9
and
10
, one
can readily obtain the diagonal elements of the FSOS-
W_{0}
self-energy along the imaginary frequency axis,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize FSOS-}W_{0}}_{n}(i\omega)=\int d{\bf r}d{\bf r^{\prime}}\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r})\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize FSOS-}W_{0}}(\mathbf{r},\mathbf{r^{\prime}};i\omega)\psi_{n}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle=\sum_{p,q,r}\int\frac{\text{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}\frac{(f_{p}-f_{q})\langle nq|W_{0}(i\omega^{\prime})|rp\rangle\langle rp|W_{0}(i\omega+i\omega^{\prime})|qn\rangle}{(i\omega+i\omega^{\prime}+\varepsilon_{\text{F}}-\varepsilon_{r})(i\omega^{\prime}+\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q})},

(11)

where
f_{p}
and
f_{q}
are the occupation numbers of the states
p
and
q
, respectively.

II.2
Implementation

The computational expression of the FSOS-
W_{0}
self-energy given in Eq.
11
is implemented in the all-electron Fritz-Haber-Institute
ab initio
simulations (FHI-aims) package
Blum et al. 2009
;
Havu et al. 2009
;
Ren et al. 2012
. In FHI-aims, both numeric atom-centered orbitals (NAOs) and gaussian-type orbitals (GTOs) can be used as basis functions. The single-particle MOs
\{\psi_{p}(\mathbf{r})\}
are expanded in terms of a set of atomic orbital (AO) basis functions
\{\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\}
,

\psi_{p}(\mathbf{r})=\sum_{j}c_{pj}\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\,.\\

(12)

Similar to the
GW
and SOSEX implementation in FHI-aims
Ren et al. 2012
;
Ren et al. 2015
, the resolution-of-identity (RI) approximation with the Coulomb metric

Dunlap et al. 1979
;
Whitten 1973
;
Vahtras et al. 1993
is used to compute the two-electron screened Coulomb integrals given in
Eq.
10
. With the RI approximation, a set of auxiliary basis functions (ABFs)
\{P_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})\}
is introduced
to represent the pair products of AO basis functions,

\phi_{i}(\mathbf{r})\phi_{j}(\mathbf{r})\approx\sum_{\mu}C^{\mu}_{ij}P_{\mu}(\mathbf{r}),

(13)

where the expansion coefficients
\{C^{\mu}_{ij}\}
are determined using the Coulomb metric
Dunlap et al. 1979
;
Whitten 1973
;
Vahtras et al. 1993
;
Ren et al. 2012
in the present implementation.
Here and in the following, the Greek letters
\mu,\nu,\alpha,\beta
denote ABFs.
To evaluate the screened Coulomb integrals, we
need to further transform the 3-index expansion coefficients from the AO basis to MO orbitals, namely,

M^{\mu}_{pr}=\sum_{i,j}c^{*}_{pi}c_{rj}C^{\mu}_{ij}\,.

(14)

Now, using Eqs
10
and
12
-
14
, we obtain

\langle pq|W_{0}(i\omega)|rs\rangle=\sum_{\mu,\nu}M^{\mu}_{pr}W_{0,\mu\nu}(i\omega)M^{\nu}_{qs}\,,

(15)

where

W_{0,\mu\nu}(i\omega)=\int d{\bf r}d{\bf r^{\prime}}P_{\mu}({\bf r})W_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r^{\prime}},i\omega)P_{\nu}({\bf r^{\prime}})\,.

(16)

To obtain the matrix form of
W_{0}
within the set of ABFs, we invoke the geometrical series expansion of

W_{0}
in terms of
v
and the non-interacting response function
\chi_{0}(i\omega)
, i.e.,
W_{0}=V+V\chi_{0}(i\omega)V+\cdots
.
Introducing
\Pi(i\omega)=V^{1/2}\chi_{0}(i\omega)V^{1/2}
, it is straightforward to see that

W_{0}(i\omega)=V^{1/2}\left[1-\Pi(i\omega)\right]^{-1}V^{1/2}

(17)

where
V
,
\chi_{0}
and
\Pi
here, similar to
W_{0}
, are matrices within the auxiliary basis set. Here

V_{\mu\nu}=\int\text{d}\mathbf{r}\text{d}\mathbf{r}^{\prime}\,\frac{P_{\mu}(\mathbf{r})P_{\nu}(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|},

(18)

is the Coulomb matrix, and
V^{1/2}
is the square root of the
V
matrix.
To derive the computational expression for the
\Pi
matrix, one should be aware that the
\chi_{0}
matrix
is defined to represent the real-space expression of
\chi_{0}
,

\chi_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r^{\prime}},i\omega)=\sum_{\mu,\nu}P_{\mu}({\bf r})\chi_{0,\mu\nu}(i\omega)P_{\nu}({\bf r^{\prime}})\,.

(19)

which is given by,

\chi_{0}({\bf r},{\bf r^{\prime}},i\omega)=\sum_{m,n}\frac{(f_{m}-f_{n})\psi^{\ast}_{m}({\bf r})\psi_{n}({\bf r})\psi_{n}^{\ast}({\bf r^{\prime}})\psi_{m}^{\ast}({\bf r^{\prime}})}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}-i\omega}\,,

(20)

Using Eqs.
13
and
14
, one immediately arrives at,

\chi_{0,\mu\nu}(i\omega)=\sum_{m,n}\frac{(f_{m}-f_{n})M_{mn}^{\mu}M_{nm}^{\nu}}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}-i\omega}\,.

(21)

For convenience, one may further introduce another set of 3-index integrals by
multiplying
V^{1/2}
with
M
,

O^{\mu}_{pr}=\sum_{i,j}\sum_{\nu}V^{1/2}_{\mu\nu}M^{\nu}_{pr}\,,

(22)

and it follows that

\Pi_{\mu\nu}(i\omega)=\sum_{m,n}\frac{(f_{m}-f_{n})O_{mn}^{\mu}O_{nm}^{\nu}}{\epsilon_{m}-\epsilon_{n}-i\omega}\,.

(23)

Using Eqs.
15
,
17
and
22
, we eventually obtain

\langle pq|W_{0}(i\omega)|rs\rangle=\sum_{\mu,\nu}O^{\mu}_{pr}[\,1-\Pi(i\omega)\,]^{-1}_{\mu\nu}O^{\nu}_{qs}\,.

(24)

Finally, based on Eq.
11
and
24
,
the FSOS-
W_{0}
self-energy is expressed as,

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize FSOS-}W_{0}}_{n}(i\omega)=\sum_{p,q,r}\sum_{\mu,\nu}\sum_{\alpha,\beta}(f_{p}-f_{q})\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\frac{\text{d}\omega^{\prime}}{2\pi}

\displaystyle\frac{O^{\mu}_{nr}[\,1-\Pi(i\omega^{\prime})\,]^{-1}_{\mu\nu}O^{\nu}_{qp}O^{\alpha}_{rq}[\,1-\Pi(i\omega+i\omega^{\prime})\,]^{-1}_{\alpha\beta}O^{\beta}_{pn}}{(i\omega+i\omega^{\prime}+\varepsilon_{\text{F}}-\varepsilon_{r})(i\omega^{\prime}+\varepsilon_{p}-\varepsilon_{q})}\,.\quad\kern 5.0pt

The detailed algorithm for implementing Eq.

, including the loop structure and

is

In our present implementation, the

\displaystyle\Sigma^{G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,}(i\omega)

\displaystyle=\cfrac{a_{1}}{1+\cfrac{a_{2}(i\omega-i\omega_{1})}{1+\cfrac{a_{3}(i\omega-i\omega_{2})}{1+\cdots}}}

with
\{i\omega_{k},\Sigma^{G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,}(i\omega_{k}),\,k=1,\cdots,n\}

G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,

are determined by the recursive relation.
The analytic continuation for each of the diagonal self-energy element is done by substituting
i\omega
with

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize$G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,$}}(i\omega)\xrightarrow{\text{analy. continu.}}\kern 5.0pt\Sigma^{\text{\scriptsize$G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,$}}(\omega).

Finally, the

Scaling behavior

A straightforward implementation of Eq.
25
is computationally demanding, since the dynamically screened Coulomb interaction needs to be evaluated at two different frequency points. By carefully designing the loop structure and manipulating the different terms (see Appendix
B
), our implementation makes
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
calculations affordable and facilitates the application of
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
to interesting molecular systems with tens of atoms.

Here we demonstrate the scaling behavior of the computational cost (in terms of CPU hours) with respect to the system size, as well as the parallel efficiency with respect to the number of CPU cores. For this purpose, we choose the oligoacenes
(C
4n+2
H
4n+2
) from benzene (
n=1
) to hexacene (
n=6
) as test systems. The gaussian aug-cc-pVQZ

T. H. Dunning 1989
basis set is used, leading to 2686 one-electron basis functions for
n=6
.

In the left panel of Fig.
2
, the CPU time as a function of the total number of states
N
is shown. The three data sets correspond to the total timing, the timing for evaluating Eq.
25
, and that for calculating the screened Coulomb matrix, respectively. The solid or dashed lines are power-law fits. The number of molecular states
N
shown on the
x
-axis is the sum of occupied (
N_{\text{occ}}
) and unoccupied (
N_{\text{unocc}}
) states, and, in our calculations, equals the number of one-electron basis functions.

Figure
2
reveals that the overall scaling with system size is
O(N^{4.1})
and thus better than the formal scaling of O(
N^{5}
). The formal scaling of the
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
method can be inferred from the rate-determining step in the evaluation of the FSOS-
W_{0}
part of the self-energy in Eq.
25
. Specifically, as shown in the computational algorithm schematic in Fig.
9
in Appendix
B
, this step scales as

O(N_{n}NN_{\text{occ}}N_{\text{unocc}}N_{\text{aux}})
where
N_{\text{occ}}
and
N_{\text{unocc}}
are the numbers of occupied and unoccupied states,
N_{\text{aux}}
is the number of ABFs and
N_{n}
the number of quasiparticle states to be calculated. Since
N_{n}
,
N_{\text{occ}}
,
N_{\text{unocc}}
, and
N_{\text{aux}}
are all proportional to
N
, the formal scaling should be
O(N^{5})
.

The
O(N^{4.1})
scaling of our implementation arises from the constructing of
\Pi
matrix and the screened Coulomb interaction
W_{0}
(i.e., the matrix inversion operation) in the large auxiliary basis space involving two frequency variables. These steps are computationally heavy but with a lower scaling (
O(N^{4.0})
for
\Pi
and
O(N^{2.9})
for
(1-\Pi)^{-1}
). Due to the carefully designed loop structure, the final step of assembling the FSOS-
W_{0}
self-energy scales as
O(N^{4.3})
for the tested system sizes, as indicated by the red dashed line in the left panel of Fig.
2
.

We also parallelized our
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
implementation. To check the parallel efficiency, we take the hexacene molecule as a test system, and present in the right panel of Fig.
2
the execution time as a function of the number of CPU cores employed in the calculations.
As can be seen from the plot, our parallel implementation scales up to several hundreds of CPU cores. From 57 to 266 CPU cores, the wall-clock time is
reduced by a factor of 2.3, indicating a parallel efficiency of approximately
50\%
with the number of cores increasing by 5 times.
In brief, our implementation renders it feasible to perform
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
calculations for molecules containing a few tens of atoms
with moderate computational resources.

III
Results and Discussions

In this section, we examine the performance of
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
for quasiparticle energies, in particular the
HOMO and LUMO levels for selected molecular test sets. For this purpose, we take two molecular test sets:
the
GW
100 set
van Setten et al. 2015
and
a set of 24 organic acceptor molecules (termed Acceptor24 in the following)
Richard et al. 2016
;
Knight et al. 2016
. While
GW100
contains a diverse set of closed shell atoms and small molecules, Acceptor24 provides medium-sized molecules. In addition, we investigate the energy ordering of the benzene molecule and screening effects in the CO molecule. The reference results used here to benchmark
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
are taken from the literature, obtained either with the coupled-cluster method with singles, doubles, and perturbative triples [CCSD(T)] excitations
Katharina Krause and Klopper 2015
;
Richard et al. 2016
or the equation-of-motion coupled-cluster with single and double excitations [EOM-CCSD]
Lange and Berkelbach 2018
.

Similar to
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}
, the
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
method also depends on the reference state on which it is based. To obtain a more complete picture of the behavior of
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
, in this work we shall carry out benchmark
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
calculations with three different starting points, namely, PBE
Perdew et al. 1996a
, PBE0
Perdew et al. 1996b
;
Adamo and Barone 1999
and HF.

For
GW100
, the Gaussian basis set def2-TZVPP
Weigenda and Ahlrichsb 2005
is employed, to be consistent with the coupled-cluster calculations from which the reference results are obtained
Rangel et al. 2016
;
Lange and Berkelbach 2018
. For the Acceptor24 set, the FHI-aims-2009
Blum et al. 2009

tier
4 basis set is used, to be consistent with the choice made in Ref.
Knight et al. 2016
where the Acceptor24 test set was originally proposed. Previous benchmark calculations show that
tier
4 comes to within 0.1 eV of
the complete basis set limit
Ren et al. 2012
;
Ren et al. 2015
.

As mentioned above, the Pad
\acute{\text{e}}
approximant
Gunnarsson et al. 2010
;
Duchemin and Blase 2020
is for analytically continuing the self-energy from the imaginary frequency axis to the real one. Instead of fixing the number of Pad
\acute{\text{e}}
parameters empirically, we increase the number of Pad
\acute{\text{e}}
parameters as well as the number of frequency grid points to ensure that the quasiparticle energies are converged. We had to exclude a couple of systems from the reported results, because we could not obtain stable QP energies from the Pad
\acute{\text{e}}
-based analytical continuation.

III.1
Ionization potentials and electron affinities of the
GW
100 test set

GW
100 is
van Setten et al. 2015
a test set that contains 100 small closed-shell atoms and molecules, first assembled by van Setten
et al.
van Setten et al. 2015
.
GW

adiabatic

Maggio and Kresse 2017

Ma et al. 2019b

GW1
.

GW

In the present work, we chose to benchmark
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
against quantum chemistry methods (CCSD(T) or EOM-CCSD) and not against experiment. In addition, we fix the one-electron basis and do not extrapolate the results to the complete basis set (CBS) limit. This all-theory comparison has the advantage that it is internally consistent. To then compare our results to experiment would require CBS extrapolation and the addition of vibrational and zero-point motion effects.

Next we revisit a problem in the photoemission spectrum (PES) of benzene (C

IV
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we implemented the one-shot version of the
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
scheme (
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
) in an atomic-orbital basis set framework.
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
goes beyond
GW
by including the full second-order self-energy within Hedin’s formalism. The formal scaling of the
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
scheme is
O(N^{5})
, but we achieved an improved scaling of
O(N^{4.2})
for medium-sized molecules.

Benchmarks for the
GW
100 and Acceptor24 test sets reveal that
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
significantly outperforms
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}
for PBE and PBE0 starting points, whereas HF is not a good starting point for
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
. We further observe that the accuracy of
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}\Gamma^{(1)}_{0}\,
is similar to the previously developed
G_{\text{0}}W_{\text{0}}
+SOSEX approach despite their different diagrammatic structure. We also checked the influence of static screened Coulomb approximations, and found that they significantly underestimate the magnitude of the vertex corrections.

Compared to
GW
+SOSEX,
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
is a conserving approximation in the sense that its self-energy diagrams can be derived from an associated
\Psi
-diagram
Baym and Kadanoff 1961
;
Almbladh et al. 1999
;
Dahlen et al. 2006
. Thus a meaningful self-consistent
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
scheme can be defined, similar to the
GW
case. However, the behavior of such a self-consistent
GW\Gamma^{(1)}
scheme, in particular, how the non-positiveness of the spectral function observed in jellium
Uimonen et al. 2015
;
Stefanucci et al. 2014
is manifested in molecular systems, remains to be studied.

We will not go into the details of the algorithm, but only briefly explain the main considerations behind this particular loop structure, which we found by extensive testing of different choices. The key point is to make the frequency
\omega^{\prime}
the outermost variable of the loop structure, because
\omega^{\prime}
is present in all matrix multiplications. We therefore gain computational and memory efficiency. Within the
\omega^{\prime}
loop, we first prepare intermediate quantities like the
\Pi
matrices at two different frequencies before looping over the orbital index
p
. The orbital
p
is special compared to
i
and
a
, as it runs over both occupied and unoccupied states and couples to frequency
\omega
. A further reduction of the prefactor can be gained from a careful arrangement of the orbital indices
i
and
a
since the number of unoccupied states is typically much larger than that of the occupied ones.

Despite the algorithmic optimization, the evaluation of the FSOS-W
0
self-energy is still computationally expensive. To reduce computational load and memory storage, we need an efficient parallelization. The key part of our paralleization is the treatment of the 3-dimensional
O
array, which is distributed with Message Passing Interface (MPI) processes across the auxiliary basis index
\nu
, and needs to be collected when computing the
\Pi

9
, all the heavy computation steps are parallelized except for several unavoidable light product operations that will not affect the overall performance.
