A SYMMETRIC PRIMAL-DUAL ALGORITHMIC FRAMEWORK FOR SADDLE POINT PROBLEMS
Hongjin He, Kai Wang, Jintao Yu

Abstract
In this paper, we propose a new primal-dual algorithmic framework for a class of convex-concave saddle point problems frequently arising from image processing and machine learning. Our algorithmic framework updates the primal variable between the twice calculations of the dual variable, thereby appearing a symmetric iterative scheme, which is accordingly called the s ymmetric p r i mal- d ual a lgorithm (SPIDA). It is noteworthy that the subproblems of our SPIDA are equipped with Bregman proximal regularization terms, which make SPIDA versatile in the sense that it enjoys an algorithmic framework to understand the iterative schemes of some existing algorithms, such as the classical augmented Lagrangian method (ALM), linearized ALM, and Jacobian splitting algorithms for linearly constrained optimization problems. Besides, our algorithmic framework allows us to derive some customized versions so that SPIDA works as efficiently as possible for structured optimization problems. Theoretically, under some mild conditions, we prove the global convergence of SPIDA and estimate the linear convergence rate under a generalized error bound condition defined by Bregman distance. Finally, a series of numerical experiments on the basis pursuit, robust principal component analysis, and image restoration demonstrate that our SPIDA works well on synthetic and real-world datasets.

Introduction

Recently, saddle point problems have received considerable attention in the signal/image processing, machine learning, and optimization communities, e.g., see
[
10
,
11
,
19
,
53
,
63
]
, to name just a few.
In this paper, we are interested in the convex-concave saddle point problem with a bilinear coupling term, which takes the following form:

\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{y\in{\mathcal{Y}}}\left\{{\mathscr{L}}(x,y):=f(x)+\langle Ax,y\rangle-g(y)\right\},

(1)

where
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
are two closed nonempty convex sets, both
f(\cdot):{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
and
g(\cdot):{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]
are proper closed convex (possibly nonsmooth) functions,
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represents the standard inner product of vectors, and
A:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a bounded linear operator. It is interesting that (
1
) provides a unified framework for the treatment of convex composite optimization problem

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{f(x)+g^{*}(Ax)\right\}

and the canonical convex minimization problem with linear constraints (see Section
4
), where
g^{*}(\cdot)
is the Fenchel conjugate of function
g(\cdot)
.

To efficiently exploit the min-max structure of (
1
), a seminal work can be traced back to the Arrow-Hurwicz Primal-Dual (AHPD) method
[
1
]
, which updates the primal and dual variables in a sequential order by solving two optimization subproblems as follows:

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,y^{k}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{-g(y)+\langle Ax^{k+1},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

(2)

where
\mu
and
\gamma
are two positive proximal regularization parameters serving as step sizes for updating. In the literature, such a method is also reemphasized as primal-dual hybrid gradient (PDHG) method with fruitful applications in image processing
[
5
,
19
,
27
,
63
]
. Although some convergence properties have been established under additional conditions
[
19
,
30
,
46
]
, the most recent work
[
29
]
showed that the AHPD method with any constant step size is not necessarily convergent for solving generic convex-concave saddle point problems.
In 2011, Chambolle and Pock
[
10
]
judiciously introduced a first-order primal-dual algorithm by absorbing an extrapolation step for algorithmic acceleration. Note that such an algorithm is commonly denoted by PDHG in the optimization literature. Therefore, we also use PDHG to represent the first-order primal-dual algorithm
[
10
]
throughout this paper. For given the
k
-th iterate
(x^{k},y^{k})
, the iterative scheme of the PDHG
[
10
]
reads as

\displaystyle x^{k+1}=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,y^{k}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\},

\displaystyle\tilde{x}^{k+1}=x^{k+1}+\tau(x^{k+1}-x^{k}),

(3)

\displaystyle y^{k+1}=\arg\max_{y\in\mathcal{Y}}\left\{-g(y)+\langle A\tilde{x}^{k+1},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},

where
\tau\in[0,1]
is an extrapolation parameter and both
\mu>0
and
\gamma>0
are regularization parameters. In particular, they further proved some convergence properties for the special case
\tau=1
under the following requirement:

\|AA^{\top}\|<\mu\gamma.

(4)

It is notable that the PDHG not only requires weaker convergence-guaranteeing conditions, but also runs faster than the AHPD method (see
[
10
,
12
]
). In recent years, there are some papers contributed to further studies on the extrapolation step, e.g., see
[
8
,
13
,
31
,
33
,
58
]
. As aforementioned, both
\mu
and
\gamma
serve as step sizes for updating. From computational perspective, larger step sizes usually lead to faster convergence, which accordingly encourages researchers to relax condition (
4
), e.g., see
[
26
,
38
,
41
,
42
]
. When the
x
- and
y
-subproblems are not easily to be solved or the maximum eigenvalue of
AA^{\top}
cannot be easily evaluated in some cases, a better way is to solve the underlying subproblems in an inexact way or the employment of line search for avoiding the calculation of
\|AA^{\top}\|
, e.g., see
[
14
,
36
,
37
,
44
,
52
]
. In recent years, saddle point problems and primal-dual algorithms received much attention in the fields of machine learning, imaging science, and optimization. Here, we only refer the reader to
[
16
,
39
,
53
,
56
]
for recent surveys and references therein along this direction.

It is well-known that the saddle point problem (
1
) provides a powerful treatment for linearly constrained convex optimization problems (e.g., see Section
4
). In this application, the dual variable
y
in (
1
) serves as the so-called Lagrangian multiplier. However, as shown in the excellent overview on a phenomenon of slow convergence of optimization algorithms
[
35
]
(also see some comments
[
20
,
45
,
47
,
54
]
), critical multipliers are nonempty for optimal solutions with nonunique Lagrangian multipliers, which play a crucial negative role in numerical optimization yielding slow convergence of major primal-dual algorithms, including Newton and Newton-related methods, the Augmented Lagrangian Method (ALM), and the sequential quadratic programming method. Therefore, these surprising discoveries clearly demonstrate that the updating scheme of the dual variable (i.e., Lagrangian multiplier) is very important for algorithmic acceleration, which motivates us to develop some “dual stabilization techniques” for solving (
1
). Besides, most of saddle point problems arising from machine learning and image processing display unbalanced primal and dual subproblems in the sense that the dual problem is often easier than the primal one (e.g., see
[
10
,
11
]
and also Section
4
). As studied in the most recent work
[
32
,
43
]
, balancing the subproblems of the classical ALM is able to greatly speed up the convergence of solving linearly constrained optimization problems. Therefore, how to balance both subproblems and what will be produced by some balancing technique for (
1
) are also motivations of this paper.

Considering the different complexity of primal and dual subproblems, we in this paper employ the symmetric spirit to design a new primal-dual algorithmic framework for saddle point problem (
1
), where the primal variable is updated once between the twice calculations of the dual variable. To a certain extent, our algorithm is able to balance the computation of primal and dual subproblems, and the one more calculation of the dual variable can be regarded as some dual stabilization technique from numerical perspective.
Since the proposed algorithm appears a symmetric updating order on the dual variable, we call it symmetric primal-dual algorithm and denote it by SPIDA for simplicity.
A toy example shows that our SPIDA has a nice convergence behavior, while the AHPD fails to converge and the PDHG runs a little slower than our SPIDA for some proximal parameters.
Notice that each subproblem is equipped with a Bregman proximal term to make our algorithm versatile so that we can easily derive the iterative schemes of some classical first-order optimization methods, including the ALM and its linearized version for one-block linearly constrained convex optimization problems, and some Jacobian splitting algorithms for multi-block linearly constrained convex minimization problems.
Particularly, our algorithmic framework is of benefit for producing some customized variants for linearly constrained optimization problems. Theoretically, we prove that our SPIDA is globally convergent under standard conditions, while the linear convergence rate is also estimated under a generalized error bound condition defined by Bregman distance.
Finally, a series of numerical experiments on basis pursuit, robust principal component analysis (RPCA), and image restoration demonstrate that our SPIDA performs better than some state-of-the-art primal-dual algorithms in many cases.

The remainder of paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we recall some notations and definitions that will be used throughout this paper. In Section
3
, we first describe the details of our proposed SPIDA for (
1
). Then, we prove its global convergence and estimate its linear convergence rate. In Section
4
, we apply our SPIDA to solve the linearly constrained convex minimization problems, and show that some classical first-order optimization methods are special cases of the proposed SPIDA. In Section
5
, we conduct the numerical performance of our SPIDA on solving some structured optimization problems with synthetic and real datasets. Finally, we complete this paper with drawing some conclusions in Section
6
.

Definition 2.1

(ii)

(iii)

\varrho

Let

\partial f(x)=\left\{\;\xi\,|\,f(z)\geq f(x)+\left\langle z-x,\xi\right\rangle,\,\forall\,z\in\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(f)}\;\right\}.

In what follows, we denote
\mathrm{\bf dom}\,{(\partial f)}:=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\;|\;\partial f(x)\neq\emptyset\}

Let

3.1

The extra calculation of the dual variable accordingly balances the speed of both variables approaching to their optimal solutions, which can also be regarded as one stabilization on the dual variable. \nabla g(y)-Ax\end{array}\right).

Consequently, we can gainfully employ some efficient algorithms, such as the projection methods and extragradient methods (e.g.,
[
40
,
49
,
51
,
62
]
) tailored for VIPs to deal with those saddle point problems with differentiable objectives. However, many real-world problems do not necessarily satisfy the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla{\mathscr{L}}({\bm{u}})
required in convergence analysis. Besides, the aforementioned VIP-type methods such as the well-known extragradient methods (e.g.,
[
40
,
49
,
51
,
62
]
) treat both
x
and
y
as a single entity, thereby potentially ignoring the distinct properties associated with each individual variable. As a consequence, when implementing the aforementioned extragradient methods, we must update
x
and
y
simultaneously, thereby reducing the implementability of extragradient methods for (
1
). Comparatively, our Algorithm
1
does not require the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla{\mathscr{L}}({\bm{u}})
. More promisingly, our method is able to maximally exploit the structure of (
1
) so that each subproblem is easily implemented for many real-world problems (see Section
5
).

Remark 3.4
.

After we finished this manuscript, we were brought to the so-named Alternating Extragradient Method (AEM) introduced by Bonettini and Ruggiero
[
4
,
6
]
and the Primal-Dual Fixed Point algorithm (PDFP) proposed by Chen et al.
[
15
]
. Firstly, under the same differentiability requirement on
{\mathscr{L}}(x,y)
, the AEM is an improved variant of the classical extragradient methods
[
40
,
49
,
51
]
, where the respective structure associated with
x
and
y
can be efficiently explored. Moreover, the AEM enjoys an effective adaptive stepsize for primal and dual subproblems. However, the Lipschitz continuity assumption for the AEM would possibly preclude its applicability to some nonsmooth real-world problems. Comparatively, although our Algorithm
1
shares the similar symmetric spirit to update the primal and dual variables with the AEM, our Algorithm
1
is easily applicable to nonsmooth saddle point problems with a bilinear coupling term. Moreover, as a theoretical complement, we not only prove the global convergence of Algorithm
1
, but also estimate the linear convergence rate under the generalized error bound defined by Bregman distance. Secondly, when comparing with the PDFP in
[
15
]
, we can see that PDFP shares a highly similar iterative scheme with our SPIDA by computing the
y
-subproblem twice at each iteration. In particular, when the proximal term in the
x
-subproblem (
9
) of SPIDA is chosen as the
Euclidean norm (i.e.,
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,x^{k})=\frac{1}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}
), the iterative form for the
x
-subproblem coincides with the one of PDFP except the appearance of proximal parameter
\mu
. On the other hand, our SPIDA has versatile Bregman proximal terms making it flexible for many structured optimization problems. From this point, our SPIDA is more general than the PDFP.

{assumption}

associated with the

Theorem 3.10
.

Suppose that Assumption

y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, the following inequalities hold

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(x)-f(x^{k+1})+\left\langle x-x^{k+1},\;A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}+\mu\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\mu\nabla\psi(x^{k})\right\rangle\geq 0,\\
&g(y)-g(y^{k+1})+\left\langle y-y^{k+1},\;-Ax^{k+1}+\gamma\nabla\phi(y^{k+1})-\gamma\nabla\phi(y^{k})\right\rangle\geq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

Therefore, for all
x\in{\mathcal{X}}
and
y\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, the subsequence
\{(x^{k_{j}},\tilde{y}^{k_{j}},y^{k_{j}})\}
satisfies

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(x)-f(x^{k_{j}+1})+\left\langle x-x^{k_{j}+1},\;A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k_{j}+1}+\mu\nabla\psi(x^{k_{j}+1})-\mu\nabla\psi(x^{k_{j}})\right\rangle\geq 0,\\
&g(y)-g(y^{k_{j}+1})+\left\langle y-y^{k_{j}+1},\;-Ax^{k_{j}+1}+\gamma\nabla\phi(y^{k_{j}+1})-\gamma\nabla\phi(y^{k_{j}})\right\rangle\geq 0.\end{aligned}\right.

(40)

Consequently, taking limit as
j\to\infty
over the subsequence
{k_{j}}
in (
40
), it follows from (
39
) that

\left\{\begin{aligned} &f(x)-f(x^{\infty})+\left\langle x-x^{\infty},\;A^{\top}y^{\infty}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall x\in{\mathcal{X}},\\
&g(y)-g(y^{\infty})+\left\langle y-y^{\infty},\;-Ax^{\infty}\right\rangle\geq 0,\quad\forall y\in{\mathcal{Y}},\end{aligned}\right.

which, together with (
5
), means that the limit point
(x^{\infty},y^{\infty})
is a saddle point of
{\mathscr{L}}(x,y)
in (
1
). So
(x^{*},y^{*})
can be replaced by
(x^{\infty},y^{\infty})
in the the sequence
\{\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{*},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{*},x^{k})\}
. Thus
\{\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\infty},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\infty},x^{k})\}
is monotone decreasing and bounded, we obtain

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\left(\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\infty},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\infty},x^{k})\right)=\lim_{j\rightarrow\infty}\left(\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\infty},y^{k_{j}})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\infty},x^{k_{j}})\right)=0,

which means that
x^{k}\rightarrow x^{\infty}
and
y^{k}\rightarrow y^{\infty}
as
k\rightarrow\infty
. We complete the proof of this theorem.

3.3
Linear convergence

In this subsection, we turn our attention to establishing the linear convergence in the context of a generalized error bound conditions. First, we make the following assumption, which is an extended version used in the literature, e.g.,
[
37
,
38
,
58
,
61
]
.

{assumption}

Assume that, for any
\omega>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that

\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}({\bm{u}},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})\leq\eta\mathrm{\bf dist}(0,{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{u}},1)),\;\;\;\forall\|{\bm{u}}\|\leq\omega,\;{\bm{u}}\in{\mathcal{U}},

(41)

where
{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{u}},1)
is given by (
7
) with
t=1
. Let
\gamma>0
,
\mu>0
and
\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}({\bm{u}},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})
be defined by

\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}({\bm{u}},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})=\min\left\{\sqrt{\mathscr{B}({\bm{u}}^{\star},{\bm{u}})}\;\big|\;{\bm{u}}^{\star}\in{\mathcal{U}}^{\star}\right\}

(42)

with
\mathscr{B}({\bm{u}}^{\star},{\bm{u}}):=\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y)+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x)
. Moreover, we assume that the gradients of
\psi(\cdot)
and
\phi(\cdot)
are Lipschitz continuous with modulus
L_{\psi}
and
L_{\phi}
, respectively.

Hereafter, we establish the global linear convergence of Algorithm
1
under Assumption
3.3
in the context of a generalized error bound condition defined by Bregman distance. We begin our analysis with the following lemma.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
\{(x^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k+1})\}
be a sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(0,{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\|AA^{\top}\|+\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\right)\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\qquad+2\mu^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}+2\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}.

(43)

where
L_{\phi}
and
L_{\psi}
are the Lipschitz continuity constants of
\nabla\phi(\cdot)
and
\nabla\psi(\cdot)
, respectively.

Proof 3.15
.

Letting
\xi^{k+1}\in\partial f(x^{k+1})
, it then follows from the first-order optimality condition of
x
-subproblem (
9
) that

x^{k+1}=\Pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left[x^{k+1}-\left(\xi^{k+1}-A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}+\mu\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})\right)\right],

(44)

where
\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})=\langle\nabla\psi(x^{k+1})-\nabla\psi(x^{k}),x^{k+1}-x^{k}\rangle
. By using (
44
) and the nonexpansiveness of projection operator
\Pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}(\cdot)
, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(0,{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(x^{k+1},\Pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left[x^{k+1}-(\partial f(x^{k+1})+A^{\top}y^{k+1})\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left[x^{k+1}-\left(\xi^{k+1}+A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}+\mu\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})\right)\right]-\Pi_{{\mathcal{X}}}\left[x^{k+1}-(\xi^{k+1}+A^{\top}y^{k+1})\right]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|A^{\top}(y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\mu\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|AA^{\top}\|\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\right\|^{2}+2\mu^{2}\left\|\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{k+1},x^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\|AA^{\top}\|\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\right\|^{2}+2\mu^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2},

(45)

where the second inequality is derived by the fact that
\|a+b\|^{2}\leq 2\|a\|^{2}+2\|b\|^{2}
holds for all
a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
and the last inequality follows from the Lipschitz continuity of
\nabla\psi(\cdot)
.

Similarly, it follows from the first-order optimality condition of the
y
-subproblem (
10
) that

y^{k+1}=\Pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\left[y^{k+1}-\left(\zeta^{k+1}-Ax^{k+1}+\gamma\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right)\right].

Then, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(0,{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(y^{k+1},\Pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\left[y^{k+1}-\left(\partial g(y^{k+1})-Ax^{k+1}\right)\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\Pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\left[y^{k+1}-\left(\zeta^{k+1}-Ax^{k+1}+\gamma\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right)\right]-\Pi_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\left[y^{k+1}-(\zeta^{k+1}-Ax^{k+1})\right]\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}\left\|\nabla\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},y^{k})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|y^{k+1}-\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right\|^{2}+2\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}.

(46)

Consequently, combining (
3.15
) and (
3.15
) leads to

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(0,{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(0,{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{X}}}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)+\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}(0,{\mathcal{E}}_{{\mathcal{Y}}}\left({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq 2\left(\|AA^{\top}\|+\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\right)\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\|^{2}+2\mu^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}+2\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}.

Hence, the assertion of this lemma is obtained.

Now, we establish the linear convergence rate of Algorithm
1
by the following theorem.

Theorem 3.16
.

Let
\{(x^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1},{y}^{k+1})\}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm
1
. Suppose that Assumptions
3.2
and
3.3
hold. Then, we have

(1+\vartheta)\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})\leq\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star}),

where
\vartheta
is a positive constant given by

\vartheta=\min\left\{\;\frac{\varrho}{4\gamma\eta^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}},\;\frac{\gamma\alpha\varrho-1}{4\alpha\eta^{2}(\|AA^{\top}\|+\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2})},\;\frac{\kappa}{4\mu\eta^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}}\;\right\}.

Proof 3.17
.

It first follows from (
3.13
) that

\displaystyle\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k+1})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})

\displaystyle\quad-\left(\gamma-\frac{1}{\alpha\varrho}\right)\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\widehat{\beta}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}.

(47)

In accordance with Assumption
3.3
, i.e., inequality (
41
), it follows from (
3.14
) that

\displaystyle\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}\left({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{2}\mathrm{\bf dist}^{2}\left(0,{\mathcal{E}}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},1)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{2}\left[2\left(\|AA^{\top}\|+\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\right)\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\right\|^{2}+2\mu^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+2\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}\right].

(48)

Consequently, by the definition of
\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}({\bm{u}},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})
given by (
42
), we have

\displaystyle(1+\vartheta)\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})+\vartheta\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\left[\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k+1})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k+1})\right]+\vartheta\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k})-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k})-\left(\gamma-\frac{1}{\alpha\varrho}\right)\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1})-\widehat{\beta}\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad+\vartheta\eta^{2}\left[2\left(\|AA^{\top}\|+\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\right)\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\right\|^{2}+2\mu^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2}+2\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k})-\left[\frac{\gamma\varrho}{2}-2\vartheta\eta^{2}\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\right]\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\frac{\gamma\alpha\varrho-1}{2\alpha}-2\vartheta\eta^{2}\left(\|AA^{\top}\|+\gamma^{2}L_{\phi}^{2}\right)\right]\left\|\tilde{y}^{k+1}-y^{k+1}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\quad-\left[\frac{\widehat{\beta}\kappa}{2}-2\vartheta\eta^{2}\mu^{2}L_{\psi}^{2}\right]\left\|x^{k+1}-x^{k}\right\|^{2},

(49)

where the second inequality is derived by (
3.17
), and the third inequality follows from the strong convexity of
\mathscr{B}_{\phi}

and
\mathscr{B}_{\psi}
. As a consequence, by using the definition of
\vartheta
, inequality (
3.17
) implies that

(1+\vartheta)\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})\leq\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k})-\Gamma(x^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k+1}),

(50)

where
\Gamma(x^{k+1},\tilde{y}^{k+1},y^{k+1})
is a positive term composed by the last three terms of (
3.17
).
Then, setting
\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})=\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k})
in (
50
)
immediately arrives at

(1+\vartheta)\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k+1},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star})\leq\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y^{\star},y^{k})+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x^{\star},x^{k})=\mathrm{\bf dist}_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}({\bm{u}}^{k},{\mathcal{U}}^{\star}).

The assertion of this theorem is obtained.

4
Applications to Linearly Constrained Convex Minimization

In this section, we shall show that our Algorithm
1
will recover the iterative schemes of some classical first-order algorithms, when applying it to linearly constrained convex optimization problems.

4.1
One-block case: Augmented Lagrangian method

In this subsection, we consider the following one-block convex minimization problem with linear constraints:

\min_{x}\left\{\;f(x)\;|\;Ax=b,\;\;x\in{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\right\},

(51)

where
f(\cdot):{\mathcal{X}}\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is a proper closed convex function and
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
is a given matrix, and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a given vector.
Accordingly, its augmented Lagrangian function reads as

{\mathscr{L}}_{\gamma}(x,y)=f(x)+\langle Ax-b,y\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|Ax-b\|^{2},\quad\gamma>0,

and the augmented Lagrangian method for (
51
) is

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax-b,y^{k}\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|Ax-b\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

or equivalently,

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|Ax-b+\gamma y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

(52)

It is not difficult to observe that directly solving the
x
-subproblem in (
52
) is not an easy task (at least is not easily implementable), when
f(x)
is a nonsmooth function (e.g.,
\|x\|_{1}
or nuclear norm for matrices) and
A
is a general matrix, even for the case
{\mathcal{X}}={\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(e.g., see
[
60
]
). Therefore, a natural way to make (
52
) implementable is linearizing the quadratic penalty term at
x^{k}
as follows:

\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|Ax-b\|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\gamma}\|Ax^{k}-b\|^{2}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\langle A^{\top}(Ax^{k}-b),x-x^{k}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2},

(53)

where
\mu\gamma\geq\|A^{\top}A\|
.
Consequently, using the above approximation (
53
) instead of quadratic penalty term in (
52
), we immediately obtain the so-called Linearized Augmented Lagrangian Method (LALM) for (
51
), i.e.,

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|x-\left(x^{k}+\frac{1}{\mu}A^{\top}\left(y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k}-b)\right)\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

(54)

When applying our Algorithm
1
to (
51
), we first reformulate (
51
) as the following saddle point problem:

\min_{x\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{{\mathscr{L}}(x,y):=f(x)+\langle Ax,y\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle\right\}.

(55)

Then, the specific iterative scheme of Algorithm
1
for (
55
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle Ax^{k},y\rangle-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\},\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}(x,x^{k})\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle Ax^{k+1},y\rangle-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(56)

Clearly, by setting the Bregman kernel functions as
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
and
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{A^{\top}A}
, the iterative scheme (
56
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle Ax^{k},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|Ax-Ax^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle Ax^{k+1},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

which, by using the first-order optimality conditions of both
y
-subproblems, can be immediately simplified as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k}-b),\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|Ax-\left(Ax^{k}-\frac{1}{\mu}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

It is trivial that substituting
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
into the update of
x^{k+1}
and setting
\mu=1/\gamma
immediately yields the ALM (
52
).

On the other hand, by setting the Bregman kernel functions as
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
and
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
, the iterative scheme (
56
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle Ax^{k},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\langle Ax,\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle Ax^{k+1},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

which, by using the first-order optimality conditions of both
y
-subproblems, can be immediately simplified as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k}-b),\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|x-\left(x^{k}-\frac{1}{\mu}A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(Ax^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

Clearly, plugging the formula of
\tilde{y}^{k+1}
into the update scheme of
x^{k+1}
immediately yields the LALM (
54
).

It is interesting to notice that our algorithmic framework allows us to take different Bregman kernel functions. Therefore, we here follow the novel idea of the newly introduced balanced augmented Lagrangian method
[
32
]
to further consider taking
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}_{(AA^{\top}+\epsilon I)}
and
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}
for model (
51
), where
I
stands for an identity matrix which always automatically matches the size for operations, and
I_{n}
specially denotes the
n
-dimensional identity matrix. Specifically, the concrete iterative scheme of Algorithm
1
reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(AA^{\top}+\epsilon I)^{-1}(Ax^{k}-b),\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|x-\left(x^{k}-\frac{1}{\mu}A^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}(AA^{\top}+\epsilon I)^{-1}(Ax^{k+1}-b).\end{aligned}\right.

(57)

It should be noted that, for the case where
n\ll m
, we can employ the well-known Sherman-Morrison-Woodbury theorem to reduce the computational cost of computing
(AA^{\top}+\epsilon I)^{-1}
, i.e.,
(AA^{\top}+\epsilon I)^{-1}=\epsilon^{-1}\left(I_{m}-A(\epsilon I_{n}+A^{\top}A)^{-1}A^{\top}\right)
. In what follows, we call the scheme (
57
) Doubly Balanced Augmented Lagrangian Method (DBALM). Moreover, when
f(x)
is a quadratic function, i.e.,
f(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|Bx-q\|^{2}
, we can also take
\psi(x)=\frac{1}{2}\|x\|^{2}_{(\tau I-B^{\top}B)}
to derive a linearized version of DBALM to deal with the case where
{\mathcal{X}}
is a simple convex set whose projection is easily calculated.

4.2
Multi-block case: Jacobian splitting method

In this part, we are concerned with the multi-block linearly constrained convex minimization, which takes the form

\min_{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{p}}\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(x_{i})\;\Big|\;\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}=b,\;\;x_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{i}},\;i=1,2,\ldots,p\right\},

(58)

where
f_{i}(\cdot):\mathcal{X}_{i}\rightarrow(-\infty,+\infty]
for
i=1,2,\cdots,p
are proper closed convex functions,
A_{i}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n_{i}}
are given matrices, and
b\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
is a given vector.
In the past decades, the multi-block model (
58
) has received much considerable attention due to its widespread applications in computer sciences and automatic control, e.g., see
[
23
,
55
]
. Although such a model is also a linearly constrained optimization problem, it cannot be easily solved via the aforementioned ALM (
52
) since the linear constraints make the ALM suffer from coupled subproblems so that the separability of the objective function cannot be fully exploited in algorithmic implementation. Accordingly, a series of augmented Lagrangian-based splitting methods were developed in the optimization literature, e.g., see
[
23
,
24
,
25
,
28
,
34
,
59
]
and references therein.

Below, we first show that our Algorithm
1
is applicable to solving (
58
). In particular, we can easily derive that our Algorithm
1
is indeed the fully Jacobian splitting algorithm
[
28
,
57
]
by choosing appropriate Bregman kernel functions. Moreover, we can obtain some new Jacobian splitting methods for (
58
).

First, it is clear that (
58
) can be rewritten into a compact form as follows:

\min_{{\bm{x}}}\left\{{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})\;|\;{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}=b,\;\;{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}\right\},

(59)

where
{\bm{x}}:=(x_{1}^{\top},x_{2}^{\top},\ldots,x_{p}^{\top})^{\top}
,
{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{p}f_{i}(x_{i})
,
{\bm{A}}:=[A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{p}]
, and
{\mathcal{X}}:={\mathcal{X}}_{1}\times{\mathcal{X}}_{2}\times\ldots\times{\mathcal{X}}_{p}
. Therefore, we can reformulate (
59
) as the form of (
55
), i.e.,

\min_{{\bm{x}}\in{\mathcal{X}}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{{\mathscr{L}}({\bm{x}},y):={\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})+\left\langle{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}},y\right\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle\right\}.

(60)

Then, the specific iterative scheme of Algorithm
1
for (
55
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}^{k},y\rangle-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\},\\
{\bm{x}}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{{\bm{x}}\in\mathcal{X}}\left\{{\bm{f}}({\bm{x}})+\langle{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}},\tilde{y}^{k+1}\rangle+\mu\mathscr{B}_{\psi}({\bm{x}},{\bm{x}}^{k})\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{-\langle b,y\rangle+\langle{\bm{A}}{\bm{x}}^{k+1},y\rangle-\gamma\mathscr{B}_{\phi}(y,y^{k})\right\}.\end{aligned}\right.

(61)

Now, we denote

M:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\beta_{1}A_{1}^{\top}A_{1}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\beta_{2}A_{2}^{\top}A_{2}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&\beta_{p}A_{p}^{\top}A_{p}\end{array}\right).

Then, by setting the Bregman kernel functions as
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
and
\psi({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2}_{M}
and using the separability of the objective function, for
\mu=1
, the iterative scheme (
61
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k}-b\right),\\
x_{i}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\left\{f_{i}(x_{i})+\frac{\beta_{i}}{2}\left\|Ax_{i}-\left(Ax_{i}^{k}-\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\;i=1,\ldots,p,\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k+1}-b\right),\end{aligned}\right.

(62)

which actually corresponds to the proximal Jacobian splitting method studied in
[
28
]
, especially precisely coincides with the augmented Lagrangian-based parallel splitting method
[
57
]
by setting
\beta_{i}=1/\gamma
for
i=1,2\cdots,p
.

More interestingly, by setting the Bregman kernel functions as
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
and
\psi({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2}
, the specific iterative scheme of Algorithm
1
for (
60
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k}-b\right),\\
x_{i}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\left\{f_{i}(x_{i})+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|x_{i}-\left(x_{i}^{k}-\frac{1}{\mu}A_{i}^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\;i=1,\ldots,p,\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k+1}-b\right),\end{aligned}\right.

(63)

which is a linearized parallel splitting method for (
58
). Comparing with the methods discussed in
[
21
,
23
,
25
]
, the variant (
63
) enjoys relatively simpler iterative scheme without correction steps. Combining the ideas of (
62
) and (
63
), we can take

\widehat{M}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccc}\beta_{1}A_{1}^{\top}A_{1}&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&\beta_{l}A_{l}^{\top}A_{l}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&\beta_{l+1}I&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&\cdots&0&0&\cdots&\beta_{p}I\end{array}\right)

so that
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}
and
\psi({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2}_{\widehat{M}}
, thereby producing a Partially Linearized Jacobian Splitting Method (PLJSM) for (
58
) with setting
\mu=1
, i.e.,

\left\{\begin{aligned} \tilde{y}^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k}-b\right),\\
x_{i}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x_{i}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{i}}\left\{f_{i}(x_{i})+\frac{\beta_{i}}{2}\left\|Ax_{i}-\left(Ax_{i}^{k}-\frac{1}{\beta_{i}}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\;i=1,\ldots,l,\\
x_{j}^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x_{j}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{j}}\left\{f_{j}(x_{j})+\frac{\beta_{j}}{2}\left\|x_{j}-\left(x_{j}^{k}-\frac{1}{\beta_{j}}A_{j}^{\top}\tilde{y}^{k+1}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\;j=l+1,\ldots,p,\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\frac{1}{\gamma}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}x_{i}^{k+1}-b\right),\end{aligned}\right.

which, to our best knowledge, is not discussed in the literature. Of course, we can also follow the spirit of the DBALM (
57
) to specify
\phi(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|y\|^{2}_{(\sum_{i=1}^{p}A_{i}A_{i}^{\top}+\epsilon I)}
and
\psi({\bm{x}})=\frac{1}{2}\|{\bm{x}}\|^{2}_{\widehat{M}}
to develop a doubly balanced PLJSM for (
58
).

5.1
Basis pursuit

As discussed in Section
4
, our Algorithm
1
(SPIDA) is applicable to dealing with linearly constrained optimization problem (
51
). In this part, we are interested in the basis pursuit problem, which can be expressed mathematically as an
\ell_{1}
-norm minimization problem with linear constraints, i.e.,

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\;\left\{\;\|x\|_{1}\;|\;Ax=b\;\right\},

(64)

where
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
is a sample matrix and
b\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
is a measurement vector. Such a model (
64
) is a fundamental problem in compressed sensing
[
18
]
and can be efficiently solved via a large number of optimization solvers. Here, we just employ this example to investigate the ability of our SPIDA on solving linearly constrained optimization problems, in addition to showing the superiority of SPIDA over some popular first-order optimization methods.

First, by the Lagrangian function, we reformulate (
64
) as the following min-max saddle point problem:

\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\max_{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}\left\{\;{\mathscr{L}}(x,y)=\|x\|_{1}+\langle Ax,y\rangle-\langle b,y\rangle\;\right\}.

We compare our SPIDA with PDHG (setting
\tau=1
in (
3
)) and GRPDA
[
13
]
. Most recently, He and Yuan
[
32
]
introduced a novel Balanced Augmented Lagrangian Method (BALM) for linearly convex programming, which is a great improvement of the classical ALM (
52
). The iterative scheme of BALM for (
64
) reads as

\left\{\begin{aligned} x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}\left\{f(x)+\frac{\gamma}{2}\left\|x-\left(x^{k}-\frac{1}{\gamma}A^{\top}y^{k}\right)\right\|^{2}\right\},\\
y^{k+1}&=y^{k}+\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}AA^{\top}+\epsilon I\right)^{-1}\left(A(2x^{k+1}-x^{k})-b\right).\end{aligned}\right.

(65)

In this part, we also compare our SPIDA with the BALM (
65
). Moreover, we will follow the idea of BALM to produce a doubly balanced augmented Lagrangian method (see (
57
) and denote it by SPIDA-II) via choosing the Type II Bregman kernel function in Table
1
. As discussed in Section
4.1
, when the Bregman kernel functions are specified as the Type I of Table
1
, our SPIDA reduces to the LALM (
54
), which will be denoted by SPIDA-I in our numerical comparison.

In the experiments, we first construct a randomly
s
-sparse vector
x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
s
is the number of nonzero components. Then, we randomly generate a sample matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
to construct the measurement vector
b
via
b=Ax^{*}
. Here, we consider two different ways to generate the sample matrix
A
:

•

A
is a random Gaussian matrix;

•

A
is a random partial DCT (discrete cosine transform) matrix.

We conduct different sizes of the problems by setting
(m,n,s)=(180i,960i,30i)
with
i=1,2,\cdots,10
. Besides, to implement these algorithms, we take
(\gamma,\mu)=(1,1)
for PDHG,
(\varrho,\tau,\sigma)=(2,\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2},\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2})
for GRPDA (where
\varrho

is the parameter associated with the Golden ratio step, and
(\tau,\sigma)
are the proximal parameters for the
x
- and
y
-subproblems, respectively),
(\gamma,\epsilon)=(1.5,0.015)
for BALM (
65
),
(\gamma,\mu)=(0.6,0.6)
for SPIDA-I and
(\gamma,\mu,\epsilon)=(0.6,0.6,0.01)
for SPIDA-II. All algorithms start with zero initial points and stop at

\text{Tol}:=\frac{\|(x^{k+1},y^{k+1})-(x^{k},y^{k})\|}{\|(x^{k},y^{k})\|}\leq\varepsilon.

(66)

\varepsilon=10^{-6}

5.2
RPCA

In this subsection, we give a numerical feedback to the applicability of our SPIDA to multi-block convex programming discussed in Section
4.2
. Therefore, we consider a well-studied RPCA model
[
9
]
, which refers to the task of recovering a sparse matrix and a low-rank one. Specifically, the RPCA under consideration takes the form

\min_{X,Z}\;\left\{\;\|X\|_{*}+\lambda\|Z\|_{1}\;|\;X+Z=H\;\right\},

(67)

where
\|X\|_{*}
is the nuclear norm (i.e., the sum of all singular values of
X
) for promoting the low-rankness of
X\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
,
\|Z\|_{1}
represents the
\ell_{1}
-norm for inducing the sparsity of
Z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
, and
H\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m\times n}
is a given matrix. Obviously, the RPCA model (
67
) is a special case of the multi-block model (
58
) with two-block structure. Therefore, we can easily reformulate (
67
) as the following separable saddle point problem:

\min_{X,Z}\max_{Y}\;\left\{\;{\mathscr{L}}(X,Z,Y)=\|X\|_{*}+\lambda\|Z\|_{1}+\langle X+Z,Y\rangle-\langle H,Y\rangle\;\right\}.

In this part, we also mainly compare SPIDA with PDHG and GRPDA. Here, we consider the RPCA with synthetic and real data sets to verify the reliability of our algorithm for multi-block convex programming.

We first conduct the numerical performance of these algorithms on synthetic data sets. In this situation, we generate a low-rank matrix
X^{*}
via
X^{*}=UV
, where
U\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n\times r}
and
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r\times n}
are independently random matrices whose entries are drawn from Gaussian distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. Then, we generate a sparse matrix
Z^{*}
by randomly choosing a support set
\Omega
of size
0.1\times n^{2}
(i.e.,
10\%
nonzero components), and all elements are independently sampled from a uniform distribution in
[-50,50]
. Finally, we let
H=X^{*}+Z^{*}
be the observed matrix. Clearly,
(X^{*},Z^{*})
is the true solution of (
67
). Throughout our experiments, we still employ the stopping criterion (
66
) with setting
\varepsilon=10^{-5}
for all algorithms. Besides, we take
(\gamma,\mu)=(70.7107,0.0283)
for PDHG,
(\psi,\tau,\sigma)=(1.618,\frac{70.7107}{\sqrt{1.618}},\frac{0.0283}{\sqrt{1.618}})
for GRPDA,
(\gamma,\mu)=(0.77*70.7107,0.0283)
for SPIDA. In Table
4
, we additionally report the rank of the obtained low-rank matrix (
\text{rank}(\hat{X})
), the number of nonzero components of the obtained sparse matrix (

where

and

respectively represent the low-rank and sparse matrices obtained by the algorithms. It can be seen from Table

.

5.3
Image restoration

In this subsection, we apply the proposed SPIDA to solve an image restoration model with pixels constraints introduced in
[
22
]
. Such a model takes the form

\min_{x\in\mathbb{B}}\;\left\{\||{\bm{D}}x|\|_{1}+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|Kx-b\|^{2}\;\right\},

(68)

where
\mathbb{B}
is a box area characterizing the pixels of an image (indeed,
\mathbb{B}=[0,1]
and
\mathbb{B}=[0,255]
if the image are double precision and 8-bit gray scale, respectively);
\lambda
is a positive trade-off parameter between the data-fidelity and regularization terms;
\bm{D}:=(\partial_{1},\partial_{2})
denotes the gradient operator with
\partial_{1}
and
\partial_{2}
being the discretized derivatives in the horizontal and vertical directions, respectively;
K
is the matrix representation of a blur operator and
b
is a corrupted image with additive noise. Clearly, model (
68
) is equivalent to the following saddle point problem:

\min_{x\in\mathbb{B}}\max_{y\in\mathbb{B}_{\infty}}\;\left\{\langle{\bm{D}}x,y\rangle+\frac{\lambda}{2}\|Kx-b\|^{2}\;\right\},

(69)

where
\mathbb{B}_{\infty}:=\left\{y\;|\;\|y\|_{\infty}\leq 1\right\}
. Consequently, when applying PDHG (
3
) to (
69
), the iterative scheme is specified as

\left\{\begin{aligned} y^{k+1}&=\arg\max_{y\in\mathbb{B}_{\infty}}\left\{\langle{\bm{D}}x^{k},y\rangle-\frac{\gamma}{2}\|y-y^{k}\|^{2}\right\}\equiv\Pi_{\mathbb{B}_{\infty}}(y^{k}+\gamma^{-1}\bm{D}x^{k}),\\
x^{k+1}&=\arg\min_{x\in\mathbb{B}}\left\{\frac{\lambda}{2}\|Kx-b\|^{2}+\langle{\bm{D}}x,2y^{k+1}-y^{k}\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|x-x^{k}\|^{2}\right\},\end{aligned}\right.

(70)

where the updating order of
x
and
y

Conclusions

In this paper, we proposed a new primal-dual algorithmic framework for convex-concave saddle point problems by applying the symmetric idea to compute the dual variable twice. Notice that a Bregman proximal regularization term is embedded in each subproblem, which is of benefit for us designing customized algorithms for some structured optimization problems. Moreover, we can gainfully understand the classical (linearized) augmented Lagrangian method and some parallel augmented Lagrangian-based splitting methods for linearly constrained convex optimization. A series of experiments demonstrate that our new algorithm works better than the other two compared methods as long as the dual subproblem (i.e.,
y
-subproblem) is easy enough with cheap computational cost. In the future, we will consider some acceleration techniques such as extrapolation on the method. Besides, we notice that all subproblems of our algorithm are required to be solved exactly, which is expensive or impossible in some cases (e.g., see experiments in image restoration). Therefore, designing a practical inexact version of the proposed algorithm is also one of our future concerns.
