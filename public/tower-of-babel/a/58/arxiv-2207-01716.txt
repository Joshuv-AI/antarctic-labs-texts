On stability for generalized linear differential equations and applications to impulsive systems
Claudio A. Gallegos, Gonzalo Robledo V

Abstract
In this paper, we are interested in investigating notions of stability for generalized linear differential equations (GLDEs). Initially, we propose and revisit several definitions of stability and provide a complete characterisation of them in terms of upper bounds and asymptotic behaviour of the transition matrix. In addition, we illustrate our stability results for GLDEs to linear periodic systems and linear impulsive differential equations. Finally, we prove that the well known definitions of uniform asymptotic stability and variational asymptotic stability are equivalent to the global uniform exponential stability introduced in this article. Keywords: Generalized ordinary differential equations; Kurzweil integral; Stability; Asymptotic stability; Impulsive equations. MSC 2020 subject classification: Primary: 34A06, 34D20. Secondary: 34A30, 34A37.

Let us to commence by introducing the notion of generalized equation above mentioned. Given a function
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
\Omega=\mathcal{O}\times\mathbb{R}
, and
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open set; a function
x:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, with
[a,b]\subset\mathbb{R}
, is called a
solution of the generalized ODE

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=DF(x,t)

(1.1)

on the interval
[a,b]
, if
(x(t),t)\in\Omega
for every
t\in[a,b]
, and

x(d)-x(c)=\int_{c}^{d}DF(x(\tau),t),\quad\textnormal{whenever $[c,d]\subseteq[a,b]$}.

(1.2)

It is important to emphasize two aspects of this concept: at first, equation (
1.1
) is defined via its solution. Secondly, the integral on the right-hand side of (

) is understood in the

In this paper, we focus our attention in the stability theory for GODEs (

F(x,t)=A(t)x+g(t)

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=D[A(t)x+g(t)].

(1.3)

When the above function

1.3

and is denoted by

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=D[A(t)x].

(1.4)

This type of linear problem was intensively studied since 1971 by Š. Schwabik, who developed –in collaboration with M. Tvrdý– a qualitative linear theory in a saga of papers devoted to the existence and uniqueness theorems, the representation of the unique solution for the homogeneous GLDE (
1.4

through a transition matrix

, a variation of constants formula for GLDEs (

), among other interesting results, see

[

,
34
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

In 2019, Federson
et al.

]
have been considering stability notions in the sense of Lyapunov for GODEs (

Motivated by the aforementioned articles of stability concerned to GODEs (

In Theorem

•

In Theorem

We introduce definitions of global asymptotic stability and global uniform exponential stability for the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (

By using the Floquet theory

2
Preliminaries

Throughout the text,
X
will always denote a Banach space endowed with a norm
\|\cdot\|_{X}
, and the set
\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
denotes the vector space consisting of all
n\times n
- matrices with real components endowed with the operator norm. In order to give a brief overview of the Kurzweil integral theory, we will introduce the following definitions:

•

A subset
P=\{\alpha_{0},\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{\nu(P)}\}\subset[a,b]
, with
\nu(P)\in\mathbb{N}
, is said to be a
partition
of
[a,b]
if
\alpha_{0}=a<\alpha_{1}<...<\alpha_{\nu(P)}=b
. We denote the set which contains all partitions of
[a,b]
by
\mathcal{P}([a,b])
.

•

The pair
(P,\tau):=\{([\alpha_{j-1},\alpha_{j}],\tau_{j}):j=1,...,\nu(P)\}
, where
P\in\mathcal{P}([a,b])
and
\tau_{j}\in[\alpha_{j-1},\alpha_{j}]
, is called a
tagged partition
of
[a,b]
.

•

Any positive function
\delta:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is called a
gauge
on
[a,b]
.

•

If
\delta
is a gauge on
[a,b]
, a
tagged partition
(P,\tau)
is called
\delta
-
fine
if

[\alpha_{j-1},\alpha_{j}]\subset\left(\tau_{j}-\delta(\tau_{j}),\tau_{j}+\delta(\tau_{j})\right),\;\;j=1,\ldots,\nu(P).

•

Let
f:[a,b]\to X
be a function. We denote the
variation
of
f
over the interval
[a,b]
by

\text{var}_{a}^{b}(f)=\sup_{P\in\mathcal{P}([a,b])}\sum_{j=1}^{\nu(P)}\|f(\alpha_{j})-f(\alpha_{j-1})\|_{X}.

The vector space consisting of all functions
f\colon[a,b]\to X
for which
\text{var}_{a}^{b}(f)<\infty
is denoted by
BV([a,b],X)
, and it is a Banach space for the norm

\|f\|_{BV}=\|f(a)\|_{X}+\text{var}_{a}^{b}(f).

We denote by
BV_{loc}([0,+\infty),X)
the set consisting of all functions
f\colon[0,+\infty)\to X
of locally bounded variation,
i.e.
all functions
f
such that
\text{var}_{a}^{b}(f)<\infty
for every compact interval
[a,b]\subset[0,+\infty)
.

Now, we are in a position to define the Kurzweil integral introduced in
[
22
]
:

Definition 2.1
.

A function
G\colon[a,b]\times[a,b]\to X
is called
Kurzweil integrable
on
[a,b]
, if there is an element
\mathcal{I}\in X
having the following property:
for every
\varepsilon>0
, there is a gauge
\delta(\cdot)
on
[a,b]
such that

\left\|\sum_{j=1}^{\nu(P)}[G(\tau_{j},\alpha_{j})-G(\tau_{j},\alpha_{j-1})]-\mathcal{I}\right\|_{X}<\varepsilon,

for all
\delta
–fine tagged partition
(P,\tau)
of
[a,b]
. In this case,
\mathcal{I}
is called the
Kurzweil integral
of
G
over
[a,b]
and will be denoted by
\int_{a}^{b}DG(\tau,t)
.

If
\int_{a}^{b}DG(\tau,t)
exists, then we define
\int_{b}^{a}DG(\tau,t)=-\int_{a}^{b}DG(\tau,t)
and we set
\int_{c}^{c}DG(\tau,t)=0
for all
c\in[a,b]
.

Remark 2.1
.

Definition
2.1
has sense due to the fact that given a gauge
\delta
on
[a,b]
, there always exists a
\delta
-fine tagged partition
(P,\tau)
of the
interval
[a,b]
. See
[
34
, Cousin’s lemma 1.4]
or
[
8
, Lemma 3.1]
.

The Kurzweil integral satisfies the usual properties of integration such as linearity, integrability of subintervals, additivity on adjacent intervals, among others (see Chapter I in
[
34
]
). Let us emphasize certain facts of the Kurzweil integrability notion. The
Henstock-Kurzweil integral
definition –also called
gauge integral
– of a function
x\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is obtained by considering in Definition
2.1
the function
G(\tau,t)=x(\tau)t
for all
(\tau,t)\in[a,b]\times[a,b]
. It is well known that this integral contains the classical ones of Riemann and Lebesgue, see
e.g.

[
8
,
20
]
. Moreover, by considering the function
G(\tau,t)=x(\tau)g(t)
, where
g:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
, the Kurzweil integral turns out be equivalent to the Perron–Stieltjes integral of the function
x(\cdot)
with respect to
g
, see
[
22
]
.

In order to provide an appropriate notation for the subsequent sections, we present the following example

Example 2.2
.

Consider functions
x\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
A:[a,b]\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. By defining
G(\tau,t)=A(t)x(\tau)
in Definition
2.1
, we obtain the Kurzweil–Stieltjes integral of the function
x
respect to the function
A
. This particular case is also called Perron–Stieltjes integral. Note that the integral can be approximated by a Stieltjes sum in the following sense

\int_{a}^{b}DG(\tau,t)=\int_{a}^{b}D[A(t)x(\tau)]\sim\sum_{j=1}^{\nu(P)}[A(s_{j})-A(s_{j-1})]x(\tau_{j}).

Therefore, we use the following conventional notation:

\int_{a}^{b}D[A(t)x(\tau)]=\int_{a}^{b}{\rm d}[A(s)]x(s).

For a list of fundamental properties of the Perron–Stieltjes integral, we refer to
[
27
,
28
,
32
]
.

An ubiquitous Banach space in the functional setting of the Perron–Stieltjes integral is given by the set
G([a,b],X)
, consisting of all regulated functions
f:[a,b]\to X
, endowed with the uniform convergence norm
\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[a,b]}\|f(t)\|_{X}
. We point out that
BV([a,b],X)\subset G([a,b],X)
. For a detailed discussion about this space we refer the reader to
[
14
]
.

We recall an existence result for the Perron–Stieltjes integral which will be crucial in the development of generalized linear differential equations.

Theorem 2.3
.

(
[
27
, Proposition 2.1]
)

Assume that
A\in BV([a,b],\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
f\in G([a,b],\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Then, the Perron–Stieltjes integral
\int_{a}^{b}{\rm d}[A(s)]f(s)
exists and we have

\left\|\int_{a}^{b}{\rm d}[A(s)]f(s)\right\|\leqslant\int_{a}^{b}\|f(s)\|{\rm d}[{\rm var}_{a}^{s}(A)]\leqslant\|f\|_{\infty}{\rm var}_{a}^{b}(A).

Given an arbitrary function
\varphi:[a,b]\to X
in
G([a,b],X)
, throughout this paper we will use the following notations

\Delta^{+}\varphi(t):=\varphi(t^{+})-\varphi(t),\quad\text{ and }\quad\Delta^{-}\varphi(t):=\varphi(t)-\varphi(t^{-}),

where
\varphi(t^{+})
–respectively
\varphi(t^{-})
– denotes the right-hand (left-hand) limit at
t
.

2.1
Generalized ODEs

We recall the concept of generalized ordinary differential equations (GODEs), along with some well known results. In the next statements, we assume that
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a given
\mathbb{R}^{n}
–valued function, where
\Omega=\mathcal{O}\times[t_{0},+\infty)
,
\mathcal{O}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
t_{0}\geqslant 0
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
J\subset[t_{0},+\infty)
be a nondegenerate interval. A function
x\colon J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, is called a
solution of the generalized ordinary differential equation (GODE)

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=DF(x,t)

(2.1)

on the interval
J
, if
(x(t),t)\in\Omega
for every
t\in J
, and

x(d)-x(c)=\int_{c}^{d}DF(x(\tau),t),

(2.2)

whenever
[c,d]\subseteq J
.

The integral on the right-hand side of (
2.2
) is understood in the sense of Definition
2.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

As pointed out in
[
34
, pp.99–100]
, a generalized ordinary differential equation denoted by (
2.1
) is a formal equation being defined via its solution. Actually, the derivative symbol involved in (
2.1
) does not imply a priori that the function
x(\cdot)
, which is a solution of (
2.1
), possess a derivative in some sense.

Proposition 2.5
.

(
[
34
, Proposition 3.5]
)

If
x:J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of the GODE (
2.1
) on the interval
J\subset[t_{0},+\infty)
, then for every fixed
\gamma\in J
we have

x(s)=x(\gamma)+\int_{\gamma}^{s}DF(x(\tau),t),\qquad\text{ for any }s\in J.

(2.3)

Furthermore, if a function
x:J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies the integral equation (
2.3
) for some
\gamma\in J
and all
s\in J
, then
x(\cdot)
is a solution of the GODE (
2.1
).

With the purpose to obtain more specific information about the solutions of GODEs, we precise extra conditions over the function
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For that reason, we define a broad class of functions
F
for which are included the theory of ordinary differential equations, measure differential equations, and impulsive differential equations, among others. See
[
23
]
or Chapter V in
[
34
]
.

Definition 2.6
.

If there exists a nondecreasing function
h\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
such that
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies

(F1)

\|F(x,s_{2})-F(x,s_{1})\|\leqslant|h(s_{2})-h(s_{1})|
,
for all
(x,s_{i})\in\Omega
, with
i=1,2
,

(F2)

\|F(x,s_{2})-F(x,s_{1})-F(y,s_{2})+F(y,s_{1})\|\leqslant|h(s_{2})-h(s_{1})|\|x-y\|
, for all
(x,s_{i}),(y,s_{i})\in\Omega
, with
i=1,2
,

then we say that
F
belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}(\Omega,h)
or simply
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Omega,h)
.

Example 2.7
.

Consider
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
[a,b]\subset[t_{0},+\infty)
be a compact interval. Assume that
A\in BV([a,b],\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
g\in BV([a,b],\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Note that for every
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times[a,b]
, we can define

F(x,t)=A(t)x+g(t).

(2.4)

Consider
\Omega={B_{c}(x_{0})}\times[a,b]
, with
c\geqslant 1
. The function
F
defined by (
2.4
) belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}(\Omega,h)
, where the function
h:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
is defined by
h(t)=(c+\|x_{0}\|){\text{var}}_{a}^{t}(A)+{\text{var}}_{a}^{t}(g),
for all
t\in[a,b]
.

The next lemma describes properties of the solutions of GODEs when the function
F
satisfies the condition (F1).

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
F\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a function that satisfies condition
(F1)
. If
x\colon J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of
the GODE (
2.1
) on the interval
J
, then the inequality

\|x(s_{2})-x(s_{1})\|\leqslant|h(s_{2})-h(s_{1})|

holds for every
s_{1},s_{2}\in J
.

Remark 2.3
.

When the function
F
satisfies the condition (F1) and
x(\cdot)
is a solution of the GODE (
2.1
) on
J
, since the function
h
is locally of bounded variation on
J
(nondecreasing function defined on
J
), it follows that
x(\cdot)
is also locally of bounded variation on
J
. In addition, the continuity points of the function
h
are continuity points of the solution
x(\cdot)
of the GODE (
2.1
) on
J
.

The next theorem concerns with the existence-uniqueness of solutions for GODEs defined on a maximal interval, see
[
11
]
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{F}(\Omega,h)
, where
h\colon[t_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is a nondecreasing left–continuous function. Assume that
\Omega=\Omega_{F}:=\{(x,t)\in\Omega\colon x+F(x,t^{+})-F(x,t)\in\mathcal{O}\}
. Then, for every
(x_{0},s_{0})\in\Omega
there exists a unique maximal solution
x\colon[s_{0},\omega(x_{0},s_{0}))\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the GODE (
2.1
), with
x(s_{0})=x_{0}
and
\omega(x_{0},s_{0})\leqslant+\infty
.

The condition
\Omega=\Omega_{F}:=\{(x,t)\in\Omega:x+F(x,t^{+})-F(x,t)\in\mathcal{O}\}
ensures that there are no points in
\Omega
for which the solution of the generalized ODE (
2.1
) can escape of
\mathcal{O}
, see
[
34
, p.127]
.

2.2
Generalized linear differential equations

In this subsection, we put our attention in the special case of GODEs which was regarded in Example
2.7
. Throughout this paper, we consider that
J=[0,+\infty)
, and
A\colon J\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a
n\times n
- matrix valued function of locally bounded variation on
J
.

As it was explained in Chapter VI of
[
34
]
, if
g\in BV_{loc}(J,\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then a particular case of the GODE (
2.1
) is obtained if we consider the function
F\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\times J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
defined by (
2.4
). This case in literature is known as generalized linear differential equation (GLDE), and it is denoted by

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=D[A(t)x+g(t)].

(2.5)

It follows from Definition
2.4
that a function
x:J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of (
2.5
) on
J
if for every
a,b\in J
we have

x(b)-x(a)=\int_{a}^{b}D[A(t)x(\tau)+g(t)],

where the integral is considered in the sense of Definition
2.1
. Taking into consideration the comments in Example
2.2
, the integral on the right-hand side can be decomposed as follows

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}D[A(t)x(\tau)+g(t)]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}D[A(t)x(\tau)]+g(b)-g(a)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{a}^{b}{\rm d}[A(s)]x(s)+g(b)-g(a).

Summarising, a function
x\colon J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of (
2.5
) if for every
a,b\in J
we have

x(b)-x(a)=\int_{a}^{b}{\rm d}[A(s)]x(s)+g(b)-g(a).

Remark 2.4
.

Similarly as in the classical theory of ordinary differential equations, in the case that the function
g:J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is identically null, Eq. (
2.5
) is called
homogeneous GLDE
and it is denoted by

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=D[A(t)x].

(2.6)

To develop a qualitative theory for the GLDE (
2.5
), it is neccesary to introduce conditions over the function
A
. For a detailed discussion about this aspect, see
[
34
, Proposition 6.4]
. Consider the following condition

(D)

The matrix
[I-\Delta^{-}A(t)]
is invertible for all
t\in(0,+\infty)
and the matrix
[I+\Delta^{+}A(t)]
is invertible for every
t\in J
.

Now, we will see that for every
(x_{0},s_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times J
there is a unique solution
x(\cdot)
of (
2.5
) with initial condition
x(s_{0})=x_{0}
defined on the whole interval
[s_{0},+\infty)
,
i.e.
in the generalized linear case, under appropriate assumptions, we always can prolongate the solution and consider
\omega(s_{0},x_{0})=+\infty
.

Theorem 2.10
.

(
[
34
, Theorem 6.5]
)

Let
g\in BV_{loc}(J,\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Assume that
A\in BV_{loc}(J,\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
(D)
holds. Then for every
(x_{0},s_{0})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times J
there exists a unique solution
x(\cdot)\in BV_{loc}(J,\mathbb{R}^{n})
of the GLDE (
2.5
) with initial condition
x(s_{0})=x_{0}
.

In the next, we define the concept of fundamental matrix for the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
).

Definition 2.11
.

A matrix valued function
X:J\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is called a
fundamental matrix
of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) if

satisfies the identity

3
Stability for generalized linear differential equations

In this section, we will establish Lyapunov-type stability results for the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
). Throughout this section, we will consider that
J=[0,+\infty)
,
A\in BV_{loc}(J,\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and condition (D) holds, the function
F:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times J\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the GODE (
2.1
) will be defined by
F(x,t)=A(t)x
, for all
(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times J
,
i.e.
we are concerned with the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
). It is clear from definition that the trivial solution
x\equiv 0
is a
solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
).

Since Theorem
2.13
, we can guarantee that the unique solution
x(\cdot,s_{0},x_{0})
of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) with initial condition
x(s_{0})=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined on the whole interval
[s_{0},+\infty)
, and
x(t,s_{0},x_{0})=U(t,s_{0})x_{0}
, for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, where
U(t,s_{0})
is the transition matrix defined by (
2.8
).

Let us recall stability concepts for the trivial solution
x\equiv 0
of the generalized ODE (
2.1
), which were introduced in
[
12
]
. These notions emulate the classical Lyapunov–type stability definitions for nonautonomous ODEs, see for instance
[
9
, Def.4.1.12]
. We reformulate the statement adapted for the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
).

Definition 3.1
.

The trivial solution
x\equiv 0
of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is said to be

•

Stable
, if for every
s_{0}\geqslant 0
and every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta=\delta(s_{0},\varepsilon)>0
such that if
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\|x_{0}\|<\delta
, then
\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|=\|x(t)\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\in[s_{0},+\infty)
.

•

Uniformly stable
, if it is stable with
\delta>0
independent of
s_{0}
.

•

Uniformly asymptotically stable
, if it is uniformly stable and if there exists
\delta_{0}>0
such that, for each
\varepsilon>0
,
there is a
T=T(\varepsilon)\geqslant 0
such that if
s_{0}\geqslant 0
and
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

\|x_{0}\|<\delta_{0},

then

\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|=\|x(t)\|<\varepsilon,

for all
t\in[s_{0}+T(\varepsilon),+\infty)
.

Furthermore, we consider the following notions of global asymptotic stability for GLDEs.

Definition 3.2
.

The trivial solution
x\equiv 0
the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is said to be

•

Globally asymptotically stable
, if it is stable and for any
x(s_{0})=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we get
\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|\longrightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow+\infty
.

•

Globally uniformly exponentially stable
, if there exist constants
\alpha,K>0
such that for any
x(s_{0})=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we get
\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|<K\left\|x_{0}\right\|e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}
for all
t\in[s_{0},+\infty)
.

•

Globally non uniformly exponentially stable
, if there exist a constant
\alpha>0
and a nondecreasing function
K\colon[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
such that for any
x(s_{0})=x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we get
\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|<K(s_{0})\left\|x_{0}\right\|e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}
for all
t\in[s_{0},+\infty)
.

Let us emphasise that the global exponential stabilities –uniform and non uniform– are particular cases of the global asymptotic stability notion.

Remark 3.1
.

Let
B_{c}
be the open ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
centered at the origin with radius
c>0
, and
\Omega=B_{c}\times[t_{0},+\infty)
where
t_{0}\geqslant 0
. The notions of asymptotical stability and exponential asymptotical stability of the origin have been introduced in
[
15
, Definition 3.1]
for nonlinear generalized ODEs of type

\frac{dx}{d\tau}=DF(x,t),

where the function
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}(\Omega,h)
and satisfies
F(0,t)-F(0,s)=0
for all
t,s\geqslant t_{0}
. In this case, the initial conditions are assumed in
B_{\rho}
with
0<\rho<c
. Nevertheless, when
x\mapsto F(t,x)
is a linear map, the initial conditions can be localized in a ball with infinite radius. Taking this fact into account, we highlight that the first two global stabilities stated in Definition 3.2 for GLDEs are directly recovered from
[
15
, Definition 3.1]
, considering in this last mentioned notion the limit case of infinite radius. For that reason, the adjective global is additioned in the linear context. Moreover, we emphasize that the global non uniform exponential stability stated in Definition 3.2 is a novel definition in the GLDEs framework but emulates the classical definition in the linear ODE context, namely, the contractive case of the non uniform exponential dichotomy, see
[
5
,
40
]
.

We are in a position to establish the first stability results for the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
).

Theorem 3.3
.

The trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is uniformly stable if and only if the transition matrix
U(t,s_{0})
is bounded for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Assume that the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is uniformly stable. Let
\varepsilon=1
. There exists
\delta=\delta(1)>0
such that

\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|=\|U(t,s_{0})x_{0}\|<1,

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, and
\|x_{0}\|<\delta
. Therefore, for an arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|z\|\leqslant 1
, we have
\|x(t,s_{0},(\delta/2)z)\|=\|U(t,s_{0})(\delta/2)z\|<1
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
. Hence, we deduce that

\|U(t,s_{0})\|=\sup_{\|z\|\leqslant 1}\|U(t,s_{0})z\|\leqslant 2/\delta,

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

On the other hand, suppose that there exists a positive constant
C>0
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant C
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
. Given
\varepsilon>0
, we can take
\delta=\varepsilon/C
, and it follows that

\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|=\|U(t,s_{0})x_{0}\|\leqslant\|U(t,s_{0})\|\|x_{0}\|<\varepsilon,

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, and
\|x_{0}\|<\delta
. Therefore, the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is uniformly stable.
∎

In the previous Theorem
3.3
, the boundedness of the transition matrix is independent of the initial time
s_{0}\in J
. It is straightforward to see that the stability can be characterised with a bound depending on the initial time
s_{0}\in J
. Specifically, we can state the following result

Corollary 3.4
.

The trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is stable if and only if for every
s_{0}\in J
, there is
C=C(s_{0})>0
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant C
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
.

In the next, we characterise the global asymptotic stability of the trivial solution.

Theorem 3.5
.

The trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is globally asymptotically stable if and only if for every
s_{0}\in J
, it follows that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\longrightarrow 0
as
t\to+\infty
.

Proof.

Let
s_{0}\in J
and assume that the trivial solution of the generalized linear ODE (
2.6
) is globally asymptotically stable. Then it follows that

\|U(t,s_{0})\|=\sup_{\|z\|\leqslant 1}\|U(t,s_{0})z\|=\sup_{\|z\|\leqslant 1}\|x(t,s_{0},z)\|\longrightarrow 0,\qquad\text{ as }t\to+\infty.

On the other hand, suppose that for every
s_{0}\in J
,
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\longrightarrow 0
as
t\to+\infty
. We will prove the stability of the trivial solution. Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. There exists
N\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant N
. In addition, since
U(\cdot,s_{0}):[s_{0},N]\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a function of bounded variation, it follows that the function
\|U(\cdot,s_{0})\|:[s_{0},N]\to\mathbb{R}
is of bounded variation on
[s_{0},N]
. Hence, there exists a
K(\varepsilon,s_{0})>0
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|<K(\varepsilon,s_{0})
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
.

Now, if we choose
\delta=\varepsilon/K(\varepsilon,s_{0})
, then we have

\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|=\|U(t,s_{0})x_{0}\|=\|U(t,s_{0})\|\|x_{0}\|<\varepsilon,

for all
\|x_{0}\|<\delta
, and we conclude the stability. In addition, since
x(t,s_{0},x_{0})=U(t,s_{0})x_{0}
, and
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\longrightarrow 0
as
t\to+\infty
, it follows that
x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\longrightarrow 0
as
t\to+\infty
(independent if
x_{0}
is close to zero).
∎

The next result is a characterisation of the uniform asymptotic stability for the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) and proves that the uniform asymptotic stability in the linear case is equivalent to the global uniform exponential stability.

Theorem 3.6
.

The trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is uniformly asymptotically stable if and only if there exist positive constants
\alpha,K>0
such that

\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}

(3.1)

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Assume that there exist positive constants
\alpha,K>0
such that (
3.1
) holds for all
t\geqslant s\geqslant 0
. Clearly, the transition matrix is bounded, with
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant K
, for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
. Hence, by Theorem
3.3
, the trivial solution of the linear generalized ODE (
2.6
) is uniformly stable.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. We can always assume that
K>\varepsilon
. Consider
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
s_{0}\geqslant 0
. Note that we have the following equivalence

Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}<\varepsilon\;\Longleftrightarrow\;t>s_{0}+\dfrac{\ln(K/\varepsilon)}{\alpha}.

Therefore, considering
\delta_{0}=1
and
T(\varepsilon)=\dfrac{\ln(K/\varepsilon)}{\alpha}
, we obtain that

\|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})\|=\|U(t,s_{0})x_{0}\|\leqslant\|x_{0}\|Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}<\varepsilon,

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T(\varepsilon)
. Hence, we conclude that the trivial solution of the generalized ODE (
2.6
) is uniformly asymptotically stable.

On the other hand, suppose that the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is uniformly asymptotically stable. Let
0<\varepsilon<1
be fixed. There exists
\delta_{0}>0
independent of
\varepsilon
, and there is
T=T(\varepsilon)>0
, such that

\|U(t,s_{0})x_{0}\|<\dfrac{\varepsilon\delta_{0}}{2},

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T
and
\|x_{0}\|<\delta_{0}
. Now, for an arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|z\|\leqslant 1
we obtain
\|U(t,s_{0})(\delta_{0}/2)z\|<(\varepsilon\delta_{0}/2)
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T
, which implies that
\|U(t,s_{0})z\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T
. Hence,

\|U(t,s_{0})\|<\varepsilon,

(3.2)

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T
.

From the uniform stability assumption of (
2.6
) and Theorem
3.3
, we deduce the existence of a positive constant
C>0
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant C
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

Consider
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, and let
N\geqslant 1
be the smallest integer such that
t\leqslant s_{0}+NT
. Then, by the transition property of the transition matrix we have the following decomposition

U(t,s_{0})=U(t,s_{0}+(N-1)T)U(s_{0}+(N-1)T,s_{0}+(N-2)T)\cdots U(s_{0}+T,s_{0}).

By (
3.2
) and the previous equality we obtain

\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant\|U(t,s_{0}+(N-1)T)\|\varepsilon^{N-1}\leqslant C\varepsilon^{N-1},\qquad\text{for all }t\geqslant s_{0}.

(3.3)

Consider an arbitrary
\alpha>0
. In the case that
N=1
, i.e.
s_{0}<t\leqslant s_{0}+T
, we have

\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant C=Ce^{\alpha(t-s_{0})}e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}\leqslant Ce^{\alpha T}e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}=Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})},

where
K=Ce^{\alpha T}
. In addition, in the case that
N>1
, for
s_{0}+(N-1)T\leqslant t\leqslant s_{0}+NT
, we deduce

e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}e^{\alpha T}\geqslant e^{-\alpha(N-1)T}.

(3.4)

Therefore, if we take
\alpha=-\dfrac{\ln(\varepsilon)}{T}>0
, then from (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) it follows that

\displaystyle\|U(t,s_{0})\|

\displaystyle\leqslant Ce^{-\alpha(N-1)T}\leqslant Ce^{\alpha T}e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}=Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}.

(3.5)

Gathering all the previous observations, for
\alpha=-\dfrac{\ln(\varepsilon)}{T}>0
and
K=Ce^{\alpha T}>0
, we obtain

\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})},\qquad\text{for all }t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0,

getting the desired result.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

Theorem
3.6
ensures that the uniform asymptotic stability is equivalent to the global uniform exponential stability. In fact, if the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (

satisfying (

). Then, for an arbitrary

and the global uniform exponential stability follows. The other implication follows easily from the second paragraph of the proof of Theorem

[

. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, it has not been verified for the homogeneous GLDE (

Theorem 3.7

Assume that

Similarly as in the classical Floquet theory, the stability of a

Corollary 3.8
.

Assume the hypotheses of Theorem
3.7
. The trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (

are inside of the unit circle.

Proof.

Suppose that every eigenvalue
\rho
of
X(\omega)
verifies
|\rho|<1
. We will prove that for any
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0

3.1
) holds. Note that as

is invertible, it is easy to deduce from (

t\in J
the matrix

is invertible. Now, from Remark

U(t,s_{0})=P(t)e^{Q(t-s_{0})}P^{-1}(s_{0}),\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0$},

where
P^{-1}\colon J\to\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is also
\omega

and the
\omega

By (

P(t)=X(t)e^{-Qt}
. Now, by using Remark
2.6

a constant

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|P(t)\|&\leqslant&\|U(t,0)\|\|e^{-Qt}\|\\
&\leqslant&M\,\max\limits_{t\in[0,\omega]}\|e^{-Qt}\|,\end{array}

and we conclude the existence of

such that

\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\omega]}\|P(t)\|\leqslant M_{1}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\sup\limits_{t\in[0,\omega]}\|P^{-1}(t)\|\leqslant M_{2},

where the second inequality can be obtained in a similar way. Now, the above estimations and the

\omega
–periodicity of
P
and
P^{-1}
implies that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|U(t,s_{0})\|&\leqslant&\|P(t)\|\,\|P^{-1}(s_{0})\|\,\|e^{Q(t-s_{0})}\|\\
&\leqslant&K_{0}\,\|e^{Q(t-s_{0})}\|,\end{array}

(3.7)

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, where
K_{0}=M_{1}M_{2}
. In addition, note that every eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}
of
Q
,
has the form

\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{\omega}[\ln|\rho_{k}|+i\,(\arg\rho_{k}+2m\pi)]\quad\textnormal{with $m\in\mathbb{Z}$},

where
\rho_{k}
is an eigenvalue of the matrix
X(\omega)
, we refer the reader
to
[
1
, p.20]
for details. Moreover, as all the eigenvalues of
X(\omega)

are inside the unit circle, from
[
30
, Th.1.9.2]
we infer the existence of positive constants
\alpha>0
and
K_{1}>0
such that
\|e^{Q(t-s_{0})}\|\leqslant K_{1}e^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
. This fact combined with (
3.7
) leads to the estimate

\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0$},

where
K=K_{0}K_{1}
. Finally, gathering Theorem
3.6
and Remark
3.2
we conclude that the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is globally uniformly exponentially stable.

On the other hand, suppose that the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is globally uniformly exponentially stable. We will prove that any eigenvalue
\rho
of

X(\omega)
belongs to the interior of the unit circle. By contradiction, assume that there exists an eigenvalue
\rho
satisfying
|\rho|\geqslant 1
with

U(\omega,0)x_{0}=\rho\,x_{0},

where
x_{0}
is an eigenvector associated to
\rho
. Then, it is straightforward to prove that

U(k\omega,0)x_{0}=\rho^{k}x_{0}\quad\textnormal{for any $k\in\mathbb{N}$},

since
U(t+\omega,s+\omega)=U(t,s)
for all
t,s\geqslant 0
. The above identity combined with (
3.1
) leads to

|\rho|^{k}\|x_{0}\|=\|U(k\omega,0)x_{0}\|\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha k\omega}\|x_{0}\|,

for some
K>0
and
\alpha>0
. As
|\rho|\geqslant 1
, a contradiction is obtained letting
k\to\infty
.
∎

4
Applications to scalar impulsive equations

A noteworthy result of Schwabik
[
34
, pp.193–196]
establishes that every linear impulsive differential equation can be written as a particular homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) provided some mild conditions. In this section, we will consider the scalar case in order to study the stability properties of the impulsive differential equation

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\dot{x}=a(t)x,\qquad\textnormal{for $t\neq\tau_{k}$}\\
\\
\Delta^{+}x(\tau_{k})=b_{k}x(\tau_{k}),\end{array}\right.

(4.1)

where
t\geqslant 0
, the function
a\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is locally Lebesgue integrable on
[0,+\infty)
,
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is a divergent sequence without cluster points, and the real sequence
\{b_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies
1+b_{k}\neq 0
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

As above mentioned, the impulsive differential equation (
4.1
) can be written as a scalar homogeneous GLDE

\frac{dx}{d\tau}=D[A(t)x],

(4.2)

by considering the function
A\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by

A(t)=\int_{0}^{t}a(r)\,dr+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}H_{\tau_{k}}(t),\qquad\text{for all }t\geqslant 0,

(4.3)

where
H_{\tau_{k}}(\cdot)
is defined by
H_{\tau_{k}}(t)=H(t-\tau_{k})
for all
t\geqslant 0
, and
H(\cdot)
denotes the Heaviside function. Note that the function
A(\cdot)
is continuous from the left, locally of bounded variation on
[0,+\infty)
, and satisfies condition (D) from Section 2. Therefore, the unique forward solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) passing through
x_{0}
at time
t=s_{0}
has the form

x(t,s_{0},x_{0})=U(t,s_{0})x_{0}
, for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, where
U(t,s_{0})
is the transition matrix described by Theorem
2.12
.

Following the techniques employed in
[
34
, pp.195-196]
, we can state that for
t,s\geqslant 0
the transition matrix for the homogeneous scalar GLDE (
4.2
) has the form

U(t,s)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}\Phi(t,s)&,&\tau_{j}<t,s\leqslant\tau_{j+1},\\
\Phi(t,s)\prod\limits_{k=j+1}^{i}(1+b_{k})&,&\tau_{j}<s\leqslant\tau_{j+1}\leqslant\tau_{i}<t\leqslant\tau_{i+1},\\
\Phi(t,s)\prod\limits_{k=j+1}^{i}\left(\frac{1}{1+b_{k}}\right)&,&\tau_{j}<t\leqslant\tau_{j+1}\leqslant\tau_{i}<s\leqslant\tau_{i+1},\par\end{array}\right.

(4.4)

where
\Phi(t,s)=e^{\int_{s}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau}
denotes the transition matrix associated to the continuous part of the impulsive differential equation (
4.1
).

On the other hand, note that the transition matrix (
4.4
) coincides with the transition matrix corresponding to the impulsive equation (
4.1
) which is well known for the classical theory of impulsive linear systems developed
in the pioneering works of
[
3
,
17
,
25
,
31
]
. Hence, both the impulsive differential equation (
4.1
) and the scalar homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) have the same transition matrix, which in turn implies that its solutions passing through
x_{0}
at time
s_{0}
are the same and are given by

x(t,s_{0},x_{0})=U(t,s_{0})x_{0},\qquad\text{ for all $t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0$.}

The stability notions described in Definitions
3.1
and
3.2
for homogeneous GLDEs
have some similarities with the stability theory for linear impulsive
equations. In fact, the concepts of stability, uniform stability and global asymptotic stability of GLDEs are equivalent to those for impulsive systems stated in
[
26
, p.35]
and
[
31
, pp.56–57]
respectively. Moreover, the stability theory of linear impulsive systems allows to classify several types of global asymptotic stabilities that can be described as a particular case of dichotomies with identity projector. A definition tailored to the scalar impulsive case (
4.1
) –adapted from
[
40
, Def.2.2]
– is the following:

Definition 4.1
.

The scalar impulsive differential equation (
4.1
):

(a)

Has a
uniform contraction
if there exist
a couple
(K,\alpha)
of positive constants and an increasing function
h\colon[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
with

h(0)=1
and
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}h(t)=+\infty
such that

|U(t,s)|\leqslant K\left(\frac{h(t)}{h(s)}\right)^{-\alpha}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geqslant s\geqslant 0$}.

(b)

Has a
non uniform contraction
if there exist three positive constants
\{K,\alpha,\varepsilon\}
and two increasing functions
h,\mu\colon[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
with

h(0)=\mu(0)=1
and
\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}h(t)=\lim\limits_{t\to+\infty}\mu(t)=+\infty
such that

|U(t,s)|\leqslant K\left(\frac{h(t)}{h(s)}\right)^{-\alpha}\mu(s)^{\varepsilon}\quad\textnormal{for any $t\geqslant s\geqslant 0$}.

In the next, we illustrate with simple examples our stability results for the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) established in the previous section and we compare them with the stability notions in the impulsive framework.

Example 4.2
.

If we assume that the function
a(\cdot)
is Lebesgue integrable on
[0,+\infty)
and the sequence
\{b_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is such that the series
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\ln(|1+b_{k}|)
is absolutely convergent, then the origin of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) is uniformly stable. In fact, the integrability of the function
a\colon[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and the absolute convergence of the series imply the existence of constants
L_{1}\in\mathbb{R}
and
L_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(t,s)=e^{\int_{s}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau}\leqslant e^{L_{1}}\quad\textnormal{and}\quad\prod\limits_{k=j+1}^{i}|1+b_{k}|\leqslant e^{\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}|\ln(|1+b_{k}|)|}\leqslant e^{L_{2}}.

By using (
4.4
) we obtain that
|U(t,s)|\leqslant e^{L_{1}+L_{2}}
for all
t\geqslant s\geqslant 0
and the uniform stability of the null solution of (
4.2
) follows from Theorem
3.3
.

As we mentioned above, the definition
of the uniform stability is the same for
GLDEs and linear impulsive equations, which is determined by the uniform boundedness of the transition matrix.
Hence, the origin is also a uniformly stable solution for the impulsive equation (
4.1
).

Now, we provide an example of global (non uniform) asymptotic stability for the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
).

Example 4.3
.

Consider the function
a:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
defined by
a(t)=-\omega-ct\sin(t)
, for all
t\geqslant 0
,
with
0<c<\omega
, and assume that the series

\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\ln(|1+b_{k}|)
is absolutely convergent. Then the origin of (
4.2
) is globally asymptotically stable. In fact, it follows from
[
5
, Proposition 2.3]
that the
assumption
0<c<\omega
implies the existence of a positive constant
K_{0}>0
such that

\Phi(t,s)=e^{\int_{s}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau}\leqslant K_{0}e^{-(\omega-c)(t-s)}e^{2cs},\qquad\textnormal{for $t\geqslant s\geqslant 0$}.

Therefore, using (
4.4
) and the absolute convergence of the series as in Example
4.2
, we obtain a positive constant
K>0
such that

|U(t,s)|\leqslant Ke^{-(\omega-c)(t-s)+2cs}=K(s)e^{-(\omega-c)(t-s)},\qquad\textnormal{for $t\geqslant s\geqslant 0$}.

(4.5)

In consequence, by Theorem
3.5
we have that the origin of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) is globally asymptotically stable. Moreover, since
\omega>c
, this global stability corresponds to the specific case of global non uniform exponential stability
stated in Definition
3.2
.

In Example
4.3
, the definition
of global non uniform exponential stability for GLDEs can be seen as a specific case of non uniform contraction
of the impulsive differential equation (
4.1
) with functions
h,\mu\colon[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
defined by
h(t)=e^{t}
and
\mu(t)=e^{2t}
, while the corresponding positive constants are given by
\alpha=\omega-c
and
\varepsilon=c
.

The last two examples will illustrate the uniform asymptotic stability of the trivial solution for the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
).

Example 4.4
.

Let
a(\cdot)
be a bounded and piecewise continuous function verifying the average condition

\limsup\limits_{t-s\to+\infty}\frac{1}{t-s}\int_{s}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau\leqslant-\alpha,\quad\textnormal{for $t>s$},

(4.6)

where
\alpha>0
. Assume further that the series

\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\ln(|1+b_{k}|)
is absolutely convergent. Then the origin of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) is uniformly asymptotically stable. In fact, the average condition (
4.6
) means that the upper Bohl exponent
of the equation
\dot{x}=a(t)x
is smaller than
-\alpha<0
(see
[
4
]
,
[
18
, pp.258–259]
for details), which in turn implies the existence of
K_{0}>0
such that

\displaystyle\Phi(t,s)=e^{\int_{s}^{t}a(\tau)\,d\tau}\leqslant K_{0}e^{-\alpha(t-s)}\quad\textnormal{for all $t\geqslant s\geqslant 0$}.

In addition, as in the previous Example
4.2
, by the absolute convergence of the series we get an estimation for the product
\prod\limits_{k=j+1}^{i}|1+b_{k}|
. Now, using (
4.4
) and the above estimations, we deduce the existence of a positive constant
K>0
such that

|U(t,s)|\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha(t-s)}
for all
t\geqslant s\geqslant 0
. Hence, the uniform asymptotic stability follows from Theorem
3.6
.

Let us emphasise that Example
4.4
is also an example of global uniform exponential stability for the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
). In fact, for an arbitrary
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
and
s_{0}\geqslant 0
we get

|x(t,s_{0},x_{0})|=|U(t,s_{0})x_{0}|\leqslant|x_{0}|Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})},\qquad\text{for all }t\geqslant s_{0}.

Moreover, the definition
of global uniform exponential stability for GLDEs can be regarded as a particular case of uniform contraction
of the impulsive differential equation (
4.1
) with function
h\colon[0,+\infty)\to[1,+\infty)
defined by
h(t)=e^{t}
.

In the above examples, the asymptotic behaviour of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
)
is dominated by the continuous part while the discrete one is harmless. Our last example inverses this behaviour.

Example 4.5
.

Consider a positive valued function
a:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)

Lebesgue integrable on
[0,+\infty)
. Moreover, assume that
the sequence of impulses
\{\tau_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies the condition

a)

There exists
c>0
such that

\inf\limits_{i+1>j}\left\{\frac{i-j}{\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{j}}\right\}=c,

while the sequence
\{b_{k}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
verifies the condition

b)

There exists
\theta,\eta\in(0,1)
such that
\eta<|1+b_{k}|\leqslant\theta
for all
k\in\mathbb{N}
.

In the case that
t>s
with
s\in(\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
and
t\in(\tau_{i},\tau_{i+1}]

with
i\geqslant j+1
. By using the asbolute integrability of
a(\cdot)
,
combined with properties a) and b), and defining
\alpha_{0}=-c\ln(\theta)>0

we have that:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}|U(t,s)|&\leqslant&K\,\theta^{i-j}\\
&\leqslant&K\,e^{\left[\frac{i-j}{\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{j}}\right](\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{j})\ln(\theta)}\\
&\leqslant&K\,e^{-\alpha_{0}(\tau_{i+1}-\tau_{j})},\\
&\leqslant&K\,e^{-\alpha_{0}(t-s)}.\end{array}

In the case that
t>s
with
t,s\in(\tau_{j},\tau_{j+1}]
, we choose a
s_{0}\in(\tau_{j+1},\tau_{j+2}]
and decompose
U(t,s)=U(t,s_{0})U(s_{0},s)
. Then, by using the previous estimations and condition (b), we obtain

\begin{split}|U(t,s)|&\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha_{0}(s_{0}-s)}|[U(s_{0},t)]^{-1}|\\
&\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha_{0}(t-s)}e^{\left|-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}a(\xi)d\xi\right|}\dfrac{1}{|1+b_{j+1}|}\\
&\leqslant K^{2}\eta^{-1}e^{-\alpha_{0}(t-s)}.\end{split}

Summarising, there exist positive constants
N>0
and
\alpha_{0}>0
such that
|U(t,s)|<Ne^{-\alpha_{0}(t-s)}
, for all
t>s\geqslant 0
. Therefore, by Theorem
3.6
, we deduce that the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
4.2
) is uniformly asymptotically stable.

5
Variational stability for generalized linear differential equations

In this section, we recall the concepts of variational stability and stability with respect to perturbations for homogeneous GLDEs introduced in
[
35
]
. We will study the relation of these concepts with the notion of stability given in the previous Section 3.

Throughout this section, we assume that the function
A\in BV_{loc}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
is continuous from the left and condition (D) holds. Since
A
is left-continuous, we just assume that
[I+\Delta^{+}A(t)]
is invertible for all
t\geqslant 0
because the case when the matrix
[I-\Delta^{-}A(t)]
is invertible evidently holds.

We begin by introducing the notion of variational stability.

Definition 5.1
.

The trivial solution
x\equiv 0
of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is said to be

•

Variationally stable
, if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
y:[s_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
s_{0}\geqslant 0
, is a function of locally bounded variation on
[s_{0},+\infty)
and left-continuous on
(s_{0},+\infty)
with
\|y(s_{0})\|<\delta
and

\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}\left(y(s)-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{\rm d}[A(\xi)]y(\xi)\right)<\delta,

then
\|y(t)\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
.

•

Variationally attracting
, if there exists
\delta_{0}>0
and for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
T=T(\varepsilon)\geqslant 0
and
\gamma=\gamma(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
y:[s_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
s_{0}\geqslant 0
, is a function of locally bounded variation on
[s_{0},+\infty)
and left-continuous on
(s_{0},+\infty)
with
\|y(s_{0})\|<\delta_{0}
and

\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}\left(y(s)-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{\rm d}[A(\xi)]y(\xi)\right)<\gamma,

then
\|y(t)\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T(\varepsilon)
and
s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

•

Variationally asymptotically stable
, if it is variationally stable and variationally attracting.

We cite the following reflection about the variational stability notion stated in
[
34
, Remark 10.7]
: “This concept comes from the following intuitive idea: if a certain function
y(\cdot)
given on some interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]\subset[0,+\infty)
is such that the initial value
y(t_{0})
is close to zero and on the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
the function
y
is almost a solution of (
2.1
), i.e. the variation of the function

y(s)-y(t_{0})-\int_{t_{0}}^{s}DF(y(\tau),t)

on
[t_{0},t_{1}]
is small enough, then
y
is close to zero on the interval
[t_{0},t_{1}]
”.

Remark 5.1
.

Note that in Definition
5.1
when it is considered the function
y(\cdot)
as a solution of homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
), then we recover the Definition
3.1
of stability given in Section 3. In particular, the variational stability notion can be seen as the uniform stability, and the variational asymptotic stability notion can be seen as the uniform asymptotic stability.

In the next, we will define a concept of stability respect to perturbations which is closely related with the previous notion of variational stability. For our purpose, consider the following generalized perturbed equation

\dfrac{dx}{d\tau}=D[A(t)x+P(t)],

(5.1)

where
P:[0,+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a function.

Definition 5.2
.

The trivial solution
x\equiv 0
of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is said to be

•

Stable with respect to perturbations
, if for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a
\delta=\delta(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|y_{0}\|<\delta
, and
P\in BV_{loc}([s_{0},+\infty))
is a left-continuous function on
(s_{0},+\infty)
such that

\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)<\delta,

then
\|y(t,s_{0},y_{0})\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
, where
y(\cdot,s_{0},y_{0})
is the unique solution of (
5.1
) with initial condition
y(s_{0},s_{0},y_{0})=y_{0}
.

•

Attractive with respect to perturbations
, if there exists
\delta_{0}>0
and for every
\varepsilon>0
there is a
T=T(\varepsilon)\geqslant 0
and
\gamma=\gamma(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|y_{0}\|<\delta_{0}
, and
P\in BV_{loc}([s_{0},+\infty))
is a left-continuous function on
(s_{0},+\infty)
such that

\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)<\gamma,

then
\|y(t,s_{0},y_{0})\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T(\varepsilon)
, where
y(\cdot,s_{0},y_{0})
is the unique solution of (
5.1
) with initial condition
y(s_{0},s_{0},y_{0})=y_{0}
.

•

Asymptotically stable with respect to perturbations
, if it is stable with respect to perturbations and attractive with respect to perturbations.

We continue with the reflection of Schwabik stated in
[
34
, Remark 10.7]
: “The stability with respect to perturbations is motivated by the desire that the solutions of the perturbed equation (
5.1
) be close to zero on a certain
[t_{0},t_{1}]
whenever the value
y(t_{0})
is close to zero and the perturbing term
P
in (
5.1
) is small in the sense that var
{}_{t_{0}}^{t_{1}}(P)
is small”.

Remark 5.2
.

When the perturbation
P
is identically null, we recover again the notion of stability given in Section 3. Specifically, considering
P\equiv 0
, the stability with respect to perturbations emulates the uniform stability, and the asymptotic stability with respect to perturbations emulates the uniform asymptotic stability from Definition
3.1
.

The following theorem shows the equivalence between the notion of variational stability and stability with respect to perturbations. For a proof of the next result, we refer to the reader
[
32
, Theorem 10.8]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

The trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is

•

Variationally stable if and only if it is stable with respect to perturbations.

•

Variationally attracting if and only if it is attractive with respect to perturbations.

•

Variationally asymptotically stable if and only if it is asymptotically stable with respect to perturbations.

The following theorem is also due to Schwabik and characterise the variational stability with respect to the transition matrix
U
given by (
2.8
). We will include the original proof for the reader’s convenience, see
[
35
, p.405]
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
A\in BV_{loc}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be continuous from the left and condition
(D)
holds. Then, the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is variationally stable if and only if the transition matrix
U(t,s_{0})
is bounded for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Assume that there exists a constant
C>0
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant C
, for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
. Since Theorem
5.3
, we will prove that the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is stable with respect to perturbations. Consider a function
y(\cdot,s_{0},y_{0})
which is a solution of the perturbed linear equation (
5.1
) with initial condition
y(s_{0},s_{0},y_{0})=y_{0}
.

From equation (
2.11
), the left continuity of
A
, and Theorem
2.12
item (e)-(ii), it follows that for every
t\geqslant s_{0}

\begin{split}\|y(t,s_{0},y_{0})\|&=\left\|U(t,s_{0})y_{0}+\int_{s_{0}}^{t}U(t,s){\rm d}[P(s)-P(s_{0})]+\sum_{s_{0}\leqslant\tau<t}\Delta^{+}U(t,\tau)\Delta^{+}P(\tau)\right\|\\
&\leqslant C\|y_{0}\|+C\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)+2C\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)\\
&\leqslant C\|y_{0}\|+3C\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P).\end{split}

Therefore, for a given
\varepsilon>0
, we can choose
\delta=\dfrac{\varepsilon}{4C+1}>0
, which satisfies

\|y_{0}\|<\delta\;\text{ and }\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)<\delta\;\Longrightarrow\|y(t,s_{0},y_{0})\|<\varepsilon,\qquad\text{for all }t\geqslant s_{0}.

Hence, the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is variationally stable.

On the other hand, assume that (
2.6
) is variationally stable. For
\varepsilon=1
, there exists a
\delta>0
such that if
x\colon[s_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of (
2.6
), i.e.
\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(x(s)-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{\rm d}[A(\xi)]x(\xi))=0
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
, with
\|x(s_{0})\|<\delta
, then
\|x(t,s_{0},x(s_{0}))\|<1
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
.

Therefore, for an arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|z\|\leqslant 1
, we have
\|U(t,s_{0})(\delta/2)z\|<1
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, because
U(t,s_{0})(\delta/2)z
is a solution of (
2.6
) with initial condition
(\delta/2)z
. Hence, we obtain

\|U(t,s_{0})\|=\sup_{\|z\|\leqslant 1}\|U(t,s_{0})z\|\leqslant 2/\delta,

for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, which implies the boundedness of the transition matrix.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

If we think about the previous Remarks
5.1
and
5.2
, along with a careful reading of the results obtained in Theorem
3.3
and Theorem
3.6
, it seems reasonable to expect a possible characterisation of the variational asymptotic stability in terms of the transition matrix
U
.

The following theorem is the main result of this section. We provide a characterisation of the asymptotic variational stability and the global uniform exponential stability, see Definition
3.2
.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
A\in BV_{loc}([0,+\infty),\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
be continuous from the left and condition
(D)
holds. Then, the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
) is variationally asymptotically stable if and only if there exist positive constants
\alpha,K>0
such that (
3.1
) holds for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Assume that there exist positive constants
\alpha,K>0
such that (
3.1
) holds for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
. Clearly, the transition matrix is bounded, with
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant K
, for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, and thus the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is variationally stable.

Since Theorem
5.3
, we will prove that the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is attractive with respect to perturbations. Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed, assume that
K>\varepsilon
, and let
y_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Consider a function
y(\cdot,s_{0},y_{0})
, which is a solution of the perturbed linear equation (
5.1
) with initial condition
y(s_{0},s_{0},y_{0})=y_{0}
, where
P\in BV_{loc}([s_{0},+\infty),\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a left-continuous function on
(s_{0},+\infty)
.

For every
t\geqslant s_{0}
we have the estimate

\begin{split}\|y(t,s_{0},y_{0})\|&=\left\|U(t,s_{0})y_{0}+\int_{s_{0}}^{t}U(t,s){\rm d}[P(s)-P(s_{0})]+\sum_{s_{0}\leqslant\tau<t}\Delta^{+}U(t,\tau)\Delta^{+}P(\tau)\right\|\\
&\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}\|y_{0}\|+3K\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)\\
.\end{split}

Let us consider
\delta_{0}=1
,
0<\gamma<\varepsilon/3K
, and
T(\varepsilon)=[\ln(K/(\varepsilon-3K\gamma))]/\alpha
. Therefore, it follows that for
\|y_{0}\|<\delta_{0}
, and
\displaystyle\sup_{t\geqslant s_{0}}\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(P)<\gamma
we get

\|y(t,s_{0},y_{0})\|\leqslant Ke^{-\alpha(t-s_{0})}+3K\gamma<\varepsilon,\qquad\text{ for all }t\geqslant s_{0}+T(\varepsilon).

Hence, we conclude that the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is attractive with respect to perturbations.

On the other hand, assume that the trivial solution of (
2.6
) is variationally asymptotically stable. Let
0<\varepsilon<1
be fixed. From the variational attracting assumption, there exists a
\delta_{0}>0
independent of
\varepsilon
, and there is a
T=T(\varepsilon)>0
such that if
x:[s_{0},+\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a solution of (
2.6
), i.e.
\text{var}_{s_{0}}^{t}(x(s)-\int_{s_{0}}^{s}{\rm d}[A(\xi)]x(\xi))=0
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}
, with
\|x(s_{0})\|<\delta_{0}
then

\|x(t,s_{0},x(s_{0}))\|=\|U(t,s_{0})x(s_{0})\|<\dfrac{\varepsilon\delta_{0}}{2},\qquad\text{ for all }t\geqslant s_{0}+T(\varepsilon).

Hence, for an arbitrary
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\|z\|\leqslant 1
we obtain
\|U(t,s_{0})(\delta_{0}/2)z\|<\varepsilon\delta_{0}/2
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T
, which in turn implies that
\|U(t,s_{0})z\|<\varepsilon
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}+T
. And we get

\|U(t,s_{0})\|=\sup_{\|z\|\leqslant 1}\|U(t,s_{0})z\|<\varepsilon,\qquad\text{ for all }t\geqslant s_{0}+T.

Now, from the variational stability assumption of (
2.6
) and Theorem
5.4
, we deduce the existence of a positive constant
C>0
such that
\|U(t,s_{0})\|\leqslant C
for all
t\geqslant s_{0}\geqslant 0
, and the rest of the proof follows exactly analogue to the final part of the proof given in Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The notion of uniform stability of the trivial solution of the homogenous GLDE (
2.6
) given in Section 3 is equivalent to the notion of variational stability, both concepts are comparable to the uniform boundedness of the transition matrix
U
defined by (
2.8
), namely, a bound independent of the initial time
s_{0}\geqslant 0
. In addition, the uniform asymptotic stability notion from Section 3 is equivalent to the variational asymptotic stability of the trivial solution of the homogeneous GLDE (
2.6
), and as we seen in Theorem
3.6
and Theorem
5.5
, both of these concepts are equivalent to the uniform exponential decay of the transition matrix described by (
3.1
), and consequently coincide with the global uniform exponential stability notion given in Definition
3.2
.
