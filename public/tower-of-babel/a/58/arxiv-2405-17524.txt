Reverse Shock Revisited in Cassiopeia A with Chandra
Yin Wu (吴垠), X. J. Yang (杨雪娟)

Abstract
Using data from the Chandra X-Ray Observatory, we revisited the reverse shock in the supernova remnant (SNR) Cassiopeia A. Based on the spectroscopic of a series of annuli in the northwest (NW) and southeast (SE), we get the radial profiles of the S/Si K α \alpha line flux ratio and Fe K α \alpha line centroid energy. They both show monotonic increase, confirming that the Si- and Fe-rich ejecta are heated by the reverse shock. The abrupt change of the S and Si line flux ratio is clearly observed in Cassiopeia A, leading to the determination of the reverse shock location ( ∼ \sim 1.71 ′ ± {}^{\prime}\pm 0.16 ′ and ∼ \sim 1.35 ′ ± {}^{\prime}\pm 0.18 ′ in the NW and SE, with respect to the central source). By comparing the radial profiles of S and Si line flux, we find that the reverse shock is moving outward in the frame of the observer, and the velocities are ∼ \sim 3950 ± \pm 210 km s -1 and ∼ \sim 2900 ± \pm 260 km s -1 in the NW and SE, respectively. In contrast, the velocities become ∼ \sim 1150 km s -1 (NW) and ∼ \sim 1300 km s -1 (SE) in the ejecta frame. Our measured reverse shock velocities are quite consistent with those obtained from the X-ray and/or optical images. It therefore supplies a crosscheck of the accuracy for the two available methods to measure the reverse shock velocity in SNRs. Both the location and the velocity of the reverse shock show apparent asymmetry, suggesting that the asymmetric explosion of the progenitor plays a key role in the interaction between the reverse shock and the ejecta, ultimately shaping complex features observed in SNRs.

I
Introduction

During the supernova (SN) explosion, the fast-moving ejecta propagates into the ambient medium, generating a shock wave known as forward shock. The forward shock accelerates, compresses, and heats the gas, giving rise to X-ray emission
[
35
]
. In turn, the shocked gas decelerates the ejecta, giving rise to a shock wave moving back toward the expansion center, called reverse shock. It is usually a strong source of thermal X-ray emission
[
23
]
and an excellent astrophysical laboratory for understanding the SN explosion properties
[
32
, e.g.,]
.

The X-ray line emission is closely related to the reverse shock heating history
[
23
]
. According to the reverse shock scenario, it passes through the outer layers of the ejecta earlier during the supernova remnant (SNR) evolution
[
4
,
39
]
. Therefore, the ejecta in the outer shells should be more highly ionized than those in the inner
[
5
]
, leading to the expected radial variation of ionization age
[
18
,
41
,
\tau=n_{e}t
, where
n_{e}
is the number density of the electron and
t
is the time since the shock heating,]
. However, it is difficult to obtain precise
\tau
of the plasma in a specific region, since the observed plasma usually consists of many small-scale knots and clumps with various ionization states due to the projection effects
[
20
,
28
]
. With this regard, it may be smoother to trace the reverse shock using the radial profiles of X-ray line emission properties, since such profiles average the effect of different ionization states over a larger area.

The radial profiles of Fe K
\alpha
centroid energy (
E_{\rm Fe}
), metal line emissivity, and S/Si K
\alpha
line flux ratio (
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
) have been widely used to study the reverse shock in SNRs
[
13
,
48
,
21
, e.g.,]
. The reverse shock may heat the ejecta in the outer region earlier, leading to a higher ionization degree and thus larger
E_{\rm Fe}
and
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}

[
48
,
21
]
.
Furthermore, the ejecta heated by the reverse shock dominates the thermal emission in SNRs. The metal line emissivity may rise sharply near the current location of the reverse shock
[
13
]
. Therefore, these properties at different radii will provide an opportunity to study the reverse shock heating history and constrain the location of the reverse shock.

Cassiopeia A (Cas A) is an ideal object to study the reverse shock. With the age of
\sim
350 yr
[
38
,
11
]
, it is one of the most well-known young core-collapse (CC) SNR, arising from asymmetric Type IIb explosion
[
19
,
31

. In this remnant, the reverse shock has not propagated into the most inner region, therefore it is possible to trace the time evolution of the reverse shock. Viewed in the X-ray, Cas A shows faint thin filaments with a radius of
\sim
2.5
′

[

and a bright emission ring with a radius of
\sim

[

\sim

[

9

44

[

,
15

7
,
17

. According to the theoretical framework, nucleosynthesis in CC SNe occurs in a staged manner
[

,
37

. Fe-rich ejecta is expected to locate inside Si. However, spatially resolved spectroscopy shows that there is an overturn between the Fe-rich and Si-rich ejecta

It is quite challenging to trace the reverse shock in young SNRs since the reverse shocked ejecta seems to be in knots or clumps

Moreover, the velocity of the reverse shock in Cas A remains to be studied. The theoretical model has predicted that the reverse shock is moving outward during the early phase of SNR

II.3
Deprojection

The surface flux of Cas A is the integration of the flux per unit volume. To eliminate the projection effect, we characterize the structure of Cas A as a spherical object
and obtain the radial profiles of the deprojected Si flux (
F_{\rm Si}
) and S flux (
F_{\rm S}
).
Under the spherical assumption, the integral flux
F(r)
is defined as
[
46
,
14
,
21
, e.g.,]
:

{}F(r)=\int_{r}^{R}F_{d}(r^{\prime})\dfrac{r^{\prime}}{\sqrt{r^{\prime 2}-r^{2}}}dr^{\prime},

(1)

where
R
is the outer radius of the object,
F_{d}(r^{\prime})
the deprojected flux. The deprojection was performed from the outer radius to the inner radius by Equation
1
using the Lucy-Richardson technique
[
22
]
, and stopped when encountering the negative value.
We note here that although Cas A is very asymmetric, the deprojected radial profiles show little difference with the unprocessed ones.
The reason might be that the asymmetry of Cas A is mainly on a large scale, i.e., in different quadrants.
When it comes to the individual quadrant, e.g., SE, it is symmetric and therefore the projection is not significant.

IV
Discussion

IV.1
The Reverse Shock Heating History

We observe an overall radial monotonic increase of
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
in the NW and SE, as shown in Figure
4
.
According to
[
21
]
, a higher
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
can be related to a higher temperature and/or ionization age.
Since there is no abrupt change of temperature in the NW and SE
[
45
,
50
,
17
, e.g.,]
,
the most reasonable explanation of the radial monotonic increase is the radial increase of
\tau
.

Following
[
21
]
, we investigate the contribution of
n_{e}
and
t
to the variation of
\tau
.
In the VNEI model, the normalization is in the units of
\dfrac{10^{-14}}{4\pi d^{2}}\int{n_{e}n_{H}}dV
cm
-5
scaled with the EM,
where
d
is the angular diameter distance to the source in the units of cm.
Assuming fully ionized H-dominant plasma (
n_{e}\approx
1.2
n_{H}
)
[
34
]
,
EM is proportional to
n_{e}^{2}
.
Therefore, the radial profile of
n_{e}
can be simply estimated from that of the thermal continuum.
We calculate the thermal continuum fluxes in the 0.85–1.5 keV plus 3.0–10.0 keV bands
with the nonthermal contribution eliminated.
The nonthermal emission can be estimated by the extrapolation of the
4.2–6.0 keV spectra, since in this energy range it is nonthermal-dominated.
In the extrapolation, we use an index of 3.1 for all the spectra,
which is the average index of the entire remnant
[
14
,
33
]
.

N_{H}
is set as 1.3
\times
10
22
, a mean value for the absorption in the NW and SE
[
50
,
17
]
.
We also do a deprojection to the thermal continuum fluxes.

The results are plotted in the bottom panel of Figure
4
. We can see that the radial profiles of the thermal continuum flux are distinctly different from
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
. Thus, to explain the observed monotonic increase of
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
,
t
should play the key role, i.e., the ejecta in the exterior was heated earlier, implying that the reverse shock does pass through and heat the ejecta.

The radial profiles of
E_{\rm Fe}
also show monotonic increases. Similarly, we can deduce that the Fe-rich ejecta has been heated by the reverse shock. The reason is that a larger
E_{\rm Fe}
corresponds to a higher temperature and/or
\tau

[
48
]
,
and no significant change in temperature is seen in these regions
[
45
,
50
,
17
, e.g.,]
.

Though the monotonic increase is observed in the radial profiles of both
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
and
E_{\rm Fe}
, the radius range is quite different.
Since the range of the monotonic increase of
E_{\rm Fe}
is far outside the average position of the reverse shock,
we suggest that this range may not be the precise position where the reverse shock heats the Fe-rich ejecta.
This is in good agreement with some special models to explain the interaction between the reverse shock and the ejecta,
such as the “piston” model
[
8
]
and the Fe-rich plumes
[
25
,
26
]
.
The Fe-rich ejecta may be passed through and heated by the reverse shock in the interior of Cas A together with the Si-rich ejecta.
After the propagation of the reverse shock, the denser Fe-rich ejecta is slightly decelerated
and continues to move outward the remnant at a higher speed (at least higher than that of the Si-rich ejecta).
Finally, the Fe-rich ejecta will break through the average position of the reverse shock and occupy the outer
region of the Si-rich ejecta, resulting in the observed spatial inversion
between the Fe-rich and Si-rich ejecta
[
15
,
17
,
40
]
.
Under this situation, the Fe-rich ejecta may be located outside the average radius of the reverse shock,
leading to larger radius ranges. Therefore, the observed large range of monotonic increase in the radial profiles of
E_{\rm Fe}
is acceptable.

Since the variation of
F_{\rm Si}
,
F_{\rm S}
, and
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
begin to be distinctly different in the exterior of the reverse shock,
the reverse shock may interact with the ejecta differently in the NW and SE. The relatively high
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
,
as well as the overall higher
E_{\rm Fe}
, suggests a higher ionization degree in the SE than in the NW.
Different ionization degrees may be related to different reverse shock actions caused by different
ejecta densities
[
40
, e.g.,]
. The reverse shock may pass through the ejecta with lower
density more quickly and fully heat the ejecta, resulting in a higher ionization degree.
Accordingly, the ejecta density in the NW should be higher than that in the SE, which consists well with our proper motion measurements.
Nevertheless, due to the complex real density distribution of Cas A, higher quality data, such as the James Webb Space Telescope,
are needed for further understanding the interaction of the ejecta with the reverse shock
[
24
,
42
]
.

IV.2
The Proper Motion of the Reverse Shock

We quantitatively estimate the proper motion (
\mu
) and velocity of the reverse shock by a
\chi^{2}
-test method described in
[
36
]
.
We take the observation in 2000 (ObsID. 114) as the calibration standard, and then artificially move
the radial profiles of
F_{\rm Si}
and
F_{\rm S}
for the four other observations at different epochs.
Then we search for the best match shifts (

IV.3
The Deceleration to the Ejecta for the Reverse Shock

The ejecta will be decelerated when it passes through the reverse shock.
Such deceleration can be quantified more precisely if the effect of ejecta expansion is reduced,
and the reverse shock velocity in the ejecta frame
v_{\rm{rs,ej}}
will be a good indicator.
A larger
v_{\rm{rs,ej}}
means more deceleration
[
29
]
.
We therefore make a rough estimate of
v_{\rm{rs,ej}}

According to the reverse shock dynamics
[
29
,
32
,
44

v_{\rm{rs,ej}}
is defined as:

v_{\rm{rs,ej}}=|{v_{\rm{ej}}}-v_{\rm{rs,obs}}|,

(4)

where
v_{\rm{ej}}
is the velocity of the unshocked ejecta.

v_{\rm{ej}}

{v_{\rm{ej}}}=R_{\rm{rs}}/t_{\rm SNR}

assuming the ejecta will be free expanding until encountering the reverse shock.
Here
R_{\rm{rs}}
is the radius of the reverse shock, and
t_{\rm SNR}
the age of the remnant.
Our estimated location of the reverse shock in Section
III.2
corresponds to
the angular distance of
\sim

in the NW and
\sim

in the SE,

We wondering if the different deceleration arises from different densities of the medium that the ejecta encounter.
The shock velocity and the density of the unshocked ejecta are related by the following equation given by
[
44
]
:

\rho_{\rm{ej,0}}v_{\rm{rs,ej}}^{2}=\xi\rho_{\rm{csm,0}}v_{\rm{fs}}^{2},

(5)

Therefore, different reverse shock velocities should be attributed to different density distribution

A higher

(smaller
\rho_{\rm{ej,0}}

\rho_{\rm{csm,0}}

It has been suggested that different reverse shock velocities between the NW and SE might be associated
with different densities of the ejecta
[
9
,
40
]
.
When the reverse shock generates and passes through, the ejecta with lower density may suffer more deceleration.
Recently, considering the real density distribution of circumstellar medium,
a series of 3D hydrodynamic simulations have reproduced asymmetric reverse shock velocities in the NW and SE of Cas A
[
27

.
Their results suggest that the asymmetric velocities of the reverse shock are caused by the interaction
of the remnant with an asymmetric dense circumstellar shell.
However, their simulations do not reproduce all the sub-features in the velocity
profiles of the reverse shock measured by
[
44
]
.
Therefore, the real density distribution of Cas A is very complex and needs further research.

V
Summary

In this paper, we revisited the reverse shock in Cas A.
We perform a spatially resolved spectroscopy of Cas A with
Chandra
observations
at different epochs.
We give the deprojected radial profiles of
F_{\rm Si}
,
F_{\rm S}
,
E_{\rm Fe}
and
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
, respectively.

\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
increases monotonically with radius, indicating that the Si-rich ejecta has experienced the propagation of the reverse shock.
Similarly, the monotonic increase of the
E_{\rm Fe}
suggests reverse shock heating in the Fe-rich ejecta.
In the SE, overall larger
E_{\rm Fe}
and
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
indicate higher ionization degree.
This might be related to different reverse shock actions. In the SE, the reverse shock may pass through the ejecta more quickly and fully heat the ejecta.

Based on
\gamma_{\rm S/Si}
, the reverse shock location is estimated to be
R\sim
1.71
{}^{\prime}\pm
0.16
′
in the NW and

R\sim
1.35
{}^{\prime}\pm
0.18
′
in the SE with respect to the central X-ray source.
Within the confidence range, our estimated location is well consistent with previous results.
The reverse shock velocities in different directions are also given, by comparing the radial profiles of
F_{\rm Si}

and
F_{\rm S}
at different epochs by the
\chi^{2}
-test method.
In the frame of the observer, the reverse shock is moving outward at
\sim
3950
\pm
210 km s
-1

and
\sim
2900
\pm
260 km s
-1
in the NW and SE, respectively.
We marginally detected the reverse shock deceleration with a factor of
\sim
5% from 2010–2019.
In the ejecta frame, the reverse shock velocities are
\sim
1150 km s
-1
and
\sim
1300 km s
-1
in the NW and SE, respectively.
The velocity in the ejecta frame suggests that the ejecta was more decelerated by the reverse shock in the SE,
and we suggest that
this should arise from the larger density that the ejecta encounters or the smaller density of the ejecta itself.

Overall, both the proper motion and heating history of the reverse shock show asymmetric distribution.
This should be closely related with the asymmetric explosion of the progenitor.
During the explosion process, there may be an asymmetric mechanism that leads to asymmetric density distribution of the ejecta.
Or the explosion occurred in an asymmetric circumstellar medium.
Although we cannot conclude what the real situation is,
our results provide new insights on the effect of the asymmetric explosion in the progenitor of Cas A.
