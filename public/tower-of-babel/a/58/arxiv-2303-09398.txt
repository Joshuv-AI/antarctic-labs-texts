Abstract
. An n × n n\times n matrix M = [ m i ​ j ] M=[m_{ij}] with m i ​ j ∈ U n = { 1 , 2 , … , n } m_{ij}\in U_{n}=\{1,2,\ldots,n\} will be called a cycle matrix if ( U n , ⋅ ) (U_{n},\cdot) is a cycle set, where i ⋅ j = m i ​ j i\cdot j=m_{ij} . We study these matrices in this article. Using these matrices, we give some recipes to construct solutions, which include the multipermutation level 2 2 solutions. Introduction

V. G. Drinfeld suggested the study of the set-theoretic solution to the quantum Yang-Baxter equation (QYBE) (see
[
Dr90
]
).
A
set-theoretic solution to the QYBE
is an ordered pair
(X,R)
, where
X
is a set and
R:X\times X\longrightarrow X\times X
is a map, satisfying

\displaystyle R^{12}R^{13}R^{23}=R^{23}R^{13}R^{12},

with
R^{ij}:X\times X\times X\longrightarrow X\times X\times X
being the map acting on the
(i,j)
-th position by
R
.
This has been an important research topic for the last two decades after the breakthrough
[
EtScSo99
]
by P. Etingof, T. Schedler and A. Soloviev.
Although such solutions were constructed by A. D. Weinstein and P. Xu (see
[
WeXu
]
) and by J. H. Lu, M. Yan and Y. C. Zhu (see
[
LuYaZh00
]
) independently,
the paper by Etingof
et. al.
further studies solutions with additional conditions of
nondegeneracy
of
R
(i.e. if
R(x,y)=(g_{x}(y),f_{y}(x))
then
g_{x},f_{y}
are bijective for all
x,y\in X
) and
R
being involutive (i.e.
R^{2}=\text{Id}_{X\times X}
) and derive powerful consequences. In this current article, by a
solution
, we will mean finite non-degenerate involutive set-theoretic solution to the QYBE (in short, SYBE). Before proceeding further we will mention a few definitions. The
permutation group of a solution
is the group defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=\langle f_{x}:x\in X\rangle.

A solution
(X,R)
is said to be
decomposable
if there exists disjoint subsets
X_{1},X_{2}
of
X
such that
R(X_{i}\times X_{i})\subseteq X_{i}\times X_{i}
,
(X_{i},R|_{X_{i}\times X_{i}})
is a solution and
X=X_{1}\bigsqcup X_{2}
.
The solution will be called
indecomposable
in case of the non-existence of such a pair
X_{1},\penalty\ X_{2}
(it is equivalent that the natural action of the permutation group
\mathcal{G}
on
X
is transitive
[
EtScSo99
]
).
In the paper
[
EtScSo99
]
, the authors have shown that there exists a unique (up to
isomorphism) indecomposable solution order
p
, where
p
is
a prime (see section
2.5
and
2.6
therein). After that several attempts have been made to look after and analyze solutions in case
|X|
is a composite number.
For example, W. Rump has studied the decomposability of square-free solutions in
[
Ru05
]
, S. Ramírez and L. Vendramin have studied the decomposability of solutions in
[
RaVe22
]
.
Recently in an article Agore, Chirvasitu and, Militaru have proved many counting results for solutions of Frobenius-Separability (FS) type using the category of pointed Kimura semigroups (see
[
AgChMi23
]
).
Our goal here is to introduce counting methods for SYBEs using algebraic tools. Furthermore, our results give glimpses of the structure of solutions in the case of isomorphic classes of decomposable solutions, in terms of these matrices.

The concept of cycle sets was introduced by W. Rump in
[

]
and has been a source of SYBEs (see
[

]

\displaystyle(x\cdot y)\cdot(x\cdot z)=(y\cdot x)\cdot(y\cdot z),

An important result about cycle sets states that there is a bijection between non-degenerate cycle sets and set of all SYBEs (
[

For two solutions

and

We further restrain ourselves to solutions coming from cycle sets.

A solution
(X,\cdot)

for some permutation

Results in generalities

We start with the definition of a cycle matrix. This will be followed by a few examples and properties of such a matrix.

Definition 2.1

(Cycle matrix)
.

An
n\times n
matrix
M=(m_{ij})
with all entries from
\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
is said to be a
cycle matrix
if
(U_{n},\cdot)
is a non-degenerate cycle set and,
i\cdot j=m_{ij}
. Two such matrices
(m_{ij}),(m^{\prime}_{ij})
will be called
isomorphic
if the corresponding cycle sets are isomorphic to each other. The set of all
n\times n
cycle matrices will be denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{n}
. Further, a cycle matrix will be called
indecomposable
, if the corresponding SYBE (coming from the cycle set of the matrix) is indecomposable.

Lemma 2.3
.

For any permutation
\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n}
, there is a cycle matrix whose diagonal is
\sigma
. In particular
\mathscr{C}_{n}\neq\emptyset
.

Proof.

Consider the permutation solution corresponding to
\sigma
. Note that in that case, the cycle matrix will be

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\sigma(1)&\sigma(2)&\cdots&\sigma(n)\\
\sigma(1)&\sigma(2)&\cdots&\sigma(n)\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sigma(1)&\sigma(2)&\cdots&\sigma(n)\end{matrix}\right].

This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Cycle matrices are not symmetric.

Proof.

Consider the cycle set
(X,\cdot)
arising from the cycle matrix. Note that for
i\neq j
, by the non-degeneracy we have that
i\cdot i\neq j\cdot j
for all
i\neq j
. If possible let us assume
m_{ij}=m_{ji}
for some
i\neq j
. Then we have that

\displaystyle(i\cdot j)\cdot(i\cdot i)

\displaystyle=(j\cdot i)\cdot(j\cdot i)

\displaystyle=(i\cdot j)\cdot(j\cdot j),

which is not possible, since
i\cdot i\neq j\cdot j
.
∎

Note that a cycle matrix
(a_{ij})
is not only non-symmetric but also
a_{ij}\neq a_{ji}
for all
i,\penalty\ j.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
(X,\cdot)
and
(Y,\star)
are two cycle sets, then
(X\times Y,\circ)
is a cycle set, where
(x_{1},y_{1})\circ(x_{2},y_{2})=(x_{1}.x_{2},y_{1}\star y_{2}).

The cycle set

We connect this with the concept of the tensor product of matrices. Recall that, for two matrices

\displaystyle\left(A\otimes B\right)_{ij}=\left(a_{ij}B\right).

Proposition 2.9
.

Consider two cycle matrices
A\in\mathscr{C}_{m}
and
B\in\mathscr{C}_{n}
. If the corresponding cycle sets are given by
(U_{m},\cdot)
and
(U_{n},\star)
respectively, then the matrix

is a cycle matrix whose cycle set is isomorphic to
(U_{m}\times U_{n},\circ)

Proof.

Recall from

2.8
, the operation ‘
\circ

is determined by
(a,b)\circ(c,d)=(a\cdot c,b\star d)
, for all

and

We need to relabel the elements

Define

by

\displaystyle\varphi(i,j)=(i-1)n+j.

Clearly, this function is bijective. Indeed
\varphi(i_{1},j_{1})=\varphi(i_{2},j_{2})

\displaystyle x\bullet y=\varphi(\varphi^{-1}(x)\circ\varphi^{-1}(y)),

∎

Remark 2.10
.

Chose any column say
C_{j}
of a cycle matrix
(m_{ij})
