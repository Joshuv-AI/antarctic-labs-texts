Envy-free matchings with cost-controlled quotas
Girija Limaye, Meghana Nasre

Abstract
We consider the problem of assigning agents to programs in the presence of two-sided preferences, commonly known as the Hospital Residents problem. In the standard setting each program has a rigid upper-quota which cannot be violated. Motivated by applications where quotas are governed by resource availability, we propose and study the problem of computing optimal matchings with cost-controlled quotas – denoted as the 𝖢𝖢𝖰 {\sf CCQ} setting. In the 𝖢𝖢𝖰 \sf CCQ setting we have a cost associated with every program which denotes the cost of matching a single agent to the program. Our goal is to compute a matching that matches all agents, respects the preference lists of agents and programs and is optimal with respect to the cost criteria. We consider envy-freeness as a notion of optimality and study two optimization problems with respect to the costs – minimize the total cost ( 𝖬𝖨𝖭𝖲𝖴𝖬 \sf MINSUM ) and minimize the maximum cost at a program ( 𝖬𝖨𝖭𝖬𝖠𝖷 \sf{MINMAX} ). We show that there is a sharp contrast in the complexity status of these two problems – 𝖬𝖨𝖭𝖬𝖠𝖷 \sf{MINMAX} is polynomial time solvable whereas 𝖬𝖨𝖭𝖲𝖴𝖬 \sf MINSUM is 𝖭𝖯 \sf NP -hard and hard to approximate within a constant factor unless 𝖯 = 𝖭𝖯 \sf P=\sf NP even under severe restrictions. On the positive side, we present approximation algorithms for the 𝖬𝖨𝖭𝖲𝖴𝖬 \sf MINSUM for the general case and a special hard case. We achieve the approximation guarantee for the special case via a technically involved linear programming (LP) based algorithm. We remark that our LP is for the general case of the problem.

Introduction

The problem of computing optimal matchings in the many-to-one setting and two-sided preferences
has been extensively investigated since it is applicable in several real-world applications like
assigning students to schools
[
2
]
or elective courses
[

,

[

This setting, commonly called as the Hospital Residents (

For a vertex

Each vertex

If

In the standard setting a program

A

\sf HR

Let

matched to program

A program

\mid\hskip-2.84544ptM(p)\hskip-2.84544pt\mid

Stability

Definition 1
(Stable matchings in
\sf HR

A pair

It is well known that every
\sf HR

admits a stable matching and all stable matchings are of the same size

Size of a matching plays a very important role in real-world applications where leaving agents unmatched is undesirable and sometimes even

In applications like school choice
[

Relaxing stability in order to enable larger size matchings has been investigated in literature

5

In all such works, there is an inherent assumption that the quotas of programs
are
rigid
and cannot be compromised.

However, there are real-world applications where quotas
are determined by logistic considerations like resource availability, classroom size and these may be flexible.
For instance, every semester elective allocation for under-graduate students at an educational institute
happens via an automated procedure and once the preferences
are available to the academic office, course instructors are consulted to adjust class capacities if appropriate.
A recent work by Gajulapalli et al.
[
10
]
studies the
school choice problem in a two round-setting – in the first round the quotas given as input are considered rigid. In
the second round though, some schools may increase their quota as suggested by the mechanism
in order to match

Motivated by applications where
\mathcal{A}
-perfectness (matching

we introduce and study the setting where costs control the number of agents matched to a program.
We denote this as the Cost Controlled Quota (
\sf CCQ
) setting.
An instance
H=(\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{P},E)
in the
\sf CCQ
setting is similar to an
\sf HR

except that programs do not have input quotas,

a program

denoting
the cost of matching an agent to
p

A matching
M\subseteq E
in the
\sf CCQ
setting is
an assignment of agents to programs
where a program
p
can be matched to as many agents as possible in
\mathcal{N}(p)

and the number of agents assigned is controlled by costs.
If a program is not assigned any agents in a matching, we call the program as being
closed
.
Throughout, our goal is to compute an
\mathcal{A}
-perfect matching which is optimal
with respect to preferences as well as costs.
We remark that a very recent work by Santhini et al.
[
1
]
studies the
cost-based setting for matchings with one-sided preferences. However, the
problems considered in
[
1
]
are very different from ours.

We first define the notion of optimality
in the
\sf CCQ
setting w.r.t. the preferences and subsequently with respect to the costs.
Stability is inherently defined using the input quotas.
Envy-freeness
[
25
]

a natural substitute since it is defined

In absence of input quotas, envy-freeness is equivalent to stability.

Given a matching

Our Contributions:

This is the first work that investigates the cost-controlled quotas
under two-sided preferences. We present an efficient algorithm for the

\sf{MINMAX}
problem whereas in a sharp contrast, the
\sf MINSUM
turns out to be
\sf NP
-hard under
severe restrictions. To address the hardness, we present a novel linear program for the
\sf MINSUM
problem
and give primal dual approximation algorithm for a special case.
We state our results formally below.

Theorem 1.1

The
\sf{MINMAX}
problem is solvable in
O(m\log{m})
time where
m=\mid\hskip-2.84544ptE\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
.

Theorem 1.2

The following hardness results hold for the
\sf MINSUM
problem.

1.

The
\sf MINSUM
problem is strongly
\sf NP
-hard even when every agent has a preference list of length
exactly
f\geq 2
, there is a master list
ordering on agents and programs and the instance has two distinct costs.

2.

The
\sf MINSUM
problem cannot be approximated within a factor
\frac{7}{6}-\epsilon,\epsilon>0

even when the instance has three distinct costs, unless
\sf P=\sf NP
.

We say that the instance has a
master list

[
17
]
on agents if there exists a fixed ordering of agents such that the preference lists of every program obeys that ordering. A similar definition holds for a master list on programs.
In the
\sf CCQ
setting since
\mathcal{A}
-perfectness is guaranteed, it is natural for agents to submit short preference lists.
However, since there is a guarantee that every agent is matched, the central authority
is likely to impose a minimum requirement on the length of the preference list
[
14
]
.
We further note that it is easy to compute the optimal solution for the
\sf MINSUM
problem
under the two extreme scenarios
– when the preference lists are unit length or when the preference lists are complete.

We complement our hardness results with the following approximation algorithms.
Let
{\sf CCQ_{c1,c2}}
denote the
\sf CCQ
instance with two distinct costs
c_{1}
and
c_{2}

such that
0\leq c_{1}<c_{2}
.
Let
\ell_{a}
(respectively
\ell_{p}
) denote the length of the longest preference
list of an agent (respectively a program).

Theorem 1.3

The
\sf MINSUM
problem admits an
\ell_{a}
-approximation algorithm on
{\sf CCQ_{c1,c2}}
instances.

We achieve our approximation guarantee via a technically involved linear programming (LP) based algorithm and show that our analysis is tight.
We remark that our LP is for the general
\sf MINSUM
problem but the approximation guarantee is for the restricted setting.
In many real-world applications,
\ell_{a}
is typically small, in many cases a constant
[
16
,
15
]
.

Finally, we present two simple approximation algorithms for
\sf MINSUM
on general instances.

Theorem 1.4

The
\sf MINSUM
problem admits the following approximation algorithms.

1.

a linear time
\ell_{p}
-approximation algorithm.

2.

a
\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approximation algorithm.

The analysis of our
\ell_{p}
-approximation algorithm uses a natural lower-bound on the
\sf MINSUM
problem. We also present a family of instances which shows that
\ell_{p}
is the best guarantee that can be achieved using the particular lower-bound.
We establish
that an optimal solution of the
\sf{MINMAX}
problem also serves as a lower-bound for the
\sf MINSUM
problem on the same instance and this gives us the
\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approximation algorithm.

Empirical Evaluation.

We implement our
\ell_{p}
-approximation and
\mid\hskip-3.41418pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-3.41418pt\mid
-approximation algorithms
presented in our paper for the general
\sf CCQ

Relation to other models.

There are alternate ways to formulate an optimization problem in the
\sf CCQ
setting:
(i) given a total budget
\mathcal{B}
,
compute a largest envy-free matching with cost at most
\mathcal{B}
.
(ii) given an
\sf HR
instance and a cost for every program,
augment
the input quotas to compute
an
\mathcal{A}
-perfect envy-free matching with minimum total cost.
The
\sf NP
-hardness for both these problems can be proved
by easily modifying the
\sf NP
-hardness reduction for
\sf MINSUM
.
As mentioned earlier, Gajulapalli et al.
[
10
]
consider the school choice problem
in a two-round setting.
In the second round, their goal is to match
all
agents
in a particular set derived from the matching in the first round and they need to match them in an envy-free manner (called stability preserving in their work).
The
\sf CCQ
setting generalizes the matching problem in round-2 as follows:
If
c(p)
denotes the cost of matching an agent to a program in round-2 then
\sf MINSUM

computes an
\mathcal{A}
-perfect envy-free matching where the total cost of matching agents in round-2 is minimized.
Let
d(p)
denote the deviation of program
p
, that is, the additional number of agents
matched to
p
in round-2 beyond its input quota
q(p)
.
Then by setting
c(p)=1
for every program, the
\sf{MINMAX}
problem computes an
\mathcal{A}
-perfect envy-free
matching in round-2 such that maximum deviation of a program is minimized.
We remark that in
[
10
]
the authors state that a variant of
\sf MINSUM
problem (Problem 33, Section 7)
is
\sf NP
-hard. However, they do not investigate the problem in detail.

2.2
Challenges

A standard primal-dual approach for the
\sf MINSUM
problem would be to begin with
a dual feasible solution. The algorithm then repeatedly updates the dual till we obtain a primal feasible solution using the tight edges w.r.t. to
the dual setting.
We illustrate the challenges in using such an approach for the general
\sf MINSUM

\ell_{a}
-approximation algorithm for
\sf MINSUM
on
{\sf CCQ_{c1,c2}}

In this section we show that when the
\sf CCQ
instance has only two distinct costs
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
,
we are able to circumvent the challenges and obtain an
\ell_{a}
-approximation algorithm for the
\sf MINSUM
problem.
We recall from Theorem
1.2
that even in this restricted setting,
the problem remains
NP
-hard.

High-level idea of the algorithm.

Our LP based algorithm begins with an initial
feasible dual setting and an envy-free matching
M
which need not be
\mathcal{A}
-perfect.
As long as
M
is not
\mathcal{A}
-perfect, we pick an unmatched agent
a
and increase the dual variable
y_{a}
.
We show that for an unmatched agent such an increase is possible and
all
edges incident on
a
become tight due to the update.
However, none of the edges incident on
a
may be matchable (since for every
p\in\mathcal{N}(a)
,
thresh(p)\neq a
).
Under the restricted setting of two distinct costs we ensure that after a bounded number of updates to the

z
variables,
at least one edge incident on
a
is matchable.
Throughout we maintain the following invariants with respect to the matching
M
.

•

M
is envy-free, not necessarily
\mathcal{A}
-perfect and every matched edge is tight.

•

For an agent
a

,
either (i)
(a,p)

is tight and
thresh(p)\neq a
or (ii)
slack(a,p)=c_{2}-c_{1}
.

We remark that when the matching is modified, thresholds may change,
due to which a tight, non-matchable edge may become matchable.
As long as there exists such an edge, we match it.
This is achieved by the
free-promotions
routine.
The free-promotions routine checks if there exists a matchable edge
(a,p)
.
If there is no such edge, the routine terminates.
Otherwise, it matches
(a,p)
, re-computes the threshold agents and repeats the search.
Checking for a matchable edge and computing threshold agents takes

O(m)
time where
m
is the number of edges in the underlying graph. Since, no agent is demoted in this process,
the free-promotions routine runs in
O(m^{2})

6
Experiments

We present empirical results for the algorithms presented in our paper
on data sets constructed from real world data as well as on synthetically generated data sets.
The evaluations are performed on a 64-bit machine running 5.13.0-30-generic ubuntu kernel.
The machine has 32GB of RAM and Intel(R) Xeon(R) Silver 4210R 2.40GHz CPU with 20 cores.
Our algorithms are implemented and executed using Python 3.8.
We use IBM CPLEX solver academic version
for solving the Integer Linear Program.

6.1
Data sets

Since we introduce the cost-based allocation under two-sided preference setting,
we do not have
\sf CCQ

We generate

We use real
\sf HR
data sets (

the preferences of students (agents) and courses (programs)
collected at an educational institute
over a period of three semesters. Each student ranks a subset of courses and

The courses in these data sets have a fixed input quota associated with them.

We generate synthetic
\sf HR

The generator takes as input the number of students (agents), the number of programs (courses) and the length of preference list of

6.2

We convert the

q(p)

In practice, a program with high demand, that is, large value of

We have three cost functions as described below.
The

Each of

of distinct costs. Both the cost functions generate the same number of distinct costs. In the
linear

the absolute cost values span a larger range.
Let

.

•

median(c).

The cost function

c\geq 1
.
Let
m
be the median of the set of ratios
\{ratio(p)\mid p\in\mathcal{P}\}

c(p)=0

c(p)=c
.

•

linear.
Order programs using the distinct ratios
ratio(p)
in increasing order and assign indices
0,1,\ldots
to the programs in that order. Then assign
c(p)=j
if index of
p
is
j
.

•

exponential(c).

The cost function
exponential
is parameterized by a constant
c\geq 2
.
Order programs using the distinct ratios
ratio(p)
in increasing order and assign indices
0,1,\ldots
to the programs in that order. Then assign
c(p)=c^{j}
if index of
p
is
j
.

In our experiments, we have selected
c=10
for the
median
cost function and
c=2
for the
exponential
cost function.
Additionally, we have evaluated our algorithms for the instances generated by setting
c=5
and
c=100

We omit the detailed results for these settings since the results follow a similar pattern as the results we have presented for our selected choice of

Parameters related to the corresponding
\sf HR
instance.

Since we derive our
\sf CCQ
instances from
\sf HR
instances, we evaluate the output matching with respect to
the original
\sf HR
instance.
Recall that our output matchings are envy-free.
However, an agent may prefer program
p
over its matched partner in the output matching
such that
p
is under-subscribed w.r.t. the input quota in the corresponding
\sf HR
instance.
Note that such blocking pairs are not relevant in the
\sf CCQ
instance since input quotas are absent.
The following two parameters capture this.

1.

bp%
: the percentage of the number of blocking pairs. Let
bp
denote the number of blocking pairs
w.r.t. the output matching
M
and the input quotas and
m
denote the number of edges in the instance. Then

bp\%=\frac{bp\cdot 100}{m-\mid M\mid}=\frac{bp\cdot 100}{m-\mid\mathcal{A}\mid}
since
M
is
\mathcal{A}
-perfect.

2.

ba%
: the number of blocking agents. Let
ba
denote the number of blocking agents
w.r.t. the output matching
M
and the input quotas. Then

ba\%=\frac{ba\cdot 100}{\mid\mathcal{A}\mid}
.

In order to achieve
\mathcal{A}
-perfectness, the output matching in the
\sf CCQ
instance
derived from the
\sf HR
instance is expected to violate the input quotas,
that is,
\mid\hskip-2.84544ptM(p)\hskip-2.84544pt\mid>q(p)
for
some program
p
.
This is the price that the
\sf CCQ
setting pays in order to achieve

\mathcal{A}
-perfectness.
We compute the percentage of total
violation
of the output matching.

3.

vio%
: Let
violation
of program
p
be
the difference between
\mid\hskip-2.84544ptM(p)\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
and
q(p)
if positive, undefined otherwise.
The total violation is the sum of individual violation of the programs whose violation is defined.
The total input quota is the sum of input quotas of programs whose violation is defined.
Then
vio\%=\frac{\text{total violation}\cdot 100}{\text{total input quota}}
.

6.5
Summary of the empirical evaluations

As mentioned earlier, Table
2
and Table
3
report our results.
In each column, the symbols
(\uparrow)
and
(\downarrow)
respectively indicate whether the larger or smaller value
of the corresponding parameter is better.

We make the following observations from our results.

•

The approximation ratio for all three algorithms
(
\sf ALG
, Algorithm
2
and the

\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approximation algorithm) is upper bounded by 2.5
except for the data set
S1
.
Although, for
S1
and the
median
cost function, the absolute value of the ratio is large (around 16 for
Algorithm
2
and around 19 for
\sf ALG
) we note
that the performance is significantly better than the theoretical guarantee. Furthermore even on this instance, the

\mid\hskip-3.98337pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-3.98337pt\mid
-approx ratio is bounded by 2.5.
In all our data sets, among the two
\ell_{p}
-approximation algorithms,
Algorithm
2
always performs better than
\sf ALG
in terms of the approximation ratio.
However, as seen in Section
4.1
neither of them is strictly better than the other.

•

All our algorithms are simple to implement and the maximum speedup is of the order of
10^{3}
. The speedup is inverse of
column
time ratio
in Table
2
and Table
3
.
It is seen that there is a variation in the speedups obtained on different instances – this is attributed to the
variation in the absolute time the LP
OPT
takes on specific data sets.
For example in the data set

S3
and
linear
cost function, the CPLEX
solver takes a prohibitively large time (more than 3 hours).

•

The average rank and the percentage of agents matched to their top choices are parameters of practical interest
in real-world applications like course allocation, or job assignment. Since the output matching in the
\sf CCQ
setting is
\mathcal{A}
-perfect, it
is expected that average rank is small (smaller value is better) and percentage of agents matched to top choices is large.
We observe that for real data sets all the approximation algorithms perform very well with respect to these two parameters.
For synthetic data sets, there is variation across different algorithms. We note that for a fixed instance
the LP
{\sf OPT}
and
\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approx have similar values and
\sf ALG
and Algorithm
2
outperform them on most instances with respect to the two parameters.

•

The parameters
\succ
A-opt stable%
and
\prec
P-opt stable%
allow us to compare our output with any stable matching in the original
\sf HR
instance.
For all data sets except
R3
we observe that we consistently have large values for the first parameter and small values for the second.
For the data sets
R3
, the underlying
\sf HR
instance admits a close to
\mathcal{A}
-perfect stable matching. This possibly explains the
unusual values for the two parameters for all cost functions corresponding to
R3
.

•

The percentage of blocking pairs and blocking agents are parameters of interest when translating the
solution from
\sf CCQ
setting back to the underlying
\sf HR
instance.
We note that for real instances (except
R3
), the percentage of
blocking pairs is bounded by 5% whereas percentage blocking agents is bounded by 20%. We see a variation of these parameters
across different algorithms (especially in synthetic data sets – see for instance
{\bf S1}
where
\sf ALG
, Algorithm
2
perform significantly better as compared to LP
{\sf OPT}
and
\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approx).
As mentioned earlier, the percentage of violation is the price the
\sf CCQ

setting pays to achieve
\mathcal{A}
-perfectness. It is noted that LP
{\sf OPT}
also suffers large violation of the quotas
to achieve
\mathcal{A}
-perfectness. We observe that in almost all instances
\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approximation algorithm
achieves the minimum value of percentage total violation.

From our empirical evaluations, we conclude that in terms of approximation guarantee as well as other parameters of interest

\mid\hskip-2.84544pt\mathcal{P}\hskip-2.84544pt\mid
-approximation algorithm seems a practically appealing algorithm.
An added advantage of the algorithm is that the same algorithm provides an exact solution for the

\sf{MINMAX}
problem on the same
\sf CCQ
instance.

7
Concluding Remarks

In this work we propose cost-based allocation for the bipartite matching problem under the two-sided preference lists setting.
We propose and investigate the problem of computing
\mathcal{A}
-perfect envy-free matchings under two optimization criteria, namely
\sf{MINMAX}
and
\sf MINSUM
problems and prove a sharp contrast in their complexity.
We present an efficient algorithm for
\sf{MINMAX}
problem and
three approximation algorithms for
\sf MINSUM
on general instances.
Our empirical evaluations indicate that the approximation algorithms perform reasonably well even on the parameters like percentage of blocking agents
and average rank, for which the optimization is not inherent in the problem.
We present a novel LP for
\sf MINSUM
and a primal dual approximation algorithm for a special hard case.
We remark that our
\sf NP
-hardness result implies that the
\sf MINSUM
problem is para-
\sf NP
-hard when
parameterized on the length of an agent’s preference list or the number of distinct costs in the instance.

We note that for the
\sf MINSUM
problem, there is a gap between the upper bound and the lower bound
shown in this work. A specific open direction is to show that
\sf MINSUM
does not admit a constant factor approximation.
It will be also interesting to see if the
\sf NP
-hardness holds even when the instance has distinct costs.
Another open direction is to design cost functions that capture the logistic requirements in real-world.
