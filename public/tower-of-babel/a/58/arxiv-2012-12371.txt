How Discrete Spectrum and Resonances Influence the Asymptotics of the Toda Shock Wave
Iryna EGOROVA a and Johanna MICHOR b

Abstract
We rigorously derive the long-time asymptotics of the Toda shock wave in a middle region where the solution is asymptotically finite gap. In particular, we describe the influence of the discrete spectrum in the spectral gap on the shift of the phase in the theta-function representation for this solution. We also study the effect of possible resonances at the endpoints of the gap on this phase. This paper is a continuation of research started in [arXiv:2001.05184].

Introduction

The Toda shock wave describes the motion of an infinite chain of particles with nonlinear nearest neighbor interactions
when the chain is excited with shock type initial conditions. We are interested in the effect the eigenvalues in the spectral gap of the associated Lax operator have on the
asymptotic behavior of the shock wave.
The Toda shock wave is generated by the solution of the following initial value problem for the Toda lattice
[
23
,
24
]

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\tilde{b}(n,t)=2\big(\tilde{a}(n,t)^{2}-\tilde{a}(n-1,t)^{2}\big),

\displaystyle\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\tilde{a}(n,t)=\tilde{a}(n,t)\big(\tilde{b}(n+1,t)-\tilde{b}(n,t)\big),\qquad(n,t)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{+},

(1.1)

with a steplike initial profile
\big\{\tilde{a}(n,0),\tilde{b}(n,0)\big\}
such that

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(n,0)\to a_{\pm},\qquad\tilde{b}(n,0)\to b_{\pm},\qquad\text{as}\quad n\to\pm\infty,

(1.2)

where
a_{\pm}>0
and
b_{\pm}\in\mathbb{R}
satisfy the condition

\displaystyle b_{-}+2a_{-}<b_{+}-2a_{+}.

(1.3)

This condition fixes the position of the background spectra relative to each other;
their mutual location produces essentially different types of asymptotic solutions
[
20
]
.
The notion of the Toda shock wave
[
4
,
5
]
was traditionally associated with symmetric initial data

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(n-1,0)=\tilde{a}(-n,0),\qquad\tilde{b}(n,0)=-\tilde{b}(-n,0),

(1.4)

and the background constants
a_{-}=a_{+}=\frac{1}{2}
,
b_{+}=-b_{-}>1
. The asymptotic of the solution of (
1.1
) for the particular case

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(n,0)=\frac{1}{2},\qquad\tilde{b}(n,0)=b\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}n,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},\qquad\text{where}\quad\mathop{\mathrm{sgn}}0=0,

(1.5)

was studied in the pioneering work
[
25
]
by Venakides, Deift, and Oba in 1991. By use of the Lax–Levermore approach they established that in a middle region of the half plane
(n,t)\in\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the asymptotic of the shock wave (
1.1
), (
1.5
) is described by a 2-periodic solution of the Toda lattice. They also showed that the asymptotic undergoes a phase shift caused by the presence of a single eigenvalue at

\lambda=0

Notations and statement of the initial holomorphic RHP

To maintain generality of the presentation while keeping notations short, we formulate all preliminary facts on the inverse scattering transform for the steplike initial profile in terms of the spectral variables
z_{\pm}
associated with the initial data (
1.2
), (
1.3
). First of all, let us list some well known properties of the scattering data for the steplike Jacobi operator
\tilde{H}(t)
involved in the Lax representation
\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\tilde{H}(t)=\big[\tilde{H}(t),\tilde{A}(t)\big]
for the initial value problem (
1.1
), (
1.2
). This problem has a unique solution (cf.
[
23
]
). Assume that the coefficients of the initial Jacobi operator
\tilde{H}(0)
tend to the limiting (or background) constants
a_{\pm}
,
b_{\pm}
with a first summable moment of perturbation, that is,
n(\tilde{a}(n,0)-a_{\pm})\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{\pm})
and
n(\tilde{b}(n,0)-b_{\pm})\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{\pm})
. Then the unique solution
\big\{\tilde{a}(t),\tilde{b}(t)\big\}
of (
1.1
) satisfies

\displaystyle n\big(\tilde{a}(n,t)-a_{\pm}\big)\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{\pm}),\qquad n\big(\tilde{b}(n,t)-b_{\pm}\big)\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z}_{\pm}).

(2.1)

With this condition fulfilled, introduce some notations and notions.

•

The background
Jacobi operators

\displaystyle H_{\pm}y(n):=a_{\pm}y(n-1)+b_{\pm}y(n)+a_{\pm}y(n+1),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z},

have spectra
\sigma_{\pm}=[b_{\pm}-2a_{\pm},b_{\pm}+2a_{\pm}]
which do not overlap, and by (
1.3
) satisfy
\sup\sigma_{-}<\inf\sigma_{+}
.

•

The Joukovski maps

z_{\pm}=z_{\pm}(\lambda)
of the spectral parameter
\lambda
are given by

\displaystyle\lambda=b_{\pm}+a_{\pm}\big(z_{\pm}+z^{-1}_{\pm}\big),\qquad z_{\pm}\colon\ \mathop{\mathrm{clos}}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma_{\pm})\mapsto|z_{\pm}|\leq 1.

The map
z_{+}\mapsto z_{-}
is one-to-one between the domains
\mathcal{D}_{+}
and
\mathcal{D}_{-}
, where

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{\pm}=\{z_{\pm}\colon|z_{\pm}|<1,z_{\pm}\notin z_{\pm}(\sigma_{\mp})\}.

The functions
\big\{z_{\pm}^{\pm n}\big\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
are called the
free exponents
. They solve the background spectral equations
H_{\pm}y(n)=\lambda y(n)
.

•

The operator
\tilde{H}(t)
has an absolutely continuous spectrum on the set
\sigma_{+}\cup\sigma_{-}
and
a finite discrete spectrum
\sigma_{\rm d}
, which we divide into three parts,

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm d}^{\rm left}=\{\lambda_{j}\in\sigma_{\rm d}\colon\lambda_{j}<b_{-}-2a_{-}\},\qquad\sigma_{\rm d}^{\rm right}=\{\lambda_{j}\in\sigma_{\rm d}\colon\lambda_{j}>b_{+}+2a_{+}\},

\displaystyle\sigma_{\rm d}^{\rm gap}=\{\lambda_{j}\in\sigma_{\rm d}\colon b_{-}+2a_{-}<\lambda_{j}<b_{+}-2a_{+}\}.

The points
z_{j}^{\pm}=z_{\pm}(\lambda_{j})\in\mathcal{D}_{\pm}\cap(-1,1)
,
\lambda_{j}\in\sigma_{\rm d}
, are also called points of the discrete spectrum.

•

The Jost solutions
of the spectral equation

\displaystyle\tilde{a}(n-1,t)\psi^{\pm}(\lambda,n-1,t)+\big(\tilde{b}(n,t)-\lambda\big)\psi^{\pm}(\lambda,n,t)+\tilde{a}(n,t)\psi^{\pm}(\lambda,n+1,t)=0

are normalised as

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\pm\infty}(z_{\pm})^{\mp n}\psi^{\pm}(\lambda,n,t)=1,\qquad\lambda\in\ \mathop{\mathrm{clos}}(\mathbb{C}\setminus\sigma_{\pm}).

We can consider them as functions of
z_{\pm}
in the closures of

\mathcal{D}_{\pm}
. Their
Wronskian

\displaystyle\tilde{W}(\lambda):=a(n-1,0)\big(\psi_{-}(\lambda,n-1,0)\psi_{+}(\lambda,n,0)-\psi_{+}(\lambda,n-1,0)\psi_{-}(\lambda,n,0)\big)

(2.2)

is an important spectral characteristic of the steplike scattering problem. It can be treated as an analytic function of
z_{\pm}
in
\mathop{\mathrm{clos}}\mathcal{D}_{\pm}
.
The points
z_{j}^{\pm}
are its simple zeros, and
\psi_{\pm}(\lambda_{j},n,t)
are the (dependent) eigenfunctions.

•

The normalising constants
are introduced by

\displaystyle\bigg(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\psi_{\pm}^{2}(\lambda_{j},n,t)\bigg)^{-2}=\gamma_{j}^{\pm}(t)=\gamma_{j}^{\pm}(0)\mathrm{e}^{z_{j}^{\pm}-(z_{j}^{\pm})^{-1}}.

•

The scattering relations

\displaystyle T_{\pm}(\lambda,t)\psi_{\mp}(\lambda,n,t)=R_{\pm}(\lambda,t)\psi_{\pm}(\lambda,n,t)+\overline{\psi_{\pm}(\lambda,n,t)}

(2.3)

hold on the sets

.

•

The time evolution
of the scattering data is given by

\displaystyle R_{\pm}(\lambda,t)=R_{\pm}(\lambda,0)\mathrm{e}^{\pm z_{\pm}\mp z_{\pm}^{-1}}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad|z_{\pm}|=1,

\displaystyle|T_{\pm}(\lambda,t)|^{2}=|T_{\pm}(\lambda,0)|^{2}\mathrm{e}^{\pm z_{\mp}\mp z_{\mp}^{-1}}\qquad

\displaystyle\text{for}\quad|z_{\mp}|=1.

(2.4)

k=1,2,3
, uniformly with respect to
\xi\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}^{j}
.

The proof of Theorem
5.2
essentially repeats the arguments used in
[
11
, Section 7]
.
But since this theorem provides the justification of our asymptotic analysis, we will briefly describe the key points of the final analysis as applied to our case.

According to the standard approach (cf.
[
3
,
16
]
), to perform the conclusive analysis we first have to evaluate in
\mathbb{C}
the “error vector”

\displaystyle m^{\rm err}(z,n,t,j):=m^{(2)}(z,n,t)\big[M^{\rm mod}_{j}(z,n,t)\big]^{-1},

(5.4)

where
M^{\rm mod}_{j}(z,n,t)
is a matrix solution of the model RHP. Recall that by Remark
4.7
, the model jump problem

\displaystyle M^{\rm mod}_{j,+}(z,n,t)=M^{\rm mod}_{j,-}(z,n,t)v^{\rm mod}_{j}(z,n,t),\qquad z\in I\cup I^{*}\cup J\cup J^{*},\qquad\frac{n}{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{j}_{\varepsilon},

(5.5)

has a piecewise constant (with respect to
\xi
) jump matrix
v^{\rm mod}(z)
given by (
4.20
). The choice of such a matrix solution is not unique. However, a proper solution
M_{j}(z):=M_{j}^{\rm mod}(z,n,t)
should be invertible and satisfy at least the symmetry
M_{j}\big(z^{-1}\big)=\sigma_{1}M_{j}(z)\sigma_{1}
, because the vector
m^{\rm err}(z,n,t,j)
should fulfill the standard symmetry property. Moreover, the singularities of
m^{\rm err}
should be removable outside the jump contour and should not be more than
L^{2}
-integrable on the contour. By construction (
5.4
),
m^{\rm err}
does not have a jump on
I\cup I^{*}\cup J\cup J^{*}
. Since
m^{(2)}(z)
has singularities of order
O(z-\tilde{q})^{-1/4}
at
\tilde{q}\in\mathcal{J}
(
2.20
), the matrix
[M_{j}(z)]^{-1}
should have singularities of order not exceeding
o(z-\tilde{q})^{-3/4}
at
\tilde{q}\in\mathcal{J}
and should be less than poles at its other singular points outside the contour. As it is shown in
[
13
]
for the KdV shock wave (and the same is true for the Toda shock case) such an invertible solution with weak singularities does not exist for certain arbitrary large
t
at any direction
\xi\in\mathcal{I}_{\delta}
. Instead, we choose a matrix solution with poles at points
1,-1
, such that its determinant is equal to 1 and
m^{\rm err}
has removable singularities at these points.

To explain the construction of our solution in more detail, recall that the quasimomentum (
3.1
) has the jump on
J\cup J^{*}
defined by (
3.10
). Introduce the function

\displaystyle G(z):=\exp\bigg(\int_{b-2a}^{p(z)}\omega_{\infty_{+},\infty_{-}}\bigg),\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\big[q,q^{-1}\big].

Recall that for
z\in\mathcal{D}
we have
p(z)=\big(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2},+\big)\in\mathbb{M}
(upper sheet), and for
z\in\mathcal{D}^{*}
we have
p(z)=\big(\frac{z+z^{-1}}{2},-\big)
. Then
G(z)
has the following properties
[
11
, Section 5]
:

•

G(z)
is holomorphic on
\mathbb{C}\setminus\big[q,q^{-1}\big]
and satisfies
G\big(z^{-1}\big)=G^{-1}(z)
.

•

Its jumps are given by

\displaystyle G_{+}(z)=G_{-}(z)\mathrm{e}^{-\Lambda},\qquad z\in J,

\displaystyle G_{+}(z)=G_{-}(z)\mathrm{e}^{\Lambda},\qquad z\in J^{*},

\displaystyle G_{\pm}(z)=\big[G_{\pm}\big(z^{-1}\big)\big]^{-1},\qquad z\in I\cup I^{*}.

•

The following asymptotic expansion is valid,

\displaystyle G(z)=-\frac{\tilde{a}}{2z}\big(1+2\tilde{b}z+O\big(z^{2}\big)\big),\qquad G(z^{-1})=-\frac{2z}{\tilde{a}}\big(1-2\tilde{b}z+O\big(z^{2}\big)\big),

where
\tilde{a}=\mathrm{Cap}\,\mathfrak{S}
and
\tilde{b}\in\mathbb{R}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

Introduce the following vector function holomorphic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus([q,q^{-1}]\cup\{0\})
,

\displaystyle m^{\#}(z,n,t,j)=m^{\rm mod}(z,n+1,t,j)[G(z)]^{-\sigma_{3}}.

Then
m^{\#}(z)
solves the jump problem

\displaystyle m^{\#}_{+}(z,n,t,j)=m^{\#}_{-}(z,n,t,j)v^{\rm mod}_{j}(z,n,t),\qquad z\in I\cup I^{*}\cup J\cup J^{*},\qquad\frac{n}{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{j}_{\varepsilon},

where
v^{\rm mod}_{j}(z,n,t)
is given by (
4.20
).
The vector
m^{\#}(z)
satisfies the symmetry condition
m^{\#}_{2}\big(z^{-1}\big)=m^{\#}_{1}(z)
.
The normalization condition is not fulfilled, instead we have

\displaystyle m^{\#}_{1}(z,n,t,j)=-\frac{2z}{\tilde{a}}m^{\rm mod}_{1}(0,n+1,t,j)(1+O(z)),

\displaystyle m^{\#}_{2}(z,n,t,j)=-\frac{\tilde{a}}{2z}m^{\rm mod}_{1}(\infty,n+1,t,j)(1+O(z)),\qquad\text{as}\quad z\to 0.

Proof.

The proof follows immediately from the properties of
G
and
m^{\rm mod}
above.
∎

The function

\displaystyle\rho(z)=\rho(z,n,t,j)=\frac{m^{\rm mod}_{2}(0,n,t,j)m^{\rm mod}_{2}(\infty,n+1,t,j)}{2\tilde{a}\big(z^{-1}-z\big)}

is defined for all
z\neq\pm 1
and is odd,

\rho(z)=-\rho\big(z^{-1}\big)
.
The function
\rho
does not have jumps, therefore the vector
\rho(z)m^{\#}(z)
solves the same jump problem with (
4.20
). However, it is bounded as
z\to 0
,
z\to\infty
and has simple poles at
1
and
-1
instead.
In conclusion, the vector

\displaystyle\Psi(z,n,t,j)=\frac{1}{2}m^{\rm mod}(z,n,t,j)+\rho(z)m^{\#}(z,n,t,j)

solves our vector model RHP, and the same is true for
\Psi\big(z^{-1},n,t,j\big)\sigma_{1}
.
From here on, we fix the parameters
n
,
t
,
j
and omit them to shorten notations when necessary. In particular, the symmetry conditions for
m^{\rm mod}
and
m^{\#}
and oddness of
\rho
imply for the components of

\Psi
that

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}(z)=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}^{\rm mod}(z)+\rho(z)m^{\#}_{1}(z),\qquad\Psi_{2}(z)=\frac{1}{2}m_{2}^{\rm mod}(z)+\rho(z)m^{\#}_{2}(z),

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}\big(z^{-1}\big)=\frac{1}{2}m_{2}^{\rm mod}(z)-\rho(z)m^{\#}_{2}(z),\quad\Psi_{2}\big(z^{-1}\big)=\frac{1}{2}m_{1}^{\rm mod}(z)-\rho(z)m^{\#}_{1}(z).

(5.6)

[

)

(i)

The matrix

\displaystyle M^{\rm mod}(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\Psi_{1}(z)&\Psi_{2}(z)\\

with

(ii)

(5.7)

(iii)

The following equality is valid,

\displaystyle m^{\rm mod}(z,n,t)=(1,1)M^{\rm mod}(z,n,t).

(5.8)

(iv)

The determinant of

is a constant with respect to

,

,

,

Using this lemma, we can establish the properties of the error vector function (

Theorem 5.5
.

The vector function

is holomorphic in

\displaystyle\Xi_{\delta}:=\mathcal{L}_{\delta}\cup\mathcal{L}_{\delta}^{*}\cup C_{\delta}\cup C_{\delta}^{*}\cup\bigcup_{z_{k}\in\sigma_{\rm d}}\left(\mathbb{T}_{\delta,k}\cup\mathbb{T}_{\delta,k}^{*}\right)=\mathcal{K}_{\delta}\setminus(I\cup I^{*}\cup J\cup J^{*}),

(ii)

(5.11)

(iii)

\displaystyle m^{\rm err}(z)=m^{\rm err}\big(z^{-1}\big)\sigma_{1},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Xi_{\delta}.

(5.12)

In particular,

\displaystyle m_{-}^{\rm err}\big(z^{-1}\big)=m_{-}^{\rm err}(z)\sigma_{1},\qquad z\in\Xi_{\delta},

(5.13)

\displaystyle m^{\rm err}_{2}(0)=m_{1}^{\rm err}(\infty)=\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\frac{m^{(2)}_{1}(0)}{m^{\rm mod}_{1}(0)}+\frac{m^{\rm mod}_{1}(0))}{m^{(2)}_{1}(0)}\bigg):=\tau>0.

(5.14)

Here
m^{\rm mod}_{1}(0)=m^{\rm mod}_{1}(0,n,t,j)

Proof.

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
5.2
. We follow the well-known approach via singular integral equations (see, e.g.,
[

]

[

In particular, if we want to preserve the symmetry condition (

, we should use a matrix Cauchy kernel (cf.
[

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}(s,z)=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{1}{s-z}&0\\
0&\frac{1}{s-z}-\frac{1}{s}\end{pmatrix}{\rm d}s,\qquad s\in\Xi_{\delta},\qquad z\notin\Xi_{\delta}.

Since

\displaystyle\hat{\Omega}\big(s,z^{-1}\big)=\sigma_{1}\hat{\Omega}\big(s^{-1},z\big)\sigma_{1},

\displaystyle\int_{\Xi_{\delta}}m^{\rm err}_{-}(s)W(s)\hat{\Omega}(s,z)=\int_{\Xi_{\delta}}m^{\rm err}_{-}(s)W(s)\hat{\Omega}\big(s,z^{-1}\big)\sigma_{1}.

\displaystyle m^{\rm err}(z)=\big(m_{1}^{\rm err}(\infty),m_{2}^{\rm err}(0)\big)+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Xi_{\delta}}m^{\rm err}_{-}(s)W(s)\hat{\Omega}(s,z)

\displaystyle\hphantom{m^{\rm err}(z)}{}=\tau(1,1)+\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Xi_{\delta}}m^{\rm err}_{-}(s)W(s)\hat{\Omega}(s,z).

Recall that according to Lemma

\displaystyle\|W(z)\|=O\big(\mathrm{e}^{-C(\delta)t}\big),\qquad\text{uniformly with respect to}\quad z\in\Xi_{\delta}\quad\text{and}\quad n,t,j\colon\frac{n}{t}\in\mathcal{I}^{j}_{\varepsilon}.

In turn, it implies the following

Lemma 5.6
.

Uniformly with respect to
\frac{n}{t}\in\mathcal{I}_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\big\|z^{k}W(z)\big\|_{L^{p}(\Xi_{\delta})}=O\big(\mathrm{e}^{-C(\delta)t}\big),\qquad p\in[1,\infty],\qquad k=0,1.

Let
\mathfrak{C}
denote the Cauchy operator associated with

\displaystyle(\mathfrak{C}h)(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\Xi_{\delta}}h(s)\hat{\Omega}(s,z),\qquad s\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Xi_{\delta},

where

\displaystyle\big\|(\mathbb{I}-\mathfrak{C}_{W})^{-1}\big\|=\big\|(\mathbb{I}-\mathfrak{C}_{W})^{-1}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Xi_{\delta})\to L^{2}(\Xi_{\delta})}\leq\frac{1}{1-O\big(\mathrm{e}^{-C(\varepsilon)t}\big)}

\displaystyle\|\mu(s)-(\tau,\tau)\|_{L^{2}(\Xi_{\delta})}\leq\big\|(\mathbb{I}-\mathfrak{C}_{W})^{-1}\big\|\|\mathfrak{C}_{-}\|\|W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Xi_{\delta})}=O\big(\mathrm{e}^{-C(\varepsilon)t}\big).
