Abstract
. We show that for all homogeneous polynomials f m f_{m} of degree m m , in d d variables, and each j = 1 , … , d j=1,\dots,d , we have ⟨ x j 2 ​ f m , f m ⟩ L 2 ​ ( 𝕊 d − 1 ) ≥ π 2 4 ​ ( m + 2 ​ d + 1 ) 2 ​ ⟨ f m , f m ⟩ L 2 ​ ( 𝕊 d − 1 ) . \left\langle x_{j}^{2}f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\left(m+2d+1\right)^{2}}\left\langle f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}. This result is used to establish the existence of entire harmonic solutions of the Dirichlet problem, when the data are given by entire functions of order sufficiently low on nonhyperbolic quadratic hypersurfaces.



1.
Introduction

The Dirichlet problem has been solved for very general domains of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. S. J. Gardiner proved that solutions exist, and
not just for continuous boundary data on unbounded domains, but even for
locally bounded Borel data (cf. the characterization given in

[
9
, Theorem 1]
and the comments following it). A different question that naturally arises
is whether harmonic solutions can be extended beyond the boundary when the
data is sufficiently regular, perhaps yielding global solutions. Additional information on these matters can be found in reference

[
10
]
.

One way of ensuring the existence of global solutions is to prove
that if the data is given by a polynomial, or more generally, by an entire function, the solution
is also entire. Following a line of research started in
[
17
]
, D. Khavinson and H. S. Shapiro showed that for every entire function
f
on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, the solution
h

of the Dirichlet problem for the ellipsoid with data function
f
(restricted to
the boundary) has a harmonic continuation to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and hence it extends to an entire function on
\mathbb{C}^{d}
, see
[
13
, Theorem 1]
. They also asked whether this property characterized ellipsoids,
cf.
[
13
, Page 460]
, a problem that is still open in full generality, though
under some additional assumptions a positive answer is known (see
[
14
, Theorem 27]
). An equivalent version of the Khavinson-Shapiro problem appears in
[
15
]
.

The aim of the present paper is to show that results related to the Khavinson-Shapiro Theorem hold for Dirichlet
problems on some specific, not necessarily bounded domains in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, namely, on nonhyperbolic quadratic hypersurfaces. The Dirichlet problem for polynomial data on such hypersurfaces
was studied in
[
5
]
, where the authors present an algorithm to compute polynomial harmonic solutions.
Next we recall
[
5
, Definition 4]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{d}
,
b_{1},\ldots,b_{d}
and
c
, be real numbers.
A nonhyperbolic quadratic polynomial
q
has the form

q(x):=\sum_{j=1}^{d}a_{j}^{2}x_{j}^{2}+\sum_{j=1}^{d}b_{j}x_{j}+c,

where at least one
a_{j}\neq 0
. A nonhyperbolic quadratic hypersurface is the zero set
\{q=0\}
.

Writing
q=P_{2}+P_{1}+P_{0}
, where
P_{i}
denotes the homogeneous part of
q
of degree
i
, we set

\beta=1
if
P_{1}\neq 0
and
\beta=0
otherwise. Then we have

Theorem 2
.

Let
d\geq 2
, and let
q
be a nonhyperbolic quadratic polynomial. If
f
is an entire function of order
\rho<1-\beta/2
, then then there is a harmonic entire function of order bounded by
\rho
, which coincides with
f

on
\{q=0\}
.

As special cases we mention paraboloids, where
q(x)=a_{2}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+a_{d}^{2}x_{d}^{2}-x_{1}

and
\rho<1/2
, ellipsoidal cylinders, where
q(x)=a_{2}^{2}x_{2}^{2}+\cdots+a_{d}^{2}x_{d}^{2}-1

and
\rho<1
, and slabs, where
q(x)=a_{d}^{2}x_{d}^{2}-1

and
\rho<1
. The case of the ellipsoidal cylinders had already been treated in
[
11
]
by potential-theoretic methods, under the same hypothesis
\rho<1
, but without reaching the conclusion that the
harmonic solution has finite order bounded by
\rho
.

The question whether a version of Theorem
2
holds without any assumptions on the order of
f
, is both open and interesting. For slabs, the existence of harmonic entire solutions
is obtained in
[
12
]
, under no assumptions on the order of the entire data functions. But there the argument depends on the Schwarz reflection principle for harmonic functions vanishing on hyperplanes, so this technique is not available for general quadratic surfaces. In passing, we mention that when
d=2
, harmonic entire functions vanishing on a parabola are characterized in
[
7
, p. 427]
; it seems plausible that in this case the answer might be affirmative.

Finally, in the (bounded) case of ellipsoids, we have already mentioned the Khavinson-Shapiro Theorem for arbitrary entire functions. They also note that if the data function is of exponential type, so is the harmonic solution (cf.
[
13
, P. 459 Remarks]
). In
[
3
, Theorem 1]
D. Armitage shows that the order of the solution is bounded by the order of the data function, and if the order is the same, then the type of the solution is bounded by the type of the data function.
Thus, for ellipsoids Theorem
2
only yields strictly weaker results: since via a rotation any ellipsoid can be expressed as
q(x)=a_{1}^{2}x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+a_{d}^{2}x_{d}^{2}-1=0
, we take
\beta=0
and thus,
\rho<1
.

The results presented here are obtained by generalizing to arbitrary dimensions Theorems 3, 4 and 5 from

[
16
]
, proven there only for the case
d=2
. Theorem
2
is obtained via the following estimate, together with
[
16
, Theorem 1]
).

Theorem 3
.

For all homogeneous polynomials
f_{m}
of degree
m
in
d
variables,
and each
j=1,\dots,d
, we have

\left\langle x_{j}^{2}f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\left(m+2d+1\right)^{2}}\left\langle f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}.

2.
Jacobi polynomials

In the following we need the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)
of degree
n\geq 0
and parameter

\alpha>-1,

see
[
18
, 2.4.1, p. 29]
. Here
x\in\mathbb{R}
. For
n\geq 1
the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)
satisfy the recurrence relation

xP_{n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)=a_{n}\left(\alpha\right)P_{n+1}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}+g_{n}\left(\alpha\right)P_{n-1}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)

with coefficients
a_{n}\left(\alpha\right)
and
g_{n}\left(\alpha\right)
defined by

\displaystyle a_{n}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\left(n+1\right)\left(n+2\alpha+1\right)}{\left(2n+2\alpha+1\right)\left(n+\alpha+1\right)},

\displaystyle g_{n}\left(\alpha\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+\alpha}{2n+2\alpha+1},

and initial conditions
P_{0}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)=1
and
P_{1}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)=\left(\alpha+1\right)x.
It is easy to see that for
n\geq 2
,

(1)

x^{2}P_{n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)=\widetilde{a}_{n}\left(\alpha\right)P_{n+2}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)+\widetilde{b}_{n}\left(\alpha\right)P_{n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)+\widetilde{g}_{n}\left(\alpha\right)P_{n-2}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right),

where
\widetilde{a}_{n}=a_{n}a_{n+1}
,
\widetilde{g}_{n}=g_{n}g_{n-1}
,
and
\widetilde{b}_{n}=a_{n}g_{n+1}+g_{n}a_{n-1}.

Write
0\leq y:=x^{2}
and
u:=2n
. Note that since

P_{2n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)
is even (cf.
[
18
, (4.1.3)]
) the function

r_{u}(y):=P_{2n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(\sqrt{y}\right)
is a polynomial on
[0,\infty)
, and furthermore,

(2)

y\ r_{u}\left(y\right)=\widetilde{a}_{u}\left(\alpha\right)r_{u+1}\left(y\right)+\widetilde{b}_{u}\left(\alpha\right)r_{u}\left(y\right)+\widetilde{g}_{u}\left(\alpha\right)r_{u-1}\left(y\right).

Next we need an estimate of the first positive root of
P_{2n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right).
Using the work of Á
. Elbert and D. Siafarikas (see
[
6
]
) we can derive quickly the following result:

Theorem 4
.

Let
x_{2n,1}\left(\alpha\right)
be the first positive zero of
P_{2n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)
, where
n\geq 3
and
\alpha\geq-1/2.
Then

x_{2n,1}\left(\alpha\right)\geq\frac{\pi}{4\sqrt{\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}+n\right)\left(n+2\right)}}.

Proof.

For
\alpha>-1/2
it is well known that
P_{2n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(x\right)
is equal to a multiple of the Gegenbauer (or ultraspherical)
polynomial
C_{2n}^{\lambda}\left(x\right)
of degree
2n
with parameter

\lambda=\alpha+1/2.
Denote the first positive zero of
C_{2n}^{\lambda}

by
y_{2n,1}\left(\lambda\right)
, so
x_{2n,1}\left(\alpha\right)=y_{2n,1}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right).
In
[
6
, p. 32]
, the authors show
that on
\left(0,\infty\right)
, the function
f_{2n}
defined by

f_{2n}\left(\lambda\right):=\sqrt{\lambda+\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}}\ y_{2n,1}\left(\lambda\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}}\ y_{2n,1}\left(\lambda\right),

is increasing.

By
[
18
, Theorem 6.21.1]
, as
\lambda\downarrow 0
, so
\alpha\downarrow-1/2
,
we have
x_{2n,1}\left(\alpha\right)\uparrow x_{2n,1}\left(-1/2\right)
. Since

f_{2n}\left(\lambda\right)
is increasing, we conclude that for every
\lambda>0
,

f_{2n}\left(\lambda\right)\geq\lim_{\lambda\downarrow 0}f_{2n}\left(\lambda\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}}\ x_{2n,1}\left(-1/2\right).

Now by
[
18
, pg. 60]
, we have
P_{2n}^{\left(-1/2,-1/2\right)}\left(x\right)=c_{2n}T_{2n}\left(x\right)
, where
c_{2n}
is a constant depending only on
n
and
T_{2n}\left(x\right)
is the corresponding Chebyshev
polynomial of the first kind. Since the zeros of
T_{2n}

are given by
\cos\left(\frac{2k-1}{2n}\frac{\pi}{2}\right)
for
k=1,\dots,2n,
we conclude that

\displaystyle x_{2n,1}\left(-1/2\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\cos\left(\frac{2n-1}{2n}\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\frac{2n-1}{2n}\frac{\pi}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sin\frac{\pi}{4n}\geq\frac{\pi}{4n+2},

where we have used that
\cos x=\sin\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-x\right)
, and for the last
inequality,
[
16
, Lemma 14]
. Since

\sqrt{\alpha+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}}\ y_{2n,1}\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}\right)\geq\sqrt{\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}}\ x_{2n,1}\left(-1/2\right),

it follows that

\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}+\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}\right)\left(x_{2n,1}\left(\alpha\right)\right)^{2}\geq\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}\frac{\pi^{2}}{\left(4n+2\right)^{2}}.

From

n\geq\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}\mbox{ \ and \ }\frac{8n^{2}+1}{8n+2}\frac{1}{\left(4n+2\right)^{2}}\geq\frac{1}{16\left(n+2\right)}

we obtain the
estimate

\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}+n\right)\left(x_{2n,1}\left(\alpha\right)\right)^{2}\geq\frac{1}{16\left(n+2\right)}\pi^{2}.

∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
3

Denote the unit sphere by

\mathbb{S}^{d-1}=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\left|\theta\right|=1\right\},

and its surface area measure by
d\theta
. The
L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})
inner product and its associated norm, are defined in the usual way:

(3)

\left\langle f,g\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}:=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}f\left(\theta\right)\overline{g\left(\theta\right)}d\theta\text{ and }\left\|f\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\sqrt{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}}.

We shall need some results from the theory of spherical harmonics. It is well known that one can
construct
orthogonal
spherical harmonics by induction over the
dimension
d
. In order to emphasize the dependence on
d
, we shall denote an orthogonal basis of the space
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
of all homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
k
in
d

variables, by

Y_{k,l}^{d}\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\right)\text{ for }l=1,\dots,a_{k}^{\left(d\right)},

where we have written
a_{k}^{\left(d\right)}:=\dim\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right).
We also use
x=\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d}\right)
and
\left|x\right|=\sqrt{x_{1}^{2}+\cdots+x_{d}^{2}}.

The following result can be found, for instance, in
[
1
, p.
461]
or in
[
8
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
d\geq 2
, let
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and for each
s=0,1,2,\dots,k
, let

\{Y_{s,l}^{d-1}:l=1,\dots,a_{s}^{\left(d-1\right)}\}

be an orthogonal basis of
\mathcal{H}_{s}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d-1}\right)
.
Then the system of polynomials given by

(4)

Y_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right):=Y_{s,l}^{d-1}\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1}\right)\cdot\left|x\right|^{k-s}P_{k-s}^{\left(s+\left(d-3\right)/2,s+\left(d-3\right)/2\right)}\left(\frac{x_{d}}{\left|x\right|}\right),

where
s=0,1,2,\dots,k
and
l=1,\dots,a_{s}^{(d-1)}
, forms an orthogonal basis
of
\mathcal{H}_{k}\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)
.

Using the abreviation
\alpha_{s}:=s+\left(d-3\right)/2
, for
k\geq 1
identity (
4
) becomes

Y_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)=Y_{s,l}^{d-1}\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1}\right)\cdot\left|x\right|^{k-s}P_{k-s}^{\left(\alpha_{s},\alpha_{s}\right)}\left(\frac{x_{d}}{\left|x\right|}\right).

Note that the same factor
Y_{s,l}^{d-1}\left(x_{1},\dots,x_{d-1}\right)
appears in the preceding expression for different values of
k
, when
\left|x\right|=1
.
The recurrence relation (
1
) together with (
4
) imply that for
k\geq 2
,

x_{d}^{2}Y_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)=\widetilde{a}_{k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)Y_{k+2,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)+\widetilde{b}_{k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)Y_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)+\widetilde{g}_{k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)Y_{k-2,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right).

Define the normalized spherical
harmonic by

\widetilde{Y}_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}=d_{k,\left(s,l\right)}\cdot Y_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\text{ where }d_{k,\left(s,l\right)}:=\left\|Y_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\right\|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}^{-1}.

It follows that

(5)

\displaystyle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\frac{d_{k,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{k+2,\left(s,l\right)}}\widetilde{Y}_{k+2,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)+\widetilde{b}_{k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\widetilde{Y}_{k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)

(6)

\displaystyle+\ \widetilde{g}_{k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\frac{d_{k,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{k-2,\left(s,l\right)}}\widetilde{Y}_{k-2,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right).

The following result, cf.
[
16
, Lemma 12]
, shows that to prove Theorem
3
we only need
to consider homogeneous polynomials
f_{2m}
of even degree.

Lemma 6
.

Let
P_{2k}
be a homogeneous polynomial of degree
2k>0
.
Suppose that for each
m\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists a constant
C_{2m}>0
such that for all homogeneous polynomials
f_{2m}
of degree
2m
, we have

\left\langle P_{2k}f_{2m},f_{2m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\geq C_{2m}\left\langle f_{2m},f_{2m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}.

Then for all homogeneous polynomials
f_{2m+1}
of degree
2m+1
,

\left\langle P_{2k}f_{2m+1},f_{2m+1}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\geq C_{2m+2}\left\langle f_{2m+1},f_{2m+1}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}.

Let us recall Theorem
3
.

Theorem 7
.

For all homogeneous polynomials
f_{m}
of degree
m
in
d
variables,
and each
j=1,\dots,d
, we have

\left\langle x_{j}^{2}f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\left(m+2d+1\right)^{2}}\left\langle f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{S}^{d-1}\right)}.

Proof.

Without loss of generality we set
j=d
.
By the preceding Lemma it suffices
to show that for all even indices we can take

C_{2m}:=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\left(2m+2d\right)^{2}}.

By the Gauss
decomposition (cf. Theorem 5.5 in
[
4
]
or Theorem 5.7 in the 2001 edition) there exist homogeneous
harmonic polynomials
h_{2k}
of degree
2k
for
k=0,\dots,m
, such that

(7)

f_{2m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left|x\right|^{2m-2k}h_{2k}(x)\text{.}

Each harmonic polynomial
h_{2k}

of degree
2k
for
k=0,\dots,m
is a linear combination of the orthonormal
spherical harmonics
\widetilde{Y}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}
with
s=0,1,2,\dots,2k
and
l=1,\dots,a_{s}^{(d-1)}
. Thus, we can write

h_{2k}=\sum_{s=0}^{2k}\sum_{l=1}^{a_{s}^{\left(d-1\right)}}c_{k,s,l}^{d}\widetilde{Y}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}

for suitable complex coefficients
c_{k,s,l}^{d}.

Recall that
\lfloor x\rfloor
denotes the integer part of
x
. By reordering it is easy
to see that

f_{2m}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\left|x\right|^{2m-2k}h_{2k}(x)=\sum_{s=0}^{2m}\sum_{l=1}^{a_{s}^{\left(d-1\right)}}\sum_{k=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}c_{k,s,l}^{d}\left|x\right|^{2m-2k}\widetilde{Y}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}(x).

By orthogonality

(8)

\left\langle f_{2m},f_{2m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\sum_{s=0}^{2m}\sum_{l=1}^{a_{s}^{\left(d-1\right)}}\sum_{k=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}\left|c_{k,s,l}^{d}\right|^{2}

(since we are integrating over the unit sphere).
Now
\left\langle x_{d}^{2}f_{2m},f_{2m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}

is equal to

\sum_{s_{1}=0}^{2m}\sum_{l_{1}=1}^{a_{s_{1}}^{\left(d-1\right)}}\sum_{k_{1}=\left\lfloor\frac{s_{1}+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}\sum_{s_{2}=0}^{2m}\sum_{l_{2}=1}^{a_{s_{2}}^{\left(d-1\right)}}\sum_{k_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{s_{2}+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}c_{k_{1},s_{1},l_{1}}^{d}\overline{c_{k_{2},s_{2},l_{2}}^{d}}\left\langle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k_{1},\left(s_{1},l_{1}\right)},\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k_{2},\left(s_{2},l_{2}\right)}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})},

so by orthogonality (using the relation (
5
-
6
) for
x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}
)
we obtain

(9)

\left\langle x_{d}^{2}f_{2m},f_{2m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\sum_{s=0}^{2m}\sum_{l=1}^{a_{s}^{\left(d-1\right)}}\sum_{k_{1}=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}\sum_{k_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}c^{d}_{k_{1},s,l}\overline{c^{d}_{k_{2},s,l}}A_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(s,l\right)

where

A_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(s,l\right):=\left\langle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k_{1},\left(s,l\right)},\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k_{2},\left(s,l\right)}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}.

In view of (
8
) and (
9
), it suffices to show that for each
s=0,\dots,2m
and each
l=1,\dots,a_{s}^{\left(d-1\right)}
,

(10)

\sum_{k_{1}=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}\sum_{k_{2}=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}c^{d}_{k_{1},s,l}\overline{c^{d}_{k_{2},s,l}}A_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(s,l\right)\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{4\left(2m+2d\right)^{2}}\sum_{k=\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor}^{m}\left|c^{d}_{k,s,l}\right|^{2}.

The integer
s
is either even or odd,
so we can write
s=2\sigma+\delta
with
\delta\in\left\{0,1\right\}.

Then

\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor=\left\lfloor\frac{2\sigma+1+\delta}{2}\right\rfloor=\sigma+\delta.

Let us define the symmetric
\left(m-\sigma-\delta+1\right)\times\left(m-\sigma-\delta+1\right)

A_{m}\left(s,l\right):=\left(A_{k_{1},k_{2}}\left(s,l\right)\right)_{k_{1},k_{2}=\sigma+\delta,\dots,m}.

Writing
c^{t}=\left(c^{d}_{\left\lfloor\frac{s+1}{2}\right\rfloor,s,l},\dots,c^{d}_{m,s,l}\right)
, it is clear that the left hand side of (
10
) equals

c^{t}\ A_{m}(s,l)\ \overline{c},

so it is enough to show that the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{m,s,l}^{\ast}
of

A_{m}\left(s,l\right)

satisfies the inequality

\lambda_{m,s,l}^{\ast}\geq\pi^{2}/4\left(2m+2d\right)^{2}.

Since
A_{m}\left(2\sigma+1,l\right)
is the submatrix of
A_{m}\left(2\sigma,l\right)
obtained by deleting the first row and the first column, its lowest eigenvalue is at least as large as
\lambda_{m,2\sigma,l}^{\ast}
. Thus, it suffices to prove (
10
) for
A_{m}\left(2\sigma,l\right)
, or equivalently, for
\delta=0.

We now use the recurrence relation (
5
-
6
) (with
2k

instead of
k
)

\displaystyle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\widetilde{a}_{2k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\frac{d_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2k+2,\left(s,l\right)}}\widetilde{Y}_{2k+2,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)+\widetilde{b}_{2k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\widetilde{Y}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right)

\displaystyle+\widetilde{g}_{2k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\frac{d_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2k-2,\left(s,l\right)}}\widetilde{Y}_{2k-2,\left(s,l\right)}^{d}\left(x\right).

From the orthonormality relations we see that

A_{k,k}\left(s,l\right)=\left\langle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)},\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\widetilde{b}_{2k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right),

A_{k,k+1}\left(s,l\right)=\left\langle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)},\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k+2,\left(s,l\right)}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\widetilde{a}_{2k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\frac{d_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2k+2,\left(s,l\right)}},

and

A_{k,k-1}\left(s,l\right)=\left\langle x_{d}^{2}\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k,\left(s,l\right)},\widetilde{Y}^{d}_{2k-2,\left(s,l\right)}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\widetilde{g}_{2k-s}\left(\alpha_{s}\right)\frac{d_{2k,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2k-2,\left(s,l\right)}},

for
k=\sigma,\dots,m
and
s=2\sigma
, with all the other entries being equal to zero. It follows that
A_{m}\left(s,l\right)
is an
\left(m-\sigma+1\right)\times\left(m-\sigma+1\right)-
matrix of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\widetilde{b}_{0}&\widetilde{a}_{0}\frac{d_{2\sigma,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2\sigma+2,\left(s,l\right)}}&&\\
\widetilde{g}_{2}\frac{d_{2\sigma+2,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2\sigma,\left(s,l\right)}}&\widetilde{b}_{2}&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\widetilde{a}_{2m-2-2\sigma}\frac{d_{2m-2,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2m,\left(s,l\right)}}\\
&&\widetilde{g}_{2m-2\sigma}\frac{d_{2m,\left(s,l\right)}}{d_{2m-2,\left(s,l\right)}}&\widetilde{b}_{2m-2\sigma}\end{array}\right),

We recall some known facts about tridiagonal
n\times n
-matrices of the form

J_{n}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\beta_{0}&\alpha_{0}&&\\
\gamma_{1}&\beta_{1}&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\alpha_{n-2}\\
&&\gamma_{n-1}&\beta_{n-1}\end{array}\right).

where all the entries
\alpha_{k}
and
\gamma_{k}
are non-zero. In order to compute
the characteristic polynomial
\det\left(J_{n}-\lambda I_{n}\right)

of
J_{n}
, we define a sequence of polynomials
p_{0}\left(x\right):=1
,

p_{1}\left(x\right):=\left(x-\beta_{0}\right)/\alpha_{0}
,
and for
0<k<n
, we use the recurrence relation

(11)

xp_{k}\left(x\right)=\gamma_{k}p_{k-1}\left(x\right)+\beta_{k}p_{k}\left(x\right)+\alpha_{k}p_{k+1}\left(x\right).

Then an inductive argument for
n\geq 1
, together with the expansion of the determinant along the last column, yields that
p_{n}\left(x\right)
is a constant multiple
of the characteristic polynomial of
J_{n}
,
and more specifically, that

\det\left(J_{n}-\lambda I_{n}\right)=\left(-1\right)^{n}\alpha_{0}\dots\alpha_{n-2}p_{n}\left(\lambda\right).

Thus the eigenvalues of
J_{n}
are the zeros of the polynomials
p_{n}\left(x\right)
defined by (
11
). Next we consider the special form
A_{m}\left(s,l\right)
of
J_{n}
that appears in our argument, with an additional simplification. Replacing
p_{k}
with
q_{k}:=d_{k}p_{k}
(where
d_{k}\neq 0)
in
(
11
) we obtain the recurrence relation

xq_{k}\left(x\right)=\alpha_{k}\frac{d_{k}}{d_{k+1}}q_{k+1}\left(x\right)+\beta_{k}q_{k}\left(x\right)+\gamma_{k}\frac{d_{k}}{d_{k-1}}q_{k-1}\left(x\right),

with the initial conditions transformed to
q_{0}\left(x\right)=d_{0}

and
q_{1}\left(x\right)=d_{1}\left(x-\beta_{0}\right)/\alpha_{0}.

It follows that the matrix
A_{m}\left(s,l\right)
, for
s=2\sigma
, has the
same eigenvalues as the matrix

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}\widetilde{b}_{0}&\widetilde{a}_{0}&&\\
\widetilde{g}_{2}&\widetilde{b}_{2}&\ddots&\\
&\ddots&\ddots&\widetilde{a}_{2m-2-2\sigma}\\
&&\widetilde{g}_{2m-2\sigma}&\widetilde{b}_{2m-2\sigma}\end{array}\right).

Next we replace the even indices
2n
appearing
in the preceding matrix with
u=2n
,
and we recognize it as the Jacobi matrix
whose eigenvalues are computed via the polynomials appearing in the recurrence relation (
2
).
Denote by
y_{u}
the first positive zero of
r_{u}(y)=P_{2n}^{\left(\alpha,\alpha\right)}\left(\sqrt{y}\right),

with
n=m-\sigma+1
and
\alpha=s+\left(d-3\right)/2.
Then the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{m,s,l}^{\ast}
of
A_{m}\left(s,l\right)
is
y_{u}
.
Note that
\sigma
ranges from
0
to
m
, so
n=m-\sigma+1
ranges from
1
to
m+1.
Furthermore,

\alpha=s+\left(d-3\right)/2=2\left(m+1-n\right)+\left(d-3\right)/2.

By Theorem
4
,

\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}+n\right)\left(n+2\right)y_{u}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{16}.

For
n=m-\sigma+1
we estimate

\displaystyle\left(\alpha+\frac{1}{2}+n\right)\left(n+2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{n=1,\dots,m+1}\left(2\left(m+1-n\right)+\left(d-3\right)/2+1/2+n\right)\cdot\left(n+2\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(m+d\right)^{2},

using the fact that

\sigma\geq 0

3\sigma-\sigma^{2}-(d\sigma)/2\leq 1

It follows that

\lambda_{m,s,l}^{\ast}=y_{u}\geq\frac{\pi^{2}}{16\left(m+d\right)^{2}}.

The

We use the special case of

and

.

Theorem 8
.

Let

2

and

with

(12)

\left\langle P_{2}f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}\geq\frac{1}{C\left(m+D\right)^{2}}\left\langle f_{m},f_{m}\right\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}
