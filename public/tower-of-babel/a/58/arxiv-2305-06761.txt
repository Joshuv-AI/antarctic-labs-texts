Isoperiodic foliation of the stratum ℋ ⁡ ( 1 , 1 , − 2 ) \mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
Gianluca Faraco, Guillaume Tahar, Yongquan Zhang

Abstract
. On a Riemann surface, periods of a meromorphic differential along closed loops define a period character from the absolute homology group into the additive group of complex numbers. Fixing the period character in strata of meromorphic differentials defines the isoperiodic foliation where the remaining degrees of freedom are the relative periods between the zeroes of the differential. In strata of meromorphic differentials with exactly two zeroes, leaves have a natural structure of translation surface. In this paper, we give a complete description of the isoperiodic leaves in marked stratum ℋ ⁡ ( 1 , 1 , − 2 ) \mathcal{H}(1,1,-2) of meromorphic 1 1 -forms with two simple zeroes and a pole of order two on an elliptic curve. For each character, the corresponding leaf is a connected Loch Ness Monster. The translation structures of generic leaves feature a ramified cover of infinite degree over the flat torus defined by the lattice of absolute periods. By comparison, isoperiodic leaves of the unmarked stratum are complex disks endowed with a half-translation structure having infinitely many singular points. Finally, we give a description of the large-scale conformal geometry of the wall-and-chamber decomposition of the leaves.



1.
Introduction

Strata of meromorphic
1
-forms with prescribed orders of zeroes and poles are endowed with the so-called
period atlas
, see
[
1
, Theorem 2.1]
for a a general construction. Local coordinates of the period atlas are:

•

integrals of the differential along closed loops on the surface punctured at the poles (
absolute periods
);

•

integrals of the differential along paths joining distinct zeroes (
relative periods
).

Fixing the absolute periods defines in each stratum the
isoperiodic foliation
. In the present paper, we give a complete description of isoperiodic leaves for stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
formed by pairs
(X,\omega)
where
X
is an elliptic curve while
\omega
is a meromorphic
1
-form with two marked simple zeroes and one double pole.

1.1.
Main results

Absolute periods are encompassed into the
period character
. We begin with the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Any pair
(X,\omega)\in\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
defines a homomorphism
\chi\colon H_{1}(X^{\ast},\mathbb{Z})\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
where
X^{\ast}
is Riemann surface
X
punctured at the unique pole of
\omega
.
Period character

\chi
is defined by

(1.1)

\gamma\mapsto\int\limits_{\gamma}\omega.

We have the identification
H_{1}(X^{\ast},\mathbb{Z})\simeq\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and changes of basis preserving the standard symplectic structure are identified with
\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
. The
absolute period group

\Gamma
is the image of character
\chi
. It is a subgroup of
\mathbb{C}
with at most two generators. The first result of the paper is that each nonzero period character is realized by exactly one (connected) leaf of the isoperiodic foliation.

Theorem 1.2
.

In the marked stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, every non-trivial period character
\chi
is realized, up to a symplectic change of basis, by a unique isoperiodic leaf.

With trivial period character we mean the representation that associates every element
\gamma\in H_{1}(X^{*},\,\mathbb{C})
to
0
. Conversely,
[
9
, Theorem B]
states that the trivial period character cannot be realized in stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
.

For this stratum, isoperiodic leaves are complex curves. The relative period between the two simple zeroes defines a system of local coordinates that endows the leaf with a structure of
translation surface
, see Section
3
. Thus, we can also investigate the geometry of isoperiodic leaves.

Theorem 1.3
.

In the marked stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, (the metric completion of) each isoperiodic leaf is topologically a Loch Ness Monster. Its translation structure is determined by an Abelian differential with infinitely many double zeroes, that is conical singularities of angle
6\pi
. Moreover, the leaf has a natural involution, whose quotient is a half translation surface. This surface is the corresponding leaf in the unmarked stratum. It is a complex disk endowed with a quadratic differential having infinitely many simple zeroes, that is conical singularities of angle
3\pi
. In the case of a positive leaf or an arithmetic real leaf, the quadratic differential also has a simple pole, namely a conical singularity of angle
\pi
.

In fact, for a generic unmarked leaf (when
\Gamma
is a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
), we construct an explicit biholomorphic map to the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}
, which is conformally a disk. Given a translation surface
L
(possibly of infinite type), a homeomorphism
f:L\to L
is called
affine
if it is given by an affine map in each local coordinate defining the translation structure. The derivative of an affine homeomorphism is a well-defined element in
\textnormal{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
, and the set of all such elements is the
Veech group
of
L
. See §
2
for an equivalent definition. We also determine the Veech groups of isoperiodic leaves for stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, which are surprisingly large.

Theorem 1.4
.

In the marked stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, the Veech group of each isoperiodic leaf is a conjugate of:

•

\textnormal{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})
if
\Gamma
is not contained in a real line;

•

\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}u&b\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{R},a>0,u\in G\right\}
if
\Gamma
is proportional to a non-discrete subgroup
\Gamma^{\prime}
of a real quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
so that
G=\{u>0:u\Gamma^{\prime}=\Gamma^{\prime}\}
is nontrivial;

•

\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{R},a>0\right\}
otherwise.

Each leaf is equipped with a wall-and-chamber decomposition, according to the combinatorics of the translation surfaces it contains (see §
3
). The most prevalent chambers are of
cylinder
type (see §
4
). We show that each chamber of cylinder type is conformally a half plane. In a generic unmarked leaf, the chambers of cylinder type are parametrized by primitive elements in the period group
\Gamma
, and via the biholomorphic map to
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}
and the identification
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}\cong\mathbb{H}
, we can draw these chambers as subsets of the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}
. Recall that
{\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}
acts on
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}
as mapping classes, and correspondingly on the unit disk as hyperbolic isometries.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be a generic unmarked leaf with period group
\Gamma
. Under a suitable conformal identification
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathcal{T}_{1,1}\cong\mathbb{H}
, the closure of each cylinder chamber in
\mathbb{H}\cup\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
is a rational number or
\infty
, and every element in
\mathbb{Q}\cup\{\infty\}
arises this way. Moreover, the actions of
{\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}
on
\mathcal{L}\cong\mathbb{H}
as the Veech group and hyperbolic isometries agree on the boundary
\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
.

Finally, for each cylinder chamber, it does not contain any horodisk based at the same point at infinity, nor is it contained in the
K
-neighborhood of a hyperbolic geodesic ray for any
K>0
.

We give more precise information about the conformal geometry of cylinder chambers in §
9
. Figure
1
shows a schematic picture of an unmarked positive leaf, where some cylinder chambers are shown in red.

1.2.
Relations with other works

Under a much bigger perspective, our paper can be seen as a follow-up of a series of papers and works developed along the years. The period map is defined as the association

(1.2)

\text{Per}\colon\Omega\mathcal{S}_{g,n}\longrightarrow\text{Hom}\Big(\textnormal{H}_{1}(S_{g,n},\mathbb{Z}),\,\mathbb{C}\,\Big)

where
\Omega\mathcal{S}_{g,n}
denote the moduli space of homologically marked translation surfaces. For holomorphic differentials on compact Riemann surfaces (
i.e.

n=0
) the image of the period map has been studied by Haupt in
[
18
]
and subsequently rediscovered by Kapovich in
[
21
]
by using Ratner’s theory. In the same spirit of
[
21
]
, Le Fils has provided in
[
17
]
necessary and sufficient conditions for a representation
\chi
to be a character in a given stratum of holomorphic differentials and, around the same time, Bainbridge-Johnson-Judge-Park in
[
5
]
have provided, with an independent and alternative approach, necessary and sufficient conditions for a representation to be realized in a connected component of a prescribed stratum. For meromorphic differentials on compact Riemann surfaces, equivalently holomorphic differentials on punctured complex curves (
i.e.

n\geq 1
), the image of the period mapping has been recently determined in
[
12
]
(a special version in which all zeros and poles are at the punctures), in
[
9
]
for strata of meromorphic differentials and subsequently extended by
[
8
]
to connected components of strata.

All previous works concern the study of the period map, and the study of the fibres is the natural follow-up. For a representation
\chi
, the fibre
\text{Per}^{-1}(\chi)
is known as an isoperiodic leaf and it comprises all translation surfaces with the same absolute periods. Isoperiodic foliation has already been studied in several recent works. In the case of strata of holomorphic
1
-forms, ergodicity of the foliation has been obtained in
[
24
]
(in low genus), in
[
7
]
(for principal strata
1
1

1

strata of holomorphic
1
-forms where every zero is simple
in the general case) and
[
16
]
(with independent methods). This has recently been generalized to most strata in
[
30
]
. In
[
6
]
, methods of
[
7
]
have been extended to meromorphic differentials with a pair of simple poles, obtaining a classification result on the closure of leaves. A study of isoperiodic foliation has been carried out in
[
22
]
in the specific case of meromorphic differentials with an absolute periods group contained in
\mathbb{R}
(
real-normalized differentials
) and a unique double pole. Theorem
1.2
of the present paper confirms the counting of leaves realizing a given group of absolute periods in the case of genus one
[
22
, Theorem 9]
.

For strata of meromorphic
1
-forms on the Riemann sphere, isoperiodic foliation coincides with the
isoresidual fibration
defined by the map sending a differential to the vector of its residues at the (labelled) poles, see
[
14
]
for details. In contrast with the results of the present paper, isoresidual fibers in genus zero have compact closures because they are defined by algebraic equations (residues at the poles are rational functions of the locations of zeroes and poles).

In comparison, stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
is the simplest example of isoperiodic foliation on a stratum of nonzero genus. Even in this easy case, isoperiodic leaves are of infinite genus and contain essential singularities. Wild translation surfaces have already been studied in several works, see
[
19
,
25
,
29
]
, mostly with motivations coming from the field of dynamical systems. Isoperiodic leaves provide a family of examples of wild translation surfaces having an algebro-geometric origin.

1.3.
Organization of the paper

In Section §
2
, we give general background on translation surfaces and their moduli spaces. In particular, we introduce the notion of core and the walls-and-chambers structure it defines in strata.

In Section §
3
, we introduce the isoperiodic foliation, give the classification of leaves and describe the translation structure of leaves: conical singularities and decomposition into chambers.

Each leaf contains chambers of "cylinder type" where the core of the parametrized translation surfaces is a cylinder. The geometric description and classification of these chambers is given in Section §
4
.

Proofs of Theorems
1.2
,
1.3
and
1.4
are split into the next four sections depending on the type of the isoperiodic leaf as follows:

•

Positive leaves
,
i.e.

\Gamma
is a lattice of
\mathbb{C}
and
\chi
has positive volume, are treated in Section §
5
;

•

Negative leaves
,
i.e.

\Gamma
is a lattice of
\mathbb{C}
and
\chi
has negative volume, are treated in Section §
6
;

•

Non-arithmetic real leaves
,
i.e.

\Gamma
is a dense subgroup of a real line of
\mathbb{C}
, are treated in Section §
7
;

•

Arithmetic real leaves
,

Finally, in §

2.
Geometry of translation structures

We begin by introducing basic concepts of the main objects of interest.

2.1.
Elliptic curves and strata of differentials

An
elliptic curve

E
is simply a compact Riemann surface of genus one. By uniformization Theorem, any elliptic curve is biholomorphic to the quotient space
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
where
\Lambda\cong{\mathbb{Z}}^{2}
is a rank-two lattice of
\mathbb{C}
. Two lattices, say
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\prime}
, define the same complex structure if and only if there exists
\alpha\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
such that
\Lambda^{\prime}=\alpha\Lambda
. Let
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
denote the moduli space of elliptic curves with a marked point. This space is well-known to be a one-dimensional complex orbifold with two singularities given by those elliptic curves admitting non-trivial complex automorphisms.

We define strata
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
as the moduli space of pairs
(X,\omega)
where
X
is an elliptic curve and
\omega
is a meromorphic
1
-form with two simple zeroes. Two pairs
(X,\omega)
and
(X^{\prime},\omega^{\prime})
are identified if there is a biholomorphic map
f
between
X
and
X^{\prime}
such that
\omega
coincides with the pullback
f^{\ast}\omega^{\prime}
. Stratum
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
is defined in the same way for differentials with a double zero and a double pole.

Assuming the marked point is the double pole, a meromorphic
1
-form with two simple zeroes and one double pole is characterized by the position of its zeroes – a principal divisor
A
+
B
on the elliptic curve – and a scaling factor
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
. If the two zeroes coincide, we obtain a differential with a double pole and Abel-Jacobi relations imply that the double zero is a
2
-torsion point of the elliptic curve.

A second parametrization uses the fact that any meromorphic function with a unique double pole at the lattice point is of the form
a\wp+b
where
a\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
and
b\in\mathbb{C}
. Consequently, any meromorphic
1
-form of
\overline{\mathcal{H}}=\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)\cup\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
is a linear combination of
\wp dz
and
dz
. Such a meromorphic
1
-form is clearly characterized by these coefficients.

2.2.
Translation structures

On a compact Riemann surface
X
endowed with a meromorphic
1
-form
\omega
, we denote by
X^{\ast}
the complement of the zeroes and poles of
\omega
in
X
. Local primitives of
\omega
are locally injective around each point of
X^{\ast}
, they provide an atlas of
\mathbb{C}
-valued local coordinates. Since local primitives of the same differential differ by a constant, transition maps of this atlas are translations of the complex plane. Vice versa, such an atlas defines an underlying complex structure
X
and the pullbacks of the
1
-form
dz
on
\mathbb{C}
via local charts globalize to a holomorphic differential
\omega
on
X^{\ast}
.

Remark 2.1
.

For a translation structure
(X,\omega)
on a surface
S_{g,n}
, local charts globalize to a holomorphic mapping
\text{dev}\colon\widetilde{S}_{g,n}\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
called the
developing map
, where
\widetilde{S}_{g,n}
is the universal cover of
S_{g,n}
. The translation structure on
S_{g,n}
lifts to a translation structure
(\widetilde{X},\widetilde{\omega})
and the developing map turns out to be locally univalent away from the zeroes of
\widetilde{\omega}
. The developing map, in particular, satisfies an equivariance property with respect to a representation
\chi\colon\text{H}_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
called
period character
of the translation structure.

A translation structure locally written as
f(z)dz
induces a flat metric
|f(z)|dz
on the surface and biholomorphisms conjugating the meromorphic form are isometries for this metric.

2.3.
Local models of singularities

For a translation structure induced by a meromorphic
1
-form, there are canonical models for neighborhoods of zeroes and poles, see
[
4
,
9
,
26
]
for details.

In a neighborhood of a zero of order
a
, a local primitive is a cyclic cover of degree
a+1
of the disk totally ramified at the center of the disk. In other words, the flat metric induced by the
1
-form extends to the zero as a conical singularity of angle
(2a+2)\pi
.

The present work requires only one kind of poles: double poles with a residue equal to zero. Notice that this is far from being restrictive. In fact, for the one-punctured torus, any simple essential loop around the (sole) pole in
X^{*}
is the commutator of a pair of simple essential closed curves generating
\textnormal{H}_{1}(X^{*},\mathbb{Z})
and commutators always have trivial period. A neighborhood of such a pole is isometric to a neighborhood of infinity in the flat plane.

2.4.
Action of
\text{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})

Given a translation surface
(X,\omega)
, elements of
\text{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
acts by composition with coordinate functions induced by
\omega
. A more geometric way to see this action is the following. If
(X,\omega)
is constructed by identifying parallel sides of a collection of polygons
P_{i}
in
\mathbb{C}
, then
g\cdot(X,\omega)
is obtained by identifying the corresponding sides of the polygons
g(P_{i})
, viewing
g
as a linear function on
\mathbb{C}\cong\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

It is easy to see that this induces an action on each stratum, see
[
31
]
. It follows from definition that period character
\chi
commutes with the action of
\text{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

The
\text{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
-action as defined above applies to translation surfaces of inifinite type as well. Recall that the
Veech group
of a translation surface is the set of derivatives of all affine homeomorphisms. Equivalently,
g\in\text{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
lies in the Veech group of
(X,\omega)
if and only if
g\cdot(X,\omega)
is isomorphic (as translation surfaces) to
(X,\omega)
.

2.5.
Volume

For a symplectic basis
\alpha,\beta
of the absolute homology of a torus,
\mathfrak{Im}(\overline{\chi(\alpha)}\chi(\beta))
is called the
volume
, and denoted by
Vol_{\chi}
, of character
\chi
. It can be checked directly that it does not depend on the choice of a specific symplectic basis. The geometry of an isoperiodic leaf crucially depends on the sign of
Vol_{\chi}
(positive, negative or zero). See
[
8
]
for further details about volume.

2.6.
Saddle connections

Given a translation surface
(X,\omega)
, a
saddle connection
is a geodesic segment, in the flat metric induced by
\omega
connecting two zeroes of
\omega
, so that no interior points of the segment are singularities. We allow the case where the two zeroes are the same; in this case, we shall say the saddle connection is
closed
. On a translation surface
(X,\omega)
, every saddle connection represents a class in the relative homology group
\textnormal{H}_{1}(X\setminus\{P\},\{Z\},\mathbb{Z})
where
\{Z\}
and
\{P\}
are respectively the set of the poles and the set of the zeroes of the meromorphic
1
-form.

2.7.
Core

The notion, first introduced in
[
15
]
is used systematically in
[
13
,
26
,
27
]
to study the geometry of translation structures induced by meromorphic differentials.

Definition 2.2
.

A subset
C
of a translation surface
(X,\omega)
is said to be
convex
if, for any two points
x,y\in C
joined by a geodesic segment
[x,y]
, the entire geodesic segment
[x,y]
is contained in
C
. The
core
of
X
, denoted by
\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)}
, is the convex hull of the zeroes of
\omega
, i.e. the smallest closed convex set containing all the zeroes.

The following lemma has been proved in
[
26
, Propositions 4.4 and 4.5]
. We refer to this paper for the proof.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a translation surface such that
\omega
has at least one pole. Then
\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)}
is a topological retract of surface
X
punctured at the poles of
\omega
. Each connected component of
X\setminus\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)}
is a topological disk containing exactly one pole called polar domain. The boundary of each polar domain is formed by finitely many saddle connections. By convexity, each interior angle of a polar domain is at least
\pi
.

Differentials of stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
have a simple enough topological structure to make possible a topological classification of cores, see
[
26
, Section §4]
for general background.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a translation surface in the stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
. Then the interior of the core can be:

(i)

a torus with a slit (we call this a
torus type
);

(ii)

a cylinder (
cylinder type
);

(iii)

empty (
degenerate type
).

Proof.

We denote by
\beta
the number of sides of the unique polar domain. Applying Gauss-Bonnet formula to the boundary of this polygon, we deduce that the total angle of interior angles of the domain is
(\beta+2)\pi
– the local model of the double pole is the end of an infinite plane. The interior of the core is a translation surface with boundary
\mathcal{I}
. It has been proved in
[
28
, Lemma 2.2]
that any such surface can be decomposed into finitely flat triangles glued along saddle connections. The total angle of
\mathcal{I}
is
(6-\beta)\pi
because each of the two simple zeroes is a conical singularity of angle
4\pi
. It follows that
\mathcal{I}
is formed by exactly
t=6-\beta
triangles. We will investigate possible values of
\beta
. First note that since the residue at the double pole is zero, its polar domain cannot be bounded by a unique saddle connection, because its period would be zero. It follows that
\beta\geq 2
. Case
\beta=6
is the degenerate case. We need to consider the remaining cases
t=1,2,3,4
.

When
t=4
, we have
\beta=2
. In this case, the polar domain is a copy of the complex plane with a slit. The two end points of the slit cannot be identified, for otherwise the cone angle is strictly bigger than
4\pi
. The boundary of the core consists of two parallel geodesic segments. If we glue these two segments together, we obtain a flat torus with two marked points. In other words,
\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)}
is a torus with slit along a geodesic segment.

When
t=2
, we have
\beta=4
. In this case, the domain of the pole is complex plane minus a convex quadrilateral
Q
. The interior of the core is either a quadrilateral, whose four sides connects to the domain of the pole, or a cylinder. The former cannot happen, since otherwise the surface is just a complex plane with four marked points. In the latter case,
Q
is a parallelogram. Two opposite sides of
\mathbb{C}\setminus Q
are identified, and the other two sides are connected to the ends of the cylinder.

The cases
t=1,3
cannot happen. Indeed, when
t=1
, we have
\beta=5
. The polar domain has five sides
A_{1},A_{2},A_{3},A_{4},A_{5}
; two being identified in the surface. These two identified sides cannot be adjacent (otherwise one of the two singularities would be a marked point). Without loss of generality, we assume they are
A_{1}
and
A_{3}
. Thus, the sum of the angles corresponding to corners
A_{1}A_{2}
and
A_{2}A_{3}
is
3\pi
. These corners contribute to the same conical singularity
Z_{1}
. Since the two conical singularities have an angle of
4\pi
, no other corner can contribute to
Z_{1}
. If the three last corners contribute to the other singularity
Z_{2}
, then the three vertices of the triangle forming the interior of the core should contribute to
Z_{2}
. Thus, the two ends of side
A_{2}
should contribute to
Z_{2}
. This is a contradiction.

Similarly, when
t=3
, we have
\beta=3
. So the domain of the pole is complex plane minus a triangle. The interior of the core is a pentagon, two sides of which are identified. The distribution of 3 corners in the domain of the pole among the two singularities must be “2+1", so one singularity has contribution strictly bigger than
3\pi
from the polar domain. On the other hand, in the interior, the distribution of the five corners among the two singularities must be “3+2". In particular, the contribution to the total angle at either singularity from the core is at least
\pi
. But then one singularity has angle strictly bigger than
4\pi
, which is impossible.
∎

The volume of period character
\chi
provides additional constraints on the shape of the core.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
(X,\omega)
be a translation surface in the stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
realizing period character
\chi
. If
(X,\omega)
is of torus type, then
Vol_{\chi}>0
. If
(X,\omega)
is of degenerate type, then
Vol_{\chi}\leq 0
. In this latter case,
Vol_{\chi}<0
if and only if
(X,\omega)
is obtained by removing a convex hexagon in an infinite plane and identifying pairs of opposite sides.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)}
is a torus with a slit, we consider a pair
(\alpha,\beta)
of loops disjoint from the slit defined a symplectic basis of
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. Gluing the two slides of the slit on each other defines a flat torus
\mathcal{T}
. Pair
(\alpha,\beta)
is a symplectic basis of the homology of
\mathcal{T}
. Since the torus has positive area, it follows that
Vol_{\chi}>0
.

If the interior of
\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)}
is empty, then
(X,\omega)
is obtained by identification of sides in the boundary of the unique polar domain which is a topological disk. It follows from the computation of Euler characteristic that the polar domain has six sides and
\mathcal{C_{(X,\omega)}}
is formed by three edges.

We obtain
(X,\omega)
by identifying three pairs of opposite sides (gluing pattern corresponding to word
abcabc
). Gluing pattern corresponding to words
aabbcc
and
aabccb
imply the existence of more than two conical singularities. In the last pattern
abcacb
, four angular sectors of the boundary of the polar domain contribute to one singularity while the two others contribute to the second one. It follows that the distribution of angles is
(\pi,\pi,2\pi,\pi,\pi,2\pi)
. This corresponds to a slit in the infinite plane and some identified sides are then on the same side of the slit. This is impossible in a translation structure.
(X,\omega)
is thus obtained from the complement in
\mathbb{C}
of a topological hexagon
H
by identification of pairs of opposite sides. Since the total angle of each singularity is
4\pi
and every angular sector is of magnitude at least
\pi
, see Lemma
2.3
, the magnitude of each angular sector belongs to interval
[\pi,2\pi]
. Besides, the total angle of three consecutive angular sectors in the boundary of the polar sector has to be exactly
4\pi
(by symmetry). It follows that
H
is a convex hexagon.

The three vertices corresponding to the same singularity define a triangle of area
-\frac{Vol_{\chi}}{2}
, see Figure
2
. It follows that
Vol_{\chi}\neq 0
.
∎

3.
Period coordinates and isoperiodic foliation

3.1.
Period atlas on strata

We now introduce a key notion of the present paper. Let
X
be a compact Riemann surface of genus
g
. An Abelian differential
\omega\in\Omega(X)
always determines a representation, say
\chi\colon\textnormal{H}_{1}(X\setminus P(\omega),\,\mathbb{Z})\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}
, where
P(\omega)
denotes the set of poles of
\omega
whenever this is a meromorphic differential, which is defined by the integral;
i.e.

(3.1)

\chi(\gamma)=\int_{\gamma}\omega\in\mathbb{C}.

For a symplectic basis
\{\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\dots,\alpha_{g},\beta_{g}\}
of
\textnormal{H}_{1}(X,\,\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}^{2g}
, the evaluation of
\omega
along this collection of curves provide a system of
2g
local coordinates called
absolute periods
and the residues of
\omega
– if it is meromorphic – computed around the poles of
\omega
provide additional
p-1
local coordinates, where
p
is the cardinality of
P(\omega)
. Generally, absolute periods and residues do not determine a translation surface even locally. In order to have a local determination of a translation surface one has to consider more parameters. These correspond to the so called
relative periods
. In principle, these periods are not encoded by a period character
\chi
. For a translation surface
(X,\omega)
we denote by
Z(\omega)
the set of zeroes of
\omega
. If we label the zeroes of
\omega
as
\{z_{1},\dots,z_{n}\}
and we choose the latter as a base-point, the relative periods are then defined as follow

(3.2)

\int_{z_{i}}^{z_{n}}\omega\quad\textnormal{ for }i=1,\dots,n-1.

In order to keep track of relative periods we need to consider the relative homology
\textnormal{H}_{1}(X\setminus P(\omega),\,Z(\omega),\,\mathbb{Z})
. If
Z(\omega)=\{z_{1},\dots,z_{k}\}
; let
\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{n-1}
be a collection of arcs connecting
z_{1},\dots,z_{k-1}
with
z_{k}
respectively. Moreover, if
\omega
is a meromorphic differential, then
P=\{p_{1},\dots,p_{n}\}\neq\phi
and denote by
\delta_{i}
a peripheral loop around
p_{i}
. The family of curves
\alpha_{1},\dots,\beta_{g},\gamma_{1},\dots,\gamma_{k-1},\delta_{1},\dots,\delta_{n-1}
provides a basis for
\textnormal{H}_{1}(X\setminus P(\omega),\,Z(\omega),\,\mathbb{Z})
. In the case a translation surface
(X,\omega)
belongs to a stratum
\mathcal{H}_{g}(\kappa;-\nu)
, the mapping

(3.3)

\textnormal{Per}\colon\mathcal{H}_{g}(\kappa;-\nu)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2g+n+k-2}

given by

(3.4)

(X,\omega)\longmapsto\left(\,\int_{\alpha_{1}}\omega\,,\dots,\int_{\beta_{g}}\omega\,,\int_{\gamma_{1}}\omega\,,\dots,\int_{\gamma_{k-1}}\omega,\,\int_{\delta_{1}}\omega\,,\dots,\int_{\delta_{n-1}}\omega\,\right)

provides a system of local coordinates around
(X,\omega)
. There is a natural mapping
\textnormal{Abs}\colon\mathcal{H}_{g}(\kappa;-\nu)\longrightarrow\mathbb{C}^{2g}
that associates to any translation surface
(X,\omega)
its absolute periods and forgets relative periods and residues. The image of this mapping has been completely determined. In fact, in
[
9
, Theorem A]
the authors showed that for every
2g
-tuple the fibres is not-empty. Moreover, the provide necessary and sufficient condition for a given
2g
-tuple to appear as the absolute periods of some translation surface with poles in a prescribed stratum. This map is simply defined as

(3.5)

(X,\omega)\longmapsto\left(\,\int_{\alpha_{1}}\omega\,,\dots,\int_{\beta_{g}}\omega\,\right).

The so-called
isoperiodic foliation

\mathcal{AP}_{g}
, also known as
kernel foliation
or
absolute period foliation
, is the foliation locally defined by the fibers of the mapping
Abs
. Namely, two translation surfaces are in the same leaf of
\mathcal{AP}_{g}
if one can be continuously deformed into the other without changing absolute periods.

3.2.
Classification of period characters

In the specific case of strata
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, the underlying topological surface is of genus one and there is only one pole. Consequently, the image by the period map of the homology group of the surface punctured at the pole is a subgroup
\Gamma
of
\mathbb{C}
with two generators. We have the following

Lemma 3.1
.

The absolute period group
\Gamma
of a translation surface of
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
is either a lattice of
\mathbb{C}
or a nontrivial subgroup of a real line of
\mathbb{C}
.

Proof.

Since
\Gamma
is the image of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
into
\mathbb{C}
by a homomorphism we just have to prove that it cannot be trivial. It follows from
[
9
, Theorem B]
, see also
[
8
]
, where the authors provide necessary and sufficient conditions to realise the trivial representation in a given stratum.
∎

Remark 3.2
.

A different argument for Lemma
3.1
is as follows. Let
(X,\omega)\in\mathcal{H}(1,1;-2)
and let
\chi
be its period character. If the period of every closed loop is trivial;
i.e.

\chi
is the trivial representation, then there is no closed saddle connection and the saddle connections between
B
and
W
have the same period. Since every saddle connection thus belongs to the same direction, there is no flat triangle inside the core. Consequently, the surface is just formed by the domain of the pole – a topological disk, see subsection §
2.7
– the two conical singularities and three saddle connections (this number is determined by Euler characteristic). At each conical singularity, two saddle connections with the same period are separated by an angular sector which is an integer multiple of
2\pi
. Here, zeroes are simple so they correspond to conical singularities of angle
4\pi
. Therefore, the number of saddle connections between them is at most two.

Based on this lemma, we can thus distinguish four types of isoperiodic leaves:

1.

Non-arithmetic real leaves if
\Gamma
is dense in a real line of
\mathbb{C}
;

2.

Arithmetic real leaves if
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}a
with
a\in\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
(in this case, we can always find a closed simple loop whose period is zero);

3.

Positive leaf if
\Gamma
is a lattice and the image of a symplectic basis of the homology is a direct basis of
\mathbb{C}
(positive volume);

4.

Negative leaf if
\Gamma
is a lattice and the image of a symplectic basis of the homology is an indirect basis of
\mathbb{C}
(negative volume).

3.3.
Isoperiodic leaves: translation structure

We focus on isoperiodic leaves in
\mathcal{H}(1,1;-2)
– the stratum we are interested in this paper. The isoperiodic foliation is defined by fixing periods of the differential along
2
loops forming a symplectic basis of the homology of
X
. Notice that there is only one pole so its residue is automatically zero. The unique local parameter of deformation of
(X,\omega)
is the relative period of the differential on a path between the zeroes. Besides, the zeroes are labelled as Black (
B
) and White (
W
). Notice that this corresponds to fix two marked points on the underlying complex structure.

Remark 3.3
.

Since the two zeroes are now labelled, we are technically looking at a
marked
stratum, which is a double cover over the one in our definition. In what follows we shall study isoperiodic leaves in this space.

Since any homotopy class of paths in
X
punctured at the pole has at least one geodesic representative, any such class of paths from one conical singularity to the other has a representative which is a chain of saddle connections,
i.e.
geodesic segments, one of which is between the black zero and the white one. Therefore, there is at least one of these relative homotopy class represented by a saddle connection.

Around
(X,\omega)
, for any homotopy class
\gamma
of paths from
B
to
W
in
X
punctured at the poles, the period
z_{\gamma}
of the differential along
\gamma
can serve as a local complex coordinate in the isoperiodic leaf. For any other class
\gamma^{\prime}
, the difference
z_{\gamma}-z_{\gamma^{\prime}}
is the period of the differential along the closed loop
\gamma\cup-\gamma^{\prime}
. Such an absolute period is fixed all along the isoperiodic leaf. Therefore, the arbitrary choice of
\gamma
results in a coordinates system defined up to a constant, up to a translation in
\mathbb{C}
. These coordinates form a
translation atlas
in the isoperiodic leaf.

3.4.
Isoperiodic leaves: cover structure

In an isoperiodic leaf where the absolute period group
\Gamma
is a lattice, the period coordinates of the leaf are defined up to a period of
\Gamma
. Consequently, we have

Proposition 3.4
.

Suppose the absolute period group
\Gamma
of an isoperiodic leaf is a lattice in
\mathbb{C}
. Then a translation structure on the leaf is induced by a covering of torus
\mathbb{C}/\Gamma
ramified over the lattice point.

Proof.

Indeed, the conical singularities on the leaf correspond to translation surfaces obtained by degeneration,
i.e.
shrinking a saddle connection between the two zeroes.
∎

Because of the action of
\textnormal{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
, every generic leaf is conjugated to one where
\Gamma
is normalized to
\mathbb{Z}[\,i\,]
. In particular, the ramified covering over the square torus endows such isoperiodic leaves with a structure of square-tiled translation surface.

3.5.
Isoperiodic leaves: walls-and-chambers structure

Each isoperiodic leaf and stratum can be decomposed into
chambers
, where the qualitative shape of the core is the same. The
discriminant
is the locus that separates different chambers. It corresponds to a collinerarity condition between boundary saddle connections of polar domains, see
[
26
, Section §4]
for details.

Definition 3.5
.

A translation surface
(X,\omega)
belongs to the
discriminant
of the leaf (or stratum) if there exist two consecutive saddle connections on the boundary of a domain of pole forming an angle of
\pi
. Each connected component of the complement of the discriminant is a
chamber
of the leaf.

In each chamber, the topological pair
(X,\partial\mathcal{C}_{(X,\omega)})
is the same up to homeomorphism. The possible shapes have been classified in Proposition
2.4
. Therefore, we decompose each leaf into:

•

chambers of torus type
;

•

chambers of cylinder type
;

•

chambers of degenerate type
.

The stratum
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)

We now recall the walls-and-chambers structure of the stratum
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
. This was extensively discussed in
[
27
]
. For this, note that there is a natural
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
-action on each stratum by scaling the meromorphic differential. A
projectivized
stratum is the quotient of a stratum by this action. The projectivized stratum
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
is biholomorphic to the modular curve
X_{1}(2)
. The modular curve
X_{1}(2)
is a complex curve of genus zero with two cusps and one orbifold point of order
2
. The (projectivized) discriminant is an arc connecting one cusp to itself, dividing
X_{1}(2)
into two chambers:

•

One chamber contains the other cusp of
X_{1}(2)
. This chamber consists of translation surfaces with cylindrical cores, so we call it a chamber of
cylinder type
. Translation surfaces in this chamber can be constructed by gluing a cylinder to a copy of the complex plane along a slit. The cusp contained in this chamber corresponds to an “infinitely long" cylinder.

•

The other chamber contains the orbifold point. This chamber consists of degenerate translation surfaces, so we call it a chamber of
degenerate type
. Translation surfaces in this chamber can be constructed by removing a parallelogram from a copy of the complex plane, and then identifying opposite sides. The orbifold point corresponds to the case where the parallelogram is a square.

Translation surfaces in the discriminant can be constructed similarly as degenerate types, by removing a “degenerate parallelogram", whose sides are all parallel.

3.6.
Isoperiodic leaves: conical singularities

As already alluded above, each isoperiodic leaf admits a natural translation atlas that turns the leaf into a translation surface. As such, an isoperiodic leaf admits conical singularities corresponding to translation surfaces obtained by means of degeneration. A translation surface degenerates if a saddle connection shrinks or if its length becomes infinite. Let
(X,\omega)\in\mathcal{H}(1,1;-2)
be a translation surface with period character
\chi
. For a deformation inside an isoperiodic leaf in the stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, the lengths of closed saddle connections are fixed. Therefore, the only saddle connections that can shrink are between the two conical singularities
B
and
W
.

If several saddle connections shrink simultaneously, then they have the same period all along the deformation. Consequently, the angles at the two singularities between them is an integer multiple of
2\pi
. Since conical singularities of translation surfaces of
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
have an angle of
4\pi
, at most two saddle connections can shrink simultaneously. Notice that their union is a simple loop, say
\gamma
, of period zero. In the case
\Gamma
is not a cyclic group, then this happens only if
\gamma
is a peripheral loop around the (unique) pole of the surface. In this case, the degeneration disconnects the surface into flat plane – the domain of the pole – and a flat torus. Such a degeneracy occurs only in the chamber of torus type, see Section §
5
for a precise definition. The local model of this degeneration is just a pointed disk. We compactify by adding a marked point.

For an arithmetic real leaf the picture is quite different from the above one because there is a closed simple loop
\gamma
such that
\Gamma=\chi(\gamma)\,\mathbb{Z}
. If two saddle connection shrink simultaneously in such isoperiodic leaves, the torus degenerates and we get a translation surface of genus zero with a pole of order two and thus two marked points connected by a saddle connection of period
\chi(\gamma)
. The local model of this degeneration is also a pointed disk (because of the symmetries). Once again, we compactify it by adding a marked point.

Otherwise, only one saddle connection shrinks. Algebraically, if the two zeroes of a differential in the stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
collide, then we get a differential with only one zero, that is a differential of the minimal stratum
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
. Conversely, in a translation surface with a single zero of order two, we can perform a surgery called
breaking up
that splits the zero into two simple zeroes connected by an arbitrarily small saddle connection, see
[
4
,
11
]
for details. This surgery consists in replacing a small disk around the zero of order two, see Figure
3
.

Figure 3.
Breaking up a zero of order two into two zeroes of order one, with the courtesy of Corentin Boissy.

By using the period of the saddle connection as a continuous parameter of an analytic deformation we can see that every translation surface of
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
that can be obtained as the deformation of some translation surface in the stratum
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
. The surgery is local and does not affect the absolute periods. What we get is a cover of order three of the pointed disk as a local model for the degeneration. We compactify by adding a conical singularity of angle
6\pi
. The position of the conical singularities of an isoperiodic leaf in
\mathbb{P}\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
depend on its type.

Proposition 3.6
.

In a real leaf, conical singularities belong to the discriminant of
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
. In positive leaves, conical singularities belong to the chamber of cylinder type. Finally, in negative leaves, conical singularities belong to the chamber of degenerate type.

Proof.

The absolute period group
\Gamma
of a conical singularity of an isoperiodic leaf is the same as that of the other differentials of the leaf (breaking up zeroes is a local surgery). For translation surfaces in a stratum with a single zero, saddle connections are necessarily closed and then their periods generate
\Gamma
. In
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
, elements of
\Gamma
are real collinear if and only if the translation surface has a degenerate core formed by a pair of saddle connections with the same direction. In the case of
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
, this locus coincides with the discriminant. For an element of the chamber of cylinder type of
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
, a symplectic basis of the homology is a pair of oriented loops, say
\alpha
and
\beta
with an intersection number of
+1
. We may choose
\alpha
as a closed geodesic of the cylinder and
\beta
as a loop crossing the cylinder (and turning around the slit). The image of their periods in
\mathbb{C}
is clearly a direct basis. Therefore, such conical singularities can only appear in positive leaves. Conversely, the neighborhood of any such differential in
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
(obtained by breaking up) is a positive leaf. Finally, for an element of the chamber of degenerate type of
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
, the translation surface is a flat plane with a parallelogram removed (it contains exactly two saddle connections). We consider
\alpha
and
\beta
two closed loops such that each of them crosses one saddle connection and then turns around the parallelogram removed. The period of each such loop is that of one of the two closed saddle connections. We check easily that if they intersect positively, the parallelogram their periods define in
\mathbb{C}
is negatively oriented.
∎

3.7.
Isoperiodic leaves are hyperbolic Riemann surfaces

For the purpose of proving this fact, we exhibit non-constant holomorphic maps from any isoperiodic leaf to the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
of elliptic curves.

Lemma 3.7
.

For every isoperiodic leaf
L
in either the marked or unmarked stratum
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
,
there is a non-constant holomorphic map
\phi_{L}
from
L
to the moduli space
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
of elliptic curves.

Proof.

For each translation surface of
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
, we forget the location of the zeroes of the differential and mark the double pole in the underlying elliptic curve. In this way, we define a holomorphic map from
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
to
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
. Since the period map defines holomorphic charts and the isoperiodic foliation is a holomorphic foliation, for each isoperiodic leaf
L
, we obtain a holomorphic map
\phi_{L}
from
L
to
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
. If
\phi_{L}
were constant to some complex structure
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
, then
L
would have been contained in the bi-dimensional vector space
V
of meromorphic
1
-forms on
\mathbb{C}/\Lambda
with at most one double pole at the marked point; generated by
dz
and
\wp dz
. Since deformations inside
L
are isoperiodic, then
L
is an affine complex line of
V
modeled on the subspace of exact
1
-forms in
V
. Since there is no nontrivial exact
1
-form in
V
, we obtain a contradiction and
\phi_{L}
is a non-constant holomorphic map.
∎

Corollary 3.8
.

For every isoperiodic leaf
L
in either the marked or unmarked stratum
\overline{\mathcal{H}}
,
L
admits a non-constant bounded holomorphic function.

Proof.

Since
\mathcal{M}_{1,1,}
is biholomorphic to a quotient of the complex disk
\mathbb{D}
,
\phi_{L}
lifts to a non-constant holomorphic map to
\mathcal{D}
.
∎

4.
Chambers of cylinder type

We give a detailed description of these chambers appearing in every isoperiodic leaf of
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
. Following the proof of Proposition
2.4
, a translation surface
(X,\omega)
of cylinder type has a polar domain with four boundary saddle connections, two of which being glued on the sides of a cylinder (formed by two flat triangles). The only possible gluing pattern implies that the two opposite sides are glued on each other identified by a translation. Consequently, the polar domain is the complement of a parallelogram
Q
.

A cylinder is constructed by identifying a pair of opposite sides in a parallelogram
P
. For a translation surface of
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, each boundary side is a closed saddle connection. Each of the two conical singularities belong to exactly one boundary component of the cylinder. Cylinder is foliated by a
1
-parameter family of closed geodesics, each representing the same absolute homology class
\gamma
. We choose the orientation of
\gamma
in such a way that singularity
B
belongs to the left boundary while singularity
W
belongs to the right boundary, see Figure
4
. Homology class
\gamma
has to be primitive and its period
u=\chi(\gamma)
has to be nonzero. We choose the
relative period

z
of the identified sides of parallelogram
Q
as coordinate in the chamber. Any diagonal of the cylinder, oriented from singularity
B
to singularity
W
, has a period of the form
z-v
where
v
is an absolute period such that pair
(u,v)
is the image of symplectic basis by period character
\chi
.

Figure 4.
General construction of a surface of cylinder type, with side identification labeled in red. The two singularities are distinguished by black or white. For an oriented side signified by an arrow, the letter that appears nearby denotes the complex number representing that vector.

Proposition 4.1
.

In stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, we consider the locus formed by translation surfaces realizing a nonzero period character
\chi
(up to a symplectic change of basis) and a nontrivial absolute period
u\in\Gamma\setminus\{{0\}}
such that
u
is the period of a primitive homology class. Then

•

if
Vol_{\chi}\neq 0
or
\Gamma
is dense in a real line of
\mathbb{C}
, there is exactly one chamber
CC_{u}
formed by translation surfaces of cylinder type realizing character
\chi
and such that the appropriately oriented core geodesic of the cylinder realizes a homology class
\gamma
of period
\chi(\gamma)=u
;

•

if
\Gamma=a\,\mathbb{Z}
for some
a\neq 0
and
u=ka
for some nonzero integer
k
, then for each integer
0\leq l<|k|
with
\gcd(l,k)=1
, there exists exactly one chamber
CC_{u,v}=CC_{k,l}
formed by translation surfaces of cylinder type realizing character
\chi
and such that the appropriately oriented core geodesic of the cylinder realizes a homology class
\gamma
of period
\chi(\gamma)=u
, and
v=la
is the period of another homology class
\eta
, where
(\gamma,\eta)
forms a symplectic basis of the homology group.

Moreover, in the translation structure of the isoperiodic leaf containing
CC_{u}
or
CC_{u,v}
, the chamber is a half-plane defined by inequality
\mathfrak{Im}(\bar{u}z)<\min(0,Vol_{\chi})
.

Proof.

By adopting the notation above, a translation surface of cylinder type is completely characterized by complex numbers
u,v,z
. Moreover,
v
is defined up to elements of
u\,\mathbb{Z}
and is required to satisfy the equality
\mathfrak{Im}(\bar{u}v)=Vol_{\chi}
, because
(u,v)
is the image of a symplectic basis. This condition fixes
v
up to
u\,\mathbb{Z}
when
\Gamma
is not a cyclic group.

Suppose
\Gamma=a\,\mathbb{Z}
and let
u=ak
for some integer
k
. For each integer
0\leq l<|k|
, the chambers
CC_{u,v}
with
v=al
, up to
u\,\mathbb{Z}
, are distinct from each other. Indeed, for a fixed parameter
z
, the heights of the cylinders determined by parallelograms having sides of length
u
and
z-v
are distinct, even up to an element
u\,\mathbb{Z}
. Thus, assuming
\chi
and
u
are fixed, and so is
v
in the arithmetic case, translation surfaces with a cylinder of absolute period
u
are parameterized by relative period
z
. The only constraint is that the areas of parallelogram
P
and
Q
should not vanish. These conditions amount to the two inequalities:

•

\mathfrak{Im}\big(\,\bar{u}(z-v)\,\big)<0
or equivalently
\mathfrak{Im}(\bar{u}z)<Vol_{\chi}
;

•

\mathfrak{Im}(\,\bar{u}z\,)<0
.

These constraints combined together yields the condition
\mathfrak{Im}(\bar{u}z)<\min(0,Vol_{\chi})
. Hence translation surfaces with a cylinder of absolute period
u
form a half-plane in an isoperiodic fiber realizing character
\chi
.
∎

Remark 4.2
.

Period
-u
gives the same chamber in the unmarked stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
, but a different chamber in the marked double cover. Also, by the description above, in the arithmetic case where
\Gamma=a\,\mathbb{Z}
and
u=ak
for some nonzero integer
k
, there are
\varphi(|k|)
distinct chambers formed by translation surfaces with a cylinder of absolute period
u
, where
\varphi
is Euler’s totient function.

Finally, we note that the boundary line of chamber
CC_{u}
or
CC_{u,v}
(in the direction
u\,\mathbb{R}
) corresponds to the degeneration of:

•

cylinder
P
(if
Vol_{\chi}<0
);

•

parallelogram
Q
(if
Vol_{\chi}>0
);

•

both of them (if
Vol_{\chi}=0
).

5.
Positive leaves

In this section, we give a complete description of positive leaves. Note that by applying the
\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
-action, we may assume
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}[\,i\,]
.

5.1.
Chamber of torus type

A surface of torus type is constructed as follows: take a flat torus and a complex plane, cut parallel slits on both, and then glue them together via translations; see Figure
5
.

Figure 5.
A surface of torus type, with a long slit

The holonomy vector
\alpha
of the slit, oriented from singularity
B
to singularity
W
, serves as a coordinate on the chamber. Note that as
\alpha\to 0
, we obtain a torus and a complex plane glued at a point, as mentioned in Section §
3.6
. We add this point to the chamber to obtain a metric completion of the leaf.

The holonomy vector of the slit cannot be arbitrary. Indeed, the slit cannot wrap onto itself. In particular, the holonomy vector
\alpha\neq t\gamma
where
\gamma\in\Gamma
is a primitive element, and
t\geq 1
. In local coordinate
\alpha
, the torus chamber is thus a copy of
\mathbb{C}
minus slits of the form
\{t\gamma:t\geq 1\}
for all primitive elements
\gamma\in\Gamma
; see Figure
6
.

5.2.
Geometry of a positive leaf

We can now describe the global geometry of a positive leaf. The whole leaf is obtained by gluing cylinder chambers to the torus chamber.

Indeed, recall that the chamber of torus type is a complex plane with slits of the form
\{t\gamma:t\geq 1\}
for all primitive elements
\gamma\in\Gamma
. For the each slit
\{t\gamma:t\geq 1\}
, approaching it from the left we end up on (half of) the boundary of the cylinder chamber corresponding to
\gamma
, and approaching it from the right we end up on (half of) the boundary of the cylinder chamber corresponding to
-\gamma
. Similarly, the other half of these boundaries are glued to the slit
\{-t\gamma:t\geq 1\}
.

Every chamber of cylinder type is connected to the chamber of torus type so there is only one isoperiodic leaf realizing a given period character
\chi
satisfying
Vol_{\chi}>0
. Besides, there are no chambers of degenerate type in positive leaves, see Lemma
2.5
. This proves Theorem
1.2
in the case of positive leaves.

After gluing, at the point corresponding to each primitive element
\gamma
, the leaf has angle
6\pi
. The Abelian differential for the translation structure on this leaf thus has double zeroes at these points.

It is clear that the leaf supports an involution, given by
\alpha\mapsto-\alpha
in local coordinate we gave on the chamber of torus type, and maps the chamber of cylinder type corresponding to
\gamma
to the chamber of cylinder type corresponding to
-\gamma
. In the quotient, the point
p
gives a simple pole of the quadratic differential, and other singularities have cone angle
3\pi
, so they are simple zeros of the quadratic differential.

We note that the quotient is simply connected. We just have to observe that the quotient of the chamber of torus type is a half-plane with infinitely many slits starting at primitive periods, up to a sign. Each side of the same slit is glued on a half-line bounding the same chamber of cylinder type. In other words, we insert a half-plane inside each slit. We clearly obtain a surface homeomorphic to a plane. In terms of complex structures, the surface is either a complex plane or a complex disk. It follows then from Corollary
3.8
that the surface is biholomorphic to a disk.

Combined with the following lemma, we conclude that a positive leaf is a Loch Ness Monster, i.e. a surface of infinite genus with one end. This gives Theorem
1.3
for positive leaves.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
S
be a topological surface, and let
\mathcal{P}
denote a topological surface homeomorphic to the plane
\mathbb{C}
. Suppose there exists a branched double cover
\pi:S\to\mathcal{P}
, branched over a infinite, discrete set of points. Then
S
is a Loch Ness Monster.

Proof.

First we show that
S
has one end. Suppose not. Since
\pi
is a branched double cover and
\mathcal{P}
is one-ended,
S
has two ends, each covering the unique end of
\mathcal{P}
. Thus for some large enough simply connected compact set
K\subset\mathcal{P}
, the preimage of
\mathcal{P}-K
consists of two connected components. But
\mathcal{P}-K
contains ramification points of
\pi
, so its preimage must be connected, a contradiction.

Next we show that
S
has infinite genus. Indeed, take a disk
D
in
\mathcal{P}
containing exactly two ramification points. Then
\pi^{-1}(D)
is connected, and by Riemann-Hurwitz formula, has Euler characteristic
0
. It has either one or two boundary components. If it has one, then by capping off the boundary component we obtain a closed surface with Euler characteristic
1
, which cannot be. So
\pi^{-1}(D)
has two boundary components, and is therefore a cylinder. There are infinitely many disjoint pairs of ramification points, so we obtain infinitely many handles of
S
. By classification of topological surfaces,
S
is thus a Loch Ness Monster.
∎

Finally, we can also determine the Veech group of a positive leaf as a translation surface, Theorem
1.4
for positive leaves.

Proposition 5.2
.

The Veech group of a positive leaf is a conjugate of
\textnormal{SL}(2,\,\mathbb{Z})
.

Proof.

The action of
g\in\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
takes one positive leaf with absolute period group
\Gamma
to one with
g\Gamma
. Since the translation structure of the leaf is characterized by its absolute period group, the Veech group is exactly the stabilizer of
\Gamma
in
\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
, which is a conjugate of
\textnormal{SL}(2,\,\mathbb{Z})
. This can also be proved by looking at the torus chamber: any element in the Veech group must leave the set of primitive elements in
\Gamma
invariant.
∎

Negative leaves

7.
Non-arithmetic real leaves

Instead of giving a direct description of non-arithmetic real leaves, we obtain their geometry as a limit of negative leaves for the action of a subgroup of
\textnormal{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
.

7.1.
Contraction flow

Definition 7.1
.

For every pair
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}
of directions in
\mathbb{RP}^{1}
,
contraction flow

\mathcal{C}_{\theta_{1},\theta_{2},t}
is the subgroup of
\textnormal{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
conjugated to

(7.1)

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}e^{-t}&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}:t>0\right\}

contracting exponentially direction
\theta_{1}
while preserving direction
\theta_{2}
.

Let
\mathcal{L}_{0}
be the (unique) negative leaf whose absolute period group is
\mathbb{Z}[\,i\,]
while
(\mathcal{C}_{\theta,h,t})_{t\geq 0}
is the contraction flow preserving the horizontal direction while contracting some other direction
\theta
with
\theta\notin\{0,\,\pi\}
. We denote by
\mathcal{L}_{t}
the image of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
under the action of
\mathcal{C}_{h,t}
. If
\Gamma_{t}
is the group of absolute periods of translation surfaces contained in
\mathcal{L}_{t}
, we have
\Gamma_{t}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{\tan(\theta)}\mathbb{Z}
; the action of
\textnormal{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
commutes with the period character. Contracting directions
\theta
for which
\tan(\theta)\notin\mathbb{Q}
provide any dense subgroup of
\mathbb{R}
with two generators.

The action of the contraction flow on the walls-and-chambers structure of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, described in Section §
6
, is the following:

•

chambers of cylinder type remain half-planes but the direction of their boundary line converges to the horizontal line;

•

chambers of degenerate type converge to zero area triangles with three horizontal sides;

•

no boundary side of chambers of degenerate type shrinks provided
\tan(\theta)\notin\mathbb{Q}
.

The family of leaves
(\mathcal{L}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
converges to limit surface
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
as

because all surfaces of the family keep the
discrete singularities
property described in
[
25
]
. We recall that:

Definition 7.2
.

A translation surface has

if for each conical singularity

\epsilon>0
such that the ball
\mathbb{B}(x,\epsilon)
of radius
\epsilon
centered at

does not contain any other singularity.

Limit surface
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
is a non-arithmetic real leaf with:

•

a chamber of cylinder type for each nonzero primitive period of
\Gamma_{t}\longrightarrow\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{\tan(\theta)}\mathbb{Z}
;

•

infinitely many conical singularities of angle
6\pi
;

•

a gluing pattern of boundary segments of chambers inherited from the gluing patterns of chambers of degenerate type in leaf
\mathcal{L}_{0}
.

Leaf
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
is thus a Loch Ness Monster satisfying the properties of Theorem

. It contains chamber of cylinder type

for every possible

. Besides, in a real periodic leaf, there is no chamber of degenerate type. Every surface of degenerate type belongs to the boundary of the chamber of cylinder type. It follows that
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
is the unique leaf realizing character

for non-arithmetic real leaves.

Finally, the quotient of
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}
by the standard involution is simply connected for the same reasons as in the case of negative leaves. In terms of complex structures, the surface is then either a complex plane or a complex disk. It follows from Corollary
3.8

7.2.
Veech group

Finally, we determine the Veech group of a non-arithmetic real leaf as a translation surface. We first need a number-theoretic lemma. To prepare for the statement of the lemma, recall that given a square-free integer
D\geq 2
, the ring of integers of the quadratic field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
is given by
\mathbb{Z}[\gamma]
, where

\gamma=\begin{cases}\sqrt{D}&\text{if }D\equiv 2,3\mod 4\\
\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}&\text{if }D\equiv 1\mod 4\end{cases}

is the standard generator of the ring. The conjugate of any element
s+t\sqrt{D}
with
s,t\in\mathbb{Q}
is given by
\bar{x}=s-t\sqrt{D}
, and the norm of
x
is defined as
N(x)=x\bar{x}
. The norm is multiplicative,
i.e.

N(xy)=N(x)N(y)
.

Note that
x\in\mathbb{Z}[\gamma]
if and only if
N(x)
is an integer, and
x
is a unit in
\mathbb{Z}[\gamma]
if and only if
N(x)=\pm 1
. The units of
\mathbb{Z}[\gamma]
form a group, which is generated by
-1
and a positive fundamental unit
u
.

Let
\tau=(t,l,m)
be a triple of integers so that
t,m\neq 0
and
\gcd(t,l,m)=1
. Consider the
\mathbb{Z}
-module defined as
\Gamma=t\,\mathbb{Z}+(l+m\gamma)\,\mathbb{Z}\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
. For any positive real number
a
, if
a\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma
, then
a
acts as a matrix in the basis
\{t,l+m\gamma\}
. We use
\det(a)
to denote the determinant of this matrix. We have

Lemma 7.4
.

Consider the group

G_{D,\tau}:=\{a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}:a\Gamma=\Gamma,\det(a)=1\}.

We have

(1)

G_{D,\tau}
is a subgroup of units in
\mathbb{Z}[\gamma]
;

(2)

G_{D,\tau}
contains a nontrivial element
\eta=\alpha+\beta\gamma
with
N(\eta)=1
if and only if
m
divides
\beta
, and
t
divides
(\beta/m)N(l+m\gamma)
;

(3)

Let
k
be the smallest non-negative integer so that
u^{k}\in G_{D,q}
. Then
G_{D,\tau}=\langle u^{k}\rangle
.

Proof.

Set
N(\Gamma):=\min\{|{N(x)}|:x\in\Gamma,x\neq 0\}
. Clearly
N(\Gamma)>0
, and
N(a\Gamma)=|N(a)|N(\Gamma)
. This immediately implies that every element in
G_{D,\tau}
is a unit.

For the second part, first note that

\eta\cdot t=\alpha t+\beta t\gamma=\left(\alpha-\frac{\beta l}{m}\right)t+\frac{\beta t}{m}(l+m\gamma).

For this to be in
\Gamma
, we must have
m
divides
\beta t
, and
m
divides
\beta l
. If
\gcd(m,\beta)\neq|m|
, then
t
and
l
share a nontrivial factor with
m
, contradicting the assumption
\gcd(t,l,m)=1
. So we must have
m
divides
\beta
. Set
\beta=\beta_{0}m
for some integer
\beta_{0}
. Next we can calculate

\begin{split}\eta\cdot(l+m\gamma)&=\alpha l+\beta m\gamma^{2}+(\beta l+\alpha m)\gamma=\alpha l+\beta mD-(\beta_{0}l+\alpha)l+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha)(l+m\gamma)\\
&=\beta_{0}m^{2}D-\beta_{0}l^{2}+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha)(l+m\gamma)\\
&=-\beta_{0}N(l+m\gamma)+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha)(l+m\gamma)\end{split}

when
\gamma=\sqrt{D}
, and

\begin{split}\eta\cdot(l+m\gamma)&=\alpha l+\beta m\left(\gamma+\frac{D-1}{4}\right)+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha)m\gamma\\
&=\alpha l+\beta_{0}m^{2}\frac{D-1}{4}-(\beta_{0}l+\alpha+\beta)l+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha+\beta)(l+m\gamma)\\
&=\beta_{0}\left(m^{2}\frac{D-1}{4}-l^{2}-ml\right)+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha+\beta)(l+m\gamma)\\
&=-\beta_{0}N(l+m\gamma)+(\beta_{0}l+\alpha+\beta)(l+m\gamma)\end{split}

when
\gamma=\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}
. In either case, for this to be in
\Gamma
, we must have
t
divides
\beta_{0}N(l+m\gamma)
.

On the other hand, we have when
\gamma=\sqrt{D}
,

\begin{split}\det(\eta)&=(\alpha-\beta_{0}l)(\beta_{0}l+\alpha)+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)=\alpha^{2}-\beta_{0}^{2}l^{2}+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)\\
&=\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}D+\beta_{0}^{2}(m^{2}D-l^{2})+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)\\
&=N(\eta)-\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)=1,\end{split}

and when
\gamma=\frac{1+\sqrt{D}}{2}
,

\begin{split}\det(\eta)&=(\alpha-\beta_{0}l)(\beta_{0}l+\alpha+\beta)+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)\\
&=\alpha^{2}-\beta_{0}^{2}l^{2}+\alpha\beta-\beta\beta_{0}l+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)\\
&=\alpha^{2}+\alpha\beta+\beta^{2}\frac{1-D}{4}-\beta_{0}^{2}\left(m^{2}\frac{1-D}{4}+ml-l^{2}\right)+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)\\
&=N(\eta)-\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)+\beta_{0}^{2}N(l+m\gamma)=1.\end{split}

Thus it is easy to see that when the divisibility conditions are met, we indeed have
\eta\in G_{D,\tau}
. Finally, the third part follows from the fact that any positive unit is a power of the fundamental unit
u
.
∎

Note that if
G_{D,\tau}
contains a nontrivial element
\eta
,
\eta^{2}
is always of norm
1
, so the lemma above covers all cases.

Proposition 7.5
.

The Veech group of a non-arithmetic real leaf is a conjugate of one of the following groups:

•

V=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{R},a>0\right\}
if the ratios of periods do not all belong to a fixed quadratic number field;

•

V_{D,\tau}=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}u&b\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{R},a>0,u\in G_{D,\tau}\right\}
if up to scaling
\Gamma=t\mathbb{Z}+(l+m\gamma)\mathbb{Z}
for some triple of integers
\tau=(t,l,m)
with
m\neq 0
and
\gcd(t,l,m)=1
, and
\gamma
is the standard generator of the ring of integers in
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
for some square free integer
D\geq 2
.

Proof.

Indeed, any element in the Veech group for the leaf with absolute period group contained in
\mathbb{R}
must preserve the real line. Such an element is of the form
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
0&c\end{pmatrix}
where
a,c\in\mathbb{R}^{\ast}
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
. Besides, elements of the Veech group have to map
\mathbb{Z}+\frac{1}{\tan(\theta)}\mathbb{Z}
to itself. In other words, scaling by
a
preserve group
\Gamma
. Unless
\frac{1}{\tan(\theta)}
satisfies a quadratic equation with integer coefficients, this forces
a
to be either
1
or
-1
. We obtain precisely the group
V
. Conversely, any element in
V
leaves each chamber invariant and its boundary fixed, so is indeed in the Veech group. In the remaining cases, ratios of absolute periods belong to a quadratic number field
\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D})
where
D\geq 2
is a square-free integer. We can consider, up to a scaling of
\chi
, that
\Gamma
is of the form
\Gamma=t\,\mathbb{Z}+(l+m\gamma)\,\mathbb{Z}
. It follows from Lemma
7.4
that the coefficient
a
belongs to
G_{D,\tau}
; provided
a>0
. Conversely, any element in
V_{D,\tau}
simply permute the chambers.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

Note that it is possible
G_{D,\tau}=\{1\}
,
e.g.
we may choose
D=m=3
, in which case
\beta
is never a multiple of
3
, so the second group can be the same as the first for some parameters. On the other hand, Lemma
7.4
gives a criterion to construct parameters where this group is nontrivial.

8.
Arithmetic real leaves

In this section, we give a complete description of arithmetic real leaves. In this case, the absolute period group
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}a
for some nonzero complex number
a
. Up to the
\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
-action, we may assume
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
.

We have discussed chambers of cylinder type in an arithmetic real leaf in §
4
, and it is easy to see that chambers of degenerate type do not exist in such a leaf. So it remains to understand when and how chambers of cylinder type are glued together.

8.1.
Degeneration towards boundary

We first discuss how surfaces in a chamber of cylinder type degenerate as we approach the boundary of the chamber. For each positive integer
k
and any integer
l
so that
0\leq l<k
and
\gcd(k,l)=1
, there are two chambers of cylinder type in the marked stratum, each is a half plane bounded by
\mathbb{R}
. One of them, denoted by
CC^{+}_{k,l}
, corresponds to the parameters
u=k
and
v=l
, while the other, denoted by
CC^{-}_{k,l}
, corresponds to
u=-k
and
v=-l
. In the coordinate
z
,
CC^{+}_{k,l}
is the lower half plane, while
CC^{-}_{k,l}
is the upper half plane. They are images of each other under
z\mapsto-z
, so we focus on
CC^{+}_{k,l}
.

To simplify our discussion, let
S(l_{1},l_{2},l_{3})
, where
l_{1},l_{2},l_{3}>0
, be the translation surface of degenerate type obtained as follows. Cut a complex plane along a horizontal slit of length
l_{1}+l_{2}+l_{3}
. The upper slit is divided into three segments from left to right: segment
A
of length
l_{1}
, segment
B
of length
l_{2}
, and segment
C
of length
l_{3}
. The lower slit is similarly divided into three segments from left to right: segment
C^{\prime}
of length
l_{3}
, segment
B^{\prime}
of length
l_{2}
, and segment
A
of length
l_{1}
. Identify
A
with
A^{\prime}
,
B
with
B^{\prime}
, and
C
with
C^{\prime}
by translations we obtain a translation surface in
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
.

Notice that if we allow one of
l_{i}
’s to be zero, we obtain a surface in
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
.

The case
\bm{l=0}

Then
k=1
. Consider the surface with coordinate
z=n+t+i\epsilon
, where
n\geq 1
is an integer,
0\leq t<1
, and
\epsilon>0
. Letting
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain a surface of degenerate type, and it is easy to see that it is precisely
S(t,1-t,n+t)
.

The point
z=0
does not lie in the leaf it self, but its metric completion. It corresponds to a pinched torus: the surface is obtained by identifying two points on a complex plane. This gives the nodal curve in the Deligne-Mumford compactification of
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
.

Translation surfaces corresponding to coordinates with negative real part can be described similarly – they are, in fact reflections of those with positive real part. In particular, the nonzero integers on
\mathbb{R}
correspond to points in
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
.

The case
\bm{l\neq 0}

Consider the surface with coordinate
z=t+i\epsilon
, so the diagonal of the cylinder is given by
z-v=t-l+i\epsilon
. We consider four cases.

•

l-k<t<0
In this case,
-k<\mathfrak{Re}(z-v)<-l
. Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, the cylinder degenerates, and we obtain the surface
S(|t|,k-l-|t|,l+|t|)
.

•

0<t<l
In this case,
-l<\mathfrak{Re}(z-v)<0
. Letting
\epsilon\to 0
, we get the surface of degenerate type
S(k+t-l,l-t,t)
.

•

-(n+1)k+l<t<-nk+l
for some integer
n\geq 1
In this case,
-(n+1)k<\mathfrak{Re}(z-v)<-nk
. Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we get the surface of degenerate type
S(|t|,(n+1)k-l-|t|,l+|t|-nk)
.

•

nk+l<t<(n+1)k+l
for some integer
n\geq 0
In this case,
nk<\mathfrak{Re}(z-v)<(n+1)k
. Taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we get the surface of degenerate type
S(t-l-nk,(n+1)k+l-t,t)
.

In particular, the points
nk+l
for any integer
n
together with
0
on the real line correspond to surfaces in
\mathcal{H}(2,-2)
.

The description for the boundary of the chamber
CC^{-}_{k,l}
can be obtained by taking
w\mapsto-w
. Note that for surfaces of degenerate type on the boundary, taking
w\mapsto-w
yields the same surface but with a different marking of its zeros.

8.2.
Gluing of chambers

We now describe how different chambers are glued together. Fix a pair of integers
(k,l)
with
0\leq l<k
and
\gcd(k,l)=1
. As discussed above, the corresponding cylinder chamber
CC^{+}_{k,l}
is the lower half plane bounded by the real line, which is divided by the points
nk+l
for
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
0
into segments.

First consider the segment
[l-k,0]
on the boundary of
CC^{+}_{k,l}
. Let
k^{\prime}=k-l
,
n^{\prime}
be the unique integer satisfying
\frac{2l-k}{k-l}<n^{\prime}\leq\frac{l}{k-l},
and
l^{\prime}=(n^{\prime}+1)l-n^{\prime}k
. Then the chamber
CC^{-}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
has a boundary segment
[-(n^{\prime}+1)k^{\prime}-l^{\prime},-n^{\prime}k^{\prime}-l^{\prime}]=[l,k]
. One can easily check from our discussion in the last section that the corresponding surfaces on this segment match those on
[l-k,0]
of
CC^{+}_{k,l}
.

Next consider the segment
[-(n+1)k+l,-nk+l]
for some natural number
n\geq 1
. Set
k^{\prime}=(n+1)k-l
and
l^{\prime}=k
. The chamber
CC^{-}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
has a boundary segment
[-l^{\prime},0]=[-k,0]
, where the corresponding flat surfaces match those on
[-(n+1)k+l,-nk+l]
of
CC^{+}_{k,l}
.

For the segment
[0,l]
(which does not exist for
l=0
), set
k^{\prime}=l
,
l^{\prime}=(n^{\prime}+1)l-k
where
n^{\prime}
is the unique integer
\geq k/l-1
and
<k/l
. It is easy to see that flat surfaces on the segment
[n^{\prime}k^{\prime}-l^{\prime},(n^{\prime}+1)k^{\prime}-l^{\prime}]=[k-l,k]
on the boundary of
CC^{-}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
match those on
[0,l]
of
CC^{+}_{k,l}
. In fact, it is the “inverse" of the case above.

For the segment
[nk+l,(n+1)k+l]
, where
n
is a nonnegative integer, set
k^{\prime}=(n+1)k+l
and
l^{\prime}=nk+l
. Then it is easy to see that flat surfaces on the segment
[0,k^{\prime}-l^{\prime}]=[0,k]
on the boundary of
\mathcal{C}^{-}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
match those on
[nk+l,(n+1)k+l]
of
\mathcal{C}^{+}_{k,l}
. In fact, it is the “inverse" of the first case.

This description of gluing implies the following:

Lemma 8.1
.

For any period character
\chi
such that
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
, the locus in marked stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
realizing character
\chi
, up to a symplectic change of basis, is connected.

Proof.

Since
CC^{+}_{1,0}
is glued to
CC^{-}_{1,0}
along the segment
[-1,1]
, we only need to show that via gluing we can reach any pair of admissible indices
(k,l)
from
(1,0)
. We note that from
(k,l)
, we can then reach
((n+1)k-l,k)
for any natural number
n
.

We prove this by induction on
l
. Since we can reach
(n+1,1)
for any natural number
n
from
(1,0)
, the statement holds for
l=1
.

Suppose the statement holds for any
l\leq l_{0}
. For
l=l_{0}+1
, take
t\leq l_{0}
with
\gcd(t,l)=1
. By inductive hypothesis,
(l,t)
can be reached by gluing from
(1,0)
. Then we can also reach
(nl+(l-t),l)
for any natural number
n
. This in fact gives all admissible pairs with second index
l
. So the statement also holds for
l_{0}+1
. By induction, any admissible pair of index can be reached by gluing, as desired.
∎

8.3.
Geometry of arithmetic real leaves

We start with the following:

Lemma 8.2
.

The singularities of any arithmetic real leaf in the marked stratum
\mathcal{H}(1,1,-2)
all have cone angle
6\pi
.

Proof.

Note that by the description of gluing, every singularity comes from the points
nk+l
and
0
on the boundary of
CC^{+}_{k,l}
, or
nk-l
and
0
on the boundary of
CC^{-}_{k,l}
. Moreover, it is easy to see that every such point is glued to
0
on the boundary of some chamber. So it suffices to show that
6
chambers are glued at
0
for each
CC^{+}_{k,l}
.

It is then easy to check the following, based on our description of gluing:

•

When
2l-k>0
, the point
0
on
\partial CC^{+}_{k,l}
is glued to the point
-l
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
where
k^{\prime}=k-l
and
l^{\prime}=(n^{\prime}+1)l-n^{\prime}k
for the unique integer
n^{\prime}\in(\frac{2l-k}{k-l},\frac{l}{k-l}]
, as well as the point
-k+l
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{l,2l-k}
, the point
0
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{k,l}
,
l
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{k^{\prime},l^{\prime}}
, and
k-l
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{l,2l-k}
. So
6
chambers are glued here.

•

When
2l-k<0
, the point
0
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{k,l}
is glued to the point
-l
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{k-l,l}
, as well as the point
-k+l
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{l,l^{\prime}}
where
l^{\prime}=(n^{\prime}+2)l-k
for the unique integer
n^{\prime}\in[k/l-2,k/l-1)
, the point
0
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{k,l}
,
l
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{k-l,l}
, and
k-l
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{l,l^{\prime}}
. So
6
chambers are glued here.

•

When
k=2l
, the only possible pair is
(k,l)=(2,1)
. In this case,
0
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{2,1}
is glued to both
-1
and
1
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{1,0}
, both
-1
and
1
on
\partial{CC}^{+}_{1,0}
, and
0
on
\partial{CC}^{-}_{2,1}
. Counting the chambers
{CC}^{\pm}_{1,0}
twice, we still have
6
chambers glued here.

We have dealt with all possibilities, so we are done.
∎

Recall that the point
0
on the boundary of
{CC}^{\pm}_{1,0}
corresponds a pinched torus, so it does not lie on the leaf, but in its metric completion. In this completion, it does not produce a singularity, since the two chambers
CC^{\pm}_{1,0}
are glued along the segment
[-1,1]
. We refer to the corresponding point on the completion of the leaf as its
center
.

As in the case of positive and negative leaves, a real arithmetic leaf supports an involution
\iota
, given by identifying
{CC}^{\pm}_{k,l}
via
w\mapsto-w
. We have

Lemma 8.3
.

The quotient of the completion of the leaf with absolute period group
\mathbb{Z}
by the involution
\iota
is a complex disk. The projection is ramified at the cone points and the center.

Proof.

The only statement that requires a proof is the topology of the quotient. The others follow immediately from the description of gluing and the definition of
\iota
.

Under
\iota
,
CC_{k,l}^{+}
is identified with
CC_{k,l}^{-}
, so we will use
CC_{k,l}
to denote the corresponding chamber in the quotient. Starting with
CC_{1,0}
, we can obtain the whole leaf inductively as follows.

•

We start by gluing the segment
[0,1]
to
[-1,0]
on
\partial CC_{1,0}
. This creates a cone angle of
\pi
at the center. Let the resulting surface be
\mathcal{L}_{0}
. The boundary of
\mathcal{L}_{0}
is topologically a line, being images of
[1,\infty)
and
(-\infty,-1]
on
\partial CC_{1,0}
, where
\pm 1
maps to the same point
V^{0}
.

•

The segment
[-1,1]
on
\partial\mathcal{CC}_{2,1}
is glued to
\mathcal{L}_{0}
, so that
0
on
\partial\mathcal{CC}_{2,1}
is glued to
V^{0}
. Let this new surface be
\mathcal{L}_{1}
. It has two boundary components, and each boundary component consists of two rays coming from the boundary of two different chambers meeting at a point. Let this point be
V^{1}_{1}
and
V^{1}_{2}
for the two components.

•

Inductively, the surface
\mathcal{L}_{n}
has
2^{n}
boundary components, each with a distinguished point
V^{n}_{i}
. The next surface
\mathcal{L}_{n+1}
is obtained from
\mathcal{L}_{n}
by gluing the origin of same chamber
CC_{k_{i},l_{i}}
to
V^{n}_{i}
for each
i
. This creates
2^{n+1}
boundary components, each with a distinguished point
V^{n+1}_{j}
.

It is easy to see that the resulting surface
\mathcal{L}_{\infty}=\bigcup_{n\geq 0}\mathcal{L}_{n}
is thus topologically a plane. In fact, the set of surfaces of degenerate type in the quotient form a binary tree rooted at the center; see Figure
9
. The branching points of the tree are precisely the zeros of the quadratic differential. If we identify the ends of the tree with a Cantor set, the chambers can be identified with the intervals complementary to the Cantor set.

In terms of complex structures, the completion of the leaf is either a complex plane or a complex disk. It follows from Corollary
3.8
that the leaf itself must be a punctured disk, so the completion is biholomorphic to a disk.
∎

The root and branching points of the binary tree are the pole and the zeros, respectively, of the quadratic differential defining the flat structure of the leaf

The gluing described above implies that real arithmetic leaves satisfy the properties of Theorem
1.3
. Indeed, applying Lemma
5.1
again, we conclude that the leaf is a Loch Ness Monster. Moreover, in the quotient by involution, the simple pole of the quadratic differential defining the flat structure comes from the center of the leaf.

Again, we remark that while this gives the same topology as positive and negative leaves, the translation structure is very different.

8.4.
Veech group

Finally, we determine the Veech group of an arithmetic real leaf as a translation surface,
i.e.
we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Proposition 8.4
.

The Veech group of an arithmetic real leaf is a conjugate of the following group:

V=\left\{\pm\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\
0&a\end{pmatrix}:a,b\in\mathbb{R},a>0\right\}.

Proof.

Indeed, any element in the Veech group must preserve the real line, and maps
\mathbb{Z}
to itself. This gives precisely the group
V
. Conversely, any element in
V
leaves each chamber invariant and its boundary fixed, so is indeed in the Veech group.
∎

9.
Conformal geometry of leaves

In this section, we discuss the conformal geometry of leaves in the unmarked stratum. This is written as a separate section to include more details without diverging too much from the main focus in previous sections.

9.1.
Biholomorphic maps to the Teichmüller space

Generic case

In the generic case, we construct an explicit map from each leaf to the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}
, which is a lift of the map to
\mathcal{M}_{1,1}
.

Suppose first that the period group
\Gamma
is a lattice of
\mathbb{C}
. For simplicity, we may assume
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}[i]
. Given a point
(X,\omega)
on the leaf, we have an isomorphism
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})\cong\mathbb{Z}[i]
via integration of
\omega
. Moreover, if we stay on a positive (resp. negative) leaf, this isomorphism also preserves (reverses) the symplectic pairing. Thus each point is a
homologically marked
translation surface. In genus one, the space of homologically marked elliptic curve is the same as the Teichmüller space, as the Torelli group is trivial.

Clearly the map defined above is injective: if
(X_{1},\omega_{1})
and
(X_{2},\omega_{2})
are two translation surfaces giving the same marked surface, then
X_{1}=X_{2}
, and
\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}
is an exact holomorphic 1-form. Thus
\omega_{1}=\omega_{2}
as well. The map is also surjective. Indeed, given any elliptic curve
\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau)
where
\tau\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, a holomorphic differential with a double pole is given by
\omega=(a+b\wp_{\tau})dz
, where
\wp_{\tau}
is the Weierstrass
\wp
-function. Solving for
a,b
in

(9.1)

\int_{0}^{1}(a+b\wp_{\tau})dz=1\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{\tau}(a+b\wp_{\tau})dz=\pm i

we obtain a differential with period group
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Non-arithmetic real case

Essentially the same argument works for the non-arithmetic real case as well: without loss of generality we may assume
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}+\omega\mathbb{Z}
, where
\omega
is a irrational number. Replacing
\pm i
with
\omega
in the arguments above, we obtain the desired map.

Arithmetic real case

Next consider the case of arithmetic real leaves. Again, for simplicity, we may assume
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}
. Recall that the leaf
\mathcal{L}
with period group
\Gamma
is biholomorphic to a punctured disk. We can construct a covering map from
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}
to
\mathcal{L}
as follows. Indeed, for any
\tau\in\mathbb{H}^{2}
, we can solve for
a,b
in

\int_{0}^{1}(a+b\wp_{\tau})dz=0\qquad\textnormal{and}\qquad\int_{0}^{\tau}(a+b\wp_{\tau})dz=1

as above. This determines a point
(X,\omega)
on the leaf with
X\cong\mathbb{C}/(\mathbb{Z}+\mathbb{Z}\tau)
and
\omega=a+b\wp_{\tau}
. However, since the horizontal period is zero, it is easy to see that the translation surface we obtain for
\tau
is the same as that for
\tau+1
. In particular, the map described above from
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}\cong\mathbb{H}^{2}
to
\mathcal{L}
descends to a map from
\mathbb{H}^{2}/\langle z\mapsto z+1\rangle
to
\mathcal{L}
.

We claim this latter map is bijective. Indeed, an inverse can be constructed as follows. Given
(X,\omega)
, we can choose a symplectic basis
\alpha,\beta
for
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
so that
\int_{\alpha}\omega=0
and
\int_{\beta}\omega=1
. This gives a marking on
H_{1}(X,\mathbb{Z})
. Given
\alpha
, the choice of
\beta
is not unique, since we can always replace
\beta
by
\beta+n\alpha
for any integer
n
. So we obtain a map from
\mathcal{L}
to
\mathbb{H}/\langle z\mapsto z+1\rangle
, which is clearly the inverse of the map defined in the previous paragraph.

9.2.
The Teichmüller geometry of cylinder chambers

Recall that the Teichmüller space
T(S)
is endowed with a mapping class group invariant complete metric
d_{T}
called the Teichmüller metric. Given two points in Teichmüller space
T(S)
represented by
f:S\to X
and
g:S\to Y
, the Teichmüller distance is given by

(9.2)

d_{T}([f],[g]):=\frac{1}{2}\log\inf\{K:h:X\to Y\text{ is $K$-quasiconformal, and homotopic to }g\circ f^{-1}\}.

For
S=S_{1,1}
, the Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}=\mathcal{T}(S_{1,1})
is bi-holomorphic to
\mathbb{H}^{2}
, and the Teichmüller metric there agrees with the hyperbolic metric (up to a constant multiple).

Recall that every cylinder chamber
\mathcal{C}
is a half plane, whose boundary is parallel to
\mathbb{R}u
for some complex number
u
. Denote by
X_{t}
the point in Teichmüller space
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}
corresponding to
tu
on the boundary of
\mathcal{C}
. Since the each leaf is biholomorphic to (the quotient) of
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}\cong\mathbb{H}^{2}
, we may map
\mathcal{C}
conformally (and hence isometrically) to a region in the upper half plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
so that the limit point at infinity of the chamber is
\infty
, and
X_{0}
corresponds to the point
i
. Let
\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}(t)
denotes the point
X_{t}
corresponds to.

The main goal of this section is to prove the following:

Theorem 9.1
.

For any cylinder chamber
\mathcal{C}
, we have
\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}(t)\asymp t+i\log t
. More precisely, there exist constants
D,T
depending only on the leaf containing
\mathcal{C}
so that
d_{\mathbb{H}^{2}}(\sigma_{\mathcal{C}}(t),t+i\log t)\leq D
when
|t|\geq T
.

In particular,
\mathcal{C}
does not contain any horodisks, and is also not contained in a
D
-neighborhood of some complete Teichmüller geodesic for any
D>0
.

As before, for simplicity, we assume the period group
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}[i]
. The result for other leaves can be obtained via the
\mathrm{GL}^{+}(2,\mathbb{R})
-action.

First consider the torus obtained as follows. Let
P
be the unit square
[0,1]\times[0,1]
and
Q
the rectangle
[0,1]\times[0,s]
for some
s>0
. As in §
4
, we obtain a torus
T_{s}
by gluing the vertical sides of
P
, the vertical sides of
\hat{\mathbb{C}}-Q
, and the corresponding horizontal sides of
P
and
\hat{\mathbb{C}}-Q
. By horizontal and vertical reflection symmetry, it is easy to see that
T_{s}
corresponds to a point
i\tau(s)
on the imaginary axis of
\mathcal{T}_{1,1}\cong\mathbb{H}^{2}
. We can estimate
\tau(s)
as follows.

Lemma 9.2
.

The function
\tau(s)
satisfies
\tau(s)\asymp\log(s)
.

Conjugacy of action of

Recall that

by isometries.

Proposition 9.5
.

{\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}
on the boundary at infinity.

For simplicity, we focus on positive leaves. The case for negative leaves is entirely similar. Without loss of generality, we may also assume the period group is
\mathbb{Z}[i]
.

Recall that a cylinder chamber is parametrized by a primitive element

, up to multiplication by

are relatively prime integers. An element

maps the chamber
\mathcal{C}_{u}
associated with

On the other hand, fixing a base surface

and a symplectic basis
(\alpha,\beta)
for

, each point

on

corresponds to a pinched hyperbolic surface where the simple closed curve

is pinched. Moreover, an element
\gamma\in{\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})}

\gamma\cdot p/q=\gamma\cdot\begin{pmatrix}p\\

Thus to prove Proposition

is precisely

in

and the continuity of actions. But the discussions in the previous section imply that the point at infinity of

corresponds to pinching the curve with period

Appendix A
Equilateral triangulation, true trees and conformal structure of arithmetic leaves

In this appendix, we describe another perspective of the conformal structure of arithmetic leaves, inspired by recent work of Ivrii, Lin, Rohde, and Sygal
[
20
]
.

Equilateral triangulation and true trees

A
Belyi function
is a holomorphic map
f
from a Riemann surface
X
to
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
with only
3
critical values (usually taken to be
0,1,\infty
). Equivalently,
X
admits an
equilateral triangulation
, by taking the inverse images under
f
of the upper and lower half planes in
\mathbb{C}
. Conformally, we may reconstruct
X
by gluing identical pieces of equilateral triangles according to the combinatorics given by the triangulation.

Note that not every Riemann surface admits an equilateral triangulation. A classical result of Belyi states that a compact Riemann surfaces admits an equilateral triangulation iff it is algebraic
[
2
]
. On the other hand, by a recent result of Bishop and Rempe, every noncompact Riemann surface admits an equilateral triangulation
[
3
]
.

If
X=\hat{\mathbb{C}}
and the Belyi function
f
is a polynomial, then it is also called a
Shabat polynomial
. It is easy to see that in this case
f^{-1}([0,1])
is a tree. Such a tree is called a
true tree
, and up to Möbius transformations, every finite tree in the plane is equivalent to a unique true tree.

True trees are
conformally balanced
, in the sense that the harmonic measure seen from infinity of every edge on either side is the same. Equivalently, the Riemann mapping from the complement of a true tree in
\hat{\mathbb{C}}
to
\hat{\mathbb{C}}-\mathbb{D}
fixing
\infty
maps each side of the edges to an circular arc of equal length.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be the (unmarked) arithmetic real leaf with period group
\mathbb{Z}
. Then
\mathcal{L}
admits an equilateral triangulation. Indeed, each chamber is identified with the upper half plane, which can be tiled by vertical half strips bounded by vertical rays starting at integers. Mapping the vertex at infinity of each triangle to
\infty
, we get the corresponding Belyi function
f:\mathcal{L}\to\mathbb{C}
. The preimage
f^{-1}([0,1])
gives precisely the walls of the chambers, shown in Figure
9
.

Approximation by finite trees

Let
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
be the trivalent infinite tree with a distinguished vertex
v
, and
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the subtree induced by all vertices of distance
n
from
v
. Denote also by
\mathcal{T}_{n}
the conformally balanced form of the tree, normalized so that the Riemann mapping
\varphi_{n}:\hat{\mathbb{C}}-\mathbb{D}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}-\mathcal{T}_{n}
has expansion
z\mapsto z+O(1/z)
at infinity. Then the main results in
[
20
]
state:

•

The trees
\mathcal{T}_{n}
converge in the Hausdorff topology to an infinite trivalent tree union a Jordan curve
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\cup\partial\Omega
;

•

The corresponding Shabat polynomials
p_{n}
converge to a Belyi function
F:\Omega\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, so that
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}=F^{-1}([0,1])
;

•

Each connected component
\mathcal{C}
of
\Omega-\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is a disk, whose boundary meets
\partial\Omega
at a unique point and otherwise consists of edges of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
.

•

More precisely, the domain
\Omega
is the so-called
developed deltoid
, studied in
[
23
]
.

In particular,
\Omega
is conformally equivalent to the surface obtained by gluing half planes along the infinite tree
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
, in the spirit of Figure
9
.

Inspired by this, we consider the following way of producing a conformal image of the arithmetic leaf
\mathcal{L}
. Instead of looking at the rooted binary tree in Figure
9
, we look at a degree
3
cover of it ramified over the root vertex
v
. This tree is combinatorially the same as
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
, but each edge is assigned a weight according to its length in the period coordinates, as marked in Figure
9
. We still denote this tree by
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
. Similarly we have the level
n
subtree
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and the corresponding weight function on its edges.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
be the tree obtained from
\mathcal{T}_{n}
by adding
t-1
interior vertices to an edge with weight
t
. In this way, in the corresponding true form, the harmonic measure of an arc between branching points viewed from infinity is proportional to the weight of the corresponding edge in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. We similarly obtain
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{*}
. Again, we use the same symbols
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*},\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{*}
to denote the corresponding true trees, normalized so that the Riemann mapping
\varphi_{n}^{*}:\hat{\mathbb{C}}-\mathbb{D}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}-\mathcal{T}^{*}_{n}
has expansion
z\mapsto z+O(1/z)
at infinity. The estimates in
[
20
]
can be adapted to prove the following:

•

A subsequence of the trees
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
converge in the Hausdorff topology to
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{*}
union a Jordan curve
\partial\Omega^{*}
;

•

The corresponding Shabat polynomials
p_{n}^{*}
converge to a Belyi function
F^{*}:\Omega^{*}\to\hat{\mathbb{C}}
, so that
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{*}={F^{*}}^{-1}([0,1])
;

•

Each connected component
\mathcal{C}
of
\Omega-\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
is a disk, whose boundary meets
\partial\Omega^{*}
at a unique point, and otherwise consists of edges of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}^{*}
.

•

In fact
\Omega^{*}
is conformally equivalent to a degree
3
cover of the leaf
\mathcal{L}
, with the cusp at the center filled in.

See Figure

[

]
, although due to a break in symmetry, many estimates need to be adapted here. We leave the details to an upcoming sequel.

In
