From Ball’s cube slicing inequality to Khinchin-type inequalities for negative moments
Giorgos Chasapis, Hermann König, Tomasz Tkocz

Abstract
We establish a sharp moment comparison inequality between an arbitrary negative moment and the second moment for sums of independent uniform random variables, which extends Ball’s cube slicing inequality.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification.
Primary 60E15; Secondary 26D15.

Key words.
cube slicing, Khinchin inequality, sharp moment comparison, sums of independent random variables, uniform random variables, uniform spherically symmetric random variables.

1
Introduction

Ball’s celebrated cube slicing inequality established in
[
3
]
states that the maximal volume cross-section of the centred cube
[-1,1]^{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by a hyperplane (a subspace of codimension
1
) equals
2^{n-1}\sqrt{2}
, attained by the hyperplane with normal vector
(\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},0,\dots,0)
(see also
[
4
]
). Khinchin-type inequalities provide moment comparison, typically for weighted sums of independent identically distributed (i.i.d.) random variables. The classical one concerns symmetric random signs and goes back to the work
[
18
]
of Khinchin. Such inequalities are instrumental in studying unconditional convergence and are used extensively in (functional) analysis and geometry, particularly in (local) theory of Banach spaces. We refer to several works
[
2
,
13
,
20
,
23
,
25
,
26
,
30
,
31
,
34
,
35
]
for further background and references (particularly,
[
2
]
provides a detailed historic account on Khinchin inequalities with sharp constants).

The main motivation for this article and its starting point is a fact well-known to experts that Ball’s inequality can be viewed as a Khinchin-type inequality (the dual question of extremal volume hyperplane-projections of convex bodies is also linked to Khinchin-type inequalities, see for example
[
5
,
6
,
9
]
). An elementary derivation can be sketched as follows. For a unit vector
a=(a_{1},\dots,a_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let
f
be the density of
X=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}
, where
U_{1},\dots,U_{n}
are i.i.d. uniform on
[-1,1]
. Then the
(n-1)
-volume of the cross-section of the cube
[-1,1]^{n}
by the hyperplane
a^{\perp}
perpendicular to
a
is
\Vol_{n-1}\big([-1,1]^{n}\cap a^{\perp}\big)=2^{n}f(0)
. On the other hand, for every symmetric unimodal bounded random variable
X
with density
f
, we have

f(0)=\|f\|_{\infty}=\lim_{p\to 1-}\frac{1-p}{2}\mathbb{E}|X|^{-p}

(
X
is called symmetric if it has the same distribution as
-X
).
Thus Ball’s inequality, put probabilistically, says that for every unit vector
a
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\lim_{p\to 1-}(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}\right|^{-p}\leqslant\sqrt{2}.

Our main result shows in particular that not only does this inequality hold in the limit, but also for every
p\in(p_{0},1)
, where
p_{0}=0.793...\,
. To view this inequality as actual moment comparison, let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\dots
be i.i.d. random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
uniform on the centered Euclidean unit sphere
S^{2}
. As a result of Archimedes’ hat-box theorem and rotational invariance, the left hand side can be rewritten as
\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-1}
, where
\|\cdot\|
stands for the standard Euclidean norm on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(see Lemma
3
below). We thus have the following identity for a unit vector
a
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

2^{1-n}\Vol_{n-1}\big([-1,1]^{n}\cap a^{\perp}\big)=\lim_{p\to 1-}(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}\right|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-1}.

(1)

For a generalisation, see Proposition 3.2 in
[
24
]
.
As a result, we can rephrase Ball’s inequality as the following sharp
L_{-1}-L_{2}
Khinchin-type inequality: for every
n
and every reals
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
,

\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-1}\leqslant\sqrt{2}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{-1/2}.

(2)

We extend this to a sharp
L_{-p}-L_{2}
moment comparison for
p\in(0,1)
with arbitrary matrix-valued coefficients (Corollary
4
below). We refer to
[
2
,
20
,
23
,
27
]
for sharp results for positive moments.

We describe our results in the next section and then present our proofs, preceded with a short overview of them. We conclude with a summary highlighting possible future work.
Throughout,
\!\left\langle x,y\right\rangle\!=\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}y_{j}
denotes the standard scalar product on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\|x\|=\sqrt{\!\left\langle x,x\right\rangle\!}
is the Euclidean norm whose unit sphere and closed unit ball are denoted by
S^{d-1}
and
B_{2}^{d}
, respectively. Moreover,
e_{j}
is the
j
-th vector of the standard basis whose
j
-th coordinate is
1
and the rest are
0
.

2
Results

Let
U_{1},U_{2}
be i.i.d. random variables uniform on
[-1,1]
and let
Z
be a standard Gaussian random variable (mean
0
, variance
1
). For
p\in(0,1)
, we define the constants

\begin{split}c_{2}(p)&=\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{U_{1}+U_{2}}{\sqrt{2}}\right|^{-p}=2^{p/2}\int_{-2}^{2}|x|^{-p}\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{|x|}{4}\right)\mathrm{d}x=\frac{2^{1-p/2}}{(1-p)(2-p)},\\
c_{\infty}(p)&=\mathbb{E}\left|\frac{Z}{\sqrt{3}}\right|^{-p}=\frac{3^{p/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|x|^{-p}e^{-x^{2}/2}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(3/2)^{p/2}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\Gamma\left(\frac{1-p}{2}\right)\end{split}

(3)

and

C_{p}=\max\{c_{2}(p),c_{\infty}(p)\}.

(4)

By comparing
c_{2}(p)
and
c_{\infty}(p)
as done in Lemma
7
from Section
4
below, in fact we have

C_{p}=\begin{cases}c_{\infty}(p),&\text{if }p\in(0,p_{0}),\\
c_{2}(p),&\text{if }p\in(p_{0},1),\end{cases}

where
p_{0}
is the unique
p\in(0,1)
such that
c_{2}(p)=c_{\infty}(p)
. Our main result is the following
L_{-p}-L_{2}
Khinchin-type inequality for sums of symmetric uniform random variables.

Theorem 1
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
C_{p}
be defined by (
4
). Let
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables uniform on
[-1,1]
. For every
n
and every reals
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}\right|^{-p}\leqslant C_{p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

(5)

Remark 2
.

Applying (
5
) to
n=2
,
a_{1}=a_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
and to
n
large,
a_{1}=\dots=a_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
(with the aid of the central limit theorem) shows that the value of
C_{p}
in (
5
) is sharp.

Moments of a Euclidean norm of weighted sums of independent random vectors uniform on
S^{d+1}
and
B_{2}^{d}
,
d\geqslant 1
, are proportional (see Proposition 4 in
[
23
]
or its generalisation, Theorem 4 in
[
2
]
). We recall a special case of this result relevant for us and for convenience sketch its proof (particularly because the proofs available in the literature treat the case of positive moments, but of course they repeat verbatim to negative moments).

Lemma 3

(Proposition 4,
[
23
]
)
.

Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random vectors uniformly distributed on the unit sphere
S^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
U_{1},U_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables uniform on
[-1,1]
. For a vector
a=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
p\in(-\infty,1)
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}=(1-p)\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}\right|^{-p}.

Proof.

We reproduce here an argument utilising rotational invariance from
[
23
]
attributed to Latała. Let
\theta
be a random vector uniform on
S^{2}
, independent of all the other variables. By rotational invariance, for a vector
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

\mathbb{E}|\!\left\langle x,\theta\right\rangle\!|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}|\!\left\langle e_{1}\|x\|,\theta\right\rangle\!|^{-p}=\|x\|^{-p}\mathbb{E}|\theta_{1}|^{-p},

where
\theta_{1}
denotes the first component of
\theta
, so

\|x\|^{-p}=\frac{\mathbb{E}|\!\left\langle x,\theta\right\rangle\!|^{-p}}{\mathbb{E}|\theta_{1}|^{-p}}.

Applying this to
x=\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}
and taking the expectation gives

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\frac{|\!\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k},\theta\right\rangle\!|^{-p}}{\mathbb{E}|\theta_{1}|^{-p}}=\frac{1}{\mathbb{E}|\theta_{1}|^{-p}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\!\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k},\theta\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}.

By the rotational invariance of
\sum a_{k}\xi_{k}
, we also have

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\!\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k},\theta\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\!\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k},e_{1}\|\theta\|\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}.

However,
\theta
is a unit vector and the random variables
\!\left\langle\xi_{k},e_{1}\right\rangle\!
are i.i.d. uniform on
[-1,1]
, therefore

\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\!\left\langle\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k},e_{1}\|\theta\|\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\!\left\langle\xi_{k},e_{1}\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}_{U}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}\right|^{-p}.

Since
\theta_{1}
is uniform on
[-1,1]
, we get
\mathbb{E}|\theta_{1}|^{-p}=\int_{0}^{1}x^{-p}\mathrm{d}x=\frac{1}{1-p}
. Putting these equations together finishes the proof.
∎

It follows from Lemma
3
that (
5
) is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}\leqslant(1-p)C_{p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

(6)

We extend this to matrix-valued coefficients using isometrical embeddings into
L_{p}
spaces (Orlicz-Szarek’s argument, see Remark 3 in
[
35
]
). This offers a sharp version of the very general result of Gorin and Favarov from
[
12
]
(see Corollary 2 therein) in the case of uniform vectors on
S^{2}
and the
L_{-p}-L_{2}
moment comparison. For a matrix
A
,
\|A\|_{HS}
stands for its Hilbert-Schmidt norm.

Corollary 4
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
and let
C_{p}
be defined by (
4
). Let
\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random vectors uniform on the unit sphere
S^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For every
n
and every real
3\times 3
matrices
A_{1},\dots,A_{n}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}\leqslant C_{p}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A_{k}\|_{HS}^{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

(7)

Remark 5
.

Both (
6
) and (
7
) are sharp. The constant in (
7
) is larger than in (
6
) . The former specialised to the case when each matrix
A_{k}
is proportional to the matrix
\left[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right]
reduces to (
5
).

Remark 6
.

A sharp reversal of (
6
) (analogously of (
7
)) is immediate from convexity,

\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}\geqslant\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|\right)^{-p}\geqslant\left(\left(\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{-p}=\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

By (
1
), the case
p=1
of this inequality gives yet another simple proof of Hadwiger’s and Hensley’s result (see
[
14
]
and
[
16
]
, see also Theorem 2 in
[
3
]
).

3
Proof overview

Haagerup’s work
[
13
]
can perhaps be seen as a landmark in the pursuit of sharp Khinchin-type inequalities. Later, Nazarov and Podkorytov in
[
31
]
offered an informative exposition of
[
13
]
(and
[
3
]
), developing novel tools which allowed for significant simplications of the most technically demanding parts of
[
13
]
(as well as of
[
3
]
). We shall closely follow their approach which comprises two main steps. (For other works which used techniques from
[
31
]
to establish sharp Khinchin-type inequalities, we refer for instance to
[
20
,
29
]
.)

Step I (Section
5.2
).
We prove (
5
) in the case that all weights
a_{k}
are “small”, that is for the sequences
a=(a_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
with
\max_{k\leqslant n}|a_{k}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
(call it Case A). This in turn is accomplished by a Fourier-analytic expression for negative moments (used for instance in
[
12
]
), which allows to leverage independence. As in
[
3
]
, by the use of Hölder’s inequality, the following integral inequality allows to finish the whole argument,

s^{p/2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\sin t}{t}\right|^{s}t^{p-1}\mathrm{d}t\leqslant 2^{p-1}\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-p}{2}\right)}C_{p},\qquad 0<p<1,s\geqslant 2.

(8)

This inequality is an extension of Ball’s integral inequality from
[
3
]
and is proved with the methods of
[
31
]
. For other refinements and extensions of Ball’s integral and cube slicing inequalities see
[
8
,
17
,
21
,
22
,
28
]
.

Step II (Section
5.3
).
With the aid of the result of Step I, we use induction on
n
to prove a certain strengthening of (
5
) for
all
sequences
a=(a_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
in order to handle those which do not satisfy Case A, that is have a “large” weight (call those Case B). Were (
8
) true for all
s\geqslant 1
, this step would have been spared. In
[
31
]
the inductive step is possible thanks to an algebraic identity obtained by averaging with respect to one random sign. In our setting, for uniform
[-1,1]
random variables, such an identity does not seem to present itself. To overcome this obstacle, we work with
S^{2}
-uniform random vectors for which certain algebraic identities allowing for induction are much more natural. For Ball’s inequality (
2
) (case
p=1
), this step was in
[
3
]
taken care of by a simple projection argument, but its analogue for
p<1
is not sufficient (see Remark
21
at the end of Section
5.3
).

We remark that in the range
p\in(0,p_{0})
when
C_{p}=c_{\infty}(p)
and the extremizing sequence is
a_{1}=\dots=a_{n}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
with
n\to\infty
, it is only Case A which admits equality (attained asymptotically as
n\to\infty
), whereas in the range
p\in(p_{0},1)
when
C_{p}=c_{2}(p)
and the extremizing sequence is
a_{1}=a_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}
,
n=2
, both Case A and B admit equality (in Case B when taking
n=2
and
a_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2-\delta}}
,
a_{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2+\delta}}
,
\delta\to 0+
) and hence both Step I and II have to be subtle enough to overcome this difficulty.

As a final comment here, convexity-type arguments leading to more precise results such as Schur-convexity of moments of sums with a
fixed
number of summands
n
(see
[
1
,
2
,
7
,
9
,
11
,
15
,
19
,
23
,
33
]
) do not seem to be available here. One of the obstacles is for instance the fact that the function
t\mapsto\mathbb{E}|U_{1}+\sqrt{t}U_{2}|^{-p}
is not convex/concave on the whole half-line
(0,+\infty)
(it is concave on
(0,1)
and convex on
(1,+\infty)
).

4
Technical lemmas

We gather several elementary but technical results needed in our proofs. The first one explains the comparison between the constants
c_{2}(p)
and
c_{\infty}(p)
arising from two different extremizing sequences of weights
a_{k}
in our Khinchin inequality.

Lemma 7
.

Let
c_{2}(p),c_{\infty}(p)
be defined in (
3
). The equation
c_{2}(p)=c_{\infty}(p)
has a unique solution
p_{0}=0.793...
on
(0,1)
. Moreover,
c_{2}(p)>c_{\infty}(p)
for
p\in(p_{0},1)
, whilst
c_{2}(p)<c_{\infty}(p)
for
p\in(0,p_{0})
.

Proof.

For
p\in(0,1)
, the difference
c_{2}(p)-c_{\infty}(p)
has the same sign as

f(p)=2\sqrt{\pi}3^{-p/2}-(1-p)(2-p)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-p}{2}\right).

Claim.
The function
p\mapsto\log\left((1-p)(2-p)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-p}{2}\right)\right)
is strictly concave on
(0,1)
.

Note that
f(0+)=0
,
f(1-)=2(\sqrt{\pi/3}-1)>0
(
u\Gamma(u)\to 1
as
u\to 0
) and
f(\frac{2}{3})=2\sqrt{\pi}3^{-1/3}-\frac{4}{9}\Gamma(\frac{1}{6})<-0.016
. In view of the claim (after taking the logarithm and noting that a linear function intersects a strictly concave function at most twice), the proof of the lemma is finished.

To prove the claim, we let
u=\frac{1-p}{2}
and
h(u)=-\log\left(u\left(u+\frac{1}{2}\right)\Gamma(u)\right)
. We want to show that
h
is strictly convex on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Recall
(\log\Gamma(u))^{\prime\prime}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(u+k)^{2}}
. Thus for
u\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
,

\displaystyle h^{\prime\prime}(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{u^{2}}+\frac{1}{(u+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(u+k)^{2}}>\frac{1}{(u+\frac{1}{2})^{2}}-\frac{1}{(u+1)^{2}}-\frac{1}{(u+2)^{2}}-\sum_{k=3}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k^{2}}.

We now show that the right hand side is positive on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Call it
h_{1}(u)
and note that

\frac{1}{2}h_{1}^{\prime}(u)=\frac{1}{(u+1)^{3}}+\frac{1}{(u+2)^{3}}-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{1}{2})^{3}}<\frac{2}{(u+1)^{3}}-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{1}{2})^{3}}.

The right hand side has the same sign as
\left(\frac{u+\frac{1}{2}}{u+1}\right)^{3}-\frac{1}{2}
which is clearly increasing in
u
, thus at most
\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{3}-\frac{1}{2}=-\frac{11}{54}<0
for
u\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Thus
h_{1}(u)
is decreasing for
u\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Going back to the lower bound
h^{\prime\prime}(u)>h_{1}(u)
, we conclude that
h^{\prime\prime}(u)>h_{1}(u)>h_{1}(\frac{1}{2})=\frac{1481}{900}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}>0.0006
, for
u\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. This shows that
h
is strictly convex on
(0,\frac{1}{2})
.
∎

The next three lemmas are elementary facts about functions showing up in calculations from Step I (Section
5.2
) needed to prove the integral inequality (
8
).

Lemma 8
.

\sup_{t\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\cos t-\frac{\sin t}{t}\right|<\frac{6}{5}
.

Proof.

Since both
\cos t
and
\frac{\sin t}{t}
are even, it suffices to consider positive
t
. By the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, we have
\left|\cos t-\frac{\sin t}{t}\right|\leqslant\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{t^{2}}}
, so it suffices to consider
t\leqslant\frac{5}{\sqrt{11}}
. It remains to note that
\frac{5}{\sqrt{11}}<\frac{\pi}{2}
and that on
(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, we have
\left|\cos t-\frac{\sin t}{t}\right|=\frac{\sin t}{t}-\cos t<1+0=1
.
∎

Lemma 9
.

Let
y_{1}=\max_{t\in[\pi,2\pi]}\left|\frac{\sin t}{t}\right|
. For
y\in(0,y_{1})
, let
t=t_{0}
be the unique solution to
\frac{\sin t}{t}=y
on
(0,\pi)
. Then
t_{0}>2
.

Proof.

Since
y_{1}<\frac{1}{\pi}
, for every
y\in(0,y_{1})
, we have

\frac{\sin t_{0}}{t_{0}}=y<\frac{3\sqrt{3}}{4\pi}=\frac{\sin(2\pi/3)}{2\pi/3}
.
Since
\frac{\sin t}{t}
is decreasing on
(0,\pi)
, it follows that
t_{0}>\frac{2\pi}{3}>2
.
∎

Lemma 10
.

For every
p\in(0,1)
, we have
(1-p)(2-p)\Gamma\left(\frac{1-p}{2}\right)\geqslant 2^{\frac{3-p}{2}}
.

Proof.

Thanks to the claim from Lemma
7
, it suffices to check the stated inequality at the endpoints: for
p=0
, it becomes
2\sqrt{\pi}\geqslant 2^{3/2}
which clearly holds, whereas for
p\to 1-
, it becomes equality.
∎

The following lemma is an important step in the proof of (
8
). Essentially it is a consequence of convexity of sums of exponential functions.

Lemma 11
.

For
p\in(0,1)
and
m=1,2,\dots
, we set

R_{m}(p)=\frac{5}{3}\Big(\pi^{-1/2}2^{1/2-p}\Big)\frac{\Big(\log\big(\pi(m+3/2)\big)\Big)^{1-p/2}}{\pi(m+3/2)}\left(2^{p}+2\pi^{p}\sum_{k=1}^{m}k^{p}\right).

We have,
R_{m}(p)>1
.

Proof.

For
m=1,2,\dots
, we let

\displaystyle A_{m}

\displaystyle=\frac{5}{3}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\frac{\log\big(\pi(m+3/2)\big)}{\pi(m+3/2)},

\displaystyle a_{0,m}

\displaystyle=\Big(\log\big(\pi(m+3/2)\big)\Big)^{-1/2},

\displaystyle a_{k,m}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi k}{2}\Big(\log\big(\pi(m+3/2)\big)\Big)^{-1/2},\quad k=1,2,\dots,m.

Then

R_{m}(p)=A_{m}\left(a_{0,m}^{p}+2\sum_{k=1}^{m}a_{k,m}^{p}\right),

which is a sum of convex functions, thus
R_{m}(p)
is convex.

Case
m=1
.
We have,
R_{1}^{\prime}(p)\leqslant R_{1}^{\prime}(1)=A_{1}(a_{0,1}\log a_{0,1}+2a_{1,1}\log a_{1,1})<-0.019
, so
R_{1}
is decreasing on
(0,1)
. Thus for every
p\in(0,1)
, we have
R_{1}(p)\geqslant R_{1}(1)=A_{1}(a_{0,1}+2a_{1,1})>1.006
, as desired.

Case
m\geqslant 2
.
We have,

The final lemma in this section lies at the heart of the base case of the inductive argument from Step II (Section

Lemma 12
.

For
x\in[0,1]
and
p\in[0,2]
, let

h(p,x)=\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{2-p}-\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{2-p}+x\left(\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

Then for every
x\in(0,1)
,
p\mapsto h(p,x)
is strictly concave and decreasing on
[0,2]
. In particular,
h(p,x)\leqslant h(0,x)=2x
, for every
x\in[0,1]
,
p\in[0,2]
.

Proof.

First we show concavity. Fix
x\in(0,1)
. We have,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)=\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{2-p}\log^{2}\frac{1+x}{2}

\displaystyle-\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{2-p}\log^{2}\frac{1-x}{2}

\displaystyle+\frac{x}{4}\left(\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}\right)^{-p/2}\log^{2}\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}.

Then
(\frac{1-x}{2})^{p}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)
is a strictly convex function of
p
as being of the form
Aa^{p}+Bb^{p}-C
with positive
a,A,b,B,C
. Therefore, in order to show that
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)
is negative for
p\in(0,2)
, it suffices to check that it is nonpositive at the endpoints
p=0
and
p=2
.

At
p=0
, using
0\leqslant\log(1+t)\leqslant t
,
t\geqslant 0
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)|_{p=0}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{2}\log^{2}\frac{1+x}{2}-\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{2}\log^{2}\frac{1-x}{2}+\frac{x}{4}\log^{2}\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{2}\log^{2}\frac{1+x}{2}-\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{2}\log^{2}\frac{1-x}{2}+\frac{x}{4}\left(\frac{1-x^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}.

Let
f(t)=\left(\frac{\log t}{1-t}\right)^{2}
,
t\in(0,1)
. With
a=\frac{1-x}{2}
and
b=\frac{1+x}{2}
, then the right hand side can be written as
(ab)^{2}\big(f(b)-f(a)+b-a\big)
. Note that the power-series expansion of
f(1-t)=\left(\frac{\log(1-t)}{t}\right)^{2}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{t^{k}}{k+1}\right)^{2}
has all the coefficients positive. In particular,
f
is convex on
(0,1)
. Moreover, a direct computation shows that
\lim_{t\to 1-}f^{\prime}(t)=-1
. Thus,
f(b)-f(a)\leqslant f^{\prime}(b)(b-a)\leqslant-(b-a)
, which gives
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)|_{p=0}\leqslant 0
, as desired.

At
p=2
, using
\log^{2}\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}<1
and
3-x^{2}>2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)|_{p=2}

\displaystyle=\log^{2}\frac{1+x}{2}-\log^{2}\frac{1-x}{2}+\frac{x}{2(3-x^{2})}\log^{2}\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}

\displaystyle<\log^{2}\frac{1+x}{2}-\log^{2}\frac{1-x}{2}+\frac{x}{4}.

Note that the right hand side at
x=0
is
0
, so it suffices to show that it is decreasing in

. The derivative of the right hand side equals
\frac{2}{1+x}\log\frac{1+x}{2}+\frac{2}{1-x}\log\frac{1-x}{2}+\frac{1}{4}
. With the aid of
\log t\leqslant t-1
,
t>0
, we upper bound this by
\frac{9}{4}-2\left(\frac{1}{1+x}+\frac{1}{1-x}\right)=4\left(\frac{9}{16}-\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}\right)<0
. Thus,
\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial p^{2}}h(p,x)|_{p=2}<0
, as desired. This finishes the proof of the concavity of
p\mapsto h(p,x)
.

To show that
p\mapsto h(p,x)
is decreasing on
[0,2]
, since it is concave, it suffices to show that
\frac{\partial}{\partial p}h(p,x)|_{p=0}\leqslant 0
. We let

g(x)=\frac{\partial}{\partial p}h(p,x)|_{p=0}=-\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{2}\log\frac{1+x}{2}+\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{2}\log\frac{1-x}{2}-\frac{x}{2}\log\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}.

The rest of the argument is a tedious analysis of the derivatives of
g
, which we only sketch. Since
g^{\prime\prime\prime}(x)=\frac{2x^{2}(x^{4}-14x^{2}+9)}{(1-x^{2})(3-x^{2})^{3}}
and
x^{4}-14x^{2}+9
on
[0,1]
changes sign only once from positive to negative, we get that
g^{\prime\prime}(x)
on
(0,1)
is first increasing and then decreasing. Moreover,
g^{\prime\prime}(0+)=0
and
g^{\prime\prime}(1-)=-\infty
. Thus
g^{\prime\prime}(x)
on
(0,1)
changes sign only once from positive to negative. Therefore,
g^{\prime}(x)
on
(0,1)
is first increasing and then decreasing. Since
g^{\prime}(0+)=-\frac{1}{2}+\frac{1}{2}\log\frac{8}{3}<0
and
g^{\prime}(1-)=0
, we infer that
g^{\prime}(x)
on
(0,1)
changes sign only once from negative to positive. Thus
g(x)
on
(0,1)
first decreases and then increases. Since
g(0+)=0=g(1-)
, we get that
g(x)<0
on
(0,1)

∎

n
to work. To this end, we define

\phi_{p}(x)=(1+x)^{-p/2}

and

\Phi_{p}(x)=\begin{cases}\phi_{p}(x),&x\geqslant 1,\\
2\phi_{p}(1)-\phi_{p}(2-x),&0\leqslant x\leqslant 1.\end{cases}

By this construction, the graph of
\Phi_{p}(x)
on
[0,1]
is the graph of
\phi_{p}(x)
on
[1,2]
reflected about the point
(1,\phi_{p}(1))
. In particular, to the left of
x=1
,
\Phi_{p}
and
\phi_{p}
share the common tangent line at
x=1
. Consequently,
\Phi_{p}(x)\leqslant\phi_{p}(x)
for every
x
. By homogeneity, (
5
) is equivalent to

\mathbb{E}\left|U_{1}+\sum_{k=2}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}\right|^{-p}\leqslant C_{p}\phi_{p}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right).

We shall inductively show a strengthening. As it will be clear from the proof, it is natural to run the inductive argument for spherically symmetric random vectors
\xi_{k}
.

Theorem 18
.

For every
p\in(0,1)
, every
n\geqslant 2
and every vectors
v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

\mathbb{E}\left|\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\!\left\langle v_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}\leqslant C_{p}\Phi_{p}\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}\|v_{k}\|^{2}\right).

(17)

Since
\!\left\langle v_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\!
has the same distribution as
\|v_{k}\|U_{k}
, (
17
) gives (
5
).

Proof of Theorem
18
.

We use induction on
n
. For
n=2
, we have the following lemma, the proof of which we defer for now.

Lemma 19
.

For every vector
v
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we have

\mathbb{E}|\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!+\!\left\langle v,\xi_{2}\right\rangle\!|^{-p}\leqslant c_{2}(p)\Phi_{p}(\|v\|^{2}).

(18)

Let
n\geqslant 3
and suppose (
17
) holds for every sequence of
n-1
vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
x=\|v_{2}\|^{2}+\dots+\|v_{n}\|^{2}
. We want to show (
17
). There are 3 cases.

Case (a):

\|v_{k}\|>1
for some
2\leqslant k\leqslant n
. Then
x>1
, so (
17
) coincides with

\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\!\left\langle v_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}\leqslant C_{p}(\|v_{1}\|^{2}+\|v_{2}\|^{2}+\ldots+\|v_{n}\|^{2})^{-p/2},

(19)

where
v_{1}=e_{1}
.
Let
v_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,v_{n}^{\ast}
be a rearrangement of
v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}
such that
\|v_{k}^{\ast}\|\geqslant\|v_{k+1}^{\ast}\|
for every
k=1,\ldots,n-1
and let
v^{\prime}_{k}=\frac{v_{k}^{\ast}}{\|v_{1}^{\ast}\|}
for every
k=1,\ldots,n
, so that
\|v^{\prime}_{1}\|=1
and
\|v^{\prime}_{k}\|\leqslant 1
for
k=2,\ldots,n
. Then due to the homogeneity of (
19
) and the fact that
\!\left\langle v^{\prime}_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!
has the same distribution as
\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!
, it is enough to prove

\mathbb{E}\left|\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\rangle+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\langle v^{\prime}_{k}\xi_{k}\rangle\right|^{p}\leqslant C_{p}\Phi_{p}(\|v^{\prime}_{2}\|^{2}+\dots+\|v^{\prime}_{n}\|^{2}),

which is handled by the next cases.

Case (b):

\|v_{k}\|\leqslant 1
for every
2\leqslant k\leqslant n
and
x\geqslant 1
. Then again (
17
) coincides with the homogeneous estimate (
5
). Moreover, we have that

\max_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}\|v_{k}\|^{2}=1\leqslant\frac{1}{2}(1+x)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|v_{k}\|^{2},

so this case reduces to Theorem
14
where all the
\|v_{k}\|
are
small
.

Case (c):

\|v_{k}\|\leqslant 1
for every
2\leqslant k\leqslant n
and
x<1
. Since the pair
(\xi_{n-1},\xi_{n})
has the same distribution as
(\xi_{n-1},Q\xi_{n-1})
for a random orthogonal matrix
Q
independent of all the
\xi_{k}
, we have,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\!\left\langle v_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\!\right|^{p}=\mathbb{E}\left|\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!+\!\left\langle v_{2},\xi_{2}\right\rangle\!+\dots+\!\left\langle v_{n-1},\xi_{n-1}\right\rangle\!+\!\left\langle Q^{\top}v_{n},\xi_{n-1}\right\rangle\!\right|^{p}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\left[\mathbb{E}_{(\xi_{k})_{k=2}^{n-1}}\left|\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!+\!\left\langle v_{2},\xi_{2}\right\rangle\!+\dots+\!\left\langle v_{n-2},\xi_{n-2}\right\rangle\!+\!\left\langle v_{n-1}+Q^{\top}v_{n},\xi_{n-1}\right\rangle\!\right|^{p}\right].

By the inductive hypothesis applied to the sequence
(v_{2},\dots,v_{n-2},v_{n-1}+Q^{\top}v_{n})
(conditioned on the value of
Q
), we get

\mathbb{E}\left|\!\left\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\right\rangle\!+\sum_{k=2}^{n}\!\left\langle v_{k},\xi_{k}\right\rangle\!\right|^{p}\leqslant C_{p}\mathbb{E}_{Q}\Phi_{p}(\|v_{2}\|^{2}+\dots+\|v_{n-2}\|^{2}+\|v_{n-1}+Q^{\top}v_{n}\|^{2}).

Note that

\|v_{2}\|^{2}+\dots+\|v_{n-2}\|^{2}+\|v_{n-1}+Q^{\top}v_{n}\|^{2}=x+2\!\left\langle v_{n-1},Q^{\top}v_{n}\right\rangle\!,

thus, by the symmetry of
Q
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Q}\Phi_{p}(\|v_{2}\|^{2}+\dots+\|v_{n-2}\|^{2}+\|v_{n-1}+Q^{\top}v_{n}\|^{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Q}\frac{\Phi_{p}\left(x+2\!\left\langle v_{n-1},Q^{\top}v_{n}\right\rangle\!\right)+\Phi_{p}\left(x-2\!\left\langle v_{n-1},Q^{\top}v_{n}\right\rangle\!\right)}{2}.

We shall now need a lemma about concavity of
\Phi_{p}
, the proof of which we also defer.

Lemma 20
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
. For every
a,b\geqslant 0
with
\frac{a+b}{2}\leqslant 1
, we have

\frac{\Phi_{p}(a)+\Phi_{p}(b)}{2}\leqslant\Phi_{p}\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right).

This lemma applied to
a=x+2\!\left\langle v_{n-1},Q^{\top}v_{n}\right\rangle\!
and
b=x-2\!\left\langle v_{n-1},Q^{\top}v_{n}\right\rangle\!
(which satisfy
a,b\geqslant 0
and
\frac{a+b}{2}=x<1
) finishes the proof of the inductive step.

It remains to show the lemmas we have used.
∎

Proof of Lemma
19
.

First note that if
\|v\|>1
then, due to rotational invariance,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle v,\xi_{2}\rangle|^{-p}

\displaystyle=\|v\|^{-p}\mathbb{E}\left|\left\langle\frac{e_{1}}{\|v\|},\xi_{1}\right\rangle+\left\langle\frac{v}{\|v\|},\xi_{2}\right\rangle\right|^{-p}

\displaystyle=\|v\|^{-p}\mathbb{E}|\langle v^{\prime},\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle e_{1},\xi_{2}\rangle|^{-p}

for any
v^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
such that
\|v^{\prime}\|=\frac{1}{\|v\|}<1
, while at the same time

\Phi_{p}(\|v\|^{2})=\phi_{p}(\|v\|^{2})=\|v\|^{-p}\phi_{p}(\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}).

This shows that the desired inequality is then equivalent to

\mathbb{E}|\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle v^{\prime},\xi_{2}\rangle|^{-p}\leqslant c_{2}(p)\phi_{p}(\|v^{\prime}\|^{2}),\quad\|v^{\prime}\|\leqslant 1.

Since
\Phi_{p}(x)\leqslant\phi_{p}(x)
for
x\in[0,1]
, it is sufficient to prove the lemma in the case
\|v\|\leqslant 1
.

Fix
v\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
with
x=\|v\|\leqslant 1
. To compute explicitly the left hand side of (
18
), recall that for any
w\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\langle w,\xi\rangle
has the same distribution as
\|w\|U
where
\xi
and
U
are uniformly distributed on
S^{2}
and
[-1,1]
, respectively. Then, we have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}|\langle e_{1},\xi_{1}\rangle+\langle v,\xi_{2}\rangle|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}|U_{1}+xU_{2}|^{-p}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}|u_{1}+xu_{2}|^{-p}\mathrm{d}u_{1}\mathrm{d}u_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+x)^{2-p}-(1-x)^{2-p}}{2(1-p)(2-p)x}.

Recalling the definition of
c_{2}(p)
and
\Phi_{p}
on
[0,1]
, we thus get that (
18
) becomes

\frac{(1+x)^{2-p}-(1-x)^{2-p}}{2x}\leqslant 2^{1-p/2}(2^{1-p/2}-(3-x^{2})^{-p/2})

for every
0<x\leqslant 1
. Note that we can write this as

\left(\frac{1+x}{2}\right)^{2-p}-\left(\frac{1-x}{2}\right)^{2-p}+x\left(\frac{3-x^{2}}{2}\right)^{-p/2}\leqslant 2x,

so Lemma
12
finishes the proof.
∎

Proof of Lemma
20
.

We can assume without loss of generality that
a<b
. If
b\leqslant 1
, the desired inequality follows from the concavity of
\Phi_{p}
on
[0,1]
. So, assume that
b>1
. Then using the facts
a<b
and
\frac{a+b}{2}\leqslant 1
, we can write

\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\Phi_{p}\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\Phi_{p}}{dx}\Big|_{x=\frac{a+b}{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{2}\frac{d\Phi_{p}}{dx}\Big|_{x=a}=\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\frac{\Phi_{p}(a)+\Phi_{p}(b)}{2},

using the fact that the derivative of
\Phi_{p}^{\prime}
is decreasing on
[0,1]
. This implies that

\Phi_{p}\left(\frac{a+b}{2}\right)-\frac{\Phi_{p}(a)+\Phi_{p}(b)}{2}

is a decreasing function of
a
, so to prove the desired inequality, it suffices to show that the latter is nonnegative for the maximum value of
a
, that is
a=a_{0}=2-b
. Since
b>1
,
a_{0}<1
and by the definition of
\Phi_{p}
,
\Phi_{p}(a_{0})=2\phi_{p}(1)-\phi_{p}(2-(2-b))=2\phi_{p}(1)-\phi_{p}(b)
and
\Phi_{p}(b)=\phi_{p}(b)
, we get

\frac{\Phi_{p}(a_{0})+\Phi_{p}(b)}{2}=\frac{2\phi_{p}(1)-\phi_{p}(b)+\phi_{p}(b)}{2}=\phi_{p}(1)=\Phi_{p}\left(\frac{a_{0}+b}{2}\right),

that is, the desired inequality is in fact an equality in this case.
∎

Remark 21
.

Let
p\in(0,1)
. Let
X
be a rotationally invariant random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For every nonzero vector
y
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, observing that
\|X+y\|^{2}
has the same distribution as
\|X\|^{2}+\|y\|^{2}+2\|X\|\|y\|U
, where
U
is uniform on
[-1,1]
, independent of
X
, we have

\mathbb{E}\|X+y\|^{-p}=\mathbb{E}\frac{\big(\|X\|+\|y\|\big)^{2-p}-\big|\|X\|-\|y\|\big|^{2-p}}{2(2-p)\|X\|\|y\|}.

In particular, by the concavity of
t\mapsto t^{1-p}
,

\mathbb{E}\|X+y\|^{-p}\leqslant\mathbb{E}\|X\|^{-p}.

This combined with independence gives

\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}\leqslant\min_{1\leqslant k\leqslant n}|a_{k}|^{-p}.

For
p=1
, this immediately gives (
2
) in the case of a large weight,
\max_{k\leqslant n}|a_{k}|>\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
and Theorem
18
is not needed (this corresponds to the simple projection argument from
[
3
]
handling this case). For
p<1
, this argument yields the nonsharp constant
2^{p/2}
instead of
(1-p)C_{p}
.

5.4
Proof of Corollary
4

Let
G=(G_{1},G_{2},G_{3})
be a standard Gaussian random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(mean
0
, covariance
I
), independent of the sequence
(\xi_{k})_{k=1}^{n}
. Then for every vector
x
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, since
\!\left\langle x,G\right\rangle\!
has the same distribution as
\|x\|G_{1}
, we have
\|x\|^{-p}=\alpha_{p}\mathbb{E}|\!\left\langle x,G\right\rangle\!|^{-p}
with
\alpha_{p}=(\mathbb{E}|G_{1}|^{-p})^{-1}
. Therefore,

\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}=\alpha_{p}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\!\left\langle\xi_{k},A_{k}^{\top}G\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}.

(20)

Using this and inequality (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{-p}

\displaystyle=\alpha_{p}\mathbb{E}_{G}\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\!\left\langle\xi_{k},A_{k}^{\top}G\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}\leqslant C_{p}\alpha_{p}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A_{k}^{\top}G\|^{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

Rewriting the sum of squares using the second moment, applying Minkowski’s inequality (with the negative exponent
-\frac{2}{p}
) and using (
20
) again, we get

\displaystyle C_{p}\alpha_{p}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A_{k}^{\top}G\|^{2}\right)^{-p/2}

\displaystyle=C_{p}\alpha_{p}\mathbb{E}_{G}\left(3\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\!\left\langle\xi_{k},A_{k}^{\top}G\right\rangle\!\right|^{2}\right)^{-p/2}

\displaystyle\leqslant 3^{-p/2}C_{p}\alpha_{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left(\mathbb{E}_{G}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}\!\left\langle\xi_{k},A_{k}^{\top}G\right\rangle\!\right|^{-p}\right)^{-2/p}\right)^{-p/2}

\displaystyle=3^{-p/2}C_{p}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{2}\right)^{-p/2}.

Finally,
3\mathbb{E}_{\xi}\left\|\sum_{k=1}^{n}A_{k}\xi_{k}\right\|^{2}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\|A_{k}\|_{HS}^{2}
.

\square

6
Conclusion

Continuing a long line of work and particularly addressing some questions raised in
[
2
]
, we have established a sharp Khinchin-type
L_{-p}-L_{2}
moment comparison inequality when
p\in(0,1)
for weighted sums of independent random variables uniform on
[-1,1]
, equivalently uniform vectors on the unit sphere
S^{2}
in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. In this case, this provides a sharp version of the very general results from
[
12
]
.

We have not tried to optimise various technical numerical estimates which would certainly allow to extend our results to
p\in(0,p_{1})
,
p_{1}=1.38
(the negative moments of order
-p
for
S^{2}
–uniform vectors exist for all
p<2
). The arguments seem robust enough to handle cases of
S^{r}
–uniform vectors for other values of
r
(most notably the case of
r=1
corresponding to Steinhaus random variables as well as the case of
r=3
which would provide extensions of the polydisc slicing inequality of Oleszkiewicz and Pełczyński from
[
32
]
, just as our result extends Ball’s cube slicing inequality from
[
3
]
). Moreover, the question of a sharp
L_{p}-L_{2}
,
p\in(0,1)
, moment comparison for
\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}
remains open with a natural conjecture that
\inf_{n}\inf_{a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{\mathbb{E}|\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}U_{k}|^{p}}{\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}a_{k}^{2}\right)^{p/2}}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left|\sum_{k=1}^{n}n^{-1/2}U_{k}\right|^{p}
(see Question 5 and Proposition 15 in
[
10
]
). All this is the topic of ongoing and future work.
