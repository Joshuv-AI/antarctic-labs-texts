Global optimality under amenable symmetry constraints
Peter Orbanz

Abstract
Consider a convex function that is invariant under an group of transformations. If it has a minimizer, does it also have an invariant minimizer? Variants of this problem appear in nonparametric statistics and in a number of adjacent fields. The answer depends on the choice of function, and on what one may loosely call the geometry of the problem—the interplay between convexity, the group, and the underlying vector space, which is typically infinite-dimensional. We observe that this geometry is completely encoded in the smallest closed convex invariant subsets of the space, and proceed to study these sets, for groups that are amenable but not necessarily compact. We then apply this toolkit to the invariant optimality problem. It yields new results on invariant kernel mean embeddings and risk-optimal invariant couplings, and clarifies relations between seemingly distinct ideas, such as the summation trick used in machine learning to construct equivariant neural networks and the classic Hunt-Stein theorem of statistics.



1 Introduction

We consider the following problem: Given are a group
\mathbb{G}
of linear bijections of a (topological) vector space
X
,
and a function
{f:X\to\mathbb{R}\cup{\{\infty\}}}

If

\mathbb{G}
-invariant, i.e. a simultaneous fixed point of all
{\phi\in\mathbb{G}}
.
That is obviously true if
f
is strictly convex—since
f
is invariant, its minimizers form an invariant set, and if
the minimizer is unique, it must itself be invariant—but there are a range of problems
where
X
is infinite-dimensional and one cannot assume strict convexity.
Examples include the Hunt-Stein theorem in statistics
[
30
,
17
]
,
various problems that arise in machine learning
applications to science (such as whether certain energies in a crystalline solid have symmetric ground states)

[
40
, e.g.]
, the existence of invariant optimal transportation plans,
and the principle of symmetric criticality in variational analysis
[
53
]
.
Although these problems seem to differ at first glance, we show in the following that they all share the same
underlying structure, and studying this structure in its own right leads to new applications.
We use the remainder of this section to summarize our approach and results, and postpone a detailed review
of related work to
Section

10

Problem sketch

The linear transformations

Given a function

Clearly, the vector

For invariant functions, we can therefore turn an arbitrary minimizer

-invariant

We are interested in problems where

In this case, (

Suppose for the moment that

is countable. (The uncountable case requires some additional formalism,
but is conceptually similar, see

3

We approximate (
2
)
by partial group averages

\mathbf{F}_{n}(x)\;:=\;\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{|\A_{n}|}$}}\msum_{\phi\in\mathbf{A}_{n}}\phi x\;,

where
{\mathbf{A}_{1},\mathbf{A}_{2},\ldots}
is a sequence of finite subsets of

that satisfy

\frac{|\mathbf{A}_{n}\cap\phi\mathbf{A}_{n}|}{|\mathbf{A}_{n}|}\;\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\;1\qquad\text{ for each }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\;.

Such a sequence is called a Følner sequence, and a group that contains

One can show that, if a limit point

-invariant element of

We combine this with the fact that, if

-invariant, its sublevel sets

If

is also compact, we also know that (
3

and our problem is solved: If

is a minimizer, it is in the

{\argmin f=[f\leq\min f]}

, which is therefore again a minimizer.

2 Notation and terminology

Throughout, we work with a Polish space

\mathbb{G}
, and a topological

|{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|

We shorten notation to

The action is

The action on

on

and, if it is measurable, on the vector space of signed measures

4 Duality

Let

Remark
.

Recall a topology on

An orbitope in

4.1. Dual actions

Given a linear continuous action of
\mathbb{G}
on
X
, we define the
dual action

as the unique action of
\mathbb{G}
on
Y
that satisfies

(15)

\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{\phi x,\phi y}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;=\;\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,y}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\qquad\text{ for each }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and }(x,y)\in X\!\times\!Y\;.

We then say that
\mathbb{G}

acts dually
on
X
and
Y
.
Since (
15
) is equivalent to choosing each map

{\phi:Y\rightarrow Y}
as the adjoint of the bounded linear operator
{\phi^{-1}:X\rightarrow X}
,
the dual action is linear and weak* continuous. An action on a normed space has
bounded orbits

if
{\sup_{\phi\in\mathbb{G}}\|\phi x\|<\infty}
for all
{x\in X}
. It is

isometric
if it leaves the norm invariant,
{\|\phi x\|=\|x\|}
for all
\phi
and
x
.
Clearly, isometric actions have bounded orbits.

Lemma 7
.

Consider a linear continuous action on a normed space
X
.

(i) The action has bounded orbits if and only if the dual action had bounded orbits.

(ii) The action is isometric if and only if the dual action is isometric.

(iii) If the orbits are bounded, all consistent orbitopes in
Y
are weak* compact.

Proof.

See
Appendix

C
.

4.2. Support functions

The support function of a set
E
in
X
or
Y
is defined as

H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|E)\;:=\;\begin{cases}\;\sup{\{\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,},y}\mkern 2.0mu\right>|\,y\in E\}}&\text{ if }E\subset Y\\
\;\sup{\{\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}}\mkern 2.0mu\right>|\,x\in E\}}&\text{ if }E\subset X\\
\end{cases}\;.

This function is convex, and weakly lsc or weak* lsc.
It takes values in
\mathbb{R}\cup{\{\infty\}}
, is proper if
E
is not empty, and is finite if

is compact, see

[
9
,
2
]
.
If
\mathbb{G}
acts dually, substituting (

shows that

characterizes invariance of

H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|E)\text{ is $\mathbb{G}$-invariant }\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad E\text{ is $\mathbb{G}$-invariant.}

Since a set and its closed convex hull have the same support function
[
9
]
,
we also have

(16)

H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|\Pi(y))\;=\;H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|\mathbb{G}(y))\;.

This implies the duality

(17)

H(x|\Pi(y))\;=\;H(y|\Pi(x))\qquad\text{ for }(x,y)\in X\!\times\!Y\;.

Theorem

6
implies a simple but useful optimality principle:

Proposition 8
.

Let a nice amenable group
\mathbb{G}
act dually on a nice space
X

and its dual
Y
, with bounded orbits. Let
{E\subset X}
be
\mathbb{G}
-invariant, and

{F\subset Y}
closed, convex and
\mathbb{G}
-invariant. If the
optimization problem

\text{minimize }\quad H(x|E)\qquad\text{ subject to }y\in F

has a solution

\Pi(\hat{y})
contains a
\mathbb{G}
-invariant solution

, and

-H(-x\,|\Pi(\hat{y}))\;\leq\;\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,\bar{y}}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;\leq\;H(x\,|\Pi(\hat{y}))\qquad\text{ for each }x\in E\;.

Proof.

The orbitope
\Pi(\hat{y})
is compact, by
Lemma

7
. It contains a
\mathbb{G}
-invariant element
\bar{y}
, by
Theorem

6
.
Since
H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|E)
is convex lsc and
\mathbb{G}
-invariant,
\argmin H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|E)
is a convex, closed,
\mathbb{G}
-invariant set.
Since orbitopes are the smallest such sets,
{\hat{y}\in\argmin H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|E)}
implies
{\bar{y}\in\Pi(\hat{y})\subset\argmin H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|E)}
.
To obtain the upper bound, observe that

\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,\bar{y}}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;\leq\;\sup\nolimits_{z\in\Pi(\bar{y})}\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,z}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;\leq\;\sup\nolimits_{z\in\Pi(\hat{y})}\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,z}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;=\;H({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|\Pi(\hat{y}))\;,

since
{\bar{y}\in\Pi(\hat{y})}
implies
{\Pi(\bar{y})\subset\Pi(\hat{y})}
.
The lower bound follows analogously.

4.3. Orbitopes and annihilators

In the examples in
Figures

1
and
2
, all orbitopes are orthogonal
to the subspace

of invariant elements. The next result shows that this is no coincidence.
To say that

is in the orthocomplement of

\Pi(x)-x\;=\;\overline{\text{\rm co}}\,(\mathbb{G}(x)-x)\;=\;\overline{\text{\rm co}}\,{\{\phi x-x|\phi\in\mathbb{G}\}}\;.

is known as a

of

[
7
, e.g.]
.
The set
\Pi(x)-x
is hence the closed convex hull of the image
\mathbb{G}x-x
of a coboundary.

Lemma 9
.

If
\mathbb{G}
acts dually on
X
and its dual

, the

and

,
and the vectors
{\phi x-x}
similarly lie dense in

. All orbitopes satisfy

\Pi(x)\;\subset\;Y_{\mathbb{G}}^{\perp}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Pi(y)\;\subset\;X_{\mathbb{G}}^{\perp}\;\qquad\text{ for }x\in X\text{ and }y\in Y\;.

4.4. Contraction under averages

If
\mathbf{F}_{n}(z)
is well-defined at each point

(Contraction of orbitopes)

Let an amenable group

.

(i) If an orbitope

, and satisfies

.

,
and each

in the convex hull of
\mathbb{G}(y)

5.1. Orbitopes in Hilbert spaces

If
X
is a Hilbert space, it can be identified with its dual, and the dual pairing
becomes an inner product. If
\mathbb{G}
acts dually, it therefore leaves the inner

(19)

\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{\phi x,\phi y}\mkern 2.0mu\right>=\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,y}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\quad\text{ and hence }\quad\|\phi x\|=\sqrt{\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{\phi x,\phi x}\mkern 2.0mu\right>}=\|x\|\quad\text{ for all }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\;.

Linear actions that satisfy (

,
since (

) makes each map
{\phi:X\to X}

a unitary linear operator
[
18
]

has an inner product, the annihilator
{X_{\mathbb{G}}^{\perp}}
is the
orthocomplement of
X_{\mathbb{G}}

of

has a unique

Since the weak and weak* topology coincide in Hilbert spaces, the norm-, weak- and weak* topologies
define the same orbitopes.
Orbitopes of unitary actions are contained in closed discs orthogonal to the (possibly infinite-dimensional) axis

.

If
X
is a Hilbert space and (

) holds,

is weakly compact and orthogonal to

for each

satisfy

{\bar{y}=\bar{x}}
and
{\|y\|\leq\|x\|}
. If
\mathbb{G}
is amenable, then

{\Pi(x)\cap X_{\mathbb{G}}={\{\bar{x}\}}}
.

Proof.

See
Appendix

D
.
∎

Fact 12

(Mean ergodic theorem
[
18
,
52
, e.g.]

Let a nice amenable

\Big\|\,\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{|\A_n|}$}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathbf{A}_{n}^{-1}}\phi x\,|d\phi|\,-\,\bar{x}\,\Big\|\;\xrightarrow{n\rightarrow\infty}\;0\qquad\text{ for each }x\in X\;,

is the projection of

.

By

Proof.

The orbitope of

5.2. Orbitopes in

For a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
on
\Omega
and
{p\in[1,\infty]}

denote by
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)
the space of (equivalence classes of) functions

on
\Omega
with

{\|f\|_{p}:=(\int|f|^{p}d\mu)^{1/p}}
.
Recall that
\mathbf{L}_{p}
is a Banach space with dual
{\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)^{\prime}=\mathbf{L}_{q}(\mu)}
for
{1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty}
and
{1/p+1/q=1}
.
If
\mu
is
\mathbb{G}
-invariant, each map
{\phi:\Omega\rightarrow\Omega}
takes null sets to null sets, so we can define

(20)

(\mu\text{-equivalence class of }x)\circ\phi\;:=\;\mu\text{-equivalence class of }(x\circ\phi)\quad\text{ for }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\;.

Since
\phi

\mu
implies
{\mu(x\circ\phi)=\mu(x)}
for every integrable function

, we have

\|x\circ\phi\|_{p}\;=\;\|x\|_{p}\quad\text{ and }\quad\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x\circ\phi,y\circ\phi}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;=\;\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,y}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;.

In particular, the orbit of every
{x\in\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)}
is again in
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)
.
It is straightforward to verify that (
20
) is a linear action on
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)
, which is continuous since the norm is invariant.
Let
{\Pi_{p}(x)}
be the
\mathbf{L}_{p}
-norm orbitope of

. Since the

-norm dominates the

-norm for
{p\leq q}
, we have
{\mathbb{G}(x)\subset\Pi_{q}(x)\subset\Pi_{p}(x)}
,
and taking closures shows

\Pi_{q}(x)\;=\;\text{norm-dense subset of }\Pi_{p}(x)\qquad\text{ whenever }1\leq p\leq q\leq\infty\;.

Invariance of the norm also implies the orbits are bounded, so
Lemma

7
applies.
In summary, we have shown:

Lemma 13
.

If
\mathbb{G}
acts measurable on
\Omega
and leaves
\mu
invariant, (

) defines linear continuous actions
on
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)
and
\mathbf{L}_{q}(\mu)

{1/p+1/q=1}
, the actions are dual, and every weak* orbitope in
\mathbf{L}_{q}(\mu)
is compact.

Let
{\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}}
denote the
\sigma
-algebra
of
\mathbb{G}
-invariant Borel sets. A measurable function
{f:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}}
is
\mathbb{G}
-invariant if and only if
it is
\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}
-measurable.
The
\mathbf{L}_{p}
of equivalence classes of

-measurable functions is hence

\mathbf{L}_{p}(\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}},\mu)\;=\;{\{\text{equivalence class of }f\,|\,f\text{ $\mathbb{G}$-invariant and }\|f\|_{p}<\infty\}}\;,

which is the set of elements of
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)
invariant under the action (

if we choose

as
{\mathbf{L}_{p}(\mu)}
, then
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}},\mu)

5.3. Illustration: The Hunt-Stein theorem

Consider a testing problem, in which a set
M
of probability measures on
\Omega
is partitioned into two sets

H
(the hypothesis) and
A
(the alternative).
Recall that a set
M
of probability measures is

dominated
if there is a
\sigma
-finite measure
\mu
such that
{M\ll\mu}
.
If so, we denote the corresponding set of densities

dM/d\mu:={\{dP/d\mu\,|\,P\in M\}}\subset\mathbf{L}_{1}(\mu)\;.

Given an observation
{x\in\Omega}
, one must decide
whether
x
was generated by an element of
H
(“accept the
hypothesis”) or of

(“reject”). The decision procedure
may be randomized, and is represented by a
critical function

is read as the probability (under the randomization of the test)
that the test rejects when

is observed.
A measurable function

is a valid critical function
if it takes values in
[0,1]

P
-almost surely, for all
{P\in M}
.
If

6.3. Duality

We briefly discuss a somewhat technical matter, namely
how orbitopes in
\mathcal{P}
relate to our previous results on duality.
The answer is straightforward if the Polish space
\Omega
is also compact—in this case,

\mathbf{ca}
is the dual space of the space
{(\mathbf{C}_{b},\|{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}\|_{\sup})}
of bounded continuous functions
on
\Omega
. For a general Polish space, the answer is somewhat more involved.
The dual of
\mathbf{C}_{b}
is a larger space than
\mathbf{ca}
, namely

\mathbf{C}_{b}^{\prime}\;=\;\mathbf{ba}_{n}\;:=\;{\{\mu:\mathcal{A}\to\mathbb{R}\,|\,\mu\text{ finitely additive and inner regular}\}}

where inner regular means
{\mu(A)=\sup{\{\mu(F)|F\subset A\text{ closed}\}}}
for all
{A\in\mathcal{A}}
.
We denote the dual norm by
{\|\mu\|^{\prime}:=\sup_{f}|\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{f,\mu}\mkern 2.0mu\right>|/\|f\|_{\sup}}
. For details on the dual pair

{\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{\mathbf{C}_{b},\mathbf{ba}_{n}}\mkern 2.0mu\right>}
, see
[
2
]
. Those relevant for our purposes are:

\bullet

The set
\mathbf{ca}
is the linear subspace
{\mathbf{ca}={\{\mu\in\mathbf{ba}_{n}|\mu\text{ countably additive}\}}}
of
\mathbf{ba}_{n}
.

\bullet

The sets
{\mathbf{ca}}
and
{\mathcal{P}}
are not weak* closed in
\mathbf{ba}_{n}
(unless
\Omega
is compact).
The weak* closure of a set
{M\subset\mathcal{P}}
may therefore contain elements of
\mathbf{ba}_{n}
that are not
countably additive.

\bullet

The restriction of the weak* topology to
\mathcal{P}
is the topology of convergence in distribution. In other
words, a set
{M\subset\mathcal{P}}
is closed iff there is a closed set
{M^{\prime}\in\mathbf{ba}_{n}}
such that
{M=M^{\prime}\cap\mathcal{P}}
.

\bullet

In contrast,
\mathbf{ca}
and
\mathcal{P}
are closed in the dual norm, and the restriction of
\|{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}\|^{\prime}
to
\mathbf{ca}

is precisely the total variation norm.

\bullet

Every continuous linear functional on
\mathbf{C}_{b}
is of the form
{f\mapsto\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{f,\mu}\mkern 2.0mu\right>}
for some
{\mu\in\mathbf{ba}_{n}}
. For
{\mu\in\mathbf{ca}}
,
we have
{\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{f,\mu}\mkern 2.0mu\right>=\int fd\mu}
, by a version of Riesz’ theorem.

Now assume again that
\mathbb{G}
acts continuously on
\Omega
, and consider the induced actions
{f\mapsto f\circ\phi^{-1}}
and

{\mu\mapsto\mu\circ\phi^{-1}}
on functions and finitely additive measures.
By (
11
),

\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{\phi(f),\phi(\mu)}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;=\;\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt(f\circ\phi^{-1})d(\phi_{*}\mu)\;=\;\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998ptfd\mu\;=\;\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{f,\mu}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\qquad\text{ for each }\mu\in\mathbf{ca}\;,

so the restriction of the action to the subspace
\mathbf{ca}
behaves like a dual action, and we show as part of the next result
that duality extends to the entire space
\mathbf{ba}_{n}
.
For a probability measure
P
, we can now define orbitopes by taking the closure
\Pi^{\prime}(P)
of
\text{\rm co}\,\mathbb{G}(P)
in the dual norm topology of
\mathbf{ba}_{n}
,
or weak* closure
\Pi_{*}(P)
. All our results on orbitopes in dual spaces now apply to
\Pi_{*}(P)
(though not to
\Pi^{\prime}(P)
, since the weak* topology
is consistent with the dual pair, whereas the norm topology is not).

Proposition 21
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
act continuously on a Polish space
\Omega
, and let

be a probability measure. Then
\Pi_{*}(P)
is weak* compact, and

\Pi_{{\text{\rm\tiny TV}}}(P)\;=\;\Pi^{\prime}(P)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Pi_{\text{\tiny\rm D}}(P)\;=\;\Pi_{*}(P)\cap\mathcal{P}\qquad\text{ for each }P\in\mathcal{P}\;.

If
\Pi_{*}(P)
and
\Pi_{\text{\tiny\rm D}}(P)
differ, this difference consists entirely of set functions that are not countably additive.
Moreover,
\Pi_{*}(P)
and
\Pi_{\text{\tiny\rm D}}(P)
have the same support function, and

H(f|\Pi_{\text{\tiny\rm D}}(P))\;=\;H(f|\Pi_{*}(P))\;=\;H(P|\Pi(f))\qquad\text{ for each }P\in\mathcal{P}\text{ and }f\in\mathbf{C}_{b}\;.

The compactness of weak* orbitopes is an instance of
Lemma

7
.
Since compactness of
\Pi_{*}(P)

7 Application I: Kernel mean embeddings

Mean embeddings of probability measures are used in machine learning to represent distributions

[
45
,
47
, e.g.]
.
Like a probability density, a mean embedding represents a probability measure
P
as
a function. Whereas a density is defined relative to a reference measure
\mu
and lives in
\mathbf{L}_{1}(\mu)
,
a mean embedding is defined relative to a reproducing kernel, and lives in an RKHS.

7.1. Invariant optimal embeddings

A Hilbert space
\mathbf{H}
of functions
{x:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}

is a reproducing kernel Hilbert space, or
RKHS
, if there is a
positive definite function
{\kappa:\Omega\times\Omega\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
such that

x(\omega)\;=\;\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{x,\kappa(\omega,{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\qquad\text{ for all }x\in\mathbf{H}\text{ and }\omega\in\Omega\;.

If so,
\kappa
is a
reproducing kernel
for
\mathbf{H}
. For each
{\omega\in\Omega}
, the function

{\kappa(\omega,{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})}
is an element of
\mathbf{H}
, and the map

{\Delta:\Omega\rightarrow\mathbf{H}}
defined by
{\omega\mapsto\kappa(\omega,{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})}
is
called the
feature map

[
48
]
.
Let
P
be a probability measure on
\Omega
. A function
{m(P)\in\mathbf{H}}
is the

mean embedding
of
P
if it satisfies either of the equivalent properties

\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998ptfdP\;=\;\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{f,m(P)}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\;\text{ for all }f\in\mathbf{H}\quad\text{ or }\quad m(P)\;=\;\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt\Delta(\omega)P(d\omega)\;,

where the integral on the right is again a Bochner integral
[
45
]
.
We define

\mathcal{M}\;:=\;{\{x\in\mathbf{H}\,|\,x\text{ is mean embedding of a probability measure}\}}\;.

A kernel
\kappa
is
bounded
if
{\sup_{\omega}\kappa(\omega,\omega)<\infty}
, and

characteristic
if the map
{m}
is injective on its domain in
{\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}
.

Convention
.
Call a reproducing kernel
nice
if it is continuous, bounded, characteristic,
and the function
{\kappa({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,},\omega)}
vanishes at infinity for each
{\omega\in\Omega}
.

Now suppose a group
\mathbb{G}
acts on
\Omega
.
We consider the induced action
{x\mapsto x\circ\phi}
on functions
{x:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}}
.
Our next result makes the following assumptions.

(22)

\begin{split}\text{$\Omega$ is locally }&\text{compact Polish, $\mathbb{G}$ is nice and acts continuously on $\Omega$,}\\[-1.99997pt]
&\text{and $\kappa$ is nice and diagonally $\mathbb{G}$-invariant.}\end{split}

Nice kernels on locally compact spaces are a standard assumption in the mean embedding literature
that ensures
m(P)
exists and is well-behaved

[
45
,
47
]
.
As we will see below, diagonal invariance ensures the natural action
{x\mapsto x\circ\phi}
on
functions also defines an action on the RKHS, and that this action is unitary.
For mean embeddings, our general results can be assembled into the following result; the proof
is given at the end of this section.

Theorem 22
.

If (
22
) holds, the maps
{x\mapsto x\circ\phi}

{\phi\in\mathbb{G}}

define a unitary action of

on

(i) If a convex, weakly lsc and

8 Application II: Invariant couplings

Let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be probability measures on Polish spaces
\Omega_{1}
and

\Omega_{2}
. A
coupling
of these measures is a joint
distributions
P
on
{\Omega:=\Omega_{1}\!\times\Omega_{2}}
with marginals
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
.
Let
{\Lambda=\Lambda(P_{1},P_{2})}
be the set of all such couplings.
A
cost
is a lsc function
{c:\Omega_{1}\!\times\Omega_{2}\rightarrow[0,\infty]}
,
and the
risk
of a coupling
P
is the expectation
P(c)
. Regarded as a functional

{P\mapsto P(c)}
, the risk is linear and lsc on
\Lambda
.

8.1. Invariance

Suppose a group
\mathbb{G}
acts measurably on
\Omega_{1}
and on
\Omega_{2}
,
and leaves
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
invariant. The actions define an action on the product space,

\phi(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\;:=\;(\phi\otimes\phi)(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\;=\;(\phi\omega_{1},\phi\omega_{2})\qquad\text{ for }(\omega_{1},\omega_{2})\in\Omega\text{ and }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\;.

The set of couplings of
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
invariant under this action is then

\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}\;:=\;\Lambda\cap\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{G}}\quad\text{ where }\quad\mathcal{P}_{\mathbb{G}}\;:=\;{\{P\in\mathcal{P}(\Omega)\,|\,(\phi\otimes\phi)_{*}P=P\text{ for all }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\}}

Since invariance of the marginals does not imply couplings are invariant (see
Example

33
(ii)),
the set
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}
is in general a proper subset of
\Lambda
. It is non-emtpy, since the product measure

{P_{1}\otimes P_{2}}
is always invariant. Elements of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}
are known as

joinings
in ergodic theory, and various aspects of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}
are well-studied
[
21
]
.

8.2. The set of invariant couplings

In light of
Theorem

6
, we are interested in how a linear lsc functionals, such as the risk,
behave on the extreme points of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}
. Denote by

\Sigma=\Sigma_{\mathbb{G}}
the
\sigma
-algebra of
\mathbb{G}
-invariant Borel sets in
\Omega
, and
by
\Sigma_{i}
the
\mathbb{G}
-invariant
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega_{i}
. Recall from
Section

5.2
that
\mathbf{L}_{p}(\Sigma,P)

is the subspace of
\mathbb{G}
-invariant elements of
\mathbf{L}_{p}(P)
.

Theorem 30

(Extremal invariant couplings)
.

Let a nice group
\mathbb{G}
act measurably on
\Omega_{1}
and on
\Omega_{2}
,
let
P_{1}
and
P_{2}
be
\mathbb{G}
-invariant, and let
{P\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}}

(i)

is an extreme point of
\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}
.

(ii) The set

{\{g_{1}+g_{2}\,|\,g_{i}\in\mathbf{L}_{1}(\Sigma_{i},P_{i})\}}

\mathbf{L}_{1}(\Sigma,P)
.

(iii) There is no non-zero
{f\in\mathbf{L}_{\infty}(\Sigma,P)}
such that

\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pt(g_{1}+g_{2})fdP\;=\;0\qquad\text{ for all }g_{i}\in\mathbf{L}_{1}(\Sigma_{i},P_{i})\;.

{f\in\mathbf{L}_{\infty}(\Sigma,P)}
such that, for
{(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})\sim P}
,

\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|\xi_{1}]=0=\mathbb{E}[f(\xi_{1},\xi_{2})|\xi_{2}]\qquad\text{almost surely.}

Proof.

See
Appendix

G
. The result generalizes results of J. Lindenstrauss and
K. R. Parthasarthy; see
Section

10
for a comparison and references.

8.3. Invariant optimal couplings

As an application of
Theorems

6
and
30
, we recall the Monge-Kantorovich theorem

[
42
, e.g.]
: There is a coupling
P^{*}
that minimizes the risk over

\inf{\{P(c)\,|\,P\in\Lambda\}}\;=\;P^{*}(c)\;=\;\sup{\{P_{1}(f_{1})+P_{2}(f_{2})\,|\,(f_{1},f_{2})\in\Gamma(c)\}}\;,

to the subset

This may turn the equality

For risks invariant under an amenable group, there is no such duality gap:

(Invariant optimal couplings)

and

and

are

is minimized over
\Lambda

by a
\mathbb{G}
-invariant coupling

that satisfies

\inf{\{P(c)\,|\,P\in\Lambda_{\mathbb{G}}\}}\;=\;\bar{P}(c)\;=\;\sup{\{P_{1}(f_{1})+P_{2}(f_{2})\,|\,(f_{1},f_{2})\in\Gamma_{\mathbb{G}}(c)\}}\;.

The convex set

is compact in

-invariant, since

and

are.

on

is
\mathbb{G}
-invariant by (

By

, the subset

is compact and

If

9 Cocycles

In machine learning problems, the summation trick is often used to obtain equivariant (rather than invariant) functions.
Using a simple device from algebra, called a cocycle, we can transform equivariance and other symmetry properties
into invariance under a surrogate action, which then makes our other results applicable.

9.1. Surrogate actions

Let
\mathbb{G}
and
\mathbb{H}
be groups, and
\mathcal{S}
a set. A
map
{\theta:\mathbb{G}\times\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}}
that satisfies

\theta(\psi\phi,s)\,=\,\theta(\psi,\phi s)\circ\theta(\phi,s)\quad\text{and}\quad\theta(\text{identity element of }\mathbb{G},s)=\,s\quad\text{ for }s\in\mathcal{S}

is called a
cocycle

[
55
]
. We use this definition as follows:
Let
\mathcal{T}
be another set, and
\mathcal{F}
the set of all functions
{x:\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}}
.
We let
{\mathbb{G}}
act on
\mathcal{S}
and
\mathbb{H}
on
\mathcal{T}
, and consider properties of functions
x

that can be formulated as

(27)

\theta(\phi,s)\circ x(s)\;=\;x\circ\phi^{-1}(s)\qquad\text{ for all }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and }s\in\mathcal{S}\;.

We rewrite (
27
) as invariance under a surrogate action, by defining a map

(28)

\Theta:\mathbb{G}\times\mathcal{F}\rightarrow\mathcal{F}\qquad\text{ as }\qquad\Theta(\phi,x)(s)\;:=\;\theta(\phi,s)\circ x\circ\phi(s)\;.

If and only if
\theta
is a cocycle,
\Theta
is a valid action of
\mathbb{G}
on the function set
\mathcal{F}

[
55
]
.
Clearly,
x
satisfies (
27
) if and only if it is invariant under the action
\Theta
.
The orbitope and Følner average of
x
under this action are

\Pi^{\theta}(x)\;:=\;\overline{\text{\rm co}}\,{\{\Theta(\phi,x)\,|\,\phi\in\mathbb{G}\}}\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathbf{F}^{\theta}_{n}(x)\;=\;\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\genfrac{}{}{0.4pt}{}{1}{|\A_n|}$}}\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits_{\kern-4.00002pt\mathbf{A}_{n}}\Theta(\phi,x)|d\phi|\;.

We can therefore apply
Theorem

6
to find functions that satisfy (
27
) as follows.

Corollary 34
.

Let a nice amenable group
\mathbb{G}
act on a locally convex space
X
whose elements are functions
{\mathcal{S}\rightarrow\mathcal{T}}
.
Let
\theta
be a cocycle such that
{\Theta(\phi,{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,})}
is linear and continuous for each
{\phi\in\mathbb{G}}
.
If
\Pi^{\theta}(x)

Example 35

9.2. Illustration: Equivariant conditional probabilities

As a more detailed example of the use of surrogate actions, we consider the existence of equivariant conditional distributions.
Suppose

acts measurably on

be a probability measure on
\Omega
,
and let

{\eta:\Omega\rightarrow\mathcal{P}(\Omega)}

For each

the value
{\eta(t)}
is a probability measure, and we write
{\eta(A,t):=\eta(t)(A)}
for a Borel set

.
The kernel is

\eta(\phi^{-1}{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,},t)\;=\;\eta({\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,},\phi t)\qquad\text{ for all }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and }P\text{-almost all }t\;,

or if
{\phi_{*}\eta=\eta\circ\phi}
in short. If

and

are two random elements of
\Omega
,
where
{\zeta\sim P}
and

{\zeta=t}

almost equivariance of

\mathbb{P}(\xi\in{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|\zeta=\phi t)\;=\;\mathbb{P}(\phi\xi\in{\,\vbox{\hbox{\tiny$\bullet$}}\,}|\zeta=t)\qquad\text{ for each }\phi\in\mathbb{G}\text{ and almost all }t\in\Omega\;.

Given a kernel
\eta
, an equivariant
\bar{\eta}
can be constructed as follows:

1.

Choose the cocycle
{\theta(\phi,\eta):=\phi^{-1}}
, which defines

{\Theta(\phi,\eta)=\phi^{-1}_{*}\eta\circ\phi}
. A kernel is

-equivariant if and only if it is invariant under

2.

Equip the set of probability kernels with its natural topology—this is the “weak topology”
that arises, for example, in the context of stable convergence and central limit theorems for
dependent variables
[
24
]
. We show
that this makes the orbitope of
\eta
under the action

compact.

3.

Verify
\Theta
is linear and continuous. By

,
the orbitope contains a probability kernel

that satisfies

for all

-equivariant.

Filling in the technical details (see the proof in
Appendix

Proposition 37
.

Let
\mathbb{G}
be a amenable, and let

be a

-invariant probability measures on

\eta
is a probability kernel
whose marginal distribution

-tight, there
exists a

-equivariant kernel

that satisfies

\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pth(s,t)\bar{\eta}(ds,t)P(dt)\;\leq\;\sup\nolimits_{\phi\in\mathbb{G}}\,\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998pth(\phi s,\phi t)\eta(ds,t)P(dt)

The summation formula (

Orbitopes

The term

.

Amenability
was introduced by von Neumann to generalize certain properties of compact
groups, see e.g.
[
22
,
7
]

The Hunt-Stein theorem is among the earliest applications, and led to a period of

,
30
,
51
,
17
]
.
This literature uses von Neumann’s definition, which is as follows:
Given a nice group
\mathbb{G}

\mathbf{L}_{\infty}(\mathbb{G})
with respect to Haar measure. A
mean

is a linear functional
{\chi:\mathbf{L}_{\infty}(\mathbb{G})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}

{\chi(1)=1}

{\chi(x)\geq 0}
for all non-negative
{x\in\mathbf{L}_{\infty}(\mathbb{G})}

,

,
28

.

Day’s theorem

) seems to be known mostly to specialists, but an equivalent fact

set has an invariant probability measure

,
7
, e.g.]

(Combining this with Choquet’s theorem and the fact that invariant measures have invariant barycenters

uses Day’s theorem—though without

The proof constructs a convex
\mathbb{G}
-invariant risk with compact sublevel sets,
and applies Day’s theorem to these sublevel sets. This is precisely the idea described in the introduction,
minus the use of Følner sequences.
(In our terminology, each of Le Cam’s sublevel sets contains the orbitope of the minimax solution
\hat{w}
in
the Hunt-Stein theorem.)
Another variant of the general idea appears in variational
analysis:

Fact 38

(Symmetric criticality principle,

.

Let

be a group of isometries of a Riemannian manifold

function invariant under

is in fact a critical point of

This statement is weaker than those we use, as it
does not guarantee existence of
\bar{x}

Various generalizations exist in physics a variational analysis, see e.g.
[
53
]
.

Examples of amenable groups and cocycles

A.1. Amenable groups

Many groups used in statistics and machine learning are
amenable, and this section lists some examples. For more on the mathematical implications of
amenability, see
[
7
,
52
,
22
]
.
See
[
4
]
on how the averages
\mathbf{F}_{n}
can be interpreted
as estimators, and for a range of examples in statistics.

1) All finite and compact groups are amenable (since we can choose all sets in the
Følner sequence as the group itself). All results throughout
therefore hold for compact groups (though many become trivial).
Compact groups relevant to statistics include the
symmetric
groups

\mathbb{S}_{n}
, which consists of all permutations of the
set
{\{1,\ldots,n\}}
, and the
orthogonal group

\mathbb{O}_{d}
of rotations of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

2) The
finitary symmetric group

\mathbb{S}=\cup_{n}\mathbb{S}_{n}
, which consists of all
finitely supported permutations of
\mathbb{N}
.
The subgroups
\mathbb{S}_{n}
form a Følner sequence.

3) The
discrete shifts groups

{(\mathbb{Z}^{d},+)}
. The sets

{\{-n,\ldots,n\}}^{d}

and
{\{0,\ldots,n\}}^{d}
both form Følner sequences.

4) The
continuous shift groups

{(\mathbb{R}^{d},+)}
, with Følner sequences

{[-n,n]^{d}}
or
{[0,n]^{d}}
.

5) The
Euclidean groups
of all isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which
can be identified with
{\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{O}_{d}}
.
The sets
{[0,n]^{d}\times\mathbb{O}_{d}}
form a Følner sequence.

6) The
scale group

(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\,\cdot\,)
, with
the sets
{[1,e^{n}]}
as approximating sequence. This group is
homomorphic to
{(\mathbb{R},+f)}
via the group homomorphism

{x\mapsto\exp(x)}
.

5) The
finitary special orthogonal group

\mathbb{SO}_{\infty}
, which can be
identified with
{\cup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\,\mathbb{SO}_{n}}
, and has Følner sequence
{(\mathbb{SO}_{n})_{n}}
.
Invariance under this group is called rotatability and characterizes certain Gaussian processes

[
26
]
.

6) A
finitary unitary group
can be defined similarly

[
10
]
.

7) All
abelian
, all
nilpotent
, and all
solvable
groups are
amenable
[
22
]
.

8) The group of non-singular, upper-triangular matrices is amenable (as it is solvable).

9) Any multiplicative group of upper triangular matrices whose diagonal
entries are all 1 is amenable (since it is nilpotent).

10) A special case is the
Heisenberg group
over an algebraic field
\mathbb{K}

[
55
]
,

{\{M[a,b,c]\,|\,a,b,c\in\mathbb{K}\}}\quad\text{ where }\quad M[a,b,c]\;:=\;\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&a&b\\[1.00006pt]
0&1&c\\[1.00006pt]
0&0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\;.

The sets

form a Følner sequence
if
\mathbb{K}
is
\mathbb{Z}
or
\mathbb{R}
.

11) The
crystallographic groups
. A group
\mathbb{G}
of isometries of
\mathbb{R}^{k}

is crystallographic if it tiles

with a convex polytope

for

A.2. Examples of cocycles

We have already seen in
Example

35
that equivariance, and skew-symmetry can be represented
as cocycles. We mention a few more case, but the list is far from exhaustive.

1)
Quasi-invariance
. A function is quasi-invariant under
\mathbb{G}
if it is invariant up to positive scaling.
Thus,
x
is quasi-invariant iff it is
\Theta
-invariant for any map
{\theta(\phi,s)=\theta(\theta)}

into the multiplicative group
{(\mathbb{R}_{>0},\cdot)}
.

2)
Characters
. Choose
\mathbb{H}
as the complex circle

{{\{z\in\mathbb{C}|\,|z|=1\}}}
with multiplication as operation.
A continuous function
{\theta:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{H}}

with
{\theta(e_{\mathbb{G}})=1}
is a
character
of
\mathbb{G}
.
This implies (
9.1
), so the characters
of
\mathbb{G}
are precisely the continuous simple cocycles into
\mathbb{H}
. For
{\mathbb{G}=(\mathbb{R},+)}
,
every character is of the form
{\phi\mapsto e^{i\psi\phi}}
for some
{c\in\mathbb{R}}
, which is, of course, the Fourier kernel.

3)
The modulus
. On every nice group exists a
unique function

{\theta:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
, called the
modulus
of
\mathbb{G}
,
that satisfies

\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998ptf(\phi\psi^{-1})|d\phi|\,=\,\theta(\psi)\mathop{\mathord{\raise 0.49991pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\int$}}}\nolimits\kern-0.99998ptf(\phi)|d\phi|\qquad\text{ for all }\phi,\psi\in\mathbb{G}

for every compactly supported continuous
{f:\mathbb{G}\rightarrow(0,\infty)}
, see
[
16
]
.
The definition implies
{\theta(\phi\psi)=\theta(\phi)\theta(\psi)}
, so the modulus is a simple cocycle.

4) The
coboundary

{(\phi x-x)(s)=:\theta(\phi,s)}
of a function
x
(
Section

4.3
) is a cocycle.

5)
Gradient fields
.
An isometry of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a map
{\phi(\omega)=A_{\phi}\omega+b_{\phi}}
, where
A_{\phi}
is an orthogonal matrix and
{b_{\phi}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}
.
Let
\mathbb{G}
be a group of isometries.
If a differentiable function
{f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
is
\mathbb{G}
-invariant, its gradient
vector field
{F(\omega)=(\nabla x)(\omega)}
satisfies
{A_{\phi}F=F\circ\phi}
for all
{\phi\in\mathbb{G}}
.
This is
\Theta
-invariance for the simple cocycle
{\theta(\phi,\omega):=A_{\phi}^{t}}
.
See
[
1
]
for more on such
\Theta
-invariant fields.

To prove

Lemma 40
.

Let

be a Radon probability measure on a locally convex Hausdorff space

whose barycenter exists.
Then
{f(\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{P}\mkern 2.0mu\right>)\leq P(f)}
holds for every proper convex lsc function
{f:X\to\mathbb{R}}
.
If the closed convex hull of a set
{M\subset X}
is compact, we also have

{f\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{P}\mkern 2.0mu\right>\leq\sup{\{f(x)|x\in M\}}}
for every propability measure
P
on
\overline{M}
.

Proof.

1^{\circ}
A proper convex lsc function is the pointwise supremum
{f(x)=\sup a(x)}
, taken over all affine continuous
functions
{a:X\rightarrow\mathbb{R}}
that satisfy
a<f

[
2
, 7.6]
.
Since
{P(a)=a\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{P}\mkern 2.0mu\right>}
holds by the definition of barycenters whenever

is linear, it also
holds if

is affine. We hence have

{f\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{P}\mkern 2.0mu\right>=\sup_{a<f}a\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{P}\mkern 2.0mu\right>=\sup_{a<f}P(a)\leq P(f)}
.

2^{\circ}
By Bauer’s maximum principle, each
a
attains its maximum on the compact convex set
{K:=\overline{\text{\rm co}}\,M}
at an extreme point, so

f\left<\mkern 2.0mu\smash{P}\mkern 2.0mu\right>=\sup_{a<f}P(a)\leq\sup_{a<f}\sup_{x\in K}a(x)\leq\sup_{a<f}\sup_{x\in\text{\rm ex}K}a(x)=\sup_{x\in\text{\rm ex}K}\sup_{a<f}a(x)=\sup_{x\in\text{\rm ex}K}f(x)\;.

All extreme points of
K
are in the closure of

[
41
, 1.5]
, so
{\sup_{\text{\rm ex}K}f=\sup_{\overline{M}}f}
, and since

{\sup_{\,\overline{M}}f=\sup_{M}f}
.
∎
