Phonon Hall viscosity from phonon-spinon interactions
Yunchao Zhang, Yanting Teng, Rhine Samajdar, Subir Sachdev, Mathias S. Scheurer

Abstract
Motivated by experimental observations, Samajdar et al. [Nature Physics 15 , 1290 (2019)] have proposed that the insulating Néel state in the parent compounds of the cuprates is proximate to a quantum phase transition to a state in which Néel order coexists with semion topological order. We study the manner in which proximity to this transition can make the phonons chiral, by inducing a significant phonon Hall viscosity. We describe the spinon-phonon coupling in a lattice spinon model coupled to a strain field, and also using a general continuum theory constrained only by symmetry. We find a nonanalytic Hall viscosity across the transition, with a divergent second derivative at zero temperature.



I
Introduction

Quantum spin liquids (QSLs) are exotic phases of matter arising from highly correlated spins with frustrated interactions, in which zero-point fluctuations are so strong that spin ordering is prevented even down to zero temperature
Anderson 1987
;
Kivelson et al. 1987
. QSLs often host a wide variety of collective phenomena, including topological degeneracy and long-range entanglement
Wen 2002
;
Balents 2010
;
Savary and Balents 2016
;
Lee 2008
that make them ideal for theoretical study. Most remarkably, QSLs are characterized by nonlocal fractionalized excitations
Senthil and Fisher 2001
, such as charge-neutral “spinons” coupled to emergent gauge fields
Sachdev 2018
. The spinons can be either gapped or gapless and may be bosons or fermions, depending on the scenario
Read and Sachdev 1991
;
Senthil and Fisher 2000
.

While there have been extensive experimental efforts towards detection of QSLs, unambiguous evidence remains elusive
Broholm et al. 2020
. The measurement of topological properties of QSLs is difficult since unlike conventional quasiparticles, spinons are invisible to local probes. One current line of thinking therefore aims to study QSLs by looking for signatures of fractionalization through the interactions of spinons with other degrees of freedom in the system.

In this paper, we will study the coupling of spinons to lattice excitations. Specifically, we will be interested in a response coefficient called the Hall viscosity
Avron et al. 1995
;
Avron 1998
. Similar to the Hall conductance, the phonon Hall viscosity can appear for phonons coupled to a gapped electronic system that breaks time-reversal symmetry. The Hall viscosity tensor
\eta_{ijkl}
characterizes the system’s viscoelastic response to a strain deformation as

\displaystyle\expectationvalue{\pdv{H}{\epsilon^{}_{ij}}}=\lambda_{ijkl}\epsilon_{kl}+\eta_{ijkl}\dot{\epsilon}_{kl},

(1)

where
\epsilon_{ij}\equiv(\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{i})/2
is the symmetrized strain tensor, and the time derivative is represented by the overdot. In the presence of
C_{4}
symmetry in two dimensions (
2
D), there is only one independent component of the Hall viscosity tensor,
\eta_{xxxy}

Lifshitz et al. 1986
. Contrary to a viscosity that is dissipative, the Hall viscosity is antisymmetric with respect to the pairs of indices
(ij)
and
(kl)
and hence, nondissipative
dis
.

The Hall viscosity was first studied in the context of the quantum Hall effect, in which it was shown to be proportional to the square of the electron filling density for integer quantum Hall fluids
Avron 1998
;
Read 2009
;
Read and Rezayi 2011
;
Bradlyn et al. 2012
;
Hoyos and Son 2012
;
Barkeshli et al. 2012
;
Ganeshan and Abanov 2017
;
Park and Haldane 2014
;
Parrikar et al. 2014
;
Hughes et al. 2011
;
Hughes et al. 2013
. In these systems, the Hall viscosity can be calculated as the response of an appropriate continuum field theory to a variation of the underlying geometry or spatial metric,
g_{ij}
. This Hall viscosity, which acts as a Chern-Simons-type term for the frame field, was termed the
gravitational
Hall viscosity in Ref.
Barkeshli et al. 2012
. Instead, our focus will be on the response of systems of phonons and the resulting
phonon
Hall viscosity. While the Hall viscosity originates from the chiral spinons, we use the term
phonon
Hall viscosity to indicate that the stress tensor is coupled to the lattice acoustic phonons instead of a background spatial metric. This is appropriate because the resulting equations of motion for the phonons have a corresponding Hall viscosity term.

Theoretically, the phonon Hall viscosity has been studied for electronic systems and topological insulators
Barkeshli et al. 2012
;
Shapourian et al. 2015
;
Hoyos and Son 2012
;
Shitade and Kimura 2014
;
Cho et al. 2014
. For lattice systems, such as discrete tight-binding models, there are
a priori
many different ways to model the viscoelastic response, including coupling to a lattice frame field
Shapourian et al. 2015
or using momentum polarization methods
Tu et al. 2013
;
Zaletel et al. 2013
. We will adopt a more physical “geometric bond stretching” approach, realizing the strain as a modification to the tight-binding overlap integrals originating from the lattice sites being displaced from their equilibrium positions. This coincides with the approach of viewing the phonon Hall viscosity as the adiabatic response of a system to acoustic phonons
Barkeshli et al. 2012
, analogous to the Hall conductance. Using the Kubo formula, the Hall viscosity can also be recognized as a type of Berry curvature of the ground-state wavefunction.

While a measurement of the Hall viscosity would provide valuable information for identifying phases with topological order, it has been difficult to do so in practice. Nevertheless, it is possible to experimentally detect the phonon Hall viscosity through other physical quantities that share the broken symmetries. For example, one such quantity is the phonon thermal Hall conductivity, which can be nonzero only with broken time-reversal and (in-plane) mirror symmetries. In fact, recent experiments by
Grissonnanche et al. 2020
and
Boulanger et al. 2020
suggest that chiral phonons are responsible for the large thermal Hall conductivities measured in the insulating phase of several cuprate superconductors. A nonzero phonon Hall viscosity could be a mechanism for intrinsic phonon chirality in these systems. The phonon Hall viscosity leads to both intrinsic and extrinsic contributions to the thermal Hall conductivity: the intrinsic contribution is discussed in Section
VI
, while the extrinsic contribution is discussed in Ref.
Guo and Sachdev 2021
.

We will study the phonon Hall viscosity induced by lattice strain couplings to a chiral spin liquid on the square lattice. In particular, we are interested in a spin-liquid ansatz in which the orbital coupling of the applied magnetic field drives the conventional confining Néel insulator to a state with semion topological order
Samajdar et al. 2019a
;
Wang et al. 2017
. Recent optical experiments by
de La Torre et al. 2021

indicate the presence of mirror-plane-symmetry breaking which is compatible with this scenario.
In our paper, we will analyze the behavior of the Hall viscosity in both the lattice tight-binding model and the continuum Dirac field theory. We find that the above mentioned quantum phase transition (QPT) in the spinon sector is reflected by a divergence in the second derivative of the phonon Hall viscosity.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. We begin in Sec.
II
by reviewing the general definition of the phonon Hall viscosity and linear response theory. Section
III
introduces the mean-field chiral spin liquid model on the square lattice. We study the spinon-phonon interactions in two settings. On the lattice, we consider phonon-fermion coupling by “bond stretching” in Sec.
IV
, whereas for the continuum field theory, we couple phonons and spinons based on symmetry considerations in Sec.
V
. After commenting on some physical consequences in Sec.
VI
, we summarize and discuss our results in Sec.
VII
.

II
Phonon Hall Viscosity

II.1
Phonon effective action with broken time-reversal symmetry

For gapped fermionic systems, the low-energy dynamics of acoustic phonons is captured by an effective action for
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{r})
, describing the displacement of an atom from its original location. The effective action obtained by integrating out the fermionic degrees of freedom is

\displaystyle\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\int\mathcal{D}\bar{\psi}\,\mathcal{D}\psi\,\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}\;e^{-S(\boldsymbol{u},\bar{\psi},\psi)}=\int\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{u}\;e^{-S_{\text{eff}}(\boldsymbol{u})}.

(2)

In the long-wavelength limit, the phonon effective action is determined by the mass density
\rho
and the elastic moduli tensor
\lambda_{ijkl}
,

S_{\rm eff}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\,dt\;\left(\rho\dot{u}_{j}\dot{u}_{j}-\lambda_{ijkl}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}u_{l}\right).

(3)

For gapless states such as metals, the phonon action will generally be nonlocal and thus cannot be written as above.

When time-reversal symmetry is broken, there is an allowed, nondissipative Hall viscosity term
Barkeshli et al. 2012
;
Avron et al. 1995
;
Avron 1998

\displaystyle\delta S=\frac{1}{2}\int d^{d}x\,dt\;\eta_{ijkl}\partial_{i}u_{j}\partial_{k}\dot{u}_{l},

(4)

with
\eta_{ijkl}=-\eta_{klij}
antisymmetric under the exchange of pairs of indices. The number of independent components of
\eta_{ijkl}
can, in general, be determined using symmetry. For example, one can show that
\eta_{ijkl}
will always vanish for a three-dimensional isotropic system. As we are concerned with phonons in a spin-liquid background, we will restrict ourselves to
d=2
in the subsequent analysis. For simplicity, we will also assume
C_{4}
-rotation symmetry, though this requirement can easily be relaxed.

Following Ref.
Barkeshli et al. 2012
, we can knead the Hall viscosity in Eq. (
4
) into a more convenient form by defining the strain tensor
\epsilon_{ij}
and the vorticity (also called rotation) tensor
\theta_{ij}
according to

\epsilon_{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u_{j}+\partial_{j}u_{i}),\quad\theta_{ij}\equiv\frac{1}{2}(\partial_{i}u_{j}-\partial_{j}u_{i}).

(5)

Dropping boundary terms, Eq. (
4
) can then be rewritten as

\displaystyle\delta S

\displaystyle=2\int d^{2}{x}\,d{t}\left[\eta^{H}\hskip-3.00003pt\left(\epsilon_{xx}-\epsilon_{yy}\right)\dot{\epsilon}_{xy}+\eta^{M}\hskip-3.00003pt\left(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}\right)\dot{\theta}_{xy}\right].

(6)

Here, we have defined
\eta^{H}

=

(\eta_{xxxy}+\eta_{xxyx})/2
and
\eta^{M}=(\eta_{xxxy}-\eta_{xxyx})/2
. While boundary terms can modify surface phonon dispersions for topological insulators and generate interesting effects such as phonon Faraday rotation
Shapourian et al. 2015
;
Tuegel and Hughes 2017
, we will ignore these phenomena in our discussion. Finally, it can also be useful to rewrite the action
\delta S
in Eq. (
6
) directly in terms of the deformation field
\boldsymbol{u}
. In that case, there ends up being one effective Hall viscosity coefficient
\eta\equiv\eta_{xxxy}=\eta^{H}+\eta^{M}
,

\displaystyle\delta S=\int d^{2}{x}\,d{t}\left[\frac{-\eta}{2}\left(\nabla^{2}u_{x}\dot{u}_{y}-\nabla^{2}u_{y}\dot{u}_{x}\right)\right].

(7)

In the calculations hereafter, however, we will follow Eq. (
6
) and discuss
\eta^{H}
and
\eta^{M}
separately.

II.2
Definition as a response function

We can also view the Hall viscosity as a response function. To begin, we make the adiabatic assumption that the time scale of the lattice motion is infinitely slower than that of the fermions’ motion, so that the electronic configuration is always in its instantaneous ground state with respect to its lattice configuration. This implies that the electronic quasiparticles only couple to phonons that are well below their energy gap. The lattice deformation fields
\boldsymbol{u}
then act to modify the effective hopping terms in the tight-binding Hamiltonian
H_{\rm{t.b.}}
for the electronic system and can be treated as external parameters.

In Fourier space, viewing
u(\boldsymbol{q})=\sum_{n}u({\boldsymbol{r}})e^{i\boldsymbol{q}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}}/\sqrt{L}
as parameters in
H_{\rm{t.b.}}
, we can first define the two-component Hall tensor from linear response theory through the Kubo formula
Barkeshli et al. 2012
;
Bradlyn et al. 2012

\eta_{ab}(\boldsymbol{q})=\lim_{\omega\rightarrow 0}\dfrac{1}{\omega}\dfrac{1}{L^{d}}\int dt\;e^{i\omega t}\left\langle\left[\dfrac{\partial H_{\rm t.b.}(t)}{\partial u_{a,\boldsymbol{q}}},\dfrac{\partial H_{\rm t.b.}(0)}{\partial u_{b,-\boldsymbol{q}}}\right]\right\rangle,

(8)

where it is clear that the Hall tensor is, by construction, antisymmetric, i.e.,
\eta_{ab}=-\eta_{ba}
.
This leads to an effective action of the form

\delta S=\dfrac{1}{2}\int\dfrac{d^{d}{\boldsymbol{q}}\,d{t}}{(2\pi)^{d}}\eta_{ab}(\boldsymbol{q})u_{a}(-\boldsymbol{q},t)\dot{u}_{b}(\boldsymbol{q},t).

(9)

From Eq. (
9
), we can obtain the Hall viscosity tensor by taking the appropriate derivatives

\displaystyle\eta_{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}\partialderivative{{q}^{}_i}\partialderivative{{q}^{}_k}\eta_{jl}(\boldsymbol{q}).

(10)

III
Spin Liquid Ansatz on the Square Lattice

Our model of interest, studied in Ref.
Samajdar et al. 2019a
, describes
S=1/2
antiferromagnets on the square lattice with the spin Hamiltonian
H_{\rm spin}=H_{0}+H_{B}
, where

\displaystyle H_{0}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i<j}J_{ij}\boldsymbol{S}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{j}+\cdots,

(11a)

\displaystyle H_{B}

\displaystyle=J_{\chi}\sum_{\triangle}\boldsymbol{S}_{i}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{j}\times\boldsymbol{S}_{k}-\sum_{i}\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}\cdot\boldsymbol{S}_{i}.

(11b)

H_{0}
describes nearest-neighbor spin interactions and other possible exchange terms that are invariant under all spacetime symmetries.
H_{B}
describes the coupling of the electrons to an applied magnetic field
Sen and Chitra 1995
. The
J_{\chi}
term couples to the scalar spin chirality and is induced by the orbital coupling of the magnetic field to the electrons. The value of
J_{\chi}
is proportional to
the small magnetic flux penetrating the square lattice. The second term in
H_{B}
is the Zeeman coupling of the magnetic field, with the electron magnetic moment absorbed in the definition of
\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}
. Therefore, the physical magnetic field is included in our model through both an orbital coupling (
J_{\chi}
) and a Zeeman coupling (
\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}
). While
H_{\rm spin}
could, in principle, also include Dzyaloshinskii-Moriya exchange terms, we do not consider the effect of spin-orbit interactions here.

Numerical studies of
H_{\rm spin}
on the square
Nielsen et al. 2013
and other lattices
Haghshenas et al. 2019
;
Gong et al. 2017
;
Saadatmand and McCulloch 2017
;
Hu et al. 2016
;
Wietek and Läuchli 2017
;
He and Chen 2015
;
Bauer et al. 2014
have found evidence of a chiral spin liquid phase at small nonzero
J_{\chi}
, and it was argued in Ref.
Samajdar et al. 2019a
that near a critical spin liquid,
J_{\chi}
would be a relevant perturbation leading to semion topological order. Consequently, one finds an enhanced thermal Hall conductivity
\kappa_{xy}
even in the antiferromagnetic Néel state
Samajdar et al. 2019a
stemming from the discontinuity of the zero-temperature thermal Hall response
|\Delta\kappa_{xy}/T|=(\pi/6)(k_{B}^{2}/\hbar)
between the trivial and topological phases
Cappelli et al. 2002
.
On the other hand, we will find the phonon Hall viscosity to be continuous (but nonanalytic) across this QPT.

III.1
Mean-field theory

We begin our mean-field analysis by considering the square-lattice Néel state as the confining phase of an SU(2) gauge theory of fluctuations
about a
\pi
-flux mean-field state
Wang et al. 2017
. Transforming to the parton representation
Affleck and Marston 1988
;
Marston and Affleck 1989
, the spin operator at each site is decomposed as

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}_{i}=\dfrac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}f_{i}.

(12)

Here,
f_{i}\equiv(f_{i\uparrow},f_{i\downarrow})^{T}
represents the two-component fermionic spinon operator while
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
denotes the Pauli matrices. The mapping from the spin-1/2 Hilbert space to the fermionic one expands the Hilbert space, and we must impose a single-site occupancy constraint in order to remain within the physical Hilbert space. Therefore, the fermionic band structure of spinons is always constrained to be at half-filling. Furthermore, Eq. (
12
) has an SU(2) gauge redundancy
Lee et al. 2006
;
Hermele 2007
and a full treatment of
H_{\rm spin}
would also require analysis of the SU(2) gauge field associated with
f

Affleck et al. 1988
;
Coleman and Andrei 1988
;
Andrei and Coleman 1989
.

In our mean-field treatment, we ignore the SU(2) gauge fluctuations. Instead, we will be interested in a mean-field saddle point which breaks this SU(2) gauge symmetry down to U(1)
Wen 2002
;
Wen 2004
. Inserting the parton representation of
\boldsymbol{S}_{i}
into
H_{\rm spin}
and mean-field factorizing while respecting the spacetime and gauge symmetries, we obtain the spinon Hamiltonian
Wang et al. 2017
;
Wen et al. 1989
;
Wen 2002
;
Scheurer and Sachdev 2018
;
Samajdar et al. 2019a

\displaystyle{H}_{\rm t.b.}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i<j}\left(t_{ij}f_{j}^{\dagger}f_{i}+t_{ij}^{*}f_{i}^{\dagger}f_{j}\right)

\displaystyle-\dfrac{1}{2}\sum_{i}(\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}+\zeta_{i}\boldsymbol{N})\cdot f_{i}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}f_{i}.

(13)

Our ansatz for the spinon hopping terms
t_{ij}
is shown in Fig.
1
. The nearest-neighbor hopping terms,
t_{1}
, arise from the factorization of the Heisenberg exchange couplings in
H_{0}
[Eq. (
11a
)]. The second nearest-neighbor hopping terms,
\pm it_{2}
, originate from the scalar spin chirality
J_{\chi}
in Eq. (
11b
), and they have the same symmetry as the orbital coupling of the electrons to an applied magnetic field orthogonal to the lattice plane. In particular, the field-induced couplings
t_{2}
break time-reversal and reflection symmetries but preserve their composition. We have also assumed a nonzero Néel order
\boldsymbol{N}
, with
\zeta_{i}=\pm 1
on the two checkerboard sublattices (
A/B
) of the square lattice. The Néel order is temperature-dependent in general but for simplicity, here, we regard
N
as fixed. In order to minimize the energy of the antiferromagnet with a Zeeman coupling, we take
\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}\cdot\boldsymbol{N}=0
. The Zeeman coupling along the
\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
-axis originates from the perpendicular applied external field, so
\boldsymbol{N}
lies in the
xy
-plane. As there is no spin-orbit coupling, we will perform a rotation in spin space for convenience, so that
\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}\propto\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
and
\boldsymbol{N}\propto\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
. Equation (
13
) can be written in momentum space, with
f_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv\sum_{i}e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{r}_{i}}f_{i}/\sqrt{L}=\quantity(f_{\vec{k} A \uparrow}, f_{\vec{k} B \uparrow}, f_{\vec{k} A \downarrow}, f_{\vec{k} B \downarrow})^{T}
, as

\displaystyle H_{\rm t.b.}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{\boldsymbol{k}}f_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{\dagger}h_{\boldsymbol{k}}f^{\phantom{\dagger}}_{\boldsymbol{k}},

(14)

\displaystyle h_{\boldsymbol{k}}

\displaystyle=2t_{1}\cos(k^{}_x)\tau^{x}-2t_{1}\sin(k^{}_y)\tau^{y}

\displaystyle+4t_{2}\sin(k^{}_x)\cos(k^{}_y)\tau^{z}+\frac{N}{2}\sigma^{z}\tau^{z}+\frac{\absolutevalue{\vec{B}^{}_Z}}{2}\sigma^{x},

(15)

where the Pauli matrices acting in sublattice and spin spaces are denoted by
(\tau^{x},\tau^{y},\tau^{z})
and
(\sigma^{x},\sigma^{y},\sigma^{z})
, respectively.

The mean-field phase diagram for this ansatz is sketched in Fig.
2
. With our choice of a two-site unit cell, we obtain a total of four spinon bands, which are half filled. When the net Chern number of the occupied bands is zero, one obtains a conventional Néel state, and the theory for the gauge fluctuations will have no Chern-Simons term, leading to confinement. However, when the net Chern number of the filled bands is two, we obtain a state with semion topological order coexisting with the Néel order. With a fixed Néel order, one can thus move between the two phases by tuning the orbital (
t_{2}
) and Zeeman (
\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}
) couplings of an applied field. We discuss this point further in Sec.
IV.3
.

While this specific ansatz may appear to break lattice symmetries at first sight, the representation Eq. (
12

f_{i}\rightarrow f_{i}e^{i\vartheta_{i}},\quad t_{ij}\rightarrow t_{ij}e^{i(\vartheta_{i}-\vartheta_{j})}.

(16)

Accordingly, the representation of lattice symmetries can be supplemented by an appropriate gauge transformation, and so the spinons
f
form a projective representation of the lattice symmetry group, called the projective symmetry group

;
Wen 2004
;
Bieri et al. 2016

{H}_{\rm t.b.}
in Eq. (

, as shown in Ref.
Samajdar et al. 2019a
. We will also consider the projective symmetries in detail when we analyze the continuum spinon theory in Sec.
V
.

IV
Hall viscosity from spinon couplings to lattice strain fields

Given our tight-binding ansatz in Sec.
III
, we will model the spinon-phonon coupling through the microscopic deformation of the hopping amplitudes as the result of lattice strain, i.e, bond stretching. As mentioned previously, we assume that the spinons only couple to low-energy phonons with frequencies well below the spinon energy gap. There are two equivalent ways of computing the resulting response to the lattice distortion by bond stretching. The first is to compute the one-loop phonon effective action by integrating out the spinons [shown in Fig.
3
(b)]; the second is to use the linear response formalism and compute the adiabatic Berry curvature as the result of the variation of the strain field
Barkeshli et al. 2012
;
Shapourian et al. 2015
. We use the first approach here as it more closely makes contact with our later continuum calculations.

IV.1
Geometric coupling through bond stretching

To introduce the method of geometric bond stretching, we will consider a generic tight-binding model

\displaystyle{H}_{\rm t.b.}=\sum_{ij}t_{ij}c^{\dagger}_{j}c_{i},

(18)

where the hopping amplitude
t_{ij}
represents the overlap integral between the orbitals at site
i
and site
j
with bond length (spatial separation)
\absolutevalue{\vec{r}_0}
. In models with multiple orbitals with nontrivial symmetry properties, the change of hopping amplitudes can also have an angular dependence. In our case here, however, Eq. (
13
) is a model with only one type of orbital (
d_{x^{2}-y^{2}}
) on each site of the square lattice, so, to leading order, the hopping amplitude only depends on the distance between the two sites.
Following the approach of Ref.
Shapourian et al. 2015
, suppose now that the bond length becomes a variable
\boldsymbol{r}
so that we can introduce a bond stretching of
\delta\boldsymbol{r}=\boldsymbol{r}-\boldsymbol{r}_{0}
, illustrated in the inset of Fig.
1
. Assuming that
t_{ij}
is a smooth function of such small deformations, the hopping amplitude then becomes

\displaystyle t(\boldsymbol{r})\equiv t_{\boldsymbol{r}_{i},\boldsymbol{r}_{j}}\simeq t(\boldsymbol{r}_{0})+\delta\boldsymbol{r}\cdot\evaluated{\grad t(\vec{r})}_{\vec{r}_0}+\order{\delta\vec{r} ^2}.

(19)

For example, the nearest-neighbor (horizontal) hopping amplitude from site
\boldsymbol{n}
to
\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{x}
is
t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{x}}=t(\boldsymbol{x})
where
\boldsymbol{x}=a\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
. Letting
u_{x}(\boldsymbol{n})
and
u_{x}(\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{x})
be the deformations along the
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
-axis of the two sites, the hopping amplitude is approximated as

\displaystyle t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{x}}

\displaystyle\simeq t(\boldsymbol{x})+\evaluated{\pdv{t}{r} }_{\vec{x}}\big(u_{x}(\boldsymbol{n}+\boldsymbol{x})-u_{x}(\boldsymbol{n})\big)+\order{\delta\vec{r} ^2},

\displaystyle=t(\boldsymbol{x})+a\evaluated{\pdv{t}{r} }_{\vec{x}}\big(\partial_{x}u_{x}\big),

(20)

where, on the second line, we have assumed that the lattice distortion is a smooth function on the lattice scale. This is consistent with our assumption of considering only adiabatic spinon-phonon interactions. Carrying through this procedure with our mean-field ansatz defined in Eq. (
13
), we obtain the modified hopping amplitudes as

\displaystyle|t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}\pm\boldsymbol{x}}|

\displaystyle\simeq t_{1}+\lambda_{1}\epsilon_{xx},

(21a)

\displaystyle|t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}\pm\boldsymbol{y}}|

\displaystyle\simeq t_{1}+\lambda_{1}\epsilon_{yy},

(21b)

\displaystyle|t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}\pm(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y})}|

\displaystyle\simeq t_{2}+\lambda_{2}(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}+2\epsilon_{xy}),

(21c)

\displaystyle|t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}\pm(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})}|

\displaystyle\simeq t_{2}+\lambda_{2}(\epsilon_{xx}+\epsilon_{yy}-2\epsilon_{xy})\,,

(21d)

expressed in terms of the strain tensor
\epsilon_{ij}
in Eq. (
5

IV.3
Hall viscosity near the spinon critical point

Compared to the quantized thermal Hall conductivity or the ordinary Hall conductance, the Hall viscosity plotted in Fig.
5
is continuous and, at first sight, does not seem to encode any signatures of a QPT. However, as seen in Fig.
5
(a), it is possible for the derivatives of the Hall viscosity to have a discontinuity or divergence at the critical point.

In the mean-field ansatz given in Eq. (
13

t_{2}
and Néel order
\boldsymbol{N}

=

N\hat{\boldsymbol{z}}
, one can tune across the topological phase transition. In particular, at
\boldsymbol{B}_{Z}

=

0
, the critical points at
N

=

\pm 8t_{2}
describe the transition between a confining Néel state and a state where the Néel order coexists with a chiral spin liquid. At both critical points, the spectra have pairs of Dirac cones at
\pm\mathbf{Q}
where
\mathbf{Q}=\quantity(\pi/2, 0)
. For example, when
N=8t_{2}
, fermions in the spin-down (
-
) sector have a Dirac cone at
\mathbf{Q}
so that
|\mathbf{H}_{-,\mathbf{Q}}|=0
; a similar statement follows for the spin-up (
+
) sector. To examine
\eta^{H}
near the QPT, we expand the spinon momentum around
-\mathbf{Q}
as
\boldsymbol{k}=-\mathbf{Q}+\boldsymbol{q}
for small momentum
\boldsymbol{q}
. Then, we find—to leading order in
\boldsymbol{q}
—for
\eta^{H}
at
T=0
:

\displaystyle\lambda_{4}\quantity[ \frac{\qty(t^{}_2 - \frac{N}{8})q_x^2 \lambda^{}_1 - t^{}_1 \lambda^{}_2 q^2 }{8\, \abs{q^2+ 4 \qty(t^{}_2-\frac{N}{8})^2}^{3/2}} + \frac{\qty(t^{}_2 + \frac{N}{8})q_x^2 \lambda^{}_1 - t_1 \lambda^{}_2 q^2}{64\, \abs{t^{}_2 + \frac{N}{8}}^3} ].

(29)

As mentioned earlier, we observe that the nonvanishing leading terms above arise from the 4NN spinon-phonon couplings in Eq. (
23
). The second term in Eq. (
29

-\mathbf{Q}
. The first term appears divergent but is actually finite when we take into account the summation over momentum, which comes with measure
\absolutevalue{\vec{q}}d\absolutevalue{\vec{q}}
.

While
\eta^{H}
seems well-behaved, its derivatives with respect to the time-reversal-breaking
t_{2}
can have singularities and signal a QPT of the spinons. It is convenient to rewrite our expression as a function of the Dirac masses

with respect to

where we have written
\lambda_{2}\propto t_{2}\propto m_{1}+m_{2}
. As previously noted, the second derivative’s divergence manifests as a kink in
\eta^{H}
at the QPT. Note that without a nonzero

V.4

V
Hall viscosity from spinon couplings to continuum strain fields

In previous sections, we studied how phonon chirality could emerge from an underlying chiral spin liquid on the square lattice. The calculation of the continuum phonon Hall viscosity is qualitatively the same as that for the lattice phonon Hall viscosity: we begin by defining the spinon Hamiltonian
H_{\rm sp}
and symmetry considerations constrain the allowed spinon-phonon couplings. However, instead of taking into account lattice displacements through the strain dependence of tight-binding parameters, we will see how phonon couplings emerge from a projective symmetry analysis of the underlying spin liquid. In particular, the lattice space group can have significant effects on the topological quantization of the phonon Hall viscosity. Our representation-theoretic approach follows Ref.
Serbyn and Lee 2013
. We also note that the symmetry-based approach to electron-phonon interactions has been well studied in the case of graphene
Rachel et al. 2016
;
Basko 2008
;
Castro Neto et al. 2009
;
Guinea et al. 2009
;
Levy et al. 2010
;
Mañes 2007
.

, but the main difference in our spin-liquid system is that the analysis needs to account for the projective symmetry group of our ansatz. A universal procedure that does exactly this is provided by
Serbyn and Lee 2013
, and we will reproduce their method here to provide background.

We begin by specifying the form of the spinon-phonon interaction Hamiltonian,

\mathcal{H}_{\rm sp-ph}=\int\dfrac{d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}\,d^{2}\boldsymbol{q}}{(2\pi)^{4}}\Psi_{a}^{\dagger}(\boldsymbol{k+q}){h}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})\Psi_{a}(\boldsymbol{k}).

(39)

Expanding
\boldsymbol{k}
around the Dirac points at
\Gamma
, we allow the presence of terms of zeroth order,
{h}^{(0)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{q})
, and linear order,
{h}^{(1)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})
, in the spinon momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
so that the total interaction Hamiltonian can be written as

{h}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})={h}^{(0)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{q})+{h}^{(1)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}).

(40)

Often, only the zeroth-order contribution
{h}^{(0)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{q})
needs to be considered, but as we will find for the nonchiral
\pi
-flux state,
{h}^{(0)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{q})=0
by symmetry. In the case of nonzero
t_{2}
, there is a single symmetry-allowed zeroth-order phonon coupling. Either way, to obtain a nonzero
\eta^{H}
, it will be necessary to also take
{h}^{(1)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})
into account. The
{h}^{(1)}_{\rm sp-ph}
term can be understood as a deformation of the spinon band structure near the Dirac points at
\Gamma
by acoustic phonons.

Acoustic phonons can only couple to the spinons through spatial derivatives of the phonon field, so they enter into
{h}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})
through the
\boldsymbol{q}
Fourier component of
\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{r})
. As in the previous section, we expect couplings to the time derivative of
\boldsymbol{u}
to be suppressed by the ratio of the sound and Fermi velocities, so we will ignore them in our analysis. Since both the phonon fields and the phonon momenta transform under the vector representation
E_{1}
of
C_{4v}
, we can decompose the set of terms
\partial_{i}u_{j}(\boldsymbol{r})\sim-iq_{i}u_{j}(\boldsymbol{q})
into irreducible representations as

E_{1}^{\rm ph}\otimes E_{1}^{\rm ph}=\oplus_{j}D_{j}^{\rm ph},

(41)

where
D_{j}^{\rm ph}
labels irreducible representations of
C_{4v}
. As spinons are fermionic while phonons are bosonic, the leading-order coupling of phonons must be to bilinears of the continuum spinon fields
\psi
. Even though
\psi
realizes a projective representation of the lattice symmetry group
C_{4v}^{\prime}
, the space of local spinon bilinears,

G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}=\left\{\psi^{\dagger}\mathbb{I}\psi,\;\psi^{\dagger}\tau^{i}\psi,\;\psi^{\dagger}\mu^{i}\psi,\;\psi^{\dagger}(\mu^{i}\tau^{j})\psi\right\},

(42)

realizes regular representations in our Abelian U(1) spin liquid because the U(1) gauge factors cancel. For non-Abelian SU(2) spin liquids, as studied in Refs.
Hermele et al. 2004
;
Hermele et al. 2005
;
Hermele et al. 2008
, we must restrict ourselves to spin singlet bilinears to obtain regular representations. We omit the spin degrees of freedom since we assume that the phonons couple to both
\psi_{\uparrow}
and
\psi_{\downarrow}
bilinears equally. In similar fashion to the phonons, we can decompose the representation of all bilinears into irreducible representations,

G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}=\oplus_{j}D_{j}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}.

(43)

As
h^{(1)}_{\rm sp-ph}
includes terms that couple spinon momenta
\boldsymbol{k}
(transforming in the vector representation) and bilinears, we must also consider

E_{1}^{\rm sp}\otimes G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}=\oplus_{j}D_{j}^{\boldsymbol{k},\psi^{\dagger}\psi}.

(44)

We observe that
\mathcal{H}_{\rm sp-ph}
, which could possibly contain terms like

\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\left(D_{i}^{\rm ph}\otimes D_{j}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}+D_{i}^{\rm ph}\otimes D_{j}^{\boldsymbol{k},\psi^{\dagger}\psi}\right)

(45)

must be invariant under all symmetries. This is only possible if the representations are equal; that is,
D_{i}^{\rm ph}=D_{j}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
or
D_{i}^{\rm ph}=D_{j}^{\boldsymbol{k},\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
. Therefore, pairing together basis functions of equivalent irreducible representations between Eqs. (
41
) and (
43
) and (
44
) will give us all possible couplings in
h_{\rm sp-ph}
. Furthermore, the additional SU(2) symmetries of time-reversal and charge conjugation will impose further constraints on allowed couplings, as the phonon strain field
\partial_{i}u_{j}
is invariant under both symmetries.

Applying this formalism to our lattice symmetry group
C_{4v}
, the underlying symmetry group of the phonons, we have

\oplus_{j}D_{j}^{\rm ph}=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus B_{1}\oplus B_{2},

(46)

in Eq. (
41
), with basis elements
\partial_{x}u_{x}+\partial_{y}u_{y},

\partial_{x}u_{y}-\partial_{y}u_{x},

\partial_{x}u_{x}-\partial_{y}u_{y},
and
\partial_{x}u_{y}+\partial_{y}u_{x}
, respectively.
For the spinon sector, we can decompose the bilinears into representations of
C_{4v}^{\prime}
,

\displaystyle G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}

\displaystyle=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus\cdots,

(47)

\displaystyle E_{1}^{\rm sp}\otimes G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}

\displaystyle=A_{1}\oplus A_{2}\oplus B_{1}\oplus B_{2}\oplus\cdots,

(48)

where the ellipsis stands for irreducible representations of
C_{4v}^{\prime}
that transform nontrivially under lattice translations; these cannot be coupled to the phonons, which transform trivially under translations. The full results and explicit basis elements are tabulated in Section III of Ref.
Serbyn and Lee 2013
.

The ostensibly allowed couplings between the
(A_{1},A_{2})
components in
G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
and
E_{1}^{\rm ph}\otimes E_{1}^{\rm ph}
turn out to be forbidden by time-reversal symmetry, as the
(A_{1},A_{2})
components in
G_{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
,

D_{A_{1}}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}=\left\{\psi^{\dagger}\mathbb{I}\psi\right\},\quad D_{A_{2}}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}=\left\{\psi^{\dagger}\tau^{z}\psi\right\},

(49)

are both time-reversal odd. However, there is an allowed coupling to the
A_{2}
channel through the orbital current
t_{2}
. Since the orbital current
t_{2}
also transforms as
A_{2}
, by coupling
t_{2}\propto(m+M)
and
D_{A_{2}}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
together, we obtain a term that transforms trivially (as
A_{1}
). This term is permitted because
D_{A_{2}}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
, like
t_{2}
, is time-reversal odd, so the product
t_{2}\cdot D_{A_{2}}^{\psi^{\dagger}\psi}
can couple to the phonon density fluctuations. Therefore, we find

h_{\rm sp-ph}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{q})=ig_{0}\quantity(m+M)\tau^{z}\quantity(q^{}_xu^{}_x+q^{}_yu^{}_y),

(50)

with
g_{0}
labeling some phenomenological coupling coefficient.
Note that we cannot couple
m\tau^{z}
to phonons as
m
itself is not an irreducible representation (it includes the Néel order), but the combination
m+M=t_{2}
is irreducible.

The bilinears in Eq. (
48
) suffer no such restriction as they are all time-reversal and charge-conjugation invariant. The basis elements for the irreducible representations in Eq. (
48
) are analogous to those in Eq. (
46
), with the replacement
u_{i}\rightarrow\tau^{i}
. Now, we can couple each of the first four irreducible representations in Eq. (
48
) to its partner in Eq. (
46
). For example, the
A_{1}
spinor-bilinear component is of the form

\displaystyle D_{A_{2}}^{\boldsymbol{k}\psi^{\dagger}\psi}=\left\{\psi^{\dagger}(k_{x}\tau^{x}+k_{y}\tau^{y})\psi\right\},

(51)

so that the
A_{1}
-
A_{1}
coupling contribution to
{h}_{\rm sp-ph}
will be of the form
ig_{A_{1}}(q_{x}u_{x}+q_{y}u_{y})(k_{x}\tau^{x}+k_{y}\tau^{y})
for some coupling constant
g_{A_{1}}
.
After some simplification, the end result is

\displaystyle{h}^{(1)}_{\rm sp-ph}(\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q})=

\displaystyle i(

\displaystyle g_{1}q_{x}k_{x}\tau^{x}+g_{2}q_{y}k_{y}\tau^{x}+g_{3}q_{y}k_{x}\tau^{y}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle g_{4}q_{x}k_{y}\tau^{y})u_{x}+(x\leftrightarrow y),

(52)

for phenomenological couplings
g_{i}
. The
g_{i}
label combinations of irreducible representations, with
g_{1,4}=g_{A_{1}}\pm g_{B_{1}}
and
g_{2,3}=g_{B_{2}}\pm g_{A_{2}}
.

As in Eqs. (

(53a)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}^{x}

\displaystyle=i(g_{1}q_{x}k_{x}\tau^{x}+g_{2}q_{y}k_{y}\tau^{x}+g_{3}q_{y}k_{x}\tau^{y}+g_{4}q_{x}k_{y}\tau^{y}+g_{0}q_{x}(m+M)\tau^{z}),

(53b)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}^{y}

(53c)

The last term coming from the coupling of the orbital current
t_{2}=m+M

has no dependence on spinon momentum
\boldsymbol{k}

We can write the phonon self-energy, as in Fig.

\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}2\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\lambda^{y}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}G(\boldsymbol{k},i\omega_{n})\lambda^{x}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{q}}G(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{m})\right],

(54)

where
G(\boldsymbol{k},i\omega_{n})
denotes the Dirac fermion Green’s function,

G(k,\omega)=\dfrac{\omega\mathbb{I}+\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot\boldsymbol{\tau}}{\omega^{2}-\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}},\quad\mathbf{H}_{\boldsymbol{k}}\equiv(k_{x},k_{y},m).

(55)

In Eq. (
54
), we define
\int_{k,\omega_{n}}

\equiv

T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\int d^{2}\boldsymbol{k}/(2\pi)^{2}
, and we have also included a factor of
2
to account for the two species of Dirac fermions. The Hall viscosity originates from the off-diagonal, antisymmetric component of
\Pi^{xy}
. In real frequency, Eq. (
54
) contributes a term to the phonon effective action of the form

\delta S_{\rm{eff}}=\int_{\boldsymbol{q},\Omega}\Pi^{\mu\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\Omega)u_{\mu}(-\boldsymbol{q},-\Omega)u_{\nu}(\boldsymbol{q},\Omega)

(56)

from which we can extract

\displaystyle\eta^{H}

\displaystyle=\lim_{q\rightarrow 0}\lim_{\Omega\rightarrow 0}-\dfrac{1}{\Omega L^{d}}\partial^{2}_{\boldsymbol{q}}\text{Im}\left[\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},\Omega)\right]

(57)

In our continuum model, we did not consider any coupling to the rotational strain field, so
\eta^{M}=0
. Other terms in the phonon self-energy, such as the diagonal and symmetric components, will renormalize the real part of the phonon propagator. This is a small effect that does not contribute to phonon chirality, so we will not consider it here. As we are only interested in the leading-order contributions of
q
and
\Omega

\lambda^{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{q}}=\lambda^{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{q}}+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{2})

(58)

\displaystyle\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{q}^{2}(g_{1}g_{2}-g_{3}g_{4})\dfrac{m\Omega}{8\pi}\left(\Lambda-2|m|\right)+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}g_{0}(g_{2}-g_{3})(m+M)\dfrac{\Omega}{8\pi}\left(\Lambda-2|m|\right),

where
\Lambda
is a UV cutoff near the Dirac points. Then, Eq. (
57
) gives us

\displaystyle\eta^{H}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{1}{4\pi L^{2}}\left[(g_{1}g_{2}-g_{3}g_{4})m+g_{0}(g_{2}-g_{3})(m+M)\right](\Lambda-2|m|),

(59)

after analytic continuation to real
\Omega

\displaystyle\eta^{H}\sim-\quantity[g^{}_0(g^{}_2-g^{}_3)(m+M)]|m|\sim M|m|;

(60)

we see that the second derivative
\partial^{2}{\eta^{H}}/\partial^{2}{m}

\propto

\delta(m)

From Eq. (

V.4
Discussion and comparison to the lattice results

Our analysis highlights that the lattice symmetries strongly constrain the allowed spinon-phonon couplings. Therefore, though most spin-liquid phases of interest have similar Dirac excitations and effective theories in the continuum, the allowed spinon-phonon interactions and resulting Hall viscosity
\eta^{H}
in the continuum are sensitive to microscopic information about the phase.

We contrast our result with the quantized Hall viscosity found in Refs.
Vinkler-Aviv and Rosch 2018
;
Ye et al. 2020
for Majorana fermions in the gapless B phase of the Kitaev honeycomb model
Kitaev 2006
. This is a special feature of the lattice symmetry group
C_{6v}
, as in addition to a trivial density fluctuation coupling, the zero-flux phase
Basko 2008
;
Serbyn and Lee 2013
on a honeycomb lattice allows a spinon-phonon interaction in the
E_{2}
channel of the form

h_{\rm{sp-ph}}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{q})\sim\left[(q_{x}u_{x}-q_{y}u_{y})\tau^{x}-(q_{x}u_{y}+q_{y}u_{x})\tau^{y}\right]\mu^{z}

(61)

to zeroth order in the spinon momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
near the Dirac point, where
\boldsymbol{q}
is the phonon momentum. This additional coupling, in which the spinon momentum
\boldsymbol{k}
does not appear, should be understood as a consequence of the special symmetries of the honeycomb lattice. Integrating out the spinons on the honeycomb lattice then leads to a discontinuous Hall viscosity
Vinkler-Aviv and Rosch 2018
;
Ye et al. 2020
,

\eta^{H}\sim\mathrm{sign}(m),

(62)

that depends only on the sign of the Dirac mass
m
. Moreover, it was found in Ref.
Ye et al. 2020
that for the Kitaev spin liquid,
\eta^{H}
decreased as the magnitude of the time-reversal-symmetry-breaking perturbation increased.

In our analysis for the square lattice, we see that the nonanalytic behavior of the continuum
\eta^{H}
agrees with the lattice result, Eq. (
28
), at low energies and near the Dirac point. However, it should be noted that the continuum viscosity is in general regularization-dependent, and only the difference in
\eta^{H}
between two phases is universal
Hughes et al. 2011
;
Hughes et al. 2013
. With this in mind, we observe that the difference in
\eta^{H}
across the QPT scales, at leading order, linearly with the Dirac mass
m=2t_{2}-N/4
in both the lattice and continuum formulations. This differs from the Hall viscosity obtained for the Dirac Chern insulator on a square lattice
Shapourian et al. 2015
in which case, the Hall viscosity of the tight-binding Hamiltonian scales quadratically with the Dirac mass. For the continuum Dirac field theory of the Chern insulator, introducing suitable Pauli-Villars regulators and counterterms eliminates the dependence of the viscosity on the UV cutoff
\Lambda
and also leads to a quadratic dependence of
\eta^{H}
on
m

Hughes et al. 2013
.

In our chiral spin liquid ansatz in Eq. (
13
), the phonons are coupled directly to the orbital current
t_{2}
but not the effective Dirac mass
m=2t_{2}-N/4
. This is caused by the presence of Néel order, which does not couple to lattice distortions as it is purely an on-site term [see Eq. (
13
)]. In the limit
N\rightarrow 0
, the two Dirac masses coincide and for our calculation on the lattice,
\eta^{H}\sim M|m|=m|m|
, as in the case of the Chern insulator. In the continuum, we reproduce the field theory of the Chern insulator as in Refs.
Hughes et al. 2011
;
Hughes et al. 2013
, and with further regularization, the same scaling is obtained for the Hall viscosity. In both the lattice and the continuum, the divergence in
\eta^{H}
is then only visible in its third derivative with respect to
m
.

VI
Physical Consequences

For acoustic phonons, the dispersion is assumed to be
\omega_{\rm{ph}}\propto|\boldsymbol{q}|+\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{2})
, so, according to Eq. (
4
), the Hall viscosity’s contribution to the phonon effective action is of order
\partial u\partial\dot{u}\sim|\boldsymbol{q}|^{3}u^{2}
. This is more relevant than the leading anharmonic correction, which is of order
\boldsymbol{q}^{4}
. Note that the other possible
\mathcal{O}(\boldsymbol{q}^{3})
contribution to the phonon action

\int d^{2}x\,dt\;D_{ijklm}\partial_{i}\partial_{j}u_{k}\partial_{l}u_{m}

(65)

vanishes in the presence of inversion symmetry. In two-dimensional isotropic systems, it was found that the Hall viscosity mixes the longitudinal and transverse modes and renormalizes the phonon spectrum
Barkeshli et al. 2012
;
Ye et al. 2020
,

\Delta\omega_{\rm{ph}}\sim\eta^{2}q^{3}.

(66)

However, the exact numerical prefactor of the correction, estimated to be very small by
Barkeshli et al. 2012
, requires knowledge of the energies associated with the appropriate spin-lattice couplings, and the phonon spectrum cannot distinguish the sign of the Hall viscosity. Another consequence of a Hall viscosity is phonon Faraday rotation, which describes the rotation of the linear polarization vector of transverse acoustic phonons due to splitting in the circularly-polarized velocities
Wang and Crow 1971
;
Sytcheva et al. 2010
;
Shapourian et al. 2015
.

Recently, the thermal Hall effect has emerged as a powerful probe of neutral excitations such as spinons, prompting extensive experimental and theoretical studies in a variety of correlated quantum materials, including the cuprate superconductors
Grissonnanche et al. 2019
;
Grissonnanche et al. 2020
;
Samajdar et al. 2019b
;
Han et al. 2019
;
Samajdar et al. 2019a
;
Li and Lee 2019
;
Chen et al. 2020
;
Guo et al. 2020

and Kitaev materials like
\alpha
-RuCl
3

Kasahara et al. 2018a
;
Kasahara et al. 2018b
;
Hentrich et al. 2019
;
Balz et al. 2019
;
Yokoi et al. 2020
;
Yamashita et al. 2020
;
Ye et al. 2018
;
Vinkler-Aviv and Rosch 2018
;
Cookmeyer and Moore 2018
;
Go et al. 2020
;
Teng et al. 2020
.
Here, we observe that a phonon Hall viscosity, in general, implies a nonzero phonon thermal Hall conductivity by imparting a Berry curvature to the phonon energy bands. Moreover, their relative signs can be determined given the coupling constants. As previously studied, a phonon thermal Hall response can arise from a coupling of phonons to the magnetization of the system
Zhang et al. 2010
. More recently, in ferroelectric insulators
Chen et al. 2020
, the flexoelectric coupling of acoustic phonons to the dipole density was shown to lead to a thermal Hall response. In our case, the Hall viscosity appears in the phonon effective action as a term analogous to those flexoelectric couplings. For example, consider isotropic phonons in two dimensions,

\displaystyle S_{\rm ph}=\dfrac{1}{2}\int d^{2}x\,dt\;\rho\dot{\boldsymbol{u}}^{2}+\mu_{1}\gradient\boldsymbol{u}^{2}+\mu_{2}(\gradient\cdot\boldsymbol{u})^{2},

(67)

with mass density
\rho
and elastic constants
\mu_{i}
.
Assuming the Hall viscosity in Eq. (
7
), the thermal Hall conductivity
Qin et al. 2012
, in the low-temperature limit, reads

\displaystyle\kappa_{xy}^{2d}(T)

\displaystyle=-\eta\dfrac{3\zeta(3)k_{B}^{3}}{\pi\hbar^{2}}\left[\frac{g_{1}}{\sqrt{\mu_{1}}}-\frac{g_{2}}{\sqrt{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}}\right]T^{2},

(68)

\displaystyle g_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{4\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{2\sqrt{\mu_{1}}\mu_{2}};\quad g_{2}\equiv\frac{4\mu_{1}+3\mu_{2}}{2\mu_{2}\sqrt{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}}.

Hence, given that
\eta
plateaus at low temperature from our analyses in Secs.
IV.2
and
V.4
, we see that
\kappa_{xy}^{2d}/T\propto T
as
T\rightarrow 0
(note that we expect that
\kappa_{xy}^{3d}/T\propto T^{2}
at low temperature
Qin et al. 2012
). While we are unable to make quantitative estimates of the strength of this response, we have demonstrated that this effect generically exists for both the conventional Néel phase and the Néel state coexisting with semion topological order in Fig.
2
, providing an intrinsic source of phonon chirality. With an eye towards recent experiments on the phonon thermal Hall response in cuprates
Grissonnanche et al. 2020
;
Boulanger et al. 2020
, our proposal lays the foundation for work on possible enhancement of heat transport due to extrinsic mechanisms in these topological systems
Guo and Sachdev 2021
.

VII
Conclusion and Outlook

In this paper, we have analyzed the phonon Hall viscosity arising from the coupling to spin degrees of freedom on the square lattice in a magnetic field.

We employed a fermionic spinon formulation and obtained a low-energy effective action for the phonon fields by integrating out the spinons. Two complementary approaches were studied: first, starting from the lattice spinon model of Ref.
Samajdar et al. 2019a
, we introduced the coupling to lattice vibrations using the physical model of bond stretching (or equivalently, adiabatic response). In the second approach, only the relevant low-energy spinon degrees of freedom were retained, and the resulting continuum Dirac theory was coupled to lattice vibrations purely by symmetry considerations.

Even in the continuum limit, microscopic details about the lattice symmetry were shown to have drastic effects on the critical behavior of
\eta^{H}
: as opposed to the discontinuity of
\eta^{H}
when changing the sign of the effective Dirac mass
m
at the transition on the honeycomb lattice, we demonstrated that the symmetries of the square lattice lead to a Hall viscosity that varies linearly with the effective Dirac mass
m
, in both the continuum and the lattice theory. We also calculated
\eta^{H}
at finite temperature and determined a scaling form for the ratio
\eta^{H}/(TM)
.

The Hall viscosity is a measure of time-reversal symmetry breaking in the spinon sector, and its nonanalyticities can serve as signatures of the field-driven topological quantum phase transition.
We found that the second derivative of the Hall viscosity with respect to the mass,
\partial_{m}^{2}\eta^{H}
, diverges at the transition (
m=0
) between the two phases in Fig.
2
at zero temperature. This leads to a kink in the field dependence of
\eta^{H}
[see Figs.
5
(a) and
5
(c)]. We showed that this enhanced singular behavior—as compared to the square-lattice Chern insulator where
\partial_{m}^{3}\eta^{H}
diverges
Shapourian et al. 2015
, can be traced back to the presence of Néel order.

In addition to previous studies which have proposed measuring the Hall viscosity through various phononic properties of the material, we have shown how the Hall viscosity also leads to an intrinsic thermal Hall response. This response can potentially be enhanced by extrinsic scattering mechanisms and may be detectable in experiments which indicate that phonons are the dominant contribution to heat transport. As more and more experiments probe the exotic nature of topological phases and possible spin liquid candidates, we believe that the phonon Hall viscosity can be a powerful tool for detecting fractionalization and quantum critical phenomena.

Finally, we note that our computations were carried out in the setting of a spinon mean-field theory. Gauge fluctuations can potentially change the spinon phase diagram as well as renormalize the spinon-phonon couplings
Guo et al. 2020
. However, we expect the nonanalytic signature of a quantum phase transition to persist. The exact effects of gauge-field fluctuations on phonon dynamics remain an open question, and their consequences are avenues for further study.

Appendix A
Hall viscosity in the absence of a Zeeman field

To evaluate the effective action, we have to compute (using
\Tr[\tau^{\alpha}\tau^{\beta}\tau^{\delta}]=2i\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}

\Tr\quantity[\tau^\beta G (\vec{k},i\omega^{}_n)\tau^\alpha G (\vec{k},i(\omega^{}_n+\Omega^{}_m))]=\sum_{\pm}\dfrac{2\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}\Omega_{m}\mathbf{H}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\delta}}{(\mathbf{H}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2})(\mathbf{H}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+(\omega_{n}+\Omega_{m})^{2})}=\sum_{\pm}\dfrac{2\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\delta}\Omega_{m}\mathbf{H}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}^{\delta}}{(\mathbf{H}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2})^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(\Omega_{m}^{2}),

(69)

where we have only kept the terms antisymmetric and linear in
\Omega
since these are the only ones that will contribute to the Hall viscosity.
This approximation is valid because we are only extracting the first-order (in
\Omega
) contribution to the effective action. The Matsubara summation yields

\displaystyle T\sum_{\omega_{n}}\dfrac{1}{(\mathbf{H}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2})^{2}}=\dfrac{1-2n_{F}(\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}})+2\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}}n_{F}^{\prime}(\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}})}{4\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}}^{3}}.

(70)

Therefore, we can extract from

\displaystyle S_{\text{eff}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2L^{2}\beta^{2}}\sum_{\omega_{n},\Omega_{m},\boldsymbol{k}}\Tr\quantity[K^{}_{\vec{k},i\Omega_m}G (\vec{k},i\omega^{}_n)K^{}_{\vec{k},-i\Omega_m}G (\vec{k},i(\omega^{}_n+\Omega^{}_m))]

(71)

the Hall viscosity

Writing out the summation over

Appendix B
Rotational strain field coupling

The rotational strain field
\theta_{ij}=\quantity(\partial_i u_j - \partial_j u_i)/2
, representing a vorticity, is ordinarily not considered in geometric treatments of phonon interactions. However, given the symmetries of our ansatz, we can couple the time derivative of
\partial{\theta_{ij}}/\partial{t}
to the orbital current
t_{2}

Scheurer and Sachdev 2018
;
Sen and Chitra 1995

\displaystyle|t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}\pm(\boldsymbol{x}+\boldsymbol{y})}|=|t_{\boldsymbol{n},\boldsymbol{n}\pm(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y})}|\simeq t_{2}+\lambda_{3}\partialderivative{\theta^{}_{xy}}{t}.

(73)

Since the rotational strain field has only one polarization

23

,

\displaystyle\tilde{\gamma}_{\mu\nu}^{z}(\Omega)\tau^{z}=4\Omega\lambda_{3}\sin(k^{}_x)\cos(k^{}_y)\tau^{z},\quad\mu\nu=xy.

(74)

This additional coupling is interesting to consider since it can, in principle, lead to a finite Hall viscosity. Following the same procedure for calculating
\eta^{H}
, we state the analytic answer for

below:

\displaystyle\eta^{M}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\pm}\tilde{\gamma}_{xy}^{z}\Bigg[\frac{2\mathbf{H}^{x}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}\mathbf{H}^{z}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{xx}^{x}+\quantity(-(\mathbf{H}^x_{\pm,\vec{k}})^2 - (\mathbf{H}^y_{\pm,\vec{k}})^2 + (\mathbf{H}^z_{\pm,\vec{k}})^2)\gamma_{xx}^{z}}{4\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}}^{3}}\quantity[1-2n^{}_F(\abs{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}})+2\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}n_F'(\abs{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}})]

\displaystyle+\frac{\gamma_{xx}^{z}}{4\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm, \vec{k}}}}\quantity[-1+2n^{}_F(\abs{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}})+2\abs{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}}n_F'(\abs{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm,\vec{k}}})]\Bigg].

(75)

This expression simplifies at
T=0
to
\eta^{M}=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{L^{2}}\sum_{\boldsymbol{k},\pm}2\tilde{\gamma}_{xy}^{z}\frac{\mathbf{H}^{x}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}\mathbf{H}^{z}_{\pm,\boldsymbol{k}}\gamma_{xx}^{x}-\quantity((\mathbf{H}^x_{\pm,\vec{k}})^2 + (\mathbf{H}^y_{\pm,\vec{k}})^2 )\gamma_{xx}^{z}}{\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{\pm, \vec{k}}}^{3}}.

(76)

At the critical point, where
m_{1}=0

\boldsymbol{q}
near the Dirac point at
\mathbf{Q}=\quantity(\frac{\pi}{2}, 0)

\displaystyle\evaluated{\eta^M}_{m_1 = 0}\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\frac{m_{2}^{2}}{\absolutevalue{\vec{q}}}.

(77)

Although the above term seems to have a singularity, it is remedied by the integration measure

. Therefore,
\eta^{M}
goes to a finite value as
\boldsymbol{q}\rightarrow 0

\eta^{H}

\eta^{M}

\delta

\displaystyle\evaluated{\pdv[2]{\eta^M}{m_1}}_{m_1 = 0}\sim\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\partialderivative[2]{m_1}\frac{q^{2}}{\absolutevalue{\mathbf{H}^{}_{+,\vec{k}}}^{3}}\sim\partial_{m_{1}}^{2}|m_{1}|\xrightarrow[m_{1}\rightarrow 0]{}\infty,

Hamiltonian and projective symmetry of the chiral
\pi
-flux state

From the couplings given by Eqs. (
33

34a

34b

\displaystyle+it_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}0&0&\tilde{K}_{A}&0\\

For the equations above, we have defined

\displaystyle K_{1,2}

\displaystyle\equiv e^{i\boldsymbol{k}\cdot\boldsymbol{a}_{1,2}},

(92)

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{A}

\displaystyle\equiv-1-K_{1}^{*}-K_{2}^{*}-K_{1}^{*}K_{2}^{*},

(93)

\displaystyle\tilde{K}_{B}

\displaystyle\equiv 1+K_{1}+K_{2}^{*}+K_{1}K_{2}^{*}.

(94)

Expanding the Hamiltonian

around the Dirac points at
\Gamma

36

To specify the projective symmetry of the continuous spinor fields
\psi_{\alpha\sigma}
defined in Eq. (
35
), we need to know how the spinons transform under the relevant symmetry group generators. Following closely the analyses of Ref.
Serbyn and Lee 2013
, we begin by specifying the projective action of the symmetry operations on the lattice fermions. The symmetries of the
\pi
-flux ansatz, as in Sec.
V
, are generated by translation by
a\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
,
T_{x}:\boldsymbol{r}\rightarrow T_{x}\boldsymbol{r}=\quantity(r_x + a, r_y)
; reflection about the
\hat{\boldsymbol{x}}
-axis,
R_{x}

:

\boldsymbol{r}

\rightarrow

R_{x}\boldsymbol{r}

=

\quantity(-r_x, r_y)
; and rotation by
\pi/2
,
C_{4}

:

\boldsymbol{r}

\rightarrow

C_{4}\boldsymbol{r}

=

\quantity(r_y, -r_x)
; together, these make up the symmetry group
C_{4v}^{\prime}
. Furthermore, there are two additional SU(2) symmetries of our ansatz, given (in momentum space) by time-reversal
\mathcal{T}:f_{\boldsymbol{k}i\sigma}\rightarrow f_{\boldsymbol{k}i\sigma}^{\dagger}
and charge-conjugation symmetry,
\mathcal{C}:f_{\boldsymbol{k}i\sigma}\rightarrow f_{-\boldsymbol{k}i-\sigma}^{\dagger}
. Note that
\mathcal{T}
is also accompanied by complex conjugation. While
\mathcal{T}
flips the spin operator
\boldsymbol{S}_{i}=\frac{1}{2}f_{i}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\sigma}f_{i}
,
\mathcal{C}
leaves it invariant. To leave the Hamiltonian invariant under these symmetry operations, we may need to supplement the symmetries with additional gauge transformations; hence, the symmetry is implemented
projectively
. For a U(1)-symmetric ansatz, the gauge factors can be conveniently chosen to be
\pm 1
. For example,
T_{x}
is implemented as

\displaystyle T_{x}\;:\;

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{r},1}\rightarrow-f_{T_{x}^{-1}\boldsymbol{r},2},\quad f_{\boldsymbol{r},4}\rightarrow f_{T_{x}^{-1}\boldsymbol{r},3}

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{r},2}\rightarrow-f_{T_{x}\boldsymbol{r},1},\quad f_{\boldsymbol{r},3}\rightarrow f_{T_{x}\boldsymbol{r},4}

(95)

and the other transformations can be found similarly:

\displaystyle R_{x}\;:\;

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{r},1,3}\rightarrow f_{R_{x}\boldsymbol{r},2,4},\quad f_{\boldsymbol{r},2,4}\rightarrow-f_{R_{x}\boldsymbol{r},1,3}

(96a)

\displaystyle C_{4}\;:\;

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{r},1}\rightarrow-f_{C_{4}\boldsymbol{r},2},\quad f_{\boldsymbol{r},2,3,4}\rightarrow f_{C_{4}\boldsymbol{r},3,4,1}

(96b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}\;:\;

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{k}1,3}\rightarrow f_{\boldsymbol{k}1,3}^{\dagger},\quad f_{\boldsymbol{k}2,4}\rightarrow f_{\boldsymbol{k}2,4}^{\dagger}

(96c)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}\;:\;

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{k}n\uparrow}\rightarrow f_{-\boldsymbol{k}n\downarrow}^{\dagger},\quad f_{\boldsymbol{k}n\downarrow}\rightarrow-f_{-\boldsymbol{k}n\uparrow}^{\dagger}

(96d)

From the form of Eq. (
35
), we can now deduce the action of a symmetry generators on the continuous fields:

\displaystyle T_{x}

\displaystyle=\mu^{y},

(97a)

\displaystyle R_{x}

\displaystyle=i\mu^{z}\tau^{y},

(97b)

\displaystyle C_{4}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\quantity(\mu^x + \mu^y)(1+i\tau^{z}).

(97c)

For example, under
T_{x}
, we have
\psi\rightarrow T_{x}\psi
.
Likewise, for time-reversal and charge-conjugation symmetries, we find

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle:\psi\rightarrow-\mu^{z}\tau^{z}(\psi^{\dagger})^{T},

(98a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}

\displaystyle:\psi\rightarrow\sigma^{y}\mu^{x}\tau^{x}(\psi^{\dagger})^{T}.

(98b)

With the symmetries now defined, we can determine how the fermion bilinnears split into irreducible representations. Further details, including background on representation theory and the structure of
C_{4v}^{\prime}
, can be found in Ref.
Serbyn and Lee 2013

Appendix D
Continuum phonon self-energy

In order to find

\displaystyle\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}2\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\lambda^{y}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}G(\boldsymbol{k},i\omega_{n})\lambda^{x}_{\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},-\boldsymbol{q}}G(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{m})\right]

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}2\cdot\mathrm{Tr}\left[\lambda^{y}_{\boldsymbol{k},\boldsymbol{q}}G(\boldsymbol{k},i\omega_{n})\lambda^{x}_{\boldsymbol{k},-\boldsymbol{q}}G(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{m})\right]+\mathcal{O}(q^{3}),

(99)

it will be convenient to first define

\widetilde{\Pi}^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta\cdots}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})=\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\mathrm{Tr}\left[(k_{\gamma}k_{\delta}\cdots)\tau^{\alpha}G(\boldsymbol{k},i\omega_{n})\tau^{\beta}G(\boldsymbol{k}+\boldsymbol{q},i\omega_{n}+i\Omega_{m})\right]

(100)

because
\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})
is a linear combination of terms of the form
q^{2}\widetilde{\Pi}
. As with the vertex in Eq. (
58
), we can make simplifications based on the fact that we are working in the linear-response regime. We only consider terms of order
\Omega_{m}
and
\boldsymbol{q}^{2}
in
\Pi^{xy}
for the Hall viscosity, so we just have to keep terms of order
\mathcal{O}(\Omega_{m}^{1})

\mathcal{O}(q^{0})

\widetilde{\Pi}
.
We can also observe that of the 25 possible contractions of terms between
\lambda^{y}
and

\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})=\boldsymbol{q}^{2}(g_{1}g_{2}-g_{3}g_{4})\widetilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}-\boldsymbol{q}^{2}g_{0}(g_{2}-g_{3})(m+M)\widetilde{\Pi}^{31}_{y}.

Here, we used
\widetilde{\Pi}^{\alpha\beta}_{xx}=\widetilde{\Pi}^{\alpha\beta}_{yy}

\Tr[\tau^{\alpha}\tau^{\beta}\tau^{\gamma}]=2i\varepsilon^{\alpha\beta\gamma}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\;\dfrac{2m\Omega_{m}k_{x}^{2}}{(\omega_{n}^{2}+k^{2}+m^{2})\quantity[(\omega^{}_n+\Omega^{}_m)^2+(\vec{k}+\vec{q})^2+m^2]}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}du\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\;\dfrac{2m\Omega_{m}k_{x}^{2}}{[u(\omega_{n}+\Omega_{m})^{2}+(1-u)\omega_{n}^{2}+k^{2}+u(1-u)q^{2}+m^{2}]^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(q^{2}),

(103)

where we have introduced Feynman parameters in the second line and shifted
\boldsymbol{k}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{k}-u\boldsymbol{q}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Pi}^{31}_{y}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}du\int_{\boldsymbol{k},\omega_{n}}\;\dfrac{2\Omega_{m}k_{y}^{2}}{[u(\omega_{n}+\Omega_{m})^{2}+(1-u)\omega_{n}^{2}+k^{2}+u(1-u)q^{2}+m^{2}]^{2}}=-\dfrac{\widetilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})}{m}.

(104)

Continuing, we define
\Delta(u)=u(\omega_{n}+\Omega_{m})^{2}+(1-u)\omega_{n}^{2}+u(1-u)q^{2}+m^{2}

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=2m\Omega_{m}\int_{0}^{1}du\int_{\omega_{n}}\dfrac{dk_{x}dk_{y}}{4\pi^{2}}\;\dfrac{k_{x}^{2}}{[k^{2}+\Delta]^{2}}.

(105)

Imposing a UV cutoff
\Lambda
and taking the limit

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\dfrac{m\Omega_{m}}{8\pi}\int_{0}^{1}du\;\left(\Lambda-2\sqrt{u(1-u)q^{2}+m^{2}}\right)+\mathcal{O}(\Omega_{m}^{2}).

Now taking the

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})=\dfrac{m\Omega_{m}}{8\pi}\left(\Lambda-2|m|\right)

so that, using Eq. (

\displaystyle\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{q}^{2}(g_{1}g_{2}-g_{3}g_{4})\dfrac{m\Omega_{m}}{8\pi}\left(\Lambda-2|m|\right)+\boldsymbol{q}^{2}g_{0}(g_{2}-g_{3})(m+M)\dfrac{\Omega_{m}}{8\pi}\left(\Lambda-2|m|\right)

\displaystyle\implies\eta^{H}

Now, to obtain the finite-temperature result, we go back to Eq. (

\displaystyle\tilde{\Pi}^{21}_{xx}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=2m\Omega_{m}\int_{0}^{1}du\int_{\omega_{n}}\dfrac{dk_{x}dk_{y}}{4\pi^{2}}\;\dfrac{k_{x}^{2}}{[k^{2}+\Delta]^{2}}

\displaystyle\Pi^{xy}(\boldsymbol{q},i\Omega_{m})

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{q}^{2}\left[(g_{1}g_{2}-g_{3}g_{4})m+g_{0}(g_{2}-g_{3})(m+M)\right]\dfrac{\Omega_{m}}{8\pi}\cdot\left(D_{\Lambda}(m,T)-2|m|-4T\log\left(1+e^{-|m|/T}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\eta^{H}

\displaystyle\sim|m|+2T\log\left({1+e^{-|m|/T}}\right)

\displaystyle=|m|+2T\log(2e^{-|m|/2T}\cosh(|m|/2T))

\displaystyle=2T\log(2\cosh(|m|/2T)),

which leads to Eq. (

From Eq. (

and

\displaystyle g_{1}g_{2}-g_{3}g_{4}

\displaystyle\propto g_{A_{1}}g_{A_{2}}+g_{B_{1}}g_{B_{2}},

\displaystyle g_{0}(g_{2}-g_{3})

\displaystyle\propto g_{A_{1}}g_{A_{2}},

transform under the

to transform trivially under all symmetries (under

) as the phonon effective action must be invariant under all symmetries. This still holds true because

can only exist in the presence of broken reflection symmetry, in which case, the symmetry of the phonon action is reduced

. Since the phonon field transforms under the vector representation

correspond to the component(s) of

within
\bigwedge^{2}\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(E_{1})
(with
\mathrm{Sym}^{2}
and
\bigwedge^{2}
denoting the symmetrized and antisymmetrized tensor product, respectively). In our ansatz, we can illustrate this algebraically as

{\textstyle\bigwedge}^{2}\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(E_{1})={\textstyle\bigwedge}^{2}(A_{1}\oplus B_{1}\oplus B_{2})=A_{2}^{a}\oplus B_{1}^{a}\oplus B_{2}^{a},

(120)

in
C_{4v}
, which descends to
A_{1}^{a}\oplus 2B_{1}^{a}
in
C_{4}
, so the Hall viscosity has one component. This procedure can also be carried out for other lattices. For example, as was shown for phonons with

symmetry

{\textstyle\bigwedge}^{2}\mathrm{Sym}^{2}(E_{1})={\textstyle\bigwedge}^{2}(A_{1}\oplus E_{2})=A_{2}^{a}\oplus E_{2}^{a},

(121)

which descends to
A_{1}^{a}\oplus E_{2}^{a}
in
C_{6}
, giving one independent component of
\eta^{H}
. As

originated from

, we know that
\eta^{H}
must scale as

.
