Abstract




Finiteness of quantum gravity with matter on a PL spacetime

E-mail: amikovic@ulusofona.pt

We study the convergence of the path integral for General Relativity with matter on a picewise linear (PL) spacetime that corresponds to a triangulation of a smooth manifold by using a path-integral measure that renders the pure gravity path integral finite. This measure depends on a parameter
p
, and in the case when the matter content is just scalar fields, we show that the path integral is absolutely convergent for
p>0,5
and not more than 2 scalar fields. In the case of Yang-Mills fields, we show that the path integral is absolutely convergent for the
U(1)
group and
p>0,5
. In the case of Dirac fermions, we show that the path integral is absolutely convergent for any number of fermions and a sufficiently large
p
. When the matter content is given by scalars, Yang-Mills fields and fermions, as in the case of the Standard Model, we show that the path integral is absolutely convergent for
p>46,5
. Hence one can construct a finite quantum gravity theory on a PL spacetime such that the classical limit is General Relativity coupled to the Standard Model.

1
Introduction

A minimalist version of the problem of constructing a quantum gravity (QG) theory would be the following: given a 4-dimensional spacetime
M
of the topology
\Sigma\times I
, where
\Sigma
is a smooth 3-manifold and
I
an interval from
{\bf R}
, construct a quantum theory such that its classical limit is given by the General Relativity (GR) coupled to the Standard Model (SM) on
M
, see
[
1
]
. The simplest approach is just applying the quantum field theory (QFT) formalism to GR plus the SM, but as it is well known, such a QFT theory is perturbatively non-renormalizable, see
[
2
,
3
]
, and hence non-physical, because it requires a knowledge of infintely many coupling constants. A formalism of non-perturbative renormalisability, called asymptotic safety, has been proposed by Weinberg in 1977, which could be applied to gravity
[
4
,
5
]
, so that only finitely many coupling constants will be physical. It is a promissing idea to solve the problem, since it works in some special cases
[
6
]
, but the main drawback is that there is no proof for the general case.

The most celebrated proposal for a QG theory so far is the string theory,
[
7
,
8
]
, which circumvents the non-renormalizability of GR by constructing a finite quantum pertubation theory. However, this is achieved by introducing extra spatial dimensions and many more fields than what we have in GR and SM, which is the main drawback of string theory since a solution which corresponds to the SM at low energies still has not been found.

Another notable proposal is Loop Quantum Gravity (LQG), see
[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. LQG has two formulations: the canonical quantization formulation
[
10
]
and the path integral (PI) formulation
[
9
,
11
]
. In the canonical quantization formulation, the main problem is solving the Wheeler-de Witt equation and obtaining solutions that have a semi-classical (SC) expansion which would correspond to the perturbative QFT expansion. In the path integral formulation, which is known as the spin foam (SF) models, one must use a piecewise linear (PL) spacetime
T(M)
which corresponds to a triangulation of
M
in order to define a SF model. Although a SF state sum can be made finite by an appropriate choice of the weights
[
12
]
, the main problem is that it is not known how to define the smooth-manifold limit of a spin foam model. Another major problem is that it is very difficult to couple fermions in a SF model, because a fermion on
T(M)
couples to gravity via vierbeins while in a SF model one only has the 2-forms which correspond to the wedge products of the vierbein 1-forms.

Note that a SF model represents a generalization of the Regge path integral, and also note that there is no problem in coupling fermions in the Regge calculus, see
[
13
,
19
]
. In the Regge approach to QG, see
[
13
]
, there is also the problem of the smooth-manifold limit, but there is a promissing idea to solve it. One can use the Wilsonian approach, i.e. to look for the critical points of the Regge path integral as a function of the GR couplings (the Newton constant
G_{N}
and the cosmological constant), see
[
13
]
. Near a critical point, the correlation length becomes very large, so that the effective theory becomes a field theory on a smooth manifold. However, the QFT perturbative expansion corresponds to small
G_{N}
values, and this requires a nonperturbative evaluation of the path integral. Such a nonperturbative evaluation of the path integral would be a way to prove the main assumption of the asymptotic safety approach, but so far one can only do the numerical simulations, see
[
14
]
.

A promissing new framework, which is based on the Regge calculus, was proposed in
[
15
]
, see also
[
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. The idea is to use the triangulations with a large number of simplicial cells, but without taking the smooth limit, since it is assumed that the short-distance structure of the spacetime is given by the PL manifold
T(M)
. Then the PL manifold appears to be smooth at the distances much larger than the average edge length, and this can be clearly seen by using the effective action formalism from QFT. Another novelty with respect to the standard quantum Regge calculus, is utilization of a non-trivial path integral measure, whose form is constrained by the requirements of the finitenness of the path integral and the existence of the correct classical limit for the effective action, see
[
15
,
18
]
.

One can then choose a simple form of the measure, see eq. (
8
),
which depends on a length parameter
L_{0}
and a dimensionless parameter
p
, such that
p>1/2
insures the absolute convergence of the GR path integral
[
18
]
. However, there has been no investigation of the finitennes of the PLQG path integral when matter is included, except in the case of a minisuperspace model
[
20
]
. In this paper we are going to explore the convergence of the path integral of GR coupled to scalar, Yang-Mills and spinor fields on
T(M)
with the measure (
8
). In section 2 we briefly describe the PLQG formalism for GR. In section 3 we explore the case when one couples the scalar fields, while in section 4 we study the case of Yang-Mills fields. In section 5 we study the case of Dirac spinors, while in section 6 we investigate the convergence of the PLQG path integral when we have the matter fields of the Standard Model. In section 7 we present our conclussions.

GR path integral

Let
M
be a smooth 4-manifold and
\Sigma
a three-dimensional submanifold. We will restrict the topology of
M
in the following way: when
M
has no
boundary,
M
is equivalent to the result of gluing two 4-manifolds
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
to the boundaries of the manifold
\Sigma\times I
, where
I
is an interval from
{\bf R}
. Therefore

M=M_{1}\cup\left(\Sigma\times I\right)\cup M_{2}=M_{1}\cup M_{12}\cup M_{2}\,,

such that
\partial M_{1}=\partial M_{2}=\Sigma
.

The topology of
M_{1}
and
M_{2}
can be arbitrary, but in the context of quantum cosmology, when
M
has a single boundary, then

M=M_{1}
or
M=M_{1}\cup M_{12}
. In this case one can choose
M_{1}
to have the simplest possible topology. For example, when
\Sigma=S^{3}
, one can take
M_{1}=S^{4}
, see
[
20
]
. In the case when we are interested in the effective action, we take
M=M_{12}=\Sigma\times I
, see
[
17
]
.

Let
T(M)
be a PL manifold corresponding to a regular

triangulation of
M
. A metric on
T(M)
is given by a set of edge lengths
L_{\epsilon}
such that
L_{\epsilon}^{2}\in{\bf R}
.
We can then choose
L_{\epsilon}\in{\bf R}_{+}
or
L_{\epsilon}\in i{\bf R}_{+}
, which corresponds to having a spacelike or a timelike distance between the vertices of
T(M)
.

We also require that
|L_{\epsilon}|>0
for a compact
M
, i.e. we exclude the light-like edges and the zero-vector edges. For a noncompact
M
, we take
|L_{\epsilon}|>0
for the edges in a
T(B_{3}\times I)\subset T(\Sigma\times I)
, where
B_{3}
is a 3-dimensional ball in
\Sigma
. For the edges outside of
T(B_{3}\times I)
we take
L_{\epsilon}=0
as zero vectors. In the case of
M_{1}
or
M_{2}
, we take
|L_{\epsilon}|>0
for the edges in a
T(B_{4})\subset T(M_{i})
.

Given a set of the edge lengths, one can define a metric on the PL manifold
T(M)
such that the PL metric in each 4-simplex
\sigma
of
T(M)
is flat. This can be done by using the Cayley-Menger (CM) metric, see
[
13
,
19
]

G_{\mu\nu}(\sigma)=L_{0\mu}^{2}+L_{0\nu}^{2}-L_{\mu\nu}^{2}\,,

(1)

where the five vertices of
\sigma
are labeled as
0,1,2,3,4
and
\mu,\nu=1,2,3,4
.

Although the CM metric is flat in a four-simplex, it is not dimensionless and hence it is not diffeomorphic to

g_{\mu\nu}=diag(\pm 1,\pm 1,\pm 1,\pm 1)\,.

This can be remedied by defining a new PL metric
[
16
]

g_{\mu\nu}(\sigma)={G_{\mu\nu}(\sigma)\over|L_{0\mu}||L_{0\nu}|}\,.

(2)

In order to insure that we are dealing with a PL Lorentzian manifold, one has to choose the edge lengths such that
g_{\mu\nu}
is difeomorphic in each
\sigma
to

\eta_{\mu\nu}=diag(-1,1,1,1)\,.

This can be done by embedding
T(M)
into
{\bf R}^{5}
with a 5-dimensional Minkowski metric, see
[
20
]
.

The Einstein-Hilbert (EH) action on
M
is given by

S_{EH}=\int_{M}\sqrt{|\textrm{det}g|}\,R(g)\,d^{4}x\,,

(3)

where
R(g)
is the scalar curvature associated to a metric
g
. On
T(M)
the EH action becomes the Regge action

S_{R}(L)=\sum_{\Delta\in T(M)}A_{\Delta}(L)\,\delta_{\Delta}(L)\,,

(4)

when the edge lengths correspond to a Eucledean PL geometry. In (
4
),
A_{\Delta}
denotes the area of a triangle
\Delta
, while the deficit angle
\delta_{\Delta}
is given by

\delta_{\Delta}=2\pi-\sum_{\sigma\supset\Delta}\theta_{\Delta}^{(\sigma)}\,,

(5)

where a dihedral angle
\theta_{\Delta}^{(\sigma)}
is defined as the angle between the 4-vector normals associated to the two tetrahedrons that share the triangle
\Delta
.

In the case of a Lorentzian geometry, a dihedral angle can take complex values, so that it is necessary to modify the formula (
4
) such that the Regge action takes only the real values. This can be seen from the formula

\sin\theta_{\Delta}^{(\sigma)}=\frac{4}{3}{v_{\Delta}v_{\sigma}\over v_{\tau}v_{\tau^{\prime}}}\,,

(6)

where
v_{s}
is the volume
V_{s}
of the simplex
s
(
V_{s}\geq 0
by definition) if the CM determinant is positive, while
v_{s}=\textrm{i}V_{s}
if the CM determinant is negative. Consequently, the value of
\sin\theta_{\Delta}^{(\sigma)}
may not be restricted to the interval
[-1,1]
, but
\sin\theta_{\Delta}^{(\sigma)}\in\bf R
or
\sin\theta_{\Delta}^{(\sigma)}\in\textrm{i}{\bf R}
. This implies that the Lorentzian dihedral angles can take the complex values, see
[
14
,
18
]
.

Consequently, the Regge action (
4
) will give a complex number when the spacelike triangles are present. One can modify the Regge action as

S^{*}_{R}(L)=Re\left(\sum_{\Delta(s)}A_{\Delta(s)}\,\frac{1}{\textrm{i}}\,\delta_{\Delta(s)}\right)+\sum_{\Delta(t)}A_{\Delta(t)}\,\delta_{\Delta(t)}\,,

(7)

where
\Delta(s)
denotes a spacelike triangle, while
\Delta(t)
denotes a timelike triangle, so that it is always real and corresponds to the Einstein-Hilbert action on
T(\Sigma\times I)
, see
[
14
,
20
,
18
]
.

Consequently

Z(T(M))=\int_{D}\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}dL_{\epsilon}\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S^{*}_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,,

where
dL_{\epsilon}=d|L_{\epsilon}|
and
\mu(L)
is a mesure that ensures the finiteness and gives the effective action with a correct semiclassical expansion, see
[
15
,
18
]
. The integration region
D
is a subset of
{\bf R}_{+}^{N}
, consistent with a choice of spacelike and timelike edges.

It was shown in
[
18
]
that
Z(T(M))
is convergent for the measure

\mu(L)=e^{-V(M)/L_{0}^{4}}\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}\left(1+{|L_{\epsilon}|^{2}\over l_{0}^{2}}\right)^{-p}\,,

(8)

where

p>1/2\,.

(9)

The convergence of
Z
for large
|L_{\epsilon}|
is guaranteed by the
(1+|L_{\epsilon}|^{2}/l_{0}^{2})^{-p}
factors, since the exponentially damped measure

\mu_{0}(L)=e^{-V_{4}(L)/L_{0}^{4}}\,,

(10)

is not sufficient to guarantee the convergence, due to the existence of degenerate configurations, i.e. regions in
D
such that
V_{4}(L)\to 0
while some or all
|L_{\epsilon}|\to\infty
, see
[
18
]
.

The measure (
8
) also insures that the corresponding effective action (EA) can be approximated by a smooth-manifold EA. This happens when the number of the edges is large, i.e.
N\to\infty
, and
L_{\epsilon}
are sufficiently small, i.e.
|L_{\epsilon}|\leq\gamma l_{0}/N
, where
\gamma
is a constant, see
[
18
]
.

The smooth-manifold effective action is then given by the QFT effective action for GR with a momentum cutoff
K
determined by the average edge length, so that

\Gamma(L)\approx\Gamma_{K}[g_{\mu\nu}(x)]\,,

(11)

when
|L_{\epsilon}|\to 0
and
N\to\infty
, where
K=2\pi/\bar{L}
, see
[
19
]
. The smooth metric in (
11
) obeys

g_{\mu\nu}(x)\approx g_{\mu\nu}^{(\sigma)}(L)\,,\quad\textrm{for}\,\,x\in\sigma\,.

Furthermore, if
|L_{\epsilon}|
are small, but still much larger than
l_{P}
, then
\Gamma_{K}(g)
is given by the QFT perturbative expansion

\Gamma_{K}(g)=S_{EH}(g)+l_{P}^{2}\,\Gamma_{K}^{(1)}(g)+l_{P}^{4}\,\Gamma_{K}^{(2)}(g)+\cdots\,,

where
\Gamma_{K}^{(n)}
is the
n
-loop QFT effective action for GR, see
[
15
]
.

Note that one can also add the cosmological constant (CC) term to the Regge action, so that

S_{R}^{*}(L)\to S_{R}^{*}(L)+\Lambda_{c}V_{4}(L)\,.

Then the condition for the semi-classical expansion of the EA,
|L_{\epsilon}|\gg l_{P}
and
L_{0}\gg l_{P}
, is substituted by
|L_{\epsilon}|\gg l_{P}
and

L_{0}\gg\sqrt{l_{P}L_{c}}\,,

(12)

where
|\Lambda_{c}|=1/L_{c}^{2}

[
16
]
.

Scalar fields

When the matter fields
\Phi
on
T(M)
are present, they are described by a finitely many degrees of freedom (DOF), since the uncountably many values of
\phi(x)
,

, are replaced by finitely many values
\Phi(\pi)
, where
\pi
are the vertices of
T(M)
. One can also use as the matter DOF the values of
\Phi(v)
, where
v
are the dual vertices

3

Given a PL manifold
T(M)
, the dual PL manifold
T^{*}(M)
has one vertex
v

\sigma
of
T(M)
and a dual edge

and

\Gamma(L,\Phi)\approx\Gamma_{K}[g_{\mu\nu}(x),\phi(x)]\,,

g_{\mu\nu}(x)\approx g^{(\sigma)}_{\mu\nu}(L)\,,\quad\phi(x)\approx\Phi(v)\quad\textrm{for}\,\,x\in\sigma\,\,\textrm{and}\,\,v\in\sigma\,.

We also have

\Gamma_{K}(g,\phi)=S_{EH}(g)+S_{m}(g,\phi)+l_{P}^{2}\,\Gamma_{K}^{(1)}(g,\phi)+l_{P}^{4}\,\Gamma_{K}^{(2)}(g,\phi)+\cdots\,,

and small

\Gamma_{K}^{(n)}
is the
n

]
. However, the non-perturbative effective action

will be only defined if the gravity plus matter path integral is finite. In this section we will study the case of scalar fields for the PI measure (
8
).

When the matter is added, one expects that the PI measure (
8
) will be sufficient, since the integrals over the discrete matter fields do not have the problem of the degenerate configurations. However, the convergence of the matter path integral

Z_{m}(L)=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}d^{n}\varphi\,e^{\textrm{i}S_{m}(\varphi,L)/\hbar}\,,

where
\varphi
is a matter field and
n
is the number of 4-symplex cells in
T(M)
,
is still not guaranteed.

This problem can be resolved by passing to a Eucledean geometry defined by the edge lengths

\tilde{L}_{\epsilon}=|L_{\epsilon}|\,,

so that all the Eucledean edge lengths are positive real numbers. This is equvalent to a Wick rotation where
\tilde{L}_{\epsilon}=L_{\epsilon}
if
\epsilon
is a spacelike edge and
\tilde{L}_{\epsilon}=(-\textrm{i})L_{\epsilon}
, if
\epsilon
is a timelike edge.

Then we will consider the integral

\tilde{Z}_{m}(\tilde{L})=\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}d^{n}\varphi\,e^{-\tilde{S}_{m}(\varphi,\tilde{L})/\hbar}\,,

(13)

where
\tilde{S}_{m}
is the Euclidian matter action. Since
\tilde{S}_{m}(\varphi,\tilde{L})
is a positive function of
\varphi
, and

\tilde{S}_{m}(\varphi,\tilde{L})\to+\infty\,,\quad\textrm{for}\,\,|\varphi|\to+\infty\,,

then the integral
\tilde{Z}_{m}
will be convergent. Hence we will define

Z_{m}(L)=\tilde{Z}_{m}(\tilde{L}){\Big|}_{\tilde{L}=w(L)}\,,

(14)

where
w
is the Wick rotation.

Therefore the gravity plus matter path integral will be given by

Z=\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S^{*}_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,Z_{m}(L)\,,

(15)

and the convergence of the integral
Z
will be studied.

Consider a scalar massive field on
T(M)
. Then

\tilde{S}_{m}(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\sigma\in T(M)}V_{\sigma}(\tilde{L})g_{\sigma}^{\mu\nu}(\tilde{L})\Delta_{\mu}\varphi\Delta_{\nu}\varphi+\sum_{\pi\in T(M)}U(\varphi_{\pi})V_{\pi}(\tilde{L})\,,

(16)

where

U(\varphi)=\frac{1}{2}m^{2}\varphi^{2}+\lambda_{0}\varphi^{4}\,,

and
V_{\pi}
is the 4-volume of the dual cell around the vertex
\pi
of
T(M)
. The PL derivatives
\Delta_{\mu}
are defined as

\Delta_{\mu}\phi(\sigma)=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{\pi_{0}\in\sigma}{\varphi(\pi_{\mu})-\varphi(\pi_{0})\over\tilde{L}_{0\mu}}\,,

where
\pi_{0}
and
\pi_{\mu}
,
\mu=1,2,3,4
, are the verices of a 4-symplex
\sigma
.

The action (
16
) can be written as

\tilde{S}_{m}=\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}K_{\pi\pi^{\prime}}(\tilde{L})\varphi_{\pi}\varphi_{\pi^{\prime}}+m^{2}\sum_{\pi}V_{\pi}(\tilde{L})\varphi^{2}_{\pi}+\lambda_{0}\sum_{\pi}V_{\pi}(\tilde{L})\varphi^{4}_{\pi}\,.

It will be important to notice that
K(L)
and
V(L)
are homogenious functions of degree 2 and 4, so that

K(\lambda L)=\lambda^{2}K(L)\,,\quad V(\lambda L)=\lambda^{4}V(L)\,.

Let us now introduce the
N
-dimensional spherical coordinates in
{\bf R}_{+}^{N}

|L_{\epsilon}|=r\,f_{\epsilon}(\theta_{1},\cdots,\theta_{N-1})\,,

(17)

such that

r^{2}=\sum_{\epsilon=1}^{N}|L_{\epsilon}|^{2}\,,

(18)

and

\theta=(\theta_{1},...,\theta_{N-1})\in\Omega_{+}=\left[0,\frac{\pi}{2}\right]^{N-1}\,.

(19)

Let us make the change of variables
\xi_{\pi}=r\varphi_{\pi}
. The scalar-field action can be then written as

\tilde{S}_{m}(\varphi,\tilde{L})=\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}k_{\pi\pi^{\prime}}(\theta)\xi_{\pi}\xi_{\pi^{\prime}}+m^{2}r^{2}\sum_{\pi}v_{\pi}(\theta)\xi^{2}_{\pi}+\lambda_{0}\sum_{\pi}v_{\pi}(\theta)\xi^{4}_{\pi}\,,

where

K(\tilde{L})=r^{2}k(\theta)\,,\quad V(\tilde{L})=r^{4}v(\theta)\,.

When
m=0
, then one can show that

\tilde{Z}_{0}(\tilde{L})=r^{-n}F_{n}(\theta)\,,

(20)

and consequently

Z_{0}(L)=r^{-n}F_{n}(\theta)\,.

(21)

The relation (
20
) follows from

\tilde{Z}_{0}(\tilde{L})=r^{-n}\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}d^{n}\xi\,e^{-s(\theta,\xi)}=r^{-n}\,F_{n}(\theta)\,,

where

s(\theta,\xi)=\tilde{S}_{m}(\tilde{L},\varphi){\Big|}_{m=0}=\sum_{\pi,\pi^{\prime}}k_{\pi\pi^{\prime}}(\theta)\xi_{\pi}\xi_{\pi^{\prime}}+\lambda_{0}\sum_{\pi}v_{\pi}(\theta)\xi^{4}_{\pi}\,.

The relation (
21
) follows from the fact that the points
(\tilde{L}_{1},\cdots,\tilde{L}_{N})
and
(L_{1},\cdots,L_{N})
have the same spherical coordinates.

Since the integral which defines
F_{n}(\theta)
is finite and positive for any
\theta\in\Omega_{+}
, therefore
F_{n}(\theta)
is a bounded positive function on
\Omega_{+}
. Since
\Omega_{+}
is a compact set, then

0<F_{n}(\theta)\leq C_{n}\,.

Let us now study the convergence of the total path integral (
15
). Let us divide the integration region
D
such that

D=D_{1}\cup D_{2}\,,

where

D_{1}=D\cap B(R)\,,\,\,D_{2}=D\cap\bar{B}(R)\,,

where
B(R)
is a ball
r\leq R
and
\bar{B}(R)={\bf R}_{+}^{N}\setminus B(R)
.

Therefore

Z=Z_{1}+Z_{2}\,,

where

Z_{k}=\int_{D_{k}}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S^{*}_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,Z_{0}(L)\,.

Consequently

|Z|\leq|Z_{1}|+|Z_{2}|\,,

and we have

|Z_{1}|={\Big|}\int_{D_{1}}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S^{*}_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,Z_{0}(L){\Big|}\leq\int_{D_{1}}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,|Z_{0}(L)|\,.

Since
0<\mu(L)\leq 1
, then

|Z_{1}|\leq\int_{D_{1}}d^{N}L\,|Z_{0}(L)|=\textrm{const.}\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1-n}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta J_{N}(\theta)F_{n}(\theta)\,,

(22)

where
\Omega
is the set of angles for the points in
D
.

The radial integral in (
22
) will be convergent for

N>n\,,

or

N_{1}>N_{0}\,,

(23)

which is satisfied for many regular triangulations. The angular integral is also finite, since
\Omega\subseteq\Omega_{+}
, and
J_{N}
and
F_{n}
are bounded functions on
\Omega_{+}
. Hence
Z_{1}
is absolutely convergent.

In the case of the integral
Z_{2}
we have

|Z_{2}|={\Big|}\int_{D_{2}}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S^{*}_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,Z_{0}(L){\Big|}\leq\int_{D_{2}}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,|Z_{0}(L)|\,,

so that

\displaystyle|Z_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{D_{2}}d^{N}L\,e^{-V_{4}(L)/L_{0}^{4}}\,\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}{l_{0}^{2p}\over(l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2})^{p}}\,{F_{n}(\theta)\over r^{n}}

(24)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{D_{2}}d^{N}L\,e^{-V_{4}(L)/L_{0}^{4}}\,\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}{l_{0}^{2p}\over(l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2})^{p}}\,{C_{n}\over R^{n}}\,,

since
F_{n}(\theta)\leq C_{n}
for
\theta\in\Omega_{D}\subseteq\Omega_{N}^{+}
and

. Then

\displaystyle|Z_{2}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle{C_{n}\over R^{n}}\int_{D_{2}}d^{N}L\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}{l_{0}^{2p}\over{l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2}}^{p}}

(25)

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle{C_{n}\over R^{n}}\int_{{\bf R}_{+}^{N}}d^{N}L\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}{l_{0}^{2p}\over(l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2})^{p}}={C_{n}\over R^{n}}\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{\infty}{l_{0}^{2p}\,dL_{\epsilon}\over(l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2})^{p}}\,.

Since
p>1/2
, this implies that
Z_{2}
is absolutely convergent and consequently
Z
is convergent for a massless scalar field.

When the scalar field mass

is different from zero, we have

0<Z_{m>0}(L)<Z_{m=0}(L)\,,

because

\tilde{S}_{m}(\tilde{L},\varphi){\Big|}_{m>0}>\tilde{S}_{m}(\tilde{L},\varphi){\Big|}_{m=0}\,.

Therefore

|Z|\leq\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,|Z_{m}(L)|<\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,|Z_{0}(L)|\,,

(26)

and since we have showed that the last integral in (
26
) is convergent, this implies the absolute convergence of
Z
in the case of a massive scalar field.

When we have
k
scalar fields, the absolute convergence condition changes to

N_{1}>kN_{0}\,.

(27)

Note that a regular triangulation has a dual one-simplex which is a five-valent graph, so that
5N_{0}^{*}=2N_{1}^{*}
. One can conjecture that

{N\over n}={N_{1}\over N_{0}}\geq{N_{1}^{*}\over N^{*}_{0}}=\frac{5}{2}\,,

(28)

which can be checked in the case of regular triangulations of
S^{4}
with a small number of vertices. For example, it holds in the case of the triangulation with
N_{0}=6
and
N_{1}=15
(
T_{1}(S^{4})
= 5-simplex graph), or for a triangulation with
N_{0}=12
and
N_{1}=55
(
T_{1}(S^{4})
= two 5-simplex graphs joined by 5 parallel edges).

In an arbitrary spacetime dimension
d
, we will have

{N_{1}\over N_{0}}\geq{N_{1}^{*}\over N^{*}_{0}}=\frac{d+1}{2}\,,

(29)

which can be checked for regular triangulations of
S^{d}
with a small number of vertices. For example, in the case
d=2
, the inequality (
29
) holds for the triangulation with
N_{0}=4
and
N_{1}=6
(
T_{1}(S^{2})
= tetrahedron graph) and for triangulations with
N_{0}=8
and
N_{1}=18
(two tetrahedron graphs joined by three parallel edges).

Since
N_{1}/N_{0}\geq 5/2
for a regular triangulation, from (
27
) it follows that for
k\geq 3
the convergence of the PI is not guaranteed.

Yang-Mills fields

Consider a gauge field
A_{\mu}(x)=A_{\mu}^{I}(x)T_{I}
on
M
, where
T_{I}
is a basis of the Lie algebra for a Lie group
G
. The Yang-Mills (YM) action is given by

S_{YM}=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{M}d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\,g^{\mu\rho}g^{\nu\iota}\,Tr\left(F_{\mu\nu}F_{\rho\iota}\right)\,.

On
T(M)
it is natural to use the variables

A_{\epsilon}=\frac{1}{|L_{\epsilon}|}\int_{\epsilon}A_{\mu}dx^{\mu}\,,

so that one can define the edge holonomies

U_{\epsilon}=\exp(\textrm{i}|L_{\epsilon}|A_{\epsilon})\,.

(30)

The edge holonomies transform under the gauge transformations as

U_{\epsilon}\to g_{\pi}\,U_{\epsilon}\,g_{\pi^{\prime}}^{-1}\,,

where
g_{\pi}\in G
, so that the field strengths can be associated to the triangles and one has

F_{\Delta}=F_{123}\approx Im\,{U_{12}U_{23}U_{31}-Id\over A_{123}}=Im\,{U_{\Delta}-Id\over A_{\Delta}}

for
A_{\Delta}\to 0
.

One can then write a gauge invariant action

S_{YM}(U,L)=-\frac{1}{40}\sum_{\sigma\in T(M)}V_{\sigma}(L)\,\sum_{\epsilon\in\sigma}Tr\left(F_{\Delta}F_{\Delta^{\prime}}\right)\,,

(31)

where
\Delta,\Delta^{\prime}\in\sigma
and
\Delta\cap\Delta^{\prime}=\epsilon
.

The action (
31
) is a function of the Wilson loop variables
U_{\Delta}
, which are qauge invariant, and this is the reason why they are used in QCD
4
4

4

Since QCD is formulated on a flat spacetime, one uses the plaquetes instead of triangles
[
21
]
.
. However, for the purposes of studying the finitenness of the PLQG path integral, it is more convinient to use the local gauge fields
A_{\mu}(x)

as in the case of the scalar field.

For a point

we have

\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}(x)\to\Delta_{\mu}A_{\nu}(\sigma)=\frac{1}{5}\sum_{\pi_{0}\in\sigma}{A_{\nu}(\pi_{\mu})-A_{\nu}(\pi_{0})\over\tilde{L}_{0\mu}}\,,

(32)

so that one can define

F_{\mu\nu}(\sigma)=\Delta_{[\mu}A_{\nu]}+\frac{g_{0}}{5}\sum_{\pi\in\sigma}[A_{\mu}(\pi),A_{\nu}(\pi)]\,,

which can be associated to the field strength

F_{\mu\nu}(x)=\partial_{[\mu}A_{\nu]}(x)+g_{0}[A_{\mu}(x),A_{\nu}(x)]\,,

where

is the YM coupling constant.

As far as the gauge invariance

A_{\mu}(x)\to g(x)(A_{\mu}(x)+\partial_{\mu})g^{-1}(x)\,,

is concerned, one can define it on

A_{\mu}(\pi)\to g_{\pi}(A_{\mu}(\pi)+\Delta_{\mu})g_{\pi}^{-1}

\Delta_{\mu}f(\pi)={f(\pi_{\mu})-f(\pi)\over\tilde{L}_{0\mu}}\,.

The path integral for the YM action on

The path integral on

Z_{YM}=\int{\cal D}A\,{\cal D}c\,{\cal D}\bar{c}\,e^{\textrm{i}(S_{YM}(A)+S_{gf}(A)+S_{gh}(A,\bar{c},c))/\hbar}\,,

where

\displaystyle S_{YM}

\displaystyle S_{gf}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-{1\over 2}\int_{M}d^{4}x\,\sqrt{g}\,g^{\mu\nu}g^{\rho\sigma}Tr\left(\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}\,\partial_{\rho}A_{\sigma}\right)\,,

\displaystyle S_{gh}

\displaystyle-\int_{M}d^{4}x\,\sqrt{g}\,Tr\left(\partial^{\mu}\bar{c}\,(\partial_{\mu}c-\textrm{i}\,g_{0}A_{\mu}c)\right)\,.

(37)

Although the PI (

Hence we define on
T(M)

\tilde{Z}_{YM}=\int\prod_{\pi,\mu,a}dA_{\mu}^{a}(\pi)\,\prod_{\pi,a}d\bar{c}^{a}_{\pi}\,dc^{a}_{\pi}\,e^{-(\tilde{S}_{YM}(A)+\tilde{S}_{gf}(A)+\tilde{S}_{gh}(A,\bar{c},c))/\hbar}\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{YM}

\displaystyle{1\over 4}\sum_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}(L)\,g^{\mu\rho}(\sigma)g^{\nu\sigma}(\sigma)Tr\left(F_{\mu\nu}(\sigma)F_{\rho\sigma}(\sigma)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{gf}

\displaystyle{1\over 2}\sum_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}(L)\,g^{\mu\nu}(\sigma)g^{\rho\sigma}(\sigma)Tr\left(\Delta_{\mu}A_{\nu}(\sigma)\,\Delta_{\rho}A_{\sigma}(\sigma)\right)\,,

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{gh}

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}(L)\,g^{\mu\nu}(\sigma)\,Tr\left(\Delta_{\mu}\bar{c}\,(\Delta_{\nu}c-\textrm{i}\,g_{0}A_{\nu}c)\right)\,,

are the Eucledean PL geometry versions of (

The action in the exponent of the integrand in (

\tilde{S}_{YM}+\tilde{S}_{gf}=\sum_{p,q}K_{pq}(L)A_{p}A_{q}+g_{0}\sum_{p,q,r}I_{pqr}(L)A_{p}A_{q}A_{r}\,,

+\,g_{0}^{2}\sum_{p,q,r,s}J_{pqrs}(L)A_{p}A_{q}A_{r}A_{s}\,,

+\,g_{0}^{2}\sum_{p,q,r,s}\tilde{J}_{pqrs}(L)\bar{c}_{p}c_{q}A_{r}A_{s}\,.

We can integrate the Grassman variables

\tilde{Z}_{YM}=\int\prod_{\pi,\mu,a}dA_{\mu}^{a}(\pi)\,\,e^{-\tilde{S}_{YM}(A)-\tilde{S}_{gf}(A))/\hbar}\,\textrm{det}\left(\tilde{K}+g_{0}\tilde{I}A+g_{0}^{2}\tilde{J}AA\right)\,,

where the matrix
\tilde{K}+g_{0}\tilde{I}A+g_{0}^{2}\tilde{J}AA

\tilde{K}_{pq}+g_{0}\sum_{r}\tilde{I}_{pqr}A_{r}+g_{0}^{2}\sum_{r,s}\tilde{J}_{pqrs}A_{r}A_{s}\,.

Since

K(\lambda L)=\lambda^{2}K(L)\,,\quad I(\lambda L)=\lambda^{3}I(L)\,,\quad J(\lambda L)=\lambda^{4}J(L)\,,

and the same holds for
\tilde{K}

\tilde{J}

\tilde{L}

\xi_{p}=rA_{p}\,,\quad\forall p\in T_{0}(M)\times I_{G}\,,

where
I_{G}=\{1,2,...,|G|\}

\tilde{S}_{YM}+\tilde{S}_{gf}=\sum_{p,q}k_{pq}(\theta)\xi_{p}\xi_{q}+g_{0}\sum_{p,q,r}i_{pqr}(\theta)\xi_{p}\xi_{q}\xi_{r}+g_{0}^{2}\sum_{p,q,r,s}j_{pqrs}(\theta)\xi_{p}\xi_{q}\xi_{r}\xi_{s}\,,

where

K=r^{2}k(\theta)\,,\quad I=r^{3}i(\theta)\,,\quad J=r^{4}j(\theta)\,.

Consequently we have

\tilde{Z}_{YM}(\tilde{L})=r^{-4n|G|}\int_{{\bf R}^{n}}\prod_{p}d\xi_{p}\,e^{-s_{YM}(\theta,\xi)}\,\textrm{det}\left(r^{2}(\tilde{k}(\theta)+g_{0}\tilde{i}(\theta)\xi+g_{0}^{2}\tilde{j}(\theta)\xi\xi)\right)

Z=\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S_{R}^{*}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,r^{-2n|G|}G_{n}(\theta)\,.

(45)

Dirac spinors

Let us consider a massive Dirac field
\psi^{\alpha}(x)
on
T(M)
with the edge lengths
L_{\epsilon}
. The Dirac action on
M
can be written as

S_{D}=\int_{M}\epsilon^{abcd}e_{b}\wedge e_{c}\wedge e_{d}\wedge\bar{\psi}\left(\textrm{i}\gamma_{a}\,\textrm{d}+\textrm{i}\gamma_{a}\,\omega-m\,e_{a}\right)\psi\,,

(47)

where
\omega=\omega^{ab}[\gamma_{a},\gamma_{b}]/8
and
\gamma_{a}
ar the gamma matrices.

The action (
47
) can be written as

S_{D}=\int_{M}\epsilon^{abcd}B_{bcd}\wedge J_{a}\,,

(48)

where the 3-forms
B
are given by

B_{abc}=e_{a}\wedge e_{b}\wedge e_{c}\,,

while the one-forms
J
are given by

J_{a}=\bar{\psi}(\textrm{i}\gamma_{a}(\textrm{d}+\omega)-me_{a})\psi\,.

The action (
48
) is an integral of a product of a 3-form with a one-form, and any such action on
T(M)
can be written as

\sum_{\tau\in T(M)}B_{\tau}J_{l}\,,

where
l
is the dual edge which corresponds to a tetrahedron
\tau
in
T(M)
and

B_{\tau}=\int_{\tau}B\,,\quad J_{l}=\int_{l}J\,.

Therefore we obtain

S_{D}=S_{D}^{(1)}+S_{D}^{(2)}+S_{D}^{(3)}\,,

where

\displaystyle S_{D}^{(1)}(\psi,L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in T(M)}V_{\tau}(L)|L_{l}|\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(\tau)\,\bar{\psi}(v)\gamma_{d}\,{\psi(v^{\prime})-\psi(v)\over|L_{l}|}

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\tau\in T(M)}V_{\tau}(L)\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(\tau)\,\bar{\psi}(v)\gamma_{d}\,(\psi(v^{\prime})-\psi(v))\,,

and

B_{abc}(\tau)=e_{ai}(\tau)e_{bj}(\tau)e_{ck}(\tau)\epsilon^{ijk}\,,

while
v
and
v^{\prime}
are the vertices of the dual edge
l
which corresponds to a tetrahedron
\tau
. The vierbeins on
T(M)
are defined by

g_{\mu\nu}(\sigma)=e_{\mu}^{a}(\sigma)e_{\nu a}(\sigma)\,,

see
[
19
]
.

For the second term of the Dirac action one has

S^{(2)}_{D}(\psi,L)=\sum_{\tau\in T(M)}V_{\tau}|L_{l}|\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(\tau)\,\bar{\psi}(v)\gamma_{d}\,\omega(l)\psi(v^{\prime})\,,

(50)

while for the mass term one has

S^{(3)}_{D}(\psi,L)=m\sum_{\sigma\in T(M)}V_{\sigma}(L)\bar{\psi}(v)\psi(v)\,.

(51)

Note that the Dirac action has been written in terms of the fermionic variables
\psi(v)
, where
v\in T^{*}(M)
is a dual vertex. The Dirac action can be also written in terms of the fermionic variables
\psi(\pi)
, where
\pi
is a vertex of
T(M)
. In this case we have

S_{D}^{(1)}(\psi^{*},L)=\sum_{p}B_{p}J_{\epsilon}\,,

where
p
is the dual 3-polytope to an edge
\epsilon
. Furthermore

\displaystyle S_{D}^{(1)}(\psi^{*},L)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in T^{*}(M)}V_{p}(L)|L_{\epsilon}|\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(p)\,\bar{\psi}(\pi)\gamma_{d}\,{\psi(\pi^{\prime})-\psi(\pi)\over|L_{\epsilon}|}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{p\in T^{*}(M)}V_{p}(L)\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(p)\,\bar{\psi}(\pi)\gamma_{d}\,(\psi(\pi^{\prime})-\psi(\pi))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\epsilon\in T(M)}V^{*}_{\epsilon}(L)\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(\epsilon)\,\bar{\psi}(\pi)\gamma_{d}\,(\psi(\pi^{\prime})-\psi(\pi))\,,

where
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
are the vertices of the edge
\epsilon
while

B_{abc}(p)=B_{abc}(\epsilon)={1\over n_{4}(\epsilon)}\sum_{\sigma;\,\epsilon\in\sigma}e_{ai}(\sigma)e_{bj}(\sigma)e_{ck}(\sigma)\epsilon^{ijk}\,,

(53)

and
n_{4}(\epsilon)
is the number of
\sigma
that share an edge
\epsilon
which is dual to
p
.

For the second term of the Dirac action
we obtain

S^{(2)}_{D}(\psi^{*},L)=\sum_{p\in T^{*}(M)}V_{p}|L_{\epsilon}|\,\epsilon^{abcd}B_{abc}(p)\,\bar{\psi}(\pi)\gamma_{d}\,\omega(\epsilon)\psi(\pi^{\prime})\,,

(54)

where
\pi,\pi^{\prime}\in\epsilon
and

\omega(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{n_{3}(\epsilon)}\,\sum_{\tau;\,\epsilon\in\tau}\omega(l)\,.

(55)

Here
\tau
is a tetrahedron dual to
l
and
n_{3}(\epsilon)
is the number of
\tau
that share an edge
\epsilon
. For the mass term one has

S^{(3)}_{D}(\psi^{*},L)=m\sum_{\pi\in T(M)}V_{\pi}(L)\bar{\psi}(\pi)\psi(\pi)\,,

(56)

where
V_{\pi}
is the volume of the 4-polytope
q
dual to a vertex
\pi
.

In any case, the Dirac action on
T(M)
can be then written as

S_{D}(\psi,L)=S_{DK}+S_{Dm}\,,

where

S_{DK}=\sum_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}\bar{\psi}_{\bf u}C_{{\bf u}{\bf v}}(L)\psi_{\bf v}\,,

and

S_{Dm}=\sum_{{\bf v}}\bar{\psi}_{\bf v}M_{{\bf v}}(L)\psi_{\bf v}\,,

such that
{\bf v}=(v,\alpha)\in T^{*}_{0}(M)\times I_{D}
or
{\bf v}=(\pi,\alpha)\in T_{0}(M)\times I_{D}
where
I_{D}=\{1,2,3,4\}
.

The fermionic path integral can be then written as

Z_{D}=\int\prod_{{\bf v}=1}^{4n}d\bar{\psi}_{\bf v}\,d\psi_{\bf v}\,e^{\textrm{i}(S_{DK}+S_{Dm})/\hbar}\,,

where we now assume that
\psi_{\bf v}
and
\bar{\psi}_{\bf v}
are the generator elements of the corresponding Grassmann algebra, satisfying

\{\psi_{\bf v},\psi_{{\bf v}^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{{\bf v},{\bf v}^{\prime}}\,,\quad\{\psi_{\bf v},\bar{\psi}_{{\bf v}^{\prime}}\}=0\,,\quad\{\bar{\psi}_{\bf v},\bar{\psi}_{{\bf v}^{\prime}}\}=\delta_{{\bf v},{\bf v}^{\prime}}\,.

The integration on a Grassman algebra is defined as a diferentiation, so that

\int\prod_{{\bf v}=1}^{F}d\bar{\psi}_{\bf v}\,d\psi_{\bf v}f(\psi,\bar{\psi})={\partial^{2F}\,f(\psi,\bar{\psi})\over\partial\psi_{1}\cdots\partial\psi_{F}\partial\bar{\psi}_{1}\cdots\partial\bar{\psi}_{F}}\,,

where
F=4n
, so that one obtains

Z_{D}=\textrm{const.}\,\,\textrm{det}\left(C_{{\bf u}{\bf v}}(L)+\delta_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}M_{\bf v}(L)\right)\,.

Because of the scaling properties

C(\lambda L)=\lambda^{3}C(L)\,,\quad M(\lambda L)=\lambda^{4}M(L)\,,

it is convenient to use the spherical coordinates for a vector
L
, so that we obtain

Z_{D}=\textrm{const.}\,\textrm{det}\left(r^{3}c_{{\bf u}{\bf v}}(\theta)+\delta_{{\bf u},{\bf v}}r^{4}\,m_{\bf v}(\theta)\right)=r^{12n}\,P(r,\theta)\,,

where

P(r,\theta)=\sum_{k=0}^{4n}r^{k}a_{k}(\theta)\,.

Note that in order to calculate
Z_{D}
we did not have to perform a Wick rotation on
L
’s as in the case of bosonic matter. But even if we did use the Eucledean path integral

\tilde{Z}_{D}(\tilde{L})=\int\prod_{{\bf v}=1}^{4n}d\bar{\psi}_{\bf v}\,d\psi_{\bf v}\,e^{-(\tilde{S}_{DK}+\tilde{S}_{Dm})/\hbar}\,,

and then used
Z_{D}(L)=\tilde{Z}_{D}(w(L))
, the same results would be obtained.

The convergence of the total path integral

Z=\int_{D}\prod_{\epsilon=1}^{N}dL_{\epsilon}\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,Z_{D}(L)

can be now studied. We use the same approach as in the massless scalar field, so that

Z=Z_{1}+Z_{2}\,,

where

Z_{k}=\int_{D_{k}}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S^{*}_{R}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}\,Z_{D}(L)\,.

Consequently

|Z|\leq|Z_{1}|+|Z_{2}|\,,

and we have

|Z_{1}|\leq\int_{D_{1}}d^{N}L\,|Z_{D}(L)|=\textrm{const.}\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1+24n}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta J_{N}(\theta)|P(r,\theta)|

\leq\textrm{const.}\sum_{k=0}^{4n}\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1+12n+k}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta J_{N}(\theta)|a_{k}(\theta)|\,,

and all these integrals are convergent.

For
Z_{2}
we have

|Z_{2}|\leq\int_{D_{2}}d^{N}L\,|Z_{D}(L)|\prod_{\epsilon}{l_{0}^{2p}\over(l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2})^{p}}\,,

so that

|Z_{2}|\leq\textrm{const.}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{-(2p-1)N-1+12n}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta J_{N}(\theta)\,h(\theta,l_{0}/r)\,|P(r,\theta)|\,,

where

\prod_{\epsilon}{l_{0}^{2p}\over(l_{0}^{2}+L_{\epsilon}^{2})^{p}}=r^{-2Np}\prod_{\epsilon}{l_{0}^{2p}\over(\frac{l_{0}^{2}}{r^{2}}+f_{\epsilon}^{2}(\theta))^{p}}=r^{-2Np}h_{N}(\theta,l_{0}/r)\,.

(57)

Then

|Z_{2}|\leq\textrm{const.}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{-(2p-1)N-1+12n}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta J_{N}(\theta)\,h_{N}(\theta,l_{0}/r)\,A_{n}r^{4n}\,,

since for a sufficiently large positive constant
A_{n}
, we have

|P(r,\theta)|\leq A_{n}r^{4n}

for
r\geq R
. Consequently

|Z_{2}|\leq\textrm{const.}\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{-(2p-1)N-1+16n}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta J_{N}(\theta)\,h_{N}(\theta,l_{0}/r)\,,

so that the absolute convergence requires

(2p-1)N>16n\,,

(58)

or

(2p-1)N_{1}>16N_{4}\,.

(59)

In the case that we use for the fermionic DOF the verices of
T(M)
, then
n=N_{0}
and
N=N_{1}
, so that

(2p-1)N_{1}>16kN_{0}\,,

(60)

where
k
is the number of the Dirac fermions.

Note that the PI convergence criterion for fermions (
60
) now comes from the integration over the region of large edge lengths, in contrast to bosons, where such a criterion comes from the integral over the small edge lengths.

Also note that (
60
) will be satisfied for

p-1/2>16k/5\,,

(61)

since
N_{1}/N_{0}\geq 5/2
for a regular triangulation. Hence for a sufficiently big
p
we will insure the convergence of the fermionic PLQG path integral.

In the case of chiral Dirac spinors, also known as the Weyl spinors, which are relevant for the Standard Model,

\psi_{L}={1-\gamma_{5}\over 2}\psi\,,\quad\psi_{R}={1+\gamma_{5}\over 2}\psi\,.

In this case we have only 2 independent components per spinor, so that the convergence condition (
61
) becomes

p-1/2>8k/5\,.

(62)

6
Standard Model

The action for the Standard Model on
M
contains massles scalar fields, YM gauge fields and chiral Dirac spinors. We can write it as

S_{0}=S_{H}+S_{YM}+S_{f}+S_{Y}=\int_{M}d^{4}x\,\sqrt{g}\left({\cal L}_{H}+{\cal L}_{YM}+{\cal L}_{f}+{\cal L}_{Y}\right)\,,

where

{\cal L}_{H}=\frac{1}{2}D^{\mu}\phi^{\dagger}D_{\mu}\phi-\lambda_{0}^{2}(\phi^{\dagger}\phi-\phi_{0}^{2})^{2}\,,

{\cal L}_{YM}=-\frac{1}{4}\,Tr\left(F^{\mu\nu}F_{\mu\nu}\right)\,,

{\cal L}_{f}=\sum_{k=1}^{48}\epsilon^{abcd}e_{b}\wedge e_{c}\wedge e_{d}\,\bar{\psi}_{k}\left(\textrm{i}\gamma_{a}(\textrm{d}+\textrm{i}\omega+\textrm{i}g_{0}A)\right)\psi_{k}\,,

{\cal L}_{Y}=\sum_{i,j}Y_{ij}\,\langle\bar{\psi}_{i}\psi_{j}\phi\rangle\,,

and

D_{\mu}\phi=(\partial_{\mu}+\textrm{i}\,(g_{0}A)_{\mu})\phi\,.

Here the gauge group
G
is given by
U(1)\times SU(2)\times SU(3)
and

\phi=\tau_{a}\phi^{a}\,,\quad A=T_{a}A^{a}\,,

where
\tau_{a}
are the Lie algebra generators of
G
in the Higgs field representation, while
T_{a}
are the corresponding matrices in the adjoint representation of
G
. We also use a short-hand

g_{0}A=\sum_{l=1}^{3}g_{0l}A_{l}\,,

where
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
and
A_{3}
belong to the Lie algebras of
U(1)
,
SU(2)
and
SU(3)
, respectively and
g_{0l}
are the corresponding coupling constants.

A fermion
\psi_{k}
is a Weyl spinor
\psi_{R}
or
\psi_{L}
, can be a lepton or a quark, and belongs to an appropriate representation of
G
. There are 3 generations of fermions which gives 48 Weyl spinors. We use
\langle...\rangle
in
{\cal L}_{Y}
to denote the corresponding group invariant.

On
T(M)
we will use as the DOF the values of these fields at the vertices of
T(M)
, so that

\tilde{S}_{H}=\sum_{\sigma}V_{\sigma}(L)\,s_{HK}+\sum_{\pi}V_{\pi}^{*}(L)\,s_{HP}\,,

where

s_{HK}=g^{\mu\nu}_{\sigma}\left(\frac{\phi(\pi_{\mu})-\phi(\pi_{0})}{|L_{0\mu}|}+\textrm{i}g_{0}A_{\mu}(\pi_{0})\phi_{\pi_{0}}\right)^{\dagger}\left(\frac{\phi(\pi_{\nu})-\phi(\pi_{0})}{|L_{0\nu}|}+\textrm{i}g_{0}A_{\nu}(\pi_{0})\phi_{\pi_{0}}\right)

and

s_{HP}=\lambda_{0}^{2}\left(\phi^{\dagger}(\pi)\phi(\pi)-\phi_{0}^{2}\right)^{2}\,.

Note that
\tilde{S}_{H}
is a Euclidean action, so that
\tilde{S}_{H}>0
, which we need in order to define the matter path integral. The euclidean PL geometry will be defined by a Wick rotation
L_{\epsilon}\to\tilde{L}_{\epsilon}=|L_{\epsilon}|
, see section 3. The same applies to the YM action, which will be given by (
39
) plus the gauge-fixing term (
40
) and the ghost action (
41
) .

The fermion action on
T(M)
is given by

\tilde{S}_{f}=\sum_{\epsilon}V_{\epsilon}^{*}(L)\,s_{f}+\sum_{\pi}V_{\pi}^{*}(L)\,\,s_{YMf}\,,

where

s_{f}=\sum_{i}\epsilon^{abcd}\,B_{abc}(p)\,\bar{\psi}_{i}(\pi)\,\textrm{i}\gamma_{d}\,(|L_{\epsilon}|\,\textrm{i}\omega_{\epsilon}(L)\,\psi_{i}(\pi^{\prime})+\psi_{i}(\pi^{\prime})-\psi_{i}(\pi))\,,

and

s_{YMf}=\sum_{i}\bar{\psi}_{i}(\pi)\,g_{0}\gamma^{\mu}(\pi)A_{\mu}(\pi)\,\psi_{i}(\pi)\,.

(63)

In (
63
) we used

\gamma^{\mu}(\pi)=e^{\mu}_{a}(\pi)\gamma^{a}\,,

where

e^{\mu}_{a}(\pi)={1\over n_{\sigma}(\pi)}\sum_{\sigma;\,\pi\in\sigma}e^{\mu}_{a}(\sigma)\,.

The Yukawa action on
T(M)
is given by

\tilde{S}_{Y}=\sum_{\pi}V_{\pi}^{*}(L)\,s_{Y}\,,

where

s_{Y}=\sum_{i,j}Y_{ij}\langle\bar{\psi}_{i}(\pi)\psi_{j}(\pi)\phi(\pi)\rangle\,.

Note that

V_{\epsilon}^{*}(L)=V_{p}(L)\,,\quad V_{\pi}^{*}(L)=V_{q}(L)\,,

where
p
is the 3-polytope dual to an edge
\epsilon
and
q
is the 4-polytope dual to a vertex
\pi
of
T(M)
.
Also note that there are no mass terms for the fermions, since they are generated through the Yukawa couplings.

If we pass to the spherical coordinates for the edge lengths, then we can represent schematically the gauge-fixed SM action on
T(M)
as

S=S_{1}+S_{2}+\tilde{S}_{2}+S_{3}\,,

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{3}\langle\bar{\psi}\psi\rangle+r^{4}\langle\bar{\psi}\psi A\rangle+r^{4}\langle\bar{\psi}\psi\phi\rangle

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{4}\langle(Ar^{-1}+g_{0}A^{2})^{2}\rangle

\displaystyle\tilde{S}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{3}\langle\bar{c}(r^{-1}+g_{0}Ac)\rangle

\displaystyle S_{3}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle r^{4}\langle(\phi r^{-1}+g_{0}A\phi)^{2}\rangle+\lambda_{0}^{2}r^{4}\langle(\phi^{2}-\phi_{0}^{2})^{2}\rangle\,,

(64)

where a bracket
\langle XY\cdots\rangle
represents

\sum_{\alpha,\beta,...}c^{\alpha\beta...}(\theta)X_{\alpha}Y_{\beta}\cdots\,.

Then we have

\int d^{nn_{f}}\bar{\psi}\,d^{nn_{f}}\psi\,e^{-S}=e^{-S_{2}-\tilde{S}_{2}-S_{3}}\,\textrm{det}\left(r^{3}\langle 1+rA+r\phi\rangle_{kl}\right)

=r^{3n\cdot n_{f}}\,e^{-S_{2}-\tilde{S}_{2}-S_{3}}\,\textrm{det}\left(\langle 1+rA+r\phi\rangle_{kl}\right)\,,

where
n_{f}
is the number of complex components of the fermionic fields of the Standard Model. Since the SM has 16 Weyl spinors in a fermionic generation and there are 3 generations, then

n_{f}=2\cdot 16\cdot 3=96\,.

(65)

Therefore the matrix
\langle 1+rA+r\phi\rangle
has the size
96n\times 96n
, and an entry
\langle 1\rangle_{kl}
denotes a function of
\theta
.

Integration of the YM ghosts gives

r^{3n_{f}n+2|G|n}\,e^{-S_{2}-S_{3}}\,\textrm{det}\left(\langle 1+rA+r\phi\rangle_{kl}\right)\,\textrm{det}\left(\langle 1+rA\rangle_{rs}\right)\,,

where
\langle 1+rA\rangle_{rs}
is a
2|G|n\times 2|G|n
matrix.

Now we make the change of variables

\xi=rA\,,\quad\chi=r\phi\,,

so that we obtain

\tilde{Z}_{m}=r^{(3n_{f}-2|G|-4)n}\,\int d^{4n}\chi\,d^{4|G|n}\xi\,e^{-S_{2}-S_{3}}\,\textrm{det}\langle 1+\xi+\chi\rangle\,\textrm{det}\langle 1+\xi\rangle\,,

where

S_{2}+S_{3}=\langle(\xi+g_{0}\xi^{2})^{2}\rangle+\langle(\chi+g_{0}\xi\chi)^{2}\rangle+\lambda_{0}^{2}\langle(\chi^{2}-r^{2}\phi_{0}^{2})^{2}\rangle\,.

Since

S_{2}+S_{3}>\langle(\xi+g_{0}\xi^{2})^{2}\rangle+\langle(\chi+g_{0}\xi\chi)^{2}\rangle\equiv s(\theta,\xi,\chi)\,,

then

|\tilde{Z}_{m}(\tilde{L})|<r^{(3n_{f}-2|G|-4)n}\,\int d^{4n}\chi\,d^{4|G|n}\xi\,e^{-s(\theta,\xi,\chi)}\,\textrm{det}\langle 1+\xi+\chi\rangle\,\textrm{det}\langle 1+\xi\rangle\,,

\equiv r^{(3n_{f}-2|G|-4)n}\,F_{n}(\theta)\,.

Given that

Z=\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,e^{\textrm{i}S_{R}^{*}(L)/l_{P}^{2}}Z_{m}(L)\,,

(66)

where

Z_{m}(L)=\tilde{Z}_{m}(w(L))

then

|Z|\leq\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,|Z_{m}(L)|\,<\,\int_{D}d^{N}L\,\mu(L)\,r^{(3n_{f}-2|G|-4)n}\,F_{n}(\theta)\equiv I\,.

Since

I=I_{1}+I_{2}

where

I_{1}=\int_{0}^{R}r^{N-1}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta\,J_{N}(\theta)\,\mu(r,\theta)r^{(3n_{f}-2|G|-4)n}\,F_{n}(\theta)

and

I_{2}=\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{N-1}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta\,J_{N}(\theta)\,\mu(r,\theta)r^{(3n_{f}-2|G|-4)n}\,F_{n}(\theta)\,,

then the convergence of
I_{1}
is determined by the integral for small
R
, while the convergence of
I_{2}
is determined by the integral for large
R
.

Since

\mu(r,\theta)\approx 1

for
r\to 0
, while

\mu(r,\theta)\approx r^{-2pN}h_{N}(\theta,0)

for
r\to\infty
, see eq. (
57
), then

I_{1}\approx\int_{0}^{R}r^{cn+N-1}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta\,J_{N}(\theta)\,F_{n}(\theta)\,,

for small
R
, so that it is convergent when

cn+N>0\,.

(67)

Since

c=3n_{f}-2|G|-4=3\cdot 96-2\cdot(1+3+8)-4=230>0\,,

(68)

then
I_{1}
will be convergent. When
R\to\infty
, we obtain

I_{2}\approx\int_{R}^{\infty}r^{cn-(2p-1)N-1}dr\int_{\Omega}d^{N-1}\theta\,h_{N}(\theta,0)\,J_{N}(\theta)\,F_{n}(\theta)\,,

which is convergent for

cn-(2p-1)N<0\,,

or

{c\over(2p-1)}<{N\over n}\,.

(69)

Therefore the PI for GR and SM will be convergent for triangulations which satisfy (
69
). For a regular triangulation we have

{N\over n}={N_{1}\over N_{0}}\geq{N_{1}^{*}\over N^{*}_{0}}=\frac{5}{2}\,.

Hence if we choose
p
such that

{c\over(2p-1)}<\frac{5}{2}\leq{N\over n}\,,

then the path integral will be absolutely convergent. Since in the case of the SM
c=230
, see (
68
), we then obtain

p-\frac{1}{2}>46\,.

(70)

Therefore we can insure the absolute convergence of the path integral
Z
for GR and SM if we choose the parameter
p
to be sufficiently large, i.e.
p>46,5
.

7
Conclusions

The potential divergencies of a path integral for gravity and bosonic matter on a PL spacetime
T(M)
come from the region of small edge lengths, i.e. when
r\to 0
. In this case we showed that the path integral is convergent when there is a small number of fields and
p>1/2
. In the case of fermionic matter, the potential divergencies come from the region of large edge lengths, i.e. when
r\to\infty
. We then showed that the path integral is convergent for sufficiently large values of the parameter
p
.

If we combine bosonic and fermionic matter, as in the case of the SM, the short distance divergencies of the bosonic fields are cured by the fermionic fields, since in the SM the triple number of fermionic fields components is greater than the number of bosonic fields components, see (
68
). The long-distance divergencies of the fermionic fields are cured by a choice of a sufficiently large value for
p
, which in the SM case is given by
p>46,5
.

The path integral for GR coupled to the SM may be convergent for a lower value of
p
than
46,5
, but in order to explore this, one needs to find more powerfull techniques than the ones used here. However, at this stage it is more important to show that an interval of values of
p
exists which ensures the convergence of the path integral. Consequently, the PLQG theory presented here is the first example of a mathematically complete QG theory. It is a complete QG theory in the sense that it is finite, and the corresponding effective action has the correct classical limit and there is a semiclassical expansion that corresponds to a perturbative QFT quantization of GR coupled to the SM. This semiclassical expansion is given by the usual QFT perturbative expansion around a background metric, with a physical cutoff determined by the average edge length in the local patch
T(B_{3}\times I)
.

Note that the string theory is also a finite QG theory, but the classical limit is a 10-dimensional supergravity theory, so that one has to show that there is a compactification and a symmetry breaking such that we see only the SM particles at the LHC experiments. In the case of LQG, the spin foam models can be also made finite on a PL spacetime
[
12
]
. However, the classical limit of the spin foam effective action is not the Regge action, but it is given by the area-Regge action
[
23
]
. Furthermore, it is not known how to couple the fermionic matter to a SF model.

The question whether a PL quantum gravity (PLQG) theory is realized in Nature or not, can be decided by experiments and observations. At the moment there are no QG phenomena which can be studied in an experiment, but it is concievable that in a not so distant future, an elementary particle collider of the energies of 100 TeV or 1000 TeV will be built. One will then probe the distancies of order
10^{-22}
m, which could be close enough to the average edge length in the corresponding local patch
T(B_{3}\times I)
. In this case one could detect the deviations from the smooth spacetime QFT scattering results.

Another type of tests for a PLQG theory would be a comparison of the present cosmological observations to the corresponding theoretical predictions. For example, in
[
16
,
17
]
it was shown that the observed value of the cosmological constant (CC) belongs to the interval of possible values for the CC in a PLQG theory. Note that in the proof given in
[
16
,
17
]
, the fundamental assumption was the finitenness of the path integral for gravity and matter. Hence our main result closes the gap in the proof that the PLQG CC spectrum contains the observed value of the CC.

Note that in the case of string theory, proving that the observed CC value belongs to the CC spectrum is a complicated problem, because a natural value for the string CC is negative and the spectrum is discrete
[
24
]
. That is why a complete proof in string theory has not been achieved yet, and only the plausibility arguments were given.

As far as the question why the CC takes the value that we observe, that is a more difficult question, and answering this question would require a wider theoretical framework than the one used in this paper. Note that the formulation of PLQG used in this paper has three free parameters
(L_{0},l_{0},p)
, but one can also add the fourth parameter
\Lambda_{c}
, which corresponds to the classical CC term in the action. The demonstration that a PLQG theory with the SM matter is finite for
p>46,5
, gives us the first example of a simple and mathematically complete theory of quantum gravity.
