Abstract
. Drawing from the theory of stochastic differential equations, we introduce a novel sampling method for known distributions and a new algorithm for diffusion generative models with unknown distributions. Our approach is inspired by the concept of the reverse diffusion process, widely adopted in diffusion generative models. Additionally, we derive the explicit convergence rate based on the smooth ODE flow. For diffusion generative models and sampling, we establish a dimension-free particle approximation convergence result. Numerical experiments demonstrate the effectiveness of our method. Notably, unlike the traditional Langevin method, our sampling method does not require any regularity assumptions about the density function of the target distribution. Furthermore, we also apply our method to optimization problems.



1.
Introduction

1.1.
Background

In statistics, machine learning, and data science, two fundamental challenges are commonly encountered: drawing samples from a known distribution and learning an unknown distribution from observed data to generate new samples.

For the former problem, when we have a well-defined probability distribution (e.g., Gaussian, binomial, exponential) with known parameters, we can generate random samples that follow this distribution. This process is essential for tasks like Monte Carlo simulations, hypothesis testing, and generating synthetic data for modeling purposes. Nowadays, various algorithms exist for sampling from known distributions, depending on the nature of the distribution. For example, the inverse transform method, rejection sampling, and Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) methods such as the Metropolis-Hastings algorithm and Gibbs sampling are commonly used techniques for generating samples from different types of distributions (see
[
22
,
26
]
).

For the latter problem, the goal is to infer the underlying probability distribution that generated a given dataset without prior knowledge of the distribution’s form. This process involves using statistical techniques and algorithms to estimate the parameters or structure of the distribution based on the observed data. Methods for learning unknown distributions include parametric and non-parametric approaches. Parametric methods assume a specific functional form for the distribution (e.g., Gaussian, Poisson) and estimate the parameters that best fit the data. Non-parametric methods, on the other hand, make fewer assumptions about the distribution’s shape and instead focus on estimating the distribution directly from the data, often using techniques like kernel density estimation or histogram methods (see
[
14
,
25
,
17
,
3
]
).

In recent years, diffusion generative models have emerged as powerful frameworks for generating high-quality synthetic data,
particularly in the realm of image generation. These models operate by iteratively transforming a simple noise distribution into
a complex data distribution, effectively reversing a diffusion process.
Diffusion generative models leverage the principles of probabilistic modeling and stochastic processes to generate data that closely mimics real-world distributions. Introduced by Sohl-Dickstein et al. in 2015
[
8
]

and popularized by subsequent works such as those by Ho et al. in 2020
[
18
]
,
these models have demonstrated remarkable success in various generative tasks, particularly in generating high-quality images.

In these contexts, two notable probabilistic generative models have been developed: Score Matching with Langevin Dynamics (SMLD), introduced by Song and Ermon in 2019
[
29
]
, and Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Modeling (DDPM), introduced by Ho et al. in 2020
[
18
]
.
Both models provide robust frameworks for generative modeling.
The SMLD method estimates the score function (i.e., the gradient of the log probability density with respect to the data) at each noise scale and uses Langevin dynamics to sample from a sequence of decreasing noise scales during generation. On the other hand, the DDPM method aims to capture the data distribution by progressively transforming a noise distribution into the target data distribution through an iterative diffusion process.

Since their introduction, numerous studies have explored and expanded upon diffusion generative models. Notably, Song et al. in 2021
[
28
]

proposed a general score-based generative model using stochastic differential equations (SDEs) to unify the methods of SMLD and DDPM.
The crucial idea is to learn the score function
(t,x)\mapsto\nabla_{x}p_{t}(x)
through a forward SDE by slowly injecting noise into the data.
This approach allows solving a reverse-time SDE (see
[
2
]
and
[
16
]
) to generate high-quality samples starting from a normal distribution.
See below for an explanation of the basic ideas (adapted from
[
28
, Figure 1]
).

.

To learn the score function using neural networks, well-known methods typically employ black-box approaches that involve constructing suitable loss functions. Recent advancements have also introduced new algorithms for sampling and improved diffusion models, further enhancing the quality and efficiency of generative modeling (cf.
[
24
]
and the survey paper
[

]
). Additionally, several works focus on the convergence analysis of diffusion generative models (see
[

).

1.2.
Main results

Inspired by the reversed diffusion process, we propose a more explicit probability flow ordinary differential equation (ODE) as in
[
28
]
to generate samples. The key observation is that through the probability flow of the ODE, we can establish a connection between any probability distribution and the standard normal distribution. Since the coefficient of our ODE depends explicitly on the distribution, our method can be considered a white-box approach, in contrast to methods that learn the score function based on data.

More precisely, let
\mu_{0}
be any given distribution in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
.
To generate a sample, it suffices to solve the following probability flow ODE:

\displaystyle\dot{Y}_{t}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(\eta-Y_{t})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Big\|\frac{Y_{t}-t\eta}{1-t}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]}{(1-t)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Big\|\frac{Y_{t}-t\eta}{1-t}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]}=:b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t}),\quad Y_{0}\sim N(0,\mathbb{I}_{d}),

(1.1)

where the initial value
Y_{0}
is an independent
d
-dimensional standard normal distribution, for
q\in[1,\infty]
,
\|\cdot\|_{q}
stands for the usual
\ell^{q}
-norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and the expectation is taken only with respect to
\eta
. In particular, we show that the law of
Y_{t}
weakly converges to
\mu_{0}
as
t\uparrow 1
.

The following theorem is a combination of Lemma
2.7
and Theorem
2.8
below.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose that
\mu_{0}\in{\mathcal{P}}({\mathbb{R}}^{d})
has compact support and
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
. Then for each
t\in[0,1)
,

y\mapsto b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth and for all
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\langle x-y,b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)\rangle\leqslant\left[\frac{t\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|^{2}_{2}}{(1-t)^{2}}-1\right]\frac{\|x-y\|^{2}_{2}}{1-t},

(1.2)

and for each
Y_{0}\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
independent of
\eta
,
there is a unique solution
Y_{t}
to ODE (
1.1
) on
[0,1)
.
Moreover, let
\mu_{t}
be the law of
Y_{t}
and

{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
be the usual Wasserstein metric, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{2}(\mu_{t},\mu_{0})\leqslant\left(\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{2}+\sqrt{d}\right)(1-t),

(1.3)

and for any
M>0
and
t\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}\geqslant t\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{\infty}+\sqrt{2}(1-t)M\right)\leqslant 1-\left(1-\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{M}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{d}.

(1.4)

Remark 1.2
.

From (
1.4
), one sees that as
t
approaches
1
, with probability 1,

\varlimsup_{t\to 1}\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{\infty}.

An open question here is to show that
Y_{t}
almost surely converges to a random variable
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
as
t\to 1
, rather than merely exhibiting weak convergence as in (
1.3
). Numerical experiments indicate that when
\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\delta_{a_{i}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)
is a discrete distribution, for any sample
Y_{0}\sim N(0,\mathbb{I}_{d})
,
Y_{t}
always converges to some point
a_{i}
as
t\to 1
. See Section 5 below.

In practical applications, we usually need to consider the particle approximation of ODE (
1.1
):

\displaystyle\dot{Y}^{N}_{t}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[(\eta_{j}-Y^{N}_{t})\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Big\|\frac{Y^{N}_{t}-t\eta_{j}}{1-t}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]}{(1-t)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\Big\|\frac{Y^{N}_{t}-t\eta_{j}}{1-t}\Big\|_{2}^{2}\right)\right]},\quad Y^{N}_{0}=Y_{0}\sim N(0,\mathbb{I}_{d}),

(1.5)

where
\{\eta_{j},j=1,\cdots,N\}
is a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with common distribution
\mu_{0}
and it is also independent of
Y_{0}
. One may ask whether
Y^{N}_{t}
converges to
Y_{t}
. In this aspect,
we have the following dimension-free convergence result,
which has independent interest (see Theorem
3.2
).

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
K:=\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{2}<\infty
. Then it holds that for all
t\in[0,1)
,

\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\sqrt{N}{\mathbb{E}}\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}\right)\leqslant 2Kt\mathrm{e}^{(Kt/(1-t))^{2}}.

Remark 1.4
.

It appears that the aforementioned result provides the initial quantitative estimate for approximating generative models of diffusions. Although the exponential component grows rapidly as
t\to 1
, in practical numerical experiments,
we typically set
t<1
, such as
t=0.9
. Additionally, through scaling techniques, we can always choose a small
K
, for instance,
K=0.1
, ensuring that the exponential part remains less than 1.

Now we explain how to sample from a given distribution
\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\propto f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
using the ODE (
1.1
), where

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is bounded by
\Lambda
and has support in
\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\}
.

In this special case, let
\xi
be the uniformly distributed random variable in

\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\}
, and
\xi^{\prime}\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
.
Then we can write for
t\in(0,1)

b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[(\xi-y)\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{y-t\xi}{1-t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)f(\xi)\right]}{(1-t)\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\left\|\frac{y-t\xi}{1-t}\right\|_{2}^{2}\right)f(\xi)\right]},

and by the integration by parts, an alternative expression is

b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}[(y-\xi^{\prime})f((y-(1-t)\xi^{\prime})/t)]}{t{\mathbb{E}}f((y-(1-t)\xi^{\prime})/t)}.

Since it easily generates a sequence of i.i.d. uniformly or normally distributed random variables, we can calculate
b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)
using the Monte Carlo method as shown in (
1.5
).
Thus, we can solve a similar ODE as in (
1.5
) using the classical Euler discretization method. Alternatively, under (
1.2
), we can employ the recently developed Poisson’s discretization approximation for the ODE (
1.5
) as presented in
[
32
]
. Note that the method in
[
32
]
does not require any time regularity of
b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)
.

Nowadays, the popular sampling method is the Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), whose stationary distribution is the target distribution, based on ergodicity theory. Note that MCMC sampling is also a key component in the popular simulated annealing technique for both discrete and continuous optimization. Theoretically, the MCMC method requires the target density function to have some smoothness to ensure the iterations converge.
Compared with MCMC, our new method does not make any regularity assumptions about the density function,
and it is easy to be realized. Numerical experiments exhibit robust sampling ability.

1.3.
Related works

Since the seminal works of
[
18
]
and
[
28
]
, numerous papers have explored diffusion generative models (for an overview, see the recent survey
[
31
]
and
[
6
]
). Notably, Albergo et al.
[
1
]
introduced a general framework for stochastic interpolants that unifies various diffusion models. Specifically, let
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
be two density functions in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and let
(x_{0},x_{1})
be a coupling of
(\rho_{0},\rho_{1})
, represented as
\mathbb{R}^{2d}
-valued random variables with marginal distributions
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
. Define
z
as a normally distributed variable. The stochastic interpolants between
x_{0}
and
x_{1}
are defined by

x_{t}:=I(t,x_{0},x_{1})+\gamma(t)z,\quad t\in[0,1],

where
I\in C^{2}([0,1];(C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}))^{d})
satisfies
I(0,x_{0},x_{1})=x_{0}
,
I(1,x_{0},x_{1})=x_{1}
, and

|\partial_{t}I(t,x_{0},x_{1})|\leqslant C|x_{0}-x_{1}|,

while
\gamma:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)
satisfies
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=0
,
\gamma(t)>0
for
t\in(0,1)
, and
\gamma^{2}\in C^{2}([0,1])
. Under certain regularity assumptions on
\rho_{0}
and
\rho_{1}
, the authors in
[
1
, Theorem 1]
demonstrated that the density
\rho_{t}
of
x_{t}
satisfies the following Fokker-Planck equation:

\partial_{t}\rho+\text{div}(b\cdot\rho)=0,\quad b(t,x)=\mathbb{E}[\dot{x}_{t}|x_{t}=x].

Our equation in Lemma
2.4
below represents a special case of the aforementioned general equation. Unlike
[
1
, Theorem 1]
, we do not impose any regularity assumptions on the target distribution.
Following the general framework of
[
1
]
, Gao, Huang, and Jiao
[
13
]
studied the Gaussian interpolant flow and proved several analytic results for the associated ODE flows under various assumptions.

About the sampling from a given distribution, in a similar vein, Huang et al.
[
19
]
consider the Schrödinger-Föllmer diffusion (also known as the Föllmer process
[
11
,
9
]
):

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}=\nabla\log P_{1-t}f(Y_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad Y_{0}=0,

where
P_{t}f(x):=\mathbb{E}[f(x+W_{t})\mathrm{e}^{\|x+W_{t}\|_{2}^{2}/2}]
. It was shown that the law of
Y_{1}
is
\mu_{0}
.
Huang et al.
[
20
]
consider the reverse diffusion process:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}=[Y_{t}+2\nabla\log p_{T-t}(Y_{t})]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},

where
p_{t}
is the density of the forward Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=-X_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}
with
X_{0}\propto\mathrm{e}^{-f}
.
Another approach is based on stochastic localization
[
15
]
, which is:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}=u_{t}(Y_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sigma{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\quad Y_{0}=0,

where
u_{t}(y)=y/t+\sigma^{2}\nabla\log p_{t}(y)
and
p_{t}(y)=(2\pi t\sigma^{2})^{-d/2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|y-tx\|^{2}_{2}/(2t\sigma^{2})}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
. It was shown that the law of
Y_{t}
converges to
\mu_{0}
as
t\to\infty
.
The main difference between our sampling method and the above approaches is that we utilize the probability flow of ODEs rather than SDEs. Importantly, we have explicit convergence rates.

Since this paper primarily focuses on theoretical aspects, and my expertise lies more in theory than in practical algorithms, I will not provide a comparison between our algorithm and various well-known algorithms.

1.4.
Organization

This paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we first recall the basic idea of diffusion generative models used in
[
28
]
. Then, we present our main theoretical result, providing quantitative estimates about the probability flow based on ODE, which is important for devising an algorithm. In Section 3, we demonstrate how to generate samples randomly from a family of high-dimensional discretized distributions.
In Section 4, we apply our theoretical results to the sampling problem from a known distribution, illustrating the effectiveness of our method through various examples, including discontinuous density functions and multimodal distributions. In Section 5, we discuss potential applications in optimization problems based on sampling the density function.

2.1.
Reverse diffusion processes

We fix two continuous functions

\displaystyle{\mathord{{\rm d}}}X_{t}=-f_{t}X_{t}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t+\sqrt{2g_{t}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t},\ \ t\in[0,1),

X_{t}=\mathrm{e}^{-\int^{t}_{0}f_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s}X_{0}+\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-\int^{t}_{s}f_{r}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r}\sqrt{2g_{s}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}.

\displaystyle X_{t}=h_{t}X_{0}+h_{t}\int^{t}_{0}\sqrt{2g_{s}}/h_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{s}=:h_{t}X_{0}+\xi_{t}.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\bar{p}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=-\bar{g}_{t}\Delta\bar{p}_{t}(x)-\mathord{{\rm div}}(\bar{f}_{t}x\,\bar{p}_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=-\mathord{{\rm div}}((\bar{g}_{t}\nabla\log\bar{p}_{t}(x)+\bar{f}_{t}x)\bar{p}_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{t}\Delta\bar{p}_{t}(x)-\mathord{{\rm div}}(\bar{b}_{t}(x)\bar{p}_{t}(x)),

\epsilon_{t}:[0,1]\to[0,\infty)

\displaystyle\bar{b}_{t}(x):=(\bar{g}_{t}+\epsilon_{t})\boxed{\nabla\log\bar{p}_{t}(x)}+\bar{f}_{t}x.

(2.10)

In the literature of diffusion models, the function
\nabla\log\bar{p}_{t}(x)
is called the score function, which depends on the unknown distribution.
By the superposition principle (for example, see
[
27
, Theorem 1.5]
), formally,
\bar{p}_{t}(x)
will be the density of the following SDE:

{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}=\sqrt{2\epsilon_{t}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}W_{t}+\bar{b}_{t}(Y_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}t,\quad Y_{0}\sim N(0,\mathbb{I}_{d}).

In particular, if
\epsilon_{t}=\bar{g}_{t}
, then
(Y_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}
has the same law as the reverse diffusion process
(\bar{X}_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}

(cf.
[
2
, Theorem 2.1]
).

2.2.
Probability measure flows

Motivated by the above discussion,
in what follows, we fix a terminal time
T

\displaystyle\phi_{t}(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y),\ \ t\in(0,T),

(2.11)

where
\sigma_{t},\beta_{t}:[0,T)\to[0,\infty)

(H)

\sigma_{t}
is stricly decreasing and for any

,
\sigma^{\prime}_{t}

, and

\lim_{t\downarrow 0}\sigma_{t}=1,\ \ \lim_{t\uparrow T}\sigma_{t}=0,\ \ \beta_{t}=1-\sigma_{t}.

,

We first show the following simple lemma about the density function
\phi_{t}(x)

Lemma 2.4
.

\phi_{t}(x)
solves the following first order PDE:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(x)=\left(\log\sigma_{t}\right)^{\prime}\mathord{{\rm div}}([{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-x]\phi_{t}(x)),\ \ (t,x)\in(0,T)\times{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

(2.12)

where
{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)
is defined by

\displaystyle\boxed{{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x):=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\eta)\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\eta)}=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}},

(2.13)

and
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
, and
\Gamma_{t}(x,y):=\|x-\beta_{t}y\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t}).

Moreover, if we let
Y_{t}:=\beta_{t}\eta+\sigma_{t}\xi
, where
\xi\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
is independent with
\eta
, then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)={\mathbb{E}}(\eta|Y_{t}=x).

(2.14)

Proof.

First of all, recalling (
2.7
),
one sees that
\rho_{\sqrt{t}}(x)=(2\pi t)^{-d/2}\mathrm{e}^{-\|x\|^{2}_{2}/(2t)}
is the density of a standard Brownian motion in
{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
. Therefore,

\partial_{t}\rho_{\sqrt{t}}(x)=\tfrac{1}{2}\Delta\rho_{\sqrt{t}}(x),\ \ t>0,\ \ x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

Now by the chain rule and
\beta_{t}=1-\sigma_{t}
, we have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{(\sigma^{2}_{t})^{\prime}}{2}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\Delta\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)-\beta_{t}^{\prime}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\cdot\nabla\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\sigma_{t}\Delta\phi_{t}(x)+\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\mathord{{\rm div}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)

\displaystyle=\mathord{{\rm div}}\left[\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\sigma_{t}\nabla\phi_{t}(x)+\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right].

On the other hand, noting that by definition (
2.7
),

\displaystyle\nabla\phi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{\beta_{t}y-x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}\Big)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)-\frac{x\phi_{t}(x)}{\sigma^{2}_{t}},

we further have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\mathord{{\rm div}}\left[\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)-x\phi_{t}(x)\right].

From this we derive the desired equation (
2.12
).
For (
2.14
), it is direct by definition since the density of
Y_{t}
is given by
\phi_{t}(x)={\mathbb{E}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\eta)
.
∎

Remark 2.5
.

Compared with (

), the coefficient of PDE (

) depends on the unknown distribution

in an explicit way.
Moreover, for any
\epsilon_{t}
, we also have

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\phi_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{t}\Delta\phi_{t}(x)+\mathord{{\rm div}}(((\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}[{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-x]-\epsilon_{t}\nabla\log\phi_{t}(x))\phi_{t}(x))

\displaystyle=\epsilon_{t}\Delta\phi_{t}(x)+\mathord{{\rm div}}\Big(\sigma^{-2}_{t}\Big[\big(\sigma_{t}\sigma^{\prime}_{t}-\epsilon_{t}\beta_{t}\big){\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)+\big(\epsilon_{t}-\sigma_{t}\sigma^{\prime}_{t}\big)x\Big]\phi_{t}(x)\Big).

(2.15)

In particular, if we choose
\epsilon_{t}=\sigma_{t}\sigma^{\prime}_{t}/\beta_{t}<0
and let
p_{t}(x):=\phi_{1-t}(x)
and
g_{t}:=-\epsilon_{1-t}
,

f_{t}:=\frac{\epsilon_{1-t}-\sigma_{1-t}\sigma^{\prime}_{1-t}}{\sigma^{2}_{1-t}}=\frac{\sigma^{\prime}_{1-t}}{\beta_{1-t}},

then (
2.15
) becomes the forward heat equation associated with the adding noise process

\partial_{t}p_{t}(x)=g_{t}\Delta p_{t}(x)+\mathord{{\rm div}}(f_{t}xp_{t}(x)).

We have the following derivative estimate for nonlinear function
{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)
given in (
2.13
).

Lemma 2.6
.

Let
\Gamma_{t}(x,y):=\|x-\beta_{t}y\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})
and
V^{\otimes k}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes k}
be the
k
-order tensor product of a vector
V\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
.
Let
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
.
For any
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, if we write

{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x):=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes k}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}\in({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes k},

then it holds that

\nabla{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)=\frac{\beta_{t}({\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}(x)-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x))}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}.

In particular,
\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\otimes{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
is symmetric and positive definite, and for any
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\langle z\cdot\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x),z\rangle=\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\left[\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle z,\eta\rangle^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}-\left(\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle z,\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}\right)^{2}\right].

(2.16)

Moreover, if
\eta
is bounded, then for any
q\in[1,\infty]
and all
t\in(0,T)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)\|_{q}\leqslant 2\Big(\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\Big)\sup_{\omega,\omega^{\prime}}\|\eta(\omega)\otimes\eta(\omega^{\prime})\|_{q},

(2.17)

where
\|\cdot\|_{q}
stands for the
\ell^{q}
-norm in
({\mathbb{R}}^{d})^{\otimes 2}
(see (
2.1
)).

Proof.

By the chain rule we have

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes k}\nabla\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}-\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes k}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)\otimes{\mathbb{E}}\nabla\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}{({\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)})^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes k}\nabla\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\nabla\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}.

Noting that

\nabla_{x}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,y)}=\left(\frac{\beta_{t}y-x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\right)\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,y)},

we further have

\displaystyle\nabla{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes k}\otimes(\beta_{t}\eta-x)\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)}{\sigma^{2}_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes\frac{{\mathbb{E}}((\beta_{t}\eta-x)\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)})}{\sigma^{2}_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes(k+1)}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)-{\mathbb{E}}\big(\eta^{\otimes k}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\big)\otimes x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}}-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes\left(\frac{\beta_{t}{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x)-x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{t}{\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}(x)-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes\left(\frac{\beta_{t}{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x)-x}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\right)=\frac{\beta_{t}({\mathcal{I}}_{k+1}(x)-{\mathcal{I}}_{k}(x)\otimes{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x))}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}.

Equality (
2.16
) and estimate (
2.17
) follow by

\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)=\nabla{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x)=\frac{\beta_{t}({\mathcal{I}}_{2}(x)-{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x)\otimes{\mathcal{I}}_{1}(x))}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

2.3.
Random flows of ODEs

In this subsection we consider the random flow determined by the following ODE starting from a normal distribution:

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y_{t}}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}=(\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}[Y_{t}-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t})]=b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t}),\ \ Y_{0}\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d}),

(2.18)

where

\displaystyle b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x):=(\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}[x-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)].

(2.19)

We have the following simple lemma.

Lemma 2.7
.

For any
x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, it holds that

\langle x-y,b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)\rangle\leqslant(\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}\|x-y\|^{2}_{2}\left[1-\beta_{t}\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{2}^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{t}\right].

Proof.

Note that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)=(x-y)\cdot\int^{1}_{0}\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(\theta x+(1-\theta)y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}\theta.

By (
2.16
), we have

\langle x-y,{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(y)\rangle\leqslant\beta_{t}\|x-y\|^{2}_{2}\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{2}^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{t}.

The desired estimate follows by
(\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}<0
.
∎

The following is the main theoretical result of this section.

Theorem 2.8
.

Let
Y_{0}=(Y^{1}_{0},\cdots,Y^{d}_{0})
, where
Y^{i}_{0}\sim N(0,1)
is a family of i.i.d. standard normal random variables.
Let
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
be independent with
Y_{0}
and satisfy that for some
q\in[1,\infty]
,

\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{q}\leqslant K.

Under
(H)
, there is a unique solution
Y_{t}
to ODE (
2.18
) before reaching the terminal time
T
.
Moreover, for each
t\in[0,T)
, the law
\mu_{t}
of
Y_{t}
has a density given by
\phi_{t}(x)
, and for any
p\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{q,p}(\mu_{t},\mu_{0})\leqslant\left[K+\left({\mathbb{E}}\|Y_{0}\|^{p}_{q}\right)^{1/p}\right]\sigma_{t},\ \ t\in[0,T),

(2.20)

and for all
t\in[0,T)
and
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\|Y_{t}-\beta_{t}z\|_{q}\leqslant\sigma_{t}\|Y_{0}\|_{q}+\beta_{t}\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)-z\|_{q}.

(2.21)

In particular, for any
M>0
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\beta_{t}\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{\infty}+\sigma_{t}M\right)\leqslant 1-(1-{\rm erfc}(M/\sqrt{2}))^{d}=:\mathfrak{p}(M,d),

(2.22)

where
{\rm erfc}(r):=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{r}^{\infty}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

Proof.

We divide the proof into four steps.

(Step 1).
Since
\mu_{0}
has compact support, by Lemma
2.6
we have

\displaystyle\|\nabla b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|\frac{{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}}{\sigma_{t}}\right|\left[\|\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)\|_{2}+\sqrt{d}\right]\leqslant C_{d}\left|\frac{{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}}{\sigma_{t}}\right|\left[\Big(\frac{\beta_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\Big)K^{2}+1\right].

In particular, by
(H)
, for each
t_{0}<T
, there is a constant
C=C(d,t_{0})>0
such that

\sup_{t\in[0,t_{0}]}\left(\|b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(0)\|_{2}+\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\|\nabla b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)\|_{2}\right)\leqslant C.

Hence, by the classical theory of ODE, for each starting point
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, there is a unique solution
X_{t}=X_{t}(x)
to ODE

X_{t}(x)=x+\int^{t}_{0}b^{\mu_{0}}_{s}(X_{s}(x)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,\ \ t\in[0,T).

Moreover,
x\mapsto X_{t}(x)
forms a smooth diffemorphism flow and the Jacobi flow
J_{t}(x):=\nabla X_{t}(x)
satisfies

J_{t}(x)={\mathbb{I}}+\int^{t}_{0}\nabla b^{\mu_{0}}_{s}(X_{s}(x))J_{s}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

In particular, the solution of ODE (
2.18
) starting from the normal distribution
Y_{0}
is given by

Y_{t}=X_{t}(Y_{0}).

Now, for any bounded measurable
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, by the change of variable, we have

{\mathbb{E}}f(Y_{t})=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(X_{t}(x))\rho_{1}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)\rho_{1}(X^{-1}_{t}(x))\det(\nabla X^{-1}_{t}(x)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,

which implies that
Y_{t}
has a density
\psi_{t}(x)
given by

\psi_{t}(x)=\rho_{1}(X^{-1}_{t}(x))\det(\nabla X^{-1}_{t}(x)).

(Step 2).
By the chain rule, for any
f\in C^{1}_{b}({\mathbb{R}}^{2})
, we also have

f(Y_{t})=f(Y_{0})+\int^{t}_{0}b^{\mu_{0}}_{s}(Y_{s})\cdot\nabla f(Y_{s}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Taking expectations and by the arbitrariness of
f
, we get

\psi_{t}(x)=\psi_{0}(x)-\int^{t}_{0}\mathord{{\rm div}}(b^{\mu_{0}}_{s}(x)\psi_{s}(x)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Let
h_{t}(x)=\psi_{t}(x)-\phi_{t}(x)
. Since
\psi_{0}=\phi_{0}=\rho_{1}
, by (
2.19
) and (
2.12
), we have

h_{t}(x)=-\int^{t}_{0}\mathord{{\rm div}}(b^{\mu_{0}}_{s}(x)h_{s}(x)){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

In particular, by the integration by parts,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|h_{t}(x)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathord{{\rm div}}(b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)h_{t}(x))h_{t}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)h_{t}(x))\nabla h_{t}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)\nabla|h_{t}(x)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=-\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathord{{\rm div}}b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)|h_{t}(x)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,

and

\partial_{t}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|h_{t}(x)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\leqslant\|\mathord{{\rm div}}b^{\mu_{0}}_{t}\|_{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|h_{t}(x)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

By Gronwall’s inequality, we get
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}|h_{t}(x)|^{2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=0
for each
t\in[0,T)
. Hence,

\psi_{t}(x)=\phi_{t}(x),\ \ t\in[0,T).

On the other hand, by the expression (
2.11
),
\phi_{t}(x)
is the density of
\beta_{t}\eta+\sigma_{t}\xi,
where
\xi\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
.
Estimate (
3.7
) now follows by definition (
2.6
) (see Lemma
2.1
).

(Step 3).
To show the uniform estimate (
2.21
), for
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, we introduce

Z_{t}(z):=(Y_{t}-\beta_{t}z)/\sigma_{t}.

By the chain rule, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z_{t}(z)}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}=\frac{Y_{t}^{\prime}-\beta_{t}^{\prime}z}{\sigma_{t}}-\frac{(Y_{t}-\beta_{t}z)\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

Since
1-\beta_{t}=\sigma_{t}
, by (
2.18
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z_{t}(z)}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}[Y_{t}-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t})]+\sigma^{\prime}_{t}z}{\sigma_{t}}-\frac{(Y_{t}-\beta_{t}z)\sigma_{t}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}=\frac{\sigma_{t}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}[z-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t})].

Noting that

\Gamma_{t}(Y_{t},z)=\|Y_{t}-\beta_{t}z\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})=\|Z_{t}(z)\|_{2}^{2}/2,

and recalling the definition (
2.13
), we have for each
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Z_{t}(z)}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}=\left(\frac{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\right)\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big((z-\eta)\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(Y_{t},\eta)}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(Y_{t},\eta)}}=\left(\frac{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}{\sigma^{2}_{t}}\right)\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big((z-\eta)\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{t}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{t}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}},

where the expectation is taken with respect to
\eta
.
In other words, for each
t\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle Z_{t}(z)=Y_{0}+\int^{t}_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{s}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big((z-\eta)\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(2.23)

Hence,

\displaystyle\|Z_{t}(z)\|_{q}

\displaystyle\leqslant\|Y_{0}\|_{q}+\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)-z\|_{q}\int^{t}_{0}\left(-\frac{\sigma_{s}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\|Y_{0}\|_{q}+\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)-z\|_{q}\left(\frac{1}{\sigma_{t}}-\frac{1}{\sigma_{0}}\right),

which gives (
2.21
).

(Step 4).
By (
2.21
) with
z=0
, we have

\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}\leqslant\sigma_{t}\|Y_{0}\|_{\infty}+\beta_{t}\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{\infty}.

Hence, by the independence of
Y^{1}_{0},\cdots,Y^{d}_{0}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|Y_{t}\|_{\infty}\geqslant\beta_{t}\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{\infty}+\sigma_{t}M\right)\leqslant{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|Y_{0}\|_{\infty}\geqslant M\right)

\displaystyle\qquad=1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(\|Y_{0}\|_{\infty}<M\right)=1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(|Y^{1}_{0}|<M\right)^{d}=1-(1-{\mathbb{P}}\left(|Y^{1}_{0}|\geqslant M\right))^{d}.

Note that

{\mathbb{P}}(|Y^{1}_{0}|\geqslant M)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int^{\infty}_{M}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}/2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int^{\infty}_{M/\sqrt{2}}\mathrm{e}^{-x^{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x={\rm erfc}(M/\sqrt{2}).

The desired estimate (
2.22
) now follows.
∎

Next starting from (
2.23
), we derive an useful equation for
{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{t}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}
.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
g_{t}:={\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(Y_{t},\eta)}
, where
\Gamma_{t}(x,y):=\|x-\beta_{t}y\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})
and the expectation is only taken with respect to
\eta
, not for
Y_{t}
.
Then
g_{0}=\mathrm{e}^{-\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}/2}
and it holds that

\displaystyle g_{t}^{\prime}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\beta_{t}}{\sigma^{3}_{t}}\right)\left[\Lambda_{2}(t,Y_{t})-\frac{\|\Lambda_{1}(t,Y_{t})\|^{2}_{2}}{g_{t}}\right],

(2.24)

where

\displaystyle\Lambda_{1}(t,x):={\mathbb{E}}\left[\eta\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\right],\ \ \Lambda_{2}(t,x):={\mathbb{E}}\left[\|\eta\|^{2}_{2}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\eta)}\right].

(2.25)

In particular, suppose that
\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{2}\leqslant K
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-[\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}+(K\beta_{t}/\sigma_{t})^{2}]/2}\leqslant g_{t}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}/2},\ \ t\in[0,T).

(2.26)

Proof.

Recall
Z_{t}(z):=(Y_{t}-\beta_{t}z)/\sigma_{t}.

By (
2.23
) and the chain rule, we have for each
z\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

\|Z_{t}(z)\|_{2}^{2}/2=\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}/2+\int^{t}_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{s}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle Z_{s}(z),z-\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

and

\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{t}(z)\|_{2}^{2}/2}=\mathrm{e}^{-\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}/2}-\int^{t}_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{s}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle Z_{s}(z),z-\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-(\|Z_{s}(z)\|_{2}^{2}+\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2})/2}\big)}{{\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2}/2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

Let
\tilde{\eta}
be an independent copy of
\eta
, which is also independent with
Y_{0}
. Then replacing the above
z
by
\tilde{\eta}
and taking expectations with respect to
\tilde{\eta}
, we obtain

\displaystyle g_{t}={\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{-\|Z_{t}(\tilde{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}/2}=\mathrm{e}^{-\|Y_{0}\|_{2}^{2}/2}-\int^{t}_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{s}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}\right)\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta}),\tilde{\eta}-\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-(\|Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}+\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2})/2}\big)}{g_{s}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(2.27)

Recalling that
Z_{t}(\tilde{\eta}):=(Y_{t}-\beta_{t}\tilde{\eta})/\sigma_{t}
and
\eta,\tilde{\eta}
are independent, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\big(\langle Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta}),\tilde{\eta}-\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-(\|Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}+\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2})/2}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sigma_{s}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle Y_{s}-\beta_{s}\tilde{\eta},\tilde{\eta}-\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-(\|Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}+\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2})/2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sigma_{s}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle\tilde{\eta},\eta-\tilde{\eta}\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-(\|Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}+\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2})/2}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta_{s}}{\sigma_{s}}\left[\|\Lambda_{1}(t,Y_{s})\|^{2}_{2}-\Lambda_{2}(t,Y_{s})g_{s}\right],

where we have used that (the expectations are taken only with respect to
\tilde{\eta}
and
\eta
)

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\langle Y_{s},\tilde{\eta}-\eta\rangle\mathrm{e}^{-(\|Z_{s}(\tilde{\eta})\|_{2}^{2}+\|Z_{s}(\eta)\|_{2}^{2})/2}\right]=0.

Substituting it into (
2.27
), we obtain equation (
2.24
).

To prove (
2.26
), note that by Hölder’s inequality,

\|\Lambda_{1}(t,Y_{t})\|^{2}_{2}\leqslant g_{t}\Lambda_{2}(t,Y_{t}).

Since
\sigma^{\prime}_{t}<0
and
\sup_{\omega}\|\eta(\omega)\|_{2}\leqslant K
, we have

\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\beta_{t}}{\sigma^{3}_{t}}\right)K^{2}\,g_{t}\leqslant g^{\prime}_{t}\leqslant 0.

Solving this differential inequality, we derive that

\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\int^{t}_{0}\sigma_{s}^{\prime}\beta_{s}/\sigma^{3}_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s}g_{0}\leqslant g_{t}\leqslant g_{0},\ \ t\in(0,T).

Since
\beta_{t}=1-\sigma_{t}
, by the change of variable
\sigma_{s}=r
, it is easy to see that

\displaystyle\int^{t}_{0}\sigma_{s}^{\prime}\beta_{s}/\sigma^{3}_{s}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\int^{\sigma_{0}}_{\sigma_{t}}(1-r)/r^{3}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=-\frac{\beta^{2}_{t}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}.

(2.28)

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 2.12
.

By using the definitions (
2.25

Y^{\prime}_{t}=\left(\frac{\sigma_{t}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{t}}\right)\left[Y_{t}-\frac{\Lambda_{1}(t,Y_{t})}{g_{t}}\right].

In other words,
(Y_{t},g_{t})
solves the following ODE system:

\left\{\begin{aligned} Y_{t}&=Y_{0}+\int^{t}_{0}\left(\frac{\sigma_{s}^{\prime}}{\sigma_{s}}\right)\left[Y_{s}-\frac{\Lambda_{1}(s,Y_{s})}{g_{s}}\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,\\

3.
Application to diffusion generative models

In this section we aim to introduce an algorithm for generating a sample from the dataset

{\mathcal{X}}_{0}=\{\eta^{1}_{0},\cdots,\eta^{N}_{0}\},

where each data
\eta^{j}_{0}
in
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
obeys the same distribution
\mu_{0}
. We suppose that

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mu_{0}$ has support in $\big\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\big\}$}.

(3.1)

Let
\mu^{N}_{0}
be the empirical measure of
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y):=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\delta_{\eta^{j}_{0}}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y).

(3.2)

Consider the particle approximation coefficients:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}^{N}}_{t}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}.

(3.3)

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\phi_{t}(x)
be defined by (
2.11
) and
{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)
be defined by (
2.13
).
We have

\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu^{N}_{0}}_{t}(x)\|_{2}\leqslant{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{t}(x)/\phi_{t}(x),

where

\displaystyle{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{t}(x):=\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right\|_{2}+K\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right|.

(3.4)

Moreover, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}^{N}}_{t}(x)\|_{2}\leqslant 2\beta_{t}K^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{t}.

(3.5)

Proof.

By definition we have

\displaystyle\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu^{N}_{0}}_{t}(x)\|_{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}-\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}\right\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right\|_{2}}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}

\displaystyle+\frac{\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right\|_{2}}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu^{N}_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right\|_{2}}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)}+\frac{K\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right|}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)},

which gives the first estimate. As for (
3.5
), it follows by (
2.17
).
∎

Let
Y_{0}\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
be independent of
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
.
We consider the following approximation ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y^{N}_{t}}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}=\left(\frac{{\sigma^{\prime}_{t}}}{\sigma_{t}}\right)[Y^{N}_{t}-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu^{N}_{0}}_{t}(Y^{N}_{t})]=b^{N}_{t}(Y^{N}_{t}),\ \ Y^{N}_{0}=Y_{0}.

(3.6)

We have the following convergence estimate.

Theorem 3.2
.

Under
(H)
and (
3.1
), it holds that for all
t\in[0,T)
,

\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\sqrt{N}{\mathbb{E}}\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}\right)\leqslant 2K\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta_{t}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})},

where the expectation is taken with respect to
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and
Y_{0}
.

Proof.

Note that

Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}=\int^{t}_{0}(b_{s}(Y_{s})-b^{N}_{s}(Y^{N}_{s})){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\int^{t}_{0}(b_{s}(Y_{s})-b^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s+\int^{t}_{0}(b^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})-b^{N}_{s}(Y^{N}_{s})){\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

By the chain rule and Lemmas
2.7
and
3.1
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}

\displaystyle=\frac{\langle Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t},b_{t}(Y_{t})-b^{N}_{t}(Y_{t})\rangle+\langle Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t},b^{N}_{t}(Y_{t})-b^{N}_{t}(Y^{N}_{t})\rangle}{\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|\sigma_{t}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{t}}\left(\|{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t})-{\mathcal{D}}^{\mu^{N}_{0}}_{t}(Y_{t})\|_{2}+\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}\left[K^{2}\beta_{t}/\sigma^{2}_{t}-1\right]\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{|\sigma_{t}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{t}}\left(\frac{{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{t}(Y_{t})}{\phi_{t}(Y_{t})}+\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}\left[K^{2}\beta_{t}/\sigma^{2}_{t}-1\right]\right).

Note that by (
2.28
),

\int^{t}_{0}\frac{|\sigma_{s}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{s}}\left[K^{2}\beta_{s}/\sigma^{2}_{s}-1\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=K^{2}\beta^{2}_{t}/(2\sigma_{t}^{2})+\log\sigma_{t}.

By Gronwall’s inequality, we get

\displaystyle\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{\int^{t}_{s}\frac{|\sigma_{r}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{r}}\left[K^{2}\beta_{r}/\sigma^{2}_{r}-1\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r}\frac{|\sigma_{s}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{s}}\frac{{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})}{\phi_{s}(Y_{s})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s

\displaystyle=\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta^{2}_{t}/(2\sigma_{t}^{2})}\sigma_{t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-K^{2}\beta^{2}_{s}/(2\sigma_{s}^{2})}\frac{|\sigma_{s}^{\prime}|}{\sigma^{2}_{s}}\frac{{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})}{\phi_{s}(Y_{s})}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s,

which implies by taking expectations that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta^{2}_{t}/(2\sigma_{t}^{2})}\sigma_{t}\int^{t}_{0}\mathrm{e}^{-K^{2}\beta^{2}_{s}/(2\sigma_{s}^{2})}\frac{|\sigma_{s}^{\prime}|}{\sigma^{2}_{s}}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})}{\phi_{s}(Y_{s})}\right]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s.

(3.7)

Noting that
Y_{s}
is independent with
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and has density
\phi_{s}(x)
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})}{\phi_{s}(Y_{s})}\right]=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}[{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(x)]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x,

where
{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}
stands for the expectation with respect to
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
.
Since for a function
\varphi:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
,

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\varphi(y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{1}{N}\left({\mathbb{E}}\|\varphi(\eta)\|^{2}_{2}-\left\|{\mathbb{E}}\varphi(\eta)\right\|^{2}_{2}\right),

where
\eta\sim\mu_{0}
is independent with
{\mathcal{X}}_{0}
and
Y_{0}
, by (
3.1
),
we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}\left\|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(\|\eta\|^{2}_{2}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}\eta))}{N}\leqslant\frac{K^{2}{\mathbb{E}}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}\eta)}{N},

and also,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}\left|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}y)(\mu_{0}-\mu^{N}_{0})({\mathord{{\rm d}}}y)\right|^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{{\mathbb{E}}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}\eta)}{N}.

Hence, by Hölder’s inequality and (
4.3
),

{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}[{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(x)]\leqslant\Big({\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}[{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(x)]^{2}\Big)^{1/2}\leqslant\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}\eta)\right)^{1/2},

and by Jensen’s inequality and recalling the expression (
2.7
),

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(Y_{s})}{\phi_{s}(Y_{s})}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}^{{\mathcal{X}}_{0}}[{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{s}(x)]{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\leqslant\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}\eta)\right)^{1/2}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}\rho^{2}_{\sigma_{s}}(x-\beta_{s}\eta)\rho^{-1}_{\sigma_{s}}(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{(\|x\|^{2}_{2}-2\|x-\beta_{s}\eta\|^{2}_{2})/(2\sigma^{2}_{s})}}{(2\pi\sigma^{2}_{s})^{d/2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\left({\mathbb{E}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{(2\|\beta_{s}\eta\|^{2}_{2}-\|x-2\beta_{s}\eta\|^{2}_{2})/(2\sigma^{2}_{s})}}{(2\pi\sigma^{2}_{s})^{d/2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\right)^{1/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\Big({\mathbb{E}}\mathrm{e}^{\|\beta_{s}\eta\|^{2}_{2}/\sigma^{2}_{s}}\Big)^{1/2}\leqslant\frac{2K}{\sqrt{N}}\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta^{2}_{s}/(2\sigma^{2}_{s})}.

Substituting this into (
3.7
) and by
\beta_{s}=1-\sigma_{s}
, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{2K\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta^{2}_{t}/(2\sigma_{t}^{2})}\sigma_{t}}{\sqrt{N}}\int^{t}_{0}\frac{|\sigma_{s}^{\prime}|}{\sigma_{s}^{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}s=\frac{2K\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta^{2}_{t}/(2\sigma_{t}^{2})}\beta_{t}}{\sqrt{N}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

Remark 3.3

\Gamma_{t}(x,y):=-\|x-\beta y\|^{2}_{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})

Using this expression

to

;

;

•

:

as generated sample.

4.
Application to sampling from a known distribution

In this section we use ODE (
2.18
) to introduce a new algorithm for sampling data from a known distribution

\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\varpropto f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
, where

f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
is bounded by
\Lambda
and has support in
\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\}
.

(4.1)

We emphasize that unlike the classical Langevin dynamic method,
we do not make any regularity assumption about
f
. In this case, we need to calculate the function

{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{t}(x):={\mathcal{D}}^{\mu_{0}}_{t}(x)
, that is,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{t}(x)=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y}=\frac{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,y)}f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y}{\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,y)}f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y},

(4.2)

where
\rho_{\sigma}
is defined by (
2.7
) and
\Gamma_{t}(x,y)=\|x-\beta_{t}y\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})
.
When the dimension
d
is small, the integral
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,y)}f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y
can be calculated by the classical numerical method.
When
d
is large, the traditional numerical method becomes inefficient. However, we can use the Monte-Carlo method to
approximate
{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{t}(x)
.

Fix
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Let
\Xi:=\{\xi_{j},j=1,\cdots,N\}
be
a sequence of i.i.d random variables with common uniformly distribution in the ball
\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\}
.
Consider the approximation coefficients:

{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{N,t}(x)=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\xi_{j})f(\xi_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\xi_{j})f(\xi_{j})}=\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\xi_{j})}f(\xi_{j})}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{e}^{-\Gamma_{t}(x,\xi_{j})}f(\xi_{j})}.

The following lemma is completely the same as Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
\eta
be the uniformly distributed random variable in
\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\}
and

\phi_{t}(x):=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y={\mathbb{E}}(\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\eta)f(\eta)).

We have

\|\nabla{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{N,t}(x)\|_{2}\leqslant 2\beta_{t}K^{2}/\sigma^{2}_{t},\ \ \|{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{t}(x)-{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{N,t}(x)\|_{2}\leqslant{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{t}(x)/\phi_{t}(x),

where

\displaystyle\begin{split}{\mathscr{I}}^{N}_{t}(x)&:=\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\xi_{j}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\xi_{j})f(\xi_{j})-{\mathbb{E}}(\eta\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(\eta))\right\|_{2}\\
&\quad+K\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\xi_{j})f(\xi_{j})-{\mathbb{E}}(\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}\eta)f(\eta))\right|.\end{split}

(4.3)

Now we can consider the following approximation ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}Y^{N}_{t}}{{\mathord{{\rm d}}}t}=(\log\sigma_{t})^{\prime}\left[Y^{N}_{t}-{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{N,t}(Y^{N}_{t})\right],\ \ Y^{N}_{0}\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d}).

(4.4)

By Lemma
4.1
and as in the proof of Theorem
3.2
, we also have the following dimension-free convergence result.

Theorem 4.2
.

Under
(H)
and (
4.1
), it holds that for all
t\in[0,1)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{N\in{\mathbb{N}}}\left(\sqrt{N}{\mathbb{E}}\|Y_{t}-Y^{N}_{t}\|_{2}\right)\leqslant 2K\Lambda\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{K^{2}\beta_{t}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{t})},

(4.5)

where the expectation is taken with respect to
\Xi
and
Y_{0}
.

Remark 4.3
.

For larger
K
, by (
4.5
), the convergence becomes worse. However, we can use the scaling technique to overcome this difficulty. Indeed, if we fix a scaling parameter
\epsilon\in(0,1)
and let

f_{K,\epsilon}(x):=f\left(Kx/\epsilon\right),

then
f_{K,\epsilon}
has support in the ball
\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant\epsilon\}
. Thus, one can replace
K

in (
4.5
) with
\epsilon
.

It remains to discretize ODE (
4.4
) by Euler’s scheme. Since
x\mapsto{\mathcal{D}}^{f}_{N,t}(x)
is smooth (see Lemma
2.6
) and there are numerous references about the convergence analysis of Euler’s scheme, we will not discuss this topic.
Here is the concrete algorithm for sampling by taking
\sigma_{t}=1-t
.

Algorithm 2.
Generate samples from known distributions.

1.

Data
:
\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
with
f
being a density function in
B_{K}:=\{x:\|x\|_{2}\leqslant K\}
.

2.

Parameters
:
M
iteration number,
n
the number of Monte-Carlo sampling,
\epsilon
scaling parameter.

3.

Initial values
: Generate a normal random variable
Y_{0}\sim N(0,{\mathbb{I}}_{d})
.

4.

Iteration:
for
k=0
to
M-1

•

Generate i.i.d. uniformly distributed random variables
\xi_{1},\cdots,\xi_{n}
in
B_{\epsilon}
.

•

\sigma_{k}=1-k/M
;
\beta_{k}=k/M
.

•

Y_{k+1}=Y_{k}+\left[\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\xi_{j}\mathrm{e}^{d/2-\|Y_{k}-\beta_{k}\xi_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{k})}f(K\xi_{j}/\epsilon)\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{N}\big(\mathrm{e}^{d/2-\|Y_{k}-\beta_{k}\xi_{j}\|_{2}^{2}/(2\sigma^{2}_{k})}f(K\xi_{j}/\epsilon)\big)}-Y_{k}\right]/(M-k)
.

5.

Output
: a sample
Y_{M}/\varepsilon\sim\mu_{0}
.

First of all, by definition and the change of variable we have

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=(\beta_{t})^{-d-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-y)f(y/\beta_{t}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle\qquad=\beta_{t}^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-y_{1})\rho_{\beta_{t}}(y_{1})\left(\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}y\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}^{*}-y_{1}^{*})\rho_{\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha y_{1}}}(y_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}^{*}\right){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}.

By elementary calculations, one sees that

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\rho_{\sigma}(x-y)\rho_{\sigma^{\prime}}(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{\prime 2}}}(x),

and

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y\rho_{\sigma}(x-y)\rho_{\sigma^{\prime}}(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y=\frac{x\sigma^{\prime 2}}{\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{\prime 2}}\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma^{2}+\sigma^{\prime 2}}}(x).

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}y_{1}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}^{*}-y_{1}^{*})\rho_{\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha y_{1}}}(y_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}^{*}=y_{1}\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}}(x_{1}^{*})

and

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d-1}}y^{*}_{1}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}^{*}-y_{1}^{*})\rho_{\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{\alpha y_{1}}}(y_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}^{*}=\frac{x^{*}_{1}\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}}(x_{1}^{*}).

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y_{1}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=\beta_{t}^{-1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y_{1}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-y_{1})\rho_{\beta_{t}}(y_{1})\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}}(x_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}y_{1}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-\beta_{t}y_{1})\rho_{1}(y_{1})\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha\beta_{t}y_{1}}}}(x_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\Big[\xi\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-\beta_{t}\xi)\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha\beta_{t}\xi}}}(x_{1}^{*})\Big]

and

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}y^{*}_{1}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x-\beta_{t}y)f(y){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y

\displaystyle=x^{*}_{1}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\frac{\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-y_{1})\rho_{\beta_{t}}(y_{1})\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}}(x_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left[\frac{x^{*}_{1}\beta_{t}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha\beta_{t}\xi}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha\beta_{t}\xi}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-\beta_{t}\xi)\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha\beta_{t}\xi}}}(x_{1}^{*})\right].

\displaystyle=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}}\rho_{\sigma_{t}}(x_{1}-y_{1})\rho_{\beta_{t}}(y_{1})\rho_{\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}+\beta_{t}^{2}\mathrm{e}^{2\alpha y_{1}}}}(x_{1}^{*}){\mathord{{\rm d}}}y_{1}

5.
Application to optimizing problems

Let
U:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function. In many optimization problems, we need to find the minimum point of
U
, that is,

x_{*}=\operatorname{argmin}(U(x)).

When
U
is convex, there are many ways to find its global minimum, for example, the gradient descent method or the stochastic Langevin method. However, when
U
is non-convex and the dimension
d
is large, the problem becomes quite challenging. In this case, the probabilistic method would be a good choice.

Now consider the probability density function

f_{\beta}(x)=\exp(-\beta U(x)),\quad\beta>0.

Intuitively, the maximum of
f_{\beta}
will correspond to the minimum of
U
, and as
\beta
becomes large,
f_{\beta}
will concentrate on the minimum of
U
. In fact, it has been proved that as
\beta\to\infty
,
f_{\beta}
weakly converges to the global minimum of
U
(see
[
10
]
).

In this section we use the sampling method in Section
4
to construct an algorithm to seek the minimum of
U
.
We first show the following simple result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function.
Suppose that for some
x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
,

f^{*}:=f(x^{*})=\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x)<\infty.

Let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with common distribution
\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=\rho(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
.
Then for any
\varepsilon\in(0,f^{*})
and
N\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+(f^{*})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta_{\varepsilon}},

(5.1)

where
\delta_{\varepsilon}:=\int_{f(x)>f^{*}-\varepsilon}\rho(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
.

Proof.

Let
Y_{n}:=f(X_{n})
. Then
Y_{n}
is a sequence of i.i.d. nonnegative random variables. Let

Y^{*}_{N}:=\sup_{n=1,\cdots,N}Y_{n}

Then we clearly have

{\mathbb{P}}(Y^{*}_{N}\leqslant r)=\prod_{n=1}^{N}{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{n}\leqslant r)={\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}\leqslant r)^{N}.

Hence, for any
\varepsilon\in(0,f^{*})
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|Y^{*}_{N}-f^{*}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\int^{f^{*}}_{0}(f^{*}-r){\mathbb{P}}(Y^{*}_{N}\leqslant r){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=2\int^{f^{*}}_{0}(f^{*}-r){\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}\leqslant r)^{N}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle=2\left(\int^{f^{*}}_{f^{*}-\varepsilon}+\int^{f^{*}-\varepsilon}_{0}\right)(f^{*}-r){\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}\leqslant r)^{N}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+2{\mathbb{P}}(Y_{1}\leqslant f^{*}-\varepsilon)^{N}\int^{f^{*}-\varepsilon}_{0}(f^{*}-r){\mathord{{\rm d}}}r

\displaystyle\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+(1-{\mathbb{P}}(f(X_{1})>f^{*}-\varepsilon))^{N}(f^{*})^{2}.

(5.2)

Note that

{\mathbb{P}}(f(X_{1})>f^{*}-\varepsilon)=\int_{f(x)>f^{*}-\varepsilon}\rho(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\delta_{\varepsilon},

and for
x\in(0,1)
,

\log(1-x)\leqslant-x\Rightarrow(1-x)^{N}=\mathrm{e}^{N\log(1-x)}\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{-Nx}.

Substituting these into (
5.2
), we get

{\mathbb{E}}\left|Y^{*}_{N}-f^{*}\right|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+(1-\delta_{\varepsilon})^{N}(f^{*})^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+(f^{*})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta_{\varepsilon}}.

The proof is complete.
∎

We have the following corollary.

Corollary 5.2
.

In the situation of Theorem
5.1
, if

K_{f}:=\frac{1}{2}\sup_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\sum_{i,j=1,\cdots,d}|\partial^{2}_{ij}f(x)|<\infty,\ \ \lambda:=\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\in(0,\infty),

then for any
\beta<4/d
and
N>(f^{*})^{2/\beta}
,

{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{2}\leqslant N^{-\beta}+(f^{*})^{2}\exp\Big\{-(f^{*}-N^{-\beta/2})K_{f}^{-d/2}N^{1-d\beta/4}/\lambda\Big\},

where
X_{n}\sim\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)=f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\lambda
. In particular,

\lim_{N\to\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{2}=0.

Proof.

By Taylor’s expansion, we have for some
y=\theta x^{*}+(1-\theta)x
with
\theta\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle f(x^{*})-f(x)

\displaystyle=f(x^{*})-f(x)-(x^{*}-x)\cdot\nabla f(x^{*})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}((x^{*}-x)\otimes(x^{*}-x)\cdot\nabla^{2}f(y))

\displaystyle\leqslant\|x^{*}-x\|^{2}_{\infty}K_{f}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\int_{f(x)>f^{*}-\varepsilon}f(x){\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\geqslant(f^{*}-\varepsilon){\rm vol}\{x:f(x^{*})-f(x)<\varepsilon\}

\displaystyle\geqslant(f^{*}-\varepsilon){\rm vol}\{x:\|x^{*}-x\|^{2}_{\infty}K_{f}<\varepsilon\}=(f^{*}-\varepsilon)(\varepsilon/K_{f})^{d/2}.

Thus by Theorem
5.1
with
\rho(x)=f(x)/\lambda
, we get

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}f(X_{n})-f^{*}\right|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+(f^{*})^{2}\mathrm{e}^{-N(f^{*}-\varepsilon)(\varepsilon/K_{f})^{d/2}/\lambda}.

Taking
\varepsilon=N^{-\beta/2}
, we obtain the desired estimate.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

In the above corollary, the key quantities are
\lambda
and
K_{f}
. Smaller
\lambda
and
K_{f}
will lead to faster convergence.

Now we consider the problem of seeking the minimum of a continuous function.
The following result does not make any regularity assumption about
U
.

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
U:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\to[0,\infty)
be a continuous function. Suppose that there is a minimum point
x_{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
so that

U_{*}:=\inf_{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}U(x)=U(x_{*})=0,

and for some
0<\kappa_{0}\leqslant\kappa_{1}<\infty
and
\ell\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}{\mathbf{1}}_{\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}\geqslant\ell}\leqslant U(x)\leqslant\kappa_{1}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}.

(5.3)

Fix
\beta\geqslant 1
and
let
X_{1},\cdots,X_{N}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables with common distribution

\mu_{0}({\mathord{{\rm d}}}x)\varpropto\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
.
Then for any
\varepsilon>0
, it holds that

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X_{n})}-1\right)^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}(\ell)},

(5.4)

where for
\gamma(d,a):=\int^{a}_{0}u^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-u}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}u
(lower incomplete gamma function),

\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}(\ell):=\frac{(\kappa_{0}/\kappa_{1})^{d/2}\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\beta\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2})-\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\ell\beta)+(\ell\beta)^{d/2}/d}.

Proof.

Applying (
5.1
) to
f(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}
and
\rho(x)=\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(\mathrm{e}^{-\inf_{n=1,\cdots N}U(X_{n})}-1\right)^{2}={\mathbb{E}}\left|\sup_{n=1,\cdots N}\mathrm{e}^{-U(X_{n})}-1\right|^{2}\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}+\mathrm{e}^{-N\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}},

(5.5)

where

\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}=\int_{\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}>1-\varepsilon}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x/\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x.

Let us first estimate the numerator of
\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}
.
Noting that

1-\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}\leqslant U(x)\leqslant\kappa_{1}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2},

we have

\Big\{x:\kappa_{1}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}<\varepsilon\Big\}\subset\Big\{x:U(x)<\varepsilon\Big\}\subset\Big\{x:\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}>1-\varepsilon\Big\}.

Hence, by (
5.3
) and the change of variable,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathrm{e}^{-U(x)}>1-\varepsilon}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{\kappa_{1}\|x\|^{2}_{2}<\varepsilon}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\leqslant\int_{\kappa_{1}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}<\varepsilon}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\kappa_{1}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\|x\|^{2}_{2}<\beta\varepsilon}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\|x\|^{2}_{2}}}{(\beta\kappa_{1})^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\beta\varepsilon)}{(\beta\kappa_{1})^{\frac{d}{2}}\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})},

where we have used that for
a>0
,

\displaystyle\int_{\|x\|_{2}<a}\mathrm{e}^{-\|x\|^{2}_{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\frac{2\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\int^{a}_{0}r^{d-1}\mathrm{e}^{-r^{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}r=\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},a^{2})}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}.

(5.6)

Next, we look at the denominator of
\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}
. We decompose it into two parts:

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\int_{\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}>\ell}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x+\int_{\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}\leqslant\ell}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=:I_{1}(\ell)+I_{2}(\ell).

For
I_{1}(\ell)
, by (
5.3
), the change of variable and (
5.6
), we have

\displaystyle I_{1}(\ell)\leqslant\int_{\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}>\ell}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x

\displaystyle=\int_{\kappa_{0}\|x\|^{2}_{2}>\ell\beta}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\|x\|^{2}_{2}}}{(\beta\kappa_{0})^{\frac{d}{2}}}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x=\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{(\beta\kappa_{0})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\left(1-\frac{\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\ell\beta)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2})}\right).

For
I_{2}(\ell)
, we clearly have

I_{2}(\ell)\leqslant{\rm vol}(\{x:\kappa_{0}\|x-x_{*}\|^{2}_{2}<\ell\})=\tfrac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2+1)}(\ell/\kappa_{0})^{d/2}=\tfrac{\pi^{d/2}}{d\Gamma(d/2)}(\ell/\kappa_{0})^{d/2}.

Hence,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{d}}\mathrm{e}^{-\beta U(x)}{\mathord{{\rm d}}}x\leqslant\frac{\pi^{\frac{d}{2}}}{(\beta\kappa_{0})^{\frac{d}{2}}}\left(1-\frac{\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\ell\beta)}{\Gamma(\tfrac{d}{2})}+\frac{(\ell\beta)^{d/2}}{d\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})}\right).

Combining the above calculations, we obtain

\delta_{\beta,\varepsilon}\geqslant\left(\frac{\kappa_{0}}{\kappa_{1}}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}}\frac{\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\beta\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(\frac{d}{2})-\gamma(\tfrac{d}{2},\ell\beta)+(\ell\beta)^{d/2}/d}.

The proof is complete.
∎
