The Fisher-Rao geometry of CES distributions
Florent Bouchard, Arnaud Breloy, Antoine Collas, Alexandre Renaux, Guillaume Ginolhac

Abstract
When dealing with a parametric statistical model, a Riemannian manifold can naturally appear by endowing the parameter space with the Fisher information metric. The geometry induced on the parameters by this metric is then referred to as the Fisher-Rao information geometry. Interestingly, this yields a point of view that allows for leveraging many tools from differential geometry. After a brief introduction about these concepts, we will present some practical uses of these geometric tools in the framework of elliptical distributions. This second part of the exposition is divided into three main axes: Riemannian optimization for covariance matrix estimation, Intrinsic Cramér-Rao bounds, and classification using Riemannian distances.

1.1
Reminders on CES distributions

Circular complex elliptically symmetric (C-CES) distributions
kai1990generalized
refer to a large family of multivariate distributions.
Very comprehensive and detailed reviews on the topic can be found in the references
ollila2011complex
;
ollila2012complex
, and of course, the background chapter of this book.
A vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
follows a centered (zero-mean) C-CES distribution, denoted
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
, if it admits the following stochastic representation:

\mathbf{x}{=}_{d}\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}}~\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}~\mathbf{u},

(1)

where:

\bullet

The notation
{=}_{d}
means that random variables on both sides have the same cumulative distribution function.

\bullet

The vector
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}
follows a uniform distribution on the complex unit sphere
\mathbb{C}\mathcal{S}^{p}=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}~|~\left\|\mathbf{u}\right\|=1\right\}
, denoted
\mathbf{u}\sim\mathcal{U}\left(\mathbb{C}\mathcal{S}^{p}\right)
.

\bullet

The scalar
\mathcal{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is non-negative real random variable of probability density function
f_{\mathcal{Q}}
, independent of
\mathbf{u}
, and called the second-order modular variate (while
\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}}
is called the modular variate).

\bullet

The matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\in\mathbb{C}^{p\times p}
is a factorization of the scatter matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\frac{H}{2}}
.
If the covariance matrix of
\mathbf{x}
exists, it is proportional to the scatter matrix, i.e.,
\text{E}\left[\mathbf{x}\mathbf{x}^{H}\right]\propto\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
If we then choose the normalization convention
\text{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}\right]=1
, these two matrices are equal.
Thus we will abusively refer to the scatter matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
as the covariance matrix, as it is a more familiar terminology.

We focus only on the absolutely continuous case where the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is full rank (cf. Section 2.3 of the background chapter).
In this case, the probability density function of
\mathbf{x}
is given as

f_{\mathbf{x}}\left(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)\propto|\mathbf{\Sigma}|^{-1}g\left(~\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}~\right),

(2)

where the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathbb{R}^{+}
is called the density generator.
The density generator satisfies the finite moment condition

\delta_{p,g}=\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{p-1}g(t)dt<\infty
.
This function
g
is directly related to the probability density function of the second-order modular variate by the relation

f_{\mathcal{Q}}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)=\delta_{p,g}^{-1}\mathcal{Q}^{p-1}g\left(\mathcal{Q}\right).

(3)

Given a
n
-sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, assumed to be independent and identically distributed (iid) from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
, its log-likelihood is given as:

\mathcal{L}_{g}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(g\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\right)-n\log|\mathbf{\Sigma}|.

(4)

The C-CES model being defined, we move to the notion of information brought by the Fisher information matrix: intuitively, the more the sample set
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
depends on
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, the more sampling from the likelihood (
4
) (increasing
n
) will reveal information about
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
The score vector is a tool that will help in quantifying this notion of information:
to define this quantity, we now consider a parameterization of the covariance matrix through a real-valued vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
of appropriate dimension
2
2

2

In this chapter,
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is not assumed to have a specific structure, so
\boldsymbol{\nu}
is typically of dimension
p^{2}
and stores the entries of the diagonal and upper triangle of the covariance matrix (where the coordinates are split in terms of real and imaginary part).
However, the definition extends to any parameterization, e.g., from a choice of decomposition in the case of structured matrices
meriaux2019robust
.

, denoted
\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
.
The score vector
\mathbf{s}
is then defined entry-wise as

\left[\mathbf{s}\right]_{j}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{g}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\right)}{\partial\nu_{j}},

(5)

which therefore reflects the variation of the log-likelihood of the sample set
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
with respect to the parameter
\nu_{j}
.
Under mild regularity conditions (satisfied by
\mathcal{L}_{g}
in our case), this vector has zero mean, i.e.,
\text{E}\left[\mathbf{s}\right]=\mathbf{0}
.
However, its covariance matrix is a fundamental quantity referred to as the Fisher information matrix, denoted
\mathbf{F}
, and defined as:

\left[\mathbf{F}\right]_{j,k}=\text{E}\left[\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{g}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\right)}{\partial\nu_{j}}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}_{g}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})\right)}{\partial\nu_{k}}\right].

(6)

This matrix quantifies, on average, how much information about the vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
we can obtain from a sample set
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
.
In practice, the entries of the Fisher information matrix
\mathbf{F}
for centered C-CES can be obtained thanks to Slepian-Bangs type formula from
besson2013fisher
, also presented in the Section 6.5 of the background chapter.
The latter is briefly recalled below using an alternate expression that is consistent whit the upcoming discussions:

Theorem 1.1

(Fisher Information matrix of centered C-CES)

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
be a covariance matrix parameterized by the real-valued vector
\boldsymbol{\nu}
.
Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a
n
-sample of iid from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
.
The entries of the Fisher information matrix are

\left[\mathbf{F}\right]_{j,k}=n\alpha_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k})+n\beta_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}),

(7)

with

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}=\frac{\partial\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})}{\partial\nu_{j}},

(8)

and where the coefficients
\alpha_{g}
and
\beta_{g}
are defined by

\displaystyle\alpha_{g}=1-\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}^{2}\phi^{\prime}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)\right]}{p(p+1)}

\displaystyle~~~~\text{and}

\displaystyle\beta_{g}=\alpha_{g}-1,

(9)

with
\phi(t)=g^{\prime}(t)/g(t)
.

In the statistical signal processing community
kay1993fundamentals
, the Fisher information matrix has been extensively leveraged thanks to the Cramér-Rao inequality:

\mathbb{E}\left[(\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}-\boldsymbol{\nu})(\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}-\boldsymbol{\nu})^{\top}\right]\succcurlyeq\mathbf{F}^{-1}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}-\boldsymbol{\nu}\right\|_{F}^{2}\geq\tr(\mathbf{F}^{-1}),

(10)

that yields a lower bound for the mean squared error of any unbiased estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
of

\boldsymbol{\nu}
(built from a set of observations
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
).
On the other hand, the seminal work of Rao
rao1945information
;
rao1992information
also discusses using the Riemannian geometry of the parameter space when the Fisher information matrix is used as a metric tensor.
The study of such spaces is now broadly referred to as the Fisher-Rao information geometry, which is introduced in the next section.
Before this, we conclude this brief reminder by the example of multivariate (Student’s)
t
distribution (also discussed with more details in Section 5.2 of the background chapter).

Example 1.1.1

The
t
-distribution with
d\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
degrees of freedom is obtained for the C-CES representation
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g_{d})
with

g_{d}(t)=\left(1+\frac{t}{d}\right)^{-(d+p)},

(11)

and the second-order modular variate is distributed as

\mathcal{Q}{=}_{d}{\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{X}_{p}^{2}}}/{\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{X}_{d}^{2}/d}}

where
\mathbb{C}_{\mathcal{X}_{x}^{2}}
denotes the Chi-squared distribution with
x
degrees of freedom. Hence
\mathcal{Q}
follows a scaled
\mathcal{F}
-distribution.
We have

\phi(t)=-\frac{d+p}{d+t},

(12)

and the expectation

\mathbb{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}^{2}\phi^{2}\left(\mathcal{Q}\right)\right]=\frac{(d+p)p(p+1)}{d+p+1},

(13)

that allows to obtain the coefficients

\alpha_{g}=\frac{d+p}{d+p+1}~~~~~~~~\text{and}~~~~~~~~\beta_{g}=\frac{-1}{d+p+1}.

(14)

for the Fisher information metric as in Theorem
2.1
.
The
t
-distribution also encompasses the well known multivariate Gaussian model

\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma})

(of density generator
g_{\mathcal{N}}(t)=\exp(-t)
) as limit case when

.
The corresponding Fisher information metric coefficient are then
\alpha_{g}=1
and
\beta_{g}=0

coincide with the classical Slepian-Bangs formula

;

.

1.2

From the Fisher information matrix to information geometry

This section aims at linking notions of Riemannian geometry to the classical expression of the Fisher information matrix from Theorem
1.1
.
The goal is to shortly build the intuition on why the C-CES statistical model naturally induces a certain geometry for covariance matrices, while the corresponding framework will be presented in details in Section
2
.
We first need to re-interpret the expression of the Fisher information matrix
from two key points:

•

Covariance matrices belong to the smooth manifold
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}

The matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\nu})
is a point in the space of covariance matrices, i.e., the set of
p\times p
positive definite Hermitian matrices

\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}=\left\{\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}:~~\forall~\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{C}^{p}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\},~\mathbf{x}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{x}>0\right\},

(15)

where
\mathcal{H}_{p}
denotes the set of
p\times p
Hermitian matrices.
As it is an open of the linear space
\mathcal{H}_{p}
, the space
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is a smooth manifold.
This means that it admits a differential structure, and notably, a tangent space at each point
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, denoted
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
For any point
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, this tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
turns out to be identifiable to be
\mathcal{H}_{p}
(which again, comes from the fact that
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is an open subspace of
\mathcal{H}_{p}
).
An abstract representation of these spaces is presented in Figure

.

•

The Fisher information matrix represents an inner product on
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}

The entries of the Fisher information matrix of Theorem
1.1
can be compactly denoted as

[\mathbf{F}]_{j,k}=\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}
, whose expression is identified directly from (
7
).
We then remark that the matrices
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}
in (
8
) are, in fact, elements of
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
The expression in (
7
) can thus be generalized to any pair of matrices
\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\in T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, which results in a bi-linear form, denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}:T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\times T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\to\mathbb{R}
.
Because this bi-linear form is positive definite, it defines a metric, i.e., an inner product on the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
This inner product on
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is referred to as the Fisher information metric
3
3

3

Note that the Fisher information
matrix
being obtained as
\left[\mathbf{F}\right]_{j,k}=\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{k}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}
, it is actually a matrix representation (metric tensor) of the Fisher information
metric

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}
when the set
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}\}
is chosen as a basis of coordinates for the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
.

These two points being stated, we can last notice that the obtained Fisher information metric
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}
varies smoothly with
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
This enables the transition from statistical models to Riemannian geometry: the branch of differential geometry studying smooth manifolds endowed with smooth local inner products (referred to as Riemannian metrics).
Such framework indeed applies to parametric statistical models, as it allows us to investigate the geometry of the parameter space equipped with the Fisher information metric.
The resulting Riemannian geometry is generally referred to as the Fisher-Rao information geometry.
Back to our central example, we have presented enough elements to explicit that the title of this chapter “The Fisher-Rao Geometry of CES distributions” more precisely stands short for “the Riemannian geometry of Hermitian positive definite matrices (covariance matrices) induced by the Fisher information metric of centered circular complex elliptically symmetric distributions”, which will be studied in the next sections.

1.3

Outline of the chapter

The previous section showed why an inherent geometry of the parameter space can naturally result from a statistical model.
Studying such geometry in detail requires introducing tools from the framework of Riemannian geometry, which is done in section

.
The C-CES distributions will be used as an example throughout the exposition.
Hence, we will obtain most tools related to the Fisher-Rao Geometry of C-CES distributions: the Levi-Civita connection, the geodesics (and geodesic distance) between two covariance matrices, as well as the Riemannian exponential and logarithm mappings.

On a larger perspective, the second part of this chapter illustrates where tools obtained from the Fisher-Rao information geometry of C-CES can be leveraged within signal processing and machine learning tasks.
In details:

\bullet

addresses covariance matrix estimation problems, i.e., given a sample set

, we infer

to perform a task (covariance analysis, filtering, metric learning, etc.).
In this setup, we illustrate how the concepts of geodesic convexity and Riemannian optimization can be helpful in problems related to covariance matrix estimation.

\bullet

Still related to covariance matrix estimation problems, Section

\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}

The set of

is an open subspace of the space of
p\times p
Hermitian matrices
\mathcal{H}_{p}
.
Since
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
has the same dimension as its embedding space

, it is a smooth manifold of dimension
p^{2}

(

.
As every smooth manifolds,

.
The elements of

are called tangent vectors, and correspond to the directional derivatives of curves in

(cf. Figure

is open in

, the tangent space

can be identified as

.

Remark 1

The space
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is often referred to as the convex cone of positive definite matrices.
It is indeed a cone because
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
implies that

a\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p},~\forall~a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
. It is furthermore a convex cone because any linear combination
a\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}+b\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}
is also in
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
,
\forall~\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and
\forall~a,b\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{*}
.
This cone visually appears in the real
2\times 2
case of
\mathcal{S}_{2}^{++}
, which is often used to represent
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
Still, this chapter will rely on the representation of Figure
1
,
which is more convenient to illustrate the generic concepts and tools of Riemannian geometry.

In order to further harness the differential structure of
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, we endow it with a Riemannian metric.
This consists in a mapping that equips every tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
with an inner product
4
4

4

An inner product is a bilinear, symmetric, positive definite function.

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
that varies smoothly with respect to the point
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
This allows notably for locally defining the notion of angle and length for vectors in
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
A smooth manifold equipped with such Riemmanian metric is then referred to as a Riemannian manifold.
Notice that the definition of the metric is a choice that induces a corresponding geometry.
In particular, if
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is endowed with the Euclidean metric
\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\mathcal{E}}=\reel(\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\eta}))
, where
\reel(\cdot)
returns the real part of its argument, all the geometrical objects of the manifold
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
are exactly the same as those of the space
\mathcal{H}_{p}
.
In this case, there is no distinction between
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and
\mathcal{H}_{p}
from a geometrical point of view, and the true structure of
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
cannot be exploited.
This motivates the use of other metrics, that induce a more meaningful geometry on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
(e.g., ensuring that the boundaries of the space are not reachable).
In this scope, various options have been considered, such as the affine invariant metric
bhatia2009positive
;
moakher2005differential
, the log-Euclidean metric
arsigny2006log
, or the Bures-Wasserstein one
bhatia2019bures
;
han2021riemannian
.
Overviews of the different metrics and their corresponding geometries can be found in
thanwerdas22theseis
;
thanwerdas2023n
.

When dealing with a statistical model the Fisher information metric is generally to be favored, as it is naturally suited to the underlying geometry of the data.
Without resorting to the tedious parameterization and identification of Section
1.2
, a general expression of this metric can directly be obtained following
(
smith2005covariance
, Theorem 1)
as:

\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}=\text{E}\left[\Diff\mathcal{L}_{g}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]\cdot\Diff\mathcal{L}_{g}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right]=-\text{E}\left[\Diff^{2}\mathcal{L}_{g}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}]\right],

(16)

where
\Diff\mathcal{L}_{g}
and
\Diff^{2}\mathcal{L}_{g}
are the first and second order directional derivatives of the log-likelihood
\mathcal{L}_{g}
of the distribution with respect to
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
Recall from
higham2008functions
that the first and second derivatives of a function
L:\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\to\mathbb{R}
at
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
in directions
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
are defined as

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Diff L(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]&=&L(\mathbf{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\xi})-L(\mathbf{\Sigma})+o(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|)\\
\Diff^{2}L(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}]&=&\Diff L(\mathbf{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\eta})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]-\Diff L(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]+o(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|).\end{array}

(17)

Notice that
\Diff^{2}L(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}]
is symmetrical with respect to
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}
.

Theorem 2.1
(Fisher Information metric of centered CES)

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be a
n
-sample of iid from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
.
The Fisher information metric is obtained
\forall~\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\in T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
as

\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}=n\alpha_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})+n\beta_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}),

with
\alpha_{g}
and
\beta_{g}
defined in (
9
).

Proof

The first things to compute to obtain the Fisher information metric are the derivatives
\Diff\mathcal{L}_{g}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]
and
\Diff^{2}\mathcal{L}_{g}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}]
at
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
in directions
\boldsymbol{\xi}
and
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
To do so, recall that
\Diff\log\det(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
\Diff(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
.
It follows that

\Diff\mathcal{L}_{g}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=-n\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}).

Moreover, also recall that the trace is invariant to any permutation of the product of three Hermitian matrices.
Thus,

\Diff^{2}\mathcal{L}_{g}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}]=\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})\\
+2\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}))\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\\
+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\phi^{\prime}(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}))\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}).

We now need to compute the expectation.
To do so, we exploit the stochastic representation
\mathbf{x}_{i}{=}_{d}\sqrt{\mathcal{Q}_{i}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{u}_{i}
.
Recall that
\mathcal{Q}_{i}
and
\mathbf{u}_{i}
are independent,
\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{u}_{i}=1
and
\text{E}\left[\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\right]=\frac{1}{p}\mathbf{I}_{p}
(since
\mathbf{u}_{i}\sim\mathcal{U}(\mathbb{C}\mathcal{S}^{p})
).
Furthermore, from (
3
),
\text{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}\phi(\mathcal{Q}_{i})\right]=-p
.
It follows that

\displaystyle\text{E}\left[\phi(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}))\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\right]

\displaystyle=\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\text{E}\left[\phi(\mathcal{Q}_{i})\mathcal{Q}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\right])=\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\text{E}\left[\phi(\mathcal{Q}_{i})\mathcal{Q}_{i}\right]\text{E}\left[\mathbf{u}_{i}\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\right])

\displaystyle=-\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}).

For the second expectation, from
besson2013fisher
, we need

\text{E}\left[(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{u}_{i})^{2}\right]=\frac{\tr(\boldsymbol{A}^{2})+(\tr(\boldsymbol{A}))^{2}}{p(p+1)}.

Applying the polarization formula
\frac{1}{4}[(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}(\boldsymbol{A}+\boldsymbol{B})\mathbf{u}_{i})^{2}-(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}(\boldsymbol{A}-\boldsymbol{B})\mathbf{u}_{i})^{2}]
, we get

\text{E}\left[(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\boldsymbol{A}\mathbf{u}_{i})(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\boldsymbol{B}\mathbf{u}_{i})\right]=\frac{\tr(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})+\tr(\boldsymbol{A})\tr(\boldsymbol{B})}{p(p+1)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\text{E}\left[\phi^{\prime}(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}))\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\right]

\displaystyle=\text{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{Q}_{i})(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{u}_{i})(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{u}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\text{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{Q}_{i})\right]\text{E}\left[(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{u}_{i})(\mathbf{u}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{u}_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{\text{E}\left[\mathcal{Q}_{i}^{2}\phi^{\prime}(\mathcal{Q}_{i})\right]}{p(p+1)}\left(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})+\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})\right)

From there, basic manipulations yield the result with coefficients
\alpha_{g}
and
\beta_{g}
defined in (
9
).
Notice that the dependency on
i
in
\mathcal{Q}
is omitted since these parameters are assumed iid.

The Fisher information metric of C-CES thus corresponds to a general form of the well known affine invariant metric on

bhatia2009positive

Many affine connections can be defined on a manifold.
However, there is a unique one that is in accordance with the chosen Riemannian metric, which is referred to as the Levi-Civita connection.
This Levi-Civita connection, denoted
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
, is the unique solution in the tangent space

to the Koszul formula

2\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})=2\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\Diff\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}],\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})\\
+\Diff\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}](\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})+\Diff\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}](\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})-\Diff\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}](\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}),

(19)

where we use the alternate notation of the metric, i.e.,
\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\cdot,\cdot)=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
.
Notice that the presented formula is simpler than the general case

.
It is because the Lie bracket is
[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}]=\Diff\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}]-\Diff\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}]
since
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is an open subset of a vector space, i.e.,
\mathcal{H}_{p}
.
The Levi-Civita connection of
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
associated with the Riemannian metric (
18

Theorem 2.2
(Levi-Civita connection)

The Levi-Civita connection on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
associated with the affine invariant metric (
18
) is defined for
\boldsymbol{\xi}
,
\boldsymbol{\eta}\in\mathfrak{X}(\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p})
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, as

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}}\,\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\Diff\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}]-\symm(\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}),

where
\symm(\cdot)
returns the Hermitian part of its argument.

Proof

First recall that for
\boldsymbol{A}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
,
\tr(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=\tr(\boldsymbol{A}\symm(\boldsymbol{B}))
.
Further recall that the trace is invariant to any permutation of the product of three Hermitian matrices.
Since
\Diff(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=-\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
, we have

\Diff\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))[\boldsymbol{\nu}]=-2\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}))

and

\Diff\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}))[\boldsymbol{\xi}]+\Diff\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}))[\boldsymbol{\eta}]-\Diff\reel\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))[\boldsymbol{\nu}]\\
=-2\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}))=-2\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\symm(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu})).

We also have

\Diff\left(\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\right)[\boldsymbol{\nu}]=-\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\\
-\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})).

It follows that

\Diff\left(\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}))\right)[\boldsymbol{\xi}]+\Diff\left(\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu}))\right)[\boldsymbol{\eta}]\\
-\Diff\left(\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\right)[\boldsymbol{\nu}]=-2\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\hskip 7.0pt\\
=-2\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\nu})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\symm(\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))).

From there, we can deduce that

\Diff\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}](\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})+\Diff\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}](\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})-\Diff\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}[\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}](\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}})\\
=-2\mathfrak{g}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\symm(\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}),\boldsymbol{\nu}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}).

Injecting this into the Koszul formula yields the result.

and

2.3

Geodesics, Riemannian exponential, logarithm and distance

One of the main reasons why the Levi-Civita connection is so crucial is because it allows to define geodesics.
The geodesics generalize the concept of straight lines on a manifold.
These are curves
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
with no acceleration, where acceleration is defined thanks to the Levi-Civita connection.
They are parameterized by the choice of starting point
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and either initial direction
\dot{\gamma}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
or ending point
\gamma(1)=\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
An illustration of geodesics is provided in Figure
6
.
Formally, the geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is the solution to the differential equation

\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}(t)}\,\dot{\gamma}(t)=\boldsymbol{0}.

(20)

The geodesics on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
according to the Levi-Civita connection of Theorem
2.2
are given in Theorem
2.3
along with the proof.

Theorem 2.3
(Geodesics)

The geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
such that
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
is defined as

\gamma(t)\;=\;\mathbf{\Sigma}\exp(t\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\;=\;\exp(t\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\;=\;\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\exp(t\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

where
\exp(\cdot)
denotes the matrix exponential.
Equivalently, one can define the geodesic
\gamma:[0,1]\to\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
such that
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and
\gamma(1)=\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
by

\gamma(t)=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\right)^{t}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}},

where
(\cdot)^{t}=\exp(t\log(\cdot))
,
\log(\cdot)
denoting the matrix logarithm.

Proof

We only provide the proof for
\gamma(0)=\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
The result for
\gamma(1)=\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
is obtained by choosing

\boldsymbol{\xi}\;=\;\mathbf{\Sigma}\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\;=\;\log(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1})\mathbf{\Sigma}\;=\;\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Notice that the equality between the three versions of
\gamma(t)
given in the theorem above and of
\boldsymbol{\xi}
given here solely rely on the fact that
\boldsymbol{A}\exp(\boldsymbol{B})\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=\exp(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})
and
\boldsymbol{A}\log(\boldsymbol{B})\boldsymbol{A}^{-1}=\log(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B}\boldsymbol{A}^{-1})
.

The differential equation (
20
) for the Levi-Civita connection defined in Theorem
2.2
is

\ddot{\gamma}(t)-\dot{\gamma}(t)\gamma(t)^{-1}\dot{\gamma}(t)=\boldsymbol{0}.

Recall that
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\exp(t\boldsymbol{A})=\boldsymbol{A}\exp(t\boldsymbol{A})
.
Thus, with
\gamma(t)=\mathbf{\Sigma}\exp(t\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})
, we have
\dot{\gamma}(t)=\boldsymbol{\xi}\exp(t\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})
and
\ddot{\gamma}(t)=\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\exp(t\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})
.
From there, we easily obtain
\dot{\gamma}(t)\gamma(t)^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
.
Simple computations show that
\gamma(t)
satisfies the differential equation above, which is enough to conclude.

Theorem 2.4
(Fisher-Rao distance of C-CES distributions)

and

\delta^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\alpha\|\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\|_{F}^{2}+\beta(\log\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}))^{2}.

Proof

\dot{\gamma}(t)\gamma(t)^{-1}=\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}
for all

for all

\delta^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\langle\dot{\gamma}(0),\dot{\gamma}(0)\rangle_{\gamma(0)}

and
\dot{\gamma}(0)=\mathbf{\Sigma}\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})

\tr(\log(\boldsymbol{A}))=\log\det(\boldsymbol{A})

Remark 6

This means that the Fisher-Rao distance (Riemannian distance according to the Fisher in formation geometry) actually depends on the underlying C-CES distribution.

Finally, Section
3.3

shortly presents how this framework can be leveraged to more general regularized covariance matrix estimation problems and points to references on the matter.

3.1

Reminders on covariance matrix estimation within CES

Given a
n
-sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
assumed to be iid from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
, with unknown covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
, we consider inferring this matrix.
The most common approach to tackle this problem consists in maximizing the log-likelihood function in (
4
).
The maximum likelihood estimator is thus obtained as a solution to the optimization problem

\minimize_{\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}}\quad L(\mathbf{\Sigma})

(24)

where
L
denotes in short the negative log-likelihood of the sample set
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, i.e.:

L(\mathbf{\Sigma})=-\mathcal{L}_{g}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}|\mathbf{\Sigma}\right),

(25)

with
\mathcal{L}_{g}
defined in (
4
).
The solution of (
24
) yields the MLE in the form of a fixed point equation

\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi\left(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}{=}_{d}\mathcal{T}_{\psi}\left(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\right),

(26)

where
\psi(t)=-g^{\prime}(t)/g(t)
.
This solution is most commonly evaluated thanks to a fixed-point algorithm

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k+1)}=\mathcal{T}_{\psi}\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)}\right)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}.

(27)

The existence and uniqueness of the fixed-point solution (
26
), as well as the convergence of the fixed-point algorithm (
27
) is subject subject to conditions on
the function
\psi
(resp. the density generator
g
) and the sample set
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, e.g., obtained in
(
ollila2012complex
, Theorems 6 and 7)
. A notable condition in the absolutely continuous case is that the sample size is required to be larger than the dimension, i.e.,
n>p
.

Remark 7

In practice, the true density generator
g
may not be known or accurately specified.
In the robust estimation theory, an
M
-estimator of the scatter matrix
maronna1976robust
;
tyler1987distribution
refers to an estimator built from (
26
)-(
27
) using a function
\psi(t)
that is not necessarily linked to the density generator
g
(cf. Section 6.3 of the background chapter).
In this chapter, we focus on the example of the MLE, but the tools that will be presented apply to any generic cost function
L
.

3.2

Computing MLEs with Riemannian optimization

Riemannian optimization
absil2009optimization
;
boumal2023introduction
is a general framework to solve optimization problems on manifolds.
This extends to Riemannian manifolds classical Euclidean optimization methods such as steepest gradient descent, conjugate gradient, Broyden-Fletcher-Goldfarb-Shanno (BFGS) algorithm, Newton method, trust region,
etc
.
This section introduces Riemannian optimization on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
as a framework to solve (
24
) that can leverage tools from the Fisher-Rao information geometry.
At the end of this section, we will see that this framework actually yields the fixed point algorithm (
27
) as a special case (specifically, a Riemannian steepest gradient descent with a specific choice of metric, retraction, and step-size).

We consider an optimization problem of the from (
24
) that has no obvious closed-form solution on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
In order to evaluate this solution, we resort to iterative methods, i.e., methods that yield a sequence of iterates
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)}\}
in
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
from a starting point
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(0)}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
This sequence is constructed so that it eventually converges to a critical point of the objective in (
24
).
When the variable is constrained to lie in the manifold
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, a generic first-order Riemannian optimization method operates as follows:

1.

At iterate
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, a descent direction in the tangent space, denoted
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{(k)}\in T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{p}
, is computed by leveraging the Riemannian gradient.

2.

The direction descent
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{(k)}
is used to obtain the next iterate
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k+1)}
on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
. This is achieved through a retraction on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, which is an operator that maps tangent vectors back onto the manifold.

An illustration of such an optimization process is presented in Figure
7
, while the design of these two steps is discussed to solve (
24
) on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
in the following.

,

For the first step, the steepest descent direction is given by the gradient, which is defined through the metric in the Riemannian setting.
The Riemannian gradient of the negative log-likelihood
L
at
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
according to the metric of Theorem
2.1
is the unique tangent vector
\grad L(\mathbf{\Sigma})\in T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{p}
such that, for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
, we have

\langle\grad L(\mathbf{\Sigma}),\boldsymbol{\xi}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\Diff L(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}].

(28)

This Riemannian gradient is provided in Proposition
1
.

Proposition 1
(Riemannian gradient of
L
)

The Riemannian gradient
\grad L(\mathbf{\Sigma})
of the negative log-likelihood
L
defined in (
25
) at
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
according to metric (
18
) is

\grad L(\mathbf{\Sigma})=\left(\frac{n}{\alpha+p\beta}+\frac{\beta}{\alpha(\alpha+p\beta)}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\mathbf{\Sigma}\\
-\frac{1}{\alpha}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}.

Proof

From the beginning of the proof of Theorem
2.1
, we get that the directional derivative of
L
at
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
in direction
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\Diff L(\mathbf{\Sigma})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]&=&n\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{g^{\prime}}{g}(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\\
&=&\tr\left(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\left(n\mathbf{\Sigma}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i}\right)\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\right).\end{array}

Thus, for
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
, we immediately get the result by identification.
To obtain the result in the general case, notice that, given
\boldsymbol{A}
and
\boldsymbol{B}
in
\mathcal{H}_{p}
, if we set
\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\boldsymbol{A}-\frac{\beta}{\alpha(\alpha+p\beta)}\tr(\boldsymbol{A})\boldsymbol{I}_{p}
, then we have
\tr(\boldsymbol{A}\boldsymbol{B})=\alpha\tr(\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}}\boldsymbol{B})+\beta\tr(\boldsymbol{\tilde{A}})\tr(\boldsymbol{B})
.
Taking
\boldsymbol{A}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(n\mathbf{\Sigma}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
and
\boldsymbol{B}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}
, and basic calculations allow to conclude.

To perform the second step, it remains to define a retraction that maps tangent vectors back onto the manifold.
Formally, given
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, a retraction is a mapping
R_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}:T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{p}\to\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
such that, for all
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
,

R_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\xi}+o(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|).

(29)

From a geometric point of view, the Riemannian exponential mapping
provides the ideal retraction for a manifold equipped with a Riemannian metric (in the sense that it is the most reflective of the considered geometry).
In our case, it is defined in (
21
) and illustrated in Figure
6
.
However, this retraction involves computing the matrix exponential of some Hermitian matrix, which can be computationally costly and/or numerically unstable, as the exponential tends quickly to infinity or zero.
From a practical point of view, it might thus be more advantageous to employ alternate retractions.
Notice that (
29
) means that a proper retraction is (at least) a first-order approximation of the Riemannian exponential mapping.
Since
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is open in
\mathcal{H}_{p}
, a proper first order approximation is simply obtained as

R^{(1)}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\xi}.

(30)

The main limitation of
R^{(1)}
is that, given
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, there are many
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
such that
R^{(1)}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})\notin\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
This means that the iterative algorithms that employ this retraction are not guaranteed to be numerically stable.
To overcome this issue, Proposition
2
provides a retraction that is a second-order approximation of the Riemannian exponential (
21
) (initially proposed in
jeuris2012survey
), that does not suffer the same limitation as
R^{(1)}
.

Proposition 2
(Second order retraction)

The retraction
R^{(2)}
such that, for all
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
,

R^{(2)}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{\Sigma}+\boldsymbol{\xi}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}

is a second order approximation of the Riemannian exponential mapping (
21
).
Furthermore, for all
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
and
\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
,
R^{(2)}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})
belongs to
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.

Proof

Recall that the matrix exponential of
\boldsymbol{A}
is
\exp(\boldsymbol{A})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{\boldsymbol{A}^{k}}{k!}
.
Hence the second order approximation is
\exp(\boldsymbol{A})=\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\boldsymbol{A}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{A}^{2}+o(\|\boldsymbol{A}\|^{2})
.
Applying this to (
21
), we obtain
\exp_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})+o(\|\boldsymbol{\xi}\|^{2})
.
Basic calculations yield the result.
Moreover, it is obviously a proper retraction.
It remains to show that we always get a matrix in
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
To do so, notice that

R^{(2)}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}(\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}+\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})^{2})\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

Let the eigenvalue decomposition
\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}=\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\boldsymbol{U}^{H}
.
Then

R^{(2)}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\boldsymbol{\xi})=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{U}(\boldsymbol{I}_{p}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}+\frac{1}{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{2})\boldsymbol{U}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

The result follows from the fact that the second order polynomial
\lambda\mapsto 1+\lambda+\frac{1}{2}\lambda^{2}
is strictly positive for all values of
\lambda
.

We now have everything needed to define an iterative algorithm that solves the MLE optimization problem (
24
).
Given the retraction
R
, we can, for instance, define the Riemannian gradient descent that yields the sequence of iterates

\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k+1)}=R_{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)}}(-\lambda_{k}\grad L(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{(k)})),

(31)

where
\lambda_{k}
is the step size, which can be set by the user or computed through a line search; see e.g.
absil2009optimization
;
boumal2023introduction
.

Our final point in this section is to show that the fixed point algorithm (
27
) is, in fact, a particular case of (
31
).
Indeed, if we choose
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
, the Riemannian gradient of Proposition
1
is

\grad L(\mathbf{\Sigma})=n\mathbf{\Sigma}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi(\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{x}_{i})\,\mathbf{x}_{i}\mathbf{x}_{i}^{H}.

Algorithm (
27
) is then obtained from (
31
) by choosing the first order retraction (
30
) and constant step size
\lambda_{k}=\frac{1}{n}
.
Notice that in this particular case, the choice of the first order retraction (
30
) is a valid choice because the particular structure of the gradient ensures that all iterates remain in
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
Though alternate choices of
\alpha
and
\beta
in the metric, step size, and retraction could improve the convergence speed in some cases, this fixed-point is generally a good all-purpose candidate to compute MLEs as in (
26
).
However, having recast it from the prism of Riemannian geometry opens many perspectives, which are discussed in the next section.

3.3

Beyond MLE and fixed-point algorithms

The MLEs (and
M
-estimators) are known for their good asymptotic performance in terms of estimation accuracy
couillet2015random
;
dravskovic2019asymptotics
;
dravskovic2018new
;
ollila2011complex
;
zhang2014marchenko
.
Still, they suffer from two limitations:

i
) they do not exist when the sample set is lower than the dimension (
n<p
);

ii
) they can be inaccurate when
n\simeq p
, as they do not leverage any bias-variance trade-off improvement.
These limitations motivated the development of generalized estimation procedures by expressing new estimators as solutions to penalized optimization problems of the form:

\minimize_{\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}}\quad L(\mathbf{\Sigma})+\lambda h(\mathbf{\Sigma})

(32)

where
L
is the negative log-likelihood as in (
25
),
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
is a regularization parameter, and
h
is a penalty function that promotes some form of regularization.
Among many options considered in the literature for
h
, we can mention shrinkage to a target matrix
ollila2014regularized
;
pascal2014generalized
;
sun2014regularized
, shrinkage of the eigenvalues
Wiesel2012unified
;
breloy2019spectral
, promoting a sparse graphical structure
hippert2022learning
;
zhang2013multivariate
, or pooling from groups of observations
collas2022robust
;
ollila2016simultaneous
.
For appropriate choices of regularization penalty and parameters, the regularized estimators, as formulated in (
32
), can overcome the aforementioned issues of their non-regularized counterparts.
In this scope, the Riemannian geometry provides useful tools to address and study (
32
), which is discussed next.

Riemannian options for computing solutions of (
32
)

The optimization problems expressed in (
32
) generally do not exhibit closed-form or fixed-point solutions and, thus, require the use of iterative algorithms to be evaluated.
In this setup, the Riemannian optimization framework is a good candidate in order to ensure that the variable remains in
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
along the iterations.
Beyond the introduction of the Riemannian gradient descent presented in Section
3.2
, this flexible framework extends to many other algorithms:

•

Conjugate gradient, or BFGS-type algorithms, require the notion of Riemannian vector transport operator
(
boumal2023introduction
, Section 10.3)
, which allows to transport tangent vectors between tangent spaces at different points.

•

Second-order methods, such as trust region or Newton methods
require the definition of the Riemannian Hessian
(
boumal2023introduction
, Section 5.5)
.

•

For large dimensional datasets, stochastic optimization methods can also be extended to the Riemannian setting
bonnabel2013stochastic
;
zhang2016riemannian
;
bouchard2020riemannian
.

A last remark is that in these algorithms, the metric is left as a choice that conditions the gradient and possibly the retraction.
There are various options for
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
(cf. Section
2.1
), with their respective pros and cons.
It is still noticed that the gradient obtained from the Fisher information metric, also referred to as the natural gradient
amari1998natural
, is generally experienced to yield a faster convergence when dealing with a cost function related to the statistical model of the data (cf. examples in
han2021riemannian
;
collas2023riemannian
).

Geodesic convexity on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}

The classical results on the existence and uniqueness of the MLEs
(
ollila2012complex
, Theorems 6 and 7)
do not directly extend to the formulation in (
32
), so one might inquire about the optimally of the solution obtained by reaching a local minimum of this problem.
In this scope, the Riemannian perspective offers some answers by generalizing the property of convexity.
First, we recall that the geometry induced by the Fisher information metric (
18
) yields geodesic curves
\gamma(t)
as defined in Theorem
2.3
between any two points
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
A function
f
is then said to be geodesically convex (
g
-convex) on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
if
\forall\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, it satisfies the inequality

f(\gamma(t))\leq(1-t)f(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{0})+tf(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}),~\forall t\in[0,1].

(33)

If the above inequality is strict, the function is then said to be strictly
g
-convex.
The
g
-convexity enjoys properties similar to those of the convexity in the standard Euclidean case, in particular:

Theorem 3.1

(Global minimizer of
g
-convex functions on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
).

Let
f:\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\to\mathbb{R}
be
g
-convex as defined in (
33
), then any local minimum of
f
over
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is a global minimum. Furthermore, if
f
is strictly
g
-convex, this global minimum is unique.

This property offers an alternate proof for the uniqueness of MLEs as in (
24
)
ollila2014regularized
, and had practical impacts for the design of regularized covariance matrix estimators as in (
32
): many examples of penalty functions (with various regularization effects) can be found in the overviews in
wiesel2015structured
;
duembgen2016geodesic
, and the references
auderset2005angular
;
Wiesel2012unified
;
Wiesel2012geodesic
;
wiesel2015structured
;
ollila2014regularized
;
duembgen2016geodesic
.

4

Intrinsic Cramér-Rao Bound for covariance matrix estimation

The Cramér-Rao inequality is a staple tool in statistics that characterizes the optimal mean-squared error an unbiased estimator can reach given a model and setup
kay1993fundamentals
.
This tool can either be used to validate estimation procedures, or to design systems so that a certain level of accuracy is guaranteed to be theoretically reachable.
While the Euclidean formulation of this inequality was briefly introduced in Section
1.1
, the so-called intrinsic Cramér-Rao bounds extend it to parameters living in a manifold, and for any chosen Riemannian metric.
This perspective is especially interesting as:

i
) some metrics can be more meaningful to assess the estimation performance in a given application;
ii)
a suitable Riemannian geometry (as opposed to the Euclidean one) can reveal hidden properties that make the bound more informative (such as curvature terms, intrinsic biases, etc.).
First, Section
4.1
introduces the background on intrinsic Cramér-Rao bound from
smith2005covariance
, where the C-CES model is used as a driving example.
We also refer the reader to
(
boumal2014thesis
, Chapter 6)
and the reference
barrau2013note
, for more details on the topic.
Then, Cramér-Rao bounds are derived for various distances in the context of covariance matrix estimation within C-CES distributions
breloy2018intrinsic
in Section
4.2
.

4.1

Introduction to intrinsic Cramér-Rao bounds

This subsection will present tools that can be applied to any chosen Riemannian geometry on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
The needed objects are the Riemannian metric, logarithm mapping and square of the distance, which are denoted
\prec\cdot,\cdot\succ_{\cdot}
,
\mathfrak{log}_{\cdot}(\cdot)
and
\mathfrak{d}^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
, respectively.
As in Section
3
, we consider the problem of estimating the matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
from a given
n
-sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
assumed to be iid from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
.
We denote
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
an estimator of this parameter; e.g. the MLE presented in Section
3
.
We then consider the evaluation of the performance of such estimator
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
.
To do so, we exploit the chosen Riemannian metric
\prec\cdot,\cdot\succ_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
.
Such metric can, for example, be the Fisher information one (
18
), or one of the many other options from the literature
thanwerdas2023n
.
The performance criterion is the resulting square of the Riemannian distance, i.e., the error is measured through
\mathfrak{d}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})
.
The intrinsic Cramér-Rao theory from
smith2005covariance
then allows us to obtain a lower bound on the expectation of this error for any unbiased estimator
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
.
Eventually, this retrieves the well-known inequality “
\mathbf{C}\succeq\mathbf{F}^{-1}
,” with
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times p^{2}}
being the covariance matrix of the estimation error and
\mathbf{F}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}\times p^{2}}
being the Fisher information matrix, where
p^{2}=\dim(\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p})
.
However, these parameters have different definitions due to the specific nature of the considered objects.
The point of this section is to briefly present the key ingredients to obtain such inequality and the corresponding main theorem.

First, we need to generalize the notion of estimation error vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}}
to the Riemannian context.
Notice that, in the Euclidean case, such vector is generally constructed by vectorizing the entry-wise subtraction of the covariance matrix
\mathbf{\Sigma}
to its estimate
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{\mathcal{E}}=\vech(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma})
, where
\vech(\cdot)
denotes the half-vectorization operator.
As it happens, from a Riemannian geometry point of view,
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}
corresponds to the Euclidean logarithm mapping at
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
Therefore, the Riemannian logarithm
\mathfrak{log}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})
provides a natural way to extend the error to any geometry.
It is indeed an element of the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
that “points towards”
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
, and whose norm corresponds to the Riemannian distance.
It remains to actually get an error vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}}
from
\mathfrak{log}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})
.
To do so, we leverage a basis
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}}
of
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}\simeq\mathcal{H}_{p}
that is orthonormal with respect to the chosen metric
\prec\cdot,\cdot\succ_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
.
In practice, such a basis can be obtained either analytically from mathematical calculations or numerically, thanks to the Gram-Schmidt orthonormalization process.
This basis yields the decomposition

\mathfrak{log}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\sum_{q=1}^{p^{2}}\varepsilon_{q}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q},

(34)

and we denote
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}=[\varepsilon_{1},\cdots,\varepsilon_{p^{2}}]\in\mathbb{R}^{p^{2}}
the corresponding coordinates error vector, obtained as

\varepsilon_{q}=\;\prec\mathfrak{log}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}),\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\succ_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}.

(35)

Moreover, the norm of this vector corresponds to the Riemannian distance between
\mathbf{\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
, i.e.,

\mathfrak{d}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\,\prec\mathfrak{log}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}),\mathfrak{log}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\succ_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}=\|\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\|_{2}^{2},

(36)

which will be instrumental in the next derivations.
The basis
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}}
also yields a Fisher information matrix
\mathbf{F}
, with entries

\mathbf{F}_{q\ell}=\,\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q},\boldsymbol{\xi}_{\ell}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}.

(37)

The matrix
\mathbf{F}
represents the Fisher information metric of Theorem
2.1
according to this system of coordinates.
Then, from
(
smith2005covariance
, Corrolary 2)
, we obtain Theorem
4.1
.

Theorem 4.1
(Intrinsic Cramér-Rao bound)

Let
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
Let
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
a iid
n
-sample from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
. Let
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
an unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
with corresponding error vector
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
defined in (
35
).
Then

\mathbf{C}=\text{E}\left[\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}^{T}\right]\succeq\mathbf{F}^{-1}+\textup{ curvature terms},

where
\mathbf{F}
is the Fisher information matrix in (
37
) and the curvature terms – which are not detailed here – depend on the Riemannian curvature tensor corresponding to the chosen geometry and on
\mathbf{F}
; see
smith2005covariance
;
boumal2013intrinsic
;
boumal2014thesis
for further details.

In practice, the curvature terms can usually be neglected in Theorem
4.1
.
Furthermore, taking the trace of the inequality yields the desired result, i.e.,

\text{E}\left[\mathfrak{d}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\right]\geq\tr(\mathbf{F}^{-1}).

(38)

It offers a bound that can be derived for any chosen Riemannian distance
\mathfrak{d}^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
(and corresponding metric
\prec\cdot,\cdot\succ_{\cdot}
).

Remark 8

The inequality in Theorem
4.1
interestingly takes into account the curvature of the manifold, which, for
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, only depends on the chosen metric.
In the Euclidean case, such curvature term is null, and we recover the standard Cramér-Rao inequality.
We also notice that the theorem in
smith2005covariance
also incorporates an intrinsic bias terms, which was excluded here for the sake of conciseness.
This intrinsic bias (expectation of the Riemannian logarithm) depends on the estimator and the chosen metric, and can reveal unexpected properties.
A main example is that the MLE of the covariance matrix of the Gaussian model appears unbiased in the Euclidean setting, but is, in fact, biased when using the Fisher information metric
smith2005covariance
.
Such analysis thus opens prospects for improved estimation from the intrinsic perspective.

4.2

Bounds for various matrix distances in C-CES distributions

This Section presents the derivation of special cases of Theorem
4.1
when considering various usual metrics.
Hence, it yields intrinsic Cramér-Rao bounds for the problem of covariance matrix estimation in C-CES distributions for the corresponding Riemannian distances.
Since the Fisher information metric is already obtained in Theorem
2.1
, the derivation boils down to the following steps:

a)

Selecting the performance metric
\prec\cdot,\cdot\succ_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}
and computing
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}}
, a corresponding orthonormal basis of
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
;

b)

Computing the elements of the Fisher information matrix with this basis, according to (
37
);

c)

Inverting the Fisher information matrix, applying Theorem
4.1
, then (
38
).

These operations are conducted in the following for the Euclidean metric, the so-called natural Riemannian metric (the affine invariant metric (
18
) with
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
), and the Fisher-Rao metric of the assumed model (i.e., the metric of Theorem
2.1
: (
18
) with
\alpha=\alpha_{g}
and
\beta=\beta_{g}
, where
\alpha_{g}
and
\beta_{g}
are defined in (
9
)).
In order for the chosen values of
\alpha
and
\beta
to be clear, in this subsection, the metric (
18
) is denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\cdot}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and the distance of Theorem
2.4
is denoted
\delta_{(\alpha,\beta)}^{2}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Euclidean distance

We first recall the elementary tools of the Euclidean metric for
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\text{Metric:}&&\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\mathcal{E}}=\reel(\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\\[2.84544pt]
\text{Logarithm:}&&\log^{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}\\[2.84544pt]
\text{Distance:}&&\delta_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\|\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}-\mathbf{\Sigma}\|_{2}^{2}.\end{array}

(39)

A basis of the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
that is orthonormal with respect to the metric in (
39
) can be obtained as follows:

1.

For
1\leq i\leq p
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{ii}
is a
p\times p
symmetric matrix whose
i^{\textup{th}}
diagonal element is one, zeros elsewhere

2.

For
1\leq i<j\leq p
,
\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}
is a
p\times p
symmetric matrix whose
ij^{\textup{th}}
and
ji^{\textup{th}}
elements are both
\nicefrac{{1}}{{\sqrt{2}}}
, zeros elsewhere.

3.

For
1\leq i<j\leq p
,
\bar{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{\mathcal{E}}_{ij}
is a
p\times p
Hermitian matrix whose
ij^{\textup{th}}
and
ji^{\textup{th}}
elements are
\nicefrac{{\sqrt{-1}}}{{\sqrt{2}}}
and
\nicefrac{{-\sqrt{-1}}}{{\sqrt{2}}}
, respectively, zeros elsewhere.

To shorten notations, we simply denote this basis
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}^{\mathcal{E}}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}},
where the
p^{2}
elements are ordered following items 1), 2), and 3).
The squared Euclidean distance between an estimator
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
and the true value
\mathbf{\Sigma}
also corresponds to the summed squared errors on the coordinates in this basis.
We then have the following result:

Theorem 4.2
(Cramér-Rao bound on Euclidean distance)

Let
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
an unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{\Sigma}
built from iid data
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
.
The Euclidean distance between
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is bounded in expectation as

\mathbf{C}_{\mathcal{E}}=\text{E}\left[\delta_{\mathcal{E}}^{2}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}},\mathbf{\Sigma})\right]\geq\tr(\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{E}}^{-1}),

where

\left[\mathbf{F}_{\mathcal{E}}\right]_{q\ell}=\reel(n\alpha_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{q}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}))+n\beta_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{q})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell})),

with
\alpha_{g}
and
\beta_{g}
defined in (
9
).

2.1
.

Proof

The result is a direct application of Theorem
4.1
and (
38
) using the basis
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{j}^{\mathcal{E}}\}_{j=1}^{p^{2}}
.

Remark that this corresponds to the Euclidean Cramér-Rao bounds obtained for several distributions in
greco2013cramer
;
pascal2010statistical
;
besson2013fisher
;
mitchell1989information
. Also notice that we retrieve the same result as
(
smith2005covariance
, Theorem 5)
for the Gaussian distribution, i.e.,
\alpha_{g}=1
and

.

Natural Riemannian distance

The natural Riemannian distance refers to the distance induced by the affine invariant metric (
18
) with the standard choice of coefficients
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
.
The elementary tools for this metric for
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
are

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\text{Metric:}&&\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(1,0)}=\reel(\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\\[2.84544pt]
\text{Logarithm:}&&\log_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\;=\;\mathbf{\Sigma}\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\\[2.84544pt]
\text{Distance:}&&\delta_{(1,0)}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\|\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\|_{2}^{2}.\end{array}

(40)

Recall that the full description of this geometry is provided in Section
2
.
A basis of the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
that is orthonormal with respect to the metric in (
40
) can be obtained by coloring the canonical basis of previous section as

\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}^{(1,0)}=\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}^{\mathcal{E}}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(41)

The whole basis is denoted
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}^{(1,0)}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}}
.
We then have the following result:

Theorem 4.3
(Cramér-Rao bound on natural Riemannian distance)

Let
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
an unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
built from iid data
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
.
The Riemannian distance between
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is bounded in expectation as

\text{E}\left[\delta_{(1,0)}^{2}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}},\mathbf{\Sigma})\right]\geq\frac{1}{n}\left(\frac{p^{2}-1}{\alpha_{g}}+\frac{1}{\alpha_{g}+p\beta_{g}}\right),

(42)

with
\alpha_{g}
and
\beta_{g}
defined in (
9
).

Proof

Plugging the basis
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{q}^{(1,0)}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}}
of
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
defined in (
41
) in (
37
) yields

\left[\mathbf{F}_{(1,0)}\right]_{q\ell}=\langle\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1,0)}_{q},\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(1,0)}_{\ell}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}=\reel(n\alpha_{g}\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell})+n\beta_{g}\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{q})\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell})).

Hence, from the relations

\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{q}\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell})=\delta_{q\ell}~~~~~~~~\text{and}~~~~~~~~\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{q})\tr(\boldsymbol{\xi}^{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell})=\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}1~\text{if}~(q,\ell)\in\llbracket 1,p\rrbracket^{2}\\
0~\text{otherwise},\end{array}\right.

we obtain the Fisher information matrix

\mathbf{F}_{(1,0)}=n\alpha_{g}\mathbf{I}_{p^{2}}+n\beta_{g}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{1}_{p\times p}&\mathbf{0}_{p\times p(p-1)}\\
\mathbf{0}_{p(p-1)\times p}&\mathbf{0}_{p(p-1)\times p(p-1)}\end{bmatrix},

which is expressed as
\mathbf{F}_{(1,0)}=n\alpha_{g}\mathbf{I}_{p^{2}}+np\beta_{g}\mathbf{v}\mathbf{v}^{T}
with unitary vector
\mathbf{v}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{p}}\left[~\mathbf{1}_{p}~|~\mathbf{0}_{p(p-1)}~\right]
, i.e.
\mathbf{v}^{T}\mathbf{v}=1
.
Hence, the inverse of the Fisher information matrix can be obtained by the Sherman-Morrison formula.
In particular, its vector of eigenvalues can be directly identified as
\frac{1}{n}\left[(\alpha_{g}+p\beta_{g})^{-1},\alpha_{g}^{-1},\ldots,\alpha_{g}^{-1}\right]
and summed to obtain its trace. Theorem
4.1
and (
38
) are then applied to conclude.

Remark 9

Contrarily to the Euclidean case of Theorem
4.2
, the bound on the natural Riemannian distance in Theorem
4.3
does not depend on the parameter
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
This is generally a desirable property, as it offers an interpretation grounded solely on intrinsic dimensions of the problem.
Additionally, simulation examples in Section
4.3
show that assessing the error with such criterion (that is more in accordance with the nature of the parameter) can also reveal unexpected properties of the estimates.

Fisher-Rao distance

The Fisher-Rao distance refers to the geodesic distance associated with the Fisher information metric (cf. Section
2.3
).
A subtlety is that we voluntarily omit the dependency on
n
of the Fisher information metric of Theorem
2.1
, i.e., the bound will be obtained for using a generic metric in (
18
) with
\alpha=\alpha_{g}
and
\beta=\beta_{g}
.
This distinction has two main reasons:

i
) it appears more logical to evaluate performance with a distance whose expression does not vary with the sample support of the scenario
n
;

ii
) this allows us to also stress that, though identical, two metrics play a separate role in the derivations: one is inherent to the statistical model, the other is a choice made to measure estimation accuracy.
Hence, the elementary tools on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
are

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\text{Metric:}&&\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\boldsymbol{\eta}\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}=\reel(\alpha_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi}\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta})+\beta_{g}\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\xi})\tr(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\eta}))\\[2.84544pt]
\text{Logarithm:}&&\log_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\;=\;\mathbf{\Sigma}\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\\[2.84544pt]
\text{Distance:}&&\delta_{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}^{2}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\alpha_{g}\|\log(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})\|_{2}^{2}+\beta_{g}(\log\det(\mathbf{\Sigma}^{-1}\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}))^{2}.\end{array}

(43)

Recall that full details on this geometry are provided in Section
2
.
Contrary to previous geometries, since the considered metric is the Fisher information one, we do not actually need to compute a basis of the tangent space
T_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
to obtain the bound.
However, notice that if needed, such a basis can be obtained using the Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization process.
In this case, the Cramér-Rao bound is:

Theorem 4.4
(Cramér-Rao bound on Fisher-Rao distance)

Let
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
be an unbiased estimator of
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
built from iid data
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
drawn from
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma},g)
.
The Fisher-Rao distance between
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}
is bounded in expectation as

\text{E}\left[\delta_{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}^{2}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}},\mathbf{\Sigma})\right]\geq\frac{p^{2}}{n}.

Proof

By definition, we have
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{\textup{FIM}}=n\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}
.
Hence, since the basis of interest, denoted
\{\boldsymbol{\xi}^{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}_{q}\}_{q=1}^{p^{2}}
, is orthonormal according to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{\mathbf{\Sigma}}^{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}
, it follows that
\mathbf{F}_{(\alpha_{g},\beta_{g})}=n\mathbf{I}_{p^{2}}
.
The trace of its inverse is therefore
p^{2}/n
and the proof is concluded by applying Theorem
4.1
and (
38
).

We notice that Theorems
4.3
and
4.4
coincide in the Gaussian case (
\alpha_{g}=1
and
\beta_{g}=0
).

Remark 10

Theorem
4.4
actually exemplifies a more of universal result, which illustrates that the Fisher-Rao distance is the most in accordance with the underlying statistical model.
Indeed, the proof strategy of Theorem
4.4
holds for any geometry induced by a statistical model (parameter manifold and probability density function).
Thus, the Fisher-Rao distance will always be bounded by a ratio between the intrinsic problem dimension and the number of samples.

5

Riemannian classification with the Fisher-Rao distance

Classification is a ubiquitous task in machine learning.
From a statistical point of view, the problem generally consists of attributing a class to each sample (or batch of samples) from an unlabelled mixture of different distributions.
The Fisher-Rao geometry provides a tool that can be efficiently leveraged in this context: as most classification methods are based on the Euclidean distance between samples, these can be transposed to the Riemannian setting by using the Fisher-Rao distance on the statistical feature space (i.e., the parameters of the assumed model).
Such transposition is often beneficial as it leverages a metric that is in accordance with the model (e.g., it can account for its natural geometric invariance).
In this regard, Section
5.1
presents a generic framework driven by the Fisher-Rao geometry.
An example based on CES models and the nearest centroïd classifier is derived in Section
5.2
and applied to EEG recordings in Section
5.3
.

5.1

A Fisher-Rao Riemannian classification framework

The use of statistical features (or descriptors) is common in batch sample classification, as these tend to be more discriminative than raw data.
Interestingly, when assuming a statistical model for the batches, the model parameters appear as a natural choice for such statistical features, and the Fisher-Rao distance as a natural tool to compare them.
For example, assuming two C-CES models with the same probability density function
f
, but different parameters
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}
and
\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}
, the Fisher-Rao distance (cf. Theorem
2.4
and (
43
)) acts distance between statistical models through the following relation:

\underbrace{\delta_{\rm FR}(f(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1}),f(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}))}_{\text{dist. between models}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\underbrace{\delta_{\textup{FR}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{1},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{2}).}_{\text{FR-dist. between parameters}}

(44)

In practice, we handle empirical distributions (i.e., batches of samples), so this distance can be evaluated as:

\underbrace{\hat{\delta}_{\textup{FR}}(\{\mathbf{x}_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{\mathbf{x}_{i,2}\}_{i=1}^{n})}_{\text{dist. between batches}}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\underbrace{\delta_{\textup{FR}}(\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{1},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{2}),}_{\text{FR-dist. between estimated covariances}}

(45)

where
\{\mathbf{x}_{i,1}\}_{i=1}^{n}
(resp.
\{\mathbf{x}_{i,2}\}_{i=1}^{n}
)
denotes a sample batch, and
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{1}
(resp.
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{2}
)
denotes an estimate of its covariance matrix, such as the maximum likelihood estimator presented in Section
3
.
From this perspective, a batch classification problem then turns into a problem of classifying covariance matrices on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
Such a task can be achieved by using a standard classification algorithm in which criteria and objects are carefully transposed according to the Fisher-Rao distance (rather than the Euclidean one).
For examples related to this setup: the Riemannian nearest centroïd (or minimum distance to mean) classifier
barachant2011multiclass
;
tuzel2008pedestrian
; the Riemannian
K
-means on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
was, e.g., used in
collas2021probabilistic
;
hippert2022robust
, Kernel methods based on Riemannian distances were studied in
barachant2013classification
;
jayasumana2016kernels
;
jayasumana2013kernel
, and Riemannian Gaussian mixture models on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
were proposed in
said2017riemannian
;
said2017gaussian
.
The following section presents the Riemannian counterpart of the nearest centroid classifier for
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.

Remark 11

Beyond C-CES models, the presented framework generalizes to a generic (model-driven) Riemannian classification methodology, which can be summarized as follows:

i
) Model selection: we assume an underlying statistical model, whose parameters should differ between classes;

ii
) Statistical Feature extraction: we estimate the corresponding parameters for each batch;

iii
) Riemannian classification: the extracted features are classified by leveraging the Fisher-Rao distance.

5.2

Nearest centroïd classifier on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
for C-CES models

The Riemannian center of mass corresponding to the framework discussed in Section
5.1
when assuming a Gaussian model has been the reference method to classify electroencephalography (EEG) recordings for the past decade
barachant2011multiclass
.
This section extends this methodology to the C-CES distributions, and presents the necessary tools to compute the Riemannian center of masses on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.

Formally, we focus here on the supervised classification of batches of data.
Formally, given an unknown batch of a
n
-sample
\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
and
z
fixed classes, a classifier
\mathcal{C}:(\mathbb{C}^{p})^{n}\to\llbracket 1,\dots,z\rrbracket
infers the class label
y\in\llbracket 1,\dots,z\rrbracket
, i.e.,

y=\mathcal{C}\left(\{\mathbf{x}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\right).

(46)

To provide accurate results, the classifier
\mathcal{C}
is trained on
m
batches of
n
samples
\{\{\mathbf{x}_{i,j}\}_{i=1}^{n},y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
associated to known class labels
y_{j}\in\llbracket 1,\dots,z\rrbracket
.
In practice, one usually aims to evaluate the accuracy of a classifier on some dataset
\mathcal{T}
.
To do so, the dataset is split into training and test sets, denoted

\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{test}}
, respectively.
The classifier
\mathcal{C}
is trained on
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}
and prediction is performed on the testing set
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{test}}
.
Predicted labels are then compared to actual labels, which yields the accuracy of
\mathcal{C}
on the considered dataset.
Notice that there are different ways to build
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{test}}
from
\mathcal{T}
, see e.g., the documentation of scikit-learn
pedregosa2011scikit
for more details.

For the model selection step, we consider that each batch
\{\mathbf{x}_{i,j}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is distributed according to
\mathbf{x}\sim\text{C-CES}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j},g)
.
The statistical parameter extraction step is then performed by maximum likelihood estimation on each batch (cf. Section
3
).
From there, the feature classification problem is set as
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{j},y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
We then exploit the Fisher-Rao distance
\delta
of C-CES distribution defined in Theorem
2.4
to generalize the nearest centroïd classifier, also referred to as minimum distance to mean (MDM) classifier, to
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, .
This classification algorithm consists of two steps:

•

First, it computes the center of mass of each class, also called class center, from covariance matrices in the training set
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}
.

•

Then, it assigns the label of the closest class center to each covariance in
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{test}}
.

Since the covariance matrices lie on the Riemannian manifold
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, the geodesic distance
\delta
from Theorem
2.4
is leveraged in both steps.

We now detail the first step.
For every class
y\in\llbracket 1,\dots,z\rrbracket
, one must compute the class center
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{(y)}
from the training set
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}
.
It is the center of mass of the set
\{\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}:y_{j}=y\}
.
We thus need to be able to compute the center of mass
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}
of a set
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
of matrices in
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
according to the Fisher-Rao distance in Theorem
2.4
.
Following
karcher1977riemannian
, the Riemannian center of mass is defined in the following Definition
1
.

Definition 1
(Riemannian center of mass on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
)

The center of mass
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{\star}
of
\{\mathbf{\Sigma}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is defined as the minimizer of the variance computed with the geodesic distance

\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{\star}=\argmin_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}}\quad V(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})

(47)

with
\quad V(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})\overset{\text{def}}{=}\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\delta^{2}(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}},\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}).

Remark that if the Riemannian distance
\delta
is replaced by its Euclidean counterpart,
\delta_{\mathcal{E}}(\mathbf{\Sigma},\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}})=\|\mathbf{\Sigma}-\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}\|_{2}
, then the minimizer of
V
becomes the arithmetic mean
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}
.
Unfortunately, for the Riemannian case, a closed-form solution of (
47
) remains unknown
moakher2005differential
except in very specific cases (
m=2
, commuting matrices, …).
Hence, one must turn to an iterative optimization procedure.
As in
pennec2006riemannian
, we focus here on a Riemannian gradient descent on
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
Recall from Section
3
that, to employ this algorithm, we need to compute the Riemannian gradient of (
47
), choose a retraction and a step size rule.
The Riemannian gradient of
V
was derived in
karcher1977riemannian
;
moakher2005differential
, and is provided in Proposition
48
.

Proposition 3
(Riemannian gradient of
V
)

the Riemannian gradient
\grad V(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})
of the variance
V
defined in (
47
) at
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
is

\grad V(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\log_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})=-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\log(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.

(48)

Proof

In
moakher2005differential
, a technical proof directly deriving the distance of Theorem
2.4
for
\alpha=1
and
\beta=0
is provided.
Here, we propose a more general Riemannian geometry proof, which do not depend on the distance or the manifold.
The proved result is well-known and can for instance be found in
pennec2017hessian
without proof.
Given
\mathbf{\Sigma}\in\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
, we aim to show that the gradient of the function
v(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})=\frac{1}{2}\delta^{2}(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}},\mathbf{\Sigma})
is
\grad v(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})=-\log_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})
, where
\log_{\cdot}(\cdot)
is the Riemannian logarithm mapping corresponding to the Riemannian distance
\delta(\cdot,\cdot)
.
Let
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}(t)
the geodesic such that
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}(0)=\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}
and
\dot{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}\in\mathcal{H}_{p}
.
It follows that
\Diff v(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}(t))\right|_{t=0}
.
Let
\gamma_{t}
the geodesic joining
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}(t)
to
\mathbf{\Sigma}
.
By construction,
H(s,t)=\gamma_{t}(s)
is a variation of the geodesic
\gamma_{0}

(
gallot1990riemannian
, Definition 3.24)
.
Furthermore, we have
\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}v(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}(t))\right|_{t=0}=\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(\gamma_{t})\right|_{t=0}
, where
E(\gamma_{t})=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{1}\langle\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{t}(s)}\mathrm{d}s
is the energy of the geodesic
\gamma_{t}
.
Let
Y(s)
such that
H(t,s)=\exp_{\gamma_{0}(s)}(tY(s))
.
From
(
gallot1990riemannian
, Theorem 3.31)
, we get the first variation formula of energy

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(\gamma_{t})=[\langle Y(s),\dot{\gamma}_{0}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{0}(s)}]_{0}^{1}-\int_{0}^{1}\langle Y(s),\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}_{0}(s)}\dot{\gamma}_{0}(s)\rangle_{\gamma_{0}(s)}\mathrm{d}s.

Since
\gamma_{0}
is a geodesic,
\nabla_{\dot{\gamma}_{0}(s)}\dot{\gamma}_{0}(s)=0
.
Hence, the second term vanishes.
Moreover,
Y(1)=\frac{1}{t}\log_{\gamma_{0}(1)}(\gamma_{t}(1))
.
Since
\gamma_{0}(1)=\gamma_{t}(1)=\mathbf{\Sigma}
,
Y(1)=0
.
We also have
Y(0)=\frac{1}{t}\log_{\gamma_{0}(0)}(\gamma_{t}(0))=\frac{1}{t}\log_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}(t))=\frac{1}{t}t\boldsymbol{\xi}=\boldsymbol{\xi}
.
It follows that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}E(\gamma_{t})=-\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},\dot{\gamma}_{0}(0)\rangle_{\gamma_{0}(0)}=\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},-\log_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\rangle_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}.

We thus get
\Diff v(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}})[\boldsymbol{\xi}]=\langle\boldsymbol{\xi},-\log_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}(\mathbf{\Sigma})\rangle_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}}
.
The result follows by identification.
One can then conclude the proof of the proposition by using the sum property of the gradient operator.

Then, the most common choice for the retraction is to take the Riemannian exponential mapping (
21
).
Furthermore, the stepsize in this case is often simply set to
1
.
It follows that, given some initialization
\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(0)}
, the sequence of iterates is

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k+1)}&=&\exp_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k)}}\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log_{\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k)}}(\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j})\right)\\[7.0pt]
&=&\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k)}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\exp\left(\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m}\log(\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k)}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\mathbf{\Sigma}_{j}\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k)}^{-\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}})\right)\mathbf{\bar{\Sigma}}_{(k)}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}.\end{array}

(49)

The variance
V
(
47
) is a strictly geodesically convex function over
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}

tang2021CESmean
.
Hence, its minimizer is unique.

The computation of the class centers being solved, we now turn to the second step of the nearest centroïd classifier: the assignment to a class

5.3

Application to EEG classification

One usually needs to classify EEG recordings in the context of brain-computer interfaces (BCI), where a subject interacts with a computer through brain activity.
There are several paradigms for BCI based on EEG.
The three main ones are: steady-states visually evoked potentials (SSVEP)
kalunga2016online
, motor imagery (MI)
tangermann2012review
, and event-related potentials (ERP)
arico2014influence
.
This example focuses on ERP data, where subjects are exposed to some stimuli (most often a visual one).
These induce a signal response in the brain: the so-called P300, which is a positive wave occurring 300 ms after the stimulus.
An ERP dataset consists in a set of trials separated into two classes: a target class (TA), for which the subject is exposed to a stimulus; and a non-target class (NT), for which there is no stimulus.
More specifically, we consider the BNCI2014
\_
009 dataset
arico2014influence
, which is available on the MOABB platform
5
5

5

https://github.com/NeuroTechX/moabb –
A standard benchmark platform for BCI.
.
This dataset contains data from 10 subjects, with 3 sessions each.
Data were acquired on 16 electrodes at 256 Hz and bandpass filtered between 0.1 Hz and 20 Hz.
Recordings were then downsampled to 128 Hz.
Each session of each subject contains 1728 trials of
0.8
s: 288 target and 1440 non-target ones.
Hence, each dataset (one session of one subject) yields
\mathcal{T}=\{\mathbf{X}_{j},y_{j}\}_{j=1}^{m}
in
\mathbb{R}^{p\times n}\times\{\textup{TA},\textup{NT}\}
, where
p=16
,
n=102
,
m=1728
and
z=2
.

To perform classification of ERPs, raw data are not directly used.
Instead, following
barachant2014plug
, augmented data are leveraged.
Given the training set
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}=\{\mathbf{X}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{{m_{\textup{train}}}}

\mathbf{P}_{\textup{TA}}=\frac{1}{m_{\textup{TA}}}\sum_{\underset{y_{j}=\textup{TA}}{j=1}}^{{m_{\textup{train}}}}\mathbf{X}_{j},

(51)

where
m_{\textup{TA}}
is the number of target trials in the training set
\mathcal{T}_{\textup{train}}
.
From there, augmented trials are defined as

\mathbf{\tilde{X}}_{j}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{P}_{\textup{TA}}\\
\mathbf{X}_{j}\end{bmatrix}.

(52)

Covariance matrices
\mathbf{\hat{\Sigma}}_{j}
are then estimated from these augmented trials both in the training and testing sets.
Finally, the nearest centroïd classifier in Algorithm
1
is applied on these augmented covariance.
We compare two different versions here:

1.

Gaussian version
: covariance matrices estimated through the sample covariance matrix (SCM) and nearest centroïd classifier employed with

and
\beta=0
.

2.

t

.

Considering that the SCM is much simpler to compute than the MLE of the
t

6

Conclusion

This chapter presented the Fisher-Rao geometry of C-CES distributions, and its practical uses in statistical signal processing and machine learning.
Remark that the methodology that consists in obtaining a Riemannian geometry from the Fisher information metric generalizes to any statistical model (assuming that the parameter space is a smooth manifold).
Hence, the approaches presented in this introduction can extend to many other models and applications.
Among other examples, such intrinsic analysis has been conducted for the estimation of rotations matrices
boumal2014cramer
and for other Lie groups related to tracking problems
labsir2021joint
;
labsir2023barankin
.
In other scopes more directly related to elliptical distributions, we can also mention that geometric tools were used for:

•

Structured covariance matrices
:
In many applications, the covariance matrix is known to satisfy some form of structural constraint, that can be exploited to reduce the dimension of the estimation problem (see, e.g.,
wiesel2015structured
;
sun2016robust
).
Geometric tools can then be leveraged by expressing the constrained space as a sub-manifold of
\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
.
For example: the Fisher information metric was used to obtain structured estimators in
meriaux2019robust
;
meriaux2020matched
;
A geometry of Toeplitz matrices was studied in
arnaudon2013riemannian
;
A framework for in probabilistic component analysis (low-rank structured covariance matrices) in C-CES was proposed in
bouchard2021riemannian
;
Kronecker products preserve geodesic convexity
Wiesel2012geodesic
,
ans such structure was considered in online covariance matrix estimation in
bouchard2021line
; geometry and structured covariance have also been considered for blind source separation
bouchard2021riemannian2
.

•

Non-centered models
:
The geodesics and Fisher-Rao distance of the model
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{CES}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})
for the mean-and-covariance product manifold
\mathbb{C}^{p}\times\mathcal{H}^{++}_{p}
remains intractable in the general case.
Even for the Gaussian distribution
\mathbf{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\mathbf{\Sigma})
, only special cases and approximations from geodesic triangles can be obtained
calvo1991explicit
;
tang2018information
;
collas2022use
.
Numerical methods to evaluate these geodesics and corresponding distances were proposed in
nielsen2023simple
;
nielsen2023fisher
.
Concerning estimation problems, Riemannian optimization was leveraged for non-centered mixture of scaled Gaussian distributions (a sub-family of C-CES distributions) in
collas2021tyler
;
collas2023riemannian
.

•

Mixture models
:
Mixtures of C-CES can occur within the samples (the observation is the sum of multiple independent contributions) or within batches (the sample set aggregating multiple classes of C-CES).
The within-sample mixture is typically used to cast robust models for probabilistic principal component analysis
chen2009robust
;
sun2015low
;
hong2021heppcat
.
In this context, geometric tools were developed for low-rank scaled Gaussian signal corrupted by white Gaussian noise in
collas2021probabilistic
.
The within-batch mixture corresponds to a typical sample-wise classification problem.
For this purpose,
g
-convex relaxations for Gaussian mixture models were studied in
hosseini2015matrix
.

As a final note, we also point out that information geometry also refers to a much broader field than the scope covered by this chapter
amari2016information
;
amari2021information
.
For comprehensive overviews of the many geometric structures behind families of probability distributions, we refer the readers to
nielsen2020elementary
;
nielsen2022many
.
