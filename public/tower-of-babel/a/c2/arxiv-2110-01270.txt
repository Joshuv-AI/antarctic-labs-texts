Completeness of the primitive recursive ω \omega -rule Thanks: The author was supported by John Templeton Foundation (“A new dawn of Intuitionism: Mathematical and Philosophical advances”, grant ID 60842).
Emanuele Frittaion

Abstract
. Shoenfield’s completeness theorem (1959) states that every true first order arithmetical sentence has a recursive ω \omega -proof encodable by using recursive applications of the ω \omega -rule. For a suitable encoding of Gentzen style ω \omega -proofs, we show that Shoenfield’s completeness theorem applies to cut free ω \omega -proofs encodable by using primitive recursive applications of the ω \omega -rule. We also show that the set of codes of ω \omega -proofs, whether it is based on recursive or primitive recursive applications of the ω \omega -rule, is Π 1 1 \Pi^{1}_{1} complete. The same Π 1 1 \Pi^{1}_{1} completeness results apply to codes of cut free ω \omega -proofs.

1.
Introduction

The
\omega
-rule

\displaystyle\penalty A(0)\ \ A(1)\ \ \cdots\ \ A(n)\ \ \cdots

{\omega\text{-}rule}

\displaystyle\forall xA(x)

has been subject to proof theoretic investigations since the 1930s. For a detailed account on the
\omega
-rule, including historical references, see Sundholm
[
12
]
. The most important application related to the
\omega
-rule is probably due to Schütte
[
9
]
with his ordinal analysis of Peano arithmetic (subsequently extended to ramified analysis) via cut elimination for
\omega
-arithmetic. Another milestone is Shoenfield’s completeness theorem
[
11
]
, which asserts that the recursive
\omega
-rule is complete for true arithmetical statements. (That this holds good for the unrestricted
\omega
-rule is almost trivial, by induction on the build up of a true sentence.)
Shoenfield’s result is essential for another completeness result, this time due to Feferman
[
2
]
, asserting the completeness for true arithmetical statements of certain transfinite recursive sequences (progressions) of arithmetic theories based on iterated reflection principles.

An
\omega
-proof is naturally represented as either a countable transfinite sequence of formulas (Hilbert style) or a countable well founded infinite tree of sequents (Gentzen style). In both cases, we are dealing with a second order object (countable set). Shoenfield’s completeness theorem refers to an inductive definition of Gödel numbers of Hilbert style
\omega
-proofs. The coding is based on recursive applications of the
\omega
-rule. Roughly, the index of a recursive enumeration
f
of Gödel numbers

of
\omega
-proofs of
A(n)
is the Gödel number of an
\omega
-proof of
\forall xA(x)
. The coding is
local
as opposed to
global
. The latter involves indices of recursive
\omega
-proofs. We shall refer to local codes based on recursive applications of the
\omega
-rule as to recursive local codes. Then Shoenfield’s completeness theorem says that an arithmetical sentence
A
is true if and only if there is a recursive local code of an
\omega
-proof of
A
. As expected, a recursive local code is the code of a recursive
\omega
-proof. Indeed, an
\omega
-proof is recursive if and only if it has a recursive local code.

It is natural to ask whether, under a suitable local coding of Gentzen style
\omega
-proofs, the same completeness result applies to codes of cut free
\omega
-proofs. For mere convenience, we will consider Tait one-sided sequent calculus. Sequents are finite sets of sentences
\Gamma
to be interpreted disjunctively. Let
\mathcal{C}_{rec}
be the set of recursive local codes of
\omega
-proofs based on Tait one-sided sequent calculus (Definition
2.1
). By implementing the
proof-search
completeness proof of
\omega
-arithmetic, one can see that this is the case. Therefore an arithmetical sentence
A
is true if and only if there is a local code in
\mathcal{C}_{rec}
of a cut free
\omega
-proof of
A
(cf. Theorem
2.2
).

It is also natural to ask what the complexity of
\mathcal{C}_{rec}
is. We show, not surprisingly, that this set is as complicated as possible, that is,
\Pi^{1}_{1}
complete (Theorem
2.3
). The author was not able to find this result in the literature. It turns out that a very small cut free fragment of
\mathcal{C}_{rec}
is already
\Pi^{1}_{1}
complete. Indeed, we just need the
\omega
-rule.

Note that by implementing the

\omega
-arithmetic (for languages with at least second order objects) with respect to
\omega
-models, one obtains in a rather neat way that the set of indices (global codes) for primitive recursive higher order
\omega
-proofs is
\Pi^{1}_{1}

[
5

, there exists a primitive recursive function

holds iff

is the index of a primitive recursive cut free

(One has to add logical axioms of the form

What about the primitive recursive
\omega

\mathcal{C}_{prec}
be the set of local codes based on primitive recursive applications of the
\omega
-rule. We shall refer to local codes in
\mathcal{C}_{prec}

\omega
-rule

An

.

is true. In particular, cut elimination does not hold unless we include vacuous rules such as, e.g., the repetition rule.

Let
\forall x\exists yA(x,y)
be a true
\Pi_{2}
sentence. For
\sigma=\langle n_{0},\ldots,n_{l}\rangle\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}}
, let
\Gamma_{\sigma}
be the sequent

\forall x\exists yA(x,y),\exists yA(\bar{n}_{0},y),\ldots,\exists yA(\bar{n}_{l},y).

Let
g
be a primitive recursive function such that
g(\sigma,m)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
is the code of a cut free
\omega
-proof of the sequent
\Gamma_{\sigma},A(\bar{n}_{0},\bar{m})
whenever
\sigma\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}}
and
A(\bar{n}_{0},\bar{m})
is true.

Define
h
as

\displaystyle h(e,\sigma)

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{cases}0&\text{ if }\sigma\notin\mathrm{\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}};\\
\langle\exists,\Gamma_{\sigma},\exists yA(\bar{n}_{0},y),g(\sigma,m)\rangle&\text{ if }\sigma(0)=n_{0}\text{ and }m<|\sigma|\\
&\text{ is least such that }A(\bar{n}_{0},\bar{m})\\
&\text{ holds};\\
\langle\omega,\Gamma_{\sigma},\forall x\exists yA(x,y),\Lambda n.\{e\}(\sigma{{}^{\smallfrown}}n)\rangle&\text{ otherwise. }\end{cases}

The function
h
is primitive recursive. By the recursion theorem, there is an index
e
such that
\{e\}(x)\simeq h(e,x)
for every
x
. Therefore
e
is the index of a primitive recursive function. Let

a=\langle\omega,\forall x\exists yA(x,y),\{e\}(\langle\rangle)\rangle.

Then
a
is as desired.

We now turn to the general case. We will obtain the completeness of the cut free fragment
\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-}
by showing how to convert codes in
\mathcal{C}_{rec}
into codes in
\mathcal{C}_{prec}
that preserve cut free
\omega
-proofs (Theorem
3.8
).

We first need a lemma. Let
\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
consist of all total functions from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{N}
.

Definition 3.6
.

For every
a\in\mathbb{N}
and
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
define

a(f)=\langle a_{0},a_{1},\ldots\rangle\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}}\cup\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
such that
a_{0}=a
and, for every
i
,

•

if
a_{i}
is
\langle\land,\Gamma,A\land B,b_{0},b_{1}\rangle
or
\langle cut,\Gamma,C,b_{0},b_{1}\rangle
, then
a_{i+1}=b_{f(i)(\mathrm{mod}\ 2)}
,

•

if
a_{i}
is
\langle\lor,\Gamma,A\lor B,b\rangle
, then
a_{i+1}=b
,

•

if
a_{i}
is
\langle\omega,\Gamma,\forall xA(x),e\rangle
and
\{e\}(f(i))
is defined, then
a_{i+1}=\{e\}(f(i))
,

•

a_{i+1}
is undefined and
a(f)=\langle a_{0},\ldots,a_{i}\rangle
otherwise.

Lemma 3.7
.

If
a\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
then
a(f)\in\mathrm{\mathbb{N}^{<\mathbb{N}}}
for every
f\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the code.
∎

Theorem 3.8
.

There exists a primitive recursive function
f
such that:

(i)

a\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
if and only if
f(a)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
;

(ii)

a\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}^{-}
if and only if
f(a)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-}
;

(iii)

a\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
implies
\End(a)=\End(f(a))
.

The idea is to delay the application of a rule preceding an
\omega
-inference by iterated applications of the
\omega
-rule.

{\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty\enskip{\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{\kern 2.64117pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma_{i}\ \ (i\in|R|)\ \ $}}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=73.19904pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt${R}$}}}\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\Gamma,A(\bar{n})\ \ (n\in\omega)$}}}}\enskip\penalty$}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=90.86919pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt${\omega\text{-}rule}$}}}\hbox{\kern 18.1221pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\Gamma,\forall xA(x)\ \ $}}}}\ \ \ \rightsquigarrow\ \ \ {\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty\enskip{\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty\enskip{\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty\enskip{\vbox{\hbox spread0.0pt{\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{\kern 9.74301pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\penalty\Gamma_{i},\forall xA(x),A(\bar{u}),A(\bar{v})\ \ (i\in|R|)$}}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=165.22217pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt${R}$}}}\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\Gamma,\forall xA(x),A(\bar{n}),A(\bar{u}),A(\bar{v})\ \ \ (v\in\omega)$}}}}\enskip\penalty$}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=182.89232pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt${\omega\text{-}rule}$}}}\hbox{\kern 23.4369pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\Gamma,\forall xA(x),A(\bar{n}),A(\bar{u})\ \ (u\in\omega)$}}}}\enskip\penalty$}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=220.80096pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt${\omega\text{-}rule}$}}}\hbox{\kern 54.61348pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\Gamma,\forall xA(x),A(\bar{n})\ \ (n\in\omega)$}}}}\enskip\penalty$}\hskip 0.0pt}\hbox{\hbox{\kern 0.0pt\vrule height=2.75002pt,depth=-2.24998pt,width=258.70961pt\hbox{\kern 3.00003pt${\omega\text{-}rule}$}}}\hbox{\kern 107.04231pt\hbox{$\displaystyle\Gamma,\forall xA(x)$}}}}

Suppose that the proof on the left-hand side has code
\langle\omega,\Gamma,\forall xA(x),a\rangle
in
\mathcal{C}_{rec}
. Then for every
n
we iterate the
\omega
-rule until
\{a\}(n)
converges. For the sake of local correctness, we repeat the main formula
\forall xA(x)
. Once we know
\{a\}(n)
we keep working on the proof coded by
\{a\}(n)
. If it does not converge, the resulting (sub)proof of
\Gamma,\forall xA(x),A(\bar{n})
on the right-hand side is ill founded but still locally correct.

Proof.

By Kleene’s normal form theorem, there is a primitive recursive predicate
T
such that

\{a\}(n)\simeq U(\mu z.T(a,n,z)),

where
U
is primitive recursive.

By the recursion theorem we can find a primitive recursive function
g
such that

g(e,\Gamma,\Delta,\forall xA(x),a,n,z,u)\simeq

\displaystyle\simeq\begin{cases}\weak(\{e\}(U(z)),\Delta\cup A(\bar{u})\setminus A(\bar{n}))\\
\qquad\text{ if }T(a,n,z)\text{ and }\End(U(z))=\Gamma,A(\bar{n});\\
\langle\omega,\Delta\cup A(\bar{n})\cup A(\bar{u}),\forall xA(x),\Lambda v.g(e,\Gamma,\Delta\cup A(\bar{u}),\forall xA(x),a,n,z+1,v)\rangle\\
\qquad\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

By another application of the recursion theorem, there is an index
e
such that

\displaystyle\{e\}(\langle\text{Ax},\Gamma\rangle)

\displaystyle\simeq\langle\text{Ax},\Gamma\rangle,

\displaystyle\{e\}(\langle R,\Gamma,A,a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\rangle)

\displaystyle\simeq\langle R,\Gamma,A,\{e\}(a_{1}),\ldots,\{e\}(a_{n})\rangle,

\displaystyle\{e\}(\langle\omega,\Gamma,\forall xA(x),a\rangle)

\displaystyle\simeq\langle\omega,\Gamma\cup\forall xA(x),\forall xA(x),b\rangle,

with

b=\Lambda n.g(e,\Gamma,\Gamma\cup\forall xA(x),\forall xA(x),a,n,0,n).

By direct inspection, the index
e
defines a primitive recursive function
f
.

The proof that
f
is as desired is by induction on (the length of) the codes. We show items (i) and (iii). Item (ii) is analogous. The only case that requires some attention is when we are given a code
c
of the form
\langle\omega,\Gamma,\forall xA(x),a\rangle
. By definition,

f(c)=\langle\omega,\Gamma\cup\forall xA(x),\forall xA(x),b\rangle,

where
b
is as above. Note that
b
is the index of a primitive recursive function.

Suppose
c=\langle\omega,\Gamma,\forall xA(x),a\rangle\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
. Then
\{a\}
is total and
\{a\}(n)\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
with end sequent
\Gamma,A(\bar{n})
for every
n
. By the induction hypothesis,
f(\{a\}(n))\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
with end sequent
\Gamma,A(\bar{n})
and so
\weak(f(\{a\}(n)),\Delta)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
with end sequent
\Gamma,\Delta,A(\bar{n})
for every
\Delta
and every
n
. Therefore it is sufficient to show that for every
n
we have
g(e,\Gamma,\Gamma\cup\forall xA(x),\forall xA(x),a,n,0,n)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
with end sequent
\Gamma,\forall xA(x),A(\bar{n})
. Fix
n
and let
z
be least such that
T(a,n,z)
. Prove by induction on
i\leq z
that for every
\Delta\supseteq\Gamma,\forall xA(x)
and for every
u

g(e,\Gamma,\Delta,\forall xA(x),a,n,z-i,u)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}

with end sequent
\Delta,A(\bar{n}),A(\bar{u})
. For
i=0
, we have

g(e,\Gamma,\Delta,\forall xA(x),a,n,z,u)\simeq\\
\weak(f(\{a\}(n)),\Delta\cup A(\bar{u})\setminus A(\bar{n})).

This case follows by the above assumption on
f(\{a\}(n))
. The case with
i+1\leq z
follows by the induction hypothesis on
i
. In fact,

g(e,\Gamma,\Delta,\forall xA(x),a,n,z-i-1,u)\simeq\\
\langle\omega,\Delta\cup A(\bar{n})\cup A(\bar{u}),\forall xA(x),\Lambda v.g(e,\Gamma,\Delta\cup A(\bar{u}),\forall xA(x),a,n,z-i,v)\rangle.

For
i=z
,
\Delta=\Gamma\cup\forall xA(x)
and
u=n
, we obtain the desired result.

For the converse direction, let
c=\langle\omega,\Gamma,\forall xA(x),a\rangle
and suppose
f(c)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
. We aim to show that
\{a\}
is total and
\{a\}(n)\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
with end sequent
\Gamma,A(\bar{n})
for every
n
. Fix
n
. We know that
\{b\}(n)
is defined and
\{b\}(n)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
. We claim that
\{a\}(n)
is defined with
\End(\{a\}(n))=\Gamma,A(\bar{n})
. Suppose not. Fix any function
h\colon\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
. Then
(\{b\}(n))(h)=\langle b_{0},b_{1}\ldots\rangle
is infinite and every
b_{i}
is of the form
\langle\omega,\Delta,\forall xA(x),c_{i}\rangle
. This is seen by induction on
i
. By Lemma
3.7
, we obtain that
\{b\}(n)\notin\mathcal{C}_{prec}
, a contradiction.
Hence
\{a\}(n)
is defined and
\End(\{a\}(n))=\Gamma,A(\bar{n})
for every
n
. It remains to show that
\{a\}(n)\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
. By construction (see the first clause in the definition of
g
), there is a
\Delta
such that
\weak(f(\{a\}(n)),\Delta)
is a subcode of
f(c)
. Therefore
\weak(f(\{a\}(n)),\Delta)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}
and so is
f(\{a\}(n))
. Since
|f(\{a\}(n))|=|\weak(f(\{a\}(n)),\Delta\})|<|f(c)|
, we can apply the induction hypothesis and conclude
\{a\}(n)\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
, as desired. ∎

Note that for every
a\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
,
|a|\leq|f(a)|
, and every rule appearing in
\pi(f(a))
already occurs in
\pi(a)
.

Corollary 3.9
.

For every true arithmetical sentence
A
there is primitive recursive local code
a\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-}
such that
\pi(a)
is a primitive recursive cut free
\omega
-proof of
A
. Therefore,
\thm(\mathcal{C}_{rec})=\thm(\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-})
coincides with the set of true arithmetical sentences.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
2.2
and Theorem
3.8
.
The only part that requires some explanation is the claim that the
\omega
-proof encodable in
\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-}
is indeed primitive recursive. In fact, we have already observed that primitive recursive local codes do not correspond to primitive recursive
\omega
-proofs. Now, by Theorem
2.2
, a true sentence
A
has a primitive recursive cut free
\omega
-proof
\pi
with a local code
a
in
\mathcal{C}_{rec}^{-}
. It follows by Theorem
3.8
that
f(a)\in\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-}
is a local code of some recursive
\omega
-proof
\pi(f(a))
of
A
. The point is that the
\omega
-proof
\pi(f(a))
is primitive recursive for every
a\in\mathcal{C}_{rec}
(be
\pi(a)
primitive recursive or not). We leave this as an exercise for the reader.
∎

Corollary 3.10
.

\mathcal{C}_{prec}
and
\mathcal{C}_{prec}^{-}
are
\Pi^{1}_{1}
complete.

Proof.

It follows from Theorem
2.3
and Theorem
3.8
.
∎
