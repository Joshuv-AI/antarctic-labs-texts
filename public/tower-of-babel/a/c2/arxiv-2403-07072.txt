Explainable Learning with Gaussian Processes
Kurt Butler kurt.butler@stonybrook.edu

Abstract
The field of explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) attempts to develop methods that provide insight into how complicated machine learning methods make predictions. Many methods of explanation have focused on the concept of feature attribution, a decomposition of the model’s prediction into individual contributions corresponding to each input feature. In this work, we explore the problem of feature attribution in the context of Gaussian process regression (GPR). We take a principled approach to defining attributions under model uncertainty, extending the existing literature. We show that although GPR is a highly flexible and non-parametric approach, we can derive interpretable, closed-form expressions for the feature attributions. When using integrated gradients as an attribution method, we show that the attributions of a GPR model also follow a Gaussian process distribution, which quantifies the uncertainty in attribution arising from uncertainty in the model. We demonstrate, both through theory and experimentation, the versatility and robustness of this approach. We also show that, when applicable, the exact expressions for GPR attributions are both more accurate and less computationally expensive than the approximations currently used in practice. The source code for this project is freely available under MIT license at https://github.com/KurtButler/2024_attributions_paper .



Kurt Butler, Guanchao Feng, and Petar M. Djurić

1
Introduction

With the widespread adoption of machine learning (ML) methods across academia and industry, there has been an ever increasing desire to explain how complex artificial intelligence (AI) models make predictions
Samek et al. 2021
;
Angelov et al. 2021
. The ability to explain AI models, in this sense, is important to promote user trust in AI systems, and to mitigate the misuse of dubious predictions
Hess and Ditzler 2023
. However, the process of interpreting a model to gain physical insight is often difficult unless the model was intentionally designed to be interpretable
Peng et al. 2022
. Interpretable models are often transparent in how predictions are made, as is the case for linear models
Arrieta et al. 2020
and shallow decision trees
Silva et al. 2020
, or they contain components in their architecture that are designed for interpretability, such as physics-informed neural networks
Huang and Wang 2022
. The sentiment that many ML methods are ‘black boxes’ reflects the inherent difficulty of interpreting complicated, data-driven models like deep neural networks and kernel machines
Zhang et al. 2018
;
Rudin 2019
. Despite this, the ML paradigm has remained immensely useful, and the exploration of post-hoc explanations for black box models has been a popular subject in recent years, often referred to as explainable artificial intelligence (XAI).

A wide variety of distinct notions of explainability exist in the literature
Jin et al. 2022
. Many XAI approaches can be described as being either model-specific or model agnostic. Model-specific approaches attempt to formulate explanations by exploiting the known architecture of a ML model. In the context of neural networks, this includes methods that interpret activation weights
Springenberg et al. 2014
;
Bach et al. 2015
, graphs
Ying et al. 2019
, or attention mechanisms
Voita et al. 2019
to explain how predictions were made. Model-specific approaches also include the use of models that are inherently interpretable, including decision trees
Silva et al. 2020
and kernel machines with Automatic Relevance Determination (ARD)
Neal 1996
.

In contrast, model-agnostic approaches seek notions of explainability that can be applied to a wide range of models, with only generic assumptions about the type of data or properties of the model. Explanations of this type might measure the sensitivity of the prediction to perturbations at the input
Baehrens et al. 2010
;
Simonyan et al. 2014
or measuring changes in the model prediction after masking or perturbing input features
Robnik-Šikonja and Kononenko 2008
;
Štrumbelj et al. 2009
. In prediction tasks where images are inputs, a popular tool of this type is that of saliency maps, which are images that visually highlight the relevant features in a prediction
Adebayo et al. 2018
.
Another class of methods attempt to explain a prediction by showing relevant similar examples that were observed in the training set, sometimes called example-based explanations

van der Waa et al. 2021
.
Reverse image searching approaches attempt to find similar training examples to a given prediction and have been of particular interest in the field of image diffusion models
Carlini et al. 2023
.
Finally, many XAI methods can be understood through the invocation of an approximate, interpretable model, as seen in Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME)
Ribeiro et al. 2016
and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)
Lundberg and Lee 2017
.

Many XAI methods attempt to explain a prediction by associating a numerical score with each input feature. A natural approach to assigning this score is to attempt to measure the contribution of each feature to the output, that is, how much of the resulting prediction do we
attribute
to each input feature. This approach to explainability, called
feature attribution
, was popularized by
Štrumbelj and Kononenko 2014
, who introduced Shapley values to XAI. Shapley values were proposed by
Shapley 1953
as a method for determining what fraction of the total profit each player is owed in a coalitional game. In the context of XAI, this was reinterpreted to quantify how much each feature contributed to a numerical prediction. Since then, several other feature attribution approaches have been proposed, which we review in the next section.

In this paper, we will focus on Gaussian process regression (GPR) as a general non-parametric machine learning paradigm and assess the utility of post-hoc explanations for GPR models. This setting is intriguing because (a) interesting theoretical results are obtained from computing the attributions of a GPR model and (b) the resulting form of the GPR result is heavily dependent on the observed data set.
Since the GPR paradigm is data-driven, any post-hoc explanation in this setting must be compatible with the lack of explicit structural knowledge in the GPR approach. Acknowledging critiques of post-hoc explanations and the XAI paradigm
Rudin 2019
;
Ghassemi et al. 2021
;
de Bruijn et al. 2022
, we will argue that the attributions can remain useful to characterize aspects of the learned model, as long as the practitioners’ expectations are tempered to be in line with what information can actually be recovered from the attributions.

1.1
Related Work

Since our primary notion of explainability in this work is feature attribution, we briefly highlight existing related work in this area. The most well-known approach to feature attribution is that of Shapley values, originally developed from game theoretic principles for econometric applications by
Shapley 1953
. Shapley values benefit from an axiomatic definition that uniquely defines them, but this original formulation is only valid for discrete features
Aumann and Shapley 2015
. In continuous domains, the original axioms are insufficient to uniquely define the method
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
. Several extensions of the Shapley value to continuous features exist, including Aumann-Shapley
Aumann and Shapley 2015
and Shapley-Shubik
Friedman and Moulin 1999
. Other approaches to bring Shapley values to continuous domains have attempted the inclusion/deletion of features
Grömping 2007
, or through the invocation of a proxy model that locally explains the original model, including LIME
Ribeiro et al. 2016
and SHAP
Lundberg and Lee 2017
. The Integrated Gradients (IG) framework of
Sundararajan et al. 2017
generalizes the framework of Aumann-Shapley.
Sturmfels et al. 2020
and
Lundberg and Lee 2017
considered variations on the IG attribution theory where baselines are given by a distribution of possible baselines.

While the majority of research in feature attribution has concentrated on neural models, there are authors who explore Gaussian process (GP) models.
Chau et al. 2023
proposed a GP-based version of SHAP analysis to provide uncertainty quantification of Shapley values.
Seitz 2022
considered the application of IG attributions to GP models for regression and classification. Previous research has also focused on designing GPR models to be interpretable, typically through kernel design. This can be seen in the work on ARD
Neal 1996
and additive kernels
Duvenaud et al. 2011
.
Yoshikawa and Iwata 2021
considered a GP-based hierarchical model in which predictions are made using conditionally linear models of interpretable features, with the model weights derived from nonlinear functions of other non-interpretable features.

1.2
Contributions

This paper examines several aspects of attribution theory in the context of GP models. We show that the attributions represent a decomposition of the predictive model into a sum of component functions, and this decomposition preserves the differentiability of the original model. When a GP probability distribution is used to model the function space, we show that the attribution functions are also distributed according to GPs.

We derive exact expressions for the attributions of various common GPR models. The resulting expressions are straightforward to implement and check in code. We also comment on extensions of these results to more sophisticated kernels and GPR models.

Using the attribution GP, we gain the ability to measure the confidence of each attribution. We demonstrate through experimental data cases where these considerations are relevant to ML practitioners in medicine and industry.

We also compare the performance of exact attributions to various approximations that have appeared in the literature. These approximations are often asymptotic, and to the knowledge of the authors no experimental work has been done to ascertain how the convergence of these approximate solutions relates to the measured attributions.

2
The Feature Attribution Problem

Consider the setting of multivariable regression. Given observations
y_{n}
, for
n=1,...,N
, of an unknown function with corresponding input feature vectors
\mathbf{x}_{n}
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
, the goal is to estimate a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
that approximates the data, that is,
y_{n}\approx F(\mathbf{x}_{n})
. Once we have learned a particular model
F
for the data, we can make new predictions, and we may be interested to know how each of the
D
input features contribute to a given prediction. For example, if the function
F
is supposed to represent a clinical prediction, such as the time needed to recovery from an injury, then we may be interested to know what features in the prediction model are the most important for determining the patient’s outcome. The goal is that by understanding the relative importance of features used for prediction, we may be able to explain how the model leverages this information to make predictions.

As stated, the explainability problem is still vague, and there have been various interpretations of this idea that make the problem more concrete.
In the
baseline attribution problem

Lundstrom et al. 2022
, one ‘explains’ a prediction
F(\mathbf{x})
at a location
\mathbf{x}
by measuring how it differs from the prediction at a baseline location
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
(a reference point) and allocating a fraction of the observed difference to each input feature
x_{i}
. Generally, the attributions to each feature should add up to the difference between the prediction
F(\mathbf{x})
and the reference prediction
F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, which is called the
completeness
property. Many feature attribution approaches use a similar approach, most notably approaches based on Shapley values
Shapley 1953
. We will focus on the baseline attribution problem in this paper.

Notation:

Let
F:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function representing a regression model,
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a feature vector from which we are interested in making a prediction, and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
be a reference point.
The attribution of the prediction to the feature
x_{i}
is denoted by
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)
. When the function
F
is understood, we may suppress it from the notation and write simply
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})
.
Using this notation, the completeness property can be written as

F(\mathbf{x})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{D}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F).

(1)

We sometimes have the need to compute the attributions of a vector-valued function
\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})=[F_{1}(\mathbf{x})\cdots F_{Q}(\mathbf{x})]^{\top}
. In this case, we define the attribution vectors
\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{F})
by computing the attribution for each component of
\mathbf{F}
:

\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{F})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F_{1})\\
\vdots\\
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F_{Q})\end{bmatrix},\qquad i=1,...,D.

(2)

Finally, we use the notation
C^{k}(\mathcal{X})
to denote the set of all
k
-times continuously differentiable functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
k=1,2,...
or
k=\infty
. We may write
C^{k}
when the functions’ domain is clear from context.

2.1
Motivation: Bayesian Linear Regression

To motivate our work in this paper, it will be instructive to briefly consider the problem of Bayesian linear regression
(
Murphy 2022
, sec.11.7)
. Our reasons are twofold: Firstly, linear models provide a canonical notion of attribution which is generalized by the IG method, and second, it explicates how uncertainty in the model gives rise to uncertainty in the attributions.

Consider the typical Bayesian linear regression model, in which predictions
y
at a point
\mathbf{x}
are given a model of the form

y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x},\sigma^{2}),

where the noise (or residual) is tacitly assumed to be zero-mean, and
\sigma^{2}
is its variance. Predictions from this model are made using the expected value, so our predictive function is simply
F(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}(y|\mathbf{x},\mathbf{w})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
. After choosing an appropriate baseline point
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
, the difference of a given prediction to the baseline can always be expressed as a sum over the feature index
i
,

F(\mathbf{x})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\mathbf{w}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})=\sum_{i=1}^{D}w_{i}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i}).

In this way, the quantity
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})=w_{i}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})
is the canonical choice for the attribution to the
x_{i}
feature. Attributions of this form satisfy the completeness property, and are straightforward to interpret. The challenge of attribution methods for nonlinear models is to generalize this notion in a manner that is consistent with the linear case.

Now let us consider the situation in which the model parameters
\mathbf{w}
are random variables, and not fixed unknown parameters. In this way, attributions in a Bayesian setting should account for the uncertainty in the model. The proper way to do this is to take the attributions above, and to use the posterior distribution over
\mathbf{w}
to quantify our uncertainty in
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. We now do this for a simple example.

Suppose that we have a data set
\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n})|n=1,...,N\}
, which we compactly represent in matrix form:

\mathbf{X}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{x}_{N}^{\top}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times D},\qquad\mathbf{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
y_{N}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

If we assume a Gaussian prior for the model weights,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}),

(3)

for some fixed hyperparameters
\boldsymbol{\mu},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}
, then the posterior distribution for
\mathbf{w}
is also Gaussian and given by,

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}),

(4)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{X}\right)^{-1},

(5)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{\Sigma}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}+\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbf{X}^{\top}\mathbf{y}\right).

(6)

With the posterior distribution in mind, and observing that
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})=w_{i}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})
is just a scaled copy of
w_{i}
, it becomes easy to see thet the marginal distribution for each attribution is given by

\displaystyle attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})|\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{i}^{\prime}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i}),\Sigma_{ii}^{\prime}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}).

(7)

From this expression, we can immediately observe two important facts: the variance or uncertainty in the attribution comes from uncertainty in the model, and this uncertainty scales based on the distance from the baseline in the
x_{i}
coordinate. In Section
4
, we obtain similar results for the GPR model, which is a Bayesian non-parametric regression method. We note that the GPR case parallels the basic results shown here.

2.2
Integrated Gradients

Consider a function
F:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
which we use as a predictive model and for which we want to compute the attributions. The Integrated Gradient (IG) method defines the
attribution
of the prediction to the
i
th input feature, given a specific input
\mathbf{x}
, to be

attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}}dt.

(8)

The expression in (
8
) can be motivated from two perspectives, one using geometric ideas and the other using axioms. Both perspectives are useful for gaining insight into how we interpret IG attributions.

2.2.1
Geometric Interpretation

The fundamental theorem of line integrals states that the line integral of a gradient equals the difference of the function evaluated at the endpoints
(
Lee 2012
, p. 291)
. For a continuously differentiable function
F:\mathbb{R}^{D}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\int_{\Gamma}\nabla F\cdot d\mathbf{x}=F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(1))-F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(0)),

where

\nabla=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{1}}&\cdots&\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{D}}\end{bmatrix}^{\top}

is the gradient operator, and
\Gamma
is a path in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
parameterized by
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)
for
0\leq t\leq 1
. The left-hand side of the equation can be expanded to be expressed in terms of the partial derivatives, which reveals that the integral may be expressed as a sum of path integrals. In particular, if we define the path
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, then we may write

\displaystyle\int_{\Gamma}\nabla F\cdot d\mathbf{x}=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial\gamma_{i}}\frac{d\gamma_{i}}{dt}dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial\gamma_{i}}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})dt

(9)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{D}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}}dt.

(10)

The terms in the sum in (
10
) are precisely the IG attributions in (
8
). This perspective allows us to see that the completeness property, (
1
), is automatically satisfied for any attributions that are defined by a path, although we must pick a specific path to yield the IG attributions.
The integral
\int_{0}^{1}\partial F/\partial x_{i}dt
measures the average value of
\partial F/\partial x_{i}
between the baseline
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
and the prediction at
\mathbf{x}
, and acts as a nonlinear analogue to the weights of a linear model.

If the function
F
has constant partial derivatives,
\partial F/\partial x_{i}=w_{i}
, for each
i
, then
F
is a linear function. In this case,
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\partial F/\partial x_{i}dt=w_{i}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})
, which agrees with what we defined for the linear model. For nonlinear functions
F
, the IG attributions generalize the linear case by considering the average value of the gradient between the baseline and the new prediction.

Since the fundamental theorem of line integrals is not exclusive to differentiable models, but also applies to any continuous, piecewise-differentiable model, one can apply IG attributions to a much wider family of models and maintain the same geometric motivation. This includes ReLU networks
Glorot et al. 2011
;
Krizhevsky et al. 2012
, splines
Wahba 1990
, and GPs with deep kernel learning
Wilson et al. 2016
.

2.2.2
Axiomatic Interpretation

In the geometric approach to defining the IG attributions, we arbitrarily chose
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)
to be the straight-line path
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
. In principle, any path
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)
that connects
\mathbf{x}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
could define a set of attributions, but a different choice of path can yield different values for the attributions. To this end,
Sundararajan et al. 2017
and
Lundstrom et al. 2022
investigated the uniqueness of IG attributions. Building upon earlier work by
Friedman 2004
, they proved that IG attributions, with
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, are uniquely determined by the seven axioms listed below.

We note that several of these axioms are related to those that define Shapley values
Mitchell et al. 2022
. However, Shapley values were originally defined for discrete covariates, and additional axioms are required to uniquely extend Shapley values to the continuous setting. If a different set of axioms are selected, then the resulting attribution operator may be different and represent a different attribution theory.
Sundararajan and Najmi 2020
further discuss how different axioms induce different theories of feature attribution.

1.

Sensitivity(a).
If
\mathbf{x}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
differ in one coordinate (
x_{i}\neq\tilde{x}_{i}
and
x_{j}=\tilde{x}_{j}
for
j\neq i
), and also
F(\mathbf{x})\neq F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, then
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\neq 0
.

2.

Sensitivity(b)
, also known as Dummy
Friedman 2004
. If
F
is a constant function with respect to
x_{i}
, then
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=0
. In particular, this means that if
\partial F/\partial x_{i}=0
everywhere, then the attribution is zero.

3.

Implementation invariance.

The attributions should depend only on the mathematical properties of the predictive model, and not on a particular architecture or implementation.
If
F,G
are two models such that for all
\mathbf{x}
,
F(\mathbf{x})=G(\mathbf{x})
, then

attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|G).

4.

Linearity
. If
F
can be expressed as a weighted sum,
F(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}F_{m}(\mathbf{x})
, then the attributions of
F
are also a weighted sum:

attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=attr_{i}\left(\mathbf{x}\bigg|\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}F_{m}\right)=\sum_{m=1}^{M}w_{m}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F_{m}).

5.

Completeness
. The attributions sum to the difference between the prediction and baseline,

\sum_{i=1}^{D}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=F(\mathbf{x})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}).

6.

Symmetry-preserving
. For a fixed pair
(i,j)
, define
\sigma_{ij}(\mathbf{x})
to be
\mathbf{x}
but with the entries
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
swapped. We say that
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are
symmetric w.r.t. a function
F
if
F(\sigma_{ij}(\mathbf{x}))=F(\mathbf{x})
.
An attribution method is
symmetry-preserving
if

attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=attr_{j}(\mathbf{x}|F),

whenever
x_{i}=x_{j}
,
\tilde{x}_{i}=\tilde{x}_{j}
, and
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are symmetric w.r.t.
F
.

7.

Non-decreasing positivity
. A path
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)
in
\mathbb{R}^{D}
is called
monotone
for each
i=1,...,D
, we have that either
d\gamma_{i}(t)/dt>0
or
d\gamma_{i}(t)/dt<0
for all
t
.
Non-decreasing positivity
asserts that if
F
is non-decreasing along
every
monontone path from
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
to
\mathbf{x}
, then
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\geq 0
for all
i=1,...,D
.

Given these seven axioms, uniqueness can be claimed when
F\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
,
that is, when
F
is continuously differentiable
Lundstrom et al. 2022
.
Whether or not a machine learning model satisfies requirements like
F\in C^{1}
depends on the model architecture.
The differentiability requirement is often automatically satisfied when using GPR, which we discuss in Section
3.2
.

2.3
Linear Operators

The basic motivation for feature attribution so far has focused on the case in which we have a fixed input
\mathbf{x}
from which we want to make a prediction. However, as we vary the point
\mathbf{x}
continuously throughout the input space, the attributions
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})
also change continuously. For this reason, it may be fruitful to not only consider
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)
as a fixed quantity, but rather as a function of the input
\mathbf{x}
.

We define the
attribution operator

attr_{i}
to be the linear operator that takes a base function
F
and produces the attribution function
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)
. In this way, completeness can be viewed as a decomposition of the predictive function
F
into components that correspond to the attributions to each feature. The attribution operators also preserve smoothness, since
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
whenever
F\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
holds.
1
1

1

To see this, notice that IG attributions in (
8
) can be defined in 4 steps: taking a derivative, precomposing with a path
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, integrating against
t
, and then scaling by
x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i}
. All of these operations preserve the
C^{\infty}
property.
Changing the baseline
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
will yield a different set of attribution operators, but they share the same properties regardless of the particular choice of
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
. For this reason, the baseline
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
may be considered as a hyperparameter.

In Section
4
, linearity of the attribution operator is the main property needed to derive results for GPR models.
While we only analyze IG attributions in detail, the operator theoretic perspective clarifies how our results can be extended to other attribution theories.

3
Gaussian Process Regression

In this section, we briefly review the basics of GPR and establish our notation. The basic GPR model assumes that the data to be predicted
y_{n},n=1,...,N
, can be represented by an unknown function
F
of known input vectors
\mathbf{x}_{n}
for each point, with a constant additive noise:

y_{n}=F(\mathbf{x}_{n})+\varepsilon_{n},\qquad\varepsilon_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle iid}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),\qquad n=1,...,N.

(11)

The goal of GPR is to infer the function
F
in a non-parametric and Bayesian manner, given the set of training data
\{(\mathbf{x}_{n},y_{n});n=1,...,N\}
.
To learn the function, one must first put a prior distribution over
F
. Generally, the prior is used to encode qualitative properties of the function such as continuity, differentiability or periodicity
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
. In GPR, the prior for
F
is taken to be a
Gaussian process
with mean function
m
and covariance function
k
, denoted as

F\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k).

The function
F({\mathbf{x}})
is a GP if for any finite set of inputs
\mathbf{x}_{n}
, the function’s values
F(\mathbf{x}_{n})
have a jointly Gaussian distribution specified by
m
and
k
, that is,

\begin{bmatrix}F(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
F(\mathbf{x}_{N})\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}m(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
m(\mathbf{x}_{N})\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1})&\cdots&k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{N})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
k(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{1})&\cdots&k(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{N})\end{bmatrix}\right).

(12)

It is conventional to introduce some notation here. We write

\mathbf{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}m(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
m(\mathbf{x}_{N})\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{K}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{1})&\cdots&k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x}_{N})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
k(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{1})&\cdots&k(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x}_{N})\end{bmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}k(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{x})\\
\vdots\\
k(\mathbf{x}_{N},\mathbf{x})\end{bmatrix},

to make the equations more concise.

To learn functions using GPR, we consider what happens when we augment (
12
) with a new data point
\mathbf{x}_{*}
, separate from the data used during training, and we attempt to predict
F(\mathbf{x}_{*})
, the value of the function at this location. From (
11
) and (
12
), we may write that

\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{y}\\
F(\mathbf{x}_{*})\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{m}\\
m(\mathbf{x}_{*})\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N}&\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*})\\
\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}&k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{*})\end{bmatrix}\right),

(13)

where
\mathbf{y}=[y_{1}\cdots y_{N}]^{\top}
and
\mathbf{I}_{N}
is the
N\times N
identity matrix. Since the values of
\mathbf{y}
were already observed, we can condition on this data to obtain a posterior probability distribution for
F(\mathbf{x}_{*})
, which is again Gaussian,

\displaystyle F(\mathbf{x}_{*})|\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mu_{*},\sigma^{2}_{*}\right),

(14)

\displaystyle\mu_{*}

\displaystyle=m(\mathbf{x}_{*})+\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{m}),

(15)

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}_{*}

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{*})-\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*}).

(16)

We omit the derivation of these expressions, as they can be found in several standard references
(
Murphy 2022
, p. 84)
.

Viewing (
14
) as a function of
\mathbf{x}_{*}
, we gain the ability to reason about the function
F
in a Bayesian manner.
An important fact about these expressions is that the posterior expected value of the function,
\mathbb{E}(F(\mathbf{x}_{*})|\mathbf{y})
, can be expressed as a weighted sum of kernel functions,

\mathbb{E}(F(\mathbf{x}_{*})|\mathbf{y})=\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{m})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}k(\mathbf{x}_{*},\mathbf{x}_{n})\alpha_{n},

(17)

where

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{1}\\
\vdots\\
\alpha_{N}\end{bmatrix}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{m}).

(18)

The weighted sum in (
18
) is in particular useful to analyze the GPR model, allowing us to perform calculations that would otherwise be intractable.

3.1
Covariance Functions

In many applications, the default kernel used in GPR is the squared exponential (SE) kernel
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
, given by

k_{\text{SE}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{0}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\ell^{2}}\right),

(19)

where
\ell
and
\sigma^{2}_{0}
are hyperparameters. A minor modification that improves the fitting power of the SE kernel is to let each input dimension
x_{i}
have its own length-scale parameter
\ell_{i}
, in which case we have the automatic relevance detection squared exponential (ARD-SE) kernel
Neal 1996
:

k_{\text{ARD-SE}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{0}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\ell_{i}^{2}}\right).

(20)

3.2
Derivatives and Integrals of GPs

For computing the IG attributions, we will need to integrate and differentiate GPs.
We already remarked that functions sampled from a GP will be differentiable when the GP mean and kernel functions are also sufficiently differentiable
Adler and Taylor 2007
. In this way, it also makes sense to ask what is the probability distribution of
\partial F/\partial x_{i}
when
F\sim\mathcal{GP}
. The answer is well known;
\partial F/\partial x_{i}
is also distributed according to a GP:

Lemma 1

(Derivatives of GPs)
.

If
F\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
, where
m\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D})
, then

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}\sim\mathcal{GP}\left(\frac{\partial m}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}\right).

This result is well-established in the literature
Solak et al. 2002
;
Le Gall 2016
, but for clarity we derive it in Appendix
A
.

In a similar spirit, the integral of a GP is also Gaussian, but we should be careful to clarify what type of integration we are considering. In one-dimension, the indefinite integral of a GP requires taking a limit that may not be well defined, depending on the mean and covariance functions of the GP. However, we can define the definite integral fairly easily, which turns out to be a non-stationary GP.

Lemma 2

(Integral of a GP)
.

Suppose
f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
, where
m\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R})
and
k\in C^{0}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R})
, and let
a\in\mathbb{R}
be fixed. The definite integral
F(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(t)dt
is a GP in
x
,

F(x)=\int_{a}^{x}f(t)dt\sim\mathcal{GP}(M,K),

where

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{x}m(t)dt,

\displaystyle K(x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{a}^{x}\int_{a}^{x^{\prime}}k(s,t)dsdt.

The integral of a GP could be established more generally, but the one-dimensional case is all we require in the current work. Non-stationarity arises from the fact that when
x=a
, the integral is exactly 0, but otherwise the integral can accumulate variance as the distance between
x
and
a
increases. As before, we prove this lemma in Appendix
A
.

3.3
Spectral Approximations to GPR

Owing to the poor scalability of the GPR model to large numbers of training samples, several approximations are often employed in practice to apply GPR models to large data sets
Hensman et al. 2013
. In this section, we consider the class of
spectral approximations
to the GPR model.

According to Bochner’s theorem, every stationary covariance function, expressed as
r(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, can be represented as the Fourier transform of a finite positive measure
Stein 1999
. When this positive measure has a spectral density
S
, the Wiener-Khintchin theorem
(
Rasmussen and Williams 2006
, Chapter 4)
states that
r
and
S
are Fourier duals. Since the measure represented by
S
is finite and positive, it can be normalized to yield a probability density function
p(\mathbf{v})
.
The random feature GP (RFGP) model proposed by
Lázaro-Gredilla et al. 2010
samples from this probability distribution to generate
random features
used to approximate the full GP kernel. Under this approach, we approximate

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})\approx\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

using
M
samples from the power spectral density of the covariance function, where

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{1})\\
\cos(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{1})\\
\vdots\\
\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{M})\\
\cos(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{M})\end{bmatrix},

and

\mathbf{v}_{m}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{iid}}}{{\sim}}p(\mathbf{v}),\qquad m=1,...,M.

The Wiener-Khintchin theorem is particularly useful because it allows us to explicitly compute the density
p
in a number of cases. For example, the Fourier dual of a Gaussian function
\exp(-\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{K}^{-1}\mathbf{x})
, where
\mathbf{K}
is a positive definite matrix, is again a Gaussian. Thus, we can produce random features from SE and ARD-SE kernels by sampling from Gaussian distributions.
Having this sparse approximation of the kernel function, we can derive the predictive model for RFGPs. From
Lázaro-Gredilla et al. 2010
, the predictive model is given by

\mathbb{E}(F(\mathbf{x}_{*}))=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\bf{\Phi}\mathbf{y},

(21)

and

\text{Cov}(F(\mathbf{x}_{*}),F(\mathbf{x}_{*}^{\prime}))=\sigma^{2}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{*})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{*}^{\prime}),

(22)

where we have defined the matrices

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}=\boldsymbol{\Phi}\boldsymbol{\Phi}^{\top}+\frac{M\sigma^{2}_{n}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\mathbf{I}_{2M},

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Phi}=\left[\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{1}),\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{2}),\cdots,\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}_{N})\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{2M\times N},

for convenience.
According to the Wiener-Khintchin theorem, frequency vectors for an ARD-SE kernel, (
20
), can be sampled as

\mathbf{v}_{m}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\mathbf{0},\text{diag}(\boldsymbol{\ell})^{-2}\right),\qquad m=1,...,M,

where
\boldsymbol{\ell}=[\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{D}]^{\top}
.
This approach leaves us with
\dim(\mathbf{x})+2
hyperparameters in this model,

\boldsymbol{\theta}=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma^{2}_{n}\\
\sigma^{2}_{0}\\
\boldsymbol{\ell}\end{bmatrix}.

We close this section by noting that other variants of the RFGP exist, taking different approaches to sample random features. In general, RFGPs are not usually optimal, because the particular sample of frequency vectors
\mathbf{v}_{m}
strongly determines the quality of the approximation. Additionally, other approaches may yield feature-based GP models with the same form as (
21
). In particular, Hilbert space approximations to GPR have a similar form to RFGPs, but these models see improvements in performance because their features are optimally selected as opposed to being randomly sampled
Riutort-Mayol et al. 2023
.

4
Attributions in GPR Models

In this section, we introduce our main theorem: GP models produce GP attributions. We then apply this theorem to several common GPR models to derive explicit expressions for their attributions.

Theorem 1

(Integrated gradients preserve Gaussianity)
.

If
F\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
, where we assume
m\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D}\times\mathbb{R}^{D})
, then the IG attributions follow a GP:

\displaystyle attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\sim\mathcal{GP}(\mu_{i},\kappa_{i}),\qquad i=1,...,D,

(23)

where

\displaystyle\mu_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial m(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}}dt,

(24)

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+s(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}dtds.

(25)

Furthermore, the attributions satisfy a GP version of the completeness axiom:

\sum_{i=1}^{D}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=F(\mathbf{x})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\sim\mathcal{GP}(\tilde{m},\tilde{k}),

(26)

where

\displaystyle\tilde{m}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=m(\mathbf{x})-m(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),

(27)

\displaystyle\tilde{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-k(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}).

(28)

The basic motivation for this theorem is that since
attr_{i}
is a linear operator, it preserves Gaussianity
Solak et al. 2002
. The proof is given in Appendix
A.4
. Here we decompose the attribution operator into a sequence of operations through which we can compute the form of the GP.
Other attribution theories, formulated in terms of linear operators as mentioned in
2.3
, would admit proofs in a similar manner. It should be noted that the attribution functions are always
heteroscedastic
, meaning that their variance is location-dependent. This can be seen by observing that
\kappa_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})
generally grows as
x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i}
increases, but also that
\text{Var}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=\kappa_{i}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x})=0
whenever
x_{i}=\tilde{x}_{i}
.

Knowing the expressions for the mean and covariance of the GPR posterior predictive distribution, we may derive expressions for the attributions of a GPR model. For a bit of notation, recall from
(
2
)
that the attribution vector
\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x}))
is a vector-valued function whose entries are the attribution operator applied to each entry of
\mathbf{k}(\mathbf{x})
, that is,

\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})=\begin{bmatrix}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{1}))\\
\vdots\\
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{N}))\end{bmatrix}.

Using this notation, we present the attributions of the GPR predictive posterior model in (
14
) via the following corollary.

Corollary 1

(Attributions of the GPR Posterior-Predictive)
.

If
F
is distributed according to the GP posterior-predictive distribution (
14
), (
15
), and (
16
), then the IG attributions
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x})
are GPs such that

\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|m)+\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I}_{N})^{-1}(\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{m}),

(29)

and

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}),attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})

\displaystyle(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+s(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}dsdt

(30)

\displaystyle-\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})^{\top}(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\mathbf{k}),

The tractability of the expressions in the corollary depends precisely on the choice of mean and covariance functions used in the GP prior. In the following subsections, we compute these results for some noteworthy GPR models.

4.1
SE and ARD-SE Kernels

The attributions for the GPR model can be derived in closed form when we assume an ARD-SE kernel (
20
), although the derivation can be somewhat tedious. Assuming a zero mean prior over
F
with ARD-SE kernel, the attributions of the GPR model are given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}A_{n,i}(\mathbf{x}),

(31)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=B_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{m=1}^{N}\Lambda_{n,m}A_{n,i}(\mathbf{x})A_{m,i}(\mathbf{x}),

(32)

where we have defined

\displaystyle A_{n,i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{\frac{-a-b-c}{2}}\left[\sqrt{2\pi}e^{\frac{(2a+b)^{2}}{8a}}\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{b}{2\sqrt{2a}}\right)-\text{erf}\left(\frac{2a+b}{2\sqrt{2a}}\right)\right)(bd-2af)+4\sqrt{a}d\left(e^{\frac{a+b}{2}}-1\right)\right]}{4a^{3/2}},

\displaystyle B_{i}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)(aw+v)}{a^{3/2}}-\frac{2e^{-\frac{a}{2}}\left(e^{a/2}-1\right)(aw+2v)}{a^{2}}\right),

where
\text{erf}(\cdot)
is the error function and

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{L}=\text{diag}(\ell_{1},\cdots,\ell_{D}),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=2(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{n}),\quad

\displaystyle v=-\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{4}},

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{n}),

\displaystyle w=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\ell_{i}^{2}},

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{2}},

\displaystyle[\Lambda_{n,m}]=(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}_{n}\mathbf{I})^{-1},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(\tilde{x}_{i}-x_{n,i})}{\ell_{i}^{2}},

\displaystyle[\alpha_{n}]=(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}_{n}\mathbf{I})^{-1}\mathbf{y},

are constants which implicitly depend upon
\mathbf{x}
,
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
,
\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
i
.
The derivation of these expressions is provided in Appendix
B
.

4.2
Random Feature GPs

Based on (
21
), it is easy to see that IG attributions for the RFGP model can also be derived. The mean attribution and variance can be given as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{y},

(33)

\displaystyle\text{Var}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}\sigma^{2}_{n}\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x}),

where for clarity, we introduced the notation

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}\frac{v_{1,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\\
\frac{v_{1,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\\
\vdots\\
\frac{v_{M,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\cos(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\cos(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\\
\frac{v_{M,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\sin(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\sin(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M\times 1},

and

\mathbf{A}=\boldsymbol{\Phi\Phi}^{\top}+\frac{M\sigma^{2}_{n}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\mathbf{I}_{2M}.

The derivation of these expressions is provided in Appendix
B
.

4.3
Comments on Other GP Models

While we only provided closed-form results for the SE and ARD-SE kernels, Theorem
1
applies generally to all GPR models. We now briefly discuss how these results extend to other GP models.

4.3.1
Additive GPs

In GP kernel design, it is common to add and multiply kernels to produce new composite kernels
(
Gibbs 1998
, p.19)
.
If
\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
and
\mathcal{GP}(m^{\prime},k^{\prime})
are two GPs, then the direct sum GP is defined to be
\mathcal{GP}(m+m^{\prime},k+k^{\prime})
. Using Corollary
1
, analytical expressions for the direct sum GP can be obtained whenever the component kernels
k
and
k^{\prime}
are tractable.

Duvenaud et al. 2011
defines the
full additive kernel
by summing over all possible combinations of kernels that one may obtain through including or exclusion of features. Given a choice of base kernel
k_{i}
for each input feature
x_{i}
, the full additive kernel is defined to be

k_{\textrm{add}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sum_{d=1}^{D}\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq D}\sigma^{2}_{d}\prod_{i=i_{1},...,i_{d}}k_{i}(x_{i},x_{i}^{\prime}),

where
\sigma^{2}_{d},d=1,...,D,
are hyperparameters. Because of the linearity property of the attribution operator, attributions can be written in closed form for the full additive kernel whenever the integrals of the base kernel are tractable.

4.3.2
Nonstationary GPs

Nonstationary GPs
are GPs whose covariance functions are not translation-invariant, and thus may exhibit different amplitudes, length scales and other properties depending on one’s location in the input space. Several approaches to using nonstationary GPs for inference exist: scaling the kernel by parametric functions to introduce heteroscedasticity
(
Gibbs 1998
, pp.16-18)
, deep kernel learning
Wilson et al. 2016
and deep GPs
Damianou and Lawrence 2013
. Theorem
1
applies to directly nonstationary GPs with no modification.

Taipei Housing Data: Demonstrating Affine Scale Invariance

In this example, we consider real estate data in New Taipei City obtained from the UCI Machine Learning Repository
mis 2018
in order to demonstrate a property of IG attributions called affine scale invariance (ASI). In this data set, there are 6 features used to predict the value of a house. After training a GPR model with ARD-SE kernel to predict the house value, we wish to attribute these predictions to each of the input features to understand how this information contributed to the predicted value. We take the baseline
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
to be the average across all houses, and consider a prediction for a new house excluded from the training set.

As in many ML applications, we normalized the input data before training the GPR model. However, we would like to know if this procedure has distorted our predicted attributions. Fortunately, the ASI property says that the IG attributions are invariant to scaling and translations in the input feature space
Lundstrom et al. 2022
. To formalize this statement, let us consider a mapping
T(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{\lambda}\odot\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{v}
, where
\odot
represents a Hadamard (entrywise) product of vectors and all
\lambda_{i}\neq 0
. The mapping
T
scales each coordinate
x_{i}
by
\lambda_{i}
and then translates by
v_{i}
. ASI asserts that

attr_{i}(T(\mathbf{x}),T(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})|F\circ T^{-1})=attr_{i}(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}|F),\qquad i=1,...,D,

for any such transformation
T
.

An important note about this result is that while the attributions do not theoretically depend upon normalization, the training of a ML model still may benefit from normalization, and this is especially true when using GPR. Thus, the ASI property suggests that normalization doesn’t distort attributions by itself, but rather any change in the resulting attributions will be due to the change in how the ML model tunes it’s parameters.

In Figure
3
, we consider the attributions for a particular house both with and without normalization. We observe that although the input feature vectors appear noticeably different, the resulting attributions are identical. The normalized model also retains a degree of interpretability; we see that the given house has an above average distance to the MRT (subway station), and as a result this contributed negatively to the house value.

(36)

6
Conclusion

In this work, we develop a bridge between the theory of feature attribution and GPR. By representing attributions as distributions, we can quantify a model’s confidence in its feature attributions. When the selected GPR model admits analytically tractable attributions, using closed-form expressions for the attributions is more computationally efficient, and it mitigates the need for computationally expensive or otherwise inaccurate approximations. While non-parametric methods like GPR, at face value, offer a non-interpretable approach to ML, our study of feature attribution may provide more trust in GPR models. Furthermore, these results will extend the theoretical and practical scope of GPR models to more XAI domains.

Proofs

In this appendix, we provide mathematical proofs for the lemmas and main theorem of the paper. We divide Appendix A. into four sections: In Section
A.1
, we introduce some additional technical lemmas.
In Section
A.2
, we derive the expression for the derivatives of a GP, and in Section
A.3
, we do the same for definite integrals of a GP.
Finally, in Section
A.4
we prove that the IG attributions of a GPR model are GPs.

We use the notation
C^{\ell}(\mathcal{X})
to denote the set of all
\ell
-times continuously differentiable functions
\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}
, where
\ell=1,2,...
or
\ell=\infty
. We may write
C^{\ell}
when the functions’ domain is clear from context.

A.1
Operations that Preserve Gaussianity

We say that a random process
(X(t))_{t\in T}
, with index set
T
, is a GP if for every finite set of indices
t_{1},...,t_{D}
, the vector
(X(t_{1}),...,X(t_{D}))
has a multivariate (possibly degenerate) Gaussian distribution. We use the notation
X(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
, where
m(t)=\mathbb{E}(X(t))
and
k(t,t^{\prime})=\text{Cov}(X(t),X(t^{\prime}))
.
From this definition, we can immediately show that scaling and precompositions preserve Gaussianity:

Lemma 3

(Scaling preserves Gaussianity)
.

Let
X(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
be a GP with index set
T
and let
g:T\to\mathbb{R}
be a function.
Then
g(t)X(t)
is a GP, and for any finite set of indices
t_{1},...,t_{D}
, we have that

\begin{bmatrix}g(t_{1})X(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
g(t_{D})X(t_{D})\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}g(t_{1})m(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
g(t_{D})m(t_{D})\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}g(t_{1})^{2}k(t_{1},t_{1})&\cdots&g(t_{1})g(t_{D})k(t_{1},t_{D})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
g(t_{D})g(t_{1})k(t_{D},t_{1})&\cdots&g(t_{D})^{2}k(t_{D},t_{D})\end{bmatrix}\right).

(37)

Proof.

For any set of indices
t_{1},...,t_{D}
, the pointwise scaling by
g(t)
can be represented in matrix form,

\begin{bmatrix}g(t_{1})X(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
g(t_{D})X(t_{D})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}g(t_{1})&&\\
&\ddots&\\
&&g(t_{D})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}X(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
X(t_{D})\end{bmatrix}.

Multiplying a Gaussian vector by a matrix preserves Gaussianity, so the GP property is upheld for the scaled process.
∎

Lemma 4

(Precompositions preserve Gaussianity)
.

Let
X(t)\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
be a GP with index set
T
, and let
\gamma:S\to T
be a function between sets. Then
X(\gamma(s))
is a GP indexed by
S
, and for any finite set of indices
s_{1},...,s_{D}
,

\begin{bmatrix}X(\gamma(s_{1}))\\
\vdots\\
X(\gamma(s_{D}))\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}m(\gamma(s_{1}))\\
\vdots\\
m(\gamma(s_{D}))\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}k(\gamma(s_{1}),\gamma(s_{1}))&\cdots&k(\gamma(s_{1}),\gamma(s_{D}))\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
k(\gamma(s_{D}),\gamma(s_{1}))&\cdots&k(\gamma(s_{D}),\gamma(s_{D}))\end{bmatrix}\right).

(38)

Proof.

Let
t_{i}=\gamma(s_{i})
for
i=1,...,D
. Then
t_{1},...,t_{D}
is a set of indices in
T
and hence

\begin{bmatrix}X(\gamma(s_{1}))\\
\vdots\\
X(\gamma(s_{D}))\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}X(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
X(t_{D})\end{bmatrix}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\begin{bmatrix}m(t_{1})\\
\vdots\\
m(t_{D})\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}k(t_{1},t_{1})&\cdots&k(t_{1},t_{D})\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\
k(t_{D},t_{1})&\cdots&k(t_{D},t_{D})\end{bmatrix}\right).

The only thing to notice here is that if
\gamma
is not injective (one-to-one), then the new GP is degenerate, regardless if the original GP was non-degenerate.
∎

A.2
Derivatives of GPs

As noted in the main text, differentiation and integration also preserve Gaussianity. To show this, we use the following proposition from
(
Le Gall 2016
, p.3)
:

Proposition 1
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of real random variables such that, for every
n\geq 1
,
X_{n}
follows a
\mathcal{N}(m_{n},\sigma^{2}_{n})
distribution. Suppose that
X_{n}
converges in
L^{2}
to
X
. Then:

1.

The random variable
X
follows the
\mathcal{N}(m,\sigma^{2})
distribution, where
m=\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{n}
and
\sigma^{2}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sigma^{2}_{n}
.

2.

This convergence also holds in all
L^{p}
spaces,
1\leq p<\infty
.

We note that the
L^{2}
convergence is automatically satisfied whenever the sequences
m_{n}
and
\sigma^{2}_{n}
converge to something reasonable (in essence, when
0<\sigma^{2}<\infty
).
Using this proposition, we can now prove results for derivatives and integrals of GPs.

Proof.

of Lemma
1

To start, we recall the typical definition of a partial derivative:

\frac{\partial F}{\partial x_{i}}=\lim_{h\to 0}\frac{F(\mathbf{x}+h\mathbf{e}_{i})-F(\mathbf{x})}{h},

where
\mathbf{e}_{i}
is the unit vector in the
i
-th coordinate direction. To take the limit, define a sequence
h_{n}=1/n
, for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
, and consider the sequence of functions
F_{n}(\mathbf{x})=(F(\mathbf{x}+h_{n})-F(\mathbf{x}))/h_{n}
. We see that each
F_{n}
is a GP, and thus we have a sequence of GPs,
F_{n}\sim\mathcal{GP}(m_{i,n},k_{i,n})
. By Proposition
1
, a sequence of Gaussian variables converges to a Gaussian if the mean and variance converge. In this case,

\lim_{n\to\infty}m_{i,n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i})-F(\mathbf{x})}{1/n}\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{m(\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i})-m(\mathbf{x})}{1/n}=\frac{\partial m}{\partial x_{i}},

which exists since
m\in C^{1}
. For the covariance, we have that

k_{i,n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\text{Cov}\left(\frac{F(\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i})-F(\mathbf{x})}{1/n},\frac{F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i})-F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{1/n}\right),

which can then be expressed as

k_{i,n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\frac{k(\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i})-k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i})-k(\mathbf{x}+\frac{1}{n}\mathbf{e}_{i},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{n^{2}}.

Taking the limit then yields

\lim_{n\to\infty}k_{i,n}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\frac{\partial^{2}k}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}},

which exists because
k\in C^{2}
. Thus, by Proposition
1
we have that
\partial F/\partial x_{i}
is Gaussian whose mean and covariance are specified by the functions
\partial m/\partial x_{i}
and
\partial^{2}k/\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}
.
To conclude the proof, notice that for any finite set of points
\mathbf{x}_{1},...,\mathbf{x}_{L}
, the vector
[F(\mathbf{x}_{1})\cdots F(\mathbf{x}_{L})]
will again be multivariate Gaussian with mean and covariance controlled by these functions. Since this property holds arbitrarily, the Kolmogorov extension theorem asserts that a GP exists with the desired mean and covariance functions.
∎

We might say that a GP is
\ell
-times continuously differentiable if the mean and covariance functions are
C^{\ell}
and
C^{2\ell}
, respectively, since this ensures that functions sampled from the GP are
C^{\ell}
almost-surely.
Using this notion of differentiability, we can discuss how scaling and precomposition affect differentiability. The equations (
37
) and (
38
) show that the output mean and covariance functions are
C^{\ell}
whenever
g
,
\gamma
and the original
m
and
k
functions are
C^{\ell}
. Hence, scaling and precompositions by smooth functions preserve both smoothness and Gaussianity.
Lemma 1 shows that differentiating a
C^{\ell}
GP yields a
C^{\ell-1}
GP.

A.3
Integrals of GPs

The integration of a GP is established using similar techniques to differentiation. For computing attributions, we only need to know how to compute a one-dimensional integral, over a line segment.

Proof.

of Lemma
2

Since the integral is being taken over a line segment, it suffices to consider the Riemannian definition of the integral as the limit over increasingly fine partitions of the line. Hence we define

F_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{1}{n}f\left(a+\frac{i(x-a)}{n}\right),

and
F(x)
to be the limit of
F_{n}(x)
as
n\to\infty
. Similar in the proof for derivatives of a GP, we need to compute the mean and covariance functions
F_{n}(x)
, show that they converge, and apply Proposition
1
. For the mean, the summation becomes

\mathbb{E}(F_{n}(x))=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{E}(f(a+i(x-a)/n))}{n}=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{m(a+i(x-a)/n)}{n},

and since
m
is continuous on the interval, the Riemann sum converges to the integral. Thus,

\mathbb{E}(F_{n}(x))\to\int_{a}^{x}m(t)dt=M(x),

as
n\to\infty
, and we denote the result as a function
M
of
x
.
For the covariance, we have that

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(F_{n}(x),F_{n}(x^{\prime})\right)

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left(\sum_{i=0}^{n}\frac{f(a+i(x-a)/n)}{n},\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{f(a+j(x^{\prime}-a)/n)}{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{n^{2}}\text{Cov}\left(f\left(a+\frac{i(x-a)}{n}\right),f\left(a+\frac{j(x^{\prime}-a)}{n}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{n}\frac{1}{n^{2}}k\left(a+\frac{i(x-a)}{n},a+\frac{j(x^{\prime}-a)}{n}\right),

which converges, again because
k
is continuous, to the double integral, which we define to be
K
, i.e.,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{Cov}\left(F_{n}(x),F_{n}(x^{\prime})\right)=\int_{a}^{x}\int_{a}^{x^{\prime}}k(s,t)dsdt=K(x,x^{\prime}),

as
n\to\infty
. Since the mean and covariance functions converge, we can conclude the convergence of
F_{n}(x)\to F(x)
for a given point
x
.
As in the proof of Lemma 1, for any finite set of points
x_{1},...,x_{L}
, we want to show that the joint distribution of
F(x_{1}),...,F(x_{L})
coincides with the Gaussian distribution specified by the functions
M
and
K
. Notice that for fixed
n
,

\mathbf{F}_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{L})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}\begin{bmatrix}f\left(a+\frac{i(x_{1}-a)}{n}\right)\\
\vdots\\
f\left(a+\frac{i(x_{L}-a)}{n}\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}f\left(a+\frac{i(x_{1}-a)}{n}\right)\\
\vdots\\
\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{1}{n}f\left(a+\frac{i(x_{L}-a)}{n}\right)\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}F_{n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
F_{n}(x_{L})\end{bmatrix},

and thus

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{F}_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{L})=\lim_{n\to\infty}\begin{bmatrix}F_{n}(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
F_{n}(x_{L})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}F(x_{1})\\
\vdots\\
F(x_{L})\end{bmatrix}.

From the above calculations, we then know that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbf{F}_{n}(x_{1},...,x_{L})
obeys a multivariate Gaussian law with mean and covariance determined by
M
and
K
, respectively. By the Kolmogorov extension theorem, there exists a GP determined by these functions.∎

A.4
Attributions of a GP

Given the previous lemmas, we are now equipped to compute the IG attributions of a GPR model and prove the theorem in Section
4
. Let
F(\mathbf{x})
be a GP over
\mathbb{R}^{D}
. We fix a baseline point
\tilde{\mathbf{x}}
and recall the definition of the
attr_{i}
operator:

attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}}dt.

The attribution is defined by four sequential operations,

1.

Taking the partial derivative
\partial/\partial x_{i}
of
F
,

2.

Precomposition of
\partial F/\partial x_{i}
with the function
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
,

3.

Integration from
t=0
to
t=1
, and

4.

Scaling the result by
(x_{i}-\tilde{x})
.

Together, we observe that these operations all preserve Gaussianity, and thus to find the attribution GP it suffices to compute its mean and covariance, whenever they exist. As a simple condition to ensure existence, we require that
m\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{D})
and
k\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{D},\mathbb{R}^{D})
, which guarantees that we can differentiate the GP and later integrate it.

Proof.

of Theorem
1

The existence of the attribution GP follows directly from the lemmas.
We show the computation for the reader’s convenience, implicitly invoking Lemmas 1, 2, 3, and 4:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(F(\mathbf{x}))

\displaystyle=m(\mathbf{x}),

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\partial F(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial m(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial m(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}},

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}}dt\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial m(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}}dt,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left((x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}}dt\right)

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial m(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}}dt.

The resulting mean attribution function is defined since
m\in C^{1}
implies that
\partial m/\partial x_{i}
is
C^{0}
. Since the derivative is continuous, it is also integrable over the interval
[0,1]
.
The calculation for the covariance function is more tedious, but follows the same logic. Let us define
\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
for conciseness. We find that

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(F(\mathbf{x}),F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}),

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\frac{\partial F(\mathbf{x})}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s))}{\partial x_{i}},\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial^{2}k(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\text{Cov}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s))}{\partial x_{i}}ds,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}}dt\right)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}k(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}dsdt,

and finally,

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}),attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}\left((x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s))}{\partial x_{i}}ds,(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}}dt\right)

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\text{Cov}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s))}{\partial x_{i}}ds,\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial F(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}}dt\right)

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}k(\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x},s),\boldsymbol{\beta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},t))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}dsdt.

Again, to see that this expression is defined, we recall that
k\in C^{2}
, and therefore
\partial^{2}k/\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}\in C^{0}
, and thus is integrable over the unit square.

To show completeness of the output GP, we need to know that almost-surely every sample from a differentiable GP is differentiable. Let
f
be any differentiable sample from the GP. The attributions
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|f)
satisfy completeness by definition, and thus
\sum_{i}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|f)=f(\mathbf{x})-f(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
. Since this holds for almost-every
f
, the distributions of the left and right hand sides coincide. We can see from either side that the distribution is Gaussian, and we can compute that

\mathbb{E}(F(\mathbf{x})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))=m(\mathbf{x})-m(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),

and

\displaystyle\text{Cov}(F(\mathbf{x})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})-F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle=\text{Cov}(F(\mathbf{x}),F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))+\text{Cov}(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))

\displaystyle\quad-\text{Cov}(F(\mathbf{x}),F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))-\text{Cov}(F(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),F(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))

\displaystyle=k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})+k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle\quad-k(\mathbf{x},\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-k(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\tilde{\mathbf{x}}).

∎

If
F\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)
is the GPR posterior predictive distribution, then
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\sim\mathcal{GP}
whenever
m\in C^{1}
and
k\in C^{2}
. If we satisfy the more refined assumption that
m\in C^{\infty}
and
k\in C^{\infty}
, then we have that
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|F)\in C^{\infty}
as well.

Appendix B
Derivations for Specific Models

In this section, we derive the expressions for the mean and variance of the attributions for specific models.

ARD-SE Kernel

To derive the GPR attributions when using an ARD-SE kernel, it is instructive to work with Corollary 1. From here, we observe that one must derive expressions for
\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})
and the double integral in (
30
). In the main text, we defined

A_{n,i}(\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|k(\cdot,\mathbf{x}_{n})),

which are the entries of the vector
\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})=[A_{n,i}]_{n=1:N}
. To compute
A_{n,i}
, we must apply the
attr_{i}
operator to the kernel function
k
, which we know to be the ARD-SE function,

k_{\text{ARD-SE}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{f}\exp\left(-\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{2\ell_{i}^{2}}\right).

If we let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=\mathbf{x}_{n}
and
\mathbf{L}=\text{diag}([\ell_{1}\cdots\ell_{D}])
, then we can write out the kernel function in matrix form:

k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{n})=\sigma^{2}_{f}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{n})\right).

The partial derivative of this is

\frac{\partial k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}_{n})}{\partial x_{i}}=\sigma^{2}_{f}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}_{n})\right)\left(-\frac{x_{i}-x_{n,i}}{\ell_{i}^{2}}\right).

The expression begins to look hairy when we precompose with
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial k(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t),\mathbf{x}_{n})}{\partial x_{i}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{f}\exp{\left(-\frac{1}{2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})-\mathbf{x}_{n})\right)}

\displaystyle\times\left(-\frac{\tilde{x}_{i}+t(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})-x_{n,i}}{\ell_{i}^{2}}\right).

At this point, the most challenging task is to compute the integral from
t=0
to
t=1
. The trick is to notice that the expression above is of the form

\exp(-at^{2}-bt-c)(d\times t+f)

as a function of
t
, where
a,b,c,d
and
f
are constants that we later plug in. From here, one can produce closed-form expressions for the integral. It can be checked that

\int t\exp(-at^{2}-bt-c)dt=-\frac{e^{-at^{2}-bt-c}}{2a}-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{(b^{2}/4a)-c}\text{erf}\left(\frac{2at+b}{2\sqrt{a}}\right)}{4a^{3/2}},

and

\int\exp(-at^{2}-bt-c)dt=-\frac{\sqrt{\pi}e^{(b^{2}/4a)-c}\text{erf}\left(\frac{2at+b}{2\sqrt{a}}\right)}{2\sqrt{a}},

where
erf
is the error function,

\text{erf}(t)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{0}^{z}e^{-t^{2}}dt.

From the indefinite integral, we can compute the definite integral by evaluating the expressions at
t=0
and
t=1
. Based on the expression above, the appropriate definitions for the constants
a,b,c,d
and
f
are

\displaystyle a

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),

\displaystyle b

\displaystyle=2(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{n}),

\displaystyle c

\displaystyle=(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{n})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}-\mathbf{x}_{n}),

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{2}},

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(\tilde{x}_{i}-x_{n,i})}{\ell_{i}^{2}}.

After some algebra we deduce that

A_{n,i}(\mathbf{x})=\frac{e^{\frac{-a-b-c}{2}}\left[\sqrt{2\pi}e^{\frac{(2a+b)^{2}}{8a}}\left(\text{erf}\left(\frac{b}{2\sqrt{2a}}\right)-\text{erf}\left(\frac{2a+b}{2\sqrt{2a}}\right)\right)(bd-2af)+4\sqrt{a}d\left(e^{\frac{a+b}{2}}-1\right)\right]}{4a^{3/2}},

and thus

\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\alpha_{n}A_{n,i}(\mathbf{x}).

Computation of the variance is also tractable, but requires some more work. Let
\mathbf{\Lambda}=(\mathbf{K}+\sigma^{2}_{n}\mathbf{I})^{-1}
. Then we observe that

\text{Var}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=B_{i}(\mathbf{x})-\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})^{\top}\mathbf{\Lambda}\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k}),

where we have defined

B_{i}(\mathbf{x})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}k(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s),\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}dsdt.

We already know
\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{k})
from computing the mean attribution, and all that remains is to compute
B_{i}(\mathbf{x})
. Like before, our goal is to convert
B_{i}(\mathbf{x})
into a generic expression and then use a known result to solve the integral. Firstly, we compute the second derivative, which we may express as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=1}^{D}\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{2}}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{i}^{2}}-\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime})}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{i}^{2}}-\frac{(x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{4}}\right).

The next step is to precompose and integrate. When
\mathbf{x}\neq\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, the general integral is complicated. The formulas simplify considerably if we constrain our attention to the case in which
\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
, meaning that we only compute the variance of an attribution, and not the full covariance function. In this case, we let
\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)=\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
, and then precomposition yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}k(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s),\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\boldsymbol{\gamma}(s)-\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t))}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{i}^{2}}-\frac{(\gamma_{i}(s)-\gamma_{i}(t))^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}_{0}\exp\left(-\frac{(s-t)^{2}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\ell_{i}^{2}}-\frac{(s-t)^{2}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{4}}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-\frac{a(s-t)^{2}}{2}\right)\left(w+v(s-t)^{2}\right),

where
a=(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{L}^{-2}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})
is the same as in the calculation for the mean, and where we additionally defined

\displaystyle v

\displaystyle=-\sigma^{2}_{0}\frac{(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}}{\ell_{i}^{4}},

\displaystyle w

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\ell_{i}^{2}}.

In this form, the integral can be evaluated, yielding

\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}e^{-a(s-t)^{2}/2}(w+v(s-t)^{2})dsdt=\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)(aw+v)}{a^{3/2}}+\frac{2e^{-\frac{a}{2}}\left(e^{a/2}-1\right)(aw+2v)}{a^{2}}.

We may then write

\displaystyle B_{i}(\mathbf{x})=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})^{2}\left(\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\text{erf}\left(\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)(aw+v)}{a^{3/2}}-\frac{2e^{-\frac{a}{2}}\left(e^{a/2}-1\right)(aw+2v)}{a^{2}}\right).

Random Feature GPs

To derive the RFGP attributions, we can use Theorem
1
directly with
(
21
) and (
22
).
From (
21
), we see that the RFGP mean can be expressed as a weighted sum. If we define the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\Delta}}{{=}}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{y}
, then we may write

\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{y}=\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\sum_{m=1}^{2M}\phi_{m}(\mathbf{x})\alpha_{m},

where
\alpha_{m}
are constants and

\phi_{m}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{cases}\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{(m+1)/2}),&\text{if $m$ odd}\\
\cos(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}_{m/2}),&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

In either case,
\phi_{m}(\mathbf{x})
is of the form
\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v})
or
\cos(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v})
, for some value of
\mathbf{v}
. By linearity, it suffices to compute the attribution for this generic form, and then plug in to find the resulting expression.

Since
\cos(x)
can be represented as
\sin(x+b)
where
b=\pi/2
, we show the generic calculation for
\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+b)
that will produce the desired result for both the sine and cosine cases.
Let
\phi(\mathbf{x})=\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+b)
. Then

\displaystyle attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\phi)

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial\phi(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}}dt

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\cos((\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))^{\top}\mathbf{v}+b)v_{i}dt

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\left[\sin((\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))^{\top}\mathbf{v}+b)\right|_{t=0}^{1}\frac{v_{i}}{(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{v}}

\displaystyle=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\left(\sin(\mathbf{x}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+b)-\sin(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}^{\top}\mathbf{v}+b)\right)\frac{v_{i}}{(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})^{\top}\mathbf{v}}.

From this calculation, we can derive the expression for the vector of
\phi
attributions:

\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\phi})=\begin{bmatrix}attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\phi_{1})\\
\vdots\\
attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\phi_{2M})\end{bmatrix}=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\begin{bmatrix}\frac{v_{1,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\\
\frac{v_{1,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\\
\vdots\\
\frac{v_{M,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\cos(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\cos(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\\
\frac{v_{M,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\sin(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\sin(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\end{bmatrix}

and thus

\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\phi})^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}.

In the main text, we use the notation

\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{v_{1,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\cos(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\\
\frac{v_{1,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}(\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\sin(\mathbf{v}_{1}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))\\
\vdots\\
\frac{v_{M,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\cos(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\cos(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\\
\frac{v_{M,i}}{\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}})}\left(\sin(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\mathbf{x})-\sin(\mathbf{v}_{M}^{\top}\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\end{bmatrix}

so that we may compactly write

\mathbb{E}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}))=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\top}\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Phi}\mathbf{y}.

This notation is also useful for deriving the covariance, since when we write

\text{Cov}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}),attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))=(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\partial^{2}k(\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+s(\mathbf{x}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}),\tilde{\mathbf{x}}+t(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\tilde{\mathbf{x}}))}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{i}^{\prime}}dtds,

for
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime})=\sigma^{2}_{n}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\phi}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime})
, we can split up the derivatives and integrals.
Without writing everything out, the desired result is

\text{Cov}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}),attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))=\sigma^{2}_{n}\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\phi})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\mathbf{attr}_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|\boldsymbol{\phi}),

which immediately yields

\text{Cov}(attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}),attr_{i}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}))=\sigma^{2}_{n}(x_{i}-\tilde{x}_{i})(x_{i}^{\prime}-\tilde{x}_{i})\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\zeta}(\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

C.1
Wine: Quadrature Rules

In this section, we provide some additional figures to analyze the convergence of the approximated integral.
First, we visualize the convergence of the approximated integral to the true IG attributions, for the Wine data set in Figure
6
, as only one feature was shown in Figure
4(a)
. Figure
6
shows that the rate of convergence is similar across all input features, and does not obviously correspond to the magnitude of the exact attribution.
The ARD-SE kernel also provides a measure of feature importance, where relevance of a (normalized) feature is defined to be
r_{i}=1/\ell_{i}
. In this case, we observe that for features with relevance, their attributions converge much more quickly. This is expected since a low feature relevance indicates that model training has preferentially underused a given feature in making predictions.

. The last two subfigures show the relative sizes of each attribution in the exact result, and also the relative importance of each input feature (as measured by the ARD kernel), respectively.

Second, we visualize the impact of different quadrature rules. The formula in (
36
) uses the right-hand rule to approximate the integral of a function on the interval
[0,1]
, but there exist other quadrature rules, or methods for numerical integration, that may achieve noticeable improvements in performance with only changes to the weights in the sum used to approximate the integral
Heath 2018
.
In Figure
7
, we compare the application of the right-hand rule with two other quadrature rules: the trapezoid rule and Simpson’s rule. Each method approximates the integral using a partition of the unit interval:

0=t_{0}<t_{1}<\cdots<t_{L-1}<t_{L}=1.

For each
l=1,...,L
, each quadrature approximates a portion of the integral along that segment. For a general function
g(t)
, the three quadratures use the partition to approximate the integral differently:

\int_{0}^{1}g(t)dt\approx\sum_{l=1}^{L}S_{l}

Right-hand:

\displaystyle S_{l}=g(t_{l}),

Trapezoid:

\displaystyle S_{l}=\frac{1}{2}\left(g(t_{l-1})+g(t_{l})\right),

Simpson’s rule:

\displaystyle S_{l}=\frac{1}{4}\left(g(t_{l-1})+2g\left(\frac{t_{l-1}+t_{l}}{2}\right)+g(t_{l})\right).

We note that Simpson’s rule includes an extra evaluation per partition. As a result, Simpson’s rule should be compared to other methods relative to the number of function calls, instead of
L
.
In Figure
7
, we compare the convergence of each quadrature rule against the number of partitions
L
as well as the number of required function evaluations. We additionally visualize the mean-squared error (MSE) of the means of each attribution GP. Both the trapezoid rule and Simpson’s rule demonstrate a marked improvement over the right-hand rule.
