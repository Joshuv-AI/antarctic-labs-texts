Smooth nonradial stationary Euler flows on the plane with compact support
Alberto Enciso, Antonio J. Fernández, David Ruiz

Abstract
. We prove the existence of nonradial classical solutions to the 2D incompressible Euler equations with compact support. More precisely, for any positive integer k k , we construct compactly supported stationary Euler flows of class C k ​ ( ℝ 2 ) C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2}) which are not locally radial. The proof uses a degree-theory-based bifurcation argument which hinges on three key ingredients: a novel approach to stationary Euler flows through elliptic equations with non-autonomous nonlinearities; a set of sharp regularity estimates for the linearized operator, which involves a potential that blows up as the inverse square of the distance to the boundary of the support; and overcoming a serious problem of loss of derivatives by the introduction of anisotropic weighted functional spaces between which the linearized operator is Fredholm.

1.
Introduction

Let us consider stationary solutions to the incompressible Euler equations on the plane

(1.1)

v\cdot\nabla v+\nabla p=0\quad\textup{ and }\quad\Div v=0\quad\textup{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

which describe the steady flows of an ideal fluid. Since the velocity field
v
is solenoidal, one can write it as the perpendicular gradient of the stream function
\psi
, i.e.,
v=\nabla^{\perp}\psi:=(\partial_{x_{2}}\psi,-\partial_{x_{1}}\psi)
. In terms of the stream function, the stationary Euler equations can be equivalently written as

(1.2)

\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot\nabla\Delta\psi=0\quad\textup{ in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\,.

In this paper we are concerned with compactly supported stationary solutions to the Euler equations. In the three-dimensional case, the existence of such solutions was a long-standing open problem, and stationary Euler flows with compact support were obtained only recently
[
10
,
6
]
. In contrast, in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, the construction of compactly supported solutions to (
1.1
) is elementary: it suffices to pick any radially symmetric, compactly supported stream function. More generally, one can take
\psi
as a linear combination of radially symmetric functions with disjoint compact supports, possibly centered at distinct points. The corresponding stationary flows are then
locally radial
, and their support is a union of disjoint balls and annuli.

Although in the last few years there has been an emergence of rigidity results for steady two-dimensional fluids, the existence of nonradial classical solutions to the stationary Euler equations with compact support remains a well-known open problem. There do exist smooth nonradial stationary solutions with finite energy, which are not compactly supported, as a byproduct of the results in
[
27
]
. Moreover, in the context of wild solutions, which are only
L^{\infty}
, a wealth of compactly supported solutions can be constructed using convex integration
[
3
]
.

From the point of view of the regularity of nonradial compactly supported solutions, the best results to date only give Lipschitz velocities. Specifically, by means of a hard proof involving several clever observations and a Nash–Moser iteration scheme, Gómez-Serrano, Park and Shi
[
12
]
constructed nonradial solutions of vortex patch type with compactly supported velocity. More precisely, the vorticity
-\Delta\psi
is a linear combination of three indicator functions, so
v
is piecewise smooth but not
C^{1}
. Another family of nonradial compactly supported solutions of the same regularity, which are not of vortex patch type, was subsequently obtained by the authors and Sicbaldi in
[
7
]
as a byproduct of a result (somewhat related to the so-called Schiffer conjecture
[
31
, Problem 80]
) on nontrivial Neumann eigenfunctions that are locally constant on the boundary. Here, the stream function
\psi
satisfies an equation of the form

(1.3)

\Delta\psi+f(\psi)=0\,,

in the support of the velocity, and in fact the function
f
is linear.

Our objective in this paper is to show that there are smooth nonradial stationary flows with compact support.

Theorem 1.1
.

For any positive integer
k
, there exist compactly supported stationary Euler flows of class
C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
that are not locally radial.

1.1.
Strategy of the proof

Theorem
1.1
relies on a bifurcation argument: nonradial stationary flows with compact support branch out from a suitably chosen family of radially symmetric, compactly supported flows.
These radial flows are described by a one-parameter family of radial stream functions
\psi_{a}
which are supported on certain annuli
\Omega_{a}
and which vanishes on
\partial\Omega_{a}
to a high order
m\geqslant 1
. In a nutshell, the way one aims to implement a bifurcation argument is by ensuring that, inside their support,
\psi_{a}
satisfies certain differential equation. The gist of the argument is to show that, for some value of the parameter
a
, one can consider a smooth small nonradial deformation
\psi
of
\psi_{a}
which satisfies the same equation on a slightly deformed domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
. It is then easy to see that if
\psi
also vanishes on the boundary of the deformed domain to order
m
, then the vector field defined by
v:=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
on the domain and
v:=0
outside is of class
C^{m-1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. The equation satisfied by
\psi
must therefore ensure that its perpendicular gradient
v:=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
is a stationary solution to the Euler equations (
1.3
).

For a bifurcation argument, it is known that one cannot directly use the Euler equation (
1.2
), since its linerization is a completely unmanageable operator with an infinite-dimensional kernel. Vortex patch solutions are not
C^{1}
, so it is not clear how one could adapt the strategy of
[
12
]
. Also, a variation of Gavrilov’s construction can only give locally radial solutions of compact support
[
30
]
. One would naively think that the elliptic equation (
1.3
) should be the way to go, but in fact this is not true: in Theorem
A.2
we show that any compactly supported stationary flow of class
C^{2}
whose stream function satisfies a semilinear equation of the form (
1.3
) must be locally radial.

Hence, in this problem, even the choice of the equation one should consider is rather nontrivial. For us, the starting point of the paper is the construction of a
non-autonomous
nonlinearity
f_{a}
enabling us to effectively use the equation

(1.4)

\Delta\psi_{a}+f_{a}(|x|,\psi_{a})=0\,,

to construct compactly supported solutions. To
our best knowledge, this is the first time that non-autonomous elliptic equations have been used for a similar purpose.

Still, passing from this rough idea to an actual proof is remarkably hard. This is because the above outline does not address the three essential difficulties that the problem entails:

(i)

The radial stream function
\psi_{a}
that we consider is a positive solution to an equation of the form (
1.4
) on an annulus
\Omega_{a}
, which vanishes to
m
-th order on
\partial\Omega_{a}
. The deformation
\psi
will satisfy the same equation on the deformed domain
\widetilde{\Omega}
and tend to zero as
\tilde{\rho}^{m}
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
. However, even with our choice of the nonlinearity, if
\psi
is a nonradial solution to (
1.4
) in
\widetilde{\Omega}
,
\nabla^{\perp}\psi
does
not
satisfy the stationary Euler equations: this is only true if
\psi
is close to
\psi_{a}
in a certain sense (see Lemma
2.1
).

(ii)

Suppose that the function
\psi
vanishes to order
m\geqslant 3
on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, where
\widetilde{\Omega}
can be thought of as a slightly deformed annulus. For concreteness, we can think that
\psi=\tilde{\rho}^{m}u
, where
u
is a smooth function that does not vanish on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
and where
\tilde{\rho}
is a boundary defining function, that is, a positive function on
\widetilde{\Omega}
that vanishes on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
exactly to first order. Since
\Delta\psi
goes like
\tilde{\rho}^{m-2}
near
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, if
\psi
satisfies a semilinear equation like (
1.4
), the nonlinearity
f(|x|,t)
can only be Hölder continuous and must behave like
|t|^{1-\frac{2}{m}}
near
0
. Thus, the linearization of this equation, which one expects to encounter in any bifurcation argument, will be controlled by an operator of the form

L=-\Delta+\frac{c}{\tilde{\rho}^{2}}
for some nonzero constant
c
(modulo terms that are less singular). The potential term is then critically singular (i.e., it scales like the Laplacian), so it cannot be treated as a perturbation of
\Delta
: a new set of estimates is necessary.

(iii)

To control the deformation of the domain, one would naively parametrize the deformed domain, say in polar coordinates, as
\widetilde{\Omega}:=\{a_{-}+b(\theta)<r<a_{+}+B(\theta)\}
, where
b,B
are small functions on the circle
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. However, this approach is known to lead to a serious loss of derivatives
[
7
,
8
]
which one does not know how to compensate using a Nash–Moser iteration scheme.

Furthermore, the complexity of the problem resides on the fact that these difficulties are strongly interrelated. As we have already mentioned, the starting point of our approach is the construction of a non-autonomous nonlinearity
f_{a}(r,t)
such that any solution to (
1.4
) that is close enough to our radial solution
\psi_{a}
does define a stationary Euler flow. This has to be done carefully, as typical non-autonomous nonlinearities do not have this crucial property. Roughly speaking, the key idea is to construct the non-autonomous nonlinearity
f_{a}
and the radial function
\psi_{a}
such that
\psi_{a}
solves an autonomous equation of the form
\Delta\psi_{a}+f_{a,p}(\psi_{a})=0
in a small neighborhood of each point
p
. This local property suffices to show that the vector field
\nabla^{\perp}\psi
satisfies the stationary Euler equation, and we can effectively control
\psi
in the arguments using the global equation (
1.4
).

Near
t=0
, the non-autonomous nonlinearity
f_{a}(r,t)
has the asymptotic behavior described in item (i) above, and the most singular part of the linearized operators we need to consider in the bifurcation argument is indeed like the aforementioned operator
L
. In general, the theory of uniformly degenerate elliptic operators
[
26
,
16
,
24
]
(which is essentially a sophisticated PDE analog of the Frobenius theory for ODEs with regular singular points) is well suited to the study of operators such as
L
. The key concept here is that of the
indicial roots
of the operator, defined as the constants
\nu
for which
L(\tilde{\rho}^{\nu})=O(\tilde{\rho}^{\nu-1})
in a neighborhood of a boundary component. Denoting by
\underline{\nu},\overline{\nu}
the smallest and largest indicial roots of
L
, the rule of thumb for well behaved operators of this kind is that
\tilde{\rho}^{2}L
defines a Fredholm map between spaces of functions that are of order
\tilde{\rho}^{\nu}
near
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
, provided that
\nu
is not an indicial root and
\underline{\nu}<\nu<\overline{\nu}
. These function spaces are defined using the scale-natural Hölder or Sobolev norms

(1.5)

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{C^{j,\alpha}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\|\tilde{\rho}^{-\nu+l}\nabla^{l}\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\widetilde{\Omega})}+\sup_{x,x^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\Omega}}\tilde{\rho}(x)^{-\nu+j+\alpha}\frac{|\nabla^{j}\psi(x)-\nabla^{j}\psi(x^{\prime})|}{|x-x^{\prime}|^{\alpha}}\,,

\displaystyle\|\psi\|_{H^{j}_{\nu}}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{l=0}^{j}\|\tilde{\rho}^{-\nu+l-1}\nabla^{l}\psi\|_{L^{2}(\widetilde{\Omega})}\,.

However, in our problem we find two major complications that prevent us from basing our approach on off-the-shelf estimates and spaces. Firstly, we find that our linearized operator has exactly two indicial roots, which are completely determined by the decay rate
m
as
\underline{\nu}=2-m
and
\overline{\nu}=m-1
, and we crucially need to control functions that are critical in that they behave like
\tilde{\rho}^{\overline{\nu}}
. Secondly, the fact that the function
\tilde{\rho}
vanishes on
\partial\widetilde{\Omega}
makes the norms (
1.5
) strongly unsuitable to control the nonlinearities that arise in our problem. Hence we need to develop from scratch a sharp regularity theory for this kind of operators that is adapted to the situation at hand.

This has a major impact in the way we address the loss of derivatives. In the approach introduced in
[
8
]
and used in the papers
[
8
,
7
]
about analogs of the Schiffer problem, the loss of derivatives is overcome by the use of anisotropic Hölder spaces where the functions are one derivative smoother in the radial variable than they are in the angular variable, since in this setting the linearized map turns out to be a Fredholm operator of index
0
. In our case, finding a functional setting where one can eliminate the loss of derivatives and where the linearized operators are Fredholm operators of index 0 turns out to be rather subtle, as illustrated by the fact that we eventually consider nonlinear maps from the Hilbert space

{\mathbf{X}}^{j}:=\Big\{u\in H^{j}(\Omega):\rho\,\partial_{R}u\in H^{j}(\Omega)\Big\}\,,

into the Banach space

{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}:=\Big\{\rho u_{1}+u_{2}:u_{1}\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j-2}\,,\;u_{2}\in H^{j-1}(\Omega)\Big\}\,,

both spaces being equipped with their natural norms.
Here
(R,\theta)
are polar coordinates on the annulus
\Omega:=\{1<R<7\}
, the weight
\rho(R,\theta):=\frac{1}{6}(R-1)(7-R)
vanishes on
\partial\Omega
, and
j
is a large integer. The switch from Hölder to Sobolev spaces is not incidental. Roughly speaking, an essential ingredient to establish Fredholmness are sharp regularity estimates for the linearized map, which are standard when this map is uniformly elliptic with smooth coefficients as in
[
7
,
8
]
but certainly not in the present setting. In our context,
L^{2}
-based Sobolev norms are much better suited than Hölder norms to effectively capture the interplay between the singular potential and the high frequencies which underlies the regularity of solutions.

Once these issues have been settled, one can indeed prove the bifurcation result that translates into Theorem
1.1
. In view of the fairly delicate analytic setting in which we need to work, proving the technical transversality conditions necessary to use an implicit-theorem-based bifurcation theorem such as Crandall–Rabinowitz seems to be highly impractical. Nevertheless, we can resort to Krasnoselskii’s degree-theory-based global bifurcation theorem, where in fact the construction of the nonlinearity
f_{a}
and the radial solution
\psi_{a}
readily enables us to ensure that the crossing number condition of this theorem is satisfied, provided that we additionally restrict our functional setting to functions that are invariant under a certain discrete group of rotations.

1.2.
Related results

There is an extensive literature on stationary solutions to the incompressible Euler equations on the plane. In particular, much is known about rigidity conditions, that is, hypotheses which determine the geometry of the possible solutions in certain cases. Among these so-called Liouville theorems, one can mention the results of Hamel and Nadirashvili, which ensure that smooth stationary Euler flows without stagnation points (i.e., with
\nabla\psi\neq 0
) on a bounded planar domain must inherit the symmetry of the domain under suitable assumptions
[
18
,
19
,
20
]
, particularly in the case of disks and annuli. In the case of compactly supported stationary flows on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
which we consider in this paper, sufficient conditions for (local) radial symmetry are the absence of stagnation points
[
29
]
or the fact that the vorticity
-\Delta\psi
does not change sign
[
13
]
. Other results about the rigidity and flexibility of planar stationary fluids in various geometric settings include
[
5
,
4
,
25
,
17
]
for smooth solutions to the Euler equations,
[
9
,
12
,
13
,
15
,
14
]
for vortex patches and sheets, and
[
22
,
2
]
for Navier–Stokes and for aggregration equations.

Organization of the paper

In Section
2
we present the proof of Theorem
1.1
. The proofs of most of the results stated there will be postponed to further sections. Specifically, Section
3
is devoted to the construction of the non-autonomous semilinear equation we will use to obtain stationary Euler flows. In Section
4
we elaborate on the functional setting introduced in Section
2
, and in Section
5
we analyze the basic properties of the nonlinear map
{\mathcal{G}}_{a}
used to prove the main theorem. Sections
6
and

are devoted to the proof of sharp regularity estimates in our weighted spaces for the linearization of the operator
{\mathcal{G}}_{a}
. Finally, the analysis of the spectral properties of the linearized operator is performed in Section

. The paper concludes with an appendix where we show that stationary Euler flows of class
C^{2}
with compact support cannot be obtained via an autonomous semilinear equation for the stream function.

2.
Proof of the main result

In this section we present the proof Theorem
1.1
. To streamline the presentation, we will state several key auxiliary results whose proofs are relegated to later sections. Throughout, we consider that
m\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{Z}
is a fixed constant,
j\geqslant 4
is a fixed integer, and
a\geqslant 4
is a real constant. We also assume that
m\geqslant j+2
.

Step 1: Stationary Euler flows via non-autonomous nonlinearities

The proof of Theorem
1.1
starts with an elementary but crucial remark connecting the 2D stationary Euler equations to a non-autonomous semilinear elliptic PDE. The results of this section will be proved in Section
3
.

For this, let
\chi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
be a smooth function such that

\chi=0\quad\textup{on }(-\infty,-1]\quad\textup{and}\quad\chi=1\quad\textup{on }[1,+\infty)\,.

Suppose that
\psi\in C_{c}^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a solution to a non-autonomous semilinear equation of the form

(2.1)

\Delta\psi+f_{a}(|x|,\,\psi)=0\qquad\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

where

f_{a}(r,t):=\chi(r-a)f_{-}(t)+[1-\chi(r-a)]f_{+}(t)\,,

for some continuous functions
f_{\pm}
. A straightforward calculation shows that the velocity field given by

(2.2)

v:=\nabla^{\perp}\psi

defines a compactly supported solution to (
1.1
) on the plane if and only if

(2.3)

\chi^{\prime}(|x|-a)\big[f_{-}(\psi)-f_{+}(\psi)\big]\,\nabla^{\perp}\psi\cdot e_{r}=0\quad\textup{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\,,

where
e_{r}
is the unit radial vector.
In this case, the pressure can be recovered from
v
via the formula

p=-\Delta^{-1}\Div(v\cdot\nabla v)\,.

The take away message here is that, if for some functions
f_{\pm}
we are able to find a solution
\psi\in C_{c}^{k+1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to (
2.1
) which is not locally radial and satisfies
f_{-}(\psi)=f_{+}(\psi)
in
\mathbb{D}_{a+1}\setminus\overline{\mathbb{D}}_{a-1}
, then the vector field
v
defined in (
2.2
) is a nonradial, compactly supported solution to (
1.1
) of class
C^{k}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
. In what follows we shall show how to implement this strategy to prove Theorem
1.1
.

Specifically, we will eventually construct nonradial solutions whose support is a nonradial small perturbation of an annulus that we can describe in polar coordinates
(r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}
as

\Omega_{*}:=\{(r,\theta)\in(a_{-}^{*},a_{+}^{*})\times\mathbb{T}\,\}\,.

Here
a^{*}
is some large constant that we will choose later on and
\mathbb{T}:=\mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}
. In this formula and in what follows, for any real constant
a>3
, we use the notation
a_{\pm}:=a\pm 3
.

The nonlinearities that we will consider in Equation (
2.1
) are

f_{\pm}(t):=|t|^{1-\frac{2}{m}}g_{\pm}(t)\,,

where
g_{-},g_{+}
are smooth functions that we will specify later on. This function depends smoothly on the parameter
a\geqslant{4}
, but we will not reflect this dependence notationally. We shall also use the notation

(2.4)

f_{a}(r,t):=\chi(r-a)\,|t|^{1-\frac{2}{m}}g_{-}(t)+[1-\chi(r-a)]\,|t|^{1-\frac{2}{m}}g_{+}(t)\,.

Although the one-variable functions
\chi
and
g_{\pm}
will be smooth, the nonlinearity
f_{a}
is only a Hölder continuous function on its second argument (with exponent
1-\tfrac{2}{m}
).

The first key ingredient in the proof of the main theorem is the following lemma. To state it we need to make precise the rate at which functions vanish on the boundary of the annulus

(2.5)

\Omega_{a}:=\{(r,\theta)\in(a_{-},a_{+})\times\mathbb{T}\,\}\,.

To this end, let us introduce the function

\rho_{a}(r):=\frac{1}{6}(a_{+}-r)(r-a_{-})\,,

which is a convenient choice of a radial function that is positive in
\Omega_{a}
and vanishes linearly on
\partial\Omega_{a}
. Also, note that
|\rho^{\prime}_{a}(a_{\iota})|=1
, where here and in what follows
\iota\in\{-,+\}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

There exist a constant
\varepsilon>0
and functions
\Psi_{a},g_{\pm}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that:

(i)

The function
\psi_{a}:=\rho_{a}^{m}\,\Psi_{a}\in C^{m}(\overline{\Omega}_{a})
is a radial solution to (
2.1
) in
\Omega_{a}
.

(ii)

\inf_{a_{-}<r<a_{+}}\Psi_{a}(r)>0
and
g_{\iota}(a_{\iota})\neq 0
for
\iota\in\{-,+\}
.

(iii)

If
\Omega\subset\{a_{-}-\varepsilon<r<a_{+}+\varepsilon\}
is a planar domain with
C^{2,\alpha}
boundary and
\psi\in C^{2,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})
satisfies the semilinear equation (
2.1
) in
\Omega
and the bound

\|\psi-\psi_{a}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega_{a})}+\|\psi\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\backslash\Omega_{a})}<\varepsilon\,,

then the vector field
v:=\nabla^{\perp}\psi\in C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)
satisfies the stationary Euler equation (
1.1
) in
\Omega
(with some
C^{2,\alpha}
pressure).

Step 2: Setting up the problem

Taking into account Step 1 and Lemma
2.1
, we aim to find non radial solutions to (
2.1
) bifurcating from
\psi_{a}
. The technical results of this step are developed in Section
4
, where we deal with the functional setting, and Section
5
. Given a constant
a\geqslant{4}
and functions
b,B\in C^{j-2}(\mathbb{T})
bounded e.g. as

\|b\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}+\|B\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}<\tfrac{1}{10}\,,

we consider bounded domains defined in polar coordinates by

(2.6)

\Omega_{a}^{b,B}:=\{(r,\theta)\in\mathbb{R}^{+}\times\mathbb{T}:a_{-}+b(\theta)<r<a_{+}+B(\theta)\}\,.

Then, as explained in Step 1, Theorem
1.1
follows from the following result:

Theorem 2.2
.

There exist a number
a^{*}>4
and sequences
(a_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset[4,+\infty)
,
(b_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty},(B_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C^{j-2}(\mathbb{T})
, and
(\psi_{n})_{n=1}^{\infty}\subset C^{j-2}(\overline{\Omega}_{a_{n}}^{\,b_{n},B_{n}})
such that:

(i)

a_{n}\to a^{*}
as
n\to\infty
.

(ii)

b_{n}
and
B_{n}
are nonconstant functions which tend to
0
in the
C^{j-2}
-norm.

(iii)

\psi_{n}
are positive solutions to

(2.7)

\Delta\psi_{n}+f_{a_{n}}(|x|,\,\psi_{n})=0\quad\text{ in }\Omega_{a_{n}}^{\,b_{n},B_{n}}\,,

such that
\psi_{n}(x)\dist(x,\partial\Omega_{a_{n}}^{b_{n},B_{n}})^{-m}
is uniformly bounded in
\overline{\Omega}_{a_{n}}^{\,b_{n},B_{n}}
. Moreover, if we extend
\psi_{n}
by
0
outside the domains
\Omega_{a_{n}}^{\,b_{n},B_{n}}
, we have that
\psi_{n}\to\psi_{a}
in
C^{j-2}
.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of this result. It is convenient to transform the equations and to work on a fixed domain. For this, we map the fixed annulus

\Omega:=\Omega_{4}=\big\{(R,\theta)\in(1,7)\times\mathbb{T}\big\}

into
\Omega_{a}^{b,B}
through the diffeomorfism

(2.8)

\Phi_{a}^{b,B}:\Omega\to\Omega_{a}^{b,B}

defined in polar coordinates as
\Omega\ni(R,\theta)\mapsto(r,\theta)\in\Omega_{a}^{b,B}
, with

(2.9)

r(R,\theta):=\frac{1}{6}\Big[[a_{+}+B^{\flat}(R,\theta)](R-1)+[a_{-}+b^{\sharp}(R,\theta)](7-R)\Big]\,.

Here,
b^{\sharp}
and
B^{\flat}
are defined as follows: we fix an even cutoff function
\widetilde{\chi}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
such that

(2.10)

\widetilde{\chi}(s)=1\quad\textup{if }|s|<\tfrac{1}{2}\,,\quad\textup{ and }\quad\widetilde{\chi}(s)=0\quad\textup{ if }|s|>1\,,

and then set

(2.11)

b^{\sharp}(R,\theta):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}b_{n}e^{in\theta}\widetilde{\chi}\big((1+|n|)(R-1)\big)\,,\quad B^{\flat}(R,\theta):=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}B_{n}e^{in\theta}\widetilde{\chi}\big((1+|n|)(R-7)\big)\,,

in terms of the Fourier coefficients

b_{n}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}b(\theta)e^{-in\theta}d\theta\,,\qquad\qquad B_{n}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}B(\theta)e^{-in\theta}d\theta\,.

Note that the diffeomorphism is as smooth (say in the scale of Hölder norms) as
b,B
are. In general, throughout the paper we will regard the sharp and flat operators maps from functions on
\mathbb{T}
to functions on
\Omega
. Let us point out that
b^{\sharp},B^{\flat}
can be understood as trace liftings of the functions
b,B
, respectively. For future reference, let us record here the following bound. Since
\Omega
does not contain the origin, for convenience we will define the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-norm using the measure
dRd\theta
on
(1,7)\times\mathbb{T}
unless specified otherwise, that is

\|u\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}^{2}:=\int_{\Omega}u^{2}\,dR\,d\theta\,.

The proof of this result will be given in Section
4
, where we actually provide a more precise statement.

Lemma 2.3
.

For
b^{\sharp}
and
B^{\flat}
as in (
2.11
), it follows that

\|b^{\sharp}\|_{H^{j}(\Omega)}+\|\rho\,\partial_{R}b^{\sharp}\|_{H^{j}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|b\|_{H^{j-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}\,,\qquad\|B^{\flat}\|_{H^{j}(\Omega)}+\|\rho\,\partial_{R}B^{\flat}\|_{H^{j}(\Omega)}\leqslant C\|B\|_{H^{j-\frac{1}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}\,.

For later purposes, we also introduce the shorthand notation

\Phi_{a}:=\Phi_{a}^{0,0}\,,

and denote by

The following assertions hold true:

Remark 2.5
.

Let us provide an intrinsic motivation for the definitions in (

introduced in Lemma

and

and that

with

Hence, it is natural to look for a solution to (

with

Step 3: The linearized operator

To find nontrivial zeros of the operator
{\mathcal{G}}_{a}
, we will use a local bifurcation argument. The Fréchet derivative of

will play a major role in the argument. In this step we compute the Fréchet derivative of

and study its properties.

In the first lemma, which will be proved in Section

{\mathcal{G}}_{a}
at

:

Lemma 2.6
.

The map
{\mathcal{G}}_{a}:{\mathbf{O}}^{j}\to{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is of class

. Furthermore,

is the linear operator given by

D{\mathcal{G}}_{a}(0)w=\rho^{2-m}\mathfrak{L}_{a}(\rho^{m-1}w)\,,

where

(2.22)

\mathfrak{L}_{a}(v):=L_{a}^{0,0}\,v+f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\,\widetilde{\psi}_{a})\,v\,.

Our next objective is to understand the structure of
D{\mathcal{G}}_{a}(0)
. A first observation is that the function

appearing in Lemma
2.6
is radial, diverges as the inverse square of the distance to the boundary of
\Omega
, and is smooth in the interior. More precisely, we have the following result, which we will prove in Section
3
:

Lemma 2.7
.

The function
R\mapsto\rho^{2}\,f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})
is of class
C^{\infty}([1,7])
. Furthermore,

\rho^{2}f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})=-(m-1)(m-2)+O(\rho)\,.

In view of Lemma
2.6
and Lemma

, let us decompose the Fréchet derivative of

at 0 as

D{\mathcal{G}}_{a}(0)={\mathcal{T}}_{a}+{\mathcal{K}}_{a}\,,

where

(2.23)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{a}w

\displaystyle:=\rho\left[\rho^{1-m}L_{a}^{0,0}(\rho^{m-1}w)-\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho^{2}}w\right]\,,

(2.24)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{K}}_{a}w

\displaystyle:=\rho\left[f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})+\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho^{2}}\right]w\,.

One should think of

as a positive-definite essentially self-adjoint operator of second order with a critically singular potential (in the sense that

Theorem 2.8
.

The operator
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}:{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\to{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is an isomorphism, and
{\mathcal{K}}_{a}:{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\to{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is compact.

Step 4: The bifurcation argument

The proof of Theorem
2.2
will follow from Kranoselskii’s local bifurcation theorem. The details of this step are gathered in Section
8
. For the benefit of the reader, we start by providing a precise statement of Kranoselskii’s theorem

1

1

This is in fact a slightly refined version of the Krasnoselskii bifurcation theorem, as one typically assumes the existence of an isolated point where the differential
D_{w}G(0,a)
is degenerate. However, the usual proof of the theorem, as presented in

Theorem 2.9

(Kranoselskii)
.

Let
U\subset Y
be an open subset of a Banach space
Y
, which we assume to contain
0
, and let
A\subset\mathbb{R}
be an open interval. Let
\widehat{\mathcal{G}}:U\times A\rightarrow Y
be a
C^{1}
map such that:

(i)

\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(0,a)=0
for all
a\in A
.

(ii)

The map
w\mapsto\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(w,a)-w
is compact for each
a\in A
.

(iii)

Denoting by
\ind(a)
the index of
D_{w}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(0,a)
(that is, the sum of the algebraic multiplicities of all negative eigenvalues of
D_{w}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(0,a)
), we assume that there exist values of the parameter
a_{1}<a_{2}
in
A
such that:

(a)

The differential
D_{w}\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(0,a_{i})
is non degenerate for

.

(b)

\ind(a_{1})
and
\ind(a_{2})
have different parity.

Then there exists a bifurcation point
a_{*}\in(a_{1},a_{2})
, in the sense that
(0,a_{*})\in Y\times A
is an accumulation point of the set of nontrivial solutions
\{(w,a)\in(Y\backslash\{0\})\times A:\widehat{\mathcal{G}}(a,w)=0\}
.

Our aim now is to rewrite the equation
{\mathcal{G}}_{a}(w)=0
in an equivalent way, which is well adapted to the use of Krasnoselskii’s bifurcation theorem. On this purpose, we first note that the operator

\mathfrak{L}_{a}:H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\Omega)\,,\quad v\mapsto L_{a}^{0,0}v+f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})\,v\,,

defines a positive symmetric bilinear form
{\mathcal{B}}:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}(v,w)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}\big(L_{a}^{0,0}v+f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})v\big)w\,(R+a-4)\,dR\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left[\partial_{R}v\,\partial_{R}w+\frac{\partial_{\theta}v\,\partial_{\theta}w}{(R+a-4)^{2}}+\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho(R)^{2}}vw-P_{a}(R)vw\right](R+a-4)\,dR\,d\theta\,.

Here
P_{a}
is the radial function

P_{a}(R):=f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})+\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho(R)^{2}}\,.

Note that this bilinear form and the operator
\mathfrak{L}_{a}:H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\to H^{-1}(\Omega)
are well defined thanks to the Hardy inequality. Moreover, by the properties of
f_{a}^{\prime}
proved in Lemma
2.7
,
\rho(R)\,P_{a}(R)
is a smooth function on
\overline{\Omega}
.

It is standard that there exists a sequence of eigenvalues of
-\mathfrak{L}_{a}
with finite multiplicity, which we denote by
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda_{k}(a)

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leqslant\lambda_{3}\leqslant\cdots

Of course,
\lambda_{k}
tends to infinity as

. There exists a orthonormal basis of

consisting of eigenfunctions of
-\mathfrak{L}_{a}
, which we denote by
\phi_{k}\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)

{\mathcal{B}}(\phi_{k},v)=\lambda_{k}\int_{\Omega}\phi_{k}\,v\,dx\qquad\forall\ v\in H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\,.

By the regularity properties of the linearized operator (Proposition
6.9
), the eigenfunctions
\phi_{k}
actually belong to
\rho^{m-1}\,{\mathbf{X}}^{j}
. Writing
\phi_{k}=\rho^{m-1}w_{k}
with
w_{k}\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j}
, one then has

(2.25)

-\mathfrak{L}_{a}\,\phi_{k}=\lambda_{k}\phi_{k}\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ -D{\mathcal{G}}_{a}(0)\,w_{k}=\lambda_{k}\rho\,w_{k}\,.

In order to apply Kranoselskii’s bifurcation theorem, we need to study the sign of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}
and to find some degeneracies. On that purpose, we need to take an integer
\ell\geqslant 3
and to restrict our attention to the space of
\mathbb{Z}_{\ell}\,
- symmetric functions, i.e., functions that are invariant under the action of the isometry group of an
\ell

(2.26)

\displaystyle H^{j}_{\ell}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\Big\{w\in H^{j}(\Omega):w(R,\theta)=w(R,-\theta)\,,\ u(R,\theta)=u\Big(R,\frac{2\pi}{\ell}\Big)\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle H^{j}_{0,\ell}(\Omega)

\displaystyle:=\big\{w\in H^{j}_{\ell}(\Omega):w|_{\partial\Omega}=0\big\}\,,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}^{j}_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{w\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j}:w(R,\theta)=w(R,-\theta)\,,\ u(R,\theta)=u\Big(R,\frac{2\pi}{\ell}\Big)\Big\}\,,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{X}}^{j}_{{\rm{D}},\ell}

\displaystyle:=\big\{w\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j}_{\ell}:w|_{\partial\Omega}=0\big\}\,,

\displaystyle{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}_{\ell}

\displaystyle:=\Big\{w\in{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}:w(R,\theta)=w(R,-\theta)\,,\ u(R,\theta)=u\Big(R,\frac{2\pi}{\ell}\Big)\Big\}\,.

Of course, the eigenvalues depend on the symmetry chosen and on the parameter

\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda_{k}^{\ell}(a)

In the following proposition we establish the degeneracy property of eigenvalues that is required to apply the Kranoselskii’s bifurcation theorem. More precisely, we have the following:

Proposition 2.10
.

Given
\varepsilon>0
, there exist
a_{1}>a_{0}\geqslant 4
,
\ell\in\mathbb{N}

\ell\geqslant 3

(i)

For any

\lambda_{1}^{\ell}(a)<\lambda_{2}^{\ell}(a)<-\varepsilon<\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a)

(ii)

\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a_{0})>0
and
\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a_{1})<0

(iii)

If

\lambda_{4}^{\ell}(a)>0

First of all, recall that

Then, taking into account Proposition

Remark 3.2
.

Such example cannot be built for a semilinear problem of the form

\Delta\psi+f(\psi)=0\,,

for any continuous function
f
. Indeed, if
\psi

\psi^{\prime\prime}(r)+\psi^{\prime}(r)/r+f(\psi(r))=0\,.

Multiplying by

and integrating we then obtain that

\Big(\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}+F(\psi(r))\Big)^{\prime}=-\psi^{\prime}(r)^{2}/r\leqslant 0\,,

with equality holding only on the critical points of
\psi
. Here,
F
denotes a primitive of
f
. Hence, in this case, the function

is strictly decreasing in

.

If we now choose

and impose that
\psi(a_{+})=\psi^{\prime}(a_{+})=\psi(a_{-})=0
, we would have that

\frac{1}{2}\,\psi^{\prime}(a_{-})^{2}+F(0)=\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime}(a_{-})^{2}+F(\psi(a_{-}))=P(a_{-})>P(a_{+})=\frac{1}{2}\psi^{\prime}(a_{+})^{2}+F(\psi(a_{+}))=F(0)\,,

and so that
\psi^{\prime}(a_{-})\neq 0
.

More precisely, given an open set

We recall that the function

is positive in

and vanishes on

is an integer.

We also define the closed subset of elements with zero boundary trace,

{\mathbf{X}}^{j}_{\rm{D}}(\Omega^{\prime}):=\big\{w\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j}(\Omega^{\prime}):w|_{\partial\Omega^{\prime}}=0\big\}

and the Banach space

{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}(\Omega^{\prime}):=\rho\,{\mathbf{X}}^{j-2}(\Omega^{\prime})+H^{j-1}(\Omega^{\prime})\,,

endowed with the canonical norm for the sum of two embedded Banach spaces:

\|w\|_{{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}(\Omega^{\prime})}:=\inf\Big\{\|{w_{1}}\|_{{\mathbf{X}}^{j-2}(\Omega^{\prime})}+\|w_{2}\|_{H^{j-1}(\Omega^{\prime})}:w=\rho w_{1}+w_{2}\textup{ with }{w_{1}}\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j-2}(\Omega^{\prime}),\ w_{2}\in H^{j-1}(\Omega^{\prime})\Big\}\,.

\Omega
, we omit this fact notationally and write
{\mathbf{X}}^{j}:={\mathbf{X}}^{j}(\Omega)
,
{\mathbf{X}}^{j}_{\rm{D}}:={\mathbf{X}}^{j}_{\rm{D}}(\Omega)
, and
{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}:={\mathbf{Y}}^{j}(\Omega)
.

Lemma 4.1

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|b_{n}|^{2}n^{2j_{1}}(1+|n|)^{2j_{2}+2l}\int_{1}^{2}\rho^{2l+2k}\big[\widetilde{\chi}^{(j_{2}+l)}\big((1+|n|)(R-1)\big)\big]^{2}dR

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|b_{n}|^{2}n^{2j_{1}}(1+|n|)^{2j_{2}+2l}\int_{1}^{2}(R-1)^{2l+2k}\big[\widetilde{\chi}^{(j_{2}+l)}\big((1+|n|)(R-1)\big)\big]^{2}dR

For any

Theorem 4.3
.

(

Let us fix two weight functions

holds for all functions

Let

has good bounds in Sobolev spaces. Note that the sharp term in

6.
Fredholmness and invertibility properties of the linearized operator

This section is devoted to the proof of Theorem
2.8
. Using Lemma
4.4
, it is easy to show the compactness of the operator
{\mathcal{K}}_{a}
defined in (
2.24
). The hardest part is to show that
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
(introduced in (
2.23
)) is an isomorphism. We split the proof into two parts. In the first part, we shall prove an easy existence and uniqueness result on low regularity spaces for the linear elliptic equation defined by the operator
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
. In the second part, which is considerably harder, we prove sharp regularity estimates for the solutions. Theorem
2.8
then follows. In particular,
D{\mathcal{G}}_{a}(0)
is a Fredholm operator of index 0. The proof of a key estimate (Lemma
6.6
) is presented in Section
7
.

Lemma 6.1
.

For all
j\geqslant 2
, the operator
{\mathcal{K}}_{a}:{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\to{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is compact.

Proof.

By Lemma
4.4
we know that the embedding
{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\hookrightarrow{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is compact. Also, note that
\rho\,\partial_{R}
maps
{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\to H^{j}(\Omega)
. It is apparent that
H^{j}(\Omega)
is compactly embedded in
{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
, so
\rho\,\partial_{R}:{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\to{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is compact.

Now, observe that, by Lemma
2.7
, the operator
{\mathcal{K}}_{a}
is of the form

{\mathcal{K}}_{a}w=K_{1}\,\rho\,\partial_{R}w+K_{2}\,w\,,

where
K_{1},K_{2}:\overline{\Omega}\to\mathbb{R}
are smooth radial functions. The compactness of
{\mathcal{K}}_{a}
then follows.
∎

6.1.
Existence and uniqueness in a low regularity space

Let us consider the equation

(6.1)

{\mathcal{T}}_{a}w=F

which one can rewrite using (
2.23
) as

(6.2)

L_{a}^{0,0}(\rho^{m-1}w)-\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho^{2}}\rho^{m-1}w=\rho^{m-2}F\,.

Let us recall that the differential operator
L_{a}^{0,0}
was introduced in (
2.14
). The following result is then elementary:

Proposition 6.2
.

For each
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
, there exists a unique
w\in\rho^{1-m}H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}w=F
. Furthermore,

\|\rho^{m-1}w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

Proof.

Let us write
W:=\rho^{m-1}w\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
, so that (
2.23
) reads as

(6.3)

L_{a}^{0,0}W-\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho^{2}}W=\rho^{m-2}F\,.

Noting that
(R+a-4)\,dRd\theta
is a positive measure on
\Omega
because
a\geqslant 4
and
R\in(1,7)
, let us set

(6.4)

{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(U,V):=\int_{\Omega}\left[\partial_{R}U\,\partial_{R}V+\frac{\partial_{\theta}U\,\partial_{\theta}V}{(R+a-4)^{2}}+\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho(R)^{2}}UV\right]\,(R+a-4)\,dRd\theta\,.

This is the bilinear form associated with the equation (
6.3
), as can be seen from the fact that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(U,V)=-\int_{\Omega}\left[L_{a}^{0,0}U-\frac{(m-1)(m-2)}{\rho^{2}}U\right]V\,(R+a-4)\,dR\,d\theta

if
U,V\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
.

Note that
{\mathcal{B}}_{0}:H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\times H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\to\mathbb{R}
is well-defined by Hardy’s inequality, and obviously

{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(U,U)\geqslant\|U\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}^{2}\,.

By Lax–Milgram (or by the Riesz representation theorem, as the quadratic form is symmetric), we conclude that for each
F\in L^{2}(\Omega)
there exists a unique
W\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
such that

{\mathcal{B}}_{0}(W,V)=\int_{\Omega}\rho^{m-2}FV\,(R+a-4)\,dRd\theta\qquad\text{for all }V\in H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)\,,

which is moreover bounded as

\|W\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|\rho^{m-2}F\|_{H^{-1}(\Omega)}\lesssim\|F\|_{L^{2}(\Omega)}\,.

The proposition then follows. ∎

Although this elementary result yields solution to the equation
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}w=F
, it is not obvious a priori that
w\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j}
when
F\in{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
, so we cannot conclude from this result that
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
is an isomorphism between these spaces. What we do obtain from this result and from the fact that
{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\subset\rho^{1-m}H^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
is that the kernel of
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}
is trivial:

Corollary 6.3
.

The map
{\mathcal{T}}_{a}:{\mathbf{X}}^{j}\to{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
is injective.

6.2.
Regularity estimates

Our objective now is to show that
w\in{\mathbf{X}}^{j}
whenever
F\in{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}
. Away from
\partial\Omega
, the elliptic operator in (

Lemma 6.8

The operator

Proposition 6.9

Let

\|w\|_{{\mathbf{X}}^{j}}\lesssim\|F\|_{{\mathbf{Y}}^{j}}+\|\rho^{m-1}w\|_{H^{1}(\Omega)}\,.

7.
Analysis of the operator
{\mathcal{L}}

In this section we shall prove the key estimate for the equation
{\mathcal{L}}v=G
presented in Lemma
6.6
. For clarity, we will divide the proof into two parts. In the first one we shall estimate the solution to certain ODE which depends on a parameter
\alpha\geqslant 0
. The point of these estimates is that one needs to capture the sharp dependence of the constants on the parameter
\alpha
. In the second part of this section, we shall see that this ODE arises from the PDE
{\mathcal{L}}v=G
after taking the Fourier transform in the angular variable, with the parameter
\alpha
being essentially the Fourier frequency multiplied by the small scale parameter
\varepsilon
introduced in Lemma
6.6
. Sharp ODE bounds then capture the interplay between large frequencies and the effect of the singularity on the boundary of the annular domain
\Omega
, and translate into sharp estimates for
{\mathcal{L}}
.

7.1.
Auxiliary ODE estimates

A straightforward variation on Proposition

Theorem 7.2
.

(

Proof of Lemma

In the estimates, we need to consider the cases

To capture the effect on the large parameter

\displaystyle t\partial_{t}^{2}u_{W}(t)+(2m-2)\partial_{t}u_{W}(t)-tu_{W}(t)

\displaystyle=\alpha^{-1}\,W_{f}(t)\,.

.

Note that two linearly independent solutions for the Bessel-type homogeneous equation

t\partial_{t}^{2}h+(2m-2)\partial_{t}h-th=0\qquad\text{in }(0,\infty)\,,

are

(7.10)

h_{1}(t):=t^{\frac{3}{2}-m}I_{m-\frac{3}{2}}(t)\quad\textup{ and }\quad h_{2}(t):=t^{\frac{3}{2}-m}K_{m-\frac{3}{2}}(t)\,,

where
I_{m}
and
K_{m}
denote respectively the modified Bessel functions of the first and second kind and of order
m
.
These solutions have been chosen so that
h_{1}(t)
tends to a nonzero constant at 0 and grows exponentially fast for
t\gg 1
, while
h_{2}(t)
diverges like
t^{3-2m}
at 0 but tends to zero exponentially fast at infinity. More precisely, for each
\nu\geqslant 0
one has

|h_{1}^{(\nu)}(t)|\lesssim\widetilde{h}_{1}(t)\,,\qquad|h_{2}^{(\nu)}(t)|\lesssim\widetilde{h}_{2,\nu}(t)

with

(7.11)

\widetilde{h}_{1}(t):=\mathbbm{1}_{(0,1)}(t)+t^{1-m}e^{t-1}\mathbbm{1}_{(1,\infty)}(t)\,,\qquad\widetilde{h}_{2,\nu}(t):=t^{3-2m-\nu}\mathbbm{1}_{(0,1)}(t)+t^{1-m}e^{1-t}\mathbbm{1}_{(1,\infty)}(t)\,.

Furthermore,
h_{1}(t)
and
t^{2m-3}h_{2}(t)
(respectively,
t^{m-1}e^{-t}h_{1}(t)
and
t^{m-1}e^{t}h_{2}(t)
) are smooth functions of
t\in[0,\infty)
(respectively, of
t^{-1}\in[0,\infty)
) which do not vanish at the closed endpoint.

From the variation of parameters formula and the asymptotic behavior of
h_{j}
, we infer that the functions
u_{k},u_{W}
admit the representation formulas

(7.12)

\displaystyle u_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=-\alpha^{-k-1}f^{(k)}(0)\bigg(h_{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m+k-3}h_{1}(s)\widetilde{\chi}\Big(\Big(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big)s\Big)\,ds

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+h_{1}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m+k-3}h_{2}(s)\widetilde{\chi}\Big(\Big(1+\frac{1}{\alpha}\Big)s\Big)\,ds\bigg)\,,

(7.13)

\displaystyle u_{W}(t)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\alpha}\left(h_{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)W_{f}(s)\,ds+h_{1}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)W_{f}(s)\,ds\right)\,.

Let us start proving (i.a) and (ii.a) using this representation formula. We start with
u_{k}
, which only depends on
f
in an extremely simple way. Indeed,

(7.14)

u_{k}(t)=-\alpha^{-k-1}f^{(k)}(0)\,U_{k}(t)\,,

where the smooth function
U_{k}
is independent of
f
. Using the bound
|h_{j}|\leqslant\widetilde{h}_{j}
and the fact that
\widetilde{\chi}(r)=0
for all
r\geqslant 1
in the above representation formula, one easily sees that

(7.15)

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t^{2})(U_{k}^{(p)}(t))^{2}dt\lesssim 1

for any nonnegative integer
p
.
Thus, we arrive at the
L^{2}
-estimate

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\|u_{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}=\frac{|f^{(k)}(0)|^{2}}{\alpha^{2k+3}}\|U_{k}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}\leqslant\frac{|f^{(k)}(0)|^{2}}{\alpha^{2k+3}}\|U_{k}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\lesssim\frac{|f^{(k)}(0)|^{2}}{\alpha^{2k+3}}\,,

and and the
H^{\ell+1}
-estimate

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{k}^{(\ell+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2(\ell+1)-1}\|u_{k}^{(\ell+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2(\ell-k)-1}|f^{(k)}(0)|^{2}\|U_{k}^{(\ell+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2(\ell-k)-1}|f^{(k)}(0)|^{2}\,.

Similarly, we infer that that

\displaystyle\|z\varphi_{k}^{(\ell+2)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\alpha^{2(\ell+1)-1}\|zu_{k}^{(\ell+2)}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}\lesssim\alpha^{2(\ell-k)-1}|f^{(k)}(0)|^{2}\,.

Combining these three estimates we get (i.a) and (ii.a).

The proofs of (iii.a) and (iv.a) for
\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}
are similar. Indeed, setting

\widetilde{u}_{k}(t):=\widetilde{\varphi}_{k}(z(t))

and arguing as above, we see that

\widetilde{u}_{k}(t)=-\alpha^{-k-2}g^{(k)}(0)\,\widetilde{U}_{k}(t)\,,

with

\int_{0}^{\infty}(1+t^{2})(\widetilde{U}_{k}^{(p)}(t))^{2}dt\lesssim 1

for each integer
p\geqslant 0
. Hence (iii.a) and (iv.a) follows using the same reasoning.

We now deal with the regularity of
\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}
. We start by proving (i.b) and (ii.b). The starting point is a formula for the
p
-th derivative of
u_{W}
. Thanks to the representation formula (
7.13
), fairly direct computations yield the following pointwise estimate:

Lemma 7.4
.

For each integer
p\geqslant 0
, there exists a constant
C>0
independent of
\alpha
such that

(7.16)

\displaystyle\alpha\,|u_{W}^{(p)}(t)|\leqslant\left|\,h_{2}^{(p)}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\,\right|

\displaystyle+\left|\,h_{1}^{(p)}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\,\right|+C\,p(p-1)\sum_{\nu=0}^{p-2}\left(\frac{1}{t^{\,p-1-\nu}}+\frac{1}{t}\right)|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|\,.

Proof.

For
p=0,1
, the result is immediate consequence of (
7.13
). Note that the factor
p(p-1)
accounts for the fact that the third term in the right hand side of (
7.16
) does not appear in these estimates.

Differentiating (
7.13
) twice, one obtains the formula

\displaystyle-\alpha u_{W}^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=h_{2}^{\prime\prime}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)W_{f}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle\quad\,+h_{1}^{\prime\prime}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)W_{f}(s)\,ds+(h_{2}^{\prime}h_{1}-h_{1}^{\prime}h_{2})t^{2m-3}W_{f}(t)

\displaystyle=h_{2}^{\prime\prime}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)W_{f}(s)\,ds+h_{1}^{\prime\prime}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)W_{f}(s)\,ds-\frac{W_{f}(t)}{t}\,.

To pass to the last line, we have used that the Wronskian of the two solutions is
h_{2}^{\prime}(t)h_{1}(t)-h_{1}^{\prime}(t)h_{2}(t)=-t^{2-2m}
. This yields the formula in the statement in the case
p=2
.

For
p\geqslant 2
, the result follows by differentiating this formula and noting that, for any nonnegative integers
\mu,\nu
, the following pointwise estimate holds

t^{2m-3}\left|h_{1}^{(\mu)}(t)h_{2}^{(\nu)}(t)-h_{1}^{(\nu)}(t)h_{2}^{(\mu)}(t)\right|\lesssim\begin{cases}t^{-\mu-\nu}&\text{if }t<1\,,\\
t^{-1}&\text{if }t>1\,.\end{cases}

by the asymptotic formulas for
h_{j}
that we established above. Of course, the commutator is zero if
\mu=\nu
.
∎

The
L^{2}
and
H^{\ell+2}
estimates for
\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}
follow from (
7.16
) using suitable Hardy-type estimates. For conciseness, we shall only present the proof of the
H^{\ell+2}
-estimate in detail, as the simpler
L^{2}
-estimate

\|\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}+\|z\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{\prime}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}\lesssim\frac{1}{\alpha}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}\,,

follows using the same argument.

Let us start with an estimate for
\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{(\ell+1)}
. Combining (
7.13
) with (
7.16
), we get

\displaystyle\|\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{(\ell+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\alpha^{2(\ell+1)-1}\|u_{W}^{(\ell+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\alpha^{2\ell-1}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left|h_{2}^{(\ell+1)}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left|h_{1}^{(\ell+1)}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\,\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\ell(\ell+1)\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell-1}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2(\,\ell-\nu)}}dt+\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2}}dt\bigg)\bigg]\,.

Now let us estimate each term of the right hand side separately. First, observe that

\alpha^{2\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2(\,\ell-\nu)}}dt=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}(z)|^{2}}{z^{2(\ell-\nu)}}dz\,.

Moreover, observe that
{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(p)}(0)=0
for all
0\leqslant p\leqslant\ell-1
. Thus, by Theorem
4.3
applied with
\omega_{-}(z):=z^{-2(\ell-\nu)}
and
\omega_{+}(z):=1
, it follows that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}(z)|^{2}}{z^{2(\ell-\nu)}}dz\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\ell)}(z)|^{2}dz\,,\quad\textup{ for all }0\leqslant\nu\leqslant\ell-1\,.

Hence, we have that

(7.17)

\ell(\ell+1)\,\alpha^{2\ell-1}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2(\,\ell-\nu)}}dt\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\ell)}(z)|^{2}dz\,.

Likewise, if follows that

\alpha^{2\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2}}=\alpha^{2(\ell-\nu-1)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}(z)|^{2}}{z^{2}}dz\,.

Moreover, by Theorem
4.3
applied with
\omega_{-}(z):=z^{-2}
and
\omega_{+}(z):=1
(i.e. by the classical Hardy inequality), we get that

\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}(z)|^{2}}{z^{2}}dz\lesssim\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu+1)}(z)|^{2}dz\,.

Thus, we infer that

(7.18)

\ell(\ell+1)\,\alpha^{2\ell-1}\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2}}dt\lesssim\sum_{\nu=1}^{\ell}\alpha^{2(\ell-\nu)}\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}(z)|^{2}dz

Next, to estimate the first term, we apply Theorem
7.2
with
\omega_{-}(t):=(\widetilde{h}_{2,\ell+1}(t))^{2}
and
\omega_{+}(t):=t^{6-4m}\widetilde{h}_{1}(t)^{-2}(1+t^{-2\ell})
, where we recall that
\widetilde{h}_{1}
and
\widetilde{h}_{2,\ell+1}
were introduced in (
7.11
). Indeed, taking into account the asymptotics

\displaystyle\omega_{-}(t)\sim t^{6-4m-2(\ell+1)}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\omega_{+}(t)\sim t^{-2\ell+6-4m}\,,

\displaystyle\textup{as }t\to 0^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{-}(t)\sim t^{2-2m}e^{-2t}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\omega_{+}(t)\sim t^{4-2m}e^{-2t}\,,

\displaystyle\textup{as }t\to\infty\,,

one can check that

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{+}(\tau)^{-1}d\tau\right)^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{-}(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\omega_{+}(s)^{-1}ds\right)^{2}d\tau\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim 1\,,\quad\textup{as }t\to 0^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{+}(\tau)^{-1}d\tau\right)^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{-}(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\omega_{+}(s)^{-1}ds\right)^{2}d\tau\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,,\quad\textup{as }t\to\infty\,.

Note that in the last estimate we are using that, for
\tau\gg 1
,

\int_{1}^{\tau}s^{\alpha}e^{\beta s}ds\sim\tau^{\alpha}e^{\beta\tau}\,,\quad\textup{ for all }\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\,.

Thus, we infer that (
7.7
) holds, and so that

(7.19)

\displaystyle\alpha^{2\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\left|h_{2}^{(\ell+1)}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha^{2\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{h_{1}(t)}{\widetilde{h}_{1}(t)}\right)^{2}\left(\frac{|W_{f}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2\ell}}+|W_{f}(t)|^{2}\right)dt

\displaystyle\lesssim\alpha^{2\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left(\frac{|W_{f}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2\ell}}+|W_{f}(t)|^{2}\right)dt=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}(z)|^{2}}{z^{2\ell}}dz+\alpha^{2\ell}\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}(z)|^{2}dz\,.

Finally, applying now the dual Hardy inequality in Remark
7.3
with
\omega_{-}(t):=t^{4m-6}\,\widetilde{h}_{2,0}(t)^{2}
and
\omega_{+}(t):=(\widetilde{h}_{1}(t))^{-2}
we estimate the second term. Observe that we now have the asymptotics

\displaystyle\omega_{-}(t)\sim 1\,,\quad

\displaystyle\omega_{+}(t)\sim 1\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\textup{as }t\to 0^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\omega_{-}(t)\sim t^{2m-4}e^{-2t}\,,\quad

\displaystyle\omega_{+}(t)\sim t^{2m-2}e^{-2t}\,,\qquad

\displaystyle\textup{as }t\to\infty\,,

and thus

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{+}(\tau)^{-1}d\tau\right)^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{-}(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\omega_{+}(s)^{-1}ds\right)^{2}d\tau\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim t^{2}\,,\quad\textup{as }t\to 0^{+}\,,

\displaystyle\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{+}(\tau)^{-1}d\tau\right)^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{t}\omega_{-}(\tau)\left(\int_{0}^{\tau}\omega_{+}(s)^{-1}ds\right)^{2}d\tau\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{t^{2}}\,,\quad\textup{as }t\to\infty\,.

Hence, we get that

(7.20)

\alpha^{2\ell-1}\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left|h_{1}^{(\ell+1)}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)W_{f}(s)ds\right|^{2}dt\lesssim\alpha^{2\ell}\int_{0}^{1}|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}(z)|^{2}dz\,.

Combining (
7.17
)–(
7.20
), we thus find

(7.21)

\|\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{(\ell+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}\lesssim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell}\alpha^{\ell-\nu}\,\|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}\,.

Let us now estimate
z\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{(\ell+2)}
. By (
7.16
),

\displaystyle\|z\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{(\ell+2)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\alpha^{2(\ell+1)-1}\|tu_{W}^{(\ell+2)}\|_{L^{2}((0,\alpha))}^{2}

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\alpha^{2\ell-1}\bigg[\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left|th_{2}^{(\ell+2)}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-3}h_{1}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+\int_{0}^{\alpha}\left|th_{1}^{(\ell+2)}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-3}h_{2}(s)\,W_{f}(s)\,ds\,\right|^{2}dt

\displaystyle\hskip 56.9055pt+(\ell+1)(\ell+2)\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell}\bigg(\int_{0}^{\alpha}\frac{|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}}{t^{2(\,\ell-\nu)}}dt+\int_{0}^{\alpha}|W_{f}^{(\nu)}(t)|^{2}dt\bigg)\bigg]\,.

Hence, we can argue exactly as in the proof of (
7.21
) to estimate each term of the right hand side, thereby showing that

(7.22)

\|z\varphi_{\mathcal{R}}^{(\ell+2)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}\lesssim\sum_{\nu=0}^{\ell}\alpha^{\ell-\nu}\,\|{\mathcal{R}}_{f}^{(\nu)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}\,.

Combining (
7.21
) and (

) we obtain (ii.b).

To prove the estimates (iii.b) and (iv.b), one argues just as in the case of (i.b) and (ii.b), discussed above in detail, after replacing
f
by

. In fact, writing

\widetilde{u}_{W}(t):=\widetilde{\varphi}_{\mathcal{R}}(z(t))\,,\qquad W_{g}(t):={\mathcal{R}}_{g}(z(t))\,,

and using that

, one can write a representation formula for

:

(7.23)

\widetilde{u}_{W}(t)=-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\left(h_{2}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-2}h_{1}(s)W_{g}(s)\,ds+h_{1}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-2}h_{2}(s)W_{g}(s)\,ds\right)\,.

Lemma
7.4
thus yields the pointwise bound

\displaystyle\alpha^{2}

\displaystyle|\widetilde{u}_{W}^{(p)}(t)|\leqslant\left|\,h_{2}^{(p)}(t)\int_{0}^{t}s^{2m-2}h_{1}(s)\,W_{g}(s)\,ds\,\right|+\left|\,h_{1}^{(p)}(t)\int_{t}^{\infty}s^{2m-2}h_{2}(s)\,W_{g}(s)\,ds\,\right|

\displaystyle+C\,p(p-1)\bigg(\sum_{\nu=0}^{p-2}\Big(\frac{1}{t^{p-2-\nu}}+1\Big)|W_{g}^{(\nu)}(t)|+\sum_{\nu=0}^{p-3}\frac{1}{t}\,|W_{g}^{(\nu)}(t)|+\sum_{\nu=1}^{p-2}\sum_{\mu=0}^{\nu-1}\frac{1}{t^{p-1-\nu}}|W_{g}^{(\mu)}(t)|\bigg)\,.

so one can argue essentially as in the proof of (i.b) and (ii.b) to obtain the bounds (iii.b) and (iv.b).

7.2.
PDE estimates

We shall next show how we can use Lemma
7.1
to derive the key regularity estimates for the only solution
v\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})
to the equation

(7.24)

{\mathcal{L}}v=G\,,

which we stated as Lemma
6.6
in the previos section. In fact, in the following lemma we state a somewhat more detailed set of a priori estimates; Lemma
6.6
stems from them simply by keeping track of the factors of
\varepsilon
in the definition of the norms (Equation (
6.11
)).

Equivalently, in terms of the Fourier components

(7.25)

v_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}v(z,\theta)\,e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta\,,\qquad G_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(z,\theta)\,e^{-in\theta}\,d\theta\,,

one has the system of ODEs

(7.26)

{\mathcal{L}}_{n}v_{n}=G_{n}\qquad\text{in }(0,1)\,,

with

{\mathcal{L}}_{n}u:=z\partial_{z}^{2}u-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}n^{2}}{a_{-}^{2}}z\,u+(2m-2)\partial_{z}u\,.

Lemma 7.5
.

For any integer
j\geqslant 1
, the unique solution
v\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})
to (
6.10
) is bounded as

(7.27)

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{j-1}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{j-j_{2}}\Big(\|\partial_{z}^{j_{1}}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}^{j_{1}+1}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\Big)\lesssim\|G\|_{H^{j-1}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\,,

(7.28)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\|\partial_{\theta}^{j-1}G\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\,.

Additionally, when
G(z)=\varepsilon zH(z)
, and
j\geqslant 2
, one has

(7.29)

\displaystyle\sum_{j_{2}=0}^{j-1}\sum_{j_{1}=0}^{j-j_{2}}\Big(\|\partial_{z}^{j_{1}}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}^{j_{1}+1}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\Big)

\displaystyle\hskip 136.5733pt\lesssim\|H\|_{H^{j-2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}H\|_{H^{j-2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\,,

(7.30)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\,\|\partial_{\theta}^{j-2}H\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\,.

The implicit constants are independent of
\varepsilon
, and the dependence on
a_{-}
is uniform in compact subsets of
[1,+\infty)

Proof.

Let us divide the proof into two steps for the sake of clarity.

Step 1: Reduction to ODE estimates.
Taking the Fourier transform in the
\theta

G\in H^{j-1}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})
as

G(z,\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}G_{n}(z)e^{in\theta}\,,\qquad G_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}G(z,\theta)e^{-in\theta}d\theta\,.

The unique solution
v\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})
to (
7.24
) is therefore

(7.31)

v(z,\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}v_{n}(z)e^{in\theta}\,,

where each Fourier component is the only solution
v_{n}\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1))
to the ODE

(7.32)

{\mathcal{L}}_{n}v_{n}=G_{n}\qquad\textup{in }(0,1)\,,

where

{\mathcal{L}}_{n}u:=z\partial_{z}^{2}u+(2m-2)\partial_{z}u-\alpha_{n}^{2}z\,u\,.

Note that this is the ordinary differential operator (
7.1
) that we studied in Subsection
7.1
, and that the parameter

\alpha_{n}:=\frac{\varepsilon|n|}{a_{-}}\in[0,\infty)\,,

now depends on the Fourier frequency
n
and on the scale
\varepsilon
.

Given an integer
j_{1}\geqslant 1
, for each Fourier mode we argue as in Subsection
7.1
, decomposing
G_{n}

G_{n}(z)=:\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}G_{n}^{(k)}(0)\widetilde{\chi}((1+\alpha_{n})z)+{\mathcal{R}}_{G_{n}}(z)\,.

Thus the function
v_{n}\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1))

(7.33)

v_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}\,v_{k,n}+v_{{\mathcal{R}},n}\,,

where
v_{k,n},v_{{\mathcal{R}},n}\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1))
are the only solutions to the ODEs

(7.34)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{n}v_{k,n}

\displaystyle=z^{k}G_{n}^{(k)}(0)\widetilde{\chi}((1+\alpha_{n})z)\,,

(7.35)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{n}v_{{\mathcal{R}},n}

\displaystyle={\mathcal{R}}_{G_{n}}

in
(0,1)
. Note that the index
k
will eventually range from 0 to
j_{1}-2
.

The case where
G(z)=\varepsilon zH(z)
with
H,z\partial_{z}H\in H^{j-2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})
is handled the same way. We write
H

H(z,\theta)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}H_{n}(z)e^{in\theta}\,,\qquad H_{n}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}H(z,\theta)e^{-in\theta}d\theta\,,

and note that the Fourier coefficients in (
7.31
) can be similarly written as (
7.33
), where now
\widetilde{v}_{k,n},\widetilde{v}_{{\mathcal{R}},n}\in z^{1-m}H_{0}^{1}((0,1))
are the only solutions to

(7.36)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\widetilde{v}_{k,n}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon z^{k+1}H_{n}^{(k)}(0)\widetilde{\chi}((1+\alpha_{n})z)\,,

(7.37)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{n}\widetilde{v}_{{\mathcal{R}},n}

\displaystyle=\varepsilon z{\mathcal{R}}_{H_{n}}\,.

Here,
{\mathcal{R}}_{H_{n}}
is defined as

H_{n}(z)=:\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{z^{k}}{k!}H_{n}^{(k)}(0)\widetilde{\chi}((1+\alpha_{n})z)+{\mathcal{R}}_{H_{n}}(z)\,,

and

v_{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}\widetilde{v}_{k,n}+\widetilde{v}_{{\mathcal{R}},n}\,.

Step 2: Sum over Fourier modes.

Lemma
7.5
now follows from Lemma
7.1
(with
\alpha_{n}:=\varepsilon|n|/a_{-}
) using the Parseval identity.

First of all, observe that Lemma
7.1
(i.b) applied with
\ell:=0
implies that

(7.38)

\displaystyle\|v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\Big(\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}v_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)\lesssim 2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\|G_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\|G\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,,

and that, for all
j\geqslant 1
,

\displaystyle\|\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j}\Big(\|v_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}v_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{2\pi}{\varepsilon^{2}}\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2(j-1)}\|G_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\|\partial_{\theta}^{j-1}G\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,.

Likewise, Lemma
7.1
(iii.b) applied with
\ell:=0
implies that, for all
j\geqslant 2
,

\|\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}\partial_{\theta}^{j}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\lesssim\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}\,\|\partial_{\theta}^{j-2}H\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,.

Once we have proved (
7.27
) and (
7.29
), we focus on the more involved estimates (
7.28
) and (
7.30
). Applying Lemma
7.1
(ii.b) with
\ell:=0
, we obtain that, for all nonnegative integer
j_{2}
with
j_{2}\leqslant j-1

(7.39)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}^{2}\partial_{z}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\Big(\|\partial_{z}v_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}^{2}v_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim 2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\|G_{n}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}=\|\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}G\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,.

Likewise, Lemma
7.1
(iv.b) applied
\ell:=0
implies that, for all nonnegative integer
j_{2}
with
j_{2}\leqslant j-1
, it follows that

(7.40)

\|\partial_{z}^{2}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}^{3}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\lesssim\|\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}H\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}H\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,.

We finally deal with the case where
j_{1}
and
j_{2}
are nonnegative integers with
j_{1}\geqslant 2
and
0\leqslant j_{2}\leqslant j-j_{1}
. By direct computations, we get

(7.41)

\displaystyle\|\partial_{z}^{j_{1}}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}+\|z\partial_{z}^{j_{1}+1}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\Big(\|v_{n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|zv_{n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\lesssim\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}\Big(\|v_{k,n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|zv_{k,n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+\|v_{{\mathcal{R}},n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|zv_{{\mathcal{R}},n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\bigg)\,.

On one hand, by Lemma
7.1
(ii.a) applied with
\ell:=j_{1}-1
, we infer that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}\Big(\|v_{k,n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|zv_{k,n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}(1+n^{2})^{j_{1}-1+j_{2}-k-\frac{1}{2}}|G_{n}^{(k)}(0)|^{2}\lesssim\|\partial_{z}^{k}G(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{j_{1}+j_{2}-k-\frac{3}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,.

On the other hand, by Lemma
7.1
(ii.b) applied with
\ell:=j_{1}-1

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\big(\|v_{{\mathcal{R}},n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|zv_{{\mathcal{R}},n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\big)\lesssim\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\sum_{\nu=0}^{j_{1}-1}\bigg((1+n^{2})^{j_{1}-1-\nu}\|{\mathcal{R}}_{G,n}^{(\nu)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\bigg)

Thus, it follows that

(7.42)

\|\partial_{z}^{j_{1}}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}^{j_{1}+1}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\|G\|_{H^{j-1}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\,.

The bound (
7.27
) then follows from (

), (
7.39
) and (
7.42
).

Let us now assume that
j_{1}\geqslant 3
. On one hand, by Lemma
7.1
(iv.a) applied with
\ell:=j_{1}-2
, we get that

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\bigg(\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}\Big(\|\widetilde{v}_{k,n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|z\widetilde{v}_{k,n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\lesssim\sum_{k=0}^{j_{1}-2}\frac{1}{k!}(1+n^{2})^{j_{1}-2+j_{2}-k-\frac{1}{2}}|H_{n}^{(k)}(0)|^{2}\lesssim\|\partial_{z}^{k}H(0,\cdot)\|_{H^{j_{1}+j_{2}-k-\frac{5}{2}}(\mathbb{T})}^{2}\,.

On the other hand, by Lemma
7.1
(iv.b) applied with
\ell:=j_{1}-2
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\Big(\|\widetilde{v}_{{\mathcal{R}},n}^{(j_{1})}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|z\widetilde{v}_{{\mathcal{R}},n}^{(j_{1}+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad\lesssim\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}n^{2j_{2}}\sum_{\nu=0}^{j_{1}-2}\bigg((1+n^{2})^{j_{1}-2-\nu}\Big(\|{\mathcal{R}}_{H,n}^{(\nu)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}+\|z{\mathcal{R}}_{H,n}^{(\nu+1)}\|_{L^{2}((0,1))}^{2}\Big)\bigg)\,.

Thus, substituting into (
7.41
) with
v_{k,n}
replaced by
\widetilde{v}_{k,n}
, and
v_{{\mathcal{R}},n}
replaced by
\widetilde{v}_{{\mathcal{R}},n}
, we conclude that

(7.43)

\|\partial_{z}^{j_{1}}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}v\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}^{j_{1}+1}\partial_{\theta}^{j_{2}}\|_{L^{2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\|H\|_{H^{j-2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}+\|z\partial_{z}H\|_{H^{j-2}((0,1)\times\mathbb{T})}\,.

Combining (

), (

) and (

). The lemma then follows.
∎

8.
Spectral properties of
\mathfrak{L}_{a}

In this section we study the spectral properties of the operator
\mathfrak{L}_{a}
, depending on the parameters
a
and
\ell
. Specifically, we prove Propositions
2.10
and
2.11
. These results show a phenomenon of crossing of eigenvalues, which is essential for our bifurcation result.

The proof of Proposition
2.10
will follow from several lemmas. Let us recall the positive symmetric bilinear form
{\mathcal{B}}:H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{a})\times H_{0}^{1}(\Omega_{a})\to\mathbb{R}
associated to the linear operator
\mathfrak{L}_{a}
, namely

\displaystyle{\mathcal{B}}(v,w)

\displaystyle:=-\int_{\Omega}\big(L_{a}^{0,0}v+f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})v\big)w\,(R+a-4)\,dR\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\left[\partial_{R}v\,\partial_{R}w+\frac{\partial_{\theta}v\,\partial_{\theta}w}{(R+a-4)^{2}}-f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})vw\right](R+a-4)\,dR\,d\theta\,.

Also, we recall that there exists a sequence of eigenvalues of
-\mathfrak{L}_{a}
with finite multiplicity, which we denote by
\lambda_{k}\equiv\lambda_{k}(a)
, such that

\lambda_{1}<\lambda_{2}\leqslant\lambda_{3}\leqslant\cdots

Of course,
\lambda_{k}(a)
tends to infinity as
k\to\infty
, and depends continuously on
a
.

As the potential is radial, the radial eigenfunctions of this operator, which define an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}((1,7),(R+a-4)\,dR)
, can be equivalently obtained from the symmetric bilinear form
{\mathcal{B}}^{\rm rad}:H_{0}^{1}(1,7))\times H_{0}^{1}(1,7))\to\mathbb{R}
given by

{\mathcal{B}}^{\rm rad}(v,w):=\int_{1}^{7}\left[\partial_{R}v\,\partial_{R}w-f_{a}^{\prime}(\Phi_{a,1},\widetilde{\psi}_{a})vw\right](R+a-4)\,dR\,.

The corresponding quadratic form is
{\mathcal{Q}}^{\rm rad}(v):={\mathcal{B}}^{\rm rad}(v,v)
.

We denote the eigenvalues corresponding to the radial eigenfunctions by

\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad}(a)<\lambda_{2}^{\rm rad}(a)<\cdots<\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}(a)<\cdots

When there is no risk of confusion, we simply write
\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}\equiv\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}(a)
. It is standard that
\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}(a)\to\infty
as
k\to\infty
, and that
\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}(a)\neq\lambda_{j}^{\rm rad}(a)
whenever
k\neq j
. To see this, note that, the eigenvalue equation for a radial eigenfunction
\phi
of
\mathfrak{L}_{a}
(which, by Proposition
6.9
, is in
\rho^{m-1}{\mathbf{X}}^{j}
) reduces to the ODE. The classical argument showing that radial Dirichlet eigenvalues have multiplicity 1 then follows from Frobenius’ theory for ODEs.

We start the proof of Proposition
2.10
with the following lemma:

Lemma 8.1
.

For all
a\geqslant 4
,
\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad}(a)<\lambda_{2}^{\rm rad}(a)<0
.

Proof.

By composing with the diffeomorphism
\Phi_{a}
, it suffices to show that the quadratic form

(8.1)

\tilde{{\mathcal{Q}}}(v):=\int_{a_{-}}^{a^{+}}\left[(\partial_{r}v)^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(r,\psi_{a})v^{2}\right]r\,dr\,,

is negative definite on a two dimensional subspace of
H_{0}^{1}((a_{-},a_{+}))
. Recall that
\psi_{a}
solves the equation

\partial_{r}^{2}\psi_{a}+\frac{\partial_{r}\psi_{a}}{r}+f_{a}(r,\psi_{a}(r))=0\quad\textup{ in }(a_{-},a_{+})\,.

Moreover, taking the derivative with respect to
r
, we get that
\xi(r):=\partial_{r}\psi_{a}(r)
solves

\partial_{r}^{2}\xi+\frac{\partial_{r}\xi}{r}-\frac{\xi}{r^{2}}+f_{a}^{\prime}(r,\psi_{a}(r))\xi=0\quad\textup{ in }(a_{-},a_{+})\,.

It is worth stressing that, in the above expression, we are using that
\partial_{r}f_{a}(r,\psi_{a}(r))\equiv 0
in
(a_{-},a_{+})
.

Next, we recall that
m_{a}
is the unique maximum of the function
\psi_{a}
, and define

\xi_{1}(r):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{r}\psi_{a}(r)\,,\ &&r\in[a_{-},m_{a})\,,\\
&0&&r\in[m_{a},a_{+})\,,\end{aligned}\right.\quad\textup{ and }\quad\xi_{2}(r):=\left\{\begin{aligned} &0\,,\ &&r\in[a_{-},m_{a}]\,,\\
&\partial_{r}\psi_{a}(r)&&r\in(m_{a},a_{+}]\,.\end{aligned}\right.

Clearly,
\xi_{1}
,
\xi_{2}\in H_{0}^{1}((a_{-},a_{+}))
and

\displaystyle\tilde{{\mathcal{Q}}}(\xi_{1})

\displaystyle:=\int_{a_{-}}^{m_{a}}\left[(\partial_{r}\xi_{1})^{2}\,-f_{a}^{\prime}(r,\psi_{a})\xi_{1}^{2}\right]r\,dr=\int_{a_{-}}^{m_{a}}\left(r(\partial_{r}\xi_{1})^{2}\,+r\xi_{1}\partial_{r}^{2}\xi_{1}+\xi_{1}\partial_{r}\xi_{1}-\frac{\xi_{1}^{2}}{r}\right)dr

\displaystyle=\int_{a_{-}}^{m_{a}}\left(\partial_{r}(r\xi_{1}\partial_{r}\xi_{1})-\frac{\xi_{1}^{2}}{r}\right)dr=-\int_{a_{-}}^{m_{a}}\frac{\xi_{1}^{2}}{r}\,dr<0\,.

Analogously, it follows that

\tilde{{\mathcal{Q}}}(\xi_{2})<0\,.

Since
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
have disjoint support, we conclude that

\tilde{{\mathcal{Q}}}(\xi)<0\,,
for all
\xi\in{\rm span}\{\xi_{1},\ \xi_{2}\}\setminus\{0\}\,.

This implies that
\lambda_{2}^{\rm rad}(a)<0
and concludes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We are now interested in the behavior of the eigenvalues
\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}
as
a\to+\infty
. On this purpose, let us recall (see Lemma
3.1
) that the limit function
\overline{\psi}
is a solution to the limit problem

\overline{\psi}^{\prime\prime}+\overline{f}(\overline{\psi})=0\quad\textup{ in }(1,7)\,.

We denote the bilinear and quadratic forms associated with the linearized operator, and defined on functions belonging to
H_{0}^{1}((1,7))
by

\overline{{\mathcal{B}}}(v,w):=\int_{1}^{7}\left(v^{\prime}(R)w^{\prime}(R)-\overline{f}^{\prime}(\overline{\psi}(R))v(R)w(R)\,\right)dR\,,\qquad\overline{{\mathcal{Q}}}(v):=\overline{{\mathcal{B}}}(v,v)\,.

Just as in the case of the radial eigenvalues of
\mathfrak{L}_{a}
, it is standard that there is an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}((1,7))
consisting of eigenfunctions, whose eigenvalues we label as

\overline{\lambda}_{1}<\overline{\lambda}_{2}<\cdots<\overline{\lambda}_{k}<\cdots

Furthemore, the eigenvalues tend to infinity and are simple:
\overline{\lambda}_{j}\neq\overline{\lambda}_{k}
for
j\neq k
.

We have the following important convergence result:

Lemma 8.2
.

As
a\to\infty
, the following limits hold true:

\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad}(a)\to\overline{\lambda}_{1}<0,\qquad\lambda_{2}^{\rm rad}(a)\to\overline{\lambda}_{2}=0,\qquad\lambda_{3}^{\rm rad}(a)\to\overline{\lambda}_{3}>0\,.

Proof.

We first observe that, for any
v\in H^{1}_{0}((1,7))
,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}^{\rm rad}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}v)^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))v^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR

\displaystyle=a\left[\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}v)^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))v^{2}\right]\,dR+o(1)\|v\|_{H^{1}(1,7)}^{2}\right].

Moreover, by Lemma
3.1
(iv), we have that

\displaystyle\left|\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}v)^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))v^{2}\right]\,dR-\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}v)^{2}-\overline{f}^{\prime}(\overline{\psi}(R))v^{2}\right]\,dR\,\right|

\displaystyle\quad=\bigg|\int_{1}^{7}\Big(-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))+\overline{f}^{\prime}(\psi_{a}(R+a-4))\Big)v^{2}\,dR

\displaystyle\qquad\ +\int_{1}^{7}\Big(-\overline{f}^{\prime}(\psi_{a}(R+a-4))+\overline{f}^{\prime}(\overline{\psi}(R))\Big)v^{2}\,dR\,\bigg|

\displaystyle\quad\leqslant\frac{m-1}{a}\int_{1}^{7}\frac{v^{2}}{\rho}\,dR+o(1)\,\|v\|_{H^{1}(1,7)}^{2}=o(1)\|v\|_{H^{1}((1,7))}^{2}\,,\quad\textup{ as }a\to+\infty\,,

and so that

{\mathcal{Q}}^{\rm rad}(v)=a\,\big[\,\overline{{\mathcal{Q}}}(v)+o(1)\|v\|_{H^{1}((1,7))}^{2}\,\big]\quad\textup{ as }a\to+\infty\,.

On the other hand, it is easy to see that

\int_{1}^{7}v(R)^{2}(R+a-4)\,dR=a\,[1+o(1)]\int_{1}^{7}v(R)^{2}\,dR\quad\textup{ as }a\to+\infty\,.

This implies the convergence of the corresponding Rayleigh quotients, in the sense that

\frac{{\mathcal{Q}}^{\rm rad}(v)}{\|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}((1,7),\,(R+a-4)\,dR)}}\,\xrightarrow{a\to+\infty}\,\frac{\overline{{\mathcal{Q}}}(v)}{\ \|v\|^{2}_{L^{2}((1,7))}}\,,\quad\mbox{ for any fixed radial function }v\,.

By the min-max characterization of the eigenvalues we infer that

\lambda_{k}^{\rm rad}\to\overline{\lambda}_{k}
, as
a\to+\infty
, for any fixed
k
. Thus, to conclude the proof we only need to verify that
\overline{\lambda}_{2}=0
. On that purpose, observe that
\overline{\psi}^{\prime}\in H_{0}^{1}((1,7))
is a solution of the linearized problem

\xi^{\prime\prime}+\overline{f}^{\prime}(\overline{\psi})\xi=0\,.

Hence,
\overline{\psi}^{\prime}
is an eigenfunction with associated eigenvalue
0
. Since
\overline{\psi}^{\prime}
changes sign exactly once, we conclude that
\overline{\lambda}_{2}=0
.
∎

As we can see in the previous result, the first radial eigenvalue is negative and remains bounded away from
0
as
a\to+\infty
. This is the reason for the appearance of nonradial degeneracies, as we shall see. Our analysis has to be precise enough to avoid interferences with the second radial eigenvalue, which is converging to
0
from below. In what follows we consider general eigenvalues with
\ell
-symmetry of the operator
-\mathfrak{L}_{a}
, as defined in (
2.26
) and below. In next lemma we fix the values of
a_{0}
and
\ell
which will be used throughout this section.

Lemma 8.3
.

Let
c:=\frac{1}{3}\min\{-\overline{\lambda}_{1},\overline{\lambda}_{3}\}
. According to Lemma
8.2
we take
a_{0}\geqslant 4
so that
|\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad}-\overline{\lambda}_{1}|<c
and
|\lambda_{3}^{\rm rad}-\overline{\lambda}_{3}|<c
.
Then, there exists
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a_{0})>0
.

Proof.

If
\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a_{0})=\lambda_{3}^{\rm rad}(a_{0})
then it is positive and we are done. If not, the corresponding eigenfunction
\phi_{3}
can be written in Fourier as

\phi_{3}(R,\theta)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\varphi_{k}(R)\cos(k\ell\theta)\,.

Hence, it suffices to show that for all functions of the form
v(R,\theta)=\varphi(R)\cos(k\ell\theta)
the quadratic form
{\mathcal{Q}}(v)
is strictly positive for
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
sufficiently large. It turns out that, for
v
as above,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}(v)

\displaystyle=\int_{1}^{7}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\left[(\partial_{R}v)^{2}+\frac{(\partial_{\theta}v)^{2}}{(R+a-4)^{2}}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))v^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR\,d\theta

\displaystyle=\pi\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}\varphi)^{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}k^{2}}{(R+a-4)^{2}}\varphi^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))\varphi^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR.

Thus, if we choose
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\frac{\ell^{2}}{(a_{0}-3)^{2}}+\overline{\lambda}_{1}-2c>0
, we conclude that, for
v
as above,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}(v)

\displaystyle=\pi\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}\varphi)^{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}k^{2}}{(R+a-4)^{2}}\varphi^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))\varphi^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR

\displaystyle\geqslant\pi\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}\varphi)^{2}+(c-\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad})\varphi^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))\varphi^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR

\displaystyle\geqslant\pi c\int_{1}^{7}\varphi^{2}\,(R+a-4)\,dR>0\,.

The lemma then follows.
∎

In the next lemma we show that
\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a)
becomes negative if
a>a_{0}
is large enough.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
a_{0}
,
\ell
and
c
as in Lemma
8.3
. Then, there exists
a_{2}>a_{0}
such that
\lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a_{2})<0
.

Proof.

Let
a_{2}>a_{0}
be such that
\frac{\ell^{2}}{(a_{2}-3)^{2}}+\bar{\lambda}_{1}+2c<0
, and let
\phi_{1}
be the eigenfunction associated to
\lambda_{1}(a_{2})=\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad}(a_{2})
. Then, we choose and fix the test function

v(R,\theta):=\phi_{1}(R)\cos(\ell\theta)\,.

By construction,
v
is
L^{2}
-orthogonal to the two radial eigenfunctions
\phi_{1}
,
\phi_{2}
associated to the eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad}(a_{2})
,
\lambda_{2}^{\rm rad}(a_{2})
. Hence, it suffices to show that
{\mathcal{Q}}(v)<0
. Arguing as in the proof of Lemma
8.3
, we then conclude that

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}(v)

\displaystyle=\pi\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}\phi_{1})^{2}+\frac{\ell^{2}}{(R+a-4)^{2}}\phi_{1}^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))\phi_{1}^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR

\displaystyle\leqslant\pi\int_{1}^{7}\left[(\partial_{R}\phi_{1})^{2}+(-c-\lambda_{1}^{\rm rad})\phi_{1}^{2}-f_{a}^{\prime}(R+a-4,\psi_{a}(R+a-4))\phi_{1}^{2}\right](R+a-4)\,dR

\displaystyle=-\pi c\int_{1}^{7}\phi_{1}^{2}\,(R+a-4)\,dR<0\,,

and the lemma follows.
∎

We can now prove Proposition
2.10
.

Proof of Proposition
2.10
.

We fix
a_{0}
,
c
,
\ell
and
a_{2}
as in the previous lemmas and take
\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that
\varepsilon_{0}<\min\{c,-\lambda^{\rm rad}_{2}(a)\}
for all
a\in[a_{0},a_{2}]
. This can be ensured thanks to Lemma
8.1
. Then, we set

A:=\big\{a\in\mathbb{R},\ a\geqslant a_{0}:\ \lambda_{3}^{\ell}(a)<0\,\big\}\,.

Clearly,
a_{2}\in A

Appendix A
Smooth compactly supported solutions given by

elliptic equations with autonomous nonlinearities are locally radial

Our objective in this short appendix is to show that one cannot obtain non-radial smooth stationary Euler flows with compact support using the usual formulation in terms of (autonomous) semilinear elliptic equations.

To see this, suppose that
v\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
is a classical solution of the stationary Euler equations (
1.1
) on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, and that there exists a region with
C^{1}
boundary
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that

(A.1)

v=\begin{cases}\nabla^{\perp}\bar{\psi}&\text{in }\Omega,\\
0&\text{in }\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\Omega.\end{cases}

Here
\bar{\psi}\in C^{2}(\overline{\Omega})
is a solution of a semilinear elliptic equation of the form

(A.2)

\Delta\psi+f(\psi)=0

in the domain
\Omega
, for some continuous function
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
. We assume that
\Omega
is bounded, and that
\partial\Omega
consists of
J\geqslant 1
connected components
\Gamma_{j}
.

The first observation is that one can equivalently assume that
v
is globally given by the perpendicular gradient of a solution of the semilinear equation (
A.2
):

Proposition A.1
.

The vector field (
A.1
) can be equivalently written as
v=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
, where
\psi\in C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
satisfies Equation (
A.2
) in all of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
.

Proof.

Since
v\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, it follows that
\nabla\bar{\psi}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
and
\nabla^{2}\bar{\psi}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, which in particular implies that
\Delta\bar{\psi}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
, and that there exist constants
c_{j}
such that
\bar{\psi}|_{\Gamma_{j}}=c_{j}
. Here,
\{\Gamma_{j}\}_{j=1}^{J}
denote the connected components of
\partial\Omega
. By Equation (
A.2
) and the continuity of
f
, one must then have
f(c_{j})=0
for all
1\leqslant j\leqslant J
. Let us then denote by
\Omega_{1},\ldots,\Omega_{J}
the different connected components of
\mathbb{R}^{2}\setminus\overline{\Omega}
, relabeling the boundary components if necessary so that
\partial\Omega_{j}=\Gamma_{j}
, and define the
C^{2}
function

\psi:=\begin{cases}\bar{\psi}&\text{in }\Omega\,,\\
c_{j}&\text{in }\Omega_{j}\text{ for each }1\leqslant j\leqslant J\,.\end{cases}

The functions
\Delta\psi
and
f(\psi)
are continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, identically zero in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\backslash\overline{\Omega}
, and coincide with
\Delta\bar{\psi}
and
f(\bar{\psi})
, respectively, in
\Omega
, where they satisfy (
A.2
) by hypothesis. This immediately implies that
\psi
is a solution to (
A.2
) in the whole of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
as claimed.
∎

Theorem A.2
.

Let
\psi\in{C^{3}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}
satisfy a semilinear equation of the form (
A.2
) in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
with
f\in C(\mathbb{R})
. If
\nabla\psi
is compactly supported, then
\psi
is locally radial. In other words, define:

D=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ \nabla\psi(x)\neq 0\}.

Then,

D=\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\,,

where
I
is a countable set and where
\{A_{i}\}_{i\in I}
are disjoint annuli or disks. Moreover,
\psi
is radially symmetric in each set
A_{i}
.

Proof.

Since
\nabla\psi
has compact support, we have that
\psi
is constant outside a bounded set. By adding a constant if necessary, we can assume that
\psi
itself has compact support. Let us also point out that
f(0)=0
. We now consider separately two cases.

Case 1:
\psi
does not change sign.
The case where
\psi\geqslant 0
immediately follows from
[
1
]
. Obviously so does the case
\psi\leqslant 0
, simply by considering the function
-\psi
.

Case 2:
\psi
changes sign.
We start by proving that
\nabla\psi=0
on the zero set
\psi^{-1}(0)
. For this, we argue by contradiction, and assume that
\psi(p)=0
and
\nabla\psi(p)\neq 0
for some
p\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then, we follow the ideas of
[
18
,
19
]
: for some
\delta>0
, let
\sigma:(-\delta,\delta)\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
be the solution to the ODE

\left\{\begin{aligned} \sigma^{\prime}(t)&=\nabla\psi(\sigma(t))\,,\quad t\in(-\delta,\delta)\,,\\
\sigma(0)&=p\,.\end{aligned}\right.

As
\nabla\psi(p)\neq 0
, we have that
g^{\prime}(0)>0
, where
g=\psi\circ\sigma
. By taking
\delta>0
smaller if necessary and suitable
\varepsilon_{i}>0
, we have that
g:(-\delta,\delta)\to(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})
is a diffeomorphism. In this way, we can write
f
as

f|_{(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})}=-\Delta\psi\circ\sigma\circ g^{-1}.

Since
\psi
is
C^{3}
in
\Omega
, then
f|_{(-\varepsilon_{1},\varepsilon_{2})}
is a
C^{1}
function. But this ensures that the problem
\Delta\psi+f(\psi)=0
has a unique continuation property on the zero level set, which implies that
\psi\equiv 0
, which is a contradiction. Hence, we conclude that
\nabla\psi(x)=0
at every point satisfying
\psi(x)=0
.

Next, let us define

\psi_{+}(x):=\max\{\psi(x),0\}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &\psi(x)&&\text{if }x\in\Omega_{+}\,,\\
&0&&\text{if }x\notin\Omega_{+}\,,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\Omega_{+}:=\{x\in\Omega:\ \psi(x)>0\}
. Clearly,
\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega_{+}
, so it follows that
\nabla\psi=0
on
\partial\Omega_{+}
. This implies that
\psi_{+}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
, and then it follows that it is a classical nonnegative solution to (
A.1
). As in
Case 1
, we then conclude that
\psi_{+}
is locally radial.

The same argument can be applied to
\psi_{-}(x):=\max\{-\psi(x),0\}
, so the theorem follows.
∎
