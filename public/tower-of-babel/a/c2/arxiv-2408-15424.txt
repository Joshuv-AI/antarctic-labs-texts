Abstract
Semiclassical methods are essential in analyzing quantum mechanical systems. Although they generally produce approximate results, relatively rare potentials exist for which these methods are exact. Such intriguing potentials serve as crucial test cases for semiclassical approximations. Recent research has demonstrated a deep connection between supersymmetric quantum mechanics and the exactness of semiclassical methods. Specifically, the mathematical form of conventional shape-invariant potentials guarantees exactness in several related situations. In this manuscript, we review these recent results and discuss their significance. Keywords: SUSYQM; WKB; SWKB; Semiclassical Methods.

2.1
Semiclassical WKB, SWKB, and BSWKB Approximations

We consider a quantum system to be exactly solvable when its eigenvalues can be analytically expressed in terms of the system’s parameters
[
15
,
25
]
.
Since this is not true of most potentials, solving the Schrödinger equation often requires approximation methods. While perturbation theory is typically employed for systems that are “close” to being exactly solvable, the WKB approximation offers a non-perturbative approach to approximating eigenenergies and wavefunctions by assuming a potential
V(x)
with slow spatial variation in
x
. In this paper, we focus on the eigenvalues
E_{n}
of the system, which are approximated using the WKB quantization condition:

\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-V(x)}~dx=\left(n+\nu\right)\pi\hbar~,

(1)

where
\nu
is a fractional number known as the Maslov index, which depends on the specific properties of the potential. The integration limits
{x_{L}}
and
{x_{R}}
are the classical turning points where
V(x)
equals
E_{n}
.

The quantization condition from Eq. (
1
) generally provides good approximations for large values of
n
. However, for certain smooth potentials, its validity can extend
[
40
]
to
n\sim 1
. Remarkably, this usually approximate condition yields exact eigenvalues for both the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator and Morse potentials with
\nu=\frac{1}{2}
.

In 1926, Kramers
[
41
]
discovered that the WKB method was inaccurate for the Coulomb potential’s radial wave function near the origin; however, adding an
ad hoc
term
\frac{\hbar^{2}}{4r^{2}}
to
V(r)
leads to the correct behavior of the wave function near the singularity. This adjustment also makes the quantization condition exact for the eigenergies of this system
[
67
]
. Langer later showed that the same correction made the radial oscillator energies exact, and argued that the correction was needed due to the semi-infinite domain of Coulomb and the radial oscillator; this correction became known as the Langer correction.
[
42
,
43
,
2
,
24
]

SUSYQM relates “partner potentials” through the use of a superpotential
W(x,a)
, as we will discuss in Sec.
2.2
. In this context, a modified version of WKB (known as SWKB) applies to the superpotential
[
13
,
22
,
20
,
21
,
36
,
29
]
, which states that

I_{S}\equiv\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}(a)-W^{2}(x,a)}~dx=n\hbar\pi~,\quad\mbox{where}~n=1,2,3,\cdots~,

(2)

where the integration limits are given by the zeros of the integrand. In the case of unbroken supersymmetry (which we will discuss in Sec.
2.2
), this SWKB condition is exact for an entire class of potentials, known as conventional shape-invariant potentials, without the need for a Langer-type correction
[
1
]
.

While the above condition applies for unbroken SUSY, Inomata and Junker
[
36
,
34
,
35
,
39
]
, and Eckhardt
[
22
]
independently proposed a modified SWKB condition for systems with broken supersymmetry, which we will call the BSWKB condition:

\displaystyle I_{S}\equiv\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}(a)-W^{2}(x,a)}~dx=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\hbar~,\quad\mbox{where}~n=0,1,2,\cdots~.

(3)

This condition is exact for all conventional SIPs with bound states in the broken-SUSY phase
[
27
]
.

2.2
SUSYQM

Supersymmetric quantum mechanics is a generalization of the algebraic “ladder” method for the harmonic oscillator developed by Dirac and Föck. In SUSYQM, a Hamiltonian is written as the product of two linear differential operators:
A^{\pm}\equiv\mp\,\hbar\;d/dx+W(x,a)
, which are Hermitian conjugates of each other. The function
W(x,a)
is called the superpotential. These operators generate two “partner” Hamiltonians,
H_{\mp}
:

A^{\pm}A^{\mp}~=~H_{\mp}~=~-\hbar^{2}\frac{d^{2}}{dx^{2}}+V_{\mp}(x)~,

(4)

with corresponding potentials
V_{\mp}(x)=W^{2}\mp\hbar\;dW/dx
.
These Hamiltonians
H_{\mp}
are intertwined:

A^{-}H_{-}=H_{+}A^{-}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad A^{+}H_{+}=H_{-}A^{+}~.

(5)

Since these Hamiltonians are positive definite, the eigenenergies of
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
are non-negative. If either of the Hamiltonians has a zero-energy ground state, we say that we have unbroken supersymmetry. If neither Hamiltonian has a zero-energy groundstate, then SUSY is said to be broken. A given superpotential
W
may have both broken- and unbroken- SUSY phases depending on the values of relevant parameters.

For unbroken SUSY, without loss of generality, we choose the Hamiltonian
H_{-}={A}^{+}{A}^{-}
to have a zero energy ground state; i.e.,
E_{0}^{(-)}=0
. Then Eq. (
5
) leads to the isospectral relations

\frac{~~~{A}^{-}}{\sqrt{E^{+}_{n}}}~\psi^{(-)}_{n+1}=~\psi^{(+)}_{n}~~;~~~~\frac{~~~{A}^{+}}{\sqrt{E^{+}_{n}}}~\psi^{(+)}_{n}=~\psi^{(-)}_{n+1}~,

(6)

and

E_{n+1}^{(-)}=E_{n}^{(+)},\quad~n=0,1,2,\cdots~,

(7)

where
E_{n}^{(\pm)}
and
\psi^{(\pm)}_{n}
are, respectively, the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
H_{\pm}
.

Since
H_{-}\psi_{0}^{(-)}(x)={A}^{+}{A}^{-}\psi_{0}^{(-)}(x)=0
, we have
{A}^{-}\psi_{0}^{(-)}(x)=0
, and hence

\psi_{0}^{(-)}(x,a)={\mathcal{N}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}W(x,a)dx}~,

(8)

where
{\mathcal{N}}
is the normalization constant and
x_{0}
is an arbitrary but finite point on the real axis.
Note that
H_{+}
and
H_{-}
cannot both have normalizable ground states with zero energy,
4
4

4

If
H_{+}
also had a zero energy ground state, the corresponding wave function would be
\psi_{0}^{(+)}(x,a)={\mathcal{N}}\,e^{+\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}W(x,a)dx}=1/\psi_{0}^{(-)}(x,a)~
, which would not be normalizable.
so the ground state energy of
H_{+}
must be positive.

Since
\psi_{0}^{(-)}
must be normalizable for unbroken SUSY, we must have
\int_{x_{0}}^{L,R}W(x,a)\,dx=\infty
which requires that
W
be negative as
x\to L
(the left boundary of the domain) and be positive as
x\to R
(the right boundary). If the signs of
W
at the limits of the domain are reversed, then
H_{+}
would be the Hamiltonian corresponding to the zero-energy ground state.

If the asymptotic values of
W
at
x\to{L,R}
have the same sign, then the zero-energy state is non-normalizable and the system is in a broken SUSY phase. In this case, the ground state energy is positive for both potentials. For broken SUSY, Eq. (
5
) gives

E_{n}^{B}\equiv E^{-}_{n}=E^{+}_{n},\quad\mbox{where}~n=0,1,2,\cdots~.

(9)

Here we present an example of the 3-D harmonic oscillator (or radial oscillator) superpotential.
The superpotential is

W(r,\omega,\ell)=\frac{\omega r}{2}-\frac{\ell}{r}~~;\qquad 0<r<\infty~.

(10)

This superpotential exhibits both unbroken and broken phases, depending on the values of parameters
\omega
and
\ell
.
For
\omega>0
, the supersymmetry is unbroken for
\ell>0
and broken for
\ell<0
.

2.3
Shape Invariance

In SUSYQM, a potential is called “shape invariant” if it satisfies the shape invariance condition
[
33
,
47
,
31
]
:

\displaystyle V_{+}(x,a_{0})+g(a_{0})=V_{-}(x,a_{0})+g(a_{1}).

(11)

Here,
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
are parameters related by
a_{1}=f(a_{0})
. In terms of superpotential, this condition reads

W^{2}(x,a_{0})+\hbar\,\frac{d\,W(x,a_{0})}{dx}+g(a_{0})=W^{2}(x,a_{1})-\hbar\,\frac{d\,W(x,a_{1})}{dx}+g(a_{1}).

(12)

In this paper we only consider additive, or translational shape invariance, where
a_{1}=a_{0}+\hbar
.

For a system with unbroken supersymmetry, shape invariance guarantees exact solvability, as we will now show.
Adding the kinetic energy terms on both sides of Eq. (
12
) yields

H_{+}(x,a_{0})+g(a_{0})=H_{-}(x,a_{1})+g(a_{1})~.

Thus, the ground states for the partner potentials are related by

E_{0}^{(+)}(a_{0})+g(a_{0})=E_{0}^{(-)}(a_{1})+g(a_{1})~.

For unbroken SUSY, we automatically know the groundstate eigenvalue and eigenfunction for
H_{-}(x,a_{1})
:

E_{0}^{(-)}(a_{1})=0\quad;\quad\psi^{(-)}_{0}(x,a_{1})=~{\mathcal{N}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}W(x,a_{1})dx}~.

Thus, using Eq. (
7
) and (
8
), we obtain

E_{1}^{(-)}(a_{0})=g(a_{1})-g(a_{0})\quad;\quad\psi^{(-)}_{1}(x,a_{0})=\frac{~~~{A}^{-}(a_{0})}{\sqrt{E^{+}_{n}(a_{0})}}\quad{\mathcal{N}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}W(x,a_{1})dx}~.

(13)

Iterating this procedure determines the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions for excited states of
H_{-}(x,a_{0})
:

\displaystyle E_{n}^{(-)}(a_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(a_{n})-g(a_{0})~,

(14)

\displaystyle\psi^{(-)}_{n}(x,a_{0})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{+}{(a_{0})}{\mathcal{A}}^{+}{(a_{1})}\cdots{\mathcal{A}}^{+}{(a_{n-1})}}{\sqrt{E_{n}^{(-)}(a_{0})\,E_{n-1}^{(-)}(a_{1})\cdots E_{1}^{(-)}(a_{n-1})}}\quad\psi^{(-)}_{0}(x,a_{n})

(15)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{{\mathcal{A}}^{+}{(a_{0})}{\mathcal{A}}^{+}{(a_{1})}\cdots{\mathcal{A}}^{+}{(a_{n-1})}}{\sqrt{E_{n}^{(-)}(a_{0})\,E_{n-1}^{(-)}(a_{1})\cdots E_{1}^{(-)}(a_{n-1})}}\quad{\mathcal{N}}\,e^{-\frac{1}{\hbar}\int_{x_{0}}^{x}W(x,a_{n})dx}~.

(16)

Thus, unbroken SUSY provides the ground state energy and wavefunction, from which shape invariance allows us to derive higher energy levels. Therefore, all shape-invariant potentials are exactly solvable in their unbroken phase.

In the next section, we show that shape invariance constrains the possible functional forms for
W
; we categorize SIPs based on their form and examine connections between these categories.

3
Categorizing Shape-Invariant Potentials

The shape invariance condition, Eq. (
12
), is a difference-differential equation. It connects superpotentials
W(x,a)
and
W(x,a+\hbar)
, and their derivatives, for two different values of the parameter
a
. By requiring that this relation holds for all values of
\hbar
, including the limit
\hbar\rightarrow 0
, we expand Eq. (
12
) in powers of
\hbar
. By equating powers of
\hbar
, we express shape-invariance through a set of differential equations. These differential equations allow us to categorize SIPs.

Until recently, the known shape-invariant superpotentials
[
33
,
19
]
did not depend explicitly on
\hbar
; we now call these superpotentials “conventional.”
In 2008, researchers discovered
[
60
,
61
]
and generalized
[
51
,
52
,
63
,
53
,
54
]
a new family of “extended” superpotentials, which have been shown
[
4
,
5
]
to depend explicitly on
\hbar
.

3.1
Conventional SIPs and Their Classes

We now expand Eq. (
12
) in powers of
\hbar
. Assuming the superpotential is conventional,
\hbar
can only appear in
W
through evaluation at
a+\hbar
. At the lowest order,
[
26
]
we get

W\,\frac{\partial W}{\partial a}-\frac{\partial W}{\partial x}+\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{dg}{da}~=0,

(17)

and all higher order equations reduce to
[
4
,
5
]

\frac{\partial^{3}}{\partial a^{2}\partial x}~W(x,a)=0~.

(18)

Eq. (
18
) implies that conventional superpotentials
W(x,a)
must be of the form:

W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)+u(a)~.

(19)

This structure, originally conjectured by Infeld et al.
[
33
]
in piecemeal form in the 1950s, has since served as an ansatz for other researchers
[
10
,
9
,
12
]
. We divide conventional potentials into three classes as follows:

•

Class I:
f_{1}(x)
is a constant, so the only
x
-dependence term in
W
is
f_{2}(x)
.

•

Class II:
f_{2}(x)
is a constant, so the only
x
-dependence term in
W
is
af_{1}(x)
.

•

Class III: Neither
f_{1}(x)
nor
f_{2}(x)
are constant, so that both
af_{1}(x)
and
f_{2}(x)
contain functional
x
-dependence.

The authors of Ref.
[
4
]
proved that the known list of conventional potentials are the solutions of Eqs. (
17
) and (
18
), and that the list is complete. We include this list in Appendix
A
, together with the class to which each potential belongs.

3.2
Extended SIPs

For extended superpotentials, we expand
W
in powers of
\hbar
, as

W(x,a,\hbar)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{i}W_{i}(x,a)~;

(20)

this can be written as

W(x,a,\hbar)=W_{0}(x,a)+W_{h}(x,a,\hbar),

(21)

where the “kernel”
W_{0}
does not depend on
\hbar
and
W_{h}=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\hbar^{i}W_{i}(x,a)
includes all explicit
\hbar
-dependence.

Substituting Eq. (
20
) into Eq. (
12
), and requiring that Eq. (
12
) hold for any value of
\hbar
, the coefficients of the series must separately equal zero for each power of
\hbar
.
To first order in
\hbar
, this gives:

\displaystyle W_{0}\,\frac{\partial W_{0}}{\partial a}-\frac{\partial W_{0}}{\partial x}+\frac{1}{2}\,\frac{dg}{da}~=0,

(22)

and for order of
\hbar^{j}
where
j\geq 2
, we get

2\,\frac{\partial W_{j-1}}{\partial x}-\sum_{s=1}^{j-1}\sum_{k=0}^{s}\frac{1}{(j-s)!}\frac{\partial^{j-s}}{\partial a^{j-s}}W_{k}\,W_{s-k}+\sum_{k=2}^{j-1}\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\frac{\partial^{k}\,W_{j-k}}{\partial a^{k-1}\,\partial x}=0~.

(23)

Since
g(a)
appears in Eq. (
22
) but not in Eq. (
23
), we see
[
4
,
5
]
that
the energy spectrum is determined solely by
W_{0}
.

All conventional SIPs satisfy Eq. (
22
), so each conventional SIP could serve as a kernel for extended SIPs. We note that conventional potentials have
W_{i}=0
for all
i>0
; in this case, Eq. (
23
) reduces to Eq. (
18
), which is solved by Eq. (
19
).
It is possible, in principle, for
W_{0}
to be a function that is not a conventional potential, as long as it satisfies Eq. (
22
) and all
W_{i}
satisfy Eq. (
23
). However, every known extended SIP has a conventional SIP as its kernel, and thus each known extended SIP is isospectral with its conventional kernel.

As an example, the authors of Refs.
[
4
,
5
]
began with the harmonic oscillator superpotential
W_{0}(r,\ell)=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega r-\frac{\ell}{r}
as a kernel, and determined
W_{i}(r,\ell),\,{\rm for\,all}~i>0
from Eq. (
23
), to generate the extended superpotential

W(r,\ell,\hbar)=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega r-\frac{\ell}{r}+\left(\frac{2\omega r\hbar}{\omega r^{2}+2\ell-\hbar}-\frac{2\omega r\hbar}{\omega r^{2}+2\ell+\hbar}\right)~.

(24)

This reproduces the extended superpotential first discovered by Quesne in Ref.
[
60
]
, with
\hbar=1
.

3.3.1
Point Canonical Transformations (PCT)

A PCT transforms a given Schrödinger equation to a new one by a change of the independent variable
x
and a corresponding multiplicative transformation of the wavefunction
[
3
,
17
]
.
A change of variable from
x\to z
, where

, must be accompanied by a change in wave function
\psi\to\tilde{\psi}
, where
\psi(x)=\tilde{\psi}(z)\,\sqrt{du/dz}
.
This transforms the Schrödinger equation

-\frac{d^{2}\psi(x)}{dx^{2}}+V(x,a_{i})\,\psi(x)=E(a_{i})\psi(x)~,

(25)

into

\left[-\frac{d^{2}}{dz^{2}}+\left\{{u}^{\prime\,2}\,\left[V(u(z),a_{i})-E(a_{i})\right]+{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{3{u^{\prime\prime}}^{\,2}}{2{u^{\prime}}^{\,2}}-\frac{{u^{\prime\prime\prime}}}{{u^{\prime}}}\right)\right\}\right]\tilde{\psi}\left(z\right)=0,

(26)

where primes denote derivatives with respect to
z
, so that
{u}^{\prime}\equiv du/dz
. For Eq. (
26
) to be a Schrödinger equation, the expression

{u^{\prime}}^{\,2}\,\left[V(u(z),a_{i})-E(a_{i})\right]
must have a term that is independent of
z
, corresponding to the energy, thus constraining possible choices for the function
u(z)
.

As Fig.

3.3.2
Projections

Although they provide connections within a type, PCTs do not connect type-I SIPs to type-II. However, “projections” transform type-I to type-II. Type-I superpotentials correspond to the hypergeometric differential equation, which has three regular singular points. The equation reduces to a confluent hypergeometric equation corresponding to type-II in the appropriate limit in which two of the singularities merge.

3.3.3
Isospectral Extension

Point canonical transformations connect SIPs within a type (type-I or type-II), but cannot go from one type to another. Projections reduce one parameter, so they only go from type-I to type-II. As we have already seen in Sec.
3.2
, adding
\hbar

23

Additionally, the differential equations required by the
\hbar
-expansion can generate a “restricted” isospectral extension that produces a second parameter in a type-II SIP, creating a pathway from type-II to type-I. This was done in Ref.
[
46

to generate a path from Morse to Scarf.

We begin with a Morse superpotential as kernel:
W_{0}=-a-e^{-x}

W_{2k-1}=0
for all positive integers

W_{2k}=\left(-Q\right)^{k-1}e^{-(2k-1)x}\left(2P+Qe^{-2x}+2a~Qe^{-x}\right)~

satisfies Eq. (

k
, where
P
and
Q
are constant parameters.
The infinite sum
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\hbar^{j}W_{j}(x,a)
, produces a shape-invariant superpotential that is an isospectral extension of Morse:

W(x,a,\hbar)=-a-e^{-x}+\frac{\hbar^{2}\left(2Pe^{x}+2a\,Q+Q\,e^{-x}\right)}{e^{2x}+Q\,\hbar^{2}}~.

(27)

Substituting
W(x,a,\hbar)
into Eq. (
12
) verifies that this procedure maintains shape-invariance, as it must, where
g(a)=-a^{2}
. The corresponding energies are
E^{(-)}_{n}=g(a+n\hbar)-g(a)=a^{2}-(a+n\hbar)^{2}
.

This particular extension has unusual features. The
\hbar
-dependence in
W(x,a,\hbar)
is superficial and can be absorbed by redefinition of parameters as follows:

\hbar^{2}P\to P^{\,\prime},~\hbar^{2}Q\to e^{2\beta},~(2P^{\,\prime}-1)\,e^{-\beta}\to 2B,~-a\to A,~x-\beta\to x~.

(28)

These transformations convert
W(x,a,\hbar)
into the Scarf hyperbolic, a conventional type-I superpotential:

-a-e^{-x}+\frac{\hbar^{2}\left(2P\,e^{x}+2a\,Q+Q\,e^{-x}\right)}{e^{2x}+Q\,\hbar^{2}}~\to W_{\rm Scar\!f}=A\tanh x+B\,\textrm{sech}\,x~.

(29)

Thus, we have constructed a “restricted extension”
R_{a1}
which provides a path from a type-II to a type-I superpotential.
Thus all SIPs are connected by reversible paths.

4
SWKB Exactness for Conventional Potentials in the Unbroken Phase

In Ref.
[
29
]
, the authors proved that exactness of the SWKB quantization condition, Eq. (
2
), for conventional SIPs follows from additive shape invariance. In this section, we revisit the proofs of Ref.
[
29
]
using a more direct path.

Henceforth, we denote differentiation of any function
f(a,x)
with respect to
a
by a dot,
\dot{f}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial a}
, and with respect to
x
by a prime,
{f^{\prime}}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
. We will also use
E_{n}
(without superscript) for
E_{n}^{-}
.

For a given superpotential
W
and energy
E_{n}
the SWKB quantization condition, Eqs. (
2
) and (
3
), is exact if
I_{S}=n\pi\hbar
for unbroken SUSY and
I_{S}=(n+1/2)\pi\hbar
for broken SUSY.
We will prove that SWKB exactness directly follows from the following minimal set of assumptions for a conventional superpotential
W
:

•

From Eq. (
17
), we require that
W\dot{W}-W^{\prime}+\dot{g}/2=0
.

•

From Eq. (
18
), we require that
\ddot{W}^{\prime}=0
.
The general solution is

W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)+u(a)
.

•

To avoid level crossing, we require that
E_{n+1}>E_{n}
for all bound states.

We distinguish between the broken and unbroken phases of the superpotential based on the behavior at the limits of the domain.

•

For the unbroken phase,
W
has opposite signs at the two ends of the domain. From Eq. (
14
),
E_{n}=g(a_{n})-g(a_{0})
, where
a_{n}=a_{0}+n\hbar
, such that
E_{0}=0
. To avoid level crossing
\dot{g}(a)|_{a_{n}}>0
for all bound states. In this section we evaluate
I_{S}
and show that it is exact.

•

For the broken phase,
W
has the same sign at the two ends of the domain. Therefore,
E_{n}-W^{2}
can only have two roots if
W
has at least one extremum within the domain.
In the broken phase the groundstate energy is not known
a priori
, therefore, in the next section we find the energies using a discrete SI symmetry and prove the exactness of
I_{S}
.

We begin with the general form
W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x)+u(a)
and identify three classes:

Class I
:
f_{1}
is a constant;

Class II
:
f_{2}
is a constant;

Class III
: Neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{2}
are constants.

Note that
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
cannot both be constant because that would result into a trivial superpotential.
We now examine each of these classes, and show that SWKB is exact.

4.1
Class I Potentials:
f_{1}
is a constant

Because
f_{1}
is a constant, we absorb it into
u(a)
, so
W(x,a)=f_{2}(x)+u(a)
. From Eq. (
17
)
\big[f_{2}(x)+u(a)\big]\dot{u}(a)-f_{2}^{\prime}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\dot{g}(a)~=0
. Since
f_{2}
is not constant, we must have

-\left(f_{2}\dot{u}-f_{2}^{\prime}\right)=u\dot{u}+\dot{g}/2=\omega/2

(30)

for some constant
\omega
.
Then
\dot{u}=\alpha
so
u=\alpha a+\beta
for some constants
\alpha
and
\beta
. We absorb
\beta
in
f_{2}
, giving

W=f_{2}+\alpha a,

(31)

f_{2}^{\prime}=\alpha f_{2}+\omega/2,

(32)

\dot{g}=\omega-2\alpha^{2}a.

(33)

We now consider two subclasses: Subclass IA, where
\alpha=0
, and Subclass IB, where
\alpha\neq 0
.

4.1.1
Subclass IA:
W(x,a)=f_{2}(x)

In this case,
W=f_{2}
, and Eq. (
32
) mandates that
W^{\prime}=f_{2}^{\prime}=\omega/2
.
Therefore, we note the following:

•

Unbroken SUSY:
Since
W=f_{2}
, which is monotonic in
x
and whose range is
-\infty<f_{2}<\infty
, this superpotential is always in the unbroken phase. In this case,
E_{n}=\omega n\hbar
, where
\omega>0
.

•

Broken SUSY:
Due to the above argument, there is no broken phase.

We therefore have

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}(x,a)}\quad{\rm d}x=\int_{-\sqrt{E_{n}}}^{\sqrt{E_{n}}}\frac{\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}}\quad{\rm d}W}{W^{\prime}}

(34)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\omega}\int_{-\sqrt{E_{n}}}^{\sqrt{E_{n}}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}}\quad{\rm d}W.

The integral is of the form of
I_{0}
from Appendix
C
, with
y_{1}=-\sqrt{E_{n}}
and
y_{2}=\sqrt{E_{n}}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\omega}\frac{\pi}{8}\left(\sqrt{E_{n}}+\sqrt{E_{n}}\right)^{2}=\frac{2}{\omega}\frac{\pi}{8}\left(2\sqrt{\omega n\hbar}\right)^{2}=n\pi\hbar.

(35)

4.1.2
Subclass IB:
W(x,a)=f_{2}(x)+\alpha a
,
\alpha\neq 0

In this case, we have the form
W=f_{2}(x)+\alpha a
, where
\alpha\neq 0
. We define

y\equiv f_{2}+\omega/(2\alpha)=W+\dot{g}/(2\alpha),

(36)

which implies
y^{\prime}=f_{2}^{\prime}=W^{\prime}=\alpha y
. For nontrivial potentials,
y
and
y^{\prime}
cannot cross zero within the domain and thus both must have fixed signs.

Therefore, we note the following regarding the broken and unbroken phases:

•

Unbroken SUSY:
We note that
W=y-\dot{g}/(2\alpha)
, where
\dot{g}=\omega-2\alpha^{2}a>0
, and
y
cannot cross zero within the domain. Therefore, SUSY is unbroken when
y
and
\alpha
have the same sign;
i.e.,

\alpha y>0
. The energies are given by

E_{n}=\omega n\hbar-\alpha^{2}\left(n^{2}\hbar^{2}+2a_{0}n\hbar\right).

•

Broken SUSY:

Since
W
is monotonic in
x
, the BWSKB integral does not have two integration limits.

For the unbroken phase, the integration limits occur when
W=\pm\sqrt{E_{n}}
, which corresponds to

y_{L}=-\sqrt{E_{n}}+\frac{\dot{g}}{2\alpha}
and
y_{R}=\sqrt{E_{n}}+\frac{\dot{g}}{2\alpha}.
Since
y
and
\alpha
must have the same sign, this requires
y^{\prime}>0
.

The SWKB quantization condition thus becomes

I_{S}=\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}(x,a)}\quad{\rm d}x=\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{E_{n}-\left[y-(\frac{\dot{g}}{2\alpha})^{2}\right]}\quad{\rm d}y}{\alpha y}.

which simplifies to

I_{S}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{(y_{R}-y)(y-y_{L})}\quad{\rm d}y}{y}.

For
y>0
the integral is of the form
I_{1a}
while for
y<0
it is of the form
I_{1b}
from Appendix
C
.
We get

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}(y_{L}+y_{R})-\varepsilon\pi\sqrt{y_{L}\,y_{R}}\right],

(37)

where
\varepsilon=1
if
y>0
and
\varepsilon=-1
if
y<0
.
Substituting the expressions for
y_{L}
and
y_{R}
, along with the known values of
E_{n}
and
\dot{g}
, Eq. (
37
) becomes

I_{S}=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left[\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\left({\omega-2\alpha^{2}a}\right)-\frac{\pi}{2\alpha}\left(\omega-2\alpha^{2}a-2\alpha^{2}n\hbar)\right)\right]=\pi n\hbar.

(38)

4.2
Class II Potentials:
f_{2}
is a constant

For Class II superpotentials we absorb the constant
f_{2}
into
u(a)
and write the superpotential as
W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+u(a)
. Differentiating Eq. (
17
) twice with respect to
a
yields
f_{1}\left(3\ddot{u}+a\dddot{u}\right)+3\dot{u}\ddot{u}+u\dddot{u}+\dddot{g}/2=0.

Since
f_{1}
cannot be constant and
u(a)
is independent of
x
, this requires that
3\ddot{u}+a\dddot{u}=0
, so
u=B/a+\mu a+\nu
. We absorb
\mu a
in
af_{1}
so
u(a)=B/a+\nu
for constants
\nu
and
B
.

Inserting the form

W=f_{1}+\nu+B/a
into Eq. (
17
) yields

a\left(f_{1}^{2}-f_{1}^{\prime}\right)+\nu f_{1}-\frac{B}{a^{3}}-\frac{B\nu}{a^{2}}+\frac{\dot{g}}{2}=0.

Since
f_{1}
is not constant,
\nu=0
. Thus,
a\left(f_{1}^{2}-f_{1}^{\prime}\right)=\frac{B^{2}}{a^{3}}-\frac{\dot{g}}{2}=a\lambda

for some constant
\lambda
.
This leads to

W=af_{1}+B/a,

(39)

f_{1}^{\prime}=f_{1}^{2}-\lambda,

(40)

\dot{g}=\frac{2B^{2}}{a^{3}}-2a\lambda.

(41)

Consequently, Class II splits into Subclass IIA, where
\lambda=0
, and Subclass IIB where
\lambda\neq 0.

4.2.1
Subclass IIA:
W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+B/a
;
f_{1}^{\prime}=f_{1}^{2}
;
\lambda=0

In this case,
f_{1}
and
f_{1}^{\prime}
cannot cross zero for nontrivial potentials. We therefore note the following regarding the unbroken and broken phases:

•

Unbroken SUSY:
Since
\dot{g}=2B^{2}/a^{3}>0
, to avoid level crossing we must have
a>0
, and the energy levels are given by
E_{n}=B^{2}\left[\frac{1}{a^{2}}-\frac{1}{(a+n\hbar)^{2}}\right]
. Additionally,
B
and
f_{1}
must have opposite signs.

•

Broken SUSY:

Since
W
is monotonic in
x
, the BWSKB integral does not have two integration limits.

For the unbroken phase we change integration variables in
I_{S}
so that
y=af_{1}
, and
y^{\prime}=y^{2}/a
. The SWKB condition therefore becomes

I_{S}=\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}(x,a)}\,{\rm d}x=a\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{E_{n}-(y+\frac{B}{a})^{2}}}{y^{2}}\,{\rm d}y~,

(42)

where the integration limits are
y_{L}=-\sqrt{E_{n}}-B/a
and
y_{R}=\sqrt{E_{n}}-B/a
.
This simplifies to

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{(y_{L}-y)(y-y_{R})}}{y^{2}}\,{\rm d}y.

(43)

Since
y
cannot change sign and
a>0
, we have one of two cases. Either
y_{R}>y_{L}>0
, which requires
B<-a\sqrt{E_{n}}<0
, or
y_{L}<y_{R}<0
, which requires
B>a\sqrt{E_{n}}>0
.
For
y>0
the integral is of the form
I_{2a}
while for
y<0
it is of the form
I_{2b}
from Appendix
C
.
We get

I_{S}=\frac{a\pi}{2}\left(\frac{\varepsilon y_{L}+\varepsilon y_{R}-2\sqrt{y_{L}y_{R}}}{\sqrt{y_{1}y_{2}}}\right),

(44)

where
\varepsilon=1
when
y>0
and
\varepsilon=-1
when
y<0
.
Substituting the integration limits and the energy levels, Eq. (

I_{S}=\pi a(a+n\hbar)\left(\frac{1}{a}-\frac{1}{a+n\hbar}\right)=n\pi\hbar.

(45)

4.2.2
Subclass IIB:
W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+B/a
;
f_{1}^{\prime}=f_{1}^{2}-\lambda
;
\lambda\neq 0

In this case,
f_{1}^{2}\neq\lambda
and
f_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
within the domain for nontrivial potentials. We note the following regarding the unbroken and broken phases:

•

Unbroken SUSY:
In this case,
\dot{g}=\frac{2B^{2}}{a^{3}}-2a\lambda>0
, and
E_{n}=\frac{B^{2}}{a^{2}}-\frac{B^{2}}{(a+n\hbar)^{2}}+\lambda\left[a^{2}-(a+n\hbar)^{2}\right].

•

Broken SUSY:

Since
W
is monotonic in
x
, the BWSKB integral does not have two integration limits.

For the unbroken phase, we define
y\equiv f_{1}/\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}
and obtain

y^{\prime}=\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}\left(y^{2}-\varepsilon_{1}\right)
,
where
\varepsilon_{1}=1
when
\lambda>0
and
\varepsilon_{1}=-1
when
\lambda<0

\varepsilon_{1}\lambda=|\lambda|
.
Therefore

W=ay\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}+B/a~;\quad W^{\prime}=a\varepsilon_{1}\lambda\left(y^{2}-\varepsilon_{1}\right)~.

(46)

Note that
y^{2}
cannot cross
\varepsilon_{1}
within the domain for non-trivial potentials;
W^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
cannot cross zero and the sign of

is the same as that of

We define
\varepsilon_{2}

\varepsilon_{2}=1
when

\varepsilon_{2}=-1
when

, and
\varepsilon_{3}
such that
\varepsilon_{3}=1

\varepsilon_{3}=-1

We change our integration variable of the SWKB quantization condition such that

I_{S}=\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}(x,a)}\,{\rm d}x=\frac{\varepsilon_{3}}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}}\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{E_{n}-(ay\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}+B/a)^{2}}}{y^{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}\,dy~,

where

y_{L}=\frac{1}{a\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}}\left(-\varepsilon_{2}\sqrt{E_{n}}-\frac{B}{a}\right)~;\quad y_{R}=\frac{1}{a\sqrt{\varepsilon_{1}\lambda}}\left(\varepsilon_{2}\sqrt{E_{n}}-\frac{B}{a}\right)~.

This simplifies to

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\varepsilon_{2}\varepsilon_{3}\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{\left(y_{R}-y\right)\left(y-y_{L}\right)}}{y^{2}-\varepsilon_{1}}\,dy~.

(47)

We evaluate the integral in Eq.(

Case IIB1:
\lambda<0

For this case, because

With these values, Eq.(

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{\left(y_{R}-y\right)\left(y-y_{L}\right)}}{y^{2}+1}\,dy=aI_{3}~,

(48)

where
I_{3}
is given in Appendix

. This evaluates to

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{(1+y_{L}^{2})(1+y_{R}^{2})}-y_{L}y\,_{R}+1\right]^{1/2}-\pi a

(49)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a\pi}{\sqrt{2}}\left[\sqrt{\frac{\left[B^{2}-\lambda(a+n\hbar)^{4}\right]^{2}}{a^{4}(a+n\hbar)^{4}\lambda^{2}}}-\frac{-B^{2}-n\hbar(a+n\hbar)^{2}(2a+n\hbar)\lambda}{a^{2}(a+n\hbar)^{2}\lambda}\right]^{1/2}-\pi a

Case IIB2:
\lambda>0
and

.

For this case,
\varepsilon_{1}=1
and
\varepsilon_{2}=-1
; moreover
\frac{B^{2}}{a^{3}}>a\lambda

\displaystyle=

where

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a\pi}{2}\left[\sqrt{(1-y_{L})(1-y_{R})}+\sqrt{(1+y_{L})(1+y_{R})}-2\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi a}{2}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\left[B+(a+n\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{\lambda}\right]^{2}}{a^{2}(a+n\hbar)^{2}\lambda}}+\sqrt{\frac{\left[B-(a+n\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{\lambda}\right]^{2}}{a^{2}(a+n\hbar)^{2}\lambda}}-2\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\pi a}{2}\left[\frac{B+(a+n\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{\lambda}-B+(a+n\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{\lambda}-2a(a+n\hbar)\sqrt{\lambda}}{a(a+n\hbar)\sqrt{\lambda}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\pi\hbar~.

For this case,

\varepsilon_{2}=1,

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle a\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{\left(y_{R}-y\right)\left(y-y_{L}\right)}}{y^{2}-1}\,dy=aI_{5}~,

where

from Appendix

Either way, this leads to

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\pi a}{2}\left[\frac{-B+(a+n\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{\lambda}+B+(a+n\hbar)^{2}\sqrt{\lambda}-2a(a+n\hbar)\sqrt{\lambda}}{a(a+n\hbar)\sqrt{\lambda}}\right]

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle n\pi\hbar.

4.3
Class III Potentials: Neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{2}
are constant

For Class III potentials, neither
f_{1}
nor
f_{2}
are constant. They must be linearly independent of each other, otherwise they would be equivalent to Class II. Therefore, we have
W=af_{1}+f_{2}+u
. We differentiate Eq. (
17
) twice with respect to
a
, obtaining

f_{1}\left(3\ddot{u}+a\dddot{u}\right)+f_{2}\dddot{u}=-3\dot{u}\ddot{u}-u\dddot{u}-\dddot{g}/2.

Since
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are linearly independent,
f_{2}\dddot{u}=0
, which means
\dddot{u}=0
. Thus
f_{1}\left(3\ddot{u}+a\dddot{u}\right)=0
, so
\ddot{u}=0
. Therefore,
u(a)=\mu a+\nu
for constants
\mu
and
\nu
. We can regroup terms to include
\mu
in
f_{1}
and
\nu
in
f_{2}
, leaving us with

W(x,a)=af_{1}(x)+f_{2}(x).

(54)

Returning to Eq. (
17
), we now have

\left(f_{1}^{2}-f_{1}^{\prime}\right)a+f_{1}f_{2}-f_{2}^{\prime}+\dot{g}/2=0.

Grouping terms by powers in
a
, each power must separately reduce. We therefore have

\displaystyle f_{1}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}^{2}-\lambda,

(55)

\displaystyle f_{2}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}f_{2}+\omega/2,

(56)

\displaystyle\dot{g}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2a\lambda+\omega,

(57)

for constants
\lambda
and
\omega
.

We define Subclass IIIA, where
\lambda=0
, and Subclass IIIB, where
\lambda\neq 0
.

4.3.1
Subclass IIIA:
W=af_{1}+f_{2}
;
f_{1}^{\prime}=f_{1}^{2}

Because
\lambda=0
, we have
f_{1}^{\prime}=f_{1}^{2}
, so
f_{1}\neq 0
and
f_{1}^{\prime}\neq 0
within the domain. We also have
f_{2}^{\prime}=f_{1}f_{2}+\omega/2
. These are solved by functions of the form

f_{2}=\alpha f_{1}-\frac{\omega}{4f_{1}}.

Consequently
W=(a+\alpha)f_{1}-\frac{\omega}{4f_{1}}
. With a redefinition of
a
, this leads to

W=af_{1}-\frac{\omega}{4f_{1}}.

(58)

The SWKB integral becomes

I_{S}=\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-\left(af_{1}-\frac{\omega}{4f_{1}}\right)^{2}}\,{\rm d}x=\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{E_{n}f_{1}^{2}-\left(af_{1}^{2}-\frac{\omega}{4}\right)^{2}}}{\varepsilon_{1}f_{1}}\,{\rm d}x,

where
\varepsilon_{1}=1
when
f_{1}>0
, and
\varepsilon_{1}=-1
when
f_{1}<0
.
We change integration variable by defining

. In this case,

, so

has the same sign as
f_{1}
, and the SWKB integral becomes

I_{S}=\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{E_{n}y-\left(ay-\omega/4\right)^{2}}}{2y^{2}}\,{\rm d}y,

(59)

where

y_{L}=\frac{2E_{n}+a\omega-2\sqrt{E_{n}}\sqrt{E_{n}+a\omega}}{4a^{2}}~~,\qquad y_{R}=\frac{2E_{n}+a\omega+2\sqrt{E_{n}}\sqrt{E_{n}+a\omega}}{4a^{2}}.

The integral can be written as

I_{S}=\frac{a}{2}\int_{y_{L}}^{y_{R}}\frac{\sqrt{(y_{R}-y)(y-y_{L})}}{2y^{2}}\,{\rm d}y=\frac{a}{4}\,I_{2B},

where
I_{2B}
is given in Appendix
C
. Thus

\displaystyle I_{S}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{a\pi\left(y_{L}+y_{R}-2\sqrt{y_{L}y_{R}}\right)}{4\sqrt{y_{L}y_{R}}}.

Substituting the integration limits gives

6
Generalized Langer Correction

Recent work
[
28
]
demonstrated that a universal correction renders the WKB quantization condition exact for all conventional SIPs in their unbroken phase, and that this correction follows from the exactness of SWKB described in Sec.
4
. We review this result here.

As discussed in Sec.
2.1
, the WKB quantization condition given by

\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-{V}(x)}~dx=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\hbar~
yields exact energies for the cases of the one-dimensional harmonic oscillator and Morse potentials, which correspond to Class I SIPs. However, the energy spectrum is not exact for SIPs of other classes.

We will now introduce a generalized term
\Delta V(x)
such that the corrected potential
\tilde{V}(x)\equiv V_{-}(x)+\Delta V(x)
yields exact eigenenergies for all conventional SIPs:

\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-\tilde{V}(x)}~dx=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\hbar~.

(102)

In Sec.
4
, we showed that the SWKB quantization condition yields exact eigenvalues for all conventional SIPs in the unbroken supersymmetric phase:

\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}(x)}~dx=n\pi\hbar~.

(103)

We begin our proof by observing that we can treat the quantum number
n
in Eq. (
103
) as a parameter; i.e., the condition is exact for any value of
n
, provided
E_{n}-W^{2}(x)
has two roots. In particular, for
n\to n+1/2
, the condition becomes

\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n+1/2}-W^{2}(x)}~dx=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\hbar~.

(104)

Integrating Eq. (
17
) we obtain:

\int_{a-\hbar/2}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial a}\Big(W^{2}(x,a)+g(a)\Big)da=2\int_{a-\hbar/2}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\,W(x,a)\,da~,

(105)

which, along with Eq. (
19
), leads to

W^{2}(x,a)-\hbar\Big(af_{1}^{\prime}(x)+f_{2}^{\prime}(x)\Big)+\frac{1}{4}\,\hbar^{2}f_{1}^{\prime}(x)+g(a)=W^{2}(x,a-\hbar/2)+g(a-\hbar/2)~.

(106)

Subtracting
g(a+n\hbar)
from both sides of Eq. (
106
) and using Eq. (
14
), we get

E_{n}(a)-\left(V_{-}(x,a)+\frac{1}{4}\,\hbar^{2}f_{1}^{\prime}(x)\right)=E_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(\tilde{a})-W^{2}(x,\tilde{a})~,

(107)

where
\tilde{a}\equiv a-\hbar/2
and

E_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(\tilde{a})=g(\tilde{a}+(n+\tfrac{1}{2})\hbar)-g(\tilde{a}).

Eq. (
107
) suggests the following generalized Langer correction

\Delta V\equiv\frac{1}{4}\,\hbar^{2}f_{1}^{\prime}(x).

(108)

Because
\tilde{V}(x)=V_{-}(x)+\Delta V(x)
, we have arrived at the main result:

\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n}(a)-\tilde{V}(x,a)}~dx=\int_{x_{L}}^{x_{R}}\sqrt{E_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(\tilde{a})-W^{2}(x,\tilde{a})}~dx=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\pi\hbar.

(109)

Thus, the exactness of SWKB guarantees that adding the generalized Langer-correction term
\frac{1}{4}\,\hbar^{2}f_{1}^{\prime}(x)
to the potential
V_{-}(x,a)
makes the WKB quantization condition exact for all conventional SIPs.

7
SWKB for Extended Shape Invariant Superpotentials

In Section
3
, we saw that additive shape invariant superpotentials belong to one of two classes: conventional and extended. In this section we will show that extended superpotentials do not satisfy SWKB exactness by providing a counterexample.

Extended superpotentials
W
depend explicitly on
\hbar
. They can be written as

W(x,a,\hbar)=W_{0}(x,a)+W_{h}(x,a,\hbar),

(110)

where the “kernel”
W_{0}
does not depend explicitly on
\hbar
, and
W_{h}
includes all the explicit
\hbar
-dependence. For all known cases, the kernel
W_{0}(x,a)
is a conventional superpotential. The authors of Refs.
[
4
,
5
]
showed that the energy spectrum of extended superpotentials is determined solely by
W_{0}
. Thus each known extended SIP is isospectral with its conventional counterpart.

Despite the exactness of SWKB for conventional SIPs, extended superpotentials do not necessarily satisfy the SWKB condition in spite of being additively shape invariant.
This was proven numerically in Ref.
[
6
]
by considering a particular example:

W\left(x,\ell,\hbar\right)=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega x-\frac{\ell}{x}+\frac{2\omega x\hbar}{\omega x^{2}+2\ell-\hbar}-\frac{2\omega x\hbar}{\omega x^{2}+2\ell+\hbar}~.

(111)

This extended superpotential
[
60
]
has the 3-D oscillator superpotential

W_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\,\omega x-\frac{\ell}{x}~

(112)

as its kernel, and the
\hbar
-dependent extension

W_{h}=\frac{2\omega x\hbar}{\omega x^{2}+2\ell-\hbar}-\frac{2\omega x\hbar}{\omega x^{2}+2\ell+\hbar}~.

(113)

The extended superpotential
W(x,\ell,\hbar)
of Eq. (
111
) is additively shape invariant and isospectral with the 3-D oscillator.

We introduce the dimensionless variable
z=x\sqrt{\omega/\hbar}
and the dimensionless parameter
\lambda=\ell/\hbar
, to obtain

W(z,\lambda,\hbar)=\sqrt{\hbar\omega}\left[\frac{z}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{z}+\frac{2z}{z^{2}+2\lambda-1}-\frac{2z}{z^{2}+2\lambda+1}\right]~.

(114)

Thus, the SWKB integral
I\equiv\int_{x_{1}}^{x_{2}}\sqrt{E_{n}-W^{2}(x)}~{\rm d}x
becomes

I=\hbar\int_{z_{1}}^{z_{2}}\sqrt{\eta\left(z,\lambda\right)}\quad{\rm d}z,

(115)

where
\eta(z,\lambda)
is the dimensionless quantity:

\eta(z,\lambda)=2n-\left[\frac{z}{2}-\frac{\lambda}{z}+\frac{2z}{z^{2}+2\lambda-1}-\frac{2z}{z^{2}+2\lambda+1}\right]^{2}~.

(116)

Thus, the SWKB integral reduces to Eq. (
115
) which is
\hbar
times a dimensionless integral.
Numerical analysis proved
[
6
]
that the integral is not equal to
n\pi
, therefore the extended superpotential of Eq. (
111
) does not satisfy the SWKB condition. Consequently, extended superpotentials are not necessarily SWKB exact.

In Ref.
[
49
]
, the authors use numerical analysis to demonstrate that several other examples of extended potentials are similarly not SWKB exact; the authors initially claimed that their analysis also improves upon the treatment of
\hbar
used in Ref.
[
6
]
; however, the two approaches are equivalent, as demonstrated in Ref.
[
7
]
.

8
Conclusions

Exactly solvable potentials are relatively rare in quantum mechanics, so approximation methods such as WKB continue to play an important role in analyzing quantum systems.
Solvable potentials are highly instructive in deepening our understanding of approximation methods and suggest new directions for future development.
In this manuscript, we reviewed recent advances related to mathematical properties that ensure exact solutions of the WKB and related SWKB conditions for all conventional shape-invariant superpotentials.

•

The SWKB quantization condition has long been known to produce exact eigenvalues for conventional SIPs in their unbroken phase
[
13
,
20
,
22
,
62
,
21
]
. In Sec.
4
, we reviewed recent scholarship
[
29
]
proving that this exactness is a consequence of the mathematical structure underlying conventional shape-invariance. This result provides a key to the entire set of advances discussed in this manuscript.

•

In the broken phase, the related BSWKB condition applies
[
36
,
34
,
35
,
39
,
22
]
. In Sec
5
, we reviewed recent work
[
29
]
showing that BSWKB is exact for all conventional SIPs in which the relevant integral is properly defined. This exactness results from a discrete shape-invariance connecting the broken and unbroken phases, and thus follows from the exactness of the corresponding unbroken phase.

•

The WKB condition is exact for the harmonic oscillator and Morse potentials, but not for the other conventional SIPs. However, adding the “Langer correction,” initially identified by Kramers
[
41
]
, to the Coulomb and 3D-oscillator potentials makes them exact as well
[
42
,
43
,
2
,
24
]
. In Sec
6
, we reviewed recent work that generalized the Langer correction to all conventional SIPs
[
28
]
. This result derives directly from the SWKB exactness of these superpotentials.

•

Extended SIPs are additive shape-invariant, however recent numerical analysis found extended SIPs that are not SWKB-exact
[
6
,
49
,
7
]
. Therefore, the SWKB exactness of conventional SIPs does not arise from additive shape-invariance alone, but also assumes
\hbar
-independence of the corresponding superpotentials. We discussed the SWKB condition for extended SIPs in Sec.
7
.

Together, these recent advances demonstrate that the mathematical framework of conventional SIPs has several important consequences. First, it ensures the exactness of the SWKB condition for these SIPs in their unbroken-SUSY phase. It also guarantees their BSWKB-exactness and provides for a generalized Langer correction; these two results can be directly related to the SWKB-exactness. Finally, extended SIPs are not necessarily SWKB exact, despite being additive shape-invariant. Thus, the form of conventional SIPs has wide-ranging consequences for semiclassical approximations, giving these potentials an important status for further developing our understanding of semiclassical methods.
