Abstract
. We prove a sharp stability estimate for Schur iterates of contractive analytic functions in the open unit disk. We then apply this result in the setting of the inverse scattering approach and obtain a fast algorithm for solving the discrete integrable nonlinear Schrödinger equation (Ablowitz-Ladik equation) on the integer lattice, ℤ \mathbb{Z} . We also give a self-contained introduction to the theory of the nonlinear Fourier transform from the perspective of Schur functions and orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle.

1.
Introduction

1.1.
Schur’s algorithm

The Schur class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|<1\}
of the complex plane,
\mathbb{C}
, consists of analytic functions
F
in
\mathbb{D}
such that

\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|F(z)|\leqslant 1.

For
F\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
, we write
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
if
F
is not a finite Blaschke product. Take
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
, set
F_{0}=F
, and define the sequence
\{F_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
using Schur’s algorithm:

(1.1)

zF_{n+1}=\frac{F_{n}-F_{n}(0)}{1-\overline{F_{n}(0)}F_{n}},\qquad n\geqslant 0.

By construction and Schwarz lemma, the resulting functions
F_{0}
,
F_{1}
,
F_{2}
,
\ldots
will belong to the class
\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
as well. In the case where
F\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})\setminus\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
is a Blaschke product of order
N\geqslant 0
, the same construction gives a finite sequence of Blaschke products
F_{0}
,
F_{1}
, …
F_{N}
of orders
N
,
N-1
, …
0
, correspondingly. In particular,
F_{N}
is a constant of unit modulus and the Schur’s algorithm stops.

Note that
|F_{n}(0)|<1
for each
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
,
n\geqslant 0
, by the maximum modulus principle.
Therefore, each function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
generates a sequence of numbers
\{F_{n}(0)\}_{n\geqslant 0}\subset\mathbb{D}
. They are called the recurrence coefficients of
F
. It can be shown that the mapping

F\mapsto\{F_{n}(0)\}_{n\geqslant 0}

is a homeomorphism from
\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
with the topology of convergence on compact subsets of
\mathbb{D}
onto the space of sequences
q:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{D}
with the topology of elementwise convergence, see Section 1.3.6 in
[
17
]
. Here,
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,+\infty)
. In particular, for every sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}\subset\mathbb{D}
there exists a unique function
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
such that
\alpha_{n}=F_{n}(0)
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. In this paper we study stability of Schur’s algorithm. We prove a sharp estimate for
|F_{n}(0)-G_{n}(0)|
in terms of
F-G
for functions
F,G\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
from the Szegő class, whose definition we now recall.

Let
m
denote the Lebesgue measure on the unit circle
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
normalized by
m(\mathbb{T})=1
. The following theorem can be found, e.g., in Section 2.7.8 of
[
17
]
.

Theorem 1.1

(Szegő theorem)
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
, and let
\{F_{n}(0)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
be its recurrence coefficients. Then

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|F|^{2})\,dm=\log\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-|F_{n}(0)|^{2}),

where both sides are finite or infinite simultaneously.

We will refer to functions
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
such that

(1.2)

\displaystyle\eta(F)=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}(1-|F_{n}(0)|^{2})>0,

as Schur functions of Szegő class. Given any
r\in(0,1)
, and an analytic function
F
in
\mathbb{D}
, we set

\|F\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{T}}|F(r\xi)|^{2}\,dm(\xi)\right)^{1/2}.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
F
,
G
be Schur functions of Szegő class, and let
\eta>0
satisfy
\min(\eta(F),\eta(G))\geqslant\eta
. Then for every
r\in(0,1)
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, the estimate

(1.3)

\|F_{n}-G_{n}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C(\eta,r)r^{-n}\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})},

holds with the constant
C(\eta,r)=\exp\bigl(\log\eta^{-1}\cdot\bigl(2+\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-\eta}}\bigr)(\frac{4}{(1-r)^{2}}+1)\bigr)
depending only on
\eta
,
r
.

The order of the exponential factor
r^{-n}
in Theorem
1.2
is sharp. Indeed, one can take
\delta\in(0,1)
and set
F=\delta z^{n}
,
G=0
. Then
F_{n}(z)=\delta
,
G_{n}(z)=0
for all
z\in\mathbb{D}
. So, we have
\|F_{n}-G_{n}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}=\|\delta\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}=\delta
and
\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}=\|\delta z^{n}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}=\delta r^{n}
in this case. Since
\eta(F)=1-\delta^{2}
,
\eta(G)=1
do not depend on
n
, a consideration of large
n
’s shows that the order of growth
r^{-n}
in (
1.3
) cannot be improved within the Szegő class.

Theorem
1.2
can be used to estimate
|F_{n}(0)-G_{n}(0)|
if we know that Schur functions
F
,
G
are sufficiently close to each other in the disk
|z|\leqslant r
. Indeed, by Bessel inequality, we have

(1.4)

|F_{n}(0)-G_{n}(0)|\leqslant\|F_{n}-G_{n}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})},

because the system
\{z^{k}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
is orthogonal in
L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})
. We want to emphasize that the constant
C(\eta,r)
in Theorem
1.2
is uniform for functions
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
with the Szegő constant
\eta(F)
separated from zero. This is the most important feature of (
1.3
) when it compared with another stability result from the inverse spectral theory – Sylvester-Winebrenner theorem
[
20
]
. In the language of Schur functions, this theorem says that Schur’s algorithm defines a homeomorphism in appropriate metric spaces:

Theorem 1.3

(Sylvester–Winebrenner theorem)
.

The mapping

F\mapsto\{F_{n}(0)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
that takes a Schur function into the sequence of its recurrence coefficients is a homeomorphism from the metric space
X_{+}=\{F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D}):\eta(F)>0\}

with the metric
\rho_{s}(F,G)^{2}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log\bigl(1-\bigl|\frac{F-G}{1-\bar{F}G}\bigr|^{2}\bigr)\,dm
onto the metric space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
of square summable sequences
q:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{D}
with the metric

\|q-\tilde{q}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}|q(n)-\tilde{q}(n)|^{2}
.

We prove this version of Sylvester–Winebrenner theorem in Section
6
. It is very natural to expect that the modulus of continuity of the homeomorphism in Theorem
1.3
is controlled by
\eta
on the subset of
F\in X_{+}
with
\eta(F)>\eta>0
. This is, however, not the case! See Proposition
6.12
below. On the other hand, the uniform character of estimate (
1.3
) will be crucial for the application of (
1.3
) to the discrete integrable nonlinear Schrödinger equation (Ablowitz-Ladik equation). Let us discuss it next.

1.2.
AL: statement of the problem

Consider the defocusing Ablowitz-Ladik equation (AL) on the integer lattice,
\mathbb{Z}
,

(1.5)

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}q(t,n)=i\bigl(1-|q(t,n)|^{2}\bigr)\bigl(q(t,n-1)+q(t,n+1)\bigr),\quad q(0,n)=q_{0}(n),\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

The variable
t\in\mathbb{R}
is considered as time,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
is the discrete space variable. Ablowitz-Ladik equation is the integrable model introduced in
[
1
]
,
[
2
]
as a spatial discretization of the cubic non-linear Schrödinger equation (NLS), see
[
3
]
for a general context and modern exposition. If we change variables to
u=e^{-2it}q
, then (
1.5
) becomes

\displaystyle i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(t,n)

\displaystyle=-\bigl(1-|u(t,n)|^{2}\bigr)\bigl(u(t,n-1)+u(t,n+1)\bigr)+2u(t,n)

\displaystyle=-u(t,n-1)+2u(t,n)-u(t,n+1)+|u(t,n)|^{2}(u(t,n-1)+u(t,n+1)),

which is indeed a discretization of the continuous defocusing NLS equation,

(1.6)

i\frac{\partial}{\partial t}u(t,x)=-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}u(t,x)+2|u(t,x)|^{2}u(t,x),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.

We are going to present a new solution method for (
1.5
) based on Schur’s algorithm. The rate of its convergence will be estimated using Theorem
1.2
. We deal with the following problem:

Problem 1.4
.

Given
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,
t\in\mathbb{R}
,
n_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}
, and a sequence
q_{0}
on
\mathbb{Z}
such that
|q_{0}(n)|<1
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
,

\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})\geqslant\eta>0,

evaluate the solution
q
of (
1.5
) at
(t,n_{0})
with the absolute error at most
\varepsilon
.

The quantity
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q(t,n)|^{2})=\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})
is conserved under the flow of AL equation. So, it is a natural characteristic for results on stability/accuracy of solutions of AL equation.

We introduce the algorithm which solves Problem
1.4
in
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
operations, where
\mathbf{n}=t+\log\varepsilon^{-1}
. Thus, to have accuracy
e^{-n}
at the moment of time
t=1
, one need to take at most
c_{\eta}n\log^{2}n
arithmetic operations for some constant
c_{\eta}>0
depending only on
\eta
. The basic Runge-Kutta scheme RK4 requires
n
time steps (
\sim n\cdot k
operations) for computing
u(1,j)
,
-k\leqslant j\leqslant k
, to guarantee accuracy
O(1/n^{4})
if we additionally assume that the impact of
u(t,j)
,
|j|\geqslant k
, is negligible for
0\leqslant t\leqslant 1
.

1.3.
AL: localization

Our solution method is a modification of the classical inverse scattering approach. From a bird-view look, the standard procedure (see Chapter 2 in
[
23
]
) of solving (
1.5
) by means of the inverse scattering theory (IST) looks as follows: given an initial datum
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
, define the so-called reflection coefficient
{\bf r}_{q_{0}}
by

(1.7)

{\bf r}_{q_{0}}(z)=\frac{b(z)}{a(z)},\qquad\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix}=\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q_{0}(k)|^{2}}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q_{0}(k)}z^{-k}\\
q_{0}(k)z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\in\mathbb{T},

and find
q(t,\cdot):\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
such that
{\bf r}_{q(t,\cdot)}=e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q_{0}}
on
\mathbb{T}
. It turns out that
q(t,\cdot)
will solve (
1.5
) for initial datum
q_{0}
provided
q_{0}
decays fast enough (say,
\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|q_{0}(k)|<\infty
). A fundamental problem appearing when one tries to solve (
1.5
) by IST with merely
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
initial datum
q_{0}
(i.e., for general
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
such that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})>0
) is that we can have
{\bf r}_{q_{0}}={\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{0}}
for
q_{0}\neq\tilde{q}_{0}
. This phenomenon was first observed by Volberg and Yuditskii in
[
24
]
on the level of Jacobi matrices, and then by Tao and Thiele
[
22
]
in the setting of the nonlinear Fourier transform, NLFT. It shows that when we pass to reflection coefficients
{\bf r}_{q_{0}}
,
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{0}}
, some information gets lost and there are no chances to solve (
1.5
) for
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
initial data by using IST approach directly. To overcame this difficulty (non-injectivity of NLFT), we first prove the following localization estimate.

Theorem 1.5
.

Let
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
be such that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})\geqslant\eta
for some
\eta>0
and let
q
be the solution of (
1.5
) for the initial datum
q_{0}
. Take
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, consider the sequence
q_{0,N}
defined by

\displaystyle q_{0,N}(n)=\begin{cases}q_{0}(n),&|n|\leqslant N,\\
0,&|n|>N,\end{cases}

and let
q_{N}
be the corresponding solution of (
1.5
). Then, for
N\geqslant|j|
,
t>0
and all
r\in(0,1)
, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle|q(t,j)-q_{N}(t,j)|\leqslant\frac{4e^{t/r}C(\eta,r)}{1-r}r^{N-|j|},

where
C(\eta,r)
is the function from Theorem
1.2
.

Having in mind a possible future development of a parallel theory for continuous NLS equation, we use only “spectral” methods in the proof of Theorem
1.5
. The reader interested in short and elementary proof of Theorem
1.5
by means of a direct approach, could find it in Section
7
.

1.4.
AL: compactly supported initial data

Having Theorem
1.5
, it remains to solve (
1.5
) for compactly supported initial data
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
. This can be done by a variety of methods, both theoretically and numerically. In particular, the standard IST approach works in this case, but accuracy estimates for numerical schemes based on IST and
\ell^{2}
-bounds are missed in the literature. Taking into account the non-injectivity of NLFT, we see that the problem, in fact, is fairly nontrivial: some distant compactly supported data
q_{0}
,
\tilde{q}_{0}
correspond to almost identical reflection coefficients
{\bf r}_{q_{0}}
,
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{0}}
. Indeed, it is enough to take different
q_{0},\tilde{q}_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
with the same reflection coefficient and consider restrictions of
q_{0}
,
\tilde{q}_{0}
to a large discrete interval
[-N,N]
. Then the corresponding reflection coefficients will almost coincide by continuity of NLFT. This phenomenon, when ignored, leads to instabilities. Below we describe a procedure that can be used to get the solution with prescribed accuracy.

Consider
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
supported on
\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,\ell]
for some
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Note that
q
solves (
1.5
) if and only if
q(t,\cdot+j)
solves (
1.5
) for the initial datum
q_{0}(\cdot+j)
. Therefore, we do not loss generality when assuming
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits q_{0}\subset[0,\ell]
. Moreover, it is easy to see that
q(t,n)
solves (
1.5
) if and only if
-q(-t,n)
solves (
1.5
) with the initial data
-q_{0}
. So, we can also assume that
t>0
.

Consider the Fourier expansion of the inverse scattering multiplier
e^{it(z+1/z)}
:

(1.9)

\displaystyle e^{it(z+1/z)}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}i^{k}J_{k}(2t)z^{k},\qquad z\in\mathbb{T}.

Here,
J_{k}
are the standard Bessel functions
[
6
]
of order
k
, i.e.,

\displaystyle J_{k}(2t)=i^{-k}\int_{\mathbb{T}}e^{it(z+1/z)}\bar{z}^{k}\,dm=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{m}t^{2m+k}}{(m+k)!}.

Let
P_{n,t}=\sum_{|k|\leqslant n}i^{k}J_{k}(2t)z^{k}
be the Laurent trigonometric polynomial of
e^{it(z+1/z)}
of order
n
. Define the function
G_{n,t}
by

(1.10)

\displaystyle G_{n,t}=(1-\delta_{n,t})z^{n}P_{n,t},\quad\delta_{n,t}=\frac{t^{n}e^{t}}{n!}.

We will be interested in the situation when
n>ct
with some
c>e
. In this case this “
\delta_{n,t}
-correction” is very small but important: it places
G_{n,t}
into Schur class.
Given a sequence
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
supported on
[0,\ell]
, define the coefficients
a
,
b
of
q_{0}
by (
1.7
). Note that the product in (
1.7
) contains at most
\ell+1
nontrivial terms. One can check that
a
,
\bar{b}
in (
1.7
) coincide on
\mathbb{T}
with analytic polynomials in
z
of degree at most
\ell
, and, moreover,
|\bar{b}(z)|<|a(z)|
if
|z|\leqslant 1
. Set
{\bf f}_{q_{0}}=\bar{b}/a
.
The function
F_{n,0}=G_{n,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}
is rational and belongs to the Schur class
\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
(see Proposition
3.1
below). Fix
j\in\mathbb{Z}
and use Schur’s algorithm (
1.1
)
to find rational functions
F_{n,0}
,
F_{n,1}
,
F_{n,2},\ldots,F_{n,n+j},\ldots
(Schur iterates of
F_{n,0}
). Set

(1.11)

\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)=\begin{cases}F_{n,n+j}(0),&j\geqslant-n,\\
0&j<-n.\end{cases}

The following theorem shows that
\tilde{q}_{n}
approximates the solution
q
of (
1.5
) with very high accuracy.

Theorem 1.6
.

Let
t>0
, and let
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
be a sequence compactly supported on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Assume that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})\geqslant\eta
for some
\eta>0
. Then, the function
\tilde{q}_{n}
in (
1.11
) satisfies

(1.12)

\displaystyle|q(t,j)-\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)|\leqslant 2^{j}C(\eta,1/2)\frac{12e^{5t}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{2et}{n}\right)^{n},

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
such that
n+j\geqslant 0
,
n>t
, and
\delta_{n,t}<1
, see (
1.10
). Here
q
is the solution of (
1.5
) and
C(\eta,r)
is the function from Theorem
1.2
.

Note that the right hand side in (
1.12
) is very small when
n
is much larger than
2et
and
j
is fixed. The estimate does not depend on the size of the support of
q_{0}
. In fact, Theorem
1.6
remains true if we assume only
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits q_{0}\subset[0,+\infty)
and
\prod_{0}^{+\infty}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})>0
. In this case, it is known that the product in (
1.7
) converges in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
(see Section
6
) and defines coefficients
a
,
b
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover,
{\bf f}_{q_{0}}=\bar{b}/a
will coincide with non-tangential values of a function of Schur class
\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
. Then
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)
are well-defined by (
1.11
), and (
1.12
) will hold for them.

1.5.
AL: algorithm for Problem
1.4

Let us summarize the algorithm that solves Problem
1.4
based on Theorems
1.5
and
1.6
. At first, one need to choose a window
\Delta=[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]
where
N
is such that
|q(t,n_{0})-q_{N}(t,n_{0})|\leqslant\varepsilon/2
for the exact solution
q_{N}
with the truncated initial datum
q_{0,N}=\chi_{\Delta}q_{0}
. Then, one need to shift
q_{0,N}
by
n_{0}-N
to make it supported on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}\cap[0,2N]
and use the algorithm described in Section
1.4
to find the approximate solution
\tilde{q}_{n}
with accuracy
\varepsilon/2
at
j=N
for the shifted sequence. Taking
N=5+[4et+\log_{2}\frac{C(\eta,1/2)}{\varepsilon}]
,
n=2N
, we will get
|\tilde{q}_{n}(t,N)-q(t,n_{0})|\leqslant\varepsilon
, see Section
5
. In Section
5
we check that the whole procedure requires
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
operations for
\mathbf{n}=t+\log\varepsilon^{-1}
. In fact, the sequence
\tilde{q}_{n}
approximates
q
with accuracy
O(\varepsilon)
on the interval
[n_{0}-N/2,n_{0}]
, not only at the point
n_{0}
. Considering reflection of
q_{0}
and applying the algorithm twice, one can construct approximation to
q
on
[n_{0}-N/2,n_{0}+N/2]
in
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
operations.

, finite case

1.7.
The nonlinear Fourier transform

The last part of the paper can be regarded as the introduction to the theory of the nonlinear Fourier transform, NLFT. The main results in this area are due to Thiele and Tao, see the paper
[
22
]
or its extended version by Thiele, Tao, and Tsai
[
23
]
, where Ablowitz-Ladik equation appears in the setting of NLFT. Papers
[
22
]
,
[
23
]
influenced much on the present work. We decided to give a short introduction to the theory of NLFT in the language of Schur functions and orthogonal polynomials to make the paper more self-contained. We hope that our arguments will be of independent interest for the orthogonal polynomials community.

For
1\leqslant p<\infty
, let
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
be the set of sequences
q:\mathbb{\mathbb{Z}}\to\mathbb{D}
such that
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|q(n)|^{p}<\infty
. We endow it with the usual distance
\|q_{1}-q_{2}\|_{\ell^{p}}=\bigl(\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|q_{1}(n)-q_{2}(n)|^{p}\bigr)^{1/p}
. Note that
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
is not a linear space. Using formula (
1.7
), define the nonlinear Fourier transform (or the scattering map) by

\mathcal{F}_{sc}:q\mapsto{\bf r}_{q},

on the set
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
. Here we consider
\mathcal{F}_{sc}
as the map from
\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
to
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. Later on, the domain of
\mathcal{F}_{sc}
will be extended, while the target space will be changed to a narrower one. Define the metric space

(1.13)

\displaystyle X=\{h\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}):\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant 1,\;\log(1-|h|^{2})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})\},

with the Sylvester–Winebrenner metric
\rho_{s}
(see
[
20
]
) given by

(1.14)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(h_{1},h_{2})=\sqrt{-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log\left(1-\Bigl|\frac{h_{1}-h_{2}}{1-\bar{h}_{1}h_{2}}\Bigr|^{2}\right)\,dm}.

For
\delta\in[0,1)
, denote
B[\delta]=\{h\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T}):\;\|h\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\delta\}.
We have
B[\delta]\subset X
for every
\delta\in[0,1)
. So, let us consider
B[\delta]
as the subspace of
X
with induced metric topology. As we will see below,
\mathcal{F}_{sc}
uniquely extends to the continuous map from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
to
X
. Set
\mathcal{G}[\delta]=\mathcal{F}_{sc}^{-1}(B[\delta])

where
\mathcal{F}_{sc}^{-1}(E)
is the full preimage of a set
E
under the mapping
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})\to X
.

With this definitions at hand, we are ready to summarize the basic properties of
\mathcal{F}_{sc}
.

Theorem 1.7
.

The nonlinear Fourier transform
\mathcal{F}_{sc}
has the following properties:

(1)

the map
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})\to L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
extends uniquely to the continuous map
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})\to X
;

(2)

the map
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})\to X
is closed;

(3)

we have
\mathcal{F}_{sc}(q(\cdot-n))=z^{-n}\mathcal{F}_{sc}(q)
for every
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
;

(4)

the map
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})\to X
is surjective;

(5)

the map
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})\to X
is not injective;

(6)

the map
\mathcal{F}_{sc}:\mathcal{G}[\delta]\to B[\delta]
is a homeomorphism for every
\delta\in(0,1)
;

(7)

if
q=q(t,n)
is the solution of (
1.5
) with the initial datum
q_{0}\in\mathcal{G}[\delta]
, then
q(t,\cdot)\in\mathcal{G}[\delta]
for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
, and

q(t,\cdot)=\mathcal{F}_{sc}^{-1}(e^{it(z+1/z)}\mathcal{F}_{sc}(q_{0}))
.

Assertion
(2)
in Theorem
1.7
is new. It implies, in particular, that
\mathcal{F}_{sc}
is a homeomorphism on the set of potentials
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
that are completely determined by the reflection coefficient
{\bf r}_{q}
. Assertion
(7)
is not proved in our paper (we did not found a sufficiently short argument), see
[
23
]
for the proof. Some ideas in the proof of Theorem
1.7
are due to S. Denisov, the authors would like to thank him for his contribution.

2.
Schur’s algorithm. Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

In this section we prove Theorem
1.2
. For an analytic function
F
in
\mathbb{D}
, it will be convenient to set

M_{F}(r)=\max_{|z|\leqslant r}|F(z)|.

At first, we prove the following lemma.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
F\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
, and let
F_{k}
be its Schur iterates defined by (
1.1
). Then

(2.1)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)\leqslant\frac{4}{(1-r)^{2}}\cdot\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|F_{k}(0)|^{2}\leqslant\frac{4}{(1-r)^{2}}\cdot\log(\eta(F)^{-1}),\qquad r\in[0,1),

where
\eta(F)
is defined by (
1.2
).

Proof.
Let us check the second inequality first. For
x\in(0,1)
, we have
(1-x)^{-1}\geqslant e^{x}
, therefore

\displaystyle\eta(F)^{-1}=\prod_{k\geqslant 0}(1-|F_{k}(0)|^{2})^{-1}\geqslant\prod_{k\geqslant 0}e^{|F_{k}(0)|^{2}}=e^{\sum_{k\geqslant 0}|F_{k}(0)|^{2}},

which implies the required bound
\log(\eta(F)^{-1})\geqslant\sum_{k\geqslant 0}|F_{k}(0)|^{2}
. Now we focus on the first inequality in (
2.1
). Set
\alpha_{j}=F_{j}(0)
,
j\geqslant 0
. We will use the estimate
(1.3.58)
in
[
17
]
which reads

|F(z)|\leqslant 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{j}||z|^{j},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Applying it to
F_{k}
in place of
F
for
|z|=r
, we get
M_{F_{k}}(r)\leqslant 2\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{k+j}|r^{j}
, hence

\displaystyle M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)\leqslant 4\left(\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{k+j}|r^{j/2}\cdot r^{j/2}\right)^{2}\leqslant 4\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{k+j}|^{2}r^{j}\cdot\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}r^{j}=\frac{4}{1-r}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{k+j}|^{2}r^{j},

by Cauchy inequality.
Summing up over
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we get

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)=\frac{4}{1-r}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{k+j}|^{2}r^{j}

\displaystyle=\frac{4}{1-r}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{s}|^{2}\sum_{j=0}^{s}r^{j}\leqslant\frac{4}{(1-r)^{2}}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}|\alpha_{s}|^{2}.

This ends the proof. ∎

Remark 2.2
.

Lemma
2.1
holds with a better (for small
r
) estimate with
\frac{1}{(1-r)^{4}}
in place of
\frac{4}{(1-r)^{2}}
. To prove this, one need to use expression for
F_{k}
from Theorem 8.70 in
[
12
]
. A consideration of functions
F=\delta z^{n}
for large
n
’s and small
\delta
’s shows that the constant in Lemma
2.1
cannot be smaller than
\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
F,G\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
. We have

(2.2)

\displaystyle z(F_{1}-G_{1})=\frac{F-F(0)}{1-\overline{F(0)}F}-\frac{G-G(0)}{1-\overline{G(0)}G}=\frac{P}{Q}.

Here, the numerator is

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle=(F-F(0))(1-\overline{G(0)}G)-(G-G(0))(1-\overline{F(0)}F)

\displaystyle=\bigl[F-F(0)-G+G(0)\bigr]+FG(\overline{F(0)}-\overline{G(0)})+\bigl[F(0)\overline{G(0)}G-\overline{F(0)}G(0)F\bigr].

We have

\displaystyle F(0)\overline{G(0)}G-\overline{F(0)}G(0)F=

\displaystyle F(0)\overline{G(0)}(G-F)+FF(0)(\overline{G(0)}-\overline{F(0)})+

\displaystyle+F\overline{F(0)}(F(0)-G(0)).

It follows that

\displaystyle\|P\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant

\displaystyle\|F-F(0)-G+G(0)\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}+M_{F}(r)M_{G}(r)|F(0)-G(0)|+

\displaystyle+|F(0)||G(0)|\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}+2M_{F}(r)|F(0)||F(0)-G(0)|.

For an analytic function
H
in
\mathbb{D}
, we have

\displaystyle|H(0)|\leqslant\|H\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})},\quad\|H-H(0)\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\|H\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})},

by orthogonality of system
\{z^{k}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
. Applying this to
H=F-G
and using
2xy\leqslant x^{2}+y^{2}
, we get

\displaystyle\|P\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant

\displaystyle\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\left(1+M_{F}(r)M_{G}(r)+|F(0)||G(0)|+2M_{F}(r)|F(0)|\right),

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\left(1+\frac{3M_{F}(r)^{2}+M_{G}(r)^{2}+3|F(0)|^{2}+|G(0)|}{2}\right).

Since
|P|
remains the same when we swap
F
,
G
, we also have

\|P\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\left(1+\frac{M_{F}(r)^{2}+3M_{G}(r)^{2}+|F(0)|^{2}+3|G(0)|}{2}\right).

Taking a half-sum, we get

(2.3)

\|P\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\left(1+M_{F}^{2}(r)+M_{G}^{2}(r)+|F(0)|^{2}+|G(0)|^{2}\right).

Further, for
z\in r\mathbb{T}
, we estimate the denominator
Q
in (
2.2
) as follows:

\displaystyle|Q(z)|=|(1-\overline{G(0)}G)(1-\overline{F(0)}F)|\geqslant(1-|G(0)|M_{G}(r))(1-|F(0)|M_{F}(r)),

where we use the fact that both brackets above are positive. Substitution of the bounds for
P
,
Q
into (
2.2
) gives

\displaystyle r\|F_{1}-G_{1}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\frac{1+M_{F}^{2}(r)+M_{G}^{2}(r)+|F(0)|^{2}+|G(0)|^{2}}{(1-|G(0)|M_{G}(r))(1-|F(0)|M_{F}(r))}.

The latter inequality applied to
F_{k}
and
G_{k}
in place of
F
,
G
for
k=0,\ldots,n-1
implies

(2.4)

\displaystyle r^{n}\|F_{n}-G_{n}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\|F-G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}C_{k},

for

C_{k}=\frac{1+M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)+M_{G_{k}}^{2}(r)+|F_{k}(0)|^{2}+|G_{k}(0)|^{2}}{(1-|G_{k}(0)|M_{G_{k}}(r))(1-|F_{k}(0)|M_{F_{k}}(r))}.

It remains to estimate
\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}C_{k}
. For
\delta\in(0,1)
, denote by
c(\delta)
the minimal positive number such that
\frac{1}{1-x}\leqslant 1+c(\delta)x
for all
x\in(0,1)
satisfying
1-x^{2}>\delta
.
It is not difficult to check that

(2.5)

\displaystyle c(\delta)=\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-\delta}}\in\left[1,\;\frac{2}{\delta}\right].

Observe that

(2.6)

\displaystyle 1-|F_{k}(0)|^{2}M_{F_{k}}(r)^{2}\geqslant 1-|F_{k}(0)|^{2}\geqslant\prod_{m=0}^{+\infty}(1-|F_{m}(0)|^{2})=\eta(F)>\eta

by our assumption. Then,

\frac{1}{1-|F_{k}(0)|M_{F_{k}}(r)}\leqslant 1+c(\eta)|F_{k}(0)|M_{F_{k}}(r)\leqslant 1+\frac{c(\eta)}{2}(M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)+|F_{k}(0)|^{2}).

A similar estimate holds for functions
G_{k}
. It follows that

\displaystyle C_{k}\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)+M_{G_{k}}^{2}(r)+|F_{k}(0)|^{2}+|G_{k}(0)|^{2})

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(1+\frac{c(\eta)}{2}(M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)+|F_{k}(0)|^{2})\right)\left(1+\frac{c(\eta)}{2}(M_{G_{k}}^{2}(r)+|G_{k}(0)|^{2})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle\exp\left(\left(1+\frac{c(\eta)}{2}\right)(M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)+M_{G_{k}}^{2}(r)+|F_{k}(0)|^{2}+|G_{k}(0)|^{2})\right),

where we used the elementary inequality
1+x\leqslant e^{x}
three times.
Then, from Lemma
2.1
we get

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}C_{k}\leqslant\exp\left(\left(1+\frac{c(\eta)}{2}\right)\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{F_{k}}^{2}(r)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}M_{G_{k}}^{2}(r)+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|F_{k}(0)|^{2}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}|G_{k}(0)|^{2}\right)\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\exp\left(\left(1+\frac{c(\eta)}{2}\right)\left(\frac{8\log\eta^{-1}}{(1-r)^{2}}+2\log\eta^{-1}\right)\right).

Substitution of the latter into (
2.4
) and the bound (
2.5
) imply (
1.3
) with

(2.7)

\displaystyle C(\eta,r)=\exp\left(\log\eta^{-1}\cdot\left(2+\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-\eta}}\right)\left(\frac{4}{(1-r)^{2}}+1\right)\right).

This ends the proof. ∎

Remark 2.3
.

The function
C(\eta,r)
is very large if

is not close to
1
or if
r
is close to
1
.
We have, e.g.,
5\cdot 10^{27}\leqslant C(1/2,1/2)\leqslant 6\cdot 10^{27}
,

-initial data can be approximated by the solutions of equation (

3.
Estimates for the multipliers. Proof of Theorem
1.6

Recall the definition (
1.10
) of
G_{n,t}
and
P_{n,t}
:

(3.1)

\displaystyle P_{n,t}=\sum_{|k|\leqslant n}i^{k}J_{k}(2t)z^{k},\qquad G_{n,t}=(1-\delta_{n,t})z^{n}P_{n,t},\qquad\delta_{n,t}=\frac{t^{n}e^{t}}{n!},\qquad t>0.

In this section we first prove a bound for
G_{n,t}
and estimate the rate of convergence of
G_{n+1,t}-zG_{n,t}
to zero. Then we prove Theorem
1.6
. Throughout this section, we assume that
t>0
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
z\in\mathbb{T}
and let
n>t>0
be such that
\delta_{n,t}<1
for
\delta_{n,t}=\frac{t^{n}e^{t}}{n!}
from (
1.10
). Then we have
|G_{n,t}(z)|<1
. In particular, for every
q_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
with
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits q_{0}\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we have
G_{n,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
and the construction described in Section
1.4
is correct.

Proof.
We have

\displaystyle\left|P_{n,t}(z)-e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|=\left|\sum_{|k|>n}i^{k}J_{k}(2t)z^{k}\right|\leqslant 2\sum_{k>n}r^{-k}|J_{k}(2t)|,\qquad|z|=r.

The standard estimate (see, e.g., page 91 in
[
6
]
)
|J_{\nu}(2t)|\leqslant{|t|^{\nu}/\Gamma(\nu+1)}
implies

(3.2)

\displaystyle\left|P_{n,t}(z)-e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2\sum_{k>n}\frac{r^{-k}t^{k}}{k!}\leqslant\frac{2t^{n}r^{-n}e^{t/r}}{(n+1)!}\leqslant\frac{t^{n}r^{-n}e^{t/r}}{n!},

(3.3)

\displaystyle\left|P_{n,t}(z)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant\left|e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|+\frac{t^{n}r^{-n}e^{t/r}}{n!}.

In particular, for
z\in\mathbb{T}
this gives
|P_{n,t}(z)|\leqslant 1+\delta_{n,t}
, where
\delta_{n,t}=\frac{t^{n}e^{t}}{n!}
is from (
1.10
). Therefore, we have

\displaystyle|G_{n,t}(z)|=(1-\delta_{n,t})|P_{n,t}(z)|\leqslant 1-\delta_{n,t}^{2}<1,\qquad z\in\mathbb{T},

where the factor
(1-\delta_{n,t})
is positive by our assumption. For compactly supported
q_{0}
with
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits q_{0}\subset[0,\ell]
, it is not difficult to check that
{\bf f}_{q_{0}}
is a Schur function by considering partial products in (
1.7
) and using induction. For the general case, see formula (
6.20
) below. Then, we have
G_{n,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
by construction. ∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
n
,
t
be as in Lemma
3.1
. Then we have
\max_{|z|=r}|G_{n,t}|\leqslant e^{t/r}(r^{n}+3\delta_{n,t})
for
r\in(0,1)
, and, moreover,

\displaystyle\max_{|z|=r}\left|G_{n+1,t}(z)-zG_{n,t}(z)\right|\leqslant S_{n}(t,r),\quad S_{n}(t,r)=6\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}.

Proof.
Take
z\in\mathbb{D}
such that
|z|=r
. By (
1.10
) and (
3.3
), we have

\displaystyle\left|z^{-n}G_{n,t}(z)-P_{n,t}(z)\right|

\displaystyle=\delta_{n,t}|P_{n,t}(z)|\leqslant\delta_{n,t}\left(|e^{it(z+1/z)}|+\frac{t^{n}r^{-n}e^{t/r}}{n!}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{n,t}\left(e^{t(1/r-r)}+\frac{t^{n}r^{-n}e^{t/r}}{n!}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\delta_{n,t}\left(e^{t/r}+\delta_{n,t}r^{-n}e^{t/r}\right)=\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}(1+\delta_{n,t}r^{-n}).

Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\left|z^{-n}G_{n,t}(z)-e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|\leqslant\left|z^{-n}G_{n,t}(z)-P_{n,t}(z)\right|+\left|P_{n,t}(z)-e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|.

The last two estimates together with (
3.2
) imply

\displaystyle\left|z^{-n}G_{n,t}(z)-e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|\leqslant\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}(1+\delta_{n,t}r^{-n})+\frac{t^{n}r^{-n}e^{t/r}}{n!}\leqslant\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}(1+2r^{-n}).

This gives

\max_{|z|=r}|G_{n,t}|\leqslant r^{n}e^{t(1/r-r)}+3\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}\leqslant e^{t/r}(r^{n}+3\delta_{n,t}).

So, we have

\max_{|z|=r}\left|zG_{n,t}(z)-z^{n+1}e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|\leqslant\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}r^{n+1}(1+2r^{-n})=\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}(r^{n+1}+2r),

and

\max_{|z|=r}\left|G_{n+1,t}(z)-z^{n+1}e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|\leqslant\delta_{n+1,t}e^{t/r}r^{n+1}(1+2r^{-(n+1)})\leqslant\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}(r^{n+1}+2),

where we used the inequality
\delta_{n+1,t}\leqslant\delta_{n,t}
for
n>t>0
. It remains to write

\displaystyle\left|G_{n+1,t}(z)-zG_{n,t}(z)\right|\leqslant\left|G_{n+1,t}(z)-z^{n+1}e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|+\left|zG_{n,t}(z)-z^{n+1}e^{it(z+1/z)}\right|

and use the last two estimates. ∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For every
n>0
,
t>0
,
r\in(0,1)
, we have

\sum_{k\geqslant n}S_{k}(t,r)r^{-k}\leqslant 6\delta_{n,t}e^{2t/r}\cdot r^{-n}

For

\displaystyle\leqslant 6\delta_{n,t}e^{t/r}r^{-n}e^{t/r}=6\delta_{n,t}e^{2t/r}\cdot r^{-n}.

|G_{k,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}|\leqslant|{\bf f}_{q_{0}}|

\displaystyle\min(\eta(G_{k+1,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}),\eta(zG_{k,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}))\geqslant\eta({\bf f}_{q_{0}})\geqslant\eta

by Szegő theorem

the

(see (

are denoted by

. By Theorem
1.2
, we have

\displaystyle\|F_{k+1,k+1+j}-F_{k,k+j}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\|(G_{k+1,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}})_{k+1+j}-(G_{k,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}})_{k+j}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle=\|(G_{k+1,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}})_{k+1+j}-(zG_{k,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}})_{k+1+j}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(\eta,r)r^{-k-1-j}\|G_{k+1,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}-zG_{k,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}

\displaystyle\leqslant C(\eta,r)r^{-k-1-j}\|G_{k+1,t}-zG_{k,t}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}.

Using Lemma
3.2
for
n_{1}>t>0
such that
\delta_{n_{1},t}<1
, we can proceed as follows:

\displaystyle\|F_{k+1,k+1+j}-F_{k,k+j}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}\leqslant C(\eta,r)r^{-k-j-1}\max_{|z|=r}|G_{k+1,t}-zG_{k,t}|\leqslant C(\eta,r)S_{k}(t,r)r^{-k-j-1}.

From Lemma
3.3
we now see that

\displaystyle|\tilde{q}_{n_{2}}(t,j)-\tilde{q}_{n_{1}}(t,j)|

\displaystyle\leqslant r^{-j-1}C(\eta,r)\cdot\sum_{k=n_{1}}^{\infty}S_{k}(t,r)r^{-k}\leqslant 6C(\eta,r)\delta_{n_{1},t}e^{2t/r}\cdot r^{-n_{1}-j-1}.

Recall that
\delta_{n_{1},t}=\frac{e^{t}t^{n_{1}}}{n_{1}!}
decays very rapidly as
n_{1}\to\infty
, thus,
\{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)\}_{n\geqslant-j}
is a Cauchy sequence for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Denote its limit by
\tilde{q}(t,\cdot)
.
Letting
n_{1}=n
and taking the limit in as
n_{2}\to+\infty
, we obtain

\displaystyle|\tilde{q}(t,j)-\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)|\leqslant 6C(\eta,r)\delta_{n,t}e^{2t/r}\cdot r^{-n-j-1}.

Taking
r=1/2
(any other
r\in(0,1)
will do) and using the inequality
n!\geqslant\sqrt{2\pi n}(n/e)^{n}
, we get

\displaystyle|\tilde{q}(t,j)-\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)|\leqslant 6C(\eta,1/2)\frac{e^{t}t^{n}}{n!}e^{4t}2^{n+j+1}=2^{j}C(\eta,1/2)\frac{12e^{5t}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{2et}{n}\right)^{n},

where
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
,
j\in\mathbb{Z}
,
t>0
are such that
n+j\geqslant 0
,
n>t>0
, and
\delta_{n,t}<1
.

It remains to show that

\tilde{q}(t,j)=q(t,j)
, i.e.,
\tilde{q}
solves Ablowitz-Ladik equation (
1.5
) with the initial datum
q_{0}
. By assertions
(6)
,
(7)
of Theorem
1.7
it is suffices to check that
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}}={\bf r}_{q}
, equivalently,
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}}=e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q_{0}}
.

Note that
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot-n)
is supported on
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, moreover, we have

\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j-n)=F_{n,j}(0)
for
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Let us denote the coefficients in (
1.7
) for
q_{0}
,
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot-n)
, by
a
,
b
, and
a_{n,0}
,
b_{n,0}
, respectively. We have
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot-n)}=b_{n,0}/a_{n,0}
and
F_{n,0}={\bf f}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot-n)}=\overline{b_{n,0}}/a_{n,0}
, where equality
F_{n,0}={\bf f}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot-n)}
holds by Lemma
3.4
because the recurrence coefficients of
F_{n,0}
coincide with the sequence
\{\tilde{q}(t,j-n)\}_{j\geqslant 0}
. By (
3.2
), we also have

\displaystyle z^{-n}\overline{b_{n,0}}/a_{n,0}=z^{-n}F_{n,0}=z^{-n}G_{n,t}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}\to e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf f}_{q_{0}}=e^{it(z+1/z)}\bar{b}/a

uniformly on
\mathbb{T}
. We now will use well-known properties of coefficients
a
,
b
in (
1.7
).
Namely, the functions
a
,
a_{n,0}
are outer, have positive values at
z=0
, and satisfy
|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=1
,
|a_{n,0}|^{2}-|b_{n,0}|^{2}=1
on
\mathbb{T}
(for the proof, see Section
6
). Convergence
z^{-n}\overline{b_{n,0}}/a_{n,0}\to e^{it(z+1/z)}\bar{b}/a
then implies
|a_{n,0}|^{2}\to|a|^{2}
,
\log|a_{n,0}|^{2}\to\log|a|^{2}
uniformly on
\mathbb{T}
, hence
a_{n,0}\to a
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
by properties of outer functions (more precisely, by the weak continuity of the Hilbert transform, see a discussion next to formula (
6.30
)). It follows that
z^{n}b_{n,0}\to e^{it(z+1/z)}b
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Therefore,

(3.4)

{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot)}=z^{n}{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot-n)}=z^{n}b_{n,0}/a_{n,0}\to e^{it(z+1/z)}b/a=e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q(t,\cdot)},

in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
(the first equality in (
3.4
) is assertion
(3)
of Theorem
1.7
).
On the other hand, as
n\to+\infty
the quantities

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot)}|^{2})\,dm=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(|a_{n,0}|^{-2})\,dm=\log|a_{n,0}(0)|^{-2}

tend to

\log|a(0)|^{-2}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|{\bf r}_{q}|^{2})\,dm=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q}|^{2})\,dm.

Then, taking into account (
3.4
), we see that
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot)}\to e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q}
in the metric space
X
by Proposition
6.10
. Moreover, the quantities
\esssup_{\mathbb{T}}(|{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot)}|^{2})=1-\esssup_{\mathbb{T}}|a_{n,0}|^{-2}
are uniformly separated from
1
because
a_{n,0}
converge uniformly on
\mathbb{T}
to the bounded function
a
. Then continuity of the inverse NLFT map (i.e., assertion
(6)
in Theorem
1.7
) gives us the convergence of
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot)
to
\mathcal{F}_{sc}^{-1}(e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q})
in a subspace
\mathcal{G}(\delta)
,
\delta\in(0,1)
, of the metric space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
. Since the sequence
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,\cdot)
converges elementwise to
\tilde{q}(t,\cdot)
as
n\to+\infty
, we get
\tilde{q}(t,\cdot)=\mathcal{F}_{sc}^{-1}(e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q})
on
\mathbb{Z}
. Then,
{\bf r}_{\tilde{q}(t,\cdot)}=\mathcal{F}_{sc}(\tilde{q}(t,\cdot))=e^{it(z+1/z)}{\bf r}_{q}

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
, and the proof is completed. ∎

Remark 3.5
.

In the proof of Theorem
1.6
, we have used the fact that (

) is solvable for compactly supported initial data. This can be proved by a variety of methods, see Appendix for a direct proof in a much more general situation. Assertions
(6)
,

in Theorem

.

Localization. Proof of Theorem
1.5

The following lemma is well-known, see, e.g., (1.3.43) in
[
17
]
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
F,G\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
, and let
F_{k}
,
G_{k}
be their Schur iterates (
1.1
). Assume that
F_{k}(0)=G_{k}(0)
for
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
. Then

\max_{|z|=r}|F(z)-G(z)|\leqslant 2r^{n+1}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
F,G\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
be such that
\min(\eta(F),\eta(G))\geqslant\eta
for some
\eta>0
. Denote by
F_{k}
,
G_{k}
their Schur iterates (
1.1
), and consider the solutions of (
1.5
) with the initial value

\displaystyle q_{0,F}=\begin{cases}F_{n}(0),&n\geqslant 0,\\
0,&n<0,\end{cases}\qquad q_{0,G}=\begin{cases}G_{n}(0),&n\geqslant 0,\\
0,&n<0.\end{cases}

Denote them by
q_{F}
and
q_{G}
, respectively.
Then for every
n>t>0
,
r\in(0,1)
, the inequality

\displaystyle|q_{F}(t,j)-q_{G}(t,j)|\leqslant r^{-j}e^{t/r}C(\eta,r)\sup_{|z|=r}|F(z)-G(z)|,

holds for all
j\in\mathbb{Z}
. Here
C(\eta,r)
is the function from Theorem
1.2
.

Proof.
For a function
H\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D})
, let us denote by
(H)_{k}
its Schur iterates (
1.1
). By Theorem
1.6
, we have

\displaystyle q_{F}(t,j)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(G_{n,t}F)_{n+j}(0),\qquad q_{G}(t,j)=\lim_{n\to\infty}(G_{n,t}G)_{n+j}(0),\qquad j\in\mathbb{Z}.

Therefore, we can apply Theorem
1.2
and the bound
|G_{n,t}|<e^{t/r}(r^{n}+3\delta_{n,t})
from Lemma
3.2
to get

\displaystyle|q_{F}(t,j)-q_{G}(t,j)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}|(G_{n}F)_{n+j}(0)-(G_{n}G)_{n+j}(0)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}\|(G_{n,t}F)_{n+j}-(G_{n,t}G)_{n+j}\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}

(4.1)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}C(\eta,r)r^{-n-j}\|G_{n,t}F-G_{n,t}G\|_{L^{2}(r\mathbb{T})}

(4.2)

\displaystyle\leqslant\limsup_{n\to\infty}C(\eta,r)r^{-j}e^{t/r}(1+3\delta_{n,t}r^{-n})\sup_{|z|=r}|F(z)-G(z)|

\displaystyle=r^{-j}e^{t/r}C(\eta,r)\sup_{|z|=r}|F(z)-G(z)|,

where we have used in (
4.2
) the convergence
\delta_{n,t}r^{-n}\to 0
as
n\to\infty
. ∎

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.
Recall that
q_{0}:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
is such that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})\geqslant\eta>0
, the sequence
q_{0,N}
is defined by

\displaystyle q_{0,N}(n)=\begin{cases}q_{0}(n),&|n|\leqslant N,\\
0,&|n|>N,\end{cases}

and
q_{N}
is the corresponding solution of (
1.5
) (see Remark
3.5
). Let
C(\eta,r)
be the function from Theorem
1.2
. We want to prove the inequality

(4.3)

\displaystyle|q_{N+K}(t,j)-q_{N}(t,j)|\leqslant\frac{4e^{t/r}C(\eta,r)}{1-r}r^{N-|j|},\qquad K\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Then
\{q_{N}(t,j)\}
will be a Cauchy sequence for each
t,j
, and its limit, to be denoted by
q
, solves (
1.5
). This is easy to check if one rewrites (
1.5
) in the integral form. Estimate (
1.8
) will follow from (
4.3
) by taking the limit as
K\to+\infty
.

For integer numbers
A\leqslant B
, consider the sequences
q_{0,[A,B]}
,
\overleftarrow{q}_{0,[A,B]}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
defined by

\displaystyle q_{0,[A,B]}(j)=q_{0}(j+A)\mathbf{1}_{[0,B-A]}(j),\qquad\overleftarrow{q}_{0,[A,B]}(j)=q_{0}(-j+B)\mathbf{1}_{[0,B-A]}(j),

where
\mathbf{1}_{S}
is the indicator function of a set
S
. These sequences both supported on
[0,B-A]
and their entries are symmetric on this segment. Denote the corresponding solutions of (
1.5
) by
q_{[A,B]}
and
\overleftarrow{q}_{[A,B]}
. By properties of (
1.5
), the symmetry relation

(4.4)

\displaystyle q_{[A,B]}(t,j)=\overleftarrow{q}_{[A,B]}(t,B-A-j),\quad t\in\mathbb{R},\quad j\in\mathbb{Z},

holds for each
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Moreover, comparing this with the definition of
q_{N}
, we see that
q_{N}(t,j)=q_{[-N,N]}(t,j+N).
The inequality (
4.3
) will follow by summing up a telescoping series if we check the estimate

(4.5)

\displaystyle|q_{N}(t,j)-q_{N+1}(t,j)|\leqslant 4C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{N-|j|},\qquad N\geqslant|j|.

In the new notation the latter takes the form

(4.6)

\displaystyle|q_{[-N,N]}(t,j+N)-q_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,j+N+1)|\leqslant 4C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{N-|j|},\quad N\geqslant|j|.

For
A\leqslant B
let
f_{[A,B]}
and
\overleftarrow{f}_{[A,B]}
be the Schur functions which recurrence coefficients are
q_{0,[A,B]}\mid_{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and
\overleftarrow{q}_{0,[A,B]}\mid_{\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
respectively. The Schur functions
f_{[-N,N]}
,
f_{[-N,N+1]}
have the same first
2N+1
Schur coefficients. Hence by Lemmas
4.1
and
4.2
we get

\displaystyle\left|q_{[-N,N]}(t,n)-q_{[-N,N+1]}(t,n)\right|

\displaystyle\leqslant r^{-n}\cdot C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}\sup_{|z|=r}\left|f_{[-N,N]}(z)-f_{[-N,N+1]}(z)\right|

(4.7)

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{2N-n+1},

for all
n\geqslant 0
. Similarly, the functions
\overleftarrow{f}_{[-N,N+1]}
and
\overleftarrow{f}_{[-N-1,N+1]}
have coinciding first
2N+1
Schur coefficients, therefore

(4.8)

\displaystyle\left|\overleftarrow{q}_{[-N,N+1]}(t,n)-\overleftarrow{q}_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,n)\right|\leqslant 2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{2N-n+1},\qquad n\geqslant 0.

Notice that

|q_{[-N,N]}(t,n)-q_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,n+1)|\leqslant\\
\leqslant|q_{[-N,N]}(t,n)-q_{[-N,N+1]}(t,n)|+|q_{[-N,N+1]}(t,n)-q_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,n+1)|\leqslant\\
\leqslant 2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{2N-n+1}+|q_{[-N,N+1]}(t,n)-q_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,n+1)|.

By relation (
4.4
), the last term equals

\displaystyle|\overleftarrow{q}_{[-N,N+1]}(t,2N+1-n)-\overleftarrow{q}_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,2N+1-n)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{2N-(2N+1-n)+1}

\displaystyle=2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{n},

where we used (
4.8
) in the first inequality.
Therefore, we have
|q_{[-N,N]}(t,n)-q_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,n+1)|\leqslant 2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}(r^{2N-n+1}+r^{n}).

Substitution of
n=j+N
then gives

\displaystyle|q_{[-N,N]}(t,j+N)-q_{[-N-1,N+1]}(t,j+N+1)|

\displaystyle\leqslant 2C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}(r^{N-j+1}+r^{j+N})

\displaystyle\leqslant 4C(\eta,r)e^{t/r}r^{N-|j|},

which is (
4.6
).
∎

5.
Complexity of the algorithm

In the introduction, we claimed that the algorithm outlined in Section
1.5
takes
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
operations for
\mathbf{n}=t+\log\varepsilon^{-1}
. Here we prove this estimate.

Let
q_{0}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
be such that
\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{0}(n)|^{2})\geqslant\eta>0
, and let
t>0
. Take
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, set
r=1/2
, and choose
N\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
such that
the right hand side in (
1.8
) does not exceed
\varepsilon/2
at
j=0
:

8e^{2t}C(\eta,1/2)2^{-N}\leqslant\varepsilon/2,\qquad C(\eta,1/2)=\exp\left(17\log\eta^{-1}\cdot\left(2+\frac{1}{1-\sqrt{1-\eta}}\right)\right).

Since
8e^{2t}C(\eta,1/2)2^{-N}\leqslant 2^{-N+4+3t}C(\eta,1/2)/2
, one can take any
N\geqslant 5+[3t+\log_{2}\frac{C(\eta,1/2)}{\varepsilon}]
.
Then, choose the window
\Delta=[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]
, truncate
q_{0}
by setting
q_{0}=0
on
\mathbb{Z}\setminus\Delta
, and shift
q_{0}
by
n_{0}-N
to make it supported in
[0,2N]
. Denote the resulting sequence by
q_{0,[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]}
. Choose
n>t
so that
\delta_{n,t}<1
and

2^{j}C(\eta,1/2)\frac{12e^{5t}}{\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{2et}{n}\right)^{n}\leqslant\varepsilon/2,\quad j=N.

Since we already have
8e^{2t}C(\eta,1/2)2^{-N}\leqslant\varepsilon/2
, it suffices to choose
n
so that

2^{2N}e^{3t}\frac{12}{8\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{2et}{n}\right)^{n}\leqslant 1.

For
n\geqslant 2N\geqslant 8et\geqslant 5t
, we have

2^{2N}e^{3t}\frac{12}{8\sqrt{2\pi n}}\left(\frac{2et}{n}\right)^{n}\leqslant 2^{2N+5t}\left(\frac{2et}{n}\right)^{n}\leqslant 2^{5t}\left(\frac{2et}{N}\right)^{2N}\leqslant\left(\frac{4et}{N}\right)^{2N}\leqslant 1,

therefore, one can take
n=2N
,
N=5+[4et+\log_{2}\frac{C(\eta,1/2)}{\varepsilon}]
. Note that with this choice

\delta_{n,t}=\frac{t^{n}e^{t}}{n!}\leqslant\left(\frac{te}{n}\right)^{n}e^{t}\leqslant\left(\frac{te^{2}}{n}\right)^{n}\leqslant\left(\frac{8et}{2N}\right)^{n}<1.

We see that for
n=2N
,
N=5+[4et+\log_{2}\frac{C(\eta,1/2)}{\varepsilon}]
, Theorem
1.6
applied to
q_{0,[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]}
in place of
q_{0}
will give a sequence
\tilde{q}_{n}
approximating the corresponding solution
q_{[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]}
with accuracy
|\tilde{q}_{n}(t,N)-q_{[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]}(t,N)|\leqslant\varepsilon/2
. Then
|\tilde{q}_{n}(t,N+1)-q(t,n_{0})|<\varepsilon
and it remains to estimate the number of operations that are needed to construct
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,N)
from
q_{0}
for
n=2N
.

Having
q_{0}
,
t_{0}
,
n_{0}
,
\varepsilon
,
\eta
, we set
N=5+[4et+\log_{2}\frac{C(\eta,1/2)}{\varepsilon}]
and define array
q_{0,[n_{0}-N,n_{0}+N]}
of
2N+1
elements. Then we use formula (
1.7
) to find
a
,
b
. This can be done either by a direct multiplication of
2N+1
matrices in
O(N^{2})
operations or by using a dyadic divide-and-conquer multiplication algorithm together with the fast Fourier transform (FFT) in
O(N\log^{2}N)
operations. Next, define coefficients of polynomials
P=G_{n,t}\bar{b}
,
Q=a
(two arrays of length
2n+1+2N+1
,
2N+1
, respectively). This takes
O(N^{2})
operations in naive realization of multiplications of polynomials or
O(N\log N)
operations with FFT. Taking
n+N+1
steps of Schur’s algorithm for
P/Q
, we find
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,j)
on
[0,N]
, which solves the problem. Straightforward realization of Schur’s algorithm based on its definition requires
O(N^{2})=O(\mathbf{n}^{2})
operations (recall that
\mathbf{n}=\log\varepsilon^{-1}+t
). It could be fastened up to
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
operations with more delicate realization, see Section 2.2 in
[
4
]
. Notice that the numerical experiments in
[
4
]
use arithmetic of real numbers, while complexity estimate
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
given on page 192 in
[
4
]
holds for complex data. As reader can see from the algorithm, the same
O(\mathbf{n}\log^{2}\mathbf{n})
operations (with worsted constant) are sufficient to find
\tilde{q}_{n}(t,N)
on
[0,2N]
and approximate
q(t,\cdot)
with accuracy
\varepsilon
on the interval
[n_{0}-N/2,n_{0}+N/2]
, not only at the point
n_{0}
. It is also worth mentioning that the question of numerical stability (in our case – estimating round-off errors and taking into account issues related to arithmetic of long numbers) deserves a special consideration, it does not treated neither in
[
4
]
nor in this paper.

6.
The nonlinear Fourier transform. Proof of Theorem
1.7

In this section we collect some basic facts about the nonlinear Fourier transform (NLFT). Some of them were used in the first part of the paper. The reader can find more information in the preprint
[
22
]
or in its extended version
[
23
]
.

The exposition in this section is independent from the first part of the paper. Let us recall the definition of the NLFT map for the reader’s convenience. For
p\geqslant 1
, define
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
as a set of sequences
q\colon\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{D}
satisfying
|q(n)|<1
for every
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}|q(n)|^{p}<\infty
. The set
\ell^{p}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
is defined similarly with
\mathbb{Z}_{+}=\mathbb{Z}\cap[0,+\infty)
. Take a sequence
q\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
and define
a
,
b
by

(6.1)

\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix}=\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q(k)|^{2}}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q(k)}z^{-k}\\
q(k)z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\in\mathbb{T}.

Here, the product
\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}T_{k}
of matrices
T_{k}
is understood as the limit

\lim_{n\to+\infty}T_{-n}T_{-n+1}\cdot\ldots\cdot T_{n-1}T_{n}
. Assumption
q\in\ell^{1}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
guarantees that the product converges uniformly on
\mathbb{T}
.
We will see in Section
6.2
that the product in (
6.1
) has the form
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&\bar{a}\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some
a
,
b
. The authors of
[
22
]
define NLFT as the map that sends
q
to the pair
\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\end{matrix}\right)
. We will use an equivalent definition and consider the so-called reflection coefficient
{\bf r}_{q}=\frac{b}{a}
in place of
\left(\begin{matrix}a&b\end{matrix}\right)
. So, in our case, NLFT takes
q
into
{\bf r}_{q}
. In the next two subsections we define the reflection coefficient as an object of the theory of orthogonal polynomials on the unit circle. We also prove equivalence of the two definitions of NLFT map.

6.1.
Szegő measures and Szegő functions

Let
\mu
be a probability measure supported on an infinite subset of the unit circle
\mathbb{T}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z|=1\}
of the complex plane,
\mathbb{C}
. For
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, denote by
\Phi_{n}
the monic orthogonal polynomial of degree
n
generated by
\mu
, and set
\Phi^{*}_{n}=z^{n}\overline{\Phi_{n}(1/\bar{z})}
. These polynomials satisfy the following relation:

(6.2)

\displaystyle\Phi_{n+1}=z\Phi_{n}-\bar{\alpha}_{n}\Phi_{n}^{*},\quad n\geqslant 0,\quad\Phi_{0}=1,

where the
recurrence coefficients
,
\alpha_{n}
,
n\geqslant 0
, lie in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\;|z|<1\}
. Conversely, any sequence
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}\subset\mathbb{D}
gives rise to a unique probability measure

\mu
on
\mathbb{T}
whose closed support
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\mu
contains infinitely many points. These two facts can be found in Section 1.7 of
[
17
]
. The Schur function
f
of a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}
is defined by

(6.3)

\frac{1+zf(z)}{1-zf(z)}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1+\bar{\xi}z}{1-\bar{\xi}z}\,d\mu(\xi),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

Notice that (
6.3
) provides a bijective correspondence between Schur functions and measures on
\mathbb{T}
. Taking the real part in both sides of this equality, we get

(6.4)

\frac{1-|zf(z)|^{2}}{|1-zf(z)|^{2}}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|1-\bar{\xi}z|^{2}}\,d\mu(\xi),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

From (
6.3
), (
6.4
), and Schwarz lemma we see that
f
indeed belongs to the Schur class
\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
, i.e., it is analytic in
\mathbb{D}
and satisfies
\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|\leqslant 1
. Recall that the Schur iterates of
f=f_{0}
are defined by

(6.5)

\displaystyle zf_{n+1}=\frac{f_{n}-f_{n}(0)}{1-\overline{f_{n}(0)}f_{n}},\qquad n\geqslant 0.

Geronimus theorem says that recurrence coefficients
\alpha_{n}
in (
6.2
) coincide with recurrence coefficients in Schur’s algorithm:
\alpha_{n}=f_{n}(0)
,
n\geqslant 0
. See Chapter 3 in
[
17
]
for the proof.

Let
\mu=w\,dm+\mu_{\mathbf{s}}
be the Radon-Nikodym decomposition of
\mu
into the absolutely continuous and singular parts, where
m
is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
normalized by
m(\mathbb{T})=1
. Denote by
\{\alpha_{n}\}
the set of recurrence coefficients of the measure
\mu
and let
f
be its Schur function. An extended version of Szegő theorem (Theorem
1.1
) says that conditions
\log w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,
\log(1-|f|^{2})\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
,
\{\alpha_{n}\}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
are equivalent, and, moreover,

(6.6)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log w(\xi)\,dm(\xi)=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|f(\xi)|^{2})\,dm(\xi)=\log\prod_{n\geqslant 0}(1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2}).

It is not difficult to see that the three quantities in (
6.6
) are defined for any triple
\mu
,
f
,
\{\alpha_{n}\}
, but could be
-\infty
. In fact, Szegő theorem implies that quantities in (
6.6
) are finite (i.e.,
>-\infty
) or not simultaneously. Measures of Szegő class

\mathrm{Sz}(\mathbb{T})=\bigl\{\mu=w\,dm+\mu_{\mathbf{s}}:\;\mu(\mathbb{T})=1,\;\log w\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})\bigr\}

and their orthogonal polynomials have many interesting properties that constitute rich Szegő theory. We will use its part related to a discrete scattering. For this we will need the notion of the dual orthogonality measure, the Szegő function, and the dual Szegő function.

Consider a probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{T}
with infinite support. Let, as before,
f
denote the Schur function of
\mu
. The dual measure
\mu_{d}
is defined as the probability measure on
\mathbb{T}
corresponding to the Schur function
-f
:

(6.7)

\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1+\bar{\xi}z}{1-\bar{\xi}z}\,d\mu_{d}(\xi)=\frac{1+zf_{d}(z)}{1-zf_{d}(z)}=\frac{1-zf(z)}{1+zf(z)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

It is not difficult to check that if
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is the sequence of recurrence coefficients of
\mu
, then
\{-\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
is the sequence of recurrence coefficients of
\mu_{d}
.
Monic orthogonal polynomials for
\mu_{d}
will be denoted by
\Psi_{n}
. We also will need the normalized orthogonal polynomials for
\mu
and
\mu_{d}
:

(6.8)

\varphi_{n}=\frac{\Phi_{n}}{\|\Phi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}},\qquad\varphi^{*}_{n}=\frac{\Phi^{*}_{n}}{\|\Phi^{*}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}},\quad\psi_{n}=\frac{\Psi_{n}}{\|\Psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{d})}},\qquad\psi^{*}_{n}=\frac{\Psi^{*}_{n}}{\|\Psi^{*}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{d})}}.

In fact,

(6.9)

\|\Phi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\|\Phi^{*}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}^{2}=\|\Psi_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{d})}^{2}=\|\Psi^{*}_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{d})}^{2}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(1-|\alpha_{k}|^{2}),

for all
n\geqslant 1
, see Chapter 3.2 in
[
17
]
. The Szegő function,
D_{\mu}
, of a measure
\mu=w\,dm+\mu_{\mathbf{s}}
from Szegő class
\mathrm{Sz}(\mathbb{T})
is the outer function in the open unit disk
\mathbb{D}
such that
D_{\mu}(0)>0
and
|D_{\mu}|^{2}=w
Lebesgue almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
in the sense of nontangential boundary values. It could be defined by the formula

(6.10)

\displaystyle D_{\mu}(z)=\exp\left(\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1+\bar{\xi}z}{1-\bar{\xi}z}\log w(\xi)\,dm(\xi)\right),\qquad z\in\mathbb{D}.

It follows from the Szegő theorem (see (
6.6
)) that
\mu\in\mathrm{Sz}(\mathbb{T})
if and only if
\mu_{d}\in\mathrm{Sz}(\mathbb{T})
. We will denote the Szegő function of
\mu_{d}
by
D_{\mu_{d}}
.
It is known that
\varphi_{n}^{*}\to D_{\mu}^{-1}
,
\psi_{n}^{*}\to D_{\mu_{d}}^{-1}
as
n\to\infty
in
\mathbb{D}
and

(6.11)

\displaystyle\frac{1+zf}{1-zf}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\Psi_{n}^{*}(z)}{\Phi_{n}^{*}(z)}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\psi_{n}^{*}(z)}{\varphi_{n}^{*}(z)}=\frac{D_{\mu_{d}}^{-1}(z)}{D_{\mu}^{-1}(z)},\qquad z\in\mathbb{D},

see Theorem 2.4.1 and Chapter 3.2 in
[
17
]
. In particular, we have

(6.12)

\Re\left(D_{\mu_{d}}^{-1}\overline{D_{\mu}^{-1}}\right)=\Re\left(\frac{D_{\mu_{d}}^{-1}}{D_{\mu}^{-1}}\right)|D_{\mu}|^{-2}=\frac{1-|zf|^{2}}{|1-zf|^{2}}|D_{\mu}|^{-2}=w|D_{\mu}|^{-2}=1

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
in the sense of non-tangential boundary values.

6.2.
Reflection coefficients

Let us now define a reflection coefficient of a sequence
q
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
. To simplify notation, we set
q_{n}=q(n)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
. Consider the sequences
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
and
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
defined by
\alpha_{n}=q_{n}
, for
n\geqslant 0
and
\beta_{0}=0
,
\beta_{n}=-\overline{q_{-n}}
for
n\geqslant 1
,

(6.13)

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}&&&&\alpha_{0}&\alpha_{1}&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{3}&\ldots\\
\ldots&q_{-3}&q_{-2}&q_{-1}&q_{0}&q_{1}&q_{2}&q_{3}&\ldots\\
\ldots&\beta_{3}&\beta_{2}&\beta_{1}&0.&&&\end{matrix}

Define the measures
\mu^{+}
,
\mu^{-}
with the recurrence coefficients
\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
,
\{\beta_{n}\}_{n\geqslant 0}
, respectively. Let also
\mu_{d}^{\pm}
be the dual measures corresponding to
\mu^{\pm}
.
Define the Wall analytic functions in
\mathbb{D}
by

(6.14)

\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}=\frac{D_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}^{-1}+D_{\mu^{\pm}}^{-1}}{2},\qquad\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}=\frac{D_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}^{-1}-D_{\mu^{\pm}}^{-1}}{2z}.

The fact that
D^{-1}_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}(0)=D^{-1}_{\mu^{\pm}}(0)
follows from (
6.11
).
Using (
6.12
), we obtain
|\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}|^{2}-|\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}|^{2}=\Re\left(D_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}^{-1}\overline{D_{\mu^{\pm}}^{-1}}\right)=1
Lebesgue almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
in the sense of non-tangential boundary values. Also, we have

(6.15)

\frac{1+z\frac{\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}}{\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}}}{1-z\frac{\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}}{\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}}}=\frac{D_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}^{-1}(z)}{D_{\mu^{\pm}}^{-1}(z)}=\frac{1+zf^{\pm}}{1-zf^{\pm}},

for the Schur functions
f^{\pm}
of
\mu^{\pm}
, hence
f^{\pm}=\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}/\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}
. On
\mathbb{T}
, we set

(6.16)

a=\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{-}-\mathfrak{b}^{+}\mathfrak{b}^{-},\qquad b=\mathfrak{a}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{+}}-\mathfrak{b}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}.

Below we will use the fact that
a
is defined by (
6.16
) not only on
\mathbb{T}
but also in
\mathbb{D}
and is analytc there. Note that

|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}=(|\mathfrak{a}^{+}|^{2}-|\mathfrak{b}^{+}|^{2})(|\mathfrak{a}^{-}|^{2}-|\mathfrak{b}^{-}|^{2})=1

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. Next, define the reflection coefficient,
{\bf r}_{q}
, of the sequence
q=\{\alpha_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
by

(6.17)

{\bf r}_{q}=\frac{b}{a}.

It is possible to associate with
q
an operator on
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})\oplus\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z})
in a way that will place the reflection coefficient
{\bf r}_{q}
into the setting of a discrete scattering theory, see
[
23
]
. Our first proposition collects the properties of objects defined in the present section.

Proposition 6.1
.

For every
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
the functions
a,\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}
are outer,
a(0)>0
. The reflection coefficient
{\bf r}_{q}=b/a
of
q
belongs to the unit ball of
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. It is completely determined by
b
, and, conversely, it determines the pair
a
,
b
uniquely.

Proof.
By definition and (
6.11
), we have

(6.18)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}=\frac{1}{2}D_{\mu}^{-1}\cdot\left(1+\frac{1+zf^{\pm}}{1-zf^{\pm}}\right),\qquad a=\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{-}\left(1-\mathfrak{b}^{+}\mathfrak{b}^{-}/\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{-}\right)=\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{-}\left(1-f^{+}f^{-}\right).

We know that
\tfrac{1}{2}D_{\mu}^{-1}
is outer,
1+\frac{1+zf^{\pm}}{1-zf^{\pm}}
and
1-f^{+}f^{-}
are analytic in
\mathbb{D}
and have positive real part hence they are also outer, see Corollary 4.8 in
[
7
]
. Therefore
\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}
,
a
are outer as the products of outer functions. Next,
D_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}(0)=D_{\mu^{\pm}}(0)>0
hence
\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}(0)
are real and positive. We have
\beta_{0}=0
, therefore
f^{-}(0)=0
(recall Schur’s algorithm (
1.1
)) and
\mathfrak{b}^{-}(0)=0
. Thus
a(0)=\mathfrak{a}^{+}(0)\mathfrak{a}^{-}(0)>0
. From (
6.17
) we have

(6.19)

1-|{\bf r}_{q}|^{2}=\frac{|a|^{2}-|b|^{2}}{|a|^{2}}=\frac{1}{|a|^{2}}\geqslant 0

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. In particular,
{\bf r}_{q}
belongs to the unit ball of
L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
. We proved that
a
is outer hence it is completely defined by
|a|
.
Therefore, knowing the coefficient
b
, one can recover
|a|=\sqrt{1+|b|^{2}}
and
a
.
In particular, the numerator
b
determines the whole fraction
{\bf r}_{q}=b/a
. Conversely, if the function
{\bf r}_{q}
is given, then
|a|
is defined by (
6.19
), hence the pair
a
,
b
could be found from the fraction
{\bf r}_{q}=b/a
. ∎

Next proposition shows that (
6.1
) has sense for all
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
, and, moreover, the definitions of
a
,
b
in (
6.16
), (
6.1
) are equivalent.

Proposition 6.2
.

For every
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
, the product in (
6.1
) converges in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, the functions
a
,
b
in (
6.1
) coincide with those in (
6.16
).

Proof.
Denote by
\Phi_{\pm,n}
,
\Psi_{\pm,n}
the monic orthogonal polynomials of
\mu^{\pm}
and
\mu^{\pm}_{d}
, and let
\varphi_{\pm,n}
,
\psi_{\pm,n}
be the corresponding normalized polynomials, see (
6.8
). For each
n\geqslant 0
,
z\in\mathbb{T}
, we have

\displaystyle\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{\alpha_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
\alpha_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\psi_{+,n+1}^{*}+\varphi_{+,n+1}^{*}}{2}&\overline{\frac{\psi^{*}_{+,n+1}-\varphi^{*}_{+,n+1}}{2z}}\\
\frac{\psi^{*}_{+,n+1}-\varphi^{*}_{+,n+1}}{2z}&\overline{\frac{\psi_{+,n+1}^{*}+\varphi_{+,n+1}^{*}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}.

The proof is a routine verification of the identity

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\psi_{+,n+1}^{*}+\varphi_{+,n+1}^{*}}{2}&\overline{\frac{\psi^{*}_{+,n+1}-\varphi^{*}_{+,n+1}}{2z}}\\
\frac{\psi^{*}_{+,n+1}-\varphi^{*}_{+,n+1}}{2z}&\overline{\frac{\psi_{+,n+1}^{*}+\varphi_{+,n+1}^{*}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\frac{\psi_{+,n}^{*}+\varphi_{+,n}^{*}}{2}&\overline{\frac{\psi^{*}_{+,n}-\varphi^{*}_{+,n}}{2z}}\\
\frac{\psi^{*}_{+,n}-\varphi^{*}_{+,n}}{2z}&\overline{\frac{\psi_{+,n}^{*}+\varphi_{+,n}^{*}}{2}}\end{pmatrix}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{n}|^{2}}}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{\alpha_{n}}\bar{z}^{n}\\
\alpha_{n}z^{n}&1\end{pmatrix},

using relations (
6.2
) and (
6.9
).
It is known that
\varphi_{\pm,n}^{*}\to D_{\mu^{\pm}}^{-1}
,
\psi_{\pm,n}^{*}\to D_{\mu_{d}^{\pm}}^{-1}
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
, see (2.4.34) in
[
17
]
. Therefore, we have

(6.20)

\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
q_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\alpha_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{\alpha_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
\alpha_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}^{+}&\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{+}}\\
\mathfrak{b}^{+}&\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}\end{pmatrix},

where the product converges in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
.
Recall that
\beta_{k}=-\overline{q_{-k}}
for
k\geqslant 1
,
\beta_{0}=0
. We have

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{k=-n}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=-n}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
q_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=0}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\beta_{k}|^{2}}}\begin{pmatrix}1&-\beta_{k}z^{k}\\
-\overline{\beta_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k=0}^{n}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|\beta_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=0}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta_{k}z^{k}\\
\overline{\beta_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}.

Note that for each
k\geqslant 0
we have

j_{0}\begin{pmatrix}1&\beta_{k}z^{k}\\
\overline{\beta_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}j_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{\beta_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
\beta_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix},\qquad j_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\\
\end{pmatrix},

and
\{\beta_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 0}
coincides with the sequence of recurrence coefficients of
\mu^{-}
.
So, we obtain

j_{0}\left(\prod_{k=-n}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=-n}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
q_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}\right)^{-1}j_{0}\to\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}^{-}&\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{-}}\\
\mathfrak{b}^{-}&\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{-}}\end{pmatrix},

where the convergence is in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Taking the inverses (note that
|\mathfrak{a}^{-}|^{2}-|\mathfrak{b}^{-}|^{2}=1
from the consideration of determinants), we obtain

\prod_{k=-\infty}^{-1}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=-\infty}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
q_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}=j_{0}\begin{pmatrix}\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{-}}&-\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{-}}\\
-\mathfrak{b}^{-}&\mathfrak{a}^{-}\end{pmatrix}j_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}^{-}&-\mathfrak{b}^{-}\\
-\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{-}}&\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{-}}\end{pmatrix}.

Eventually, we get

(6.21)

\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-|q_{k}|^{2}}}\cdot\prod_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q_{k}}\bar{z}^{k}\\
q_{k}z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}^{-}&-\mathfrak{b}^{-}\\
-\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{-}}&\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{-}}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}^{+}&\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{+}}\\
\mathfrak{b}^{+}&\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\overline{b}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix},

with
a=\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{-}-\mathfrak{b}^{+}\mathfrak{b}^{-}
,
b=\mathfrak{a}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{+}}-\mathfrak{b}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}
, as claimed. ∎

We can now prove Lemma
3.4
from Section
3
.

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.
Propositions
6.2
,
6.1
imply that the definitions of
a
,
b
in (
1.7
) and (
6.16
) are equivalent. Note that for
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
with
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits q\subset\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we have
\mathfrak{a}^{-}=1,\mathfrak{b}^{-}=0
hence

{\bf f}_{q}=\frac{\bar{b}}{a}=\frac{\mathfrak{b}_{+}}{\mathfrak{a}_{+}}=f^{+}.

In particular, the recurrence coefficients of
{\bf f}_{q}
coincide with those of
f^{+}
,
\mu^{+}
, i.e., with the sequence
\{q(k)\}_{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. ∎

Proposition 6.3
.

We have
{\bf r}_{q(\cdot-n)}=z^{-n}{\bf r}_{q}
for every compactly supported
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.
We have

\displaystyle\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q(k-n)}z^{-k}\\
q(k-n)z^{k}&1\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&z^{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q(k-n)}z^{-(k-n)}\\
q(k-n)z^{k-n}&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&z^{-n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&z^{n}\end{pmatrix}\left[\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\begin{pmatrix}1&\overline{q(k-n)}z^{-(k-n)}\\
q(k-n)z^{k-n}&1\end{pmatrix}\right]\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&z^{-n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&z^{n}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
\bar{b}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&z^{-n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a&bz^{-n}\\
\bar{b}z^{n}&\bar{a}\end{pmatrix}.

Hence
{\bf r}_{q(\cdot-n)}=bz^{-n}/a=z^{-n}{\bf r}_{q}
by (
6.17
) and Proposition
6.2
. ∎

Proposition 6.4
.

There are
q_{1}\neq q_{2}
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
such that
{\bf r}_{q_{1}}={\bf r}_{q_{2}}
.

Proof.
Following
[
22
]
, let us consider an imaginary-valued function
\mathfrak{b}
on
\mathbb{T}
of Smirnov class in the unit disk. One can take, say,
\mathfrak{b}=\frac{1+z}{1-z}
. Let
\mathfrak{a}
be the outer function in
\mathbb{D}
such that
|\mathfrak{a}|^{2}-|\mathfrak{b}|^{2}=1
almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. The function
f=\mathfrak{b}/\mathfrak{a}
is a Schur function of Szegő class. Indeed,
\log(1-|f|^{2})=\log|a|^{-2}
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Therefore, we can define the sequences
q_{1},\tilde{q}_{2}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
by

\displaystyle\begin{matrix}n:\phantom{=}&\ldots&-3&-2&-1&0&1&2&\ldots&\\
q_{1}=(&\ldots&0,&0,&0,&f_{0}(0),&f_{1}(0),&f_{2}(0),&\ldots&)\\
\tilde{q}_{2}=(&\ldots&-\overline{f_{2}(0)},&-\overline{f_{1}(0)},&-\overline{f_{0}(0)},&0,&0,&0,&\ldots&).\end{matrix}

For these sequences, we have

\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{+}_{q_{1}}=\mathfrak{a},\;\;\mathfrak{b}^{+}_{q_{1}}=\mathfrak{b},\qquad\mathfrak{a}^{-}_{q_{1}}=1,\;\;\mathfrak{b}_{q_{1}}^{-}=0,\qquad\mathfrak{a}^{+}_{\tilde{q}_{2}}=1,\;\;\mathfrak{b}^{+}_{\tilde{q}_{2}}=0.

Furthermore, from the proof of Proposition
6.3
we obtain
\mathfrak{b}_{\tilde{q}_{2}}^{-}=z\mathfrak{b}
and
\mathfrak{a}^{-}_{\tilde{q}_{2}}=\mathfrak{a}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle a_{q_{1}}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot 1-\mathfrak{b}\cdot 0=\mathfrak{a},

\displaystyle b_{q_{1}}=1\cdot\overline{\mathfrak{b}}-0\cdot\overline{\mathfrak{a}}=\overline{\mathfrak{b}},

\displaystyle a_{\tilde{q}_{2}}=1\cdot\mathfrak{a}-0\cdot z\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{a},

\displaystyle b_{\tilde{q}_{2}}=\mathfrak{a}\cdot\overline{0}-z\mathfrak{b}\cdot\overline{1}=-z\mathfrak{b}.

Then

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. Note that

by Proposition

. Now set

. ∎

Proposition 6.5
.

For every

(6.22)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|{\bf r}_{q}|^{2})\,dm=-\log|a(0)|^{2}=\log\prod_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q(n)|^{2}).

Proof.

Proposition 6.6
.

For every
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
, the functions
\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}/{a}
,
\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}/{a}
belong to the unit ball of the Hardy class
H^{2}(\mathbb{D})
.

Proof.
Since
a
,
\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}
are outer in
\mathbb{D}
and
\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}
are in the Smirnov class (see (
6.14
)), we need to show only that
\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}/{a}
belong to the unit ball of
L^{2}(\mathbb{T})
. Denote, as before,
f^{\pm}=\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}/\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}
, and recall that
f^{\pm}
are Schur functions. The function

h=\frac{1-|f^{-}f^{+}|^{2}}{|1-f^{-}f^{+}|^{2}}=\Re\left(\frac{1+f^{-}f^{+}}{1-f^{-}f^{+}}\right)

is positive and harmonic in
\mathbb{D}
, therefore, it coincides with the Poisson integral of a finite positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover,
h
is equal to the density of the absolutely continuous part of that measure almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. Hence,
h\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
(we borrowed this trick from
[
22
]
) and

\displaystyle\|h\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}=\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{1-|f^{-}f^{+}|^{2}}{|1-f^{-}f^{+}|^{2}}\,dm=\Re\left(\frac{1+f^{-}(0)f^{+}(0)}{1-f^{-}(0)f^{+}(0)}\right)=1,

because
f^{-}(0)=0
. On the other hand, by (
6.18
) we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}|^{2}}=1-\frac{|\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}|^{2}}{|\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}|^{2}}=1-|f^{\pm}|^{2},\qquad\frac{1}{|a|^{2}}=\frac{(1-|f^{+}|^{2})(1-|f^{-}|^{2})}{|1-f^{+}f^{-}|^{2}}.

almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. It gives us

\left|\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}}{a}\right|^{2}=\frac{(1-|f^{+}|^{2})(1-|f^{-}|^{2})}{(1-|f^{\pm}|^{2})|1-f^{+}f^{-}|^{2}}=\frac{1-|f^{\mp}|^{2}}{|1-f^{+}f^{-}|^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1-|f^{-}f^{+}|^{2}}{|1-f^{-}f^{+}|^{2}}=h.

Therefore

, as claimed. ∎

Proposition 6.7
.

Suppose that
q_{1},q_{2}\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
are such that
{\bf r}_{q_{1}}={\bf r}_{q_{2}}
. If

\|{\bf r}_{q_{1,2}}\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})}<1
,

.

Proof.
Let
a_{1,2}
,

) corresponding to

,
{\bf r}_{q_{2}}
, respectively.
By Proposition

, we have

,

,

. Then (

) gives four identities

a=\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{+}\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{-}-\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{+}\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{-},\qquad b=\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{+}}-\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{+}},\qquad k=1,2,

for the functions
\mathfrak{a}_{1,2}^{\pm}
,
\mathfrak{b}_{1,2}^{\pm}
corresponding to

,

A simple algebra yields

\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{+}&\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{+}\\

-\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{+}&\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{-}\end{pmatrix}}=\begin{pmatrix}|\mathfrak{a}^{+}_{k}|^{2}-|\mathfrak{b}^{+}_{k}|^{2}&-\mathfrak{a}^{+}_{k}\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{-}_{k}}+\mathfrak{b}_{k}^{+}\overline{\mathfrak{a}_{k}^{-}}\\

almost everywhere on

\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{+}&\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{+}\\
\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}&\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{-}\end{pmatrix}\overline{\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{+}&-\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}\\

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{+}&\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{+}\\
\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{-}&\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{-}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{+}&\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{+}\\
\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}&\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{-}\end{pmatrix}=\overline{\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{+}&-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{-}\\
-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{+}&\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{-}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{+}&-\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}\\
-\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{+}&\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{-}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}},

(6.24)

\displaystyle I:=\frac{1}{a}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{-}&-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{+}\\
-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{-}&\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{+}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{+}&\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{+}\\
\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}&\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{-}\end{pmatrix}=\overline{\frac{1}{a}\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{+}&-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{-}\\
-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{+}&\mathfrak{a}_{2}^{-}\end{pmatrix}}\overline{\begin{pmatrix}\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{-}&\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}\\
\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{+}&\mathfrak{a}_{1}^{+}\end{pmatrix}}.

Equating the

\displaystyle\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{+}_{1}\mathfrak{a}^{-}_{2}-\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{-}\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{+}}{a}=\overline{\left(\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{+}_{2}\mathfrak{a}^{-}_{1}-\mathfrak{b}_{2}^{-}\mathfrak{b}_{1}^{+}}{a}\right)}.

It follows that

, which, in turn, is equivalent to

6.3.
Convergence in the space
X

We first prove a version of Sylvester–Winebrenner theorem
[
20
]
for Schur functions. Let us recall its statement.

Proposition 6.8

(Sylvester–Winebrenner theorem)
.

The mapping

f\mapsto\{f_{n}(0)\}_{n\geqslant 0}
that takes a Schur function into the sequence of its recurrence coefficients is a homeomorphism from the metric space
X_{+}=\{f\in\mathcal{S}_{*}(\mathbb{D}):\eta(F)>0\}

with the metric
\rho_{s}(f,g)^{2}=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log\bigl(1-\bigl|\frac{f-g}{1-\bar{f}g}\bigr|^{2}\bigr)\,dm
onto the metric space
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
of sequences
q:\mathbb{Z}_{+}\to\mathbb{D}
with the metric

\|q-\tilde{q}\|_{\ell^{2}}^{2}=\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}|q(n)-\tilde{q}(n)|^{2}
.

Proof.
Assume that
f_{n},f\in X_{+}
are such that
\rho_{s}(f_{n},f)\to 0
. Let
q_{n}
,
q
be the sequences of recurrence coefficients of
f_{n}
,
f
, respectively. By Szegő theorem, we have
q_{n},q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
, and, moreover,

-\log\prod_{k\geqslant 0}(1-|q_{n}(k)|^{2})=\rho_{s}(f_{n},0)\to\rho_{s}(f,0)=-\log\prod_{k\geqslant 0}(1-|q(k)|^{2}).

The convergence
q_{n}\to q
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
will follow if we check that
q_{n}(k)\to q(k)
for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
(indeed, we then have
\sum_{k\geqslant N}|q_{n}(k)|^{2}\to 0
as
N\to+\infty
uniformly in
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
). To this end, note that assumption
\rho_{s}(f_{n},f)\to 0
implies that the sequence
\{f_{n}\}
converges to
f
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
, and, since
|f_{n}|\leqslant 1
,
|f|\leqslant 1
on
\mathbb{T}
, the functions
f_{n}
converge to
f
uniformly on compacts in
\mathbb{D}
. Now the fact that
q_{n}(k)=(f_{n})_{k}(0)
tends to
(f)_{k}(0)=q(k)
as
n\to+\infty
for every
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
follows from Schur’s algorithm (
6.5
). We see that the mapping
f\mapsto q
is continuous from
X_{+}
to
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
.

Turning to the inverse mapping, we introduce the quantities (see
[
20
]
)

(6.25)

\displaystyle E(f,g)=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-\bar{f}g)\,dm,\qquad E(f)=E(f,f).

We have
1-\bigl|\frac{f-g}{1-\bar{f}g}\bigr|^{2}=\frac{(1-|f|^{2})\cdot(1-|g|^{2})}{|1-\bar{f}g|^{2}}
, hence

(6.26)

\displaystyle\rho_{s}(f,g)^{2}=E(f)+E(g)-2\Re E(f,g).

Suppose that
q_{n},q
are sequences in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
such that
q_{n}\to q
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
. Denote by
f_{n}
,
f
the Schur functions corresponding to these sequences. We have
f_{n},f\in X_{+}
by Szegő theorem, see (
6.6
). Let us prove that
\rho_{s}(f_{n},f)\to 0
as
n\to+\infty
. Since

E(f_{n})\to E(f)
by Szegő theorem, relation (
6.26
) shows that we only need to check that
E(f_{n},f)\to E(f,f)
. We have

E(f_{n},f)=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{k\geqslant 0}\frac{(\bar{f}_{n}f)^{k}}{k}\,dm=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{(\bar{f}_{n}f)^{k}}{k}\,dm-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\bar{f}_{n}f)^{k}}{k}\,dm,

and

\left|\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{(\bar{f}_{n}f)^{k}}{k}\,dm\right|\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{|\bar{f}_{n}f|^{k}}{k}\,dm\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{T}}\sum_{k=N+1}^{\infty}\frac{|f|^{k}}{k}\,dm,

which tends to zero as
N\to+\infty
by Lebesgue dominated convergence theorem (the majorant is
\log\frac{1}{1-|f|}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
). Next, let us show that for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
we have

(6.27)

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\bar{f}_{n}f)^{k}\,dm\to\int_{\mathbb{T}}|f|^{2k}\,dm,\qquad n\to+\infty.

Indeed, the first
m
Taylor coefficients of
f
are polynomials in
q(0),\overline{q(0)},\ldots,q(m-1),\overline{q(m-1)}
and similarly for
f_{n}
, see Lemma
4.1
of Section 1.3 in
[
17
]
. Hence Taylor coefficients of
f_{n}^{k}
tend to those of
f^{k}
as
n\to\infty
. Rewrite quantity in (
6.27
) as

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{T}}(\bar{f}_{n}f)^{k}\,dm=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\overline{c_{m}(f_{n}^{k})}c_{m}(f^{k})=\sum_{m=0}^{M}\overline{c_{m}(f_{n}^{k})}c_{m}(f^{k})+\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}\overline{c_{m}(f_{n}^{k})}c_{m}(f^{k}).

The second sum can be estimated using the Cauchy inequality by

\displaystyle\|f_{n}^{k}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}^{2}\cdot\left(\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}|c_{m}(f^{k})|^{2}\right)\leqslant\sum_{m=M+1}^{\infty}|c_{m}(f^{k})|^{2},

because
f_{n}^{k}\in\mathcal{S}(\mathbb{D})
and consequently
\|f_{n}^{k}\|_{H^{2}(\mathbb{D})}^{2}\leqslant 1
. Hence it tends to
0
as
M\to\infty
. The first sum tends to
\sum_{m=0}^{M}|c_{m}(f^{k})|^{2}
as
n\to\infty
and (
6.27
) follows. Relation (
6.27
) shows that
E(f_{n},f)\to 0
,
\rho_{s}(f_{n},f)\to 0
, and thus the mapping
q\mapsto f
is continuous from
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z}_{+},\mathbb{D})
to the metric space
X_{+}
. ∎

The following lemma is elementary. It is known as Scheffé’s lemma, see Section 5.10 in
[
25
]
.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let measurable functions
g
,
g_{n}
on
\mathbb{T}
be such that
g_{j}\to g
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
\|g_{j}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\to\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}
as
j\to\infty
. Then
\|g-g_{j}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\to 0
.

Proof.
If
\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}=0
, then the lemma is trivial, otherwise we can reduce the statement of the lemma to the case
\|g_{j}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}=\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}=1
by changing
g
and
g_{n}
to
g/\|g\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}
and
g_{n}/\|g_{n}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}
respectively. Consider any subsequence
g_{n_{k}}
of the sequence
g_{n}
. Let
g_{n_{k_{j}}}
be its subsequence converging Lebesgue almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. The limit of
g_{n_{k_{j}}}
coincides with
g
Lebesgue almost everywhere on
\mathbb{T}
. To simplify notation, we denote the new sequence
g_{n_{k_{j}}}
by
\tilde{g}_{j}
. Let
\varepsilon>0
. By Egorov’s theorem and integrability of
g
, there is
K_{\varepsilon}\subset\mathbb{T}
such that
m(K_{\varepsilon})<\varepsilon,\|g\|_{L^{1}(K_{\varepsilon})}<\varepsilon
and
\tilde{g}_{j}\to g
uniformly on
\mathbb{T}\setminus K_{\varepsilon}
. In particular, we have

\int_{\mathbb{T}\setminus K_{\varepsilon}}|\tilde{g}_{j}|\,dm\to\int_{\mathbb{T}\setminus K_{\varepsilon}}|g|\,dm\geqslant 1-2\varepsilon,\qquad\limsup_{j\to\infty}\int_{K_{\varepsilon}}|\tilde{g}_{j}|\,dm\leqslant 2\varepsilon.

Now, we only need to write

\limsup_{j\to\infty}\|g-\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\leqslant\limsup_{j\to\infty}\|g-\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T}\setminus K_{\varepsilon})}+\limsup_{j\to\infty}\|\tilde{g}_{j}\|_{L^{1}(K_{\varepsilon})}+\|g\|_{L^{1}(K_{\varepsilon})}\leqslant 3\varepsilon.

Since
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrary, we see that
\tilde{g}_{j}\to g
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. In other words, we have shown that any subsequence of
g_{n}
contains a subsequence converging to
g
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Then
g_{n}\to g
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
and the lemma follows. ∎

Recall that the space
X
and the metric
\rho_{s}
on
X
are defined in (
1.13
) and (
1.14
). For
r\in X
, define the function
E(r)
by (
6.25
).

Proposition 6.10
.

Let
r_{n},r\in X
. The following assertions are equivalent:

(a)

r_{n}
converges to
r
in
X
;

(b)

r_{n}
converges to
r
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
and
\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}E(r_{n})=E(r)
.

Proof.
Assume that
r_{n}\to r
in
X
as
n\to+\infty
. The convergence in measure follows immediately. For all
n\geqslant 0
, we have
|1-\overline{r_{n}}r|\geqslant 1-|r|
and
\log\frac{1}{1-|r|}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
. Hence by the dominated convergence theorem we have

(6.28)

\displaystyle E(r_{n},r)=-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-\overline{r_{n}}r)\,dm\to-\int_{\mathbb{T}}\log(1-|r|^{2})\,dm=E(r).

Thus, from (
6.26
) we see that

0=\lim_{n\to+\infty}\rho_{s}(r_{n},r)^{2}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}(E(r)+E(r_{n})-2\Re E(r,r_{n}))=\lim_{n\to+\infty}(E(r_{n})-E(r)),

which gives us the required assertion. On the other hand, if we assume
(b)
, then (
6.28
) will follow by the same argument and similarly by (
6.26
) we will get

(6.29)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to+\infty}\rho_{s}(r_{n},r)^{2}=\lim_{n\to+\infty}(E(r)+E(r_{n})-2\Re E(r,r_{n}))=0,

which is the convergence in
X
.

∎

Proposition 6.11
.

If
q_{n}\to q
in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
, then
{\bf r}_{q_{n}}\to{\bf r}_{q}
in
X
.

Proof.
We want to apply the criteria from Proposition
6.10
. Convergence in
\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
implies the convergence

\displaystyle\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q_{n}(k)|^{2})\to\prod_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}(1-|q(k)|^{2}),\quad n\to\infty,

which yields
E({\bf r}_{q_{n}})\to E({\bf r}_{q})
by Proposition
6.5
. Thus, it is suffices to show only that
{\bf r}_{q_{n}}\to{\bf r}_{q}
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Recall that for every
q\in\ell^{2}(\mathbb{Z},\mathbb{D})
, we have
f^{\pm}=\mathfrak{a}^{\pm}/\mathfrak{b}^{\pm}
, where
f^{\pm}
are the Schur functions generated by
q
, hence

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{q}=\frac{\mathfrak{a}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{b}^{+}}-\mathfrak{b}^{-}\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}}{\mathfrak{a}^{+}\mathfrak{a}^{-}-\mathfrak{b}^{+}\mathfrak{b}^{-}}=\frac{\overline{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}}{\mathfrak{a}^{+}}\frac{\overline{f^{+}}-f^{-}}{1-f^{+}f^{-}}=\exp(-2i\mathcal{H}(\log|\mathfrak{a}^{+}|))\frac{\overline{f^{+}}-f^{-}}{1-f^{+}f^{-}}.

Here
\mathcal{H}
denotes the Hilbert transform and we used the fact that
\mathfrak{a}^{+}
is an outer function. Furthermore we have
1/|\mathfrak{a}^{+}|^{2}=1-|\mathfrak{b}^{+}|^{2}/|\mathfrak{a}^{+}|^{2}=1-|f^{+}|^{2}
hence

(6.30)

\displaystyle{\bf r}_{q}=\exp(i\mathcal{H}(\log(1-|f^{+}|^{2})))\frac{\overline{f^{+}}-f^{-}}{1-f^{+}f^{-}}.

Similar formulae with Schur functions
f_{n}^{\pm}
in place of
f^{\pm}
hold for
q_{n}
. Proposition
6.8
implies the convergence
f_{n}^{\pm}\to f^{\pm}
in Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{T}
. Moreover, by the Szegő theorem,
\|1-|f_{n}^{+}|^{2}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}\to\|1-|f^{+}|^{2}\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{T})}
hence Lemma
6.9
can be applied to functions

\displaystyle g_{n}=\log(1-|f_{n}^{+}|^{2}),\qquad g=\log(1-|f^{+}|^{2}).

It gives the convergence of
\log(1-|f_{n}^{+}|^{2})
to
\log(1-|f^{+}|^{2})
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
.
Weak continuity of the Hilbert transform
\mathcal{H}
(see Section III.2 in
[
7
]
) then implies that

Then solvability of (
7.2
) is equivalent to the existence of a fixed point for
\Phi:Y\mapsto Y
. Let us show that
\Phi
is a contraction acting on the set
B_{Y}=\{u\in Y\colon\|u\|_{Y}\leqslant 2\}
. Notice that

\displaystyle|F(u)(s,n)|\leqslant 6\|u\|_{Y},\quad s\leqslant t,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z},

\displaystyle|\Phi(u)(t,n)|\leqslant|q_{0}(n)|+\int_{0}^{t}|F(u)(s,n)|\,ds\leqslant 1+6t\|u\|_{Y},

\displaystyle\|\Phi(u)\|_{Y}\leqslant 1+6t\|u\|_{Y}.

In particular, if
u\in Y
, then
\Phi(u)\in Y
. Furthermore, from (
7.1
) we see that for
u,v\in Y
we have

\displaystyle|\Phi(u)(t,n)-\Phi(v)(t,n)|

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{0}^{t}|F(u)(s,n)-F(v)(s,n)|\,ds\leqslant 6t\|u-v\|_{Y}.

We have
6t<1
, hence
\Phi
is a contraction and (
1.5
) has a solution on
[0,t]
. By Lemma
7.1
,
q(t,\cdot)
also satisfies
q(t,n)<1
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
, hence the fixed point algorithm can be applied to find the solution on the segment
[t,2t]
. Iterating this procedure, we obtain the existence of a solution on
[0,\infty)
. The similar argument works for negative
t
, hence the proof is concluded. ∎
