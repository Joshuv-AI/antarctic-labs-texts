Lipschitz stability of an inverse conductivity problem with two Cauchy data pairs
Martin Hanke

Abstract
In 1996 Seo proved that two appropriate pairs of current and voltage data measured on the surface of a planar homogeneous object are sufficient to determine a conductive polygonal inclusion with known deviating conductivity. Here we show that the corresponding linearized forward map is injective, and from this we deduce Lipschitz stability of the solution of the original nonlinear inverse problem. We also treat the case of an insulating polygonal inclusion, in which case a single pair of Cauchy data is already sufficient for the same purpose.

polygonal inclusion, conductivity equation, shape derivative

Introduction

We consider the boundary value problem

\nabla\cdot(\sigma\nabla u)\,=\,0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax$}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\,=\,f\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,,

(1)

for the electric potential
u
in a planar object
\mathchar 266\relax
,
when a (quasi-)static boundary current
f
with vanishing mean
is applied on its boundary. To be specific we focus on the situation
that the object contains a so-called inclusion
{\mathscr{D}}
of some other conducting
material, such that the spatial conductivity distribution is given by

\sigma\,=\,\begin{cases}k&\text{in ${\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\\
1&\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\end{cases}

(2)

with a nonnegative value
k\neq 1
.

The inverse conductivity problem that we are interested in
seeks to recover the inclusion
(i.e., location and shape of
{\mathscr{D}}
) from measurements of the potential on
the boundary of the object.
The question whether this inverse problem is uniquely solvable
has a long-standing history. In the formal (degenerate) case
k=0
, i.e.,
when the inclusion is taken to be insulating
(cf. the discussion in Sect.
5
) and its complement
is a connected set, then it is known that
the Cauchy data of
u
on
\partial\mathchar 266\relax
do indeed uniquely determine
the inclusion; for a proof of this result, cf., e.g.,
Beretta and Vessella
[
15
]
.

Less is known, however, when the conductivity in the inclusion is a
nonzero constant different from one. For this case
Friedman and Isakov
[
18
]
proved that a convex polygonal inclusion
is uniquely determined by this data, provided its conductivity
is known and the diameter of
{\mathscr{D}}
is
smaller than its distance to the boundary of the object.
Barceló, Fabes, and Seo
[
9
]
, and
Alessandrini and Isakov
[
5
]
were able to drop the size constraint
on the polygonal inclusion for the prize of accepting only certain admissible
boundary currents
f
for probing the object.
Finally, Seo
[
22
]
proved that the convexity assumption
on the polygon can also be omitted when the boundary potentials
for two appropriate probing currents
f_{1},f_{2}
are given;
see Assumption (
10
) in Section
3
.

It is known that inverse conductivity problems in general are badly ill-posed
in the sense that the solution lacks continuous dependence on the given data;
some conditional stability – generically of logarithmic type –
can be restored by providing further a priori information on the conductivity,
cf., e.g.,
[
10
,
15
,
17
]
.

The past twenty years have seen increasing activities in deriving Lipschitz
stability estimates for inverse conductivity problems
under very restrictive conditions on the set of admissible conductivities.
One of the first results in this direction was
obtained by Alessandrini and Vessella
[
6
]
who showed that if

all
Cauchy pairs for
u
in (
1
)
are known, i.e., if the full (or the local) Neumann-Dirichlet operator
associated with the differential equation in (
1
)
is given, then the
values
of a piecewise constant conductivity

\sigma
with respect to a known partitioning of
\mathchar 266\relax

into finitely many subdomains depend Lipschitz continuously on these data.
Later it was proved by Harrach
[
20
]
that already a finite number of
Cauchy pairs is sufficient for this result to hold;
see also the work by Alberti and Santacesaria
[
2
,
3
]
.

Whereas these results assume the spatial structure of
\sigma
to be known
and the quantitative details are being searched for,
the conductivity Ansatz (
2
) with known
k
but
unknown form and location of the inclusion, was treated by
Beretta and Francini
[
14
]
.
They established Lipschitz stability in terms of the Hausdorff distance
between the boundaries of two admissible inclusions, if it is a priori
known that they have the shape of a polygon. In contrast to the aforementioned
uniqueness results by Beretta/Vessella and by Seo, however, the stability
result in
[
14
]
again requires the full Neumann-Dirichlet map as data.
See also
[
7
,
13
]
for extensions of this finding to layered
– instead of homogeneous – background media and to polyhedral inclusions
in three space dimensions, respectively.

Recently, Alberti, Árroyo, and Santacesaria
[
1
]
showed
(for triangular inclusions) that the Lipschitz result by Beretta and Francini
remains valid, if the given data correspond to a sufficiently large,
but finite number of probing currents. Here we prove the following statement
in the spirit of Seo’s original uniqueness result:
If only two pairs of Cauchy data are given which fulfill
Seo’s uniqueness assumption for a conductive inclusion,
then this minimal dataset is enough to have Lipschitz stability.
We also consider the degenerate case of an insulating polygonal inclusion
and establish Lipschitz stability for a single (nontrivial) Cauchy data pair.
We mention in passing that for a somewhat related setting,
namely the reconstruction of a linear crack within a homogeneous planar object,
Lipschitz stability with only two pairs of Cauchy data had been
established by Alessandrini, Beretta, and Vessella
[
4
]
back in 1996.

In contrast to the analysis in the above works which, in principle,
allow for an evaluation of the corresponding Lipschitz constant, our method
is non-quantitative. Rather, we use a general methodology worked out
by Bourgeois
[
16
]
, building on earlier work by
Bacchelli and Vessella
[
8
]
.
As is transparent from their results, the key ingredients for Lipschitz
stability in general nonlinear inverse problems are

1.

a specification of the quantity of interest in terms of finitely
many parameters,

2.

the restriction of these parameters to a compact set,

3.

injectivity of the forward operator on this compact set,

4.

continuous differentiability of the forward operator, and

5.

injectivity of the Jacobian of the forward operator.

Since we can build on the uniqueness results by Seo and Beretta/Vessella,
respectively, and since differentiability results are also available,
it remains for us to investigate the injectivity of the associated Jacobian.

To do so we need a specification of this Jacobian (the so-called shape
derivative) in terms of an inhomogeneous transmission problem for the
Laplace equation; see (
9
). This specification – which was well-known
for inclusions with smooth boundaries – has been verified for polygonal
inclusions in a companion paper
[
19
]
submitted elsewhere, which is
currently under peer-review. It should be emphasized that all the theorems
in the present paper hinge upon this auxiliary result.

The outline of this paper is as follows.
We start in Section
2
by reviewing known properties of the
solution
u
of (
1
), when the inclusion
{\mathscr{D}}
is a
conductive polygon, including the aforementioned differentiability result
with respect to its shape. In Section
3
we specify the
associated inverse problem as it has been introduced by Seo, and we prove
that the respective Jacobian is injective.
Then, in Section
4
, we adapt the method
from
[
8
,
16
]
to our needs and establish the corresponding
Lipschitz stability result (Theorem
3
).
We conclude the paper by treating the case of an insulating polygonal
inclusion in Section
5
.

2
The forward problem for a conductive polygonal inclusion

We assume throughout that
\mathchar 266\relax
is a two-dimensional bounded domain
with smooth boundary, and that the inclusion
{\mathscr{D}}
is a
polygonal domain with simply connected closure
\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}\subset\mathchar 266\relax
.
We denote by
\nu
the outer normal vector on the boundaries of
{\mathscr{D}}

and of
\mathchar 266\relax

Concerning the spatial conductivity distribution (
2

When the probing boundary current

f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)\,=\,\Bigl\{\,f\in L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)\,:\,\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}f\,\mathrm{d}s=0\,\Bigr\}\,,

then the corresponding electric potential

u\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\mathchar 266\relax)\,=\,\Bigl\{\,u\in H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax)\,:\,\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}u\,\mathrm{d}s=0\,\Bigr\}

of (
1
).

Let
x_{i}
and
\mathchar 256\relax_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,n\geq 3
,
denote the vertices and (relatively open) edges of
{\mathscr{D}}
,
respectively, where we assume that
x_{i}
connects
\mathchar 256\relax_{i}
and
\mathchar 256\relax_{i+1}
.
Here and throughout we identify
\mathchar 256\relax_{n+1}
with
\mathchar 256\relax_{1}
,
and also
x_{i+n}
with
x_{i}
for

On
\mathchar 256\relax_{i}

. We stipulate the general assumption that the induced orientation of

\partial{\mathscr{D}}
is counterclockwise,
and that the interior angles
\alpha_{i}\in(0,2\pi)

The two components
u_{-}=u|_{\mathscr{D}}

u_{+}=u|_{\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}}

u_{-}\,=\,u_{+}\qquad\text{and}\qquad k\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u_{-}\,=\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u_{+}

on every edge
\mathchar 256\relax_{i}
.
Therefore both components can be extended by reflection
across each of the edges of
{\mathscr{D}}
, and hence, they both are infinitely smooth
and all their derivatives extend continuously onto the edges.
Concerning the behavior of
u
at the vertices we introduce
a local coordinate system for

x\in{\cal B}_{r_{0}}(x_{i})\,=\,\bigl\{x\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2}\,:\,|x-x_{i}|<r_{0}\bigr\}\,,

with
r_{0}
sufficiently small, namely

x\,=\,x_{i}\,+\,\bigl(r\cos(\theta_{i}+\theta),r\sin(\theta_{i}+\theta)\bigr)\,,\qquad 0<r<r_{0}\,,\ 0\leq\theta<2\pi\,,

(3)

where
\theta_{i}
is such that the values

\theta=0
,
\theta\in(0,\alpha_{i})
,
\theta=\alpha_{i}
, and

\theta\in(\alpha_{i},2\pi)
correspond to points on
\mathchar 256\relax_{i+1}
, in
{\mathscr{D}}
,
on
\mathchar 256\relax_{i}
, and in
\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
, respectively.
It has been shown in
[

that in this coordinate system

has an asymptotic expansion

where

(0,\alpha_{i})
and in

(\alpha_{i},2\pi)
,
respectively, and the exponents
\gamma_{ij}

\bigl|\hskip 0.43057pt\sin\gamma(\alpha_{i}-\pi)\bigr|\,=\,\lambda\hskip 0.43057pt\bigl|\hskip 0.43057pt\sin\gamma\pi\bigr|\,,\qquad\lambda\,=\,\Bigl|\frac{k+1}{k-1}\Bigr|\,,

which we assume to be in increasing order. Both,

and
\gamma_{ij}

are independent of the probing current; only the expansion coefficients

\beta_{ij}=\beta_{ij}[f]\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
depend
linearly on

. See
[
11
]
,
[
22
]
, or
[
19
]
for

We refer to Figure

for a graphical illustration of

\frac{1}{2}\,<\,\gamma_{i1}\,<\,1\,,\qquad 1\,<\,\gamma_{i2}\,<\,\frac{3}{2}\,,\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{ij}\,>\,\frac{3}{2}\quad\text{for $j\geq 3$}\,.

(6)

Fig. 1:
The graphs of the two functions of

We denote by

\mathchar 259\relax_{f}\,:\,{\mathscr{D}}\,\mapsto\,u|_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}\,,

(7)

the map, which takes an admissible polygon

onto the trace of the
solution

of (
1
) on
\partial\mathchar 266\relax
.
Let

,

Then we define a vector field

belong to the space of linear splines

\partial{\mathscr{D}}
with the vertices

If

\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})\in{\cal L}(\mathord{\cal S}_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}^{2},L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))

\partial{\mathscr{D}}
except for the vertices, where in general

\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}\,:\,{\mathscr{D}}\,\mapsto\,\bigl(\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1}}({\mathscr{D}}),\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})\bigr)

(17)

j=1,2

Now we recall that

(18)

and
j=1,2

.
Inserting this into (

(19)

where we have introduced the abbreviations

for general

.

Likewise we can use (

of

on

, which gives

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1-k}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}(\gamma_{j}-1)\Bigl(\beta_{j}\gamma_{j}y_{j}(0)c_{j}^{\prime}\,-\,k\beta_{j}y_{j}^{\prime}(0)s_{j}^{\prime}\Bigr)r^{\gamma_{j}-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,+\,\frac{1-k}{k}\,b_{2}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{j}\Bigl(\beta_{j}\gamma_{j}y_{j}(0)c_{j}\,-\,k\beta_{j}y_{j}^{\prime}(0)s_{j}\Bigr)r^{\gamma_{j}-1}

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{1-k}{k}(\gamma_{1}-1)\Bigl(\beta_{1}\gamma_{1}y_{1}(0)c_{1}^{\prime}\,-\,k\beta_{1}y_{1}^{\prime}(0)s_{1}^{\prime}\Bigr)r^{\gamma_{1}-2}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\,+\,\frac{1-k}{k}(\gamma_{2}-1)\Bigl(\beta_{2}\gamma_{2}y_{2}(0)c_{2}^{\prime}\,-\,k\beta_{2}y_{2}^{\prime}(0)s_{2}^{\prime}\Bigr)r^{\gamma_{2}-2}\,+\,O(r^{-1/2})

\displaystyle\,=\,\frac{k-1}{k}\,\gamma_{1}(\gamma_{1}-1)a\beta_{1}y_{1}(\alpha_{1})r^{\gamma_{1}-2}\,+\,\frac{k-1}{k}\,\gamma_{2}(\gamma_{2}-1)a\beta_{2}y_{2}(\alpha_{1})r^{\gamma_{2}-2}

The four equations in (

\beta_{j}B_{j}\end{array}\right]\,=\,\left[\hskip-5.0pt\begin{array}[]{*\c@MaxMatrixCols c}kas_{j}-s_{j}^{\prime}&-k(ac_{j}+c_{j}^{\prime})\\

0\end{array}\right],\qquad j=1,2\,.

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\,(kas_{j}-s_{j}^{\prime})(as_{j}-ks_{j}^{\prime})\,+\,k(ac_{j}+c_{j}^{\prime})^{2}

4
Lipschitz stability for a conductive polygonal inclusion

The idea of obtaining Lipschitz stability for Seo’s inverse problem originates
from the fact that each admissible polygon
{\mathscr{D}}
with
n
vertices
can be described by a
2n
-dimensional vector

{\mathbf{x}}\,=\,[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]\,\in\,({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}

(26)

with the coordinates of its vertices in counterclockwise ordering.
However, since we have the freedom of choosing any vertex of
{\mathscr{D}}
for
x_{1}
,
this vector is not uniquely specified.

To account for this problem we introduce the following metric in the set of
all admissible polygons with
n
vertices
(which corresponds to a pseudometric in
({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}
):
If
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
is a second admissible polygon with
n
vertices
x_{i}^{\prime}
in
counterclockwise order, let

d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,=\,\min_{j=0,\dots,n-1}\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|x_{i+j}-x_{i}^{\prime}|\,.

(27)

Clearly,
d
is nonnegative and symmetric;
it vanishes, if and only if
{\mathscr{D}}={\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
.
To see that
d
also satisfies the triangle inequality, let
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime}

be a third admissible polygon with vertices
x_{i}^{\prime\prime}
,
i=1,\dots,n
.
Without loss of generality we can assume that the enumeration
of the vertices of
{\mathscr{D}}
and
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
is chosen in such a way that

\displaystyle d({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime},{\mathscr{D}})\,=\,\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|x_{i}^{\prime\prime}-x_{i}|\,,

and likewise, that

\displaystyle d({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,=\,\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|x_{i}^{\prime\prime}-x_{i}^{\prime}|\,,

Then we readily conclude that

\displaystyle d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})

\displaystyle\leq\,\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|\,\leq\,\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\bigl(|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\,+\,|x_{i}^{\prime\prime}-x_{i}^{\prime}|\bigr)

\displaystyle\leq\,\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime\prime}|\,+\,\max_{j=1,\dots,n}|x_{j}^{\prime\prime}-x_{j}^{\prime}|\,=\,d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime})\,+\,d({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime\prime},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,.

Take note that
d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
majorizes the Hausdorff distance

d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}},\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
between
\partial{\mathscr{D}}
and
\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
.
For, if
x\in\partial{\mathscr{D}}
then there is
some vertex
x_{i}
of
{\mathscr{D}}
and
c\in[0,1)
, such that

x\,=\,cx_{i}\,+\,(1-c)x_{i+1}\,;

assuming further without loss of generality that the minimum in (
27
)
is attained for
j=0
, then it follows that

\bigl|x\,-\,(cx_{i}^{\prime}+(1-c)x_{i+1}^{\prime})\bigr|\,\leq\,c\,|x_{i}-x_{i}^{\prime}|\,+\,(1-c)\,|x_{i+1}-x_{i+1}^{\prime}|\,\leq\,d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,.

Since
cx_{i}^{\prime}+(1-c)x_{i+1}^{\prime}\in\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
this shows that the distance
between any
x\in\partial{\mathscr{D}}
and
\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
is at most
d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
,
and hence,

d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}},\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,\leq\,d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,.

(28)

Another useful ingredient to achieve Lipschitz stability is compactness.
Therefore, following Beretta and Francini
[
14
]
, we define for a
given
\delta>0
the union
{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
of all admissible polygons
with
n
vertices, such that

(i)

|x_{i}-x|\geq\delta
for every
i=1,\dots,n
and every

x\in\mathchar 256\relax_{j}
with
j\notin\{i,i+1\}
;

(ii)

\delta\leq\alpha_{i}\leq 2\pi-\delta
and

|\alpha-\pi|\geq\delta
for every
i=1,\dots,n
;

(iii)

|x-y|\geq\delta
for every
x\in\partial{\mathscr{D}}
and every

y\in\partial\mathchar 266\relax
.

We denote by
{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}\subset({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}
the set of coordinate vectors (
26
),
which describe some
{\mathscr{D}}\in{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
, and we emphasize that
{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}
is
compact. Further,
{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}\subset{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta/2}
, and when
({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}

is equipped with the norm

\|{\mathbf{d}}\|\,=\,\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|d_{i}|\qquad\text{for every \ ${\mathbf{d}}=[d_{1},\dots,d_{n}]$}\,,

(29)

then there is an open set
{{\mathcal{U}}}_{n,\delta}\subset({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}
, such that

{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}\,\subset\,{{\mathcal{U}}}_{n,\delta}\,\subset\,{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta/2}\,.

In
{{\mathcal{U}}}_{n,\delta}
we define

F:\begin{cases}\phantom{{\mathbf{x}}\!\!}{{\mathcal{U}}}_{n,\delta}\to\,(L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}\,,\\
\phantom{{{\mathcal{U}}}_{n,\delta}\!\!\!}{\mathbf{x}}\,\mapsto\,\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})\,,\end{cases}

(30)

where
{\mathscr{D}}\in{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
is the polygon associated with
{\mathbf{x}}
.
Further, for the same pair of
{\mathbf{x}}
and
{\mathscr{D}}
we introduce

S_{{\mathbf{x}}}:\begin{cases}\phantom{{\mathbf{d}}\!\!}({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}\to\,\mathord{\cal S}_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}\,,\\
\phantom{({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}\!\!\!}{\mathbf{d}}\,\mapsto\,h\,,\end{cases}

where for

(31)

With the norms introduced in (
8
) and (

is an isometry.

Proposition 2
.

The operator

belongs to

,
and for

Proof.

\displaystyle\|F({\mathbf{x}}+{\mathbf{d}})\,-\,F({\mathbf{x}})\,-\,F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{d}}\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}\,=\,

\displaystyle\|\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,-\,\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})\,-\,\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})h\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}\,,

is sufficiently small. Accordingly,

\displaystyle\|F({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})-F({\mathbf{x}})\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,\int_{0}^{1}\|F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}+t({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{x}})\bigr)({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{x}})\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}t

Theorem 3
.

Let

\mathchar 266\relax

(36)

{\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}\in{\cal A}_{n,\delta}

Proof.

We assume (
36

{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}

(37)

where

Since
{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}
is compact we can find a subsequence
(m_{l})_{l\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}}

of indices, such that the associated subsequences

({\mathbf{x}}_{m_{l}})_{l}
and
({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}_{m_{l}})_{l}
converge,
i.e., there exist
{\mathbf{x}},{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}
with

{\mathbf{x}}_{m_{l}}\to{\mathbf{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbf{x}}_{m_{l}}^{\prime}\to{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\qquad\text{for $l\to\infty$}\,.

For ease of notation we consider these subsequences to be the
original sequences that we have started with.
For the two polygons
{\mathscr{D}}
and
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
in
{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
associated with
{\mathbf{x}}
and

{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
, respectively, we then have

d({\mathscr{D}}_{m},{\mathscr{D}})\,\to\,0\quad\text{and}\quad d({\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,\to\,0\qquad\text{for $m\to\infty$}\,,

and hence,

d({\mathscr{D}}_{m},{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\,\to\,d({\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,<\,\infty\,.

(38)

We further conclude that

F({\mathbf{x}}_{m})\,\to\,F({\mathbf{x}})\quad\text{and}\quad F({\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\,\to\,F({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})\qquad\text{for $m\to\infty$}\,,

because
F
is differentiable.
Together with (
37
) and (
38
) this implies
that
F({\mathbf{x}})=F({\mathbf{x}}^{\prime})
. In other words,

\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})=\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
,
and by Seo’s uniqueness result we necessarily have
{\mathscr{D}}={\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
.
Without loss of generality we can assume in the sequel that
{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}
;
otherwise, we reenumerate the vertices of
{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime}
for every
m\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
in such
a way that the enumeration of the vertices of
{\mathscr{D}}
and
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
is the same.

For
m
sufficiently large we now rewrite

\displaystyle F({\mathbf{x}}_{m})-F({\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}F^{\prime}\bigl({\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}+t({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\bigr)({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\,F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}})({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\,+\,\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl(F^{\prime}\bigl({\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}+t({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\bigr)-F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}})\Bigr)({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

and hence, introducing

{\mathbf{d}}_{m}\,=\,\frac{{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}}{\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\|}

and

\varepsilon_{m}\,=\,\int_{0}^{1}\bigl\|F^{\prime}\bigl({\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}+t({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\bigr)-F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}})\bigr\|\,\mathrm{d}t\,,

we arrive at

\|F({\mathbf{x}}_{m})-F({\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}\,\geq\,\Bigl(\|F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{d}}_{m}\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}\,-\,\varepsilon_{m}\Bigr)\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\|\,.

(39)

Take note that
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\neq{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}
because
d({\mathscr{D}}_{m},{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})>0
according to
(
37
), and that
\varepsilon_{m}\to 0
as
m\to\infty

by the continuity of
F^{\prime}
and the fact that

\bigl\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}+t({\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime})-{\mathbf{x}}\bigr\|\,\leq\,t\,\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}\|+(1-t)\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}-{\mathbf{x}}\|\,\to\,0

as
m\to\infty
, uniformly for
t\in[0,1]
.
Inserting (
39
) into (
37
) we thus conclude that

\|F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{d}}_{m}\|_{(L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax))^{2}}\,<\,\varepsilon_{m}\,+\,\frac{1}{\eta_{m}}\,\frac{d({\mathscr{D}}_{m},{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})}{\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\|}

(40)

for
m
sufficiently large.
Since
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\to 0
and the vertices of
{\mathscr{D}}_{m}

(and of
{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime}
, respectively) are at least
\delta
apart by requirement (i)
in the definition of
{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
, we conclude from (
27
) that

d({\mathscr{D}}_{m},{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\,=\,\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\|\,,\qquad\text{if \ $\|{\mathbf{x}}_{m}-{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\|\,<\,\delta/2$}\,,

and hence, the right-hand side of (
40
) goes to zero as
m\to\infty
.

Since
({\mathbf{d}}_{m})_{m}
consists of unit vectors from
({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}
we can find a
convergent subsequence – again denoted by
({\mathbf{d}}_{m})_{m}
– with

{\mathbf{d}}_{m}\,\to\,{\mathbf{d}}\,,\qquad m\to\infty\,,

where the limit
{\mathbf{d}}=[d_{1},\dots,d_{n}]\in({\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}^{2})^{n}
also has norm one.
It thus follows from (
40
) that

0\,=\,F^{\prime}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{d}}\,=\,\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})h\,,

where
0\neq h\in\mathord{\cal S}_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}
is given by (
31
). But this violates our
injectivity result in Theorem
1
, and hence, we
have the desired contradition to (
37
).
∎

As we have mentioned in the introduction the idea of this proof
is borrowed from
[
8
,
16
]
. We rearranged the argumentation,
though, to deal with the difficulties that the operator
F
of (
30
)
fails to be injective and its domain
{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}
is not convex.

For ease of completeness we also state the following Lipschitz stability
result for admissible polygons with
at most

N
vertices.

Corollary 4
.

Let
\delta>0
and
{\cal B}_{N,\delta}=\bigcup_{n=3}^{N}{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
for some
N\geq 3
.
Further, let the two piecewise constant probing currents

f_{1},f_{2}\in L_{\diamond}^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)
fulfill Seo’s Assumption
(
10
)
.
Then there is a Lipschitz constant
L_{N,\delta}
, depending only on

N
,
\delta
,
k
,
\mathchar 266\relax
, and
f_{1,2}
, such that

d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}},\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\,\leq\,L_{N,\delta}\,\|\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})-\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)^{2}}

for every pair of polygons
{\mathscr{D}},{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}\in{\cal B}_{N,\delta}
, where
d_{H}
denotes the
Hausdorff metric.

Proof.

As in the previous proof we assume to the contrary that there are sequences

({\mathscr{D}}_{m})_{m},({\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})_{m}\subset{\cal B}_{N,\delta}
with

d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}}_{m},\partial{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\,>\,\eta_{m}\,\|\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}_{m})-\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)^{2}}\,,

(41)

where
\eta_{m}\to\infty
as
m\to\infty
. Then there are infinitely many
indices
m_{l}
,
l\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
, and two natural numbers
n,n^{\prime}\in\{3,\dots,N\}
such
that all
{\mathscr{D}}_{m_{l}}
are
n
-gons and all
{\mathscr{D}}_{m_{l}}^{\prime}
are
n^{\prime}
-gons.
Again we assume that these two subsequences have been the original ones,
and as in the proof of Theorem
3
we can further assume
without loss of generality that the corresponding vectors
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n,\delta}

and
{\mathbf{x}}_{m}^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{n^{\prime},\delta}
with the coordinates of the vertices of

{\mathscr{D}}_{m}
and
{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime}
, respectively, converge. If we denote the polygons
corresponding to the two limit vectors
by
{\mathscr{D}}\in{\cal A}_{n,\delta}
and
{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}\in{\cal A}_{n^{\prime},\delta}
this implies that

\displaystyle\|\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}})-\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)^{2}}\,=\,\lim_{m\to\infty}\|\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}_{m})-\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq\,\lim_{m\to\infty}\frac{1}{\eta_{m}}\,d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}}_{m},\partial{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\,=\,0\,,

because
d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}}_{m},\partial{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\to d_{H}(\partial{\mathscr{D}},\partial{\mathscr{D}}^{\prime})
.
From Seo’s uniqueness result therefore follows that
{\mathscr{D}}={\mathscr{D}}^{\prime}
,
and in particular, that
n=n^{\prime}
.
We can thus apply Theorem
3
to conclude that

d({\mathscr{D}}_{m},{\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\,\leq\,\ell_{n,\delta}\,\|\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}_{m})-\mathchar 259\relax_{f_{1},f_{2}}({\mathscr{D}}_{m}^{\prime})\|_{L^{2}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)^{2}}

(42)

for all
m\in{\mathord{\mathbb{N}}}
. Combined with (
41
) and (
28
)
this implies that the sequence
(\eta_{m})_{m}
is bounded,
which is the desired contradiction.
∎

5
The case of an insulating polygonal inclusion

So far we have assumed that the conductivity
k
of the inclusion is positive.
The limiting case
k=0
corresponds to an insulating inclusion, which is
more adequately modeled by the boundary value problem

\Delta u=0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u=0\quad\text{on $\partial{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u=f\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,.

(43)

If
{\mathscr{D}}
is an admissible polygon, then (
43
) has a unique
weak solution in

u\in H^{1}_{\diamond}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})\,=\,\Bigl\{\,u\in H^{1}(\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}})\,:\,\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}u\,\mathrm{d}s=0\,\Bigr\}\,,

and we now use

\mathchar 259\relax_{f}\,:\,{\mathscr{D}}\,\mapsto\,u|_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}\,\in\,L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)

as the associated forward operator. Recall from the introduction that

{\mathscr{D}}
is uniquely determined by
\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})
, provided that
f\neq 0
,
cf.
[
15
]
.

Again,
\mathchar 259\relax_{f}
turns out to be shape differentiable for polygonal
inclusions, cf.
[
19
]
: For
h\in\mathord{\cal S}_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}
the shape derivative

\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h
is given by the trace on
\partial\mathchar 266\relax
of
the solution
u^{\prime}
of the Neumann boundary value problem

\begin{array}[]{c}\Delta u^{\prime}\,=\,0\quad\text{in $\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}$}\,,\qquad{\displaystyle\int_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}u^{\prime}\,\mathrm{d}s\,=\,0\,,}\\[12.91663pt]
\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u^{\prime}\,=\,\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\tau}\Bigl((h\cdot\nu)\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\tau}u\Bigr)\quad\text{on $\partial{\mathscr{D}}$}\,,\qquad\dfrac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u^{\prime}\,=\,0\quad\text{on $\partial\mathchar 266\relax$}\,.\end{array}

(44)

Note that
u
is defined only in the exterior of
{\mathscr{D}}
, but using
the reflection principle it can be extended as a harmonic function across
any edge of
{\mathscr{D}}
into the interior of
{\mathscr{D}}
. Therefore
u
is smooth on

\partial{\mathscr{D}}
, except for the vertices of
{\mathscr{D}}
.

We now establish injectivity of
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})
.

Theorem 5
.

Let
{\mathscr{D}}
be an admissible polygon and

f\in L^{2}_{\diamond}(\partial\mathchar 266\relax)
be a nontrivial probing current.
Then
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})
is injective.

Proof.

Again we assume to the contrary that there exists some nontrivial
h\in\mathord{\cal S}_{\partial{\mathscr{D}}}^{2}
,
for which
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h=0
. Further, as in the proof of
Theorem
1
, we stipulate without loss of generality
that

h(x)\cdot\nu(x)\,=\,1+b\,|x-x_{1}|\,,\qquad x\in\mathchar 256\relax_{2}\,,

(45)

for some
b\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
. Throughout we use the same notation for the vertices,
edges, and interior angles of
{\mathscr{D}}
as in Section
2
,
and introduce the same local coordinates (
3
) near
the vertices.

Let
u^{\prime}
be the solution of (
44
) for this particular
h
.
Since
u^{\prime}|_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}=\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})h=0
it follows from
Holmgren’s theorem that
u^{\prime}
is vanishing in all of

\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
. Therefore,
the Neumann boundary condition for
u^{\prime}
on
\partial{\mathscr{D}}
in (
44
)
must be zero, i.e., there is some constant
c\in{\mathord{\mathbb{R}}}
, such that

(h\cdot\nu)\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}u\,=\,c\qquad\text{on $\mathchar 256\relax_{2}$}\,.

It thus follows from (
45
) that

u(x)\,=\,\begin{cases}u(x_{1})\,+\,c\,|x-x_{1}|\,,&b=0\,,\\[4.30554pt]
u(x_{1})\,+\,\dfrac{c}{b}\log\bigl|1+b\,|x-x_{1}|\bigr|\,,&b\neq 0\,,\end{cases}\qquad x\in\mathchar 256\relax_{2}\,.

(46)

Together with the insulating boundary condition
\partial u/\partial\nu=0

on
\mathchar 256\relax_{2}
this yields a Cauchy problem for
u

with real analytic data on
\mathchar 256\relax_{2}
, which has a unique solution
in
\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
. Consider first the case
b=0
.
In this case the solution of the Cauchy problem is obviously given by

u(x)\,=\,u(x_{1})+c\,r\cos\theta\,,\qquad 0<r<r_{0}\,,\ \alpha_{1}\leq\theta\leq 2\pi\,,

in the local coordinate system (
3
), and hence,

\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u(x)\right|_{\mathchar 256\relax_{1}}\!\!\,=\,\left.\!\frac{1}{r}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}u(x)\right|_{\theta=\alpha_{1}}\!\!\,=\,-c\sin\alpha_{1}\,,\qquad 0<r<r_{0}\,.

But this must be zero in the insulating case, proving that
c=0
,
because
\alpha_{1}\notin\{0,\pi,2\pi\}
.

In the case when
b\neq 0
in (
45
) we have

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\,=\,u(x_{1})\,+\,\frac{c}{b}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}\,b^{j}|x-x_{1}|^{j}

according to (
46
) for
x
near
x_{1}
on

\mathchar 256\relax_{2}
, and the solution of the Cauchy problem is given by

\displaystyle u(x)

\displaystyle\,=\,u(x_{1})\,+\,c\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{j+1}}{j}b^{j-1}r^{j}\cos j\theta

in the local coordinate system (
3
) with
0<r=|x-x_{1}|<r_{0}

and
\alpha_{1}\leq\theta\leq 2\pi
, and hence,

0\,=\,\left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u(x)\right|_{\mathchar 256\relax_{1}}\!\!\,=\,c\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{j+1}\hskip 0.43057ptb^{j}r^{j}\sin(j+1)\alpha_{1}

for
0<|x-x_{0}|<r_{0}
. Again this is only possible if
c=0
.

In either case we have shown that
c=0
in
(
46
), which implies that
u
is constant
in
\mathchar 266\relax\setminus\overline{{\mathscr{D}}}
.
But this is a contradiction to the assumption
that
f
is a nontrivial boundary current, i.e., that

f\,=\,\left.\!\frac{\partial}{\partial\nu}u\right|_{\partial\mathchar 266\relax}\,\neq\,0\,.

We have thus established the injectivity of
\partial\mathchar 259\relax_{f}({\mathscr{D}})
.
∎

\mathchar 266\relax
