Stability for the Erdős-Rothschild problem
Oleg Pikhurko, Katherine Staden

Abstract
Given a sequence 𝒌 := ( k 1 , … , k s ) \bm{k}:=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s}) of natural numbers and a graph G G , let F ⁡ ( G , 𝒌 ) F(G;\bm{k}) denote the number of colourings of the edges of G G with colours 1 , … , s 1,\dots,s such that, for every c ∈ { 1 , … , s } c\in\{1,\dots,s\} , the edges of colour c c contain no clique of order k c k_{c} . Write F ⁡ ( n , 𝒌 ) F(n;\bm{k}) to denote the maximum of F ⁡ ( G , 𝒌 ) F(G;\bm{k}) over all graphs G G on n n vertices. This problem was first considered by Erdős and Rothschild in 1974, but it has been solved only for a very small number of non-trivial cases. In previous work with Yilma, we constructed a finite optimisation problem whose maximum is equal to the limit of log 2 ⁡ F ⁡ ( n , 𝒌 ) / ( n 2 ) \log_{2}F(n;\bm{k})/{n\choose 2} as n n tends to infinity and proved a stability theorem for complete multipartite graphs G G . In this paper we provide a sufficient condition on 𝒌 \bm{k} which guarantees a general stability theorem for any graph G G , describing the asymptotic structure of G G on n n vertices with F ⁡ ( G , 𝒌 ) = F ⁡ ( n , 𝒌 ) ⋅ 2 o ⁡ ( n 2 ) F(G;\bm{k})=F(n;\bm{k})\cdot 2^{o(n^{2})} in terms of solutions to the optimisation problem. We apply our theorem to systematically recover existing stability results as well as all cases with s = 2 s=2 . The proof uses a version of symmetrisation on edge-coloured weighted multigraphs.

Introduction

Let a non-increasing sequence
\bm{k}=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}

s

(or

\bm{k}
-
valid

c

Write

In a previous paper with Yilma
[
24

The case when

[
7

8

A trivial lower bound on

Any

(1.1)

In particular, Erdős and Rothschild conjectured that, when

The conjecture was verified for all

Proving Yuster’s extension of the conjecture, Alon, Balogh, Keevash and Sudakov

The proof of this result uses Szemerédi’s Regularity Lemma, so the graphs to which it applies are very large indeed.
However, the assertions are not true for all numbers

As was remarked in

Thus, for this range of

Hàn and Jiménez

used graph containers to obtain an essentially optimal lower bound for the order

In this paper we are only interested in large

It was proved in

It can be easily seen that the proof from

The authors of

Many other versions of the Erdős-Rothschild problem have been studied, where the goal is to maximise the number of colourings of some discrete object when one forbids certain coloured substructures.
Erdős and Rothschild themselves considered the generalisation where
one forbids a monochromatic graph

[
1

the authors showed that the trivial lower bound (
1.1

when
H
is

, that is, the removal of any edge
from

(Note that every clique is colour-critical.)
In a further generalisation, Balogh
[
3
]
considered edge-colourings in which a specific colouring of a fixed graph

is forbidden.
Other authors have addressed this question in the cases of forbidden monochromatic matchings, stars, paths, trees and some other graphs in
[
12
,
13
]
, matchings with a prescribed colour pattern in
[
14
]
, rainbow stars in
[
17
]
and multicoloured cliques in
[
15
]
.
A so-called ‘
q
-analogue’ was addressed in
[
16
]
, which considers a related problem in the context of vector spaces over a finite field
GF(q)
.
Alon and Yuster
[
2
]
studied a directed version of the problem, to determine the maximum number of
T
-free orientations of an
n
-vertex graph, where
T
is a given
k
-vertex tournament.
The problem of counting monochromatic
H
-free edge-colourings in hypergraphs was studied in
[
11
,
20
,
21
]
.
Additive versions have also been studied, where an underlying group
[

]

[
22

1.1
An optimisation problem

This paper concerns the relation between the structure of almost extremal graphs for
F(n,\bm{k})
and optimal solutions of a certain optimisation problem, Problem
Q_{t}
, which we now define.

Problem
Q_{t}
:

Given a sequence
\bm{k}:=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
of natural numbers and
t\in\{0,1,2\}
, determine

Q_{t}(\bm{k}):=\max_{(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{t}(\bm{k})}q(\phi,\bm{\alpha}),

(1.3)

the maximum value of

q(\phi,\bm{\alpha}):=2\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle 1\leq i<j\leq r}}{{\phi(ij)\neq\emptyset}}}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\log_{2}|\phi(ij)|

(1.4)

over the set
\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{t}(\bm{k})
of
feasible solutions
, that is, triples

(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
such that

•

r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi\in\Phi_{t}(r;\bm{k})
, where
\Phi_{t}(r;\bm{k})
is the set of all
functions
\phi:\binom{[r]}{2}\rightarrow 2^{[s]}

such that

\phi^{-1}(c):=\left\{ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}:c\in\phi(ij)\right\}

is
K_{k_{c}}
-free for every colour
c\in[s]
and
|\phi(ij)|\geq t

for all
ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}
;

•

\bm{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r})\in\Delta^{r}
, where
\Delta^{r}
is the set of all
\bm{\alpha}\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
with
\alpha_{i}\geq 0
for all
i\in[r]
, and
\alpha_{1}+\ldots+\alpha_{r}=1
.

Note that for
t=1,2
, a triple
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{t}(\bm{k})
necessarily has
r<R(\bm{k})

where
R(\bm{k})
is the
Ramsey number
of
\bm{k}
(i.e. the minimum
R
such that
K_{R}
admits no
\bm{k}
-valid
s
-edge-colouring).
Thus the maximum in (
1.3
) is attained for
t=1,2
since
q(r,\phi,\cdot)
is continuous for each of the finitely many pairs
(r,\phi)
, and
\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{t}(\bm{k})
is a (non-empty) compact space.
It is also attained for
t=0
by (
1.5
) below.
We call
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r;\bm{k})
a
colour pattern
and
\bm{\alpha}\in\Delta^{r}
a
vertex weighting
.
A triple
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
is called
Q_{t}
-optimal
if it attains the maximum, that is,

(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{t}(\bm{k})
and
q(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})=Q_{t}(\bm{k})
.
One can easily show
[
24
, Lemma 6]
that

Q(\bm{k}):=Q_{2}(\bm{k})=Q_{1}(\bm{k})=Q_{0}(\bm{k}).

(1.5)

Note that a
Q_{0}
-optimal triple can have
r
arbitrarily large, by e.g. adding vertices of weight 0 or splitting an existing vertex into two clones.
Let
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}_{t}(\bm{k})
be the set of
Q_{t}
-optimal triples
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
.
Let
\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
be the set of
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{2}(\bm{k})
with
\alpha_{i}>0
for every
i\in[r]
.
Let
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
be the set of
basic optimal solutions
, which are
Q_{2}
-optimal triples
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
with
\alpha_{i}>0
for every
i\in[r]
.

Given vectors
\bm{a}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})
and
\bm{b}=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{t})
, write
\bm{a}\leq\bm{b}
if
a_{i}\leq b_{i}
for all
i\leq\max\{s,t\}
where
a_{i}:=0
for all
i>s
and
b_{i}:=0
for all
i>t
.
We write
\|\bm{a}-\bm{b}\|_{1}:=\sum_{i\leq\max\{s,t\}}|a_{i}-b_{i}|
for the
\ell^{1}
-distance
between
\bm{a}
and
\bm{b}
.
In this paper we always take
\log
to the base
2
; from now on we omit any subscript.

One should think of feasible triples
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
as vertex-weighted edge-coloured multigraphs.
It is not hard to show that
F(\bm{k})\geq Q(\bm{k})
.
Indeed, given
\bm{k}
, a
Q_{1}
-optimal triple
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, take the complete
r
-partite
n
-vertex graph

whose parts
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
satisfy
|\,|X_{i}|-\alpha_{i}n\,|\leq 1
for all
i\in[r]
.
Consider those
s
-edge-colourings of

in which, for
x\in X_{i}
and

, we only allow colours in

to be used on

Every such colouring is
\bm{k}
-valid since every
\phi^{-1}(c)
is
K_{k_{c}}
-free.
Clearly

is bounded below by the number of such colourings of

, which is

\prod_{ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}}|\phi(ij)|^{|X_{i}||X_{j}|}=2^{q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})n^{2}/2+O(n)}=2^{Q(\bm{k})n^{2}/2+O(n)}.

Taking the limit as

, we have
F(\bm{k})\geq Q(\bm{k})
.
With Yilma, we proved the following results relating the determination of

to Problem

, including a matching upper bound.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
24
]

The following hold for every
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
.

(i)

For every

, at least
one of the

\bm{k}
-extremal graphs of order

(ii)

F(n;\bm{k})=2^{Q(\bm{k}){n\choose 2}+o(n^{2})}
, that is,

.

(iii)

For every
\delta>0
there are

and

such that if

with (non-empty) parts

and

then there is a

-optimal triple
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha^{\prime}})
such that

\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}^{\prime}\|_{1}\leq\delta
, where

\bm{\alpha}:=(\frac{|V_{1}|}{n},\dots,\frac{|V_{r}|}{n})
.

Thus, by (

), to determine

, it suffices to find the optimal solutions to Problem

, which has the smallest feasible set among the problems

.
Unfortunately this is difficult even when

Given a pair

, one can use the method of Lagrange multipliers to find a best possible

.

1.2
New results

The main contribution of this paper is a general stability theorem that determines the structure of any
n
-vertex graph
G
which is almost
\bm{k}
-extremal, i.e. with
F(G,\bm{k})=F(n;\bm{k})\cdot 2^{o(n^{2})}
. This will show that the structure of any such graph is similar to an optimal solution to Problem
Q_{0}
, and almost all valid colourings almost follow an optimal colour pattern.
This stability result holds for all
\bm{k}
satisfying a rather general condition which we call the
extension property
. Given
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
, one can easily check whether this condition holds.
Indeed, we show that in almost all instances for which
F(\bm{k})
is known,
\bm{k}
satisfies a strong version of this property.

Definition 1.2

(Clones and extension property)
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
.
Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r;\bm{k})

is

•

a

(under
\phi
)
if
\phi(ik)=\phi(jk)

and
|\phi(ij)|\leq 1
;

•

a
strong clone of
j
(under
\phi
)
if additionally
\phi(ij)=\emptyset
.

We say that
\bm{k}
has

•

the
extension property
if, for every
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
and
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r^{*}+1;\bm{k})
such that
\phi|_{\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}}=\phi^{*}
and
\sum_{i\in[r^{*}]:\phi(\{i,r^{*}+1\})\neq\emptyset}\alpha_{i}\log|\phi(\{i,r^{*}+1\})|=Q(\bm{k})
,
there exists

such that

is a clone of
j

\phi
;

•

the

if in fact

is a strong clone of

.

We explain the intuition and motivation behind this property in Section

.
For now we remark that it is generally easy to check whether

has the extension property, when
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
is known.
We check it for some cases in Section

.
For
\bm{k}
with the extension property, one can describe all solutions to
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}_{0}(\bm{k})
in terms of basic optimal solutions.
Every solution can be obtained by ‘blowing up’ a basic optimal solution
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
; that is, taking arbitrarily many clones of the vertices
and modifying part sizes so that the sum of vertex weights of clones of

equals
\alpha_{j}^{*}
for every

; and then possibly adding colour

, without creating a

,
where

is the colour with the largest forbidden clique. Without loss of generality,
we assume

, so that

.
If
k_{1}=k_{2}
then one cannot add any colour

creating a forbidden clique.

Our main result is the following stability theorem.
The

of two graphs

of the same order is the minimum number of edges that need to be added/removed to make

isomorphic to

.
Given graphs
G
and
H
and
\delta>0

is
\delta

\delta|V(G)|^{2}

Given disjoint

and

is
(\delta,d)
-regular
if
d_{G}(A,B):=e_{G}(A,B)|A|^{-1}|B|^{-1}\in(d-\delta,d+\delta)

|d_{G}(X,Y)-d_{G}(A,B)|<\delta

,

.
We are now ready to state the stability theorem.
It says that, for any large

-vertex graph
G
which has close to the maximum number of valid colourings; that is,
F(G;\bm{k})=F(n,\bm{k})\cdot 2^{o(n^{2})}
,
for almost all of its valid colourings
\chi
, there is a solution to
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}_{0}(\bm{k})
which describes the structure of
\chi
: it looks like a ‘blow-up’ of the solution.
Lemma
1.3
describes the structure of solutions in terms of basic optimal solutions,
and therefore there is a basic optimal solution
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
which describes the structure of
\chi
.
Part
(ii)
implies that, not only is there a partition of
V(G)
such that
\chi
has many edges of every colour
c\in\phi^{*}(ij)
between the
i
-th and
j
-th parts (and few edges of any other colour), these edges are in fact well-distributed and of roughly equal densities between these parts.

Theorem 1.4

(Stability)
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
with
k_{1}\geq\ldots\geq k_{s}
has the extension property.
Then for all
\delta>0
there exist
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and

such that the following holds.
If

vertices such that

\frac{\log F(G;\textbf{k})}{\binom{n}{2}}\geq Q(\textbf{k})-{\varepsilon},

then, for at least
(1-2^{-{\varepsilon}n^{2}})F(G;\bm{k})

which are
\bm{k}
-valid, there are

and a partition

of

such that the following hold.

(i)

For all

.

For all

and

(iii)

\bm{k}
does not have the strong extension property, and
all but at most

Moreover if

Somewhat conversely, if

One should note the similarities between the statements of Theorem

and Lemma

.
Indeed, this parallel shows that the gist of Theorem

is ‘near-extremal graphs look like blow-ups of solutions to

’.
This is not quite true within parts, as here

could be very far from a complete partite graph.
Note also that the partition

may be different for different colourings
\chi
.

We illustrate these statements with the example
\bm{k}=(5,3)
.
As it is not hard to show (or see Lemma

),
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}((5,3))
consists of just one element, namely
(2,\phi^{*},(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}))
, where
\phi^{*}(12):=\{1,2\}
.
Thus by Theorem
1.4
and the remark after it, the set of almost extremal graphs can be described as consisting of graphs that are
o(n^{2})
-close to
T_{2}(n)
with triangle-free graphs added into each part, or a

-free graph added into one part. Note that the partition

of Theorem

may depend on the colouring.
For example, if

is

with parts
V_{1},\ldots,V_{4}
, then Theorem
1.4
gives that for a typical colouring, there are disjoint pairs

such that almost all edges between

and

and between

and

have to be

and

, up to changing

If
\bm{k}
has the strong extension property, then

is close to a complete multipartite graph by Theorem

.
So the pairs

associated with the colourings specified by the theorem are close to each other up to a relabelling of colours.
We have the following corollary of Theorem

for

.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}

Then for all
\delta>0

n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and

Let

with

.
Moreover, for at least
(1-2^{-{\varepsilon}n^{2}})\cdot F(G;\bm{k})

\bm{k}
-valid

-edge-colourings

of

, there exists

Recall from Theorem

(i)
that at least one extremal graph is complete multipartite.
The following conjecture was made in
[
24
]
:

Conjecture 1.6
.

For every
\bm{k}
,
every
\bm{k}
-extremal graph is complete multipartite.

In
[

, we will use Corollary

\alpha_{1}^{*},\ldots,\alpha_{r^{*}}^{*}
for some

1.3
Applications

Armed with Theorem
1.4
, to determine asymptotically
\bm{k}
-extremal graphs, one need only solve Problem
Q_{2}
for
\bm{k}
, and then check for the extension property using the optimal solutions.

We apply our stability theorem to reprove stability for most of the cases in which
F(n;\bm{k})
has already been determined, in a systematic fashion.
For this, it suffices to solve Problem
Q_{2}
(which follows from these earlier works), and to prove the extension property. Proving the extension property is straightforward: there are
O(s^{r})
possible attachments of a new vertex to some
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
so a computer could check these for reasonable
s,r
. Actually, when optimal solutions have a particular nice form, which they do in almost all known cases, one can reduce the problem to determining solutions to some simple exponential equation over the integers (see Lemma
5.3
).
We cannot prove stability for
\bm{k}=(3,3,3,3,3)
, which
does not
have the extension property, strong or otherwise.

Theorem 1.7
.

Each
\bm{k}
among
(k,k)
,
(k,k,k)
,
(3,3,3,3)
,
(4,4,4,4)
has the strong extension property, for all integers

1.4
applies to every such
\bm{k}
.

As an example, we solve the optimisation problem for
s=2
and state a version of Theorem

to illustrate it.
Note that it would not be too difficult to prove stability for

directly.

.

Let

.

We write

.

1.4
A sketch of the proof of Theorem
1.4

Since the proof of Theorem
1.4
is quite involved, we give a fairly detailed sketch first.
Let
G
be a graph on
n
vertices such that
\log F(G;\bm{k})\geq(Q(\bm{k})-{\varepsilon})\binom{n}{2}
.
There are several ingredients to the proof.

Regularity lemma:
G
is close to some nearly optimal
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
in
\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{0}(\bm{k})
.

The multicolour version of Szemerédi’s regularity lemma was already used to prove the existing results on
F(n;\bm{k})
in
[
1
,
25
]
.
(For definitions and statements related to the regularity lemma, see Section
4.1
.)
Given a
\bm{k}
-valid colouring
\chi
of
G
, we obtain an equitable partition
U_{1}\cup\ldots\cup U_{r}
in which almost all pairs are regular in all colours in
[s]
.
Define a colour pattern
\phi:\binom{[r]}{2}\rightarrow 2^{[s]}
by adding the colour
c
to
\phi(ij)
if
\chi^{-1}(c)[V_{i},V_{j}]
is regular, and has density that is not too small.
The embedding lemma (Lemma
4.6
) implies that
\phi^{-1}(c)
is
K_{k_{c}}
-free for all
c\in[s]
.
In this way, much of the information carried by
\chi
is transferred to the tuple
\mathop{\mathrm{RL}}(\chi):=(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
, where
\mathcal{U}:=\{U_{1},\ldots,U_{r}\}
.

Of course, one still needs to prove that this process is in some sense reversible: that the structure of
G
itself, as well as the structure of its colourings, can be recovered from
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
.
This may not always be the case: we could have chosen some pathological
\chi
to generate
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
.
For example, in the case
\bm{k}=(3,3)
, the unique extremal graph is
T_{2}(n)=K_{\lfloor n/2\rfloor,\lceil n/2\rceil}
, but we could have chosen
\chi
which colours every edge with colour
1
.
Then we cannot recover many further colourings from
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
.

For this reason, we only wish to consider tuples
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
which are the image of many colourings; that is, some non-trivial proportion of all colourings.
Such a tuple is called
popular
; and we think of colourings
\chi
which map to this tuple as being good representatives of the set of all colourings of
G
.
Since, as we show in Proposition
4.10
, almost every colouring maps to a popular tuple, it suffices to fix a popular tuple
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
and only consider colourings which map to this tuple.
Intuitively, all such colourings should be similar.

Let
\alpha_{i}:=1/r
for all
i\in[r]
.
Then
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{0}(\bm{k})
.
So the regularity lemma allows us to pass from
G
to a feasible solution to Problem
Q_{0}
.
It turns out that since
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
is popular, we have that

q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})\geq Q(\bm{k})-2{\varepsilon},

and moreover that
G[U_{i},U_{j}]
is almost complete for all
ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}
(see Claim
4.1
).
Since we can choose
r
large (but still bounded), the number of edges of
G
within any
U_{i}
can be made very small.
Therefore the structure of
G
can be recovered from
(r,\phi,\mathcal{U})
.

Symmetrisation: from
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{0}(\bm{k})
to some
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
.

This is the main part of the proof (Lemma
3.1
), and in it we forget about
G
entirely, and instead concentrate on
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
.
We think of this object as a vertex-weighted coloured multigraph: the weights are given by
\bm{\alpha}
, and the coloured edges by
\phi
.
We will apply a version of symmetrisation to
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
.
Symmetrisation was originally used in (ordinary) graphs by Zykov
[
30
]
to give a new proof of Turán’s theorem.
In its most basic form, it is the process of considering two non-adjacent vertices
x
and
y
in a graph
G
, and replacing
x
by a clone of
y
, i.e. a vertex
y^{\prime}
whose neighbourhood is the same as that of
y
.
With Yilma
[
24
]
we used symmetrisation to modify any
\bm{k}
-extremal graph into one which is both extremal and complete multipartite (Theorem
1.1
(i)
).
Here, we use a version of symmetrisation as follows.
Suppose that there is some
ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}
such that
|\phi(ij)|\leq 1
.
Then we create a new feasible solution on
r
parts by making vertex
j
a clone of vertex
i
, or vice versa.
One of these choices will be such that the new solution
(r,\phi^{\prime},\bm{\alpha})
satisfies
q(\phi^{\prime},\bm{\alpha})\geq q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})
.
At the end of this process, we will obtain
(r,\phi_{f},\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{0}(\bm{k})
(where
f
is for
final
) such that
|\phi_{f}(ij)|\geq 2
whenever
i,j
are not clones and

q(\phi_{f},\bm{\alpha})\geq Q(\bm{k})-2{\varepsilon}.

(In fact we split each step into small steps to get slowly evolving colour patterns
\phi=\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{f}
.)
The solution
(r,\phi_{f},\bm{\alpha})
corresponds to a smaller solution
(r_{f},\psi_{f},\bm{\alpha}_{f})
in which all clones are merged, so
(r_{f},\psi_{f},\bm{\alpha}_{f})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{2}(\bm{k})
(and
q(\psi_{f},\bm{\alpha}_{f})=q(\phi_{f},\bm{\alpha})
is near-optimal).
A compactness argument (Lemma
2.3
) shows that there is some basic optimal solution
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
which is very close to the near-optimal
(r_{f},\phi_{f},\bm{\alpha}_{f})
in a very strong sense:
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
and
\bm{\alpha}_{f}
are close in
\ell^{1}
-distance, and
\phi_{f}
equals
\phi^{*}
between pairs with non-negligible weights.

The extension property:
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
and
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
are close.

Here, we mean ‘close’ in the sense of Lemma
1.3
.
So we would like to show that when we merge pairs
ij
with
|\phi(ij)|\leq 1
in
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
we obtain a weight vector
\bm{u}
which is close to
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
in
\ell^{1}
-distance, and
\phi\subseteq\phi^{*}
on pairs of non-negligible size. (This turns out to be a simplification; see below.)
It is of course far from clear that we have not changed
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
drastically to obtain
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
.
That this is not so is effectively a consequence of the extension property.

Having obtained
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
from symmetrisation, we can now, with the benefit of hindsight, follow the procedure backwards.
At each stage, we check whether the attachments of each vertex are large; if not we sequentially put bad vertices into an exceptional set, called
U_{i}^{0}
at the
i
-th step.
At each stage, the extension property implies that every vertex
x
is either in
U_{i}^{0}
or it corresponds to some vertex
k
in the optimal solution
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
; that is,
\phi_{i}(xy)\subseteq\phi^{*}(kj)
for all
j\in[r^{*}]\setminus\{k\}
and all vertices
y
corresponding to
j
.
Let
U_{i}^{k}
be the set of vertices corresponding to
k
at Step
i
.
Note that
|\phi_{i}(xy)|\leq 1
if
x,y\in U_{i}^{k}
.

So, going back through the procedure, at each step there are a small fraction of vertices
x
which were exceptional but are no longer exceptional, and these are moved to the
U_{i}^{k}
for which
x
corresponds to
k
;
and there are a small fraction of vertices moved into
U_{i}^{0}
.
When we return to Step 0, we want to show that

\phi\subseteq\phi^{*}
between any two classes
U_{0}^{j}
and
U_{0}^{k}
, even though
the attachment between
U_{0}^{0}
and the rest of the vertices, as well as the colour pattern within a class
U_{i}^{k}
may have changed.
Therefore, if we can show that
U_{0}^{0}
is small (Claim
3.1.2
) and every
U_{0}^{k}
,
k>0
, is about the same size throughout, then the procedure did not really change
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
much at all.
Furthermore, we have a partition
U_{0}^{0},U_{0}^{1},\ldots,U_{0}^{r^{*}}
of
[r]
such that
\phi\subseteq\phi^{*}
between
U_{0}^{i},U_{0}^{j}
,
i,j\neq 0
; and
|\phi(ij)|\leq 1
within a class.
In fact, the above sketch is a simplification, and though the exceptional set is always small, the other parts could change significantly in size. Nevertheless, we show that for each
i
there are
\tilde{r}_{i}
nonzero nonexceptional parts with part ratios given by some
\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{i}
, and
(\tilde{r}_{i},\tilde{\phi}_{i},\tilde{\bm{\alpha}}_{i})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
, where
\phi_{i}\subseteq\tilde{\phi}_{i}
between pairs and
|\phi_{i}(jk)|\leq 1
within a class.

Recovering
G
from
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
.

We now transfer the information we have gleaned about
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
back to
G
itself.
The partition of
[r]
induces a partition
X_{0},X_{1},\ldots,X_{r^{*}}
on
V(G)
.
For every
\chi\in\mathop{\mathrm{RL}}^{-1}((r,\phi,\mathcal{U}))
, we almost always have
\chi(xy)\in\phi^{*}(ij)
when
ij\in\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}
and
x\in X_{i},y\in X_{j}
.
In Claims
4.2
and
4.3
, we show for almost all these
\chi
, that
\chi^{-1}(c)[X_{i},X_{j}]
is regular.
Indeed, if
\chi^{-1}(c)[X_{i},X_{j}]
is not regular, then Lemma
4.4
implies that there is
c^{*}\in[s]
and large sets
X\subseteq X_{i}
and
Y\subseteq X_{j}
between which the density of colour
c^{*}
edges is significantly less than it is between
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
.
But Corollary
4.8
implies that very few
s
-edge colourings of
G[X_{i},X_{j}]
are such that some colour class (
c\in\phi^{*}(ij)
) has size much less than the average
|\phi^{*}(ij)|^{-1}
.

The final step is to look inside the classes.
We discretise by applying the regularity lemma again within each class (which is large by Lemma
2.8
).
This allows us to apply Lemma
2.5
to prove
(iii)
.

1.5
Organisation

Most of the paper concerns optimal triples for Problem
Q_{t}
rather than graphs.
In Section
2
we collect some tools concerning these optimal triples, and some consequences of the extension property.
The main result in Section

is the stability of optimal solutions given the extension property which is the key component of the proof of our graph stability result, Theorem
1.4
.
In Section
4
we transfer statements on optimal triples to graphs, and prove Theorem
1.4
, after stating and proving some tools concerning the regularity lemma.
Then we prove Corollary
1.5
.
Section
5
concerns applications of Theorem

, in particular it contains the derivations of Theorems
1.7
and

.
We finish with some concluding remarks in Section

.

2
Optimal solutions of Problem
Q_{t}

We will now explore some properties of optimal solutions, which will be useful in later sections.
The following proposition states that, in an optimal solution
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}_{0}(\bm{k})
, every vertex
i
of positive weight
attaches optimally
, in that the normalised contribution
q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})
to the sum

q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})=\sum_{i\in[r]}\alpha_{i}q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})\quad\text{where}\quad q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha}):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in[r]\setminus\{i\}\\
\phi(ij)\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\alpha_{j}\log|\phi(ij)|

is equal to
Q(\bm{k})
.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
and suppose that
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}_{0}(\bm{k})
.
For every
i\in[r]
with
\alpha_{i}>0
, we have that

q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})=Q(\bm{k})
.

Proof.

Without affecting the statement, we can remove all indices
i
with
\alpha_{i}=0
.
So assume that
\alpha_{i}>0
for all
i\in[r]
.
We use the method of Lagrange multipliers.
Recall that the constraint is that
g(\bm{\alpha})=0
, where
g(\bm{\alpha}):=\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}-1
.
Fix
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r;\bm{k})
and let

\mathcal{L}(\bm{\alpha},\lambda):=q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})-\lambda g(\bm{\alpha}).

Since the optimal vertex weighting
\bm{\alpha}
is in the interior of
\Delta^{r}
(and
\mathcal{L}
is continuously differentiable there), there is
\lambda
such that
(\bm{\alpha},\lambda)
is a critical point of
\mathcal{L}
. Thus

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\alpha_{i}}=2q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})-\lambda=0\ \ \text{ and }\ \ \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\lambda}=\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}-1=0\quad\text{for all }i\in[r].

We see that
q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})
,
i\in[r]
, are equal to each other and the common value is
\lambda/2=\|\bm{\alpha}\|_{1}\lambda/2=\sum_{i\in[r]}\alpha_{i}q_{i}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})=q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})=Q(\bm{k})
.
Therefore every
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}_{0}(\bm{k})
satisfies the equation in the statement of the proposition.
∎

The next proposition shows that the objective function
q(\phi,\cdot)
is Lipschitz continuous.

Proposition 2.2
.

[
24
, Proposition 11]

Let
s,r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
.
Let
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r;\bm{k})
and
\bm{\alpha},\bm{\beta}\in\Delta^{r}
.
Then

|q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})-q(\phi,\bm{\beta})|<2(\log s)\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\beta}\|_{1}.

The next lemma states that whenever we have a feasible solution
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{1}(\bm{k})
which is almost optimal, there is some vertex weighting
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
which is close to
\bm{\alpha}
such that

is an optimal solution.

Lemma 2.3
.

[
24
, Claim 15]

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
.
For all
\delta>0

{\varepsilon}>0

Let
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{1}(\bm{k})
be such that

Then there exists

Consider the following definition.

Definition 2.4

(Capacity)
.

Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
, a graph
G
with vertex set
[r]
, and positive integers
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r}
, we write
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r})G
to denote the graph obtained from
G
by, for each
i\in[r]
, replacing vertex
i
with a clique
K_{\ell_{i}}
, and joining every vertex in
K_{\ell_{i}}
to all vertices in the
cliques
K_{\ell_{j}}
such that
j
is a neighbour of
i
in
G
.

Let
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(G,k)
be the set of those
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r})\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
for which
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r})G
is
K_{k}
-free.

Since
(1,\ldots,1)G=G
, certainly
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(G,k)\neq\emptyset
whenever
G
is
K_{k}
-free.
It is easy to see that, if
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
satisfies
\bm{b}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(G,k)
, then
\bm{a}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(G,k)
for all
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{r}
with
\bm{a}\leq\bm{b}
.
We think of
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(G,k)
as being a measure of how far a
K_{k}
-free graph

is from containing a copy of
K_{k}
.
For example,
(1,2,2)\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(K_{3},6)
but
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(K_{3},4)=\{(1,1,1)\}
.

We now prove some facts about the capacity of
(\phi^{*})^{-1}(c)
in a basic optimal solution
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})
,
i.e.
q(\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})=Q(\bm{k})
,
|\phi^{*}(ij)|\geq 2
for all
ij\in\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}
, and
\alpha_{i}^{*}>0
for all
i\in[r^{*}]
.
The most important part is
(ii)
, which is essentially of a consequence of the fact that maximally
K_{k}
-free graphs on
r
vertices have non-trivial capacity if and only if
r<k-1
.
This lemma will be important in proving Theorem
1.4
(iii)
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
with
k_{1}\geq\ldots\geq k_{s}
.
Let

(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
and define
J_{c}:=([r^{*}],(\phi^{*})^{-1}(c))
.
Then the following statements hold.

(i)

J_{c}
is maximally
K_{k_{c}}
-free.

(ii)

With
\bm{1}\in\mathbb{N}^{r^{*}}
denoting the all-
1
vector, we have

\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(J_{c},k_{c})=\begin{cases}\{\bm{1}\}\cup\{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}^{r^{*}}:\|\bm{\ell}\|_{1}\leq k_{c}-1\}&\mbox{if }c=1\text{ and }k_{1}>k_{2}\\
\{\bm{1}\}&\mbox{otherwise;}\end{cases}

furthermore, if
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}(J_{1},k_{1})\neq\{\bm{1}\}
, then
J_{1}\cong K_{r^{*}}
.

(iii)

r^{*}\geq k_{2}-1
.

Proof.

Suppose that
(i)
does not hold for some
c\in[s]
.
Then there exist distinct
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}\in[r^{*}]
such that
i^{\prime}j^{\prime}\notin E(J_{c})
, and
J_{c}\cup\{i^{\prime}j^{\prime}\}
is
K_{k_{c}}
-free.
Let
\phi^{\prime}:\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}\rightarrow 2^{[s]}
be defined by setting
\phi^{\prime}(ij):=\phi^{*}(ij)
whenever
ij\neq i^{\prime}j^{\prime}
; and setting
\phi^{\prime}(i^{\prime}j^{\prime}):=\phi^{*}(ij)\cup\{c\}
.
By construction,
\phi^{\prime}\in\Phi_{2}(r^{*};\bm{k})
.
So
(r^{*},\phi^{\prime},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{2}(\bm{k})
. But

q(\phi^{\prime},\bm{\alpha}^{*})-q(\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})=2\alpha^{*}_{i^{\prime}}\alpha^{*}_{j^{\prime}}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{|\phi^{*}(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})|}\right)\geq 2\alpha^{*}_{i^{\prime}}\alpha^{*}_{j^{\prime}}\log\left(1+\frac{1}{s}\right)>0,

a contradiction. This proves
(i)
.

We now prove
(ii)
.
To do this, we need the following simple claim.

Claim 2.5.1
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
and let
H
be maximally
K_{k}
-free. Then every
x\in V(H)
lies in a copy of
K_{k-1}
if and only if
|V(H)|\geq k-1
.

Proof of Claim.

To prove the claim, note that the ‘only if’ direction is trivial.
Suppose now that the other direction does not hold; that is,
r:=|V(H)|\geq k-1
and there exists
x\in V(H)
which does not lie in a copy of
K_{k-1}
.
First consider the case when
N_{H}(x)=V(H)\setminus\{x\}
.
Then
H-x
is a
K_{k-2}
-free graph on at least
k-2
vertices.
Thus there is a non-edge
e
in
H-x
. Let
H^{\prime}:=H\cup\{e\}
.
Then
H^{\prime}-x
is
K_{k-1}
-free, and since
x
not does lie in a
K_{k-1}
in
H
,
x
does not lie in a copy of
K_{k}
in
H^{\prime}
.
Therefore
H^{\prime}
is
K_{k}
-free, a contradiction.

Consider now the case when there is some
y\in V(H-x)
such that
xy\notin E(H)
.
Then
H^{\prime\prime}:=H\cup\{xy\}
is
K_{k}
-free, since any clique which lies in
H^{\prime\prime}
but not
H
must contain
x
.
Again, this is a contradiction, proving the claim.
∎

Suppose that
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(c),k_{c})\neq\{\bm{1}\}
.
Then there exists
j\in[r^{*}]
such that
\bm{1}+\bm{e}_{j}\in\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(c))
.
Observe that the graph
(\bm{1}+\bm{e}_{j})J_{c}
is obtained from
J_{c}
by inserting a twin
j^{\prime}
of
j
and adding the edge
jj^{\prime}
.
If
r^{*}\geq k_{c}-1
, then
j
lies in a copy of
K_{k_{c}-1}
in

by Part
(i)
and the claim.
So
j^{\prime}
together with the vertices in this copy form a copy of
K_{k_{c}}
in
(\bm{1}+\bm{e}_{j})J_{c}
, a contradiction.
So
r^{*}\leq k_{c}-2
.

Suppose instead that
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(c),k_{c})=\{\bm{1}\}
.
Then
(\bm{1}+\bm{e}_{j})J_{c}
contains a copy of
K_{k_{c}}
for every
j\in[r^{*}]
, and this copy necessarily contains
j
(since
J_{c}
itself is
K_{k_{c}}
-free).
Trivially
r^{*}=|V(J_{c})|\geq k_{c}-1
.
We have proved that

\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(c),k_{c})=\{\bm{1}\}\ \text{ if and only if }\ r^{*}\geq k_{c}-1.

(2.1)

Let
C:=\{c\in[s]:r^{*}\leq k_{c}-2\}
.
If
C=\emptyset
, then
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(c),k_{c})=\{\bm{1}\}
for all
c\in[s]
.
Note also that
\{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}^{r^{*}}:\|\bm{\ell}\|_{1}\leq k_{c}-1\}\subseteq\{\bm{1}\}
.
So we are done in this case, and may assume that
C\neq\emptyset
.

By Part
(i)
, for all
c\in C
we have
(\phi^{*})^{-1}(c)\cong K_{r^{*}}
since this is the unique maximally
K_{k_{c}}
-free graph on
r^{*}
vertices.
Define a new solution on
r^{*}+1
vertices as follows.
Suppose, without loss of generality, that
\alpha_{r^{*}}\geq\alpha_{i}
for all
i\in[r^{*}]
.
Let

\displaystyle\phi(ij)

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\phi^{*}(ij)&\mbox{if }ij\in\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}\\
\phi^{*}(ir^{*})&\mbox{if }i\in[r^{*}-1],j=r^{*}+1\\
C&\mbox{if }\{i,j\}=\{r^{*},r^{*}+1\}\end{cases}

and

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle:=\begin{cases}\alpha^{*}_{i}&\mbox{if }i\in[r^{*}-1]\\
\alpha^{*}_{r^{*}}/2&\mbox{if }i\in\{r^{*},r^{*}+1\}.\end{cases}

Then
\phi^{-1}(c)
is
K_{k_{c}}
-free for all

, so
(r^{*}+1,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}_{1}(\bm{k})
.
Furthermore,

0\geq q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})-q(\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})=2\left(\frac{\alpha_{r^{*}}^{*}}{2}\right)^{2}\log|C|

and so
|C|=1
.
Let
C=\{c^{*}\}
.
Then
k_{c^{*}}-2\geq r^{*}\geq k_{c}-1
for all

.
That is,

and
k_{1}>k_{2}
.
Suppose that
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r^{*}})\in\mathbb{N}^{r^{*}}
.
Since

, the graph
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{r^{*}})(\phi^{*})^{-1}(1)
is a clique of order

.
Therefore
\mathop{\mathrm{Cap}}((\phi^{*})^{-1}(1),k_{1})=\{\bm{\ell}\in\mathbb{N}^{r^{*}}:\|\bm{\ell}\|_{1}\leq k_{1}-1\}
(note that this set contains

).
This completes the proof of
(ii)
.

Finally, Part
(iii)
is an immediate consequence of Part
(ii)
and (

).
∎

2.1
The extension property

In this section, we explore the consequences of the extension property.
Recall the equality from Proposition
2.1
which is necessary for all vertices of positive weight in an optimal solution.
Suppose we wish to extend an optimal solution by adding a new vertex of zero weight.
The following proposition shows that the normalised contribution of this new vertex cannot be more than
Q(\bm{k})
.
Given
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r+1,\bm{k})
and
\bm{\alpha}\in\Delta^{r}
, let

\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,\bm{\alpha}):=q_{r+1}(\phi,(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r},0))=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in[r]\\
\phi(\{i,r+1\})\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\alpha_{i}\log|\phi(\{i,r+1\})|,

which is the “normalised contribution” of the zero-weighted vertex
r+1
to
q(\phi,(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{r},0))
.

Proposition 2.6
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
.
Suppose that
(r,\phi^{\prime},\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
.
Let
\phi\in\Phi(r+1,\bm{k})
be such that
\phi|_{\binom{[r]}{2}}=\phi^{\prime}
.
Then
\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})\leq Q(\bm{k})
.

Proof.

Suppose not.
We will show that we can transfer a small amount of weight from
[r]
to
r+1
and in so doing increase
q(\phi,\cdot)
.
Let
\gamma>0
satisfy
\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})=(1+\gamma)Q(\bm{k})
.
Let
{\varepsilon}\in(0,2\gamma/(2\gamma+1))
.
Define
\bm{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{r+1}
by setting

\beta_{i}:=\begin{cases}(1-{\varepsilon})\alpha_{i}&\mbox{if }i\in[r]\\
{\varepsilon}&\mbox{if }i=r+1.\end{cases}

Then
\bm{\alpha}\in\Delta^{r}
implies that
\bm{\beta}\in\Delta^{r+1}
.
Now,

\displaystyle q(\phi,\bm{\beta})-q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})

\displaystyle={\varepsilon}({\varepsilon}-2)q(\phi^{\prime},\bm{\alpha})+2(1-{\varepsilon}){\varepsilon}\cdot\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})={\varepsilon}(2\gamma-{\varepsilon}(1+2\gamma))Q(\bm{k})>0,

a contradiction.
∎

This motivates the
extension property
, which we repeat for the reader’s convenience:

Definition 2.7

(Clones and extension property)
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
.
Given
r\in\mathbb{N}
and
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r;\bm{k})
, we say that
i\in[r]
is

•

a
clone of
j\in[r]\setminus\{i\}

(under
\phi
)
if
\phi(ik)=\phi(jk)
for all
k\in[r]\setminus\{i,j\}
and
|\phi(ij)|\leq 1
;

•

a
strong clone of
j
(under
\phi
)
if additionally
\phi(ij)=\emptyset
.

We say that
\bm{k}
has

•

the
extension property
if, for every
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
and
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r^{*}+1;\bm{k})
such that
\phi|_{\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}}=\phi^{*}
and
\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,\bm{\alpha}^{*})=Q(\bm{k})
;
there exists
j\in[r^{*}]
such that
r^{*}+1
is a clone of
j
under
\phi
;

•

the
strong extension property
if in fact
r^{*}+1
is a strong clone of
j
.

The extension property says that if we extend any basic optimal solution by adding an infinitesimal part with optimal contribution
Q(\bm{k})
, then the new vertex clones an existing one (with perhaps one colour on the pair spanned by the two clones).
Assuming that
\bm{k}
has the extension property, we can prove some properties of elements in
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
, including a uniform lower bound for vertex weightings in
Q^{*}
-optimal solutions.

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and suppose that
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
has the extension property.
Then there exists
\mu>0
such that
\alpha_{i}^{*}>\mu
for all
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
and
i\in[r^{*}]
.

Proof.

Suppose not; then, for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exists
(r^{*}_{n},\phi^{*}_{n},\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{n})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
and
i_{n}\in[r^{*}_{n}]
such that
\alpha^{*}_{i_{n}}<1/n
.
By passing to a subsequence, since
r^{*}_{n}<R(\bm{k})
, we may assume that
r^{*}_{n}\equiv r
and
\phi^{*}_{n}\equiv\phi
and without loss of generality that
i_{n}\equiv r
.
Since
\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{n}\in\Delta^{r}
and the simplex is closed and bounded, the Heine-Borel theorem implies that
\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{1},\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{2},\ldots
has a convergent subsequence
\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{i_{1}},\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{i_{2}},\ldots
, with limit
\bm{\beta}\in\Delta^{r}
.
Observe that
\beta_{r}=0
.
Without loss of generality, assume that
\bm{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t},0,\ldots,0)
, where
t\in[r-1]
and
\beta_{j}>0
for all
j\in[t]
.
By continuity (Proposition
2.2
),

q(\phi,(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t}))=q(\phi,\bm{\beta})=Q(\bm{k})
, so

(t,\phi,(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{t}))\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
.
Recall that
\alpha^{*}_{i_{m},r}>0
for all
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
By continuity and Proposition
2.1
,

\sum_{j\in[t]}\beta_{j}\log|\phi(rj)|=\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{r-1}))=\lim_{m\to\infty}q_{r}(\phi,(\alpha^{*}_{i_{m},1},\ldots,\alpha^{*}_{i_{m},r}))=Q(\bm{k}).

The extension property implies that there exists
i\in[t]
such that
\phi(rj)=\phi(ij)
for all

and

, a contradiction to
\phi\in\Phi_{2}(r;\bm{k})
.
This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

2.3
Non-optimal attachments

We derive a further quantifiable consequence of the extension property in the following lemma, which shows that if a basic optimal solution is extended by an infinitesimal part, if it is not a clone of an existing vertex then the deficit of its contribution is bounded away from zero.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
have the extension property.
Then there exists
\eta>0
such that the following holds.
Let
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
and
\phi\in\Phi_{0}(r^{*}+1,\bm{k})

\phi|_{\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}}=\phi^{*}
and

\phi
.

\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi,\bm{\alpha}^{*})\leq Q(\bm{k})-\eta
.

Proof.

Suppose that the statement of the lemma does not hold.
Then for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
(r^{*}_{n},\phi^{*}_{n},\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{n})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})

and

\phi_{n}|_{\binom{[r^{*}_{n}]}{2}}=\phi^{*}_{n}
and
r^{*}_{n}+1
is not a clone of any
i\in[r^{*}_{n}]
under
\phi_{n}
, but

\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}(\phi_{n},\bm{\alpha}_{n}^{*})>Q(\bm{k})-\frac{1}{n}
.
By passing to a subsequence (since
r_{n}^{*}<R(\bm{k})
), we may assume that
r^{*}_{n}\equiv r
;
\phi^{*}_{n}\equiv\phi^{*}
and
\phi_{n}\equiv\phi

(2.5)

and

is not a clone of any
i\in[r]
under
\phi
.
Since
\Delta^{r}
is compact, we may choose a convergent subsequence

\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{1},\bm{\alpha}^{*}_{2},\ldots
, with limit
\bm{\beta}
.
Now, since

2.2

Now the extension property implies that there is some

is a clone of

\psi^{\prime}(i^{\prime}j^{\prime})=\psi(ij)

and

, and

.

Let
(r^{*},\phi^{*},\bm{\alpha}^{*})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})

4
Stability of asymptotically extremal graphs

4.1
Tools for large graphs

One of our main tools is Szemerédi’s regularity lemma, which allows us to discretise a large edge-coloured graph and thus approximate it by a feasible solution to Problem

.
We will need the following definitions relating to regularity.

Definition 4.1

(Edge density, regularity of pairs and partitions)
.

Given a graph
G
and disjoint non-empty sets
A,B\subseteq V(G)
, we define the
edge density
between
A
and
B
to be

d_{G}(A,B):=\frac{e_{G}(A,B)}{|A||B|}.

Given
{\varepsilon},d>0

(A,B)
is called

•

{\varepsilon}
-regular
if for every
X\subseteq A
and
Y\subseteq B
with
|X|\geq{\varepsilon}|A|
and
|Y|\geq{\varepsilon}|B|
, we have that
|d(X,Y)-d(A,B)|\leq{\varepsilon}
.

•

({\varepsilon},d)
-regular
if
(A,B)
is

{\varepsilon}
-regular and
d_{G}(A,B)=d\pm{\varepsilon}
.

•

({\varepsilon},\geq\!d)
-regular
if it is
{\varepsilon}
-regular and has density at least
d-{\varepsilon}
.

An
equitable partition
of a set
V
is a partition of
V
into parts
V_{1},\ldots,V_{m}
such that
|\,|V_{i}|-|V_{j}|\,|\leq 1
for all

Our first tool states that a subgraph of a regular pair is still regular, provided both parts are not too small.

Proposition 4.3
.

[

Let

The next proposition states that, given a set of edge-disjoint subgraphs
G_{1},\ldots,G_{s}
of a bipartite graph, if at least one of the graphs
G_{i}
is not regular of density
s^{-1}
, then there is a
G_{j}
whose density on a pair of large sets is reduced.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
A,B
be disjoint sets of vertices,
s\in\mathbb{N}
and let
{\varepsilon}>0
be a constant with
1/|A|,1/|B|\ll{\varepsilon}\ll 1/s
.
Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{s}
be pairwise edge-disjoint subgraphs of
K[A,B]
.
Suppose that not all of
G_{1},\ldots,G_{s}
are
({\varepsilon},s^{-1})
-regular graphs.
Then there exists
c\in[s]
and
X\subseteq A
,
Y\subseteq B
with
|X|=\lceil{\varepsilon}|A|\rceil
and
|Y|=\lceil{\varepsilon}|B|\rceil
such that

d_{G_{c}}(X,Y)\leq\frac{1}{s}\left(1-\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}\right).

Proof.

Given
c\in[s]
,
X\subseteq A,Y\subseteq B
, let

\text{diff}_{c}(X,Y):=s^{-1}|X||Y|-e_{G_{c}}(X,Y).

If
G_{c}
is not
({\varepsilon},s^{-1})
-regular, then either

(i)

|\text{diff}_{c}(A,B)|>\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}|A||B|
; or

(ii)

there is some
X\subseteq A
and
Y\subseteq B
with
|X|\geq{\varepsilon}|A|
and
|Y|\geq{\varepsilon}|B|
such that

\bigg|\frac{e_{G_{c}}(X,Y)}{|X||Y|}-\frac{e_{G_{c}}(A,B)}{|A||B|}\bigg|>{\varepsilon};

or both.
(The immediate implication from the definition of
({\varepsilon},s^{-1})
-regular would have (i) replaced by
|\text{diff}_{c}(A,B)|>{\varepsilon}|A||B|
, which is stronger than the statement of (i).)
To prove the proposition, it is enough to exhibit
c^{*}\in[s]
,
X^{\prime}\subseteq A
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq B
with
|X^{\prime}|\geq{\varepsilon}|A|
and
|Y^{\prime}|\geq{\varepsilon}|B|
so that

\text{diff}_{c^{*}}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\geq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2s}|X^{\prime}||Y^{\prime}|.

(4.1)

Indeed, if we can find such
c^{*},X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
, then, setting
k_{1}:=\lceil{\varepsilon}|A|\rceil
and
k_{2}:=\lceil{\varepsilon}|B|\rceil
, we have that

\displaystyle\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle X\subseteq X^{\prime}}}{{|X|=k_{1}}}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle Y\subseteq Y^{\prime}}}{{|Y|=k_{2}}}}\text{diff}_{c^{*}}(X,Y)

\displaystyle=\binom{|X^{\prime}|}{k_{1}}\binom{|Y^{\prime}|}{k_{2}}s^{-1}k_{1}k_{2}-\binom{|X^{\prime}|-1}{k_{1}-1}\binom{|Y^{\prime}|-1}{k_{2}-1}e_{G_{c^{*}}}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\binom{|X^{\prime}|-1}{k_{1}-1}\binom{|Y^{\prime}|-1}{k_{2}-1}\text{diff}_{c^{*}}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime});

so, by averaging, there is some
X\subseteq X^{\prime}
and
Y\subseteq Y^{\prime}
with
|X|=k_{1}
,
|Y|=k_{2}
such that

\text{diff}_{c^{*}}(X,Y)\geq\text{diff}_{c^{*}}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})\cdot\frac{k_{1}k_{2}}{|X^{\prime}||Y^{\prime}|}=\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2s}|X||Y|,

as required.
So we will now concentrate on finding
c^{*},X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}
so that (
4.1
) holds.
Suppose first that (i) holds for some
c\in[s]
.
If
\text{diff}_{c}(A,B)>0
, then we are done by setting
c^{*}:=c
,
X^{\prime}:=A
and
Y^{\prime}:=B
.
So we may assume that
\text{diff}_{c}(A,B)<0
.
Observe that

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[s]}\text{diff}_{i}(A,B)\geq 0.

So
\sum_{i\in[s]\setminus\{c\}}\text{diff}_{i}(A,B)\geq{\varepsilon}|A||B|/2
.
By averaging, there is some
c^{\prime}\in[s]
such that

\text{diff}_{c^{\prime}}(A,B)\geq\frac{{\varepsilon}|A||B|}{2(s-1)}\geq\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2s}|A||B|.

So we are done by setting
c^{*}:=c^{\prime}
,
X^{\prime}:=A
and
Y^{\prime}:=B
.

Suppose instead that (ii) holds for some
c\in[s]
.
So there are
X\subseteq A
,
Y\subseteq B
with
|X|\geq{\varepsilon}|A|
,
|Y|\geq{\varepsilon}|B|
such that

{\varepsilon}<\bigg|\frac{\text{diff}_{c}(X,Y)}{|X||Y|}-\frac{\text{diff}(A,B)}{|A||B|}\bigg|<\frac{|\text{diff}_{c}(X,Y)|}{|X||Y|}+\frac{{\varepsilon}}{2}.

Therefore
|\text{diff}_{c}(X,Y)|>{\varepsilon}|X||Y|/2
.
Again, we may assume that
\text{diff}_{c}(X,Y)<0
, or we are done.
Then an almost identical argument to the one above yields

such that
\text{diff}_{c^{\prime}}(X,Y)\geq{\varepsilon}|A||B|/2s
.
This completes the proof.

4.1.1
Binomial tails

In order to prove Part
(ii)
of Theorem
1.4
, we will need Corollary
4.8
below, which is a simple consequence of the Chernoff inequality.
The combinatorial interpretation of this fact is that almost every partition of
[n]
into
k
parts is such that every part has size roughly
n/k
.
Write
X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)
if a random variable
X
is binomially distributed with parameters
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
p\in(0,1)
.

Proposition 4.7
.

[
18
, Theorem 2.1]

Suppose
X\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)
where
0<p<1
.
Let
k\leq np
.
Then

\mathbb{P}(X\leq k)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-(np-k)^{2}}{2np}\right).

Corollary 4.8
.

Let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta\in\mathbb{R}
, where
0<1/n\ll\delta\ll 1/k
.
Then

\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor(k^{-1}-\delta)n\rfloor}\binom{n}{i}(k-1)^{n-i}\leq e^{-\delta^{2}kn/3}\cdot k^{n}.

Proof.

Let
X\sim\text{Bin}(n,k^{-1})
be a binomial random variable.
Then Proposition
4.7
implies that

\mathbb{P}(X\leq\lfloor(k^{-1}-\delta)n\rfloor)\leq\exp\left(\frac{-(n/k-\lfloor(k^{-1}-\delta)n\rfloor)^{2}}{2n/k}\right)\leq e^{-\delta^{2}kn/3}.

But

\mathbb{P}(X\leq\lfloor(k^{-1}-\delta)n\rfloor)=\sum_{i=0}^{\lfloor(k^{-1}-\delta)n\rfloor}\binom{n}{i}\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{i}\left(1-\frac{1}{k}\right)^{n-i}=k^{-n}\cdot\sum_{i=0}^{(k^{-1}-\delta)n\rfloor}\binom{n}{i}(k-1)^{n-i},

as required.
∎

We will also need the following simple bound, which we state without proof.
Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and

such that

Then

\binom{n}{\leq{\varepsilon}n}\leq 2^{\delta n},

where for integers

e(G[V_{i},V_{j}])\geq\sum_{c\in\phi^{*}(ij)}|\chi^{-1}(c)[V_{i},V_{j}]|\geq\sum_{c\in\phi^{*}(ij)}\left(\phi^{*}(ij)^{-1}-\delta^{\prime}\right)|V_{i}||V_{j}|\geq\left(1-s\delta^{\prime}\right)|V_{i}||V_{j}|.

So

\sum_{ij\in\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}}e(\overline{G}[V_{i},V_{j}])\leq\frac{\delta}{5}\cdot\sum_{ij\in\binom{[r^{*}]}{2}}|V_{i}||V_{j}|\leq\frac{\delta n^{2}}{10}.

Finally, by Part
(iii)
of Theorem
1.4
,

\sum_{i\in[r^{*}]}e(G[V_{i}])\leq\delta n^{2}/(5s)
.
Together with (
4.21
), we have
d_{\text{edit}}(G,K_{\bm{\alpha}^{*}}(n))\leq\delta n^{2}/5
.
Suppose
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})
is another triple associated with one of the specified colourings.
Then

\|\bm{\alpha}-\bm{\alpha}^{*}\|_{1}\cdot n^{2}/2+o(n^{2})\leq d_{\text{edit}}(K_{\bm{\alpha}}(n),K_{\bm{\alpha}^{*}}(n))\leq 2\delta n^{2}/5
.
Moreover, each entry of
\bm{\alpha}
and
\bm{\alpha}^{*}
is at least
\mu
by Lemma
2.8
, and the above inequality can only be satisfied if
r=r^{*}
. This completes the proof.
∎

Applications

5.1
Recovering some previous results

Previous works
[
1
,
25
]
have (implicitly) solved the optimisation problem by solving a linear program with real variables
\bm{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{t})
such that any
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
corresponds to some feasible
\bm{x}
(but not necessarily vice versa).
If, for every optimal
\bm{x}
, there is some
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
which corresponds to it, then this triple is a basic optimal solution.
Unfortunately, for all but a few small cases, the optimal solutions of the linear program do not correspond to a feasible triple.

We define a ‘basic’ linear program, to which we will then add extra constraints.

Problem
L
:

Given a sequence
\bm{k}:=(k_{1},\ldots,k_{s})\in\mathbb{N}^{s}
of natural numbers,
determine

\ell^{\rm max}(\bm{k}):=\max_{\bm{d}\in D(\bm{k})}\ell(\bm{d})
,
the maximum value of

\ell(\bm{d}):=\sum_{2\leq t\leq s}\log t\cdot d_{t}

over the set
D(\bm{k})
of tuples
\bm{d}=(d_{2},\ldots,d_{s})

such that
0\leq d_{t}\leq 1
for all
2\leq t\leq s
, and
\sum_{2\leq t\leq s}td_{t}\leq\sum_{c\in[s]}\left(1-(k_{c}-1)^{-1}\right)
.

Say that
\bm{d}
which is feasible for Problem
L
is
realisable
if there is some
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
with

d_{t}=2\sum_{ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}:|\phi(ij)|=t}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}\quad\text{for all }2\leq t\leq s

(5.1)

and call such a feasible triple a
realisation (of
\bm{d}
)
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then
Q(\bm{k})\leq\max_{\bm{d}\in D(\bm{k})}\ell(\bm{d})
.
Moreover, the following is true.
Suppose that at least one optimal solution
\bm{d}
to Problem
L
is realisable. Then
\max_{\bm{d}\in D(\bm{k})}\ell(\bm{d})=Q(\bm{k})

and
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
is the set of all
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
which are realisations of some optimal (realisable)
\bm{d}
.

Proof.

Let
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
.
For all
L\subseteq[s]
, let

f_{L}:=2\sum_{ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}:\phi(ij)=L}\alpha_{i}\alpha_{j}
and
for all
2\leq t\leq s
let
d_{t}:=\sum_{|L|=t}f_{L}
.
Then

q(\phi,\bm{\alpha})=\ell(\bm{d})
.
We have

\sum_{2\leq t\leq s}td_{t}=\sum_{L\subseteq[s]}|L|f_{L}=\sum_{c\in[s]}\sum_{L\subseteq[s]\setminus c}f_{L\cup\{c\}},

so it suffices to show that
\sum_{L\subseteq[s]\setminus\{c\}}f_{L\cup\{c\}}\leq 1-(k_{c}-1)^{-1}
for all
c\in[s]
.
For this, let
n\in\mathbb{N}
and let
H_{c}:=H^{n}_{c}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})
be the graph on
n
vertices with vertex classes
X_{1},\ldots,X_{r}
where
|\,|X_{i}|-\alpha_{i}n\,|\leq 1
for all
i\in[r]
and
xy\in E(H_{c})
for
x\in X_{i}
,
y\in X_{j}
if and only if
c\in\phi(ij)
.
Then
H_{c}
is
K_{k_{c}}
-free since
\phi^{-1}(c)
is.
Therefore Turán’s Theorem
[
28
]
implies that

e(H_{c})\leq(1-(k_{c}-1)^{-1})n^{2}/2
.
Let
c\in[s]
.
So

\displaystyle\frac{n^{2}}{2}\sum_{L\subseteq[s]\setminus\{c\}}f_{L\cup\{c\}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{L\subseteq[s]\setminus\{c\}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}}}{{\phi(ij)=L\cup\{c\}}}}\alpha_{i}n\cdot\alpha_{j}n\leq\sum_{L\subseteq[s]\setminus\{c\}}\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}}}{{\phi(ij)=L\cup\{c\}}}}|X_{i}||X_{j}|+2s^{2}n

\displaystyle=\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}}}{{c\in\phi(ij)}}}|X_{i}||X_{j}|+2s^{2}n=e(H_{c})+2s^{2}n\leq\left(1-\frac{1}{k_{c}-1}\right)\frac{n^{2}}{2}+2s^{2}n.

Dividing through by
n^{2}/2
and taking the limit as
n\rightarrow\infty
gives
the required inequality.
∎

We wish to add more constraints to Problem

.

only yields realisable solutions in some very special cases, for example
\bm{k}=(k,k)
or
\bm{k}=(k,k,k)

.

-constraint

Let Problem

with the constraints in

be the optimal solution of Problem

.
We will still discuss

and

of
\bm{d}
for Problem
(L,I)
without referring to

when it is clear from the context.

For our purposes, it suffices to consider constraints as follows.
Let
T\subseteq\{2,\ldots,s\}
.
Next, given
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
, let

H_{\phi}(T):=\left\{ij\in\binom{[r]}{2}:|\phi(ij)|\in T\right\}.

(5.2)

Suppose that
H_{\phi}(T)
is
K_{k}
-free for all
(r,\phi,\bm{\alpha})\in\mathop{\textsc{feas}}^{*}(\bm{k})
. Then

\sum_{t\in T}d_{t}\leq 1-\frac{1}{k-1}

is a valid constraint.
This follows as in the proof of Lemma
5.1
from defining
H^{n}_{T}(\phi,\bm{\alpha})
to be the
n
-vertex
\bm{\alpha}
-blow-up of
H_{\phi}(T)
(in analogy with

) and using the observation that

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
s\in\mathbb{N}
and
\bm{k}\in\mathbb{N}
.
Let
I
be a set of valid
(T,k)
-constraints where each

and

is an integer.
Then
Q(\bm{k})\leq\ell^{\rm max}_{I}(\bm{k})
.
Moreover, the following is true.
Suppose that at least one optimal solution
\bm{d}
to Problem

is realisable. Then
\ell^{\rm max}_{I}(\bm{k})=Q(\bm{k})

and
\mathop{\textsc{opt}}^{*}(\bm{k})
is the set of all

which are realisations of some optimal (realisable)

.
∎

Let

there is

be the unique member of

Then

.

is determined completely by

and

6
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have proved a stability theorem which roughly says that all almost optimal graphs for the Erdős-Rothschild problem are similar in structure to the blow-up of a basic optimal solution with graphs of controlled clique number added inside parts.
From this, one can systematically recover almost all known stability results.
Unfortunately Problem
Q_{2}
is difficult to solve in general.
It would be very interesting to see it solved in further cases. Currently, all known solutions have been obtained by relaxing it to a linear program (which is easy to solve), whose variables are graph densities and whose constraints essentially replace combinatorial constraints such as some graph being
K_{k}
-free, with the linear constraint that its density must be at most
1-\frac{1}{k-1}
, by Turán’s theorem.
For some few cases, solutions of this linear program correspond to feasible solutions of Problem
Q_{2}
, but in general they do not.
So one possible avenue to solve it in more cases is to add more sophisticated constraints to decrease the feasible set of the linear program, which is typically much larger than that of Problem
Q_{2}
.

In
[
23
]
we apply our stability theorem to prove an exact result for every
\bm{k}
with the strong extension property, proving a part of Conjecture
1.6
.
Given Theorem
1.7
, this will systematically recover most existing exact results (see Table
1
).
For the weak extension property, it is harder to obtain an exact result as there is typically a large family of asymptotically extremal graphs, with similar structures, and these graphs could have small parts.
