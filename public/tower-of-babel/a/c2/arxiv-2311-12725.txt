Exponential Asymptotics of Ricci Flow Neckpinch
Hamidreza Mahmoudian

Abstract




Abstract

In this article we examine the formation of cylindrical neckpinch singularities in Ricci flow in compact manifolds. Rigorous examples of neckpinch singularity for rotationally symmetric initial data were first constructed by Angenent and Knopf in
[
AK04
]
, and examples with given asymptotic behaviour were constructed in
[
AK05
]
and
[
AIK15
]
. Here we discuss the asymptotic behaviour of the flow under Type-I assumption for general symmetric initial data, and show the asymptotic profiles in
[
AK05
]
and
[
AIK15
]
are the only possibilities.

1
Introduction

Let
M^{n+1}
be a closed Riemannian manifold and let
g(t)
be a solution of Ricci flow

\frac{\partial g}{\partial t}=-2Rc[g(t)]\qquad 0\leq t<T

on
M
with first singularity time
T
. By Hamilton’s classical result
[
Ham82
]
this is equivalent to

\limsup_{t\to T}\sup_{M}|Rm|=\infty

For any such Ricci flow, we can define its
dynamic blow-up
by

\tilde{g}(\tau)=\frac{g(t)}{T-t}\quad\text{where}\quad\tau=-\log(T-t)

More generally, we can define a
sequential blow up
to be any pointed sequence of the form
(M,g_{i}(t),p_{i})
, where
p_{i}\in M
and

g_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i}g(t_{i}+\frac{t}{\lambda_{i}})

for some
t_{i}\to T
and
\lambda_{i}\to\infty
.

We say the flow
develops a neckpinch singularity at
(p,T)
if

(M,\tilde{g}(\tau),p)\to(\mathbb{S}^{n}\times\mathbb{R},2(n-1)g_{can}\oplus g_{euc},p_{\infty})

in the smooth Gromov-Hausdorff sense. Here
g_{can}
and
g_{euc}
are the canonical round metric of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and the Euclidean metric of
\mathbb{R}
. In this work, we examine the specific rate of convergence to cylinder and the asymptotic shape of
\tilde{g}
for a neckpinch under the assumption of rotational symmetry.

In recent years, there has been much progress regarding both analysis of limit spaces arising after blow ups of Ricci flow solutions, and applications of fine asymptotic analysis of such convergence.

Recall that the flow is said to be
(globally) Type-I
if it satisfies

\sup_{M}|Rm(p,t)|\leq\frac{C}{T-t}

Limits of Type-I blowups around a sequence
(p,t_{i})
of space-time points, with a fixed
p\in M
, have been studied by Naber
[
Nab10
]
, Enders-Muller-Topping
[
EMT10
]
and Muller-Mantegazza
[
MM15
]
. Under Type-I assumption, such blowup sequences will converge to a gradient shrinker, which will be non-flat if
p
is a singular point (c.f.
[
EMT10
]
). For sequences
(p_{i},t_{i})
with varying base point in
M
, Bamler’s recent theory of
F-convergence
shows convergence to (possibly singular) Ricci solitons in a weak sense. It is worth pointing out that Ricci shrinkers with vanishing Weyl tensor have been classified by combined work of
[
PW10
]
and
[
MS11
]
: The only examples are the round spheres
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, the round cylinders
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, and the flat space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

On the other hand, when discussing asymptotic shrinkers arising from rescaling ancient solutions, a fine description of the convergence has been proven useful in classification of the ancient solutions. For instance, under the assumptions of rotational symmetry, such asymptotic methods have been used to prove uniqueness of Bryant soliton among certain classes of complete
\kappa-
solutions; in dimension
3
among solutions with nonnegative curvature and positive sectional curvature by Brendle
[
Bre20
]
, and in dimension
n\geq 4
among uniformly PIC and weakly PIC2 solutions by Li and Zhang
[
LZ22
]
(See also
[
BN23
]
for removing the symmetry assumption). Similarly, for
compact
ancient Ricci flows, the precise asymptotic behaviour derived in
[
ABDS19
]
has been used in
[
BDSN22
]
to show uniqueness of Perelman solutions as the only possibility besides round spheres. Similar classifications have been done for mean curvature flow.

In the context of neckpinch singularities, the first rigorous examples of the phenomenon were constructed by Angenent and Knopf in
[
AK04
]
. These are Type-I examples starting from a dumbbell-shaped initial data, with two large spheres joined by a thin cylinder neck. For an open set of such initial data, precise asymptotic behavior was derived in
[
AK05
]
. As one might expect, these examples become singular and
pinch off
in a single point. On the other hand, examples of
degenerate
neckpinches were constructed by Angenent, Isenberg and Knopf in
[
AIK15
]
. By degenerate, we mean the rescaled flow converges to a cylinder or the Bryant soliton, depending on the choice of blow up sequence. Contrary to the previous case, these examples are Type-II and become singular on an entire compact subset of the manifold.

In this work, it is shown that the behaviors constructed in those examples are the only possibilities in rotationally symmetric Ricci flows. One motivation for deriving fine asymptotic information is proving that singularities are generically isolated points. More generally, knowledge of asymptotic behavior on large scales combined with knowledge of possible singularity models will lead to information about the structure of high curvature regions. In our situation, it would be desirable to exclude
long tubes
that do not close with a cap. These would correspond to a compact subset somewhere in the middle of the manifold (as opposed to a region containing the umbilical
tips
on either end) that is becoming singular.

2
Main Result and Outline of The Proof

2.1
Statement of The Main Theorem

In order to state our result precisely, we follow the terminology in
[
AIK15
,
AK04
,
AK05
]
.

We assume
M
is diffeomorphic to
S^{n+1}
which we regard as
[-1,1]\times S^{n}
with
\{-1\}\times S^{n}
and
\{1\}\times S^{n}
identified to single points
S
and
N
. We say
g(t)
is
rotationally symmetric
, if it is preserved by the
O(n+1)
action on
S^{n}
fibres. In that case we can write

g(t)=\varphi^{2}(t,x)dx^{2}+\psi^{2}(t,x)g_{can}

We call
\psi
the
radius
, and the
S^{n}
-orbit
x=0

the equator
. We say
g(t)
has
reflection symmetry
if
\varphi
and
\psi
can be chosen to be even functions of
x
(Both rotation and reflection symmetry are preserved by the flow).

It is convenient to work with the geometric coordinate
s
, defined as

\displaystyle s(t,x)=\int_{0}^{x}\varphi(t,r)\,dr

It measures the geodesic distance between the
O(n+1)
-orbits at the equator and at
x
. We call a local minimum of
\psi
a
neck
and a local maximum of
\psi
a
bump
. We also refer to the points at
x=\pm 1
as
Poles
or
Tips
and to the regions between a pole and the first bump next to it as a
Polar Caps
.

Our main result is the following Theorem:

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(M,g(t)),\,0\leq t<T
be a Type-I solution to Ricci flow with first singular time
T
that has rotational and reflection symmetry as above.

Assume
\lim_{t\to T}\psi(0,t)=0
, and that
g(t)
develops a neckpinch singularity at the equator as
t\to T
. In particular,

u(\tau,\sigma)=\frac{\psi(t,s)}{\sqrt{2(n-1)(T-t)}}\to 1\qquad\text{uniformly for}\,\,|\sigma|\leq R\,\,\text{as}\,\,\tau\to\infty

for
\sigma=\frac{s}{\sqrt{T-t}}
and
\tau=-\log(T-t)
. Then one of the following holds:

1.

|u-1|\leq C_{A}\exp(-A\tau)
for every
A
, for
\tau\geq\tau_{A}
and
|\sigma|\leq A\sqrt{\tau}
.

2.

We have

u(\tau,\sigma)=1+\frac{\sigma^{2}-2}{8\tau}+o(\frac{1}{\tau})

uniformly over compact sets
|\sigma|\leq R
.

3.

For some positive integer
m\geq 3
and some constant
C
, we have

u(\tau,\sigma)=1+Ce^{\frac{2-m}{2}\tau}h_{m}(\sigma)+o(e^{\frac{2-m}{2}\tau})

uniformly over compact sets
|\sigma|\leq R
. Here
h_{m}
is the
m
-th modified Hermite polynomial.

2.2
Remarks About Assumptions

Some remarks about the assumptions in our main Theorem are in order. The reflection symmetry assumption is for controlling the location of the singularity. Without reflection symmetry, we have two natural choices: We can rescale at a neck and choose
\sigma=0
to correspond to the time dependent location of a minimum
\xi(t)
, or we can rescale at a fixed point
p
. The problem with both these choices is the lack of information about how the necks are moving. The first choice gives us terms coming from
\xi^{\prime}(t)
that we cannot control. With the second choice, the region with good asymptotic might be too far from the origin once we rescale. Considering the examples in
[
AIK15
]
, this will happen if we pick the wrong point.

The Type-I assumption plays two roles in our analysis. On one hand, it is important for convergence to the cylinder. However, it is reasonable to expect that a local Type-I bound should suffice for establishing the convergence. On the other hand, once we assume the convergence, our work does not use the Type-I condition in any essential way. What we actually need is a lower diameter bound on the part of
M
that is away from the tips.

Considering the results in
[
MM15
]
and Kotschwar’s classification in
[
Kot08
]
, assuming cylindrical neckpinches is not too restrictive. Note, however, that assuming the common definition of non-degenerate neckpinch singularity, which says the smooth Gromov-Hausdorff limit of parabolically rescaled metric around every sequence of space-time points is a cylinder, does not automatically imply
u\to 1
. This is because the functions
\psi
and
u
are not geometric quantities. Ideally this should be proven only using the PDE structure, similar to classic blow up results for heat equation. We expect the results of
[
BHZ22
]
to be useful.

The first case in our theorem is unlikely considering the non-degeneracy result of Lax
[
Lax56
]
. Similar situations have been dealt with by Sesum
[
Ses06
]
and Kotschwar
[
Kot20
]
. In
[
Kot20
]
, the argument depends heavily on Lojaseiwicz inequality satisfied by Perelman’s
\mu
functional near compact solitons, which was proven in
[
SW13
]
by Sun and Wang. Such an inequality is shown for cylinders in mean curvature flow by Colding and Minicozzi, but it is not yet available for Ricci flow.

2.3
Outline of The Proof

We now discuss the precise setup of the problem and explain the main outline of the proof. We follow the conventions and notations in
[
AK05
]
, and review some of the computations presented in
[
AK04
]
and
[
AK05
]
without proof.

If
g(t)
is a rotationally symmetric Ricci flow, the radius function
\psi
and the function
\varphi
satisfy:

\displaystyle\psi_{t}=\psi_{ss}-(n-1)\frac{1-\psi_{s}^{2}}{\psi}

(1)

\displaystyle\varphi_{t}=n\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi}\varphi

These can be derived using the equation for
g(t)
once we note

\displaystyle Ric=-n\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi}\,ds^{2}+\big(-\psi\psi_{ss}-(n-1)\psi_{s}^{2}+(n-1)\big)g_{can}

Note that the variables
s
and
t
do not commute. Their commutator is given by

\displaystyle[\frac{\partial}{\partial t},\frac{\partial}{\partial s}]=-n\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi}\frac{\partial}{\partial s}

We define the blown-up radius and the self-similar variables as

\displaystyle u(\tau,\sigma)=\frac{\psi(t,s)}{\sqrt{2(n-1)(T-t)}}

(2)

\displaystyle\sigma=\frac{s}{\sqrt{T-t}}

\displaystyle\tau=-\log(T-t)

These variables satisfy

\displaystyle[\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau},\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}]=-\left(\frac{1}{2}+n\frac{u_{\sigma\sigma}}{u}\right)\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}

The blown up radius satisfies the following equation in non-commuting variables

\displaystyle u_{\tau}=u_{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)+(n-1)\frac{u_{\sigma}^{2}}{u}

(3)

We can compute the equation for
u
in commuting variables by adding an integral term
J
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assuming reflection symmetry for all
t
close enough to
T
, the equation for
u
in commuting variables is

\displaystyle u_{\tau}=u_{\sigma\sigma}-\frac{\sigma}{2}u_{\sigma}-nJ(\tau,\sigma)u_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)+(n-1)\frac{u_{\sigma}^{2}}{u}

(4)

\displaystyle J(\tau,\sigma)=\int_{0}^{\sigma}\frac{u_{\sigma\sigma}}{u}\,d\sigma=\frac{u_{\sigma}}{u}(\tau,\sigma)+\int_{0}^{\sigma}\left(\frac{u_{\sigma}}{u}\right)^{2}\,d\sigma

(5)

We will work with the quantities

\displaystyle U=\log u\qquad f=U_{\sigma}=\frac{u_{\sigma}}{u}

(6)

The following evolution equations are straightforward to derive.

Lemma 2.2
.

The quantities
U=\log u
and
f=\frac{u_{\sigma}}{u}
satisfy

\displaystyle U_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}U+\frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{-2U}-2U\right)-n\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\right)f

(7)

\displaystyle f_{\tau}=\mathcal{A}f+\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1\right)f-nf^{3}-n\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\right)f_{\sigma}

(8)

where the operators
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{A}
are defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}+1

(9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{L}-\frac{1}{2}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}

(10)

Proof.

The equation for
U
is easy to derive. For the equation for
f=U_{\sigma}
, we can differentiate the equation for
U
to get (not that there are no commutator terms)

\displaystyle f_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}U_{\sigma}-\frac{1}{2}U_{\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\left(2e^{-2U}-2\right)U_{\sigma}-nf^{3}-n\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\right)f_{\sigma}

\displaystyle=(\mathcal{L}-\frac{1}{2})f+\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1\right)f-nf^{3}-n\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\right)f_{\sigma}

∎

When
u
is
C^{2}
-close to
1
, we have
U\approx u-1
and we expect the non-linearities to be small of quadratic and cubic orders.
In asymptotic analysis of ancient solutions such as
[
Bre20
]
or
[
ABDS19
]
, barriers related to Bryant soliton play an important role. These barriers are singular near the tip and have controlled boundary conditions near the center and near the tip. In our case, we have candidate barriers from
[
AIK15
]
. However, we are dealing with a forward equation and controlling the boundary conditions for the solution needs somewhat sharp estimates which we do not have. We therefore choose to follow ideas of Filippas and Kohn from
[
FK92
]
instead, which we now briefly describe.

Consider the equation

\displaystyle q_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}q+q^{2}\qquad q\to 0\,\,\text{on compact sets}

The operator
\mathcal{L}
is self-adjoint on the weighted Hilbert space

\mathfrak{H}=L^{2}(\rho\,d\sigma)\qquad\rho(\sigma)=\exp(\frac{-\sigma^{2}}{4})

with eigenvalues

-\mu_{m}=1-\frac{m}{2}\qquad m=0,1,2,\dots

Since
q\to 0
, we expect the projections of
q
on neutral and negative eigenspaces to dominate the projections on positive eigenspaces. Define
x(\tau),y(\tau),z(\tau)
to be the norms of projections of
q
on unstable, neutral and stable eigenspaces of
\mathcal{L}
respectively. These quantities satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-\|q^{2}\|

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq\|q^{2}\|

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}z+\|q^{2}\|

In order to control
\|q^{2}\|
, note that if
q\to 0
on compact sets, for any
k
and any
\varepsilon
and
\delta
we eventually have

\int q^{4}\rho=\int_{|\sigma|\leq\delta^{-1}}q^{4}\rho+\int_{|\sigma|>\delta^{-1}}q^{4}\rho\leq\varepsilon^{2}\int q^{2}\rho+\delta^{k}\int q^{4}\sigma^{k}\rho

It can be shown that for large
k
, for any
\varepsilon
the quantity
I^{2}=\int q^{4}\sigma^{k}\rho
eventually satisfies the inequality

\frac{d}{d\tau}I\leq-\frac{1}{2}I+\varepsilon\|q\|

Therefore by replacing
z
with
\zeta=z+I
we get the system of inequalities

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-\varepsilon(x+y+\zeta)

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq\varepsilon(x+y+\zeta)

(11)

\displaystyle\frac{d\zeta}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\zeta+\varepsilon(x+y+\zeta)

and we can apply the ODE lemma of Merle and Zaag
[
MZ98
]
to conclude
x+\zeta=o(y)
or
x+y=o(\zeta)
.

The strategy described above is not directly applicable to
u-1
or
U
for multiple reasons. First, the non-linear terms are non-local and depend on the first order term
f=\frac{u_{\sigma}}{u}
. For this reason, we choose to work with
f

instead. This basically allows us to control the non-local term with only weak
C^{2}
control of
u
. However, this comes at the cost of losing information about the first mode of
U\approx u-1
corresponding to eigenvalue
1
(this can be thought of as a constant which is killed with differentiation). We therefore need to control this mode separately.

Another major complication is the error terms coming from localizing the quantities with a cut-off function. We expect the convergence to cylinder to be slower than
\exp(-\Lambda\tau)
for some
\Lambda\gg 1
. Therefore, due the presence of Gaussian weight, it is natural to take a cut-off with a support of the size
A\sqrt{\tau}
. To deal with such error terms, we use a technical modification of the well known Merle-Zaag ODE lemma, Lemma
4.2
. Among other things, we show that the lower bound
\exp(-\Lambda\tau)
on a sequence of times implies the same bound for all times. Therefore, by choosing
A
in the cut-off large compared to
\Lambda
, we can guarantee negligible errors. However, for all of this to make sense, we need to show that the diameter of the rescaled metric is larger than
A\sqrt{\tau}
for any
A
. This is done using the Type-I condition, essentially by showing the existence of some non-singular region on either side of the cylindrical part.

In section
3
, we derive some apriori estimates for
u
. First, we use the Type-I condition to show that the two tips cannot become singular. This will show that the tip regions move away exponentially fast. In particular, the distance between the neckpinch and the tip regions will be greater than
A\sqrt{\tau}
for all
A
, and we have
u\geq\frac{1}{2}
on
|\sigma|=O(\sqrt{\tau})
. Then, we combine this lower bound, the Hamilton-Ivey pinching estimate proven by Zhang in
[
Zha15
]
and a barrier argument to derive
C^{2}
bounds. As discussed below, Sturmian theory for parabolic equations allows us to assume a fixed number of necks and bumps, and we essentially have singularities only at the points with
\psi\to 0
. The barrier argument is good for the region between a neck and a bump that are both becoming singular, and the Hamilton-Ivey estimate is useful for the region between the last singular neck and the bump next to it.

In section
4
, we derive the necessary estimates to imitate the Filippas-Kohn approach for
f
. We then state and apply our modified ODE lemma to prove that exactly one of the following scenarios happen: The neutral eigenfunction of
\mathcal{L}
controls
f
, or
\|f\|
converges to zero exponentially, or
\|f\|
decays faster than any exponential. We also show how to control the remaining mode of
u-1
using
f
, and present the proof of our main Theorem.

In sections
5
and
6
, we deal with the neutral and exponential cases and derive the ODE for the coefficient of the dominant mode. This will allow us to prove the asymptotic behavior of
u-1
on compact sets described in the remaining two cases of Theorem
2.1
.

3
Apriori Estimates

The goal of this section is to prove the following apriori bounds: For
|\sigma|\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}
and
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
we have

\displaystyle 1-\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}\leq u\leq C_{A}

(12)

\displaystyle|u_{\sigma}|\leq\frac{C_{A}}{\sqrt{\tau}}

(13)

Note that by the asymptotic analysis in
[
AK05
]
, these bounds are sharp.

3.1
Analysis of Polar Caps

We first show that the poles cannot be singular points themselves.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
p
denote one of the poles of
M
. For any sequence of times
t_{i}\to T
, let
M_{i}=M
,
g_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{T-t_{i}}g(t_{i}+t(T-t_{i}))
. Then the pointed sequence
(M_{i},g_{i}(0),p)
has a subsequence converging to the flat space.

Proof.

Since
T-t_{i}\to 0
, a subsequence (still indexed by
i
) converges to a gradient shrinking soliton
(M_{\infty},g_{\infty},p_{\infty})
by
[
MM15
]
. Since
(M,g(t))
has vanishing Weyl tensor, so does
M_{\infty}
. The results of
[
MS11
]
and
[
PW10
]
show that
M_{\infty}
is either the sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
, the cylinder
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
, or the flat space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
.

The Cylinder Case
: This is the easiest case to exclude. We simply note that the point
p
is an umbilical point for
(M,g_{i}(0))
, where as the cylinder
\mathbb{S}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}
has no umbilical points.

The Sphere Case
: The idea is to note that on the geodesics of
M_{i}
starting from
p
we will eventually see the cylinder geometry, whereas on a geodesic of the sphere we always see positive curvatures. There is an exhaustion
\mathcal{U}_{i}
of
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
and diffeomorphisms
\phi_{i}:\mathcal{U}_{i}\to M_{i}
such that
\phi_{i}(p_{\infty})=p
and
\|g_{\infty}-\phi_{i}^{*}g_{i}(0)\|_{g_{\infty}}\to 0
.

Let
g_{i}
denote
\phi_{i}^{*}g_{i}(0)
for short, and let us identify
p
and
p_{\infty}
and work on the sphere. Consider the
g_{i}
-geodesic
\gamma_{i}
starting at
p
with
g_{i}
-unit tangent vector
V_{i}
. Since
g_{i}\to g_{\infty}
on
T_{p}\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
, there is a subsequence of
V_{i}
that converges to a
g_{\infty}
-unit vector
V
with respect to
g_{\infty}
. Let
\gamma
be the
g_{\infty}
-geodesic starting at
p
and tangent to
V
(so it is a great circle). We may take a neighborhood
\mathcal{U}
of
\gamma
and assume
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{i}
.

Parametrize
\gamma_{i}
by the
g_{i}
-length and
\gamma
by
g_{\infty}
-length. For any fixed
D
,
\gamma_{i}(r)
uniformly converges to
\gamma(r)
with respect to
g_{\infty}
for
0\leq r\leq D
. Since
\gamma
will pass through
p
again for the first time after a fixed length
D
, we conclude
\gamma_{i}(D)
converges to
p
with respect to
g_{\infty}
and therefore with respect to
g_{i}
. We may also assume that
\gamma_{i}(r)
lies in
\mathcal{U}
for
0\leq r\leq D
and that there is a point
q_{i}
on
\gamma_{i}
which is at least
D/4
away with respect to
g_{i}
.

Now if we pull
\gamma_{i}
back to
M_{i}
with
\phi_{i}
(which is possible sine
\gamma_{i}\subset\mathcal{U}_{i}
), we see that
\gamma_{i}
is a geodesic on
M_{i}
that starts at
p
, has points at least
D/4
distance away from
p
, and is such that
\gamma_{i}(D)
is arbitrary close to
p
. Since
M_{i}
is rotationally symmetric with a pole at
p
, the only way this is possible is for
\gamma_{i}
to pass the other pole as well. But this is not possible if there is a neck-like region between the two poles.
∎

Now we show that an almost flat rotationally symmetric manifold must have an almost linear profile function.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
p
be a pole of
M
and let
s_{1}(x,t)
denote the
g(t)
-distance of
x
from the pole
p
. Consider a sequence of times
t_{i}\to T
and define
g_{i}(t)=\frac{1}{T-t_{i}}g(t_{i}+t(T-t_{i}))
. If the sequence
(M,g_{i}(0),p)
converges to the flat space
(\mathbb{R}^{n+1},\delta,0)
, the rescaled radii
u(\tau_{i},\sigma_{1})
converge to
\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sqrt{2(n-1)}}
uniformly on compact sets as
i\to\infty
. Here
\sigma_{1}=\frac{s_{1}}{\sqrt{T-t}}
.

Proof.

Fix a number
R
and consider the sectional curvature of
(M,g_{i}(0))
equal to
\frac{1-2(n-1)u_{\sigma}^{2}}{u^{2}}
. Since we have convergence to flat space, this quantity goes to zero uniformly on fixed
g_{i}
distances from the pole
p
. If for some small
\varepsilon
we have
|\frac{1-2(n-1)u_{\sigma}^{2}}{u^{2}}|\leq\varepsilon
, then as long as
u\leq 2R
we get
\frac{1-4\varepsilon R^{2}}{2(n-1)}\leq u_{\sigma}^{2}\leq\frac{1+4\varepsilon R^{2}}{2(n-1)}
. This implies

\sqrt{1-4\varepsilon R^{2}}\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sqrt{2(n-1)}}\leq u\leq\sqrt{1+4\varepsilon R^{2}}\frac{\sigma_{1}}{\sqrt{2(n-1)}}

By taking
\varepsilon
small depending on
R
, the right hand side will be strictly less than
2R
if
\sigma_{1}\leq R
and we are done.
∎

3.2
Estimates For
u
and
u_{\sigma\sigma}

We first recall two simple lemmas proven in
[
AK04
]
. Following
[
AK04
]
, we consider the derivative of the radius and the scale invariant difference of sectional curvatures

\displaystyle v=\psi_{s}

\displaystyle a=\psi\psi_{ss}-\psi_{s}^{2}+1

These quantities satisfy the equations

\displaystyle v_{t}=v_{ss}+\frac{n-2}{\psi}vv_{s}+(n-1)\frac{(1-v^{2})}{\psi^{2}}v

\displaystyle a_{t}=a_{ss}+(n-4)\frac{\psi_{s}}{\psi}a_{s}-4(n-1)\frac{\psi_{s}^{2}}{\psi^{2}}a

Lemma 3.2
.

We have the bounds

\displaystyle\sup|v(t,s)|\leq\max\{1,\sup|v(0,s)|\}

\displaystyle\sup|a(t,s)|\leq\sup|a(0,s)|

\displaystyle|\psi\psi_{ss}|\leq C

\displaystyle|2\psi\psi_{t}|\leq C

\displaystyle|Rm|\leq\frac{C}{\psi^{2}}

Proof.

Apply the maximum principle to equations for
v
and
a
for the first two bounds. They imply the third. The other two follow from Proposition 5.3 and Lemma 7.1 of
[
AK04
]
since only the first two bounds are used in their proofs.
∎

According to Sturmian theory, for
0<t
the number of roots of
v=\psi_{s}
is finite and non-increasing. Therefore we may assume the number of necks and bumps is constant and their location defines well-defined non-intersecting curves. Our assumptions imply
v(t,0)=0
and
\psi(t,0)\to 0
as
t\to T
, so the rough shape of the singularity around
s=0
is a number of consecutive necks and bumps (and the region between them and possibly the polar caps) having
\psi\to 0
. We can say a little more.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\xi(t)
be a local minimum or maximum of the radius and let
r(t)=\psi(\xi(t),t)
. Then
\lim_{t\to T}r(t)
exists. If this limit is zero, then for a minimum we have

\displaystyle-\frac{n-1}{r(t)}\leq r^{\prime}(t)\leq-\frac{n-1}{2r(t)}+\frac{C}{2n}r(t)

\displaystyle(1-o(1))\sqrt{2(n-1)(T-t)}\leq r(t)\leq\sqrt{2(n-1)(T-t)}

and for a maximum we have

\displaystyle-\frac{C}{r(t)}\leq r^{\prime}(t)\leq-\frac{n-1}{r(t)}

\displaystyle\sqrt{2(n-1)(T-t)}\leq r(t)\leq K\sqrt{(T-t)}

for
C
and
K
depending only on
g_{0}
and
n
.

Therefore, any neck or bump with
r\to 0
eventually satisfies
\frac{1}{2}\leq u\leq K
, and any such neck satisfies
u<1
and
u\to 1
.

Proof.

See Proposition 5.4 and Lemma 6.1 and the Remark after it in
[
AK04
]
. We can use the bounds from previous Lemma in case of local maximum and say
\frac{-C}{r}\leq\psi_{ss}
.
∎

An almost linear profile function implies large values of rescaled radius next to the poles. We can show that such regions of large radius move away from the neck exponentially fast.

Lemma 3.4
.

The
u
value of the left and right-most bumps is bigger than
D\exp(\tau/2)
for large
\tau
where
D
depends only on
g_{0}
.

Proof.

If
\psi\to 0
at the last bump, then
u\leq K
by the previous Lemma. However, because of almost linear
u
, we know
u
becomes arbitrary large at the bumps. Hence
\lim_{t\to T}\psi>D>0
and therefore
u>D\exp(\tau/2)
.
∎

Now we can prove a lower bound for
u
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For
A>0
there exist
\tau_{0}=\tau_{0}(g_{0},A)
such that for
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
and
|\sigma|\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}
we have
u\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

Our Lemma
3.4
implies there is a first bump on each side of the origin with
\lim\psi>D>0
and
u>D\exp(\tau/2)
. This together with
|u_{\sigma}|=|\psi_{s}/\sqrt{2(n-1)}|\leq C(g_{0})
imply that the region around the right (left) bump where
\frac{D}{2}e^{\tau/2}\leq u
has length (measured in
\sigma
) at least
c\exp(\tau/2)
for some positive
c=c(g_{0},D)
. So at the right (left) bump we have
|\sigma|\geq c\exp(\tau/2)>5A\sqrt{\tau}
for
\tau\geq\tau_{0}(g_{0},D,A)
. In particular the region
|\sigma|\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}
does not reach the polar caps. Since there are only finitely many local minima of
u
and each is converging to infinity or
1
, we can conclude that for
|\sigma|\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}
,
u(\tau,\sigma)
is bounded below by
\frac{1}{2}
.
∎

We will use the following application of Hamilton-Ivy pinching estimate, which was generalized to conformally flat metric in
[
Zha15
]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Assume the eigenvalues of Ricci tensor
\lambda=-\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi}+(n-1)\frac{1-\psi_{s}^{2}}{\psi^{2}}
and
\nu=-n\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi}
satisfy
\lambda,\nu\geq-1
initially, and assume
\log(1+T)>3
(We can achieve both by rescaling the initial metric). There is some
C_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
depending only on
g_{0}
with the following property: For
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
and at any point, the bounds

0<u_{\sigma\sigma}<\frac{1}{4L}\qquad|u_{\sigma}|\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{4(n-1)}}\qquad\frac{1}{2}\leq u\leq L

for some
L
imply the stronger bound

u_{\sigma\sigma}<\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}

Proof.

At the given point we have the bound

|R|\leq\frac{C}{\psi^{2}}=\frac{C}{(T-t)u^{2}}\leq\frac{C}{T-t}

since we have
u\geq\frac{1}{2}
. The derivative bounds imply

\displaystyle\nu=-n\frac{\psi_{ss}}{\psi}=-\frac{n}{T-t}\frac{u_{\sigma\sigma}}{u}<0

\displaystyle\lambda=\frac{1}{2(T-t)}\left(\frac{(1-2(n-1)u_{\sigma}^{2}-2uu_{\sigma\sigma}}{u^{2}}\right)>0

So by the Hamilton-Ivy estimate we have

\displaystyle\frac{C}{T-t}\geq R\geq-\nu\left(\log(-\nu)+\log(1+t)-3\right)\geq-\nu\log(-\nu)=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{T-t}\frac{u_{\sigma\sigma}}{u}\log\left(\frac{n}{T-t}\frac{u_{\sigma\sigma}}{u}\right)=\frac{n}{T-t}\alpha(\log n+\tau+\log\alpha)

where
\alpha=\frac{u_{\sigma\sigma}}{u}
, and we assume
t
is close enough to
T
. Now if
\alpha>\frac{\max\{4C/n,1\}}{\tau}
and
\tau
is large enough so that
\frac{\tau}{2}\geq\log\tau
, we get

\displaystyle\frac{C}{n}\geq\alpha(\tau+\log\alpha)\geq\alpha(\tau+\log\frac{\max\{4C/n,1\}}{\tau})\geq\alpha(\frac{\tau}{2})\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{2C/n}{\tau}\geq\alpha

which is a contradiction.
∎

The previous Lemma gives us our first apriori estimates.

Lemma 3.7
.

There is some
C_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
depending only on
g_{0}
such that for any neck
\sigma_{*}(\tau)
with radius
\psi
becoming zero at
t=T
and
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
, we have

\displaystyle u_{\sigma\sigma}(\tau,\sigma_{*})\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}

\displaystyle 1-\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}\leq u(\tau,\sigma_{*})

Proof.

Let
u_{*}=u(\tau,\sigma_{*})
. Since
\psi\to 0
, we must have
u_{*}\to 1
. We may assume
\tau_{0}
is large enough so that Lemma
3.6
holds,
\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}\leq\frac{1}{8}
in that Lemma, and
\frac{1}{2}\leq u_{*}\leq 1
. Since
u_{*}\to 1
,
u_{\sigma\sigma}(\tau,\sigma_{*})\geq\frac{1}{4}
for large
\tau
would imply

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}u_{*}^{2}=2u_{*}u_{\tau}(\tau,\sigma_{*})=2u_{*}u_{\sigma\sigma}(\tau,\sigma_{*})+u_{*}^{2}-1\geq\frac{1}{8}

which cannot hold for too long since
u_{*}\leq 1
. But if we ever have
u_{\sigma\sigma}\leq\frac{1}{4}
at the neck, then Lemma
3.6
is applicable (since
u_{\sigma}=0
at the neck) and we get
u_{\sigma\sigma}\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}\leq\frac{1}{8}<\frac{1}{4}
from that point on.

This in particular implies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}u(\tau,\sigma_{*}(\tau))=u_{\tau}(\tau,\sigma_{*})=

\displaystyle u_{\sigma\sigma}(\tau,\sigma_{*})+\frac{1}{2}(u(\tau,\sigma_{*})-\frac{1}{u(\tau,\sigma_{*})})\leq\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}+\frac{u(\tau,\sigma_{*})}{2}-\frac{1}{2}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}(e^{-\tau/2}u_{*})\leq e^{-\tau/2}\left(\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\Rightarrow

\displaystyle 0-e^{-\tau/2}u_{*}=\left.e^{-\tau/2}u_{*}\right\rvert_{\tau}^{\infty}\leq\int_{\tau}^{\infty}e^{-r/2}\left(\frac{C_{0}}{r}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,dr\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\tau}^{\infty}e^{-r/2}\left(\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\,dr=\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}2e^{-\tau/2}-e^{-\tau/2}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle 1-\frac{C}{\tau}\leq u(\tau,\sigma_{*})

∎

3.3
Estimate For
u_{\sigma}

We now move on to the apriori decay estimate for
u_{\sigma}
. Any point with
|\sigma|\leq A\sqrt{\tau}
is between the left-most and right-most bumps. So, at any
\tau
, it would either be next to a bump with
u\to\infty
, or between a neck with
u\to 1
and a bump with
u\leq K
. In the first case we can integrate the estimate in Lemma
3.6
from the nearest neck. But in the second case, the nearest neck might be too far and this idea does not work. We can integrate Lemma
3.6
only up to a distance
o(\tau)
because the upper bound for
u_{\sigma}
is not guaranteed for larger scales.

We use a barrier argument instead. Consider a neck and focus on the region on the right or left side of it between the neck and the next bump. In such a region, we can use
u
as a variable and compute the evolution equation of
\psi_{s}^{2}
in this new variable. This has been done in multiple previous works, such as
[
AIK15
]
. We will however include the proof for completeness. After computing the equation, we construct an upper barrier in terms of
u
. The boundary conditions are controlled using the estimates for
u
in the previous subsection.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
Z(\tau,u)
be a function such that

\psi_{s}^{2}(t,x)=2(n-1)u_{\sigma}^{2}(\tau,\sigma)=Z(\tau,u(\tau,\sigma))

The function
Z
satisfies the following equation
\mathcal{F}[Z]=0
with commuting variables
u
and
\tau
:

\displaystyle ZZ_{uu}-\frac{1}{2}Z_{u}^{2}+\frac{n-1-Z}{u}Z_{u}+\frac{2(n-1)}{u^{2}}(1-Z)Z-(n-1)uZ_{u}-2(n-1)Z_{\tau}=0

(14)

Proof.

We differentiate both sides of the defining relation for
Z
with respect to
\tau
. To compute the equation for
u_{\sigma}^{2}
, we start from the equation for
u
and differentiate. Let
z=u_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2(n-1)}}\sqrt{Z}
. In the non-commuting variables we have

\displaystyle u_{\tau}=u_{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)+(n-1)\frac{u_{\sigma}^{2}}{u}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle z_{\tau}=z_{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\left(z+\frac{z}{u^{2}}\right)+2(n-1)\frac{zz_{\sigma}}{u}-(n-1)\frac{z^{3}}{u^{2}}-\left(\frac{1}{2}+n\frac{z_{\sigma}}{u}\right)z

\displaystyle=z_{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\frac{z}{u^{2}}+(n-2)\frac{zz_{\sigma}}{u}-(n-1)\frac{z^{3}}{u^{2}}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle Z_{\tau}=2(n-1)(z^{2})_{\tau}=Z_{\sigma\sigma}-4(n-1)(z_{\sigma})^{2}+\frac{1}{u^{2}}Z+(n-2)\frac{zZ_{\sigma}}{u}-\frac{1}{u^{2}}Z^{2}

where we have used
2zz_{\sigma\sigma}=(z^{2})_{\sigma\sigma}-2(z_{\sigma})^{2}
and
2z^{2}z_{\sigma}=z(z^{2})_{\sigma}
. Now note

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}=u_{\sigma}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}=z\frac{\partial}{\partial u}=\sqrt{\frac{Z}{2(n-1)}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}

So we get
zZ_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}ZZ_{u}
and

\displaystyle z_{\sigma}=\sqrt{\frac{Z}{2(n-1)}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\sqrt{\frac{Z}{2(n-1)}}=\frac{1}{4(n-1)}Z_{u}

\displaystyle Z_{\sigma\sigma}=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\sqrt{Z}\frac{\partial}{\partial u}\left(\sqrt{Z}Z_{u}\right)=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\left(\frac{1}{2}Z_{u}^{2}+ZZ_{uu}\right)

and hence we have

\displaystyle 2(n-1)Z_{\tau}=ZZ_{uu}+\frac{2(n-1)}{u^{2}}Z(1-Z)+(n-2)\frac{ZZ_{u}}{u}

To get the equation in commuting variables, we must add
2(n-1)Z_{u}u_{\tau}
to the right hand side. We have
2u_{\sigma}u_{\sigma\sigma}=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}Z_{\sigma}=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}u_{\sigma}Z_{u}
, therefore

\displaystyle u_{\tau}=u_{\sigma\sigma}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)+(n-1)\frac{u_{\sigma}^{2}}{u}=\frac{1}{4(n-1)}Z_{u}+\frac{1}{2}\left(u-\frac{1}{u}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\frac{Z}{u}\Rightarrow

\displaystyle 2(n-1)Z_{u}u_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2}Z_{u}^{2}+(n-1)\left(+(u-\frac{1}{u})Z_{u}+\frac{ZZ_{u}}{u}\right)

Putting everything together, we are done.
∎

We now construct a super-solution for the equation we derived.

Lemma 3.9
.

Consider the equation

z_{\tau}=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\left(zz_{uu}-\frac{1}{2}z_{u}^{2}+\frac{n-1-z}{u}z_{u}+\frac{2(n-1)}{u^{2}}(1-z)z-(n-1)uz_{u}\right)

(15)

For every positive number
c
and any
L>1
, there exist
B_{0}
and
\tau_{0}
depending on
c
and
L
such that for
B\geq B_{0}
and
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
the function

\bar{Z}(\tau,u)=B\frac{1}{\tau}\left(1-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{2}}\right)

is a super-solution for
1-\frac{c}{\tau}\leq u\leq L
.

Proof.

For notational simplicity, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[z](\tau,u)=\left.\left(\frac{2}{u^{2}}z+(\frac{1}{u}-u)z_{u}\right)\right\rvert_{(\tau,u)}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}[z](\tau,u)=\left.\left(zz_{uu}-\frac{1}{2}z_{u}^{2}-\frac{1}{u}zz_{u}-\frac{2(n-1)}{u^{2}}z^{2}\right)\right\rvert_{(\tau,u)}

so the equation for
z
can be written as

\displaystyle(n-1)\mathcal{D}[z]+\mathcal{Q}[z]-2(n-1)z_{\tau}=\mathcal{F}[z]=0

First let
Z_{1}(\tau,u)=B\frac{1}{\tau}\left(1-\frac{1}{u^{2}}\right)
. For any
L_{1}
and for
1\leq u\leq L_{1}
we have

\displaystyle(n-1)\mathcal{D}[Z_{1}](\tau,u)=0

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}[Z_{1}](\tau,u)=B^{2}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\left(\frac{2(1-n)}{u^{2}}+\frac{4(n-3)}{u^{4}}+\frac{2(4-n)}{u^{6}}\right)<-B^{2}\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}C_{L_{1}}\leq 0

for some positive
C_{L_{1}}
depending on
n
and
L_{1}
. Now assume
\tau\geq 1
and pick
L_{1}>L+c
. We have
\bar{Z}(\tau,u)=Z_{1}(\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})
, and the assertion holds essentially because a perturbation of order
\frac{1}{\tau}
creates errors of order at most
\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}
.
We compute the contribution of
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{Q}
and
\bar{Z}_{\tau}
separately. Set
\bar{Z}=\frac{B}{\tau}H
. We have

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)-\mathcal{D}[Z_{1}](\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})

\displaystyle=(\frac{2}{u^{2}}-\frac{2}{(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{2}})Z+(\frac{1}{u}-u-\frac{1}{u+\frac{c}{\tau}}+u+\frac{c}{\tau})Z_{u}

\displaystyle=\frac{2Bc}{\tau^{2}}(\frac{2u+\frac{c}{\tau}}{u^{2}(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{2}})H+\frac{Bc}{\tau^{2}}(\frac{1}{u(u+\frac{c}{\tau})}+1)H_{u}\leq C\frac{B}{\tau^{2}}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)-\mathcal{Q}[Z_{1}](\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})

\displaystyle=-(\frac{1}{u}-\frac{1}{u+\frac{c}{\tau}})\bar{Z}\bar{Z}_{u}-2(n-1)(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{2}})\bar{Z}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{B^{2}c}{\tau^{3}}\frac{1}{u(u+\frac{c}{\tau})}HH_{u}-2(n-1)\frac{B^{2}c}{\tau^{3}}(\frac{2u+\frac{c}{\tau}}{u^{2}(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{2}})H^{2}\leq C\frac{B}{\tau^{3}}

\displaystyle-\bar{Z}_{\tau}(\tau,u)=\left(B\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}(1-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{2}})+Bc\frac{1}{\tau^{3}}\frac{2}{(u+\frac{c}{\tau})^{3}}\right)\leq C\frac{B}{\tau^{2}}

We have used the fact that
H
and
H_{u}
are positive and bounded on
[1-\frac{c}{\tau},L]
. Putting these together we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)=(n-1)\mathcal{D}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)+\mathcal{Q}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)-2(n-1)\bar{Z}_{\tau}(\tau,u)=

\displaystyle(n-1)\left(\mathcal{D}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)-\mathcal{D}[Z_{1}](\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})\right)+\left(\mathcal{Q}[\bar{Z}](\tau,u)-\mathcal{Q}[Z_{1}](\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})\right)

\displaystyle+(n-1)\mathcal{D}[Z_{1}](\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})+\mathcal{Q}[Z_{1}](\tau,u+\frac{c}{\tau})-2(n-1)\bar{Z}_{\tau}(\tau,u)\leq

\displaystyle C\frac{B}{\tau^{2}}+C\frac{B}{\tau^{3}}-\frac{B^{2}}{\tau^{2}}C_{L_{1}}^{2}

for some

depending on
c
,
n
and

. Since

, by picking

and then
\tau
large depending on
c
,
n
and

we can make the last expression negative.
∎

\displaystyle Z(\tau,u)\leq B\frac{1}{\tau}\left(1-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{2C_{0}}{\tau})^{2}}\right)

, take

of Lemma

. Assume

\displaystyle\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}\leq\frac{1}{4}

\displaystyle\frac{4C_{0}A}{\sqrt{\tau}}\leq\frac{1}{2\sqrt{4(n-1)}}\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}\leq\frac{1}{4(2+16C_{0}A^{2})}

and by our choice

\displaystyle u_{\sigma}(\tau,\sigma)=\int_{\underline{\sigma}_{k+1}}^{\sigma}u_{\sigma\sigma}\,d\sigma\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}\frac{C_{0}}{\tau}=\frac{4C_{0}A}{\sqrt{\tau}}<\frac{1}{\sqrt{4(n-1)}}

\displaystyle u(\tau,\sigma)=u(\tau,\underline{\sigma}_{k+1})+\int_{\underline{\sigma}_{k+1}}^{\sigma}u_{\sigma}\,d\sigma\leq 1+16C_{0}A^{2}<L

since
\sigma-\underline{\sigma}_{k+1}\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}
. This shows that there cannot be a first point for the assumption in Lemma
3.6
to fail within
|\sigma|\leq 4A\sqrt{\tau}

The second possibility is
\underline{\sigma}_{i}(\tau)\leq\sigma\leq\overline{\sigma}_{i}(\tau)
or
\overline{\sigma}_{i}(\tau)\leq\sigma\leq\underline{\sigma}_{i+1}(\tau)
. Between any consecutive neck and bump with
\frac{1}{2}\leq u\leq K
we have

\displaystyle\psi_{s}^{2}=2(n-1)u_{\sigma}^{2}\leq B\frac{1}{\tau}\left(1-\frac{1}{(u+\frac{2C_{0}}{\tau})^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{C}{\tau}

∎

4
Asymptotic Analysis of The Profile

As mentioned before, we choose to work with the quantity

and follow the strategy of Filippas and Kohn described in section

. We start with localizing the equation for

derived in Lemma

and state the precise estimates needed at each step. Initially, we only need very weak control of

As discussed before, in order to deal with the exponentially small error terms coming from cut-offs, we use a technical modification of the Merle-Zaag ODE lemma. This is Lemma

, and it is proven in the Appendix. We postpone some of the more tedious proofs and computations to the end of the section in order to keep the flow of the arguments.

We will also discuss the following two key steps. First, we show that in the case of exponential decay, the optimal decay rate is either infinity (faster than any exponential), or an eigenvalue

of the operator

, assuming the contents of the following sections.

From now on, we fix a smooth function

All the norms and inner products are in the Gaussian weighted

We also assume, without further mention, that

We have the following estimate for

Proposition 4.2

Suppose

The proof of this proposition actually shows the following refined variant which will be needed later.

Corollary 4.3

Instead of

We now consider projections on eigenspaces. The operator

\displaystyle\text{$-\lambda_{m}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{m}{2}$ for $m=0,1,2,\dots$},

Define the quantities

\displaystyle x(\tau)^{2}=\|\pi_{+}(f\eta)\|^{2}=\langle f\eta,h_{0}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle y(\tau)^{2}=\|\pi_{0}(f\eta)\|^{2}=\langle f\eta,h_{1}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle z(\tau)^{2}=\|\pi_{-}(f\eta)\|^{2}=\sum_{m=2}^{\infty}\langle f\eta,h_{m}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle\zeta(\tau)=z(\tau)+I(\tau)

where

Proposition 4.3
.

For any
\varepsilon

and
4.2

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-\varepsilon(x+y+\zeta)-C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq\varepsilon(x+y+\zeta)+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{d\zeta}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\zeta+\varepsilon(x+y+\zeta)+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

for some

and some universal

.

We now present the following adaptation of the well known ODE lemma of Merle and Zaag
[

]
. The proof is given in the Appendix.

Lemma 4.2
.

Let

be non-negative functions of
\tau

\varepsilon
, there exists
\tau_{\varepsilon}
such that for
\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-\varepsilon(x+y+z)-B\exp(-b\tau)

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq\varepsilon(x+y+z)+B\exp(-b\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}z+\varepsilon(x+y+z)+B\exp(-b\tau)

where

and

are fixed. Then exactly one of the following holds:
(1)
We have
x\geq Ce^{\delta\tau}
for some
\delta
;
(2)
We have
x+z=o(y)
, in which case
c_{\delta}e^{-\delta\tau}\leq y\leq C_{\delta}e^{\delta\tau}
for any
\delta
;
(3)
we have
x+y\leq C(z+\exp(-b\tau))
for some
C
, in which case
x+y+z\leq C_{\delta}\exp(-(\frac{1}{2}-\delta)\tau)
for any
\delta
.

Using our ODE Lemma, we can analyze the projections of
f\eta
. Note that, by making

in the exponential term corresponding to

. Note that the bound

for

implies

\displaystyle\|f\eta\|^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}+z^{2}\leq\frac{C}{\tau}\to 0

\displaystyle I^{2}=\int f^{4}\theta^{4}\sigma^{k}\rho\leq\frac{C_{k}}{\tau^{2}}\to 0

Therefore the first case in Lemma

cannot happen. Applying the ODE lemma, we get the following.

Proposition 4.4
.

For any
A
large enough, there exists
\tau_{3}
with the following property: For

, we either have

, or

. In the second case,
{\|f\eta\|+I\leq C\exp\left(-\alpha\tau\right)}
for any

.

We can also combine our ODE lemma with the extra structure of the modes to get the following lower bound on the decay rate of

Proposition 4.5
.

Assume that for some (and hence every large enough)

, there exists a sequence of times

such that

\displaystyle\|f(\tau_{i},\sigma)\chi(\frac{\sigma}{A\sqrt{\tau_{i}}})\|\geq C_{A}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau_{i})

Then there exists

and

\displaystyle\|f\eta\|(\tau)=\|f(\tau,\sigma)\chi(\frac{\sigma}{A\sqrt{\tau}})\|\geq C_{0}\exp(-\Lambda\tau)

for

and some

. We refer to

Remark 4.1
.

The above Proposition allows us to replace the exponentially small error terms with
\varepsilon\|f\eta\|
and use the usual Merle-Zaag lemma. At first glance, this may seem to simplify things. However, due to presence of
I
in

, even with the stronger conclusion

A\gg\Lambda
from now on. Note that since
\Lambda
is fixed, we can pick
A
at the beginning and fix it throughout.

Proof.

Fix a large enough
A
so that the assumption holds, and choose
m
large such that
\lambda_{m}=\frac{m-1}{2}\gg 2cA^{2}
.
Define

\displaystyle p(\tau)^{2}=\|\pi_{<m}(f\eta)\|^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\langle f\eta,h_{k}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle q(\tau)^{2}=\|\pi_{m}(f\eta)\|^{2}=\langle f\eta,h_{m}\rangle^{2}

\displaystyle r(\tau)^{2}=\|\pi_{>m}(f\eta)\|^{2}=\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\langle f\eta,h_{k}\rangle^{2}

It is easy to see (c.f. proof of Proposition
4.3
) that for any
\varepsilon
these quantities eventually satisfy

\displaystyle\frac{dp}{d\tau}\geq\frac{m-2}{2}p-\varepsilon(p+q+r)-\varepsilon I-C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle|\frac{dq}{d\tau}-\frac{m-1}{2}q|\leq\varepsilon(p+q+r)+\varepsilon I+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{dr}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{m}{2}r+\varepsilon(p+q+r)+\varepsilon I+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

Now let
P(\tau)=e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\tau}p
, define
Q
and
R
similarly, and let

. So if
a
in Proposition
4.2
is chosen large enough to ensure

and
m
is large enough so that

, we have the inequality

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}J=e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\tau}(\frac{d}{d\tau}I+\frac{m-1}{2}I)\leq-\frac{1}{2}J+\varepsilon e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\tau}\|f\eta\|+C_{0}\exp(-b\tau)

Therefore,
P,Q
and
S=R+J
satisfy the conditions of our ODE lemma.

If case 3 of the ODE lemma holds, then we will have

\displaystyle e^{\frac{m-1}{2}\tau}\|f\eta\|\leq P+Q+S\leq C\exp(-\frac{1}{10}\tau)\Rightarrow\|f\eta\|\leq Ce^{-\frac{m-1}{2}\tau}

But this is contradicts the assumption. Hence we must have either case 1 or case 2, which imply a lower bound on
\|f\eta\|
. Since we have a lower bound for a particular choice of

, the same bound holds for all larger
A
as well.

∎

If we have exponential decay in Proposition
4.4
and the Decay Condition mentioned above, we can say more about the decay rate.

Proposition 4.6
.

Assume the Decay condition
30
holds and
x+y\leq C(\zeta+\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)
in Proposition
4.4
, and assume
A
in Proposition
4.2
is large enough (compared to
\Lambda
in the Decay Condition

). Let

\lambda=\sup\{\alpha\geq 0\,:\,\|f\eta\|+I\leq C_{\alpha}\exp\left(-\alpha\tau\right)\,\,\text{if}\,\,\tau\geq\tau_{\alpha}\,,\,\,\text{for some}\,\,C_{\alpha}\,\,\text{and}\,\,\tau_{\alpha}\}

Then

for some
m\geq 2
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\|\pi_{<m}(f\eta)\|\leq C\left(\|\pi_{\geq m}(f\eta)\|+I+\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)\right)

\displaystyle\|f\eta\|+I\leq C\exp\left(-(\lambda_{m}-\delta)\tau\right)\,\,\text{for any}\,\,\,\delta

Proof.

The proof is similar to the previous proposition. First, we have
{\lambda<C\Lambda<\infty}
because of the Decay Condition. Choose
m
such that

\frac{m-1}{2}<\lambda\leq\frac{m}{2}

and define

as before. We will show

which gives the assertion with a different choice of

Note that by our choice of

4.2
Controlling

We now deal with controlling

. When we differentiate

, its projection on the subspace spanned by

(corresponding to eigenvalue

large enough so that the results of previous sections are applicable.

The equation for

was computed in Lemma

. Localizing this equation gives

\displaystyle(U\eta)_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}(U\eta)+\mathcal{N}(U)\eta-\mathcal{G}\eta+\mathcal{E}

(31)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(U)=\frac{1}{2}\left(1-e^{-2U}-2U\right)

(32)

\displaystyle\mathcal{G}=n\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\right)f

and the cut-off error term
\mathcal{E}
is defined similar to before. Note that
u(\tau,0)\to 1
and
|f\theta|\leq\frac{C_{A}}{\sqrt{\tau}}
for large
\tau
implies the upper bounds

\displaystyle|U\eta|\leq C_{A}

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}(U)\leq C_{A}|U|^{2}

Let

\displaystyle b(\tau)=\langle U\eta\,,\,h_{0}\rangle

(33)

We can control the ODE for
b
using
\|f\eta\|
. Note that Proposition
4.6
only provides control with
\|\pi_{\geq m}(f\eta)\|+I
, and not
\|\pi_{\geq m}(f\eta)\|
. Hence the first assertion of the following lemma is actually needed.

Lemma 4.3
.

For any
\varepsilon
there exists large
\tau_{0}
such that for
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}b^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon^{2})b^{2}-C_{A}\|f\eta\|^{2}-C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

where
C_{A}
is a constant depending on
A
. Furthermore, consider the eigenfunction expansion
f\eta(\tau,\sigma)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(\tau)h_{k}(\sigma)
and assume that for some
m\geq 1
, we have the following Domination Condition

\displaystyle\lim_{\tau\to\infty}\frac{\sum_{k\neq m}a_{k}^{2}}{a_{m}^{2}}=0

(34)

Then we have the better estimate

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}b^{2}\geq(1-\varepsilon^{2})b^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\|f\eta\|^{2}-C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

for large
\tau
.

Proof.

We need the following simple property of (modified) Hermite polynomials (see Lemma
5.1
below): For any function
F
with appropriate decay and any

we have

\displaystyle\langle F\,,\,h_{m}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{2}{m}}\langle F_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m-1}\rangle

(35)

The function

satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}b^{2}=2bb_{\tau}=2b^{2}+2b\big(\langle\mathcal{N}(U)\eta,h_{0}\rangle-\langle\mathcal{G}\eta,h_{0}\rangle+\langle\mathcal{E},h_{0}\rangle\big)

Recall the bound
|f|=|\frac{u_{\sigma}}{u}|=|U_{\sigma}|\leq\frac{C_{A}}{\sqrt{\tau}}
for
|\sigma|\leq 2A\sqrt{\tau}
and large
\tau
. This gives

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\leq 2A\sqrt{\tau}\frac{C_{A}}{\tau}\leq\varepsilon\Rightarrow\|\mathcal{G}\eta\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\eta\|

We also have
\|\mathcal{E}\|\leq C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)
as always.

To control
\mathcal{N}(U)
we proceed as follows. Write

U\eta=b(\tau)h_{0}+V

We can use equality
35
and the fact that
U
is bounded on the support of
\eta
to write

\displaystyle\|V\|^{2}=\|U\eta\|^{2}-b(\tau)^{2}=\sum_{m\geq 1}|\langle U\eta,h_{m}\rangle|^{2}\leq C(\|f\eta\|^{2}+\|U\eta_{\sigma}\|^{2})

\displaystyle\leq C\|f\eta\|^{2}+C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

Now pick
R
large enough so that if

is compactly supported with
\phi=1
on
[-R,R]
, we get
\|h_{0}(1-\phi)\|\leq\varepsilon
. On the compact support of such

we can assume
|U|\leq\varepsilon
since

, which implies
\mathcal{N}(U)\leq C_{A}|U|^{2}\leq\varepsilon|U|
. We therefore have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{N}(U)\eta\|\leq\|\mathcal{N}(U)\eta\phi\|+\|\mathcal{N}(U)\eta(1-\phi)\|\leq\varepsilon\|U\eta\phi\|+C_{A}\|U\eta(1-\phi)\|\leq

\displaystyle\varepsilon\|U\eta\|+C_{A}\|b(\tau)h_{0}(1-\phi)\|+C_{A}\|V(1-\phi)\|\leq

\displaystyle 2\varepsilon|b(\tau)|+C_{A}\|f\eta\|+C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

Putting these together, the ODE can be estimated as

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}z+C\|F\|+C\|E\|

for some
C
, where for
c=\frac{1}{16}
and
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
,
F
and
E
satisfy the estimates

\displaystyle\|F\|\leq C_{0}\varepsilon\|f\eta\|+C_{\varepsilon}\|f^{2}\theta^{2}\|+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\|E\|\leq C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

Assume
k
is known in the definition of
I
and pick any
\varepsilon_{1}>0
. We can pick a small enough
\varepsilon
in Proposition
4.1
and then small enough
\varepsilon
and
\delta
in inequality
25
to get
\tau_{1}(\tau_{0},\varepsilon_{1})
, such that for
\tau\geq\tau_{1}
we have

\displaystyle C\|F\|+C\|E\|\leq\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+z)+\varepsilon_{1}\|f\theta\|+\varepsilon_{1}I+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

Now note that for
\tau\geq\tau_{0}

|\|f\theta\|^{2}-\|f\eta\|^{2}|\leq\left|\int_{A\sqrt{\tau}\leq|\sigma|\leq 2A\sqrt{\tau}}f^{2}e^{-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}}\,d\sigma\right|\leq CM^{2}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

So we can replace the
\|f\theta\|
term with
\|f\eta\|\leq x+y+z
and get the system

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-2\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+z)-\varepsilon_{1}I-C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq 2\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+z)+\varepsilon_{1}I+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}z+2\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+z)+\varepsilon_{1}I+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

By picking
a>\frac{1}{2}
in Proposition
4.2
, we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}I\leq-\frac{1}{2}I+\varepsilon_{1}\|f\theta\|+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

for
\tau\geq\tau_{2}(\tau_{0},k,A,\varepsilon_{1})
. Letting
\zeta(\tau)=z(\tau)+I(\tau)
we get

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-3\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+\zeta)-C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq 3\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+\zeta)+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\frac{d\zeta}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}\zeta+3\varepsilon_{1}(x+y+\zeta)+C_{0}\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

for some universal
c
and
\tau
sufficiently large. Since
\varepsilon_{1}
was arbitrary, we are done.

5
The Neutral Case

In this section we handle the case
x+\zeta=o(y)
in Proposition
4.4
and derive the asymptotic ODE governing the coefficient of neutral mode of
f
. In particular, we are assuming

\displaystyle\|f\eta\|\geq C_{0}\exp(-\Lambda\tau)\gg C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

(38)

Recall that we have set
U=\log u
, and we have the following equations for
U\eta
and
f\eta
.

\displaystyle(U\eta)_{\tau}=\mathcal{L}(U\eta)+\mathcal{N}(U)\eta-\mathcal{G}\eta+\mathcal{E}

\displaystyle(f\eta)_{\tau}=\mathcal{A}(f\eta)-n\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}(\xi)\,d\xi\right)f_{\sigma}\eta+\left(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1\right)f\eta-nf^{3}\eta+E

The quadratic term in the ODE for the dominant mode of
f
comes from
(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1)f
, while the other terms are essentially cubic. We will show that the inner product of these cubic terms and
h_{1}
, as well as the part of the quadratic term coming from non-dominant modes of
f
and
u
, are in fact negligible in the ODE. Controlling the quadratic term here is the main reason for the second stronger assertion in Proposition
4.7
. Since one mode of
f\eta
is dominating the rest, the unstable mode of
U=\log u\approx\frac{-1}{2}(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1)
corresponding to eigenvalue
1
gets arbitrary small compared to
f
by Proposition
4.7
.

We need some general results about projections of related functions on eigenspaces
\mathfrak{H}_{m}
. Recall that the unit eigenfunctions
h_{m}
spanning
\mathfrak{H}_{m}
are related to standard Hermite polynomials
H_{m}
through
h_{m}(\sigma)=c_{m}H_{m}(\frac{\sigma}{2})
where
c_{m}=\left(\frac{1}{2^{m}\sqrt{4\pi}m!}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}
. They satisfy the recursive relations

\displaystyle h_{m+1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2m+2}}(\sigma h_{m}-2h_{m}^{\prime})

(39)

\displaystyle h_{m+1}^{\prime}=\sqrt{\frac{m+1}{2}}h_{m}

(40)

Lemma 5.1
.

For any function
F
with appropriate smoothness and decay we have

\displaystyle\langle F_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m-1}\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{m}{2}}\langle F\,,\,h_{m}\rangle

(41)

\displaystyle\langle\sigma F\,,\,h_{m}\rangle=2\langle F_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m}\rangle+\sqrt{2m}\langle F\,,\,h_{m-1}\rangle

(42)

\displaystyle\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}F(\xi)\,d\xi\,,\,h_{0}\rangle\leq C\left(\|F\|+\|F_{\sigma}\|\right)

(43)

with the convention
h_{-1}=0
.

We use this lemma to compare the modes of
U\eta
,
f\eta
and the non-local term
\mathcal{G}
. Before continuing, we emphasize the following general remark to make the computations more tractable.

Remark 5.1
.

Suppose a function
F(\tau,\sigma)
and its partial derivative
F_{\sigma}
are bounded (or even have polynomial growth in
\sigma
), and take
\eta=\chi\left(\frac{\sigma}{A\sqrt{\tau}}\right)
and
\theta=\chi\left(\frac{\sigma}{2A\sqrt{\tau}}\right)
as before. Let
\mathcal{F}=F\theta
. We have the estimates

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}-F\mathds{1}_{supp\,\eta}\|^{2}\leq C\int_{|\sigma|\geq A\sqrt{\tau}}Fe^{\frac{-\sigma^{2}}{4}}\leq C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\|F_{\sigma}\theta-F_{\sigma}\mathds{1}_{supp\,\eta}\|^{2}\leq C\int_{|\sigma|\geq A\sqrt{\tau}}F_{\sigma}e^{\frac{-\sigma^{2}}{4}}\leq C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{F}_{\sigma}-F_{\sigma}\mathds{1}_{supp\,\eta}\|^{2}\leq C\int_{|\sigma|\geq A\sqrt{\tau}}\big(F+F_{\sigma}\big)e^{\frac{-\sigma^{2}}{4}}\leq C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

These estimates show that up to an exponentially small error, we can work with
F
and
F_{\sigma}
instead of their localizations if we are only working on the support of
\eta
. All the functions that we apply Lemma
5.1
to are bounded. Since we have chosen
A
large compared to
\Lambda
as in
38
, we do not need to worry about such errors.

Throughout this section,
a(\tau)
denotes the neutral coefficient of
f\eta
:

\displaystyle a(\tau)=\langle f\eta(\tau)\,,\,h_{1}\rangle=\int(f\eta)(\tau,\sigma)h_{1}(\sigma)e^{-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{4}}\,d\sigma

The ODE satisfied by
a(\tau)
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}a=\left\langle(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1)f\eta\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle-n\left\langle\left(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}(\xi)\,d\xi\right)f_{\sigma}\eta+f^{3}\eta\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle+\left\langle E\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle

(44)

We deal with different terms one by one.

First, we deal with the quadratic term
(\frac{1}{u^{2}}-1)f\eta=(e^{-2U}-1)f\eta
and compute the leading term of the ODE.

Proposition 5.1
.

For any
\varepsilon
and large enough
\tau
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle(e^{-2U}-1)f\eta\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle=-\frac{2}{\sqrt[4]{\pi}}a^{2}(\tau)+\epsilon(\tau)

where
|\epsilon(\tau)|\leq\varepsilon a^{2}(\tau)
.

Proof.

Recall that
h_{1}=c_{1}\sigma=\frac{1}{2\sqrt[4]{\pi}}\sigma
, and
e^{-2U}-1=-2U-2\mathcal{N}
. As before, since
u
is bounded and away from zero,
U
is bounded and we have

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}|\leq CU^{2}

\displaystyle|\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}|=|\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}(e^{-2U}-1+2U)|=|-2fe^{-2U}+2f|\leq C|f|\,|U|

for some
C
(depending on
A
).
We have

\displaystyle\left\langle(e^{-2U}-1)f\eta\,,\,\sigma\right\rangle=-2\left\langle(U+\mathcal{N})f\eta\,,\,\sigma\right\rangle=-2\left\langle(U+\mathcal{N})\sigma\,,\,f\eta\right\rangle

Using Lemma
5.1
we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\sigma U\,,\,f\eta\right\rangle=\sum_{m=0}\left\langle\sigma U\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}\big(2\left\langle f\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle+\sqrt{2m}\left\langle U\,,\,h_{m-1}\right\rangle\big)\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\left\langle f\,,\,f\eta\right\rangle+\sqrt{2}\left\langle U\,,\,h_{0}\right\rangle\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle+\sum_{m=2}\sqrt{\frac{4m}{m-1}}\left\langle f\,,\,h_{m-2}\right\rangle\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

(45)

Now
|\left\langle U\,,\,h_{0}\right\rangle|\leq\varepsilon|a(\tau)|
by Proposition
4.7
. Also, if we define
g=\sum_{m=2}\left\langle f\,,\,h_{m-2}\right\rangle h_{m}
and
\tilde{f}=\sum_{m=2}\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle h_{m}
, we have
\|g\|\leq\|f\|
and
\|\tilde{f}\|\leq\varepsilon|a(\tau)|
. Therefore

\left|\sum_{m=2}\sqrt{\frac{4m}{m-1}}\left\langle f\,,\,h_{m-2}\right\rangle\right|\leq C|\langle g\,,\,\tilde{f}\rangle|\leq C\|g\|\|\tilde{f}\|\leq\varepsilon a^{2}(\tau)

So the only significant term here is the first one, which is
2\|f\eta\|^{2}
up to an exponentially small error. This gives the leading term

-4c_{2}a^{2}=-\frac{2}{\sqrt[4]{\pi}}a^{2}

Note that as in the proof of Lemma
4.3
, we have
\|U\eta\|\leq C\|f\eta\|
. Indeed, disregarding exponentially small terms,
\|U\eta\|^{2}
can be estimated as

|\langle U\eta\,,\,h_{0}\rangle|^{2}+\sum_{m\geq 1}|\langle U\eta\,,\,h_{m}\rangle|^{2}\leq\varepsilon a(\tau)^{2}+C\sum_{m\geq 0}|\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\rangle|^{2}\leq C\|f\eta\|^{2}

Now for
\varepsilon
, pick
R
large enough so that for any compactly supported smooth function
\phi
with
0\leq\phi\leq 1
and
\phi=1
on
|\sigma|\leq R
, we have
\|h_{1}(1-\phi^{2})\|\leq\varepsilon
. If we write
f\eta=a(\tau)h_{1}+V
with
\|V\|\leq\varepsilon|a(\tau)|
we get

\displaystyle\|f\eta(1-\phi^{2})\|\leq|a|\|h_{1}(1-\phi^{2})\|+C\|V\|\leq 2\varepsilon|a(\tau)|

For such compactly supported
\phi
, we have

\displaystyle\big|\langle\sigma\mathcal{N}\,,\,f\eta\rangle\big|\leq\big|\langle\sigma\mathcal{N}\phi\,,\,f\eta\phi\rangle\big|+\big|\langle\sigma\mathcal{N}\,,\,f\eta(1-\phi^{2})\rangle\big|

On the compact support of
\phi
, we can assume
|\mathcal{N}|\leq C|U|^{2}\leq\varepsilon|U|
so
\|\sigma\mathcal{N}\phi\|\leq\varepsilon\|U\eta\|\leq\varepsilon|a(\tau)|
. On the other hand, we have
\|f\eta(1-\phi^{2})\|\leq 2\varepsilon|a(\tau)|
and
|\mathcal{N}|\leq C|U|
and
|\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}|\leq C|f|
since
U
is bounded. Therefore

\displaystyle\|\sigma\mathcal{N}\|\leq C\big(\|\mathcal{N}\|+\|\mathcal{N}_{\sigma}\|\big)\leq C\big(\|U\|+\|f\|\big)\leq C|a(\tau)|

Putting these estimates together we see

\displaystyle\big|\langle\sigma\mathcal{N}\,,\,f\eta\rangle\big|\leq\varepsilon a^{2}(\tau)

(46)

and we are done.
∎

Next, we deal with the middle term. Note that since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left(\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f\right)=\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f_{\sigma}+f^{3}

we can integrate by parts and rewrite this term as

\displaystyle n\left\langle\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f\eta_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle+n\left\langle\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f\eta\,,\,\partial_{\sigma}h_{1}-\frac{\sigma}{2}h_{1}\right\rangle

But
\eta_{\sigma}
is nonzero only for
|\sigma|\geq A\sqrt{\tau}
and we have

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma}{2}h_{1}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}h_{1}=\frac{1}{4}h_{2}

So this is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{-n}{4}\left\langle\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f\eta\,,\,h_{2}\right\rangle+O(\exp(-cA^{2}\tau))

for some universal
c
since
|f|
is bounded. We have the following estimate.

Proposition 5.2
.

For any
\varepsilon
, for large enough
\tau
we have

\displaystyle\left|\langle\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f\eta\,,\,h_{2}\rangle\right|\leq\varepsilon a^{2}(\tau)

(47)

Proof.

We can write

\displaystyle\left\langle\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)f\eta\,,\,h_{2}\right\rangle=\left\langle h_{2}\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)\,,\,f\eta\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left\langle c_{2}(\sigma^{2}-2)\big(\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\big)\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

Let

\displaystyle A_{m}=\left\langle(\sigma^{2}-2)\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

We use Lemma
5.1
. Adopting
h_{-1}=h_{-2}=0
we can write

\displaystyle A_{m}=\left\langle\sigma^{2}\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle-2\left\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}\left(\sigma\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\right)\,,h_{m}\,\right\rangle+\sqrt{2m}\left\langle\sigma\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-1}\right\rangle-2\left\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=2\left\langle\sigma f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle+2\sqrt{2m}\left\langle f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-1}\right\rangle+\sqrt{4m(m-1)}\left\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-2}\right\rangle

If
m>4
we can further simplify this to

\displaystyle 2\left\langle 2ff_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle+4\sqrt{2m}\left\langle f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-1}\right\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{8m(m-1)}{m-2}}\left\langle f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-3}\right\rangle=

\displaystyle 4\left\langle ff_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle+4\sqrt{\frac{4m}{m-1}}\left\langle 2ff_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m-2}\right\rangle+\sqrt{\frac{16m(m-1)}{(m-2)(m-3)}}\left\langle 2ff_{\sigma}\,,\,h_{m-4}\right\rangle

Define
g=\sum_{m\geq 4}A_{m}h_{m}
and
\tilde{f}=\sum_{m\geq 4}\left\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle h_{m}
. We have

\displaystyle\|g\|\leq C\|ff_{\sigma}\|\leq C\|f\|

\displaystyle\|\tilde{f}\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\eta\|

So

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{m\geq 4}A_{m}\left\langle f\eta,h_{m}\right\rangle\right|=|\langle g\,,\,\tilde{f}\rangle|\leq\|g\|\|\tilde{f}\|\leq C\varepsilon\|f\|\|f\eta\|\leq\varepsilon a^{2}(\tau)

(48)

For
m\leq 3
, note that
\sqrt{2m}
is bounded above and that
\langle\sigma f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\rangle=\langle f^{2}\,,\,\sigma h_{m}\rangle
where
\sigma h_{m}
can be written as a linear combination of finitely many
h_{i}
s with universally bounded coefficients. So we get

\displaystyle|2\left\langle\sigma f^{2}\,,\,h_{m}\right\rangle+2\sqrt{2m}\left\langle f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-1}\right\rangle||\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{m}\rangle|\leq C\|f^{2}\|\|f\eta\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\eta\|^{2}

So we are left with the
\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{m-2}\rangle
term for
m=2,3
. We have

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{1}\right\rangle\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{3}\rangle\right|=\sqrt{2}|\langle f^{2}\,,\,h_{0}\rangle||\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{3}\rangle|\leq C\|f^{2}\|\|f\eta\|\leq\varepsilon\|f\eta\|^{2}

\displaystyle\left|\left\langle\int_{0}^{\sigma}f^{2}\,,\,h_{0}\right\rangle\langle f\eta\,,\,h_{2}\rangle\right|\leq C\big(\|f^{2}\|+\|ff_{\sigma}\|\big)\varepsilon a(\tau)\leq C\|f\|\varepsilon a(\tau)\leq\varepsilon a^{2}(\tau)

∎

For the error term, since
|f|
and
|f_{\sigma}|
are bounded we have

|\langle E,h_{1}\rangle|\leq\|E\|\leq C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

as before. Putting Propositions
5.2
and
5.1
together we have the asymptotic ODE

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}a=-\frac{2}{\sqrt[4]{\pi}}a^{2}+o(1)a^{2}

which, together with
a(\tau)\to 0
as
\tau\to\infty
, gives

\displaystyle a(\tau)=\frac{\sqrt[4]{\pi}}{2\tau}+o(\frac{1}{\tau})

(49)

Note that the Domination Condition
6.1
holds by assumption. Together with Proposition
4.7
and
\langle U,h_{2}\rangle=\langle f,h_{1}\rangle
, the expression for
a(\tau)
implies case 2 in our main Theorem.

Remark 5.2
.

Note that our normalization for
h_{2}
is different from that of
[
AK05
]
but the final result is the same.

6
The Exponential Case

In this section we deal with the case of exponential decay in Proposition
4.4
, and establish the third case in our main Theorem. As in Proposition
4.6
, we let
\lambda=\lambda_{m}
for
m\geq 2
be the supremum of all exponential decay rates. We prove that in this case, the
m
-th mode of
f\eta
dominates the other modes, and derive the ODE for the coefficient of
m
-th mode. From now on, by virtue of Proposition
4.6
, we may assume

\displaystyle\|f\eta\|+I\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)

for any
\beta<\lambda=\lambda_{m}=\frac{m-1}{2}
and large enough
\tau
. We may also assume the scale
A
is the cut-off
\eta
is taken large enough so that

\displaystyle e^{-\lambda}\|f\eta\|\geq C_{0}\exp(-2\Lambda\tau)\gg C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)

(50)

The main steps of the argument are as follows: First, we derive pointwise bounds for
f\eta
using standard techniques. Then, using this bound and Lemma
4.4
and sharper estimates recorded in Corollaries
4.1
,
4.2
and
4.3
, we show that a bound
\|f\eta\|\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)
results in a better bound
\|F\|\leq C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)
for the non-linearity in the equation for
f\eta
. Using the variation of constants formula, it will then follow that for any
k>m
, the
k
-th mode will have a decay rate strictly faster than
\exp(-\lambda_{m}\tau)
. Finally, we will derive the ODE for the coefficient of
m
-th mode and show that it must have the exact
\exp(-\lambda_{m}\tau)
decay rate.

We have the following standard apriori estimate. We refer to
[
HV93
]
for a proof.

Theorem 6.1
.

Consider the initial value problem

\displaystyle f_{\tau}=(\mathcal{L}+\frac{l}{2})u+F(\tau,\sigma)

\displaystyle f(0,\sigma)=f_{0}

where
l
is an integer and the operator
\mathcal{L}
is defined as

\mathcal{L}=\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\sigma^{2}}-\frac{\sigma}{2}\frac{\partial}{\partial\sigma}+1

Assume
F(\tau,\sigma)\in L^{2}_{loc}\big((0,\infty);L^{2}_{\rho}\big)
and
f_{0}\in L^{2}_{\rho}
. Then the initial value problem has a unique solution in the weighted Sobolev space
H^{1}_{\rho}
, and we have the following estimates for the
L^{2}_{\rho}
norms for
\tau\in(0,T)
:

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}(\tau)+\tau\|f_{\sigma}\|^{2}(\tau)+\int_{0}^{T}\tau^{2}\|f_{\sigma\sigma}\|^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau\leq C_{T}\left(\|f_{0}\|^{2}+\int_{0}^{T}\|F\|^{2}(\tau)\,d\tau\right)

Here
T
is arbitrary and
C_{T}
is a constant depending only on
T
and
l
.

Lemma 6.1
.

On any compact set, we have

\displaystyle|f\eta|\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)

Proof.

Note that in the equation for
f\eta
, the right hand side
F+E
satisfies

\|F+E\|\leq C(\|f\eta\|+\|f^{2}\theta^{2}\|)\leq C(\|f\eta\|+I)\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)

So the Theorem applied on
[\tau-2,\tau]
gives

\displaystyle 2\|(f\eta)_{\sigma}\|^{2}(\tau)\leq C_{2}\left(\|f\eta\|^{2}(\tau-2)+\int_{\tau-2}^{\tau}\|F\|^{2}(s)+\|E\|^{2}(s)\,ds\right)\leq

\displaystyle Ce^{-2\beta(\tau-2)}+\frac{C}{2\beta}\big(e^{-2\beta(\tau-2)}-e^{-2\beta\tau}\big)\leq C\exp(-2\beta\tau)

By Sobolev inequality, we get the desired estimate.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

If
a
in Proposition
4.2
and
A
in our cut-offs are large compared to
\Lambda
, the bound
\|f\eta\|\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)
implies
I\leq C\exp(-2\beta\tau)
and
\|F\|+\|E\|\leq C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)
for large
\tau
.

Proof.

Consider the differential inequality for
I
in Proposition
4.2
. In Corollary
4.3
, we choose
\delta
to be a small number. By the previous Lemma, we have
|f|\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)
for
|\sigma|\leq\delta^{-1}
. So
I
satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}I\leq-aI+Ce^{-\beta\tau}\|f\theta\|+C\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)\leq-aI+Ce^{-\beta\tau}\|f\eta\|\leq-aI+Ce^{-2\beta\tau}

where we have used the smallness of
\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)
compared to
Ce^{-\beta\tau}\|f\eta\|
. Now if
a\geq 2\Lambda>2\beta
, we get

\displaystyle I(\tau)\leq e^{a(\tau_{0}-\tau)}I(\tau_{0})+Ce^{-a\tau}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{as}e^{-2\beta s}\,ds\leq e^{a(\tau_{0}-\tau)}I(\tau_{0})+Ce^{-2\beta\tau}\leq Ce^{-2\beta\tau}

Now, in Corollary
4.2
, we can pick
\delta
to be a small number and use the pointwise bound
\varepsilon(\tau)=C\exp(-\beta\tau)
for
|f|
to get

\displaystyle\|f^{2}\theta^{2}\|\leq e^{-\beta\tau}\|f\eta\|+\delta I\leq e^{-2\beta\tau}

On the other hand, by Lemma
4.4
, we have
\frac{1}{u^{2}(\tau,0)}-1\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)
. So, in Corollary
4.1
, we can take
\delta(\tau)\leq Ce^{-\beta\tau}
and
\varepsilon(\tau)=\exp(-\frac{\beta}{2}\tau)
and get

\displaystyle\|F\|\leq Ce^{-\frac{\beta}{2}\tau}\|f\eta\|+Ce^{\frac{\beta}{2}\tau}\|f^{2}\theta^{2}\|\leq Ce^{-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau}

∎

We recall the variation of constants formula: Suppose
\mathcal{A}
is an operator with eigenvalues
-\lambda_{k}
and eigenfunctions
h_{k}(\sigma)
for
k=0,1,2,\dots
, and assume
f(\tau,\sigma)
is a solution to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial\tau}f=\mathcal{A}f+F\qquad f(\tau_{0},\sigma)=f_{0}(\sigma)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}h_{k}

Then
f
can be expressed as

\displaystyle f=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\left(e^{\lambda_{k}(\tau_{0}-\tau)}a_{k}+\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{\lambda_{k}(s-\tau)}\langle F,h_{k}\rangle\,ds\right)h_{k}

(51)

The following is inspired by
[
HV93
]
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Assume
\|f\eta\|\leq C\exp(-\beta\tau)
with
\lambda_{m-1}<\beta<\lambda_{m}
and
m\geq 2
. We have the estimate

\displaystyle\|\pi_{\geq m+1}(f\eta)\|\leq C\exp\left(-\beta^{*}\tau\right)

where
\beta^{*}=\min\{\frac{4}{3}\beta,\lambda_{m+1}\}
. Since
m\geq 2
, by choosing
\beta
close to
\lambda_{m}
and taking
\delta
small enough, we have

\lambda_{m}+\delta<\beta^{*}

Proof.

For
\tau\geq\tau_{0}
,
f\eta
satisfies the equation

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}f\eta=\mathcal{A}(f\eta)+F+E

\displaystyle\|F+E\|\leq C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)

By the variation formula
51
,
\pi_{\geq m+1}(f\eta)
is equal to

\displaystyle\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}e^{\lambda_{k}(\tau_{0}-\tau)}\langle f\eta(\tau_{0}),h_{k}\rangle h_{k}+\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{\lambda_{k}(s-\tau)}\langle F+E,h_{k}\rangle\,ds\right)h_{k}

For the first sum, since
k\geq m+1
, we have for
\tau>\tau_{0}+1

\displaystyle\left\|\sum_{k=m+1}^{\infty}e^{\lambda_{k}(\tau_{0}-\tau)}\langle f\eta(\tau_{0}),h_{k}\rangle h_{k}\right\|\leq

\displaystyle e^{-\frac{m}{2}(\tau-\tau_{0})}\|f\eta(\tau_{0})\|\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}e^{\frac{-k}{2}(\tau-\tau_{0})}\leq Ce^{-\frac{m}{2}\tau}e^{(\frac{m}{2}-\beta)\tau_{0}}

For the second sum, we proceed as follows. For any term with
m+1\leq k\leq 12m
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{\lambda_{k}(s-\tau)}\langle F+E,h_{k}\rangle\,ds\right|\leq\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{\lambda_{k}(s-\tau)}\|F+E\|\,ds\leq Ce^{-\lambda_{k}\tau}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\tau}e^{(\lambda_{k}-\frac{3}{2}\beta)s}\,ds

which is bounded by
C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)
if
\lambda_{k}>\frac{3}{2}\beta
and by
C\exp(-\lambda_{k}\tau)
if
\lambda_{k}<\frac{3}{2}\beta
(We can assume
\frac{3}{2}\beta\neq\lambda_{k}
for every
k
by slightly changing
\beta
if necessary).

Now consider the terms for
k\geq 12m+1
. We estimate the integral on
[\tau_{0},8\tau/9]
and on
[8\tau/9,\tau]
separately. On
[\tau_{0},8\tau/9]
the norm is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\frac{8\tau}{9}}\left(\sum_{k=12m+1}^{\infty}e^{2\lambda_{k}(s-\tau)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\|F+E\|(s)\,ds\leq

\displaystyle e^{-6m\frac{\tau}{9}}\int_{\tau_{0}}^{\frac{8\tau}{9}}C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta s)\,ds\leq Ce^{-6m\frac{\tau}{9}}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau_{0})

where we have used
\sum_{k=12m+1}^{\infty}e^{-2\lambda_{k}r}\leq Ce^{-12mr}
for
r\geq 1
. On
[8\tau/9,\tau]
we have
e^{\lambda_{k}(s-\tau)}\leq 1
and the norm is bounded by

\displaystyle\int_{\frac{8\tau}{9}}^{\tau}\|F+E\|\,ds\leq\int_{\frac{8\tau}{9}}^{\tau}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta s)\,ds\leq C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta.\frac{8\tau}{9})=C\exp(-\frac{4}{3}\beta\tau)

∎

Now we derive the ODE for the coefficient of the
m
-th mode.

Proposition 6.1
.

Let

\displaystyle f\eta=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}(\tau)h_{k}(\sigma)

(52)

be the eigenfunction expansion of
f\eta
in
L^{2}(\rho\,d\sigma)
. Assume we have exponential convergence in Proposition
4.4
. If the supremum of all exponential decay rates is
\lambda=\lambda_{m}
, then

a_{m}(\tau)=C\exp(-\lambda_{m}\tau)(1+o(1))

Furthermore, for any
\varepsilon
, there exists
\tau_{\varepsilon}
such that
\|\pi_{\neq m}(f\eta)\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon a_{m}^{2}
for
\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
.

Proof.

Choose
\beta
close enough to
\lambda_{m}
according to Lemma
6.3
. By the previous Lemmas, we have
I\leq Ce^{-2\beta\tau}
and
\|F+E\|\leq Ce^{-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau}
. The ODE for the coefficient
a_{m}
is

\displaystyle\frac{d}{d\tau}a_{m}=-\lambda_{m}a_{m}+\langle F+E\,,\,h_{m}\rangle

First, assume that for some
\varepsilon_{0}
and some
\tau_{*}
, we have
\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)\geq\varepsilon_{0}|a_{m}|
for
\tau\geq\tau_{*}
. This would imply
|a_{m}|\leq C\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)
. But by Lemma
6.3
, we have
\|\pi_{\geq m+1}(f\eta)\|\leq C\exp(-(\lambda_{m}+\delta)\tau)
, and combining this with Proposition
4.6
we have

\|\pi_{\leq m-1}(f\eta)\|\leq C\left(\|\pi_{\geq m}(f\eta)\|+I+\exp(-cA^{2}\tau)\right)\leq C\exp(-(\lambda_{m}+\delta)\tau)

which gives a better bound for
\|f\eta\|
and contradicts
\lambda_{m}
being the supremum of decay rates. Therefore, for any
\varepsilon
we have

\displaystyle\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau_{i})\leq\varepsilon|a_{m}(\tau_{i})|\qquad\text{for}\,\,\tau_{i}\to\infty

Now let
\|F+E\|\leq C_{0}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)
, and choose any small
\varepsilon
and
\tau_{j}
such that
\lambda_{m}+\varepsilon<\frac{3}{2}\beta
and
2C_{0}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau_{j})\leq\varepsilon|a_{m}(\tau_{j})|
. We may assume
a_{m}(\tau_{j})
is positive infinitely often since the other case is similar. Then, as long as
C_{0}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)\leq\varepsilon|a_{m}(\tau)|
holds, we would actually have

\displaystyle|\frac{d}{d\tau}a_{m}+\lambda_{m}a_{m}|\leq|\langle F+E\,,\,h_{m}\rangle|\leq\|F+E\|\leq C_{0}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)\leq\varepsilon|a_{m}|

\displaystyle\Rightarrow a_{m}(\tau)\geq a_{m}(\tau_{j})e^{-(\lambda_{m}+\varepsilon)(\tau-\tau_{j})}\geq\frac{2C_{0}}{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau_{j}}e^{-(\lambda_{m}+\varepsilon)(\tau-\tau_{j})}>\frac{2C_{0}}{\varepsilon}e^{-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau}

Therefore
2C_{0}\exp(-\frac{3}{2}\beta\tau)\leq\varepsilon|a_{m}(\tau)|
for all
\tau\geq\tau_{j}
. Hence, the ODE for
a_{m}
follows. The estimate on
\|\pi_{\neq m}(f\eta)\|
follows from previous statements.
∎

The estimate

\|\pi_{\neq m}(f\eta)\|^{2}\leq\varepsilon a_{m}^{2}

is our Domination Condition
6.1
, so we can establish case
3
in our main Theorem as well.

Appendix: Proof of ODE lemma
4.2

We discuss the proof of our adaptation of Merle-Zaag lemma. The proof is a modification and a more careful analysis of the original argument in
[
MZ98
]
, so we only discuss the differences. Consider the system

\displaystyle\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq\frac{1}{2}x-\varepsilon(x+y+z)-B\exp(-b\tau)

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq\varepsilon(x+y+z)+B\exp(-b\tau)

(53)

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}z+\varepsilon(x+y+z)+B\exp(-b\tau)

for
\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
. We first show the following:

Claim 1
: Either
x(\tau)\geq Ce^{\frac{1}{10}\tau}
, or for every
\varepsilon\leq\frac{1}{10}
we have
x\leq 20Be^{-b\tau}+4\varepsilon(y+z)
.

Proof
: Fix some small
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{10}
, and consider
\beta=x-4\varepsilon(y+z)
. Assume that at some
\tau_{0}\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
, we have
x(\tau_{0})>20Be^{-b\tau_{0}}
and
\beta(\tau_{0})\geq 0
. Similar to
[
MZ98
]
, we can show

x(\tau)>10Be^{-b\tau}\,\,,\,\,\beta(\tau)\geq 0\Rightarrow\beta^{\prime}(\tau)>0

But as long as
\beta>0
, the inequality for
x
gives

\frac{dx}{d\tau}\geq(\frac{1}{4}-\varepsilon)x-B\exp(-b\tau)

which implies
x\geq Ce^{\frac{1}{8}\tau}
. So for our fixed
\varepsilon
, we can assume
x\leq 20Be^{-b\tau_{0}}
or
x\leq 4\varepsilon(y+z)
at any time. Since
\varepsilon
was arbitrary, we have the claim.

By substituting
x\leq 20Be^{-b\tau}+4\varepsilon(y+z)
and taking
\varepsilon
small, we get

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq 2\varepsilon(y+z)+2B\exp(-b\tau)

(54)

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-\frac{1}{2}z+2\varepsilon(y+z)+2B\exp(-b\tau)

Claim 2
: Fix an
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{100}
and a number
\alpha>10
such that
\alpha\varepsilon<\frac{1}{100}
. Consider
\gamma=\alpha\varepsilon y-z-\frac{10B}{b}\exp(-b\tau)
. If
\gamma(\tau_{1})>0
for some
\tau_{1}\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
, then
\gamma(\tau)\geq 0
for all
\tau\geq\tau_{1}
, and
c_{\varepsilon}e^{-4\varepsilon\tau}\leq y(\tau)\leq C_{\varepsilon}e^{4\varepsilon\tau}
.

Proof
: Similar to
[
MZ98
]
, we can show that at any point
\tau\geq\tau_{1}
we have

\gamma(\tau)=0\Rightarrow\gamma^{\prime}(\tau)>0

In
[
MZ98
]
, the fixed coefficient
\alpha=8
is considered, but we need and arbitrary number later in the Appendix. Note that the
\frac{10B}{b}\exp(-b\tau)
term is
\gamma
and the bound on
\alpha\varepsilon
help us absorb the exponential terms after differentiation. Substituting
z+\frac{10B}{b}\exp(-b\tau)\leq\alpha\varepsilon y
in the inequalities before Claim 2 and using
\alpha\varepsilon<C
and
b\gg 1\gg\varepsilon
we get

\displaystyle|\frac{dy}{d\tau}|\leq 4\varepsilon y+4B\exp(-b\tau)\Rightarrow c_{\varepsilon}e^{-4\varepsilon\tau}\leq y(\tau)\leq C_{\varepsilon}e^{4\varepsilon\tau}

(55)

Claim 3
: Fix an
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{100}
and a number
\alpha>10
such that
\alpha\varepsilon<\frac{1}{100}
, and consider
\gamma
as in Claim 2. If
\gamma\leq 0
for all
\tau\geq\tau_{\varepsilon}
, then

\displaystyle x+y\leq C(1+\frac{1}{\varepsilon})\left(z+B\exp(-b\tau)\right)

(56)

\displaystyle\frac{dz}{d\tau}\leq-(\frac{1}{2}-2\varepsilon-\frac{2}{\alpha})z+4B\exp(-b\tau)

and hence
z\leq C_{\varepsilon}\exp\left(-(\frac{1}{2}-2\varepsilon-\frac{2}{\alpha})\tau\right)

To finish the proof of the ODE lemma, note that if Claim 2 holds for
some
small
\varepsilon
, then Claim 3 cannot hold for
any
small
\varepsilon^{\prime}
, because one implies slow decay of
y
with rate
4\varepsilon
at most, and the other implies fast decay of a rate at least
\frac{1}{2}-2\varepsilon-\frac{2}{\alpha}
. So exactly one of the claims holds for
every

\varepsilon
. In Claim 3, by choosing
\alpha
large and
\varepsilon
small we can get as close to
\frac{1}{2}
as we want.
