Height and contour processes of Crump-Mode-Jagers forests (III): The binary, homogeneous universality class
Emmanuel Schertzer, Florian Simatos

Abstract
. This paper belongs to a series of papers aiming to investigate scaling limits of Crump–Mode–Jagers (CMJ) trees. In the previous two papers we identified general conditions under which CMJ trees belong to the universality class of Galton–Watson and Bellman–Harris processes. In this paper we identify general conditions for CMJ trees to belong to the universality class of binary, homogeneous CMJ trees. These conditions state that the offspring process should ‘look like’ a renewal process, and also that it should not accumulate too many atoms near the origin. We show in particular that any renewal process satisfies these conditions

Introduction

This paper is the third paper in a series of papers
[
18
,
19
]
whose goal is to investigate scaling limits of Crump–Mode–Jagers (CMJ) trees, i.e., the chronological trees describing the genealogy of CMJ branching processes. A CMJ branching process is characterized by a pair

with

{\mathcal{P}}

1.1.
Three classes

Three important classes of CMJ processes are particularly relevant for the present discussion:

Galton–Watson (GW) branching processes:

they correspond to the case
V=1
and
{\mathcal{P}}=\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\rvert\delta_{1}
, i.e., all offspring occurs at time
1
(here and throughout
\delta_{x}
denotes the Dirac measure at

);

Bellman–Harris (BH) branching processes:

they correspond to the case where

and
{\mathcal{P}}
are independent and
{\mathcal{P}}=\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\rvert\delta_{V}
, i.e., offspring is independent of life length and occurs at death;

Binary, homogeneous branching processes:

they correspond to the case where
{\mathcal{P}}=\mu\mid_{V}
with
\mu
a Poisson process independent from
V
(here and throughout, for a measure
\pi

(0,\infty)
and

\pi\mid_{v}

Scaling limits of the random chronological trees corresponding to these branching processes, as encoded by their height and contour processes (see in
[
18
,
19
]
and below for definitions), are well-known. The first results were obtained for GW processes with finite variance offspring distribution, whose scaling limit was shown to be the Brownian tree
[
1
,
2
,
16
]
. A striking feature in this case, the limiting Brownian excursion coding the tree and the depth-first exploration process (or Lukasiewicz path, see Remark
2.3
) coincide: as will be seen, this is a feature that we will also encounter in the present paper. Later, the finite variance assumption was relaxed
[
7
,
6
]
, leading to a complete picture in the GW case: scaling limits belong to the class of Lévy trees.

Seeing a BH process as a GW process where edges have been stretched by i.i.d. positive weights, it is natural to describe the scaling limits of BH processes in the same way starting from the Lévy tree of the underlying genealogical GW process. Performing this construction on the limiting continuous object amounts to considering the so-called Lévy snake, obtained from a Lévy tree by adding Poissonian marks, and indeed it was shown that scaling limits of BH processes are described by Lévy snakes
[
10
,
17
]
.

Finally, the binary, homogeneous case exhibits the striking feature that, if properly defined, its contour process is a spectrally positive Lévy process
[
12
]
. This observation has far-reaching consequences, but in particular, it immediately entails that the scaling limit of the chronological tree is a continuous tree coded by a spectrally positive Lévy process.

1.2.
The GW and BH universality classes

Our goal in this on-going project is to describe scaling limits of CMJ trees. In our earlier work we identified general conditions guaranteeing that CMJ processes belong to the universality class of GW or BH processes.

Namely, we showed in
[
18
]
that CMJ with ‘short’ edges belong to the GW universality class. Intuitively, this is not surprising since if edges are ‘short’ in a well-defined sense, then it does not really matter when births occur precisely, and the chronological tree should look like the underlying genealogical tree. We identified in
[
18
]
a precise ‘short edge’ condition in the form of finite moments
{\mathbb{E}}(R),{\mathbb{E}}(V)<\infty
. Here
R
represents the “typical” age of an individual at child-bearing, and its mean is simply given by
{\mathbb{E}}(R)={\mathbb{E}}(\int_{0}^{\infty}u{\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{d}u))
.

In
[
19
]
we showed a more subtle result: we allowed for edges to be long and considered the case where
{\mathbb{E}}(R)={\mathbb{E}}(V)=\infty
, but we imposed that individuals who live for a long time do not have a large offspring. This is what happens in the BH case, and indeed we identified a general condition under which scaling limits of CMJ processes belong to the BH universality class. Moreover, we found the very surprising result (at least, to us) that any CMJ process with finite variance offspring distribution satisfies this condition, and thus belongs to the BH universality class.

We will also make use of general convergence results established in
[
18
,
19
]
, which make it possible to reduce the joint convergence of the Lukasiewicz path, height and contour processes to the study of a multi-dimensional renewal process constructed from the ladder height process associated to the Lukasiewicz path , see the discussion following Theorem
2.1
.

1.3.
The binary, homogeneous universality class

Consider now the binary, homogenous case. Here the situation is opposite to the BH case: because individuals give birth at constant rate during their lifetime, individuals who live for a long time have a large offspring.

In the present paper we consider the case
{\mathbb{E}}(R)=\infty
, i.e., the “typical” age at child-bearing is large, but
{\mathbb{E}}(V)<\infty
, which is intermediate between the two-aforementioned cases (see Remark
4.10
). Our goal is to identify a large class of CMJ processes that have the same scaling limit as binary, homogeneous CMJ processes. Heuristically, we consider a case similar to the binary, homogeneous case where
{\mathcal{P}}=\mu\mid_{V}
with
\mu
and
V
independent, and we identify precise conditions for
\mu
to ‘look like’ a renewal process so that the CMJ process has the same scaling limit as the corresponding binary, homogeneous CMJ process.

In the present paper we only treat the non-triangular case. For the GW and BH universality classes, non-triangular results readily extend to the triangular case. However, here we found that the situation is more subtle and that we may have
\mu
which ‘looks like’ a renewal process and yet, the scaling limit be different from the scaling limit of the corresponding binary, homogeneous CMJ process.

Our result states that the height and contour processes coincide asymptotically with the Lukasiewicz path up to a deterministic space-time change. This result is in the same spirit of

2.5.
Example showing the necessity of Assumption R2

As already explained, our goal is to generalize known results on the binary, homogeneous case. Intuitively, we expect a CMJ process where births occur ‘like’ a renewal process to behave similarly: upon scaling, only long edges matter, and so if upon scaling the offspring measure
{\mathcal{P}}
looks like Lebesgue measure, the considered CMJ process should belong to the binary, homogeneous universality class. This is exactly the rational behind Assumption R1.

However, the following example shows that Assumptions V, C and R1 may be satisfied and yet,
S_{p}
does not converge (and thus, the conclusion of Theorem
2.1
fails). Thus, additional conditions are called upon, and
the following example shows that Assumption R2 (which prevents accumulation of atoms near
0
) is sharp since if it is not satisfied then the conclusion may not hold.

Let
\tau
be an integer-valued random variable in the domain of attraction of a
\gamma^{\prime}
-stable distribution with
1<\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
:
{\mathbb{P}}(\tau\geq x)=\ell(x)/x^{\gamma^{\prime}}
for some slowly varying function
\ell
. Assuming that
\tau
and
V
are independent, we then consider

\mu=\tau\delta_{1/2}+\sum_{k\geq 1}\delta_{k}.

We then have
\mu(t)=[t]+\tau 1_{t\geq 1/2}
so that R1 is satisfied. Choose moreover
V
independent from
\mu
such that V and C are satisfied, in particular
V
is in the domain of attraction of a
\gamma
-stable distribution. However, for every
a^{\prime}<a
and
x>0

\liminf_{p\to\infty}p{\mathbb{P}}(v_{p}\mu(x/v_{p})/x\geq 1/a^{\prime})\geq\liminf_{p\to\infty}p{\mathbb{P}}(v_{p}\tau/x\geq 1/a^{\prime})=\infty

so that R2 is not satisfied. (Here we use the fact that
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
so that the tail of
\tau
is heavier than the tail of
V
.)

Let us now argue that Theorem
2.1
does not hold. We have
\mu(V)=[V]+\tau 1_{V\geq 1/2}
and so
\mu(V)
is roughly the sum of two independent random variables with tails
\propto x^{-\gamma}
and
\propto x^{-\gamma^{\prime}}
. In this case it is clear that the random variable with the heavier tail will dominate, which in our case is
\tau
since
\gamma^{\prime}<\gamma
. In particular, we have the approximation
{\mathbb{P}}(\mu(V)\geq n)\approx n^{-\gamma^{\prime}}
. In this case, we know that
S([p])
is of the order of
p^{1/{\gamma^{\prime}}}\gg p^{1/\gamma}
, so that
\lvert S_{p}(1)\rvert\Rightarrow+\infty
.

Remark 2.5
.

Beside providing a counter-example to Theorem
2.1
when Assumption R2 fails, we believe that this case is interesting in its own. We conjecture that in this case, the height and Lukasiewicz do not have the same scaling, and it would be interesting to study its scaling limit.

3.
Two models

For the sake of illustration, we propose two explicit models for which Assumptions R1 and R2 are also satisfied.

Model 1 (renewal).
We first show that our results apply to any renewal process. More precisely, let
(\xi_{n},n\geq 1)
be non-negative i.i.d. random variables with finite mean and
\mu=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\delta_{\sum_{k=1}^{n}\xi_{k}}
the renewal process with step distribution
\xi_{1}
and no delay.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
\mu
is the aforementioned renewal process, then Assumptions
R1
and
R2
hold with
a={\mathbb{E}}(\xi_{1})
and any
a^{\prime}<a
.

Proof.

Assumption R1 is a direct consequence of the strong law of large numbers, while R2 comes from Markov inequality: for any
\theta>0
,

p{\mathbb{P}}(v_{p}\mu(x/v_{p})/x\geq 1/a^{\prime})=p{\mathbb{P}}(A(x/(a^{\prime}v_{p}))\leq x/v_{p})\leq pe^{\theta/v_{p}+\log{\mathbb{E}}(e^{-\theta\xi})/(v_{p}a^{\prime})}

and since
{\mathbb{E}}(\xi)=a>a^{\prime}
, we can choose
\theta
such that
\theta+\log{\mathbb{E}}(e^{-\theta\xi})/a^{\prime}=\varphi<0
, so that

p{\mathbb{P}}(v_{p}\mu(x/v_{p})/x\geq 1/a^{\prime})\leq pe^{-\varphi/v_{p}}.

It is well known that
1/v_{p}\geq p^{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
(see for instance
[
4
, Proposition
1.3.6
]
), so that
p{\mathbb{P}}(v_{p}\mu(x/v_{p})/x\geq 1/a^{\prime})\to 0
as desired.
∎

Model 2 (switching Poisson).
We now consider a second model where
\mu
is a Poisson process but where the intensity evolves randomly. The model we consider illustrates how Assumptions R1 and R2 can be checked, it could probably be generalized in various directions.

Let
(\sigma_{n},n\geq 0)
be a sequence of positive i.i.d. real-valued random variables with finite mean
m
and
\Sigma_{k}=\sigma_{1}+\cdots+\sigma_{k}
for
k\geq 0
with
\Sigma_{0}=0
. Let
L\subset(0,\infty)
be a finite set and
\Lambda=(\Lambda_{n},n\geq 0)
an
L
-valued irreducible Markov chain with unique invariant distribution
\Lambda_{\infty}
.

The offspring process
\mu
that we consider is defined as follows: conditional on
\Lambda
, for every
k\geq 1
,
\mu
restricted on the interval

[\Sigma_{k-1},\Sigma_{k})
is an independent Poisson point process with intensity
\Lambda_{k-1}
.

Lemma 3.2
.

If
\mu
is the aforementioned measure, then Assumptions
R1
and
R2
are satisfied with
a=1/{\mathbb{E}}(\Lambda_{\infty})
and any
a^{\prime}<1/\max L
.

Proof.

Let us first check Assumption R1. For
t\geq 0
let
N(t)=\min\{n\geq 0:\Sigma_{n}\geq t\}
: then
t\leq N(t)
and so

\frac{\mu(t)}{t}\leq\frac{1}{t}\mu\left(\Sigma_{N(t)}\right)=\frac{N(t)}{t}\times\frac{1}{N(t)}\sum_{k=1}^{N(t)}\mu\left([\Sigma_{k-1},\Sigma_{k})\right),

The strong law of large numbers implies
N(t)/t\to 1/m
almost surely. Moreover,
(\mu([\Sigma_{k-1},\Sigma_{k})),k\geq 1)
is by construction an ergodic Markov chain with ergodic measure a mixed Poisson process with random intensity
\sigma_{0}\Lambda_{\infty}
, the two terms
\sigma_{0}
and
\Lambda_{\infty}
being independent in this product. Thus the ergodic theorem implies that

\frac{1}{N(t)}\sum_{k=1}^{N(t)}\mu\left([\Sigma_{k-1},\Sigma_{k})\right)\to{\mathbb{E}}\left(\sigma_{0}\Lambda_{\infty}\right)=m{\mathbb{E}}\left(\Lambda_{\infty}\right),

the convergence taking place almost surely and in
L^{1}
. This implies that
\mu(t)/t\to{\mathbb{E}}(\Lambda_{\infty})
in
L^{1}
.

Let us now check Assumption R2. Consider
\mu^{*}
a Poisson process with intensity
\ell^{*}:=\max L
. We can couple
\mu
and
\mu^{*}
such that
\mu\leq\mu^{*}
, so that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{v_{p}}{x}\mu\left(\frac{x}{v_{p}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{a^{\prime}}\right)\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\mu^{*}\left(\frac{x}{v_{p}}\right)\geq\frac{x}{a^{\prime}v_{p}}\right).

If
X
is a Poisson random variable with parameter
\kappa
, then for
y>\kappa
we have the classical large-deviation bound

{\mathbb{P}}(X\geq y)\leq\exp\left(-y\varphi\left(\frac{y}{\kappa}\right)\right)\ \text{ with }\varphi(u)=u-1-\log(u)\geq 0.

Since
\mu^{*}
is a Poisson process with intensity
\ell^{*}
, for
a^{\prime}\ell^{*}<1
this gives

p{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{v_{p}}{x}\mu\left(\frac{x}{v_{p}}\right)\geq\frac{1}{a^{\prime}}\right)\leq p\exp\left(-\frac{x}{a^{\prime}v_{p}}\varphi\left(\frac{1}{a^{\prime}\ell^{*}}\right)\right).

Since
a^{\prime}\ell^{*}<1
, we have in particular
\varphi(1/(a^{\prime}\ell^{*}))\neq 0
. Moreover, as already mentioned above we have
1/v_{p}\geq p^{\delta}
for some
\delta>0
which implies that the previous upper bound vanishes. This shows that Assumption R2 holds and concludes the proof.
∎

4.1.
Preliminary results

4.1.1.
Tail behavior of
\mu(V)

Assumption C is the usual criticality assumption, which makes the Lukasiewicz path a random walk with mean

to be in the domain of attraction of a stable law. We now show that under Assumption A, the scaling of
S
is governed by that of
V
.

Lemma 4.1
.

If Assumption
A
holds, then
p{\mathbb{P}}(\mu(V)\geq x/v_{p})\to(ax)^{-\gamma}
for every
x>0
. In particular,
S_{p}\Rightarrow S_{\infty}
.

Proof.

Let
\overline{F}(x)={\mathbb{P}}(V\geq x)
, so that
v_{p}
satisfies by definition
p\overline{F}(xv_{p})\to x^{-\gamma}
.

First a preliminary result: if
x_{p}\to x
, then
p\overline{F}(x_{p}/v_{p})\to x^{-\gamma}
. Indeed, let
\varepsilon>0
and
p
large enough so that
(1-\varepsilon)x\leq x_{p}\leq(1+\varepsilon)x
. Then by monotonicity of
\overline{F}
,

p\overline{F}((1+\varepsilon)x/v_{p})\leq p\overline{F}(x_{p}/v_{p})\leq p\overline{F}((1-\varepsilon)x/v_{p})

and so

\limsup_{p}p\overline{F}(x_{p}/v_{p})\leq((1-\varepsilon)x)^{-\gamma}\to x^{-\gamma}

with the last limit obtained as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. A similar result is obtained for the
\liminf
, which proves the claim.

Next, define
X_{p}=v_{p}A(x/v_{p})
and write

\displaystyle p{\mathbb{P}}(\mu(V)\geq x/v_{p})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(p\overline{F}(A(x/v_{p}))\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p})\right)

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p}));X_{p}\geq a^{\prime}x\right)+{\mathbb{E}}\left(p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p});X_{p}<a^{\prime}x\right).

Since
X_{p}\Rightarrow ax
by Assumption R1 and
a^{\prime}<a
, we have

p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p})1(X_{p}\geq a^{\prime}x)\Rightarrow(ax)^{-\gamma}

by the preliminary result, and since

p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p})1(X_{p}\geq a^{\prime}x)\leq p\overline{F}(a^{\prime}x/v_{p}/2)\to(a^{\prime}x)^{-\gamma},

we get by domination

{\mathbb{E}}\left(p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p}));X_{p}\geq a^{\prime}x\right)\to(ax)^{-\gamma}.

As for the second term, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left(p\overline{F}(X_{p}/v_{p});X_{p}<a^{\prime}x\right)

\displaystyle\leq p{\mathbb{P}}(X_{p}\leq a^{\prime}x/2)

\displaystyle=p{\mathbb{P}}(A(x/v_{p})\leq a^{\prime}x/v_{p})

\displaystyle=p{\mathbb{P}}(x/v_{p}\leq\mu(a^{\prime}x/v_{p}))

which is assumed to vanish by Assumption R2.

This proves that
p{\mathbb{P}}(\mu(V)\geq x/v_{p})\to(ax)^{-\gamma}
. In order to prove convergence of the rescaled random walk
S_{p}
, it is sufficient to prove convergence of
S_{p}(1)
. We compute its Laplace transform and show that it is given by (
2.1
). We have just proved that
\mu(V)
is in the domain of attraction of a
\gamma
-stable distribution, and so there exists
\ell
slowly varying with
{\mathbb{P}}(\mu(V)\geq x)=\ell(x)/x^{\gamma}
. In particular, the result that we have just proved says that
pv_{p}^{\gamma}\ell(1/v_{p})\to a^{-\gamma}
. We then have
{\mathbb{P}}(\mu(V)-1\geq x)=\ell(x+1)/(x+1)^{\gamma}
: since
\ell(x+1)\sim\ell(x)
because
\ell
is slowly varying (as a consequence of the uniform convergence theorem
[
4
, Theorem 1.2.1]
), Theorem 8.1.6 in
[
4
]
gives

{\mathbb{E}}(e^{-s(\mu(V)-1)})=1+s^{\gamma}\ell^{\prime}(1/s)+o(s^{\gamma}\ell^{\prime}(1/s))

with
\ell^{\prime}(x)=\ell(x)/(-\Gamma(1-\gamma))
, as
s\downarrow 0
, and so

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(e^{-\lambda S_{p}(1)})

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}(e^{-\lambda v_{p}(\mu(V)-1)})^{p}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(p\log(1+\lambda^{\gamma}v_{p}^{\gamma}\ell^{\prime}(1/(\lambda v_{p}))+o(v^{\gamma}_{p}\ell^{\prime}(1/v_{p})))\right).

We have

pv^{\gamma}_{p}\ell^{\prime}(1/v_{p})=\frac{pv_{p}^{\gamma}\ell(1/v_{p})}{-\Gamma(1-\gamma)}\to\frac{1}{-a^{\gamma}\Gamma(1-\gamma)}

so that

{\mathbb{E}}(e^{-\lambda S_{p}(1)})={\mathbb{E}}(e^{-\lambda v_{p}(\mu(V)-1)})^{p}\to\exp\left(\lambda^{\gamma}/(-a^{\gamma}\Gamma(1-\gamma))\right)

which gives the result.
∎

4.1.3.
Two lemmas

In the sequel we let

denote a random variable distributed as

for

. The following two simple lemmas will be crucial for the next arguments.

Lemma 4.4
.

As
x\to\infty
we have
V_{x}\Longrightarrow V_{\infty}
in
L^{1}
.

Proof.

Since
V
is in the domain of attraction of a
\gamma
-stable distribution, there exists
\ell
slowly varying with

. For

{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq yx\mid V\geq x)=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq yx)}{{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq x)}=\frac{\ell(xy)/\ell(x)}{y^{\gamma}}

and so the convergence
V_{x}\Rightarrow V_{\infty}
follows from the fact that
\ell
is slowly varying. Let us now prove that
V_{x}
is uniformly integrable. For
K\geq 1
we have

{\mathbb{E}}(V_{x};V_{x}\geq K)={\mathbb{E}}(V/x1(V/x\geq K)\mid V\geq x)=\frac{{\mathbb{E}}(V;V\geq xK)}{x{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq x)}.

We can compute

{\mathbb{E}}(V;V\geq xK)=xK{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq xK)+\int_{xK}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq u)\mathrm{d}u.

Theorem
1.5.11
in
[
bingham89:0
]
entails that, as

,

{\mathbb{E}}(V;V\geq xK)\sim\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}xK{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq xK)

and so

{\mathbb{E}}(V_{x};V_{x}\geq K)\sim\frac{\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}xK{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq xK)}{x{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq x)}=\frac{\gamma}{\gamma-1}\frac{\ell(xK)/(xK)^{\gamma-1}}{\ell(x)/x^{\gamma-1}}\sim\frac{\gamma}{(\gamma-1)K^{\gamma-1}}

which shows the uniform integrability of
V_{x}
.
∎

4.2.
Convergence of
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})

We prove in this section that
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})\Rightarrow(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})
. It is well-known that in order to prove the convergence of a random walk such as
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})
, one needs to control the law of its increments
(R(1),{\mathcal{Z}}(1))
, and we begin by describing this law.

We enrich the probability space so as to consider a triple
(V,\mu,U)\in(0,\infty)\times{\mathcal{M}}\times[0,1]
such that under
{\mathbb{P}}
,
U
is independent from
(V,\mu)
and is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
. From this triplet
(V,\mu,U)
we construct the following random variables:

\widehat{Z}=[U\mu(V)]\ \text{ and }\ \widehat{R}=A(\widehat{Z}).

Thus, under
{\mathbb{P}}
,
\widehat{Z}
is the rank of an atom of
{\mathcal{P}}=\mu\mid_{V}
chosen uniformly at random and
\widehat{R}
is its location. We also consider the measure
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
which is absolutely continuous with respect to
{\mathbb{P}}
with Radon–Nikodym derivative
\mu(V)
: for any random variable
X\geq 0
,

\widehat{\mathbb{E}}(X)={\mathbb{E}}\left(\mu(V)X\right).

The measure
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
is the usual
offspring-biased
measure, see for instance
[
14
,
15
]
.

Lemma 4.6
.

(V_{T(1)-1},{\mathcal{Z}}(1),R(1))
under
{\mathbb{P}}
is equal in distribution to
(V,\widehat{Z},\widehat{R})
under
\widehat{\mathbb{P}}
. In particular, for any
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
we have

{\mathbb{E}}\left[f\left(V_{T(1)-1},{\mathcal{Z}}(1),R(1)\right)\right]={\mathbb{E}}\left[\mu(V)f(V,\widehat{Z},\widehat{R})\right].

Proof.

Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{N}}\times{\mathbb{N}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be measurable. By definition,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}(f(V,\widehat{Z},\widehat{R}))

\displaystyle={\mathbb{E}}\left(\mu(V)f(V,\widehat{Z},A(\widehat{Z}))\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geq 1}x{\mathbb{E}}\left(f(V,[Ux],A([Ux]));\mu(V)=x\right).

Since
U
under
{\mathbb{P}}
is uniformly distributed on
[0,1]
,
[Ux]
is uniformly distributed on
\{0,\ldots,x-1\}
and so

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathbb{E}}(f(V_{T(1)-1},\widehat{Z},\widehat{R}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geq 1}\sum_{z=0}^{x-1}{\mathbb{E}}\left(f(V,z,A(z));\mu(V)=x\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x\geq 1,z\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}\left(f(V,z,A(z));\mu(V)=x+z\right).

On the other hand, we have according to
[
19
, Equation (2.3)]

{\mathbb{E}}(f({\mathcal{Z}}(1),R(1)))=\sum_{x\geq 1,z\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}\left(f(z,A_{\mathcal{P}}(z));\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\rvert=x+z\right)

and the argument of the proof of this equality readily generalizes to give

{\mathbb{E}}(f(V_{T(1)-1},{\mathcal{Z}}(1),R(1)))=\sum_{x\geq 1,z\geq 0}{\mathbb{E}}\left(f(V,z,A_{\mathcal{P}}(z));\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\rvert=x+z\right).

Since
\lvert{\mathcal{P}}\rvert=\mu(V)
and
A(z)=A_{{\mathcal{P}}}(z)
for
z\leq\mu(V)
, the two expressions match, i.e.,
{\mathbb{E}}(f(V_{T(1)-1}{\mathcal{Z}}(1),R(1)))=\widehat{\mathbb{E}}(f(V,\widehat{Z},\widehat{R}))
, which proves the result.
∎

This expression for the law of
(V_{T(1)-1},R(1),{\mathcal{Z}}(1))
makes it possible to prove the two following technical lemmas.

Lemma 4.7
.

Let
g:{\mathbb{R}}^{2}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be measurable, bounded and such that the Lebesgue measure of its set of discontinuity points is zero. Then for any
\delta>0
we have

pv_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(g\left(v_{p}R(1),v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\right);V_{T(1)-1}\geq\delta/v_{p}\right)\to\frac{1}{a\delta^{\gamma-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(V_{\infty}g\left(\delta UV_{\infty},\frac{\delta UV_{\infty}}{a}\right)\right)

where
U
and
V_{\infty}
are independent.

Proof.

Lemma
4.6
gives

pv_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(g(v_{p}R(1),v_{p}Z(1));V_{T(1)-1}\geq\delta/v_{p}\right)=pv_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\mu(V)g(v_{p}\widehat{R},v_{p}\widehat{Z});V\geq\delta/v_{p}\right).

Let
x=\delta/v_{p}
: recalling that, by definition,
V_{x}
is equal in distribution to
V/x
conditioned on
V\geq x
and that
\mu
and
V
are independent, we can write

pv_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(\mu(V)g(v_{p}\widehat{R},v_{p}\widehat{Z});V\geq\delta/v_{p}\right)\\
=p(\delta/x){\mathbb{P}}(V\geq x){\mathbb{E}}\left(\mu(xV_{x})g((\delta/x)A([U\mu(xV_{x})]),(\delta/x)[U\mu(xV_{x})])\right)

with
\mu
and
V_{x}
independent. Introducing

\Phi(u,v)=\frac{\mu(uv)}{uv}g\left(\frac{\delta A([U\mu(uv)])}{u},\frac{\delta[U\mu(uv)]}{u}\right)\ \text{ and }\ \phi(u,v)={\mathbb{E}}(\Phi(u,v)),

we can rewrite this as

(4.1)

pv_{p}{\mathbb{E}}\left(g(v_{p}R(1),v_{p}Z(1));V_{T(1)-1}\geq\delta/v_{p}\right)=p\delta{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq\delta/v_{p}){\mathbb{E}}\left(V_{x}\phi(x,V_{x})\right).

By choice of
v_{p}
we have
p{\mathbb{P}}(V\geq\delta/v_{p})\to\delta^{-\gamma}
and so it remains to show that
{\mathbb{E}}(V_{x}\phi(x,V_{x}))\to{\mathbb{E}}(V_{\infty}g(\delta UV_{\infty},\delta UV_{\infty}/a))/a
. Since
V_{x}\Rightarrow V_{\infty}
and
V_{x}
is uniformly integrable (Lemma
4.4
) and
\lvert\phi(u,v)\rvert\leq\sup\lvert g\rvert f(uv)
with
f
bounded (Lemma
4.5
), we only have to show that
\phi(x,\alpha(x))\to a^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}[g(\delta Uv_{\infty},\delta Uv_{\infty}/a)]
as
x\to\infty
, for any function
\alpha
with
\alpha(x)\to v_{\infty}\in[1,\infty)
as
x\to\infty
.

For such
\alpha
, we have as

\frac{\mu(x\alpha(x))}{x\alpha(x)}\Rightarrow\frac{1}{a}\ \text{ and }\ \left(\frac{\delta A([U\mu(x\alpha(x))])}{x},\frac{\delta[U\mu(x\alpha(x))]}{x}\right)\Rightarrow(\delta Uv_{\infty},\delta Uv_{\infty}/a).

Since by assumption,

is almost surely continuous at

, we obtain
\Phi(x,\alpha(x))\to g(\delta Uv_{\infty},\delta Uv_{\infty}/a)/a

Next, since

converges to

implies that
\phi(x,\alpha(x))\to{\mathbb{E}}[g(\delta Uv_{\infty},\delta Uv_{\infty}/a)]/a

∎

Corollary 4.9
.

For any
x,y>0
we have

pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x,v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y\right)\to\frac{1}{a(\gamma-1)(\max(x,ay))^{\gamma-1}}.

In particular,
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})\Rightarrow(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})
.

Proof.

Consider
\delta>0
and write

pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x,v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y\right)=pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x,v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y,V_{T(1)-1}\geq\delta/v_{p}\right)\\
+pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x,v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y,V_{T(1)-1}<\delta/v_{p}\right).

For
\delta
small enough, the second term vanishes by Lemma
4.8
, so that we obtain by Lemma
4.7

pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x,v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y\right)\to\frac{1}{a\delta^{\gamma-1}}{\mathbb{E}}\left(V_{\infty};\delta UV_{\infty}\geq x,\delta UV_{\infty}\geq ay\right).

Let
d=\max(x,ay)
: then for
\delta<d
, we have by definition of
V_{\infty}
and (

)

{\mathbb{E}}\left(V_{\infty};\delta UV_{\infty}\geq x,\delta UV_{\infty}\geq ay\right)=\int_{d/\delta}^{\infty}u^{-\gamma}\mathrm{d}u=\frac{(\delta/d)^{\gamma-1}}{\gamma-1}

which gives the desired convergence. This convergence implies that
(R_{p}(1),{\mathcal{Z}}_{p}(1))
converges in distribution to a bivariate stable random variable by an application of a bivariate Tauberian theorem that generalizes the argument of Gnedenko and Kolmogorov
[
8
]
mentioned above.
One can for instance use the results in
[
5
]
.

It remains to identify the limit. The same arguments as above lead to

pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y\right)\to\frac{1}{a^{\gamma}(\gamma-1)y^{\gamma-1}}

and so the limit of
pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x,v_{p}{\mathcal{Z}}(1)\geq y\right)
is the same as the limit obtained by replacing
R(1)
by
a{\mathcal{Z}}(1)
. This shows that
(R_{p}(1),{\mathcal{Z}}_{p}(1))\Rightarrow(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty}(1),{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty}(1))
, and this convergence implies the desired result.
∎

Remark 4.10
.

With the same arguments as in the previous proof, one can show that
pv_{p}{\mathbb{P}}\left(v_{p}R(1)\geq x\right)\to 1/(a(\gamma-1)x^{\gamma-1})
. In particular,
R(1)
is in the domain of attraction of stable distribution with index
\gamma-1\in(0,1)
, so that
{\mathbb{E}}(R(1))=\infty
. This proves the claim made in the introduction that we focus in the present paper in the case
{\mathbb{E}}(V)<\infty
and
{\mathbb{E}}(\int u{\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{d}u))=\infty
(since
{\mathbb{E}}(\int u{\mathcal{P}}(\mathrm{d}u))={\mathbb{E}}(R(1))
).

4.3.
Proof of Theorem
2.1

We now proceed to the proof of Theorem
2.1
, i.e., we assume that Assumption A holds and we prove that

(4.3)

({\mathbb{H}}_{p},{\mathbb{C}}_{p},\underline{S}_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{fdd}}}{{\Rightarrow}}\ \left(a\underline{S}_{\infty},a\underline{S}_{\infty}(\cdot/2{\mathbb{E}}(V)),\underline{S}_{\infty}\right).

4.3.1.
It is enough to prove that
({\mathbb{H}}_{p},\underline{S}_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{fdd}}}{{\Rightarrow}}\ (a\underline{S}_{\infty},\underline{S}_{\infty})

First, we recall Theorem
4.1
in
[
18
]
which, in the non-triangular case of the present paper, can be stated as follows.

Theorem 4.11

(Theorem
4.1
in
[
18
]
)
.

If the following conditions hold:

•

{\mathbb{E}}(V)<\infty
and
{\mathbb{E}}(\mu(V))=1
;

•

S_{p}\Rightarrow S_{\infty}
;

•

{\mathbb{H}}_{p}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{fdd}}}{{\Rightarrow}}{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
for some process
{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}
which is (almost surely) continuous at
0
and satisfies the condition
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}(t)>0)
for every
t>0
;

then
({\mathbb{H}}_{p},{\mathbb{C}}_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{fdd}}}{{\Rightarrow}}({\mathbb{H}}_{\infty},{\mathbb{H}}_{\infty}(\,\cdot\,/(2{\mathbb{E}}(V)))).

As the two first assumptions of this result hold under Assumption A (see Lemma
4.1
for the convergence

), it follows from this result that in order to prove (

) it is enough to prove that

.

4.3.2.
Proof of
({\mathbb{H}}_{p},\underline{S}_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{fdd}}}{{\Rightarrow}}\ (a\underline{S}_{\infty},\underline{S}_{\infty})

By definition of tightness and of the product topology, tightness of two sequences
(X^{1}_{p})
and
(X^{2}_{p})
is equivalent to the tightness of the pair of sequences
(X^{1}_{p},X^{2}_{p})
in the product topology. However, the following stronger result holds for random walks.

Lemma 4.12
.

Let
(X^{1}_{p},X^{2}_{p},X^{3}_{p})
be a three dimensional random walk. If each sequence
(X^{1}_{p})
,
(X^{2}_{p})
and
(X^{3}_{p})
is tight in
D({\mathbb{R}})
, then
(X^{1}_{p},X^{2}_{p},X^{3}_{p})
is tight in
D({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

Proof.

The proof follows by the equivalence of the following statements:

(1)

(X_{p}^{1})
,
(X_{p}^{2})
and
(X_{p}^{3})
are each tight in
D({\mathbb{R}})
;

(2)

(X_{p}^{1}(1))
,
(X_{p}^{2}(1))
and
(X_{p}^{3}(1))
are each tight in
{\mathbb{R}}
;

(3)

(X_{p}^{1}(1),X_{p}^{2}(1),X_{p}^{3}(1))
is tight in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
;

(4)

(X_{p}^{1},X_{p}^{2},X_{p}^{3})
is tight in
D({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
.

The implications (1)
\Rightarrow
(2), (2)
\Rightarrow
(3) and (3)
\Rightarrow
(4) are immediate, while the implication (4)
\Rightarrow
(1) holds true because
(X^{1}_{p},X^{2}_{p},X^{3}_{p})
is a random walk, see for instance
[
9
, Corollary VII.3.6]
.
∎

Since
(T_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})\Rightarrow(T_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})
by Corollary
4.2
and
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})\Rightarrow(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})
by Corollary
4.9
, it follows that
(T_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p},R_{p})\Rightarrow(T_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})
, the convergence taking place in
D({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
by Lemma
4.12
. We now show that the convergence of this trivariate renewal process implies that
({\mathbb{H}}_{p},\underline{S}_{p})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\textnormal{fdd}}}{{\Rightarrow}}\ (a\underline{S}_{\infty},\underline{S}_{\infty})
.

By the Skorohod embedding theorem, we can assume without loss of generality that
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p},T_{p})
converges to
(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},T_{\infty})
almost surely. Fix in the rest of the proof some
t\geq 0
. For
p\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup\{\infty\}
let
\overline{S}_{p}(t)=\sup_{[0,t]}S_{p}-S_{p}(t)
be the process
S_{p}
reflected above its past supremum and
L_{p}
its local time process at
0
, which is the right-continuous inverse of
T_{p}
:

L_{p}(t)=\inf\left\{s\geq 0:T_{p}(s)>t\right\}.

First, note that because
S_{\infty}
is spectrally positive, is not a subordinator or a pure drift and has unbounded variation,
0
is regular for
(-\infty,0)
according to
[
3
, Theorem VII.1]
and also for
(0,\infty)
according to
[
3
, Corollary VII.5]
.

Since
S_{\infty}
is spectrally positive, we have
{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{S}_{\infty}(t)=0)={\mathbb{P}}(\inf_{[0,t]}S_{\infty}=0)
by
[
3
, Proposition VI.3]
. We deduce that
\overline{S}_{\infty}(t)>0
almost surely. Since
\Delta T_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t))
corresponds to the size of the excursion of
\overline{S}_{\infty}
away from
0
straddling
t

[
3
, Proposition IV.5]
, an immediate consequence of
\overline{S}_{\infty}(t)>0
is that
T_{\infty}
jumps at time
L_{\infty}(t)
.

Also, this implies that
T_{p}\circ L_{p}(t)\to T_{\infty}\circ L_{\infty}(t)
. Indeed, for
p\in{\mathbb{N}}\cup{\infty}
we have

T_{p}(L_{p}(t))=\inf\{s>t:\overline{S}_{p}(s)=0\}.

(For
p\in{\mathbb{N}}
this comes from the various definitions, and for
p=\infty
see for instance
[
3
, Proposition IV.7]
.)
A a consequence,
T_{p}(L_{p}(t))-t
is the hitting time of
0
by
\overline{S}_{p}(t\,+\,\cdot)
. Because the reflection does not kick in before
\overline{S}_{\infty}
hits
0
, conditionally on
\overline{S}_{\infty}(t)=x
(which is almost surely
>0
by the previous remark) this is the same in distribution as the entrance time in
[0,\infty)
of
S_{\infty}
started in
-x
. Since
0
is regular for
(0,\infty)
, this entrance time is continuous with respect to the Skorohod topology at
S_{\infty}
, see for instance
[
9
, Proposition VII.2.11]
or
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
. We thus obtain
T_{p}(L_{p}(t))\to T_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t))
. Since
t-T_{p}(L_{p}(t)-)
is the hitting time of
S_{p}
looked backward in time, the same argument shows by reversing time that
T_{p}(L_{p}(t)-)\to T_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t)-)
and so
\Delta T_{p}(L_{p}(t))\to\Delta T_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t))
.

Since
\Delta T_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t))\neq 0
,
T_{p}\to T_{\infty}
in
D({\mathbb{R}})
and
L_{p}(t)\to L_{\infty}(t)
, the convergence
\Delta T_{p}(L_{p}(t))\to\Delta T_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t))
means that the jump of
T_{\infty}
at
L_{\infty}(t)
is carried in the pre-limit at time
L_{p}(t)
, see for instance
[
9
, Proposition VI.2.1]
. Since
(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p},T_{p})\to(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},T_{\infty})
in
D({\mathbb{R}}^{3})
, this implies that

(4.4)

(R_{p},{\mathcal{Z}}_{p})\big(L_{p}(t)-\big)\to(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})\big(L_{\infty}(t)-\big)

by standard properties of the Skorohod topology, see for instance
[
9
, Proposition VI.2.1]
.

By Theorem
4.3
, there exists some sequence
\rho_{p}\to\infty
such that for each
t\geq 0
we have

({\mathbb{H}}_{p}(t),\underline{S}_{p}(t))=(R_{\rho_{p}},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\rho_{p}})\big(L_{\rho_{p}}(t)-\big),\ \ \mbox{in law}.

In view of this equality in distribution, the convergence (
4.4
) therefore implies that
({\mathbb{H}}_{p}(t),\underline{S}_{p}(t))\Rightarrow(a{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty},{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty})(L_{\infty}(t)-)
. Since on the other hand
\underline{S}_{p}\Rightarrow\underline{S}_{\infty}
by Corollary
4.2
, we see that
\underline{S}_{\infty}(t)
and
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\infty}(L_{\infty}(t)-)
are equal in distribution, the previous convergence achieves the proof of Theorem
2.1
.
