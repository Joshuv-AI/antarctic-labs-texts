Can radial temperature profiles be inferred using NH 3 (1, 1) and (2, 2) observations? 2023 Can radial temperature profiles be inferred using NH 3 (1, 1) and (2, 2) observations? – A.6
Robert Estalella, Aina Palau, Gemma Busquet

Abstract
A number of works infer radial temperature profiles of envelopes surrounding young stellar objects using several rotational transitions in a pixel-by-pixel or azimuthally-averaged basis. However, in many cases the assumption that the rotational temperature is constant along the line of sight is made, while this is not the case when a partially resolved envelope, assumed to be spherically symmetric, is used to obtain values of temperature for different projected radii. This kind of analysis (homogeneous analysis) is intrinsically inconsistent. By using a spherical envelope model to interpret NH 3 ( 1 , 1 ) (1,1) and ( 2 , 2 ) (2,2) observations, we tested how robust it is to infer radial temperature profiles of an envelope. The temperature and density of the model envelope are power laws of radius, but the density can be flat for an inner central part. The homogeneous analysis was applied to obtain radial temperature profiles, and resulted that for small projected radii, where the optical depth of the lines is high, the homogeneous temperature can be much higher than the actual envelope temperature. In general, for larger projected radii, both the temperature and the temperature power-law index can be underestimated by as much as 40%, and 0.15, respectively. We applied this study to the infrared dark cloud G14.225 − - 0.506 for which the radial temperature profile was previously derived from the dust emission at submillimeter wavelengths and the spectral energy distribution. As expected, the homogeneous analysis underestimated both the temperature and the temperature power-law index.

Introduction

Characterizing the envelopes surrounding young stellar objects is important to correctly infer their properties and compare them to theoretical models.
A typical assumption used to describe these envelopes is to consider that their density and temperature decrease with radius following power-laws. This has widely been determined from observations
(
Beuther et al. 2002
;
Mueller et al. 2002
;
Hatchell &
van der Tak 2003
;
Williams et al. 2005
;
Butler &
Tan 2012
;
Giannetti
et al. 2013
;
Palau et al. 2014
;
Palau et al. 2021
;
Gieser
et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
and is also predicted from theoretical models
(
Larson 1969
;
Shu 1977
;
Gómez et al. 2021
, e.g.,)
.

The density and temperature structure of the envelopes surrounding young stellar objects have been determined in many cases using the radial intensity profiles from the continuum emission.
Even though it is widely known that molecular clouds are largely filamentary, it is also true that the most active star-forming sites are located at the centers of converging filaments, giving rise to the so-called hub-filament systems
(
Dewangan et al. 2020
;
Wang et al. 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2022
;
Liu et al. 2023
, e.g.,)
. It has been shown that the hubs are reasonably reproduced by models of spherical symmetry, such as those studied in
Beuther
et al. 2023
. Actually,
Kainulainen et al. 2013
find that fragmentation of molecular clouds at spatial scales 0.5–10 pc is in agreement with the fragmentation expected for a self-gravitating cylinder, and therefore a filamentary structure, while at scales
<0.5
pc the fragmentation is in agreement with spherical Jeans fragmentation.
In addition, it has been shown that assuming spherical geometry is reasonable for cores embedded within filamentary structures for aspect ratios up to 2
(
Roy et al. 2014
)
.
Thus, even in the era of filamentary molecular clouds, assuming spherical symmetry to model the hubs where active star formation is taking place seems a reasonable first approximation.

In the case of spherical symmetry, and if one assumes optically thin emission, the Rayleigh-Jeans approximation, and an envelope with infinite radius, the intensity as function of the projected distance to the source,
b
, follows a power-law with index
1-(p+q)
, where
p
and
q
are the power-law indices of the density and temperature
(
Beltrán et al. 2002
;
Beuther et al. 2002
;
Palau et al. 2014
, e.g.,)
.
Therefore, given an observed radial intensity profile, one needs to make a specific assumption about either the density or the temperature to infer the other.

Molecular line observations, including transitions of NH
3
, H
2
CO, CH
3
CN, HCOOCH
3
, and complex organic molecules, of partially resolved envelopes have been used to derive the temperature radial profile of the envelope
(
Ahmadi
et al. 2018
;
Beltrán
et al. 2018
;
Gieser
et al. 2019
;
Gieser
et al. 2021
;
Busch et al. 2022
, e.g.,)
.
The standard procedure to infer the temperature from rotational diagrams, for instance, of NH
3
, assumes that, along the line of sight, the medium is homogeneous and in particular that the rotational temperature is constant.
However, the derived values of
T_{\mathrm{rot}}
for different projected radii are interpreted as the radial profile of temperature of the envelope.
This kind of analysis is inconsistent because the gas is assumed to be homogeneous along the line of sight and, at the same time, inhomogeneous in the radial direction.

In this paper, we aim at studying whether the determination of the radial temperature profiles from the NH
3

(1,1)
and
(2,2)
data are a reasonable approach to the actual temperature profile.
Since the collisional coefficients for the quadrupole-hyperfine transitions of NH
3
are not available in the literature, the existing numerical radiative transfer models are of limited usefulness.
Thus, we developed a numerical radiative transfer calculation for the NH
3

(1,1)
and
(2,2)
hyperfines, which is described in Appendix A. A comparison with a full radiative transfer calculation is also included.
The structure of the paper is as follows:
in Section 2 we present the basics of the standard NH
3
analysis,
in Section 3 we present the limitations of this analysis, in Section 4 the spherical model used here is described,
in Section 5 the optically thin case is considered,
in Section 6 a radiative transfer calculation is used for different parameters of the envelope,
in Section 7 the results found here are applied to the G14.225
-
0.506 case and
in Section 8 we summarize our main conclusions.

To obtain the rotational temperature,
T_{\mathrm{rot}}
, and the total NH
3
column density,
N(\mathrm{NH_{3}})
, it is necessary to determine the fraction,
f_{jk}
, of ammonia molecules that are in the
(J,K)
rotational level,

n_{jk}=f_{jk}\,n(\mathrm{NH_{3}}),

(6)

from which the total hydrogen gas density is derived adopting an ammonia fractional abundance,
X
, assumed to be constant,

n(\mathrm{NH_{3}})=X\,n(\mathrm{H_{2}}).

(7)

The usual assumption for moderate temperatures is that only the metastable levels
(J=K)
of ammonia are populated, and that they are populated according to a unique excitation temperature, called the rotational temperature
T_{\mathrm{rot}}
.
The rotational temperature is given by the Boltzmann equation,

\frac{n_{22}}{n_{11}}=\frac{g_{22}}{g_{11}}\exp[-(E_{22}-E_{11})/kT_{\mathrm{rot}}],

(8)

where
g_{jk}
is the degeneracy and
E_{jk}
the energy of the level
(J,K)
(see Table
2
).
The latter equation is used routinely to derive the rotational temperature by assuming that
N_{22}/N_{11}\simeq n_{22}/n_{11}
,

T_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{(E_{22}-E_{11})/k}{\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{g_{22}}{g_{11}}\frac{N_{11}}{N_{22}}\right)}.

(9)

The rotational temperature can also been expressed as a function of the optical depths of the
(1,1)
and
(2,2)
main lines by using Eq.
5
,

T_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{(E_{22}-E_{11})/k}{\displaystyle\ln\left(\frac{A_{22}\,R_{22\mathrm{m}}\,g_{22}}{A_{11}\,R_{11\mathrm{m}}\,g_{11}}\frac{\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})}{\tau(2,2;\mathrm{m})}\right)},

(10)

resulting, in practical units, in

\left[\frac{T_{\mathrm{rot}}}{\mathrm{K}}\right]=\frac{40.99}{\displaystyle\ln\left(3.544\frac{\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})}{\tau(2,2;\mathrm{m})}\right)}.

(11)

The rotational temperature is a good approximation to the kinetic temperature if the radiative transitions up the
K
-ladder are negligible, and only collisional transitions between the metastable levels occur.
The rotational temperature can be corrected to estimate the kinetic temperature
(
Tafalla
et al. 2004
)
.
An improved version of the correction is given in
Estalella 2017
,

T_{\mathrm{rot}}=\frac{T_{\mathrm{k}}}{1+\displaystyle\frac{T_{\mathrm{k}}}{40.99}\ln\left[1+0.73\exp(-16.26/T_{\mathrm{k}})\right]}.

(12)

The last equation has to be solved iteratively to obtain
T_{\mathrm{k}}
.

3
Limitations of the standard NH
3
analysis

There is an implicit assumption in the approximations made to derive the expressions derived so far: that the gas is homogeneous and, in particular, that the rotational temperature is constant along the line of sight.
This has no importance as long as the clump of gas analyzed is assumed to be homogeneous. However, this is not the case when we analyze ammonia data of a partially resolved envelope, assumed to be spherically symmetric, and use Eq.
9
to obtain values of
T_{\mathrm{rot}}
for different projected radii (the radial profile of temperature of the envelope).
This kind of analysis is intrinsically inconsistent. On the one hand, Eq.
9
assumes that the gas is homogeneous along the line of sight, and on the other hand, these results are used to infer a radial dependence of
T_{\mathrm{rot}}
, implying that
T_{\mathrm{rot}}
is not constant along the line of sight.

In addition, the assumption of homogeneity along the line of sight can be incompatible with the observed intensities of the lines when observing a region with strong gradients of density or temperature along the line of sight.
Let us examine the three equations used to derive

\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})
(Eq.
3
),

\tau(2,2;\mathrm{m})
, (Eq.
4
), and

T_{\mathrm{rot}}
(Eq.
11
).
For simplicity, in this discussion we will call

\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}=\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,

\tau_{2\mathrm{m}}=\tau(2,2;\mathrm{m})
,

T_{1\mathrm{m}}=T_{\mathrm{L}}(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,

T_{1\mathrm{s}}=T_{\mathrm{L}}(1,1;\mathrm{s})
,

T_{2\mathrm{m}}=T_{\mathrm{L}}(2,2;\mathrm{m})
, and

r_{21}
the ratio of
(2,2)
to
(1,1)
intensities,
r_{21}=T_{2\mathrm{m}}/T_{1\mathrm{m}}
.

Let us call
r_{11}
the intensity ratio of main to satellite
(1,1)

r_{11}=T_{1\mathrm{m}}/T_{1\mathrm{s}}
.
Equation
3

(1,1)

1<r_{11}<\frac{R_{11\mathrm{m}}}{R_{11\mathrm{s}}}=3.6.

(18)

For

we have
\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}\to+\infty
,
while for
r_{11}=3.6
, the optical depth is zero.
If
r_{11}
is less than 1, no value of the optical depth
\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}
can be derived, and if it is greater than 3.6, Eq.
3

The derivation of the rotational temperature (Eq.
11
) requires that

\tau_{2\mathrm{m}}<a\,\tau_{1\mathrm{m}},

(19)

where
a=(A_{22}\,R_{22\mathrm{m}}\,g_{22})/(A_{11}\,R_{11\mathrm{m}}\,g_{11})=3.544
.
The relation between

,
\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}
, and
\tau_{2\mathrm{m}}

4

\frac{1-\exp(-\tau_{1\mathrm{m}})}{1-\exp(-\tau_{2\mathrm{m}})}\,r_{21}=1.

(20)

By substituting Eq.

and
\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}
,

r_{\tau}=\frac{1-\exp(-\tau_{1\mathrm{m}})}{1-\exp(-a\,\tau_{1\mathrm{m}})}\,r_{21}<1.

(21)

The values of
r_{\tau}
for the limiting values of
\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}
are

r_{\tau}\to r_{21}/a

, and

In order to fulfill Eq.

and
(1,1)

The intensity ratio must be
r_{21}<a=3.544

If

(2,2)

(1,1)

r_{21}>1
,
\tau_{1\mathrm{m}}
must be below a maximum value

\frac{1-\exp(-\tau_{\mathrm{max}})}{1-\exp(-a\tau_{\mathrm{max}})}\,r_{21}=1.

(22)

For

, while for

, we have

(see Fig.

4
Spherical envelope model

Let us assume a spherically symmetric envelope, with a density that at large radii is a power-law of radius with index
-p
,

n(r)=n_{0}\,(r/r_{0})^{-p},

(23)

but that may have a flat inner part, the so-called Plummer-like density, with a characteristic radius
r_{c}
,

n(r)=n_{c}\,\left[1+(r/r_{c})^{2}\right]^{-p/2}.

(24)

The central density, at
r=0
, is
n_{c}
.
For small radii,
r\ll r_{c}
, the density is flat,
n\simeq n_{c}
, while for large radii,
r\gg r_{c}
, the density follows a power law,
n\simeq n_{c}(r/r_{c})^{-p}
.
We can consider the Plummer-like density as a generalization of the power-law density.
This can be seen by writing the Plummer-like function as

n(r)=n_{0}\left[(r_{c}/r_{0})^{2}+(r/r_{0})^{2}\right]^{-p/2}.

(25)

This expression of the Plummer-like function is useful because for
r_{c}=0
we obtain directly a power-law function, with the reference radius
r_{0}
and the density parameter
n_{0}
well defined, while on the contrary, the central density
n_{c}\to\infty
.
In the small and large radii limits we obtain

\displaystyle(r\ll r_{c})

\displaystyle n(r)\simeq n_{0}\,(r_{c}/r_{0})^{-p},

\displaystyle(r\gg r_{c})

\displaystyle n(r)\simeq n_{0}\,(r/r_{0})^{-p}.

(26)

Thus, the central density
n_{c}
and
n_{0}
are related through

n_{c}=n_{0}\,(r_{c}/r_{0})^{-p}.

(27)

The density parameter
n_{0}
is not an actual density.
It can be interpreted as the density at the reference radius
r_{0}
of the extrapolation of the Plummer-like function for
r\gg r_{c}
(see Fig.
4
).
In the case
r_{c}>r_{0}
,
n_{0}
is even higher than the maximum density of the envelope,
n_{c}
.

We consider that the Plummer-like law for the density is valid up to the envelope radius, taken as the radius for which the density is a given minimum value,
n_{\mathrm{min}}
, the ambient gas density.

The red and blue dots indicate the actual density of the envelope at
r_{0}
,
n(r_{0})
, while the black dot indicates the density parameter
n_{0}
(see text).

Regarding the temperature of the envelope, we consider a a power law of the radius, with a power-law index
-q
,

T(r)=T_{0}(r/r_{0})^{-q},\\

(28)

where
T_{0}
is the temperature at the reference radius
r_{0}
.
The gas temperature has also a minimum value,
T_{\mathrm{min}}
, which corresponds to a radius such that for larger radii the envelope temperature is taken constant, equal to
T_{\mathrm{min}}
.
The temperature power-law index for a centrally heated envelope, with the dust and the gas well coupled, depends on the dust opacity index
\beta
,
q=2/(4+\beta)
.
For the usual values of
\beta
,
0<\beta<4
, the temperature power-law index results in
0.25<q<0.5
.

In the following we will derive the homogeneous temperature,
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
, for a spherical envelope as a function of the projected radius,
b
.
Two different approximations will be considered:

(i)

Rough approximation, optically thin case, power-law density.

(ii)

Radiative transfer calculation, with an excitation temperature given by the two-levels model, Plummer-like density.

5
Optically thin case, power-law density

Figure 5:

Geometry of a spherically-symmetric envelope.

Let us assume that the emission is optically thin, that the Rayleigh-Jeans approximation is valid, that
T_{\mathrm{ex}}\gg T_{\mathrm{bg}}
, and that
T_{\mathrm{rot}}\simeq T_{\mathrm{k}}
.
In this case, the intensities of the
(1,1)
and
(2,2)
main lines are given by

T_{\mathrm{L}}(j,k;\mathrm{m})=T_{\mathrm{ex}}\,\tau(j,k;\mathrm{m}).

(29)

Equation
5
giving the column density as a function of the optical depth, in the optically thin case, can be simplified to

N_{jk}=\sqrt{\frac{\pi}{4\ln 2}}\,\frac{16\pi k\nu_{jk}^{2}}{hc^{3}A_{jk}R_{jk\mathrm{m}}}\,T_{\mathrm{ex}}\,\tau(j,k;\mathrm{m})\,\Delta v,

(30)

so that, in the optically thin case, the column densities are proportional to the line intensities, and their ratio can be obtained directly from the ratio of line intensities,

\frac{N_{11}}{N_{22}}=\frac{A_{22}R_{22\mathrm{m}}}{A_{11}R_{11\mathrm{m}}}\frac{T_{\mathrm{L}}(1,1;\mathrm{m})}{T_{\mathrm{L}}(2,2;\mathrm{m})}.

(31)

Thus, the homogeneous temperature can be simply derived from the ratio of intensities of the
(1,1)
and
(2,2)
lines. From Eqs.
31
and
9
, we have

\left[\frac{T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}}{\mathrm{K}}\right]=\frac{40.99}{\displaystyle\ln\left(3.544\frac{T(1,1;\mathrm{m})}{T(2,2;\mathrm{m})}\right)}.

(32)

Let us estimate, for a given projected radius
b
, the column densities
N_{11}
and
N_{22}
,

N_{jk}(b)=\int_{\mathrm{line~of~sight}}n_{jk}(r)\,dz,

(33)

where
dz
is the elementary length along the line of sight, and
r=\sqrt{b^{2}+z^{2}}
(see Fig.
5
).
The function that is integrated, the number density
n_{jk}(r)
, depends on
the fractional abundance
f_{jk}
, which depends on the envelope temperature (Eq.
14
), and the envelope density,
n(r)
,

n_{jk}(r)=f_{jk}\left(T(r)\right)\,X\,n(r).

(34)

If we assume that the density is given by a simple power law, the maximum of
n_{jk}
occurs at the minimum value of
r
,
r_{\mathrm{min}}=b
, with a value
n_{jk}(b)
, and we can roughly approximate the integral along the line of sight by

\int_{\mathrm{line~of~sight}}\!\!\!\!n_{jk}(r)\,dz\simeq n_{jk}(b)\,2b.

(35)

Thus, with this approximation, the column density ratio for a projected radius
b
equals the fractional density radio for
r=b
,

\frac{N_{11}(b)}{N_{22}(b)}=\frac{n_{11}(r=b)}{n_{22}(r=b)}.

(36)

Since the envelope temperature gives the fractional density ratio
f_{11}/f_{22}
(Eq.
15
), and the same expression for the column density ratio gives the homogeneous temperature (Eq.
9
), both temperatures coincide.

In conclusion, in the optically thin case and with the rough approximation made for the integral along the line of sight, and for a power-law density, the homogeneous temperature coincides with the envelope temperature,

T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}(b)=T(b)=T_{0}\,(b/r_{0})^{-q}.

(37)

However, the NH
3

(1,1)
emission is in many cases partially thick, and the optically thin approximation does not hold.
In addition, even for a power-law density, the maximum NH
3

(1,1)
volume density
n_{11}
is not necessarily found at the minimum distance of the line of sight to the envelope center, because for temperatures greater than
\sim 20
K, the density
n_{11}
decreases with temperature (Fig.
2
), and the approximation used to estimate the column density does not hold.

Up to now we have not considered the effect of beam smoothing on the observed profiles.
Beam smoothing will produce a flattening of the observed radial profiles at the center of the envelope, in a region with a size of the order of the beam size.

In order to model the effect of a finite beam size, the radial profiles of the line intensities

,
T_{\mathrm{L}}(1,1;\mathrm{s})
, and
T_{\mathrm{L}}(2,2;\mathrm{m})
were converted to 2D-maps.
The maps were then 2D-convolved with a Gaussian beam pattern, and the resulting maps were ring-averaged to obtain the beam-smoothed radial profiles of the line intensities.
From these, the homogeneous analysis was performed to obtain the radial profile of homogeneous temperature
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
.

8
Discussion and Conclusions

The spherical envelope model, using a radiative transfer calculation of the intensities of the NH
3

(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,
(1,1;\mathrm{s})
, and
(2,2;\mathrm{m})
lines, allowed us to compare the kinetic temperature derived from the standard NH
3
analysis, the homogeneous temperature
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
, with the actual temperature of the envelope.

Tests were performed with different characteristics of the envelope, similar to the typical values derived for massive dense cores and clumps.
In many cases we found that there is a critical value of the projected radius below which no value of
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
could be calculated, when the lines become optically thick enough.
Near the critical value, the homogeneous temperature can be much higher than the envelope temperature.
For larger projected radii, far from the critical value, the homogeneous temperature found from the standard NH
3
analysis was similar to the envelope temperature, but
systematically underestimates the actual temperature
T_{0}
and temperature power-law index
q
.
In the case of a power-law density,
n\propto r^{-p}
,
T_{0}
is underestimated by
\sim 20
%, and
q
by
\sim 0.05
.
However, for a density with a central flat region, that is, a Plummer-like density,
n\propto(r_{c}^{2}+r^{2})^{-p/2}
, the discrepancy between the homogeneous temperature and the actual envelope temperature can be much higher, up to
\sim 40
% in
T_{0}
and
\sim 0.15
in
q
, for
r_{c}\simeq 10
kau.

We applied this study to the infrared dark cloud G14.2.
We used the NH
3

(J,K)=(1,1)
and
(2,2)
data obtained with the Very Large Array and the Effelsberg radio telescope for Hub N and Hub S.
The data were analyzed using the homogeneous analysis, that is, standard NH
3
analysis with the assumption of homogeneity along the line of sight, providing a radial profile of homogeneous temperature,
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
.
The radial profiles were fitted by the convolution of a power law
T_{\mathrm{h0}}(r/r_{0})^{-q_{\mathrm{h}}}
with a Gaussian beam of
7\aas@@fstack{\prime\prime}56
.
The homogeneous temperatures obtained from the G14.2 data were compared with the dust temperatures of
Busquet et al. 2016
.
The lack of dust-gas coupling in the lower-density parts of the hubs could contribute to the observed discrepancies (
\sim 40
% in the temperature
T_{0}
and
\sim 0.1
in the temperature power-law index
q
), but the discrepancies are in accordance with the expected results for envelopes with a central flat region.

Several conclusions can be drawn from this work.
Regarding the homogeneous analysis of NH
3

(1,1)
and
(2,2)
radial profiles, two results are relevant.
Firstly, for small projected radii, where the optical depth of the lines is high, the homogeneous temperature can be much higher then the actual envelope temperature.
Secondly, for larger projected radii, the temperature values and power-law indices derived from the homogeneous analysis could be seriously underestimated, depending on the density profile of the envelope.
For power-law density profiles, with a very high density at small radii,
characteristic of very young, Class 0 objects, the temperature can be underestimated by
\sim 20
%, and the power-law index by
\sim 0.05
.
However, for density profiles with a flat central region,
characteristic of more evolved, Class I and beyond objects, the effect is more severe.
The temperature can be underestimated by as much as
\sim 40
%, and the power-law index by
\sim 0.15
.

A similar study for other commonly used dense gas tracers such as H
2
CO and CH
3
OCHO should be carried out to properly assess the validity of the temperature radial profiles inferred from dense gas thermometers.

No new data were analysed in support of this research.
The data used were already published in

.

Appendix A
Radiative transfer calculation

In the following we will derive the intensities of the NH
3

(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,
(1,1;\mathrm{s})
, and
(2,2;\mathrm{m})
lines for a spherical envelope by integration of the radiative transfer equation, and calculate the homogeneous optical depths
\tau_{\mathrm{hom}}(1,1;\mathrm{s})
and

\tau_{\mathrm{hom}}(2,2;\mathrm{s})
, and the homogeneous temperature
T_{\mathrm{k}}^{\mathrm{hom}}
derived from the homogeneous analysis.

A.1
The radiative transfer equation

The radiative transfer equation is evaluated for every projected radius
b
.
Let us consider the slab of geometrical width
\Delta z
, and optical depth
\Delta\tau
, located at position
z
along the line of sight (see Fig.
5
), with
z^{2}+b^{2}=r^{2}
.
For this elementary slab with optical depth
\Delta\tau_{\nu}
,
the incoming intensity at
z
is attenuated a factor
\exp(-\Delta\tau_{\nu})
, and is increased by the average source function of the slab, times
[1-\exp(-\Delta\tau)]
,

I_{\nu}(z+\Delta z)=I_{\nu}(z)\exp(-\Delta\tau)+S_{\nu}(\bar{z})[1-\exp(-\Delta\tau)],

(39)

where
\bar{z}
can be approximated by the center of the slab,
\bar{z}=z+\Delta z/2
.
By subtracting
I_{\nu}(z)
on both sides of the equation and rearranging terms the equation can be written as

\Delta I_{\nu}=[S_{\nu}(\bar{z})-I_{\nu}(z)][1-\exp(-\Delta\tau)].

(40)

The source function
S_{\nu}
is defined by the excitation temperature
T_{\mathrm{ex}}

S_{\nu}=\frac{2h\nu^{3}}{c^{2}}\,\frac{1}{\exp(h\nu/kT_{\mathrm{ex}})-1}=\frac{2k\nu^{2}}{c^{2}}J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{ex}}),

(41)

Equation
40
can be written in terms of the brightness temperature
T_{\mathrm{b}}
(giving the intensity) and the excitation temperature
T_{\mathrm{ex}}
(giving the source function),

\Delta J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}})=[J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{ex}})(\bar{z})-J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}})(z)][1-\exp(-\Delta\tau)].

(42)

A.2
Excitation temperature
T_{\mathrm{ex}}

Assuming that the excitation temperature of the
(1,1)
transition is given by the two-levels model,
T_{\mathrm{ex}}
can be expressed as a weighted average of the kinetic and brightness background temperatures,
T_{\mathrm{k}}
and
T_{\mathrm{bg}}
,

J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{ex}})=\frac{J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{k}})+yJ_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{bg}})}{1+y},

(43)

where the weight factor
y
is

y=\frac{A_{11}}{n\gamma_{11}}\,\frac{1}{1-e^{-h\nu/kT_{\mathrm{k}}}},

(44)

A_{11}
is the spontaneous emission coefficient,

n
is the hydrogen density,
n(\mathrm{H}_{2})
, and

\gamma_{11}
is the de-excitation collisional coefficient,

\left[\frac{\gamma_{11}}{\mathrm{s^{-1}cm^{3}}}\right]=2.27\times 10^{-11}\,\left[\frac{T_{\mathrm{k}}}{\mathrm{K}}\right]^{1/2}.

(45)

Thus, for the slab of material between
z
and
z+dz
we consider
T_{\mathrm{k}}=T(r)
, and a background temperature given by the brightness temperature of the incident radiation from the preceding slab,
T_{\mathrm{bg}}=T_{\mathrm{b}}(z)
.
Then, the excitation temperature of the
(1,1)
transition for this slab is obtained from Eq.
43
.
For the
(2,2)
transition we take the same excitation temperature.

A.3
Optical depth
\tau

Once the excitation temperature is known, we can estimate the elementary optical depths of the
(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,
(1,1;\mathrm{s})
, and
(2,2;\mathrm{m})
lines for the slab,

\Delta\tau(j,k;l)=\sqrt{\frac{4\ln 2}{\pi}}\frac{c^{3}A_{jk}R_{jkl}}{8\pi\nu_{jk}^{3}\Delta v}\,\frac{\exp{(h\nu_{jk}/kT_{\mathrm{ex}})}-1}{\exp{(h\nu_{jk}/kT_{\mathrm{ex}})}+1}\,n_{jk}\,\Delta z,

(46)

with the volume densities
n_{11}
and
n_{22}
given by

\displaystyle n_{11}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!f_{11}(T(r))\,X\,n(r)

(47)

\displaystyle n_{22}\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!f_{22}(T(r))\,X\,n(r)

(48)

where the fractional abundances
f_{11}
and
f_{22}
are given by Eqs.
14
and
15
, with the rotational temperature calculated from the kinetic temperature (Eq.
12
).

A.4
Iterative scheme

For any given projected radius
b
, the integral along the line of sight has to be evaluated from
-z_{\mathrm{max}}
to
z_{\mathrm{max}}
, where
z_{\mathrm{max}}=\sqrt{r_{\mathrm{env}}^{2}-b^{2}}
.

The initial conditions at
z=-z_{\mathrm{max}}
are

\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})=\tau(1,1;\mathrm{s})=\tau(2,2;\mathrm{m})=0
,

T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{m})=T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{s})=T_{\mathrm{b}}(2,2;\mathrm{m})=T_{\mathrm{bg}}=2.752
K.

For a given
z
, we have determined from the preceding step the values at
z
of
the optical depths
\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,
\tau(1,1;\mathrm{s})
,
\tau(2,2;\mathrm{m})
, and
the brightness temperatures of the lines,
T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,
T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{s})
,
T_{\mathrm{b}}(2,2;\mathrm{m})
.

For this step, from
z
to
z+\Delta z
, we take

\bar{z}=z+\Delta z/2

(
Abramowitz &
Stegun 1972
, this is equivalent to the second order Runge-Kutta method,)
,

r=\sqrt{b^{2}+\bar{z}^{2}}
,
Eq.
43
is used with
T_{\mathrm{k}}=T(r)
,
T_{\mathrm{bg}}=T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{m})
, and
n=n(r)
to calculate
T_{\mathrm{ex}}
.
The densities
n_{11}
and
n_{22}
are calculated,
n_{jk}=f_{jk}\,X\,n(r)
, and Eq.
46
is used to estimate

\Delta\tau(1,1;\mathrm{m})
,
\Delta\tau(1,1;\mathrm{s})
, and
\Delta\tau(22;\mathrm{m})
.
Finally, Eq.
42
is used to calculate

\Delta J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{m}))
,
\Delta J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{s}))
, and
\Delta J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}}(2,2;\mathrm{m}))
.

An example of the integration along the line of sight is shown in Fig.
15
, for a power-law density with three different values of the density. For

cm

T_{\mathrm{L}}(1,1;\mathrm{m})=J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{m}))-J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{bg}})
,

T_{\mathrm{L}}(1,1;\mathrm{s})=J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{b}}(1,1;\mathrm{s}))-J_{\nu}(T_{\mathrm{bg}})

The Line Modelling Engine (LIME)
(
Brinch &
Hogerheijde 2010
)
was used to compare the spherical envelope model with a full radiative transfer calculation.
LIME assumes that the gas and dust temperatures are the same.
Unfortunately, the collision coefficients of the quadrupole-hyperfine transitions of NH
3
are not available in the literature.

LIME was run using the molecular data for para-NH
3
from the Leiden Atomic and Molecular Database (LAMDA)
(
Schöier et al. 2005
)
, which contains energy levels, transition frequencies, Einstein coefficients of the inversion transitions, and the collision rates with para-H
2
.
The output of LIME provided us the radial profiles of the intensities and optical depths of the inversion transitions NH
3

(1,1)
and
(2,2)
,
T_{\mathrm{L}}(j,k)
and
\tau(j,k)
.
The intensity of the main and inner satellite quadrupole-hyperfine lines was then estimated as

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{L}}(j,k;\mathrm{s})\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!T_{\mathrm{L}}(j,k)\,\frac{1-\exp[-R_{jks}\,\tau(j,k)]}{1-\exp[-\tau(j,k)]},

(49)

\displaystyle T_{\mathrm{L}}(j,k;\mathrm{m})\!\!\!

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\!\!\!T_{\mathrm{L}}(j,k)\,\frac{1-\exp[-R_{jkm}\,\tau(j,k)]}{1-\exp[-\tau(j,k)]}.

From these intensities, the standard NH
3
analysis was performed to obtain the homogeneous temperature radial profile.

The LIME calculation assumes that the five quadrupole-hyperfine lines of each inversion transition are completely blended, with an optical depth that is the total optical depth of the inversion transitions.
So, the inversion optical depths calculated by LIME are higher than the actual optical depths of the hyperfine lines.
As a consequence, for partially thick emission, the region contributing to the LIME intensity is closer to the observer (that is, farther from the envelope center, and so cooler and less dense) than for the hyperfine intensity.
Thus, we expect discrepancies between LIME calculations and the radiative transport calculations of this work, which could be more important in the optically thick regime.
