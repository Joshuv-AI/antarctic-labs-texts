Abstract
. A countable structure is indivisible if for every coloring with finite range there is a monochromatic isomorphic subcopy of the structure. Each indivisible structure naturally corresponds to an indivisibility problem which outputs such a subcopy given a presentation and coloring. We investigate the Weihrauch complexity of the indivisibility problems for two structures: the rational numbers ℚ \mathbb{Q} as a linear order, and the equivalence relation ℰ \mathscr{E} with countably many equivalence classes each having countably many members. We separate the Weihrauch degrees of both corresponding indivisibility problems from several benchmarks, showing in particular that the indivisibility problem for ℚ \mathbb{Q} cannot solve the problem of finding a monochromatic rational interval given a coloring for which there is one; and that the Weihrauch degree of the indivisibility problem for ℰ \mathscr{E} is strictly between those of 𝖱𝖳 2 \mathsf{RT}^{2} and 𝖲𝖱𝖳 2 \mathsf{SRT}^{2} , two widely studied variants of Ramsey’s theorem for pairs whose reverse-mathematical separation was open until recently.

1.
Introduction

Reverse mathematics is a branch of logic which seeks to classify theorems based
on their logical strength. This is often done by working over a relatively
weak base system for mathematics, such as a fragment of second-order arithmetic,
and then looking at the truth of various implications in models of that fragment.
It turns out that one can frequently obtain more fine-grained distinctions
between theorems by comparing their intrinsic computational or combinatorial
power, rather than being limited to considerations of provability only. For
theorems expressible as
\Pi^{1}_{2}
statements of second-order arithmetic, this
can be achieved by identifying a theorem with an instance-solution pair, where
instances are all sets
X
to which the hypothesis of the theorem apply, and
solutions to
X
are all sets
Y
witnessing the truth of the conclusion of the
theorem for
X
.
One then introduces various reducibilities to compare, among different
instance-solution pairs, the difficulty of finding a solution from a given
instance.

Instance-solution pairs can be formalized as problems:

{defi}

A
problem
is a partial multivalued function on Baire space,
P\colon\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}\rightrightarrows\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}
. Any
x\in\dom P
is called an

instance
of
P
, and any
y\in P(x)
is a
solution
to
x
with
respect to
P
.

In the present work, we focus on problems arising from indivisible structures,
and use mainly Weihrauch reducibility to gauge their uniform computational content.
We assume familiarity with computability theory and the basic terminology of
computable structure theory, as in the introductory sections of
[
Mon21
]
.
All our structures are countable and we will take them to have domain
\mathbb{N}

{defi}

Indivisibility belongs properly to Ramsey theory, the study of combinatorial
structures which cannot be “too disordered” in that any large subset must
contain a highly organized substructure. This field goes back to 1930, when
Ramsey’s theorem first appeared, and principles arising in Ramsey theory have
long been objects of interest in reverse mathematics.

Before continuing to the next section, we pause to make some observations about
p.c.e.t. problems. Pointwise c.e. traceability can be viewed as a
generalization of c.e. traceability, which is the special case where
P
is a
single-valued function from
\mathbb{N}
to
\mathbb{N}
(see for instance
[
Soa16
, §11.4]

for the definition). It is also related to the notion of a pointwise finite
problem, defined in
[
GPV21
]
, which is a problem such that every instance
has finitely many solutions. However, a p.c.e.t. problem is not necessarily
pointwise finite, one counterexample being
\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
. And the existence of functions

\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
which are not c.e. traceable shows that even first-order pointwise
finite problems may not be p.c.e.t.

The class of p.c.e.t. problems has some attractive algebraic properties:

•

If
P
and
Q
are p.c.e.t., then so are
P\times Q
and
P^{\ast}
.

•

If
P
is p.c.e.t. and
Q\leq_{\mathrm{W}}P
, then
Q
is p.c.e.t.

(These properties hold for problems
P
and
Q
on any represented spaces, as is
straightforward to show.) On the other hand,

•

P\ast Q
may not be p.c.e.t. even if both
P
and
Q
are. For
example,
\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
and
\mathsf{LPO}^{\prime}
are both p.c.e.t. but
\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}\ast\mathsf{LPO}^{\prime}\geq_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{N}}\geq_{\mathrm{W}}\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{2}}

[
BG21
, Corollary 8.10]
.

•

Neither
P^{u\ast}
nor
P^{\diamond}
need be p.c.e.t. if
P
is. It
follows from
[
SV23
, Theorem 7.2]
that
(\mathsf{LPO}^{\prime})^{u\ast}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}(\mathsf{LPO}^{\prime})^{\diamond}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}^{\prime}
, and

\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}^{\prime}>_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{N}}

[
BG21
, Corollary 8.14]
. (See
[
SV23
]
for definitions of the
undefined notation used here.)

Just as the fact that
\mathsf{LPO}^{\prime}|_{\mathrm{W}}\lim

(which is known in the literature, see e.g.
[
BG21
]
) shows that
pointwise c.e. traceability is logically incomparable with limit computability,
the fact that
\mathsf{LPO^{\prime}}
is p.c.e.t. shows the concept to be strictly more general
than computability with finitely many mind changes: the latter can be
characterized by Weihrauch reducibility to
\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
, which is p.c.e.t. and known
to be Weihrauch incomparable with

The study of p.c.e.t. problems has been continued by Pauly

]

\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
and lowercase Greek letters for their initial
segments.

We will find (noncomputably) a finite sequence of strings
\sigma_{0}\subset\sigma_{1}\subset\cdots
such that for some
s
, there is an

\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
-instance
c\in[\sigma_{s}]
which defeats the Weihrauch
reduction. To accomplish this, we exploit the requirement for
\Delta
to
produce a stable coloring in order to restrict our attention to sets of
columns
H
which are guaranteed to extend to an
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
-solution as long
as there is any such solution of the same color as
[H]^{2}
, no matter how we
continue to extend
\sigma_{s}
.
Then we use these sets as oracles to diagonalize against
\Delta
and
\Psi

by adding finitely many locks on the columns of
E
(to be explained below),
represented by a lock function
L=\bigcup_{s}L_{s}\colon\subseteq\mathbb{N}\to 2

that will be updated finitely many times. These locks are in fact the same as
those used in Cohen forcing with locks, but we do not formally use a notion of
forcing here and the argument does not necessarily produce a generic
c
.
After we lock a column, either
\Psi
will be forced to output a
non-monochromatic set, or
\Delta
will be forced to prevent
H
from being an
initial segment of any solution—and so will prevent there from being

any
solution of
H
’s color. This can be carried out for every color
used by
\Delta
, so eventually the diagonalization will succeed.

For any string
\sigma
and lock function
L
, let
[\sigma,L]
be the set of
(finite or infinite)
\tau\in[\sigma]
such that whenever
L(x)\in\{0,1\}

is defined, if
(x,y)
is newly colored by
\tau
, then

\tau(x,y)=L(x)
. “Newly colored” simply means that
\tau
is
defined on
(x,y)
while
\sigma
is not. So
[\sigma,L]
is the set
of extensions of
\sigma
which respect the lock function
L
by only adding
points of color
L(x)
in column
x
. Observe that
[\sigma,L^{\prime}]\subset[\sigma,L]
if
L^{\prime}\supset L
.

The central claim that makes this proof work is as follows: for any
\sigma
,
any
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
-column
n
, and any lock function
L
,

\displaystyle\exists\tau\in[\sigma,L]~\exists i\in\mathbb{N}~\forall\rho\in[\tau,L]~(\text{if $\Delta^{\rho}$ newly colors }

\{n,m\}
where
m>n
,

\displaystyle\text{then $\Delta^{\rho}\{n,m\}=i$}).

That is, we can pass to an extension of
\sigma
such that among all further
extensions respecting the lock function,
\Delta
will never change its mind
again about the stable color of column
n
, and in fact will never again add a
point of any other color to it.
We say
\tau

L
-forces column
n
to be color
i
when this happens. If the
claim were false for some
\sigma
,
n
, and
L
, then for every
\tau\in[\sigma,L]
and
i\in k
, there would be a further
\rho(\tau)\in[\tau,L]

such that
\Delta^{\rho(\tau)}
adds a point not of color
i
into column
n
.
Letting
\tau_{0}=\sigma
and
\tau_{s+1}=\rho(\tau_{s})
for all
s
, the

\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
-instance
d=\lim_{s}\tau_{s}
is such that
\Delta^{d}
has
infinitely many points of at least two different colors in column
n
, so is
not an
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
-instance, a contradiction. A key point is that this argument
works for any
L
independently of
\sigma
, so that even if we change
L

partway through the proof we can always extend any
\sigma_{s}
to a

\sigma_{s+1}
which
L
-forces a fresh column to be some color. A column
already
L
-forced remains
L^{\prime}
-forced to be the same color for any
L^{\prime}\supset L
.

Now we describe the procedure to find
(\sigma_{s})
and
(L_{s})
in detail.
Let
D\subseteq k
be the set of “already-diagonalized” colors, starting
with
D=\emptyset
; we will use
D
to keep track of which colors have
already witnessed convergence of
\Psi
and triggered an update of the lock
function. As long as
D\subsetneq k
,
the colors in
D
at stage
s
will be exactly those of which no

\Delta^{c}
-solution can be found for any
c\in[\sigma_{s},L_{s}]
.
Let
L_{0}=\emptyset
and let
\sigma_{0}
be a string
L_{0}
-forcing column
0
to
be some color. At stage
s>0
, let
\sigma_{s}\in[\sigma_{s-1},L_{s-1}]
be
either a string which
L_{s-1}
-forces column
s
to be some color if it had
not yet been so forced, or an arbitrary string otherwise.
For each
i\notin D
, search for a pair
(x,y)
already colored by
\sigma_{s}
with

x\notin\dom L_{s-1}
(i.e., with column
x
not already locked), together
with a finite set
H
such that
[H]^{2}
is
\Delta^{\sigma_{s}}
-monochromatic
with color
i
, such that
\sigma_{s}

L_{s-1}
-forces every column in
H
to
be color
i
, and such that
\Psi^{\sigma_{s}\oplus H}(x,y)\mkern-5.0mu\downarrow=1
.
Such
x
,
y
, and
H
must eventually be found for some
i
and
s
if

\Psi
is to compute an
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
-solution, and we can take
x\notin\dom L_{s-1}
because
\Psi
will output elements of infinitely many columns
of
E
whereas
\dom L_{s-1}
is finite.
That we can also take
i\notin D
is justified below.
If
x
and
y
are found at stage
s
, diagonalize by setting
L_{s}(x)=1-\sigma_{s}(x,y)
; that is, we lock column
x
to be the opposite color that

\Psi
already committed to there. End stage
s
by putting
i\in D
. If
instead no such
x
,
y
, or
H
are found for any
i
, let
L_{s}=L_{s-1}

and end the stage.

If
H
is as in the previous paragraph, then
H
is a valid initial segment of
some solution to
\Delta^{d}
for
any

d\in[\sigma_{s},L_{s-1}]
for which
infinitely many
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
-columns stabilize to
i
, and in particular for any
such
d\in[\sigma_{s},L_{s}]
. This is because starting from any

\Delta^{d}
-solution
G
, one can truncate
G
to
\hat{G}
by selecting only
the columns whose indices are higher than the point at which any of the
columns in
H
stabilize, producing an infinite
\hat{G}
with
[H\cup\hat{G}]^{2}
monochromatic. Hence either
H
extends to a solution of color
i

(for some
d
as above), or there is no solution of color
i
(for any
d
as
above). In the first case,
d
would witness the failure of the Weihrauch
reduction, because if
H
extends to a
\Delta^{d}
-solution
K
, then

\Psi^{d\oplus K}
outputs a point in

E
whose color is shared by only finitely many other points in the same
column. If such a
d
exists then the procedure ends at this stage. In
particular, this must happen if
D=k
, because then some
H
,
x
, and
y

were already found for every
i\in k
, and for any
d\in[\sigma_{s},L_{s}]
, at
least one of these
H
s must extend to a valid solution of
\Delta^{d}
.

Otherwise, for this
i
and indeed for all
i\in D
, there is no
d\in[\sigma_{s},L_{s}]
with
\Delta^{d}
having a solution of color
i
. This means
that only finitely many columns of any such
\Delta^{d}
stabilize to a color in

D
, and it must still be possible to
L_{s}
-force infinitely many columns to
be a color not in
D
. Hence we can continue the procedure to extend

\sigma_{s}
and search for a suitable
H
of some color
i\notin D
.
This completes the proof, since the procedure will eventually diagonalize
against every color of which it is still possible for there to be an

\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
-solution.

6.
Further directions

Solutions of
\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
are rather nebulous: for example, if
x
and
y
are two
elements of a solution
H
, one can delete the whole interval
[x,y]
from
H

and still obtain a solution. The seeming weakness of
\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
may be a
consequence of this nebulosity, and it is unclear how much power
it derives from the fact that it outputs a second-order object, i.e., a
set of rationals.
An investigation of these properties can be put on precise footing with the
following notions introduced in
[
DSY24
]
and
[
GPV21
]
, respectively:

•

The
first-order part
of a problem
P
,
{}^{1}P
, is the strongest
first-order problem Weihrauch reducible to
P
.

•

The
deterministic part
of
P
,
\Det P
, is the strongest
problem Weihrauch reducible to
P
for which every instance has a unique
solution.

{qu}

How strong exactly are
{}^{1}\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
and
\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}})
?

The first-order part of
\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
has been studied in recent work by Dzhafarov,
Solomon, and Valenti on the tree pigeonhole principle
\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{+}

[
DSV25
]
, which as mentioned earlier is Weihrauch equivalent to
\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
.
They showed that none of the problems
\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{k}
,
\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{+}
, or

\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{\mathbb{N}}
are Weihrauch equivalent to any first-order problem, and
indeed
{}^{1}\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{\mathbb{N}}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{\mathbb{N}}
. But
{}^{1}\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{k}

turns out to be strictly stronger than
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
for all
k\geq 2
; whether
{}^{1}\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{k}
and
{}^{1}\mathsf{TT}^{1}_{+}
have precise characterizations in
terms of known Weihrauch degrees is open. Regarding
\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}})
, Manlio
Valenti has pointed out to the author that since
\Det(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k})\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\lim_{k}
and

\Det(\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{N}}^{\ast})\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\lim_{\mathbb{N}}
, where
\lim_{k}
maps an
eventually constant element of
k^{\mathbb{N}}
to its limit and similarly for
\lim_{\mathbb{N}}
,
we must have
\lim_{k}<_{\mathrm{W}}\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}})<_{\mathrm{W}}\lim_{\mathbb{N}}
for all
k
. (The second
reduction is strict by ?THM?
.) Precise characterizations of the
degrees of
\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{k}})
and of
\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}})
have not been
established. On the other hand,
\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\mathbb{N}}})\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
since
\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
and, using
[
GPV21
, Theorem 3.9]
,
\Det(\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{N}}^{\ast}\ast\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}})\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\Det(\mathsf{TC}_{\mathbb{N}}^{\ast})\ast\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}\ast\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\mathsf{C}_{\mathbb{N}}
.

One consequence of the above facts is that both the first-order and the
deterministic parts of
\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
are p.c.e.t., and so one cannot replace

\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{2}}
with
{}^{1}\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{2}}
or
\Det(\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}_{{2}})
in ?THM?
.

Turning to
\mathscr{E}
, ?THM?
is intriguing given the great interest in

\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
in reverse mathematics since the 1970s.
Cholak, Jockusch, and Slaman in
[
CJS01
]
proved that
\mathsf{RT}^{2}_{2}
can be
logically decomposed into the conjunction of
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{2}
and
\mathsf{COH}
,
where
\mathsf{COH}
is the so-called cohesive principle which states that for any
sequence
(R_{i})
of subsets of
\mathbb{N}
, there is a set which for each
i
is up to
finite error a subset of either
R_{i}
or its complement.
This work sparked a major investigation into the logical separation of

\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{2}
from
\mathsf{COH}
which was completed a few years ago by Monin and Patey

[
MP21
]
, who showed that
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{2}
does not imply
\mathsf{COH}
even in

\omega
-models of
\mathsf{RCA}_{0}
, the converse nonimplication having been
established earlier in
[
HJKH+08
]
.
One is led to wonder what relationship
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
has to

\mathsf{COH}
: clearly
\mathsf{COH}
cannot imply
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
in
\omega
-models, but
whether
\mathsf{COH}
is reducible in any sense to
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}
is open, as is the
question of the separation between
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{k}}
and
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
under

\leq_{\mathrm{c}}
.
We conjecture that

Conjecture 20
.

\mathsf{COH}\not\leq_{\mathrm{c}}\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{\mathbb{N}}}
and

\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{2}}\not\leq_{\mathrm{c}}\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{\mathbb{N}}
, or at least
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{k}}\not\leq_{\mathrm{c}}\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}
.

Of course, if either statement is false, then the other would immediately
follow.
We briefly mention a way to view
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{k}}
in terms of
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
which
might suggest a route to the resolution of both conjectures.
Several authors have observed that
\mathsf{COH}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\widehat{(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2})^{\mathrm{FE}}}
,
where
P^{\mathrm{FE}}
is the “finite error” version of
P
with
\dom P^{\mathrm{FE}}=\dom P

and
x\in P^{\mathrm{FE}}(p)
iff there is a
y\in P(p)
with
x
and
y
differing only
on a finite set. (See for instance
[
DM22
, Corollary 8.4.15]
.)
The next definition was inspired by the principle
\mathsf{RCOH}
, a
weakening of
\mathsf{COH}
introduced in
[
CDHP20
]
which corresponds to “solving
infinitely many columns” of an
\widehat{(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2})^{\mathrm{FE}}}
-instance.

{defi}

The
weak parallelization
of a problem
P
is the problem

\widetilde{P}
such that
\dom\widetilde{P}=\dom\widehat{P}
, and where
the solutions to the instance
\langle\mkern-0.5mup_{0},p_{1},\dotsc\mkern-0.5mu\rangle
are all sets of the form

\bigcup_{n\in A}\{\,\langle\mkern-0.5mun,x\mkern-0.5mu\rangle\,:\,x\in P(p_{n})\,\},

where
A
is an infinite subset of
\mathbb{N}
.

In other words,
\widetilde{P}
picks infinitely many parallel instances of
P

to solve out of a given instance of
\widehat{P}
. Thus
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{k}}
is an a
priori stronger variant of
\widetilde{\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}}
in which the solutions of the
parallel
\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}
-instances represented in an
\widetilde{\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}}
-solution are
all of the same color. However, by nonuniformly picking a color shared by
infinitely many columns of a solution of
\widetilde{\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}}
, it is not hard
to see that
\Indtext{\mathscr{E}}_{{k}}\equiv_{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde{\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}}
. Moreover,
\mathsf{SRT}^{2}_{k}\equiv_{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde{\mathsf{SRT}^{1}_{k}}
by additionally computing a homogeneous set
using a standard argument as in the proof of ?THM?
. Then
?THM?
can be rephrased in these terms as

Conjecture 21
.

\widehat{(\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2})^{\mathrm{FE}}}\not\leq_{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde{\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{2}}
and

\widetilde{\mathsf{RT}^{1}_{k}}\not\leq_{\mathrm{c}}\widetilde{\mathsf{SRT}^{1}_{k}}
.

Finally, there are of course many indivisible structures other than
\mathbb{Q}
and

\mathscr{E}
which could be investigated along similar lines as in the present work,
such as the Rado graph or nonscattered linear orders.
As a followup to ?THM?
, it would be interesting to obtain a
characterization of the linear orders
\mathcal{L}
such that
\Indtext{\mathcal{L}}\equiv_{\mathrm{W}}\Indtext{\mathbb{Q}}
.
It might also be fruitful to continue the study of general
properties of indivisibility problems initiated in Section
3
.
Arguably one of the most basic questions we leave open is the following:

{qu}

Is it the case that
\Indtext{\mathcal{S}}_{{k+1}}\not\leq_{\mathrm{W}}\Indtext{\mathcal{S}}_{{k}}
for every
indivisible structure
\mathcal{S}
?
