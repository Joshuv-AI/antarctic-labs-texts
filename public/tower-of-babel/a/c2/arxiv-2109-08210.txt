Saturated and linear isometric transfer systems for cyclic groups of order p m ​ q n p^{m}q^{n}
Usman Hafeez, Peter Marcus, Kyle Ormsby, Angélica M. Osorno

Abstract
. Transfer systems are combinatorial objects which classify N ∞ N_{\infty} operads up to homotopy. By results of A. Blumberg and M. Hill [ BH15 ] , every transfer system associated to a linear isometries operad is also saturated (closed under a particular two-out-of-three property). We investigate saturated and linear isometric transfer systems with equivariance group C p m ​ q n C_{p^{m}q^{n}} , the cyclic group of order p m ​ q n p^{m}q^{n} for p , q p,q distinct primes and m , n ≥ 0 m,n\geq 0 . We give a complete enumeration of saturated transfer systems for C p m ​ q n C_{p^{m}q^{n}} . We also prove J. Rubin’s saturation conjecture for C p ​ q n C_{pq^{n}} ; this says that every saturated transfer system is realized by a linear isometries operad for p , q p,q sufficiently large (greater than 3 3 in this case).

2.1.

In order to frame our work on transfer systems, we need to recall Blumberg–Hill’s notion of an

Definition 2.1

A

🖙

for all

🖙

is empty or contractible, and

🖙

for all

\mathscr{O}(n)^{G}
is nonempty.

A

\varphi\colon\mathscr{O}_{1}\to\mathscr{O}_{2}

-spaces. We denote the associated category of

For a map

Note that

denote the graph of a permutation representation of

Definition 2.2
.

A
G
-
N_{\infty}
operad
\mathscr{O}

admits norms
for a finite

-set

when

is nonempty.

Particular examples of

operads include linear isometries operads defined on

-universes
[

]
. We recall the definition.

Definition 2.3
.

A
G
-universe

is a countably infinite-dimensional real

The

2.2.
Transfer systems

A
G
-transfer system is a combinatorial object defined as a particular sub-poset of
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
, the subgroup lattice of
G
. For
H\leq G
and
g\in G
, let
{}^{g}H=gHg^{-1}
denote the
g
-conjugate of
H
.

Definition 2.4
.

A
G
-transfer system
is a relation
\to
on
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
that refines the inclusion relation
2
2

2

This means that
K\to H
implies
K\leq H
.
and satisfies the following properties:

🖙

(reflexivity)
H\to H
for all
H\leq G
,

🖙

(transitivity)
K\to H
and
L\to K
implies
L\to H
,

🖙

(closed under conjugation)
K\to H
implies that
{}^{g}K\to{}^{g}H
for all
g\in G
,

🖙

(closed under restriction)
K\to H
and
M\leq H
implies
(K\cap M)\to M
.

We denote the collection of transfer systems by
\operatorname{Tr}(G)
and view it as a poset under the refinement relation.

In other words, a transfer system is a sub-poset of
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
where the relation is closed under conjugation and restriction. Note that conjugation is trivial when
G
is Abelian; in this case transfer systems only depend on the lattice structure of
\operatorname{Sub}(G)
. Saturated transfer systems have an additional two-out-of-three property:

Definition 2.5
.

A
G
-transfer system
\to
is
saturated
if it additionally satisfies the following property:

🖙

(two-out-of-three) if
L\leq K\leq H\leq G
and two of the three relations
L\to K
,
L\to H
,
K\to H
hold, then so does the third.

We denote the collection of saturated transfer systems by
\operatorname{STr}(G)
.

By transitivity and closure under restriction, the two-out-of-three property may be rephrased as follows:

\text{if }L\leq K\leq H\text{ and }L\to H\text{, then }K\to H.

The link between
N_{\infty}
operads and transfer systems is provided by the following construction. Given a
G
-
N_{\infty}
operad
\mathscr{O}
, define the relation
\to_{\mathscr{O}}
by the rule

K\to_{\mathscr{O}}H\text{ if and only if }K\leq H\text{ and }\mathscr{O}([H:K])^{\Gamma(H/K)}\neq\varnothing

where
\Gamma(H/K)
is the graph of some permutation representation
H\to\mathfrak{S}_{[H:K]}
of
H/K

Theorem 2.6
.

The assignment

\displaystyle N_{\infty}\text{-}\mathbf{Op}^{G}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\operatorname{Tr}(G)

\displaystyle\mathscr{O}

\displaystyle\longmapsto(\to_{\mathscr{O}})

induces an equivalence

\operatorname{Ho}(N_{\infty}\text{-}\mathbf{Op}^{G})\simeq\operatorname{Tr}(G)

The main result of this section is a closed formula for the number of

4.1.
Recursive formula

We first prove a recursive formula for the number
of saturated transfer systems. This

and

in

.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let

. Then

s(m,n+1)=s(m,n)+\sum_{k=0}^{m}{{m+1\choose k}s(k,n)}.

In essence, we build saturated covers on

Proposition 4.4

4.2.
Closed form

The recursion for the number of saturated transfer
systems on
C_{p^{m}q^{n}}
from the previous section allows us to prove a
closed form for
s(m,n)
. We first recall the following definition.

Definition 4.5
.

For
\ell,k\geq 0
, the
Stirling number of the second kind

\begin{Bmatrix}\ell\\
k\end{Bmatrix}
counts the number of partitions of a set with
\ell
elements into
k
non-empty subsets.

Remark 4.6
.

Stirling numbers of the second kind satisfy the recurrence

\begin{Bmatrix}\ell+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}=k\begin{Bmatrix}\ell\\
k\end{Bmatrix}+\begin{Bmatrix}\ell\\
k-1\end{Bmatrix}

for
k>0
with

\begin{Bmatrix}0\\
0\end{Bmatrix}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\begin{Bmatrix}n\\
0\end{Bmatrix}=\begin{Bmatrix}0\\
n\end{Bmatrix}=0

for
n>0
. They are given by the closed formula

\begin{Bmatrix}\ell\\
k\end{Bmatrix}=\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{i=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-i}\binom{k}{i}i^{\ell}.

Theorem 4.7
.

For all
m,n\geq 0
, the sequence
s(m,n)
satisfies

s(m,n)=\sum_{j=2}^{m+2}(-1)^{m-j}\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}\frac{j!}{2}j^{n}.

Proof.

We prove this by induction. We begin by proving
the base case, when
n=0
. It can be seen that the saturated covers on
[m]\times[0]
correspond to
m
-long bitstrings (because each column may have 0 or 1 horizontal edges). Therefore,
s(m,0)=2^{m}
. We need to prove that

s(m,0)=2^{m}=\sum_{j=2}^{m+2}(-1)^{m-j}\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}\frac{j!}{2}.

From
[
Sta12
, (1.94d)]
, we know that

x^{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}{\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}(x-1)_{k-1}},

where
(x)_{k}:=x(x-1)\dots(x-(k-1))
. In particular, when
x=-2
,

(-2)^{m}=\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}(-3)(-4)\dots(-k-1)=\sum_{k=1}^{m+1}(-1)^{k+1}\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
k\end{Bmatrix}\frac{(k+1)!}{2},

from which the base case follows.

For the inductive step we fix
m,n\geq 0
, assume the statement for all
(k,n)
with
k\leq m
, and prove the case for
(m,n+1)
. Using the recursive formula from
Section 4.1
and the inductive hypothesis, and changing the order of summation, we have that

s(m,n+1)=\sum_{j=2}^{m+2}\left[(-1)^{m-j}\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}+\sum_{k=j-2}^{m}(-1)^{k-j}\binom{m+1}{k}\begin{Bmatrix}k+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}\right]\frac{j!}{2}j^{n}.

Thus, to prove the inductive step it suffices to prove that for all
2\leq j\leq m+2
,

\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}\cdot j=\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}+\sum_{k=j-2}^{m}(-1)^{m-k}\binom{m+1}{k}\begin{Bmatrix}k+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}.

This follows directly by applying the combinatorial identity in
Section 4.2
below, with
\ell=m+1
and
r=j-1
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\ell,r\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

0\leq r\leq\ell
. Then,

(\ell-r)\begin{Bmatrix}\ell\\
r\end{Bmatrix}=\sum_{t=1}^{\ell-r}(-1)^{t+1}{\ell\choose t+1}\begin{Bmatrix}\ell-t\\
r\end{Bmatrix}

Proof.

We prove the above identity via a combinatorial proof. To do so, we consider
marked paritions
.
Given two nonnegative integers
\ell
and
r
with
r\leq\ell
a

marked partition
is a pair
(\mathcal{P},q)
consisting
of a partition
\mathcal{P}
of
\{1,\dots,\ell\}
into
r
nonempty subsets
together with a distinguished
q\in\{1,\cdots,\ell\}

such that
q
is not the minimum of its subset.
Note that
the left hand side of the equation enumerates the number of marked partitions for given
\ell
and
r
,
as there are
\begin{Bmatrix}\ell\\
r\end{Bmatrix}
ways to
choose the partition
\mathcal{P}
and
(\ell-r)
possibilities for

q
.

To prove the identity, we count the set of marked partitions using the inclusion-exclusion principle with the following subsets. For
i<j\leq\ell
, we define the subset of the marked partitions

A_{ij}:=\{(\mathcal{P},j)\mid i,j\text{ are in the same subset in }\mathcal{P}\}.

The union of the
A_{ij}
is the set of all marked partitions. For
i<j
and
k<l
,
A_{ij}\cap A_{kl}
is empty unless
j=l
, as the elements marked will be distinct. Note moreover that for any
j\leq\ell
, and distinct
i_{1},\dots,i_{t}<j
,

|A_{i_{1}j}\cap\dots\cap A_{i_{t}j}|=\begin{Bmatrix}\ell-t\\
r\end{Bmatrix}.

Indeed, suppose we have
(\mathcal{P},j)\in A_{i_{1}j}\cap\dots\cap A_{i_{t}j}
. Then
i_{1},\dots,i_{t}
are in the
same subset as
j
in the marked partition. Thus, the number of such partitions is equal to partitioning
the remaining
\ell-t-1
numbers and the subset
\{i_{1},\dots,i_{t},j\}
into
r
parts, which can be done in

\begin{Bmatrix}\ell-t\\
r\end{Bmatrix}
ways. Moreover, there are
{\ell\choose t+1}
nonempty
t
-fold
intersections, because each nonempty
t
-fold intersection is uniquely determined by choice of
t+1
numbers
i_{1},\dots,i_{t},j\leq\ell
. By the principle of inclusion-exclusion the above equation follows.
∎

Remark 4.9
.

Notably, the closed formula for
s(m,n)
in
Section 4.2
is not symmetric in
m
and
n
, although by definition we know that
s(m,n)=s(n,m)
.

The authors conjectured
Section 4.2
via the following process.
We first observed that for small cases of
m
and
n
, we could write

s(m,n)=\sum_{j=2}^{m+2}b_{mj}j^{n},

for some integers
b_{mj}
that were independent of
n
. To prove that that is indeed the case, let
B_{m}(x)
denote the generating function for
s(m,n)
as a sequence in
n
. The recursion in
Section 4.1
implies that

B_{m}(x)=\frac{2^{m}+x\sum_{k=0}^{m-1}\binom{m+1}{k}B_{k}(x)}{1-(m+2)x}.

From this, one may prove that
B_{m}(x)
is a rational function with denominator
\prod_{j=2}^{m+2}(1-jx)
, whence

B_{m}(x)=\sum_{j=2}^{m+2}\frac{b_{mj}}{1-jx}

for some
b_{mj}\in\mathbb{Q}
. It follows that

s(m,n)=\sum_{j=2}^{m+2}b_{mj}j^{n},

but we did not succeed in producing the values of
b_{mj}
via this method. (The residue method for partial fractions would give the answer if we knew the numerator of
B_{m}(x)
.) Instead, we computed the values of
b_{mj}
in a range and guessed that

b_{mj}=(-1)^{m-j}\begin{Bmatrix}m+1\\
j-1\end{Bmatrix}\frac{j!}{2}

via an act of OEIS-enabled perspicacity.

4.3.
Exponential generating function

We now consider the two-variable exponential generating function corresponding to
\{s(m,n)\}
. The content of this section is mostly due to Igor Kriz.

Definition 4.10
.

Let

f(x,y)=\sum_{m,n\geq 0}\frac{s(m,n)}{m!n!}x^{m}y^{n}

be the exponential generating function corresponding to
\{s(m,n)\}
.

The recursive formula in
Section 4.1
allows us to get a closed formula for
f(x,y)
.

Theorem 4.11
.

The exponential generating function for
\{s(m,n)\}
satisfies

f(x,y)=\frac{e^{2x+2y}}{(e^{x}+e^{y}-e^{x+y})^{3}}.

Proof.

Using standard techniques for exponential generating functions together with
Section 4.1
shows that
f
is a solution to the PDE

\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}=(e^{x}+1)f+(e^{x}-1)\frac{\partial f}{\partial x},

subject to the initial conditions

f(x,0)=e^{2x}\quad\text{and}\quad f(0,y)=e^{2y}.

These initial conditions follow from the fact that
s(m,0)=2^{m}
and
s(0,n)=2^{n}
.
The general solution is of the form

f(x,y)=\frac{\phi(\frac{e^{x}-1}{e^{x-y}})e^{x}}{(e^{x}-1)^{2}},

with
\phi
an arbitrary function. The initial conditions give the result.
∎

5.
Saturation Conjecture for
C_{pq^{n}}

In this section we show that the saturation conjecture is true for
G=C_{pq^{n}}
for all
n\geq 0
.

We first recall Rubin’s characterization of linear isometric transfer systems in the case of finite cyclic groups. Let
k
be a positive integer, and let
G=C_{k}
be the cyclic group of order

.

Definition 5.1
.

Call
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/k\mathbb{Z}

that contains

and is closed under additive inverses an
index set
. Given an index set
I
,
define the
I
-modular
transfer system
\mathscr{F}_{I}
,
by the following condition: given
d\mid e\mid k
,

(C_{d}\rightarrow C_{e})\in\mathscr{F}_{I}\iff(I\text{ mod }e)+d=(I\text{ mod }e).

We say
I
is an
index set
for
\mathscr{F}_{I}
.

Proposition 5.2

(
[
Rub20
, Proposition 5.15]
)
.

A
G
-transfer system is
I
-modular for some
I
if and only if it is linear isometric.

The proof is done in two main steps. First, every
G
-universe can be expressed as the direct sum of infinitely many copies of the 2-dimensional representations given by rotation by
2\pi m/k
for
m\in I
, for some set
I
that contains 0 is closed under additive inverses. Second, one translates the general characterization of the transfer system associated to a linear isometries operad of
[
BH15
, Theorem 4.18]
in terms of this specific decomposition.

Section 5
implies that to verify the saturation conjecture it is sufficient to build an index set
I
for each saturated transfer system. Note that the following proposition follows directly from definitions and will allow us to recursively construct index sets via the
\ell=pq^{n}
,

case.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
\ell\mid k
, and suppose that
J
is an index set for
C_{k}
. Let

. Then

is an index set for
C_{\ell}
, and

\mathscr{F}_{I}=(\mathscr{F}_{J})|_{C_{\ell}}.

∎

Our task now is to
recursively generate index sets for

C_{pq^{n+1}}
from index sets for saturated

C_{pq^{n}}
. We use the language of saturated covers from
Section 3.1
, and follow the strategy of

to split

into four equivalence classes, based on the fibers of the map

of

.

The four equivalence classes have the following representative top rows. For a saturated cover

, the circles denote the vertices in

, the edges in solid black are the edges that must be in
S
, and the edges in dashed red are the edges that

doesn’t contain. The remaining edges may or may not be in

.

\bullet
\bullet
\bullet

\bullet

\bullet

As explained in

.

In order for the inductive step to work in all cases, we need to prove the following stronger statement.

Theorem 5.4
.

Suppose
p,q
are primes greater than 3 and
n\geq 0
, and let
\mathscr{F}
be a saturated transfer system on
C_{pq^{n}}
. Then there exists an index set
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/pq^{n}\mathbb{Z}
such that
\mathscr{F}=\mathscr{F}_{I}
and

contains a nonzero multiple of

.

Proof.

We first prove the statement for type I directly, and we then prove the cases for types II, III and IV by induction. The strategy for all cases is to take a saturated transfer system
\mathscr{F}
, restrict it to a certain subgroup, take an index set for the restriction, and construct an index set for
\mathscr{F}
based on the index set for the restriction. We will write
(i,j)
for the subgroup
C_{p^{i}q^{j}}\leq C_{pq^{n}}
when it is convenient.

Type I:
Let
\mathscr{F}
be a saturated transfer system on
C_{pq^{n}}
of type I, and consider its restriction
\mathscr{F}\mid_{C_{q^{n}}}
. By
[
Rub20
, Theorem 5.18]
, this saturated transfer system on
C_{q^{n}}
is induced by an index set
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/{q^{n}}\mathbb{Z}
. Set

J:=\{\alpha q^{n}+i\mid 0\leq\alpha<p,~i\in I\}.

By construction we have that
J\text{ mod }{q^{n}}=I
, so
Section 5
implies that the restrictions of
\mathscr{F}_{J}
and
\mathscr{F}
to

coincide. By construction again we have that

, thus showing that

\mathscr{F}_{J}=\mathscr{F}
. Moreover, taking
\alpha=1
shows that

.

For the remainder of the proof, we proceed by induction. For the base case, when
n=0
, we are considering the two saturated systems for

as long as

and the complete one is induced by

. Note that the latter index set is the one we obtain from the direct proof above.

Our inductive hypothesis is that the statement of the theorem holds for some

\mathscr{F}

, and consider its restriction to

containing

for some

\mathscr{F}_{I}=\mathscr{F}\mid_{C_{pq^{n}}}.

In all three cases, we will produce an index set

such that
J\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}=I
, so that by
Section 5
we get that
\mathscr{F}_{J}
and
\mathscr{F}
coincide in the restriction to
C_{pq^{n}}
. We will then show that
J
does the right thing in the top square (depending on the type) and contains a nonzero multiple of
q^{n+1}
.

Type II:
Suppose
\mathscr{F}
has type II and take
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/pq^{n}\mathbb{Z}
as described above. Set

J:=\{\alpha pq^{n}+i\mid 0\leq\alpha<q,~i\in I\}.

In a manner similar to the type I argument, the reader may verify that
\mathscr{F}_{J}=\mathscr{F}
. To find a nonzero multiple of
q^{n+1}
in
J
, take
\alpha
with residue class
-ap^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}^{\times}
and
i=aq^{n}
.

Type III:
Suppose
\mathscr{F}
has type III and take
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/pq^{n}\mathbb{Z}
as described above. We need to produce an index set
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/pq^{n+1}\mathbb{Z}
such that
J\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}=I
, some
0\neq bq^{n+1}\in J
, and — since
\mathscr{F}
has type III — such that
(1,n)\to(1,n+1)\not\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
and
(0,n)\to(0,n+1)\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
. By the saturation axioms, these conditions are enough to ensure
\mathscr{F}_{J}=\mathscr{F}
.

Set

J^{\prime}:=\{\alpha pq^{n}+i\mid 0\leq\alpha<q,~i\in I\}

and

J:=J^{\prime}\smallsetminus\{aq^{n},pq^{n+1}-aq^{n}\}.

Then
J
is an index set and it is clear that
J\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}\subseteq I
with only
aq^{n}
and
-aq^{n}
possibly in the set difference. For
q>2
and
\alpha=1
, the element
pq^{n}+aq^{n}
is in
J
and reduces to
aq^{n}\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}
. Similarly, the mod
pq^{n+1}
negative of this element is in
J
and reduces to
-aq^{n}\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}
.

We now check that
(0,n)\to(0,n+1)\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
, which amounts to
(J\text{ mod }q^{n+1})+q^{n}=J\text{ mod }q^{n+1}
. We claim that
J\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}}=J^{\prime}\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}}
, which suffices for this result. To verify the claim, take
\alpha
that reduces to
ap^{-1}\text{ mod }q
. Then

aq^{n}\equiv\alpha pq^{n}\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}},

so
aq^{n}\in(J\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}})
as needed.

To show that
(1,n)\to(1,n+1)\not\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
, we must verify that

J+pq^{n}\neq J.

Note that
aq^{n}\not\in J
, but
(q-1)pq^{n}+aq^{n}\in J
, so
aq^{n}\in J+pq^{n}
.

Finally, we need to show that
J
contains some nonzero multiple
bq^{n+1}
of
q^{n+1}
. If
a\neq q
, then
J
contains

\alpha pq^{n}+aq^{n}=(\alpha p+a)q^{n}

which is divisible by
q^{n+1}
for some
0<\alpha<q
. If
a=q
(which is possible when
q<p
), then we actually must modify the definition of
J
, setting

J:=J^{\prime}\smallsetminus\{aq^{n}+pq^{n},pq^{n+1}-(aq^{n}+pq^{n})\}.

The above argument still goes through and we can then check that some
0\neq bq^{n+1}\in J
.

Type IV:
Suppose
\mathscr{F}
has type IV and take
I\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/pq^{n}\mathbb{Z}
as described above. We construct an index set
J\subseteq\mathbb{Z}/pq^{n+1}\mathbb{Z}
containing a nonzero multiple of
q^{n+1}
such that
J\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}=I
,
(0,n)\to(0,n+1)\not\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
and
(0,n+1)\to(1,n+1)\not\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
. The fact that
\mathscr{F}
is of type IV and the saturation axioms imply then that
\mathscr{F}_{J}=\mathscr{F}
.

Let
i\in I\smallsetminus 0
. Then by
Section 5
(stated and proved immediately after this proof), there exists
0\leq\alpha_{i}<q
such that
\alpha_{i}pq^{n}+i\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}}
lies in the interval
[0,q^{n})
. By Sunzi’s theorem, there exists
0<c<pq
such that
c
is a multiple of
q
and
c\equiv a\text{ mod }{p}
. We set

J:=\{0,cq^{n},pq^{n+1}-cq^{n}\}\cup\left\{\alpha_{i}pq^{n}+i,pq^{n+1}-(\alpha_{i}pq^{n}+i)\mid i\in I\smallsetminus\{0,aq^{n},pq^{n}-aq^{n}\}\right\}.

Since
c
is a nonzero multiple of
q
, we know
cq^{n}
is a nonzero multiple of
q^{n+1}
. The other condition on
c
implies
cq^{n}\equiv aq^{n}\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}
, and we thus have that
J\text{ mod }{pq^{n}}=I
.

To prove that
(0,n)\to(0,n+1)\not\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
we need to check that

(J\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}})+q^{n}\neq(J\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}}).

We have that

(J\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}})=\{0\}\cup\{\alpha_{i}pq^{n}+i,q^{n+1}-(\alpha_{i}pq^{n}+i)\mid i\in I\smallsetminus\{0,aq^{n},pq^{n}-aq^{n}\}\}.

When considering this set in terms of representatives
\{0,1,\dots,q^{n+1}-1\}
, its elements are concentrated in the intervals
[0,q^{n}-1]
and
[q^{n+1}-q^{n}+1,q^{n+1}-1]
. Basic arithmetic shows that if
q>2
, the translation by
q^{n}
of any element in the first interval does not land in either of the two intervals, showing our result.

A similar argument using that
p,q>3
can be used to prove that
J+q^{n+1}\neq J
, thus showing that
(0,n+1)\to(1,n+1)\not\in\mathscr{F}_{J}
, as needed. This finishes the proof.
∎

Lemma 5.5
.

Let
i
be an integer such that
0<i<pq^{n}
. Then there exists
0\leq\alpha<q
such that the residue of
\alpha pq^{n}+i\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}}
lies in the interval
[0,q^{n})
.

Proof.

Let
r
be the residue class of
i\text{ mod }{q^{n}}
. Thus,
0\leq r<q^{n}
, and there exists
0\leq k<p
such that
i=kq^{n}+r
. By Bézout’s identity, there exist
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
cp+dq=1
. Let
\beta=-ck\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then basic arithmetic shows that

\beta pq^{n}+i\equiv r\text{ mod }{q^{n+1}}.

Finally, letting
\alpha
be the residue of
\beta\text{ mod }{q}
achieves the result.
∎
