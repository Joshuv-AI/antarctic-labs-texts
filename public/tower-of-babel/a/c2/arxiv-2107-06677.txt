Hybrid Model and Data Driven Algorithm for Online Learning of Any-to-Any Path Loss Maps
M. A. Gutierrez-Estevez, Martin Kasparick, Renato L. G. Cavalvante, Sławomir Stańczak

Abstract
Learning any-to-any (A2A) path loss maps, where the objective is the reconstruction of path loss between any two given points in a map, might be a key enabler for many applications that rely on device-to-device (D2D) communication. Such applications include machine-type communications (MTC) or vehicle-to-vehicle (V2V) communications. Current approaches for learning A2A maps are either model-based methods, or pure data-driven methods. Model-based methods have the advantage that they can generate reliable estimations with low computational complexity, but they cannot exploit information coming from data. Pure data-driven methods can achieve good performance without assuming any physical model, but their complexity and their lack of robustness is not acceptable for many applications. In this paper, we propose a novel hybrid model and data-driven approach that fuses information obtained from datasets and models in an online fashion. To that end, we leverage the framework of stochastic learning to deal with the sequential arrival of samples and propose an online algorithm that alternatively and sequentially minimizes the original non-convex problem. A proof of convergence is presented, along with experiments based firstly on synthetic data, and secondly on a more realistic dataset for V2X, with both experiments showing promising results.



I
Introduction

Many applications in wireless networks can benefit from information related to the spatial distribution of path loss. Among them, applications involving peer-to-peer communication are the most challenging ones because of fast increase of communication links when the number of nodes grows. Such applications include sensor networks,
MTC
(
MTC
) or
V2V
(
V2V
) communications. As an example consider a platoon of vehicles that have to constantly exchange information about their position, acceleration, and so on. If the path loss between any two vehicles along the route was known in advance, this information would give the vehicles enough time to adapt their distance accordingly and save a considerable amount of fuel
[
1
]
. Other benefits include reliability of communications and safety.

A2A
(
A2A
) maps describe the spatial distribution of radio signals between any two given locations of a map, which makes them very suited for those applications. But the challenge is to cope with the rapid increase of complexity when the map size increases, while keeping high prediction accuracy.

Some approaches for radio maps estimation are pure data-driven methods
[
2
,
3
,
4
]
in the sense that no physical model for the propagation of radio signals is considered, but instead they exploit the expected spatial correlation of the channel characteristics. Other approaches either rely on fixed mathematical models to describe the propagation of radio signals
[
5
,
6
,
7
]
, or they attempt at learning such models without context information
[
8
,
9
]
. Model-based methods have the advantage that they can generate reliable estimations with low computational complexity and little to no side information. However, they are rigid in the sense that they cannot exploit information coming from data to adapt to the environment and to reduce model uncertainty. On the other hand, pure data-driven methods can achieve good performance without assuming any physical model, but their complexity and their lack of robustness against changes in the environment (e.g., underlying distribution of the data) is not acceptable for many applications.

Against this background, we introduce a novel hybrid data- and model-driven approach with the intention of extracting the best of both worlds: we start with the notion that a mathematical model can coarsely represent the physical world, but we endow our method with the flexibility to modify the original model based on the acquired measurements. Further, our method is online because both the physical characteristics of the environment may vary (disposition of buildings, environmental conditions like rain or fog, etc…), and also because an online method can deal with the high complexity of the problem for large scenarios.

A different approach exploits the concept of the Fresnel zone

5

In order to overcome the limitations of a mathematical model, the authors in

I-B

Contributions

In the following we enumerate the contributions of this work:

1.

We propose a new problem to learn the

based on
A2A
path loss measurements, while, at the same time,

the original model into another one better represented by the data. This strategy results in a non-convex optimization problem, but it is marginally convex, i.e., the problem becomes convex if a subset of variables is fixed. In contrast to

8
,
19

, we constrain the updates to remain close to the original model.

2.

The problem of learning the model derived from the
TPT
would be extremely ill-posed, so we propose a non-linear kernel approach based on the

(
RBF
) similar to
[
9
,
8
,
19
]
.

3.

As in

9
,
19
,
5
]
, the structure representing the

for the reconstruction of the

4.

Similar to the seeding paper of this work
[
19
]
, we define a majorizing function that

the original objective function, i.e., the new function is grater or equal than the original one in its entire domain. This strategy is known from stochastic approximation
[

]
and has been exploited for online learning in different application domains
[

,
5
,
24
]
. Unlike in our previous work, we prove that the new function is indeed a surrogate of the original one, i.e., both functions tend to the same real value when the number of iterations grows towards infinity.

5.

We propose a novel online algorithm similar to the seeding publication in the sense that it is also a “descent” version of the
gAM
. In this case however, the method to update the model is the projected gradient descent, while the iterative procedure for the
SLF
is based on the forward-backward splitting method
[
25
]
.

6.

As main contribution of this work, we study the convergence of the proposed algorithm both in the objective and in the arguments. To this end, we first prove some regularity of the functions involved. More precisely, we require local Lipschitz-continuity, uniformly strong convexity and uniform boundedness. After proving that the majorizing function is a surrogate of the original objective function under some reasonable assumptions, we show that the iterations of the online algorithm converge to a point in the set of stationary points of the original problem.

7.

Unlike in the seeding publication, we evaluate our algorithm firstly with synthetic data representing the Madrid scenario
[
26
]
for a
V2V
network, and secondly with measurements generated with the
GEMV2
(
GEMV2
)
[
27
]
, a simulator which has been shown to generate
V2V
path loss datasets very close to real measurements
[
27
]
. In both cases, we show the gains of our hybrid model and data driven approach.

The objective of this section is to introduce the mathematical machinery required for this study. We further introduce notation and results in mathematics that are necessary to keep the presentation as self-contained as possible.

Definition 1

(Marginal convexity)
.

A continuous function of two variables
k:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow\real
is considered marginally convex in its first variable if for every value of
\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathcal{Y}}
, the function
k_{\boldsymbol{y}}:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\real
is convex, i.e. for every
{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}\in{\mathcal{X}}

such that

k_{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{2})\geq k_{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1})+\langle{\boldsymbol{g}},{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1}\rangle.

In case
k
was differentiable in
{\boldsymbol{x}}
, the subgradient can be substituted by the corresponding gradient:

k_{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{2})\geq k_{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1})+\left\langle\nabla k_{\boldsymbol{y}}({\boldsymbol{x}}^{1}),{\boldsymbol{x}}^{2}-{\boldsymbol{x}}^{1}\right\rangle.

A similar argument can be made for the second variable
\boldsymbol{y}
.

Definition 2

(Marginal optimum coordinate)
.

Let
k:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow\real.
For any point
\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathcal{Y}},
we say that
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}
is a marginally optimal coordinate with respect to
\boldsymbol{y},
and use the shorthand
\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}}\in\textup{mopt}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}),
if
k(\tilde{\boldsymbol{x}},\boldsymbol{y})\leq k(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y})~\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}}.
Similarly, for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in{\mathcal{X}},
we say
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}\in\textup{mopt}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
if
\tilde{\boldsymbol{y}}
is a marginally optimal coordinate with respect to
\boldsymbol{x}.

Definition 3

(Bistable point)
.

Given a function
k:{\mathcal{X}}\times{\mathcal{Y}}\rightarrow\real
, a point

\boldsymbol{y}\in\textup{mopt}_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
\boldsymbol{x}\in\textup{mopt}_{k}(\boldsymbol{y}),
i.e. both coordinates are marginally optimal with respect to each other.

Definition 4

(Proximal operator)
.

The proximity operator of a function

is given by:

\textup{prox}_{\gamma k}:\real^{n}\rightarrow\real^{n},(\boldsymbol{f})\mapsto\underset{\boldsymbol{y}\in\real^{n}}{\textup{argmin}}~k(\boldsymbol{y})+\gamma\|\boldsymbol{f}-\boldsymbol{y}\|_{2}^{2},

where

Definition 5

(Directional derivative)
.

Let
h:{\mathcal{X}}\rightarrow\real
be a convex function, where
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\real^{p}
is a closed convex set. The directional derivative of the function

Definition 6

(Stationary point)
.

Let

be a function, where
{\mathcal{X}}\subseteq\real^{p}
is a convex set. The point

Definition 7

(Contraction mapping)
.

Let
{\mathcal{E}}
be an Euclidian space. Then

(\forall\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\in{\mathcal{E}})~\|T(\boldsymbol{x})-T(\boldsymbol{y})\|\leq\kappa\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{y}\|.

Definition 8

(Non-expansive mapping)

Let

The operator

Definition 9

(Equicontinuous function)

Let

Definition 10

(Quasi-martingale)

A martingale is a stochastic process for which, at a particular time, the conditional expectation of the next value in the sequence, given all prior values, is equal to the present value, i.e., a stochastic process

Further, a stochastic process is said to be quasi-martingale if it has a decomposition into the sum of a martingale process and a sequence of functions having almost every sample of bounded variation

Consider a real valued stochastic process

For each

III
System Model and Problem Statement

Consider a two-dimensional area
\mathcal{A}\subset\real^{2}
. We model the long-term average path loss between any two points
\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}\in\mathcal{A}
in logarithmic scale by:

\mathrm{pl}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\mathrm{pl}_{0}+10\delta\log_{10}\left(\frac{||\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}||_{2}}{d_{0}}\right)+s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})+\epsilon_{s},

(1)

where
\mathrm{pl}_{0}
is the path loss at a reference distance
d_{0}
,
\delta>0
is the path loss exponential decay,
s:\real^{2}\times\real^{2}\rightarrow\real
represents the shadowing function between
\mathbf{x}_{i}
and
\mathbf{x}_{j}
, and
\epsilon_{s}>0
is a scalar that accounts for the error in the measurements.

As in
[
6
,
8
,
5
,
9
]
, we model the shadow fading with a
TPT
. More precisely, we consider the shadowing to be modeled as follows:

s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})=\sum_{p=1}^{P_{x}P_{y}}w(\phi_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}),\phi_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{p},\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}))f(\mathbf{x}_{p}),

(2)

where
w:\real\times\real\rightarrow\real
is the window function;
f:\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\real
is the
SLF
function; the distance functions
\phi_{1}:\real^{2}\times\real^{2}\rightarrow\real_{+},(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})\mapsto||\mathbf{x}_{i}-\mathbf{x}_{j}||_{2}
and
\phi_{2}:\real^{2}\times\real^{2}\times\real^{2}\rightarrow\real_{+},(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{p})\mapsto||\mathbf{x}_{p}-\mathbf{x}_{i}||_{2}+||\mathbf{x}_{p}-\mathbf{x}_{j}||_{2}
are functions that measure the length of the direct link and the length of the path going through an intermediate point, respectively;
P_{x}
,
P_{y}
is the number of horizontal and vertical pixels of the map, respectively, and
\mathbf{x}_{p}
is the coordinate of the pixel
p\in\overline{1,P}
, with
P=P_{x}P_{y}
. Intuitively speaking, the shadowing between any two points in a map is potentially influenced by the SLF at any point of the map. This assumption is due to the multi-path nature of radio signals propagation. To capture these effects, the SLF is weighted by a window function that models the influence of each position on a link.

There are several models in the literature that exploit the concept of the Fresnel zone and try to model the window function in an statistical form
[
13
]
. For example, the normalized elliptical model considers that an ellipsoid with foci at each node location determines the influence for each link in the area
[
14
]
.
Mathematically speaking, the window function is defined according to the normalized elliptical model as follows
[
6
,
12
]
:

w(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}):=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&\textup{if}~\phi_{2}>\phi_{1}+\eta/2\\
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\phi_{1}}}&\textup{otherwise},\end{matrix}\right.

(3)

with
\eta
being in this case the signal wavelength.

The normalized elliptical model assumes that all the points inside the ellipse have equal weight. Another model called inverse area elliptical model considers that some parts of the ellipse have a greater contribution than others
[
6
]
. The reasoning behind this is that signal paths closer to the edge of the ellipse travel longer distances than those closer to the line of sight, so their contribution to the shadowing should be lower. Mathematically, the model is described as follows:

w(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}):=\left\{\begin{matrix}0&\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\textup{if}~\phi_{2}>\phi_{1}+\eta/2\\
\textup{min}(\Gamma(\phi_{1},\phi_{2}),\Gamma(\phi_{1},\phi_{1}+\nu))&\textup{otherwise},\end{matrix}\right.

with
\nu>0
being an user-selected parameter and

\Gamma(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=\frac{4}{\pi\phi_{2}\sqrt{\phi_{2}^{2}-\phi_{1}^{2}}}.

We now proceed to write (
2
) in a matrix form. To this end, let
{\boldsymbol{f}}=[f_{1},...,f_{P}]^{\top}\in\real^{P}
, where
f_{p}:=f(\mathbf{x}_{p})
. Assuming channel reciprocity in the path loss between any two points, the total number of links
T
in a map of
P
pixels is
T:=P(P-1)/2,
and the index set
\mathcal{M}
of all links is
\mathcal{M}:=\overline{1,...,T}
. We define a bijective mapping

B:\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{P}\rightarrow\mathcal{M}:~m\mapsto P(j-1)+i

that maps any two indexes
i,j\in\mathcal{P}
onto a link index
m\in\mathcal{M}
.
By
W\in\real^{T\times P}
we define a matrix containing all possible weight values of the map, where
w_{m,p}:=w(\phi_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j}),\phi_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{p}))
with
m:=B(i,j)
. The shadow fading vector
{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\real^{T}
is generated by stacking all possible values of
s(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
, such that:

{\boldsymbol{s}}=W{\boldsymbol{f}}.

(4)

Consider that measured path loss values arrive at a central entity at different time instants. Let
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}{{}_{t}}\in\real^{M_{t}}
be the (noisy) shadowing measurements acquired at time instant
t\in{\mathbb{N}}
, and
\Omega_{t}:=\{\omega_{t}^{1},...,\omega_{t}^{M_{t}}\}
is the measurements index set with cardinality
M_{t}

[
36
,
11
]
. For the sake of simplicity, we assume that
M_{1}=M_{2}=\cdots=M
for the reminder of this manuscript. The elements in
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}
represent a selection of all elements contained in
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\in\real^{T}
, which is in turn a vector containing all possible (noisy) measurements in a map. Analogously, the matrix
W_{t}\in\real^{M\times P}
denotes the weight matrix whose rows correspond to the link measurements received at time
t
. Let
(\forall i\in\Omega_{t})~\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\in\real^{P}
be the rows of
W_{t}
. The convex set
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
is defined as the
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
r
around the vector
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
:

{\mathcal{C}}_{i}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{P}|\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{w}_{i}\|_{2}\leq r\}.

Through the sets
{\mathcal{C}}_{i}
we add the context information related to a certain model such as the elliptical model, or the normalized elliptical previously introduced. But, instead of assuming that the window function has to follow one of these or any other model, we encode this information in the form of a constraint convex set to our optimization problem. Such an approach allows us to include information coming from a model, since a solution to our problem is expected to be close to the model, but it also gives us
some
freedom in case the physical environment is not precisely expressed by it. Note that we have restricted our constraint sets to the
\ell_{2}
-ball of radius
r
and centered in
\boldsymbol{w}_{i}
. One can change or add the constraint sets to include more context information, provided that the sets are convex ones and their intersection is not empty
[
37
]
.

The proposed approach is based on the assumption that the
SLF
vector
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is a block-sparse vector
[
38
]
. The block-sparsity of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
is justified by the fact that most pixels of a map represent the free space, whose absorption value is negligible compared to the absorption of solid bodies, and therefore assumed to be zero. Further, non-zero entries of
{\boldsymbol{f}}
are those belonging to walls and other physical structures, therefore they are concentrated in groups.

In light of the above assumption, an intuitive approach to estimate the
SLF
is to minimize the least squares error regularized by the elastic net to improve block-sparsity.

Previous studies such as
[
8
,
9
]
in this particular application domain have shown that attempts at minimizing with respect to
W
fail to give good results because the problems are in general severely ill-posed. To address this limitation, we impose additional structure on
W
by considering a non-linear kernel approach similar to
[
9
]
.

With some abuse of notation, we define the vector
{\boldsymbol{c}}:=[c_{1},...,c_{M}]^{\top}\in\real^{M}
, where
c_{m}:=\phi(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j})
. Similarly, denote
{\boldsymbol{d}}:=[d_{1,1},...,d_{M,1},d_{1,2},...,d_{M,P}]^{\top}\in\real^{MP}
, where
d_{m,p}:=\phi_{2}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{x}_{j},\mathbf{x}_{p})
. Define
{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{m,p}:=[c_{m},d_{m,p}]^{\top}\in\real^{2}_{+}
as a two-dimensional vector with arbitrary
c_{m},d_{m,p}
stacked together; and we assume that the window function can be written as a function of a positive definite kernel in the following form:

w({\boldsymbol{\Phi}})=\sum_{m=\omega_{t}^{1}}^{\omega_{t}^{M}}\sum_{p=1}^{P}\alpha_{q(m,p)}\kappa({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{m,p},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}),

(5)

where
\alpha_{q(m,p)}\in\real
are appropriate scalars to be determined,
\omega_{t}^{1},...,\omega_{t}^{M}
are the ordered elements of
\Omega_{t}
, and
q(m,p)
is an index obtained as
q(m,p):=P(m-1)+p
. In particular, in this study we use
RBF
as kernel:

\kappa_{q,q^{\prime}}:=\kappa({\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{q},{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{q^{\prime}})=\exp\left(-\frac{\left\|{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{q}-{\boldsymbol{\Phi}}_{q^{\prime}}\right\|^{2}_{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right),

where
\sigma>0
is the width of the kernel.
Define the kernel matrix
K_{t}\in\real^{MP\times MP}
with the
(q,q^{\prime})
element given by
\kappa_{q,q^{\prime}}
, and also define the vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
as
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}=[\alpha_{q_{1}},\cdots,\alpha_{q_{MP}}]^{\top}\in\real^{MP}
.

Let
j\in\Omega_{t}
, and define the convex set

{\mathcal{Q}}_{t,j}:=\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{P}|\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}\|_{2}\leq r\},

where
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}\in\real^{P}
is a vector given by
\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}:=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}]_{j}
, with
[\cdot]_{j}
representing the selection of entries
P(j-1)+1
until
Pj
of a vector.
The set in the
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
-parameter space
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\alpha}
is defined at time
t
as

{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\alpha}:=\bigtimes_{j=1}^{M}{\mathcal{Q}}_{t,j}\subset\real^{MP},

which is also convex, since the Cartesian product of convex sets is a convex set
[
37
]
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}\in\real^{P}
. The projection of
\boldsymbol{x}
onto the set
{\mathcal{Q}}_{t,j}
is given by:

\Pi_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{t,j}}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left\{\mathbf{q}\in{\mathcal{Q}}_{t,j}|\mathbf{q}=r_{\textup{min}}\frac{\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}}{\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}\|_{2}}+\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}\right\},

with
r_{\textup{min}}=\min(r,\|\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{a}_{t,j}\|_{2})
.
We can construct the projection of a vector
\boldsymbol{\alpha}\in\real^{MP}
onto
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\alpha}
as
[
37
, Proposition 8]
:

\Pi_{C_{t}^{\alpha}}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=\bigtimes_{j=1}^{M}\Pi_{{\mathcal{Q}}_{t,j}}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j}),

(6)

where
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}:=[\boldsymbol{\alpha}]_{j}\in\real^{P}
.

Considering the non-linear kernel approach, we can write an optimization problem over
\boldsymbol{f},\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
as follows:

\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{\tau},\boldsymbol{f}\in\real^{P}}{\textup{minimize}}\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\tau}-A_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}K_{\tau}{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\tau}\right\|^{2}_{2}\\
+\lambda_{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}+\lambda_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{3}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}\|^{2}_{2},

(7)

where
\lambda_{1}
,
\lambda_{2}
and
\lambda_{3}
are nonnegative regularization parameters
[
21
]
,
A_{{\boldsymbol{f}}}=I_{M}\otimes\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\in\real^{M\times MP}
is the Kronecker product between the identity matrix
I_{M}\in\real^{M\times M}
and
\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}
, and the term
\lambda_{3}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}\|^{2}_{2}
is included to guarantee stability of the solutions.

Problem (
7
) can be viewed as the minimization of the empirical cost of a model-constrained least squares regression regularized with the elastic net. Note however that (
7
) is not jointly convex in
{\boldsymbol{f}},{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t}
, but it is convex in
{\boldsymbol{f}}
if
{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{t}
are fixed, and vice versa
[
20
]
. As a result, we consider an alternating minimization strategy to address (
7
) where, at time
t
of arrival of new measurements, one set of variables is updated while the remaining ones are kept constant. This process is carried out until a stopping criterion is met.

IV
Online Path Loss Learning

In scenarios like the one presented in the previous section, one is in general interested in obtaining an estimate
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t}
of
\boldsymbol{f}
at time
t
, instead of waiting for all measurements to arrive before solving Problem (
7
). In these cases, a standard alternative is to try to optimize an empirical cost function
[
23
]
. To this end, we first define
(\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}})~l(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t},\boldsymbol{f})
as the optimal value of the partial optimization problem with respect to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
:

l_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})\triangleq l(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t},\boldsymbol{f})=\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{t}}{\textup{min}}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}-A_{\boldsymbol{f}}K_{t}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{3}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\|^{2}_{2},

(8)

and then we define the empirical cost function of the
SLF
problem as

h_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\ell_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{f})\triangleq l_{\tau}(\boldsymbol{f})+\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{2}^{2}.

(9)

The problem of online
SLF
learning corresponds to the minimization of (
9
):

\underset{\boldsymbol{f}\in\real^{P}}{\textup{minimize}}~h_{t}(\boldsymbol{f}).

(10)

Note that (
7
) and (
10
) are equivalent problems in the sense that their set of minimizers are identical. However, given a finite training set, one should not spend too much time on accurately minimizing the empirical cost, since it is only an approximation of the expected cost and might not provide good solutions, especially when
t
is small
[
39
]
. Another limitation of directly minimizing (
9
) is the fact that the complexity increases with the acquired number of samples, making the approach unsuitable for online settings. Therefore, our interest lies on the minimization of the expected cost of
h_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
:

h(\boldsymbol{f})\triangleq\mathbb{E}_{\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}}[\ell_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})]=\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\textup{lim}}h_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})~a.s.,

(11)

where the expectation, which for now is supposed to be finite, is taken relative to the probability distribution
p(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}})
of the measurements. Later in Section
V
we will state the necessary conditions for (
11
) to be true.

IV-A

Addressing the elastic net subproblem

Instead of minimizing the empirical cost in (
9
), we propose the minimization at time instant
t
of a new function
(\forall\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in\real^{MP})

\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})=\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}-A_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\tau}}{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{1}\left\|{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}\left\|{\boldsymbol{f}}\right\|^{2}_{2},

(12)

where
A_{{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\tau}}=\sum_{n=1}^{M}{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n}\otimes({\boldsymbol{\alpha}}_{\tau}^{\top}K_{\tau})\in\real^{M\times P}
, and
{\boldsymbol{e}}_{n}\in\real^{M}
is a unitary vector with all zeros but the
nth
entry one.

The motivation behind this approach lays on the fact that
\check{h}_{t}
is convex in
\boldsymbol{f}
, and also because one can readily show that it upperbounds the empirical cost function
h_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
. Indeed, we will prove in Section
V
that
\check{h}_{t}
acts as a surrogate of
h_{t}
, i.e.
\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
and
h_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
converge to the same limit when
t\rightarrow\infty
.

We can rewrite
\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
in a more convenient way for our online algorithm in the following way:

\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})\!=\!\frac{\textup{Tr}(\bar{A}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\!)\!\!-\!\!\textup{Tr}(\textbf{b}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}^{\top}\!)}{2t}\!+\!\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1}+C,

(13)

where
C=\textup{Tr}(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}^{\top})/2t
is a constant,
\bar{A}_{t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}A_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}}^{\top}A_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}}\in\real^{P\times P}
, and
\textbf{b}_{t}=\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}A^{\top}_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\tau}\in\real^{P}.
Note that the structures
\bar{A}_{t}
and
\textbf{b}_{t}
do not change size with increasing
t
. This suggests an algorithm in which, at time
t
, we keep track and update
\bar{A}_{t}
and
\textbf{b}_{t}
, and a new estimate
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t}
is found after minimizing function
\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
in (
13
) w.r.t.
\boldsymbol{f}
. Note that
(\forall\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in\real^{MP})
the function
\check{h}_{t}
is coercive, proper and strongly convex, therefore

\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t}\in\textup{argmin}_{\boldsymbol{f}}~\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})

(14)

exists and is unique
[
40
]
.

The function
\check{h}_{t}
can be expressed as the sum of two functions
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
, where
g_{1}
is convex and differentiable, while
g_{2}
is convex but non-smooth. More precisely, define

g_{1}\!:\!\real^{P}\!\rightarrow\!\real\!:\!\boldsymbol{f}\!\mapsto\!\frac{1}{2t}\!\left(\!\textup{Tr}(\bar{A}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}\boldsymbol{f}^{\top})\!-\!\textup{Tr}(\textbf{b}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}^{\top})\!+\!t\lambda_{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|^{2}_{2}\!\right)\!+\!C,

(15)

and

g_{2}:\real^{P}\rightarrow\real:\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\lambda_{1}||\boldsymbol{f}||_{1}.

(16)

The problem
\textup{min}_{\boldsymbol{f}}~\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})
can then be formulated as:

\underset{\boldsymbol{f}}{\textup{min.}}~g_{1}(\boldsymbol{f})+g_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}).

(17)

This kind of problems are well understood and there is a plethora of algorithms to solve them
[
41
,
42
,
40
,
25
]
. We propose using the forward-backward splitting method
[
25
]
due to its good performance compared to other methods in this particular application domain. It can be shown
[
25
]
that, if
g_{1}
is Lipschitz-differentiable, Problem (
17
) admits one solution and that, for certain
\gamma\in]0,\epsilon[
and
\epsilon>0
, its solution is characterized by the fixed point equation

\boldsymbol{f}=\textup{prox}_{\gamma g_{2}}(\boldsymbol{f}-\gamma\nabla g_{1}(\boldsymbol{f})),

(18)

where
\textup{prox}_{\gamma g_{2}}
is the proximal operator of
g_{2}
with attracting factor
\gamma
.

An iterative solution to (
17
) is then given by

\boldsymbol{f}^{(n+1)}=\textup{soft}_{\lambda_{1}}\left(\boldsymbol{f}^{(n)}+\gamma\left(\frac{1}{2t}\textbf{b}^{\top}_{t}-\frac{1}{t}\bar{A}_{t}\boldsymbol{f}^{(n)}-\lambda_{2}\boldsymbol{f}^{(n)}\right)\right),

(19)

where
\textup{soft}_{\lambda_{1}}(\cdot)
is the soft thresholding function with threshold
\lambda_{1}
, and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the iteration index.

IV-B

Addressing the constraint least squares subproblem

Unlike the minimization of
\boldsymbol{f}
in Problem (
7
), the minimization over
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
is not coupled in the summation of functions through its variables, meaning that we can separate Problem (
7
) with respect to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
, while keeping
\boldsymbol{f}
fixed.

The problem of minimizing
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
is defined
(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in\real^{P})
as follows:

\underset{\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{t}}{\textup{min}}~k_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})\triangleq\frac{1}{2}\left\|\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}-A_{K_{t}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\right\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{3}\|\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\|^{2}_{2},

(20)

where
A_{K_{t}}=A_{\boldsymbol{f}}K_{t}\in\real^{M\times MP}.
Problem (
20
) has
(\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}})
a unique solution because
(\forall\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}\in\real^{MP})~k_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})
is a quadratic function and the lowest eigenvalue of its Hessian is at least
\lambda_{3}
. Such solution can be attained with the projected gradient method:

\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(n+1)}\!=\!\Pi_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{t}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(n)}\!+\!\mu\!\left(A_{K_{t}}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}\!-\!A_{K_{t}}^{\top}A_{K_{t}}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(n)}\!-\!\lambda_{3}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(n)}\right)\right)\!,

(21)

where
\Pi_{{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{t}}
is the projection onto the convex set
{\mathcal{C}}^{\alpha}_{t}
given by (
6
),
\mu>0
is the step size, and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
is the iteration index.
The reason for the selection of an iterative method such as the projected gradient to solve (
20
) instead of solving the dual problem will become apparent in the next section, but for now we mention the need of the intermediate values
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(n)}
for our algorithmic solution.

IV-C

Algorithmic Solution

The missing piece in the online
SLF
learning problem is the combination in an algorithm of the estimates
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t}
in (
14
) with the iterative solutions of
\boldsymbol{f}^{(n)}
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(n)}_{t}
in (
19
) and (
21
), respectively.

One important caveat of the online algorithm is that we implement a “descent” version of the outlined alternating minimization process. This means that instead of running the iterations in (
19
) until a stopping criterion is met, and then proceed with the iterations in (
21
) again until improvements are small enough, we take only one step at a time of the iterations in (
19
), and another step of iterations in (
21
), alternatively until a combined stopping criterion is met. The rationale behind this is that the improvement from
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{(1)}
to
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}^{(N)}
and from
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(1)}
to
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(N)}
might not be relevant enough to justify finding the optimal solutions of the two convex sub-problems in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}
alternatively. Indeed, simulations for this particular application have consistently shown better performance and shorter execution time with the “descent” strategy.

Consider that the samples
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{1},\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{2},...
are i.i.d samples drawn from a common distribution
p(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t})
. In order to guarantee stability of the iterations, we need to choose the update parameters
\mu
and
\gamma
for every iteration index
n
before updating
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}
, respectively. More precisely, we need to choose
\mu
(
\gamma
) strictly smaller than the Lipschitz constant of
\nabla k_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})
(
\nabla g_{1}(\boldsymbol{f})
), given by
L_{k}=\|A_{\boldsymbol{f}}^{\top}A_{\boldsymbol{f}}+\lambda_{3}I_{MP}\|_{2}
(
L_{g}=\|\bar{A}_{t}+\lambda_{2}I_{P}\|_{2}
), where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
here is the spectral norm of a matrix. Section
V
deals with the convergence analysis of the algorithm and provides formal proof for the selection of
\mu
and
\gamma
. One important element to guarantee the convergence of the algorithm is the selection of an
\epsilon
such that
\epsilon\in]0,1[
.

Finally, since
\check{h}_{t}
is expected to be close to
\check{h}_{t-1}
for large values of
t
, so are under suitable conditions
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t}
and
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t-1}
, which makes it efficient to use
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t-1}
as “warm” initialization for computing
\check{\boldsymbol{f}}_{t}
. Our procedure is summarized in Algorithm
1
.

\boldsymbol{f}^{(0)}_{1}

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3},\epsilon

\bar{A}_{0}\in\real^{P\times P}\leftarrow 0
,

for

t=1,...,t_{\textup{max}}

do

Draw
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}
from
p(\hat{\boldsymbol{s}})

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(n)}_{t}

while

stopping criterion NOT met

do

\mu\in]0,(1-\epsilon)/L_{k}]

\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(n+1)}_{t}
according to (

)

\textbf{b}_{t}\leftarrow\textbf{b}_{t-1}+A_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(n+1)}_{t}}^{\top}\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{t}

\gamma\in]0,(1-\epsilon)/L_{g}]

\boldsymbol{f}^{(n+1)}_{t}

)

IV-D

Complexity

The complexity of Alg.
1
is dominated by the two matrix multiplications (one to compute
\boldsymbol{\alpha}^{(n+1)}_{t}
, the other one to compute
\boldsymbol{f}^{(n+1)}_{t}
) required in each iteration
n
, which we assume
\mathcal{O}(n^{3})
. We also assume that the stopping criterion in both cases is given by a maximum number of iterations
N
. The complexity is then given by
\mathcal{O}(t_{\textup{max}}N(P^{3}M^{3}+P^{2}M))
. Because the complexity is dominated by the first term (
P^{3}M^{3}
), we see that it scales linearly with the total number of iterations (
t_{\textup{max}}N
), and cubicly for the number of pixels in the map (
P
) times the number of samples acquired at
t
(
M
).

V
Convergence Analysis

In this section, we provide a convergence analysis of our proposed algorithm both in the objective and in the arguments. We focus our analysis on a modified version of Alg.
1
, presented in Alg.
2
. Algorithm
2
represents the non-descent version of Alg.
1
, in the sense that, at time
t
, both sub-problems in
\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}
and
\boldsymbol{f}
are solved sequentially, instead of alternating step by step between the two sub-problems as in Alg.
1
. We begin with the necessary (and reasonable) assumptions for the convergence of both algorithms. Several lemmata are then presented as intermediate statements to achieve the first of the two most relevant results, which is the proof of convergence of Alg.
2
to a stationary point of Problem (
10
). After this, we trace back the connection with the original Alg.
1
and also prove its convergence in the arguments.

Input :

\boldsymbol{f}_{0}

(\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}})_{j=1}^{\infty}
, there exists a uniformly continuous function
\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f})
such that

(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})~\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\textup{lim}}\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f})=\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}),

(34)

and therefore

\underset{j\rightarrow\infty}{\textup{lim}}\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{t_{j}})=\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*}).

(35)

We know by definition that
(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})~\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f})\geq h_{t_{j}}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}).
Letting
j\rightarrow\infty
, we obtain

(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})~\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f})\geq h^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}).

(36)

Using Lemma
3
(ii), (
33
) and (
35
), Eq. (
32
) yields.

(iv) Define the function

u:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow\real:\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f})-h^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}).

From (
36
), we know that
(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})~u(\boldsymbol{f})\geq 0,
and
u
attains a minimum at
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
due to (
32
). Since
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}\in\mathrm{int}({\mathcal{F}}),

the first order optimality condition in
u(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})
implies that
\nabla u(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})=\nabla\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})-\nabla h^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})=0,
or equivalently

\nabla\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})=\nabla h^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*}).

(37)

Using the updates of Alg.
2
, we have

(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})~\check{h}_{t_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{t_{j}})=\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{t_{j}})+g_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}_{t_{j}})\leq\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}}(\boldsymbol{f})+g_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}).

Letting
j\rightarrow\infty
, and using (
35
) and the fact that the sequence
(\check{h}^{1}_{t_{j}})_{j=1}^{\infty}
is equicontinuous, it yields

(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})~\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})+g_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*})\leq\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f})+g_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}).

(38)

We have to show the existence of the directional derivative
(\forall\boldsymbol{d}\in\real^{P})~\check{h}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{f};\boldsymbol{d}),
which is not guaranteed because the set
{\mathcal{F}}
is compact by Assumption
2
. To obtain its existence, let us first define a new function

\tilde{h}:\real^{P}\rightarrow\real:~\boldsymbol{f}\mapsto\underset{t\rightarrow\infty}{\textup{lim}}~\check{h}_{t}(\boldsymbol{f})~a.s,

which, provided that the limit exists, it is a continuous and convex function. From Fact
7
, we have that for any
\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}}\in\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}~\tilde{h})
, the directional derivative
(\forall\boldsymbol{d}\in\real^{P})~\tilde{h}^{\prime}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{f}};\boldsymbol{d})
is a real number. Note that, since
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathrm{int}(\mathrm{dom}~\tilde{h}),
the function
\check{h}:{\mathcal{F}}\rightarrow\real
is the restriction of the function
\tilde{h}
over the compact set
{\mathcal{F}}
. Therefore, we conclude that
(\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}})(\forall\boldsymbol{d}\in\real^{P})~\check{h}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{f};\boldsymbol{d})
exists. Equation (
38
) implies that
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
is a minimizer of
\check{h}
, and, combining this fact with the existence of its directional derivative, we have

(\forall\boldsymbol{d}\in\real^{P})~\left\langle\nabla\check{h}^{1}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*}),\boldsymbol{d}\right\rangle+g_{2}^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*};\boldsymbol{d})\geq 0.

Combining this result with (

, we obtain

(\forall\boldsymbol{d}\in\real^{P})~h^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{f}^{*};\boldsymbol{d})\geq 0,

which means that
\boldsymbol{f}^{*}
is a stationary point of

(iv)

(\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}})
suppose that the stopping criterion of Alg.

iterations. Let
(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{(N)},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{f}_{t})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\times...\times{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}\times{\mathcal{F}}

Proof.

For this proof we exploit concepts of contraction and non-expansive mappings. The outline of the proof is as follows: first, we show that each iteration of Alg.
1
is the composition of a (firmly) non-expansive mapping and a contraction mapping, which is also a contraction mapping. Second, we show that the iterations of Alg.
1
can be seen as the composition of infinitely many contraction mappings, and such a mapping sends each point in
{\mathcal{F}}
onto its unique representation
\{\boldsymbol{f}_{t}\}
. In other words, the range of the mapping is a singleton. With this we obtain the convergence of Alg.
1
in the arguments. The final step of the proof consists in showing that
\boldsymbol{f}_{t}
belongs to the set of stationary points
{\mathcal{F}}^{*}
of Problem (
10
) when
t\rightarrow\infty
.

(i) Note that the
nth
SLF-iteration of Alg.
1
in Equation (
19
) can be expressed as the evaluation of
T
at
\boldsymbol{f}^{(n-1)}.
Also note that
T_{1}
represents the famous gradient descent algorithm. Given the fact that
g_{1}
is strongly convex and Lipschitz-differentiable with its gradient’s Lipschitz constant
L_{g}=\|A_{\alpha^{(n)}}^{\top}A_{\alpha^{(n)}}+\lambda_{2}\|_{2}
(here
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the spectral norm of a matrix), and that
\gamma\leq(1-\epsilon)/L_{g}
due to algorithmic design with
\epsilon>0
, it can be shown that
T_{1}
is a contraction mapping with contraction factor
c\in[0,1-\epsilon]
, as follows: let
\boldsymbol{f}_{1},~\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\in{\mathcal{F}},
then

\displaystyle\|T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\gamma\nabla_{g_{1}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}+\gamma\nabla_{g_{1}}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}-\gamma\nabla^{2}g_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}})(\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|(\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2})(I-\gamma\nabla^{2}g_{1}({\boldsymbol{z}}))\|

\displaystyle\overset{(b)}{\leq}\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\|(1-L_{g}\gamma)=c\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\|,

where
{\boldsymbol{z}}=\eta\boldsymbol{f}_{1}+(1-\eta)\boldsymbol{f}_{2}
for some
\eta\in[0,1]
. We used in step (a) the mean value theorem of vector calculus (Fact
8
), and step (b) stems from the fact that
\nabla^{2}g_{1}\succeq L_{g}I.

Note that due to Assumption
1

belongs to the compact set
{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\alpha}
. Following similar arguments as with

, we obtain the range of
\mu\in[0,(1-\epsilon)/L_{k}]
, with
L_{k}
being the Lipschitz constant of the gradient of

from Eq. (

, where
\|\cdot\|_{2}
here is the spectral norm of a matrix.

On the other hand, we know from Fact

T_{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{f}_{1}^{\prime}=T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})
and
\boldsymbol{f}_{2}^{\prime}=T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})

\displaystyle\|T_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{1})-T_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{1})-T_{2}(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle+\|(\textup{Id}-T_{2})(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{1})-(\textup{Id}-T_{2})(\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{2})\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\|\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}^{\prime}_{2}\|^{2}.

Thus, we can write

\displaystyle\|T_{2}T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-T_{2}T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|

\displaystyle=\|T(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-T(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq\|T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-T_{1}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|

\displaystyle\leq c\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\|,

which follows from the definition of
T
and from the fact that
T_{1}
is a contraction mapping.

(ii) By induction, we obtain that
F_{n}
is also a contraction mapping, since

\|F_{n}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-F_{n}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|\leq C\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\|,

with

is the composition of
T^{(1)},T^{(2)},...,T^{(n)}
when

\|F_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{f}_{1})-F_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{f}_{2})\|\leq\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\textup{lim}}~\prod_{j=1}^{n}c_{j}~\|\boldsymbol{f}_{1}-\boldsymbol{f}_{2}\|=0,

, and therefore the range of
F_{\infty}
is a singleton.

(iii) Let
\{\boldsymbol{f}_{t}\},~\boldsymbol{f}_{t}\in{\mathcal{F}}
, be the range of
F_{\infty}

is a constant sequence given by

(\forall t\in{\mathbb{N}})~(\boldsymbol{f}^{(n)}_{t})_{n=1}^{\infty}\rightarrow\boldsymbol{f}_{t}\in{\mathcal{F}}

(iv) Consider the function

\displaystyle u_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t},\boldsymbol{f})

\displaystyle\triangleq\frac{1}{t}\sum_{\tau=1}^{t}\frac{1}{2}\|\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}_{\tau}-A_{\boldsymbol{f}}K_{\tau}\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{\tau}\|^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle+\lambda_{1}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{1}+\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{2}\|\boldsymbol{f}\|_{2}^{2}.

It is easy to see that

is a bistable point (Definition

, since
u_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{(N)},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{f}_{t})

\leq u_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{(N)},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{f})~\forall\boldsymbol{f}\in{\mathcal{F}},
and
u_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1}^{(N)},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t}^{(N)},\boldsymbol{f}_{t})\leq u_{t}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t},\boldsymbol{f}_{t})~\forall(\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{1},...,\boldsymbol{\alpha}_{t})\in{\mathcal{C}}_{1}^{\alpha}\times...\times{\mathcal{C}}_{t}^{\alpha}
due to monotonicity
1
1

is strongly convex

.
in the iterates of the inner loop of Alg.

GEMV2
adopts location-specific propagation modeling with respect to large objects in the vicinity of the communicating vehicles such as buildings and foliage. More specifically, the model uses the real-world locations and dimensions of nearby buildings, foliage, and vehicles to determine the
LOS
(
LOS
) or
NLOS
(
NLOS
) conditions for each link. Starting from this premise,
GEMV2
uses simple geographical descriptors of the simulated environment (outlines of buildings, foliage, and vehicles on the road) to classify V2V links into three groups, namely,
LOS
links, NLOSv, i.e., links whose LOS is obstructed by other vehicles; and NLOSb, i.e., links whose LOS is obstructed by building or foliage. Based on this link classification, GEMV2 deterministically calculates the large-scale signal variation (i.e., path loss and shadowing) for each link type, and it adds a fast-fading term based on a particular random distribution. As shown in
[
27
]
, the model fits real-life
V2V
measurements for different urban scenarios well, making the model a good proxy for the evaluation of our algorithm with a realistic path loss dataset.

We collected a dataset of
V2V
received power measurements based on the GEMV2 model for a map located in the Lower-Manhattan area. To generate the dataset, we simulated the communication of only two vehicles moving around the map. The reason behind this is the aim to focus the study on the influence on the path loss coming from free space attenuation and fixed objects, so the scenario remains as static as possible.

Before feeding the training data to the algorithm, the shadowing of each link has to be derived from the dataset of received powers generated by the GEMV2 software. To this end, we first calculate the path loss from the received power values, given by

\mathrm{pl}^{(t)}=p_{\mathrm{tx}}-p^{(l)}_{\mathrm{rx}},

(40)

where
p_{\mathrm{tx}}
is the transmit power, and
p^{(l)}_{\mathrm{rx}}
is the received power in decibels of link index
l
. The shadowing is then obtained from Eq. (
1
) by subtracting the free space path loss given by

\mathrm{pl}^{(t)}_{\mathrm{free}}=\mathrm{pl}_{0}+10\delta\log_{10}\left(\frac{||\mathbf{x}^{(t)}_{i}-\mathbf{x}^{(t)}_{j}||_{2}}{d_{0}}\right)

to the path loss obtained from Eq. (
40
). In our case,
\delta=2.9
and
\mathrm{pl}_{0}=75
dB. The dataset contains 21103 unique measurements representing received powers of links connecting only road locations. The frequency band is
5.89
GHz, transmit power is
12
dBm, and we assign each link to a Tx/Rx pair in a grid of
30\times 22
pixels, with each pixel having
6\times 6
squared meters.

Figure
4
shows the NMSE of the reconstructed shadowing vector
\hat{\boldsymbol{s}}
versus the size of the training dataset, for
r=0
,
r=0.1
and
r=1
. As expected, the larger the training set, the better the performance. However, the improvement between 6330 and 10551 training samples is small, which hints that a reasonable reconstruction performance can be achieved even for small training set sizes. The experiment with

.

VII
Conclusions

In this paper, we have addressed the online learning of path loss maps through a hybrid model and data driven approach. In order to estimate the shadowing experienced by a radio link connecting any two locations in a map, we have formulated a problem to simultaneously obtain an estimate of both the
SLF
and the model from
TPT
. We have considered the elastic net as regularization because the
SLF
is assumed to be group-sparse. The resulting problem is highly ill-posed, so we have added structure by considering a non-linear kernel approach.

We have proposed an online algorithm based on stochastic optimization and alternating minimization to tackle the high complexity of the problem even for small maps, and we have proven the convergence of the online algorithm both in the objective and in the arguments. Finally, we have shown by simulations with synthetic data as well as with realistic data that the proposed method outperforms other state-of-the-art techniques when the data do not fit perfectly the model.
