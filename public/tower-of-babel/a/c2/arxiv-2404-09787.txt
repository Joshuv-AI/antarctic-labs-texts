Extremely high extinction ratio electro-optic modulator via frequency upconversion to visible wavelengths Journal: opticajournal
Alessandra Sabatti, Jost Kellner, Fabian Kaufmann, Robert J. Chapman, Giovanni Finco, Tristan Kuttner, Andreas Maeder, Rachel Grange

Abstract
Intensity modulators are fundamental components for integrated photonics. From near infrared to visible spectral ranges, they find applications in optical communication and quantum technologies. In particular, they are required for the control and manipulation of atomic systems such as atomic clocks and quantum computers. Typical integrated electro-optic modulators operating at these wavelengths show high bandwidth and low voltage operation, but their extinction ratios are moderate. Here we present an integrated thin-film lithium niobate electro-optic modulator operating in the C-band, which uses a subsequent periodically poled waveguide to convert the modulated signal from 1536 nm 1536\text{\,}\mathrm{nm} to 768 nm 768\text{\,}\mathrm{nm} using second harmonic generation. We demonstrate that the upconverted signal retains the characteristics of the modulated input signal, reaching a measured high bandwidth of 40 GHz 40\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz} . Due to the nature of the nonlinear process, it exhibits, with respect to the fundamental signal, a doubled extinction ratio of 46 dB 46\text{\,}\mathrm{dB} , which is the highest recorded for near-infrared light on this platform.

The development of photonics in the spectral range spanning from the visible to the near infrared (NIR) is crucial for numerous applications, including free space communications

, LiDAR

, biosensing

. Moreover, most of the atoms used in ion traps and cold atoms experiments, including calcium, caesium, and rubidium, have transitions in the visible or near-infrared spectrum

One of the key components of integrated photonic circuits are intensity modulators. Besides being largely used in telecommunication, modulators in the visible and NIR range are especially suited for the excitation and control of atomic systems, where the most crucial parameters are large bandwidth and high extinction ratio (ER)
[
12
,
14
]
.
Because of its strong electro-optic coefficient, wide transparency window, and low-loss waveguide architectures, lithium niobate on insulator (LNOI) is particularly well suited for realising such devices.

Visible modulators in LNOI have been demonstrated in an electro-optic (EO) Mach-Zehnder interferometer (MZI) configuration at a wavelength of
738\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
and a bandwidth of
35\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}

[
15
]
, and at
450\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
and a bandwidth of
20\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}

[
16
]
.
However, these devices only achieve ERs of
21\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
and
12\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
respectively, far below the requirements for applications such as control of trapped ions
[
12
]
.

In this work, we present a technique for modulating signals with very high ER. We realised a device that combines high-speed modulation in the C-band by means of an EO MZI, and second harmonic (SH) generation of the modulated signal in the NIR with a periodically poled lithium niobate (PPLN) waveguide
[
17
]
. The high-speed modulation is not performed directly on the visible light, but on the input light in the C-band, at
1536\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
. The SH signal subsequently generated at
768\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
inherits the modulation speed and the half-wave voltage value V
\pi
from the C-band signal, but it shows a doubled ER due to the quadratic dependence of the second harmonic power with respect to the fundamental. With our device, we measure a bandwidth of
40\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and the increase in the ER by a factor of two, from
23\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
in the fundamental to
46\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
in the SH, which to the best of our knowledge is the highest reported value to date
[
18
]
.
This result demonstrates the possibility of realising extremely high ER devices working in the visible and NIR range, for example by frequency doubling the signal from devices that already show ER over
50\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
in C-band
[
19
,
20
]
. Additionally, the wavelength range is not constrained, and by changing the modulator and PPLN design, the same method can be adopted for various wavelengths.

2
Design

Figure
1
(a) illustrates the design and working principle of the electro-optic modulator in combination with the PPLN waveguide for SH generation.
For light coupling to and from the chip, grating couplers at the two design wavelengths of
1550\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
for the fundamental harmonic (FH) signal and
775\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
for the SH signal are used, with coupling efficiencies of
-4\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
and
-7\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
respectively. The waveguide top width is set to
1.15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
for low loss operation in the C-band. The device features two input ports with FH grating couplers. The light is split and recombined with 50:50 directional couplers in an MZI, generating the modulated FH signal (red) as shown in Fig.
1
(a). Each of the two outputs is routed to a
7\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
long PPLN region where the FH signal is converted to the SH signal (purple), inheriting from the modulated FH the V
\pi
and modulation speed with a doubled ER. To independently probe the FH light and the generated SH light, a wavelength division multiplexer (WDM) is placed after the PPLN region. Both FH and SH are coupled out of the chip using the respective grating couplers. The device can be operated using either of the inputs, and the output is chosen such that the MZI is in a cross-port configuration to maximize the ER. The presence of two output ports allows us to select the one with the PPLN waveguide with the quasi-phase matching condition closer to the designed FH of
1550\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
.

We use coplanar waveguide electrodes in a push-pull configuration to modulate the light at the FH wavelength. Using a finite element simulation, the ideal electrode design for index and impedance matching is obtained. For high-frequency bandwidth, we match the group index n
g
at the FH wavelength to the radio frequency (RF) phase velocity index n
RF
at
50\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
. The optimal configuration is found for a
6\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
gap between ground and signal electrodes with a width of
30\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
for the signal electrode. The value of n
g
and n
RF
simulated for our waveguide configuration are
2.19\text{\,}
and
2.17\text{\,}
respectively.

The PPLN period is chosen as
2.48\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
for an etch depth of
235\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
and waveguide top-width of
1.15\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
, with an average initial film thickness of
312\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
. The theoretically achievable nonlinear conversion efficiency is
4280\text{\,}\%\mathrm{W}^{-1}\mathrm{c}\mathrm{m}^{-2}

[
21
]
, whereas, considering a
7\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
long nonlinear region, the ideal conversion efficiency of the full device is
2100\text{\,}\%\mathrm{W}^{-1}
.

3.2
Electro-optical characterisation

The 7.3 mm long MZI modulator is optically characterised in the low-frequency range by applying a constant voltage to the electrodes via ground-signal-ground (GSG) probes (FormFactor ACP40) and acquiring the optical response on a high dynamic range power meter (Keysight N7744C). As illustrated in the measurement setup representation in Fig.
3
(a), an arbitrary waveform generator is used to apply the electrical signal. The FH signal from a tunable laser source (Keysight N7776C) is amplified in an erbium doped fibre amplifier (EDFA, KEOPSYS). An example of such a trace is presented in Fig.
3
(b). The
V_{\pi}
for the FH signal and the upconverted signal is
2.5\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
, measured without termination of the modulator electrodes. The extinction ratio of the FH is
23\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
and the one measured for the SH is
46\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
, exactly double as predicted in the materials and methods section. For illustration purposes, we add the squared signal of the FH response which demonstrates an excellent overlap with the measured SH response.

Figure
3
(c) presents the electro-optic response of our modulator. For this measurement, a vector network analyser (VNA, R&S ZNA) is used together with a high-speed photodetector (PD, Newport 1014) as displayed in Fig.
3
(a). Additionally, we electrically terminate the modulator with 50
\Omega
to prevent undesired electrical back reflections and standing waves. We acquire the response from
20\text{\,}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
to
60\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and measure a bandwidth for the FH beyond our setup capabilities and a bandwidth of
40\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
for the SH. The bandwidth of the SH here is limited by the noise floor of the VNA and not by the working principle. Due to low conversion efficiencies of the PPLN region, the generated SH signal is weak and results in a
33\text{\,}\mathrm{d}\mathrm{B}
lower RF power compared to the FH. Both measurements show the same features and we report a
1\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
difference between the FH and SH signal from
20\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
to
20\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}
in EO response. Since the group index for
300\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
LNOI at visible wavelengths is higher than the RF index, it is hard to index match them, while for the infrared wavelength (FH) it is naturally index matched
[
15
]
. This gives the possibility to make monolithic high-bandwidth modulators for the NIR and visible wavelength range without the limits of index matching or the need for a hybrid platform and complex engineering of the electrodes
[
24
]
.

4
Conclusion

We have presented a LNOI device that modulates a fundamental harmonic (FH) signal in the telecom C-band at
1536\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
with a bandwidth of
60\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
and upconverts the modulated FH signal in an
7\text{\,}\mathrm{mm}
long PPLN region to the second harmonic (SH) signal at a wavelength of
768\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
via the SHG process. The modulated FH shows a measured extinction ratio (ER) of
23\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
, while the ER of the SH signal is doubled due to the nature of the non-linear conversion, achieving a value of
46\text{\,}\mathrm{dB}
with a bandwidth of
40\text{\,}\mathrm{G}\mathrm{H}\mathrm{z}
, limited by the photodiode range. The measured conversion efficiency from the entire device, featuring a
7\text{\,}\mathrm{m}\mathrm{m}
long PPLN region, is
35\text{\,}\%\mathrm{W}^{-1}
. This much lower conversion efficiency is a consequence of the losses experienced by the FH along the poled region of device, and the thin film thickness variations, which cause a change in the phase matching condition in the device. Further engineering of the periodic poling could enable a highly efficient device with strong light outputs at both wavelengths. However, the generated second harmonic signal allows us to measure the modulated signal in the visible with enhanced ER with respect to the fundamental. The concept of the device shows potential in applications such as cold atom traps and atomic optical clocks, where the resulting increase of the ER for the SH signal by a factor of two, due to the nonlinearity, allows for well defined on- and off-states of the light used to manipulate and control atomic level transitions.

5
Materials and methods

5.1
Fabrication

The device is fabricated on a commercially available
300\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
thick,
5\%
magnesium oxide doped lithium niobate thin film on a
2\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}
thick silicon dioxide insulation layer on a silicon handle.
The periodic poling is realized through the application of a high voltage pulse of
520\text{\,}\mathrm{V}
to
100\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
thick Cr electrodes, defined by electron beam lithography (EBL), electron beam evaporation and lift-off. Electrodes are subsequently removed and the optical layer is defined in a second EBL step using hydrogen silsesquioxane resist and etched into the thin-film using an optimised argon etching process
[
25
]
. A cleaning step is then performed with KOH to remove the etching by-products and with buffered HF to remove the remaining mask. The RF electrodes are defined by direct laser writing lithography and standard lift-off process and electron beam evaporation of
850\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
of Au with a
5\text{\,}\mathrm{n}\mathrm{m}
thick Cr adhesion layer.

5.2
Theory

The intensity response of a Mach-Zehnder intensity modulator can be described in the following way:

I_{FH}\left(\Delta\phi\right)=I_{FH,0}\left(r_{1}^{2}t_{2}^{2}+t_{1}^{2}r_{2}^{2}+2r_{1}t_{1}r_{2}t_{2}\cos{\left(2\Delta\phi\right)}\right)

(1)

where
r_{i},t_{i}
are the reflection and transmission coefficients of the first and second directional coupler of the MZI. We can simplify the intensity response to
I_{FH}=I_{FH,0}\cos^{2}{\left(\Delta\phi\right)}+I_{FH,min}
. Depending on these coefficients the extinction ratio is defined by
ER_{FH}=10\log_{10}{\frac{I_{FH,min}}{I_{FH,0}}}
. Without loss of generality we can assume
I_{FH,0}=1
. If the modulated electric field from the modulator is sent to the PPLN region we can describe the intensity of the upconverted signal after the nonlinear interaction length
L

I_{SH}\left(\Delta\phi\right)=\eta L^{2}I_{FH}\left(\Delta\phi\right)^{2}

(2)

where
\eta
is the nonlinear interaction coefficient
[
26
]
. With this knowledge we can now give the equation for the extinction ratio of the SH signal

ER_{SH}=10\log_{10}{\frac{I_{FH,min}^{2}}{I_{FH,0}^{2}}}=20\log_{10}{\frac{I_{FH,min}}{I_{FH,0}}}=2ER_{FH}.

(3)

A similar calculation can be performed for the bandwidth of the electro-optic modulation to verify that the bandwidth of the SH signal is inherited from the FH.
