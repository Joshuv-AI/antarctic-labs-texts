Moore machines duality
Jacques Peyrière

Abstract
. We present a simple algorithm to find the Moore machine with the minimun number of states equivalent to a given one.



1991 Mathematics Subject Classification
2020 classification: 68Q45, 68Q70, 03D05

1.
Introduction

It is known that there is a unique, up to isomorphism, deterministic
finite automaton with a minimum number of states which recognizes the
same language as a given one. As explained in
[
7
]
(pages
29–30), the states of this minimal automaton can be taken as the
classes modulo an equivalence relation on the input words.

Several algorithms to minimize a deterministic finite automaton has
been proposed
[
4
,
5
,
8
]
. According to
[
2
]

these algorithms fall within three categories. Some of them apply as
well to non deterministic finite automata and to Moore machines.

In Section
2.5
we describe an algorithm to minimize a Moore
machine, and establish its consistency. This algorithm lies in the
same family as Brzozowski’s one
[
5
]
. But its description as
well as the proof of its correctness are very simple and do not
require much apparatus. Furthermore, this gives another proof, simple
and elementary, of the existence and uniqueness of a minimum automaton
(see Section 3). In Section 4 we extend these results to morphisms of
free monoids.

2.
Moore machine and duality

Definition 1
.

A Moore machine is a 6-tuple

\mathscr{M}=(Q,\Sigma,\Delta,\delta,{\lambda},{\mathsf{i}})
where

–

Q
is a finite set, the set of states,

–

\Sigma
is a finite set, the input alphabet,

–

\Delta
is a finite set, the output alphabet,

–

\delta
is a mapping from
Q\times\Sigma
to
Q
,
called the transition function,

–

{\lambda}\in\Delta^{Q}
is a mapping from
Q
to
\Delta
,

–

{\mathsf{i}}\in Q
is the initial state.

2.1.
Actions of words on
Q

The set of words on the alphabet
\Sigma
, including the empty
word
\epsilon
, is denoted by
\Sigma^{*}
. The concatenation of the
words
u
and
v
is simply written
uv
.

The transition function
\delta
can be extended in two ways as a
mapping from
Q\times\Sigma^{*}
to
Q
.

Right action.
We define, by recursion on the
length of
w
a mapping, written
(a,w)\mapsto a\!\cdot\!w
, from

Q\times\Sigma^{*}
to
Q
:

–

for
a\in Q
,
a\!\cdot\!\epsilon=a
,

–

for
a\in Q
and
j\in\Sigma
,
a\!\cdot\!j=\delta(a,j)
,

–

a\!\cdot\!wj=(a\!\cdot\!w)\!\cdot\!j
for
a\in Q,\ w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and

j\in\Sigma
.

Left action.
In the same way we define a mapping,
written
(w,a)\mapsto w\!\cdot\!a
, from
\Sigma^{*}\times Q
to
Q
:

–

for
a\in Q
,
\epsilon\!\cdot\!a=a
,

–

for
a\in Q
and
j\in\Sigma
,
j\!\cdot\!a=\delta(a,j)
,

–

jw\!\cdot\!a=j\!\cdot\!(w\!\cdot\!a)
for
a\in Q,\ w\in\Sigma^{*}
, and

j\in\Sigma
.

2.2.
Right and left machines

We can feed
{\mathscr{M}}
with a word in
\Sigma^{*}
on the right or
on the left and get an output:

{\mathscr{M}}\!\cdot\!w={\lambda}({\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)\text{\quad and \quad}w\!\cdot\!{\mathscr{M}}={\lambda}(w\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}}).

If a state does not belong to the set

\{{\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w\ :\ w\in\Sigma^{*}\}
, it is useless and can be
removed. From now on we assume that

G=\{{\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w\ :\ w\in\Sigma^{*}\}
.

Of course, since
w\!\cdot\!{\mathscr{M}}={\mathscr{M}}\!\cdot\!\overline{w}

(where
\overline{w}
stands for the mirror word of
w
) it would be
enough to consider right actions only. But using both actions will
prove convenient in what follows.

We say that two Moore machines
{\mathscr{M}}
and
{\mathscr{M}}^{\prime}
,
with the same
\Sigma
and
\Delta
, are equivalent if, for all

w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
{\mathscr{M}}\!\cdot\!w={\mathscr{M}}^{\prime}\!\cdot\!w
.

Let
{\mathscr{M}}
and
{\mathscr{M}}^{\prime}
be two Moore machines. If there
exists a bijection
\xi
from
Q
onto
Q^{\prime}
such that, for all

a\in Q
and
j\in\Sigma
,
\delta^{\prime}(\xi(a),j)=\xi(\delta(a),j)

and
{\lambda}^{\prime}\bigl(\xi(a)\bigr)={\lambda}(a)
, we say that these
two machines are isomorphic.

2.3.
Duals

Actions of words on functions

If
f
is a function from
Q
to some set and
w\in\Sigma^{*}
we
define two functions
w\!\cdot\!f
and
f\!\cdot\!w
:

\text{for all\quad}a\in Q,\quad(w\!\cdot\!f)(a)=f(a\!\cdot\!w)\text{\quad and\quad}(f\!\cdot\!w)(a)=f(w\!\cdot\!a)

Then
\bigl((vw)\!\cdot\!f\bigr)(a)=f\bigl(a\!\cdot\!(vw)\bigr)=(w\!\cdot\!f)(a\!\cdot\!v)=\bigl(v\!\cdot\!(w\!\cdot\!f)\bigr)(a)
. So,

(vw)\!\cdot\!f=v\!\cdot\!(w\!\cdot\!f)

and similarly

f\!\cdot\!(vw)=(f\!\cdot\!v)\!\cdot\!w

Right dual

This is the Moore machine

{\mathscr{M}}_{\star}=(Q_{\star},\Sigma,\Delta,\delta_{\star},{\lambda}_{\star},{\mathsf{i}}_{\star})

defined as follows:

Q_{\star}=\{w\!\cdot\!\lambda\ :\ w\in\Sigma^{*}\}\subset\Delta^{Q}

\delta_{\star}(f,j)=j\!\cdot\!f

{\lambda}_{\star}\ :\ Q_{\star}\longrightarrow\Delta
so defined:

{\lambda}_{\star}(f)=f({\mathsf{i}})

{\mathsf{i}}_{\star}={\lambda}
.

Then, for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,

{\lambda}_{\star}(w\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}}_{\star})=(w\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}}_{\star})({\mathsf{i}})=(w\!\cdot\!{\lambda})({\mathsf{i}})={\lambda}({\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)
, which means

w\!\cdot\!{\mathscr{M}}_{\star}={\mathscr{M}}\!\cdot\!w

Left dual

We define the machine

\vphantom{\mathscr{M}}{}_{\star}\mathscr{M}=(\vphantom{Q}_{\star}Q,\Sigma,\Delta,\vphantom{\delta}_{\star}\delta,\vphantom{{\lambda}}_{\star}{\lambda},\vphantom{{\mathsf{i}}}_{\star}{\mathsf{i}})
in the same way:

\vphantom{Q}{}_{\star}Q=\{{\lambda}\!\cdot\!w\ :\ w\in\Sigma^{*}\}\subset\Delta^{Q}

\vphantom{\delta}{}_{\star}\delta(f,j)=f\!\cdot\!j

\vphantom{{\lambda}}{}_{\star}{\lambda}\ :\ Q_{\star}\longrightarrow\Delta

so defined:
{\lambda}_{\star}(f)=f({\mathsf{i}})
,

\vphantom{{\mathsf{i}}}{}_{\star}{\mathsf{i}}={\lambda}
.

Then, for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\vphantom{{\lambda}}{}_{\star}{\lambda}(\vphantom{{\mathsf{i}}}_{\star}{\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)=(\vphantom{{\mathsf{i}}}_{\star}{\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)({\mathsf{i}})=({\lambda}\!\cdot\!w)({\mathsf{i}})={\lambda}(w\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}})
, which means

\vphantom{\mathscr{M}}{}_{\star}\mathscr{M}\!\cdot\!w=w\!\cdot\!{\mathscr{M}}

2.4.
Bidual

To avoid cumbersome notation, set

{\mathscr{M}}^{\natural}=\vphantom{({\mathscr{M}}_{\star})}_{\star}({\mathscr{M}}_{\star})
. Then both

\mathscr{M}
and
{\mathscr{M}}^{\natural}
are right machines and are
equivalent in that sense that they give the same outputs.

There is a natural mapping
\tau
from
Q
into
\Delta^{Q_{\star}}
:

let
a\in Q
and
f\in Q_{\star}
, then
f\in\Delta^{Q}
, so

f(a)\in\Delta
; set
\tau_{a}(f)=f(a)
. Then

\tau\,:\,a\mapsto\tau_{a}
is a mapping from
Q

to
\Delta^{Q_{\star}}
.

We have
({\lambda}_{\star}\!\cdot\!w)(f)={\lambda}_{\star}(w\!\cdot\!f)=(w\!\cdot\!f)({\mathsf{i}})=f({\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)=\tau_{{\mathsf{i}}\cdot w}(f)

for all
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
f\in Q_{\star}
. So

{\lambda}_{\star}\!\cdot\!w=\tau_{{\mathsf{i}}\cdot w}\text{\quad for all\quad}w\in\Sigma^{*}.

Since

Q^{\natural}=\{\lambda_{\star}\!\cdot\!w\ :\ w\in\Sigma^{*}\}\subset\Delta^{Q_{\star}}
, then
\tau
maps
Q

onto

{Q}^{\natural}
,
and
\card{Q}^{\natural}\leq\card Q
.

One would like to know when
\card{Q}^{\natural}=\card Q
, that is
when
\tau
is one-to-one. Let
a\in Q
and
b\in Q
. Then

\displaystyle\tau_{a}=\tau_{b}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall f\in Q_{\star},f(a)=f(b)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},(w\!\cdot\!\lambda)(a)=(w\!\cdot\!\lambda)(b)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},{\lambda}(a\!\cdot\!w)={\lambda}(b\!\cdot\!w).

Also, one can consider the mapping
\tau_{\star}
from
Q_{\star}
to

\Delta^{{Q}^{\natural}}
defined in the same way as
\tau
is:

\tau_{\star,a}(f)=f(a)\text{\quad for \quad}a\in Q\_\star\text{ and }f\in Q^{\natural}.

Let
a_{\star}\in Q_{\star}
and
b_{\star}\in Q_{\star}
. Then

\displaystyle\tau_{\star,a_{\star}}=\tau_{\star,b_{\star}}

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall f\in Q^{\natural},f(a_{\star})=f(b_{\star})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},(\lambda_{\star}\!\cdot\!w)(a_{\star})=(\lambda_{\star}\!\cdot\!w)(b_{\star})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},\lambda_{\star}(w\!\cdot\!a_{\star})=\lambda_{\star}(w\!\cdot\!b_{\star})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},(w\!\cdot\!a_{\star})({\mathsf{i}})=(w\!\cdot\!b_{\star})({\mathsf{i}})

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall w\in\Sigma^{*},a_{\star}({\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)=b_{\star}({\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle\forall a\in Q,a_{\star}(a)=b_{\star}(a)

\displaystyle\Longleftrightarrow

\displaystyle a_{\star}=b_{\star}.

This means that
\tau_{\star}
is one-to-one and that

{\mathscr{M}}_{\star}
is equal to its bidual. In the same way

\vphantom{\mathscr{M}}{}_{\star}\mathscr{M}
is equal to its bidual. Therefore

{\mathscr{M}}^{\natural}
is equal to its bidual.

Here
Q=\{{\mathsf{i}},a,b\}
,
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
, and
\Delta=\{0,1\}
. The

whose members are elements of
\Delta^{Q}
. We start with the stack
whose
\lambda
is the only element.We say that an element
\alpha
of

0\!\cdot\!\alpha
and
1\!\cdot\!\alpha
are both
elements of the stack. To add elements to the stack proceed as
follows: starting from the bottom take the first unhappy element,
say
\alpha
, put
0\!\cdot\!\alpha
on the top, set

\delta_{\star}(\alpha,0)=0\!\cdot\!\alpha
, and do the same
with
1\!\cdot\!\alpha
.Repeat until all the elements are happy. Then

Q_{\star}
is the set of elements of the stack and the transition
function is
\delta_{\star}
.

w\!\cdot\!\lambda

{\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w,\,a\!\cdot\!w
, and
b\!\cdot\!w
, the right one
contains
w\!\cdot\!\lambda
, i.e.,

\lambda({\mathsf{i}}\!\cdot\!w),\,\lambda(a\!\cdot\!w)
, and
\lambda(b\!\cdot\!w)

3.
Minimal Moore machine

Here we give another proof of the existence and uniqueness of the
minimal machine.

3.1.
Product of machines

Let

{\mathscr{M}}_{1}=(Q_{1},\Sigma,\Delta_{1},\delta_{1},\lambda_{1},{\mathsf{i}}_{1})

and

{\mathscr{M}}_{2}=(Q_{2},\Sigma,\Delta_{2},\delta_{2},\lambda_{2},{\mathsf{i}}_{2})

be two Moore machines,
\Delta
a finite set, and
\gamma
a mapping
from
\Delta_{1}\times\Delta_{2}
to
\Delta
. We define a new machine

{\mathscr{M}}_{1}\otimes_{\gamma}{\mathscr{M}}_{2}=\bigl(Q,\Sigma,\Delta,\delta,\gamma\circ(\lambda_{1}\times\lambda_{2}),({\mathsf{i}}_{1},{\mathsf{i}}_{2})\bigr)

by setting

Q=\{({\mathsf{i}}_{1}\!\cdot\!w,{\mathsf{i}}_{2}\!\cdot\!w)\ :w\in\Sigma^{*}\}\text{ and }\delta\bigl((a_{1},a_{2}),j\bigr)=\bigl(\delta_{1}(a_{1},j),\delta_{2}(a_{2},j)\bigr).

3.2.
Minimal Moore machine

Let
{\mathscr{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{M}}_{2}
be two equivalent right
machines such that each one is isomorphic to its bidual.

Let
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
be the projections of
Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
onto
Q_{1}

and
Q_{2}
. Consider the machines

{\mathscr{B}}_{k}={\mathscr{M}_{1}}\otimes_{p_{k}}{\mathscr{M}}_{2}
, for

k=1,2
.

For
w\in\sigma^{*}
,
k\in\{1,2\}
, and
(a_{1},a_{2})\in Q_{1}\times Q_{2}
we have

\displaystyle\Bigl(w\!\cdot\!\bigl(p_{k}\circ(\lambda_{1}\times\lambda_{2})\bigr)\Bigg)(a_{1},a_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle p_{k}\circ(\lambda_{1}\times\lambda_{2})(a_{1}\!\cdot\!w,a_{2}\!\cdot\!w)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(a_{k}\!\cdot\!w)=(w\!\cdot\!\lambda_{k})(a_{k}).

This means

w\!\cdot\!\bigl(p_{k}\circ(\lambda_{1}\times\lambda_{2})\bigr)=(w\!\cdot\!\lambda_{y})\circ p_{k}
, which implies

{\mathscr{B}}_{k\star}={\mathscr{M}}_{k\star}
. Therefore

{\mathscr{B}}_{k}^{\natural}={\mathscr{M}}_{k}^{\natural}={\mathscr{M}}_{k}

Because
{\mathscr{M}}_{1}
and
{\mathscr{M}}_{2}
are equivalent we have

\lambda_{1}(a_{1})=\lambda_{2}(a_{2})
for all
(a_{1},a_{2})\in Q
(i.e.,

a_{1}={\mathsf{i}}_{1}\!\cdot\!w
and
a_{2}={\mathsf{i}}_{2}\!\cdot\!w
for some
w\in\Sigma^{*}
). Therefore
{\mathscr{B}}_{1}={\mathscr{B}}_{2}
.

As a conclusion, for any Moore machine, there is a unique (up to
isomorphism) simplest equivalent machine which is the bidual. One
could notice that this algorithm provides a normal form for a Moore
machine.

4.
Substitutions

Consider a 5-tuple

{\mathscr{S}}=(Q,\Delta,\sigma,\lambda,{\mathsf{i}})
where

–

Q
and
\Delta
are finite sets,

–

\sigma
is an endomorphism of the free monoid
Q^{*}

generated by
Q
,

–

\lambda
is a mapping from
Q
to
\Delta
,

–

{\mathsf{i}}\in Q
.

The endomorphism
\sigma
, also called substitution, is determined by
the images of the generators, i.e., by the
words
\{\sigma(a)\}_{a\in Q}
. We adopt the following notation: if

w
is a word on some alphabet,
|w|
stands for its length (i.e., the
number of letters it is made of), and
w_{j}
stands for its
(j+1)
-th
letter from the left (the letters are numbered 0, 1, 2, …).
Let
q=\displaystyle\max_{a\in Q}|\sigma(a)|
.

When
\delta({\mathsf{i}},0)={\mathsf{i}}
, the words

\bigl(\sigma^{n}({\mathsf{i}})\bigr)_{n\geq 0}
have a limit

\sigma^{\infty}({\mathsf{i}})
in
Q^{\mathbb{N}}
. Such sequences as

\lambda\bigl(\sigma^{\infty}({\mathsf{i}})\bigr)
, called projections of
fixed points of substitutions, have been studied in many contexts,
namely for their algebraic, dynamic, and combinatoric
properties
[
1
,
6
,
3
]
.

Constant length substitutions

This is the cas when
|\sigma(a)|=q
for all
a\in Q
. We then set

\Sigma=\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}
and
\delta(a,j)=\sigma(a)_{j}
for

a\in Q
and
0\leq j<q
. So we have a Moore machine:

{\mathscr{M}}=(Q,\Sigma,\Delta,\delta,\lambda,{\mathsf{i}}).

In this context if
k\geq 0
and
n=n_{0}+n_{1}q+\dots n_{k-1}q^{k-1}

(the base
q
expansion of
n
) it is easy to see that the letter of
index
n
in the word
\sigma^{k}(a)
is
n_{0}n_{1}\dots n_{k-1}\!\cdot\!a
,
i.e.,

(1)

\bigl(\sigma^{k}(a)\bigr)_{n}=n_{0}n_{1}\dots n_{k-1}\!\cdot\!a.

This is the connection between Moore machines and substitutions. The
bidual of the left Moore machine
\mathscr{M}
defines a substitution
on a smaller alphabet equivalent to the original one.

Non constant length substitutions

Let
\omega
be a symbol not in
Q
. Set
Q^{\prime}=Q\cup\{\omega\}
and

\Sigma=\{0,1,\dots,q-1\}
. For
0\leq j<q
set

\delta(\omega,j)=\omega
, and for
a\in Q
set

\delta(a,j)=\bigl(\sigma(a)\omega^{\nu}\bigr)_{j}
, where

\nu=q-|\sigma(a)|
.

We just have defined a finite
automaton
\mathscr{A}=(Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\delta,{\mathsf{i}},Q)
, where
{\mathsf{i}}
is
the initial state and
Q
is the set of final states. Let

\mathscr{L}
be the language recognized by
\mathscr{A}
considered as
a left automaton :

{\mathscr{L}}=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\ :\ w\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}}\neq\omega\}
. If we
define
\lambda(\omega)
arbitrarily, we also have a Moore machine,

{\mathscr{M}}=(Q^{\prime},\Sigma,\Delta,\delta,\lambda,{\mathsf{i}})
. Let

{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}={\mathscr{L}}\setminus\{\epsilon\}
. Observe that, if

w\in{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}
, the path leading to
w\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}}
does not
include any
\omega
.

If
w\in{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}
, set

\varphi(w)=\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq j<|w|}w_{j}q^{j}
. Then the
order on
\mathbb{N}
induces a well-order on
{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}
:

v\prec w\Leftrightarrow\varphi(v)<\varphi(w)
. So, we can define

\psi(n)
to be the
n
-th element of
{\mathscr{L}}^{\prime}
(starting
from
0
). Then the counterpart of (
1
) is

\bigl(\sigma^{k}({\mathsf{i}})\bigr)_{j}=\psi(j)\!\cdot\!{\mathsf{i}}

for
k\geq 0
and
0\leq j<|\sigma^{k}({\mathsf{i}})|
.

As previously, the bidual of the left Moore machine
\mathscr{M}

defines a substitution on a smaller alphabet equivalent to the
original one.

Nevertheless, observe that instead of appending some
\omega
’s one
could have padded the
\sigma(a)
’s up to size
q
by
inserting
\omega
’s in various places. Then, of course, the
language
\mathscr{L}
would be different, but the above analysis still
holds.
