Slice closures of indexed languages and word equations with counting constraints
Laura Ciobanu, Georg Zetzsche

Abstract
. Indexed languages are a classical notion in formal language theory. As the language equivalent of second-order pushdown automata, they have received considerable attention in higher-order model checking. Unfortunately, counting properties are notoriously difficult to decide for indexed languages: So far, all results about non-regular counting properties show undecidability. In this paper, we initiate the study of slice closures of (Parikh images of) indexed languages. A slice is a set of vectors of natural numbers such that membership of 𝒖 , 𝒖 + 𝒗 , 𝒖 + 𝒘 \bm{u},\bm{u}+\bm{v},\bm{u}+\bm{w} implies membership of 𝒖 + 𝒗 + 𝒘 \bm{u}+\bm{v}+\bm{w} . Our main result is that given an indexed language L L , one can compute a semilinear representation of the smallest slice containing L L ’s Parikh image. We present two applications. First, one can compute the set of all affine relations satisfied by the Parikh image of an indexed language. In particular, this answers affirmatively a question by Kobayashi: Is it decidable whether in a given indexed language, every word has the same number of a a ’s as b b ’s. As a second application, we show decidability of (systems of) word equations with rational constraints and a class of counting constraints: These allow us to look for solutions where a counting function (defined by an automaton) is not zero. For example, one can decide whether a word equation with rational constraints has a solution where the number of occurrences of a a differs between variables X X and Y Y .

Introduction

Indexed languages, introduced in the late 1960s by
Aho
Aho, 1968
, are a classical notion in formal language
theory. They significantly extend context-free languages, but retain
decidability of basic properties such as emptiness. Until the beginning of the
1990s, they have been studied with respect to equivalence with second-order
pushdown automata and safe second-order recursion
schemes

;
Damm, 1982
;
Damm & Goerdt, 1986

Higher-order model checking

Over the last two decades, indexed languages have seen renewed interest because of their role in
higher-order model checking
: In this approach to verification, one models the behavior of a functional program using a
higher-order recursion scheme
(HORS), for example using Kobayashi’s translation
Kobayashi, 2009
. A HORS generates a (potentially infinite) tree, but may also have a semantics as a finite-word language. The translation into HORS enables the application of a wealth of algorithmic tools available for HORS. Here, a landmark result is the decidability of monadic second-order logic for HORS by Ong
Ong, 2006
(with alternative proofs by Kobayashi & Ong
Kobayashi & Ong, 2009
, Hague, Murawski, Ong, and Serre

Zetzsche, 2015
;
Hague et al., 2016
;

Counting properties

Despite this rich set of algorithmic
results, HORS are notoriously difficult to analyze with respect to

counting (without an apriori bound) of letters. So far, all results about

To mention a particularly basic property: it is undecidable whether

In fact, even inclusion in the Dyck language (the set of all

Given this state of

Slices

In this paper, we identify slices as a surprisingly
useful concept for analyzing indexed languages. Introduced by Eilenberg and
Schützenberger in 1969
Eilenberg & Schützenberger, 1969
, a
slice
is a
subset
S
of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that if the vectors
\bm{u}
,
\bm{u}+\bm{v}
, and
\bm{u}+\bm{w}

belong to
S
, so does
\bm{u}+\bm{v}+\bm{w}
. Eilenberg and Schützenberger proved
that every slice is a semilinear set
Eilenberg & Schützenberger, 1969
, Prop. 7.3

(an alternative proof, partly inspired by Hirshfeld’s proof of the case of
congruences
Hirshfeld, 1994
, can be found
in
Ganardi et al., 2022
). Eilenberg and Schützenberger
deduce from this their much more well-known result that every congruence on

\mathbb{N}^{k}
is semilinear. Recall that an equivalence relation
\equiv
on
\mathbb{N}^{k}
is
a
congruence
if
\bm{u}\equiv\bm{v}
and
\bm{u}^{\prime}\equiv\bm{v}^{\prime}
, then

\bm{u}+\bm{u}^{\prime}\equiv\bm{v}+\bm{v}^{\prime}
. When viewed as a subset of
\mathbb{N}^{k}\times\mathbb{N}^{k}
, it is
easy to see that every congruence is a slice in
\mathbb{N}^{k}\times\mathbb{N}^{k}
.

The semilinearity of congruences (for which alternative proofs are by
Redei

, Freyd
Freyd, 1968
, and
Hirshfeld
Hirshfeld, 1994
) has been used in general
decidability results for bisimulation in Petri
nets

, implies semilinearity of mutual
reachability relations in Petri
nets

;
Leroux, 2022
, and
has a well-studied connection to polynomial
ideals

;
Koppenhagen & Mayr, 1999
;
Koppenhagen & Mayr, 2000
;
Koppenhagen & Mayr, 2001
.
Although slices are apparently more general than congruences and share
the properties of semilinearity and the ascending chain
condition

, slices have received surprisingly
little attention: We are only aware of Eilenberg and
Schützenberger

, results by Grabowski on
effectively semilinear slices

, and an
algorithm for reachability in bidirected pushdown

.

Contribution

Our main result is Theorem
1
, which shows that given an indexed language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, one can
effectively compute a semilinear representation of the smallest slice
containing
\Psi(L)\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
(with
k=|\Sigma|
), the Parikh image of

L
. Note that since the intersection of any collection of slices is again a
slice, every set
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
has a smallest slice containing
K
. We
call this the
slice closure
of
K
and denote it
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(K)
.
Hence, we show (in Section
3
) that for every indexed
language
L
, its slice closure
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(L))
is effectively semilinear.

Theorem 1.

The slice closure of each indexed language is effectively semilinear.

We thus obtain an effectively semilinear overapproximation of
\Psi(L)
that
is amenable to algorithmic analysis (recall that semilinear sets are precisely
those expressible in Presburger arithmetic).

Moreover, this overapproximation preserves pertinent counting properties of

L
, meaning that
Theorem

1
yields (to our knowledge) the first
(non-regular) decidable counting properties of indexed languages:

Corollary 2.

If
L
is an indexed language on
\{a,b\}
given by an indexed grammar, it is decidable whether every word has the same number of
a
’s as
b
’s.

To prove this, the key fact is that the vectors in

\Psi(L)
all satisfy a system of linear equations over the integers if and only if
the vectors in
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(L))
do. One implication is clear. For the other direction, suppose that all vectors in
\Psi(L)
satisfy the system of linear equations
A\bm{x}=\bm{b}
with
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times k}
,
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
. Since
the set
S
of solutions to this system in
\mathbb{N}^{k}
is a slice (see Example
1
) and
\Psi(L)\subseteq S
, we must have
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(L))\subseteq S
by the minimality of
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(L))
; thus the vectors in
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(L))
satisfy the system
A\bm{x}=\bm{b}
. Therefore, in particular, we give a positive answer to
Kobayashi’s question and prove
Corollary

2
: One can decide the inclusion (
1
)
by checking whether
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(L))\subseteq\{(m,m)\mid m\in\mathbb{N}\}
.

Combined with a result by Karr
Karr, 1976

(see
Müller-Olm & Seidl, 2004
, Theorem 1
for a simpler and more
explicit proof),
Theorem

1
also implies that we can compute the “strongest affine invariant” of

\Psi(L)
.
An
affine relation
is a vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
and a number
c\in\mathbb{Z}
,
and it is
satisfied by
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
if
\bm{u}^{\top}\bm{x}=c
for every

\bm{x}\in K
.
The (rational)
affine hull
of a set
U\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{k}
is the set

\mathsf{aff}(U)=\{\lambda_{1}\bm{u}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}\bm{u}_{n}\mid n\geq 0,~\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{n}\in U,\\
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{Q},~\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{n}=1\}.

A non-empty set
A\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{k}
is an
affine space
if
\mathsf{aff}(A)=A
. It
is a basic fact in linear algebra that affine spaces are exactly those subsets
of the form
\bm{v}+V
, where
\bm{v}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}
and
V\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{k}
is a linear
subspace. Equivalently, affine spaces are exactly the non-empty solution sets
in
\mathbb{Q}^{k}
to equation systems
B\bm{x}=\bm{c}
with
B\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times k}
and

\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
. By
computing
\mathsf{aff}(U)
for some given set
U\subseteq\mathbb{Q}^{k}

we mean computing some
B\in\mathbb{Q}^{m\times k}
and
\bm{c}\in\mathbb{Q}^{m}
such that

\mathsf{aff}(U)=\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Q}^{k}\mid B\bm{x}=\bm{c}\}
. Some authors call this equation system the

strongest affine invariant
of
U
, meaning a finite set of affine relations satisfied by

\Psi(L)
that implies every affine relation satisfied by
\Psi(L)
.

Corollary 3.

Given an indexed language
L
, we can effectively compute
\mathsf{aff}(\Psi(L))
.

We derive
Corollary

3
from
Theorem

1
in
Section

2
.

Word equations with counting constraints

We also present an application of
Theorem

1
to word equations.
Let
A
and
\Omega
be finite alphabets, where we consider
\Omega=\{X_{1},\dots,X_{n}\}
as a collection of
variables and
A
as letters. A
word equation
over
(A,\Omega)
is a
pair
(U,V)
with
U,V\in(A\cup\Omega)^{*}
, where
U
and
V
should be seen as the two sides
of an equation. The core question is then whether the equation has any solutions, that is, whether we can substitute the variable occurrences in
U

and
V
by words over
A
so that the two sides become equal.
More formally, a solution to
(U,V)
is a morphism
\sigma:(A\cup\Omega)^{*}\to A^{*}
that fixes each
a\in A
such that
\sigma(U)=\sigma(V)
.

Example 4.

Let
A=\{a,b\}
and
\Omega=\{X,Y,Z\}
. The pair of words
(X^{2}aY,YZ^{2}a)
represents the word equation
X^{2}aY=YZ^{2}a
. One possible solution is
\sigma(X)=ab,\sigma(Y)=a,\sigma(Z)=ba
.

The study of word equations has a long history. Decidability (of solvability) was shown by Makanin
Makanin, 1977
in 1977, and a
\mathsf{PSPACE}
upper bound by Plandowski
Plandowski, 2004
. This was extended to word equations with
rational constraints
by Diekert, Gutiérrez, and Hagenah
Diekert et al., 2005
.

However, imposing
counting constraints
, e.g. that the number of
a
’s in
X_{1}
is the same as in
X_{2}
, makes word equations much more difficult. In a parallel to indexed languages, allowing such conditions without restrictions (we make this more precise in
Section

4
) leads to undecidability, as shown by Büchi and Senger
Büchi & Senger, 1990
. Whether word equations with rational constraints and
length equations
, which asks e.g. that
X_{1}
have the same length as
X_{2}
, are decidable remains a major open problem
Ganesh et al., 2012
, even in the case of quadratic equations
Lin & Majumdar, 2021
. More generally, despite a lot of recent interest in extensions of word equations, most results show undecidability. Moreover, there is no decidability result that extends general word equations with rational constraints and any kind of counting constraints. See
Amadini, 2021
;
Lin & Majumdar, 2021
;
Day et al., 2018
for recent surveys.

Our application of
Theorem

1
introduces a class of counting constraints—we call them
counting inequations
—for which word equations (with rational constraints) remain decidable:

Theorem 5.

The problem of word equations with rational constraints and counting inequations is decidable.

Here, we rely on a recent result by Ciobanu, Diekert, and
Elder
Ciobanu et al., 2015
saying that solution sets of word
equations (with rational constraints) are EDT0L, and thus indexed languages.
For example, it is decidable whether there is a solution
\sigma
to a word
equation
(U,V)
such that
|\sigma(X_{1})|_{a}\neq|\sigma(X_{2})|_{a}
. To our
knowledge, this is the first strict extension of word equations with rational
constraints by any kind of counting constraints. We prove

Theorem

5
in
Section

4
.

Key ingredients

Our algorithm for
Theorem

1
is a
saturation procedure
where for some monotone operator
F
, we compute a fixpoint of
F
by applying
F
repeatedly. By relying on an ascending chain condition, such algorithms terminate with a fixpoint after finitely many steps.

Saturation is a ubiquitous idea, but closest to our approach are algorithms where the ascending chain condition comes from (i) dimension of vector spaces or affine spaces, (ii) Hilbert’s Basis Theorem, which implies that chains of polynomial ideals become stationary, (iii) well-quasi-orderings, where chains of upward closed sets become stationary. Examples of (i) are Eilenberg’s algorithm for equivalence of weighted automata over fields
Eilenberg, 1974
, p.143–145
and Karr’s computation of strongest affine invariants
Karr, 1976
(see also
Müller-Olm & Seidl, 2004
and the stronger result
Hrushovski et al., 2023
) and applications
Harju & Karhumäki, 1991
. Examples for (ii) are the algorithm for polynomial invariants of affine programs
Müller-Olm & Seidl, 2004a
, several algorithms for equivalence of transducers or register machines
Benedikt et al., 2017
;
Seidl et al., 2015
;
Filiot & Reynier, 2017
;
Boiret et al., 2018
(see
Bojańczyk, 2019
for a unifying result). An example of (iii) is the classical backward search algorithm to decide coverability in well-structured transition systems
Finkel & Schnoebelen, 2001
;
Abdulla et al., 1996
. Saturation over finite domains—also an example of (iii)—is used prominently in the analysis of pushdown systems
Bouajjani et al., 1997
.
(Some algorithms do not compute an ascending chain, but rather guess a fixpoint of
F
, e.g.
Chistikov et al., 2022
;
Ganardi et al., 2022
;
Bojańczyk, 2019
;
Blondin et al., 2018
).

The key insight of this work is that the slice closure

(iii), this is obvious, because the upward closure of indexed languages does
not preserve any precise counting properties

A fixpoint characterization

On the technical level, one ingredient is a fixpoint characterization

In fact, the proof could easily be modified to yield a

This fixpoint characterization—via an operator called

2.
Basic notions: Slices and indexed grammars

Notation

For a finite alphabet
\Sigma
, we write
\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
for the set of maps

\Sigma\to\mathbb{N}
. We will consider
\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
as a commutative monoid with
pointwise addition. If
|\Sigma|=k
, then
\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
is clearly isomorphic to

\mathbb{N}^{k}
and so we will often transfer notation and terminology from one to the
other (in case the chosen isomorphism doesn’t matter). Moreover, we identify
each element
a\in\Sigma
with the element
\bm{u}_{a}\in\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
with

\bm{u}_{a}(a)=1
and
\bm{u}_{a}(b)=0
for all
b\in\Sigma\setminus\{a\}
. For each
finite alphabet
\Sigma
, the
Parikh map

\Psi\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{N}^{\Sigma}
is the map with
\Psi(w)(a)=|w|_{a}

for
w\in\Sigma^{*}
and
a\in\Sigma
, where
|w|_{a}
is the number of occurrences
of
a
in the word
w
.
For a set
S
, we denote its powerset by
\mathbb{P}(S)
.

Semilinear sets

A subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
is
linear
if there is a vector
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and a finite subset
P=\{\bm{p}_{1},\ldots,\bm{p}_{r}\}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
such that
K=\bm{b}+\langle P\rangle
, where we use the notation

\bm{b}+\langle P\rangle=\{\bm{b}+\lambda_{1}\bm{p}_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{r}\bm{p}_{r}\mid\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{r}\in\mathbb{N}\}.

A subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
is
semilinear
if it is a finite union of linear sets.
In particular, a semilinear set has a
semilinear representation

R=(\bm{b}_{1},P_{1};\ldots,\bm{b}_{n},P_{n})
consisting of vectors
\bm{b}_{1},\ldots,\bm{b}_{n}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
and finite subsets
P_{1},\ldots,P_{n}\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
. The set
represented by
R
, denoted
\langle R\rangle
, is then
\bm{b}_{1}+\langle P_{1}\rangle\cup\cdots\cup\bm{b}_{n}+\langle P_{n}\rangle
.

In this work, we distinguish between (i) sets for which we
can prove semilinearity (potentially non-constructively) and (ii) sets for
which we can even
compute
a semilinear representation algorithmically,
from the available data. In the latter case, we speak of
effectively
semilinear sets
. When we use this terminology, it will always be clear from
the context what the available data is (e.g. an indexed grammar, a semilinear
representation of a related set, etc.).

Presburger arithmetic
is the first-order theory of
(\mathbb{N},+)
, the natural numbers with addition. It is well-known that a subset of
\mathbb{N}^{k}
is definable in Presburger arithmetic if and only if it is semilinear
Ginsburg & Spanier, 1966
, Theorem 1.3
. This equivalence is effective, meaning we can algorithmically translate between Presburger formulas and semilinear representations.

Slices and slice closures

A subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
is a
slice
if for any
\bm{u},\bm{u}+\bm{v},\bm{u}+\bm{w}\in K
with
\bm{u},\bm{v},\bm{w}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
, we also have
\bm{u}+\bm{v}+\bm{w}\in K
. We also say that

\bm{u}+\bm{v}+\bm{w}
is
obtained from
\bm{u},\bm{u}+\bm{v},\bm{u}+\bm{w}
using the slice
operation
. Clearly, the intersection of any collection of slices is also a slice. Thus, for every subset
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
, there is a smallest slice that includes
K
. We call it the
slice closure of
K
and denote it by
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(K)
.

Example 1.

The following are three kinds of examples of slices.

(i)

The first is
solution sets to linear diophantine equations
: For a matrix
A\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times k}
and a vector
\bm{b}\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, the solution set
\{\bm{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\mid A\bm{x}=\bm{b}\}
is a slice: If
\bm{u},\bm{u}+\bm{v},\bm{u}+\bm{w}
are solutions, then clearly
A\bm{v}=\bm{0}
and
A\bm{w}=\bm{0}
and thus
A(\bm{u}+\bm{v}+\bm{w})=\bm{b}
.

(ii)

Another example is
upward closed sets
: We say that
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
is
upward closed
if
\bm{u}\in K
and
\bm{u}\leq\bm{v}
imply
\bm{v}\in K
, where
\leq
is component-wise inequality. Clearly, every upward closed set is a slice.

(iii)

Third, subtractive monoids are slices: we say that
K\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
is a
subtractive submonoid
if
\bm{u},\bm{u}+\bm{v}\in K
imply that
\bm{v}\in K
.

We will rely on two facts about slices by Eilenberg and Schützenberger. The first is that every slice is semilinear:

Theorem 2 (Eilenberg & Schützenberger).

Every slice is semilinear.

This is shown in
Eilenberg & Schützenberger, 1969
, Proposition 7.3
(see
also
Ganardi et al., 2022
for an alternative proof).
Note that whether a given slice is
effectively
semilinear depends on how it is represented.
In fact, there are many situations in which one cannot compute a semilinear representation for a given slice. For example, the set of reachable configurations in a lossy counter system is always a slice (since it is downward closed), but it cannot be computed for a given lossy counter system
Mayr, 2003
, Remark 14
.
We will also rely on the ascending chain condition:

Proposition 3 (Eilenberg Schützenberger).

If
S_{1}\subseteq S_{2}\subseteq\cdots\subseteq\mathbb{N}^{k}
are slices, then
there is an index
m
with
S_{m}=\bigcup_{i\geq 0}S_{i}
.

This was also shown by Eilenberg and Schützenberger
Eilenberg & Schützenberger, 1969

3.2.
Slices as fixpoints

It will be helpful to view slice closures as fixpoints, by way of the

To simplify notation, we apply much of the terminology about subsets of

A set tuple

3.3.
Computing slice closures

In our algorithm to compute the slice closure of indexed languages, we will proceed in two steps. We will first compute the slice closure of the tuple
\Delta(G)
, which contains all Parikh images of sentential forms of

from the slice closure of

can be reconstructed from the slice closure of

. More specifically, we can compute it by way of

, because of Lemma

. We use the convention that for any set

, the intersection

refers to the tuples where all the coordinates indexed by

are zero.

Lemma 5.

For every set

, we have
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(U\cap\mathbb{N}^{T})=\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(U)\cap\mathbb{N}^{T}
.

Proof.

The inclusion “
\subseteq
” holds because
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(U\cap\mathbb{N}^{T})
is
clearly included in
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(U)
and in
\mathbb{N}^{T}
.

For “

\mathcal{S}(U)\cap\mathbb{N}^{T}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(U\cap\mathbb{N}^{T})
. Indeed, for each element

added by

with

, the vectors
\bm{u}
,

\bm{u}+\bm{v}

\bm{u}+\bm{w}

Slice closure vs. other closure operators

This is a good moment to highlight why the notion of the slice closure is so important in our proofs—even if one just wants to show
Corollary

3
: One might wonder whether

could be obtained by somehow computing the affine hull of

and then use that to compute the affine hull of

\Delta(G)_{S}
is not enough, even for just computing the affine hull of

.

We even show something slightly stronger: Even if we could compute the Zariski closure (which encodes more information than the affine hull) of
\Delta(G)_{S}
, this would not contain enough information to reconstruct the affine hull of
\Psi(L)
. Let us first define the Zariski closure. A subset

is an

if it is of the form

where

are finitely many polynomials. It
is a simple consequence of Hilbert’s Basis Theorem that the intersection of any
collection of affine varieties is again an affine variety. Therefore, we can
assign to every subset

the smallest affine variety including

U
, denoted
\overline{U}
. The closure
\overline{U}
is also called the

of

.

Suppose we have

and

. Then we identify
\mathbb{N}^{N\cup T}
with
\mathbb{N}^{3}
, where the left-most entry corresponds to

, the second to

, and the third to

. Consider the set

We claim that then for every subset

, we have
\overline{U\cup W}=\overline{U}
. This is because every polynomial

that vanishes on

must also vanish on

: for any

, we know that

must have every positive natural number as a root and thus be identically zero; meaning

also vanishes on

. However, this means

does not encode enough information to recover anything about

.
Lemma

Word equations with counting constraints

Let
\Omega
be a finite collection of
variables and
A
a finite set of letters, and consider the word equation
(U,V)
over
(A,\Omega)
.
Recall that a
solution
to
(U,V)
is a morphism

\sigma\colon(\Omega\cup A)^{*}\to A^{*}
that fixes
A
point-wise and such that
\sigma(U)=\sigma(V)
.

A
word equation with rational constraints
is a word equation
(U,V)

together with a regular language
R_{X}\subseteq A^{*}
for each variable

X\in\Omega
. In this situation, we say that
\sigma\colon\Omega\to A^{*}
is a

solution
if it is a solution to the word equation
(U,V)
and also
satisfies
\sigma(X)\in R_{X}
for each
X\in\Omega
.

Representing solutions

We will rely on a result that provides a language-theoretic description of the set of solutions of an equation, which requires a representation as words. Given a map
\sigma\colon\Omega\to A^{*}
with
\Omega=\{X_{1},\ldots,X_{k}\}
, we define

\mathsf{enc}(\sigma)~=~\sigma(X_{1})\#\cdots\#\sigma(X_{k}),

where
\#
is a fresh letter not in
A
. Hence,
\mathsf{enc}(\sigma)\in(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}
.

Counting functions

Counting constraints will be expressed using counting functions, which are functions

f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
that are rational when viewed as a relation
\subseteq(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}
.
A
counting transducer
is a finite automaton
\mathcal{T}=(Q,\Sigma,E,q_{0},F)
, where
Q
is a finite set of
states
,
\Sigma
is its
input alphabet
,
E\subseteq Q\times\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}\times Q
is its
edge relation
,
q_{0}\in Q
is its
initial state
, and
F\subseteq Q
is its set of
final states
. A
run
is a sequence

q_{0}(w_{1},\bm{x}_{1})q_{1}\cdots(w_{m},\bm{x}_{m})q_{m}

with
q_{0},\ldots,q_{m}\in Q
,
w_{1},\ldots,w_{m}\in\Sigma^{*}
, and
\bm{x}_{1},\ldots,\bm{x}_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that each
(q_{i},w_{i+1},\bm{x}_{i+1},q_{i+1})\in E
, and
q_{m}\in F
. The relation
defined
by the counting transducer, denoted
R(\mathcal{T})
is the set of all pairs
(w_{1}\cdots w_{m},\bm{x}_{1}+\cdots+\bm{x}_{m})\in\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}
for runs as above. The counting transducer is called
functional
if for every word
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, there is at most one vector
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that
(w,\bm{x})\in R(\mathcal{T})
. A function
f\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called a
counting function
if its graph
\{(w,f(w))\mid w\in\Sigma^{*}\}\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is defined by a functional counting transducer. In particular, the graph of
f
is a rational subset of
\Sigma^{*}\times\mathbb{Z}^{n}

We will apply counting functions to encodings of solutions. Let us see some examples.

(1)

Letter counting: The function
f_{X,a}
with
f_{X,a}(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=|\sigma(X)|_{a}
for some letter
a\in A
and variable
X\in\Omega
. Here, the transducer increments the counter for each
a
between the
i
-th and
(i+1)
-st occurrence of
\#
, where
X=X_{i}
.

(2)

Counting positions with MSO properties. Consider monadic second-order logic (MSO) formulas evaluated in the usual way over finite words. Suppose we have an MSO formula
\varphi(x)
with one free first-order variable
x
. Then we can define the function
f_{\varphi}
such that
f_{\varphi}(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))
is the number of positions
x
in
\mathsf{enc}(\sigma)
where
\varphi(x)
is satisfied. Then
f_{\varphi}
is a counting function, which follows from the fact that MSO formulas define regular languages. For example, we could count the number of
a
’s such that there is no
c
between the
a
and the closest even-distance
b
.

(3)

Linear combinations: If
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}\colon\Sigma^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
are counting functions, then so is
f
with
f(w)=\lambda_{1}f_{1}(w)+\cdots+\lambda_{m}f_{m}(w)
for some
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
. This can be shown with a simple product construction.

(4)

Length function: The function
L_{X}
with
L_{X}(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=|\sigma(X)|
for some
X\in\Omega
. For this, we can take a linear combination of letter counting functions.

The following is the problem of
word equations with rational and counting constraints
:

Given:

A word equation
(U,V)
with rational constraints and a counting function
f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Question:

Is there a solution
\sigma
that satisfies
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=0
?

As Büchi and Senger showed
Büchi & Senger, 1990
, Corollary 4
,
this problem in full generality is undecidable. However, it remains a well-known open problem
whether this problems is decidable when we restrict to
length
constraints
. We say that
f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a
length
constraint
if each projection to a component of
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is a linear combination
of length functions as in
Item

4
above. Thus, a length
constraint can express that
\sigma(X)
has the same length as
\sigma(Y)
for

X,Y\in\Omega
. The problem of
word equations with rational and length
constraints
is the following:

Given:

A word equation
(U,V)
with rational constraints and a length constraint
f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Question:

Is there a solution
\sigma
that satisfies
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=\bm{0}
?

We will show here that a restriction of word equations with rational
constraints and counting constraints is decidable. In this restriction, we
require
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))\neq\bm{0}
rather than
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=\bm{0}
. The
problem of
word equations with rational constraints and counting
inequations
is the following:

Given:

A word equation
(U,V)
with rational constraints and a counting function
f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}

Question:

Is there a solution
\sigma
that satisfies
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))\neq\bm{0}
?

Here, we show that word equations with rational constraints and counting inequations are decidable (
Theorem

5
).

Before we come to the proof, let us mention that
Theorem

5
yields a decidable fragment of word equations with length constraints. This is because in the special case that
f
is a length constraint, the inequation
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))\neq\bm{0}
can be expressed using length
equations
by standard tricks: Suppose
f=\lambda_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{m}f_{m}
is a linear combination of length functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{m}
, with
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}>0
and
\lambda_{k+1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}<0
, where
f_{i}(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=|\sigma(X_{i})|
for variable
X_{i}\in\Omega
. Then
\sigma
is a solution with
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))\neq\bm{0}
if and only if there is a letter
a\in A
and a system with

\displaystyle X=X_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots X_{k}^{\lambda_{k}},

\displaystyle Y=X_{k+1}^{|\lambda_{k+1}|}\cdots X_{m}^{|\lambda_{m}|},

\displaystyle{|X|=|YaZ}|

or

\displaystyle X=X_{1}^{\lambda_{1}}\cdots X_{k}^{\lambda_{k}},

\displaystyle Y=X_{k+1}^{|\lambda_{k+1}|}\cdots X_{m}^{|\lambda_{m}|},

\displaystyle|Y|=|XaZ|,

depending on whether
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))>\bm{0}
or
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))<\bm{0}
. where
X,Y,Z
are fresh variables. This can easily be expressed by word
equations with length constraints (relying on the standard fact that several
equations for words can be translated into a single equation). Thus,

Theorem

5
yields in particular a decidable
non-trivial fragment of word equations with length constraints.
Similarly, counting inequations can always be encoded in word equations with counting constraints.

One can view
Theorem

5
as showing that it is
decidable whether
all solutions

\sigma
to a word equation
(U,V)
with
rational constraints satisfy
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=\bm{0}
for some given counting
function
f
. One might wonder whether in all these cases, satisfaction of

f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=\bm{0}
for all solutions
\sigma
is already implied by the
equality
\Psi(\sigma(U))=\Psi(\sigma(V))
and the rational constraints.
The following example shows that this is not the case.

Example 1.

Suppose
\Omega=\{X_{1},X_{2},X_{3}\}
,
A=\{a,b,c\}
, and consider the word equation

abX_{1}cX_{1}ba=X_{1}abcX_{2}X_{3}

with rational constraints
X_{1},X_{3}\in\{a,b\}^{*}
, and

X_{2}\in\{ab\}^{*}ba
. Under these constraints, the equation is equivalent
to the pair of equations
abX_{1}=X_{1}ab
and
X_{1}ba=X_{2}X_{3}
. It is a
basic fact from word combinatorics (see e.g.
Berstel, 1979
, Exercise
2.5
) that the equation
abX_{1}=X_{1}ab
is equivalent to

X_{1}\in\{ab\}^{*}
. This means,
X_{1}ba=X_{2}X_{3}
and
X_{2}\in\{ab\}^{*}ba

imply
|X_{3}|=0
. Thus, the length constraint

f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}
with
f(u\#v\#w)=|w|
satisfies

f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=0
for every solution
\sigma
. However, if we
take the equation

abX_{1}cX_{1}ba=abX_{1}cX_{2}X_{3}

instead (with the same rational constraints), then
abX_{1}=abX_{1}
is
satisfied for any
X_{1}
. In particular, for every
k\geq 1
, we have a
solution
\sigma
with
\sigma(X_{1})=ab(ba)^{k}
,
\sigma(X_{2})=abba
,

\sigma(X_{3})=(ba)^{k}
, where
f(\mathsf{enc}(\sigma))=2k
. This shows that the
satisfaction the satisfaction of counting constraints for all solutions
of (U,V) doesn’t depend only on
(\Psi(U),\Psi(V))
and the
rational constraints imposed on the variables.

The remainder of this section is devoted to proving

Theorem

5
.

Transductions

First, we need a standard notion from language theory. A
finite-state
transducer
is defined like a counting transducer, except that instead of the

\mathbb{Z}^{n}
component on the edges, we have words over some output alphabet

\Gamma^{*}
. It recognizes a relation
R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
which
is defined in the same way as for counting transducers (except that instead of
adding output vectors, we concatenate output words). A relation

R\subseteq\Sigma^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
is
rational
if it is recognized by
some finite-state transducer. For a language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, we define

RL:=\{v\in\Gamma^{*}\mid\exists(u,v)\in R\colon u\in L\}
. It is well-known
that indexed languages are (effectively) closed under applying rational
transductions. In other words, for every indexed language
L
, we can compute
an indexed grammar for the language
RL
.

Solution sets of word equations

Our proof crucially relies on a structural description of solution sets of word
equations with rational constraints from
Ciobanu et al., 2015
, Theorem
1
:

Theorem 2 (Ciobanu, Elder, Diekert 2015).

For every word equation
(U,V)
with rational constraints, the language

(9)

\{\mathsf{enc}(\sigma)\mid\text{$\sigma\colon\Omega\to A^{*}$ is a solution}\}

is an EDT0L language.

Here, we do not need the exact definition of an EDT0L language, it suffices to
know that the EDT0L languages are (effectively) a subclass of the indexed
languages. This allows us to prove
Theorem

5
.

Proof of
Theorem

5
.

Given a word equation
(U,V)
with rational constraints and a counting function
f\colon(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\to\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, we first construct an indexed grammar for the language
Eq.

9
, which we denote
L
. Next, from
f
, we construct a finite-state transducer for a relation
R_{f}\subseteq(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}\times\Gamma^{*}
with
\Gamma=\{a_{1},\bar{a}_{1},\ldots,a_{n},\bar{a}_{n}\}
such that for every
u\in(A\cup\{\#\})^{*}
, there is a word
v\in\Gamma^{*}
such that

f(u)=(|v|_{a_{1}}-|v|_{\bar{a}_{1}},\ldots,|v|_{a_{n}}-|v|_{\bar{a}_{n}})

and
(u,v)\in R_{f}
. This is easy: Given the counting transducer for
f
, we just
turn each edge labeled
(w,(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}))
into one labeled

(w,a_{1}^{(x_{1})}\cdots a_{n}^{(x_{n})})
, where
a_{i}^{(x_{i})}
is defined as
a_{i}^{x_{i}}
if

x_{i}\geq 0
and as
\bar{a}_{i}^{|x_{i}|}
if
x_{i}<0
. Now clearly, our instance of word equations with rational constraints and counting inequations is
negative
if and only if
\Psi(R_{f}L)\subseteq S
, where

S=\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{N}^{\Gamma}\mid\bm{z}(a_{1})=\bm{z}(\bar{a}_{1}),~\ldots,~\bm{z}(a_{n})=\bm{z}(\bar{a}_{n})\}.

Notice that
S
is a slice, since it is the solution set of a homogeneous
linear equation. Therefore, our instance is negative if and only if

\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(R_{f}L))\subseteq S
, which we can decide because we can
construct an indexed grammar for
R_{f}L
and
Theorem

1
allows us to
compute a Presburger formula for
\mathcal{S}^{\omega}(\Psi(R_{f}L))
.
∎

Remark on the proof of
Theorem

5

It should be noted that if
L\subseteq\{a_{1},\bar{a}_{1},...,a_{n},\bar{a}_{n}\}
is
an EDT0L language, one can use affine programs (i.e. the results by
Karr
Karr, 1976
and Müller-Olm and
Seidl
Müller-Olm & Seidl, 2004
) or weighted automata over a field
Eilenberg, 1974
, p.143–145
(see also
Bojańczyk & Czerwiński, 2018
, Chapter 8
) to decide whether for every word in
L
, the number of

a_{i}
is the same as the number of
\bar{a}_{i}
. (As far as we can tell, this
has not been observed in the literature, but it is not difficult to show.)
However, it is crucial in our proof that we can do this for the language

R_{f}L
, which is obtained using the rational transduction
R_{f}
from an EDT0L language,
and EDT0L languages are not closed under rational transductions (see, e.g.
Ehrenfeucht et al., 1976
, Lemma 3.7
), whereas indexed languages are. It seems possible that for
some (but most likely not all) counting functions
f
, one can modify the proof of

Ciobanu et al., 2015
, Thm. 1
to directly yield an EDT0L language
for
R_{f}L
, and then apply the aforementioned methods. This remains to be
explored.

5.
Conclusion

This work initiates the study of slices closures of indexed languages, and thus
models of higher-order recursion. There are many questions that arise. The most
immediate question is whether slice closure computation (or decidability of
(
1
)) is also possible for safe or unsafe HORS of orders
\geq 3
.

Second: What is the complexity of computing
slice closures of indexed languages? The algorithm presented here is a
saturation algorithm where each step involves an enumeration-based procedure for
computing the slice closure of a semilinear set. The first challenge is the lack
of complexity bounds for computing the slice closure of a semilinear
set. However, even with such a bound, it would not be clear how to bound the saturation. Since the ascending chain condition
for slices (and thus termination of our algorithm) ultimately results from
Dickson’s lemma, it seems tempting to use length function theorems for vector
orderings (e.g.
Schmitz, 2017
, Thm. 3.15
) to at least obtain an Ackermannian
upper bound. In
Ganardi et al., 2022
, Prop. 4.8
, such an
approach has been developed for ascending chains of
congruences
, but it
is not clear how to extend this to slices.

Finally: Can we decide whether every vector in
\Psi(L)

satisfies a given polynomial equation? For affine programs, this is
decidable
Müller-Olm & Seidl, 2004a
, and one can even compute the
Zariski closure
Hrushovski et al., 2023
.
