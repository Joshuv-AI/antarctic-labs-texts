Hypersurfaces with capillary boundary evolving by volume preserving power mean curvature flow
Carlo Sinestrari, Liangjun Weng

Abstract
. In this paper, we introduce a volume- or area-preserving curvature flow for hypersurfaces with capillary boundary in the half-space, with speed given by a positive power of the mean curvature with a non-local averaging term. We demonstrate that for any convex initial hypersurface with a capillary boundary, the flow exists for all time and smoothly converges to a spherical cap as t → + ∞ t\to+\infty .

Primary: 53E10. Secondary: 35B40, 35K93

1.
Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the behavior of a curvature flow of capillary hypersurfaces with a prescribed contact angle condition at the boundary. Curvature flows have been widely investigated in the case of a closed manifold, starting from the classical paper of Huisken
[
27
]
on mean curvature flow, which is a time-dependent family of immersions
X:M^{n}\times[0,T)\to\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of an
n
-dimensional manifold satisfying

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=-H\nu,

where
\nu
is the outer normal and
H
is the mean curvature. In
[
27
]
it was proved that the mean curvature flow collapses arbitrary initial convex hypersurface to round points in finite time, and this result was the starting point of a fruitful line of research on the formation of singularities which led to relevant geometric applications through the decades.

Later, Schulze
[
45
,
46
,
47
]
considered a flow of the form
\partial_{t}X=-H^{\alpha}\nu
for a general power
\alpha>0
of the mean curvature. He proved that convex hypersurfaces collapse to a point and that the asymptotic shape is round if
\alpha>1
and if the principal curvatures of the initial surface are sufficiently pinched. The analysis of a general power has various motivations. As observed in
[
45
]
, the
H^{\alpha}
-flow is the gradient flow of the area functional of
\Sigma_{t}:=X(M^{n},t)
with respect to the
L^{\frac{\alpha+1}{\alpha}}
-norm, generalizing a well-known property in the
\alpha=1
case. Furthermore, a weak formulation of the
H^{\alpha}
-flow was later used in
[
48
]
to prove isoperimetric inequalities in Riemannian manifolds. We further mention two very recent papers, where it is shown that the power mean curvature flow occurs respectively as the limit of the level set flow of a time-fractional Allen-Cahn equation
[
19
]
and of a suitable minimizing movement evolution of sets
[
10
]
. In addition to the powers of the mean curvature, a vast literature has been devoted to hypersurface flows where the speed is given by general nonlinear functions of the principal curvatures, see e.g.
[
2
,
4
,
7
,
8
,
13
]
and the references therein.

In
[
28
]
, Huisken considered a a
non-local
variant of the mean curvature flow,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=(h(t)-H)\nu,

where
h(t):=\frac{1}{|\Sigma_{t}|}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}HdA
is chosen to ensure that the volume enclosed by
\Sigma_{t}
remains constant. A remarkable feature of this flow is that the area
|\Sigma_{t}|
is monotone decreasing so that the isoperimetric ratio of the enclosed domain improves with the flow. Huisken proved that if
\Sigma_{0}
is convex then the flow (
1.1
) has a solution for all times and converges smoothly to a round sphere as
t\to+\infty
. McCoy
[
35
]
obtained the same convergence result by modifying the term
h(t)
in (
1.1
) in order to keep the area
|\Sigma_{t}|
constant; in this case, the enclosed volume is increasing and the isoperimetric ratio is again improving. Similar results were then obtained for other constrained curvature flows in various settings
[
3
,
14
,
15
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
.

In
[
49
]
, the first author studied the flow analogous to
[
28
]
for a general power
\alpha>0
of the mean curvature,

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=(h(t)-H^{\alpha})\nu.

The main observation was that the monotonicity properties of the constrained flows can be used as a tool to obtain better convergence results than in the standard case (similar ideas had already been used in
[
3
,
14
]
in the case
\alpha=1
). In
[
49
]
it was proved that (
1.2
) drives any convex initial data to a round sphere as
t\to\infty
for a general
\alpha>0
. We remark that, for the standard (local) power mean curvature flow, the smooth convergence to a round profile is only known under strong curvature pinching assumptions on the data if
\alpha>1

[
46
]
, and it is even false for some values of
\alpha<1
, see the introduction in
[
49
]
for a more detailed discussion. Similar monotonicity-based techniques, coupled with powerful results of convex analysis, have been applied to more general classes of flows in recent years, see
[
6
,
9
,
11
,
12
]

In
[
22
]
, Guan-Li invented a local type of mean curvature flow, which is also volume preserving and area decreasing as the non-local flow in
[
28
]
. Its speed function is given by
\partial_{t}X=(n-\langle X,\nu\rangle H)\nu
, and this flow drives star-shaped closed hypersurfaces to a round sphere as
t\to+\infty
. See also
[
16
,
21
,
23
,
24
]
, which give many other significant developments for similar flows in various settings afterward. In particular, we point out that the monotonicity properties of these flows, both in the local and nonlocal case, have been applied to obtain geometric inequalities of the Alexandrov-Fenchel type, possibly for more general domains than in the classical setting of convex analysis.

While all the above references deal with closed hypersurfaces, our interest in this paper lies in studying hypersurfaces
with boundary

satisfying a Neumann condition. This case has also been studied for a long time, see e.g.
[
50
]
, but initially fewer results were known compared to the closed case. In recent years, research has made great strides in constructing flows for hypersurfaces with boundaries and obtaining geometric inequalities. For instance, works such as
[
43
]
and
[
55
]
have investigated the inverse nonlinear curvature flow for hypersurfaces with free and capillary boundaries in the Euclidean unit ball respectively, and have derived some new Alexandrov-Fenchel type inequalities. For capillary hypersurfaces in the half-space, references such as
[
39
]
and
[
26
,
53
,
54
]
have explored mean curvature flow and inverse curvature flow with capillary boundaries in the half-space, respectively.
Very recently, the asymptotic behavior of capillary convex hypersurface evolving by the Gauss curvature type flow was analyzed in
[
40
]
.
All these works on flows with boundary concern local type flows. In this paper, instead, we design and analyze a
nonlocal
flow corresponding to (
1.2
) for hypersurfaces with capillary boundaries in the half-space.
To the best of our knowledge, non-local curvature flows with boundary in the smooth setting have only been studied in dimension
n=1
, such as the area-preserving curve shortening flow with free boundary in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
analyzed by Mäder-Baumdicker
[
32
,
33
]
.

Typically, non-local type flows pose different challenges compared to their local counterparts. In particular, some maximum principle arguments can no longer be applied and well-known properties of the local case, such as the avoidance principle, do no longer hold. The capillary setting also poses additional difficulties because it requires analysis of the boundary behavior of geometric quantities. On the other hand, it is remarkable that, by suitably modifying the geometric quantities, we are able to recover in the capillary case the essential properties used for the convergence result in the closed case
[
49
]
, in particular the monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio adapted to the capillary setting.

To describe our results, we introduce some notation and definitions.
Let
\Sigma
be a compact hypersurface in the Euclidean closed half-space
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}:=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\langle x,E_{n+1}\rangle\geq 0\}
(
n\geq 1
), with boundary
\partial\Sigma
lying in
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
, where
E_{n+1}=(0,\ldots,0,1)
is the unit inner normal to
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
. Such a hypersurface is called a

capillary hypersurface
if

\Sigma
intersects
\partial{{\mathbb{R}}}_{+}^{n+1}
at a constant contact angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
along
\partial\Sigma
, that is,

(1.3)

\displaystyle\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle=\cos\theta,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ on }\partial\Sigma,

where
\nu
is the unit outward normal of
\Sigma
. In particular, if
\Sigma
meets the boundary orthogonally, i.e.
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
, it is called a hypersurface with
free boundary
.
Denote
\widehat{\Sigma}
the bounded domain in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
enclosed by
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and by
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
.
The boundary of
\widehat{\Sigma}
consists of two parts: one is
\Sigma
and the other, which
will be denoted by
\widehat{\partial\Sigma}
, lies on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
.

\Sigma
and
\widehat{\partial\Sigma}
have a common boundary
\partial\Sigma
. The well-known
capillary area
functional (see for instance the comprehensive books by Finn
[
20
, Section 1.4]
and Maggi
[
34
, Chapter 19]
) for
\Sigma
is defined as

\displaystyle W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma}):=|\Sigma|-\cos\theta|\widehat{\partial\Sigma}|.

Moreover, it is easy to see that (see e.g.
[
41
, Lemma 2.14]
or
[
42
, Remark 2.1]
)

(1.4)

\displaystyle W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})=\int_{\Sigma}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA.

Let
M
be a
n
-dimensional smooth compact manifold with boundary
\partial M
and
\Sigma_{0}:=X_{0}(M)\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
be a capillary hypersurface with contact angle
\theta\in(0,\pi)
. We consider a family of smooth capillary hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{t}
in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
starting from
\Sigma_{0}
, given by the embeddings
X:M\times[0,T)\to\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and satisfying

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{llll}\partial_{t}X&=&(\phi(t)-H^{\alpha})\widetilde{\nu},&\hbox{ in }M\times[0,T),\\
\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle&=&\cos\theta&\hbox{ on }\partial M\times[0,T),\\
X(\cdot,0)&=&X_{0}(\cdot)&\hbox{ on }M,\end{array}\right.

where

\widetilde{\nu}:=\nu-\cos\theta E_{n+1}
is the
capillary outward normal
of
\Sigma_{t}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
.
It is easy to observe that the capillary boundary condition
\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle=\cos\theta
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\langle\widetilde{\nu},E_{n+1}\rangle=0,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \text{ on }\partial M.

This ensures that the boundary of
\partial\Sigma_{t}
evolves inside
\partial\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}

along the flow (
1
).

In this paper, we choose the non-local term
\phi
in (
1
) to be either

•

(1.9)

\displaystyle\phi(t):=\frac{1}{W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha}dA,

or

•

(1.10)

\displaystyle\phi(t):=\frac{\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha+1}dA}{\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)HdA}.

The motivation for such choices is that in case (
1.9
) the flow (
1
) is
volume preserving
, i.e. the volume of
\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}
remains constant, while under (
1.10
) it is
area preserving
, in the sense that the capillary area
W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
is constant. In both cases, after defining the capillary isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
, see (
2.22
), we will see in Proposition
3.1
that
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
is non-increasing along the flow, with strict monotonicity unless
\Sigma_{t}
is a spherical cap.

The main result of this paper is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}
be a smooth strictly convex capillary hypersurface in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
with contact angle
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
. Then the flow (
1
), with
\phi
defined as in (
1.9
) (resp. (
1.10
)) has a unique, smooth solution
\Sigma_{t}
which is a strictly convex capillary hypersurface for all
t\in[0,+\infty)
. Moreover, the hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{t}
converge smoothly as
t\to+\infty
to a spherical cap with boundary angle
\theta
and having the same volume (resp. capillary area) as
\Sigma_{0}
.

In the rest of this article, we assume
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2})
, since the case
\theta=\frac{\pi}{2}
can be reduced to the closed hypersurface counterpart
[
28
,
35
,
49
]
by using a simple reflection argument along
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. We leave out the case
\theta>\frac{\pi}{2}
, where the curvature estimates are not available due to the bad sign of the boundary terms (cf. (
3.3
) and (
3.4
) in the proof of Proposition
3.2
). A similar restriction of the contact angle occurs in
[
26
,
53
]
and
[
55
]
.

On the other hand, we believe that some of the techniques introduced in this paper can be used to study a broad spectrum of quermassintegral-preserving non-local flows for capillary hypersurfaces, as explored in
[
6
,
9
,
12
,
15
,
37
,
38
]
, among others. In particular, by exploiting some Alexandrov-Fenchel inequality in the capillary case
[
39
,
41
]
, coupled with curvature estimates, we plan to establish the convergence of the flow by a symmetric polynomial function of the curvature in the capillary setting in a forthcoming work.

Let us summarize our strategy for proving Theorem
1.1
. Initially, we introduce the capillary isoperimetric ratio and we extend to the capillary setting a pinching estimate for the radii of convex sets obtained in
[
29
]
. More precisely, in Proposition
2.4
we show that the capillary isoperimetric ratio controls the ratio between the capillary outer and inner radius of our hypersurface. This result is of independent interest and can potentially offer insights into other capillarity-type problems. Together with the monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio, this estimate allows to control of the geometry of
\Sigma_{t}
as long as the flow exists. In particular, we can adapt Tso’s technique
[
51
]
to derive a uniform upper bound on the mean curvature, replacing the support function with the capillary support function
\bar{u}
. We then apply the boundary tensor maximum principle from Hu-Wei-Yang-Zhou
[
26
]
to demonstrate the preservation of strict convexity along our flow. The last major step consists of proving a uniform positive bound from below for the mean curvature. This is important because the parabolic operator associated with our problem has a coefficient proportional to
H^{\alpha-1}
, see (
2.6
), thus if
\alpha\neq 1
the flow becomes either degenerate or singular as
H
approaches zero. To bound
H
from below, we use a variant of Tso’s technique developed by Bertini-Sinestrari
[
11
, Section 4]
, combined with an estimate on the position of
\Sigma_{t}
based on an Alexandrov reflection argument. In this way, we obtain two-sided curvature bounds which ensure the long-time existence and uniform
C^{\infty}
-estimates of the solution to the flow. As in
[
49
, Section 4.2]
or
[
11
, Section 4]
, we can again use the monotonicity of the isoperimetric ratio to show that the flow must converge to a spherical cap.

The paper is organized as follows
: In Section
2
, we recall and derive the relevant evolution equations for our flow and prove the pinching estimate on the capillary outer and inner radius. In Section
3
, we show the monotonicity of the capillary isoperimetric ratio and the invariance of the strict convexity, and we obtain the curvature estimates that imply the convergence of the flow.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we collect some preliminaries and recall some evolution equations along the flow (
1
), which will serve as basic tools in the subsequent sections. In addition, we establish a capillary-type pinching estimate (cf. Proposition
2.4
) concerning the capillary outer and inner radius of the convex hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
.

2.1.
Evolution equations

Let
\Sigma_{t}
denote a family of smooth, embedded hypersurfaces with capillary boundary in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, defined by the embeddings
X(\cdot,t):M\to\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, which evolves according to the general flow

(2.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}X=\mathcal{F}\nu+\mathcal{T},

for some speed function
\mathcal{F}
and tangential vector field
\mathcal{T}\in T\Sigma_{t}
. Our flow (
1
) can be written in the form (
2.1
) by choosing

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)f,

(2.2)

\displaystyle\qquad\mathcal{T}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f\cos\theta E_{n+1}^{T}=f\cos\theta\,(\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle\nu-E_{n+1}),

where

f:=\phi-H^{\alpha}

and
E_{n+1}^{T}
is the tangential projection of
E_{n+1}
onto
T\Sigma_{t}.

Let
\mu
be the unit outward co-normal of
\partial\Sigma_{t}
in
\Sigma_{t}
. From the capillary boundary condition in (
1
) we know that

(2.3)

\displaystyle E_{n+1}=\cos\theta\nu-\sin\theta\mu,\quad\text{ on }\partial\Sigma_{t},

which implies

(2.4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}|_{\partial\Sigma_{t}}=f\sin\theta\cos\theta\mu=\mathcal{F}\cot\theta\mu.

see
[
53
, Section 2.5]
for more details. We also recall the useful property that
\mu
is a principal direction for the second fundamental form, that is,

(2.5)

\qquad\qquad h(V,\mu)=0,\quad\hbox{ on }\partial\Sigma_{t},

for any
V
tangent vector to
\partial\Sigma_{t}
, see e.g.
[
1
, Lemma 2.2]
.

We now recall the evolution equations for the induced metric
g_{ij}
, the unit normal
\nu
, the second fundamental form
(h_{ij})
, the Weingarten tensor
(h^{i}_{j})
and the mean curvature
H
of the hypersurfaces
\Sigma_{t}
along our flow. We denote by
\nabla
and
\Delta
the Levi-Civita connection and Laplace operator on
\Sigma_{t}
with respect to the induced metric, and we set
(h^{2})_{ij}:=\sum_{k}h_{ik}h^{k}_{j}
, and
|h|^{2}:=\sum_{i,j}h_{ij}h^{j}_{i}
. Then the following result holds, see
[
55
, Proposition 2.11]
for a detailed proof.

Proposition 2.1

(
[
55
]
)
.

Along the flow (
2.1
), there holds

(1)

\partial_{t}g_{ij}=2\mathcal{F}h_{ij}+\nabla_{i}\mathcal{T}_{j}+\nabla_{j}\mathcal{T}_{i}
.

(2)

\partial_{t}\nu=-\nabla\mathcal{F}+h(e_{i},\mathcal{T})e_{i}
.

(3)

\partial_{t}h_{ij}=-\nabla^{2}_{ij}\mathcal{F}+\mathcal{F}h_{ik}h_{j}^{k}+\nabla_{\mathcal{T}}h_{ij}+h_{j}^{k}\nabla_{i}\mathcal{T}_{k}+h_{i}^{k}\nabla_{j}\mathcal{T}_{k}.

(4)

\partial_{t}h^{i}_{j}=-\nabla^{i}\nabla_{j}\mathcal{F}-\mathcal{F}h_{j}^{k}h^{i}_{k}+\nabla_{\mathcal{T}}h^{i}_{j}.

(5)

\partial_{t}H=-\Delta\mathcal{F}-|h|^{2}\mathcal{F}+\langle\nabla H,\mathcal{T}\rangle
.

The flow (
1
) is parabolic if
H>0
and the local existence of a smooth solution is obtained by standard techniques, see e.g.
[
46
]
and
[
5
, Ch. 18]
for the case without boundary, and
[

, Ch. 2]
,
[
53
, Section 4]
for the extension to the capillary setting. In addition, the solution remains smooth, with bounds on all derivatives, as long as the curvature is bounded and the parabolicity remains strict. It follows that, for any smooth initial data with
H>0
satisfying the boundary condition, flow (
1
) has a unique smooth solution defined in a maximal time interval
[0,T^{*})
, where
T^{*}\leq+\infty
. Moreover, if
T^{*}
is finite, then either the second fundamental form of
\Sigma_{t}
becomes unbounded as

, or the infimum of
H
approaches zero.

For the flow (
1
), we introduce the linearized parabolic operator as

(2.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}:=\partial_{t}-\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}\Delta.

In the following computations, we use the convention of Einstein summation and the indices appearing after the semi-colon denote the covariant derivatives.

For any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the

was introduced in

, Eq. (2.6)]
as

(2.7)

\displaystyle\bar{u}:=\frac{\langle X-z,\nu\rangle}{1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle},

and is a capillary variant of the support function

. This function will play an important role for us in deriving the curvature estimates of flow (

) in the next section. We compute the evolution equations for

and
\bar{u}
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Along the flow (
1
), the functions

and
\bar{u}
satisfy

(2.8)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}u=(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)\left[\phi(t)-(1+\alpha)H^{\alpha}+\alpha uH^{\alpha-1}|h|^{2}\right],

and

(2.9)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\bar{u}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi-(1+\alpha)H^{\alpha}+\alpha\bar{u}H^{\alpha-1}|h|^{2}

\displaystyle+{2\alpha H^{\alpha-1}}\left\langle\nabla\bar{u},\nabla(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)\right\rangle.

On
\partial\Sigma_{t}
, there hold

(2.10)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}u=\cot\theta h(\mu,\mu)u,

and

(2.11)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\bar{u}=0.

Proof.

Since (
2.10
) and (
2.11
) have been shown in

) and (

From the Codazzi formula,

\displaystyle\Delta u=\langle X-z,\nabla H\rangle+H-u|h|^{2},

then

\displaystyle\partial_{t}u

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\partial_{t}X,\nu\rangle+\langle X-z,\partial_{t}\nu\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)+\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}\langle X-z,\nabla H\rangle

\displaystyle-f\langle X-z,\nabla(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)\rangle+h((X-z)^{T},\mathcal{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}(\Delta u-H+u|h|^{2})+(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)f

\displaystyle+f\cos\theta h((X-z)^{T},E_{n+1}^{T})+h((X-z)^{T},\mathcal{T}),

where
(X-z)^{T}
and

denote the tangential projection of
X-z
and
E_{n+1}
onto

respectively. The two terms in the last row cancel each other by (

) and we obtain (

).

Next, we derive (
2.9
). By using the Codazzi formula again,

(2.12)

\displaystyle\Delta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle=\langle\nabla H,E_{n+1}\rangle-\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle|h|^{2},

then

\displaystyle\langle\nabla f,E_{n+1}\rangle=-\alpha H^{\alpha-1}(\Delta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle+\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle|h|^{2}).

From Proposition
2.1
(2),

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\langle\nabla(f(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)),E_{n+1}\rangle+h(\mathcal{T},E_{n+1}^{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}(\Delta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle+|h|^{2}\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)

\displaystyle+f\cos\theta\,h(E_{n+1}^{T},E_{n+1}^{T})+h(\mathcal{T},E_{n+1}^{T})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}(\Delta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle+|h|^{2}\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle=\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle|h|^{2},

and also

(2.13)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)=-\alpha\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-1}|h|^{2}.

From (
2.8
) and (

), we derive

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\bar{u}

\displaystyle=

2.2.
Pinching estimate

Recall that for a convex body
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, the inner radius and outer radius of
\Omega
are defined as

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(\Omega):=\sup\{r>0:B_{r}(x_{0})\subset\Omega\text{ for some }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\},

and

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\Omega):=\inf\{r>0:\Omega\subset B_{r}(x_{0})\text{ for some }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\},

where
B_{r}(x_{0})
is the ball of radius
r
centered at
x_{0}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. The classical isoperimetric ratio of
\Omega
is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\Omega):=\frac{|\partial\Omega|^{n+1}}{|\Omega|^{n}}.

For a convex capillary hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
, we introduce the notion of the capillary inner radius of
\Sigma
as

(2.20)

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta):=\sup\{r>0\penalty\ :\penalty\ \widehat{\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(x_{0})}\subset\widehat{\Sigma}\text{ for some }x_{0}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\},

and
the capillary outer radius of
\Sigma
as

(2.21)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta):=\inf\{r>0\penalty\ :\penalty\ \widehat{\Sigma}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(x_{0})}\text{ for some }x_{0}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}\},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(x_{0}):=\{x\in\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}\penalty\ :\penalty\ |x-(x_{0}-r\cos\theta E_{n+1})|=r\}

is the spherical cap centered at
x_{0}-r\cos\theta E_{n+1}
with radius
r>0
. For simplicity, when
x_{0}
is the origin, we just write
\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}
to represent
\mathcal{C}_{r,\theta}(0)
and
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
to represent
\mathcal{C}_{1,\theta}(0)
.

The
capillary isoperimetric ratio
of
\Sigma
is defined as

(2.22)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma}):=\frac{(|\Sigma|-\cos\theta|\widehat{\partial\Sigma}|)^{n+1}}{|\widehat{\Sigma}|^{n}}=\frac{W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})^{n+1}}{|\widehat{\Sigma}|^{n}}.

As in the classical case, there holds a capillary isoperimetric inequality (cf.
[
34
, Theorem 19.21]
or
[
53
, (1.6)]
) stating that

(2.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})\geq\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}).

Moreover, equality holds in (
2.23
) if and only if
\Sigma
is a spherical cap.

In the classical case, it is known that an upper bound on the isoperimetric ratio of a convex body implies a pinching estimate on the outer and inner radii, see
[
29
, Lemma 4.4]
or
[
3
, Proposition 5.1]
. We prove now that an analogous result holds in the capillary case when
0<\theta\leq\frac{\pi}{2}
. This is a general property independent of the flow, which has its own interest and may be useful for other capillarity problems.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\Sigma
be a convex capillary hypersurface in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
and
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
. If the enclosed domain satisfies
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})\leq c_{1}
for some positive constant
c_{1}
, then there exists some positive constant
c:=c(n,c_{1},\theta)
such that

(2.24)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\leq c\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta).

Proof.

First we observe that the capillary isoperimetric ratio controls from above the classical one.
For any
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
, there holds
(1-\cos\theta)|\Sigma|\leq W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma}),
which yields

\displaystyle|\partial(\widehat{\Sigma})|=|\Sigma|+|\widehat{\partial\Sigma}|\leq 2|\Sigma|\leq\frac{2}{1-\cos\theta}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma}).

For a convex capillary domain
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
with
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})\leq c_{1}
, we deduce

\displaystyle\mathcal{I}(\widehat{\Sigma})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{|\partial(\widehat{\Sigma})|^{n+1}}{|\widehat{\Sigma}|^{n}}\leq\left(\frac{2}{1-\cos\theta}\right)^{n+1}\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2}{1-\cos\theta}\right)^{n+1}c_{1}.

By
[
29
, Lemma 4.4]
applied to
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, we conclude

(2.25)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})\leq c_{2}\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma}),

for some positive constant
c_{2}=c_{2}(n,c_{1},\cos\theta)
. This shows that the classical outer and inner radii satisfy a pinching property.

To obtain our result, we now have to show that the capillary radii are controlled by the classical ones. We start by estimating from below
\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)
in terms of
\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma})
. By definition,
\widehat{\Sigma}
contains a ball
B_{0}
, centered at some point
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and of radius
\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma})
. We denote by
B_{0}^{\prime}
and
x_{0}^{\prime}
the corresponding projections of
B_{0}
and
x_{0}
onto
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. Observe that
B_{0}^{\prime}\subset\widehat{\partial\Sigma}
, by the convexity of
\Sigma
and the condition
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
. Let us now consider the spherical cap with angle
\theta
having the same boundary as
B_{0}^{\prime}
, which is
\mathcal{C}_{r_{0},\theta}(x_{0}^{\prime})\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
with
r_{0}=\frac{\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{\sin\theta}
. Then it is easy to see that
\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{r_{0},\theta}}\subset\widehat{\Sigma}
, see Figure
1
. From the definition of capillary inner radius (
2.20
), we deduce

(2.26)

\displaystyle\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\geq r_{0}=\frac{\rho_{-}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{\sin\theta}.

Figure 1.
Figure 1.

Let us now estimate
\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)
. Let
B_{0}
be a ball of center
x_{0}
and radius
\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})
such that
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\overline{B_{0}}
and let again
x^{\prime}_{0}
be the projection of
x_{0}
onto
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. Since
\widehat{\Sigma}\subset\overline{B_{0}}
intersects
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
, the same does
B_{0}
, which implies
\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})\geq|x_{0}-x^{\prime}_{0}|
. If we now set

R=\frac{2\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})}{1-\cos\theta},

we see that any

satisfies

\displaystyle|x-x_{0}^{\prime}+R\cos\theta E_{n+1}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|x-x_{0}|+|x_{0}-x_{0}^{\prime}|+|R\cos\theta E_{n+1}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})+\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma})+R\cos\theta=R.

This shows that
\overline{B_{0}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{R,\theta}(x_{0}^{\prime})}
, which in turn implies

(2.27)

\displaystyle\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma},\theta)\leq R=\frac{2}{1-\cos\theta}\rho_{+}(\widehat{\Sigma}).

In view of (

), (
2.27
) and (
2.25
), the proof of (

) is complete.
∎

3.1.
Monotonicity of capillary isoperimetric ratio

A key property of our flow (

Proposition 3.1
.

Along the flow (
1
), the capillary isoperimetric ratio
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
is monotone non-increasing in time
t>0
. Moreover, monotonicity is strict unless
\Sigma_{t}
is a spherical cap.

Proof.

Let us first look at the case that
\phi(t)
is chosen as in (
1.9
). We have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}|=\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(\phi-H^{\alpha})(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA=0.

Let us denote

q(t):=\frac{\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA}{W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})}=\frac{\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA}{\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA},

where we are using (
1.4
).
Then the definitions immediately imply the identities

\phi(t)\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA=q(t)\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{\alpha}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA.

\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA-q(t)\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA=0.

From the first variational formula in
[
53
, Theorem 2.7]
, we deduce, using the two identities above,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(\phi(t)-H^{\alpha})H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(q(t)H^{\alpha}-H^{\alpha+1})(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle+q^{\alpha}(t)\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle-q^{\alpha+1}(t)\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(H-q(t))(H^{\alpha}-q^{\alpha}(t))(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle 0,

where the last inequality follows from
\alpha>0
. Observe in addition that the last inequality is strict unless
H=q(t)
everywhere om
\Sigma_{t}
, which means that
\Sigma_{t}
is a spherical cap.

If
\phi(t)
is chosen as in (
1.10
), we define
q(t)
as above and we obtain this time

\phi(t)\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA=\frac{1}{q(t)}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{\alpha+1}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA.

Using again
[
53
, Theorem 2.7]
we obtain, with computations similar to the previous case,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})=0,

and

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}|\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(\phi-H^{\alpha})(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q(t)}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(H-q(t))(H^{\alpha}-q^{\alpha}(t))(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 0.

In both cases, we conclude that
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
is monotone non-increasing in
t
.
∎

3.2.
Curvature estimates

Now we prove that the convexity is preserved along our flow (

Proposition 3.2
.

If
\Sigma_{0}\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}

To demonstrate Proposition

[

Lemma 3.3

(

Let

By continuity, we can assume that
(h_{ij})
has all eigenvalues

distinct and in increasing order at

as

. From the minimality of

(cf.
[

, we have that

for all

. By choosing

for

and
\Lambda^{1}_{k}\equiv 0
for
1\leq k\leq n
, we have

\displaystyle 2\Lambda_{k}^{l}\nabla_{1}h^{k}_{l}-\Lambda_{k}^{i}\Lambda^{jk}h_{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\left(2\frac{\nabla_{1}h_{kl}}{h_{ll}}\nabla_{1}h_{kl}-\left(\frac{\nabla_{1}h_{kl}}{h_{ll}}\right)^{2}h_{ll}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{l=2}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(\nabla_{1}h_{kl})^{2}}{h_{ll}}\geq\sum_{l=2}^{n}\frac{(\nabla_{1}h_{ll})^{2}}{h_{ll}}\geq\frac{(\nabla_{1}H)^{2}}{H},

where in the last step we have used the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle|\nabla_{1}H|=\left|\sum_{l=2}^{n}\frac{\nabla_{1}h_{ll}}{\sqrt{h_{ll}}}\,\sqrt{h_{ll}}\,\right|\leq\left(\sum_{l=2}^{n}\frac{(\nabla_{1}h_{ll})^{2}}{h_{ll}}\right)^{1/2}H^{1/2}.

Substituting into (
3.5

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-2}\left[(\alpha-1)(\nabla_{1}H)^{2}+2{(\nabla_{1}H)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(\alpha+1)(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)H^{\alpha-2}(\nabla_{1}H)^{2}\geq 0,

that is, (
3.5
) holds also in this case.
In summary, the theorem follows by using Lemma
3.3
.
∎

Proposition 3.4

Proposition

implies the existence of a capillary spherical cap with radius

Lemma 3.5

Let
\Sigma_{t}
be the solution of (

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\frac{r_{0}}{2},\theta}(z_{*})}\subset\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}

for all

Proof.

The idea of the proof is to compare
\Sigma_{t}
with the capillary spherical cap centered at
z_{*}-r(t)\cos\theta E_{n+1}
with shrinking radius

\displaystyle r^{\prime}(t)=-\left(\frac{n}{r(t)}\right)^{\alpha},

with

\displaystyle F(t):=\frac{1}{2}\left(\min_{M}|X(\cdot,t)-(z_{*}-r(t)\cos\theta E_{n+1})|^{2}-r^{2}(t)\right),

for any

such that
\Sigma_{t}
and

It is easy to see that

Suppose

be the first time such that

Next we divide the proof into two cases:
p_{0}\in\mathring{M}
and

.

(1)

. Then at

we have

(3.10)

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{X-(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})}{|X-(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})|}=\frac{X-(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})}{r(t_{0})},

which also implies

(3.11)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}=\nu-\cos\theta E_{n+1}=\frac{X(p_{0},t_{0})-z_{*}}{r(t_{0})}.

Moreover, the spherical cap
\mathcal{C}_{r(t_{0}),\theta}(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})

\Sigma_{t_{0}}
at

of

\displaystyle\kappa_{i}(p_{0},t_{0})\leq\frac{1}{r(t_{0})},\penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ 1\leq i\leq n.

Then

(3.12)

\displaystyle H(p_{0},t_{0})\leq\frac{n}{r(t_{0})}.

(2)

p_{0}\in\partial M
. By (
2.5
), if we choose
\{e_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}
to be an orthonormal frame on
T\partial\Sigma_{t_{0}}\subset T\mathbb{R}^{n}
and set
e_{n}:=\mu
, then
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
is an orthonormal frame on
T\Sigma_{t_{0}}
.
From
[
53
, Proposition 2.4 (2)]
, we know

(3.13)

\displaystyle\kappa_{\alpha}=\sin\theta\,\widehat{\kappa}_{\alpha},

where
\{\widehat{\kappa}_{\alpha}\}_{\alpha=1}^{n-1}
are the principal curvatures of
\partial\Sigma_{t}
in
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}=\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

By definition of
p_{0}
, we have that

\displaystyle|X(p_{0},t_{0})-(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{M}|X(\cdot,t_{0})-(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})|^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\partial M}|X(\cdot,t_{0})-(z_{*}-r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1})|^{2}.

On the other hand, for all

\qquad\qquad|X(p,t_{0})-z_{*}+r(t_{0})\cos\theta E_{n+1}|^{2}=|X(p,t_{0})-z_{*}|^{2}+r^{2}(t_{0})\cos^{2}\theta.

It follows that
p_{0}
attains the minimal value of
\min\limits_{\partial M}|X(\cdot,t_{0})-z_{*}|^{2}
. Then we have that the horizontal component of
\nu
is parallel to
X-z_{*}
at
p_{0}
. In addition, if we regard
\partial\Sigma_{t_{0}}
as a subset of
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}=\mathbb{R}^{n}

n

B_{|X(p_{0},t_{0})-z_{*}|}^{n}(z_{*})\subset\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}

\partial\Sigma_{t_{0}}
from the inside at

. This yields

(3.14)

\displaystyle\widehat{\kappa}_{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{|X(p_{0},t_{0})-z_{*}|},\quad\forall\penalty\ 1\leq\alpha\leq n-1,

and

(3.15)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\nu}=\frac{|\widetilde{\nu}|}{|X-z_{*}|}(X-z_{*})=\frac{|\nu-\cos\theta E_{n+1}|}{r(t_{0})\sin\theta}(X-z_{*})=\frac{X-z_{*}}{r(t_{0})},

where the last equality used (

We have seen that, in both cases, we have (

We can now prove a uniform upper bound for the curvature of our flow.

Proposition 3.6
.

If
\Sigma_{t}
be a solution of the flow (

\displaystyle H\leq C\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{t},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ t\in[0,T^{*}),

\displaystyle\frac{\mathcal{L}H^{\alpha}}{\bar{u}-\varepsilon}-\frac{H^{\alpha}\mathcal{L}\bar{u}}{(\bar{u}-\varepsilon)^{2}}+\frac{2\alpha H^{\alpha-1}}{\bar{u}-\varepsilon}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)\langle\nabla\Phi,\nabla\bar{u}\rangle

Corollary 3.7
.

Let
\Sigma_{t}
be the solution of the flow (
1
). Then there exists
C>0
depending only on
\Sigma_{0}
such that the the principal curvatures of
\Sigma_{t}
satisfy

\max\limits_{1\leq i\leq n}\kappa_{i}\leq C,\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ t\in[0,T^{*}).

From the upper bound of the mean curvature, we obtain the following estimate for the non-local term
\phi(t)
of flow (
1
).

Proposition 3.8
.

Along the flow (
1
), there holds

(3.25)

\displaystyle b\leq\phi(t)\leq b^{-1},

for some positive constant
b
, which only depends on the initial datum.

Proof.

The upper bound in (
3.25
) follows directly from Proposition
3.4
and Proposition
3.6
.

For the proof of the lower bound, we will need the following Minkowski-type inequality for capillary hypersurfaces in the half-space (see Theorem 1.2 in
[
54
]
and the following remarks)

(3.26)

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\geq c_{n,\theta}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma})^{\frac{n-1}{n}},

where
c_{n,\theta}:=n(n+1)^{\frac{1}{n}}|\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}|^{\frac{1}{n}}.

Let us first look at the case that
\phi(t)
is chosen as in (
1.9
). If
\alpha\geq 1
, by the Hölder inequality and Minkowski inequality (
3.26
), we have

\displaystyle c_{n,\theta}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\leq\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{\alpha}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\right)^{1-\frac{1}{\alpha}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{\frac{1}{\alpha}}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA,

which implies

\displaystyle\phi(t)\geq c_{n,\theta}^{\alpha}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{-\frac{\alpha}{n}}.

Taking into account Proposition
3.4
, the conclusion follows.

If
0<\alpha<1
, we obtain from Minkowski inequality (
3.26
) and Proposition
3.6
,

\displaystyle c_{n,\theta}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\max_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{1-\alpha}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{\alpha}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle C_{1}\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{\alpha}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA.

Together with Proposition
3.4
, this implies

\displaystyle\phi(t)\geq\frac{c_{n,\theta}}{C_{1}}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{-\frac{1}{n}}\geq b,

for some positive constant
b
, depending on the initial datum.

In the case
\phi(t)
is chosen as in (
1.10
), for any
\alpha>0
, by Hölder inequality,

\displaystyle\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H^{\alpha+1}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}}(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\right)^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\phi^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\right)^{\frac{1}{\alpha+1}}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{\frac{\alpha}{\alpha+1}}.

Using again the Minkowski inequality (
3.26
),

\displaystyle\phi(t)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\int_{\Sigma_{t}}H(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle)dA\right)^{\alpha}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{-\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle c_{n,\theta}^{\alpha}W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})^{-\frac{\alpha}{n}},

and we obtain the desired lower bound for
\phi
by Proposition
3.4
.
∎

Before proving a uniform lower bound on the mean curvature, we need to estimate the position of our evolving hypersurface by showing that it cannot drift arbitrarily far during the flow. For constrained flows of closed hypersurfaces, McCoy has used an Alexandrov-type reflection argument due to Chow and Gulliver to show that the solution is contained for all times in a suitable fixed ball, see e.g.
[
35
, Proposition 3.4]
.

Here we use a similar approach by using a different reflection argument, again due to Chow and Gulliver. We adapt the notation of
[
17
, Section 2]
to describe the reflection of a capillary hypersurface across a vertical hyperplane. Given
V\in\mathbb{S}^{n}
such that
\langle V,E_{n+1}\rangle=0
(equivalently,
V\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\cap\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
) and given
c\in\mathbb{R}
, we consider the hyperplane
\Pi_{V}^{c}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\langle x,V\rangle=c\}.

Let
H^{+}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
(resp.
H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
) be the halfspace
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\langle x,V\rangle>c\}
(resp.
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\,|\,\langle x,V\rangle<c\}
).

Let
\Sigma\subset\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}
be a capillary hypersurface which bounds the domain
\widehat{\Sigma}
and let
\Sigma^{\Pi}
be the reflection of
\Sigma
about
\Pi_{V}^{c}
, i.e.
\Sigma^{\Pi}=\{x-2(\langle x,V\rangle-c)V\,|\,x\in\Sigma\}
. Clearly,
\Sigma^{\Pi}
is again a capillary hypersurface with the same contact angle in
\overline{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}}

We say that
\Sigma
can be strictly reflected at
\Pi_{V}^{c}
if
\Sigma^{\Pi}\cap H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c})\subset(\widehat{\Sigma}\setminus\Sigma)\cap H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
and
V
is not tangent to
\Sigma
at the points in
\Sigma\cap\Pi_{V}^{c}
.

Then we have the following result.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
\Sigma_{t}
, for
t\in[0,T^{*})
, be a smooth family of capillary hypersurfaces solving the flow (
1
). Let
V\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\cap\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
and let
c\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\Sigma_{0}
can be reflected strictly at
\Pi_{V}^{c}
, then
\Sigma_{t}
can be reflected strictly at
\Pi_{V}^{c}
for all time
t\in[0,T^{*})
.

We just outline the proof of Theorem
3.9
, which is almost the same as
[
17
, Theorem 2.2]
, by using standard arguments of the Alexandrov moving plane method. The possibility that
\Sigma_{t}^{\Pi}
and
\Sigma_{t}
first touch at a point of the capillary boundary on
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
is ruled out by the same contact angle condition of
\Sigma_{t}^{\Pi}
and
\Sigma_{t}
, similar to the case where
V
becomes tangent to
\Sigma_{t}
at a point in
\Sigma_{t}\cap\Pi_{V}^{c}
. We observe that the maximum principle argument used in the proof is not affected by the presence of the nonlocal term, since
\phi(t)
has the same value for the original hypersurface and for the reflected one.

It is interesting to note that the convexity of
\Sigma_{t}
is not required for Theorem
3.9
and that it suffices to assume
H>0
; in addition, the contact angle can be an arbitrary value
\theta\in(0,\pi)
.

Remark 3.10
.

Observe that, if a capillary hypersurface
\Sigma
is contained in the halfspace
H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
, then
\Sigma^{\Pi}\cap H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c})=\emptyset
and it trivially satisfies the strict reflection property. Conversely, if
\Sigma\subset H^{+}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
, it necessarily violates the reflection property. Therefore Theorem
3.9
has the following corollary: if the initial hypersurface satisfies
\Sigma_{0}\subset H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
for some vertical hyperplane
\Pi_{V}^{c}
, then we cannot have
\Sigma_{t}\subset H^{+}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
, for any
t>0
.

Now we are ready to show the solution of flow (
1
) remains inside a suitably large spherical cap with a fixed center point for all time.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
\Sigma_{t}
be the solution of (
1
). Then there exists
z^{*}\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
and
R^{*}>0

such that

(3.27)

\displaystyle\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{{R^{*}},\theta}(z^{*})}

for all
t\in[0,T^{*})
.

Proof.

Let us fix any
R>R_{0}
, with
R_{0}
given by Proposition
3.4
, and let
\mathcal{C}_{R,\theta}(z^{*})
be a spherical cap enclosing
\Sigma_{0}
in its interior. For simplicity of notation, we assume that
z^{*}=0
. Then, for any horizontal unit vector
V
and
X\in\Sigma_{0}
we have
V\cdot X<R\sin\theta
and so
\Sigma_{0}\subset H^{-}(\Pi_{V}^{c_{0}})
with
c_{0}=R\sin\theta
.

Given any time
t>0
, Proposition
3.4
shows that
\Sigma_{t}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{R,\theta}(z)}
for a suitable spherical cap centered at some point
z=z(t)\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
. Then, for any horizontal unit vector
V
and
X\in\Sigma_{t}
we have

V\cdot X>V\cdot z-R\sin\theta,

and therefore
\Sigma_{t}\subset H^{+}(\Pi_{V}^{c})
for
c=V\cdot z-R\sin\theta
. By Remark
3.10
, this implies that
c<c_{0}
, that is,

V\cdot z<2R\sin\theta.

Since
V\in\partial\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
is an arbitrary unit vector, this implies that

(3.28)

\displaystyle|z|\leq 2R\sin\theta.

Let us now set
R^{*}:=\left(1+\frac{2\sin\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\right)R
. In view of
\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{R,\theta}(z)}
and (
3.28
), we find, for any
X\in\Sigma_{t}
,

\displaystyle|X+\cos\theta R^{*}E_{n+1}|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle|X-(z-\cos\theta RE_{n+1})|+|z|+(R^{*}-R)\cos\theta

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle R+2R\sin\theta+(R^{*}-R)\cos\theta

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle R\left(1+2\sin\theta+\frac{2\sin\theta\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta}\right)=R^{*},

showing that
\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}\subset\widehat{\mathcal{C}_{{R^{*}},\theta}(0)}
for all
t>0
.
∎

To obtain a lower bound for
H
, we adapt the method in
[
11
, Section 4.1]
, which reverses the sign of the test function in (
3.18
). This idea had been previously used in
[
44
, Lemma 4.3]
by Schnürer in the different setting of a local expanding curvature flow.
The lower bound will ensure that the flow (
1
) is strictly parabolic uniformly in time. In contrast with the proof the upper bound in Proposition
3.6
, the nonlocal term
\phi
in the equation plays here a crucial role and Proposition
3.8
is essential.

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\Sigma_{t}
be a solution of the flow (
1
). Then there holds

\displaystyle H\geq C\mbox{ on }\Sigma_{t},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\ \forall\penalty\ t\in[0,T^{*}),

where
C
is a positive constant, depending on the initial datum.

Proof.

For any time
t\geq 0
, let
z^{*}
be chosen as in Lemma
3.11
. Then we know that

\displaystyle 0\leq\bar{u}:=\frac{\langle X-z^{*},\nu\rangle}{1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle}\leq\frac{c}{2},

for some positive constant
c:=2R^{*}>0
, where
R^{*}
is given in Lemma
3.11
. In turn

(3.29)

\displaystyle\frac{c}{2}\leq c-\bar{u}\leq c,

for all
t\in[0,T^{*})
. We then consider the function

(3.30)

\displaystyle\Psi:=\frac{H^{\alpha}}{c-\bar{u}},

which is well-defined for
t\in[0,T^{*})
. From (
3.21
),

(3.31)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\Psi=0\text{ on }\partial M.

With computations similar to the proof of Proposition
3.6
, we have

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\Psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(\alpha H^{\alpha-1}|h|^{2}-\Psi)\frac{\phi}{\bar{u}-c}+\frac{c\alpha H^{2\alpha-1}|h|^{2}}{(c-\bar{u})^{2}}-(1+\alpha)\Psi^{2}

\displaystyle+2\alpha H^{\alpha-1}\langle\nabla\Psi,\nabla(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1})\rangle

\displaystyle+\frac{2\alpha(1-\cos\theta\langle\nu,E_{n+1}\rangle H^{\alpha-1}}{\bar{u}-c}\langle\nabla\Psi,\nabla\bar{u}\rangle

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(\alpha H^{\alpha-1}|h|^{2}-\Psi)\frac{\phi}{\bar{u}-c}-(1+\alpha)\Psi^{2},\text{ mod }\nabla\Psi.

If the minimum value of
\Psi
is reached at
t=0
, then we are done. Otherwise, from
\nabla_{\mu}\Psi=0
on
\partial M
in (
3.31
) and the Hopf boundary point lemma, we have that
\Psi
attains its minimum value at some interior point, say
p_{0}\in{\rm int}(M)
. Let us choose
\varepsilon_{0}>0
sufficiently small so that
0<H\leq\varepsilon_{0}
implies

(3.32)

\displaystyle\alpha H\leq\frac{1}{2c},\quad 2(1+\alpha)H^{\alpha}\leq\frac{b}{4},

where
b>0
is the uniform lower bound of
\phi(t)
in Proposition
3.8
. Let us now suppose that
H(p_{0})\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. From the evolution equation for
\mathcal{L}\Psi
, and using
|h|^{2}\leq H^{2}
, Proposition
3.8
, (
3.11
), (
3.29
) and (
3.32
), we have at
p_{0}
,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\Psi\geq\frac{\phi\Psi}{c-\bar{u}}-\frac{\alpha H^{\alpha+1}\phi}{c-\bar{u}}-(1+\alpha)\Psi^{2}\quad\text{ mod }\nabla\Psi

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Psi\left[\phi\left(\frac{1}{c}-\alpha H\right)-(1+\alpha)\Psi\right]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\Psi\left[\frac{b}{2c}-2(1+\alpha)\frac{H^{\alpha}}{c}\right]>0,

which is a contradiction. Hence we conclude that

\displaystyle\Psi\geq\min\left\{\Psi(\cdot,0),\frac{\varepsilon_{0}^{\alpha}}{c}\right\}.

Together with (
3.29
), this yields the uniform lower bound for
H
.

∎

3.3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

Theorem 3.13
.

The solution of flow (
1
) exists for all time and satisfies uniform
C^{\infty}
-estimates.

Proof.

In order to show the long-time existence of flow (
1
), it is convenient to represent the convex hypersurface
\Sigma_{t}
as the radial graph over semi-sphere
\bar{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}
for some function
\rho(x,t)
with
(x,t)\in\bar{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}\times[0,T^{*})
, and obtain uniform estimates on the derivatives of
\rho
, see for instance
[
53
, Section 4]
or
[
55
, Section 3.4]
. Then equation (
1
) reduces to a scalar parabolic equation for the function
\rho
with a capillary boundary value condition on
\partial\mathbb{S}^{n}_{+}
. From Proposition
3.4
and Corollary
3.7
, we know that
\rho
is uniformly bounded in
C^{2}(\bar{\mathbb{S}}^{n}_{+}\times[0,T^{*})
. The corresponding scalar flow for
\rho
is uniformly parabolic (due to Proposition
3.12
and Proposition
3.6
) and the boundary value condition of
\rho
satisfies a uniformly oblique property due to
|\cos\theta|<1
. From standard parabolic theory (see e.g.
[
18
, Theorem 6.1, Theorem 6.4 and Theorem 6.5]
, also
[
31
, Theorem 14.23]
), we obtain uniform
C^{\infty}
-estimates for
\rho
. In turn, the long-time existence of solution (
1
) follows.
∎

Finally, we conclude the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
.

For the convergence of the flow (
1
), we can argue using the same way as
[
49
, Section 4]
(or
[
11
, Section 4.2]
).
We exploit the monotonicity of
W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
(resp. of
|\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}|
) along our flow. Taking into account the uniform estimates of Theorem
3.13
, we see that the time derivative of
W_{\theta}(\widehat{\Sigma_{t}})
(resp. of
|\widehat{\Sigma_{t}}|
) must tend to zero as
t\to+\infty
. From the explicit expression of these derivatives in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, we see that this implies

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\max_{\Sigma_{t}}|H(\cdot,t)-q(t)|=0,

with
q(t)
as in the proof of Proposition
3.1
, which in turn yields

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}\max_{\Sigma_{t}}|H^{\alpha}(\cdot,t)-\phi(t)|=0.

Therefore, using from Theorem
3.13
and compactness, we see that any possible limit of subsequences of
\Sigma_{t}
has constant mean curvature with capillary boundary.
Combining with the conclusion in
[
30
, Corollary 1.2]
(see also
[
56
]
) we know that the limit is a spherical cap, with radius uniquely determined by the constraint on the volume (resp. on the capillary area). By a standard procedure, cf.
[
53
, Section 4.5]
or
[
55
, Section 3.4]
, one can show that since any limit of a convergent subsequence is uniquely determined, then the whole family
\Sigma_{t}
smoothly converges to a spherical cap.
∎
