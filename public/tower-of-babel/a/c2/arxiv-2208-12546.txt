A generalized metric-type structure with some applications
Hallowed O. Olaoluwa, Aminat O. Ige, Johnson O. Olaleru

Abstract
The paper introduces the class of O-metric spaces, a novel generalization of metric-type spaces, classifying almost all possible metric types into upward and downward O-metrics. We list some topologies arising from O-metrics and discuss convergence, sequential continuity, first countability and T 2 separation. The topology of an O-metric space can be generated by an upward O-metric on the space hence the focus on upward O-metric spaces. A theorem on the existence and uniqueness of a fixed point of some contractive-like map is proved and related with some other well known fixed point results in literature. Applications to the estimation of distances, polygon inequalities, and optimization of entries in infinite symmetric matrices are also given.

Introduction

The concept of metric spaces is fundamental in analysis, and many authors have worked around it to arrive at abridged concepts such as partial metric spaces, multiplicative metric spaces, b-metric spaces, etc. In several generalization attempts, the axioms of a metric space have been perturbed to derive other spaces. 2.1.1

One can generate an uncountable number of O-metrics from an existing one on a given set

@A{}A{\theta\times\theta}A@A{}A{\theta}A\\

(1)

i.e. such that

2.1.3
Upward O-metric spaces and downward O-metric spaces

The following proposition states amongst other things, the relationship between some type of upward O-metrics and downward O-metrics.

Proposition 2.16
.

Suppose
(X,d_{\textnormal{{o}}},a)
is an O-metric space, with
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}:X\times X\to I_{a}
and
\textnormal{{o}}:I_{a}\times I_{a}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where
I_{a}
be an interval in
[0,\infty)
containing
a\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Suppose there is a function
\phi:I_{a}\times I_{a}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

w\leq\textnormal{{o}}(u,v)\Longleftrightarrow\phi(w,v)\leq u,~\forall u,v,w\in I_{a}.

(2)

Then,

(a)

The reverse triangle
\bar{\phi}
-inequality
\bar{\phi}(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,z),d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(z,y))\leq d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y)
holds for all
x,y,z\in X
, where
\bar{\phi}(w,v)=\max\{\phi(w,v),\phi(v,w)\}
.

(b)

If
\theta:I_{a}\cup\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\phi)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a continuous function decreasing on
I_{a}
, then
(X,d_{\xi},\theta(a))
is an O-metric space, with
d_{\xi}=\theta\circ d_{\textnormal{{o}}}
and for all
u,v\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\theta)
,
\xi(u,v)=\theta(\phi(\theta^{-1}(u),\theta^{-1}(v)))
.

(c)

If, in addition to (b),
\theta([a,\infty))\subset[0,a]
with
\theta(a)=a
, then
(X,d_{\textnormal{{o}}},a)
is an upward O-metric space if and only if
(X,d_{\xi},a)
is a downward O-metric space.

Proof.

(a) holds from equation (
2
), the symmetry of
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}
, and the triangle
o
-inequality.

(b) Suppose
\theta:I_{a}\cup\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits(\phi)\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a continuous mapping decreasing on
I_{a}
. Since X is an O-metric space,

d_{\xi}(x,y)=\theta(a)\Longleftrightarrow\theta(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y))=\theta(a)\Longleftrightarrow d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y)=a\Longleftrightarrow x=y
, and
d_{\xi}(x,y)=\theta(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y))=\theta(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(y,x))=d_{\xi}(y,x)
, for all
x,y\in X
.

Taking the images by
\theta
of the two sides of the inequality in (a), for all
x,y,z\in X
,

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\theta(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y))&\leq&\theta\left(\phi(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,z),d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(z,y))\right)\\
d_{\xi}(x,y)&\leq&\xi(d_{\xi}(x,z),d_{\xi}(z,y)).\end{array}

The continuity of
\theta
ensures that
d_{\xi}
has its values in
\theta(I_{a})
, an interval containing
\theta(a)
. Thus
(X,d_{\xi},\theta(a))
is an O-metric.

(c) With the additional condition
\theta([a,\infty))\subset[0,a]
and
\theta(a)=a
,
(X,d_{\textnormal{{o}}},a)
is an upward O-metric space if and only if
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y)\geq a
for all
x,y\in X
, if and only if
d_{\xi}(x,y)=\theta(d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y))\leq a
for all
x,y\in X
, if and only if
(X,d_{\xi},a)
is a downward O-metric space.
∎

The proposition below states that the Cartesian product of two O-metric spaces is an O-metric space. The proof is omitted.

Proposition 2.19
.

Let
(X_{1},d_{\textnormal{{o}}_{1}},a)
and
(X_{2},d_{\textnormal{{o}}_{2}},a)

and

, where
I_{a}
is an interval in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
containing
a\geq 0
, and
\textnormal{{o}}_{1}:I_{a}\times I_{a}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
\textnormal{{o}}_{2}:I_{a}\times I_{a}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}

on

Proposition 2.20
.

2.2
Topology induced by an O-metric

Definition 2.21
.

Let
(X,d_{\textnormal{{o}}},a)
be an O-metric space. Given
x\in X
and
r>0
, the open ball centered on
x
and with radius
r
is given by

B(x,r)=\{y\in X:~|d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y)-a|<r\}.

If
X
is an
a
-upward O-metric, i.e. if
I_{a}=[a,\infty)
, then for
r>0
and
x\in X
,
B(x,r)=\{y\in X|~a\leq d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y)<a+r\}
. This conforms to the definition of open balls in metric spaces and b-metric spaces (when
a=0
), and multiplicative metric spaces (when
a=1
). If
X
is an
a
-downward O-metric, i.e. if
I_{a}=[0,a]
,
B(x,r)=\{y\in X|~a-r<d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x,y)\leq a\}
for
r>0
and
x\in X
.

The following proposition lists and compares some topologies defined on an O-metric:

Proposition 2.22
.

An O-metric
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}:X\times X\to I_{a}
generates the following topologies on
X
:

\mathcal{T}_{1}=\left\{A\subset X|~\forall x\in A~\exists r\in(\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\textnormal{{o}})\setminus\{a\},~B(x,|r-a|)\subset A\right\}

(6)

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\left\{A\subset X:~\forall x\in A~\exists r>0,~B(x,r)\subset A\right\}

(7)

\mathcal{T}_{3}=\left\{A\subset X:~\forall\{x_{n}\}\subset X~d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x_{n},x)\to a\mbox{ and }x\in A\implies\exists n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}:x_{n}\in A~~\forall n\geq n_{0}\right\}

(8)

We also have
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{3}.

Proof.

(a)

\emptyset\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
vacuously while
X\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
trivially. Now, let
\{A_{i}:i\in I\}
be a family of subsets of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. For each
x\in\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i},
there exists
j\in I
such that
x\in A_{j}
hence there is
r\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\textnormal{{o}}\setminus\{a\}
satisfying
B(x,|r-a|)\subset A_{j}\subset\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}
. Thus
\bigcup_{i\in I}A_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}.

Now, given two members
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
of
\mathcal{T}_{1}
,
x\in A_{1}\cap A_{2}
implies that there are radii
r_{1},r_{2}\in\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\textnormal{{o}}\setminus\{a\}
such that
B(x,|r_{i}-a|)\subset A_{i}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, since
x\in A_{1}
and
x\in A_{2}
. Taking
r_{j}\in\{r_{1},r_{2}\}
such that
|r_{j}-a|=\min\{|r_{1}-a|,|r_{2}-a|\}
,
B(x,|r_{j}-a|)\subset A_{1}\cap A_{2}
hence
A_{1}\cap A_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{1}
. We have thus proved that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is a topology on
X
.

(b)
In fact, by substituting
\mathop{\rm Im}\nolimits\textnormal{{o}}
by
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
|r-a|
with
r
in
(a)
, we obtain that
\mathcal{T}_{2}
is also a topology on
X
.

(c)

Consider a family
\{B_{i}:i\in I\}
of subsets of
\mathcal{T}_{3}
, and a sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset X
such that
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x_{n},x)\to a
, where
x\in\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}
. There exists
j\in I
such that
x\in B_{j}\in\mathcal{T}_{3}
hence there is
n_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\forall n\geq n_{0}
,
x_{n}\in B_{j}\subset\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}
. Thus
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{3}
. In the case that
I=\{1,2\}
and
\{y_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a sequence in
X
such that
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(y_{n},y)\to a
with
y\in B_{1}\cap B_{2}
, then
y\in B_{1}
and
y\in B_{2}
hence there are integers
n_{1},n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
y_{n}\in B_{1}

\forall n\geq n_{1}
and
y_{n}\in B_{2}

\forall n\geq n_{2}
; thus taking
n_{0}=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
,
y_{n}\in B_{1}\cap B_{2}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. Thus
B_{1}\cap B_{2}\in\mathcal{T}_{3}
. Thus
\mathcal{T}_{3}
is a topology on
X
.

The inclusion
\mathcal{T}_{1}\subset\mathcal{T}_{2}
is obvious.

Let
A\in\mathcal{T}_{2}
. Then for each
x\in A
, there is
r>0
such that
B(x,r)\subset A
. Let
(x_{n})
be a sequence in
X
such that
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x_{n},x)\to a
with

2.2.1
Convergence and sequential continuity

We have the following definition:

Definition 2.23
.

Given an O-metric
d_{\textnormal{{o}}}:X\times X\to I_{a}
where
I_{a}
is an interval of
\mathbb{R}_{+}
containing
a\geq 0

\textnormal{{o}}:I_{a}\times I_{a}\to[0,\infty)

(i) converges to

\lim_{n\to\infty}d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x_{n},x)=a

(ii) is a Cauchy sequence if
\lim_{n,m\to\infty}d_{\textnormal{{o}}}(x_{n},x_{m})=a
.

The space
(X,d_{\textnormal{{o}}},a)
is said to be complete if every Cauchy sequence of
X
converges in
X
.

From the above definition, the following proposition on sequential continuity immediately follows:

Proposition 2.24
.

Let
(X_{1},d_{\textnormal{{o}}_{1}},a)
and
(X_{2},d_{\textnormal{{o}}_{2}},b)
be two O-metric spaces, and
f:X_{1}\to X_{2}
a map. The following are equivalent:

(i)

f
is sequentially continuous at
\tilde{x}\in X_{1}
i.e. for any sequence
\{x_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of points in
X_{1}
,
x_{n}\to\tilde{x}\implies f(x_{n})\to f(\tilde{x})
;

(ii)

\forall\epsilon>0
,
\exists\delta>0:

|d_{\textnormal{{o}}_{1}}(x,\tilde{x})-a|<\delta\implies|d_{\textnormal{{o}}_{2}}(f(x),f(\tilde{x}))-b|<\epsilon
.

4.1
A more precise triangle inequality

The concept of triangle
o
-inequality in O-metric spaces can be used to provide sharp inequalities for distances in metric spaces. We begin by recalling the following definition:

Definition 4.1
.

[
17
]

Let
(X,d)
be a metric space, with three distinct points
a,b,c
. The point
b
is said to lie between
a
and
c
if
d(a,c)=d(a,b)+d(b,c)
, and one writes
[abc]
when this holds.

The line generated by two points
a,b\in X
in a metric space, denoted
\overline{ab}
, is defined to be the set consisting of
a,b
and all other points
c
satisfying
[acb]
,
[abc]
or
[bac]
.

We can now state the following definitions:

Definition 4.2
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is said to admit a metric betweenness (or a non-trivial line) if there exists a distinct triple
x,y,z\in X
such that
d(x,z)=d(x,y)+d(y,z)
. In other words,
X
is said to admit a non-trivial line if there is a line in
X
with at least three points.

Definition 4.3
.

A sequence
\{x_{n}\}
of distinct points of a metric space
(X,d)
such that
d(x_{1},x_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(x_{i},x_{i+1})
for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
, is said to be a metric betweenness sequence. In such case, the points
x_{n}
all belong to a line, hence
X
has a line that is at least countably infinite.

Example 4.4
.

The Euclidean metric
d(x,y)=\|x-y\|
in
\mathbb{R}^{\textup{N}}
admits a metric betweenness sequence (hence, a metric betweenness).

Example 4.5
.

Given a set
X
with at least three distinct elements, the discrete metric does not admit any metric betweenness.

On the other hand, a set
X
with at least three distinct elements can also be endowed with a metric for which there is a metric betweenness sequence. This is shown in the following lemma:

Lemma 4.6
.

Given any set
X
with at least three distinct elements, there is a metric
d
on
X
that admits a metric betweenness. In fact, if
X
is infinite, there is some metric
d
that admits a metric betweenness sequence.

Proof.

Suppose
X=\{x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots\}
is countable (possibly finite), with
x_{1}
,
x_{2}
,
x_{3}
all distinct. The function
d(x_{i},x_{j})=|i-j|
is a metric on
X
and admits a metric betweenness
[x_{1}x_{2}x_{3}]
. In the case where
X
is infinite, the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
is a metric betweenness sequence. Suppose now that
X
is uncountable, with a bijective map
\varphi:X\to\mathbb{R}
. The function
d(x,y)=|\varphi(x)-\varphi(y)|
is a metric on
X
and the sequence
\{\varphi^{-1}(n)\}
is a metric betweenness sequence.
∎

Now, let
(X,\tilde{d})
be a metric space and
\{x_{n}\}
a sequence with no metric betweenness. For each
i\in\mathbb{N}
, the following strict inequality holds

\tilde{d}(x_{1},x_{n+1})<\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tilde{d}(x_{i},x_{i+1}).

(11)

In this case, it is interesting to find a more precise triangle inequality.

Observe that the metric space
(X,\tilde{d})
is also an
\tilde{\textnormal{{o}}}
-metric space, with

\tilde{\textnormal{{o}}}(u,v)=\sup\{\tilde{d}(a,c):~\tilde{d}(a,b)=u,~\tilde{d}(b,c)=v\},

(12)

since the triangle
\tilde{\textnormal{{o}}}
-inequality
\tilde{d}(x,z)\leq\tilde{\textnormal{{o}}}(\tilde{d}(x,y),\tilde{d}(y,z))~\forall x,y,z\in X
, holds. In fact,
\bar{\textnormal{{o}}}(u,v)\leq u+v
for all
u,v\geq 0
.

It may not be convenient to work with
\tilde{\textnormal{{o}}}
. We consider the case where
\tilde{d}
is a composition of some function and a metric.
Suppose that there is another metric
d
on
X
such that
\tilde{d}=\theta\circ d
, where
\theta:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is an increasing sub-additive map. By simple verification,

-metric space with

\textnormal{{o}}(u,v)=\theta(\theta^{-1}(u)+\theta^{-1}(v))~~\forall u,v\geq 0.

(13)

One can check that
\tilde{\textnormal{{o}}}(u,v)\leq\textnormal{{o}}(u,v)\leq u+v

[xyz]

A straightforward computation of the triangle

\tilde{d}(x_{1},x_{n+1})\leq\theta\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\theta^{-1}(\tilde{d}(x_{i},x_{i+1}))\right).

(14)

With the aid of (

\tilde{d}(x_{i},x_{i+1})=\alpha

\tilde{d}(x_{1},x_{n+1})\leq n\alpha~~\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

\tilde{d}(x_{1},x_{n+1})\leq\theta(n\theta^{-1}(\alpha))~~\forall n\in\mathbb{N}.

\theta(n\theta^{-1}(\alpha))\leq n\alpha

Example 4.7

In
