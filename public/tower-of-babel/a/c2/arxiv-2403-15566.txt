Non-existence of Ulrich modules over Cohen-Macaulay local rings
Srikanth B. Iyengar, Linquan Ma, Mark E. Walker, Ziquan Zhuang

Abstract
. Over a Cohen-Macaulay local ring, the minimal number of generators of a maximal Cohen-Macaulay module is bounded above by its multiplicity. In 1984 Ulrich asked whether there always exist modules for which equality holds; such modules are known nowadays as Ulrich modules. We answer this question in the negative by constructing families of two dimensional Cohen-Macaulay local rings that have no Ulrich modules. Some of these examples are Gorenstein normal domains; others are even complete intersection domains, though not normal.

1.
Introduction

This work concerns the question of existence of certain objects over a commutative noetherian local ring
R
. A prime example of such an
R
is the local ring of functions at a point on an algebraic variety. In this case the structural properties of
R
reflect the geometric properties of the variety at that point. For simplicity of exposition, we assume the ring
R
is a domain, which in the geometric context corresponds to varieties that are irreducible at the point represented by the variety. As for groups, one can study
R
through its representations, which in this context are called modules. In what follows we focus on a class of special class of modules called maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules, and assume that
R
itself is Cohen-Macaulay; this ensures that there are many maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules. When
R
is a regular local ring (which is the analogue of a smooth point on a variety), maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules are all free, and in fact this property characterizes regularity. So the failure of maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules to be free is one measure of the singularity of the ring
R
.

Two of the most basic invariants attached to any module
M
are the minimal number of elements needed to generate it, which is denoted
\nu_{R}(M)
, and the largest cardinality of a linearly independent set, which is called its rank and denoted
\mathrm{rank}_{R}(M)
. Clearly
\mathrm{rank}_{R}(M)\leq\nu_{R}(M)
, but in general there is no a priori bound on the gap between these invariants. However, when
M
is a maximal Cohen-Macaulay module, Ulrich
[
22
]
proved that there is an inequality
\nu_{R}(M)\leq e(R)\cdot\mathrm{rank}_{R}(M)
. Here
e(R)
is the Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity of
R
, which is one measure of singularity of
R
. For instance, regular local rings
R
are characterized by the property that
e(R)=1
.

Ulrich asked whether there always exist a module
M
for which
\nu_{R}(M)=e(R)\cdot\mathrm{rank}_{R}(M)
; such modules were subsequently named
Ulrich modules
.
It follows from the discussion above that
R
itself is an Ulrich module precisely when
R
is regular. Inspired by Ulrich’s work, in 2003 Eisenbud and Schreyer
[
4
]
introduced an analogous notion for projective varieties in algebraic geometry, which they called
Ulrich sheaves
. When
R
has an Ulrich module, one can often reduce problems over
R
to the case of regular rings, where they are more tractable. In the almost four decades since Ulrich modules were introduced, it was discovered that many open conjectures in commutative algebra and algebra geometry are simple consequences of the existence of Ulrich modules and sheaves. In the meantime, Ulrich modules and sheaves have been constructed in many special cases, under various algebraic and geometric assumptions.

In this paper we construct Cohen-Macaulay rings of dimension two and higher, some of which are even Gorenstein and normal, and others that are complete intersections, that possess no Ulrich modules. Our technique for producing such examples has a geometric flavor, as it involves a relationship between Ulrich modules on
R
and Ulrich sheaves on a projective variety naturally associated to
R
, namely, the exceptional fiber of the blow-up of
R
at its maximal ideal. Ulrich sheaves that arise thus are special in that they can be extended to the entire blow-up.

While this paper settles Ulrich’s question in the negative on the algebraic side, the existence of Ulrich sheaves remains an open and interesting question.

A more detailed introduction

An

The mere existence of an Ulrich

2.
Ulrich modules and Ulrich sheaves

Let
(R,\mathfrak{m},k)
be a noetherian local ring with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
and residue field
k
.
Let
M
be a nonzero finitely generated
R
-module. The Krull dimension and depth of
M
are denoted
\dim_{R}M
and
0pt_{R}(M)
, respectively. The
R
-module
M
is
maximal Cohen-Macaulay
if it is finitely generated and
0pt_{R}(M)=\dim R
. We write
\nu_{R}(M)
for the minimal number of generators of
M
, and
e_{R}(M)
for its Hilbert-Samuel multiplicity with respect to the ideal
\mathfrak{m}
.

Ulrich modules

Any maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module
M
satisfies
\nu_{R}(M)\leq e_{R}(M)
; see
[
22
, Section 3]
. When
e_{R}(M)=\nu_{R}(M)
and
M
is nonzero it is said to be an
Ulrich module
.

Assume
k
is infinite and let
J\subseteq\mathfrak{m}
be a minimal reduction of
\mathfrak{m}
. For every maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module
M
, we have

e(\mathfrak{m},M)=e(J,M)=\ell_{R}(M/JM)\geq\ell_{R}(M/\mathfrak{m}M)=\nu_{R}(M)\,.

Thus a nonzero maximal Cohen-Macaulay module
M
is Ulrich if and only if
JM=\mathfrak{m}M
for one (or equivalently, every) minimal reduction
J
of
\mathfrak{m}
.

Ulrich sheaves

Let
X
be a projective scheme over a field and let
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
be a very ample line bundle on
X
. A coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
is called an
Ulrich sheaf for
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
if it is nonzero and one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

(1)

\operatorname{H}^{i}(X,\mathcal{F}(j))=0
for all
-\dim X\leq j\leq-1
, and all
i
.

(2)

\operatorname{H}^{i}(X,\mathcal{F}(j))=0
except when
i=0
,
j\geq 0
or
i=\dim X
,
j\leq-\dim X-1
.

Suppose
\pi\colon X\to\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k}
is a finite map such that
\pi^{*}\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k}}(1)=\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
; such a map exists when
k
is infinite. Then a coherent sheaf
\mathcal{F}
on
X
is Ulrich if and only if
\pi_{*}\mathcal{F}\cong\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^{n}_{k}}^{r}
for some integer
r\geq 1
;
see Eisenbud and Schreyer
[
4
]
.

The definition of an Ulrich sheaf depends on the very ample line bundle
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
. We suppress
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
when it is clear from the context. For instance, when
R
is a standard graded noetherian ring over a field and
X=\operatorname{Proj}(R)
, we take
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
to be the coherent sheaf associated to
R(1)
.

Remark 2.1
.

If
\dim X=1
, then
\mathcal{F}
is an Ulrich sheaf if and only if
\operatorname{H}^{i}(X,\mathcal{F}(-1))=0
for all
i
and
\mathcal{F}\neq 0
. Thus the existence of an Ulrich sheaf for
(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}(1))
is equivalent to the existence of a nonzero coherent sheaf on
X
with no cohomology, and the latter property does not depend on the choice of
\mathcal{O}_{X}(1)
.

Let

Proof.

Since

Here

3.
Criteria for non-existence

In this section we identify obstructions to the existence of an Ulrich module over a local ring in terms of the geometry of the blowup at its maximal ideal.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
R
be a noetherian ring and
\mathfrak{m}\subset R
a maximal ideal with
\dim R_{\mathfrak{m}}\geq 2
. Let
\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)\to\operatorname{Spec}R
be the blowup at
\{\mathfrak{m}\}
and
E
the exceptional fiber of the blowup. If there exists a locally principal ideal sheaf
{\mathcal{I}}
on
\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)
and an integer

such that
{\mathcal{I}}^{n}
defines
E
in
\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)

R_{\mathfrak{m}}
nor its

-adic completion has any Ulrich modules.

Proof.

Let
\widehat{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}
denote the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic completion of
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
. Since the maps
R\to R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and

are flat, it is easy to verify that the hypotheses carry over to both
R_{\mathfrak{m}}
and
\widehat{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}

R
is local, with maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}
, and that

.

In what follows we write

\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)

{\mathcal{I}}^{n}\cong\mathcal{O}_{B}(1)

be the subscheme of

.

We first consider the case when

; equivalently,

Assume to the contrary that there exists an Ulrich

and

is ample,
\operatorname{S}(V,\mathcal{L})
is a finitely generated graded
k
-algebra.

Corollary 3.3
.

Let
V
be a projective
k
-scheme with
\dim V\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{L}
be an ample line bundle on
V
, and set
S=\operatorname{S}(V,\mathcal{L})
. Suppose there is an integer
a\geq 2
so that the following conditions hold:

(1)

S_{0}=k
and
S_{j}=0
for
1\leq j\leq a-1
;

(2)

the multiplication map
S_{a}\otimes_{k}S_{j}\to S_{a+j}
is surjective for all
j\geq a
.

Then for
\mathfrak{m}=S_{\geqslant 1}
, neither the local ring
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
nor its
\mathfrak{m}
-adic completion admits an Ulrich module.

Proof.

We claim that as schemes over
\operatorname{Spec}S
one has

\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{Spec}}_{V}(\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{L}))\quad\text{where $\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{L})$ is the sheaf of algebras $\bigoplus_{i\geqslant 0}\mathcal{L}^{i}$.}

To verify this, we first verify by an induction on
j
that
\mathfrak{m}^{j}=S_{\geqslant ja}
for all
j\geq 0
.

The base case
j=1
is clear. Assuming the desired equality holds for some
j\geq 1
, one gets equalities

\mathfrak{m}^{j+1}=\mathfrak{m}\mathfrak{m}^{j}=S_{\geqslant a}\cdot S_{\geqslant aj}=S_{\geqslant(j+1)a}

where the second one is by the induction hypothesis, and the last one is immediate from condition (2) in the statement.

The equality
\mathfrak{m}^{j}=S_{\geqslant ja}
justifies the first isomorphism below:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)\cong\operatorname{Proj}\big(\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 0}S_{\geqslant ja}t^{j}\big)\cong\operatorname{Proj}\big(\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 0}S_{\geqslant j}t^{j}\big)\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{Spec}}_{V}(\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{L}))\,,

The second one holds as
\oplus_{j\geqslant 0}S_{\geqslant ja}t^{j}
is the
a
-th Veronese subring of
\oplus_{j\geqslant 0}S_{\geqslant j}t^{j}
, and the last isomorphism holds because
\mathcal{L}
is ample; see
[
8
, Section 8.7.3]
or
[
12
, Paragraph 6.2.1]
. These isomorphisms are compatible with projections to
\operatorname{Spec}S
.

This justifies the claim about the blowup.

The exceptional fiber
E
of the blowup
\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)\to\operatorname{Spec}S
is the subscheme of
\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)
cut out by
\mathcal{L}^{a}
, that is to say

E\cong\operatorname{\mathbf{Spec}}_{V}\left(\frac{\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{L})}{\mathcal{L}^{a}\,\mathrm{Sym}(\mathcal{L})}\right)\,.

As
V
is the subscheme of
\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)
cut out by
\mathcal{L}
and
a\geq 2
, we can apply Theorem
3.1
to get the desired result.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

Conditions (1) and (2) imply that
S
is generated as a
k
-algebra by its components in degrees
[a,2a-1]
. However the latter condition is weaker: the
k
-algebra
k[x,y]
with
|x|=2
and
|y|=3
is generated by its components in degrees
[2,3]
, but the map
S_{2}\otimes_{k}S_{4}\to S_{6}
is not surjective because
y^{2}
is not in the image.

Corollary
3.3
brings up the question: When is a noetherian graded ring over a field the section ring of an ample line bundle on a projective scheme? Here is one answer; it is extrapolates from
[
13
, Proposition 2.1]
, where the result is stated for normal domains. The argument does not require these conditions, and we sketch a proof, to convince ourselves.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
k
be a field and
S
a noetherian
\mathbb{N}
-graded
k
-algebra with
S_{0}=k
and
0ptS\geq 2
. Then
S
is the section ring of an ample line bundle on a projective scheme over
k
if and only if there exist homogeneous elements
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
in
S
such that
\sqrt{(x_{0},\dots,x_{n})}=S_{\geqslant 1}
and the
\mathbb{Z}
-graded rings
S_{x_{i}}
have a unit of degree
1
.

Proof.

Suppose
S=\operatorname{S}(X,\mathcal{L})
. Choose an integer
m\gg 0
such that
\mathcal{L}^{m}
and
\mathcal{L}^{m+1}
are very ample (see
[
10
, Theorem II, 7.6]
and
[
16
, Theorem 1.2.6]
) and let
s_{1},\dots,s_{r}
and
t_{1},\dots,t_{l}
be bases of
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{L}^{m})
and
\Gamma(X,\mathcal{L}^{m+1})
, respectively. One can check that the radical of the ideal generated by
x_{ij}=s_{i}t_{j}
, for
1\leq i\leq r
and
1\leq j\leq l
, is
S_{\geqslant 1}
and that
t_{j}^{2}/x_{ij}
in
S_{x_{ij}}
is a unit of degree 1.

For the converse, by computing global sections using the Čech cover associated
to the sequence
\boldsymbol{x}=x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
, one gets an exact sequence

0\longrightarrow\operatorname{H}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{0}(S)\longrightarrow S\xrightarrow{\ \sigma\ }\bigoplus_{j\geqslant 0}\operatorname{H}^{0}(\operatorname{Proj}S;\widetilde{S(j)})\longrightarrow\operatorname{H}_{\mathfrak{m}}^{1}(S)_{j\geqslant 0}\longrightarrow 0\,,

where
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq S_{\geqslant 1}
, the homogeneous maximal ideal of
S
. Since
0ptS\geq 2
, we deduce that the map
\sigma
, which is a map of graded
k
-algebras, is an isomorphism. The hypothesis on
\boldsymbol{x}
translates to the existence of an open cover

\operatorname{Proj}S=D(x_{0})\cup\cdots\cup D(x_{n})

such that one has an isomorphism of
\mathbb{Z}
-graded rings
S_{x_{i}}\cong[S_{x_{i}}]_{0}[t,t^{-1}]
, where
t
has degree
1
. Using this it is easy to check that the natural map

\widetilde{S(i)}\otimes\widetilde{S(j)}\longrightarrow\widetilde{S(i+j)}

is an isomorphism for all integers
i,j
. Hence
\mathcal{L}\coloneqq\widetilde{S(1)}
is invertible and
\widetilde{S(j)}\cong\mathcal{L}^{j}
. It follows that
S
is the section ring of the ample line bundle
\mathcal{L}
on
\operatorname{Proj}S
.
∎

Remark 3.6
.

(Yhee’s example revisited)

Let
k
be a field,
S=k[\boldsymbol{x}]
the polynomial ring over
k
in indeterminates
\boldsymbol{x}=x_{1},\dots,x_{d}
, and set
\mathfrak{m}=(\boldsymbol{x})
. Fix an integer
n\geq 2
, set
J=\mathfrak{m}^{n}
and
R=k\oplus J
, viewed as a subring of
S
. Then
R
is not a section ring.

We claim that the ring
R
satisfies the hypothesis of Corollary
3.2
.

Indeed, the cokernel of the inclusion
{R}_{J}(R)\subseteq{R}_{J}(S)
is
S/R
, and in particular finite dimensional as a
k
-vector space. Thus one gets the first isomorphism below:

\operatorname{Bl}_{J}(R)\xleftarrow{\ \cong\ }\operatorname{Bl}_{J}(S)\cong\operatorname{Bl}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)\cong\mathbb{P}^{d-1}_{S}\,.

The second isomorphism holds as
{R}_{J}(S)
is the
n
th Veronese subring of
{R}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)
, since
J=\mathfrak{m}^{n}
. In particular
\operatorname{Bl}_{J}(R)
is regular. The exceptional fiber of the blowup is
E=\operatorname{Proj}(\operatorname{gr}_{J}(R))
where

\operatorname{gr}_{J}(R)=k\oplus\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{n}}{\mathfrak{m}^{2n}}\oplus\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{2n}}{\mathfrak{m}^{3n}}\oplus\cdots.

Observe that the ideal sheaf, say
\mathcal{I}
, corresponding to
\mathfrak{m}^{n+1}\operatorname{gr}_{J}(R)
is nilpotent, of order
n
, and nonzero; the latter holds because
n\geq 2
. So
E
is not reduced. It is irreducible because
E/\mathcal{I}
is the projective scheme associated to the
k
-algebra

k\oplus\bigoplus_{i\geqslant 1}\frac{\mathfrak{m}^{ni}}{\mathfrak{m}^{ni+1}},

which is isomorphic to the
n
th Veronese subring of
S
, so
E/\mathcal{I}\cong\mathbb{P}^{d-1}_{k}
.

4.
Examples: Complete intersections

In this section we construct complete intersection local rings, some of which are domains, that have no Ulrich modules. Let
k
be a field and consider the
k
-algebra

S\coloneqq\frac{k[s,t,x,y,z]}{(s^{2}-x^{3},st-f(x,y,z),t^{2}-z^{3})}

where
f(x,y,z)
is a homogeneous polynomial in
k[x,y,z]
of the form

f(x,y,z)=y^{3}+f_{2}(x,z)y^{2}+f_{1}(x,z)y+f_{0}(x,z)

where each
f_{i}(x,z)
is in
k[x,z]
. Set the degree of
x,y,z
to
2
and that of
s,t
to
3
. Then
S
is an
\mathbb{N}
-graded ring, with homogeneous maximal ideal
\mathfrak{m}\coloneqq S_{\geqslant 2}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

For any choice of
f(x,y,z)
satisfying the condition above neither the local ring
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
nor its
\mathfrak{m}
-adic completion has an Ulrich module. They are both complete intersections of dimension
2
.

Proof.

It suffices to verify that the graded
k
-algebra
S
has the following properties:

(a)

It is a complete intersection ring, with
\dim S=2
;

(b)

It satisfies the conditions (1) and (2) of Corollary
3.3
, with
a=2
;

(c)

It is the section ring of an ample line bundle on a projective curve.

Proof of (a): It is clear that
\operatorname{gr}_{\mathfrak{m}}(S)
is a quotient of the ring

\frac{k[s,t,x,y,z]}{(s^{2},st,t^{2},x^{3}z^{3}-f(x,y,z)^{2})}\,.

Since
f(x,y,z)
is monic in
y
we get that
\dim S\leq 2
. On the other hand,
S
is defined by three relations, so it must be a complete intersection ring, with
\dim S=2
.

Proof of (b): Evidently
S_{0}=k
and
S_{1}=0
. Let
R
be the second Veronese subring of
S
. It is clear from the relations defining
S
that
R
is the
k
-sub-algebra of
S
generated by
x,y,z
. As an
R
-module,
S
is generated by
1,s,t
. It follows that
S_{2}\cdot S_{j}=S_{j+2}
for
j\geq 2
.

Proof of (c): We use the criterion from Lemma
3.5
. The elements
x,z
form a homogeneous system of parameters for
S
. The element
s/x
is of degree
1
in
S_{x}
, and invertible, with inverse
s/x^{2}
. By symmetry,
t/z
is a unit of degree
1
in
S_{z}
.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

The preceding theorem provides a family of complete intersections with no Ulrich modules. Special values of
f(x,y,z)
yield rings with further interesting properties.

The case
f(x,y,z)=y^{3}+x^{2}z

Then the ring in question is a domain

S\coloneqq\frac{k[s,t,x,y,z]}{(s^{2}-x^{3},st-(y^{3}+x^{2}z),t^{2}-z^{3})}.

For
k=\mathbb{Q}
, this claim can be verified using, for instance, Macaulay2
[
7
]
. Here is a sketch of an argument for general
k
: Since
x
is a parameter in
S
, it is not a zero-divisor, so it suffices to check that the ring
S[x^{-1}]
is a domain. One has

S[x^{-1}]=\frac{A[x^{-1},s]}{(s^{2}-x^{3})}\quad\text{where}\quad A=\frac{k[x,y,z]}{((y^{3}+x^{2}z)^{2}-x^{3}z^{3})}

It thus suffices to verify that
A
is a domain and
x^{3}
does not have a square root in the fraction field of
A
.

The property that
A
is a domain translates to the irreducibility of the polynomial
(y^{3}-x^{2}z)^{2}-x^{3}z^{3}
. Specializing
y=1
, it suffices to verify that the polynomial

x^{4}z^{2}-x^{3}z^{3}-2x^{2}z+1

is irreducible. This can be done easily using the Newton polytope associated to this polynomial, as described in Gao
[
5
]
: The polytope is a triangle with vertices

v_{0}=(0,0),v_{1}=(3,3),v_{2}=(4,2).

The greatest common divisor of the coordinates of
v_{0}-v_{1}
and
v_{0}-v_{2}
is
1
, so the polytope is integrally indecomposable, by
[
5
, Corollary 4.5]
. We conclude that polynomial in question is irreducible; see
[
5
, p. 507]
.

Finally, if
x^{3}
has a square root in the fraction field of
A
, then so is
x
. Thus there exist nonzero elements
a,b\in A
such that
a^{2}x=b^{2}
. Since
A
can be standard graded, with degrees of
x,y,z
equal to
1
, this is not possible since the highest degree term in
a^{2}x
has odd degree while the highest degree term in
b^{2}
has even degree.

The case
f(x,y,z)=y^{3}
and
k
is algebraically closed

The complete intersection in question is then

S\coloneqq\frac{k[s,t,x,y,z]}{(s^{2}-x^{3},st-y^{3},t^{2}-z^{3})}

and this is closely connected to Yhee’s construction
[
23
]
of domains (not Cohen-Macaulay) that have no Ulrich modules.

Indeed, according to Macaulay2
[
7
]
, the ring
S
has three minimal prime ideals. Evidently, for any of minimal prime
\mathfrak{p}
the domain
S/\mathfrak{p}
(or rather it localization at the maximal ideal) cannot have Ulrich modules, for any Ulrich module over it would be Ulrich over
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
. Again according to Macaulay2
[
7
]
, one of the prime ideals is

\mathfrak{p}=(s^{2}-x^{3},st-xyz,z^{3}-t^{2},y^{2}-xz,sz^{2}-xyt,yzs-x^{2}t)

as an ideal in the underlying polynomial ring. The assignment

s\mapsto u^{3},t\mapsto v^{3},x\mapsto u^{2},y\mapsto uv,z\mapsto v^{2}

induces an isomorphism of
k
-algebras

S/\mathfrak{p}\cong k[u^{2},uv,v^{2},u^{3},v^{3}]\subset k[u,v].

This subring differs from the subring
k+(u,v)^{2}
considered in
[
23
]
, as discussed in the previous section, only in containing
u^{2}v,uv^{2}
, and one can argue as in
[
23
]
(or as in the previous section) to deduce that
S/\mathfrak{p}
does not have Ulrich modules.

The same analysis carries over to the quotient of
S
by the other minimal primes. Summing up, the complete intersection ring
S
above can be thought of being obtained by gluing three copies of the (non Cohen-Macaulay) ring constructed by Yhee in
[
23
]
, as discussed in the previous section.

Remark 4.2
.

More generally, consider the
k
-algebra

S\coloneqq\frac{k[s_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{ij}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\leq n]}{(s_{i}s_{j}-x_{ij}^{3}\mid 1\leq i\leq j\leq n)}\,.

Then
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
is a complete intersection of dimension
n
, and an argument similar to the proof of Theorem
4.1
shows that
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
does not admit Ulrich modules. Moreover the ideal
(x_{11},\dots,x_{nn})
is a minimal reduction of
\mathfrak{m}
; it is easy to check the other variables are integral over this ideal. Thus

\displaystyle e(S_{\mathfrak{m}})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{length}_{S}\left(\frac{S}{(x_{11},\dots,x_{nn})}\right)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{length}_{S}\left(\frac{k[s_{1},\dots,s_{n},x_{ij}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n]}{(s_{1}^{2},\dots,s_{n}^{2},s_{i}s_{j}-x_{ij}^{3}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n)}\right)

\displaystyle=2^{n}\cdot 3^{\binom{n}{2}}.

where the last equality follows because the ring in question is a finite free extension of
k[s_{1},\dots,s_{n}]/(s_{1}^{2},\dots,s_{n}^{2})
of degree
3^{\binom{n}{2}}
. Note that
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
is not a strict complete intersection, as the product of the
\mathfrak{m}
-adic orders of the defining equations is
2^{\binom{n+1}{2}}
. One thus gets a family of complete intersections with no Ulrich modules, and for which the difference between the orders and multiplicity is arbitrarily large.

5.
Examples: Gorenstein normal domains

In this section we construct Gorenstein normal domains with no Ulrich modules.

Let
C
be a smooth non-hyperelliptic curve of genus
g\geq 3
(for example, a smooth plane quartic) over an algebraically closed field
k
. For simplicity, we assume that char
(k)=0
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a non-effective theta characteristic on
C
; in other words

\mathcal{L}^{\otimes 2}\cong\omega_{C}\quad\text{and}\quad\Gamma(C,\mathcal{L})=0\,.

It is a classical result
[
18
]
(see also
[
1
]
) that non-effective theta characteristic exists on any smooth curve. Consider, for example, the curve

\operatorname{Proj}\frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]}{(x^{4}+y^{4}+z^{4})}\,.

For any plane quartic, there are
2^{2g}=64
theta characteristics, but only
28
of them are effective (corresponding to the
28
bitangents); see
[
10
, Exercise IV, 2.3(h)]
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Set
S\coloneqq\operatorname{S}(C,\mathcal{L})
and let
\mathfrak{m}
be the homogeneous maximal ideal of
S
. Then neither the local ring
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
nor its completion at
\mathfrak{m}
admits an Ulrich module. They are both Gorenstein and normal.

Proof.

To begin with
S
is normal because it is the section ring of smooth projective variety, with respect to an ample invertible sheaf; see
[
20
, Section 1]
. It is also Gorenstein by
[
15
, Proposition 3.14(4)]
. These conditions are then inherited by
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
and its completion. For the rest, we show that the conditions in Corollary
3.3
are satisfied with
a=2
.

Indeed, since
C
is not hyperelliptic, the canonical ring
\operatorname{S}(C,\omega_{C})
is generated in degree 1 by a classical theorem of Max Noether. As
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes 2}\cong\omega_{C}
, we deduce that the multiplication map
\mathrm{Sym}^{m}(S_{2})\to S_{2m}
is surjective for all integers
m
, hence
S_{2}\otimes_{k}S_{j}\to S_{2+j}
is also surjective for all even integers
j\geq 2
. On the other hand, the line bundle
\mathcal{L}^{\otimes 3}
on
C
is Castelnuovo-Mumford
0
-regular (with respect to the very ample line bundle
\omega_{C}
). To see this, it suffices to note that
\chi(\mathcal{L})=0
by Riemann-Roch, and hence

\operatorname{H}^{1}(C,\mathcal{L}^{\otimes 3}\otimes\omega_{C}^{-1})=\operatorname{H}^{1}(C,\mathcal{L})=\operatorname{H}^{0}(C,\mathcal{L})=0\,.

By
[
16
, Theorem 1.8.3]
, it follows that the multiplication map
S_{3}\otimes\mathrm{Sym}^{m}(S_{2})\to S_{2m+3}
is also surjective for all integers
m
, which in turn implies the surjectivity of
S_{2}\otimes_{k}S_{j}\to S_{2+j}
for all odd integers
j\geq 3
. This verifies the condition (2) from Corollary
3.3
, while condition (1) is clear from the choice of
\mathcal{L}
.
∎

Remark 5.2
.

With
g
the genus of the curve
C
, a direct computation gives that the Hilbert series of the ring
S
in Theorem
5.1
is

\frac{(1-2t+(g+1)t^{2}-2t^{3}+t^{4})}{(1-t)^{2}}

If
S
were a complete intersection, the numerator would be product of cyclotomic polynomials. One can check that this is not the case.

The ring
S_{\mathfrak{m}}
in Theorem
5.1
is also not a rational singularity. In fact, rational surface singularities have minimal multiplicities—see
[
19
, Theorem on page 94]
—and such rings admit Ulrich modules, see
[
2
, Proposition 2.5]
. It is an interesting question whether there exist rational singularities with no Ulrich modules.
