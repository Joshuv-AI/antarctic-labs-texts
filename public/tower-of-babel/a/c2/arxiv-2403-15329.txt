Optimal Data-Driven Prediction and Predictive Control using Signal Matrix Models
Roy S. Smith, Mohamed Abdalmoaty, & Mingzhou Yin

Abstract
Data-driven control uses a past signal trajectory to characterise the input-output behaviour of a system. Willems’ lemma provides a data-based prediction model allowing a control designer to bypass the step of identifying a state-space or transfer function model. This paper provides a more parsimonious formulation of Willems’ lemma that separates the model into initial condition matching and predictive control design parts. This avoids the need for regularisers in the predictive control problem that are found in other data-driven predictive control methods. It also gives a closed form expression for the optimal (minimum variance) unbiased predictor of the future output trajectory and applies it for predictive control. Simulation comparisons illustrate very good control performance.



I
Introduction

Model-based control design has a long history. Obtaining a suitable model can be a challenging task and
is often addressed by a combination of first principles modeling and identification from data. The modeling and
design steps are not independent and the best model depends strongly on the control design objectives.
The recent resurgence in learning methods has driven a renewed interest in designing control systems
directly from the measured system signal data. One potential benefit of these data-driven methods
is that bypassing the modeling step in the design process might allow the process to be simplified, or
even semi-automated, avoiding the costly and time-consuming identification step. However many
of the experiment design considerations that go into system identification process arise in obtaining
signal data for the data-driven methods.

This class of data-driven control should not be considered as being “model-free”. Rather, the
parameterisation of the model is in terms of a set of past data. This data-based model is used
for prediction of the system responses to future control input signals.
Our term for this class of models is Signal Matrix Models (SMM).

We start from the framework used in the current data-driven
control work (summarised in
[
1
,
2
]
), and formulate
the problem as decoupled prediction and predictive control steps. This allows us to pose and solve
optimal prediction problems which are tailored to the predictive control objective. One major advantage is
that regularisation is not required for the prediction and control steps, in contrast to the current
data-driven approaches.

Our development will bring out the strong connection between Subspace Predictive Control
(SPC)
[
3
]
and the more recent data-driven methods based on Willems’
Lemma
[
4
,
5
]
). Data Enabled Predictive Control
(DeePC)
[
6
]
is a prototypical approach and uses a single vector variable
to parametrise both the initial condition matching, or equivalently the prediction, and the
predictive control steps. Achieving the best trade-off between these conflicting conditions is
achieved via regularisation.

The method developed by
[
7
,
8
]
, known as
\gamma
-DDPC,
reformulates the characterisation of the dynamics and provides a different regularisation
approach. One choice of regularisers gives an equivalence with DeePC. Generalised
Data-Driven Predictive Control (GDPC), described in
[
9
]
, splits the control
input in two with one part using the SPC prediction methods to predict the output response
and the other optimised online to satisfy the control objectives.
GDPC also requires regularisation to achieve the prediction-control
trade-off for the optimised part of the control input signal.

High fidelity simulations, or digital twins, generate ideal data for this approach. If
dedicated experiments are possible noise and excitation problems can be addressed
through experiment design. Data taken from historical operation measurements can
have both noise and persistency of excitation problems. The regularisation approaches
of DeePC,
\gamma
-DDPC, and GDPC, can help ameliorate the noise problem, but
a theoretical basis for this is incomplete. The work in
[
10
]

develops maximum likelihood methods for the case where the SMM is corrupted by
stochastic noise. Its use in predictive control requires some heuristics.

Our approach is somewhat different. We will consider
H_{y}

to be noise-free and derive the optimal predictor to be used in the predictive control step.
In the case that there is noise in
H_{y}
this amounts to considering a high-order
model—one that fits the noise in addition to the system response. The method given
here can be further improved by applying denoising methods to the SMM
matrices (see
[
11
]
).

II
Data-Driven Control

II-A

Problem Formulation

The problem to be considered is the control of a linear time-invariant system,
G
,
with input,
u(k)\in{\cal R}^{n_{u}}
, and output
y(k)\in{\cal R}^{n_{y}}
. We assume that system

G
has finite order,
n_{x}
. Rather than specify
G
in terms of
a order-
n_{x}
minimal state-space representation, our characterisation of
G
is in
terms of a past record of input-output data over
K
consecutive time-steps,
(
u^{\text{d}}(k),y^{\text{d}}(k)
),
k=1,\dots,K
.
The superscript,
d
, is used to contrast the past data with current or future values of
u
and
y
.
In the SMM development we assume that
u^{\text{d}}
and
y^{\text{d}}
are noise-free.

The predictive controller uses input and measured output signals from
an immediate past horizon of length-
T_{\text{p}}
to calculate the optimal control
input over a length-
T_{\text{f}}
horizon into the future. The measured output
contains i.i.d. noise. At every time-step
k
,

y_{\text{meas}}(k)=y(k)+v(k),

(1)

with
{\cal E}\!\left\{v(k)\right\}=0
, and
\operatorname{var}\!\left(v(k)\right)=\Sigma_{v}
.
The length-
T_{\text{p}}
sequence of past inputs and outputs are written
as vectors,

u_{\text{p}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}u(k-T_{\text{p}}+1)\\
\vdots\\
u(k)\end{bmatrix}\text{ and }y_{\text{p}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}y(k-T_{\text{p}}+1)\\
\vdots\\
y(k)\end{bmatrix}.

(2)

Future inputs and outputs are indexed from the next time step,

u_{\text{f}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}u(k+1)\\
\vdots\\
u(k+T_{\text{f}})\end{bmatrix}\quad\text{and}\quad y_{\text{f}}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}y(k+1)\\
\vdots\\
y(k+T_{\text{f}})\end{bmatrix}.

(3)

The control problem is posed in terms of a cost function over the length-
T_{\text{f}}

future horizon. For simplicity we will consider a quadratic cost, defined by

P\in{\cal S}_{+}^{\,n_{y}T_{\text{f}}}
and
R\in{\cal S}_{+}^{\,n_{u}T_{\text{f}}}
, but more
general cost functions are easily handled.

Problem 1
(Data-driven predictive control)

Given an immediate past input and measured output signals,
u_{\text{p}}(k)
and
y_{\text{p,meas}}(k)
,
find a future input sequence,
u_{\text{f}}(k)
, solving

\displaystyle\minimise_{u_{\text{f}},y_{\text{f}}}\quad

\displaystyle\|y_{\text{f}}\|_{P}+\|u_{\text{f}}\|_{R},

\displaystyle\st\quad

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}y_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}=G\left(\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
u_{\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}\right),\quad u_{\text{f}}\in{\cal U},\quad y_{\text{f}}\in{\cal Y}.

(4)

In the standard receding horizon predictive control structure only the first time step of
u_{\text{f}}

is applied to the plant. At the next time-step new measurement data is available and the
entire problem is resolved.

The sets
{\cal U}
and
{\cal Y}
denote input and output constraint sets, which are typically
assumed to be convex. There are several difficulties in the above formulation. To begin,
we have only the measured past output,
y_{\text{p,meas}}
, but the characterisation of the
dynamics applies to (at best) the noise-free past output,
y_{\text{p}}
. In addition, the
characterisation of the plant input-output mapping above is in a generic operator form.
Standard model predictive control assumes that
the future output,
y_{\text{f}}
, is characterised in terms of a measured state and the future
input,
u_{\text{f}}
. Data-driven predictive control, on the other hand, uses the characterisation
of
y_{\text{f}}
directly in terms of
u_{\text{p}}
,
y_{\text{p}}
, and
u_{\text{f}}
. The basis of this is the Willems’ Lemma
described in the next section.

II-B

Willems’ Fundamental Lemma

Our model of the system is specified in terms of a single, length-
K
, trajectory.
The extension to multiple trajectories is straightforward and given
in
[
12
]
. The input-output model will characterise all length-
T
input-output
data sequences that can be generated by the system.

Hankel matrices, of dimension
(n_{u}T)\times M
and dimension
(n_{y}T)\times M
are created,

H_{u}=\begin{bmatrix}u^{\text{d}}(1)&u^{\text{d}}(2)&\cdots&u^{\text{d}}(M)\\
u^{\text{d}}(2)&u^{\text{d}}(2)&\cdots&u^{\text{d}}(M+1)\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
u^{\text{d}}(T)&u^{\text{d}}(T+1)&\cdots&u^{\text{d}}(M+T-1)\end{bmatrix}

and

H_{y}=\begin{bmatrix}y^{\text{d}}(1)&y^{\text{d}}(2)&\cdots&y^{\text{d}}(M)\\
y^{\text{d}}(2)&y^{\text{d}}(3)&\cdots&y^{\text{d}}(M+1)\\
\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\
y^{\text{d}}(T)&y^{\text{d}}(T+1)&\cdots&y^{\text{d}}(M+T-1)\end{bmatrix}.

We assume that
T
has been chosen so that
n_{y}T_{\text{p}}\geq n_{x}
.
Furthermore
M
has been chosen such that
M\geq 2T(n_{u}+n_{y})
.
The input sequence
u
is assumed to be
persistently exciting of order at least
n_{u}T+n_{x}
. These conditions
require that the data length satisfies,

K\geq M+T-1.

Under the persistency of excitation assumption

H_{u}
has full row rank, equal here to
n_{u}T
.
Generically
H_{y}
also has full row rank (equal to

n_{y}T
).

Lemma 2
(Willems’ Fundamental Lemma)

Under the matrix-size and persistency
of excitation assumptions, the length-
T
input-output pair (
u
,
y
) is
a trajectory of the system
G
, iff there exists
g\in{\cal R}^{M}

such that,

\begin{bmatrix}u\\
y\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u}\\
H_{y}\end{bmatrix}g.

(5)

See Theorem 1 in
[
4
]
and Lemma 2 in
[
13
]
.

It follows that the matrix of stacked Hankel matrices,
\begin{bmatrix}H_{u}^{T}&H_{y}^{T}\end{bmatrix}^{T}
,
has rank equal to
n_{u}T+n_{x}
. The if and only if nature of the result
means that the matrix multiplication in (
5
) can be used
as a complete characterisation of the trajectories specified by Equation
4

in Problem
1
.

II-C

Data-Driven Predictive Control: DeePC

To apply (
5
) to predictive control we partition the signal sequences into
past and future, as given in (
2
) and (
3
), satisfying

T=T_{\text{p}}+T_{\text{f}}
.
We also partition the
H_{u}
and
H_{y}
correspondingly,

H_{u}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{u\text{p}}\\
\hline\cr H_{u\text{f}}\end{array}\right],\text{ with }H_{u\text{p}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{u}T_{\text{p}})\times M},H_{u\text{f}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{u}T_{\text{f}})\times M},

and

H_{y}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}H_{y\text{p}}\\
\hline\cr H_{y\text{f}}\end{array}\right],\text{ with }H_{y\text{p}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{y}T_{\text{p}})\times M},H_{y\text{f}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{y}T_{\text{f}})\times M}.

We assume that this partition satisfies
n_{y}T_{\text{p}}\geq n_{x}
so that
the assumptions in Lemma
2
also apply to
H_{yu\text{p}}
(defined subsequently).

As reordering the rows of both
u
and
y
commensurately with reordering the rows of the
stacked Hankel matrices in (
5
) does not affect
g
, we can replace (
5
)
with the following characterisation of the dynamics.

\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{p}}\\
u_{\text{f}}\\
y_{\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{p}}\\
H_{y\text{p}}\\
H_{u\text{f}}\\
H_{y\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}g

(6)

Applying this characterisation of the dynamics in Problem
1
gives the DeePC problem formulation.

Problem 3
(DeePC)

Given an immediate past input and measured output signals,
u_{\text{p}}(k)
and
y_{\text{p,meas}}(k)
,
find a future input sequence,
u_{\text{f}}(k)
, solving

\displaystyle\minimise_{g,u_{\text{f}},y_{\text{f}},\sigma_{y}}\quad

\displaystyle\|y_{\text{f}}\|_{P}+\|u_{\text{f}}\|_{R}+\lambda_{1}\|\sigma_{y}\|^{2}_{2}+\lambda_{2}\rho(g)

\displaystyle\st\quad

\displaystyle\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{p,meas}}-\sigma_{y}\\
u_{\text{f}}\\
y_{\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{p}}\\
H_{y\text{p}}\\
H_{u\text{f}}\\
H_{y\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}g,

(7)

\displaystyle u_{\text{f}}\in{\cal U},\quad y_{\text{f}}\in{\cal Y}.

The DeePC formulation above has several variants all of which differ in significant ways from the
generic Problem
1
. One difference is that as
y_{\text{p,meas}}
contains noise, it is
almost certain that,

\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{p,meas}}\end{bmatrix}\notin\range\left(\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{p}}\\
H_{y\text{p}}\end{bmatrix}\right).

The variable
\sigma_{y}
acts as a surrogate for this noise, making (
7
) solvable, and its
size minimisation is included as a regularisation term,
\lambda_{1}\|\sigma_{y}\|_{2}^{2}
.

The other aspect is that
g
plays a dual role. The first two block rows of (
7
) are constraining

g
such that the initial conditions (specified by the immediate past data) are close to being satisfied. The
second role is the use of
g
in finding the optimal
u_{\text{f}}
and
y_{\text{f}}
. The potential conflict can be seen by noting
that
g
may select a “best-case” noise
\sigma_{y}
from the point of view of the minimisation of
the quadratic cost. The regularisation of
g
, specified by
\lambda_{2}\rho(g)
, is used
to trade-off between these potential conflicting roles of
g
. In the case where
H_{y}
is actually
created from noisy measurements the matrix of stacked past Hankel matrices is full rank and (
7
)
does not impose any constraint on the initial condition matching (see
[
14
]
). In this case the
regularisation is essential to achieving good performance with DeePC.

The more recent data-driven predictive control formulations,
\gamma
-DDPC and GDPC, improve upon the
DeePC formulation by compressing the representation and partially separating the initial condition matching and
predictive control parameterisations. Regularisations are still required although
[
1
]
provides
simulations that illustrate that they can outperform DeePC.

III
Optimal Multi-step Prediction

Our formulation is conceptually based on the idea that we will separate the
g
vector in (
6
)
into two orthogonal parts,

\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{p}}\\
u_{\text{f}}\\
y_{\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{p}}\\
H_{y\text{p}}\\
H_{u\text{f}}\\
H_{y\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}(g_{\text{p}}+g_{\text{f}}),

(8)

where
g_{\text{p}}
matches the initial conditions specified by
u_{\text{p}}
and
y_{\text{p}}
, and

g_{\text{f}}
specifies the mapping between
u_{\text{f}}
and
y_{\text{f}}
. While
g_{\text{p}}
also specifies a future
input-output trajectory,
H_{u\text{p}}g_{\text{f}}=H_{y\text{p}}g_{\text{f}}=0
. Furthermore
g_{\text{p}}\perp g_{\text{f}}
and so
g_{\text{f}}

can completely determine the
u_{\text{f}}
,
y_{\text{f}}
mapping for the predictive control step.
See
[
15
]
for the use of a similar concept for DeePC regularisation.

III-A

Derivation of a Parsimonious Formulation

The mapping in (
6
) will be condensed into a minimal parameterisation,
similar in motivation to the parameterisations used in
\gamma
-DDPC and GDPC.

Consider stacked Hankel matrices representing the past
and future input-output sequences,

H_{yu\text{p}}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{p}}\\
H_{y\text{p}}\end{bmatrix},\text{ and }H_{yu\text{f}}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{f}}\\
H_{y\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}.

The matrix of immediate past data,
H_{yu\text{p}}
, is factorised
into,

H_{yu\text{p}}=L_{\text{p}}Q_{\text{p}}^{T},

where
L_{\text{p}}\in{\cal R}^{((n_{u}+n_{y})T_{\text{p}})\times M}
is lower triangular
and
Q_{\text{p}}\in{\cal R}^{M\times M}
is unitary.
This can be further partitioned into,

\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{p}}\\
H_{y\text{p}}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ c | c | c}L_{u\text{p}}&0&0\\
\hline\cr L_{yu\text{p}}&L_{y\text{p}}&0\\
\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{ccc}&Q_{u\text{p}}^{T}&\\
\hline\cr&Q_{y\text{p}}^{T}&\\
\hline\cr&Q_{\text{np}}^{T}&\end{array}\right],

(9)

where both
L_{u\text{p}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{u}T_{\text{p}})\times(n_{u}T_{\text{p}})}
and
L_{y\text{p}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{y}T_{\text{p}})\times n_{x}}

are lower triangular. The fact that
L_{y\text{p}}
has only
n_{x}
columns follows from the observation
that Lemma
2
specifies that
\rank(H_{yu\text{p}})=n_{u}T_{\text{p}}+n_{x}
and the persistency of
excitation gives
\rank(L_{u\text{p}})=n_{u}T_{\text{p}}
. This also implies that the dimension of the null space of
H_{yu\text{p}}

(which is also the column dimension of
Q_{\text{np}}
) is
M-n_{u}T_{\text{p}}-n_{x}
.

We can use this factorisation to simplify our representation of all length-
T_{\text{p}}
sequences that could
have been generated by the system. By solving for
x_{u}\in{\cal R}^{n_{u}T_{\text{p}}}
and
x_{y}\in{\cal R}^{n_{x}}

satisfying,

u_{\text{p}}=L_{u\text{p}}x_{u}

and

y_{\text{p}}=L_{yu\text{p}}x_{u}+L_{y\text{p}}x_{y},

we can rewrite the past data part of Willems’ condition as,

\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{p}}\end{bmatrix}=\left[\begin{array}[]{ c | c }L_{u\text{p}}&0\\
\hline\cr L_{yu\text{p}}&L_{y\text{p}}\\
\end{array}\right]\begin{bmatrix}x_{u}\\
x_{y}\end{bmatrix}.

While we will parametrise the solutions in terms of
x_{u}
and
x_{y}
,
it also possible to write
g_{\text{p}}
in (
8
) as,

g_{\text{p}}=\begin{bmatrix}Q_{u\text{p}}&Q_{y\text{p}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{u}\\
x_{y}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that all
g
in the null-space of
H_{yu\text{p}}
are given
by
g=Q_{\text{np}}x_{\text{np}}
where
x_{\text{np}}\in{\cal R}^{M-n_{u}T_{\text{p}}-n_{x}}

and that
g_{\text{p}}
is orthogonal to every column of
Q_{\text{np}}
. We will
use this orthogonal decomposition to characterise all possible future
input-output trajectories that match a specific past input-output trajectory.
The specific
g_{\text{p}}
for a given
u_{\text{p}}
,
y_{\text{p}}
trajectory depends of course
on the trajectory. However the null-space parameterisation
Q_{\text{np}}x_{\text{np}}

is independent of the past trajectory being matched. The consequence of
this independence is that the past input-output trajectory affects the future
y_{\text{f}}

(as we would expect from the dynamics) and also the future
u_{\text{f}}
.

We will now apply the LQ decomposition approach to the Hankel matrices
that generate the length-
T_{\text{f}}
future input-output trajectories. As we want
these to also match a past length-
T_{\text{p}}
input-output trajectory we will constrain
the choice of
g_{\text{f}}
to those that lie within the null-space of
H_{yu\text{p}}
.

To do this the LQ factorisation is applied to
H_{yu\text{f}}Q_{\text{np}}
and

H_{yu\text{f}}Q_{\text{np}}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{f}}\\
H_{y\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}Q_{\text{np}}=L_{\text{f}}Q_{\text{f}}^{T},

(10)

gives a lower triangular
L_{\text{f}}\in{\cal R}^{((n_{u}+n_{y})T_{\text{f}})\times(M-n_{u}T_{\text{p}}-n_{x})}
and a unitary

Q_{\text{f}}\in{\cal R}^{(M-n_{u}T_{\text{p}}-n_{x})\times(M-n_{u}T_{\text{p}}-n_{x})}
. If
Q_{\text{np}}
has at least

(n_{u}+n_{y})T_{\text{f}}
columns, Equation
10
can be partitioned conformally
with the future inputs and outputs,

H_{yu\text{f}}Q_{\text{np}}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c | c}L_{u\text{f}}&0\\
\hline\cr L_{yu\text{f}}&L_{y\text{f}}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}Q_{yu\text{f}}^{T}\\
\hline\cr Q_{\text{nf}}^{T}\end{array}\right],

(11)

where
L_{u\text{f}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{u}T_{\text{f}})\times(n_{u}T_{\text{f}})}
and
L_{yu\text{f}}\in{\cal R}^{(n_{y}T_{\text{f}})\times(n_{u}T_{\text{f}})}
.
Note that the
Q_{\text{np}}
column dimension assumption is satisfied for any
n_{x}\leq n_{y}T_{\text{p}}

if
M\geq 2T(n_{u}+n_{y})
.
The following lemma, proven in the Appendix, illustrates several interesting structural properties of the decomposition
in (
11
).

Lemma 4

The decomposition in (
11
) satisfies:

a)
\rank(L_{u\text{f}})=n_{u}T_{\text{f}}
and b)
L_{y\text{f}}=0
.

We can now reduce the dimension of the
H_{yu\text{f}}Q_{\text{np}}
parametrisation to the column
dimension of
Q_{yu\text{f}}
. Consider

g_{\text{f}}=Q_{\text{np}}Q_{yu\text{f}}z,

with

z\in{\cal R}^{n_{u}T_{\text{f}}}
,
which results in,

H_{yu\text{f}}\,g_{\text{f}}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{u\text{f}}\\
L_{yu\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}z.

Because
L_{u\text{f}}
is full rank, any specified future input will uniquely determine
z
. This in turn
specifies a unique future output via
L_{yu\text{f}}
. However we must also account for the contribution of

x_{u}
and
x_{y}
to the future input and output.

We can now reformulate (
6
) more parsimoniously as

\begin{bmatrix}u_{\text{p}}\\
y_{\text{p}}\\
u_{\text{f}}\\
y_{\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}L_{u\text{p}}&0&0\\
L_{yu\text{p}}&L_{y\text{p}}&0\\
S_{uu}&S_{uy}&L_{u\text{f}}\\
S_{yu}&S_{yy}&L_{yu\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}x_{u}\\
x_{y}\\
z\end{bmatrix},

(12)

where we have defined the
S
matrices via

\begin{bmatrix}S_{uu}&S_{uy}\\
S_{yu}&S_{yy}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}H_{u\text{f}}\\
H_{y\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}Q_{u\text{p}}&Q_{y\text{p}}\end{bmatrix}.

Note that the lower triangular matrix in (
12
) is full column rank,
with rank equal to
n_{u}T+n_{x}
.

III-B

Optimal multi-step predictor

We will now apply the parametrisation in (
12
) to a
T_{\text{f}}
-step ahead
prediction of the future output. Optimality in this context refers to finding the minimum
variance unbiased predictor of the future output.
To begin we pose the prediction problem.

Problem 5

Given a length-
T_{\text{p}}
input-output signal sequence,
u_{\text{p}}
,
y_{\text{p,meas}}
, generated by
the system
G
with output measurement noise specified by (
1
),
and given a specified contiguous length-
T_{\text{f}}
future input
sequence,
u_{\text{f}}
, estimate the future system output,
y_{\text{f}}
(with the estimate
denoted by
\hat{y}_{\text{f}}
).

Consider the following linear form of the estimator,

\hat{y}_{\text{f}}=E_{u\text{p}}u_{\text{p}}+E_{y\text{p}}y_{\text{p,meas}}+E_{u\text{f}}u_{\text{f}},

(13)

where the predictor matrices are given by,

\displaystyle E_{u\text{f}}

\displaystyle=L_{yu\text{f}}L_{u\text{f}}^{-1},\quad E_{yu\text{p}}=L_{yu\text{p}}L_{u\text{p}}^{-1},\quad\Psi=S_{yy}-E_{u\text{f}}^{-1}S_{uy},

\displaystyle E_{u\text{p}}

\displaystyle=(S_{yu}-E_{u\text{f}}S_{uu})L_{u\text{p}}^{-1}-\Psi E_{x_{y}}E_{yu\text{p}},

\displaystyle E_{y\text{p}}

\displaystyle=\Psi E_{x_{y}},

and the matrix
E_{x_{y}}
is given by,

E_{x_{y}}=\left(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}}\right)^{-1}L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1},

where
\Sigma_{V}=I_{T_{\text{p}}}\otimes\Sigma_{v}
.
Note that the predictor matrices
E_{u\text{p}}
,
E_{y\text{p}}
, and
E_{u\text{f}}
, can be calculated
offline.

In SPC a linear predictor of the form in (
13
) is obtained as the
least-squares solution to the data matrix equation,

H_{y\text{f}}=E_{u\text{p}}H_{u\text{p}}+E_{y\text{p}}H_{y\text{p}}+E_{u\text{f}}H_{u\text{f}}.

This is a good heuristic but not optimal. In contrast…

Theorem 6

Given: an LTI system,
G
, uniquely specified by SMM matrices;
a length-
T_{\text{p}}
immediate past data sequence (
u_{\text{p}}
,
y_{\text{p}}
); and
a future input,
u_{\text{f}}
. The estimate
\hat{y}_{\text{f}}
, given in (
13
),
is the best linear unbiased estimate (BLUE) of
y_{\text{f}}
.
Furthermore the covariance of
\hat{y}_{\text{f}}
is equal to

\cov(\hat{y}_{\text{f}})=\Psi(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}})^{-1}\Psi^{T}.

We now have an optimal (BLUE) estimator to apply to the MPC problem.

IV
Signal Matrix Model Predictive Control

One can immediately apply this approach to an MPC
problem with (
13
) specifying the dynamics. The following MPC problem
is an alternative to the DeePC approach given in Problem
3
.

Problem 7
(SMMPC)

Given an immediate past input and measured output signals,
u_{\text{p}}
and
y_{\text{p,meas}}
,
find a future input sequence,
u_{\text{f}}
, solving

\displaystyle\minimise_{u_{\text{f}},\hat{y}_{\text{f}}}\quad

\displaystyle\|\hat{y}_{\text{f}}\|_{P}+\|u_{\text{f}}\|_{R}

\displaystyle\st\quad

\displaystyle\hat{y}_{\text{f}}=E_{u\text{p}}u_{\text{p}}+E_{y\text{p}}y_{\text{p,meas}}+E_{u\text{f}}u_{\text{f}},

\displaystyle u_{\text{f}}\in{\cal U},\quad\hat{y}_{\text{f}}\in{\cal Y}.

In contrast to DeePC,
\gamma
-DDPC, and GDPC, this formulation does not
require the designer to select regularisation parameters. It also has fewer
optimisation variables and as
E_{u\text{p}}
,
E_{y\text{p}}
and
E_{u\text{f}}
are calculated offline
it will run more quickly. As with almost all MPC formulations it is common practice
that the constraint
\hat{y}_{\text{f}}\in{\cal Y}
is specified with a penalised slack variable
to avoid potential infeasibility.

V
Conclusions

As is clear from Willems’ fundamental lemma the SMM matrices specify a
unique causal LTI system of finite order. The model specification in terms of
past input-output trajectories avoids the need to specify a state representation.
But of course the SMM matrix representation is equivalent, in terms of input-output
responses, to a state-space representation. So the strong similarities, such as
the dimension of
x_{y}
being equal to the system order, should not be
surprising.

Another defining characteristic of state-space systems is that the entire past
input-output behaviour affects the future input-output behaviour only via the
initial state.
A similar thing occurs here as
u_{\text{p}}
and
y_{\text{p}}
completely determine
x_{u}
and

x_{y}
which in turn uniquely specify the future input-output relationship.
The difference in the SMM case is that we use
x_{u}
and
x_{y}
to specify a
future input as well as a future output. This contrasts with state-space representations
where the initial state specifies the future output corresponding to a future
input of zero. The benefit of the SMM approach here is that it allows us to
maintain linearity in the input-output relationships making it simpler to
formulate an optimal prediction problem. However the part of the future
input arising from
x_{u}
must be taken into consideration when selecting
the future input for predictive control purposes. This is done via (
13
).

The optimal prediction problem given here is easily solved because the
underlying system is causal and the dynamics are linear. Regularisation
is not required to trade-off between the prediction and control parts of the
problem as they are decoupled. However this doesn’t necessarily mean that
regularisation is not beneficial. The recent developments in using
kernel-based regularisers for identification have given significant benefits
in the system identification domain
[
17
]
and could be
applied here to reduce noise in
H_{y}
when the signals come from experimental
data.

Note also that the variance of the predictor (Theorem
6
)
depends directly on the experimental data used to generate the SMM
matrices
H_{u}
and
H_{y}
. This makes it clear that the issues arising in
experiment and input design for system identification also arise here.

Proof of Lemma
4
:

To show
a)
note that the persistency of excitation assumption
implies that
H_{u}
has rank
n_{u}T
and so
\rank(L_{u\text{f}})=\rank(H_{u\text{f}})

as
n_{u}T-n_{u}T_{\text{p}}=n_{u}T_{\text{f}}
.

To show
b)
Assume that
L_{y\text{f}}\neq 0
and choose a vector

\hat{w}\in{\cal R}^{M-n_{u}T-n_{x}}
such that
L_{y\text{f}}\hat{w}\neq 0
.
Calculate the vector
\hat{g}\in{\cal R}^{M}
via

\hat{g}=Q_{\text{np}}Q_{\text{nf}}\hat{w}.

Now consider the input-sequence corresponding to,

\begin{bmatrix}H_{yu\text{p}}\\
H_{yu\text{f}}\end{bmatrix}\hat{g}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
H_{yu\text{f}}\hat{g}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
L_{\text{f}}Q_{\text{f}}^{T}Q_{\text{nf}}\hat{w}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\begin{bmatrix}0\\
L_{y\text{f}}\hat{w}\end{bmatrix}\end{bmatrix}.

This corresponds to an input-output sequence with

u_{\text{p}}=u_{\text{f}}=y_{\text{p}}=0
,
y_{\text{f}}=L_{y\text{f}}\hat{w}
.
This contradicts the uniqueness assumption on the system
G
as
y_{\text{f}}=0
is also a solution
to this case. Therefore
L_{y\text{f}}=0
.

Proof of Theorem
6

To remove the affine deterministic terms define

\zeta_{\text{f}}=y_{\text{f}}-E_{u\text{p}}u_{\text{p}}-E_{u\text{f}}u_{\text{f}},\text{ and }\nu_{\text{p}}=y_{\text{p}}-E_{yu\text{p}}u_{\text{p}},

and note that the noise-free signals satisfy,

\zeta_{\text{f}}=\Psi x_{y}\text{ and }\nu_{\text{p}}=L_{y\text{p}}x_{y}.

The BLUE of
x_{y}
is,

\hat{x}_{y}=E_{x_{y}}\nu_{\text{p,meas}},

where
\nu_{\text{p,meas}}=y_{\text{p,meas}}-E_{yu\text{p}}u_{\text{p}}
.
The estimator in (
13
) can be reformulated as

\hat{\zeta}_{\text{f}}=\Psi\hat{x}_{y},

(14)

and as it is a linear scaling of BLUE of
x_{y}
, it is the BLUE
of
\hat{\zeta}_{\text{f}}
. The covariance is given by

\cov{\hat{y}_{\text{f}}}=\cov{\hat{\zeta}_{\text{f}}}=\Psi(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}})^{-1}\Psi^{T}.

To show that this is the BLUE consider all linear estimators,

\hat{\zeta}_{\text{f}}=Z^{T}\nu_{\text{p,meas}}.

To be unbiased they must satisfy

{\cal E}\!\left\{Z^{T}\nu_{\text{p,meas}}\right\}=\zeta_{\text{f}},

or

Z^{T}{\cal E}\!\left\{\nu_{\text{p,meas}}\right\}-\Psi x_{y}=0,\quad\text{for all }x_{y}.

As
{\cal E}\!\left\{\nu_{\text{p,meas}}\right\}=L_{y\text{p}}x_{y}
, we must have,

Z^{T}L_{y\text{p}}-\Psi=0.

This implies that
\range(Z^{T})=\range(\Psi)
and
Z^{T}
can be
expressed as a factorisation,

Z^{T}=\Psi F
with
FL_{y\text{p}}=I_{n_{x}}
.

The covariance of the
Z^{T}
estimator is given by
\cov(\hat{\zeta}_{\text{f}})=Z^{T}\Sigma_{V}Z
.
The difference with respect to the estimator in (
14
) is

\displaystyle Z^{T}\Sigma_{V}Z

\displaystyle-\Psi(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}})^{-1}\Psi^{T}

\displaystyle=\Psi F\Sigma_{V}F^{T}\Psi^{T}-\Psi(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}})^{-1}\Psi^{T},

and by exploiting the fact that
I_{n_{x}}=FL_{yu\text{p}},

\displaystyle=\Psi F(\Sigma_{V}-L_{y\text{p}}(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}})^{-1}L_{yu\text{p}}^{T})F^{T}\Psi^{T}

\displaystyle=\Psi F\Theta\Sigma_{V}^{-1}\Theta^{T}F^{T}\Psi^{T}\geq 0,

where
\Theta=\Sigma_{V}-L_{y\text{p}}(L_{y\text{p}}^{T}\Sigma_{V}^{-1}L_{y\text{p}})^{-1}L_{y\text{p}}^{T}
. Hence the
estimator in (
14
) achieves the minimum covariance.

\Box
