Minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance and the Closest Separable state to arbitrary 2 × 2 2\times 2 and 2 × 3 2\times 3 states Preprint: APS/123-QED
Palash Pandya, Marcin Wieśniak

Abstract
In this article we provide a three step algorithm to obtain the Closest Separable State to the given state in Hilbert space dimensions 2 × 2 2\times 2 and 2 × 3 2\times 3 , or in the higher dimensional Hilbert spaces, Closest Positive Partial Transpose (PPT) state for the chosen bipartition. In the process, a tight lower bound to the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance is brought forth together with the relation between the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance and Negativity. This also leads us to discuss the validity of the said distance from the set of separable quantum states as an entanglement measure. Any Entanglement measure defined as the minimum of a distance measure to the set of separable states needs to follow certain widely accepted rules. Most significantly, contractiveness of the distance (also, CP non-expansive property) under LOCC maps. While the Hilbert-Schmidt distance does not have this property, it is still an open question if the measure constructed using it is non-increasing under LOCC operations. While we outline some of the difficulties in such a proof, we also provide numerical evidence that brings one step closer to closing the question.



I
Introduction

Detecting entanglement or separability in a given quantum state are significant problems with a great number of applications. Numerous criterion to detect separability and verify entanglement have been suggested that accomplish the task in a variety of ways.
For example, the well-known Positive Partial Transpose (PPT) criterion or the Peres-Horodecki criterion
Peres 1996
;
Horodecki et al. 1996
checks the positive semi-definiteness of the density matrix under partial transposition of a subsystem. In the
2\times 2
and
2\times 3
dimensional Hilbert spaces, the PPT criterion provides a necessary sufficient condition to detect separability and entanglement, while in the higher dimensions, this is not the case, as positive semi-definiteness under partial transpose only indicates the absence of entanglement, because of the presence of bound entangled states. Therefore, the PPT criterion in higher dimensions simply indicates the presence of entanglement and not of separability.
A few notable examples of entanglement measures include, Entanglement of Formation, an operational measure that quantifies in a sense, the number of singlets required to reconstruct the given state
Wootters 1998a
; Relative Entropy of Entanglement, which is a distance based measure that quantifies the distance from the set of separable states
Vedral and Plenio 1998
; Negativity, which quantifies the violation of the PPT criterion by the given state
Vidal and Werner 2002
; Concurrence,
Hill and Wootters 1997
which is also related to Entanglement of Formation
Wootters 1998b
. The last two measures, Negativity and Concurrence, are the only two known efficiently computable measures for an arbitrary two-qubit state.
The majority of measures and criterion fail to generalize to
N

d
-dimensional systems. Even the extension of a measure to the mixed states usually involves a convex roof extension, which is a hard optimization problem, even in the simplest case of two-qubits. Another approach is to certify entanglement instead, using Entanglement Witnesses. An entanglement witness is an operator that (conventionally) has a positive expecation over the set of separable states, and a negative expectation for at least some entangled state (e. g., the state that we want to certify). In this article we study the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance from the set of separable states, which can arguably do both of the above. We will look at this distance as a potential entanglement measure, and in the process of calculating it, provide
optimal
entanglement witness using the Closest Separable State (CSS).
By providing a simple three step algorithm to calculate the Closest Separable state (CSS) and the corresponding minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance for any qubit-qubit and qubit-qutrit state we show that the distance is efficiently calculable.
We also provide a relation between the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance to Negativity and provide a tight lower bound on the distance.

The article is structured as follows. In
section II
we review the important rules a measure of entanglement and the corresponding distance between quantum states need to follow. In
section III
we discuss the Hilbert-Schmidt distance from this perspective and in
section IV
we discuss our simple algorithm for constructing the Closest Separable state from the given state and provide detailed discussion of the qubit-qubit and qubit-qutrit scenario. Then in
section V
follows a discussion on the behaviour of minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance under LOCC operations. In
section VI
, we take some example states and then illustrate with the GHZ and W states, how the algorithm also provides the Closest PPT state in the higher dimensional Hilbert spaces.

Salient properties of an Entanglement Measure

A candidate quantity,
E
to be called an entanglement measure needs to follow the following rules for a given quantum state,
\rho

Vedral et al. 1997

E1

If the state

E(\rho)=0
, otherwise

E2

E(\rho)
should be invariant under local unitary transformations,

E(\rho_{12\cdots N})=E(U_{1}\otimes\cdots U_{N}\rho U_{1}^{\dagger}\otimes\cdots U_{N}^{\dagger})\,.

(1)

E3

Local operations and Classical communication between subsystems does not increase

, i. e.,

E(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}(\rho))\leq E(\rho),

(2)

for any LOCC map
\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}
.

In
Vedral et al. 1997
, a general way to construct such an entanglement measure

(that follows the above rules) is given using a measure of

,

, between two density matrices:

E(\rho)\coloneqq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}D(\rho,\sigma)\,,

(3)

where
\rho
is the density matrix of the reference state, and minimization is done over all density matrices
\sigma
in the set of separable states
\mathcal{S}
.

Then it is easy to see that such a measure satisfies
E1
above, by requiring the distance measure to be zero,
D(\rho,\sigma)=0
, iff
\rho=\sigma
and
E2
by observing the invariance of the set of separable states
\mathcal{S}
under Unitary operations. The third condition is satisfied if the distance measure is chosen to be
contractive
under LOCC operations,
D(\rho,\sigma)\geq D(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\rho,\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\sigma)
.

To see how, consider a state
\varsigma
such that
E(\rho)=D(\rho,\varsigma)=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}D(\rho,\sigma)
. Then,

\displaystyle E(\rho)=D(\rho,\varsigma)

\displaystyle\geq D(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\rho,\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\varsigma)

\displaystyle\geq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}D(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\rho,\sigma)=E(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\rho).

(4)

Therefore, the entanglement of a given state can be quantified as the distance of the state from the set of separable states, and can be equivalently defined as the search for the separable state
\varsigma
that is the
closest separable state
(CSS) under the chosen distance measure. An example of an entanglement measure constructed using such a definition is the following:

E(\rho)=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}S(\rho\parallel\sigma)

(5)

where
S(\rho\parallel\sigma)=\Tr(\rho\log\rho-\rho\log\sigma)
is the relative entropy of the states
\rho
and
\sigma
.

In
Vedral and Plenio 1998
, the authors provide a more comprehensive set of sufficient requirements that the distance measure
D(\rho,\sigma)
should meet in order for
E(\rho)
constructed using

to be an entanglement measure.

D1

D(\rho,\sigma)\geq 0
, equality iff
\rho=\sigma
.

D2

Unitary invariance:
D(\rho,\sigma)=D(U\rho U^{\dagger},U\sigma U^{\dagger})
.

D3

D(\Tr_{A}\rho,\Tr_{A}\sigma)\leq D(\rho,\sigma)
, where
\Tr_{A}
is partial trace on subsystem
A
.

D4

\sum p_{i}D(\rho_{i}/p_{i},\sigma_{i}/q_{i})\leq\sum D(\rho_{i},\sigma_{i})
, where
p_{i}=\Tr(\rho_i)
,
q_{i}=\Tr(\sigma_i)
,
\rho_{i}=V_{i}\rho V_{i}^{\dagger}
and
\sigma_{i}=V_{i}\sigma V_{i}^{\dagger}
. The operators
V_{i}
constitute the Kraus operator representation of a Completely Positive Trace Preserving (CPTP) map.

D5a

For a set of orthogonal projectors
P_{i}
,

\displaystyle D(\sum_{i}P_{i}\rho P_{i},\sum_{i}P_{i}\sigma P_{i})=\sum_{i}D(P_{i}\rho P_{i},P_{i}\sigma P_{i})

D5b

D(\rho\otimes P_{\alpha},\sigma\otimes P_{\alpha})=D(\rho,\sigma)
, where
P_{\alpha}
is a projector.

They go on to prove that indeed the above set of requirements if satisfied by a distance leads to a valid Entanglement measure, using the examples of relative entropy and Bures metric.

III
Hilbert-Schmidt Distance

The distance measure that we want to focus on in this work is the
Hilbert-Schmidt distance
(HSD) defined using the Hilbert-Schmidt norm (also known as the Frobenius norm):

D_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma)=\left[\Tr(\rho-\sigma)^{2}\right].

(6)

It has been considered earlier as a candidate for an entanglement measure in
Witte and Trucks 1998
.
It can be easily seen that the Hilbert-Schmidt distance follows
D1
,
D2
and
D5
. Although, one can check numerically that
D3
is not followed when the purity of states does not match, there is no conclusive proof of the Hilbert-Schmidt distance following
D4
as well. The difficulty in proving
D4
follows from the fact that under a CPTP map the Hilbert-Schmidt distance can increase as was demonstrated using an example in
Ozawa 2000
. While this contractive property allows one to directly prove that the resulting Entanglement measure will not increase under LOCC operations, the absence of this property does not directly remove the possibility of proving that the said Entanglement measure is non-increasing under LOCC.

In fact, the CPTP map used in
Ozawa 2000
to disprove the CP non-expansive property for Hilbert-Schmidt distance maps every two qubit state to a separable state. Therefore, the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance after applying the map is zero and is less or equal to the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance of any two qubit state, which is encouragement enough to take a closer look.

In Eq. (
II
), this contractive nature is used as a key step, without which we would need to prove, in another manner, that

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}D(\rho,\sigma)\geq\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}D(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\rho,\sigma)\,,

(7)

and therefore,
E(\rho)\geq E(\Lambda_{\scriptscriptstyle\text{LOCC}}\rho)
.

In case of the Hilbert-Schmidt distance, the candidate entanglement measure, henceforth called the Hilbert-Schmidt measure, is defined as:

D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho)=\min_{\sigma\in\mathcal{S}}D^{2}_{\text{HS}}(\rho,\sigma).

(8)

So that,
D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho)\geq 0
with equality iff
\rho\in\mathcal{S}
.

The Hilbert-Schmidt measure is an attractive candidate due to it’s easily generalizable definition to not only a Hilbert space of arbitrary dimension, but also the fact that by changing the convex set of states over which the minimization is done we can quantify different types of entanglement in the multipartite scenario. For example, by considering the set of biseparable states in Eq. (
8
) instead of the fully separable states
\mathcal{S}
, one can quantify genuine multipartite entanglement.

Another important factor is the ease of calculation of an entanglement measure for an arbitrary state. In
Pandya et al. 2020
we used a simplified version of the Gilbert’s algorithm to provide a computationally undemanding way of getting an upper bound on the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance for a given state in an arbitrary Hilbert space. In the process the algorithm also provides a very good approximation of the Closest Separable state to the given reference state.

While this was the only possible way to approximate the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance for an arbitrary quantum state up until now, in following section we provide a simple way to calculate both the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance and the Closest Separable state for qubit-qubit and qubit-qutrit systems. In doing so we shall bring forth the relation with Negativity.

\rho_{\text{CSS}}=\rho^{(2)}_{f}=(f(\rho^{(2)})+\rho^{(2)})/2
,

\rho^{(2)}_{f}\,:\ \lambda_{1}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}},\ \lambda_{2}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}},\ \lambda_{3}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}},\\
\lambda_{4}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}},\ 0,\ 0;

(39)

For case B, one more iteration of the algorithm leads to the Closest Separable state
\rho_{\text{CSS}}=\rho^{(3)}_{f}=(f(\rho^{(3)})+\rho^{(3)})/2
,

\rho^{(3)}_{f}\,:\ \lambda_{1}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}}+\frac{N_{2}}{r_{2}},\ \lambda_{2}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}}+\frac{N_{2}}{r_{2}},\\
\lambda_{3}+\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}}+\frac{N_{2}}{r_{2}},\ 0,\ 0,\ 0;

(40)

The minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance in the two cases,

D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho)=D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho^{\Gamma},\rho^{(2)}_{f})\\
=4\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}}^{2}+\lambda_{5}^{2}+\lambda_{6}^{2}=\frac{(\lambda_{5}+\lambda_{6})^{2}}{4}+\lambda_{5}^{2}+\lambda_{6}^{2};

(41)

and,

D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho)=D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho^{\Gamma},\rho^{(2)}_{f})\\
=3(\frac{N_{1}}{r_{1}}+\frac{N_{2}}{r_{2}})^{2}+\lambda_{4}^{2}+\lambda_{5}^{2}+\lambda_{6}^{2}\\
=\frac{(\lambda_{5}+\lambda_{6}-\lambda_{4})^{2}}{3}+\lambda_{4}^{2}+\lambda_{5}^{2}+\lambda_{6}^{2}\\
=\frac{(\lambda_{5}+\lambda_{6})^{2}}{4}+\lambda_{5}^{2}+\lambda_{6}^{2}\\
+\frac{1}{12}(\lambda_{5}+\lambda_{6}-4\lambda_{4})^{2};

(42)

IV.3
Lower bound of minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance

In the previous subsections, we only looked at four of the possible scenarios pertaining to the eigenvalues of the partial transpose of the given state. All other cases can be similarly worked out and follow the same pattern. One can observe that in all the expressions of minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance in the previous sections there was a expression of the form:

\displaystyle\frac{(\sum\mbox{$-$ve Eigenvalues})^{2}}{\rank(\rho^\Gamma+f(\rho^\Gamma))}

\displaystyle+\sum(\mbox{$-$ve Eigenvalues})^{2}

(43)

\displaystyle\implies\frac{(\Tr(f(\rho^\Gamma)-\rho^\Gamma))^{2}}{\rank(\rho^\Gamma+f(\rho^\Gamma))}

\displaystyle+\norm{f(\rho^\Gamma)-\rho^\Gamma}_{HS}^{2}

(44)

where
\norm{X}_{HS}
denotes the Hilbert-Schmidt norm. When this is only a sub-expression, note that the additional terms added are perfect squares, which makes the above expression a true lower bound for the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance.

\displaystyle D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho)\geq\frac{(\Tr(f(\rho^\Gamma)-\rho^\Gamma))^{2}}{\rank(\rho^\Gamma+f(\rho^\Gamma))}

\displaystyle+\norm{f(\rho^\Gamma)-\rho^\Gamma}_{HS}^{2}

(45)

The expression
\Tr(f(\rho^\Gamma)-\rho^\Gamma)=\mathcal{N}(\rho)
, where
\mathcal{N}
denotes Negativity of the state.
The expression of the lower bound lends itself to a similar proof of not increasing under LOCC as Negativity in
Vidal and Werner 2002
. If the square of the sum of the negative eigenvalues does not increase under LOCC operations, the sum of squares of the negative eigenvalues does not increase either. Therefore, the lower bound is non-increasing under LOCC operations.

It remains to be seen if it can be extended to the whole formula of the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance for a given state, it would be useful to look at the trace,

\Tr(\rho^\Gamma)\\
=\sum(\mbox{$+$ve Eigenvalues})-\sum(\mbox{$-$ve Eigenvalues})\\
=1.

(46)

So that if the LOCC operation reduces the sum of negative eigenvalues by a certain amount, the sum of the positive eigenvalues has to reduce by the same amount.
To get around this and get some indication about the behaviour of the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance we performed numerical calculations by generating random LOCC maps. The details are described in Appendix
B
. The results combined with the above argument strongly favour the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance as an entanglement monotone.

Next we will look at some examples and discuss the application of the algorithm to calculate the Closest PPT state in higher dimensional Hilbert spaces.

V
Behaviour under Local Operations and Classical Communication

The paradigm of Local Operations and Classical Communication has a simple physical description, while simultaneously being difficult to characterize mathematically. The simple physical description entails the parties involved performing local operations on their own subsystems and communicating with other parties information about their operations and outcomes. This can happen over several rounds, where one round is where all parties chose and apply an operation to their subsystems. Followed by sending desired classical information to some or all of the participants. The information received then can be used in subsequent rounds to influence the choice of local operations. In general, an LOCC operation can consist of infinite rounds of local operations followed by classical communication. While all LOCC operations can be represented as CPTP maps on the space of density operators, the precise structure of these maps is not known
Chitambar et al. 2014
.

Faced with this difficulty, we investigate CPTP maps using a unitary on an extended Hilbert space followed by tracing out the extension. Let’s consider the bipartite case, where we have the state
\rho_{AB}
in the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
. Then, the extended Hilbert space is simple
\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{e}
. The action of such a map can be defined as

\rho_{AB}\mapsto\Tr_{e}(U(\rho_{AB}\otimes\sigma_{e})U^{\dagger})=\sum_{i}V_{i}\rho V_{i}^{\dagger},

(47)

where the operators
V_{i}
are Kraus operators for the CPTP map in question. From trace preserving property of the map we have
\sum_{i}V_{i}^{\dagger}V_{i}=\mathbb{I}
.

In particular, let’s consider a two qubit system and a two qubit environment, that start out in an uncorrelated state,
\rho_{AB}\otimes\sigma_{CD}
where
\sigma_{CD}
is the environment. Then the total system undergoes unitary evolution,
U_{AC}\otimes U_{BD}
, following which we trace out the environment.

\rho_{AB}\mapsto\Tr_{CD}(U_{AC}\otimes U_{BD}(\rho_{AB}\otimes\sigma_{CD})U_{AC}\otimes U_{BD}^{\dagger})\\
=\sum_{i}V_{i}\rho V_{i}^{\dagger}=\Phi(\rho_{AB}),

(48)

In order to verify the validity of Eq. (
7
), we would calculate the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance for both
\rho_{AB}
and
\Phi(\rho_{AB})
. In this scenario, the relation between
D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\rho_{AB})
and
D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\Phi(\rho_{AB}))
depends on the way the unitary operator
U_{AC}\otimes U_{BD}
alters the eigenvalues of
D^{2}_{\text{HS}_{\min}}(\Phi(\rho_{AB}))
. Therefore, a relation between the eigenvalues of the two density matrices under the map
\Phi
would be helpful.

Consider a CPTP map
\Phi
with the Kraus representation,
\Phi(\rho)=\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}
, and a partially transposed density matrix with the eigendecomposition,
\rho^{\Gamma}=\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\outerproduct{e_j}{e_j}
.
Then the resulting state is

\displaystyle\Phi(\rho)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}K_{i}\rho K_{i}^{\dagger}=\sum_{i}K_{i}\left(\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}\outerproduct{e_j}{e_j}\right)K_{i}^{\dagger}

(49)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{j}K_{i}\outerproduct{e_j}{e_j}K_{i}^{\dagger}\,,

(50)

and is not necessarily diagonal anymore. It can be diagonalized in turn, and admits an eigendecomposition,
D=U\Phi(\rho)U^{\dagger}=\sum_{j}\sigma_{l}\outerproduct{f_l}{f_l}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle D=U\Phi(\rho)U^{\dagger}=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{j}UK_{i}\outerproduct{e_j}{e_j}K_{i}^{\dagger}U^{\dagger}\,.

(51)

Here, the set of operators
M_{i}=UK_{i}
are also Kraus operators. To see the relation between the eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
and
\sigma_{l}
, consider
\expectationvalue{D}{f_l}
,

\displaystyle\sigma_{l}

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{D}{f_l}=\expectationvalue{\left(\sum_{i,j} \lambda_j M_i\op{e_j}M_i^\dag\right) }{f_l}

(52)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{j}\matrixelement{f_l}{M_i}{e_j}\matrixelement{e_j}{M_i^\dag}{f_l}

(53)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\absolutevalue{\mel{f_l}{M_i}{e_j}}^{2}\lambda_{j}=\sum_{j}(\sum_{i}c_{i,j}^{l})\lambda_{j}\,,

(54)

where
c_{i,j}^{l}
s are real numbers. Observe that the sum
\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}^{l}
, can be simplified as following,

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}c_{i,j}^{l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\matrixelement{f_l}{K_i}{e_j}\matrixelement{e_j}{K_i^\dag}{f_l}

(55)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\expectationvalue{K_i \sum_j \op{e_j} K_i^\dag}{f_l}

(56)

\displaystyle=\expectationvalue{\sum_i K_i K_i^\dag}{f_l}=\expectationvalue{\Phi(\mathbb{I})}{f_l}\,.

(57)

For a
unital
map we have
\Phi(\mathbb{I})=\mathbb{I}
, otherwise
\Phi(\mathbb{I})=\rho_{\mathcal{S}}
for some separable state
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}
.
Only in the former case the value of the right hand side in
Equation 57
equals one, and therefore, the eigenvalues of the resulting state are convex combinations of the eigenvalues of the original density matrix. Otherwise, it is a linear combination, with the sum of the coefficients in the interval
[0,1]
as they are the elements of the diagonal of
\rho_{\mathcal{S}}
when written in the eigenbasis of
\Phi(\rho)
.

As a convex combination of real numbers pulls in the maximum and minimum, the maximum eigenvalue of
\Phi(\rho)
is less or equal to the maximum eigenvalue of the state
\rho
,
\sigma_{1}\leq\lambda_{1}
. In addition, the negative eigenvalue becomes less negative,
\lambda_{4}\geq\sigma_{4}
, which also supports non-increasing nature of Negativity. This can be shown in the following manner. Consider as before
\{\lambda_{i}\}
and
\{\sigma_{i}\}
to be the eigenvalues of
\rho
and
\Phi(\rho)
respectively. We know from the above discussion that
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j}c^{i}_{j}\lambda_{j}
such that
\sum_{j}c^{i}_{j}=1
. From the fact
\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}=1
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\sigma_{i}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\sum_{j}c^{i}_{j}\lambda_{j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}(\sum_{i}c^{i}_{j})\lambda_{j}=1\,.

(58)

The last step implies
\sum_{i}c^{i}_{j}=1
, because
\sum_{j}\lambda_{j}=1
. Therefore the action of the map
\Phi
on the eigenvalues of the matrix can be expressed as a
bistochastic
or
doubly stochastic
matrix
[C]_{ij}=c^{i}_{j}
. A well known result from the theory of majorization then lets us impose an ordering on the real valued vectors of eigenvalues
\Lambda=\{\lambda_{i}\}
and
\Sigma=\{\sigma_{i}\}
, which is
\Sigma\preceq\Lambda
when
\Sigma=C\Lambda
.
The majorization implies the following two equivalent sets of inequalities in case of a qubit-qubit system,

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}

(59)

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{3}-\sigma_{4}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{3}-\lambda_{4}=1\,,

and,

\displaystyle-\sigma_{4}

\displaystyle\geq-\lambda_{4}

(60)

\displaystyle-\sigma_{4}+\sigma_{3}

\displaystyle\geq-\lambda_{4}+\lambda_{3}

\displaystyle-\sigma_{4}+\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{2}

\displaystyle\geq-\lambda_{4}+\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{2}

\displaystyle-\sigma_{4}+\sigma_{3}+\sigma_{2}+\sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{4}+\lambda_{3}+\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{1}.

One can immediately show from the second set of inequalities that the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance does not increase under any unital map
\Phi
in a qubit-qubit system.

A similar observation is difficult to make in the case of non-unital maps where we have a linear combination of eigenvalues instead of a convex combination. We can express the situation as,
\sigma_{i}=\sum_{j}d^{i}_{j}\lambda_{j}
where
\sum_{j}d^{i}_{j}=\expectationvalue{\Phi(\mathbb{I})}{f_i}=r_{ii}
is the
i
th diagonal element.
The sum
\sum_{i}r_{ii}=1=\sum_{i}(\sum_{j}d^{i}_{j})
, tells us only that the sum of elements of the matrix
[D]_{ij}=d^{i}_{j}
equals 1 and as such an ordering is difficult to establish. But one might observe that division on both sides by
r_{ii}
gives us a convex combination,

\frac{\sigma_{i}}{r_{ii}}=\sum_{j}\frac{d^{i}_{j}}{r_{ii}}\lambda_{j}

(61)

from which one can conclude
\sigma_{i}\leq\frac{\sigma_{i}}{r_{ii}}\leq\lambda_{1}
, where the first inequality results from
r_{ii}\in[0,1]
and the second is the property of a convex combination. The lack of further analytical conclusions that can be drawn in this case leads us to a numerical investigation which is described in the Appendix
B
.
To summarize the numerical investigation, a search for a CPTP map that will increase the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance always results in a map that preserves the eigenvalues while the eigenvectors are not.

VII
Conclusions and Outlook

In this article, we briefly looked at the generally accepted set of conditions for constructing Entanglement measures defined in terms of a distance measure, and the set of conditions that the distance measure in turn has to satisfy to give a valid entanglement measure. Our candidate distance measure is the Hilbert-Schmidt distance, which unfortunately does not satisfy all the conditions mentioned before. This stops us from obtaining a simple proof that the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance from the set of separable states is an Entanglement measure using these conditions. This in turn does not imply non-existence of such a proof. The most important step that needs to be shown to hold is given in Eq. (
7
).

Using the results obtained from the application of Gilbert’s algorithm to a large number of randomly generated density matrices, we find that the reference state and the closest separable state are both diagonal in the same basis, which leads to the main result of this article a simple 3-step algorithm to obtain the Closest Separable State for any arbitrary qubit-qubit or qubit-qutrit state, using the PPT criterion as its basis.
The same algorithm can immediately be applied to find the Closest PPT state to any given state across a particular bipartition.
For both Closest Separable state and Closest PPT state we provide examples that corroborate existing literature.
Using the algorithm we find a lower bound to the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance which is completely characterised by the negative eigenvalues of the partially transposed state. The lower bound related to Negativity of the state is non-increasing under the application of LOCC operations. We also provide a partial proof of the non-increasing nature of the minimum Hilbert-Schmidt distance, with numerical simulations to support the hypothesis.The task that remains is to show, analytically, that the distance measure does not increase under non-unital maps as well. Therefore, there is a strong indication that the distance between quantum states used to construct the Entanglement measure does not need to follow the CP non-expansive property in order to be non-increasing under LOCC operations.

Finally, using the algorithm provided in this article, we have solved the problem of obtaining
optimal
Entanglement Witnesses for arbitrary 2-qubit and qubit-qutrit states. An optimal entanglement witness,
\mathcal{W}
for a given state
\rho
, is directly constructed using the Closest Separable State,
\rho_{\text{CSS}}
:

\mathcal{W}=\frac{\rho_{\text{CSS}}-\rho-Tr(\rho_{\text{CSS}}(\rho_{\text{CSS}}-\rho))\mathbb{I}}{\norm{\rcss-\rho}_{HS}}\,.

(87)
