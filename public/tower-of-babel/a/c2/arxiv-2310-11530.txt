Scaling limits of slim and fat trees
Vladislav Kargin

Abstract
We consider Galton–Watson trees conditioned on both the total number of vertices n n and the number of leaves k k . The focus is on the case in which both k k and n n grow to infinity and k = α ​ n + O ⁡ ( 1 ) k=\alpha n+O(1) , with α ∈ ( 0 , 1 ) \alpha\in(0,1) . Assuming the exponential decay of the offspring distribution, we show that the rescaled random tree converges in distribution to Aldous’ Continuum Random Tree with respect to the Gromov–Hausdorff topology. The scaling depends on a parameter σ ∗ \sigma^{\ast} which we calculate explicitly. Additionally, we compute the limit for the degree sequences of these random trees.

Introduction

In this paper, we investigate the Galton–Watson (“GW”) trees conditioned both on the total number of vertices and on the number of leaves, when the total number of vertices is large and the number of leaves is far from its expected value. For a situation with small number of leaves, we can expect long leafless branches, while in the situation when a tree has a large number of leaves, the branches are short and bushy, and this is why we call these trees “slim” and “fat,” respectively.

The general setup is as follows. Let
L
be a non-negative, integer-valued random variable with the probability mass function
\mathbb{P}(L=i)=w_{i}
,
i=0,1,2,\ldots
, which is assumed to satisfy the following conditions:

\mathbb{E}L=1,\text{ and for some $a>0$, }\mathbb{E}e^{aL}<\infty.

(1)

We also assume that the distribution of
L
is not trivial:
w_{1}\neq 1
.

A random tree
T
is the family tree of a Galton–Watson process with one individual in generation 0 and with offspring of each individual distributed independently as the random variable
L
. We use ordered trees so that the information on the birth order of family tree branches is known. For a formal description, see Sect. 3 in the paper of Le Gall and Le Jan
legall_lejan98
.

The probability space
\Omega
is the space of all ordered rooted trees with the probability distribution
\mathcal{P}
induced by the distribution of random variable
L
. We write
\Omega_{n}\subset\Omega
for trees with
n
vertices and
\Omega_{k,n}\subset\Omega_{n}
for trees with
k
leaves and
n
vertices. We say that
(k,n)\in PP(w)
for an offspring distribution
w=(w_{0},w_{1},\ldots)
if the set of trees
\Omega_{k,n}
has positive probability. In this case, both
\Omega_{n}
and
\Omega_{k,n}
are endowed with conditional probability laws
\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
\mathcal{P}_{k,n}
induced from law
\mathcal{P}
on
\Omega
.

Note that a probability distribution
(w_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
can be replaced by an equivalent probability distribution
(\tilde{w}_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
without changing the measure on
\Omega_{n}
(see Sect. 4 in Janson
janson2012
). In particular, by Remark 4.3 in
janson2012
, any equivalent probability distribution is determined by its expectation, and possible expectations are in
(0,\nu]
, where
\nu
is a certain parameter. It is easy to check by using the definition of
\nu
(see formulas (3.8) - (3.10) in
janson2012
) that if
(w_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
has an exponentially declining tail then
\nu\geq 1
. Therefore, for every such
(w_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
we can always find an equivalent probability distribution
(\tilde{w}_{j})_{j=0}^{\infty}
so that its expectation is
1
. In particular, the condition
\mathbb{E}L=1
in (
1
) does not impose an additional restriction on measure of trees in
\Omega_{n}
and
\Omega_{k,n}
but is used only to fix a specific equivalent weight distribution.

We consider the case when the number of leaves
k
approximately proportional to the number of vertices
n
, more specifically,
k=\alpha n+O(1)
, where
\alpha\in(0,1)
. Recall that if the offspring distribution
\{w_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
has finite variance, then the expected number of leaves for a tree in
\Omega_{n}
is
\sim w_{0}n
(see Theorem II.5.1 in
kolchin84
). We are mainly interested in the case when
\alpha\neq w_{0}
.

1.1
Main Results

In Theorem
1.1
, we will show that as
n\to\infty
, a rescaled tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
converges in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology to a random metric space called the Continuum Random Tree (“CRT”). This space was first introduced by Aldous in
aldous91a
,
aldous91b
,
aldous93

1.1.1
\alpha
-Shifted Offspring Distribution

Let
\theta(t):=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}w_{i}t^{i}
, where
\{w_{i}\}
is the offspring distribution, and let
\rho
denote the radius of convergence for this series. By assumption (
1
),

\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}w_{i}=1
and
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}iw_{i}=1.

Therefore,
\theta(1)=1
and
\theta^{\prime}(1)=1
. In particular,
1\leq\rho\leq\infty
. The assumption
w_{1}\neq 1
implies additionally that
w_{0}\neq 0
.

Define

\widehat{\psi}(t):=\frac{t\theta^{\prime}(t)}{\theta(t)-w_{0}}.

(2)

(Janson in
janson2012
uses
\psi(t)
to denote
\frac{t\theta^{\prime}(t)}{\theta(t)}
.)
In Lemma
2.1
, we show that
\widehat{\psi}(t)
is increasing on the interval
[0,\rho)
.
Hence, we can define

\widehat{\nu}:=\lim_{t\uparrow\rho}\widehat{\psi}(t)\in[1/(1-w_{0}),\infty].

(3)

(
\widehat{\nu}\geq 1/(1-w_{0})
because
\widehat{\psi}(1)=1/(1-w_{0})
and
\rho\geq 1
.)

Definition 1
.

Let
\alpha\in(0,1)
, and let
w_{i}^{\ast}
be a probability mass distribution with unit mean, such that
w_{0}^{\ast}=\alpha
and
w_{j}^{\ast}=Cw_{j}(t^{\ast})^{j-1}
for all
j>0
and for some
C>0
and
t^{\ast}>0
. We call
w_{0}^{\ast},w_{1}^{\ast},\ldots
, the
\alpha
-shifted offspring distribution
. Let
L^{\ast}
denote a random variable that have the distribution
\mathbb{P}(L^{\ast}=j)=w_{j}^{\ast}
for
j=0,1,2,\ldots
.

In other words, in the
\alpha
-shifted distribution,
w_{0}^{\ast}
is set to
\alpha
and the remaining weights are exponentially re-weighted in such a way that the result is still a probability mass distribution with unit mean. It is easy to check that
C
and
t^{\ast}
can be determined from the following equations,

(1-\alpha)t^{\ast}\theta^{\prime}(t^{\ast})=\theta(t^{\ast})-w_{0},\text{ and }C=\frac{1}{\theta^{\prime}(t^{\ast})}.

(4)

In Lemma
2.2
, we show that these equations are solvable if
\alpha<1-1/\widehat{\nu}
. (In particular, they are always solvable for
\alpha\leq w_{0}
. Intuitively,
\alpha>1-1/\widehat{\nu}
corresponds to the situation when the number of leaves is not sustainable by the structure of the offspring distribution.)

In Lemma
2.3
, we show that the variance of the
\alpha
-shifted distribution
w_{j}^{\ast}
is

(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}=\frac{t^{\ast}\theta^{\prime\prime}(t^{\ast})}{\theta^{\prime}(t^{\ast})}.

(5)

1.1.2
Rescaled Convergence in the Gromov–Hausdorff Metric

Let us consider trees in
\Omega_{k,n}
as metric spaces with a marked point (‘root’) by using the usual graph theoretic distance between vertices and extending it to the edges linearly. We will denote the distance between points
u
and
v
as
d(u,v)
.

Recall the definition of the Gromov–Hausdorff metric. If
(E,\delta)
is a metric space, then the Hausdorff distance between compact subsets of
E
is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\delta_{\textrm{Haus}}(K,K^{\prime})=\inf\{\alpha>0:K\subset U_{\alpha}(K^{\prime})\text{ and }K^{\prime}\subset U_{\alpha}(K)\},

where
U_{\alpha}(K):=\{x\in E:\delta(x,K)\leq\alpha\}
. Then, if
T
and
T^{\prime}
are two rooted compact metric spaces, with roots
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
, the Gromov–Hausdorff distance is defined as

\displaystyle d_{GH}(T,T^{\prime})=\inf\Big\{\delta_{\textrm{Haus}}\big(\phi(T),\phi^{\prime}(T^{\prime})\big)\vee\delta\big(\phi(\rho),\phi^{\prime}(\rho^{\prime})\big)\Big\},

where the infimum is over all choices of a metric space
(E,\delta)
and all isometric embeddings
\phi:T\to E
and
\phi^{\prime}:T^{\prime}\to E
. Two rooted compact metric spaces
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
are equivalent if there is a root-preserving isometry between these two spaces and obviously
d_{GH}(T,T^{\prime})
depends only on the equivalence classes of
T
and
T^{\prime}
. It is a fact that
d_{GH}(T,T^{\prime})
defines a metric on the set of equivalence classes of rooted compact metric spaces. (For the unrooted case, see Theorem 7.3.30 in Burago et al.
burago_burago_ivanov2001
. The proof for the rooted case can be obtained after some adjustments.) Moreover, the set of isometry classes of real trees equipped with the Gromov–Hausdorff metric a complete and separable metric space. See Theorem 1 in a paper of Evans et al.
evans_pitman_winter2006
.

Recall that we say that
(k,n)\in PP(w)
for an offspring distribution
w=(w_{0},w_{1},\ldots)
if the set of trees with
n
vertices and
k
leaves has a positive probability under the probability law
\mathcal{P}
induced by this offspring distribution. We have the following convergence result.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let the offspring distribution
w=(w_{j})_{j=0,1,\ldots}
satisfy Condition (
1
), and assume that
\big((k_{i},n_{i})\big)_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence in
PP(w)
such that
n_{i}\to\infty
and
k_{i}=\alpha n_{i}+O(1)
where
\alpha\in(0,1-1/\widehat{\nu})
. Let
T_{n_{i}}
be a tree chosen in
\Omega_{k_{i},n_{i}}
according to the probability law
\mathcal{P}_{k_{i},n_{i}}
.
Then,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\sqrt{n_{i}}}T_{n_{i}}\to\frac{2}{\sigma^{\ast}}\mathcal{T},

where
\mathcal{T}
is the Aldous continuum random tree and the convergence is in distribution with respect to the Gromov–Hausdorff topology on the space of compact metric spaces.

1.1.3
Convergence of Contour Processes

Theorem
1.1
follows from a stronger result that asserts the convergence of the rescaled contour process associated with the tree
T_{n}\in\Omega_{k,n}
to a Brownian excursion. For details of this implication, see the proof of Theorem 2.5 in Le Gall’s survey
legall2005
. The key fact is Lemma 2.4 in
legall2005
, which states that if
T
and
T^{\prime}
are two real trees with contour functions
C(x)
and
C^{\prime}(x)
then
d_{GH}(T,T^{\prime})\leq 2\|g-g^{\prime}\|
, where
\|g-g^{\prime}\|
stands for the uniform norm of
g-g^{\prime}
.

Before stating the stronger result, let us define the contour and related processes for an ordered rooted tree. While we need only the contour process to formulate the result, the other processes will be useful in the proof.

Let
T
be an ordered tree with
n
vertices.

Figure 1:
The contour, Łukasiewicz, and height processes for a tree on 8 vertices. The red points
\big(m(l),H_{n}(l)\big)
are an embedding of the height process in the contour process (see Definition
2
).

The depth-first search (DFS)
: The sequence
f_{i}
, with
i=0,\ldots,2n-2,
is a sequence of vertices, defined as follows: (i)
f_{0}=root
; (ii) given
f_{i}=v,
choose, if possible, the left-most child
w
of
v
that has not already been visited and set
f_{i+1}=w
. If this is not possible, let
f_{i+1}
be the parent of
v
, (iii) the sequence ends when
f_{2n-2}=root
and all vertices has been visited at least once.

The contour process

C_{n}(i)
,
i=0,\ldots,2n-2,
is defined as follows:

C_{n}(i)=d(root,f_{i})=\text{height of }f_{i},\text{ the i-th vertex in the DFS}.

(6)

Between the integer points, the path
C_{n}(t)
is defined by linear interpolation. See Fig.
1
for an illustration.

We also define

the Łukasiewicz path process

S_{n}(i)
, for
i=0,\ldots,n
. (In the paper of Marckert and Mokkadem
marckert_mokkadem2003
, this process is called the Depth First Queue Process (DFQP); we follow the terminology in the book of Flajolet and Sedgewick
flajolet_sedgewick2009
.) Assume that the vertices in a planar tree are denoted
v_{j}
,
j=0,\ldots,n-1
, in the order of their appearance in the Depth First Search (and without repetition, unlike
f_{i}
). Let
\xi_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, be the number of children of vertex
v_{i-1}
. Then,
S_{n}(0)=0
, and

S_{n}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\xi_{i}-1),\text{ for }1\leq j\leq n.

(7)

Similar to the contour process, we extend the Łukasiewicz process to the intervals between integers by linearity. For illustration, see Fig.
1
.

A Łukasiewicz path, by definition, is a piecewise continuous map from
[0,n]\to\mathbb{R}
that has the following properties: its increments
S_{n}(j+1)-S_{n}(j)\in\{-1,0,1,\ldots\}
for all integer
0\leq j\leq n-1
; it is non-negative,
S_{n}(j)\geq 0
, for every
0\leq j\leq n-1
;
S_{n}(0)=0
, and
S_{n}(n)=-1
. The construction (
7
) maps an ordered rooted tree with
n
vertices to a Łukasiewicz path. In fact, every Łukasiewicz path can be obtained in this way, and therefore this construction gives a bijective map from the set of ordered trees
\Omega_{n}
to the set of Łukasiewicz paths on interval
[0,n]
.

Finally, we define the
height process

H_{n}(i)
,
i=0,\ldots,n-1
, as

H_{n}(i)=d(root,v_{i})=\text{height of }v_{i}.

(8)

Now let us formulate the key result. In the theorem below, the convergence of stochastic processes is the weak convergence with respect to the space of continuous functions on
[0,1]
with the topology induced by the uniform norm:
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}f(t)
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let the offspring distribution
w=(w_{j})_{j=0,1,\ldots}
satisfy Condition (
1
), and assume that
\big((k_{i},n_{i})\big)_{i=1}^{\infty}
is a sequence in
PP(w)
such that
n_{i}\to\infty
and
k_{i}=\alpha n_{i}+O(1)
where
\alpha\in(0,1-1/\widehat{\nu})
. Let
T_{n_{i}}
be a tree chosen in
\Omega_{k_{i},n_{i}}
according to the probability law
\mathcal{P}_{k_{i},n_{i}}
and let
C_{n_{i}}
be the contour process for this tree. Then, as
n_{i}\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{C_{n_{i}}(2n_{i}t)}{\sqrt{n_{i}}}\bigg)_{t\in(0,1)}\xrightarrow{\text{weakly}}\frac{2}{\sigma^{\ast}}e(t),

where
e(t)
is a standard Brownian excursion, and
\sigma^{\ast}
is as defined in (
5
).

This theorem implies convergence results for continuous functionals on trees, in particular, the following result about the height of trees.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
h_{n_{i}}
be the height of a random tree
T_{n_{i}}\in\Omega_{k_{i},n_{i}}
, and suppose that the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
are satisfied. Then,
the distribution of
\sigma^{\ast}h_{n_{i}}/(2\sqrt{n_{i}})
converges to the distribution of the maximum of a standard Brownian excursion.

Proof.

The height of tree
T_{n_{i}}
corresponds to the maximum of the tree’s contour process. The maximum is a continuous functional on functions in
C[0,1]
with the uniform metric. Therefore, by Theorem
1.2
, the distribution of the maximum for the re-scaled contour process
C_{n}
converges to the distribution of the maximum of a standard Brownian excursion.

∎

1.1.4
Convergence of the Degree Sequence

Now, let
n_{j}(T)
denote the number of vertices of out-degree
j
in tree
T
. (Recall that the out-degree of a vertex in a rooted tree is the number of edges that lead away from the root. For Galton–Watson trees it is the number of children of an individual represented by the vertex.) We say that the sequence
(n_{0}(T),n_{1}(T),\ldots)
is the
degree sequence
of tree
T
. A probability measure on trees
\Omega_{k,n}
induces a probability measure on degree sequences. By definition,
n_{0}(T)=k
for a tree
T\in\Omega_{k,n}
. In addition,
\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}n_{i}(T)=n
, where
n
is the number of vertices in
T
, and therefore,
\{\frac{n_{i}(T)}{n}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
is a random probability measure.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let the offspring distribution
w=(w_{j})_{j=0,1,\ldots}
satisfy Condition (
1
), and assume that
(T_{n}\in\Omega_{k,n})
is a sequence of random trees with
k\to\infty
,
n\to\infty
, and
(k,n)\in PP(w)
. Suppose that
k=\alpha n+O(1)
as
n\to\infty
, and
\alpha\in(0,1-1/\widehat{\nu})
. Then, almost surely,
\frac{n_{j}(T_{n})}{n}\to w_{j}^{\ast}
for
j=0,1,2,\ldots
, where
\{w_{j}^{\ast}\}
is the
\alpha
-shifted offspring distribution.

1.1.5
Examples

Here are some explicitly calculated
\alpha
-shifted distributions together with the parameter
(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}
.

Example 1

(GW trees with geometric distribution)
.

Consider GW trees with the geometric offspring distribution
w_{j}=2^{-(j+1)}
,
j=0,1,\ldots
conditioned to have
n
vertices. This distribution is an exponential rescaling of the weight sequence
w_{j}=1
, where
j=0,1,\ldots
, and therefore these trees have the uniform distribution on
\Omega_{n}
. (This is Example 10.1 in Janson
janson2012
.)
For this distribution, we calculate
\theta(t)=(2-t)^{-1}
and
\widehat{\nu}=+\infty
. Therefore, equations in (
4
) are solvable for every
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The solution is
t^{\ast}=2\alpha
and
C=4(1-\alpha)^{2}
. This implies
w_{0}^{\ast}=\alpha
and
w_{j}^{\ast}=\alpha^{j-1}(1-\alpha)^{2}
for
j=1,2,\ldots
. The variance is
(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}=2\alpha/(1-\alpha)
. Note that as
\alpha
(i.e., the limiting ratio of the number of leaves to the number of vertices) increases, the variance parameter of the
\alpha
-shifted offspring distribution,
(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}
, also increases. In particular, we should expect that the height of the random tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
decreases. (A tree with many leaves is “fat” and “short”.)

Example 2

(Unary-Binary GW trees)
.

Consider unary-binary GW trees with weight sequence
w_{0}=p
,
w_{1}=1-2p
,
w_{2}=p
, and
w_{i}=0
for all other
i
, where
0<p<1/2
. In this case,
\widehat{\nu}=2
and equations (
4
) are solvable for every
\alpha\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. (It is not possible to solve these equations for
\alpha>1/2
because the number of leaves and the number of vertices are related by the inequality
n\geq 2k-1
in the unary-binary trees, with the equality reached for binary trees.) The setup of this example includes Examples 10.4 and 10.5 in Janson
janson2012
. After
\alpha
-shifting we get
w_{0}^{\ast}=w_{2}^{\ast}=\alpha
,
w_{1}^{\ast}=1-2\alpha
, with variance
(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}=2\alpha
. We can see that, as
\alpha
increases, the variance also increases, as in the previous example. However, in this case
\alpha
is bounded from above, therefore variance of the offspring distribution is bounded from above, and the tree cannot be made arbitrarily short.

1.1.6
Algorithm for Generating Trees in
\Omega_{k,n}

The proof of Theorems
1.2
and
1.4
is based on a new algorithm that generates trees in
\Omega_{k,n}
with weight sequence
w_{1},w_{2},\ldots
. In order to explain the algorithm, we introduce a useful map between ordered trees and balls-in-boxes allocations. The general idea of the algorithm is to generate a suitable random allocation and then recover a random tree from this allocation.

First, we define a map
\sigma
that sends an ordered tree on
n
vertices to the sequence of the vertex offspring sizes. In this sequence the vertices are ordered by the DFS, but the repeats of the vertices are omitted:

\sigma:T\to\sigma(T)=(\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n}).

(9)

For example in Fig.
1
,
\sigma(T)=(3,0,1,0,2,1,0,0)
.

This sequence can be identified with an allocation of
n-1

n
boxes (corresponding to vertices). The set of all possible allocations is denoted
B_{n-1,n}
.
The map
\sigma:\Omega_{n}\to B_{n-1,n}
is injective but not surjective.

By using the cycle lemma
1

1.2
Relation to Previous Work

Excellent reviews of the vast literature about the asymptotic behavior of finite random trees can be found in a book
flajolet_sedgewick2009
by Flajolet and Sedgewick, and in review articles
legall2005
by Le Gall and
janson2012
by Janson.

From early on, it was observed that different families of random trees often have some similar macroscopic properties. For example, the tree height distribution has the same functional form for many tree families, although the distribution parameters could depend on the specific choice of the family.

In a sequence of papers,
aldous91a
,
aldous91b
,
aldous93
, Aldous provided an explanation for this phenomenon by showing that certain sequences of finite random trees converge under rescaling in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology to universal objects that he called the
continuum random trees (CRTs)
. In particular, he showed that Galton–Watson trees with a finite-variance offspring distribution converge under rescaling to a special CRT, which he called the
Brownian CRT
. (We will sometime refer to this limit as the Aldous CRT.) The tree is characterized by the property that its contour process is the Brownian excursion. Aldous’ proof of this result is based on graph theoretical arguments which showed that all such Galton–Watson trees converge under rescaling to the same limit independent of the specific offspring distribution. Then proof was concluded by an investigation of a particular case of GW trees with geometric offspring distribution.

Marckert and Mokkadem in
marckert_mokkadem2003
gave a different proof of this result under the condition that the offspring distribution has exponentially declining tails. In their proof, they establish the convergence (under rescaling) of the Łukasiewicz process to the Brownian excursion and then, show that the paths of the height process must be uniformly close to the paths of the Łukasiewicz process. For this implication, they use a relation of Galton–Watson trees to queues and an explicit formula for the height process in terms of the Łukasiewicz process. Then one can handle the height process by using methods from the classic theory about ladder heights (Feller
feller71
, Chapter XII). Finally, the rescaled convergence of the height process implies the rescaled convergence of the contour process.

Duquesne in
duquesne2003
extends Aldous’ result to Galton–Watson trees that may have infinite variance. He relies heavily on the machinery developed in papers of Duquesne and Le

Gall
duquesne_legall2002
and of Le

Gall and Le

Jan
legall_lejan98
. While their approach is similar to the approach of Marckert and Mokkadem in
marckert_mokkadem2003
, however, in the case of infinite variance the Łukasiewicz process converges under rescaling to an excursion of a Lévy process, and it is not immediately clear what is the candidate for the rescaled limit of the associated height process. The authors of these papers defined a height process for the Lévy process and then, showed that the height process of the GW trees converges under rescaling to this continuous-time height process. In this way, they showed that Galton–Watson trees with infinite variance offspring distribution converge under rescaling to
Lévy CRTs
, encoded by these height processes. (There are several such CRTs, depending on the parameter of the Lévy process.)

An alternative proof of Duquesne’s theorem was given by Kortchemski in
kortchemski2013
. The proof is still based on the results in Duquesne and Le

Gall
duquesne_legall2002
. However, Kortchemski uses the fact that the probability measure of events conditioned on the size of a GW tree equal to
n
is absolutely continuous with respect to the probability measure of the events conditioned on the size of the tree being at least
n
, and this type of conditioning was already handled in Sect. 2.5 of
duquesne_legall2002
. A similar absolute continuity relation can be obtained for the continuous limit objects, and Kortchemski concludes the proof by showing the uniform convergence of the Radon–Nikodym densities.

By a similar method, in
kortchemski2012
, Kortchemski proved that if one conditions a Galton–Watson tree on the number of leaves, instead of the total number of vertices, then one still has the convergence under rescaling to the Brownian CRT or a Lévy CRT. The key additional ingredient in the proof is the fact that a GW tree with
k
leaves has
k/w_{0}
total vertices with high probability. He also proved an extension of this result to the case when one conditions the GW tree on the number of vertices that have degrees in a given non-empty set of integers.

The applicability of these universality results has been significantly enlarged in
haas_miermont2012
by Haas and Miermont. They considered a big class of trees with Markov branching property. In random trees with this property, the subtrees above some given height are independent with a law that depends only on the tree size, and the size is measured either as the number of leaves or vertices. Their result is that these trees converge under rescaling to universal random objects, called self-similar fragmentation trees.

In particular, Haas and Miermont show in
haas_miermont2012
that the convergence under rescaling holds in the case when the Galton–Watson trees are conditioned either on the total progeny, or on the number of leaves (but not both). In
rizzolo2015
, Rizzolo used the method of
haas_miermont2012
to show the convergence under rescaling of the Galton–Watson trees conditioned on the number of vertices that have degrees in a specified set. The proof of these results is based on the theory of fragmentation processes, and this gives a completely different approach to results by Aldous, Duquesne, Le Gall, Le Jan, and Kortchemski.

In this paper, we use the approach based on random walks and their continuous-time limits and not on fragmentations processes.

The difference of this paper from earlier studies described above is that we focus on the situation when a Galton–Watson random tree is conditioned on both the number of total progeny and the number of leaves. In particular,
we consider a situation in which these two requirements are at odds with each other. In a sense, the obtained results are about an atypical event since they are concerned with the structure of trees under an additional condition super-imposed on the usual conditional Galton–Watson tree.

A similar approach is taken in papers
labarbe_marckert2007
by Labarbe and Marckert, and
broutin_marckert2014
by Broutin and Marckert. The paper
labarbe_marckert2007
considers Bernoulli random walks (that is, walks with increments in
\{-1,+1\}
) and associated processes, – bridges, meanders and excursions, – conditioned on a given number of peaks. A well-known bijection maps the contour process of an ordered tree to a Bernoulli excursion, and the results in
labarbe_marckert2007
imply the convergence of uniformly distributed ordered trees with a given number of leaves to the Aldous continuum random tree (see Sect. 1.1 in their paper).

3

3

In addition, the paper
labarbe_marckert2007
studies the counting process for leaves under DFS exploration of the tree. For extension of these results to GW trees, see a recent paper of Thévenin
thevenin2020
.
This is a particular case of our Theorem
1.1
for the case when weights

are geometric,
w_{j}=2^{-j-1}

For ordered trees with non-uniform distributions, the contour process is still an (interpolated) excursion with increments
\{-1,+1\}
but its distribution is more complicated and its rescaled convergence is difficult to establish directly. So, instead of the contour process, we use the Łukasiewicz process, that has a more tractable distribution, in order to prove the rescaled convergence to the Brownian excursion. Then, we infer that this implies the convergence for the contour process as well, by applying the techniques developed by Marckert and Mokkadem in
marckert_mokkadem2003
.
This allows us to derive the convergence under re-scaling for a broader class of random trees than that considered in the paper of Labarbe and Marckert
labarbe_marckert2007
.

From the technical viewpoint, the methods by which we obtain the re-scaled convergence of the Łukasiewicz process to the Brownian excursion, also differ from methods in
labarbe_marckert2007
.
In particular, in
labarbe_marckert2007
the authors use combinatorics to explicitly evaluate the number of simple walk paths with a given number of peaks between two lattice points. Then, they use the Stirling formula and obtain the finite-dimensional distributions of the limit by calculating the asymptotics of these explicit expressions. The combinatorics at the heart of this approach becomes significantly harder for paths with a more general set of increments, and we choose to use a different approach.

For tightness,
labarbe_marckert2007
uses a combinatorial trick that maps simple walks with increments
\pm 1
to walks with
\{0,1\}
increments in a non-trivial fashion: peaks and valleys of the original walk path correspond to increment
1
, and the other points between steps of the original path to increment
0
. This map is injective and converts conditioning on the number of peaks to conditioning on the final point of the walk, which is easier to handle. Then, it is noted that a formula for the inverse map involves summation of independent geometric random variables, and this fact is used in order to show that the tightness also holds for the original random walk. Additional conditioning on the final point of the original random walk (needed for bridges and excursions) is handled by using the explicit formulas for the number of paths with a given number of peaks.

This argument is hard to generalize to walks with more general increments (and so to Łukasiewicz walks) because of the absence of the required combinatorial trick.

Instead, we use the connection of discrete walks with the balls-in-boxes model, as described by Kolchin
kolchin84
and Janson
janson2012
, and with exchangeable random variables. This allows us to apply the results in Billingsley’s book
billingsley1968
that establish the re-scaled convergence for partial sums of a certain class of exchangeable random variables.

), hence, the expectation of the sum is
(n-k)/(1-\alpha)=n
. By using the local central limit theorem (valid under the assumption that the variance of pmf
\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}
is finite), the probability that
\sum_{i=1}^{n-k}\hat{\zeta}_{i}=n-1
is bounded from below by
\Omega(1/\sqrt{n})
. Therefore, the expected time that we draw a sample
N_{j}
such that
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}jN_{j}=n-1
is
\tau_{n}\sqrt{n}=o(n)
.

The time for other operations (adding zeros and random shuffling) is linear in
n
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

The proof consists of several steps. In the first step, we will show that the Łukasiewicz path of a random tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
converges to a Brownian excursion.
This will be accomplished in Lemmas
2.7
and
2.8
by analyzing random sequences produced by Algorithm A.

In the second step of the proof, we are going to show that the Łukasiewicz path of a random tree from
\Omega_{k,n}
is close to the height process of the tree. This result is formulated in Lemma
2.10
.

In the third step, we will show that with high probability the height process is close to the contour process. See Lemma
2.11
.

The final step is to bring all these results together and show that the contour process for a tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
converges to the Brownian excursion.

In all lemmas in this section (Lemmas
2.5
-
2.13
), we assume that the assumptions of Theorem
1.2
are satisfied. That is, condition (
1
) holds, and the sequence of pairs
(k,n)
is such that
(k,n)\in PP(w)
and, as
n\to\infty
,
k=\alpha n+O(1)
, where
\alpha\in(0,1-1/\widehat{\nu})
.

Step 1: Łukasiewicz path converges to Brownian excursion.

Let
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1},\ldots,\xi^{\prime\prime}_{n}
be the sequence of random variables produced by Step 2 of Algorithm A. Some properties of these random variables are proved in Lemmas

and
2.6

and

Lemma 2.5
.

As
n\to\infty
, we have:

(a)

For each integer
s\geq 0
,

\mathbb{E}((\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{s})\to\mathbb{E}((L^{\ast})^{s})
, where
L_{\ast}
is a random variable with distribution
w_{j}^{\ast}
. In particular,
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})\to(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}
;

(b)

For each integer pair
s,t\geq 0
,
\mathrm{Cov}\Big((\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{s},(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{2})^{t}\Big)\to 0
.

(c)

For every
\beta>0
, there exists a
\gamma>0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\max_{j}|\xi^{\prime\prime}_{j}|>n^{\beta})\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\beta}}.

Proof.

(a)
First, consider variables
\xi^{\prime}_{i}
. They have the distribution:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\xi^{\prime}_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,\xi^{\prime}_{n-k}=j_{n-k}\Big)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j_{1},\ldots,\hat{\zeta}_{n-k}=j_{n-k}\Big|S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big),

where
\hat{\zeta}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\zeta}_{n-k}
are i.i.d. random variables with distribution
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j)=\widehat{w}^{\ast}_{j}=w^{\ast}_{j}/(1-\alpha)
, if
j\geq 1
, and
0
if
j=0
. Here
S_{\hat{\zeta}}(p)
denotes
\sum_{i=1}^{p}\hat{\zeta}_{i}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{\prime}_{1}=j)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j\Big|S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)=\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j,\sum_{i=2}^{n-k}\hat{\zeta}_{i}=n-1-j\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j)\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-1)=n-1-j\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)}.

The distribution of the random variable
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}
can be obtained as a mix distribution:
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}=0
with probability
(k/n)
and
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}=\xi^{\prime}
with probability
(1-k/n)
, where
\xi^{\prime}
has the same distribution as
\xi^{\prime}_{1}
. Also, the distribution
L^{\ast}
is a mix of the trivial distribution with probability
\alpha
and the distribution of
\hat{\zeta}
with probability
(1-\alpha)
. So, for
j>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}=j)

\displaystyle=(1-k/n)\mathbb{P}(\xi^{\prime}_{1}=j),

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(L^{\ast}=j)

\displaystyle=(1-\alpha)\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j),

and therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}=j)=\mathbb{P}(L^{\ast}=j)\frac{1-k/n}{1-\alpha}\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-1)=n-1-j\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)}.

Next, both
k
and
n
grow to infinity in such a way that
k/n\to\alpha\in(0,1)
, hence
\mathbb{E}S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=(n-k)\mathbb{E}\hat{\zeta}_{1}=n(1-k/n)/(1-\alpha)=n+o(1)
and similarly
\mathbb{E}S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-1)=n+o(1)
. We apply the local limit law (Theorem I.4.2 in Kolchin
kolchin84
) and find that for any
\varepsilon>0
we can find
n(\varepsilon)
such that for all
n>n(\varepsilon)
and all
j<n^{1/4}
,

\displaystyle\bigg|\frac{1-k/n}{1-\alpha}\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-1)=n-1-j\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)}-1\bigg|<\varepsilon.

In addition, this ratio of probabilities is bounded uniformly in
j
and
n
.

Then, for arbitrary
\varepsilon>0
and
n>n(\varepsilon)
, and for some
c>0
.

\displaystyle\bigg|\mathbb{E}\Big[(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{s}\Big]-\mathbb{E}\Big[(L^{\ast})^{s}\Big]\bigg|=\varepsilon\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor}j^{s}\,\mathbb{P}(L^{\ast}=j)+c\sum_{j={\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor}+1}^{\infty}j^{s}\,\mathbb{P}(L^{\ast}=j).

By assumption, the probability distribution of
L
has exponentially declining tails, which implies that
L^{\ast}
has exponentially declining tails, so the last term is bounded by

\displaystyle c\sum_{j={\lfloor n^{1/4}\rfloor}+1}^{\infty}j^{s}\,e^{-aj}\to 0,

as
n\to\infty
,
and we conclude that all moments of random variable
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}
converge to the corresponding moments of
L^{\ast}
, as
n\to\infty
. In particular,
\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})\to(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}
.

(b) By a similar argument, we can write for
j_{1}>0,j_{2}>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}=j_{1},\xi^{\prime\prime}_{2}=j_{2})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(L_{1}^{\ast}=j_{1},L_{2}^{\ast}=j_{2})\Big(\frac{1-k/n}{1-\alpha}\Big)^{2}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-2)=n-1-j_{1}-j_{2}\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)},

where
L_{1}^{\ast}
,
L_{2}^{\ast}
are two independent random variables that have the
\alpha
-shifted distribution. By using the local limit law, we find that the ratio of probabilities approaches
1
as
n\to\infty
, uniformly for
j_{1}<n^{1/4},j_{2}<n^{1/4}
. In addition, this ratio is bounded uniformly in
n,j_{1},j_{2}
. Together with assumption (1), this ensures that
\mathbb{E}\Big((\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{s}(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{2})^{t}\Big)\to\mathbb{E}\Big((L^{\ast}_{1})^{s}(L^{\ast}_{2})^{t}\Big)
as
n\to\infty
, for all integer
s\geq 0,t\geq 0
. In particular, this implies that
\mathrm{Cov}\Big((\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1})^{s},(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{2})^{t}\Big)\to 0
, as
n\to\infty
.

(c) We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle(\max_{i}\xi^{\prime}_{i}\leq n^{\beta})=\mathbb{P}\Big(\xi^{\prime}_{1}\leq n^{\beta},\ldots,\xi^{\prime}_{n-k}\leq n^{\beta}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}\Big(\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta},\ldots,\hat{\zeta}_{n-k}\leq n^{\beta}\Big|S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)

\displaystyle=\Big[\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta})\Big]^{n-k}\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)\Big|\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta},\ldots,\hat{\zeta}_{n-k}\leq n^{\beta}\Big)}{\mathbb{P}(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)}.

Define truncated random variables
\hat{\zeta}_{i}^{(n)}
,
i=1,\ldots,n-k
as i.i.d random variables with distribution

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}^{(n)}=j)=\mathbb{P}\Big(\hat{\zeta}_{1}=j\Big|\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta}\Big).

Then we can write:

\mathbb{P}(\max_{i}\xi^{\prime}_{i}\leq n^{\beta})=\Big[\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta})\Big]^{n-k}\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}^{(n)}}(n-k)=n-1)\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)}.

(16)

By the assumption on the offspring probabilities,
\widehat{\zeta}_{1}
has exponentially declining tails and therefore, for some
a>0
, we can write:

\displaystyle(n-k)\log\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta})\geq(n-k)\log(1-e^{-an^{\beta}})\geq-2ne^{-an^{\beta}},

where the last inequality follows because by a choice of
a
we can ensure
e^{-an^{\beta}}<3/4
and we have
\log(1-x)\geq-2x
for
0\leq x\leq 3/4
. Then,

\displaystyle\Big[\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta})\Big]^{n-k}

\displaystyle\geq e^{-2ne^{-an^{\beta}}}\geq 1-2ne^{-an^{\beta}}.

This implies that for sufficiently small
\gamma>0
,

\big[\mathbb{P}(\hat{\zeta}_{1}\leq n^{\beta})\big]^{n-k}\geq 1-e^{-\gamma n^{\beta}}
.

To estimate the denominator in the ratio of probabilities in (
16
), we can use the local limit theorem (Theorem I.4.2 in Kolchin
kolchin84
). For the numerator, this theorem is not applicable directly, since the distribution of random variables
\hat{\zeta}_{i}^{(n)}
is changing with
n
. However, it can be adapted to this more general setting along the lines in the proof of Theorem I.4.2 in Kolchin. (We provide the proof of the modified local limit theorem in “Appendix”.)
Then, we find that, for
r>0
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}^{(n)}}(n-k)=n-1)\Big)}{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1\Big)}\to 1

as
n\to\infty
. It follows that for some smaller
\gamma>0
,
\mathbb{P}(\max_{i}\xi^{\prime}_{i}\leq n^{\beta})\geq 1-e^{-\gamma n^{\beta}}
. In a similar fashion we can show that
\mathbb{P}(\min_{i}\xi^{\prime}_{i}\geq-n^{\beta})\geq 1-e^{-\gamma n^{\beta}}
. The sequence
\xi^{\prime\prime}_{(1)},\ldots,\xi^{\prime\prime}_{(n)}
is different from the sequence
\xi^{\prime}_{(1)},\ldots,\xi^{\prime}_{(n-k)}
only by addition of
k
zeros. It follows that some
\gamma>0
,

\mathbb{P}(\max_{i}|\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}|\leq n^{\beta})\geq 1-e^{-\gamma n^{\beta}}
, which implies the claim of the lemma.

∎

Lemma 2.6
.

As
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\Big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}-1+\frac{1}{n}\Big)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}.

Proof.

We have
\mathbb{E}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}=1-1/n
and
\mathbb{E}\big[(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}-1+\frac{1}{n})^{2}\big]\to(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}
by Lemma
2.5
(a).
Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}

\displaystyle\Big(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2}\Big)=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big[\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\Big(\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle+2\sum_{i<j}\mathrm{Cov}\Big(\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2},\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{j}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\Big[n\mathbb{V}\mathrm{ar}\Big(\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2}\Big)+n(n-1)\mathrm{Cov}\Big(\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{1}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2},\big(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{2}-1+\frac{1}{n}\big)^{2}\Big)\Big]

\displaystyle\to 0,

by Lemma
2.5
(a) and (b). By Chebyshev’s inequality, this implies the statement of the lemma.
∎

Let
S^{\prime\prime}_{n}(0)=0
and

\displaystyle S^{\prime\prime}_{n}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i},\text{ where }j=1,\ldots,n,

and extend

to all

by linear interpolation.

In the lemmas below, the convergence of stochastic processes is the weak convergence with respect to the space of continuous functions on

with the topology induced by the uniform norm:
\|f\|_{\infty}=\sup_{t\in[0,1]}f(t)
.

Lemma 2.7
.

As
n\to\infty

\displaystyle\frac{S^{\prime\prime}_{n}(tn)-tn}{\sigma^{\ast}\sqrt{n}}\Big|_{t\in[0,1]}\xrightarrow{weakly}b(t)\Big|_{t\in[0,1]},

where
b(t)
is the standard Brownian bridge on the interval
[0,1]
, and
\sigma^{\ast}
is as defined in (
5
).

Proof.

Define

x_{j}=(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{j}-1+1/n)/(\sqrt{n}\sigma^{\ast})

and
X_{n}(t)=\sum_{j=1}^{[tn]}x_{j}
, with
X_{n}(t)=0
for
0\leq t<1/n
.
Then,
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}=0
and
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}1
by Lemma
2.6
. In addition, by Lemma
2.5
(c),
\max_{j}|x_{j}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\scriptscriptstyle\mathbb{P}}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
. This means that the assumptions of Theorem 24.2 in Billingsley’s book
billingsley1968

. This implies the claim of the lemma.
∎

Next, we consider the application of Step 3 in Algorithm A. By Theorem

, this step produces the Łukasiewicz path of a random tree in

.

Lemma 2.8
.

As
n\to\infty
, the normalized Łukasiewicz path
S_{n}(tn)/(\sigma^{\ast}\sqrt{n})
of a random tree from
\Omega_{k,n}
weakly converges to a standard Brownian excursion
e(t)
.

Proof.

Let
b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)=(S^{\prime\prime}_{n}(tn)-tn)/(\sigma^{\ast}\sqrt{n})
. By the previous lemma,
b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)
converges to
b(t)
in distribution. According to Vervaat’s Theorem (Theorem 1 in
vervaat79
), the location of the absolute minimum of Brownian bridge
b(t)
is unique with probability
1
, and, if this time is denoted
\tau_{m}
, the process
b(t+\tau_{m})-b(\tau_{m})
is distributed as a Brownian excursion
e(t)
. (Here, the addition in the argument is modulo
1
.)

Since
b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)
converges to the Brownian bridge
b(t)
in distribution, we can couple the random variables
b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)
and
b(t)
, so that the time at which
b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t)
achieves its first minimum,
\tau^{\prime\prime}_{m}
, converges in probability to
\tau_{m}
. Then,

\displaystyle b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t+\tau^{\prime\prime}_{m})-b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\tau^{\prime\prime}_{m})\Big|_{t\in[0,1]}\xrightarrow{dist}b(t+\tau_{m})-b(\tau_{m})\Big|_{t\in[0,1]},

which is distributed as a Brownian excursion by Vervaat’s theorem.

If we write
S_{n}(j)=\sum_{i=1}^{j}(\widehat{\xi}^{\prime\prime}_{i}-1)
, where
(\widehat{\xi}^{\prime\prime}_{i})
is the sequence
(\xi^{\prime\prime}_{i})
after the cyclic shift, then the process
b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(t+\tau^{\prime\prime}_{m})-b^{\prime\prime}_{n}(\tau^{\prime\prime}_{m})
is equal to

S_{n}(t)/(\sigma^{\ast}\sqrt{n})
. By Theorem
1.6
,
S_{n}(t)
is distributed as the Łukasiewicz path of a random tree from
\Omega_{k,n}
.

Hence, we have shown that the normalized Łukasiewicz path
S_{n}(tn)/(\sigma^{\ast}\sqrt{n})
of a random tree from
\Omega_{k,n}
converges to a standard Brownian excursion
e(t)
.
∎

Step 2: Łukasiewicz path is close to the height process.

The height process for an ordered tree is defined in (
8
). In Lemma 1 of Marckert and Mokkadem
marckert_mokkadem2003
, it was shown that if
S_{n}(j)
is the Łukasiewicz path of an ordered tree, then the height process
H_{n}(l)
,
l=0,\ldots,n-1
, can be written as follows:

H_{n}(l)=\#\Big\{i:0\leq i\leq l-1,S_{n}(i)=\min_{i\leq k\leq l}\big\{S_{n}(k)\big\}\Big\}

(17)

In words,
H_{n}(l)
is the number of (weak) right minima of
S_{n}
on the interval
[0,l]
(not counting the trivial right minimum at
i=l
).

For example, in Fig.
1
,
H_{8}(3)=2
, and this corresponds to the right minima at
i=0
and
i=2
of the Łukasiewicz path
S_{8}(i)
on interval
[0,3]
.

Now, let
W_{n}(j)
,
j=0,\ldots,n
, be a path of a random walk with independent increments
x_{i}
that can take values
s=-1,0,1,\ldots
with the following probabilities:

\mathbb{P}(x_{i}=s)=w^{\ast}_{s+1},

(18)

where

\{w^{\ast}_{k}\}_{k\geq 0}
is the
\alpha
-shifted weight distribution from Definition
1
. For a path of this random walk, we can also define the number of weak right minima on the interval
[0,l]
,

l=0,\ldots,n-1
:

RM_{n}(l)=\#\Big\{i:0\leq i\leq l-1,W_{n}(i)=\min_{i\leq k\leq l}\big\{W_{n}(k)\big\}\Big\}

(19)

The key result about the process
RM_{n}(l)
was proved in
marckert_mokkadem2003
.

Lemma 2.9

(Corollary 5 in
marckert_mokkadem2003
)
.

Let
W_{n}(j)
,
j=0,\ldots,n
, be a path of a random walk with independent increments that have the law as in (
18
), and let
RM_{n}(l)
be as defined in (
19
). Then, for some
\gamma>0
and
\nu>0
, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{0\leq l<n}\Big|W_{n}(l)-\min_{0\leq i\leq l}W_{n}(i)-RM_{n}(l)\frac{(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}}{2}\Big|\geq n^{1/4+\nu}\bigg)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}.

(20)

Note that if
W_{n}(j)
happens to be an excursion, then
\min_{0\leq i\leq l}W_{n}(i)=0
and in this case Lemma
2.9
says that
RM_{n}(l)
after scaling by
(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}/2
is close to the excursion
W_{n}(l)
.

The proof of this result in
marckert_mokkadem2003
is based on a reduction of the problem to a problem about ladder heights of a random walk, for which appropriate tools are available in Feller’s book (
feller71
, Chapter XII).

By using this result, we can complete the second step of the proof.

Lemma 2.10
.

Let
S_{n}(l)
and
H_{n}(l)
be the Łukasiewicz and the height processes for a random tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
.
For any
\nu>0
, there exists constants
\gamma>0
and
N>0
such that for all
n\geq N
and
(n,k)\in PP(w)
,

\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{0\leq l<n}\Big|S_{n}(l)-\frac{(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}}{2}H_{n}(l)\Big|\geq n^{1/4+\nu}\bigg)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}.

(21)

Proof.

From (
17
) and (
19
), it can be seen that the probability of the event in (
21
) equals the probability of the event in (
20
), denoted as
\mathfrak{A}_{n}
, conditional on the event
\mathfrak{B}_{n}
that
W_{n}(i)
,
i=0,\ldots,n
is an excursion with exactly
k
steps equal to
-1
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{n}|\mathfrak{B}_{n})\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{n})}{\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})}\leq\frac{e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}}{\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})}.

It follows that it is enough to show that
\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})
is sufficiently large. Let us introduce two other events:
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
is the event that
W_{n}(i)
has exactly
k
steps equal to
-1
and
\mathfrak{E}_{n}
is the event that
W_{n}(i)
is an excursion. Then
\mathfrak{B}_{n}=\mathfrak{C}_{n}\cap\mathfrak{E}_{n}
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})=\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{E}_{n}|\mathfrak{C}_{n})\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{C}_{n}).

The probability of the increment
-1
in the random walk
W_{n}
equals to
w^{\ast}_{0}=\alpha
, so by the local central limit theorem the probability of the event
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
is asymptotically equal to
a/\sqrt{n}
with
a>0
.

In order to calculate
\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{E}_{n}|\mathfrak{C}_{n})
we note that the distribution of the increments conditional that they are non-negative is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(x_{j}=s|x_{j}\geq 0)=\frac{w^{\ast}_{s+1}}{1-\alpha},\text{ for }s=0,1,2,\ldots

The conditional expectation of the increment equals

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|x_{j}\geq 0)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{s=0}^{\infty}sw^{\ast}_{s+1}=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}(k-1)w^{\ast}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}kw^{\ast}_{k}-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}w^{\ast}_{k}\Big)=\frac{1}{1-\alpha}\Big(1-(1-\alpha)\Big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}.

Then the expectation of the sum
W_{n}(n)
conditional that exactly
k
of the increments are equal to
-1
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(W_{n}(n)|\mathfrak{C}_{n})

\displaystyle=-k+\mathbb{E}(x_{j}|x_{j}\geq 0)(n-k)

\displaystyle=-\alpha n+\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}(1-\alpha)n+O(1)=O(1).

Again, by using the local central limit theorem, we find that the probability that
W_{n}
is a random walk bridge, conditional on the event
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
, is asymptotically equal to
b/\sqrt{n}
with
b>0
.
Then, all
n
cyclic shifts of this random walk bridge have the same probability by exchangeability of the increments and exactly one of these shifts is an excursion by the cyclic lemma. Hence, conditional on
\mathfrak{C}_{n}
, the random walk
W_{n}
is an excursion with probability
\sim b/n^{3/2}
. Altogether we conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}_{n})\sim\frac{ab}{n^{5/2}},

with
ab>0
.
Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{A}_{n}|\mathfrak{B}_{n})\leq\frac{1}{ab}n^{5/2}e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}\leq e^{-\gamma^{\prime}n^{\nu}},

for a suitably chosen
\gamma^{\prime}
and this completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

Step 3. The height process is close to the contour process.

Here we introduce a couple of purely combinatorial results from Marckert and Mokkadem
marckert_mokkadem2003
that relate the height and contour processes.

Definition 2
.

Let
T\in\Omega_{n}

are located on the graph of the contour process. Note also that for all
k\in\big[m(l),m(l+1)\big)

H_{n}(l+1)-1\leq C_{n}(k)\leq H_{n}(l).

(see Lemma 3 in

Intuitively,
\big(m(l),H_{n}(l)\big)
interpolate the contour process, and
l\mapsto m(l)
can be thought as a time change that connects the contour process to the height process. See the illustration in Fig.

.

Another important combinatorial result proved in
marckert_mokkadem2003
is that for every
l\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
,

m(l)=2l-H_{n}(l).

(23)

Since for large
l
, we can expect that
H_{n}(l)
has the order
\sqrt{l}
with large probability, this identity suggests that the time change function
m(l)\sim 2l
with large probability, and that the deviations of
m(l)
from
2l
are of order
\sqrt{l}
.

Now, let us define
\tau(nt)
as the integer such that

\lfloor 2nt\rfloor\in\Big[m\big(\tau(nt)\big),m\big(\tau(nt)+1\big)\Big).

(24)

(Intuitively, this function plays the role of the inverse of
m(l)
scaled by
2
.)

Then we have the following result.

Lemma 2.11
.

For all
a>0
and
\nu>0
, there exists such
\gamma>0
that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|H_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)-C_{n}(2nt)\Big|\geq an^{1/4+\nu}\Big)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}

Proof.

By (
22
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|H_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)-C_{n}(2nt)\Big|\geq an^{1/4+\nu}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}}\Big|H_{n}\big(l\big)-H_{n}\big(l+1\big)+1\Big|\geq an^{1/4+\nu}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}}\Big|H_{n}\big(l\big)-\frac{2}{(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}}S_{n}(l)\Big|\geq\frac{a}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\Big)

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}}\Big|H_{n}\big(l+1\big)-1-\frac{2}{(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}}S_{n}(l+1)\Big|\geq\frac{a}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\Big),

\displaystyle+\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-2\}}\frac{2}{(\sigma^{\ast})^{2}}\Big|S_{n}\big(l+1\big)-S_{n}(l)\Big|\geq\frac{a}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\Big),

and by Lemmas
2.5
(c) and
2.10
, the sum of these three probabilities can be bounded by
e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}
with a suitable
\gamma>0
.
∎

By using Lemmas
2.10
and
2.11
, we can relate the Łukasiewicz process
S(\tau(nt))
and the contour process
C_{n}(nt)
. To move further, we need to estimate the difference between
\tau(nt)
and
nt
.

Lemma 2.12
.

Let
\tau(nt)
be as in (
24
) for the random time
m(l)
defined for a random tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|\tau(nt)-nt\big|>n^{1/2+\nu}\Big)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}.

Proof.

By the triangle inequality, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|2\tau(nt)-2nt\big|\leq\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|2\tau(nt)-m(\tau(nt))\big|+\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|m(\tau(nt))-2nt\big|.

By applying Equation (
23
) with
l=\tau(nt)
, we find that the first term is bounded by
\sup_{t}H_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)
. For the second term, we note that by definition of
\tau(nt)
, it is bounded by

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|m\big(\tau(nt)+1\big)-m\big(\tau(nt)\big)\Big|

\displaystyle=\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}\big|m(l+1)-m(l)\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}H_{n}(l).

Altogether, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|2\tau(nt)-2nt\big|\leq 2\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}H_{n}(l).

By (
17
),
H_{n}(l)
is the number of right minima for
S_{n}
on the interval
[0,l]
. It was shown in Lemma 5 in
marckert_mokkadem2003
, that the number of right minima for the random walk
W_{n}
satisfies the bound

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}RM_{n}(l)\geq n^{1/2+\nu}\Big)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}

for a
\gamma>0
. An inequality of this form also holds for the number of right minima of
S_{n}
by a conditioning argument similar to the argument that we used in the proof of Lemma
2.10
.

So we have, for some
\gamma>0
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|\tau(nt)-nt\big|\leq n^{1/2+\nu}\Big)\geq\mathbb{P}\Big(2\sup_{l\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}}H_{n}(l)\leq n^{1/2+\nu}\Big)\geq 1-e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}

and

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|\tau(nt)-nt\big|>n^{1/2+\nu}\Big)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}

∎

Lemma 2.13
.

Let
S_{n}(t)
and
C_{n}(t)
be the Łukasiewicz and the contour processes for a tree
T\in\Omega_{k,n}
. Then, for every
\nu>0
, there exist two positive constants,
\gamma>0
and
N>0
, such that for all
n\geq N
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|S_{n}(nt)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{n}(2nt)\Big|\geq n^{1/4+\nu}\bigg]\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}.

Proof.

We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|S_{n}(nt)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}C_{n}(2nt)\big|\geq n^{1/4+\nu}\Big)\leq A+B+C+D,

where

\displaystyle A=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|S_{n}(nt)-S_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)\Big|\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{1/4+\nu},\sup_{t}\big|\tau(nt)-nt\big|<n^{1/2+\nu}\bigg),

\displaystyle B=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|S_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}H_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)\Big|\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\bigg),

\displaystyle C=\mathbb{P}\bigg(\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|H_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)-C_{n}(2nt)\Big|\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\bigg),

\displaystyle D=\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\big|\tau(nt)-nt\big|>n^{1/2+\nu}\Big).

The quantities
B
,
C
, and
D
can be estimated using Lemmas
2.10
,
2.11
, and
2.12
, respectively. For quantity
A
, note that we can estimate a similar quantity for unconditional random walk
W_{n}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg(\sup_{t\in[0,1]}\Big|W_{n}(nt)-W_{n}\big(\tau(nt)\big)\Big|\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{1/4+\nu},\sup_{t}\big|\tau(nt)-nt\big|<n^{1/2+\nu}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq n^{1/2+\nu}}\sup_{0\leq j\leq n-k}\big|W_{n}(j)-W_{n}(j+k)\big|\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\Big(\sup_{k\leq n^{1/2+\nu}}\big|W_{n}(k)\big|\geq\frac{1}{3}n^{1/4+\nu}\Big)\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}.

The last step holds by Theorem III.15 from Petrov’s book
petrov75
(see also Appendix in
marckert_mokkadem2003
), and here we use the assumption that
\mathbb{E}e^{aL}<\infty
for some
a>0
.

This estimate remains valid for the process
S_{n}
instead of
W_{n}
, perhaps with a different
\gamma>0
, by a conditioning argument similar to the argument that was used in the proof of Lemma
2.10
. Therefore, we can find such
\gamma>0
, that
A+B+C+D\leq e^{-\gamma n^{\nu}}
.
∎

Now we can conclude the proof of Theorem
1.2
. In Lemma
2.8
we proved that the normalized Łukasiewicz path
S_{n}(tn)/(\sigma^{\ast}\sqrt{n})
of a random tree from
\Omega_{k,n}
converges to a standard Brownian excursion
e(t)
. Then Lemma
2.13
implies that the process
\sigma^{\ast}C_{n}(2tn)/(2\sqrt{n})
also converges to the excursion
e(t)
. This concludes the proof.

For the proof of Theorem
1.4
, we need a lemma, which allows us to compare the probabilities of various events for unconditional random walks and corresponding random bridges. As in the proof of Theorem
1.6
, let
\hat{\zeta}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\zeta}_{n-k}
be an i.i.d sequence with
\hat{\zeta}_{1}
having distribution
\widehat{w}_{0}^{\ast}=0,\hat{w}_{i}^{\ast}=w_{i}^{\ast}/(1-\alpha)
,
i=1,\ldots
. Let

\displaystyle m:={\lfloor(n-k)/2\rfloor},

and let
\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(1/2)}
denote the sigma-algebra generated by random variables
\hat{\zeta}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\zeta}_{m}
.

Lemma 2.14
.

Suppose
(n,k_{n})\in PP(w)
,
k_{n}=\alpha n+O(1)
, and the distribution
\{w_{i}\}
has finite variance. For every event
\mathfrak{B}\in\mathcal{F}_{n}^{(1/2)}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}|\hat{\zeta}_{1}+\ldots+\hat{\zeta}_{n-k}=n-1)\leq c\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}),

where
c
is a positive constant that depends on
\{w_{i}\}
but not on
n
.

Proof.

For the joint probability, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B},S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathfrak{B},S_{\hat{\zeta}}(m)=x\Big)

\displaystyle\times\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-m)=n-1-x)\Big)

Hence, for the conditional probability,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}|S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathfrak{B},S_{\hat{\zeta}}(m)=x\Big)

\displaystyle\times\frac{\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-m)=n-1-x)\Big)}{\mathbb{P}(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)}.

By using the local limit law (Theorem I.4.2 in Kolchin
kolchin84
) we have
\mathbb{P}(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)\geq c_{1}/\sqrt{n}
for some
c_{1}>0
. By Theorem 2.21 on p.76 in Petrov
petrov95
,
\sup_{x}\mathbb{P}\Big(S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k-m)=n-1-x)\Big)\leq c_{2}/\sqrt{n}
for some
c_{2}>0
. Altogether, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}|S_{\hat{\zeta}}(n-k)=n-1)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\Big(\mathfrak{B},S_{\hat{\zeta}}(m)=x\Big)=\frac{c_{2}}{c_{1}}\mathbb{P}(\mathfrak{B}).

∎

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Let

\displaystyle n_{\xi}(i)

\displaystyle=\Big|\{j:\xi_{j}=i,1\leq j\leq n\}\Big|,

\displaystyle n_{\xi^{\prime}}(i)

\displaystyle=\Big|\{j:\xi^{\prime}_{j}=i,1\leq j\leq n-k\}\Big|,

\displaystyle n_{\hat{\zeta}}(i)

\displaystyle=\Big|\{j:\hat{\zeta}_{j}=i,1\leq j\leq n-k\}\Big|,

where sequences
(\xi_{j}),(\xi^{\prime}_{j})
and
(\hat{\zeta}_{j})
are as defined in the definition of Algorithm A and in the proof of Theorem
1.6
. By Theorem
1.6
, the random variable
n_{i}(T)
has the same distribution as
n_{\xi}(i)
. For
i=0
, we have
n_{\xi}(0)=k
and
k/(n-1)\to\alpha
by assumption, so
n_{0}(T)/(n-1)\to\alpha=w_{0}^{\ast}
. For
i>0
, we can see from the algorithm that it is enough to show that
n_{\xi^{\prime}}(i)/(n-k)\to\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}=w_{i}^{\ast}/(1-\alpha)
almost surely.

Let us define additionally,

\displaystyle n_{\hat{\zeta}}^{(1/2)}(i)

\displaystyle=\Big|\Big\{j:\hat{\zeta}_{j}=i,1\leq j\leq\frac{n-k}{2}\Big\}\Big|,

\displaystyle n_{\xi^{\prime}}^{(1/2)}(i)

\displaystyle=\Big|\Big\{j:\xi^{\prime}_{j}=i,1\leq j\leq\frac{n-k}{2}\Big\}\Big|.

As before, for shortness of formulas, we define
m:=\lfloor\frac{n-k}{2}\rfloor
.

Then,

\displaystyle\frac{n_{\hat{\zeta}}^{(1/2)}(i)}{m}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\mathbbm{1}[\hat{\zeta}_{j}=i],

which implies
\mathbb{E}\Big(\frac{n_{\hat{\zeta}}^{(1/2)}(i)}{m}\Big)=\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}
. Then, by using the independence of random variables
\hat{\zeta}_{j}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Big|\frac{n_{\hat{\zeta}}^{(1/2)}(i)}{m}-\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}\Big|^{4}=\frac{1}{m^{4}}\mathbb{E}\Big(\sum_{j=1}^{m}(\mathbbm{1}[\hat{\zeta}_{j}=i]-\widehat{w}_{j}^{\ast})\Big)^{4}=O(m^{-2}).

Then, by Markov’s inequality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big|\frac{n_{\hat{\zeta}}^{(1/2)}(i)}{m}-\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}\Big|>\varepsilon\bigg]=O\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}m^{2}}\Big)

By using Lemma
2.14
, we infer that this estimate holds also for the conditioned variables
\xi^{\prime}_{j}
:

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big|\frac{n_{\xi^{\prime}}^{(1/2)}(i)}{m}-\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}\Big|>\varepsilon\bigg]=O\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}m^{2}}\Big).

(25)

Let

\displaystyle n_{\xi^{\prime}}^{(2/2)}(i)=\Big|\Big\{j:\xi^{\prime}_{j}=i,m<j\leq n-k\Big\}\Big|.

Since
\{\xi^{\prime}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{n}
is an exchangeable sequence, we have

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big|\frac{n_{\xi^{\prime}}^{(2/2)}(i)}{m}-\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}\Big|>\varepsilon\bigg]=O\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}m^{2}}\Big).

(26)

Together, Eqs. (
25
) and (
26
) imply that

\mathbb{P}\bigg[\Big|\frac{n_{\xi^{\prime}}(i)}{n-k}-\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast}\Big|>\varepsilon\bigg]=O\Big(\frac{1}{\varepsilon^{4}((n-k)^{2}}\Big).

(27)

So, by Borel–Cantelli lemma, as
n\to\infty
,

\displaystyle\frac{n_{\xi^{\prime}}(i)}{n-k}\to\widehat{w}_{i}^{\ast},

almost surely. This implies the statement of the theorem.

∎

3
Conclusion and Remaining Questions

In this paper, we investigated the scaling limit of Galton–Watson trees conditioned on a fixed number of vertices
n
and leaves
k
. We considered the case when
both
k
and
n
grow to infinity and
k=\alpha n+O(1)
, with
\alpha\in(0,1-1/\hat{\nu})
. The offspring distribution is assumed to have the exponentially declining tails (
\mathbb{E}e^{aL}<\infty
for some
a>0
).

In this case, we found that the limit under rescaling is the Aldous continuum random tree, and calculated the relevant parameter. We also showed that the height of a tree in
\Omega_{k,n}
converges under rescaling to the distribution of the maximum of the Brownian excursion. Finally, we showed that the normalized degree sequence of the tree converges almost surely to the
\alpha
-shifted distribution.

Some other questions remain open. First, what happens when the assumption of exponentially declining tails is relaxed? Similar to a comment in Marckert and Mokkadem
marckert_mokkadem2003
, we can argue that some number of finite moments and the assumption
\mathbb{E}L=1
should be sufficient to ensure that our convergence results stay intact.
However, the infinite variance of the offspring distribution or power tails with
\mathbb{E}L<1
will likely lead to considerably different results.

Another question is what happens in the intermediate regime when both
k
and
n
grow but
k/n\sim n^{-\beta}
or
\big(k/n-(1-\widehat{\nu})\big)\sim n^{-\beta}
with
0<\beta<1
. The first case is when the tree has very small but still growing number of leaves. The second case is about the regime when the number of leaves in the tree is close to the maximal capacity that the offspring distribution can sustain. The results in the paper of Labarbe and Marckert
labarbe_marckert2007
about Bernoulli random walks suggest that even in this case the limit is Aldous’ Brownian continuum random tree, although the scaling factor may be different from
n^{1/2}
.

Besides the scaling limit in the Gromov–Hausdorff topology, it could also be interesting to study the characteristics of the conditioned trees in these situations, such as their limiting degree sequences.
