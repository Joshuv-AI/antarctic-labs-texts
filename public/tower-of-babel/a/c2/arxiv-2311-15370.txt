Difference ascent sequences
Mark Dukes

Abstract
Let α = a 1 ​ a 2 ​ … ​ a n \alpha=a_{1}a_{2}\ldots a_{n} be a sequence of nonnegative integers. The ascent set of α \alpha , Asc ⁡ α \Asc\alpha , consists of all indices k k where a k + 1 > a k a_{k+1}>a_{k} . An ascent sequence is α \alpha where the growth of the a k a_{k} is bounded by the elements of Asc ⁡ α \Asc\alpha . These sequences were introduced by Bousquet-Mélou, Claesson, Dukes and Kitaev and have many wonderful properties. In particular, they are in bijection with unlabeled ( 2 + 2 ) (2+2) -free posets, permutations avoiding a particular bivincular pattern, certain upper-triangular nonnegative integer matrices, and a class of matchings. A weak ascent of α \alpha is an index k k with a k + 1 ≥ a k a_{k+1}\geq a_{k} and weak ascent sequences are defined analogously to ascent sequences. These were studied by Bényi, Claesson and Dukes and shown to have analogous equinumerous sets. Given a nonnegative integer d d , we define a difference d d ascent to be an index k k such that a k + 1 > a k − d a_{k+1}>a_{k}-d . We study the properties of the corresponding d d -ascent sequences, showing that some of the maps from the weak case can be extended to bijections for general d d while the extensions of others continue to be injective (but not surjective). We also make connections with other combinatorial objects such as rooted duplication trees and restricted growth functions.

A
restricted growth function (RGF) of length
n
is a sequence of nonnegative integers
\rho=r_{1}r_{2}\ldots r_{n}
satisfying

(r1)

, and

\rho_{k}=r_{1}r_{2}\ldots r_{k}
and the maximum of a sequence is the maximum value of its elements as integers.
Since
\rho_{k+1}=\rho_{k}r_{k+1}
and
\rho
is an RGF, it must be that for all
k<n
we have

\max\rho_{k}\leq\max\rho_{k+1}\leq\max\rho_{k}+1,

(15)

where
\rho_{k+1}

.
Let

\RGF_{n}=\{\rho\mid\text{$\rho$ is a restricted growth function of length $n$}\}.

It is not hard to see that

the set of length
n

Also, the RGFs of length

are in natural bijection with the partitions of
[n]
so that

(16)

We will show that there is a bijection
\rp:\RGF_{n}\rightarrow\Av_{n}(P_{3})

\max\rho_{j}<r_{k}
. So, by transitivity,
\max\rho_{i}<r_{k}
which is equivalent to
i<_{P}j
.

We show that
P
avoids a special
P_{3}
by induction on
n
, where the statement is obvious for
n\leq 2
. Assume, that for RGFs of length
n-1
their image under
\rp
is special
P_{3}
-free. Now consider an RGF
\rho
of length
n
and assume, towards a contradiction, that
P=\rp(\rho)
contains a special
P_{3}
labeled as

j
k
j+1

where
j+1<k
.
Now the prefix
\rho^{\prime}=\rho_{n-1}
of
\rho
is of length
n-1
. So, by induction,
P^{\prime}=\rp(\rho^{\prime})

is special
P_{3}
-free. But by (
17
), the poset
P^{\prime}
is the restriction of
P
to the elements of the set
[n-1]
. So the only way that
P^{\prime}
could avoid
P_{3}
while
P
contains a copy would be if that copy includes
n
. Since
k
is the largest integer in our copy, it must be that
k=n
.

Now
j<_{P}n
and
j+1\nless_{P}n
implies
\max\rho_{j}<r_{n}\leq\max\rho_{j+1}
.
Combining the previous sentence with equation (
15
) and the comment which follows it, we must have
r_{j+1}=\max\rho_{j+1}=\max\rho_{j}+1
.
But
j\nless_{P}j+1
and so
\max\rho_{j}\geq r_{j+1}
which contradicts the previous sentence.
∎

In order to provide an inverse for
\rp
, we will need the following lemmas. For
k
in a factorial poset
P
we will use the notation

M(k)=\max\{i\in P\mid i<_{P}k\},

where the maximum is taken in the integers. It follows easily from properties of factorial posets that if
M(k)=m
then

\{i\in P\mid i<_{P}k\}=[m].

(18)

Lemma 7.2
.

If
\rho=r_{1}r_{2}\ldots r_{n}
and
P=\rp(\rho)
then for all
k\in[n]
we have

r_{k}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}0&\mbox{if $k$ is minimal in $P$,}\\
\max\rho_{M(k)}+1&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

(19)

Proof.

Suppose first that
k
is minimal in
P
. If
k=1
then
r_{1}=0
by definition of an RGF. If
k>1
then we must have
\max\rho_{j}\geq r_{k}
for all
j<k
. Letting
j=1
this becomes
0=\max\rho_{1}\geq r_{k}
which forces
r_{k}=0
.

Now assume that
k
is not minimal and let
m=M(k)
. Since
m<_{P}k
we have
\max\rho_{m}<r_{k}
. On the other hand,
m+1\nless_{P}r_{k}
so that
\max\rho_{m+1}\geq r_{k}
.
Using the previous inequalities and equation (
15
) gives
\max\rho_{m}<r_{k}\leq\max\rho_{m}+1
. This implies
r_{k}=\max\rho_{m}+1
as desired.
∎

Now we defined a map
\rp^{-1}:\Av_{n}(P_{3})\rightarrow\RGF_{n}
by letting
\rp^{-1}(P)
be the sequence
\rho
defined by equation (
19
).
Again, we need to worry about whether the map is well defined.

Lemma 7.3
.

If
P\in\Av_{n}(P_{3})
then
\rho=\rp^{-1}(P)\in\RGF_{n}
.

Proof.

We claim that since
P
is factorial we must have that
1
is a minimal element. Suppose this were not the case. Then the set of elements below
1
would not form an inverval of the form
[m]
for some
m>2
since all such intervals contain
1
, which contradicts (
18
). But
1
being minimal means that
\rho_{1}=0
as needed for an RGF.

We now prove that (r2) in the definition of an RGF holds. If
k
is minimal then
r_{k}=0
and the inequality is trivial. If
k
is not minimal then let
m=M(k)
. Since
P
is factorial we have
m\leq k-1
.
It follows that
\max\rho_{m}\leq\max\rho_{k-1}
. Combining this with (
19
) gives

r_{k}=\max\rho_{m}+1\leq\max\rho_{k-1}+1

which is what we wished to prove.
∎

We can now show that the maps
\rp
and
\rp^{-1}
are indeed inverses. We compose functions from right to left.

Theorem 7.4
.

The map
\rp:\RGF_{n}\rightarrow\Av_{n}(P_{3})
is a bijection with inverse
\rp^{-1}
.
Thus

\#\Av_{n}(P_{3})=B_{n}.

Proof.

By (
16
), the cardinality result will follow once we know that
\rp
is a bijection.

Since equation (
19
) was shown to hold for
\rp
and then used to define
\rp^{-1}
, we automatically have that
\rp^{-1}\circ\rp
is the identity map. For the other composition,
consider
P\in\Av_{n}(P_{3})
and let
\rho=\rp^{-1}(P)
and
P^{\prime}=\rp(\rho)
. By induction on
n
we can assume that
P
equals
P^{\prime}
when both are restricted to
[n-1]
. There are now two cases depending on the element
n
. Note that since
P
and
P^{\prime}
are both naturally labeled, it suffices to consider the elements below
n
in the two posets.

Suppose first that
n
is minimal in
P
. By the definition of
\rp^{-1}
we have
r_{n}=0
. But then there is no
i
with
\max\rho_{i}<r_{n}
. So
n
is also minimal in
P^{\prime}
and we are done.

If
n
is not minimal in
P
then let
m=M(n)
computed in
P
. We need to show that
m
is the element of maximum label below
n
in
P^{\prime}
.
First note that by (
19
) we have

r_{n}=\max\rho_{m}+1.

So, in particular,
\max\rho_{m}<r_{n}
and the definition of
\rp
implies
m<_{P^{\prime}}n
. Thus we will be done if we can prove that
m+1\nless_{P^{\prime}}n
.
If, instead, we have
m+1<_{P^{\prime}}n
then this is equivalent to
\max\rho_{m+1}<r_{n}
. Combining this with the previous displayed equation gives

\max\rho_{m}\leq\max\rho_{m+1}<r_{n}=\max\rho_{m}+1.

It follows that

\max\rho_{m+1}=\max\rho_{m}.

Now recall that
P
has no special
P_{3}
. But
m<_{P}n
and
m+1\nless_{P}n
so we must have
m+1>_{P}m
otherwise
m
,
m+1
, and
n
form a special
P_{3}
. And since
m,m+1
are consecutive integers this forces
m=M(m+1)
computed in
P
. Now (
19
) implies
r_{m+1}=\max\rho_{m}+1
. This in turn yields
\max\rho_{m+1}=\max\rho_{m}+1
which directly contradicts the previous displayed equation.
∎

We will now make a connection between RGFs and pattern avoidance.
Claesson
[
Cla01
]
showed that the number of permutations in
S_{n}
avoiding any of the following vincular patterns is the Bell number,
B_{n}
:

1|23,12|3,1|32,21|3,23|1,3|12,3|21,32|1

These are trivially Wilf equivalent to the bivincular patterns

\overline{1}23,1\overline{2}3,\overline{1}32,\overline{2}13,23\overline{1},31\overline{2},3\overline{2}1,32\overline{1}.

We will translate a version of one of his bijections from set partitions into the language of RGFs. Given
\rho=r_{1}r_{2}\ldots r_{n}\in\RGF_{n}
we form a permutation
\pi=p_{1}p_{2}\ldots p_{n}\in S_{n}
as follows.
Suppose the indices
for which
r_{i}=0
are

1=i_{1}<i_{2}<\ldots<i_{k}.

(20)

Then
\pi
will start with the initial segment

\pi_{k}=i_{k}i_{k-1}\ldots i_{1}.

Now let the indices
for which
r_{j}=1
be

j_{1}<j_{2}<\ldots<j_{l}.

(21)

Continue building
\pi
so that

\pi_{k+l}=\pi_{k}j_{l}j_{l-1}\ldots j_{1}.

The rest of
\pi
is built
in a similar manner.

Proposition 7.5

(
[
Cla01
]
)
.

The map
\rho\mapsto\pi
is a bijection
\RGF_{n}\rightarrow S_{n}(23|1)
. ∎

There is also a standard way to associate with any RGF a strictly upper-triangular,

with rows and columns indexed by

. Start with

being the zero matrix. Now consider the indices in (

) and for each adjacent pair

set

giving

nonzero entries. Continue with the sequence (

Proposition 7.6
.

The map

is a bijection between

and

Comments and open questions

8.1
More on matrices

Notice that Theorem
2.1
only applies for
d\geq 1
. But a similar result holds for
d=0
and the set of ascent sequences
\A_{n}
. Specifically, factor an ascent sequence
\alpha=\delta_{0}\delta_{1}\ldots\delta_{m}
by breaking at every ascent. So the
\delta_{i}
are maximal weakly decreasing factors of
\alpha
. Now use (
4
) to construct a matrix
\mx(\alpha)
. Note that since the
\delta_{i}
weakly decrease, it is possible to add the same
E_{i,j}
multiple times so that the entries of the final matrix can be larger than one. In fact, it is not hard to see that the image of the map is the set
\Mtx_{n}
of all matrices
M
who entries sum to
n
and have the following properties.

(Ma)

M
is upper triangular with nonnegative integer entries.

(Mb)

There are no zero columns in
M
, and for all column indicies
j

\rmax c_{j}>\rmin c_{j-1}.

Thus, we have the following result

Theorem 8.1
.

For
n\geq 0
the map
\mx:\A_{n}\rightarrow\Mtx_{n}
is a bijection. Consequently

{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}\qed}\hfill{\displaystyle\#\A_{n}=\#\Mtx_{n}}\hfill\qed

Dukes and Parviainen
[
DP10
]
have shown that ascent sequences are in bijection with another set of upper triangular matrices. Let
\Mtx^{\prime}_{n}
be the set of all matrices
M
whose entries sum to
n
and which satisfy (Ma) and

(Mc)

There are no zero rows or columns in
M
.

Theorem 8.2

(
[
DP10
]
)
.

There is a bijection
\mx^{\prime}:\A_{n}\rightarrow\Mtx^{\prime}_{n}
. ∎

Problem 8.3
.

Find a direct bijection
\Mtx_{n}\rightarrow\Mtx^{\prime}_{n}
without composing

and

.

Does
