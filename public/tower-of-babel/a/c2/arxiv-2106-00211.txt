Evolution of the metallic state in LaNiO 3 /LaAlO 3 superlattices measured by 8 Li β \beta -detected NMR
Victoria L. Karner, Aris Chatzichristos, David L. Cortie, Derek Fujimoto, Robert F. Kiefl, C. D. Philip Levy, Ruohong Li, Ryan M. L. McFadden, Gerald D. Morris, Matthew R. Pearson, Eva Benckiser, Alexander V. Boris, Georg Cristiani, Gennady Logvenov, Bernhard Keimer, W. Andrew MacFarlane

Abstract
Using ion-implanted 8 Li β \beta -detected NMR, we study the evolution of the correlated metallic state of LaNiO 3 in a series of LaNiO 3 / LaAlO 3 superlattices as a function of bilayer thickness. Spin-lattice relaxation measurements in an applied field of 6.55 T reveal two equal amplitude components: one with metallic ( T T -linear) 1 / T 1 1/T_{1} , and a second with a more complex T T -dependence. The metallic character of the slow relaxing component is only weakly affected by the LaNiO 3 thickness, while the fast component is much more sensitive, exhibiting the opposite temperature dependence (increasing towards low T T ) in the thinnest, most magnetic samples. The origin of this bipartite relaxation is discussed.

Methods:
To measure the
\beta
-NMR, a spin-polarized radioactive
8
Li
+
ion beam was implanted into the sample. The measured
\beta
-decay asymmetry is proportional to the average longitudinal nuclear spin-polarization
Ackermann et al. 1983
;
MacFarlane 2015
, with a proportionality constant
A_{0}
depending on the detector geometry and decay properties.
The asymmetry was obtained by combining the count rates in two opposing scintillation detectors placed along the polarization axis.
The experiments were performed at the ISAC
facility at TRIUMF in Vancouver, Canada
MacFarlane 2015
.
The NMR isotope,
8
Li
has nuclear spin
I=

2
, gyromagnetic ratio
\gamma/2\pi=

6.3016\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}\text{\,}{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}
, nuclear electric quadrupole moment
Q=

+32.6\text{\,}\mathrm{mb}
, and radioactive lifetime
\tau=

1.21\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
. The implantation energy was
4.9\text{\,}\mathrm{keV}
, corresponding to a mean depth of
\sim 21\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
with a straggle of
11\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}
Ziegler et al. 2010
.

To measure the SLR, the asymmetry was monitored both during and after the
4\text{\,}\mathrm{s}
beam pulse, during which it approaches a dynamic steady-state, while afterwards it relaxes to near zero.
Unlike conventional NMR, the
8
Li
is hyperpolarized in-flight by optical pumping, so no radio frequency (RF) field is required.
The SLR rates were measured from 5 to 300 K in an applied field of
B_{0}=

6.55\text{\,}\mathrm{T}
normal to the surface.

Results:

Figure

The relaxation is easily measurable in all cases.
Generally, we expect it to be fastest in the (presumably most magnetic)

To quantify its evolution, a model relaxation function is required.
The simplest choice that provides a good fit is a biexponential, to which we add a non-relaxing term
to account for a small fraction of

Discussion:
Having outlined the results, we now discuss their implications.
Like the implanted muon in
\mu
SR,
8
Li
+
is typically located at a high symmetry interstitial site in the host lattice.
In perovskite oxides, this site (denoted
P
) is midway between adjacent
A
-site ions at the center of a square
B
O
plaquette
MacFarlane et al. 2003
;
Karner et al. 2018a
;
Karner et al. 2019
.
Analogous to the implanted muon and native NMR nuclei,
8
Li
is coupled via the hyperfine interaction to the electronic system of the host.
However, it is much longer lived than the muon, making it sensitive to phenomena on longer timescales such as Korringa relaxation.

With only one site, biexponential relaxation is unexpected.
In bulk
LaNiO
3
, where both rates are
T
-linear, we attributed it to two subtly different
P
-sites in an unconventional distorted perovskite structure
Karner et al. 2019
.
This was reasonable, since the rates differed only in magnitude, not temperature dependence.
However, it is incompatible with the present data where the two components exhibit distinct
T
dependences. With no evidence for other sites, we instead propose that the electronic state of
LaNiO
3
(even in a metallic single crystal) is
intrinsically inhomogeneous
, i.e., microscopically separated into two equally abundant phases with distinct electronic properties.

In the Kondo lattice compound
\text{YbRh}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{Si}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
,
\hphantom{{}^{\text{29}}_{\text{}}}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}{}^{\mathchoice{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\displaystyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{29}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\textstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{29}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{29}$}}{\hbox to0.0pt{\hss$\scriptscriptstyle\vphantom{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{29}$}}}\kern 0.0pt\text{Si}
NMR revealed a similar phase separation into distinct metallic states in the vicinity of the field induced quantum critical point
Kambe et al. 2014
. Interestingly, the phase fraction (and not the relaxation rate) evolved with magnetic field and temperature, but converged toward
f_{f}=

0.5
(
R=

1
in their notation) at the lowest temperatures.

LaNiO
3
has been suggested to have a Kondo lattice character with a much larger energy scale
Anisimov et al. 1999
.
The phase separation revealed in our data, persists at least up to
300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
, even in the single crystal; so, if it is analogous, we cannot expect its properties to be as purely electronic as the low-
T

\text{YbRh}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{Si}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, and lattice dynamics should play some role.

Phase separation between metallic and insulating phases is known in other
R
NiO
3
Mattoni et al. 2016
;
Preziosi et al. 2018
, but only near the MIT.
There, the insulating volume fraction varies from
01
through a narrow coexistence region around the transition.
In contrast, our volume fraction is constant over the whole temperature range, including in the metallic limit of the single crystal
Karner et al. 2019
.
If this phenomenon is related, then the metal-insulator phase separation must reflect some underlying inhomogeneity present in the high temperature metallic phase.
This is likely structural in origin, as suggested by its correlation with step-edges, and the stability of its microstructure through temperature cycling
Mattoni et al. 2016
.
Candidate sources of structural heterogeneity are competing distorted perovskite structures
(octahedral rotation pattern) or bond disproportionation (octahedral size)
Alonso et al. 1999
;
Catalano et al. 2018
.
We note the precise structure of
LaNiO
3
remains elusive. Although it is considered rhombohedral, recent neutron scattering results indicate a lower local symmetry
Li et al. 2015
;
Shamblin et al. 2018
.
It has also been suggested to contain nanoscopic monoclinic insulating pockets
Li et al. 2015
, which is inconsistent with the two distinct metallic phases we find in bulk
Karner et al. 2019
.

In summary, the biexponential relaxation strongly suggests
microscopic electronic phase separation
in
LaNiO
3
that persists up to at least
300\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
.
The phase separation is static on the second time scale and is robust across multiple
LaNiO
3
samples, including single crystal
Karner et al. 2019
, thin film
Karner et al. 2019
, and SLs.
Confirming it may require other local probes, such as conventional NMR, or scanning probe methods that could directly image its spatial distribution
Preziosi et al. 2018
.

In similar SLs, low energy
\mu
SR (in zero and low fields) finds a broad distribution of static fields (150 G width) below
50\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
at
n=

2
which pervade the entire sample volume
Boris et al. 2011
, seemingly inconsistent with our results.
However,
1/T_{1}
does not probe the static fields directly, instead being determined by field fluctuations at the Larmor frequency (
41.27\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
).
On the other hand, static fields could propagate out from an incomplete magnetic volume fraction into the nonmagnetic phase, provided the two were intimately mixed at the nanoscale.
This may explain why the scale of the internal fields is more than
10\times
smaller than bulk
R
NiO
3
Frandsen et al. 2016
.
Alternatively, in analogy with
\text{YbRh}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{Si}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}
, we may have stabilized a distinct phase-separated ground state with the large
B_{0}
field.

Now we turn to what the evolution of
1/T_{1}
can tell us about the MIT at small
n
.
In a conventional metal, the Korringa
T
-linear dependence is a consequence of Fermi statistics and a density of states (DOS) that is smooth and featureless on the scale of
kT
.
The Korringa slope is then proportional to the square of the DOS at the Fermi level
\rho(E_{F})
.
Considering
Figure

3
(a), there is a 2-fold increase in the slow component’s slope for
n<

4
, consistent with an effective narrowing of the conduction band on approaching the 2D limit.
However, for all
n
, we find a finite intercept, inconsistent with simple metallic behaviour.
More generally, for a correlated metal susceptible to magnetic order, the Moriya expression
Moriya 1963
relates
1/T_{1}
to the imaginary part of the generalized susceptibility
\chi(\boldsymbol{q},\omega)
,

\frac{1}{T_{1}T}=\frac{4k_{B}}{\hbar}\sum_{\boldsymbol{q}}\frac{\lvert A(\boldsymbol{q})\rvert^{2}}{\left(\gamma_{e}\hbar\right)^{2}}\frac{1}{\hbar\omega_{0}}\chi^{\prime\prime}\left(\boldsymbol{q},\omega_{0}\right),

(2)

where
\gamma_{e}
is the electron gyromagnetic ratio,
\omega_{0}
is the NMR frequency, and
A\left(\boldsymbol{q}\right)
is the hyperfine form factor which acts as a filtering function on magnetic fluctuations at wavevector
\boldsymbol{q}
.
For a conventional metal, the summation in
Equation

2
is independent of
T
for
kT\ll E_{F}
.
A finite intercept implies that, in addition to a constant part, the sum contains a term proportional to
1/T
.
This is seen clearly in the corresponding plot of
1/(T_{1}T)
(on a log scale) in
Figure

2
(c).
Such a Curie-like term suggests a population of dilute uncoupled local moments, as has been considered near the MIT in doped semiconductors
Gan and Lee 1986
.
Interestingly, this term appears independent of
n
, so it is probably not caused by
Ni
moments at the
LaAlO
3
interface.

Aside from the intercept, the slow relaxing phase exhibits a metallic character that is not very strongly modified from the bulk, even for the smallest
n
.
In contrast, the fast relaxing phase exhibits a much more dramatic sensitivity to thickness.
In the bulk, the factor of
4
between the Korringa slopes in the fast and slow phases
Karner et al. 2019
would imply a factor of
2
in
\rho(E_{F})
in an uncorrelated metal.
In the SLs, compared to the slow component,
(1/T_{1})_{f}
shows a
10\times
larger
T
-independent term (vertical shift) and an upturn below
\sim 50\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
with
(1/T_{1})_{f}
doubling between
50\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
and
4\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
at
n=2
(inset of
Figure

2
(d)).
Similar
n=

2
SLs have a zero field Néel transition at
T_{N}

\sim 50\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
Boris et al. 2011
;
Frano et al. 2013
near the upturn and an ordering wavevector
\boldsymbol{q}_{\mathrm{AF}}=(1/4,1/4,1/4)
Frano et al. 2013
. The
P

A(\boldsymbol{q}_{\mathrm{AF}})\neq 0
, so
8
Li
senses fluctuations at
\boldsymbol{q}_{\mathrm{AF}}
.
However, even the thicker SLs with
n=

410
(that are thought to remain nonmagnetic and metallic) show a similar, albeit muted feature.
The upturn implies a term in
Equation

2
increasing more steeply than
1/T
at low
T
.
This is likely related to magnetic ordering, but probably modified by the strong applied field (cf. the transition in
Mn
-doped
\text{Bi}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{2}}}\text{Te}{\vphantom{\text{X}}}_{\smash[t]{\text{3}}}

Note the vertical scale in
Figure

2

Figure

Finally, we return to the sublinearity in
(1/T_{1})_{s}
above
200\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
(vertical arrow in
Figure

2
). The deviation is subtle, sample dependent, and does not vary monotonically with
n
, as it is smallest at
n=4
.
A similar deviation was observed in bulk
LaNiO
3
, although there it had the opposite sense for the slow and fast components.
This feature is probably related to small changes in the
Ni
–
O
–
Ni
angle around
200\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
Li et al. 2015
which also coincides with a decrease in the carrier density from RF conductivity
Shamblin et al. 2018
.
In addition, the magnetic susceptibility
crosses over from a low temperature Pauli-like regime to a high temperature Curie-Weiss dependence
Zhang et al. 2017
at about
200\text{\,}\mathrm{K}
. It is reasonable to expect such a small structural change would differ between a crystal, SL, and film, and it may even depend on the precise thermal history.

Conclusion:

\beta
-NMR spin-lattice relaxation measurements of implanted
8
Li
in a series of
LaNiO
3
/
LaAlO
3
SLs reveal biexponential relaxation, consistent with single crystal
LaNiO
3
Karner et al. 2019
; but, in contrast, the two components exhibit very different temperature dependences.
Thus, we propose
LaNiO
3
is electronically phase separated.
The slow relaxing phase is metallic, not strongly modified from the bulk, and persists to low
T
, even for the thinnest (
n=

2
) SL.
As
n\rightarrow

2
, the slope and the DOS are enhanced consistent with band narrowing towards the 2D limit.
In contrast, the fast relaxing phase is much more sensitive to
n
and appears magnetic at low
T
.
