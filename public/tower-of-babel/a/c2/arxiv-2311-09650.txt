Levinson’s theorem for two-dimensional scattering systems: it was a surprise, it is now topological !
A. Alexander, D. T. Nguyen, A. Rennie, S. Richard

Abstract
We prove a general Levinson’s theorem for Schrödinger operators in two dimensions with threshold obstructions at zero energy. Our results confirm and simplify earlier seminal results of Bollé, Gesztesy et al., while providing an explicit topological interpretation. We also derive explicit formulas for the wave operators, and so show that they are elements of a C ∗ C^{*} -algebra introduced by Cordes. As a consequence of our approach, we provide an evaluation of the spectral shift function at zero in the presence of p p -resonances.

Introduction

Scattering theory for two-dimensional Schrödinger operators is a challenging subject
that has been the focus of many studies.
It is known that the
0
-energy behaviour of the underlying self-adjoint operator
is fairly complicated and plays a crucial role.
Namely, the possible coexistence of a
0
-energy bound state and of two types
of
0
-energy resonances has an impact on propagation properties of the evolution group
and on boundedness of the wave operators in various spaces.
A short review of the corresponding literature is provided below.

The intricate
0
-energy behaviour of the resolvent of two-dimensional
Schrödinger operators has also a tremendous impact on the so-called
Levinson’s theorem. It was shown in
[
7
]
, and later confirmed in

[
6
]
, that the presence of an
s
-resonance does not play any role in this context
while the presence of
p
-resonances leads to a contribution similar to bound states.
These properties are in sharp contrast from the one-dimensional or three-dimensional
situation, and for that reason it was announced as
a surprise

in the first of the two mentioned papers.
Unfortunately, the proofs of the results of

[
6
]
are based on double asymptotic expansions of the resolvent, which make
them strenuous to follow.

The aim of the present paper is threefold. We firstly confirm the results
obtained in
[
6
]
for Levinson’s theorem.
Secondly, we recast their proof in an updated framework
with more powerful tools. Thirdly, we restore the topological nature
of Levinson’s theorem by exhibiting it as an index theorem in scattering theory.
This general approach has been described in the review paper
[
35
]

and illustrated in several examples
[
2
,
16
,
17
,
24
,
25
,
26
,
27
,
28
,
29
,
32
,
34
,
36
]
. In doing so, we recover the analytic formula for the number of bound states stated in
[
6
, Thm. 6.3]
.

Before presenting our results in more detail, let us provide a brief (and non-exhaustive) description of the literature related to our work.
A decade after the two surprising papers
[
7
,
6
]
,
a renewed interest in the two-dimensional case has been triggered by
the work
[
47
]
and then
[
22
]
on the
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!p}
-boundedness of the wave operators. However,
these works were conducted under the assumption that
0
-energy bound states and

0
-energy resonances are absent (the so-called regular or generic case). The next
breakthrough came with the derivation in
[
21
]
of a simplified resolvent
expansion, no longer given as a two parameter expansion, but in terms of powers of a
single parameter. Subsequently, numerous works took advantage of this simplified
resolvent expansion, as for example
[
4
,
12
,
38
,
41
]
in which the
assumption of the absence
0
-energy bound states and
0
-energy resonances remains. In
other works, it was assumed that
0
-energy bound states and
p
-resonances are absent,
as for example in
[
44
]
, or that only the
p
-resonances are absent, as in

[
11
]
.
The first results on the behaviour of the Schrödinger evolution in the general case
appeared then in
[
13
]
. More recently, two-dimensional
Schrödinger operators with point interactions have been investigated: the boundedness of
the wave operators in
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!p}
-spaces in the regular case has been discussed in

[
9
]
, while a full picture has been provided in
[
48
]
.
Simultaneously, results on the scattering operator in the general setting
have been exhibited in
[
39
]
, together with an analysis of the wave operators
in the absence of
p
-resonance.
In particular, this paper contains the confirmation of the
0
-energy behaviour of the scattering matrix, namely
\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}S(\lambda)=1
, obtained in
[
6
]

based on the double asymptotic expansion of the resolvent.
Finally, building on
[
48
]
,
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!p}
-boundedness for more general Schrödinger operators with threshold obstructions has been fully investigated in
[
49
]
.

Our approach for obtaining Levinson’s theorem as an index theorem in scattering theory
is based on a detailed study of the wave operators. Let us recall their definition,
and refer to Section
2
for more details.
We consider the scattering system given by the pair of operators
(H,H_{0})
, where
H_{0}

is the Laplacian in the Hilbert space
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
and
H:=H_{0}+V
with
V
a real
potential decaying rapidly at infinity. Under quite general conditions on
V
it is
known that the wave operators

W_{\pm}:=\slim_{t\to\pm\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}

exist and are complete. In particular, they are Fredholm operators with no kernel
and with a cokernel given by the subspace spanned by the eigenfunctions
of
H
. This subspace is of finite dimension for sufficiently fast decaying potential.
Another important operator in this context is the scattering operator
defined by
S:=W_{+}^{*}W_{-}
. This operator is unitary in
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
.
Since
S
strongly commutes with
H_{0}
, the operator
S

decomposes in the spectral representation of
H_{0}
.
Thus, if we denote by
\mathscr{F}_{0}
the unitary map from
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
to
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})\big)
satisfying
\big(\mathscr{F}_{0}H_{0}f\big)(\lambda)=\lambda\big(\mathscr{F}_{0}f\big)(\lambda)
for any
f

in the domain of
H_{0}
, then one has

\mathscr{F}_{0}S\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}=\{S(\lambda)\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
, meaning that
S
is unitarily
equivalent to a family of unitary operators
\{S(\lambda)\}_{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}}
in

\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})
. For historical reasons, the operator
S(\lambda)
is called the
scattering matrix at energy
\lambda
, even though it acts on an infinite-dimensional
Hilbert space
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})=:\mathfrak{h}
.

By using the stationary representation of the wave operators, our first result is a new formula for the wave operator
W_{-}
. More precisely, for
V
decaying fast enough, we show that the following equality holds:

\mathscr{F}_{0}\big(W_{-}-1\big)\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}=\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi A_{+})\big)\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}\big)\big(S(L)-1\big)-N_{2}\;\!\Xi\;\!B+K,

where
A_{+}
corresponds to the generator of the dilation group in
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,

S(L)
denotes the operator of multiplication by the function
\lambda\mapsto S(\lambda)
,
and
K
is a compact operator. So far, the product
N_{2}\;\!\Xi\;\!B
of three bounded
operators is not really meaningful, but let us stress that this term is non-compact whenever

H
admits one or two
p
-resonances at
0
. Note that this formula for
W_{-}
is at the root of the topological version of Levinson’s theorem, and similar formulas have been obtained in several contexts, see for example
[
5
,
15
,
18
,
19
,
31
,
33
,
37
,
42
]
.

In order to get a better understanding of the new term
N_{2}\;\!\Xi\;\!B
,
a new representation is better suited. By conjugation with a suitable unitary rescaling,
the wave operator
W_{-}
can be realised on
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}\big(\mathbb{R};\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})\big)
, and by the
decomposition into even and odd functions on
\mathbb{R}
, we end up studying the
wave operator in the Hilbert space
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}\big(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})\big)^{2}
.
In this representation, the operator
W_{-}
takes the form

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\tanh\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{-\Delta_{\rm N}}\big)\phi(A_{+})\\
\overline{\phi}(A_{+})\tanh\big(\tfrac{\pi}{2}\sqrt{-\Delta_{\rm N}}\big)&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{S}_{\rm e}(L)-1&\tilde{S}_{\rm o}(L)\\
\tilde{S}_{\rm o}(L)&\tilde{S}_{\rm e}(L)-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)\\
&\qquad+\left(\begin{smallmatrix}(\tilde{N}_{2})_{\rm e}(L)&(\tilde{N}_{2})_{\rm o}(L)\\
(\tilde{N}_{2})_{\rm o}(L)&(\tilde{N}_{2})_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\frac{2}{1+i2A_{+}}&0\\
0&\frac{2}{1+i2A_{+}}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}\tilde{B}_{\rm e}(L)&\tilde{B}_{\rm o}(L)\\
\tilde{B}_{\rm o}(L)&\tilde{B}_{\rm e}(L)\end{smallmatrix}\right)+K,\end{split}

(1.1)

with
\phi(A_{+}):=-\tanh(\pi A_{+})+i\cosh(\pi A_{+})^{-1}
,
the indices
{\rm e}
and
{\rm o}
for the even or odd part of a function defined on
\mathbb{R}
,
and the tilde functions meaning a rescaling, as for example
\tilde{S}(x):=S\big(\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2x}\big)

for any
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Again, the operator
K
is a compact operator.
Let us emphasise some of the specific features of the previous formulas. It involves functions of three natural operators acting on
\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, namely the Neumann Laplacian
-\Delta_{\rm N}
, the operator
L
of multiplication by the variable, and the generator
A_{+}
of the unitary dilation group. In addition, it is shown in the following sections that all functions involved in this
expression are continuous functions having limits either at
\pm\infty
, or at
0
and
+\infty
.

Obtaining formula (
1.1
) involves purely analytical tools, starting from the asymptotic expansion of the resolvent provided by
[
21
]
, and using various analytical tricks for
studying the stationary formula for the wave operators.
Note that some of these tricks have been suggested by
[
49
]
, even if the
aims and the methods are different.
These investigations are presented in Section
3
and in the first half of
Section
4
. The next key observation is that a
C^{*}
-algebra
\mathscr{E}
generated
by functions of the three generators mentioned above has been thoroughly studied
in
[
8
, Chap. 5]
. In particular, a precise description of
the quotient of this algebra by the set of compact operators is provided:
the quotient consists of continuous functions defined on the edges of a hexagon
(this hexagon is illustrated in Section
5
).
By considering
M_{2}(\mathscr{E})
, the set of
2\times 2
matrices with values in
\mathscr{E}
,
enlarging this algebra by a tensor product with
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})\big)
, and adding a unit,
one ends up with a
C^{*}
-algebra in which the expression (
1.1
) for the wave operator is natural.

Once in this framework, the rest of the investigation is more algebraic, and is presented
in the second half of Section
4
and in Section
5
.
It firstly consists in computing the image of (
1.1
) in the quotient algebra.
Since
W_{-}
is a Fredholm operator, this image is given by a continuous function
\Gamma

defined on the edges of the hexagon and taking unitary values in
\mathbb{C}+M_{2}\big(\mathscr{K}(\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S}))\big)
. This function is provided in Proposition
4.4
and in Lemma
4.6
.

The operators
\tilde{N}_{2}
and
\tilde{B}
take
a much more explicit and interesting form in the quotient algebra: together they are the image of a
projection
P_{p}
which is directly linked with the
p
-resonance of
H
.
Secondly, using a
K
-theoretic argument, the function
\Gamma
can be linked
to the projection
E_{\rm p}(H)
on the subspace spanned by the eigenvectors of
H
.
This construction is presented in Section
5
and is based on a description of the
index map borrowed from
[
40
, Prop. 9.2.4.(ii)]
. Thirdly, by applying traces, one infers
a numerical equality. This equality involves the index of a Fredholm operator
W_{S}
defined by

W_{S}-1=\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi A_{+})\big)\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}\big)\big(S(L)-1\big),

and the operator trace of the bound state projection
E_{\rm p}(H)
. It results in the following equality:

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})+\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})=-\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H).

Note that the dimension of
P_{p}
corresponds to
the number of
p
-resonances, and is
1
or
2
.
By taking care of the high energy behaviour of the scattering matrix, one finally deduces
the relation

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})=-\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H).

(1.2)

Simultaneously, we also determine the value of the spectral shift function at zero in the presence of
p
-resonances.
We refer to Section
5
for more precise statements.

The equality (
1.2
)
confirms that each
p
-resonance provides a contribution of
1
to Levinson’s theorem, while the
s
-resonance does not provide any contribution. For comparison, let us recall the version of
Levinson’s theorem obtained in
[
6
, Thm. 6.3]
under the
assumption of exponential decay of the potential and the condition

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x\neq 0
.
In the framework of
[
6
]
, Levinson’s theorem is expressed as

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\big((H-\lambda-i0)^{-1}-(H_{0}-\lambda-i0)^{-1}\big)\;\!\mathrm{d}\lambda=-\pi N_{-}+\pi\;\!\Delta_{-1,-1}-\tfrac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\;\!\mathrm{d}x,

(1.3)

where
N_{-}
is the number of strictly negative eigenvalues of
H
and

-\Delta_{-1,-1}
is equal to the number of
0
-energy eigenvalues and
p
-resonances.
Taking into account the formal identity
[
6
, Eq. (6.45)]
:

\mathop{\mathrm{Im}}\nolimits\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big((H-\lambda-i0)^{-1}-(H_{0}-\lambda-i0)^{-1}\big)=-\tfrac{i}{2}\tfrac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(\ln(S(\lambda))\big),

it follows that (
1.3
) corresponds to (
1.2
).

Let us finally mention one main difference between (
1.2
) and (
1.3
):
the contribution of the
p
-resonance is not on the same side of the equality, and the same remark
holds for the expression involving the integral of
V
. Our r.h.s. term contains only (minus) the trace of
E_{\rm p}(H)
, which corresponds to the Fredholm index of
W_{-}
.
In our approach, the contribution of the
p
-resonance projection
P_{p}
is coming from the function
\Gamma

mentioned above, which describes the image of the wave operator
W_{-}
under the quotient map.
In that respect, the
p
-resonance data has to stay on the same side as the scattering operator, which
is also coming from
\Gamma
. On the other hand, the term involving the integral of
V

is due to a regularization process for the computation of
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})
.
For that reason, it also has to stay on the l.h.s. of the equality (
1.2
).
Altogether, these contribution coming from scattering theory
are equal to the contribution due to index theory, namely (minus) the trace on
E_{\rm p}(H)
.
Even though
\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})
is an integer, moving it to the other side of the equality sign would remove
the topological character of this equality.
As said in the title: it was a surprise, it is now topological !

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly recall some notations and preliminary results
introduced in
[
39
, Sec. 2]
.

2.1
Free operator

Set
\mathfrak{h}:=\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{S})
and
\mathscr{H}:=\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathfrak{h})
, and let
H_{0}
be the (positive)
self-adjoint operator in
\mathcal{H}=\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})
given by minus the Laplacian
-\Delta
on

\mathbb{R}^{2}
. Then, the unitary operator
\mathscr{F}_{0}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathscr{H}
defined by

\big((\mathscr{F}_{0}f)(\lambda)\big)(\omega)=2^{-1/2}(\mathscr{F}f)(\sqrt{\lambda}\;\!\omega),\quad f\in\mathscr{S},~\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~\omega\in\mathbb{S},

(2.1)

is a spectral transformation for
H_{0}
in the sense that

\big(\mathscr{F}_{0}H_{0}f\big)(\lambda)=\lambda\;\!\big(\mathscr{F}_{0}f\big)(\lambda)=(L\mathscr{F}_{0}f)(\lambda),\quad\hbox{$f\in\mathcal{H}^{2}$, a.e. $\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}$,}

with
L
the maximal multiplication operator by the variable
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
in

\mathscr{H}
. Moreover, for each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the operator
\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda):\mathscr{S}\to\mathfrak{h}

given by
\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)f:=(\mathscr{F}_{0}f)(\lambda)
extends to an element of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{s}_{t},\mathfrak{h})

for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>1/2
, and the function

\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni\lambda\mapsto\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{s}_{t},\mathfrak{h})
is continuous.

The asymptotic expansion of
\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)
as
\lambda\searrow 0
plays an
important role. By expanding the exponential

\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-i\sqrt{\lambda}\omega\cdot x}
in a Taylor series, one gets

\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)=\gamma_{0}+\sqrt{\lambda}\;\!\gamma_{1}+\lambda\gamma_{2}+o(\lambda),\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

(2.2)

with
\gamma_{j}:\mathscr{S}\to\mathfrak{h}
(
j=0,1,2
) the operator given by

(\gamma_{j}f)(\omega):=\tfrac{(-i)^{j}}{2^{3/2}\pi\;\!(j!)}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x\,(\omega\cdot x)^{j}\;\!f(x),\quad f\in\mathscr{S},~\omega\in\mathbb{S}.

The operator
\gamma_{j}
extends to an element of
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{s}_{t},\mathfrak{h})
for any

s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>j+1
, which implies that the expansion (
2.2
) holds in

\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H}^{s}_{t},\mathfrak{h})
as
\lambda\searrow 0
for any
s\in\mathbb{R}
and
t>3
. We shall sometimes
use the abbreviated notation
\gamma_{2}(\lambda)
, or
O(\lambda)
, for the sum

\lambda\gamma_{2}+o(\lambda)
in (
2.2
).

2.2
Perturbed operator

Let us now consider a potential
V\in\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{2};\mathbb{R})
satisfying for some
\rho>1
the
bound

|V(x)|\leq{\rm Const.}\;\!\langle x\rangle^{-\rho},\quad\hbox{a.e. $x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}$.}

(2.3)

Then, the perturbed Hamiltonian
H:=H_{0}+V
is a short range perturbation of
H_{0}
, and
it is known that the corresponding wave operators

W_{\pm}:=\slim_{t\to\pm\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{itH}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-itH_{0}}

(2.4)

exist and are complete. As a consequence, the scattering operator
S:=W_{+}^{*}W_{-}
is
unitary in
\mathcal{H}
. Now, define for
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
the resolvents of
H_{0}
and
H

R_{0}(z):=(H_{0}-z)^{-1}\quad\hbox{and}\quad R(z):=(H-z)^{-1}.

In order to recall properties of
R_{0}(z)
and
R(z)
as
z
approaches the real axis,
it is convenient to decompose the potential
V
according to the following rule: for
a.e.
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
set

v(x):=|V(x)|^{1/2}\quad\hbox{and}\quad u(x):=\begin{cases}+1&\hbox{if $V(x)\geq 0$}\\
-1&\hbox{if $V(x)<0$,}\end{cases}

(2.5)

so that
u
is self-adjoint and unitary and
V=uv^{2}
. Then, using the fact that
H

does not have any positive eigenvalues
[
23
, Sec. 1]
and that a limiting absorption
principle holds for
H_{0}
and
H

[
1
, Thm. 4.2]
, we infer that the limits

vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)v\quad\hbox{and}\quad vR(\lambda\pm i0)v:=\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}vR(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)v,

exist in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
and are continuous in the variable
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. This, together
with the relation

u-uvR(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)vu=\big(u+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i\varepsilon)v\big)^{-1},\quad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+},~\varepsilon>0,

implies the existence and the continuity of the function

\mathbb{R}_{+}\ni\lambda\mapsto(u+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v)^{-1}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
. Furthermore, one has

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}(u+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v)^{-1}=u
in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
, since

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}vR_{0}(\lambda+i0)v=0
in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})

[
46
, Prop. 7.1.2]
. On
the other hand, the existence in
\mathscr{B}(\mathcal{H})
of the limits

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}(u+vR_{0}(\lambda\pm i0)v)^{-1}
depends on the presence or
absence of eigenvalues or resonances at
0
-energy. This problem has been studied in
detail in
[
21
]
in dimensions
1
and
2
. We recall here the main result in
dimension
2

[
21
, Thm. 6.2(ii)]
: Take

3

W_{-}

We then decompose this expression into smaller pieces, which will be analysed separately.
This analysis is taking place in the spectral representation of

Since the subsequent developments are based on the asymptotic expansions

\displaystyle\big\langle\mathscr{F}_{0}\big(W_{-}-1\big)\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}\varphi,\psi\big\rangle_{\mathscr{H}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\mu\,\big\langle\mathscr{F}_{0}(\mu)v{\mathbf{1}}\big(u+vR_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}\delta_{\varepsilon}(L-\lambda)\varphi,(\mu-\lambda+i\varepsilon)^{-1}\psi(\mu)\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{h}},

Note that we have artificially inserted an identity operator

in the above expression.

{\mathbf{1}}=S_{2}+S_{2}^{\bot},

which then provides two expressions from (
3

0

[
39
]

We shall now carefully decouple the low energy and the high energy parts of this expression.
For that purpose, let us fix
\varrho\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};[0,1])

\varrho(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\lambda<\frac{1}{4}\\
1&\lambda>\frac{3}{4}.\end{cases}

We shall also use two auxiliary functions
\varrho_{1}\in C(\mathbb{R}_{+};[0,1])

\varrho_{1}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}0&\lambda<\frac{1}{8}\\
1&\lambda>\frac{1}{4}\end{cases}\quad\mbox{and}\quad\varrho_{0}(\lambda)=\begin{cases}1&\lambda<\frac{3}{4}\\
0&\lambda>\frac{7}{8}.\end{cases}

As a consequence,
\varrho\varrho_{1}=\varrho
and that
\varrho^{\bot}\varrho_{0}=\varrho^{\bot}
.
Finally, for
\varphi\in\mathscr{H}
and
\varepsilon>0

S_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\mu\,\big\langle\mathscr{F}_{0}(\mu)vS_{2}(\mu-\lambda-i\varepsilon)^{-1}S_{2}\big(u+vR_{0}(\lambda+i\varepsilon)v\big)^{-1}v\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}\delta_{\varepsilon}(L-\lambda)\varrho(L)\varphi,\psi(\mu)\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{h}}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\mu\,\big\langle\mathscr{F}_{0}(\mu)vS_{2}\varrho^{\bot}(\mu)\frac{1}{\sqrt{\mu}}\big(\sqrt{\mu}-\sqrt{\lambda}+\sqrt{\lambda}\big)(\mu-\lambda-i\varepsilon)^{-1}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\psi(\mu)\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{h}}

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\lambda\,\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathrm{d}\mu\,\big\langle\mathscr{F}_{0}(\mu)vS_{2}\varrho(\mu)\frac{1}{\mu}\big(\mu-\lambda+\lambda\big)(\mu-\lambda-i\varepsilon)^{-1}\tilde{\varphi}_{\varepsilon}(\lambda),\psi(\mu)\big\rangle_{\mathfrak{h}}

5
Topological Levinson’s theorem

In this section, we briefly recall the
C^{*}
-algebraic framework leading
to a topological version of Levinson’s theorem, and show that our current
investigations fit into this framework.
We refer to the survey paper
[
35
]
for additional information on this program
and for the presentation of several examples.

It has already been shown that the unital

-algebra

\big(M_{2}(\mathscr{E})\otimes\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+}
plays the important role of containing

In addition, this algebra contains the ideal

\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\Gamma_{4}(s)\big)
for
s\in[+\infty,-\infty]
. However, based on the content
of Lemma
4.6
and since
P_{p}
is a finite dimensional projection,
this computation is easy. By using again Lemma
5.2
one infers that

\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\Gamma_{4}(s)\big)=\Big(\frac{i2s-1}{i2s+1}\Big)^{\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})}.

(5.2)

Before the explicit computation of the winding number of the pointwise determinant,
it is useful to divide the computation of the Fredholm index of
W_{-}
into two contributions.
For that purpose, we define the operator
W_{S}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
by the equality

W_{S}-1:=\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi A_{+})\big)\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}\big)\big(S(L)-1\big).

(5.3)

We then directly obtain its main properties:

Lemma 5.4
.

The operator
W_{S}
is a Fredholm operator.

Proof.

It is sufficient to observe that the operator
W_{S^{*}}
defines an inverse for
W_{S}
,
up to compact operators. Indeed, this can be easily checked by firstly recalling that

[\vartheta(A_{+})\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}},S(L)]\in\mathscr{K}\big(\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathfrak{h})\big)
,
with
\vartheta
defined in (
3.4
).
In addition, since

S(0)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}S(\infty)=1
and since
\vartheta-\vartheta^{2}
vanishes at
\pm\infty
, operators
of the form
\big(\vartheta(A_{+})\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}-\vartheta^{2}(A_{+})\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}\big)\big(S(L)-1\big)

or
\big(\vartheta(A_{+})\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}-\vartheta^{2}(A_{+})\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}\big)\big(S^{*}(L)-1\big)
belong
to
\mathscr{K}\big(\mathsf{L}^{\>\!\!2}(\mathbb{R}_{+};\mathfrak{h})\big)
.
∎

For a Freholm operator
W
, let us denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W)
its Fredholm index. Then the following
statement holds:

Proposition 5.5
.

If
V
satisfies (
2.3
) with
\rho>11
, then the following equality holds:

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})+\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})=-\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H).

(5.4)

Proof.

Let
W_{S}
be as in (
5.3
) and define
W_{p}:=1-N_{2}\Theta B
.
It follows from (
4.1
) that

\mathscr{F}_{0}W_{-}\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}=W_{S}+(W_{p}-1)+K

for a compact operator
K
.
By construction, the operators
\mathscr{V}\mathscr{U}W_{S}\mathscr{U}^{*}\mathscr{V}^{*}

and
\mathscr{V}\mathscr{U}(W_{p}-1)\mathscr{U}^{*}\mathscr{V}^{*}

belong to
\big(M_{2}(\mathscr{E})\otimes\mathscr{K}(\mathfrak{h})\big)^{+}
.
For
j\in\{1,2,3,4,5,6\}
let
\Gamma_{S,j}
and
\Gamma_{p,j}
denote the components of the images
q(\mathscr{V}\mathscr{U}W_{S}\mathscr{U}^{*}\mathscr{V}^{*})
and
q(\mathscr{V}\mathscr{U}(W_{p}-1)\mathscr{U}^{*}\mathscr{V}^{*})
in the quotient algebra. Then a proof similar to Proposition
4.4
and Lemma
4.6
leads to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,1}(s):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(S(1)-1\big)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&\phi(s)\\
\overline{\phi}(s)&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle s\in[-\infty,+\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,2}(\ell):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(S\big(\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{2\ell}\big)-1\big)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&-1\\
-1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle\ell\in[0,+\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,3}(\xi):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle\xi\in[+\infty,0],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,4}(s):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle s\in[+\infty,-\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,5}(\xi):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle\xi\in[0,+\infty],

\displaystyle\Gamma_{S,6}(\ell):=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\right)+\tfrac{1}{2}\big(S\big(\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\ell}\big)-1\big)\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle\ell\in[+\infty,0],

and to

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p,j}:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle j\in\{1,2,3,5,6\},

\displaystyle\Gamma_{p,4}(s):=-\tfrac{1}{2}\frac{2}{1+i2s}P_{p}\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
1&1\end{smallmatrix}\right),

\displaystyle s\in[+\infty,-\infty].

Explicit computation shows that
\Gamma_{S,j}^{*}\Gamma_{p,j}=\Gamma_{p,j}=0
for
j\neq 4
and
\Gamma_{S,4}^{*}\Gamma_{p,4}=\Gamma_{p,4}
. Thus we find
q(\mathscr{V}\mathscr{U}(W_{S}^{*}(W_{p}-1)\mathscr{V}^{*}\mathscr{U}^{*})=q(\mathscr{V}\mathscr{U}(W_{p}-1)\mathscr{U}^{*}\mathscr{V}^{*})
. Since their image under the quotient map agrees, we have
1+W_{S}^{*}(W_{p}-1)=W_{p}+K
for some compact operator
K
.
Now we note the equalities

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}_{0}W_{-}\mathscr{F}_{0}^{*}

\displaystyle=W_{S}+(W_{p}-1)+K=W_{S}\big(1+W_{S}^{*}(W_{p}-1)\big)+K^{\prime},

for some compact operators
K
and
K^{\prime}
,
which lead to

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{-})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})+\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits\big(1+W_{S}^{*}(W_{p}-1)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})+\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{p}).

Clearly, one has
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{-})=-\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H)
.
On the other hand, the value
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{p})
can be computed with the winding number
of the poinwise determinant of
1+\Gamma_{p,4}
, as mentioned in Remark
5.1
.
More precisely, one has

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{p})=-\mathop{\mathrm{Wind}}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(1+\Gamma_{p,4})\big)=-\mathop{\mathrm{Wind}}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(\Gamma_{4})\big)

with
\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(\Gamma_{4})
provided in (
5.2
).
However, since
s\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\Gamma_{4}(s)\big)
has to be computed from
+\infty

to
-\infty
, and that on this path the increase of the argument is anti-clockwise, one gets

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{p})=\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})
, leading directly to the statement.
∎

Our next aim is to compute
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{-})
in terms of
\Gamma_{S}
, as introduced in
the proof of Proposition
5.5
.
Due to the high energy behaviour of the scattering matrix, some regularization is necessary to obtain an analytic formula. Indeed, even though the map
\lambda\mapsto S(\lambda)

converges to
1
in the norm on
\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
, the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)

does not converge to
1
. A more precise statement is provided in Lemma
5.9
.

For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
we define the self-adjoint operator
A(\lambda)
in
\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
by

\displaystyle A(\lambda)

\displaystyle=4\tan^{-1}(\lambda)\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)V\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

The main properties of this operator are gathered in the following statement.

Lemma 5.6
.

For each
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
the operator
A(\lambda)
is self-adjoint and trace class.

Proof.

Since
V
is real-valued, the self-adjointness property is clear.
Based on the definition of
\mathscr{F}_{0}
given in (
2.1
), we can write explicitly
the integral kernel of
A(\lambda)
as

\displaystyle A(\lambda,\omega,\omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=2\tan^{-1}(\lambda)(2\pi)^{-2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}e^{-i\sqrt{\lambda}(\omega-\omega^{\prime})\cdot x}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Integrating along the diagonal shows that the trace of
A(\lambda)
is

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\tan^{-1}(\lambda)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

(5.5)

The computation is justified by writing

4\tan^{-1}(\lambda)\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)vuv\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}

(5.6)

with
u,v
introduced in (
2.5
), and by observing
that (
5.6
) constains two factors which are Hilbert-Schmidt.
∎

By the properties of the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)
exhibited in
[
39
, Lem. 4.8]

one infers that the operator-valued map
\lambda\mapsto A(\lambda)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
is continuous and has norm limits

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}A(\lambda)=0
and
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}A(\lambda)=0
. As a consequence of (
5.5
), one also observes that the map

\lambda\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
and satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)=0
and

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\mathrm{d}x
.

Based on these observations, let us now define the unitary operator in
\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})

\beta(\lambda):=\exp\big(iA(\lambda)\big)

which clearly satisfies

\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(A(\lambda))}

for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. We also define the operator
W_{\beta}\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
by the equality

W_{\beta}-1=\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\big(1-\tanh(\pi A_{+})\big)\otimes 1_{\mathfrak{h}}\big)\big(\beta(L)-1\big).

Our main interest for this operator is related to the properties shown in the next statement.

Lemma 5.7
.

The operator
W_{\beta}
is a Fredholm operator satisfying
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{\beta})=0
.

Proof.

Observe firstly that we have the norm limits
\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\beta(\lambda)=1
and that

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\beta(\lambda)=1
.
Thus, by the same argument provided in the proof of Lemma
5.4
one gets that
the operator
W_{\beta^{*}}
defines an inverse for
W_{\beta}
, up to compact operators.
It directly follows that
W_{\beta}
is a Fredholm operator.

It remains to show that
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{\beta})=0
. To see this we consider, for fixed
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the map
[0,1]\ni t\mapsto A_{t}(\lambda)
with
A_{t}(\lambda)
defined by

\displaystyle A_{t}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=4\tan^{-1}((1-t)\lambda)\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)V\mathscr{F}_{0}(\lambda)^{*}.

The map
A_{t}(\lambda)
defines a norm continuous path in
\mathscr{B}(\mathfrak{h})
from
A(\lambda)
to
0
. Defining the path
A_{t}=A_{t}(L)\in\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})

we then obtain a norm continuous path in
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
from
A
to
0
. As a consequence,
\beta_{t}=\exp(iA_{t})
defines a norm continuous path of unitary operators in
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
from
\beta
to
1
. Hence the path
W_{\beta_{t}}
defines a norm continuous path in
\mathscr{B}(\mathscr{H})
from
W_{\beta}
to the identity, along which the Fredholm index is constant, and so equal to
0
.
∎

Lemma 5.8
.

The Fredholm operators
W_{S}
and
W_{\beta}
satisfy

\ W_{S}W_{\beta}-W_{S\beta}\in\mathscr{K}(\mathscr{H})\

and

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S\beta}).

Proof.

The equality
W_{S}W_{\beta}=W_{S\beta}
up to compact operators follows from one more commutator computation as provided in the proof of Lemmas
5.4
and
5.7
.
The index claim follows from the fact that
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{\beta})=0
and the composition rule for Fredholm index.
∎

The next statement shows that the operator
\beta
provides the correct regularization for the operator
S
, and consequently
W_{\beta}
will provide the correct regularization
to the operator
W_{S}
.
The proof is using some properties of the spectral shift function developed in
[
46
, Chap. 9]
.

Lemma 5.9
.

The map
\lambda\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)
satisfies

\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=1

and

\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=1.

Proof.

Let us firstly recall the Birman-Kreĭn formula linking the scattering operator and the spectral shift function, namely
\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\pi i\xi(\lambda)}
.
By
[
46
, Thm. 9.1.14]
there exists a continuous function
\xi_{2}
(the regularised spectral shift function) such that

\displaystyle\xi(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\xi_{2}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

with
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\xi_{2}(\lambda)=0
.
In addition, since
S(0)=1
, it follows that
\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}\xi(\lambda)\in\mathbb{Z}
. We can thus consider the function
\lambda\mapsto f(\lambda)
with

\displaystyle f(\lambda):=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\xi(\lambda)+i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\pi i\Big[\xi_{2}(\lambda)+\frac{1}{4\pi}\left(1-\frac{2}{\pi}\tan^{-1}(\lambda)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x\Big],

which satisfies
\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}f(\lambda)=0

and
\lim_{\lambda\searrow 0}f(\lambda)\in(-2\pi i)\mathbb{Z}
.
It finally remains to observe that

\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{-2\pi i\xi(\lambda)}\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits(A(\lambda))}=\mathop{\mathrm{e}}\nolimits^{f(\lambda)}

and so the map
\lambda\mapsto\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)

satisfies the properties stated.
∎

We finally recall from
[
46
, Eq. (9.1.22)]
that the spectral shift function
\xi
satisfies for
\lambda>0
the equality

\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)=-2\pi i\xi^{\prime}(\lambda),

(5.7)

with the differentiability of
\xi
being guaranteed by
[
46
, Thm. 9.1.18]
.
We can thus state the main result of this section.

Proposition 5.10
.

The following equality holds:

\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.

Proof.

By Lemma
5.8
one has
\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S})=\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S\beta})
.
Note that by
[
43
, Thm. 3.5(a)]
we have
\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(S\beta)=\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(S)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(\beta)
. By Lemma
5.9
,
\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(S\beta)
defines a loop, and using Gohberg-Kreĭn theory (cf.
[
14
]
and
[
30
, Thm. 4.9]
) we can compute the index of
W_{S\beta}
as

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{Index}}\nolimits(W_{S\beta})

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{Wind}}\nolimits\big(\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits(S\beta)\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\big[\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)\big]}{\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)\big)\mathop{\mathrm{det}}\nolimits\big(\beta(\lambda)\big)}\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\lambda}\Big(-2\pi i\xi(\lambda)+i\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\lambda)\big)\Big)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(-2\pi i\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)\right)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\frac{1}{2\pi}\left[\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(\infty)\big)-\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(A(0)\big)\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,

as claimed.
∎

By collecting the content of Proposition
5.5
and of Proposition
5.10
,
we can now confirm the statement of
[
6
, Thm. 6.3]
, namely:

Theorem 5.11
.

If
V
satisfies (
2.3
) with
\rho>11
, then the following equality holds:

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x+\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p})=-\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H).

We can then complement the content of
[
46
, Thm. 9.1.14]
about the spectral shift function.

Corollary 5.12
.

If
V
satisfies (
2.3
) with
\rho>11
, then the spectral shift function for the pair
(H,H_{0})
satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\xi(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=-\#\sigma_{\rm p}(H)-\mathop{\mathrm{dim}}\nolimits(P_{p}).

Proof.

By
[
46
, Thm. 9.1.14]
we have

\displaystyle\xi(\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}.

By taking the equality (
5.7
) into account, we observe that

\displaystyle\lim_{\varepsilon\searrow 0}{\xi(\varepsilon)}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}{\xi^{\prime}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}+\xi(\infty)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\mathop{\mathrm{tr}}\nolimits\big(S(\lambda)^{*}S^{\prime}(\lambda)\big)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}+\frac{1}{4\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}}{V(x)\,\mathrm{d}x}.

The result now follows from Theorem
5.11
.
∎

Lemma 5.13

Let
\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}:=\frac{1}{ck^{2}}\Big[\big(c||\psi||^{4}+|\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle|^{2}\big)

\displaystyle|\varphi\rangle\langle\varphi|-\big(c||\psi||^{2}\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle+||\varphi||^{2}\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\big)|\varphi\rangle\langle\psi|

\displaystyle-

,
\langle\psi,T^{\dagger}\psi\rangle=\overline{c}

, and

\langle\psi,T^{\dagger}\varphi\rangle=0

We first observe that

T\varphi=||\varphi||^{2}\varphi+c\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\psi\qquad T\psi=\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\varphi+c||\psi||^{2}\psi\ .

We can also compute

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}\varphi=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{ck^{2}}\Bigl[\Bigl(\big(c||\psi||^{4}+|\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle|^{2}\big)||\varphi||^{2}-\big(c||\psi||^{2}\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle+||\varphi||^{2}\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\big)\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\Bigr)\varphi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{ck^{2}}\Bigl[c||\psi||^{2}\big(||\psi||^{2}||\varphi||^{2}-|\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle|^{2}\big)\varphi+c\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\big(|\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle|^{2}-||\psi||^{2}||\varphi||^{2}\big)\psi\Bigr]

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\bigl[||\psi||^{2}\varphi-\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\psi\bigr],

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}\psi=

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{ck^{2}}\Bigl[\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\big(|\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle|^{2}-||\psi||^{2}||\varphi||^{2}\big)\varphi+||\varphi||^{2}\big(||\psi||^{2}||\varphi||^{2}-|\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle|^{2}\big)\psi\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{ck}\big[-\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\varphi+||\varphi||^{2}\psi\big].

From these we obtain that

\displaystyle T^{\dagger}T\varphi=

\displaystyle||\varphi||^{2}T^{\dagger}\varphi+c\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle T^{\dagger}\psi

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{||\varphi||^{2}}{k}\bigl[||\psi||^{2}\varphi-\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\psi\bigr]+\frac{c\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle}{ck}\big[-\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\varphi+||\varphi||^{2}\psi\big]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi,

and

\displaystyle TT^{\dagger}\varphi=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\bigl[||\psi||^{2}T\varphi-\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle T\psi\bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{k}\Bigl[||\psi||^{2}\big(||\varphi||^{2}\varphi+c\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\psi\big)-\langle\psi,\varphi\rangle\big(\langle\varphi,\psi\rangle\varphi+c||\psi||^{2}\psi\ \big)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\varphi.

By a similar computation, one also gets
T^{\dagger}T\psi=\psi
and

.
The remaining equalities can also be obtained straightforwardly.
