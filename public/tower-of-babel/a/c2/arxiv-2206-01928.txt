Diffusion Approximation for Multi-Scale McKean-Vlasov SDEs Through Different Methods
Wei Hong, Shihu Li, Xiaobin Sun

Abstract
. In this paper, we aim to study the diffusion approximation for multi-scale McKean-Vlasov stochastic differential equations. More precisely, we prove the weak convergence of slow process X ε X^{\varepsilon} in C ⁡ ( [ 0 , T ] , ℝ n ) C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n}) towards the limiting process X X that is the solution of a distribution dependent stochastic differential equation in which some new drift and diffusion terms compared to the original equation appear. The main contribution is to use two different methods to explicitly characterize the limiting equations respectively. The obtained diffusion coefficients in the limiting equations have different form through these two methods, however it will be asserted that they are essential the same by a comparison.

1.
Introduction

The diffusion approximation problem mainly concerns the convergence of singularly perturbed ordinary differential equations with random inputs, which involves a homogenization term, tends to stochastic differential equations (SDEs for short). To our knowledge, the diffusion approximation for multi-scale stochastic differential equations was first studied by Papanicolaou, Stroock and Varadhan
[
30
]
on a compact state space.
A simple example is the following

(1.1)

\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},X^{\varepsilon}_{t}/\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}\right)dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}K\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},X^{\varepsilon}_{t}/\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}\right)dt+\sigma\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},X^{\varepsilon}_{t}/\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}\right)dW_{t},\penalty\ X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},

where the small enough parameter
\varepsilon>0
, and
\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}K\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},X^{\varepsilon}_{t}/\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}\right)
is the homogenization term which has its own interest in the theory of partial differential equations (cf.
[
16
,
17
]
) and is also very useful in many physical systems (cf.
[
27
,
29
,
33
]
).
Denote
\mu^{{\varepsilon}}
by the distribution of solution
X^{\varepsilon}
in
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Under some proper conditions on the coefficients, it was proved that

\mu^{{\varepsilon}}\rightarrow\mu\penalty\ \penalty\ \text{weakly},\penalty\ \text{as}\penalty\ {\varepsilon}\rightarrow 0,

where
\mu
is the distribution of the limiting process (see
[
3
, Theorem 6.1]
). In the diffusion approximation theory, due to the appearance of homogenization term in
(
1.1
)
, an extra diffusion term will appear in the limiting equation typically.

Note that if we denote
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}=X^{\varepsilon}_{t}/\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}
, then
(
1.1
)
is equivalent to the following coupled stochastic system

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}K\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dt+\sigma\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dW_{t},\\
\displaystyle dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}K\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}}b\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}}\sigma\left(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}\right)dW_{t},\\
\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x,\penalty\ Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x/\sqrt{{\varepsilon}}.\end{array}\right.

Hence, the classical diffusion approximation problem can be reduced to a more general type

(1.2)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}K(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\sigma(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\displaystyle dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}h(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}g(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=x,\penalty\ Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}=y.\end{array}\right.

When
h\equiv 0
, Pardoux and Veretennikov
[
31
,
32
]
have studied the asymptotic behavior of the solution
X^{{\varepsilon}}
to (
1.2
) in
C([0,\infty);\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the main technique is based on the auxiliary Poisson equation and applying the martingale problem approach to characterize the limiting process. Recently, Röckner and Xie
[
36
]
developed the regularity of Poisson equation and established a more general result of diffusion approximation for the multi-scale SDEs. For more results on this subject, we refer to
[
2
,
4
,
11
,
15
,
23
,
25
,
43
,
42
,
45
]
.

In this paper, we are interested in the theory of diffusion approximation for a class of multi-scale McKean-Vlasov SDEs.
The McKean-Vlasov SDEs in the form of

(1.3)

dX_{t}=b(X_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}})dt+\sigma(X_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}})dW_{t},

where
\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}}
stands for the distribution of
X_{t}
, have attracted considerable attention in recent years.
This type of models arose in
[
28
]
by McKean, which could be view as
the limit of
N
-interacting particle systems in a mean-field way (called the
propagation of chaos
) while
N
goes to
infinity, we refer the reader to the lecture notes
[
40
]
. Another important application of McKean-Vlasov SDEs is it can be used to characterize the nonlinear Fokker-Planck-Kolmogorov (FPK for short) equations (cf.
[
40
,
41
]
). More specifically, the distribution density (denoted by
\rho_{t}
) of solution to (
1.3
) solves the following nonlinear PDE

\partial_{t}\rho_{t}=L^{*}\rho_{t},\penalty\ \penalty\ t\geqslant 0,

where
L
is a second order nonlinear operator and
L^{*}
denotes its adjoint operator. In particular, it can be applied to solve the homogenous Landau equations, i.e.,

\displaystyle\partial_{t}f_{t}=\frac{1}{2}{\rm{div}}\Big\{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}a(\cdot-z)(f_{t}(z)\nabla f_{t}-f_{t}\nabla f_{t}(z))dz\Big\},

for some reference coefficients
a:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}
. It is well-known that the Landau equation can be seen as an approximation of the Boltzmann equation in the asymptotic of grazing collisions. In the classical paper
[
13
]
, Funaki investigated the diffusion approximation problem of the homogenous Boltzmann equation with soft potentials and showed that the Boltzmann martingale problem towards to the Landau martingale problem as the scale parameter
\varepsilon\to 0
, see also
[
12
]
for the case of the Boltzmann equation of Maxwellian molecules.

From the standpoint of McKean-Vlasov SDEs, we consider the following multi-scale McKean-Vlasov stochastic systems

(1.4)

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}K(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\sigma(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\displaystyle dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}h(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}g(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW_{t},\\
\displaystyle X^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\xi,\penalty\ Y^{\varepsilon}_{0}=\zeta,\end{array}\right.

where
\{W_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
is a
d
-dimensional standard Brownian motion on a complete filtration probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\{\mathscr{F}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0},\mathbb{P})
,
\xi
and
\zeta
are
\mathscr{F}_{0}
-measurable
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued random variables respectively,
\varepsilon
is a small positive parameter describing the ratio of the time-scale between the slow component
X^{\varepsilon}_{t}
and fast component
Y^{\varepsilon}_{t}
. Multi-scale systems are ubiquitous in many fields of sciences and engineering such as chemistry, fluids dynamics and climate dynamics, the reader can see
[
1
,
33
]
and the reference therein for more precise background and applications.

Since the widely separated time-scales and the cross interactions of slow and fast modes, it is often difficult to study the multi-scale system directly. Hence the asymptotic behavior (specifically, the averaging principle) of the system and a simplified equation which governs the evolution of the system for small
\varepsilon
are widely studied in the literature. Very recently, the averaging principle of multi-scale McKean-Vlasov stochastic systems

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\displaystyle dX^{\varepsilon}_{t}=b(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\sigma(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW^{1}_{t},\\
\displaystyle dY^{\varepsilon}_{t}=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}f(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dt+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon}}g(X^{\varepsilon}_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X^{\varepsilon}_{t}},Y^{\varepsilon}_{t})dW^{2}_{t},\end{array}\right.

where
\{W^{1}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
and
\{W^{2}_{t}\}_{t\geqslant 0}
are mutually independent
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
-dimensional Brownian motions, was established by Röckner et al.
[
35
]
, which
can be seen as the classical functional law of large numbers. For more averaging principle results, we refer the reader to e.g.
[
7
,
10
,
14
,
18
,
22
,
34
,
39
]
and reference therein for the classical SDEs or SPDEs (i.e. distribution independent case) and to
[
19
,
37
,
44
]
for the case of distribution dependence.

As the continuation of
[
35
]
, we are going one step further in the present paper. Namely, we will investigate the theory of diffusion approximation, which is closely relate to the averaging principle, for more general type of multi-scale McKean-Vlasov stochastic systems (
1.4
) compared to
[
35
]
. As a powerful tool of characterizing the limiting process, some regularities on the Wasserstein space of solution to the auxiliary Poisson equation depending on parameter measures are derived. Furthermore, it is worth pointing out an independent interest of the paper. More precisely, we shall use two different methods to characterize the limiting process. One is the
martingale problem approach
, and the other is
martingale characteriziation
. As known to all, the former method is used frequently to characterize the limiting process is the solution of corresponding martingale problem (see
[
31
,
32
]
), however it seems nontrivial to characterize the explicit form of limiting equation, which could be completed in a straightforward way by our second method.
Here we give the details and present our strategy for the readers briefly.

Firstly, we prove that
X^{\varepsilon}
is tight in
C([0,T],\mathbb{R}^{n})
, then there exists a subsequence of any sequence
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
, which we keep denoting by
\{\varepsilon_{k}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
, tending to
0
such that
X^{\varepsilon_{k}}
converges weakly to the limit denoted by
X
in
C([0,T];\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Secondly, we identify the weak limiting process of sequence
\{X^{\varepsilon_{k}}\}_{k\geqslant 1}
as
k\to\infty
.
To do this, we denote by
\Phi
the solution of the following Poisson equation with measure dependence, i.e.,

-\mathscr{L}_{2}(x,\mu)\Phi(x,\mu,y)=K(x,\mu,y),

where the operator
\mathscr{L}_{2}(x,\mu)
is generator of the frozen equation (see (
3.1
) below) corresponding to the fast component of (
1.4
). We shall use two mentioned methods above to present our main results.

•

Martingale problem approach:
The limiting process is the solution of the
martingale problem associated to the operator
L_{\mu}
given by

L_{\mu}:=\sum^{n}_{i=1}\Theta_{i}(x,\mu)\partial_{x_{i}}+\frac{1}{2}\sum^{n}_{i=1}\sum^{n}_{j=1}(\Sigma\Sigma^{\ast})_{ij}(x,\mu)\partial_{x_{i}}\partial_{x_{j}},

where

\displaystyle\Theta(x,\mu):=\overline{b+\partial_{x}\Phi_{K}+\partial_{y}\Phi_{h}+\text{Tr}\left[\partial^{2}_{xy}\Phi_{\sigma g^{*}}\right]}(x,\mu),

\displaystyle\Sigma(x,\mu):=\Big(\overline{(K\otimes\Phi)+(K\otimes\Phi)^{\ast}+(\sigma g^{*})\partial_{y}\Phi+[(\sigma g^{*})\partial_{y}\Phi]^{\ast}+(\sigma\sigma^{\ast})}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(x,\mu).

Then such limiting process is also the weak solution of following equation

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Theta(X_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}})dt+\Sigma(X_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}})d\hat{W}_{t},\quad X_{0}=\hat{\xi},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

where initial value
\hat{\xi}
coincides in law with
\xi
,
\hat{W}_{t}
is a
n
-dimensional standard Brownian motion.

Note that there is a gap about the diffusion coefficient
\Sigma
, that is, it is unclear whether the term

\overline{(K\otimes\Phi)+(K\otimes\Phi)^{\ast}+(\sigma g^{*})\partial_{y}\Phi+[(\sigma g^{*})\partial_{y}\Phi]^{\ast}+(\sigma\sigma^{\ast})}

is positive semi-definite so that the square root makes sense. In order to fill this gap, we use the following second method to characterize the limiting process.

•

Martingale characterization:
The limiting process is the weak solution of following equation

\displaystyle dX_{t}=\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\Theta(X_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}})dt+\tilde{\Sigma}(X_{t},\mathscr{L}_{X_{t}})d\tilde{W}_{t},\quad X_{0}=\tilde{\xi},\penalty\ \penalty\ \penalty\

where initial value
\tilde{\xi}
coincides in law with
\xi
,
\tilde{W}_{t}
is a
n
-dimensional standard Brownian motion and

\displaystyle\Theta(x,\mu):=\overline{b+\partial_{x}\Phi_{K}+\partial_{y}\Phi_{h}+\text{Tr}\left[\partial^{2}_{xy}\Phi_{\sigma g^{*}}\right]}(x,\mu),

\displaystyle\tilde{\Sigma}(x,\mu):=\Big(\overline{(\partial_{y}\Phi_{g}+\sigma)(\partial_{y}\Phi_{g}+\sigma)^{*}}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}(x,\mu).

The meaning of these notations in
\Theta,\Sigma,\tilde{\Sigma}
can be founded in section 2.

Note that, by using these two methods, the obtained drift coefficients in the limiting equation have the same form but the diffusion coefficients are different, however it will be asserted that they are essential the same, see Remark
2.9
for the detailed explanations.

This manuscript is organized as follows. In section 2, we first introduce some notations and assumptions, then we state our main results more details through by two different methods, see Theorem
2.6
and Theorem
2.8
respectively. In section 3, we give some a priori estimates of the solution
(X^{{\varepsilon}},Y^{{\varepsilon}})
and study the regularity of the solution of Poisson equation. Finally, the detailed proof of Theorem
2.6
is given in subsection 4.1, and the detailed proof of Theorem
2.8
is given in subsection 4.2. Note that throughout this paper
C
and
C_{T}
denote positive constants which may change from line to line, where the subscript
T
is used to emphasize that the constant depends on certain parameter.

2.
Framework and main results

We first recall some notations that will be frequently used throughout the present paper. We denote by
|\cdot|
the Euclidean vector norm and by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the usual Euclidean inner product. Let
\|\cdot\|
be the matrix norm or the operator norm if there is no confusion possible. For a vector-valued or matrix-valued function
u(x,y)
defined on
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, for any
v,q\in\{x,y\}
, we use
\partial_{v}u
to denote the first order partial derivative of
u
with respect to (w.r.t.) the component
v
, and
\partial^{2}_{vq}u
to denote its mixed second order derivatives of
u
w.r.t. the components
v
and
q
.

Let
\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be the set of all probability measures on
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\mathscr{B}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))
and
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
be

\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):=\Big\{\mu\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{n}):\mu(|\cdot|^{2}):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{2}\mu(dx)<\infty\Big\},

then
\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
is a Polish space under the Wasserstein distance

\mathbb{W}_{2}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf_{\pi\in\mathscr{C}_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}}}\left[\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x-y|^{2}\pi(dx,dy)\right]^{1/2},\penalty\ \mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where
\mathscr{C}_{\mu_{1},\mu_{2}}
is the set of all couplings for
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
.

In the sequel, we recall the notion of Lions derivative on Wasserstein space. For any
u:\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
U
its "extension" to
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, which is defined by

U(X)=u(\mathscr{L}_{X}),\quad X\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P};\mathbb{R}^{n}).

We say
u
is differentiable at
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if there exists
X\in L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P};\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that
\mathscr{L}_{X}=\mu
and
U
is Fréchet differentiable at
X
. By the Riesz representation theorem, the Fréchet derivative
DU(X)
, which can be seen as an element of
L^{2}(\Omega,\mathbb{P};\mathbb{R}^{n})
, can be represented by

DU(X)=\partial_{\mu}u(\mathscr{L}_{X})(X),

where
\partial_{\mu}u(\mathscr{L}_{X}):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called the Lions derivative of
u
at
\mu=\mathscr{L}_{X}
. Moreover,
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)\in L^{2}(\mu;\mathbb{R}^{n})
for
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. In addition, if
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is differentiable, we denote its derivative by
\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In addition,
we say a vector-valued or matrix-valued function
u(\mu)=(u_{ij}(\mu))
is differentiable at
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, if all its components are differentiable at
\mu
, and set

\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)=(\partial_{\mu}u_{ij}(\mu)),\penalty\ \penalty\ \|\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\partial_{\mu}u_{ij}(\mu)(z)|^{2}\mu(dz).

Similarly, we call that
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(\cdot)
is differentiable if all its components are differentiable, and set

\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)=(\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u_{ij}(\mu)(z)),\penalty\ \penalty\ \|\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(\cdot)\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\mu)}=\sum_{i,j}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u_{ij}(\mu)(z)\|^{2}\mu(dz).

For sake of simplicity, we recall the following definitions.

Definition 2.1
.

For a map
u(\cdot):\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{R}
, we say
u\in C^{(1,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{R})
, if this map is continuously differentiable at any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
and its derivative
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z):\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
is continuously differentiable at any
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, moreover, the derivatives
\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)
and
\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)
are jointly continuous at any
(\mu,z)
, and uniformly bounded, i.e.
\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)\|<\infty
and
\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2},z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)\|<\infty
. For a vector or matrix-valued map
u(\cdot):\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\to\mathbb{K}
, where
\mathbb{K}
is a vector or matrix space, we say
u\in C^{(1,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{K})
if all the components belong to
C^{(1,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 2.2
.

We say
u\in C^{(2,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{R})
, if
u\in C^{(1,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{R})
and its Lions derivative of order two
\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)(z^{\prime})
exists and is jointly continuous at any
(\mu,z,z^{\prime})
and uniformly bounded in

-sense, i.e.
\sup_{\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\|\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}u(\mu)(z)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}<\infty
. For a vector or matrix-valued map we say
u\in C^{(2,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{K})
if all the components belong to
C^{(2,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 2.3
.

For a map
u(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{R}
, we say
u\in C^{3,3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R})
, if all the
k
-order (mixed) partial derivatives of

are jointly continuous and uniformly bounded w.r.t.

, for

. For a vector or matrix-valued map
u(\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{K}
, we say
u\in C^{3,3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{K})
if all the components belong to
C^{3,3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{R})
.

Definition 2.4
.

For a vector or matrix-valued map
u(\cdot,\cdot,\cdot):\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\to\mathbb{K}
, we say
u\in C^{3,(2,1),3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{K})

u(x,\cdot,y)\in C^{(2,1)}_{b}(\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n});\mathbb{K})
for any
(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
u(\cdot,\mu,\cdot)\in C^{3,3}_{b}(\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{m};\mathbb{K})
for any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, moreover, all the
k
-order (mixed) partial partial derivatives of
u
w.r.t.
(x,y)
, for
1\leqslant k\leqslant 3
, are uniformly bounded w.r.t.
(x,\mu,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\partial_{\mu}u(x,\mu,y)(z)
,
\partial_{z}\partial_{\mu}u(x,\mu,y)(z)
and
\|\partial_{\mu}\partial_{\mu}u(x,\mu,y)(z)\|_{L^{2}(\mu)}
are uniformly bounded w.r.t.
(x,\mu,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

Suppose that the coefficients

\displaystyle b:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n};

\displaystyle K:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n};

\displaystyle\sigma:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d};

\displaystyle f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m};

\displaystyle h:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m};

\displaystyle g:\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathscr{P}_{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\times\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{d}

\displaystyle|f(x_{1},\mu_{1},y_{1})-f(x_{2},\mu_{2},y_{2})|+|h(x_{1},\mu_{1},y_{1})-h(x_{2},\mu_{2},y_{2})|

(2.2)
