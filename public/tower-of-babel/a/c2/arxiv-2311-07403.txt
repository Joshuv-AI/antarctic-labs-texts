Moving mirrors and event horizons in non-flat background geometry Preprint: FTPI-MINN-23-21
Evgenii Ievlev

Abstract
Moving mirrors have been used for a long time as simple models for studying various properties of black hole radiation, such as the thermal spectrum and entanglement entropy. These models are typically constructed to mimic the collapse of a spherically symmetric distribution of matter in the Minkowski background. We generalize this correspondence to the case of non-trivial background geometry and consider two examples, the Schwarzschild – de Sitter black hole and the Bañados–Teitelboim–Zanelli (BTZ) black hole. In the BTZ case we were also able to show that this approach works for the spinning black hole which has only axial symmetry.



I
Introduction

It has been known for a long time that the vacuum in a quantum system can change its structure when the system undergoes a transition, either smooth or discontinuous.
This implies that even if the system has started as a state with lowest possible energy and no particles, at late times particles may appear.

In the Heisenberg picture of quantum mechanics this is seen from the evolution of the Hamiltonian and its spectrum.
Such an evolution means that, although the wave function
\ket{\Psi}
is not changing, its decomposition into the energy eigenstates might not be the same at early and late times.
Recall that any Hilbert space vector
\ket{\Psi}
can be decomposed in a sum over the spectrum of a Hamiltonian with certain coefficients. If the spectrum of the Hamiltonian changes, these coefficients are also modified, like coordinates under a change of the basis.

The upshot of this is that even if the initial state
\ket{\Psi}
was the lowest energy eigenstate of the early-times Hamiltonian, at late times it may have contributions from excited eigenstates.

In quantum field theory (QFT), one can analyze vacuum fluctuation of the quantum field.
Under the influence of, for example, a black hole horizon in 3+1d
Hawking 1975
or a dynamical boundary condition in 1+1d
DeWitt 1975
;
Fulling and Davies 1976
;
Davies and Fulling 1977
, these fluctuations can be amplified, which leads to a detectable energy/particle flux being produced.

The moving mirror model, or dynamical Casimir effect (DCE), in the simplest case is a QFT of a massless scalar field with a Dirichlet boundary condition (b.c.): the field is set to zero at the point where the mirror is located.
The non-trivial dynamics occurs if the mirror is accelerated

Moore 1970
;
DeWitt 1975
;
Fulling and Davies 1976
;
Davies and Fulling 1977
;
Walker 1985
;
Ford and Vilenkin 1982
;
Carlitz and Willey 1987
;
Chen and Mourou 2017
;
Chen et al. 2022
;
Good et al. 2021a
;
Chen and Mourou 2020
,
see also e.g. the textbooks
Birrell and Davies 1984
;
Fabbri and Navarro-Salas 2005
.

It was quickly realized that the phenomena of particle production near a black hole horizon and in moving mirror model bear striking resemblance, and that this resemblance can be used for understanding certain aspects of the QFT in 3+1-dimensional curved spacetime by studying a simpler 1+1 flat spacetime model
DeWitt 1975
;
Fulling and Davies 1976
;
Davies and Fulling 1977
.
Later a specific recipe was constructed
Wilczek 1993
that relates a collapsing lightlike shell to a specific moving mirror trajectory.
However, this recipe is valid as-is only in asymptotically flat spacetimes with spherical symmetry and with a flat region inside the shell.
Two such examples have been considered in the literature, the Schwarzschild
Good et al. 2016
and Reissner–Nordström
Good and Ong 2020
black hole mirror analogies.

There have also been attempts to apply this recipe to spacetimes that are not asymptotically flat
Good et al. 2020
;
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022a
;
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022b

or have only axial symmetry
Good et al. 2021b
;
Foo and Good 2021
.
However, as these examples violate the initial prescription assumptions (asymptotic flatness and spherical symmetry), there is no guarantee that the results will be sensible; and indeed, inconsistencies may arise
Good 2018
.

In this work we generalize the black hole – mirror correspondence to the case when the spacetime is not asymptotically flat, and the shell’s interior region is not flat eigher.
More specifically, we consider a black hole that is formed from a collapsing shell of null dust immersed in a spacetime that had non-vanishing curvature to begin with.
The thin shell approximation allows to capture the essential features of the process while keeping full tractability.
We were also able to demonstrate consistency of relaxing the spherical symmetry assumption: instead of requiring that the spacetime itself should be spherically symmetric,
we only require that the spherical symmetry is restored in the
s
-wave sector of massless particles’ geodesics.
This might explain the consistency of the results on the Kerr(-Newman) black holes
Good et al. 2021b
;
Foo and Good 2021
;
Rothman 2000
.

Review of particle production in dynamical models

In this section we will briefly review the concept of particle production in some models with externally varied conditions.
This Section focuses on the key features of moving mirrors and black holes that would help us to understand the connection between moving mirrors and collapsing geometries.

;

The mirror location is taken to be preset externally.
Its trajectory can be specified in a variety of ways. In terms of the usual Minkowski coordinates

(1)

There are two qualitatively different cases that depend on whether or not the mirror asymptotically approaches the speed of light.
If the mirror is always timelike, then all incident rays get reflected, see Fig.

If the mirror is lightlike at late times, then it develops a horizon, as late rays never reach the mirror and do not get reflected, see Fig.

A comprehensive discussion of the moving mirror classification can be found in

Consider a ray that starts at the past lightlike infinity
\mathcal{I}^{R}_{-}

v=v_{-}

\omega_{-}

If this ray does get reflected from the mirror, then it travels to the future lightlike infinity

1

The red shift is determined by a standard Doppler factor

\frac{\omega_{+}}{\omega_{-}}=\frac{1-|\dot{z}_{m}|}{1+|\dot{z}_{m}|}=\derivative{v_m}{u}=\left(\derivative{u_m}{v}\right)^{-1}\,,

(4)

where the dot denotes a derivative with respect to time

If the mirror is stationary or moves at a constant speed, we can always do a Lorenz boost to a frame where the mirror is at
z=z_{m}(t)\equiv 0

Such a boost does not change the scalar field energy-momentum tensor.
If we start in the vacuum state, we will always stay in the vacuum state.
We can reformulate this in the following way: if the red shift in Eq. (
4

However, if the mirror moves non-uniformly (i.e. the speed is not constant in time), the situation drastically changes.
In the CFT language, we can still perform a conformal map so that the mirror is at the origin, but the stress-energy tensor of the scalar field
\Phi
become
non-trivial.
In terms of the trajectory
v=v_{m}(u)
, one finds the energy flux at
\mathcal{I}^{R}_{+}

Fulling and Davies 1976

\left\langle T_{uu}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{24\pi}\left[\frac{3}{2}\left(\frac{v_{m}^{\prime\prime}(u)}{v_{m}^{\prime}(u)}\right)^{2}-\frac{v_{m}^{\prime\prime\prime}(u)}{v_{m}^{\prime}(u)}\right]\,,

(5)

where primes denote derivatives with respect to
u

In the usual field theory language, while we start in the trivial vacuum
\ket{0}_{\text{in}}

\ket{0}_{\text{in}}
is no longer a vacuum. Rather, it is a non-trivial linear combination of a new vacuum and excited states. This is reflected by non-trivial Bogolyubov coefficients.
The relevant equation is

;
Birrell and Davies 1984
;
Good et al. 2013
;
Good et al. 2017

\beta^{R}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}}\int\limits_{-\infty}^{v_{\mathcal{H}}}dve^{-i\omega^{\prime}v-i\omega u_{m}(v)}\,.

(6)

Here,
v_{\mathcal{H}}
is the position of the horizon (
v_{\mathcal{H}}=+\infty

Using these Bogolyubov coefficients one can calculate the particle number registered at
\mathcal{I}^{R}_{+}

\expectationvalue{N_\omega}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\,|\beta^{R}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}|^{2}

(7)

as well as the corresponding energy

\expectationvalue{E}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\,\omega\expectationvalue{N_\omega}=\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\omega^{\prime}\,\omega|\beta^{R}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}|^{2}\,.

(8)

For further details we refer the interested reader to the textbooks
Birrell and Davies 1984
;
Parker and Toms 2009

We consider a shell of zero electrical charge and uniform surface energy density, with total energy

Upon this, we consider a neutral massless scalar field

The shell particles and the theory of gravity are taken as classical, while the field

Generally, there are two qualitatively different possibilities.

In the other a horizon develops, as the shell collapses. This happens e.g. in the famous case of the lightlike shell and corresponding Vaidya spacetime, see Fig.

III
Mirror-gravity correspondence in flat background

In this section we are going to discuss the correspondence between the collapsing 3+1d geometry on one hand, and the moving mirror in 1+1d on the other hand.

We are going to start with a review of some known results on the mirror-gravity correspondence.
We will focus on the case when the black hole is modeled as a collapse of a thin shell made of null dust immersed in flat spacetime.
For such a shell one can construct a moving mirror that has (under approximations to be discussed below) the same radiation spectrum as that of the black hole under consideration.

The discussion presented here focuses on the key details that will be needed for further generalizations, which are also discussed below.

III.1
Lightlike shell collapse

Let us begin by briefly recounting the lightlike shell model.
We take the bulk as the 3+1 Minkowski spacetime and introduce in it a collapsing lightlike shell.
The corresponding mirror was worked out in
Wilczek 1993
.
In this setup, the space inside the shell is flat, while outside it is given by the Schwarzschild solution:

\displaystyle ds_{\text{in}}^{2}

\displaystyle=dt_{\text{in}}^{2}-dr^{2}-r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,\quad r<r_{\text{sh}}(t)\,,

(12)

\displaystyle ds_{\text{out}}^{2}

\displaystyle=F(r)dt^{2}-\frac{1}{F(r)}dr^{2}-r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,\quad r>r_{\text{sh}}(t)\,.

Here,
r_{\text{sh}}(t)
is the radius of the collapsing shell,
M
is the shell’s energy, while the metric function
F
is given by the standard expression

F(r)=1-\frac{2M}{r}\,.

(13)

Note that the time coordinate is different on the inside and on the outside; see Appendix
A
for the details.

We introduce the Regge-Wheeler tortoise coordinate
r_{*}(r)
defined by

\derivative{r_*}{r}=\frac{1}{F(r)}\,.

(14)

The light cone coordinates outside and inside the shell are then defined, respectively, as

\displaystyle v^{\text{out}}=t+r_{*}(r)\,,

\displaystyle u^{\text{out}}=t+r_{*}(r)\,;

(15)

\displaystyle v^{\text{in}}=t_{\text{in}}+r\,,

\displaystyle u^{\text{in}}=t_{\text{in}}+r\,.

Note that in terms of
r_{*}
, the
(t,r_{*})
components of the metric outside can be rewritten as
ds_{\text{out}}^{2}=F(r)\left(dt^{2}-dr_{*}^{2}\right)
.
Note that this form of the metric is conformally flat. Therefore, it allows for solving the Klein-Gordon wave equation for a propagating massless scalar field.

The correspondence with the moving mirror is made through the light cone coordinates.
In order to do that, we need to find the map that related the inner and outer regions of the shell.

First of all, one identifies
v^{\text{in}}=v^{\text{out}}\equiv v
.
This can be done because our shell is comprised of the incoming null dust.
Indeed, following e.g. Sec. 29.2 of
Blau
, we write down the kinematic equation for such a shell as

v^{\text{in}}=v_{\text{sh}}^{\text{in}}
or, equivalently, as
v^{\text{out}}=v_{\text{sh}}^{\text{out}}
for some constants
v_{\text{sh}}^{\text{in,out}}
. By making appropriate shifts one can arrange for
v_{\text{sh}}^{\text{in}}=v_{\text{sh}}^{\text{out}}
, so that the identification
v^{\text{in}}=v^{\text{out}}
is consistent on the collapsing shell.
There could still be a proportionality factor between them, but it turns out to be 1.

To determine the transformation function
u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})
, we study what has been called
“the trajectory of the origin”
.

From Eq. (
15
) it follows that the point
r=0
is determined by the equation
v^{\text{in}}=u^{\text{in}}
.
Requiring the shell’s area to be a gauge invariant we can write

r_{*}\left(\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})}{2}\right)=\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u^{\text{out}}}{2}\,,

(16)

where
v_{\text{sh}}=t+r_{\text{sh}}(t)
is the shell’s location in spacetime.
Since for a given metric
F(r)
the tortoise coordinate
r_{*}(r)
is known,
Eq. (
16
) determines the transformation function
u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})
.

III.2
Statement of the correspondence

Now let us re-formulate the known mirror-gravity correspondence to be generalized below.
One identifies this 3+1d collapsing geometry with a 1+1d moving mirror.
The mirror’s trajectory is taken to be the trajectory of the origin; in a certain sense
Wilczek 1993
, the distance from a one-dimensional observer to the mirror is taken to be the same as the distance to the origin as
“seen”
by the three-dimensional observer near a massive object, see Fig.
3
for a schematic representation.
In the spherical coordinates a ray sent to the origin is seen to be
“reflected”
,
see Fig.
2
, which looks very similar to one side of the mirror Fig.
1
.

To pass from the qualitative to the quantitative, we note that the ray tracing function
G(u^{\text{out}})
(see Sec.
II.2
) is the same as the mirror’s trajectory function
v_{m}(u)
.
They both map the incoming ray
u
-coordinate to the reflected outgoing ray
v
-coordinate.
The Doppler shift is given by the derivative of this function, see Eq. (
4
) and Eq. (
10
);
the radiated energy flux is given by the Schwarzian derivative of this function, see Eq. (
5
) and Eq. (
11
).
The beta Bogolyubov coefficients, which are easily constructed for the mirror (see Eq. (
6
)), can also be constructed (to some extent) on the gravity side
Hawking 1975
;
Fabbri and Navarro-Salas 2005
; the results are also in agreement with each other.

As a next step we note that the transformation function
u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})
is actually the same as the ray tracing function
G
defined in
Eq. (
9
).
This is evident from Fig. (
2b
): the ray starts inside the shell at
v^{\text{in}}_{-}\equiv v^{\text{out}}_{-}
, propagates at constant

, then reaches the origin where it starts propagating at constant
u^{\text{in}}=v^{\text{in}}_{+}
(recall that
u^{\text{in}}=v^{\text{in}}
for

) until it hits the shell.
After the shell it propagates at constant
u^{\text{out}}=u^{\text{out}}_{+}
such that
u^{\text{in}}=u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})
, see Eq. (
16
).
Tracing this ray back we obtain

v^{\text{out}}_{-}=u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}}_{+})\,.

(17)

One can compare Eq. (
17
) with the definition of the
G
-function Eq. (
9
).

All this allows to present the corresponding mirror’s trajectory.

Given the transformation function
u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})
, one takes the mirror’s trajectory in the form

(see Sec.
II.1
) as

v_{m}(u)=u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}}=u)\,.

(18)

Identification (
18
) gives a mirror that produces radiation with the same spectrum as the corresponding black hole, assuming the geometric optics approximation on the gravity side (see e.g.
Hawking 1975
;
Ford and Parker 1978
;
Fabbri and Navarro-Salas 2005
). Moreover, it captures only the

III.3
Possible generalizations

Let us list a few key assumptions of the above analysis.

1.

The spacetime under consideration is spherically symmetric.

2.

The collapsing shell is lightlike, i.e. it collapses at the speed of light.

3.

The spacetime inside the shell is flat.

4.

The spacetime outside the shell is given by the Schwarzschild solution.

One can immediately generalize assumption
4
to a charged black hole
Good and Ong 2020
, but beyond that one has to proceed with care.
For example, the cases of Kerr
Good et al. 2021b
and Schwarzschild – de Sitter
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022a
spacetimes require relaxing of the assumptions
1
and
3
-
4
, respectively.

Below we are going to completely relax assumptions
3
and
4
and consider the shell collapsing in an already non-trivial background.
We will also comment a possible way to relax assumption
1
and consider an example of a spacetime that is only axially symmetric.
Generalization to timelike shells in connection to the mirror is going to be presented in a subsequent paper.

IV
The mirror and the non-trivial background geometry

In this Section we are going to implement the generalizations announced above and derive the main result of this paper.
First of all, when we introduce a dust shell in an already non-trivial spacetime (for example, with a cosmological constant present), the metric inside the shell is no longer flat.
Instead of Eq. (
12
) we will now have for the metric inside and outside the shell

\displaystyle ds_{\text{in}}^{2}

\displaystyle=F_{\text{in}}(r)dt_{\text{in}}^{2}-\frac{1}{F_{\text{in}}(r)}dr^{2}-r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,\quad r<r_{\text{sh}}(t)\,;

(19)

\displaystyle ds_{\text{out}}^{2}

\displaystyle=F_{\text{out}}(r)dt^{2}-\frac{1}{F_{\text{out}}(r)}dr^{2}-r^{2}d\Omega^{2}\,,\quad r>r_{\text{sh}}(t)\,.

Note that the outside metric
F_{\text{out}}(r)
is also no longer Schwarzschild but rather some fairly arbitrary function (of course, we assume it to be smooth and positive).

Since the inside is no longer flat, we need to introduce two corresponding tortoise coordinates instead of one:

\derivative{ r_{*\text{in}} }{r}=\frac{1}{F_{\text{in}}(r)}\,,\quad\derivative{ r_{*\text{out}} }{r}=\frac{1}{F_{\text{out}}(r)}\,.

(20)

The spacetime in coordinates
(t_{\text{in}},r_{*\text{in}})
on the inside, and
(t,r_{*\text{out}})
on the outside is conformally flat, which motivates introduction of the lightlike coordinates

\displaystyle v^{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=t_{\text{in}}+r_{*\text{in}}\,,\quad u^{\text{in}}

\displaystyle=t_{\text{in}}-r_{*\text{in}}\,;

(21)

\displaystyle v^{\text{out}}

\displaystyle=t+r_{*\text{out}}\,,\quad u^{\text{out}}

\displaystyle=t-r_{*\text{out}}\,.

We still can identify
v^{\text{in}}=v^{\text{out}}\equiv v

and describe the ingoing lightlike shell by
v=v_{\text{sh}}=\text{const}
.

The tortoise coordinates defined via Eq. (
20
) are determined up to an integration constant.
While for the outer coordinate
r_{*\text{out}}
this constant does not matter (it just effectively shifts
u^{\text{out}}
), the inner coordinate is fixed by requiring that
v^{\text{in}}=u^{\text{in}}
at the origin
r=0
, which implies

r_{*\text{in}}(r=0)=0\,.

(22)

Following the flat-background prescription, we still want to match the
r
coordinate at the shell. From Eq. (
21
) we infer that on the shell
r=r_{\text{sh}}

\begin{cases}r_{*\text{in}}&=\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u^{\text{in}}}{2}\,,\\
r_{*\text{out}}&=\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u^{\text{out}}}{2}\,,\end{cases}\quad\text{on the shell}

(23)

from which follows a generalization of Eq. (
16
)

r_{*\text{out}}\left[r_{*\text{in}}^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u^{\text{in}}(u^{\text{out}})}{2}\right)\right]=\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u^{\text{out}}}{2}\,.

(24)

Note the appearance of the inverse function
r_{*\text{in}}^{-1}
.

The argument of Sec.
III.2
can be repeated, and we identify the mirror’s trajectory
v_{m}(u)=u^{\text{in}}(u)
.

Therefore, the generalized prescription can be formulated as

r_{*\text{out}}\left[r_{*\text{in}}^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-v_{m}(u)}{2}\right)\right]=\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u}{2}\,.

(25)

An alternative prescription for the same trajectory but in the form
u=u_{m}(v)
would

r_{*\text{out}}\left[r_{*\text{in}}^{-1}\left(\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-v}{2}\right)\right]=\frac{v_{\text{sh}}-u_{m}(v)}{2}\,.

(26)

The mirror’s horizon is then located at
v_{\mathcal{H}}=v_{\text{sh}}-2r_{*\text{in}}(r_{\mathcal{H}})
, where
r=r_{\mathcal{H}}
is the position of the collapsed BH horizon in this geometry (in the simple Schwarzschild case
r_{\mathcal{H}}=2M
).
Note that if the inside is actually flat (
r_{*\text{in}}(r)=r
) then Eq. (
25
) reduces to the known prescription Eq. (
16
).

What we have done here is nothing but the generalization for the ray-tracing function
G(u)
.
The fundamental principle — identification of the ray-tracing functions on the mirror side and on the gravity side — remains unchanged.

Formula (
25
) gives the trajectory of the mirror corresponding to a given collapsing geometry;
this means that the Fulling-Davies-Unruh radiation coming from this mirror is the same as the Hawking radiation from the given collapsing geometry (in the geometric optics approximation).

V
Applications

Now let us apply the prescription derived in the previous section to some interesting cases.

V.1
Schwarzschild – de Sitter

The case of a black hole in de Sitter universe with a cosmological constant
\Lambda>0
is interesting because of several reasons.
Our Universe is also expanding, and it was particularly important during the inflation
Bousso and Hawking 1998
;
Chao 1998
.
Moreover, the cosmological horizon by itself also produces radiation with thermal characteristics
Gibbons and Hawking 1977
.
Such spacetimes are also interesting from the point of view of holography, see e.g.
Marolf et al. 2011
and references therein.

From the QFT perspective, thermodynamics of the Schwarzschild – de Sitter (SdS) spacetime was first studied in
Gibbons and Hawking 1977
, followed by many others (see e.g.
Kastor and Traschen 1996
;
Bhattacharya 2018
and references therein), and not only in 3+1 dimensions
Kanti et al. 2014
;
Kanti and Pappas 2017
;
Pappas et al. 2016
.
The analysis of quantum fields in curved spacetimes such as SdS presents difficulties which are sometimes hard to overcome.
From this perspective the duality between spacetime geometries and moving mirrors seems attractive, as it allows one to employ QFT in flat 1+1d spacetime with a boundary to study properties (such us the radiation and its thermality) of higher-dimensional theories with gravity.

In
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022a
;
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022b
the authors studied a mirror that corresponds to the following geometry: collapsing shell of null dust, positive cosmological constant
\Lambda>0
outside of the shell, and
\Lambda=0
inside the shell

so that the metric inside the shell is flat.
As we can see, in such a setup
\Lambda
is actually not a constant, but varies over the spacetime.
Here we consider a more realistic scenario with the cosmological constant is positive and actually remains a constant
everywhere, both inside and outside the shell.

V.1.1
Mirror’s trajectory

The metric of the 3+1-dimensional SdS spacetime is of the form Eq. (
19
) with the metric functions

F_{\text{in}}(r)=1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}\,,\quad F_{\text{out}}(r)=1-\frac{r^{2}}{L^{2}}-\frac{2M}{r}\,.

(27)

The cosmological constant is related to the parameter
L

L=\sqrt{3/\Lambda}

From Eq. (
20

27

\displaystyle r_{*\text{in}}(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}L\ln( \frac{L + r}{L - r} )=L\tanh[-1](\frac{r}{L})\,,

(28)

\displaystyle r_{*\text{in}}^{-1}(R)

\displaystyle=L\tanh( \frac{R}{L} )\,.

Note the normalization

The outer tortoise coordinate reads

\displaystyle r_{*\text{out}}(r)

(29)

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{C}}\ln\left(1-\frac{r}{r_{C}}\right)

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2\kappa_{C}}-\frac{1}{2\kappa_{B}}\right)\ln\left(1+\frac{r}{r_{B}+r_{C}}\right)\,.

In Eq. (

\displaystyle r_{B}

(30)

\displaystyle r_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{2L}{\sqrt{3}}\cos\left[\frac{1}{3}\cos^{-1}\left(\frac{3\sqrt{3}M}{L}\right)-\frac{\pi}{3}\right]\,.

The corresponding surface gravities are given by

\displaystyle\kappa_{B}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r_{C}-r_{B})(2r_{B}+r_{C})}{2L^{2}r_{B}}\,,

(31)

\displaystyle\kappa_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{(r_{C}-r_{B})(2r_{C}+r_{B})}{2L^{2}r_{C}}\,.

Now, applying the recipe Eq. (

\displaystyle u_{m}

\displaystyle(v)=-\frac{1}{\kappa_{B}}\ln\left[-\frac{L}{r_{B}}\tanh( \frac{v}{2L} )-1\right]

(32)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\kappa_{C}}\ln\left[1+\frac{L}{r_{C}}\tanh( \frac{v}{2L} )\right]

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{\kappa_{B}}-\frac{1}{\kappa_{C}}\right)\cdot\ln\left[1-\frac{L}{r_{B}+r_{C}}\tanh( \frac{v}{2L} )\right]\,.

Formula (

V.1.2
Analysis of the dynamics

Our trajectory in Eq. (
32
) can be compared to the trajectory derived in
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022a
(see Eq. (20-22) in that paper).
There are several distinct features.
We start by checking the limiting cases.

For a vanishing cosmological constant
\Lambda\to 0

r_{B}\approx 2M
,
\kappa_{B}\approx 1/(4M)
and
r_{C}\approx L

Decomposing the logs in Eq. (

Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022a
;
Wilczek 1993

However, the limit of empty dS space turns out to be different from
Fernández-Silvestre et al. 2022a

In this limit we take
M\to 0

r_{B}\approx 2M\to 0

\kappa_{B}\approx 1/(4M)\to\infty
and
r_{C}\approx L

\kappa_{C}\approx 1/L

The trajectory (

u_{m}(v)=v+\text{const}\,,

(34)

which is nothing but the stationary mirror, and there is no radiation in this case.
The reasons for such behavior will be discussed below.

Now let us turn to the radiation spectrum.
In principle, the spectrum of the radiation (Davies-Fulling-Unruh effect) can be obtained by plugging the trajectory (
32

6

This is already a strong result, as it presents the spectrum as a one-dimensional integral of an analytic function easily tractable by numerical analysis.

The most important contributions to the spectrum can be easily derived analytically in the near-horizon approximation.
The trajectory equation (
32

v=v_{\mathcal{H},B}
and
v=v_{\mathcal{H},C}
determined by the equations

\tanh( \frac{ v_{\mathcal{H},i} }{2L} )=-\frac{r_{i}}{L}\,,\quad i=B,C\,.

(35)

We assume
3\sqrt{3}M/L<1

In this case, simple analysis of Eq. (

0<r_{B}<L/\sqrt{3}
and
L/\sqrt{3}<r_{C}<L

v_{\mathcal{H},C}<v_{\mathcal{H},B}<0

(There is also a non-physical third

When

\displaystyle v\to v_{\mathcal{H},B}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow u_{m}(v)\approx-\frac{1}{\kappa_{B}}\ln[-(v-v_{\mathcal{H},B})]\,,

(36)

\displaystyle v\to v_{\mathcal{H},C}

\displaystyle\Rightarrow u_{m}(v)\approx-\frac{1}{\kappa_{C}}\ln[v-v_{\mathcal{H},C}]\,.

These are nothing but examples of the Carlitz-Willey mirror

.
For this mirror the radiated particle spectrum is known exactly: it is the 1+1 Planck distribution with a well-defined temperature
T

\displaystyle N_{\omega\omega^{\prime},i}

\displaystyle=|\beta_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}|^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa_{i}\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\omega/\kappa_{i}}-1}\,,

(37)

\displaystyle T_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\kappa_{i}}{2\pi}\,,\quad i=B,C\,.

Thus we obtain the thermal radiation with the temperature that agrees with known results.
The total radiation spectrum is a sum of the two found temperature curves plus some residual non-thermal radiation.
The latter piece can be prominent only during the intermediate times, but is negligible at the late times, when the black hole horizon forms.

V.1.3
Discussion of the results

In this example we saw that in order to derive the spectrum of the black hole radiation it is not necessary to employ the full machinery of QFT in curved spacetime.
Instead, one can build an analogous mirror model and arrive at this spectrum with less effort.

The main results, namely, the two-temperature spectrum and the expressions for the temperatures, agree with the literature.
However, there is one seeming peculiarity: the fact that in the limit of empty
dS
spacetime,
M\to 0
, we get a mirror that does not radiate, see (
34
).
This happened because in our dynamical lightlike shell collapse actually models only the black hole radiation.
The asymptotically de Sitter space is static in this model, and it is not captured by the mirror, which is inherently dynamical.
The mirror corresponding to the eternally empty dS space is a static mirror and does not radiate.

One might wonder, why then we see radiation with the cosmological temperature
T_{C}
at all.
It turns out that this radiation is also caused by the shell’s movement, but in the distant past.
Let us explain this in more detail.

The lightlike shell in this construction, before collapsing to form a black hole, comes from the past lightlike infinity.
Since the cosmological horizon is static in this model, the lightlike shell actually started from that horizon, meaning that in the limit
t\to-\infty
the shell’s radius actually approaches the de Sitter horizon
r_{C}
, see Eq. (
30
).
There is then thermal radiation dynamically generated by this shell in the past.

In other words, the timelike shell approaches the black hole horizon in the future, and the cosmological horizon in the past.
The corresponding moving mirror also displays the two horizons, the future and the past one, see Eq. (
36
).
And in the limit
M\to 0
there is no shell and, therefore, no dynamically generated radiation.

The fact that the moving mirror models do not capture static spacetimes is also true for the pure Schwarzschild black hole case.
All known constructions of this sort do not deal with eternal black holes; instead, they involve some dynamical models for the black hole collapse, for example, the shell of null dust.
This inherent property of the mirror-gravity correspondence is not a serious drawback, in our view, as realistic astrophysical black holes are not eternal, but rather form in a collapse, either in the process of the stellar evolution or in fluctuations shortly after the Big Bang.

One can also consider a model that dynamically incorporates the dS cosmological horizon as well.
One such construction was proposed in
Good et al. 2020
, where a bubble of de Sitter vacuum was expanding into the flat Minkowski exterior.
Incorporation of another collapsing lightlike shell into this spacetime is left for a future work.

V.2
BTZ

The Bañados–Teitelboim–Zanelli (BTZ) black hole
Banados et al. 1992
is an important solution of the (topological) gravity in 2+1-dimensional AdS spacetime.
Since it is possible to model this black hole as a collapse of a null dust shell, the mirror dual can be easily constructed.

This example also uncovers some new advantages of the moving mirror approach.
Quantum theory in the BTZ spacetime has several distinct features.

1.

AdS space, if taken as-is, admits closed timelike curves. To rectify the situation one should pass to the universal covering space (CAdS).

2.

AdS space has a timelike boundary, though which information can escape. In other words, if a light ray is emitted from the origin, it reaches the infinity in a finite coordinate time.

3.

BTZ black hole is topological in the sense that it is not a compact object curving the spacetime.
3
3

3

This feature actually does not present problems in QFT, but we list it for completeness.

Rather, BTZ is just a discrete quotient of the empty AdS
3
.
It was first noted in 2+1 dimensions
Banados et al. 1992
;
Banados et al. 1993
, then generalized to 3+1
Aminneborg et al. 1996
and to higher-dimensional spaces
Banados 1998
, see also
Steif 1996
for a detailed explanation.

Below we will see that all these features are easily addressed in the moving mirror approach.

Mirror’s trajectory

In our setup we consider a lightlike shell of effective mass

Similar setup — AdS inside, BTZ outside — has been also considered in the gravity side

The BTZ black hole can be modeled by a lightlike shell collapse

In the Eddington-Finkelstein coordinates the Vaidya-like metric in this case is

ds^{2}=-\left(m(v)+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}\right)dv^{2}-2dvdr-r^{2}d\phi^{2}\,,

\displaystyle ds_{\text{in}}^{2}

\displaystyle r_{*\text{in}}^{-1}(R)

\displaystyle=\ell\tan( \frac{R}{\ell} )\,.

\displaystyle r_{*\text{out}}(r)

\displaystyle r_{*\text{out}}^{-1}(R)

u_{m}(v)=v_{\text{sh}}+\frac{2\ell}{\sqrt{M}}\tanh[-1]( \frac{\sqrt{M}}{ \tan( \frac{v_\text{sh} - v}{2\ell} ) } )\,,

Here,

V.2.2
AdS peculiarities in the mirror

Now, let us see how the difficulties outlined at the beginning of this section are resolved in the moving mirror formalism.

1.

Presence of closed timelike curved in AdS manifested itself in the fact that the expression (
44
) is a periodic function in
v
.
To rectify this, it suffices to simply consider any one particular branch of this function.

2.

AdS boundary manifested itself in the fact that, in addition to the expected black hole future horizon, the expression (
44
) has a past horizon.
It turns out that, in order to rectify this, we should take into account only a half of the trajectory.
Let us consider this in detail.

From the AdS tortoise coordinate in Eq. (
42
) one can see that it takes
\pi\ell
amount of time for a light ray to cross the whole of AdS.
Therefore, a light ray emitted too early, i.e. at
v<v_{\text{crit}}=v_{\text{sh}}-\pi\ell
, never actually encounters the lightlike shell propagating at
v=v_{\text{sh}}
, see Fig.
4
.
Therefore such rays should not be taken into account, and in the moving mirror one should only consider
v>v_{\text{crit}}
.

The future and the past horizons are defined by the equations

u_{m}(v_{\mathcal{H},\text{past}})=-\infty\,,\quad u_{m}(v_{\mathcal{H},\text{fut}})=+\infty

(45)

that give (choosing a particular branch)

\displaystyle v_{\mathcal{H},\text{past}}

\displaystyle=v_{\text{sh}}+2\ell\tan[-1]( \sqrt{M} )-2\pi\ell\,,

(46)

\displaystyle v_{\mathcal{H},\text{fut}}

\displaystyle=v_{\text{sh}}-2\ell\tan[-1]( \sqrt{M} )\,.

As it turns out, using only
v>v_{\text{crit}}=v_{\text{sh}}-\pi\ell
cuts out exactly a half of this trajectory.
The other half at
v<v_{\text{crit}}
is just a fictitious reflection of it.

Plugging the resulting trajectory in the beta Bogolyubov integral with the appropriate limits, we finally arrive at the formula for the BTZ radiation spectrum,

\beta^{R}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac{\omega^{\prime}}{\omega}}\int\limits_{v_{\text{sh}}-\pi\ell}^{v_{\mathcal{H},\text{fut}}}dv\,e^{-i\omega^{\prime}v-i\omega u_{m}(v)}\,.

(47)

V.2.3
Radiation and temperature

Now let us actually analyze the BTZ mirror trajectory and investigate the radiation spectrum.

First of all we note that, just as it was in the previous example, in the limit of empty AdS space (i.e. with a shell of vanishing mass
(M+1)\to 0
we obtain a stationary mirror:

u_{m}(v)=-2\ell\tan^{-1}\left[\cot( \frac{v}{2\ell} )\right]=v-\pi\ell\,.

(48)

There is no radiation in this case.
This is emphasizes the fact that our current model describes dynamically only the black hole.
The AdS bulk is static, and possible radiation from the AdS boundary is not captured in this model.

Next, we want to extract the temperature of the thermal radiation spectrum.
Again, in principle the spectrum is given by the exact formula (
47
).
The temperature can be easily extracted in the near-horizon approximation, when the trajectory Eq. (
44
) simplifies to

u_{m}(v)\approx-\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{M}}\ln[-(v-v_{\mathcal{H},\text{fut}})\big]+\text{const}\,.

(49)

This gives the particle spectrum

N_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}=|\beta^{R}_{\omega\omega^{\prime}}|^{2}\approx\frac{1}{2\pi\kappa\omega^{\prime}}\frac{1}{e^{2\pi\omega/\kappa}-1}\,,\quad\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{M}}{\ell}\,.

(50)

Thus the spectrum is Planck with the temperature

T_{\text{BTZ}}=\frac{\sqrt{M}}{2\pi\ell}\,.

(51)

The spectrum and the temperature just obtained coincide with known results from the gravity side
Banados et al. 1992
;
Hyun et al. 1994
.

V.2.4
Note on a spinning black hole

To conclude this section, we want to make a remark concerning the case of BTZ with angular momentum.
Although the prescription described in this paper is not a priori guaranteed to work in this case, it nevertheless gives the right temperature.

For a BTZ black hole with mass
M
and angular momentum
J
, the metric has the form
Banados et al. 1992

ds_{\text{out}}^{2}=F_{\text{out}}(r)dt^{2}-\frac{1}{F_{\text{out}}(r)}dr^{2}+r^{2}\left(d\phi-\frac{J}{2r^{2}}dt\right)^{2}

(52)

with

F_{\text{out}}(r)=-M+\frac{r^{2}}{\ell^{2}}+\frac{J^{2}}{4r^{2}},.

(53)

Introducing

r_{\pm}=\ell\sqrt{\frac{M}{2}\left(1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{J}{M\ell}}\right)}\,,

(54)

one can write the expression for the tortoise coordinate in a convenient form

r_{*\text{out}}(r)=\frac{\ell^{2}}{r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}}\left[r_{-}\tanh[-1](\frac{r_-}{r})-r_{+}\tanh[-1](\frac{r_+}{r})\right]\,.

(55)

Now let us take a lightlike shell collapsing to form this spinning BTZ black hole.
Assuming AdS metric inside, one can easily write down the mirror trajectory:

\displaystyle u_{m}(v)=v_{\text{sh}}

\displaystyle+\frac{\ell^{2}}{r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}}\Bigg[r_{-}\tanh[-1](\frac{r_-}{ \tan( \frac{v_\text{sh} - v }{2\ell} ) })

(56)

\displaystyle-r_{+}\tanh[-1](\frac{r_+}{ \tan( \frac{ v_\text{sh} - v }{2\ell} ) })\Bigg]\,.

On the gravity side, the surface
r=r_{+}
is the black hole horizon.
The corresponding mirror horizon
v_{\mathcal{H},\text{fut}}
is determined by the equation
\tan( v / 2\ell)=r_{+}
; in the near-horizon approximation the mirror becomes

u_{m}(v)\approx-\frac{\ell^{2}r_{+}}{r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}}\ln(- (v - v_{\mathcal{H},\text{fut}} ) )+\text{const}\,.

(57)

This is again a Carlitz-Willey mirror, which gives thermal radiation spectrum with the temperature

T_{\text{BTZ},J}=\frac{r_{+}^{2}-r_{-}^{2}}{2\pi\ell^{2}r_{+}}\,.

(58)

This again coincides with known results
Banados et al. 1992
;
Hyun et al. 1994
.

When the angular momentum is zero,
J=0
, the two radii become
r_{+}=\ell\sqrt{M}
,
r_{-}=0
, and we recover the results from the above.

In the extremal limit
J=M\ell
we have
r_{+}=r_{-}=\ell\sqrt{M/2}
, and the temperature in Eq. (
58
) drops to zero.
This does not mean that such a black hole does not radiate, it only means that the radiation spectrum is no longer thermal.
Indeed, the mirror trajectory in this case becomes

\displaystyle u_{m}(v)=v_{\text{sh}}

\displaystyle-\frac{\ell}{\sqrt{2M}}\Bigg[\tanh[-1]( x )-\frac{1}{x-1/x}\Bigg]\,,

(59)

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\equiv\frac{\ell\sqrt{M/2}}{\tan( \frac{v_\text{sh} - v}{2\ell} )}\,.

In the near-horizon limit
x\to(1\,-\!0)
the first term in the square brackets gives the expected log, but the second term has a pole and spoils thermality.
This is consistent with known results on the extremal Kerr black hole
Rothman 2000
.

Now, the important question is, why has our prescription given the correct results, even though the spacetime in Eq. (
52
) was not spherically symmetric?
The answer is that, although there is no strict spherical symmetry, the current setup is still spherically symmetric in a sort of a
weak
sense, meaning that the tortoise coordinate in Eq. (
55
) still does not depend on the angle variable.

In the spinless case
J=0
the meaning of the coordinate
r_{*\text{out}}(r)
is clear: it describes the optical path of massless field waves.
When
J\neq 0
, this is not so straightforward, because massless particles’ geodesics generically do not satisfy
\phi=\text{const}
.
However, consider a ray sent in the direction of the origin (the
s
-wave approximation).
From the BTZ geodesics equations
Cruz et al. 1994
at zero angular momentum (
L=0
in the notation of that paper) it follows that

\derivative{\phi}{t}=\frac{J}{2r^{2}}\,.

(60)

Thus the last term of Eq. (
52
) vanishes, and we are left with the spherically symmetric interval for the massless
s
-wave.
This property can be called
s
-wave angular (or spherical) symmetry.

Therefore we see that in the case of rotating BTZ black hole we still recover spherical symmetry in the
s
-wave sector, and that is the reason why the moving mirror picture still works.
It would be interesting to rigorously analyze the lightlike geodesics and the mirror approach for the Kerr black hole in 3+1 dimensional spacetime; we leave this problem for another paper.

VI
Conclusions

In this paper we presented a generalization of the correspondence between black holes and moving mirrors.

This construction allows one to study certain aspects of black holes, like the radiation and temperature, by modeling such a black hole by a collapse of a shell made of null dust, and then constructing a dynamical moving mirror in 1+1d that corresponds to the spacetime of interest.
This paper focuses on constructing a mirror that corresponds to a given black hole geometry. Once a mirror trajectory is constructed, the usual methods can be employed to study the beta Bogolyubov coefficients.

The moving mirror approach is simpler as compared to the direct analysis of QFT in the curved spacetime of interest.
We have demonstrated this by studying the spectrum of the Hawking radiation of the Schwarzschild – de Sitter and BTZ black holes by relating it to the Fulling-Davies-Unruh effect in 1+1d.

In this paper we focused on generalizing and testing the moving mirror approach.

Previously this correspondence was well-motivated only in spherically symmetric asymptotically flat cases.

Here we extended it 1) to the non-trivial (asymptotically non-flat) background geometry, and 2) we were also able to somewhat relax the spherical symmetry assumption: the spacetime does not have to be spherically symmetric,
only the
s
-wave sector of massless particles’ geodesics should have spherical symmetry.
It is plausible that this last assumption can also be lifted, allowing for a rigorous study of e.g. Kerr black holes via the moving mirror;
however, further investigation is needed to clarify this.
