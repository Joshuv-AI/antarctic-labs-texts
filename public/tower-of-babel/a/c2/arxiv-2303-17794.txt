Abstract
. In this paper we study certain variational aspects of the volume product functional restricted to the space of small projective deformations of a fixed convex body. In doing so, we provide a short proof of a theorem by Klartag: a strong version of the slicing conjecture implies the Mahler conjecture. We also exhibit an interesting family of critical convex bodies in dimension 2 2 containing saddle points for this functional.

1.
Introduction

The volume product of a convex body
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
whose interior contains the origin is the quantity
\vp(K)\coloneqq|K|\cdot|K^{\circ}|
. Here
|\cdot|
denotes the standard Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and
K^{\circ}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid\langle x,y\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}y_{i}\leq 1\,\,\forall x\in K\}
is the polar body of
K
. While it is well known
[
Santaló 1949
]
that the volume product among convex bodies whose barycenter is centered at the origin is maximized only by ellipsoids, the lower bound is still unknown in dimension greater than
2
. More precisely, the Mahler conjecture claims that any convex body
K
containing the origin in its interior should satisfy the following optimal inequality:

\vp(K)\geq\frac{(n+1)^{n+1}}{(n!)^{2}}.

The conjectured lower bound is reached by any
n
-simplex whose vertices span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and sum up to zero. Mahler actually proved the inequality in dimension
n=2

[
Mahler 1939
]
, but so far the conjecture remains open for higher dimensions.

If we endow the space of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the usual Hausdorff topology, the volume product becomes a continuous function over the space
\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}
of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the origin in their interior. A standard compactness argument then shows that the functional
\vp:\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
attains its minimum. According to
[
Santaló 1949
]
any local minimizer
K
of the volume product satisfies that its barycenter
\mathbb{G}_{K}
and the barycenter
\mathbb{G}_{K^{\circ}}
of its polar coincide with the origin.
Now recall that the covariance matrix
\cov(K)
of a convex body
K
whose barycenter is centered at the origin is the symmetric matrix with the following entries:

\cov(K)_{ij}=\frac{1}{|K|}\int_{K}x_{i}x_{j}dx.

In this paper we propose a short proof of the following result due to Klartag.

Theorem 1.1
.

[
Klartag 2018
]

Let
K
be a local minimizer of the volume product. Then

\cov(K^{\circ})\geq\frac{1}{(n+2)^{2}}\cdot\cov(K)^{-1}

in the sense of symmetric matrices.

It follows from this result that a strong version of the slicing conjecture implies the Mahler conjecture. This strong slicing conjecture states that the homogeneous quantity
\det[\cov(K)]/|K|^{2}
is maximized among convex bodies with barycenter at the origin by any
n
-simplex whose vertices span
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and sum up to zero, see
[
Klartag 2018
]
for further details.

Klartag’s proof of Theorem
1.1
is based on a variational argument in the space of projective images of
K
that makes substantial use of the Laplace transform. In this paper we will also use a variational argument in the space of projective images of
K
in order to recover Theorem
1.1
, but our computation will be considerably simpler.
More precisely, in section
2
we consider the functional
\mathcal{F}_{K}(x,\xi)\coloneqq\vp\left((K^{\circ}+\xi)^{\circ}+x\right)
which is well-defined for any convex body
K
in
\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}
on an open neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
. By
[
Santaló 1949
]
we will easily observe (Proposition
2.1
) that the origin is a critical point of the functional
\mathcal{F}_{K}
if and only if
\mathbb{G}_{K}=\mathbb{G}_{K^{\circ}}=0
. Then an elementary computation (Proposition
2.2
) will lead us to the following expression for the Hessian matrix at such a critical point:

\Hess_{(0,0)}\mathcal{F}_{K}=(n+1)\vp(K)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(n+2)\cov(K^{\circ})&-I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&(n+2)\cov(K)\end{array}\right).

Finally we will check (Proposition
2.3
) that the above Hessian is positive semi-definite if and only if the symmetric matrix inequality
(n+2)\cdot\cov(K^{\circ})\geq[(n+2)\cdot\cov(K)]^{-1}
holds true. This implies Theorem
1.1
.

Our functional
\mathcal{F}_{K}
is defined using special projective transformations of the convex body, which are obtained as specific combinations of translations of the polar body with translations of the body itself. In section
3
we will prove that this functional captures all the relevant information about variational properties of the volume product on the space of projective images of a given convex body.
More precisely, we will first prove in Proposition
3.1
that once a suitable affine coordinate chart is fixed, the projective transformations close to the identity map transform the convex body
K
into a new convex body which still contains the origin in its interior. We will then show in Proposition
3.2
that the volume product of these small projective perturbations of our convex body factors through the map
\mathcal{F}_{K}
.

Finally in section
4
we will exhibit an interesting family of convex bodies in dimension
2
that are critical points of the volume product on the space of their own projective images.
More precisely, we will show the following.

Theorem 1.2
.

There exists a
1
-parameter deformation
\{P_{q}\}_{q\in[-1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2}]}
of an equilateral triangle
\Delta=P_{-1/\sqrt{2}}
by axially symmetric pentagons satisfying the following properties.

i)

Every pentagon
P_{q}
is a critical point of the volume product in its own projective orbit; more precisely, the function
\mathcal{F}_{P_{q}}
has a critical point at the origin.

ii)

The pentagon
P_{0}
is a saddle point; more precisely the Hessian matrix
\Hess_{(0,0)}\mathcal{F}_{P_{0}}
is non-degenerate and non-definite.

iii)

For
q
close enough to
-1/\sqrt{2}
we have

(1.1)

\tr[\cov(P_{q})\cov(P_{q}^{\circ})]>\tr[\cov(\Delta)\cov(\Delta^{\circ})]=1/8.

The main relevant property of this family is that part of these critical points are saddle points. The last property that there exists small deformations of
\Delta
verifying (
1.1
)
is also remarkable for the following reason. Recall that the
2
-dimensional Euclidean unit ball
B
also satisfies
\tr[\cov(B)\cov(B^{\circ})]=1/8
, and that Kuperberg in a private communication to Klartag shows that the Euclidean ball of any dimension is a local maximizer of the functional
K\mapsto\tr[\cov(K)\cov(K^{\circ})]
in the class of centrally-symmetric convex bodies with sufficiently smooth boundary, see
[
Klartag 2018
, p.78]
. At some point it was not clear if this upperbound was a global one, until it was disproved in
[
Klartag 2018
, Proposition 1.5]
for large dimensions. Our family discards any non-symmetric analog of Kuperberg’s local result near the
n
-simplex, even in low dimensions.

2.
A simple variational study of the volume product

The purpose of this section is to give a short proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Fix a convex body
K
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
containing the origin in its interior, and consider the functional

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{K}:\,\,U_{K}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle(0,\infty)

\displaystyle(x,\xi)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\vp\left((K^{\circ}+\xi)^{\circ}+x\right)

which is well-defined in an open neighbourhood
U_{K}
of
(0,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The Jacobian matrix of
\mathcal{F}_{K}
at the origin is the following:

\Jac_{(0,0)}\mathcal{F}_{K}=-(n+1)\vp(K)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}\mathbb{G}_{K^{\circ}}&\mathbb{G}_{K}\end{array}\right).

Recall the barycenter of a convex body
A
is defined by the formula
\mathbb{G}_{A}\coloneqq|A|^{-1}\int_{A}xdx
.

Proof.

Equivalently, we shall prove that for any
(v,\eta)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the following holds:

d_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}(v,\eta)=-(n+1)\vp(K)(\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K^{\circ}},v\rangle+\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K},\eta\rangle).

First recall that Santaló proved in
[
Santaló 1949
]
the following formula:

\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}|(K-tv)^{\circ}|=(n+1)\cdot|K^{\circ}|\cdot\left\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K^{\circ}},v\right\rangle.

Then we easily compute that

d_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}(v,0)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\vp(K+tv)=-(n+1)\cdot\vp(K)\cdot\left\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K^{\circ}},v\right\rangle.

The equality
d_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}(0,\eta)=-(n+1)\cdot\vp(K)\cdot\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K},\eta\rangle
easily follows from the previous computation using that
\vp(K)=\vp(K^{\circ})
.
∎

In particular the origin is a critical point of the functional
\mathcal{F}_{K}
if and only if
\mathbb{G}_{K}=\mathbb{G}_{K^{\circ}}=0
.

Proposition 2.2
.

Suppose that the origin is a critical point of
\mathcal{F}_{K}
. Then its Hessian matrix is the following:

\Hess_{(0,0)}\mathcal{F}_{K}=(n+1)\vp(K)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}(n+2)\cov(K^{\circ})&-I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&(n+2)\cov(K)\end{array}\right).

Proof.

First observe that for any
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\Hess_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}[(v,0),(w,0)]

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{0}\mathcal{F}_{K}(tv+sw,0)

\displaystyle(\text{using the previous proposition})

\displaystyle=-(n+1)\cdot\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\vp(K+tv)\cdot\left\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{(K+tv)^{\circ}},w\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-(n+1)\cdot|K|\cdot\left\langle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}[|(K+tv)^{\circ}|\cdot{\mathbb{G}}_{(K+tv)^{\circ}}],w\right\rangle.

Now, using the radial function
\rho_{A}
of a convex body
A\in\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}
, the support function
h_{A^{\circ}}
of its polar set, and the fact that
\rho_{A}=1/h_{A^{\circ}}
we compute

\displaystyle\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}[|(K+tv)^{\circ}|\cdot{\mathbb{G}}_{(K+tv)^{\circ}}]

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\int_{(K+tv)^{\circ}}xdx

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\int_{S^{n-1}}\int_{0}^{\rho_{(K+tv)^{\circ}}(u)}(ru)\,r^{n-1}drdu

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\int_{S^{n-1}}\rho_{(K+tv)^{\circ}}(u)^{n+1}u\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{u}{h_{K+tv}(u)^{n+1}}du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n+1}\cdot\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{1}{(h_{K}(u)+t\langle v,u\rangle)^{n+1}}\,udu

\displaystyle=-\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{\langle v,u\rangle}{h_{K}(u)^{n+2}}\,udu.

Therefore

\displaystyle\Hess_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}((v,0),(w,0))

\displaystyle=(n+1)\cdot|K|\cdot\int_{S^{n-1}}\frac{\langle v,u\rangle\cdot\langle w,u\rangle}{h_{K}(u)^{n+2}}\,du

\displaystyle=(n+1)\cdot(n+2)\cdot|K|\cdot\int_{K^{\circ}}\langle v,x\rangle\cdot\langle w,x\rangle\,dx

\displaystyle=(n+1)\cdot\vp(K)\cdot(n+2)\cdot v^{t}\cov(K^{\circ})\ w

as claimed. The computation of the dual term

\Hess_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}((0,\xi),(0,\eta))=(n+1)\cdot\vp(K)\cdot(n+2)\cdot\xi^{t}\cov(K)\ \eta.

for any
\xi,\eta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is similar. It remains to compute the mixed term as follows:

\displaystyle\Hess_{(0,0)}\,\mathcal{F}_{K}((v,0),(0,\eta))

\displaystyle=\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{0}\left(|(K^{\circ}+s\eta)^{\circ}|\cdot\left.\frac{d}{dt}\right|_{0}|((K^{\circ}+s\eta)^{\circ}+tv)^{\circ}|\right)

\displaystyle=-(n+1)\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{0}\left(\vp(K^{\circ}+s\eta)\cdot\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K^{\circ}+s\eta},v\rangle\right)

\displaystyle(\text{the origin being a critical point})

\displaystyle=-(n+1)\cdot\vp(K^{\circ})\cdot\left.\frac{d}{ds}\right|_{0}\langle{\mathbb{G}}_{K^{\circ}+s\eta},v\rangle

\displaystyle=-(n+1)\cdot\vp(K)\cdot\langle\eta,v\rangle.

This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 2.3
.

Suppose that the origin is a critical point of
\mathcal{F}_{K}
. Then

\Hess_{(0,0)}\mathcal{F}_{K}\geq 0\Longleftrightarrow\cov(K^{\circ})\geq\frac{1}{(n+2)^{2}}\cdot\cov(K)^{-1}.

Moreover

(2.1)

\det(\Hess_{(0,0)}\mathcal{F}_{K})=(n+1)^{n}\vp(K)^{n}\det[(n+2)^{2}\cov(K^{\circ})\cov(K)-I_{n}].

As observed in the introduction, the first part of this statement implies Theorem
1.1
. The second part will be useful in the last section.

Proof.

Just remark that if
A
and
B
are two symmetric positive definite matrices, and if we set

M\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&-I_{n}\\
-I_{n}&B\end{array}\right)\ \ \text{and}\ \ P\coloneqq\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{n}&A^{-1}\\
0&I_{n}\end{array}\right),

then
P
being invertible the following holds true:

M\geq 0\ \ \Longleftrightarrow P^{t}\ M\ P=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&B-A^{-1}\end{array}\right)\geq 0\ \Longleftrightarrow B\geq A^{-1}.

Since
\det P=1
,

\det M=\det\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}A&0\\
0&B-A^{-1}\end{array}\right)=\det(A)\det(B-A^{-1})=\det(AB-I_{n}).\qed

3.
Projective transformations and volume product

The purpose of this section is to show that the volume product on the space of projective images of a given convex body
K
factors through the map
\mathcal{F}_{K}
. Therefore the functional
\mathcal{F}_{K}
captures all the relevant information about variational properties of the volume product on the space of projective images of a given convex body.

First recall some material about projective transformations (also called homographies).
Consider the real projective space
\mathbb{RP}^{n}=(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus\{0\})/\mathbb{R}^{*}
.
We will make use of the projective coordinates
[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]
for points in
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
. Recall that a
projective transformation
is a diffeomorphism of
\mathbb{RP}^{n}
induced by a linear isomorphism of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as follows: given
A=(a_{ij})\in\GL_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})
the induced projective transformation
\mathbb{P}{A}
is

\mathbb{P}{A}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]=\left[\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{0i}x_{i},\ldots,\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{ni}x_{i}\right].

This allows to identify the group of projective transformations
\Proj(\mathbb{RP}^{n})
with the projective linear group
\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})=\GL_{n+1}(\mathbb{R})/(\mathbb{R}^{\ast}\cdot I_{n+1})
and defines a natural topology on it. Here
I_{n+1}
denotes the identity matrix.
We will use the
affine coordinate chart

\displaystyle F:\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow

\displaystyle\mathbb{RP}^{n}\setminus H_{0}

\displaystyle y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})

\displaystyle\longmapsto

\displaystyle[1,y]=[1,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}],

where
H_{0}=\{[x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}]\mid x_{0}=0\}
. Its inverse map is
F^{-1}[x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}]=(x_{1}/x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}/x_{0})
.

Given two nested open balls centered at the origin
B_{1}\varsubsetneq B_{2}
, we define the open subset of
\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}

\mathcal{V}\coloneqq\{K\in\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}\mid\cl({B}_{1})\subset\intr(K)\subset K\subset B_{2}\}

where
\cl
and
\intr
refer to the closure and the interior respectively.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
of the identity in
\Proj(\mathbb{RP}^{n})
such that
\forall K\in\mathcal{V}
and
\forall\pi\in\mathcal{U}
we have
\pi\circ F(K)\subset\mathbb{RP}^{n}\setminus H_{0}
and
F^{-1}\circ\pi\circ F(K)\in\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}
.

Proof.

Let us introduce the following local parametrization

\displaystyle\Phi:\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\Proj(\mathbb{RP}^{n})\simeq\PGL_{n}(\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle(M,x,\xi)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\xi^{t}\\
x&M\end{pmatrix}.

Clearly
\Phi
is a diffeomorphism onto its image which is an open neighbourhood of the identity.
We easily compute for any
\pi=\Phi(M,x,\xi)
and every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
that

\pi\circ F(y)=[1+\xi^{t}y,x+My].

So
\pi\circ F(y)\in\mathbb{RP}^{n}\setminus H_{0}
if and only if
1+\xi^{t}y\neq 0
, and the condition that
\pi\circ F(K)\subset\mathbb{RP}^{n}\setminus H_{0}
amounts to the condition that
-\xi\in\intr(K^{\circ})
. Now if
-\xi\in\intr(K^{\circ})
the well-defined set
F^{-1}\circ\pi\circ F(K)
is automatically a convex body, that contains the zero in its interior if and only if
-x\in\intr(MK)
.
Therefore the open set
\mathcal{U}\coloneqq\Phi\left(\{(M,x,\xi)\mid-\xi\in B_{2}^{\circ}\,\,\text{and}\,\,-x\in MB_{1}\}\right)
satisfies the conclusion of the proposition.
∎

Now we will reparametrize this open neighbourhood
\mathcal{U}
in order to show that the volume product of projective images of
K
factors through the map
\mathcal{F}_{K}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

There exists an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{W}
of
(I_{n},0,0)
in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a map
\Psi:\mathcal{W}\to\Proj(\mathbb{RP}^{n})
such that
\Psi(I_{n},0,0)=\Id_{\mathbb{RP}^{n}}
,
\Psi
is a diffeomorphism onto its image, and the following holds:

\vp(F^{-1}\circ\Psi(M,x,\xi)\circ F(K))=\mathcal{F}_{K}(x,\xi)

for any convex body
K\in\mathcal{V}
and for any
(M,x,\xi)\in\Psi^{-1}(\mathcal{U})
.

Therefore, in order to study the variational properties of the volume product of projective images of a convex body
K\in\mathcal{K}_{0}^{n}
, it is enough to study the associated functional
\mathcal{F}_{K}
.

Proof.

Consider the map

\displaystyle\alpha:\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle(N,y,\eta)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\left(N(I_{n}+y\eta^{t}),Ny,\eta\right).

The map
\alpha
fixes points of the form
(M,0,0)
for any
M\in\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})
, and there exists an open neighbourhood
\mathcal{W}
of
(I_{n},0,0)
in
\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that the restriction of
\alpha
to
\mathcal{W}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image whose inverse is
\alpha^{-1}(M,x,\xi)=\Big(M-x\xi^{t},(M-x\xi^{t})^{-1}x,\xi\Big)
.
Then define the map

\displaystyle\Psi:\GL_{n}(\mathbb{R})\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}

\displaystyle\to

\displaystyle\Proj(\mathbb{RP}^{n})

\displaystyle(N,y,\eta)

\displaystyle\mapsto

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&N\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
y&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\eta^{t}\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}.

Because

\Phi\circ\alpha(N,y,\eta)=\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\eta^{t}\\
Ny&N(I_{n}+y\eta^{t})\end{pmatrix},

while

\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&N\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
y&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\eta^{t}\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
Ny&N\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\eta^{t}\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\eta^{t}\\
Ny&N(I_{n}+y\eta^{t})\end{pmatrix},

we have that
\Psi=\Phi\circ\alpha
which implies that the restriction of
\Psi
to
\mathcal{W}
is a diffeomorphism onto its image. By construction
\Psi(I_{n},0,0)=\Id_{\mathbb{RP}^{n}}
.
Still denote by
\Psi:\mathcal{W}\to\Proj(\mathbb{RP}^{n})
the restriction of the map
\Psi
to
\mathcal{W}
.
Now for any convex body
K\in\mathcal{V}
and for any
(M,x,\xi)\in\Psi^{-1}(\mathcal{U})
the following holds:

\displaystyle F^{-1}\circ\Psi(M,x,\xi)\circ F(K)

\displaystyle=F^{-1}\circ\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&M\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
x&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\cdot\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&\xi^{t}\\
0&I_{n}\end{pmatrix}\circ F(K)

\displaystyle=F^{-1}\circ\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&M\end{pmatrix}\cdot\Psi(I_{n},x,\xi)\circ F(K)

\displaystyle=F^{-1}\circ\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&M\end{pmatrix}\circ F\circ F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},x,\xi)\circ F(K).

But for all
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

F^{-1}\circ\mathbb{P}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&M\end{pmatrix}\circ F(y)=My,

which implies using the invariance of the volume product by the linear group that

\vp(F^{-1}\circ\Psi(M,x,\xi)\circ F(K))=\vp(F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},x,\xi)\circ F(K)).

Now observe that

F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},x,\xi)\circ F(K)=(K^{\circ}+\xi)^{\circ}+x.

Indeed write
F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},x,\xi)\circ F=F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},x,0)\circ F\circ F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},0,\xi)\circ F
. First
F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},x,0)\circ F(y)=x+y
while
F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},0,\xi)\circ F(y)=y/(1+\xi^{t}y)
. By a straightforward computation it implies that
F^{-1}\circ\Psi(I_{n},0,\xi)\circ F(K)=(K^{\circ}+\xi)^{\circ}
.

It follows that
\vp(F^{-1}\circ\Psi(M,x,\xi)\circ F(K))=\vp\left((K^{\circ}+\xi)^{\circ}+x\right)
as claimed.
∎

4.
A family of critical pentagons

Fix two numbers
b>0
and
q\in[-\frac{1}{b},b]
, and set
r=\sqrt{1+q^{2}}
and
c=\frac{1+bq}{\sqrt{1+q^{2}}}
.

We consider the pentagon
P_{q,b}\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
whose vertices are
(-\frac{1}{b},0)
,
(q,\pm r)
and
(b,\pm c)
. By construction
P=P_{q,b}
is symmetric with respect to the
x
-axis:

Lemma 4.1
.

The dual of
P
is
-P
.

Proof.

This is a direct consequence of the following general fact for convex polygons: a point
(A,B)
is a vertex of
P^{\circ}
if and only if the line
Ax+By=1
contains a side of
P
. In this way:

•

the line through
(-\frac{1}{q},0)
and
(0,\pm\frac{1}{r})
containing a side of
P
corresponds to the vertex
(-q,\pm r)
of
P^{\circ}
,

•

the line through
(-\frac{1}{b},0)
and
(0,\pm\frac{1}{c})
containing a side of
P
corresponds to the vertex
(-b,\pm c)
of
P^{\circ}

By the previous lemma, if the barycenter of
P
lies at the origin, the same holds for its dual
-P

P
is a critical point for the volume product on the space of projective images of
P

The next lemma gives the exact condition on the pair
(q,b)
which ensures that
P
is such a critical point.

Lemma 4.2
.

The barycenter of
P
lies at the origin if and only if

f(q,b)\coloneqq q^{3}b-q^{2}b^{2}-q^{2}+2qb^{5}+qb+3b^{4}=1.

Proof.

Since
P
is symmetric, its barycenter lies on the
x
-axis, and therefore only the abscissa of the barycenter is relevant. This coordinate can be computed by decomposing the pentagon into six pairwise symmetrical triangles, with one vertex at the origin and one common side with
P
.
The condition on the barycenter then becomes

\frac{(b+b)}{3}\begin{vmatrix}b&b\\
0&c\end{vmatrix}+\frac{(b+q)}{3}\begin{vmatrix}b&q\\
c&r\end{vmatrix}+\frac{(q-\frac{1}{b})}{3}\begin{vmatrix}q&-\frac{1}{b}\\
r&0\end{vmatrix}=0.

Multiplying by
3b^{2}r
and using that
r^{2}=1+q^{2}
and
rc=1+bq
gives the desired condition.
∎

In addition to these few simple concrete examples, the next lemma

Lemma 4.3
.

The set

\biggl\{(q,b)\in[-\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}]\times[\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\sqrt{2}]\biggm|q^{3}b-q^{2}b^{2}-q^{2}+2qb^{5}+qb+3b^{4}=1\biggr\}

is the graph of a decreasing smooth function
b(q)
in the variable
q
.

As mentioned above, the extremal pentagons
P_{\pm 1/\sqrt{2}}
degenerate to equilateral triangles.

Proof.

According to the implicit function theorem, the result follows from elementary computations that both partial derivatives of the function
f(q,b)=q^{3}b-q^{2}b^{2}-q^{2}+2qb^{5}+qb+3b^{4}
are strictly positive on this domain, except at point
(1/\sqrt{2},1/\sqrt{2})
where the first partial derivative is zero.

Indeed first observe that
\partial_{q}f=3bq^{2}-2(b^{2}+1)q+2b^{5}+b
is a quadratic polynomial in the variable
q
. One can check that its discriminant
-6b^{6}+b^{4}-b^{2}+1
is always negative for
b\in(1/\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}]
, and zero at
1/\sqrt{2}
, by studying its first and second derivative.

Next we compute that
\partial_{b}f=q^{3}-2bq^{2}+(10b^{4}+1)q+12b^{3}
and check through the study of
\partial_{bq}f
that
\partial_{b}f(q,b)\geq h(b)\coloneqq\partial_{b}f(-1/\sqrt{2},b)
which is a polynomial of degree
4
. A straightforward variational study of
h
ensures its positivity on the desired domain.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

The inertia matrix of a triangle
\Delta
with vertices
(0,0)
,
(x_{1},y_{1})
and

I(\Delta)=\frac{D}{12}\begin{pmatrix}I_{xx}(\Delta)&I_{xy}(\Delta)\\
I_{xy}(\Delta)&I_{yy}(\Delta)\end{pmatrix}

where

\left\{\begin{aligned} I_{xx}(\Delta)&=x_{1}^{2}+x_{1}x_{2}+x_{2}^{2}\\
I_{xy}(\Delta)&=x_{1}y_{1}+\frac{x_{1}y_{2}+x_{2}y_{1}}{2}+x_{2}y_{2}\\
I_{yy}(\Delta)&=y_{1}^{2}+y_{1}y_{2}+y_{2}^{2}\end{aligned}\right.

and

D=\begin{vmatrix}x_{1}&x_{2}\\
y_{1}&y_{2}\end{vmatrix}=|x_{1}y_{2}-x_{2}y_{1}|

is twice the area of the triangle.

Proof.

Since we will not need the terms
I_{xy}(\Delta)
in what follows, we will only do the computations for
