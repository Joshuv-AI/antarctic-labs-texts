Symplectic cuts and open/closed strings I
Luca Cassia

Abstract
This paper introduces a concrete relation between genus zero closed Gromov–Witten invariants of Calabi–Yau threefolds and genus zero open Gromov–Witten invariants of a Lagrangian A A -brane in the same threefold. Symplectic cutting is a natural operation that decomposes a symplectic manifold ( X , ω ) (X,\omega) with a Hamiltonian U ⁡ ( 1 ) U(1) action into two pieces glued along an invariant divisor. In this paper we study a quantum uplift of the cut construction defined in terms of equivariant gauged linear sigma models. The nexus between closed and open Gromov–Witten invariants is a quantum Lebesgue measure associated to a choice of cut, that we introduce and study. Integration of this measure recovers the equivariant quantum volume of the whole CY3, thereby encoding closed Gromov–Witten invariants. Conversely, the monodromies of the quantum measure around cycles in Kähler moduli space encode open Gromov–Witten invariants of a Lagrangian A A -brane associated to the cut. Both in the closed and the open string sector we find a remarkable interplay between worldsheet instantons and semiclassical volumes regularized by equivariance. This leads to equivariant generating functions of GW invariants that extend smoothly across the entire moduli space, and which provide a unifying description of standard GW potentials. The latter are recovered in the non-equivariant limit in each of the different phases of the geometry.

Introduction

In this work we introduce a new direct relation between genus zero closed and open topological string potentials.
We find an explicit and computable description of this relation by studying a stringy quantization of a classical geometric construction known as symplectic cutting.

A symplectic
d
-dimensional manifold
(M,\omega)
with a Hamiltonian
U(1)
action admits a well-known reduction to another manifold
(M_{0},\omega_{0})=(M,\omega)\sslash U(1)
of dimension
d-2
, known as the symplectic quotient.
The symplectic cut of
M
consists of the union of two parts
(\overline{M}_{\pm},\omega_{\pm})
each of dimension
d
, glued along a codimension-two locus isomorphic to
(M_{0},\omega_{0})
.
Symplectic cutting and the inverse operation of symplectic gluing are basic constructions of classical symplectic geometry and thanks to their functoriality property, it is possible to relate classical and quantum geometric data of
(M,\omega)
to those of
(\overline{M}_{\pm},\omega_{\pm})
and
(M_{0},\omega_{0})
.

The symplectic cut construction has a very broad range of applications, and throughout the paper we focus on the setting of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds.
Every toric CY3
(X,\omega)
admits a Hamiltonian
U(1)^{3}
action, and the corresponding moment map defines a polytope with boundaries and corners where the action degenerates, see Figure
1
.
The toric setting admits a natural class of symplectic cuts associated with hyperplanes of rational slope within the moment polytope of
(X,\omega)
.
As we explain in the main body of the paper, there is a close relationship between this class of symplectic cuts and a class of Lagrangian submanifolds known as “toric Lagrangians” studied extensively in the physics literature.

The first main result of this paper is the observation that equivariance ties together semiclassical and instanton contributions to quantum volumes in highly nontrivial ways, and that this leads to a unification of solutions of PF equations from different chambers of the Kähler moduli space into globally defined expressions.

A conventional way of expressing solutions to Picard–Fuchs equations in the physics literature is through power series in

The analytic properties of the quantum Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{H}^{D}
play a central role in our work, ultimately establishing the link between open and closed string invariants.
A remarkable feature of the analytic structure of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
is its universality: it is both invariant under a change of framing (of the toric brane), and under a change of topology of the brane across different chambers of the open string moduli space.
We illustrate these points with exact computations of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
in examples below, where we further elaborate on these important structural properties.

The aim of this paper is to provide a self-contained and pedagogical illustration of the main ideas, with explicit computations for selected examples. To keep the paper readable we have postponed a more systematic treatment of several technical details, including the relation to mirror symmetry, to future works

Relation to previous work

Our work has several direct connections to previous literature on open and closed topological strings and to studies of Gromov–Witten theory in the context of symplectic cutting. Indeed many of the results that we obtain have been obtained in earlier work, but the connections that we establish among these fields, and our concrete approach to explicit results, are new.
Here we give a brief overview of how our work connects to previous literature.

The problem of computing genus-zero open Gromov–Witten invariants for toric
A
-branes was solved in the seminal papers
[
AV00
,
AKV02
]
.
These works identify the moduli space of a toric
A
-brane with a circle fibration over the moduli space of the underlying toric Lagrangian. It is argued that worldsheet instantons regularize singularities in the moduli space and turn it into a smooth algebraic curve
\Sigma
.
The superpotential
W_{\mathrm{AKV}}
was obtained from open periods of a certain 1-form on
\Sigma
, and shown to satisfy integrality properties in several examples.

In this paper we consider a generalization of
W_{{\mathrm{AKV}}}
, the equivariant superpotential (
1.7
).
As already stressed above, there is a stark contrast between the global behaviour of the equivariant superpotential and
W_{{\mathrm{AKV}}}
: while the former is encoded by
\mathcal{H}^{D}
, which extends smoothly across the entire moduli space, the latter is only defined phase-by-phase and features jumps among different phases.
The relation between the two originates from the splitting (
1.7
) into singular and regular parts.
We show by explicit computation that the regular part of
W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
coincides exactly with
W_{{\mathrm{AKV}}}
to all instanton orders, in several examples.
Agreement between
W_{\mathrm{reg}}
and
W_{{\mathrm{AKV}}}
is far from trivial however, since the definition (
1.8
) differs significantly from the Abel–Jacobi map proposed in
[
AKV02
,
AV00
]
.
A physical argument explaining why these compute the same quantity is sketched in Section
6
.
While at the level of worldsheet instantons our results are simply in agreement with
[
AKV02
,
AV00
]
,
the equivariant superpotential includes much more information.
In particular, it is natural to expect that the leading singular terms
W_{\mathrm{sing}}
encode classical geometric data of the toric lagrangian.

A central role in our work is played by the quantum Lebesgue measure, which has not been studied previously.
A key property of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
is that it encodes both closed (
1.5
) and open (
1.8
) string potentials in a unified treatment.
Previous connections between closed and open strings have been obtained by means of a rather different nature.
In the context of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds both the open and the closed string potentials are encoded by the topological vertex formalism
[
Aga+05
]
whose underpinnings lie on the relation between topological strings and Chern–Simons theory via large-
N
geometric transitions
[
GV99
,
OV00
]
.
The topological vertex is a powerful framework that computes all-genus open string instantons in the presence of (stacked) toric
A
-branes in arbitrary positions.
In the absence of branes the vertex computes closed topological string instantons at all genera.
However, the relation between open and closed string invariants that we find appears to be of a starkly different nature.
In the context of the vertex, the closed and open string partition functions are related by a discrete sum over partitions, while in our case
W
and
\mathcal{F}^{D}
are related by a change of contour in a continuous integral.
It should be noted that
\mathcal{F}^{D}
does not coincide with the closed string free energy, but is related to it in a nontrivial way (see footnote
2
).
It seems reasonable to expect that a relation between our framework and topological vertex may be better understood in terms of the mirror
B
-model. The open-closed string relation in that context was elucidated in
[
Bou+09
]
, and we plan to return to this in
[
CLZ24
]
.

Another approach to the relation between open and closed strings in genus zero was developed by
[
May02
,
LM01
]
. This approach relates the toric
A
-brane open string superpotential to the closed topological strings on a Calabi–Yau fourfold. While the geometric setup appears to be rather different from ours, there are certain analogies with our framework. In particular one may regard the fourfold as an extension of the CY3, where the additional direction corresponds to the position

A difference with our work is that the domain of applicability of the fourfold setup appears to have certain limitations due to technical issues.

However much less is known about

A proposal due to

In

2.3
Cuts of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds

A toric Calabi–Yau threefold
X
admits a description as a symplectic quotient

X=\mathbb{C}^{r+3}\sslash U(1)^{r}

(2.17)

such that the matrix of charges describing the
U(1)^{r}
-action satisfies

\sum_{i=1}^{r+3}Q^{a}_{i}=0\,,\qquad(a=1,\dots,r)\,,

(2.18)

If this condition is satisfied, the canonical bundle of

is trivial and therefore the quotient is a Calabi–Yau manifold.
However, it is well known that the metric induced by the ambient space
\mathbb{C}^{r+3}
is not Ricci-flat.
When considering string theory on

infrared to a CFT with a metric that is Ricci-flat at large radius

]
.
Since the RG flow only affects D-terms and not the superpotential,
properties of topological strings and mirror symmetry can be studied using the GLSM.

We will now describe symplectic cuts of toric threefolds.
The main takeaway of the upcoming discussion is that the cut is defined by the

\mathbb{C}^{r+3}

h^{b}

can
be chosen
to be homogeneous, i.e. one can always choose
c^{1}=c
and
c^{2}=0
after a
suitable linear combination of the charge vectors
q^{b}_{i}
.
The hyperplane equations (
2.27
) then become

h^{1}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{r+3}q^{1}_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}=c\,,\qquad h^{2}:\ \sum_{i=1}^{r+3}q^{2}_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}=0\,.

(2.33)

Even after fixing the choice of vanishing
c^{2}
, there is a residual
framing ambiguity
in the description of toric Lagrangians
[
AKV02
]
. Indeed the definition of the first hyperplane can always be shifted by the second charge vector

q^{1}_{i}\sim q^{1}_{i}+f\cdot q^{2}_{i}\qquad(f\in\mathbb{Z})

(2.34)

and for any
f
these define the same Lagrangian classically.
The framing ambiguity is however resolved by quantum effects.

The connection between
framed
toric lagrangians and symplectic cuts of
toric Calabi–Yau threefolds is now easily stated.
Both are defined by a choice of an affine hyperplane

h\equiv h^{1}

(2.35)

in the moment polytope
B
of the ambient space
\mathbb{C}^{r+3}
.
However, the correspondence is not one-to-one for at least two reasons:

•

While symplectic cuts can be defined for generic choices of
h
, the affine
hyperplane
h^{1}
associated to
L
must obey the condition (
2.32
).

•

Toric Lagrangians also involve in their definition a second hyperplane
h^{2}
, which is homogeneous.
However, this will not play a role in the connection to symplectic cuts for
reasons that will become clear later and further elaborated in
[
CLZ24
]
.
The main point is that a symplectic cut can describe toric Lagrangians in more
than one phase, e.g. for
c^{1}>0,c^{2}=0
and for
c^{1}=0,c^{2}<0
.

2.5
Equivariance and abelian GLSM

So far we have reviewed basic constructions of classical symplectic geometry.
We close this section by introducing two further constructions that will be
central in our story, one of them is equivariance and the other is the
gauged linear sigma model.
The presentation and conventions follow closely
[
CPZ23
]
.

Recall from Section
2.1
that toric Calabi–Yau threefolds admit
a description as abelian symplectic quotients
X=\mathbb{C}^{N}\sslash U(1)^{r}
with
N=r+3
,
where the
U(1)^{r}
action is specified by a charge matrix
Q_{i}^{a}
encoding the
moment map in (
2.4
).

To define the equivariant volume of
X
we introduce
N
equivariant parameters

\epsilon_{i}
associated to the natural
U(1)^{N}
action on
\mathbb{C}^{N}
by rotations of
the coordinates
z_{i}
. We define
p_{i}=|z_{i}|^{2}
to be the so called
momenta
,
i.e. coordinates on the moment polytope of the ambient space.
The equivariant volume is then

\mathcal{F}(\bm{t},\epsilon)=\frac{1}{(2\pi\mathrm{i})^{N}}\int_{\mathbb{C}^{N}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}z_{i}\wedge\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\overline{z}_{i}~\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{i}\epsilon_{i}|z_{i}|^{2}}\prod_{a=1}^{r}\delta\left(\mu^{a}_{Q}(z)-t^{a}\right)~,

(2.36)

If
X
is compact it is possible to take
\epsilon=0
unambiguously, and in this
limit
\mathcal{F}(\bm{t},\epsilon)
recovers the standard symplectic volume of
X

(with symplectic structure induced by that of
\mathbb{C}^{N}
).
However, the Calabi–Yau condition (
2.18
) implies that
X
is
always non-compact in our case.
This implies on the one hand that we always need to require that
\Re(\epsilon_{i})>0

for the convergence of the integral.
On the other hand, non-compactness also means that the geometric interpretation
of the equivariant volume is more subtle
[
NPZ22
]
.
Equivariance regularizes the infinite classical volume of
X
,
this phenomenon will play a crucial role in many of our constructions later.

The formula (
2.36
) can equivalently be written as the contour integral

\mathcal{F}(\bm{t},\epsilon)=\oint_{\mathrm{JK}}\prod_{a=1}^{r}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\frac{\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a}\phi_{a}t^{a}}}{\prod_{i=1}^{N}(\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}Q_{i}^{a})}\,.

(2.37)

To obtain this expression one trades the
\delta
-functions for their integral
representation and performs integration over coordinates
z_{i}
.
In particular this means that the integration of
\phi_{a}
is taken along
\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
.
To fully define the integral, one must then choose a closure for these contours,
and this is done according to the Jeffrey–Kirwan (JK) prescription
[
GHJ03
]
related
to the choice of chamber for
\bm{t}
.

The threefold
X
has
N
toric divisors defined by
z_{i}=0
for
i\in\{1,\dots,N\}
.
To each divisor one can associate a differential operator defined as follows

\cD_{i}=\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a=1}^{r}Q_{i}^{a}\frac{\partial}{\partial t^{a}}~.

(2.38)

such that
\cD_{i}\mathcal{F}
computes the equivariant volume of that divisor.
The equivariant volume
\mathcal{F}(\bm{t},\epsilon)
is annihilated by specific products of

\cD_{i}
’s whenever the corresponding divisors do not intersect.
These constraints on
\mathcal{F}
encode the equivariant cohomology relations of
X
.
In this sense, more generally
\mathcal{F}(\bm{t},\epsilon)
can be regarded as a generating
function of equivariant integrals of all relevant characteristic classes of
X
.

The equivariant volume admits a natural quantum deformation, where classical
geometry is replaced by a 2d gauged linear sigma model from a disk
D
into
X

[
Wit93
,
HR13
,
HO15
,
ST13
]
.
The symplectic quotient (
2.3
) is replaced by a two-dimensional

(2,2)
theory of
N
chiral fields coupled to
r
gauge fields with charges
Q_{i}^{a}
.
Equivariance can be introduced by identifying
\epsilon_{i}
as mass parameters of the chiral fields.
Localization allows to express the partition function as a contour integral over
the Coulomb branch, where the integrand includes contributions from 1-loop
determinants of the matter fields

\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon,\lambda)=\lambda^{-N}\oint_{\mathrm{QJK}}\prod_{a=1}^{r}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a}\phi_{a}t^{a}}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\Ga\Big(\frac{\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}Q_{i}^{a}}{\lambda}\Big)\,.

(2.39)

The GLSM partition function (
2.39
) can be regarded as a
direct generalization of the equivariant volume contour integral (
2.37
),
where the rational functions are replaced by
\Ga
functions.
Indeed the two are related by the limit

\mathcal{F}(\bm{t},\epsilon)=\lim_{\lambda^{-1}\to 0}\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon,\lambda)\,.

(2.40)

To make the match precise, one should also specify how the “quantum” contour
QJK is taken according to the choice of chamber for
\bm{t}

[
Bon+15
]
.
The relevant prescription for selected examples will be explained below, while
the more general definition of QJK contours can be found in
[
CPZ23
, Definition 3.1]
.

We refer to
\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon,\lambda)
as the
disk partition function
,
or
quantum volume
of
X
.
An important remark is that this should
not
be confused with the
topological string free energy, but should instead be regarded as a
regularization of the period associated to a space-filling brane on
X
.
The quantum volume is however related to the free energy, since by mirror symmetry
it encodes the genus zero closed Gromov–Witten invariants
[
CPZ23
]
.
Since it is a (non-compact) period,
\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon,\lambda)
satisfies equivariant
Picard–Fuchs (PF) equations, i.e. it is annihilated by the PF operator

\prod_{\{i|\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}>0\}}\left(\lambda^{-1}\cD_{i}\right)_{\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}}-\mathrm{e}^{-\lambda\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}t^{a}}\prod_{\{i|\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}\leq 0\}}\left(\lambda^{-1}\cD_{i}\right)_{-\sum_{a}\gamma_{a}Q^{a}_{i}}

(2.41)

where
(z)_{n}=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(z+k)
is the Pochhammer symbol
14
14

14

Observe that, for
n
a positive integer, the Pochhammer symbol
(z)_{n}
is polynomial in
z

z
with a differential operator such as
\lambda^{-1}\cD_{i}
.
(see (
A.1

\bm{\gamma}=(\gamma^{1},\dots,\gamma^{r})\in\mathbb{Z}^{r}
is an integer vector subject to
certain chamber-dependent positivity constraints.
Indeed, there is one such operator for each
\bm{\gamma}
, but only finitely many of
them are independent.
We refer to
[
CPZ23
]
for more details on the general structure of
equivariant PF operators, while their explicit form will be given explicitly
for the examples that we consider below.
These PDEs encode the equivariant quantum cohomology and reduce to the standard
Picard–Fuchs equations in the limit
\epsilon_{i}\to 0
.
Equivariant PF equations encode all equivariant periods of
X
,
including the overall volume
\mathcal{F}^{D}
, as solutions with prescribed semiclassical asymptotics.
In this sense, the set of equivariant PF equations can be taken as the defining
property of the quantum geometry described by the GLSM.
In particular, the GLSM partition function is the unique solution with asymptotics

\int_{X}\mathrm{e}^{\omega-H_{\epsilon}}\,\widehat{\Ga}(TX)

(2.42)

where
\omega
is the symplectic form of
X
,

is the Hamiltonian
for a one-parameter subgroup of the torus

and

,
NPZ22
,
CPZ23
]
.
Other interesting equivariant quantities can be studied by inserting different
equivariant classes under the integral (
2.39
).

Moreover, it is known that the disk function
\mathcal{F}^{D}
itself can be regarded as a classical integral of the form (
2.42
) together with the insertion of an
appropriate equivariant cohomology class. This class is the equivariant Givental

]
which allows to take into account all contributions coming from the instanton corrections. In fact, it was shown in
[

, Proposition 7.2]
that
\mathcal{F}^{D}
admits the alternative integral expression

\mathcal{F}^{D}=\int_{X}\mathrm{e}^{\omega-H_{\epsilon}}\,\widehat{\Ga}(TX)\,I_{X}=\int_{X^{T}}\frac{\iota^{\ast}(\mathrm{e}^{\omega-H_{\epsilon}}\,\widehat{\Ga}(TX)\,I_{X})}{e_{T}(N_{X^{T}/X})}

(2.43)

where the second equality follows from equivariant localization to the

T

.
In particular, we observe that the localization formula (
2.43
) is reminiscent of the definition of the “equivariant
H
-function” in
[
Iri09
, Sec. 4.4.2]
.

It is interesting to observe that
using the representation of the
\Ga

\Ga(x)=\int^{\infty}_{0}y^{x-1}\mathrm{e}^{-y}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y~,~~~~\Re(x)>0

(2.44)

the equivariant quantum volume (

\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon,\lambda)=\lambda^{-N}\int^{\mathrm{i}\infty}_{-\mathrm{i}\infty}\prod_{a=1}^{r}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi_{a}}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}~\mathrm{e}^{\sum_{a}\phi_{a}t^{a}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}y_{i}~y_{i}^{\lambda^{-1}(\epsilon_{i}+\sum_{a}\phi_{a}Q_{i}^{a})-1}\mathrm{e}^{-\sum_{i}y_{i}}

where the contour for the

This manipulation parallels the Hori–Vafa derivation of mirror symmetry for
toric Calabi–Yau threefolds
[

]
, but it has the advantage of
being on a firm mathematical footing thanks to equivariance,
since a proper analytic definition of the integral requires
\Re(\epsilon_{i})>0
.
Another advantage of this representation over the original QJK contour
prescription (

), is that in (

it becomes manifest that the quantum volume does not depend on the choice of chamber.
The smooth transition across different phases of the geometry of

is a familiar
feature of mirror symmetry, and can be understood physically as a consequence
of quantum effects that smooth out classical singularities
[

]
.

In this paper we will not develop a general theory of this type of integrals,
leaving further details about the general formalism to the upcoming work
[
CLZ24
]
.

\mathbb{C}^{3}

Having reviewed the classical notion of symplectic cuts in the previous section,
we now turn to discussing the role of cuts in topological string theory.
We start from the simplest example, namely the flat

While this CY3 is trivial as a toric quotient, it still admits interesting symplectic cuts.
Another feature of this example is that the key relations between open and closed

Symplectic cut and equivariant volumes

Consider

We denote its equivariant volume by

The twofold defines a cut of
\mathbb{C}^{3}

In the spirit of Lebesgue integration, one may regard
\mathcal{H}
as a sort of measure for computing the volume of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
by slicing it along copies of the 2-fold.

We now examine how the simple geometric considerations of the previous section generalize at the quantum level.
To this end we consider a GLSM from a disk to
\mathbb{C}^{3}

The trivial nature of this function is related to the absence of Gromov–Witten invariants for

3.3
Holomorphic disk potential from quantum Lebesgue measure

We have shown by direct computation that the quantum Lebesgue measure encodes
the quantum volume of the Calabi–Yau threefold. We will now show that
\mathcal{H}^{D}

also captures information about the open string superpotential in the presence
of an
A
-brane supported on the toric Lagrangian associated to the cut.

First we recall how the genus zero part of the toric brane superpotential is
computed by open-string mirror symmetry, following
[
AV00
,
AKV02
]
.
According to the definition of toric Lagrangian reviewed in Section (
2.4
),
we consider a brane defined by the affine line (
2.33
)

p_{2}-p_{3}=c\,,\qquad p_{1}-p_{2}=0\,.

(3.21)

Choosing the phase

gives a Lagrangian
L
that is a
U(1)^{2}
fibration over an affine
half-line
\ell

p_{1}=p_{2}=0

p_{3}=-c

b_{1}(L)=1
its
moduli space is one-dimensional, and corresponds to displacements of the line
parameterized by

p_{3}
-axis.
Other phases of the Lagrangian moduli space are reached by going through the
origin of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
where
L
becomes singular.

In the quantum theory this picture gets modified. An
A
-brane on
L
is described
at weak string coupling by an abelian local system, whose holonomy complexifies
c

into the open string modulus
x=\mathrm{e}^{c}
valued in
\mathbb{C}^{\ast}
.
The moduli space of the
A
brane in the absence of disk instantons would
therefore be the union of three cones, with a shared tip at
c=0
.
Disk instantons smooth out the singularity, and give a moduli space that is
topologically a sphere with three punctures.
This can be described as the algebraic curve

\Sigma:\ 1+x+y=0\quad\subset\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\times\mathbb{C}^{\ast}\,,

(3.22)

where a choice of framing for the toric brane has been implicitly made.

The geometry of
\Sigma
encodes contributions of open string instantons that
smooth out the singularity of the bare classical moduli space.
The disk potential can therefore be recovered from
\Sigma
, by the following formula

W_{{\mathrm{AKV}}}(x)=-\int^{x}\log(-y(x))\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\log x=\Li_{2}(-x)\,.

Next we explain how to recover (

) from the quantum Lebesgue measure.
As we explained in Section
2.4
, to a framed toric Lagrangian
L

we can naturally associate a symplectic cut. The key step in the identification
is the choice of affine hyperplane in the description of
L
, and to adopt it
as the defining plane for the cut, see (

).
We claim that the genus zero part of the open-string superpotential is related
to the monodromy of the quantum Lebesgue measure as follows

is an equivariant generalization of the open string

In these definitions we have promoted

from a real-valued variable to a

-plane.
Indeed, an important feature of both

For illustration we consider the region

6
Monodromy as domain wall tension

We have presented concrete evidence that the monodromy of the quantum Lebesgue measure computes the open topological string genus-zero superpotential for toric
A
-branes in several different examples. The key relation is

\partial_{c}W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Big(\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c+2\pi\mathrm{i},\epsilon)-\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)\Big)\,,

(6.1)

or its integrated version

W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\Big(\mathcal{F}^{D}_{<}(\bm{t},c+2\pi\mathrm{i},\epsilon)-\mathcal{F}^{D}_{<}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)\Big)~\,.

(6.2)

where
W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
is an equivariant generalization of the toric brane superpotential from which the latter can be recovered.

In this section we sketch the physical meaning of these formulae by drawing a parallel between our approach and previous works on open string mirror symmetry.
We stress from the outset that, while the overall physical interpretation generally agrees with previous proposals, the details of its realization will differ significantly.
As already remarked in the introduction, a key feature of the equivariant superpotential
W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
is that it comes from
\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
, which extends smoothly over the whole Kähler moduli space.
By contrast, open string superpotentials considered in previous literature are always defined within a single phase of the moduli space, and typically feature jumps from one phase to another.
In these regards equivariance acts as a regulator of certain divergencies in the open string potential, and
W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
provides a unifying description of previous results obtained in different phases.

7
Summary and outlook

In this paper we introduced and studied a quantum uplift of the symplectic cut construction in the setting of toric Calabi–Yau threefolds.
Our approach is based on the equivariant gauged linear sigma model, whose target is the threefold after cutting.
One the main characters in our story is the quantum Lebesgue measure
\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
associated to the choice of cut. The measure corresponds to the equivariant quantum volume of the divisor that is invariant under the
U(1)
action defining the cut.

The quantum measure encodes both closed and open topological string invariants.
Integrating
\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
over all values of the transverse modulus
c
recovers the equivariant quantum volume
\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon)
of the ambient CY3

\mathcal{F}^{D}(\bm{t},\epsilon)=\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}c\,,

(7.1)

which is further related to the closed Gromov–Witten potential.
The monodromy of
\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
along a circle in the complexified
c
-plane defines instead an equivariant open string superpotential
W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)

W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int^{c+2\pi\mathrm{i}}_{c}\mathcal{H}^{D}(\bm{t},s,\epsilon)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,.

(7.2)

The standard disk instanton potential for a toric Lagrangian associated to the cut can be recovered from
W(\bm{t},c,\epsilon)
by a suitable (phase dependent) Laurent expansion in the equivariant parameters
\epsilon_{i}
.
Through equations (
7.1
)-(
7.2
), the quantum Lebesgue measure provides a direct relation between closed and open string invariants.

Equivariant partition functions
\mathcal{F}^{D},\mathcal{H}^{D}
are globally defined across the entire Kähler moduli space (extended by open string moduli for the latter).
It is interesting to consider how this works out from the viewpoint of series expansions near large radius points and near conifold points, such as the ones we obtained.
To this end, recall that equivariant partition functions include not only instantons, but also semiclassical contributions due to constant maps (which would diverge for non-compact targets without equivariance).
Neither the instanton nor the semiclassical contributions are globally defined on their own, since they both exhibit jumps across different phases of the geometry.
Remarkably, their sum turns out to be globally well-defined (see also Remark
2.5
for a mathematical explanation of this fact).
This perspective extends and unifies previous large-volume computations of GW invariants, in very concrete ways.
In particular, the partition function of the equivariant GLSM produces all-instanton resummed expressions for
\mathcal{F}^{D},\mathcal{H}^{D}
that can be written down in closed form and which can be analytically continued across different phases with no difficulty.
A prototypical example of this are the large-radius and the conifold-point expressions (
4.13
)-(
4.14
) and (
4.15
) in terms of hypergeometric functions.

Further developments

Throughout this work we have come across several important questions that deserve to be studied in greater detail.

Normalization factors.

On a purely technical level, there are certain issues with the normalization of the physical superpotentials obtained in the non-equivariant limit.
In this paper we have largely neglected these, since the normalization can be unambiguously fixed by hand by matching the first open-string instanton contribution with that of the toric brane in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
.
Nevertheless, it would certainly be satisfying to find
a coherent explanation for the overall factors.

Mirror symmetry.

The global nature of quantum volumes
\mathcal{F}^{D},\mathcal{H}^{D}
hinges in a crucial way on equivariance.
In our story this is naturally introduced by a deformation of the GLSM, where
\epsilon_{i}
correspond to masses of chiral fields.
A simple manipulation of the GLSM Coulomb branch integral leads to an equivariant generalization of the Hori–Vafa mirror geometry, as remarked around (
2.45
).
On the one hand, this would be key to place (
6.8
) on a more firm footing, by providing the appropriate equivariant generalization of the right-hand side.
On the other hand, studying the equivariant deformation of Hori–Vafa mirror symmetry would also open up the possibility to study non-compact
B
-branes as mirror non-compact
A
-branes.
Due to multivaluedness of the holomorphic top-form, we expect calibration to produce non-compact mirror sLags very similar in nature to the non-compact
A
-branes studied in the context of wrapped Fukaya categories.
For mirrors of toric threefolds these can be studied concretely by a straightforward extension of the analysis of compact
A
-branes initiated in
[
BLR23
]
.

Other phases, other cuts and framing.

In this work we focused on a few examples, and analyzed the relation to open strings only in certain phases of the
c
-plane.
However a strength of our framework lies precisely in the global global nature of
\mathcal{H}^{D}
, whose different monodromies tie together equivariant superpotentials associated to different phases.
Some of the additional phases are most naturally accessible by changing the type of cut under consideration. In addition to this, the open string superpotential is known to change under so-called framing of the brane. Although we have partially addressed some of these points in the examples considered in this paper, it would be very interesting to extend our discussion further to include all these global issues in a coherent and systematic fashion.

Higher genus.

Although we have restricted attention to genus-zero worldsheets, there are good reasons to expect an extension of our observations to all-genera.
From the viewpoint of GLSMs, the summation over genera incurs in notorious technical complications related to the holomorphic anomaly equations
[
Ber+94
]
.
On the other hand, in recent years the study both closed and open instanton partition functions has advanced significantly through a variety of approaches, including the topological vertex
[
Aga+05
]
, the TS/ST correspondence
[
GHM16
]
,
the study of resurgent structures of both closed and open strings
[
Mar08a
,
Mar08
,
PS10
,
Cou+16
,
GM23
]
, and geometric interpretations and extensions of the latter from in terms of Riemann-Hilbert problems
[
Bri19
,
Bri20
,
CPT23
,
Ali+23
,
GHN23
,
AHT23
,
CLT20
,
GGH23
]
.
In many of these works, higher genus worldsheet instantons play an important role in the analytic continuation across the moduli space. In our work we observed that equivariance plays a similar role in the analytic continuation of genus zero string partition functions. It would therefore be interesting to explore the role of equivariance in the higher genus setting.

Lift to M-theory.

It would be certainly desirable to gain a better grasp on the physics behind quantum symplectic cuts in the context of string theory.
A promising direction seems to be through an uplift to M-theory
[
AKV02
,
AV01a
]
where the symplectic cut of
X
appears naturally.
In presence of the D6 brane on
L\times\mathbb{R}^{4}
, going to M-theory replaces
X
with a
G_{2}
manifold fibered by a circle over
X
which shrinks at
L
.
If we perform a 9/11 flip and compactify instead on a (choice of)
S^{1}
from the
U(1)^{3}
fibration of
X
we obtain a new manifold whose topology resembles the symplectic cut (
2.25
).

We plan to continue our discussion of these and other questions in upcoming works

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=\oint_{(1),(2)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{t\phi}\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{3}-\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{4}-\phi)

(B.1)

where the contour includes two towers of poles
-\epsilon_{1}-n
and
-\epsilon_{2}-n
for
n=0,1,2,\dots
.
After performing the integrals the answer can be rewritten as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{1}t}\Ga(\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1})\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{4})\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3},\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{4};\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2}+1;\mathrm{e}^{-t}\right)

(B.2)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{2}t}\Ga(\epsilon_{1}-\epsilon_{2})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{4})\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3},\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{4};\epsilon_{2}-\epsilon_{1}+1;\mathrm{e}^{-t}\right)\,.

The disk partition function satifies the following equivariant PF equation

\Big(\cD_{1}\cD_{2}-\mathrm{e}^{-t}\cD_{3}\cD_{4}\Big)\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)\equiv\Big((\epsilon_{1}+\partial_{t})(\epsilon_{2}+\partial_{t})-\mathrm{e}^{-t}(\epsilon_{3}-\partial_{t})(\epsilon_{4}-\partial_{t})\Big)\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=0

(B.3)

and semiclassically it goes to the equivariant volume (with some insertions of
\Ga
-class).
It is natural to write it in the following way

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)={\rm{s.c.}}+t\Li_{2}(\mathrm{e}^{-t})-2\Li_{3}(\mathrm{e}^{-t})+O(\epsilon)~,

(B.4)

where the semiclassical terms contain singularities in
\epsilon
’s and instanton terms (the poles
n=1,2,\dots
)
are regular (for more details see
[
CPZ23
]
).

Using the functional definition (
B.2
) and the identity (
A.7
) we can rewrite the disk function as

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=\frac{\Ga(\epsilon_{3}+\epsilon_{2})\Ga(\epsilon_{4}+\epsilon_{1})\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{4})}{\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}+\epsilon_{4})}\times\\
\times\mathrm{e}^{-\epsilon_{1}t}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{4},\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3};\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}+\epsilon_{4};1-\mathrm{e}^{-t})\,,

(B.5)

or, equivalently, using (
A.8
) we can write

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=\frac{\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{4})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{4})}{\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}+\epsilon_{4})}\times\\
\times\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon_{3}t}\,{}_{2}F_{1}(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3},\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3};\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3}+\epsilon_{4};1-\mathrm{e}^{t})~.

(B.6)

This form of the disk partition function is naturally related to the chamber
t<0
and can also be obtained via the following contour integral

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=

\displaystyle-\oint_{(3)(4)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{t\phi}\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{3}-\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{4}-\phi)

(B.7)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon_{3}t}\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3})\Ga(\epsilon_{4}-\epsilon_{3})\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{3},\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{3};\epsilon_{3}-\epsilon_{4}+1;\mathrm{e}^{t}\right)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{e}^{\epsilon_{4}t}\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{4})\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{4})\Ga(\epsilon_{3}-\epsilon_{4})\,{}_{2}F_{1}\left(\epsilon_{1}+\epsilon_{4},\epsilon_{2}+\epsilon_{4};\epsilon_{4}-\epsilon_{3}+1;\mathrm{e}^{t}\right)

where one can use (
A.7

B.6

The extra minus in front of the contour integral (
B.7
)
appears due to the fact that closing the contour along the imaginary

We remark that all these different formulas can obtained from the following integral expression

(B.8)

which is the representation (
2.45

Appendix C
Disk partition function for local
\mathbb{P}^{2}

Using the conventions from Section
5

\mathbb{P}^{2}

for
t>0

\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=\oint_{(1),(2),(3)}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\phi}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\,\mathrm{e}^{t\phi}\Ga(\epsilon_{1}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{2}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{3}+\phi)\Ga(\epsilon_{4}-3\phi)~,

\Big(\cD_{1}\cD_{2}\cD_{3}-\mathrm{e}^{-t}(2+\cD_{4})(1+\cD_{4})\cD_{4}\Big)\mathcal{F}^{D}(t,\epsilon)=0~.

(C.2)

The expansion in equivariant parameters
\epsilon

]
