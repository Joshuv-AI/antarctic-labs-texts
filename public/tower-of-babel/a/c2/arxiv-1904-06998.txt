An optimal polynomial approximation of Brownian motion
James Foster, Terry Lyons, Harald Oberhauser

Abstract
In this paper, we will present a strong (or pathwise) approximation of standard Brownian motion by a class of orthogonal polynomials. The coefficients that are obtained from the expansion of Brownian motion in this polynomial basis are independent Gaussian random variables. Therefore it is practical (requires N N independent Gaussian coefficients) to generate an approximate sample path of Brownian motion that respects integration of polynomials with degree less than N N . Moreover, since these orthogonal polynomials appear naturally as eigenfunctions of the Brownian bridge covariance function, the proposed approximation is optimal in a certain weighted L 2 ​ ( ℙ ) L^{2}(\mathbb{P}) sense. In addition, discretizing Brownian paths as piecewise parabolas gives a locally higher order numerical method for stochastic differential equations (SDEs) when compared to the piecewise linear approach. We shall demonstrate these ideas by simulating Inhomogeneous Geometric Brownian Motion (IGBM). This numerical example will also illustrate the deficiencies of the piecewise parabola approximation when compared to a new version of the asymptotically efficient log-ODE (or Castell-Gaines) method.

Introduction

Brownian motion is a central object for modelling real-world
systems that evolve under the influence of random perturbations
[
1
]
. In applications
where methods discretize Brownian motion, usually only increments of the path are
generated
[
2
]
. In this setting, the best
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
approximation of Brownian motion that
is measurable with respect to these increments is given by the piecewise linear path
that agrees on discretization points
[
3
]
. This motivates the following natural question:

Are there better discrete approximations of Brownian motion than piecewise linear?

The next simplest approximant would be a piecewise polynomial, though it is not clear
whether this would be advantageous for tackling problems such as SDE simulation.
This paper can be viewed as a logical continuation of
[
4
]
, where a polynomial wavelet
representation of Brownian motion was proposed. These wavelets were constructed to
capture certain “geometrical features” of the path, namely the integrals of the
Brownian motion against monomials. We shall investigate the practical applications
of these polynomials and their geometrical features in the numerical analysis of SDEs.

Theorem 1
.

Let

[\hskip 0.28453pt0,1]

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u^{k}\,dW_{u}^{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}u^{k}\,dW_{u}\hskip 0.35565pt,\hskip 8.53581pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69054ptk=0\hskip 0.7113pt,1\hskip 0.7113pt,\cdots\hskip 0.7113pt,n-1\hskip 0.7113pt.

, where

The above theorem has a simple yet striking conclusion, namely that polynomials can
be unbiased approximants of Brownian motion. In addition, the first non-trivial case
(

One reason is that parabolas can capture the “space-time area” of Brownian motion.

Brownian motion can be expressed as a (random) parabola plus independent noise.
Moreover, the approximating parabola has the same increment and time integral as the original path.

Therefore discretizing Brownian motion using a piecewise parabola gives a locally high
order methodology for numerically solving one-dimensional SDEs. However, since
certain triple iterated integrals of Brownian motion and time are partially matched by
these parabolas, we expect this method to have only an
O(h)
rate of convergence
(where
h

Theorem 2

Let
\wideparen{W}
be the (unique) quadratic polynomial with a root at

\displaystyle\wideparen{W}_{1}=W_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt,\hskip 14.22636pt\int_{0}^{1}\wideparen{W}_{u}\,du=\int_{0}^{1}W_{u}\,du\hskip 0.7113pt.

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\int_{0}^{1}W_{u}^{2}\,du\,\Big|\,W_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt,\int_{0}^{1}W_{u}\,du\hskip 0.7113pt\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\wideparen{W}_{u}^{2}\,du+\frac{1}{15}\hskip 0.7113pt.

\displaystyle I_{k}:=\int_{0}^{1}B_{t}\cdot\frac{e_{k}\left(t\right)}{t(1-t)}\,dt,

\displaystyle\left\|X\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{P})}:=\sqrt{\hskip 0.7113pt\mathbb{E}\hskip-0.7113pt\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\int_{0}^{1}\left(X_{s}\right)^{2}\,d\mu(s)\right]},

Proof.

Our argument is that of the Karhunen-Loève theorem in general

Note that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\int_{0}^{1}(B_{s})^{2}\,d\mu(s)\right]=\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{E}\big[(B_{s})^{2}\big]\,d\mu(s)=\int_{0}^{1}s(1-s)\cdot\frac{1}{s(1-s)}\,ds=1<\infty.

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(B_{t}-\sum_{k=1}^{N}I_{k}e_{k}(t)\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle=k_{B}(t)+\mathbb{E}\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\sum_{i,j=1}^{N}I_{i}I_{j}\,e_{i}(t)\hskip 0.7113pte_{j}(t)\right]-2\hskip 0.7113pt\mathbb{E}\left[\hskip 0.7113ptB_{t}\sum_{k=1}^{N}I_{k}e_{k}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=k_{B}(t)+\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}e_{i}^{2}(t)-2\hskip 0.7113pt\mathbb{E}\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\sum_{i=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}B_{t}\,e_{i}(s)\hskip 0.7113pte_{i}(t)\,d\mu(s)\right]

\displaystyle=k_{B}(t)-\sum_{i=1}^{N}\lambda_{i}e_{i}^{2}(t),

which converges to
0
by Mercer’s theorem (
8
).

All that remains is to prove optimality for the truncated series expansions of (
6
).
Let
\left\{f_{k}\right\}_{k\geq 1}
denote an orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([\hskip 0.28453pt0,1],\mu)
such that

\displaystyle B=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}J_{k}f_{k},\hskip 8.53581pt\text{where}\hskip 8.53581ptJ_{k}:=\int_{0}^{1}B_{t}\,f_{k}(t)\,d\mu(t),\hskip 8.53581pt\forall k\geq 1.

For
n\geq 1
, we consider an error process associated with the above:
r_{n}:=\sum\limits_{k=n+1}^{\infty}J_{k}f_{k}\,
.

Then the square
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
norm of the
n
-th error process admits the following expansion,

\displaystyle\left\|r_{n}(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\hskip-0.7113pt\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}J_{i}J_{j}f_{i}(t)f_{j}(t)\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\hskip-0.7113pt\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}B_{s}B_{t}\,f_{i}(s)f_{j}(t)\,d\mu(s)\,d\mu(t)\right]\hskip-0.7113ptf_{i}(t)f_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=n+1}^{\infty}\sum_{j=n+1}^{\infty}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}K_{B}(s,t)\,f_{i}(s)f_{j}(t)\,d\mu(s)\,d\mu(t)\right)f_{i}(t)f_{j}(t).

Integrating the above with respect to
\mu
and using the orthogonality of
\{f_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
gives

\displaystyle\left\|r_{n}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}=\int_{0}^{1}\left\|r(t)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}\,d\mu(t)=\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}K_{B}(s,t)f_{k}(s)f_{k}(t)\,d\mu(s)\,d\mu(t).

Note that any optimal orthonormal basis of
L^{2}([\hskip 0.28453pt0,1],\mu)
solves the following problem:

\displaystyle\min_{f_{k}}\left\|r_{n}\right\|_{L_{\mu}^{2}(\mathbb{P})}^{2}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{subject to}\hskip 8.53581pt\left\|f_{k}\right\|_{L^{2}([\hskip 0.28453pt0,1],\mu)}=1.

By introducing Lagrange multipliers
\nu_{k}
, we wish to find functions
\{f_{k}\}
that minimize

\displaystyle E_{n}[\left\{f_{k}\right\}]:=\hskip-2.84526pt\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-0.99585ptK_{B}(s,t)f_{k}(s)f_{k}(t)d\mu(s)d\mu(t)-\nu_{k}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\hskip-1.42262pt\big(f_{k}(s)\big)^{2}d\mu(s)-1\right)\hskip-0.99585pt.

We will now consider the following square integrable functions, defined for
s,t\in\left(0,1\right)
:

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{k}(t):=f_{k}(t)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\tilde{K}_{B}(s,t):=K_{B}(s,t)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{s(1-s)}}\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}}\,.

Therefore it is enough to find a family of functions
\{\tilde{f}_{k}\}
in
L^{2}([\hskip 0.28453pt0,1])
which minimizes

\displaystyle\tilde{E}_{n}[\{\tilde{f}_{k}\}]:=\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{K}_{B}(s,t)\tilde{f}_{k}(s)\tilde{f}_{k}(t)\,ds\,dt-\nu_{k}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\big(\tilde{f}_{k}(s)\big)^{2}\,ds-1\right)\hskip-0.7113pt.

To find a minimizer, we set the functional derivative of
\tilde{E}_{n}
with respect to
\tilde{f}_{k}
to zero.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\tilde{E}_{n}}{\partial\tilde{f}_{k}(t)}=2\int_{0}^{1}\tilde{K}_{B}(s,t)\tilde{f}_{k}(s)\,ds-2\nu_{k}\tilde{f}_{k}(t)=0.

By using the definitions of
\tilde{f}_{k}
and
\tilde{K}_{B}
, it is trivial to show the above is equivalent to

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}K_{B}(s,t)f_{k}(s)\,d\mu(s)=\nu_{k}f_{k}(t),

which is satisfied if and only if
f_{k}
are eigenfunctions of
T_{K}
.
∎

2.1
Properties of orthogonal polynomials

Although Theorem
5
and
Theorem
6
are interesting results from a theoretical point of view, both lack an
explicit construction of the polynomials
\left\{e_{k}\right\}
that could be implemented in practice.
On the other hand, it was shown that the defining eigenfunction property of each
e_{k}

implies that its derivative
e_{k}^{\prime}
is proportional to the
k
-th shifted Legendre polynomial.
Hence the family
\left\{e_{k}\right\}
is the (normalized) shifted
(\alpha,\beta)
-Jacobi polynomials but with

\alpha=\beta=-1
. Since Jacobi polynomials are typically studied with
\alpha,\beta>-1
, it is necessary to show there exists a well-defined limit when the parameters approach
-1
.

Fig. 4:
Sample paths of Brownian motion with corresponding polynomial approximations.

Definition 8
.

For
k\geq 2
, the
k
-th degree
(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)
-Jacobi polynomial
P_{k}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}
is

\displaystyle P_{k}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}:=\lim_{\alpha,\beta\rightarrow-1^{+}}P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}.

Naturally, for this definition to be unambiguous, we will require the following lemma.

Lemma 9
.

Let
P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
denote the
k
-th degree
(\alpha,\beta)
-Jacobi polynomial on
[-1,1]
.
Then for
k\geq 2
, there exists a real-valued polynomial
P_{k}
such that

\big\|P_{k}-P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}\big\|_{\infty}\rightarrow 0
as
\alpha,\beta\rightarrow-1^{+}
.

Proof.

Below is an identity for Jacobi polynomials, with
\alpha,\beta>-1
, given in
[
21
]
.

\displaystyle P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{k+\alpha+\beta+1}{2}\int_{-1}^{x}P_{k-1}^{(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}(u)\,du,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69054ptk\geq 2.

(17)

Therefore, we shall define the
k
-th degree polynomial
P_{k}
over the interval
[-1,1]
by

\displaystyle P_{k}(x):=\frac{k-1}{2}\int_{-1}^{x}P_{k-1}^{(0,0)}(u)\,du,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69054ptk\geq 2.

(18)

It is straightforward to verify that
\lim_{\hskip 0.7113pt\alpha,\beta\,\rightarrow\,0}\|\hskip 1.42262ptP_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}-\hskip 0.7113ptP_{n}^{(0,0)}\hskip 0.7113pt\|_{\infty}=0
for
n\in\{0,1\}
.
So by induction and the recurrence relation for Jacobi polynomials (see
[
21
]
), we have:

\displaystyle\big\|\hskip 1.42262ptP_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}-\hskip 0.7113ptP_{n}^{(0,0)}\hskip 0.7113pt\big\|_{\infty}\rightarrow 0\hskip 7.11317pt\text{as}\,\,\,\alpha,\beta\,\rightarrow\,0,

for all
n\geq 0
. Hence by the dominated convergence theorem with (
17
) and (
18
), it follows that
P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
will converge pointwise to
P_{k}
as
\alpha,\beta\,\rightarrow\,-1
for each
k\geq 1
. Finally, the result follows as
P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
and
P_{k}
are always polynomials with degree
k
.
∎

Using the above definition for
(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)
-Jacobi polynomials, we can give an
explicit formula for the orthonormal polynomials
\{e_{k}\}_{k\geq 1}
appearing in Theorems

and

Theorem 10
.

Suppose each
e_{k}
has a positive leading coefficient. Then for
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle e_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{k(k+1)(2k+1)}\,P_{k+1}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(2t-1),\hskip 11.38109pt\forall t\in[\hskip 0.28453pt0,1].

(19)

Proof.

The following identity for
(\alpha,\beta)
-Jacobi polynomials is stated in
[
21
]
:

\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}\big(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\big)^{2}\,dx=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\,\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,n!}\,,

for
n\geq 1
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
. Applying the change of variables,
t:=\frac{1}{2}(x+1)
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}t^{\beta}(1-t)^{\alpha}\big(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(2t-1)\big)^{2}dt=\frac{1}{2n+\alpha+\beta+1}\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\,\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)\,n!}\,,

for
n\geq 1
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
. By definition
8

\alpha,\beta\rightarrow-1^{+}
will yield

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\left(P_{n}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(2t-1)\right)^{2}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n-1}\frac{(n-1)!\,(n-1)!}{n!\,(n-2)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2n-1}\frac{n-1}{n}\,,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 5.69054ptn\geq 2.

Therefore by setting
k:=n-1
, we have

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{t(1-t)}\left(\frac{1}{k}\sqrt{k(k+1)(2k+1)}\,P_{n}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(2t-1)\right)^{2}\,dt=1,\hskip 14.22636pt\text{for all}\hskip 3.55658ptk\geq 1.

Recall that
e_{k}^{\prime}
is proportional to the

.
Similarly, we saw in the proof of Lemma

that the derivative of

is
\frac{k}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptP_{k}^{(0,0)}
.
As

are zero at their respective endpoints, we have that each

∎

Having identified an explicit formula for the eigenfunctions
\{e_{k}\}
in (

Jacobi-like polynomials
\big\{P_{k}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}\big\}

The first approach is to use the three-term recurrence relation in the theorem below.

Theorem 11

(Recurrence relation for
(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)
-Jacobi polynomials)
.

For
n\geq 2

\displaystyle n(n+2)P_{n+2}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)

\displaystyle=(n+1)(2n+1)\hskip 0.7113ptxP_{n+1}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)-n(n+1)P_{n}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)\hskip 0.7113pt,

(20)

where the initial polynomials are given by

\displaystyle P_{2}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\hskip 0.7113pt(x-1)(x+1)\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle P_{3}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptx(x-1)(x+1)\hskip 0.7113pt.

Proof.

The below recurrence relation for Jacobi polynomials is presented in
[
21
]
,

\displaystyle 2(k+1)(k+\alpha+\beta+1)(2k+\alpha+\beta)P_{k+1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt=(2k+\alpha+\beta+1)\hskip-0.99585pt\left((2k+\alpha+\beta)(2k+\alpha+\beta+2)x+\alpha^{2}-\beta^{2}\right)P_{k}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 24.18483pt-2(k+\alpha)(k+\beta)(2k+\alpha+\beta+2)\,P_{k-1}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\hskip 0.7113pt,

for
k\geq 1
and
\alpha,\beta>-1
. By definition
8
, it is possible to take the limit
\alpha,\beta\rightarrow 1^{+}

provided that
k\geq 3
. Therefore, taking this limit and setting

\displaystyle 4n^{2}(n+2)P_{n+2}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)=4n(n+1)(2n+1)xP_{n+1}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)-4n^{2}(n+1)\,P_{n}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)\hskip 0.7113pt,

Finally the below formula, stated in
[

\displaystyle P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{n}}{2^{n}n!}\left(1-x\right)^{-\alpha}\left(1+x\right)^{-\beta}\frac{d^{n}}{dx^{n}}\hskip-0.7113pt\left(\left(1-x\right)^{\alpha+n}\left(1+x\right)^{\beta+n}\right),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{for}\,\,n\geq 2\hskip 0.7113pt.

In addition to computing these polynomials via a recurrence relation, it is also

as the difference of two (rescaled) Legendre polynomials.
Since the Legendre polynomials already have efficient implementations in the majority

Theorem 12

(Relationship between the Jacobi-like and Legendre polynomials)
.

For
n\geq 1

\displaystyle P_{n+1}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)=\frac{n}{4n+2}\hskip 0.7113pt\big(Q_{n+1}(x)-Q_{n-1}(x)\big),

denotes the

-th degree Legendre polynomial defined on
[-1,1]

Recall that
\frac{d}{dx}\big(P^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}_{n+1}\hskip 0.7113pt\big)=\frac{n}{2}P^{(0,0)}_{n}
for
n\geq 1
, where
P^{(0,0)}_{n}(=Q_{n})
is the
n
-th degree Legendre polynomial. Therefore differentiating both sides of (

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptn(n+1)(n+2)\hskip 0.7113ptQ_{n+1}(x)

\displaystyle=(n+1)(2n+1)P_{n+1}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)+\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptn(n+1)(2n+1)\hskip 0.7113ptx\hskip 0.7113ptQ_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt-\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptn(n-1)(n+1)\hskip 0.7113ptQ_{n-1}(x)\hskip 0.7113pt.

Hence by simplifying and rearranging the above, we have that for
n\geq 1

\displaystyle(2n+1)P_{n+1}^{(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)}(x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptn\hskip 0.7113pt\big(Q_{n+1}(x)-Q_{n-1}(x)\big)

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\frac{1}{2}\hskip 0.7113ptn\hskip 0.7113pt\big((n+1)\hskip 0.7113ptQ_{n+1}(x)-(2n+1)\hskip 0.7113ptx\hskip 0.7113ptQ_{n}(x)+n\hskip 0.7113ptQ_{n-1}(x)\big).

We see the last term is zero by a recurrence relation for Legendre polynomials
[
20
]
.
∎

In addition to viewing the polynomials
\{e_{k}\}
as orthogonal with respect to the

In particular, for
1\leq k\leq n-1

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\int_{0<s_{1}<\cdots<s_{k}<1}e_{n}(s_{1})\,ds_{1}\cdots\,ds_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}e_{n}(s)\,d\hskip-0.7113pt\left(\frac{1}{k!}s^{k}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{(k-1)!}\int_{0}^{1}s^{k-1}e_{n}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{k!}\int_{0}^{1}s^{k}e_{n}^{\prime}(s)\,ds

\displaystyle=0\hskip 0.7113pt,\hskip 55.48286pt(\text{by the orthogonality of $e_{n}^{\prime}$}).

,

is a polynomial with degree

that has roots at
0
and
1
as

-th iterated

k
-th polynomial

.

To conclude this section, we will address the relationship between the orthogonal

From a numerical perspective, the most challenging terms presented in (
22

that involve non-trivial third order iterated integrals of Brownian motion and time.
Moreover, the most significant source of discretization error that high order numerical
methods will experience is generally due to approximating these stochastic integrals.
By representing Brownian motion as a (random) polynomial plus independent noise,
we shall derive a new optimal and unbiased estimator for these third order integrals.

Theorem 14
.

Let

[\hskip 0.28453pt0,1]

Let

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}u^{k}\,dW_{u}^{n}

is a centered Gaussian process independent of

Furthermore,

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(Z^{n}_{s},Z^{n}_{t})=K_{B}(s,t)-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{k}e_{k}(s)e_{k}(t),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69054pts,t\in[\hskip 0.28453pt0,1],

where

denotes the standard Brownian bridge covariance function and
\{\lambda_{k}\}
,
\{e_{k}\}

are the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions that were defined in the proof of Theorem
5

Proof.

It follows from the integration by parts formula that
W^{n}
matches the
increment and
n-1

Hence
W^{n}
is also the polynomial defined in Theorem

where

\displaystyle W^{n}

\displaystyle=W_{1}e_{0}+\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}I_{k}e_{k}\hskip 0.35565pt,

\displaystyle Z^{n}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}I_{k}e_{k}\hskip 0.35565pt.

Then by Theorem

is a centered Gaussian process that is independent of

\displaystyle\operatorname{cov}(Z^{n}_{s},Z^{n}_{t})

\displaystyle=\operatorname{cov}\left(\sum_{i=n}^{\infty}I_{i}e_{i}(s),\sum_{j=n}^{\infty}I_{j}e_{j}(t)\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}e_{k}(s)e_{k}(t)

\displaystyle=K_{B}(s,t)-\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\lambda_{k}e_{k}(s)e_{k}(t),\hskip 8.53581pt\text{for}\hskip 5.69054pts,t\in[\hskip 0.28453pt0,1].

f_{1}
.
Then
F
is smooth, and it follows from the classical Taylor’s theorem for ODEs that

\displaystyle Y_{1}^{\text{log}}

\displaystyle=y_{s}+F(y_{s})+\frac{1}{2}F^{\prime}(y_{s})F(y_{s})+\frac{1}{6}F^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(F(y_{s}),F(y_{s}))+\frac{1}{6}F^{\prime}(y_{s})F^{\prime}(y_{s})F(y_{s})

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\frac{1}{24}F^{\prime}(y_{s})F^{\prime}(y_{s})F^{\prime}(y_{s})F(y_{s})+\frac{1}{24}F^{\prime}(y_{s})F^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(F(y_{s}),F(y_{s}))

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\,\frac{1}{8}F^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(F^{\prime}(y_{s})F(y_{s}),F(y_{s}))+\frac{1}{24}F^{\prime\prime\prime}(y_{s})(F(y_{s}),F(y_{s}),F(y_{s}))

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+\frac{1}{24}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{4}\frac{d^{5}}{du^{5}}\big(Y^{\text{log}}\big)(u)\,du.

We shall first consider the remainder term, which can be directly estimated as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\,\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{4}\frac{d^{5}}{du^{5}}\big(Y^{\text{log}}\big)(u)\,du\,\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}

\displaystyle\leq\sup_{u\in[\hskip 0.28453pt0,1]}\left\|\frac{d^{5}}{du^{5}}\big(Y^{\text{log}}(u)\big)\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{P})}.

One can define the degree of each term in the above Taylor expansion by counting
the number of times functions from
\{F,F^{\prime},F^{\prime\prime},\cdots\}
appear. Therefore, after expanding the fifth derivative of
Y^{\text{log}}
we can see
that the remainder term has a degree of five.
Since the largest component of
F
is
f_{1}(\cdot)\hskip 0.7113ptW_{s,t}
, both
F
and its derivatives are
O(h^{\frac{1}{2}})
.
Hence the remainder term in the above Taylor expansion will be
O(h^{\frac{5}{2}})
as in (
23
).
Moreover, the only terms of degree four that are not
O(h^{\frac{5}{2}})
are those involving
W_{s,t}^{4}
.

It is now enough to analyse just the terms appearing in the first line of the expansion.
By substituting the formula for
F
given by (
27
) into the first line and then rearranging
the resulting terms, we can obtain a Taylor expansion for
Y_{1}^{\text{log}}
that resembles (
22
) as

\displaystyle F(y_{s})

\displaystyle=f_{0}(y_{s})\hskip 0.7113pth+f_{1}(y_{s})\hskip 0.7113ptW_{s,t}+\big(f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})-f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}(y_{s})\big)\hskip 0.7113pthH_{s,t}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\big(f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})+f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{1}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt-2\hskip 0.7113ptf_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})-2\hskip 0.7113ptf_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{0}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}(y_{s})+f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{1}(y_{s}),f_{0}(y_{s}))\big)\hskip-1.42262pt\left(\frac{1}{30}\hskip 0.7113pth^{2}+\frac{3}{5}\hskip 0.7113pthH_{s,t}^{2}\right)\hskip-1.42262pt,

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(y_{s})F(y_{s})

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})\hskip 0.7113ptW_{s,t}^{2}+\big(f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})+f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}(y_{s})\big)\hskip 0.7113pthW_{s,t}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\big(f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})-f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}(y_{s})

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt+f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{1}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))-f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{0}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))\big)\hskip 0.7113pthW_{s,t}H_{s,t}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}(y_{s})\hskip 0.7113pth^{2}+O\big(h^{\frac{5}{2}}\big),

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(y_{s})F^{\prime}(y_{s})F(y_{s})

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})\hskip 0.7113ptW_{s,t}^{3}+f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})\hskip 0.7113pthW_{s,t}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+\big(f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}(y_{s})+f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{1}^{\prime}(y_{s})f_{0}(y_{s})\big)\hskip 0.7113pthW_{s,t}^{2}+O\big(h^{\frac{5}{2}}\big),

\displaystyle F^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})\big(F(y_{s}),F(y_{s})\big)

\displaystyle=f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{1}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))\hskip 0.7113ptW_{s,t}^{3}+2\hskip 0.7113ptf_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{0}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))\hskip 0.7113pthW_{s,t}^{2}

\displaystyle\hskip 14.22636pt+f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y_{s})(f_{1}(y_{s}),f_{1}(y_{s}))\hskip 0.7113pthW_{s,t}^{2}+O\big(h^{\frac{5}{2}}\big).

Therefore, by summing the above formulae for
F
(and its derivatives) we can derive an expansion of
Y_{1}^{\text{log}}
in terms of
f_{0},f_{1}
and
\big(h,W_{s,t},H_{s,t}\big)
that has an
O\big(h^{\frac{5}{2}}\big)
remainder.
By comparing this with the stochastic Taylor expansion (
22
), the result (
29
) follows.

Arguing (
30
) is fairly straightforward and does not require extensive computations.
Using the substitution
\wideparen{Y}_{u}=z_{\frac{1}{h}(u-s)}
for
u\in[s,t]
, the ODE (
28
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle d\wideparen{Y}_{u}

\displaystyle=f_{0}\big(\hskip 1.42262pt\wideparen{Y}_{u}\big)\hskip 0.7113ptdu+f_{1}\big(\hskip 1.42262pt\wideparen{Y}_{u}\big)\hskip 0.7113ptd\wideparen{W}_{u}\hskip 0.35565pt,

(31)

\displaystyle\wideparen{Y}_{s}

\displaystyle=y_{s}\hskip 0.35565pt,

where
\wideparen{W}
denotes the Brownian parabola defined by
(W_{s,t},H_{s,t})
on the interval
[s,t]
.

By emulating the derivation of the Stratonovich-Taylor expansion (
22
), it is possible
to Taylor expand (
31
) in the same fashion. The only difference is that Stratonovich
integrals with respect to
W
are replaced with Riemann-Stieltjes integrals against
\wideparen{W}
.

In particular, by the change-of-variable formula for ODEs (exercise 3.17) given in
[
24
]
, we see that the remainder term of such a Taylor expansion will have the below form:

\displaystyle\wideparen{R}\,=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1},\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots,\hskip 0.7113pti_{n}\in\{0,1\}\\
i_{1}\hskip 0.7113pt+\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots\hskip 0.7113pt+\hskip 0.7113pti_{n}\hskip 0.7113pt=\hskip 0.99585pt2n-4\end{subarray}}\int_{s\hskip 0.7113pt<\hskip 0.35565ptr_{1}\hskip 0.35565pt<\hskip 0.35565pt\cdots\hskip 0.35565pt<\hskip 0.35565ptr_{n}<\hskip 0.7113ptt}f_{i_{1},\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots,\hskip 0.7113pti_{n}}\big(\hskip 1.42262pt\wideparen{Y}_{r_{1}}\big)-f_{i_{1},\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots,\hskip 0.7113pti_{n}}\big(\hskip 1.42262pt\wideparen{Y}_{s}\hskip 0.7113pt\big)\,d\wideparen{W}_{r_{1}}^{i_{1}}\,\cdots\,d\wideparen{W}_{r_{n}}^{i_{n}}\hskip 0.7113pt,

where we have identified an additional “zero” coordinate of
\wideparen{W}
with time,
\wideparen{W}_{t}^{0}:=t
, and for each index
(i_{1},\cdots i_{n})
, the function
f_{i_{1},\hskip 0.7113pt\cdots,\hskip 0.7113pti_{n}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
consists of finitely many compositions of
f_{0},f_{1}
along with their derivatives (and thus is Lipschitz continuous).

Therefore each term in the expansion of (
31
) can be estimated in
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
by applying
the natural Brownian scaling to the corresponding iterated integral of
\wideparen{W}
with time.
As before, the largest differences are the
O(h^{2})
terms involving third order integrals.
Fortunately, iterated integrals of the Brownian parabola can be computed explicitly:

\displaystyle\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u}\int_{s}^{v}\circ\,dW_{r}\circ\,dW_{v}\,du-\int_{s}^{t}\int_{s}^{u}\int_{s}^{v}\,d\wideparen{W}_{r}\,d\wideparen{W}_{v}\,du

\displaystyle=L_{s,t}-\frac{3}{5}H_{s,t}^{2}\hskip 0.35565pt.

The result (
30
) is now a direct consequence of Theorem
3.22
along with the above.

Theorem
3.29
shows that both methods give a one-step approximation error of
O(h^{2})
.
This means that the log-ODE and parabola-ODE methods are both locally high order;
however there is a significant difference in how these methods propagate local errors.
The reason is that the
O(h^{2})
components of the log-ODE local errors give a martingale,
whilst the
O(h^{2})
part for each parabola-ODE local error has non-zero expectation.
Thus the log-ODE method is globally high order whilst the parabola method is not.
However, since the parabola-ODE method is straightforward to implement and locally
high order, one could expect it to perform well compared to other low order methods.
In the numerical example, we shall see that the parabola method has the same order
of convergence as the piecewise linear approach but gives significantly smaller errors.
To conclude this section, we will present the orders of convergence for both methods.

Definition 3.31

(Strong convergence)
.

A numerical solution
Y
for (
21
) is said
to converge in a strong sense with order
\alpha
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\displaystyle\left\|Y_{N}-y_{T}\right\|_{L^{2}\left(\mathbb{P}\right)}\leq Ch^{\alpha},

for all sufficiently small step sizes
h=\frac{T}{N}
.

Definition 3.32

(Weak convergence)
.

A numerical solution
Y
for (
21
) is said
to converge in a weak sense with order
\beta
if for any polynomial
p
there exists
C_{p}>0

such that

\displaystyle\big|\,\mathbb{E}\hskip-0.7113pt\left[\,p\hskip 0.7113pt(\hskip 0.7113ptY_{N})\right]-\mathbb{E}\hskip-0.7113pt\left[\,p\hskip 0.7113pt(\hskip 0.7113pty_{T})\right]\big|\leq C_{p}\,h^{\beta},

for all sufficiently small step sizes
h=\frac{T}{N}
.

Theorem 3.33

(Orders of convergence)
.

For a general SDE (
21
), the log-ODE method converges in a strong sense with order 1.5 and a weak sense with order 2.0.
The parabola-ODE method converges in both a strong and weak sense with order 1.0.

Proof 3.34
.

Note that Theorem
3.29
establishes the Taylor expansions of both methods.
The strong convergence can then be shown as in the proof of Theorem 11.5.1 in
[
22
]
.
Moreover, the proof of Theorem 11.5.1 also provides the orders of strong convergence.
Similarly weak convergence follows directly from the Taylor expansions (
29
) & (
30
),
and the rate of convergence can be shown as in the proof of Theorem 14.5.2 in
[
22
]
.

4
A numerical example

We shall demonstrate the ideas presented so far
using various discretizations of Inhomogeneous Geometric Brownian Motion (IGBM)

\displaystyle dy_{t}=a(b-y_{t})\,dt+\sigma\hskip 0.35565pty_{t}\,dW_{t}\hskip 0.35565pt,

(32)

where
a\geq 0
and
b\in\mathbb{R}
are the mean reversion parameters and
\sigma\geq 0
is the volatility.
As the vector fields are smooth, the SDE (
32
) can be expressed in Stratonovich form:

\displaystyle dy_{t}=\tilde{a}(\tilde{b}-y_{t})\,dt+\sigma\hskip 0.35565pty_{t}\circ dW_{t}\hskip 0.35565pt,

(33)

where
\tilde{a}:=a+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}
and
\tilde{b}:=\frac{2ab}{2a+\sigma^{2}}
denote the “adjusted” mean reversion parameters.

IGBM is an example of a one-factor short rate model and has seen recent attention in
the mathematical finance literature as an alternative to popular models
[
16
,
17
]
.
IGBM is also one of the simplest SDEs that has no known method of exact simulation.
We will investigate the strong and weak convergence rates of the following methods:

1.

Log-ODE method
(see definition
3.27
)

Since the vector fields of (
33
) give constant Lie brackets, this method becomes

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{log}}

\displaystyle:=Y_{k}^{\text{log}}e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}

\displaystyle\hskip 11.38109pt+abh\left(1-\sigma H_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}\hskip-1.42262pt+\sigma^{2}\left(\frac{3}{5}H_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}^{2}\hskip-1.42262pt+\frac{1}{30}h\right)\right)\frac{e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}-1}{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}\,,

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{\text{log}}

\displaystyle:=y_{0}\hskip 0.35565pt.

2.

Parabola-ODE method
(see definition
3.28
)

As the SDE (
33
) is quite analytically tractable, this method is expressible as

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{para}}

\displaystyle:=e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}\left(Y_{k}^{\text{para}}+ab\int_{t_{k}}^{t_{k+1}}e^{\tilde{a}\left(s-t_{k}\right)-\sigma\wideparen{W}_{t_{k},s}}\,ds\right),

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{\text{para}}

\displaystyle:=y_{0}\hskip 0.35565pt.

The integral above will be computed by
3
-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature.

3.

Piecewise linear method
(see
[
25
]
for definition and proof of convergence)

Just as above, this method can be simplified to give a straightforward formula.

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{lin}}

\displaystyle:=Y_{k}^{\text{lin}}e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}+abh\,\frac{e^{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}-1}{-\tilde{a}h+\sigma W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}}\,,

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{\text{lin}}

\displaystyle:=y_{0}\hskip 0.35565pt.

4.

Milstein method
(see section 6 of
[
2
]
and section 10.3 of
[
22
]
for overviews)

For this method, we shall take the positive part to guarantee non-negativity.

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{mil}}

\displaystyle:=\Big(Y_{k}^{\text{mil}}+\tilde{a}(\tilde{b}-Y_{k}^{\text{mil}})h+\sigma\,Y_{k}^{\text{mil}}\,W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}\hskip-1.42262pt+\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}\,Y_{k}^{\text{mil}}\,W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}^{2}\Big)^{+},

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{\text{mil}}

\displaystyle:=y_{0}\hskip 0.35565pt.

5.

Euler-Maruyama method
(see sections 4, 5 of
[
2
]
and section 10.2 of
[
22
]
)

Just as above, we take the positive part of each step to ensure non-negativity.

\displaystyle Y_{k+1}^{\text{eul}}

\displaystyle:=\Big(Y_{k}^{\text{eul}}+a(b-Y_{k}^{\text{eul}})h+\sigma\,Y_{k}^{\text{eul}}\,W_{t_{k},t_{k+1}}\Big)^{+},

\displaystyle Y_{0}^{\text{eul}}

\displaystyle:=y_{0}\hskip 0.35565pt.

Note that the explicit formula for the log-ODE method comes from the Lie brackets:

\displaystyle[f_{1},f_{0}](y)

\displaystyle=f_{0}^{\prime}(y)f_{1}(y)-f_{1}^{\prime}(y)f_{0}(y)

\displaystyle=-\tilde{a}\sigma y-\tilde{a}\sigma(\tilde{b}-y)

\displaystyle=-ab\sigma\hskip 0.7113pt,

\displaystyle[f_{1},[f_{1},f_{0}]](y)

\displaystyle=f_{0}^{\prime}(y)f_{1}^{\prime}(y)f_{1}(y)-2f_{1}^{\prime}(y)f_{0}^{\prime}(y)f_{1}(y)+f_{1}^{\prime}(y)f_{1}^{\prime}(y)f_{0}(y)

\displaystyle\hskip 22.76219pt+f_{0}^{\prime\prime}(y)(f_{1}(y),f_{1}(y))-2\hskip 0.7113ptf_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)(f_{0}(y),f_{1}(y))+f_{1}^{\prime\prime}(y)(f_{1}(y),f_{0}(y))

\displaystyle=-\tilde{a}\sigma^{2}y+2\hskip 0.7113pt\tilde{a}\sigma^{2}y+\tilde{a}\sigma^{2}(\tilde{b}-y)

\displaystyle=ab\sigma^{2}\hskip 0.7113pt,

and the formula for the parabola-ODE method was derived using a change of variable.

The Euler-Maruyama and Milstein methods are included in the numerical experiment
as benchmarks to test how the proposed methods compare to well-known methods.

5
Conclusion

There are primarily three new results established in this paper:

\bullet

An efficient strong polynomial approximation of Brownian motion

The main result allows one to construct a “smoother” Brownian motion as a
finite sum of
(\text{-}1,\text{-}1)
-Jacobi polynomials with independent Gaussian weights.
Moreover, it was shown that the approximation is optimal in a weighted
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})

sense and the surrounding noise is an independent centered Gaussian process.

\bullet

Unbiased approximation of third order Brownian iterated integrals

Iterated integrals of Brownian motion and time are important objects in the
study of SDEs as they appear naturally within stochastic Taylor expansions.
We have derived the
L^{2}(\mathbb{P})
-optimal estimator for a class of such integrals that is
measurable with respect to the path’s increment and space-time Lévy area.

\bullet

Simulation of Inhomogeneous Geometric Brownian Motion (IGBM)

IGBM is a mean-reverting short rate model used in mathematical finance and
also one of the simplest SDEs that has no known method of exact simulation.

By incorporating the new iterated integral estimator into the log-ODE method
we have developed a high order state-of-the-art numerical method for IGBM.

Furthermore, the results of this paper naturally lead to the following open questions:

\bullet

Which weight functions give “explicit eigenfunctions” for Brownian motion?
(For example, we could try
w(x)=x
or
w(x)=\frac{1}{x}
with
K_{W}(s,t)=\min(s,t)
)

\bullet

Is it possible to generalize the main theorem to fractional Brownian motion?

\bullet

What are the most efficient Runge-Kutta methods for general one-dimensional
SDEs that correctly use the new estimator for third order iterated integrals?

\bullet

Is this polynomial expansion optimal for approximating Lévy area? (see
[
13
]
)

\bullet

Which conditional moments can be computed for a given stochastic integral?

\bullet

How might we construct a piecewise linear path
\gamma
with the below properties?

\displaystyle 1.\,\,\,\,\gamma_{s}=W_{s}\hskip 0.7113pt,\hskip 4.97922pt\gamma_{t}=W_{t}\hskip 0.7113pt.

\displaystyle 2.\,\,\,\int_{s}^{t}\gamma_{s,u}\,du=\int_{s}^{t}W_{s,u}\,du\hskip 0.7113pt.

\displaystyle 3.\,\,\,\int_{s}^{t}\gamma_{s,u}^{2}\,du=\mathbb{E}\hskip-0.7113pt\left[\hskip 0.7113pt\int_{s}^{t}W_{s,u}^{2}\,du\,\Big|\,\cdots\right]\hskip-1.42262pt.

\bullet

Would this method of construction lead to effective cubature paths? (see
[
26
]
)

Given such a path, we can approximate (
