Twice Regularized Markov Decision Processes: The Equivalence between Robustness and Regularization
\name Esther Derman, \name Yevgeniy Men, \name Matthieu Geist, \name Shie Mannor

Abstract
Robust Markov decision processes (MDPs) aim to handle changing or partially known system dynamics. To solve them, one typically resorts to robust optimization methods. However, this significantly increases computational complexity and limits scalability in both learning and planning. On the other hand, regularized MDPs show more stability in policy learning without impairing time complexity. Yet, they generally do not encompass uncertainty in the model dynamics. In this work, we aim to learn robust MDPs using regularization. We first show that regularized MDPs are a particular instance of robust MDPs with uncertain reward. We thus establish that policy iteration on reward-robust MDPs can have the same time complexity as on regularized MDPs. We further extend this relationship to MDPs with uncertain transitions: this leads to a regularization term with an additional dependence on the value function. We then generalize regularized MDPs to twice regularized MDPs (R 2 MDPs), i.e. , MDPs with both value and policy regularization. The corresponding Bellman operators enable us to derive planning and learning schemes with convergence and generalization guarantees, thus reducing robustness to regularization. We numerically show this two-fold advantage on tabular and physical domains, highlighting the fact that R 2 preserves its efficacy in continuous environments.



Keywords:

reinforcement learning, robust Markov decision processes, robust optimization, regularization, Fenchel-Rockafellar duality

Introduction

MDPs provide a practical framework for solving sequential decision problems under uncertainty
(
Puterman 2014
)
. However, the chosen strategy can be very sensitive to sampling errors or inaccurate model estimates. This can lead to complete failure in common situations where the model parameters vary adversarially or are simply unknown
(
Mannor et al. 2007
)
. Robust MDPs aim to mitigate such sensitivity by assuming that the transition and/or reward function
(P,r)
varies arbitrarily inside a given
uncertainty set

\mathcal{U}

(
Iyengar 2005
;
Nilim and El Ghaoui 2005
)
. In this setting, an optimal solution maximizes a performance measure under the worst-case parameters. It can be thought of as a dynamic zero-sum game with an agent choosing the best action while Nature imposes it the most adversarial model. As such, solving robust MDPs involves max-min problems, which can be computationally challenging and limits scalability.

In recent years, several methods have been developed to alleviate the computational concerns raised by robust reinforcement learning (RL).
Apart from
Mannor et al. 2012
;
Mannor et al. 2016
;
Goyal and Grand-Clement 2022
who consider specific types of coupled uncertainty sets, all rely on a rectangularity assumption without which the problem can be NP-hard
(
Bagnell et al. 2001
;
Wiesemann et al. 2013
)
. This assumption is key to deriving tractable solvers of robust MDPs such as robust value iteration
(
Bagnell et al. 2001
;
Grand-Clément and Kroer 2021
)
or more general robust modified policy iteration (MPI)
(
Kaufman and Schaefer 2013
)
. Yet, reducing time complexity in robust Bellman updates remains challenging and is still researched today
(
Ho et al. 2018
;
Grand-Clément and Kroer 2021
;
Behzadian et al. 2021
;
Ho et al. 2021
;
Ho et al. 2022
)
.

At the same time, the empirical success of regularization in policy search methods has motivated a wide range of algorithms with diverse motivations such as improved exploration
(
Haarnoja et al. 2017
;
Lee et al. 2018
)
or stability
(
Schulman et al. 2015
;
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
.
Geist et al. 2019
proposed a unified view from which many existing algorithms can be derived. Their regularized MDP formalism opens the path to error propagation analysis in approximate MPI
(
Scherrer et al. 2015
)
and leads to the same bounds as for standard MDPs. Nevertheless, as we further show in Sec.
3
, policy regularization accounts for reward uncertainty only: it does not encompass uncertainty in the model dynamics.
Despite a vast literature on
how
regularized policy search works and convergence rates analysis
(
Shani et al. 2020
;
Cen et al. 2022
)
, little attention has been given to understanding
why
it can generate strategies that are robust to external perturbations
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
.

To our knowledge, the only works that relate robustness to regularization in RL are

(
Derman and Mannor 2020
;
Husain et al. 2021
;
Eysenbach and Levine 2022
;
Brekelmans et al. 2022
)
.
Derman and Mannor 2020
employ a distributionally robust optimization approach to regularize an empirical value function. Unfortunately, computing this empirical value necessitates several policy evaluation procedures, which is quickly unpractical.
Husain et al. 2021
;
Brekelmans et al. 2022
provide a dual relationship with robust MDPs under uncertain reward. Their duality result applies to general regularization methods and gives a robust interpretation of soft-actor-critic
(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
. Although these two works justify the use of regularization for ensuring robustness, they do not enclose any algorithmic novelty. Similarly,
Eysenbach and Levine 2022
specifically focus on maximum entropy methods and relate them to either reward or transition robustness. We shall further detail on these most related studies in Sec.
9
.

The robustness-regularization duality is well established in statistical learning theory
(
Xu et al. 2009
;
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al. 2015
;
Kuhn et al. 2019
)
, as opposed to RL theory.
In fact, standard setups such as classification or regression may be considered as single-stage decision-making problems,
i.e.
, one-step MDPs, a particular case of RL setting. Extending this robustness-regularization duality to RL would yield cheaper learning methods with robustness guarantees. As such, we introduce a regularization function
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}
that depends on the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
and is defined over both policy and value spaces, thus inducing a
twice regularized
Bellman operator (see Sec.
5
). We show that this regularizer yields an equivalence of the form
v_{\pi,\mathcal{U}}=v_{\pi,\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}}
, where
v_{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the robust value function for policy
\pi
and
v_{\pi,\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}}
the regularized one. This equivalence is derived through the objective function each value optimizes. More concretely, we formulate the robust value function
v_{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
as an optimal solution of the robust optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v\leq\inf_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v,

(
RO
)

where
T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}
is the evaluation Bellman operator
(
Puterman 2014
)
. Then, we show that
v_{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is also an optimal solution of the convex (non-robust) optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v\leq T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v-\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi,v),

(
CO
)

where
(P_{0},r_{0})
is the
nominal model
. This establishes equivalence as the two problems admit the same optimum for any policy.
Moreover, the inequality constraint of (
CO
) enables to derive a
twice regularized
(R
2
) Bellman operator defined according to
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}
, a policy and value regularizer. For ball-constrained uncertainty sets,
\Omega_{\mathcal{U}}
has an explicit form and under mild conditions, the corresponding R
2

Bellman operators are contracting. The equivalence between the two problems (
RO
) and (
CO
) together with the contraction properties of R
2

Bellman operators enable to circumvent robust optimization problems at each Bellman update. As such, it alleviates robust planning and learning algorithms by reducing them to regularized ones, which are known to be as complex as classical methods.

To summarize, we make the following contributions:
(i) We show that regularized MDPs are a specific instance of robust MDPs with uncertain reward. Besides formalizing a general connection between the two settings, our result enables to explicit the uncertainty sets induced by standard regularizers. (ii) We extend this duality to MDPs with uncertain transition and provide the first regularizer that recovers robust MDPs with
s
-rectangular balls and arbitrary norm. (iii) We introduce twice regularized MDPs (R
2

MDPs) that apply both policy and value regularization to retrieve robust MDPs. We establish contraction of the corresponding Bellman operators. This leads us to proposing an R
2

MPI algorithm with similar time complexity as vanilla MPI. (iv) We also introduce a model-free algorithm, R
2

q
-learning, that solves R
2

MDPs, and for which we establish theoretical convergence. (v) With the aim of extending R
2

q
-learning to large state-spaces, we provide an easy method for estimating the value regularization term when a tabular representation is no longer available. Experiments on tabular and continuous domains prove the efficiency of R
2

for both planning and learning, thus opening new perspectives towards practical and scalable robust RL.

Preliminaries

This section describes the background material that we use throughout our work. Firstly, we introduce some notations. Secondly, we recall useful properties in convex analysis. Thirdly, we address classical discounted MDPs and their linear program (LP) formulation. Fourth, we briefly detail regularized MDPs and the associated operators and lastly, we focus on the robust MDP setting.

2.1
Notations

We designate the extended reals by
\overline{\R}:=\R\cup\{-\infty,\infty\}
.
Given a finite set
\Z

-norm coincides with its dual norm,

\lVert\av\rVert=\max_{\lVert\bv\rVert\leq 1}\langle\av,\bv\rangle=:\lVert\av\rVert_{*}
.
Let a function

is

Given a set

,

is
\delta_{\mathfrak{Z}}(\av)=0
if

;

\sigma_{\mathfrak{Z}}(\y)=\max_{\av\in\mathfrak{Z}}\langle\av,\y\rangle

(

.

Let

be a convex set and
\Omega:C\to\R
a strongly convex function.
Throughout this study, the function
\Omega
plays the role of a policy and/or value regularization function.
Its Legendre-Fenchel transform
\Omega^{*}
satisfies several smoothness properties, hence its alternative name
smoothed max operator

(

)
.
Our work makes use of the following result
(
Hiriart-Urruty and Lemarechal 2004
;

)
.

Proposition 1
.

Given

(i)

\nabla\Omega^{*}(\y)=\argmax_{\av\in C}\langle\av,\y\rangle-\Omega(\av),\forall\y\in\R^{\Z}
.

(ii)

2.2
Discounted MDPs and LP formulation

Consider an infinite horizon MDP
(\St,\A,\mu_{0},\gamma,P,r)
with
\St
and
\A
finite state and action spaces respectively,
0<\mu_{0}\in\Delta_{\St}
an initial state distribution and
\gamma\in(0,1)
a discount factor. Denoting

\X:=\St\times\A
,

P\in\Delta_{\St}^{\X}
is a transition kernel
mapping each state-action pair to a probability distribution over
\St
and
r\in\R^{\X}
is a reward function.
A policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
maps any state
s\in\St
to an action distribution
\pi_{s}\in\Delta_{\A}
, and we evaluate its performance through the following measure:

\displaystyle\rho(\pi):=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})\biggm|\mu_{0},\pi,P\right]=\langle v_{(P,r)}^{\pi},\mu_{0}\rangle.

(1)

Here, the expectation is conditioned on the process distribution determined by
\mu_{0},\pi
and
P
,
and for all
s\in\St,

v_{(P,r)}^{\pi}(s)=\mathbb{E}[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r(s_{t},a_{t})|s_{0}=s,\pi,P]
is the
value function
at state
s
.
Maximizing (
1
) defines the standard RL objective,
which can be solved thanks to the Bellman operators:

\begin{split}T^{\pi}_{(P,r)}v&:=r^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}v\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{\St},\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St},\\
T_{(P,r)}v&:=\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}}T^{\pi}_{(P,r)}v\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{\St},\\
\mathcal{G}_{(P,r)}(v)&:=\{\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}:T^{\pi}_{(P,r)}v=T_{(P,r)}v\}\quad\forall v\in\mathbb{R}^{\St},\end{split}

where
r^{\pi}:=[\langle\pi_{s},r(s,\cdot)\rangle]_{s\in\St}
and
P^{\pi}=[P^{\pi}(s^{\prime}|s)]_{s^{\prime},s\in\St}
with
P^{\pi}(s^{\prime}|s):=\langle\pi_{s},P(s^{\prime}|s,\cdot)\rangle.

Both
T^{\pi}_{(P,r)}
and
T_{(P,r)}
are
\gamma
-contractions with respect to (w.r.t.) the supremum norm, so each admits a unique fixed point
v^{\pi}_{(P,r)}
and
v^{*}_{(P,r)}
, respectively. The set of greedy policies w.r.t. value
v
defines
\mathcal{G}_{(P,r)}(v)
, and any policy
\pi\in\mathcal{G}_{(P,r)}(v^{*}_{(P,r)})
is optimal
(
Puterman 2014
)
. For all
v\in\R^{\St}
, the associated function
q\in\R^{\X}
is given by
q(s,a)=r(s,a)+\gamma\langle P(\cdot|s,a),v\rangle\quad\forall(s,a)\in\X
. In particular, the fixed point
v^{\pi}_{(P,r)}
satisfies
v^{\pi}_{(P,r)}=\langle\pi_{s},q^{\pi}_{(P,r)}(s,\cdot)\rangle
where
q^{\pi}_{(P,r)}
is its associated
q
-function.

The problem in (
1
) can also be formulated as an LP.
Given a policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, we characterize its performance
\rho(\pi)
by the following

-LP
(
Puterman 2014
;
Nachum and Dai 2020
)

\min_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ subject to (s.t.) }v\geq r^{\pi}+\gamma P^{\pi}v.

(P
π
)

This primal objective provides a policy view on the problem. Alternatively, one may take a state visitation perspective by studying the dual objective instead:

\displaystyle\max_{\mu\in\R^{\St}}\langle r^{\pi},\mu\rangle\text{ s. t. }\mu\geq 0\text{ and }(\mathbf{Id}_{\R^{\St}}-\gamma P_{*}^{\pi})\mu=\mu_{0},

(D
π
)

where
P_{*}^{\pi}
is the
adjoint policy transition operator
2
2

2

It is the adjoint operator of
P^{\pi}
in the sense that
\langle P^{\pi}v,v^{\prime}\rangle=\langle v,P_{*}^{\pi}v^{\prime}\rangle\quad\forall v,v^{\prime}\in\R^{\St}
.

[P_{*}^{\pi}\mu](s):=\sum_{\bar{s}\in\St}P^{\pi}(s|\bar{s})\mu(\bar{s})\quad\forall\mu\in\R^{\St},

is the identity function in

.

Let
\mathbf{I}(s^{\prime}|s,a):=\delta_{s^{\prime}=s}\quad\forall(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\X\times\St
the trivial transition matrix and
define its

\mathbf{I}_{*}\mu(s):=\sum_{(\bar{s},\bar{a})\in\X}\mathbf{I}(s|\bar{s},\bar{a})\mu(\bar{s},\bar{a})\quad\forall s\in\St

The correspondence between occupancy measures and policies lies in the one-to-one mapping

2.3
Regularized MDPs

A regularized MDP is a tuple
(\St,\A,\mu_{0},\gamma,P,r,\Omega)
with
(\St,\A,\mu_{0},\gamma,P,r)
an infinite horizon MDP as above, and
\Omega:=(\Omega_{s})_{s\in\St}
a finite set of functions such that for all
s\in\St
,
\Omega_{s}:\Delta_{\A}\to\R
is strongly convex. Each function
\Omega_{s}
plays the role of a policy regularizer
\Omega_{s}(\pi_{s})
. With a slight abuse of notation, we shall denote by
\Omega(\pi):=(\Omega_{s}(\pi_{s}))_{s\in\St}
the family of state-dependent regularizers.
3
3

3

In the formalism of
Geist et al. 2019
,
\Omega_{s}
is initially constant over
\St
. However, later in the paper
(
Geist et al. 2019
, Sec. 5)
, it changes according to policy iterates. Here, we alternatively define a family
\Omega
of state-dependent regularizers, which accounts for state-dependent uncertainty sets (see Sec.
5
below).
The regularized Bellman evaluation operator is given by

\displaystyle[T^{\pi,\Omega}_{(P,r)}v](s):=T^{\pi}_{(P,r)}v(s)-\Omega_{s}(\pi_{s})\quad\forall v\in\R^{\St},s\in\St,

and the regularized Bellman optimality operator by
T^{*,\Omega}_{(P,r)}v:=\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}}T^{\pi,\Omega}_{(P,r)}v\quad\forall v\in\R^{\St}

(
Geist et al. 2019
)
. The unique fixed point of
T^{\pi,\Omega}_{(P,r)}
(respectively
T^{*,\Omega}_{(P,r)}
) is denoted by
v^{\pi,\Omega}_{(P,r)}
(resp.
v^{*,\Omega}_{(P,r)}
) and defines the
regularized value function
(resp.
regularized optimal value function
).
Although the regularized MDP formalism stems from the aforementioned Bellman operators in
(
Geist et al. 2019
)
, it turns out that regularized MDPs are MDPs with modified reward. Indeed, for any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, the regularized value function is
v^{\pi,\Omega}_{(P,r)}=(\mathbf{I}_{\St}-\gamma P^{\pi})^{-1}(r^{\pi}-\Omega(\pi)),
which corresponds to a non-regularized value with expected reward
\tilde{r}^{\pi}:=r^{\pi}-\Omega(\pi)
. Note that the modified reward
\tilde{r}^{\pi}(s)
is no longer linear in
\pi_{s}
because of
\Omega_{s}
being strongly convex. Also, this modification does not apply to the reward function
r
but only to its expectation
r^{\pi}
, as we cannot regularize the original reward without making it policy-independent.

2.4
Robust MDPs

In general, the MDP model is not explicitly known but rather estimated from sampled trajectories. As this may result in over-sensitive outcome
(
Mannor et al. 2007
)
, robust MDPs reduce such performance variation. Formally, a robust MDP
(\St,\A,\mu_{0},\gamma,\mathcal{U})
is an MDP with uncertain model belonging to
\mathcal{U}:=\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{R}
,
i.e.
, uncertain transition
P\in\mathcal{P}\subseteq\Delta_{\St}^{\X}
and
reward
r\in\mathcal{R}\subseteq\R^{\X}

(
Iyengar 2005
;
Wiesemann et al. 2013
)
.
The uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
typically controls the confidence level of a model estimate, which in turn determines the agent’s level of robustness.
It is given to the agent, who seeks to maximize performance under the worst-case model
(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}
. Although intractable in general, this problem can be solved in polynomial time for
rectangular
uncertainty sets,
i.e.
, when
\mathcal{U}=\times_{s\in\St}\mathcal{U}_{s}=\times_{s\in\St}(\mathcal{P}_{s}\times\mathcal{R}_{s})

(
Wiesemann et al. 2013
;
Mannor et al. 2012
)
. For any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}

and state
s\in\St,
the
robust value function
at
s
is
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}(s):=\min_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}v_{(P,r)}^{\pi}(s)

and the
robust optimal value function

v^{*,\mathcal{U}}(s):=\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{\St}^{\A}}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}(s)
.
Each of them is the unique fixed point of the respective robust Bellman operators:

\displaystyle[T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v](s)

\displaystyle:=\min_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v(s)\quad\forall v\in\R^{\St},s\in\St,\pi\in\Delta_{\St}^{\A},

(2)

\displaystyle[T^{*,\mathcal{U}}v](s)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}}[T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v](s)\quad\forall v\in\R^{\St},s\in\St,

(3)

which are
\gamma
-contractions. For all
v\in\R^{\St}
, the associated robust
q
-function is given by

q(s,a)=\min_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}\{r(s,a)+\gamma\langle P(\cdot|s,a),v\rangle\}\quad\forall(s,a)\in\X
, so that
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}=\langle\pi_{s},q^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}(s,\cdot)\rangle
where
q^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the robust
q
-function associated to
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
.

3
Reward-robust MDPs

This section focuses on reward-robust MDPs,
i.e.
, robust MDPs with uncertain reward but known transition model. We first show that regularized MDPs represent a particular instance of reward-robust MDPs, as both solve the same optimization problem. This equivalence provides a theoretical motivation for the heuristic success of policy regularization. Then, we explicit the uncertainty set underlying some standard regularization functions, thus suggesting an interpretable explanation of their empirical robustness.

We first show the following proposition
2
, which applies to general robust MDPs and random policies. It slightly extends
(
Iyengar 2005
)
, as Lemma 3.2 there focuses on uncertain-transition MDPs and deterministic policies. For completeness, we provide a proof of Prop.
2
in Appx.
Proposition
.

Proposition 2
.

For any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the robust optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v\leq T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v\text{ for all }(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}.

(P
U
)

In the robust optimization problem (
PU
), the inequality constraint must hold over the whole uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}
. As such, a function
v\in\R^{\St}
is said to be
robust feasible
for (
PU
) if
v\leq T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v
for all
(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}
or equivalently, if
\max_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}\{v(s)-T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v(s)\}\leq 0
for all
s\in\St.
Therefore, checking robust feasibility requires to solve a maximization problem. For properly structured uncertainty sets, a closed form solution can be derived, as we shall see in the sequel.
As standard in the robust RL literature
(
Roy et al. 2017
;
Ho et al. 2018
;
Badrinath and Kalathil 2021
)
, the remaining of this work focuses on uncertainty sets centered around a known
nominal model
.
Formally, given
P_{0}
(resp.
r_{0}
) a nominal
transition kernel (resp. reward function), we consider uncertainty sets of the form

(P_{0}+\mathcal{P})\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
. The size of
\mathcal{P}\times\mathcal{R}
quantifies our level of uncertainty or alternatively, the desired degree of robustness.

3.1
Reward-robust and regularized MDPs: an equivalence

We now focus on reward-robust MDPs,
i.e.
, robust MDPs with
\mathcal{U}=\{P_{0}\}\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R}).
Thm.
1
establishes that reward-robust MDPs are in fact regularized MDPs whose regularizer is given by a support function. Its proof can be found in Appx.
A.2
. This result brings two take-home messages: (i) policy regularization is equivalent to reward uncertainty; (ii) policy iteration on reward-robust MDPs has the same convergence rate as regularized MDPs, which in turn is the same as standard MDPs
(
Geist et al. 2019
)
.

Theorem 1

(Reward-robust MDP)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{U}=\{P_{0}\}\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R}).
Then, for any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v(s)\leq T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})\text{ for all }s\in\St,

where
\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}
is the support function of the reward uncertainty set (see definition in Sec.
2.1
).

Thm.
1
clearly highlights a convex regularizer
\Omega_{s}(\pi_{s}):=\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})\quad\forall s\in\St
. We thus recover a regularized MDP by setting

[T^{\pi,\Omega}v](s)=T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})\quad\forall s\in\St
. In particular, when
\mathcal{R}_{s}
is a ball of radius
\alpha_{s}^{r}
, the support function (or regularizer) can be written in closed form as
\Omega_{s}(\pi_{s}):=\alpha_{s}^{r}\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert
, which is strongly convex. We formalize this below (see proof in Appx.
A.3
).

Corollary 1
.

Let
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
and
\mathcal{U}=\{P_{0}\}\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
. Further assume that for all
s\in\St
, the reward uncertainty set at
s
is
\mathcal{R}_{s}:=\{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}:\lVert r_{s}\rVert\leq\alpha_{s}^{r}\}
. Then, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v(s)\leq T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\alpha_{s}^{r}\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert\text{ for all }s\in\St.

While regularization induces reward-robustness,
Thm.
1
and Cor.
1
suggest that, on the other hand, specific reward-robust MDPs recover well-known policy regularization methods. In the following section, we explicit the reward-uncertainty sets underlying some of these regularizers.

3.2
Related Algorithms

Consider a reward uncertainty set of the form
\mathcal{R}:=\times_{(s,a)\in\X}\mathcal{R}_{s,a}
. This defines an
(s,a)
-rectangular
\mathcal{R}
(a particular type of
s
-rectangular
\mathcal{R}
) whose rectangles
\mathcal{R}_{s,a}
are independently defined for each state-action pair. For the regularizers below, we derive appropriate
\mathcal{R}_{s,a}
-s that recover the same regularized value function. Detailed proofs are in Appx.
A.4
. There, we also include a summary table that reviews the properties of some RL regularizers, as well as our R
2

function which we shall introduce later in Sec.
5
. Note that the reward uncertainty sets here depend on the policy. This is due to the fact that standard regularizers are defined over the policy space and not at each state-action pair. It similarly explains why the reward modification induced by regularization does not apply to the original reward function, as already mentioned in Sec.
2.3
.

Negative Shannon entropy:

Let
\mathcal{R}_{s,a}^{\textsc{NS}}(\pi):=\left[\ln\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\pi_{s}(a)}}\right),+\infty\right)\quad\forall(s,a)\in\X
.
The associated support function enables to write:

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}^{\textsc{NS}}(\pi)}(-\pi_{s})=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}r(s,\cdot):r(s,a^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}_{s,a^{\prime}}^{\textsc{NS}}(\pi),a^{\prime}\in\A\end{subarray}}\sum_{a\in\A}-r(s,a)\pi_{s}(a)=\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)\ln(\pi_{s}(a)),

where the last equality comes from maximizing
-r(s,a)
over
\left[\ln\left(\nicefrac{{1}}{{\pi_{s}(a)}}\right),+\infty\right)
for each
a\in\A
.
We thus recover the negative Shannon entropy
\Omega(\pi_{s})=\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)\ln(\pi_{s}(a))

(
Haarnoja et al. 2018
)
.

Kullback-Leibler divergence:
Given an action distribution
0<d\in\Delta_{\A}
, let
\mathcal{R}_{s,a}^{\textsc{KL}}(\pi):=\ln\left(d(a)\right)+\mathcal{R}_{s,a}^{\textsc{NS}}(\pi)\quad\forall(s,a)\in\X
. It amounts to translating the interval

\mathcal{R}_{s,a}^{\textsc{NS}}
by the given constant. Writing the support function yields
\Omega(\pi_{s})=\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)\ln\left(\nicefrac{{\pi_{s}(a)}}{{d(a)}}\right)
, which is exactly the KL divergence
(
Schulman et al. 2015
)
.

Negative Tsallis entropy:
Letting
\mathcal{R}_{s,a}^{\textsc{T}}(\pi):=\left[\nicefrac{{(1-\pi_{s}(a))}}{{2}},+\infty\right)\quad\forall(s,a)\in\X
, we recognize the negative Tsallis entropy
\Omega(\pi_{s})=\frac{1}{2}(\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert^{2}-1)

(
Lee et al. 2018
)
.

The worst-case rewards derived for the KL divergence and the Tsallis entropy are consistent with those obtained by
Brekelmans et al. 2022
. Indeed, in both cases, taking the finite endpoint of the interval recovers the same worst-case reward. Yet, the dual view adopted there yields larger reward uncertainty, which may yield more conservative solutions when using approximate solvers.

3.3
Policy-gradient for reward-robust MDPs

The equivalence between reward-robust and regularized MDPs leads us to wonder whether we can employ policy-gradient
(
Sutton et al. 1999
)
on reward-robust MDPs using regularization. The following result establishes that a policy-gradient theorem can indeed be established for reward-robust MDPs (see proof in Appx.
A.5
).

Proposition 3
.

Assume that
\mathcal{U}=\{P_{0}\}\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
with
\mathcal{R}_{s}=\{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}:\lVert r_{s}\rVert\leq\alpha_{s}^{r}\}
. Then, the gradient of the reward-robust objective
J_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi):=\langle v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}},\mu_{0}\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle\nabla J_{\mathcal{U}}(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a)\sim\mu_{\pi}}\left[\nabla\ln\pi_{s}(a)\left(q^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}(s,a)-\alpha_{s}^{r}\frac{\pi_{s}(a)}{\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert}\right)\right],

where
\mu_{\pi}
is the occupancy measure under the nominal model
P_{0}
and policy
\pi
.

Although Prop.
3
is an application of
(
Geist et al. 2019
, Appx. D.3)
for a specific regularized MDP, its reward-robust formulation is novel and suggests another simplification of robust methods. Indeed, previous works that exploit policy-gradient on robust MDPs involve the occupancy measure of the worst-case model
(
Mankowitz et al. 2018
)
, whereas our result sticks to the nominal. In practice, Prop.
3
enables to learn a robust policy by sampling transitions from the nominal model instead of all uncertain models. This has a twofold advantage: (i) it avoids an additional computation of the minimum as done in
(
Pinto et al. 2017
;
Shashua and Mannor 2017
;
Mankowitz et al. 2018
;
Derman et al. 2018
)
, where the authors sample next-state transitions and rewards based on all parameters from the uncertainty set, then update a policy based on the worst outcome; (ii) it releases from restricting to finite uncertainty sets. In fact, our regularizer accounts for robustness regardless of the sampling procedure, whereas the parallel simulations of
Pinto et al. 2017
;
Mankowitz et al. 2018
;
Derman et al. 2018
require the uncertainty set to be finite. Technical difficulties are yet to be addressed for generalizing our result to transition-uncertain MDPs, because of the interdependence between the regularizer and the value function (see Secs.
4
-
5
). We detail more on this issue in Appx.
A.5
. Recently,
Wang and Zou 2022
established a robust policy gradient for transition-uncertain MDPs, but their setting focuses on a fixed mixture between the nominal kernel and an arbitrary transition matrix,
i.e.
,
\mathcal{U}=((1-R)P_{0}+R\Delta_{\St}^{\X})\times\{r_{0}\}
. By design, this yields an
(s,a)
-rectangular uncertainty set included in an
\ell_{\infty}
-ball of size
R
around the nominal, whereas our setup considers more general
s
-rectangular uncertainty.

4
General robust MDPs

Now that we have established policy regularization as a reward-robust problem, we would like to study the opposite question: can any robust MDP with both uncertain reward and transition be solved using regularization instead of robust optimization? If so, is the regularization function easy to determine? In this section, we answer positively to both questions for properly defined robust MDPs. This greatly facilitates robust RL, as it avoids the increased complexity of robust planning algorithms while still reaching robust performance.

The following theorem establishes that similarly to reward-robust MDPs, robust MDPs can be formulated through regularization (see proof in Appx.
B.1
). Although the regularizer is also a support function in that case, it depends on both the policy and the value objective, which may further explain the difficulty of dealing with robust MDPs.

Theorem 2

(General robust MDP)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{U}=(P_{0}+\mathcal{P})\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
. Then, for any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v(s)\leq T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})-\sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(-\gamma v\cdot\pi_{s})\text{ for all }s\in\St,

(4)

where
[v\cdot\pi_{s}](s^{\prime},a):=v(s^{\prime})\pi_{s}(a)\quad\forall(s^{\prime},a)\in\X
.

The upper-bound in the inequality constraint (
4
) is of the same spirit as the regularized Bellman operator: the first term is a standard, non-regularized Bellman operator on the nominal model
(P_{0},r_{0})
to which we subtract a policy and value-dependent function playing the role of regularization.
This function reminds that of
Derman and Mannor 2020
also coming from conjugacy. This is the only similarity between both regularizers: in
(
Derman and Mannor 2020
)
, the Legendre-Fenchel transform is applied on a different type of function and results in a regularization term that has no closed form but can only be bounded from above. Moreover, the setup considered there is different since it studies distributionally robust MDPs. As such, it involves general convex optimization, whereas we focus on the robust formulation of an LP.

The support function further simplifies when the uncertainty set is a ball, as shown below. Yet, the dependence of the regularizer on the value function prevents us from readily applying the regularized MDP tool-set. We shall study the properties of this new regularization function in Sec.
5
.

Corollary 2
.

Assume that
\mathcal{U}=(P_{0}+\mathcal{P})\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
with
\mathcal{P}_{s}:=\{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}:\lVert P_{s}\rVert\leq\alpha_{s}^{P}\}
and
\mathcal{R}_{s}:=\{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}:\lVert r_{s}\rVert\leq\alpha_{s}^{r}\}
for all
s\in\St
. Then, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v(s)\leq T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\alpha_{s}^{r}\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert-\alpha_{s}^{P}\gamma\lVert v\rVert\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert\text{ for all }s\in\St.

(5)

We restrict our statement to the
\ell_{2}
-norm for notation convenience only, the dual norm of
\ell_{2}
being
\ell_{2}
itself. In fact, one can consider two different norms for reward and transition uncertainties. Thus, Cor.
2
can be rewritten with an arbitrary norm, which would reveal a dual norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{*}
instead of
\lVert\cdot\rVert
in Eq. (
5
) (see proof in Appx.
B.2
). As a result, our regularization function recovers a robust value function independently of the chosen norm, which extends previous results from
(
Ho et al. 2018
;
Grand-Clément and Kroer 2021
)
. Indeed,
Ho et al. 2018
reduce the complexity of robust planning under the
\ell_{1}
-norm only, while
Grand-Clément and Kroer 2021
focus on KL and
\ell_{2}
ball-constrained uncertainty sets. Both works rely on the specific structure induced by the divergence they consider to derive more efficient robust Bellman updates. Differently, our method circumvents these updates using a generic, problem-independent regularization function while still encompassing
s
-rectangular uncertainty sets as in
(
Ho et al. 2018
;
Grand-Clément and Kroer 2021
)
.

5
R
2

MDPs

In Sec.
4
, we showed that for general robust MDPs, the optimization constraint involves a regularization term that depends on the value function itself. This adds a difficulty to the reward-robust case where the regularization only depends on the policy. Yet, we provided an explicit regularizer for general robust MDPs that are ball-constrained. In this section, we introduce R
2

MDPs, an extension of regularized MDPs that combines policy and value regularization. The core idea is to further regularize the Bellman operators with a value-dependent term that recovers the support functions we derived from the robust optimization problems of Secs.
3
-
4
.

Definition 1

(R
2

Bellman operators)
.

For all
v\in\R^{\St}
, define
\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}:\Delta_{\A}\to\R
as

\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s}):=\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert(\alpha_{s}^{r}+\alpha_{s}^{P}\gamma\lVert v\rVert)
. The R
2

Bellman evaluation and optimality operators are defined as

\displaystyle[T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v](s)

\displaystyle:=T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})\quad\forall s\in\St,

\displaystyle[T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v](s)

\displaystyle:=\max_{\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}}[T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v](s)=\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}^{*}(q_{s})\quad\forall s\in\St.

For any function
v\in\R^{\St}
, the associated unique greedy policy is defined as

\pi_{s}=\argmax_{\pi_{s}\in\Delta_{\A}}T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v(s)=\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}^{*}(q_{s}),\quad\forall s\in\St,

that is, in vector form,
\pi=\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}^{*}(q)=:\mathcal{G}_{\Omega_{\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}}(v)\iff T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v=T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v
.

The R
2

Bellman evaluation operator is not linear because of the functional norm appearing in the regularization function. Yet, under the following assumption, it is contracting and we can apply Banach’s fixed point theorem to define the R
2

value function.

Assumption 1

(Bounded radius)
.

For all
s\in\St
, there exists
\epsilon_{s}>0
such that

\alpha_{s}^{P}\leq\min\left(\frac{1-\gamma-\epsilon_{s}}{\gamma\sqrt{\lvert\St\rvert}};\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}_{\A}\in\R^{\A}_{+},\lVert\mathbf{u}_{\A}\rVert=1\\
\mathbf{v}_{\St}\in\R^{\St}_{+},\lVert\mathbf{v}_{\St}\rVert=1\end{subarray}}\mathbf{u}_{\A}^{\top}P_{0}(\cdot|s,\cdot)\mathbf{v}_{\St}\right).

Asm.
1
requires to upper bound the ball radius of transition uncertainty sets. The first term in the minimum is needed for establishing contraction of R
2

Bellman operators (item (iii) in Prop.
4
), while the second one is used for ensuring monotonicity (item (i) in Prop.
4
). We remark that the former depends on the original discount factor
\gamma
: radius
\alpha_{s}^{P}
must be smaller as
\gamma
tends to
1
but can arbitrarily grow as
\gamma
decreases to
0
, without altering contraction. Indeed, larger
\gamma
implies longer time horizon and higher stochasticity, which explains why we need tighter level of uncertainty then. Otherwise, value and policy regularization seem unable to handle the mixed effects of parametric and stochastic uncertainties. The additional dependence on the state-space size comes from the
\ell_{2}
-norm chosen for the ball constraints. In fact, for any
\ell_{p}
-norm of dual
\ell_{q}
,
\lvert\St\rvert^{\frac{1}{q}}
replaces
\sqrt{\lvert\St\rvert}
in the denominator, so the bound becomes independent of
\lvert\St\rvert
as
(p,q)
tends to
(1,\infty)
(see Appx.
C.1
). Although we recognize a generalized Rayleigh quotient-type problem in the second minimum
(
Parlett 1974
)
, its interpretation in our context remains unclear. Asm.
1
enables the R
2

Bellman operators to admit a unique fixed point, among other nice properties. We formalize this below (see proof in Appx.
C.1
).

Proposition 4
.

Suppose that Asm.
1
holds. Then, the following properties hold:

(i)

Monotonicity: For all
v_{1},v_{2}\in\R^{\St}
such that
v_{1}\leq v_{2}
, we have
T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{1}\leq T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{2}
and

T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{1}\leq T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{2}
.

(ii)

Sub-distributivity: For all
v_{1}\in\R^{\St},c\in\R
, we have
T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(v_{1}+c\mathbbm{1}_{\St})\leq T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{1}+\gamma c\mathbbm{1}_{\St}
and
T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(v_{1}+c\mathbbm{1}_{\St})\leq T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{1}+\gamma c\mathbbm{1}_{\St}
,
\forall c\in\R
.

(iii)

Contraction: Let
\epsilon_{*}:=\min_{s\in\St}\epsilon_{s}>0
. Then, for all
v_{1},v_{2}\in\R^{\St}
, we have

\lVert T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{1}-T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{2}\rVert_{\infty}\leq(1-\epsilon_{*})\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert_{\infty}
and

\lVert T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{1}-T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v_{2}\rVert_{\infty}\leq(1-\epsilon_{*})\lVert v_{1}-v_{2}\rVert_{\infty}
.

We emphasize that the contracting coefficient
1-\epsilon^{*}
from Prop.
4
is different from the original discount factor
\gamma
. Yet, as Asm.
1
suggests it, an intrinsic dependence between
\gamma
and

\epsilon^{*}
makes the R
2

Bellman updates similar to the standard ones: when
\gamma
tends to
0
, the value of
\epsilon^{*}
required for Asm.
1
to hold increases, which makes the contracting coefficient
1-\epsilon^{*}
tend to 0 as well,
i.e.
, the two contracting coefficients behave similarly.
The contracting feature of both R
2

Bellman operators finally leads us to introduce R
2

value functions.

Definition 2

(R
2

value functions)
.

(i)

The R
2

value function
v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}
is defined as the unique fixed point of the R
2

Bellman evaluation operator:
v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}=T^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}
. The associated

-function is
q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)=r_{0}(s,a)+\gamma\langle P_{0}(\cdot|s,a),v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rangle
.

(ii)

The R
2

optimal value function
v^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}
is defined as the unique fixed point of the R

v^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}=T^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}v^{*,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}

7.3

2

The current expression of R
2

2
) suggests that we have access to the whole
q

We thus keep track of a replay buffer that memorizes and updates past information online. At each iteration, we randomly extract a batch
\mathcal{B}_{t}

of samples thanks to which we derive an empirical estimate of the norm. Formally,
\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}^{2}:=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{B}_{t}}v_{t}(s)^{2}
,
where the index in
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}
indicates the empirical nature of the norm. Finally, our approximate setting motivates us to stabilize value norm estimates. Thus, in the same spirit as
(

;
Vieillard et al. 2020
)

i.e.
, at iteration
t+1

\beta\lVert v_{t}\rVert_{\mathcal{B}_{t}}^{2}+(1-\beta)\lVert v_{t+1}\rVert_{\mathcal{B}_{t+1}}^{2}
.
To evaluate the reliability of our norm estimate, we compared it to an oracle. Practically, in Mars Rover, we stored the tabular value function obtained after convergence of R
2

q
-learning, then measured how the norm estimated from batches evolves across iteration steps.

Related Work

In statistical learning, regularization has long been used as a computationally low-cost tool for reducing over-fitting
(
Hastie et al. 2009
)
. Later on, the theory of robust optimization
(
Bertsimas and Den Hertog 2022
)
has enabled establishing formal connections between regularization and robustness in standard learning settings such as support vector machines
(
Xu et al. 2009
)
, logistic regression
(
Shafieezadeh-Abadeh et al. 2015
)
or maximum likelihood estimation
(
Kuhn et al. 2019
)
. As stated in Sec.
1
, these represent particular RL problems as they concern a single-stage decision-making process. In that regard, the generalization of robustness-regularization duality to sequential decision-making has seldom been studied in the RL literature.

It is only recently that policy regularization started being viewed from a robustness perspective
(
Husain et al. 2021
;
Brekelmans et al. 2022
;
Eysenbach and Levine 2022
;
Derman et al. 2021b
;
Kumar et al. 2022
)
. In
(
Husain et al. 2021
)
, regularization is applied to the dual objective instead of the primal. This has two shortcomings: (i) It prevents the formulation of regularized Bellman operators and dynamic programming methods; (ii) The feasible set is that of occupancy measures, so the connection with standard policy regularization remains unclear. Whilst
Brekelmans et al. 2022
address this shortcoming by providing relevant reward sets, their focus on the dual problem yields larger uncertainty. As mentioned in Sec.
3.2
, although it is consistent with our results at optimality, using approximate solvers may result in overly conservative performance. Moreover, the optimization problem studied there is unrelated to robust dynamic programming, which hinders the facilitation of robust RL. Also, both of these works focus on reward robustness. Differently,
Eysenbach and Levine 2022
address reward
and
transition uncertainty by showing that policies with maximum entropy regularization solve a particular type of robust MDP. Yet, their analysis separately treats the uncertainty on
P
and
r
, which questions the robustness of the resulting policy when the whole model
(P,r)
is adversarial. Moreover, the dual relation they establish between entropy regularization and transition-robust MDPs is weak and applies to specific uncertainty sets. Both of these works treat robustness as a side-effect of regularization more than an objective on its own, whereas we aim to do the opposite, namely, use regularization to solve robust RL problems.

Two works that do use regularization as a tool for achieving robust policies are
(
Derman and Mannor 2020
;
Kumar et al. 2022
)
. Through distributionally robust MDPs,
Derman and Mannor 2020
show upper and lower bounds between transition-robustness and regularization. There again, duality is weak and reward uncertainty is not addressed. Moreover, since the exact regularization term has no explicit form, it is usable through its upper bound only. Finally, regularization is applied on the mean of several value functions
v^{\pi}_{(\hat{P}_{i},r)}
, where each
\hat{P}_{i}
is a transition model estimated from an episode run. Computing this quantity requires as many policy evaluations as the number of model estimates available, which results in a linear complexity blowup at least.
Kumar et al. 2022
extend the conference version of this work
(
Derman et al. 2021b
)
by additionally constraining all kernels in
P_{0}+\mathcal{P}
to reside in a probability simplex. This yields less conservative policies at the expense of additional computational cost. Moreover, similarly as
Derman et al. 2021b
,
Kumar et al. 2022
focus on model-based methods, whereas we provably extend R
2

regularization to model-free learning.

Previous studies analyze robust planning algorithms to provide convergence guarantees. The works
(
Bagnell et al. 2001
;
Nilim and El Ghaoui 2005
)
propose robust value iteration, while
Iyengar 2005
;
Wiesemann et al. 2013
introduce robust policy iteration.
Kaufman and Schaefer 2013
generalize both schemes by proposing a robust MPI and determine the conditions under which it converges. The polynomial time within which all these works guarantee a robust solution is often insufficient, as the complexity of a Bellman update grows cubically in the number of states
(
Ho et al. 2018
)
.

In order to reduce the time complexity of robust planning algorithms,
Ho et al. 2018
;
Ho et al. 2021
propose faster methods able to compute robust Bellman updates in
\mathcal{O}(\lvert\St\rvert\lvert\A\rvert\log(\lvert\St\rvert\lvert\A\rvert))
operations for
\ell_{1}
-constrained uncertainty sets, while the algorithms from
(
Behzadian et al. 2021
)
require
\mathcal{O}(\lvert\St\rvert\lvert\A\rvert\log(\lvert\St\rvert))
operations for
\ell_{\infty}
-robust updates. Advantageously, our regularization approach reduces each such update to its standard, non-robust complexity of
\mathcal{O}(\lvert\St\rvert\lvert\A\rvert)
.
Moreover, although
Ho et al. 2018
;
Ho et al. 2021
;
Behzadian et al. 2021
address both
(s,a)
and
s
-rectangular uncertainty sets,
i.e.
, sets that are independently defined over each state-action pair or state only, they focus on transition uncertainty, whereas we tackle both reward and transition uncertainties in the general
s
-rectangular case. Finally, their contribution relies on LP formulations that necessitate restricting to one predefined norm while our method applies to any norm. This may come from the fact that our main Theorems (Thms.
1
and
2
) use Fenchel-Rockafellar duality
(
Rockafellar 1970
;
Borwein and Lewis 2010
)
, a generalization of LP duality (see Appx.
A.2
and
B.1
). More recently,
Grand-Clément and Kroer 2021
proposed a first-order method to accelerate robust value iteration under
s
-rectangular uncertainty sets that are either ellipsoidal or KL-constrained, while
Ho et al. 2022
provide an algorithmic speedup for general
f
-divergence robust MDPs. To our knowledge, our study is the first one reducing the time complexity of robust to standard planning when the uncertainty set is
s
-rectangular and constrained with an arbitrary norm.

Despite its theoretical guarantees, robust RL has been sparingly applied to deep settings. We believe this is due to its computational challenges, which this work aims to address. As mentioned in Sec.
3.3
, previous methods require samples from several models to train a robust policy
(
Pinto et al. 2017
;
Roy et al. 2017
;
Mankowitz et al. 2018
;
Derman et al. 2018
)
. Besides their increased sample complexity, these parallel simulations bind the learning process to finite uncertainty sets. Differently, the robust fitted value iteration method of
Lutter et al. 2021
learns a robust policy based on the nominal system’s observations only. It derives the worst-case model in closed form, so it can iterate over the value function in a standard way. Yet, the dynamics should be deterministic and satisfy structural assumptions while the nominal model needs to be known. In contrast, we study a general MDP setting without structure in the environment and propose a model-free algorithm that is guaranteed to converge to a robust optimal policy.

Recently,
Zhang et al. 2022
leveraged the R
2

regularization technique to derive robust policies. There, as in
(
Kumar et al. 2022
)
, an R
2

Bellman equation is provided for
q
-values without a formal equivalence to its robust analog being established. In fact, the uncertainty they consider here is not rectangular: the transition model is parameterized by uncertain parameters (like in
(
Wiesemann et al. 2013
)
), but these lie in a ball regardless of the state or action. Finally, while several notions of robustness may be addressed in deep RL methods,
e.g.
, action perturbation
(
Tessler et al. 2019
)
, execution delay
(
Derman et al. 2021a
)
or adversarial observations
(
Lutter et al. 2021
;
Zhang et al. 2020
)
, these are outside the scope of our study which focuses on robustness w.r.t. the transition and/or reward functions.

10
Conclusion and future work

In this work, we established a strong duality between robust MDPs and twice regularized MDPs. This revealed that the regularized MDPs of
Geist et al. 2019
are in fact robust MDPs with uncertain reward, which enabled us to derive a policy-gradient theorem for reward-robust MDPs. When extending this robustness-regularization duality to general robust MDPs, we found that the regularizer depends on the value function besides the policy. We thus introduced R
2

MDPs, a generalization of regularized MDPs with both policy and value regularization. The related R
2

Bellman operators lead us to propose a converging R
2

MPI (resp. R
2

q
-learning) algorithm that achieves the optimal robust value function within a similar computing time as standard MPI (resp.
q
-learning), as confirmed by our tabular experiments. Then, we devised a scalable method to estimate the value regularizer in an approximate setting. Finally, we tested the resulting R
2

DDQN algorithm on physical domains.

This study settles the theoretical foundations for scalable robust RL. We should note that our results naturally extend to continuous but compact action spaces in the same manner as standard MDPs do
(
Puterman 2014
)
. Theoretical extension to infinite state space would be more involved because of the state-dependent regularizer in R
2

MDPs. In fact, it would be interesting to study the R
2

MDP setting under function approximation, as such approximation would have a direct effect on the regularizer. Similarly, one could analyze approximate dynamic programming for R
2

MDPs in light of its robust analog
(
Tamar et al. 2014
;
Badrinath and Kalathil 2021
)
.
Apart from its practical effect, we believe our work opens the path to more theoretical contributions in robust RL. For example, extending R
2

MPI to the approximate case
(
Scherrer et al. 2015
)
would be an interesting problem to solve because of the R
2

evaluation operator being non-linear. So would be a sample complexity analysis for R
2

MDPs with a comparison to robust MDPs
(
Yang and Zhang 2021
)
. Another line of research is to extend policy-gradient to R
2

MDPs, as this would avoid parallel learning of adversarial models
(
Derman et al. 2018
;
Tessler et al. 2019
)
and be very useful for continuous control.

Proposition
.

For any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the robust optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v\leq T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v\text{ for all }(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}.

Proof

Let
v^{*}
an optimal point of (

v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}=T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}=\min_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}\leq T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
for all
(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}
, so the robust value is feasible and by optimality of
v^{*}

\langle v^{*},\mu_{0}\rangle\geq\langle v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}},\mu_{0}\rangle.
Now, we aim to show that any feasible
v\in\R^{\St}

Let an arbitrary
\epsilon>0

, there exists
(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})\in\mathcal{U}
such that

\displaystyle T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}+\epsilon>T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}.

(8)

This yields:

\displaystyle v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle=v-T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle[v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}=T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}]

\displaystyle<v+\epsilon-T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle[\text{By Eq.~\eqref{eq: epsilon inf}}]

\displaystyle\leq T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v+\epsilon-T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle[v\text{ is feasible for }\eqref{eq: iyengar ro primal}]

\displaystyle\leq T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}v+\epsilon-T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle[T^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}v\leq T_{(P,r)}^{\pi}v\text{ for all }(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}]

\displaystyle=T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}(v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}})+\epsilon.

\displaystyle[\text{By linearity of }T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}]

\displaystyle v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle\leq T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}(v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}})+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}(T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}(v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}})+\epsilon)+\epsilon

\displaystyle[u\leq w\implies T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}u\leq T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}w]

\displaystyle=(T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi})^{2}(v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}})+\gamma\epsilon+\epsilon

\displaystyle\leq(T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi})^{2}(T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}(v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}})+\epsilon)+\gamma\epsilon+\epsilon

\displaystyle v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}

\displaystyle[T_{(P_{\epsilon},r_{\epsilon})}^{\pi}\text{ is }\gamma\text{-contracting}]

v-v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}\leq\frac{\epsilon}{1-\gamma}

Proof of Theorem
1

Theorem

(Reward-robust MDP)
.

Assume that
\mathcal{U}=\{P_{0}\}\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R}).
Then, for any policy
\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}
, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of the convex optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }v(s)\leq T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})\text{ for all }s\in\St.

Proof

For all
s\in\St
, define:
F(s):=\max_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\pi}(s)-\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)\right\}
.
It corresponds to the robust counterpart of (
PU
) at
s\in\St
. Thus, the robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }F(s)\leq 0\text{ for all }s\in\St.

(9)

Based on the structure of the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}=\{P_{0}\}\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
, we compute the robust counterpart:

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=\max_{r^{\prime}\in r_{0}+\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\prime\pi}(s)-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{r^{\prime}:r^{\prime}=r_{0}+r,r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\prime\pi}(s)-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-(r_{0}^{\pi}(s)+r^{\pi}(s))-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle[(r_{0}+r)^{\pi}=r_{0}^{\pi}+r^{\pi}\quad\forall\pi\in\Delta_{\A}^{\St}]

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\pi}(s)-r_{0}^{\pi}(s)-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\pi}(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle[T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)=r_{0}^{\pi}(s)+\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)]

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\{-r^{\pi}(s)\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=\max_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{-r^{\pi}(s)-\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r^{\prime})\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{r^{\pi}(s)+\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s).

\displaystyle[r^{\pi}(s)=\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle]

By the rectangularity assumption,
\mathcal{R}=\times_{s\in\St}\mathcal{R}_{s}
and for all
r:=(r_{s})_{s\in\St}\in\R^{\X}
, we have

\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\St}\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s^{\prime}}}(r_{s^{\prime}})
. As such,

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\St}\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s^{\prime}}}(r_{s^{\prime}})\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(r_{s})\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s),

where the last equality holds since the objective function is minimal if and only if
r_{s}\in\mathcal{R}_{s}
.

We now aim to apply Fenchel-Rockafellar duality to the minimization problem. Let the function

f:\R^{\A}\to\R
defined as
r_{s}\mapsto\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle
, and consider
the support function
\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}:\R^{\A}\to\overline{\R}
together with
the identity mapping
\mathbf{Id}_{\A}:\R^{\A}\to\R^{\A}
.
Clearly,
\mathrm{dom}(f)=\R^{\A}
,
\mathrm{dom}(\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}})=\mathcal{R}_{s}
, and
\mathrm{dom}(\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}})-\mathbf{Id}_{\A}(\mathrm{dom}(f))=\mathcal{R}_{s}-\R^{\A}=\R^{\A}
. Therefore,
\mathrm{core}(\mathrm{dom}(\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}})-A(\mathrm{dom}(f)))=\mathrm{core}(\R^{\A})=\R^{\A}
and
0\in\R^{\A}
. We can thus apply Fenchel-Rockafellar duality: noting that
\mathbf{Id}_{\A}=(\mathbf{Id}_{\A})^{*}
and
(\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}})^{*}(y)=\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(y)
, we get

\displaystyle\min_{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}}\{f(r_{s})+\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(r_{s})\}

\displaystyle=-\min_{y\in\R^{\A}}\{f^{*}(-y)+(\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}})^{*}(y)\}=-\min_{y\in\R^{\A}}\{f^{*}(-y)+\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(y)\}.

It remains to compute

\displaystyle f^{*}(-y)=\max_{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}}-\langle r_{s},y\rangle-\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle=\max_{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}}\langle r_{s},-y-\pi_{s}\rangle=\begin{cases}0\text{ if }-y-\pi_{s}=0\\
+\infty\text{ otherwise}\end{cases},

and obtain

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=\min_{y\in\R^{\A}}\{f^{*}(-y)+\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(y)\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)=\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s).

We can thus rewrite the optimization problem (
9
) as:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)\leq 0\text{ for all }s\in\St,

which concludes the proof.

\displaystyle\qquad+\gamma\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)\langle P_{0}(\cdot|s,a),\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rangle-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})\quad[\text{Linearity of gradient \& product rule}]

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\A}\nabla\pi_{s}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)+\gamma\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)\langle P_{0}(\cdot|s,a),\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rangle-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad[q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)=r_{0}(s,a)+\gamma\langle P_{0}(\cdot|s,a),v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rangle]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)(\nabla\ln\pi_{s}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)+\gamma\langle P_{0}(\cdot|s,a),\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rangle)-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})\quad[\nabla\pi_{s}=\pi_{s}\nabla\ln(\pi_{s})]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)(\nabla\ln\pi_{s}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})+\gamma\langle P_{0}(\cdot|s,a),\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rangle).

Thus, the components of
\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}
are the non-regularized value functions corresponding to the modified reward
R(s,a):=\nabla\ln\pi_{s}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})
. By the fixed point property of the standard Bellman operator, it results that:

\displaystyle\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{I}_{\St}-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi})^{-1}\left(\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{\cdot}(a)(\nabla\ln\pi_{\cdot}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}({\cdot},a)-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{\cdot}))\right)(s)

and

\displaystyle\nabla J_{\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\St}\mu_{0}(s)\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\St}\mu_{0}(s)(\mathbf{I}_{\St}-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi})^{-1}\left(\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{\cdot}(a)(\nabla\ln\pi_{\cdot}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}({\cdot},a)-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{\cdot}))\right)(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\in\St}d_{\mu_{0},\pi}(s)\left(\sum_{a\in\A}\pi_{s}(a)\nabla\ln\pi_{s}(a)q^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s,a)-\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})\right),

by definition of
d_{\mu_{0},\pi}
.

The subtraction by
\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})
also appears in
(
Geist et al. 2019
)
. However, here, the gradient includes partial derivatives that depend on both the policy and the value itself. Let’s try to compute the gradient of the double regularizer
\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})=\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert(\alpha_{s}^{r}+\alpha_{s}^{P}\gamma\lVert v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rVert)
. By the chain-rule we have that:

\displaystyle\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\A}\frac{\partial\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}}{\partial\pi_{s}(a)}\nabla\pi_{s}(a)+\sum_{s\in\St}\frac{\partial\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}}{\partial v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)}\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in\A}(\alpha_{s}^{r}+\alpha_{s}^{P}\gamma\lVert v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rVert)\frac{\pi_{s}(a)}{\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert}\nabla\pi_{s}(a)+\sum_{s\in\St}\alpha_{s}^{P}\gamma\lVert\pi_{s}\rVert\frac{v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)}{\lVert v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}\rVert}\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s).

We remark here an interdependence between
\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})
and
\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)
: computing the gradient
\nabla\Omega_{v,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(\pi_{s})
requires to know
\nabla v^{\pi,\textsc{r\textsuperscript{2}}}(s)
and vice versa. There may be a recursion that still enables to compute these gradients, which we leave for future work.

Theorem

[v\cdot\pi_{s}](s^{\prime},a):=v(s^{\prime})\pi_{s}(a)\quad\forall(s^{\prime},a)\in\X

Proof

The robust value function
v^{\pi,\mathcal{U}}
is the optimal solution of:

\displaystyle\max_{v\in\R^{\St}}\langle v,\mu_{0}\rangle\text{ s. t. }F(s)\leq 0\text{ for all }s\in\St,

(12)

where
F(s):=\max_{(P,r)\in\mathcal{U}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\pi}(s)-\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)\right\}
is the robust counterpart of (
PU
) at
s\in\St
. Let’s compute it based on the structure of the uncertainty set
\mathcal{U}=(P_{0}+\mathcal{P})\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})
:

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=\max_{(P^{\prime},r^{\prime})\in(P_{0}+\mathcal{P})\times(r_{0}+\mathcal{R})}\left\{v(s)-r^{\prime\pi}(s)-\gamma P^{\prime\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}P^{\prime}:P^{\prime}=P_{0}+P,P\in\mathcal{P}\\
r^{\prime}:r^{\prime}=r_{0}+r,r\in\mathcal{R}\end{subarray}}\left\{v(s)-r^{\prime\pi}(s)-\gamma P^{\prime\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P},r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-(r_{0}^{\pi}(s)+r^{\pi}(s))-\gamma(P_{0}^{\pi}+P^{\pi})v(s)\right\}\quad[(P_{0}+P)^{\pi}=P_{0}^{\pi}+P^{\pi},

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad(r_{0}+r)^{\pi}=r_{0}^{\pi}+r^{\pi}]

\displaystyle=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P},r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-r_{0}^{\pi}(s)-r^{\pi}(s)-\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)-\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P},r\in\mathcal{R}}\left\{v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)-r^{\pi}(s)-\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)\right\}\quad[T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)=r_{0}^{\pi}(s)+\gamma P_{0}^{\pi}v(s)]

\displaystyle=\max_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\{-\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)\}+\max_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\{-r^{\pi}(s)\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{P\in\mathcal{P}}\{\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)\}-\min_{r\in\mathcal{R}}\{r^{\pi}(s)\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{P\in\R^{\X\times\St}}\{\gamma P^{\pi}v(s)+\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(P)\}-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{r^{\pi}(s)+\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\}

\displaystyle\qquad+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{P\in\R^{\X\times\St}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(P)\}-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\}

\displaystyle\qquad+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s).\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad[P^{\pi}v(s)=\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle,r^{\pi}(s)=\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle]

As shown in the proof of Thm.
1
,
\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\}=\min_{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(r_{s})\}
thanks to the rectangularity assumption. Similarly, by rectangularity of the transition uncertainty set, for all
P:=(P_{s})_{s\in\St}\in\R^{\X}
, we have

\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(P)=\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\St}\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s^{\prime}}}(P_{s^{\prime}})
. As such,

\displaystyle\min_{P\in\R^{\X\times\St}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{P}}(P)\}

\displaystyle=\min_{P\in\R^{\X\times\St}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\sum_{s^{\prime}\in\St}\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s^{\prime}}}(P_{s^{\prime}})\}

\displaystyle=\min_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(P_{s})\},

where the last equality holds since the objective function is minimal if and only if
P_{s}\in\mathcal{P}_{s}
.
Finally,

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(P_{s})\}-\min_{r_{s}\in\R^{\A}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(r_{s})\}+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s).

Referring to the proof of Thm.
1

-\min_{r\in\R^{\X}}\{\langle r_{s},\pi_{s}\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{R}}(r)\}=\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})

\displaystyle F(s)

\displaystyle=-\min_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(P_{s})\}+\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s).

v\cdot\pi_{s}\in\R^{\X}

[v\cdot\pi_{s}](s^{\prime},a):=v(s^{\prime})\pi_{s}(a)
for all
(s^{\prime},a)\in\X
. Further define

\varphi(P_{s})=\gamma\langle P_{s},v\cdot\pi_{s}\rangle.

Then, we have that:

\displaystyle\min_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\{\gamma\langle P^{\pi}_{s},v\rangle+\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(P_{s})\}=\min_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\{\varphi(P_{s})+\delta_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(P_{s})\}=-\min_{\B\in\R^{\X}}\{\varphi^{*}(-\B)+\sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(\B)\},

where the last equality results from Fenchel-Rockafellar duality and the fact that

It thus remains to compute the convex conjugate of
\varphi

\displaystyle\varphi^{*}(-\B)

\displaystyle=\max_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\left\{\langle P_{s},-\B\rangle-\varphi(P_{s})\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\left\{\langle P_{s},-\B\rangle-\gamma\langle P_{s},v\cdot\pi_{s}\rangle\right\}

\displaystyle=\max_{P_{s}\in\R^{\X}}\langle P_{s},-\B-\gamma v\cdot\pi_{s}\rangle

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0\text{ if }-\B-\gamma v\cdot\pi_{s}=0\\

which yields
\min_{\B\in\R^{\X}}\{\varphi^{*}(-\B)+\sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(\B)\}=\sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(-\gamma v\cdot\pi_{s})

Finally, the robust counterpart rewrites as:
F(s)=\sigma_{\mathcal{P}_{s}}(-\gamma v\cdot\pi_{s})+\sigma_{\mathcal{R}_{s}}(-\pi_{s})+v(s)-T_{(P_{0},r_{0})}^{\pi}v(s),

and plugging it into the optimization problem (
12
) yields the desired result.
