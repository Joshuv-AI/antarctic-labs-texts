Spectral Galerkin method for the zero dispersion limit of the fractional Korteweg-de Vries equation
a Mukul Dwivedi * and b Tanmay Sarkar

Abstract
. We present a fully discrete Crank-Nicolson Fourier-spectral-Galerkin (FSG) scheme for approximating solutions of the fractional Korteweg-de Vries (KdV) equation, which involves a fractional Laplacian with exponent α ∈ [ 1 , 2 ] \alpha\in[1,2] and a small dispersion coefficient of order ε 2 , ε ≪ 1 \varepsilon^{2},~\varepsilon\ll 1 . The solution in the limit as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 is known as the zero dispersion limit. We demonstrate that the semi-discrete FSG scheme conserves the first three integral invariants, thereby structure preserving, and the fully discrete FSG scheme is L 2 L^{2} -conservative, ensuring stability. Using the compactness argument, we constructively prove the convergence of the approximate solution to the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation in C ⁡ ( [ 0 , T ] , H p 1 + α ​ ( I ) ) C([0,T];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I)) for the periodic initial data in H p 1 + α ​ ( I ) H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I) . The devised scheme achieves spectral accuracy for the initial data in H p r , r ≥ 1 + α H_{p}^{r},~r\geq 1+\alpha and exponential accuracy for the analytic initial data. Additionally, we establish that the approximation of the zero dispersion limit obtained from the fully discrete FSG scheme converges to the solution of the Hopf equation in L 2 L^{2} as ε → 0 \varepsilon\to 0 , up to the gradient catastrophe time t c t_{c} . Beyond t c t_{c} , numerical investigations reveal that the approximation converges to the asymptotic solution, which is weakly described by the Whitham’s averaged equation within the oscillatory zone for α = 2 \alpha=2 . Numerical results are provided to demonstrate the convergence of the scheme and to validate the theoretical findings.

1.
Introduction

In recent years, differential equations involving the fractional Laplacian operator have become significant tools across various scientific, economic, and engineering fields due to their capability to model processes with anomalous diffusion and non-local interactions
[
5
,
37
]
. The fractional Laplacian, defined as a non-local operator, is particularly effective in capturing localized phenomena, such as image processing, fluid flow in porous media, and complex plasma dynamics (see
[
28
,
37
]
and references therein).

This paper focuses on the Cauchy problem for the fractional Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation, defined by

\begin{cases}u_{t}+6uu_{x}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u_{x}=0,&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}_{T}:=\mathbb{R}\times(0,T],\\
u(x,0)=u_{0}(x),&x\in\mathbb{R},\end{cases}

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}:=(-\Delta)^{\alpha/2}
represents the fractional Laplacian with exponent
\alpha\in[1,2]
,
\varepsilon^{2}
is a small dispersion coefficient,
u_{0}
is the given initial condition, and
u:\mathbb{R}_{T}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is the solution to be determined. The fractional Laplacian is defined in terms of the Fourier transform for the exponent
\alpha\in(0,2]
as

\widehat{[\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}u]}(\xi)=|\xi|^{\alpha}\widehat{[u]}(\xi),

(1.2)

where
\widehat{[u]}
denotes the Fourier transform of
u
. This operator introduces non-local effects into the dynamics of the equation, thereby extending the classical KdV model to incorporate fractional dispersion.

The fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) is a nonlinear, non-local dispersive model that arises in the study of weakly nonlinear long internal waves
[
9
,
11
,
23
,
24
,
35
]
. When
\alpha=2
, the equation reduces to the classical KdV equation, known for modeling solitons and nonlinear wave phenomena
[
38
,
2
,
9
,
27
]
. For
\alpha=1
, it corresponds to the Benjamin-Ono (BO) equation, which describes one-dimensional internal waves in deep stratified fluids
[
14
,
25
,
40
,
35
]
. The well-posedness of the fractional KdV equation has been extensively studied in the Sobolev spaces
H^{s}(\mathbb{R})
, with significant results including local well-posedness for
s>3/4
(see
[
23
,
24
]
), and global well-posedness for
L^{2}
initial data using frequency-dependent renormalization techniques
[
18
]
.

Despite these theoretical advancements, numerical methods for solving the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) for
\alpha\in(1,2)
are limited. Existing approaches include operator splitting
[
9
]
, Galerkin methods
[
12
]
, and finite difference schemes
[
11
]
. For the classical cases of
\alpha=1
and
\alpha=2
, numerous numerical methods have been developed, such as finite difference methods

1.1.
Challenges and Motivations

While numerical schemes for the KdV and BO equations have been well studied, challenges remain in the convergence analysis of the Fourier Galerkin scheme to the unique solution of the corresponding equations. Specifically, for spectral methods, a significant gap exists in establishing rigorous convergence proofs for the approximations, particularly in non-local, fractional settings. For instance, the convergence of spectral Galerkin methods to the unique solution of the KdV and BO equations is still underdeveloped. Furthermore, the numerical approximation of the zero dispersion limit where
\varepsilon\to 0
poses additional challenges, especially after the critical time
t_{c}
when gradient catastrophe occurs, leading to the emergence of oscillatory behavior in the solution.
The foundational works of Lax and Levermore
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
on the zero dispersion limit of the KdV equation, and numerical studies followed by Grava and Klein
[
16
,
17
]
, established that numerical methods can accurately capture the oscillatory structures arising due to small dispersion coefficients. However, up to our knowledge, there is quite limited literature available on numerical schemes that effectively capture these oscillations, particularly for fractional KdV equations. This gap motivates our work to approximate the zero-dispersion limit of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) accurately and capture the correct asymptotic behavior of solutions.

The spectral Galerkin method
[
1
,
46
]
, in particular, offers distinct advantages over traditional finite difference or finite element approaches when dealing with periodic and smooth initial data. These methods are higher order accurate and possess efficient resolution of fine-scale structures due to their global basis functions, making them well-suited for handling the oscillatory nature of dispersive waves. However, the lack of comprehensive convergence analysis, especially for the fractional equations, necessitates a detailed investigation into their stability and convergence behavior. Moreover, we would like to study the spectral Galerkin method in the small dispersion limit.

1.2.
Objectives and Contributions

The primary objective of this paper is to develop and analyze a fully discrete Crank-Nicolson Fourier spectral Galerkin (FSG) scheme for approximating solutions to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) with periodic initial data. Our main contributions are as follows:

•

Conservation and stability:
We demonstrate that the semi-discrete FSG scheme preserves the first three integral invariants for all
\alpha\in[1,2]
, ensuring structure preservation. For the fully discrete scheme, we establish
L^{2}
-conservation, which guarantees
L^{2}
-stability of the numerical solution. In addition, we prove that the numerical approximations are uniformly bounded in the periodic
H_{p}^{1+\alpha}
space, and that the temporal derivative of the approximations is bounded in the periodic
L^{2}
space, contributing to the overall stability and reliability of the scheme.

•

Convergence to unique solution:
Using compactness arguments, we prove that the numerical approximation converges to the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) in
C([0,T];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(\mathbb{R}))
. While our convergence proof, as by product, can be viewed as a constructive proof of the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
).

•

Error analysis:
We derive error estimates for the proposed scheme, demonstrating spectral accuracy in space and second order accuracy in time for periodic initial data in
H_{p}^{r}
with
r\geq 1+\alpha
, and exponential accuracy for analytic initial data.

•

Zero dispersion limit:
We numerically analyze the zero dispersion limit of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) by analyzing the behavior of solutions as
\varepsilon\to 0
. Prior to the critical time
t_{c}
, the solution of the classical KdV equation converges to that of the Hopf equation for smooth initial data as
\varepsilon
approaches zero. We extend this result to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) by using the Kato’s theory
[
22
]
. Beyond the critical time
t_{c}
, within the oscillation zone, the solution is locally described by Whitham’s averaged equation, while outside this zone, it aligns with the Hopf equation. Our numerical results indicate that the fully discrete FSG scheme captures this asymptotic behavior, showing convergence to the asymptotic solution of the classical KdV equation for values of
\alpha
close to 2.

•

Numerical validation:
We provide numerical examples to validate the theoretical analysis and to demonstrate the convergence and accuracy of the proposed scheme in approximating the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
).

In this paper,
C
denotes a generic constant whose value can change in each step and it is independent of both the spatial discretization parameter
N
and time discretization parameter
\Delta t
.

Notations and Preliminary Results

2.1.
Periodic Sobolev Spaces and Fourier Analysis

We consider functions defined on
\mathbb{R}
that are
2\pi
-periodic. For simplicity, we restrict these functions to the interval
I:=[-\pi,\pi]
and extend them periodically to the entire real line. The Hilbert space
L^{2}(I)
consists of all square-integrable functions over
I
, with the norm and inner product defined by

\left\|u\right\|=\left(\int_{I}|u(x)|^{2}\,dx\right)^{1/2}\quad\text{and}\quad(u,v)=\int_{I}u(x)\overline{v(x)}\,dx

respectively, for all
u,v\in L^{2}(I)
, where
\overline{v}
denotes the complex conjugate of
v
.

For
r\geq 0
, the Sobolev space
H^{r}(I):=W^{r,2}(I)
is equipped with the norm
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{r}
. We denote the periodic Sobolev spaces of exponent
r\geq 0
by
H_{p}^{r}(I)
, consisting of all
2\pi
-periodic functions in
H^{r}(I)
. For a function
f\in H_{p}^{r}(I)
, the norm is defined as

\left\|f\right\|_{r}:=\left(\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}(1+|k|^{2})^{r}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\right)^{1/2},

where
\hat{f}
represents the Fourier coefficient of
f
, given by

\hat{f}(k):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{I}f(x)e^{-ikx}\,dx,\quad\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}.

The Fourier expansion of
f\in H_{p}^{r}(I)
,
r\geq 0
, is given by

f(x)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},

(2.1)

which converges almost everywhere. Clearly,
H_{p}^{r}(I)
is a subspace of
H^{r}(I)
with the norm
\|\cdot\|_{r}
, and
L_{p}^{2}(I):=H^{0}_{p}(I)
with the norm
\|\cdot\|:=\|\cdot\|_{0}
.
The function space
C_{c}^{1}\big([0,T];H^{r}_{p}(I)\big)
for
r\geq 0
is defined as

{C_{c}^{1}\big([0,T];H^{r}_{p}(I)\big)=\left\{\varphi\in C^{1}([0,T];H^{r}_{p}(I))\;\middle|\;\,\text{Supp}(\varphi(x,\cdot))\subset[0,T]\text{ for fixed }x\in I\right\}.}

For any
N\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the approximation space consisting of real-valued trigonometric polynomials of degree
N
by

V_{N}:=\text{span}\{e^{ikx}:-N\leq k\leq N\}.

The spaces
V_{N}
,
N\in\mathbb{N}
, serve as natural approximations of
L_{p}^{2}(I)
by periodic functions. Moreover, the basis functions
\{e^{ikx}\}_{k=-N}^{N}
of
V_{N}
are orthogonal. We define the projection operator
P_{N}:L^{2}(I)\to V_{N}
by

P_{N}f(x):=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},

(2.2)

and due to the orthogonality of the basis functions,
P_{N}
is an orthogonal projection onto
V_{N}
. Equivalently, we have

(P_{N}f-f,\phi)=0,\quad\forall\,\phi\in V_{N}.

(2.3)

Additionally, for any
f\in H^{r}(I)
,
r\geq 0
, it holds that
P_{N}f\to f
in
L^{2}(I)
as
N\to\infty
. In particular, we have the following result.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
r\geq 0
be any real number and
r\geq s\geq 0
. Then, for
f\in H^{r}(I)
, there exists a positive constant
C
, independent of
N
, such that the following estimate holds

\left\|f-P_{N}f\right\|_{s}\leq CN^{-r+s}\left\|f\right\|_{r}.

(2.4)

The above result is well-known and can be found in
[
19
]
. However, for completeness, we provide a proof for the case
s=0
.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
: Using Parseval’s identity and the projection property (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\left\|f-P_{N}f\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|f\right\|^{2}-(f,P_{N}f)-(P_{N}f,f)+\left\|P_{N}f\right\|^{2}=2\pi\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}-2\pi\sum_{k=-N}^{N}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{|k|>N}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}=2\pi\sum_{|k|>N}\frac{1}{(1+|k|^{2})^{r}}(1+|k|^{2})^{r}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi N^{-2r}\sum_{|k|>N}(1+|k|^{2})^{r}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\leq CN^{-2r}\left\|f\right\|_{r}^{2}.

This completes the proof.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
f
be an analytic function. Then, there exist positive constants
C
and
c
, independent of
N
, such that

\left\|f-P_{N}f\right\|\leq Ce^{-cN}\left\|f\right\|.

(2.5)

Proof.

For all
r\geq 0
, it is known
[
39
]
that

\left\|f\right\|_{r}\leq Cr!\left\|f\right\|.

Utilizing Proposition
2.1
and Stirling’s formula
[
39
]
, we have

\displaystyle\left\|f-P_{N}f\right\|\leq CN^{-r}\left\|f\right\|_{r}\leq C\frac{r!}{N^{r}}\left\|f\right\|\leq C\frac{r^{r}e^{-r}}{N^{r}}\left\|f\right\|\leq Ce^{-cN}\left\|f\right\|,

where we have assumed that
r
is proportional to
N
. This completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Fractional Laplacian and its properties

We define the fractional Laplacian
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}
for
\alpha\geq 0
using the Fourier series expansion (
2.1
) of a function
f\in H^{r}_{p}(I)
as follows

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f(x)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx}.

(2.6)

The following lemma states several important properties of the fractional Laplacian (
2.6
).

Lemma 2.3
.

The fractional Laplacian (
2.6
) satisfies the following properties

i)

For
f,g\in H^{\alpha}_{p}(I)
,
\alpha\geq 0
, we have

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f,g\right)=\left(f,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g\right),

(2.7)

and

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f_{x},f\right)=0.

(2.8)

ii)

Let
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}\geq 0
, then for all
f,g\in H_{p}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}(I)
, we have

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}f,g\right)=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}f,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}g\right),

(2.9)

and equivalently,

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}f=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}f=\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}f.

(2.10)

iii)

For an orthogonal projection
P_{N}
defined by (
2.3
) and
f\in H^{r}_{p}(I)
,
r\geq\alpha\geq 0
, the fractional Laplacian (
2.6
) with exponent
\alpha
commutes with
P_{N}
, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(P_{N}f(x))=P_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f(x).

(2.11)

Proof.

By using the Fourier expansion (
2.1
) for
f
and
g
, and the orthogonality of
\{e^{ikx}\}
, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f,g\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{g}(k)e^{ikx}\right)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(k)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{g}(k)e^{ikx}\right)=\left(f,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g\right).

Now using (
2.7
) and periodicity of
f
, we have

\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f_{x},f\right)=-\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f,f_{x}\right)=-\left(f,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f_{x}\right)=-\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f_{x},f\right),

which yields (
2.8
). Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}f,g\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{g}(k)e^{ikx}\right)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(k)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha_{1}}|k|^{\alpha_{2}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(k)=\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha_{1}}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx},\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}|k|^{\alpha_{2}}\hat{g}(k)e^{ikx}\right)

\displaystyle=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{1}}f,\mathcal{D}^{\alpha_{2}}g\right).

which implies (
2.9
). The identity (
2.10
) follows directly from the definition (
2.6
).

For (
2.11
), using definitions (
2.2
) and (
2.6
), we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(P_{N}f(x))

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{k=-N}^{N}|k|^{\alpha}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx}=P_{N}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f(x).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
f,g\in H^{\alpha}_{p}(I)
,
\alpha\geq 0
. Then the following estimate holds

\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(fg)\leq C(\alpha)\left(f\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g+g\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f\right),

(2.12)

where
C(\alpha)
is a constant depending on
\alpha
. Furthermore, there holds

\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(fg)\right\|\leq C(\alpha)\left(\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g\right\|+\left\|g\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f\right\|\right),

(2.13)

where
C(\alpha)=\max\left\{1,2^{\alpha-1}\right\}.

Proof.

To prove (
2.12
), we start by considering the Fourier series representation of the product
fg
. The Fourier coefficient of
fg
is given by

\widehat{fg}(m)=\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(m-k).

(2.14)

Applying the fractional derivative operator
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}
to the product
fg
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(fg)(x)

\displaystyle=\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|m|^{\alpha}\widehat{fg}(m)e^{imx}=\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}|m|^{\alpha}\left(\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(m-k)\right)e^{imx}.

We employ the inequality
|m|^{\alpha}\leq C(\alpha)\left(|k|^{\alpha}+|m-k|^{\alpha}\right)
for
\alpha\geq 0
where
m,k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
C(\alpha)=\max\left\{1,2^{\alpha-1}\right\}
. Furthermore, incorporating the inequality (
2.14
), we can estimate the expression as follows

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(fg)(x)

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\Big[\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}{|k|^{\alpha}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(m-k)e^{imx}

\displaystyle\qquad+\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}{|m-k|^{\alpha}}\hat{f}(k)\hat{g}(m-k)e^{imx}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\Big[\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f}(k)\hat{g}(m-k)e^{imx}+\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\sum\limits_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\hat{f}(k)\widehat{\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g}(m-k)e^{imx}\Big]

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\Big[\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{g\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f}(m)e^{imx}+\sum\limits_{m\in\mathbb{Z}}\widehat{f\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g}(m)e^{imx}\Big]

\displaystyle=C(\alpha)\big(g(x)\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f(x)+f(x)\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g(x)\big).

This proves inequality (
2.12
).
To prove (
2.13
), we take the
L^{2}
-norm on both sides of (
2.12
)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(fg)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(\alpha)\left(\left\|f\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}g\right\|+\left\|g\right\|_{\infty}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}f\right\|\right).

Hence the result follows.
∎

3.
Fourier-Spectral-Galerkin Scheme: Stability and Convergence

The Fourier-Spectral-Galerkin (FSG) scheme for the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) is formulated as follows. Let
u_{0}\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
, we seek an approximation
U_{\varepsilon}\in V_{N}
such that for all
\phi\in V_{N}
, the approximation
U_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left((U_{\varepsilon})_{t}+6U_{\varepsilon}(U_{\varepsilon})_{x}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\right)=0,\qquad 0\leq t\leq T,\ \varepsilon\in\mathbb{R},\\
U_{\varepsilon}(0)=P_{N}u_{0},\end{cases}

(3.1)

where
P_{N}
denotes the projection operator and is defined by (
2.2
). Given the nonlinearity present in the scheme, it is crucial to establish the existence and uniqueness of the solution to (
3.1
). We state this in the following lemma. The approximation
U_{\varepsilon}\in V_{N}
represents the spectral Galerkin projection of the exact solution to (
1.1
), where the subscript emphasizes the dependence on the dispersion coefficient
\varepsilon
. Following the work of Kenig et al.
[
23
]
, the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) possesses the following conserved quantities:

\displaystyle I_{1}(u):=\int_{I}u\,dx,\qquad I_{2}(u):=\int_{I}u^{2}\,dx,\qquad I_{3}(u):=\int_{I}\left(\varepsilon^{2}(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}u)^{2}-2u^{3}\right)dx,

where
I_{1}
,
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
represents the
mass, momentum and energy
, respectively. In the following lemma, we establish discrete analogs of these conserved quantities for our numerical scheme (
3.1
).

Lemma 3.1
.

There exists a unique solution
U_{\varepsilon}
to the equation (
3.1
). Moreover, the solution satisfies the first three conserved quantities of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
), specifically

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}U_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(U_{\varepsilon}(x,t))^{2}\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left[\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}U_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\right)^{2}-2U^{3}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\right)\,dx\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(3.4)

Proof.

We choose test functions
\phi=e^{ikx}
, for
k=-N,-N+1,\ldots,N-1,N
in the scheme (
3.1
) and use the fact that since
u_{0}
is real-valued,
U_{\varepsilon}(0)
is also real-valued. This implies
\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,t)=\overline{\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,t)}
, leading to the following system of equations for the Fourier coefficients
\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,t)
of
U_{\varepsilon}

\displaystyle\begin{cases}(\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,t))_{t}=-3ik\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}\ast\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,t)+ik|k|^{\alpha}\varepsilon^{2}\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,t),\\
\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}(k,0)=\hat{u}_{0}(k),\end{cases}

(3.5)

for
k=-N,-N+1,\ldots,N-1,N
.
The right-hand side of (
3.5
) is locally Lipschitz continuous in
\hat{U}_{\varepsilon}
with respect to the
L^{2}
norm. By the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem, there exists a unique solution to (
3.5
). This implies that there exists a maximal time
t_{0},0<t_{0}\leq T
, such that for all
t<t_{0}
, (
3.5
) admits a local unique solution, which implies (
3.1
) has a unique solution
{U}_{\varepsilon}(t)
for all
t<t_{0}
. Now, choosing the test function
\phi=U_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.1
) and using the property (
2.8
) yields

\displaystyle((U_{\varepsilon})_{t},U_{\varepsilon})

\displaystyle=-6(U_{\varepsilon}(U_{\varepsilon})_{x},U_{\varepsilon})+\varepsilon^{2}(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U_{\varepsilon})_{x},U_{\varepsilon})=0,

which further implies

\|U_{\varepsilon}(T)\|=\|U_{\varepsilon}(0)\|=\|u_{0}\|,

(3.6)

which implies (
3.3
) and ensures that the solution does not blow up for all
t\leq T
, and
T
can be chosen sufficiently large. This establishes the global existence of the solution to (
3.1
).
To show (
3.2
), we choose the test function
\phi=1
in (
3.1
) and use the periodicity of
U_{\varepsilon}
, which gives (
3.2
).
In order to show (
3.4
), we choose the test function
\phi=P_{N}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)
in (
3.1
) to get

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(U_{\varepsilon})_{t}P_{N}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dx=-\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\right)_{x}P_{N}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dx.

(3.7)

We estimate the left-hand side of the above equation utilizing the identity (
2.3
) and using the fact that
(U_{\varepsilon})_{t}
is in
V_{N}
, which implies

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(U_{\varepsilon})_{t}P_{N}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(U_{\varepsilon})_{t}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}(U_{\varepsilon}^{3})_{t}-\varepsilon^{2}(U_{\varepsilon})_{t}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\,dx

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(U^{3}_{\varepsilon}(x,t)-\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\big(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha/2}U_{\varepsilon}(x,t)\big)^{2}\right)\,dx,

(3.8)

where we have used the semigroup property (
2.9
) of the fractional Laplacian.
On the other hand, we estimate the right-hand side of (
3.7
) by utilizing the orthogonal property (
2.3
) of
P_{N}
and using the fact that
P_{N}
commutes with derivatives

\displaystyle\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U_{\varepsilon})\right)_{x}P_{N}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)\,dx=\frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(P_{N}\big(3U_{\varepsilon}^{2}-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U_{\varepsilon}\big)\right)^{2}_{x}\,dx=0.

(3.9)

Thus, from identities (
3.7
), (
3.8
), and (
3.9
), we obtain (
3.4
). This completes the proof.
∎

We employ the Crank-Nicolson method for time discretization in (
3.1
) to define the fully discrete FSG scheme. Let
\Delta t
be the time step size, and define
U_{\varepsilon}^{m}=U_{\varepsilon}(t_{m})
, where
t_{m}=m\Delta t
for
m=0,1,\ldots,M
, with
t_{M}=T
for a given
0<T<\infty
. The fully discrete FSG scheme is formulated as follows: given
U_{\varepsilon}^{m}\in V_{N}
, find
U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon}\in V_{N}
such that, for all test functions
\phi\in V_{N}
, the scheme satisfies

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\left(U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon},\phi\right)=\left(U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\phi\right)-6\Delta t\left(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\right),\\
U^{0}_{\varepsilon}=P_{N}u_{0},\end{cases}

(3.10)

for
m=0,1,\ldots,M-1
and
U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}=(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon})/2
. Clearly, we have
\|U^{0}_{\varepsilon}\|=\|P_{N}u_{0}\|\leq\|u_{0}\|
.
Note that the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
) conserves the
L^{2}
norm, leading to the following stability result.

Lemma 3.2
.

The fully discrete scheme (
3.10
) is
L^{2}-stable
. Moreover, for the approximation
U^{m}_{\varepsilon}
, we have the following
L^{2}
-bound

\left\|U_{\varepsilon}^{m}\right\|\leq C,\qquad\text{ for all }n\text{ and }\varepsilon,

(3.11)

where
C
is a constant independent of
m
and
\varepsilon
.

Proof.

By choosing the test function
\phi=U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}
in (
3.10
) and utilizing identity (
2.8
) yields

\left\|U_{\varepsilon}^{m+1}\right\|^{2}=\left\|U_{\varepsilon}^{m}\right\|^{2},

which implies the estimate (
3.11
) and scheme (
3.10
) is
L^{2}
-stable.
∎

3.1.
Solvability of the fully discrete FSG scheme

Given that the non-linear part of the scheme involves an implicit term, it is necessary to address the solvability of the system at each time step. To solve the non-linear system, we introduce an iterative sequence
\{v^{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
such that for each
\ell\geq 0
,
v^{\ell}\in V_{N}
satisfy the following system

\begin{cases}(v^{\ell+1},\phi)=(U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\phi)-\Delta t\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell}}{2}\right),\phi\right)+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell+1})_{x},\phi\right),\\
v^{0}=U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\end{cases}

(3.12)

for all test functions
\phi\in V_{N}
, where
\mathcal{B}(v)=6vv_{x}
.
The above iterative scheme can be considered as an FSG scheme for a linear problem involving the bilinear form in
v^{\ell+1}
and
U^{m}_{\varepsilon}
. The scheme (
3.12
) can be rewritten as

\left(\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}\right)v^{\ell+1},\phi\right)=(U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\phi)-\Delta t\left(\mathcal{B}\left(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell}}{2}\right),\phi\right)+\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\right).

Setting
d_{ij}=\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}\phi_{j},\phi_{i}\right)=-\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\right)=-d_{ji}
, where
\phi_{j}\in V_{N}
for all
-N\leq i,j\leq N
, forming an orthogonal basis for
V_{N}
. Choosing
\phi=\phi_{j}
for
-N\leq j\leq N
and writing
v^{\ell+1}
as a linear combination of
\phi_{j}
, the left-hand side coefficient matrix becomes
I-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t}{2}D
, where
D=(d_{ij})
is skew-symmetric. Since
I-\frac{\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t}{2}D
is non-singular, the existence and uniqueness of
v^{\ell+1}
in (
3.12
) is ensured.
The solvability of the iterative scheme (
3.12
) is established in the next lemma. Specifically, we prove that for a given
U_{\varepsilon}^{m}
, the iteration
v^{\ell}
converges to the next time step solution
U_{\varepsilon}^{m+1}
as
\ell\to\infty
. Additionally, we prove that the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
) remains stable in higher-order Sobolev spaces. We begin by demonstrating the stability of the approximation at each time step.

Lemma 3.3
.

Assume
U^{m}_{\varepsilon}
is an approximate solution obtained by the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
) at the
m
-th time step. Suppose
(m+1)\Delta t\leq T
, for some
T>0
, and let
v^{\ell}
solve the iterative scheme (
3.12
). Furthermore, assume that
\Delta t
is sufficiently small such that at the
m
-th step it satisfies

N\Delta t\leq\frac{\zeta}{C\eta\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}},

(3.13)

where
\zeta\in(0,1)
,
\eta=\frac{8-\zeta}{1-\zeta}>8
,
\alpha\in[1,2]
, and
C
is some constant independent of
\Delta t
and
N
. Then the sequence
\{v^{\ell}\}_{\ell\geq 0}
converges, and

\lim\limits_{\ell\to\infty}v^{\ell}=U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon}.

(3.14)

Moreover, the approximation
U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon}
satisfies

\left\|U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\eta\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.15)

Proof.

We start with the iteration (
3.12
) for
\ell=0
. For
v^{1}
, it satisfies

(v^{1},\phi)=(U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\phi)-\Delta t\left(\mathcal{B}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}),\phi\right)+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{1})_{x},\phi\right).

(3.16)

Choosing the test function
\phi=\mathcal{D}^{2\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{1})_{xx}{\in V_{N}}
in (
3.16
), and using identity (
2.8
) and Young’s inequality, we have

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v^{1}_{x}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}+\Delta t\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{1})_{x}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}+{2}\Delta t^{2}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}+\frac{1}{4}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v^{1}_{x}\right\|^{2},

implies

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v^{1}_{x}\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}+\frac{8}{3}\Delta t^{2}\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}.

(3.17)

Using the Lemma
2.4
, Lemma
A.1
and
C(\alpha)=\max\{1,2^{\alpha-1}\}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 6\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}((U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x})^{2}\right\|^{2}+6\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{xx})\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 6\Big(4C(\alpha)^{2}\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|^{2}+2C(\alpha)^{2}\|(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{xx}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}

\displaystyle+2C(\alpha)^{2}\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}^{2}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{xx}\|^{2}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq CN^{2}\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}^{4},

(3.18)

for some constant
C
independent of
\Delta t
and
N
. Applying the Sobolev inequalities from Lemma
A.1
and estimate (
3.18
) in (
3.17
), we get

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v^{1}_{x}\right\|\leq(2+{C}N^{2}\Delta t^{2}\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}^{2})^{1/2}\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.19)

The time step condition (
3.13
) implies

(2+{C}N^{2}\Delta t^{2}\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}^{2})^{1/2}\leq 2.

As a consequence, (
3.19
) reduces to

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v^{1}_{x}\right\|\leq 2\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.20)

Similarly, we estimate the lower order derivatives
\left\|v^{1}_{xx}\right\|,\left\|v^{1}_{x}\right\|
, and
\left\|v^{1}\right\|
, which leads to

\displaystyle\left\|v^{1}\right\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\eta\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.21)

Afterwards, the iteration (
3.12
) can be rewritten as

\left(\Big(1-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}\Big)v^{\ell+1},\phi\right)=(U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\phi)-\Delta t\left(\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell}}{2}\Big),\phi\right)+\frac{\Delta t}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\right).

Subtracting the consecutive iterations, we have

\left(\Big(1-\frac{\Delta t}{2}\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}\Big)\delta(v^{\ell}),\phi\right)=-\Delta t\left(\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell}}{2}\Big)-\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell-1}}{2}\Big),\phi\right),

(3.22)

where
\delta(v^{\ell})=v^{\ell+1}-v^{\ell}
. Choosing
\phi=\mathcal{D}^{2\alpha}\delta(v^{\ell})_{xx}\in V_{N}
in (
3.22
), we have

\displaystyle\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\delta(v^{\ell})_{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-\Delta t\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell}}{2}\Big)_{x}-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell-1}}{2}\Big)_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\delta(v^{\ell})_{x}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\Delta t\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell}}{2}\Big)_{x}-\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}\Big(\frac{U^{m}_{\varepsilon}+v^{\ell-1}}{2}\Big)_{x}\right\|\left\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\delta(v^{\ell})_{x}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq CN\Delta t\max\{\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{2},\|v^{\ell}\|_{1+\alpha},\|v^{\ell-1}\|_{1+\alpha}\}\|\delta(v^{\ell-1})\|_{1+\alpha}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\delta(v^{\ell-1})_{x}\|,

which simplifies to

\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\delta(v^{\ell})_{x}\|\leq CN\Delta t\max\{\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha},\|v^{\ell}\|_{1+\alpha},\|v^{\ell-1}\|_{1+\alpha}\}\|\delta(v^{\ell-1})\|_{1+\alpha}.

Similar estimates can be obtained for the lower order derivatives as well. Consequently, we end up with

\|\delta(v^{\ell})\|_{1+\alpha}\leq CN\Delta t\max\{\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha},\|v^{\ell}\|_{1+\alpha},\|v^{\ell-1}\|_{1+\alpha}\}\|\delta(v^{\ell-1})\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.23)

In particular, for
\ell=1
, estimate (
3.23
) yields

\displaystyle\|\delta(v^{1})\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq CN\Delta t\max\{\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha},\|v^{1}\|_{1+\alpha}\}\|\delta(v^{0})\|_{1+\alpha}

(3.24)

\displaystyle\leq CN\Delta t\eta\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\|\delta(v^{0})\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\zeta\|\delta(v^{0})\|_{1+\alpha},

where we have used the estimate (
3.21
) and time step condition (
3.13
). With the help of estimates (
3.21
) and (
3.24
), we proceed by induction. Let us assume that the following estimates hold

\displaystyle\|v^{\ell}\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\eta\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha},

(3.25)

\displaystyle\|\delta(v^{\ell})\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\zeta\|\delta(v^{\ell-1})\|_{1+\alpha},

(3.26)

for
\ell=1,2,\cdots,d
. We claim that the above estimates hold for
\ell=d+1
. Using the triangle inequality, assumption (
3.26
), and time step condition (
3.13
), we obtain

\displaystyle\|v^{d+1}\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{d}\|\delta(v^{\ell})\|_{1+\alpha}+\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\|v^{1}-U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\sum\limits_{\ell=0}^{d}\zeta^{\ell}+\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\zeta}(\|v^{1}\|_{1+\alpha}+\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha})+\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\frac{8-\zeta}{1-\zeta}\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}=\eta\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|_{1+\alpha}.

Again using the time step condition (
3.13
) and estimate (
3.23
), we have

\|\delta(v^{d+1})\|_{1+\alpha}\leq CN\Delta t\eta\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\|\delta(v^{d})\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\zeta\|\delta(v^{d})\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.27)

Hence the estimate (
3.26
) is obtained for all
d\geq 0
. Moreover, estimate (
3.26
) implies that
v^{\ell}
is a Cauchy sequence, and hence it converges, completing the proof of the claim.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

We have established that the devised scheme (
3.10
) is solvable at time
(m+1)\Delta t
, given the approximate solution
U^{m}_{\varepsilon}
at time
m\Delta t
, under the time step restriction (
3.13
). Note that this condition depends on the
H^{1+\alpha}
-norm of
U^{m}_{\varepsilon}
, rather than directly on the initial data. However, to ensure that the CFL condition depends only on the initial data

, it is necessary to derive
a priori
bounds for the numerical solution
U_{\varepsilon}^{m}
. This will be established in the next lemma.

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
U_{\varepsilon}^{m}
be an approximate solution to equation (
1.1
) obtained via the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
). Assume that the initial data
u_{0}\in H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I)
,
\alpha\in[1,2]
. Further, suppose that the time step satisfies the CFL condition

N\Delta t\leq\frac{\zeta}{C\eta Z},

(3.28)

where
Z
is a constant depending on
\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha}
, and
\zeta
,
\eta
and
C
are as defined in Lemma
3.3
. Then there exists a finite time
\bar{T}
such that the following estimates hold:

\displaystyle\|U_{\varepsilon}^{m}\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.29)

\displaystyle\|D_{+}^{t}U_{\varepsilon}^{m}\|

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.30)

for all
m\Delta t\leq\bar{T}
, where
D_{+}^{t}U_{\varepsilon}^{m}=\frac{1}{\Delta t}(U_{\varepsilon}^{m+1}-U_{\varepsilon}^{m})
, and
C
depends only on
\alpha
,
\varepsilon
, and
\bar{T}
.

Proof.

We choose
\phi=\mathcal{D}^{2\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{xx}\in V_{N}
as the test function in (
3.10
), yielding

\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|^{2}-\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|^{2}=2\Delta t\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right),

(3.31)

where we used the identity (
2.8
) to eliminate the fractional term and periodicity to shift derivatives.
To estimate the right-hand side of (
3.31
), we employ the Sobolev inequality from Lemma
A.1
and Lemma
2.4
. Applying multiple instances of the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality and Lemma
2.4
, we obtain

\displaystyle\Big|\Big(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\mathcal{B}

\displaystyle(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\Big)\Big|\leq 6C(\alpha)\left|\left(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{xx}+(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{xx}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\right)\right|

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt+12C(\alpha)\|(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq 3C(\alpha)\left|\left((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x})^{2}\right)\right|+6C(\alpha)\|(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{xx}\|\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|

\displaystyle\hskip 18.49988pt\hskip 18.49988pt+12C(\alpha)\|(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq{C}\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2},

for some constant
C
independent of
\Delta t
and
N
.
Substituting this into (
3.31
) and using the triangle inequality, we get

\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|-\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|\leq 2\Delta t\frac{C\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}}{\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|+\|\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m}_{\varepsilon})_{x}\|}\leq C\Delta t\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}.

(3.32)

A similar approach yields estimates for the terms with lower order derivatives, and combining with (
3.32
), we obtain

\|U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}+C\Delta t\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}.

(3.33)

Consider the differential equation

z^{\prime}(t)=\frac{C}{4}(1+\eta)^{2}z(t)^{2},\quad t>0;\qquad z(0)=\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha},

(3.34)

where
C
is a constant independent of

. The solution

is increasing and convex for

. In particular, if

\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq z(t_{m})\leq Z,\quad\text{for }t_{m}\leq\bar{T}.

(3.35)

The claim holds for
m=0
. Assume it holds for

. The CFL condition (
3.28
) ensures the time step condition (
3.13
), and Lemma
3.3
provides

\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\frac{1+\eta}{2}\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}.

(3.36)

Substituting this into (

\displaystyle\|U^{d+1}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\|U^{d}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}+\frac{C}{4}\Delta t(1+\eta)^{2}\|U^{d}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}\leq z(t_{d})+\frac{C}{4}\Delta t(1+\eta)^{2}z(t_{d})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq z(t_{d})+\int_{t_{d}}^{t_{d+1}}\frac{C}{4}(1+\eta)^{2}z(s)^{2}\,ds\leq z(t_{d})+\int_{t_{d}}^{t_{d+1}}z^{\prime}(s)\,ds=z(t_{d+1}).

This confirms (
3.35
) and establishes (
3.29
).
To prove (
3.30
), consider the scheme (
3.10
) which implies

\left(D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\phi\right)=-\left(\mathcal{B}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}),\phi\right)+\varepsilon^{2}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\right).

(3.37)

Choosing

in (
3.37
), and applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality along with the estimate (

), we obtain

\displaystyle\|D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq C\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}\|D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|+\varepsilon^{2}\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}\|D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|,

implies

\displaystyle\|D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|\leq C\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}+\varepsilon^{2}\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}^{2}\leq C.

This completes the proof.
∎

3.2.
Existence, Uniqueness, and Convergence Analysis

We will prove that the approximations obtained by the scheme (
3.10
) converge to the classical solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) for initial data
u_{0}\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I),~\alpha\in[1,2]
. This convergence result can also be interpreted as an existence and uniqueness result for the solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
).
We first interpolate the approximation
U^{m}_{\varepsilon}
in
[0,\bar{T}]
as follows:

U_{\varepsilon,N}(x,t)=\begin{cases}(1-\theta_{m}(t))U^{m-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}(x)+\theta_{m}(t)U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}(x),&t\in[t_{m-\frac{1}{2}},t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}),\quad m\geq 1,\\
\left(1-\frac{t}{\Delta t/2}\right)U^{0}_{\varepsilon}(x)+\frac{t}{\Delta t/2}U^{\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}(x),&t\in(0,t_{\frac{1}{2}}),\end{cases}

(3.38)

where
\theta_{m}(t)=\frac{1}{\Delta t}(t-t_{m-\frac{1}{2}})
. The notation
U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)\in V_{N}
for
t\in[0,\bar{T}]
denotes the interpolation of approximate solution
\{U_{\varepsilon}^{m}\}_{m\Delta t\leq\bar{T}}
, where
N
represents the spatial discretization parameter.
We show that the approximation
U_{\varepsilon,N}
converges uniformly to the classical solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
). More precisely, the next theorem can be seen as the constructive proof of the existence and uniqueness result for the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
).

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
u_{0}\in H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I)
with
\alpha\in[1,2]
and
T>0
. Then there exists a unique solution
u\in C([0,T];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}_{p}(I))
to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
).

The proof of Theorem
3.6
follows from the lemma and the remark below, which establishes the existence of the limit of
U_{\varepsilon,N}
as
N\to\infty
and asserts that the identified limit is the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
).

Lemma 3.7
.

Let
U_{\varepsilon,N}
be an approximate solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) obtained via the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
), assuming the CFL condition (
3.28
). Then there exist a finite time
\bar{T}
such that the following bounds hold

\displaystyle\left\|U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.39)

\displaystyle\left\|U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)_{t}\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.40)

\displaystyle\left\|U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{1+\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq C,

(3.41)

for all
t\leq\bar{T}
, where
C=C(\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{1+\alpha},\varepsilon,\alpha,\bar{T})
. Moreover, the approximation
U_{\varepsilon,N}
converges to the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) in
C([0,\bar{T}];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
.

Proof.

Using the triangle inequality in the definition (
3.38
) of
U_{\varepsilon,N}
, we have

\left\|U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)\right\|\leq\|U^{m-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|+\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|\leq C,

using (
3.11
), for all
m\geq 0
and
m\Delta t<\bar{T}
. The time derivative is given by

(U_{\varepsilon,N}(x,t))_{t}=D_{+}^{t}U^{m-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}(x)\qquad t\in[t_{m-\frac{1}{2}},t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}),

and thus, for
m\geq 1
and
m\Delta t<\bar{T}
,

\left\|U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)_{t}\right\|=\|D_{+}^{t}U^{m-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|\leq C,\qquad t\in[t_{m-\frac{1}{2}},t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}),

(3.42)

using (
3.30
), and similarly for
t\in(0,t_{1/2})
. Lastly, using (
3.29
) and the triangle inequality, we obtain

\left\|U_{\varepsilon,N}(\cdot,t)\right\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\|U^{m-\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}+\|U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq C,\qquad t\in[t_{m-\frac{1}{2}},t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}),

and similarly for
t\in(0,t_{1/2})
. This completes the proof of (
3.39
)-(
3.41
).

The bound (
3.40
) implies that
U_{\varepsilon,N}\in\text{Lip}([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
for all
N
. From (
3.39
), we employ the Arzelà-Ascoli theorem, which ensures the sequential compactness of
\{U_{\varepsilon,N}\}_{N\in\mathbb{N}}
in
C([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
. Consequently, there exists a subsequence
N_{k}
such that

U_{\varepsilon,N_{k}}\to\bar{u}\text{ uniformly in }C([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))\text{ as }N_{k}\to\infty.

(3.43)

We now show that
\bar{u}
is the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
). First, we claim that
\bar{u}
satisfies

\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}\Big(\bar{u}\varphi_{t}+3\bar{u}^{2}\varphi_{x}-\varepsilon^{2}\bar{u}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\varphi_{x}\Big)\,dx\,dt+\int_{I}\varphi(x,0)u_{0}\,dx=0,

(3.44)

for all
\varphi\in C_{c}^{1}([0,\bar{T}];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))
.

For
\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
and
t_{m}<\bar{T}
, we choose the test function
\phi=P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})
in (
3.10
) and summing it over
m
after multiplying by
\Delta t
to obtain

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon},P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=-3\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})^{2}_{x},P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon^{2}\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right).

(3.45)

Using (
2.3
) and the uniform convergence of
U_{\varepsilon,N}
from (
3.43
), it follows that

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon},P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(D_{+}^{t}U^{m}_{\varepsilon},\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=-\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(U^{m}_{\varepsilon},D_{+}^{t}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)-(u_{0},\varphi(\cdot,0))

\displaystyle\to-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}\bar{u}\varphi_{t}\,dx\,dt-\int_{I}u_{0}\varphi(x,0)\,dx,

(3.46)

as
N\to\infty
. Similarly, using (
2.7
) and (
2.3
), we have

\displaystyle\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon},\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})_{x}\right)\to-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}\bar{u}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\varphi_{x}\,dx\,dt,

(3.47)

as
N\to\infty
. Also, by (
3.38
),

\displaystyle 3\Delta t\sum\limits_{m\Delta t<\bar{T}}\left((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})^{2}_{x},P_{N}\varphi(\cdot,t_{m})\right)\to-\int_{0}^{\bar{T}}\int_{I}3\bar{u}^{2}\varphi_{x}\,dx\,dt,

(3.48)

as
N\to\infty
. Substituting the results of (
3.46
), (
3.47
), and (
3.48
) into (
3.45
), we obtain that (
3.44
) holds, proving that
\bar{u}
is a weak solution of (
1.1
). Finally the bounds (
3.39
)-(
3.41
) implies that
\bar{u}\in C([0,\bar{T}];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
is actually a strong solution which satisfy fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) as an
L^{2}
-identity.
To establish the uniqueness, let
\bar{u}
and
\bar{w}
be two solutions of (
1.1
) in
C([0,\bar{T}];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
with initial conditions
\bar{u}(\cdot,0)=\bar{w}(\cdot,0)=u_{0}
. Define
v=\bar{u}-\bar{w}
. Then
v
satisfies the following

\begin{cases}v_{t}+6(\bar{u}\bar{u}_{x}-\bar{w}\bar{w}_{x})-\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}v_{x}=0,\qquad&(x,t)\in I\times(0,\bar{T}],\\
v(x,0)=0,&x\in I,\\
v(-\pi,t)=v(\pi,t),&\forall t\in[0,\bar{T}].\end{cases}

(3.49)

Taking inner product of
v
and (
3.49
), and applying the identity (
2.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left\|v(\cdot,t)\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=-12(\bar{u}\bar{u}_{x}-\bar{w}\bar{w}_{x},v)=-12(v\bar{u}_{x}+\bar{w}v_{x},v)=-12(\bar{u}_{x},v^{2})+6(\bar{w}_{x},v^{2})

\displaystyle\leq 18C\left\|v(\cdot,t)\right\|^{2},

where we have utilized the Sobolev inequality
\|\bar{u}_{x}\|_{\infty}\leq C\|\bar{u}\|_{1+\alpha}
and the estimates on solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
). Applying the Grönwall’s inequality, we obtain

\left\|v(\cdot,t)\right\|^{2}\leq e^{18C\bar{T}}\left\|v(\cdot,0)\right\|^{2},\qquad\forall t<\bar{T}.

Since
v(\cdot,0)=0
, it follows that
v(\cdot,t)=0
for all
t<\bar{T}
. This establishes the uniqueness of the solution. Consequently, we conclude that the sequence of approximations
U_{\varepsilon,N}
converges uniformly to the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) in
C([0,\bar{T}];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,\bar{T}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
.
∎

Remark 3.8
.

We now adapt the argument of Sjöberg
[
38
]
to establish global existence for arbitrary time
T>0
. The key observation is that the length of the time interval
[0,\bar{T}]
of existence depends only on the
H^{1+\alpha}_{p}
norm of the initial data
u_{0}
. Since the exact solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) preserves this norm, the solution can be extended iteratively.

At time
\bar{T}
, we define the new initial data via projection

U^{\overline{T}}_{\varepsilon}=P_{N}u(\cdot,\bar{T}).

This allows us to extend the solution to the interval
[\bar{T},2\bar{T}]
. Repeating this process inductively, we obtain a solution that exists for all
t>0
, as the
H^{1+\alpha}_{p}
norm remains uniformly bounded in time.

The above Lemma
3.7
and Remark
3.8
together demonstrate the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) in
C([0,T];H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}_{p}(I))
, completing the constructive proof of Theorem
3.6
.

3.3.
Error Estimate

To obtain the optimal order of convergence of the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
), we employ the standard approach and define the error function
\mathcal{E}_{N}^{m}\in V_{N}
by

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{m}=P_{N}u(\cdot,t_{m})-U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\quad\text{so that}\quad\mathcal{E}_{N}^{0}(x)=P_{N}u_{0}-P_{N}u_{0}=0.

(3.50)

We will demonstrate that the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
) is spectral accurate of order
N^{-r}
for initial data in Sobolev spaces
H^{r}_{p}(I)
and exponentially accurate for analytic initial data.

Theorem 3.9
.

Let
u_{0}\in H^{r}_{p}(I)
with
r\geq 3
and let
u
be the exact solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
). Let
U_{\varepsilon,N}
be the approximate solution obtained by the fully discrete scheme (
3.10
) and assume that the CFL condition (
3.28
) holds. Then the following estimate holds

\left\|u(t_{m})-U^{m}_{\varepsilon}\right\|\leq C(N^{-r}+\Delta t^{2}).

(3.51)

Proof.

For all
\phi\in V_{N}
, we have

\displaystyle\Bigg(\frac{\mathcal{E}^{m+1}_{N}-\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}}{\Delta t},\phi\Bigg)

\displaystyle=\Bigg(\frac{P_{N}u(t_{m+1})-P_{N}u(t_{m})}{\Delta t},\phi\Bigg)-\Bigg(\frac{U^{m+1}_{\varepsilon}-U^{m}_{\varepsilon}}{\Delta t},\phi\Bigg)

\displaystyle=(\xi^{m+\frac{1}{2}},\phi)+(u_{t}(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}),\phi)+3\Big((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}^{2},\phi\Big)-\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\phi\Big)

\displaystyle=(\xi^{m+\frac{1}{2}},\phi)+3\Big((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}^{2}-u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})^{2}_{x},\phi\Big)-\varepsilon^{2}\Big(\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}-u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}))_{x},\phi\Big),

(3.52)

where

\xi^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{u(t_{m+1})-u(t_{m})}{\Delta t}-u_{t}(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}).

Let us define the error at the average time
t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}
by

\displaystyle U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}-P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})=-\mathcal{E}_{N}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}+P_{N}\theta^{m+\frac{1}{2}},

where

\mathcal{E}_{N}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{\mathcal{E}_{N}^{m+1}+\mathcal{E}_{N}^{m}}{2},\quad\text{and}\quad\theta^{m+\frac{1}{2}}=\frac{u(t_{m+1})+u(t_{m})}{2}-u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}).

Using the Taylor’s expansion, it is straightforward to show that

\|P_{N}\theta^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}_{l}\leq C\Delta t^{3}\sup\limits_{t<T}\|u_{tt}(t)\|^{2}_{l},\quad\text{and}\quad\|\xi^{m+\frac{1}{2}}\|^{2}\leq C\Delta t^{3}\sup\limits_{t<T}\|u_{ttt}(t)\|^{2},

(3.53)

for all
l\geq r-2
. By taking
\phi=\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}
in (
3.52
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m+1}_{N}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta t\Big[C\Delta t^{3}+\frac{1}{8}\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2}+3\Big((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}^{2}-(u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})^{2})_{x},\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)

\displaystyle\qquad+\varepsilon^{2}C\Delta t^{3}+\frac{1}{4}\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2}\Big].

(3.54)

We estimate the nonlinear term in the above equation as follows

\displaystyle 3\Big(

\displaystyle(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}^{2}-(u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})^{2})_{x},\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=6\Big(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x}-P_{N}(u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})_{x}),\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)

\displaystyle=6\Big((U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}-P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}))(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}-P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}}))_{x},\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)+6\Big(P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon})_{x},\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad-6\Big(P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})_{x},\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)+6\Big(P_{N}u_{x}(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})(U^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{\varepsilon}-P_{N}u(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})),\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 12C\Delta t^{3}+\frac{3}{4}\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2}+12\|u_{x}(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})\|_{\infty}\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2}+12\|u_{x}(t_{m+\frac{1}{2}})\|_{\infty}\Big(\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2}+C\Delta t^{3}\Big)

\displaystyle\leq 12C\Delta t^{3}+C_{0}\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2},

(3.55)

where
C
and
C_{0}
are constants independent of
\Delta t
, and the result follows from the use of (
3.53
), Lemma
3.5
, and (
3.28
).
Substituting (
3.55
) into (
3.54
), we obtain

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m+1}_{N}\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}\right\|^{2}+2\Delta t\Big[C\Delta t^{3}+C_{1}\|\mathcal{E}^{m+\frac{1}{2}}_{N}\|^{2}\Big]\leq(1+C_{1}\Delta t)\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}\right\|^{2}+C\Delta t^{4}+C_{1}\Delta t\|\mathcal{E}^{m+1}_{N}\|^{2},

where constant
C
and
C_{1}
are generic and independent of
\Delta t
. We choose
\Delta t
sufficiently small such that
1-\Delta tC_{1}\geq\frac{1}{2}
and use the fact that
\mathcal{E}^{0}_{N}=0
. The above estimate implies

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m+1}_{N}\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1+C_{1}\Delta t}{1-C_{1}\Delta t}\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}\right\|^{2}+\frac{C\Delta t^{4}}{1-C_{1}\Delta t}\leq\left(\frac{1+C_{1}\Delta t}{1-C_{1}\Delta t}\right)^{m+1}\left\|\mathcal{E}^{0}_{N}\right\|^{2}+e^{CT}\Delta t^{4}\leq e^{CT}\Delta t^{4}.

Furthermore, since
u_{0}\in H_{p}^{r}(I)
, we have

\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}-P_{N}u(t_{m})\right\|\leq\left\|P_{N}u(t_{m})-u(t_{m})\right\|+\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}\right\|\leq C(N^{-r}+\Delta t^{2}),

(3.56)

where
C
is dependent on
\alpha,\varepsilon,T,
and
\left\|u_{0}\right\|_{r}
. This completes the proof.
∎

We observe that, if the the initial data
u_{0}
is analytic and hence the solution of fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) is analytic, then the estimate (
3.56
) and the Proposition
2.2
implies

\left\|U^{m}_{\varepsilon}-P_{N}u(t_{m})\right\|\leq\left\|P_{N}u(t_{m})-u(t_{m})\right\|+\left\|\mathcal{E}^{m}_{N}\right\|\leq C(e^{-cN}+\Delta t^{2}).

(3.57)

4.
Zero Dispersive Limit

In this section, we investigate the behavior of solutions to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) as the dispersion parameter
\varepsilon
tends to zero. This regime is known as the zero dispersive limit. The analysis is divided into two cases: for time
t<t_{c}
, where
t_{c}
is the critical times corresponding to the onset of gradient catastrophe in the inviscid Burgers’ equation, and for times
t>t_{c}
.

For
t<t_{c}
, the limiting equation

u_{t}+6uu_{x}=0,

(4.1)

with smooth initial data
u_{0}
, admits a smooth solution. Previously, we established that for each
\varepsilon>0
,
T>0
, and initial data
u_{0}\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
, there exists a unique solution
u(x,t;\varepsilon)
to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) such that

u(x,t;\varepsilon)\in C([0,T];H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,T];L^{2}_{p}(I)).

(4.2)

Our goal is to examine the limiting behavior of
u(x,t;\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
, particularly the quantity
\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim\limits_{N\to\infty}U_{\varepsilon,N}=\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}u(x,t;\varepsilon)
.

Case 1:
t<t_{c}

For
t<t_{c}
, the solution remains smooth, and the convergence of
u(x,t;\varepsilon)
to the solution
v^{b}(x,t)
of the limiting equation (
4.1
) is straightforward. This is captured in the following theorem.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
T_{c}
be any positive time less than the critical time
t_{c}
. Let
u(x,t;\varepsilon)\in C([0,T_{c}];H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,T_{c}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
be the unique solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
). Then the map
\varepsilon\mapsto u(x,t;\varepsilon)
is continuous from
\mathbb{R}^{+}
to
C([0,T_{c}];H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,T_{c}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
. Moreover, we have

\lim\limits_{\varepsilon\to 0}\left\|u(t;\varepsilon)-v^{b}(t)\right\|_{1+\alpha}=0,

(4.3)

uniformly in
t\in[0,T_{c}]
, where
v^{b}(x,t)
satisfies the limiting equation

v^{b}_{t}+6v^{b}v^{b}_{x}=0,\qquad v^{b}(x,0)=u_{0}(x).

(4.4)

To prove this, we first establish an abstract continuity result for parameter-dependent quasilinear equations. Borrowing the idea from
[
33
, Theorem 4-5]
and
[
21
, Theorem 7]
, we have the following result.

Theorem 4.2

(Abstract parameter continuity)
.

Let
X,Y
be Hilbert spaces with
Y\subset X
densely and continuously embedded. Consider the family of quasilinear problems

\frac{du}{dt}=A(u;\varepsilon)u,\quad u(0)=u_{0}\in Y,\quad\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}^{+},

(4.5)

where
A:Y\times\mathbb{R}^{+}\to\mathcal{L}(Y,X)
and assume that
\|A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)\|_{Y,X}
is uniformly bounded in
u
and
\varepsilon
. Furthermore, assume that

(H1)

Well-posedness
: For each
\varepsilon>0
and
T>0
, (
4.5
) has a unique solution
u(\cdot;\varepsilon)\in C([0,T];Y)\cap C^{1}([0,T];X)
.

(H2)

Lipschitz continuity in
u
: There exists
L>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>0
,

\|A(u;\varepsilon)-A(v;\varepsilon)\|_{Y,X}\leq L\|u-v\|_{Y}.

(4.6)

(H3)

Parameter regularity
:

\|A(u;\varepsilon)-A(u;\varepsilon^{\prime})\|_{Y,X}\leq C|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}|.

(4.7)

(H4)

Commutator structure
: There exists an isometric isomorphism
\Lambda:Y\to X
such that

\|[\Lambda,A(u;\varepsilon)]\Lambda^{-1}\|_{X}\leq C\|u\|_{Y},

(4.8)

where the commutator operator is defined as
[\Lambda,A(u;\varepsilon)]u:=\Lambda A(u;\varepsilon)u-A(u;\varepsilon)\Lambda u
.
(The above relation should be satisfied in the strict sense, including the domain relation. Thus
x\in X
is in domain of
A(u;\varepsilon)
if and only if
\Lambda^{-}x
is in domain of
A(u;\varepsilon)
with
A(u;\varepsilon)\Lambda^{-}x\in Y
. For more details we refer to
[
21
, Assumption (A2)]
.)

Then the solution map
\varepsilon\mapsto u(\cdot;\varepsilon)
is continuous from
\mathbb{R}^{+}
to
C([0,T];Y)\cap C^{1}([0,T];X)
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Condition (H1) provides the well-posedness framework, while (H2)-(H4) give the required continuity properties.
By (H1), for each
\varepsilon>0
, the solution
u(\cdot;\varepsilon)
exists on
[0,T]
. Since
u_{0}\in Y
is fixed and
A(\cdot;\varepsilon)
is locally Lipschitz (from (H2)), a standard energy estimate (using Grönwall’s inequality) shows that
\|u(t;\varepsilon)\|_{Y}
is uniformly bounded for all
t\in[0,T]
and
\varepsilon>0
.

Let
u(\varepsilon)
and
u(\varepsilon^{\prime})
be solutions corresponding to parameters
\varepsilon,\varepsilon^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
. The difference
w=u(\varepsilon)-u(\varepsilon^{\prime})
satisfies

w_{t}=A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)w+E,

(4.9)

where
E=(A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)-A(u(\varepsilon^{\prime});\varepsilon^{\prime}))u(\varepsilon^{\prime})
.
To estimate
\|w(t)\|_{Y}
, apply the isometric isomorphism
\Lambda:Y\to X
and compute

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{Y}^{2}

\displaystyle=\langle\Lambda w,\Lambda A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)\Lambda^{-}(\Lambda w)\rangle_{X}+\langle w,E\rangle_{Y}

\displaystyle=\langle\Lambda w,A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)\Lambda w\rangle_{X}+\langle\Lambda w,[\Lambda,A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)]\Lambda^{-}(\Lambda w)\rangle_{X}+\langle w,E\rangle_{Y},

where
[\Lambda,A(u;\varepsilon)]\Lambda^{-1}=\Lambda A(u;\varepsilon)\Lambda^{-1}-A(u;\varepsilon)
. Using (H4), the commutator term is bounded as

\displaystyle|\langle\Lambda w,[\Lambda,A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)]w\rangle_{X}|

\displaystyle\leq\|\Lambda w\|_{X}\|[\Lambda,A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)]\Lambda^{-1}\|_{X}\|\Lambda w\|_{X}

\displaystyle\leq C\|u(\varepsilon)\|_{Y}\|w\|_{Y}^{2}.

Furthermore,
E
can be decomposed as

E=(A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)-A(u(\varepsilon^{\prime});\varepsilon))u(\varepsilon^{\prime})+(A(u(\varepsilon^{\prime});\varepsilon)-A(u(\varepsilon^{\prime});\varepsilon^{\prime}))u(\varepsilon^{\prime}).

By (H2) and (H3), we have:

\|E\|_{Y}\leq L\|w\|_{Y}\|u(\varepsilon^{\prime})\|_{Y}+C|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}|\|u(\varepsilon^{\prime})\|_{Y}.

Note that
\|u(\varepsilon^{\prime})\|_{Y}<C
by (H1) for each
\varepsilon>0
, so

\|E\|_{Y}\leq C\|w\|_{Y}+C|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}|.

Since
\|A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)\|_{Y,X}
is uniformly bounded in
u
and
\varepsilon
, from isometry, we have

|\langle\Lambda w,A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)\Lambda w\rangle_{X}|\leq\|A(u(\varepsilon);\varepsilon)\|_{Y,X}\|\Lambda w\|_{X}^{2}\leq C\|u(\varepsilon)\|_{Y}\|w\|_{Y}^{2}.

Combining the above estimates and using the isometry of
\Lambda
, we end up with

\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{Y}^{2}\leq C\|w\|_{Y}^{2}+C|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}|\|w\|_{Y}.

Using the Young’s inequality we obtain

\frac{d}{dt}\|w\|_{Y}^{2}\leq C\|w\|_{Y}^{2}+C|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}|^{2}.

The Grönwall’s inequality further yields

\|w(t)\|_{Y}\leq C|\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}|e^{CT}\quad\text{for all }t\in[0,T].

This establishes the continuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto u(\cdot;\varepsilon)
in
C([0,T];Y)\cap C^{1}([0,T];X)
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
4.1
.

We employ the Kato’s theory
[
21
]
and rewrite the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) as

\displaystyle\frac{du}{dt}=A(u;\varepsilon)u,\qquad u(0)=u_{0}\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I),

(4.10)

\displaystyle A(u;\varepsilon)=-6u\partial_{x}+\varepsilon^{2}\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x},

(4.11)

where
A(u;\varepsilon)
can be considered as the perturbed operator of the Burgers’ operator corresponding to
\varepsilon>0
.
We verify the hypotheses of Theorem
4.2
for the fractional KdV operator
A(u;\varepsilon)
for
u\in H_{p}^{1+\alpha}
with
X=L^{2}_{p}(I)
and
Y=H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
, and
X
and
Y
are Hilbert Space. The well-posedness of (
4.10
) was established in Section
3
which verifies the hypothesis (H1) of Theorem
4.2
. The fractional operator
\mathcal{D}^{\alpha}\partial_{x}
is a bounded operator from
H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
to
L^{2}_{p}(I)
. For
u\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
, the nonlinear term
6u\partial_{x}:H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)\to H^{\alpha}_{p}(I)\hookrightarrow L^{2}_{p}(I)
is bounded by

\|u\partial_{x}v\|\leq\|u\|_{\infty}\|\partial_{x}v\|\leq C\|u\|_{1+\alpha}\|v\|_{1+\alpha}

(4.12)

using the Sobolev embedding

\|u\|_{\infty}\leq C\|u\|_{1+\alpha}
. Hence using these boundedness of operators, for
u,v\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)

\displaystyle\|A(u;\varepsilon)-A(v;\varepsilon)\|_{H_{p}^{1+\alpha},L_{p}^{2}}\leq C\|u-v\|_{1+\alpha}.

(4.13)

This implies that
A(u;\varepsilon)
is a Lipschitz operator from
H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I)
to
L^{2}_{p}(I)
, for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{R}
. For fixed
u\in H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
,

\|A(u;\varepsilon)-A(u;\varepsilon^{\prime})\|_{H_{p}^{1+\alpha},L_{p}^{2}}\leq C|\varepsilon^{2}-\varepsilon^{\prime 2}|.

(4.14)

Let
\Lambda^{1+\alpha}=(1-\partial_{x}^{2})^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}
be the isometric isomorphism
H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)\to L^{2}_{p}(I)
. Then

B(u;\varepsilon):=(\Lambda^{1+\alpha}A(u;\varepsilon)-A(u;\varepsilon)\Lambda^{1+\alpha})\Lambda^{-(1+\alpha)}=[\Lambda^{1+\alpha},A(u;\varepsilon)]\Lambda^{-(1+\alpha)}

(4.15)

where
B(u;\varepsilon)
satisfies the commutator estimate (
[
33
, Lemma 1]
,
[
21
, Lemma A.2]
)

\|B(u;\varepsilon)\|\leq C(\alpha)\|u\|_{1+\alpha}.

(4.16)

Finally, estimates (
4.13
)-(
4.16
) verify conditions (H2)-(H4) of Theorem
4.2
, and hence the map
\varepsilon\mapsto u(x,t;\varepsilon)
is continuous from
\mathbb{R}^{+}
to
C([0,T_{c}];H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I))\cap C^{1}([0,T_{c}];L^{2}_{p}(I))
.

Afterwards, we consider the solution operator
\Phi_{\varepsilon}
for the fractional KdV equation, which maps initial data to solutions at time
t

\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)u_{0}(x)=u(x,t;\varepsilon).

We have that
\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)
depends continuously on
\varepsilon
in the operator norm topology of
\mathcal{L}(H_{p}^{1+\alpha}(I),H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I))
, uniformly for
t\in[0,T_{c}]
. The key observation at
\varepsilon=0
is that the fractional KdV operator reduces to
A(u;0)=-6u\partial_{x}
, which generates exactly the Burgers’ equation flux. Thus, at
\varepsilon=0
, the operator
\Phi_{0}(t)
exactly corresponds to the solution map for the Burgers’ equation (
4.4
)

\Phi_{0}(t)u_{0}(x)=v^{b}(x,t).

The classical theory of Burgers’ equation guarantees that
v^{b}\in C([0,T_{c}];H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I))
for initial data in
H^{1+\alpha}_{p}(I)
when
T_{c}<t_{c}
. Finally, the uniform convergence follows from the operator norm continuity

\sup_{t\in[0,T_{c}]}\|\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)u_{0}-\Phi_{0}(t)u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha}\leq\sup_{t\in[0,T_{c}]}\|\Phi_{\varepsilon}(t)-\Phi_{0}(t)\|_{H_{p}^{1+\alpha},H_{p}^{1+\alpha}}\|u_{0}\|_{1+\alpha}.

The right-hand side tends to zero as
\varepsilon\to 0
by the uniform continuity of
\varepsilon\mapsto\Phi_{\varepsilon}
. This proves the uniform convergence (
4.3
).

Case 2:

After the gradient catastrophe arises in the limiting equation, the behavior of the solution to the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) becomes more intricate, as it does not converge to the solution of the limiting equation (
4.4
) when
\varepsilon\to 0
. We examine the limit of the fractional KdV solution as
\varepsilon\to 0
for times
t>t_{c}
. A numerical comparison is made between the approximations
U_{\varepsilon,N}
for
\alpha
close to
2
and the corresponding KdV equation.

Building on the foundational work of Lax and Levermore
[
29
,
30
,
31
]
, Venakides
[
42
]
, and Grava and Klein
[
16
,
17
,
26
]
, it is known that after the time
t_{c}
, oscillatory zone
[x^{-}(t),x^{+}(t)]
arises, which is independent of
\varepsilon
for single hump, rapidly decreasing initial data. The boundaries
x^{-}(t)
and
x^{+}(t)
are determined by the initial data and satisfy
x^{-}(t_{c})=x^{+}(t_{c})=x_{c}
, where
x_{c}
denotes the spatial coordinate of the gradient catastrophe at the break time
t_{c}
of the limiting equation. Prior to implementing the numerical scheme (
3.10
) for solving the zero dispersive limit of the limiting equation for
t>t_{c}
, we introduce the notion of asymptotic solutions for the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) across different values of
\alpha
. This introduction allows us to evaluate the performance of the devised scheme (
3.10
) by comparing it with the asymptotic behavior of the solution of the fractional KdV equation (
1.1
) as
\varepsilon
approaches zero.

Outside the oscillatory zone
[x^{-}(t),x^{+}(t)]
, the asymptotic solution
u(x,t;\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0
for all
\alpha\in[1,2]
converges to the solution of the limiting equation. Within the oscillatory zone, for
\alpha=2
and sufficiently small
\varepsilon
, the solution
u(x,t;\varepsilon)
is approximately described by the elliptic solution of the KdV equation

u_{t}+6uu_{x}+\varepsilon^{2}u_{xxx}=0,

(4.17)

which is given by
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
42
,
17
]
as:

u(x,t;\varepsilon)\approx\tilde{u}+2\varepsilon^{2}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\log\mu\left(\frac{\sqrt{\beta_{1}-\beta_{3}}}{2\varepsilon K(s)}\left[x-2t(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3})-q\right];\mathcal{T}\right),

(4.18)

where
\tilde{u}=\tilde{u}(x,t)
is the weak limit of
u(x,t;\varepsilon)
as
\varepsilon\to 0

[
29
]
, has the following form

\tilde{u}=\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}+2\beta,\text{ and }\beta=-\beta_{1}+(\beta_{1}-\beta_{3})\frac{E(s)}{K(s)},

with
K(s)
and
E(s)
being the first and second kind complete elliptic integrals. Note that

K^{\prime}(s)=K(\sqrt{1-s^{2}}),\qquad\mathcal{T}=i\frac{K^{\prime}(s)}{K(s)},\qquad s^{2}=\frac{\beta_{3}-\beta_{1}}{\beta_{3}-\beta_{1}}.

Furthermore, the Jacobi elliptic function
\mu
is defined by the Fourier series

\mu(\xi;\mathcal{T})=\sum\limits_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{\pi in^{2}\mathcal{T}+2\pi in\xi}.

The quantities
\beta_{i}(x,t)
,
i=1,2,3
, evolve according to the Whitham equations
[
44
]

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\beta_{i}+v_{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\beta_{i}=0,\quad i=1,2,3,\quad\text{and}\quad v_{i}=4\frac{\prod_{j\neq i}(\beta_{i}-\beta_{k})}{\beta_{i}+\beta}+2(\beta_{1}+\beta_{2}+\beta_{3}),\quad i=1,2,3.

(4.19)

The formula for
q
in (
4.17
) is given by
[
17
]

\displaystyle q(\beta_{1},\beta_{2},\beta_{3})=\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2}\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{f_{-}(A)}{\sqrt{1-\theta}\sqrt{1-\gamma^{2}}}\,d\theta\,d\gamma,

where
f_{-}
is the inverse function in the decreasing part of the initial data and
A=\frac{1+\theta}{2}(\frac{1+\gamma}{2}\beta_{1}+\frac{1-\gamma}{2}\beta_{2})+\frac{1-\theta}{2}\beta_{3}
.

6.
Concluding Remarks

In this study, we have developed a structure-preserving Fourier spectral Galerkin (FSG) scheme which conserves mass, momentum, and energy, and demonstrated that the fully discrete Crank-Nicolson (CN) FSG scheme is stable and convergent. Additionally, we provided a constructive proof for the existence and uniqueness of the solution to the fractional KdV equation using compactness arguments. The proposed scheme achieves optimal spectral accuracy for periodic initial data in
H^{r}
, and exponential accuracy for analytic initial data, resulting in significantly reduced computational time during numerical simulations compared to our previous works.

Moreover, we investigated the zero-dispersion limit of the fractional KdV equation. Through extensive numerical simulations, we confirmed that the proposed CN-FSG scheme accurately captures the oscillations generated by small dispersion. We also examined the behavior of the numerical solutions as
\alpha
varies within
[1,2]
, and evaluated the performance of the scheme for small values of
\varepsilon
beyond the critical time
t_{c}
.
However, further work remains, both theoretically and numerically, to better understand the asymptotic behavior of the zero dispersion limit of the fractional KdV equation beyond
t_{c}
.

We collect a number of elementary technical results that were used earlier in the article.

Lemma A.1

(Inverse and Sobolev-type inequalities for trigonometric polynomials)
.

Let
f\in V_{N}
. Then:

(1)

(Inverse inequality) For the
L^{2}
-norm, we have

\|f_{x}\|\leq N\|f\|.

(A.1)

(2)

(Sobolev inequality) We have

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}\|f\|_{1}.

(A.2)

(3)

(Sobolev embedding) For any
r\geq 0
, we have

\|f\|\leq\|f\|_{r}.

(A.3)

Proof.

Note that for
f\in V_{N}
we have

f(x):=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx}~\text{ and }~f_{x}(x):=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}ik\hat{f}(k)e^{ikx}.

(1)

For the inverse inequality, we compute using the Parseval’s identity

\displaystyle\|f_{x}\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}|k|^{2}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\leq N^{2}\sum_{k=-N}^{N}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}=N^{2}\|f\|^{2}.

Taking square roots gives the result.

(2)

For the Sobolev inequality, we first bound the sup-norm and use the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\displaystyle\|f\|_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=-N}^{N}|\hat{f}(k)|=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\frac{(1+k^{2})^{1/2}|\hat{f}(k)|}{(1+k^{2})^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{1+k^{2}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{k=-N}^{N}(1+k^{2})|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

The second factor is exactly
\|f\|_{1}
. We now bound the summation factor

\displaystyle S_{N}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}\frac{1}{1+k^{2}}=1+2\sum_{k=1}^{N}\frac{1}{1+k^{2}}\leq 1+2\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{1+k^{2}}=1+2\left(\frac{\pi\coth\pi-1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\pi\coth\pi<\frac{10}{3}.

Hence, we have

\|f\|_{\infty}\leq\sqrt{\frac{10}{3}}\|f\|_{1}.

(3)

For the Sobolev embedding (
r\geq 0
)

\displaystyle\|f\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}=\sum_{k=-N}^{N}(1+k^{2})^{0}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}\leq\sum_{k=-N}^{N}(1+k^{2})^{r}|\hat{f}(k)|^{2}=\|f\|_{r}^{2},

since
(1+k^{2})^{r}\geq 1
for all
k
when
r\geq 0
.

∎
