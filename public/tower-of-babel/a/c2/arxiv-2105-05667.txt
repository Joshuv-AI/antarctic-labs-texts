Quantum state control of a Bose-Einstein condensate in an optical lattice
N. Dupont 1 , G. Chatelain 1 , L. Gabardos 1 , M. Arnal 1 , J. Billy 1 , B. Peaudecerf 1 , D. Sugny 2 , D. Guéry-Odelin 1

Abstract
We report on the efficient design of quantum optimal control protocols to manipulate the motional states of an atomic Bose-Einstein condensate (BEC) in a one-dimensional optical lattice. Our protocols operate on the momentum comb associated with the lattice. In contrast to previous works also dealing with control in discrete and large Hilbert spaces, our control schemes allow us to reach a wide variety of targets by varying a single parameter, the lattice position. With this technique, we experimentally demonstrate a precise, robust and versatile control: we optimize the transfer of the BEC to a single or multiple quantized momentum states with full control on the relative phase between the different momentum components. This also allows us to prepare the BEC in a given eigenstate of the lattice band structure, or superposition thereof.



I
Introduction

Quantum simulation amounts to using a tunable quantum system to simulate another less controllable one, and is particularly suited for studying model dynamics in large Hilbert spaces
Altman et al. 2021
.
This field has been growing very fast in the past years as an alternative to the all-purpose quantum computer and quantum simulations are currently under study on a wide variety
of quantum platforms (see
Altman et al. 2021
;
Georgescu et al. 2014
;
Cirac and Zoller 2012
;
Argüello-Luengo et al. 2019
and references therein).

Key requirements for quantum simulation are the ability to generate the desired Hamiltonian, to reliably prepare the initial quantum state of interest, and to measure the subsequent time-evolved state.
Many innovative techniques have been developed to produce synthetic Hamiltonians, using, e.g., properly designed coupling
between external fields and atoms
Georgescu et al. 2014
;
Dalibard et al. 2011
;
Cooper et al. 2019
or periodic driving of the system’s parameters
Eckardt 2017
;
Goldman and Dalibard 2014
.
The time evolution can either be probed continuously, or investigated at fixed time intervals in the case of a digitized time evolution
Lanyon et al. 2011
or to study discretized dynamics
Arnal et al. 2020
;
Sacha and Zakrzewski 2017
.

Satisfying the requirement of the reliable preparation of a desired initial state often calls for manipulating a dynamical system by means of external time-dependent parameters. In practice it amounts to steering that system from a state easily provided by an experimental setup to the desired one in a minimum time and with a very high fidelity, while satisfying experimental constraints and limitations. Many quantum control methods such as shortcuts to adiabaticity
Guéry-Odelin et al. 2019
, composite pulses
Genov et al. 2014
, machine learning approaches
Wigley et al. 2016
or protocols based on optimal control theory with a minimal use of resources
Liberzon 2012
;
Bonnard and Sugny 2012
;
Pontryagin et al. 1962
;
Glaser et al. 2015
;
Boscain et al. 2020
have been developed and even combined to reach this goal in a variety of contexts.

In particular, optimal control theory, which has its roots in engineering
Bonnard and Sugny 2012
, has been transposed with success to quantum systems first in the context of physical chemistry
Brif et al. 2010
;
Koch et al. 2019
, then applied in Nuclear Magnetic Resonance for the control of spin dynamics
Nielsen et al. 2010
;
Khaneja et al. 2005
;
Lapert et al. 2010
, and nowadays in quantum technologies
Glaser et al. 2015
. For quantum systems with complex dynamics, efficient iterative algorithms have been developed to solve optimal control problems
Khaneja et al. 2005
;
Reich et al. 2012
;
Werschnik and Gross 2007
.
This has recently led to experimental realizations of optimal control protocols aimed at engineering quantum states or operations in various platforms, such as NV centers
Waldherr et al. 2014
;
Nöbauer et al. 2015
, photonic states in a cavity
Heeres et al. 2017
, internal states of atoms in low-excitation states
Anderson et al. 2015
;
Saywell et al. 2020
or in a Rydberg manifold
Larrouy et al. 2020
.

Atomic Bose-Einstein condensates (BECs), in which all atoms occupy a macroscopic wave function, lend themselves naturally to the use of optimal control techniques.
Theoretical studies have investigated how to manipulate BECs via a modulation of the magnetic confinement potential in a one-
Hohenester et al. 2007
;
Jäger et al. 2014
;
Sørensen et al. 2018
;
Hocker et al. 2016
but also three-dimensional case
Mennemann et al. 2015
, and on atom chips
Corgier et al. 2018
;
Amri et al. 2019
;
Chen et al. 2011
;
Zhang et al. 2016
, outperforming simpler control protocols. Experimental evidence of the efficiency of optimal control laws has been provided for the manipulation of vibrational states in a single trap
Bücker et al. 2013
;
van Frank et al. 2016
, in particular for interferometry applications
van Frank et al. 2014
. Other investigations extended to the optimal driving of strongly interacting atoms, including through a phase transition
Doria et al. 2011
;
van Frank et al. 2016
.

The control of a BEC in optical lattices is of particular interest for quantum simulation
Gross and Bloch 2017
. Simple momentum state superpositions can be generated
Pötting et al. 2001
in order to realize a shaken-lattice interferometer
Weidner et al. 2017
;
Weidner and Anderson 2018a
;
Weidner and Anderson 2018b
. Optimal control at quantum speed limit in a two-level quantum system realized in a lattice was demonstrated in
Bason et al. 2012
. Efficient control targeting specifically the eigenstates of optical lattices has been achieved in
Zhou et al. 2018
, but required combined optimized manipulations of both the lattice position and amplitude.

In this study, we use quantum optimal control (QOC) protocols to shape the momentum distribution of a BEC in a one-dimensional optical lattice. The Hilbert space has a large unbound size provided by a momentum comb of fixed spacing. In contrast with previous studies
Bücker et al. 2013
;
van Frank et al. 2014
;
Anderson et al. 2015
;
Heeres et al. 2017
;
Larrouy et al. 2020
, we drive the system through this large space by continuously varying a
single
parameter, the lattice position.
With this simple but sufficient scheme, we experimentally achieve a robust and versatile control of the BEC wave function, and prepare arbitrary states, in a short time window (typically of the order of
\displaystyle 100\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
) compared to commonly used adiabatic protocols. This allows us to reach a wide variety of states for which there is no known analytical solution of the control problem. We demonstrate the transfer of the BEC to single and multiple quantized momentum states, with the ability to control the relative phases between the different momentum components. As a further application, we prepare the BEC in arbitrary superpositions of Bloch eigenstates of the lattice. The implementation of the computed optimal control systematically leads to an excellent agreement between the numerical target and the experimental result.

II
Experimental setup and algorithm

II.1
BEC Experimental setup

We perform our experiments in a hybrid trap
Fortun et al. 2016
in which we obtain pure rubidium-87 BECs of typically
\displaystyle 2\cdot 10^{5}
atoms in the lowest hyperfine state
\displaystyle|F=1,m_{F}=-1\rangle
. These BECs are loaded in a one-dimensional optical lattice produced by two laser beams of wavelength
\displaystyle\lambda=$\displaystyle 1064\text{\,}\mathrm{nm}$
counterpropagating along the
\displaystyle x
-axis. They are superimposed on the optical dipole beams and the quadrupolar magnetic field of the hybrid trap. Along the optical lattice axis, the atoms experience the potential

U(x,t)=-\frac{s}{2}E_{\mathrm{L}}\cos\left(k_{\mathrm{L}}x+\varphi(t)\right)+U_{\mathrm{hyb}}(x),

(1)

where
\displaystyle k_{\mathrm{L}}\equiv 2\pi/d=2\pi/(\lambda/2)
and
\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{L}}=\hbar^{2}k_{\mathrm{L}}^{2}/(2m)
(with
\displaystyle m
the mass of an atom and
\displaystyle\hbar
the reduced Planck constant) are respectively the wavevector and the energy associated with the lattice (
\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{L}}=4E_{\mathrm{R}}
, with
\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{R}}
the recoil energy). The harmonic potential of the hybrid trap
\displaystyle U_{\mathrm{hyb}}(x)
has an angular frequency
\displaystyle\omega_{x}=2\pi\times 50
Hz.
The dimensionless depth of the lattice
\displaystyle s
is independently and precisely calibrated
Cabrera-Gutiérrez et al. 2018
for each experiment presented here.

We adiabatically load the atoms in the ground state of the lattice potential, i.e. in a Bloch wave of spatial period
\displaystyle d

Chatelain et al. 2020
. As a result, the momentum distribution is made up of equally spaced peaks separated by
\displaystyle\hbar k_{\mathrm{L}}
. Experimentally, we image this momentum distribution after a sufficiently long ballistic expansion following a sudden switch off of the confining potentials. The final spatial density
\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r})
then reproduces the initial momentum density
\displaystyle\tilde{n}(\mathbf{p})
up to a scaling factor:
\displaystyle n(\mathbf{r},t=t_{\mathrm{TOF}})=\tilde{n}(\mathbf{p}=m\mathbf{r}/t_{\mathrm{TOF}},t=0)
, with
\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{TOF}}
the time-of-flight duration.

The phase
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
of the lattice potential is our control parameter. Varying
\displaystyle\varphi
as a function of time amounts to moving the lattice position along the
\displaystyle x
-axis. This parameter is set by the relative phase between the two phase-locked acousto-optic modulators controlling the lattice beams. In the following section, we explain how to engineer
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
to tailor at will the momentum distribution. As the phase is varied, we only apply global transformations to the lattice: as a consequence the quasi-momentum
\displaystyle q
in the laboratory reference frame remains equal to its value for the initially prepared ground state of the lattice,
\displaystyle q=0
. Therefore, in that reference frame, we engineer arbitrary momentum superposition states of the form

|\Psi\rangle=\sum_{\ell\in\mathds{Z}}c_{\ell}\ket{\chi_{\ell}},

(2)

where the vector
\displaystyle\ket{\chi_\alpha}
is the eigenstate of the momentum operator with eigenvalue
\displaystyle\alpha\,\hbar k_{\mathrm{L}}
(and
\displaystyle\chi_{\alpha}(x)=e^{i\alpha k_{\mathrm{L}}x}/\sqrt{2\pi}
).

II.2
Control algorithm

Our objective is to tailor the time variation of the control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
over a fixed time interval
\displaystyle[0,t_{\mathrm{f}}]
, in order to assign a desired value to the
\displaystyle c_{\ell}
coefficients. For this purpose, we use a standard first-order gradient algorithm
Bryson and Ho 1975
;
Werschnik and Gross 2007
;
Khaneja et al. 2005
. As the quasi-momentum
\displaystyle q=0
is fixed in the laboratory reference frame, we recast the Schrödinger equation involving
only
the optical lattice potential in the following matrix form:

i\dot{C}=\mathcal{M}(\varphi(t))C,

(3)

where the state of the system is denoted by the vector
\displaystyle C
with coordinates
\displaystyle\{c_{\ell}\}
which satisfy

i\dot{c}_{\ell}=\ell^{2}c_{\ell}-\frac{s}{4}(e^{i\varphi(t)}c_{\ell-1}+e^{-i\varphi(t)}c_{\ell+1}),

(4)

with a rescaling of time:
\displaystyle t\rightarrow E_{\mathrm{L}}t/\hbar
.
The initial condition is given by the vector associated to the lowest Bloch state at
\displaystyle q=0
for the lattice depth
\displaystyle s
.
The optimal control problem is defined with respect to a figure of merit
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}(C(t_{\mathrm{f}}),C^{\dagger}(t_{\mathrm{f}}))
to be maximized at the fixed final time
\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{f}}
of the control process. The choice of the function
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
depends on the objective of the control (see below). We do not put any other constraint on the control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
. The optimal solution is formulated from the Pontryagin Hamiltonian
Boscain et al. 2020
;
Liberzon 2012

H_{\mathrm{P}}=\real[\langle D|\dot{C}\rangle]=\imaginary[\langle D|\mathcal{M}|C\rangle],

(5)

where
\displaystyle\real[\cdot]
and
\displaystyle\imaginary[\cdot]
denote respectively the real and the imaginary parts of a complex number. We introduce
\displaystyle D
, the adjoint vector, solution of

i\dot{D}=\mathcal{M}(\varphi(t))D,

(6)

with the final condition

D(t_{\mathrm{f}})=\frac{\partial\mathbb{F}(C(t_{\mathrm{f}}),C^{\dagger}(t_{\mathrm{f}}))}{\partial C^{\dagger}(t_{\mathrm{f}})}.

(7)

The correction
\displaystyle\delta\varphi
to first order of the control field is proportional to the derivative of
\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{P}}
with respect to
\displaystyle\varphi(t)

\delta H_{\mathrm{P}}(t)=\frac{\partial H_{\mathrm{P}}}{\partial\varphi(t)}=\imaginary\left[\langle D|\frac{\partial\mathcal{M}}{\partial\varphi(t)}|C\rangle\right].

(8)

We thus consider the following gradient algorithm:

1.

Choose a guess field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)=\varphi_{0}(t)
.

2.

Propagate forward the state
\displaystyle C(t)
up to the final state
\displaystyle C(t_{\mathrm{f}})
.

3.

Propagate backward the adjoint state of the system from Eq. (
6
), with the final condition given by Eq. (
7
).

4.

Compute the correction
\displaystyle\delta\varphi(t)
to the control field,
\displaystyle\delta\varphi(t)=\varepsilon\delta H_{\mathrm{P}}(t)
, where
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is a small positive parameter.

5.

Define the new control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)\mapsto\varphi(t)+\delta\varphi(t)
.

6.

Repeat from step 2 until the desired value of the figure of merit
\displaystyle\mathbb{F}
is reached.

In the numerical simulations,
\displaystyle\varepsilon
is determined from a line search method
Bryson and Ho 1975
. A constant phase adapted to the target state is generally a good starting control field for the algorithm. For the experimental implementation under study, we have verified that the accuracy of a first-order algorithm is sufficient, and second-order corrections are therefore not necessary
de Fouquieres et al. 2011
;
Dalgaard et al. 2020
.

II.3
Implementation

In practice the optimal control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
manipulated by the algorithm is a piecewise constant function. The control sequence is divided into a large number of time steps
\displaystyle\Delta t
(each of the order of a few hundreds of ns). The relatively smooth optimal pulses derived from the algorithm justifies this approximation.
Its successful implementation in the experiment imposes stringent technical requirements, such as the ability to vary the phase in an arbitrary fashion with an accuracy of less than a percent and on a frequency scale much higher than the characteristic frequencies of the atomic dynamics (
\displaystyle\sim 10
kHz)

1
1

1

In this work this is achieved through the use of DDS synthesizers (
Keysight 33612a
), computer-controlled and synchronized on a same clock signal. This typically give us the ability to sample modulation ramps with frequencies up to
\displaystyle 3\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}
, in steps as low as
\displaystyle 2\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}
. These modulation ramps are then applied to the RF drive of the acousto-optic modulator controlling the phase
\displaystyle\varphi(t)/2
of the lattice light.

.

Finally, the choice of the time duration
\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{f}}
of the phase control is a compromise between two opposite requirements. It should be larger than the natural time scale of evolution for the atoms in the lattice since the algorithm takes advantage of the dynamics, but not too long to mitigate the deleterious effects of the accumulation of small errors in the control, due to experimental fluctuations or imperfections. In practice, we choose a control time
\displaystyle t_{\mathrm{f}}\sim 1.5\,T_{0}
, where
\displaystyle T_{0}
is the time associated with the energy difference between the two lowest eigenstates at
\displaystyle q=0
(
\displaystyle T_{0}\simeq

\displaystyle 70\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}
for
\displaystyle s\simeq 5
). This experimentally leads to the highest fidelities to the desired targets.

III
Control of momentum state populations

In a first set of experiments, we investigate the control of the populations
\displaystyle p_{\ell}=|c_{\ell}|^{2}
in the desired momentum orders (
\displaystyle\textstyle\sum_{\ell}p_{\ell}=1
). To reach a specific target with populations
\displaystyle\{p_{\mathrm{t},\ell}\}
, we run the previously described algorithm with the figure of merit

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{F}

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}(|c_{\ell}|^{2}-p_{\mathrm{t},\ell})^{2}

(9)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=1-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell}(C^{\dagger}O_{\ell}C-p_{\mathrm{t},\ell})^{2},

where
\displaystyle O_{\ell}
is the projection operator onto the state
\displaystyle\ell

Jozsa 1994
. Using this definition, and the algorithm described in Sec.
II.2
, we present optimal control ramps leading first to the preparation of a single momentum state (Sec.
III.1
), and then to arbitrary-weight superpositions of two or more momentum components (Sec.
III.2
).

III.1
Populating a single momentum state

We first optimize the control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
to populate a single momentum state
\displaystyle\ket{\chi_{n}}
. In doing so, we somehow “erase” in momentum space the information on the periodicity of the wavefunction. We compare the experimentally obtained momentum states to the targeted ones by computing the average fidelity

{\cal F}(p_{\mathrm{exp}},p_{\mathrm{t}})=\left[\sum_{\ell}\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{exp},\ell}}\sqrt{p_{\mathrm{t},\ell}}\right]^{2},

(10)

where the
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{exp},\ell}=|c_{\mathrm{exp},\ell}|^{2}
correspond to the populations of the different momentum components of the experimental distribution
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{exp}}
, obtained by averaging over 10 experimental realizations. The ideal targeted momentum distribution
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{t}}
is here given by
\displaystyle p_{\mathrm{t,\ell}}=\delta_{\ell,n}
. We compare the experimental fidelity to the theoretical value
\displaystyle{\cal F}_{\mathrm{num}}
obtained from numerical simulations using the same control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
. We use this fidelity to assess our results rather than the figure of merit of Eq. (
9
) as it is closer to the usually defined fidelity in quantum physics (the modulus squared of the states overlap). The chosen figure of merit for the algorithm leads however to a simpler gradient computation, and both quantities should reach the value 1 for an optimal control solution.

In the experiments shown in Fig.
1

\displaystyle s\simeq 5
and populate various positive single momentum states up to the momentum state
\displaystyle p=10\,\hbar k_{\mathrm{L}}
. The measured experimental fidelities are very close to the numerical ones, and both show a similar trend, with slowly decreasing values as we go from low momentum targets to high momentum targets. At the same time, the control phase profile gets more complex, as seen in Fig.

\displaystyle p=10\,\hbar k_{\mathrm{L}}
(Fig.

We recover here a standard result of optimal control, namely that the reachable set increases for larger control times. It is worth noticing that the QOC algorithm allows us to reach momentum values that are much higher than the ones accessible from the classical free dynamics

IV
Full state control of momentum superpositions

In Sec.
III
, the control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
was optimized toward a target defined solely in terms of the populations
\displaystyle{p_{\ell}}
of the momentum peaks regardless of their relative phases. In this section, we improve the degree of control by targeting both the populations of and the relative phases between different momentum components. To fulfill these requirements, we run our optimization algorithm with a figure of merit sensitive to phase differences, the standard quantum fidelity:

\mathbb{F}=\mathcal{F}_{Q}=|\langle C(t_{\mathrm{f}})|C_{\mathrm{t}}\rangle|^{2},

(11)

with
\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{t}}
the target vector.

IV.2
Preparing lattice eigenstates

To further illustrate our control on the relative phase in momentum superpositions, we use the very same optimal control algorithm to prepare eigenstates of the lattice potential. For a given lattice quasi-momentum
\displaystyle q\equiv\tilde{q}k_{\mathrm{L}}
, the
\displaystyle n^{\mathrm{th}}
Bloch function reads:

\ket{\psi_{n,q}}=\sum_{\ell}c_{\ell}^{(n,q)}\ket{\chi_{\ell+\tilde{q}}},

(13)

where the coefficients
\displaystyle c_{\ell}^{(n,q)}
are
solutions of the stationary Schrödinger equation

E_{n,q}{c}_{\ell}=(\ell+\tilde{q})^{2}c_{\ell}-\frac{s}{4}(c_{\ell-1}+c_{\ell+1}),

(14)

with
\displaystyle E_{n,q}
in units of
\displaystyle E_{\mathrm{L}}
.

As the atoms are initially prepared in the ground state of the lattice, for which
\displaystyle q=0
, we first target eigenstates in that subspace. When
\displaystyle q=0
, the parity of the quantum state in the
\displaystyle S
,
\displaystyle D
,
\displaystyle G
… bands is even (
\displaystyle c_{\ell}=c_{-\ell}
), while it is odd in the
\displaystyle P
,
\displaystyle F
, … bands (
\displaystyle c_{\ell}=-c_{-\ell}
). The capability to adjust the relative phase between the different momentum components is therefore of utmost importance for such targets.

The result of such a preparation is shown in Fig.

(

). Here we first apply a control field
\displaystyle\varphi(t)
to generate the momentum superposition corresponding to the eigenstate in the
\displaystyle P
band (at
\displaystyle q=0
), and we monitor the evolution of the momentum distribution over the following
\displaystyle 110\text{\,}\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro s}

\displaystyle\simeq 1.5\,T_{0}
, to reveal the lattice dynamics. The measured momentum distribution is shown in Fig.

(

\displaystyle P

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Q,\mathrm{num}}=99\%
). Finally in Fig.

We can also effectively reach lattice eigenstates at
\displaystyle q=q_{0}\neq 0
by exploiting a change of reference frame. To that end we first use the optimal control algorithm to prepare the momentum superposition with the coefficients
\displaystyle c_{\ell}^{(n,q_{0})}
(see Eq. (
13
)). If, as in all previous experiments, the lattice was returned to a constant phase
\displaystyle\varphi=0
after preparation, then the obtained state would not be an eigenstate. If however we set the lattice in motion, applying right after preparation a phase
\displaystyle\varphi(t>t_{\mathrm{f}})=2\pi(\hbar q_{0}/m)(t-t_{\mathrm{f}})/d
, we translate the prepared superposition into the
\displaystyle q=q_{0}
subspace in the reference frame of the lattice, making it a proper eigenstate.

In Fig.
4
(
c

\displaystyle D
band at
\displaystyle q=0.25\,k_{\mathrm{L}}
. As expected, the populations of the prepared superposition do not evolve in time, demonstrating again that we have generated an eigenstate (the expected fidelity of the prepared state to the ideal eigenstate is
\displaystyle\mathcal{F}_{Q,\mathrm{num}}=99\%
). Note that the same state could in principle be reached by a direct optimization in the laboratory frame with an additional force breaking the translational symmetry.

Finally we can use optimal control to prepare more complex superpositions, such as the superposition of two lattice eigenstates. This is illustrated in Fig.

\displaystyle F

\displaystyle S

\displaystyle D

\displaystyle q=0.25\,k_{\mathrm{L}}
(Fig.

V
Conclusion and perspectives

We have demonstrated a versatile optimal control scheme for ultracold atoms in an optical lattice, that relies on the modulation of a
single
parameter, the lattice position. With this simple scheme it is possible to prepare arbitrary periodic states in the lattice, with full control on the populations and relative phases of the momentum components. We have exploited this technique to reach hundreds of different targets with a high fidelity.
Interestingly, it is possible to produce in a fast manner quantum states that cannot be reached by adiabatic transformations. This method enables us to efficiently prepare lattice eigenstates in a straightforward way, as compared to periodic modulations of phase or amplitude
Cabrera-Gutiérrez et al. 2019
, and is also robust to the presence of a small external confinement.

This work is a promising step forward in the context of quantum simulation as it could be extended to the tailoring of the wavefunction to either load specific semi-classical orbits in the phase-space of the lattice
Arnal et al. 2020
, or to optimally prepare initial states for coupled atomic momentum lattices
An et al. 2021
or lattice-based Floquet systems
Ozawa and Price 2019
;
Eckardt 2017
;
Goldman and Dalibard 2014
. Our protocol can be readily generalized to lattices of higher dimensions. A stimulating perspective for quantum simulation is to extend this single parameter scheme to strongly interacting systems. Besides, optimal control opens new perspectives in quantum sensing, when the control function is tailored in order to magnify the response of a quantum system to a specific parameter, such as an external force.
