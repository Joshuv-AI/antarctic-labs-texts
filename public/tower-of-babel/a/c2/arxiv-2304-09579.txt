Cauchy relations in linear elasticity: Algebraic and physics aspects
Yakov Itin

Abstract
The Cauchy relations distinguish between rari- and multi-constant linear elasticity theories. These relations are treated in this paper in a form that is invariant under two groups of transformations: indices permutation and general linear transformations of the basis. The irreducible decomposition induced by the permutation group is outlined. The Cauchy relations are then formulated as a requirement of nullification of an invariant subspace. A successive decomposition under rotation group allows to define the partial Cauchy relations and two types of elastic materials. We explore several applications of the full and partial Cauchy relations in physics of materials. The structure’s deviation from the basic physical assumptions of Cauchy’s model is defined in an invariant form. The Cauchy and non-Cauchy contributions to Hooke’s law and elasticity energy are explained. We identify wave velocities and polarization vectors that are independent of the non-Cauchy part for acoustic wave propagation. Several bounds are derived for the elasticity invariant parameters.

(4)

This elasticity tensor subspace is preserved under arbitrary invertible transformations of the basis, i.e., it is invariant under the action of the group
GL(3,\mathbb{R})

In Section 5, we study the implication of the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts in the propagation of acoustic waves. We demonstrate that the velocity of the pure longitudinal wave and the polarization of the pure shear wave are both independent of the elasticity tensor’s non-Cauchy part. In the Conclusion section, we briefly recall the results presented in the paper.

2
Cauchy relations—algebraic aspects

We apply the decomposition of the elasticity tensor into smaller sub-tensors as the first step in understanding the algebraic meaning of the Cauchy relations (
1
). In the literature, the harmonic decomposition of the tensor
C^{ijkl}
under the group of space rotation is commonly used; see, e.g.,
[
2
]
. The elasticity tensor is decomposed into five sub-tensors using this method: two scalar tensors, two traceless second-order tensors with five components each, and one traceless fourth-order tensor with nine components. Since the sub-tensors of equal dimensions belong to isomorphic subspaces, they can be replaced by arbitrary independent linear combinations. Consequently, such decomposition is not unique.
A similar approach can be traced back to the classical Lamé’s representation of the elasticity tensor of an isotropic material by two scalars
\lambda
and
\mu
. For an anisotropic material, an analogous construction was applied in
[
5
]
.
When a non-unique decomposition of the elasticity tensor is used, one cannot expect to achieve a defined physical meaning of specific irreducible parts.

In this paper, we advocate a
two-level decomposition
strategy as an alternative. Such an approach was initially put forth by Sirotin
[
41
]

Since the permutation of indices is involved naturally in the very definition of a tensor, we start with the irreducible decomposition that is invariant under the permutation group

partial Cauchy relations.

2.1
Elasticity tensor and its groups of transformations

We start with the basic definition of a tensor as a linear map between vector spaces. Consider a vector space
V
over the real number field
{\mathbb{R}}
. In elasticity theory, the space
V
is assumed to be isomorphic to the ordinary 3-dimensional space
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
(the 3-dimensional translation space of a Euclidean 3-dimensional affine space). A general fourth-order tensor
C
is defined as a multi-linear map of the Cartesian product of four copies of
V
into the field
{\mathbb{R}}
, see, e.g.,
[
27
]

C:V\times V\times V\times V\to{\mathbb{R}}\,.

(8)

Using the basis
e_{i}
of the vector space
V
the tensor
C
is expressed via the tensor basis
e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\cdots
as

C=C^{ijkl}e_{i}\otimes e_{j}\otimes e_{k}\otimes e_{l}.

(9)

We observe that the basic definition of a tensor naturally involves
two different groups of transformations:

•

A
discrete permutation group
that is related to the order of the spaces
V
in the (non-commutative) Cartesian product (
8
). For the fourth-order elasticity tensor, it is the permutation (symmetry) group of four distinct elements, denoted by
S_{4}
. This group is only dependent on the tensor’s order. It is unaffected, for example, by the geometric properties of the space
V
, such as metric, dimension, and so on.

•

A
continuous group
of tensor transformations that is generated by the geometric structure defined in the vector space
V
. It is a group of general linear transformations,
GL(3,\mathbb{R})
, for a bare vector space without any additional structure like inner product, norm, and so on.
For a vector space endowed with the metric tensor, it is the group of orthogonal transformations
O(3,\mathbb{R})
with its subgroup of proper rotations
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
. This group is the same for all tensors, regardless of their order.

Owing to the Schur-Weyl duality theorem (see
[
44
]
, Chapter III, Section 4), the decomposition of a tensor relative to the symmetry group is invariant under the action of the general linear group and its subgroups. Consequently, the two groups listed above can be applied independently.

2.3
Cauchy relations

Two sub-tensors
S^{ijkl}
and
A^{ijkl}
are completely independent and both inherit the basic minor and major symmetries of
C^{ijkl}
. Thus these sub-tensors can be considered elasticity tensors in their own right. One can even imagine materials in which only one of these sub-tensors is nonzero.

As we will discuss in more detail in the following section, the fully symmetric tensor
S^{ijkl}
must be regarded as an essential non-zero part of the elasticity tensor.
Then all we have to do is try to eliminate the mixed-symmetric part
A^{ijkl}
,

A^{ijkl}=2C^{ijkl}-C^{ilkj}-C^{iklj}=0.

(26)

It’s worth noting that there are just 6 linearly independent conditions here. Substituting the values
i,j,\cdots=1,2,3
we obtain from (
26
) the Cauchy relations (
1
). Hence Eq. (
26
) presents the Cauchy relations in a covariant form. Moreover, this equation is irreducible under the action of the permutation group
S_{4}
. It means that when we apply to Eq. (
26
) an arbitrary symmetrization (or skew-symmetrization) operation, the expression on the left-hand side is preserved or vanishes identically. With the symmetric tensor
\Delta_{mn}
at hand, we get an equivalent compact formulation of the Cauchy relations

\Delta_{mn}=0.

(27)

Various compact expressions for the Cauchy relations can be found in the elasticity literature. In
[
15
]
, for example, they are given as

C^{iijk}-C^{ijik}=0.\qquad\mbox{--- no summation in $i$.}

(28)

This formula is not invariant under general linear transformations of the basis because the terms on the left-hand side are not tensors. Another widely-used compact form of the Cauchy relation, see, e.g.,
[
35
]
,
[
40
]
,

C^{ijkl}-C^{ikjl}=0

(29)

is of a proper covariant nature and coincides with the relation
N^{ijkl}=0
discussed above.
This equation does not fulfill the basic symmetries (
2
) of the elasticity tensor, despite being equivalent to the Cauchy relations (
1
). As a result, the relation (
29
) does not define a subspace of the elasticity tensor space.

In fact, the criterion of the
S_{4}
-invariance produces the Cauchy relations nearly exclusively for any arbitrary linear relationship between the components of
C^{ijkl}
.
Let us assume the existence of a
certain invariant linear relation
between the components of the elasticity tensor. This is in addition to the elasticity tensor’s basic minor and major symmetries.
By taking into account the symmetries (
2
) and (
3
), we conclude that a most general linear invariant relation between the components of the elasticity tensor has a form

Z^{ijkl}=\alpha C^{ijkl}+\beta C^{iklj}+\gamma C^{ilkj}=0\,,

(30)

where
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
are numerical parameters.
In general, Eq. (
30
) is a system of 21 linear equations.

First, we apply the full symmetrization operation of
Z^{ijkl}
to all its four indices. As a result, we obtain a sub-system of 15 linearly independent equations

Z^{(ijkl)}=(\alpha+\beta+\gamma)S^{ijkl}=0\,,

(31)

As we mentioned above, the fully symmetric part of the elasticity tensor
S^{ijkl}
is essential and non-zero. Consequently, Eq. (
31
) yields a relation between the parameters
\alpha=-\beta-\gamma
.
When this value of
a
is substituted into Eq. (
30
), we get

\beta\left(C^{iklj}-C^{ijkl}\right)+\gamma\left(C^{ilkj}-C^{ijkl}\right)=0\,.

(32)

This is the most general form of the Cauchy relations.
In particular, with the parameters
\beta=\gamma=1
we get back to Eq. (
26
). Eq. (
29
) is also included in the family (
32
) when the parameters
\beta=1,\,\gamma=-1
are used. Equivalently, Eq. (
32
) can be written in term of the tensor
A^{iklj}

\beta\left(A^{iklj}-A^{ijkl}\right)+\gamma\left(A^{ilkj}-A^{ijkl}\right)=0\,.

(33)

If the parameters
\beta,\gamma
do not vanish simultaneously, Eq. (
33
) is equivalent to
\Delta_{mn}=0
.
This fact can be derived by multiplying both sides of Eq. (
33
) by a pair of permutation pseudo-tensors.

Hence we have seen that the Cauchy relation is a result of an arbitrary non-trivial linear covariant relation
Z^{ijkl}=0
between the entries of the elasticity tensor. Furthermore, there is an infinite family of covariant expressions of the Cauchy relation.
Since they are all equal to
\Delta_{mn}=0
, they are also all equal to one another.

However, from an algebraic standpoint, the form (
26
) occupies a distinct position in this infinite family. Indeed, the equations from the set (
33
) define various subspaces of dimension 6 in the 81-dimensional space of fourth-order tensors for different values of the parameters
\beta,\gamma
. Even still, there is only one of these subspaces that belongs to the elasticity tensor’s 21-dimensional space.
This subspace is defined by Eq. (
26
).
With this fact in hand, we can formulate the Cauchy relations (
1
) in an invariant algebraic form:

Cauchy relations:
The invariant subspace
{\cal A}
of the elasticity space
{\cal C}
is nullified.

2.4
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
-decomposition of the elasticity tensor

According to the irreducible decomposition method, Cauchy relations are characterized by the disappearance of the
S_{4}
-invariant subspace
{\cal{A}}\subset{\cal{S}}
. Recall that the vector space
{\cal{C}}
has only two non-trivial subspaces,
{\cal{S}}
and
{\cal{A}}
, which are invariant under the group
GL(3,\mathbb{R})
. Under a smaller subgroup
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
of spatial rotations, an additional decomposition of the elasticity tensor space is available. Due to Weyl
[
44
]
, Chapter V, such decomposition is directly connected to the metric tensor
g_{ij}
. Formally, it can be treated as a decomposition of a pair of tensors
\{C^{ijkl}\,,g_{ij}\}
rather than of the elasticity tensor
C^{ijkl}
itself. Applying the Euclidean metric tensor
g_{ij}={\rm diag}(1,1,1)
, the trace of a tensor can be expressed in a covariant form. Then we are able to identify the trace and traceless subspases.

2.4.1
Cauchy part

Let us start with the fully symmetric part
S^{ijkl}
. By contracting this quantity with the metric tensor, we can define a unique scalar and a unique traceless second-order tensor. Define a scalar
S
and a tensor
P^{ij}
as follows:

S=g_{ij}g_{kl}S^{ijkl}\,,

(34)

and

P^{ij}=g_{kl}S^{ijkl}-\frac{1}{3}Sg^{ij}\,.

(35)

Observe that the latter tensor is traceless
P^{ij}g_{ij}=0
.
Then two irreducible parts of the tensor
S^{ijkl}
are expressed as

{}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}=\frac{1}{5}Sg^{(ij}g^{kl)}=\frac{S}{15}\left(g^{ij}g^{kl}+g^{ik}g^{jl}+g^{il}g^{jk}\right).

(36)

and

{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}=\frac{6}{7}P^{(ij}g^{kl)}=\frac{1}{7}\left(P^{ij}g^{kl}+P^{ik}g^{jl}+P^{il}g^{jk}+P^{jk}g^{il}+P^{jl}g^{ik}+P^{kl}g^{ij}\right).

(37)

Denote the residue part as

R^{ijkl}=S^{ijkl}-{}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}-{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}.

(38)

The coefficients chosen in (
36
) and (
37
) ensure that the tensor
R^{ijkl}
is fully symmetric and traceless, as shown in
[
19
]
. Hence, it is the third irreducible component of
S^{ijkl}

{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}=R^{ijkl}\,.

(39)

As a result, the Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor is decomposed into three irreducible pieces, each of which is invariant under the rotation group
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
,

S^{ijkl}={}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}+{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}+{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}\,.

(40)

In terms of vector spaces, this decomposition is presented as a direct sum of three subspaces of the space
{\cal S}

{\cal S}={}^{(1)}\!{\cal S}\oplus{}^{(2)}\!{\cal S}\oplus{}^{(3)}\!{\cal S}\,.

(41)

The dimensions of the space
{\cal S}
is distributed between its subspaces, respectively, as

15=1+5+9.

(42)

The sub-tensors in (
40
) are mutually orthogonal. It means that for
A,B=1,2,3
with
A\neq B
, we have

{}^{(A)}\!S_{ijkl}\quad\!\!\!\!{}^{(B)}\!S^{ijkl}=0.

(43)

These relations follow from the fact that the tensors
P^{ij}
and
R^{ijkl}
are traceless and symmetric.

2.4.2
Non-Cauchy part

For dealing with the non-Cauchy part
A^{ijkl}
, we define a unique linearly independent scalar invariant

A=g_{ij}g_{kl}A^{ijkl}.

(44)

In terms of Voigt’s notation it reads

A=\frac{4}{3}\left[\left(C^{12}-C^{44}\right)+\left(C^{13}-C^{55}\right)+\left(C^{23}-C^{66}\right)\right].

(45)

Then we construct a fourth-order tensor

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}=\frac{A}{12}\left(2g^{ij}g^{kl}-g^{il}g^{jk}-g^{ik}g^{jl}\right)\,,

(46)

that satisfies the symmetry identities of the non-Cauchy part (
15
) and (
16
).
The leading coefficient in Eq. (
46
) is chosen in such a way that the equation
A=g_{ij}g_{kl}{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}
holds.
Now we are able to define the decomposition of the non-Cauchy tensor into the scalar and traceless sub-tensors

A^{ijkl}={}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}+{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}\,.

(47)

The corresponding subspaces intersect only at zero. The symmetries of the sub-tensors
{}^{(1)}A^{ijkl}
and
{}^{(2)}A^{ijkl}
are the same as those of the tensor
A^{ijkl}
. As a result, they constitute invariant subspaces of the
\cal A
space: Consequently we are dealing with the direct sum decomposition

{\cal A}={}^{(1)}\!{\cal A}\oplus{}^{(1)}\!{\cal A},\qquad 6=1+5.

(48)

To demonstrate that this decomposition is irreducible,
the matrix representation
\Delta_{ij}
is useful. We can define a unique scalar trace
\Delta=g^{ij}\Delta_{ij}

and then split the matrix
\Delta_{ij}
into the trace and the traceless pieces,

\Delta_{ij}=\frac{1}{3}g_{ij}\Delta+Q_{ij}\,,

(49)

where the residue part
Q_{ij}
satisfies the traceless relation
Q_{ij}g^{ij}=0
.
In terms of the second-order tenors the sub-tensors of the non-Cauchy part are expressed as (
46
) with
A=2\Delta
.

The second sub-tensor reads

{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\epsilon^{ikm}\epsilon^{jln}+\epsilon^{ilm}\epsilon^{jkn}\right)Q_{mn}\,,

(50)

Its explicit expression is given by

{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}=\frac{1}{2}\left(g^{il}Q^{jk}+g^{ik}Q^{jl}+g^{jl}Q^{ik}+g^{jk}Q^{il}-2g^{ij}Q^{kl}-2g^{kl}Q^{ij}\right)\,,

(51)

The decomposition (
49
) of the symmetric second-order tensor
\Delta_{ij}
is irreducible and unique. Since this tensor is completely equivalent to the fourt-order tensor
A^{ijkl}
, the decomposition (
47
) is irreducible and unique also.

2.4.3
Two-level decomposition

We described the two-level decomposition of the elasticity tensor. At the first level, the tensor
C^{ijkl}
is decomposed uniquely into a sum of two subtensors—the Cauchy part
S^{ijkl}
and the non-Cauchy part
A^{ijkl}
. These sub-tensors are invariant simultaneously under the
S_{4}
-permutations of the indices and under the general linear transformation of the basis,
GL(3,{\mathbb{R}})
. At the second level, the tensors
S^{ijkl}
and
A^{ijkl}
are decomposed uniquely under the group of spatial rotations,
SO(3,{\mathbb{R}})
. As a result, two levels of the decomposition are presented as

\displaystyle C^{ijkl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{ijkl}+A^{ijkl}

(52)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}+{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}+{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}\right)+\left({}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}+{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}\right).

In terms of the corresponding vector spaces, we have a unique two-level decomposition

\displaystyle{\cal C}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle{\cal S}\oplus{\cal A}

(53)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({}^{(1)}\!{\cal S}\oplus{}^{(2)}\!{\cal S}\oplus{}^{(3)}\!{\cal S}\right)\oplus\left({}^{(1)}\!{\cal A}\oplus{}^{(2)}\!{\cal A}\right)

with the dimensions distributed, respectively, as

21=15+9=(1+5+9)+(1+5)\,.

(54)

2.5
Uniqueness of the elasticity tensor decomposition

The decomposition (
52
) is constructed by two scalars,
S
and
A
, two second-order traceless tensors,
P_{ij}
and
Q_{ij}
, and a fully symmetric and traceless fourth-order tensor
R_{ijkl}
.
The same kinds of tensors arise in the frequently used
harmonic decomposition of the elasticity tensor
; see, e.g.,
[
2
]
,
[
3
]
,
[
5
]
,
[
6
]
,
[
7
]
,
[
11
]
, and
[
12
]
.
The latter decomposition is generated from the harmonic polynomials, i.e., the polynomial solutions of the Laplace equation. The corresponding tensors are required to be completely symmetric and totally traceless.
The most concise expression of this type was proposed by Cowin
[
5
]
,

\displaystyle C^{ijkl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle ag^{ij}g^{kl}+b\left(g^{ik}g^{jl}+g^{il}g^{jk}\right)+\left(g^{ij}{\hat{A}}^{kl}+g^{kl}{\hat{A}}^{ij}\right)+

(55)

\displaystyle\left(g^{ik}{\hat{B}}^{jl}+g^{il}{\hat{B}}^{jk}+g^{jk}{\hat{B}}^{il}+g^{ik}{\hat{B}}^{jl}\right)+Z^{ijkl}\,.

The first two terms here repeat the well-known Lamé’s description of the elasticity tensor for isotropic materials. Observe that the five individual terms in in (
55
) are reducible because they do not meet the basic symmetries of the elasticity tensor.

An alternative presentation due to Backus
[
2
]
,

\displaystyle C^{ijkl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle H^{ijkl}+\left(H^{ij}g^{kl}+H^{ik}g^{jl}+H^{il}g^{jk}+H^{jk}g^{il}+H^{jl}g^{ik}+H^{kl}g^{ij}\right)+

(56)

\displaystyle H\left(g^{ij}g^{kl}+g^{ik}g^{jl}+g^{il}g^{jk}\right)+h\left(g^{ij}g^{kl}-\frac{1}{2}g^{il}g^{jk}-\frac{1}{2}g^{ik}g^{jl}\right)+

\displaystyle\left(h^{ij}g^{kl}+h^{kl}g^{ij}-\frac{1}{2}h^{jl}g^{ik}-\frac{1}{2}h^{ik}g^{jl}-\frac{1}{2}h^{jk}g^{il}-\frac{1}{2}h^{il}g^{jk}\right),

does not suffer from these restrictions. In fact, it is equivalent (up to the leading coefficients) to the result of our two-level decomposition (
52
).
However, it is not unique when the decomposition (
56
) is solely studied on a
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
-invariant level. This is due to the isomorphism of the tensor spaces of the same dimension. In particular, the one-dimension spaces in (
56
) presented by the scalars
h
and
H
are isomorphic to one another. Thus they can be mixed into two arbitrary independent linear combinations as in (
55
). Similarly, the five-dimensional spaces, presented by the tensors
h^{ij}
and
H^{ij}
are isomorphic to one another. Thus they can be rearranged into two other spaces presented in (
55
) by the tensors
{\hat{A}}^{ij}
and
{\hat{B}}^{ij}
.

The uniqueness of the sub-tensors is a highly sought quality since only unequally defined tensors may be given an
invariant physical sense
.
The two-level decomposition overcomes the problem of uniqueness. In particular, the decomposition (
52
) presents the one-dimensional subspaces
{}^{(1)}\!{\cal S}
and
{}^{(1)}\!{\cal A}
as originating from separate sources, namely the Cauchy and non-Cauchy spaces
{\cal S}
and
{\cal A}
, respectively. As a result, even though they have the same algebraic dimensions, these subspaces cannot be combined into a new pair of 1-dimensional subspaces. Similarly, the five-dimensional subspaces
{}^{(2)}\!{\cal S}
and
{}^{(2)}\!{\cal A}
cannot be mixed into independent linear combinations, even if they are isomorphic. In this sense, the sub-tensors given in (
52
) are defined uniquely.

Consider a simple electromagnetic analogy: Electric and magnetic fields belong to three-dimensional vector spaces. These spaces are algebraically isomorphic one to another. The fields, on the other hand, cannot be mixed, i.e. rearranged into two independent linear combinations. The reason for this is that the electric and magnetic vector fields have distinct origins—twisted and untwisted tensors, respectively. See
[
16
]
for more details.

2.6
Partial Cauchy relations—two classes of materials

Under the action of the rotation group, the 6-dimensional vector space
{\cal A}
is decomposed into the direct sum of two proper subspaces: the 1-dimensional subspace of scalar (trace) tensors
{}^{(1)}\!{\cal A}
and the 5-dimensional subspace of traceless tensors
{}^{(2)}\!{\cal A}
.
Thus the Cauchy relations
A^{ijkl}=0
also can be divided into a system of two independent equations:

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\,,\quad{\rm and}\quad{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\,.

(57)

We may now suggest a way to relax the classical Cauchy conditions by introducing a notion of
partial Cauchy relations
. In particular, we can consider an invariant relations that nullifies only one subspace of the tensor
A^{ijkl}
.
We define the
partial Cauchy relations
as

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}\neq 0\,,\quad{\rm but}\quad{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\,.

(58)

This is a system of 5 independent equations. This partial Cauchy relation can be written in terms of the traceless second-order tensor

Q_{ij}=0\,.

(59)

We have the following expression (
45
) for the scalar
A
.

A=(4/3)\left(C^{12}+C^{13}+C^{23}-C^{44}-C^{55}-C^{66}\right).

(60)

Then the
components of the tensor
Q^{ij}
are expressed as

\displaystyle Q^{11}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4/3)\left(C^{23}-C^{44}\right)-(1/3)A,\qquad Q^{12}=C^{45}-C^{36},

(61)

\displaystyle Q^{22}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4/3)\left(C^{13}-C^{55}\right)-(1/3)A,\qquad Q^{13}=C^{46}-C^{25},

(62)

\displaystyle Q^{33}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(4/3)\left(C^{12}-C^{66}\right)-(1/3)A,\qquad Q^{23}=C^{56}-C^{14}.

(63)

Thus the partial Cauchy relations are given by the system

\displaystyle C^{23}-C^{44}=(1/4)A,\qquad C^{45}-C^{36}=0,

(64)

\displaystyle C^{13}-C^{55}=(1/4)A,\qquad C^{46}-C^{25}=0,

(65)

\displaystyle C^{12}-C^{66}=(1/4)A,\qquad C^{56}-C^{14}=0.

(66)

Due to the relation
Q^{11}+Q^{22}+Q^{33}=0
, we have here only five independent equations.

The analysis of the irreducible parts of crystals
[
20
]
shows that the isotropic media and the cubic class crystals satisfy the partial Cauchy relation of this type.
We get to the conclusion that the scalar
A
is crucial, and the partial Cauchy relation (
58
) is worth to consider. The scalar relation
A\neq 0
is the only one that holds for any natural material.
Using the non-zero scalar
A
, we are now in a position to divide the variety of elastic materials into two distinct classes:

•

The
A^{+}
-materials
with a positive scalar parameter
A
,

•

The
A^{-}
-materials
with a negative scalar parameter
A.

We demonstrate in the following section that these two categories of materials do indeed have certain unique physical characteristics.

From the pure algebraic view, we can try to require nullification of the scalar sub-space
{}^{(1)}{\cal A}
only, i.e. to define the
partial Cauchy relations of the second type
:

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\,,\quad{\rm but}\quad{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}\neq 0\,.

(67)

We have here only one condition on the components of the elasticity tensor.
To our knowledge, there are no materials that satisfy the partial Cauchy relation of this type.

2.7
Results

Let us briefly recall the results of the algebraic consideration.
A two-level decomposition of the elasticity tensor was considered. Two distinct pieces—Cauchy parts and non-Cauchy parts—are identified at the first level. These parts are split into rotational covariant sub-tensors at the second level. It’s worth noting that the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts are structurally similar. Each of them contains both the 1-dimensional subspaces

{}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}\sim S\,,\qquad{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}\sim A\,,

(68)

and the 5-dimensional subspaces

{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}\sim P_{ij}\,,\qquad{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}\sim Q_{ij}\,.

(69)

The Cauchy part contains an additional 9-dimensional subspace
{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}\sim R_{ijkl}
.
According to the analysis of the crystal systems
[
20
]
, the crystal symmetry cannot distinguish between the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts. The matrices
P_{ij}
and
Q_{ij}
, in particular, have the same symmetry structure. They are even proportional to one another in high symmetry systems. In all cases, the
algebraic differences
between two components of the elasticity tensor must result in some differences in their physical properties. In the next sections, we will discuss how these
physical differences
can be recognized.

3
Cauchy relations—physics aspects

Owing to the algebraic invariance of the Cauchy relations, we conclude that they can have a defined physical meaning even without being strongly fulfilled. In this section, we examine certain physical issues where Cauchy relations show up.

3.1
Cauchy relations for natural materials

We start with a brief account of the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts of the isotropic materials as well as of some high-symmetric crystals.

3.1.1
Isotropic materials

Since the spatial directions have no influence on the elasticity properties of the isotropic material, only the scalar invariants of
C^{ijkl}
and the metric tensor
g_{ij}
may be used to define the elasticity tensor.
Usually, the tensor
C^{ijkl}
of the isotropic material is described by two Lamé moduli
\lambda
and
\mu
, see e.g.
[
23
]
,
[
25
]
, as

C^{ijkl}=\lambda\,g^{ij}g^{kl}+\mu\left(g^{ik}g^{lj}+g^{il}g^{jk}\right).

(70)

Observe that the two terms here are reducible under the action of the permutation group.
The irreducible decomposition of
C^{ijkl}
is provided by two other tensors, which are independent linear combinations of the terms in (
70
)

S^{ijkl}=\frac{S}{15}\left(g^{ij}g^{kl}+g^{ik}g^{lj}+g^{il}g^{jk}\right),\quad{\rm where}\quad S=5(\lambda+2\mu),

(71)

and

A^{ijkl}=\frac{A}{12}\left(2g^{ij}g^{kl}-g^{ik}g^{lj}-g^{il}g^{jk}\right),\quad{\rm where}\quad A=4(\lambda-\mu).

(72)

As a result, the isotropic material’s elasticity tensor is expressed as the sum of its two irreducible scalar parts—–one Cauchy and one non-Cauchy. These parts are also rotational invariant, thus we have a decomposition

C^{ijkl}={}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}+{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}.

(73)

The Cauchy relations are equivalent to one scalar equation

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad C^{12}-C^{44}=0.

(74)

In term of the Lamé moduli, it reads
\lambda=\mu
. In terms of the Poisson ratio, it yields the classical critical value

\nu=\frac{\lambda}{2\lambda+2\mu}=0.25\,.

(75)

Note that the partial Cauchy relations,
A\neq 0
and
Q_{ij}=0
, hold identically for all isotropic materials. Then the non-zero parameter
A
allows for a covariant identification of
two types of isotropic materials:

•

The
A^{+}
-materials with
\lambda>\mu
, or, equivalently,
\nu>0.25
;

Examples: Gold—
\nu=0.43
, Aluminum—
\nu=0.32
, Copper —
\nu=0.355
.

•

The
A^{-}
-materials with
\lambda<\mu
, or, equivalently,
\nu<0.25
;

Examples: Concrete—
\nu=0.2
, Cast Iron—
\nu=0.24
, Glass—
\nu=0.22
.

It is worthwhile to note the recently discussed Poisson’s ratio lower bound for isotropic materials,
\nu>0.2
(see
[
29
]
).

3.1.2
Cubic crystals

With three independent elasticity modules
C^{11},C^{12}
, and
C^{44}
, the cubic system represents the simplest anisotropic material. In Voigt’s notation, the elasticity modules are arranged into a
6\times 6
matrix as follows:

C^{ijkl}=\begin{bmatrix}C^{11}&C^{12}&C^{12}&0&0&0\\
*&C^{11}&C^{12}&0&0&0\\
*&*&C^{11}&0&0&0\\
*&*&*&C^{44}&0&0\\
*&*&*&*&C^{44}&0\\
*&*&*&*&*&C^{44}\,\end{bmatrix}.

(76)

The Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor is presented by two sub-tensors, see
[
20
]
,

S^{ijkl}={}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}+{}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}.

(77)

There is only one independent component in each of these sub-tensors. In (

{}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}
is represented as a real scalar. In the case of the cubic crystal, the second part
{}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}
can be expressed as a dimensionless tensor multiplied by the scalar

The non-Cauchy part of the cubic class elasticity tensor contains the first scalar part only

A^{ijkl}={}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}.

(78)

This tensor depends of only one scalar invariant
A
.
Consequently, the elasticity tensor is represented as

\displaystyle C^{ijkl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{ijkl}+A^{ijkl}

(79)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}+{}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}\right)+\left({}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}\right).

Three corresponding tensor spaces are one-dimensional.
Cauchy relations are equivalent to only one scalar equation, just like isotropic material

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad C^{12}-C^{44}=0.

(80)

The partial Cauchy relation is fulfilled for all cubic system materials.
We identify
two types of cubic materials.

\bullet

.

\bullet

3.1.3
Transversely isotropic system

The transversely isotropic (hexagonal) system is characterized by five independent elasticity parameters.
In Voigt’s notation, the elasticity tensor is presented
[
23
]
by a symmetric
6\times 6
matrix,

C^{ijkl}=\begin{bmatrix}C^{11}&C^{12}&C^{13}&0&0&0\\
*&C^{11}&C^{13}&0&0&0\\
*&*&C^{33}&0&0&0\\
*&*&*&C^{44}&0&0\\
*&*&*&*&C^{44}&0\\
*&*&*&*&*&C^{66}\,\end{bmatrix}

(81)

with an additional linear relation

C^{66}=\frac{1}{2}(C^{11}-C^{12}).

(82)

The two-level decomposition of this elasticity tensor is presented by

\displaystyle C^{ijkl}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle S^{ijkl}+A^{ijkl}

(83)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left({}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}+{}^{(2)}S^{ijkl}+{}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}\right)+\left({}^{(1)}A^{ijkl}+{}^{(2)}A^{ijkl}\right).

All the five tensor spaces in the last line are one-dimensional. The two isotropic sub-tensors
{}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}
and
{}^{(1)}A^{ijkl}
, in particular, are represented by two linear invariants
S
and
A
, respectively. The three remaining anisotropic parts can be written as scalars multiplied by dimensionless tensors using quadratic invariants, see
[
19
]
. In particular,

{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}\sim P=\sqrt{P_{ij}P^{ij}},\qquad{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}\sim Q=\sqrt{Q_{ij}Q^{ij}},\qquad{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}\sim R=\sqrt{R_{ijkl}R^{ijkl}}.

(84)

The classical Cauchy relations are expressed by two equations

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}=0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}=0.

(85)

In terms of elasticity invariants, we only have two independent scalar conditions on the elements of the elasticity tensor,

A=0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad Q=0.

(86)

The partial Cauchy relations discussed above require a weaker condition
Q=0
. In this case, the invariant
A
is not zero so we can define two types of transversely isotropic materials:

\bullet
The
A^{+}
transversely isotropic materials satisfy the relation

C^{11}+2C^{13}-2C^{44}-3C^{66}>0

(87)

\bullet
The
A^{-}
transversely isotropic materials satisfy the relation

C^{11}+2C^{13}-2C^{44}-3C^{66}<0

(88)

3.2
Deviation from the Cauchy model

Even though no naturally occurring material perfectly satisfies the conventional Cauchy relations, it can be fascinating to look into how much a material’s elasticity tensor deviates from the ideal Cauchy model.
A detailed discussion of this problem is given in
[
15
]
and the references therein. Although the deviation expression is based solely on macro-characteristics (the entries of the elasticity tensor), it may nevertheless be able to provide information on the micro-structure of the material.
Thus, the question arises:

How can the deviation of the elastic properties of natural materials from the ideal Cauchy model be expressed in a unique covariant form?

This question brings us back to the irreducible decomposition of tensors. To highlight the issue, we recall an alternative splitting of the elasticity tensor into two independent terms,
M^{ijkl}
and
N^{ijkl}
, as in (
24
). Despite the fact that the skew-symmetric part
N^{ijkl}
has 6 independent components, its co-partner
M^{ijkl}
has a total of 21 separate components. The latter number is lowered to 15 only when the Cauchy relations are precisely met. As a result, the tensor
N^{ijkl}
is unable to explain the elasticity tensor’s deviation from the Cauchy model.

The
S_{4}
-irreducible decomposition

C^{ijkl}=S^{ijkl}+A^{ijkl}\,,

(89)

with the sub-tensors given in
Eqs.(
12
) and (
13
) fulfills all necessary algebraic requirements:
(i) The sub-tensors satisfy the symmetries of the elasticity tensor,
(ii) Their dimensions are
21=15+6
as required by the direct sum decomposition of the vector space, and
(iii) The corresponding subspaces are orthogonal to one another,
S^{ijkl}A_{ijkl}=0
.

As a result, the tensors
S^{ijkl}
and
A^{ijkl}
may be referred to as the Cauchy and non-Cauchy components of the elasticity tensor, respectively. In other words, the deviation of the elasticity tensor from the Cauchy model is expressed by the fourth-order tensor
A^{ijkl}
or, equivalently, by the second-order tensor
\Delta_{ij}
.
In
[
15
]
, a numerical value of the deviation of the elasticity tensor from the Cauchy model was given using some specific entries of the matrix
\Delta_{ij}
. There was also an attempt to attach a physical significance to the sign of this deviation.
Because the value (and sign) of a specific component of
\Delta_{ij}
is not retained when a basis is transformed, such a consideration is obviously non-invariant.
However, it is helpful in the simple (high symmetry) cases of cubic crystals and isotropic materials.

In order to give an invariant meaning to the deviation from Cauchy’s model we apply the Frobenius (Euclidean) norm of the elasticity tensor

||C||=\sqrt{C^{ijkl}C_{ijkl}}\,.

(90)

For the partial tensors
S^{ijkl}
and
A^{ijkl}
, the norm is expressed similarly,

||S||=\sqrt{S^{ijkl}S_{ijkl}}\,,\quad{\rm and}\quad||A||=\sqrt{A^{ijkl}A_{ijkl}}\,.

(91)

The scalar
||A||
presents an invariant expression for the deviation of a material from the ideal Cauchy model. Moreover, we can define a dimensionless
Cauchy factor
for an elastic material

{\cal F}_{\rm Cauchy}=\frac{||S||}{||C||}=\frac{||S||}{\sqrt{||S||^{2}+||A||^{2}}}=\sqrt{\frac{S^{ijkl}S_{ijkl}}{C^{ijkl}C_{ijkl}}}\,.

(92)

Consequently,
0\leq{\cal F}_{\rm Cauchy}\leq 1.
A
pure Cauchy material is determined by
{\cal F}_{\rm Cauchy}=1.

From the energy consideration, the Cauchy part is essential (non-zero) for every material, thus
{\cal F}_{\rm Cauchy}>0.
A material with a higher Cauchy factor must have a microscopic structure that is closer to spherical symmetry. The deviation factors for isotropic and cubic materials are shown in
[
19
]
.

Haussühl
[
15
]
also proposed to use the sign of deviation from the Cauchy model as a characteristic of the microstructure of materials. In particular, the positive deviations are related to the predominantly ionic bonds, while the negative deviation is related to the directional bonding, in particular, with strong covalent bonds.
The deviation can be characterized in invariant form by a simple scalar parameter
A
presented in (
44
). Its sign is well-defined and invariant under the coordinate transformations. This representation can be seen as a covariant realization of Haussühl’s proposal. In particular, it is expressed in the division of elastic materials into two classes:
{}^{+}A
-materials and
{}^{-}A
-materials. The scalar
A
is a unique expression for deviation from the Cauchy model when the partial Cauchy relations,
A\neq 0,\,Q_{ij}=0
, hold.

3.3
Hooke’s law decomposed

Let us recall Hooke’s law

\sigma^{ij}=C^{ijkl}\varepsilon_{kl}.

(93)

and its diagrammatic presentation, Fig. 1.

Figure 1:
Anisotropic Hooke’s Law.

In this section, we are looking for how the irreducible decomposition of the elasticity tensor shows up in Hooke’s law. In this instance, Hooke’s law is reduced to a system of two invariant equations, where the invariant components of the elasticity tensor are represented in distinct ways.

The rotation group allows for an invariant decomposition of the second-order strain tensor
\varepsilon_{ij}
into two separate parts.

\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon g_{ij}+u_{ij},\qquad{\rm where}\quad\varepsilon=g^{ij}\varepsilon_{ij},\quad{\rm and}\quad g^{ij}u_{ij}=0\,.

(94)

Here, the first term is the
mean strain tensor (hydrostatic compression)
. It is proportional to the trace of the strain tensor
\varepsilon
.
The second traceless part,
u_{ij}
, is referred to as the
strain deviator (shear strain)
. Due to the traceless condition, this term is defined unique.

Similar to this, the second-order stress tensor
\sigma^{ij}
is decomposed irreducibly into two parts—the trace and the traceless tensors:

\sigma^{ij}=\frac{1}{3}\sigma g^{ij}+s^{ij},\qquad{\rm where}\quad\sigma=g_{ij}\sigma^{ij},\quad{\rm and}\quad g_{ij}s^{ij}=0\,.

(95)

The first term here is referred to as the
mean (hydrostatic) stress
,
while the second term is called the
stress deviator (stress shear)
.
The decompositions of strain and stress are substituted in Hooke’s law (
93
) to give the following:

\frac{\sigma}{3}g^{ij}+s^{ij}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}C^{ijkl}g_{kl}+C^{ijkl}u_{kl}

(96)

To extract the equations for the scalar
\sigma
and the deviator
s^{ij}
, we first compute the trace on both sides of this equation.
Thus we have

\sigma=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}C^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}+C^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}

(97)

To calculate the first term of (
97
), we substitute the decomposition (
11
) and apply the following traceless identities:

{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}={}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}={}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}=0\,.

(98)

Thus we obtain

\frac{\varepsilon}{3}C^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\left({}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}+{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}\right)=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}(S+A)\,.

(99)

For the second term in (
97
), we apply the traceless identities:

{}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}={}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}=0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{}^{(1)}A^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}=0.

(100)

The two remaining expressions are then presented in the form

{}^{(2)}S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}=P^{kl}u_{kl}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{}^{(2)}A^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}=-Q^{kl}u_{kl}.

(101)

As a result, the scalar part of Hooke’s law for the mean value of stress is

\sigma=\frac{S+A}{3}\varepsilon+(P^{kl}-Q^{kl})u_{kl}.

(102)

The shear part is extracted from (
97
) as

s^{ij}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}C^{ijkl}g_{kl}+C^{ijkl}u_{kl}-\frac{\sigma}{3}g^{ij}

(103)

We apply the relations

{}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}g_{kl}=\frac{S}{3}g^{ij},\qquad{}^{(2)}S^{ijkl}g_{kl}=P^{ij},\qquad{}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}g_{kl}=0,

(104)

and

{}^{(1)}A^{ijkl}g_{kl}=\frac{A}{3}g^{ij},\qquad{}^{(2)}A^{ijkl}g_{kl}=-Q^{ij}.

(105)

Consequently, the first term in the right-hand side of (
103
) reads

\frac{\varepsilon}{3}C^{ijkl}g_{kl}=\left(\frac{A+S}{9}g^{ij}+\frac{P^{ij}-Q^{ij}}{3}\right)\varepsilon.

(106)

For the second term in the right-hand side of (
103
), we first derive the relations

{}^{(1)}S^{ijkl}u_{kl}=\frac{2S}{15}u^{ij},\quad{}^{(2)}S^{ijkl}u_{kl}=\frac{1}{7}\left(2P^{ik}u^{j}{}_{k}+2P^{jk}u^{i}{}_{k}+P^{kl}u_{kl}g^{ij}\right),\quad{}^{(3)}S^{ijkl}u_{kl}=R^{ijkl}u_{kl},

(107)

and

{}^{(1)}A^{ijkl}u_{kl}=-\frac{A}{6}u^{ij},\qquad{}^{(2)}A^{ijkl}u_{kl}=Q^{ik}u^{j}{}_{k}+Q^{jk}u^{i}{}_{k}-Q^{kl}u_{kl}g^{ij}.

(108)

Consequently,

C^{ijkl}u_{kl}=\frac{4S-5A}{30}u^{ij}+\left(\frac{2}{7}P^{ik}+Q^{ik}\right)u^{j}{}_{k}+\left(\frac{2}{7}P^{jk}+Q^{jk}\right)u^{i}{}_{k}+\left(\frac{1}{7}P^{kl}-Q^{kl}\right)u_{kl}g^{ij}+R^{ijkl}u_{kl}.

(109)

As a result, the shear part of the stress tensor is expressed as

\displaystyle s^{ij}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{P^{ij}-Q^{ij}}{3}\varepsilon+\frac{4S-5A}{30}u^{ij}+R^{ijkl}u_{kl}+

(110)

\displaystyle\frac{2}{7}\left(P^{in}g^{jm}+P^{jn}g^{im}-\frac{2}{3}P^{mn}g^{ij}\right)u_{mn}+\left(Q^{in}g^{jm}+Q^{jn}g^{im}-\frac{2}{3}Q^{mn}g^{ij}\right)u_{mn}.

Consequently, the system of two equations (
102
) and (
110
) is equivalent to the Hooke’s law (
93
). Fig. 3 depicts a graphical representation of this system. The arrows represent the left-hand side terms of the equations with regard to the invariant irreducible components of the elasticity tensor.

Figure 2:
Anisotropic Hooke’s Law decomposed.

Consider several consequences of these equations

•

We notice that the scalar terms
S
and
A
appear simultaneously. The second-order tensors
P
and
Q
are the same. As a result, Hooke’s law does not distinguish between Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts of the elasticity tensor.

•

The pair of scalars
(S,A)
gives direct relationships between mean stress and mean strain, as well as shear stress and shear strain. As a result, they might be considered essential components of the elasticity tensor.

•

The fourth-order
R
-term does not contribute to the mean-stress equation. As a result, it can be thought of as a pure shear part of the elasticity tensor.

•

The mixed relations between the mean and shear components of the stress and strain are provided by the second-order tensors
(P,Q)
. The mean-value and the shear effects are entirely separated in the particular situation,

P^{ij}=Q^{ij}.

(111)

Let us observe the simplest special case:
In the case of isotropic media, when the
P
,
Q
, and
R
-terms vanish, we have the decomposed Hooke’s law in the form

\sigma=\frac{S+A}{3}\varepsilon,\qquad s^{ij}=\frac{4S-5A}{30}u^{ij}.

(112)

As a result, the shear value and mean value variables are separated. The analogs of the one-dimensional Hooke’s coefficients for mean value and shear are the coefficients

K_{\it mean}=\frac{S+A}{3}\qquad K_{\it shear}=\frac{4S-5A}{30}.

(113)

The Cauchy scalar invariant
S
must be positive in order for the positive stress to result in material compression. We notice the following interesting facts regarding the non-Cauchy scalar invariant
A
:

•

When applied to materials with positive
A
, the non-Cauchy portion raises the
K_{\it mean}
coefficient while lowering the
K_{\it shear}
coefficient.

•

Materials with negative
A
have the opposite effect.

We describe how this effect manifests in the energy of the deformed material in the next section.

4
Cauchy relations and elasticity energy

In this section, we discuss the contributions of the irreducible parts of
C^{ijkl}
to the elasticity energy of deformed material. We are especially interested in how to distinguish between the Cauchy and non-Cauchy contributions.

4.1
Three parts of the elasticity energy

In accordance with Hooke’s law, the quadratic form of the strain tensor variables represents the elasticity energy,

E=\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{ij}\varepsilon_{kl}=\frac{1}{2}C^{ijkl}\varepsilon_{ij}\varepsilon_{kl}\,.

(114)

The major symmetry of the elasticity tensor
C^{ijkl}=C^{klij}
follows namely from the latter expression.
We begin with the conventional decomposition of the strain tensor into two independent parts

\varepsilon_{ij}=\frac{1}{3}\varepsilon g_{ij}+u_{ij}\,

(115)

in order to characterize the contributions of the various components of the elasticity tensor into (
114
). We substitute (
115
) into the energy expression (
114
). As a result, the elastic energy is divided into three components

E=E_{c}+E_{m}+E_{s}\,.

(116)

Here, the compression part of the energy
E_{c}
depends of the mean strain tensor only,

E_{c}=\frac{1}{18}C^{ijkl}\varepsilon^{2}g_{ij}g_{kl}\,.

(117)

The shear part of the energy
E_{s}
depends of the shear strain only,

E_{s}=\frac{1}{2}C^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}\,.

(118)

The mixed part
E_{m}
is influenced by both the compression and shear components of the strain,

E_{m}=\frac{1}{3}C^{ijkl}\varepsilon g_{ij}u_{kl}\,.

(119)

The decomposition of the elasticity tensor into Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts can be substituted now in these three energy expressions. We use the representation given in
[
20
]
.

4.2
The compression part of the energy

By substituting the decomposition (
11
) into the compression part of the energy (
117
), we have

{}^{(c)}E=\frac{1}{18}S^{ijkl}\varepsilon^{2}g_{ij}g_{kl}+\frac{1}{18}A^{ijkl}\varepsilon^{2}g_{ij}g_{kl}\,.

(120)

The traceless properties yield

{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}={}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}={}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}g_{ij}g_{kl}=0\,.

(121)

Hence, only the scalar invariants of the elasticity tensor contribute to the compression part of the energy. The latter is given as a sum of Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts

E_{c}={}^{(C)}\!E_{c}+{}^{(nC)}\!E_{c}\,.

(122)

The two energy expressions here are determined by the scalars
S
and
A
, respectively. Indeed,

{}^{(C)}\!E_{c}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}S}{18}\,,\qquad{\rm and}\qquad^{(nC)}\!E_{c}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}A}{18}\,.

(123)

The total compression part of the energy is expressed now as

E_{c}=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{18}(S+A)\,.

(124)

Assuming that a material’s hydrodynamic compression can be accomplished physically without shear deformation, this energy expression must be positive for any non-zero
\varepsilon
. Consequently, we have an inequality

S+A>0\,.

(125)

4.3
The mixed part of the energy

Using the decomposition (
11
), we can express the mixed part of the energy (
119
) as a sum of Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts

E_{m}={}^{(C)}\!E_{m}+{}^{(nC)}\!E_{m}=\frac{1}{3}S^{ijkl}\varepsilon g_{ij}u_{kl}+\frac{1}{3}A^{ijkl}\varepsilon g_{ij}u_{kl}\,.

(126)

Substituting here the decomposition (
40
) of the Cauchy part into three rotational invariant pieces we derive

^{(C)}E_{m}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\left({}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}+^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}+^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}\right)

(127)

Since the pure shear
u_{ij}
is traceless, the first term vanishes.
The third term includes a completely traceless part
{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}=0
, thus it likewise vanishes. As a result, all that is left is the contribution of the second component of the elasticity tensor. Explicitly,

{}^{(C)}E_{m}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\,^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\,P^{kl}u_{kl}\,.

(128)

The decomposition of the non-Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor into the rotation invariant terms yields

{}^{(nC)}E_{m}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}\left({}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}+{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}g_{ij}u_{kl}\right)

(129)

The first term in this equation is zero due to the tracelessness of the shear strain
u_{kl}
. As for the second term, we have

{}^{(nC)}E_{m}=-\frac{\varepsilon}{3}Q^{kl}u_{kl}.

(130)

The total mixed energy is expressed as

E_{m}=\frac{\varepsilon}{3}(P^{kl}-Q^{kl})u_{kl}\,.

(131)

Consequently, only two second-order deviators—out of the elasticity tensor’s five irreducible components—

contribute to the mixed energy. The mixed portion of the energy disappears if these two terms are not present. Both isotropic materials and cubic crystals exhibit this behavior. These two cases were discussed in the literature; see
[
37
]
,
[
38
]
. We find that the mixed energy can disappear even in less symmetric materials when the particular relation
P^{kl}=Q^{kl}
between the components is valid.

4.4
The shear part of the energy

The quadratic form of shear strain energy given in (
118
) is decomposed into the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts

E_{s}=^{(C)}\!E_{s}+^{(nC)}\!E_{s}=\frac{1}{2}S^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}+\frac{1}{2}A^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}\,.

(132)

All three irreducible sub-tensors,
{}^{(1)}\,S_{ijkl},{}^{(2)}\,S_{ijkl},
and
{}^{(3)}\,S_{ijkl}
, contribute to the Cauchy portion of the shear energy.
Explicitly, we have the first part

{}^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}=\frac{2S}{15}\,u_{ij}u^{ij}\,,

(133)

the second part

{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}=\frac{4}{7}\,P^{ik}u^{j}{}_{k}u_{ij}\,,

(134)

and the third part

{}^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}=R^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}\,.

(135)

Consequently, the Cauchy part of the shear energy reads

{}^{(C)}\!E_{s}=\frac{S}{15}\,u_{ij}u^{ij}+\frac{2}{7}\,P^{ik}u_{ij}u^{j}{}_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\,R^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}\,.

(136)

The non-Cauchy part of the shear energy also includes contributions of all its irreducible subtensors
{}^{(1)}A_{ijkl},
and
{}^{(2)}A_{ijkl}
.
Respectively,

{}^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}=-\frac{A}{6}\,u_{ij}u^{ij}\,,

(137)

and

{}^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}u_{ij}u_{kl}=2Q^{ik}u^{j}{}_{k}u_{ij}\,.

(138)

As a result, the non-Cauchy part of the shear elasticity energy is expressed as

{}^{(nC)}\!E_{s}=-\frac{A}{12}\,u_{ij}u^{ij}+Q^{ij}u_{jk}u^{k}{}_{i}\,.

(139)

Fig.3 illustrates the elasticity energy decomposition graphically. Take into account that each of the three energy portions is contributed to by both the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts of the elasticity tensor.

are identified.

4.5
Cauchy and non-Cauchy energy contributions

To explicitly characterize the distinction between the Cauchy and non-Cauchy contributions to the energy expression, we consider the most basic case of an isotropic medium.
In this example, the mixed portion
{}^{(m)}E
is not present.
Cauchy and non-Cauchy portions of the compression energy
{}^{(c)}E
are separated as

{}^{(c)}\!E=\frac{S}{18}\varepsilon^{2}+\frac{A}{18}\varepsilon^{2}\,,

(140)

respectively.
The shear energy also decomposed into Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts

{}^{(s)}\!E=\frac{S}{15}(u_{ij})^{2}-\frac{A}{12}\,(u_{ij})^{2}\,.

(141)

We can infer the following facts from these formulas:

•

Since pure shear and compression can both be achieved independently, we demand that the compression and shear components of the energy be positive independently.
As a result of Eqs.(
140
,
141
), the scalar invariant
S
must be strictly positive. This suggests that the Cauchy component is crucial, making pure non-Cauchy materials with
S=0,A\neq 0
impossible.

•

With a positive parameter
A
, the non-Cauchy portion contributes in the opposite direction to the energy expressions, increasing hydrostatic compression energy while decreasing pure shear energy. The contributions are inverted when the parameter
A
is negative. With the use of this property, we are able to physically discriminate between the Cauchy and non-Cauchy components of the elasticity tensor. It is important to note that this interesting property is only disclosed by the irreducible scalar invariants
S
and
A
. When the reducible decomposition with Lamé’s parameters
\lambda,\mu
is applied, it is not visible.

•

Positiveness of the full energy requires

S+A>0,\qquad 4S-5A>0\,.

(142)

Note that these inequalities must hold only for a positive parameter
S
. Consequently, the non-Cauchy scalar
A
is bounded as

0.8S>A>-S

(143)

In term of Poisson’s ratio, this inequality identical to the classical thermodynamics bound
[
23
]

0.5>\nu>-1.

(144)

5
Acoustic waves and Cauchy relations

In this section, we look at how the Cauchy and non-Cauchy components of the elasticity tensor affect acoustic wave propagation properties.

5.1
Acoustic wave equation

The propagation of acoustic waves in anisotropic media is given by a system of three second-order partial differential equations:

{\rho g^{il}\ddot{u_{l}}-C^{ijkl}\,{\partial_{j}\partial_{k}u_{l}}=0\,.}

(145)

Here, the displacement covector
u_{l}=u_{l}(t,x^{i})
is assumed to be a
smooth function of the time coordinate
t
and of the spatial position
x^{i}
of a point. The mass density
\rho
, the elasticity tensor
C^{ijkl}
, and the metric tensor
g^{il}
are treated as constant parameters. Moreover, we use Cartesian coordinates, so the Euclidean metric is presented by the unit matrix
g^{ij}=\text{diag}(1,1,1)
.

We consider a
plane wave solution
of Eq. (
145
), with the
notation as in
[
30
]
:

u_{l}=U_{l}e^{i\left(\zeta n_{j}x^{j}-\omega t\right)}\,.

(146)

Here,
U_{i}
is the amplitude covector,
\zeta
is the wave-number,
n_{j}
is the unit propagation
covector,
\omega
is the angular frequency, and
i^{2}=-1
. In the
plane-wave approximation, all these parameters are assumed to be constant. Then, by substituting (
146
) into (
145
), a system of three homogeneous linear algebraic equations is obtained,

\left(\rho\,\omega^{2}g^{il}-C^{ijkl}\zeta^{2}n_{j}n_{k}\right)U_{l}=0\,.

(147)

It has a non-trivial solution if and only if the characteristic matrix is singular. As a result,

\det\left(\rho\omega^{2}g^{il}-C^{ijkl}\zeta^{2}n_{j}n_{k}\right)=0\,.

(148)

5.2
Christoffel tensor

With the definitions of the
Christoffel tensor

\Gamma^{il}:=\frac{1}{\rho}\,C^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}\,

(149)

and of the phase velocity
v:=\omega/\zeta
,
Eq. (
147
) becomes an ordinary eigenvalue system

\left(v^{2}g^{il}-\Gamma^{il}\right)U_{l}=0\,,

(150)

whereas the characteristic equation (
148
) reads

\det\left(v^{2}g^{il}-\Gamma^{il}\right)=0\,.

(151)

Due to the minor and major symmetries of the elasticity tensor (
2
,
3
), the Christoffel tensor turns out to be symmetric

{\Gamma^{ij}=\Gamma^{ji}\,.}

(152)

Consequently, the eigenvalues
v_{1}^{2},v_{2}^{2},v_{3}^{2}
are real. Furthermore, these eigenvalues must be positive, implying that the symmetric matrix
\Gamma^{ij}
must be positive-definite.

Due to the dependence of the matrix
\Gamma^{ij}
on
n_{i}
, each positive real solution
v_{i}
corresponds to an acoustic wave propagating in the direction of the wave covector
n_{i}
. Thus, in general, for a given propagation covector
{\mathbf{n}}=(n_{1},n_{2},n_{3})
, three different acoustic waves are possible. The relations below are worth observing:

v_{1}^{2}+v_{2}^{2}+v_{3}^{2}={\rm tr}(\Gamma^{ij}),

(153)

and

v_{1}v_{2}v_{3}=\sqrt{{\rm det}(\Gamma^{ij})}.

(154)

The cases with
zero solutions (null modes) and negative solutions (standing waves)
are classified as nonphysical since they do not satisfy the standard causality requirements. As a result, non-degenerate wave propagation is only possible in the directions
{\mathbf{n}}
that meet the relations

{\rm tr}\left(\Gamma^{ij}({\mathbf{n}})\right)>0,\qquad{\rm det}\left(\Gamma^{ij}({\mathbf{n}})\right)>0.

(155)

5.3
S_{4}
-decomposition of Christoffel’s tensor

Christoffel’s tensor can be separated into Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts since it is linear in the elasticity tensor.
Substituting the irreducible
S\!A
-decomposition into
(
149
), we define

\Gamma^{il}={\cal S}^{il}+{\cal A}^{il}\,,

(156)

where

{\cal S}^{il}:=\frac{1}{\rho}\,S^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}\,,\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\mathcal{A}}^{il}:=\frac{1}{\rho}\,A^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}\,.

(157)

These two tensors are symmetric due to the fundamental symmetries of the elasticity tensor,

{\cal S}^{il}={\cal S}^{li},\qquad{\rm and}\qquad{\cal A}^{il}={\cal A}^{li}.

(158)

They correspond to the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts of the elasticity tensor. Therefore, we shall refer to
{\cal S}^{ij}
as the
Cauchy Christoffel tensor
and to
{\cal A}^{ij}
as the
non-Cauchy Christoffel tensor.

Substituting (
12
) and
(
13
) into (
157
), we find the explicit expressions

{\cal S}^{il}=\frac{1}{3\rho}(C^{ijkl}+C^{iklj}+C^{iljk})n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{1}{3\rho}(2C^{ijkl}+C^{iljk})n_{j}n_{k}\,

(159)

and

{\cal A}^{il}=\frac{1}{3\rho}\left(2C^{ijkl}-C^{iklj}-C^{ilkj}\right)n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{1}{3\rho}\left(C^{ijkl}-C^{ilkj}\right)n_{j}n_{k}\,.

(160)

We can learn something valuable from the latter expression.
The non-Cauchy part of the Christoffel tensor satisfies the relation

{\cal A}^{il}n_{l}=0\,.

(161)

Indeed, if we contract the covector
n_{l}
with
{\cal A}^{il}
, we obtain using the symmetry relation (
16
)

{\cal A}^{il}n_{l}=A^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}=A^{i(jkl)}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}=0\,.

(162)

Eq. (
161
) can be viewed as a linear relation between the rows of the matrix
{\mathcal{A}}^{il}
.
Consequently, this matrix is singular. Thus we derive that the determinant of the non-Cauchy Christoffel tensor is equal to zero,

\det\,{\cal A}^{ij}=0\,.

(163)

This relation demonstrates that a medium with only a non-Cauchy part
{\cal A}^{ij}
is impossible since the second inequality in (
155
) yields zero propagation velocity. We come to the conclusion that wave propagation in any elastic material requires the Cauchy portion of the Christoffel tensor
{\cal S}^{ij}
to be non-zero.

5.4
SO(3)
-decomposition of Christoffel’s tensor

The invariance of the Christoffel tensor under the action of the rotation group
SO(3)
allows for its successive decomposition.
Let us first compute the Christoffel tensor contributions of the three irreducible parts that compose the fully symmetric tensor
S^{ijkl}
.
For the scalar part (
36
), we have

{}^{(1)}\!{\cal S}^{il}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,^{(1)}\!S^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{S}{15\rho}(g^{il}+2n^{i}n^{l}).

(164)

The trace of this tensor is positive

{}^{(1)}\!{\cal S}^{il}g_{il}=\frac{S}{3\rho}

(165)

Applying (
37
), the second part
{}^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}
is given as

{}^{(2)}\!{\cal S}^{il}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,^{(2)}\!S^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{1}{7\rho}\left(P^{il}+g^{il}P^{jk}n_{j}n_{k}+2P^{il}n_{j}n^{l}+2P^{lj}n_{j}n^{i}\right)

(166)

with the trace

{}^{(2)}\!{\cal S}^{il}g_{il}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,P^{ij}n_{i}n_{j}\,.

(167)

The third part contributes into the Christoffel tensor as

{}^{(3)}\!{\cal S}^{il}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,^{(3)}\!S^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{1}{\rho}R^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}\,.

(168)

Due to the traceless properties of
R^{ijkl}
, the trace of this tensor is zero

{}^{(3)}\!{\cal S}^{il}g_{il}=R^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}g_{il}=0\,.

(169)

As a result, the Cauchy part of the Christoffel tensor is presented as

{\cal{S}}^{il}=\frac{S}{15\rho}(g^{il}+2n^{i}n^{l})+\frac{1}{7\rho}\left(2P^{ij}n_{j}n^{l}+2P^{lj}n_{j}n^{i}+P^{il}+P^{jk}n_{j}n_{k}g^{il}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho}R^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}\,.

(170)

The trace of this expression is expressed as

{\rm tr}({\cal{S}}^{ij})=\frac{S}{3\rho}+\frac{1}{\rho}\,P^{ij}n_{i}n_{j}.

(171)

It is worth noting that the tensor
R^{ijkl}
(of 9 independent components of the elasticity tensor) does not contribute to the latter expression.

Let us compute now the non-Cauchy contributions into Christoffel’s tensor. The scalar part contribution is given by applying (
46
)

{}^{(1)}\!{\cal A}^{il}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,^{(1)}\!A^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{A}{12\rho}\left(n^{i}n^{l}-g^{il}\right)

(172)

with the trace

{}^{(1)}\!{\cal A}^{il}g_{il}=-\frac{A}{6\rho}.

(173)

This contribution is positive for the
A^{-}
-materials and negative for the
A^{+}
-materials.

The contribution of the second non-Cauchy part can be calculated using (
51
). We have

{}^{(2)}\!{\cal A}^{il}=\frac{1}{\rho}\,^{(2)}\!A^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}=\frac{1}{2\rho}\left(Q^{il}+g^{il}Q^{jk}n_{j}n_{k}-Q^{ik}n^{l}n_{k}-Q^{lk}n^{i}n_{k}\right)

(174)

with the trace

{}^{(2)}\!{\cal A}^{il}g_{il}=\frac{1}{2\rho}Q^{ij}n_{i}n_{j}\,.

(175)

Consequently, the non-Cauchy contribution to Christoffel’s tensor takes the form

{\cal A}^{il}=\frac{A}{12\rho}\left(n^{i}n^{l}-g^{il}\right)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\left(Q^{il}+g^{il}Q^{jk}n_{j}n_{k}-Q^{ik}n^{l}n_{k}-Q^{lk}n^{i}n_{k}\right)

(176)

with the trace

{\rm tr}({\cal A}^{il})=-\frac{A}{6\rho}+\frac{1}{2\rho}Q^{ij}n_{i}n_{j}\,.

(177)

Finaly, Christoffel’s tensor is represented in terms of the irreducible parts of the elasticity tensor as

\displaystyle\Gamma^{il}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{S}{15\rho}(g^{il}+2n^{i}n^{l})+\frac{1}{7\rho}\left(2P^{ij}n_{j}n^{l}+2P^{lj}n_{j}n^{i}+P^{il}+P^{jk}n_{j}n_{k}g^{il}\right)+\frac{1}{\rho}R^{ijkl}n_{j}n_{k}+

(178)

\displaystyle\frac{A}{12\rho}\left(n^{i}n^{l}-g^{il}\right)+\frac{1}{2\rho}\left(Q^{il}+g^{il}Q^{jk}n_{j}n_{k}-Q^{ik}n^{l}n_{k}-Q^{lk}n^{i}n_{k}\right).

The trace of this expression is given by

{\rm tr}(\Gamma^{ij})=g_{ij}\Gamma^{ij}=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\,\left(2P^{ij}+Q^{ij}\right)n_{i}n_{j}.

(179)

5.5
Acoustic velocities

Let us consider certain consequences of the Christoffel’s tensor represented in terms of the irreducible parts of the elasticity tensor.

5.5.1
Sum of squared velocities

The second coefficient of the characteristic equation that is represented by the trace of the matrix
{\rm tr}(\Gamma^{il})={\rm tr}({\cal S}^{il}+{\cal A}^{il})
is equal to the sum of the eigenvalues.
Thus, using (
153
) we have for a given propagation direction
{\mathbf{n}}
the sum of three squared velocities

\sum_{a=1}^{3}v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}})=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\,(2P^{ij}+Q^{ij})n_{i}n_{j}

(180)

The preceding expression takes a much simpler form in the special case

2P^{ij}+Q^{ij}=0.

(181)

The sum of the squared velocities of three separate waves propagating in the direction
{\mathbf{n}}
is then
independent
of the propagation vector
{\mathbf{n}}

\sum_{a=1}^{3}v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}})=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}\,.

(182)

Using the bound (
143
), we obtain that in this special case

0.2\,\frac{S}{\rho}<\sum_{a=1}^{3}v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}})<0.5\,\frac{S}{\rho}\,.

(183)

Thus the velocities of three acoustic waves are bounded by the Cauchy scalar invariant
S
only. Once more, we can observe that this invariant has to be strictly positive
S>0
.

Notice that only five linear constraints between the 21 independent components of
C^{ijkl}
are required in Eq. (
181
).
As a result, a large class of elasticity tensors with 16 arbitrary components can exhibit this essential property (
182
).
In particular, this case is realized for cubic crystals and isotropic materials.

5.5.2
Critical directions for the sum of squared velocities

The sum of squared velocities in anisotropic media generally depends on the propagation vector
{\mathbf{n}}
.
Let us look for the directions where this sum achieves its minimum and maximum values. We will refer to these directions as
critical directions
.
Let us write the function given in the right-hand side of (
180
) as

K({\mathbf{n}})=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}+\frac{1}{2\rho}\,L^{ij}n_{i}n_{j},

(184)

where
L^{ij}=2P^{ij}+Q^{ij}
.
The critical values of this expression on the sphere
|{\mathbf{n}}|=1
are given by the characterictic equation

L^{ij}n_{j}=\lambda n^{i},

(185)

where
\lambda
is Lagrangian’s multiplier. As a result, the critical directions
{\mathbf{n}}
are the eigenvector of the matrix
L^{ij}
, and the parameter
\lambda
is the eigenvalue of this matrix.

5.5.3
Sum of squared velocities for three orthogonal direction

Let us assume that the three orthonormal vectors
{\mathbf{n}},{\mathbf{m}},{\mathbf{k}}
represent three separate propagation directions.
The components of these vectors are connected by a well-known relation

n_{i}n_{j}+m_{i}m_{j}+k_{i}k_{j}=g_{ij}.

(186)

Using three copies of equation (
180
) for three orthogonal directions we obtain the well-known invariant expression, see e.g.
[
1
]
, that in our notations reads

\sum_{a=1}^{3}\big(v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}})+v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{m}})+v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{k}})\big)=\frac{2S-A}{2\rho}\,.

(187)

Thus, for every elastic material, the sum of the squared norms of the three propagation velocities in every three orthogonal directions depends only on the scalar invariants of the elasticity tensor. In particular, it is independent of the propagation vectors. This amount ranges from
0.6S/\rho
to
1.5S/\rho
.

5.5.4
Acoustic velocities in isotropic media and cubic system

It is instructive to apply the formulas for the Christoffel tensor in the isotropic material. Three tensors,
P^{ij}
,
Q^{ij}
, and
R^{ijkl}
are disappearing in this instance.
The Christoffel tensor then takes the form

\Gamma^{ij}=\frac{4S-5A}{60\rho}g^{ij}+\frac{8S+5A}{60\rho}n^{i}n^{j}\,.

(188)

As a result, characteristic equation (
147
) becomes

{\det}\left(\left(v^{2}-\frac{4S-5A}{60\rho}\right)g^{ij}-\frac{8S+5A}{60\rho}n^{i}n^{j}\right)=0.

(189)

Instead of directly solving this cubic equation with respect to velocity
v^{2}
, we can deduce the velocities using simple algebraic reasons.
We observe that this equation can be rewritten as the standard characteristic equation,
{\det}\left(\lambda g_{ij}-n_{i}n_{j}\right)=0
. Since the matrix
n_{i}n_{j}
has a rank of one this equation has two zero eigenvalues. There are therefore two equal velocities,

v_{1}^{2}=v_{2}^{2}=\frac{4S-5A}{60\rho}.

(190)

The third velocity is derived from (
187
) as

v_{3}^{2}=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}-2v_{1}^{2}=\frac{S}{5\rho}.

(191)

In term of the Lamé moduli
\lambda
and
\mu
, we come back to the well-known textbook’s expressions

v_{1}^{2}=v_{2}^{2}=\frac{\mu}{\rho},\qquad v_{3}^{2}=\frac{\lambda+2\mu}{\rho}.

(192)

The velocity
v_{3}
in the isotropic case therefore solely depends on the Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor. The possibility to generalize this fact to a more general anisotropic case will be discussed in the following section.

The tensors
P^{ij}
and
Q^{ij}
disappear in cubic system crystals, while
R^{ijkl}
only has one independent component. Now, the Christoffel tensor is dependent on the propagation vector as

\Gamma^{ij}=\frac{4S-5A}{60\rho}g^{ij}+\frac{8S+5A}{60\rho}n^{i}n^{j}+R^{ijkl}n_{k}n_{l}.

(193)

The trace of this expression gives exactly the same sum of squared velocities as in the isotropic case.

5.5.5
Critical directions for some crystal systems

In trigonal, tetragonal, and transverse isotropy systems, the tensors
P^{ij}
and
Q^{ij}
can be represented simultaneously in the form proportional to the matrix
{\rm diag}(1,1,-2)
; see
[
20
]
for explicit formulae.
Consequently, the matrix
L^{ij}
appearing in (
184
) is given by

L^{ij}=(2P+Q){\rm diag}(1,1,-2).

(194)

The explicit expressions for the scalars
P,Q
can be find in
[
20
]
. Then the eigenvalues are
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=1
while
\lambda_{3}=-2
. The standard axes serve as the eigenvectors.
Then in two orthogonal directions
{\mathbf{n}_{1}}=(1,0,0)
and
{\mathbf{n}_{2}}=(0,1,0)
(corresponding to the eigenvalue
\lambda=1
), the sum of squared velocities takes the value

\sum_{a=1}^{3}v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}_{1}})=\sum_{a=1}^{3}v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}_{2}})=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}+\frac{1}{2\rho}(2P+Q)

(195)

while in the third direction
{\mathbf{n}_{3}}=(0,0,1)
(corresponding to the the eigenvalue
\lambda=-2
) this sum is given by

\sum_{a=1}^{3}v_{a}^{2}({\mathbf{n}_{3}})=\frac{2S-A}{6\rho}-\frac{1}{\rho}(2P+Q).

(196)

We can derive from these equations a bound for the scalars
P
and
Q

\frac{2S-A}{6}>2P+Q>-\frac{2S-A}{3}.

(197)

Using the energy bound (
143
) we have

0.5S>2P+Q>-S.

(198)

Although these inequalities are only derived in a special case, they highlight an essential fact: the tensor parts of the elasticity tensor are not fully arbitrary.
They are constrained by the scalar part parameters.

5.6
Polarization of acoustic waves

The propagation of acoustic waves in a homogeneous anisotropic elastic media is represented by equation (
150
).
The three eigenvalues of this eigenvector problem indicate the velocities of three distinct acoustic waves.
In general, three distinct real positive eigenvalues correspond to three distinct eigenvectors called
acoustic polarizations
:
{}^{(1)}\mathbf{U}
,
{}^{(2)}\mathbf{U}
, and
{}^{(3)}\mathbf{U}
.

For isotropic materials, the three polarization vectors
{}^{(i)}\mathbf{U}
are strongly correlated with the propagation vector
\mathbf{n}
.
One
longitudinal or compression wave
exhibits polarization along the propagation vector i.e.,

\mathbf{U}\times\mathbf{n}=0\,.

(199)

Two additional
transverse (or shear) waves
have polarizations perpendicular to the propagation direction, i.e.,

\mathbf{U}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\,.

(200)

These three
pure polarizations
can be found in any direction of the vector
\mathbf{n}
.

In anisotropic material, three pure modes only arise in specific directions of
\mathbf{n}
. It is critical to identify these pure modes and the wave covector directions that correspond to them. Let us look at how the irreducible decomposition of the elasticity tensor can be utilized in this context.

5.6.1
Pure longitudinal wave

Let in a given direction
\mathbf{n}
the polarization be pure longitudinal, i.e., the polarization vector is proportional to the wave covector,
{}^{(1)}\mathbf{U}\sim\mathbf{n}
.
Two additional waves must have polarization vectors
{}^{(2)}\mathbf{U}
and
{}^{(3)}\mathbf{U}
that are normal to the wave covector
\mathbf{n}
, i.e. they are pure transverse.
For the pure longitudinal wave, we can replace
{}^{(1)}\mathbf{U}
with
\mathbf{n}
. Thus, Eq. (
150
) becomes

\Gamma^{ij}n_{j}=v_{L}^{2}g^{ij}n_{j}\,.

(201)

By substituting the decomposition of the Christoffel tensor into Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts, we obtain

{\cal S}^{ij}n_{j}+{\cal A}^{ij}n_{j}=v_{L}^{2}g^{ij}n_{j}\,.

(202)

Because of Eq. (
161
), the second term on the left-hand side is omitted, leaving us with

{\cal S}^{ij}n_{j}=v_{L}^{2}g^{ij}n_{j}\,.

(203)

The velocity
v_{\rm{L}}
of the pure longitudinal wave in the direction of
\mathbf{n}
is then determined solely by the Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor:

v^{2}_{L}={{\cal S}^{ij}n_{j}n_{i}}\,.

(204)

By substituting this expression into Eq. (
203
), we obtain

{\cal S}^{ij}n_{j}=\left({{\cal S}^{rs}n_{r}n_{s}}\right)n^{i}.

(205)

Being a vector equation for a single vector variable, it allows for the computation of pure longitudinal wave directions
{\mathbf{n}}
in a material with a specified elasticity tensor. This direction is then independent of the non-Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor. Furthermore, because two extra pure shear waves are always normal to a pure longitudinal wave, we can regard Eq. (
205
) as a
condition for the existence of three pure waves
in a certain direction of
\mathbf{n}
.

In isotropic media, the condition (
205
) is trivially satisfied for any arbitrary
\mathbf{n}
. Moreover, according to this equation, even some anisotropic materials can have three pure polarizations in any direction. For this, the elasticity tensor must have an isotropic Cauchy part, whereas the non-Cauchy part might be arbitrary.

Let us explicitly express the longitudinal velocity in terms of elasticity tensor components. By substituting (
170
) in (
204
), we obtain

v^{2}_{\rm{L}}={\frac{1}{\rho}\left({\frac{S}{15}+\frac{6}{7}P^{ij}n_{i}n_{j}+R^{ijkl}n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}}\right)}\,.

(206)

Consequently, for the two pure shear velocities we have

v_{S1}^{2}+v_{S2}^{2}=\frac{1}{\rho}\left(\frac{8S-5A}{30}+\frac{1}{14}\,(2P^{ij}+7Q^{ij})n_{i}n_{j}-R^{ijkl}n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}\right).

(207)

Two shear velocities are equal one to another in the case of an acoustic axes
[
31
]
,

v_{S1}=v_{S2}
. In this case, the shear velocity is given by

v_{S}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\rho}\left(\frac{8S-5A}{30}+\frac{1}{14}\,(2P^{ij}+7Q^{ij})n_{i}n_{j}-R^{ijkl}n_{i}n_{j}n_{k}n_{l}\right).

(208)

Hence, the shear velocities are affected by the Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts of the elasticity tensor.

5.7
Pure transverse wave

Consider a more general case whereby a pure transverse wave
{\mathbf{U}}
propagates in a certain direction
{\mathbf{n}}
.
Then,
{\mathbf{U}}\cdot{\mathbf{n}}=0
. Two more waves are normal to
{\mathbf{U}}
but can have any angle with the vector
{\mathbf{n}}
.
When we multiply both sides of the equation

\Gamma^{ij}U_{j}=v^{2}_{S}g^{ij}U_{j}

(209)

by the vector
{\mathbf{n}}
, we obtain

{\cal S}^{ij}U_{j}n_{i}=0.

(210)

where the relation
\Gamma^{ij}n_{i}={\cal S}^{ij}n_{i}
is applied.
Thus, we are dealing with a system of two scalar equations

{\cal S}^{ij}n_{i}U_{j}=0\qquad{\rm and}\qquad g^{ij}n_{i}U_{j}=0

(211)

for two independent components of the shear polarization vector
{\mathbf{U}}
.
We suppose that they are not pure longitudinal waves, i.e.,
\Gamma^{ij}n_{i}\neq Cg^{ij}n_{i}
.
The shear polarization
{\mathbf{U}}
is then uniquely determined by our system of equations. To resolve the system (
211
) explicitly, we note that this system can be interpreted as the fact that the vector
{\mathbf{U}}
is orthogonal to two given vectors,
{\cal S}^{ij}n_{i}
and
g^{ij}n_{i}
. As a result, the pure shear polarization vector can be expressed as a vector product

U_{i}=U_{0}\varepsilon_{ijk}n^{j}{\cal S}^{kl}n_{l}\,,

(212)

In addition to the explicit expression, we discover that the vector
{\mathbf{U}}
is independent of the non-Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor when only one (shear) polarization appears.

With the polarization vector in hand, we can compute the
shear velocity
.
Multiplying both sides of Eq. (
209
) by
U_{j}
, we obtain

v_{S}^{2}=\frac{\Gamma^{ij}U_{i}U_{j}}{|U|^{2}}.

(213)

This quantity is determined by the full Christoffel’s tensor.

Substituting (
212
) into (
209
), we derive

\varepsilon_{imk}\left(\Gamma^{ij}-v_{S}^{2}g^{ij}\right){\cal S}^{kl}n^{m}n_{l}=0\,.

(214)

There is only one independent variable in this system,
{\mathbf{n}}
. As a result, it can be regarded as a
condition for the presence of the pure shear wave
in the direction
{\mathbf{n}}
.

Let us provide an explicit example. Consider the propagation vector to be directed in the
z
-axis,
{\mathbf{n}}=(0,0,1)
and the metric tensor
g_{ij}={\rm diag}(1,1,1)
.
Then Eq. (
212
) yields

U_{i}=U_{0}\left(S_{13},S_{23},0\right).

(215)

The shear velocity (
213
) is then given as

v_{S}^{2}=\frac{\Gamma_{11}S_{13}^{2}+2\Gamma_{12}S_{13}S_{23}+\Gamma_{22}S_{23}^{2}}{S_{13}^{2}+S_{23}^{2}}.

(216)

The shear conditions (
214
) are presented as a system

\displaystyle\Gamma_{11}S_{23}-\Gamma_{12}S_{13}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}_{S}S_{23}

\displaystyle\Gamma_{22}S_{13}-\Gamma_{12}S_{23}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle v^{2}_{S}S_{13}

\displaystyle A_{13}S_{23}-A_{23}S_{13}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0\,.

(217)

6
Conclusion

The Cauchy relations of linear elasticity are examined from a pure algebraic perspective.

•

We demonstrate that the Cauchy relations are related to the permutation group symmetry of the elasticity tensor. The linear space of the elasticity tensor is then uniquely split into Cauchy and non-Cauchy subspaces.

•

The Cauchy relations are expressed as the condition for nullification of the 6-dimensional non-Cauchy subspace of the elasticity tensor space.

•

We argue that almost arbitrary invariant linear relation between elasticity tensor components is equivalent to the system Cauchy relations.

•

We propose identifying Cauchy and non-Cauchy parts of the elasticity tensor since natural materials do not exactly meet Cauchy relations.

•

The Cauchy and non-Cauchy subspaces are separated into smaller invariant subspaces by successive
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
-group decomposition.

•

The
SO(3,\mathbb{R})
-decomposition of the non-Cauchy subspace allows for the definition of partial Cauchy relations of five independent conditions. Unlike the full Cauchy conditions, these partial conditions hold for isotropic materials as well as cubic system crystals.

•

We define two types of materials: the
A^{+}
-materials and the
A^{-}
-materials.

We analyze the physical consequences of the Cauchy relations in the following aspects:

•

The deviation of natural materials from the ideal Cauchy model is described in an invariant form. We also provide some examples of the
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
materials.

•

The decomposition of Hooke’s law enables the identification of the contribution of various components of the elasticity tensor. We find that the
A^{+}
and
A^{-}
materials behave differently.

•

We analyze the partitioning of elasticity energy into compression, shear, and mixed portions. The contributions of the invariant sub-tensors of the elasticity tensor to distinct energy expressions are detected.

•

We derive some constraints on the invariant elasticity parameters from the positiveness of the energy.

•

We present a unique decomposition of the acoustic Christoffel tensor into Cauchy and non-Cauchy portions for acoustic wave propagation.

•

The contributions of the irreducible elements of the elasticity tensor to the velocities and propagation vector of pure polarized acoustic waves are identified. The pure longitudinal velocity is demonstrated to be only reliant on the Cauchy part of the elasticity tensor. When there is only one shear wave, its polarization vector is independent of the non-Cauchy part.
