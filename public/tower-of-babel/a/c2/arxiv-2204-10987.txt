Koopman-based Policy Iteration for Robust Optimal Control
Alexander Krolicki, Sarang Sutavani, Umesh Vaidya

Abstract
Classically, the optimal control problem in the presence of an adversary is formulated as a two-player zero-sum differential game or an H ∞ H_{\infty} control problem. The solution to these problems can be obtained by solving the Hamilton-Jacobi-Issac equation (HJIE). We provide a novel Koopman-based expression of the HJIE, where the solutions can be obtained through the approximation of the Koopman operator itself. In particular, we developed a data-driven and model based policy iteration algorithm for approximating the optimal value function using a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator and generator.



I
Introduction

The control of complex dynamical systems in applications such as vehicle autonomy, robotics, and advanced manufacturing involves interactions of system dynamics with the environment. There are two common approaches to account for these interactions: robust control formulation using
H_{\infty}
optimal control or control with adversary using two-player zero-sum differential games
[
1
]
. These two formulations are intimately connected and essentially reduce to solving the HJIE
[
2
]
. The HJIE is a nonlinear partial differential equation (PDE) that is difficult to solve for nonlinear dynamical systems. Numerical methods are often used for the approximate solution to the HJIE. In this paper, we provide a novel Koopman-based perspective to the HJIE. The Koopman operator provides a linear lifting of a nonlinear system in the space of observables or functions
[
3
]
. The linearity of the Koopman operator provides a powerful tool in the development of data-driven analysis and synthesis methods for nonlinear systems
[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
12
]
.

Given the significance of
H_{\infty}
optimal control and two-player zero-sum differential game problems, there is extensive literature on the computational aspects of this problem.

Many of the algorithms that we consider require an initial stabilizing controller and follow a nested loop structure
[
13
,
14
,
15
]
. The inner loop iterates to find the worst adversary or disturbance, given a controller. The outer loop works to find the best response to the adversary; for more details, we refer the reader to
[
13
]
. Our algorithm will utilize the same architecture. In contrast to nested loops, the algorithm in
[
16
]
computes the adversary and control updates simultaneously. Other techniques for solving the HJIE are adapted from works developed to solve the Hamilton-Jacobi-Bellman equation (HJBE). Recent works have adapted the Kleinman algorithm
[
17
]
by converting the problem of solving a Riccati equation, with a sign indefinite quadratic term, into one of generating successive iterations of solutions of LQ-type Riccati equations, each with negative semidefinite quadratic terms
[
18
]
. These results were extended for solving the HJIE with guarantees on the local convergence rate and no requirements for an initial admissible control
[
19
]
. These works and others can benefit from the Koopman-based HJIE as we provide new insights into solutions for these equations.

The main contribution of this paper is a Koopman-based derivation of the HJIE. In this paper, we provide model-based and data-driven algorithms for the approximate solution of the HJIE. Given the intimate connection between the HJIE and
H_{\infty}
optimal control, our proposed Koopman-based computational framework will solve either of these problems.

The paper is organized as follows. Preliminaries on the Koopman operator and its data-driven approximation are presented in Section II. Results involving the connection between the HJIE and the Koopman operator are presented in Section III. Model-based and data-driven algorithms for obtaining iterative solutions of the HJIE based on the approximation of the Koopman operator are presented in Section IV. Simulation results of the developed framework on nonlinear system examples are presented in Section V, followed by conclusions in Section VI.

Notation:

II-A

Koopman Operators and Generators

Consider a dynamical system

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\bf f}({\mathbf{x}}),\;\;{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbf{X}}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n},

(1)

where the vector field is assumed to be
{\bf f}({\mathbf{x}})\in{\cal C}^{1}({\mathbf{X}},{\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. The nonlinear dynamics in the state space can be lifted to infinite dimension space of functions using the Koopman operator defined as follows.

The Koopman operator
\mathbb{U}_{t}:{\cal L}_{\infty}({\mathbf{X}})\to{\cal L}_{\infty}({\mathbf{X}})
for dynamical system (
1
) is defined as

[\mathbb{U}_{t}\varphi]({\mathbf{x}})=\varphi({\mathbf{s}}_{t}({\mathbf{x}})),\;\;\varphi\in{\cal L}_{\infty}.

The infinitesimal generator for the Koopman operator

\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\mathbb{U}_{t}\varphi-\varphi}{t}={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\cdot\nabla\varphi({\mathbf{x}})=:{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}}\varphi.

(2)

Since
{\mathbb{U}}_{t}
is semi-group with generator
{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}}
it satisfies

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi={\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}}{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi.

(3)

Next we describe algorithms for the finite dimensional approximation of Koopman operator.

II-B

Data-driven Approximation of Koopman Operator

Extended dynamic mode decomposition (EDMD) method is one of the popular algorithms for the data-driven approximation of the Koopman operator
[
20
]
. The basic idea of the algorithm can be explained as follows. For the continuous-time dynamical system (
1
), consider snapshots of time-series data from single or multiple trajectories

\displaystyle{\mathcal{X}}=[{\mathbf{x}}_{1},{\mathbf{x}}_{2},\ldots,{\mathbf{x}}_{M}],\;\;\;\;{\cal Y}=[\mathbf{y}_{1},\mathbf{y}_{2},\ldots,\mathbf{y}_{M}],

(4)

where
{\mathbf{x}}_{i}\in{\mathbf{X}}
and
\mathbf{y}_{i}\in{\mathbf{X}}
. The pair of data sets are assumed to be two consecutive snapshots i.e.,
\mathbf{y}_{i}={\mathbf{s}}_{\Delta t}({\mathbf{x}}_{i})
, where
{\mathbf{s}}_{\Delta t}
is solution of (
1
) with
\Delta t
the discretization time-step. Let
{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}=[\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N}]^{\top}
be the choice of basis functions.
The EDMD algorithm provides a finite-dimensional approximation of the Koopman operator as the solution of the following least square problem
\min\limits_{\bf K}\parallel{\bf G}{\bf K}-{\bf A}\parallel_{F}^{2}
.
With
{\bf K},{\bf G},{\bf A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
,
\|\cdot\|_{F}
is the Frobenius norm. The matrices are computed from data
{\bf G}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({{\mathbf{x}}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({{\mathbf{x}}}_{m})^{\top}
and
{\bf A}=\frac{1}{M}\sum_{m=1}^{M}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({{\mathbf{x}}}_{m}){\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\mathbf{y}}_{m})^{\top}
.
The solution to the least square problem is

\displaystyle{\mathbb{U}}_{\Delta t}\approx{\bf K}_{edmd}=\bf{G}^{\dagger}\bf{A}.

(5)

Where
\dagger
stands for pseudo-inverse. The convergence of EDMD towards the true Koopman operator as the number of data points and basis functions go to infinity are provided in
[
21
,
22
]
. The EDMD-based approximation of the Koopman operator can be used to approximate the Koopman generator as follows,

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}}\approx\frac{{\mathbf{K}}-I}{\Delta t}=:{\mathbf{L}}.

(6)

III
Koopman and Hamilton-Jacobi Equations

There are different ways in which control problems in the presence of an adversary can be formulated.
In particular, the adversary can be viewed as a passive disturbance as in the
H_{\infty}
problem, or it can be viewed as an active player as in the two-player zero-sum differential game problem. In this paper, we use the latter approach. Consider an affine in control and disturbance system of the form

\displaystyle\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})u(t)+{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\omega(t),

(7)

where
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
,
u\in{\mathbb{R}}
, and
\omega\in{\mathbb{R}}
are the state, control input, and adversarial input respectively. For the simplicity of presentation, we restrict the discussion to the case of scalar control and adversary inputs. We make the following assumptions on the vector fields.

Assumption 1

We assume that the vector fields
{\mathbf{f}},{\mathbf{g}}
, and
{\mathbf{h}}
are
{\cal C}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and that
{\mathbf{f}}(0)=0
so that the origin is the equilibrium point of the system in the absence of control and adversarial inputs.

In the two-player zero-sum differential game, we proceed with the following performance index.

\begin{split}J({\mathbf{x}},u,w)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(q({\mathbf{x}})+ru^{2}(t)-\gamma^{2}\omega^{2}(t)\right)dt,\end{split}

(8)

where
q({\mathbf{x}})\geq 0
is assumed to be a state cost with
q({\mathbf{x}})\in{\cal C}^{1}({\mathbb{R}})
and
\gamma,r
are positive constants. The two-player zero-sum differential game is defined as the following min-max optimization problem

\displaystyle\min_{u[0,\infty)}\max_{w[0,\infty)}J({\mathbf{x}},u,w),

(9)

where the objective of the control and adversarial inputs is to minimize and maximize the performance, respectively.
The game yields a unique solution
V^{\star}
(known as the value of the game) if a saddle point
(u^{\star},w^{\star})
exists that satisfies the following condition,
V^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})=\min_{u}\max_{w}J({\mathbf{x}},u,w)=\max_{w}\min_{u}J({\mathbf{x}},u,w)
.
This saddle point follows the so called Nash equilibrium condition, namely

J({\mathbf{x}},u^{\star},w)\leq J\left({\mathbf{x}},u^{\star},w^{\star}\right)\leq J\left({\mathbf{x}},u,w^{\star}\right)
.
It is known that the optimal value function
V^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})
is obtained from the solution of the HJIE

\displaystyle 0=q+{\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})\cdot\nabla V-\frac{r^{-1}}{4}\nabla V^{\top}{\mathbf{g}}(x){\mathbf{g}}^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\nabla V

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{4\gamma^{2}}\nabla V^{\top}{\mathbf{h}}(x){\mathbf{h}}^{\top}({\mathbf{x}})\nabla V,\quad V(0)=0

(10)

and, the optimal control and adversarial input obtained from
V^{\star}
is as follows:

u({\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{1}{2}r^{-1}{\mathbf{g}}\cdot\nabla V^{\star},\ w({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}{\mathbf{h}}\cdot\nabla V^{\star}.

(11)

The minimum positive semi-definite (PSD) solution gives the value of the game or the Nash value. It has been shown that a unique minimum PSD solution exists for all
\gamma\geq\bar{\gamma}
, where the
\bar{\gamma}
corresponds to the solution of the
H_{\infty}
control problem. For more details on this connection between the differential games and the
H_{\infty}
problem, refer to
[
2
]
.

In the following, we derive the HJIE using the Koopman theory. This result is novel and will lay the foundation for the further development of Koopman-based computational frameworks. Consider the min-max performance index subject to the constraints of system dynamics as follows

\displaystyle\min_{u}\max_{w}J({\mathbf{x}},u,w)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\;\;\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})u(t)+{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\omega(t),

(12)

where
J
is defined in (
8
). We make following assumption on the min-max problem (
12
).

Assumption 2

We assume that the solution to the min-max problem (
12
) for the control and adversarial inputs is feedback in nature i.e.,
u=k({\mathbf{x}})\in{\cal C}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
and
w=\ell({\mathbf{x}})\in{{\cal C}}^{1}({\mathbb{R}}^{n})
. Furthermore,
\gamma
is assumed to be larger than
\bar{\gamma}
so that the optimal value function,
V^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})\geq 0
, is finite for any finite value of
{\mathbf{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.

Following Assumption
2
, we can write

\displaystyle V^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})=\min_{k}\max_{\ell}J({\mathbf{x}},k,\ell)

\displaystyle{\rm s.t.}\;\;\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})k({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\ell({\mathbf{x}}).

(13)

The above assumption could be restrictive, and in fact, the HJIE for solving the min-max problem is derived under a less restrictive assumption
[
2
]
. For example, the assumption rules out the possibility for the existence of viscosity based solution of the HJIE
[
23
]
. Essentially, the viscosity-based solutions allow for a continuous function to be defined as a unique solution of the HJIE.

Theorem 1

For the min-max optimization problem (
12
) satisfying Assumption
2
and system dynamics Assumption
1
. The optimal cost function
V^{\star}
can be obtained as the solution of following equation

\begin{split}&{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}k+{\mathbf{h}}\ell}V^{\star}=-q-r(k^{\star})^{2}+\gamma^{2}(\ell^{\star})^{2}\\
&k^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{1}{2}r^{-1}{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{g}}}V^{\star},\ \ell^{\star}({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{h}}}V^{\star},\end{split}

(14)

where
{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}k+{\mathbf{h}}\ell}
,
{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{g}}}
, and
{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{h}}}
are the Koopman generators where the subscript denotes the vector field.

Proof:

Following Assumption
2
, we write the feedback system as

\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{f}}({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{g}}({\mathbf{x}})k({\mathbf{x}})+{\mathbf{h}}({\mathbf{x}})\ell({\mathbf{x}}).

Let
{\mathbb{U}}_{t}^{c}
be the Koopman operator for this feedback system. Using the definition of Koopman operator, we can write performance measure
J({\mathbf{x}},k,\ell)
as

\displaystyle V({\mathbf{x}})=\int_{0}^{\infty}[{\mathbb{U}}_{t}^{c}\varphi]({\mathbf{x}})dt,

(15)

where
\varphi({\mathbf{x}}):=(q+rk^{2}-\gamma^{2}\ell)({\mathbf{x}})
. We next claim that
V({\mathbf{x}})
satisfies

\displaystyle{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}k+{\mathbf{h}}\ell}V=-q-rk^{2}+\gamma^{2}\ell^{2}.

(16)

Substituting (
15
) in the LHS of (
16
), we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}k+{\mathbf{h}}\ell}{\mathbb{U}}_{t}^{c}\varphi dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{d}{dt}{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi dt

\displaystyle={\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi|_{t=0}^{\infty}=\lim_{t\to\infty}{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi-\varphi,

(17)

where we have used the infinitesimal generator property of the Koopman semi-group (
3
). Then, we can show that
\lim_{t\to\infty}[{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi]({\mathbf{x}})=0
. To prove this we use the Assumption
2
that
V({\mathbf{x}})
is finite for any finite
{\mathbf{x}}
. Furthermore,
[{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi]
is uniformly continuous w.r.t. time which follows from the definition of the Koopman operator semi-groups and the fact that the solution of the closed loop system is uniformly continuous w.r.t. time.
Hence, we can apply the Barbalat Lemma which states that for any function
G(t)\in{\cal C}^{1}
, and
\lim_{t\to\infty}G(t)=\alpha
. If
G^{\prime}(t)
is uniformly continuous, then
\lim_{t\to\infty}G^{\prime}(t)=0
. Applying the Barbalat Lemma with
G^{\prime}(t)=[{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi]({\mathbf{x}})
for a fixed
{\mathbf{x}}
, we obtain
\lim_{t\to\infty}[{\mathbb{U}}_{t}\varphi]({\mathbf{x}})=0
. Hence, we prove the claim (
16
). The optimal control and adversarial inputs are obtained as the critical or extremum point of (
16
). This extremum is obtained by differentiating (
16
) w.r.t.
k
and
\ell
leading to

\displaystyle k({\mathbf{x}})=-\frac{1}{2}r^{-1}{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{g}}}V,\;\;\;\;\ell({\mathbf{x}})=\frac{1}{2\gamma^{2}}{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{h}}}V.

(18)

Substituting (
18
) in (
16
) we obtain the desired HJIE (
14
) for optimal value function
V^{\star}
.
∎

Note that the Koopman-based perspective developed here can be easily extended to the HJBE and the
H_{\infty}
control problem. The HJBE appears in the optimal control problem in absence of the adversary and can be obtained from (
10
) when
\gamma\to\infty
.

IV
Computational Methods

The complexity associated with the nonlinear nature of the PDE is overcome by developing an iterative algorithm for solving the HJIE. In the following, we present the Koopman Policy Iteration (KPI) algorithm.

IV-A

Koopman policy iteration approximation of HJIE

In the approximation of the HJIE, we can assume that we have access to the system vector fields in the form of
{\mathbf{f}},{\mathbf{g}}
, and
{\mathbf{h}}
. If we do not, then EDMD based methods can be employed to approximate these vector fields. For a given control and adversarial input at the iteration step
(i,j)
i.e.,
k^{i}
and
\ell^{(i,j)}

\dot{\mathbf{x}}={\mathbf{f}}+{\mathbf{g}}k^{i}+{\mathbf{h}}\ell^{(i,j)}=:{\mathbf{f}}_{c}^{(i,j)}
is used to construct the approximation of the closed loop Koopman generator
{\cal K}_{{\mathbf{f}}_{c}^{(i,j)}}\approx{\mathbf{L}}_{(i,j)}^{{\mathbf{f}}_{c}}
for a given choice of basis functions
{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}=(\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N})^{\top}
.
Similarly, the right hand side of (
16
) can be approximated as follows:

\displaystyle-q({\mathbf{x}})-r(k^{i})^{2}({\mathbf{x}})+\gamma^{2}(\ell^{(i,j)})^{2}({\mathbf{x}})\approx{\mathbf{b}}_{(i,j)}^{\top}{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\mathbf{x}}),

where the coefficient vector
{\mathbf{b}}_{(i,j)}
is obtained as the solution of following least square problem.
Let
\{{\mathbf{x}}_{t}\}_{t=0}^{M}
be the time series data collected by sampling uniformly over the state space. Construct the matrices for the lifted states
\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}=[{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\mathbf{x}}_{0}),...,{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}({\mathbf{x}}_{M})]^{\top}
, state cost
\bar{\mathbf{q}}=[q({\mathbf{x}}_{0}),...,q({\mathbf{x}}_{M})]^{\top}
, control cost
\bar{{\mathbf{k}}}=[(k^{i})^{2}({\mathbf{x}}_{0}),...,(k^{i})^{2}({\mathbf{x}}_{M})]^{\top}
, and adversary cost
\bar{{\mathbf{l}}}=[(\ell^{(i,j)})^{2}({\mathbf{x}}_{0}),...,(\ell^{(i,j)})^{2}({\mathbf{x}}_{M})]^{\top}
.
Where the coefficient vector
{\mathbf{b}}_{(i,j)}
is obtained as the solution of following least square problem,

\min\limits_{\bf{\mathbf{b}}_{(i,j)}}\parallel\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}({\mathbf{x}}){\mathbf{b}}_{(i,j)}-(-\bar{\mathbf{q}}-r\bar{{\mathbf{k}}}^{i}+\gamma^{2}\bar{{\mathbf{l}}}^{(i,j)})\parallel_{2}^{2},

(19)

which admits the following analytical solution.

{\mathbf{b}}_{(i,j)}=\bar{{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}}^{\dagger}(-\bar{\mathbf{q}}-r\bar{{\mathbf{k}}}^{i}+\gamma^{2}\bar{{\mathbf{l}}}^{(i,j)}).

The value function is approximated as

VI
Conclusions

We presented a Koopman-based policy iteration (KPI) algorithm for solving the HJIE. This iterative algorithm can be implemented in the model-based and data-driven setting and relies on approximation of the Koopman operator. Future research efforts will focus on understanding the role of the Koopman spectrum in the data-driven approximation of the HJIE.
