MaxCUCL: Max − - Consensus with Deterministic Convergence in Networks with Unreliable Communication Links footnoteinfo
Apostolos I. Rikos, Themistoklis Charalambous, Karl H. Johansson

Abstract
In this paper, we present a novel distributed algorithm (herein called MaxCUCL) designed to guarantee that max − \max- consensus is reached in networks characterized by unreliable communication links (i.e., links suffering from packet drops). Our proposed algorithm is the first algorithm that achieves max − \max- consensus in a deterministic manner (i.e., nodes always calculate the maximum of their states regardless of the nature of the probability distribution of the packet drops). Furthermore, it allows nodes to determine whether convergence has been achieved (enabling them to transition to subsequent tasks). The operation of MaxCUCL relies on the deployment of narrowband error-free feedback channels used for acknowledging whether a packet transmission between nodes was successful. We analyze the operation of our algorithm and show that it converges after a finite number of time steps. Finally, we demonstrate our algorithm’s effectiveness and practical applicability by applying it to a sensor network deployed for environmental monitoring.

Introduction

The relentless surge of interconnectivity in the modern world has led to a growing need for effective control and coordination within networks of multiple agents, such as groups of sensors
[
1
]
and mobile autonomous agents
[
2
]
.
A problem of particular interest in distributed control and coordination is the
consensus
problem
[
3
,
4
,
5
]
.
The objective of the
consensus
problem is to develop distributed algorithms that can be used by a group of agents in order to reach agreement to a common decision (or state).
The agents engage in the process to reach consensus with different initial states/data and communicate locally via information exchange with neighboring agents with which communication can be established.
The concept of
\max-
consensus refers to the process of achieving consensus to the maximum state among a set of states (or data) held by different nodes in a network.
Knowledge of the maximum value of a state is of paramount importance for various applications such as leader election
[
6
]
, clock and data synchronization
[
7
,
8
]
, termination mechanisms
[
9
]
, distributed clustering
[
10
]
, fault detection
[
11
]
, and resource allocation
[
12
,
13
,
14
]
.

Related Work.

The problem of
\max-
consensus has received significant attention in the literature.
Asynchronous operation has been a focal point in several studies
[
15
,
16
,
14
]
.
In
[
15
]
randomized algorithms with asynchronous operations were explored, demonstrating convergence within finite time with high probability.
Furthermore, in
[
16
]
, algorithms operating asynchronously with transmissions affected by time delays were presented.
Additionally, in
[
14
]
, asynchronous
\max-
consensus algorithms leveraging quantized communication were proposed.
Conversely, synchronous operation took precedence in other works
[
17
,
18
,
19
]
.
For example in
[
17
]
nodes operated synchronously and achieved
\max-
consensus within finite time over static directed graphs.
In
[
18
]
scenarios involving synchronous algorithms with transmission delays were examined to detect convergence of the underlying protocol.
Furthermore, in
[
19
]
max-plus algebra was employed to analyze the convergence rate of synchronous
\max-
consensus algorithms for time-invariant networks.
The dynamics of time-varying networks were analyzed in
[
20
,
21
]
.
In
[
20
]
, a family of consensus algorithms was introduced for time-varying networks, demonstrating connections to Bellman-Ford and mean hitting time iterations under certain modifications.
In a related manner,
[
21
]
focused on
\max-
consensus over time-varying and state-dependent graphs, establishing necessary and sufficient conditions for convergence.
Unreliable networks were the main focus in
[
22
,
23
,
24
]
.
Specifically,
[
24
]
explored cases where edges were absent with non-zero probabilities independently across edges and time,
[
22
]
calculated tighter convergence bounds for two algorithms achieving max consensus in asynchronous networks, and
[
23
]
considered scenarios where messages were dropped based on non-zero probabilities.
Additionally, prior approaches on the
\max-
consensus problem considered also a diversity of alternative perspectives (which however fall outside the specific scope of this paper).
For instance, in
[
25
]
, the focus was on scenarios where the size of the communication network underwent dynamic changes.
In
[
26
]
, two distinct algorithms were proposed to address the tracking of time-varying maximum states within networks, addressing scenarios where the maximum state evolves over time.
The work in
[
27
]
delved into the domain of privacy preservation during maximum state calculations.
Furthermore, the effects of additive communication noise were studied in
[
24
,
28
]
, and max-plus algebra was employed as a tool for analyzing the ergodic process.

Motivation.

All aforementioned works typically offer probabilistic convergence guarantees to the maximum state (lacking deterministic convergence guarantees) or their convergence relies on specific conditions regarding network disturbances.
Hence, the challenge of achieving deterministic convergence to the maximum state in networks with unreliable communication links (i.e., packet losses) remains an open question.
Furthermore, in most works, nodes do not possess a distributed mechanism to identify whether convergence to the maximum state has been reached, thus limiting their ability to proceed with subsequent operations.

Main Contributions.

In this paper, we present the first distributed algorithm (called MaxCUCL) addressing the challenge of achieving
\max-
consensus in a deterministic manner in networks with unreliable communication links (see Algorithm
1
).
More specifically, by leveraging the concept of narrowband error-free feedback channels, we show that MaxCUCL not only enables nodes to deterministically compute the maximum state in finite time, but also allows them to distributively determine whether to terminate their operation despite the presence of unreliable links (see Theorem
4.2
).
Finally, we present a motivating application where we demonstrate the algorithm’s effectiveness and practicality by applying it to a sensor network deployed for environmental monitoring (see Section
5
).

Preliminaries and Problem Formulation

Notation.
The sets of real, rational, integer, and natural numbers are denoted by
\mathds{R},\mathds{Q},\mathds{Z}
, and
\mathds{N}
, respectively.
The symbol
\mathds{Z}_{+}
denotes the set of nonnegative integers and the symbol
\mathds{N}_{0}
denotes the set of natural numbers that also includes zero.
The
div
operation represents the division of one number by another, returning the whole number quotient.
For example,
\alpha\ div\ \beta
denotes the division of the dividend
\alpha
by the divisor
\beta
resulting in the largest whole number quotient.
The
mod
operation represents the modulo or remainder when one number is divided by another.
For example, in the expression
\alpha\ mod\ \beta
the dividend
\alpha
is divided by the divisor
\beta
, and the mod operation returns the remainder.
The
max
operation, also known as the maximum operation, returns the greater of two given values.
It can be represented as
max(\alpha,\beta)
where
\alpha
and
\beta
are the values being compared.
The expected value of a random variable
x
is denoted as
\mathrm{E}[x]
, and represents the theoretical mean or average outcome that would be obtained if the random variable were repeatedly observed.
For any real number
a\in\mathds{R}
, the floor
\lfloor a\rfloor
denotes the greatest integer less than or equal to
a
while the ceiling
\lceil a\rceil
denotes the least integer greater than or equal to
a
.

Communication Network.

The communication network consists of
n
(
n\geq 2
) nodes communicating only with their immediate neighbors and can be captured by a
static
directed graph (digraph).
A static digraph is defined as
\mathcal{G}_{d}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
n=|\mathcal{V}|
nodes and
m=|\mathcal{E}|
edges.
The set of nodes is defined as
\mathcal{V}=\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}\}
, and the set of edges (self-edges excluded) is defined as
\mathcal{E}\subseteq\mathcal{V}\times\mathcal{V}-\{(v_{j},v_{j})\ |\ v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}\}
.
A directed edge from node
v_{i}
to node
v_{j}
is denoted by
(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}
, and captures the fact that node
v_{j}
can receive information from node
v_{i}
(but not the other way around).
The subset of nodes that can directly transmit information to node
v_{j}
is called the set of in-neighbors of
v_{j}
and is represented by
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{-}=\{v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}\;|\;(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}\}
.
The subset of nodes that can directly receive information from node
v_{j}
is called the set of out-neighbors of
v_{j}
and is represented by
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{+}=\{v_{l}\in\mathcal{V}\;|\;(v_{l},v_{j})\in\mathcal{E}\}
.
The cardinality of
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{-}
is called the
in-degree
of
v_{j}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{-}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{-}=|\mathcal{N}_{j}^{-}|
).
The cardinality of
\mathcal{N}_{j}^{+}
is called the
out-degree
of
v_{j}
and is denoted by
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{+}
(i.e.,
\mathcal{D}_{j}^{+}=|\mathcal{N}_{j}^{+}|
).
The diameter
D
of a digraph is defined as the longest shortest path between any two nodes
v_{j},v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
in the network.

2.1
System Model for Packet Dropping Links and Feedback Channels

In many real-world scenarios, distributed systems and networks often operate in environments characterized by unreliable communication links
[
29
,
30
]
.
Understanding and addressing the challenges posed by such unreliable networks is of utmost importance for ensuring the robustness and efficiency of distributed algorithms.

Modelling Packet Dropping Links.

In our setting, each communication link in the distributed network is unreliable (i.e., it is subject to packet drops or losses).
Packet drops can occur due to network congestion, channel impairments, or other factors.
The probability of a packet being dropped (or lost) at link
(v_{j},v_{i})
is denoted by
q_{ji}
(where
0\leq q_{ji}<1
), and is assumed to be independent and identically distributed across different links and time instances.
The occurrence of packet drops is modeled by utilizing a Bernoulli random variable.
Specifically, we define
x_{k}(j,i)
as an indicator variable (
x_{k}(j,i)=1
indicates a successful transmission from node
v_{i}
to node
v_{j}
at time step
k
).
The probability distribution of
x_{k}(j,i)
is given by

Pr\{x_{k}(j,i)=m\}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}q_{ji},&\mbox{if $m=0$,}\\
1-q_{ji},&\mbox{if $m=1$,}\end{array}\right.

(1)

where
q_{ji}
represents the probability of a packet being dropped at the link
(v_{j},v_{i})
, and
1-q_{ji}
represents the probability of a successful transmission.

Modelling Feedback Channels.

In various message transmission protocols such as Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and High-Level Data Link protocol, a mechanism called Automatic Repeat reQuest (ARQ) is utilized
[
31
,
32
]
.
ARQ serves as an error control protocol, ensuring reliable packet transmissions over unreliable communication channels.
The operation of ARQ relies on a feedback signal transmitted from the data receiver to the data transmitter.
The feedback signal indicates whether a packet has been successfully received or not.
This feedback signal is transmitted via a narrowband feedback channel, for which we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

For each directed link
(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}
from node
v_{i}
to node
v_{j}
, there exists a narrowband error-free feedback channel from node
v_{j}
to node
v_{i}
.

The feedback channel introduced in Assumption
1
usually comprises messages of
1
bit (i.e., it is not used for data transmission).
For this reason, it is reasonable to assume that it is error-free
[
33
]
.
Such a feedback mechanism is necessary for acknowledging whether a packet has been received or not.
In our setting, the feedback mechanism is also utilized to notify each node in the network whether the operation of the algorithm could be terminated or continue to be executed.

2.2
Node Operation

At time step
k
, each node
v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
maintains the following variables:

x_{j}[k]
is the node’s state;
x^{old}_{j}
is used for checking whether the node’s state was updated during a set of
\max-
consensus operations;
\text{dflag}_{j}
is a flag that is used to notify nodes in the network whether the operation of the algorithm is terminated or needs to be continued;
R_{ji}
is used from node
v_{j}
to mark its incoming links from which it received information at least once.

2.3
Problem Formulation

Consider a digraph
\mathcal{G}_{d}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
.
Each node
v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
has an initial state
x_{j}[0]\in\mathds{R}
and
q_{m}
is the maximum state among the initial states defined as

q_{m}=\max_{v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}}\{x_{j}[0]\}.

(2)

Furthermore, let us assume that each communication link in the digraph
\mathcal{G}_{d}
is subject to packet drops (see Section

).

In this paper, we aim to develop a distributed algorithm that allows nodes to (i) calculate
q_{m}
in a finite number of time steps and, (ii) terminate their operation
simultaneously
after they calculate

.
More specifically, we require that there exists a time step
k_{0}
so that for every
v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
we have

\displaystyle x_{j}[k_{0}]=q_{m},

(3a)

\displaystyle\text{nodes terminate operation at time step}\ k_{0},

(3b)

where
q_{m}
is defined in (
2
).

3
Main Results

In this section, we present our MaxCUCL algorithm that solves the problem described in Section
2.3
.
Before presenting our algorithm, we consider the following assumptions which are important for the development of the results in this paper.

Assumption 2
.

We assume that the digraph
\mathcal{G}_{d}
is strongly connected.

Assumption 3
.

We assume that every node
v_{j}
knows the diameter
D
of the digraph
\mathcal{G}_{d}
, or an upper bound on the diameter denoted as
D^{\prime}
(i.e.,
D^{\prime}\geq D
).

Assumption
2
means that for each pair of nodes
v_{j},v_{i}\in\mathcal{V}
,
v_{j}\neq v_{i}
, there exists a directed
path
from
v_{i}
to
v_{j}
, i.e., information from every node can reach any other node in the network.
Assumption
3
is important for coordinating the actions of nodes in the network, and notifying them whether convergence has been achieved.
Note that the
\max-
consensus has been extensively used in the literature as part of other processes
[
34
]
.
Therefore, the need to know the network diameter
D
is for completing the current procedure and moving on to the next process.

The operation of MaxCUCL is based on two phases:
Phase1
and
Phase2
.

Phase1
is executed in Iteration-step
1
and lasts
D
time steps.
At the beginning of this phase, each node
v_{j}
marks each incoming link with the value one.
Then, for
D
time steps, each node
v_{j}
executes the
\max-
consensus protocol.
During the execution of
\max-
consensus,
if node
v_{j}
received at least one message from in-neighbor
v_{i}\in\mathcal{N}^{-}_{j}
, it marks the link
(v_{j},v_{i})
with the value zero.

Phase2
is executed in Iteration-step
2
and also lasts
D
time steps.
At the beginning of this phase, each node
v_{j}
checks the following:

i)

if during
Phase1
, there is at least one incoming link from which it did not receive any message (i.e., there is at least one incoming link marked with value one).
Then, it sets its
\text{dflag}_{j}
equal to one.

ii)

if during
Phase1
, its value was updated (i.e., increased), then it sets its
\text{dflag}_{j}
equal to one.

Then, for
D
time steps it transmits its
\text{dflag}_{j}
to its in-neighbors.
It receives the
dflags
of its out-neighbors and updates its own
\text{dflag}_{j}
to be equal to the maximum value between the stored and the received dflags.
Finally, at the end of
Phase2
(i.e., after
D
time steps), if its
\text{dflag}_{j}
is equal to zero, it terminates its operation. Otherwise, if its
\text{dflag}_{j}
is equal to one, it goes to
Phase1
and repeats the operation.

.

4
Convergence Analysis

We now analyze the convergence of MaxCUCL algorithm.
We first introduce a theorem where we show that during the algorithm’s operation there exists a time step
k_{\mathrm{terminal}}
for which (
3a
), (
3b
) hold for every node
v_{j}
after a finite number of time steps.
Then, we present a proposition we calculate an explicit upper bound on time step

.

Theorem 4.2
.

Consider a strongly connected digraph
\mathcal{G}_{d}=(\mathcal{V},\mathcal{E})
with
n=|\mathcal{V}|
nodes and
m=|\mathcal{E}|
edges.
Each node
v_{j}\in\mathcal{V}
has an initial state
x_{j}[0]\in\mathds{R}

In the following proposition, we establish a probabilistic bound on the number of time steps needed for every node to calculate the maximum state in the network.

Proposition 4
.

The termination step

for MaxCUCL (i.e., Algorithm

), and (

(6)

with probability at least

\displaystyle k_{0}\geq\Bigl\lceil\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon_{1})}{\log(1/q_{\text{max}})}\Bigr\rceil,

and

\displaystyle k_{1}\geq\Bigl\lceil\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon_{2})}{\log(1/p_{1})}\Bigr\rceil,

(8)

PROOF.

In
Part3
of Theorem
4.2
, we showed that MaxCUCL converges to the maximum state after a finite number of time steps in a deterministic fashion.
However, it is possible to calculate an upper bound on the time steps required for achieving convergence with high probability.

Let us consider the maximum packet drop probability among all links in the network defined as
q_{\text{max}}=\max_{(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}}\{q_{ji}\}
.
Also, let us define an epoch as the number of
D
time steps.
We calculate a time step
k_{0}
for which every link
(v_{j},v_{i})\in\mathcal{E}
will have performed at least one successful transmission with probability almost equal to one.
To achieve this, we solve the inequality

1-q_{\text{max}}^{k_{0}}\geq 1-\varepsilon_{1},

(9)

where
\varepsilon_{1}
represents a probability almost equal to zero.
In (
9
) we solve for
k_{0}
and take the ceiling of the result (because time steps are discrete values).
Thus, we obtain

k_{0}\geq\Bigl\lceil\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon_{1})}{\log(1/q_{\text{max}})}\Bigr\rceil.

(10)

Eq. (
10
) represents the time step
k_{0}
at which every link will have performed at least one successful transmission with probability
(1-\varepsilon_{1})
almost equal to one.
Let us suppose that we execute
Phase1
of MaxCUCL for
Dk_{0}
time steps (
k_{0}
fulfills (
10
)).
This means that each link has at least one successful transmission during each one of the
D

(1-\varepsilon_{1})^{D}
.
Therefore, by setting
\varepsilon_{1}
small enough, we have that after
Dk_{0}
time steps during

of our algorithm each node fulfills (

).
Continuing the algorithm’s operation, consider the maximum packet drop probability for

The probability

p_{1}\leq\sum_{\lambda=1}^{|\mathcal{E}|}\binom{|\mathcal{E}|}{\lambda}q_{\text{max}}^{\lambda D}(1-q_{\text{max}}^{(|\mathcal{E}|-\lambda)D}),

where

is the number of links in the network,

We now calculate a number of epochs

for which no link has

To achieve this we solve the inequality

1-p_{1}^{k_{1}}\geq 1-\varepsilon_{2},

(12)

where
\varepsilon_{2}
is a probability almost equal to zero.
Focusing on (

k_{1}\geq\Bigl\lceil\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon_{2})}{\log(1/p_{1})}\Bigr\rceil.

(13)

In (

epochs of
D

Let us now combine the results in (

In (

Dk_{0}

Therefore, after

Note that
Dk_{0}
are the required time steps of

5
Application: Distributed Environmental Monitoring in Sensor Networks

We now present an application of the MaxCUCL algorithm.
Our application focuses on the scenario where a sensor network is deployed in an outdoor environment, and sensor nodes are tasked with monitoring various environmental parameters
[
35
,
36
]
.
These parameters may include temperature, humidity, air quality, or other relevant factors; in our application, we will focus on temperature monitoring. Specifically, in the scenario under consideration, nodes aim to achieve consensus on the maximum observed temperature.
This information is pivotal in deciding whether specific environmental factors require active control or intervention.
Additionally, consensus needs to be reached in finite time, since in real-world scenarios nodes may be operating under resource constraints (e.g., battery-powered operation).
For this reason nodes need to conclude their operation successfully, in order to ensure that the calculated consensus is not only accurate but also usable for subsequent decision-making processes.
Moreover, in realistic outdoor environments, communication among sensor nodes is susceptible to challenges such as unreliability and packet drops.
These issues can stem from factors like signal interference, obstacles in the terrain, or limited energy resources for wireless communication.
We will show that MaxCUCL serves as a valuable tool for extracting critical information from the sensor network’s data, as it allows nodes to achieve consensus in finite time on the maximum observed temperature even in the presence of unreliable communication links.

During the environmental monitoring operation each sensor node continuously measures the temperature of its surroundings, and stores the maximum locally measured temperature.
At the end of each day, sensor nodes execute the MaxCUCL algorithm in order to calculate the maximum globally measured temperature in the environment.
In our scenario, nodes calculate the maximum globally measured temperature at the end of a single day.
Let us note however, that nodes can repeat this operation (collecting measurements, storing the maximum locally measured temperature and then, executing our algorithm to calculate the maximum globally measured temperature) every single day in order to calculate the maximum measured temperature for a specific time period (e.g., a month).

Remark 5.3
.

In our analysis, we observed that as we increase the diameter
D
, the number of executions of Phase1 and Phase2 decreases.
Thus, increasing

improves the algorithm’s convergence.
However, let us note that increasing the diameter
D
also affects the total number of time steps required for convergence of our algorithm.
As a result, although increasing the diameter
D
results in Phase1 and Phase2 being executed fewer times, each individual execution of Phase1 and Phase2 lasts longer (i.e., because Phase1 and Phase2 are executed for
D+D=2D
time steps).
This trade-off between the number of executions of Phase1 and Phase2 and the duration of the execution of MaxCUCL, highlights the importance of carefully considering the choice of diameter
D
in applications of where convergence speed and communication overhead are critical factors.

Remark 5.4
.

Let us note here that another interesting application of our algorithm is for the case where nodes aim to calculate the average observed temperature in a finite time frame, with the added objective of terminating their operations upon calculation completion.
Calculation of the average temperature is achieved via average consensus algorithms that operate over networks consisted of unreliable communication links (e.g., see
[
37
]
and references therein).
Our proposed algorithm is able to operate in parallel with a robust average consensus algorithm.
This guarantees that nodes will be able to terminate their operation in a finite time frame and the average calculated temperature will be utilized for subsequent
decision-making processes.

6
Conclusions and Future Directions

We introduced the MaxCUCL algorithm for achieving
\max-
consensus in finite time over networks with unreliable communication links.
We showed that our algorithm enables nodes to calculate the maximum of their states deterministically by relying on narrowband error-free feedback channels.
It also allows nodes to identify whether convergence has been achieved and terminate the process.
We analyzed the algorithm’s operation and established its convergence.
Finally, we presented a motivating application in sensor networks for environmental monitoring where we demonstrated the effectiveness and practical applicability of our proposed algorithm.

Our future research includes extending our algorithm to handle dynamic network conditions and more complex tasks that could provide valuable insights into its adaptability and scalability.
Finally, developing mechanisms to ensure our algorithm’s resilience against adversarial attacks and Byzantine faults (where nodes may provide malicious or incorrect information) is an essential direction to enhance its robustness and reliability.
