Fermi and Luttinger arcs: two concepts, one surface
Paul Worm, Matthias Reitner, Karsten Held, Alessandro Toschi

Abstract
We present an analytically solvable model for correlated electrons, which is able to capture the major Fermi surface modifications occurring in both hole- and electron-doped cuprates as a function of doping. The proposed Hamiltonian qualitatively reproduces the results of numerically demanding many-body calculations, here obtained using the dynamical vertex approximation. Our analytical theory provides a transparent description of the formation of disconnected segments along the Fermi surface (the highly debated “Fermi arcs”) as well as of the opening of a pseudogap at hole- and electron-doping. This occurs through a specific mechanism: The electronic states on the Fermi arcs remain intact, while the Fermi surface part where the gap opens transforms into a Luttinger arc.



Introduction
— The emergence of isolated arcs on the Fermi
surface (FS) of strongly correlated systems, such as those characterizing the famous pseudogap regime

;
Norman et al. 1998
of high-temperature superconducting cuprates
Keimer et al. 2015
, has been the object of an incessant and quite vivid theoretical debate in the last thirty-five years.

In the specific case of the cuprates, the presence of Fermi arcs is not only suggested by the evolution of the ARPES spectra as a function of temperature and doping
Vishik 2018
;
Reber et al. 2012
;
Kunisada et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2005
;
Kordyuk 2015
;

;
Hashimoto et al. 2014
;
Vishik et al. 2010
;
Vishik et al. 2012
;
Hashimoto et al. 2015

but also by the persistence of clear Fermi liquid features in the qualitative behavior of transport experiments performed in the whole normal phase of these compounds
Barišić et al. 2013
;
Barišić et al. 2019
.

At the same time, the emergence of Fermi arcs remains quite puzzling from a theoretical point of view: On the one hand, they would be forbidden in conventional band theory
Wan et al. 2011
;
Schrunk et al. 2022
(where bands are continuous and Fermi surfaces closed)
and, on the other hand, the complexity associated with the explicit inclusion of electronic correlations hinders the identification of a transparent mechanism explaining their formation.

In the latter respect, the quest for explaining the enigmatic properties of the celebrated pseudogap phase in the cuprates has led many groups to perform a huge number of theoretical calculations, ranging from phenomenological

modeling of the pseudogap self-energy

Yang et al. 2006
;
Yamaji and Imada 2011
;
Rice et al. 2011
;
Robinson et al. 2019
;
Fabrizio 2022
;

to advanced quantum many-body calculations (mostly of hole- or electron-doped Hubbard/Hubbard-like models)

;
Schäfer et al. 2021
;
Krien et al. 2022
;
Tremblay et al. 2006
. In several cases, the theoretical results have provided spectral functions and transport properties compatible with the experimental observations in the cuprates. Further, the application of the fluctuations diagnostics approach

;
Rohringer 2020
;

;
Schäfer and Toschi 2021

.

However, neither the phenomenological approaches nor the most advanced numerical studies have so far provided an intuitive and physically intelligible picture of the observed phenomena.

In this paper we approach the problem from a different perspective: We introduce an
exactly solvable
many-electron model Hamiltonian on a two-dimensional (2D) lattice, which is able to describe, depending on the parameter regimes considered, the formation of Fermi arcs in different sectors of the FS and to capture the qualitative differences in the spectral properties between electron- and hole-doped compounds, including the pseudogap properties.
It is worth underlining already here that, while the conception of our Hamiltonian is –to a given extent– inspired by the Hatsugai-Kohmoto model (HKM)
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
, which has recently attracted an increasing interest both for its physical content and its formal properties
Yeo and Phillips 2019
;
Phillips et al. 2020
;
Li et al. 2022
;
Mai et al. 2023
;
Wysokiński and Brzezicki 2023
;
Zhao et al. 2023
;
Setty et al. 2023a
;
Setty et al. 2023b
, its key ingredient presents a fundamental difference: The electronic interaction of our model is
not
purely diagonal in momentum space, as it was in the HKM, but couples all momenta differing by the transfer momentum of
{\bm{Q}}=(\pi,\pi)
associated to antiferromagnetic (AF) spin fluctuations. As we demonstrate in the following, this feature enables our model, differently from the HKM, to capture the fundamental momentum-dependent modifications of the FS occurring in 2D correlated electron systems as a function of hole/electron doping, in reasonable agreement to those we obtain by means of cutting-edge quantum many-body schemes, such as the dynamical vertex approximation (D
\Gamma
A)
Toschi et al. 2007
;
Katanin et al. 2009
;
Worm 2023
.
In this context, the invaluable insight gained by mastering an analytically solvable model provides a new route to clarify the hotly debated questions of the emergence of Fermi arcs, of the mechanisms controlling their evolution, and, consequently, of the intrinsic nature of the pseudogap phase.

Model Hamiltonian
— We introduce here our model Hamiltonian for a square lattice, which (i) allows for a general exact analytical solution and (ii) displays Fermi arcs, defined as disconnected parts of the FS.
It reads:

H=\sum_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}\left[(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu)\hat{n}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}+\frac{\mathcal{V}}{2}\hat{n}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}\hat{n}_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}-\sigma}\right],

(1)

where
\mu
is the chemical potential,
\hat{n}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}\!=\!\hat{c}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}^{\dagger}\hat{c}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}^{\phantom{\dagger}}
the occupation operator,
\mathcal{V}
the interaction strength that couples momenta
{\bm{k}}
and
{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}
, with
{\bm{Q}}\!=\!(\pi,\pi)
describing the characteristic momentum of the coupling. For the dispersion relation
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
, we consider:

\begin{split}\epsilon_{\bm{k}}=&-2t(\cos(k_{x})+\cos(k_{y}))-4t^{\prime}\cos(k_{x})\cos(k_{y})\\
&-2t^{\prime\prime}(\cos(2k_{x})+\cos(2k_{y})),\end{split}

(2)

where we use
t^{\prime}=-0.2
and
t^{\prime\prime}=0.1
(while
t=1
sets the unit of energy) consistent with typical values used in low-energy tight-binding models for cuprates
Nicoletti et al. 2010
;
Worm 2023
. See SM
sup
for a brief discussion regarding the role of the sign of the hopping parameters.

Relation to the Hatsugai-Kohmoto model
— As mentioned in the introduction, our Hamiltonian can be linked to the Hatsugai-Kohmoto model (HKM)
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
, which can be retrieved by setting
{\bm{Q}}=0
in Eq. (
1
) and also allows for an exact solution.
We recall here that the HKM can be mapped to a set of decoupled atomic limit models [
H_{AL}=U\hat{n}_{\uparrow}\hat{n}_{\downarrow}-\mu_{\rm{eff}}(\hat{n}_{\uparrow}\!+\!\hat{n}_{\downarrow})
] in the absence of a magnetic field, one for each
{\bm{k}}
point, whose difference is only in the value of their effective chemical potentials
\mu_{\rm{eff}}=\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
. This property, which renders the model solvable, also allows for a description of the appearance of Luttinger zeros at the Fermi energy and of Mott-like insulating phases
Hatsugai and Kohmoto 1992
;
Continentino and Coutinho-Filho 1994
;
Vitoriano et al. 2000
;
Yeo and Phillips 2019
, though recent work
Sangiovanni and Fabrizio 2023
has pointed out possible inconsistencies in the corresponding linear response expressions.

The crucial difference of the HKM w.r.t. our model arises from the nonzero value of
{\bm{Q}}=(\pi,\pi)
in Eq. (
1
). This captures the coupling of electrons at different momenta (absent in the momentum-diagonal HKM), mediated by AF fluctuations, eventually allowing the emergence of momentum-selective behaviors. To a given extent, Eq. (
1
) can be viewed as a set of atomic limit models in momentum space in the presence of
a magnetic field

h_{{\bm{k}}}=(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\epsilon_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}})/2
and chemical potential
\tilde{\mu}_{{\bm{k}}}=\mu-(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}+\epsilon_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}})/2
acting on two states of opposite spin at different momenta (i.e.,
{{\bm{k}}\!\uparrow}
,
{{\bm{k}}\!+\!{\bm{Q}}\!\downarrow}
).

Analytic solution
—To analyze the evolution of the FS, and in particular the possible emergence of disconnected sections, we start by computing the single-particle Green’s function as a function of the Matsubara frequency
\nu_{n}
:

G_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}(\mathrm{i}\nu_{n})\equiv-\int_{0}^{\beta}\!d\tau\,\langle{\hat{c}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}^{\phantom{\dagger}}(\tau)\hat{c}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}^{\dagger}(0)}\rangle\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\nu_{n}\tau}.

For the model proposed in
Eq.

1
,
G_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}
can be analytically computed directly on the real-frequency axis

G_{{\bm{k}}}(\omega)=\frac{1-n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}}{\omega+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}+\mathrm{i}0^{+}}+\frac{n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}}{\omega+\mu-\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mathcal{V}+\mathrm{i}0^{+}},

(3)

where
n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}=\langle{\hat{n}_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}\sigma}}\rangle

can be calculated according to Eq. (
4
) below.
We note that the presence of
n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}
(instead of
n_{{\bm{k}}}
as in the HKM), allows here for the coupling of different momenta, and hence for the possible emergence of momentum-selective features like Fermi arcs.
Specifically, there are three different (momentum-dependent) possibilities for the Green’s function: (i) for all momenta
{\bm{k}}
at which
n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}=0
, we have the non-interacting Green’s function; (ii) for all
{\bm{k}}
at which
n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}=1
only the second term in the r.h.s. of Eq. (
3
) is nonzero, yielding the non-interacting solution but with a chemical potential shifted by
\mathcal{V}
; and (iii) for all
{\bm{k}}
at which
0\!<n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}<\!1
, both branches of
G
in Eq. (
3
) are active and the spectrum splits into two peaks with a gap in between.

For the four many-body states of each pair
{{\bm{k}}\!\uparrow}
,
{{\bm{k}}\!+\!{\bm{Q}}\!\downarrow}
,
we can calculate

n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}=\frac{\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\xi_{{\bm{k}}}+\xi_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}+\mathcal{V})}}{1+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{\bm{k}}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta\xi_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}}+\mathrm{e}^{-\beta(\xi_{{\bm{k}}}+\xi_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}+\mathcal{V})}},

(4)

where
\beta=1/T
is the inverse temperature, and
\xi_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{{\bm{k}}}-\mu
.
This yields the evolution of the FS as a function of the chemical potential
\mu
and the interaction strength
\mathcal{V}
in the low-
T
limit (
\beta\!\rightarrow\infty
) shown in
Fig.

1
. Here, the color plot displays the corresponding filling, and the lines separate regions of qualitatively different Fermi surfaces. A representative quadrant of the corresponding FS is shown as inset in each region.

This phase diagram can be understood further. Let us start with the arguably most interesting hole-doped case (V). For the part of the FS, that lies inside the AF zone boundary (AFZB),
i.e. inside the AFZB lines
\pm k_{x}\pm k_{y}=\pi
,

{\bm{k}}

couples via
{\bm{Q}}
to empty states with
n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}\!=\!0
. Thus we obtain the non-interacting spectrum
A(\omega,{\bm{k}})\!=\!\delta(\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}})
; the FS remains intact. Contrary, the part around the antinode [around
{\bm{k}}=(\pi,0)
], which is outside of the AFZB, is coupled to filled states with
n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}\!=\!1
. Consequently, the spectrum
A(\omega,{\bm{k}})\!=\!\delta(\omega-\xi_{\bm{k}}-\mathcal{V})
is shifted to energies above the Fermi level.
Hence, there is no FS for such momenta.
Instead, a Luttinger surface (LS) appears
Dzyaloshinskii 2003
, defined
by
G_{\bm{k}}(\omega=0)\!=\!0
(i.e., with both real and imaginary part vanishing). Resolving
Eq. (
3
) for this condition yields

\xi_{\bm{k}}=\epsilon_{{\bm{k}}}-\mu\!=\!(n_{{\bm{k}}+{\bm{Q}}}-1)\mathcal{V}
,
which for
\beta\to\infty
becomes fulfilled close to the AFZB.

The result is a disconnected FS, or more precisely: a transition of a FS into a LS at the AFZB. This insight of an exactly solvable model is crucial for understanding Fermi arcs: It shows that Fermi surfaces do not need to be connected, but can display a direct transition into Luttinger arcs, which are invisible to ARPES experiments. The underlying combined surface (LS and FS) defined by
\Re G_{\bm{k}}(\omega=0)\!=\!0

remains continuous and connected. However, it splits into Fermi arcs
[poles of
\Im G_{\bm{k}}(\omega=0)
] and Luttinger arcs [zeros of
\Im G_{\bm{k}}(\omega=0)
], see also SM
sup
.
The location of the Fermi arcs in our model is qualitatively consistent with ARPES experiments in cuprates
Vishik 2018
;
Reber et al. 2012
;
Kunisada et al. 2020
;
Shen et al. 2005
;
Kordyuk 2015
;
Lee et al. 2007
;
Hashimoto et al. 2014
;
Vishik et al. 2010
;
Vishik et al. 2012
;
Hashimoto et al. 2015
;
He et al. 2011
.
Further, the evolution of the FS/LS as a function of doping is directly reflected in the corresponding low-
T
behavior of transport properties, such as the electronic specific heat for
T\rightarrow 0
(see SM
sup
).

For the electron-doped case (II), the scenario gets reversed w.r.t. to the AFZB, see panels (a) and (c) of Fig.
2
. This again agrees with the experimental observation in cuprates
Armitage et al. 2001
;
Armitage et al. 2002

It is also worth stressing here that, while the close proximity of Luttinger and Fermi arcs, which characterizes the pseudogap regimes (II) and (V), may lead one to suspect a mismatch in the Luttinger count

n\!=\!\sum_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}\Theta(\Re G_{\bm{k}}(\omega\!=\!0))

In fact, for momenta close to the Luttinger surface one does find that
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}\mathrm{d}\nu\,\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}\nu 0^{+}}G_{\bm{k}}(\mathrm{i}\nu)\frac{\partial\Sigma_{\bm{k}}(\mathrm{i}\nu)}{\partial\mathrm{i}\nu}\neq 0

Eventually, we briefly discuss the other physical regimes shown in Fig.
1
. In particular, since the Fermi surface becomes disconnected only at the AF zone boundary, all values of
\mu
,
\mathcal{V}
for which
\xi_{\bm{k}}=0
and
\xi_{\bm{k}}+\mathcal{V}=0
do not cross the AF zone boundary, will show only a regular continuous hole- (I) or electron-like (VI) Fermi surface or a completely gapped half-filled system (IV). From this restriction we can deduce the two regions: (II),
4(t^{\prime}-t^{\prime\prime})+\mathcal{V}<\mu<4t^{\prime\prime}+\mathcal{V}

Matsui et al. 2007

Connection to the Hubbard model
—After illustrating the mechanisms through which
Eq.

3
displays Fermi arcs, an important question to be addressed is how our findings connect to the physics described by the microscopical Hamiltonian typically adopted for describing the low-energy properties of cuprates, namely the two-dimensional Hubbard model (HM):

H=\sum_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}(\epsilon_{\bm{k}}-\mu)\hat{n}_{{\bm{k}}\sigma}+\frac{U}{2}\sum_{i\sigma}\hat{n}_{i\sigma}\hat{n}_{i-\sigma},

(5)

where
\beta=12.5
(for lower temperatures and computational details see SM
sup
),
\epsilon_{\bm{k}}
is the same dispersion as in Eq. (
1
) and
U\!=\!8t
is the strength of the on-site electrostatic repulsion. As it is known, any reliable calculation of the spectral properties of the HM requires advanced numerical many-body approaches
Qin et al. 2022
. Here, we resort to the dynamical vertex approximation (
\textrm{D}\Gamma\textrm{A}
)
Toschi et al. 2007
;
Katanin et al. 2009
;
Worm 2023
, which is a diagrammatic extension
Rohringer et al. 2018
of the dynamical mean-field theory
Georges et al. 1996
, capable of treating non-local spatial correlations
Rohringer and Toschi 2016
even in the intermediate-to-strong coupling regimes of cuprate physics. In fact, while not exact,
\textrm{D}\Gamma\textrm{A}
compares well to numerically exact quantum Monte Carlo, where such solutions are available
Schäfer et al. 2021
and was recently also successfully applied to the study of nickelate superconductors
Held et al. 2022
.

Conclusions
—We have introduced a model Hamiltonian that, despite being
analytically solvable
, does capture the doping evolution of the Fermi surface as experimentally observed in hole/electron-doped cuprates
and
in cutting-edge numerical calculations for the doped Hubbard model, like the D
\Gamma
A employed here.

The advantage of having a full analytical description at our disposal has allowed us to rigorously address fundamental questions about the spectral properties of the normal phase of unconventional superconductors.
In particular, our results (i) demonstrate that the emergence of disconnected part of the FS (“Fermi arcs”) is possible, (ii) unveil the specific way of how this can be realized, as well as (iii) capture the differences between electron and hole doping.
In particular, we have shown how Fermi arcs and Luttinger arcs, the latter ones invisible to ARPES, can live on the same surface and
continually evolve
into one another, lifting the apparent necessity for closed Fermi surfaces and/or Fermi pockets with strongly varying spectral intensity. More precisely, the connection between FS and LS occurs at the
{\bm{k}}
-points where both cross the AFZB, while the Fermi or Luttinger nature of the resulting segments (internal or external w.r.t. the AFZB) depends on the hole vs. electron kind of the doping, consistent with the differences of the spectra at the Fermi level characterizing electron- and hole-doped cuprates.

Eventually, we have transparently interpreted the analytically calculated transformation of a Fermi surface into Fermi and Luttinger arcs in terms of the physics of an interaction-driven momentum-selective insulator, arising from the coupling of electrons whose momenta are linked by AF fluctuations.
