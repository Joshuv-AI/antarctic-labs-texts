Optimal transport in the frame of abstract Lax-Oleinik operator revisited
Wei Cheng, Jiahui Hong and Tianqi Shi

Abstract
. This is our first paper on the extension of our recent work on the Lax-Oleinik commutators and its applications to the intrinsic approach of propagation of singularities of the viscosity solutions of Hamilton-Jacobi equations. We reformulate Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality theorem in the theory of optimal transport in terms of abstract Lax-Oleinik operators, and analyze the relevant optimal transport problem in the case the cost function c ⁡ ( x , y ) = h ⁡ ( t 1 , t 2 , x , y ) c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y) is the fundamental solution of Hamilton-Jacobi equation. For further applications to the problem of cut locus and propagation of singularities in optimal transport, we introduce corresponding random Lax-Oleinik operators. We also study the problem of singularities for c c -concave functions and its dynamical implication when c c is the fundamental solution with t 2 − t 1 ≪ 1 t_{2}-t_{1}\ll 1 and t 2 − t 1 < ∞ t_{2}-t_{1}<\infty , and c c is the Peierls’ barrier respectively.

1.1.
Singularities of
c
-concave functions

Let
X
and
Y
be two Polish spaces and
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
. The function
c
is called
cost function
. We consider an abstract setting of Lax-Oleinik operators. For any
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
we define the
abstract Lax-Oleinik operators

\displaystyle T^{-}\phi(y):=\inf_{x\in X}\{\phi(x)+c(x,y)\},\qquad T^{+}\psi(x):=\sup_{y\in Y}\{\psi(y)-c(x,y)\},\qquad x\in X,y\in Y.

Recall that a function
\psi:Y\to[-\infty,+\infty)
is said to be
c
-concave
if it is the infimum of a family of functions
c(x,\cdot)+\alpha(x)
. Analogously,
\phi:X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is said to be
c
-convex
if it is the supreme of a family of functions
\beta(y)-c(\cdot,y)
. Given
\psi:Y\to[-\infty,+\infty)
and
\psi(y)>-\infty
we define the
c
-superdifferential as

\displaystyle\partial^{c}\psi(y):=\{x\in X:\psi(\cdot)-c(x,\cdot)\ \text{attains maximum at}\ y\}.

For any
c
-concave function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
,
y\in Y
is called a
singular point
of
\psi
if
\partial^{c}\psi(y)
is not a singleton. For any
c
-concave function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\text{Sing}^{c}(\psi)
the set of all singular points of
\psi
.

By using this setting we first obtain the characterization of
c
-concavity via Lax-Oleinik commutators: the following statements are equivalent (see Theorem
3.6
).

(1)

T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi=\psi
.

(2)

\partial^{c}\psi(y)\not=\varnothing
for all
y\in Y
, where
\partial^{c}\psi(y):=\{x\in X:\psi(\cdot)-c(x,\cdot)\ \text{attains maximum at}\ y\}
.

(3)

There exists
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
such that
\psi=T^{-}\phi
.

(4)

\psi
is
c
-concave.

The problem of singularities of semiconcave functions plays an important role in calculus of variation and optimal control, PDE, Hamiltonian dynamical systems and geometry. For convex Lagrangian on Euclidean space or finite dimensional manifold, the maximal regularity of the associated value function is semiconcavity. More precisely, for any Tonelli Lagrangian
L:\mathbb{R}\times TM\to\mathbb{R}
with
M
an
d
-dimensional smooth manifold and a function
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
, let

\displaystyle u(t,x):=\inf_{\gamma}\left\{\phi(\gamma(0))+\int^{t}_{0}L(s,\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\ ds\right\},\qquad t>0,x\in M,

where
\gamma:[0,t]\to M
is taken over the set of absolutely continuous curves such that
\gamma(t)=x
. Then the function
u
is locally semiconcave on
(0,+\infty)\times M
. We say a point
(t,x)\in(0,+\infty)\times M
a singular point of
u
if
u
is not differentiable at
(t,x)
. We denote by
\text{Sing}\,(u)
the set of all singular points of
u
. If
u
is only a locally semiconcave function on
M
, we also denote the singular set of
u
by
\text{Sing}\,(u)
.

There is a large amount of literatures on the structure of
\text{Sing}\,(u)
from topological, measure theoretic and even to dynamical aspects. For a survey the readers can refer to
[
14
]
. The rectifiability result of
\text{Sing}\,(u)
plays an important role in the solvability issue of Monge problem in optimal transport problem, for cost function which is the squared distance on Euclidean space or Riemannian manifold. However, for general cost functions, the relation between
\text{Sing}^{c}(\psi)
and the solution of relevant Monge problem is much more complicated. In
[
7
]
, the authors discuss this problem under rather general conditions. However, invoking our recent work on the Lax-Oleinik commutators with applications to the singularities in the context of weak KAM theory, we realize it is useful to understand
\text{Sing}^{c}(\psi)
and their dynamical nature.

In the context of weak KAM theory, we suppose
X=Y=M
with
M
a compact and connected manifold without boundary. Given any
x,y\in M
, and
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we denote by
\Gamma_{x,y}^{t_{1},t_{2}}
the set of absolutely continuous curves
\gamma\in\text{AC}([t_{1},t_{2}],M)
with
\gamma(t_{1})=x
and
\gamma(t_{2})=y
. Recall that

\displaystyle h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{x,y}^{t_{1},t_{2}}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(s,\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\,ds,\qquad t_{1}<t_{2},\quad x,y\in M,

and
h(x,y)=\liminf_{t_{2}-t_{1}\to\infty}h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
is the Peierls’ barrier with a time-independent Lagrangian
L
and the Mañé’s critical value is
0
.

To study the singularities of
c
-concave functions, we will consider three types of cost functions in different cases:
c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
with
t_{2}-t_{1}\ll 1
,
c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
with finite
t_{1}<t_{2}
and
c(x,y)=h(x,y)
.

A key observation for the first case is due to a theorem by Marie-Claude Arnaud (
[
6
]
) on the evolution of the 1-graph of semiconcave functions under Hamiltonian flow for short time. In this case, we conclude
\text{\rm Sing}^{c}(\psi)=\text{\rm Sing}\,(\psi)
(Proposition
3.12
). In the case with finite
t_{1}<t_{2}
, one can only have the inclusion
\text{Sing}^{c}(\psi)\subset\text{Sing}\,(\psi)
(Proposition
3.14
). For the case
c(x,y)=h(x,y)
and
u^{-}
is a weak KAM solution, we prove for any
y\in M
, there exists a static class
\mathcal{A}_{y}\subset\partial^{c}u^{-}(y)
(Proposition
3.15
). Recall that the static class is the equivalence class determined by the pseudo-distance
d(x,y)=h(x,y)+h(y,x)
on the Aubry set
\mathcal{A}
(
[
25
,
21
]
). As a consequence, if each static class is not a singleton, then
\mbox{\rm Sing}^{c}(u^{-})=M
.

1.2.
An alternative formulation of Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality

Let
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
. Set
c_{\phi}(x,y):=\phi(x)+c(x,y)
and
c^{\psi}(x,y):=\psi(y)-c(x,y)
. For any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(Y)
, we try to find a function
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and a function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(K
-
)

\displaystyle\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi\ d\nu=

\displaystyle\,\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c_{\phi}(x,y)\ d\pi,

(K
+
)

\displaystyle\int_{X}T^{+}\psi\ d\mu=

\displaystyle\,\sup_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c^{\psi}(x,y)\ d\pi.

In terms on abstract Lax-Oleinik operators, we also proved the equivalence of the problems (
K-
) and (
K+
) and well known Rubinstein-Kantorovich duality (see Theorem
4.2
). More precisely,

–

if
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution of (
K-
), then
(\phi,T^{-}\phi)
is a solution of Kantorovich problem. Conversely, if
(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of Kantorovich problem, then
\phi
solves (
K-
);

–

if
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution of (
K+
), then
(T^{+}\psi,\psi)
is a solution of Kantorovich problem. Conversely, if
(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of Kantorovich problem, then
\psi
solves (
K+
),

where

\displaystyle I_{c}:=\{(\phi,\psi):\psi(y)-\phi(x)\leqslant c(x,y)\ \text{for all}\ x\in X,y\in Y\}.

Invoking the equivalence above between problem (
K-
) (or (
K+
)) and the Rubinstein-Kantorovich duality, it is useful to refine the analysis of the dynamical nature of the associated optimal transport problem initiated by Bernard and Buffoni (
[

,

,

]
).

To study the singularities in the optimal transport, we have to introduce some “Random” Lax-Oleinik operators (Definition

, a connected closed manifold,

is the fundamental solution of the associated Hamilton-Jacobi equation and the terminal marginal measure

is Dirac. The usual deterministic optimal transport problem can be understood well in terms of the associated Hamiltonian flow. Our main result (Proposition

) in this case is: let

,
\nu=\delta_{y_{0}}
,

and
\{\Phi_{H}^{t_{1},t_{2}}\}_{t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}}
is the associated Hamiltonian flow from

to

.

(1)

For any
\rho\in\mathscr{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\mathrm{supp}\,(\rho)\subset D^{*}T^{-}\phi(y_{0})

1

For a semiconcave function

,

is the set of reachable gradients.
, there exists
\mu_{\rho}:=(p_{x}\circ\Phi_{H}^{t,0})_{\#}(\delta_{y_{0}}\times\rho)
, which makes (

) holds true;

(2)

For each

1.3.
Random Lax-Oleinik operators

The introduction of random Lax-Oleinik operators is a key step for the problem of cut locus and propagation of singularities in optimal transport. We use the word “random” to clarify the essential difference of the stochastic setting of this problem, where sample paths of the relevant processes is not absolutely continuos.

For a metric space
(X,d)
, let
(\mathscr{P}_{p}(X),W_{p})
be the
p
-Wasserstein space for
p\in[1,+\infty)
, where
W_{p}
is the
p
-order Wasserstein metric. Let
s,t\in\mathbb{R}
with
s<t
,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(Y)
. We follow the setting of
[
27
]
. The dynamical cost functional associated to
c^{s,t}
is

\displaystyle C^{s,t}(\mu,\nu):=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c^{s,t}(x,y)\,d\pi=\inf_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathrm{law}(x)=\mu\\
\mathrm{law}(y)=\nu\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}(c^{s,t}(x,y)),

where
x(\omega)
and
y(\omega)
in the last term are
X
-valued and
Y
-valued random variables of some probability space
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
respectively.

Given
\phi\in L^{0}(X;(-\infty,+\infty])
which is bounded from below, associated with
\phi
we define a functional of
\mathscr{P}_{p}(X)
, called
the potential energy associated to
\phi
(see
[
4
, Definition 4.30]
or
[
5
, Example 9.3.1]
),

\displaystyle\phi(\mu):=\int_{X}\phi\,d\mu,\qquad\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{p}(X).

Let
X=Y=M
and
c^{s,t}(x,y):=h(s,t,x,y)
, the fundamental solution from weak KAM theory. For any function
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to[-\infty,+\infty]
,
t>0
,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, we define

\displaystyle P_{t}^{-}\phi(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\inf_{\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{\phi(\nu)+C^{0,t}(\nu,\mu)\},

\displaystyle P_{t}^{+}\phi(\mu)

\displaystyle:=\sup_{\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\{\phi(\nu)-C^{0,t}(\mu,\nu)\}.

We call
P_{t}^{\pm}\phi
the positive and negative
random Lax-Oleinik operator
.

Our main result is: suppose
\phi
is a uniformly continuous function on
M
,
t>0
. Then, for any
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(M)
the operator
P_{t}^{-}\phi(\nu)
is finite-valued, there exist
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(M)
and
\xi\in L_{\mu,\nu}^{0,t}
such that

\displaystyle P_{t}^{-}\phi(\nu)

\displaystyle=\phi(\mu)+C^{0,t}(\mu,\nu)

\displaystyle=\int_{\Omega}\phi(\xi(0,\omega))+\int_{0}^{t}L(\xi(s,\omega),\dot{\xi}(s,\omega))\,dsd\mathbb{P}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\int_{T\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)+L(x,v)\,d\tilde{\mu}_{s}ds,

where
\tilde{\mu}_{s}:=\mathrm{law\,}(\xi(s,\cdot),\dot{\xi}(s,\cdot))
is determined by the associated Euler-Lagrangian flow. Moreover,
P_{t}^{-}\phi(\nu)=T_{t}^{-}\phi(\nu)
for any
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(M)
, i.e., for any
\nu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(M)
, there exists
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(M)
solving (
K-
).

We remark finally if the Lagrangian
L
is a Riemannian metric, the associated “Random” Hopf-Lax semigroup has already been widely used in the community of optimal transport (see, for instance,
[
3
]
). However, we emphasize the abstract and random Lax-Oleinik operators for general Lagrangian is useful for our program to deal with the problem of propagation of singularities based on our intrinsic approach of this problem in the context of calculus of variation (
[
15
,
13
,
16
]
).

The paper is organized as follows. In section 2, we introduce some necessary facts from weak KAM theory, calculus of variation and optimal transport. In Section 3, we define the abstract negative and positive Lax-Oleinik operators
T^{\pm}
and characterize the conditions
T^{-}\circ T^{+}=Id
and
T^{+}\circ T^{-}=Id
. We also discuss the relations between the singular set of semiconcave functions and
c
-concave function in the context of weak KAM theory when
c
is the fundamental solutions and Peierls’ barrier. Section 4 is composed of three parts. We obtain an equivalent form of the Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality using the Lax-Oleinik operators
T^{\pm}
. We also discuss some basic problems of optimal transport in the frame of Lax-Oleinik operators
T^{\pm}
. In the last subsection, we introduce the random Lax-Oleinik operators and discuss some fundamental properties of these operators in frame of abstract Lax-Oleinik operators. We give the proof of a possible known lemma in the appendix.

Preliminaries

2.1.
Semiconcave functions

We first recall some basic relevant notions on semiconcavity.

–

Let
\Omega
be an open convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A function
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is called a semiconcave function (of linear modulus) with constant
C\geqslant 0
if for any
x\in\Omega
there exists
p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that

(2.1)

\phi(y)\leqslant\phi(x)+\langle p,y-x\rangle+\frac{C}{2}|x-y|^{2},\qquad\forall y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

–

The set of covectors
p
satisfying (
2.1
) is called the proximal superdifferential of
\phi
at
x
and we denote it by
D^{+}\phi(x)
.

–

Similarly,
\phi
is semiconvex if
-\phi
is semiconcave. The set of
D^{-}\phi(x)=-D^{+}(-\phi)(x)
is called the proximal subdifferential of
\phi
at
x
.

–

The set
D^{+}\phi(x)
is a singleton if and only if
\phi
is differentiable at
x
, and
D^{+}\phi(x)=\{D\phi(x)\}
. A point
x
is called a singular point of a semiconcave function
\phi
if
D^{+}\phi(x)
is not a singleton. We denote by
\text{Sing}\,(\phi)
the set of all singular points of
\phi
.

–

We call
p\in D^{*}\phi(x)
, the set of reachable gradients, if there exists a sequence
x_{k}\to x
as
k\to\infty
,
\phi
is differentiable at each
x_{k}
and
p=\lim_{k\to\infty}D\phi(x_{k})
. We have
D^{*}\phi(x)\subset D^{+}\phi(x)
and
D^{+}\phi(x)=\text{co}\,D^{*}\phi(x)
.

For more on the semiconcave functions, the readers can refer to
[
19
]
.

Proposition 2.1
.

A function
\phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
is a semiconcave function with constant
C\geqslant 0
if and only if there exists a family of
C^{2}
-functions
\{\phi_{i}\}
with
D^{2}\phi_{i}\leqslant CI
such that

\displaystyle\phi=\inf_{i}\phi_{i}.

Proposition
2.1
is an important and useful characterization of semiconcavity. Let
S
be a compact topological space and
F:S\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a continuous function such that

(a)

F(s,\cdot)
is of class
C^{2}
for all
s\in S
and
\|F(s,\cdot)\|_{C^{2}}
is uniformly bounded by some constant
C
,

(b)

D_{x}F(s,x)
is continuous on
S\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

(c)

\phi(x)=\inf\{F(s,x):s\in S\}
.

We call such a function
\phi
a marginal function of a family of
C^{2}
-functions.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\phi(x)=\inf\{F(s,x):s\in S\}
be a marginal functions of the family of
C^{2}
-functions
\{F(s,\cdot)\}_{s\in S}
. Let
M(x)=\arg\min\{F(s,x):s\in S\}
.

(1)

\phi
is semiconcave with constant
C
.

(2)

D^{*}\phi(x)\subset Y(x)=\{D_{x}F(s,x):s\in M(x)\}
.

(3)

For each
x

\displaystyle D^{+}\phi(x)=\begin{cases}D_{x}F(s,x),&M(x)=\{s\}\ \text{is a singleton};\\
\mbox{\rm co}\,\{D_{x}F(s,x):s\in M(x)\},&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Remark 2.3
.

If
M
is a connected and compact smooth manifold without boundary, a function
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
is called semiconcave if there exists a family of
C^{2}
-functions
\{\phi_{i}\}
such that
\phi=\inf_{i}\phi_{i}
, and the Hessians of
\phi_{i}
’s are uniformly bounded above. The readers can refer to the appendix of the paper
[
23
]
for more discussion of the semiconcavity of functions on (even noncompact) manifold. However, because of the local nature of our main discussion, we will work on Euclidean space instead.

2.2.
Fundamental solutions and Lax-Oleinik representation

A function
L=L(t,x,v):\mathbb{R}\times TM\to\mathbb{R}
is called time-dependent
Tonelli Lagrangian
, if it is a function of class
C^{2}
and satisfies the following conditions:

(L1)

The function
v\mapsto L(t,x,v)
is strictly convex for all
(t,x)\in\mathbb{R}\times M
.

(L2)

There exist a superlinear function
\theta:[0,+\infty)\to[0,+\infty)
and
L^{\infty}_{\rm loc}
function
c_{0}:\mathbb{R}\to[0,+\infty]
, such that

\displaystyle L(t,x,v)\geqslant\theta(|v|_{x})-c_{0},\qquad\forall(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times TM.

(L3)

There exist
C_{1},C_{2}>0
, such that

\displaystyle|L_{t}(t,x,v)|\leqslant C_{1}+C_{2}L(t,x,v),\qquad\forall(t,x,v)\in\mathbb{R}\times TM.

We call the Lagrangian
H
associated with
L
a
Tonelli Hamiltonian
, defined by

\displaystyle H(t,x,p)=\sup_{v\in T_{x}M}\{p(v)-L(t,x,v)\},\qquad(t,x,p)\in\mathbb{R}\times T^{*}M.

Given any
x,y\in M
, and
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we denote by
\Gamma_{x,y}^{t_{1},t_{2}}
the set of absolutely continuous curves
\gamma\in\text{AC}([t_{1},t_{2}],M)
with
\gamma(t_{1})=x
and
\gamma(t_{2})=y
.

Definition 2.4
.

Given a Tonelli Hamiltonian
H
with
L
the associated Tonelli Lagrangian. We call the function
h=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y):\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times M\times M\to\mathbb{R}
the
fundamental solution
of the associated Hamilton-Jacobi equation, defined by

\displaystyle h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y):=\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma_{x,y}^{t_{1},t_{2}}}\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}L(s,\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\,ds,\qquad t_{1}<t_{2},\quad x,y\in M.

Definition 2.5
.

Given a Tonelli Hamiltonian
H
with
L
the associated Tonelli Lagrangian. For any
\phi:M\to\mathbb{R}
,
x\in M
, and
t_{1}<t_{2}
, we define

\displaystyle T_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\phi(x):=\inf_{y\in M}\{\phi(y)+h(t_{1},t_{2},y,x)\},

\displaystyle\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\phi(x):=\sup_{y\in M}\{\phi(y)-h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)\}.

The families
\{T_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\}
and
\{\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\}
of operators are called
negative and positive Lax-Oleinik evolution
respectively.

Remark 2.6
.

If
\phi\in\text{\rm BUC}\,(M)
, the set of (bounded) uniformly continuous functions on
M
, both
\{T_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\}
and
\{\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\}
satisfy Markov property. Moreover, if
L
is independent of
t
, then both of them are semigroups on
\text{\rm BUC}\,(M)
. We denote
T_{t}^{-}:=T_{0}^{t}
and
T_{t}^{+}:=\breve{T}_{0}^{t}
for brevity.

3.
Optimal transport and singularities of
c
-concave function

Let
X
and
Y
be two Polish spaces and
c:X\times Y\to\mathbb{R}
. The function
c
is called
cost function
. The problem when
c=h(t_{1},t_{2},\cdot,\cdot)
for some fixed
t_{1}<t_{2}
has very natural feature. The basic relation among optimal transport, Hamilton-Jacobi equations, Mather theory and weak KAM theory in this context has been understood by many authors (
[
9
,
10
,
23
]
). Main part of this section is motivated by our recent work on the Lax-Oleinik commutators and singularities. We begin with the analysis without touching the measure theoretic aspect of the theory at first.

3.1.
Abstract Lax-Oleinik commutators

Definition 3.1
.

For any
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
we define the
abstract Lax-Oleinik operators

\displaystyle T^{-}\phi(y):=\inf_{x\in X}\{\phi(x)+c(x,y)\},\qquad T^{+}\psi(x):=\sup_{y\in Y}\{\psi(y)-c(x,y)\},\qquad x\in X,y\in Y.

We always assume the infimum and supremum in the definitions of
T^{-}\phi(y)
and
T^{+}\psi(x)
can be achieved respectively.

Definition 3.2
.

A function
\psi:Y\to[-\infty,+\infty)
is said to be
c
-concave
if it is the infimum of a family of functions
c(x,\cdot)+\alpha(x)
. Analogously,
\phi:X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
is said to be
c
-convex
if it is the supreme of a family of functions
\beta(y)-c(\cdot,y)
.

It is clear that
T^{-}\phi
is
c
-concave and
T^{+}\psi
is
c
-convex.

Definition 3.3
.

Given
\phi:X\to(-\infty,+\infty]
and
\phi(x)<+\infty
we define the
c
-subdifferential as

\displaystyle\partial_{c}\phi(x):=\{y\in Y:\phi(\cdot)+c(\cdot,y)\ \text{attains minimum at}\ x\}.

Analogously, given
\psi:Y\to[-\infty,+\infty)
and
\psi(y)>-\infty
we define the
c
-superdifferential as

\displaystyle\partial^{c}\psi(y):=\{x\in X:\psi(\cdot)-c(x,\cdot)\ \text{attains maximum at}\ y\}.

Now, we will concentrate on the Lax-Oleinik commutators
T^{-}\circ T^{+}
and
T^{+}\circ T^{-}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi\geqslant\psi
and
T^{+}\circ T^{-}\phi\leqslant\phi
.

(2)

T^{-}\circ T^{+}\circ T^{-}\phi=T^{-}\phi
and
T^{+}\circ T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi=T^{+}\psi
. In particular, both
T^{-}\circ T^{+}
and
T^{-}\circ T^{+}
are idempotent.

Proof.

From the definition we have

\displaystyle T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi(y)=

\displaystyle\,\inf_{x\in X}\{\sup_{z\in Y}(\psi(z)-c(x,z))+c(x,y)\}\geqslant\inf_{x\in X}\{\psi(y)-c(x,y)+c(x,y)\}=\psi(y),

\displaystyle T^{+}\circ T^{-}\phi(x)=

\displaystyle\,\sup_{y\in Y}\{\inf_{z\in X}(\phi(z)+c(z,y))-c(x,y)\}\leqslant\sup_{y\in Y}\{\phi(x)+c(x,y)-c(x,y)\}=\phi(x),

and (1) follows. By (1) we observe

\displaystyle T^{-}\phi\leqslant T^{-}\circ T^{+}\circ T^{-}\phi\leqslant T^{-}\phi.

Thus, the inequalities above are equalities and this completes the proof of the first equality in (2). The proof of the rest of (2) is similar.
∎

Lemma 3.5
.

(1)

For any
y\in Y
,
T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi(y)=\psi(y)
if and only if
\partial^{c}\psi(y)\not=\varnothing
.

(2)

For any
x\in X
,
T^{+}\circ T^{-}\phi(x)=\phi(x)
if and only if
\partial_{c}\phi(x)\not=\varnothing
.

Proof.

We only prove (1) since the proof of (2) is similar. If
\partial^{c}\psi(y)\not=\varnothing
, then there exists
x\in X
such that
T^{+}\psi(x)=\psi(y)-c(x,y)
. Thus,

\displaystyle\psi(y)=T^{+}\psi(x)+c(x,y)\geqslant T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi(y),

and we have
T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi(y)=\psi(y)
since the converse inequality holds automatically by Lemma
3.4
. Now, suppose
T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi(y)=\psi(y)
, then there exists
x\in X
such that
\psi(y)=T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi(y)=T^{+}\psi(x)+c(x,y)
. This implies
\partial^{c}\psi(y)\not=\varnothing
.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

The following statements are equivalent.

(1)

T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi=\psi
(resp.
T^{+}\circ T^{-}\phi=\phi
).

(2)

\partial^{c}\psi(y)\not=\varnothing
(resp.
\partial_{c}\phi(x)\not=\varnothing
) for all
y\in Y
(resp.
x\in X
).

(3)

There exists
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
(resp.
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
) such that
\psi=T^{-}\phi
(resp.
\phi=T^{+}\psi
).

(4)

\psi
(resp.
\phi
) is
c
-concave (resp.
c
-conconvex).

Proof.

The equivalence between (1) and (2) is immediately from Lemma
3.5
. The equivalence between (1) and (3) can be obtained by Lemma
3.4
(2).

If
T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi=\psi
then
\psi
is
c
-concave by definition. Now, suppose
\psi
is
c
-concave which is the infimum of a family of
c
-affine functions
c(x,\cdot)+\beta(x)
. That means
\psi=T^{-}\beta
. This leads to
T^{-}\circ T^{+}\psi=\psi
by the equivalence of (1) and (4).
∎

3.2.
Singularities of
c
-concave functions

Definition 3.7
.

For any
c
-concave function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
,
y\in Y
is called a
regular point
of
\psi
if
\partial^{c}\psi(y)
is a singleton and is called
singular point
of
\psi
if
\partial^{c}\psi(y)
is not a singleton. For any
c
-concave function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
, we denote by
\text{Sing}^{c}(\psi)
the set of all singular points of
\psi
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Suppose
c
is continuous. Then for any continuous
c
-concave function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
, the set-valued map
y\mapsto\partial^{c}\psi(y)
is upper-semicontinuous.

Proof.

Suppose
\{y_{k}\}\subset Y
is a sequence such that
\lim_{k\to\infty}y_{k}=y
. Let
x_{k}\in\partial^{c}\psi(y_{k})
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, such that
x_{k}\to x
as
k\to\infty
, i.e.,

\displaystyle T^{+}\psi(x_{k})=\psi(y_{k})-c(x_{k},y_{k}),\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Since
T^{+}\psi
is lower-semicontinuous we have

\displaystyle T^{+}\psi(x)\geqslant\psi(y)-c(x,y)=\liminf_{k\to\infty}T^{+}\psi(x_{k})\geqslant T^{+}\psi(x).

Thus
T^{+}\psi(x)=\psi(y)-c(x,y)
and this implies
x\in\partial^{c}\psi(y)
.
∎

Definition 3.9
.

A point
x\in\partial^{c}\psi(y)
is called a
c
-reachable gradient of
\psi
at
y
if there exists
y_{k}\to y
as
k\to\infty
, each
\partial^{c}\psi(y_{k})
is a singleton, say
\partial^{c}\psi(y_{k})=\{x_{k}\}
, and
\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=x
. We denote by
\partial^{*}_{c}\psi(y)\subset\partial^{c}\psi(y)
the set of
c
-reachable gradients of
\psi
at
y
.

For the application of the singularities of
c
-concave functions to optimal transport, we need clarify the difference of the singularities between usual semiconcave function and
c
-concave function, even when
c
is semiconcave. For the rectifiability property of the singular set
\partial^{c}\psi
of a
c
-concave function under more general assumptions, the readers can refer to
[
7
]
. We will discuss this problem for three types of specific cost functions from weak KAM theory:

1.

c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
with
t_{2}-t_{1}\ll 1
;

2.

c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
with finite
t_{2}-t_{1}
;

3.

c(x,y)=h(x,y)
with
h
the Peierls’ barrier.

Let
M
be a connected and closed manifold and
\text{SCL}\,(M)
be the set of semiconcave functions on
M
. Now let us consider the case on the time-independent Lagrangian
L
and Hamiltonian
H
. For any
\phi\in\text{SCL}\,(M)
, set

\displaystyle t_{\phi}:=

\displaystyle\,\sup\{t>0:T^{+}_{t}\phi\in C^{1,1}(M)\},

where
T_{t}^{+}\phi:=\breve{T}_{0}^{t}\phi
. Moreover, we denote by

\displaystyle\text{graph}\,(D^{+}\phi):=

\displaystyle\,\{(x,p)\in T^{*}M:x\in M,p\in D^{+}\phi(x)\},

\displaystyle\text{graph}\,(DT_{t}^{+}\phi):=

\displaystyle\,\{(x,DT_{t}^{+}\phi(x))\in T^{*}M:x\in M\}.

We call the set
\text{\rm graph}\,(D^{+}\phi)
the
pseudo-graph
of
D\phi
or
1-graph
of
\phi
. Now, we recall a result by Marie-Claude Arnaud (
[
6
]
) at first.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\phi\in\text{\rm SCL}\,(M)
. Then
t_{\phi}>0
, and when
0<t<t_{\phi}
,
T_{t}^{+}\phi\in C^{1,1}(M)
. Furthermore, for such
t\in(0,t_{\phi}]
,

(3.1)

\text{\rm graph}\,(DT_{t}^{+}\phi)=\Phi_{H}^{-t}(\text{\rm graph}\,(D^{+}\phi)),

where
\{\Phi^{s}_{H}\}_{s\in\mathbb{R}}
is the Hamiltonian flow with respect to any Tonelli Hamiltonian
H:T^{*}M\to\mathbb{R}
.

Remark 3.11
.

The original proof of Arnaud’s theorem is based on some approximation in the sense of Hausdorff distance of the graph for time-independent Lagrangian (see also
[
26
]
for the case of time dependent Lagrangian). The relation (
3.1
) defines a diffeomorphism and bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism between
\text{\rm graph}\,(D^{+}\phi)
and
\text{\rm graph}\,(DT^{+}_{t}\phi)
by the Hamiltonian flow. This allows us to understand the singularities of
\phi
by means of the latter Lipschitz graph. Except for some finer results obtain in
[
1
]
, this idea also leads to more topological results of the singular set of semiconcave functions and
c
-concave functions from the theory of optimal transport (
[
26
]
).

Since Proposition
3.10
only holds for short time
t_{2}-t_{1}\ll 1
, we have to consider our problem for
c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
in the following three cases:
t_{2}-t_{1}\ll 1
,
t_{2}-t_{1}
is finite and
t_{2}-t_{1}\to\infty
.

3.2.1.
c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
with
t_{2}-t_{1}\ll 1

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
M
be a connected closed manifold and
\psi:M\to\mathbb{R}
be a
c
-concave function. Then
\psi
be semiconcave in usual sense. For the cost function
c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
,
x,y\in M
, with
0<t_{2}-t_{1}<t_{\psi}
as in Proposition
3.10
, there exists a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism between
\partial^{c}\psi(y)
and
D^{+}\psi(y)
for all
y\in M
. In particular,

\displaystyle\mbox{\rm Sing}^{c}(\psi)=\mbox{\rm Sing}\,(\psi),

where
\mbox{\rm Sing}\,(\psi)
is the set of the points of non-differentiability of
\psi
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.10
(see also
[
26
, Theorem 3.14]
), we have

\displaystyle\Phi_{H}^{t_{1},t_{2}}(\text{\rm graph}\,(D\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi))=\text{\rm graph}\,(D^{+}\psi),

where
\{\Phi^{t_{1},t_{2}}_{H}\}
is the non-autonomous Hamiltonian flow from
t_{1}
to
t_{2}
. Given
y\in M
, and
\forall p\in D^{+}\psi(y)
we denote by
(\gamma(\cdot),p(\cdot))
the Hamiltonian trajectory determined by endpoint condition
(\gamma(t_{2}),p(t_{2}))=(y,p)
. Recalling that
p(t_{2})=L_{v}(t_{2},\gamma(t_{2}),\dot{\gamma}(t_{2}))\in D^{+}\psi(y)
, it follows
0\in D^{+}(\psi(y)-h(t_{1},t_{2},\gamma(t_{1}),y))
by Fermat rule, or equivalently that
y
is a maximizer of the function
\phi(\cdot)-h(t_{1},t_{2},\gamma(t_{1}),\cdot)
. Therefore, for
x=\gamma(t_{1})
we have
\breve{T}^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\psi(x)=\psi(y)-h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
which implies
x\in\partial^{c}\psi(y)
since
D\breve{T}^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\psi(x)=p(t_{1})=L_{v}(t_{1},\gamma(t_{1}),\dot{\gamma}(t_{1}))
. The converse direction can be handled in a similar way. For any
y\in M
, set

\displaystyle\Gamma(y):=\{(x,p):x\in\partial^{c}\psi(y),p=D\breve{T}^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}\psi(x)\}\subset\text{\rm graph}\,(D\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi).

Then, this leads to the relation

\displaystyle(y,D^{+}\psi(y))=\Phi_{H}^{t_{1},t_{2}}(\Gamma(y)),\qquad y\in M.

Notice that
\text{\rm graph}\,(D\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi)
is the graph of a Lipschitz function. This shows there is a bi-Lipschitz homeomorphism between
\partial^{c}\psi(y)
and
D^{+}\psi(y)
for each
y\in M
.
∎

3.2.2.
c(x,y)=h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
with finite
t_{2}-t_{1}

Lemma 3.13
.

Let
S
be a compact topological space and
F:S\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Suppose
F
is continuous and
F(s,\cdot)
is uniformly semiconcave with constant
C\geqslant 0
for
s\in S
. Set
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\displaystyle u(x)=\inf\{F(s,x):s\in S\},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

For any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let
M(x)=\{s\in S:F(s,x)=u(x)\}
. Then
u
is semiconcave with constant
C
and
D^{+}u(x)=\mbox{\rm co}\,\{D^{+}_{x}F(s,x):s\in M(x)\}
.

Lemma
3.13
is a refinement of Proposition
2.2
. We give a proof in the appendix.

Proposition 3.14
.

Under the same assumption of Proposition
3.12
except for that
0<t_{2}-t_{1}<t_{\psi}
. Then the following statements hold.

(1)

D^{+}\psi(y)
is a singleton implies
\partial^{c}\psi(y)
is a singleton. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Sing}^{c}(\psi)\subset\mathrm{Sing}\,(\psi).

(2)

There exists a Lipschitz map from
D^{*}\psi(y)
to
\partial^{*}_{c}\psi(y)
.

Proof.

Let
y\in M
. We suppose
y
is a point of differentiability of
\psi
. Since
T_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\circ\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi=\psi
, then
h(t_{1},t_{2},x,\cdot)
is differentiable at
y
and
D_{y}h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
coincides for any
x\in\arg\min\{\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi(\cdot)+h(t_{1},t_{2},\cdot,y)\}
by Lemma
3.13
. Recall that each
D_{y}h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)=L_{v}(t_{2},\gamma_{x}(t_{2}),\dot{\gamma}_{x}(t_{2}))
with
\gamma_{x}\in\Gamma^{t_{1},t_{2}}_{x,y}
the unique minimizer for
h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
. Since each
\gamma_{x}
satisfies the same endpoint condition

\displaystyle\gamma_{x}(t_{2})=y,\qquad\dot{\gamma}_{x}(t_{2})=H_{p}(t_{2},y,D_{y}h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)),

we conclude that there exists a unique
x\in M
such that

\displaystyle\psi(y)=T_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\circ\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi(y)=\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi(x)+h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)

and
D\psi(y)=D_{y}h(t_{1},t_{2},x,y)
. The first part of (1) implies
\text{Sing}^{c}(\psi)\subset\text{Sing}\,(\psi)
. This completes the proof of (1).

Now, suppose
p\in D^{*}\psi(y)
. Then there exists a sequence
y_{k}\to y
as
k\to\infty
,
\psi
is differentiable at each
y_{k}
and
p=\lim_{k\to\infty}D\psi(y_{k})
. From the first part of the proof, for each
k
there exists a unique
x_{k}
such that
\partial^{c}\psi(y_{k})=\{x_{k}\}
. As in the proof of the first part, let
\gamma_{x_{k}}\in\Gamma^{t_{1},t_{2}}_{x_{k},y_{k}}
be the unique minimal curve for
h(t_{1},t_{2},x_{k},y_{k})
. Applying Ascoli-Arzela theorem, any convergent subsequence of
\{\gamma_{x_{k}}\}
has a limiting curve
\gamma\in\Gamma^{t_{1},t_{2}}_{x,y}
under
C^{2}
-topology satisfying the same endpoint condition
\gamma(t_{2})=y
and
L_{v}(t_{2},\gamma(t_{2}),\dot{\gamma}(t_{2}))=p
. This implies the sequence
\{\gamma_{x_{k}}\}
converges a curve
\gamma:[t_{1},t_{2}]\to M
connecting
x
to
y
. By continuity, we have

\displaystyle\psi(y)=\breve{T}_{t_{1}}^{t_{2}}\psi(\gamma(0))+\int^{t_{2}}_{t_{1}}L(s,\gamma(s),\dot{\gamma}(s))\ ds.

This implies
\lim_{k\to\infty}x_{k}=x
and
x\in\partial^{*}_{c}\psi(y)
. Let
i
be the inclusion from
D^{*}\psi(y)
to the graph
\{(y,p):p\in D^{*}\psi(y)\}
. We finish the proof by observing that
\pi_{x}\circ\Phi^{t_{1},t_{2}}_{H}\circ i
defines a Lipschitz map from
D^{*}\psi(y)
to
\partial^{*}_{c}\psi(y)
.
∎

3.2.3.
c(x,y)=h(x,y)
with
h
the Peierls’ barrier

Now, consider the case when
L
is time-independent and the cost function
c(x,y)=h(x,y)
, where
h(x,y)=\liminf_{t\to\infty}h(0,t,x,y)+c[0]t
is the Peierls’ barrier (
[
22
]
). In
[
25
]
, Mather introduced a pseudo-distance on the Aubry set. We suppose
c[0]=0
and let

\displaystyle d(x,y)=h(x,y)+h(y,x),\qquad\forall x,y\in\mathcal{A}.

Mather proved
d
is a pseudo-distance on
\mathcal{A}
and each equivalence class of the relation
d(x,y)=0
is called a static class (see more details in
[
21
]
).

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
L
be a time-independent Tonelli Lagrangian,
u^{-}
be a weak KAM solution and and
c=h
with
h
the Peierls’ barrier. Then, for any
y\in M
, there exists a static class
\mathcal{A}_{y}\subset\partial^{c}u^{-}(y)
.

Proof.

Recall that
u^{-}=T^{-}_{t}\circ T^{+}_{t}u^{-}
for all
t\geqslant 0
(
[
17
]
), where
T_{t}^{-}\phi:=T_{0}^{t}\phi
. Fix
y\in M
and let
p\in D^{*}u^{-}(y)=D^{*}T^{-}_{t}\circ T^{+}_{t}u^{-}(y)
. Then there exists
\gamma_{t}:[-t,0]\to M
with
\gamma_{t}(0)=y
and
L_{v}(\gamma_{t}(0),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(0))=p
such that

\displaystyle u^{-}(y)=T^{-}_{t}\circ T^{+}_{t}u^{-}(y)=T^{+}_{t}u^{-}(\gamma_{t}(-t))+\int^{0}_{-t}L(\gamma_{t}(s),\dot{\gamma}_{t}(s))\ ds=T^{+}_{t}u^{-}(\gamma_{t}(-t))+h(-t,0,\gamma_{t}(-t),y).

Observe that since all the curves
\gamma_{t}
are extremals with the same endpoint condition. Thus, there exists
\gamma:(-\infty,0]\to M
with
\gamma(0)=y
and
L_{v}(\gamma(0),\dot{\gamma}(0))=p
such that
\gamma_{t}=\gamma|_{[-t,0]}
. From classical weak KAM theory we know that
T^{+}_{t}u^{-}
converges uniformly to
u^{+}
, the conjugate function of
u^{-}
, as
t\to+\infty
. Then, for any limiting point
x
of
\gamma(-t)
as
t\to+\infty
, we have

\displaystyle u^{-}(y)=u^{+}(x)+h(x,y).

Such points
x\in\mathcal{A}
, the projected Aubry set.

For any
x,x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
belonging in the same static class we have

\displaystyle h(x,x^{\prime})+h(x^{\prime},x)=0,

and

\displaystyle u^{+}(x^{\prime})-u^{+}(x)=h(x,x^{\prime}).

Therefore, if
x\in\mathcal{A}
is given in the first part of the proof, which satisfies
u^{+}(x)=u^{-}(y)-h(x,y)
, then for any
x^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}
belonging in the same static class as
x
we have

\displaystyle u^{+}(x^{\prime})=u^{+}(x)+h(x,x^{\prime})=u^{-}(y)-h(x,y)-h(x^{\prime},x).

By the property of Peierls’ barrier

\displaystyle h(x^{\prime},y)\leqslant h(x^{\prime},x)+h(x,y).

It follows

\displaystyle u^{+}(x^{\prime})\leqslant u^{-}(y)-h(x^{\prime},y),\qquad\text{or}\qquad u^{-}(y)-u^{+}(x^{\prime})\geqslant h(x^{\prime},y).

The second inequality is indeed an equality because
(u^{-},u^{+})
is an admissible Kantorovich pair. This completes the proof.
∎

The following corollary is immediate by Proposition
3.15
and the definition of
\text{Sing}^{c}(u^{-})
.

Corollary 3.16
.

If each static class is not a singleton, then
\mbox{\rm Sing}^{c}(u^{-})=M
.

4.
Application of abstract Lax-Oleinik operators to optimal transport

4.1.
An alternative formulation of Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality

For any Polish space
X
let
\mathscr{P}(X)
be the space of Borel probability measures on
X
. Given two Polish spaces
X
and
Y
, we call
\pi\in\mathscr{P}(X\times Y)
a transport plan between
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
if

\displaystyle(p_{X})_{\#}\pi=\mu,\qquad(p_{Y})_{\#}\pi=\nu

where
p_{X}
and
p_{Y}
are the projection from
X\times Y
to
X
and
Y
respectively. We denote by
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
the set of all transport plans between
\mu
and
\nu
.

For any Borel cost function
c:X\times Y\to\overline{\mathbb{R}}
uniformly bounded below, we consider the following problem of Kantorovich

(K)

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu):=\inf\Big\{\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\ d\pi:\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)\Big\}

We denote by
\Gamma_{o}(\mu,\nu)
the set of optimal transport plans.

To formulate the important theorem of Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality, we introduce

\displaystyle I_{c}:=

\displaystyle\,\{(\phi,\psi):\psi(y)-\phi(x)\leqslant c(x,y)\ \text{for all}\ x\in X,y\in Y\},

\displaystyle K_{c}:=

\displaystyle\,\{(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}:\psi=T^{-}\phi,\phi=T^{+}\psi\}.

It is obvious that

\displaystyle I_{c}=\{(\phi,\psi):\psi\leqslant T^{-}\phi,\phi\geqslant T^{+}\psi\}\supset K_{c}.

In the literature, the elements in
K_{c}
is called
admissible Kantorovich pair
.

Theorem 4.1

(Kantorovich-Rubinstein duality)
.

(KR)

\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu)=\sup_{(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}}\Big\{\int_{Y}\psi\ d\nu-\int_{X}\phi\ d\mu\Big\}=\sup_{(\phi,\psi)\in K_{c}}\Big\{\int_{Y}\psi\ d\nu-\int_{X}\phi\ d\mu\Big\}.

For our purpose, we try to reformulate Theorem
4.1
and connect it directly to the Lax-Oleinik operators
T^{\pm}
. Let
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
. Set
c_{\phi}(x,y):=\phi(x)+c(x,y)
and
c^{\psi}(x,y)=\psi(y)-c(x,y)
. For any
\mu\in\mathscr{P}(X)
and
\nu\in\mathscr{P}(Y)
, we try to find a function
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
and a function
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
such that

(K
-
)

\displaystyle\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi\ d\nu=

\displaystyle\,\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c_{\phi}(x,y)\ d\pi,

(K
+
)

\displaystyle\int_{Y}T^{+}\psi\ d\mu=

\displaystyle\,\sup_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c^{\psi}(x,y)\ d\pi.

Theorem 4.2
.

(1)

If
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution of (
K-
), then
(\phi,T^{-}\phi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of (
KR
). Conversely, if
(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of (
KR
), then
\phi
solves (
K-
).

(2)

If
\psi:Y\to\mathbb{R}
is a solution of (
K+
), then
(T^{+}\psi,\psi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of (
KR
). Conversely, if
(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of (
KR
), then
\psi
solves (
K+
).

Proof.

For any
\phi:X\to\mathbb{R}
we observe that

(4.1)

\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi\ d\nu\leqslant\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c_{\phi}(x,y)\ d\pi,

since for any
\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
,

\displaystyle\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi\ d\nu=\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi\ d\pi\leqslant\int_{X\times Y}\phi(x)+c(x,y)\ d\pi(x,y)=\int_{X}\phi\ d\mu+\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\ d\pi.

Thus inequality (
4.1
) reads

\displaystyle\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi\ d\nu-\int_{X}\phi\ d\mu\leqslant\mathcal{C}(\mu,\nu).

Since
\phi
is a solution of (
K-
), then the inequality above is indeed an equality. Notice
(\phi,T^{-}\phi)\in I_{c}
since
T^{-}\phi(y)-\phi(x)\leqslant c(x,y)
. This implies
(\phi,T^{-}\phi)
is a solution of (
KR
) by Theorem
4.1
.

Conversely, suppose
(\phi,\psi)\in I_{c}
is a solution of (
KR
). Then

\displaystyle\int_{Y}\psi\ d\nu\geqslant\int_{X}\phi\ d\mu+\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\ d\pi=\int_{X\times Y}c_{\phi}(x,y)\ d\pi.

Since
\psi\leqslant T^{-}\phi
, then
(\phi,T^{-}\phi)
is also a solution of (
KR
). Together with (
4.1
),
\phi
is a solution of (
K-
). This completes the proof of (1). The proof (2) is similar.
∎

Assume that

(2)

(1)\Rightarrow(2)

\displaystyle\int_{X}c(x,T(x))\,d\mu=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma\,(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X\times Y}c(x,y)\,d\pi.

Choose some

(3)\Rightarrow(1)

\displaystyle=\int_{Y}T^{-}\phi(y)\,d\nu

4.3.
Random Lax-Oleinik operators

Suppose

\displaystyle W_{p}(\mu,\nu):=\left\{\inf_{\gamma\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{X^{2}}d^{p}(x,y)\,d\gamma\right\}^{\frac{1}{p}}.

If

Set

-Lipschitz in the large

\displaystyle|\phi(x)-\phi(y)|\leqslant\kappa_{1}d(x,y)+\kappa_{2}.

Remark 4.15
.

For any function

, we have:

(1)

\phi\in\mathrm{Lip\,}(X)
implies
\phi
is
(\mathrm{Lip\,}(\phi),0)
-Lipschitz in the large;

(2)

if

is compact,
\phi
is
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
-Lipschitz in the large if and only if
\phi
is bounded;

(3)

if
X
is a geodesic space,
\phi\in\mathrm{UC}\,(X)
if and only for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
K_{\varepsilon}>0
, such that
\phi
is

-Lipschitz in the large.

Lemma 4.16
.

Assume that
\phi
is
(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
-Lipchitz in the large,
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then
\phi\in L^{1}(\mu)
. Particularly, if
\phi\in\mathrm{UC}\,(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\mu\in\mathscr{P}_{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\phi\in L^{1}(\mu)
.

Proof.

The proof is directly from the definition of Lipschitz in the large. Indeed, for arbitrary
x_{0}\in X
,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}|\phi(x)-\phi(x_{0})|\,d\mu\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}(\kappa_{1}d(x,x_{0})+\kappa_{2})\,d\mu<+\infty.

Thus, this completes the proof with Remark
4.15
.
∎

Now, we turn to the proof of (2). We only need to prove the inclusion
\mathrm{supp}\,((p_{x})_{\#}\pi)\subset p_{x}(\mathrm{supp}\,(\pi))
. Let
x_{0}\in\mathrm{supp}\,((p_{x})_{\#}\pi)
, then (
A.1
) holds true for any neighborhood
U_{x_{0}}
of
x_{0}\in X
. Thus, it suffices to prove that there exists
y_{0}\in Y
such that
\pi\,(U_{x_{0},y_{0}})>0
for any
U_{x_{0},y_{0}}\subset X\times Y
. We prove by contradiction. Assume that for any
y\in Y
there exists an open set
V_{x_{0},y}
containing
(x_{0},y)
such that
\pi\,(V_{x_{0},y})=0
. More precisely, for each
y
, there exist
R_{y}^{X},R_{y}^{Y}>0
with
B(x_{0},R_{y}^{X})\times B(y,R_{y}^{Y})\subset V_{x_{0},y}
, and
\pi\,(B(x_{0},R_{y}^{X})\times B(y,R_{y}^{Y}))=0
. Due to the compactness of
Y
, the open cover
\{B(x_{0},R_{y}^{X})\times B(y,R_{y}^{Y})\}_{y\in Y}
of
\{x_{0}\}\times Y
admits a finite subcover
\{B(x_{0},R_{y_{i}}^{X})\times B(y_{i},R_{y_{i}}^{Y})\}_{i=1}^{N}
.
Set
R_{x_{0}}:=\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant N}\{R_{y_{i}}^{X},R_{y_{i}}^{Y}\}>0
. Then

\displaystyle\{x_{0}\}\times Y\subset B(x_{0},R_{x_{0}})\times Y\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\left\{B(x_{0},R_{y_{i}}^{X})\times B(y_{i},R_{y_{i}}^{Y})\right\}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\pi\,(B(x_{0},R_{x_{0}})\times Y)

\displaystyle\leqslant\pi\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^{N}\left\{B(x_{0},R_{y_{i}}^{X})\times B(y_{i},R_{y_{i}}^{Y})\right\}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{N}\pi\left(B(x_{0},R_{y_{i}}^{X})\times B(y_{i},R_{y_{i}}^{Y})\right)=0.

Therefore,
B(x_{0},R_{x_{0}})
, as a neighborhood of
x_{0}
, does not satisfy (
A.1
). This leads to a contradiction. ∎
