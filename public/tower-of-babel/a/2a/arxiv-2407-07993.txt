Abstract
. We give an explicit description of the Białynicki-Birula cells on the Hilbert scheme of points on 𝔸 2 \mathbb{A}^{2} with isolated fixed points, with the Hilbert-Burch matrices as a main tool. If the fixed point locus is positive dimensional we obtain an étale rational map to the cell. We prove Conjecture [ HW23 , Conjecture 4.2] which we realize as a special case of our construction. We also show examples when the construction provides a rational étale map to the Hilbert scheme which is not contained in any Białynicki-Birula cell. Finally, we give an explicit description of the formal deformations of any ideal in the Hilbert scheme of points on the plane.



1.
Introduction

The Hilbert scheme of
d
-points on a surface is a moduli space of closed subschemes of finite length lying on the surface. It is a classical object in algebraic geometry, very well studied and admits a large number of applications, e.g.
[
ES87
,
Eva04
,
EGL01
,
Hai98
]
.
In this article, we are interested in the Hilbert scheme of points on the plane.

Ellingsrud and Strømme in
[
ES87
]
have computed Betti numbers of the Hilbert scheme by constructing a particular basis of homology coming from the Białynicki-Birula cells. To do so, they have studied
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-fixed points on
\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
, the fixed points on the level of algebra are the monomial ideals of colength
d
.
They described the tangent space in terms of first-order deformations of the Hilbert-Burch matrix. Such a matrix induces a free resolution of the monomial ideal.
Subsequently in
[
ES88
]
they have exploited those techniques further and claimed to describe the Białynicki-Birula cells explicitly. Some issues with arguments in that paper were pointed out and addressed in
[
Hui05
]
, as a consequence the description for one-dimensional tori with isolated fixed points was completed.
Prior to that, Laurent Evain, coming from a different perspective, has fixed these issues in some cases, see
[
Eva04
, Remark 16]
.

Since the foundational work of Ellingsrud and Strømme many people have studied points of this Hilbert scheme explicitly, see
[
CV08
,
Con11
,
HW21
,
HW23
]
. The main method revolves around deforming the Hilbert-Burch matrix for a given monomial ideal.
This way, for a given monomial ideal
E
, Conca and Valla described Gröbner cells centred at
E
for the lexicographic term order.
Later Roser Homs and Anna-Lena Winz, motivated by the study of the punctual Hilbert scheme, studied Gröbner cells for the negative degree lexicographical monomial order. In
[
HW23
]
they have described explicitly those cells for lex monomial ideals, see
[
HW21
, Theorem 5.7]
. They have conjectured that the description extends to Gröbner cells for any monomial ideal
E
, see
[
HW23
, Conjecture 4,2]
.

It remained open to construct all the Białynicki-Birula/Gröbner cells explicitly in terms of Hilbert-Burch matrices. There is also a question when such constructions provide us with explicit open subschemes of the Hilbert scheme.
In this paper, we answer these questions.

6.
Dream scenario and its failure

In the previous section, we have considered the infinitesimal behaviour of our family around the origin, in this section we consider the global question.

The dream scenario would be if the map
\pi
was finite and flat, thus induced a map
\phi\colon B\to\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
. Then its induced map of tangent spaces at the origin would be
d\phi
, described in the previous section. This way we would end up with a generically étale equivariant map to the Hilbert scheme constructed in a very explicit manner. The open subsets obtained that way would contain the Białynicki-Birula cells for any one-dimensional subtorus that has finitely many fixed points on the Hilbert scheme, see Lemma
8.6
. As a consequence, we would construct an interesting collection of explicit
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-stable open subsets giving more information than the Białynicki-Birula cells.

The construction would go in the following manner. We take the spread-out matrix
\mathcal{M}
, see Definition
3.1
, it give us a sequence

R[\mathbf{a}]^{\oplus t}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}R[\mathbf{a}]^{\oplus t+1}\to I_{t}(\mathcal{M})\to 0

just as in the Hilbert-Burch Theorem
[
Eis95
, Theorem 20.15b]
. If
\mathcal{M}
satisfied the condition of the Hilbert-Burch Theorem on every fibre of
\pi
and
\pi
was finite, from the local criterion for flatness, we would get that
\pi
is flat morphism, thus inducing map
\phi
to the Hilbert scheme.
The problem is that the dream scenario falls apart,
\mathcal{M}
does not need to satisfy the condition of the Hilbert-Burch Theorem. The morphism
\pi
can admits positive dimensional fibres and even if we restrict it to the locus where it does have only zero-dimensional fibres it doesn’t have to be finite.

\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=x^{2}-xya_{2,1,1},

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=-xy^{2},

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=y^{2}(1-a_{2,1,1}a_{1,2,1})+xya_{1,2,1}.

If
a_{2,1,1}a_{1,2,1}=1
then we get the ideal generated by
(x^{2},xy)
. The vanishing locus of such an ideal contains the line
V(x)\subset\mathbb{A}^{2}
. Thus not every fibre of
\pi
is zero-dimensional.
This means, by the Hilbert-Burch Theorem
[
Eis95
, Theorem 20.15a]
, that the sequence

0\to R[\mathbf{a}]^{\oplus t}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}R[\mathbf{a}]^{\oplus t+1}\to I_{t}(\mathcal{M})\to 0

is not exact.

The existence of positive dimensional fibres is not a major issue by itself, there always is an open neighbourhood of the origin on which the morphism
\pi
is flat and has zero-dimensional fibres, see Lemma
7.3
below. The next example shows the main problem.

Example 6.2
.

The morphism
\pi
does not need to be finite over any open neighborhood of the origin. Let us take
E=(y^{3},x^{2})=(x^{2},xy^{3},y^{3})
.
The colength of
E
is
6
, as the quotient algebra has a basis
\{1,x,xy,xy^{2},y,y^{2}\}

In this case, the Hilbert-Burch matrix is

M_{E}=\begin{pmatrix}y^{3}&0\\
-x&1\\
0&-x\end{pmatrix}.

Instead of working over the full base
B
, let us restrict ourselves to the linear subspace spanned by
a_{1,2,2}
and
a_{2,1,2}
. Then we consider the family of matrices restricted to this subspace

\mathcal{M}=\begin{pmatrix}y^{3}&y^{2}a_{1,2,2}\\
-x+y^{2}a_{2,1,2}&1\\
0&-x\end{pmatrix}.

Let us consider maximal minors of the matrix
\mathcal{M}

\displaystyle f_{0}

\displaystyle=x^{2}-xy^{2}a_{2,1,2},

\displaystyle f_{1}

\displaystyle=-xy^{3},

\displaystyle f_{2}

\displaystyle=-y^{4}a_{2,1,2}a_{1,2,2}+y^{3}+xy^{2}a_{1,2,2}.

If
a_{2,1,2},a_{1,2,2}\neq 0
, then the set
\{1,x,xy,xy^{2},y,y^{2},y^{3}\}
is linearly independent in the quotient algebra, thus the colength of the ideal is at least
7
which is more than it should be. The morphism
\pi
is flat on some open neighbourhood of the origin
0\in B
, see Lemma

, thus if it was finite, it would have a constant length of the fibres on some neighbourhood.

7.
Resultant strategy

We have seen that the dream scenario falls apart as shown in Examples
6.1
,
6.2
.
The strategy to deal with those issues is to create manageable criteria for finiteness and flatness for algebras obtained from the Hilbert-Burch-like matrices, from Definition
3.1
. Since the minor that is obtained after removing the first row of
\mathcal{M}
has a form

x^{t}+\text{(elements of lower degree with respect to $x$)},

to show finiteness of
\pi
it is enough to show that the element
y
in
R[\mathbf{a}]/I_{t}(\mathcal{M})
is integral over
\Bbbk[\mathbf{a}]
. That is why we adopt the technique from elimination theory.

Definition 7.1
.

Let us consider the matrix
\mathcal{M}
from Definition
3.1
. The vanishing set of the ideal generated by its maximal minors defines the map
\pi
, see Definition
3.1
.
Let us take the first and last maximal minors of the matrix
\mathcal{M}
, that is, determinants of
\mathcal{M}
after excluding the first and last row respectively.
We treat those two minors as polynomials of variable
x
over
\Bbbk[\mathbf{a},y]
. We consider their resultant with respect to the variable
x
, it is a polynomial in
\Bbbk[\mathbf{a},y]
, and we denote it by
\res_{E}
.

Remark 7.2
.

Since the first minor is monic with respect to
x
, by Lemma
2.3
, after changing the base
\res_{E}
continues to be the resultant of two minors of the restricted matrix.

The following lemma resolves the issue exhibited in Example
6.1
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
B^{\prime}
be the subscheme of
B
defined as the complement of vanishing locus of all coefficients of the resultant
{\res_{E}\in k[\mathbf{a}][y]}
as a polynomial of the variable
y
.
Then
B^{\prime}
is a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-stable open subscheme of
B
. The map
\pi
is flat over
B^{\prime}
.

Proof.

For the first part of the statement, the resultant of two homogeneous elements with respect to a variable is homogeneous. This implies that the
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-action restricts to
B^{\prime}
.

For the second part of the statement,
let
\mathfrak{p}\subset k[\mathbf{a}]
be a maximal ideal corresponding to a closed point of
B^{\prime}\subset B
, let
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})
be a residual field of
\mathfrak{p}
.
We check that the ideals
(I_{t}(\mathcal{M}))_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
I_{t}(\mathcal{(}M))\otimes\kappa(\mathfrak{p})
have the depth equal to
2
.
Let us denote
I=I_{t}(\mathcal{M})
.
Let
f_{0},f_{t}\in R[\mathbf{a}]
be the first and the last maximal minor of
\mathcal{M}
. If they do not have a common factor then they constitute a regular sequence of length
2
so
\depth(f_{0},f_{t})=2
, and thus
\depth(I)\geq 2
. Since
\pi
is a surjective map,
\codim(I)\leq 2
. By
[
Eis95
, Proposition 18.2.]

\codim(I)\geq\depth(I)
, implying that
\depth(I)=2
.
The definition of the resultant says that the resultant
\res_{E}
vanishes if and only if
f_{0}
and
f_{t}
admit a common factor that does not belong to
k[\mathbf{a},y]
.
Since
f_{0}
is monic with respect to
x
, it does not admit factors that do not depend on
x
. Therefore the resultant vanishes if and only if they do admit a common factor. This implies that the depth of ideals
(I_{t}(\mathcal{M}))_{\mathfrak{p}}
and
I_{t}(\mathcal{(}M))\otimes\kappa(\mathfrak{p})
over each point of
B^{\prime}
is constant and equal to
2
.

Since the depth of
(I_{t}(\mathcal{M}))_{\mathfrak{p}}
is equal to
2
, the matrix
\mathcal{M}
, by the Hilbert-Burch Theorem
2.1
, induces the resolution:

0\to R[\mathbf{a}]_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\oplus t}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}R[\mathbf{a}]_{\mathfrak{p}}^{\oplus t+1}\to R[\mathbf{a}]_{\mathfrak{p}}\to R[\mathbf{a}]_{\mathfrak{p}}/I_{t}(\mathcal{M})\to 0,

by the local criterion of flatness
[
Eis95
, Theorem 6.8]
it is enough to check that

0\to(R[\mathbf{a}]/{\mathfrak{p}})^{\oplus t}\xrightarrow{\mathcal{M}}(R[\mathbf{a}]/{\mathfrak{p}})^{\oplus t+1}\to R[\mathbf{a}]/{\mathfrak{p}}\to R[\mathbf{a}]/{\mathfrak{p}}/I_{t}(\mathcal{M})\to 0,

is an exact sequence of
\kappa(\mathfrak{p})
-modules, but again since the depth of
I_{t}(\mathcal{(}\mathcal{M}))\otimes\kappa(\mathfrak{p})
is
2
, this sequence satisfies the assumptions of Hilbert-Burch Theorem
2.1
.
∎

To understand finiteness of the map
\pi
we first need to see what is the resultant over the origin.

Example 7.4
.

On the origin of
B
, the first minor
f_{0}
is
x^{t}
and the last one is
y^{m_{t}}
, the resultant
\res_{x}(x^{t},y^{m_{t}})
is equal to
y^{tm_{t}}
as it is a determinant of the
t\times t
Sylvester matrix

\begin{pmatrix}y^{m_{t}}&0&0&0&\ldots&\ldots\\
0&y^{m_{t}}&0&0&\ldots&\ldots\\
0&0&y^{m_{t}}&0&\ldots&\ldots\\
0&0&0&y^{m_{t}}&\ldots&\ldots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\ldots&0&0&0&\ldots&y^{m_{t}}\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Now we state the crucial corollary of this paper.

Corollary 7.5
.

On the locally closed subscheme
S\subset B^{\prime}
on which the resultant is a polynomial of degree exactly
tm_{t}
, the map
\pi
is finite and flat.

Proof.

As the statement is local on the target we substitute
S
with an affine open subscheme.
As we have seen in the computation above, Example
7.4
, the resultant
\res_{E}
is equal to
y^{tm_{t}}
modulo the ideal generated by parameters
(a_{i,j})
. In particular,
S
is non-empty. On
S
the resultant is of the form

uy^{tm_{t}}+\text{terms with lower degrees with respect to }y

where
u
is invertible on
S
. Since
\res_{E}\in I_{t}(\mathcal{M})
, this relation makes
y\in H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})[x,y]/I_{t}(\mathcal{M})
integral over
H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})
. As the maximal minor
f_{0}
is monic with respect to
x
it makes
x
integral over
H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})[y]
, and thus we obtain that
H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})[x,y]/I_{t}(\mathcal{M})
is finite over
H^{0}(S,\mathcal{O}_{S})
.
Since the resultant has degree exactly
tm_{t}
it is not zero independently on the values of parameters
a_{i,j,k}
, thus by Lemma
7.3
we get flatness.
∎

Remark 7.6
.

In general, the subscheme
S
is not closed nor open in
B
since it is given by:

•

One open condition that the coefficient with
y^{tm_{t}}
does not vanish.

•

Closed conditions that coefficients with higher exponents of
y
are zero.

To see how the resultant strategy works in practice, let us look at Example

and Example

8.
Białynicki-Birula decomposition

In the previous section, we have developed a tool that allows us to control the finiteness and flatness of the map
\pi
. In this section, we use this tool to show that our family describes the Białynicki-Birula cells of the Hilbert scheme of points. Since the nature of the Białynicki-Birula cells is equivariant, we heavily use the equivariance of all of the previous results. We start with a general discussion about the Białynicki-Birula decomposition on the Hilbert scheme.

We consider the affine plane
\mathbb{A}^{2}
with the standard
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-action

(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\cdot(x,y)=(\lambda_{1}x,\lambda_{2}y),

This action induces one on
\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
, and for a choice of monomial ideal
E
also on the base scheme
B_{E}
, see Definition
3.1
. Choosing a nonzero cocharacter:

\displaystyle\psi\colon\mathbb{G}_{m}

\displaystyle\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}

\displaystyle t

\displaystyle\mapsto(t^{\alpha},t^{\beta})

is equivalent to endowing
R=\Bbbk[x,y]
with
\mathbb{Z}
-grading, denoted by
\deg_{\psi}
, such that
\deg_{\psi}(x)=\alpha
and
\deg_{\psi}(y)=\beta
.
Such a choice of
\psi
induces the Białynicki-Birula decomposition on
B
and
\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
. For specific reference for the Hilbert scheme see
[
ES87
]
. For general reference on the Białynicki-Birula decomposition see
[
JS19
, Theorem 1.5]
. The decomposition associated to
\psi
is denoted by adding superscript
+,\psi
, e.g.
\Hilb(\mathbb{A}^{2})^{d,+,\psi}
.
Note that the only fixed points of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-action on
\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
are the monomial ideals and for
B
the only
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-fixed point is the origin. Thus for any choice of
\psi
those subschemes are contained in the fixed point locus of
\psi(\mathbb{G}_{m})
acting respectively on the Hilbert scheme and
B
.

Example 8.1
.

We choose a cocharacter
\psi
such that
\psi(\mathbb{G}_{m})
admits only finitely many fixed points.
We describe the
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-action as follows:

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{G}_{m}\times\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})

\displaystyle\to\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2}),

(5)

\displaystyle(t,[I])

\displaystyle\mapsto(\psi(t)\cdot[I]),

Then the Białynicki Birula cell associated to
\psi
and centred at
[E]
consists if the ideals
[I]\in\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
such that

\lim_{t\to 0}t\cdot[I]=[E].

In general, the subtorus given by the cocharacter
\psi
has more fixed points.

Definition 8.2
.

The Białynicki Birula cell of
\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
(respectively,
B
) associated to the cocharacter
\psi
and centred at the monomial ideal
E
is the connected component of the smooth variety
\Hilb^{d,+,\psi}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
(respectively
B^{+,\psi}
) that contains the point
[E]
(respectively, the origin).

Since
B
is an affine space with a linear
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-action, it is relatively easy to establish what is
B^{+,\psi}
. It is an affine subspace spanned by those variables
a_{i,j,k}
with bi-degree
(w_{x},w_{y})
such that the induced degree
\alpha w_{x}+\beta w_{y}
is non-negative, see
[
JS19
, Proposition 2.15]
.

In other words,
\psi
induces a functional on the space of all characters of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
. For any chosen parameter
a_{i,j,k}
, the line
\langle{a_{i,j,k}}\rangle
can be treated as a character of
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
, and then
B^{+,\psi}
is spanned by those characters that are positive with respect to the cocharacter
\psi
.

Example 8.3
.

Let us take an ideal
E=(x^{2},xy,y^{2})
.
The spread-out matrix
\mathcal{M}
is

\begin{pmatrix}y+a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}\\
-x+a_{2,1}&y+a_{2,2}\\
a_{3,1}&-x+a_{3,2}\end{pmatrix},

where for simplicity of notation
a_{i,j}=a_{i,j,0}
.
Let us plot the weights of variables
a_{i,j}
on the plane

We choose a cocharacter
\psi\colon t\to(t^{1},t^{-1})
. This induces a functional on the space of weights, with the kernel being a line
x=y

The parameters which weights lie in the upper half-plane span the Białynicki-Birula cell associated to the cocharacter
\psi
.

Let us take a one-parameter subgroup
\psi
acting on
\mathbb{A}^{2}
, that attracts the line
x=0
to the origin.
Now we show that the spread-out matrix, Definition
3.1
, provides a rational map to the cell on the Hilbert scheme associated to such a
\psi
.

Theorem 8.4
.

Choose a cocharacter
\psi\colon\mathbb{G}_{m}\to\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
, such that
y
has a non-negative degree with respect to the induced grading.
Let us restrict
B
to
B^{+,\psi}
the Białynicki-Birula cell associated to
\psi
. The morphism
\pi
induces a rational
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-equivariant map
\phi^{+}\colon B^{+,\psi}\to\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
. The image of
\phi^{+}
lies in the Białynicki-Birula cell associated to
\psi
and centred at
E
. The map
\phi^{+}
is étale in a
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-stable neighbourhood of the origin.

Proof.

As observed in Example
7.4
the resultant
\res_{E}
is of degree
\deg_{\psi}(y)tm_{t}
. We need to show that on
B^{+}
the resultant has no terms with degree higher than that with respect to
y
. As noted in Lemma
7.3
the resultant is homogeneous. If there is a non-zero term
W(\mathbf{a})y^{N}
for
N>tm_{t}
, then
\deg_{\psi}(W(\mathbf{a}))=\deg_{\psi}(y)(tm_{t}-N)<0
. Since
B^{+}
is the Białynicki-Birula cell,
W(\mathbf{a})
depends only on parameters with non-negative degrees with respect to
\psi
, thus
\deg_{\psi}(W(\mathbf{a}))\geq 0
.

Now, let us assume that
\deg_{\psi}(y)=0
.
That means that
\psi
has a form:

t\to(t^{\alpha},1),

for
\alpha\neq 0
.
Again we consider
W(\mathbf{a})
as before, since
\res_{E}
is homogeneous with respect to the full
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
-grading,
W(\mathbf{a})
is also homogeneous and has degree
(0,tm_{t}-N)
.
Let us consider what parameters can
W(\mathbf{a})
depend on. Since we restrict ourselves to the Białynicki-Birula cell, it can only depend on those parameters
a_{i,j,k}
such that their degree
(w_{x},w_{y}):=(i-j,m_{j}-m_{i-1})
satisfy the following inequality:

\alpha w_{x}+0\cdot w_{y}\geq 0.

The only way that one can obtain
0
as a sum of non-negative/non-positive numbers is by taking a sum of
0
-s.
Thus
W(\mathbf{a})
depend only on parameters lying on the main diagonal
(i,i)
.
As a consequence, we only need to study the resultant coming from the matrix with non-zero parameters only on the main diagonal,
a_{i,j,k}=0
for
i\neq j
. In that case, the first minor is just
x^{t}
, and the last one is a product of elements on the diagonal, in particular, it is a polynomial in
y
of degree
m_{t}
.

Therefore in both cases above, the resultant is the last minor to the power of
t
, so has the predicted degree. By Lemma
7.3
we get that
\pi
is flat over an open neighbourhood of the origin, and by Lemma
7.5
we get that the family
\pi
is finite.

The last part follows from Theorem
5.5
.
∎

Remark 8.5
.

Since the map
\phi^{+}
in Theorem
8.4
above is étale and equivariant, it restricts to étale map between the
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-fixed point loci.
In particular the dimension of the connected
\mathbb{G}_{m}
-fixed point component of
\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
containing
[E]
is equal to the dimension of the connected fixed point component of
B
through the origin.

The only
\mathbb{G}_{m}^{2}
-fixed points on the Hilbert scheme are the monomial ideals, thus for a general choice of the one-dimensional subtorus
\mathbb{G}_{m}
, they are the only fixed points. In this special case, we will get that our map is an isomorphism onto the cell.

Lemma 8.6
.

Let us take the setup from Theorem
8.4
, but also let us assume that the only
\psi(\mathbb{G}_{m})
-fixed point on
B^{+}
is the origin. Then the induced map
\phi^{+}
is an isomorphism onto the Białynicki-Birula cell.

Proof.

Let us consider the coefficient
W
with
y^{tm_{t}}
in
\res_{E}
. Since the resultant is homogeneous we have
\deg_{\psi}W=0
. The only fixed point is the origin, therefore
\deg_{\psi}(a_{i,j,k})>0
for every parameter appearing in
B^{+}
. As a consequence,
W=1
and we get that
\pi
is flat over whole
B^{+}
.
By the proof of Theorem
8.4
, we get that globally the resultant has the degree at most
tm_{t}
, thus we obtain a map
\phi\colon B^{+}\to\Hilb^{d}(\mathbb{A}^{2})
to the Hilbert scheme, globally.

The morphism
\phi
is a map between the Białynicki-Birula cells (with single fixed points) that is an isomorphism on tangent spaces, Theorem
5.4
. By Lemma
2.5
, the map
\phi
is an isomorphism.
∎
