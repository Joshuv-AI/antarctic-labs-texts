Universality in prelimiting tail behavior for regular subgraph counts in the Poisson regime
Mriganka Basu Roy Chowdhury

Abstract
. Let N N be the number of copies of a small subgraph H H in an Erdős–Rényi graph G ∼ 𝒢 ⁡ ( n , p n ) G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p_{n}) where p n → 0 p_{n}\to 0 is chosen so that 𝔼 ​ N = c \mathbb{E}N=c , a constant. Results of Bollobás [ Bol81 ] show that for regular graphs H H , the count N N weakly converges to a Poisson random variable. For large but finite n n , and for the specific case of the triangle, investigations of the upper tail ℙ ⁡ ( N ≥ k n ) \mathbb{P}(N\geq k_{n}) by [ GHN22 ] revealed that there is a phase transition in the tail behavior and the associated mechanism. Smaller values of k n k_{n} correspond to disjoint occurrences of H H , leading to Poisson tails, with a different behavior emerging when k n k_{n} is large, guided by the appearance of an almost clique. We show that a similar phase transition also occurs when H H is any regular graph, at the point where k n 1 − 2 / q ​ log ⁡ k n = log ⁡ n k_{n}^{1-2/q}\log k_{n}=\log n ( q q is the number of vertices in H H ). This establishes universality of this transition, previously known only for the case of the triangle.



1.
Introduction

Let
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
be the Erdős–Rényi random graph where each edge is present independently with probability
p
. Over the last few decades, various properties of these graphs have been investigated, resulting in a very fine understanding of this model. For a fixed graph
H
, let
Q_{H}(G)
be the number of copies of
H
in
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p)
. Starting with the seminal paper of Erdős and Rényi
[
ER60
]
, the distribution of
Q_{H}(G)
, both for fixed
p
and for
p
varying with
n
at different rates, has been the subject of intense research. For example, the probability of
Q_{H}(G)>0
was studied by
[
Sch79
]
where
H
was a complete graph. This work was later extended to other subgraphs by Bollobás
[
Bol81
]
. In addition to this, in many cases we also know asymptotic distributions of
Q_{H}(G)
. A criterion for asymptotic normality of
Q_{H}(G)
was obtained by Ruciński
[
Ruc88
]
. This however must exclude the regime of
p_{n}
where we expect
\Theta(1)
copies of the subgraph in expectation where asymptotic normality cannot hold. A natural guess for the limiting distribution in this case is Poisson, but it turns out that it is not true in general. However, for the large class of
strictly balanced graphs
(which includes all regular graphs), asymptotic Poisson behavior was established by Bollobás
[
Bol81
]
(see the discussion at the beginning of section
3
for the definition of strictly balanced graphs).

A particularly active direction of research has been the study of the upper tail probability
\mathbb{P}(Q_{H}(G)\geq k_{n})
, the so-called “infamous upper tail”. The seminal paper of Chatterjee and Varadhan
[
CV11
]
achieved asymptotically sharp estimates on this for the case of fixed
p_{n}=p>0
, using the theory of dense graph limits and graphons (see
[
Lov12
]
or
[
Cha17
]
) as well as the celebrated Szemerédi regularity lemma
[
Sze75
]
, by reducing the computation of the upper tail probability to a natural “mean-field” variational problem. However due to poor quantitative estimates in the regularity lemma, arguments in
[
CV11
]
could only be extended to
p_{n}
decaying as a negative power of
\log n
, see
[
LZ17
]
. The most widely studied case is of
H=K_{3}
, the triangle. Here the arguments of Chatterjee and Dembo
[
CD16
]
and Lubetzky and Zhao
[
LZ17
]
extended previously known results down to
p_{n}\geq n^{-1/42}\log n
. However the mean-field variational formulation is expected to hold as long as
p_{n}\gg\log n/n
. After a series of improvements (Augeri
[
Aug18
]
, Cook and Dembo
[
CD20
]
, Eldan
[
Eld18
]
), a breakthrough by Harel, Mousset and Samotij
[
HMS22
]
essentially solved the upper tail problem in the regime
p_{n}\gg 1/n
, using methods inspired by the classical moment arguments of Janson, Oleszkiewicz and Ruciński
[
JOR04
]
, leaving the interesting case of
p_{n}=\Theta(1/n)
still open.
Sharp understanding of the upper tail (for the case of
K_{3}
) in this sparse regime was finally achieved by Ganguly, Hiesmayr and Nam
[
GHN22
]
and Chakraborty, van der Hofstad and den Hollander
[
CHH21
]
, thereby completing our understanding of the upper tail in the case of the triangle.

Our interest is primarily in an analogous regime for general regular graphs
H
, where
p_{n}\to 0
in such a way that
\mathbb{E}Q_{H}(G)=\Theta(1)
as
n\to\infty
. To see what this rate of decay of
p_{n}
might be, let
H
be a
q
-vertex
\Delta
-regular graph. Observe that the expected number of copies of
H
in
G
is

\displaystyle\binom{n}{q}p_{n}^{-q\Delta/2}

up to constants depending only on
H
. Since
(n/q)^{q}\leq\binom{n}{q}\leq(ne/q)^{q}
, the choice
p_{n}=\Theta(n^{-2/\Delta})
ensures that this quantity is
\Theta(1)
. As discussed earlier, the results of
[
Bol81
]
show that
Q_{H}(G)
is asymptotically Poisson in this case (hence the term “Poisson regime”). However, the arguments there do not yield effective quantitative prelimiting estimates. In the case of
H=K_{3}
, the triangle, the prelimiting upper tail was investigated in
[
GHN22
]
, where it was shown that the behavior of
\mathbb{P}(Q_{H}(G)\geq k_{n})
undergoes a transition when
k_{n}
is around
O(\log^{3}n)
(that the behavior after this transition persists till the maximum of
k_{n}=\Theta(n^{3})
was proved in
[
CHH21
]
). The transition is as follows: when
k_{n}
is smaller than the threshold, the dominant mechanism is the occurrence of many disjoint copies, but when
k_{n}
is above the threshold, the mechanism changes to having an (almost) clique.

In this paper we show that this phase transition also happens when
H
is any regular graph. As above, we will fix
H
to be a connected
q
-vertex
\Delta
-regular graph (with
q\geq 3
) throughout this paper. Our main result is the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
k_{n}\geq 2
and
p_{n}=n^{-2/\Delta}
. If
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p_{n})
, the probability of
Q_{H}(G)\geq k_{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle C_{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}L_{n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}(Q_{H}(G)\geq k_{n})\leq C_{3}\exp\left(-C_{4}L_{n}\right),\quad\text{where,}

\displaystyle L_{n}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\min(k_{n}\log k_{n},k_{n}^{2/q}\log n).

Here the constants
C_{1},C_{2},C_{3},C_{4}
that only depend on
H
. (This choice of
p_{n}=n^{-2/\Delta}
ensures that
\mathbb{E}Q_{H}(G)=\Theta({1})
.)

It is easy to extend this result for
p_{n}=c^{\prime}n^{-2/\Delta}
for some constant
c^{\prime}
, but we make this choice for notational simplicity. Theorem
1
almost achieves the sharp phase transition at
k_{n}
satisfying
k_{n}^{1-2/q}\log k_{n}=\log n
, as obtained in
[
GHN22
]
for the special case of
H=K_{3}
.

Note:
Throughout this paper, a
copy
of a graph
H
in
G
is counted only up to automorphisms. Equivalently, it is a subset of edges of
G
forming a graph isomorphic to
H
. Of course, even if we did count automorphisms separately, the form of our result would not change due to the presence of
H
dependent constants.

2.
Idea of proof

We begin by setting up the notations. For any graph
G
, we will use
V(G),E(G)
to denote the vertex set and the edge set respectively, while
v(G),e(G)
will be the respective sizes of these sets. We will write
d_{v}
to denote the degree of a vertex
v
.
C=C(H)
will always denote a constant that depends only on
H
.

There are two main subparts to the proof. The first part deals with the case when
k_{n}
is small, and the second part is for the case of large
k_{n}
. It turns out that arguments in the first part works for all
k_{n}\leq n^{c}
for some small
c
that depends on
H
, and the second part works for all
k_{n}
that grow faster than everything that is
\mathrm{poly}\log n
. It is then clear that these two proofs cover all possible values of
k_{n}
. The phase transition will occur in the former case, while the purpose of the latter is to show that the behavior shown in the first part persists after the phase transition all the way up to the maximum possible value of
k_{n}
.

The proof for the case of small
k_{n}
is via a reduction to the analysis of the structure of “spanned graphs”. This is inspired by
[
GHN22
]
which treats the same problem in the case of the triangle. However, informally, that paper uses the fact that since the critical probability for a triangle is
p_{n}=O(1/n)
, the probability that a given subgraph with
v
vertices and
e
edges occurs as an isomorphic copy is at most

\displaystyle\binom{n}{v}p_{n}^{-e}\leq n^{-(e-v)}.

Thus this probability is directly related to the quantity
e-v+1
which can be interpreted as the number of “excess edges” in the subgraph after a spanning tree has been chosen. Such an idea fails in our case because for a general
\Delta
-regular graph the critical probability is instead

p_{n}=n^{-2/\Delta}
, so that the above bound is now
n^{-(2e/\Delta-v)}
, for which a similar interpretation is not available.
To circumvent this issue,
we prove that even in this general case, any “spanned graph”
G^{\prime}
must satisfy
2e(G^{\prime})/\Delta-v\geq Ce(G^{\prime})
for some constant
C
, see Lemma
9
. Armed with this inequality, we bound the probability that the graph contains a certain spanned graph
G^{\prime}
which has many copies of
H
. Due to the last inequality, this is now related to the minimal number of edges in a candidate
G^{\prime}
containing, say,
\ell
copies of
H
, which is at least
\ell^{2/q}
up to constants (due to Lemma
6
). It turns out that the concavity of this map
\ell\mapsto\ell^{2/q}
implies that
it is either optimal to have all the copies be in a large spanned component, or occur disjointly, depending on how large
k_{n}
is. As a consequence of this dichotomy, we obtain our phase transition. However, a formal proof requires one to also control the entropy stemming from the number of possible ways to distribute
k_{n}
copies of
H
among spanned graphs. This is done using a dyadic decomposition argument where we classify spanned components into logarithmic many collections depending on the number of copies they contain. See Lemma
11
for more details.

In the case of large
k_{n}
, the main idea is that if a graph has too many copies of
H
, then there must occur certain subgraphs, such that the expected count of
H
conditioned on their occurrence in the random graph is near optimal (see Lemma
12
for more details). This idea first appeared in
[
HMS22
]
(who called such subgraphs “cores”), and was adopted by
[
CHH21
]
to prove a result similar to ours but only for the triangle. However a näive implementation of the approaches found in these papers fail in our case. As Lemma
12
will show, the upper tail bound essentially boils down to upper bounding
the occurrence probability of a core
. This involves an efficient union bound over all cores. To illustrate the difficulties involved, let us see why a näive direct count approach already fails for the case of
H=C_{4}
, the cycle on 4 vertices. In the case
k_{n}\sim n
, it turns out that all the complete bipartite graphs
K_{2,m}

(i.e. 2 vertices on one side) with
m=O(\sqrt{n})
qualify as cores.
Note that their count is of the order of
\binom{n}{m}=\exp(O(\sqrt{n}\log n))
whose exponent exactly matches the probability bound of
C^{\prime}\exp(-C\sqrt{n}\log n)
as claimed by Theorem
1
in this case. This reduces the direct union bound approach to a delicate game of constants.

Our proof avoids these issues, and instead crucially uses two estimates:

(1)

an upper bound on the number of copies of
H
in another graph
G
in terms of
e(G)
,

(2)

and, a lower bound on the product of degrees
d_{u}d_{v}
for all edges
(u,v)
in a core.

It turns out that for simple graphs
H
, these estimates can follow from direct combinatorial arguments that exploit the specific structure of
H
. For example, in the case of cycles, a standard spectral argument is sufficient to deduce an (essentially optimal) upper bound on the number of copies of it in a graph with
e
edges. Further, the inequality involving the product of degrees can also be established via direct combinatorial methods. We expect other simple classes of graphs to also yield to similar arguments. Note that this is similar in spirit to
[
CHH21
]
whose analysis also crucially relied on the geometry of the triangle. But since we wish to deal with all regular graphs at once, we must employ alternate means to deduce information about the structure of these cores.
For this reason, we develop a novel strategy invoking
Finner’s inequality

[
Fin92
]
. This is a form of Hölder’s inequality which has proved to be particularly useful in the context of graphons, as demonstrated, for example, in
[
LZ15
]
(see section
4
). A straightforward application of this inequality yields the desired bound on the number of copies of
H
in a graph with
e
edges, as proved in Lemma
6
. But this alone is not sufficient for the proof. A much more involved analysis also allows us to also prove bounds on the expected count of
H
in a graph containing a specific edge (Lemma
7
). These results allow us to not
only show that cores themselves contain many copies of
H
(Lemma
14
), but also that each edge in the core has the interesting
property of having a large product of degrees (Lemma
15
), as asserted in point (2) above. All these facts are finally combined in the dyadic decomposition argument of Lemma
16
,
which essentially exploits the fact that since the product of degrees is high, very low degree vertices can only have edges to vertices with very high degree. Since there are few vertices with very high degree, this allows us to cheaply account for the neighbor sets of these low degree vertices, which are
the main sources of trouble in the proof.

We end this section with a word about asymptotic notation. In addition to the usual
O(\cdot),\Omega(\cdot),\Theta(\cdot)
, we also use
\widetilde{O}(g(n))\ni f(n)
to mean that
f(n)/\mathrm{poly}\log n=O(g(n))
, and
\widetilde{\Omega}(g(n))\ni f(n)
to mean that
f(n)\mathrm{poly}\log n=\widetilde{\Omega}(g(n))
. Finally,
\widetilde{o}(g(n))\ni f(n)
if and only if
f(n)h(n)=o(g(n))
for every
h(n)
that is
\mathrm{poly}\log n
. So, for example,
n^{0.9}=\widetilde{o}(n)
but
n/\log^{100}n\neq\widetilde{o}(n)
. In fact
n/\log^{100}n=\widetilde{\Omega}(n)
.

3.
Lower bounds

This short section collects the necessary results allowing us to assert the lower bound in our main theorem.

Define the
density
of a graph
H
to be the ratio
d(H)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}e(H)/v(H)
(see
[
JRL11
, Chapter 3]
), and call a connected graph
strictly balanced
if for any proper subgraph
H^{\prime}\subsetneq H
,
d(H^{\prime})<d(H)
. It is easy to see that
\Delta
-regular graphs are strictly balanced, because the entire graph has density
\Delta/2
, but any proper subgraph must have some vertex with degree
<\Delta
, so that the density is strictly smaller than
\Delta/2
. By the Poisson limit theorem for subgraph counts at the threshold
1
1

1

This terminology is often used in the literature to mean a choice of
p_{n}
for which
\mathbb{E}Q_{H}(\mathcal{G}(n,p_{n}))=\Theta(1)
.
due to Bollobás
[
Bol81
]
, the asymptotic distribution of
Q_{H}(\mathcal{G}(n,p_{n}))
is therefore Poisson. This fact is also used in the proof of the lemma below, which asserts a lower bound on the probability of having disjoint copies of
H
.

Lemma 2

(Disjoint occurrence)
.

Let
D_{s}
denote the event that there are
s
disjoint copies of
H
in
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p=p_{n})
. Fix
\varepsilon>0
. If
s\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{s})\geq C\exp\left(-C^{\prime}s\log s\right)

for all sufficiently large
n
depending on
\varepsilon
. Here
C,C^{\prime}
are constants depending on
H
.

The matching upper bound is stated in Lemma
8
.

Proof.

Divide the vertex set
[n]
of
K_{n}
into
s
(almost) equal sized groups of size either
{\lfloor n/s\rfloor}
or
{\lceil n/s\rceil}
. Set
m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}{\lfloor n/s\rfloor}
. Then,

(1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(s\text{ disjoint copies of }H)\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}(m,p_{n})\text{ has a copy of }H)^{s}

To lower bound the probability of the latter, let
q=m^{-2/\Delta}
, and observe that
p_{n}=n^{-2/\Delta}\geq(2ms)^{-2/\Delta}
, as long as

\displaystyle 2ms\geq n\iff 2{\lfloor n/s\rfloor}\geq n/s,

which happens for all sufficiently large
n
depending on
s
(and uniformly in
s\leq n^{1-\varepsilon}
depending on
\varepsilon
). We assume this holds below. Then

(2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}(m,p_{n})\text{ has a copy of }H)\geq\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}(m,(2ms)^{-2/\Delta})\text{ has a copy of }H).

We now employ a two stage sampling procedure as follows: to sample a
\mathcal{G}(m,(2ms)^{-2/\Delta})
, we first sample a
\mathcal{G}(m,m^{-2/\Delta})
, and then keep each of its edges independently with probability
(2s)^{-2/\Delta}
. It is clear that the resulting graph is a sample from
\mathcal{G}(m,(2ms)^{-2/\Delta})
. But crucially, since the expected number of copies of
H
in
\mathcal{G}(m,m^{-2/\Delta})
is
\Theta(1)
, we can employ the classical fact that the distribution of subgraph counts at the threshold is Poisson for strictly balanced
H
(see, for instance,
[
Bol81
]
or
[
JRL11
, Theorem 3.19]
), to conclude that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}(m,m^{-2/\Delta})\text{ has a copy of }H)\geq c,

for a constant
c
depending on
H
for all sufficiently large
m
. A given copy of
H
in
\mathcal{G}(m,m^{-2/\Delta})
survives the second round of the procedure only if all its edges survive, which happens with probability

\displaystyle\left((2s)^{-2/\Delta}\right)^{e(H)}=(2s)^{-\frac{2}{\Delta}\frac{q\Delta}{2}}=(2s)^{-q}.

Putting these together,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{G}(m,(2ms)^{-2/\Delta})\text{ has a copy of }H)\geq c(2s)^{-q},

so that we can invoke (
1
) and (
2
) to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(s\text{ disjoint copies of }H)\geq\left(c(2s)^{-q}\right)^{s}\geq C\exp\left(-C^{\prime}s\log s\right),

for sufficiently large
n
depending on
\varepsilon
.
∎

The next lemma is a lower bound on clique occurrence probabilities.

Lemma 3

(Clique)
.

The probability that there is a clique of size
s\geq 2
in
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p=p_{n})
is at least
\exp\left(-cs^{2}\log n\right)
where
c
depends only on
\Delta
.

Proof.

The probability of having a clique of size
s
is at least that of the event that any fixed set of
s
vertices have all edges in between themselves. The probability of this latter event is exactly:

\displaystyle p_{n}^{\binom{s}{2}}\geq\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}(s-1)^{2}\log(1/p_{n})\right)=\exp\left(-cs^{2}\log n\right),

where
c=c(\Delta)
. This proves the result.
∎

These two results together imply

Lemma 4

(Lower bound of Theorem
1
)
.

In the notation above,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q_{H}(G)\geq k_{n})\geq C\exp\left(-C^{\prime}L_{n}\right)=C\exp\left(-C^{\prime}\min(k_{n}\log k_{n},k_{n}^{2/q}\log n)\right)

for constants
C,C^{\prime}
depending on
H
.

Proof.

Given the lemmas above, the only remaining part is that the smallest clique containing at least
k_{n}
copies of
H
is of size
Ck_{n}^{2/q}
, but this is immediate from a counting argument.
∎

4.
Finner’s inequality

As described earlier, one of the most important results we require throughout the sequel is the following variant of Hölder’s inequality. As far as we could tell, applications of this inequality in the context of large deviations on random graphs first appeared in
[
LZ15
]
, where they used it to analyze the variational problem associated with the upper tail deviations for
\mathcal{G}(n,p)
with fixed
p
.

Lemma 5

(Finner’s inequality,
[
Fin92
]
)
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\ldots,\mu_{n}
be probability measures on
\Omega_{1},\Omega_{2},\ldots,\Omega_{n}
respectively and define
\Omega=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\Omega_{i}
and
\mu=\prod_{i=1}^{n}\mu_{i}
. Also let
A_{1},A_{2},\ldots,A_{m}
be nonempty subsets of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and for any subset
A\subseteq[n]
, denote
\mu_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}\mu_{i}
and
\Omega_{A}=\prod_{i\in A}\Omega_{i}
. Suppose
f_{i}\in L^{p_{i}}(\Omega_{A_{i}},\mu_{A_{i}})
for each
i\in[m]
, such that for all
x\in[n]
,

\displaystyle\sum_{i:x\in A_{i}}p_{i}^{-1}\leq 1.

Then we have the inequality

\displaystyle\int\prod_{i=1}^{m}|f_{i}|d\mu\leq\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left(\int|f_{i}|^{p_{i}}d\mu_{A_{i}}\right)^{1/p_{i}}.

Here, on the left-hand side, one should think of each
f_{i}
extended to a function on
\Omega
which depends only on the coordinates in
A_{i}
.

Our primary application of this will be to count homomorphisms from
H
to
G
, using the
graphon representation
of
G
.

Definition 1

(Graphon representation of a finite graph
G
)
.

Let
G
be a finite graph on
n
vertices, and without loss of generality, suppose its vertices are
v_{0},\ldots,v_{n-1}
. The graphon (representation) of
G
is defined as a function
f_{G}:[0,1]^{2}\to\{0,1\}
as follows.

\displaystyle f_{G}(x,y)=1\iff\exists\ 0\leq i,j<n:\frac{i}{n}\leq x<\frac{i+1}{n},\frac{j}{n}\leq y<\frac{j+1}{n},(v_{i},v_{j})\in E(G)

else it is zero.

It is a classical result following from direct computation that the number of homomorphisms from
H
to
G
is given by (see, for instance,
[
Lov12
, Chapter 7]
, or
[
Cha17
, Chapter 3]
),

\displaystyle n^{q}\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E(H)}f(t_{u},t_{v})\prod_{u\in V(H)}dt_{u}.

This would enable us to bound the number of homomorphisms from
H
to
G
, and therefore the number of copies of
H
in
G
.

Later, we will need a slightly more general result relaxing the regularity requirement for
H
. While it may also be deduced from
[
LZ15
, Corollary 3.2]
, we provide a proof for completeness.

Lemma 6
.

Let
H
be
q
-vertex
e
-edge (not necessarily connected) graph with each degree bounded above by
\Delta
. Let
G
be a graph with graphon
f_{G}
. Write
n=|V(G)|
, and
m=|E(G)|
. Then the number of homomorphisms of
H
in
G
,
N_{H}(G)
is at most

\displaystyle N_{H}(G)\leq Cn^{q-2e/\Delta}m^{e/\Delta}.

In particular for a regular
H
,
2e/\Delta=q
, so we have
N_{H}(G)\leq Cm^{q/2}
.

Proof.

We apply Finner’s inequality with
\Omega_{i}=[0,1]
for all
i\in[n]
, and
\mu_{i}
being the uniform measure on
[0,1]
. For each edge
e=(u,v)
of
H
we choose
A_{e}=\{u,v\}
and
p_{e}=\Delta
. Then the conditions are satisfied, and we get

\displaystyle\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\prod_{(u,v)\in E(H)}f_{G}(t_{u},t_{v})\prod_{u\in V(H)}dt_{u}

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{(u,v)\in E(H)}\left(\int_{[0,1]^{2}}f_{G}(t_{u},t_{v})^{\Delta}dt_{u}dt_{v}\right)^{1/\Delta}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{2m}{n^{2}}\right)^{e/\Delta}

\displaystyle\leq Cm^{e/\Delta}n^{-2e/\Delta},

where we use the fact that
f_{G}
is zero-one valued, so,
f_{G}^{\Delta}=f_{G}
. This finishes the proof.
∎

The graphon representation and the formula for homomorphism density is also useful in bounding the
expected number of copies
of a particular graph
H
in a random graph
G
with independent edges. For a given collection of probabilities
0\leq p_{uv}\leq 1
for
u<v\in[n]
, consider the random graph model on the vertex set
[n]
where the edge
(u,v)
is present with probability
p_{uv}
, independently of other edges (undirected edges). For such a model we define:

Definition 2

(Graphon representation of a finite random graph
G
with independent edges)
.

For the model described above, the associated graphon is defined as a function
f_{G}:[0,1]^{2}\to[0,1]
satisfying

\displaystyle f_{G}(x,y)=p_{ij}\iff\exists\ 0\leq i,j<n:\frac{i}{n}\leq x<\frac{i+1}{n},\frac{j}{n}\leq y<\frac{j+1}{n},(i,j)\in E(G)

else it is zero.

One of the key ingredients in our argument is the following novel result about the number of copies of
H
in
G
containing a given edge

, which, as alluded to in section
2
, will be crucial in our analysis of the core, see Lemma

. We are not aware of a similar previous result.

Lemma 7
.

Let
{G^{*}}
be a subgraph of
K_{n}
(with vertex set
[n]
), and let
f
be the graphon associated with the random graph model
G
on
[n]
where the edges in
{G^{*}}
are present with probability 1, and every other edge is present with probability
p
independently. Suppose
{G^{*}}
has a distinguished edge
(a,b)
, and let
H
be a
\Delta
-regular graph with
q
vertices. Then, the expected number of copies of
H
in
G
containing
(a,b)
is at most:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Q_{H}(G)\leq C(H)\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{q/2-2+1/\Delta}

where
e=e({G^{*}})
and
d_{a},d_{b}
are the degrees of
a,b
respectively in
{G^{*}}
.

In addition, if
Q_{H}^{\prime}(G)
is the number of expected number of copies of
H
in
G
containing
(a,b)
where some edge must come from outside
{G^{*}}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}Q^{\prime}_{H}(G)

\displaystyle\leq C(H)\max(B_{1},B_{2},B_{3})

where

\displaystyle B_{1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{q/2-2+1/\Delta}pn^{1/\Delta},

\displaystyle B_{2}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta}}\left(e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{q/2-2+1/\Delta}pn^{1/\Delta},

\displaystyle B_{3}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{q/2-2}pn^{2/\Delta}.

The terms
B_{1},B_{2},B_{3}
correspond to the cases when the edge coming from outside
{G^{*}}
is adjacent to
a
,
b
or none, respectively.

Proof.

We begin by proving the first claim. Observe that

\displaystyle Q_{H}(G)\leq\sum_{u\sim v}N(u,v),

where

(3)

\displaystyle N(u,v)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:V(H)\to[n]\\
\phi(u)=a,\phi(v)=b,\\
\phi\text{ injective}\end{subarray}}\prod_{x\sim u}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),\phi(x))\in E(G)}\prod_{y\sim v}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(v),\phi(y))\in E(G)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(w),\phi(z))\in E(G)}.

N(u,v)
counts all (injective) mappings
\phi:H\to[n]
preserving edges in
H
, such that the edge
(u,v)
maps to
(a,b)
.
Here by
x\sim y
we mean
(x,y)\in E(H)
(for brevity, in this proof we also adopt the convention that for
u
, if we write
x\sim u
, we assume that
x\neq v
, and similarly for
v
). Therefore, it is sufficient to fix
u\sim v
and prove the result, because summing over all distinct
u\sim v
will only change the constant
C(H)
. For fixed
u\sim v
we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\phi}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{x\sim u}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),\phi(x))\in E(G)}\prod_{y\sim v}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(v),\phi(y))\in E(G)}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(w),\phi(z))\in E(G)}\right],

where
\phi
is understood to satisfy the conditions in (
3
). Since
\phi
is injective, all the edge random variables in the expectation above are independent, so we can write:

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi}\prod_{x\sim u}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),\phi(x))\in E(G)}\right]\prod_{y\sim v}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(v),\phi(y))\in E(G)}\right]\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(w),\phi(z))\in E(G)}\right].

For any vertex
\alpha\in[n]
, define the block of
\alpha
as
B_{\alpha}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}[\alpha/n,(\alpha+1)/n)
. Then note that, for instance,
\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(x),\phi(y))\in E(G)}\right]=f(t_{x},t_{y})
for any
t_{x}\in B_{\phi(x)},t_{y}\in B_{\phi(y)}
. For any collection
\{t_{x}\in B_{\phi(x)}:x\in H\}
, we then have that:

\displaystyle=\sum_{\phi}\prod_{x\sim u}f(t_{u},t_{x})\prod_{y\sim v}f(t_{v},t_{y})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}f(t_{w},t_{z}).

Fix
t_{u}\in B_{a},t_{v}\in B_{b}
. Since the summand is a constant over all choices of
(t_{x})_{x\neq u,v}
as long as
(t_{x})_{x\neq u,v}\in\prod_{x\neq u,v}B_{\phi(x)}
, the above sum is the same as

\displaystyle=n^{q-2}\sum_{\phi}\int_{\prod_{x\neq u,v}B_{\phi(x)}}\prod_{x\sim u}f(t_{u},t_{x})\prod_{y\sim v}f(t_{v},t_{y})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}f(t_{w},t_{z})\prod_{x\neq u,v}dt_{x},

where the factor of
n^{q-2}
is because the volume of each block
B_{\phi(x)}
is
n^{-1}
. Finally observe that across various choices of
\phi
, the domains
\prod_{x\neq u,v}B_{\phi(x)}
are disjoint, so that the above is

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\int_{[0,1]^{q-2}}\prod_{x\sim u}f(t_{u},t_{x})\prod_{y\sim v}f(t_{v},t_{y})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}f(t_{w},t_{z})\prod_{x\neq u,v}dt_{x}.

At this point we apply Finner’s inequality with the sets given by
\{x\}
for all
x\sim u
,
\{y\}
for all
y\sim v
, and
\{w,z\}
for all
w\sim z
with
w,z\neq u,v
. We use the weights
p_{i}\equiv\Delta
for all these sets. It is easy to verify that the conditions in Lemma
5
hold, and therefore:

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\prod_{x\sim u}\left(\int f(t_{u},t_{x})^{\Delta}dt_{x}\right)^{1/\Delta}\prod_{y\sim v}\left(\int f(t_{v},t_{y})^{\Delta}dt_{y}\right)^{1/\Delta}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.0pt\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}\left(\int f(t_{w},t_{z})^{\Delta}dt_{w}dt_{z}\right)^{1/\Delta}.

In these products, the values for
x,y,w,z
do not matter because the integrals are always over
[0,1]
for each variable, so we can simplify to (we drop the domains
[0,1]
and
[0,1]^{2}
from the notation):

\displaystyle=n^{q-2}\left(\int f(t_{u},t_{x})^{\Delta}dt_{x}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\int f(t_{v},t_{y})^{\Delta}dt_{y}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\int f(t_{w},t_{z})^{\Delta}dt_{w}dt_{z}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}},

where we use the fact that the number of edges with no endpoint among
u,v
is
q\Delta/2-(2\Delta-1)=q\Delta/2-2\Delta+1
. As the last step, we use the fact that
f(t_{u},t_{x})
is
1
when
t_{x}\in B_{c}
with
c\sim a
in
{G^{*}}
and otherwise it is
p
. Similar observations also hold for
f(t_{v},t_{y})
and
f(t_{w},t_{z})
. Therefore the above is

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\left(\frac{d_{a}}{n}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\frac{d_{b}}{n}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\frac{2e}{n^{2}}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}},

because
f(t_{u},t_{x})=1
if and only if
\phi(u)\sim\phi(x)=a
in
{G^{*}}
(otherwise it is
p
). A similar argument holds for the other two integrals. The factor of
2
(in
2e
) in the last integral is because each edge in
{G^{*}}
is counted twice. Simplifying the above,

\displaystyle=\underbrace{n^{q-2}\cdot n^{-2\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}-2\left(\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}\right)}}_{=1}\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(2e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}},

which completes the proof by absorbing the factor of
2
in front of
e
into the constant
C(H)
.

We now modify this to prove the second claim. Similar to (
3
), define

\displaystyle N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\phi:V(H)\to[n]\\
\phi(u)=a,\phi(v)=b,\\
\phi\text{ injective}\end{subarray}}\prod_{x\sim u}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(u),\phi(x))\in E(G)}\prod_{y\sim v}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(v),\phi(y))\in E(G)}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.0pt\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(w),\phi(z))\in E(G)}\cdot\mathbf{1}_{(\phi(\alpha),\phi(\beta))\notin E({G^{*}})}

for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in E(H)
such that
(\alpha,\beta)\neq(u,v)
. This counts the same quantity as
N(u,v)
, except now,
(\alpha,\beta)
maps to an edge outside
{G^{*}}
. Observe that

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}_{H}(G)\leq\sum_{(\alpha,\beta)\in E(H)}\sum_{E(H)\ni(u,v)\neq(\alpha,\beta)}N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)

so that it is again sufficient to prove the bound in question for
N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)
for fixed
\alpha,\beta,u,v\in H
instead of
Q^{\prime}_{H}(G)
. Using calculations similar to the above we then obtain that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)\leq n^{q-2}\int_{[0,1]^{q-2}}\prod_{x\sim u}f(t_{u},t_{x})\prod_{y\sim v}f(t_{v},t_{y})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z,\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}f(t_{w},t_{z})\cdot\mathbf{1}_{f(t_{\alpha},t_{\beta})=p}\prod_{x}dt_{x},

because
f(t_{\alpha},t_{\beta})=p
if and only if
(\phi(\alpha),\phi(\beta))\notin E({G^{*}})
.
As indicated in the remark after the statement, now there are several cases depending on if
\alpha=u
,
\alpha=v
or
\alpha,\beta\neq u,v
(the cases for
\beta
being similar).

\bullet

\alpha=u,\beta\neq v
: The integral is then bounded by Finner’s inequality similar to the above to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\prod_{\beta\neq x\sim u}\left(\int f(t_{u},t_{x})^{\Delta}dt_{x}\right)^{1/\Delta}\prod_{y\sim v}\left(\int f(t_{v},t_{y})^{\Delta}dt_{y}\right)^{1/\Delta}

\displaystyle\hskip 80.0pt\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\end{subarray}}\left(\int f(t_{w},t_{z})^{\Delta}dt_{w}dt_{z}\right)^{1/\Delta}\left(\int f(t_{u},t_{\beta})^{\Delta}\mathbf{1}_{f(t_{u},t_{\beta})=p}dt_{\beta}\right)^{1/\Delta}.

Observe that the last factor is
\leq p
, so the above is bounded by

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\left(\frac{d_{a}}{n}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta}}\left(\frac{d_{b}}{n}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\frac{2e}{n^{2}}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}}\cdot p

\displaystyle=\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(2e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}}pn^{1/\Delta}.

\bullet

\alpha=v,\beta\neq u
: A calculation similar to the previous case will yield:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)\leq\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-2}{\Delta}}\left(2e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2+\frac{1}{\Delta}}pn^{1/\Delta}.

\bullet

\alpha,\beta\neq u,v
: Then,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}N^{\alpha,\beta}(u,v)

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\prod_{x\sim u}\left(\int f(t_{u},t_{x})^{\Delta}dt_{x}\right)^{1/\Delta}\prod_{y\sim v}\left(\int f(t_{v},t_{y})^{\Delta}dt_{y}\right)^{1/\Delta}

\displaystyle\hskip 60.0pt\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}w\sim z\\
w,z\neq u,v\\
(w,z)\neq(\alpha,\beta)\end{subarray}}\left(\int f(t_{w},t_{z})^{\Delta}dt_{w}dt_{z}\right)^{1/\Delta}\left(\int f(t_{\alpha},t_{\beta})^{\Delta}\mathbf{1}_{f(t_{\alpha},t_{\beta})=p}dt_{\alpha}dt_{\beta}\right)^{1/\Delta}.

Again the last factor is at most
p
, so that the above is bounded by

\displaystyle\leq n^{q-2}\left(\frac{d_{a}}{n}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\frac{d_{b}}{n}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(\frac{2e}{n^{2}}+p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2}\cdot p

\displaystyle=\left(d_{a}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(d_{b}+np^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{\Delta-1}{\Delta}}\left(2e+n^{2}p^{\Delta}\right)^{\frac{q}{2}-2}pn^{2/\Delta}.

Taking a maximum over these three cases and modifying
C=C(H)
, we obtain the desired result.
∎

5.
Few copies

In this section, we will prove our main theorem for “small” values of
k_{n}
. Henceforth we will abbreviate
Q=Q_{H}(G)
.

Definition 3

(Spanned Graph)
.

A graph
G
is called an
H
-spanned graph
(or “spanned graph” for short) if
G
is connected and each edge is contained in a copy of
H
in
G
. We say it is spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
if there are
\ell
copies of
H
in
G
such that each edge of
G
is contained in one of these copies. We will often use
\ell_{*}=\ell_{*}(G)
to indicate the smallest possible value of
\ell
given
G
.

For brevity, we will often say that a graph is spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
if it is a spanned graph and it is spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
.

This is the analog of “triangle-induced subgraphs” as defined in
[
GHN22
]
. Also note that
\ell_{*}=1
if and only if
G=H
up to isomorphism.

Next we define some useful events:

•

F_{\ell}
: there is a spanned subgraph of
G
which is spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
.

•

D_{s}
: there are
s
vertex-disjoint copies of
H
in
G

(for brevity we will write “disjoint” to mean vertex-disjoint)
.

•

E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
: there are (at least)
s+m
disjoint spanned subgraphs of
G
, with
s
of them containing exactly one copy of
H
, and the remaining
m
of them each spanned by
\ell_{i}
copies for
1\leq i\leq m
. Since the order of the
\ell_{i}
are immaterial, we will always assume that
2\leq\ell_{1}\leq\ell_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\ell_{m}
. When
s=0
, we drop
s
, and simply write
E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
. (Here
s
stands for “singleton” copies, and
m
stands for “multiple” copies)

Using
\circ
for the “disjoint occurrence” operator on events (see
[
VK85
, Section 3]
for the definition), it is easy to see that

\displaystyle E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}=D_{s}\circ F_{\ell_{1}}\circ\cdots\circ F_{\ell_{m}},

and so

(4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}})\leq\mathbb{P}(D_{s})\prod_{i=1}^{m}\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell_{i}}),

due to the BK-inequality, see
[
VK85
]
. To see why such a bound is useful, observe that if a graph
G
satisfies
Q=Q_{H}(G)\geq k_{n}
, one can drop edges in
G
not contained in any copy of
H
, and decompose the remaining graph into connected components. Clearly each remaining component is
H
-spanned, and no copies of
H
were deleted, so that there are numbers
s,\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}
such that
G\in E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
. The rest of this section will be devoted to efficiently union bounding over various choices of these numbers. But first we bound the probabilities of
D_{s}
and
F_{\ell}
individually.

Lemma 8
.

\mathbb{P}(D_{s})\leq C\exp\left(-s\log s\right)
for some
C=C(H)
.

Proof.

The number of ways we can pick
s
disjoint subsets of
[n]
is at most

\displaystyle\frac{1}{s!}\binom{n}{q}\binom{n-q}{q}\cdots\binom{n-(s-1)q}{q}=\frac{n!}{s!(q!)^{s}(n-qs)!}\leq\frac{n^{qs}}{s!(q!)^{s}}.

Given a fixed set of vertices
S
of size
q
, the probability of it having a copy of
H
is at most

\displaystyle Cp_{n}^{-e}=Cn^{-2e/\Delta}=Cn^{-q},

where the constant
C=C(H)
is due to the number of isomorphic copies of
H
in
K_{q}
. Then by a union bound, the probability of
D_{s}
satisfies

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(D_{s})\leq C\exp(qs\log n-s\log(q!)-\log(s!)-qs\log n)=C\exp(-s\log(q!)-\log(s!)).

By Stirling’s formula, we have
\log(s!)=s\log s-s+O(\log s)
. Therefore

\displaystyle s\log(q!)+s\log s-s+O(\log s)\geq s\log s,\quad\forall s\geq 1,

(using
q\geq 3
and
\log 6>1
) finishing the proof.
∎

The next lemma delivers the control on
\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S)-v(S)
, serving as a replacement for the “tree-excess edges” interpretation of
e(S)-v(S)+1
in
[
GHN22
]
as described in the idea of proof section. This will be useful for furnishing a bound on
\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell})
.

Lemma 9
.

If
S
is a graph spanned by
\ell\geq 2
copies of
H
then
\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S)-v(S)\geq Ce(S)
, where
C=C(H)
.

Proof.

Observe that the optimal number of copies
\ell_{*}=\ell_{*}(S)
needed to span
S
satisfies
\ell_{*}\geq Ce(S)
where
C=1/q^{2}
, because each copy of
H
contains at most
q^{2}
edges. Order these copies of
H
in
S
as
H_{1},\ldots,H_{\ell_{*}}
,
such that for each
i
,
V(H_{i})\cap V(S_{i-1})\neq\varnothing
, where
S_{i}
is the subgraph of
S
spanned by
H_{1},\ldots,H_{i}
(this can be done because
S
is spanned and connected). Note that in general
S_{i}
is distinct from the subgraph of
S
induced by
V(S_{i})
. Each edge incident on any
x\in V(H_{i})-V(S_{i-1})
comes from
H_{i}
, and hence the degree of any such
x
is exactly
\Delta
in
S_{i}
. For each
i>1
, also write
b_{i}\geq 1
for the number of edges between
V(H_{i})-V(S_{i-1})
and
V(S_{i-1})
(the number of boundary edges), and let
v_{i}=|V(H_{i})-V(S_{i-1})|
(the number of new vertices). See Figure
1
below for an illustration of these definitions.

Figure 1.
Variables in the proof of Lemma
9
with four copies of
H
.

We track the quantity
f_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S_{i})-v(S_{i})
. Observe that
e(S_{i})\geq e(S_{i-1})+b_{i}+e(H_{i}-S_{i-1})
, where
H_{i}-S_{i-1}
is the subgraph of
S_{i}
induced by
V(H_{i})-V(S_{i-1})
(the inequality is due to ignoring edges in
H_{i}
with both endpoints in
V(S_{i-1})
). Crucially, note that

\displaystyle 2e(H_{i}-S_{i-1})+b_{i}=\Delta v_{i}

because both sides count the edges within
V(H_{i})-V(S_{i-1})
twice. Therefore

\displaystyle e(S_{i})\geq e(S_{i-1})+b_{i}+\frac{\Delta v_{i}-b_{i}}{2}=e(S_{i-1})+\frac{b_{i}}{2}+\frac{\Delta v_{i}}{2},

and so,

\displaystyle f_{i}=\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S_{i})-v(S_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S_{i-1})+\frac{b_{i}}{\Delta}+v_{i}-v(S_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S_{i-1})+\frac{b_{i}}{\Delta}+v_{i}-v(S_{i-1})-v_{i}

(substituting
v(S_{i})=v(S_{i-1})+v_{i}
). Therefore, the above is

\displaystyle=f_{i-1}+\frac{b_{i}}{\Delta}\geq f_{i-1}+\frac{1}{\Delta}\quad\text{(since $b_{i}\geq 1$).}

Since
f_{1}=0
we get
f_{\ell_{*}}\geq\frac{\ell_{*}-1}{\Delta}\geq\frac{\ell_{*}}{2\Delta}
as long as
\ell_{*}\geq 2
which holds if
\ell\geq 2
(this is because
\ell\geq 2
implies that there are more than
e(H)
edges in
S
). To conclude the proof, recall that
\ell_{*}\geq e(S)/q^{2}=Ce(S)
.
∎

Using this structural result, we can now provide an upper bound on
\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell})
.

Lemma 10
.

\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell})\leq\exp(-C\ell^{2/q}\log n)
for all
2\leq\ell\leq n^{c}
and all sufficiently large
n
, where
c=c(H),C=C(H)
are constants.

Proof.

For a fixed graph
S
spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
, the probability of its occurrence in
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p=n^{-2/\Delta})
is at most

\displaystyle\binom{n}{v(S)}p^{e(S)}\leq n^{v(S)-\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S)},

and as we saw above in Lemma
9
, for such a graph,
\frac{2}{\Delta}e(S)-v(S)\geq Ce(S)
. Therefore this probability is at most
\exp(-Ce(S)\log n)
.

Let
F_{\ell,v,e}
be the event that there is a subgraph of
G
with
v
vertices,
e
edges, and spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
(note that
v\leq q\ell
). The number of such
S
is at most
\binom{v^{2}}{e}\leq v^{2e}
, so by a direct union bound,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell,v,e})

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-Ce\log n)v^{2e}

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-Ce\log n)\exp(2e\log(q\ell)),

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-Ce\log n),

by a different choice of
C
, as long as
\ell\leq n^{c}
for some small
c
depending on
H
. Finally, using the fact that
\ell\leq Ce^{q/2}
(using Lemma
6
) we see that the above is

\displaystyle\leq\exp(-C\ell^{2/q}\log n).

For a graph spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
we have
v,e\leq C\ell
, so by union bounding over all such possibilities, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell})\leq C\ell^{2}\exp(-C\ell^{2/q}\log n)\leq\exp(-C\ell^{2/q}\log n),

(up to changing
C
) for sufficiently large
n
.
∎

As alluded to earlier, we will now combine the estimates above using a union bound. The following definition will be useful.

Definition 4
.

For a tuple
(c_{1},c_{2},\ldots,c_{t})
, define
\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}}
to be the event that in the random graph
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p_{n})
, there are
c_{1}+\cdots+c_{t}
disjoint spanned graphs, with
c_{i}
of them spanned by
2^{i}
copies of
H
, for all
i
.

Observe that
\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}}
is a special case of events of the form
E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
where
m=\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}
and the
\ell_{i}
s are powers of 2. Also, if a graph
G
satisfies
E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
, then there is a tuple
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
such that it also satisfies
\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}}
, with

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}2^{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\ell_{j}.

This is essentially done via “dropping” copies of
H
till the components are spanned by
2^{i}
components for some
i
. More precisely, for each valid
i
, define

(5)

\displaystyle c_{i}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}|\{j:2^{i}\leq\ell_{j}<2^{i+1}\}|.

We claim that
G\in\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}}
. Let
U_{1},\ldots,U_{m}
be some choice of disjoint spanned subgraphs of
G
witnessing
E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
. Choose
j
such that
U_{j}
is spanned by
\ell_{j}
copies of
H
and let
i
be such that
2^{i}\leq\ell_{j}<2^{i+1}
. Order these copies of
H
such that the subgraph induced by any number of copies from the beginning is connected (which can always be done because
U_{j}
is connected). Then take the union of the first
2^{i}
copies. By construction this is a connected subgraph spanned by
2^{i}
copies of
H
. Doing this for each
j
proves the claim.

As we will shortly see,
\mathbb{P}(E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}})
is comparable to
\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}})
, whereas the number of possible choices is a lot less for
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
than for
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m})
, facilitating an efficient union bound.

Lemma 11
.

For
2\leq k_{n}\leq n^{c}
(for a sufficiently small
c=c(H)
), we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q\geq k_{n})\leq C\exp\left(-C^{\prime}\min(k_{n}\log k_{n},k_{n}^{2/q}\log n)\right),

where
C=C(H),C^{\prime}=C^{\prime}(H)
are constants depending only on
H
.

As described earlier, this lemma demonstrates the transition in tail behavior promised in our main theorem. However, this result only holds for
k_{n}
at most a small polynomial of
n
, and one must show that there are
no other transitions
, i.e., this behavior persists all the way up to the maximum possible value of
k_{n}
. This is achieved in the next section after this proof.

Proof.

We abbreviate
k=k_{n}
throughout this proof. Recall from the discussion below (
4
) that the event
\{Q\geq k\}
is equivalent to

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,m\geq 0,\ell_{i}\geq 2\\
s+\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i}\geq k\end{subarray}}E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}.

However any graph satisfying this also satisfies the event

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,m\geq 0,\ell_{i}\geq 2\\
s+\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i}=k\end{subarray}}E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}},

because a spanned graph spanned by
\ell
copies of
H
has a subgraph spanned by
\ell^{\prime}
copies of
H
for any
\ell^{\prime}\leq\ell
by reasoning as below (
5
). We therefore focus on bounding the probability of the latter.

Using the BK inequality (
4
) and the bound on
\mathbb{P}(F_{\ell})
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}})

\displaystyle\leq\prod_{i=1}^{t}\mathbb{P}(F_{2^{i}})^{c_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-C\log n\sum_{i=1}^{t}c_{i}2^{2i/q}\right)

We use this bound and the fact that for each collection
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}
(with each
\ell_{i}\geq 2
), we can find a vector
c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}
with

(6)

\displaystyle\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i}\geq\sum_{j\leq t}c_{i}2^{i}\geq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i},

such that
E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}\subseteq\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}}
. This allows us to conclude that for any
k
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 1,\ell_{i}\geq 2\\
\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i}=k\end{subarray}}E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}t\geq 1,c_{i}\geq 0\\
k\geq\sum_{i}c_{i}2^{i}\geq k/2\end{subarray}}\widetilde{E}_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{t}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\exp((\log k)^{2})\exp\left(-C\log n\cdot\min\sum_{i\geq 1}c_{i}2^{2i/q}\right),

where the
\min
is over all possible
c_{i}
s satisfying the condition in (
6
). The
\exp((\log k)^{2})
term is a simple upper bound on the number of possible tuples
(c_{1},\ldots,c_{t})
where
t\leq\log_{2}k
and
0\leq c_{i}\leq k/2
. By an elementary inequality (see Lemma
17
in the Appendix) we see that the above is

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(C\left(\log^{2}k-k^{2/q}\log n\right)\right).

Now observe that
E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}=D_{s}\circ E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}
. Another application of the BK inequality and our estimate of
\mathbb{P}(D_{s})
from Lemma
8
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,m\geq 0,\ell_{i}\geq 2\\
s+\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i}=k\end{subarray}}E^{s}_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{s\leq k}\mathbb{P}(D_{s})\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\geq 0,\ell_{i}\geq 2\\
\sum_{i\leq m}\ell_{i}=k-s\end{subarray}}E_{\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq C\exp(\log^{2}k+\log k)\exp\left(-C\min_{1\leq s\leq k}\left(s\log s+(k-s)^{2/q}\log n\right)\right),

at which point we invoke Lemma
18
from the Appendix to obtain that the above is

\displaystyle\leq C\exp(2\log^{2}k)\exp\left(-C\min(k\log k,k^{2/q}\log n)\right).

Finally observe that since
2\log^{2}k\ll\min(k\log k,k^{2/q}\log n)
as
k\to\infty
, by modifying constants we may drop the prefactor of
\exp(2\log^{2}k)
, yielding the required result.
∎

6.
Many copies

In this section we will consider the case of large
k_{n}
and demonstrate that the behavior described in Lemma
11
actually persists all the way up to the maximum possible value of
k_{n}
. However our results here will only hold for
k_{n}
such that
k_{n}\gg f(n)
for all
f(n)=\mathrm{poly}\log n
(we will assume this lower bound on
k_{n}
throughout this section). But this suffices to complete the proof of our main theorem since Lemma
11
holds even for
k_{n}
that are small polynomial powers of
n
.

Our arguments here will adapt a version of the techniques from
[
HMS22
]
and hence is related to the ideas in
[
CHH21
]
. The main idea is to consider a class of subgraphs (called “cores”) which are present with high probability in
G
when conditioned to have
k_{n}
triangles. Once such a subgraph is planted in
G
, the expectation of
Q
is almost as much as the required number of
k_{n}
. As in
[
HMS22
]
, before defining a “core” we define a relaxed version called a “seed”.

Let
w_{n}=1/\log n
in the sequel. In principle,
w_{n}
can be taken to be anything that is
1/\mathrm{poly}\log n
, but we fix it so for concreteness.

Definition 5

(Seed)
.

Let
S\subseteq K_{n}
be a (labeled) graph on the vertex set
V=[n]
. We call
S
a seed if and only if:

(1)

\mathbb{E}_{S}Q\geq(1-w_{n})k_{n}
,

(2)

e(S)\leq C_{s}w_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/q}\log(1/p_{n})
,

where
e(S)
is the number of edges in
S
. Here
\mathbb{E}_{S}Q=\mathbb{E}\left[Q\mid S\subseteq K_{n}\right]
is the conditional expectation of the number of copies of
H
in
G
given that the edges in
S
are present in
G
, and
C_{s}
is a constant depending on
H
that will be fixed later.

Following the arguments of
[
HMS22
]
one can deduce the following.

Lemma 12

(Must have a seed)
.

There is a sequence
\xi_{n}\to 0
such that

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q\geq k_{n})\leq(1+\xi_{n})\mathbb{P}(\text{$G$ has a seed})

where
G\sim\mathcal{G}(n,p_{n})
.

Proof.

For a subgraph
S\subseteq K_{n}
, set
Z_{S}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{1}_{\{G\cap S\text{ has no seed}\}}
(we will also let
Z\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}Z_{K_{n}}
) and for any
H
, a subset of edges of
K_{n}
forming an isomorphic copy of
H
, denote
Y_{\textsf{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\text{all edges in }\textsf{H}\text{ are present in }G\}}
. Then observe that

(7)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q\geq k_{n},\text{$G$ has no seed})=\mathbb{P}(QZ\geq k_{n})\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\ell}Z\right]}{k_{n}^{\ell}},

for any
\ell\geq 1
(we will choose
\ell
later). Since
Q=\sum_{\textsf{H}}Y_{\textsf{H}}
(sum over all possible edge subsets of
K_{n}
which are isomorphic to
H
).

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\ell}Z\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\textsf{H}_{1}}\cdots Y_{\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\cdot Z\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\textsf{H}_{1}}\cdots Y_{\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\cdot Z_{\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}}\right],\qquad\qquad\text{because $Z_{S}\geq Z_{T}$ if $S\subseteq T$},

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\mathbb{P}(\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell}\text{ are present},\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}\text{ has no seed}),

But
\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}
having (or not having) a seed is a deterministic fact, so we can reduce the sum over all collections of
\textsf{H}_{i}
where this holds. We can group the terms in the sum above by
\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}
to obtain

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}\text{ with no seed }}\mathbb{P}(\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}\text{ are present})\sum_{\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\textsf{H}_{\ell}}\mid Y_{\textsf{H}_{i}}=1,\forall i\leq\ell-1\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{H}_{1},\ldots,\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}\text{ with no seed }}\mathbb{E}\left[Y_{\textsf{H}_{1}}\cdots Y_{\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}}Z_{\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}}\right]\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}}Q}_{\leq(1-w_{n})k_{n}},

where we use the fact that
\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}
has no seed, and in particular, is not a seed itself. But we can only apply this reasoning if the total number of edges in
\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}
is at most
Cw_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/q}\log(1/p_{n})
(see Definition
5
) which happens at least as long as

(8)

\displaystyle\ell\leq C_{s}q^{-2}w_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/q}\log(1/p_{n}),

because
\textsf{H}_{1}\cup\ldots\cup\textsf{H}_{\ell-1}
has at most
q^{2}\ell
edges (and thus the only reason it is not a seed is that the conditional expectation condition is violated). Observe that the bound obtained for
\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\ell}Z\right]
has a recursive structure, which we can iterate in
\ell
to obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[Q^{\ell}Z\right]\leq(1-w_{n})^{\ell}k_{n}^{\ell},

giving us the eventual upper bound via (
7
)

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q\geq k_{n},\text{$G$ has no seed})\leq(1-w_{n})^{\ell}

for all
\ell
satisfying the bound in (
8
).
Now observe that a clique of size
ck_{n}^{1/q}
has at least
k_{n}
copies of
H
(where
c=c(H)
is a constant), and it has
Ck_{n}^{2/q}
many edges. Therefore by choosing
C_{s}
in the definition of a seed large enough, we can make this clique a seed. The probability of its occurrence is

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\text{$ck_{n}^{1/q}$ clique in $G$})\geq\exp\left(-Ck_{n}^{2/q}\log n\right)

(recall that various occurrences of
C
can mean different
H
-dependent constants).
Choose
\ell={\lfloor C_{s}q^{-2}w_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/q}\log(1/p_{n})\rfloor}
to get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Q\geq k_{n},\text{$G$ has no seed})

\displaystyle\leq(1-w_{n})^{\ell}

\displaystyle\leq\exp\left(-Cq^{-2}k_{n}^{2/q}\log(1/p_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\xi_{n}\mathbb{P}(ck_{n}^{2/q}\text{ clique})

where
\xi_{n}\to 0
as long as
C_{s}/q^{2}
is large enough. But as we saw above, this is a seed, so we can replace the bound above by

\displaystyle\leq\xi_{n}\mathbb{P}(G_{n}\text{ has seed}),

which finishes the proof.
∎

The previous lemma reduces our task to upper bounding
\mathbb{P}(G\text{ has a seed})
. This is essentially achieved via union-bounding over all possible seeds. But the number of possible seeds is extremely high, so we instead consider a more restrictive structure called “cores”, as defined below. Each seed contains a core, so we have a version of the preceding lemma with
\{\text{$G$ has a seed}\}
replaced by
\{\text{$G$ has a core}\}
.

Definition 6

(Core)
.

Let
{G^{*}}\subseteq K_{n}
be a (labeled) graph on
V=[n]
. We call it a core if it has no isolated vertices and,

(1)

\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}Q\geq(1-2w_{n})k_{n}
,

(2)

e({G^{*}})\leq C_{s}w_{n}^{-1}k_{n}^{2/q}\log(1/p_{n})
,

(3)

\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}Q-\mathbb{E}_{{G^{*}}-f}Q\geq t_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\displaystyle\frac{w_{n}^{2}k_{n}^{(q-2)/q}}{C_{s}\log(1/p_{n})}\left(=\widetilde{\Omega}(k_{n}^{(q-2)/q})\right)
, for all edges
f\in E({G^{*}})
,

that is, it is a (slightly weaker) seed, but now each edge has to contribute significantly.

As indicated earlier, every seed contains a core:

Lemma 13
.

If
G
is a seed, then there is a subset
{G^{*}}\subseteq G
which is a core.

Proof.

We iteratively delete edges violating (3). Removing such an edge does not reduce
\mathbb{E}_{G}Q
by more than
t_{n}
. Therefore over all
e(G)
edges, the maximum possible decrease is
t_{n}e(G)\leq w_{n}k_{n}
, so that the reduced graph still satisfies (1). If we cannot remove an edge, it is already a core.
∎

The strict restrictions on cores allow us to prove a few crucial structural results about them.

Lemma 14

(Cores have many copies of
H
)
.

For a core
{G^{*}}
, let
Q_{H}({G^{*}})
be the number of copies of
H
in
{G^{*}}
. Then
Q_{H}({G^{*}})\geq k_{n}-o(k_{n})
. In particular, via Lemma
6
, there is a
C
such that
e({G^{*}})\geq Ck_{n}^{2/q}
.

Proof.

We denote by
H
an isomorphic copy of
H
in
K_{n}
, i.e. a subset of edges which are isomorphic to
H
. Then we know that

\displaystyle(1-2w_{n})k_{n}\leq\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}Q=\sum_{\textsf{H}}p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|}\leq Q_{H}({G^{*}})+\sum_{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|\geq 1}p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|},

where
\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}
is the collection of edges in
H
that are not in
{G^{*}}
.
For a given copy
H
in
K_{n}
, fix an isomorphism
\phi_{\textsf{H}}
mapping
E(H)\to\textsf{H}
. Let
c
be the number of edges in
\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}
with both endpoints in
V({G^{*}})
,
b
be the number of edges in
\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}
with one endpoint in
V(K_{n})-V({G^{*}})
and other in
V({G^{*}})
, and let
x
be the number of vertices in
H
outside
{G^{*}}
.
Also denote by
X\subseteq H
the subgraph which is mapped (under
\phi_{\textsf{H}}
) to outside
{G^{*}}
(so that
x=|V(X)|
), and let
Y\subseteq H
be the subgraph of
H
whose vertices are mapped (under
\phi_{\textsf{H}}
) to
{G^{*}}
and whose edges are mapped to
E({G^{*}})
(so that
V(X)\cup V(Y)=V(H)
but
E(X)\cup E(Y)
is not
E(H)
; the edges counted in
b
and
c
are the ones in
E(H)-(E(X)\cup E(Y))
). Observe that
Y
may contain isolated vertices. Denote by
y=|V(Y)|
. See Figure
2
below for an illustration of these definitions.

Figure 2.
Variables in the proof of Lemma
14
.

We will now upper-bound the contribution of all copies
H
which satisfy the configuration specified by
(b,c,X,Y)
. Observe that

\displaystyle x+y

\displaystyle=q,

\displaystyle|E(Y)|+|E(X)|+b+c

\displaystyle=E(H)=q\Delta/2,

\displaystyle 2|E(X)|+b

\displaystyle=\Delta|V(X)|=x\Delta.

Solving these equations we get

\displaystyle|E(X)|

\displaystyle=\frac{x\Delta-b}{2},

\displaystyle|E(Y)|

\displaystyle=\frac{q\Delta}{2}-b-c-\frac{x\Delta}{2}+\frac{b}{2}=\frac{q\Delta-x\Delta-b-2c}{2}.

Since
b+|E(X)|+c
edges come from outside
{G^{*}}
, for each such
H
,
p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|}
is at most

\displaystyle Cn^{-\frac{2}{\Delta}\cdot(b+|E(X)|+c)}=Cn^{-\frac{2}{\Delta}\cdot\frac{x\Delta+b+2c}{2}}=Cn^{-x-\frac{b}{\Delta}-\frac{2c}{\Delta}}.

Now we need to count the number of such copies of
H
. Firstly, the image of
X
can be chosen in (at most)
n^{x}
ways, which can be included in the “cost” to obtain
n^{-\frac{b}{\Delta}-\frac{2c}{\Delta}}
. The final cost will be the product of this with the number of copies of
Y
in
{G^{*}}
(fixing it will fix all the vertices, and therefore all the edges counted in
b
and
c
).

To count the number of copies of
Y
in
{G^{*}}
, observe that
Y
being a subgraph of
H
has max-degree
\Delta
. So we can apply Lemma
6
to obtain that the count is at most

Ce^{|E(Y)|/\Delta}v^{y-2|E(Y)|/\Delta}

where
v=v({G^{*}})
and
e=e({G^{*}})
. Putting everything together, the total cost is at most

\displaystyle Ce^{\frac{q-x}{2}-\frac{b+2c}{2\Delta}}v^{q-x-2\frac{q-x}{2}+\frac{b+2c}{\Delta}}n^{-\frac{b}{\Delta}-\frac{2c}{\Delta}}=Ce^{\frac{q-x}{2}-\frac{b+2c}{2\Delta}}v^{\frac{b+2c}{\Delta}}n^{-\frac{b+2c}{\Delta}}\leq Ce^{\frac{q}{2}-\frac{x+b+2c}{2\Delta}}

as
v\leq n
and
\Delta\geq 1
. If
b+2c+x\geq 1
, then this is
\widetilde{o}(k_{n})
because
e=\widetilde{O}(k_{n}^{2/q})
(and
k_{n}\gg\mathrm{poly}\log n
as assumed at the beginning of this section). But this is indeed the case since
H
is connected and
|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|\geq 1
, implying that the contribution of the part of the sum corresponding to the configuration
(b,c,X,Y)
is
\widetilde{o}(k_{n})
.

Considering all possible distinct configurations of
(b,c,X,Y)
(of which there are
C=C(H)
many), we see that the total contribution of the second term in the first display of the proof is
\widetilde{o}(k_{n})
. This concludes the proof.
∎

As indicated in the idea of proof section, the following “product of degrees” lemma is a key combinatorial input in our proof, derived via an application of Finner’s inequality.

Lemma 15

(Product of degrees)
.

There is a function
g(n)=\widetilde{\Omega}(k_{n}^{1/q})
, depending only on
H
, such that the following holds for all sufficiently large
n
. Suppose
{G^{*}}
is a core. For every edge
f=(a,b)\in E({G^{*}})
, we have
d_{a}d_{b}\geq g^{2}(n)
and
d_{a},d_{b}\geq\Delta
. Therefore every vertex in a core has degree at least
\Delta
since none of them are isolated.

Every vertex which is a part of some copy of
H
in
{G^{*}}
must have degree at least
\Delta
. But the lemma above does not claim that every vertex in
{G^{*}}
is a part of some copy of
H
, just the weaker statement that every vertex has degree at least
\Delta
. Specifically, it does not rule out the case of a vertex which has a very large degree in
{G^{*}}
but is not part of any copy of
H
in
{G^{*}}
.

Proof.

This proof is a direct application of Lemma
7
. Observe that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{G^{*}}Q-\mathbb{E}_{{G^{*}}-f}Q

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{H}}p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|}-\sum_{\textsf{H}}p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-({G^{*}}-f)|}

where
|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|
is again a shorthand for the number of edges in
H
not in
{G^{*}}
. Therefore the above is

\displaystyle=\sum_{\textsf{H}}p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|}-p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-({G^{*}}-f)|}.

Now observe that for any
H
which does not contain
f
, the term is zero, and for any
H
containing
f
,
|\textsf{H}-({G^{*}}-f)|=1+|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|
. Therefore, the above is

\displaystyle=(1-p_{n})\sum_{\textsf{H}\ni f}p_{n}^{|\textsf{H}-{G^{*}}|},

where we sum over all
H
containing
f
. This is bounded by

The next lemma proves the theorem of this paper in the case of large
k_{n}
. As mentioned earlier, combined with Lemma
11
, this will complete the proof of the main theorem.

The following is a high-level overview of the main steps involved. The proof proceeds via successively specializing the class of cores whose (collective) probability we have to bound. We bi-partition the vertices of the core into high degree and low degree vertices and using Lemma
15
ensure that edges from low degree vertices can only go to sufficiently high degree vertices. Since there are a relatively small number of high degree vertices, we can fix all of them in
V(K_{n})=[n]
(without incurring too high a cost from the union bound). At this point the case where at least half of the edges in the core comes from the edges within this high-degree set is dealt with using straightforward binomial tail probabilities, leaving us with the more complicated case when at least half of the edges go between the high and low degree sets. The remainder of the proof assumes that this is the case. Now we attempt to fix all the low degree vertices as well. But the straightforward bound of
e=E({G^{*}})
on their count is too lossy, thus necessitating further control. However, by a dyadic decomposition, we can fix all of them except the ones with very low degree. But vertices with extremely low degree can have edges only to vertices with very high degree, again by Lemma
15
,
whose
number is very small allowing us to also fix all the neighbor sets of these low degree vertices (and therefore their degrees as well). However, since each vertex in the core has degree at least
\Delta
(Lemma
15
), the number of such low degree vertices cannot exceed
1/\Delta
times the number of edges between the high and low degree sets. This improved bound by a factor of
1/\Delta
indeed turns out to be sufficient to balance the cost of fixing the low degree vertices against the probability of these edges actually existing (recall that we have already fixed the neighbor sets for these vertices). A calculation then shows that this works out favorably, finishing the proof.

Lemma 17
.

Let
p>1
be a real. Then for any sequence of nonnegative reals
(x_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
we have
\sum_{i}x_{i}^{1/p}\geq\left(\sum_{i}x_{i}\right)^{1/p}
.

Proof.

Let
s=\sum x_{i}
, and
y_{i}=x_{i}/s
for
i\in[n]
. Then
y_{i}\leq 1
, so
y_{i}^{1/p}\geq y_{i}
. Therefore
\sum_{i}y_{i}^{1/p}\geq\sum_{i}y_{i}=1
. Substituting back the values of
y_{i}
, we obtain the result.
∎

Lemma 18

Lemma 19

(Binomial tails)
.

Suppose
N=N(n),M=M(n)\to\infty
and
p_{n}=o(M/N)
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(N,p_{n})\geq M)\leq\exp\left(-(1-o(1))M\log\left(\dfrac{M}{Np_{n}}\right)\right).

Proof.

By Chernoff’s method we know that,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Bin}(N,p_{n})\geq M)\leq\exp\left(-NH_{p_{n}}(M/N)\right),

where
H_{p}(t)=D_{\text{KL}}(\mathrm{Ber}(t)\ \|\ \mathrm{Ber}(p))
,
so it suffices to show that
H_{p_{n}}\left(\dfrac{M}{N}\right)\geq(1-o(1))\frac{M}{N}\log\left(\dfrac{M}{Np_{n}}\right)
, which can be reduced to proving that for
p\ll x<1
, we have

\displaystyle H_{p}(x)\geq(1-o(1))x\log(x/p).

It is indeed well-known that
H_{p}(x)\sim x\log(x/p)
in this regime, see for instance
[

. For the purposes of completeness we include a proof of this weaker variant.
To see this recall that

\displaystyle H_{p}(x)=x\log\left(\dfrac{x}{p}\right)+\underbrace{(1-x)\log\left(\dfrac{1-x}{1-p}\right)}_{<0},

so it suffices to show that

\displaystyle(1-x)\log\frac{1-p}{1-x}=o(1)\cdot x\log\frac{x}{p}\iff\frac{1-x}{x}\frac{\log\frac{1-p}{1-x}}{\log\frac{x}{p}}\to 0\iff\frac{1-x}{x}\frac{\log\left(1+\frac{x-p}{1-x}\right)}{\log\frac{x}{p}}\to 0.

Now
\log(1+x)\leq x
, so this is implied by

\displaystyle\frac{1-x}{x}\frac{x-p}{1-x}\log^{-1}(x/p)\to 0

which is clear because
x-p\leq x
and
x/p\to\infty
.
∎
