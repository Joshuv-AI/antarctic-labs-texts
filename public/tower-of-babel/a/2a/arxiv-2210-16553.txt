Abstract
Diophantine exponents are ones of the simplest quantitative characteristics responsible for the approximation properties of linear subspaces of a Euclidean space. This survey is aimed at describing the current state of the area of Diophantine approximation which studies Diophantine exponents and relations they satisfy. We discuss classical Diophantine exponents arising in the problem of approximating zero with the set of the values of several linear forms at integer points, their analogues in Diophantine approximation with weights, multiplicative Diophantine exponents, and Diophantine exponents of lattices. We pay special attention to the transference principle.



Key words:
Diophantine approximation, geometry of numbers, Diophantine exponents, transference principle.

1
Introduction

Given a square with side length 1, is the length of its diagonal equal to a ratio of two integers? Can one construct a circle with the same area as that square using straightedge and compass? These questions date back to the times of Ancient Greece – about two and a half thousand years ago. The Greeks answered the first question immediately, whereas the second one remained open until only little more than a century ago.

The answer to the first question proved the existence of irrational numbers. However, explicit examples of such numbers, as a rule, were algebraic, i.e. they were roots of polynomials with rational coefficients. And until the 19-th century, it had been unclear whether there exist non-algebraic, i.e. transcendental, numbers. It was Liouville who managed to answer this question
1
1

1

Of course, it is obvious nowadays that transcendental numbers do exist, as the set of algebraic numbers is countable and therefore it is a set of zero measure. But the thing is that cardinality theory was developed by Cantor only in the seventies of the 19-th century. Before him, such concepts had been unavailable. Thus, the result by Liouville, which he obtained in 1844, was truly outstanding.
by showing that algebraic numbers cannot be approximated by rationals “too well”, after which he easily constructed an example of a transcendental number. This is how the theory of
Diophantine approximation
was born. It was this theory that provided a proof of the transcendence of
\pi
and, therefore, an answer to the second of the two questions mentioned above – that it is impossible to construct a circle with the same area as a given square using straightedge and compass.

Hence came the understanding that real numbers can be ranked by their “irrationality measure” – the better an irrational number can be approximated by rationals, the more irrational it is considered. The simplest quantitative characteristic of how well an irrational number
\theta
can be approximated by rationals, is its
Diophantine exponent
– the supremum of real
\gamma
such that the inequality

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{q^{1+\gamma}}

admits infinitely many solutions in integer
p
,
q
.

The problem of approximation of a real number with rationals has a very natural geometric interpretation, which helps working with more general problems – when we need to find so called
simultaneous approximations
, i.e. when several numbers are to be approximated by rationals with
the same
denominator. In this context there also appear
Diophantine exponents
as the simplest measure of deviation from “rationality”.

It is worth mentioning that the problem of simultaneous approximations to the powers of a single number
\theta
, i.e. to the numbers
1,\,\theta,\,\theta^{2},\,\theta^{3},\ldots,\,\theta^{n}
, provides one of the most important tools for studying algebraic numbers. For instance, in his proof of transcendence of
e
, Hermite actually constructed “good” simultaneous approximations to the powers of
e
.

For the past several years, the theory of Diophantine approximation experienced a significant progress: the number of new theorems exceeded the number of those proved before. In this survey we tried to gather as many results as possible concerning various Diophantine exponents – and there are more than a dozen types of them nowadays. We have no claim for exhaustiveness and we provide only the proofs that are concise enough and essential for understanding the methods of working with the objects under study. Detailed proofs can be found in original papers. The multidimensional setting will be of most interest to us, as multidimensionality delivers a variety of natural ways to define Diophantine exponents. We pay special attention to the so called
transference principle
, which connects “dual” problems. We note also that we almost do not mention
intermediate
Diophantine exponents, since a proper account of what is currently known about them would double the size of the paper.

Approximation of a real number by rationals

2.1
Diophantine exponent and irrationality measure

In Introduction we actually gave the following

Definition 1
.

Let
\theta
be a real number. Its
Diophantine exponent

\omega(\theta)
is defined as the supremum of real
\gamma
such that the inequality

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{q^{1+\gamma}}

(1)

admits infinitely many solutions in integer
p
,
q
.

In the case of a single number, it is more traditional, however, to talk about the
irrationality measure
of
\theta
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\theta
be a real number. Its
irrationality measure

\mu(\theta)
is defined as the supremum of real
\gamma
such that the inequality

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{q^{\gamma}}

(2)

admits infinitely many solutions in integer coprime
p
,
q
.

Definition
2
differs from
1
only by the absence of 1 in the exponent in (
2

p
and
q
should be coprime. Thus, for an irrational
\theta
we have

\mu(\theta)=\omega(\theta)+1.

Whereas, if
\theta\in\mathbb{Q}
, then
\omega(\theta)=\infty
,
\mu(\theta)=1
(see Proposition
1
below).

Most of results describing the measure of deviation of a number from rationality are usually stated in terms of
\mu(\theta)
. However, the most reasonable way to define Diophantine exponents in the multidimensional setting gives exactly
\omega(\theta)
in the particular one-dimensional case – not
\mu(\theta)
. Therefore, we prefer to formulate all our statements in terms of
\omega(\theta)
.

2.2
Dirichlet’s theorem

As a rule, talks on the elements of Diophantine approximation start with mentioning the respective Dirichlet theorem. Let us keep to this tradition. Being rather elementary, Dirichlet’s theorem on the approximation of real numbers by rationals is as well a most fundamental statement contained, in this form or another, in most of the existing theorems on Diophantine approximation.

Theorem 1

(G. Lejeune Dirichlet, 1842)
.

Let
\theta
and
t
be real numbers,
t>1
. Then there are integers
p
and
q
such that
0<q<t
and

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{qt}\,.

The original statement of the theorem proved in Dirichlet’s paper
[
1
]
of the year 1842 is actually different. Ir much more resembles the statement of Corollary
1
below and it was given for a linear form in arbitrarily many variables (see also Theorems
8
,
9
). However, Theorem
1
immediately follows from Dirichlet’s original argument, and it so happened that this way of formulating Dirichlet’s theorem is considered to be classical.

There are two most known proofs of this theorem – an arithmetic one, involving the pigeon-hole principle, and a geometric one, involving Minkowski’s convex body theorem. The latter approach is discussed in more detail in Section
2.4
.

For irrational
\theta
Theorem
1
provides an estimate, to some extent trivial, for the Diophantine exponent of an irrational number.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\theta
be an irrational real number. Then there are infinitely many pairs of coprime integers
p
,
q
such that

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|<\frac{1}{q^{2}}\,.

Corollary 2
.

If
\theta
is irrational, then
\omega(\theta)\geqslant 1
,
\mu(\theta)\geqslant 2
.

The irrationality assumption cannot be omitted, as is shown by the following

Proposition 1
.

If
\theta
is rational, then
\omega(\theta)=\infty
,
\mu(\theta)=1
.

Proof.

Let
\theta=a/b
,
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
,
b>0
.

Then for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
and every
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}

\bigg|\theta-\frac{ka}{kb}\bigg|=0<\frac{1}{q^{\gamma}}\,,

whence it immediately follows that
\omega(\theta)=\infty
.

Furthermore, for every rational
p/q
distinct from
\theta
we have

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|=\frac{|aq-bp|}{bq}\geqslant\frac{1/b}{q}\,,

whence it is clear that for every positive
\gamma
inequality (
1
) admits only a finite number of solutions. Thus,
\mu(\theta)\leqslant 1
. Finally, we come to
\mu(\theta)=1
by noticing that the linear Diophantine equation

ax-by=1

with coprime
a
and
b
admits infinitely many solutions.
∎

2.3
Diophantine and Liouville numbers

In 1844, two years after Dirichlet’s paper was published, Liouville
[
2
]
constructed the first examples of transcendental numbers. The main idea of his proof led to the following statement, which was published in 1851 in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 2

(J. Liouville, 1844–1851)
.

Let
\theta
be an algebraic number of degree
n
. Then there is a positive constant
c
depending only on
\theta
such that for every integer
p
and every positive integer
q
we have

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|>\frac{c}{q^{n}}\,.

Theorem
2
immediately implies an estimate for the irrationality measure (as well as for the Diophantine exponent) of an algebraic number, which generalises the estimate provided by Proposition
1
.

Corollary 3
.

If
\theta
is an algebraic number of degree
n
, then
\mu(\theta)\leqslant n
. Respectively, for
n\geqslant 2
, we have
\omega(\theta)\leqslant n-1
.

Hence we conclude that if
\mu(\theta)=\infty
(which, for irrational
\theta
, is equivalent to
\omega(\theta)=\infty
), then
\theta
cannot be algebraic, therefore, it is transcendental.

Definition 3
.

Let
\theta
be an irrational number. If
\omega(\theta)=\infty
, then
\theta
is called a
Liouville number
. If
\omega(\theta)<\infty
, then
\theta
is called a
Diophantine number
.

Thus, algebraic numbers are Diophantine. But what are the possible values of their Diophantine exponents? Corollaries
2
and
3
give us an analogue of Proposition
1
for quadratic irrationalities.

Proposition 2
.

If
\theta
is a quadratic irrationality, i.e. an algebraic number of degree
2
, then
\omega(\theta)=1
,
\mu(\theta)=2
.

For algebraic numbers of higher degrees Liouville’s theorem had been improved upon subsequently by Thue
[
4
]
, Siegel
[
5
]
, Dyson
[
6
]
, Gelfond
[
7
]
, until Roth
[
8
]
obtained in 1955 a result, for which he was awarded the Fields Medal in 1958.

Theorem 3

(K. Roth, 1955)
.

If
\theta
is an irrational algebraic number, then for every positive
\varepsilon
the inequality

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|<\frac{1}{q^{2+\varepsilon}}

has no more than a finite number of solutions in integer
p
,
q
.

Corollary 4
.

If
\theta
is an irrational algebraic number, then
\omega(\theta)=1
,
\mu(\theta)=2
.

Thus, irrational algebraic numbers have the smallest possible value of the Diophantine exponent. Which means that algebraic numbers are badly approximable by rationals. However, the classical notion of
badly approximable
numbers is slightly more subtle. It assumes that the strongest possible way to improve on Corollary
1
is by decreasing the constant.

Definition 4
.

An irrational number
\theta
is called
badly approximable (by rationals)
, if there is a constant
c>0
such that for every rational
p/q
we have

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\geqslant\frac{c}{q^{2}}\,.

It follows from Liouville’s theorem that algebraic numbers of degree
2
are badly approximable. It is an open question whether algebraic numbers of higher degrees are badly approximable as well. By now, even Lang’s conjecture (1945) is still open. It claims that for every irrational algebraic number
\theta
there exists
\delta>1
such that the inequality

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|<\frac{1}{q^{2}(\log q)^{\delta}}

has no more than a finite number of solutions in integer
p
,
q
.

2.5
Relation to continued fractions

Let us remind the algorithm of continued fraction expansion of a real number
\theta
. Denoting by
[\,\cdot\,]
the integer part, let us define the sequences
(\alpha_{k})
,
(a_{k})
,
(p_{k})
,
(q_{k})
by

\begin{aligned} \alpha_{0}=\theta,\quad\alpha_{k+1}=(\alpha_{k}-[\alpha_{k}])^{-1},\quad a_{k}=[\alpha_{k}],\\
\begin{aligned} p_{-2}&=0,\quad\,\ p_{-1}=1,\\
q_{-2}&=1,\quad\,\ q_{-1}=0,\end{aligned}\quad\,\ \begin{aligned} p_{k}&=a_{k}p_{k-1}+p_{k-2},\\
q_{k}&=a_{k}q_{k-1}+q_{k-2},\end{aligned}\end{aligned}\qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots

(3)

These sequences are infinite, provided
\theta
is irrational. If
\theta
is rational, they stop as soon as
\alpha_{k}
becomes integer.

Then (see
[
10
]

\theta=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{k-1},\alpha_{k}]=a_{0}+\cfrac{1}{a_{1}+\cfrac{1}{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\ddots}}{{\phantom{|}}}+\,\cfrac{1}{a_{k-1}+\cfrac{1}{\alpha_{k}}}}}\,,\qquad\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}].

The numbers

are called

of

and
p_{k}/q_{k}
are called

of
\theta

2.5.1
Diophantine exponent and growth of partial quotients

The convergents of
\theta
satisfy (see
[
9
]
,
[

]
,
[

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{q^{2}}\,.

On the other hand, if a rational

[
12
]
,
[

]
,
[
9
]
)

\theta

]

]
,
[

]
) any rational number

\bigg|\theta-\frac{p}{q}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{2q^{2}}

coincides with a convergent of
\theta

Thus, the order of approximation of
\theta
by rationals is determined by how fast the differences between
\theta
and its convergents decrease. For these differences there exist classical estimates (see
[
9
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
11
]
).

Proposition 3
.

Let
\theta=[a_{0};a_{1},a_{2},\ldots]
be the continued fraction expansion of
\theta
and let
p_{k}/q_{k}=[a_{0};a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}]
be its convergents. Then

\text{a) }\ \frac{1}{q_{k}(q_{k}+q_{k+1})}<\bigg|\theta-\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{q_{k}q_{k+1}}\,,

\text{\,b) }\ \ \frac{1}{q_{k}^{2}(a_{k+1}+2)}<\bigg|\theta-\frac{p_{k}}{q_{k}}\bigg|\leqslant\frac{1}{q_{k}^{2}a_{k+1}}\,.

Corollary 6
.

An irrational number is badly approximable if and only if its partial quotients are bounded.

Corollary 7
.

Given an irrational number
\theta
, within the notation of Proposition
3
, we have

\omega(\theta)=\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{q_{k+1}}{q_{k}}=1+\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{a_{k+1}}{q_{k}}\,.

(4)

Thus, the order of approximation of
\theta
by rationals is determined by the growth of the partial quotients of
\theta
.

The following theorem establishes the relation between continued fractions and a construction which goes back to Klein

Taking into account that there are no points of

3
Simultaneous approximation and approximation of zero by the values of a linear form

3.1
Two more Dirichlet’s theorems

In the previous Section our main question was how small the quantity

\theta x-y

can be for integer
x
,
y
. The multidimensional case, i.e. the case of
n
numbers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
instead of one number
\theta
, admits two ways to generalise this problem. We can study how small the quantities

\theta_{1}x-y_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}x-y_{n}

can be, or we can study how small the values of the linear form

\theta_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+\theta_{n}x_{n}-y

can be.

The first multidimensional result in Diophantine approximation belongs to Dirichlet and refers to both of these questions. In the aforementioned paper
[
1
]
of the year 1842 he proved the following statement.

Theorem 8

(G. Lejeune Dirichlet, 1842)
.

Let
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, for each
t\geqslant 1
, there are integers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y
satisfying the inequalities

\begin{cases}0<\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|\leqslant t\\
|\theta_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+\theta_{n}x_{n}-y|\leqslant t^{-n}\end{cases}.

(9)

It is worth making a remark similar to the one we made right after Theorem
1
: the original Dirichlet’s statement is weaker than the statement of Theorem
8
, but his argument proves Theorem
8
as well. In that same paper, Dirichlet generalises his result to the case of several linear forms. For the forms
\theta_{1}x-y_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}x-y_{n}
, this generalisation reads as follows.

Theorem 9

(G. Lejeune Dirichlet, 1842)
.

Let
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, for each
t\geqslant 1
, there are integers
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
satisfying the inequalities

\begin{cases}0<|x|\leqslant t\\
\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|\leqslant t^{-1/n}\end{cases}.

(10)

Thus, Theorem
8
provides an estimate for the order of approximation of zero by the values of the linear form

L_{\Theta}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)=\theta_{1}x_{1}+\ldots+\theta_{n}x_{n}-y

at integer
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y
, and Theorem
9
provides an estimate for the order of simultaneous approximation of the numbers
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
by rationals with the same denominator. This is how we get the first estimates for the Diophantine exponents of the
n
-tuple
\Theta
and of the linear form
L_{\Theta}
, whose definitions are given in Section
3.2
.

It is worth noting that, same as Theorem
1
, Theorems
8
and
9
follow immediately from Minkowski’s convex body theorem in the form of Corollary
5
applied to the systems (
9
) and (
10
).

3.2
Regular and uniform exponents

Definition 8
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that there exist arbitrarily large
t
such that the system

\begin{cases}0<|x|\leqslant t\\
\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma}\end{cases}

(11)

admits a solution in integer
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
is called the
regular Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\omega(\Theta)
.

Definition 9
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that there exist arbitrarily large
t
such that the system

\begin{cases}0<\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|\leqslant t\\
|L_{\Theta}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)|\leqslant t^{-\gamma}\end{cases}

(12)

admits a solution in integer
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y
is called the
regular Diophantine exponent
of
L_{\Theta}
and is denoted by
\omega(L_{\Theta})
.

Substituting the words “there exist arbitrarily large
t
such that” with “for every
t
large enough”, we get
uniform
analogues of regular exponents.

Definition 10
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that for every
t
large enough the system (
11
)
admits a solution in integer
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
is called the
uniform Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
.

Definition 11
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that for every
t
large enough the system (
12
)
admits a solution in integer
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y
is called the
uniform Diophantine exponent
of
L_{\Theta}
and is denoted by
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})
.

As we said before, Theorems
8
and
9
provide “trivial” bounds for these four exponents:

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant 1/n,\qquad\omega(L_{\Theta})\geqslant\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})\geqslant n.

(13)

These inequalities are sharp in the sense that there exist
n

\Theta
for which each of them turns into an equality.
For instance, Perron
[

proved the following statement.

Theorem 10

(O. Perron, 1921)
.

Let
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
be elements of a real algebraic number field of degree

, linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then there is a constant

depending only on

such that the inequality

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|<cx^{-1/n}

admits only finitely many solutions in integer
x,y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
.

Corollary 9
.

If
\Theta

\omega(\Theta)=\hat{\omega}(\Theta)=1/n
.

Particularly, for
\Theta=(\theta,\theta^{2},\ldots,\theta^{n})
, where

, we have
\omega(\Theta)=1/n
.

In his proof of Theorem

Perron uses an argument which also proves the implication

\omega(L_{\Theta})=n\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \omega(\Theta)=1/n\,.

(14)

The inverse implication

\omega(\Theta)=1/n\ \ \Longrightarrow\ \ \omega(L_{\Theta})=n

(15)

follows from the proof of the main result of Khintchine’s paper
[
26
]
(see also the collection of selected papers by Khintchine
[
27
]
). This gives us an analogue of Corollary
9

Corollary 10
.

If
\Theta

, then
\omega(L_{\Theta})=\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})=n
.

In contrast to the case
n=1
, uniform exponents
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
,
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})
are no longer trivial for

. They can attain finite values strictly greater than
1/n
and

respectively. The existence of linear forms

with
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})=+\infty
that do not vanish at nonzero integer points, as well as the existence of

with
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)=1
, was proved by Khintchine in 1926 in one of his most famous papers
[
28
]
. Such
n
-tuples and linear forms belong to a somewhat wider class of

(see Khintchine’s papers
[
29
]
,
[
30
]
, and also the remarkable survey by Moshchevitin
[
31
]
).

Finite values strictly greater than
1
cannot be attained by
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
. The reason is the very same effect that implies triviality of the uniform exponent in the case
n=1
. Indeed, let
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathbb{Q}^{n}
and let
\varepsilon>0
be given. Then, for irrational
\theta_{i}
, Proposition
4
guarantees the existence of arbitrarily large

such that the system

\begin{cases}0<x<t\\

has no solution in integer
x
,

. If
t
is large enough, the system

\begin{cases}0<x\leqslant t\\
\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|\leqslant t^{-1-\varepsilon}\end{cases}

neither has any solution in integer
x
,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
.

Thus, for every
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathbb{Q}^{n}
we have

\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\leqslant 1.

(16)

3.3
Transference principle

Perron and Khintchine proved actually something stronger than (
14
) and (
15
). Their constructions have “local” nature, therefore, they prove both the equivalence

\omega(\Theta)=1/n\ \ \Longleftrightarrow\ \ \omega(L_{\Theta})=n

(17)

and the fact that
\Theta
and
L_{\Theta}
are simultaneously
badly approximable
.

Definition 12
.

An
n
-tuple
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
is called
badly approximable
if there is a constant
c>0
depending only on
\Theta
such that for every tuple of integers
x
,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
with
x\neq 0
we have

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|\geqslant c|x|^{-1/n}.

Definition 13
.

A linear form
L_{\Theta}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)
is called
badly approximable
if there is a constant
c>0
depending only on
\Theta
such that for every tuple of integers
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,
y
with
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
not all zero we have

|L_{\Theta}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)|\geqslant c\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|x_{i}|^{-n}.

Apparently, Perron’s and Khintchine’s constructions were the first examples of exploiting the “duality” of the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero by the values of a linear form. We discuss the essence of this phenomenon in detail in Section
3.4
.

3.3.1
Inequalities for regular exponents

The first truly outstanding result relating the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero by the values of a linear form is Khintchine’s theorem, which was also proved in the aforementioned paper
[
28
]
. In that same paper, he named the phenomenon he had discovered the
transference principle
.

Theorem 11

(A. Ya. Khintchine, 1926)
.

\frac{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})}{1+\omega(\Theta)}\geqslant n,\qquad\frac{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{1+\omega(\Theta)^{-1}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\,.

(18)

As Jarník showed in his papers
[
32
]
,
[
33
]
, inequalities (
18
) are sharp in the sense that for every
\gamma\in[n,+\infty]
there are two
n
-tuples
\Theta^{\prime}
and
\Theta^{\prime\prime}
such that

\omega(L_{\Theta^{\prime}})=\omega(L_{\Theta^{\prime\prime}})=\gamma,\qquad\frac{1+\gamma}{1+\omega(\Theta^{\prime})}=n,\qquad\frac{1+\gamma^{-1}}{1+\omega(\Theta^{\prime\prime})^{-1}}=\frac{1}{n}\,.

3.3.2
Inequalities for uniform exponents

The method used to prove inequalities (
18
) enables proving the same inequalities for the uniform exponents
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
and
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})
:

\frac{1+\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})}{1+\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant n,\qquad\frac{1+\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{1+\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{-1}}\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\,.

(19)

However, these exponents satisfy stronger inequalities. For
n=2
Jarník presented in his paper
[
34
]
a surprising fact: in this case the uniform exponents are connected by an equality.

Theorem 12

(V. Jarník, 1938)
.

If
n=2
and the components of
\Theta
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}+\hat{\omega}(\Theta)=1.

(20)

Later on, in 1948, Khintchine published in
[
35
]
a rather simple proof of Theorem
12
.

For
n\geqslant 3
, in that same paper
[
34
]
, Jarník proved that, if
\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n}
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then, along with (
19
), the following inequalities hold:

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{1}{n-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})-2n+4}\right),\ \ \text{ if }\ \hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})>n(2n-3),

(21)

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\leqslant 1-\frac{1^{\vphantom{|}}}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})-n+2}\,,\qquad\qquad\quad\,\ \text{ if }\ \hat{\omega}(\Theta)>(n-1)/n.

In 2012, inequalities (
19
), (
21
) were improved by the author in
[
36
]
,
[
37
]
.

Theorem 13

(O. G., 2012)
.

For every
\Theta=(\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\backslash\mathbb{Q}^{n}
we have

\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})\geqslant\frac{n-1}{1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\,,\qquad\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{1-\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{n-1}\,.

(22)

It was shown by Marnat
[
38
]
and (independently) by Schmidt and Summerer
[
39
]
that both inequalities (
22
) are sharp. More specifically, they showed that for every
\gamma\in[n,+\infty]
and every

\delta\in\bigg[\frac{1-\gamma^{-1}}{n-1}\,,\,1-\frac{n-1}{\gamma}\bigg]

(23)

there are continuously many tuples
\Theta
with components linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
such that
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})=\gamma
,
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)=\delta
(we note that, if
\gamma\geqslant n
, then the segment in (
23
) is correctly defined and non-empty).

3.3.3
“Mixed” inequalities

Despite the fact that inequalities (
18
) for the regular exponents are sharp, they can be improved if the uniform exponents are taken into account. For the first time, it was made by Laurent and Bugeaud in papers
[
40
]
,
[
41
]
. They proved the following.

Theorem 14

(M. Laurent, Y. Bugeaud, 2009)
.

If the components of
\Theta
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\frac{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})}{1+\omega(\Theta)}\geqslant\frac{n-1}{1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\,,\qquad\frac{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{1+\omega(\Theta)^{-1}}\geqslant\frac{1-\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{n-1}\,.

(24)

Laurent
[
42
]
proved that for
n=2
these inequalities are sharp.
However, for
n\geqslant 3
, it was shown by Schleischitz
[
43
]
that inequalities (
24
) are no longer sharp. This is not surprising in the light of the following inequalities obtained in 2013 by Schmidt and Summerer
[
44
]
(see also papers
[
45
]
,
[
46
]
, where shorter proofs of their theorem are proposed).

Theorem 15

(W. M. Schmidt, L. Summerer, 2013)
.

If the components of
\Theta
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})\leqslant\frac{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})}{1+\omega(\Theta)}\,,\qquad\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\leqslant\frac{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{1+\omega(\Theta)^{-1}}\,.

(25)

It is easy to see that inequalities (
25
) and (
22
) immediately imply inequalities (
24
).

Thus, since Khintchine’s inequalities follow from those of Laurent and Bugeaud, all the currently known transference inequalities that connect
\omega(\Theta)
,
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
with
\omega(L_{\Theta})
,
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})
are implied by inequalities (
22
) and (
25
) (and, of course, by “trivial” inequalities (
13
) and (
16
)).

3.3.4
Inequalities between regular and uniform exponents

There is another series of inequalities which, technically, cannot be classified as transference inequalities, as they connect regular and uniform exponents within the frames of one of the two problems under consideration – the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero by the values of a linear form. Nevertheless, it seems quite reasonable to place them alongside with transference inequalities, for transference inequalities themselves provide rather nontrivial relations of this kind (see Theorem
20
below).

In 1950-s Jarník discovered that if
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
is large, then
\omega(\Theta)
cannot be too small. In papers
[
47
,
48
]
, he published the following estimates for
n=2
.

Theorem 16

(V. Jarník, 1954)
.

If
n=2
and the components of
\Theta
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})}\geqslant\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})-1,\qquad\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant\frac{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}{1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\,.

(26)

These inequalities are sharp. Their sharpness was proved by Laurent
[
42
]
. Jarník
[
48
]
also obtained inequalities in the higher dimensions. He showed that the second inequality in (
26
) holds for every
n\geqslant 2
and that

\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})}\geqslant\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{1/(n-1)}-3,

provided
\omega(\Theta)>(5n^{2})^{n-1}
.

In 2012, Moshchevitin
[
49
]
obtained an optimal result for
n=3
in the problem of simultaneous approximation.

Theorem 17

(N. G. Moshchevitin, 2012)
.

If
n=3
and the components of
\Theta
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta),

(27)

where
G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta)
is the greatest root of the polynomial

(1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta))x^{2}-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)x-\hat{\omega}(\Theta).

(28)

A year later, Schmidt and Summerer
[
50
]
proved an analogue of Theorem
17
for the linear form problem.

Theorem 18

(W. M. Schmidt, L. Summerer, 2013)
.

If
n=3
and the components of
\Theta
are linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
, then

\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})}\geqslant G_{\textup{lin}}(L_{\Theta}),

(29)

where
G_{\textup{lin}}(\Theta)
is the greatest root of the polynomial

x^{2}+x+(1-\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})).

(30)

In 2018, Marnat and Moshchevitin generalised (
26
), (
27
), and (
29
) to the case of arbitrary
n\geqslant 2
. Their result was published in 2020 in
[
51
]
.

Theorem 19

(A. Marnat, N. G. Moshchevitin, 2020)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
and let the components of
\Theta
be linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
. Then

\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta),\qquad\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})}\geqslant G_{\textup{lin}}(\Theta),

(31)

where
G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta)
and
G_{\textup{lin}}(\Theta)
are the greatest roots of the polynomials

(1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta))x^{n}-x^{n-1}+\hat{\omega}(\Theta),\qquad\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}x^{n}-x+(1-\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1})

(32)

respectively.

In that same paper
[
51
]
, Marnat and Moshchevitin showed that their inequalities (
31
) are sharp. A year later, a somewhat different proof of Theorem
19
was proposed by Rivard-Cooke in his PhD thesis
[
52
]
(see also papers
[
53
]
,
[
54
]
).

Note that using the notation
G_{\textup{sim}}
and
G_{\textup{lin}}
both in Theorems
17
,
18
and in Theorem
19
is correct, since for
n=3
the first (resp. second) polynomial in (
32
) equals the first (resp. second) polynomial in (
28
) multiplied by
x-1
(resp. by
\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}(x-1)
).

It is easy to verify with the help of (
20
) that for
n=2
the right-hand sides of inequalities (
26
) coincide and equal

\frac{1-\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\,.

It is interesting that Schmidt–Summerer’s inequalities (
25
) imply that this very expression bounds from below the ratios
\omega(\Theta)/\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
and
\omega(L_{\Theta})/\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})
for every
n\geqslant 2
.
This can be observed from the following result obtained in
[
46
]
.

Theorem 20

(O. G., N. G. Moshchevitin, 2022)
.

Let
n\geqslant 2
and let the components of
\Theta
be linearly independent with the unity over
\mathbb{Q}
. Define
G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta)
and
G_{\textup{lin}}(\Theta)
the same way as in Theorem
19
. Then

\begin{split}\min\bigg(\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\,,\,\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})}\bigg)&\geqslant\frac{1+\omega(\Theta)}{1+\omega(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}\geqslant\\
&\geqslant\frac{1-\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})^{-1}}{1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant\min\big(G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta),G_{\textup{lin}}(\Theta)\big).\end{split}

(33)

Another interesting corollary to Theorem
20
is the fact that Schmidt–Summerer’s inequalities (
25
) imply at least one of Marnat–Moshchevitin’s inequalities (
31
) – the one corresponding to the smallest number among
G_{\textup{sim}}(\Theta)
and
G_{\textup{lin}}(\Theta)
.

3.4
Ideas and methods

3.4.1
Mahler’s method

Ten years after Khintchine published Theorem
11
describing transference principle, Mahler
[
55
]
found a very simple proof for it, which vividly demonstrated that the problem of simultaneous approximation is “dual” to the problem of approximating zero by the values of a linear form.

Mahler himself called his method arithmetic (see
[
55
]
), and though he exploits Minkowski’s convex body theorem, he applies it in the form of Corollary
5
, which allows not to go deep into geometry.

Let us explain
his argument from paper
[
55
]
, where he proves the right-hand inequality (
18
), i.e. the inequality

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{(n-1)\omega(L_{\Theta})+n}\,.

(34)

To this end let us “embed” the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero by the values of a linear form into the same
(n+1)
-dimensional Euclidean space. Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1}
be the Cartesian coordinates in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Let us identify the aforementioned variables
x
,
y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}
respectively with
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1}
, and variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,
y
– respectively with
u_{2},\ldots,u_{n+1}
,
-u_{1}
.
By analogy with Section
2.4
we denote by
\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}(\Theta)
the one-dimensional subspace with the generating vector
(1,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
, and by
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
– the orthogonal complement to
\mathcal{L}
.
Then
\mathcal{L}
and
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
coincide with the spaces of solutions to

\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|=0\qquad\text{ and }\qquad L_{\Theta}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y)=0.

Assuming that there exist
t
large enough, positive
\gamma
, and a nonzero point
\mathbf{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n+1})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
such that

\begin{cases}\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|v_{i+1}|\leqslant t\\
|v_{1}+\theta_{1}v_{2}+\ldots+\theta_{n}v_{n+1}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma}\end{cases},

(35)

Mahler applies Minkowski’s theorem – more specifically, Corollary
5
– to the parallelepiped consisting of the points
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n+1})
satisfying the inequalities

\begin{cases}|v_{1}u_{1}+\ldots+v_{n+1}u_{n+1}|<1\\
\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}u_{1}-u_{i+1}|\leqslant|v_{1}+\theta_{1}v_{2}+\ldots+\theta_{n}v_{n+1}|^{1/n}.\end{cases}

(36)

Determinant of the set of linear forms involved in (
36
) equals

\det\left(\begin{matrix}v_{1}&\theta_{1}&\theta_{2}&\cdots&\theta_{n}\\
v_{2}&-1&0&\cdots&0\\
v_{3}&0&-1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
v_{n+1}&0&0&\cdots&-1\end{matrix}\right)=(-1)^{n}(v_{1}+\theta_{1}v_{2}+\ldots+\theta_{n}v_{n+1}),

which is equal by the absolute value to the product of the right-hand sides of (
36
). Therefore, by Corollary
5
system (
36
) admits a nonzero integer solution
\mathbf{w}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n+1})
.

Since
\mathbf{v}
is integer, it follows from the first inequality (
36
) that

v_{1}w_{1}+\ldots+v_{n+1}w_{n+1}=0,

(37)

whence

w_{1}(v_{1}+\theta_{1}v_{2}+\ldots+\theta_{n}v_{n+1})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}v_{i+1}(\theta_{i}w_{1}-w_{i+1}).

Hence by the second inequality (
36
)

|w_{1}|\leqslant n\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|v_{i+1}||v_{1}+\theta_{1}v_{2}+\ldots+\theta_{n}v_{n+1}|^{\frac{1}{n}-1}.

(38)

Combining (
35
), (
36
), (
38
), we get

\begin{cases}|w_{1}|\leqslant nt^{1-\gamma\big(\frac{1}{n}-1\big)}=nt^{\frac{(n-1)\gamma+n}{n}}=t^{\frac{(n-1)\gamma+n}{n}+\frac{\ln n}{\ln t}}\\
\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|\theta_{i}w_{1}-w_{i+1}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma/n}=\Big(t^{\frac{(n-1)\gamma+n}{n}+\frac{\ln n}{\ln t}}\Big)^{-\frac{\gamma}{(n-1)\gamma+n}+o(1)}\end{cases}.

(39)

This proves (
34
).

The key ingredient in Mahler’s method is relation (
37
), which says that the required point happens to be in the orthogonal component to the line spanned by
\mathbf{v}
. More specifically, in the intersection of this orthogonal complement with the cylinder determined by the second inequality of (
36
), whose axis is the line spanned by
(1,\theta_{1},\ldots,\theta_{n})
.

Mahler generalised his method by proving in
[
56
]
his famous
theorem on a bilinear form
.

Theorem 21

(K. Mahler, 1937)
.

Assume two sets of homogeneous linear forms in
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are given:

forms
f_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,f_{d}(\mathbf{u})
with matrix
F
,
\det F\neq 0
, and

forms
g_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,g_{d}(\mathbf{u})
with matrix
G
,
\det G=1
.

Suppose the bilinear form

\Phi(\mathbf{u}^{\prime},\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{i=1}^{d}f_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime})g_{i}(\mathbf{u}^{\prime\prime})

(40)

has integer coefficients. Suppose the system

|f_{i}(\mathbf{u})|\leqslant\lambda_{i},\ \ i=1,\ldots,d

(41)

admits a solution in
\mathbb{Z}^{d}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}
.
Then so does the system

|g_{i}(\mathbf{u})|\leqslant(d-1)\lambda/\lambda_{i},\ \ i=1,\ldots,d,

(42)

where

\lambda=\Big(\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}\Big)^{\frac{1}{d-1}}.

(43)

As we shall see in Section
4
, this theorem provides a rather simple proof of transference inequalities in the most general problem of homogeneous linear Diophantine approximation – when zero is to be approximated simultaneously by the values of several linear forms at integer points. We shall actually reformulate it in terms of pseudocompounds (Theorem
22
below). Thus formulated, Mahler’s theorem becomes rather concise and very convenient for applications.

3.4.2
Pseudocompounds and dual lattices

In 1955, in his papers
[
57
]
,
[
58
]
, Mahler developed the theory of
compound bodies
(see also Gruber and Lekkerkerker’s book
[
59
]
). This theory appeared to be rather fruitful in the context of problems related to the transference principle. We shall reformulate Theorem
21
with the help of a construction from Schmidt’s book
[
11
]
, which is a simplification of what Mahler calls the
(d-1)
-th compound
of a parallelepiped.

Definition 14
.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}
be positive real numbers. Consider the parallelepiped

\mathcal{P}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,|z_{i}|\leqslant\eta_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}.

(44)

Then the parallelepiped

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,|z_{i}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}

is called the
(d-1)
-th pseudocompound
of
\mathcal{P}
.

We shall often call
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
simply the
compound
of
\mathcal{P}
, omitting “
(d-1)
-th”.

We also recall the definition of the dual lattice.

Definition 15
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then its
dual
lattice is defined as

\Lambda^{\ast}=\big\{\,\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\big|\ \langle\mathbf{z},\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }\mathbf{z}^{\prime}\in\Lambda\,\big\},

where
\langle\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,\rangle
denotes the inner product.

Note that the relation of duality is symmetric in the case of lattices, i.e.

(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\ast}=\Lambda.

Let
F
and
G
be the matrices from Theorem
21
. Consider the lattices
F\mathbb{Z}^{d}
and
G\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. In view of Definition
15
, the fact that the coefficients of the form (
40
) are integer means exactly that each of these two lattices is a sublattice of the other’s dual one. Set
\Lambda=G\mathbb{Z}^{d}
. Then
F\mathbb{Z}^{d}\subseteq\Lambda^{\ast}
.

Given positive
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}
, let
\lambda
be defined by (
43
). Set
\eta_{i}=\lambda/\lambda_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,d
. Consider the parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}
determined by (
44
). Then

\frac{1}{\eta_{i}}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\eta_{j}=\frac{\lambda_{i}\lambda^{d-1}}{\prod_{j=1}^{d}\lambda_{j}}=\lambda_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,

(45)

i.e.

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,|z_{i}|\leqslant\lambda_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}.

Thus, Theorem
21
states actually that there is a nonzero point of the unimodular lattice
\Lambda
in
(d-1)\mathcal{P}
, provided that
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
contains a nonzero point of some sublattice of
\Lambda^{\ast}
. It is clear that in this statement the words “of some sublattice” can be omitted. We get the following reformulation of Theorem
21
.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with determinant
1
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a parallelepiped in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at the origin with faces parallel to the coordinate planes. Then

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies(d-1)\mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.

Note that, since
(\Lambda^{\ast})^{\ast}=\Lambda
, we can interchange
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\ast}
in Theorem
22
.

In this form, Mahler’s theorem admits a rather vivid purely geometric proof. It can be described as follows.

Suppose
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
contains a nonzero point
\mathbf{v}
of
\Lambda^{\ast}
. We can assume that
\mathbf{v}
is primitive. Consider
(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v})^{\perp}
– the orthogonal complement to the one-dimensional subspace spanned by
\mathbf{v}
and the section
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{P}\cap(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v})^{\perp}
(see Fig.
7
).

\mathbf{v}
\mathbf{0}
\mathcal{P}^{\ast}
\mathcal{P}
\mathcal{S}

Figure 7:
Section
\mathcal{S}
of
\mathcal{P}
orthogonal to
\mathbf{v}

The set
\Gamma=\Lambda\cap(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v})^{\perp}
is a lattice of rank
d-1
with determinant equal to
|\mathbf{v}|_{2}
, where
|\cdot|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean norm. Hence, if a constant
c
is chosen so that the
(d-1)
-dimensional volume of
c\mathcal{S}
is smaller than
2^{d-1}|\mathbf{v}|_{2}
, then by Minkowski’s convex body theorem there is a nonzero point of
\Gamma
in
c\mathcal{S}
and, therefore, there is a nonzero point of
\Lambda
in
c\mathcal{P}
.

Finding an appropriate constant
c
can be arranged with the help of Vaaler’s theorem
[
60
]
on central sections of a cube. Consider the cube
\mathcal{B}=[-1,1]^{d}
. By Vaaler’s theorem the volume of any central
(d-1)
-dimensional section of
\mathcal{B}
is not less than
2^{d-1}
.
Consider the diagonal operators
A=\textup{diag}\big(\eta_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\eta_{d}^{-1}\big)
and
C=\textup{diag}\big(\lambda_{1}^{-1},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{-1}\big)
. Then

A\mathcal{P}=C\mathcal{P}^{\ast}=\mathcal{B}.

By (
45
)
C
coincides with the cofactor matrix of
A
, i.e.
C=(\det A)(A^{\ast})^{-1}
. Therefore, since
\mathcal{S}\perp\mathbf{v}
, we have

\frac{\vol(\mathcal{S})}{|\mathbf{v}|_{2}}=(\det A)^{-1}\frac{\vol(A\mathcal{S})}{\big|(A^{\ast})^{-1}\mathbf{v}\big|_{2}}=\frac{\vol(A\mathcal{S})}{|C\mathbf{v}|_{2}}\geqslant\frac{2^{d-1}}{\sqrt{d}}\,.

Thus, by choosing
c=\big(\sqrt{d}\big)^{1/(d-1)}
we get
\vol(c\mathcal{S})\geqslant 2^{d-1}|\mathbf{v}|_{2}
. As it was said before, the rest is done by applying Minkowski’s convex body theorem to
c\mathcal{S}
and
\Gamma
.

The argument we have just discussed actually proves a stronger statement than Theorem
22
. It proves that

\mathcal{P}^{\ast}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c\mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}

(46)

with
c=\big(\sqrt{d}\big)^{1/(d-1)}
– a constant which is less than
d-1
and, moreover, which tends to
1
as
d\to\infty
. However, it is worth mentioning that a combination of Mahler’s theorem on successive minima proved in
[
61
]
with Minkowski’s theorem on successive minima (both theorems can be found in Schmidt’s book
[
11
]
) provides an improvement of Theorem
21
with the constant
c^{2}
. A detailed account can be found in
[
62
]
. In that same paper some further improvements of Theorem
21
are presented.

3.4.3
Two two-parametric families of parallelepipeds

Let us address once again Theorem
11
of Khintchine, this time – in the light of Theorem
22
.
Consider the lattice

\Lambda=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
-\theta_{1}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
-\theta_{2}&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\phantom{-}\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\theta_{n}&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}\right)\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}.

Then the dual lattice is

\Lambda^{\ast}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&\theta_{1}&\theta_{2}&\cdots&\theta_{n}\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}\right)\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}.

For every positive
t
,
\gamma
,
s
,
\delta
define the parallelepipeds

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{1}|\leqslant t\\
|z_{i}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma},\ \ i=2,\ldots,n+1\end{array}\Bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{1}|\leqslant s^{-\delta}\\
|z_{i}|\leqslant s,\quad\,\ i=2,\ldots,n+1\end{array}\Bigg\}.

Then

\displaystyle\omega(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\gamma\geqslant\frac{1}{n}\,\bigg|\ \forall\,t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,t>t_{0}:\text{\,we have }\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\},

(51)

\displaystyle\omega(L_{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\,\delta\geqslant n\ \bigg|\ \forall\,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,s>s_{0}:\text{\,we have }\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\}.

Each parallelepiped from (
3.4.3
) is the pseudocompound of some parallelepiped from (
3.4.3
), and vice versa. Indeed, if

t=s^{((n-1)\delta+n)/n},\qquad\gamma=\frac{\delta}{(n-1)\delta+n}\,,

(52)

then
\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)=\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)^{\ast}
. Conversely, if

s=t^{1/n},\qquad\delta=n\gamma+n-1,

(53)

then
\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)=\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)^{\ast}
.

Let us apply Mahler’s theorem in disguise of Theorem
22
.

Assuming (
52
) holds, we have

\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies(d-1)\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\},

whence it follows, in view of (
51
), that

\omega(L_{\Theta})\geqslant\delta\implies\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\gamma=\frac{\delta}{(n-1)\delta+n}\,.

Thus,

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{\omega(L_{\Theta})}{(n-1)\omega(L_{\Theta})+n}\,.

(54)

Assuming (
53
) holds, we have

\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies(d-1)\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\},

whence it follows, once again, in view of (
51
), that

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\gamma\implies\omega(L_{\Theta})\geqslant\delta=n\gamma+n-1.

Thus,

\omega(L_{\Theta})\geqslant n\omega(\Theta)+n-1.

(55)

It can be easily verified that (
54
) and (
55
) are nothing else but the right-hand and the left-hand inequalities (
18
) respectively. The proof of Khintchine’s theorem is completed.

3.4.4
Uniform exponents and an analogue of Mahler’s theorem

The proof of Theorem
22
given in Section
3.4.2
is based on Minkowski’s convex body theorem, Vaaler’s theorem on central sections of a cube, and the fact that for any primitive vector
\mathbf{v}
of a unimodular lattice
\Lambda
the set
\Lambda^{\ast}\cap(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v})^{\perp}
is a lattice of rank
d-1
with determinant equal to the Euclidean norm of
\mathbf{v}
. The latter fact can be reformulated as the equality of the determinants of the lattices
\Lambda\cap(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v})
and
\Lambda^{\ast}\cap(\mathbb{R}\mathbf{v})^{\perp}
of ranks
1
and
d-1
respectively.

A more general statement holds. It is very useful when working with uniform exponents.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\det\Lambda=1
. Let
\mathcal{L}
be a
k
-dimensional subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and let the lattice
\Gamma=\mathcal{L}\cap\Lambda
have rank
k
. Consider the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{L}^{\perp}
and set
\Gamma^{\perp}=\mathcal{L}^{\perp}\cap\Lambda^{\ast}
. Then
\Gamma^{\perp}
is a lattice of rank

and

This statement is rather well known, its proof can be found, for instance, in

[

The nature of uniform exponents requires working with sublattices of rank
2

. Respectively, when working with two-dimensional and
(d-2)
-dimensional subspaces, it is natural to involve the

-th pseudocompounds instead of the

-th ones.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{e}_{d}
be the standard basis of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For each multivector
\mathbf{Z}\in\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us consider its representation

\mathbf{Z}=\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant d}Z_{ij}\mathbf{e}_{i}\wedge\mathbf{e}_{j}.

Definition 16
.

Let
\eta_{1},\ldots,\eta_{d}
be positive real numbers. Consider the parallelepiped

\mathcal{P}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,|z_{i}|\leqslant\eta_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}.

Then the parallelepiped

\mathcal{P}^{\circledast}=\bigg\{\mathbf{Z}\in{\textstyle\bigwedge^{2}\mathbb{R}^{d}}\,\bigg|\,|Z_{ij}|\leqslant\frac{1}{\eta_{i}\eta_{j}}\prod_{k=1}^{d}\eta_{k},\ 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant d\bigg\}

(d-2)
-th pseudocompound
of
\mathcal{P}
.

Given a full rank lattice
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its dual lattice
\Lambda^{\ast}
, let us denote by
\Lambda^{\circledast}
the set of all the decomposable elements of the lattice
\bigwedge^{2}\Lambda^{\ast}
, i.e.

\Lambda^{\circledast}=\Big\{\mathbf{z}_{1}\wedge\mathbf{z}_{2}\,\Big|\,\mathbf{z}_{1},\mathbf{z}_{2}\in\Lambda^{\ast}\Big\}.

The following theorem proved in
[

is an analogue of Theorem

Theorem 23
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with determinant
1
. Let
\mathcal{P}
be a parallelepiped in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
centered at the origin with faces parallel to the coordinate planes. Then

\mathcal{P}^{\circledast}\cap\Lambda^{\circledast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies c\mathcal{P}\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\},

where
c=\big(\frac{d(d-1)}{2}\big)^{\raisebox{3.01389pt}{$\frac{1}{2(d-2)}$}}
.

The proof of Theorem

resembles pretty much the proof of Theorem
22
described in Section

. It is also based on three facts. The first one is that same Minkowski’s convex body theorem. The second one is Vaaler’s theorem on central sections of a cube – this time it is applied to
(d-2)

5
with
k=2

Theorem

3.4.8
Parametric geometry of numbers

Developing the ideas proposed by Schmidt in his paper
[
65
]
, Schmidt and Summerer published a fundamental paper
[
66
]
in 2009, where they proposed a new point of view at the problems under discussion. They called their approach “parametric geometry of numbers”.

Most generally parametric geometry of numbers can be outlined as follows. Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with determinant
1
.
Consider the cube
\mathcal{B}=[-1,1]^{d}
. Define the
space of parameters
by

\mathcal{T}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,\tau_{1}+\ldots+\tau_{d}=0\Big\}.

For each
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}
set

\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\textup{diag}(e^{\tau_{1}},\ldots,e^{\tau_{d}})\mathcal{B}.

Let
\mu_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\Lambda)
,
k=1,\ldots,d
, denote the
k
-th successive minimum of
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
w.r.t.
\Lambda
, i.e. the smallest positive
\mu
such that
\mu\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
contains not less than
k
linearly independent vectors of
\Lambda
. Finally, for each
k=1,\ldots,d
, let us define the functions

L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=L_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})=\log\big(\mu_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\Lambda)\big),\qquad S_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})=\sum_{1\leqslant j\leqslant k}L_{j}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}).

Many problems in Diophantine approximation can be formulated as questions concerning the asymptotic behaviour of
L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and
S_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. Each problem requires a proper choice of
\Lambda
and of a subset of
\mathcal{T}
, with respect to which the asymptotics of these functions is to be studied. In the case of the problems we discuss in this Section
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is supposed to tend to infinity along certain one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{T}
. Let
\mathfrak{T}
be the path in
\mathcal{T}
determined by a mapping
s\mapsto\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)
,
s\in[0,\infty)
. For our purpose, we may assume that this mapping is linear.

Definition 17
.

Suppose a lattice
\Lambda
and a path
\mathfrak{T}
are given. Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. The quantities

\underline{\varphi}_{k}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})=\liminf_{s\to+\infty}\frac{L_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}(s))}{s}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\varphi}_{k}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})=\limsup_{s\to+\infty}\frac{L_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}(s))}{s}

are respectively called the
k
-th lower
and
upper Schmidt–Summerer exponents of the first type
.

Definition 18
.

Suppose a lattice
\Lambda
and a path
\mathfrak{T}
are given. Let
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
. The quantities

\underline{\Phi}_{k}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})=\liminf_{s\to+\infty}\frac{S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}(s))}{s}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\Phi}_{k}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})=\limsup_{s\to+\infty}\frac{S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}(s))}{s}

are respectively called the
k
-th lower
and
upper Schmidt–Summerer exponents of the second type
.

For the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero with the values of a linear form, the lattices and the paths can be chosen as follows. Set
d=n+1
.

As in Section
3.4.3
, consider the lattices

\Lambda=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0&0&\cdots&0\\
-\theta_{1}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
-\theta_{2}&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\phantom{-}\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\theta_{n}&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}\right)\mathbb{Z}^{n+1},\qquad\Lambda^{\ast}=\left(\begin{matrix}1&\theta_{1}&\theta_{2}&\cdots&\theta_{n}\\
0&1&0&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&1\end{matrix}\right)\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}.

(67)

Define the paths
\mathfrak{T}
and
\mathfrak{T}^{\ast}
respectively by the mappings

\displaystyle s\mapsto\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)=\big(\tau_{1}(s),\ldots,\tau_{n+1}(s)\big),

(68)

\displaystyle\tau_{1}(s)=s,\quad\tau_{2}(s)=\ldots=\tau_{n+1}(s)=-s/n

and

\displaystyle s\mapsto\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\ast}(s)=\big(\tau^{\ast}_{1}(s),\ldots,\tau^{\ast}_{n+1}(s)\big),

\displaystyle\tau^{\ast}_{1}(s)=-ns,\quad\tau^{\ast}_{2}(s)=\ldots=\tau^{\ast}_{n+1}(s)=s.

Then
\boldsymbol{\tau}^{\ast}(s)=-n\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)
, i.e.
\mathfrak{T}\cup\mathfrak{T}^{\ast}
is a one-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{T}
. As we noted in Section
3.4.3
,
\Lambda^{\ast}
is the dual of
\Lambda
.

Regular and uniform Diophantine exponents are related to Schmidt–Summerer exponents by the equalities

\displaystyle\big(1+\omega(\Theta)\big)\big(1+\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})\big)=\big(1+\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\big)\big(1+\overline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})\big)=(n+1)/n,

(69)

\displaystyle\big(1+\omega(L_{\Theta})\big)\big(1+\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda^{\ast},\mathfrak{T}^{\ast})\big)=\big(1+\hat{\omega}(L_{\Theta})\big)\big(1+\overline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda^{\ast},\mathfrak{T}^{\ast})\big)=n+1.

They are deduced directly from the definitions (see
[
66
]
,
[
37
]
). We note that analogous relations for Schmidt–Summerer exponents with indices greater than
1
hold in the context of problems of approximating a given subspace by rational subspaces of fixed dimension. In those problems so called
intermediate
exponents arise. They are studied in papers
[
67
]
,
[
40
]
,
[
41
]
,
[
37
]
, the latter of which contains the equalities relating Schmidt–Summerer exponents and intermediate exponents.

By (
69
) each relation proved for Schmidt–Summerer exponents generates a relation for regular and uniform Diophantine exponents. As for relations for Schmidt–Summerer exponents, they are obtained by analysing the properties of the functions
L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and
S_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
. The first observation that can be made is that
L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and
S_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
are continuous and piece-wise linear on
\mathcal{T}
and their restrictions to the paths
\mathfrak{T}
and
\mathfrak{T}^{\ast}
have two possible slopes on linearity intervals. Next, a very important role in studying the behaviour of
L_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
and
S_{k}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
is played by two classical theorems on successive minima – theorem of Minkowski
[
14
]
and theorem of Mahler
[
61
]
. Let us state these theorems for parallelepipeds
\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}}
and lattices
\Lambda
,
\Lambda^{\ast}
(assuming that
d=n+1
).

Theorem 24

(H. Minkowski, 1896)
.

For each
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}
we have

\frac{1}{d!}\leqslant\prod_{i=1}^{d}\mu_{i}(\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\Lambda)\leqslant 1.

(70)

Theorem 25

(K. Mahler, 1938)
.

For each
k=1,\ldots,d
and each
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}
we have

\frac{1}{d}\leqslant\mu_{k}(\mathcal{B}_{\boldsymbol{\tau}},\Lambda)\mu_{d+1-k}(\mathcal{B}_{-\boldsymbol{\tau}},\Lambda^{\ast})\leqslant d!\,.

(71)

The following local properties holding for every
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}
are easily deduced from Theorems
24
,
25
(see
[
68
]
):

(i)

L_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})=-L_{d+1-k}(\Lambda^{\ast},-\boldsymbol{\tau})+O(1),\ \ k=1,\ldots,d
;
\frac{\big|}{|}

(ii)

S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})=S_{d-k}(\Lambda^{\ast},-\boldsymbol{\tau})+O(1),\ \ k=1,\ldots,d-1
;
\frac{\big|}{|}

(iii)

\displaystyle S_{1}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\frac{S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{k}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\frac{S_{d-1}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d-1}\leqslant\frac{S_{d}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d}=O(1)
;

(iv)

\displaystyle\frac{S_{1}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d-1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\frac{S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d-k}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant S_{d-1}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})
.

The constants implied by
O(\cdot)
depend only on
d
. These properties, as well as Theorems
24
and
25
, are valid for every unimodular lattice. Particularly, they remain valid if
\Lambda
is replaced with
\Lambda^{\ast}
.

Properties (ii) and (iii) immediately imply the inequality

S_{1}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})\leqslant\dfrac{S_{1}(\Lambda^{\ast},-\boldsymbol{\tau})}{d-1}+O(1),

(72)

whence we get (by applying (
72
) to
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\ast}
) the following inequalities for Schmidt–Summerer exponents corresponding to the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero with the values of a linear form:

\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda^{\ast},\mathfrak{T}^{\ast})\leqslant\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda,\mathfrak{T})\leqslant\dfrac{\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda^{\ast},\mathfrak{T}^{\ast})}{n^{2}}\,.

(73)

Rewriting (
73
) in terms of
\omega(\Theta)
,
\omega(L_{\Theta})
, with the help of (
69
), we get Khintchine’s inequalities (
18
). It is shown in
[
37
]
that this way of proving Khintchine’s inequalities enables to improve them by splitting each of them into a chain of inequalities between the aforementioned intermediate exponents. The first to obtain such a chain of inequalities were Laurent and Bugeaud (see
[
40
]
,
[
41
]
).

Restrictions of
L_{1}(\boldsymbol{\tau}),\ldots,L_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
to the path
\mathfrak{T}
enjoy a series of elementary properties: they are continuous, piece-wise linear, they have two possible slopes on linearity intervals, at each point their values are ordered as
L_{1}(\boldsymbol{\tau})\leqslant\ldots\leqslant L_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
, their sum
S_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})=L_{1}(\boldsymbol{\tau})+\ldots+L_{d}(\boldsymbol{\tau})
is nonpositive and bounded. In all other respects the behaviour of
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
is rather chaotic. Nevertheless, there is a class of
d
-tuples of functions having quite regular behaviour, enjoying the mentioned properties of
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
, and approximating
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
up to bounded functions. This outstanding result belongs to Roy
[
69
]
. Its formulation requires the concept of a
d
–system
. We adapt the respective definition from
[
70
]
to the path
\mathfrak{T}
defined by (
68
) (we remind that this path corresponds to the problem of simultaneous approximation). As before, we assume that
d=n+1
.

Definition 19
.

Let
I\subset[0,\infty)
be an interval with nonempty interior. A continuous piece-wise linear mapping
\mathbf{P}=(P_{1},\ldots,P_{d}):I\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called a
d
–system
on
I
if the following conditions are fulfilled:

(i)

for each
s\in I
we have
P_{1}(s)\leqslant\ldots\leqslant P_{d}(s)
and
P_{1}(s)+\ldots+P_{d}(s)=0
;

(ii)

for each open interval
J\subset I
on which
\mathbf{P}
is differentiable, there is an index
i
such that the slope of
P_{i}
equals
-1
on
J
, while the slope of every
P_{j}
with
j\neq i
equals
1/(d-1)
;

(iii)

for every point
s
in the interior of
I
at which
\mathbf{P}
is not differentiable and all indices
i,j
such that
i<j
and
P^{\prime}_{i}(s-0)=P^{\prime}_{j}(s+0)=-1
, we have
P_{i}(s)=P_{i+1}(s)=\ldots=P_{j}(s)
.

Assuming that the path
\mathfrak{T}
and the lattice
\Lambda=\Lambda(\Theta)
are defined by (
68
) and (
67
), let us define the mapping
\mathbf{L}_{\Theta}:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
by

s\mapsto\Big(L_{1}\big(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\big),\ldots,L_{d}\big(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau}(s)\big)\Big).

Theorem 26

(D. Roy, 2015)
.

For each nonzero
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exist
s_{0}\geqslant 0
and a
d
–system
\mathbf{P}
on
[s_{0},\infty)
such that
\mathbf{L}_{\Theta}-\mathbf{P}
is bounded on
[s_{0},\infty)
. Conversely, for each
d
–system
\mathbf{P}
on
[s_{0},\infty)
with arbitrary
s_{0}\geqslant 0
there exists a nonzero
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\mathbf{L}_{\Theta}-\mathbf{P}
is bounded on
[s_{0},\infty)
.

Roy’s theorem proved to be much in demand for proving the existence of
\Theta
with prescribed Diophantine properties. It was Theorem
26
that enabled proving sharpness of many transference inequalities, and also Theorem
19
.

4
Several linear forms

4.1
General setting for the main problem of homogeneous linear Diophantine approximation

Given a matrix

\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}&\cdots&\theta_{1m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n1}&\cdots&\theta_{nm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\theta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\quad m+n\geqslant 3,

consider the system of linear equations

\Theta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

with variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In the most general form, the main question of homogeneous linear Diophantine approximation is how small the quantity
|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|
can be with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying the restriction
0<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant t
. By
|\cdot|
we denote hereafter the sup-norm.

It is easy to see that with
m=1
we get the problem of simultaneous approximation, and with
n=1
we get the problem of approximating zero with the values of a linear form. Same as in those problems, the respective Diophantine exponents are naturally defined. The following definition contains as particular cases Definitions
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
.

Definition 20
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that there exist arbitrarily large
t
such that (resp. for every
t
large enough) the system

|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant t,\qquad|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma}

(74)

admits a nonzero solution
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called the
regular
(resp.
uniform
)
Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\omega(\Theta)
(resp.
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
).

4.2
Dirichlet’s theorem again

In the case of several linear forms an analogue of Theorems
1
,
8
,
9
holds. It was also proved in Dirichlet’s paper
[
1
]
, though, same as with these theorems, he originally formulated it in a weaker form. We remind that
|\cdot|
denotes the sup-norm. Let us also denote by
\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
the set of
n\times m
real matrices.

Theorem 27

(G. Lejeune Dirichlet, 1842)
.

Let
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Then, for each
t\geqslant 1
, there is a nonzero pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant t,\qquad|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|\leqslant t^{-m/n}.

(75)

We note again that Theorem
27
follows immediately from Minkowski’s convex body theorem in the form of Corollary
5
applied to the system (
75
).

Corollary 11
.

For each
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
we have

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant m/n.

(76)

These inequalities, same as inequalities (
13
), are sharp. Moreover, Theorem
10
of Perron can be generalised to the matrix setting. Perron’s idea can be used to prove (for instance, this is done in Schmidt’s book
[
11
]
) that there exist matrices with algebraic entries that are
badly approximable
.

Definition 21
.

A matrix
\Theta
is called
badly approximable
if there is a constant
c>0
depending only on
\Theta
such that for every pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}
we have

|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|^{n}|\mathbf{x}|^{m}\geqslant c.

In that same Schmidt’s book
[
11
]
it is proved that a matrix is badly approximable if and only if so is its transpose. In Section
4.4.2
we show how to deduce this fact immediately from Mahler’s theorem. This fact is a manifestation of the
transference principle
, which we already paid attention to in Section
3.3
.

4.3
Transference theorems

In Section
3.3
we discussed the
transference principle
discovered by Khintchine, which relates the problem of simultaneous approximation and the problem of approximating zero by the values of a linear form. This relation can be generalised to the case of an arbitrary matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
. Let us denote by
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
the transposed matrix.

4.3.1
Inequalities for regular exponents

In 1947 Dyson
[
71
]
generalised Khintchine’s transference principle (Theorem
11
) as follows.

Theorem 28

(F. Dyson, 1947)
.

For every matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
we have

\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\omega(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\omega(\Theta)+m}\,.

(77)

Note that it follows from
77
and
76
that

\omega(\Theta)=m/n\iff\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})=n/m.

(78)

A year later, Khintchine published in
[
35
]
a simpler proof of Theorem
28
. But actually, Theorem
28
could have been derived in 1937 as a corollary to Theorem
21
of Mahler. We discuss this in more detail in Section
4.4
.

Note that any statement proved for
an arbitrary matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
with arbitrary
n
,
m
automatically generates an analogous statement for
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
. We simply need to swap the triple
(n,m,\Theta)
for the triple
(m,n,{\Theta}^{\intercal})
. Therefore, for an arbitrary matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, we also have

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\frac{m\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})+m-1}{(n-1)\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})+n}\,.

(79)

As we mentioned in Section
3.3.1
, in the case when either
m=1
, or
n=1
, sharpness of (
77
) was proved by Jarník in
[
32
]
,
[
33
]
. For
\min(n,m)>1
sharpness of (
77
) is proved only if

\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\max\Big(\frac{m}{n},\frac{n-1}{m-1}\Big)

(particularly, if
m\geqslant n
). This result also belongs to Jarník
[
72
]
. In all the remaining cases sharpness of (
77
) remains unproved.

4.3.2
Inequalities for uniform exponents

Jarník’s transference inequalities, i.e. inequalities (
19
), (
21
), were generalised in 1951 by Apfelbeck
[
73
]
to the case of arbitrary
n
,
m
. He proved the “uniform” analogue of (
77
)

\hat{\omega}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\hat{\omega}(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\hat{\omega}(\Theta)+m}

(80)

and also the inequality

\hat{\omega}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{1}{m}\left(n+\frac{n(n\hat{\omega}(\Theta)-m)-2n(m+n-3)}{(m-1)(n\hat{\omega}(\Theta)-m)+m-(m-2)(m+n-3)}\right),

(81)

under the assumption that
m>1
,
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)>(2(m+n-1)(m+n-3)+m)/n
. In 2012 inequalities (
80
), (
81
) were improved by the author in papers
[
36
]
,
[
37
]
. The following theorem generalises Theorem
13
to the case of arbitrary
n
,
m
.

Theorem 29

(O. G., 2012)
.

For every
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
m+n\geqslant 3
, we have

\hat{\omega}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\begin{cases}\dfrac{n-1}{m-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\quad\ \ \text{ if }\ \hat{\omega}(\Theta)\leqslant 1,\\
\dfrac{n-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{-1\vphantom{\big|}}}{m-1}\quad\text{ if }\ \hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant 1.\end{cases}

(82)

Note that, generally,
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
and
\hat{\omega}({\Theta}^{\intercal})
may equal
+\infty
, and this gives sense to (
82
) when some of the denominators equals zero.

If
\min(n,m)>1
, the question whether (
82
) is sharp remains open. We remind that in the case when either
m=1
or
n=1
sharpness of (
82
) was proved by Marnat
[
38
]
and (independently) by Schmidt and Summerer
[
39
]
.

4.3.3
“Mixed” inequalities

In those same papers
[
36
]
,
[
37
]
the following theorem is proved. It generalises Theorem
14
of Laurent and Bugeaud and refines Theorem
28
of Dyson.

Theorem 30

(O. G., 2012)
.

Given
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
m+n\geqslant 3
, assume that the space of rational solutions of the equation

\Theta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

is not one-dimensional. Then we have

\displaystyle\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\omega(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\omega(\Theta)+m}\,,

(83)

\displaystyle\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{(n-1)(1+\omega(\Theta))-(1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta))}{(m-1)(1+\omega(\Theta))+(1-\hat{\omega}(\Theta))}\,,

(84)

\displaystyle\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{(n-1)(1+\omega(\Theta)^{-1})-(\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{-1}-1)}{(m-1)(1+\omega(\Theta)^{-1})+(\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{-1}-1)}\,.

(85)

Clearly, (
83
) coincides with (
77
). This inequality is stronger than (
84
) and (
85
) if and only if

\hat{\omega}(\Theta)<\min\left(\frac{(m-1)\omega(\Theta)+m}{m+n-1}\,,\,\frac{(m+n-1)\omega(\Theta)}{(n-1)+n\omega(\Theta)}\right).

For instance, if
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)<(m+n-1)/n
and
\omega(\Theta)
is large enough. Thus, if
\min(n,m)>1
, inequalities (
84
), (
85
) are not guaranteed to improve upon Dyson’s inequality. Their sharpness is also doubtful, for, as we noted in Section
3.3.3
, in the cases
m=1
,
n\geqslant 3
and
n=1
,
m\geqslant 3
they are not sharp.

We note also that, up to now, no analogue of Schmidt–Summerer’s inequality (
25
) for the case when
\min(n,m)>1
is known. It would be very interesting to find such an analogue that, combined with (
82
), it would give (
84
) and (
85
) – same as (
25
) combined with (
22
) gives (
24
).

4.3.4
Inequalities between regular and uniform exponents

In paper
[
48
]
Jarník obtained the following result.

Theorem 31

(V. Jarník, 1954)
.

Given
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
, assume that the equation

\Theta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

admits no nonzero integer solutions. Then

(i) for
m=2
we have

\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)-1;

(86)

(ii) for
m\geqslant 3
and
\hat{\omega}\geqslant(5m^{2})^{m-1}
we have

\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{1/(m-1)}-3.

It is easy to see that (

) is stronger than the trivial estimate
\omega(\Theta)\geqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
only for
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)>2
. In 2013 Moshchevitin
[
74
]
improved upon Jarník’s result for

,

. His inequality is stronger than (

) and than the trivial inequality for each
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)>1
.

Theorem 32

(N. G. Moshchevitin, 2013)
.

Let
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
,
n\geqslant 2
. Assume that among the rows of
\Theta
there are two ones that are linearly independent with the vectors
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
over
\mathbb{Q}
. Assume also that
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant 1
. Then

\frac{\omega(\Theta)}{\hat{\omega}(\Theta)}\geqslant G(\Theta),

where
G(\Theta)
is defined as the greatest root of the polynomial

\hat{\omega}(\Theta)x^{2}-\big(\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{2}-\hat{\omega}(\Theta)+1\big)x-\big(\hat{\omega}(\Theta)-1\big)^{2},

if
1\leqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\leqslant 2
, and as the greatest root of the polynomial

\hat{\omega}(\Theta)x^{2}-\big(\hat{\omega}(\Theta)^{2}-1\big)x-\big(\hat{\omega}(\Theta)-1\big),

if
\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant 2
.

For
m=2
,
n=2
Theorem
32
is not the first improvement of inequality (
86
). In his survey
[
31
]
Moshchevitin presented an improvement of (

), however, it is indeed an improvement only if

. Theorem

is stronger than this result.

It is worth mentioning that

in Moshchevitin’s paper
[

]
contains two misprints. First, Moshchevitin’s proof works for every

, however, in the statement of the theorem the restriction

is imposed. Second, there is a misprint in the formula which defines the constant

.

Currently, there are no estimates for the ratio
\omega(\Theta)/\hat{\omega}(\Theta)
in the case when

stronger than Theorems

and

of Jarník and Moshchevitin.

4.4
Embedding into
\mathbb{R}^{m+n}

Let us demonstrate how to use the constructions discussed in Section
3.4
to prove transference theorems. Let us generalise the approach described in Section
3.4.3
by “embedding” the problems corresponding to
\Theta
and
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
into an

-dimensional Euclidean space. Set

Consider the lattices

\Lambda=\Lambda(\Theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&\\
-\Theta&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d},\qquad\Lambda^{\ast}=\Lambda^{\ast}(\Theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{I}_{m}&{\Theta}^{\intercal}\\
&\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(87)

and two families of parallelepipeds

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)

\displaystyle=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{l}|z_{j}|\leqslant t,\qquad\ \ j=1,\ldots,m\\
|z_{m+i}|\leqslant t^{-\gamma},\ \ i=1,\ldots,n\end{array}\Bigg\},

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)

\displaystyle\omega(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\delta\geqslant\frac{n}{m}\ \bigg|\ \forall\,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}\,\ \exists\,s>s_{0}:\text{\,we have }\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\},

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}({\Theta}^{\intercal})

\displaystyle=\sup\bigg\{\delta\geqslant\frac{n}{m}\ \bigg|\ \exists\,s_{0}\in\mathbb{R}:\ \forall\,s>s_{0}\text{ we have }\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\bigg\}.

4.4.1
Dyson’s theorem

If

t=s^{((n-1)\delta+n)/(d-1)},\qquad\gamma=\frac{m\delta+m-1}{(n-1)\delta+n}\,,

Let us interpret the problem of approximating zero with the values of several linear forms in the spirit of parametric geometry of numbers. According to Section

5
Multiplicative exponents

As in the previous Section, given a matrix

\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}&\cdots&\theta_{1m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n1}&\cdots&\theta_{nm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\theta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\quad m+n\geqslant 3,

let us consider the same system of linear equations

\Theta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

with variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Up until now, we have been dealing with the question how small the quantity
|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|
can be with
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}
,
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
satisfying the restriction
0<|\mathbf{x}|\leqslant t
, where
|\cdot|
denotes
\ell^{\infty}
-norm (sup-norm). The choice of
\ell^{\infty}
-norm for estimating the “magnitude” of a vector is quite conditional. Considering
\ell^{2}
-norm slightly alters the problem preserving, however, the values of Diophantine exponents. The reason is that any two norms in a finite dimensional space are known to be equivalent. The problem changes essentially if a vector’s “magnitude” is measured by the product of its coordinates. If we associate
\ell^{1}
-norm with the arithmetic mean, then the product of coordinates can be associated with the geometric mean. Such an approach is also absolutely classical. As an example, we can recall the famous Littlewood conjecture.

Littlewood’s Conjecture
.

For any
\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
and any
\varepsilon>0
the inequality

\prod_{i=1,2}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|\leqslant\varepsilon|x|^{-1}

admits infinitely many solutions in
(x,y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with nonzero
x
.

Respectively, it is natural to consider as a multiplicative analogue of the regular Diophantine exponent of a pair
(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})
the supremum of real
\gamma
such that the inequality

\prod_{i=1,2}|\theta_{i}x-y_{i}|^{1/2}\leqslant|x|^{-\gamma}

admits infinitely many solutions in
(x,y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}
with nonzero
x
. This is an example for the case
n=2
,
m=1
.

In order to give the definition of multiplicative exponents in the general case, let us set for each
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{k})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}

\Pi(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant k\end{subarray}}|z_{i}|^{1/k}\quad\text{ and }\quad\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\max\big(1,|z_{i}|\big)^{1/k}.

Definition 22
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that there exist arbitrarily large
t
such that (resp. for every
t
large enough) the system

\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})\leqslant t,\qquad\Pi(\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})\leqslant t^{-\gamma}

(99)

admits a nonzero solution
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}
is called the
regular
(resp.
uniform
)
multiplicative Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\omega_{\times}(\Theta)
(resp.
\hat{\omega}_{\times}(\Theta)
).

Ordinary and multiplicative exponents are related by the inequalities

\displaystyle\omega(\Theta)\leqslant\omega_{\times}(\Theta)\leqslant\begin{cases}m\omega(\Theta),\quad\text{ if }n=1,\\
+\infty,\qquad\ \text{ if }n\geqslant 2,\end{cases}

(100)

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\leqslant\hat{\omega}_{\times}(\Theta)\leqslant\begin{cases}m\hat{\omega}(\Theta),\quad\text{ if }n=1,\\
+\infty,\qquad\ \text{ if }n\geqslant 2,\end{cases}

which follow from the fact that for each
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
we have

\Pi(\mathbf{z})\leqslant|\mathbf{z}|,

and for each
\mathbf{z}\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}
we have

|\mathbf{z}|^{1/k}\leqslant\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{z})\leqslant|\mathbf{z}|.

Corollary
11
to Dirichlet’s theorem implies “trivial” inequalities

\displaystyle\omega_{\times}(\Theta)\geqslant\omega(\Theta)\geqslant m/n,\qquad\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\omega({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant n/m,

\displaystyle\hat{\omega}_{\times}(\Theta)\geqslant\hat{\omega}(\Theta)\geqslant m/n,\qquad\hat{\omega}_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\hat{\omega}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant n/m,

where
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
, as before, denotes the transposed matrix.

5.1
Analogue of Dyson’s theorem

In 1979 Schmidt and Wang showed in
[
75
]
that, same as in the case of ordinary Diophantine exponents (see (
78
)), we have

\omega_{\times}(\Theta)=m/n\iff\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})=n/m.

(101)

Again, this is a manifestation of the transference principle. As Bugeaud noted in
[

]
, the argument Schmidt and Wang used to prove (

[

]

Theorem 33

(O. G, 2011)

For every

we have

\omega_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\omega_{\times}(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\omega_{\times}(\Theta)+m}\,.

(102)

The method (
102

\hat{\omega}_{\times}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{n\hat{\omega}_{\times}(\Theta)+n-1}{(m-1)\hat{\omega}_{\times}(\Theta)+m}\,.

(103)

However, nothing stronger concerning uniform multiplicative exponents is currently known. The method Theorem
29
concerning ordinary uniform exponents was proved with does not work in the multiplicative setting because of the non-convexity of the functionals

and
\Pi^{\prime}(\cdot)
. Besides that, there exist neither “mixed” inequalities in the spirit of Theorems

,

,
30
, nor inequalities relating regular and uniform exponents – even in the case

.

The standard application of Mahler’s theorem – either in the form of Theorem

5.2.4
Refinement of Theorem

We actually prove something stronger than Theorem
34
in Section

. Implication (

. First, we note that for

foe each

is an analogue of (

\lambda_{j_{1}}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{j_{m}}

\lambda_{j_{1}}<1

to be equal to the greatest index such that
\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{j_{k}}<1

\lambda_{j_{1}}\geqslant 1
, we set

. Finally, we define the tuple
\hat{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\hat{\lambda}_{1},\ldots,\hat{\lambda}_{m})
by

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{j_{i}}=1,\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\ i=1,\ldots,k,

(129)

\displaystyle\hat{\lambda}_{j_{i}}=\lambda_{j_{i}}\big(\lambda_{j_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot\lambda_{j_{k}}\big)^{1/(m-k)},\qquad\ \ i=k+1,\ldots,m.\vphantom{1^{\big|}}

Arguing in the manner of Section
5.2.3
, we come to the following refinement of Theorem

.

Theorem 35
.

Let
\Lambda
,
\Lambda^{\ast}
be defined by (
104

Moreover,

\displaystyle\min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\hat{\lambda}_{j}\geqslant 1,

\,\ \min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mu^{\ast}_{i}\geqslant 1\implies\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mu_{i}\leqslant\pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu}).

We note also that the constant

5.3
Multiplicatively badly approximable matrices

The multiplicative setting also admits talking about badly approximable matrices.

Definition 23
.

A matrix
\Theta
is called
multiplicatively badly approximable
if there is a constant
c>0
depending only on
\Theta
such that for each pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}
we have

\Pi^{\prime}(\mathbf{x})^{m}\Pi(\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y})^{n}\geqslant c.

While the existence of badly approximable matrices can be proved rather easily, the question whether there exist multiplicatively badly approximable matrices is an open problem. Even in the simplest case
n=2
,
m=1
, the statement that multiplicatively badly approximable matrices exist is precisely the negation of Littlewood’s conjecture (its formulation can be found in the beginning of Section
5
).

In 1955 Cassels and Swinnerton-Dyer
[
78
]
proved that if
n=2
,
m=1
and
\Theta
is multiplicatively badly approximable, then so is
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
. Theorem
35
enables proving the converse statement as well as its analogue for an arbitrary matrix. Indeed, by analogy with the deduction of (
110
), it can be shown that
\Theta
is multiplicatively badly approximable if and only if there is a constant
c>0
such that none of the parallelepipeds in the family

\Big\{\,\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})\ \Big|\ \pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})=c,\ \min_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}\lambda_{j}\geqslant 1\Big\}

contains nonzero points of
\Lambda
(for the notation, see (
104
), (
105
), and (
113
)). Similarly,
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
is multiplicatively badly approximable if and only if there is a constant
c>0
such that none of the parallelepipeds in the family

\Big\{\,\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})\ \Big|\ \pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})=c,\ \min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}\mu_{i}\geqslant 1\Big\}

contains nonzero points of
\Lambda^{\ast}
. Since
\pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast},\boldsymbol{\mu}^{\ast})=\pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda},\boldsymbol{\mu})^{d-1}
, it follows immediately from Theorem
35
that if
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
is not multiplicatively badly approximable, then neither is
\Theta
. Thus, the following theorem is valid.

Theorem 36
.

A matrix
\Theta
is multiplicatively badly approximable if and only if so is
{\Theta}^{\intercal}
.

6
Diophantine approximation with weights

As before, let us consider a matrix

\Theta=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}&\cdots&\theta_{1m}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n1}&\cdots&\theta_{nm}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\theta_{ij}\in\mathbb{R},\quad m+n\geqslant 3,

and the system of linear equations

\Theta\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{y}

with variables
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
,
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

In previous Sections we observed two approaches in measuring the “magnitude” of
\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
: the first one uses the sup-norm, the second one uses the geometric mean of the absolute values of coordinates. There is also a, so to say, intermediate approach – so called
Diophantine approximation with weights
.

Let us fix
weights

\boldsymbol{\sigma}=(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{m})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{m}
,
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\ldots,\rho_{n})\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}^{n}
,

\sigma_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\sigma_{m},\qquad\rho_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\rho_{n},\qquad\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sigma_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{i}=1,

and define the
weighted norms

|\cdot|_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
and
|\cdot|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}
by

|\mathbf{x}|_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}=\max_{1\leqslant j\leqslant m}|x_{j}|^{1/\sigma_{j}}\qquad\text{ for }\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{m}),

|\mathbf{y}|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}|y_{i}|^{1/\rho_{i}}\qquad\,\ \ \text{ for }\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}).\

Consider the system of inequalities

\begin{cases}|\mathbf{x}|_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}\leqslant t\\
|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\leqslant t^{-\gamma}\end{cases}.

(131)

Definition 24
.

The supremum of real
\gamma
satisfying the condition that there exist arbitrarily large
t
such that (resp. for every
t
large enough) the system (
131
) admits a nonzero solution
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is called the
regular
(resp.
uniform
)
weighted Diophantine exponent
of
\Theta
and is denoted by
\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)
(resp.
\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)
).

It is easily verified that in the case of “trivial” weights, i.e. when all the
\sigma_{j}
are equal to
1/m
and all the
\rho_{i}
are equal to
1/n
, we are dealing with ordinary Diophantine exponents, as in this case we have

\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)=\frac{n}{m}\omega(\Theta)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)=\frac{n}{m}\hat{\omega}(\Theta).

Minkowski’s convex body theorem applied to the system (
131
) gives “trivial” inequalities

\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)\geqslant\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)\geqslant 1,

analogous to (
76
), that hold for every choice of
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}
.

6.1
Schmidt’s Bad-conjecture

In the case
m=1
,
n=2
we can observe a very close relationship between Diophantine approximation with weights and Littlewood’s conjecture, which relates to multiplicative Diophantine approximation (the formulation of this conjecture is given in the beginning of Section
5
). As we noted in Section
5.3
, the negation of this conjecture is equivalent to the statement that for
m=1
,
n=2
multiplicatively badly approximable matrices exist.

Definition 25
.

A matrix
\Theta
is called
badly approximable with weights
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}
if there is a constant
c>0
depending only on
\Theta
such that for each pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\in\mathbb{Z}^{m}\oplus\mathbb{Z}^{n}
with nonzero
\mathbf{x}
we have

|\mathbf{x}|_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}|\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}|_{\boldsymbol{\rho}}\geqslant c.

In paper
[
65
]
Schmidt noted that Davenport’s construction from paper
[
79
]
can be easily modified to prove that for
m=1
,
n=2
there exist badly approximable matrices
\Theta
with arbitrary weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}=(\rho_{1},\rho_{2})
and conjectured that for any two distinct sets of weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}
there exist matrices that are simultaneously badly approximable with weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
and with weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}
. It is easy to see that the existence of a counterexample to this conjecture, i.e the existence of two sets of weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
and
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}
such that any matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
is not badly approximable with at least one of them, implies Littlewood’s conjecture.

In 2011 Badziahin, Pollington, and Velani published the paper
[
80
]
, where they proved Schmidt’s conjecture formulated above. Note that not only did they prove the existence of matrices
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2\times 1}
that are badly approximable with weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime}
and with weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{\prime\prime}
, but they also proved that there are pretty many such matrices. More specifically, they proved that for any given
k
sets of weights
\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\rho}^{(k)}
the Hausdorff dimension of the set of matrices that are badly approximable with each of these
k
sets of weights equals
2
.

6.2
Transference theorems

The first transference inequality for Diophantine approximation with weights generalising Dyson’s inequality (
77
) was obtained in paper
[
81
]
. In this paper the authors showed that we have

\omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{(m+n-1)\big(\rho_{n}^{-1}+\sigma_{m}^{-1}\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)\big)+\sigma_{1}^{-1}\big(\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)-1\big)}{(m+n-1)\big(\rho_{n}^{-1}+\sigma_{m}^{-1}\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)\big)-\rho_{1}^{-1}\big(\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)-1\big)}\,.

(132)

As it turned out later, inequality (
132
) is not optimal. It was improved in paper
[
64
]
.

Theorem 37

(O. G., 2020)
.

For every matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and arbitrary weights
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}
we have

\omega_{\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\frac{\big(\rho_{n}^{-1}-1\big)+\sigma_{m}^{-1}\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)}{\rho_{n}^{-1}+\big(\sigma_{m}^{-1}-1\big)\omega_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)}\,.

(133)

In that same paper
[
64
]
a transference theorem for uniform weighted exponents is proved. It generalises Theorem
29
.

Theorem 38

(O. G., 2020)
.

For every matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
,
m+n\geqslant 3
, and arbitrary weights
\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}
we have

\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\rho},\boldsymbol{\sigma}}({\Theta}^{\intercal})\geqslant\begin{cases}\dfrac{1-\sigma_{m}\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)^{-1}}{1-\sigma_{m}}\hskip 8.53581pt\text{ if }\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)\geqslant\sigma_{m}/\rho_{n}\\
\hskip 5.69054pt\dfrac{1-\rho_{n}\vphantom{1^{\big|}}}{1-\rho_{n}\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)}\hskip 17.92523pt\text{ if }\hat{\omega}_{\boldsymbol{\sigma},\boldsymbol{\rho}}(\Theta)\leqslant\sigma_{m}/\rho_{n}\end{cases}.

(134)

As before, if some denominator happens to be equal to zero, we assume the respective expression to take value
+\infty
.

6.2.2
Deriving the theorem on regular exponents

For

(140)

Such a correspondence provides the inequalities

\displaystyle s^{-\delta\sigma_{j}}

\displaystyle\leqslant t^{-\sigma_{j}+1-\gamma},\qquad j=1,\ldots,m,

\displaystyle s^{\rho_{i}}

\displaystyle\leqslant t^{\gamma\rho_{i}+1-\gamma},\qquad\,\ i=1,\ldots,n,

which imply that

\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\subseteq\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)^{\ast}\ ,

\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)^{\ast}=\Bigg\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Bigg|\begin{array}[]{lr}|z_{j}|\leqslant t^{-\sigma_{j}+1-\gamma},&j=1,\ldots,m\\

\mathcal{Q}(s,\delta)\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies(d-1)\mathcal{P}(t,\gamma)\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.

7
Diophantine exponents if lattices

In previous Sections we considered a matrix
\Theta\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times m}
and dealt with the question how fast the vector
\Theta\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{y}
can tend to zero if
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
are assumed to range through integer vectors. In other words, we considered
n
linear forms with coefficients written in the rows of the matrix

\begin{pmatrix}\Theta&-\mathbf{I}_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\theta_{11}&\cdots&\theta_{1m}&-1&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\theta_{n1}&\cdots&\theta_{nm}&0&\cdots&-1\end{pmatrix}

and studied their values at integer points. We considered several ways to measure the “magnitude” of a vector. The first one uses the sup-norm, the second one uses the geometric mean of the absolute values of coordinates, the third one uses weighted norms. But each time we worked with
n
linear forms in
d=m+n
variables. I.e. every time the number of linear forms was strictly less than the dimension of the ambient space. This section is devoted to problems concerning
d
-tuples of linear forms in
d

Let
L_{1},\ldots,L_{d}
be linearly independent linear forms in
d
variables. Consider the lattice

\Lambda=\Big\{\big(L_{1}(\mathbf{u}),\ldots,L_{d}(\mathbf{u})\big)\,\Big|\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}\Big\}.

Using a norm to measure the “magnitude” of the elements of
\Lambda
is not very productive, as such a norm is bounded away from zero at nonzero points of
\Lambda
. But the geometric mean of the absolute values of coordinates leads to very interesting and sometimes very difficult problems. As in Section
5
, let us set for each
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\Pi(\mathbf{z})=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant i\leqslant d\end{subarray}}|z_{i}|^{1/d}.

We remind also that
|\cdot|
denotes the sup-norm.

Definition 26
.

Supremum of real
\gamma
such that the inequality

\Pi(\mathbf{z})\leqslant|\mathbf{z}|^{-\gamma}

admits infinitely many solutions in
\mathbf{z}\in\Lambda
is called the
Diophantine exponent
of
\Lambda
and is denoted as
\omega(\Lambda)
.

7.1
Spectrum of lattice exponents

It follows from Minkowski’s convex body theorem that for every lattice
\Lambda
the “trivial” inequality

\omega(\Lambda)\geqslant 0

holds. It is clear that
\omega(\Lambda)=0
whenever the functional
\Pi(\mathbf{x})
is bounded away from zero at nonzero points of
\Lambda
. For instance, this is the case if
\Lambda
is the lattice of a complete module in a totally real algebraic extension of
\mathbb{Q}
, i.e. if

\Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\sigma_{1}(\omega_{1})&\sigma_{1}(\omega_{2})&\cdots&\sigma_{1}(\omega_{d})\\
\sigma_{2}(\omega_{1})&\sigma_{2}(\omega_{2})&\cdots&\sigma_{2}(\omega_{d})\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\sigma_{d}(\omega_{1})&\sigma_{d}(\omega_{2})&\cdots&\sigma_{d}(\omega_{d})\end{pmatrix}\mathbb{Z}^{d},

(149)

where
\omega_{1},\ldots,\omega_{d}
is a basis of a totally real extension
E
of
\mathbb{Q}
of degree
d
and
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{d}
are the embeddings of
E
into
\mathbb{R}
. Such lattices are often called
algebraic
. A detailed account on algebraic lattices can be found, for instance, in Borevich and Shafarevich’s book
[
83
]
.

There is a wider class of lattices with
\omega(\Lambda)=0
. Their existence is provided by Schmidt’s famous subspace theorem, which was published by Schmidt in 1972 in paper
[
84
]
. Its formulation and proof can also be found in Bombieri and Gubler’s book
[
85
]
. It is shown in papers
[
86
]
,
[
87
]
that if the coefficients of linear forms

are algebraic and for every

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})
,

,

, the coefficients of the multivector

are linearly independent over
\mathbb{Q}
, then by the subspace theorem we have
\omega(\Lambda)=0
.

It was shown in paper
[
87
]
that weakening the condition of linear independence formulated above enables constructing examples for
d\geqslant 3
of lattices with exponents attaining values

\frac{\,ab\,}{cd}\,,\qquad\begin{array}[]{l}a,b,c\in\mathbb{N},\\
a+b+c=d.\end{array}

(150)

It is natural to conjecture that there are lattices
\Lambda
with any prescribed nonnegative
\omega(\Lambda)
. For
d=2
this is easily proved with the help of continued fractions (see Section
2.5
). For

this question is still open. It is proved in paper
[
88

that the interval

\bigg[3-\frac{d}{(d-1)^{2}}\,,\,+\infty\bigg]

(151)

is contained in the spectrum of the values of

. The question whether there are positive numbers in this spectrum that are not in the interval (

) and do not equal any of the numbers (

) is still open for

.

7.2
Lattices with positive norm minimum

For lattices, the analogue of the property of a number to be badly approximable is the property to have positive
norm minimum
.

Definition 27
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Its
norm minimum
is defined as

N(\Lambda)=\inf_{\mathbf{z}\in\Lambda\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}}\Pi(\mathbf{z})^{d}.

As we said in the previous Section, if
\Lambda
is an algebraic lattice, then
N(\Lambda)>0
. Cassels and Swinnerton-Dyer showed in paper
[
78
]
that Littlewood’s conjecture (see the formulation in the beginning of Section
5
) can be derived from the following assumption, which is an analogue of Oppenheim’s conjecture on quadratic forms for decomposable forms of degree
d
. The latter was proved by Margulis in the end of 1980s (see
[
89
]
).

Cassels–Swinnerton-Dyer’s Conjecture
.

Let
d\geqslant 3
and let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then the condition
N(\Lambda)>0
is equivalent to the existence of a non-degenerate diagonal operator
D
such that
D\Lambda
is an algebraic lattice, i.e. satisfies (
149
).

The main tool in the proof of the fact that Littlewood’s conjecture follows from Cassels–Swinnerton-Dyer’s conjecture is Mahler’s compactness criterion (see
[
15
]
and
[
78
]
).

For
d=2
the statement of Cassels–Swinnerton-Dyer’s conjecture is not true. Indeed, in view of Proposition
3
and geometric interpretation of continued fractions described in Section
2.5
, in the two-dimensional case the lattice

\Lambda=\Big\{\big(L_{1}(\mathbf{u}),L_{2}(\mathbf{u})\big)\,\Big|\,\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\Big\}

has positive norm minimum if and only if the ratio of the coefficients of
L_{1}
and the ratio of the coefficients of
L_{2}
are badly approximable numbers. Thus, if these two ratios are irrational numbers with bounded partial quotients, but the sequences of these partial quotients are not periodic, then
N(\Lambda)>0
, but there is no diagonal operator
D
such that
D\Lambda
is an algebraic lattice.

Due to Dirichlet’s theorem on algebraic units, algebraic lattices have rich symmetry groups consisting of diagonal operators. Details can be found, for instance, in books
[
83
]
,
[
19
]
and also in paper
[
90
]
. This observation generalises to the multidimensional case the fact that quadratic irrationalities have periodic continued fractions. Thus, Cassels–Swinnerton-Dyer’s conjecture claims that for
d\geqslant 3
the inequality
N(\Lambda)>0
(which, we remind, is an analogue of the property of a number to be badly approximable) implies that
\Lambda
has a rich group of symmetries consisting of diagonal operators. This statement admits reformulation in terms of multidimensional continued fractions. To be more specific, in terms of
Klein polyhedra
.

Definition 28
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In each orthant
\mathcal{O}
, let us consider the convex hull

\mathcal{K}(\Lambda,\mathcal{O})=\conv\big(\Lambda\cap\mathcal{O}\backslash\{\mathbf{0}\}\big).

Each of the
2^{d}
convex hulls thus obtained is called a
Klein polyhedron
.

In the case
d=2
Definition
28
differs from Definition

of Klein polygons given in Section
2.5.4

are a geometric interpretation of a continued fraction of a single number. If we take a pair of distinct numbers, then the respective pair of lines divides the plane into four angles and the respective pair of linear forms determines a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. This construction precisely corresponds to Definition
28
, but it interprets geometrically the continued fractions of a pair of numbers. We discuss it in detail in Section

We say that a lattice
\Lambda
is

if none of its nonzero points have zero coordinates. If a lattice is irrational, then each of the

[
91

[
92

2^{d}
Klein polyhedra has only compact faces. Particularly, each face has a finite number of vertices.

We showed in Section

that in the two-dimensional case the role of partial quotients is played by edges and vertices of Klein polygons equipped with linear integer invariants such as integer length of an edge and integer angle at a vertex. In the multidimensional case it is natural to consider facets (i.e.

]
,
[

]

[

It is clear that if

According to Corollary

|q|
large enough

\Pi(\mathbf{v})\asymp|\mathbf{v}|^{-\gamma}\iff\min\big(|q\theta_{1}-p|,|q\theta_{2}-p|\big)\asymp q^{-1-2\gamma}.

(154)

Further, if all the vertices of all the Klein polyhedra of
\Lambda
satisfy the condition
\Pi(\mathbf{v})\geqslant|\mathbf{v}|^{-\gamma}
, then so do all nonzero points of
\Lambda
. This leads us to the following equality:

\omega(\Lambda)=\sup\Big\{\gamma\in\mathbb{R}\ \Big|\,\exists\,\infty\,\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V}:\,\Pi(\mathbf{v})\leqslant|\mathbf{v}|^{-\gamma}\Big\}.

Hence by (
154
)

\max\big(\omega(\theta_{1}),\omega(\theta_{2})\big)=1+2\omega(\Lambda).

Taking into account (
152
) and (
153
), we get

\omega(\Lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\displaystyle\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V},\ |\mathbf{v}|>1\\
|\mathbf{v}|\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{\log(\det\textup{St}_{\mathbf{v}})}{\log|\mathbf{v}|}\,.

(155)

Thus, relation (
155
) is an analogue of equality (
4
) from Corollary
7
for Diophantine exponents of lattices of rank
2
. It can be generalised to the multidimensional case, but what is currently known is far from an exhaustive generalisation. There is only one respective result. It was obtained in paper
[
95
]
.

Theorem 40

(E. R. Bigushev, O G., 2022)
.

Let
\Lambda
be an irrational full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the set of vertices of all the eight Klein polyhedra of
\Lambda
. Then

\omega(\Lambda)\leqslant\frac{2}{3}\displaystyle\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{v}\in\mathcal{V},\ |\mathbf{v}|>1\\
|\mathbf{v}|\to\infty\end{subarray}}\frac{\log(\det\textup{St}_{\mathbf{v}})}{\log|\mathbf{v}|}\,.

(156)

It is shown in that same paper
[
95
]
that the main statement upon which the proof of Theorem
40
is based cannot be inverted due to the existence of a counterexample. Nevertheless, it may be so that this statement is too local and its locality can be “relaxed” a bit. Another way that can probably help to turn inequality (
156
) into an equality is to find a more appropriate quantitative characteristic of multidimensional partial quotients than determinants.

7.4
Transference theorem

In the case of Diophantine exponents of lattices, there also exists a transference theorem. And same as with the theorems of Khintchine, Dyson, and their generalisations for multiplicative exponents and Diophantine approximation with weights, the simplest way to prove it is to derive it from Mahler’s theorem in disguise of Theorem
22
.

As before, we denote by
\Lambda^{\ast}
the dual lattice of
\Lambda
.
For
d=2
the lattice
\Lambda^{\ast}
coincides up to homothety with
\Lambda
rotated by
\pi/2
. Thus, in the two-dimensional case we have the obvious equality
\omega(\Lambda)=\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})
. The following theorem is proved in paper
[
87
]
.

Theorem 41
.

If one of the exponents
\omega(\Lambda)
,
\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})
equals zero, then so does the other one. If they are both nonzero, then

(d-1)^{-2}\leqslant\frac{1+\omega(\Lambda)^{-1}}{1+\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})^{-1}}\leqslant(d-1)^{2}.

(157)

Let us show how to derive Theorem
41
from Theorem
22
.

For each tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
, let us define the parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
by

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\Big\{\,\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Big|\ |z_{i}|\leqslant\lambda_{i},\ i=1,\ldots,d\Big\}.

(158)

Let us assign to each tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
the tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}=(\lambda_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,\lambda_{d}^{\ast})
,

\lambda_{i}^{\ast}=\lambda_{i}^{-1}\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{d},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d.

(159)

Then
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{\ast}=\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast})
, i.e.
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast})
is the pseudocompound of
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
(see Definition
14
in Section
3.4.2
). By Theorem
22

\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast})\cap\Lambda^{\ast}\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}\implies(d-1)\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cap\Lambda\neq\{\mathbf{0}\}.

(160)

Next, for each
\delta\geqslant 0
, let us set

\gamma=\frac{\delta}{(d-1)^{2}+d(d-2)\delta}\,.

If
\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast})=|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}|^{-\delta}
, then

\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast})^{1/(d-1)}=|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}|^{-\delta/(d-1)}

and

|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|\leqslant\frac{\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{d}}{\displaystyle\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}|\lambda_{i}|^{d-1}}=\frac{|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}|^{d-1}}{\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})^{d(d-2)}}=|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}|^{d-1+\frac{d(d-2)}{d-1}\delta}.

Therefore,

\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast})=|\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\ast}|^{-\delta}\implies\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\leqslant|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|^{-\gamma}

(161)

By (
161
) and (
160
) we have

\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})\geqslant\delta\implies\omega(\Lambda)\geqslant\gamma=\frac{\delta}{(d-1)^{2}+d(d-2)\delta}\,,

whence

\omega(\Lambda)\geqslant\frac{\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})}{(d-1)^{2}+d(d-2)\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})}

or, equivalently,

\frac{1+\omega(\Lambda)^{-1}}{1+\omega(\Lambda^{\ast})^{-1}}\leqslant(d-1)^{2}.

Since
\Lambda
and
\Lambda^{\ast}
can be swapped, we get both inequalities (
157
).

7.5
Parametric geometry of numbers

In Section
3.4.8
we described an approach developed by Schmidt and Summerer. A fundamental role in this approach is played by the space of parameters

\mathcal{T}=\Big\{\boldsymbol{\tau}=(\tau_{1},\ldots,\tau_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\,\Big|\,\tau_{1}+\ldots+\tau_{d}=0\Big\}.

We saw in Sections
3.4.8
,
4.4.4
that the problem of approximating zero with the values of several linear forms requires considering one-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{T}
, along which
\boldsymbol{\tau}
is to tend to infinity. As for Diophantine approximation with nontrivial weights, we saw in Section
6.4
that it requires working with two-dimensional subspaces of
\mathcal{T}
. In the context of problems related to Diophantine exponents of lattices, we need to work with the whole
\mathcal{T}
.

Let us set for each
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}

|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{+}=\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant d}\tau_{i}.

Every norm in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
induces a norm in
\mathcal{T}
. For instance, the sup-norm
|\cdot|
. But the functional
|\cdot|_{+}
is not a norm for
d\geqslant 3
, as the respective “unit balls” are simplices, which are obviously not symmetric about the origin. Nevertheless, the functional
|\cdot|_{+}
plays a very important role, as it is the image of the sup-norm under the logarithmic mapping: if
\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})
,
z_{i}>0
,
i=1,\ldots,d
, and
\mathbf{z}_{\log}=(\log z_{1},\ldots,\log z_{d})
, then

\log|\mathbf{z}|=|\mathbf{z}_{\log}|_{+}\,.

It is clear that
|\cdot|_{+}
generates a
monotone exhaustion
of
\mathcal{T}
, i.e.
\mathcal{T}=\bigcup_{\lambda>0}\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
, where

\mathcal{S}(\lambda)=\Big\{\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}\,\Big|\,f(\boldsymbol{\tau})\leqslant\lambda\Big\}\qquad\text{ for }\lambda>0,

each set
\mathcal{S}(\lambda)
is compact, and
\mathcal{S}(\lambda^{\prime})
is contained in the (relative) interior of
\mathcal{S}(\lambda^{\prime\prime})
for
\lambda^{\prime}<\lambda^{\prime\prime}
. Particularly,
|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{+}\to+\infty
is equivalent to
|\boldsymbol{\tau}|\to+\infty
.

Diophantine exponents of lattices require the following modification of Definition
17
.

Definition 30
.

Given a lattice
\Lambda
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the quantities

\underline{\varphi}_{k}(\Lambda)=\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\boldsymbol{\tau}|\to\infty\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\frac{L_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{+}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\varphi}_{k}(\Lambda)=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\boldsymbol{\tau}|\to\infty\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\frac{L_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{+}}

are respectively called the
k
-th lower
and
upper Schmidt–Summerer exponents of the first type
.

Definition 31
.

Given a lattice
\Lambda
and
k\in\{1,\ldots,d\}
, the quantities

\underline{\Phi}_{k}(\Lambda)=\liminf_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\boldsymbol{\tau}|\to\infty\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\frac{S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{+}}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\overline{\Phi}_{k}(\Lambda)=\limsup_{\begin{subarray}{c}|\boldsymbol{\tau}|\to\infty\\
\boldsymbol{\tau}\in\mathcal{T}\end{subarray}}\frac{S_{k}(\Lambda,\boldsymbol{\tau})}{|\boldsymbol{\tau}|_{+}}

are respectively called the
k
-th lower
and
upper Schmidt–Summerer exponents of the second type
.

It was shown in paper
[
68
]
that the Diophantine exponent of
\Lambda
is related to
\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda)
by

\big(1+\omega(\Lambda)\big)\big(1+\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda)\big)=1.

This relation is equivalent to

1+\omega(\Lambda)^{-1}=-\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda)^{-1}.

Thus, inequalities (
157
) can be rewritten as follows:

(d-1)^{-2}\leqslant\frac{\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda^{\ast})}{\underline{\varphi}_{1}(\Lambda)}\leqslant(d-1)^{2}.

And same as with Khintchine’s and Dyson’s inequalities, as well as with transference inequalities for Diophantine approximation with weights, this approach enables to split inequality (
157
) into a chain of inequalities between intermediate exponents. The respective definitions and formulations can be found in paper
[
68
]
.

7.6
Uniform exponent and Mordell’s constant

The attentive reader may have noticed that in all the previous Sections we discussed two types of Diophantine exponents –
regular
ones and
uniform
ones. But in the case of Diophantine exponents of lattices, we have been discussing only regular exponents so far, not mentioning their uniform analogue even once. There is a reason for this, and we discuss this reason in the current Section. The thing is that, same as with the problem of approximating a real number by rationals (see Section
2.6
), the uniform exponent of a lattice is “trivial” – it equals either infinity, or zero. It is natural to consider as a uniform analogue of
\omega(\Lambda)
the quantity
\hat{\omega}(\Lambda)
equal to the supremum of real
\gamma
such that for every
t
large enough and every
\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
satisfying the relations

|\boldsymbol{\lambda}|=t,\qquad\Pi(\boldsymbol{\lambda})=t^{-\gamma}

the parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
determined by (
158
) contains a nonzero point of
\Lambda
.

7.6.1
Mordell’s constant

In 1937, in his paper
[
96
]
, Mordell asked whether there exists a constant
c
depending only on dimension
d
such that for every lattice
\Lambda
with determinant
1
there is a tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
satisfying the following two conditions:

\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=c\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cap\Lambda=\{\mathbf{0}\},

where
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is the parallelepiped determined by (
158
). In that same year, Siegel proved in a letter to Mordell that the answer to this question is positive. A slightly different proof was proposed by Davenport in
[
97
]
. Davenport proved the following statement.

Theorem 42

(H. Davenport, 1937)
.

Let
\Lambda
be a full rank lattice in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with determinant
1
. Consider an arbitrary
d
-tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
such that

\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i}=1.

Let
\mu_{k}=\mu_{k}\big(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\Lambda\big)
,
k=1,\ldots,d
, denote the
k
-th successive minimum of the parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
w.r.t.
\Lambda
. Then there is a positive constant
c
depending only on
d
and a permutation
k_{1},\ldots,k_{d}
of the indices
1,\ldots,d
such that the interior of
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
, where
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}=(\lambda^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,\lambda^{\prime}_{d})
,

\lambda^{\prime}_{i}=(d!\cdot c)^{1/d}\mu_{k_{i}}\lambda_{i},\qquad i=1,\ldots,d,

contains no nonzero points of
\Lambda
.

It follows from Minkowski’s second theorem (see Theorem
24
in Section
3.4.8
) that the tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime}
from Theorem
42
satisfies

\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda^{\prime}_{i}\geqslant c.

Thus, Theorem
42
, indeed, gives a positive answer to Mordell’s question. Respectively, if we define for each lattice
\Lambda
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with determinant
1
its
Mordell’s constant
by

\kappa(\Lambda)=\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}\\
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\cap\Lambda=\{\mathbf{0}\}\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{d}\lambda_{i},

then the quantity

\kappa_{d}=\inf_{\Lambda:\,\det\Lambda=1}\kappa(\Lambda)

is positive for each
d
. The best known estimate

\kappa_{d}\geqslant d^{-d/2}

belongs to Shapira and Weiss
[
98
]
. Their result confirmed the conjecture made by Ramharter
[
99
]
that
\limsup_{d\to\infty}\kappa_{d}^{1/d\log d}>0
.

7.6.2
Triviality of the uniform exponent

Theorem
42
has local nature. It enables therefore to find “empty” parallelepipeds of fixed volume arbitrarily “far”. Let us assume for simplicity that the first coordinate axis

\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Big|\ z_{i}=0,\ i=2,\ldots,d\Big\}

is not a subset of any subspace
\mathcal{L}
of dimension
k<d
such that
\mathcal{L}\cap\Lambda
is a lattice of rank
k
. Then, for every
\varepsilon>0
, the cylinder

\Big\{\mathbf{z}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{d})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \Big|\ |z_{i}|<\varepsilon,\ i=2,\ldots,d\Big\}

contains
d
linearly independent points of
\Lambda
. Thus, for every such
\varepsilon
, there is
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and a tuple
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{d})\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{d}
such that

\lambda_{1}=\lambda,\qquad\lambda_{i}=\lambda^{-1/(d-1)},\qquad i=2,\ldots,d,

and

\mu_{d}\big(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\Lambda\big)\lambda^{-1/(d-1)}<\varepsilon.

By Theorem
42
the parallelepiped
\mu_{d}\big(\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}),\Lambda\big)\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
contains a parallelepiped
\mathcal{P}(\boldsymbol{\lambda}^{\prime})
of fixed volume that does not contain nonzero points of
\Lambda
. It immediately follows that
\hat{\omega}(\Lambda)=0
.
