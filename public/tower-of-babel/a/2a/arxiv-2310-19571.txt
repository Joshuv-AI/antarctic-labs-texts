A numerical study of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator in planar domains
Adrien Chaigneau, Denis S. Grebenkov

Abstract
We numerically investigate the generalized Steklov problem for the modified Helmholtz equation and focus on the relation between its spectrum and the geometric structure of the domain. We address three distinct aspects: (i) the asymptotic behavior of eigenvalues for polygonal domains; (ii) the dependence of the integrals of eigenfunctions on the domain symmetries; and (iii) the localization and exponential decay of Steklov eigenfunctions away from the boundary for smooth shapes and in the presence of corners. For this purpose, we implemented two complementary numerical methods to compute the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of the associated Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator for various simply-connected planar domains. We also discuss applications of the obtained results in the theory of diffusion-controlled reactions and formulate several conjectures with relevance in spectral geometry.

In this study, we focus on simply-connected planar bounded domains
\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a Lipschitz boundary
\partial\Omega

\mathcal{M}_{p}
associates to a function

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{p}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega)

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega)

(1)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\left.\left(\partial_{n}u\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega},

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}(p-\Delta)u(\boldsymbol{x})&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\

(2)

in the Sobolev space

\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)=\{u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\mid\partial_{x}u\in L^{2}(\Omega),\partial_{y}u\in L^{2}(\Omega)\},

(3)

\displaystyle H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega):=

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\left(H^{1}(\Omega)\right)

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\left\{v\in L^{2}(\partial\Omega)\mid\exists u\in H^{1}(\Omega):\operatorname{tr}(u)=u|_{\partial\Omega}=v\right\},

Throughout the paper, we focus on

\mathcal{M}_{p}v_{k}^{(p)}=\mu_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}\quad(k=0,1,2,\ldots).

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}(p-\Delta)V_{k}^{(p)}&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\

(7)

One sees that

V_{k}^{(p)}

\partial\Omega

\Omega

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}(p-\Delta)V_{k}^{(p)}&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\
V_{k}^{(p)}&=v_{k}^{(p)}&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega).\end{array}\right.

2
Numerical methods

There is a very limited number of domains whose symmetries allow for separation of variables and thus lead to fully explicit formulas for the eigenvalues and eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{M}_{p}

[
4
,
11
]
. In other cases, one needs to employ numerical methods to construct the Dirichlet-to-Neumannn operator. For instance, one can use finite-difference or finite element methods to discretize the problem on a regular lattice or a mesh and to construct a matrix representing the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{M}_{p}
that needs to be diagonalized to approximate the eigenvalues
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
and eigenfunctions
v_{k}^{(p)}
. Flexibility is one of the advantages of these techniques that can deal in the same way with more general second-order elliptic operators. In turn, the need for mesh construction and large sizes of the matrices to be diagonalized are usual drawbacks. Various improvements have been proposed to overcome these limitations: an isoparametric variant of the finite element method for solving
Steklov eigenvalue problems in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
for second-order, self-adjoint, elliptic differential operators

[
25
]
, a two-grid discretization scheme
[
26
,
27
]
, a finite element multi-scale discretization with
an adaptive algorithm based on the shifted inverse iteration
[
28
]
, an iterative multilevel approach

[
29
]
, a nonconforming finite element methods
[
30
,
31
]
. Another possibility is the reformulation of the Steklov eigenvalue problem in terms of an equivalent boundary integral equation
[
32
,
33
,
34
]
, or the method of fundamental solutions for solving the Steklov and related spectral problems for the Laplace operator
[
35
,
36
]
. In the planar case, one can also employ conformal mapping to transform the original domain into a simpler domain (e.g., a disk), at the price of dealing with generalized Robin boundary condition
[
37
]
.

For the purpose of our study, the basic finite element method provided a sufficient accuracy and moderate computational cost. Its practical implementation is detailed in
section

2.1
. In addition, we discuss an alternative technique based on the restriction of Green’s functions (
section

2.2
).

2.1
Finite element method

The first numerical method aims at representing the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator by a matrix in
two steps. First, we construct the vector
U
representing the solution
u
of the modified Helmholtz problem
eq.

2
with a finite element method.
We discretize
\Omega
into a triangular mesh, which has
N_{i}
nodes inside the domain
\Omega
, and
N_{e}
nodes on the boundary
\partial\Omega
. The interior nodes are enumerated by
i=1,\ldots,N_{i}
, while the boundary nodes are enumerated by
i=N_{i}+1,\ldots,N_{i}+N_{e}
. This discretization is equivalent to projection of the weak form of the equation onto a finite-dimensional subspace of dimension
N_{p}=N_{i}+N_{e}
. Let
\{\phi_{i}\}
, with
i=1,\dots,N_{p}
, be piecewise polynomial basis functions of a subspace of
\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega)
. In our implementation, each
\phi_{i}
is a “hat” function that is linear on each element and takes the value
0
at all nodes
x_{j}
except for
x_{i}
at which it is equal to
1
. This property ensures that the functions
\{\phi_{i}\}_{i=1,\ldots,N_{i}}
vanish on the boundary and thus can serve as a basis of
\mathcal{H}^{1}_{0}(\Omega)
.

Multiplying the modified Helmholtz equation
(p-\Delta)u=0
by a test function
\phi_{j}
with
j=1,2,...,N_{i}
, integrating over
\Omega
, and using the Green’s formula, one gets

\forall j=1,2,...,N_{i},\quad\int_{\Omega}pu\phi_{j}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla\phi_{j}=0,

(13)

without boundary terms because
\phi_{j}|_{\partial\Omega}=0
. Next, one can approximate the solution
u
as a linear combination of basis functions:

u=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}U_{i}\phi_{i},

(14)

with unknown coefficients
U_{i}
.
We split the sum as

u=\sum_{i=1}^{N_{i}}U_{i}\phi_{i}+\underbrace{\sum_{i=N_{i}+1}^{N_{i}+N_{e}}F_{i}\phi_{i}}_{=f},

(15)

where the first term represents
u
inside
\Omega
and the second one incorporates the Dirichlet boundary condition
u=f
by setting

\forall i=N_{i}+1,\ldots,N_{i}+N_{e},\quad U_{i}=F_{i},

(16)

with the coefficients
F_{i}
representing
f
. Substitution of this expansion into
eq.

13
yields for all
j=1,2,...,N_{i}

\sum_{i=1}^{N_{i}}\left(p\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{i}\nabla\phi_{j}\right)U_{i}=-\sum_{i=N_{i}+1}^{N_{i}+N_{e}}\left(p\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{i}\nabla\phi_{j}\right)F_{i}.

(17)

We denote by
K
the stiff matrix (of size
N_{p}\times N_{p}
) given by
\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{i}\nabla\phi_{j}
and by
M
the mass matrix (of size
N_{p}\times N_{p}
) given by
\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
. Since the basis functions
\phi_{i}
vanish on all the elements that do not contain the node
x_{i}
,
K_{ij}
and
M_{ij}
are zero except if
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are the vertices of the same element and thus the matrices
K
and
M
are very sparse. We get the matrix formulation:

\displaystyle(pM+K)^{ii}~U^{i}

\displaystyle=-(pM+K)^{ie}~F,

(18)

where
(pM+K)^{ii}
is the
N_{i}\times N_{i}
submatrix,
(pM+K)^{ie}
is the
N_{i}\times N_{e}
submatrix,
F
is the
N_{e}\times 1
vector, and
U^{i}
is the
N_{i}\times 1
vector that can thus be found as

U^{i}=-[(pM+K)^{ii}]^{-1}(pM+K)^{ie}~F.

(19)

Combining these coefficients with
eq.

16
, we construct the whole vector
U
of coefficients
U_{i}
as

U=\begin{pmatrix}-[(pM+K)^{ii}]^{-1}(pM+K)^{ie}\\
I^{ee}\end{pmatrix}F,

(20)

where
I^{ee}
is the identity matrix of size
N_{e}\times N_{e}
. This concludes the first step.

The second step consists in representing the action of the normal derivative. We restart from the weak formulation of
(p-\Delta)u=0

\forall v\in\mathcal{H}^{1}(\Omega),\quad\int_{\Omega}puv+\int_{\Omega}\nabla u\nabla v=\int_{\partial\Omega}hv,

(21)

with
h=\partial_{n}u
.
Substituting
eq.

14
into
eq.

21
, the weak formulation reads for any test function
\phi_{j}
as

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{N_{p}}\left(p\int_{\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}+\int_{\Omega}\nabla\phi_{i}\nabla\phi_{j}\right)U_{i}=\sum_{i=N_{i}+1}^{N_{p}}\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}\right)H_{i}\quad(j=1,...,N_{i}),

(22)

where
H_{i}
are the unknown coefficients representing
h
on the basis
\{\phi_{i}\}
.
Denoting by
M_{b}
the matrix (of size
N_{e}\times N_{e}
) given by
\int_{\partial\Omega}\phi_{i}\phi_{j}
, we get the matrix formulation

\left(pM+K\right)^{ep}U=M_{b}H,

(23)

where
\left(pM+K\right)^{ep}
is the
N_{e}\times N_{p}
submatrix.
As a consequence,
eq.

20
for
U
implies

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle={\bf M}_{p}F,

(24)

where

{\bf M}_{p}=M_{b}^{-1}\left(pM+K\right)^{ep}\begin{pmatrix}-[(pM+K)^{ii}]^{-1}(pM+K)^{ie}\\
I^{ee}\end{pmatrix}.

(25)

According to
eq.

24
the matrix
{\bf M}_{p}
transforms the Dirichlet boundary condition
u=f
, with a function
f
represented by the vector
F
, into the Neumann boundary condition
\partial_{n}u=h
, with
h
represented by the vector
H
. In other words, this is a matrix representation of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{M}_{p}
in terms of basis functions
\{\phi_{i}\}
. Once the matrix
{\bf M}_{p}
is constructed, one can apply standard numerical algorithms to diagonalize it. The obtained eigenvalues of
{\bf M}_{p}
approximate the eigenvalues
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
. As
{\bf M}_{p}
is a finite-size matrix (of size
N_{e}\times N_{e}
), only a finite number of eigenvalues
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
can be accurately approximated. In practice, we will limit our analysis to few tens of eigenvalues (say with
k
from 0 up to 20), for which the method is very accurate, as checked below.
In turn, each eigenvector
{\bf V}_{k}
of the matrix
{\bf M}_{p}
determines the coefficients of the expansion of
v_{k}^{(p)}
on the basis functions:

v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N_{e}}{\bf V}_{k,i}~\phi_{i+N_{i}}(\boldsymbol{x}).

(26)

As
\phi_{i}
are chosen to be the hat functions, one simply has
v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j+N_{i}})={\bf V}_{k,j}
at the nodes
\boldsymbol{x}_{j+N_{i}}
of the boundary. As eigenvectors
\bf V_{k}
and thus the eigenfunctions
v_{k}^{(p)}
are defined up to a multiplicative factor, we explicitly renormalize them to ensure the unit
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
norm, i.e.
\int_{\partial\Omega}|v_{k}^{(p)}|^{2}=1
.
The Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{k}^{(p)}
are obtained by replacing
F
by
v_{k}^{(p)}
in
eq.

20
.

The flexibility of the finite element method allows for various extensions. For instance, one can solve the mixed Steklov problem when the boundary
\partial\Omega
is composed of two disjoint parts:
\partial\Omega=\partial\Omega_{1}\cup\partial\Omega_{2}
. The Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator then acts as

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{p}:H^{1/2}(\partial\Omega_{1})

\displaystyle\rightarrow H^{-1/2}(\partial\Omega_{1})

(27)

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\mapsto\left.\left(\partial_{n}u\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega_{1}},

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}(p-\Delta)u(\boldsymbol{x})&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\
u(\boldsymbol{x})&=f(\boldsymbol{x})&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{1}),\\
u(\boldsymbol{x})&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{2}),\end{array}\right.

or

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}(p-\Delta)u(\boldsymbol{x})&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\
u(\boldsymbol{x})&=f(\boldsymbol{x})&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{1}),\\
\partial_{n}u(\boldsymbol{x})&=0&\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega_{2}).\end{array}\right.

In other words, one imposes an additional Dirichlet or Neumann boundary condition on
\partial\Omega_{2}
and defines the operator
\mathcal{M}_{p}
to act on functions on
\partial\Omega_{1}
.
In the context of diffusion-controlled reactions, mixed boundary conditions allow one to describe various processes in which the diffusing particle can leave
the confining domain through an escape region or be destroyed on it, before reaching the target region
[
38
]
. The implementation of the Dirichlet boundary condition to our numerical method is straightforward: one just needs to truncate the second term in
eq.

15
to the indices corresponding to the nodes on
\partial\Omega_{1}
. In this way, one incorporates the Dirichlet boundary condition
u=f
on
\partial\Omega_{1}
, while letting the homogeneous Dirichlet boundary condition
u=0
on
\partial\Omega_{2}
. The inclusion of the Neumann boundary condition is as well simple: one extends the first term in
eq.

15
to the indices corresponding to the nodes on
\partial\Omega_{2}
, i.e. the first term then represents
u
inside
\Omega
and
u
on the reflecting boundary
\partial\Omega_{2}
, while the second term (reduced to the indices corresponding to the nodes on
\partial\Omega_{1}
) encodes the Dirichlet boundary condition
u=f
on
\partial\Omega_{1}
. An extension of this method to three-dimensional domains is also straightforward.

2.2
Green’s function method

The second method was inspired by the spectral decompositions of the Green’s functions
[
10
]
. Let us introduce the Green’s function
\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
satisfying

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}(p-\Delta)\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})&=&\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\
-\partial_{n}\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})&=&q\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega),\end{array}\right.

(28)

with a constant
0\leq q\leq\infty
, and
\delta(\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
being the Dirac distribution.
Its expansion on the Steklov eigenbasis reads
[
10
]
:

\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\tilde{G}_{\infty}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})+\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})]^{*}V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\mu_{k}^{(p)}+q},

(29)

which is also possible to write as

\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\tilde{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})]^{*}V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})}{(\mu_{k}^{(p)}/q+1)\mu_{k}^{(p)}},

(30)

where asterisk denotes the complex conjugate. Setting
q=0
and restricting the points
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
onto the
boundary
\partial\Omega
in
eq.

29
, one gets the integral kernel of the inverse of the
Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator:

\tilde{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})]^{*}v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\mu_{k}^{(p)}}\quad(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\partial\Omega).

(31)

However, the Green’s function
\tilde{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
exhibits a
singularity at
\boldsymbol{x}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
that would require additional regularization and
may enhance numerical errors. It is therefore convenient to remove this
singularity by using
eq.

30
.
Restricting both
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
to the boundary, we consider the integral kernel:

\tilde{g}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\frac{\tilde{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})-\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})}{q}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{[v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})]^{*}v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})}{\mu_{k}^{(p)}(\mu_{k}^{(p)}+q)}\quad(\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\Omega).

One gets then

\int_{\partial\Omega}\tilde{g}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}=\eta_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}),

(32)

where the eigenvalues
\eta_{k}^{(p)}
of this integral operator are related to

\mu_{k}^{(p)}
as

\mu_{k}^{(p)}=\sqrt{1/\eta_{k}^{(p)}+q^{2}/4}-q/2.

(33)

The discretization of the integral at boundary points
\boldsymbol{x}_{i}
yields a
system of linear equations

\sum\limits_{j}\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,\tilde{g}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})\,v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j})=\eta_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{i}),

(34)

where
\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
are the areas of the surface elements around
\boldsymbol{x}_{j}
. In
other words, one needs to diagonalize the matrix
G_{ij}=\delta\boldsymbol{x}_{j}\,\tilde{g}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x}_{j},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{i})
.

Each Green’s function can be found either directly (e.g., by a finite element method), or from
its (truncated) spectral expansion

\tilde{G}_{q}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{[u_{k}^{(q)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})]^{*}u_{k}^{(q)}(\boldsymbol{x})}{p+\lambda_{k}^{(q)}},

(35)

where
\lambda_{k}^{(q)}
and
u_{k}^{(q)}
are the eigenvalues and the
L^{2}(\Omega)
-normalized eigenfunctions of the Laplace operator:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{r l l}-\Delta u_{k}^{(q)}=\lambda_{k}^{(q)}u_{k}^{(q)}\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega),\\
-\partial_{n}u_{k}^{(q)}=qu_{k}^{(q)}\quad(\boldsymbol{x}\in\partial\Omega).\end{array}\right.

(36)

We stress that the use of the spectral expansion
eq.

35
is not optimal from the numerical point of view. In fact, the construction of Laplacian eigenfunctions and eigenvalues is a time-consuming procedure, while the truncation of the infinite series in
eq.

35
to a finite number of terms can be the major source of numerical errors. We used this expansion as a straightforward way to access the Green’s function for validation purposes, but more efficient numerical tools can be designed for this task. In turn, the advantage of this method is that the Laplacian
eigenfunctions need to be computed only once for a given domain.

Once
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
and
v_{k}^{(p)}
are found, one can also compute the extension
V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})
by using the Green’s
function and the Neumann boundary condition
\partial_{n}V_{k}^{(p)}=\mu_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}
, so that

V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=\int_{\partial\Omega}\tilde{G}_{0}(\boldsymbol{x},p|\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\,\mu_{k}^{(p)}v_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})d\boldsymbol{x}.

(37)

Note that this relation is not applicable for
p=0
and
k=0
, for
which
\mu_{0}^{(0)}=0
and
V_{0}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=1/\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}
are already known.

\alpha_{0}^{(0)}

. However, when an effective angle exceeds
\pi

3.5
Conjecture for polygonal domains

The above iterative process can be generalized to arbitrary polygonal domains.
Let
a_{0}=\{\alpha_{0},\ldots,\alpha_{N-1}\}

be a sequence of all angles of a polygonal domain.
The coefficient
c_{0}
is set to be
c_{0}=\sin(\min\{\pi,a_{0}\}/2)
,
i.e., it
is determined by the smallest angle of the domain, say,
\alpha_{i}
.
After
that, the
i
-th element of the sequence
a_{0}
is increased by

2\alpha_{i}

to get the updated sequence
a_{1}
that determines the next coefficient:

c_{1}=\sin(\min\{\pi,a_{1}\}/2)
. At step
k
, one sets
c_{k}=\sin(\min\{\pi,a_{k}\}/2)
,
i.e., the smallest angle in the already constructed sequence
a_{k}
. If
this
angle stands on the position
j
, the
j
-th element of this sequence is
increased by
2\alpha_{j}
to produce a new sequence
a_{k+1}
, and so on. Note that if there are several equal minima in the sequence of effective angles, one choose the one, for which the increment
2\alpha_{i}
is the largest (and if the increments are identical, one chooses any of them).
It is clear that, after a number of steps, all angles in the sequence
will exceed
\pi
, so that all the remaining coefficients
c_{k}
become equal

1
. In the case of a regular polygon with
N
sides and equal angles
\alpha
, the conjecture simply implies that the first
N
coefficients are
c_{k}=\sin(\alpha/2)
, while the other
c_{k}=1
, in agreement with our earlier numerical results.

Coefficients of spectral expansions

In this section, we investigate how the coefficients
A_{k}^{(p)}
defined by
eq.

11
depend on
p
and
k
for various planar domains.
As mentioned in
section

1
,
A_{k}^{(0)}=\delta_{k,0}
due to orthogonality of eigenfunctions
v_{k}^{(0)}
to
v_{0}^{(0)}=1/\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}
. However, for
p>0
, the eigenfunction
v_{0}^{(p)}
is in general not constant so that
A_{k}^{(p)}
may be non zero for
k>0
. In the case of a disk, the rotational symmetry implies that
v_{k}^{(p)}
do not depend on
p
, so that
A_{k}^{(p)}=\delta_{k,0}
for any
p\geq 0
. As a consequence, spectral expansions involving
A_{k}^{(p)}
as coefficients are reduced to a single term
[
10
]
. One may wonder how the domain shape can alter this behavior.

One can see the coefficient
A_{k}^{(p)}
as the coefficients in the expansion of a constant function
1/\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}
over the orthogonal basis
\{v_{k}^{(p)}\}
of
L^{2}(\partial\Omega)
. As a consequence, one has

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}|A_{k}^{(p)}|^{2}=1.

(43)

In particular,
|A_{k}^{(p)}|^{2}
can be interpreted as relative weights of different eigenfunctions
v_{k}^{(p)}
in some spectral expansions. The convergence of this series implies that
|A_{k}^{(p)}|
decreases with
k
, i.e.

\lim\limits_{k\to\infty}A_{k}^{(p)}=0.

(44)

We inspect the relative contributions of
A_{k}^{(p)}
for various domains.

5
Asymptotic behavior of Steklov eigenfunctions

In this section, we analyze the decay of the Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{k}^{(p)}
away from the boundary
[
12
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
]
. In particular, Polterovich, Sher and Toth
[
15
]
proved that for any bounded domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
with a real-analytic boundary
\partial\Omega
, there exist positive constants
\eta>0
and
B>0
depending only on the geometry of
\Omega
, such that all eigenfunctions
V_{k}^{(0)}
satisfy

\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}~|V_{k}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq B\exp(-\eta~\mu_{k}^{(0)}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|),

(45)

where
|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|
is the Euclidean distance between
\boldsymbol{x}
and the boundary
\partial\Omega
and we included the prefactor
\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}
to ensure that the left-hand side is dimensionless. In other words, each Steklov eigenfunction
V_{k}^{(0)}
has an upper bound, which decays exponentially fast away from the boundary, and its decay rate is proportional to the corresponding eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
. As
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
grows with
k
up to infinity, the exponential decay becomes faster and more and more restrictive. In contrast, the upper bound
eq.

45
is in general not much informative for any finite
k
: as
V_{k}^{(0)}
is an analytic function on a bounded domain, one can always choose a large enough
B
or small enough
\eta
to fulfill the inequality
eq.

45
. In other words, without restricting estimates on
\eta
and
B
, the upper bound (
45
) does not tell much on the behavior of a given Steklov eigenfunction.
The upper bound (
45
) was further generalized to higher dimension and
p\neq 0
by Helffer and Kachmar
[
18
]
. They proved that for any bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\geq 2
), with a real-analytic boundary
\partial\Omega
, any eigenfunction
V_{k}^{(p)}
with sufficiently high eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
(i.e. sufficiently high
k
) has an upper bound that decays exponentially fast away from the boundary. In fact, for any
p>-\lambda_{0}^{D}
(where
\lambda_{0}^{D}
is the smallest eigenvalue of the Dirichlet Laplacian in
\Omega
) there exist constants
B>0
,
\eta>0
,
\epsilon>0
and
k_{0}
such that

\forall k>k_{0},~\forall\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega,\quad|\sqrt{\partial\Omega}||V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq B\left(\mu_{k}^{(p)}\right)^{\frac{d}{2}-\frac{1}{4}}\exp\left(-\eta~\mu_{k}^{(p)}\min\{\epsilon,|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\}\right),

(46)

where we included again the prefactor
\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}
.
Helffer and Kachmar also questioned whether the assumption of real-analytic boundary could be relaxed.

Being inspired by these fascinating results, we aim to check numerically to which extend the upper bounds
eqs.

45
and
46
determine the decay of any given Steklov eigenfunction
V_{k}^{(p)}
(with finite
k
). One may wonder whether the exponential decay holds in the whole domain, and whether the eigenvalue
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
is the decay rate, i.e. whether
\eta
is close to 1. To gain intuitive insights onto this behavior, one can first look at the explicit
eq.

38
for the Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{k}^{(p)}
for the disk of radius
R
. In the limit
p\to 0
one has for any
k=1,2,3,\ldots

\displaystyle|V_{2k}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi R}}\left(\frac{|\boldsymbol{x}|}{R}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi R}}\exp\left[k\ln\left(\frac{R-|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|}{R}\right)\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi R}}\exp\left(-\frac{k}{R}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\right)=\frac{\exp\left(-\mu_{2k}^{(0)}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\right)}{\sqrt{\pi R}},

(47)

where we used that
\mu_{2k}^{(0)}=k/R
; and the same applies for
V_{2k+1}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})
.
One sees how
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
controls the exponential decay of
V_{k}^{(0)}
away from the boundary, with
\eta=1
and
B=1/\sqrt{\pi R}
. In the opposite limit
p\to\infty
, the asymptotic behavior of the modified Bessel functions
I_{k}(z)
implies for
r\sqrt{p}\gg 1
:

\displaystyle|V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\lesssim\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi R}}\frac{\sqrt{R}}{\sqrt{r}}\exp\left(-\sqrt{p}(R-r)\right)

\displaystyle\simeq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi R}}\frac{\sqrt{R}}{\sqrt{r}}\exp\left(-\mu_{k}^{(p)}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\right)

(48)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{p^{1/4}}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\left(-\mu_{k}^{(p)}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\right),

where we used the asymptotic relation (
9
).
In turn, if
r
is so small that
r\sqrt{p}\ll 1
(but still
R\sqrt{p}\gg 1
) one uses

I_{k}(r\sqrt{p})\simeq\cfrac{(r\sqrt{p})^{k}}{k!}
to get

|V_{2k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x})|\ll\frac{p^{1/4}}{k!}\exp\left(-\sqrt{p}R\right)\lesssim\frac{p^{1/4}}{k!}\exp\left(-\mu_{2k}^{(p)}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\right).

(49)

In both limits, we observe an exponential decay of
V_{k}^{(p)}
away from the boundary, which is controlled by
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
, and
\eta
is close to
1
(even though the above upper bounds suggest
\eta=1
, our derivation involved some asymptotic relations, which may require having
\eta
slightly smaller than 1). In Appendix
7.2
, we analyze the explicit representation of the Steklov eigenfunctions for a rectangle and come to the same conclusion for
p=0
. In sharp contrast to the disk, the boundary of the rectangle is not real-analytic due to the corners. Nevertheless, the upper bound
eq.

45
holds and the eigenfunctions exhibit exponential decay controlled by
\mu_{k}^{(0)}
, with
\eta
close to 1.

Let us give another argument in favor of the particular choice
\eta=1
. Let
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
be a boundary point, and
\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta}=\boldsymbol{x}_{0}-\delta n_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}\in\Omega
be a bulk point at small distance
\delta
from
\partial\Omega
, where
n_{\boldsymbol{x}_{0}}
is the normal unit vector to
\partial\Omega
at
\boldsymbol{x}_{0}
oriented outward the domain. If we assume that
V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})>0
, then the Steklov boundary condition can be written as

\partial_{n}\ln\left(V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right)=\mu_{k}^{(p)}\quad(\boldsymbol{x}_{0}\in\partial\Omega)

(50)

(if
V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})<0
, one can replace
V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
by
-V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})
that yields

\partial_{n}\left.\ln\left(|V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})|\right)\right|_{\partial\Omega}=\mu_{k}^{(p)}
, with no changes in the argument; in turn, we ignore here the specific points at which
V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})=0
). Since
V_{k}^{(p)}
is analytic in
\Omega
and
V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})>0
, it is also positive in a small vicinity of the boundary, so that

\ln\left(V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta})\right)=\ln\left(V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\right)-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\delta+O(\delta^{2}),

(51)

to be consistent with
eq.

50
. As a consequence, the Steklov eigenfunction
V_{k}^{(p)}
exhibits an exponential decay near the boundary

V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta})\approx V_{k}^{(p)}(\boldsymbol{x}_{0})\exp(-\mu_{k}^{(p)}\delta),

(52)

where
\delta=|\boldsymbol{x}_{\delta}-\partial\Omega|
is the distance to the boundary. One sees that the value
\eta=1
naturally comes from the Steklov condition. The fundamental question is whether the exponential behavior (
52
) holds
approximately
far from the boundary, when the above argument is not applicable.

We address this question numerically for
p=0
and check the exponential decay of
V_{k}^{(0)}
away from the boundary for various planar domains, even when
\partial\Omega
is not real-analytic. Most importantly, we discuss whether
\eta
is close to 1 in general. The top row of
fig.

B_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})=\left|\sqrt{|\partial\Omega|}~V_{k}^{(0)}(\boldsymbol{x})\exp\left(\mu_{k}^{(0)}|\boldsymbol{x}-\partial\Omega|\right)\right|,

(53)

as if we explicitly set
\eta=1
in
eq.

6
Discussion and conclusion

In this paper, we numerically investigated the spectral properties of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator
\mathcal{M}_{p}
and their dependence on the parameter
p
and the domain geometry. We considered various shapes, including ellipses, triangles, rectangles, regular polygons and prefractal Koch snowflakes.

Our first contribution concerned the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
.
For all considered shapes, we confirmed the validity of the asymptotic relations
eqs.

40
and
10
for large and small
p
. In the limit
p\to\infty
, the coefficients
c_{k}
in
eq.

10
were known to be 1 for bounded domains with
\mathcal{C}^{1}
boundary. In turn, their values for polygonal domains were unknown. We conjectured that as
p
increases, first Steklov eigenfunctions are getting localized near the corners of a polygonal domain, and suggested an iterative procedure to obtain the coefficients
c_{k}
. This conjectural relation was numerically validated on several domains. We are unaware of earlier studies on the coefficients
c_{k}
for the asymptotic behavior of the eigenvalues
\mu_{k}^{(p)}
of the Dirichlet-to-Neumann operator. However, additional insights can be gained from the related problem of the Robin Laplacian defined in
eq.

36
.
In fact, Lacey
et al.
considered the asymptotic behavior of the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}^{(q)}
of the Robin Laplacian in the limit
q\to-\infty
, and found that
[
39
]
:

\lambda_{0}^{(q)}\simeq-q^{2}/\sin^{2}(\alpha/2)

(60)

in a corner with angle
\alpha<\pi
.
This result was generalized by Levitin and Parnovski
[
40
]
who proved
eq.

60
for a polygonal domain with angles

\alpha_{1},…\alpha_{n}
, such that
0<\alpha_{i}<\pi
, and
\alpha=\min\{\alpha_{i}\}
.
Then, Khalile
[
41
]
extended this result for the first
n
eigenvalues of
the Robin Laplacian for polygons with angles between
\pi/3
and
\pi
.
Finally, Khalile and Pankrashkin
[
42
]
considered a similar problem for an
infinite sector and showed the asymptotic behavior
\lambda_{n}^{(q)}\simeq-q^{2}/((2n+1)\alpha/2)^{2}
in the limit
\alpha\to 0
. The duality between the Robin problem
and the Steklov problem allows one to invert
eq.

60
to get
\mu_{0}^{(p)}\simeq\sin(\alpha/2)\sqrt{p}
as
p\to\infty
for a polygon. This asymptotic result, which is a direct consequence of
[
40
]
, confirms our conjecture for the smallest eigenvalue
\mu_{0}^{(p)}
. In turn, our conjectural iterative procedure relates the asymptotic behavior of all eigenvalues to the angles of a polygonal domain. Its rigorous demonstration presents an intersting open problem. Perhaps, the most challeging part is to prove that our procedure yields all coefficients
c_{k}<1
.

The second result concerned some spectral expansions that appear in the theory of diffusion-controlled reactions
[
10
]
. For symmetric domains such as ellipses, rectangles and even prefactal Koch snowflakes, we numerically observed that many coefficients
A_{k}^{(p)}
vanish for a wide range of
k
and
p
. We argued that this behavior is a consequence of domain symmetries. In turn, this property breaks for generic domains such as an arbitrary triangle.
On one hand, the cancellation of many coefficients
A_{k}^{(p)}
due to domain symmetries can considerably simplify spectral expansions and the analysis of diffusion-controlled reactions in such domains. Moreover, these symmetries can potentially be used to design domains with specific properties. On the other hand, the use of symmetric domains as examples in theoretical and numerical studies may lead to erroneous conjectures and conclusions. For instance, the cancellation of coefficients
A_{k}^{(p)}
with
k>0
for the disk is a very specific consequence of its rotational symmetry, which fails for less symmetric domains. In particular, many coefficients
A_{k}^{(p)}
contribute to the spectral expansion in the case of a generic triangle. It is therefore an open question how many terms are relevant and how their number depends on domain shape. More formally, it is known from the general arguments that
A_{k}^{(p)}\to 0
as
k\to\infty
, but the speed of decay remains unknown. This question is practically important because a rapid decay of
A_{k}^{(p)}
may allow truncating some spectral expansions to get useful approximations with a limited number of contributing eigenmodes.

Last, we investigated the localization of Steklov eigenfunctions
V_{k}^{(p)}
on the boundary in the presence of corners. Even if the boundary is polygonal and thus is not real-analytic,
fig.

8
illustrated the exponential decay of
|V_{k}^{(0)}|
away from the boundary for a broad range of indices
k
. These numerical examples could motivate further mathematical investigations in this direction. However, we also observed that there exist domains and indices
k
, for which deviations from the exponential decay, characterized by
B_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
and
U_{k}^{(p)}(\delta)
, become large, suggesting that the hypothesis
\eta\approx 1
in
eq.

45
might not be relevant for all
k
and in the whole domain. In particular, we noticed that only a slight perturbation of the disk can result in high values of
B_{k}(\boldsymbol{x})
located in the center of the domain, indicating that the related eigenfunctions decrease slower inside the domain than near the boundary. A more systematic study of this behavior through the estimation of the constants
B
and
\eta
in
eq.

45
and their relation with the domain geometry present an interesting perspective to this work.
