Diagonal F-splitting and symbolic powers of ideals
Daniel Smolkin

Abstract




Abstract.
Let
J
be any ideal in a strongly
F
-regular, diagonally
F
-split ring
R
essentially of finite type over an
F
-finite field. We show that
J^{s+t}\subseteq\tau(J^{s-\varepsilon})\tau(J^{t-\varepsilon})
for all
s,t,\varepsilon>0
for which the formula makes sense. We use this to show a number of novel containments between symbolic and ordinary powers of prime ideals in this setting, which includes all determinantal rings and a large class of toric rings in positive characteristic. In particular, we show that
\mathfrak p^{(2hn)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{n}
for all prime ideals
\mathfrak p
of height
h
in such rings.

Keywords.
Test ideals, symbolic powers, F-singularities, diagonal F-splitting, F-splitting, F-regularity, determinantal variety, Schubert variety

1. Introduction

Given an ideal
I
in a ring
R
, we define the
n^{\mathrm{th}}

symbolic power
of
I
to be

I^{(n)}=\bigcapop\displaylimits_{\mathfrak p\in\ass_{R}(R/I)}I^{n}R_{\mathfrak p}\cap R.

If
I
is radical, then one can think of
I^{(n)}
as roughly being the ideal of elements in
R
vanishing to “order at least
n
” along the variety
V(I)
. For instance, if
I
is a divisorial ideal
I=R(D)
, then
I^{(n)}=R(nD)
; see also
[
DDS+18
, Proposition 2.14]
. One quickly sees from the definitions that
I^{n}
is always contained in
I^{(n)}
, but finding a more precise relationship between ordinary and symbolic powers of ideals is a subtle matter; see
[
DDS+18
,
GS21
]
for excellent overviews of this problem and its history.

Here we address the following question posed in
[
HKV09
]
.

Question 1.1
.

Let
(R,\mathfrak m)
be complete local domain. Does there exist an integer
C\geq 1
such that
\mathfrak p^{(Cn)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{n}
for all
\mathfrak p\in\Spec R
and all integers
n\geq 1
?

So far we know this is true, under mild assumptions, for isolated singularities (see
[
HKV09
]
), Segre products of polynomial rings (see
[
CRS20
]
), and certain Hibi rings (see
[
PST18
]
). We also know that this property descends under finite extensions of rings (see
[
HKV15
]
). See also Walker’s work on monomial ideals and height 1 primes in toric rings
[
Wal18
]
.

Rings with an affirmative answer to this question are said to have the
uniform symbolic topology property
, or USTP for short. This is because one can use the symbolic powers of an ideal
\mathfrak p
to define a topology on
R
, which is generated by cosets
x+\mathfrak p^{(n)}
for
x\in R
and
n\geq 1
. Compare this to the
\mathfrak p
-adic topology, which is generated by the cosets
x+\mathfrak p^{n}
. Schenzel showed that these two topologies are equivalent, meaning

\forall a\in\mathbb{N}\colon\quad\exists b\in\mathbb{N}\colon\mathfrak p^{(b)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{a}

whenever
R
is a Noetherian domain; see
[
Sch85
]
. Swanson later showed that, in this case, one can find a number
C(\mathfrak p)
, depending on
\mathfrak p
, where
\mathfrak p^{(C(\mathfrak p)n)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{n}
for all
n
; see
[
Swa00
]
. In this sense, the two topologies are said to be
linearly equivalent
. Ein, Lazarsfeld, and Smith kicked off the study of USTP when they showed that one can find a
uniform

C
such that
\mathfrak p^{(Cn)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{n}
for all
\mathfrak p\in\Spec R
and all
n\geq 1
whenever
R
is a regular finitely generated
\mathbb{C}
-algebra; see
[
ELS01
]
. Analogous results were later shown in positive characteristic by Hochster–Huneke
[
HH02
]
and in mixed characteristic by Ma–Schwede
[
MS18
]
.

Test ideals of pairs, and their characteristic zero/mixed characteristic analogs, have been an important tool in the study of USTP; see
[
ELS01
,
HY03
,
Tak06
,
CRS20
,
MS18
,
Mur21
]
. Each of those proofs relies on some version of the “subadditivity formula,”

\tau(\mathfrak a^{s}\mathfrak b^{t})\subseteq\tau(\mathfrak a^{s})\tau(\mathfrak b^{t}).

In regular rings, this formula holds as written, but in other situations one must account for its failure. In
[
Tak06
]
, Takagi multiplies by the Jacobian ideal. In
[
CRS20
]
, we use so-called “diagonal Cartier algebras” and show that a ring has USTP if it is
diagonally
F
-regular
. Diagonal
F
-regularity is a strengthening of
diagonal
F
-splitting
(and of strong
F
-regularity), analogously to the way strong
F
-regularity is a strengthening of
F
-splitting. So far, however, interesting examples of diagonally
F
-regular rings have been quite difficult to find.

In this work, we prove that if a ring is only strongly
F
-regular and diagonally
F
-split, then it satisfies a weaker form of subadditivity which is enough for USTP. Namely, we show the following.

Theorem A
.

Let
\mathcal{k}
be an
F
-finite field of positive characteristic, and let
R
be a strongly
F
-regular, diagonally
F
-split
\mathcal{k}
-algebra essentially of finite type. Let
\mathfrak a\subseteq R
be an ideal, and let
s
and
t
be positive real numbers with
s+t\in\mathbb{Z}
. Then we have

\mathfrak a^{s+t}\subseteq\tau(\mathfrak a^{s-\varepsilon})\tau(\mathfrak a^{t-\varepsilon})

for all
\varepsilon\in(0,\min\{s,t\}]
.

From this, we follow a line of argument similar to that used in
[
CRS20
]
to get various bounds on symbolic powers; see
Theorem

4.4
for the strongest statement. In particular, we have the following.

Theorem B
.

In the same setting as Theorem A, let
\mathfrak p\subseteq R
be a prime ideal and
h=\height\mathfrak p
. Then
\mathfrak p^{(2hn)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{n}
for all
n>0
.

Observe that Theorem B implies
\mathfrak p^{(2dn)}\subseteq\mathfrak p^{n}
for all
\mathfrak p
and all
n
, where
d
is the dimension of
R
.

By work of Ramanathan
[
Ram87
]
and Lauritzen–Raben-Pedersen–Thomsen
[
LRPT06
]
, the hypotheses of these theorems are satisfied by any affine subset of a Schubert subvariety of
G/Q
, where
G
is a connected and simply connected linear algebraic group over an algebraically closed field of positive characteristic, and
Q
is a parabolic subgroup of
G
containing a Borel subgroup
B
. In particular, these theorems hold in any determinantal ring
\mathcal{k}[X_{m\times n}]/I_{t}
, where
X_{m\times n}
is an
m\times n
matrix of indeterminates, with
m\leq n
, and
I_{t}
is the ideal of size
t
minors of
X
, where
t\leq m
. (We show that
\mathcal{k}
need not be algebraically closed here in
Corollary

5.4
.) These theorems also hold in diagonally
F
-split toric rings, which were characterized in
[
CHP+18
]
. This includes, in particular, all Hibi rings (
cf.

[
PST18
, Theorem 3.7]
).

By standard “reduction mod
p
” techniques, the symbolic powers containment of Theorem B holds in the corresponding determinantal and toric rings over fields of characteristic zero; see
[
HH99
, Section 2]
(see also
[
CRS20
, Section 6]
).

2. Background on test ideals

We begin with some background on positive-characteristic commutative algebra. For a more in-depth treatment on this material, see the surveys
[
SZ15
]
and
[
ST12
]
, or the recent lecture notes
[
Hoc22
]
.

Let
R
be a ring of characteristic
p
, where
p
is prime. Then the Frobenius map,

F\colon R\longrightarrow R,\quad x\longmapsto x^{p},

is a ring homomorphism. So are its iterates
F^{e}(x)\coloneqq x^{p^{e}}
for all
e\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. We write
F^{e}_{*}
to denote restriction of scalars along
F^{e}
. More precisely,
F^{e}_{*}
is a functor from
R
-modules to
R
-modules. For any
R
-module
M
, we have
F^{e}_{*}M=\{F^{e}_{*}m\mid m\in M\}
, where addition and scalar multiplication are defined by
F^{e}_{*}m+F^{e}_{*}n=F^{e}_{*}(m+n)
and
rF^{e}_{*}m=F^{e}_{*}(r^{p^{e}}m)
for all
r\in R
and
m,n\in M
. For any
R
-linear map
\varphi\colon M\to N
, the map
F^{e}_{*}\varphi\colon F^{e}_{*}M\to F^{e}_{*}N
is given by
(F^{e}_{*}\varphi)(F^{e}_{*}m)=F^{e}_{*}(\varphi(m))
. If
R
happens to be a domain, then we can fix an algebraic closure
K
of
\operatorname{frac}(R)
, and each element of
R
will have a (unique!)
(p^{e})^{\mathrm{th}}
root in
K
. In this case,
F^{e}_{*}R
is nothing but
R^{1/p^{e}}
, the set of these
(p^{e})^{\mathrm{th}}
roots with the usual
R
-module structure. We say that
R
is
F
-finite
if
F^{e}_{*}R
is finitely generated over
R
for some (equivalently, all)
e>0
.

An
F
-splitting
of
R
is a map of
R
-modules
F^{e}_{*}R\to R
sending
F^{e}_{*}1
to
1
. Such a map splits the natural
R
-module map
R\to F^{e}_{*}R
given by
r\mapsto rF^{e}_{*}1=F^{e}_{*}r^{p^{e}}
—in this sense, it splits the Frobenius map. We say
R
is
F
-split
if it admits an
F
-splitting
F^{e}_{*}R\to R
for some (equivalently, all)
e>0
. Note that this is equivalent to the existence of an
R
-linear surjection
F^{e}_{*}R\twoheadrightarrow R
.
F
-split rings are automatically reduced.

Global assumptions

For the rest of this section,
R
denotes an
F
-finite, reduced, and Noetherian ring of characteristic
p>0
.

2.1. Test ideals and strong
\boldsymbol{F}
-regularity

Let
R^{\circ}
be the set of elements not in any minimal prime of
R
. Two important notions from Hochster and Huneke’s celebrated theory of tight closure are
big test elements
and
big test ideals
. A big test element is defined to be any
c\in R^{\circ}
such that, for all
d\in R^{\circ}
, there exist
e>0
and
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)
with
\varphi(F^{e}_{*}d)=c
. It is far from obvious, but big test elements exist in any
F
-finite, reduced, Noetherian ring, and the ideal generated by all big test elements of
R
is called the
big test ideal
of
R
, denoted by
\tau(R)
. It is given by

\tau(R)\coloneqq\sumop\displaylimits_{e>0}\sumop\displaylimits_{\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)}\varphi\left(F^{e}_{*}c\right),

where
c\in R^{\circ}
is any test element. One of the more “magic,” and extremely useful, aspects of
F
-singularity theory is that this ideal is independent of the choice of
c
(though this is a quick consequence of the definitions; perhaps the real magic is that test elements exist at all). We say
R
is
strongly
F
-regular
if
\tau(R)=R
, or equivalently if
1
is a big test element of
R
. Strong
F
-regularity is a local condition, and strongly
F
-regular local rings are normal Cohen–Macaulay domains. If
S
is a faithfully flat
R
-algebra and
S
is strongly
F
-regular, then so is
R
; see
[
Hoc22
, Lecture 10]
.

The reader should be warned that the big test ideal is sometimes denoted by
\tilde{\tau}(R)
in the literature, such as in
[
HT04
]
, whereas the notation
\tau(R)
is reserved for the “ordinary” test ideal. These two ideals are conjectured to be the same, however. In this paper we will only work with big test ideals and big test elements. Thus, we will follow earlier authors in omitting the qualifier “big” throughout.

2.2. Test ideals of pairs

Test ideals are, in a precise sense, the positive-characteristic analog of multiplier ideals from birational geometry. However, birational geometers have long worked with multiplier ideals not just of schemes
X
, but of pairs
(X,Z)
, where
Z
is a formal
\mathbb{Q}
-linear combination of subschemes of
X
. This inspired Hara and Yoshida to define the test ideals of pairs: if
\mathfrak a
is an ideal of
R
not contained in any minimal prime, and
t\geq 0
is a real number, we say that
c\in R^{\circ}
is a
test element of the pair

(R,\mathfrak a^{t})
if, for all
d\in R^{\circ}
, there exist some
e>0
and some
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)
with

c\in\varphi\left(F^{e}_{*}d\mathfrak a^{\lceil t(p^{e}-1)\rceil}\right).

Then we can define the
test ideal of the pair

(R,\mathfrak a^{t})
to be

\tau(R,\mathfrak a^{t})=\sumop\displaylimits_{e>0}\sumop\displaylimits_{\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)}\varphi\left(F^{e}_{*}c\mathfrak a^{\lceil t(p^{e}-1)\rceil}\right),

where
c
is any test element of the pair
(R,\mathfrak a^{t})
. We will denote the test ideals by
\tau(\mathfrak a^{t})
when the ambient ring
R
is clear from context.

These definitions can certainly feel quite daunting and unmotivated to the uninitiated. Fortunately, all of the facts we will need about test ideals can be summarized as follows.

Proposition 2.1

(Properties of test ideals)
.

Let
\mathfrak a
be an ideal not contained in any minimal prime of
R
, and let
t\geq 0
. Then there exists a test element of the pair
(R,\mathfrak a^{t})
; see
[
HT04
, Lemma 2.1]
,
cf.

[
Sch11
, Proposition 3.21]
. Further, we have the following:

(a)

If
W\subseteq R
is any multiplicative set, then
W^{-1}\tau(R,\mathfrak a^{t})=\tau(W^{-1}R,(W^{-1}\mathfrak a)^{t})
; see
[
HT04
, Proposition 3.1]
.

(b)

For all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
\tau((\mathfrak a^{n})^{t})=\tau(\mathfrak a^{nt})
; see
[
HY03
, Remark 6.2]
.

(c)

If
\mathfrak a
has a reduction generated by
r
elements, then
\tau(\mathfrak a^{t+n})=\mathfrak a^{n}\tau(\mathfrak a^{t})
for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and all
t\geq r-1
; see
[
HT04
, Theorem 4.2]
.

(d)

If
c\in R
is a big test element and
\varphi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)
, then
\varphi(F^{e}_{*}c\mathfrak a^{\lceil tp^{e}\rceil})\subseteq\tau(\mathfrak a^{t})
; see
[
HT04
, Lemma 2.1]
, cf.
[
BSTZ10
, Proposition 3.3(5)]
.

Remark
.

Recall that an ideal
J\subseteq I
is called a
reduction
of
I
if
JI^{n}=I^{n+1}
for all
n
sufficiently large. In Noetherian rings, this is equivalent to saying
\overline{J}=\overline{I}
, where
\overline{I}
denotes the integral closure of
I
. See
[
SH06
, Chapter 8]

Proof.

We prove part (
d
), for the reader’s convenience. Let
d
be a test element of the pair
(R,\mathfrak a^{t})
. Then, since
c
is a big test element of
R
, there exists a
\psi\colon F^{e_{0}}_{*}R\to R
with
\psi(F^{e_{0}}_{*}d)=c
. It follows that

\varphi(F^{e}_{*}c\mathfrak a^{\lceil tp^{e}\rceil})=\varphi\left(F^{e}_{*}\mathfrak a^{\lceil tp^{e}\rceil}\psi(F^{e_{0}}_{*}d)\right)\subseteq\varphi\left(F^{e}_{*}\psi(F^{e_{0}}_{*}\mathfrak a^{\lceil tp^{e}\rceil p^{e_{0}}}d)\right).

Note that
\lceil tp^{e}\rceil p^{e_{0}}
is an integer and that

\lceil tp^{e}\rceil p^{e_{0}}\geq tp^{e}p^{e_{0}}>t\left(p^{e+e_{0}}-1\right).

It follows that
\lceil tp^{e}\rceil p^{e_{0}}\geq\lceil t(p^{e+e_{0}}-1)\rceil
. Finally, since the map

F^{e+e_{0}}_{*}R\longrightarrow R,\quad F^{e+e_{0}}_{*}x\longmapsto\varphi\left(F^{e}_{*}\psi(F^{e_{0}}_{*}x)\right)

is
R
-linear, we have the desired containment.
∎

Using Proposition
2.1
, we can prove the following containment, which is key in establishing the connection between test ideals and symbolic powers.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\mathfrak p\in\Spec R
be an ideal of height
h
with infinite residue field
Rp/\mathfrak pRp

(
for instance,
\mathfrak p
can be any nonmaximal prime ideal of
R
)
. Then

\tau((\mathfrak p^{(N)})^{t})\subseteq\mathfrak p^{({\left\lfloor Nt\right\rfloor}-h+1)}

for all integers
N>0
and all real numbers
t>0
such that
Nt\geq h-1
.

Proof.

We can check this containment locally at
\mathfrak p
. Localizing, we get

\tau(R,(\mathfrak p^{(N)})^{t})Rp=\tau(Rp,(\mathfrak p^{(N)}Rp)^{t})=\tau(Rp,(\mathfrak p^{N}Rp)^{t})=\tau(Rp,(\mathfrak pR_{\mathfrak p})^{Nt}).

As the residue field at
\mathfrak p
is infinite, we know that
\mathfrak pRp
has a reduction generated by
h
elements (see
[
SH06
, Theorem 8.3.7, Corollary 8.3.9]
). Since test ideals localize, it follows from
Proposition

2.1
, part (
c
), that

\tau(Rp,(\mathfrak pRp)^{Nt})\subseteq\tau(Rp,(\mathfrak pRp)^{{\left\lfloor Nt\right\rfloor}})=\mathfrak p^{{\left\lfloor Nt\right\rfloor}-h+1}\tau(Rp,(\mathfrak pRp)^{h-1})\subseteq\mathfrak p^{{\left\lfloor Nt\right\rfloor}-h+1}Rp,

as desired.
∎

2.3. Tensor products

Let
A
be a ring of characteristic
p
and
R
an
A
-algebra.
For all
R
-modules
M
and
N
, we have a natural surjection of
R\otimes_{A}R
-modules
\Theta\colon F^{e}_{*}M\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}N\twoheadrightarrow F^{e}_{*}(M\otimes_{A}N)
, just given by
F^{e}_{*}m\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}n\mapsto F^{e}_{*}(m\otimes_{A}n)
. The kernel of this map is the submodule

\left\langle F^{e}_{*}am\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}n-F^{e}_{*}m\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}an\mid a\in A,m\in M,n\in N\right\rangle\subseteq F^{e}_{*}M\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}N.

For instance, if
A
and
R
are domains, then
F^{e}_{*}(R\otimes_{A}R)=R^{1/p^{e}}\otimes_{A^{1/p^{e}}}R^{1/p^{e}}
, and this is a homomorphic image of
F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}R=R^{1/p^{e}}\otimes_{A}R^{1/p^{e}}
. If
A=A^{p}
(
i.e.
, the Frobenius map
A\to A
is surjective), then
\Theta
is an isomorphism. If
A
is an
F
-finite field and
R
is finitely generated over
A
, then maps
F^{e}_{*}(R\otimes_{A}R)\to R\otimes_{A}R
can be expressed in terms of maps
F^{e}_{*}R\to R
, as follows.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
\mathcal{k}
be an
F
-finite field and
R
a
\mathcal{k}
-algebra essentially of finite type. Then we have a natural inclusion

\mathrm{Hom}_{R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R}(F^{e}_{*}(R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R),R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R)\subseteq\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R).

Proof.

We have a natural surjection
F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}F^{e}_{*}R\twoheadrightarrow F^{e}_{*}(R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R)
, which induces an inclusion

\mathrm{Hom}_{R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R}(F^{e}_{*}(R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R),R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R)\subseteq\mathrm{Hom}_{R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R}(F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}F^{e}_{*}R,R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R).

By
[
Smo20
, Lemma 3.9]
, we have a natural isomorphism

\mathrm{Hom}_{R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R}(F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}F^{e}_{*}R,R\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}R)\cong\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)\otimes_{\mathcal{k}}\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R).\qed

3. Cartier algebras, compatible splittings, and diagonal
\boldsymbol{F}
-splitting

In this section we define diagonal
F
-splitting and the closely related diagonal Cartier algebra. Using the language of diagonal Cartier algebras allows us to state a base-change lemma for diagonal
F
-splitting (see
Corollary

5.4
). Without this base-change lemma, we would need to assume that
\mathcal{k}
is algebraically closed in
Theorem

5.1
.

3.1. Cartier algebras

Cartier algebras give us a unified framework for discussing test ideals in a much more general setting than just pairs
(R,\mathfrak a^{t})
; see
[
Sch11
]
. We review some of the basics of this theory.

Let
R
be a ring of characteristic
p
. For each
e
, define
\mathscr{C}_{e}(R)=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,R)
.
Then
\mathscr{C}(R)=\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{e}\mathscr{C}_{e}(R)
is a graded, noncommutative ring: given
\varphi\in\mathscr{C}_{e}(R)
and
\psi\in\mathscr{C}_{d}(R)
, we define the multiplication in
\mathscr{C}(R)
by

(\varphi\cdot\psi)(F^{e+d}_{*}x)=\varphi(F^{e}_{*}\psi(F^{d}_{*}x)).

We define a
Cartier algebra
on
R
to be a graded subring
\mathscr{D}\subseteq\mathscr{C}(R)
with degree zero piece
\mathscr{D}_{0}=\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(R,R)
. We say that a Cartier algebra
\mathscr{D}
is
F
-split if
\mathscr{D}_{e}
contains an
F
-splitting for some
e>0
. Note that this is equivalent to the existence of a surjective map
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}_{e}
for some
e
: if
\varphi
is surjective, then
\varphi(F^{e}_{*}x)=1
for some
x\in R
. Letting
m_{x}\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(R,R)
be “multiplication by
x
,” we see that
\varphi\cdot m_{x}\in\mathscr{D}_{e}
and
(\varphi\cdot m_{x})(F^{e}_{*}1)=1
. Further, if
\varphi\in\mathscr{D}_{e}
is an
F
-splitting, then
\varphi^{n}\in\mathscr{D}_{ne}
is an
F
-splitting for all
n>0
.

3.2. Splittings compatible with an ideal

One sort of Cartier algebra, which is particularly relevant to us, is the Cartier algebra of maps compatible with a given ideal. Given a map
\varphi\colon F^{e}_{*}R\to R
and an ideal
I\subseteq R
, we say
\varphi
is
compatible with
I
if
\varphi(F^{e}_{*}I)\subseteq I
. If we set

\mathscr{C}_{e}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })=\left\{\varphi\in\mathscr{C}_{e}(R)\mid\varphi(F^{e}_{*}I)\subseteq I\right\},

then one quickly checks that
\mathscr{C}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })\coloneqq\bigoplusop\displaylimits_{e}\mathscr{C}_{e}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })
is a Cartier algebra on
R
; see
[
Smo20
, Proposition 3.4]
. We call this the
Cartier algebra on
R
of maps compatible with
I
.

One special property of this Cartier algebra is that maps in
\mathscr{C}_{e}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })
restrict to maps in

BV88
, Theorem 5.5]
. For the reader’s convenience, we will sketch a proof of this beautiful fact in the remainder of this subsection. A more detailed treatment is found in
[
BV88
, Chapters 4 and 5]
.

Let
\mathscr{M}\subseteq\mathbb{A}_{\mathcal{k}}^{m(m+n)}
be the set of
m\times(m+n)
matrices with full rank. Then every
m
-dimensional subspace of
\mathcal{k}^{m+n}
can be expressed as the row-span of some
M\in\mathscr{M}
. Note that two different matrices have the same row-span if and only if one can be obtained from the other by a sequence of elementary row operations.

Given any list of integers
1\leq b_{1}<\cdots<b_{m}\leq n+m
, let
\delta_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}}\colon\mathscr{M}\to\mathcal{k}
be the function sending a matrix
M
to the determinant of the submatrix of
M
obtained by taking columns
b_{1},b_{2},\ldots,b_{m}
. Note that this is a maximal minor of
M
, and there are
\binom{m+n}{m}
possible choices of such maximal minors. Set
N=\binom{m+n}{m}
and consider the map
\rho\colon\mathscr{M}\to\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
sending each matrix to a list of its maximal minors,

\rho(M)=(\delta_{1,2,\ldots,m}(M):\dots:\delta_{n+1,n+2,\ldots,n+m}(M)),

in some fixed order. Note that one of these minors must be nonzero, as
M
has maximal rank. Further, one checks that two matrices
M,N\in\mathscr{M}
have the same row-span precisely when
\rho(M)=\rho(N)
. Thus we can identify the Grassmannian
\Gr_{m}(\mathcal{k}^{m+n})
with the image of
\rho
. This is known as the
Plücker embedding
.

We get a partial ordering
\preceq
on the set of minors
\delta_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}}
by declaring

\delta_{b_{1},\ldots,b_{m}}\preceq\delta_{c_{1},\ldots,c_{m}}\Longleftrightarrow b_{i}\leq c_{i}\textrm{ for }i=1,\ldots,m.

In this way we also have a partial ordering on the coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
. Then the Schubert subvarieties of
\Gr_{m}\left(\mathcal{k}^{m+n}\right)
are the subvarieties cut out by certain subsets of these coordinates of
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
, namely the subsets which form a “poset ideal cogenerated by a single element” under this partial ordering; see
[
BV88
, Theorem 5.4]
.

It turns out that
\Gr_{m}(\mathcal{k}^{m+n})
intersected with any standard open affine
D(x_{i})\subseteq\mathbb{P}^{N-1}_{\mathcal{k}}
is a copy of
mn
-dimensional affine space. For instance, the inverse image of
D(x_{N})\cap\Gr_{m}(\mathcal{k}^{m+n})
under
\rho
is the set of matrices in
\mathscr{M}
where the determinant of the
m
rightmost columns is nonzero. Up to row operations, any such matrix can be uniquely written in the form

(5.1)

\begin{bmatrix}\alpha_{11}&\cdots&\alpha_{1n}&0&0&\cdots&1\\
\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}&&\vbox{\kern 6.0pt\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}\hbox{$.$}}\\
\alpha_{(m-1)1}&\cdots&\alpha_{(m-1)n}&0&1&\cdots&0\\
\alpha_{m1}&\cdots&\alpha_{mn}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
\end{bmatrix},

and conversely any choice of
\alpha_{ij}
gives an element of
\rho^{-1}\left(D(x_{N})\right)
.

Now, the key observation is that
every
minor of the
m\times n
matrix
\left(\alpha_{ij}\right)
equals a maximal minor of the full
m\times(n+m)
matrix in Equation (
5.1
). This means that for any collection of minors
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r}\in\mathcal{k}[X_{m\times n}]
, one can find a corresponding collection of coordinates
x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}}
on
\mathbb{P}^{N-1}
so that

\Spec\left(\mathcal{k}\left[X_{m\times n}\right]/(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{r})\right)=V(x_{i_{1}},\ldots,x_{i_{r}})\cap\Gr_{m}(\mathcal{k}^{m+n})\cap D(x_{N}).

All that is left to check is that the coordinates vanishing along the varieties in
Theorem

5.1
always form a poset ideal cogenerated by a single element, which is done in
[
BV88
, Theorem 5.3]
.

5.3. A base-change lemma for diagonal
F
-splitting

The above work proves
Theorem

5.1
whenever
\mathcal{k}
is algebraically closed. For the general case, we just need to show that the
F
-splitting of
\mathscr{D}^{(2)}(R/\mathcal{k})
can be checked after base change to an algebraically closed field. We prove a more general fact, which we hope might be of independent use.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let
A
be an
F
-finite field, and let
R
be an
A
algebra essentially of finite type. Suppose
B
is a perfect field extension of
A
, and let
I\subseteq R
be an ideal. Set
R_{B}\coloneqq R\otimes_{A}B
and
I_{B}\coloneqq I\otimes_{A}B=IR_{B}
. If
{\mathscr{C}(R_{B},I_{B}\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{I_{B}}}
is
F
-split, then
{\mathscr{C}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{I}}
is
F
-split. If
A
is perfect, then the converse holds as well.

Proof.

Suppose that
{\mathscr{C}(R_{B},I_{B}\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{I_{B}}}
is
F
-split. We wish to find a surjective map
\psi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}(R/I),R/I)
with a lift to
R
. Let
\pi
denote the map
\pi\colon R\twoheadrightarrow R/I
, so that we have
F^{e}_{*}\pi\colon F^{e}_{*}R\twoheadrightarrow F^{e}_{*}(R/I)
. Let
(F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*}
be the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(-,R/I)
applied to
F^{e}_{*}\pi
, and let
\pi_{*}
be the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}R,-)
applied to
\pi
. Observe that a map
\psi\in\mathrm{Hom}_{R}(F^{e}_{*}(R/I),R/I)
has a lift to
R
if and only if
(F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*}(\psi)=\psi\circ F^{e}_{*}\pi
is in the image of
\pi_{*}
. Further,
(F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*}(\psi)
is surjective precisely when
\psi
is. Thus we wish to show that
\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*})\cap\image(\pi_{*})
contains a surjection, or equivalently that the “evaluation at 1” map is surjective. To emphasize, this is the key point:
{\mathscr{C}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{I}}
is
F
-split precisely when the map

\eval_{1}\colon\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*})\cap\image(\pi_{*})\longrightarrow R/I,\quad\varphi\longmapsto\varphi(F^{e}_{*}1)

is surjective.

Because
R_{B}
is faithfully flat over
R
, the map
\eval_{1}
is surjective precisely when
\eval_{1}\otimes_{R}R_{B}
is, which is equivalent to
\left(\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*})\cap\image(\pi_{*})\right)\otimes_{R}R_{B}
containing a surjection. This same faithful flatness yields

\displaystyle\left(\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*})\cap\image(\pi_{*})\right)\otimes_{R}R_{B}

\displaystyle=\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*})\otimes_{R}R_{B}\cap(\image\pi_{*})\otimes_{R}R_{B}

\displaystyle=\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)^{*}\otimes_{R}R_{B})\cap\image(\pi_{*}\otimes_{R}R_{B})

\displaystyle=\image(((F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B)^{*})\cap\image(\pi\otimes_{A}B)_{*},

where
((F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B)^{*}
is the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{R_{B}}(-,R_{B}/I_{B})
applied to
(F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B
,
and
(\pi\otimes_{A}B)_{*}
is the functor
\mathrm{Hom}_{R_{B}}(F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}B,-)
applied to
\pi\otimes_{A}B
.

By assumption, there exists a surjection
\psi\colon F^{e}_{*}(R_{B}/I_{B})\to R_{B}/I_{B}
which lifts to
R_{B}
:

\textstyle{F^{e}_{*}R_{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\psi}}
\scriptstyle{F^{e}_{*}(\pi\otimes_{A}B)}
\textstyle{R_{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi\otimes_{A}B}
\textstyle{F^{e}_{*}(R_{B}/I_{B})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{R_{B}/I_{B}\hbox to0.0pt{.\hss}}

Observe that we have a natural
R_{B}
-linear map
\widehat{\alpha}\colon F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}B\to F^{e}_{*}R_{B}
given by
F^{e}_{*}r\otimes b\mapsto F^{e}_{*}(r\otimes b^{p^{e}})
—this is just the composition of the natural maps
F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}B\to F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}B
and
F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}F^{e}_{*}B\to F^{e}_{*}(R\otimes_{A}B)
described in Section 2. Similarly, we have a natural map
\alpha\colon F^{e}_{*}(R/I)\otimes_{A}B\to F^{e}_{*}(R_{B}/I_{B})
. Because
B
is perfect, both
\alpha
and
\widehat{\alpha}
are surjective. These maps fit into a larger diagram

\textstyle{F^{e}_{*}R\otimes_{A}B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\alpha}}
\scriptstyle{(F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B}
\textstyle{F^{e}_{*}R_{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\widehat{\psi}}
\scriptstyle{F^{e}_{*}(\pi\otimes_{A}B)}
\textstyle{R_{B}\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi\otimes_{A}B}
\textstyle{F^{e}_{*}(R/I)\otimes_{A}B\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\alpha}
\textstyle{F^{e}_{*}(R_{B}/I_{B})\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\psi}
\textstyle{R_{B}/I_{B}\hbox to0.0pt{.\hss}}

Thus
\psi\circ\alpha\circ((F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B)
is a surjection in
\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B)^{*}\cap\image(\pi\otimes_{A}B)_{*}
, as desired.

For the converse, suppose that
A
is perfect and
{\mathscr{C}(R,I\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{I}}
is
F
-split. By the above argument, we have a surjection in
\psi\in\image((F^{e}_{*}\pi)\otimes_{A}B)^{*}\cap\image(\pi\otimes_{A}B)_{*}
. Since
B
is perfect, the Frobenius map
B\to F^{e}_{*}B
is an isomorphism. Combining this with the assumption that
A
is perfect, we see that the maps
\alpha
and
\widehat{\alpha}
are isomorphisms. Setting
\psi=\chi\circ(F^{e}_{*}\pi\otimes_{A}B)
, we see that
\chi\circ\alpha^{-1}
is an
F
-splitting in
{\mathscr{C}(R_{B},I_{B}\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{I_{B}}}
.
∎

Corollary 5.4
.

Let
A
be an
F
-finite field, and let
R
be an
A
algebra essentially of finite type. Suppose
B
is a perfect field extension of
A
. If
\mathscr{D}^{(2)}(R_{B}/B)
is
F
-split, then
\mathscr{D}^{(2)}(R/A)
is
F
-split.

Proof.

By definition, the Cartier algebra
{\mathscr{C}(R_{B}\otimes_{B}R_{B},\ker\mu_{B}\,\scalebox{1}{\rotatebox[origin]{90.0}{$\circlearrowleft$} })}_{/{\ker\mu_{B}}}
is
F
-split, where
\mu_{B}
is the multiplication map
\mu_{B}\colon R_{B}\otimes_{B}R_{B}\to R_{B}
. But
R_{B}\otimes_{B}R_{B}=R\otimes_{A}R\otimes_{A}B
, and we can obtain
\mu_{B}
by applying the functor
-\otimes_{A}B
to the multiplication map
\mu_{A}\colon R\otimes_{A}R\to R
. By faithful flatness, we see that
\ker\mu_{B}=(\ker\mu_{A})\otimes_{A}B
. Then the result follows from
Proposition

5.3
.
∎
