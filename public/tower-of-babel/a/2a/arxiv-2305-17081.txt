Abstract
. In these notes we generalize the notion of a (pseudo) metric measuring the distance of two points, to a (pseudo) n n -metric which assigns a value to a tuple of n ⩾ 2 n\geqslant 2 points. We present two principles of constructing pseudo n n -metrics. The first one uses the Vandermonde determinant while the second one uses exterior products and is related to the volume of the simplex spanned by the given points. We show that the second class of examples induces pseudo n n -metrics on the unit sphere of a Hilbert space and on matrix manifolds such as the Stiefel and the Grassmann manifold. Further, we construct a pseudo n n -metric on hypergraphs and discuss the problem of generalizing the Hausdorff metric for closed sets to a pseudo n n -metric.

Pseudo
n
-metric, exterior calculus, matrix manifolds, hypergraph

1.
Introduction

The classical notion of a (pseudo) metric or distance on a set
X
is a map which
assigns to any two points in
X
a real value such that
d
is (semi-)definite,
symmetric, and satisfies the triangle inequality. Since the subject is vast
we just mention
[
11
]
as an elementary and
[
6
]
as an advanced monograph.
The purpose of this contribution is to investigate an
n
-dimensional
(
n\geqslant 2
) generalization of this notion.
To be precise, we call a map

\displaystyle d:X^{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}X\to{\mathbb{R}}

a
pseudo
n
-metric on
X
if it has the following properties:

(i)

(Semidefiniteness)
If
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
satisfies
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
, then

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
.

(ii)

(Symmetry)
For all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
and all

\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}

d(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

(1.1)

Here
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denotes the group of permutations of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.

(iii)

(Simplicial inequality)
For all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
and
y\in X

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

(1.2)

Clearly, a pseudo
2
-metric agrees with the notion of an ordinary
pseudo metric.
If we sharpen condition (i) to definiteness, i.e.
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=0
implies
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
,
then we call
d
an
n
-metric.

We have two basic principles of constructing pseudo
n
-metrics. The first
one is based on the Vandermonde determinant (Section
2
). It leads
to a definite
n
-metric in the complex plane (or equivalently in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
)
and can be used to define pseudo
n
-metrics in spaces of complex-valued
functions (Theorem
2.3
, Examples
2.7
and
2.8
).
Although the straightforward generalization of the Vandermonde determinant to a product
of norms does not work, we present in Section
6.1
an alternative generalization
via the norm of symmetric multilinear forms.

The second class of pseudo
n
-metrics occurs in Eudlidean
vector spaces when
d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is taken as the volume of
the simplex
\mathcal{S}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
spanned by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
, or (up to a factor of
(n-1)!
) as the
volume of the parallelepiped spanned by
x_{2}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}-x_{1}
; see Theorem
3.6
. While semidefiniteness and symmetry are obvious in this case,
the simplicial inequality reflects the fact that

\mathcal{S}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is covered by the simplices

\mathcal{S}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})
for
i=1,\ldots,n
, with
equality in case
y\in\mathcal{S}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
. For
n\geqslant 3

this pseudo
n
-metric is not definite but becomes zero when
the spanning vectors
x_{2}-x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}-x_{1}
are linearly dependent.
Instead of working directly with simplices we
present in Section
3
a more general approach which uses
pseudo
n
-norms and exterior calculus.

In differential geometry, the metric tensor of a Riemannian manifold is
defined as a
2
-tensor on the tangent bundle. In principle, this is a local
definition using charts, it does not allow us to directly compare arbitrary
points on the manifold. This is then achieved by the Riemannian
distance (or the metric in the sense of this paper) which employs
geodesics, i.e. paths of shortest length
connecting the two points, or more general metric structures;
see e.g.
[
6
, Ch.2,5]
.

In
[
14
]
,
[
13
]
the authors consider
area-metrics on Riemannian manifolds which are metric
4
-tensors and which
are more general than the canonical area metric induced by the metric

2
-tensor. Using this concept, some interesting implications for
gravity dynamics and electrodynamics are discussed.
However, there seems to be no attempt to define a pseudo
3
-metric in our
sense, i.e. an area metric which is defined for any triple of points on the manifold
and which satisfies the three properties above.
Other generalizations of the metric notion, such as the
polymetrics in
[
12
]
or the multi-metrics in
[
7
]

aim at spaces where several
2
-metrics are given, sometimes with
different multiplicities.

In Section
4
we construct a specific pseudo
n
-metric on the unit
ball of a Hilbert space, and we derive from this suitable
pseudo
n
-metrics on manifolds of matrices such as the Stiefel and
the Grassmann manifold (see
[
4
]
for a recent overview of geometric
and computational aspects).
In Section
5
we turn to metrics on discrete spaces and show
how to extend the standard shortest path distance
in an undirected graph to a pseudo
n
-metric on
a connected hypergraph; see
[
5
]
for some general theory.

Finally, in Section
6
we discuss several externsions resp. open problems raised by the first sections.
The first one deals with the generalization of the pseudo
n
-metric derived from the Vandermonde determinant.
We show that the product of norms does not work in dimensions
\geqslant 3
, but that it is possible
to construct pseudo
n
-metrics from the norm of a symmetric multilinear map which is
of degree
\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)
.
The second one is concerned with the search for a
pseudo
n
-metric on the Grassmann manifold which is a canonical extension
of the classical
2
-metric (
[
9
, Ch.6.4]
). The last
one concerns a generalization of the Hausdorff metric for closed sets,
where we show that an obvious definition fails to produce a pseudo
n
-metric.

Due to its various areas of occurrence, we think that the axiomatic
notion of a pseudo
n
-metric deserves to be studied in its own right.

2.
Basic properties and the Vandermonde example

In this secton we collect some basic constructions and properties of pseudo
n
-metrics. Our main example is based on the Vandermonde determinant which
leads to a pseudo
n
-metric that is homogeneous of degree
\frac{1}{2}n(n-1)
.
In the following, a mapping
d:X^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called nonnegative if
d(x)\geqslant 0

holds for all
x\in X^{n}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

(i)

A pseudo
n
-metric on
X
is nonnegative.

(ii)

If the mapping
d:X^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is nonnegative, semidefinite,
symmetric, and satisfies (
5
) for all pairwise different elements
y,x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
,
then
d
is a pseudo
n
-metric.

Proof.

(i):

We apply (
5
) with
y=x_{1}
and obtain from the semidefiniteness
and the symmetry

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=d(x_{2},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n})\leqslant d(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n})+d(x_{2},x_{1},x_{3},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle+\sum_{i=3}^{n}d(x_{2},x_{2},x_{3},\ldots,x_{i-1},x_{1},x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})=2d(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}).

(ii):

In case
x_{i}=x_{j}
for some
i\neq j
, the inequality (
5
) is obvious
since
d
is semidefinite and nonnegative.
Further, if
y=x_{j}
for some
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, then
the semidefiniteness implies

\displaystyle d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},y,x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Proposition 2.2
.

For a pseudo
n
-metric
d
on a set
X
the following holds:

(i)

d_{X}
defines a pseudo
n
-metric on any subset
Y\subset X
.

(ii)

If
d_{Y}
is a pseudo
n
-metric an a set
Y
, then

d_{X\times Y}\Big(\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
y_{1}\end{pmatrix},\ldots,\begin{pmatrix}x_{n}\\
y_{n}\end{pmatrix}\Big):=\Big\|\begin{pmatrix}d_{X}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\\
d_{Y}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\end{pmatrix}\Big\|

defines a pseudo
n
-metric on
X\times Y
for any monotone norm
\|\cdot\|

in
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
.

(iii)

Let
{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq X^{Y}
be a subset of functions from some
set
Y
to
X
. Further assume that a normed space

Z\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{Y}
is given with a monotone norm
\|\cdot\|_{Z}
and
the following property:

\displaystyle f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}\in{\mathcal{F}}\Longrightarrow d(f_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,f_{n}(\cdot))\in Z.

(2.1)

Then a pseudo
n
-metric on
{\mathcal{F}}
is defined by

d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})=\|d(f_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,f_{n}(\cdot))\|_{Z}.

Proof.

Since most assertions are obvious we consider only the
simplicial inequality in cases (ii) and (iii).
Recall that a norm
\|\cdot\|_{Z}

on a linear space
Z
of real-valued functions
f:Y\to{\mathbb{R}}

is called monotone (see e.g.
[
10
, Ch.5.4]
) iff for all
f_{1},f_{2}\in Z

\displaystyle|f_{1}(y)|\leqslant|f_{2}(y)|\;\forall y\in Y\Longrightarrow\|f_{1}\|_{Z}\leqslant\|f_{2}\|_{Z}.

In case (ii) one takes norms in the following vector inequality for
\xi\in X
,

\eta\in Y

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}d_{X}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\\
d_{Y}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\end{pmatrix}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\begin{pmatrix}d_{X}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},\xi,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})\\
d_{Y}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{i-1},\eta,y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{n})\end{pmatrix}.

The proof of (iii) is analogous.
For
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},g\in{\mathcal{F}}
observe the pointwise inequality

\displaystyle d(f_{1}(y),\ldots,f_{n}(y))

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}d(f_{1}(y),\ldots,f_{i-1}(y),g(y),f_{i+1}(y),\ldots f_{n}(y)),\quad\forall y\in Y,

which by the monotonicity of the norm implies

\displaystyle d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|d(f_{1}(\cdot),\ldots,f_{i-1}(\cdot),g(\cdot),f_{i+1}(\cdot),\ldots,f_{n}(\cdot))\|_{Z}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{{\mathcal{F}}}(f_{1},\ldots,f_{i-1},g,f_{i+1},\ldots,f_{n}).

Our basic example of this section employs the well-known Vandermonde determinant associated with numbers
z_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}
,
j=1,\ldots,n
(see
[
10
, Ch.0.9]
):

V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\det\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}^{n-1}&\cdots&z_{n}^{n-1}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}(z_{i}-z_{j}).

(2.2)

Theorem 2.3
.

(Vandermonde
n
-metric)
The expression

d_{V}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}|z_{i}-z_{j}|=|V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})|

(2.3)

defines an
n
-metric in
{\mathbb{C}}
and by restriction also in
{\mathbb{R}}
.

Proof.

According to Proposition
2.2
(i) it suffices to consider (
2.3
)
in
{\mathbb{C}}
.
By the definition (
2.3
) it is clear that
d_{V}
is nonegative, semidefinite, and even definite. The symmmetry follows from

\displaystyle d_{V}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})^{2}=\prod_{i\neq j}|z_{i}-z_{j}|=\prod_{\pi(i)\neq\pi(j)}|z_{\pi(i)}-z_{\pi(j)}|=d_{V}(z_{\pi(1)},\ldots,z_{\pi(n)})^{2}.

By Lemma
2.1
(ii) it is enough to show the simplicial inequality for pairwise different numbers
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n},y
. Then there is a unique
solution
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})^{\top}\in{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
to the linear system

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}z_{1}^{n-1}&\cdots&z_{n}^{n-1}\\
\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
1&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}\\
\vdots\\
a_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y^{n-1}\\
\vdots\\
1\end{pmatrix}.

The solution is given by Cramer’s rule as follows

\displaystyle a_{i}=\frac{V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})}{V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})},\quad i=1,\ldots,n,

with the Vandermonde determinant from (
2.2
).
The proof is completed by the following estimate

\displaystyle d_{V}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})=|V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}|=|\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,n)|

(2.4)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}|V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,n)|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{V}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,n).

Remark 2.4
.

It is remarkable that the proof yields the following more general
inequality

\displaystyle|y|^{k}d_{V}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}|z_{i}|^{k}d_{V}(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n}),\quad k=0,\ldots,n-1.

Let us also note that a straightforward generalization of (
2.3
)
to a product of norms fails in higher dimensional spaces; see Section

6.1
. However, in Section
6.1
we present an alternative generalization of the
Vandermonde pseudo
n
-metrics.

In case
n=3
we investigate when the simplicial inequality holds with equality.

Proposition 2.5
.

For the
3
-metric

d_{V}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=|z_{1}-z_{2}||z_{2}-z_{3}||z_{1}-z_{3}|,\quad z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in{\mathbb{C}}

the equality

\displaystyle d_{V}(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})=d_{V}(y,z_{2},z_{3})+d_{V}(z_{1},y,z_{3})+d_{V}(z_{1},z_{2},y)

(2.5)

holds with pairwise different numbers
y,z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}\in{\mathbb{C}}
if and only if
the
quadruple
(y,z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})
belongs (up to a shift and a multiplication
by a complex number and up to a permutation of
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
) to the following two parameter family
(q,s>0)
:

y=0,\quad z_{1}=1,\quad z_{2}=\frac{1}{s}\Big(-1+i\sqrt{q(1+s)}\Big),\quad z_{3}=\frac{1}{s}\Big(-1-i\sqrt{q^{-1}(1+s)}\Big).

(2.6)

Further, the equality (
2.5
) holds with
|z_{1}-y|=|z_{2}-y|=|z_{3}-y|
if and only if the triangle
z_{1},z_{2},z_{3}
is equilateral.

Proof.

From (
2.4
) we find that equality
holds in (
5
) if and only if

\big|\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})\big|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|V(z_{1},\ldots,z_{i-1},y,z_{i+1},\ldots,z_{n})|.

(2.7)

Since the numbers are distinct we may shift
y
to zero and multiply
by a complex number such that
z_{1}=1
.
Recall that the equality
|z_{1}|+|z_{2}|=|z_{1}+z_{2}|
holds for
z_{1},z_{2}\in{\mathbb{C}}

if and only if either
z_{1}=cz_{2}
or
z_{2}=cz_{1}
for some
c\geqslant 0
.
More generally, the equality
|\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}|=\sum_{i=1}^{n}|z_{i}|

holds if and only if there exists an index
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}

and real numbers
c_{i}\geqslant 0
such that
z_{i}=c_{i}z_{j}
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
Moreover, if
z_{i}\neq 0
for all
i=1,\ldots,n
then the latter
property holds for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with numbers
c_{i}>0
.
We apply this to (
2.7
) with
n=3
after normalizing
y=0

and
z_{1}=1
. Thus the equality (
2.5
) holds if and only
if there are numbers
c_{2},c_{3}>0
such that

\displaystyle V(1,0,z_{3})

\displaystyle=(-z_{3})(1-z_{3})=c_{2}V(0,z_{2},z_{3})=c_{2}(-z_{2})(-z_{3})(z_{2}-z_{3}),

\displaystyle V(1,z_{2},0)

\displaystyle=(1-z_{2})z_{2}=c_{3}V(0,z_{2},z_{3})=c_{3}(-z_{2})(-z_{3})(z_{2}-z_{3}).

Since
z_{2},z_{3}\neq 0
this is equivalent to the system

\displaystyle 1-z_{3}

\displaystyle=-c_{2}z_{2}(z_{2}-z_{3}),

\displaystyle 1-z_{2}

\displaystyle=c_{3}z_{3}(z_{2}-z_{3}).

Subtract the second from the first equation and use
z_{2}\neq z_{3}

to find the equivalent system

\displaystyle 1-z_{3}

\displaystyle=-c_{2}z_{2}(z_{2}-z_{3}),

(2.8)

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=-c_{2}z_{2}-c_{3}z_{3}.

With the last equation we eliminate
z_{3}=-c_{3}^{-1}(1+c_{2}z_{2})
from
the first to obtain the quadratic equation

\displaystyle c_{2}(c_{2}+c_{3})z_{2}^{2}+2c_{2}z_{2}+c_{3}+1=0.

The solutions to (
2.8
) are then given by

\displaystyle z_{2}^{\pm}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c_{2}+c_{3}}\Big(-1\pm i\big(\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}(1+c_{2}+c_{3})\big)^{1/2}\Big),

\displaystyle z_{3}^{\mp}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{c_{2}+c_{3}}\Big(-1\mp i\big(\frac{c_{2}}{c_{3}}(1+c_{2}+c_{3})\big)^{1/2}\Big).

Introducing the parameters
s=c_{2}+c_{3}>0
and
q=\frac{c_{3}}{c_{2}}>0

we may write this as

z_{2}^{\pm}(q,s)=\frac{1}{s}\Big(-1\pm i\sqrt{q(1+s)}\Big),\quad z_{3}^{\mp}(q,s)=\frac{1}{s}\Big(-1\mp i\sqrt{q^{-1}(1+s)}\Big).

Finally, we observe the relation

\displaystyle(z_{2}^{-},z_{3}^{+})(q^{-1},s)=(z_{3}^{-},z_{2}^{+})(q,s),\quad q,s>0,

where the latter is a permutation of
(z_{2}^{+},z_{3}^{-})(q,s)
.
Hence the family (
2.6
) covers all solutions up to permutation.

For the normalized coordinates
y=0,z_{1}=1
the condition
1=|z_{2}|=|z_{3}|

holds if and only if
s=2,q=1
. For these values the numbers

z_{2}=\frac{1}{2}(-1+i\sqrt{3})
,
z_{3}=\frac{1}{2}(-1-i\sqrt{3})

are the third roots of unity which belong to the equilateral case.

Remark 2.6
.

A simple consequence of Theorem
2.3
and
Proposition
2.5
for
y=0
,
|z_{1}|=|z_{2}|=|z_{3}|=1
is the following elementary statement: The sides
a,b,c
of a triangle
with vertices on the unit circle satisfy the inequality

\displaystyle abc\leqslant a+b+c.

Equality holds if and only if the triangle is equilateral.
Neither did we find a reference to this fact in the literature nor an elementary
geometric proof.

2.2
and Theorem
2.3
leads to

.

Let
\|\cdot\|
be any monotone norm in

\displaystyle d_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\left\|\begin{pmatrix}d_{V}(x_{1,1},\ldots,x_{n,1})\\
\vdots\\

,

Example 2.8
.

Let
(\Omega,{\mathcal{G}},\mu)

2.2

to

with the monotone norm

\|\cdot\|_{L^{p}}
and to

.
Then the expression

defines a pseudo

guarantees

) holds.

3.
Examples in linear spaces

3.1.
Pseudo
n
-norms

In vector spaces it is natural to first define
a pseudo
n
-norm and then define a pseudo
n
-metric by taking the

n
-norm of differences.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
X
be a vector space. A map
\|\cdot\|:X^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is called
a pseudo
n
-norm if it has the following properties:

(i)

(Semidefiniteness)

If
x_{i}
,
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
are linearly dependent
then
\|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|=0
.

(ii)

(Symmetry)

For all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
and for all
\pi\in S_{n}

\|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|=\|(x_{\pi(1)},\ldots,x_{\pi(n)})\|.

(3.1)

(iii)

(Positive homogeneity)

For all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
and for all

\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}

\|(\lambda x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|=|\lambda|\,\|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|.

(3.2)

(iv)

(Multi-sublinearity)

For all
x=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in X^{n}
and

y\in X
,

\|(x_{1}+y,\ldots,x_{n})\|\leqslant\|(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\|+\|(y,x_{2},\ldots,x_{n})\|.

(3.3)

A pseudo
n
-norm is called an
n
-norm if
\|(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\|=0

holds if and only if
x_{i},i=1,\ldots,n
are linearly dependent.

Clearly, a pseudo
1
-norm is a semi-norm in the usual sense. Also note
that the positive homogeneity (
3.2
) and the sublinearity (
3.3
) transfers from the first component to all other components via (
3.1
).

Next consider a matrix
A\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n,n}
and the induced
linear map
A_{X}:=A^{\top}\otimes I_{X}
on
X^{n}
given by

\displaystyle A_{X}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
\vdots\\
x_{n}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}A_{11}I_{X}&\cdots&A_{1n}I_{X}\\
\vdots&&\vdots\\

3.2.
Construction via exterior products

The previous results suggest to use exterior products in defining appropriate
pseudo
n
-norms. For this purpose recall the following calculus
for a separable reflexive Banach space
X
; see
[
15
, Ch.V]
,
[
1
, Section 6]
,
[
2
, Ch.3.2.3]
.
The linear space
\bigwedge^{k}(X)
is the set of continuous alternating

k
-linear forms on the dual
X^{\star}
, using the identification of

X
and its bidual
X^{\star\star}
.
\bigwedge^{k}X
is called the
k
-fold exterior
product of
X
. Elements of
\bigwedge^{k}(X)
are exterior products

x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k}
defined for

by the dual pairing

\displaystyle\langle x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k},(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})\rangle=\det\left(\langle f_{i},x_{j}\rangle_{i,j=1}^{k}\right)

where
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k}\in X^{\star}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle

is the dual pairing of
X^{\star}
and

.
As usual we write in the following

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}=x_{1}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k}.

By linearity one can extend exterior products to sums

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{J}c_{j}(x_{1}^{j}\wedge\ldots\wedge x_{k}^{j}),\quad c_{j}\in{\mathbb{R}},x_{i}^{j}\in X,i=1,\ldots,k,j=1,\ldots,J\in{\mathbb{N}}.

Closing the linear hull with respect to the norm

\displaystyle\|\Phi\|_{\wedge}=\sup\{|\Phi(f_{1},\ldots,f_{k})|:f_{j}\in X^{\star},\|f_{j}\|_{X^{\star}}=1,j=1,\ldots,k\},\quad\Phi\in{\bigwedge}^{k}(X)

turns
\bigwedge^{k}(X)
into a Banach space.

The following lemma is elementary (see
[
2
, Lemma 3.2.6]
)

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
\bigwedge^{k}X
be the
k
-fold exterior product of a separable reflexive Banach space
space
X
with

. Then the following holds

(i)

) and

is a basis
of

\displaystyle\{e_{i_{1}}\wedge\ldots\wedge e_{i_{k}}:1\leqslant i_{1}<i_{2}\ldots<i_{k}\leqslant m\}

.

(ii)

One has

are linearly dependent.

(iii)

then the bilinear and continuous extension of

defines an inner product on the Hilbert space

the corresponding norm

(3.9)

P_{i}\in L({\mathbb{R}}^{m},{\mathbb{R}}^{m})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
be the corresponding orthogonal projections. Then the setting

d_{{\mathcal{G}}}(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})=\big(\det(\langle P_{i},P_{j}\rangle_{k,H})_{i,j=1}^{n}\big)^{1/2}.

(4.10)

defines a pseudo
n
-metric on
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)
.

For an interpretation of (
4.10
) let us compute the inner product
of two orthogonal projections.

Lemma 4.7
.

For
A_{j}\in\mathrm{St}(k,m)
,
j=1,2
let
P_{j}=A_{j}A_{j}^{\star}
be
the corresponding orthogonal projections in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
. Then the following holds

\langle P_{1},P_{2}\rangle_{k,H}=\frac{1}{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2},

where
\sigma_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\sigma_{k}\geqslant 0
are the singular values
of
A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the SVD
A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}=Y\Sigma Z^{\star}
with
\Sigma=\diag(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
and
Y,Z\in O(k)
. Further, choose

A_{j}^{c}\in\mathrm{St}(m-k,m)
such that
\begin{pmatrix}A_{j}&A_{j}^{c}\end{pmatrix}
,
j=1,2
are orthogonal. Then we conclude

\displaystyle k\langle P_{1},P_{2}\rangle_{k,H}

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(A_{1}A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}A_{2}^{\star})=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&A_{1}^{c}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{2}^{\star}\\
A_{2}^{c\star}\end{pmatrix}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\Big(\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{2}^{\star}\\
A_{2}^{c\star}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}&A_{1}^{c}\end{pmatrix}\Big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}\begin{pmatrix}A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}A_{2}^{\star}A_{1}&A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}A_{2}^{\star}A_{1}^{c}\\
0&0\end{pmatrix}=\mathrm{tr}\big(A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}A_{2}^{\star}A_{1}\big)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{tr}(Y\Sigma Z^{\star}Z\Sigma Y^{\star})=\mathrm{tr}\Sigma^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2}.

Recall from
[
9
, Ch.6.4]
that the singular values
\sigma_{1}\geqslant\ldots\geqslant\sigma_{k}
of
A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}
define the principal angles

0\leqslant\theta_{1}\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\theta_{k}\leqslant\frac{\pi}{2}
between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
by
\sigma_{j}=\cos(\theta_{j})
,
j=1,\ldots,k
.

For
n=2
this leads to an explicit expression of (
4.10
).

Proposition 4.8
.

In case
n=2
equation (
4.10
) defines a
2
-metric on
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)

by

d_{{\mathcal{G}}}(V_{1},V_{2})=(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2})^{2}\big)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{k}\Big(k^{2}-\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos^{2}(\theta_{j})\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

(4.11)

where
0\leqslant\theta_{1}\leqslant\cdots\leqslant\theta_{k}\leqslant\frac{\pi}{2}

are the principal angles of the subspaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.

Proof.

From Lemma
4.7
and (
4.10
) we find

\displaystyle d_{{\mathcal{G}}}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle=(1-\frac{1}{k^{2}}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}^{2})^{2}\big)^{1/2}=\frac{1}{k}\Big(k^{2}-\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\cos^{2}(\theta_{j})\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2}.

6.2
we continue the discussion of
pseudo
n

2
-metrics.

5.
A pseudo
n
-metric on hypergraphs

The notion of hypergraph allows edges which connect more than two vertices;
see
[
5
]
.

Definition 5.1
.

A pair
(V,E)
is called a hypergraph if
V
is a finite set
and
E
is a subset of the power set
\mathcal{P}(V)
of
V
.
An element
e\in E
is called a hyperedge. In particular,
it is called an
n
-hyperedge if
\#e=n
.
The hypergraph is called
n
-uniform if all its hyperedges are
n
-hyperedges.

Obviously, a
2
-uniform hypergraph is an ordinary (undirected)
graph. The following definition generalizes the standard notion of connectedness.

Definition 5.2
.

Let
(V,E)
be an
n
-uniform hypergraph.

(i)

A subset
P\subset E
is called a connected component of
(V,E)

if for any two
e_{0},e_{\infty}\in P
there exist
finitely many
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in P
such that

e_{i-1}\cap e_{i}\neq\emptyset
for
i=1,\ldots,k+1
where

e_{k+1}:=e_{\infty}
.
Any subset
W\subset\bigcup_{e\in P}e
is said to be connected by
P
.

(ii)

The hypergraph is called connected if

E
is a connected component of
(V,E)
and
V=\bigcup_{e\in E}e
.

In case
n=2
this agrees with the usual notion of connected components.
For a connected hypergraph one can connect any subset of
V
by
the (maximal) connected component
E
.

Definition 5.3
.

Let
(V,E)
be an
n
-uniform and connected hypergraph. For
any tuple
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in V^{n}
define
d(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})=0

if there exist
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
i\neq j
and
v_{i}=v_{j}
.
Otherwise set
W=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
and

d(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})=\min\{\#P:P\text{ connected component of $(V,E)$ connecting}\;W\}.

Example 5.4
.

Let
V=\{1,2,3,4\}
and

\displaystyle E=\big\{\{1,2,4\},\{2,3,4\},\{1,3,4\}\big\}.

This is a
3
-uniform hypergraph satisfying

d(1,2,4)=1
,
d(2,3,4)=1
,
d(1,3,4)=1
. Moreover, we have

d(1,2,3)=2
since

\{1,2,3\}
is
not a hyperedge and
P=\big\{\{1,2,4\},\{2,3,4\}\big\}
is a connected
component of
(V,E)
which has cardinality
2
and connects
\{1,2,3\}
.
Another connected component with this property and of the same cardinality is

P^{\prime}=\big\{\{1,2,4\},\{1,3,4\}\big\}
.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
(V,E)
be an
n
-uniform and connected hypergraph. Then
the map
d:V^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
from Definition
5.3
is a
pseudo
n
-metric on
V
.

Remark 5.6
.

In case
n=2
this generates the topology on hypergraphs
discussed in
[
7
]
.

Proof.

The semidefiniteness and symmetry follow directly from Definition

5.3
.
Now consider
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\in V^{n}
. If two components agree then
(
5
) is trivially satisfied. Otherwise
W=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}

is a subset of
V
with
\#W=n
. Let
y\in V
be arbitrary.
By Lemma
2.1
it suffices to prove the simplicial inequality
for
y\notin W
.
Let
P_{1}
resp.
P_{2}
be connected components of minimal cardinality

p_{1}=d(y,v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})
resp.
p_{2}=d(v_{1},y,\ldots,v_{n})

which connect
W_{1}=\{y,v_{2},\ldots,v_{n}\}
resp.
W_{2}=\{v_{1},y,\ldots,v_{n}\}
.
Then
P=P_{1}\cup P_{2}
is a connected component which connects
\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
. To see this, note first that

\displaystyle W\subset W_{1}\cup W_{2}\subset\bigcup_{e\in P_{1}}e\cup\bigcup_{e\in P_{2}}e=\bigcup_{e\in P}e.

Let
e_{0},e_{\infty}\in P
. If both are in
P_{1}
or in
P_{2}
then they can
be connected as in Definition
5.2
(i). So assume w.l.o.g.

e_{0}\in P_{1}
,
e_{\infty}\in P_{2}
. Since
P_{1}
connects
W_{1}
there
exists a hyperedge
e_{+}\in P_{1}
such that
y\in e_{+}
and a hyperedge
e_{-}\in P_{2}
such that
y\in e_{-}
. Further, there are sequences of
hyperedges
e_{1},\ldots,e_{k}\in P_{1}
such that
e_{i-1}\cap e_{i}\neq\emptyset

for
i=1,\ldots,k+1
with
e_{k+1}=e_{+}
. Similarly, there exist
hyperedges
e_{-}=e_{0}^{\prime},e_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,e_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}
such that

e^{\prime}_{i-1}\cap e^{\prime}_{i}\neq\emptyset
for
i=1,\ldots,k^{\prime}+1
with
e^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}+1}=e_{\infty}
. Since
y\in e_{k+1}\cap e^{\prime}_{0}
we obtain that the sequence

e_{0},\ldots,e_{k},e_{+},e_{-},e^{\prime}_{1},\ldots,e^{\prime}_{k^{\prime}},e_{\infty}
lies in
P
and
has nonempty intersections of successive hyperedges. Therefore,

\displaystyle d(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})\leqslant p_{1}+p_{2}=d(y,v_{2},\ldots,v_{n})+d(v_{1},y,\ldots,v_{n}),

which proves (
5
).

The proof shows that we have a sharper inequality than (
5
)

\displaystyle d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\leqslant\max_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}

\displaystyle\big(d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},y,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})

\displaystyle+d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{j-1},y,x_{j+1},\ldots,x_{n})\big).

6.
Further Examples, Problems, and Conjectures

6.1.
Generalized Vandermonde pseudo
n
-metrics

It is tempting to conjecture that

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\prod_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|,\quad x_{i}\in X,i=1,\ldots,n,

(6.1)

generates an
n
-metric on a Euclidean space

(X,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle,\|\cdot\|)
.

However, the expression (
6.1
) does not satisfy the simplicial inequality
in dimensions
\geqslant 3
as the following example shows.

Example 6.1
.

Consider
X={\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
y=0
, and let
x_{1},\ldots,x_{4}
be the vertices
of an equilateral tetrahedron:

\displaystyle x_{1}

\displaystyle=(1,0,0),\quad x_{2}=\frac{1}{3}(-1,2\sqrt{2},0),

\displaystyle x_{3}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{3}(-1,-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{6}),\quad x_{4}=\frac{1}{3}(-1,-\sqrt{2},-\sqrt{6}).

Then one verifies

\displaystyle\|x_{j}\|

\displaystyle=1,\quad j=1,\ldots,4,

\displaystyle\|x_{i}-x_{j}\|

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{8}{3}},\quad 1\leqslant i<j\leqslant 4.

The simplicial inequality then requires

\displaystyle\big(\frac{8}{3}\big)^{3}\leqslant 4\big(\frac{8}{3}\big)^{3/2}\quad\text{or}\quad 2^{5}\leqslant 3^{3},

which is wrong.

In the following we pursue another highdimensional generalization of the Vandermonde expression (
2.3
)
which uses multilinear symmetric maps.
It is based on the following purely algebraic result.

Theorem 6.2
.

(The general Vandermonde equalities)

Let
X,Y
be vector spaces over
{\mathbb{K}}={\mathbb{R}},{\mathbb{C}}
and let

\displaystyle A\colon X^{M_{n}}\longrightarrow Y,\quad M_{n}=\frac{1}{2}n(n-1),n\geqslant 2,

be an
M_{n}
-linear and symmetric map.

(i)

For all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
the following holds:

A\Big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big)=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1}\Big).

(6.2)

(ii)

The quantity

\displaystyle V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=A\Big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big)

satisfies for all
\xi\in X
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X
the following equality:

V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},\xi,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n}).

(6.3)

Proof.

First note that it is convenient to write the arguments of the symmetric map
A
as products
since the sequence of arguments does not matter.

(i):

For
n=2
the equation (
6.2
) is obvious:

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(1,2)A(x_{2}^{\pi(2)-1})+\mathrm{sign}(2,1)A(x_{1}^{\pi(1)-1})=Ax_{2}-Ax_{1}=A(x_{2}-x_{1}).

For the induction step from
n
to
n+1
we use the symmetry of
A
:

\displaystyle A\Big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n+1}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big)=A\Big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j}),\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x_{n+1}-x_{j})\Big)

(6.4)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\prod_{j=1}^{n}(x_{n+1}-x_{j})\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)\sum_{\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|\sigma|}A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\prod_{j\in\sigma}x_{j},x_{n+1}^{n-|\sigma|}\Big).

We consider the summand for any two indices
\ell,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
with

\pi(\ell)=\pi(k)+1
(hence
\ell\neq k
) and any

\sigma\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
with
k\in\sigma,\ell\notin\sigma
.
Let
\tau_{k,\ell}
be the transposition of
k
and
\ell
and set

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}

\displaystyle=\pi\circ\tau_{k,l},\quad\tilde{\sigma}=(\sigma\setminus\{k\})\cup\{\ell\}.

Then we obtain
|\tilde{\sigma}|=|\sigma|
,
\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)
,
\tilde{\pi}(k)=\pi(\ell)
,
\tilde{\pi}(\ell)=\pi(k)
and

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})(-1)^{|\tilde{\sigma}|}A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\tilde{\pi}(j)-1},\prod_{j\in\tilde{\sigma}}x_{j},x_{n+1}^{n-|\tilde{\sigma}|}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)(-1)^{|\sigma|}A\Big(\prod_{j=1,j\neq k,\ell}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},x_{k}^{\pi(\ell)-1},x_{\ell}^{\pi(k)-1},x_{\ell},\prod_{j\in\sigma,j\neq k}x_{j},x_{n+1}^{n-|\sigma|}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)(-1)^{|\sigma|}A\Big(\prod_{j=1,j\neq k,\ell}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},x_{k}^{\pi(k)},x_{\ell}^{\pi(\ell)-1},\prod_{j\in\sigma,j\neq k}x_{j},x_{n+1}^{n-|\sigma|}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)(-1)^{|\sigma|}A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\prod_{j\in\sigma}x_{j},x_{n+1}^{n-|\sigma|}\Big).

The last term belongs to
\pi
and
\sigma
, so that these two terms
cancel each other in (
6.4
).
It is left to consider
(\pi,\sigma)
-terms for which there
is no index
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
in the sequence

\pi^{-1}(n),\pi^{-1}(n-1),\ldots,\pi^{-1}(1)
such that
\pi^{-1}(j)\notin\sigma
and
\pi^{-1}(j-1)\in\sigma
. For a given
\pi\in\mathcal{P}(n)
, the only sets

\sigma
satisfying this condition are

\displaystyle\sigma_{p}=\{\pi^{-1}(n),\ldots,\pi^{-1}(p)\}\quad\text{for some}\;\;1\leqslant p\leqslant n+1,

where we set
\sigma_{n+1}=\emptyset
. Therefore, we obtain from (
6.4
)

\displaystyle A\Big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n+1}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big)

(6.5)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\sum_{p=1}^{n+1}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)(-1)^{n+1-p}A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\prod_{j\in\sigma_{p}}x_{j},x_{n+1}^{p-1}\Big).

For
p\in\{1,\ldots,n+1\}
and
\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
define

\tilde{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
by

\displaystyle\tilde{\pi}(j)=\begin{cases}\pi(j),&\pi(j)\in\{1,\ldots,p-1\},j\leqslant n,\\
\pi(j)+1,&\pi(j)\in\{p,\ldots,n\},j\leqslant n,\\
p,&j=n+1.\end{cases}

Clearly, the map
\mathcal{P}_{n}\times\{1,\ldots,n+1\}\to\mathcal{P}_{n+1}
,

(\pi,p)\mapsto\tilde{\pi}
is a bijection which satisfies

\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})=(-1)^{n+p-1}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)
. With this setting
we may write (
6.5
) as

\displaystyle A\Big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n+1}(x_{i}-x_{j})\Big)=\sum_{\tilde{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{n+1}}\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}x_{j}^{\tilde{\pi}(j)-1}\Big),

which is our assertion.

(ii):

Consider the right-hand side of (
6.3
),use formula (
6.2
), and sort by the powers of
\xi
to obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}

\displaystyle V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{i-1},\xi,x_{i+1},\ldots,x_{n})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\xi^{\pi(i)-1}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad=\sum_{p=1}^{n}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1,\pi(j)\neq p}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\xi^{p-1}\Big).

We consider two cases:

p=1
:

In this case we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1,\pi(j)\neq 1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\xi^{p-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=V(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

2\leqslant p\leqslant n
:

We show that the corresponding terms vanish.
Let
\ell=\pi^{-1}(p)
,
k=\pi^{-1}(1)
(note
k\neq\ell
) and
define
\tilde{\pi}=\pi\circ\tau_{kl}
as above, so
that

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi),\quad\tilde{\pi}(\ell)=1,\quad\tilde{\pi}(k)=p.

The corresponding
\tilde{\pi}
-term then satisfies

\displaystyle\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})A\Big(\prod_{j=1,\tilde{\pi}(j)\neq p}^{n}x_{j}^{\tilde{\pi}(j)-1},\xi^{p-1}\Big)=-\mathrm{sign}

\displaystyle(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1,j\neq k,\ell}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},x_{\ell}^{\tilde{\pi}(\ell)-1},\xi^{p-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1,j\neq\ell}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},x_{k}^{\pi(k)-1},\xi^{p-1}\Big)

\displaystyle=-\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1,j\neq\ell}^{n}x_{j}^{\pi(j)-1},\xi^{p-1}\Big).

Hence the terms which belong to
\pi
and
\tilde{\pi}
cancel each other, and
the summand corresponding to
p\in\{2,\ldots,n\}
vanishes.

An easy consequence of Theorem
6.2
is the following construction of
a pseudo
n
-metric.

Corollary 6.3
.

(The generalized Vandermonde pseudo
n
-metric)

Let
X
be a vector space and let
(Y,\|\cdot\|)
be a normed space. Any
M_{n}
-linear
and symmetric map
A:X^{M_{n}}\to Y
defines a pseudo
n
-metric on
X
via

d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=\big\|A\big(\prod_{1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n}(x_{i}-x_{j})\big)\|,\quad x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in X.

(6.6)

Proof.

The semidefiniteness and the nonnegativity are obvious from the definition (
6.6
). The prove symmetry we apply the representation (
6.2
) for every
\sigma\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
:

\displaystyle d(x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,x_{\sigma(n)})

\displaystyle=\big\|\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{\sigma(j)}^{\pi(j)-1}\Big)\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi)A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{(\pi\circ\sigma^{-1})(j)-1}\Big)\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|\mathrm{sign}(\sigma)\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\pi\circ\sigma^{-1})A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{(\pi\circ\sigma^{-1})(j)-1}\Big)\big\|

\displaystyle=\big\|\sum_{\tilde{\pi}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}}\mathrm{sign}(\tilde{\pi})A\Big(\prod_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}^{\tilde{\pi}(j)-1}\Big)\big\|

\displaystyle=d(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Finally, the simplicial inequality follows by taking norms in (
6.3
) and
using the triangle inequality.

The
n
-mterics from Theorem
2.3
are special cases of Corollary
6.3

for
\dim(X)=\dim(Y)=1,2
when taking the multilinear form
A
induced by real resp. complex multiplication.
It will be of interest to see whether true
n
-metrics can be constructed via Corollary
6.3
for spaces
with
\dim(X)\geqslant 3
.

6.2.
More pseudo
n
-metrics on the Grassmannian
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)

The pseudo
n
-metric (
4.10
) on
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)
is not completely
satisfactory for several reasons.
First, one expects a suitable pseudo
n
-metric on
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)

to be a canonical generalization of the standard
2
-metric given by

d(V_{1},V_{2})=\|P_{1}-P_{2}\|_{2},\quad V_{1},V_{2}\in{\mathcal{G}}(k,m),

where
P_{1},P_{2}
denote the orthogonal projections onto
V_{1},V_{2}
respectively,
and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
denotes the Euclidean operator norm (spectral norm)
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{m}
; see
[
9
, Ch.6.4.3]
.
This
2
-metric may be expressed equivalently as

d(V_{1},V_{2})=\sin(\measuredangle(V_{1},V_{2}))=\sin(\theta_{k}),

(6.7)

where
\theta_{k}
is the largest principal angle between
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
;
see
[
9
, Ch.2.5, Ch.6.4]
and Proposition
4.8
.
Note that the
2
-metric (
6.7
) differs from the
2
-metric in
(
4.11
).
An alternative to (
4.10
) is to measure the exterior product

\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}
of orthogonal projections by a norm different from
the Hilbert-Schmidt norm (cf. (
4.5
)):

\displaystyle\max_{0\neq F_{\ell}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{m,m},\ell=1,\ldots,n}\frac{|\Big(\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}P_{j}\Big)(F_{1},\ldots,F_{n})|}{\prod_{j=1}^{n}\|F_{j}\|_{*}},

where
\|\cdot\|_{*}
is either the spectral norm
\|\cdot\|_{2}
or its
dual
\|\cdot\|^{D}
given by the sum of singular values; see

[
10
, Ch.5.6]
. However, neither were we able to prove
the simplicial inequality for this setting, nor did we find an
explicit expression for the associated
2
-metric comparable to
(
6.7
).

The fact that the Grassmannian may be viewed as a quotient space
of
\mathrm{St}(k,m)
(see (
4.9
)) suggests another construction
of a
2
-metric on
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)
by setting

d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{1},V_{2})=\min_{Q_{1},Q_{2}\in O(k)}d_{H}(A_{1}Q_{1},A_{2}Q_{2})=\min_{Q\in O(k)}d_{H}(A_{1},A_{2}Q),\quad

(6.8)

for
V_{j}=\mathrm{range}(A_{j})
,
A_{j}\in\mathrm{St}(k,m)
and
d_{H}
from (
4.7
),
(
4.8
).
The symmetry and definiteness of

d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}
are obvious. The triangle inequality is also easily seen:
for
A_{j}\in\mathrm{St}(k,m)
and
V_{j}=\mathrm{range}(A_{j})
,
j=1,2,3

select
Q_{1},Q_{2}\in O(k)
with

d_{H}(A_{1},A_{3}Q_{1})=d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{1},V_{3}),\quad d_{H}(A_{3},A_{2}Q_{2})=d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{3},V_{2}).

Then we obtain

\displaystyle d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{1},V_{2})

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{H}(A_{1},A_{2}Q_{2}Q_{1})\leqslant d_{H}(A_{1},A_{3}Q_{1})+d_{H}(A_{3}Q_{1},A_{2}Q_{2}Q_{1})

\displaystyle=d_{H}(A_{1},A_{3}Q_{1})+d_{H}(A_{3},A_{2}Q_{2})=d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{1},V_{3})+d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{3},V_{2}).

The next proposition gives an explicit expression of the
2
-metric (
6.8
)
in terms of the principal angles
\theta_{j}
,
j=1,\ldots,k
between the subspaces
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
.

Proposition 6.4
.

The expression (
6.8
) defines a
2
-metric on the Grassmannian
{\mathcal{G}}(k,m)
.
The metric satisfies

d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{1},V_{2})=\Big(1-k^{-2}\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}\big)^{2}\Big)^{1/2},

(6.9)

where
\sigma_{1}=\cos(\theta_{1})\geqslant\cdots\geqslant\sigma_{k}=\cos(\theta_{k})>0

denote the singular values of
A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}
.

Proof.

Consider the singular value decomposition
A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}=Y\Sigma Z^{\star}
where
Y,Z\in O(k)

and
\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{k})
.
By (
4.8
) the minimum in (
6.8
) is achieved when
maximizing

\displaystyle\langle A_{1}Q_{1},A_{2}Q_{2}\rangle_{H}^{2}

\displaystyle=k^{-2}\mathrm{tr}^{2}(Q_{1}^{\star}A_{1}^{\star}A_{2}Q_{2})=k^{-2}\mathrm{tr}^{2}(Q_{1}^{\star}Y\Sigma Z^{\star}Q_{2})

\displaystyle=k^{-2}\mathrm{tr}^{2}(\Sigma Z^{\star}Q_{2}Q^{\star}_{1}Y)\quad\text{w.r.t.}\quad Q_{1},Q_{2}\in O(k).

Setting
Q=Z^{\star}Q_{2}Q^{\star}_{1}Y\in O(k)
this is equivalent to maximizing

\mathrm{tr}^{2}(\Sigma Q)
with respect to
Q\in O(k)
.
Since the diagonal elements of
Q\in O(k)
satisfy
|Q_{jj}|\leqslant 1
we
obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{tr}^{2}(\Sigma Q)

\displaystyle=\big|\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}Q_{jj}\big|^{2}\leqslant\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}|Q_{jj}|)^{2}\leqslant\big(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\sigma_{j}\big)^{2}=\mathrm{tr}^{2}(\Sigma I_{k}).

This proves our assertion.

Note that (
6.9
) differs from both expressions (
6.7
) and
(
4.11
). Let us further note that we did not succeed to prove the simplicial inequality for the natural generalization of (
6.8
)

d_{{\mathcal{G}},H}(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})=\min_{Q_{j}\in O(k),j=1,\ldots,n}d_{H}(A_{1}Q_{1},\ldots,A_{n}Q_{n})

with
V_{j}=\mathrm{range}(A_{j})
,
A_{j}\in\mathrm{St}(k,m)
. We rather conjecture
that this is false which is suggested by analogy to a counterexample
from the next subsection.

Finally, we discuss the special case
k=1
,
when we can identify
V\in{\mathcal{G}}(1,m)

with a unit vector
v\in S_{{\mathbb{R}}^{m}}
such that
V=\mathrm{span}\{v\}
.
Proposition
4.1
then shows that a suitable pseudo
n
-metric
on
{\mathcal{G}}(1,m)
is given by

\displaystyle d(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})=\big(\det(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle_{2})_{i,j=1}^{n}\big)^{1/2},

where
V_{i}=\mathrm{span}\{v_{i}\}
,
\|v_{i}\|_{2}=1
for
i=1,\ldots,n
.
In particular, in case
n=2
we obtain

d(V_{1},V_{2})=\big(1-\langle v_{1},v_{2}\rangle^{2}\big)^{1/2}=\big(1-\cos\measuredangle(v_{1},v_{2})^{2}\big)^{1/2}=\sin\measuredangle(v_{1},v_{2}),

which is consistent with (
6.7
).
On the other hand the pseudo
n
-metric (
4.10
) leads to

d_{{\mathcal{G}}}(V_{1},\ldots,V_{n})=\big(\det(\langle v_{i},v_{j}\rangle_{2}^{2})_{i,j=1}^{n}\big)^{1/2}

which in case
n=2
differs from (
6.7
).
Therefore, we still consider it an open problem to construct a pseudo
n
-metric
on the Grassmannian which is a natural generalization
of the
2
-metric (
6.7
).

6.3.
Pseudo
n
-metric for subsets

It is natural to ask whether the classical Hausdorff distance
for closed sets of a metric space (see e.g.
[
3
, Chapter 1.5]
) has an extension to a pseudo
n
-metric.

Definition 6.5
.

Let
d:X^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a pseudo
n
-metric on a set
X
.
Then we define for nonempty subsets
A_{j}\subseteq X
,
j=1,\ldots,n
the
following quantities

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(x_{1};A_{2},\ldots,A_{n})

\displaystyle=\inf\{d(x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}):x_{j}\in A_{j},j=2,\ldots,n\},

(6.10)

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(A_{1};A_{2},\ldots,A_{n})

\displaystyle=\sup\{\mathrm{dist}(x_{1};A_{2},\ldots,A_{n}):x_{1}\in A_{1}\},

\displaystyle d_{H}(A_{1},\ldots,A_{n})

\displaystyle=\max_{j=1,\ldots,n}\mathrm{dist}(A_{j};A_{1},\ldots,A_{j-1},A_{j+1},\ldots,A_{n}).

For an ordinary
2
-metric the construction (
6.10
)
leads to the familiar Hausdorff distance

\displaystyle d_{H}(A_{1},A_{2})

\displaystyle=\max(\mathrm{dist}(A_{1};A_{2}),\mathrm{dist}(A_{2};A_{1}))

where
\mathrm{dist}(\cdot;\cdot)
is the Hausdorff semidistance defined
by

\displaystyle\mathrm{dist}(A_{1};A_{2})

\displaystyle=\sup_{x_{1}\in A_{1}}\inf_{x_{2}\in A_{2}}d(x_{1},x_{2}).

It is well known that
d_{H}
is a metric on the set of all closed subsets of
X

where ’closedness’ is defined with respect to the given metric in
X
;
see e.g.
[
3
, Ch.9.4]
.

It is not difficult to see that the map
d_{H}
defined in (
6.10
)
is semidefinite and symmetric .
While the simplicial inequality is true for
n=2
, i.e. the Hausdorff distance,
we claim that it is generally false for
n\geqslant 3
.

Example 6.6
.

(Counterexample for the simplicial inequality)

Consider the case
n=3
and four finite sets
A_{1}=\{w_{1}\}
,

A_{2}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{N}\}
,
A_{3}=\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\}
,

A_{4}=\{z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}\}
where
N\geqslant 2
.
The set
X
is defined as
X=A_{1}\cup A_{2}\cup A_{3}\cup A_{4}
.
For convenience we introduce the following abbreviations
for
i=1
and
j,k,\ell=1,\ldots,N
:

\displaystyle d_{0jk\ell}

\displaystyle=d(x_{j},y_{k},z_{\ell}),\quad d_{i0k\ell}=d(w_{i},y_{k},z_{\ell}),

\displaystyle d_{ij0\ell}

\displaystyle=d(w_{i},x_{j},z_{\ell}),\quad d_{ijk0}=d(w_{i},x_{j},y_{k}).

The values of the
3
-metric with three arguments from different sets
A_{\nu}

in
X
are defined as follows:

\displaystyle d_{0jk\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}0,&j=k=\ell,\\
1,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle d_{i0k\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&k=\ell,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle d_{ij0\ell}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}1,&j=\ell,\\
0,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

\displaystyle d_{ijk0}

\displaystyle=1.

Our first observation is

1=\min_{\ell=1,\ldots,N}(d_{ij0\ell}+d_{i0k\ell}+d_{0jk\ell}),

which implies
d_{ijk0}\leqslant d_{ij0\ell}+d_{i0k\ell}+d_{0jk\ell}=:S_{\ell}

for all
i,j,k,\ell
.
In fact, we find

\displaystyle S_{\ell}=\begin{cases}1+1+0,&k=j=\ell,\\
0+0+1,&k=j\neq\ell,\\
1+0+1,&k\neq j=\ell,\\
0+1+1,&j\neq\ell=k,\\
0+0+1,&k\neq j\neq\ell\neq k.\end{cases}

Further, the simplicial inequalities

\displaystyle d_{ij0\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{ijk0}+d_{i0k\ell}+d_{0jk\ell},

(6.11)

\displaystyle d_{i0k\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{ijk0}+d_{ij0\ell}+d_{0jk\ell}.

\displaystyle d_{0jk\ell}

\displaystyle\leqslant d_{ijk0}+d_{ij0\ell}+d_{i0k\ell},

hold, because
d_{ijk0}=1
and all metric values lie in
\{0,1\}
.

By the definition of
d_{ijk\ell}
and
N\geqslant 2
we also obtain

\displaystyle\forall i\quad\exists j,\ell

\displaystyle:d_{ij0\ell}=0;\quad\forall j\quad\exists i,\ell:d_{ij0\ell}=0;\quad\forall\ell\quad\exists i,j:d_{ij0\ell}=0;

\displaystyle\forall i\quad\exists k,\ell

\displaystyle:d_{i0k\ell}=0;\quad\forall k\quad\exists i,\ell:d_{i0k\ell}=0;\quad\forall\ell\quad\exists i,k:d_{i0k\ell}=0;

\displaystyle\forall j\quad\exists k,\ell

\displaystyle:d_{0jk\ell}=0;\quad\forall k\quad\exists j,\ell:d_{0jk\ell}=0;\quad\forall\ell\quad\exists j,k:d_{0jk\ell}=0.

Following the definition of
d_{H}
in (
6.10
) this
implies

\displaystyle d_{H}(A_{1},A_{2},A_{4})

\displaystyle=\max\big(\max_{i}\min_{j,\ell}d_{ij0\ell},\max_{j}\min_{i,\ell}d_{ij0\ell},\max_{\ell}\min_{i,j}d_{ij0\ell}\big)=0,

\displaystyle d_{H}(A_{1},A_{3},A_{4})

\displaystyle=\max\big(\max_{i}\min_{k,\ell}d_{i0k\ell},\max_{k}\min_{i,\ell}d_{i0k\ell},\max_{\ell}\min_{i,k}d_{i0k\ell}\big)=0,

\displaystyle d_{H}(A_{2},A_{3},A_{4})

\displaystyle=\max\big(\max_{j}\min_{k,\ell}d_{0jk\ell},\max_{k}\min_{j,\ell}d_{0jk\ell},\max_{\ell}\min_{j,k}d_{0jk\ell}\big)=0.

Note that we also have

\displaystyle 1=\max\big(\max_{i}(\min_{j,k}d_{ijk0}),\max_{j}(\min_{i,k}d_{ijk0}),\max_{k}(\min_{i,j}d_{ijk0})\big)=d_{H}(A_{1},A_{2},A_{3}).

This contradicts
the simplicial inequality (
5

A_{\nu}
. It remains to define the following quantities

\displaystyle d_{i,j\iota,00}=d(w_{i},x_{j},x_{\iota})

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle d_{i00,\ell\lambda}=d(w_{i},z_{\ell},z_{\lambda})
