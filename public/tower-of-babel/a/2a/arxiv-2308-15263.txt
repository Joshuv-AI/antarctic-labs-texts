Asymptotic Plateau problem via equidistant hyperplanes
Han Hong, Haizhong Li, Meng Zhang

Abstract
. We show the existence of a complete, strictly locally convex hypersurface within ℍ n + 1 \mathbb{H}^{n+1} that adheres to a curvature equation applicable to a broad range of curvature functions. This hypersurface possesses a prescribed asymptotic boundary at infinity and takes the form of a geodesic graph over a smooth bounded domain Ω \Omega at infinity. It is approximated by the shape of geodesic graphs whose boundaries rest upon equidistant hyperplanes. Through this procedure, we establish an alternative method for constructing solutions to the asymptotic Plateau problem. The resulting solutions may differ from the classical ones, particularly in cases where uniqueness cannot be assured.



1.
Introduction

In this paper we investigate a prescribing curvature problem with boundary sitting on the equidistant hyperplane in hyperbolic space, and apply this result to obtain
the existence of the hypersurface of constant curvature with prescribed asymptotic boundary at infinity.

Let

\mathbb{H}^{n+1}=\{(z,z_{n+1})\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:\ z_{n+1}>0,z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\}

with metric

g^{\mathbb{H}^{n+1}}=\frac{1}{z_{n+1}^{2}}g^{\mathbb{R}^{n+1}}

be the
n+1
dimensional hyperbolic space with the half space model. Let
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
denote the ideal boundary of
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
at infinity, which can be identified with
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times{0}
in the half space model. We assume that
\Gamma
is a compact
(n-1)
-dimensional embedded hypersurface in
\partial_{\infty}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, which can be viewed as the boundary of a smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The compactness of
\Gamma
is considered with respect to the topology in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The asymptotic Plateau problem aims to find a complete hypersurface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
satisfying the following conditions:

(1.1)

f(\kappa[\Sigma])=\sigma

with

(1.2)

\partial\Sigma=\Gamma

Here,
\kappa[\Sigma]=(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
denotes the hyperbolic principal curvatures of
\Sigma
, and
f
is a smooth symmetric function of
n
variables. The constant
\sigma
is a given positive value.

[

[

started to think of desired (minimal) objects as graphs over mean convex domains at infinity, then proved the existence by applying techniques from PDE. This idea has been applied very well in last few decades to get generalizations of previous results. For special curvature functions such as mean curvature and Gauss curvature, in a series of works by Nelli-Spruck
[

by Guan, Spruck, Szapiel and Xiao. See also recent works by Wang
[

and Lu
[

in which they find a solution for some particular curvature functions in a general cone. When the curvature function of the hypersurface is equal to a given non-costant function defined in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, Sui
[

and together with Sun

proved the existence under the assumption that the sbsolution exists.

Above mentioned results in previous paragraph are all based on upper half space model and share similar spirit, that is to first seek the existence of compact hypersurfaces of constant curvature with boundary sitting in a horizontal horosphere that has small Euclidean height to the infinity. This requirement is essential in the proof of gradient estiamtes and boundary curvature estimates. We also need this assumption in our context. Furthermore, in the Minkowski model Lopez

It is well-known that there exist four different types of complete umbilical hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space:
(1) geodesic sphere (
\kappa_{i}>1
);
(2) horosphere (
\kappa_{i}=1

(3) equidistant hyperplane (
\kappa_{i}<1
);
(4) geodesic hyperplane (

We will concentrate on equidistant hyperplanes for seeking the existence of compact hypersurfaces of constant curvature throughout this paper, for convenience we choose,
0<\epsilon<\pi/2,

(1.3)

P_{\epsilon}=\{(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n+1})\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}|z_{n}>0,\ \cos{\epsilon}z_{n+1}=\sin{\epsilon}z_{n}\}.

This is a equidistant hyperplane with constant curvature equal to
\cos\epsilon
(see Definition
2.1
).

In this paper, we shall focus on the existence of locally convex hypersurface, that is, the (hyperbolic) principal curvatures are positive everywhere. The class of interest, denoted by
\mathscr{C}_{n}
, consists of smooth symmetric functions defined on the closure of the positive cone
K_{n}^{+}:=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\lambda_{i}>0,\ \forall i=1,\ldots,n\},
which satisfy

(1.4)

f=0\text{ on }\partial K_{n}^{+},

(1.5)

\displaystyle f_{i}(\lambda)=\frac{\partial f}{\partial\lambda_{i}}>0,\ \ \forall 1\leq i\leq n,

(1.6)

f\text{ is a concave function },

(1.7)

f\ \text{is normalized}:f(1,\cdots,1)=1,

(1.8)

f\ \text{is homogeneous of degree one:}\ f(t\lambda)=tf(\lambda)\text{, }\forall\ t>0.

Also important is the subclass
\mathscr{S}_{n}
consisting of functions in
\mathscr{C}_{n}
satisfying a technical assumption:

(1.9)

\lim_{R\to+\infty}f(\lambda_{1},\cdots,\lambda_{n-1},\lambda_{n}+R)\geq 1+\epsilon_{0}\text{ uniformly in }B_{\delta_{0}}(\mathbb{1}),

where
\epsilon_{0}>0
and
\delta_{0}>0
,

is the ball of radius
\delta_{0}
centered at
\mathbb{1}=(1,\cdots,1)\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Note that the functions in

are positive in

since
f(\lambda)=\sum\lambda_{i}f_{i}>0
. The assumption (

) is purely for deriving a normal second derivative estimate. This can be removed to find the existence using ideas from Xiao
[
9
]
which we will mention before Corollary
1.3
.

One can check the standard example
f=(\frac{H_{n}}{H_{l}})^{\frac{1}{n-l}}
,
0\leq l<n
is in
\mathscr{S}_{n}
, where

is the normalized
l
-th elementary symmetric polynomial, and the proof of monontonicity and concavity can be found in Theorem 15.17 and 15.18 in
[
17
]
.
Combining (
1.6
), (
1.7
), (
1.8
), following two inequalities hold.

(1.10)

f(\lambda)\leq f(\mathbb{1})+\sum f_{i}(\mathbb{1})(\lambda_{i}-1)=\sum f_{i}(\mathbb{1})\lambda_{i}=\frac{1}{n}\sum\lambda_{i}\text{ in }K_{n}^{+}

(1.11)

\sum f_{i}(\lambda)=f(\lambda)+\sum f_{i}(\lambda)(1-\lambda_{i})\geq f(\mathbb{1})=1\text{ in }K_{n}^{+}.

Compared with previous works, we deal with angle functions that naturally appear if we consider geodesic graph (see Definition
2.2
) instead of vertical graph. Suppose
\Sigma
is a connected, orientable compact embedded hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. For any point
q=(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n},z_{n+1})\in\Sigma
, let
\gamma(q)
be the complete hyperbolic geodesic (Euclidean half circle) normal to
P_{\epsilon}
with the intersection point
q
. Then
\gamma(q)
intersects with
\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
at
(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})
and let
u(y)=\arctan{\frac{z_{n+1}}{z_{n}}}
be the Euclidean angle of
p
and
q
.

Assume that
\Sigma
is a geodesic graph over a bounded domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\{0\}
, the asymptotic Plateau problem
(\ref{equation1introduction})-(\ref{boundaryconditionintroduction})
can be written as the following Dirichlet problem for a fully nonlinear second order equation
(u>0)
:

(1.12)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}G(D^{2}u,Du,u,y)=\sigma&\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
u=0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

As we will see in Section
2
, above equation is singular where
u=0
. We then approximate the boundary condition by
u=\epsilon
on the boundary, and thus concentrate on the following new equation

(1.13)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}G(D^{2}u,Du,u,y)=\sigma&\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
u=\epsilon&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega.\end{cases}

We call solution to
(\ref{graph equation})
or
(\ref{newboundarycondition})
admissible if
graph(u)
is strictly convex, i.e., hyperbolic principal curvatures are strictly positive. Our theorem can be described as

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f\in\mathscr{S}_{n}
. Then for any
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, there exists an admissible solution
u^{\epsilon}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
of (
1.13
) for any
\sigma\in(0,\cos\epsilon)
. Moreover,
u^{\epsilon}
satisfies the a priori estimates

(1.14)

|Du^{\epsilon}|\leq C\text{ in }\Omega

and

(1.15)

\sin{u^{\epsilon}}\cdot|D^{2}u^{\epsilon}|\leq C\text{ in }\Omega

where
C
is independent of
\epsilon
.

Based on this theorem and the standard procedures, we are able to give a solution to the asymptotic Plateau problem.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded smooth domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with boundary
\Gamma
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
f\in\mathscr{S}_{n}
. Then there exists a complete locally strictly convex hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
satisfying (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with uniformly bounded principal curvatures

|\kappa[\Sigma]|\leq C\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Sigma.

Moreover,
\Sigma
is the graph of an admissible solution
u\in C^{\infty}(u)\cap C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
of the Dirichlet problem (
1.12
). Furthermore,
u^{2}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega})
and

\sqrt{1+z_{n}^{2}|Du|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \sqrt{1+z_{n}^{2}|Du|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\ \text{in}\ \Omega,

\sin u\cdot|D^{2}u|\leq C\ \ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega.

The curvature estimate
(1)
in Theorem
5.1
and the boundary curvature estimate in Theorem
4.1
demonstrate that the hyperbolic principal curvatures of the admissible solutions of equation (
1.13
) are uniformly bounded from above, regardless of
\epsilon
. Additionally, due to condition (
1.4
) of the class

, the hyperbolic principal curvatures also have a uniform positive lower bound, independent of

. Lemma
3.4
provides a lower bound for admissible solutions on compact subsets of

, ensuring that equation (
1.12
) is uniformly elliptic on such subsets. By applying the classical interior estimates of Evans and Krylov, we conclude that the admissible solutions possess uniform

estimates for any compact subdomain of
\Omega
. Ultimately, employing a diagonal sequence and standard arguments, we can establish the existence of a solution in Theorem
1.2
for any
0<\sigma<1
. It is important to note that the diagonal process also needs to be performed for
\sigma
, as we only have existence for
0<\sigma<\cos\epsilon
in the compact case.

The technical assumption (
1.9
) is added for proving the boundary estimates for the second derivatives. We can borrow idea from Xiao
[
9
]
to abandon this assumption. In fact, the global interior estimates also hold for hypersurfaces with asymptotic boundary as shown in part (2) of Theorem
5.1
. For general curvature function
f\in\mathscr{C}_{n}
, let
f^{\theta}:=\theta g+(1-\theta)f
where

, then

. Thus we can use Theorem
1.2
to get a complete locally strictly convex hypersurface

as a graph of

. Moreover, derivatives of
u^{\theta}
have bounds independent of
\theta
. Thus letting
\theta
tend to zero proves Theorem
1.2
without assumption (
1.9
). Moreover, the domain
\Omega
does not need to smooth by a standard approximation argument.

.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded
C^{2}
domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with boundary
\Gamma
and
\sigma\in(0,1)
. Suppose that
f\in\mathscr{C}_{n}
. Then there exists a complete locally strictly convex hypersurface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
satisfying (
1.1
)-(
1.2
) with uniformly bounded principal curvatures

|\kappa|\leq C\ \ \text{on}\ \ \Sigma.

Moreover,
\Sigma
is the graph of an admissible solution
u\in C^{\infty}(u)\cap C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
of the Dirichlet problem (
1.12
). Furthermore,
u^{2}\in C^{\infty}(\Omega)\cap C^{1,1}(\bar{\Omega})
and

\sqrt{1+z_{n}^{2}|Du|^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma}\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \sqrt{1+z_{n}^{2}|Du|^{2}}\leq\frac{1}{\sigma}\ \text{in}\ \Omega,

\sin u\cdot|D^{2}u|\leq C\ \ \ \text{in}\ \ \Omega.

Uniqueness results were obtained in
[
13
,
9
]
if
one of followings holds:

•

either
\Gamma
is
C^{2,\alpha}
and (Euclidean) mean convex,

•

or
\Gamma
is
C^{2}
and star-shaped about the origin,

•

or
\sum f_{i}>\sum\kappa_{i}^{2}f_{i}
in

is satisfied.

Thus under these assumptions, our solutions in Theorem
1.2
and Corollary

are exactly same as the ones obtained in
[
13
,
9
]
. In other cases, we possibly construct different solutions to asymptotic Plateau problem. It is an interesting question to determine whether our procedure provides new solutions in general cases.

The organization of the paper is as follows. In Section
2
, we calculate geometric quantities for a geodesic graph and derive certain identities that will be used later. Section
3
is dedicated to establishing basic identities on a hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, which will serve as the foundation for deriving gradient estimates. Additionally, we provide a proof of
C^{0}
estimates for the geodesic graph with a boundary located on equidistant hyperplanes. To prove the boundary estimates for the second derivatives, we employ a maximum principle for the partial linearized operator around boundary points. This analysis is presented in Section
4
. In Section
5
, we focus on proving two estimates for the maximum hyperbolic principal curvature. One estimate pertains to compact hypersurfaces, while the other relates to global interior estimates for complete noncompact hypersurfaces. We demonstrate that their proofs can be unified through Theorem
5.1
. Lastly, in the final section, we utilize the degree theory developed by Y.Y. Li to obtain a solution for the approximation equation. We note that the linearized operator may not always be invertible over the time interval
[0,1]
, rendering the method of continuity insufficient for the proof.

2.
Preliminaries

Consider the space

\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n+1}=\{(z_{1},\cdots,z_{n},z_{n+1}):z_{n+1}>0\}
with the hyperbolic metric

g=\frac{dz_{1}^{2}+\cdots+dz_{n+1}^{2}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}.

Suppose
\Sigma
can be locally represented as the geodesic graph of a smooth function
u>0
in a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
where
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}=\{y=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n}):y_{n}>0\}.
That is

\Sigma=\{(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n}\cos u,y_{n}\sin u)\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n+1}:\ y\in\Omega\}.

As mentioned in the introduction,
u
is the angle function that represents the angle between the point on the graph and the domain. Denote the canonical basis of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by
E_{1},\cdots,E_{n+1}.

In this scenario, the map
F(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n})=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n-1},y_{n}\cos u,y_{n}\sin u)
sends
\partial_{y_{i}}
and
\partial_{y_{n}}
to

F_{i}=E_{i}-(y_{n}u_{i}\sin u)E_{n}+(y_{n}u_{i}\cos u)E_{n+1},\ \ \ i=1,\ldots,n-1.

and

F_{n}=(\cos u-y_{n}u_{n}\sin u)E_{n}+(\sin u+y_{n}u_{n}\cos u)E_{n+1}.

Thus the (Euclidean) unit normal vector field to
\Sigma
is

\nu=\frac{(-y_{n}u_{1},\cdots,-y_{n}u_{n-1},-\sin u-y_{n}u_{n}\cos u,\cos u-y_{n}u_{n}\sin u)}{\omega}

where

\omega=\sqrt{1+y_{n}^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}}.

The induced Euclidean metric and second fundamental form of
\Sigma
are given by

g^{E}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+y^{2}_{n}u_{i}u_{j}

and

h_{ij}^{E}=\frac{\delta_{nj}u_{i}+\delta_{ni}u_{j}+y_{n}^{2}u_{i}u_{j}u_{n}+y_{n}u_{ij}}{\omega}.

According to
[
5
]
, the Euclidean principal curvature are the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix
A^{E}[u]=[a_{ij}^{E}]
. Here

a^{E}_{ij}=\gamma^{ik}h_{ij}^{E}\gamma^{lj}

where
\gamma^{ij}
is the inverse matrix of
\gamma_{ij}
which is the square root of the Riemmanian metric

, i.e.,
\gamma_{ij}\gamma_{jk}=g^{E}_{ik}
. Then it is not hard to see that

\gamma_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+\frac{y_{n}^{2}u_{i}u_{j}}{1+\omega}

and

\gamma^{ij}=\delta_{ij}-\frac{z_{n}^{2}u_{i}u_{i}}{\omega(1+\omega)}.

From the relation between hyperbolic and Euclidean principal curvature (see equation (
3.18
)), we get that the hyperbolic principal curvatures of the hypersurface
\Sigma
are the eigenvalues of the matrix
A[u]=[a_{ij}]
. Here,

a_{ij}=\frac{y_{n}z_{n+1}}{\omega}\gamma^{ik}u_{kl}\gamma^{\ell j}+\frac{y^{2}_{n}z_{n+1}}{\omega^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}u_{n}+\frac{z_{n+1}}{\omega^{2}}(\gamma^{in}u_{j}+\gamma^{jn}u_{i})+\nu_{n+1}\delta_{ij}.

In the calculation we have used that
\gamma^{ij}u_{j}=\frac{u_{i}}{\omega}
. Note that
\nu_{n+1}=\frac{\cos u-y_{n}u_{n}\sin u}{\omega}
and

z_{n+1}=y_{n}\sin u.

We define a vector field in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}

(2.16)

W((z_{1},\cdots,z_{n},z_{n+1}))=(0,\cdots,0,\sin\theta,-\cos\theta),

where
\cot\theta=z_{n}/z_{n+1}
. This vector is perpendicular to the equidistant hyperplane that passes through the origin and forms the angle
\theta
with the infinity (see definition below). For the convenience of the discussion, we define

Definition 2.1
.

Throughout this paper, a (equidistant) hyperplane in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}_{+}
is called

-hyperplane, denoted by
P_{\theta}
,
if it passes through the origin and forms an angle

with
\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
.

It is known that

-hyperplane is umbillic and has constant principal curvature

in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
.

Definition 2.2
.

We say that
\Sigma
is a geodesic graph over a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
if

\nu\cdot W<0,\ \ \forall\ x\in\Omega.

Let
\mathscr{M}
be the vector space of
n\times n
symmetric matrices and

\mathscr{M}_{K}=\{A\in\mathscr{S}:\lambda(A)\in K\},

where
\lambda(A)=\left(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\right)
denotes the eigenvalues of
A
. Define a function
F
by

F(A)=f(\lambda(A)),\quad A\in\mathscr{M}_{K}.

Throughout the paper we denote

F^{ij}(A)=\frac{\partial F}{\partial a_{ij}}(A),\quad F^{ij,kl}(A)=\frac{\partial^{2}F}{\partial a_{ij}\partial a_{kl}}(A).

The matrix
\left\{F^{ij}(A)\right\}
is symmetric and its eigenvalues are
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
, and therefore it is positive definite for
A\in\mathscr{M}_{K}
if

satisfies
(\ref{fi>0})
, while

implies that

, that is

F^{ij,kl}(A)\xi_{ij}\xi_{kl}\leq 0,\quad\forall\left\{\xi_{ij}\right\}\in\mathscr{M},A\in\mathscr{M}_{K}.

We have that

\displaystyle F^{ij}(A)a_{ij}

\displaystyle=\sum f_{i}(\lambda(A))\lambda_{i},

\displaystyle F^{ij}(A)a_{ik}a_{jk}

\displaystyle=\sum f_{i}(\lambda(A))\lambda_{i}^{2}.

Now, recall the function

. We calculate that

implies the equation

is elliptic for

The hyperbolic metric

Proposition 3.1
.

Under above setting, we have
that for
i,j=1,\cdots,n

\displaystyle\nabla^{E}_{ij}\nu_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{E}_{ik}h^{E}_{kj}\nu_{n}+(\nabla^{E}_{k}h^{E}_{ij})(z_{n})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}+\frac{2h^{E}_{ij}(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n})_{k}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{h^{E}_{kj}((z_{n+1})_{i}(z_{n})_{k}-(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n})_{i})}{z^{3}_{n+1}}+\frac{2h^{E}_{ik}((z_{n+1})_{j}(z_{n})_{k}-(z_{n})_{j}(z_{n+1})_{k})}{z^{3}_{n+1}}

and

\displaystyle\nabla^{E}_{ij}\nu_{n+1}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{E}_{ik}h^{E}_{kj}\nu_{n+1}+(\nabla^{E}_{k}h^{E}_{ij})(z_{n+1})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}+\frac{2h^{E}_{ij}(z_{n+1})_{k}^{2}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}.

Proof.

First, we have that for
\ell=1,\cdots,n+1,

(3.20)

\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{i}}\nu_{l}=-\frac{h_{ik}^{E}(z_{l})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}

and

(3.21)

\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{i}}\partial_{j}=\frac{(z_{n+1})_{i}\partial_{j}+(z_{n+1})_{j}\partial_{i}-\delta_{ij}(z_{n+1})_{m}\partial_{m}}{z_{n+1}},

in particular,
\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{i}}\partial_{j}=\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{j}}\partial_{i}.

Moreover,

\displaystyle\nabla^{E}_{kj}z_{\ell}

\displaystyle=h^{E}_{jk}\nu_{l}+\frac{<\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{j}}\partial_{k},\partial_{m}>(z_{l})_{m}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}

(3.22)

\displaystyle=h^{E}_{jk}\nu_{l}+\frac{(z_{n+1})_{j}(z_{l})_{k}+(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{l})_{j}-\delta_{jk}(z_{n+1})_{m}(z_{l})_{m}}{z_{n+1}}

By Codazzi equation, we have

(3.23)

h^{E}_{ikj}=h^{E}_{ijk}-h^{E}(\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{k}}\partial_{i},\partial_{j})+h^{E}(\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{j}}\partial_{i},\partial_{k})

Then it follows from (
3.20
),(
3.21
),(
3.1
) and (
3.23
) that

\displaystyle\nabla^{E}_{ij}\nu_{l}

\displaystyle=(\nu_{l})_{ij}-(\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{i}}\partial_{j})\nu_{l}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{E}_{ikj}(z_{l})_{k}+h^{E}_{ik}(z_{l})_{kj}}{z^{2}_{n+1}}+2\frac{h^{E}_{ik}(z_{l})_{k}(z_{n+1})_{j}}{z_{n+1}^{3}}+\frac{h^{E}(\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{i}}\partial_{j},\partial_{k})}{z^{2}_{n+1}}(z_{l})_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{E}_{ik}h^{E}_{kj}\nu_{l}+(\nabla^{E}_{k}h^{E}_{ij})(z_{l})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}+\frac{2h^{E}_{ik}((z_{n+1})_{j}(z_{l})_{k}-(z_{n+1})_{j}(z_{n+1})_{k})}{z^{3}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{h^{E}_{ij}(z_{n+1})_{m}^{2}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}+\frac{h^{E}(\nabla^{E}_{\partial_{k}}\partial_{i},\partial_{j})}{z^{2}_{n+1}}(z_{l})_{k}

\displaystyle=-\frac{h^{E}_{ik}h^{E}_{kj}\nu_{l}+(\nabla^{E}_{k}h^{E}_{ij})(z_{l})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}+\frac{2h^{E}_{ij}(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{l})_{k}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{h^{E}_{kj}((z_{n+1})_{i}(z_{l})_{k}-(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{l})_{i})}{z^{3}_{n+1}}+\frac{h^{E}_{ik}((z_{n+1})_{j}(z_{l})_{k}-(z_{l})_{j}(z_{n+1})_{k})}{z^{3}_{n+1}}.

This yields identities in the Proposition for
\ell=n
and
\ell=n+1
.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
satisfying
f(\kappa)=\sigma
and
f\in\mathscr{C}_{n}
. Then in any local frame, we have

(3.24)

F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\cot u=(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)\sigma+\cot u\sum f_{i}

and

(3.25)

F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)=-\sigma\cot u-(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}.

Proof.

Since
\cot u=\frac{z_{n}}{z_{n+1}}
, we have that

\partial_{j}\cot u=\frac{(z_{n})_{j}z_{n+1}-(z_{n+1})_{j}z_{n}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}

thus

\displaystyle\nabla^{E}_{ij}\cot u

\displaystyle=\frac{(z_{n})_{j}(z_{n+1})_{i}-(z_{n})_{i}(z_{n+1})_{j}+z_{n+1}h^{E}_{ij}\nu_{n}-z_{n}h_{ij}^{E}\nu_{n+1}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}

\displaystyle+\frac{-2z_{n+1}^{2}(z_{n+1})_{i}(z_{n})_{j}+2z_{n}z_{n+1}(z_{n+1})_{i}(z_{n+1})_{j}}{z_{n+1}^{4}}.

Furthermore, by (
3.19
) we get that

\displaystyle\nabla_{ij}\cot u

\displaystyle=\frac{z_{n+1}\nu_{n}-z_{n}\nu_{n+1}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}h_{ij}^{E}-\frac{z_{n+1}(z_{n})_{k}(z_{n+1})_{k}-z_{n}(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n+1})_{k}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}\delta_{ij}.

Since
h^{E}_{ij}=z_{n+1}h_{ij}-z_{n+1}\nu_{n+1}\delta_{ij}
and

(z_{n})_{k}(z_{n+1})_{k}=-z_{n+1}^{2}\nu_{n}\nu_{n+1},

(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n+1})_{k}=z_{n+1}^{2}(1-\nu_{n+1}^{2}).

Hence

\nabla_{ij}\cot u=(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)h_{ij}+\cot u\delta_{ij}.

This proves the first identity (
3.24
).

We now continue to prove the second one. We calculate
\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n}
and
\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n+1}
at first.

It follows from (
3.19
) that

\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n}=\nabla_{ij}^{E}\nu_{n}+\frac{1}{z_{n+1}}[(z_{n+1})_{i}(\nu_{n})_{j}+(z_{n+1})_{j}(\nu_{n})_{i}-(z_{n+1})_{k}(\nu_{n})_{k}\delta_{ij}].

Then

(3.26)

\displaystyle F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\nu_{n}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}F^{ij}h^{E}_{ik}h^{E}_{kj}-\frac{(z_{n})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}F^{ij}\nabla^{E}_{k}h^{E}_{ij}+\frac{2}{z_{n+1}}F^{ij}(z_{n+1})_{i}(\nu_{n})_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{2F^{ij}h^{E}_{ij}(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n})_{k}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}-\frac{1}{z_{n+1}}(z_{n+1})_{k}(\nu_{n})_{k}\sum F^{ii}.

By differentiating the equation

\sigma=F(h_{ij})=F(\{h^{E}_{ij}/u+\nu_{n+1}\delta_{ij}\}),

we obtain

F^{ij}(z_{n+1}\nabla_{k}^{E}h^{E}_{ij}-(z_{n+1})_{k}h^{E}_{ij}+(\nu_{n+1})_{k}z_{n+1}^{2}\delta_{ij})=0.

Thus, by (
3.17
) we have

F^{ij}\nabla_{k}^{E}h^{E}_{ij}=(z_{n+1})_{k}(\sigma-\nu_{n+1}\sum F^{ii})-(\nu_{n+1})_{k}z_{n+1}\sum F^{ii}.

Furthermore,

\displaystyle F^{ij}h^{E}_{ik}h^{E}_{kj}

\displaystyle=z_{n+1}^{2}F^{ij}(h_{ik}-\nu_{n+1}\delta_{ik})(h_{kj}-\nu_{n+1}\delta_{kj})

\displaystyle=z^{2}_{n+1}(\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}-2\sigma\nu_{n+1}+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\sum f_{i}).

Hence, it follows from (
3.26
) and Proposition
3.1
that

(3.27)

\displaystyle F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n}

\displaystyle=-\nu_{n}(\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}-2\sigma\nu_{n+1}+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\sum f_{i})+\frac{2}{z_{n+1}}F^{ij}(z_{n+1})_{i}(\nu_{n})_{j}

\displaystyle+\frac{(z_{n})_{k}(\nu_{n+1})_{k}-(z_{n+1})_{k}(\nu_{n})_{k}}{z_{n+1}}\sum F^{ii}+\frac{(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}(\sigma-\nu_{n+1}\sum F^{ii})

\displaystyle+2\left(\frac{F^{ij}h^{E}_{ik}((z_{n+1})_{j}(z_{n})_{k}-(z_{n})_{j}(z_{n+1})_{k})}{z^{3}_{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\nu_{n}(\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}-2\sigma\nu_{n+1}+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\sum f_{i})-\frac{2F^{ij}h^{E}_{ik}(z_{n})_{j}(z_{n+1})_{k}}{z^{3}_{n+1}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n})_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}(\sigma-\nu_{n+1}\sum F^{ii})

where we have used (
3.20
) in the second equality.

Similarly, we can calculate by following above lines to obtain

(3.28)

\displaystyle F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n+1}

\displaystyle=-\nu_{n+1}(\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}-2\sigma\nu_{n+1}+\nu_{n+1}^{2}\sum f_{i})

\displaystyle+\frac{2}{z_{n+1}}F^{ij}(z_{n+1})_{i}(\nu_{n+1})_{j}+\frac{(z_{n+1})^{2}_{k}}{z_{n+1}^{2}}(\sigma-\nu_{n+1}\sum F^{ii}).

Finally, since

\sum(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n})_{k}=z^{2}_{n+1}(E_{n+1}-\nu_{n+1}\nu)(E_{n}-\nu_{n}\nu)=-z^{2}_{n+1}\nu_{n}\nu_{n+1},

\sum(z_{n+1})_{k}(z_{n+1})_{k}=z^{2}_{n+1}(1-\nu_{n+1}^{2}),

it follows from (
3.24
),(
3.27
),
3.28
) and (
3.20
) that

\displaystyle F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)

\displaystyle=F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n}-\cot uF^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\nu_{n+1}-\nu_{n+1}f^{ij}\nabla_{ij}\cot u

\displaystyle-2F^{ij}(\nu_{n+1})_{i}(\cot u)_{j}

\displaystyle=-\sigma\cot u-(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}.

This gives the equation (
3.25
), thus completing the proof of the theorem.

∎

3.2.
C^{0}
estimate and gradient estimate

A hypersurface in hyperbolic space
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
with a constant curvature function has an equidistant hyperplane as a natural barrier. An equidistant hyperplane with a constant curvature
\sigma\in(0,1)
can either be a Euclidean half-hyperplane that intersects infinity at an angle of
\theta=\arccos\sigma
, or a hypersphere with a special center and radius. In this context, we utilize the latter to establish several bounds for the function
u
, whose graph constitutes the hypersurface.

Fix
\epsilon>0
a small positive number. Let
\Sigma
be an orientable, embedded hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
with
\partial\Sigma\subset P_{\epsilon}
(see Definition
2.1
) such that it separates
P_{\epsilon}^{+}=\{(z,\theta)\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}:\theta>\epsilon\}
into a bounded region and an unbounded region. Let
\Omega
be the bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{n}\times\{0\}
such that its rotation by angle
\epsilon
to
P_{\epsilon}
, denoted by
\Omega_{\epsilon}
, has boundary
\partial\Sigma.
Assume that
\kappa[\Sigma]\in K_{+}
and
f(\kappa[\Sigma])=\sigma\in(0,\cos\epsilon)
. The calculations are done with respect to the unit outer normal that points into the unbounded region. We remind readers that
\partial\Omega
doesn’t have to be connected. In this section, we only require
f
to be in
\mathscr{C}_{n}
, then the linearized operator of the fully nonlinear equation is elliptic so that we can make use of the maximum principle.

We let
B_{1},B_{2}
be balls in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
of radius
R
centered at
a=(a^{\prime},-\sigma R)
and
b=(b^{\prime},\sigma R)
where
\sigma\in(0,\cos\epsilon)
. Then
\partial B_{1}\cap\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
and
\partial B_{2}\cap\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
are hypersurfaces of constant curvature
\sigma
with respect to its outer unit normal and inner unit normal, respectively. We have the following result (c.f.
[
12
]
).

Lemma 3.3
.

The followings hold:

(1),

\Sigma
is contained in the wedge by
P_{\epsilon}
and the vertical geodesic hyperplane through origin.

(2),

If
\partial\Sigma\subset B_{1}
, then
\Sigma\subset B_{1}.

(3),

If
B_{1}\cap P_{\epsilon}\subset\Omega_{\epsilon}
, then
B_{1}\cap\Sigma=\emptyset.

(4),

If
B_{2}\cap\Omega^{\epsilon}=\emptyset
, then
B_{2}\cap\Sigma=\emptyset.

Proof.

For (1), If
\Sigma
goes through below
P_{\epsilon}
, we can translate
P_{\epsilon}
along positive
z_{n}
direction far away, then translate it back until there is a touching point violating the maximum principle. Similarly, we can show it does not pass through the vertical geodesic plane through origin. In fact, it does not intersect the vertical geodesic plane through the most left point of
\Omega_{\epsilon}
. The proofs of
(2),(3),(4)
follow exact same lines of
[
12
, Lemma 3.1]
by expanding or shrinking balls with respect to
(a^{\prime},0)
or
(b^{\prime},0).
We can also prove
(4)
by translating
B_{2}
horizontally and using maximum principle. The key to prove all above statements is to avoid interior touching point with same directions of curvature.
∎

The conclusion (1) of Lemma
3.3
implies natural bounds:
\epsilon<u<\pi/2
. However, this is not enough, we need an upper bound depending on
\Omega
and
\epsilon
that is away from
\pi/2
, a lower bound depending on point
z
,
\epsilon
and
\Omega
that is uniform away from zero as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
in compact subsets of
\Omega
.

Let
d(\Omega)
be the Euclidean diameter of
\Omega
and let
D_{d(\Omega)/2}(p)
be the n-dimensional ball in
P_{\epsilon}
containing
\Omega_{\epsilon}
centered at
p\in P_{\epsilon}
. Let
\ell(\Omega)
be the distance between
p
and
\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, i.e.,
\ell(\Omega)=p\cdot z_{n}
. For a fixed point
z\in\Omega
, denote
d(z)=\operatorname{dist}(z,\partial\Omega)
and
\ell(z)=z\cdot E_{n}
. Let
D_{d(z)}(\tilde{z})
be the n-dimensional ball in
P_{\epsilon}
centered at
\tilde{z}
(this is corresponding point of
z
in
\Omega_{\epsilon}
) of radius
d(z)
that is contained in
\Omega_{\epsilon}
.

Lemma 3.4
.

There exist positive constants
C_{1}
depending on
\epsilon,d(\Omega),\ell(\Omega)
and
\sigma
such that

u\leq C_{1}<\frac{\pi}{2}

and
C_{2}
depending on
\epsilon,d(z),\ell(z)
and
\sigma
such that

u(z)\geq C_{2}>\epsilon

in compact subsets of
\Omega.

Proof.

Let
B_{1}
be a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
centered at
a=(a^{\prime},-\sigma R)
with radius R such that
B_{1}\cap P_{\epsilon}=D_{d(\Omega)/2}(p)
. By (2) of Lemma
3.3
,
\Sigma
is completely contained in
B_{1}\cap P_{\epsilon}^{+}
. Then

u\leq\bar{u}

where
\bar{u}
is the maximal angle of point on
\partial B_{1}\cap P_{\epsilon}^{+}
satisfying

\cos\bar{u}=\frac{\sigma R}{R-(\ell(\Omega)-\sigma R\tan\bar{u})\sin\bar{u}}

equivalent to

\ell(\Omega)\sin\bar{u}+\sigma R\cos\bar{u}=R.

So

(3.29)

\cos\bar{u}=\frac{\sigma R^{2}+\ell\sqrt{\ell^{2}+\sigma^{2}R^{2}-R^{2}}}{\sigma^{2}R^{2}+\ell^{2}}<1.

By simple Euclidean geometry,
R
satisfies

(\ell(\Omega)\tan\epsilon+\sigma R/\cos\epsilon)^{2}+(d(\Omega)/2)^{2}=R^{2}.

This implies that

(3.30)

\frac{\sigma\ell\sin\epsilon}{\cos^{2}\epsilon-\sigma^{2}}+\frac{d\cos\epsilon}{2\sqrt{\cos^{2}\epsilon-\sigma^{2}}}\leq R\leq\frac{\ell\sin\epsilon}{\cos\epsilon-\sigma}+\frac{d\cos\epsilon}{2\sqrt{\cos^{2}\epsilon-\sigma^{2}}}.

It is not hard to check that the right-hand side of (
3.29
) attains the maximum at the lower bound of
R
. Thus a uniform upper bound follows from (
3.29
) and (
3.30
).

For the lower bound, for fixed
z\in\Omega
. Now let
B_{1}
be a ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
centered at
a=(a^{\prime},-\sigma R)
of radius R such that
B_{1}\cap P_{\epsilon}=D_{d(z)}(\tilde{z})
. By (3) of Lemma
3.3
,
\Sigma
is completely outside of
B_{1}
. Then

u(z)\geq\epsilon+\arctan\left(\frac{R}{\ell(z)}(1-\frac{\sigma}{\cos\epsilon})-\tan\epsilon\right)

where, by Euclidean geometry again,
R
satisfies

(\ell(z)\tan\epsilon+\sigma R/\cos\epsilon)^{2}+(d(z)/2)^{2}=R^{2}

which yields

R\geq\frac{\sigma\ell(z)\sin\epsilon}{\cos^{2}\epsilon-\sigma^{2}}+\frac{d(z)\cos\epsilon}{2\sqrt{\cos^{2}\epsilon-\sigma^{2}}}.

This completes the proof of the lower bound.

∎

As mentioned in the introduction, we also need the angle to be sufficiently small to have gradient estimates on the boundary.

Lemma 3.5
.

Assume
\partial\Omega
is
C^{2}
. Then there exists
\epsilon>0
sufficiently small, a positive constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\Omega,r_{2},\epsilon_{0},\sigma)
, a negative constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(\Omega,r_{1},\epsilon_{0},\sigma)
such that for any
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}

C_{2}\leq\sigma\cot u+\frac{\nu\cdot W}{\sin u}\leq C_{1},\ \ \ \text{on}\ \ \partial\Sigma

where
r_{1},r_{2}
are the maximal radii of interior and exterior spheres to
\partial\Omega
, respectively. In particular, as
\epsilon
approaches to zero,
\sigma\cos u+\nu\cdot W
tends to zero.

Proof.

Assume
r_{2}<\infty
. Fix a boundary point
z\in\partial\Omega
. Let
B_{2}
be the ball of radius
R_{2}
centered at
a=(a^{\prime},\sigma R)
such that
B_{2}\cap P_{\epsilon}
is an n-dimensional ball of radius
r_{2}
externally tangent to
\partial\Omega_{\epsilon}
at
\tilde{z}
. By
(4)
of Lemma
3.3
,
\Sigma\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
. Hence, at
\tilde{z}
,

\nu\cdot(-W)\geq\frac{\sqrt{R_{2}^{2}-r_{2}^{2}}}{R_{2}}

where we recall
\nu
is the unit normal of
\Sigma
pointing outward and
W
is defined in (
2.16
).
R_{2}
satisfies

(3.31)

\sigma R_{2}-\tilde{z}_{n+1}=\sqrt{R^{2}_{2}-r^{2}_{2}}\cos\beta+r_{2}\sin\beta

where
\tilde{z}_{n+1}=\tilde{z}\cdot E_{n+1}
is the height of point
\tilde{z}
.
\beta
is the angle between the conormal vector of
\Omega_{\epsilon}\subset P_{\epsilon}
at
\tilde{z}
and horizontal hyperplane through
\tilde{z}
, thus
\beta\in[-\epsilon,\epsilon].
Hence

\nu\cdot(-W)\geq\frac{\sigma R_{2}-\tilde{z}_{n+1}-r_{2}\sin\beta}{R_{2}\cos\beta}.

It follows that on
\partial\Omega,

\displaystyle\sigma\cot u+\frac{\nu\cdot W}{\sin u}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{\sin\epsilon}\frac{\tilde{z}_{n+1}-r_{2}\sin\beta}{R_{2}\cos\beta}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\tilde{z}_{n}+r_{2}}{R_{2}\cos\beta}.

Solving (
3.31
) gives

R_{2}=\frac{\tilde{z}_{n+1}^{2}+r_{2}^{2}+2r_{2}\tilde{z}_{n+1}\sin\beta}{\sigma\tilde{z}_{n+1}+\sigma r_{2}\sin\beta+\cos\beta\sqrt{\tilde{z}^{2}_{n+1}+r_{2}^{2}-r^{2}_{2}\sigma^{2}+2r_{2}\tilde{z}_{n+1}\sin\beta}}

Combining them yields

\sigma\cot u+\frac{\nu\cdot W}{\sin u}\leq\frac{(\tilde{z}_{n}+r_{2})(\tilde{z}_{n}(\sigma/\cos\epsilon_{0}+1)+r_{2}\sqrt{1-\sigma^{2}})}{r_{2}^{2}-2\tilde{z}_{n}r_{2}\sin^{2}\epsilon_{0}}.

As
\Omega
is a bounded domain, we get the desired upper estimate.

On the other hand, we shall deal with the interior sphere to
\partial\Omega
at point
\tilde{z}
. We obtain

\nu\cdot(-W)\leq\frac{\sqrt{R_{1}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}}{R_{1}}.

And
R_{1}
satisfies

\cos\beta\sqrt{R_{1}^{2}-r_{1}^{2}}=z_{n}(q)+\sigma R_{1}

where
q
is the center of n-ball internally tangent to
\partial\Omega_{\epsilon}
at
\tilde{z}.

In a similar manner, we are able to find a negative constant
C_{2}
depending on
\epsilon_{0},\sigma,r_{1}
and
\Omega
such that

\sigma\cot u+\frac{\nu\cdot W}{\sin u}\geq C_{2}.

∎

In order to get the gradient estimates, we need the following proposition.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
satisfying
f(\kappa)=\sigma
where
f\in\mathscr{C}_{n}
. If
\Sigma
can be locally written as a geodesic graph over a domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}_{+}
, that is

\Sigma=\{(z,u(z)):\ z\in\Omega\}
. Then

F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}(\sigma\cot u+\frac{\nu\cdot W}{\sin u})\geq 0.

Proof.

Notice that
(\nu\cdot W)/\sin u=\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u
. It follows from Theorem
3.2
that

\displaystyle F^{ij}\nabla_{ij}(\sigma\cot u+\frac{\nu\cdot W}{\sin u})

\displaystyle=\sigma^{2}(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)+\sigma\cot u\sum f_{i}-\sigma\cot u

\displaystyle-(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}

\displaystyle=(\nu_{n+1}\cot u-\nu_{n})(\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2})+\sigma\cot u(\sum f_{i}-1)

\displaystyle\geq(\sum f_{i}-1)\left(\sigma\cot u+\frac{\sigma^{2}(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u)}{\sum f_{i}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\sigma}{\sin u}(\sum f_{i}-1)(\cos u-\sigma)

where we have used
(\Sigma f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2})(\Sigma f_{i})\geq\sigma^{2}
in the first inequality. To ensure it is nonnegative, it suffices to show that
\cos u\geq\sigma
. In fact, this is an easy comparison between
\sigma
and the equation (
3.29
) in the proof of Lemma
3.4
.
∎

By above proposition and gradient estimates on the boundary, we can obtain global gradient estimates.

Theorem 3.7
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
satisfying
f(\kappa)=\sigma
where
f\in\mathscr{C}_{n}
. Then there exists a constant
C_{3}
such that for sufficiently small
\epsilon
,

|\nabla u|\leq C_{3},\ \ \ \text{in}\ \Omega

where
C
is independent of
\epsilon.

Proof.

Let
g=\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cot u
. Then

(3.32)

g_{i}=-\kappa_{i}\cdot(\cot u)_{i}

for each
i=1,\cdots,n.

Suppose
-1/g
attains the maximum at an interior point
z_{0}.
Then at
z_{0}

-\nabla_{i}\frac{1}{g}=-\frac{\kappa_{i}\cdot(\cot u)_{i}}{g^{2}}=0.

Since
\kappa_{i}
for
i=1,\cdots,n
are positive, Then we have
\nabla\cot u=0
, and thus
\nabla u=0.

So, Lemma
3.5
tells that

\displaystyle-\frac{\sin u}{\nu\cdot W}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\max_{\partial\Omega}-\frac{\sin\epsilon}{\nu\cdot W},\ \ \max_{\Omega}\sin u\right\}

(3.33)

\displaystyle\leq\max\left\{\max_{\partial\Omega}\frac{\sin\epsilon}{\sigma\cos\epsilon-C_{1}\sin\epsilon},\ \ \max_{\Omega}\sin u\right\}

\displaystyle\leq\max_{\Omega}\sin u.

for sufficiently small
\epsilon.
Thus
-\nu\cdot W\geq\sin u/\max_{\Omega}\sin u
. On the other hand, Proposition
3.6
and Lemma
3.5
yield that

-\nu\cdot W\geq\sigma\cos u-C_{1}\sin u.

If
\tan u\leq c
, then
-\nu\cdot W\geq\sigma\cos u-cC_{1}\cos u;
If
\tan u\geq c
, then
-\nu\cdot W\geq c\cos u/(\max_{\Omega}\sin u)
. Therefore choosing
c=\frac{\sigma\max_{\Omega}\sin u}{C_{1}\max_{\Omega}\sin u+1}
gives to

-\nu\cdot W\geq\frac{\sigma\cos u}{C_{1}\max_{\Omega}\sin u+1}.

This together with Lemma
3.4
completes the proof .
∎

4.

In this section, we will derive boundary estimates for the second derivatives of admissible solutions to the Dirichlet problem presented below.

(4.34)

Theorem 4.1

Differentiating it with respect to

G^{st}\dot{v}_{st}+G^{s}\dot{v}_{s}+G_{u}\dot{v}=0

By standard computation

5.
curvature estimates and higher order estimates

In this section we prove a curvature estimate for compact strictly convex hypersurfaces satisfying
f(\kappa)=\sigma
. By allowing its boundary to approach to the infinity, we also prove a global interior curvature estimate for the largest principal curvature of strictly convex graphs with uniformly bounded principal curvatures satisfying
f(\kappa)=\sigma
.

For a fixed point
x_{0}\in\Sigma
, choosing a local orthonormal frame
\{e_{i}\}_{i=1,\cdots,n}
around
x_{0}
such that

. Ricci identity and Gauss equation imply the following commutator formula.

(5.43)

h_{iijj}-h_{jjii}=(\kappa_{i}\kappa_{j}-1)(\kappa_{i}-\kappa_{j}).

Thus

(5.44)

F^{ii}h_{jjii}=F^{ii}h_{iijj}+(1+\kappa_{j}^{2})\sum_{i=1}^{n}-\kappa_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}-\kappa_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\kappa_{i}^{2}f_{i}.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\Sigma
be a smooth strictly convex hypersurface in

satisfying

and suppose that there exist constants

,

and

(5.46)

where
\cot u=y_{n}/y_{n+1}
and
z=(y_{1},\cdots,y_{n},y_{n+1})
is the position vector.
Let
\kappa_{\max}(p)
be the largest principal curvature of
\Sigma
at
p
. Then

(1)

If
\Sigma
is compact, we have

(5.47)

\underset{\Sigma}{\max}\frac{\kappa_{\max}}{-\nu\cdot W\sec u-a}\leq\max\left\{\frac{C_{3}}{a},\underset{\partial\Sigma}{\max}\frac{\kappa_{\max}}{-\nu\cdot W\sec u-a}\right\}

for a uniform constant
C_{3}
only depending on
C_{1},C_{2}
and
a
.

(2)

If
\Sigma
is complete noncompact with boundary

, we have that for

and

. In particular, letting

Remark 5.2

Due to an inequality by Andrews

which states

-F^{ij,rs}h_{ij1}h_{rs1}\geq\sum_{i\neq j}\frac{f_{i}-f_{j}}{\kappa_{j}-\kappa_{i}}h_{ij1}^{2}\geq 2\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{f_{i}-f_{1}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{i}}h_{i11}^{2}.

On the other hand, we have from (

\displaystyle h_{i11}

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\left(\frac{(g\tan u)_{i}}{g\tan u+a}-b\frac{(\tan u)_{i}}{\tan u}\right)

\displaystyle=\kappa_{1}\frac{(\cot u)_{i}}{\cot u}\left(-\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}+b\right).

Thus

(5.52)

-F^{ij,rs}h_{ij1}h_{rs1}\geq 2\kappa_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{f_{i}-f_{1}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{i}}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(-\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}+b\right)^{2}.

We now calculate
F^{ij}(g\tan u)_{ij}
. It follows

F^{ij}(g/\cot u)_{ij}=F^{ij}\left(\frac{g_{ij}}{\cot u}-\frac{2g_{i}(\cot u)_{j}}{\cot^{2}u}+\frac{2g(\cot u)_{i}(\cot u)_{j}}{\cot^{3}u}-\frac{g(\cot u)_{ij}}{\cot^{2}u}\right).

Recall (
3.25
) and (
3.24
), we have

F^{ij}(\cot u)_{ij}=\sigma g+\cot u\sum f_{i}.

Moreover, (
3.32
) says

F^{ij}g_{i}(\cot u)_{j}=-F^{ij}\kappa_{i}(\cot u)_{i}(\cot u)_{j}=-\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}(\cot u)_{i}^{2}.

Hence, combining these formulae and (
5.50
),(
5.51
),(
5.52
) imply

(5.53)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 0&\geq 2\kappa_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{f_{i}-f_{1}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{i}}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(-\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}+b\right)^{2}\\
&+\sigma\left(1+\kappa_{1}^{2}+\frac{\kappa_{1}}{g\tan u+a}+\frac{\kappa_{1}(g\tan u)^{2}}{g\tan u+a}-bg\tan u\kappa_{1}\right)\\
&+\frac{-a\kappa_{1}}{g\tan u+a}(\sum f_{i}+\sum f_{i}\kappa_{i}^{2})-b\kappa_{1}\sum f_{i}\\
&+\frac{(2b-2)\kappa_{1}}{g\tan u+a}\sum f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)_{i}^{2}}{\cot^{2}u}(g\tan u+\kappa_{i}).\end{aligned}

By the assumption (
5.46
), we can assume that
\kappa_{1}\geq\frac{1+C_{2}^{2}}{a}
, otherwise we are done with the proof. By the assumption,
b\leq\frac{a}{C_{2}}
so that the third line of (
5.53
) is nonegative. Therefore, all terms in (

C
with lower indices to denote universal constants that only depend on
\sigma,\Omega
and the dimension
n.

Define

where

Regarding the last term of (

), considering summation in

\displaystyle\frac{(2b-2)\kappa_{1}}{g\tan u+a}\sum_{J}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)_{i}^{2}}{\cot^{2}u}(g\tan u+\kappa_{i})

\displaystyle\geq\frac{(2-2b)\kappa_{1}f_{1}g\tan u}{a\delta}\sum_{J}\frac{(\cot u)_{i}^{2}}{\cot^{2}u}

For the summation in

L
of first term. Indeed,

\displaystyle 2\kappa_{1}^{2}\sum_{i\geq 2}\frac{f_{i}-f_{1}}{\kappa_{1}-\kappa_{i}}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(-\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}+b\right)^{2}

\displaystyle\geq 2(1-\delta)\kappa_{1}\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}\right)^{2}-4(1-\delta)b\kappa_{1}\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}\right)

in which there exists a term:

\displaystyle 2\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=2\kappa_{1}\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}+\frac{\kappa_{i}^{2}+(g\tan u-a)\kappa_{i}-ag\tan u}{(g\tan u+a)^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq 2\kappa_{1}\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}\right)+2\kappa_{1}\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\frac{(g\tan u-a)\kappa_{i}}{(g\tan u+a)^{2}}

\displaystyle\geq 2\kappa_{1}\sum_{L}f_{i}\frac{(\cot u)^{2}_{i}}{\cot^{2}u}\left(\frac{\kappa_{i}+g\tan u}{g\tan u+a}\right)-\frac{2(C_{2}+a)(1+C_{1}^{2})}{a^{2}}\sigma\kappa_{1}.

whose first term eliminates the part of the last term of (

6.
Proof of the theorem
1.1

In this section we give a proof to the main theorem
1.1
by the degree theory.

Consider for
0\leq t\leq 1
the family of Dirichlet problems

(6.55)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}G(D^{2}u^{t},Du^{t},u^{t},z)=t\sigma+(1-t)\cos\epsilon&\text{in}\ \Omega,\\
u^{t}=\epsilon&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\\
u^{0}\equiv\epsilon&\text{on}\ \bar{\Omega}.\end{cases}

Since
G_{u}|_{u^{0}}=-\sin\epsilon
, we can find a smooth family of solutions
u^{t}
,
0\leq t\leq t_{0}
for a small time
t_{0}
by the implicit function theorem. However, the linearized operator is not necessarily invertible all the time, we can not use method of continuity directly. Instead, we use the degree theory for second-order fully nonlinear elliptic operators developed in
Y. Y. Li
[
16
]
for the existence proof.

Let
v=u-\epsilon.
The equation (
4.34
) turns to be

(6.56)

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\tilde{G}(D^{2}v,Dv,v,z)=\sigma&\text{on}\ \Omega,\\
v=0&\text{on}\ \partial\Omega,\end{cases}

where the linearized operator of
\tilde{G}
is basically same as the one
G
. In particular,

(6.57)

\tilde{G}_{v}=(\nu_{n}-\nu_{n+1}\cos(v+\epsilon))\sum F^{ii}+\sigma\cot(v+\epsilon).

Let
E(v,t):C_{0}^{4,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})\rightarrow C^{2,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
be defined by

E(v,t)=\tilde{G}(D^{2}v,Dv,v,z)-\sigma_{t}

for
t\in[0,1]
where
\sigma_{t}=t\sigma+(1-t)\cos\epsilon
. Notice that
\sigma_{t}
has same bound as
\sigma
. Let
C
be the uniform constant obtained from previous sections such that a priori solution to
E(v,t)=0
has a uniform upper bound
\|u\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})}<C
and
I/C<A[u]<CI
. Define a large subset
O\subset C_{0}^{4,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
by

O=\{v\in C_{0}^{4,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega}):\frac{1}{C}\delta_{ij}<a_{ij}(v)<C\delta_{ij},\|v\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})}<C\}.

where

\displaystyle a_{ij}(v)=\frac{z^{2}_{n}z_{n+1}}{\omega}\gamma^{ik}u_{kl}\gamma^{\ell j}+\frac{z^{2}_{n}z_{n+1}}{\omega^{3}}u_{i}u_{j}u_{n}+\frac{z_{n+1}}{\omega^{2}}(\gamma^{in}u_{j}+\gamma^{jn}u_{i})+\nu_{n+1}\delta_{ij}.

Apparently, it is an open bounded subset under norm
\|\cdot\|_{C^{4,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})}
. Firstly, notice
E(\cdot,t)
is not zero on
\partial\Omega
. Using the Proposition 1.2 of
[
16
]
, we conclude that

\operatorname{degree}(E(\cdot,1),O,0)=\operatorname{degree}(E(\cdot,0),O,0).

It suffices to show that
\operatorname{degree}(E(\cdot,0),O,0)\neq 0
, thus it will follow immediately that
\operatorname{degree}(E(\cdot,1),O,0)\neq 0
. That is, there exists a
v_{0}
in
O
such that
\tilde{G}(D^{2}v_{0},Dv_{0},v_{0},z)=\sigma
. This will in turn give our a solution
u_{0}
to the original equation.

At
t=0
, the linearized operator
L_{0}
is invertible since (
6.57
) is negative. Furthermore, at
t=0,
the only solution to equation (
6.56
) is constant
v=\epsilon
by the geometric maximum principle. Thus due to Proposition 1.3 in
[
16
]
we have

\operatorname{degree}(E,O,0)=\operatorname{degree}(L_{0},O,0)\neq 0.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.
