Hector Galindo-Silva, Paula Herrera-Idárraga

Abstract
This study investigates the impact of integrating gender equality into the Colombian constitution of 1991 on attitudes towards gender equality, experiences of gender-based discrimination, and labor market participation. Using a difference-in-discontinuities framework, we compare individuals exposed to mandatory high school courses on the Constitution with those who were not exposed. Our findings show a significant increase in labor market participation, primarily driven by women. Exposure to these courses also shapes attitudes towards gender equality, with men demonstrating greater support. Women report experiencing less gender-based discrimination. Importantly, our results suggest that women’s increased labor market participation is unlikely due to reduced barriers from male partners. A disparity in opinions regarding traditional gender norms concerning household domains is observed between men and women, highlighting an ongoing power struggle within the home. However, the presence of a younger woman in the household appears to influence men’s more positive view of gender equality, potentially indicating a desire to empower younger women in their future lives. These findings highlight the crucial role of cultural shocks and the constitutional inclusion of women’s rights in shaping labor market dynamics.

In many societies, traditional gender norms grant men privileges, providing them with access to positions of power and authority. Conversely, women are often expected to adhere to caring roles, focusing primarily on domestic and household responsibilities. These norms impose constraints on women’s opportunities to pursue positions of power and actively participate in the labor market, subjecting them to discrimination.

Fernández 2013

Understanding the dynamics of gender norms and their impact on labor market outcomes is crucial for addressing gender inequalities and promoting inclusive economic development.

This paper aims to contribute to this understanding by examining the consequences of a significant event in Colombia’s recent history: the inclusion of gender equality as a constitutional mandate in the 1991 constitution. The study specifically investigates how this constitutional provision, in conjunction with mandatory education on the constitution, influenced individuals’ perceptions, recognition of gender equality, and labor market participation. The study focuses particularly on individuals who directly experienced the compulsory courses related to the new constitution.

To analyze these effects, we employ a difference-in-discontinuities framework, in the spirit of
Grembi et al. 2016
. Specifically, we compare the change in outcomes between cohorts associated with two distinct groups: individuals who were exposed to mandatory courses on the 1991 Colombian constitution during high school and individuals who were not exposed due to completing high school just before the introduction of these courses, or not completing high school at all. Our primary identifying assumption is that, in the absence of the treatment (i.e., mandatory courses on the 1991 Colombian constitution), the average outcomes for these two groups would have followed a similar pattern across cohorts. We justify this assumption by restricting our analysis to cohorts defined by a narrow age difference. If this age difference is sufficiently small, we expect the evolution of outcomes among individuals who did and did not complete high school to be similar in the absence of the treatment. Consistently with this parallel trends assumption, we find evidence of comparable pre-trends across cohorts between our treatment and control groups.

Our main findings indicate that the incorporation of gender equality into the Constitution had notable effects on labor market dynamics. The coefficients for the interaction term in our regression model range from 0.026 to 0.044. These estimates suggest that individuals who underwent constitutional education experience an approximate increase of 3.5 percentage points in their labor market participation. Importantly, this effect is even more pronounced for women, potentially doubling the impact.

Additionally, our evidence indicates that exposure to high school courses on the 1991 Colombian Constitution is associated with a more favorable attitude towards gender equality, particularly among men. It also leads to a reduced perception of discrimination by women. Interestingly, our results suggest that women’s increased labor market participation may not be due to reduced barriers from their male partners. Within households, there exists a disparity between men and women regarding the idea of men making decisions concerning women’s lives and the notion of the household head being male. While women tend to oppose these ideas, men tend to support them or at least display indifference. Our findings indicate that men hold more egalitarian perceptions regarding gender roles for women who are not their partners, suggesting a desire to maintain power within the household. Furthermore, our findings indicate that when at least one woman younger than 18 years old resides in the household, men tend to hold more positive views of gender equality. This observation suggests a potential desire to empower younger women and enhance their relative power compared to men in their future personal and professional lives.

We conduct several robustness checks to ensure the validity of our findings. Specifically, in addition to the previously mentioned evidence of comparable pre-trends across cohorts between our treatment and control groups, we find that our results remain robust when utilizing alternative databases and employing a simpler Regression Discontinuity Design.

This study makes several contributions to the existing literature. In addition to the aforementioned studies that examine the persistence and change of culture, and its effects on economic outcomes, this paper adds to the growing body of literature exploring the enduring nature of gender roles and their associations with various phenomena. For example, previous research has investigated the influence of grammatical gender marking on female participation in the labor market, access to credit, land ownership, and political engagement
(
Gay et al. 2013

Guiso et al. 2003
,

Another area of research examines the influence of economic incentives on attitudes toward gender equality. A crucial study on this subject is
Doepke and Tertilt 2009

Additionally, our research explores the societal implications of this transformative change. By providing insights into the influence of constitutional inclusion and the promotion of gender equality through education, our research enhances our understanding of the dynamics surrounding gender norms. It emphasizes the crucial role of constitutional frameworks in shaping outcomes related to gender equality and sheds light on the impact of societal attitudes and women’s labor force participation.

Background

Colombia, throughout the majority of the 20th century, adhered to traditional and conservative gender norms deeply embedded in the long-standing political constitution dating back to 1886. Despite a notable constitutional reform in 1936 and various legislative measures aimed at protecting women’s economic and social rights, the formal recognition of women’s rights as a constitutional requirement was only accomplished with the drafting of a new political constitution in 1991
(
Iregui-Bohórquez et al. 2020
)
.

The National Constituent Assembly (NCA) of 1991 played a pivotal role in this process. Convened specifically to draft the current political constitution, known as the Constitution of 1991, it marked a significant turning point in Colombia’s 20th-century history. The NCA aimed to establish an institutional framework to address the persistent cycles of violence and armed conflicts. The approval of the new Constitution formalized a social pact and provided the foundation for the development of a social state of law, promoting the peaceful resolution of social and political conflicts. This transformative process brought together various initiatives and fostered the expansion of representative democracy.

Under the new constitution, significant progress was made in recognizing and protecting the rights of marginalized groups, including the recognition of women’s rights that were absent from the original 1886 constitution. Subsequent reforms addressed this gap by granting women the right to vote, access higher education, exercise independent control over their assets in marriage, and abolishing marital authority, among other changes. These advancements primarily took place within the legislative domain rather than the constitutional realm. However, it is important to note that despite these legal improvements, gender-based discrimination and violence against women continued to persist.
4
4

4

Iregui-Bohórquez et al. 2020
outlined this progression towards gender equality, which unfolded in four distinct stages. During the “women left behind” phase from 1905 to 1935, women in Colombia faced challenges including limited education, high fertility rates, and exclusion from the labor market and politics. The second stage, known as the “first steps towards women’s empowerment,” extended from 1936 to 1965 the promulgation of laws and the 1936 constitutional reform took place granted economic, access to public positions and civil rights (the right of women to enter university on equal terms with men was recognized, women equal rights in the workplace and in the academia), also women’s suffrage right took place in 1954 along with the ability to choose and be elected for public offices. However, women still faced low university enrollment, high fertility and early marriage rates, and limited labor participation. From 1966 to 1985, the “rise of women’s empowerment” in Colombia saw significant reforms, including equal rights in the civil code, divorce legalization, and ratification of the Convention on the Elimination of all forms of Discrimination against Women (UN Assembly, 1979). This period also experienced a demographic transition, lower fertility rates, and increased female labor market participation. The final stage, “moving forward: still a lot to catch up,” began in 1985 and continued into the present. During this period, there was a consistent rise in female enrollment in higher education, surpassing that of men. Fertility and mortality rates continued to decline, and women’s labor force participation continued to grow, although it stagnated in the early 21st century. Despite some progress, wage gaps persisted, albeit with a reduction in magnitude. Notably, there were some advancements in political participation.

During the NCA of 1991, feminist women’s movements played a vital role in advocating for women’s rights. For instance,
Quintero 2006
highlights that although the women’s movement did not have a unified candidate for the NCA due to discussions on dual membership and the individual preference of many women to support candidates from their respective parties or movements, they actively carried out a national campaign. This campaign effectively gathered approximately 15,000 signatures from various cities in support of women’s proposals, which were subsequently submitted to the Presidency of the NCA. To amplify their message, the movements utilized radio advertisements, including a 15-day spot on the prominent Caracol program, a major radio and TV broadcaster in the country, airing from 6 am to 9 am. Additionally, they issued press statements and actively engaged in numerous discussions with constituents to raise awareness about women’s proposals. In Figure 1, Panel (a) displays a publication from El Tiempo, one of the country’s leading newspapers, prominently featuring the movement’s slogan at that time: “WITHOUT WOMEN’S RIGHTS, DEMOCRACY WON’T SUCCEED!".

All these efforts found an echo among some of the members of the constituent assembly (the assembly had 70 delegates, of which only 4 were women). For example, in Panel (b) of Figure
I
, we present a photograph of the statement titled “Women’s Rights"
(
Constituyente 1991
)
, signed by constituents Horacio Serpa, Guillermo Perry, and Eduardo Verano de la Rosa. This declaration highlighted the constitutional elevation of the principle of equality among sexes, races, ethnic groups, languages, religions, and beliefs, emphasizing the importance of fostering national awareness in the elimination of all forms of discrimination. Regarding women’s rights, the ANC received over 150 proposals, put forth by women’s groups and the constituents
(
Quintero 2006
)
.

In terms of the provisions pertaining to gender relations within the new political constitution, several noteworthy examples can be identified. Firstly, the constitution incorporated key provisions that ensure gender equality in relation to men (Article 13 and 43). Additionally, it recognized the right to form a family (Article 42) and provided protection during pregnancy (Article 43). The constitution also emphasized equal opportunities for all workers, with special protection measures for women (Article 53). Furthermore, it acknowledged the right of women to participate in the formation, exercise, and control of political power (Article 40). Therefore, the 1991 Political Constitution marked a significant shift for women, transitioning them from being seen as individuals requiring protection, with their opinions, ideas, beliefs, and needs often disregarded, to being recognized as individuals with entitlements and autonomy. They gained the responsibility to exercise their rights and make decisions regarding their own lives and public affairs.

An important characteristic of the implementation of the 1991 Constitution, relevant to our study and particularly to our identification strategy, was the requirement for mandatory courses on the constitution in high schools. This requirement was established by Law 107 in January 1994, which specifically regulated these courses. According to Article 1 of this law, completion of fifty hours of Constitutional Studies became a prerequisite for obtaining a high school diploma in any of its modalities. This law served as a means to ensure that students develop a solid understanding of the Constitution and its principles. Subsequently, in February 1994, the Congress issued the General Law of Education (Law 115), which mandated the study, understanding, and practice of the Constitution and civic instruction in all public or private educational establishments offering formal education at the preschool, primary, and secondary levels. This provision, in accordance with Article 41 of the Political Constitution, further emphasized the importance of Constitutional Studies and civic education within the curriculum.

Colombia’s Ministry of Education played a crucial role in overseeing the pedagogy of constitutional education and providing schools with pedagogical booklets containing materials and activities. Our research uncovered several Ministry initiatives aimed at promoting constitutional education. One such initiative is the 1994 pedagogical booklet titled “Combating Stereotyped Gender Roles" (see Figure
II
), which aimed to enhance students’ knowledge and awareness of fundamental rights and democratic values. Panel B of Figure
II
highlights a specific objective of the booklet, which is was to “contribute to the development of young people’s abilities to discern gender roles that do not promote the fulfillment of the potential of both men and women…". We also identified additional guides published in 1998 and 1999, indicating that it took the Ministry several years to develop materials for distribution among schools. An illustrative example is the coeducation guide presented in Figure
III
, which introduces a conceptual and methodological framework for improving training and fostering holistic individual development. Notably, Panel B of Figure

focuses on the first chapter, exploring the conceptualization and contextualization of gender non-discriminatory education. Additionally, Panel C explicitly references social equity by invoking Articles 13 and 43 of the Constitution.

Data and Empirical Strategy

3.1
Data

Our primary data source is the Political Culture Survey, conducted biennially since 2015 by the Colombian National Administrative Department of Statistics (DANE). This survey provides valuable insights into various aspects of Colombian political culture, covering the years 2015, 2017, 2019, and 2021. It encompasses the entire nation, including specific regions such as Bogota, Atlantic, Central, Oriental, and Pacific. By capturing citizens’ perceptions and practices regarding their political and social environment, the survey allows for an examination of attitudes towards gender equality and discrimination. Although the survey does not specifically target labor market outcomes, it includes essential variables on employment status and education levels that are crucial for our analysis. The dataset represents a diverse sample of individuals aged 18 years and older residing in households throughout Colombia.

In order to validate our findings on labor market participation, we also rely on the Gran Encuesta Integrada de Hogares (GEIH), a cross-sectional household survey conducted by the DANE. This survey is the main data source for official monthly labor market statistics, providing comprehensive information on labor supply, informality, and wages, enabling a gender-specific analysis of these factors. It covers the entire country, including major metropolitan areas, rural regions, and small municipalities. An important limitation of this database, which led it to be secondary in our analysis, is the absence of specific questions about attitudes towards gender equality. Nevertheless, we ensured the robustness of our baseline findings related to the labor market by incorporating this alternative database.

Finally, we enhance our analysis by incorporating the Encuesta de datos de uso del tiempo (ENUT) as an additional data source. This survey, conducted by the DANE, examines how individuals aged 10 and above allocate their time and provides insights into gender dynamics. While the ENUT focuses on activities such as paid work, unpaid work, and personal activities, it also explores attitudes towards gender roles in relation to household leadership, contribution to household income, and caregiving responsibilities. An important limitation of this database, which influenced our decision to not use it as our primary data source, is its data collection in pairs of years: 2012-2013, 2016-2017, and 2020-2021. Since our baseline econometric specification necessitates estimating the age of individuals interviewed in 1994 and relies on knowing the exact survey year, the inability of this database to provide that specific information introduces significant noise into our estimations. Additionally, it is noteworthy that the primary focus of this database centers around individuals’ attitudes towards gender equality within households. While we consider these factors, our proposed explanation for the baseline results extends beyond the realm of the domestic sphere, as we will demonstrate later.
5
5

5

It is worth noting that the data we utilize from the ENUT does not encompass the years 2020-2021. This exclusion is driven by the exceptional circumstances of the year 2020, marked by the implementation of confinement measures in response to the COVID-19 pandemic within the Colombian population. It is reasonable to expect that both the data collection process and the reported behavior of individuals during this period were significantly affected. Nevertheless, the absence of these years in our sample has not led to significant changes in our findings.

A final point to note regarding our data sources is that the surveys mentioned earlier, despite being conducted by the DANE, involve interviews with distinct individuals. Therefore, it is not feasible to merge or combine their data.

3.2
Empirical Strategy

In our empirical strategy, we investigate the consequences of a ‘cultural shock’ that, according to our hypothesis, arose following the implementation of the 1991 Colombian Constitution. This shock created a unique opportunity for analysis, as explain in Section
2
it was accompanied by the introduction of mandatory courses on the new Colombian Constitution in high schools. By considering the staggered exposure of individuals to this shock, we investigate its impact on various outcomes, such as the labor market participation of these individuals.

Our analysis focuses on individuals who were 16 years old or younger in 1994, which is the year when mandatory courses on the Colombian Constitution were first implemented. In our sample, this includes individuals aged 37 or younger in 2015, aged 39 or younger in 2017, aged 41 or younger in 2019, or aged 43 or younger in 2021. For individuals in our sample who completed high school, it was mandatory for them to have taken at least a one-year course specifically dedicated about the Colombian political constitution. We refer to this group as the ‘affected’ cohort. Conversely, individuals who were older than 16 years in 1994 never had the opportunity to attend these courses. We define this group as the ‘unaffected’ cohort.
6
6

6

We chose the year 1994 as the treatment year because, in that year, the law mandated that the courses on the 1991 Constitution should begin, at least for the final year students of high school. It is clear, then, that the selection of this year makes it highly improbable that individuals who completed their secondary education before 1994 were treated. However, it appears to be true, according to the anecdotal evidence documented in Section
2
, that although the courses started in schools that year, their implementation was expanded and improved in the subsequent years (with the introduction of more textbooks and teaching methodologies, for example). This implies that some effect is also expected for years after 1994. Although our paper does not extend our analysis to years beyond 1994, it could be an interesting extension to consider.

Our primary empirical strategy centers on a modified version of a difference-in-differences framework, specifically referred to as a difference-in-discontinuities model
(
Grembi et al. 2016
)
. We compare two distinct groups: individuals who completed high school (the treated group) and individuals of barely the same age who did not complete high school (the control group). Importantly, individuals in the treated group are considered as treated only starting from a specific year (1994 or later), while individuals who did not complete high school consistently remain untreated. This key characteristic of the model, combined with the focus on individuals who were just above and just below the threshold age of 16 in 1994, enables us to interpret it as following a difference-in-discontinuities framework.

Specifically, for our main results we estimate the following model :

A standard Regression Discontinuity Design (RDD) can be also used to examine the effects of high school courses on the 1991 Colombian Constitution on labor supply. By focusing on high school graduates just above and below the 16-year age threshold in 1994, individuals aged just above the threshold age could serve as a plausible counterfactual for individuals aged just below the threshold. However, we have decided against using it as our baseline model for two main reasons. First, because our running variable is discrete, so there may be mass points in our data. As we detail in Section
5.3
, our RDD estimates depend on how we adjust for these points (in particular, the results obtained using a data-driven procedure to choose the window), which makes us believe less in these estimates. Second, because of the inherent limitation of the RDD framework in disentangling the impacts of age attention from the specific educational experiences during high school. Although we can identify an age-related effect on labor supply specifically in 1994, it remains uncertain whether this effect primarily arises from the hypothesized educational experiences or is merely a consequence of a minor change in age. Thus, we have chosen an alternative empirical strategy that allows us to explore the relationship between high school courses and labor supply while accounting for these concerns.

The primary identifying assumption of the model in (
1
) is that, in the absence of treatment, the average outcome for both the treated and comparison groups would have followed a similar pattern across cohorts. This assumption is essential for interpreting our estimates as causal effects. However, it may not hold when examining labor force participation if individuals who did not complete high school make different decisions regarding this outcome throughout their lives (or across cohorts) in ways that systematically differ from the decisions made by individuals who completed high school.

A scenario where this ‘parallel trends’ assumption may not be valid is when women without a high school education have a higher number of children compared to women with a high school education, or when they have children at a younger age. If these fertility decisions also influence labor market participation, such as reducing participation among young women with multiple children, the ordinary least squares (OLS) estimates of the parameter
\beta_{3}
in equation (
1
) would be biased.

We argue that our main identification assumption holds in three different ways. First and foremost, we restrict our analysis to individuals who were slightly below and slightly above the age threshold of 16 years in 1994. In other words, we employ a difference-in-discontinuities model, as opposed to a difference-in-differences model. By limiting our analysis to individuals from these close cohorts and assuming that the age difference between these cohorts is small enough,
we improve the comparability within cohorts. This, in turn, increases the likelihood of satisfying our parallel trends assumption.
8
8

8

As will be shown later, we use specifications that employ different age windows. Despite the robustness of our results across a wide range of bandwidths, we favor age ranges that are within 5 to 10 years older or younger than 16 years in 1994. This preference stems from the aim of striking a balance between an adequate duration of treatment exposure (i.e., the number of years individuals who completed high school were exposed to courses on the 1991 constitution) and minimizing significant age differences between those exposed and not exposed to such courses.

Secondly, we test for preexisting differences in trends by estimating the model (
1
) for cohorts slightly younger and older than 16, but specifically focusing on years prior to 1994 (and selecting years and age windows to ensure there is no overlap). As detailed in Section
5
, our results indicate the absence of pre-existing trends.

Thirdly, we explore the possibility that the assumption of parallel trends holds only conditional the number of children in the households of individuals. In Section
5
we show that our main results are robust to conditioning for this important covariate.

4.2
Attitudes Towards Gender Equality

The explanation we put forward for the findings presented in the preceding subsection is rooted in the notion that individuals’ exposure to high school courses on the 1991 Colombian Constitution altered the prevailing social norm concerning gender equality: specifically, we argue that this exposure reshaped individuals’ attitudes towards gender equality and discrimination, incentivizing greater labor market participation, particularly among women. We offer now supporting evidence for this explanation.

We begin by acknowledging that our primary explanation hinges on a crucial assumption: that individuals’ exposure to high school courses on the 1991 Colombian Constitution influenced their attitudes towards gender equality. . Furthermore, this hypothesis is in line with the idea put forth by
Doepke and Tertilt 2009
’s that promoting women’s rights requires men to have incentives to share power with women.

Work and Home Spheres

In the preceding subsection, we briefly discussed the role of men in facilitating the transformation of social norms that affected women’s participation in the labor market. However, we need to provide more clarity on this matter. In this subsection, we will delve deeper into this topic by addressing the following questions: Which women are the beneficiaries of men’s most favorable views on gender equality? Are these views limited to women residing within the households of these men (e.g., their wives or daughters)? Or do they extend to women beyond their immediate households? Although the analysis in this subsection is exploratory in nature and the results are presented at a broader level, they provide valuable insights into the specific mechanisms underlying our main findings.

These findings indicate that the observed changes in these norms are not primarily influenced by men within households sharing power with women in a manner that significantly diminishes barriers to women’s participation in the labor market.

If not through shifts in household norms, how can we explain our findings? Our hypothesis posits that men, when adopting a more favorable attitude towards gender equality, tend to engage in power-sharing with women who are not their spouses. This aligns with the thesis proposed by
Doepke and Tertilt 2009

Doepke and Tertilt 2009
’s argument, we contend that one motivation driving men to put their more favorable opinion on gender equality into practice is their interest in empowering the younger women in their close circle (e.g., daughters, granddaughters, nieces), enhancing their power relative to men in their future personal and professional lives.

5.1
Identifying Assumptions Checks

As described in Section
3.2
, the primary underlying assumption of the model presented in Equation (
1
) is that, in the absence of the treatment, individuals with and without a high school education would have demonstrated similar patterns of labor supply and attitudes towards gender equality across different cohorts. To empirically evaluate the plausibility of this assumption, we analyze cohorts of individuals who were approximately 16 years old before 1994, a period when no individuals were exposed to high school courses related to the 1991 Colombian Constitution. By examining this period, we can determine if there were any preexisting differences in trends, as expected under the parallel trends assumption. If the parallel trends assumption holds, we should not observe any effects during those years.

Table

provides estimates of Equation (

for cohorts of individuals who were approximately 16 years old before 1994. The analysis focuses on the years 1984, 1986, 1988, 1990, and 1992. The dependent variable in columns (1) to (4) is labor market participation, while columns (4) to (6) use the stance on gender equality as the dependent variable. Columns (1) and (4) include both men and women, columns (2) and (5) focus on women, and columns (3) and (6) concentrate on men. The observation window width changes each year to ensure non-overlapping cohorts, ensuring that no individual was in high school in 1994. Importantly, nearly all of the estimates are small and statistically insignificant, providing crucial evidence supporting the validity of the parallel trends assumption.

While the results in Table

6
Conclusions

This paper investigates the impact of the constitutional inclusion of women’s rights, coupled with courses on the constitution, on the dynamics of cultural norms and economic outcomes. Our research uncovers gender variations in the magnitude of these effects, with women experiencing greater changes in their labor market participation due to cultural shocks. The estimated coefficients from our difference-in-discontinuity model demonstrate a significant increase in labor market participation, particularly for women.

Furthermore, our study uncovers additional evidence supporting the association between exposure to high school courses on the 1991 Colombian Constitution and more favorable attitudes towards gender equality, particularly among men. Moreover, this exposure has been found to reduce women’s perception of discrimination. Interestingly, our findings suggest that women’s increased labor market participation is unlikely due to reduced barriers from male partners. A disparity in opinions regarding traditional gender norms concerning household domains is observed between men and women, highlighting an ongoing power struggle within the home. However, the presence of a younger woman in the household appears to influence men’s more positive view of gender equality. This observation suggests a potential desire to empower younger women in their future personal and professional lives.

Our study provides insights into the intricate nature of gender dynamics within households. While men generally hold more egalitarian perceptions regarding gender roles for women in general, they maintain conservative views when it comes to gender roles within their own homes. This suggests a potential desire to uphold power dynamics within the household.

To effectively challenge and transform gender roles, it is crucial for women to enhance their bargaining power within the household. However, our findings indicate that combating gender roles within the domestic sphere presents unique challenges. Therefore, it is important to develop a better understanding of the interplay between different levels, such as the State, communities, and households, where gender norms need to be reevaluated
(
Agarwal 1997
)
. Recognizing and addressing the interconnections among these structures is essential, as they influence the effectiveness of policies and measures aimed at promoting gender equality.

Furthermore, future research could explore the intergenerational effects of these cultural changes. While earlier generations may encounter greater obstacles in breaking gender stereotypes within the home, subsequent generations are expected to possess improved resources and perspectives to reassess and redefine traditional gender roles. Understanding the evolution of these cultural shifts and their impact on future generations is vital for fostering sustainable progress towards gender equality. By examining the interplay between cultural transformations, generational dynamics, and gender norms, we can gain valuable insights into the long-term implications of these changes and inform targeted interventions to accelerate advancements in gender equality.
