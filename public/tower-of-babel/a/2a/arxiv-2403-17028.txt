Finitely generated dyadic convex sets Thanks: Please cite as : Matczak, K.; Mućka, A.; Romanowska, A. B.; Finitely generated dyadic convex sets; Internat. J. Algebra Comput. 33 (2023), no. 3, 585–615.
K. Matczak 1, A. Mućka 2, A.B. Romanowska 3

Abstract
. Dyadic rationals are rationals whose denominator is a power of 2 2 . We define dyadic n n -dimensional convex sets as the intersections with n n -dimensional dyadic space of an n n -dimensional real convex set. Such a dyadic convex set is said to be a dyadic n n -dimensional polytope if the real convex set is a polytope whose vertices lie in the dyadic space. Dyadic convex sets are described as subreducts (subalgebras of reducts) of certain faithful affine spaces over the ring of dyadic numbers, or equivalently as commutative, entropic and idempotent groupoids under the binary operation of arithmetic mean. The paper contains two main results. First, it is proved that, while all dyadic polytopes are finitely generated, only dyadic simplices are generated by their vertices. This answers a question formulated in an earlier paper. Then, a characterization of finitely generated subgroupoids of dyadic convex sets is provided, and it is shown how to use the characterization to determine the minimal number of generators of certain convex subsets of the dyadic plane.

Introduction

The objects we study belong to both algebra and geometry. As algebras, they have one binary operation which is idempotent, commutative and entropic. As geometric objects, they are subsets of certain affine spaces.

Recall that
dyadic rationals
are rationals whose denominator is a power of
2
. They form the principal ideal subdomain
\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{Z}[1/2]
of the ring
\mathbb{R}
of real numbers. The affine spaces of interest are certain faithful affine spaces over the ring
\mathbb{D}
(or affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces), i.e., idempotent reducts of certain faithful
\mathbb{D}
-modules. Affine spaces over the ring
\mathbb{R}
(
affine
\mathbb{R}
-spaces
) may be described as abstract algebras with uncountably many binary affine combinations indexed by the elements of
\mathbb{R}
. Affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces are subreducts (subalgebras of reducts) of affine
\mathbb{R}
-spaces. The fact that
\mathbb{D}
is a linearly ordered commutative ring with a nontrivial open unit interval makes it possible to define convex subsets of affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces in a reasonable way. For a given dimension
n
, a subset
D
of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
is called a
geometric dyadic convex set
(or briefly a
dyadic convex set
) if it is the intersection of a convex subset of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the space
\mathbb{D}^{n}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Such sets are also described as being
convex relative to
\mathbb{D}
(cf. for instance
[
1
]
.) Then
dyadic polytopes
are defined as dyadic convex sets obtained as the intersection of dyadic spaces with real polytopes whose vertices are contained in the dyadic space. In particular,
dyadic polygons
are the intersections of the dyadic plane with real polygons spanned by vertices in the dyadic plane. The one-dimensional analogs are
dyadic intervals
.
In this paper the name of a dyadic interval will be always used to denote an interval which is a dyadic polytope.

Real convex sets are described algebraically as certain barycentric algebras, subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
closed under operations of weighted means with weights from the open unit interval
I^{\circ}=\,]0,1[
of
\mathbb{R}
. Similarly, dyadic convex sets may be described as subsets of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
closed under weighted means with weights from the open dyadic unit interval
I^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{D}
. However, as shown already by Ježek and Kepka
[
5
]
, the operations determined by
I^{\circ}\cap\mathbb{D}
are all generated by the single arithmetic mean operation

x\circ y:=xy\underline{1/2}=\frac{1}{2}(x+y).

The arithmetic mean is an idempotent, commutative and entropic operation.
This fact allows one to define dyadic convex sets equivalently as groupoids with the algebraic structure of a commutative binary mode (or briefly
\mathcal{CB}
-modes), i.e. algebras with one binary idempotent, commutative and entropic operation. Such algebras have a well developed theory. (See e.g.
[
6
,
7
,
8
,
9
,
10
,
11
,
16
,
17
]
.)

Affine spaces and their subreducts, as well as
\mathcal{CB}
-modes, belong to the class of
modes
, idempotent and entropic algebras. (For the general theory of modes, see the monographs
[
16
,
17
]
.)

Dyadic and real convex sets share many properties. However, there are also essential differences. For example, real convex sets are characterized as cancellative barycentric algebras (see
[
17
, Thm. 5.8.6]
, but not all cancellative
\mathcal{CB}
-modes are dyadic convex sets (see
[
9
, § 5]
,
[
4
, § 1.2]
.) While real intervals belong to only finitely many isomorphism classes of barycentric algebras, there are infinitely many pairwise non-isomorphic dyadic intervals.
As a subgroupoid of
(\mathbb{D},\circ)
, each nontrivial interval of
\mathbb{D}
is isomorphic to some dyadic interval
\mathbb{D}_{k}=[0,k]
, where
k
is an odd positive integer. Two such intervals are isomorphic precisely when their right hand ends are equal
[
11
, § 3]
.

The problem of locating generating elements of dyadic polygons was first considered in
[
11
]
.
While real intervals which are polytopes are minimally generated by their two ends, the dyadic intervals are minimally generated by
2
or
3
elements.
The interval
\mathbb{D}_{1}
is generated by
0
and
1
, and for
k>1
, the interval
\mathbb{D}_{k}
is generated by three, but no fewer, elements. For each

, the dyadic intervals
[d,d+2^{n}]
are isomorphic to
\mathbb{D}_{1}
, and the dyadic intervals
[d,d+k2^{n}]
are isomorphic to
\mathbb{D}_{k}
. More generally, it was shown that each dyadic triangle is finitely generated, and then that each dyadic polygon is finitely generated. However, the methods used in that paper were not sufficient to determine the exact minimal number of generators. Moreover, the following question was left open: Are all dyadic polytopes finitely generated? (See
[
11
, Problem 1.1]
.)
The question arose in connection with our investigations of quasivarieties of
\mathcal{CB}
-modes
[
9
,
10
]
and of duality for dyadic polytopes
[
7
,
8
]
. A related question asks whether finitely generated subgroupoids of
(\mathbb{D}^{n},\circ)
are isomorphic to dyadic polytopes. A partial answer was then given in
[
7
]
: It was shown that each finitely generated subgroupoid of
(\mathbb{D},\circ)
is isomorphic to a dyadic interval.

In this paper we provide an affirmative solution to the problem formulated in
[
11
]
, and show that each dyadic polytope is finitely generated (Theorem
3.4
). However, there are some other subsets of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, closed under the operation
\circ
, which are not isomorphic to dyadic polytopes but are finitely generated
(Example
4.1
). To characterize finitely generated subgroupoids of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, we introduce a new class of so-called semipolytopes. A

is a subgroupoid of
a dyadic polytope such that both have the same vertices and the same interior (Definition
4.2

The main result, Theorem

(D,\circ)
of the groupoid
(\mathbb{D}^{n},\circ)
is finitely generated precisely when it is isomorphic to a semipolytope.
This result is then used in the final section to calculate the minimal number of generators of certain polygons and semipolygons.

All dyadic convex sets are considered in this paper as
\mathcal{CB}
-modes. We use notation, terminology and conventions similar to those of

2.3.
Walls

For a barycentric algebra

, which is a convex set, a subset

\forall\ a,b\in C\,,\ \forall\,\ r\in I^{o}\,,\ ab\underline{r}\in A\ \Leftrightarrow\ a\in A\mbox{ and }b\in A.

Similarly, for a dyadic convex set

, a subset

of

is a

\forall\ a,b\in C\,,\ a\circ b\in A\ \Leftrightarrow\ a\in A\mbox{ and }b\in A.

(See e.g.

Note that in the case of real polytopes, the geometric concept of a face (see e.g.

of

Using the algebraic definition of a wall, one can easily extend the geometric concept of a face of a real polytope to the case of a dyadic polytope.
This extension preserves the basic properties of faces of real polytopes.
In particular, walls of a dyadic polytope may be described as follows.

Now assume that the statement of Proposition holds for all walls of dimension smaller than

k<n
. We will show that it holds for

. So let

be a wall of

of dimension

\overline{W}
and

as before. Note that

Let

containing

If

generates a wall

of

contained in
F
of dimension smaller than

, then by induction hypotheses,

, where
\overline{U}=\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(U)
is a wall of
F
. So assume that

Recall that each interior point of

. (See

.) Indeed, proper walls of

do not contain interior points of

. Moreover,

and

are

-dimensional. Let

be any dyadic point of

and assume that its reflection

in

. Since

is a wall of

It follows that any dyadic point of

, and

.
∎

Corollary 2.8
.

Each wall of a dyadic polytope is also a dyadic polytope.

Proof.

By Propositions
2.5
and
2.7
, a wall

of an
n
-dimensional dyadic polytope

is the intersection with
\mathbb{D}^{n}
of the real polytope, namely the wall
F
of

generated by

.
∎

Propositions
2.5
and
2.7
show that there is a one-to-one correspondence between walls of
P
and walls of
C
, and for each wall

of

, one has

. In particular,

and

have the same (dyadic) vertices. It follows that

are determined by the vertices of

they contain. Proper walls of

are polytopes of a dimension smaller than the dimension of

.
Note as well that the lattices of walls of

and

Recall that dyadic subspaces of

. Then similarly as in the case of real polytopes,
the (relative) interior of a dyadic polytope

consists of the points of

which are not contained in the proper walls of

, and the boundary of

(in its affine
\mathbb{D}

Corollary

Let

of

of the wall of

generated by

. The proof is similar as in the case of dyadic polytopes, with

.
Note that the vertices of

. Note as well that

Example 2.9

To illustrate the concepts introduced above, consider the points

2.4.
Simplices

Examples of dyadic polytopes generated by their vertices are given by some models of finitely generated free commutative binary modes.
First recall that in affine geometry, a (real)
n
-dimensional simplex is usually defined as a polytope with
n+1
affinely independent vertices. It is known that proper faces of a simplex are simplices of smaller dimension
[
2
, Thm. 12.1, Thm. 12.2]
. It was shown by W. Neumann
[
13
]
that the free barycentric algebra on
n+1
generators is the
n
-dimensional simplex with its vertices as free generators. The elements of the free barycentric algebra
X\mathcal{B}
on
X
, where
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\}
, are described as follows

\Big\{x_{0}r_{0}+\dots+x_{n}r_{n}\,\Big|\,r_{i}\in I,\ \sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{i}=1\Big\}.

Here
I=[0,1]
is the closed unit interval of
\mathbb{R}
. (See
[
14
, L. 6.1, L. 6.2]
).

If
S
is an
n
-dimensional real simplex with dyadic vertices
x_{0},\dots,x_{n}
, then the subgroupoid of
(S,\circ)
generated by these vertices is called an
n
-dimensional algebraic dyadic simplex
. Its elements form the set

(2.6)

\Big\{x_{0}r_{0}+\dots+x_{n}r_{n}\,\Big|\,r_{i}\in\mathbb{D}_{1}=I\cap\mathbb{D},\ \sum_{i=0}^{n}r_{i}=1\Big\}.

Note that such a simplex is not necessarily geometric.
As proved in
[
14
, L. 6.1, L. 6.2]
, the free commutative binary mode
X\mathcal{CBM}
on a set
X=\{x_{0},\dots,x_{n}\}
is isomorphic with the
\underline{1/2}
-reduct generated by
X
of the free affine
\mathbb{D}
-space
X\mathbb{D}
on
X
. The elements of
X\mathcal{CBM}
are described by (
2.6
).

It was shown earlier in
[
5
]
and
[
16
, §4.4]
that by taking
the elements

(2.7)

e_{0}=(0,\dots,0),e_{1}=(1,0,\dots,0),\dots,e_{n}=(0,\dots,0,1)

as the generators
x_{i}
in (
2.6
), one obtains the (geometric) dyadic simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
free in the variety
\mathcal{CBM}
. The simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
is a dyadic polytope, and a subreduct of the real simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}^{r}
with the same vertices.
The proper faces of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
are polytopes of smaller dimension.
While each algebraic dyadic simplex is isomorphic to some
\mathbf{S}_{n}
,
not all of them are geometric. An example is given by the dyadic simplex generated by
e_{0}=(0,\dots,0),e^{\prime}_{1}=(3,0,\dots,0),\dots,e^{\prime}_{n}=(0,\dots,0,3)
.
In fact, any affinely independent set of points of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
generates an algebraic dyadic simplex. We will use the name
algebraic dyadic simplex
for a groupoid isomorphic to some
\mathbf{S}_{n}
, while reserving the name

for geometric dyadic simplices.

Lemma 2.10
.

[
4
, L. 2.2]

Each
n
-dimensional algebraic dyadic convex set, where
n=1,2,\dots
, contains an
n
-dimensional subgroupoid isomorphic to the dyadic simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
.

Corollary 2.11
.

Each
n
-dimensional geometric dyadic convex set

, where

, contains infinitely many geometric subgroupoids isomorphic to the dyadic simplex

.

Proof.

First note that, for each

, the elements

, where

, generate a geometric dyadic simplex

. For any interior point

of

, there is

such that all

belong to

and generate a geometric dyadic simplex, the translate of

.
∎

For a positive integer

, let

be an
n
-dimensional algebraic dyadic convex set. Take

. We say that

is

-generated
if

is minimally generated by a set of

has a

Lemma 2.12
.

Let

be an
n
-dimensional algebraic dyadic convex set, for

. Then

is isomorphic to

if and only if it is

-generated, or equivalently, it is generated by its

We already know that if

is isomorphic to a simplex

, then it is

We will show that each

-dimensional

is isomorphic to

. This is certainly true for

. Let

. Consider an

-dimensional convex set

minimally generated by the set

. Note that

would be less than

.) The generators of

generate a real

-dimensional

-generated polytope, which is obviously a real simplex. Since

is a free barycentric algebra, the mapping

.
∎

Note that the convex sets of Lemma

isomorphic to

On the other hand, if

Corollary 2.13
.

For

2.5.
Further comments

In this paper, dyadic polytopes and their subgroupoids are considered as subreducts of their affine
\mathbb{D}
-hulls. After introducing coordinate axes in such a space, a given polytope will be located in the corresponding
\mathbb{D}
-module by providing the coordinates of its vertices. An
n
-dimensional affine
\mathbb{D}
-space may be considered as a subreduct of the
n
-dimensional real space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The free generators of the dyadic simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
given by (
2.7
) are also free generators of the free affine
\mathbb{D}
-space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
and of the free affine
\mathbb{R}
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Finally, recall that automorphisms of the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
form the
n
-dimensional affine group
\mathrm{GA}(n,\mathbb{D})
over the ring
\mathbb{D}
, the group generated by the linear group
\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{D})

and the group of translations of the space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
. Moreover, each element of the affine group
\mathrm{GA}(n,\mathbb{D})
is also an automorphism of the groupoid
(\mathbb{D}^{n},\circ)
, and transforms any polytope in
\mathbb{D}^{n}
onto an isomorphic polytope. On the other hand, each automorphism of a nontrivial
n
-dimensional polytope extends to an automorphism of the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
. Indeed, by Corollary
2.11
, any such polytope contains the free simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
, with vertices generating the affine hull of the simplex and the polytope.
Isomorphisms of such polytopes in
\mathbb{D}^{n}
will be considered as restrictions of automorphisms of the affine space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
.

3.
Generating dyadic polytopes

It was shown in
[
11
]
that all dyadic intervals, all dyadic triangles, and more generally all dyadic polygons, are finitely generated. In this section we will show that the same holds for all dyadic polytopes.

Recall that dyadic polytopes, and in particular dyadic intervals, are geometric.
The following basic lemma is a direct consequence of the results of
[
7
, §3]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
a,b,c\in\mathbb{D}
with
a<b<c
. If one of the dyadic intervals
[a,b]
or
[b,c]
forms a dyadic simplex, then the groupoid generated by
\{a,b,c\}
coincides with the dyadic interval
[a,c]
.

Proof.

If the interval
[a,b]
is a dyadic simplex, then it is isomorphic to the dyadic interval
[0,1]
. Let
\iota\colon[a,b]\rightarrow[0,1]
be the required isomorphism assigning
0
to
a
and
1
to
b
. The mapping
\iota
extends uniquely to the affine space isomorphism
\bar{\iota}\colon\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
which assigns

d=\frac{c-a}{b-a}=\frac{2k+1}{2^{n}}\in\mathbb{D}

to
c
. The image
\bar{\iota}([a,c])
is generated as a groupoid by
\{0,1,d=\frac{2k+1}{2^{n}}\}
. Let
h
be the isomorphism between
\bar{\iota}([a,c])
and the subgroupoid
H
generated by
\{0,2^{n},2k+1\}
. By
[
7
, Cor. 3.9]
, the groupoid
H
coincides with the interval
[0,2k+1]
.
Now consider the isomorphism
\overline{i}h
between
[a,c]
and
H
. Then note that
(\overline{i}h)^{-1}
takes
2k+1
to
c
,
2^{n}
to
b
and
0
to
a
. Hence
[a,c]=\langle\{a,b,c\}\rangle
.

If
[b,c]
is a simplex, then we take the isomorphism
\iota\colon[b,c]\rightarrow[0,1]
assigning
0
to
c
and
1
to
b
. The remaining part of the proof goes similarly to the proof of the first part.
∎

Lemma 3.2

(The Density Lemma)
.

Let
A\neq B\in\mathbb{D}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
l(A,B)
be the line in the affine
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
going through
A
and
B
. Then for any two points
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
of

, there is a point
D
of
l(A,B)
belonging to
\mathbb{D}^{n}
and located between
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
A=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
and
B=(b_{1},\ldots,b_{n})
. The line

is described by the equations

x_{i}=(b_{i}-a_{i})t+a_{i}

for
i=1,\dots,n
. Let
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
be any points of
l(A,B)
,
C_{1}
with coordinates
(b_{i}-a_{i})t_{1}+a_{i}
and
C_{2}
with coordinates
(b_{i}-a_{i})t_{2}+a_{i}
for some
t_{1},t_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. There exists a dyadic number
d
lying between
t_{1}
and
t_{2}
. Then the point
D
with coordinates
(b_{i}-a_{i})d+a_{i}
belongs to
\mathbb{D}^{n}
and is located between
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
.
∎

.

.

Let

Theorem 3.4
.

Each
n
-dimensional dyadic polytope is finitely generated.

Proof.

The proof is by induction on
n
. Suppose that
P
is a (nontrivial)
n
-dimensional dyadic polytope. If
n=1
, then
P
is a dyadic interval, and by the results of
[
11
]
, it is (minimally) generated by
2
or

generators.

Now let

. Assume that the theorem holds for all polytopes of dimension smaller than
n
. Let the dimension of
P
be

. The polytope
P
contains finitely many
(n-1)
-dimensional walls, say
W_{1},\dots,W_{r}
. By the induction hypothesis, each of them is finitely generated. Moreover, by Corollary
2.11
, the polytope
P
contains infinitely many geometric
n
-dimensional simplices. Let
\mathbf{S}
be one of them. Obviously, each point of
\mathbf{S}
is generated by the vertices of
\mathbf{S}
.

We will show that any point
A
of
P
, contained neither in any of the walls

, nor in the simplex
\mathbf{S}
, is generated by the generators of these walls and the generators of the simplex.
By Lemma
3.3
, there is a point
B
in a maximal wall

and a point
C
in the interior
\rm{int}(\mathbf{S})
of
\mathbf{S}
such that the interval with ends
B
and
C
contains
A
. Without loss of generality, assume that this interval is
[B,C]
.
Consider the (geometric) subset
\mathbf{S}\cap[B,C]
of the line

. By Corollary
2.11
, this subset contains a one-dimensional simplex, say
[D,C]
, with
D
between
A
and
C
. Note that the points

and
C
are generated by the generators of the simplex
\mathbf{S}
, and the point

is generated by the generators of

. By Lemma

, the points

and

generate the whole interval

containing the point

. It follows that the polytope

is generated by the finite number of the generators of

and of the simplex
\mathbf{S}
.
∎

Remark 3.5
.

Recall from Section

that each

-dimensional polytope

of

As was shown in

We would like to know if every finitely generated subgroupoid of

We will show, however, that finitely generated finite-dimensional algebraic dyadic convex sets are not far from being polytopes.

Definition 4.2
.

A subgroupoid

Remark 4.3
.

In

12
]
, the concept of a

in an affine
\mathbb{R}
-space was introduced. This is a convex set

\mathbb{R}

does not need to contain the vertices of

. Now define a real semipolytope, similarly as in the dyadic case. Such real semipolytopes are simply polytopes. On the other hand, quasipolytopes may be defined in the dyadic case as well. (We consider the usual Euclidean topology on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and then restrict it to
\mathbb{D}^{n}
.) Each dyadic semipolytope is a quasipolytope. However, dyadic quasipolytopes are not necessarily semipolytopes. Examples are provided by open polytopes (interiors of polytopes). Note also that the topological closures of geometric dyadic convex sets admit a purely algebraic description
[
4
]
.

Let

of

(\rm a)

.

Consider an

(a)

Lemma 4.9
.

Let

be a finitely generated algebraic dyadic convex set with the
n
-dimensional affine
\mathbb{D}
-hull

, a subspace of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
.
Then the following hold.

(a)

The walls of
C
are the intersections with

\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)
.

(b)

The union of the maximal walls of

(c)

The equality

(d)

The dyadic convex sets

\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)
,
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{D}}(C)

(e)

The convex

\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)
of

A
-polytopes.

(f)

The dyadic convex set

Proof.

(\rm a)
and
(\rm b)
are obvious, while
(\mathrm{c})
follows directly from the fact that

.

(\rm d)
Since

is finitely generated, it follows that the barycentric algebra
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(C)
is also finitely generated. Hence it is a real polytope. Its vertices belong to

\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)
, and to
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{D}}(C)
.

(\rm e)
Since

is a dyadic polytope. Then, by (

) and

(\rm f)
This follows directly by

and
(\rm e)
.

Proposition 4.10
.

Each finitely generated algebraic dyadic convex set is isomorphic to a semipolytope.

Proof.

Let
C
be an algebraic convex subset of
\mathbb{D}^{p}
generated by a finite set
X=\{x_{0},x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}\}
. Let
A=\mathrm{aff}_{\mathbb{D}}(C)
with
\dim A=n\leq p
and
n\leq r
.

By Lemma
4.9
,
C
is a semipolytope in
A
.
Moreover,
C
,
\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)
,
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{D}}(C)
and
\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(C)
have the same set
V
of vertices contained in
X
. Moreover,
\rm{int}(C)=\rm{int}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))
.

By Lemma
2.10
,
C
contains an
n
-dimensional algebraic simplex
\mathbf{S}
isomorphic as a groupoid to the (geometric) dyadic simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. The simplex
\mathbf{S}
is also contained in
A=\rm{aff}_{\mathbb{D}}(C)
, and its vertices
s_{0},\dots,s_{n}
freely generate the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space
A
, which is a subspace of
\mathbb{D}^{n}
(isomorphic to
\mathbb{D}^{n}
) and the affine
\mathbb{R}
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

On the other hand, the space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
contains the dyadic simplex
\mathbf{S}_{n}
. Its vertices
e_{0},\dots,e_{n}
freely generate the groupoid
\mathbf{S}_{n}
, the affine
\mathbb{D}
-space
\mathbb{D}^{n}
, and the affine
\mathbb{R}
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The bijective mapping
\iota\colon s_{i}\mapsto e_{i}
assigning the vertices of
\mathbf{S}
to the vertices
of
\mathbf{S}_{n}
extends uniquely to the authomorphism
\overline{\overline{\iota}}
of the affine
\mathbb{R}
-space
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, restricting to the isomorphism
\overline{\iota}
between the affine
\mathbb{D}
-spaces
A
and
\mathbb{D}^{n}
. The authomorphism
\overline{\overline{\iota}}
restricts to the groupoid isomorphism between

and its image
\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))=\overline{\overline{\iota}}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))
in
\mathbb{D}^{n}
.

Our aim is to prove that
\overline{\iota}(C)
is a semipolytope, which implies that

is isomorphic to a semipolytope.

First note that
\overline{\iota}(C)
is a subgroupoid of
\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))
.
Since
\overline{\iota}
is an isomorphism, it follows by Lemma
4.9

\overline{\iota}(C)

\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))
have the same vertices and the same (relative) interiors.
It remains to show that

is a dyadic polytope. By (
2.4
), this means that

(4.2)

\overline{\iota}\big(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)\big)=\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))\big)\cap\mathbb{D}^{n}.

The inclusion
\overline{\iota}\big(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C)\big)\subseteq\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))\big)\cap\mathbb{D}^{n}
follows by (

first observe that

(4.3)

\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}\big(\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))\big)=\overline{\overline{\iota}}\big(\mathrm{conv}_{\mathbb{R}}(C)\big).

Now let

Then

\overline{\iota}(\mathrm{conv}_{A}(C))

Proposition 4.12
.

Each semipolytope is finitely generated.

Proof.

Let

The proof is by induction on

If the dimension of

Now assume that the result holds for all dimensions less or equal to

are intersections of the walls of the polytope

with the semipolytope

, and

has a finite number of walls. By Proposition

, all proper nontrivial walls of

(each of dimension not greater than

Now choose (dyadic) anchors

of the maximal walls

of

, and consider the corresponding inner polytope
P
of

Recall that, by the induction hypothesis, all maximal proper walls are finitely generated, and by Theorem

,

is also finitely generated.
We will show that each point of
S
, not contained in a maximal proper wall
W_{i}
or in the inner polytope

, is generated by an element of a maximal proper wall and two elements of the inner polytope
P
.
The proof is similar to the last part of the proof of Theorem
3.4
.
Let
A
be an interior point of

not contained in the inner polytope

.
By Lemma

, there is a point

in a maximal wall

of

and a point

in

such that

of the (real) line

and

and

Then, by Corollary

contains a point

generate the whole dyadic interval

5.
Main result

The results obtained in the previous sections may be summarized by the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1
.

A subgroupoid
(D,\circ)
of the groupoid
(\mathbb{D}^{n},\circ)
is finitely generated precisely if it is isomorphic to a semipolytope.

Remark 5.2
.

Recall that each
n
-dimensional semipolytope
S
is the standard sum of an inner polytope
P
and outer subsemipolytopes
P_{i}
determined by
P
.
The proof of Theorem
5.1
shows that each point of
S
contained in any of the
P_{i}
, but not contained in a maximal proper wall or in the inner polytope
P
, is generated by an element of a maximal proper wall of
S
and two elements of the inner polytope
P
. As the maximal walls and the inner polytope
P
are all finitely generated, the proof provides a recursive method of finding generators of
S
. To generate the semipolytope
S
, it is enough to take the generators of all the maximal walls, and of the polytope
P
.

The following example shows that there are geometric dyadic convex sets, with a finite number of vertices, which are not finitely generated.

Example 5.3
.

Consider the unit disc in the real plane, and its intersection
U
with the dyadic plane. The boundary of the unit disc is the circle given by the equation
x^{2}+y^{2}=1
. Note that the points
(-1,0)
,
(0,-1)
,
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
belong to the circle. For any dyadic point
(j/2^{m},k/2^{n})
of the circle, with say
0<m\leq n
, and odd integers
j
and
k
, we have

(5.1)

k^{2}=2^{2(n-m)}(2^{2m}-j^{2}).

(The case when
0<n\leq m
, will be similar.)
Note that the left-hand side of (
5.1
) is odd. If
m\neq n
, then the right-hand side of (
5.1
) is even. If
m=n
, then
j^{2}+k^{2}=2^{2n}
. Let
j=2a+1
and
k=2b+1
. Then
1/2(j^{2}+k^{2})=2(a^{2}+a+b^{2}+b)+1=2^{2n-1}
. This holds only if
n=1/2
, again a contradiction. Consequently, the unique dyadic points belonging to the circle are
(-1,0)
,
(0,-1)
,
(1,0)
and
(0,1)
. It follows that
U
consists of the dyadic points of the interior of the real unit disc and these four points. The four points generate the geometric square contained in
U
. But no finite set of elements of
U
generates the full set of points of
U
not contained in the square.

6.
Counting the number of generators

In this concluding section, we will illustrate how to use the main results of the paper to find a minimal set of generators of some semi-polygons, and estimate their number.

In
[
11
]
, it was shown that all dyadic polygons (and in particular all intervals and triangles) are finitely generated. However, the methods we used in the proof did not provide an efficient way to find a minimal set of generators, or to calculate their number.
In this section we will show how to use the results of the previous sections to solve such problems. In particular, we will show how to find a minimal set of generators of dyadic triangles and semipolytopes closing to triangles, i.e. semipolytopes with dyadic triangles as their closure. We will call such semipolytopes
semitriangles
.

We already know that finitely generated (algebraic) convex subsets of the dyadic line
\mathbb{D}
are isomorphic to intervals, and that each dyadic interval is minimally generated by
2
or
3
elements. As shown in
[
11
]
,
each nontrivial interval of
\mathbb{D}
is isomorphic to some dyadic interval

\mathbb{D}_{k}=[0,k]
, where
k
is an odd positive integer. Two such intervals are isomorphic
precisely when their right hand ends are equal. Any set of generators must contain the vertices. If
k\neq 1
, then to generate
\mathbb{D}_{k}
, one more generator is needed. This can be the number
1
, for example. As semipolytopes in
\mathbb{D}
coincide with polytopes, we do not need to consider them separately.

6.2.
Dyadic semitriangles

Next we consider semitriangles, i.e. semi-polytopes closing to triangles. Without lose of generality, we assume that a semitriangle
S
is a
representative semitriangle
, with a representative triangle
T=T_{i,j,m,n}
as its closure. The one-dimensional walls of
S
will be called its
sides
. A semitriangle
S
which is not a polytope has at least one side which is not an interval. Each side of
S
is isomorphic to an interval; hence it is generated by
2
or
3

S
must contain the minimal set of generators of the sides.) To find the remaining generators of
S
, one may use the method described in the proof of Proposition
4.12
. Since anchors belong to the sides of
S
, they are generated by the generators of the sides. So we only need to add the remaining generators of the inner polytope
P
of

. Since
P
is a dyadic triangle, the minimal number of its generators may be found using the method described in section
6.1
.
As we have seen in Example
4.1
, it is sometimes possible to find a smaller set of generators of a semitriangle.
We will show that, as in the case of triangles, each semitriangle is generated by the generators of its maximal walls and generators of a two-dimensional simplex contained in it.

For any representative semitriangle
S
in the plane
\mathbb{D}^{2}
, and any point
E
in the interior of
S
which is a barycentric combination of the three vertices of
S
, let
\mathbf{S}
be a right two-dimensional simplex contained in

with shorter sides parallel to the axes intersecting at

. The following lemma is a version of Lemma

Lemma 6.6

Let

Then for any interior point

there exist a point

on a side of
S
, a right (geometric) simplex

as described above, and a point
