Molecular electronic junctions with stochastic structural transitions
Daniel S. Kosov

Abstract
We present a theoretical approach to include dynamical aspects of molecular structural fluctuations, such as, for example, forming and breaking hydrogen bonds, isomerizations, and dynamical supramolecular structures, in nonequilibrium Green’s functions electron transport calculations. Structural transitions are treated as a stochastic telegraph process, and the primary quantity of interest is a retarded Green’s function averaged over realizations of a stochastic process. Using the Novikov-Furutsu functional stochastic calculus method, we derive equations of motion for stochastically averaged retarded Green’s function in closed form. Consequently, we obtain the expression for electric current averaged over transitions, which depends not only on probabilities of observing particular molecular structures but also on the dynamics. However, the proposed method has a significant limitation - we have to assume that the imaginary parts of retarded self-energies produced by left and right electrodes are proportional to each other; this significantly restricts possible applications of the theory. Several examples illustrate the proposed approach.



I
Introduction

Fluctuations of geometry are often present in molecular electronic junctions.
Bistable molecular conformations
Wassel et al. 2003
;
Baber et al. 2008
;
Donhauser et al. 2001
;
Auwärter et al. 2012
;
Cho et al. 2018
;
Artes et al. 2014
, forming and breaking molecule-electrode chemical bonds
Nichols et al. 2010
have been observed experimentally. The stochastic switching between several molecular junction structures is ubiquitous in the emerging field of (supra) molecular electronics, where host-guest interactions, hydrogen bonding,
\pi-\pi
interactions, and non-covalent interactions connect mechanically interlocked molecules
Chen and Fraser Stoddart 2021
.

If stochastic structural changes are slow compared to electron tunneling time, the problem becomes trivial. One simply must perform standard static electron transport calculations for several frozen molecular geometries and average the result. However, if the stochastic switching rate becomes comparable with the rate for electron transfer, the problem becomes dynamic, and the proper treatment is highly nontrivial. The development of a practical theory for electron transport, which considers the dynamics of stochastic switching, is our goal here.

We assume that the molecular junction switches randomly between two static molecular Hamiltonians with some constant switching rate as time progresses. That means we take that the telegraph process describes stochastic dynamics.
The theoretical literature on quantum transport with telegraphic switching Hamiltonians exists but the scope is limited. In the 1990s, Galperin et al. studied average transparency
Galperin et al. 1994
and low-frequency noise
Galperin and Chao 1995
through double barriers with dynamic impurity. Entin-Wohlman et al.
Entin-Wohlman et al. 2017
used nonequlibrium Green’s functions (NEGF) to study quantum heat transport via a fluctuating electronic level, proposed as a model for an applied stochastic electric field. Gurvitz et al.
Gurvitz et al. 2016
also used a fluctuating electronic level and analyzed steady-state along with transient dynamics. We have recently investigated telegraph noise in a junction with electron-phonon and electron-electron interactions within the master equation framework by adding a stochastic component into the Liouvillian
Kosov 2018
or introducing additional rates
Rudge and Kosov 2019
into quantum master equations.

The master equation method
Kosov 2018
;
Rudge and Kosov 2019
to deal with the problem suffered from the common deficiencies of the master equation approach to quantum transport, such as the Markovian assumption, weak electrode-molecule coupling, and the lack of level broadening. Previous NEGF approaches
Galperin et al. 1994
;
Galperin and Chao 1995
;
Entin-Wohlman et al. 2017
treated the problem within the wide-band approximation and restricted the treatment to a single resonant level. These approximations severely limit the applications but simplify the treatment considerably. For example, the imaginary part of retarded self-energy becomes a delta-function within the wide-band approximation enabling the simple mathematical handling of stochastic equations of motion for Green’s function, and the use of a single level trivializes the calculation of time-ordered exponents.

Here, we develop NEGF-based electron transport theory without resorting to the wide-band treatment of electrodes nor making any assumption about the size or structure of the molecular Hamiltonian.
We present the expression for electric current averaged over stochastic process associated with molecular transitions in a form ready to be used for practical calculations of large molecular electronic junctions. However, we must admit that our approach is incomplete; it suffers from a limitation (though less severe than in the methods previously developed) - we have to assume that level broadening functions of the left and right electrodes are proportional to each other. This limitation stems from our inability to compute averaged over the stochastic process lesser Green’s function - the reason for this will be made clear later in the paper.

We use atomic units for quantum transport throughout the paper.

II
Theory

II.1
Hamiltonian with telegraphic switching between two molecular isomers

The Hamiltonian of the molecular junction consists of Hamiltonians for molecule
H_{M}
, left and right electrodes,
H_{L}
and
H_{R}
, and the interaction which couples the molecule to left and right electrodes,
H_{ML}

and
H_{MR}
:

H(t)=H_{M}(t)+H_{L}+H_{R}+H_{ML}+H_{MR}.

(1)

Suppose that the molecule can switch between two states A and B described by different Hamiltonians

H_{A}
and
H_{B}
.
These transitions between A and B make the molecular Hamiltonian explicitly time-dependent
H_{M}(t)
.
We write Hamiltonians for the states A and B in the following general forms:

H_{A/B}(t)=\sum_{ij}H^{A/B}_{ij}a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j},

(2)

where

H^{A/B}_{ij}
are matrix elements computed in molecular single-particle states and
a^{\dagger}_{i}
(
a_{i}
) creates (annihilates) an electron in the molecular single-particle states
i
in the molecule.
To cast these Hamiltonians in the form of stochastic telegraphic process we write matrices
\bm{H}^{A/B}
as

\bm{H}^{A}=\bm{H}+\bm{V}

(3)

and

\bm{H}^{B}=\bm{H}-\bm{V}.

(4)

This enables us to write the time-dependent molecular Hamiltonian in the following form

H_{M}(t)=\sum_{ij}[H_{ij}+\xi(t)V_{ij}]a^{\dagger}_{i}a_{j},

(5)

where function
\xi(t)
describes stochastic telegraph progress; it switches stochastically between two states as the time progresses

\xi(t)=x(-1)^{n(0,t)},

(6)

where
n(t,t^{\prime})
is the random sequence of integer numbers describing the number of switches on the time interval
[t,t^{\prime}]
.
We assume that the initial isomer is not known, the molecule have equal probabilities to be observed in state A or B. Therefore,

x
is random variable with distribution

p(x)=\frac{1}{2}(\delta(x-1)+\delta(x+1))

(7)

We assume that the distribution of times at which the transitions
A\leftrightarrow B
occur is the Poisson stationary distribution, hence
the probability to observe
n
transitions during the time interval
[t,t^{\prime}]
is

p_{n(t,t^{\prime})=n}=\frac{{\overline{n(t,t^{\prime})}~}^{n}}{n!}e^{-\overline{n(t,t^{\prime})}}

(8)

with average number of telegraph jumps during the time interval

\overline{n(t,t^{\prime})}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}np_{n(t,t^{\prime})=n}=k_{AB}(t-t^{\prime}),

(9)

where
k_{AB}
is the average number of transitions per unit time - transition rate.
Using the distribution (
8
), we compute the first few moments of the stochastic function
\xi(t)
averaging over the realisations of the stochastic process.

\Big\langle\xi(t)\Big\rangle_{\xi}=0

(10)

where
\Big\langle...\Big\rangle_{\xi}
means the averaging over all possible realisation of stochastic process
\xi(t)
as well as the aditional averaging over distribution
p(x)
.
Two-time correlation function computed for
t_{1}\geq t_{2}
is

\Big\langle\xi(t_{1})\xi(t_{2})\Big\rangle_{\xi}=\Big\langle x(-1)^{n(0,t_{1})}x(-1)^{n(0,t_{2})}\Big\rangle_{\xi}\\
=\Big\langle x^{2}(-1)^{n(t_{2},t_{1})}\Big\rangle_{\xi}=e^{-2k_{AB}(t_{1}-t_{2})},

(11)

and all higher order correlation functions can be factorised if they are preliminary time-ordered (
t_{1}\geq t_{2}\geq t_{3}\geq...\geq t_{N}
)

\Big\langle\xi(t_{1})\xi(t_{2})\xi(t_{3})...\xi(t_{N})\Big\rangle_{\xi}\\
=\Big\langle\xi(t_{1})\xi(t_{2})\Big\rangle_{\xi}\Big\langle\xi(t_{3})...\xi(t_{N})\Big\rangle_{\xi}.

(12)

We refer to the textbook on stochastic dynamical systems which contains many details relevant to our discussion here
Klyatskin 2011
.

The electrodes are represented by non-interacting Hamiltonians with continuum spectrum

H_{L}+H_{R}=\sum_{k\alpha}\varepsilon_{k\alpha}a^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}a_{k\alpha},

(13)

the coupling between molecule and electrode is giving by tunneling interaction

H_{ML}+H_{MR}=\sum_{k\alpha i}(t_{k\alpha i}a^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}a_{i}+\mbox{h.c.}).

(14)

Here
a^{\dagger}_{k\alpha}
(
a_{k\alpha}
) creates (annihilates) an electron in the single-particle state with energy
\varepsilon_{k\alpha}
of either the left (
\alpha=L
) or the right (
\alpha=R
) electrodes, and
t_{k\alpha i}
tunneling amplitudes between single-particle states in electrodes and molecule.

II.2
Green’s functions and self-energies

The exact (computed with full time-dependent stochastic Hamiltonian) molecular space Green’s functions are

G_{ij}^{A}(t,t^{\prime})=i\theta(t^{\prime}-t)\langle\{a_{i}(t),a^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle,

(15)

G_{ij}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})=-i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\langle\{a_{i}(t),a^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime})\}\rangle,

(16)

G_{ij}^{<}(t,t^{\prime})=i\langle a^{\dagger}_{j}(t^{\prime})a_{i}(t)\rangle.

(17)

The self-energies of electrodes are not affected by the time-dependence of the molecular Hamiltonian, therefore, they are defined in standard way
Haug and Jauho 2010
. Left and right retarded self-energies are

{\Sigma}_{\alpha ij}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})=-i\theta(t-t^{\prime})\sum_{k}t^{*}_{k\alpha i}e^{-i\epsilon_{k\alpha}(t-t^{\prime})}t_{lj}.

(18)

The advanced and retarded self-energies are related to each other via Hermitian conjugation:

{\Sigma}_{\alpha ij}^{A}(t,t^{\prime})=\Big({\Sigma}_{\alpha ji}^{R}(t^{\prime},t)\Big)^{*}.

(19)

The lesser self-energies are

{\Sigma}_{\alpha ij}^{<}(t,t^{\prime})=2\pi i\sum_{\alpha}t^{*}_{k\alpha i}f_{L}(\epsilon_{k\alpha})e^{-i\epsilon_{k\alpha}(t-t^{\prime})}t_{lj}

(20)

where
f_{\alpha}
are the Fermi-Dirac occupation numbers for electrode
\alpha
.
The total self-energies are the sum of contributions from the left and right electrodes

{\Sigma}^{R,A,<}_{ij}(t,t^{\prime})={\Sigma}^{R,A,<}_{Lij}(t,t^{\prime})+{\Sigma}_{Rij}^{R,A,<}(t,t^{\prime}).

(21)

The retarded self-energies in the energy domain are defined in the usual way as a Fourier transformation of time domain self-energies defined above:

\Sigma_{\alpha ij}^{R}(\omega)=\Delta_{\alpha ij}(\omega)-\frac{i}{2}\Gamma_{\alpha ij}(\omega),

(22)

where the level-width functions are

\Gamma_{\alpha ij}(\omega)=2\pi\sum_{k}\delta(\omega-\epsilon_{k\alpha})t^{*}_{k\alpha i}t_{k\alpha j}.

(23)

The level-shift functions
\Delta_{L/Rij}(\omega)
can be computed from
\Gamma_{L/Rij}(\omega)
via Kramers-Kronig relation
Haug and Jauho 2010
.
The advanced self-energy is computed from the retarded self-energy as

\Sigma_{\alpha ij}^{A}(\omega)=(\Sigma_{\alpha ji}^{R}(\omega))^{*},

(24)

and, then the lesser self-energy calculated using advanced and retarded self-energies

\Sigma_{\alpha ij}^{<}(\omega)=f_{\alpha}(\omega)\left(\Sigma_{\alpha ij}^{A}(\omega)-\Sigma_{\alpha ij}^{R}(\omega)\right)=if_{\alpha}(\omega)\Gamma_{\alpha ij}(\omega).

(25)

II.3
Averaging of retarded Green’s function over the stochastic process

Our calculations of electric current in section (II-D) will require the knowledge of retarded Green’s functions only.
Therefore,
we begin with equation of motion for the retarded Green’ s function:

(i\partial_{t}-\bm{H}-\xi(t)\bm{V})\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\\
-\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\bm{G}^{R}(t_{1},t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime}),

(26)

the matrix multiplication is implied here and below.
Next, we average this equation of motion over the stochastic process.
The averaging gives

(i\partial_{t}-\bm{H})\langle\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}-\bm{V}\langle{\xi}(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}\\
-\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\langle\bm{G}^{R}(t_{1},t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\delta(t-t^{\prime}).

(27)

In computing the average we took into account that self-energy does not depend on stochastic process
\xi(t)
.
Alas this equation (
27
) is not in the closed form to be solved since the average
\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}
is not known.
Let us evaluate it.
We notice that the Green’s function can be considered as a functional of the stochastic process
\xi(t)

\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})=\bm{G}^{R}[\xi](t,t^{\prime}).

(28)

Using Shapiro-Loginov differential formula in stochastic calculus
Shapiro and Loginov 1978
(derivations for the derivative of
G^{R}
are shown in appendix B) gives

(\partial_{t}+2k_{AB})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\langle\xi(t)\;\partial_{t}\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}.

(29)

Substitution
\partial_{t}\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})
from the initial equation of motion (
26
) into rhs of (
29
)
gives

(i\partial_{t}+2ik_{AB}-\bm{H})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}\\
-\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t_{1},t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\bm{V}\langle\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}

(30)

Since
\Sigma^{R}(t,t_{1})
decays very quickly as
t
deviates from
t_{1}
, for example, in the extreme case of wide band approximation
\Sigma^{R}(t,t_{1})\sim\delta(t-t_{1})
, it is safe to approximate the integral as

\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t_{1},t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}\\
\simeq\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\langle\xi(t_{1})\bm{G}^{R}(t_{1},t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}

(31)

The result is the system of two integro-differential equations

\left.\begin{array}[]{l}(i\partial_{t}-\bm{H})\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})-\bm{V}\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\\
-\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(t_{1},t^{\prime})=\delta(t-t^{\prime})\\
\\
(i\partial_{t}+2ik_{AB}-\bm{H})\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\\
-\int dt_{1}\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(t,t_{1})\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})=\bm{V}\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\end{array}\right\}.

(32)

Here we introduced the following notations for stochastically averaged quantities

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi},

(33)

\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})=\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}.

(34)

The stochastically averaged retarded Green’s function
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})
depends on relative time only, and we see from the first equation in the system of equations (
32
) that

\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})
must depend on relative time only too. Therefore, we can conveniently employ the Fourier transform to remove integrals and time derivatives, and bring (
32
) to the form

\left.\begin{array}[]{c}(\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega))\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)-\bm{V}\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(\omega)=\bm{I}\\
\\
(\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)+2ik_{AB})\bm{\mathcal{F}}^{R}(\omega)=\bm{V}\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)\end{array}\right\}

(35)

Matrix
\bm{\mathcal{F}^{R}}(\omega)
can be eliminated from the system of equations resulting into the following expression for the retarded Green’s function

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)\\
=\Big[\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)-\bm{V}\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)+2ik_{AB}}\bm{V}\Big]^{-1}

(36)

Let’s check the limiting cases. If
k_{AB}\rightarrow\infty
, the junctions switches infinitely fast between conformations and a tunneling electron does not see the presence of the stochastic potential
\xi(t)\bm{V}
at all. Indeed, in this case

\bm{V}\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)+2ik_{AB}}\bm{V}\rightarrow 0

(37)

in (
36
) and, as we have expected,
the retarded Green’s function is reduced to the static expression for the junction described by time-independent Hamiltonian
\bm{H}

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)=\Big[\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)\Big]^{-1}.

(38)

In the opposite limit
k_{AB}=0
, the junction does not undego any telegraphic switching between confirmations, however, the averaging is still performed over initial state using distribution (
7
). Using stanard matrix algebra we get

\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)=\Big[\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)-\bm{V}\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)}\bm{V}\Big]^{-1}\\
=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{H}-\bm{V}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)}+\frac{1}{\omega-\bm{H}+\bm{V}-\bm{\Sigma}^{R}(\omega)}\Big),

(39)

which again is consistent with our physical expectations.

II.4
Current

The general, exact expression for electric current from
\alpha
electrode into the molecule is
Haug and Jauho 2010

J_{\alpha}(t)=2\;\text{Re}\int dt^{\prime}\text{Tr}\Big[\bm{G}^{<}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{\Sigma}^{A}_{\alpha}(t^{\prime},t)\\
+\bm{G}^{R}(t,t^{\prime})\bm{\Sigma}^{<}_{\alpha}(t^{\prime},t)\Big].

(40)

Averaging over stochastic process makes current time-independent and moving to Fourier space gives

\langle J_{L}\rangle_{\xi}=2\;\text{Re}\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}\text{Tr}\Big[\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{<}(\omega)\bm{\Sigma}^{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)+\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)\bm{\Sigma}^{<}_{\alpha}(\omega)\Big].

(41)

Making assumption that imaginary parts of retarded or advanced self-energies for left and right electrodes are proportional to each other,
the average current can be expressed only in terms of retarded Green’s function as

\langle J_{L}\rangle_{\xi}=\int\frac{d\omega}{2\pi}T(\omega)(f_{L}(\omega)-f_{R}(\omega)),

(42)

where

T(\omega)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\text{Tr}\Big[\frac{\bm{\Gamma}_{L}(\omega)\bm{\Gamma}_{R}(\omega)}{\bm{\Gamma}_{L}(\omega)+\bm{\Gamma}_{R}(\omega)}\text{Im}\{\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)\}\big]

(43)

is the transmission coefficient averaged over the stochastic process and
\bm{\mathcal{G}}^{R}(\omega)
is given by equation (
36
).

IV
Conclusions

We have developed a theory that includes dynamics of molecular stochastic structural changes in nonequilibrium Green’s functions electron transport calculations.
In our theory, we assumed that the molecular hamiltonian switches stochastically at a random time between two different operators. Furthermore, we took the distribution of times at which the transitions occur to be the Poisson stationary distribution with a constant average number of transitions per unit of time. These are standard assumptions of stochastic telegraph processes.

We derived equations of motion for stochastically averaged retarded Green’s function in closed form. The closure requires the additional differential equation for the auxiliary quantity
\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}(t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}
, which we obtained using the Novikov-Furutsu method of functional stochastic calculus. Next, we averaged electric current over time and received an expression for the transmission coefficient, which depends on the rates for structural molecular transitions.

The proposed method has a limitation - we had to assume that the imaginary parts of the left and right electrodes’ retarded self-energies are proportional to each other. This limitation significantly restricts possible molecular junction geometries to which we can apply the theory.

We illustrated the theory first by considering a simple model of electron transport through a single fluctuating current carrying molecular orbitale. Next, we studied the electrical properties of the supramolecular model complex with fluctuating links separating two structural parts.

Appendix B
Novikov-Furutsu functional approach to stochastic averaging

Following ideas of Novikov-Furutsu theory
Novikov 1965
;
Furutsu 1963
we introduce

\Big\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\xi},

(60)

where
z
is an arbitrary reasonably smooth deterministic function.
We expand
\bm{G}^{A}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})
in the Taylor-like series using functional differentiation around
\xi(t)=0

\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})=\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})+\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\frac{\delta\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})}{\delta z(t_{1})}\xi(t_{1})+\frac{1}{2!}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{2}\frac{\delta^{2}\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})}{\delta z(t_{1})\delta z(t_{2})}\xi(t_{1})\xi(t_{2})+....,

(61)

which can be compactly written with the use of the operator exponent
as

\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})=\exp\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\xi(t_{1})\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\Big\}\;\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime}).

(62)

Notice that the above derivations have subtly assume that the retarded Greens
G^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})
is a function of stochastic process
\xi(t_{1})
for
t_{1}\leq t
and it does not depend on
\xi(t_{1})
for
t_{1}>t
. It follows from the presence of step function
\theta(t-t^{\prime})
in the definition of the retarded Green’s function (
36
).
The similar calculations can be performed for
G^{A}[z](t,t^{\prime})
using
t^{\prime}
as a reference time for the functional shift. However, it is not clear to us how to perform
similar derivations for lesser Green’s function.

Let us now compute

\Big\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\xi}=\Big\langle\xi(t)\exp\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\xi(t_{1})\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\Big\}\Big\rangle_{\xi}\;\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime}).

(63)

Expanding the operator exponent, we get

\Big\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\Big\rangle_{\xi}=\\
\Big\langle\xi(t)\Big[1+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{2}....\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{k}\xi(t_{1})\xi(t_{2})...\xi(t_{k})\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{2})}...\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{k})}\Big]\Big\rangle_{\xi}\;\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})=\\
\Big[\Big\langle\xi(t)\Big\rangle_{\xi}+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}....\int_{-\infty}^{t_{k-1}}dt_{k}\Big\langle\xi(t)\xi(t_{1})\Big\rangle_{\xi}\Big\langle\xi(t_{2})...\xi(t_{k})\Big\rangle_{\xi}\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{2})}...\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{k})}\Big]\;\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})\\
=\Big[\Big\langle\xi(t)\Big\rangle_{\xi}+\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}\Big\langle\xi(t)\xi(t_{1})\Big\rangle_{\xi}\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\Big\langle\exp\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}dt^{\prime}\xi(t^{\prime})\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t^{\prime})}\Big\}\Big\rangle_{\xi}\Big]\;\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime}).

(64)

Substituting expression for moments of stochastic process yields

\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\Big[\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{-2k_{AB}(t-t_{1})}\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\Big\langle\exp\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\xi(t_{2})\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{2})}\Big\}\Big\rangle_{\xi}\Big]\;\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime}),

(65)

Differentiating the lhs and rhs of this equation with respect to
t
gives

(\partial_{t}+2k_{AB})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\Big[\int_{-\infty}^{t}dt_{1}e^{-2k_{AB}(t-t_{1})}\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{1})}\Big\langle\exp\Big\{\int_{-\infty}^{t_{1}}dt_{2}\xi(t_{2})\frac{\delta}{\delta z(t_{2})}\Big\}\Big\rangle_{\xi}\Big]\;\partial_{t}\bm{G}^{R}[z](t,t^{\prime})

(66)

Notice that when computing derivative we should not differentiate the time limit in the integral
Klyatskin 2011
.
Comparing rhs of the equation with (
65
) we get

(\partial_{t}+2k_{AB})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\langle\xi(t)\;\partial_{t}\bm{G}^{R}[\xi+z](t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}.

(67)

Setting
z(t)=0
in the above equation gives

(\partial_{t}+2k_{AB})\langle\xi(t)\bm{G}^{R}[\xi](t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}=\langle\xi(t)\;\partial_{t}\bm{G}^{R}[\xi](t,t^{\prime})\rangle_{\xi}.

(68)

This equation for time derivative of product of a stochastic functional with corresponding stochastic process is sometimes called the Shapiro-Loginov differential formula
Shapiro and Loginov 1978
.
