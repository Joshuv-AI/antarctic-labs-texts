Homology and homotopy for arbitrary categories
Suddhasattwa Das

Abstract
One of the prime motivation for topology was Homotopy theory, which captures the general idea of a continuous transformation between two entities, which may be spaces or maps. In later decades, an algebraic formulation of topology was discovered with the development of Homology theory. Some of the deepest results in topology are about the connections between Homotopy and Homology. These results are proved using intricate constructions. This paper re-proves these connections via an axiomatic approach that provides a common ground for homotopy and homology in arbitrary categories. One of the main contributions is a re-interpretation of convexity as an extrinsic rather than intrinsic property. All the axioms and results are applicable for the familiar context of topological spaces. At the same time it provides a complete framework for an algebraic characterization of objects in a general category, which also preserves a notion of Homotopy.

Category, Homology, Homotopy, Pushouts

The two pillars of modern topology are Homotopy theory and Homology theory. The former studies the continuous deformation of maps and spaces, and the various equivalences they create. On the other hand, homology provides a means of embedding the arrangement of topological spaces and continuous maps into a system of Abelian groups and group homomorphisms between them. The axiomatic approaches for homotopy

;
Riehl_homotopy_2014
and Homology

;
Kan1957css
;
Kan1958fnctrs
;
Andre1970hmlgy

(
Quillen2006hmtpy
;
DwyerSpalinski1995homotopy
, e.g.)
extends some notions from topology by formulating three abstract notions of morphisms - fibrations, cofibrations and weak equivalences. Another approach is that of
\infty
-categories
(
Cisinski2019higher
, e.g.)
, which is a reformulation of category theory itself so that notions of homotopy gets ingrained into the notions. Some other innovations in this field can be found in
(
GoerssJardine2009
;
DwyerKan1984singular
, e.g.)
.

Our goal is also to present a general axiomatic framework to establish the notions of homotopy and homology, and achieve the homology invariance with respect to homotopy equivalences. However, the axioms are much more simpler, verifiable, and have intuitive interpretations.

Categories and category theory provide a panoramic view of many of the various branches of mathematics, such as Dynamical systems theory

The concepts of Homology and Homotopy were respectively established as functor from the category of topological spaces into the category of Abelian groups, and from the category of pointed topological spaces into the category of general Groups. The concept of a functor provides a concise description of a mapping that also preserves the numerous relations between two arrangements. Simplex category

The simplex category

\Hom_{\Delta}(m;n):=\left\{\mbox{ Order preserving maps }\phi:[m]\to[n]\right\}.

The simplex category

;

;

. Our focus will be on a general functor

F:\Delta\to\mathcal{C}

(1)

thus creates an image of
\Delta

\mathcal{C}
. The objects

Generating morphisms

In general, it is not easy to make an explicit construction of a functor of the form (

0\leq i\leq n\;:\;{\color[rgb]{0.0586,0.3672,0.0742}d_{n,i}}:[n-1]\to[n],\,\quad j\mapsto\begin{cases}j&\mbox{ if }j<i\\
j+1&\mbox{ if }j\geq i\end{cases}.

(3)

0\leq i\leq n\;:\;{\color[rgb]{0.0586,0.3672,0.0742}s_{n,i}}:[n+1]\to[n],\quad j\mapsto\begin{cases}j&\mbox{ if }j\leq i\\
j-1&\mbox{ if }j>i\end{cases}.

(4)

To put more concisely, the

-th face map

is the unique map which skips the element

i

is the unique surjective map such that the pre-image of element

has two elements. The face and degeneracy maps generate every morphism
\phi:[m]\to[n]
in
\Delta

\begin{split}d_{n+1,i}d_{n,j}=d_{n+1,j+1}d_{n,i}&\mbox{ if }i<j\\
s_{n-1,j}d_{n,i}=d_{n-1,i}s_{n-2,j-1}&\mbox{ if }i<j\\
s_{n-1,j}d_{n,j}=\Id_{[n-1]}&\\
s_{n-1,j}d_{n,j+1}=\Id_{[n-1]}\\
s_{n-1,j}d_{n,i}=d_{n-1,i-1}s_{n-2,j}&\mbox{ if }i>j+1\\
s_{n-1,j}s_{n,i}=s_{n-1,i}s_{n,j+1}&\mbox{ if }i\leq j\end{split}

(5)

which are called the

Nerve construction

The general functor
F
induces a special functor called the
nerve
of the category
\mathcal{C}

Riehl2011sset
as shown below :

\begin{split}&\Nerve=\Nerve_{F}:\mathcal{C}\to\left[\text{sSet}\right],\\
&\forall c\in ob(\mathcal{C})\;:\;\Nerve(c):=\Hom\left(F\cdot;c\right):\Delta^{op}\to\left\llbracket\text{Set}\right\rrbracket.\end{split}

(8)

The functor
\Nerve_{F}
assigns a simplicial set to each object of
\mathcal{C}
. Thus it assigns a purely combinatorial identity to each object in
\mathcal{C}
. This functor will be the key ingredient to creating a notion of Homology, as we present later in (
13

2

\Nerve_{F}(X)
is the collection of all possible continuous mappings of the
n
-dimensional simplex into the topological object
X

\Nerve_{F}(X)
becomes a ledger for the topological “content” of
X
, and the entries of the ledger are all possible embeddings of
n
-dimensional disks. This feature of the nerve functor has found use in several categorical investigations, such as K-theory
(
Schwanzl1994basic
, e.g.)
, abstractions of homotopy theory
(
Rezk2001model
;
bergner2009complt
, e.g.)
, and dendroidal sets
(
Cisinski2013dendro
;
Cisinski2011dendro
, e.g.)
.

In the example of (
2

F(n+1)

. With this visual interpretation, each simplex can be interpreted to be the base / bottom face of the next higher simplex. The

. This proves that homotopy is preserved under pre- and post- composition. This bring us to the last and final property :

Composition of homotopies

Suppose there are three objects
X,Y,Z
, and homotopic pairs of morphisms
f,g:X\to Y
and
f^{\prime},g^{\prime}:Y\to Z
. Let these homotopies be
H,G
respectively. Then note that

\begin{split}f^{\prime}\circ f&\sim f^{\prime}\circ g,\quad\mbox{ post-composition of }H\mbox{ with }f^{\prime},\\
&\sim g^{\prime}\circ g,\quad\mbox{ pre-composition of }G\mbox{ with }g.\end{split}

The
\sim
denotes a homotopy relation in the Hom-set
\Hom(X;Z)
, whose transitivity has already been established. Thus we can conclude that
f^{\prime}\circ f
is homotopic to
g^{\prime}\circ g
. This relation can be summarized by saying that homotopies can be composed.

Homotopy equivalence

A morphism in
\Hom(X;X)
is said to be null-homotopic if it is homotopic to
X
. Two objects
A,B
will be called
homotopy equivalent

Riehl_homotopy_2014
if there are morphisms
f:A\to B
and
g:B\to A
such that
f\circ g\sim\Id_{B}
and
g\circ f\sim\Id_{A}
. In other words, both
f\circ g
and
g\circ f
are null-homotopic.

Under Axioms
1
,
2
,
3
and
4
the relation of being homotopic is symmetric, reflexive and transitive. Thus this relation can be extended to an equivalence relation on
\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(X;Y)
. One of the most important consequences of homotopy is that one can derive a new category
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
from
\mathcal{C}
, which has the same object-sets, but in which the Hom-set
\Hom_{\tilde{\mathcal{C}}}(X;Y)
are the homotopy equivalence classes of
\Hom_{\mathcal{C}}(X;Y)
. This is a well defined operation which retains the compositionality of
\mathcal{C}
. Note that homotopy equivalence in
\mathcal{C}
is the same as isomorphism in
\tilde{\mathcal{C}}
.

This completes the construction of the notion of homotopy. See Figure

for an overview of this process. We have now seen the constructions of homotopy and homology along two different routes. We next see how they are inter-linked, due to ground assumptions we make on

and

\begin{split}\partial_{n+1}\left(\Psi_{n}\sigma\right)&=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}(-1)^{i}\left(\Psi_{n}\sigma\right)\circ F\left(d_{n+1,i}\right)=\sigma+\sum_{i=1}^{n+1}(-1)^{i}\left(\Psi_{n}\sigma\right)\circ F\left(d_{n+1,i}\right)\\

\tau:F(n)\to F(n)

\left(\sigma\times F(1)\right)\circ P^{F(N)}_{N}(\tau)=\left(\sigma\times F(1)\right)\circ\left(\tau\times F(1)\right)\circ\beta_{N+1}=\left(\sigma\tau\times F(1)\right)\circ\beta_{N+1}.

The CCW path gives

P^{X}_{N}\left(\sigma\circ\tau\right)=\left(\sigma\tau\times F(1)\right)\circ\beta_{N+1}.

Thus the two paths are equal, and (
30
) stands verified for
n=N+1
. This completes the inductive reasoning, and completes the proof of Lemma
4.4
. ∎
