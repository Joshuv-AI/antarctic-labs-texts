Whitney Numbers of Partial Dowling Lattices
Thomas Zaslavsky

Abstract
. The Dowling lattice Q n ​ ( 𝔊 ) Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G}) , 𝔊 \mathfrak{G} a finite group, generalizes the geometric lattice generated by all vectors, over a field, with at most two nonzero components. Abstractly, it is a fundamental object in the classification of finite matroids. Constructively, it is the frame matroid of a certain gain graph known as 𝔊 ⋅ K n ( V ) \mathfrak{G}{\cdot}K_{n}^{(V)} . Its Whitney numbers of the first kind enter into several important formulas. Ravagnani suggested and partially proved that these numbers of Q n ​ ( 𝔊 ) Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G}) and higher-weight generalizations are polynomial functions of | 𝔊 | |\mathfrak{G}| . We give a simple proof for Q n ​ ( 𝔊 ) Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G}) and its generalization to a wider class of gain graphs and biased graphs, and we determine the degrees and coefficients of the polynomials.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 05B35; Secondary 05C22, 05C31

The famous Dowling lattices
Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G})
of a group
\mathfrak{G}

[
1
]
, or more precisely their matroids, are one of the two fundamental types of matroid in the classification of Kahn and Kung
[
2
]
. They generalize to arbitrary groups the matroid of all vectors with at most two nonzero components in the vector space
F^{n}
,
F
a field.
We show that their Whitney numbers of the first kind, which are the coefficients of their characteristic polynomials and consequently of some importance, are themselves polynomials in the order of the group. This is obvious from Dowling’s own formulas, but we notice that this property generalizes considerably, to what we might call partial Dowling lattices.

My attention was drawn to this property by Ravagani’s study
[
3
]
of the same property of the higher-weight Dowling lattices, which he calls
\mathcal{H}(n,q,d)
. Such a lattice is the lattice of flats of the matroid of all vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
with at most
d
nonzero coordinates. The examples of weight
d=1
gives a free matroid; but those of weight
d=2
, which are the Dowling lattices
Q_{n}(\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times})
, are more complicated; and the higher-weight Dowling lattices have resisted analysis, in particular of their important characteristic polynomials which have implications for linear coding theory. Ravagnani suggested that the coefficients of these polynomials, which were dubbed Whitney numbers of the first kind by G.-C. Rota, are polynomial functions of
q
and obtained partial results. Stimulated by this work I looked at a different generalization, still in the realm of weight 2, and obtained conclusions that are fundamentally simple but with some complicated details.

1.
Gains and Dowlings

We begin by introducing Dowling’s lattices
Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G})
of a group
[
1
]
by way of their matroids.

A
gain graph

\Phi=(\Gamma_{\Phi},\phi)
consists of a graph
\Gamma_{\Phi}=(V,E)
and a
gain mapping

\phi
from oriented edges to a group
\mathfrak{G}
. That is, the gain
\phi(e)
depends on the orientation of the edge so that reversing the orientation inverts the gain, which we express in a formula as
\phi(e^{-1})=\phi(e)^{-1}
, where
e
is an oriented edge and
e^{-1}
denotes the opposite orientation. A particular kind of gain graph is the
\mathfrak{G}
-expansion of a graph
\Gamma
, written
\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma
, whose vertex set is
V=V(\Gamma)
and which has an edge
ge
with every possible gain
g\in\mathfrak{G}
for each edge
e\in E(\Gamma)
. A
half edge
is an edge with only one endpoint; we may add to the
\mathfrak{G}
-expansion a half edge at each vertex of a subset
X\subseteq V
, giving the
partially filled

\mathfrak{G}
-expansion
\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)}
. In particular, when
X=V
we call the result the
full

\mathfrak{G}
-expansion of
\Gamma
.

The associated
frame matroid

\mathbf{F}(\Phi)
of a gain graph
\Phi
(see
[
6
, Section 2]
) is defined on the edge set
E(\Phi)
with circuits of three kinds. The gain of a circle (i.e., a “cycle” or “polygon”)
C=e_{1}\cdots e_{l}
is
\phi(C)=\phi(e_{1})\cdots\phi(e_{l})
; this is sufficiently well defined because we only care whether the gain is the neutral element or not. If the gain is the neutral element,
C
is called
neutral
. The three kinds of circuit are these:

C1.

A neutral circle.

C2.

Two disjoint non-neutral circles together with a minimal connecting path, or two distinct non-neutral circles with exactly one common vertex. Either or both circles may be replaced by a half edge in this type of circuit.

C3.

A theta subgraph whose three circles are not neutral.

A subgraph or edge set is
balanced
if every circle is neutral and there are no half edges. The rank of an edge set
S
is

\operatorname{rk}(S)=|V|-b(S),

where
b(S)
denotes the number of balanced components of the spanning subgraph
(V,S)
. An edge set is
closed
, or a
flat
of
\mathbf{F}(\Phi)
, if it is maximal with its rank, i.e., adding any edge increases the rank.

The usual cycle matroid of
\Gamma
is the frame matroid of the gain graph consisting of the graph
\Gamma
with identity gains from the trivial group. We write
\mathbf{F}(\Gamma)
for this matroid.

The
Dowling matroid
of rank
n
of a group
\mathfrak{G}
is
\mathbf{F}(\mathfrak{G}K_{n}^{(V)})
and the
Dowling lattice

Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G})
is the lattice of flats of this matroid
[
7
, Examples 3.6, 5.6]
. Dowling originally considered
Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G})
as the lattice of the matroid formed by the weight 1 and 2 vectors in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
, that is,
\mathcal{H}(n,q,2)
, in which context the group
\mathfrak{G}
is the multiplicative group
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{\times}
of order
q-1
. (Both
q
and
q-1
appear naturally in Ravagnani’s formulas for Whitney numbers of higher-weight Dowling lattices. Does this suggest that both the additive and multiplicative groups of the field play a role in determining the matroid?)

2.
Chromatics and characteristics

A gain graph has a chromatic polynomial
\chi_{\Phi}(\lambda)
(see
[
7
, Section 3]
), one of whose definitions is

\chi_{\Phi}(\lambda):=\sum_{S\subseteq E}(-1)^{|S|}\lambda^{b(S)}.

We state the essential formulas. We assume
\Phi
has order
n
and write
\gamma=|\mathfrak{G}|
. The chromatic polynomial is monic of degree
n
. If
b(\Phi)=0
(as is the case for group expansions when
\gamma>1
or
X=V
), the chromatic polynomial equals the characteristic polynomial
p_{\mathbf{F}(\Phi)}(\lambda)
of the matroid; in general,

\chi_{\Phi}(\lambda)=\lambda^{b(\Phi)}p_{\mathbf{F}(\Phi)}(\lambda)

by
[
7
, Theorem 5.1]
. Thus, the two polynomials have the same coefficients aside from a possible displacement.
From the definition, obviously

(1)

\chi_{\Phi}(\lambda)=\lambda^{n}\text{ if }E(\Phi)=\varnothing.

A useful chromatic formula is the product rule,

(2)

\chi_{\Phi_{1}\cup\Phi_{2}}(\lambda)=\chi_{\Phi_{1}}(\lambda)\chi_{\Phi_{2}}(\lambda)

if
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
are disjoint gain graphs. The usual chromatic polynomial of a graph is the chromatic polynomial of the trivial group expansion:
\chi_{\Gamma}(\lambda)=\chi_{\mathbf{1}{\cdot}\Gamma}(\lambda)
, where
\mathbf{1}
denotes the trivial group.

We need the basic formula for chromatic polynomials of full group expansions, which is a reduction to ordinary graphs
[
7
, Examples 3.6 and 6.6]
:

(3)

\chi_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}}(\lambda)=\gamma^{n}\chi_{\Gamma}\Big(\frac{\lambda-1}{\gamma}\Big).

We note that this polynomial is independent of the group structure. One of Dowling’s main discoveries was (in slightly different language) this simple formula for
\Gamma=K_{n}
, in contrast to the generally hard problem of computing characteristic polynomials of matroids.

Formula (
3
) and the fact that
\chi_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}}(\lambda)
is a polynomial of degree
n
clearly imply that the coefficients are polynomials in
\gamma
. We want more detailed information.

3.
Whitneys

The characteristic polynomial of a matroid
M
has the form

p_{M}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^{\operatorname{rk}M}w_{i}(M)\lambda^{\operatorname{rk}M-i},

the coefficients
w_{i}(M)
being the
Whitney numbers of
M
of the first kind
. The chromatic polynomial of
\Phi
therefore has the form

\chi_{\Phi}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}w_{i}(\Phi)\lambda^{n-i},

where
w_{i}(\Phi)
is defined as
w_{i}(\mathbf{F}(\Phi))
; similarly,
w_{i}(\Gamma)
is defined as
w_{i}(\mathbf{F}(\Gamma))
. The Whitney numbers of a matroid are nonzero, alternate in sign, and begin with
w_{0}=1
and
-w_{1}=|E(M)|
(the number of atoms of
M
)
[
4
]
, so the same is true for those of a gain graph; but note that
w_{i}(\Phi)=0
for
i>\operatorname{rk}\mathbf{F}(\Phi)=n-b(\Phi)
.
Similarly, the Whitney numbers
w_{i}(\Gamma)
are nonzero for
0\leq i\leq n-c
and 0 otherwise, where
c
denotes the number of components of
\Gamma
and
n-c=\operatorname{rk}\mathbf{F}(\Gamma)
.

We simplify our work by changing to the
signless chromatic polynomial
,
\bar{\chi}_{\Phi}(\lambda):=(-1)^{n}\chi_{\Phi}(-\lambda)
, and the
signless Whitney numbers
, which are
\bar{w}_{i}=(-1)^{i}w_{i}=|w_{i}|
by the alternating sign property. Thus,

\bar{\chi}_{\Phi}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^{n-b(\Phi)}\bar{w}_{i}(\Phi)\lambda^{n-i}.

Equation (
3
) becomes

(4)

\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}}(\lambda)=\gamma^{n}\bar{\chi}_{\Gamma}\Big(\frac{\lambda+1}{\gamma}\Big).

For brevity we say “Whitney number” for both signed and signless Whitney numbers; the notation will show which is meant.

In group expansions we have, for example,

\bar{w}_{0}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})=1
and
\bar{w}_{1}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})=|E(\Gamma)|\gamma+|X|,

which obviously are polynomial functions of
\gamma
.
An exercise for the reader is to compute
\bar{w}_{2}
before reading Formula (
5
).

Theorem 1
.

Given a simple graph
\Gamma
of order
n>0
, a finite group
\mathfrak{G}
of order
\gamma>0
, and an integer
i=0,1,\dots,n
, the signless Whitney number
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)})
is a polynomial function of
\gamma
of degree
\min(i,n-c)
, with positive coefficients, i.e.,

(5)

\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)})=\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)\binom{n-j}{i-j}\gamma^{j}.

Proof.

The proof from Equation (
4
) is easy.

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\gamma^{n}\bar{\chi}_{\Gamma}\Big(\frac{\lambda+1}{\gamma}\Big)=\sum_{j}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)\gamma^{j}(\lambda+1)^{n-j}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)\gamma^{j}\sum_{k}\binom{n-j}{k}\lambda^{k}

\displaystyle={\sum\sum}_{0\leq k\leq n-j\leq n}\lambda^{k}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)\gamma^{j}\sum_{k}\binom{n-j}{k}

and substituting
k=n-i
,

(6)

\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}}(\lambda)=\sum_{0\leq i\leq n}\lambda^{n-i}\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)\gamma^{j}\binom{n-j}{i-j},

which implies (
5
).
Thus, the Whitney numbers are polynomials in
\gamma
and, as promised, all coefficients of powers
\gamma^{j}
are positive when
j\leq i
and
\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)\neq 0
, i.e.,
j\leq n-c
. Thus, the degree is
\min(i,n-c)
.
∎

When
i\leq n-c
, the leading coefficient in (
5
) is
\bar{w}_{i}(\Gamma)
, which is the number of no-broken-circuit sets of
i
edges in
\Gamma

[
5
]
.
When
i>n-c
, the leading coefficient is that of
\gamma^{n-c}
, which is
\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma)\binom{c}{n-i}
. The first factor is the number of maximal forests that contain no broken circuit. The second factor is the number of ways to choose
n-i
of the
c
components of the forest. As a combinatorial interpretation of the product this seems arbitrary.
Are there meaningful combinatorial interpretations of the coefficients of powers of
\gamma
?

4.
More expansions

We generalize to group expansions
\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)}
with
X\subseteq V
, i.e., for any set of half edges. (These were studied in
[
7
, Examples 3.7 and 6.7]
.)
We say a vertex set is
stable
if it contains no edges in
\Gamma
. The number of stable sets of order
k
is
\alpha_{k}(\Gamma)
.
The complement of
X\subseteq V
is
X^{c}
. The number of isolated vertices of
\Gamma
that are not in
X
is
\zeta
.

Theorem 2
.

For any set
X\subseteq V(\Gamma)
, the Whitney number
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})
is a polynomial function of
\gamma
of degree
\min(i,n-c)
for each
i=0,1,\ldots,n-\zeta
. Specifically,

(7)

\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})=\sum_{j=0}^{i}\gamma^{j}\sum_{k=0}^{i-j}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-j-k}{n-i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c}:\,\text{stable}\\
|Y|=k\end{subarray}}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)

if
i\leq n-\zeta
and
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})=0
if
i>n-\zeta
. The leading term is
\bar{w}_{i}(\Gamma)\gamma^{i}
if
i\leq n-c
and
\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma)\binom{c-\zeta}{i-(n-c)}\gamma^{n-c}
if
n-c\leq i\leq n-\zeta
.

Proof.

The crucial formula is

\displaystyle\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)}}(\lambda)=\sum_{Y\subseteq X^{c}:\text{ stable}}(-1)^{|Y|}\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}\smallsetminus Y}(\lambda),

from
[
7
, Theorem 6.1]
.
We extract the Whitney numbers via Equation (
6
):

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c}\\
\text{stable}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}\smallsetminus Y}(\lambda)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c}\\
\text{stable}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\sum_{k=0}^{n-|Y|}\bar{w}_{k}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(V)}\smallsetminus Y)\lambda^{n-|Y|-k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c}\\
\text{stable}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\sum_{k=0}^{n-|Y|}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)\binom{n-|Y|-j}{k-j}\gamma^{j}\lambda^{n-|Y|-k}

and now substituting
i=|Y|+k
,

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{n-i}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c}\\
\text{stable}\\
|Y|\leq i\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\sum_{j=0}^{i-|Y|}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)\binom{n-j-|Y|}{n-i}\gamma^{j}.

The chromatic polynomial is therefore

(8)

\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)}}(\lambda)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\lambda^{n-i}\sum_{j=0}^{i}\gamma^{j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c}:\,\text{stable}\\
|Y|\leq i-j\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\binom{n-j-|Y|}{n-i}\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y).

As the coefficient of
\lambda^{n-i}
is
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})
, we obtain (
7
) by introducing
k:=|Y|\leq i-j
.
This quantity is a polynomial in
\gamma
of degree at most
i
. The term of degree
i
arises only from
Y=\varnothing
and its coefficient is
\bar{w}_{i}(\Gamma)
, which is nonzero as long as
i\leq n-c
. It remains to find the degree when
i>n-c
.

Let
Z
be the set of isolated vertices that are not in
X
, so
\zeta:=|Z|
. From the product formula (
2
) along with (
1
) we find that

\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)}}(\lambda)=\lambda^{\zeta}\bar{\chi}_{\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)}\smallsetminus Z}(\lambda)\text{ and }\bar{\chi}_{\Gamma}(\lambda)=\lambda^{\zeta}\bar{\chi}_{\Gamma\smallsetminus Z}(\lambda);

we conclude that
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})=0
if
i>n-\zeta
.
(This corresponds to the fact that
\operatorname{rk}\mathbf{F}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})=n-\zeta
if
\gamma>1
and is at most that if
\gamma=1
; see
[
6
, Section 2]
.)

Thus, now we assume
n-c\leq i\leq n-\zeta
. The highest-degree term in
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})
has degree at most
n-c
because
\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)=0
when
j>n-c
. We examine the coefficient of
\gamma^{n-c}
, i.e.,

(9)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq X^{c},\text{ stable}\\
|Y|\leq i-(n-c)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)\binom{c-|Y|}{n-i}.

Let
c_{Y}
denote the number of components of
\Gamma\smallsetminus Y
. The rank of
\mathbf{F}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)
is
n-|Y|-c_{Y}
, so if
n-|Y|-c_{Y}<n-c
, then
\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)=0
and the term of
Y
in (
9
) drops out. We may therefore restrict
Y
to satisfy
|Y|+c_{Y}\leq c
. Restating this as
c(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)\leq c-|Y|
shows that deleting
Y
destroys at least
|Y|
components, which is possible only if
Y
consists of isolated vertices in
\Gamma
.
As
Y\subseteq X^{c}
, this means
Y\subseteq Z
; indeed,
Y
is any subset of
Z
of the right size. It also means that
\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma\smallsetminus Y)=\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)
since the Whitney numbers do not take account of isolated vertices.

We digress to prove an identity using Vandermonde convolution, assuming
c\geq m\geq 0
:

(10)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}\binom{\zeta}{k}\binom{c-k}{c-m}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}\binom{\zeta}{k}\binom{c-k}{m-k}=(-1)^{m}\sum_{k=0}^{m}\binom{\zeta}{k}\binom{-(c-m+1)}{m-k}

\displaystyle=(-1)^{m}\binom{\zeta-c+m-1}{m-k}=\binom{c-\zeta}{m}.

Formula (
9
) becomes

(11)

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Y\subseteq Z:|Y|\leq i-(n-c)\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|Y|}\binom{c-|Y|}{n-i}

\displaystyle=\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma)\sum_{k=0}^{i-(n-c)}(-1)^{k}\binom{\zeta}{k}\binom{c-k}{n-i}.

\displaystyle=\bar{w}_{n-c}(\Gamma)\binom{c-\zeta}{i-(n-c)}

by (
10
) with
m=i-(n-c)
(so
n-i=c-m
). Note that
c-\zeta\geq i-(n-c)
because we assumed
i\leq n-\zeta
; therefore this coefficient is positive.
Thus,
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})
is a polynomial in
\gamma
of degree exactly
n-c
when
n-c<i\leq n-\zeta
.
∎

5.2.
Special coefficients

The constant term of
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})
, which is the value of the Whitney number when
\gamma=0
, is

\sum_{k=0}^{i}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-k}{i-k}\alpha_{k}(\Gamma\smallsetminus X).

Suppose
\Gamma=K_{n}
,
n>1
. Then
\zeta=0
. There are no stable sets larger than a single vertex. The constant term is

\sum_{k=0}^{1}(-1)^{k}\binom{n-k}{i-k}\alpha_{k}(K_{n}\smallsetminus X)=\binom{n-1}{n-i}|X|-(i-1)\binom{n}{i}.

Note that
\binom{n-1}{-1}=0
and that by setting
\gamma=0
we do not get the chromatic polynomial of a partially filled edgeless graph,
(K_{n}^{c})^{(X)}
.

We state the two highest terms in
\gamma
of
\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}\Gamma^{(X)})
, assuming
i\leq n-c
. The highest power,
\gamma^{i}
, has coefficient
\bar{w}_{j}(\Gamma)
. The next lower coefficient is more interesting: it is

(n+1-i)\bar{w}_{i-1}(\Gamma)-\sum_{y\notin X}\bar{w}_{i-1}(\Gamma\smallsetminus y).

5.4.
Dowling Examples

Example 1

(Dowlings)
.

Equation (
4
) immediately gives the Whitney numbers of the Dowling lattices. The base graph chromatic polynomial is
\chi_{K_{n}}(\lambda)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}s(n,n-j)\lambda^{n-j}
, where
s(n,n-j)
is the Stirling number of the first kind. Its sign is
(-1)^{n-j}
if
j<n
;
s(n,0)
is 0 if
n>0
. Write
\bar{s}(n,n-j)=(-1)^{n-j}s(n,n-j)
for the signless Stirling number. Thus,
\bar{w}_{j}(K_{n})=\bar{s}(n,n-j)
and for the Dowling lattice we have the polynomial formula

\bar{w}_{i}(Q_{n}(\mathfrak{G}))=\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}K_{n}^{(V)})=\sum_{0\leq j\leq i}\bar{s}(n,n-j)\binom{n-j}{i-j}\gamma^{j}.

Example 2

(More or Less Jointless Dowlings)
.

These are the lattices of
\mathbf{F}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}K_{n}^{(X)})
for any vertex set
X
, or in Kung’s terminology any set of joints. Kung introduced the term
joint
for the half edges in a group expansion, when viewed as elements of a standard basis for the matroid
[
2
]
. If all joints are missing, he calls the lattice
jointless
.

For
K_{n}
with
n>0
, we have
c=1
and the only stable sets
Y
are
\varnothing
and
\{y\}
for
y\in X^{c}
.
Equation (
7
) becomes

\displaystyle\bar{w}_{i}(\mathfrak{G}{\cdot}K_{n}^{(X)})=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}\left[\bar{s}(n,n-j)\binom{n-j}{i-j}-|X^{c}|\bar{s}(n-1,n-j-1)\binom{n-j-1}{i-j-1}\right]\gamma^{j}.

In particular, for
\gamma=1
this should give
\bar{s}(n,n-i)=\bar{w}_{i}(K_{n})
. Thus, we have an identity involving Stirling numbers:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{i}\left[\bar{s}(n,n-j)\binom{n-j}{i-j}-n\bar{s}(n-1,n-j-1)\binom{n-j-1}{i-j-1}\right]=\bar{s}(n,n-i).

6.
Biased expansions

An
\gamma
-fold biased expansion of
\Gamma

[
7
, Example 3.8]
, where
\gamma
is a positive integer, is a combinatorial abstraction and generalization of a group expansion. It has the same numerical properties as a group expansion
[
7
, Example 6.8]
but does not require a group (although it is true that if
\Gamma
is 3-connected, every biased expansion is a group expansion
[
8
]
). That is, all the preceding results apply equally to biased expansions.
