Integrable operators, ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} -Problems, KP and NLS hierarchy


Abstract
We develop the theory of integrable operators 𝒦 \mathcal{K} acting on a domain of the complex plane with smooth boundary in analogy with the theory of integrable operators acting on contours of the complex plane. We show how the resolvent operator is obtained from the solution of a ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} -problem in the complex plane. When such a ∂ ¯ \overline{\partial} -problem depends on auxiliary parameters we define its Malgrange one form in analogy with the theory of isomonodromic problems. We show that the Malgrange one form is closed and coincides with the exterior logarithmic differential of the Hilbert-Carleman determinant of the operator 𝒦 \mathcal{K} . With suitable choices of the setup we show that the Hilbert-Carleman determinant is a τ \tau -function of the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili (KP) or nonlinear Schrödinger hierarchies.

Introduction

A key notion in the theory of solvable integrable systems is that of
\tau
–function, (see
[
18
]
for a comprehensive historical perspective) and in many instances such
\tau
–functions coincide with the Fredholm determinant of an appropriate
integral operator.

A distinguished class of integral operators are the so called
integrable operators
:
the theory of such operators has its roots in the work of Jimbo et al.
[
26
]
that ultimately led to the construction by Its, Izergin, Korepin and Slavnov
[
22
]
of a Riemann-Hilbert problem to express the kernel of their resolvent operators.
Their original motivation for studying these operators comes from the theory of
quantum integrable models. The theory of integrable operators later found applications in many fields of mathematics such as random matrices and integrable partial differential equations: for example the
gap probabilities
in determinantal random point processes (and more generally the generating function of occupation numbers) are expressible as a Fredholm determinant
[
35
]
,
[
11
]
and this is at the core of the celebrated Tracy-Widom distribution for fluctuations of the largest eigenvalue of a random matrix in a Gaussian Unitary Ensemble
[
33
]
.
An integrable operator is an integral operator acting on
L^{2}(\Sigma,|\mathrm{d}w|)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
of the form

\mathcal{K}[v](z)=\int_{\Sigma}K(z,w)v(w)dw,\quad z\in\Sigma,

where
\Sigma
is some oriented contour in the complex plane and the kernel
K(z,w)\in Mat(n\times n,\mathbb{C})
has a special form

\displaystyle K(z,w):

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{T}(z)\,g(w)}{z-w},\quad f(z),g(z)\in Mat(r\times n,\mathbb{C}),

(1.1)

where
f
and
g
are rectangular
r\times n
matrices and for the time being we only assume that
f
and
g
are smooth
along the connected components of
\Sigma
.
The condition for
K
to be nonsingular requires

f^{T}(z)\,g(z)\equiv 0.

In the most relevant applications, the operators of the form (
1.1
) are trace class operators.
An important observation in
[
22
]
is that the resolvent operator

\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{K}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}=(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}-\mathrm{Id}\,,

(1.2)

where
\mathrm{Id}\,
is the identity operator, is in the same class, namely

\mathcal{R}[v](z)=\int_{\Sigma}R(z,w)v(w)dw,\quad z\in\Sigma,

where the resolvent kernel has also the form of an integrable operator:

\displaystyle R(z,w):

\displaystyle=\frac{F^{T}(z)\,G(w)}{z-w},\quad F(z),G(z)\in Mat(r\times n,\mathbb{C}).

(1.3)

Here
F^{T}(z)=(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}f^{T}
and
G=g(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}
,
where
(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}
in the first relation is acting to the right while in the second relation its action is to the left.
Another crucial observation of
[
22
]
(see also the introduction of
[
20
]
) is that the determination of
\mathcal{R}
is equivalent to the solution of an associated Riemann-Hilbert (RH) problem for a
r\times r
matrix
\Gamma(z)

analytic in
{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\Sigma
that satisfies the boundary value relation (sometimes referred to as “jump relation”)

\begin{split}&\Gamma_{+}(z)=\Gamma_{-}(z)M(z),\;\;z\in\Sigma,\quad M(z)={\bf 1}+2\pi if(z)g^{T}(z)\\
&\Gamma(z)\to{\bf 1},\quad\mbox{as $|z|\to\infty$}.\end{split}

(1.4)

Here
\Gamma_{\pm}(z)
denote the boundary values of the matrix
\Gamma(z)
as
z
approaches from the left and right of the oriented contour
\Sigma
and
{\bf 1}
is the identity matrix in
\mbox{Mat}(r\times r,{\mathbb{C}})
. The matrices
F
and
G
that define the resolvent kernel (
1.3
) are related to the solution
\Gamma
of the RH problem (
1.4
) by the relation

F(z)=\Gamma(z)f(z),\quad G(z)=(\Gamma(z)^{T})^{-1}g(z).

(1.5)

This connection between the Fredholm determinant and the RH problem has been exploited in several contexts where the kernel depends on large parameters and the study of the asymptotic behaviour of the Fredholm determinant
is obtained via the Deift–Zhou nonlinear steepest descent method of the corresponding RH problem
[
13
]
.
This analysis has been successfully implemented for
n=1
and
r=2
in a large class of kernels originating in random matrices, orthogonal polynomials, probability and partial differential equations see for example (see e.g.
[
13
,
18
,
5
,
12
,
21
]
).

The knowledge of the resolvent operator allows to write variational formulæ for the
Fredholm determinant of the operator

\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
as

\displaystyle\delta\log\det(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})=-\mbox{Tr}\bigl((\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}\circ\delta\mathcal{K}\bigr)=-\mbox{Tr}\bigl((\mathrm{Id}\,+\mathcal{R})\circ\delta\mathcal{K}\bigr),

(1.6)

where here and below
\delta
stands for exterior total differentiation in the space of parameters.

There exist Riemann Hilbert problems more general than (
1.4
) that describe the inverse monodromy problem of a linear system of first order ODEs in the complex plane: in those cases the deformation parameters can be introduced in such a way that the monodromy data do not depend on them.
In this context,
the Kyoto school headed by Jimbo, Miwa and Ueno
[
27
]
introduced the concept of isomonodromic
\tau
-function starting from a differential one form
\omega
(the Malgrange one form) defined on the space of isomonodromic deformation parameters.
In several situations the isomonodromic
\tau
-function can be identified with the Fredholm determinant (possibly up to multiplication by explicit factors) of an operator of integrable form
[
2
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
15
]
.

An enlargement of the class of integrable operators (
1.1
) was studied by Bertola and Cafasso
[
3
]
who
considered Hankel composition operators that have been reduced to integrable operators in Fourier space.

Recently Bothner
[
6
]
and A. Krajenbrink
[
28
]
enlarged the class of Hankel composition operators that can be studied via Riemann-Hilbert problems. Applications are obtained in
[
8
]
,
[
9
]
.

The common feature of all these works is the appearance, in one way or another, of a Riemann–Hilbert problem, namely, a boundary value problem of a matrix with discontinuities across a contour (or union thereof) with boundary values related multiplicatively by a group-like element
M
(the “jump matrix”) as in (
1.4
).

The goal of the present manuscript is to enlarge the class of integrable operators by considering operators
\mathcal{K}
acting on
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}w)\otimes\mathbb{C}^{n}
where
\mathscr{D}
is a bounded domain of the complex plane with a matrix kernel
K(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})\in Mat(n\times n,\mathbb{C})
, namely

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[v](z,\overline{z})=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}K(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})v(w)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{w}\wedge\mathrm{d}{w}}{2i},\quad z,\overline{z}\in\mathscr{D},

(1.7)

\displaystyle K(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w}):=\frac{f^{T}(z,\overline{z})\,g(w,\overline{w})}{z-w},

\displaystyle f^{T}(z,\overline{z})\,g(z,\overline{z})\equiv 0\equiv(\partial_{\bar{z}}f(z,\overline{z}))^{T}\,g(z,\overline{z}),\;\;\;\;f,g\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}(\mathscr{D},Mat(r\times n,\mathbb{C})).

(1.8)

Here and below, instead of
\overline{\partial}
, we use the symbol
\partial_{\bar{z}}
to specify the derivative with respect to
\overline{z}
. The dependence of
f
and
g
on
z
and
\overline{z}
is to remind the reader that
f
and
g
are in general smooth matrix functions on the complex plane.
The kernel
K(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})
and the corresponding integral operator
\mathcal{K}
is a Hilbert–Schmidt operator with a well–defined
and continuous value on the diagonal in
\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}
and therefore its trace and Fredholm determinant are well defined (Proposition 3.11.2 and Theorem 3.11.5
[
34
]
) as limits of finite rank operators, namely

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\rm Tr}(P_{n}\mathcal{K}P_{n})=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}K(z,\overline{z},z,\overline{z},)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2i},

(1.9)

where
P_{n}
is a finite rank projection such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}P_{n}=\mathrm{Id}\,
.
Furthermore the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}\det(\mathrm{Id}\,-P_{n}\mathcal{K}P_{n})

(1.10)

exists. If
\mathcal{K}
is trace class, then
{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{K})
and
\det(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})
coincide with the limits (
1.9
) and (
1.10
) respectively.
Our results are the following.

•

In Section
2
we show that the resolvent of the integral operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
is obtained through the solution of a

\overline{\partial}
-Problem (instead of a Riemann-Hilbert problem)
for a matrix–valued function
\Gamma
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z,\overline{z})=\Gamma(z,\overline{z})M(z,\overline{z});\qquad\Gamma(z,\overline{z})\underset{z\to\infty}{\to}{\bf 1},\\
&M(z,\overline{z})=\pi f(z,\overline{z})g^{T}(z,\overline{z})\chi_{\mathscr{D}}(z),\end{split}

(1.11)

where
\chi_{\mathscr{D}}(z)
is the characteristic function of the domain
\mathscr{D}
. Note that the matrix
M(z,\overline{z})
is nilpotent because of (
1.8
).
We show that the
\overline{\partial}
-Problem is solvable if and only if the operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}

is invertible. Furthermore we show, in analogy with integrable operators defined on contours,
that the kernel of the resolvent is

\displaystyle R(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})=\frac{F(z,\overline{z})^{T}\,G(w,\overline{w})}{z-w},\quad F(z,\overline{z})=\Gamma(z,\overline{z})f(z,\overline{z}),\ \ G(z,\overline{z})=\Gamma^{-1}(z,\overline{z})g(z,\overline{z})

where
\Gamma
solves the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
1.11
).

•

In Section
3
we consider the regularized determinant (Hilbert-Carleman determinant, equation 7.8
[
16
]
)
of the operator
\mathcal{K}
. This is defined
as the Fredholm determinant of the trace class operator
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}}:=\mathrm{Id}\,-(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})e^{\mathcal{K}}
,
namely

\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}):=\det(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{K}})=\det((\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})e^{\mathcal{K}}).

(1.12)

Using the Jacobi variational formula (
1.6
) we show that

\displaystyle\delta\log

\displaystyle\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})=\omega,

(1.13)

\displaystyle\omega:=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\left(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)\delta M(z)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i},

(1.14)

The one form
\omega
is shown to be closed. In analogy with the literature on Riemann–Hilbert problems on contours
[
1
,
2
]
we call
\omega
the Malgrange one form of the
\overline{\partial}
-Problem. The corresponding
\tau
function of the
\overline{\partial}
-Problem is henceforth defined by

\delta\log\tau=\omega\,.

Therefore we have that

\tau=\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}).

(1.15)

If the operator
\mathcal{K}
is of trace class, then the
\tau
-function can be expressed as a Fredholm determinant by the relation

\tau=\det(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}){\rm e}^{{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{K})}.

We also show (Subsection
3.1
) that the formula (
1.14
) defines a closed one–form under the less restrictive assumption that
M(z,\overline{z})
is traceless but not nilpotent, and this allows us to define a
\tau
–function (up to multiplicative constants) of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem

\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z,\overline{z})=\Gamma(z,\overline{z})M(z,\overline{z});\qquad\Gamma(z,\overline{z})\underset{z\to\infty}{\to}{\bf 1},

(1.16)

by the relation

\displaystyle\delta\log\tau=\omega.

(1.17)

Note that in this more general case the
\tau
-function of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem is well defined but it is not in general related to a
Hilbert-Carleman determinant of some integral operator.

2
Integrable operators and
\overline{\partial}
-problems

Let
\mathscr{D}\subset\mathbb{C}
be a compact union of domains with smooth boundary and denote by
\mathcal{K}
the integral operator acting on the space
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
with a kernel
K(z,w)
of the form

\displaystyle K(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w}):=\frac{f^{T}(z,\overline{z})g(w,\overline{w})}{z-w},\quad f(z,\overline{z}),g(z,\overline{z})\in{\rm Mat}(r\times n,\mathbb{C}),

(2.1)

\displaystyle f^{T}(z,\overline{z})\,g(z,\overline{z})\equiv 0\quad\mbox{and }\quad(\partial_{\bar{z}}f(z,\overline{z}))^{T}\,g(z,\overline{z})\equiv 0,\quad z,\overline{z}\in\mathscr{D}.

(2.2)

Here the matrix-valued functions
f,g
are assumed to be sufficiently smooth on
\mathscr{D}
but no analyticity is required and for this reason we indicate the dependence on both variables
z
and
\overline{z}
.
The vanishing requirements along the locus
z=w
are sufficient to guarantee that the kernel
K
admits a well-defined value on the diagonal and it is continuous on
\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}

\displaystyle\lim_{w\to z}K(z,\overline{z},w,\overline{w})=K(z,\overline{z},z,\overline{z})=\partial_{z}f^{T}(z,\overline{z})\,g(z,\overline{z}).

(2.3)

We have emphasized that the kernel and the functions are not holomorphically dependent on the variables; that said, from now on we omit the explicit dependence on
\overline{z}
, trusting that the class of functions we are dealing with will be clear by the context each time.
The operator
\mathcal{K}
acts as follows on functions

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}[\varphi](z):=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}K(z,w)\varphi(w)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{w}\wedge\mathrm{d}w}{2i},\ \ \varphi\in L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}.

(2.4)

We introduce the following
\overline{\partial}
-problem for an
r\times r
matrix-valued function
\Gamma(z,\overline{z})
.

Problem 2.1
.

Find a matrix-valued function
\Gamma(z,\overline{z})\in GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
such that

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z)=\Gamma(z)M(z);\qquad\Gamma(z)\underset{z\to\infty}{\to}{\bf 1}

(2.5)

where
{\bf 1}
is the identity in
GL_{r}(\mathbb{C})
and

M(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}{\pi}f(z)g^{T}(z),&\mbox{for $z\in\mathscr{D}$},\\
&\\
0&\mbox{for $z\in{\mathbb{C}}\backslash\mathscr{D}$}.\end{array}\right.

(2.6)

We first show that

Lemma 2.2
.

If a solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
exists, it is unique. Furthermore
\det\Gamma(z)\equiv 1
.

Proof.

If
\Gamma
is a solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
then

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}\det\Gamma={\rm Tr}\Big({\rm adj}(\Gamma)\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma\Big)={\rm Tr}\Big({\rm adj}(\Gamma)\Gamma M\Big)

(2.7)

where
{\rm adj}(\Gamma)
denotes the adjugate matrix (the transposed of the co-factor matrix).
Here
{\rm Tr}
denotes the matrix trace. Now the product in the last formula yields
{\rm adj}(\Gamma)\Gamma=(\det\Gamma){\bf 1}
,
so that

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}\det\Gamma=\det(\Gamma){\rm Tr}(M)=0

(2.8)

where the last identity follows from the fact that
M
is traceless because
{\rm Tr}(M)={\rm Tr}(M^{T})=0
. Thus
\det\Gamma
is an entire function which tends to
{\bf 1}
at infinity, and hence it is identically equal to
1
by Liouville’s theorem.

Now, if
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
are two solutions, it follows easily that
R(z):=\Gamma_{1}\Gamma_{2}^{-1}
is an entire matrix-valued function which tends to the identity matrix
{\bf 1}
at infinity and hence, by Liouville’s theorem
R(z)\equiv{\bf 1}
, thus proving the uniqueness.

\blacksquare

Theorem 2.3
.

The operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
with
\mathcal{K}
as in (
2.4
) is invertible in
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
if and only if the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
admits a solution. The resolvent
\mathcal{R}
of
\mathcal{K}
has kernel given by:

R(z,w):=\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)\left(\Gamma^{T}(w)\right)^{-1}\!\!\!g(w)}{z-w},\quad(z,w)\in\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}

(2.9)

where
\Gamma(z)
is a
r\times r
matrix that solves the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
.

Proof.

Suppose that the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
is solved by
\Gamma(z)
; we now show that the operator
(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})
is invertible.
Let us define the operator

\mathcal{R}:L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\to L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}

with kernel
R(z,w)
given by (
2.9
).
To verify that
\mathcal{R}
is the resolvent of the operator
\mathcal{K}
we need to check the following condition

\begin{split}(\mathrm{Id}\,+\mathcal{R})\circ&(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})=\mathrm{Id}\,\\
&\Downarrow\\
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{K}&=\mathcal{R}-\mathcal{K}.\end{split}

(2.10)

To this end we compute the kernel of
\mathcal{R}\circ\mathcal{K}
namely

\displaystyle(R\circ K)(z,w)

\displaystyle:=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}R(z,\zeta)K(\zeta,w)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)\overbrace{(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)}^{=-\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}}g(w)}{(z-\zeta)(\zeta-w)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{z-w}\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}\left(\frac{1}{z-\zeta}+\frac{1}{\zeta-w}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i\pi}g(w).

(2.11)

If we consider the generalized Cauchy-Pompeiu formula for the matrix
(\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}
we can express it in integral form as

(\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}={\bf 1}-\iint\limits_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}}{\zeta-z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge d\zeta}{2\pi i},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(2.12)

We substitute (
2.12
) into (
2.11
):

\displaystyle(R\circ K)(z,w)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{z-w}\Big(\left((\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}-{\bf 1}\right)-\left((\Gamma^{T}(w))^{-1}-{\bf 1}\right)\Big)g(w)

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{z-w}\Big((\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}-(\Gamma^{T}(w))^{-1}\Big)g(w)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(w))^{-1}g(w)}{z-w}-\frac{f^{T}(z)g(w)}{z-w}=R(z,w)-K(z,w).

(2.13)

This shows that indeed
\mathcal{R}
satisfies the resolvent equation (
2.10
) and hence the operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
is invertible.

Viceversa, let us now suppose that the operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
is invertible and denote

\mathcal{R}=\Big(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}\Big)^{-1}-\mathrm{Id}\,.

We now verify that
\mathcal{R}
has kernel

R(z,w)=\frac{F(z)^{T}\,G(w)}{z-w}

(2.14)

where the matrices
F(z)
and
G(z)
are defined as

\begin{split}F(z)&:=(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}^{T})^{-1}[f](z)\\
G(z)&:=(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})^{-1}[g](z),\\
\end{split}

(2.15)

with the inverse applied to each entry (and the transposition
T
acts on the matrix indices).
Indeed we verify the condition (
2.10
) with
R
given by (
2.14
):

\begin{split}(R\circ K)(z,w)=&\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{F^{T}(z)G(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)g(w)}{(z-\zeta)(\zeta-w)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}{\zeta}}{2i}=\frac{1}{z-w}\left(\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{F^{T}(z)G(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)g(w)}{(z-\zeta)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}{\zeta}}{2i}+\right.\\
&\left.+\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{F^{T}(z)G(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)g(w)}{(\zeta-w)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}{\zeta}}{2i}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{z-w}\Big(\mathcal{R}[f^{T}](z)g(w)+F^{T}(z)\mathcal{K}[G](w)\Big).\\
\end{split}

Adding and subtracting the kernels
K(z,w)
and
R(z,w)
, we obtain

\displaystyle(R\circ K)(z,w)=\frac{1}{z-w}\Big((\mathrm{Id}\,+

\displaystyle\mathcal{R})[f^{T}](z)g(w)-F^{T}(z)(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})[G](w)\Big)+

\displaystyle+R(z,w)-K(z,w).

(2.16)

With the definitions (
2.15
) the contributions in the first line of (
2.16
) cancel out and the condition (
2.10
) is satisfied.
To conclude the proof we need to verify that

\displaystyle F(z)=\Gamma(z)f(z),\ \ G(z)=\Gamma^{-1}(z)g(z)

(2.17)

where the matrix
\Gamma
solves the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
.
To this end, let us define the matrix
\widetilde{\Gamma}(z)

\widetilde{\Gamma}(z):={\bf 1}-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{F(\zeta)\,g^{T}(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i},\quad z\in{\mathbb{C}}.

(2.18)

From this definition it follows that

\begin{split}f^{T}(z)\widetilde{\Gamma}^{T}(z)&=f^{T}(z)-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{f^{T}(z)g(\zeta)F^{T}(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\\
&=f^{T}(z)+\mathcal{K}[F^{T}](z)\\
&=f^{T}(z)+F^{T}(z)-(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})[F^{T}](z)\\
&=F^{T}(z)\\
\end{split}

(2.19)

which implies

F(z)=\widetilde{\Gamma}(z)f(z).

(2.20)

We now substitute (
2.20
) in the definition (
2.18
):

\widetilde{\Gamma}(z)={\bf 1}-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\widetilde{\Gamma}(\zeta)f(\zeta)g^{T}(\zeta)}{\zeta-z}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}.

(2.21)

Then, following the general Cauchy formula (
2.12
), we find that the matrix
\tilde{\Gamma}(z)
satisfies

\partial_{\bar{z}}\tilde{\Gamma}(z)=\pi\widetilde{\Gamma}(z)f(z)g^{T}(z).

(2.22)

Finally, since the support
\mathscr{D}
of
M
is compact, the equation (
2.18
) implies that
\widetilde{\Gamma}
is analytic outside of
\mathscr{D}
and tends to
{\bf 1}
as
|z|\to\infty
. Thus
\widetilde{\Gamma}
solves the same
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
and since the solution is unique, it must coincide with
\Gamma
.

\blacksquare

3
The Fredholm determinant

In Section
2
we have linked the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
to the existence of the inverse of
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
.
From the conditions (
2.1
) we conclude that
\mathcal{K}
is a Hilbert-Schmidt operator with a well-defined and continuous diagonal in
\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}
: according to
[
34
]
this is sufficient to define the Fredholm determinant for the operator
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
, as explained in the following remark.

Remark 3.1
.

In general, for a Hilbert-Schmidt operator
\mathcal{A}
, the Fredholm determinant is not defined but we can still define a regularization of it, called the Hilbert-Carleman determinant

\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A}):=\text{{\rm det}}\left((\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A})\rm e^{\mathcal{A}}\right).

(3.1)

We observe that
\text{{\rm det}}\left((\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A})\rm e^{\mathcal{A}}\right)=\text{{\rm det}}\left(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}\right)
with
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}:=\mathrm{Id}\,-(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A})\rm e^{\mathcal{A}}
and the operator
\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}
is trace class because it has the representation

\mathcal{T}_{\mathcal{A}}=-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{n-1}{n!}\mathcal{A}^{n}.

If
\mathcal{A}
is of trace class we can rewrite the Hilbert-Carleman determinant as

\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A})=\det(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A}){\rm e}^{{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A})}.

(3.2)

Moreover, as for the Fredholm determinant, the Hilbert-Carleman determinant can be represented by a series

\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{A})=1+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(-1)^{n}}{n!}\Psi_{n}(\mathcal{A})

(3.3)

where
\Psi_{n}(\mathcal{A})
is given by the Plemelj-Smithies formula

\Psi_{n}(\mathcal{A})=\det\begin{bmatrix}0&n-1&0&\dots&0&0\\
{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{2})&0&n-2&\dots&0&0\\
{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{3})&{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{2})&0&\dots&0&0\\
.&.&.&.&.&.\\
{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{n})&{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{n-1})&{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{n-2})&\dots&{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{A}^{2})&0\\
\end{bmatrix}.

It is shown that if
\mathcal{A}
is Hilbert-Schmidt then (
3.3
) converges (
[
16
]
, Chapter 10, Theorem 3.1).

Let us now assume that
\mathcal{K}
depends smoothly on parameters
\boldsymbol{t}=(t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{j},\dots)\text{ with }t_{j}\in{\mathbb{C}}\text{ , }\forall\,j\geq 1
: we want to relate solutions of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
with the variational equations for the determinant.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let us suppose that the matrix
M(z,\overline{z})
in the
\overline{\partial}
-Problem
2.1
, depends smoothly on some parameters
\boldsymbol{t}
, while remaining identically nilpotent. Then the solution
\Gamma(z)
of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
is related to the logarithmic derivative of the Hilbert-Carleman determinant of
\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}
as follows:

\delta\log\Big[\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})\Big]=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\left(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)\delta M(z)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i},

(3.4)

where
\delta
stands for the total differential in the space of parameters
\boldsymbol{t}
.

Proof.

Using the Jacobi variational formula (
1.6
), we can rewrite the LHS of (
3.4
) as

\delta\log\Big[\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})\Big]=\delta\log\Big[\text{{\rm det}}\left((\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K})\rm e^{\mathcal{K}}\right)\Big]=-{\rm Tr}\left(\mathcal{R}\circ\delta\mathcal{K}\right),

(3.5)

where
\mathcal{R}\circ\delta\mathcal{K}
is a trace class operator, since it is the composition of two Hilbert-Schmidt operators.
Here
{\rm Tr}
denotes the trace on the Hilbert space
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
.
The composition of the two operators
\mathcal{R}\circ\delta\mathcal{K}
produces the kernel

\displaystyle(R\circ\delta K)(z,w)

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta(f^{T}(x)g(w))}{(z-\zeta)(\zeta-w)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)\delta g(w)}{(z-\zeta)(\zeta-w)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}+

(3.6)

\displaystyle+\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta f^{T}(\zeta)g(w)}{(z-\zeta)(\zeta-w)}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}

(3.7)

where we have omitted explicit notation of the dependence on
\boldsymbol{t}
of the functions
f,g,F,G,\Gamma
.

We focus on the term in (
3.6
).
Using the identity
\frac{1}{(z-\zeta)(\zeta-w)}=\frac{1}{z-w}\left(\frac{1}{z-\zeta}+\frac{1}{\zeta-w}\right)
, we obtain

\displaystyle(\ref{eq:I})=

\displaystyle\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{z-w}\left(\iint_{\mathscr{D}}(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)\left(\frac{1}{z-\zeta}+\frac{1}{\zeta-w}\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\right)\delta g(w)

(3.8)

In order to compute the trace we need to compute the kernel (
3.8
) along the diagonal
z=w
and hence we consider
\lim_{w\to z}(\ref{32})
.
Observe that
(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)f^{T}(\zeta)=-\frac{1}{\pi}\partial_{\bar{\zeta}}(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}
, and hence we can apply the formula (
2.12
) to eliminate the integral and rewrite (
3.8
) as follows

\displaystyle(\ref{32})

\displaystyle=-\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)\Big((\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}-{\bf 1}\Big)\delta g(w)}{z-w}+\frac{f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)\Big((\Gamma^{T}(w))^{-1}-{\bf 1}\Big)\delta g(w)}{z-w}

(3.9)

\displaystyle=\frac{f^{T}(z)\Big(\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(w))^{-1}-{\bf 1}\Big)\delta g(w)}{z-w}.

(3.10)

We can now easily compute the expansion of (
3.10
) along the diagonal
w\to z
by Taylor’s formula, keeping in mind that
\Gamma
is not a holomorphic function inside
\mathscr{D}
:

\displaystyle(\ref{39})

\displaystyle=f^{T}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}\delta g(z)+\frac{\bar{z}-\bar{w}}{z-w}\overbrace{f^{T}(z)g(z)}^{\equiv\,0}f^{T}(z)\delta g(w)+\mathcal{O}(|z-w|)

(3.11)

\displaystyle=f^{T}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}\delta g(z)+\mathcal{O}(|z-w|).

(3.12)

Using the above expression we conclude that the trace in
L^{2}(\mathscr{D},\mathrm{d}^{2}z)\otimes{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
of (
3.6
) is

{\rm Tr}((\ref{eq:I}))=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\big(f^{T}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)^{T}(\Gamma^{T}(z))^{-1}\delta g(z)\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z}{2i}.

(3.13)

Using the cyclicity of the trace and its invariance under transposition of the arguments, we reorder the terms (
3.13
) to the form

{\rm Tr}((\ref{eq:I}))=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\big(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)f(z)\delta g^{T}(z)\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z}{2i}.

(3.14)

We now consider the term (
3.7
). Taking its trace yields:

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}((\ref{eq:II}))=

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{{\rm Tr}\big(f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta f^{T}(\zeta)g(z)\big)}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z}{2i}

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{{\rm Tr}\big(g(z)f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta f^{T}(\zeta)\big)}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z}{2i}

(3.15)

We observe that the integrand is in
L^{2}_{loc}
because the numerator vanishes to order
\mathcal{O}(|z-\zeta|)
along the diagonal

\displaystyle{\rm Tr}\Big(g(z)f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta f^{T}(\zeta)\Big)={\rm Tr}\Big(g(\zeta)\overbrace{f^{T}(\zeta)g(\zeta)}^{=0}\delta f^{T}(\zeta)\Big)+\mathcal{O}(|z-\zeta|),

(3.16)

and hence the integrand is
\mathcal{O}(|z-\zeta|^{-1})
which is locally integrable with respect to the area measure.
We can now relate this integral to
\partial_{z}\Gamma
as follows.
Using the formula (
2.12
) and the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
we can rewrite
\Gamma^{T}(\zeta)
as

\Gamma^{T}(\zeta)={\bf 1}-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\partial_{\bar{z}}(\Gamma^{T}(z))}{z-\zeta}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i}={\bf 1}-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{M^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{z-\zeta}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i}

(3.17)

Taking the holomorphic derivative with respect to
\zeta
we get

\partial_{\zeta}\Gamma^{T}(\zeta)=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{g(z)f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2i}

Plugging the result into (
3
) we obtain

\begin{split}(\ref{eq:Tr_II})=&-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\left((\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta f(\zeta)\left(\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{g(z)f^{T}(z)\Gamma^{T}(z)}{(z-\zeta)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z}{2i}\right)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\\
=&\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\big((\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))^{-1}g(\zeta)\delta f^{T}(\zeta)\partial_{\zeta}(\Gamma^{T}(\zeta))\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\\
=&\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\big(g(\zeta)\delta f^{T}(\zeta)\partial_{\zeta}\Gamma^{T}(\zeta)(\Gamma^{-1}(\zeta))^{T}\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i}\\
=&\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\big(\Gamma^{-1}(\zeta)\partial_{\zeta}\Gamma(\zeta)\delta f(\zeta)g^{T}(\zeta)\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i},\end{split}

(3.18)

so that

{\rm Tr}((\ref{eq:II}))=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\big(\Gamma^{-1}(\zeta)\partial_{\zeta}\Gamma(\zeta)\delta f(\zeta)g^{T}(\zeta)\big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{\zeta}\wedge\mathrm{d}\zeta}{2i},

(3.19)

Combining (
3.14
) and (
3.19
) we have obtained that

\displaystyle-{\rm Tr}(\mathcal{R}\circ\delta\mathcal{K})=-{\rm Tr}((\ref{eq:I})+(\ref{eq:II}))=

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)\delta(f(z)g^{T}(z)))\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2i}=

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)\delta M(z))\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i}.

(3.20)

This concludes the proof of Proposition
3.2
.
\blacksquare

3.1
Malgrange one form and
\tau
-function

From Proposition
3.2
we define the following one form on the space of deformations, which we call
Malgrange one form
following the terminology in
[
1
]
:

\omega:=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\Big(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)\delta M(z)\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i},

(3.21)

where
\Gamma(z)
is the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
and
M(z)
is defined in (
2.6
).
For the operator
\mathcal{K}
defined in (
2.4
), the Proposition
3.2
implies that

\displaystyle\omega=\delta\log\text{{\rm det}}_{2}(\mathrm{Id}\,-\mathcal{K}),

(3.22)

and hence
\omega
is an exact (and hence closed) one form in the space of deformation parameters the operator
\mathcal{K}
may depend upon. The form
\omega
can be shown to be closed under weaker assumptions on the matrix
M
than the ones that appears in the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
as the following theorem shows.

Theorem 3.3
.

Suppose that the
r\times r
matrix
M=M(z,\overline{z};\boldsymbol{t})
is smooth and compactly supported in
\mathscr{D}
(uniformly with respect to the parameters
\boldsymbol{t}
), depends smoothly on
\boldsymbol{t}
and the matrix trace
{\rm Tr}(M)\equiv 0
.
Let
\Gamma(z,\overline{z};\boldsymbol{t})
be the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
1.16
. Then the exterior differential of the one-form
\omega
defined in (
3.21
) vanishes:

\displaystyle\delta\omega=0.

(3.23)

Proof.

From the
\overline{\partial}
-problem we obtain

\displaystyle\delta(\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma)=\Gamma\delta M+\delta\Gamma M\ \ \ \Rightarrow\ \ \ \delta\Gamma(z)=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\Gamma(w)\delta M(w)\Gamma^{-1}(w)}{(w-z)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{w}\wedge\mathrm{d}w}{2\pi i}\Gamma(z).

(3.24)

Using (
3.24
) we can compute

\displaystyle\delta\omega

\displaystyle=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\Big(\delta(\Gamma^{-1}\partial_{z}\Gamma\wedge\delta M)\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i}=

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\Big(\Gamma^{-1}\delta\Gamma\Gamma^{-1}\partial_{z}\Gamma\wedge\delta M\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i}-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\Big(\Gamma^{-1}\delta\partial_{z}\Gamma\wedge\delta M\Big)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}{z}}{2\pi i}

(3.25)

From (
3.24
) we deduce

\delta\partial_{z}\Gamma(z)=-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\Gamma(w)\delta M(w)\Gamma^{-1}(w)}{(w-z)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{w}\wedge\mathrm{d}w}{2\pi i}\Gamma(z)+\delta\Gamma(z)\Gamma(z)^{-1}\partial_{z}\Gamma(z).

(3.26)

Substituting (
3.26
) in the equation (
3.1
) we obtain:

\delta\omega=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}{\rm Tr}\left(\Gamma^{-1}(z)\left(\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\Gamma(w)\delta M(w)\Gamma^{-1}(w)}{(w-z)^{2}}\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{w}\wedge d{w}}{2\pi i}\right)\Gamma(z)\wedge\delta M(z)\right)\frac{\mathrm{d}\overline{z}\wedge\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi i}.

(3.27)

The crux of the proof is now the correct evaluation of the iterated integral:

\displaystyle\delta\omega

\displaystyle=\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}z}{\pi}\iint_{\mathcal{\mathscr{D}}}\frac{\mathrm{d}^{2}w}{\pi}\frac{F(z,w)}{(z-w)^{2}},

\displaystyle F(z,w):={\rm Tr}\Big(\Gamma(w)\delta M(w)\Gamma^{-1}(w)\wedge\Gamma(z)\delta M(z)\Gamma^{-1}(z)\Big)

(3.28)

By applying Fubini’s theorem, since the integrand is antisymmetric in the exchange of the variables
z\leftrightarrow w
, we quickly conclude that the integral is zero. However the integrand is singular along the diagonal
\Delta:=\{z=w\}\subset\mathscr{D}\times\mathscr{D}
and we need to make sure that the integrand is absolutely summable.

Recalling that
F(z,w)=-F(w,z)
, so that
F(z,z)\equiv 0
, we now compute the Taylor expansion of
F(z,w)
with respect to
w
near
z
;

\displaystyle F(z,w)=0+\partial_{w}F(z,z)(w-z)+\partial_{\bar{w}}F(z,z)(\overline{w}-\overline{z})+\mathcal{O}(|z-w|^{2}).

(3.29)

Thus
\frac{|F(z,w)|}{|z-w|^{2}}=\mathcal{O}(|z-w|^{-1})
which is integrable with respect to the area measure. Hence application of Fubini’s theorem is justified.

\blacksquare

4
\tau(\boldsymbol{t})
as a KP
\tau
-function

In this section we consider a specific type of dependence of
M
on the “times”: let
M(z,\boldsymbol{t})
be a
2\times 2
matrix that depends on
\boldsymbol{t}
in the following form

M(z,\boldsymbol{t})={\rm e}^{\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}{2}\sigma_{3}}M_{0}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}{2}\sigma_{3}}\;,

(4.1)

with

\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}z^{j}t_{j}

(4.2)

and
M_{0}(z,\bar{z})
a traceless matrix compactly supported on
\mathscr{\mathscr{D}}
.
A
\tau
-function of the Kadomtsev-Petviashvili hierarchy,
\tau(\boldsymbol{t})
, can be characterized as a function of (formally) an infinite number of variable which satisfies the Hirota Bilinear relation

\text{Res}_{z=\infty}(\tau(\boldsymbol{t}-[z^{-1}])\tau(\boldsymbol{s}+[z^{-1}]){\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})}=0

(4.3)

where
\boldsymbol{t}\pm[z^{-1}]
is the
Miwa Shift
, defined as:

\boldsymbol{t}\pm[z^{-1}]:=\left(t_{1}\pm\frac{1}{z},t_{2}\pm\frac{1}{2z^{2}},\dots,t_{j}\pm\frac{1}{jz^{j}},\dots\right).

(4.4)

The residue in (
4.3
) is meant in the formal sense, namely by considering the coefficient of
z^{-1}
in the expansion at infinity and can be thought of as
the limit of
\oint_{|z|=R}
as
R\to+\infty
. If the functions of
z
intervening in (
4.3
) can be written as analytic functions in a deleted neighbourhood of
\infty
, then the residue is a genuine integral; this is the case of interest below.

As described in
[
25
]
, the equation (
4.3
) implies that the tau function satisfy an equation of the Hirota type

\displaystyle P(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\dots)\,\tau^{2}=0

(4.5)

where
\mathcal{D}_{j}
is the Hirota derivative respect to
t_{j}
, defined as

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{j}\,p(\boldsymbol{t})q(\boldsymbol{t}):=(\partial_{t_{j}}-\partial_{t^{\prime}_{j}})(p(\boldsymbol{t})q(\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}))|_{\boldsymbol{t}=\boldsymbol{t}^{\prime}},

(4.6)

and
P(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\dots)
is a polynomial in
(\mathcal{D}_{1},\mathcal{D}_{2},\dots)
. In particular, if we consider the first three times
t_{1},t_{2}\text{ and }t_{3}
, and
t_{k}=0
for
k\geq 3
the equation (
4.3
) is equivalent to the KP equation in Hirota’s form

\displaystyle(3\mathcal{D}^{2}_{2}-4\mathcal{D}_{1}\mathcal{D}_{3}+\mathcal{D}^{4}_{1})\,\tau^{2}=0.

(4.7)

Putting

\partial^{2}_{t_{1}}\log\tau(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})=\frac{1}{2}u(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})

one obtains the celebrated KP equation

3\partial^{2}_{t_{2}}u=\partial_{t_{1}}(4\partial_{t_{3}}u-\partial^{3}_{t_{1}}u-6u\partial_{t_{1}}u)

(4.8)

The rest of this section is devoted to the verification of the Hirota bilinear relation (
4.3
) for the KP tau function.

4.1
Hirota bilinear relation for the KP hierarchy

The main result is the following.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})
be the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem

\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})=\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}{2}\sigma_{3}}M_{0}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}{2}\sigma_{3}},\;\qquad\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})\underset{z\to\infty}{\to}{\bf 1}

with the traceless matrix
M_{0}(z)
compactly supported on a bounded domain
\mathscr{D}
of the complex plane and the function

\xi
given by the formal sum
\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})=\sum_{j=1}^{+\infty}z^{j}t_{j}
.
Then the function

\tau(\boldsymbol{t})=\exp\left(\int\omega\right),

(4.9)

with
\omega
defined in (
3.21
)
is a KP
\tau
-function; i.e. it satisfies the Hirota Bilinear relation (
4.3
).

Remark 4.2
.

In this setting the KP
\tau
-function is in general complex–valued. Under appropriate additional symmetry constraints for the matrix
M_{0}
and the domain
\mathscr{D}
we can obtain a real–valued
\tau
-function.

We prove the theorem in several steps. We first analyse the effect of the Miwa shifts on the
\tau
-function.
For this purpose we need to determine how the Miwa shift acts on the matrices
\Gamma(z,\bar{z},t)
and
M(z,\bar{z},\boldsymbol{t})
. We consider
M(z,\bar{z},\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])
first.

M(z,\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])={\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])\sigma_{3}}M_{0}(z){\rm e}^{-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])\sigma_{3}}

from the definition of
\xi(z,t)
(
4.2
)

\begin{split}&\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])=\sum^{+\infty}_{j=1}z^{j}\left(t_{j}\pm\frac{1}{j\zeta^{j}}\right)=\sum^{+\infty}_{j=1}z^{j}t_{j}\pm\sum^{+\infty}_{j=1}\frac{z^{j}}{j\zeta^{j}}=\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})\mp\ln\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)\\
\end{split}

and we have that

M(z,\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])=\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{\mp\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2}}M(z,\boldsymbol{t})\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{\pm\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2}}.

(4.10)

For the matrices
\Gamma(z,\overline{z},\boldsymbol{t}\pm[\zeta^{-1}])
we need to consider the two case separately. Let us start with the negative shift
\Gamma(z,\overline{z},\boldsymbol{t}-[\zeta^{-1}])

\begin{split}\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}-[\zeta^{-1}])&=\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}-[\zeta^{-1}])M(z,\boldsymbol{t}-[\zeta^{-1}])\\
&=\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}-[\zeta^{-1}])\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{+\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2}}M(z,\boldsymbol{t})\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{-\frac{\sigma_{3}}{2}}\\

\end{split}

(4.11)

where

\displaystyle D(z,\zeta)=\begin{bmatrix}1-\frac{z}{\zeta}&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}.

From (
4.11
), we notice that the matrix
\Gamma(z,t-[\zeta^{-1}])D(z,\zeta)
satisfies the
\overline{\partial}
-problem
2.1
, i.e. there exists a connection matrix
C(z)
such that

(4.12)

where obviously

depends also on
\zeta
and
\boldsymbol{t}
.

The matrix

is determined by the conditions that both
\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})
and

\begin{split}\lim_{z\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{-1}&C(z)\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{11}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\\
\Gamma_{12}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\quad\lim_{z\to\infty}C(z)\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{21}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\\
\Gamma_{22}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\\
&\lim_{z\to\zeta}\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{-1}C(z)\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{11}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\\

0\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

-\frac{\Gamma_{21}(\zeta)}{\Gamma_{11}(\zeta)}&1\end{bmatrix}.

(4.14)

Following the same ideas, we can find a similar formula for

Also in this case, we have three conditions similar to (

) :

\begin{split}\lim_{z\to\infty}\tilde{C}(z)&\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{11}(x,\boldsymbol{t})\\
\Gamma_{12}(x,\boldsymbol{t})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}\quad\lim_{z\to\infty}\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)^{-1}\tilde{R}(z,\zeta)\begin{bmatrix}\Gamma_{21}(x,\boldsymbol{t})\\
\Gamma_{22}(x,\boldsymbol{t})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}\\

\Gamma_{22}(x,\boldsymbol{t})\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
\Gamma_{22}(\zeta,\boldsymbol{t})\end{bmatrix}\end{split}

(4.16)

and we find out that

has the following form:

\tilde{C}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}1&-\frac{\Gamma_{12}(\zeta)}{\Gamma_{22}(\zeta)}\\
-\frac{\partial_{z}\Gamma_{21}(\infty)}{\zeta}&\left(1-\frac{z}{\zeta}\right)+\frac{\partial_{z}\Gamma_{21}(\infty)\Gamma_{12}(\zeta)}{\zeta\Gamma_{22}(\zeta)}\end{bmatrix}.

(4.17)

We need to show how the Miwa shift acts on the Malgrange one form. We define

the differential deformed including the external parameter
\zeta

(4.18)

When

Now we can state the following proposition:

For

\frac{\tau(\boldsymbol{t}-[\zeta^{-1}])}{\tau(\boldsymbol{t})}=\Gamma_{11}(\zeta,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{\gamma(\zeta)}\qquad\frac{\tau(\boldsymbol{t}+[\zeta^{-1}])}{\tau(\boldsymbol{t})}=\Gamma^{-1}_{11}(\zeta,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{-\gamma(\zeta)}\,,

Proof.

From Lemma

an then, from the properties of the logarithm the statement (

\blacksquare

Remark 4.5

The exponential term

Let us now define the matrix

as

H(z):=H(z;\boldsymbol{t},\boldsymbol{s}):=\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{(\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s}))E_{11}}\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s})

Lemma 4.6
.

The matrix

defined in (

) is analytic for all

.

Proof.

For
z\notin\mathscr{D}
the statement is trivial, so we consider the case of

.

We apply the operator
\partial_{\bar{z}}
to the matrix (

)

\begin{split}\partial_{\bar{z}}H(z)&=\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{(\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s}))E_{11}}\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s})+\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{(\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s}))E_{11}}\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s})\\

&\quad-\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{(\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s}))E_{11}}M(z,\boldsymbol{s})\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s})\\
&=\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})\left({\rm e}^{\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}{2}\sigma_{3}}M_{0}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}{2}\sigma_{3}}{\rm e}^{(\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s}))E_{11}}+\right.\\
&\quad\left.-{\rm e}^{(\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s}))E_{11}}{\rm e}^{\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})}{2}\sigma_{3}}M_{0}(z){\rm e}^{-\frac{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})}{2}\sigma_{3}}\right)\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s})\\

&=0\end{split}

and this proves the statement.

\blacksquare

Proof of Theorem

.

Let us compute the residue

\displaystyle\text{Res}_{z=\infty}(\tau(\boldsymbol{t}-[z^{-1}])\tau(\boldsymbol{s}+[z^{-1}]){\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})})

\displaystyle=\tau(\boldsymbol{t})\tau(\boldsymbol{s})\text{Res}_{z=\infty}(\frac{\tau(\boldsymbol{t}-[z^{-1}])}{\tau(\boldsymbol{t})}\frac{\tau(\boldsymbol{s}+[z^{-1}])}{\tau(\boldsymbol{s})}{\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})})

\displaystyle=\tau(\boldsymbol{t})\tau(\boldsymbol{s})\text{Res}_{z=\infty}(\Gamma_{11}(z,\boldsymbol{t})(\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s}))_{11}{\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})})

\displaystyle=\tau(\boldsymbol{t})\tau(\boldsymbol{s})\lim_{R\to\infty}\oint_{|z|=R}\Gamma_{11}(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})}(\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s}))_{11}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi i}

(4.24)

Consider the first diagonal element of the matrix

. From the analyticity of

proved in Lemma

we get

\begin{split}0=\left(\oint_{|z|=R}H(z)\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi i}\right)_{11}&=\oint_{|z|=R}\Gamma_{11}(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})-\xi(z,\boldsymbol{s})}(\Gamma^{-1}(z,\boldsymbol{s}))_{11}\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi i}+\\
&\quad-\oint_{|z|=R}\Gamma_{12}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\Gamma_{21}(z,\boldsymbol{s})\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi i}.\end{split}

(4.25)

So, we can rewite (

) as

\displaystyle\penalty\ (\ref{eq:proof-hir})=\tau(\boldsymbol{t})\tau(\boldsymbol{s})\lim_{R\to\infty}\oint_{|z|=R}\Gamma_{12}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\Gamma_{21}(z,\boldsymbol{s})\frac{\mathrm{d}z}{2\pi i}

Since both

4.2
The focusing Nonlinear Schrödinger equation

In this subsection we make a specific choice of the matrix
M_{0}
of the form

M_{0}(z)=\begin{bmatrix}0&\beta(z)\chi_{\mathscr{D}}\\
-\overline{\beta(\overline{z})}\chi_{\overline{\mathscr{D}}}&0\end{bmatrix},

where
\beta(z)=\beta(z,\overline{z})
is a smooth function on
\mathscr{D}\subset{\mathbb{C}}_{+}
and
\chi_{\mathscr{D}}
(
\chi_{\overline{\mathscr{D}}}
) is the characteristic function of
\mathscr{D}
(
\overline{\mathscr{D}}
).
We observe that
M_{0}
satisfies the Schwarz symmetry

\displaystyle\overline{M_{0}(\overline{z})}=\sigma_{2}M_{0}(z)\sigma_{2},\quad\text{ where }\sigma_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}0&-i\\
i&0\end{bmatrix}.

(4.27)

Let us consider the
\overline{\partial}
-problem

\displaystyle\partial_{\bar{z}}\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})=\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t}){\rm e}^{-i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})\sigma_{3}}M_{0}(z){\rm e}^{i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})\sigma_{3}}

\displaystyle\text{for }z\in{\mathscr{D}}\cup\overline{\mathscr{D}}

(4.28)

\displaystyle\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})\underset{z\to\infty}{\to}{\bf 1}

with
\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})
as in (
4.2
). Here we have re-defined
t_{j}\to-2it_{j}
to respect the customary normalization of times in the KP-hieararchy.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})
be the solution of the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
4.28
) and let

\psi(\boldsymbol{t}):=2i\lim_{z\to\infty}z(\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})-{\bf 1})_{12}\,.

Then the function
\psi=\psi(\boldsymbol{t})
satisfies the nonlinear Schrödinger hierarchy
[
14
,
30
]
written in the recursive form

\displaystyle i\partial_{t_{m}}\psi_{1}=2\psi_{m+1},\quad\psi_{1}:=\psi,\;\;m\geq 1,

(4.29)

\displaystyle\psi_{m}=\frac{i}{2}\partial_{t_{1}}\psi_{m-1}+\psi_{1}h_{m-1},\quad\partial_{t_{1}}h_{m}=2\,{\rm Im}(\psi_{1}\overline{\psi}_{m}),

(4.30)

where
\psi_{m}
and
h_{m}
are functions of
\boldsymbol{t}
and

.

The proof of this theorem is classical and is deferred to Appendix

.
In particular the second flow gives the focusing NLS equation

i\partial_{t_{2}}\psi+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{t_{1}}\psi+|\psi|^{2}\psi=0,

where comparing with the notation in the introduction

and

. The third flows gives the so called complex modified KdV equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t_{3}}\psi+\frac{\partial^{3}_{t_{1}}\psi}{4}+\frac{3}{2}|\psi|^{2}\partial_{t_{1}}\psi=0.

Setting
t_{k}=0
for

one obtains that
v(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3}):=2|\psi_{1}(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})|^{2}
satisfies the KP equation
(
4.8
) after the rescalings

and

.

4.2.1
Reduction to a Riemann-Hilbert problem

In this section we show that for particular choices of
\beta
and of the domain
\mathscr{D}
one can reduce the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
4.28
) to a standard Riemann-Hilbert problem when
z
is outside
\mathscr{D}
and
\overline{\mathscr{D}}
.
The easiest way to solve the
\overline{\partial}
-problem (
4.28
) is to split it in components

\Gamma(z,\boldsymbol{t})=[\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\quad\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})]

so that

\begin{split}\bar{\partial}\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=0\\
\bar{\partial}\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\beta(z)e^{-2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\end{split}\text{\quad for }z\in\mathcal{D}

(4.31)

\begin{split}\bar{\partial}\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=-\beta^{*}(z)e^{2i\xi(z;\boldsymbol{t})}\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})\\
\bar{\partial}\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=0\end{split}\text{\quad for }z\in\mathcal{\overline{D}}

(4.32)

with the boundary condition

\vec{A}\sim\begin{pmatrix}1\\
0\end{pmatrix}\quad\vec{B}\sim\begin{pmatrix}0\\
1\end{pmatrix}\quad\text{ as }z\to\infty.

(4.33)

From the equations (
4.31
) and (
4.32
) one deduces that

•

\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})
is analytic for
z
in the set
\mathscr{D}
;

•

\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})
is analytic for
z
in the set
\mathscr{\overline{D}}
.

From the Cauchy-Pompeiu formula, we can rewrite the equations (
4.31
) and (
4.32
) as a system of two integral equations

\begin{split}\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\iint_{\mathscr{\overline{D}}}\frac{\vec{B}(w,\boldsymbol{t})\beta^{*}(w)e^{2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}}{w-z}\frac{d\bar{w}\wedge dw}{2\pi i}\\
\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}-\iint_{\mathscr{D}}\frac{\vec{A}(w,\boldsymbol{t})\beta(w)e^{-2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}}{w-z}\frac{d\bar{w}\wedge dw}{2\pi i}.\quad\end{split}

(4.34)

Let us assume now that
\beta(z)
is analytic in
\mathscr{D}
simply connected and the boundary of
\mathscr{D}
is sufficiently smooth so that it can be described by the so–called Schwarz function
S(z)

[
17
]
of the domain
\mathscr{D}
through the equation

\overline{z}=S(z).

The Schwarz function admits an analytic extension to a maximal domain
\mathscr{D}^{0}\subset\mathscr{D}
. Here we assume that
\mathscr{L}:=\mathscr{D}\setminus\mathscr{D}^{0}
consist of a
mother-body
, i.e., a collection of smooth arcs. An example of this is the ellipse.
Using Stokes theorem and the Schwarz function of the domain, we can reduce the area integral in (
4.34
) to a contour integral, namley

\begin{split}\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\oint_{\partial\mathscr{\overline{D}}}\frac{\vec{B}(w,\boldsymbol{t})S^{*}(w)\beta^{*}(w)e^{2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}}{w-z}\frac{d\bar{w}\wedge dw}{2\pi i}\\
\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}-\oint_{\partial\mathscr{D}}\frac{\vec{A}(w,\boldsymbol{t})S(w)\beta(w)e^{-2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}}{w-z}\frac{d\bar{w}\wedge dw}{2\pi i},\;z\notin\mathscr{D},\end{split}

(4.35)

where the boundary
\partial\mathscr{D}
is oriented anticlockwise. By analiticity, we can shrink the contour integral to the mother-body
\mathscr{L}
, namely

\begin{split}\vec{A}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\begin{bmatrix}1\\
0\end{bmatrix}+\oint_{\mathscr{\overline{L}}}\frac{\vec{B}(w,\boldsymbol{t})\Delta S^{*}(w)\beta^{*}(w)e^{2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}}{w-z}\frac{d\bar{w}\wedge dw}{2\pi i}\\
\vec{B}(z,\boldsymbol{t})&=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
1\end{bmatrix}-\oint_{\mathscr{L}}\frac{\vec{A}(w,\boldsymbol{t})\Delta S(w)\beta(w)e^{-2i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})}}{w-z}\frac{d\bar{w}\wedge dw}{2\pi i},\;z\notin\mathscr{D},\end{split}

(4.36)

where
\Delta S(w)=S_{-}(w)-S_{+}(w)
, with
S_{\pm}(z)
the boundary values of
S
on the oriented contour
\mathscr{L}
.
The orientation of
\mathscr{L}
is inherited by the orientation of
\partial\mathscr{D}
.
We can express the system (
4.36
) in matrix form

\widetilde{\Gamma}(z,\boldsymbol{t})={\bf 1}+\int_{\mathscr{L}\cup\overline{\mathscr{L}}}\frac{\widetilde{\Gamma}(w,\boldsymbol{t})\rm e^{-i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})\sigma_{3}}\widetilde{M}(w,\boldsymbol{t})\rm e^{i\xi(z,\boldsymbol{t})\sigma_{3}}}{w-z}\frac{dw}{2\pi i}

(4.37)

where

\widetilde{M}(z,\boldsymbol{t})=\begin{bmatrix}0&\Delta S(z)\beta(z)\chi_{\mathscr{L}}(z)\\
-\Delta S^{*}(z)\beta^{*}(z)\chi_{\overline{\mathscr{L}}}(z)&0\end{bmatrix}

(4.38)

Using then the Sokhotski-Plemelj formula we can rewrite the above integral equation as a Riemann-Hilbert problem for a matrix function
\widetilde{\Gamma}(z,\boldsymbol{t})
analytic in

such that
