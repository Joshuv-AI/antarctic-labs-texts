The wrong direction of Jensen’s inequality is algorithmically right
Or Zamir

Abstract
Let 𝒜 \mathcal{A} be an algorithm with expected running time e X e^{X} , conditioned on the value of some random variable X X . We construct an algorithm 𝒜 ′ \mathcal{A^{\prime}} with expected running time O ⁡ ( e 𝖤 ⁡ [ X ] ) O\left(e^{\mathsf{E}[X]}\right) , that fully executes 𝒜 \mathcal{A} . In particular, an algorithm whose running time is a random variable T T can be converted to one with expected running time O ⁡ ( e 𝖤 ⁡ [ ln ⁡ T ] ) O\left(e^{\mathsf{E}[\ln T]}\right) , which is never worse than O ⁡ ( 𝖤 ⁡ [ T ] ) O(\mathsf{E}[T]) . No information about the distribution of X X is required for the construction of 𝒜 ′ \mathcal{A}^{\prime} .

We transform an algorithm

A

We stop at the first time

Truncated evaluations are frequently used in complexity theory (for example, see the proof of the time and space hierarchies in

The first algorithmic use of such a sequence of truncated evaluations that we are aware of, is by Alt, Guibas, Mehlhorn, Karp and Wigderson

They used it to convert

Luby, Sinclair and Zuckerman

if there exists any sequence that runs in time

for the same algorithm.
Our contribution thus is two-fold: first, we prove the existence of good strategies in terms of

Not paying additional factors guarantees, due to Jensen’s inequality, that our transformed algorithm is never worse than the original algorithm.

A natural use for such theorems is the regime of exponential-time algorithms.

2.1
Optimal bound when the distribution of

The bound given by Markov’s inequality in

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable.
There exists
t\in[0,\mathsf{E}[X]+1]
such that
\frac{e^{t}}{\Pr\left(X<t\right)}\leq e^{\mathsf{E}[X]+1}
.

Proof.

Assume by contradiction that
\frac{e^{t}}{\Pr\left(X<t\right)}>e^{\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1}
for every
t\in[0,\mathsf{E}[X]+1]
.
Equivalently,

\Pr\left(X\geq t\right)=1-\Pr\left(X<t\right)>1-e^{t-\left(\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1\right)}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[X]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Pr(X\geq t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\geq\int_{0}^{\mathsf{E}[X]+1}\Pr(X\geq t)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle>\int_{0}^{\mathsf{E}[X]+1}\left(1-e^{t-\left(\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1\right)}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\left(\mathsf{E}[X]+1\right)-\left(1-e^{-\left(\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1\right)}\right)=\mathsf{E}[X]+e^{-\left(\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1\right)}

\displaystyle>\mathsf{E}[X],

which is a contradiction.

Lemma 2.4
.

Let
X
be a non-negative random variable.
It holds that either
\Pr\left(X\leq\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right)>\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1}
or
\Pr\left(X\leq\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2\right)>\frac{1}{\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2}

Proof.

Assume that
p:=\Pr\left(X\leq\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right)\leq\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1}
.
We observe that

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]

\displaystyle=p\;\mathsf{E}\left[X\;|\;X\leq\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right]+\left(1-p\right)\mathsf{E}\left[X\;|\;X>\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right]

\displaystyle\geq\left(1-p\right)\mathsf{E}\left[X\;|\;X>\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right],

and hence

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left[X\;|\;X>\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-p}\mathsf{E}[X]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{1-\frac{1}{\mathsf{E}[X]+1}}\mathsf{E}[X]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[X]+1.

Denote by
Y:=X-\left(\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]-\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]\right)
.
The above can now be rephrased as
\mathsf{E}\left[Y\;|\;Y>0\right]\leq\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1
.
Applying Markov’s inequality to

, we get

\Pr\left(Y>\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2\;|\;Y>0\right)\leq\frac{\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+1}{\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2}=1-\frac{1}{\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2}.

We conclude by noting that

\Pr\left(X>\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2\right)=\Pr\left(Y>\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2\right)\leq\Pr\left(Y>\ln\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]+2\;|\;Y>0\right).

∎

Definition 2.6
.

Let

Claim 2.7

The following hold for all

\lambda^{\left(\lambda^{\star}\left(x\right)\right)}(x)>4

(1)

In particular,

(2)

\lambda^{\left(\lambda^{\star}\left(x\right)-1\right)}(x)>5
and hence

.

(3)

We are now ready to prove a generalized version of Lemma

Lemma 2.8
.

Let
X
be a non-negative distribution and
E\geq\max\left(\mathsf{E}\left[X\right],\;5\right)
be an upper bound on its expectation.
There either exists
1\leq k\leq\lambda^{\star}(E)
such that
\Pr\left(X<E-\lambda^{(k)}\left(E\right)\right)\geq\left(\left(\lambda^{(k-1)}\left(E\right)+2\right)^{2}+1\right)^{-1}
, or it holds that
\Pr\left(X<E+10\right)\geq\frac{1}{2}
.

Proof.

We recursively denote by
E_{0}:=E
and by
E_{k}:=\lambda^{(k)}(E)+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{E_{i}}
for
1\leq k\leq\lambda^{\star}(E)
. Note that
E_{k}\geq\lambda^{(k)}(E)
and hence also

E_{k}=\lambda^{(k)}(E)+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{E_{i}}\leq\lambda^{(k)}(E)+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\lambda^{(i)}(E)}<\lambda^{(k)}(E)+2,

where the last inequality follows from Claim
2.7
.
In particular,
\lambda^{(k)}(E)\leq E_{k}<\lambda^{(k)}(E)+2
.

Assume that
\Pr\left(X<E-\lambda^{(k)}\left(E\right)\right)<\left(\left(\lambda^{(k-1)}\left(E\right)+2\right)^{2}+1\right)^{-1}<\frac{1}{\left(E_{k-1}\right)^{2}+1}
for every
1\leq k\leq\lambda^{\star}\left(E\right)
.

Denote by
Y_{k}:=X-\left(E-\lambda^{(k)}\left(E\right)\right)
for
k\geq 0
.
We prove by induction on
k
that
\mathsf{E}\left[Y_{k}\;|\;Y_{k}\geq 0\right]\leq E_{k}
.
For
k=0
the claim is straightforward as
Y_{0}=X
and
E_{0}=E
.
For the inductive step, we assume the hypothesis holds for
k-1
and show it holds for
k
.
We note that
Y_{k-1}>Y_{k}
and hence if
Y_{k}\geq 0
then
Y_{k-1}\geq 0
as well. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[Y_{k-1}\;|\;Y_{k-1}\geq 0]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left(Y_{k}\geq 0\;|\;Y_{k-1}\geq 0\right)\mathsf{E}\left[Y_{k-1}\;|\;Y_{k}\geq 0\right]

\displaystyle\geq\Pr\left(Y_{k}\geq 0\right)\mathsf{E}\left[Y_{k-1}\;|\;Y_{k}\geq 0\right].

Thus, by the induction hypothesis we have

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}\left[Y_{k-1}\;|\;Y_{k}\geq 0\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\mathsf{E}[Y_{k-1}\;|\;Y_{k-1}\geq 0]}{\Pr\left(Y_{k}\geq 0\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{E_{k-1}}{1-\frac{1}{\left(E_{k-1}\right)^{2}+1}}

\displaystyle=E_{k-1}+\frac{1}{E_{k-1}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathsf{E}[Y_{k}\;|\;Y_{k}\geq 0]

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[Y_{k-1}\;|\;Y_{k}\geq 0]+\lambda^{(k)}\left(E\right)-\lambda^{(k-1)}\left(E\right)

\displaystyle\leq E_{k-1}+\frac{1}{E_{k-1}}+\lambda^{(k)}\left(E\right)-\lambda^{(k-1)}\left(E\right)

\displaystyle=E_{k}.

In particular, we have that
\mathsf{E}\left[Y_{\lambda^{\star}\left(E\right)}\;|\;Y_{\lambda^{\star}\left(E\right)}\geq 0\right]\leq E_{\lambda^{\star}\left(E\right)}<\lambda^{\left(\lambda^{\star}\left(E\right)\right)}(E)+2\leq 7
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\Pr\left(X\geq E+10\right)

3
Conclusions and Open Problems

We showed that a Las-Vegas algorithm with expected running
e^{X}
conditioned on the value of some random variable
X
, can always be converted to a Las-Vegas algorithm with expected running time
O\left(e^{\mathsf{E}[X]}\right)
.
In particular, an algorithm whose running time is a random variable
T
can be converted to one with expected running time
O\left(e^{\mathsf{E}[\ln T]}\right)
, which is never worse than
O(\mathsf{E}[T])
.

We demonstrated a use of this theorem to simplify a proof in the regime of exponential time algorithms
[
Zam22
]
.
It is interesting to try applying it to other exponential and non-exponential time algorithms and see if it can simplify or even improve the analysis.

3.1
Considering the variance

In terms of
\mathsf{E}[X]
only, we can not get any better than
O\left(e^{\mathsf{E}[X]}\right)
as the distribution of
X
might be constant.
In that case though, the variance of
X
is zero.
Can we get a better bound just by assuming that the variance of
X
is large?
Unfortunately, with the standard definition of variance this is not the case.
For any choice of
E
and
V\geq 2E^{2}e^{-E}
consider the following distribution:

\Pr\left(X=k\right):=\begin{cases}e^{-E}&k=0\\
1-\frac{Ve^{-E}}{V-E^{2}e^{-E}}&k=E\\
\frac{\left(Ee^{-E}\right)^{2}}{V-E^{2}e^{-E}}&k=\frac{V}{Ee^{-E}}\end{cases}.

Its expectation is
E
, its variance is
V
, which can be arbitrarily large, and nevertheless
\Pr(X<E)=e^{-E}
so no strategy can beat
O\left(e^{E}\right)
.

On the other hand, the wishful thinking above is true with some other notions of variance.
For example, if we consider mean deviation instead of standard deviation (i.e.,
\mathsf{E}\left[\;|X-\mathsf{E}\left[X\right]|\;\right]
), then
it is
true that if the variance is large then we can get a better running time.
It is intriguing to find useful notion of variance for which such a statement is true, with the goal of improving the running time of algorithms by analyzing the variance of
X
.

In particular, consider the PPSZ algorithm for solving
k
-SAT
[
PPSZ05
]

[
Her14
]
.
The algorithm uses randomization in two ways: first, a random permutation of the variables in the input formulas is drawn; Then, the chosen permutation determines the number of variables we need to
guess
the value of.
In a recent improvement of the PPSZ analysis, Scheder
[
Sch22
]
showed that in some large subset of permutations the number of guessed variables is smaller than what we expect when taking a uniformly random permutation.
In particular, this implies that there is some non-negligible variance in the original algorithm’s running time.
Can we get better SAT algorithms by analyzing this variance?
