Abstract
. This article examines the asymptotic behavior of the Widom factors, denoted 𝒲 n {\mathcal{W}}_{n} , for Chebyshev polynomials of finite unions of Jordan arcs. We prove that, in contrast to Widom’s proposal in [ 54 ] , when dealing with a single smooth Jordan arc, 𝒲 n {\mathcal{W}}_{n} converges to 2 exclusively when the arc is a straight line segment. Our main focus is on analysing polynomial preimages of the interval [ − 2 , 2 ] [-2,2] , and we provide a complete description of the asymptotic behavior of 𝒲 n {\mathcal{W}}_{n} for symmetric star graphs and quadratic preimages of [ − 2 , 2 ] [-2,2] . We observe that in the case of star graphs, the Chebyshev polynomials and the polynomials orthogonal with respect to equilibrium measure share the same norm asymptotics, suggesting a potential extension of the conjecture posed in [ 18 ] . Lastly, we propose a possible connection between the S S -property and Widom factors converging to 2 2 .



Keywords
Chebyshev polynomials, Widom factors, polynomial preimages, star graphs

1.
Introduction

Let
{\mathsf{E}}
be a compact subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
with at least
n+1
points. The
n
th Chebyshev polynomial of
{\mathsf{E}}
, denoted
T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}
, is the unique monic polynomial of degree
n
which minimises the supremum norm on
{\mathsf{E}}
. In other words,
T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}
is the polynomial

T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}(z)=z^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}z^{k}

which satisfies

\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}:=\max_{z\in{\mathsf{E}}}|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}(z)|=\min_{b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{C}}}\max_{z\in{\mathsf{E}}}\left|z^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}z^{k}\right|.

Facts regarding existence and uniqueness of
T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}
can be found in, e.g.,
[
42
,
30
]
. See also
[
14
,
17
]
for a recent account on the basic theory of Chebyshev polynomials.
These polynomials were initially studied by Chebyshev
[
12
,
13
]
in the case where
{\mathsf{E}}=[-1,1]
. In this situation, he showed that the polynomials are explicitly given by the formula

(1.1)

T_{n}(x):=T_{n}^{[-1,1]}(x)=2^{1-n}\cos\bigl(n\arccos(x)\bigr),\quad|x|\leq 1.

This representation further hints at a property of the Chebyshev polynomials that holds for arbitrary compact subsets of the real line. A monic degree
n
polynomial,
P_{n}
, is the Chebyshev polynomial of
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
if and only if there exists points
x_{0}<x_{1}<\ldots<x_{n}
in
{\mathsf{E}}
such that

P_{n}(x_{k})=(-1)^{n-k}\|P_{n}\|_{\mathsf{E}},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n.

This characterising property of the Chebyshev polynomials is called
alternation
and one can use it to prove several facts concerning their asymptotic behaviour (see, e.g.,
[
14
,
15
]
).

It should be noted that alternation fails to hold for Chebyshev polynomials of non-real compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
. Instead, the asymptotics of such polynomials is typically studied using potential theoretic methods. This is an approach dating back to Faber
[
20
]
, Fekete
[
21
]
, and Szegő
[
47
]
. Faber investigated Chebyshev polynomials by constructing trial polynomials, the so-called Faber polynomials, from the associated conformal map which maps the exterior of
{\mathsf{E}}
to the exterior of the closed unit disk,
\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. Of course, the existence of this conformal map assumes that the complement of
{\mathsf{E}}
is simply connected on the Riemann sphere.

As for notation, let
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}
denote the Riemann sphere
{\mathbb{C}}\cup\{\infty\}
. Throughout the paper, we shall make use of the following important notions

•

\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})
, the logarithmic capacity of
{\mathsf{E}}

•

G_{\mathsf{E}}(\,\cdot\,):=G_{{\mathsf{E}}}(\,\cdot\,,\infty)
, the Green’s function for
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus{\mathsf{E}}
with pole at
\infty

•

\mu_{\mathsf{E}}
, the equilibrium measure of
{\mathsf{E}}

Recall also the relation

(1.2)

\int\log|x-z|\,d\mu_{\mathsf{E}}(x)=G_{\mathsf{E}}(z)+\log\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}).

For a more in depth account of potential theory, we refer the reader to, e.g.,
[
7
,
23
,
25
,
29
,
35
]
.

1.1.
Widom factors for
T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}

One way of quantatively describing the norm of the Chebyshev polynomials using logarithmic capacity is via the Faber–Fekete–Szegő theorem which states that

(1.3)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}^{1/n}=\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})

for any compact set
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
(see, e.g.,
[
35
, Chapter 5.5]
). This implies that
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})^{n}
is the leading order behaviour of
\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}}
, and (
1.3
) limits the way that the so-called Widom factors defined by

(1.4)

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}):=\frac{\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}}{\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})^{n}}

can grow as
n
increases. For a wide variety of sets, this quantity is known to be bounded in
n
and its asymptotic behaviour is of particular interest. See, e.g.,
[
5
,
6
,
17
]
for more details.

Another classical result in the theory of Chebyshev polynomials is

Theorem
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a compact set. Then

(1.5)

\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}\geq\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})^{n}

and if
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, we even have

(1.6)

\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}\geq 2\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})^{n}.

While (
1.5
) goes back to Szegő
[
47
]
, the inequality in (
1.6
) is more recent and due to Schiefermayr
[
38
]
. We shall present a proof of these statements below. Partly because our method will be used in later parts of the paper and is much shorter than the one presented in
[
38
]
, and partly for completeness. Our proof rests on the following formula for capacity of polynomial preimages.

Lemma
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}
be a compact subset of
{\mathbb{C}}
and suppose
P(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{m}a_{k}z^{k}
is a polynomial with
a_{m}\neq 0
. If

{\mathsf{E}}_{P}:=\bigl\{z:P(z)\in{\mathsf{E}}\bigr\}=P^{-1}({\mathsf{E}}),

then

(1.7)

G_{{\mathsf{E}}_{P}}(z)=\frac{1}{m}G_{\mathsf{E}}\bigl(P(z)\bigr)\quad\text{and}\quad\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{P})=\left(\frac{\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})}{|a_{m}|}\right)^{1/m}.

For a proof of this fact, see
[
35
, Theorem 5.2.5]
.

Proof of theorem.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be an arbitrary compact set. Clearly,

{\mathsf{E}}\subset(T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}})^{-1}\Big(\bigl\{z:|z|\leq\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}}\bigr\}\Big).

Applying (
1.7
) together with the facts that
\operatorname{Cap}
is monotone with respect to set inclusion and a disk of radius
r>0
has capacity equal to
r
, we obtain that

\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})\leq\operatorname{Cap}\Big(\bigl\{z:|z|\leq\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}}\bigr\}\Big)^{1/n}=\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|^{1/n}.

This proves (
1.5
).

If
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, then
T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}
is easily shown to have only real coefficients. Hence

{\mathsf{E}}\subset(T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}})^{-1}\Big(\bigl\{z:z\in\bigl[-\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}},\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}\bigr]\bigr\}\Big).

As the capacity of an interval
[a,b]
equals
{(b-a)}/{4}
, yet another application of (
1.7
) implies that

\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})\leq\operatorname{Cap}\Big(\bigl\{z:z\in\bigl[-\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}},\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}\bigr]\bigr\}\Big)^{1/n}=\left(\frac{\|T_{n}^{\mathsf{E}}\|_{\mathsf{E}}}{2}\right)^{1/n},

proving (
1.6
).
∎

Remark
.

Note that the above theorem can be restated in terms of the Widom factors as

(1.8)

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})\geq 1\;\text{ for }\;{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}

and

(1.9)

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})\geq 2\;\text{ when }\;{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}.

The sets for which the Szegő lower bound (
1.5
) is saturated for
some
value of
n
were determined in
[
16
]
. With
O\partial(\,\cdot\,)
denoting the outer boundary and
\partial{\mathbb{D}}
the unit circle, the authors proved that

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=1\quad\text{if and only if }\quad O\partial({\mathsf{E}})=P^{-1}(\partial\mathbb{D})

for some polynomial
P
of degree
n
. Regarding Schiefermayr’s lower bound, it was proven in
[
51
]
(see also
[
16
]
) that for
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
,

(1.10)

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=2\quad\text{if and only if }\quad{\mathsf{E}}=P^{-1}\bigl([-2,2]\bigr)

for some degree
n
polynomial
P
.

Now the question remains in which cases we have an asymptotic saturation of these lower bounds. More precisely, when does it happen that

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=1\text{ or }2?

It is known that if
{\mathsf{E}}
is the closure of a Jordan domain with boundary curve of class
C^{2+\epsilon}
(i.e., its coordinates are
C^{2+\epsilon}
functions

(1.11)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=1.

This was first shown in the case where
{\mathsf{E}}
has analytic boundary by Faber
[
20
]
and then extended to the case where the boundary curve is of class
C^{2+\epsilon}
by Widom
[
54
]
. However, there may well be many other connected sets
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
for which (
1.11
) holds.

For
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{R}}
, Totik
[
52

completely characterised the sets which asymptotically saturate the Schiefermayr lower bound (
1.6

(1.12)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=2

if and only if
{\mathsf{E}}
is an interval, in which case
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=2
for every
n
.

One may ask — and this is a main point of the present article — if there are more subsets
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
for which (
1.12
) holds true. Widom
[
54
]
conjectured that any sufficiently nice set which contains an arc component should satisfy (

[
50

observed that if
{\mathsf{E}}_{\alpha}=\{z:|z|=1,\,|\arg z|\leq\alpha\}
with
\alpha\in(0,\pi)

(1.13)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{\alpha})=2\cos^{2}\left(\alpha/4\right)<2.

See also
[
40
]
and
[
19
]
.

The aim of this article is to study sets
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
which satisfy (
1.12
) and investigate what properties may lie behind.
First of all, by combining results of Stahl
[
46
]
and Alpan
[
3
]

[
3
, Theorem 1.3]

Theorem 1
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a Jordan arc of class
C^{2+\epsilon}
. Then

(1.14)

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})\leq 2

and equality holds if and only if
{\mathsf{E}}
is a straight line segment.

In
[
3
]
, Alpan showed that (
1.14
) is always satisfied and gave a condition for when equality holds true in terms of the boundary behaviour of the Green’s function. He also drew the conclusion that the inequality is strict if the arc fails to be analytic. Our contribution consists of showing that the only smooth arc for which equality holds is an interval. We show this using the
S
-property which was introduced and studied in detail by Stahl (see, e.g.,
[
43
,
44
,
46
,
31
]
and Definition
1

S
-property and what are known as Chebotarev sets.

In this paper we shall exhibit several examples of sets
{\mathsf{E}}
satisfying (
1.12
) and one of the properties that these sets have in common, apart from being polynomial preimages of an interval, is the fact that they all satisfy the
S
-property. This suggests that the
S
-property could be of importance for a set
{\mathsf{E}}
to satisfy (
1.12
).

Our studies were initiated by the following family of examples

Theorem 2
.

For
m\in{\mathbb{N}}

(1.15)

{\mathsf{E}}_{m}=\bigl\{z:z^{m}\in[-2,2]\bigr\}.

Then

(1.16)

{\mathcal{W}}_{mn,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})=2,\quad n\geq 1

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})=2.

Moreover,

\lim_{m\to\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{2m-1,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})=4.

The latter part of the theorem shows that the limit in (

Theorem 3

Let

(1.19)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{2n+1,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{P})=\sqrt{2\left|c+\sqrt{c^{2}-4}\right|},

(1.21)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{2n+1,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{P})=2.

.

Note that

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})

1.2.
Orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\mu_{\mathsf{E}}

The Chebyshev polynomials are the monic polynomials minimising the
L^{\infty}
norm on a given compact set
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
. The same investigation on minimal polynomials can be undertaken for any
L^{p}
norm. We shall in particular consider the sequence of monic orthogonal polynomials with respect to equilibrium measure
\mu_{\mathsf{E}}
. These are the polynomials

P_{n}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}(z)=z^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}z^{k}

which satisfy

\int P_{n}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}(z)\overline{\displaystyle{P_{m}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}(z)}}\,d\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}}(z)=C_{n}\cdot\delta_{n,m},

where
C_{n}>0
and
\delta_{n,m}
is the Kronecker delta. It is well-known and easy to prove that all
P_{n}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}
are minimal with respect to the
L^{2}(\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}})
-norm in the sense that

\|P_{n}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\mathsf{E}})}^{2}:=\int|P_{n}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}(z)|^{2}\,d\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}}(z)=\min_{b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{C}}}\int\left|z^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}z^{k}\right|^{2}d\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}}(z).

In line with (
1.4
), we define the Widom factors corresponding to the
L^{2}
minimisers on
{\mathsf{E}}
by

(1.22)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}})

\displaystyle:=\frac{\|\displaystyle{P_{n}^{\mu_{\mathsf{E}}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{\mathsf{E}})}}{\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})^{n}}.

The name is appropriate since Widom
[
54
]
gave a complete description of the asymptotics of
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}})
in the case where
{\mathsf{E}}
is a finite union of Jordan curves and arcs of class
C^{2+\epsilon}
. Recent results of Alpan and Zinchenko
[
4
]
suggest a relation between the asymptotics of
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}})
and
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})
. In fact, the method we will use to prove Theorem
2
relies on results of Bernstein
[
8
]
where certain weighted Chebyshev polynomials are related to a class of orthogonal polynomials. This is what initially motivated our study of
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})
and we have the following result.

Theorem 4
.

With
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
as defined in (
1.15
), we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})^{2}=2.

Moreover,
n\mapsto{\mathcal{W}}_{2nm+l,2}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})^{2}

is monotonically increasing for
0<{l}/{m}<1
and monotonically decreasing for
1<{l}/{m}<2
.

Exactly the same monotonicity along subsequences was observed numerically for
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})
. However, without explicit formulas at hand this seems hard to prove. By relating Theorems
2
and
4
, we get the following result which complements the results of
[
4
]
.

Corollary 1
.

The Widom factors for
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
satisfy that

(1.23)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})^{2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{m}).

Based on the results of
[
4
]
, it was conjectured in
[
18
]
that if
{\mathsf{E}}
is a smooth Jordan arc, then

(1.24)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,2}({\mathsf{E}})^{2}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}).

It is known that (
1.24
) holds for straight line segments and circular arcs as in (
1.13
), see
[
18
]
and
[
4
]
. In our case we are not dealing with a single Jordan arc but rather a union of Jordan arcs. Nevertheless, our approach may still shed new light on the above conjecture.

2.
The
S
-property and Chebotarev sets

We now direct our focus towards compact sets
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
exhibiting a certain symmetry property which turns out to be of importance regarding the convergence behaviour
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n}({\mathsf{E}})=2
. In fact, this is exactly what we will use to prove Theorem
1
.

The symmetry property in question is called the
S
-property and was introduced by Stahl in the 1980s to study certain extremal domains in the complex plane. The “
S
” in the name is therefore ambiguous; it can be read as
symmetry
but also as
Stahl
. We will present a simplified version of the
S
-property, adapted to our needs, following
[
45
, Definition 2]
. For a more comprehensive exploration of its connection to extremal domains of meromorphic functions, we direct the reader to
[
43
,
44
,
46
]
.

Definition 1
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
be a compact set with
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})>0
and suppose
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus{\mathsf{E}}
is connected. Assume further that there exists a subset
{\mathsf{E}}_{0}\subset{\mathsf{E}}
with
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{0})=0
such that

{\mathsf{E}}\setminus{\mathsf{E}}_{0}=\bigcup_{i\in I}\gamma_{i}

where the
\gamma_{i}
’s are disjoint open analytic Jordan arcs and
I\subset{\mathbb{N}}
. Then
{\mathsf{E}}
is said to satify the
S
-property if

(2.1)

\frac{\partial G_{\mathsf{E}}}{\partial n_{+}}(z)=\frac{\partial G_{\mathsf{E}}}{\partial n_{-}}(z)

holds for all
z\in{\mathsf{E}}\setminus{\mathsf{E}}_{0}
, where
n_{+}
and
n_{-}
denote the unit normals from each side of the arcs.

Remark
.

The assumption of the arcs
\gamma_{i}
being analytic is redundant, as mild smoothness conditions coupled with the fulfillment of (
2.1
) imply the analyticity of these arcs. This observation is mentioned in
[
45
]
without providing a formal proof.

The initial question to address in the context of reducing the smoothness of individual arcs pertains to the existence of the normal derivatives in (
2.1
). The proof of
[
53
, Proposition 2.2]
invokes the Kellogg–Warschawski theorem
[
34
, Theorem 3.6]
to establish that if the coordinates of the arcs are differentiable and their derivatives are Hölder continuous, then the normal derivatives of
G_{\mathsf{E}}
exist along each
\gamma_{i}
. Consequently, it is reasonable to discuss the fulfillment of (
2.1
) for arcs belonging to the class
C^{2+\epsilon}
.
In
[
3
, Theorem 1.4]
, it is established that with this smoothness assumption, the arcs
\gamma_{i}
indeed become analytic when (
2.1
) is satisfied.

The concept of
Chebotarev sets
is closely related to the
S
-property. These sets are characterized by having minimal capacity while being constrained to include a specific collection of points. To elaborate, the Chebotarev problem, originally posed as a question to Pólya
[
33
]
, revolves around the quest for the following set:

Definition 2
.

Given a finite number of points
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in{\mathbb{C}}
, the compact connected set
{\mathsf{E}}
that contains these points and has minimal logarithmic capacity among all such sets is called the Chebotarev set of
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
. It is denoted by
{\mathsf{E}}(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{m})
.

That such sets exist and are unique was proven by Grötzsch
[
24
]
. He also characterised the Chebotarev sets in terms of the behaviour of certain quadratic differentials. Stahl
[
46
, Theorem 11]
proved that any solution to a Chebotarev problem must satisfy the
S
-property. In fact, he gave both necessary and sufficient conditions for a set to be a Chebotarev set in terms of the
S
-property and additional geometric conditions.
For further properties of Chebotarev sets, see, e.g.,
[
28
, Chapter 1]
. The case of
m=3
is studied in detail in this monograph.

Interestingly, there is also a relation between Chebotarev sets and polynomial preimages of intervals. Schiefermayr
[
39
]
proved that if
P
is a polynomial and
P^{-1}([-1,1])
is connected, then this preimage is the solution to a Chebotarev problem. More specifically, if
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
is an enumeration of the distinct simple zeros of
P^{2}-1
then

P^{-1}([-1,1])={\mathsf{E}}(\alpha_{1},\dotsc,\alpha_{m}).

In particular, any such polynomial preimage satisfies the
S
-property.

By combining Stahl’s results on minimal capacity with recent results of Alpan, we can easily prove that equality is possible in (
1.14
) only for straight line segments. In this case, as we know,
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})=2
for every
n
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}
be a Jordan arc of class
C^{2+\epsilon}
, connecting two complex points
a
and
b
. The fact that

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})\leq 2

is precisely the content of
[
3
, Theorem 1.2]
. For equality to hold, it must be so that (
2.1
) holds at all interior points of the arc. As explained in the remark following Definition
1
, this implies that
{\mathsf{E}}
is an analytic Jordan arc possessing the
S
-property. By
[
46
, Theorem 11]
,
{\mathsf{E}}
is therefore the solution to the Chebotarev problem corresponding to the points
a
and
b
. Hence
{\mathsf{E}}(a,b)=[a,b]
, where
[a,b]
denotes the straight line segment in
{\mathbb{C}}
between
a
and
b
(see, e.g.,
[
28
]
). This completes the proof.
∎

In the remainder of this article, we will consider polynomial preimages of
[-2,2]
. All the sets to be considered have in common that they satisfy the
S
-property and hence are minimal sets for a Chebotarev problem.

3.
Quadratic preimages

The general framework is sets of the form

{\mathsf{E}}_{m}:=\bigl\{z:z^{m}\in[-2,2]\bigr\}.

These are star-shaped connected sets which are invariant under rotations by
\pi/m
radians, see Figures
3
–
3
. Furthermore,
[
39
, Theorem 2]
implies that
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
is the Chebotarev set corresponding to the collection of points

\bigl\{2^{1/m}e^{i\pi k/m}:k=1,\dotsc,2m\bigr\}.

To begin with, we consider the case where
m=2
even though our results are true for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
. The motivation for this is that we more transparently can provide an intuition for the problem when
m=2
. We end the section by also considering the setting of a general quadratic preimage.

Consider the set

{\mathsf{E}}_{2}=\bigl\{z:z^{2}\in[-2,2]\bigr\}=\bigl[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}\,\bigr]\cup i\bigl[-\sqrt{2},\sqrt{2}\,\bigr],

which has the shape of a “plus sign”.
Equation (
1.7
) implies that the Green’s function for
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
with pole at
\infty
is given by

G_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)=\frac{G_{[-2,2]}(z^{2})}{2}=\log|z|+o(1),\quad z\rightarrow\infty.

From this we also see that
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{2})=1
and hence

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{2})=\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}.

Recall now that a lemma from
[
27
]
states that if
{\mathsf{E}}
is an infinite compact set and
P
a polynomial of degree
m
with leading coefficient
a_{m}
, then

(3.1)

T_{nm}^{P^{-1}({\mathsf{E}})}=(T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}}\circ P)/{a_{m}^{n}}.

For a recent proof, see
[
16
]
. In our setting this immediately implies that

T_{2n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)=T_{n}^{[-2,2]}(z^{2}),

as was also proven in
[
32
]
.
Therefore, all the Chebyshev polynomials of even degree for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
can be determined explicitly. This further implies that we can calculate “half” of the norms, that is,

{\mathcal{W}}_{2n}({\mathsf{E}}_{2})=\|T_{2n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}=\|T_{n}^{[-2,2]}\|_{[-2,2]}=2.

What is left to determine are the Chebyshev polynomials of odd degree for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
. The set
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
is symmetric since it is invariant under rotations by
\pi/2
radians. More precisely,

i{\mathsf{E}}_{2}=\{iz:z\in{\mathsf{E}}_{2}\}={\mathsf{E}}_{2},

and by uniqueness of the Chebyshev polynomials it thus follows that

(-i)^{n}T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(iz)=T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z).

From this relation we get certain conditions on the coefficients of
T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
. Several of them will vanish and considering degrees of the form
4n+l
with
l\in\{1,3\}
, we have

(3.2)

\displaystyle\begin{split}T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)&=z^{4n+l}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}z^{4k+l},\quad a_{k}\in\mathbb{R}.\end{split}

In particular,
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
has a zero at the origin of order at least
l
.
However, as we shall see below, this is the precise order of the zero.

Before stating our main characterisation of
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
, we recall that an alternating set for a function
f:I\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
is an ordered sequence of points
\{x_{k}\}\subset I
such that

f(x_{k})=\sigma(-1)^{k}\|f\|_{I},\quad|\sigma|=1.

Lemma 1
.

For
l\in\{1,3\}
, the Chebyshev polynomial
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
is characterised by alternation in the following way:

•

T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
has alternating sets consisting of
n+1
points on each of the sets
i^{k}[0,\sqrt{2}]
,
k=0,1,2,3.

•

T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
can be represented as in (
3.2
).

Proof.

We have already motivated why (
3.2
) should hold for
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
. This also implies that
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
is purely real on the real axis and purely imaginary on the imaginary axis. Now, basic theory for Chebyshev polynomials implies that
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
has at least
4n+l+1
extremal points (see, e.g.,
[
30
, Lemma 2.5.3]
). Due to symmetry, there are at least
n+1
extremal points on each of the rays

i^{k}[0,\sqrt{2}],\quad k=0,1,2,3.

It is enough to consider alternation on one of these rays since, by symmetry, the situation is entirely analogous on each of the rays. We therefore fix our attention to
[0,\sqrt{2}]
where
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
is real valued. If there were two adjacent extremal points on
[0,\sqrt{2}]
with the same sign, then this would imply that the derivative
(T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}})^{\prime}
should have at least
4\left(n+1\right)\geq 4n+l+1
zeros. But this is a contradiction since the degree of the derivative is
4n+l-1
.

To prove the other direction, we apply the intermediate value theorem in the following way. Suppose
Q
is a polynomial which can be represented as in (
3.2
). Then
Q
will be real-valued on the real line and imaginary-valued on the imaginary axis. Assume further that
Q
possesses alternating sets as in the statement of the lemma. If
Q\neq T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
, then

\|Q\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}>\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}

and it follows from the intermediate value theorem that
Q-T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
must have a zero between any two consecutive points in the alternating sets for
Q
. But this amounts to at least
4n+l

zeros in addition to the zero of multiplicity
l
at
0
. Hence the number of zeros would be greater than the degree of
Q-T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
which is impossible. Therefore, we conclude that
Q=T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
.
∎

One implication of this lemma is the fact that all the zeros of
T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
are simple, except the one at
z=0
for
T_{4n+3}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
. A further implication is that we can determine the first few Chebyshev polynomials for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
explicitly:

\displaystyle T_{1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=z,

\displaystyle T_{3}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=z^{3},

\displaystyle T_{5}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)

\displaystyle=z^{5}-\frac{5\left(3-\frac{15^{2/3}}{\sqrt[3]{9+4\sqrt{6}}}+\sqrt[3]{15(9+4\sqrt{6})}\right)^{4}}{5184}z.

Since already the expression for
T_{5}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
gets rather complicated, there seems to be no simple closed form for
T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
when
n
is large.

Lemma
1
also implies that the image of
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
under
T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
is again a plus-shaped set, namely

T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}({\mathsf{E}}_{2})=\bigcup_{k=0}^{3}i^{k}\bigl[0,\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\bigr]=\frac{\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}.

To further understand the behaviour of
\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
, we introduce the preimage

(3.3)

{\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l}:=(T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}})^{-1}\bigl(T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}({\mathsf{E}}_{2})\bigr)=(T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}})^{-1}\left(\bigcup_{k=0}^{3}i^{k}\bigl[0,\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\bigr]\right)

which clearly contains
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
as a subset.
Due to the alternating property described in Lemma
1
, we deduce that
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l}
is connected and a finite union of Jordan arcs. These in turn intersect at the zeros of
T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
. Moreover,
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l}
is nothing but the Chebotarev set corresponding to the points

(T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}})^{-1}\Bigl(\bigl\{i^{k}\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}:k=0,1,2,3\bigr\}\Bigr).

Applying (
1.7
) and using the fact that
\operatorname{Cap}(\alpha{\mathsf{E}})=|\alpha|\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}})
for any
\alpha\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we see that

(3.4)

\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l})=\operatorname{Cap}\left(\frac{\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}{\mathsf{E}}_{2}\right)^{1/{4n+l}}=\left(\frac{\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{1/{4n+l}}.

Rearranging this equality leads to the identity

(3.5)

\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}=\sqrt{2}\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l})^{4n+l}.

Since
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l}\supset{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
, we have
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l})\geq\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{2})=1
and conclude that

(3.6)

\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\geq\sqrt{2}.

Note that the reasoning outlined above follows the same method of proof that we employed to prove the lower bounds of Szegő and Schiefermayr in the introduction.
However, the conclusion is not equally strong. The lower bound in (
3.6
) is saturated for
T_{1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
, but otherwise not. Using
zT_{2n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)
as a trial polynomial, we find that

\|T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\leq\|zT_{2n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\leq\sqrt{2}\|T_{2n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}=2^{3/2}.

This upper bound is only saturated by
T_{3}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
. To close the gap between
\sqrt{2}
and
2^{3/2}
in the limit as
n\to\infty
, additional insight is needed. We shall settle this issue in the next subsection.

At this point, we would like to highlight an intriguing observation that, to date, remains open. Recall that the Remez algorithm
[
36
,
37
]

uses the theory of alternation to numerically compute Chebyshev polynomials of real compact sets. This algorithm was generalised to the complex setting by Tang
[
49
]
and further refined by Modersitzki and Fischer
[
22
]
. Using an implementation of this generalised algorithm, we can compute the norms of the Chebyshev polynomials for degree up to at least
60
. What materialised for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
is illustrated in Figure
4
.

Figure 4
.
The norm of
T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
plotted as a function of the degree
n
. The three natural subsequences are highlighted in different colors.

As we already know,
\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}=2
for all even
n
. For odd
n
, there are two natural subsequences counting modulo
4
. It appears that
n\mapsto\|T_{4n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
is monotonically increasing, while
n\mapsto\|T_{4n+3}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}
appears to be monotonically decreasing. Similar patterns emerge for the general
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
sets.

3.1.
A related weighted problem and the proof of Theorem
2
for
m=2

Rather than employing (
3.5
) and attempting to directly estimate the capacity of
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}^{n,l}
, we shall shift the minimax problem to a weighted problem on
{\mathbb{R}}
which can, in turn, be solved using a method of Bernstein
[
8
]
.

Based on (
3.2
), the Chebyshev polynomials of odd degree for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}

can be represented as

T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}(z)=z^{4n+l}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}z^{4k+l}=z^{l}\left(z^{4n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}z^{4k}\right)=z^{l}Q_{n,l}(z^{4}),

where
Q_{n,l}
is a real monic polynomial of degree
n
. Note that
Q_{n,l}
has the property that it minimises

\max_{z\in{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}|z^{l}|\bigl|Q_{n,l}(z^{4})\bigr|

among all monic polynomials of degree
n
. Since
|z^{1/4}|
is single-valued, we can change the variables via the transformation
x=z^{4}/2-1
and obtain that
Q_{n,l}
minimises

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}2^{l/4}(x+1)^{{l}/{4}}\left|Q_{n,l}\bigl(2(x+1)\bigr)\right|.

Put differently, and noting that
2^{-n}Q_{n,l}(2(x+1))
is monic, we see that

(3.7)

\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}=2^{n+{l}/{4}}\min_{b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}\in{\mathbb{R}}}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}(x+1)^{{l}/{4}}\left|x^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}x^{k}\right|.

In the case of
l=0
, we are back at the classical problem solved by Chebyshev and the polynomials
T_{n}
of (
1.1
). For
l=2
, the solution is given by the so-called Chebyshev polynomials of the third kind, denoted
V_{n}
. This is easily seen by noting that

(x+1)^{1/2}V_{n}(x)=\sqrt{2}\cos\bigl((n+1/2)\arccos(x)\bigr)

oscillates on
[-1,1]
between precisely
n+1
extrema of equal magnitude. However, we are interested in the cases
l=1,3
where no such simple formulas seem to be available.

The key is to recall that
T_{n}
and
V_{n}
are also orthogonal polynomials. Both are special cases of the Jacobi polynomials
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
which have been studied in detail and are orthogonal with respect to
(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
on
[-1,1]
.
If we have a non-negative weight function
w
defined on
[-1,1]
, we will use the notation
{T_{n}^{w}}
to represent the minimiser of

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}w(x)|P_{n}(x)|,

where
P_{n}
ranges over all monic polynomials of degree
n
. This minimizer is uniquely defined for sufficiently large
n
under the conditions that
w
does not vanish at infinitely many points and
wP
is bounded for some polynomial
P
.
A naive comparison of the
L^{\infty}
and
L^{2}
norms suggests that
{T_{n}^{w}}
could be related to the monic orthogonal polynomials with respect to
w(x)^{2}/\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
on
[-1,1]
. In our setting, the weight is given by

w_{l}(x)=(x+1)^{l/4}

and we thus search for a relation between
{T_{n}^{w_{l}}}
and
P_{n}^{(-1/2,\,\beta_{l})}
for
\beta_{l}=(l-1)/2
.
In special cases (such as
l=0,2
) we have an exact match and the polynomials coincide. But even for general
l
, there are striking similarities between the polynomials. In
[
8
]
, Bernstein proved that
w_{l}P_{n}^{(-1/2,\,\beta_{l})}
is asymptotically alternating on
[-1,1]
as
n\to\infty
and because of that,
P_{n}^{(-1/2,\,\beta_{l})}
are excellent trial polynomials when studying the limit of
\|w_{l}T_{n}^{w_{l}}\|_{[-1,1]}
.

We shall formulate Bernstein’s full result below. As the proof is only available in French or Russian, we decided to include a detailed review of this method in the appendix.
While our presentation primarily adheres to Bernstein’s original proof, we are able to streamline some arguments by integrating findings from Achieser and Chebyshev alongside Bernstein’s work.

Theorem 5

(Bernstein
[
8
]
)
.

Suppose
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
b_{k}\in[-1,1]
for
k=0,1,\ldots,m
. Consider a weight function
w:[-1,1]\rightarrow[0,\infty)
of the form

(3.8)

w(x)=w_{0}(x)\prod_{k=0}^{m}|x-b_{k}|^{\alpha_{k}},

where
w_{0}
is Riemann integrable and satisfies
1/M\leq w_{0}(x)\leq M
for some constant
M\geq 1
. Then

(3.9)

\|wT_{n}^{w}\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

With Bernstein’s theorem at hand, we can now prove Theorem
2
. We isolate the following lemma since it will be used multiple times in the rest of the article.

Lemma 2
.

For any
z\in{\mathbb{C}}
, we have

(3.10)

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log|x-z|}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\log\frac{\left|z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\right|}{2},

where
z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}
maps
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus[-1,1]
conformally onto
{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\overline{{\mathbb{D}}}
. In particular, for
z\in[-1,1]
the integral is constantly equal to
-\log 2
.

Proof.

Recall that the Green’s function for
\overline{{\mathbb{C}}}\setminus[-1,1]
with pole at
\infty
is given by

G_{[-1,1]}(z)=\log\left|z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\right|.

Since the integral on the left-hand side of (
3.10
) is nothing but the potential for the equilibrium measure of
[-1,1]
, we conclude that

\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log|x-z|}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=G_{[-1,1]}(z)+\log\operatorname{Cap}\bigl([-1,1]\bigr)=\log\frac{\left|z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\right|}{2}

for
z\notin[-1,1]
. If instead
z\in[-1,1]
, we find by continuity of
G_{[-1,1]}
on
{\mathbb{C}}
and monotone convergence that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log|x-z|}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log|x-(z+i\epsilon)|}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx

\displaystyle=\lim_{\epsilon\downarrow 0}\log\frac{\left|(z+i\epsilon)+\sqrt{(z+i\epsilon)^{2}-1}\right|}{2}=\log\frac{\left|z+\sqrt{z^{2}-1}\right|}{2}=-\log 2.

This completes the proof.
∎

We are now in position to prove the limiting result for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
. As we shall see in Section
4
, the method of proof actually handles all cases in Theorem
2
.

Proof of Theorem
2
for
m=2
.

Let
w_{l}(x)=(x+1)^{{l}/{4}}
with
l=1,3
. Theorem
5
implies that

\|T_{n}^{w_{l}}w_{l}\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{l}{4\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log(x+1)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Applying Lemma
2
gives us that the the exponential on the right-hand side reduces to

\exp\left\{\frac{l}{4}\cdot\log\frac{1}{2}\right\}=2^{-{l}/{4}}.

By (
3.7
), we therefore find that

\|T_{4n+l}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{2}}=2^{n+{l}/{4}}\|T_{n}^{w_{l}}w_{l}\|_{[-1,1]}=2\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr),

which settles the claim for
{\mathsf{E}}_{2}
.
∎

3.2.
General quadratic preimages

We proceed by considering general quadratic preimages of
[-2,2]
and show that our method can be applied to this case as well. Of course, such preimages need not be connected and we should not expect the Widom factors to always converge to
2
. As in Theorem
3
, we shall consider sets of the form

(3.11)

{\mathsf{E}}_{P}=\bigl\{z:P(z)\in[-2,2]\bigr\},

where
P(z)=z^{2}+az+b
and
a,b\in\mathbb{C}
. Now, let us present a proof of this result.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

By (
1.7
), we have
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{P})=1
and thus

{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{P})=\|T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{P}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{P}}.

The first part of the theorem (i.e., (
1.19
) for even degree) is easily handled by (
3.1
). In order to prove (
1.20
), we note that

z^{2}+az+b=\left(z+\frac{a}{2}\right)^{2}+c,\quad c=b-a^{2}/4.

Therefore, it suffices to consider the case of

{\mathsf{E}}_{P_{c}}=\bigl\{z:z^{2}+c\in[-2,2]\bigr\}.

As
z\in{\mathsf{E}}_{P_{c}}
if and only if
-z\in{\mathsf{E}}_{P_{c}}
, we deduce that

T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{P_{c}}}(z)=z^{2n+1}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{k}z^{2k+1},\quad a_{k}\in{\mathbb{C}}.

Hence, since
|\sqrt{z}|=|z|^{1/2}
is single-valued, we can apply the change of variables
x=(z^{2}+c)/2
leading to

\|T_{2n+1}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{P_{c}}}\|_{{\mathsf{E}}_{P_{c}}}=2^{n+{1}/{2}}\min_{b_{0},b_{1},\ldots,b_{n-1}\in\mathbb{R}}\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\sqrt{|x-c/2|}\left|x^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}b_{k}x^{k}\right|.

Since the weight function
\sqrt{|x-c/2|}
is of the form (
3.8
) — in fact, we only need
w_{0}
for
c\notin[-2,2]
— Theorem
5
implies that

(3.12)

Lemma 3
.

For

and

is characterised by the following two properties:

•

Proof.

we see that

For

for some integer

To prove the alternating property, one argues in the same way as in Lemma

and we leave the details to the reader.

5.
Related orthogonal polynomials

We now turn our attention to the orthogonal polynomials with respect to the equilibrium measure of
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
. Recall that if

\mu
is a probability measure supported on the outer boundary of
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
, then
\mu=\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}
precisely when (
1.2
) holds.
With this result at hand, we can explicitly determine
\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}

(see also
[
32
]
).

Lemma 4
.

Let
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
be defined as in (
1.15
). The equilibrium measure of
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
is absolutely continuous with respect to arc length measure and given by

(5.1)

d\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}(z)=\frac{|z^{m-1}|}{\pi\sqrt{4-z^{2m}}}|dz|,\quad z\in{\mathsf{E}}_{m}.

Proof.

A straightforward computation shows that the measure defined in (
5.1
) is a probability measure on
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
.
We proceed to show that it satisfies (
1.2
).
Note that if
\zeta,z\in\mathbb{C}
then

\log|\zeta-z^{m}|=\sum\log|\zeta^{1/m}-z|,

where the sum is taken over all the
m
th roots of
\zeta
. Using this, we can compute the potential for the measure in question and arrive at

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}\log|x-z|\frac{|x^{m-1}|}{\sqrt{4-x^{2m}}}|dx|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{2m}\int_{0}^{2^{1/m}}\log|e^{i\pi k/m}x-z|\frac{x^{m-1}}{\sqrt{4-x^{2m}}}dx

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\pi}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\int_{-2}^{2}\frac{\log|e^{i\pi k/m}t^{1/m}-z|}{\sqrt{4-t^{2}}}dt

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{m\pi}\int_{-2}^{2}\frac{\log|t-z^{m}|}{\sqrt{4-t^{2}}}dt=\frac{1}{m}G_{[-2,2]}(z^{m}).

The last equality follows from the fact that
dt/(\pi\sqrt{4-t^{2}})
is the equilibrium measure of
[-2,2]
which is a set of logarithmic capacity one.
By Lemma
, we have

\frac{1}{m}G_{[-2,2]}(z^{m})=G_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}(z)

and consequently (
5.1
) holds true since also
\operatorname{Cap}({\mathsf{E}}_{m})=1
.
∎

As in Section
1.2
, we shall use the notation
P_{n}^{\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}}
for the monic orthogonal polynomials with respect to
\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}
. Recall that these polynomials minimise the integrals

\int_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}\left|P_{n}\right|^{2}d\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}

among all monic polynomials of degree
n
. Just as in Lemma
3
, one can use the symmetries of
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
to show that for
n,l\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
0\leq l<2m
, we have

(5.2)

P_{2nm+l}^{\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}}(z)=z^{2nm+l}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{2km+l}z^{2km+l},\quad a_{2km+l}\in\mathbb{R}.

We are now ready to prove Theorem
4
.

Proof of Theorem
4
.

Fix
m\geq 2
, let
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and suppose
0\leq l<2m
. By Lemma
4
and with
P_{2nm+l}^{\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}}
as in (
5.2
), we have

\displaystyle\|P_{2nm+l}^{\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}})}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}\left|z^{2nm+l}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{2km+l}z^{2km+l}\right|^{2}\frac{|z^{m-1}|}{\sqrt{4-z^{2m}}}|dz|

\displaystyle=\frac{2m}{\pi}\int_{[0,{2^{1/m}}]}\left|z^{2nm}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{2km+l}z^{2km}\right|^{2}\frac{|z^{m+2l-1}|}{\sqrt{4-z^{2m}}}|dz|

(5.3)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{4}\left(x^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}a_{2km+l}x^{k}\right)^{2}\frac{x^{l/m-1/2}}{\sqrt{4-x}}dx.

Realising that the above integral is minimised precisely for the Jacobi polynomials (suitably rescaled to
[0,4]
), we can compute it explicitly. Following Szegő
[
48
, Chapter IV]
, we adopt the notation

P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)={n+\alpha\choose n}{}_{2}F_{1}\left(-n,n+\alpha+\beta+1;\alpha+1;\frac{1-x}{2}\right)

and note that the leading coefficient of
P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}
is given by

2^{-n}{2n+\alpha+\beta\choose n}=\frac{\Gamma(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)}{2^{n}\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

The integral in (
5
) can thus be written as

(5.4)

\quad\frac{2^{4n+l/m}\Gamma(n+1)^{2}\Gamma(n+l/m)^{2}}{\pi\Gamma(2n+l/m)^{2}}\int_{-1}^{1}\left(P_{n}^{(-1/2,l/m-1/2)}(x)\right)^{2}(1-x)^{-{1}/{2}}(1+x)^{l/m-1/2}dt\\
=\frac{2^{4n+2l/m}\Gamma(n+{1}/{2})\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+{l}/{m})\Gamma(n+{l}/{m}+1/2)}{\pi(2n+{l}/{m})\Gamma(2n+l/m)^{2}}\quad

since

\int_{-1}^{1}\left(P_{n}^{(\alpha,\beta)}(x)\right)^{2}(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}dx=\frac{2^{\alpha+\beta+1}\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)\Gamma(n+\beta+1)}{(2n+\alpha+\beta+1)\Gamma(n+1)\Gamma(n+\alpha+\beta+1)}.

Applying Legendre’s duplication formula, the expression in (
5.4
) reduces to

\frac{2\Gamma(2n+1)\Gamma(2n+2l/m)}{(2n+l/m)\Gamma(2n+l/m)^{2}}

and recalling that
\Gamma(x+a)/\Gamma(x+b)\sim x^{a-b}
as
n\to\infty
, we deduce that

\|P_{2nm+l}^{\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}})}^{2}\to 2\;\mbox{ as }\;n\to\infty.

In order to prove the claimed monotonicity along subsequences, we introduce the quantity

\gamma_{n}(s):=\frac{2\Gamma(2n+1)\Gamma(2n+2s)}{(2n+s)\Gamma(2n+s)^{2}},\quad s\geq 0.

Note that
\|P_{2nm+l}^{\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}}}\|_{L^{2}(\mu_{{\mathsf{E}}_{m}})}^{2}=\gamma_{n}({l}/{m})
and

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma_{n+1}(s)}{\gamma_{n}(s)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Gamma(2n+3)\Gamma(2n+2s+2)(2n+s)\Gamma(2n+s)^{2}}{\Gamma(2n+1)\Gamma(2n+2s)(2n+s+2)\Gamma(2n+s+2)^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(2n+1)(2n+2)(2n+2s)(2n+2s+1)}{(2n+s)(2n+s+1)^{2}(2n+s+2)}.

Since

\frac{(x+1)(x+2s)}{(x+s)(x+s+1)}=\frac{x^{2}+2xs+x+2s}{x^{2}+2xs+x+s^{2}+s}

and

\frac{2s}{s^{2}+s}=\frac{2}{s+1}\gtrless 1\;\mbox{ for }\;s\lessgtr 1,

it follows that
\gamma_{n+1}(s)/\gamma_{n}(s)\gtrless 1
for
s\lessgtr 1
. In this way, we have obtained the desired monotonicity statement.
∎

7.
Outlook and perspective

A key finding of this paper has been to show that, beyond intervals, there exist compact subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
for which the Widom factors converge to
2
. Specifically, the sets
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
as defined in Theorem
2
and illustrated in Figures
3
–
3
exhibit this property when
m\geq 2
, and the same holds for any other symmetric star graph with an odd number of edges.
It is reasonable to inquire whether or not there are more sets
{\mathsf{E}}\subset{\mathbb{C}}
for which
{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}})\to 2
. As explained below, we believe the answer is indeed affirmative.

Recall that if
P
is a complex polynomial and
P^{\prime}(z)=0
, then
z
is called a critical point and
w=P(z)
a critical value for
P
. Polynomials with at most two critical values, say
c_{1}
and
c_{2}
, are known as
Shabat polynomials
(or generalised Chebyshev polynomials), see
[
10
,
41
]
. Since this class of polynomials is invariant under non-degenerate linear transformations, we may assume that
c_{1}=-2
and
c_{2}=2
.
It is a known fact (see, e.g.,
[
10
]
or
[
26
]
) that for Shabat polynomials, the preimage
P^{-1}([-2,2])
is a planar tree with as many edges as the degree of
P
. In fact, one can establish a bijection between the set of bicolored planer trees and the set of equivalence classes of Shabat polynomials. Moreover, with respect to the point at
\infty
, every edge of
P^{-1}([-2,2])
has equal harmonic measure and any subset of an edge has equal harmonic measure from both sides. Trees with these properties are also called “balanced trees” and they satisfy the
S
-property of Definition
1
. A remarkable result of Bishop
[
11
]
shows that balanced trees are dense in all planar continua (i.e., compact connected subsets of
{\mathbb{C}}
) with respect to the Hausdorff metric.

For
n\geq 2
, the polynomial
P_{n}(z)=z^{n}
has one critical point (namely,
z=0
) and one critical value
w=0
. So the preimage
P_{n}^{-1}([0,4])
is a balanced tree and when
n=2m
, this tree coincides with
{\mathsf{E}}_{m}
as defined in (
1.15
). Another example of a Shabat polynomial (with critical values
w=0,1
) is

P(z)=\frac{8}{729}(z+1)\Bigl(z^{2}-\frac{3}{2}z+\frac{9}{2}\Bigr)^{3}

and the corresponding tree is pictured in Figure
15
. By use of the algorithm mentioned in Section
3
, one can compute the norms of
T_{n}^{{\mathsf{E}}_{P}}
for degree up to
40

. The outcome is illustrated in Figure

Conjecture 1
.

Let
P
be a monic Shabat polynomial with critical values in
\{-2,2\}
and consider the preimage
{\mathsf{E}}_{P}:=P^{-1}([-2,2])
. Then

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathcal{W}}_{n,\infty}({\mathsf{E}}_{P})=2.

Since any balanced tree satisfies the
S
-property, one may speculate if this conjecture can be extended to a larger class of sets. In fact, if the property of every edge having equal harmonic measure turns out to not play a role, it would be natural to formulate the conjecture for so-called generalised Shabat polynomials. This class was introduced in
[
26
]
and allows for more than two critical values as long as they all belong to

. The ideal scenario would be that the conjecture holds true for all connected sets characterised by the
S
-property.

Regrettably, our current method of proof does not exhibit a clear path for generalisation within the context of Shabat polynomials and the
S
-property. Novel strategies and ideas are required to advance our understanding in this domain.

Appendix A
Bernstein’s method

In this appendix we shall discuss a proof of Theorem
5
. Our approach will be a simplification of the method employed by Bernstein in
[
8
]
. The initial stage of
[
8
]
involved showing that if
w
is a polynomial which is strictly positive on
[-1,1]
, then the monic orthogonal polynomials
P_{n}^{w}
with respect to the weight function

{w(x)}/{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}

are well-suited trial polynomials for minimisation of the quantity

\max_{x\in[-1,1]}\left|\sqrt{w(x)}\left(x^{n}+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}c_{k}x^{k}\right)\right|.

As in Section
3
, we shall denote these minimisers by
T_{n}^{\sqrt{w}}
. More specifically, Bernstein proved that
\sqrt{w}P_{n}^{w}
is asymptotically alternating for this class of polynomial weights and an explicit analysis of
P_{n}^{w}
can then be used to unveil the asymptotic behaviour of
\|\sqrt{w}T_{n}^{\sqrt{w}}\|_{[-1,1]}
as stated in Theorem
5
.

Rather than using orthogonal polynomials as trial polynomials, we shall base our analysis on an explicit expression for certain weighted Chebyshev polynomials due to Achieser
[
2
, Appendix A]
. This formula is presented in Section
A.1
. Since the class of weights includes the reciprocal of any strictly positive polynomial on
[-1,1]
, we can — in line with Bernstein — apply a standard approximation argument to conclude that Theorem
5
holds true for all Riemann integrable weights on
[-1,1]
which are bounded above and below by positive constants. This is the second step in the proof and we outline the details in Section
A.2
.
The final and most complicated step is to allow for zeros of the weight function on
[-1,1]
. We shall present exactly the same refined argument as provided by Bernstein
[
8
]
in Section
A.3
.

A.1.
An explicit formula for certain weighted Chebyshev polynomials

Denote by
\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}:={\mathbb{R}}\cup\{\infty\}
the extended real line.
Let
a_{k}\in\overline{{\mathbb{R}}}\setminus[-1,1]
and form the product

w(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\left(1-\frac{x}{a_{k}}\right)^{-1/2},

which represents a positive weight function on
[-1,1]
. The case of an odd number of factors can be handled by taking
a_{2m}=\infty
and
|a_{k}|<\infty
for
k=1,2,\ldots,2m-1
.
Let
z\in\partial{\mathbb{D}}
and
\rho_{k}\in{\mathbb{D}}
be defined implicitly by

x=\frac{1}{2}\left(z+\frac{1}{z}\right)\;\mbox{ and }\;a_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\rho_{k}+\frac{1}{\rho_{k}}\right)\;\mbox{ for }\;k=1,2,\ldots,2m.

The following explicit representation for
wT_{n}^{w}
is given by Achieser
[
2
, Appendix A]
.

Theorem A.1
.

For
n>m
, we have

(A.1)

w(x)T_{n}^{w}(x)=2^{-n}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\sqrt{1+\rho_{k}^{2}}\left(z^{m-n}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\sqrt{\frac{1-z\rho_{k}}{z-\rho_{k}}}+z^{n-m}\prod_{k=1}^{2m}\sqrt{\frac{z-\rho_{k}}{1-z\rho_{k}}}\,\right)

and

(A.2)

\|wT_{n}^{w}\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}.

Remark
.

One can show that the expression on the right-hand side of (
A.1
) indeed becomes a polynomial (in
x
) after division by
w(x)
. Moreover, this polynomial satisfies the orthogonality conditions

\int_{-1}^{1}T_{n}^{w}(x)x^{k}\frac{w(x)^{2}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=0,\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n-1

when
n\geq m
.

By letting
a_{2k}=a_{2k-1}
for
k=1,2,\ldots,m
, we see that Theorem
A.1
in particular applies to weights of the form

w(x)=\prod_{k=1}^{m}\left(1-\frac{x}{a_{k}}\right)^{-1}.

This was in fact already proven by Chebyshev
[
13
]
.

A.2.
Norm asymptotics for more general weighted Chebyshev polynomials

We now consider generalising (
A.2
) to the case where
w
is merely a Riemann integrable function on
[-1,1]
satisfying

(A.3)

\frac{1}{M}\leq w(x)\leq M

for some
M\geq 1
. In this more general setting, we no longer have equality. But the two expressions in (
A.2
) are still asymptotically equivalent as
n\to\infty
.

The idea is to approximate
w
by reciprocals of polynomials. Set
\omega=1/w

and choose sequences
\{\omega_{u}^{k}\}_{k}
and
\{\omega_{l}^{k}\}_{k}
of polynomials with real zeros away from
[-1,1]
such that

\frac{1}{2M}\leq\omega_{u}^{k}(x)\leq\omega(x)\leq\omega_{l}^{k}(x)\leq 2M

and

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\omega_{u}^{k}(x)=\omega(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\omega_{l}^{k}(x)

for every
x\in[-1,1]
. Defining
w_{l}^{k}=1/\omega_{l}^{k}
and
w_{u}^{k}=1/\omega_{u}^{k}
, we obtain that

w_{l}^{k}(x)\leq w(x)\leq w_{u}^{k}(x)

while maintaining the pointwise convergence

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}w_{l}^{k}(x)=w(x)=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}w_{u}^{k}(x).

This in turn implies that

(A.4)

\|T_{n}^{w_{l}}w_{l}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|T_{n}^{w}w_{l}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|T_{n}^{w_{u}}w\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|T_{n}^{w_{u}}w_{u}\|_{[-1,1]}.

Furthermore, using dominated convergence we get that

\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{l}^{k}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{u}^{k}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx

and for any given
\epsilon>0
, we can therefore choose
k
large enough that

\displaystyle\left|\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{i}^{k}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}-\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\right|

\displaystyle<\epsilon,\quad i=l,u.

For
n
sufficiently large, Theorem
A.1
implies that

\displaystyle\|T_{n}^{w_{i}^{k}}w_{i}^{k}\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{i}^{k}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\},\quad i=l,u.

By combining this with (
A.4
), we thus get the inequalities

\displaystyle\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}-\epsilon\leq 2^{n-1}\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}+\epsilon.

As
\epsilon>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

(A.5)

\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. This proves Theorem
5
when
w
is Riemann integrable and satisfies (
A.3
).

A.3.
Allowing for zeros of the weight function

The final step in proving Theorem
5
is to allow for zeros at certain points of
[-1,1]
. We will adopt the approach outlined in
[
8
]
and restrict our examination to the introduction of a single zero
b_{1}=b
within the interval
[-1,1]
using the weight
|x-b|^{\alpha}
. That is, we consider weights of the form

(A.6)

w(x)=w_{0}(x)|x-b|^{\alpha},

where
w_{0}
is Riemann integrable and satisfies
1/M\leq w_{0}(x)\leq M
for some constant
M\geq 1
. More zeros can be added by repeated use of the argument we are about to explain.

Assume first that
\alpha=m
is a positive integer. For
{\epsilon}>0
, consider the weight function

w^{\epsilon}(x)=w_{0}(x)|x-b-i{\epsilon}|^{m}.

This weight is non-vanishing on
[-1,1]
and fulfils all the previous requirements for (
A.5
) to be valid. By use of Lemma
2
, we hence find that

\displaystyle\|T_{n}^{w^{\epsilon}}w^{\epsilon}\|_{[-1,1]}

\displaystyle=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{0}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log|x-b-i{\epsilon}|^{m}}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

(A.7)

\displaystyle=\|T_{n}^{w_{0}}w_{0}\|_{[-1,1]}\Biggl(\frac{\left|(b+i{\epsilon})+\sqrt{(b+i{\epsilon})^{2}-1}\right|}{2}\Biggr)^{m}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\to\infty
. Note that the second factor in (
A.7
)
converges to
2^{-m}
as
{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0
. Since
w^{\epsilon}>w
on
[-1,1]
, we deduce as in (
A.4
) that

(A.8)

\displaystyle\begin{split}\|T_{n}^{w^{\epsilon}}w^{\epsilon}\|_{[-1,1]}&\geq\|T_{n}^{w^{\epsilon}}w\|_{[-1,1]}\geq\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}\\
&\geq\|T_{n+m}^{w_{0}}w_{0}\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{-m}\|T_{n}^{w_{0}}w_{0}\|_{[-1,1]}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr).\end{split}

The combination of (
A.7
) and (
A.8
) now implies that

\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{-m}\|T_{n}^{w_{0}}w_{0}\|_{[-1,1]}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\to\infty
, and this proves that (
A.5
) is valid for weights of the form (
A.6
) with
\alpha=m\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

We next consider the case of negative integer powers. If
\alpha=-m
is a negative integer, then
T_{n+m}^{w}
has a zero of order
m
at
x=b
and hence

T_{n+m}^{w}(x)w(x)=T_{n}^{w_{0}}(x)w_{0}(x)\frac{(x-b)^{m}}{|x-b|^{m}}.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\|T_{n+m}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}

\displaystyle=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{0}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

\displaystyle=2^{1-n-m}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\to\infty
, and this proves the validity of (
A.5
) for such weights as well.

To handle arbitrary exponents
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
, it suffices to examine the case of
\alpha\in(0,1)
. The weight function
w_{0}
can namely incorporate zeros with integer exponents on
[-1,1]
as we have already addressed the asymptotics in this particular case. Let
{\epsilon}>0
and form the weight functions

\displaystyle w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}w(x),&|x-b|\geq{\epsilon}\\
w_{0}(x)|x-b|,&|x-b|<{\epsilon},\end{cases}

\displaystyle w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}(x)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}w(x),&|x-b|\geq{\epsilon}\\
w_{0}(x),&|x-b|<{\epsilon}.\end{cases}

Note that (
A.5
) applies to both
w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}
and
w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}
. Regardless of the value of
0<{\epsilon}<1
, we have the inequalities

w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}(x)\leq w(x)\leq w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}(x)

and hence

\|T_{n}^{w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}}w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}\leq\|T_{n}^{w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}}w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}\|_{[-1,1]}.

Consequently,

\qquad 2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\\
\leq\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}\leq 2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\qquad

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. By dominated convergence,

\lim_{{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx=\lim_{{\epsilon}\rightarrow 0}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx,

so for any given
r>0
, we can choose
{\epsilon}>0
small enough that

1-r\leq\frac{\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{l}^{{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}}{\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}}\leq 1,\qquad 1\leq\frac{\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w_{u}^{{\epsilon}}(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}}{\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}}\leq 1+r.

This in turn implies that

\qquad 2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}(1-r)\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\\
\leq\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}\leq 2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}(1+r)\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)\qquad

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. Since
r>0
is arbitrary, we conclude that

\|T_{n}^{w}w\|_{[-1,1]}=2^{1-n}\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{\log w(x)}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}dx\right\}\bigl(1+o(1)\bigr)

as
n\rightarrow\infty
. In other words, (
A.5
) — which coincides with (
3.9
) — applies. The addition of more weights of the form
|x-b_{k}|^{\alpha_{k}}
can be carried out by repeated use of the argument explained above.
