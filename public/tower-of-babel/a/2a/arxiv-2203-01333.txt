Liouvillian skin effect in an exactly solvable model
Fan Yang, Qing-Dong Jiang, Emil J. Bergholtz

Abstract
The interplay between dissipation, topology and sensitivity to boundary conditions has recently attracted tremendous amounts of attention at the level of effective non-Hermitian descriptions. Here we exactly solve a quantum mechanical Lindblad master equation describing a dissipative topological Su-Schrieffer-Heeger (SSH) chain of fermions for both open boundary condition (OBC) and periodic boundary condition (PBC). We find that the extreme sensitivity on the boundary conditions associated with the non-Hermitian skin effect is directly reflected in the rapidities governing the time evolution of the density matrix giving rise to a Liouvillian skin effect . This leads to several intriguing phenomena including boundary sensitive damping behavior, steady state currents in finite periodic systems, and diverging relaxation times in the limit of large systems. We illuminate how the role of topology in these systems differs in the effective non-Hermitian Hamiltonian limit and the full master equation framework.

II.1
Liouvillians of non-interacting fermions

Before addressing the specifics of our model, we give a brief review of this general
third quantization
approach
through which the quadratic Lindbladians are diagonalized in the canonical basis of Majorana fermions
[
68
,
69
,
70
]
.

Starting with
n
complex fermions, the reduced density matrix of the system
\rho=\sum_{jk}\rho_{jk}|a_{j}\rangle\langle a_{k}|
lives in a Hilbert space of dimension
2^{n}\times 2^{n}
.
One can always construct a pair of Majorana fermions out of one complex fermion, for instance,
\omega_{j}=a_{l}^{\dagger}+a_{l}
and
\omega_{k}=-i(a_{l}^{\dagger}-a_{l})
such that they become their own anti-particles
\omega_{j}^{\dagger}=\omega_{j}
and satisfy anti-commutation relations
\{\omega_{j},\omega_{k}\}=2\delta_{j,k}
.
The Hamiltonian and the dissipators take the matrix form depending on the details of the mapping:

\mathcal{H}=\sum_{j,k}w_{j}H_{j,k}w_{k}
,

L^{\nu}=\sum_{j}l_{j}^{\nu}w_{j}
.

The Hilbert space is now represented in the
2^{2n}
-dimensional Liouville space
\mathcal{K}

expanded by the new set of Majorana operators:
P_{\underline{\alpha}}=w_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}w_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}\cdots w_{2n}^{\alpha_{2n}}
with
\alpha_{j}\in\{0,1\}
.
We apply the notation
\underline{x}=(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})^{T}
to represent a vector of scalars or operators.
Over the space
\mathcal{K}
,
it is convenient to define the adjoint creation and annihilation linear maps through
\varphi
fermions:
\varphi_{j}|P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha_{j},1}|w_{j}P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle
,
\varphi_{j}^{\dagger}|P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle=\delta_{\alpha_{j},0}|w_{j}P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle
, which obey
\{\varphi_{j},\varphi_{k}^{\dagger}\}=\delta_{j,k}
. One verifies that

|w_{j}P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle=(\varphi_{j}^{\dagger}+\varphi_{j})|P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle
,

|P_{\underline{\alpha}}w_{j}\rangle=\mathcal{P}_{F}(\varphi_{j}^{\dagger}-\varphi_{j})|P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle
.
The operator
\mathcal{P}_{F}=(-1)^{\hat{\mathcal{N}}}
denotes the Fermi parity associated with the fermion number
\hat{\mathcal{N}}=\sum_{j=1}^{2n}\varphi_{j}^{\dagger}\varphi_{j}=\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\cdot\underline{\varphi}
.
Since
[\hat{\mathcal{L}},\mathcal{P}_{F}]=0
and
(\mathcal{P}_{F})^{2}=1
, the parity is conserved and takes the value
\pm 1
.

In the canonical basis
|P_{\underline{\alpha}}\rangle
, the Liouvillian in
Equation

2.1
contains the even (
+
) and odd (
-
) parity sectors:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}=

\displaystyle-4i\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\cdot H\underline{\varphi}

\displaystyle+\frac{(1+\mathcal{P}_{F})}{2}\left[-\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\cdot(M+M^{T})\underline{\varphi}+\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\cdot(M-M^{T})\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{(1-\mathcal{P}_{F})}{2}\left[-\underline{\varphi}\cdot(M+M^{T})\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}+\underline{\varphi}\cdot(M-M^{T})\underline{\varphi}\right].

(2.2)

Here, the matrix
M=\sum_{\nu}M^{\nu}
arises from the loss and gain dissipators:
M^{\nu}=(l^{\nu})^{T}(l^{\nu})^{*}
and
\nu=g,l
.

Since a physical observable must contain an even number of fermionic operators, one may focus on only a definite parity sector, e.g., the even parity sector (
\mathcal{P}_{F}=1
) in this paper.
Correspondingly, the dynamics of the system is governed by

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{+}=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\cdot&\underline{\varphi}\cdot\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-X^{\dagger}&iY\\
0&X\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\varphi}\\
\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\end{pmatrix}-A_{0},

(2.3)

with
X=-4iH+M+M^{T}
,
Y=-2i(M-M^{T})
, and
A_{0}=\frac{1}{2}\text{Tr}[X]
.
After proper diagonalization,
one is able to express the Liouvillian in terms of
rapidities

\beta_{m}
and
normal master modes
(NMMs)
b^{\prime}_{m}
,
b_{m}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{+}=-\sum_{m=1}^{2n}\beta_{m}b^{\prime}_{m}b_{m},

(2.4)

with the band index
m
and NMMs satisfying the anti-commutation relations
\{b^{\prime}_{m},b_{l}\}=\delta_{m,l}
.

Notably, the complex rapidity spectrum contains rich physics. An initial state
\rho_{0}
of positive fermion parity approaches the NESS after a long-time evolution:
\rho_{\text{ss}}=\left.e^{\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{+}t}\rho_{0}\right|_{t\to\infty}
.
While the imaginary part of the rapidity
\beta_{m}
encodes the phase oscillation frequency, the real part of
\beta_{m}
reveals the relaxation speed of the system to the steady state and
\text{Re}({\beta_{m}})\geq 0
is required naturally. To describe the asymptotic decay rate quantitively, a spectral Liouvillian gap
[
74
,
66
]
can be defined as

\displaystyle\Delta=2\min\{\text{Re}[\beta_{m}]\}.

(2.5)

Extra insight comes from the upper triangular structure of
\hat{\mathcal{L}}_{+}
. It infers that the rapidity spectrum must coincide with the eigenvalues of the matrix
X
in the diagonal block, which is NH and also called
the damping matrix
. The off-diagonal block
Y

[
71
,
72
]
.

II.2.3
Exactly solvable NMMs

We are now prepared to get an analytical set of NMMs for the Liouvillian in
Equation

2.3
, in particular, under the open boundary condition.
The essence is to remove in the upper triangular structure the off-diagonal block

that entangles adjoint fermions
\varphi_{c}
and
\varphi_{d}

More precisely,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}-X^{\dagger}&iY\\
0&X\end{pmatrix}=W\begin{pmatrix}-X^{\dagger}&0\\
0&X\end{pmatrix}W^{-1}.

(2.26)

We find a solution for the transformation above

\displaystyle W=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbb{1}_{2n\times 2n}&C\\
0&\mathbb{1}_{2n\times 2n}\end{pmatrix},

(2.27)

where the covariance matrix
C

\displaystyle X^{\dagger}C+CX=iY.

(2.28)

It is easy to discern that if
{\eta}/\gamma={\eta_{1}}/{\gamma_{1}}
or
{\eta_{2}}/{\gamma_{2}}
,
the covariance matrix holds a simple structure

\displaystyle C=\frac{i\eta}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}\\
-\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.29)

By definition in Eqs. (
2.12
), the solvable limit encompasses the following possibilities:

\displaystyle\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}=0,\quad\frac{\gamma_{1}}{\eta_{1}}=\frac{\gamma_{2}}{\eta_{2}}\neq 0\quad\text{and}\quad\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=0.

(2.30)

A deeper understanding of the covariance matrix comes from the pairing function of Majorana fermions:

{C}_{jk}(t)=-\text{Tr}[w_{j}w_{k}\rho(t)]+\delta_{jk}
. In its time evolution, by applying the Lindblad
Equation

2.1

\displaystyle\partial_{t}{C}(t)=-{C}(t)X-X^{\dagger}{C}(t)+iY.

(2.31)

For a steady state,
\partial_{t}{C}_{s}=0
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle C_{s}\equiv C.

(2.32)

It can be interpreted that in the solvable limit of the Lindbladian in Eqs. (
2.30
), the covariance matrix encodes a stationary pairing pattern favoured by Majorana fermions at large times:

\displaystyle\langle c_{j,\alpha}d_{j^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle_{\text{ss}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{i\eta}{\gamma}\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}},

\displaystyle\langle c_{j,\alpha}c_{j^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle_{\text{ss}}

\displaystyle=\langle d_{j,\alpha}d_{j^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime}}\rangle_{\text{ss}}=\delta_{j,j^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}.

(2.33)

A non-vanishing covariance matrix plays a role in counteracting the effect of the imbalanced gain and loss dissipations (
\eta
). Back to the original Hilbert space expanded by spinless fermions

,
a more physical picture can be drawn as follows. Since
n_{j,\alpha}=a_{j,\alpha}^{\dagger}a_{j,\alpha}=(1-ic_{j,\alpha}d_{j,\alpha})/2
, the dissipative fermionic SSH chain has a tendency to evolve towards a uniformly distributed
configuration with an occupation number:

\displaystyle n_{\text{ss},(j,\alpha)}=\frac{\gamma-\eta}{2\gamma}\in[0,1].

(2.34)

There is no correlation between

fermions. In general,
one can express the steady state in the form of a product state,

\displaystyle\left|\Psi_{\text{ss}}\right>=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n_{\text{tot}}}\left(\sqrt{\frac{\gamma+\eta}{2\gamma}}\left|0\right>_{j}+e^{i\theta_{j}}\sqrt{\frac{\gamma-\eta}{2\gamma}}\left|1\right>_{j}\right),

(2.35)

where

In the end, with a covariance matrix fulfilling the transformation in

2.26

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ b^{\prime}_{c,m}=\underline{\psi}_{Lm}^{*}\cdot\underline{\varphi}_{c}^{\dagger}\\

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\ b^{\prime}_{d,m}=\underline{\psi}_{Lm}^{*}\cdot\underline{\varphi}_{d}^{\dagger}\\

The anticommutation relations are guaranteed by the biorthonormality in Eqs. (

\{b^{\prime}_{c,m},b_{c,l}\}=\{b^{\prime}_{d,m},b_{d,l}\}=\delta_{m,l}

In the same manner, under PBC, the covariance matrix takes a simple analytical form in the solvable limit in Eqs. (

\displaystyle C(q)=\frac{i\eta}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}_{2\times 2}\\

(2.37)

and the set of NMMs associated with rapidities

II.3
Spectrum and topology

In this section, we reveal the topology of the Liouvillian starting from the spectral winding of the NH damping matrix.
Known for a NH system, the conventional bulk-boundary correspondence is broken. Yet the prevalence of the exceptional points (EPs) in the OBC rapidity spectrum
implies the Liouvillian skin effect, of which more physical consequences will be discussed in
Section

IV
.
We further analyze the formation of the Liouvillian gap that is found to be highly sensitive to the boundary conditions.

II.3.1
Spectral winding number and exceptional topology

Based on different classification schemes
[
73
,
79
]
, open fermion matter falls into one of the ten NH Bernard-LeClair symmetry classes.
Given a quadratic Liouvillian
[
73
]
, the classification can be defined through the damping matrix
X
, or, in our case,
X_{c(d)}
:

\displaystyle Z

\displaystyle=-iX_{c(d)},

\displaystyle=-i(\gamma_{1}+\gamma_{2})\cdot\mathbb{1}-[i\gamma_{1}+t_{2}\sin q-i\gamma_{2}\cos q]\cdot\sigma^{x}

\displaystyle\phantom{=}+[(t_{1}+t_{2}\cos q)+i\gamma_{2}\sin q]\cdot\sigma^{y}.

(2.39)

The matrix
Z
resembling the Hamiltonian in the closed limit preserves the time-reversal, particle-hole, and pseudo-anti-Hermiticity (PAH, or generalized chiral) symmetries:

Z=\sigma^{x}Z^{T}\sigma^{x}
,
Z=-Z^{*}
, and
Z=-\sigma^{x}Z^{\dagger}\sigma^{x}
.
Hence, each subspace of the damping matrix belongs to class BDI with a
\mathbb{Z}
classification in 1D.
It should be noted that at the edge, two real Majorana fermions
c
and
d
recombine into one complex fermion
a
as indicated by
Equation

2.9
. When the dissipations are turned on, this edge mode shares a finite lifetime with a contribution coming from the effective Liouvillian gap (non-vanishing as indicated by the purely imaginary total energy shift in
Z
) and another from the non-Hermiticity of the damping matrix [see
Equation

4.9
].

In the presence of the PAH symmetry, a
\mathbb{Z}
classification is captured by the topological invariant, spectral winding number. By shifting the reference point to
(0,-i\gamma)
in the complex plane, it is
equivalent to evaluate the winding of the Bloch Hamiltonian
H_{\text{S}}(q)

[
2
,
3
]
:

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}dq\ \partial_{q}\ln\{\det[H_{\text{S}}(q)]\}.

(2.40)

Remarkably, when the spectral winding number becomes non-trivial, the left and right boundary modes in the OBC spectrum in
Equation

A9
start to localize at different ends of the chain:

\displaystyle|\nu|=1,\quad\text{sgn}[\ln(|r_{L}|)]\neq\text{sgn}[\ln(|r_{R}|)].

(2.41)

For
\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2}\geq 0
, the above topological regime resides in

\displaystyle t_{1}t_{2}>0,\quad\begin{cases}\left||t_{1}|-|t_{2}|\right|<|\gamma_{1}|+|\gamma_{2}|\\
||\gamma_{1}|-|\gamma_{2}||<|t_{1}|+|t_{2}|,\end{cases}

\displaystyle t_{1}t_{2}<0,\quad\begin{cases}\left||t_{1}|-|t_{2}|\right|<||\gamma_{1}|-|\gamma_{2}||\\
|\gamma_{1}|+|\gamma_{2}|<|t_{1}|+|t_{2}|,\end{cases}

\displaystyle\phantom{t_{1}t_{2}<0\ }\text{or}\ \begin{cases}\left||t_{1}|-|t_{2}|\right|>||\gamma_{1}|-|\gamma_{2}||\\
|\gamma_{1}|+|\gamma_{2}|>|t_{1}|+|t_{2}|.\end{cases}

(2.42)

In the framework of our model, the bond dissipators entail
\gamma_{1}\geq 0,\gamma_{2}\geq 0
.

In a closed NH system, the concept of conventional

Apparently, the imaginary part of complex energy

II.4
Correlation function and comparison with the effective Hamiltonian

In this section, we derive a closed form of the single-particle correlation function from the exact eigenmodes of the damping matrix. The rapidity spectrum
determines the time-dependent part of the two-point correlator.
Any observable consisting of even number fermionic operators can then be constructed by Wick’s theorem. From the perspective of the correlation function,
we compare the physics of the effective Hamiltonian that neglects Lindblad quantum jump operators with the picture of the full Liouvillian. Notably, when the bonds are
subjected to purely loss dissipations, the two mechanisms become identical.

II.4.1
Single-particle correlator

Let us start by resolving the time-dependent pairing function for Majorana fermions. In the equation of motion in
Equation

2.31
,
the constant matrix
Y
can be replaced by the covariance (or steady state) matrix from
Equation

2.28
. Through a change of variable

\tilde{C}(t)=C(t)-C_{\text{ss}}
, one reaches

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{C}(t)=-\tilde{C}(t)X-X^{\dagger}\tilde{C}(t).

(2.49)

Starting from an arbitrary initial configuration that is not trivial
\tilde{C}(0)\neq 0
, we can integrate the above equation and implement the diagonalized damping matrix in the exponential:

\displaystyle X=\sum_{m}\sum_{\mu=c,d}\beta_{m}|\Theta^{\mu}_{Rm}\rangle\langle\Theta^{\mu}_{Lm}|,

(2.50)

\displaystyle|\Theta^{c}_{R(L)m}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\underline{\psi}_{R(L)m}\\
0\end{pmatrix},\quad|\Theta^{d}_{R(L)m}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
\underline{\psi}_{R(L)m}\end{pmatrix}.

Taking into account the biorthogonality of the basis and the fact
that the damping matrix is real,
X^{*}=X
,
we arrive at

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(t)=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{\mu,\mu^{\prime}}e^{-(\beta_{m}+\beta_{m^{\prime}})t}|\Theta^{\mu^{\prime}*}_{Lm^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\Theta^{\mu^{\prime}*}_{Rm^{\prime}}|\tilde{C}(0)|\Theta^{\mu}_{Rm}\rangle\langle\Theta^{\mu}_{Lm}|.

(2.51)

At
t=0
, without loss of generality, throughout the text we choose the system to be in a static configuration with each site completely filled:
|\Psi_{0}\rangle=\bigotimes_{j=1}^{n_{\text{tot}}}|1\rangle_{j}
, which corresponds to

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(0)=-\frac{i(\gamma+\eta)}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}\\
-\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(2.52)

Apparently,
\tilde{C}(0)
selects
\mu\neq\mu^{\prime}
. With no pairing between Majorana fermions of the same species in the initial state,

\langle c_{j}c_{k}\rangle_{t}=\langle d_{j}d_{k}\rangle_{t}=0
.

Next, we go back to the physical space and define the single-particle correlation function in the spinless fermion language:

Q_{jk}(t)=\text{Tr}[a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{k}\rho(t)]
. After the mapping to Majorana fermions in
Equation

2.9
,
one rewrites it in terms of the pairing function,

\displaystyle Q_{jk}(t)=\frac{i}{4}\sigma(j,k)\left[C_{j,k+n}(t)+C_{k,j+n}(t)\right],

(2.53)

with
n
the total number of sites. The phase factor depends on whether the correlation resides on the same sublattice or not:

\displaystyle\sigma(j,k)=\begin{cases}1,&j+k=\text{even}\\
(-1)^{j}\cdot(-i),&j+k=\text{odd}.\end{cases}

(2.54)

Combined with
Equations

2.51
and
2.52
, the single-particle correlator takes the explicit form in terms of the exact solutions of the damping matrix in Eqs. (
2.21
):

\displaystyle Q_{jk}(t)=\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{2\gamma}\right)\sigma(j,k)\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\text{tot}}}e^{-(\beta_{m}+\beta_{m^{\prime}})t}

\displaystyle\phantom{===}\psi_{Lm}^{*}(j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(k)\cdot\psi_{Rm}(l)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}(l).

(2.55)

II.4.2
Effective Hamiltonian in the absence of quantum jumps

For non-Gaussian Lindbladians, on the other hand, it is useful to study the short-time dynamics by ignoring quantum jumps in the Lindblad equation
[
55
]
:
\sum_{\mu}L_{\mu}\rho L_{\mu}^{\dagger}
.
Here, we compare the effective Hamiltonian description with the full Lindblad master equation framework.

Without quantum jumps, the time evolution of the density can be described by

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho=-i(\mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}}\rho-\rho\mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}}^{\dagger}),

(2.56)

where
H_{\text{eff}}=H-\frac{i}{2}\sum_{\mu}L_{\mu}^{\dagger}L_{\mu}
.
It turns out that the structure of the effective Hamiltonian becomes drastically different from the damping matrix
[
60
]
.
We verify that rather than the total strength
\gamma
, the non-Hermiticity of
\mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}}
is related to the imbalance
\eta
between loss and gain dissipations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}}=

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N-1}(t_{1}-\eta_{1})a_{j,A}^{\dagger}a_{j,B}+(t_{1}+\eta_{1})a_{j,B}^{\dagger}a_{j,A}

\displaystyle+(t_{2}-\eta_{2})a_{j,B}^{\dagger}a_{j+1,A}+(t_{2}+\eta_{2})a_{j+1,A}^{\dagger}a_{j,B}

\displaystyle-i\eta\sum_{(j,\alpha)}a_{(j,\alpha)}^{\dagger}a_{(j,\alpha)}-is_{0}.

(2.57)

The purely imaginary energy shift scales with the size of the system:
s_{0}=[(\gamma-\eta)/2]\cdot n_{\text{tot}}=\sum_{i=1,2}(|\gamma_{i}^{g}|/2)\cdot n_{\text{tot}}
.
It is convenient to resolve the single-particle correlator directly from
\mathcal{H}_{\text{eff}}
according to the equation of motion in
Equation

2.56
,

\displaystyle Q_{jk,\text{eff}}(t)=\sigma(j,k)\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\text{tot}}}e^{-(\beta_{m,\text{eff}}+\beta_{m^{\prime},\text{eff}})t}

\displaystyle\phantom{===}\psi_{Lm}^{\eta*}(j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{\eta*}(k)\cdot\psi_{Rm}^{\eta}(l)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}^{\eta}(l),

(2.58)

with an effective rapidity spectrum:

\displaystyle\beta_{m,\text{eff}}=\eta+iE_{m}(\eta)+\left(\frac{\gamma-\eta}{2}\right)\cdot n_{\text{tot}}.

(2.59)

To work with the same basis as the damping matrix, we have applied the transformation in Eqs. (
2.20
). In the rapidity spectrum,
s_{0}
prevents the Liouvillian gap from turning negative when
\eta=\sum_{i=1,2}(|\gamma_{i}^{l}|-|\gamma_{i}^{g}|)/2<0
.

By neglecting the quantum jumps in the Lindblad equation, we find that the dynamics of the dissipative system are not properly captured by the effective Hamiltonian at all times. For the short-time interval, regardless of the total dissipation strength
\gamma
, the imbalance
\eta
determines the Lindblad spectrum and makes the Liouvillian gap increase with the system size rather than stay a constant value as suggested by
Equation

2.16
.
In the long-time limit, the EPs of the exact solutions move to
t_{i}=\pm\eta_{i}
, leaving the Liouvillian skin effect unpredictable compared with
Equation

2.44
. Ultimately, the system always decays to an empty chain as there is no residual matrix
Y
in the equation of motion that can add up to a finite stationary occupation of fermions according to Eqs. (
2.31
)
-
(
2.35
).

In spite of all the discrepancies, however, once gain dissipators are suppressed on the bonds
\gamma_{1}^{g}=\gamma_{2}^{g}=0
, we reach one special point
where

\displaystyle\gamma=\eta,\quad Q_{jk,\text{eff}}(t)=Q_{jk}(t).

(2.60)

It infers that for the SSH chain with bond dissipations that only lead to losses, though truncated, the effective Hamiltonian encapsulates the full dynamics at arbitrary times.

Open boundary: Uniqueness of NESS

First, we look at the steady state from the OBC rapidity spectrum. As shown in

Moreover, taking into account

In particular, at
\gamma_{2}=0
,

\displaystyle\Delta^{\text{OBC}}=\begin{cases}\ 2\gamma_{1}-2\sqrt{\gamma_{1}^{2}-t_{1}^{2}},&|t_{1}|<\gamma_{1}\\
\ 2\gamma_{1},&|t_{1}|\geq\gamma_{1}.\end{cases}

m=(\nu,q)\in\{(+,0),(-,\pi)\}

III.2
Periodic boundary: Degeneracy, quasi-NESS, and stationary current

From
Section

II.3
, the Liouvillian gap can be closed by switching the boundary condition to PBC, thus lifting the degeneracy of NESS.
For non-zero
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
,
Figure

4
shows the real part of the PBC rapidity spectrum holds a gapless point at
t_{1}=t_{2}

It is easy to check that the rapidity of the bulk mode
m^{*}=(+,-\pi)
vanishes completely:
\beta_{m^{*}}=0
.
Therefore,
the right set of the steady states becomes
three-fold
degenerate:
s_{R,0}=|\text{NESS}\rangle
,
s_{R,c}=b^{\prime}_{c,m^{*}}|\text{NESS}\rangle
,
s_{R,d}=b^{\prime}_{d,m^{*}}|\text{NESS}\rangle
(accordingly, the left set
is expanded by
\langle\text{NESS}^{\prime}|,\langle\text{NESS}^{\prime}|b_{c,m^{*}}
and
\langle\text{NESS}^{\prime}|b_{d,m^{*}}
).
In contrast to OBC, after a long-time evolution, the final state of the periodic system now depends on the initial configuration and may appear as a superposition among different NESSs:
\rho_{\text{ss}}=\rho_{\text{ss}}(\rho_{0})
.

When
\gamma_{2}=0
, from
Figure

5
(a)
, the parameter regime allowing the three-fold degenerate NESS can be extended
to
|t_{1}|=|t_{2}|
where the zero-rapidity bulk mode appears at
m^{*}=(+,-\frac{\pi}{2}[\text{sgn}(t_{1}t_{2})+1])
. Meanwhile, with
\gamma_{2}
suppressed, one observes
interesting features in the relaxation behavior reflected by the Liouvillian gap [see also
Figure

4
(a)
]:

\displaystyle\Delta^{\text{PBC}}=\begin{cases}\ 0,&|t_{1}|\leq|t_{2}|\\
\ 2\gamma_{1}-2\sqrt{\gamma_{1}^{2}-(|t_{1}|-|t_{2}|)^{2}},&|t_{2}|<|t_{1}|\leq t_{c}\\
\ 2\gamma_{1}-\frac{2\gamma_{1}|t_{2}|}{\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}-\gamma_{1}^{2}}},&|t_{1}|>t_{c},\end{cases}

(3.2)

where the critical value is identified as
t_{c}=(|t_{2}|+\sqrt{t_{2}^{2}+4\gamma_{1}^{2}})/2
.
In the region
|t_{1}|<|t_{2}|
, the Liouvillian gap closes at the bulk modes
m^{*}=(\pm,\pm\arccos[-t_{1}/t_{2}])
. We call them
quasi-steady
states, which are stationary states characteristic of a vanishing decay rate and a finite
phase oscillation frequency as shown in
Figure

5
(b)
:
|\text{Re}(\beta_{m^{*}})|=0,|\text{Im}(\beta_{m^{*}})|=\sqrt{t_{2}^{2}-t_{1}^{2}}
. Similar to the degenerate NESS, the final state can also select the quasi-steady states without any decay in the probability density.
Once
|t_{1}|>|t_{2}|
, the Liouvillian gap of the PBC
spectrum opens up but remains smaller than OBC. The bulk mode that dominates the relaxation process should be the one with a minimal decay rate.
It changes from
(+,-\frac{\pi}{2}[\text{sgn}(t_{1}t_{2})+1])
to
(\pm,\pm\arccos[t_{1}t_{2}/(\gamma_{1}^{2}-t_{1}^{2})])
when the growing amplitude
|t_{1}|
goes past
t_{c}
. It should be noted that the gap solution in
Equation

3.2
is valid for both the strong (
\gamma_{1}>|t_{2}|
) and weak (
\gamma_{1}\leq|t_{2}|
) dissipations.

Compared to the trivial steady state in
Equation

2.35
, the degenerate and quasi-NESSs can be viewed as plane waves of fermions with fixed momenta
m^{*}
on top of a static uniform occupation.
It is rather important to distinguish the degenerate and quasi-NESSs from the trivial one, especially on account of the former two being a direct consequence of the closing of the Liouvillian gap.
We find the current flow
[
72
,
81
]
is such an ideal observable. Defined as
j_{c}(t)=(\frac{i}{n_{\text{tot}}})\sum_{j}[\langle a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j+1}\rangle_{t}-\langle a_{j+1}^{\dagger}a_{j}\rangle_{t}]
,
the time-dependent current flow can be conveniently obtained from the single-particle correlator in
Equation

2.55
:

\displaystyle j_{c}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2N}\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{\gamma}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in\text{all bands}}e^{-(\beta_{m}+\beta_{m^{\prime}})t}

\displaystyle\times\left[-\psi_{Lm}^{*}(2j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(2j-1)+\psi_{Lm}^{*}(2j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(2j+1)\right]

\displaystyle\times\psi_{Rm}(l)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}(l).

(3.3)

Our focal point is to study the behavior of the current in different NESSs.
At larger times, only the Liouvillian gapless modes
m^{*}
satisfying
\text{Re}(\beta_{m^{*}})=0
survive. It leads to

\displaystyle j_{\text{ss}}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\to\infty}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2N}\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{\gamma}\right)\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{l=1}^{2N}\sum_{m,m^{\prime}\in m^{*}}e^{-i\text{Im}({\beta_{m}}+\beta_{m^{\prime}})t}

\displaystyle\times\left[-\psi_{Lm}^{*}(2j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(2j-1)+\psi_{Lm}^{*}(2j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(2j+1)\right]

\displaystyle\times\psi_{Rm}(l)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}(l).

(3.4)

Let us begin with the special limit
\gamma_{2}=0
where all three types of NESSs coexist and assume
t_{2}>0
.
For the trivial steady state
m^{*}=\varnothing
, thus the current vanishes in the end

\displaystyle\left.j_{\text{ss}}\right|_{\gamma_{2}=0}=0,\quad|t_{1}|>t_{2}.

(3.5)

At phase transition points, the degenerate NESS generated by two bulk modes
m^{*}=(+,-\frac{\pi}{2}[\text{sgn}(t_{1}t_{2})+1])
supports a current flow with an amplitude

\displaystyle\left.j_{\text{ss}}\right|_{\gamma_{2}=0}=\begin{cases}\ 0,&t_{1}=-t_{2};\\
\ \frac{1}{2N}\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{\gamma}\right),&t_{1}=t_{2}.\end{cases}

(3.6)

In the intermediate gapless region, there emerges quasi-NESS from the modes
m^{*}=(\pm,\pm\arccos[-t_{1}/t_{2}])
. Analytically, the stationary current can be expanded in the orders of
1/N
:

\displaystyle\left.j_{\text{ss}}\right|_{\gamma_{2}=0}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle t\to\infty}}{{=}}\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{\gamma}\right)\left\{\frac{1}{2N}\left(\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{t_{2}}\right)\right.

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2N^{2}}\left.\left[\left(\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{t_{2}}\right)\cos(2t_{2}\alpha t)+\alpha\sin(2t_{2}\alpha t)\right]\right\}+\cdots,

(3.7)

with
\alpha=\sqrt{1-(t_{1}/t_{2})^{2}}
. The time dependence in the steady steady current arises from the finite phase oscillation frequency, a unique feature possessed by quasi-NESS.
Given sufficiently large system size, to the leading order
O(N^{-1})
, we get

\displaystyle\left.j_{\text{ss}}\right|_{\gamma_{2}=0}=\frac{1}{2N}\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{\gamma}\right)\left(\frac{t_{1}+t_{2}}{t_{2}}\right),\quad|t_{1}|<t_{2}.

(3.8)

For non-zero
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}

m^{*}=(+,-\pi)
at
t_{1}=t_{2}
, while it vanishes elsewhere. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left.j_{\text{ss}}\right|_{\gamma_{1}\cdot\gamma_{2}\neq 0}=\frac{1}{2N}\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{\gamma}\right)\delta_{t_{1},t_{2}}.

(3.9)

One immediately notices that when the gain and loss dissipations are in balance, namely
\eta=0
, the prefactor
(\gamma+\eta)/\gamma\to 1
.
The steady state current is then independent of the dissipation strengths
\gamma_{i}
.
In
Figure

in Eqs. (
3.5
)
-
(
3.8

at time

With balanced gain and loss bond dissipations, we verify the stationary current remains the same under different values of
\gamma_{1}
.
When
\gamma_{2}\neq 0

\gamma_{2}
[see also
Figure

(a)

By contrast, in the description of the effective Hamiltonian, once

the real part of the rapidity spectrum for a periodic chain in

2.59

In this section, we search for dynamical signatures of the Liouvillian skin effect in dissipative quantum systems, originating from the piling up of the NMMs exponentially close to an open boundary.
Compared with previous studies

IV.2
Density evolution under damping

Next, we focus on the chiral damping phenomena in the particle-number distribution of the dissipative chain
[
60
]
. We find the damping itself displays the Liouvillian skin effect beyond the gapless point
t_{1}=t_{2}
.
By turning on the second bond dissipators
\gamma_{2}
, we show the center of the chiral damping wavefront can be tuned via the localization parameter
r^{2}
of the bulk NMMs. It enables us to associate
the Liouvillian skin effect with the existence of the EPs in the damping matrix at which
|r^{2}|\to 0
or
\infty
. We further clarify the sensitivity of dynamical observables to the boundary conditions at longer times. Our results are consistent with one of the earlier studies
[
63

, demonstrating that probes in the short-time domain are not sufficient to distinguish the relaxation processes of open quantum matter under different boundary conditions.

IV.2.1
Link with exceptional topology

First, we associate the chiral damping of the particle-number operator with the exceptional topology of the damping matrix.
In the full Lindblad master equation framework, the evolution of the particle number to NESS,
\tilde{n}_{j}(t)=n_{j}(t)-n_{j,\text{ss}}

single particle correlator
\langle a_{j}^{\dagger}a_{j}\rangle_{t}
from

2.55

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{2\gamma}\right)\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\text{tot}}}e^{-(\beta_{m}+\beta_{m^{\prime}})t}

\displaystyle\phantom{===}\psi_{Lm}^{*}(j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(j)\cdot\psi_{Rm}(l)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}(l).

(4.1)

Under OBC, a large system size enables us to safely neglect the contributions from the boundary eigenmodes in
Equation

A9

Given exact solutions of the left and right bulk eigenmodes in

A17

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{2j-1}(t)\sim\frac{1}{r^{2j}}e^{-\Delta_{\text{eff}}t}\cdot\left(\frac{\gamma+\eta}{2\gamma}\right).

IV.2.3
Boundary sensitivity

From the chiral damping phenomena, the Liouvillian skin effect takes place upon changing the boundary from PBC to OBC.
Since the system evolves from a completely filled initial state to a uniform steady state, the damping serves as a global effect.
Nevertheless, we can also resolve the motion of a single particle and study the sensitivity of the open system to boundary conditions on broader grounds.

It is argued in Ref.
[
63
]
that in the thermodynamical limit, the single-particle Green’s function of NH systems is independent of the boundary conditions.
We show in the following within the Lindblad master equation framework, the argument is indeed true for the evolution of the density matrix at short times. However, as soon as the motion of a particle
involves the edge, due to the gap closing and the Liouvillian skin effect, there emerge drastic differences in the relaxation process under PBC or OBC.

Let us start by putting one particle in the middle of the chain:
n_{j}(0)=\delta_{j,N}
. The initial condition corresponds to a Majorana pairing configuration,

\displaystyle\tilde{C}(0)=-\frac{i(\eta-\gamma)}{\gamma}\begin{pmatrix}0&\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}\\
-\mathbb{1}_{n\times n}&0\end{pmatrix}-i\begin{pmatrix}0&D\\
-D&0\end{pmatrix},

(4.5)

where the
n\times n
matrix
D
only holds one non-zero element
D_{(N,N)}=1
. The time evolution of the particle number at the
j
th site
can be described by

\displaystyle\tilde{n}_{j}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,m^{\prime}}e^{-(\beta_{m}+\beta_{m^{\prime}})t}\psi_{Lm}^{*}(j)\psi_{Lm^{\prime}}^{*}(j)

\displaystyle\left[\psi_{Rm}(N)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}(N)+\left(\frac{\eta-\gamma}{2\gamma}\right)\sum_{l=1}^{n_{\text{tot}}}\psi_{Rm}(l)\psi_{Rm^{\prime}}(l)\right].

(4.6)

To make a direct comparison with Ref.
[
63
]
, we suppress the gain dissipations on both bonds:
\gamma_{1}^{g}=\gamma_{2}^{g}=0

without violating the solvable constraint in Eqs. (
2.30
):
{\gamma_{1}}/{\eta_{1}}={\gamma_{2}}/{\eta_{2}}=1
.
Since
\gamma=\eta
, the second term in the particle-number operator vanishes, and the trivial NESS refers to an empty chain indicated by
Equation

2.35
.

Also, we focus on
the relaxation process at the same point
t_{1}=t_{2}
as Ref.
[
63
]
, where the gap of the PBC rapidity spectrum closes for arbitrary dissipation strengths
\gamma_{1}
and
\gamma_{2}
.
The inclusion of
\gamma_{2}\neq 0
offers more tunability on the Liouvillian skin effect parameter
r^{2}
.
As shown in
Figure

Once that long-time evolution is permitted, contrary to Ref.
[
63

, the motion differs a lot under PBC or OBC. We observe a persistent current
j_{\text{ss}}=1/N
circulating along the periodic chain while
the current quickly terminates under the influence of an open boundary.

Meanwhile, we compare the responses of the particle motion to the presence of a strong or weak Liouvillian skin effect.
When the skin effect dominates [

in Figs.

One concludes that regardless of the

IV.3
Lifetime of non-equilibrium particle at the edge

In the last section, we resolved the relaxation time of dynamical observables under the Liouvillian skin effect. For a measurement, any local observable of the operator
\hat{Q}

has reached the steady state if
|\tilde{Q}(t)|=|Q(t)-Q_{\text{ss}}|\ll||\hat{Q}||=(\text{Tr}[\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}])^{1/2}
. Along the lines of Ref.
[
66
]
,
one can thus define the maximal relaxation time
\tau
of the system as that of the slowest decaying mode:
|c|e^{-\tau\Delta}=e^{-1}\ll 1
with
\Delta
the Liouvillian gap.
The presence of the Liouvillian skin effect enhances the amplitude of this mode, showing an exponential localization tendency near the boundary:
|c|\sim e^{\mathcal{O}(L/\xi)}
. Here,
\xi
denotes
the correlation length of the system. It leads to

\displaystyle\tau\sim\frac{1}{\Delta}+\frac{1}{\xi}\cdot\frac{L}{\Delta}.

(4.7)

In our dissipative SSH model, we look at the Liouvillian skin effect region
|r^{2}|<1
and take the dynamical observable as the occupation of a particle residing at the right boundary.
More importantly, we make fewer assumptions than Ref.
[
66
]
by defining the lifetime of the non-equilibrium particle according to

\displaystyle|\tilde{n}_{2N-1}(\tau)|=e^{-l}|\tilde{n}_{2N-1}(0)|,

(4.8)

where
l
is taken as a positive integer. The precision of the lifetime can be improved by increasing the value of
l
.
The definition in
Equation

4.8
has the advantage of including the contributions from all decaying modes, staying closer to a real measurement.

For simplicity, we start with a completely filled chain and balanced gain and loss dissipators
\eta_{1}=\eta_{2}=0
. Then,

|\tilde{n}_{2N-1}(0)|=1-1/2=1/2
. From the asymptotic scaling of the particle number evolution in
Equation

4.2
, we establish that

\displaystyle\tau_{2N-1}\sim\frac{1}{\Delta_{\text{eff}}}+\ln(r^{-2})\cdot\frac{N}{\Delta_{\text{eff}}}.

(4.9)

The correlation length of the system in
Equation

4.7
thus satisfies

\displaystyle\xi(r^{2})\simeq\frac{2}{|\ln(|r^{2}|)|},

(4.10)

where the factor of
2
comes from the identification
L=2N-1
. Consistent with Ref.
[
66
]
, we find the lifetime of the particle at the edge grows linearly with the system size
without a closing of the effective Liouvillian gap.

Next, based on our exact solutions, we verify numerically the above relations by varying the system size and the skin effect parameter
r^{2}
. It is also important to explore two limits, the weak and strong dissipations where
the formation of the effective Liouvillian gap differs.

At weak dissipations
\gamma_{1}<|t_{1}|
and
\gamma_{2}<|t_{2}|

Figure

the small discrepancy results from the fact that the strong polarization of the bulk eigenmodes challenges the numerical precision of the damping matrix decomposition in Eqs. (

V
Discussion

We have studied the Liouvillian skin effect in a bond-dissipative SSH model, of which the rapidity spectrum and NMMs are exactly solvable. This has illuminated the relation between the NH skin effect, two different effective Hamiltonians,
H_{\text{eff}}
,
H_{\text{S}}
, and the full quantum master equation description. A number of dynamical phenomena, such as diverging relaxation times, inherited in the quantum setup originate from the anomalous boundary sensitivity of the NH Hamiltonians and have been investigated in detail. This paper also provides a solid platform to resolve the entanglement spectrum and identify transitions between different NH topological phases
[
82
]
whose experimental realization is feasible, e.g., in ultra-cold atoms with a momentum lattice
[
59
]
.

Dynamical probes of the quantum Fisher information
[
47
]
and the application as NH topological sensors
[
46
]
provide intriguing questions for future investigation. Meanwhile, the current model reveals the Liouvillian skin effect in the relaxation process
while posing the question on the search for steady states that inherit the exceptional topology of the NH damping matrix. It is found that using monitored quantum circuits, periodic measurement allows access to biorthogonal steady state observables
[
29
]
. Future works could also be centered around the interaction effects on generic Lindbladians. For instance,
the dynamical mean-field theory can be implemented to uncover the interplay of dissipation and environmental fluctuations in the open quantum matter that consists of interacting fermions
[
79
]
or bosons
[
83
]
.
A mixture of particle species is a good path to the discovery of further variations of the Liouvillian skin effect.
From a broader theoretical point of view, effective field theories with bosonization prove powerful in studying dissipative Luttinger liquids
[
84
]
. In presence of weak symmetries, one may tackle even strongly interacting Liouvillians
[
85
]
.
The inclusion of disorder and chaos may be possible provided the solvable limit in a disorder-averaging SYK Lindbladian
[
86
]
.

APPENDIX: EXACT SOLUTION OF THE GENERALIZED NON-HERMITIAN SSH CHAIN

In the Appendix, we present a detailed derivation on the exact solution of the NH SSH chain in
Equation

2.8
.
The generic results for the simplified model
[
18
,
34
]
are extended here to allow two asymmetric hopping terms.

.1
Periodic boundary condition

First, we address the case of a periodic boundary:
\varphi_{N+1,A(B)}=\varphi_{1,A(B)}
. For convenience, we set the lattice spacing to unity. Through the Fourier transform
\varphi_{{j,\alpha}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\sum_{q}e^{iqj}\varphi_{\alpha}(q)
, with
q=2\pi m^{\prime}/N
,
m^{\prime}=-N/2,-N/2+1,\dots,0,\dots,N/2-1
, the anti-commutation relations for fermionic operators are respected:
\{\varphi_{\alpha}(q),\varphi_{\alpha^{\prime}}^{\dagger}(q^{\prime})\}=\delta_{q,q^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}
.
In the new basis
\underline{\varphi}(q)=(\varphi_{A}(q),\varphi_{B}(q))^{T}
, one rewrites the Hamiltonian into

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}=\sum_{q}\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}(q)\cdot H_{\text{S}}(q)\underline{\varphi}(q).

(A1)

In terms of Pauli matrices, the matrix elements read

\displaystyle H_{\text{S}}(q)

\displaystyle=\vec{h}(q)\cdot\vec{\sigma},

\displaystyle h_{x}(q)

\displaystyle=t_{1}+t_{2}\cos(q)+i\gamma_{2}\sin(q),

\displaystyle h_{y}(q)

\displaystyle=i\gamma_{1}-i\gamma_{2}\cos(q)+t_{2}\sin(q),

\displaystyle h_{z}(q)

\displaystyle=0.

(A2)

From the eigenvalue Eqs. (
2.19
), we solve the energy spectrum,
E_{m}^{\text{PBC}}=E^{\text{PBC}}_{\nu=\pm}(q)
,

\displaystyle E^{\text{PBC}}_{\pm}(q)

\displaystyle=\pm\left[t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}-(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})+2(t_{1}t_{2}+\gamma_{1}\gamma_{2})\cos q\right.

\displaystyle\phantom{=}\left.+2i(t_{1}\gamma_{2}+t_{2}\gamma_{1})\sin q\right]^{\frac{1}{2}},

(A3)

together with the right and left eigenvectors:

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R,\nu}(q,\gamma_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{pmatrix}(t_{1}+\gamma_{1}+(t_{2}-\gamma_{2})e^{-iq})/E_{\nu}(q)\\
1\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{L,\nu}(q,\gamma_{i})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R,\nu}(q,-\gamma_{i}).

(A4)

The second mutual relation can be proved by taking into account
H_{\text{S}}^{\dagger}(q,\gamma_{i})=H_{\text{S}}(q,-\gamma_{i})
and
E_{\nu}^{*}(q,\gamma_{i})=E_{\nu}(q,-\gamma_{i})|_{\text{PBC}}
.
The normalization of eigenvectors is chosen according to the biorthogonality:

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{L,\nu}^{*}(q)\cdot\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R,\nu^{\prime}}(q)=\delta_{\nu,\nu^{\prime}}.

(A5)

The Hamiltonian now shares a diagonalized structure:

\displaystyle H_{\text{S}}=\sum_{(\nu,q)}\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}(q)\cdot\left[E_{\nu}(q)\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R,\nu}(q)\cdot\tilde{\underline{\psi}}^{*}_{L,\nu}(q)\right]\underline{\varphi}(q).

(A6)

For the evaluation of the single-particle correlation acting on the real space in
Equation

2.55
, the components
\tilde{\psi}_{R,(\nu,q)}(j,\alpha)
,
\tilde{\psi}^{*}_{L,(\nu,q)}(j,\alpha)
are obtained by the Fourier transform:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\psi}_{R,(\nu,q)}(j,A)\\
\tilde{\psi}_{R,(\nu,q)}(j,B)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{iqj}}{\sqrt{N}}\ \tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R,\nu}(q),

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\psi}^{*}_{L,(\nu,q)}(j,A)\\
\tilde{\psi}^{*}_{L,(\nu,q)}(j,B)\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{-iqj}}{\sqrt{N}}\ \tilde{\underline{\psi}}^{*}_{L,\nu}(q),

(A7)

where we have used
\langle 0|\varphi_{\alpha^{\prime}}(q^{\prime})\varphi^{\dagger}_{\alpha}(q)|0\rangle=\delta_{\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}}\delta_{q,q^{\prime}}
.

.2
Open boundary condition

Under OBC, we break the
N
th unit cell by taking away the last
B
site.
The NH SSH Hamiltonian is then expressed in an explicit
n\times n
matrix form:
\mathcal{H}_{\text{S}}=\underline{\varphi}^{\dagger}\cdot H_{\text{S}}\underline{\varphi}
with
\varphi=(\varphi_{(1,A)},\varphi_{(1,B)},\dots,\varphi_{(N,A)})^{T}

and

\displaystyle\ \ H_{\text{S}}=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&t_{1}+\gamma_{1}&&&&&\\
t_{1}-\gamma_{1}&0&t_{2}+\gamma_{2}&&&&&&\\
&t_{2}-\gamma_{2}&0&&&&&\\
&&&&\ddots&&\\
&&&&&0&t_{2}+\gamma_{2}\\
&&&&&t_{2}-\gamma_{2}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(A8)

For
n=2N-1
an odd number of sites,
H_{\text{S}}
is shown to be
exactly solvable at
\gamma_{1}\neq 0
,
\gamma_{2}=0

[
18
,
34
]
. Below, we generalize the exact OBC solution to the region:
\gamma_{1}\neq 0
,
\gamma_{2}\neq 0
.
The spectrum recovers the known solution by setting
\gamma_{2}\to 0
and retains the main features in the new limit.

On one hand, there exist two zero-energy boundary states that are fully suppressed on the sublattice
B
,

\displaystyle E_{0}=0:\quad\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R0}=\mathcal{N}_{R}\begin{pmatrix}r_{R}\\
0\\
r_{R}^{2}\\
0\\
\dots\\
0\\
r_{R}^{N}\end{pmatrix},\ \tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{L0}=\mathcal{N}_{L}\begin{pmatrix}r_{L}\\
0\\
r_{L}^{2}\\
0\\
\dots\\
0\\
r_{L}^{N}\end{pmatrix},

(A9)

with associated parameters
r_{R}=-(t_{1}-\gamma_{1})/(t_{2}+\gamma_{2})
,
r_{L}=-(t_{1}+\gamma_{1})/(t_{2}-\gamma_{2})
. The two eigenstates are biorthogonal to each other,

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{L0}^{*}\cdot\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R0}=1,

(A10)

which brings about a constraint on the normalization factors:

\mathcal{N}_{L}^{*}\mathcal{N}_{R}={(1-r_{L}^{*}r_{R})}/[{r_{L}^{*}r_{R}(1-(r_{L}^{*}r_{R})^{N})]}
.
It is easy to discern that, depending on the absolute values of
r_{L}
and
r_{R}
, the left and right boundary modes can be localized at different ends:
\text{sgn}[\ln(|r_{L}|)]\neq\text{sgn}[\ln(|r_{R}|)]
.
We find the parameters satisfying this condition all stay in the topological regime in Eqs. (
2.42
) where the spectral winding number is non-trivial.

On the other hand, to derive the bulk spectrum, we present an intuitive approach by analogy to Refs.
[
19
,
34
]
. Our first step is the identification of the gap-closing points when the boundary is opened.
One way is to find a transformation matrix
R
such that the NH SSH Hamiltonian is mapped to its Hermitian counterpart
\bar{H}_{\text{S}}
:

\displaystyle\bar{H}_{\text{S}}=R^{-1}H_{\text{S}}R.

(A11)

A proper construction leads to
R=R_{1}R_{2}
, where
R_{1}=\text{diag}\{1,r_{1},r_{1},r_{1}^{2},r_{1}^{2},\dots,r_{1}^{N-1},r_{1}^{N-1}\}
,
R_{2}=\text{diag}\{1,1,r_{2},r_{2},r_{2}^{2},r_{2}^{2},\dots,r_{2}^{N-2},r_{2}^{N-1}\}
,
and
r_{1}=\sqrt{(t_{1}-\gamma_{1})/(t_{1}+\gamma_{1})}
,
r_{2}=\sqrt{(t_{2}-\gamma_{2})/(t_{2}+\gamma_{2})}
.
The Hermitian SSH chain is embedded with the anisotropic hopping strengths

\displaystyle\bar{t}_{1}=\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}-\gamma_{1}^{2}},\quad\bar{t}_{2}=\sqrt{t_{2}^{2}-\gamma_{2}^{2}},

(A12)

with the gapless phase transition occurring at
|\bar{t}_{1}|=|\bar{t}_{2}|
.
H_{\text{S}}
inherits from the transformation these gap closing points:

\displaystyle|t_{1}^{2}-\gamma_{1}^{2}|=|t_{2}^{2}-\gamma_{2}^{2}|.

(A13)

An alternative method of reproducing the gapless points in the OBC spectrum is to study the biorthogonal polarization of the NH system
[
18
,
34
]
, which changes its integer value at
|r_{L}^{*}r_{R}|=1
,
in consistency with our result in
Equation

A13
.

Under the transformation in
Equation

A11
, one can also link the bulk eigenstates of
H_{\text{S}}
to the ones of
\bar{H}_{\text{S}}
by
\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R}=R\bar{\underline{\psi}}_{R}
and
\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{L}=\bar{\underline{\psi}}_{L}R^{-1}
, which indicates the piling up of the right (left) states at one end with an exponential localization factor
r^{j}
(
r^{-j}
), where
r=r_{1}r_{2}=\sqrt{r_{R}/r_{L}}
. It is equivalent to a change of momentum in the Bloch phase factor
e^{iqj}
from
q
to
q-i\ln(r)
, thus allowing us to build the OBC bulk spectrum from the PBC one

[
19
,
20
]
.
More precisely, at a fixed momentum
q
, like the PBC spectrum the bulk energies always come in pairs (
\nu=\pm
):
E_{m\neq 0}=E_{\nu}^{\text{OBC}}(q)
.
The two spectra are related by

\displaystyle E_{\pm}^{\text{OBC}}(q)=E_{\pm}^{\text{PBC}}(q-i\ln(r))

\displaystyle=\pm\sqrt{t_{1}^{2}+t_{2}^{2}-(\gamma_{1}^{2}+\gamma_{2}^{2})+2\sqrt{(t_{1}^{2}-\gamma_{1}^{2})(t_{2}^{2}-\gamma_{2}^{2})}\cos(q)}.

(A14)

Given an even number of sites
n=2N
, the OBC spectrum obtained in this way applies only to the large
N
limit
[
19
]
.
For an odd number of sites
n=2N-1
, with additional mirror symmetry
[
87
,
34
]
, it can be shown that the bulk spectrum in
Equation

A14
becomes exact for any finite
N
.

Let us verify by building the exact left and right eigenvectors. We choose
q=\pi m^{\prime}/N
,
m^{\prime}=1,\dots,N-1
, so that
in total the index
m\in\{0,(\pm,q)\}
reproduces a complete set of
2N-1
bands. A trial function for the right eigenstates can be written as

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R\nu}(q,\gamma_{i})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2N}}\begin{pmatrix}\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R\nu}(q,1)\\
\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R\nu}(q,2)\\
\cdots\\
\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R\nu}(q,N)\end{pmatrix},

(A15)

where the component in the
j
th unit cell should be linked with the PBC eigenvector in
Equation

A4
by the same momentum shift:

\displaystyle\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R\nu}(q,j)\sim r^{j}e^{iqj}\tilde{\underline{\psi}}_{R\nu}^{\text{PBC}}(q-i\ln(r)).

(A16)

On top of that, mirror symmetry in the spectrum
E_{\pm}^{\text{OBC}}(q)=E_{\pm}^{\text{OBC}}(-q)
