Gradient-free optimisation via integration
Christophe Andrieu, Nicolas Chopin, Ettore Fincato, Mathieu Gerber

Abstract
We develop and analyse an approach to optimize functions l : ℝ d → ℝ l\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R} not assumed to be convex, differentiable or even continuous. The algorithm belongs to the class of model-based search methods. The idea is to fit recursively l l to a parametric family of distributions, using a Bayesian update followed by a reprojection back onto the chosen family. Remarkably, reprojection in our scenario boils down to computing expectations, which can be simply approximated through Monte Carlo. We show that when the family of distributions is appropriately chosen this approach can be interpreted as an implicit time-inhomogeneous gradient descent algorithm on a sequence of smoothed approximations of l l , providing a route to establishing convergence. We establish new results for generic inhomogeneous gradient descent algorithms, which we specialise to the model-based search algorithm in the Gaussian scenario. We illustrate the performance of the algorithm on a challenging classification task in machine learning.

Motivation

Let

lower semi-continuous, potentially non-differentiable function such that

\arg\min_{x\in K}l(x)\neq\emptyset

provided it can be evaluated pointwise.

The algorithm we study is based on the following central idea. Let
\phi

standard normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I}_{d})
, and let

\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x)\coloneq\frac{1}{\gamma^{d/2}}\phi\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\gamma}}(x-\theta)\right)

(1)

for
\theta\in\Theta\coloneq\mathbb{R}^{d}

\gamma>0
; that is, the density
of
\mathcal{N}(\theta,\gamma\mathbf{I}_{d})

Then, for a sequence

\{\gamma_{n}\geq 0,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that
\gamma_{n}\downarrow 0

sequentially the families of distributions
\{\pi_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}

1.2
Connection with gradient descent

In a standard statistical context, repeated application of Bayes’ rule is
known to lead to a concentration phenomenon around particular maximum points or
the posteriors, under general conditions
[
Kleijn and van der Vaart, 2012
]
.

The update considered here differs from standard Bayesian updating in that it
involves a reprojection step, therefore necessitating a new approach to
establishing ability of the algorithm to find minima of
l
. This
reprojection step is motivated by practical considerations: it
circumvents the need to propagate the sequence of distributions obtained by
repeated use of Bayes’ update and greatly facilitates implementation (as we
elaborate below). Notably, the reprojection step also offers an entry point for the theoretical analysis of the algorithm. The
crucial observation allowing us to prove convergence of
Algorithm
1
, in the sense that
\pi_{n}
concentrates on local
minima of
l
, is that it implicitly implements a steepest descent
algorithm tracking the minima of a sequence of differentiable approximations

\{l_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},n\geq 1\}
of
l
.
When such approximations converge to
l
, validity of the procedure
should ensue.

More precisely, the reprojection step can be shown to correspond to so-called
moment matching, a fact extensively used in variational inference

[
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
]
. Taking into account that in the present setup

\theta_{n+1}
is the first order moment of
\pi_{n+1}
, or mean, moment
matching takes the form

\theta_{n+1}=\int x\tilde{\pi}_{n+1}(x){\mathrm{d}}x=\theta_{n}+\frac{\int(x-\theta_{n})\exp\big\{-l(x)\big\}\phi\big(\frac{x-\theta_{n}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}\big){\mathrm{d}}x}{\int\exp\big\{-l(x)\big\}\phi\big(\frac{x-\theta_{n}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}\big){\mathrm{d}}x}\,.

(2)

It is the evaluation of these expectations which in practice requires a Monte
Carlo approximation with weighted samples. For
(\theta,\gamma)\in\Theta\times\mathbb{R}_{+}
, let

l_{\gamma}(\theta)\coloneq-\log\left(\int\frac{\exp\left\{-l(x)\right\}}{\gamma^{d/2}}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\theta}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\Bigr)\mathrm{d}x\right),

(3)

and for
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\theta\in\Theta
let
l_{n}(\theta):=l_{\gamma_{n}}(\theta).

Then one can write (
2
) in the familiar
form

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\gamma_{n}\nabla l_{n}(\theta_{n})\,,

(4)

and recognize a time inhomogeneous steepest-descent algorithm tracking the
sequence of stationary points of the sequence of functions

\{l_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
, again smoothed versions of
l
. It is
remarkable that while this interpretation provides us with an additional
rational for Algorithm
1
and a route to establishing its
convergence for a large class of non-differentiable functions
l
(the
subject of next sections), implementation does
not require differentiation but instead integration.

The arguments we provide in
Sections

2
,
3
and
4
lead
to the following convergence result on the inhomogeneous gradient descent
in (
4
).

Theorem 1
.

Let
l:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be lower-bounded, strongly lower semi-continuous and
assume there exists
C_{l}\in(0,\infty)
such that

|l(\theta^{\prime})-l(\theta)|\leq C_{l}+C_{l}\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}
for all

\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Let
\{\gamma_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be a sequence
with
\gamma_{n}=n^{-\beta}
for all
n\geq 1
, for some
\beta\in(0,1)
.
Let
\left\{\theta_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be the output of recursion (
4
), corresponding to Algorithm
1
. Then, there exists a subsequence
\left\{\theta_{n_{k}},\,k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
of
\{\theta_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
such that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla l_{n_{k}}(\theta_{n_{k}})\|=0
.

The most stringent assumptions on
l
is simply that its ‘jumps’ are
bounded and that its variations are at most quadratic for large increments of

\theta
. This condition is always satisfied for
l
bounded. When
combined with the characterisation of local minima
(
Theorem

3
) in our framework,

Theorem

1
constitutes a tool to identify local minima candidates. In
particular, as will shall see, this theorem implies that if the sequence

\left\{\theta_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
defined in Algorithm
1

converges to some
\theta
, then
\theta
is a candidate local minimum of

l
.
Theorem

1
therefore provides a convergence
result for Algorithm
1
under mild assumptions on
l
, but
it also leads to a number of consequences and stronger results, when more is
known on the objective function. For instance, when
\theta\mapsto l(\theta)
is convex,
the functions
\theta\mapsto l_{n}(\theta)
are also convex for
n\in\mathbb{N}
: in this case, one can easily show from Theorem

that Algorithm

converges to the minimiser of

.

1.3
Beyond Gaussian families: mirror descent

Explicitly introducing the Kullback-Leibler minimisation in
Algorithm

1
suggests natural extensions beyond Gaussian families,
and leads to a connection with mirror descent algorithms. As we establish in the Appendix, any
regular EDM (exponential dispersion models) family

[
Jorgensen, 1987
,
Jorgensen, 1997
]
could be used, i.e., probability densities of the form:

\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x)=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-A(\theta)\right]\right\}\upsilon_{\gamma}(x)\,,

where
T\colon\mathsf{X}\rightarrow\mathsf{T}
and
\theta\in\Theta
. One may
use for instance a Wishart family to perform optimisation when
\mathsf{X}
is
a space of symmetric positive definite matrices, or a Bernoulli product
family when
\mathsf{X}=\{0,1\}^{d}
.
Simple derivations (see
Sections

B.1
and
B.2
) show that, for a
generic EDM family, (
4
) becomes

\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta_{n+1})=\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta_{n})+\gamma_{n}\nabla_{\theta}\log\int\exp\left\{-l(x)\right\}\pi_{(\nabla A)^{-1}(\mu(\theta_{n})),\gamma_{n}}\big({\mathrm{d}}x\big)\,,

(5)

which is a mirror descent recursion. In the Gaussian case,
T(x)=x
,

\nabla A(\theta)=\theta
,
and one recovers the gradient descent recursion (
2
).

Section

B.3
provides some details on the Wishart case.

The motivation and interest behind EDMs in the present context is the following. According to
Section

B.2

one has
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta,\gamma}}\big(T(X)\big)=\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta)=:\mu(\theta)
and

\mathrm{var}_{\pi_{\theta,\gamma}}\big(T(X)\big)=\gamma\nabla_{\theta}^{2}A(\theta)
.
Then, for any
\theta\in\Theta
, letting
\gamma\downarrow 0
ensures that
the distribution of
T(X)
under
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}
concentrates
on
\mu(\theta)
. In the most common scenario where
T(x)=x
(or
a component of
T(x)
is
x
), which is the case of the normal example
we started with, this means that whenever
\theta\mapsto\mu(\theta)

spans
\mathsf{X}
then we can aim to adjust
\theta
to ensure

\mu(\theta)\in\arg\min_{x\in\mathsf{X}}l(x)
. The use of the symbol
\theta
instead of
x
earlier should now
be clear, since their nature is very different in the general scenario, but confounded
in the normal scenario where the mean is the sole parameter used.

Example 1
.

As an illustration of the flexibility of the framework we develop, consider the problem of optimising a function defined on the set of positive symmetric matrices (PSM). Here, rather than using a Gaussian kernel not adapted to the PSM space, we consider a domain-adapted Wishart kernel.
The Wishart kernel builds on the Wishart probability density which, after appropriate rescaling, leads to a member of the EDM family (see Appendix
B.3
for details)
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}
with potential function
A(\theta)=-1/2\log|-\theta|
, where
|\cdot|
denotes the determinant of the
d\times d
positive symmetric matrix
-\theta
.
Using the notation
\Sigma_{n}=-\theta_{n}^{-1}/2
, one has
\nabla A(\theta_{n})=\Sigma_{n}
and (
5
) yields:

\Sigma_{n+1}=\Sigma_{n}-\gamma_{n}\nabla l_{\gamma_{n}}\!\big(-\Sigma_{n}^{-1}/2\big),\qquad n\geq 0,

(6)

while setting the gradient to zero leads to the moment matching solution
\Sigma_{n+1}=\mathbb{E}_{\tilde{\pi}_{n}}[X]
, with
\tilde{\pi}_{n}\propto e^{-l}\pi_{\theta_{n},\gamma_{n}}
.

We are currently investigating extensions of our theoretical framework to non-Gaussian families, with particular attention to the Wishart case, and a more general treatment is left for future work.

1.4
Links to other optimisation schemes

Algorithm
1
belongs to the family of so-called model-based search methods, which can be traced back to
Dorigo [1992]
,
Bonet et al. [1996]
, appearing implicitly in the former and explicitly in the latter; see
Zlochin et al. [2004]
for a detailed survey. These ideas have since been rediscovered on multiple occasions, again implicitly or explicitly, and further developed, e.g.
Rubinstein [1999]
,
Ionides et al. [2006]
,
Ionides et al. [2011]
,
Osher et al. [2019]
,
Osher et al. [2023]
,
Spokoiny [2023]
. Earlier contributions were motivated by problems in discrete optimisation. The cross-entropy (CE) method of
Rubinstein [1999]
, is closest to ours. Exponential families and Kullback-Leibler minimisation are also suggested, but concentration is obtained by rescaling the objective, similarly to our discussion in Subsection
5.1
, while in the Gaussian case both the mean and covariance are estimated
[
Rubinstein and Kroese, 2004
]
. In contrast, we impose a specific form for the sequence of covariance matrices, which in turn determines a sequence of step-sizes in the time-inhomogeneous gradient descent reinterpretation of Algorithm
1
. We also identify Exponential Dispersion Models (EDMs) as a particularly well adapted family of parametric models since their variance can be controlled independently of location. To the best of our knowledge, convergence of the CE method for a general class of objective functions is still lacking while there is empirical evidence that it may not always converge; see, e.g.,
Szita and Lörincz [2006]
in the context of reinforcement learning.
Algorithm 1 in
Ionides et al. [2011]
closely resembles Algorithm
1
in the particular Gaussian case, but generality of the approach and connection to earlier literature seem to have been missed, and the focus is mainly on inference in state-space models.
Ionides et al. [2011]
also provides a convergence analysis under strong assumptions, in particular assuming that the objective is differentiable.

In recent work
[
Osher et al., 2023
,
Tibshirani et al., 2024
]
, a
recursion similar to ours is proposed, albeit with fixed stepsizes. Perspective
of their work is however significantly different. While our algorithm was motivated by Bayes’ rule, the
recursion in the aforementioned papers is obtained by considering a
Gaussian transformation to a Hamilton Jacobi system of partial differential
equations representing the Moreau envelope of a proximal minimisation problem,
and therefore relates to infinitesimal convolutions.
Spokoiny [2023]
proposes an update similar to ours in the Gaussian kernel scenario. However, this is where similarities seem to end as the motivation appears slightly different and the analysis of the properties of the algorithm significantly different, in particular requiring differentiability of
l
and using concentration properties.

Joe Watson (Applied Intelligence Lab of the Oxford Robotics Institute) has pointed out links to several studies in robotics control that illustrate compelling applications of algorithms of the type studied in this work (see, e.g.,
Watson and Peters [2023]
,
Belousov and Peters [2018]
,
Abdolmaleki et al. [2015]
,
Deisenroth et al. [2013]
).

2
Results on gradient descent with smooth approximations

2.1
Overview

In this section we review and extend essential notions and tools required to address the
minimisation of a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, assumed lower-bounded, not
necessarily differentiable, but for which there exists a sequence of
differentiable approximations
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ f_{n}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
, which converges to
f
in a sense to be made precise below. In this
scenario, it is natural to suggest the non-homogeneous gradient descent
algorithm

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\gamma_{n}\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\,,

(8)

where
\left\{\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
with
\gamma_{n}\downarrow 0
,
in the hope that tracking the sequence points in
\left\{\argmin f_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
or
\left\{\mathrm{loc-}\argmin f_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
will lead us to minima or local minima of
f
. As we shall see it is sufficient to focus exposition on global minima, as local minima are global minima of the objective function constrained to a neighbourhood, which will turn out to be sufficient for our purpose. This is however a subtle matter given the generality, as we illustrate below.

To start with, non-differentiable functions
may not have a minimum; see, e.g., the left panel of
Figure

2
.
A weak condition ensuring the existence of minima is that
f
is lower
semi-continuous (Subsection
2.2
).

A second issue is that even when perfect minimisation of
f_{n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
is possible, the intuitive set-limit
\mathrm{Lim}_{n}\left\{\argmin f_{n}\right\}=\argmin f
, properly defined in
[
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, Chatper 4]
, may not hold. This
is illustrated on the right panel of
Figure

2
: this counter-example
shows that we may not have
\mathrm{Lim}_{n}\left\{\argmin f_{n}\right\}=\argmin f
even when
f_{n}

converges uniformly to
f
.
An important point in the present paper is that using smoothed approximations for
minimisation may not work in certain scenarios.

Figure 2
:
Left: example of a function with empty
\argmin
. Right: A sequence of functions
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
(red-to-black color palette) that epi-converges to the function
f
. The picture is inspired by
[
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, Figure 7.7]
. It is clear that here
\argmin f_{n}\subset\argmin f
.

Example 2
.

Take
d=2
and consider the
function

l(\theta)\coloneq\min\{1,|\theta_{1}|\}\mathds{1}\{\theta_{1}=\theta_{2}\}+\mathds{1}\{\theta_{1}\neq\theta_{2}\}\,,

where here
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
are the two components of
\theta
.
This function is such that
\mathrm{Leb}\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\colon l(\theta)<1\}=0
and, as a consequence, for any
\theta\in\Theta
and
\gamma>0
,

l_{\gamma}(\theta)=1
, that is the smoothed functions
\theta\mapsto l_{\gamma}(\theta)
“cannot see the minimum” at zero. In general a requirement
therefore seems to be that for any
\theta_{*}\in\arg\min_{\theta\in\Theta}l(\theta)
,

\lim_{\gamma\downarrow 0}\gamma^{-d/2}\int\exp\left\{-l(x)\right\}\phi\Bigl(\frac{x-\theta_{*}}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\Bigr){\mathrm{d}}x=\exp\left\{-l(\theta_{*})\right\}\,.

(9)

In this counterexample the left hand side is equal to
\exp(-1)
while the
right hand side is equal to
1
.

Epi-convergence of
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
to
f
(properly defined in

Definition

4
) is a
suitable and flexible form of convergence to establish when smoothing techniques
combined with exact optimisation achieve their goal. Ensuring this property is
a natural prerequisite to the justification of the recursion
(
8
) to optimise
f
; essentials of Epi-convergence are covered in Subsection
2.3
where we also provide a first consequence, Theorem
2
, a weaker form of the set limit
\mathrm{Lim}_{n}\left\{\argmin f_{n}\right\}=\argmin f
.

The following result, due to
Ermoliev et al. [1995, Theorem 4.7]
, exemplifies
what one may hope to be able to deduce for the sequence (
8
). Assume that
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ f_{n}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converges to
f
, then

\theta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\ \text{local minimum of }f\ \Rightarrow\ \exists\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}\ \text{s.t.}\ \theta_{n}\to\theta_{*}\ \text{and}\ ||\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})||\to 0\,.

(10)

The practical implication of this result is that if a sequence

\left\{\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
admits a subsequence

\left\{\theta_{n_{k}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
convergent to some

\theta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and such that
\lim_{k}||\nabla f_{n_{k}}(\theta_{n_{k}})||=0
, then
\theta_{*}
is a valid candidate as a
local minimum of

, and accumulations points not satisfying the latter
condition must be rejected.
In Subsection
2.3
we establish
Theorem

3
, a generalization of
Ermoliev et al. [1995, Theorem 4.7]
where we relax their assumptions to cover our scenario. The main result of the present section is Theorem
4
where we establish conditions under which the sequence (
8
) admits a subsequence such that
\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(\theta_{n_{k}})\|\to 0
.

2.2
Lower semi-continuity

To define semi-continuous functions, we must first define lim inf for
functions.

Definition 1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\liminf_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}f(\theta)\coloneq\sup_{\epsilon>0}\left[\inf\left\{f(\theta);\ \theta\in\mathbb{B}(\theta_{0},\epsilon)\right\}\right]

where
\mathbb{B}(\theta_{0},\epsilon)
denotes a closed metric ball with center
\theta_{0}
and radius
\epsilon>0
.

Definition 2

(Lower semi-continuity)
.

[
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, Def. 1.5]

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be

1.

lower semi-continuous (
\mathrm{lsc}
) at
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
if

\liminf_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}f(\theta)\geq f(\theta_{0}).

(11)

2.

lower semi-continuous if the above holds for any
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Remark 1
.

For
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\epsilon>0
,
\inf\left\{f(\theta);\ \theta\in\mathbb{B}(\theta_{0},\epsilon)\right\}\leq f(\theta_{0})
, therefore,

\liminf_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}f(\theta)\leq f(\theta_{0})
; hence condition
(
11
) is equivalent to
\liminf_{\theta\to\theta_{0}}f(\theta)=f(\theta_{0})
.

By definition, any lower semi-continuous, lower-bounded function has a minimum on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Upper semi-continuity can be similarly defined, by replacing
\liminf
with
\limsup
and by reversing the inequality.

Definition 3

(Strong lower semi-continuity)
.

A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be

1.

strongly lower semi-continuous (
slsc
) at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

if it is lower semi-continuous at
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and there
exists a sequence
\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
,

\theta_{n}\to\theta
, with
f
continuous at every
\theta_{n}
, and
f(\theta_{n})\to f(\theta)
.

2.

strongly lower semi-continuous if the above holds for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

In words, strong lower semi-continuity is lower semi-continuity excluding
discontinuities at isolated points. Remark that we do not make any assumption
on smoothness of the function; the class of strongly lower semi-continuous
functions includes indicator functions of closed sets, step functions, ceiling
functions; but also not-everywhere differentiable continuous and discontinuous
(if there are not isolated discontinuity points) functions. Strong upper semi-continuity can be accordingly defined.

Example 3
.

The indicator function
f(\theta)=\mathds{1}\{\theta>0\}

is strongly lower semi-continuous; the indicator function

f(\theta)=\mathds{1}\{\theta\geq 0\}
is not
semi-continuous at
\theta=0
.

Intuitively, we can note that the epigraph of
f
, namely the part of the space above the graph of
f
, is not a closed set, which in
fact precludes lower semi-continuity. See
Appendix

C
for more details.

2.3
Epi-convergence and convergence in minimisation

As discussed in the introduction of this section, epi-convergence is the right
notion to formulate and establish convergence of recursions of the type of
(
8
). We therefore start with some
definitions. A more classical abstract definition in terms of set convergence of function epigraphs can be provided; see for instance
[
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, Chapter 7]
.

Definition 4

(Epi-convergence)
.

A sequence of functions
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

epi-converges to a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
if, for each
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

1.

\liminf_{n}f_{n}(\theta_{n})\geq f(\theta)
for any sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta

2.

\lim_{n}f_{n}(\theta_{n})=f(\theta)
for some sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta
.

Thus, we say that
f
is the epi-limit of
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
.

We can immediately note that if a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is the epi-limit of some sequence
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
, then
f
is necessarily lower semi-continuous.

Example 4
.

The three examples below aim to illustrate how epi-convergence differs from
standard functional limits. Consider
\left\{f_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
where

1.

f_{n}(\theta)=-\exp(-n\theta^{2})
, then the sequence epi-converges to

f(\theta)=-\mathds{1}\{\theta=0\}
. In fact, it converges pointwise to the
same limit. However, epi-convergence generally differs from e.g.
pointwise convergence, as the following example shows.

2.

f_{n}(\theta)=\exp(-n\theta^{2})
, then the sequence epi-converges to

f(\theta)=0
, as in particular
\lim_{n}f_{n}(n^{-\alpha})=0
for

\alpha\in(0,1/2)
; we see here how epi-convergence differs from e.g.
pointwise convergence since
\lim f_{n}(0)=1
here.

3.

f_{n}(\theta)=(-1)^{n}\exp(-n\theta^{2})
, then
f_{n}
does not
epi-converge.

The following theorem highlights two main consequences of epi-convergence,
describing what type of results one can expect about convergence of minima and
infima. Below, we say that a function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is eventually
level-bounded if for each
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the sequence of level-sets

\left\{\mathrm{lev}_{\leq a}f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
, defined in Appendix
C
, is eventually bounded. For
instance, a sequence of eventually lower-bounded functions is eventually
level-bounded.

Theorem 2

(
Rockafellar and Wets [1998]
, Theorem 7.33)
.

Let

1.

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a lower-bounded, lower semi-continuous function,

2.

\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be a sequence of lower semi-continuous functions such that

(a)

\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converges to
f
,

(b)

\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is eventually level-bounded.

Then,

1.

\underset{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\inf}f_{n}(\theta)\to\underset{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\inf}f(\theta)
,

2.

\mathrm{Limsup}_{n}\argmin f_{n}\subset\argmin f
.

Rockafellar and Wets [1998, Chapter 5]
argue that looking for the inclusion (point 2
above), is preferable over the stronger result
\operatorname{Lim}_{n}\left\{\argmin f_{n}\right\}=\argmin f
. Again, refer to
Figure

2
, where
f_{n}
epi-converges
to
f
(and in fact converges uniformly), but the latter does not hold.

We now turn to results characterising local minima
\theta_{*}
of
f
as
accumulation points of sequences
\left\{\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
in
the situation where an epi-convergent and differentiable approximating sequence

\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
exists. In particular the existence
of
\theta_{n}\rightarrow\theta_{*}
is established, for which
\lim_{n}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|=0
. As a consequence for any algorithm producing a sequence

\left\{\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
from which a convergent sequence

\theta_{n_{k}}\rightarrow\theta_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
can be extracted, then, if

\lim_{n_{k}}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(\theta_{n_{k}})\|\neq 0
we reject
\theta_{*}
as a
local minimum.

We begin with two Lemmata that allow us to prove the key result of this section. The following Lemma describes a key differentiability property of the convolution of two functions, one of which is of class
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Lemma 1

(
Rockafellar and Wets [1998]
, Theorem 9.67)
.

Let
f,g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be locally integrable and assume that
g\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then the function
\theta\mapsto\int f(u)g(\theta-u)\mathrm{d}u
belongs to
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with

\nabla\int f(u)g(\theta-u)\mathrm{d}u=\int f(u)\nabla g(\theta-u)\mathrm{d}u,\ \ \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The lemma below describes an important property of convergent sequences that attain
\epsilon
-optimal points of epi-convergent functions.

Lemma 2

(
Attouch [1984]
, Theorem 1.10)
.

Let
D\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
and

1.

\left\{f_{n}:D\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be epi-convergent to
f:D\to\mathbb{R}

2.

\left\{\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be such that
\theta_{n}\to\theta_{*}\in D
and for
\left\{\epsilon_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
with
\epsilon_{n}\downarrow 0

f_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq\underset{\theta\in D}{\inf}f_{n}(\theta)+\epsilon_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\,.

Then,
\theta_{*}\in\underset{\theta\in D}{\argmin}\ f
.

Finally, the following justifies an optimisation procedure for finding local minima of
l
by tracking minimisers of differentiable functions
\left\{l_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
that epi-converge to
l
. The proof of the result is a generalisation of the proof of
[
Ermoliev et al., 1995
, Theorem 4.7]
. While in the latter paper the result is stated for a strongly lower semi-continuous
f
and auxiliary mollifiers with bounded support (implying epi-convergence in their setting), in our statement epi-convergence is put as an assumption of the theorem and the other assumptions are relaxed:
f
is allowed to be just lower semi-continuous and the auxiliary mollifiers to be Gaussian. This extends its validity and widens the class of methods whose convergence can be proven in terms of the Theorem below.

Theorem 3
.

Let

1.

f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be locally integrable, lower bounded and lower semi-continuous,

2.

\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be a sequence of
differentiable functions epi-convergent to
f
.

Then for any
\theta_{*}\in\mathrm{loc-}\argmin f
there exists

\left\{\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
such that
\theta_{k}\to\theta_{*}
and
\lim_{k}||\nabla f_{k}(\theta_{k})||=0
.

Proof.

Let
\theta_{*}
be a local minimiser of
f
, define
\phi(\theta):=f(\theta)+||\theta-\theta_{*}||^{2}
and let
V
be a sufficiently small compact set such that
\theta_{*}\in V
and
\argmin_{\theta\in V}\ \phi(\theta)=\left\{\theta_{*}\right\}
- that is,
\theta_{*}
is the unique global minimiser of
\phi
on
V
. The uniqueness of the minimiser on
V
, ensured by the auxiliary function
\phi
, excludes potential issues arising from working with a locally flat function, and more generally guarantees that a (sub-) sequence converging to
\theta_{*}
as per
Lemma

2
exists, as we are going to illustrate. Consider the sequence of functions
\left\{\psi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
defined by

\psi_{n}(z):=(2\pi\gamma_{n})^{-d/2}\exp\left\{-\frac{||z||^{2}}{2\gamma_{n}}\right\}

and
\left\{\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
such that
\gamma_{n}\downarrow 0
.
From
[
Ermoliev et al., 1995
, Remark 3.14]
we have
\psi_{n}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ \int\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=1
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
, and for every
\delta>0
,

\lim_{n}\int_{\left\{||z||>\delta\right\}}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0,\ \text{and}\ \lim_{n}\int_{||z||>\delta}||z+\theta-\theta_{*}||^{2}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0

uniformly in
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Let
\beta_{n}(\theta,\theta_{*}):=\int||z+\theta-\theta_{*}||^{2}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z
,
n\in\mathbb{N},\ \theta\in V
, and define the auxiliary functions

\phi_{n}(\theta):=f_{n}(\theta)+\beta_{n}(\theta,\theta_{*}),\ n\in\mathbb{N},\ \theta\in V.

Note that

•

the functions
\left\{\beta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
are of class
C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
Lemma

1
,

•

The sequence
\left\{\beta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converge to
\theta\mapsto||\theta-\theta_{*}||^{2}
on
V
due to
[
Ermoliev et al., 1995
, Theorem 3.7 and Remark 3.14]
– in fact, they converge uniformly,

•

By assumption,
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a sequence of differentiable functions that epi-converge to
f
; therefore,
\left\{\phi_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a sequence of well-defined differentiable (hence continuous) functions on
V
that epi-converge to
\phi
on
V
.

Let
\left\{\bar{\theta}_{n}\in V,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be a sequence of minimisers of
\left\{\phi_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
, which exists since for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\phi_{n}
is continuous and
V
a compact set. From the compactness of
V
there exists a convergent subsequence
\left\{\theta_{n}\in V,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
of
\left\{\bar{\theta}_{n}\in V,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
. By
Lemma

2
, it holds that
\theta_{n}\to\theta_{*}
. We now turn to the second statement. For each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle 0=\nabla\phi_{n}(\theta_{n})=\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})+\nabla\beta_{n}(\theta_{n},\theta_{*}).

(12)

and in the limit,

\displaystyle\lim_{n}\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})=-\lim_{n}\nabla\beta_{n}(\theta_{n},\theta_{*})=0.

(13)

which proves the statement.

In order to show the last equality, by continuity of the function
\theta\mapsto||\theta||^{2}
one can use
Lemma

1
and write, for every
k
,

\displaystyle\nabla\beta_{k}(\theta_{k},\theta_{*})

\displaystyle=\nabla\int||z+\theta_{k}-\theta_{*}||^{2}\psi_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\lx@cref{creftypecap~refnum}{lemma: differentiability of averages}}}{=}\int\nabla||z+\theta_{k}-\theta_{*}||^{2}\psi_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int 2(z+\theta_{k}-\theta_{*})\psi_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=2\left\{\int z\psi_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\theta_{k}-\theta_{*}\right\}.

We can now note that, by assumption,
\int z\psi_{k}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0
for
every
k\in\mathbb{N}
; moreover,
\theta_{k}\to\theta_{*}
. Therefore, the
last equality in (
13
) holds.
∎

2.4
Convergence of time-inhomogeneous gradient descent

The following result establishes convergence of time inhomogeneous gradient algorithms to local minima of
l
. This generalizes results such as
Gupal and Norkin [1977]
, not requiring confinement of the sequence
\{\theta_{n},n\in\mathbb{N}\}
for example.

Theorem 4
.

For differentiable functions
\{f_{n}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\{\gamma_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
consider the recursion defined for some
\theta_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and for
n\geq 0

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\gamma_{n}\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\,.

(14)

Let
\alpha\in[0,2]
,
\{L_{n}\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
and
\{\delta_{n}\in[0,\infty),\ n\in\mathbb{N}\}
be such that

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\gamma_{n}L_{n}<1,\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\delta_{n}/\gamma_{n})=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(\delta_{n}/\gamma_{n+1})=0,\quad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\gamma_{n}=\infty.

Assume that the following conditions hold:

1.

\inf_{(n,\theta)\in\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}f_{n}(\theta)>-\infty
.

2.

for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

f_{n}(\theta^{\prime})\leq f_{n}(\theta)+\langle\nabla f_{n}(\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+L_{n}\|\theta^{\prime}-\theta\|^{2}.

3.

for all
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

f_{n+1}(\theta)-f_{n}(\theta)\leq\delta_{n}\big[1+\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta)\|^{\alpha}\big].

4.

One of the following conditions holds (with the convention
0\cdot\infty=0
):

(a)

Condition
3
holds with
\alpha=0
.

(b)

\frac{\delta_{n}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|f_{n+1}(\theta)\|^{\alpha/2}}{\gamma_{n+1}^{\alpha/2}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}\to 0\qquad\mbox{as }n\to\infty.

(c)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{n}\sup_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta)\|^{\alpha}}{\sum_{m=1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}\to 0\qquad\mbox{as }n\to\infty.

(d)

there exists a constant
\beta\in(0,1]
and a sequence
\{\tilde{L}_{n}\in(0,\infty)\colon n\geq 1\}
such that, for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
|f_{n}(\theta)-f_{n}(\theta^{\prime})|\leq\tilde{L}_{n}\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|^{\beta}
and such that

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{n}\tilde{L}_{n+1}^{\frac{\alpha}{2-\beta}}}{\gamma_{n+1}^{\alpha(1-\beta)/(2-\beta)}\sum_{m=1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}\to 0\qquad\mbox{as }n\to\infty.

Then, there exists a subsequence
\left\{\theta_{n_{k}},\ k\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
of
\{\theta_{n},n\geq 1\}
such that
\lim_{k\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla f_{n_{k}}(\theta_{n_{k}})\|=0
.

Proof.

Under Condition
2
and using (
14
), for all
n\geq 1
we have

\begin{split}f_{n}(\theta_{n+1})&\leq f_{n}(\theta_{n})+\langle\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n}),\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n}\rangle+L_{n}\|\theta_{n+1}-\theta_{n}\|^{2}\\
&=f_{n}(\theta_{n})-\gamma_{n}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{2}\big(1-L_{n}\gamma_{n}\big).\end{split}

(15)

Let
n_{1}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\epsilon_{1}\in(0,1)
be such that
1-\gamma_{n}L_{n}\geq\epsilon_{1}
for all
n\geq n_{1}
. Then, for all
n\geq n_{1}
we have, using (
15
) and under
Condition
3
,

\begin{split}f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})&\leq f_{n}(\theta_{n})-\gamma_{n}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{2}\big(1-L_{n}\gamma_{n}\big)+f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})-f_{n}(\theta_{n+1})\\
&\leq f_{n}(\theta_{n})-\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{1}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{2}+\delta_{n}\big(1+\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\|^{\alpha}\big).\end{split}

(16)

We now prove the result of the theorem by contradiction. To this aim, assume that there exists an
\epsilon_{2}\in(0,1)
and an
n_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|\geq\epsilon_{2}
for all
n\geq n_{2}
.

Then, for all
n\geq n_{3}:=\max\{n_{1},n_{2}\}
we have, by (
16
) and letting
\epsilon_{3}=\epsilon_{1}\epsilon_{2}^{2-\alpha}

\displaystyle f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})

\displaystyle\leq f_{n}(\theta_{n})-\epsilon_{3}\gamma_{n}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{\alpha}+\delta_{n}\big(1+\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\|^{\alpha}\big).

and thus, for all
n\geq n_{3}
, we have

\begin{split}f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\leq&f_{n_{3}}(\theta_{n_{3}})-\epsilon_{3}\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\gamma_{m}\|\nabla f_{m}(\theta_{m})\|^{\alpha}\\
&+\bigg(\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\delta_{m}\|\nabla f_{m+1}(\theta_{m+1})\|^{\alpha}\bigg)+\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\delta_{m}\\
\leq&f_{n_{3}}(\theta_{n_{3}})-\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\|\nabla f_{m}(\theta_{m})\|^{\alpha}\big(\epsilon_{3}\gamma_{m}-\delta_{m-1}\big)\\
&+\delta_{n}\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\|^{\alpha}+\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\delta_{m}\,.\end{split}

(17)

To proceed further assume without loss of generality that
n_{3}
is sufficiently large so that, for some
\epsilon_{4}\in(0,1)
, we have
\epsilon_{3}\gamma_{n}-\delta_{n-1}\geq\epsilon_{4}\gamma_{n}
for all
n\geq n_{3}
. Then, using (
17
), for all
n\geq n_{3}
we have

\begin{split}f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})&\leq f_{n_{3}}(\theta_{n_{3}})-\epsilon_{4}\epsilon_{2}^{\alpha}\Big(\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}\Big)+\delta_{n}\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\|^{\alpha}+\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\delta_{m}\\
&=f_{n_{3}}(\theta_{n_{3}})-\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}\bigg(\epsilon_{4}\epsilon_{2}^{\alpha}-\frac{\delta_{n}\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\|^{\alpha}}{\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}-\frac{\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\delta_{m}}{\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}\bigg)\end{split}

(18)

where, under the assumptions of the theorem,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\sum_{m=n_{3}}^{n}\delta_{m}}{\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}=0,\quad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}=\infty.

Therefore, if

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\delta_{n}\|\nabla f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})\|^{\alpha}}{\sum_{m=n_{3}+1}^{n}\gamma_{m}}=0

(19)

then, by (
18
), we have

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n+1}(\theta_{n+1})=-\infty
which contradicts
Condition
1
. Hence, to complete the proof it remains to show that (
19
) holds under the assumption of the theorem.

Remark first that (
19
) trivially holds under Condition
4a

and under Condition
4c
. Next, remark that, by (
15
), for
all
n\geq n_{3}
we have

\displaystyle f_{n}(\theta_{n+1})\leq f_{n}(\theta_{n})-\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{1}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{2}\Leftrightarrow\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{\alpha}\leq\bigg(\frac{f_{n}(\theta_{n})-f_{n}(\theta_{n+1})}{\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

(20)

from which we readily obtain that (
19
) holds under Condition
4b
.

Finally, by (
20
), for all
n\geq n_{3}
we have, under
Condition
4d
and using (
14
),

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{\alpha}

\displaystyle\leq\bigg(\frac{\tilde{L}_{n}\|\theta_{n}-\theta_{n+1}\|^{\beta})}{\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}=\bigg(\frac{\tilde{L}_{n}\gamma_{n}^{\beta}\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{\beta}}{\gamma_{n}\epsilon_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{\alpha(1-\beta/2)}\leq\bigg(\frac{\tilde{L}_{n}}{\gamma^{1-\beta}_{n}\epsilon_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{\alpha}{2}}\Leftrightarrow\|\nabla f_{n}(\theta_{n})\|^{\alpha}\leq\bigg(\frac{\tilde{L}_{n}}{\gamma^{1-\beta}_{n}\epsilon_{1}}\bigg)^{\frac{\alpha}{2-\beta}}

and thus (

3
Laplace functionals

We study epi-convergence of sequences of Laplace functionals based on certain kernels possessing a concentration property (called mollifiers below), of which the Gaussian density is a particular case. We also provide a new descent lemma which is based on the definition and construction of Laplace functionals.

3.1
Epi-convergence of Laplace functionals

We begin with the definition of
mollifiers.

Definition 5

(mollifiers)
.

Let

\left\{\psi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\ \ \psi_{n}\in C^{1}(\mathbb{R}^{d}),\ \ \int\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=1,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

be a sequence of functions such that for every
\delta>0
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{||z||>\delta}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0.

We call such
\psi_{n}
mollifiers.

Gaussian mollifiers are defined as
\psi_{n}(z)=\gamma_{n}^{-d/2}\phi(\gamma_{n}^{-1/2}z)
, with
\gamma_{n}\downarrow 0
, and
\phi
is the
standard Gaussian density.

Theorem

5
shows epi-convergence of mollifier-based Laplace functionals, namely of sequences
\left\{f_{n};n\geq 0\right\}
of the form

\displaystyle f_{n}(\theta)=-\log\int e^{-l(x)}\psi_{n}(x-\theta)\mathrm{d}x,\quad\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

(21)

Remark 2
.

In the Gaussian scenario, mollifiers in (
21
) can be written as

\psi_{n}(x-\theta)=\pi_{\theta,\gamma_{n}}(x)\coloneq\gamma_{n}^{-d/2}\phi\left(\gamma_{n}^{-1/2}\left(x-\theta\right)\right)\,,

for
x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\gamma_{n}>0
, which links to the notation in the Introduction and other sections. Here, notation
\psi_{n}
is used to highlight the fact that sequences of densities with such concentration property could also include kernels which do not belong to exponential families, as for instance the uniform kernels in
Gupal and Norkin [1977]
.

It is useful for later calculations to note that by a change of variable

z=x-\theta
we can equivalently write

f_{n}(\theta)=-\log\int e^{-f(\theta+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z.

The proof of
Theorem

5
is inspired by ideas of

Ermoliev et al. [1995, Theorems 3.2, 3.7 and Corollary 3.3]
but it extends their scope.

Theorem 5
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a lower-bounded, strongly lower semi-continuous function. Let
\left\{\psi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
be mollifiers.
Let
f_{n}(\theta)=-\log\int e^{-f(\theta+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
n=1,2,\ldots
. Then, the sequence
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converges to
f
.

Proof.

Fix
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We remark that by
Proposition

4
, for a
lower-bounded, integrable function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the epi-closure

\mathrm{cl_{e}}f
is a lower semi-continuous function and the hypo-closure

\mathrm{cl_{h}}f
is an upper semi-continuous function. Moreover, it holds

\mathrm{cl_{e}}f\leq f\leq\mathrm{cl_{h}}f
. Note also that if a function

f
is lower-bounded and strongly lower semi-continuous, then

e^{-f(\cdot)}
is upper bounded and strongly upper semi-continuous. We break the proof into three steps.

•

Define
g:=e^{-f}
and
g_{n}(\cdot):=\int e^{-f(\cdot+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z
. Let
\theta_{n}\to\theta
. As first step, we show that

\mathrm{cl_{e}}g(\theta)\leq\liminf_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq\limsup_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq\mathrm{cl_{h}}g(\theta)

(22)

by only using that
f
is lower-bounded and that
\left\{\psi_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a sequence of mollifiers.

Fix
\epsilon>0
.

–

By upper semi-continuity of
\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f}
, there exists
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)>0
such that

\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f(\theta+z)}\leq\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f(\theta)}+\epsilon

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
||z||\leq\delta
.

–

For the above
\delta
, by definition of the mollifiers
\left\{\psi_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
, we can choose
n=n(\epsilon,\delta)
large enough such that, for all
n\geq n(\epsilon,\delta)
,

0\leq\int_{||z||>\frac{\delta}{2}}e^{-f(\theta+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\underset{u\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\sup}e^{-f(u)}\int_{||z||\geq\frac{\delta}{2}}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z\leq\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

(23)

We first show the last inequality in equation (
22
). Let
\delta=\delta(\epsilon)
as above. For all
n\geq n(\epsilon,\delta)
large enough such that
||\theta_{n}-\theta||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
, we have
||\theta_{n}-\theta+z||\leq\delta
for any
z
such that
||z||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}
. So we can write

\displaystyle g_{n}(\theta_{n})

\displaystyle=\int e^{-f(\theta_{n}+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{||z||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}}e^{-f(\theta_{n}+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\int_{||z||>\frac{\delta}{2}}e^{-f(\theta_{n}+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z

\displaystyle\leq\int_{||z||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}}\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f(\theta_{n}+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle=\int_{||z||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}}\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f(\theta+\theta_{n}-\theta+z)}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f(\theta)}+\epsilon)\int_{||z||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

Hence, for
n
large enough we have

\displaystyle g_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq\mathrm{cl_{h}}e^{-f(\theta)}+\epsilon+\frac{\epsilon}{2}

so for any
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq\mathrm{cl_{h}}g(\theta)+\frac{3}{2}\epsilon.

Therefore, by taking
\epsilon\to 0
, we obtain

\limsup_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq\mathrm{cl_{h}}g(\theta).

(24)

The middle inequality in (
22
) is obvious, while the first can be proven in a very similar way, using positivity from equation (
23
) and the fact that, for
n
large-enough,
\int_{||z||\leq\frac{\delta}{2}}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z\geq 1-\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.

•

The next step is to show that
g_{n}
hypo-converges to
g
. Here we use strong lower semi-continuity of
f
.

–

As
g=e^{-f}
is upper semi-continuous, it holds that
g=\mathrm{cl_{h}}g
by
Proposition

4
. Hence, by equation (
24
), for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and any sequence
\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
with
\theta_{n}\to\theta
, we have

\limsup_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})\leq g(\theta).

–

It remains to show that for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\lim_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})=g(\theta)
for at least one sequence such that
\theta_{n}\to\theta
. Here we use
Lemma

4
.

•

We finally show that
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converges to
f
, based on the above results, continuity and monotonicity of
\log
, and on the fact that, by definition, if
\left\{f_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
is a sequence of functions that hypo-converge to
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, then
\left\{-f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converge to
-f
.
In detail: by continuity and monotonicity of
\log(\cdot)
and by hypo-convergence of
g_{n}
to
g
, we can first show that
\left\{\log(g_{n}),\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
hypo-converges to
\log(g)=-f
. Indeed, the following chain of inequalities holds for any sequence
\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
with
\theta_{n}\to\theta
:

\displaystyle\limsup_{n}\log\left(g_{n}(\theta_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n}\left(\sup_{m\geq n}\log(g_{m}(\theta_{m}))\right)\leq\lim_{n}\log\left(\sup_{m\geq n}g_{m}(\theta_{m})\right)

\displaystyle=\log\left(\lim_{n}\left(\sup_{m\geq n}g_{m}(\theta_{m})\right)\right)=\log\left(\limsup_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})\right)

\displaystyle\leq\log(e^{-f(\theta)})=-f(\theta)

Hence the first condition for hypo-convergence of

\left\{\log(g_{n}),\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
to
\log(g)=-f
holds. For the second condition, we just use hypo-convergence of
\left\{g_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
to
g
and continuity of
\log
. Note that the above chain of inequalities would hold for any non-decreasing continuous transformation. Finally, as

, we conclude that
\left\{f_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
epi-converge to
f
.

∎

uniformly in

is lower-bounded.

Lemma 4
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a lower-bounded, strongly lower semi-continuous function. Let

\left\{\psi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}_{+},\ \int\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=1,\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

be a sequence of functions such that, for any

\lim_{n}\int_{||z||\geq\delta}\psi_{n}(z)\mathrm{d}z=0.
Let

and

,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

. Then, for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, there is at least one sequence
\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

\lim_{n}g_{n}(\theta_{n})=g(\theta).

Proof.

The proof is given in
[
Ermoliev et al., 1995
, Theorem 3.7]
. Let
\bar{g}:=-g
and
\bar{g}_{n}:=-g_{n}
. We already now that
\bar{g}

\liminf_{n}\bar{g}_{n}(\theta_{n})\geq\bar{g}(\theta)
for all sequences
\left\{\theta_{n},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
such that
\theta_{n}\to\theta

We show
\lim_{n}\bar{g}_{n}(\theta_{n})=\bar{g}(\theta)
for at least one sequence
\theta_{n}\to\theta

\bar{g}

such that

, with
\bar{g}
continuous at
\theta_{k}

3
states that under (local) continuity we have that, for all

\lim_{n}\bar{g}_{n}(\theta_{k})=\bar{g}(\theta_{k})

(25)

Now note that set

3.2
Descent lemma for Laplace functionals

We show that a crucial convexity property from the theory of exponential families allows us to derive a descent lemma for Laplace functionals.
The descent lemma is stated here in full generality, namely for Laplace functionals based on smoothing kernels in the exponential family. This is a result of independent interest, and it also enforces the validity of a descent lemma in the more general scenarios mentioned in Subsection
1.3
. The result for the Gaussian algorithm is then recovered as a simple by-product. The derivation of the lemma connects the exponential family framework to the idea of
relative smoothness
presented in
Bolte et al. [2018]
and
Lu et al. [2017]
.

Definition 6

(Bregman divergence)
.

Let
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a differentiable function. The Bregman divergence
\mathrm{D}_{u}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
associated with
u
is defined as

\mathrm{D}_{u}(x,y)=u(x)-u(y)-\langle\nabla u(y),x-y\rangle,\ x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Properties
.

For any
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

1.

For any pair of differentiable functions
u_{1},u_{2}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\mathrm{D}_{\alpha u_{1}+\beta u_{2}}(x,y)=\alpha\mathrm{D}_{u_{1}}(x,y)+\beta\mathrm{D}_{u_{2}}(x,y).

2.

For any differentiable, convex function
u:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, we also have that

\mathrm{D}_{u}(x,y)\geq 0

with
\mathrm{D}_{u}(x,y)=0
iff
x=y
.

We can now state the general descent lemma.

Theorem 6

(Descent lemma)
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and for
\gamma>0
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

consider an exponential model (see
Section

B.1
)

\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x):=\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-A(\theta)\right]\right\}\upsilon_{\gamma}(x)

with sufficient statistic
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, log-partition function
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
and baseline probability measure
\upsilon_{\gamma}
. Let

f_{\gamma}(\theta):=-\log\int\exp(-f(x))\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x)\mathrm{d}x,\ \theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

and assume
\int\exp(-f(x))\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

f_{\gamma}(\theta^{\prime})\leq f_{\gamma}(\theta)+\langle\nabla f_{\gamma}(\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta^{\prime},\theta),

(26)

Proof.

Let
x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
\gamma>0
. Consider the exponential model from the assumption

\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x)=\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-A(\theta)\right]\right\}\upsilon_{\gamma}(x)

and let

\tilde{\pi}_{\theta,\gamma}(x)\propto\exp\big(-f(x)\big)\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x).

Note that

\tilde{\pi}_{\theta,\gamma}(x)=\exp\big(-f(x)\big)\exp\left\{\frac{1}{\gamma}\left[\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-A(\theta)\right]+f_{\gamma}(\theta)\right\}\upsilon_{\gamma}(x)\ ,

that is, the distribution
\tilde{\pi}_{\theta,\gamma}
still belongs to the regular (in the sense of
Definition

8
) exponential family, with log-partition function given by

\tilde{A}(\theta)=A(\theta)-\gamma f_{\gamma}(\theta).

The Bregman divergence
\mathrm{D}_{\tilde{A}}
is well-defined as both
\theta\mapsto A(\theta)
and
\theta\mapsto f_{\gamma}(\theta)
are differentiable functions.
By
[
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
, Proposition 3.1]
,
\tilde{A}(\theta)\ \text{is convex}
. From the properties of the Bregman divergence, we note that the convexity of
\tilde{A}
implies that for all
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

\mathrm{D}_{\tilde{A}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)\geq 0.

(27)

By the linearity property, we have

0\leq\mathrm{D}_{\tilde{A}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\mathrm{D}_{A-\gamma f_{\gamma}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)=\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)-\gamma\mathrm{D}_{f_{\gamma}}(\theta^{\prime},\theta)

and using the definition of the Bregman Divergence, one obtains

\gamma\left\{f_{\gamma}(\theta^{\prime})-f_{\gamma}(\theta)-\langle\nabla f_{\gamma}(\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle\right\}\leq\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta^{\prime},\theta).

That is,

f_{\gamma}(\theta^{\prime})\leq f_{\gamma}(\theta)+\langle\nabla f_{\gamma}(\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta^{\prime},\theta),

which concludes the proof.
∎

In the Gaussian case, as a corollary, we recover a standard descent lemma in terms of the Euclidean distance.

Corollary 1
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. For
\gamma>0
, let
f_{\gamma}(\theta):=-\log\int\exp(-f(x))\phi\left(\frac{x-\theta}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)\mathrm{d}x
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and assume
\int\exp(-f(x))\phi\left(\frac{x-\theta}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
for any
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then, for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, it holds that

f_{\gamma}(\theta^{\prime})\leq f_{\gamma}(\theta)+\langle\nabla f_{\gamma}(\theta),\theta^{\prime}-\theta\rangle+\frac{1}{2\gamma}||\theta^{\prime}-\theta||^{2}.

(28)

Proof.

This follows directly from
Theorem

6
by noting that, for
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(\cdot):=\gamma^{-d/2}\phi\left(\frac{\cdot-\theta}{\sqrt{\gamma}}\right)
, one has
A(\theta)=\frac{||\theta||^{2}}{2}
and
\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta,\theta^{\prime})=\frac{1}{2}||\theta^{\prime}-\theta||^{2}
, for any
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

If

&\geq e^{-l(\theta)}e^{-C_{l}}(1+2C_{l}\gamma_{1})^{-1/2}\end{split}

(32)

where the last inequality uses the fact that the sequence
\{\gamma_{n},\,n\in\mathbb{N}\}
is assumed to be non-increasing.

By combining (
31
) and (
32
), we obtain

\displaystyle l_{n+1}(\theta)-l_{n}(\theta)

\displaystyle\leq\bar{C}\delta_{n},\quad\bar{C}=C^{\prime}e^{C_{l}}(1+2C_{l}\gamma_{1})^{1/2}

and the proof of the lemma is complete.

∎

Implementation

for

through importance

in

To reduce the variability of the output, we may also use randomised quasi-Monte

algorithm below. We observe that it works well even for low values of

Since minimising

5.2
AUC scoring and classification

We illustrate our methodology on a staple machine learning scoring and
classification task. Given training data
\{(Z_{i},Y_{i})\in\mathbb{R}^{p}\times\{-1,1\}\colon i=1,\ldots,n_{\mathrm{data}}\}
, assumed to
arise from a probability distribution
\mathbb{P}
, we wish to construct a
score function
s\colon\mathbb{R}^{p}\rightarrow\{-1,1\}
, such that for two
independent realisations
(Z,Y)
and
(Z^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
the theoretical quantity

\mathbb{P}\big(\left[s(Z)-s(Z^{\prime})\right](Y-Y^{\prime})<0\big)

is as small as possible. This quantity
is often called the area under curve (AUC) risk function; one of the
motivations for this criterion is that it is less sensitive to class imbalance
than other more standard classification criteria.

Assuming further a particular parametric form for
s(z)
, e.g.
s(z)=s_{\theta}(z)=\theta^{\top}z
for

\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

Clémençon et al. [2008]
proposed to estimate
x
through empirical risk
minimisation, i.e.

\hat{\theta}=\arg\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{p}}u(x)\,,

where
u(\theta)
is the following U-statistic:

u(\theta)=\frac{1}{n_{\mathrm{data}}(n_{\mathrm{data}}-1)}\sum_{i,j=1}^{n_{\mathrm{data}}}\mathbf{1}\left\{\big[s_{\theta}(Z_{i})-s_{\theta}(Z_{j})\big](Y_{i}-Y_{j})<0\right\}.

(33)

This function is challenging to minimise directly, for two reasons:
(a) it is piecewise constant and therefore discontinuous; (b) it is
invariant by affine transformations for the linear model, i.e.
u(\lambda\theta)=u(\theta)

for any scalar
\lambda
.
As a result, several alternative approaches have been proposed to perform
AUC scoring; e.g. one may replace it with a convex approximation
[
Clémençon et al., 2008
, Sect. 7]

or use a PAC-Bayesian approach as in
Ridgway et al. [2014]
.

Regarding point (b), we use the inverse
\psi^{-1}
of the stereographic projection, which
transforms a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
into a point
\theta=\psi^{-1}(x)
on the
unit hyper-sphere in dimension
p
,
\mathbb{S}^{p}=\{\theta\in R^{p},\|\theta\|=1\}
. That is,
if
\theta=\psi^{-1}(x)
, then

\theta_{i}=2x_{i}/\sum_{j=1}^{d}x_{j}^{2}
, for
i=1,\dots,p-1
,

\theta_{p}=(\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}-1)/(\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}+1)
.
Then we define the objective function to be
l(x)=u\circ\psi^{-1}(x)
; thus
l:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}

with
d=p-1
. It is reasonably straightforward to show that this function is strongly
lower semi-continuous, as
\psi^{-1}
is Lipschitz, and
u
is a sum of indicator functions.

We consider two classical datasets from the UCI machine learning repository
(
https://archive.ics.uci.edu/
): Pima (short for Pima Indians Diabetes),
and Sonar. We pre-process the data so that each predictor is normalised, i.e.
the empirical mean is set to zero and the variance is set to one. We compare
our algorithm to a strategy often used in practice which relies on the
Nelder-Mead, or simplex, algorithm with random start. This approach is
considered naive in that Nelder-Mead does not require differentiability for
implementation, but is a requirement for correctness. The stepsizes are set to

\gamma_{n}=0.2/(1+n)^{0.5}
. We run the two algorithms
10
times.

In
Figure

6
Discussion

We briefly discuss possible extensions and variations we are
currently exploring or have not explored yet.

6.1
Accounting for noise

We have established the convergence of the
ideal

Algorithm

1
; what remains to be done is doing the same for the
practical variant where the distributions
\pi_{n}
and
\tilde{\pi}_{n}
are
replaced by Monte Carlo approximations.

Another way to account for noise is to consider the scenario where the objective function
can be evaluated only up to some noise. That is, one cannot evaluate
l
exactly, but one can
evaluate a random estimate
\ell(x,U)
such that
\mathbb{E}[\ell(x,U)]=l(x)
, where
U

has a certain distribution. In this case, the ideal Alg.
1
can be easily adapted by considering, at each iteration
n\geq 0
, the objective function
\ell(x,U_{n})
, where
U_{n}
has been sampled (once) at iteration
n
,
from the distribution of
U
. Accordingly, its practical implementations presented in Appendix
E
can be adapted by
replacing the instruction for computing the weights with:
w_{n}^{i}\leftarrow\exp\{-\ell(X_{n}^{i},U_{n})\}
(thus, the
N
evaluations at iteration
n
are
performed using the same random variable
U_{n}
).

We are currently developing theory for this type of noisy optimisation
scenario. In the stochastic framework the algorithm proceeds as in
Algorithm

1

and the sequence
\{\theta_{n},n\geq 1\}
is now the output of a
time inhomogeneous stochastic gradient algorithm

\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\gamma_{n}\nabla_{\theta}\left\{-\log\int e^{-\ell(x,U_{n})}\phi\biggl(\frac{x-\theta_{n}}{\sqrt{\gamma_{n}}}\biggr)\mathrm{d}x\right\},\ U_{n},\ n\geq 1\overset{{\mathrm{i}id}}{\sim}P\,,

here presented in the Gaussian scenario.

6.2
A Proximal version

For completeness, we discuss here a variation on Algorithm
1
which can be shown to be equivalent to a time-inhomogeneous proximal minimisation recursion. A natural modification of this algorithm consists of inverting the order of the distributions in the minimisation step of the
\mathrm{KL}
divergence; see Algorithm
2
.

Algorithm 2

Proximal version

objective function
l
, initial parameter
\theta_{0}
, stepsizes

(\gamma_{n})_{n\geq 0}
.

\pi_{0}\leftarrow\pi_{\theta_{0},\gamma_{0}}

while

n\geq 0

do

\tilde{\pi}_{n+1}(x)\propto\exp\left\{-l(x)\right\}\pi_{n}(x)

\theta_{n+1}\in\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\mathcal{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_{\theta,\gamma_{n}},\tilde{\pi}_{n+1})

\triangleright
Swap distributions in KL (compared to
Algorithm

1
)

\pi_{n+1}\leftarrow\pi_{\theta_{n+1},\gamma_{n+1}}

end

while

sequence of distributions
\tilde{\pi}_{n}
and parameters
\theta_{n}
.

Link to proximal minimisation can be derived via an argument analogous to that in
Lemma

7
, which demonstrates that Algorithm
2
generates a sequence
\left\{\theta_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}
such that

\displaystyle\theta_{n+1}=\argmin_{\theta}\left\{F_{\gamma_{n}}(\theta)+\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta,\gamma_{n}},\pi_{\theta_{n},\gamma_{n}})\right\},\ n\in\mathbb{N},

where
F_{\gamma}(\theta):=\int l(x)\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x)\mathrm{d}x
,
\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\gamma>0
. We see that the algorithm aims to adjust
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}
to decrease
\theta\mapsto F_{\gamma}(\theta)
subject to a proximal penalty, which is reminiscent of proximal Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithms. When
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}
is a Gaussian,
\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta,\gamma_{n}},\pi_{\theta_{n},\gamma_{n}})=(2\gamma_{n})^{-1}||\theta-\theta_{n}||^{2}
, and interpretation in terms of an inhomogeneous (vanilla) proximal minimisation algorithm should be clear. Note that, however, in the general case of exponential families we have that for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,

\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta,\gamma_{n}},\pi_{\theta_{n},\gamma_{n}})
is the Bregman divergence
\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta_{n},\theta)
(see

Lemma

6
), convex in the first variable but not
necessarily in the second. Hence Algorithm
2
above cannot always be interpreted as a proximal algorithm.
Despite its theoretical attractiveness we have not pursued this proximal approach here since its implementation seems to require another minimisation procedure, in contrast with our approach. Finally we remark that Algorithm
1
can be interpreted as a coordinate descent algorithm to optimise the functional

\Phi_{1}(\nu,\theta;\gamma)=\int l(x)\nu(x){\rm d}x+{\rm KL}(\nu,\pi_{\theta,\gamma})
, with
\nu
a probability density, while Algorithm
2
corresponds to
\Phi_{2}(\nu,\theta;\gamma)=\int l(x)\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(x){\rm d}x+{\rm KL}(\pi_{\theta,\gamma},\nu)
, where we notice that optimising
\nu
for
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}
fixed leads to
\nu=\pi_{\theta,\gamma}
, therefore agreeing with the interpretation above.

Appendix B
Exponential family background

B.1
Natural exponential families

For
x,\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a baseline density
\upsilon

on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with respect to some dominating measure, e.g., Lebesgue), we
consider a (natural) exponential family to be a family of probability
densities

\pi_{\theta}(x)=\exp\left\{\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-A(\theta)\right\}\upsilon(x)\,,

where the cumulant (log-partition) function for
T(X)
is

A(\theta)=\log\int\exp\left\{\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle\right\}\upsilon(x)\mathrm{d}x\,,

(34)

and the canonical parameter
\theta
of interest belongs to the set

\Theta:=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\ A(\theta)<\infty\right\}.

Definition 7
.

Given an exponential family with sufficient statistic
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we
say that the family is minimal if the elements of
T
are linearly
independent, that is, if there is no nonzero vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
s.t.

\sum_{i=1}^{d}a_{i}T_{i}(x)
is equal to a constant almost
everywhere. This implies that there is a unique natural parameter vector

\theta
associated with each distribution.

Definition 8
.

An exponential family with log-partition function
\theta\mapsto A(\theta)
is said to be regular when the domain
\Theta
is an open set.

Examples of minimal and regular exponential families include Bernoulli, Gaussian, Exponential, Poisson, and Beta distributions.

Proposition 1
.

[
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
, Proposition 3.1]
.
The log-partition function (
34
)
associated with any regular exponential family with sufficient statistic

T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
has the following properties:

1.

It has derivatives of all orders on its domain
\Theta
. Furthermore,

\displaystyle\nabla A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[T(X)\right],

\displaystyle\nabla^{2}A(\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{var}_{\pi_{\theta}}\left[T(X)\right].

2.

\theta\mapsto A(\theta)
is a convex function on
\Theta
, and strictly convex if the representation is minimal.

The convexity argument comes from the fact that the full Hessian
\nabla^{2}A(\theta)
is the covariance matrix of the random vector
T(X)
, and so is positive semidefinite on the open set
\Theta
, which ensures convexity.

We now report an important dual coupling property of exponential families. Let

\mathcal{M}:=\left\{\mu\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\ \exists\ \theta\ \text{s.t.}\ \mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}T(X)=\mu\right\}

be the set of so-called moment parameters. We have

Proposition 2
.

[
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
, Proposition 3.2]

The gradient mapping
\nabla A:\Theta\to\mathcal{M}
is one-to-one if and only if the exponential representation is minimal.

Theorem 7
.

[
Wainwright and Jordan, 2008
, Theorem 3.3]

In a minimal exponential family, the gradient map
\nabla A
is onto the interior of
\mathcal{M}
, denoted by
\mathcal{M}^{o}
. Consequently, for each
\mu\in\mathcal{M}^{o}
, there exists some
\theta=\theta(\mu)\in\Theta
such that
\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta}}T(X)=\mu
.

We conclude with the following relation for the
\mathrm{KL}
and Bregman divergence in the case of exponential families. A more complete statement which also includes the so-called dual function of
A
and the dual parameters is also available. See for instance
[
Nielsen and Nock, 2010
, Section 4]
for more details.

Lemma 6
.

Nielsen and Nock [2010]
.

Assume
\pi_{\theta_{1}}
and
\pi_{\theta_{2}}
belong the same minimal regular exponential family with log-partition function
A
. Suppose
\theta_{1}
,
\theta_{2}
are their natural parameters. We have

\mathrm{D}_{A}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})=\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\theta_{1}},\pi_{\theta_{2}}).

(35)

B.2
Exponential dispersion models

Consider an exponential family, with base measure
\upsilon
, canonical
statistic
T:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,
and cumulant (log-partition) function

. We may extend this family by
considering an EDM (exponential dispersion model) family
as follows

Consider values
\gamma\in\Gamma\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}

\gamma^{-1}A(\theta)
is the cumulant function of some (

\upsilon_{\gamma}

\pi_{\theta,\gamma}^{\star}(x)\coloneq\exp\left\{\langle\theta,T(x)\rangle-\gamma^{-1}A(\theta)\right\}\upsilon_{\gamma}(x)\,.

If we assume

\displaystyle\mu(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi_{\theta,\gamma}}\left(X\right)=\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta)

therefore implying concentration of
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}

on

as

.
It is then possible to use the corresponding EDM within

The general case (

probability distribution for variable

how to derive an EDM family from Wishart distributions.

B.3
EDM families and Wishart distributions

Let
\Sigma

\nu>d-1

is a distribution defined

on

Proposition 3
.

Let

0<\gamma=\nu^{-1}

Proof.

Applying the change of variable to (

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(X)

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}X\big)-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\right\}\gamma^{-\frac{d(\nu-d-1)}{2}}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{2^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}\times\nu^{d(d+1)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}\big(\Sigma^{-1}X\big)-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\right\}\gamma^{-\frac{d(\nu-d-1)}{2}}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{2^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}\times\gamma^{-d(d+1)/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\langle\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}2^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\langle-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\gamma^{-1}\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{(2\gamma)^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{(2\gamma)^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}.

where the term
\nu^{d(d+1)/2}
on the first row is the Jacobian coming from rescaling of the Lebesgue measure after the change of variable. Now note that

\displaystyle-\log|\Sigma|=\log|\Sigma^{-1}|=\log(2^{d})+\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|

and

e^{-\frac{1}{2\gamma}\log(2^{d})}=\left(\frac{1}{2^{d}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2\gamma}}=\frac{1}{2^{\frac{d}{2\gamma}}}.

Using the above identities one can write

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\frac{1}{2}\log|\Sigma|\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{(2\gamma)^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|\right)\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{\frac{1}{2^{d/(2\gamma)}}(2\gamma)^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}

\displaystyle=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|\right)\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}

where the last equality holds as
\nu=\gamma^{-1}
.

Letting
\theta:=-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}
, we see that for
A(\theta):=-\frac{1}{2}\log|-\theta|
one can also write

\displaystyle\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1},X\rangle_{F}-\left(-\frac{1}{2}\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|\right)\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle\theta,X\rangle_{F}-A(\theta)\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}

This shows that under the rescaling we have

\displaystyle\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(X)=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle\theta,X\rangle_{F}-A(\theta)\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\nu-d-1}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{\nu d}{2}}\Gamma_{d}(\nu/2)}=\frac{\exp\left\{\gamma^{-1}\left[\langle\theta,X\rangle_{F}-A(\theta)\right]\right\}|X|^{\frac{\gamma^{-1}-d-1}{2}}}{\gamma^{\frac{d}{2\gamma}}\Gamma_{d}(1/2\gamma)}.

For the last statement, use the property that for any positive symmetric matrix
B

\nabla_{B}\log|B|=(B^{-1})^{T}

to note that (using
\theta=-\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}
and symmetry)

\displaystyle\nabla_{\theta}A(\theta)=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}\log|-\theta|=-\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\theta}\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{-\theta}\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\nabla_{\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}}\log|\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\left(\frac{1}{2}\Sigma^{-1}\right)^{-1}\right]^{T}

\displaystyle=\Sigma.

∎

Note that
\pi_{\theta,\gamma}(X)
can be viewed as an EDM family, with parameter
(\theta,\gamma)
.
Using standard properties of Wishart distributions, we have

\mathbb{E}[X]=\Sigma
,
\operatorname{var}[X_{i,j}]=\gamma(\Sigma_{ij}^{2}+\Sigma_{ii}\Sigma{jj})
, and concentration occurs as
\gamma\to 0
.
The important point here is that it is the family of
rescaled

Wishart distributions (i.e., distributions for
X=\gamma Y=Y/\nu
)
which may be viewed as an EDM, not the original family.
In this
case, not all values of
\gamma
are permitted; i.e. since
\gamma=1/\nu
and

\nu>d-1
, one has
\gamma<1/(d-1)
, but that does not pose any practical
problem.

Appendix C
Lower-semicontinuity and epigraphs

Lower semi-continuity can be also defined as a property of certain sets. We report this characterisation here.

Definition 9
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function. We denote

1.

The epigraph set of
f
by

\mathrm{epi}(f):=\left\{(\theta,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R};\ f(\theta)\leq a\right\}.

2.

The hypograph set of
f
by

\mathrm{hypo}(f):=\left\{(\theta,a)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R};\ f(\theta)\geq a\right\}.

3.

The level sets of
f
by

\mathrm{lev}_{\leq\alpha}(f):=\left\{\theta\in\mathbb{R}^{d};\ f(\theta)\leq\alpha\right\},\ \ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}.

The following theorem characterises lower semi-continuity in terms of epigraphs and level-sets. A similar result can be stated in terms of upper semi-continuity and hypographs.

Theorem 8
.

[
Rockafellar and Wets, 1998
, Theorem 1.6]

For a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, the following statements are equivalent

•

f
is lower semi-continuous on
\mathbb{R}^{d}

•

Its epigraph set
\mathrm{epi}(f)

is closed in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}

•

Level sets
\mathrm{lev}_{\leq a}

are closed in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, for each
a\in\mathbb{R}
.

We report two additional examples which constitute a relevant theoretical tool in this manuscript. More details on the quantities below can be found in
Rockafellar and Wets [1998, Chapter 1, Section D]
.

Definition 10
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. We define the epigraphical closure of
f
as the function

\theta\mapsto\mathrm{cl_{e}}\ f(\theta):=\inf_{\theta_{k}\to\theta}\liminf_{k}f(\theta_{k})

and the hypographical closure
f
as

\theta\mapsto\mathrm{cl_{h}}f(\theta):=\sup_{\theta_{k}\to\theta}\limsup_{k}f(\theta_{k}).

Proposition 4
.

Consider a function
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
. Then,

•

\mathrm{cl_{e}}f
is lower semi-continuous with
\mathrm{cl_{e}}f(\theta)\leq f(\theta)

•

\mathrm{cl_{h}}f
is upper semi-continuous and
\mathrm{cl_{h}}f(\theta)\geq f(\theta)
.

Moreover,

•

When
f
is lower semi-continuous,
f(\theta)=\mathrm{cl_{e}}f(\theta)

•

When
f
is upper semi-continuous,
f(\theta)=\mathrm{cl_{h}}f(\theta)
.

Appendix D
Laplace Principle

The Laplace Principle is a known result which provides a variational representation of integrals of the form
-\log\int e^{-l(x)}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x
, where
\pi
is a probability density and
l
is an integrable function.

Lemma 7

(Laplace Principle)
.

Let
\nu,\pi
be two probability measures with
\nu\ll\pi
. Let
l:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a locally integrable function such that
\int e^{-l(x)}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x<\infty
and set
\tilde{\pi}\propto e^{-l}\pi
to be a probability measure. It holds that

\mathrm{KL}(\nu,\tilde{\pi})=\int l(x)\nu(x)\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{KL}(\nu,\pi)+\log\int e^{-l(x)}\pi(x)\mathrm{d}x.

(38)

A well-known consequence is

\tilde{\pi}=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathbb{M}^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\int l(x)\nu(x)\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{KL}(\nu,\pi)\right\}.

(39)

Proof.

By direct calculations, we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\nu,\tilde{\pi})

\displaystyle=\int\nu(x)\log\left(\frac{\nu(x)\pi(e^{-l})}{e^{-l(x)}\pi(x)}\right)\mathrm{d}x=

\displaystyle=\int\nu(x)\log\left(\frac{\nu(x)}{e^{-l(x)}\pi(x)}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\log\pi(e^{-l})

\displaystyle=\int\nu(x)\left\{\log(\nu(x))-\log(e^{-l(x)})-\log(\pi(x))\right\}\mathrm{d}x+\log\pi(e^{-l})

\displaystyle=\int\nu(x)\log\left(\frac{\nu(x)}{\pi(x)}\right)\mathrm{d}x+\int\nu(x)l(x)\mathrm{d}x+\log\pi(e^{-l})

which leads to equation (
38
). This implies

\displaystyle\argmin_{\nu\in\mathbb{M}^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\left\{\int l(x)\nu(x)\mathrm{d}x+\mathrm{KL}(\nu,\pi)\right\}=\argmin_{\nu\in\mathbb{M}^{1}_{+}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\mathrm{KL}\left(\nu,\tilde{\pi}\right)=\tilde{\pi}

as
\mathrm{KL}(\nu,\tilde{\pi})=0
if and only if
\nu=\tilde{\pi}
. Hence
relation (
39
) holds.

∎
