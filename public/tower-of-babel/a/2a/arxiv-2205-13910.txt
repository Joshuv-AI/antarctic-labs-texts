A gradient estimator via L1-randomization for online zero-order optimization with two point feedback
Arya Akhavan, Evgenii Chzhen, Massimiliano Pontil, Alexandre B. Tsybakov

Abstract
This work studies online zero-order optimization of convex and Lipschitz functions. We present a novel gradient estimator based on two function evaluations and randomization on the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -sphere. Considering different geometries of feasible sets and Lipschitz assumptions we analyse online dual averaging algorithm with our estimator in place of the usual gradient. We consider two types of assumptions on the noise of the zero-order oracle: canceling noise and adversarial noise. We provide an anytime and completely data-driven algorithm, which is adaptive to all parameters of the problem. In the case of canceling noise that was previously studied in the literature, our guarantees are either comparable or better than state-of-the-art bounds obtained by Duchi et al., 2015 and Shamir, 2017 for non-adaptive algorithms. Our analysis is based on deriving a new weighted Poincaré type inequality for the uniform measure on the ℓ 1 \ell_{1} -sphere with explicit constants, which may be of independent interest.

Introduction

In this work we study the problem of convex online zero-order optimization with two-point feedback, in which
adversary fixes a sequence
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
of convex functions and the goal of the learner is to minimize the cumulative regret with respect to the best action in a prescribed convex set
\Theta\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
This problem has received significant attention in the context of continuous bandits and online optimization
[see e.g.,

We consider the following protocol:
at each round

the algorithm chooses
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\prime},\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\prime\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(that can be queried outside of
\Theta

\displaystyle f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\prime})+\xi_{t}^{\prime}\qquad\text{and}\qquad f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}^{\prime\prime})+\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime}\kern 5.0pt,

where
\xi^{\prime}_{t},\xi^{\prime\prime}_{t}\in\mathbb{R}
are the noise variables (random or not) to be specified.
Based on the above information and the previous rounds, the learner outputs
\boldsymbol{x}_{t}\in\Theta
and suffers loss

The goal of the learner is to minimize the cumulative regret

\displaystyle\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t})-\min_{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Theta}\sum_{t=1}^{T}f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x})\kern 5.0pt.

At the core of our approach is a novel zero-order gradient estimator

We use the proposed estimator within an online dual averaging procedure to tackle the zero-order online convex optimization problem, matching or improving the state-of-the-art results.

Duchi et al., 2015

The procedure that we propose in this paper is summarized in Algorithm

Input:

Intuition behind the gradient estimate.

The form of gradient estimator
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
in Algorithm 1 is explained by Stokes’ theorem (see Theorem
5
in the appendix and the discussion that follows).
Stokes’ theorem provides a connection between the gradient of a function
f
(first order information) and
f
itself (zero order information). Under some regularity conditions, it establishes that

\displaystyle\int_{D}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{x}=\int_{\partial D}f(\boldsymbol{x})\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{x})\kern 5.0pt,

where
\partial D
is the boundary of
D
,
\boldsymbol{n}
is the outward normal vector to
\partial D
, and
\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{x})
denotes the surface measure. Introducing
\boldsymbol{U}^{D}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\partial D}
distributed uniformly on
D
and
\partial D
respectively, we can rewrite the above identity as

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{D})]=\frac{\vol_{d-1}(\partial D)}{\vol_{d}(D)}\cdot\mathbf{E}[f(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\partial D})\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{\zeta}^{\partial D})]\kern 5.0pt,

where
\vol_{d-1}(\partial D)
is the surface area of
D
and
\vol_{d}(D)
is its volume. In what follows we consider the special case
D=B^{d}_{1}
. For this choice of
D
we have
\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{x})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\cdot\sign(\boldsymbol{x})
with
\vol_{d-1}(\partial D)/\vol_{d}(D)=d^{3/2}
leading to our gradient estimate for the two-point feedback setup.

Computational aspects.

Let us highlight two appealing practical features of the
\ell_{1}
-randomized gradient estimator
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
in Algorithm
1
. First, we can easily evaluate any
\ell_{p}
-norm of
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
. Indeed, it holds that
\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\|_{p}=(d^{1+1/p}/{2h_{t}})|y_{t}^{\prime}-y_{t}^{\prime\prime}|
, i.e., computing
\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\|_{p}
only requires
O(1)
elementary operations. Second, this gradient estimator is very economic in terms of the required memory: in order to store
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
we only need
d
bits and
1
float. None of these properties is inherent to the popular alternatives based on the randomization over the
\ell_{2}
-sphere
[
22
,
16
,
5
, see e.g.,]
or on Gaussian randomization
[
23
,
24
,
19
, see e.g.,]
.

To compute
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
one needs to generate
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}
distributed uniformly on
\partial B_{1}^{d}
. The most straightforward way to do it consists in first generating a
d
-dimensional vector of i.i.d. centered scaled Laplace random variables and then normalizing this vector by its
\ell_{1}
-norm. The result is guaranteed to follow the uniform distribution on
\partial B_{d}^{1}

[see e.g.,
30
, Lemma 1]
. Furthermore, to sample from the centered scaled Laplace distribution one can simply use inverse transform sampling. Indeed, if
U
is distributed uniformly on
(0,1)
, then
\log(2U){\bf 1}{\left(U>1/2\right)}-\log(2-2U){\bf 1}{\left(U\geq 1/2\right)}
follows centered scaled Laplace distribution.

Assumptions on the noise.
We consider two different assumptions on the noises
\xi^{\prime}_{t},\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime}
.
The first noise assumption is common in the stochastic optimization context
[
14
,
33
,
19
,
23

24

Assumption 2

(Canceling noise)
.

For all
t=1,2,\ldots
, it holds that
\xi_{t}^{\prime}=\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime}
almost surely.

Formally, Assumption
2
permits noisy evaluations of function values. However, due to the fact that we are allowed to query

at two points, taking difference of

and

in the estimator of the gradient effectively erases the noise. It results in a smaller variance of our
gradient estimator. Importantly, Assumption
2
covers the case of no noise, that is, the classical online optimization setting as defined, e.g., in
[
31
]
.

Second, we consider an adversarial noise assumption, which is essentially equivalent to the assumptions used in
[
3
,
4
]
.

Assumption 3

(Adversarial noise)
.

For all
t=1,2,\ldots
, it holds that: (i)
\mathbf{E}[(\xi_{t}^{\prime})^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
and
\mathbf{E}[(\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime})^{2}]\leq\sigma^{2}
; (ii)
(\xi_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 1}
and
(\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime})_{t\geq 1}
are independent of
(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})_{t\geq 1}

Assumption
3
allows for stochastic
\xi_{t}^{\prime}
and
\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime}
that are not necessarily zero-mean or independent over the trajectory. Furthermore, it permits bounded non-stochastic adversarial noises.
Part (ii) of Assumption

is always satisfied. Indeed,
\xi_{t}^{\prime}
’s and
\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime}
’s are coming from the environment and are unknown to the learner while
\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t}
’s are artificially generated by the learner. We mention part (ii) only for formal mathematical rigor.

Note that, since the choice of function

belongs to the learner and
\Theta
is given, it is always reasonable to assume that parameter

is known. At the same time, parameters

and
\sigma
may be either known or unknown.
We will study both cases in the next sections.

\sqrt{\sigma L}
instead of
\sigma+L
. We remark that if
\sigma
is known but
L
is unknown, one can recover the
\sqrt{\sigma L}
dependency by selecting
h_{t}
depending on
\sigma
. We do not state this result that can be derived in a similar way and favor here only the fully adaptive version.

Elements of proofs

In this section, we outline major ingredients for the proofs of Theorems
1
–
4
. The full proofs can be found in Appendix
C
. Here, we only focus on novel elements without reproducing the general scheme of online dual averaging analysis
[

26
, see e.g.,]

Namely, we highlight two key facts, which are the smoothing lemma (Lemma

6.1
Bias and smoothing lemma

First, as in the prior work that was using smoothing ideas
[
22
,
16
,
33
, see e.g.,]
, we show that our gradient estimate
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
is an unbiased estimator of a surrogate version of
f_{t}
and establish its approximation properties.

Lemma 1

(Smoothing lemma)
.

Fix
h>0
and
q\in[1,\infty]
.
Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be an
L
-Lipschitz function w.r.t. the
\ell_{q}
-norm.
Let
\boldsymbol{U}
be distributed uniformly on
B^{d}_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
be distributed uniformly on
\partial B_{d}^{1}
.
Let
{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\triangleq\mathbf{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{U})]
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Then
{\sf f}_{h}
is differentiable and

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{d}{2h}\big(f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta})-f(\boldsymbol{x}-h\boldsymbol{\zeta})\big)\sign(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right]=\nabla{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\kern 5.0pt.

Furthermore, we have for all
d\geq 3
and all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq\mathrm{b}_{q}(d)Lh\kern 5.0pt.

(1)

Finally, if
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is convex,
f
is convex in
\Theta{+}hB_{1}^{d}
, then
{\sf f}_{h}
is convex in
\Theta
and
{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})\geq f(\boldsymbol{x})
for
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Theta
.

Proof.

There are three claims to prove.
For the first one, we notice that
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
has the same distribution as
-\boldsymbol{\zeta}
, hence,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{d}{2h}\big(f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta})-f(\boldsymbol{x}-h\boldsymbol{\zeta})\big)\sign(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right]=\mathbf{E}\left[\frac{d}{h}f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta})\sign(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\right]\kern 5.0pt,

and the first claim follows from Theorem
6
in the Appendix (a version of Stokes’, or divergence, theorem) applied to
g(\cdot)=f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\cdot)
with observation that
\nabla g(\cdot)=h\nabla f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\cdot)
where
\nabla f
is the gradient defined almost everywhere and whose existence is ensured by the Rademacher theorem.

We now prove the approximation property (
1
). Assuming
d\geq 3
grants that
\log(d)\geq 1
.
Since
f
is
L
-Lipschitz w.r.t. the
\ell_{q}
-norm we get that,
for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\displaystyle|{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})-f(\boldsymbol{x})|\leq Lh\,\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{q}\kern 5.0pt.

(2)

If
q\in[1,\log(d))
then (
1
) follows from Lemma
2
. If
q\geq\log(d)
then using again Lemma
2
we find

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{q}\leq\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{\log(d)}\leq\frac{\log(d)d^{\frac{1}{\log(d)}}}{d+1}=\frac{e\log(d)}{d+1}\kern 5.0pt,

which together with (
2
) yields the desired bound.

Finally, if
f
is convex in
\Theta+hB_{1}^{d}
, then for all
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\Theta
and
\alpha\in[0,1]
we have

\displaystyle{\sf f}_{h}(\alpha\boldsymbol{x}+(1-\alpha)\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})=\mathbf{E}\bigg[f\big(\alpha(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{U})+(1-\alpha)(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}+h\boldsymbol{U})\big)\bigg]\leq\alpha{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})+(1-\alpha){\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})\kern 5.0pt.

Thus
{\sf f}_{h}
is indeed convex on
\Theta
. Furthermore, again by convexity of
f
, we deduce that for any
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Theta

\displaystyle{\sf f}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x})=\mathbf{E}[f(\boldsymbol{x}+h\boldsymbol{U})]\geq\mathbf{E}\left[f(\boldsymbol{x})+\left\langle\boldsymbol{w},\,h\boldsymbol{U}\right\rangle\right]=f(\boldsymbol{x})\quad\text{where $\boldsymbol{w}\in\partial f(\boldsymbol{x})$}\,\,.

The proof of Lemma
1
relies on the control of the
\ell_{q}
-norm of random vector
\boldsymbol{U}
established in the next result.

Lemma 2
.

Let
q\in[1,\infty)
and let
\boldsymbol{U}
be distributed uniformly on
B^{d}_{1}
. Then
\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{q}\leq\tfrac{qd^{\frac{1}{q}}}{d+1}
.

Proof.

Let
W_{1},\ldots,W_{d},W_{d+1}
be i.i.d. random variables having the Laplace distribution with mean
0
and scale parameter
1
. Set
\boldsymbol{W}=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{d})
.
Then, following
[
6
, Theorem 1]
we have

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{U}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{\boldsymbol{W}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}+|W_{d+1}|}\kern 5.0pt,

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. Furthermore,
[
30
, Lemma 1]
states that the random variables

\displaystyle\frac{(\boldsymbol{W},|W_{d+1}|)}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}+|W_{d+1}|}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}+|W_{d+1}|\kern 5.0pt,

are independent.
Hence, for any
q\in[1,\infty)
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{U}\|_{q}=\frac{\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{q}}{\mathbf{E}\|(\boldsymbol{W},W_{d+1})\|_{1}}=\frac{1}{d+1}\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(a)}}{{\leq}}\frac{1}{d+1}\bigg(\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{q}^{q}\bigg)^{\frac{1}{q}}=\frac{d^{\frac{1}{q}}\Gamma^{\frac{1}{q}}(q+1)}{d+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(b)}}{{\leq}}\frac{qd^{\frac{1}{q}}}{d+1}\kern 5.0pt,

where
(a)
follows from Jensen’s inequality and
(b)
uses the fact that
\Gamma^{1/q}(q{+}1)\leq q
for
q\geq 1
.
∎

6.2
Variance and weighted Poincaré type inequality

We additionally need to control the squared
\ell_{p^{*}}
-norm of each gradient estimator
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
. This is where we get the main improvement of our procedure compared to previously proposed methods.
To derive the result, we first establish the following lemma of independent interest, which allows us to control the variance of Lipschitz functions on
\partial B^{d}_{1}
. The proof of this lemma is given in the Appendix.

Lemma 3
.

Let
d\geq 3
. Assume that
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is a continuously differentiable function, and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is distributed uniformly on
\partial B^{d}_{1}
. Then

\displaystyle\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))\leq\frac{4}{d(d-2)}\mathbf{E}\left[\|\nabla G(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\|_{2}^{2}\left(1+\sqrt{d}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{2}\right)^{2}\right]\kern 5.0pt.

Furthermore, if
G:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
is an
L
-Lipschitz function w.r.t. the
\ell_{2}
-norm then

\displaystyle\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))\leq\frac{4L^{2}}{d(d-2)}\left(1+\sqrt{\frac{2d}{d+1}}\right)^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

Remark 1
.

Since
d^{2}/(d(d-2))\leq 3
for all
d\geq 3
, the last inequality of Lemma
3
implies that

\displaystyle\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))\leq 12\big({1+\sqrt{2}}\big)^{2}\big({L}/{d}\big)^{2}\,\,,\qquad\forall d\geq 3\kern 5.0pt.

(3)

We can now deduce the following bound on the squared
\ell_{p^{*}}
-norm of
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
.

Lemma 4
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty]
and
p^{*}=\tfrac{p}{p-1}
.
Assume that
f_{t}
is
L
-Lipschitz w.r.t. the
\ell_{q}
-norm. Then, for all
d\geq 3
,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\|_{p^{*}}^{2}\leq 12(1+\sqrt{2})^{2}L^{2}d^{1+\frac{2}{q\wedge 2}-\frac{2}{p}}+\begin{cases}0&\text{under canceling noise Assumption~\ref{ass:no_noise}},\\
\frac{d^{4-\frac{2}{p}}\sigma^{2}}{h^{2}}&\text{under adversarial noise Assumption~\ref{ass:noise}}.\end{cases}

Proof.

Using the definition of
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
we get

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\|_{p^{*}}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{2}}{4h^{2}}\mathbf{E}[(f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})-f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-h\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})+\xi_{t}^{\prime}-\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\|\sign(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})\|_{p^{*}}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]

\displaystyle=\frac{d^{4-\frac{2}{p}}}{4h^{2}}\mathbf{E}[(f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})-f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-h\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})+\xi_{t}^{\prime}-\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]\kern 5.0pt.

Let
G(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\triangleq f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}+h\boldsymbol{\zeta})-f_{t}(\boldsymbol{x}_{t}-h\boldsymbol{\zeta})
. First, observe that
\mathbf{E}[G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]=0
and under both Assumption
2
and Assumption
3
(ii) it holds that
\mathbf{E}[G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})(\xi_{t}^{\prime}-\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime})\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]=0
. Using these remarks and the fact that under adversarial noise Assumption
3
,
\mathbf{E}[(\xi_{t}^{\prime}-\xi_{t}^{\prime\prime})^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]\leq 4\sigma^{2}
, we find:

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}[\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\|_{p^{*}}^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t}]\leq\frac{d^{4-\frac{2}{p}}}{4h^{2}}\left(\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t})+\begin{cases}0&\text{under cancelling noise Assumption~\ref{ass:no_noise}}\\
4\sigma^{2}&\text{under adversarial noise Assumption~\ref{ass:noise}}\end{cases}\right).

Furthermore, since
f_{t}
is
L
-Lipschitz, w.r.t. the
\ell_{q}
-norm, the map
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\mapsto G(\boldsymbol{\zeta})
is
\big(2Lhd^{\frac{1}{q\wedge 2}-\frac{1}{2}}\big)
-Lipschitz w.r.t. the
\ell_{2}
-norm. Applying (
3
) to bound
\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}_{t})\mid\boldsymbol{x}_{t})
, yields the desired result.
∎

Note that under adversarial noise Assumption
3
, the bound on squared
\ell_{p^{*}}
-norm of
\boldsymbol{g}_{t}
gets an additional term
d^{4-\frac{2}{p}}\sigma^{2}h^{-2}
. In contrast to the case of canceling noise Assumption
2
, this does not allow us to take
h
arbitrary small hence inducing the bias-variance trade-off.

8
Discussion and comparison to prior work

We introduced and analyzed a novel estimator for the gradient based on randomization over the
\ell_{1}
-sphere. We established guarantees for the online dual averaging algorithm with the gradient replaced by the proposed estimator. We provided an anytime and completely data-driven algorithm, which is adaptive to all parameters of the problem.
Our analysis is based on deriving a weighted
Poincaré type inequality for the uniform measure on the
\ell_{1}
-sphere that may be of independent interest.

Under the
canceling noise assumption
and
q\in\{1,2\}
, our setting is analogous to
[
14
,
33
]
. For the case
q=p=2
and canceling noise, we show that the performance of our method is the same as in
[
33
, Corollary 2]
up to absolute constants that were not made explicit in
[
33
]
.
For the case of
q=p=1
and canceling noise, we improved the bound
[
33
, Corollary 3]
by a
\sqrt{\log(d)}
factor. For the case
q=2
,
p\geq 1
, comparing with the lower bound in
[
14
, Proposition 1]
, shows that the result of Theorem
1
is minimax optimal. For the case
q=p=1
,
[
14
, Proposition 2]
shows that our result in Theorem
1
is optimal up to a
\log(d)
factor.

Under the
adversarial noise assumption
, Theorem
2
provides the rate
O(T^{3/4})
, that is, we get an additional
T^{1/4}
factor compared to the canceling noise case. It remains unclear whether it is optimal under adversarial noise – this question deserves further investigation. Note that, under sub-Gaussian i.i.d. noise assumption and
q=p=2
, one can achieve the rate
\tilde{O}(d^{a}\sqrt{T})
with a relatively big
a>0

[
2
,
8
,
13
,
21
]
.
In particular, with an ellipsoid type method
[
21
]
obtains the rate
\mathcal{O}(d^{4.5}\sqrt{T}\log(T)^{2})
for the cumulative regret.

Finally, let us discuss the compactness of
\Theta
.
It is straightforward to extend the results of Theorems
1
,
2
to any closed convex
\Theta
considering the regret against a fixed action
\boldsymbol{x}\in\Theta
. Indeed, using
[
26
, Corollary 7.9]
, one only needs to replace
R
appearing in both Theorems
1
,
2
by an upper bound on
\sqrt{V(\boldsymbol{x})-\inf_{\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime}\in\Theta}V(\boldsymbol{x}^{\prime})}
. The adaptive case is more complicated. One way to tackle this case is to use
[
27
, Theorem 1]
requiring a control of
\mathbf{E}\max_{t=1,\ldots,T}\|\boldsymbol{g}_{t}\|_{p^{*}}
. This term can be controlled under the canceling noise Assumption
2
using the Lipschitzness of
f_{t}
’s, so that Theorem
3
extends to unbounded
\Theta
. However, without the canceling noise assumption, following the approach outlined above,
one needs to control
\mathbf{E}\max_{t=1,\ldots,T}\tfrac{|\xi_{t}^{\prime}-\xi^{\prime\prime}_{t}|}{h_{t}}
. The adversarial noise Assumption
3
is not sufficient to reasonably control this term, so that extending Theorem
4
to unbounded
\Theta
is not possible without further assumptions.

Appendix A
Integration by parts

We first recall the following result that can be found in
[
34
, Section 13.3.5, Exercise 14a]
.

Theorem 5

(Integration by parts in a multiple integral)
.

Let
D
be an open connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with a piecewise smooth boundary
\partial D
oriented by the outward unit normal
\boldsymbol{n}=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{d})^{\top}
. Let
g
be a continuously differentiable function in
D\cup\partial D
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{D}\nabla g(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}=\int_{\partial D}g(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\boldsymbol{n}(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\kern 5.0pt.

Remark 2
.

We refer to
[
34
, Section 12.3.2, Definitions 4 and 5]
for the definition of piecewise smooth surfaces and their orientations respectively.

The idea of using the instance of Theorem
5
(also called Stokes’ theorem) with
D=B^{d}_{2}
to obtain
\ell_{2}
-randomized estimators of the gradient belongs to
Nemirovsky and Yudin, 1983
. It was further used in several papers
[
16
,
5
,
31
,
33
]
to mention just a few. Those papers were referring to
[
22
]
but
[
22
]
did not provide an exact statement of the result (nor a reference) and only tossed the idea in a discussion. However, the classical analysis formulation
as presented in Theorem
5
does not apply to Lipschitz continuous functions that were considered in
[
16
,
5
,
31
,
33
]
. We are not aware of whether its extension to Lipschitz continuous functions, though rather standard, is proved in the literature.

In this paper, we apply Theorem
5
with the
\ell_{1}
-ball
D=B^{d}_{1}
. Our aim in this section is to provide a variant of Theorem
5
applicable to a Lipschitz continuous function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
, which is not necessarily continuously differentiable on
D\cup\partial D=B^{d}_{1}\cup\partial B^{d}_{1}
. To this end, we will go through the argument of approximating
g
by
C^{\infty}(\Omega)
functions, where
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
is an open bounded connected subset of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
D\cup\partial D\subset\Omega
.
Let
g_{n}=\eta_{{1}/n}\star g
, where
\eta_{1/n}
is the standard mollifier. Let
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be a function satisfying the Lipschitz condition w.r.t. the
\ell_{1}
-norm:
|g(\boldsymbol{u})-g(\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime})|\leq L\|\boldsymbol{u}-\boldsymbol{u}^{\prime}\|_{1}
.
Since
g
is continuous in
\Omega
and, by construction
D\cup\partial D\subset\Omega
, then using basic properties of mollification
[see e.g.,
15
, Theorem 4.1 (ii)]
we have

\displaystyle g_{n}\longrightarrow g

uniformly on
D\cup\partial D
(in particular, uniformly on
\partial D
).
Furthermore, let
\nabla g
be the gradient of
g
, which by Rademacher theorem
[see e.g.,
15
, Theorem 3.2]
is well defined almost everywhere w.r.t. the Lebesgue measure and

\displaystyle\|\nabla g(\boldsymbol{u})\|_{\infty}\leq L\qquad\text{a.e.}

It follows that
\frac{\partial g}{\partial u_{j}}
is absolutely integrable on
\Omega
for any
j\in[d]
.
Furthermore, since

\displaystyle\frac{\partial g_{n}}{\partial u_{j}}=\eta_{1/n}\star\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial u_{j}}\right)\kern 5.0pt,

we can apply
[
15
, Theorem 4.1 (iii)]
that yields

\displaystyle\int_{D}\|\nabla g_{n}(\boldsymbol{u})-\nabla g(\boldsymbol{u})\|_{2}\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}\longrightarrow 0\kern 5.0pt.

Combining the above remarks we obtain that the result of Theorem
5
is valid for functions
g
that are Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. the
\ell_{1}
-norm. Thus, it is also valid when the Lipschitz condition is imposed w.r.t. any
\ell_{q}
-norm with
q\in[1,\infty]
. Specifying this conclusion for the particular case
D=B_{1}^{d}
, we obtain the following theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let the function
g:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be Lipschitz continuous w.r.t. the
\ell_{q}
-norm with
q\in[1,\infty]
. Then

\displaystyle\int_{B^{d}_{1}}\nabla g(\boldsymbol{u})\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{u}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d}}\int_{\partial B^{d}_{1}}g(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\sign(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\,\mathrm{d}S(\boldsymbol{\zeta})\kern 5.0pt,

where
\nabla g(\cdot)
is defined up to a set of zero Lebesgue measure by the Rademacher theorem.

\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{w})=\boldsymbol{w}/\left\lVert\boldsymbol{w}\right\rVert_{1}
. Introduce the notation

\displaystyle F(\boldsymbol{w})\triangleq\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{w}))\kern 5.0pt.

Lemma 1 in
[
30
]
asserts that, for
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
uniformly distributed on
\partial B_{1}^{d}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}\quad\text{and}\quad\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\text{ is independent of }\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}\kern 5.0pt.

(4)

In particular,

\displaystyle\Var(F(\boldsymbol{W}))=d\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))\kern 5.0pt.

Using the Efron-Stein inequality
[see e.g.,
11
, Theorem 3.1]
we obtain

\displaystyle\Var(F(\boldsymbol{W}))\leq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbf{E}\left[\Var_{i}(F)\right]\kern 5.0pt,

where

\displaystyle\Var_{i}(F)=\mathbf{E}\bigg[\big(F(\boldsymbol{W})-\mathbf{E}[F(\boldsymbol{W})\mid\boldsymbol{W}^{-i}]\big)^{2}\mid\boldsymbol{W}^{-i}\bigg]

with
\boldsymbol{W}^{-i}\triangleq(W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},W_{i+1},\ldots,W_{d})
.
Note that on the event
\{\boldsymbol{W}^{-i}\neq\boldsymbol{0}\}
(whose complement has zero measure), the function

\displaystyle w\mapsto F(W_{1},\ldots,W_{i-1},w,W_{i+1},\ldots,W_{d})\kern 5.0pt,

satisfies the assumptions of Lemma
5
. Thus,

\displaystyle d\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))=\Var(F(\boldsymbol{W}))\leq 4\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{E}\left[\left(\frac{\partial F}{\partial{w_{j}}}(\boldsymbol{W})\right)^{2}\right]=4\mathbf{E}\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{W})\|_{2}^{2}\kern 5.0pt.

(5)

In order to compute
\nabla F(\boldsymbol{W})
, we observe that for every
i\neq j\in[d]
we have for all
\boldsymbol{w}\neq\boldsymbol{0}
such that
w_{i},w_{j}\neq 0

\displaystyle\frac{\partial T_{i}}{\partial w_{j}}(\boldsymbol{w})=-\frac{w_{i}\sign(w_{j})}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}^{2}}\quad\text{and}\quad\frac{\partial T_{i}}{\partial w_{i}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}}-\frac{w_{i}\sign(w_{i})}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}^{2}}\kern 5.0pt.

Thus, the Jacobi matrix of
\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{w})
has the form

\displaystyle\mathbf{J}_{\boldsymbol{T}}(\boldsymbol{w})=\frac{\mathbf{I}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}}-\frac{{\boldsymbol{w}(\sign(\boldsymbol{w}))^{\top}}}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}^{2}}=\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{w}\|_{1}}\left(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{w})\big(\sign(\boldsymbol{w})\big)^{\top}\right)\kern 5.0pt.

It follows that almost surely

\displaystyle\nabla F(\boldsymbol{W})=\frac{1}{2\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))\sign(\boldsymbol{W})+\frac{1}{\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}}\bigg(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\big(\sign(\boldsymbol{W})\big)^{\top}\bigg)\nabla G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))\kern 5.0pt\kern 5.0pt.

Observe that since
\left\langle\sign(\boldsymbol{W}),\,\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\right\rangle=1
almost surely, we have

\displaystyle\big(\sign(\boldsymbol{W})\big)^{\top}\bigg(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\big(\sign(\boldsymbol{W})\big)^{\top}\bigg)\nabla G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))=0\quad\text{almost surely}\kern 5.0pt.

The above two equations imply that almost surely

\displaystyle 4\|\nabla F(\boldsymbol{W})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}}G^{2}(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))+\frac{4}{\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}}\left\lVert\bigg(\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\big(\sign(\boldsymbol{W})\big)^{\top}\bigg)\nabla G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{d}{\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}}G^{2}(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))+\frac{4}{\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}}\left\lVert\nabla G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))\right\rVert_{2}^{2}(1+\sqrt{d}\|\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\|_{2})^{2}\kern 5.0pt,

where we used the fact that the operator norm of
\mathbf{I}-\boldsymbol{a}\boldsymbol{b}^{\top}
is not greater than
1+\|\boldsymbol{a}\|_{2}\|\boldsymbol{b}\|_{2}
.
Combining the above bound with (
5
), and using the facts that
\mathbf{E}[\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}^{-1}]=\frac{1}{d-1}
,

\mathbf{E}[G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))]=\mathbf{E}[G(\boldsymbol{\zeta})]=0

and the independence of
\|\boldsymbol{W}\|_{1}
and
\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})
(cf. (
4
)) yields

\displaystyle d\left(1-\frac{1}{d-1}\right)\Var(G(\boldsymbol{\zeta}))\leq\frac{4}{d-1}\mathbf{E}\left[\|\nabla G(\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W}))\|_{2}^{2}{(1+\sqrt{d}\|\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\|_{2})^{2}}\right]\kern 5.0pt.

Rearranging, we deduce the first claim of the lemma since
\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\boldsymbol{\zeta}
.

To prove the second statement of the lemma regarding Lipschitz functions, it is sufficient to apply the first one to
G_{n}
—the sequence of smoothed versions of
G
such that
G_{n}\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
and

\displaystyle G_{n}\longrightarrow G\kern 5.0pt,

uniformly on every compact subset, and
\sup_{n\geq 1}\|\nabla G_{n}(\boldsymbol{x})\|_{2}\leq L
for almost all
\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. A sequence
G_{n}
satisfying these properties can be constructed by standard mollification due to the fact that
G
is Lipschitz continuous
[see e.g.,
15
, Theorem 4.2]
. Finally, to obtain the value
\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{T}(\boldsymbol{W})\|_{2}^{2}=\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{2}^{2}
we use Lemma
6
below.
∎

Lemma 6
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
be distributed uniformly on
\partial B^{d}_{1}
. Then,
\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{\zeta}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\frac{2}{d+1}
.

Proof.

We use the same tools as in the proof of Lemma
2
. Let
\boldsymbol{W}=(W_{1},\ldots,W_{d})
be a vector of i.i.d. random variables following the Laplace distribution with mean
0
and scale parameter
1
. By (
4
) we have that
\boldsymbol{\zeta}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\frac{\boldsymbol{W}}{\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}}
and
\boldsymbol{\zeta}
is independent of
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}
.
Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\|\boldsymbol{\zeta}\|_{2}^{2}=\frac{\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}}{\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}}\kern 5.0pt.

(6)

Here,

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert^{2}_{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\mathbf{E}[W_{j}^{2}]=d\mathbf{E}[W_{1}^{2}]=2d\kern 5.0pt.

(7)

Furthermore,
\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}
follows the Erlang distribution with parameters
(d,1)
, which implies

\displaystyle\mathbf{E}\left\lVert\boldsymbol{W}\right\rVert_{1}^{2}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(d)}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{d+1}\exp(-x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{\Gamma(d+2)}{\Gamma(d)}\kern 5.0pt.

(8)

The lemma follows by combining (
6
) – (
8
).
∎
