ENHANCING THE ACCURACY OF THE TAYLOR POLYNOMIAL BY DETERMINING THE REMAINDER TERM
J.S.C Prentice

Abstract
We determine the Lagrange function in Taylor polynomial approximation by solving an appropriate initial-value problem. Hence, we determine the remainder term which we then approximate by means of a natural cubic spline. This results in a significant improvement in the quality of the Taylor approximation. We observe improvements in the accuracy of the approximation of many orders of magnitude, including a case when the independent variable x x lies beyond the relevant radius of convergence. Key words: Taylor, approximation, remainder, Lagrange function, cubic spline



1
Introduction

In a recent paper
[
1
]
, we determined the remainder term in
Lagrange interpolation by solving suitable initial-value problem. In this
paper, we apply the same notion to Taylor approximation. We derive a
differential equation that will allow us to compute the reminder term (in
Lagrange form) of a first-order Taylor expansion. We then consider how a
cubic spline can be used to approximate the remainder term, leading to a
significant improvement in the accuracy of the approximation.

2
Relevant Concepts

Let
y\left(x\right)
be a real-valued function, and assume that
y\left(x\right)
is as differentiable as is required in this paper (we will make
comments regarding this point when necessary). Taylor’s theorem
[
2
]

provides the following result:

y\left(x\right)=y\left(x_{0}\right)+y^{\prime}\left(x_{0}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)+\frac{y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{x}\right)}{2}\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2}

(1)

where
x_{0}<\xi_{x}<x.
The third term on the RHS is the
remainder
term
, presented here in
Lagrange
form
[
3
]
. There are
other representations of the remainder term, such as the Cauchy form
[
3
]

y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{C}\right)\left(x-\xi_{C}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right),\text{ \ }x_{0}<\xi_{C}<x

and the more general form

y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{C}\right)\left(x-\xi_{g}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)\left(\frac{g\left(x\right)-g\left(x_{0}\right)}{g^{\prime}\left(\xi_{g}\right)}\right),\text{ \ }x_{0}<\xi_{g}<x

(2)

of which the Lagrange and Cauchy forms are particular cases. In (
2
), the function
g
is continuous on
\left[x_{0},x\right]
and differentiable with a non-vanishing derivative on
\left(x_{0},x\right).
However, it is the Lagrange form that will most easily
serve our purposes here.

If we define

T_{1}\left(x\right)\equiv y\left(x_{0}\right)+y^{\prime}\left(x_{0}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)

to be the so-called
Taylor polynomial of first degree
, then we have

y\left(x\right)-T_{1}\left(x\right)=\frac{y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{x}\right)}{2}\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2}.

(3)

3
The Lagrange Function

By differentiating (
3
) with respect to
x,
we find

y^{\prime}\left(x\right)-T_{1}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{x}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)+\frac{y^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{x}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2}}{2}\frac{d\xi_{x}}{dx}.

(4)

Since the LHS of this expression exists, we must assume that the RHS also
exists. Hence, we must assume that
\frac{d\xi_{x}}{dx}
exists. This
implies that
\xi_{x}
is a function of
x,
and we will sometimes write
\xi\left(x\right)
or simply
\xi
in place of
\xi_{x}.
We will refer
to
\xi\left(x\right)
as the
Lagrange function
. In (
4
), we have used the notation

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi\left(x\right)\right)

\displaystyle y^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\xi\right)

\displaystyle\equiv

\displaystyle\frac{dy^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi\left(x\right)\right)}{d\xi\left(x\right)}=\left.\frac{dy^{\prime\prime}\left(x\right)}{dx}\right|_{x=\xi}.

Rearranging (
4
) and using
T_{1}^{\prime}\left(x\right)=y^{\prime}\left(x_{0}\right)
gives

\frac{d\xi}{dx}=\frac{2\left(y^{\prime}\left(x\right)-y^{\prime}\left(x_{0}\right)-y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)\right)}{y^{\prime\prime\prime}\left(\xi\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2}}.

(5)

This is an initial-value problem that can, in principle, be solved to yield
the Lagrange function
\xi\left(x\right)
for a suitable initial value.
Once
\xi\left(x\right)
is known, the remainder term is easily computed.
Hence, if
T_{1}\left(x\right)
is taken as an approximation to
y\left(x\right),
the resulting approximation error is known. We could also obtain
differential equations for
\xi_{C}
and
\xi_{g}
in a similar fashion,
but we believe these would be more complex than (
5
), and
so we work only with
\xi
in this paper - an application of Occam’s Razor,
we suppose.

There is a subtle point to be made: in (
1
), the remainder is
derived using the Mean Value Theorem
[
4
]
, hence the constraint
x_{0}<\xi_{x}<x.
Our assumption above that
\xi_{x}=\xi\left(x\right)

may be at odds with this constraint. The Lagrange form of the remainder term
does not explicitly require that
\xi_{x}
be a smooth function of
x.

This is not a concern for us. Our task here is simply to find values for
\xi
that satisfy (
1
), by solving (
5
),
whether or not they satisfy the constraint. However, we will demonstrate
later how the solution(s) of (
5
) can be made consistent
with the constraint.

Lastly, note that our analysis up to this point requires that
y\left(x\right)
is three times differentiable, at least.

4
Numerical Examples

4.1
First example

For our first example, we consider

y\left(x\right)=e^{x/5}\sin x

for
x\in\left[1,10\right].
So we have
x_{0}=1.
To find a suitable
initial value for
\xi,
we choose a point very close to
x_{0}\
(which we
denote
x_{z}
), say
x_{z}=1.0005
. We then solve

y\left(x_{z}\right)=y\left(x_{0}\right)+y^{\prime}\left(x_{0}\right)\left(x_{z}-x_{0}\right)+\frac{y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi_{z}\right)}{2}\left(x_{z}-x_{0}\right)^{2}

(6)

numerically to find
\xi_{z}=1.000167.
Next, we use the initial value
\left(x_{z},\xi_{z}\right)
to solve (
5
) using a
seventh-order Runge-Kutta (RK7) method
[
5
]
to find
\xi\left(x\right).
This then allows us to find the remainder

R_{\xi}\left(x\right)\equiv\frac{y^{\prime\prime}\left(\xi\left(x\right)\right)}{2}\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2},

which can be compared with the actual remainder

R_{act}\left(x\right)\equiv y\left(x\right)-T_{1}\left(x\right)=y\left(x\right)-y\left(x_{0}\right)-y^{\prime}\left(x_{0}\right)\left(x-x_{0}\right).

We can measure the quality of
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
through the device
of

\Delta R\left(x\right)\equiv R_{act}\left(x\right)-R_{\xi}\left(x\right).

For this example, we find

\max_{\left[1,10\right]}\left|\Delta R\left(x\right)\right|=5.4\times 10^{-13},

suggesting that our calculation of
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
is very
accurate, indeed.

It transpires that (
6
) has another solution, leading to
\xi_{z}=3.157781.
We use this initial value in the same way as before,
eventually finding

\max_{\left[1,10\right]}\left|\Delta R\left(x\right)\right|=3.0\times 10^{-13},

which shows accuracy similar to the first case.

In Figure 1, we show
\xi\left(x\right)
for both initial values. On this
plot we show the constant
x_{0}=1
and the line
y=x.
These are the bounds
on the constraint
x_{0}<\xi_{x}<x,x\in\left[1,10\right].
For
\left(x_{z},\xi_{z}\right)=\left(1.0005,1.000167\right),
we see that
\xi\left(x\right)
lies between the two bounds up to
x\approx 5.4,
and lies
below
x_{0}=1
thereafter. By contrast, for
\left(x_{z},\xi_{z}\right)=\left(1.0005,1.000167\right),

\xi\left(x\right)>x
up to
x\approx 2.8
and thereafter lies between the bounds. Neither of these Lagrange
functions satisfies the constraint on the entire interval
\left[1,10\right].
However, the Lagrange form of the remainder term, derived using the Mean
Value Theorem, does not require that
\xi_{x}
must be smooth or even
continuous. It simply states that, for each
x\in\left[1,10\right],

there does exist an
\xi_{x}
such that
x_{0}<\xi_{x}<x
and
\xi_{x}

satisfies (
6
). If we choose values of
\xi\left(x\right)

from the first Lagrange function for
x\in\left[1,4\right],
and values
of
\xi\left(x\right)
from the second Lagrange function for
x\in\left(4,10\right],
we necessarily satisfy Taylor’s theorem for this example.

In Figure 2 we show
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
and
R_{act}\left(x\right),

and in Figure 3 we show
\Delta R\left(x\right).

4.2
Second example

For our second example, we consider

y\left(x\right)=\ln\left(1+x\right)

for
x\in\left[0,10\right].
We have
x_{0}=0.
Again, we choose
x_{z}=1.0005
. We then solve (
6
) to find
\xi_{z}=1.67\times 10^{-4}.
Application of RK7 then gives the Lagrange function
\xi\left(x\right),
shown in Figure 4, along with the bounds of the constraint
x_{0}=0
and the line
y=x.
We see that
\xi\left(x\right)
lies between
the bounds for all
x\in\left[0,10\right].
In Figure 5 we show
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
and
R_{act}\left(x\right),
and in Figure 6 we show
\Delta R\left(x\right).
We determine

\max_{\left[0,10\right]}\left|\Delta R\left(x\right)\right|=1.3\times 10^{-13}.

5
Polynomial Approximation of the Remainder

Now that we have determined the Lagrange function
\xi\left(x\right)

and, hence, the remainder term
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
for each example,
it seems reasonable to attempt to approximate the remainder term by means of
a polynomial. This polynomial can be added to the original Taylor polynomial

T_{1}\left(x\right)
to yield a more accurate approximation than
T_{1}\left(x\right).
If we denote the polynomial approximation to
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
by
P_{R}\left(x\right),
we would then present

T_{1}\left(x\right)+P_{R}\left(x\right)

as an approximation to
y\left(x\right)
on the given interval.

We choose to use a
natural
cubic spline
[
6
]
to form
P_{R}\left(x\right),
for several reasons: we have the RK nodes at our
disposal (we used
10000
nodes in the RK computations); a cubic polynomial
combines with the factor
\left(x-x_{0}\right)^{2}
in
R_{\xi}\left(x\right)
to yield a polynomial of degree five, at most; and such splines
can be generated very efficiently on our computational platform
[
7
]
. Also, it is possible to estimate a bound on the accuracy of a
natural spline although, as will be seen, the bound is not tight.

Defining

\displaystyle\Delta_{T}

\displaystyle\equiv\max_{I}\left|y\left(x\right)-T_{5}\left(x\right)\right|

\displaystyle\Delta_{CS}

\displaystyle\equiv\max_{I}\left|y\left(x\right)-\left(T_{1}\left(x\right)+P_{R}\left(x\right)\right)\right|

where
T_{5}\left(x\right)
is the Taylor polynomial of fifth degree, and
I
is the relevant interval of approximation, we show results in Table 1.

This is particularly
true for the second example, whose Taylor series has a radius of convergence
of
\left|x\right|<1
(hence the very large value of

6
Concluding Comments

We have shown how the Lagrange function in Taylor polynomial approximation
can be determined by solving an appropriate initial-value problem. This
allows the remainder term to be determined. The remainder term can then be
approximated by means of a polynomial, and this can result in a significant
improvement in the quality of the Taylor approximation overall. We have
demonstrated this effect using a cubic spline, and we note improvements in
the accuracy of the approximation of many orders of magnitude, including the
case when the independent variable
x
lies beyond the radius of
convergence. This speaks to the potential value of the idea presented here,
and in
[
1
]
.

This paper is intended as a demonstration of an idea and, as such, our
analysis has not been exhaustive. Further studies should consider the effect
of error control in the RK solution, and how this will affect the quality of
the polynomial approximation of the remainder term. It is also feasible to
approximate the remainder term by means of a least-squares fit, which might
be worth considering. Also, the multidimensional case must be investigated,
which would require the solution of a system of differential equations, but
all these aspects will be reserved for future work.
