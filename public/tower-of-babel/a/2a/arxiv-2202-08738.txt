On a Conjecture of Sun Zhi-Wei and Related Diophantine Equations
Wang Jia-Hui, Zhu Hui-Lin

Abstract
For any integer m ≥ 0 m\geq 0 , we recall that triangular numbers are those 𝐓 ⁡ ( m ) = m ⁡ ( m + 1 ) 2 \mathbf{T}(m)=\frac{m(m+1)}{2} . A conjecture of Sun Zhi-Wei states that an integer 2 n ± n 2^{n}\pm n with any n > 2 n>2 can not be a triangular number. The motivation of this work is to confirm this conjecture.

Introduction

In MathOverflow(MO403375) Sun Zhi-Wei asked his question: Is there an integer
n>2
with
2^{n}-n
or
2^{n}+n
a triangular number? I guess the answer should be negative, but I don’t know how to prove this. Any ideas? We recall triangular numbers are those

T(m)=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}(m=0,1,2,\cdots).

Clealy,

2^{1}-1=1=T(1),\ 2^{1}+1=3=T(2),\ 2^{2}+2=6=T(3).

Also see the remark 8.6 of Conjecture 8.6 in the book
[
1
]
.

We can transform the question into a generalized Ramanujan-Nagell equation as the following:

2^{n}\pm n=T(m)=\frac{m(m+1)}{2},

2^{n+3}\pm 8n=4m(m+1)=(2m+1)^{2}-1,

(2m+1)^{2}\pm 8n-1=2^{n+3}.

Let
x=2m+1
be odd and
D(n)=\pm 8n-1
. We have

x^{2}+D(n)=2^{n+3},D(n)\equiv 7(\bmod 8).

(1)

It is a generalized Ramanujan-Nagell equation of
D(n)
being a integer coefficient polynomial of
n
more than a constant.

In this work, by applying the deep results of Bauer and Bennett about Hypergeometric method
[
2
]
, we prove the equation (1) has no solution with
n>2
when
D(n)=\pm 8n-1
, i.e. we answered Sun’s conjecture positively as the following:

Theorem 1
.

Equation

2^{n}\pm n=\frac{m(m+1)}{2}

(2)

has no solution with
n\in\mathbf{Z},n>2
and
m\in\mathbf{Z},m\geq 0
.

Our method is also effective for some kind of equations of the form
x^{2}+D(n)=y^{n},
where
D(n)\in\mathbf{Z}(n),y
is a given integer more than 1. For example, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 2
.

Equation

3^{n}\pm(n^{3}+n)=x^{2}

(3)

has only one positive integer solution
3^{1}-(1^{3}+1)=1^{2}
.

Proof of Theorem 1

Case 1:

D(n)=-8n-1
.

x^{2}=2^{n+3}+8n+1.

(4)

If
n
is odd, then there exists

such that
n=2t+1
. Hence

x^{2}=2^{2t+4}+16t+9.

When
t=1
or
t=2
, it is not true since both 89 and 297 are not perfect squares.

When
t\geq 3

16t+9<2^{t+3}+1
we have

(2^{t+2})^{2}=2^{2t+4}<x^{2}<2^{2t+4}+2^{t+3}+1=(2^{t+2}+1)^{2}.

So there is no integer solution for equation (4).

If
n
is even, suppose
n=2t(t\geq 2)
, then

x^{2}=2^{2t+3}+16t+1.

When
t=2
or
t=3
, it is not true since both 161 and 561 are not perfect squares.

When
t\geq 4
, from

we have

(2^{t+\frac{3}{2}})^{2}=2^{2t+3}<x^{2}<(2^{t+\frac{3}{2}}+1)^{2}=2^{2t+3}+2^{t+\frac{5}{2}}+1,

x=[2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}]+1,

where
[2^{t+\frac{3}{2}}]
is the Gauss integer function.
Suppose

f(t)=([2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}]+1)^{2}-2^{2t+3}.

We will prove

for any

.
One may verify that when

,
f(t)\neq 16t+1
.

Suppose
t\geq 11
. Naturally,
\left\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\right\}=2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}-[2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}]
, then

f(t)=(2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\}+1)^{2}-2^{2t+3}

=\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\}^{2}+1-2^{t+2}\sqrt{2}\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\}+2^{t+2}\sqrt{2}-2\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\}

=2^{t+2}\sqrt{2}(1-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\})+(1-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\})^{2}.

Consider

\left|\frac{1-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\}}{2^{t+1}}\right|

=\left|\frac{1-2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}+[2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}]}{2^{t+1}}\right|

=\left|\frac{1+[2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}]}{2^{t+1}}-\sqrt{2}\right|.

By Lemma 1,

\left|\frac{1+[2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}]}{2^{t+1}}-\sqrt{2}\right|>\frac{1}{2^{1.48(t+1)}}.

Hence,

2^{t+2}\sqrt{2}(1-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\})+(1-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\})^{2}>2^{t+2}\sqrt{2}(1-\{2^{t+1}\sqrt{2}\})

This indicates

for any
t\geq 11

Remarks

From the proof of the two theorems we will see, for any given integer coefficient polynomial
D(n)
, with the degree
k
, the generalized Ramanujan-Nagell equation

x^{2}+D(n)=2^{n},

(5)

has at most finite solutions, which can be proved by the upper bound of
n
from Pad
\acute{e}

Question 1
.

For given integer coefficient polynomial
D(n)
with degree
k
, equation (5) has at most
N(k)
positive integer solutions. For any given
k
, what is the
N(k)

For arbitrary integer coefficient polynomial
D(n)
, we can construct some examples, in which there are arbitrary many positive integer solutions of equation (5). Actually, we find a method to construct the polynomials
D(n)
.

Let

D_{1}(n)=(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)+7,

by simple computation, we know

x^{2}+D_{1}(n)=2^{n}

has at least 3 solutions:

(x,n)=(1,3),(3,4),(5,5).

Set

D_{2}(n)=(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)+c_{2}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)+7,

Let
n=6
. By solving the equation

x^{2}\equiv 7-2^{6}(\bmod 6),

we have
x\equiv 3(\bmod 6)
. Replace
x
by 3 one has
c_{2}=8
(actually,
x
has infinitly many choices, and the same
c_{2}
), namely

D_{2}(n)=(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)+8(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)+7,

and the equation

x^{2}+D_{2}(n)=2^{n}

has at least 4 solutions:

(x,n)=(1,3),(3,4),(3,6),(5,5).

Similarly, we have

D_{3}(n)=(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)(n-7)+c_{3}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)+8(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)+7,

D_{4}(n)=(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)(n-7)(n-8)+c_{4}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)(n-7)

+c_{3}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)+8(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)+7,

D_{5}(n)=(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)(n-7)(n-8)(n-9)+c_{5}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)(n-7)(n-8)

+c_{4}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)(n-7)+c_{3}(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)(n-6)+8(n-3)(n-4)(n-5)+7.

We may choose
c_{3}=-3,c_{4}=1,c_{5}=-6

x^{2}+D_{3}(n)=2^{n},

x^{2}+D_{4}(n)=2^{n},

x^{2}+D_{5}(n)=2^{n},

(x,n)=(1,3),(1,7),(3,4),(3,6),(5,5),

(x,n)=(1,3),(1,7),(3,4),(3,6),(3,8),(5,5),

(x,n)=(1,3),(1,7),(3,4),(3,6),(3,8),(5,5),(65,9).
