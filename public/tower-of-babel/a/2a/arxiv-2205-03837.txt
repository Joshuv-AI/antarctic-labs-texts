Generalized Jouanolou duality, weakly Gorenstein rings, and applications to blowup algebras
Yairon Cid-Ruiz, Claudia Polini, Bernd Ulrich

Abstract
. We provide a generalization of Jouanolou duality that is applicable to a plethora of situations. The environment where this generalized duality takes place is a new class of rings, that we introduce and call weakly Gorenstein rings. As a consequence, we obtain a new general framework to investigate blowup algebras. We use our results to study and determine the defining equations of the Rees algebra for certain families of ideals.

13A30, 13H10, 13D45, 13D07, 14E05.

1.
Introduction

1.1.

Generalized Jouanolou duality and weakly Gorenstein rings.

In a series of seminal papers Jouanolou studied elimination theory through the lens of modern algebraic geometry (
[
38
,
36
,
37
,
35
,
34
,
33
]
).
One of his main tools was a new duality, nowadays dubbed
Jouanolou duality
.
Let
S
be a positively graded Noetherian
T
-algebra, with graded irrelevant ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}
.
When
S
is a (not necessarily flat) complete intersection over
T
and
\dim(S)=\dim(T)
, Jouanolou proved that, up to shift in degree, there are graded
S
-isomorphisms

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(S)\cong{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}(S,T)

for all
i\geq 0
, where
{}^{*}\text{Ext}
denotes the graded
Ext
functor.

The fundamental problem in elimination theory is to compute the image of a projection (see
[
17
]
*§14.1), like the natural projection
\pi:X=\text{Proj}(S)\rightarrow{\text{Spec}}(T)
.
The scheme-theoretic image of
\pi
is given by the closed subscheme
{\text{Spec}}(T/\mathfrak{a})
with
\mathfrak{a}=\text{Ker}(T\xrightarrow{\;\rm nat\;}\text{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X}))
.
We say that
\mathfrak{a}
is the
resultant ideal
.
We have the four-term exact sequence

0\rightarrow\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{0}\rightarrow T=S_{0}\xrightarrow{\;\rm nat\;}\text{H}^{0}(X,\mathcal{O}_{X})\rightarrow\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{1}(S)_{0}\rightarrow 0.

This shows that the scheme-theoretic image of
\pi
is given by

\text{Im}(\pi)\;=\;{\text{Spec}}(T/\mathfrak{a})\quad\text{with}\quad\mathfrak{a}\;=\;\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{0}.

Therefore, Jouanolou duality gives an effective method to compute the image of a projection.
Indeed, if we have the isomorphism
\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)\cong{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}(S,T)(-\delta)
, we can compute the torsion part
\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{0}
(which involves the multiplicative structure of
S
) via the
T
-module
{\text{Hom}}_{T}(S_{\delta},T)
(which can be computed as the kernel of the transpose of a presentation matrix of
S_{\delta}
as a
T
-module).
Using his duality, Jouanolou proved many beautiful formulas involving resultants (
[
38
,
36
,
37
,
35
,
34
,
33
]
).
The expository references
[
8
,
5
,
6
,
14
]
show how Jouanolou’s work (in particular, his duality) is still relevant in modern elimination theory.

Our goal is to extend this duality to algebras that are not complete intersections, in fact not even Gorenstein.
To this end, we introduce a new generalization of Gorenstein rings.
We call these rings
weakly Gorenstein
rings.
As in the classical case of Gorenstein rings where local duality takes place, our new notion of weakly Gorenstein rings is the natural environment where Jouanolou duality exists and can be generalized.
Assume
S
is Cohen-Macaulay and
\omega_{S}
is a graded canonical module of
S
.
Let
\mathfrak{a}\subset S
be a homogeneous ideal and
i\geq 0
be an integer.
We say that
S
is
i
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to
\mathfrak{a}
if there exists a fixed homogeneous element
y\in\omega_{S}
that generates
\omega_{S}
generically and locally in codimension at most
i
at every prime that contains
\mathfrak{a}
.
More precisely, we require that

\dim\big(\text{Supp}\left(\omega_{S}/Sy\right)\big)\,<\,\dim(S)\quad\text{ and }\quad\dim\big(\text{Supp}\left(\omega_{S}/Sy\right)\cap V(\mathfrak{a})\big)\,<\,\dim(S)-i.

Our first main result says that a generalization of Jouanolou duality holds for weakly Gorenstein rings.
Indeed, let
B=T[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be a positively graded polynomial ring mapping onto
S
and assume that
S
is a perfect
B
-module of codimension
c
.
If
S
is
(i+1)
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to
{\mathfrak{m}}
, then there is a graded
S
-isomorphism

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(S)\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i+c-d}(S,T)\left(\deg(y)\right)

(see
Theorem 2.8
).
When
i=0
and
S
is standard graded with
c=d
,
and
\delta:=-\deg(y)
,
we prove that there
is an isomorphism
\omicron:\,\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{\delta}\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}T
and that the multiplication maps

\,\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{j}\otimes_{T}S_{\delta-j}\rightarrow\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{\delta}\,
followed by
\omicron
are perfect pairings
inducing isomorphisms
\,\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)_{j}\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\;{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(S_{\delta-j},T\right)\,
as above (see
Theorem 2.12
). We provide explicit inverses of these isomorphisms, which we construct from any suitable element

\Delta
in the annihilator of the diagonal ideal, the kernel
of the multiplication map
S\otimes_{T}S\rightarrow S
(see
Theorem 2.18
). The significance of such explicit
inverses is that they reduce the computation of
\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(S)
, as an ideal in
S
,
to the computation of
{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}(S,T)
. Jouanolou achieved this by means of Morley forms, which our construction generalizes. We will elaborate on
this for
the special case of symmetric algebras, in the second part of the Introduction.

Surprisingly, many classes of algebras and ideals of interest satisfy the weakly Gorenstein condition.
We prove that determinantal rings tend to have this property (see
Theorem 3.3
), and so do symmetric algebras as long as they are Cohen-Macaulay (see
Theorem 4.6
).
Our approach to showing that symmetric algebras have the weakly Gorenstein property is by computing explicitly the canonical module.
The formula for the canonical module is interesting in its own right, and yields several applications.

We now describe a family of symmetric algebras that satisfy the weakly Gorenstein property, their canonical modules, and some related results that we obtain along the way.
Let
(R,{\mathfrak{m}})
be a
d
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay local ring, and
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\subset R
be an ideal minimally generated by
n
elements and of codimension
g=\text{ht}(I)\geq 2
.
One says that
I
has the property
F_{0}
if for any
\mathfrak{p}\in V(I)
the minimal number of generators satisfies
\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq\dim(R_{\mathfrak{p}})+1
.
For any
k\geq 0
, one says that
I
has the sliding depth property
{\text{SD}}_{k}
if the depth of the
i
-th Koszul homology with respect to the sequence
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
is at least
d-n+i+k
for all
i\geq 0
.
The condition
F_{0}
is necessary for the Cohen-Macaulayness of
\text{Sym}(I)
, and thus unavoidable to show the weakly Gorenstein condition.
Under the additional assumptions that
R
is Gorenstein with infinite residue field and that
I
satisfies
{\text{SD}}_{1}
, in
Theorem 4.6
, we compute explicitly the canonical module of
\text{Sym}(I)
and prove that
\text{Sym}(I)
is
d
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to the ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I).

These results are applicable in a wide range of situations since the condition
{\text{SD}}_{1}
is satisfied by several classes of ideals (for instance, strongly Cohen-Macaulay ideals, and so in particular, perfect ideals of codimension two and perfect Gorenstein ideals codimension three).
Our formula for the canonical module of symmetric algebras coincides with a formula for the canonical module of certain Rees algebras (see
[
22
]
), which leads to interesting consequences in
Section 4
.

Our main tool to compute
\omega_{\text{Sym}(I)}
is a new complex that mends one of the main drawbacks of the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
.
The approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
is ubiquitous in the study of blowup algebras, and it provides a resolution of the symmetric algebra in many cases of relevance.
However, the fact that is made up of Koszul syzygies, which are typically not free, can be a non trivial obstacle.
To remedy this problem we introduce a halfway resolution that refines
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
.
In
Section 4
, we introduce the new complex
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}
that consists of free modules in the last
g-1
positions and that coincides with
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
in the remaining positions.
The complex
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}
is acyclic when
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
is.

Furthermore, these halfway free resolutions lead to actual free resolutions of the symmetric algebra for special families of ideals such as almost complete intersections and perfect ideals of deviation two (see
Theorem 4.11
).
The construction of the complex
\mathcal{L}_{\bullet}
is notable since computing free resolutions of symmetric algebras is a problem of tall order.

1.2.

Applications to blowup algebras.

Our generalization of Jouanolou duality and the definition of weakly Gorenstein rings provide a general framework to study blowup algebras.
We are particularly interested in finding the defining equations of the Rees algebra.
Since Rees algebras appear as the coordinate ring of the blowup of a variety along a subvariety, the significance of finding their defining equations becomes apparent.
This problem has been extensively studied by algebraic geometers, commutative algebraists, and researchers in applied areas like geometric modeling (see, e.g.,
[
42
,
43
,
44
,
9
,
39
,
12
,
55
,
13
,
15
,
27
,
41
,
11
,
48
,
47
]
).

When the symmetric algebra is a complete intersection, the classical Jouanolou duality is the standard (and the most forceful) tool to compute the defining equations of the Rees algebra (see
[
9
,
42
,
39
]
).
We now describe and justify how our generalized Jouanolou duality can and does play a similar role when the symmetric algebra is only Cohen-Macaulay.

Let
\mathbb{k}
be a field,
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be a standard graded polynomial ring,
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\subset R
be the graded irrelevant ideal, and
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\subset R
be an ideal minimally generated by
n
forms of degree
D\geq 1
.
Let
T=\mathbb{k}[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
be a standard graded polynomial ring and
\mathfrak{n}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})\subset T
be the graded irrelevant ideal.
Let
B=R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}T
be a standard bigraded polynomial ring, and consider the bihomogenous epimorphism

\Phi:B\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)=R[It]=\bigoplus_{j=0}^{\infty}I^{j}t^{j}\subset R[t],\quad x_{i}\mapsto x_{i}\text{ and }y_{i}\mapsto f_{i}t.

Then
\mathcal{J}=\text{Ker}(\Phi)\subset B
is the defining ideal of
\mathcal{R}(I)
.
The graph of the rational map

\mathcal{G}:{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{d-1}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{n-1}

determined by the forms
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
is naturally given as
\Gamma={\text{BiProj}}(B/\mathcal{J})\subset{\text{BiProj}}(B)={\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{d-1}\times_{\mathbb{k}}{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{n-1}
.

Traditionally one considers the Rees algebra as a natural epimorphic image of the symmetric algebra
\text{Sym}(I)
of
I
and one studies the kernel of this map,

0\rightarrow\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\text{Sym}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0.

The kernel
\mathcal{A}
is the
R
-torsion submodule of
\text{Sym}(I)
.
The defining equations of
\text{Sym}(I)
are easily read from a minimal presentation matrix of
I
.
An almost unavoidable constraint in the study of blowup algebras is the
G_{d}
condition.
The ideal
I
satisfies
G_{d}
if
\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq\dim(R_{\mathfrak{p}})
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in V(I)
such that
\text{ht}(\mathfrak{p})<d
.
Furthermore, we need the conditions
F_{0}
and
{\text{SD}}_{1}
to show that
\text{Sym}(I)
has the weakly Gorenstein property with respect to
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I)
.
Under these conditions, in
Theorem 5.3
, we provide a general framework of dualities to study blowup algebras.
Let
\delta=(g-1)D-d
and
\beta=d-g+2
.
Suppose that
n=d+1
,
g=\text{ht}(I)\geq 2
, and
I\subset R
satisfies the conditions
G_{d}
and
{\text{SD}}_{1}
.
We then show that the following six statements hold:

(i)

\mathcal{A}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(I))
.

(ii)

For all
0\leq i\leq d-1
, there is an isomorphism of bigraded
B
-modules

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(\text{Sym}(I))\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-\delta,-\beta\right).

In particular,
\mathcal{A}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(I))\cong{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-\delta,-\beta\right)
.

(iii)

For all
i<0
and
i>\delta
, we have
\mathcal{A}_{(i,*)}=0
.
There is an isomorphism
\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,*)}\cong T(-\beta)
of graded
T
-modules.
For all
0\leq i\leq\delta
, we have the equality of
\text{Sym}(I)
-ideals

\mathcal{A}_{(\geq i,*)}\;=\;0:_{\text{Sym}(I)}{\mathfrak{m}}^{\delta+1-i}.

(iv)

\mathcal{A}
is minimally generated in
\mathbf{x}
-degree at most
(g-2)D-d+1
.

(v)

Let
0\leq i\leq\delta
.
The natural multiplication map

\mu:\mathcal{A}_{(i,*)}\;\otimes_{T}\;\text{Sym}(I)_{(\delta-i,*)}\;\rightarrow\;\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,*)},\;a\otimes b\mapsto a\cdot b

is a perfect pairing that induces the abstract isomorphism

\nu:\mathcal{A}_{(i,*)}\;\xrightarrow{\;\cong\;}\;{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I)_{(\delta-i,*)},\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,*)}\right)

seen in part (ii).

(vi)

For all
2\leq i\leq d+1
, there is an isomorphism of bigraded
B
-modules

\text{H}_{\mathfrak{n}}^{i}(\text{Sym}(I))\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{R}^{i-1}(\text{Sym}(I),R)\left(-(g-1)D,g-1\right).

A couple of words regarding the results of
Theorem 5.3
are in place.
Part (i) simply means that
I
is an ideal of linear type on the punctured spectrum on
R
.
Part (ii) comes by applying our generalization of Jouanolou duality and from the fact that we prove
\text{Sym}(I)
to be
d
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to the ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I)
.
Part (iii) shows that the graded components of
\mathcal{A}
with respect to the
\mathbf{x}
-grading can be read from the natural filtration

\mathcal{A}=0:_{\text{Sym}(I)}{\mathfrak{m}}^{\delta+1}\;\;\supset\;\;0:_{\text{Sym}(I)}{\mathfrak{m}}^{\delta}\;\;\supset\;\;\cdots\;\;\supset\;\;0:_{\text{Sym}(I)}{\mathfrak{m}}^{2}\;\;\supset\;\;0:_{\text{Sym}(I)}{\mathfrak{m}}=\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,*)}.

Part (iv) goes even further, it gives an upper bound for the
\mathbf{x}
-degree of the minimal generators of
\mathcal{A}
, and it can actually be sharp (see
§6.2
).
Part (v) implies that the abstract isomorphism

\mathcal{A}\,=\,\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(I))\,\cong\,{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-\delta,-\beta\right)

naturally comes from a multiplication map, and this becomes a fundamental fact in our approach to study blowup algebras.
Part (vi) gives a generalized Jouanolou duality statement with
R
taking the role of
T
.
To show part (vi), since
\text{Sym}(I)
is only
0
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to the ideal
\mathfrak{n}\text{Sym}(I)
(see
Section 4
), we instead rely on the isomorphism of part (ii) and a duality result of Herzog and Rahimi (
[
24
]
).

In a similar vein, we relate the freeness of
\mathcal{A}
to the depth of
\mathcal{R}(I)
.
Explicitly, we prove in
Section 5
that
\mathcal{A}
is a free
T
-module if and only if
0pt(\mathcal{R}(I))\geq d
.

A remarkable feature of the classical Jouanolou duality is that it can be made completely explicit in terms of
Morley forms
.
However, the usual notion of Morley forms is not enough in our setting.
For this reason, we devise a new reduction procedure that makes explicit the perfect pairing of part (v).
We now briefly describe our generalization of the theory of Morley forms.
Let
\mathbb{D}
be the kernel of the natural multiplication map
\text{Sym}(I)\otimes_{T}\text{Sym}(I)\rightarrow\text{Sym}(I)
.
We choose a suitable element
\Delta\in\text{Sym}(I)\otimes_{T}\text{Sym}(I)
in the annihilator of the diagonal ideal
\mathbb{D}
that is homogeneous of degree
\delta
in the
x
-grading.
Our definition of Morley forms
{\text{morl}}_{(\delta-i,i)}
comes by considering the graded components of this element
\Delta
.
By applying
Theorem 2.18
, we show that there is an explicit and computable homogeneous
T
-homomorphism

\xi\,:\,{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I)_{(\delta-i,*)},\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,*)}\right)\rightarrow\mathcal{A}_{(i,*)},\qquad u\mapsto\frac{\Phi_{\Delta}(\omicron\circ u)}{\alpha}

that gives the inverse map of the isomorphism
\nu:\mathcal{A}_{(i,*)}\rightarrow{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I)_{(\delta-i,*)},\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,*)}\right)
.
Here
\Phi_{\Delta}
is a map with target
\mathcal{A}
and
\alpha\in T
is a non zero element, both determined by
\Delta
.
For more details on the notation, see
§2.1
,
§2.2
, and
§5.1
.

Since
\text{Sym}(I)
is assumed to be Cohen-Macaulay, it is of dimension
d+1
and
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I)
is a minimal prime of
\text{Sym}(I)
. When
\text{Sym}(I)
is a complete intersection at the minimal prime
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I)
, we provide a simple and direct method to find the required element
\Delta
(see
§5.1
). Several classes of ideals satisfy the condition that
\text{Sym}(I)
is a complete intersection at
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I)
(see
§6.1
and
§6.2
).
If
\text{Sym}(I)
is a complete intersection, then
\Delta
coincides with the element used by Jouanolou in the construction of the classical Morley forms.

The last part of the paper is dedicated to applications.
We show that our results can deal with cases that were unreachable with previously existing methods.
Our presentation here is divided in terms of two families of ideals that we treat.

Zero dimensional ideals.

If
I\subset R
is additionally an
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary ideal, then we prove that
\mathcal{R}(I)
can even be approximated by two better understood algebras.
One is the usual symmetric algebra
\text{Sym}(I)
.
And the other, less standard choice is the symmetric algebra
\text{Sym}(E)
, where
E
is the module defined by the Koszul syzygies.
In this case, the ideal
I
satisfies all the conditions of
Theorem 5.3
, and so we can use
\text{Sym}(I)
and all the above results to approximate and to study
\mathcal{R}(I)
.
On the other hand, in
Theorem 6.3
, we also show that there is a short exact sequence
0\rightarrow\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\text{Sym}(E)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0
with
\mathcal{B}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(E))
, and that there is an isomorphism of bigraded
B
-modules

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(\text{Sym}(E))\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}\left(\text{Sym}(E),T\right)\left(-dD-d,-1\right)

for all
0\leq i\leq d-1
.
This opens new possibilities to study
\mathcal{R}(I)
when
I
is assumed be a zero dimensional ideal.
We plan to pursue this approach further in a subsequent paper.

Gorenstein ideals of codimension three.

In addition, suppose that
I\subset R
is a Gorenstein ideal of codimension three.
This case is covered by
Theorem 5.3
and so one can use the above results to study
\mathcal{R}(I)
(see
Theorem 6.6
).
Let
h\geq 1
be the degree of the homogeneous elements in a minimal alternating presentation matrix
\varphi\in R^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}
of
I
.
For ease of exposition, we restrict to the case
h=2
with the extra assumption that the monomial support of the entries of
\varphi
generates an almost complete intersection, and we leave the other cases to be studied in a subsequent paper.
For this case, in
Theorem 6.10
, we give a complete picture of the problem.
We show that the ideal
\mathcal{J}\subset B
of defining equations of
\mathcal{R}(I)
is minimally generated by the defining ideal of
\text{Sym}(I)
and three forms of bidegrees
(0,2d-2)
,
(1,d-1)
and
(1,d-1)
.
In fact, we explicitly compute these minimal generators of
\mathcal{J}
in terms of the Jacobian dual of
\varphi
and Morley forms!
In
Theorem 6.10
, we also prove that
\deg(\mathcal{G})=2^{d-2}
,
\deg(Y)=2d-2
,
0pt(\mathcal{R}(I))\geq d
, and
0pt(\text{gr}_{I}(R))\geq d-1
, where
\mathcal{G}:{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{d-1}\dashrightarrow{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{d}
is the corresponding rational map and
Y\subset{\mathbb{P}}_{\mathbb{k}}^{d}
is the closure of the image of
\mathcal{G}
.

Outline.

The structure of the paper is as follows.
In
Section 2
, we provide an extension of Jouanolou duality and Morley forms.
In
Section 3
, we identify determinantal rings that satisfy the weakly Gorenstein condition.
The weakly Gorenstein condition for symmetric algebras is studied in
Section 4
.
In

Lastly,

is dedicated to the study of specific families of ideals where we apply the methods developed in this paper.

2.
An extension of Jouanolou duality and Morley Forms

In this section, we provide a generalization of Jouanolou duality that can be applied to a plethora of situations.
We assume the following setup throughout.

Setup 2.1
.

Let
S
be a positively graded Noetherian ring with
S_{0}
a
factor ring of a local Gorenstein ring
T
.
Choose a positively graded polynomial ring
B=T[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
such that we have a graded surjection
B\twoheadrightarrow S
.
Let
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})
be the graded irrelevant ideal of
B
.
Set
b:=\deg(x_{1})+\cdots+\deg(x_{d})
.

Remark 2.2
.

In this paper, we freely use basic properties of canonical modules, and our standard reference is
[
2
]
*Chapter 3.
Since
T
is assumed to be a local Gorenstein ring, the graded canonical module of
B
is given by
\omega_{B}=B(-b)
(see
[
2
]
*§3.6).
As a consequence the graded canonical module of
S
can be computed as
\omega_{S}=\text{Ext}_{B}^{c}\left(S,B(-b)\right)
where
c=\dim(B)-\dim(S)
.

For a graded
B
-module
M
, we denote the graded
T
-dual as

{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}(M,T)\,:=\,\bigoplus_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}}{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left([M]_{-j},T\right),

and the corresponding right derived functor as
{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}(M,T)
.
Note that
{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}(M,T)
is naturally a graded
B
-module for all
i\geq 0
.
For more details on the functors
{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}
and their properties, the reader is referred to
[
2
]
*§1.5.

The following result yields a version of Jouanolou duality in terms of the canonical module of
S
, and it is applicable in great generality.

Theorem 2.3
.

Assume
Section 2
.
Suppose that
S
is perfect over
B
and of codimension
c
.
Then we have a graded isomorphism of
\,S
-modules

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(\omega_{S})\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i+c-d}(S,T)

for all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

First proof.

Let
F_{\bullet}:0\rightarrow F_{c}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow F_{1}\rightarrow F_{0}
be a minimal graded
B
-resolution of
S
.
Since
S
is perfect of codimension
c
,
F_{\bullet}
has length equal to
c
.
We then have the following isomorphisms of graded
B
-modules

\displaystyle{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i+c-d}(S,T)

Lemma 2.6

(i+1)
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to a homogeneous ideal

\,S

It then follows from the short exact sequence above that

3.
Weakly Gorenstein determinantal rings

In this section, we study the weakly Gorenstein condition for determinantal rings.
Our findings show that under reasonable assumptions the weakly Gorenstein property holds.
These results combined with our generalization of Jouanolou duality (
Theorem 2.22
) yield new tools to study local cohomology modules of determinantal rings.

Setup 3.1
.

Let
B
be a Noetherian ring and
\varphi\in B^{m\times n}
be an
m\times n
matrix with
m\leq n
.
Let
1\leq t\leq m
and
S
be the determinantal ring
S:=B/I_{t}(\varphi)
.
We consider the following submatrices of
\varphi

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}\delta&&*&\\
\hline\cr&&&\\
&&*&\\
&&&\\
\end{array}\right],\qquad\left[\begin{array}[]{c|ccc}&&&\\
&&&\\
\varphi^{\prime}&&*&\\
&&&\\
\end{array}\right],\qquad\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}&&\varphi^{\prime\prime}&\\
\hline\cr&&&\\
&&*&\\
&&&\\
\end{array}\right]

where
\delta\in B^{(t-1)\times(t-1)}
,
\varphi^{\prime}\in B^{m\times(t-1)}
, and
\varphi^{\prime\prime}\in B^{(t-1)\times n}
.
Let
\Delta:=\det(\delta)\in B
.

We first prove an elementary lemma.

Lemma 3.2
.

We have the inclusion
I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime})\cdot I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime\prime})S\subset\Delta S
in
S
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on
t
.
The case
t=1
is vacuous.
As we have
\varphi_{i,1}\varphi_{1,j}-\varphi_{1,1}\varphi_{i,j}\in I_{2}(\varphi)
for all
i,j
, the claim follows for the case
t=2
.

Suppose that
t\geq 3
.
Since containments are preserved under extensions, we may assume that
B={\mathbb{Z}}[x_{i,j}]
where
x_{i,j}
are independent variables and
\varphi=(x_{i,j})
is the generic
m\times n
matrix.
Since
S
is a Cohen-Macaulay domain (see
[
25
]
*Corollary 4) and
\Delta S\neq 0,
all the associated primes of
\Delta S
have height one.

Let
P\in{\text{Spec}}(S)
be a minimal prime of
(x_{1,1},x_{1,2})S
.
Set
p=P\cap{\mathbb{Z}}
,
\overline{B}=B\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\text{Quot}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p)
, and
\overline{S}=S\otimes_{\mathbb{Z}}{\text{Quot}}({\mathbb{Z}}/p)
.
Notice that
\text{ht}(P)\geq\text{ht}(P\overline{S})
.
Since
t\geq 3
, the minimal monomial generators of the initial ideal of
I_{t}(\varphi)\overline{B}
with any antidiagonal term order do not involve the variables
x_{1,1}
and
x_{1,2}
(see, e.g.,
[
46
]
*Theorem 16.28), and this implies that

form a regular sequence on
\overline{S}
(see, e.g.,
[
17
]
*Proposition 15.15).
Thus
\text{ht}(P\overline{S})\geq 2,
and so
\text{ht}(P)\geq 2.
Since this holds for every
P,
it follows that
\text{ht}((x_{1,1},x_{1,2})S)=2.

Hence, there is an element
b\in B
such that the image of
x_{1,1}+bx_{1,2}
in
S
is in no associated prime of
\Delta S
.
After elementary column operations, we obtain that
\varphi_{1,1}
is not contained in any of the associated primes of
\Delta S
, and so it suffices to show the containment after localizing at the element
\varphi_{1,1}
.
Once
\varphi_{1,1}
is invertible, we can apply standard arguments to reduce to the case
t-1
.
∎

The following result provides families of determinantal rings that have the weakly Gorenstein property.

Theorem 3.3
.

In addition to
Section 3
, assume that
B
is normal, Gorenstein, and local with infinite residue field.
Suppose that

\text{ht}\left(I_{t}(\varphi)\right)=(m-t+1)(n-t+1)\quad\text{ and }\quad\text{ht}\left(I_{t-1}(\varphi)\right)>(m-t+1)(n-t+1).

Let
\mathfrak{a}\subset S
be an ideal.

After elementary row operations, we may assume that
\,\text{ht}\left(I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime\prime})S\right)>0
.
Then

is
\,i

\mathfrak{a},\,
for

.

After elementary column operations, we may further assume that
\text{ht}\left(I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime})S\right)>0.
In this case
I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime})^{n-m}S
is the canonical module of
\,S,
and the image of
\,\Delta^{n-m}
in this ideal is a weak generator
of the canonical module.

Proof.

Since the row and column spaces of
\varphi\otimes_{B}S
have rank

, we may assume that
\text{ht}\left(I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime\prime})S\right)\geq 1
after elementary row operations and
\text{ht}\left(I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime})S\right)\geq 1

Let

From
[

]

, where

(see also
[

]

\omega_{S}\,\cong\,\omega_{S^{\prime}}\otimes_{S^{\prime}}S\,\cong\,I_{t-1}(X^{\prime})^{n-m}S^{\prime}\otimes_{S^{\prime}}S\,\twoheadrightarrow\,I_{t-1}(\varphi^{\prime})^{n-m}S.

It follows that the kernel of the above surjection is a torsion submodule of

, and hence zero because

is a maximal Cohen-Macaulay module.

Let

be the image of
\Delta^{n-m}
in

Notice that

under the isomorphism

Thus, by

,

is

-weakly Gorenstein with respect to

when

∎

To be more precise, we provide a sample that will be useful later (see

Proposition 3.4

Let

\,\text{ht}(I_{2}(\varphi))=n-1

, as required.

We now settle the proposition for an arbitrary field
\mathbb{k}
.
Let
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
be an infinite field containing
\mathbb{k}
, and set
S^{\prime}:=S\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
.
The computation above of the canonical module shows that
\dim_{\mathbb{k}^{\prime}}\big(\left[\omega_{S^{\prime}}\right]_{(d-(n-1)D,n-1)}\big)=1
.
This implies that
\dim_{\mathbb{k}}\big(\left[\omega_{S}\right]_{(d-(n-1)D,n-1)}\big)=1
.
Therefore, take an non-zero element
0\neq y\in\left[\omega_{S}\right]_{(d-(n-1)D,n-1)}
.
It necessarily follows that, up to multiplication by a unit in
\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
, the extension of
y
into
\omega_{S^{\prime}}\cong\omega_{S} \otimes_{\mathbb{k}}\mathbb{k}^{\prime}
equals the element chosen in
Claim 3.4.1
to make
S^{\prime}
weakly Gorenstein over
{\mathfrak{m}}S^{\prime}
.
This completes the proof for the general case.
∎

4.
Weakly Gorenstein symmetric algebras

The principal motivation of this section is to find a comprehensive family of ideals whose symmetric algebra has the weakly Gorenstein property.
This first goal will allow us to apply our generalized Jouanolou duality (
Theorem 2.22
) in several new cases.
However, along the way, we achieve more:

(i)

we compute explicitly the canonical module of a large family of symmetric algebras,

(ii)

when the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
is acyclic, we define a new complex that also resolves the symmetric algebra but has the advantage of having free modules in the last
g-1
positions, where
g
is the grade of the ideal,

(iii)

when the ideal is an almost complete intersection or perfect of deviation two, we give an explicit free resolution for the symmetric algebra, provided
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
is acyclic.

Modules of Koszul cycles were used before in
[
21
]
to obtain information
about the free resolution of the symmetric algebra of an ideal
I\subset R
and
about
\,\omega_{\text{Sym}(I)}\otimes_{\text{Sym}(I)}R
.

Throughout this section we assume the following setup.

Setup 4.1
.

Let
(R,{\mathfrak{m}})
be a
d
-dimensional Cohen-Macaulay local ring.
Let
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\subsetneq R
be an ideal minimally generated by
n
elements, and set
g=\text{ht}(I)\geq 1
to be the height of
I
.
Let
B=R[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}]
be a standard graded polynomial ring.
There is a surjection of standard graded
R
-algebras
B\twoheadrightarrow\text{Sym}(I)
mapping
y_{i}
to
f_{i}\in[\text{Sym}(I)]_{1}.
Let
K_{\bullet}

be the Koszul complex associated to the sequence
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
.
Let
Z_{\bullet}
and
H_{\bullet}
be the cycles and homologies of
K_{\bullet}
, respectively.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
k\geq 0
be an integer.

(G)

I
satisfies the
condition
G_{k}
if

\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq\text{ht}(\mathfrak{p})
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in V(I)\subset{\text{Spec}}(R)
with
\text{ht}(\mathfrak{p})\leq k-1
.

(F)

I
satisfies the
condition
F_{0}
if

\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})\leq\text{ht}(\mathfrak{p})+1
for all
\mathfrak{p}\in{\text{Spec}}(R)
.

(SD)

I
has the
sliding depth condition
{\text{SD}}_{k}
if
0pt(H_{i})\geq\min\{d-g,d-n+i+k\}
for all
i
.

(SCM)

I
is
strongly Cohen-Macaulay
if
H_{i}
is a Cohen-Macaulay module for all
i
.

One can check that the conditions strongly Cohen-Macaulay and
{\text{SD}}_{k}
yield the following inequalities

0pt(Z_{i})\,\geq\,\begin{cases}\min\{d,d-g+2\}&\text{ if $I$ is strongly Cohen-Macaulay}\\
\min\{d,d-n+i+k+1\}&\text{ if $I$ satisfies ${\text{SD}}_{k}$}\end{cases}

for all
i\geq 0
(see
[
23
]
*p.161).
Recall that height
2
perfect ideals and height
3
Gorenstein ideals (perfect ideals with last Betti number
1
) are strongly Cohen-Macaulay (see
[
31
]
*Proposition 0.3,
[
1
,
18
,
49
]
,
[
57
]
).

Let
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
be the approximation complex

\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}:\quad 0\rightarrow Z_{n-1}\otimes_{R}B(-n+1)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow Z_{i}\otimes_{R}B(-i)\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow Z_{1}\otimes_{R}B(-1)\rightarrow Z_{0}\otimes_{R}B

corresponding to the sequence
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n},
which is a complex of graded
B
-modules.
For more details regarding approximation complexes the reader is referred to
[
20
]
.
For the sake of completeness, we include a couple of well-known results regarding symmetric algebras.

Lemma 4.3
.

If
\,I\subset R
has
F_{0}
on the punctured spectrum of
R
, then
\dim(\text{Sym}(I))=\max\left\{n,d+1\right\}
.

Proof.

By the Huneke-Rossi formula (see
[
28
, Theorem 2.6]
,
[
56
, Theorem 1.2.1]
), we have

\dim(\text{Sym}(I))=\sup\left\{\mu(I_{\mathfrak{p}})+\dim(R/\mathfrak{p})\mid\mathfrak{p}\in{\text{Spec}}(R)\right\}.

Since
I
has
F_{0}
on the punctured spectrum of
R
, we deduce
\dim(\text{Sym}(I))=\max\left\{\mu(I),\dim(R)+1\right\}=\max\left\{n,d+1\right\}
.
∎

Lemma 4.4
.

If
\,I\subset R
satisfies the conditions
{\text{SD}}_{0}
and
F_{0}
, then the following statements hold
\,:

(i)

\text{Sym}(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay.

(ii)

The approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
is acyclic.

Proof.

See for instance
[
20
, Theorem 5.4, Theorem 10.1]
.
∎

We now construct a new complex that will allow us to compute the canonical module of
\text{Sym}(I)
in many cases.
Apparently, the existence of this quite useful complex has been unnoticed. Its construction was inspired to us by

Proposition 4.5
.

Let

The following theorem contains the main results of this section.

Theorem 4.6
.

Assume
Section 4
where
R
is Gorenstein with infinite residue field.
Suppose that the ideal
I\subset R
has height
g=\text{ht}(I)\geq 2
and satisfies the conditions
F_{0}
and
{\text{SD}}_{1}
. After changing the generators
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
of
\,I
the element
y_{1}
is a non zerodivisor on
\text{Sym}(I),
and
the following statements hold
\,:

(i)

\omega_{\text{Sym}(I)}\cong\left(f_{1},y_{1}\right)^{g-2}\text{Sym}(I)(-1).

(ii)

\text{Sym}(I)
is
d
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to the ideal
{\mathfrak{m}}\,\text{Sym}(I),
and the image of
y_{1}^{g-2}
in
\omega_{\text{Sym}(I)}
is a weak
generator of the canonical module of degree
g-1.

Proof.

The condition
F_{0}
implies, in particular, that
n\leq d+1
.
From
Section 4
and
Section 4
we have that
\text{Sym}(I)
is a Cohen-Macaulay ring of dimension
d+1
and that the approximation complex
\mathcal{Z}_{\bullet}
is acyclic.
As
\dim(S/(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})S)=\dim(R)=d,
it then follows that
\text{grade}\left((y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})S\right)\geq 1
.
So, after possibly changing the generators of
I
, we can assume that
y_{1}
is a non zerodivisor on
S,
as asserted.

The condition
{\text{SD}}_{1}
yields the following lower bounds for the depth of the Koszul cycles

0pt(Z_{i})\,\geq\,\min\left\{d,d-n+i+2\right\}\quad\text{for all $i$}.

This implies that
0pt(\mathcal{Z}_{i})\geq\min\left\{d+n,d+i+2\right\}
.
We consider the complex of graded
B
-modules

\mathfrak{L}_{\bullet}:\quad 0\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{g-2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{1}\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{0}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{n-g}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{0}\rightarrow 0

constructed in
Section 4
, which is acyclic by the current assumptions.
To simplify notation, set
S:=\text{Sym}(I)
.

Denote by
(\mathfrak{L}_{\bullet},\partial_{\bullet})
the complex
\mathfrak{L}_{\bullet}
with its differential.
Let
U_{i}={\text{Coker}}(\partial_{i+1})
.
For all
0\leq i\leq n-g
, we have the short exact sequence

0\rightarrow U_{i+1}\rightarrow\mathcal{Z}_{i}\rightarrow U_{i}\rightarrow 0

that gives the induced exact sequence

\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1-i-1}(U_{i+1},\omega_{B})\rightarrow\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1-i}(U_{i},\omega_{B})\rightarrow\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1-i}(\mathcal{Z}_{i},\omega_{B}).

For
0\leq i\leq n-g
, we have
\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1-i}(\mathcal{Z}_{i},\omega_{B})=0
since
n-i-1>\max\{0,n-i-2\}\geq d+n-0pt(\mathcal{Z}_{i})
.
Thus we obtain the surjection

\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1-i-1}(U_{i+1},\omega_{B})\twoheadrightarrow\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1-i}(U_{i},\omega_{B})

for all
0\leq i\leq n-g
.
By composing these surjections, we obtain a surjective homomorphism of graded
B
-modules

\text{Ext}_{B}^{g-2}(U_{n-g+1},\omega_{B})\twoheadrightarrow\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1}(U_{0},\omega_{B})=\text{Ext}_{B}^{n-1}(S,\omega_{B})\cong\omega_{S}.

The complex
0\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{g-2}\rightarrow\cdots\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{1}\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{0}\rightarrow 0
gives a homogeneous free
B
-resolution of
U_{n-g+1}
.

If
g=2
, then
U_{n-g+1}
is equal to the free
B
-module
B(-n+1)
.
In this case we have a surjection
{\text{Hom}}_{B}(U_{n-g+1},\omega_{B})\twoheadrightarrow\omega_{S}
, hence
\omega_{S}
is generated by one homogeneous element of degree
1;
this means that
\omega_{S}\cong S(-1)
and, in particular,
S
is Gorenstein.
Hence the proof is complete for the case
g=2
.

Assume that
g\geq 3
. We are going to show that

\text{Ext}_{B}^{g-2}(U_{n-g+1},\omega_{B}),
after tensoring with
S
and
factoring out
S
-torsion, maps isomorphically to both
\omega_{S}
and

(f_{1},y_{1})^{g-2}S(-1),
which will finish the proof of (i).

The module
\text{Ext}_{B}^{g-2}(U_{n-g+1},\omega_{B})
is the cokernel of the map

{\text{Hom}}_{B}(\mathfrak{T}_{g-3},\omega_{B})\rightarrow{\text{Hom}}_{B}(\mathfrak{T}_{g-2},\omega_{B}).

The map
\mathfrak{T}_{g-2}\rightarrow\mathfrak{T}_{g-3}
is given explicitly as

\bigoplus_{j=1}^{g-1}B(-n+g-j)\xrightarrow{\;\;\;\;}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{g-2}B^{n}(-n+g-j),

and its matrix representation with respect to the standard bases is

\mathfrak{A}\;=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{cccccc}f_{1}&y_{1}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
(-1)^{n-1}f_{n}&(-1)^{n-1}y_{n}&0&\cdots&0&0\\
\hline\cr 0&-f_{1}&y_{1}&\cdots&0&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&(-1)^{n}f_{n}&(-1)^{n-1}y_{n}&\cdots&0&0\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
\hline\cr 0&0&0&\cdots&(-1)^{g-3}f_{1}&y_{1}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\cdots&(-1)^{n+g-4}f_{n}&(-1)^{n-1}y_{n}\\
\end{array}\right]\ .

Let
\mathfrak{C}=\mathfrak{A}^{T}
and

\Omega\;:=\;{\text{Coker}}\left(\bigoplus_{j=1}^{g-2}\omega_{B}^{n}(n-g+j)\xrightarrow{\;\;\mathfrak{C}\;\;}\bigoplus_{j=1}^{g-1}\omega_{B}(n-g+j)\right).

Denote by
w_{j}
the generator of
\Omega
corresponding to the
j
-th column of
\mathfrak{C},
and write
-
for images in
S
. Since in
S
we have the relations

\overline{f_{i}y_{j}}=\overline{f_{j}y_{i}},
we obtain a surjective homomorphism
of graded
B
-modules

\Omega\twoheadrightarrow(f_{1},y_{1})^{g-2}S(-1),\;\;\text{ where }\;\;w_{j}\mapsto(-1)^{j\choose 2}\,\overline{f_{1}^{j-1}y_{1}^{g-j-1}}.

Thus we have surjective homomorphisms
of graded
S
-modules

\Omega\otimes_{B}S\twoheadrightarrow(f_{1},y_{1})^{g-2}S(-1)\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\Omega\otimes_{B}S\,\twoheadrightarrow\,\omega_{S}.

The matrix
\mathfrak{A}
has size
(g-2)n\times(g-1)
, and
g\geq 3.
So by the pigeonhole principle, every
(g-1)\times(g-1)
submatrix of
\mathfrak{A}
contains, after possibly multiplying one column by
-1,
a
2\times(g-1)
submatrix of the form

\left(\begin{array}[]{cccccccc}0&\cdots&0&(-1)^{i_{1}-1}f_{i_{1}}&(-1)^{i_{1}-1}y_{i_{1}}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&\cdots&0&(-1)^{i_{2}-1}f_{i_{2}}&(-1)^{i_{2}-1}y_{i_{2}}&0&\cdots&0\end{array}\right).

Accordingly,
I_{g-1}(\mathfrak{C})\subset\left(\left\{f_{i}y_{j}-f_{j}y_{i}\right\}_{i,j}\right)\subset B
, hence
I_{g-1}(\mathfrak{C}\otimes_{B}S)=0
.
Notice that

\overline{y_{1}^{g-2}}\in I_{g-2}(\mathfrak{C}\otimes_{B}S)

and
\overline{y_{1}^{g-2}}
is a non zerodivisor on
S
, therefore
\text{rank}_{S}(\mathfrak{C}\otimes_{B}S)=g-2
and
\text{rank}_{S}(\Omega\otimes_{B}S)=1
.
The canonical module
\omega_{S}
is faithful and the epimorphic image of a module of rank
1
, hence
\omega_{S}
is an
S
-module of rank
1
.
The ideal
(f_{1},y_{1})^{g-2}S
contains the non zerodivisor
\overline{y_{1}^{g-2}}
, and so it is a torsion-free
S
-module of rank
1
.
Finally, since
\omega_{S}
is always torsion-free, we obtain the isomorphisms

\omega_{S}\;\cong\;\frac{\Omega\otimes_{B}S}{\text{tor}\left(\Omega\otimes_{B}S\right)}\;\cong\;(f_{1},y_{1})^{g-2}S(-1).

This concludes the proof of part (i) of the theorem.

We now concentrate on part (ii) of the theorem, which becomes straightforward after having computed the canonical module
\omega_{S}
.

Since
(\overline{y_{1}},\ldots,\overline{y_{n}})\cdot\overline{f_{1}}\subset(\overline{y_{1}})
in
S
, it follows that

(\overline{y_{1}},\ldots,\overline{y_{n}})^{g-2}\,\subset\,\text{Ann}_{S}\left((\overline{f_{1}},\overline{y_{1}})^{g-2}/(\overline{y_{1}})^{g-2}\right)\,=\,\text{Ann}_{S}\left(\omega_{S}/Su_{1}\right),

where
u_{1}
denotes the image in
\omega_{S}
of the element
w_{1}\in\Omega.

As a consequence, we get

\text{ht}\left(\text{Ann}_{S}\left(\omega_{S}/Su_{1}\right)\right)\geq 1\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\text{ht}\left(\text{Ann}_{S}\left(\omega_{S}/Su_{1}\right)+{\mathfrak{m}}S\right)\geq d+1.

Therefore,
S
is
d
-weakly Gorenstein with respect to
{\mathfrak{m}}S
, and this establishes the remaining part (ii).
∎

and

Remark 4.8
.

In addition to the hypotheses of

assume that

An interesting question is whether

is weakly Gorenstein with respect to

, where

The natural choice for making

Remark 4.9
.

Our computation of the canonical module of
\text{Sym}(I)
extends or complements known results in the literature.
In

However, the result of
[

In

of

.
We exploit this connection between

and
\omega_{\mathcal{R}(I)}
in the next corollary to deduce that

is of linear type. Recall that an ideal

is said to be

if the natural map
\text{Sym}(I)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
is an isomorphism. A version of

is proved in

, with different
methods and the hypothesis that

be strongly Cohen-Macaulay as opposed to

and

Corollary 4.10
.

In addition to
Section 4

assume that
\,:

(a)

I\subset R
satisfies the conditions
F_{0}
and
{\text{SD}}_{1}
, and
I
is generically a complete intersection
(
a complete intersection locally at each of its associated primes
)
with
g=\text{ht}(I)\geq 2.

(b)

\text{gr}_{I}(R)
is a Gorenstein ring.

Then
I
is of linear type and strongly Cohen-Macaulay.

Proof.

Notice that
R
is Gorenstein because
\text{gr}_{I}(R)
is (see, e.g.,
[

, proof of Proposition 11.16]
).
We first prove that
I
is of linear type.
By
Section 4
,
we have
\dim\left(\text{Sym}(I)\right)=d+1=\dim\left(\mathcal{R}(I)\right)
.
After dualizing, the surjection
\text{Sym}(I)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
yields the natural inclusion

\varphi:\omega_{\mathcal{R}(I)}\,\hookrightarrow\,\omega_{\text{Sym}(I)}.

Since
I
is generically a complete intersection with
g\geq 1
and
\text{gr}_{I}(R)
is Gorenstein,
[

, Theorem 2.1]
shows that
\mathcal{R}(I)
is Cohen-Macaulay.
With these hypotheses it follows from

[

, Corollaries 2.5 and 2.9]
that
\omega_{\mathcal{R}(I)}\cong(f_{1},y_{1})^{g-2}\mathcal{R}(I)(-1).
Combining this with

(i) we obtain a natural homomorphism of graded
\text{Sym}(I)
-modules

Now

Setup 5.1
.

Let
\mathbb{k}
be a field,
R=\mathbb{k}[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}]
be a standard graded polynomial ring and
{\mathfrak{m}}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d})\subset R
be the graded irrelevant ideal.
Let

be an ideal minimally generated by
n
forms

f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
of the same degree

.
Let

be a standard graded polynomial ring and

Let

Since we are primarily interested in the

Let

Typically, a good way to study the Rees algebra is to approximate it by the symmetric algebra, which is much better understood, at least as far as

\mathcal{L}=\,(g_{1},\ldots,g_{u})=I_{1}\left(\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{n}\right]\cdot\varphi\right)

There is a natural exact sequence of bigraded

-modules

The other spectral sequence is obtained by a completely symmetric argument.
The two claimed isomorphisms follow immediately from the spectral sequences.

5.1.
Explicit equations via Morley forms

We are now going to apply the theory of Morley forms developed in
§2.1
to symmetric algebras of ideals.
Unlike in the classical case (where
\text{Sym}(I)
is a complete intersection), here the Morley forms
do not
give a perfect pairing.
We instead need to introduce a division/reduction to make explicit the perfect pairing seen in
Theorem 5.3
(v).
Throughout this subsection, we assume the hypotheses of
Theorem 5.3
.

Setup 5.7
.

Assume
Section 5
.
Suppose that
I=(f_{1},\ldots,f_{n})\subset R
has height
g=\text{ht}(I)\geq 2
, that
I
satisfies the conditions
G_{d}
and
{\text{SD}}_{1}
, and that

.
In particular,
Theorem 5.3
yields the isomorphism

\mathcal{A}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(I))\cong{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-\delta,-\beta\right)

where
\delta:=(g-1)D-d
and

.
Assume that the defining ideal
\mathcal{L}
of
\text{Sym}(I)
contains a bihomogeneous regular sequence
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}
that generates
\mathcal{L}
at
{\mathfrak{m}}B\in\text{Min}(\mathcal{L})
and satisfies the
conditions
\sum_{i=1}^{d}\deg_{\mathbf{x}}(\ell_{i})=\delta+d
and
\deg_{\mathbf{y}}(\ell_{i})=1

(recall that
\text{ht}(\mathcal{L})=d=\text{ht}({\mathfrak{m}}B)).

Owing to the above bigraded isomorphism for
\mathcal{A},
we have

\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,\star)}\cong T(-\delta,-\beta),
and the current definition of
\delta
is consistent with the one in
§2.1
and the
discussion following
Theorem 2.22
(see also

).

We write

\left[\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\right]\;=\;\left[x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\right]\cdot G\,

where

is a
d\times d
matrix whose entries are bihomogeneous with constant bidegrees along the columns.
Multiplying with the adjoint of

yields

\left(x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}\right)\cdot\det(G)\,\subset\,\left(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}\right)\,\subset\,\mathcal{L}.

Let
{\text{syl}}\in\text{Sym}(I)
be the image of
\det(G)
in
\text{Sym}(I)
.
Notice that
{\text{syl}}\in 0:_{\text{Sym}(I)}{\mathfrak{m}}\subset\mathcal{A}
. Moreover,
syl
is bihomogeneous
with
\text{bideg}({\text{syl}})=(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\deg_{\mathbf{x}}(\ell_{i})-d,d)=(\delta,d),
so

{\text{syl}}\in\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,d)}.

As
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d})\subset{\mathfrak{m}}B
is an inclusion of complete intersection ideals of
height
d,
we have that

(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}):_{B}{\mathfrak{m}}B=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d},\det(G))

(see
[
58
]

[
36

). Thus, up to multiplication by a unit in
\mathbb{k},
the
element
\det(G)
modulo

only depends on the ideal

(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}).
Hence, up to multiplication by a unit in
\mathbb{k},
the element

syl
is uniquely determined by
(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}).

Following standard notation in the literature, we call
syl
the
Sylvester form of
\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}
with respect to
x_{1},\ldots,x_{d}

(or the
determinant of the Jacobian dual
).

The element
{\text{syl}}\in\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,d)}

\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}
generate
\mathcal{L}
locally at the minimal prime

(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}):_{B}\mathcal{L}\,\not\subset\,{\mathfrak{m}}B.

Since
{\mathfrak{m}}B

\mathcal{L}\not\supset(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d}):_{B}{\mathfrak{m}}B=(\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{d},\det(G))
. Thus

as asserted.

We choose a generator

and
call it a
reduced Sylvester form
. It coincides with the element
\mathfrak{s}
in
Theorem 2.12
and the discussion following
Theorem 2.22
.
As
{\text{syl}}\in\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,d)}
and
\mathcal{A}_{(\delta,\star)}\cong T(-\delta,-\beta),
there
exists a unique element

so that

{\text{syl}}=\alpha\cdot{\text{rsyl}}.

In other words,

We now construct an explicit element
\Delta\in\left[0:_{\text{Sym}(I)^{e}}\mathbb{D}\right]_{(\delta,\star)}
that can be used in the definition of Morley forms, as described in
§2.1
and the
discussion following
Theorem 2.22
.
As each

is in the kernel of the multiplication map

\left[\ell_{1}\otimes 1-1\otimes\ell_{1},\;\ldots,\;\ell_{d}\otimes 1-1\otimes\ell_{d}\right]\;=\;\left[x_{1}\otimes 1-1\otimes x_{1},\;\ldots,\;x_{d}\otimes 1-1\otimes x_{d}\right]\cdot H\,,

where
H\in(B^{e})^{d\times d}
is a
d\times d
matrix whose entries are bihomogeneous with constant bidegrees along the columns.
Let

\Delta

be the image of
\det(H)
in
\text{Sym}(I)^{e}
. Notice that
\Delta\in 0:_{\text{Sym}(I)^{e}}\mathbb{D}
and that
\Delta
is bihomogeneous of bidegree
(\delta,d)
, in other words

\Delta\in[0:_{\text{Sym}(I)^{e}}\mathbb{D}]_{(\delta,d)}.

Let
\Pi:B\twoheadrightarrow T
be the homomorphism of
T
-algebras with
\Pi(x_{i})=0
for all
i
, and consider the map
\epsilon=B\otimes_{T}\Pi:B^{e}\twoheadrightarrow B
. Applying
\epsilon

to the entries of
H
we obtain a matrix
G
as above. Since
syl
is well defined up to
multiplication by a unit in
\mathbb{k},
we may assume that

\varepsilon(\Delta)={\text{syl}},

where

\varepsilon:S^{e}\twoheadrightarrow S
is defined as in
§2.1
. Recall that
{\text{syl}}=\alpha\cdot{\text{rsyl}}
and
{\text{rsyl}}=\mathfrak{s}.

On the other hand,
\varepsilon(\Delta)=\varepsilon({\text{morl}}_{(\delta,0)})
and
{\text{morl}}_{(\delta,0)}=\alpha\cdot\mathfrak{s}\otimes 1
with
\alpha
defined as in
§2.1
(i). Comparing
the two expressions for
\varepsilon(\Delta)
we see that the current definition of
\alpha
coincides with the one in
§2.1
.

Since
\varepsilon(\Delta)={\text{syl}}
and
{\text{syl}}\neq 0

where

generates the unique associated prime of the ideal

6.
Applications to certain families of ideals

In this section, we apply the results and techniques developed in previous sections.
For organizational purposes, we divide the section into two subsections.
Each subsection covers a family of ideals where our results are applied.

6.1.
Zero dimensional ideals

In this subsection, we study Rees algebras of zero dimensional ideals.
In this case, we are able to approximate the Rees algebra with two algebras: one is the symmetric algebra of the ideal, and the other is the symmetric algebra of the module whose syzyies are the Koszul syzygies of the ideal.
This provides two methods for studying Rees algebras.
We will use the following setup.

Setup 6.1
.

Assume
Section 5
with
I\subset R
an
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary ideal, and suppose that
n=d+1
.
Let
(K_{\bullet},\partial_{\bullet})
be the Koszul complex of the sequence
f_{1},\ldots,f_{n}
, and
H_{\bullet}
be its homology.
Let
E:={\text{Coker}}(\partial_{2})
be the module defined by the Koszul syzygies.

The next lemma collects some basic facts about the relation between the module
E
and the ideal
I
.

Lemma 6.2
.

The following statements hold
\,:

(i)

\text{Sym}(E)\cong B/\mathcal{K}
where
\mathcal{K}
is the determinantal ideal

\mathcal{K}\,=\,I_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{d+1}\\
f_{1}&f_{2}&\cdots&f_{d+1}\end{array}\right).

(ii)

\mathcal{R}(E)\cong\mathcal{R}(I)
, and there is a natural exact sequence

0\rightarrow\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(E))\rightarrow\text{Sym}(E)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0.

(iii)

\dim\left(\text{Sym}(E)\right)=\dim\left(\text{Sym}(I)\right)=d+1
.

Proof.

(i) By construction, a presentation of
E
is given by

K_{2}\xrightarrow{\partial_{2}}K_{1}\rightarrow E\rightarrow 0.

Let
\{e_{1},\ldots,e_{d+1}\}
be a basis of
K_{1}

with
\partial_{1}(e_{i})=f_{i}
for all
i.
Then

\{e_{i}\wedge e_{j}\}_{1\leq i<j\leq d+1}
is a basis of
K_{2},

and the map
\partial_{2}
is defined by

\partial_{2}(e_{i}\wedge e_{j})=f_{i}e_{j}-f_{j}e_{i}.

Let
\left[\partial_{2}\right]
be the matrix representation of
\partial_{2}
with respect to the chosen bases of
K_{1}
and
K_{2}
.
Then the defining ideal of
\text{Sym}(E)
is

\mathcal{K}=I_{1}\left(\left[y_{1},y_{2},\ldots,y_{d+1}\right]\cdot\left[\partial_{2}\right]\right)=\left({\{f_{i}y_{j}-f_{j}y_{i}\}}_{1\leq i<j\leq d+1}\right)=I_{2}\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}y_{1}&y_{2}&\cdots&y_{d+1}\\
f_{1}&f_{2}&\cdots&f_{d+1}\end{array}\right).

(ii) The existence of the exact sequence follows from the fact that
E_{\mathfrak{p}}\cong I_{\mathfrak{p}}=R_{\mathfrak{p}}
for every
\mathfrak{p}\in{\text{Spec}}(R)\setminus\{{\mathfrak{m}}\}
. The exact sequence in turn shows that the kernel of the natural surjection
\text{Sym}(E)\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)
is the
R
-torsion of
\text{Sym}(E).

(iii) This part follows from the Huneke-Rossi formula (see
[
28
]
,
[
56
, Theorem 1.2.1]
).
∎

As a consequence of the lemma above, we have the following two short exact sequences

0\rightarrow\mathcal{A}\rightarrow\text{Sym}(I)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0 \quad\text{ and }\quad 0\rightarrow\mathcal{B}\rightarrow\text{Sym}(E)\rightarrow\mathcal{R}(I)\rightarrow 0

where
\mathcal{A}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(I))
and
\mathcal{B}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(E))
.
The next theorem makes
\text{Sym}(I)
and
\text{Sym}(E)
good candidates to approximate
\mathcal{R}(I)
when

is
{\mathfrak{m}}
-primary.

Theorem 6.3
.

Assume
§6.1
.
Then the following statements hold
\,:

(i)

For all
0\leq i\leq d-1
, there are isomorphisms of bigraded
B
-modules

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(\text{Sym}(I))\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-dD-d-D,-2\right).

\mathcal{A}=\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{0}(\text{Sym}(I))\cong{}^{*}{\text{Hom}}_{T}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-dD-d-D,-2\right)

(ii)

-modules

Assume

\text{H}_{{\mathfrak{m}}}^{i}(\text{Sym}(I))\,\cong\,{}^{*}\text{Ext}_{T}^{i}\left(\text{Sym}(I),T\right)\left(-d(h-1),-d+1\right)

Proof.

The statement is a particular case of

We notice that with the assumptions above the ideal

is

Setup 6.7
.

In addition to
§6.2
,
assume that
I_{1}(\varphi)
is an almost complete intersection, but not a complete intersection. Let
c_{1},\ldots,c_{d+1}
be homogeneous generators of

.
The defining ideal of

is generated by the entries of the row vector

\left[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1}\right]\cdot\varphi\;=\;\left[c_{1},,\ldots,c_{d+1}\right]\cdot A,

The matrix

is referred to as the

Let

Lemma 6.8
.

Assume

. The matrix

has the following properties

(i)

\text{rank}(A)=d
.

(ii)

\text{Ker}(A)
is a free

-module generated by the vector
[y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1}]^{t}
.

(iii)

The adjoint of

is of the form

{\rm adj}(A^{t})\;=\;\left[\begin{array}[]{cccc}y_{1}\delta_{1}&y_{2}\delta_{1}&\cdots&y_{d+1}\delta_{1}\\
y_{1}\delta_{2}&y_{2}\delta_{2}&\cdots&y_{d+1}\delta_{2}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\cdots&\vdots\\
y_{1}\delta_{d+1}&y_{2}\delta_{d+1}&\cdots&y_{d+1}\delta_{d+1}\end{array}\right]

where
\delta_{i}=\Delta_{i,j}/y_{j}\in T

(iv)

The ideal

satisfies
\text{ht}(I_{d}(A))\geq 2.

(v)

\text{Ker}(A^{t})

-module generated by the vector
\left[\delta_{1}\ldots,\delta_{d+1}\right]^{t}

Proof.

(i) We first show that
\text{rank}(A)\geq d.
Let
Q
be the quotient field of

Notice that
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}:=\mathcal{L}(R\otimes_{\mathbb{k}}Q)

Thus

Since

which

(ii) By the above paragraph, we have a complex

.
This complex is exact by the Buchsbaum-Eisenbud acyclicity criterion since
\text{ht}(y_{1},\ldots,y_{d+1})\geq 2
and
\text{rank}(A)=d
.
This shows part (ii).

(iii) As

and

.
This establishes part (iii).

(iv) Suppose that
