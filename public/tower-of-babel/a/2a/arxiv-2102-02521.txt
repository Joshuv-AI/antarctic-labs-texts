Optimal control of plasticity with inertia
Stephan Walther

Abstract
The paper is concerned with an optimal control problem governed by the equations of elasto plasticity with linear kinematic hardening and the inertia term at small strain. The objective is to optimize the displacement field and plastic strain by controlling volume forces. The idea given in [ 11 ] is used to transform the state equation into an evolution variational inequality (EVI) involving a certain maximal monotone operator. Results from [ 18 ] are then used to analyze the EVI. A regularization is obtained via the Yosida approximation of the maximal monotone operator, this approximation is smoothed further to derive optimality conditions for the smoothed optimal control problem.

Introduction

We consider the following
optimal control problem governed
by the equations of
elasto plasticity with linear kinematic
hardening and the inertia term
at small strain:

\left\{\quad\begin{aligned} \min\text{ \ \ }&J(u,\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},z,f)=\Psi(u,\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},z)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|f\|_{\mathfrak{X}_{c}}^{2},\\
\text{ s.t. }\text{ \ \ }&\rho\overset{\boldsymbol{..}}{u}-\operatorname{div}\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u-z)=f,\\
&\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}\in A(\mathbb{C}\nabla^{s}u-(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})z),\\
&(u,\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},z)(0)=(u_{0},v_{0},z_{0}),\\
&u\in H^{1}(H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap H^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})),\\
&z\in H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})),\\
&f\in\mathfrak{X}_{c}.\end{aligned}\right.

(1)

Herein,

\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}

is the body under consideration
with density

\rho
,
where

d\in\mathbb{N}

is the dimension.
Its boundary is split into two
disjoints parts

\Gamma_{D}

and

\Gamma_{N}
.
Furthermore,

u:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

is the displacement field
and

z:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}

the plastic strain.
The initial data

(u_{0},v_{0},z_{0})

is given and fixed.
The volume force is given by

f:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
The time derivative of, for instance, the plastic strain
is denoted by

\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}

and the symmetric gradient by

\nabla^{s}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}(\nabla+\nabla^{\top})
.
Moreover,

\mathbb{C}

is the elasticity tensor and

\mathbb{B}

the hardening parameter.
The flow rule is represented by the
maximal monotone operator

A
,
in

Section

5

below we will choose the
von-Mises flow rule.
The control space

\mathfrak{X}_{c}

is a nonempty and closed subspace of

H^{1}([0,T];L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))

and

\alpha>0

a fixed Tikhonov parameter.
Note that higher time regularity
of the control
was also required when analyzing other
problems with a
non-smooth hyperbolic evolution structure,
e.g. in

[
28
]
.
The precise definitions and
assumptions are presented in

Section

2

below.
Note that the problem

Eq.

1

is formulated only with the
displacement and plastic strain as state
variables.
The stress is normally given by

\sigma=\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u-z)

(and can thus be easily integrated in

\Psi
),
however,
we eliminated it in

Eq.

1

for convenience.
Additionally,
the Dirichlet displacement and
Neumann boundary forces
(see the definition of the

\operatorname{div}
-operator
in

Section

2
)
are set to
zero,
cf.

Remark

3.9

below.
Regarding a detailed description
and derivation of the plasticity model,
we refer to

[
22
,
20
,
16
]
.

2
Notation and Standing Assumptions

Notation

When

X

is a normed vector space we
denote its norm by

\|\cdot\|_{X}
.
For normed vector spaces

X

and

Y

we denote the space of
linear and continuous functions on

X

with values in

Y

by

\mathcal{L}(X;Y)
.
We abbreviate

\mathcal{L}(X)\coloneqq\mathcal{L}(X;X)
.
The dual space of

X

is denoted by

X^{*}=\mathcal{L}(X;\mathbb{R})
.
The inner product
of a Hilber space

H

is denoted by

\left(\cdot,\cdot\right)_{H}
.
For the whole paper, we fix the final time
T>0
.
For
t>0
we denote the Bochner space of square-integrable functions on the
time interval
[0,t]
by
L^{2}([0,t];X)
, the Bochner-Sobolev space by
H^{1}([0,t];X)

and the space of continuous functions by
C([0,t];X)
.
We furthermore abbreviate

L^{2}(X):=L^{2}([0,T];X)
,

H^{1}(X):=H^{1}([0,T];X)
and

C(X):=C([0,T];X)
.
When
G\in\mathcal{L}(X;Y)
is a linear and continuous operator, we can define an operator
in
\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(X);L^{2}(Y))
by
G(u)(t):=G(u(t))
for all
u\in L^{2}(X)
and for almost all
t\in[0,T]
,
we denote this operator also by
G
, that is,
G\in\mathcal{L}(L^{2}(X);L^{2}(Y))
, and analog for
Bochner-Sobolev spaces, i.e.,
G\in\mathcal{L}(H^{1}(X);H^{1}(Y))
.
Given a coercive
and symmetric
operator
G\in\mathcal{L}(H)
in a real Hilbert space
H
,
we denote its coercivity constant by
\gamma_{G}
, i.e.,
\left(Gh,h\right)_{H}\geq\gamma_{G}\|h\|_{H}^{2}
for all
h\in H
.
With this operator we can define a new scalar product, which induces an equivalent norm, by

H\times H\ni(h_{1},h_{2})\mapsto\left(Gh_{1},h_{2}\right)_{H}\in\mathbb{R}
.
We denote the Hilbert space equipped with this scalar product by
H_{G}
, that is

\left(h_{1},h_{2}\right)_{H_{G}}=\left(Gh_{1},h_{2}\right)_{H}
for all
h_{1},h_{2}\in H
.
If
p\in[1,\infty]
, then we denote its conjugate exponent by

p^{\prime}
, that is
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{\prime}}=1
.
Throughout the paper, by
L^{p}(\Omega;M)
we denote Lebesgue spaces with values in
M
,
where
p\in[1,\infty]
and
M
is a finite dimensional space.
By

W^{1,p}(\Omega;M)

we denote Sobolev spaces
and

W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega;M)

is the subspace containing functions which traces
are zero on
\Gamma_{D}
.
For the dual spaces of

W^{1,p^{\prime}}(\Omega;M)

and

W^{1,p^{\prime}}_{D}(\Omega;M)

we write

W^{-1,p}(\Omega;M)

and

W^{-1,p}_{D}(\Omega;M)
.
We use the usual abbreviations

H^{1}(\Omega;M)\coloneqq W^{1,2}(\Omega;M)
,

H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;M)\coloneqq W^{1,2}_{D}(\Omega;M)
,

H^{-1}(\Omega;M)\coloneqq W^{-1,2}(\Omega;M)

and

H^{-1}_{D}(\Omega;M)\coloneqq W^{-1,2}_{D}(\Omega;M)
.
Finally, by
\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}
, we denote the space of symmetric matrices and

c,C>0
are generic constants.

Standing Assumptions

The following standing assumptions are tacitly assumed for the rest of the
paper without mentioning them every time.

Domain

The domain
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\in\mathbb{N}
, is bounded
with Lipschitz boundary
\Gamma
. The boundary consists of two disjoint
measurable parts
\Gamma_{N}
and
\Gamma_{D}

such that
\Gamma=\Gamma_{N}\cup\Gamma_{D}
. While

\Gamma_{N}
is a relatively open subset,
\Gamma_{D}
is a relatively closed
subset of
\Gamma
with positive boundary measure.
In addition, the set
\Omega\cup\Gamma_{N}
is regular in the sense of Gröger, cf.
[
12
]
.

Furthermore,
the density of
\Omega
is given by
\rho>0
.

Coefficients

The elasticity tensor and the hardening parameter
satisfy
\mathbb{C},\mathbb{B}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})

and are symmetric and coercive, i.e.,
there exist constants

\underline{c}>0

and

\underline{b}>0
such that

\left(\mathbb{C}\sigma,\sigma\right)_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}}\geq\underline{c}\ \|\sigma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}}^{2}

and
\left(\mathbb{B}\sigma,\sigma\right)_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}}\geq\underline{b}\ \|\sigma\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}}^{2}

for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}
.

We abbreviate further

\displaystyle\mathbb{D}:=\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}\mathbb{C}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\text{ \ \ \ \ }\text{and}\text{ \ \ \ \ }\mathbb{E}:=\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}\in\mathcal{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})

(2)

and note that

\mathbb{D}

is symmetric and coercive,
according to

[
11
, Lemma 4.2]
.
Moreover,
for instance,
we denote the adjoint of

\mathbb{E}

by

\mathbb{E}^{\top}
.

Initial data

We choose

u_{0},v_{0}\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})

and

z_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})

and define

q_{0}\coloneqq\mathbb{C}\nabla^{s}u_{0}-(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})z_{0}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})
.
Moreover,
we assume that

(u_{0},v_{0},q_{0})

is an element of

D(\mathcal{A})
,
where

D(\mathcal{A})

is given in

Definition

3.5
.

Operators

Throughout the paper,
\nabla^{s}:=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla+\nabla^{\top}):W^{1,p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})
denotes the linearized strain.
Its restriction to
W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})
is denoted by the same symbol and, for the adjoint of this restriction, we write

-\operatorname{div}:=(\nabla^{s})^{*}:L^{p^{\prime}}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\to W^{-1,p^{\prime}}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

The operator
A\colon L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\rightarrow 2^{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})}
is
maximal monotone with domain
D(A)
.
Furthermore,
by

A_{\lambda}\colon L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})
,

\lambda>0
,
we denote the Yosida approximation of

A

and by

R_{\lambda}=(I+\lambda A)^{-1}

the resolvent of

A
,
so that

A_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\lambda}(I-R_{\lambda})
.
Moreover,
for every

\lambda>0

the resolvent

R_{\lambda}

can be expressed pointwise,
that is,
there exists

\tilde{R}_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}

such that

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(\tau)(x)=\tilde{R}_{\lambda}(\tau(x))\text{ \ \ \ \ }\text{ f.a.a. }x\in\Omega\text{ and }\forall\tau\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}).

(3)

With a slight abuse of notation we
denote also

\tilde{R}_{\lambda}

by

R_{\lambda}
.
It is to be noted that
this is the case
for the subdifferential
of an indicator function
of a pointwise defined set,
where the resolvent is simply
the projection onto this set,
this example will be considered in

Section

5

below.
For further reference on maximal monotone operators, we refer
to
[
5
]
,
[
29
, Ch. 32]
,
[
4
, Ch. 55]
,
and
[
21
, Ch. 55]
.

Optimization problem

By

J:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\times\mathfrak{X}_{c}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
,

J(u,v,z,f)\coloneqq\Psi(u,v,z)+\frac{\alpha}{2}\|f\|_{\mathfrak{X}_{c}}^{2}

we denote the objective function,
where

\mathcal{H}

is given in

Definition

3.5

and the control space

\mathfrak{X}_{c}

is a Hilbert space and embedded into

H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.
We assume that

\Psi:L^{2}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathbb{R}

is weakly lower semicontinuous,
continuous and
bounded from below and
that the Tikhonov parameter

\alpha
is a positive constant.

3
State Equation

We begin our investigation
with the state equation.
At first we give the definition of a solution
and then transform the
state equation into an EVI
with
a new (maximal monotone) operator

\mathcal{A}
.
In

Section

3.2

we prove the existence
of a solution by showing
that the operator

\mathcal{A}
is maximal monotone,
then we can apply

[
4
, Theorem 55.A]
.
Finally,
in

Section

3.3

we can use some results in

[
18
]

to obtain convergence results.

The formal strong formulation
of the state equation
reads

\displaystyle\rho\overset{\boldsymbol{..}}{u}-\nabla\cdot\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u-z)

\displaystyle=f\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(4a)

\displaystyle\nu\cdot\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u-z)

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{N},

(4b)

\displaystyle u

\displaystyle=0\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }

\displaystyle\text{ on }\Gamma_{D},

(4c)

\displaystyle\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}

\displaystyle\in A(\mathbb{C}\nabla^{s}u-(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})z)\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega,

(4d)

\displaystyle(u,\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},z)(0)

\displaystyle=(u_{0},v_{0},z_{0})\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }

\displaystyle\text{ in }\Omega.

(4e)

Note that we have assumed
in the standing assumptions above
that the density
is constant in
\Omega
.
It is possible to consider a density which
has a spatial dependency
(that is,
a function from

\Omega

to

(0,\infty)
),
one has then in particular to verify that
the operator

Q
,
given in

3.5
,
is well defined,
that is,
the multiplication of

\rho

(and also

\nicefrac{{1}}{{\rho}}
)
with a Sobolev function
is again a Sobolev function.
However,
for simplicity we assume that

\rho

is constant.

We impose the following
assumption for the rest of this
section.

Assumption
\thetheorem

(Standing assumption for
Section

3
)
.

Let

f\in H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}))

be given.

3.1
Definition and Transformation

Let us begin with
the definition of a solution
to the state equation

Eq.

4
.

Definition 3.1

(Solution to plasticity with inertia)
.

We call

u\in H^{1}(H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap H^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))

and

z\in H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}))

solution of

Eq.

4

if

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho\overset{\boldsymbol{..}}{u}-\operatorname{div}\mathbb{C}(\nabla^{s}u-z)&=f,\\
\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{z}&\in A(\mathbb{C}\nabla^{s}u-(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})z),\\
(u,\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},z)(0)&=(u_{0},v_{0},z_{0})\end{split}

(5)

holds.

Before we can transform
the state equation
into an
EVI
we need to reformulate it,
to this end we introduce the following

Definition 3.2

(
z
to
q
mapping)
.

We define

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Q}:H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}),\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }(u,z)\mapsto\mathbb{C}\nabla^{s}u-(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})z

and its inverse (for fixed

u
)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{Z}:H^{1}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\rightarrow L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}),\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }(u,q)\mapsto(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}(\mathbb{C}\nabla^{s}u-q).

These operators will
also be used to transform the optimal
control problem in

Section

4.1

below.

For the following
lemma,
we recall the definition of

\mathbb{D}

and

\mathbb{E}

given in
the standing assumptions above,
that is,

\mathbb{D}=\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}\mathbb{C}

and

\mathbb{E}=\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}
.

Lemma 3.3

(Transformation of
z
to
q
)
.

We consider

\displaystyle\begin{split}\rho\overset{\boldsymbol{..}}{u}-\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}u+\mathbb{E}q)&=f,\\
(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{q}+A(q)-\mathbb{E}^{\top}\nabla^{s}\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u}&\ni 0,\\
(u,\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},q)(0)=(u_{0},v_{0},q_{0})&=(u_{0},v_{0},\mathfrak{Q}(u_{0},z_{0}))\end{split}

(6)

for functions

u\in H^{1}(H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))\cap H^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
,

q\in H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}))
.
Recall that

\mathbb{E}^{\top}

is the adjoint of

\mathbb{E}
.
Then the following holds:

When

(u,z)

is a solution of

Eq.

4
,
then

(u,q)=(u,\mathfrak{Q}(u,z))

solves

Eq.

6
.
Vice versa,
when
(u,q)

solves

Eq.

6
,
then

(u,z)=(u,\mathfrak{Z}(u,q))

is a solution of

Eq.

4
.

Proof 3.4
.

Both implications can be
immediately obtained by using
the definition of

\mathfrak{Q}

and

\mathfrak{Z}

and inserting

z

in

Eq.

5

and

q

in

Eq.

6
,
respectively
(note that

\mathbb{C}-\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}\mathbb{C}=(I-\mathbb{C}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1})\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{B}(\mathbb{C}+\mathbb{B})^{-1}\mathbb{C}=\mathbb{D}
).

We are now in the position to introduce the EVI,
respectively the operator

\mathcal{A}
.

Definition 3.5

(The operator
\mathcal{A}
)
.

For

p\in[1,\infty]

we set

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}_{p}\coloneqq W^{1,p}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times L^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})\text{ \ \ \ \ }\text{and}\text{ \ \ \ \ }\mathcal{H}\coloneqq\mathcal{Y}_{2}.

The scalar product on

\mathcal{H}

is defined by

\displaystyle\left((u_{1},v_{1},q_{1}),(u_{2},v_{2},q_{2})\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\qquad\coloneqq\left(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}u_{1},\nabla^{s}u_{2}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})}+\left(v_{1},v_{2}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}+\left(q_{1},q_{2}\right)_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})}

(recall that

\mathbb{D}

is symmetric and coercive).
We define

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}:D(\mathcal{A})\rightarrow 2^{\mathcal{H}},\text{ \ \ \ \ }(u,v,q)\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}-v\\
-\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}u+\mathbb{E}q)\\
A(q)-\mathbb{E}^{\top}\nabla^{s}v\end{pmatrix}

with the domain

\displaystyle D(\mathcal{A})\coloneqq\{(u,v,q)\in H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times D(A):\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}u+\mathbb{E}q)\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\}.

Moreover,
we set

We note that this transformation

.
We also mention that the fact that

Remark 3.9

(Neumann surface forces and Dirichlet displacement)
.

Let us shortly discuss some issues with
possible surface forces and Dirichlet displacements.
Regarding surface forces,
they are currently equal to zero and
contained in the domain

D(\mathcal{A})

by the requirement

\operatorname{div}(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}u+\mathbb{E}q)\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Allowing now surface forces which are
time dependent,
the domain,
and thus

\mathcal{A}

itself,
would also depend on the time.

An approach for Dirichlet displacements would be
to exchange the displacement with a
“new”
displacement minus the Dirichlet displacement,
then one could still define the domain

D(\mathcal{A})

as a subset of

H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times H^{1}_{D}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})\times D(A)
.
However,
this would again make the domain
and the operator itself time dependent
(the Dirichlet displacement would
occur also in the operator).

In both cases one could still
show that the arising operator
is maximal monotone for a fixed time,
but for different points in time
the monotonicity would be perturbed
by the time dependent functions.
Having now a closer look at
Theorem

3.21

below,
respectively

[
4
, Theorem 55.A]
,
we see that a comparison of two
different points in time is used
to derive a priori estimates.
Following this proof,
the time depend functions would
occur and a straightforward
adaption is not possible.

At this juncture,
let us also elaborate on the
underlying spaces of the operator

\mathcal{A}
.
One might try to exchange

L^{2}(\mathbb{R};\Omega)

with a negative Sobolev space
in the definition of

\mathcal{H}

to allow surface forces.
However,
with this definition of

\mathcal{H}
,
for instance,
the proof of

Lemma

3.16

(which is used to show the
monotonicity of

\mathcal{A}
)
would not be valid anymore.
Thus, our choice of

\mathcal{H}

seems reasonable.

\mathcal{F}:L^{2}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))\rightarrow H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))
.

Theorem 3.21

(Existence of a solution to the state equation)
.

There exists a unique solution

(u,v,q)\in H^{1}(\mathcal{H})

of

Eq.

7
.
Moreover,
the inequality

\displaystyle\|(\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{u},\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{v},\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{q})\|_{L^{2}(\mathcal{H})}\leq C(1+\|f\|_{H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))})

holds, where the constant
C
does not depend on
f
.

Proof 3.22
.

The tuple

(u,v,q)

is a solution of

Eq.

7

if and only if

w:=(u,v,q)

solves

\displaystyle\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{w}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}(w)\ni\frac{1}{\rho}Rf,\text{ \ \ \ \ }w(0)=(u_{0},v_{0},q_{0}),

with

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}:=Q\mathcal{A}
.
One easily verifies that

\tilde{\mathcal{A}}

is a maximal monotone operator with respect to

\mathcal{H}_{Q^{-1}}

(that is,
the space

\mathcal{H}

equipped with the scalar product

\left(Q^{-1}\cdot,\cdot\right)_{\mathcal{H}_{Q^{-1}}}
),
thus we can apply

[
4
, Theorem 55.A]

to obtain a solution

w\in H^{1}(\mathcal{H})
.
Moreover,
as can be seen in the proof of

[
4
, Theorem 55.A]

we get

\displaystyle\sqrt{\gamma_{Q^{-1}}}\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{w}_{\lambda}\|_{C(\mathcal{H})}\leq\|\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{w}_{\lambda}\|_{C(\mathcal{H}_{Q^{-1}})}\leq C(1+\frac{1}{\rho}\|Rf\|_{H^{1}(\mathcal{H})}))=C(1+\frac{1}{\rho}\|f\|_{H^{1}(L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d}))})

for all

\lambda>0
,
where

w_{\lambda}

is the solution of

\displaystyle\overset{\boldsymbol{.}}{w}_{\lambda}+\tilde{\mathcal{A}}_{\lambda}(w_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\rho}Rf,\text{ \ \ \ \ }w_{\lambda}(0)=(u_{0},v_{0},q_{0}).

Since

w_{\lambda}\rightharpoonup w

in

H^{1}(\mathcal{H})
,
we obtain the desired inequality.

Remark 3.23

(
\mathcal{A}
is not a subdifferential)
.

Let us show that
the maximal monotone operator

\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow\mathcal{H}

is not a subdifferential,
that is,
there exists no proper,
convex and lower semicontinuous function

\Phi:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]

such that

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(u,v,q)

\displaystyle=\partial\Phi(u,v,q)=\{(c,d,e)\in\mathcal{H}:\Phi(\hat{u},\hat{v},\hat{q})\geq\Phi(u,v,q)

\displaystyle\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }+\left((c,d,e),(\hat{u}-u,\hat{v}-v,\hat{q}-q)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}\ \forall(\hat{u},\hat{v},\hat{q})\in\mathcal{H}\}

holds for all

(u,v,q)\in\mathcal{H}
.
In fact,
there exists even not any function

\Phi:\mathcal{H}\rightarrow(-\infty,\infty]

such that the equation above holds,
which can be seen as follows:

Let us assume that such a

\Phi

exists and recall that

(u_{0},v_{0},q_{0})\in D(\mathcal{A})
.
Then,
using

Lemma

3.16

with

(u_{1},v_{1},q_{1})=(u+u_{0},v,q_{0})

and

(u_{2},v_{2},q_{2})=(u_{0},0,q_{0})
,

\displaystyle\Phi(u_{0},0,q_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(u+u_{0},v,q_{0})-\left(\mathcal{A}(u+u_{0},v,q_{0}),(u,v,0)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\Phi(u+u_{0},v,q_{0})-\left(\mathcal{A}(u_{0},0,q_{0}),(u,v,0)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle\geq\Phi(u_{0},0,q_{0})+\left(\mathcal{A}(u_{0},0,q_{0}),(u,v,0)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}-\left(\mathcal{A}(u_{0},0,q_{0}),(u,v,0)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\Phi(u_{0},0,q_{0})

holds for all

(u,v)

such that

(u+u_{0},v,q_{0})\in D(\mathcal{A})
,
hence,

\displaystyle\left(\mathcal{A}(u_{0},0,q_{0}),(\hat{u}-u,\hat{v}-v,0)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle=\Phi(\hat{u}+u_{0},\hat{v},q_{0})-\Phi(u+u_{0},v,q_{0})

\displaystyle\geq\left(\mathcal{A}(u+u_{0},v,q_{0}),(\hat{u}-u,\hat{v}-v,0)\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

which gives

\displaystyle 0\geq\left(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}u,\nabla^{s}\hat{v}\right)_{\mathcal{H}}-\left(\mathbb{D}\nabla^{s}\hat{u},\nabla^{s}v\right)_{\mathcal{H}}

for all

(\hat{u},\hat{v}),(u,v)

such that

(u+u_{0},v,q_{0}),(\hat{u}+u_{0},\hat{v},q_{0})\in D(\mathcal{A})
.
Choosing now an arbitrary

u\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})
,

u\neq 0
,

\hat{v}=u

and

\hat{u}=v=0
,
we obtain the desired contradiction.

In light of

Remark

3.23
,
the case of plasticity with inertia essentially
differs from the EVI analyzed in

[
18
]

in two aspects.
First,
we have more regularity in time
as explained after

Second,
we lose a certain boundedness of the

which was assumed in

[

and it is not a subdifferential.

It is also to be noted that

is independent of the operator

Maximal monotone operator
A

We consider the case of linear kinematic hardening with the von Mises yield condition,
cf.

[
13
]
for a detailed description of this model.
In this case,

is the convex subdifferential of the indicator functional

of the following set of admissible stresses

where
\tau^{D}:=\tau-\frac{1}{d}\operatorname{tr}(\tau)I

is the deviator of

\tau\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}
and
\gamma

denotes the initial uni-axial yield stress, a given material parameter.
The domain of
A=\partial I_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
is trivially
{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
,
which is nonempty, closed and convex.
For the Yosida approximation of
\partial I_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
,
one obtains by a straightforward calculation

A_{\lambda}(\tau)=\frac{1}{\lambda}(\tau-\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\tau))=\frac{1}{\lambda}\operatorname{max}\Big\{0,1-\frac{\gamma}{|\tau^{D}|}\Big\}\tau^{D},

(28)

with the resolvent
R_{\lambda}=\pi_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
,
where
\pi_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
denotes the projection on
{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}
in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})
, i.e.,

\pi_{\mathcal{K}}(\sigma):=\argmin_{\tau\in\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}\|\tau-\sigma\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})}^{2}
.
We get in particular

\displaystyle R_{\lambda}(\tau)=\pi_{\mathcal{K}(\Omega)}(\tau)=\tau-\operatorname{max}\Big\{0,1-\frac{\gamma}{|\tau^{D}|}\Big\}\tau^{D},

so that

Eq.

3

is fulfilled.

To smoothen this function let
s>0

and

\operatorname{max}_{s}:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }r\mapsto\begin{cases}\operatorname{max}\{0,r\},&|r|\geq s,\\
\tfrac{1}{4s}(r+s)^{2},&|r|<s,\end{cases}

we then set

\displaystyle R_{s}:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s},\text{ \ \ \ \ \ \ }\tau\mapsto\tau-\textnormal{max}_{s}\Big(1-\frac{\gamma}{|\tau^{D}|}\Big)\tau^{D}.

One easily checks that

and we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\|\partial I_{\lambda,s}(\tau)-\partial I_{\lambda}(\tau)\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}_{s})}\\
&\text{ \ \ \ \ }\leq\frac{1}{\lambda}\Big(\int_{\Omega}\Big|\operatorname{max}_{s}\Big(1-\frac{\gamma}{|\tau(x)^{D}|}\Big)-\operatorname{max}\Big(0,1-\frac{\gamma}{|\tau(x)^{D}|}\Big)\Big|^{2}\ |\tau(x)^{D}|^{2}\Big)^{\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}}\\

(29)

for all

. Moreover, it is also easy to verify

is monotone
and Lipschitz continuous,
