Protocol for generating an arbitrary quantum state of the magnetization in cavity magnonics
Silvia Viola Kusminskiy

Abstract
We propose and numerically evaluate a protocol to generate an arbitrary quantum state of the magnetization in a magnet. The protocol involves repeatedly exciting a frequency-tunable superconducting transmon and transferring the excitations to the magnet via a microwave cavity. To avoid decay, the protocol must be much shorter than magnon lifetime. Speeding up the protocol by simply shortening the pulses leads to non-resonant leakage of excitations to higher levels of the transmon accompanied by higher decoherence. We discuss how to correct for such leakages by applying counter pulses to de-excite these higher levels. In our protocol, states with a maximum magnon occupation of up to ∼ 9 \sim 9 and average magnon number up to ∼ 4 \sim 4 can be generated with fidelity > 0.75 >0.75 .

The hybrid system we considered is depicted in Fig.

When the magnet is much smaller than the wavelength of the MWs (
\sim$\mathrm{cm}$
), only the total magnetization
\bi{M}
couples to the MWs, so we need to consider only the uniform magnon mode known as the Kittel mode. It can be quantized via a Holstein-Primakoff transformation
[

M_{x}-iM_{y}\rightarrow 2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}\hat{M},\ M_{z}\rightarrow M_{s}-\frac{2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}^{2}}{M_{s}}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}.

(1)

where
\hat{M}
is the magnon annihilation operator satisfying the bosonic commutation relations
[\hat{M},\hat{M}^{\dagger}]=1
,
M_{s}
is the saturation magnetization, and the zero-point fluctuations of the magnetization are given by

\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}=\sqrt{\frac{\gamma\hbar M_{s}}{2V_{m}}},

(2)

\gamma>0

V_{m}
being the volume of the magnet. Assuming a spherical magnet and an external magnetic field
H_{{\rm app}}

\hat{H}_{\rm m}=\hbar\Omega_{m}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}
with
\Omega_{m}=\gamma\mu_{0}H_{{\rm app}}
(see
A
) which can be tuned to be in the range of
\sim 2\pi\times 5\text{-}$10\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
. For a YIG sample with volume
V_{m}>$1\text{\,}{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{3}$

M_{s}=$140\text{\,}\mathrm{kA}\text{\,}{\mathrm{m}}^{-1}$
, and
\gamma=2\pi\times$28\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}\text{\,}{\mathrm{T}}^{-1}$

\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}/M_{s}<8\times 10^{-6}

A flux-tunable transmon consists of two Josephson junctions in parallel with Josephson energy
E_{J1,J2}
respectively, forming a SQUID loop along with a capacitor with capacitance
C

44

45

46

E_{n}\approx\hbar\left(\Omega_{q}-\frac{A}{2}(n-1)\right)n,

(3)

where

\Omega_{q}=\sqrt{E_{C}E_{J,{\rm eff}}}

E_{J,{\rm eff}}(\Phi)=\sqrt{\left(E_{J1}+E_{J2}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}+\left(E_{J1}-E_{J2}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}}.

(4)

Here
\Phi

\Phi_{0}=h/2e
is the superconducting flux quantum. Such a set of energy levels can be labelled using an annihilation operator
\hat{Q}
and its corresponding number operator
\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}
. The number of quanta exchanged between the two Josephson junctions is given by

\hat{N}_{{\rm sc}}=\left(\frac{E_{J,{\rm eff}}}{2E_{C}}\right)^{1/4}\frac{\hat{Q}-\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}i}.

(5)

The Hamiltonian becomes

\hat{H}_{\rm tr}=\hbar\left[\Omega_{q}-\frac{\hbar A}{2}\left(\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}-1\right)\right]\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}.

(6)

The frequency
\Omega_{q}
can be tuned dynamically via the external flux in SQUID
\Phi
, to be in a typical range of
2\pi\times 5\text{-}$20\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
. Tuning via

can be achieved at a fast timescale
\ll$10\text{\,}\mathrm{ns}$

[

48

. For our case, we assume that
\Omega_{q}

\sim 2\pi\times$1\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$

We assume that the magnet and the transmon are both placed inside a MW cavity which can be modelled as a set of harmonic modes with frequencies
\Omega_{a,r}

\hat{A}_{r}

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm int}}{\hbar}=\sum_{r}\left(G_{m,r}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+G_{m,r}^{*}\hat{M}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}+G_{q,r}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+G_{q,r}^{*}\hat{Q}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\right),

(7)

where the coupling
G_{m,r}\propto\left|\bi{B}(\bi{r}_{{\rm magnet}})\right|

\bi{B}(\bi{r}_{{\rm magnet}})
the magnetic field at the location of the magnet, and
G_{q,r}\propto\left|\bi{E}(\bi{r}_{{\rm transmon}})\right|
where
\bi{E}(\bi{r}_{{\rm transmon}})
is the electric field at the location of the transmon. If for all

\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}>G_{m,r}

\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}>G_{q,r}

\hat{m}
and
\hat{q}
respectively. Up to quadratic order in the couplings
[
4
]

\frac{\hat{H}}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\left(\omega_{q}-\hat{\alpha}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]\right)\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}+\hat{m}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{g}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]+\hat{g}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{q}.

(8)

Details about this Hamiltonian are provided in
A

and transmon’s frequency

\Omega_{m}

The function
\hat{\alpha}
gives the anharmonicity of the transmon and
\hat{g}
gives the magnon-transmon coupling. Below, we use the same characters

Restricting the analysis to only the first two levels of the transmon, we get the Jaynes-Cummings Hamiltonian

\frac{\hat{H}^{(2)}}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\omega_{q}\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|+g\left(\hat{m}\hat{\sigma}_{+}+\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\right),

(9)

where
g\equiv g(0)

\hat{\sigma}_{+}=\left|g\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|

In this section we review the protocol to generate an arbitrary state of the magnetization via its coupling to a two-level system. This is discussed in
[
40
]
theoretically for a general harmonic oscillator. The protocol consists of: (1) exciting the qubit; (2) partially transfering the excitation to the oscillator and (3) repeating the process multiple times to construct the Fock-state superposition that describes the target state. In this setup, the transmon can be excited via a time-dependent drive, while the transmon and the magnons can be put in and out of resonance by tuning the magnon frequency (via an external magnetic field) or the transmon frequency (via an applied flux), effectively changing the coupling rate (see Fig.
2
). The procedure was experimentally implemented to generate quantum states of MW photons coupled to a superconducting transmon
[
48
]
.

If we start from the qubit’s state
c_{0}\left|e\right\rangle+c_{1}\left|g\right\rangle
while the magnon is in the ground state
\left|0\right\rangle
, switching the coupling on for a period
t=\pi/(2g)
in the absence of dissipation evolves the state as

\left(c_{0}\left|e\right\rangle+c_{1}\left|g\right\rangle\right)\left|0\right\rangle\rightarrow\left|g\right\rangle\left(c_{0}\left|0\right\rangle+c_{1}\left|1\right\rangle\right),

(10)

where
\left|n\right\rangle
is
n
-magnon state. Thus, we can create an arbitrary superposition of
\left|0\right\rangle
and
\left|1\right\rangle
. If we repeat this process
N
times, we can, in principle, generate an arbitrary magnon state containing up to a maximum of
N
magnons. The protocol then consists of a series of operations in which first the transmon is excited, and then the qubit’s frequency is changed to bring it in resonance with the magnons for a time of the order
\pi/g
.

To make the process concrete, consider the Hamiltonian restricted to the first two levels of the transmon
\hat{H}^{(2)}(t)=\hat{H}_{\rm s}^{(2)}+\hat{H}_{\rm d}^{(2)}(t)
, where the static part is

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm s}^{(2)}}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\omega_{q}\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|+g\left(\hat{m}\hat{\sigma}_{+}+\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\right),

(11)

and the dynamic part is

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm d}^{(2)}}{\hbar}=\Delta(t)\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|+\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{+}+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{*}(t)\hat{\sigma}_{-},

(12)

Here and in what follows, the superscript (2) indicates the approximation of the transmon as a two-level system. In this expression,
\Delta(t)
is the externally induced change in the transmon’s frequency, and
\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)
is the transmon drive amplitude. We assume that the qubit and the magnons are far detuned,
\left|\omega_{m}-\omega_{q}\right|\gg g
, implying that magnons and qubit are decoupled at
\Delta=0
.

A non-zero
\tilde{\varepsilon}
can excite a qubit. We define the time evolution operator
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{(2)}
for
\Delta=0
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)=\varepsilon(t)e^{-i\omega_{q}t+i\phi}
,

\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{(2)}\left(\theta,\phi\right)=\mathrm{Texp}\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t_{g}}dt\left(\hat{H}_{\rm s}^{(2)}-\hbar\varepsilon(t)e^{-i\omega_{q}t+i\phi}\hat{\sigma}_{+}+{\rm h.c.}\right)\right],

(13)

where
t_{g}
is a pre-determined gate time,
\varepsilon(t)
is chosen satisfying
\int_{0}^{t_{g}}d\tau\varepsilon(\tau)=\theta
and
{\rm Texp}
is the time-ordered exponential. As derived in
C
,

\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{(2)}\left(\theta,\phi\right)=e^{-i\omega_{m}t_{g}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}-i\omega_{q}t_{g}\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|}\left(\hat{I}\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\left(e^{i\phi}\hat{\sigma}_{+}+e^{-i\phi}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\right)\right),

(14)

which applies a Bloch rotation of magnitude
2\theta
around the axis
(\cos\phi,-\sin\phi,0)
. After the qubit is excited, we want to (partially) transfer the quanta to magnons. We then define the time evolution operator for a detuning
\Delta_{0}=\omega_{m}-\omega_{q}
and no qubit driving

\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(2)}\left(t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\right)=\exp\left[\frac{-i\hat{H}_{\rm s}^{(2)}t_{{\rm off}}}{\hbar}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\hat{H}_{\rm s}^{(2)}+\Delta_{0}\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|\right)t_{{\rm on}}\right].

(15)

An additional ‘off’ time,
t_{{\rm off}}
, adjusts the relative phases in the wave-function
1
1

1

In
[
40
]
, the coupling was assumed to be a freely varying complex parameter, so the phases could be adjusted via the phase of
g
.
. As derived in
B
,
\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(2)}=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\rm ph}}^{(2)}\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\rm ex}}^{(2)}
where the phases evolve as

\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\rm ph}}^{(2)}=\exp\left(-i\omega_{m}\left(t_{{\rm off}}+t_{{\rm on}}\right)\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}-i\left(\omega_{m}t_{{\rm on}}+\omega_{q}t_{{\rm off}}\right)\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|\right),

(16)

and the quanta are exchanged via

\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{{\rm ex}}^{(2)}=\cos\left(gt_{{\rm on}}\sqrt{\hat{N}}\right)-\frac{i}{\sqrt{\hat{N}}}\sin\left(gt_{{\rm on}}\sqrt{\hat{N}}\right)\left(\hat{m}\hat{\sigma}_{+}+\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{\sigma}_{-}\right),

(17)

with the total number operator
\hat{N}=\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\left|e\right\rangle\left\langle e\right|
.

A given target state
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
containing a maximum of
N_{m}
magnons, can be systematically achieved by adding magnons one by one as described above. For that, we need a set of time evolution operators
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{(2)}\equiv\hat{\mathcal{Q}}^{(2)}(\theta_{j},\phi_{j})

\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|0,g\right\rangle,\ \left|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{j}^{(2)}\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{(2)}\left|\Psi_{j}\right\rangle,\ \left|\Psi_{N_{m}}\right\rangle=\left|\psi_{T},g\right\rangle,

(18)

\left|\Psi_{j}\right\rangle
has a maximum of
j

(j-1)

\left|\psi_{j}^{e}\right\rangle
and
\left|\psi_{j}^{g}\right\rangle

The coefficients

are found by reversing the problem in order to systematically remove magnons one by one from
\left|\Psi_{N_{m}}\right\rangle
. Inductively, we assume that
\left|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle
is found. Explicitly, we find
\{t_{{\rm on},j},t_{{\rm off},j}\}
to ensure
\left\langle j+1,g\middle|\hat{{\cal T}}_{j}^{(2),\dagger}\middle|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=0
achieved via [see

Next, we find

to ensure
\left\langle j,e\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{j}^{(2),\dagger}\hat{{\cal T}}_{j}^{(2),\dagger}\middle|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=0

3
Realistic State Generation

The protocol discussed in the previous section is suitable to generate states with only a small number of excitations. States with higher number occupation require longer protocols and hence are spoiled by dissipation. The protocol duration can be decreased via two ways. Firstly, the gate time to excite the qubit can be decreased. Nevertheless, for a transmon qubit, a shorter gate time,
t_{g}\sim 1/\alpha(n)
where
\alpha
is the level-dependent anharmonicity [see below Eq. (
8
)], implies a broader frequency spectrum allowing for significant non-resonant transitions to higher transmon levels. Secondly, a stronger coupling between magnons and the transmon speeds up the excitation transfer step. However, a very strong coupling,
g(n)\sqrt{n}\sim\alpha(n)
where
g
is the level-dependent magnon-transmon coupling [see below Eq. (
8
)], implies a leakage of magnon excitations into higher transmon states. Such interferences limit the protocol time and eventually the largest size of the harmonic oscillator state that can be created. In this section, we show that transitions to higher levels can be incorporated by adding extra pulses for canceling out such transition amplitudes. The extra pulses are analogous to the ones used for correcting qubit gates
[
50
,
51
]
and can significantly decrease the protocol time allowing for states containing a large magnon number.

For clarity in presentation, we consider the case of
g(n)\equiv g
and

\alpha(n)\equiv\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(n-1\right),

(21)

while the calculations for the general case are presented in
B
and
C
. The Hamiltonian, Eq. (
8
), becomes
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm s}+\hat{H}_{\rm d}(t)
, where the static part is

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm s}}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\left(\omega_{q}-\frac{\alpha}{2}\left(\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-1\right)\right)\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}+g\left(\hat{m}\hat{q}^{\dagger}+\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right),

(22)

and the dynamic part is

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm d}(t)}{\hbar}=\Delta(t)\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}+\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)\hat{q}^{\dagger}+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{*}(t)\hat{q}.

(23)

For a target magnon state
\left|\psi\right\rangle
, we want to find a set of operations
\hat{{\cal Q}}_{j}
and
\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{j}
satisfying (see Fig.
2
)

\left|\Psi_{0}\right\rangle=\left|0,0\right\rangle,\ \left|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}\left|\Psi_{j}\right\rangle,\ \left|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{j}\left|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle,\ \left|\Psi_{N_{m}}\right\rangle=\left|\psi,0\right\rangle,

(24)

where
\left|\psi_{1},\psi_{2}\right\rangle
refers to the magnon state
\left|\psi_{1}\right\rangle
and transmon state
\left|\psi_{2}\right\rangle
. As before, starting from
\left|\Psi_{N_{m}}\right\rangle
, we inductively find
\{\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{j},\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j},\left|\Psi_{j}\right\rangle\}
where the transfer operator ensures
\left\langle j+1,0\middle|\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{j}^{\dagger}\middle|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=0
(removing a magnon) and the gate operation ensures

\left\langle j+1-s,s\middle|\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle=0,

(25)

for
s\geq 1
(removing transmon excitations).

3.1
Transfer of excitation

In
B
, we derive
\hat{{\cal T}}_{j}
satisfying the condition
\left\langle j+1,0\middle|\hat{\mathcal{T}}_{j}^{\dagger}\middle|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=0
, while we discuss the salient features of the derivation here. We start with the same ansatz as before [see Eq. (
15
)]

\hat{{\cal T}}^{{\rm ans}}\left(t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\right)=\exp\left[\frac{-i\hat{H}_{\rm s}t_{{\rm off}}}{\hbar}\right]\exp\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left(\hat{H}_{\rm s}+\Delta_{0}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right)t_{{\rm on}}\right],

(26)

except for the fact that the Hamiltonian includes all higher transmon levels. This ansatz can be simplified by noticing that the number of excitations
\hat{N}=\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}
are conserved during the evolution, i.e.
\left[\hat{N},\hat{\cal T}^{{\rm ans}}\right]=\hat{0}
. An expression for
\hat{{\cal T}}^{{\rm ans}}
restricted to the space of
\{\left|j+1-s,s\right\rangle\}_{s\geq 0}
can be found by numerically diagonalizing the two Hamiltonians,
\hat{H}_{\rm s}
and
\hat{H}_{\rm s}+\Delta_{0}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}
. Finally, we get a condition on
\{t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\}
of the form

\sum_{pqr}e^{i\lambda_{{\rm on}}^{(p)}t_{{\rm on}}}e^{i\lambda_{{\rm off}}^{(q)}t_{{\rm off}}}a_{pq}^{(r)}\left\langle j+1-r,r\middle|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=0,

(27)

where
\{\lambda_{{\rm on}}^{(p)},\lambda_{{\rm off}}^{(q)},a_{pq}^{(r)}\}
can be found in terms of the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the Hamiltonians [see
B
]. This equation can be numerically solved for
\{t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\}
.

The above ansatz yields a solution in most cases, nevertheless, it can fail for some states, for example, when
\left|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle=\left|j+1,0\right\rangle
. Intuitively, when the coupling is on, the excitation from
\left|j+1,0\right\rangle
transfers to all
\left|j+1-s,s\right\rangle
but with different oscillation frequencies
\sim\sqrt{g^{2}+\frac{\alpha^{2}s^{2}(s-1)^{2}}{4}}
. Unless these oscillation frequencies are commensurate, the amplitude of the wave-function in
\left|j+1,0\right\rangle
will stay non-zero.

In cases where this ansatz fails, we can apply a second operation,

\hat{{\cal T}}_{j}=\hat{{\cal T}}^{{\rm ans}}\left(t_{{\rm on},j}^{(2)},t_{{\rm off},j}^{(2)}\right)\hat{{\cal T}}^{{\rm ans}}\left(t_{{\rm on},j}^{(1)},t_{{\rm off},j}^{(1)}\right),

(28)

where we choose
\{t_{{\rm on},j}^{(1)},t_{{\rm off},j}^{(1)}\}
as the values found from a two-level approximation [see Eq. (
20
)], and
\{t_{{\rm on},j}^{(2)},t_{{\rm off},j}^{(2)}\}
are found by applying the above optimization to
\hat{{\cal T}}^{{\rm ans},\dagger}\left(t_{{\rm on},j}^{(1)},t_{{\rm off},j}^{(1)}\right)\left|\Psi_{j+1}\right\rangle
. In principle, this process can be continued until a solution is found. However, in our simulations, we never had to go beyond a second step.

3.2
Transmon gates

As noted before, the application of
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}
ensures that
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{\dagger}\left|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle
has no excitations above the level
j
. The results are derived in
C
and here, we discuss the salient features of the derivation. First, we want to apply a gate between transmon states
\left|0\right\rangle
and
\left|1\right\rangle
,
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{01}^{{\rm ideal}}(\theta_{j}^{01},\phi_{j}^{01})
, to “transfer” the amplitude of
\left|j,1\right\rangle
to
\left|j,0\right\rangle
, i.e. ensure
\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}^{{\rm ideal},\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle=0
, where
\{\theta_{j}^{01},\phi_{j}^{01}\}
are given by

\left\langle j,1\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\cos\theta_{j}^{01}+i\left\langle j,0\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\sin\theta_{j}^{01}e^{i\phi_{j}^{01}}=0,

(29)

which can be obtained using the same arguments as in the two-level approximation, see Eq. (
20
). To implement this with realistic signal, we use the ansatz

\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{01}(t)=\mathrm{Texp}\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t}dt\left(\hat{H}_{\rm s}-\hbar\varepsilon(\tau)e^{-i\omega_{q}\tau+i\phi_{{\rm app}}}\hat{q}^{\dagger}+{\rm h.c.}\right)\right],

(30)

where
\{\varepsilon(t),\phi_{{\rm app}}\}
are to be determined. We can separate the phases by

\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}(t)\approx\exp\left[-i\left(\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\omega_{q}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right)t\right]e^{i\phi_{{\rm app}}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}}\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}(t)e^{-i\phi_{{\rm app}}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}},

(31)

where we ignore the interaction between magnons and photons (they are far off-resonant) and

\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}(t)=\mathrm{Texp}\left[i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ \varepsilon(\tau)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ e^{-inA\tau}\left|n+1\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|+h.c.\right)\right].

(32)

In the two-level approximation, we can choose any
\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)
satisfying
\int_{0}^{t_{g}}d\tau\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)=\theta_{j}^{01}
and
\phi_{{\rm app}}^{(0)}=\phi_{j}^{01}-\omega_{q}t_{g}
. For concreteness, we choose

\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)=\frac{\pi\theta_{j}^{01}}{2t_{g}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{t_{g}}\right),

(33)

where
t_{g}
is a pre-determined gate time. In the two-level approximation, the time-dependence is given by

\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0)}(t)=\mathrm{Texp}\left[i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ \varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)\left(\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+h.c.\right)\right].

(34)

As the term in the exponential commutes with itself at all times, we can remove the time-ordering operator and simplify
\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0)}
to,

\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0)}(t)=\cos\Theta(t)\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+i\sin\Theta(t)\left(\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|,

(35)

where
\Theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)d\tau
.

However, a drive amplitude with a general form
\varepsilon(t)
can induce transitions to higher transmon levels, e.g. transitions between
\left|j,1\right\rangle
and
\left|j,2\right\rangle
. This is problematic as a significant transition amplitude
\left\langle j,2\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{j}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle
implies excitation number
j+2
, while we want
\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{j}^{\dagger}\left|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle
to have no excitations above
j
. Thus, we consider
\varepsilon(t)=\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)+\varepsilon^{(1)}(t)
, where
\varepsilon^{(1)}(t)
are higher frequency components that cancel out higher order transitions, making
\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}\approx\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0)}
.

The error in the transmon gate is given by

\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm err}}(t)=\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0),\dagger}(t)\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}(t)=\mathrm{Texp}\left[i\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\ \delta\hat{h}(\tau)-h.c.\right],

(36)

where

\delta\hat{h}(\tau)=\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0),\dagger}(\tau)\left(\varepsilon(\tau)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ e^{-inA\tau}\left|n+1\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|-\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|\right)\hat{{\cal U}}_{01}^{(0)}(\tau).

(37)

\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm err}}
cannot be found analytically. To linear order in the perturbation, we ignore the corrections due to non-commutativity, and impose

\int_{0}^{t_{g}}d\tau\ \delta\hat{h}(\tau)=\hat{0}.

(38)

This in turn implies the following set of equations that the drive amplitude
\varepsilon(t)
must satisfy

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{g}}\left(\varepsilon(\tau)-\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)\right)d\tau

\displaystyle=0,

(39)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{t_{g}}\varepsilon(\tau)e^{iA\tau\pm i\Theta(t)}d\tau

\displaystyle=0,

(40)

\displaystyle n\geq 2:\ \int_{0}^{t_{g}}\varepsilon(\tau)e^{in\tau}d\tau

\displaystyle=0.

(41)

The first condition is a no-bias condition ensuring that
\int_{0}^{t_{g}}\varepsilon(\tau)d\tau=\theta_{j}^{01}
. The second condition, with
\Theta(t)=\int_{0}^{t}d\tau\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)
ensures that the transitions
\left|1\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|2\right\rangle
cancel out at the end of the gate. Similarly, the transitions
\left|n\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|n+1\right\rangle
are cancelled by the third condition. In our simulations, we find that for typical parameters, the population beyond level
2
is negligible, so we can ignore the conditions for
n\geq 3
.

The above conditions leave sufficient freedom in choosing
\varepsilon(t)
. In what follows, we choose
t_{g}=3\pi/\alpha
and

\varepsilon(t)=\sum_{\mu=0}^{4}\varepsilon_{\mu}\sin\left(\frac{\left(2\mu+1\right)\pi t}{t_{g}}\right),

(42)

where the parameters
\varepsilon_{\mu}
are governed by an underdetermined set of linear equations that can be found from the above conditions [shown explicitly in
C
]. Given
\varepsilon(t)
, we can find
\phi_{{\rm app}}
by minimizing
\left|\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\right|
as discussed in
C.3
.

We point out that although we choose a linear approximation, the corrections are not always small. However, in our simulations we find that the error matrix
\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm err}}
is dominated by extraneous phases for all transmon levels. One might think that these can be removed by adding higher order corrections, however, we observed that such higher order corrections typically shifts the errors from
\left|2\right\rangle
to
\left|n\geq 3\right\rangle
instead of removing them
2
2

2

A similar correction scheme was followed in
[
50
,
51
]
by ignoring levels higher than
\left|2\right\rangle
where this effect was not observed.
. Thus, we account for these extra phases by modifying the next steps of the induction.

If
\left|\left\langle j-1,2\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\right|
is significant (we take the threshold as
0.1
in our simulations), a second gate between
\left|1\right\rangle
and
\left|2\right\rangle
, say
\hat{{\cal Q}}_{12}^{{\rm ideal}}(\theta_{j}^{12},\phi_{j}^{12})
, can be applied to ensure that
\left\langle j-1,2\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{12}^{{\rm ideal},\dagger}\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle=0
, with

\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\cos\theta_{j}^{12}+i\left\langle j,0\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{01}^{\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j}\right\rangle\sin\theta_{j}^{12}e^{i\phi_{j}^{12}}=0.

(43)

Essentially, the same arguments presented above hold for the gate
\hat{{\cal Q}}_{12}
defined by

\hat{\mathcal{Q}}_{12}\left[\varepsilon(t),\phi_{{\rm app}}\right]=\mathrm{Texp}\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t_{g}}dt\left(\hat{H}_{\rm s}-\frac{\hbar\varepsilon(t)}{\sqrt{2}}e^{-i\left(\omega_{q}-A\right)t+i\phi_{{\rm app}}}\hat{q}^{\dagger}+{\rm h.c.}\right)\right],

(44)

where we can choose

\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)=\frac{\pi\theta_{j}^{12}}{2t_{g}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{t_{g}}\right)

(45)

and
\varepsilon(t)
satisfying the same conditions as before.

The levels
\left|n\geq 3\right\rangle
are found to be approximately unoccupied, so we do not need to apply more gates.

4
Results

To evaluate the above algorithm, we numerically solve the time evolution that implements the protocol for the density matrix, using QuTiP
[
52
,
53
]
, satisfying

\frac{d\hat{\rho}}{dt}=-\frac{i}{\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{\rm s}+\hat{H}_{\rm d}(t),\hat{\rho}(t)]\right]+\sum_{n}\frac{1}{2}\left[2\hat{C}_{n}\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{C}_{n}^{\dagger}-\hat{\rho}(t)\hat{C}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{C}_{n}-\hat{C}_{n}^{\dagger}\hat{C}_{n}\hat{\rho}(t)\right].

(46)

For dissipation, we take the Lindblad operators pertaining to magnon decay
\hat{C}_{1}=\sqrt{\kappa_{m}}\hat{m}
, transmon decay
\hat{C}_{2}=\sqrt{\kappa_{q}}\hat{q}
and transmon dephasing
\hat{C}_{3}=\sqrt{\gamma_{q}}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}
. We did not include magnon dephasing as it has not yet been experimentally observed, although there are theoretical reasons for pure dephasing of magnons
[
54
]
. We choose typical parameters
\omega_{m}=2\pi\times$6\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
,
\omega_{q}=2\pi\times$5\text{\,}\mathrm{GHz}$
,
\kappa_{m}=10^{-4}\omega_{m}
,
\kappa_{q}=\gamma_{q}=10^{-4}\omega_{q}
,
A=2\pi\times$300\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
, and coupling
g=2\pi\times$25\text{\,}\mathrm{MHz}$
. For a given target state
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
, the algorithm described in the previous sections gives the signals
\Delta[\psi_{T}](t)
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}[\psi_{T}](t)
, along with the protocol time
T_{{\rm tot}}[\psi_{T}]
. Starting from the ground state,
\left|0,0\right\rangle
, we numerically solve the above Lindblad equation with the given signals,
\Delta
and
\tilde{\varepsilon}
, and find the reduced density matrix for the magnon,
\hat{\rho}_{{\rm mag}}[\psi_{T}]={\rm Tr}_{\hat{q}}\left[\hat{\rho}(t[\psi_{T}])\right]
:

\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\vbox to3.1pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\rm protocol}\vss}}}\{\Delta(t),\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)\}{\mathop{\longrightarrow}\limits^{\vbox to3.1pt{\kern-2.0pt\hbox{\scriptsize\rm simulation}\vss}}}\hat{\rho}_{{\rm mag}}.

(47)

Finally, we calculate the fidelity of state generation
F[\psi_{T}]=\left\langle\psi_{T}\middle|\hat{\rho}_{{\rm mag}}[\psi_{T}]\middle|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
which can be interpreted as the probability of finding the magnetization in the target state
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
.

We compare the Wigner function of the target state
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
and the achieved state
\hat{\rho}_{{\rm mag}}[\psi_{T}]
for some special cases. The Wigner function is defined as

W(\beta,\hat{\rho})=\int\frac{d^{2}\gamma}{\pi}{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}\exp\left[\left(\hat{m}-\beta\right)^{\dagger}\gamma-\gamma^{*}\left(\hat{m}-\beta\right)\right]\right].

(48)

The Wigner function can be interpreted as follows. Define a symmetric product
\left\{\hat{m}^{\dagger,p}\hat{m}^{q}\right\}_{S}
as an average over all permutations of
p

\hat{m}^{\dagger}
’s and
q

\hat{m}
’s. For example,

\left\{\hat{m}^{\dagger,2}\hat{m}\right\}_{S}=\frac{\hat{m}^{\dagger,2}\hat{m}+\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}+\hat{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger,2}}{3}.

(49)

In a given state
\hat{\rho}
, the average of the above operator is given by
[
55
,
56
]

{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}\left\{\hat{m}^{\dagger,p}\hat{m}^{q}\right\}_{S}\right]=\int\frac{d^{2}\beta}{\pi}\beta^{*,p}\beta^{q}W(\beta,\hat{\rho}).

(50)

We can convert this into expectation values of the magnetization operators
\hat{M}_{x}
and
\hat{M}_{y}
via the substitution
2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}\hat{m}\rightarrow\hat{M}_{x}-i\hat{M}_{y}
(note that we are using the dressed operator
\hat{m}
instead of the bare magnetization operator
\hat{M}
as the distinction between them is small) getting

{\rm Tr}\left[\hat{\rho}\left\{\hat{M}_{x}^{p}\hat{M}_{y}^{q}\right\}_{S}\right]=\int\frac{dM_{x}dM_{y}}{\pi}M_{x}^{p}M_{y}^{q}\ \tilde{W}\left(M_{x},M_{y},\hat{\rho}\right),

(51)

where
\tilde{W}\left(M_{x},M_{y}.\hat{\rho}\right)=W\left(\frac{M_{x}-iM_{y}}{2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}},\hat{\rho}\right)
. The above equation suggests an interpretation of
\tilde{W}
as a quasi-classical probability distribution. However, note that
\tilde{W}
is not always positive and a negative
\tilde{W}
implies a lack of a probabilistic interpretation considered to be a signature of non-classicality. The unit
\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}
, defined in Eq. (
2
), is given by

\frac{\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}}{M_{s}}=8\times 10^{-6}\sqrt{\frac{$1\text{\,}{\mathrm{\SIUnitSymbolMicro m}}^{3}$}{V_{m}}},

(52)

where
V_{m}
is the volume of the magnet. This is a very small angle but it can be measured via the magnet’s coupling to the transmon
[
31
,
32
]
.

5
Improvements

Here, we focussed on adapting and characterizing the protocol for a system with a magnet and a transmon. There are several methods by which the protocol can be improved, as we now elaborate.

Pre-processing:
We considered only a specific type of operations, where the transmon is excited and the excitation is transferred to the magnons. With the help of the MW cavity, one can also apply other operations, in particular, a displacement given by
\hat{D}(\alpha)=e^{\alpha\hat{m}^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}\hat{m}}
caused by a coherent excitation of the magnet by MWs. This can be used to decrease the time of the protocol as one can choose an appropriate
\alpha
, such that
\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
has fewer magnons than
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
. For example, if
\left\langle\psi_{T}\middle|\hat{m}\middle|\psi_{T}\right\rangle=C
, then

\left\langle\psi_{T}\middle|\hat{D}(C)\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}\hat{D}^{\dagger}(C)\middle|\psi_{T}\right\rangle=\left\langle\psi_{T}\middle|\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}\middle|\psi_{T}\right\rangle-\left|C\right|^{2},

(54)

implying that
\hat{D}^{\dagger}(C)\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
has fewer average magnons than
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
. A better
\alpha
can be found numerically that decreases the highest amplitude in
\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
to within some given tolerance. Then, we run the protocol to create
\hat{D}^{\dagger}(\alpha)\left|\psi_{T}\right\rangle
and apply a MW pulse to displace keeping a high fidelity. If there are other operations that can be easily performed, they can be used for pre-processing to decrease the size.

High fidelity gates:
In our analysis, we find that non-ideal transmon gates are responsible for a significant part of fidelity loss which increases linearly with the number of gates necessary to generate the target state. We took a simple case of correcting at first order perturbation similar to
[
50
,
51
]
. Simply adding higher order terms in a Dyson or a Magnus expansion does not necessarily improve gate fidelities. When we correct for second order term in Magnus expansion, we find that we can decrease the transitions
\left|0\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|2\right\rangle
and
\left|1\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|2\right\rangle
significantly, but at the expense of a high
\left|0\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|3\right\rangle
transition probability
3
3

3

In Refs.
[
50
,
51
]
, only a three level system was considered which does not have this problem
. This gets corrected only at the third order in Magnus expansion, at which point the
\left|0\right\rangle\leftrightarrow\left|4\right\rangle
transition might play a role. However, there is a rich literature on speeding up gates to
\sim 2\pi/\alpha
with low loss of fidelity, such as DRAG
[
67
,
68
]
or optimal control
[
69
,
70
,
71
,
72
]
, that can not only speed up but also improve the accuracy of gates.

Combining steps:
We followed a semi-analytical approach where we systematically add an excitation one by one. In principle, some steps can be combined, e.g. exciting the transmon and transferring the excitation to magnon can occur simultaneously. This becomes infeasible to handle analytically but relevant pulses can, in principle, be found using optimal control theory. A similar case has been discussed in the two-level approximation
[
73
]
for a general harmonic oscillator states with
2-3
quanta. Although this approach should result in the least possible errors, it is computationally very expensive and scaling such an approach to high magnon numbers
[
74
,
70
]
would still require a good initial guess for time-dependent functions describing the transmon driving and the transmon frequency.

6
Conclusions

We proposed and numerically demonstrated a protocol to generate an arbitrary quantum state of the magnetization using its coupling to a superconducting transmon, which is a weakly anharmonic oscillator. The protocol involves repeatedly applying transmon gates and transferring an excitation to the magnons. As longer protocol times lead to higher dissipation, we can improve fidelity of generation by reducing the gate time,
t_{g}
, and the transfer time,
t_{{\rm on}}
, in the notation of Secs.
2
and
3
. Fast gate pulses with duration
\sim 2\pi/\alpha
(
\alpha
is defined in Eq. (
21
)) would imply leakage of excitation into higher transmon levels that can be reduced by modifying the input pulses
[
47
,
69
,
70
,
67
]
. We use correction pulses by removing errors at first order in a Magnus expansion, similar to Refs.
[
50
,
51
]
, bringing the gate time down to
3\pi/\alpha
. Correcting for second order term in the expansion does not seem to decrease errors, but rather transfer the population to higher levels. The transfer time,
t_{{\rm on}}
, can be reduced with a higher magnon-transmon coupling. As the magnon-MW coupling can reach very high values, it is likely to be experimentally feasible to have high magnon-transmon couplings. However, a coupling
g\sim\alpha
causes leakage of magnons into higher transmon levels leading to loss of fidelities, so we choose
g<0.1\alpha
. For such couplings, the leakage is small and can be incorporated by renormalizing the duration of the coupling,
t_{{\rm on},j}
.

The main source of errors is the magnon dissipation putting a limit on the size of the states. We find that the protocol faithfully produces states up to a largest occupation of
\sim 9
magnons and an average of
\sim 4
magnons. We note that our protocol creates states in dressed magnons, given by
\hat{m}
instead of bare magnons
\hat{M}
. As discussed in
A
, for a sufficiently large detuning between MWs and magnons, the distinction is small. We demonstrated our protocol for cases of Fock states
\left|n\right\rangle
, Fock superpositions
\left|0\right\rangle+\left|n\right\rangle
, and cat states
\left|\alpha\right\rangle\pm\left|-\alpha\right\rangle
. While our protocol gives good fidelities for states of moderate sizes, we expect that advanced numerical techniques such as optimal quantum control
[
74
,
70
,
71
]
can improve the state generation (likely by reducing the protocol time) by using our protocol as an initial guiding guess.

With a setup to generate an arbitrary magnetization, there still lies a question of how to experimentally measure it, which we leave to future work. The figures can be generated via the publicly available codes
[
66
]
.

Appendix A
Derivation of effective Hamiltonian

In this appendix, we derive the effective Hamiltonian for a magnet coupled to a transmon indirectly via a MW cavity.

A.1
Microwaves

The MW cavity is modeled by the classical Hamiltonian density

\mathcal{H}_{\rm mw}=\frac{\epsilon_{0}\left|\bi{E}\right|^{2}+\mu_{0}\left|\bi{H}\right|^{2}}{2}.

(55)

It contains several modes labelled by
r
giving the quantization

\bi{H}(\bi{r})\rightarrow\sum_{r}\bi{H}_{r}(\bi{r})\hat{A}_{r}+\bi{H}_{r}^{*}(\bi{r})\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger},

(56)

and similar holds for
\bi{E}
. Here,
\bi{H}_{r}
and
\hat{A}_{r}
are respectively the mode profile and the annihilation operator of mode
r
. With appropriate normalization, this gives the Hamiltonian,

\hat{H}_{\rm mw}=\hbar\sum_{r}\Omega_{a,r}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r},

(57)

where
\Omega_{a,r}
is the resonance frequency of mode
r
.

A.2
Magnet

If the magnet is much smaller than MW wavelengths (
\sim$\mathrm{cm}$
), then only the total magnetization
\bi{M}
couples to the MWs. Under a DC field along
\bi{z}
with magnitude
H_{{\rm app}}
, its classical Hamiltonian density is

\mathcal{H}_{\rm m,mw}=-\mu_{0}H_{{\rm app}}M_{z}-\mu_{0}\bi{M}\cdot\bi{H},

(58)

where
\bi{H}
is the magnetic field due to the cavity. The magnetization is quantized by the Holstein-Primakoff transformation
[
41
,
42
]
, given by a form

M_{x}-iM_{y}\rightarrow 2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}\hat{M}\sqrt{\frac{1-\beta\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}}{1-\beta}},

(59)

where
\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}
and
\beta
are to be found. We know that a spin in an applied field undergoes a Larmor precession with frequency
\Omega_{m}=\gamma\mu_{0}H_{{\rm app}}
where
\gamma
is the absolute value of the gyromagnetic ratio. This implies that the Kittel mode should have the resonance frequency, i.e. after quantization

-\mu_{0}V_{m}H_{{\rm app}}M_{z}\rightarrow\hbar\Omega_{m}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}.

(60)

This implies
M_{z}=\tilde{M}_{s}-M_{{\rm sing}}M^{\dagger}M
where
\tilde{M}_{s}
(to be found) is the saturation magnetization reduced by zero point fluctuations and
M_{{\rm sing}}=\gamma\hbar/V_{m}
is the magnetization reduction because of one (delocalized) spin flip. Using
M_{x}^{2}+M_{y}^{2}+M_{z}^{2}=M_{s}^{2}
, we find the relations

\tilde{M}_{s}^{2}+2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}^{2}=M_{s}^{2},\ \mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}=\sqrt{\frac{\tilde{M}_{s}M_{{\rm sing}}}{2}},\ M_{{\rm sing}}^{2}=\frac{4\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}^{2}\beta}{1-\beta}.

(61)

This is solved explicitly by

\tilde{M}_{s}=\sqrt{M_{s}^{2}+\frac{M_{{\rm sing}}^{2}}{2}}-\frac{M_{{\rm sing}}}{2},\ \beta=\frac{M_{{\rm sing}}}{2\tilde{M}_{s}+M_{{\rm sing}}}

(62)

Typically
M_{{\rm sing}}/M_{s}<10^{-10}
, so we can resort to the leading order corrections,

\tilde{M}_{s}\approx M_{s}-\frac{M_{{\rm sing}}}{2},\ \mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}\approx\sqrt{\frac{M_{s}M_{{\rm sing}}}{2}},\ \beta\approx\frac{M_{{\rm sing}}}{M_{s}}.

(63)

For
\left\langle\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}\right\rangle\ll 1/\beta\sim 10^{10}
, we can simply substitute

M_{x}-iM_{y}\rightarrow 2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}\hat{M},\ M_{z}\rightarrow M_{s}-\frac{2\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}^{2}}{M_{s}}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}.

(64)

This gives the magnon Hamiltonian (coupled to the cavity)

\hat{H}_{\rm m,mw}=\hbar\Omega_{m}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}+\sum_{r}G_{m,r}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+G_{m,r}^{*}\hat{M}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger},

(65)

where
\Omega_{m}=\gamma\mu_{0}H_{{\rm app}}
and

\hbar G_{m,r}=-\mu_{0}\mathcal{M}_{\rm ZPF}\left(H_{x,r}-iH_{y,r}\right).

(66)

We assumed rotating wave approximation which is valid when
G\ll\Omega_{m}+\Omega_{a}
.

A.3
Transmon

We consider a flux-tunable transmon that consists of two Josephson junctions in parallel forming a SQUID loop. Its Hamiltonian is
[
45
,
44
]

\hat{H}_{\rm tr,mw}=E_{C}\left(\hat{n}+\hat{n}_{{\rm mw}}\right)^{2}-E_{J1}\cos\hat{\phi}_{1}-E_{J2}\cos\hat{\phi}_{2}.

(67)

Here,
\hat{n}
is the number of quanta exchanged between the two junctions,
\hat{n}_{{\rm mw}}
is the number of quanta excited by MWs, and
\hat{\phi}_{1,2}
are the fluxes through each junction. Under weak coupling, we have
\hat{n}_{{\rm mw}}\propto\left|\bi{E}\right|
. Flux quantization constraints
\hat{\phi}_{1}-\hat{\phi}_{2}=2\pi m+2\pi\Phi/\Phi_{0}
where
m
is an arbitrary integer,
\Phi
is the flux through the SQUID loop, and
\Phi_{0}=h/2e
is the superconducting flux quantum. Defining
2\hat{\phi}=\hat{\phi}_{1}+\hat{\phi_{2}}
,

\hat{H}_{\rm tr}=E_{C}\hat{n}^{2}-E_{J,{\rm eff}}\cos\left(\hat{\phi}+\phi_{0}\right).

(68)

where the phase is

\phi_{0}=\tan^{-1}\left(\frac{E_{J1}-E_{J2}}{E_{J1}+E_{J2}}\tan\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}\right)+n\pi

(69)

and the effective junction energy is

E_{J,{\rm eff}}(\Phi)=\sqrt{\left(E_{J1}+E_{J2}\right)^{2}\cos^{2}\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}+\left(E_{J1}-E_{J2}\right)^{2}\sin^{2}\frac{\pi\Phi}{\Phi_{0}}}.

(70)

In the transmon limit
E_{J,{\rm eff}}\gg E_{C}
, the fluctuations in
\hat{\phi}+\phi_{0}
are small, so we can expand the cosine and get an approximately quadratic Hamiltonian. This suggests the quantization,

\hat{n}=\left(\frac{E_{J,{\rm eff}}}{2E_{C}}\right)^{1/4}\frac{\hat{Q}-\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}i},\ \hat{\phi}+\phi_{0}=\left(\frac{2E_{C}}{E_{J,{\rm eff}}}\right)^{1/4}\frac{\hat{Q}+\hat{Q}^{\dagger}}{\sqrt{2}},

(71)

that reduces the Hamiltonian to

\hat{H}_{\rm q,mw}=\hbar\Omega_{q}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}-\hbar\frac{A}{2}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\left(\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}-1\right)+\hbar\sum_{r}\left(G_{q,r}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+G_{q,r}^{*}\hat{Q}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\right),

(72)

ignoring higher order terms. Here
\hbar\Omega_{q}=\sqrt{2E_{C}E_{J,{\rm eff}}}-E_{C}/4
and
\hbar A=E_{C}/4
.

A.4
Dressed magnons and transmons

The dressed states are found by diagonalizing the total Hamiltonian
\hat{H}=\hat{H}_{\rm 0}+\hat{H}_{\rm int}
where

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm 0}}{\hbar}=\sum_{r}\Omega_{a,r}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+\Omega_{m}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{M}+\Omega_{q}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}-\frac{A}{2}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\left(\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}-1\right),

(73)

and the interaction

\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm int}}{\hbar}=\sum_{r}\left(G_{m,r}\hat{M}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+G_{m,r}^{*}\hat{M}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}+G_{q,r}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+G_{q,r}^{*}\hat{Q}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\right).

(74)

However, exact diagonalization is not possible analytically, so we resort to a perturbative treatment, in particular a Schrieffer-Wolff (SW) transformation
[
75
]
. The SW transformation perturbatively gives the rotation operator
\hat{S}=-\hat{S}^{\dagger}
such that
e^{-\hat{S}}\hat{H}e^{\hat{S}}
is diagonal to a given order. We find
\hat{S}
to linear order in coupling constants
G
that gives the rotated Hamiltonian,

e^{-\hat{S}}\hat{H}e^{\hat{S}}=\hat{H}_{\rm 0}+\hat{H}_{\rm int}+\left[\hat{H}_{\rm 0},\hat{S}\right]+\left[\hat{H}_{\rm int},\hat{S}\right]+\frac{1}{2}\left[\left[\hat{H}_{\rm 0},\hat{S}\right],\hat{S}\right]+O\left(G^{3}\right).

(75)

To remove first order corrections, we choose

\hat{S}=\sum_{r}\left(\frac{G_{m,r}}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}}\hat{A}_{r}\hat{M}^{\dagger}+G_{q,r}\chi_{r}\left[\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\right]\hat{A}_{r}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\right)-h.c.

(76)

where the transmon susceptibility is

\chi_{r}\left[x\right]=\frac{1}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}-\alpha\left(x-1\right)},

(77)

ensuring
\hat{H}_{\rm int}+\left[\hat{H}_{\rm 0},\hat{S}\right]=\hat{0}
. Note that we need
\left\|\hat{S}\right\|\ll 1
that implies a limit on the number of excitations
\{N_{a,r},N_{m},N_{q}\}
s.t. for all
r
,

G_{m,r}\sqrt{N_{a,r}N_{m}}\ll\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m},\ G_{q,r}\sqrt{N_{a,r}N_{q}}\ll\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}

(78)

are satisfied. This gives the rotated Hamiltonian,

e^{-\hat{S}}\hat{H}e^{\hat{S}}=\hat{H}_{\rm 0}+\frac{1}{2}\left[\hat{H}_{\rm int},\hat{S}\right]+O\left(G^{3}\right).

(79)

As there are four terms in both
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
and
\hat{S}
, a total of
16
commutators are required. If we ignore all non-resonant terms, such as
\hat{A}_{r}^{2}\hat{Q}^{\dagger,2}
, and use the Hermiticity of
\hat{H}_{\rm int}
we are left with

\frac{1}{2\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{\rm int},\hat{S}\right]=\sum_{rs}\left[\left(G_{m,r}^{*}\hat{M}+G_{q,r}^{*}\hat{Q}\right)\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger},\left(\frac{G_{m,s}}{\Omega_{a,s}-\Omega_{m}}\hat{M}^{\dagger}+G_{q,s}\chi_{s}\left(\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\right)\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\right)\hat{A}_{s}\right]+h.c.

(80)

For
\hat{f},\hat{g}
commuting with all photon operators,

\left[\hat{f}\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger},\hat{g}\hat{A}_{s}\right]=-\hat{f}\hat{g}\delta_{rs}+\left[\hat{f},\hat{g}\right]\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{s}.

(81)

This leads to

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\hbar}\left[\hat{H}_{\rm int},\hat{S}\right]=-\sum_{r}\left(G_{m,r}^{*}\hat{M}+G_{q,r}^{*}\hat{Q}\right)\left(\frac{G_{m,r}}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}}\hat{M}^{\dagger}+G_{q,r}\chi_{r}\left(\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\right)\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\right)

\displaystyle+\delta\Omega_{a,r}\left[\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\right]\hat{A}_{r}^{\dagger}\hat{A}_{r}+h.c.

(82)

where

\delta\Omega_{a,r}\left[x\right]=\frac{\left|G_{m,r}\right|^{2}}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}}+\left|G_{q,r}\right|^{2}\left(\left(x+1\right)\chi_{r}\left(x+1\right)-x\chi_{r}\left(x\right)\right).

(83)

This procedure yields the rotated Hamiltonian. To return back to the original one, we define the dressed operators:
\hat{m}=e^{\hat{S}}\hat{M}e^{-\hat{S}}
,
\hat{q}=e^{\hat{S}}\hat{Q}e^{-\hat{S}}
, and
\hat{a}_{r}=e^{\hat{S}}\hat{A}_{r}e^{-\hat{S}}
. Up to linear order in
G
, the dressed magnon operator is

\hat{m}\approx\hat{M}-\sum\frac{G_{m,r}}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}}\hat{A}_{r}+O\left(G^{2}\right),

(84)

and the dressed transmon operator is

\hat{q}\approx\hat{Q}-\sum G_{q,r}\hat{A}_{r}\left(\chi_{r}\left[\hat{Q}\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\right]-\chi_{r}\left[\hat{Q}^{\dagger}\hat{Q}\right]\right)+O\left(G^{2}\right).

(85)

This gives the final Hamiltonian

\frac{\hat{H}}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\left(\omega_{q}+\hat{\alpha}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]\right)\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}+\hat{m}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{g}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]+\hat{g}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{q}+\frac{\hat{H}_{\rm mw,eff}}{\hbar}.

(86)

Here,
\hat{H}_{\rm mw,eff}
is the effective MW Hamiltonian that is not relevant for our discussion. The renormalized magnon’s frequency is Stark shifted by the MWs:

\omega_{m}=\Omega_{m}-\sum_{r}\frac{\left|G_{m,r}\right|^{2}}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}}.

(87)

Similarly, the transmon’s frequency is

\omega_{q}=\Omega_{q}-\sum_{r}\frac{\left|G_{q,r}\right|^{2}}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}}.

(88)

The Stark shift of higher transmon levels renormalizes the anharmonicity to

\hat{\alpha}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]=\frac{A}{2}\left(\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-1\right)-\sum_{r}\left|G_{q,r}\right|^{2}\left[\frac{1}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}-A\left(\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-1\right)}-\frac{1}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}}\right].

(89)

Finally, the coupling of each transmon transition to the magnons is given by

\hat{g}\left[\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}\right]=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{r}G_{m,r}^{*}G_{q,r}\left(\frac{1}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{q}-A\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}}+\frac{1}{\Omega_{a,r}-\Omega_{m}}\right).

(90)

Appendix B
Transfer of Quanta

In this appendix, we show how to obtain the periods required for transferring quanta from transmon to magnons. In what follows,
\left|m,q\right\rangle
denotes a state with
m
magnons and transmon at
q
th level. Given a state
\left|\Psi\right\rangle
having a maximum of
j
excitations, i.e.
\left\langle m,q\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0
for
m+q>j
and a tunable Hamiltonian

\frac{\hat{H}(t)}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\left(\omega_{q}+\Delta(t)\right)\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|+\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}g_{n}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n+1\right|+g_{n}^{*}\hat{m}\left|n+1\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|,

(91)

we want to find
\{\Delta(t),t_{{\rm tot}}\}
s.t.
\left\langle j,0\middle|\hat{{\cal T}}^{\dagger}\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0
, where the time-evolution operator is

\hat{{\cal T}}=\mathrm{Texp}\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t_{{\rm tot}}}\hat{H}(\tau)d\tau\right],

(92)

with
{\rm Texp}
being the time-ordered exponential operator. Here,
\alpha_{n}\equiv\left\langle n\middle|\hat{\alpha}\middle|n\right\rangle
and
g_{n}\equiv\left\langle n\middle|\hat{g}\middle|n\right\rangle
as derived in the previous section.

First, consider the ansatz
\Delta(t)=\Delta_{0}\left[u(t-t_{{\rm on}})-u(t)\right]
with
\Delta_{0}=\omega_{m}-\omega_{q}
, s.t. the magnons and transmons are resonant for a time-period
t_{{\rm on}}
, effectively switching on the coupling. Adding an off-period of
t_{{\rm off}}
afterwards, we get
t_{{\rm tot}}=t_{{\rm on}}+t_{{\rm off}}
and we want to find
\{t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\}
to satisfy the above condition. We can derive the time-evolution unitary explicitly using
[\hat{H}(t),\hat{N}]=0
where the total number of excitations are given by

\hat{N}=\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}.

(93)

Thus, we divide the wave-function as

\left|\Psi(t)\right\rangle=\sum_{N=0}^{\infty}\sum_{s=0}^{N}\xi_{Ns}(t)\left|N-s,s\right\rangle.

(94)

During the off-time, we have the equations of motion, [using the Schrödinger equation
\left|\dot{\Psi}\right\rangle=-i\hat{H}\left|\Psi\right\rangle
].

\dot{\xi}_{Ns}=-i\left(\omega_{m}\left(N-s\right)+\omega_{q}s-\alpha_{s}\right)\xi_{Ns}-i\left[g_{s}\sqrt{N-s}\ \xi_{N,s+1}+g_{s-1}^{*}\sqrt{N-s+1}\ \xi_{N,s-1}\right].

(95)

This is a set of linear differential equations which can be solved by diagonalizing the dynamical matrix. For the case
N=j
, we can write

\bi{\xi}_{j}(t_{{\rm on}}+t_{{\rm off}})=V_{{\rm off}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm off}}t_{{\rm off}}}V_{{\rm off}}^{\dagger}\bi{\xi}_{j}(t_{{\rm on}}),

(96)

where the vector
\bi{\xi}_{j}=\left(\xi_{j0}\ \xi_{j1}\ \dots\ \xi_{jj}\right)^{T}
, and the dynamical matrix is diagonalized by
\{-i\Lambda_{{\rm off}},V_{{\rm off}}\}
. Similar analysis with
\omega_{q}\rightarrow\omega_{m}
works for the on-time,

\bi{\xi}_{j}\left(t_{{\rm on}}+t_{{\rm off}}\right)=V_{{\rm off}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm off}}t_{{\rm off}}}V_{{\rm off}}^{\dagger}V_{{\rm on}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm on}}t_{{\rm on}}}V_{{\rm on}}^{\dagger}\bi{\xi}_{j}(0).

(97)

We want to ensure
\left\langle\Psi\middle|\hat{{\cal T}}\middle|j,0\right\rangle=0
. We find
\hat{{\cal T}}\left|j,0\right\rangle
by putting
\bi{\xi}_{j}(0)=\left(1\ 0\ \dots\ 0\right)^{T}\equiv\bi{u}_{j}
and then, the condition
\left\langle\Psi\middle|\hat{{\cal T}}\middle|j,0\right\rangle=0
becomes

\bi{\Psi}_{j}^{\dagger}\left(V_{{\rm off}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm off}}t_{{\rm off}}}V_{{\rm off}}^{\dagger}V_{{\rm on}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm on}}t_{{\rm on}}}V_{{\rm on}}^{\dagger}\right)\bi{u}_{j}=0,

(98)

where the vector
\bi{\Psi}_{j}=\left(\left\langle j,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle\ \left\langle j-1,1\middle|\Psi\right\rangle\ \dots\ \left\langle 0,j\middle|\Psi\right\rangle\right)^{T}
. This can be numerically solved for
\{t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\}
.

In the special case of a two-level approximation (ignoring higher levels) and for sufficiently large detuning
g_{0}\ll\omega_{m}-\omega_{q}
, the above condition reduces to

\cos\left(g_{0}t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}\sqrt{j}\right)\left\langle j,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle+ie^{-i\Delta_{0}t_{{\rm off}}^{(0)}}\sin\left(g_{0}t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}\sqrt{j}\right)\left\langle j-1,1\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0.

(99)

This gives

\displaystyle g_{0}\sqrt{j}t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\tan^{-1}\left|\frac{\left\langle j,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}{\left\langle j-1,1\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}\right|,

(100)

\displaystyle\Delta_{0}t_{{\rm off}}^{(0)}

\displaystyle={\rm arg}\left[\frac{\left\langle j,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}{\left\langle j-1,1\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}\right]+\frac{\pi}{2}.

(101)

In some special cases, Eq. (
98
) will have no solution. Then, we try the ansatz with a pulse of width
t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}
, followed by an off-time
t_{{\rm off}}^{(0)}
and a second pulse of an undetermined time
t_{{\rm on}}
,

\Delta(t)=\Delta_{0}\left[u\left(t-t_{{\rm on}}-t_{{\rm tot}}^{(0)}\right)-u\left(t-t_{{\rm tot}}^{(0)}\right)+u\left(t-t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}\right)-u\left(t\right)\right],

(102)

where
t_{{\rm tot}}^{(0)}=t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}+t_{{\rm off}}^{(0)}
. After another off-period of time
t_{{\rm off}}
, the optimization condition becomes (following the same analysis as before)

\bi{\Psi}_{j}^{\dagger}\left(V_{{\rm off}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm off}}t_{{\rm off}}}V_{{\rm off}}^{\dagger}V_{{\rm on}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm on}}t_{{\rm on}}}V_{{\rm on}}^{\dagger}\right)\left(V_{{\rm off}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm off}}t_{{\rm off}}^{(0)}}V_{{\rm off}}^{\dagger}V_{{\rm on}}e^{-i\Lambda_{{\rm on}}t_{{\rm on}}^{(0)}}V_{{\rm on}}^{\dagger}\right)\bi{u}_{j}=0,

(103)

which can be solved for
\{t_{{\rm on}},t_{{\rm off}}\}
. This process can be repeated indefinitely, however, in practice, we did not observe the need to go beyond a second iteration.

Appendix C
Transmon Gates

We now describe how the transmon gates can be implemented in our scheme. In what follows,
\left|m,q\right\rangle
denotes a state with
m
magnons and transmon at
q
th level. Given an initial state
\left|\Psi\right\rangle
with a maximum of
j
magnons and
(j+1)
excitations, i.e.
\left\langle m,q\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0
for
m+q>j+1
and
\left\langle j+1,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0
, and a tunable Hamiltonian

\frac{\hat{H}(t)}{\hbar}=\omega_{m}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}+\omega_{q}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|+\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)\hat{q}^{\dagger}+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{*}(t)\hat{q},

(104)

we want to find
\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)
s.t.
\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}^{\dagger}\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0
, where the time-evolution operator

\hat{{\cal Q}}=\mathrm{Texp}\left[-\frac{i}{\hbar}\int_{0}^{t_{g}}\hat{H}(\tau)d\tau\right],

(105)

with
{\rm Texp}
being the time ordered exponential operator and
t_{g}
being a pre-determined gate time.

Note that we have ignored the coupling between magnons and transmons assuming large enough detuning
\omega_{m}-\omega_{q}
. Then, the evolution is given by

\hat{{\cal Q}}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{m}t\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}}\otimes\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}(t).

(106)

Here,

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{{\cal U}_{g}}=-i\left(\omega_{q}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|+\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)\hat{q}^{\dagger}+\tilde{\varepsilon}^{*}(t)\hat{q}\right)\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}.

(107)

An ideal gate between
\left|0\right\rangle
and
\left|1\right\rangle
, with angular parameters
\{\theta,\phi\}
is

\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ideal}}=e^{i\Phi_{{\rm global}}}\left[\cos\theta\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+i\sin\theta\left(e^{i\phi}\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+e^{-i\phi}\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|\right],

(108)

barring an unimportant global phase
\Phi_{{\rm global}}
. The parameters for this gate can be found by ensuring
\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}_{{\rm ideal}}^{\dagger}\middle|\Psi\right\rangle=0
, where
\hat{{\cal Q}}_{{\rm ideal}}=e^{-i\omega_{m}t_{g}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}}\otimes\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ideal}}
giving

\theta_{{\rm ideal}}=\tan^{-1}\left|\frac{\left\langle j,1\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}{\left\langle j,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}\right|

(109)

and

\phi_{{\rm ideal}}=-\frac{\pi}{2}+\text{arg}\left[\frac{\left\langle j,1\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}{\left\langle j,0\middle|\Psi\right\rangle}\right].

(110)

To implement this, we try the ansatz
\tilde{\varepsilon}(t)=-\varepsilon(t)e^{-i\omega_{q}t+i\phi_{{\rm app}}}
with real
\{\varepsilon(t),\phi_{\rm app}\}
to be found. We separate out the phases by the transformation,

\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}(t)=e^{-i\left(\omega_{q}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|\right)t}\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ph}}\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm\theta}}(t)\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ph}}^{\dagger},

(111)

where

\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ph}}=e^{i\phi_{{\rm app}}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}}

(112)

and
\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}
satisfies

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}=i\varepsilon(t)\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ e^{-i\left(\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_{n}\right)t}\left|n+1\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|+h.c.\right)\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta},

(113)

where by definition
\alpha_{0}=\alpha_{1}=0
.

First, consider the two-level approximation, where we want to solve

\frac{d}{dt}\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}^{(0)}=i\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|+\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|\right)\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}^{(0)},

(114)

where
\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)
is the ‘bare’ solution to be chosen. The unitary becomes

\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}^{(0)}(t)=\cos\Theta(t)\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+i\sin\Theta(t)\left(\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|,

(115)

where

\Theta^{(0)}(t)=\int_{0}^{t}\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)d\tau.

(116)

For such a case, we have

\displaystyle\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}^{(0)}(t)=\left[\cos\Theta^{(0)}(t)\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+e^{-i\omega_{q}t_{g}}\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+\right.

\displaystyle\left.i\sin\Theta^{(0)}(t)\left(e^{i\phi_{{\rm app}}^{(0)}}e^{-i\omega_{q}t_{g}}\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|+e^{-i\phi_{{\rm app}}^{(0)}}\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|\right)+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}e^{-i\left(\omega_{q}-\alpha_{n}\right)t}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|\right].

This resembles the ideal gate
\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ideal}}
except for the extra phases due to the free evolution of the states. For this two-level approximation, we can ensure
\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}^{(0),\dagger}\middle|\tilde{\Psi}_{j+1}\right\rangle=0
, where
\hat{{\cal Q}}^{(0)}=e^{-i\omega_{m}t_{g}\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}}\otimes\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}^{(0)}(t_{g})
by choosing

\Theta^{(0)}(t_{g})=\theta_{{\rm ideal}},\ \phi_{{\rm app}}^{(0)}=\phi_{{\rm ideal}}+\omega_{q}t_{g}.

(117)

Notice that we can choose any
\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)
satisfying
\int_{0}^{t_{g}}d\tau\varepsilon^{(0)}(\tau)=\theta_{{\rm ideal}}
. For concreteness, we choose a sinusoidal

\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)=\frac{\pi\theta_{{\rm ideal}}}{2t_{g}}\sin\left(\frac{\pi t}{t_{g}}\right).

(118)

(119)

where the error Hamiltonian is

\delta\hat{H}=\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}^{(0)\dagger}\left(\varepsilon(t)\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ e^{-i\left(\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_{n}\right)t}\left|n+1\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|-\varepsilon^{(0)}(t)\left(\left|0\right\rangle\left\langle 1\right|+\left|1\right\rangle\left\langle 0\right|\right)+h.c.\right)\hat{{\cal U}}_{\theta}^{(0)}.

with the reduced Hamiltonians defined as

\displaystyle\delta\hat{h}_{12}

\displaystyle\delta\hat{h}_{{\rm rest}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\sqrt{n+1}\ e^{-i\left(\alpha_{n+1}-\alpha_{n}\right)t}\left|n+1\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|+h.c.

(122)

so, to ensure

C.2
Satisfying the conditions

The conditions allow for a lot of freedom in choosing
\varepsilon(t)
. Here, we take an ansatz of a sum of sinusoids, ensuring that the pulse starts and ends at
0
,

\varepsilon(t)=\sum_{\nu}\varepsilon_{\nu}\sin\left(\frac{\nu\pi t}{t_{g}}\right),

(126)

where the set of indices
\nu
can be freely chosen. Eq. (
123

\sum_{\nu\>{\rm odd}}\frac{2\varepsilon_{\nu}t_{g}}{\pi\nu}=\theta_{{\rm ideal}}.

(127)

Eq. (
125
) is

\sum_{\nu}\frac{\pi\nu\varepsilon_{\mu}t_{g}}{\left(\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{2}\right)^{2}t_{g}^{2}-\pi^{2}\nu^{2}}\left(1-\left(-1\right)^{\nu}e^{i\left(\alpha_{3}-\alpha_{2}\right)t_{g}}\right)=0.

(128)

Eq. (

(129)

(130)

giving

\int_{0}^{\pi}du\exp\left[i\zeta u+i\xi\cos u\right]=2i^{\zeta}G_{\zeta}(\xi).

(131)

For

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\>{\rm odd}}i^{\nu-1}\frac{\varepsilon_{\nu}t_{g}}{\pi}\left[G_{\zeta+\nu}\left(\frac{\pm\theta}{2}\right)+G_{\zeta-\nu}\left(\frac{\pm\theta}{2}\right)\right]

(132)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\nu\>{\rm even}}i^{\nu}\frac{\varepsilon_{\nu}t_{g}}{\pi}\left[G_{\zeta+\nu}\left(\frac{\pm\theta}{2}\right)-G_{\zeta-\nu}\left(\frac{\pm\theta}{2}\right)\right].

(133)

If we choose the gate time s.t.

for some integer

For simulation purposes, we choose the specific case of

C.3
Phase

Given
\varepsilon(t)
, we find
\phi_{{\rm app}}
to minimize
\left|\left\langle j,1\middle|\hat{{\cal Q}}^{\dagger}\middle|\Psi\right\rangle\right|
. We have
\hat{{\cal Q}}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{m}t\hat{m}^{\dagger}\hat{m}}\otimes\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}(t)
where
\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}(t)
can be decomposed as in Eq. (
111
)]. Then,

\hat{{\cal U}_{g}}(t_{g})\left|1\right\rangle=e^{-i\phi_{{\rm app}}}e^{-i\left(\omega_{q}\hat{q}^{\dagger}\hat{q}-\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\alpha_{n}\left|n\right\rangle\left\langle n\right|\right)t_{g}}\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm ph}}\hat{{\cal U}}_{{\rm\theta}}(t_{g})\left|1\right\rangle.

(140)

As the leakage into higher levels is suppressed via an appropriate choice of
\varepsilon(t)
, we expect the 2x2 sub-block of
\hat{{\cal U}}_{g}(t_{g})
for the subspace spanned by
\{\left|0\right\rangle,\left|1\right\rangle\}
to be approximately unitary. For concreteness, we find the unitary matrix closest to this sub-block, say
\hat{\cal U}_{g,01}
, in 2-norm via
\hat{\cal U}_{g,01}^{{\rm unit}}=VW^{\dagger}
using the singular value decomposition
\hat{\cal U}_{g,01}=V\Lambda W^{\dagger}
. This gives

\hat{\cal U}_{g,01}^{{\rm unit}}\left|1\right\rangle=e^{i\Phi_{G}}\left(i\sin\tilde{\theta}e^{i\delta\phi}\left|0\right\rangle+\cos\tilde{\theta}\left|1\right\rangle\right),

(141)

for some constants
\{\Phi_{G},\tilde{\theta},\delta\phi\}
. Then,

\left\langle\Psi\middle|\hat{\cal U}_{g}(t_{g})\middle|j,1\right\rangle\approx e^{-ij\omega_{m}t-i\phi_{{\rm app}}+i\Phi_{G}}\left(i\sin\tilde{\theta}e^{i\delta\phi}\left\langle\Psi\middle|j,0\right\rangle+\cos\tilde{\theta}e^{i\phi_{{\rm app}}-i\omega_{q}t_{g}}\left\langle\Psi\middle|j,1\right\rangle\right),

(142)

giving the amplitude

\left|\left\langle\Psi\middle|\hat{\cal U}_{g}(t_{g})\middle|j,1\right\rangle\right|\approx\sqrt{\left|\left\langle\Psi\middle|j,0\right\rangle\right|^{2}+\left|\left\langle\Psi\middle|j,1\right\rangle\right|^{2}}\left|\cos\theta_{{\rm ideal}}\sin\tilde{\theta}-\cos\tilde{\theta}\sin\theta_{{\rm ideal}}e^{i\phi_{{\rm app}}-i\phi_{{\rm app}}^{(0)}-i\delta\phi}\right|.

(143)

This is minimized at

\phi_{{\rm app}}=\phi_{{\rm app}}^{(0)}+\delta\phi.

(144)
