Dirichlet law for factorization of integers, polynomials and permutations
Sun-Kai Leung

Abstract
. Let k ⩾ 2 k\geqslant 2 be an integer. We prove that factorization of integers into k k parts follows the Dirichlet distribution Dir ⁡ ( 1 k , … , 1 k ) \mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right) by multidimensional contour integration, thereby generalizing the Deshouillers–Dress–Tenenbaum (DDT) arcsine law on divisors where k = 2 k=2 . The same holds for factorization of polynomials or permutations. Dirichlet distribution with arbitrary parameters can be modelled similarly.



1.
Introduction

Given an integer
n\geqslant 1
, it is natural to study the distribution of its divisors over the interval
{[1,n]}
(in logarithmic scale). Let
d
be a random integer chosen uniformly from the divisors of
n.
Then
D_{n}:=\frac{\log d}{\log n}
is a random variable taking values in
[0,1].
While one can show that
the sequence of random variables
\{D_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
does not converge in distribution, Deshouillers, Dress and Tenenbaum
[
DDT79
]
proved the
mean of the corresponding distribution functions converges to that of the arcsine law. More precisely, uniformly for
u\in[0,1],
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}\leqslant u\right)=\frac{2}{\pi}\arcsin{\sqrt{u}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{\log x}}\right),

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}\leqslant u\right):=\frac{1}{\tau(n)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d|n\\
d\leqslant n^{u}\end{subarray}}1

is the distribution function of
D_{n}
and the
error term here is optimal (see also
[
Ten15
, Chapter 6.2]
).

Recently, Nyandwi and Smati
[
NS13
]
studied the distribution of pairs of divisors of a given integer on average.
Similarly, they also proved the
mean of the corresponding distribution functions converges to that of the beta two-dimensional law uniformly together with the optimal rate of convergence.

Our main goal here is to generalize their work to higher dimensions, which they claim is very technical following the usual approach (see
[
NS18
, p. 2]
). Fix
k\geqslant 2.
Given an integer
n\geqslant 1,
let
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
be a random
k
-tuple chosen uniformly from the set of all possible factorization
\{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\,:\,n=m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\}.
Then
\boldsymbol{D}_{n}=(D_{n}^{(1)},\ldots,D_{n}^{(k)}):=\left(\frac{\log d_{1}}{\log n},\ldots,\frac{\log d_{k}}{\log n}\right)
is a multivariate random variable taking values in
[0,1]^{k}.
Similarly, we are interested in the mean

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}^{(1)}\leqslant u_{1},\ldots,D_{n}^{(k-1)}\leqslant u_{k-1}\right),

where

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(D_{n}^{(1)}\leqslant u_{1},\ldots,D_{n}^{(k-1)}\leqslant u_{k-1}\right):=\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\underset{d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}|n}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant n^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant n^{u_{k-1}}}}1

is the distribution function of
\boldsymbol{D}_{n}.

Note that since
n=d_{1}\cdots d_{k},
the multivariate random variable
\boldsymbol{D}_{n}
must satisfies

\displaystyle 1=D_{n}^{(1)}+\cdots+D_{n}^{(k)},

and so it actually takes values in the
(k-1)
-dimensional probability simplex. We now turn to the Dirichlet distribution, which
is the most natural candidate of modeling such distribution.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 2.
The Dirichlet distribution of dimension
k
with parameters
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}>0
is denoted by

\mathrm{Dir}\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right),
which is
defined on the
(k-1)
-dimensional probability simplex

\displaystyle\Delta^{k-1}:=\{(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})\in[0,1]^{k}\,:\,t_{1}+\cdots+t_{k}=1\}

having density

\displaystyle f_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k}):=\frac{\Gamma\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha_{i}\right)}{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\Gamma(\alpha_{i})}\prod_{i=1}^{k}t_{i}^{\alpha_{i}-1}.

For instance, when
k=2,
Dirichlet distribution reduces to beta distribution
\mathrm{Beta}\left(\alpha,\beta\right)
with parameters
\alpha,\beta
.
In particular,
\mathrm{Beta}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{2}\right)
is the arcsine distribution.

As we will see, factorization of integers into
k
parts follows the Dirichlet distribution
\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right).

Since for each
i
the parameter
\alpha_{i}=\frac{1}{k}
is less than
1,
the density
f_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})
blows up most rapidly at the
k
vertices of the probability simplex. Therefore, our intuition that a typical factorization of integers into
k
parts consists of one large factor and
k-1
small factors is justified quantitatively.

By definition, for
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}\geqslant 0
satisfying
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k-1}\leqslant 1,
the distribution function of
\mathrm{Dir}\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right)
is given by

\displaystyle F_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}):=\int_{0}^{u_{1}}\cdots\int_{0}^{u_{k-1}}f_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{k-1},1-t_{1}-\cdots-t_{k-1})dt_{1}\cdots dt_{k-1}.

From now on until Section
6
, we shall fix
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right)
and omit the subscript.

The main results are stated as follows.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer. Then uniformly for
x\geqslant 2
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}\geqslant 0
satisfying
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k-1}\leqslant 1,

we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\underset{d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant n^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant n^{u_{k-1}}}}1=F(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1})+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right).

The error term here is optimal if full uniformity in
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}
is required. Indeed, if we choose

u_{1}=\cdots=u_{k-2}=\frac{1}{k},u_{k-1}=0,
then one can show that the left-hand side of (
1.1
) is of order
(\log x)^{-\frac{1}{k}}
using
[
DDT79
, Théorème T]
followed by partial summation.

Remark 1.1
.

Instead of using the logarithmic scale, one may also study localized factorization of integers, say for instance the quantity

\displaystyle H^{k}(x,\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{z}):=\left|\left\{n\leqslant x\ :\begin{array}[]{c}\text{there exists $(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k-1})\in\mathbb{N}^{k-1}$ such that }\\
\text{$d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}|n$ and $y_{i}<d_{i}\leqslant z_{i}$ for $i=1,\ldots,k-1$}\end{array}\right\}\right|,

which was discussed in
[
Kou10
]
.

Note that Theorem
1.1
implies that for any axis-parallel rectangle
R\subseteq\Delta^{k-1},
we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\mathbb{P}\left(\boldsymbol{D}_{n}\in R\right)=\int_{R}dF+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{\frac{1}{k}}}\right).

Since every Borel subset of the simplex can be approximated by finite unions of such rectangles, the following corollary is an immediate consequence of Theorem
1.1
.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer. For
x\geqslant 1,
let
n
be a random integer chosen uniformly from
[1,x]
and
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
be a random
k
-tuple chosen uniformly from the set of all possible factorization
\{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\,:\,n=m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\}.
Then we have the convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{\log d_{1}}{\log n},\ldots,\frac{\log d_{k}}{\log n}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right)

as
x\to\infty.

It is a general phenomenon that the “anatomy” of polynomials or permutations is essentially the same as that of integers (see
[
Gra08
]
,
[
GG19
]
), and the main theorem here is no exception.
In the realm of polynomials, the following theorem serves as the counterpart to
Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer and
q
be a fixed prime power. Then uniformly for
n\geqslant 1
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}\geqslant 0
satisfying
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k-1}\leqslant 1
, we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{q^{n}}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{M}_{q}(n)}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(F)}\underset{D_{1}\cdots D_{k-1}\mid F}{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D_{1}\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\\
\deg D_{1}\leqslant nu_{1}\end{subarray}}\cdots\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}D_{k-1}\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\\
\deg D_{k-1}\leqslant nu_{k-1}\end{subarray}}}1=F(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1})+O\left(n^{-\frac{1}{k}}\right),

where the notations are defined in
Section
2
.

Corollary 1.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer and
q
be a fixed prime power. For
n\geqslant 1,
let
F
be a random polynomial chosen uniformly from
\mathcal{M}_{q}(n)
and
(D_{1},\ldots,D_{k})
be a random
k
-tuple chosen uniformly from the set of all possible factorization
\{(G_{1},\ldots,G_{k})\in\mathcal{M}_{q}^{k}\,:\,F=G_{1}\cdots G_{k}\}.
Then we have the convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{\deg D_{1}}{n},\ldots,\frac{\deg D_{k}}{n}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right)

as
n\to\infty.

Similarly, in the realm of permutations, the following theorem serves as the counterpart to
Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer. Then uniformly for
n\geqslant 1
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}\geqslant 0
satisfying
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k-1}\leqslant 1
, we have

(1.3)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(\sigma)}\underset{{\begin{subarray}{c}[n]=A_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup A_{k}\\
\sigma(A_{i})=A_{i},i=1,\ldots,k\\
0\leqslant|A_{i}|\leqslant nu_{i},i=1,\ldots,k-1\end{subarray}}}{\sum\cdots\sum}1=F(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1})+O\left(n^{-\frac{1}{k}}\right),

where the notations are defined in
Section
2
.

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be a fixed integer. For
n\geqslant 1,
let
\sigma
be a random permutation chosen uniformly from
S_{n}
and
(A_{1},\ldots,A_{k})
be a random
k
-tuple chosen uniformly from the set of all possible
\sigma
-invariant decomposition
\{(B_{1},\ldots,B_{k})\,:\,[n]=B_{1}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup B_{k},\sigma(B_{i})=B_{i}\text{ for }i=1,\ldots,k\}.
Then we have the convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{|A_{1}|}{n},\ldots,\frac{|A_{k}|}{n}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right)

as
n\to\infty.

In Section
7
, we will model the Dirichlet distribution with arbitrary parameters by assigning probability weights which are not necessarily uniform to each integer and to each factorization. Then, as we will see, most of the results in the literature about the distribution of divisors in logarithmic scale are direct consequences of Theorem
7.1
, which is a generalization of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Notation

Throughout the paper, we shall adopt the following list of notations:

•

we say
f(x)=O(g(x))
or
f(x)\ll g(x)
if there exists a constant
C>0
which might depend on
k,q,\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\delta}
such that
|f(x)|\leqslant C\cdot g(x)
whenever
x>x_{0}
for some
x_{0}>0;

•

[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\};

•

\tau_{k}(n):=|\{(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\,:\,n=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}\}|
and
\tau(n):=\tau_{2}(n);

•

\mathcal{M}_{q}:=\{F\in\mathbb{F}_{q}[x]\,:\,F\text{ is monic}\};

•

\mathcal{M}_{q}(n):=\{F\in\mathcal{M}_{q}\,:\,\deg{F}=n\};

•

\tau_{k}(F):=|\{(D_{1},\ldots,D_{k})\in\mathcal{M}_{q}^{k}\,:\,F=D_{1}\cdots D_{k}\}|;

•

S_{n}
denotes the group of permutations on
[n];

•

c(\sigma)
denotes the number of disjoint cycles of the permutation
\sigma;

•

\tau_{\alpha}(\sigma):={\alpha}^{c(\sigma)};

•

\left[{n\atop k}\right]:=|\{\sigma\in S_{n}\,:\,c(\sigma)=k\}|
denote (unsigned) Stirling numbers of the first kind.

3.
Properties of
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})

Both
[
DDT79
]
and
[
NS13
]
deal with the divisors one by one using
[
DDT79
, Théorème T]
followed by partial summation. However, as
k
gets larger, it is increasingly laborious to achieve full uniformity as well as the optimal rate of convergence, especially when one of the
u
’s is small or
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k-1}
is close to
1
. Instead, we would like to apply Mellin’s inversion formula to (the second derivative of) the multiple Dirichlet series
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
defined below, which allows a more symmetric approach to the problem so that all the divisors can be handled simultaneously. We first establish a few properties of the multiple Dirichlet series that are essential to the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
denote the multiple Dirichlet series

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{\infty}\frac{\tau_{k}(n_{1}\cdots n_{k})^{-1}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{k}^{s_{k}}}.

Then
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})
converges absolutely in the domain

\displaystyle\Omega:=\{(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\,:\,\text{$\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})>1$ {for} $j=1,\dots,k$}\}

and uniformly on any compact subset of
\Omega.
In particular,
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
is an analytic function of
k
variables in
\Omega.

Proof.

Let
\sigma_{j}:=\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})
for
j=1,\ldots,k.
Then, since

\displaystyle\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{\infty}\left|\frac{\tau_{k}(n_{1}\cdots n_{k})^{-1}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{k}^{s_{k}}}\right|\leqslant\zeta(\sigma_{1})\cdots\zeta(\sigma_{k})<\infty,

the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

The multiple Dirichlet series
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
can be expressed as the Euler product

\displaystyle\prod_{p}\sum_{v_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{k}=0}^{\infty}{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}+k-1\choose{k-1}}^{-1}p^{-(v_{1}s_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}s_{k})}

in the domain
\Omega
defined above.

Proof.

Let
y\geqslant 2
and
\sigma_{j}:=\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})
for
j=1,\dots,k.
Then, since

\displaystyle\sum_{v_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{k}=0}^{\infty}{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}+k-1\choose{k-1}}^{-1}p^{-(v_{1}\sigma_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}\sigma_{k})}\leqslant\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{\sigma_{1}}}\right)^{-1}\cdots\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{\sigma_{k}}}\right)^{-1}<\infty,

the finite product

\displaystyle\prod_{p\leqslant y}\sum_{v_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{k}=0}^{\infty}{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}+k-1\choose{k-1}}^{-1}p^{-(v_{1}s_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}s_{k})}

is well-defined.

Let
S(y):=\{n\geqslant 1\,:\,p|n\text{ implies }p\leqslant y\}
be the set of
y
-smooth numbers. Then, since

\displaystyle\tau_{k}(p^{v})={v+k-1\choose k-1},

we have

\displaystyle\left|\prod_{p\leqslant y}\sum_{v_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{k}=0}^{\infty}{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}+k-1\choose{k-1}}^{-1}p^{-(v_{1}s_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}s_{k})}-\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n_{1}\in S(y)}\cdots\sum_{n_{k}\in S(y)}\frac{\tau_{k}(n_{1}\cdots n_{k})^{-1}}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{k}^{s_{k}}}-\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\right|\leqslant\sum_{j=1}^{k}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}\zeta(\sigma_{i})\sum_{n_{j}>y}\frac{1}{n_{j}^{\sigma_{j}}}.

The lemma follows by letting
y\to\infty.

∎

Lemma 3.3
.

For
j=1,\ldots,k,
let
R_{j}\subseteq\left\{s_{j}\in\mathbb{C}\,:\,\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})>\frac{3}{4},\,|\mathrm{Im}(s_{j})|>\frac{1}{4}\right\}
be a
zero-free region for
\zeta(s_{j}).

Then the multiple Dirichlet series
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
can be continued analytically to the domain
\prod_{j=1}^{k}R_{j}.
Moreover, we have the bound

(3.1)

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\ll|\zeta(s_{1})|^{\frac{1}{k}}\cdots|\zeta(s_{k})|^{\frac{1}{k}}.

Proof.

Let
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}
with
\sigma_{j}:=\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})>1
for
j=1,\dots,k.
Then by Lemma
3.2
we have the Euler product expression

\displaystyle\zeta(s_{1})^{-\frac{1}{k}}\cdots\zeta(s_{k})^{-\frac{1}{k}}\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{p}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{k}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{s_{j}}}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}}\right)\sum_{v_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{k}=0}^{\infty}{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}+k-1\choose{k-1}}^{-1}p^{-(v_{1}s_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}s_{k})},

where the
k
-th root is understood as its principal branch.

For
j=1,\ldots,k,
expanding the
k
-th root as

\displaystyle\sum_{r=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{r}{\frac{1}{k}\choose r}p^{-rs_{j}},

we find that the factors of the Euler product are

1+O\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}p^{-(\sigma_{i}+\sigma_{j})}\right)
by Taylor’s theorem. Therefore, the function

(3.2)

\displaystyle\zeta(s_{1})^{-\frac{1}{k}}\cdots\zeta(s_{k})^{-\frac{1}{k}}\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})

can be continued analytically to the domain where

\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})>\frac{3}{4}
for
j=1,\dots,k,
in which it is
uniformly bounded.

On the other hand, for
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\prod_{j=1}^{k}R_{j},
we can express
\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
as

\displaystyle\zeta(s_{1})^{\frac{1}{k}}\cdots\zeta(s_{k})^{\frac{1}{k}}\left(\zeta(s_{1})^{-\frac{1}{k}}\cdots\zeta(s_{k})^{-\frac{1}{k}}\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\right),

and so the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 3.4
.

In the open hypercube

\displaystyle Q:=\left\{(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k})\in\mathbb{C}^{k}\,:\,1<\mathrm{Re}(s_{j})<7/4,\,|\mathrm{Im}(s_{j})|<3/4\text{ {for} $j=1,\dots,k$}\right\},

we have the estimate

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2k}}{\partial s_{1}^{2}\cdots\partial s_{k}^{2}}\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})=\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k^{k}}

\displaystyle(s_{1}-1)^{-\frac{1}{k}-2}\cdots(s_{k}-1)^{-\frac{1}{k}-2}

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle(1+O(|s_{1}-1|+\cdots+|s_{k}-1|)).

Proof.

By (
3.2
) and the fact that

(3.3)

\displaystyle\zeta(s)=\frac{1}{s-1}+O(1),

we have the power series representation

\displaystyle(s_{1}-1)^{\frac{1}{k}}\cdots(s_{k}-1)^{\frac{1}{k}}\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}^{k}}a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}(s_{1}-1)^{i_{1}}\cdots(s_{k}-1)^{i_{k}}

in
Q
for some constants
a_{i_{1},\dots,i_{k}}\in\mathbb{C}.

It follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2k}}{\partial s_{1}^{2}\cdots\partial s_{k}^{2}}\mathcal{D}(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})=\left(1+\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k}\frac{1}{k^{k}}

\displaystyle(s_{1}-1)^{-\frac{1}{k}-2}\cdots(s_{k}-1)^{-\frac{1}{k}-2}

\displaystyle\cdot

\displaystyle(a_{\boldsymbol{0}}+O(|s_{1}-1|+\cdots+|s_{k}-1|)),

where by (
3.3
) and Lemma
3.2
,
the leading coefficient

\displaystyle a_{\boldsymbol{0}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{v_{1}=0}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{v_{k}=0}^{\infty}{v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k}+k-1\choose{k-1}}^{-1}p^{-(v_{1}+\cdots+v_{k})}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{v=0}^{\infty}{v+k-1\choose k-1}{v+k-1\choose k-1}^{-1}p^{-v}=1.

∎

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We begin with writing

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\underset{d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant n^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant n^{u_{k-1}}}}1=\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\left(\underset{d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant x^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant x^{u_{k-1}}}}1-\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n\\
n^{u_{j}}<d_{j}\leqslant x^{u_{j}}\text{ for some $j$}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant x^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant x^{u_{k-1}}}}1\right),

where the main term is

(4.1)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\underset{d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant x^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant x^{u_{k-1}}}}1=\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant x^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant x^{u_{k-1}}}\sum_{d_{k}\leqslant x/(d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1})}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(d_{1}\cdots d_{k})},

and the error term is

(4.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n\\
n^{u_{j}}<d_{j}\leqslant x^{u_{j}}\text{ for some $j$}\end{subarray}}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant x^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant x^{u_{k-1}}}}1\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
u_{j}\neq 0\end{subarray}}^{k-1}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\sum_{n^{u_{j}}<d_{j}\leqslant x^{u_{j}}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\leqslant x^{u_{i}},i\neq j\\
d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n\end{subarray}}{\sum\cdots\sum}\quad 1.

Let us first bound the error term (
4.2
).
For
j=1,\dots,k-1
with
u_{j}\neq 0,

we write
n=d_{j}m
for some integer
m.
Then
d_{j}>n^{u_{j}}
implies
m<d_{j}^{(1-u_{j})/u_{j}},
and the number of ways of obtaining
m
as a product
d_{1}\cdots d_{j-1}d_{j+1}\cdots d_{k}
is bounded by
\tau_{k-1}(m).
It follows that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}\frac{1}{\tau_{k}(n)}\sum_{n^{u_{j}}<d_{j}\leqslant x^{u_{j}}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}d_{i}\leqslant x^{u_{i}}\text{ for }i\neq j\\
d_{1}\cdots d_{k-1}\mid n\end{subarray}}{\sum\cdots\sum}\quad 1\leqslant\sum_{d_{j}\leqslant x^{u_{j}}}\sum_{m<d_{j}^{(1-u_{j})/u_{j}}}\frac{\tau_{k-1}(m)}{\tau_{k}(d_{j}m)}.

If

One may repeat the same argument using a multiple Dirichlet series but a more direct and elementary proof is presented here.

Although (

Adopting the notations in Section

\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}\tau_{\alpha}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\left[{n\atop k}\right]\alpha^{k}.

Now any permutation

with

To begin with, there are

\displaystyle F(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)^{k}}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{i}\leqslant nu_{i}-1\\
i=1,\dots,k-1\end{subarray}}{\sum\cdots\sum}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left(\frac{m_{i}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\frac{1}{n^{k-1}}+O\left(n^{-\frac{1}{k}}\right)

\displaystyle+O\left(\frac{1}{n}\underset{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant m_{i}\leqslant nu_{i}-1\\
i=1,\dots,k-1\end{subarray}}{\sum\cdots\sum}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\frac{m_{j}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}-2}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}\left(\frac{m_{i}}{n}\right)^{\frac{1}{k}-1}\frac{1}{n^{k-1}}\right),

and the last error term here is exactly the same as (
6.9
), which is again
\ll n^{-\frac{1}{k}}.

7.
Factorization into
k
parts in the general setting

With a view to model Dirichlet distribution with arbitrary parameters, we further explore the factorization of integers into
k
parts in the general setting using multiplicative functions of several variables defined below.

Definition 7.1
.

An arithmetic function of
k
variables
F\colon\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to be multiplicative if it satisfies the condition

F(1,\ldots,1)=1
and the functional equation

\displaystyle F(m_{1}n_{1},\ldots,m_{k}n_{k})=F(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})F(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})

whenever
(m_{1}\cdots m_{k},n_{1}\cdots n_{k})=1,
or equivalently,

\displaystyle F(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=\prod_{p}F(p^{v_{p}(n_{1})},\ldots,p^{v_{p}(n_{k})}),

where
v_{p}(n):=\max\{k\geqslant 0\,:\,p^{k}|n\}.

Remark 7.1
.

Multiplicative functions of several variables, such as the “GCD function” and the “LCM function” are interesting for their own sake. See
[
Tót14
]
for further discussion.

To adapt the proof of Theorem
1.1
, we consider the following class of
multiplicative functions.

Definition 7.2
.

Let
\boldsymbol{\alpha}=(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}),\boldsymbol{\beta}=(\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{k}),\boldsymbol{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{k}),\boldsymbol{\delta}=(\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{k})

with
\alpha_{j},\beta_{j},c_{j}>0,\delta_{j}\geqslant 0
for
j=1,\ldots,k.
We denote by

\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\alpha};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\delta})
the class of non-negative multiplicative functions of
k
variables
F\colon\mathbb{N}^{k}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

(divisor bound) for
j=1,\ldots,k,
we have
|F(1,\ldots,\overbrace{n}^{\text{$j$-th}},\ldots 1)|\leqslant\tau_{\beta_{j}}(n),

where

\displaystyle\tau_{\beta}(n):=\prod_{p}{v_{p}(n)+\beta-1\choose v_{p}(n)}

is the generalized divisor function;

(b)

(analytic continuation) let
s=\sigma+it\in\mathbb{C}.
For
j=1,\ldots,k
, the Dirichlet series

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{F}(s;\boldsymbol{\alpha},j):=\sum_{p}\frac{F(1,\ldots,\overbrace{p}^{\text{$j$-th}},\ldots 1)-\alpha_{j}}{p^{s}}

defined for
\sigma>1
can be continued analytically to the domain where
\sigma>1-\frac{c_{j}}{\log(2+|t|)};

(c)

(growth rate) for
j=1,\ldots,k,
in the domain above we have the bound

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}_{F}(s;\boldsymbol{\alpha},j)\leqslant\delta_{j}\log(2+|t|).

For instance, the multiplicative function

F(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})=\tau_{k}(n_{1}\cdots n_{k})^{-1}
belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}\left(\boldsymbol{\frac{1}{k}};\boldsymbol{\frac{1}{k}},\boldsymbol{1},\boldsymbol{0}\right).

Applying the Mellin transform to (higher derivatives of) the multiple Dirichlet series

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{F}(s_{1},\ldots,s_{k}):=\sum_{n_{1}=1}^{\infty}\cdots\sum_{n_{k}=1}^{\infty}\frac{F(n_{1},\ldots,n_{k})}{n_{1}^{s_{1}}\cdots n_{k}^{s_{k}}}

as before, one can prove the following generalization of Lemma
4.3
.

Lemma 7.1
.

Given a multiplicative function of
k
variables
F\in\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\alpha};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\delta}).
Let
m\geqslant 2
be an integer and
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}\geqslant e.
We denote by
S_{F}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k};m)
the weighted sum

\displaystyle\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant x_{1}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k}\leqslant x_{k}}(\log d_{1})^{m}\cdots(\log d_{k})^{m}F(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}).

Then there exists
m_{0}=m_{0}(\boldsymbol{\alpha},\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\delta})
such that for any integer
m\geqslant m_{0},
we have

\displaystyle S_{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};m)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha_{j})}\int_{1}^{x_{j}}(\log y_{j})^{\alpha_{j}+m-1}dy_{j}+R_{F}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k};m)

with

\displaystyle R_{F}(x_{1},\dots,x_{k};m)\ll x_{1}\cdots x_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\left(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1\\
i\neq j\end{subarray}}^{k}(\log x_{i})^{\alpha_{i}+m-1}\right)(\log x_{j})^{\alpha_{j}+m-2}.

To model the Dirichlet distribution by factorizing integers into
k
parts, we consider the following class of pairs of multiplicative functions.

Definition 7.3
.

Let
\theta>0
and
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
be a positive
k
-tuple.
We denote by

\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

the class of pairs of multiplicative functions
(f;G)
satisfying the following conditions:

(a)

for
n\geqslant 1,
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})>0;

(b)

the multiplicative function
f
belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}(\theta;\beta^{\prime},c^{\prime},\delta^{\prime})
for some
\beta^{\prime},c^{\prime},\delta^{\prime};

(c)

the multiplicative function of
k
variables

\displaystyle F(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}):=f(n)\cdot\frac{G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})}{\sum_{n=e_{1}\cdots e_{k}}G(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})}

belongs to the class
\mathcal{M}(\boldsymbol{\alpha};\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\delta})
for some
\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{c},\boldsymbol{\delta},
where
n=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}.

Remark 7.2
.

By definition, we must have
\theta=\alpha_{1}+\cdots+\alpha_{k}.

Then, applying Lemma
7.1
followed by partial summation as before, one can prove the following generalization of Theorem
1.1
.

Theorem 7.1
.

Let
(f;G)
be a pair of multiplicative functions belonging to the class
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}).
Then uniformly for
x\geqslant 2
and
u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1}\geqslant 0
satisfying
u_{1}+\cdots+u_{k-1}\leqslant 1,
we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{m\leqslant x}f(m)\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}f(n)\left(\sum_{n=e_{1}\cdots e_{k}}G(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})\right)^{-1}\underset{n=d_{1}\cdots d_{k}}{\sum_{d_{1}\leqslant n^{u_{1}}}\cdots\sum_{d_{k-1}\leqslant n^{u_{k-1}}}\sum_{d_{k}\leqslant n}}G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle F_{\boldsymbol{\alpha}}(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k-1})+O\left(\frac{1}{(\log x)^{\min\{1,\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\}}}\right).

Finally, we conclude with the following generalization of Corollary
1.1
.

Corollary 7.1
.

Given a pair of multiplicative functions
(f;G)
belonging to the class
\mathcal{M}_{\theta}(\boldsymbol{\alpha}).

For
x\geqslant 1,
let
n
be a random integer chosen from
[1,x]
with probability
\left(\sum_{m\leqslant x}f(m)\right)^{-1}f(n)
and
(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
be a random
k
-tuple chosen from the set of all possible factorization
\{(m_{1},\ldots,m_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}\,:\,n=m_{1}\cdots m_{k}\}
with probability
\left(\sum_{n=e_{1}\cdots e_{k}}G(e_{1},\ldots,e_{k})\right)^{-1}G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
. Then we have the convergence in distribution

\left(\frac{\log d_{1}}{\log n},\ldots,\frac{\log d_{k}}{\log n}\right)\xrightarrow{d}\mathrm{Dir}\left(\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{k}\right)

as
x\to\infty.

Remark 7.3
.

See
[
BM17
]
,

[
BM21
]
for the cases where
k=2,3
respectively, in which
G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})
takes the form
(f_{1}\ast\cdots\ast f_{k-1}\ast 1)(d_{1}\cdots d_{k})
for some multiplicative functions
f_{1},\ldots,f_{k-1}:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}.

Example 7.1
.

For
k\geqslant 2,
let
\theta,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}>0.
We consider the pair of multiplicative functions

\displaystyle f(n)=\tau_{\theta}(n);\quad G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})=\tau_{\lambda_{1}}(d_{1})\cdots\tau_{\lambda_{k}}(d_{k}).

Then the Dirichlet distribution of dimension
k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{\theta\lambda_{1}}{\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}},\ldots,\frac{\theta\lambda_{k}}{\lambda_{1}+\cdots+\lambda_{k}}\right)

can be modelled in the sense of Corollary
7.1
. In particular, when
\theta,\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}=1,
it reduces to Theorem
1.1
.

Example 7.2
.

For
q\geqslant 3,
let
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{\varphi(q)}\}
be a reduced residue system
\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)
. We consider the pair of multiplicative functions

\displaystyle f(n)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $(n,q)=1$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases};\quad G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $p|d_{j}$ implies $p\equiv a_{j}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ q)$ for $j=1,\ldots,\varphi(q)$},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Then the Dirichlet distribution of dimension
\varphi(q)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{\varphi(q)},\ldots,\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\right)

can be modelled in the sense of Corollary
7.1
. In particular, when
q=4,
it reduces to

[
MV07
, Exercise 6.2.22]
.

Example 7.3
.

For
k\geqslant 2,
we consider the pair of multiplicative functions

\displaystyle f(n)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $n$ is a sum of two squares},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases};\quad G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\equiv 1.

Then the Dirichlet distribution of dimension
k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{2k},\ldots,\frac{1}{2k}\right)

can be modelled in the sense of Corollary
7.1
. In particular, when
k=2,
it reduces to
[
DHN15
, Theorem 2]
.

Example 7.4
.

For
k\geqslant 2,
we consider the pair of multiplicative functions

\displaystyle f(n)=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $n$ is square-free},\\
0&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases};\quad G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})\equiv 1.

Then the Dirichlet distribution of dimension
k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right)

can be modelled in the sense of Corollary
7.1
. In particular, when
k=2,
it reduces to
[
FC17
, Theorem 2]
with
y=x.

Example 7.5
.

For
k\geqslant 2
, let
\mathcal{R}
be a subset of
\{\{i,j\}\,:\,1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant k\}.
We consider the pair of multiplicative functions

\displaystyle f(n)\equiv 1;\quad G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $(d_{i},d_{j})=1$ whenever
$\{i,j\}\notin\mathcal{R},$
}\\
0&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Then the Dirichlet distribution of dimension
k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{k},\ldots,\frac{1}{k}\right)

can be modelled in the sense of Corollary
7.1
. In particular, when
k=2^{r}
for
r\geqslant 2,
it reduces to
[
BT16
, Théorème 1.1]
with a suitable subset
\mathcal{R}
via total decomposition sets (see
[
Hal96
, Theorem 0.20]
), which is itself a generalization of
[
BM12
, Theorem 2.1]
for
r=2.

Example 7.6
.

For
k\geqslant 3
, we consider the pair of multiplicative functions

\displaystyle f(n)\equiv 1;\quad G(d_{1},\ldots,d_{k})=\prod_{j=1}^{k-1}\frac{1}{\tau(d_{j}\cdots d_{k})}.

Then the Dirichlet distribution of dimension
k

\displaystyle\mathrm{Dir}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{4},\ldots,\frac{1}{2^{k-2}},\frac{1}{2^{k-1}},\frac{1}{2^{k-1}}\right)

can be modelled in the sense of Corollary
7.1
. In particular, when
k=3,
it reduces to
[
BT16
, Théorème 1.2]
.

Unsurprisingly, we expect that Theorem

should also hold for polynomials or permutations. Specifically, in the realm of permutations, the counterpart to multiplicative functions is the generalized Ewens measure (see
