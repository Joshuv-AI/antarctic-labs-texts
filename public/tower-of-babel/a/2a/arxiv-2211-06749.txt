Bertrand’s paradox on a monitor
Martin Klazar

Abstract
We investigate Bertrand’s probabilistic paradox through the lens of discrete geometry and old-fashioned but reliable discrete probability. We approximate the plane unit circle with 1 n × 1 n \frac{1}{n}\times\frac{1}{n} boxes and count the pairs of boxes separated by distance more than 3 \sqrt{3} . For n → ∞ n\to\infty the proportion of such pairs goes to 1 + 3 8 − π ⁡ ( 2 − 3 ) 96 = 0.33273 ​ … . \frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{8}-\frac{\pi(2-\sqrt{3})}{96}=0.33273\dots\;.

Introduction

We discuss and implement a discrete approach to the well
known Bertrand’s probabilistic paradox. J. Bertrand proposed it
in his textbook
[
4
, pp. 5–6]
in 1889. Another, less known, Bertrand’s paradox
is in economics
[
5
,
8
,
11
]
.
The probabilistic paradox requires to compute the probability

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}

that a random chord
T
in a plane circle
C
is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. If
C
has radius
1
, then
T

has to be longer than
\sqrt{3}
. J. Bertrand proposed three numerically
different solutions, given below as Solutions 1–3. It is paradoxical that the solution is far from unique.

Solution 1.
For
T
determined by its endpoints
\{a,b\}=C\cap T
one gets

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\frac{1}{3}
.

Solution 2.
Parametrization of
T
by the intersection point of
T
with the diameter

P
of
C
perpendicular to
T
leads to
\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\frac{1}{2}
.

Solution 3.
Parametrization of
T
by its midpoint yields
\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\frac{1}{4}
.

But one does not have to stop here.
Solution 4.
W. W. Funkenbusch
[
7
]

fixes a point
a\in C
, selects at random another point
b
on or inside
C
, and takes
the chord
T
going through
a
and
b
. By computing the corresponding area inside
C
,
he gets that

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\frac{1}{3}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2\pi}=0.60899\dots\;.

This constant appears in a different context also in
[
12
]
.

Solution 5.
Inspired by the previous solution, we propose to fix a point

a\in C
, select uniformly and at random a number
d\in(0,2]
, flip a fair coin
c\in\{h,t\}
,
and get
T
as the upper or lower chord, depending on the coin flip, with the endpoint
a

and length
d
. Thus

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=2-\sqrt{3}=0.26794\dots\;.

Solution 6.
O. Sheynin
[
22
]
mentions that R. De Montessus
[
16
]
proposed for

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}
uncountably many values.

Let us discuss the first five solutions more abstractly. We denote the open
interior disc of
C
by
D
, its closure by
\overline{D}=D\cup C
, the center
of
D
by
s
and we set

S=C\times C\setminus\{(a,\,a)\;|\;a\in C\}\;.

We denote by

\mathcal{CH}=\{\{a,\,b\}\;|\;a,\,b\in C,\,a\neq b\}

the set of all chords of
C
, and by
\mathcal{CH}_{l}\subset\mathcal{CH}
the set of chords longer than
\sqrt{3}
.
A unified approach to the above solutions selects a probability space
(\Omega,\Sigma,\mu)
, an (almost) surjective map

F\colon\Omega\to\mathcal{CH}

such that
F^{-1}[\mathcal{CH}_{l}]\in\Sigma
, and then sets

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\mu(F^{-1}[\mathcal{CH}_{l}])\;.

The following spaces are employed; by the “product probability measure” we mean product of
normalized Lebesgue measures.

Solution 1 uses
S
with the product probability measure.
Solution 2 uses
[0,\pi)\times(0,2)
with the product probability measure.
Solution 3 uses
D\setminus\{s\}
with the normalized two-dimensional Lebesgue measure.
Solution 4 uses

(C\times\overline{D})\setminus\{(a,a)\;|\;a\in C\}

with the product probability measure. In Solution 5 we use the space

C\times(0,2]\times\{h,t\}

with the product of normalized Lebesgue measures and the counting measure on
\{h,t\}
.

In Solutions 2 and 3 the map
F
is injective. In Solution 1 the map
F
is two-to-one,
two pairs
(a,b)
always determine one chord. In Solution 5 the map
F
is two-to-one,
except for
d=2
when four triples
(a,d,c)
determine a single chord. In Solution 4
the map
F
is many-to-one. No exposition of Bertrand’s paradox we know of notices that
Solution 1 differs from Solutions 2 and 3 in the non-injectivity of
F
. True, this point
is not very important because one may replace the onto and two-to-one map

F\colon S\to\mathcal{CH}
with a bijection
G\colon S^{\prime}\to\mathcal{CH}
, where
S^{\prime}

is a half of
S
; this replacement preserves the value
\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\frac{1}{3}
because
the measure is normalized to give
S^{\prime}
probability
1
.

In Solutions 1, 2, 4 and 5 the map
F
is onto but in Solution 3 the image
F[\Omega]
misses the chords
T
containing the center
s
.
One may be tempted to dismiss them on the ground that they have probability
0
,
but this is not rigorous. These chords are outside the image of
F
and, in the chosen model, simply have no probability. It is formally more satisfying to extend
F

in the obvious way to a bijection

F\colon\Omega:=(D\setminus\{s\})\cup u\to\mathcal{CH}\;,

where
u=[0,\pi)\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
is taken as a plane segment disjoint to
D
, and to use in
\Omega
again the normalized two-dimensional
Lebesgue measure. Solution 4 shows that the tacit assumption of (almost) injectivity of
F
is in reality not very strongly justified. One produces
chords
T
in
C
by random experiments,
and it may well happen that many of them yield the same outcome.

We give one more
Solution 7.
Since both sets
\mathcal{CH}_{l}

and
\mathcal{CH}\setminus\mathcal{CH}_{l}
have the same cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
of continuum, there is
a bijection
F\colon[0,1]\to\mathcal{CH}
such that

F^{-1}[\mathcal{CH}_{l}]=[0,\frac{2021}{2022}]
. So, using the normalized
Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
, we get

\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}=\frac{2021}{2022}=0.99950\dots\;.

We want to contribute to this debate by proposing a realistic mathematical model of the paradox when it is treated as
a physical experiment.
The idea of experimental resolution of the paradox is an old one, but in our opinion physical
schemes proposed so far do not employ faithful mathematical models. For example,
[
12
]

suggests to
perform the random selection of
T
by letting cosmic rays pass through a disc chamber detector, or by imagining
them doing so. But the rays are in
[
12
]
treated as lines with zero width.
The problem with such approaches is that nothing like a ray or a circle with zero width exists in reality.
Physical chords and physical circles have nonzero, positive widths and are made of discrete parts with positive sizes.
Any “simplification” that replaces a positive number with zero
is a jump in a (totally) different world. In each case it should be carefully justified and one should check that contact with
the original setup was not lost and that after the simplification one is not solving a (completely) different problem.

We think that the plethora of solutions for Bertrand’s paradox demonstrate that by working with chords and circles with zero width
we simplify too much.

We return to what it means, or should mean — in our opinion — to resolve Bertrand’s paradox in the last section.

So we want to compute the probability in Bertrand’s paradox in the old-fashioned but safe way
as the limit ratio of two
finite
numbers, namely that of favorable cases and that of all cases. We denote
this discrete version of
\mathrm{Pr_{Be}}
by

\mathrm{Pr_{Be,\,\Box}}\;.

We work in the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and for
n\to\infty
consider a sequence of square grids of axes-parallel

\frac{1}{n}\times\frac{1}{n}
squares, so called boxes, with a vertex of the grid in the origin. We consider physical
unit circles
C(n)
consisting of boxes whose interiors intersect the geometric, origin-centered unit circle

C
. Let
\mathrm{Ch}(n)\subset C(n)\times C(n)
be the pairs
(B,B^{\prime})
of boxes
in
C(n)
with distance
>\sqrt{3}
, i.e., such that some points
b
and
b^{\prime}
in the interior of
B

and
B^{\prime}
, respectively, have distance
|bb^{\prime}|>\sqrt{3}
. We denote the cardinality of a finite set
X

by
|X|
.

In Theorem
3.1
we compute that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pr_{Be,\,\Box}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\mathrm{Ch}(n)|}{|C(n)\times C(n)|-|C(n)|}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|\mathrm{Ch}(n)|}{|C(n)|^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{8}-\frac{\pi(2-\sqrt{3})}{96}=0.33273\dots\;.

The chords in
\mathcal{CH}
are unordered pairs while in
C(n)\times C(n)
they are ordered pairs, but the factor
2
cancels in the ratio.
In Corollary
2.7
we determine the limit proportion

\mathrm{Pr_{1,\,3,\,\Box}}:=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{|C_{0,\,\pi/3}(n)|}{|C(n)|}=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{16}=0.17075\dots

of the boxes lying in the first quadrant of
C(n)
in the angular positions
(0,\pi/3)
. In Corollary
2.6
we note that there are many more (ordered or unordered) pairs of boxes in
C(n)
than there are boxes intersecting the disc
D
.
Thus for square grids Bertrand’s Solution 3 cannot be implemented, there are not enough midpoints to represent bijectively all chords. Corollary
2.5
gives the formula

|C(n)|=8n-4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\,|\,n^{2}\\
d=2e+1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{e}

for the size of the physical unit circle.
For example,
|C(3)|=24-4(1-1+1)=20
.
But — despite its appearance — the formula is not efficient; we do not know how to factorize integers efficiently, and therefore the formula does
not tell us how to compute for given
n
in time polynomial in
\log n
the value
|C(n)|
.
Propositions
2.1
–
2.4
are auxiliary. In Section 4 we give some concluding remarks and address suggestions of one referee.

Circles seen today are often sets of pixels on the monitor of a computer or a TV set, selected from a fine rectangular array. Or maybe from an array with other shape, but this is not the point. Such circles inspired us to write this article, not the ideal
geometric circle with zero width. Approximating
C
by
C(n)
seems inadequate for circles drawn in media like plain paper without
the raster of graph paper, or on blackboard, slate or in sand. Is there a realistic material such that when one draws
C
on it,
the distribution of mass in the approximation
is roughly uniform with respect to the angular measure? In Bertrand’s Solution 1 this is taken for
granted. We hope to address this and similar questions at another occasion.

2
Counting vertical and horizontal boxes

By
\mathbb{N}=\{1,2,\dots\}
we denote the set of natural numbers,
\mathbb{Z}
are the integers and
\mathbb{N}_{0}=\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}=\{0,1,\dots\}
.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
we set
[n]=\{1,2,\dots,n\}
,
[0]=\emptyset
,
for
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
with
a\leq b
we set
[a,b]_{\mathbb{Z}}=\{a,a+1,\dots,b\}
;
[a,b]_{\mathbb{Z}}=\emptyset
for
a>b
. For
n\in\mathbb{N}

and
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}
we consider the
\frac{1}{n}\times\frac{1}{n}

boxes

B=B(n)=B_{i,\,j}(n)=[i/n,\,(i+1)/n]\times[j/n,\,(j+1)/n]\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}\;.

B^{o}
denotes the interior of the box
B
. Let

C=\{(x,\,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}\;|\;x^{2}+y^{2}=1\}

be the geometric, origin-centered unit circle
in the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
and let
D
,
\overline{D}
and
s=(0,0)\in D
be as above in Section 1. We define the approximations
C(n)
of
C
by boxes as

C(n)=\{B_{i,\,j}(n)\;|\;i,\,j\,\in\mathbb{Z}\wedge B_{i,\,j}(n)^{o}\cap C\neq\emptyset\},\ n\in\mathbb{N}\;.

More generally, for any
n\in\mathbb{N}
and any
X\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
we define

X(n)=\{B(n)\;|\;B(n)^{o}\cap X\neq\emptyset\}\;.

We say that a box is
exceptional
if one of its four corners lies on
C
.

Proposition 2.1

.

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, there are at most
O_{\varepsilon}(n^{\varepsilon})
exceptional boxes.

Proof.
B_{i,j}(n)
is exceptional if and only if one of the four sums
i^{2}+j^{2}
,
(i+1)^{2}+j^{2}
,
i^{2}+(j+1)^{2}

and
(i+1)^{2}+(j+1)^{2}
equals
n^{2}
. Hence the number of exceptional
boxes is
O(r_{2}(n^{2}))
, where for
m\in\mathbb{N}
the
quantity
r_{2}(m)
is the number of solutions
(i,j)\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}

of the equation
i^{2}+j^{2}=m
. There is the formula

r_{2}(m)=4\sum_{d\,|\,m\,\wedge\,d=2e+1}(-1)^{e}\;,

see
[
10
]
. Thus the number of exceptional boxes is
O(\tau(n^{2}))
,
where
\tau(m)
is the number of divisors of
m
. It is well known (
[
10
]
)
that
\tau(m)=O_{\varepsilon}(m^{\varepsilon})
.

\Box

A
chord
T(n)
in
C(n)
is any ordered pair of distinct boxes,

T(n)=(B,B^{\prime})\in C(n)\times C(n)\;\text{ and }\;B\neq B^{\prime}\;.

The
full chord
T_{B,B^{\prime}}(n)
in
C(n)
generated by the chord
(B,B^{\prime})
is the set

T_{B,\,B^{\prime}}(n)=\{A\;|\;\exists\,ab:\ a\in B^{o}\wedge b\in(B^{\prime})^{o}\wedge ab\cap A^{o}\neq\emptyset\}\;.

of all boxes
A
with interior intersected by a straight segment
ab\subset\mathbb{R}^{2}
that joins two points
inside
B
and
B^{\prime}
. But we will not use full chords. The main reason is that one may have

T_{A,A^{\prime}}(n)=T_{B,B^{\prime}}(n)
for two chords
(A,A^{\prime})
and
(B,B^{\prime})
even if
\{A,A^{\prime}\}\neq\{B,B^{\prime}\}
.
This happens for example when

A=B_{-n,\,0}(n),\ A^{\prime}=B_{n,\,-1}(n),\ B=B_{-n,\,-1}(n)\;\text{ and }\;B^{\prime}=B_{n,\,0}(n)

— then
T_{A,A^{\prime}}(n)=T_{B,B^{\prime}}(n)
is formed by the two layers of boxes intersecting the
segment
(-1,1)\times\{0\}
. We believe that this happens infrequently, so that

\frac{\text{the number of full chords in $C(n)$}}{\text{the number of chords in $C(n)$}}\to\frac{1}{2}

as
n\to\infty
.

A key tool in our computations is Abel’s summation formula (
[
24
, Thm. 0.3]
). It has a nice and
short proof, which we include here. Our proof differs from that in
[
24
]
and we actually
give a more general theorem. Recall that every bounded function
f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
with only
finitely many discontinuities is Riemann-integrable; we only use Riemann integration (
[
24
]
uses Stieltjes integrals). For a sequence
(a_{n})=(a_{1},a_{2},\dots)\subset\mathbb{R}

and a real number
x
we set (
n\in\mathbb{N}
)

A(x)=\sum_{n\leq x}a_{n}\;,

where the empty sum is as usual defined to be
0
. For a function

f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}
we use the notation
[f]_{a}^{b}=f(b)-f(a)
.

Proposition 2.2
(Abel’s summation)

.

Let
l,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
l<m
,
(a_{n})\subset\mathbb{R}
be a sequence and
f\colon[l,m]\to\mathbb{R}

be a continuously differentiable
function. Then

\sum_{l<n\leq m}a_{n}f(n)=[A(x)f(x)]_{l}^{m}-\int_{l}^{m}A(t)f^{\prime}(t)\;\mathrm{dt}\;.

Proof.
Both expressions equated in the identity are additive. This means that if
k,l,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}

with
k<l<m
, then the value of the expression on the interval
[k,m]
equals to the sum
of its values on the intervals
[k,l]
and
[l,m]
. Thus it suffices to prove the identity
for every
l\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and
m=l+1
. Then the left-hand side is
a_{m}f(m)
and the right-hand side is

\displaystyle[A(x)f(x)]_{l}^{m}-A(l)\int_{l}^{m}f^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle[A(x)f(x)]_{l}^{m}-A(l)[f(x)]_{l}^{m}=[A(x)]_{l}^{m}f(m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a_{m}f(m)

too.

\Box

See
[
25
, p. 13/14]
for another application of this technique proving sum-integral identities.

We orient
C
counter-clockwisely and call a box
B\in C(n)

vertical
(resp.
horizontal
)
if
C
enters
B
in the vertical (resp. horizontal) side of
B
. Each non-exceptional box in
C(n)
is either
vertical or horizontal, and if a box in
C(n)
is both then it is exceptional. For any two angles

\alpha\leq\beta
in
\mathbb{R}
with
\beta-\alpha\leq 2\pi
we define

C_{\alpha,\,\beta}\subset C

to be the open arc of points
p\in C
such that the length of the arc
P_{0}p\subset C
, where
P_{0}:=(1,0)
,
that goes counter-clockwisely from
P_{0}
to
p
lies, modulo
2\pi
, in the interval
(\alpha,\beta)
. We
consider the approximation
C_{\alpha,\beta}(n)
of the arc
C_{\alpha,\beta}
by boxes
and denote by
C_{\alpha,\beta,\mathrm{v}}(n)
(resp.
C_{\alpha,\beta,\mathrm{h}}(n)
) the subset
of vertical (resp. horizontal) boxes.

Proposition 2.3

.
For any fixed
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\pi
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the estimate

|C_{\alpha,\,\beta,\,\mathrm{v}}(n)|=[-\cos t]_{\alpha}^{\beta}\cdot n+O(1)\;.

For
\pi\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq 2\pi
this estimate holds with the function
\cos t
.
For any fixed
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
-\pi/2\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\pi/2
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the estimate

|C_{\alpha,\,\beta,\,\mathrm{h}}(n)|=[\sin t]_{\alpha}^{\beta}\cdot n+O(1)\;.

For
\pi/2\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq 3\pi/2
this estimate holds with the function
-\sin t
.

Proof.
Let an
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
\alpha,\beta\in[0,\pi]
with
\alpha\leq\beta
be given. Let
I\subset[-n+1,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
be the interval of the integers

i
such that the line
x=i/n
intersects the arc
C_{\alpha,\beta}
. Since the boxes
B
in
C_{\alpha,\beta,\mathrm{v}}(n)

1-1 correspond, except possibly for the rightmost one, to
I
(the right side of the box
B
, where
C_{\alpha,\beta}
enters
B
,
lies on the line
x=i/n
,
i\in I
), we have the bounds

|I|\leq|C_{\alpha,\,\beta,\,\mathrm{v}}(n)|\leq|I|+1\;.

For
i\in[-n,n]_{\mathbb{Z}}
,
let
\alpha_{i}\in[0,\pi]
be the angle at the vertex
s=(0,0)
in the triangle
p_{i}\,s\,(1,0)
, where
p_{i}\in C
is the intersection
of the upper closed semicircle of
C
with the line
x=i/n
. Clearly,
i/n=\cos\alpha_{i}
. Thus

|I|=\sum_{i\in I}1=n\sum_{i\in I}(\cos\alpha_{i+1}-\cos\alpha_{i})=n(\cos\alpha_{k+1}-\cos\alpha_{j})\;,

where
j=\min I
and
k=\max I
. Since
\cos\alpha\in[k/n,(k+1)/n]
, the numbers
\cos\alpha
and
\cos\alpha_{k+1}
differ
by at most
1/n
. Similarly for
\cos\beta
and
\cos\alpha_{j}
. Thus by the above displayed bounds,

|C_{\alpha,\,\beta,\,\mathrm{v}}(n)|=n(\cos\alpha-\cos\beta)+O(1)\;,

which is the first stated estimate. For the other range of
\alpha
and
\beta
and for the second stated estimate for horizontal boxes we argue similarly.

\Box

Proposition 2.4

.

For any fixed
\varepsilon>0
,
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
with
0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq\pi/2
and all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have the estimate

|C_{\alpha,\,\beta}(n)|=[\sin t-\cos t]_{\alpha}^{\beta}\cdot n+E_{n}-\Delta_{n}\;,

where

E_{n}=O(1)\;\text{ and }\;0\leq\Delta_{n}\leq\min\big([\sin t-\cos t]_{\alpha}^{\beta}\cdot n+E_{n},\,O_{\varepsilon}(n^{\varepsilon})\big)\;.

In the other three quadrants of
C
this estimate holds with the appropriate signs of
\sin t
and
\cos t
,

+
if the function increases and
-
if it decreases.

Proof.
This follows from the previous proposition and Proposition
2.1
:

[\sin t-\cos t]_{\alpha}^{\beta}\cdot n+E_{n}
is the sum of estimates of the numbers of
vertical and horizontal boxes in
C_{\alpha,\,\beta}(n)
and
\Delta_{n}
is the number
of the boxes which are both vertical and horizontal (and hence are exceptional).

\Box

Corollary 2.5

.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
the cardinality of the approximate unit circle
C(n)
equals

|C(n)|=8n-4\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\,|\,n^{2}\\
d=2e+1\end{subarray}}(-1)^{e}\;.

Proof.
We count the boxes in
C(n)
more carefully:

\displaystyle|C(n)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)\cup C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)|=|C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)|+|C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)|-|C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)\cap C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle|C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)|+|C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)|-|C_{\mathrm{e}}(n)|\;,

where
C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)
(resp.
C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)
) is the set of vertical (resp. horizontal)
boxes in
C(n)
and
C_{\mathrm{e}}(n)
is the set of those
boxes in
C(n)
in which
C
enters (counter-clockwisely) in a vertex. Now
|C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)|=4n

because exactly two
boxes in
C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)
correspond to each of the
2n-1
vertical lines
x=i/n
with
i=-n+1,-n+2,\dots,n-1

(the point of entrance of
C

in the box lies on the line), only one box in
C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)
corresponds to each of the two vertical lines
x=-1
and
x=1
, and this
exhausts all boxes in
C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)
. By symmetry, also
|C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)|=4n
.
Clearly,
C_{\mathrm{v}}(n)\cap C_{\mathrm{h}}(n)=C_{\mathrm{e}}(n)
and
C_{\mathrm{e}}(n)
bijectively
corresponds to the solutions

4
Suggestions of a referee and concluding remarks

A referee, dismissive of the approach here, wrote the following.

(\dots)

“It is is easy to see that in the limit
n\to\infty
this gives rise to the following distribution
on the circle: with the usual parametrization
\varphi\mapsto e^{i\varphi}
,
\varphi\in[0,2\pi)

of the unit circle, the probability density function is

f(\varphi)=\frac{|\sin(\varphi)|+|\cos(\varphi)|}{8}.

In the limit problem, one needs to choose the two endpoints of the chord independently,
each according to this distribution. So the probability in question is

\int_{0}^{2\pi}\int_{2\pi/3}^{4\pi/3}f(x)f(x+y)\,\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y.

It is a straightforward (although not pleasent [sic]) task to compute this integral.”

(\dots)

“I did not check the author’s computations (that take up roughly 12 pages) but I am certain
that they are unnecessarily complicated and the result could be obtained in a few pages
(by proving a weak convergence for the discrete approximations and computing the above integral
for the limit problem).”

(\dots)

In the sentence “It is is easy to see that
\dots
” we should get a credit and Proposition
2.4

should be mentioned because the density
f(\varphi)
follows
from the asymptotics in Proposition
2.4
, not the other way around (as
is much more common in probabilistic computations). Were the values
of the arithmetic function
r_{2}(m)
— see the proof of Proposition
2.1
— not small
enough,
f(\varphi)
would be different, if it existed at all. The obvious bound
r_{2}(m)=O(m^{1/2})

does not suffice, nor even
r_{2}(m)=O(m^{1/4})
. In reality it is not
true that “It is is easy to see that
\dots
”.

MAPLE (2022 edition) cannot compute the above double integral in a closed form, instead it expresses it,
by computing the
inner integral, in the form

I:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}I(x)\,\mathrm{d}x

with a complicated integrand
I(x)
defined by considering various cases. More precisely, this result was only
obtained for some forms of the integrand
f(x)f(x+y)
. For other trivial variations, MAPLE could not compute
anything or was running inconclusively for one hour before it was terminated. It remains to be seen if this reflects some
inherent complexity of the integral or if it results from imperfection of algorithms in MAPLE.

Fortunately, MAPLE can
compute approximations of the integral,

I=0.33273773412864161039\dots\;.

These decimal digits agree with our value of
\mathrm{Pr_{Be,\Box}}
. We have therefore a non-rigorous
“proof” of the fact that

I=\frac{1+\sqrt{3}}{8}-\frac{\pi(2-\sqrt{3})}{96}\;.

To make it rigorous would mean (i) to prove “a weak convergence for the discrete approximations” or (ii) to
compute the double integral in closed form. We are less optimistic about the simplicity of a proof in (i) than the referee but this can be decided only by providing a detailed and rigorous proof in (i).
We thank the referee for the suggested alternative approach to asymptotics of sums counting long
chords or similar quantities.

Returning to our view of Bertrand’s paradox, we suggest to restate the problem and, especially, what it means
to solve it in the following terms. One should not be searching, like some analyst of old trying to find the true sum of the series
1-1+1-1+1-1+\dots
, for the true probability of long chords in the unit geometric circle.

Instead, to resolve Bertrand’s paradox, one should devise a framework that preserves
as much as possible from the spirit of the original intuitive problem (suppose we throw erratically, “randomly”
sticks on a circle drawn by chalk on the sidewalk, what is the chance that
\dots
) and at the same time makes the solution unique.

The approximation by boxes in this article is a possible solution of this metaproblem. Of course,
one cannot get rid of non-uniqueness completely, it is only shifted to the higher level.
