Reducing NEXP-complete problems to DQBF Thanks: To appear in the proceedings of FMCAD 2022.
Fa-Hsun Chen, Shen-Chang Huang, Yu-Cheng Lu, Tony Tan

Abstract
We present an alternative proof of the NEXP-hardness of the satisfiability of Dependency Quantified Boolean Formulas (DQBF). Besides being simple, our proof also gives us a general method to reduce NEXP-complete problems to DQBF. We demonstrate its utility by presenting explicit reductions from a wide variety of NEXP-complete problems to DQBF such as (succinctly represented) 3-colorability, Hamiltonian cycle, set packing and subset-sum as well as NEXP-complete logics such as the Bernays-Schönfinkel-Ramsey class, the two-variable logic and the monadic class. Our results show the vast applications of DQBF solvers which recently have gathered a lot of attention among researchers.

It is the second one that we view more interesting since it gives us a general method to reduce

The main idea is quite standard:
We encode the accepting runs of a non-deterministic Turing machine (with exponential run time)

.
We believe our technique can be easily modified for many other natural problems.

[
9
]

-complete problems.

We also present the reductions from well known

Recent work in

We hope that the technique introduced in this short paper

II
Preliminaries

Let
\Sigma=\{0,1\}
.
We usually use the symbol
\bar{a},\bar{b},\bar{c}
(possibly indexed) to denote a string in
\Sigma^{*}

with
|\bar{a}|
denoting the length of
\bar{a}
.
We use
\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z},\bar{u},\bar{v}
to denote vectors of boolean variables.
The length of
\bar{x}
is denoted by
|\bar{x}|
.
We write
C(\bar{u})
to denote a (boolean) circuit
C
with input gates
\bar{u}
.
When the input gates are not relevant or clear from the context,
we simply write
C
.
For
\bar{a}\in\Sigma^{|\bar{u}|}
,
C(\bar{a})
denotes the value of
C

when we assign the input gates
\bar{u}
with
\bar{a}
.
All logarithms have base
2
.

A
dependency quantified boolean formula
(DQBF) in prenex normal form is a formula of the form:

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle:=\forall x_{1}\cdots\ \forall x_{n}\ \exists y_{1}(\bar{z}_{1})\cdots\ \exists y_{m}(\bar{z}_{m})\quad\psi

(1)

where each
\bar{z}_{i}
is a vector of variables from
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}

and
\psi
, called
the matrix
, is a quantifier-free boolean formula using variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
.
The variables
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
are called
the universal variables
,

y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}

the existential variables

and each
\bar{z}_{i}

the dependency set
of
y_{i}
.

A DQBF
\Psi
in the form (
1
) is
satisfiable
,
if for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
,
there is a function
s_{i}:\Sigma^{|\bar{z}_{i}|}\to\Sigma
such that
by replacing each
y_{i}
with
s_{i}(\bar{z}_{i})
,
the formula
\psi
becomes a tautology.
The function
s_{i}
is called the
Skolem function
for
y_{i}
.
In this case, we also say that
\Psi
is satisfiable by the Skolem functions
s_{1},\ldots,s_{m}
.
The problem
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})
is defined as:
On input DQBF
\Psi
in the form (
1
),
decide if it is satisfiable.

Since many
NEXP
-complete problems use circuits as the succinct representations of the inputs,
we allow the matrix
\psi
to be in
circuit form
,
i.e.,
\psi
is given as a (boolean) circuit with input gates
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
.
This does not effect the generality of our results, since
every DQBF in circuit form can be converted to one in the standard formula form
as stated in Proposition
1
.

Proposition 1
.

Every DQBF
\Psi
in the form of (
1
) in circuit form
can be converted in polynomial time into an equisatisfiable DQBF formula
\Psi^{\prime}
whose matrix is in DNF.
Moreover,
\Psi
and
\Psi^{\prime}
have the same existential variables (with the same dependency set).

The proof is by standard Tseitin’s transformation
[
36
]
.
As an example, consider the following DQBF.

\displaystyle\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}\ \exists y_{1}(x_{1})\exists y_{2}(x_{2})\ \neg\big(x_{2}\vee(y_{1}\wedge x_{1}\wedge y_{2})\big)

It is equisatisfiable with the following DQBF.

\displaystyle\forall x_{1}\forall x_{2}\ \forall u_{1}\forall u_{2}\forall u_{3}\ \forall v_{1}\forall v_{2}\ \exists y_{1}(x_{1})\exists y_{2}(x_{2})

\displaystyle\left(\begin{array}[]{l}(v_{1}\leftrightarrow y_{1})\wedge(v_{2}\leftrightarrow y_{2})\wedge(u_{1}\leftrightarrow v_{1}\wedge x_{1}\wedge v_{2})\\
\wedge(u_{2}\leftrightarrow x_{2}\vee u_{1})\wedge(u_{3}\leftrightarrow\neg u_{2})\end{array}\right)\to u_{3}

Intuitively, we use the extra variable
v_{1}
to represent the value
y_{1}
,

v_{2}
the value
y_{2}
,
u_{1}
the value
y_{1}\wedge x_{1}\wedge y_{2}
,

u_{2}
the value
x_{2}\vee(y_{1}\wedge x_{1}\wedge y_{2})

and
u_{3}
the value
\neg(x_{2}\vee(y_{1}\wedge x_{1}\wedge y_{2}))
.
Note that the matrix can be easily rewritten into DNF.

NEXP
-completeness of
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})

In this section we present two new proofs
that
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})
is
NEXP
-complete, originally proved in
[
9
]
.

Theorem 2
.

[
9
]

\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})
is
NEXP
-complete.

Note that the membership is straightforward.
So we will focus only on the hardness.

III-A

The first proof: Reduction from succinct 3-colorability

The reduction is from the problem
graph 3-colorability

where the input graphs are given in a succinct form
[
24
]
.
A (boolean) circuit
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
, where
|\bar{u}|=|\bar{v}|=n
, represents a graph
G(C)=(V,E)
where
V=\Sigma^{n}

and
(\bar{a},\bar{b})\in E
iff
C(\bar{a},\bar{b})=1
.
The problem
succinct 3-colorability
is defined as:
On input circuit
C
, decide if
G(C)
is 3-colorable.
This problem is
NEXP
-complete
[
27
]
.

The reduction to
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})
is as follows.
Let
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
be the input circuit, where
|\bar{u}|=|\bar{v}|=n
.
We represent a 3-coloring of
G(C)
as a function
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\{01,10,11\}

which can be encoded by the following DQBF.

\displaystyle\Psi:=

\displaystyle\forall\bar{x}_{1}\forall\bar{x}_{2}\ \exists y_{1}(\bar{x}_{1})\exists y_{2}(\bar{x}_{1})\ \exists y_{3}(\bar{x}_{2})\exists y_{4}(\bar{x}_{2})

\displaystyle\hskip 35.56593pt\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}\ \to\ (y_{1},y_{2})=(y_{3},y_{4})

(2)

\displaystyle\hskip 21.33955pt\wedge\ (y_{1},y_{2})\neq(0,0)\ \wedge\ (y_{3},y_{4})\neq(0,0)

(3)

\displaystyle\hskip 21.33955pt\wedge\ C(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{x}_{2})=1\ \to\ (y_{1},y_{2})\neq(y_{3},y_{4})

(4)

Intuitively, we use
y_{1},y_{2}
and
y_{3},y_{4}
to represent the first and the second bits of the image
g(\bar{x}_{1})
and
g(\bar{x}_{2})
, respectively.
Lines (2) and (3) state that
(y_{1},y_{2})
and
(y_{3},y_{4})
must represent the same function from
\Sigma^{n}
to
\Sigma^{2}

and that their images do not inclue
00
.
Line (4) states that the colors of two adjacent vertices must be different.
Thus,
G(C)
is 3-colorable iff
\Psi
is satisfiable.

III-B

The second proof: Reduction via succinct projections

Our second proof uses the notion of
succinct projection
.
We need some terminology.
Let
C(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{v}_{1},\bar{u}_{2},\bar{v}_{2})
be a circuit with input gates
\bar{u}_{1},\bar{v}_{1},\bar{u}_{2},\bar{v}_{2}

where
|\bar{u}_{1}|=|\bar{u}_{2}|=n
and
|\bar{v}_{1}|=|\bar{v}_{2}|=m
.
We say that a function
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{m}

agrees
with the circuit
C
,
if
C(w_{1},g(w_{1}),w_{2},g(w_{2}))=1
, for every
w_{1},w_{2}\in\Sigma^{n}
.
In this case, we also say that the circuit
C

describes
the function
g
.
In the following whenever we say that a function
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{m}
agrees with
C(\bar{u}_{1},\bar{v}_{1},\bar{u}_{2},\bar{v}_{2})
,
we implicitly assume that
n=|\bar{u}_{1}|=|\bar{u}_{2}|
and
m=|\bar{v}_{1}|=|\bar{v}_{2}|
.

Definition 3
.

A succinct projection for a language
L
is a polynomial time deterministic algorithm
\mathcal{M}

such that on input
w\in\Sigma^{*}
,
\mathcal{M}
outputs a circuit
C
such that

w\in L
iff there is a function
g
that agrees with
C
.

Intuitively, we can view the function
g
as the certificate for the membership of
w
in
L

and the circuit
C
as the succinct description of
g
.
Since succinct projection runs in polynomial time,
the output circuit can only have polynomially many gates.
The following theorem is a new characterization of languages in
NEXP
.

Theorem 4
.

A language
L\in\textsc{NEXP}
iff it has a succinct projection.

Proof.

(if) Suppose that
L
has a succinct projection.
Consider the following algorithm.
On input
w
, first use the succinct projection to construct the circuit
C
.
Then, guess a function
g
(of exponential size)
and verify that it agrees with
C
.
It is obvious that it runs in non-deterministic exponential time.
That it is correct follows from the definition of succinct projection.

(only if) It is essentially the Cook-Levin reduction disguised
in the form of function certificates.
We only sketch it here.
Let
L\in\textsc{NEXP}
and
M
be a 1-tape NTM that accepts
L
in time
2^{p(n)}

for some polynomial
p(n)
.
For a word
w\in L
of length
n
, its accepting run can be represented
as a function
g:\Sigma^{p(n)}\times\Sigma^{p(n)}\to\Sigma^{\ell}
,
where
g(i,j)
denotes the content of cell
i
in time
j
.
The tuples in the codomain
\Sigma^{\ell}
encode the states and the tape symbols of
M
.
To verify that
g
represents an accepting run,
it is sufficient to verify that for every
i_{1},j_{1},i_{2},j_{2}\in\Sigma^{p(n)}
,
the tuple
(i_{1},j_{1},g(i_{1},j_{1}),i_{2},j_{2},g(i_{2},j_{2}))
satisfies a certain property
P

which depends only on the input word
w
and the transitions of
M
.
The desired succinct projection constructs in polynomial time a circuit
C

describing this property
P
.
∎

The second proof of the
NEXP
-hardness of
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})
:
Let
L\in\textsc{NEXP}
.
The polynomial time (Karp) reduction from
L
to
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})

is described as Algorithm 1 below.

Algorithm 1: Reducing
L\in\textsc{NEXP}
to
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})

Input:

w\in\Sigma^{*}
.

1:
Run the succinct projection of
L
on
w
.

2:
Let
C(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}_{2})
be the output circuit where

|\bar{x}_{1}|=|\bar{x}_{2}|=n
,
|\bar{y}_{1}|=|\bar{y}_{2}|=m
,
\bar{y}_{1}=(y_{1,1},\ldots,y_{1,m})

and
\bar{y}_{2}=(y_{2,1},\ldots,y_{2,m})
.

3:
Output the following DQBF
\Psi
:

\forall\bar{x}_{1}\forall\bar{x}_{2}\ \exists y_{1,1}(\bar{x}_{1})\cdots\exists y_{1,m}(\bar{x}_{1})\ \exists y_{2,1}(\bar{x}_{2})\cdots\exists y_{2,m}(\bar{x}_{2})

\qquad\;\;\;\ C(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}_{2})\ \wedge\ \big(\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}\to\bar{y}_{1}=\bar{y}_{2}\big)

We show
w\in L
iff
\Psi
is satisfiable.
Suppose
w\in L
.
Let
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{m}
be a function that agrees with
C
.
For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
, define the Skolem function
s_{i}:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma

where
s_{i}(\bar{a})
is the
i
-th component of
g(\bar{a})
, for every
\bar{a}\in\Sigma^{n}
.
It is routine to verify that
\Psi
is satisfiable
with each
s_{i}
being the Skolem function for
y_{1,i}
and
y_{2,i}
.

Conversely, suppose
\Psi
is satisfiable.
Let
s_{j,i}:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma
be the Skolem function for
y_{j,i}
,
where
1\leqslant j\leqslant 2
and
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
.
Since
\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}\ \to\ \bar{y}_{1}=\bar{y}_{2}
,
the functions
s_{1,i}
and
s_{2,i}
must be the same, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant m
.
Define
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{m}
where
g(\bar{a})=(s_{1}(\bar{a}),\ldots,s_{1,m}(\bar{a}))

for every
\bar{a}\in\Sigma^{n}
.
Since
C(\bar{a}_{1},g(\bar{a}_{1}),\bar{a}_{2},g(\bar{a}_{2}))
is true for every
\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2}
,
the function
g
agrees with
C
.
That is, there is a function that agrees with
C
.
Hence,
w\in L
.
This completes the second proof.

Remark 5
.

Observe that when Theorem
4
is applied to languages in
NP
,
the accepting run of a non-deterministic Turing machine with polynomial run time
p(n)

is represented as a function
g:\Sigma^{\log p(n)}\times\Sigma^{\log p(n)}\to\Sigma^{\ell}

and the succinct projection outputs a circuit
C(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}_{2})

where
|\bar{x}_{1}|=|\bar{x}_{2}|=\log p(n)
and
|\bar{y}_{1}|=|\bar{y}_{2}|=\ell
.
Thus, for
L\in\textsc{NP}
,
the DQBF output by Algorithm 1 has
4\log p(n)
universal variables and
2\ell
existential variables.

IV

Some concrete reductions

In this section we show how to utilize succinct projection
to obtain the reductions from concrete
NEXP
-complete problems to
\textsf{\small SAT}(\text{DQBF})
.
These are (succinct) Hamiltonian cycle, set packing and subset sum
[
27
]
.
We use the notion of succinctness from
[
24
]

which has been explained in Sect.
III-A
.
By Algorithm 1, it suffices to present only the succinct projections.

Some useful notations

For an integer
k\geqslant 1
,
[k]
denotes the set
\{0,\ldots,k-1\}
.
For
i\in[2^{n}]
,
\small\textsf{bin}_{n}(i)
is the binary representation of
i
in
n
bits.
The number represented by
\bar{a}\in\Sigma^{n}
is denoted by
\small\textsf{num}(\bar{a})
.
For
\bar{a},\bar{b}\in\Sigma^{n}
,
if
\small\textsf{num}(\bar{a})=\small\textsf{num}(\bar{b})+1\pmod{2^{n}}
,
we say that
\bar{a}
is the successor of
\bar{b}
, denoted by
\bar{a}=\bar{b}+1
.
Note that successor is applied only on two strings with the same length
and the successor of
1^{n}
is
0^{n}
.
It is not difficult to construct a circuit
C(\bar{x},\bar{y})
(in time polynomial in
|\bar{x}|+|\bar{y}|
)
such that
C(\bar{a},\bar{b})=1
iff
\bar{a}=\bar{b}+1
.

Reduction from succinct Hamiltonian cycle

Succinct Hamiltonian cycle is defined as follows.
The input is a circuit
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
.
The task is to decide if there is a Hamiltonian cycle in
G(C)
.

Let
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
be the input circuit where
|\bar{u}|=|\bar{v}|=n
.
We use a function
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{n}

to represent a Hamiltonian cycle
(\bar{b}_{0},\ldots,\bar{b}_{2^{n}-1})
where
g(\small\textsf{bin}_{n}(i))=\bar{b}_{i}
, for every
i\in[2^{n}]
.
To correctly represent a Hamiltonian cycle,
the following must hold for every
\bar{a}_{1},\bar{a}_{2}\in\Sigma^{n}
.

(H1)

If
\bar{a}_{1}\neq\bar{a}_{2}
, then
g(\bar{a}_{1})\neq g(\bar{a}_{2})
.

(H2)

If
\bar{a}_{2}=\bar{a}_{1}+1
, then
(g(\bar{a}_{1}),g(\bar{a}_{2}))
is an edge in
G(C)
.

The succinct projection for succinct Hamiltonian cycle simply outputs the
circuit that expresses (H1) and (H2), i.e.,
it outputs the following circuit
D(\bar{x}_{1},\bar{y}_{1},\bar{x}_{2},\bar{y}_{2})
where
|\bar{x}_{1}|=|\bar{x}_{2}|=|\bar{y}_{1}|=|\bar{y}_{2}|=n
:

\displaystyle\big(\bar{x}_{1}\neq\bar{x}_{2}\to\bar{y}_{1}\neq\bar{y}_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\wedge\big(\bar{x}_{2}=\bar{x}_{1}+1\to C(\bar{y}_{1},\bar{y}_{2})=1\big)

Obviously, a function
g:\Sigma^{n}\to\Sigma^{n}
represents a hamiltonian cycle in
G(C)
iff it agrees with
D
.

Reduction from succinct set packing

In the standard representation the problem set packing is defined as follows.
The input is a collection
\mathcal{K}
of finite sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{\ell}\subseteq\Sigma^{m}
and an integer
k
.
The task is to decide whether
\mathcal{K}
contains
k
mutually disjoint sets.
We assume each
S_{i}
has a “name” which is a string in
\Sigma^{\log\ell}
.

The succinct representation of the sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{\ell}
is a circuit
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
where
|\bar{u}|=m
and
|\bar{v}|=\log\ell
.
A string
\bar{a}\in\Sigma^{m}
is in the set
S_{\bar{b}}
, if
C(\bar{a},\bar{b})=1
.
We denote by
\mathcal{K}(C)
the collection of finite sets defined by the circuit
C
.
The problem succinct set packing is defined analogously where the input is the circuit
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
and an integer
k
(in binary).

We now describe its succinct projection.
Let
C(\bar{u},\bar{v})
and
k
be the input where
|\bar{u}|=m
and
|\bar{v}|=n
.
We first assume that
k
is a power of
2
.
We represent
k
disjoint sets
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
in
\mathcal{K}(C)
as a function
g:\Sigma^{\log k}\times\Sigma^{m}\to\Sigma^{n}

where
g(\small\textsf{bin}(i),\bar{a})
is the name of the set
S_{i}
.
Note that the string
\bar{a}
is actually ignored in the definition of
g
.

For a function
g:\Sigma^{\log k}\times\Sigma^{m}\to\Sigma^{n}
to correctly represent
k
disjoint sets,
the following must hold for every
(\bar{a}_{1},\bar{b}_{1}),(\bar{a}_{2},\bar{b}_{2})\in\Sigma^{\log k}\times\Sigma^{m}
.

(P1)

If
\bar{a}_{1}=\bar{a}_{2}
, then
g(\bar{a}_{1},\bar{b}_{1})=g(\bar{a}_{2},\bar{b}_{2})
.
That is, the function
g
does not depend on
\bar{b}_{1}
and
\bar{b}_{2}
.

(P2)

If
\bar{a}_{1}\neq\bar{a}_{2}
and
\bar{b}_{1}=\bar{b}_{2}
, then

\displaystyle\big(\bar{x}_{1}=\bar{x}_{2}\to\bar{z}_{1}=\bar{z}_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\wedge

\displaystyle\big(\bar{x}_{1}\neq\bar{x}_{2}\wedge\bar{y}_{1}=\bar{y}_{2}\big)\to\neg\big(C(\bar{y}_{1},\bar{z}_{1})=C(\bar{y}_{1},\bar{z}_{2})=1\big)

T_{X,\bar{b}}:

It is not difficult to design a succinct projection

In this section we will consider the following fragments of relational first-order logic (with the equality predicate):

•

The Bernays-Schönfinkel-Ramsey (BSR) class:
The class of relational

\displaystyle\Psi_{1}

\displaystyle:=\exists x_{1}\cdots\exists x_{m}\ \forall y_{1}\cdots\forall y_{n}\ \psi

The two-variable logic (

The classic result by Scott

•

The Löwenheim/monadic class:
The class of relational

We denote by

(BSR)

and

-complete

,
29
,
30
,
31
,

]
.
The upper bound is usually established by the so called Exponential Size Model (ESM) property
stated as follows.

•

If the

is satisfiable,
then it is satisfiable by a model with size at most

[
31

•

If the
\small\textsf{FO}^{2}

m2^{n}
,

The main idea of the reduction is quite simple.
We will represent the domain of a model with size at most

as a subset of
\Sigma^{t}

and use a function
f_{0}:\Sigma^{t}\to\Sigma
as the indicator
whether an element is in the domain.
Every predicate in the input formula
can be represented as a function

where

is the arity of the predicate.
All these functions can then be encoded appropriately as existential variables in DQBF.
Note that the universal

\forall\bar{u}\ f_{0}(\bar{u})\to\cdots
.
The existential

can then be encoded as existential variables in DQBF.

The rest of this section is organized as follows.
For technical convenience, we first introduce the logic Existential Second-order Quantified Boolean Formula (
\exists\text{SOQBF}
)
– an alternative, but equivalent formalism of DQBF.
The only difference between
\exists\text{SOQBF}
and DQBF is the syntax in declaring the function symbol.
Then, we consider the problem that we call Bounded

\textsf{\small Bnd-SAT}(\small\textsf{FO})

The logic
\exists\text{SOQBF}

The class
\exists\text{SOQBF}
is the extension of QBF formulas extended with existential second-order quantifiers.
That is,
\exists\text{SOQBF}
consists of formulas of the form:

\displaystyle\Psi

\displaystyle:=\exists f_{1}\exists f_{2}\cdots\exists f_{p}\ Q_{1}v_{1}\cdots\ Q_{n}v_{n}\quad\psi

and each

is a boolean function symbol associated with a fixed arity

.
The formula

We call each

The semantics of

It is not difficult to see that DQBF and

Bounded

Reduction from

Let

We may assume that

Adding redundant quantifier, if necessary, we may assume that

is

Then, we Skolemize each existential quantifier as follows.
Let

We rewrite

\displaystyle\varphi^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\forall x_{1}\cdots\forall x_{i-1}\ Q_{i+1}x_{i+1}\cdots Q_{n}x_{n}\ \forall z

Hence,

\displaystyle\varphi

\displaystyle:=\forall x_{1}\cdots\forall x_{n}\ \psi

(5)

where
\psi
is quantifier-free formula where

In the following let

be the Skolem function symbols in

be the predicates in
\psi
.
Let
\text{ar}(g_{i})

and

Let

Construct the following

\displaystyle:=\exists f_{0}\ \exists f_{1,1}\cdots\exists f_{1,t}\cdots\exists f_{k,1}\cdots\exists f_{k,t}\ \exists f_{P_{1}}\cdots\exists f_{P_{\ell}}

The arity of

•

Proof of Proposition
1

The proof essentially uses the same idea as Tseitin’s transformation for the quantifier free boolean formulas
[
36
]
.
Let
\Psi
be a DQBF in the form (
1
) where
\psi
is in circuit form.
Let
\bar{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
.
Let
g_{1},\ldots,g_{k}
be the internal gates in
\psi
and let
g_{k}
be the output gate.
We will represent them with “fresh” boolean variables
\bar{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{k})
.
We will also have fresh variables
\bar{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{m})
to represent
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
.

Consider the following DQBF:

\displaystyle\Psi^{\prime}

\displaystyle:=\forall\bar{x}\ \forall\bar{u}\ \forall\bar{v}\ \exists y_{1}(\bar{z}_{1})\cdots\ \exists y_{m}(\bar{z}_{m})\Big(\Big(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}v_{i}\leftrightarrow y_{i}\Big)\quad\wedge\quad\Big(\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}\phi_{i}\Big)\Big)\ \to\ u_{k}

Intuitively, each
\phi_{i}
states that “the value
u_{i}
is the value of gate
g_{i}
.”
More formally, if the gate
g_{i}
is an OR-gate with inputs
g_{j_{1}},\ldots,g_{j_{t}}
,
then
\phi_{i}:=u_{i}\leftrightarrow(u_{j_{1}}\vee\cdots\vee u_{j_{t}})
.
If some
g_{j_{l}}
is an input gate
x_{h}
, then replace
u_{j_{l}}
with
x_{h}
.
If it is an input gate
y_{h}
, then replace
u_{j_{l}}
with
v_{h}
.
Similarly, if
g_{i}
is an AND-gate with inputs
g_{j_{1}},\ldots,g_{j_{t}}
,
then
\phi_{i}:=u_{i}\leftrightarrow(u_{j_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge u_{j_{t}})
.
If
g_{i}
is a NOT-gate with input
g_{j}
,
then
\phi_{i}:=u_{i}\leftrightarrow\neg u_{j}
.
It is routine to verify that
\Psi
and
\Psi^{\prime}
are equisatisfiable.

Each
\phi_{i}
can be rewritten in CNF.
Thus, the matrix can be rewritten in DNF as follows.

\displaystyle\bigvee_{i=1}^{m}(v_{i}\wedge\neg y_{i})\vee(\neg v_{i}\wedge y_{i})\quad\vee\quad\bigvee_{i=1}^{k}\neg\phi_{i}\ \vee\ u_{k}

Note that each
\phi_{i}
uses only variables from
\bar{x}
,
\bar{u}
and
\bar{v}
.
Thus, the only terms that use the existential variables are
v_{i}\wedge\neg y_{i}
or
\neg v_{i}\wedge y_{i}

which contains at most one existential variables.
This completes the proof of Proposition
1
.

(c)

\bar{a}_{2}

\bar{a}_{2}

\bar{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})

We construct a BSR sentence of the form:

\displaystyle\Phi

\displaystyle:=

\displaystyle\exists x_{0}\exists x_{1}\forall y_{1}\cdots\forall y_{n}\quad x_{0}\neq x_{1}\ \wedge\ \bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\big(y_{i}=x_{0}\ \vee\ y_{i}=x_{1}\big)\quad\wedge\quad\varphi

\varphi

\psi

u_{i}
with
y_{i}=x_{1}
, each
\neg u_{i}

y_{i}=x_{0}

f(\bar{z})
with
P(\bar{z}[u_{1}/y_{1},\ldots,u_{n}/y_{n}])

u_{i}
is replaced with
y_{i}
.

\bar{z}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}

f(\bar{z})
.

F
that makes

true

We will present a succinct projection for
\textsf{\small SAT}(\exists\text{SOQBF})
.
Let
\Psi:=\exists f\forall\bar{u}\ \psi
be an
\exists\text{SOQBF}
formula,
where
\bar{u}=(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})
and
\text{ar}(f)=k

Let there be
m
function variable
f(\bar{z}_{1}),\ldots,f(\bar{z}_{m})
in
\psi
.

We first introduce a few notations.
For a vector of variables
\bar{z}
where
\bar{z}\subseteq\{u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}\}
,
and
w=b_{1}\cdots b_{n}\in\Sigma^{n}
, we write
\bar{z}[\bar{u}/w]
to denote the string of length
|\bar{z}|

obtained by replacing each
u_{i}
with
b_{i}
.
Analogously, for a vector
\bar{v}=(v_{1},\ldots,v_{n})
, we write
\bar{z}[\bar{u}/\bar{v}]

u_{i}
with

For a function

is the string

when the variables
\bar{u}

We now describe the succinct projection for
\textsf{\small SAT}(\exists\text{SOQBF})

It constructs the following circuit
