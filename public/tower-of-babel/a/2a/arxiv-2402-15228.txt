Convolutions and Mixtures of Gamma, Stable and Mittag-Leffler Distributions
Nomvelo Karabo Sibisi sbsnom005@myuct.ac.za

Abstract
This paper uses convolutions of the gamma density and the one-sided stable density to construct higher level densities. The approach is applied to constructing a 4-parameter Mittag-Leffler density, whose Laplace transform is a corresponding Mittag-Leffler function, which is completely monotone (CM) by construction. Laplace transforms of mixtures of the stable densities with respect to the 4-parameter Mittag-Leffler distribution are compositions of the Mittag-Leffler functions with Bernstein functions, thereby generating a rich family of CM variants of the base CM Mittag-Leffler functions, including known instances as special cases.



Keywords—

Gamma, beta, stable, Mittag-Leffler, positive Linnik distributions;
Mittag-Leffler

functions; convolution, mixing; Bernstein functions;
complete monotonicity.

1
Introduction

The goal of this paper is to use convolutions and mixtures involving the familiar gamma and one-sided stable distributions
to construct higher level distributions.
We apply this approach to constructing Mittag-Leffler distributions,
whose Laplace-Stieltjes transforms are Mittag-Leffler functions in various guises.
This is a ‘forward problem’ that involves constructing distributions and evaluating their transforms,
as opposed to an ‘inverse problem’ that seeks to infer densities from given functions through complex analytic inversion
(Laplace or Stieltjes inversion, depending on the transform linking the desired density to the function at hand).
Such inversion cannot, by itself, ensure that the result will be nonnegative,
supplementary conditions typically need to be imposed.
Common though inversion methods may be, our primary objective strictly remains one of direct construction of distributions.
In any event, Mittag-Leffler distributions have applications in their own right to model, for example, random partitions of the integers
or the growth of random trees and graphs.
In such context, their property as generators of Mittag-Leffler functions through Laplace-Stieltjes transforms may only be of passing interest.

It is well-known that the Mittag-Leffler function
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\alpha})
for
0<\alpha<1
is the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of a
distribution with a straightforward density
(equivalently,
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\alpha})
is the ordinary Laplace transform of said density).
It is also known that
E_{\alpha}(-x)
for
0<\alpha<1
is the Laplace-Stieltjes transform
of a distribution known as the Mittag-Leffler distribution,
which has a known density too. The primary question is whether the relationship might also hold for some other power of of
x
(
\sigma
, say),
at least for the same restriction
0<\alpha<1
suggested by the two examples cited.
To be precise:

a) Is
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\sigma})
for certain
\sigma\notin\{1,\alpha\}
also the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of a distribution?

b) If (a) holds, what is the explicit form of the distribution associated with
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\sigma})
?

Part (a) of the question has a simple answer, which may be looked upon as arising from the concept of completely monotone functions.
A function is said to be completely monotone (CM) if it and all its derivatives of alternating sign are nonnegative.
By a theorem due to Bernstein, a function is CM if and only if it is the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of a distribution.

E_{\alpha}(-x)
and
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\alpha})
are thus examples of CM functions.
Furthermore, by a property of CM functions, if
E_{\alpha}(-x)
is CM so is the composition
E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\sigma})
for
\lambda>0
and

0<\sigma<1
. Hence we have the answer to (a): there exists a distribution whose Laplace-Stieltjes transform is
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\sigma})
for

0<\alpha<1
and
0<\sigma<1
.
This clearly includes the case
\sigma=\alpha
,
i.e.

E_{\alpha}(-x^{\alpha})
is CM because
E_{\alpha}(-x)
is CM.
It is also true that
E_{\alpha}(-(x^{\alpha})^{\sigma})
is CM because
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\alpha})
is CM, but this is narrower since
we cannot deduce that
E_{\alpha}(-x)
is CM by using the CM nature of
E_{\alpha}(-x^{\alpha})
as a starting point.

Part (b) seeks to go beyond the existence statement and determine the explicit distribution associated with
E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\sigma})
.
This is core to what this paper seeks to achieve: explicit construction of distributions that mirror corresponding CM functions.
In particular, since
E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\sigma})
is CM by virtue of the fact that
E_{\alpha}(-x)
is CM,
we shall establish that the distribution whose Laplace-Stieltjes transform is
E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\sigma})

arises from the Mittag-Leffler distribution, whose Laplace-Stieltjes transform is
E_{\alpha}(-x)
.
This is despite the fact that the well-known case
E_{\alpha}(-\lambda x^{\alpha})
has been studied directly and independently of the

E_{\alpha}(-x)
case in the literature.

1.1
Contribution

In this paper, convolutions and mixtures of gamma and stable distributions are the fundamental concepts that underpin all higher distributions.
As a starting point, we will use this methodology to construct the Mittag-Leffler distribution, which involves one parameter
0<\alpha<1

(we can include the ends of this interval with an extended definition of the stable density, but this is not a key conceptual point here).
It then becomes evident how to generalise to the construction of multi-parameter Mittag-Leffler distributions.

2.2.1
4-parameter Mittag-Leffler Distribution

Pursuing the approach of mixtures and convolutions of stable and gamma distributions,
this paper derives a 4-parameter Mittag-Leffler distribution

P_{\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\theta}\equiv{\rm ML(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\theta})

for
0<\alpha<1,\beta>\alpha\gamma,\theta>-\alpha\gamma
,
whose Laplace-Stieltjes transform is
\Gamma(\beta+\theta)E^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(-x)

(x\geq 0)
.
The difference
\beta-\alpha\gamma>0
plays an important role.
As we shall demonstrate, for
\beta-\alpha\gamma=1-\alpha
the convolution essentially simplifies to the
\alpha
-stable density (Proposition
3
below gives the precise statement).

{\rm ML(\alpha,\theta})\equiv{\rm ML(\alpha,1,1,\theta})
is one instance of
\beta-\alpha\gamma=1-\alpha
.

With the parameter restrictions stated,
E^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(-x)
is CM.
Hence
E^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(-\lambda x^{\sigma})

(\lambda>0,0<\sigma<1)
is also CM.
We derive the explicit form of the associated density,
a mixture of a
\sigma
-stable density with respect to
{\rm ML}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\theta)
,
with Laplace transform
\Gamma(\beta+\theta)E^{\gamma+\theta/\alpha}_{\alpha,\beta+\theta}(-\lambda x^{\sigma})
.
Through multiplication with other CM functions and iterative composition, we may generate a cascade of CM functions
as Laplace transforms of associated explicit densities.
We discuss the general construction principle but demonstrate only a limited selection of such possibilities.

2.3.2
Complex Analytic Inversion

There is a body of literature that places emphasis on exploring the CM character of Mittag-Leffler functions, with the fact that they are Laplace transforms
of nonnegative functions seeming to arise as a means to that end rather than as the primary objective.
Such literature might thus be described as more analytic than probabilistic.

Pollard
[
50
]
took the complex analytic Laplace inverse of the 1-parameter instance of (
2
) as the starting point
to establish the CM character of
E_{\alpha}(-x)
.
Inspired by this,
Górska
et al.

[
21
]
proved that
E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x)
is CM,
i.e.
that it is the Laplace transform of a nonnegative function
for
0<\alpha\leq 1,\beta>\alpha\gamma,\gamma>0

(also see Tomovski
et al.

[
61
, Theorem 2]
).
Górska
et al.

[
21
, Remark 2.2]
noted that
the CM nature of
E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x)
implies that:
(a)
E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x^{\alpha})
is CM by the composition property of CM functions and
(b)
x^{\beta-1}E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x^{\alpha})
is CM by the product property, with the additional constraint
\beta\leq 1
.

The CM character of (b) had previously been investigated by
Capelas de Oliveira
et al.

[
12
]
using Stieltjes inversion of the Laplace transform (
2
)
of
x^{\beta-1}E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x^{\alpha})
.
Stieltjes inversion by itself
(Titchmarsh
[
60
, 11.8 (p318)]
, Widder
[
62
, VIII.7 (p341)]
)
does not shed light on whether the inverse is a nonnegative function,
the paper determined the required parameter restrictions

0<\alpha\leq 1
,
0<\alpha\gamma\leq\beta\leq 1

using a theorem due to Gripenberg
[
22
]
.
Mainardi and Garrappa
[
38
]
pursued the same inversion approach, but with a variation on the argument
leading to the same parameter restrictions.

The condition
\beta\leq 1
arises from treating
x^{\beta-1}
and
E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x^{\alpha})

as separate CM functions, so that the product is also CM.
Amongst other things, we will show that
\beta\leq 1
is, in fact, redundant for
x^{\beta-1}E^{\gamma}_{\alpha,\beta}(-x^{\alpha})

to be the Laplace transform of a density,
i.e.
for it to be CM.

Preliminaries

Let

F(t)
,
t>0

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,dF(t)

(4)

If

is equivalently the (ordinary) Laplace transform of

\psi(x)

its derivatives of alternating sign are nonnegative:
(-1)^{n}\psi^{(n)}(x)\geq 0,n\geq 0

In fact, by a theorem due to Bernstein (Feller
[
13
, XIII.4]
, Widder
[
62

),
a function is CM if and only if it is of the form (
4
).
The topic of completely monotone functions is well-studied

[
13
, XIII.4]
, Schilling
et al.

[
55
]
, Widder
[
62

).
Properties of interest here are:

Proposition 1

(Product)
.

If
\psi_{1}(x)
and
\psi_{2}(x)
are
CM
, so is their product
\psi_{1}(x)\psi_{2}(x)
.

Proposition 2

(Composition)
.

If
\psi(x)
is
CM
, so is the composition
\psi(\eta(x))
where
\eta(x)
is a nonnegative function with a
CM
derivative,
also known as a Bernstein function.

We study properties of distributions corresponding to the properties above for CM functions.
The product case is straightforward.
If the CM functions
\psi_{1}(x),\psi_{2}(x)
are Laplace transforms of densities
f_{1}(t),f_{2}(t)
respectively,
then the convolution theorem states that the product
\psi_{1}(x)\psi_{2}(x)
is the Laplace transform of
the convolution density
\{f_{1}\star f_{2}\}(t)

\displaystyle\{f_{1}\star f_{2}\}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}f_{1}(t-u)f_{2}(u)\,du=\int_{0}^{t}f_{2}(t-u)f_{1}(u)\,du

The composition case is more subtle.
If the CM function

is the Laplace-Stieltes transform of a known distribution, what can be said about

in discussion of the Mittag-Leffler distribution.

Proposition
3
may be viewed as a particular instance of the representation of
infinitely divisible distributions on the nonnegative half-line
\mathbb{R}_{+}=[0,\infty)
, a class that includes the stable distribution,
Feller
[
13
, XIII.7]
, Steutel and van Harn
[

(also see Sato
[
54
]
). There is also an intimate link to the generalised gamma convolutions studied by Bondesson
[
10
]
.

We note that
\{\rho_{1-\alpha}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda\}(t)
may be regarded as the Riemann-Liouville fractional integral

\{I_{+}^{1-\alpha}\,f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda\}(t)
of the stable density
f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)
,
where
\{I_{+}^{\nu}\,f\}(t)
for a function
f(t)
(
t\geq 0,\nu>0
) is the convolution
\{\rho_{\nu}\star f\}(t)
,
\rho_{\nu}(t)=t^{\nu-1}/\Gamma(\nu)

(Kiryakova
[

]
, Miller and Ross
[
40
]
, Samko
et al.

[
53
]
).
We shall continue to use the general language of convolutions than that of fractional integrals.
We further note that Proposition
3
has inspired the study of generalised stable densities
f_{\alpha,m}(t)

defined by
t^{m}f_{\alpha,m}(t)=\alpha\{\rho_{1-\alpha}\star f_{\alpha,m}\}(t)
for

(Jedidi
et al.

[
32
]
, Pakes
[
44
]
, Schneider
[
56
]
).
We shall not have cause for such generalisation here.

The stable density
f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)
is only known in closed form for selected values of
\alpha
, the simplest being for
\alpha=1/2
.
For the general rational case
\alpha=l/k
for positive integers

with
k>l
, Penson and Górska
[
45
]
expressed

f_{\alpha}
in terms of the Meijer

(also see James
[

, 8]
).

Pollard
[
49
]
(also see Feller
[
13
, XVII.6]
) proved that
f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

has the infinite series representation
(with modification here to include conditioning on the scale factor
\lambda>0
)

\displaystyle f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,e^{-\lambda(e^{-i\pi}u)^{\alpha}}\,du

(16)

\displaystyle=\phantom{-}\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}e^{-i\pi\alpha k}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,u^{\alpha k}\,du

(17)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\sin(\pi\alpha k)\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}{t^{\alpha k+1}}

(18)

We may equivalently sum from

since the
k=0
term is identically 0.

4
Main Contribution

Theorem 1
.

Let
\psi(x),x\geq 0
be the Laplace-Stieltjes transform of a distribution
F(t)
,
t>0

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,dF(t)\hskip 9.24994ptx\geq 0

(19)

Then, for
0<\alpha<1
and
\lambda>0
, the composition
\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha})
is the Laplace transform of a mixture density
w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)\,dt

(20)

\displaystyle\textrm{where}\hskip 9.24994ptw_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,dF(u)

(21)

f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)
being the
\alpha
-stable density conditioned on the scale factor
\lambda u
.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

e^{-\lambda ux^{\alpha}}
(
0<\alpha<1
) is
the Laplace transform of the stable density
f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda u)
.
Hence

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha})=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}u}\,dF(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)dt\,dF(u)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,dF(u)\right]\,dt

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)\,dt

where
w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)
is the integral in square braces, thereby proving (
21
).
∎

For clarity, we may explicitly condition on
F
or its density
f
:

e.g.

w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)\to w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,F)\equiv w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,f)
.
Equivalently, we may condition on any parameters that are known to characterise
F
uniquely,

e.g.

w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,\gamma,y)
where
\gamma,y
are understood to be the shape and scale parameters of the gamma distribution
G(\gamma,y)
.

Theorem
1
states a known property that can be couched in the language of subordination,
as described by Feller
[
13
, XIII.7]
.
Of more direct interest here are the associated corollaries below.

Corollary 1.1
.

Let
\rho_{\nu}(t)=t^{\nu-1}/\Gamma(\nu)

(\nu>0)
and let
f(t)
be the density of
F(t)
.
Then the following holds

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star w_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda,F)\}(t)

\displaystyle\equiv\int_{0}^{\infty}\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda u)\}(t)\,dF(u)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\,w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,\rho_{\nu/\alpha}\star f)

(22)

\displaystyle{\rm where}\hskip 9.24994ptw_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,\rho_{\nu/\alpha}\star f)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,\{\rho_{\nu/\alpha}\star f\}(u)du

Proof of Corollary
1.1
.

By the convolution theorem, the Laplace transform of the convolution density
\{\rho_{\nu}\star w_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda,F)\}(t)
is
the product
x^{-\nu}\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha}|F)=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\left[(\lambda x^{\alpha})^{-\nu/\alpha}\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha}|F)\right]
.
The product in square braces is also the Laplace transform of
w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,\{\rho_{\nu/\alpha}\star f\})
.
Hence the validity of (
22
) by uniqueness of Laplace transforms.
∎

The point of Corollary
1.1
is that it gives two different ways to express the
convolution of the power density with the mixture density
w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,F)
–
one involving convolution with the stable density and the other involving convolution with the density of
F
.
This will have a bearing on our approach to the derivation of the Mittag-Leffler distribution below.

Corollary 1.2
.

The mixture density
w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,F)
admits the representation

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,F)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,\psi(\lambda e^{-i\pi\alpha}u^{\alpha}|F)\,du

(23)

In particular, for the convolution
\rho_{\nu}\star f

(\nu>0)

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,\rho_{\nu}\star f)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,(\lambda e^{-i\pi\alpha}u^{\alpha})^{-\nu}\psi(\lambda e^{-i\pi\alpha}u^{\alpha}|f)\,du

(24)

Proof of Corollary
1.2
.

Using the Pollard representation (
16
) for
f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)
gives

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda,F)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,dF(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tv}\,e^{-\lambda u(e^{-i\pi}v)^{\alpha}}\,dv\,dF(u)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tv}\int_{0}^{\infty}\,e^{-\lambda u(e^{-i\pi}v)^{\alpha}}\,dF(u)\,dv

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tv}\psi(\lambda e^{-i\pi\alpha}v^{\alpha}|F)\,dv

thereby proving (
23
).
The convolution theorem for
dF(u)=\{\rho_{\nu}\star f\}(u)du
leads to (
24
).
∎

Corollary 1.3
.

Let
F(t)
have finite moments
\{M_{k}\}
of all order

\displaystyle M_{k}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{k}\,dF(u)\equiv(-1)^{k}\psi^{(k)}(0)<\infty\hskip 18.49988ptk\geq 0

(25)

Then the mixture density
w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)
has the infinite series representation

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,dF(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\sin(\pi\alpha k)\,M_{k}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tv}\,v^{\alpha k}\,dv

(26)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\sin(\pi\alpha k)\,M_{k}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha k+1)}{t^{\alpha k+1}}

(27)

Proof of Corollary
1.3
.

Using the infinite series representation (
17
) of the stable density
f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)
in (
21
) gives

\displaystyle w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda u)^{k}}{k!}\sin(\pi\alpha k)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tv}\,v^{\alpha k}\,dv\right]dF(u)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\sin(\pi\alpha k)\,\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tv}\,v^{\alpha k}\,dv\right]\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{k}\,dF(u)\right]

The rightmost term is
M_{k}
, thereby proving (
26
) and hence (
27
).
∎

5
Unit Step
F

Let
F(t)
be 0 on

F(t)=\mathbbm{1}_{[1,\infty)}
,

\mathbbm{1}_{[c,\infty)}

[c,\infty)
.
The density of

(Laplace transform of

\displaystyle\psi(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,dF(t)=e^{-x}\implies\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha})=e^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}}\;(\lambda>0)

For

, Theorem
1
states that

is the Laplace transform of a mixture density

\displaystyle\psi(\lambda x^{\alpha})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}\,w_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)\,dt=e^{-\lambda x^{\alpha}}\hskip 9.24994pt0<\alpha<1

\displaystyle\textrm{where}\hskip 9.24994ptw_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,dF(u)=f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)

For

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star f\}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{t}\rho_{\nu}(t-u)\,dF(u)=\rho_{\nu}(t-1)\mathbbm{1}_{[1,\infty)}

with Laplace transform

.

Theorem 2
.

For
0<\alpha<1
,
\rho_{\nu}(t)=t^{\nu-1}/\Gamma(\nu)

(\nu>0)
,
\rho_{0}(t)=\delta(t)
and

the convolution density
\{\rho_{\nu}\star w_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)\equiv\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)

has the following equivalent forms

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,\{\rho_{\nu/\alpha}\star f\}(u)du

(32)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\rho_{\nu/\alpha}(u-1)\mathbbm{1}_{[1,\infty)}\,du

(33)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\;e^{i\pi\nu}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,u^{-\nu}\,e^{-\lambda(e^{-i\pi}u)^{\alpha}}\,du

(34)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\sin\pi(\nu-\alpha k)\frac{\Gamma(\alpha k-\nu+1)}{t^{\alpha k-\nu+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\frac{t^{\nu-\alpha k-1}}{\Gamma(\nu-\alpha k)}\equiv\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\rho_{\nu-\alpha k}(t)

\nu=0

f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)\equiv\{\rho_{0}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda\}(t)

Remark 1
.

(\ref{eq:rhoconvstableinfsum1})
may also be written as

\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)=t^{\nu-1}\phi(-\alpha,\nu,-\lambda t^{-\alpha})
,
where
\phi
is the Wright function that finds much application in fractional calculus.
Its definition as an entire function for

is (e.g. Gorenflo et al.
[

)

\displaystyle\phi(\alpha,\beta,z)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^{k}}{k!\Gamma(\alpha k+\beta)}\hskip 18.49988pt\alpha>-1,\beta\in\mathbb{C}

(37)

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Corollary
1.1
gives (
32
).
Substituting (
31
) in (
32
) gives (
33
).
Given that the Laplace transform of (
31
) is
x^{-\nu}\psi(x)=x^{-\nu}e^{-x}
,
Corollary
1.2
gives (

). Explicitly

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda\}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\rho_{\nu/\alpha}(u-1)\mathbbm{1}_{[1,\infty)}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}\,(e^{-i\pi\alpha}u^{\alpha})^{-\nu/\alpha}\,e^{-\lambda(e^{-i\pi}u)^{\alpha}}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\,{\rm Im}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}e^{i\pi(\nu-\alpha k)}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-tu}u^{\alpha k-\nu}\,du

The integral may be evaluated for
\alpha k-\nu+1>0
, which only holds for all

if
\nu<1
, to give

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\sin\pi(\nu-\alpha k)\frac{\Gamma(\alpha k-\nu+1)}{t^{\alpha k-\nu+1}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-\lambda)^{k}}{k!}\frac{t^{\nu-\alpha k-1}}{\Gamma(\nu-\alpha k)}

The last step follows from the Euler reflection formula

, where

is an entire function for

Hence

is well-defined for

imposed to evaluate the intermediate integral.
∎

This shows that the convolution density
\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)
may be replaced by a mixture involving the simple convolution between
\rho_{\nu/\alpha}
and a
\delta
density.
In turn, the mixture may optionally be represented as an infinite sum of (analytically continued) copies

\{\rho_{\nu-\alpha k}(t)\}
of the power density
\rho_{\nu}(t)
.

\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)
is the basic building block for all other densities discussed in the balance of this paper.
In the case of
\nu=1-\alpha

3

\{\rho_{1-\alpha}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)=tf_{\alpha}(t|\lambda)/\lambda\alpha
.

In addition to the forms

,

has an equivalent representation involving the beta distribution.

Corollary 2.1
.

The convolution density
\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)
can be written as

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}}{\Gamma(\nu/\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda/u)\,u^{-\nu/\alpha}(1-u)^{\nu/\alpha-1}\,\frac{du}{u}

(38)

\displaystyle\equiv\Gamma\left(1-\tfrac{\nu}{\alpha}\right)\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda/u)\,{\rm beta}\left(u|1-\tfrac{\nu}{\alpha},\tfrac{\nu}{\alpha}\right)\frac{du}{u}

This has the form of (

We shall pursue this fundamental result further later.
It suffices to note for now that there are various ways to represent the convolution
\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)
.

Proof of Corollary
2.1
.

By (

)

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|\lambda)\}(t)

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\int_{1}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\rho_{\nu/\alpha}(u-1)\,du

\displaystyle=\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda/u)\rho_{\nu/\alpha}(1/u-1)\,\frac{du}{u^{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\nu/\alpha}}{\Gamma(\nu/\alpha)}\int_{0}^{1}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda/u)\,u^{-\nu/\alpha}(1-u)^{\nu/\alpha-1}\,\frac{du}{u}

thereby proving (
38
), hence (
39
).
∎

For
\lambda=1
, (
38
) is equivalent to Ho
et al.

[

)]
,
derived by different reasoning (with the convolution

10.1
Convolutions and Special Functions

We have seen that the Mittag-Leffler distribution involves the convolution
\{\rho_{\beta-\alpha\gamma}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(1)
.
By Theorem
2
, this may be expressed as

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\beta-\alpha\gamma}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(1)

\displaystyle=t^{\beta/\alpha-\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(1|tu)\rho_{\beta/\alpha-\gamma}(u-1)\mathbbm{1}_{[1,\infty)}\,du

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^{k}}{k!}\frac{1}{\Gamma(\beta-\alpha\gamma-\alpha k)}

The representation (
18
) of the stable density leads to an alternative infinite sum representation

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\beta-\alpha\gamma}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^{k}}{k!}\sin\pi\alpha k\int_{0}^{\infty}\{\rho_{\beta-\alpha\gamma}\star\rho_{1,u}\}(1)\,u^{\alpha k-1}\,du

(122)

The observation of interest here is that
\{\rho_{\beta/\alpha-\gamma}\star\rho_{1,u}\}(1)
is a
convolution of 2 gamma densities with different scale factors
0
and
u
.
More generally, the gamma distribution has the well-known property that it is closed under convolution of densities sharing a common scale parameter,

i.e.

\{\rho_{\nu,\lambda}\star\rho_{\mu,\lambda}\}(t)=\rho_{\nu+\mu,\lambda}(t)

with scale parameter
\lambda
throughout.
The case of different scale parameters is not as simple. In particular

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\nu}\star\rho_{\mu,\lambda}\}(1)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\rho_{\nu}(1-u)\rho_{\mu,\lambda}(u)du

(123)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\mu}}{\Gamma(\nu)\Gamma(\mu)}\int_{0}^{1}(1-u)^{\nu-1}\,u^{\mu-1}e^{-\lambda u}\,du

(124)

\displaystyle=\frac{\lambda^{\mu}}{\Gamma(\mu+\nu)}\int_{0}^{1}{\rm beta}(u|\mu,\nu)\,e^{-\lambda u}\,du

(125)

We recognise the integral in (
125
) as the Laplace transform of the beta density, also denoted by

M(\mu,\mu+\nu,-\lambda)
, the confluent hypergeometric function (Abramowitz and Stegun
[
1
, 13.2.1]
).
Hence, with
\nu=\beta-\alpha\gamma
and
\mu=1
, (
122
) may be written as

\displaystyle\{\rho_{\beta-\alpha\gamma}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(1)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{(-t)^{k}\sin\pi\alpha k}{k!\Gamma(\beta-\alpha\gamma+1)}\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha k}\,M(1,\beta-\alpha\gamma+1,-u)\,du

(126)

The integral may be looked upon as a Mellin transform of
uM(1,\beta-\alpha\gamma+1,-u)
defined by

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}u^{\alpha k-1}\,\{uM(1,\beta-\alpha\gamma+1,-u)\}\,du

While this illustrates the link between Mittag-Leffler distributions and special functions and Mellin transforms,
(
126
) does not in our view provide additional conceptual insight compared to the expressions
for the convolution
\{\rho_{\beta-\alpha\gamma}\star f_{\alpha}(\cdot|t)\}(1)

given by Theorem
2
.

11
Conclusion

We have used the convolution and mixing of gamma and stable densities to construct densities of higher level distributions.
The convolution between the power density and the stable density has been the centrepiece of the discussion.
We applied the methodology to the construction of Mittag-Leffler distributions, with a 4-parameter instance as the general case subsuming
published instances as special cases.
The Laplace transforms of the Mittag-Leffler densities are Mittag Leffler functions.
We have shown that the compositions of the latter with a power law Bernstein function can be constructed from corresponding mixture densities.

\Pr(t|x)=\delta(t-x^{-\alpha})
.
The marginal density
p_{\alpha}(t)\equiv\Pr(t)
of
T=t
is

\displaystyle p_{\alpha}(t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(t-x^{-\alpha})f_{\alpha}(x)\,dx

\displaystyle(x\to u^{-1/\alpha})\;

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(t-u)f_{\alpha}(u^{-1/\alpha})u^{-1/\alpha-1}\,du

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(t^{-1/\alpha})\,t^{-1/\alpha-1}

which is the density of the Mittag-Leffler distribution
P_{\alpha}(t)
.
∎

Proposition 5
.

Let
X,Y
be independent random variables with densities
f_{\alpha}(x|\lambda)

and
f_{\alpha}(y)\equiv f_{\alpha}(y|1)
respectively.
Then
T=X/Y
has density
w_{\alpha,\alpha}(t|\lambda)
given by

\displaystyle w_{\alpha,\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)dP_{\alpha}(u)=\frac{\sin\pi\alpha}{\pi}\,\frac{\lambda\,t^{\alpha-1}}{\lambda^{2}+2\lambda\,t^{\alpha}\cos\pi\alpha+t^{2\alpha}}

(128)

Proof of Proposition
5
.

Since
X,Y
are independent, the joint density of
(T=t,X=x,Y=y)
is

\Pr(t,x,y)=\Pr(t|x,y)\Pr(x)\Pr(y)
where
\Pr(x)=f_{\alpha}(x|\lambda)
and
\Pr(t|x,y)=\delta(t-x/y)
.
The marginal density
w_{\alpha,\alpha}(t|\lambda)\equiv\Pr(t)
of
T=t
is

\displaystyle w_{\alpha,\alpha}(t|\lambda)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(t-x/y)f_{\alpha}(x|\lambda)f_{\alpha}(y)\,dxdy

\displaystyle(x\to yu)\;

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\delta(t-u)f_{\alpha}(yu|\lambda)f_{\alpha}(y)y\,dudy

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(yt|\lambda)yf_{\alpha}(y)\,dy

\displaystyle(y\to u^{-1/\alpha})\;

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(u^{-1/\alpha}t|\lambda)u^{-1/\alpha}\left[\frac{1}{\alpha}f_{\alpha}(u^{-1/\alpha})u^{-1/\alpha-1}\right]\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,p_{\alpha}(u)\,du=\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\alpha}(t|\lambda u)\,dP_{\alpha}(u)

The second form of
w_{\alpha,\alpha}(t|\lambda)
was demonstrated in the proof of Corollary
3.2
.
∎
