POCS-based framework of signal reconstruction from generalized non-uniform samples
Nguyen T. Thao, Dominik Rzepka, Marek Miśkowicz

Abstract
We formalize the use of projections onto convex sets (POCS) for the reconstruction of signals from non-uniform samples in their highest generality. This covers signals in any Hilbert space ℋ {\mathscr{H}} , including multi-dimensional and multi-channel signals, and samples that are most generally inner products of the signals with given kernel functions in ℋ {\mathscr{H}} . An attractive feature of the POCS method is the unconditional convergence of its iterates to an estimate that is consistent with the samples of the input, even when these samples are of very heterogeneous nature on top of their non-uniformity, and/or under insufficient sampling. Moreover, the error of the iterates is systematically monotonically decreasing, and their limit retrieves the input signal whenever the samples are uniquely characteristic of this signal. In the second part of the paper, we focus on the case where the sampling kernel functions are orthogonal in ℋ {\mathscr{H}} , while the input may be confined in a smaller closed space 𝒜 {\mathscr{A}} (of bandlimitation for example). This covers the increasingly popular application of time encoding by integration, including multi-channel encoding. We push the analysis of the POCS method in this case by giving a special parallelized version of it, showing its connection with the pseudo-inversion of the linear operator defined by the samples, and giving a multiplierless discrete-time implementation of it that paradoxically accelerates the convergence of the iteration.

With this recent comeback of the POCS method in nonuniform sampling, the purpose of this article is to take some distance and reflect on what this method can fundamentally achieve in this problem at the highest possible level of generality. This method is applicable in any abstract Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}
, separable or not. Whenever an input signal
x\in{\mathscr{H}}
is known to be in the intersection of closed convex subsets of
{\mathscr{H}}
, the POCS method can systematically find an element in this intersection as an estimate of
x
. This is the case when an encoder extracts from
x
a sequence of samples
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
of the form

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\langle x,h_{k}\rangle,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(1)

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product of
{\mathscr{H}}
and
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is a known collection of functions in
{\mathscr{H}}
. Indeed, this tells us that
x
belongs to the intersection of the affine hyperplanes
{\mathscr{C}}_{k}:=\{v\in{\mathscr{H}}:\langle v,h_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}\}
, which are a particular case of closed convex sets. When
x
is additionally known to be in a closed subspace
{\mathscr{A}}
of
{\mathscr{H}}
of “limited freedom”, such as bandlimitation, this subspace is naturally to be incorporated by the POCS method among the convex sets. In the basic sampling situation where
x
is a bandlimited function
x(t)
and
\mathrm{s}_{k}=x(t_{k})
at some instant
t_{k}
, then
\mathrm{s}_{k}
does yield the form of (
1
) where
h_{k}(t)
is a sinc function shifted by
t_{k}
. But as (
1
) refers to a more general situation, we call
\langle x,h_{k}\rangle
a
generalized sample
of
x

[
24
]
and
h_{k}
the corresponding
sampling kernel function
.
We give in Section
II
an overview of concrete non-uniform sampling examples that are covered by the formalism of (
1
). This includes point sampling, derivative sampling, integrate-and-fire encoding, asynchronous Sigma-Delta modulation (ASDM) with bandlimited signals in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
or more generally
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}^{N})
or
(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}))^{N}
. In the remainder of the paper, we then elaborate on the specific contributions of the POCS method in the general setting of (
1
).

Section
III
is an overview of the basic principles of the POCS method and its various versions when applied to (
1
) with

in a closed subspace
{\mathscr{A}}
as only extra assumption. When
{\sf Z}
is finite, which is always the case in practice, an outstanding property of the POCS method is the
systematic
convergence of its iteration, contrary to the algorithm of
[
11
]
, whether perfect reconstruction is theoretically possible or not. More specifically, it tends to the estimate that is closest to the initial iterate while being consistent with the samples of
x
. This automatically leads to perfect reconstruction whenever the samples are uniquely characteristic of
x
, whether one is able to prove this situation or not. Meanwhile, there is no restriction on the type of samples that can be used. One can for example mix samples from derivatives of the input of various orders together with integral values. For illustration, we show an experiment of perfect reconstruction from input extrema whose density is below the Nyquist rate. This is possible as the 0-derivative property of extrema has the effect to double the number of samples in the generalized sense.

In the second part of the paper, we focus on the special case where the sampling kernel functions
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
are orthogonal in
{\mathscr{H}}
while

remains in the smaller space
{\mathscr{A}}
. This is a case of importance as this includes the time-encoding machine of
[

]
, leaky integrate-and-fire encoding
[

]
, as well as multi-channel time encoders such as those introduced in

(after some normalization), a rigorous and simple DSP discretization of the iterative part of the method that is applicable even when the subspace
{\mathscr{A}}
is of uncountable infinite dimension (case of non-separable Hilbert space), and a multiplierless variant of it that paradoxically accelerates the convergence. This is covered in Sections

II
Generalized sampling examples

II-A

Basic point sampling in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

In the basic framework of sampling,
{\mathscr{H}}
is the space
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
of square-integrable functions equipped with the inner product

\langle u,v\rangle:=\int_{\mathbb{R}}u(t)v(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad u(t),v(t)\in L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}),

and
{\mathscr{A}}
is a space of bandlimited signals. Up to a change of time unit, one can always assume that
{\mathscr{A}}
is equal to the space
{\mathscr{B}}
of bandlimited signals of Nyquist period 1. Then the most basic example of sampling consists in acquiring from an input
x(t)\in{\mathscr{B}}
the scalar values

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=x(t_{k}),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(2)

where
{\sf Z}
is some set of consecutive integers, and
(t_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is some increasing sequence of instants. In the present context of non-uniform sampling, these instants are not assumed to be equidistant. Since
x(t)=\mathrm{sinc}(t)*x(t)
where
*
is the convolution product and

\mathrm{sinc}(t):=\sin(\pi t)/(\pi t),

we also have
\mathrm{s}_{k}=(\mathrm{sinc}*x)(t_{k})=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathrm{sinc}(t_{k}-t)x(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t
. Then
\mathrm{s}_{k}
can be put in the form of (
1
) with

h_{k}(t):=\mathrm{sinc}(t_{k}\!-t)=\mathrm{sinc}(t-t_{k}),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

II-B

Examples of generalized sampling in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})

One can consider more generally samples of the type

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=(f_{k}*x)(t_{k}),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(3)

where
(f_{k}(t))_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is some family of functions in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. With some similar derivation,
\mathrm{s}_{k}
yields the form of (
1
) with

h_{k}(t):=f_{k}(t_{k}\!-t),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

(4)

In this generalization, we see two generic examples.

II-B
1
Derivative sampling

This is the case where derivatives of
x(t)
of arbitrary orders are sampled, giving a sequence of the type

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n_{k}}x}{{\mathrm{d}}t^{n_{k}}}(t_{k}),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(5)

where
(n_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is some sequence of non-negative integers and
(t_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
may be more generally monotonically increasing (allowing for example
t_{k+1}=t_{k}
with
n_{k+1}\neq n_{k}
). This is the particular case of (
3
) where

f_{k}(t):=\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n_{k}}\mathrm{sinc}}{{\mathrm{d}}t^{n_{k}}}(t),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

Given the even symmetry of
\mathrm{sinc}(t)
, the resulting function
h_{k}(t)
of (
4
) is

h_{k}(t)=(-1)^{n_{k}}\,\frac{{\mathrm{d}}^{n_{k}}\mathrm{sinc}}{{\mathrm{d}}t^{n_{k}}}(t-t_{k}),\qquad k\in{\sf Z}.

(6)

The samples of (
2
) are themselves the particular case of (
5
) where
n_{k}=0
for all
k\in{\sf Z}
.

II-B
2
Integrate-and-fire sampling

In this case, the samples are of the type

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}f(t_{k}\!-t)\,x(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(7)

where
f(t)
is some function in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. This is the particular case of (
3
) where

f_{k}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}f(t)&,&0\leq t<t_{k}\!-t_{k-1}\\
0&,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

Meanwhile, one can see directly from (
7
) that
\mathrm{s}_{k}
yields the form of (
1
) with

h_{k}(t):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ccl}f(t_{k}\!-t)&,&t\in[t_{k-1},t_{k})\\
0&,&\mbox{otherwise}\end{array}\right..

(8)

A well-known case is leaky integrate-and-fire encoding (LIF) where

f(t):=e^{-\alpha t}

for some constant
\alpha\geq 0
. In the case
\alpha=0
, the samples are of the simple form

\mathrm{s}_{k}:=\int_{t_{k-1}}^{t_{k}}x(t)\,{\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad k\in{\sf Z}

(9)

which is also the type of samples that one can extract from an asynchronous Sigma-Delta modulator (ASDM)
[
11
,
15
]
.

II-C

This is the case where

. Each element

where

where
({\bm{t}}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is a sequence of

II-C
2
Multi-channel signals

This applies to the system shown in Fig.

and introduced in

and

III
POCS method

We now return to the general sampling setting of the introduction and give an overview of how the POCS method can be used to reconstruct or estimate an input signal
x
in a closed subspace
{\mathscr{A}}
of
{\mathscr{H}}
from samples of the type (
1
).

III-A

Consistent estimates

Looking for an estimate
u
of
x
, one wishes to make sure that
u
is at the least consistent with the samples of
x
, meaning that
\langle u,h_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}
for all
k\in{\sf Z}
. This is a natural requirement when the samples
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
are uniquely characteristic of
x
. But there is also a rational reason for doing so when the solution to (
1
) is not unique. From a set theoretic viewpoint,
u
is consistent if and only if it is in the set

{\mathscr{S}}:=\textstyle\bigcap\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}{\mathscr{S}}_{k}\quad\mbox{where}\quad{\mathscr{S}}_{k}:=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{A}}:\langle v,h_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}\big\},~~k\in{\sf Z}.

(10)

Now, because
{\mathscr{S}}
is the intersection of affine hyperplanes of
{\mathscr{A}}
, it is a closed affine subspace of
{\mathscr{A}}
(meaning the translated version of a closed linear subspace of
{\mathscr{A}}
). As
{\mathscr{S}}
contains
x
, it then follows from the Pythagorean theorem that the distance of
u
to
x
is automatically reduced by projecting
u
orthogonally to
{\mathscr{S}}
(see Fig.
2
with
{\mathscr{V}}={\mathscr{S}}
). Denoting the norm of
{\mathscr{H}}
by
\|\cdot\|
and calling
P_{\mathscr{V}}
the orthogonal projection onto any given closed affine subspace
{\mathscr{V}}
, we have formally

x\in{\mathscr{V}}~~\mbox{and}~~u\notin{\mathscr{V}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad\|P_{\mathscr{V}}u-x\|<\|u-x\|.

(11)

So if
u\notin{\mathscr{S}}
, there is at least theoretical knowledge to improve it as an estimate of
x
. Meanwhile, if
u\in{\mathscr{S}}
, there is no more deterministic knowledge to discriminate it from
x
.

In practice, there is unfortunately no closed-form expression for
P_{\mathscr{S}}
. However, if
u\notin{\mathscr{S}}
, there exists at least some
k\in{\sf Z}
for which
u\notin{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
. Then (
11
) is valid for
{\mathscr{V}}:={\mathscr{S}}_{k}
. Thus,
P_{{\mathscr{S}}_{k}}
automatically reduces the estimation error of
u
. This time,
P_{{\mathscr{S}}_{k}}
yields the following simple expression.

Proposition III.1

For all
u\in{\mathscr{H}}
,

P_{{\mathscr{S}}_{k}}u=P_{k}u:=\widetilde{u}+\frac{\mathrm{s}_{k}-\langle\widetilde{u},h_{k}\rangle}{\|\widetilde{h}_{k}\|^{2}}\,\widetilde{h}_{k}

(12)

using the general notation

\widetilde{u}:=P_{\mathscr{A}}u,\qquad u\in{\mathscr{H}}.

(13)

Proof:

The following property will be needed,

\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},v\in{\mathscr{A}},\qquad\langle u,v\rangle=\langle\widetilde{u},v\rangle

(14)

which is true since
u-\widetilde{u}
is by construction orthogonal to
{\mathscr{A}}
and hence to
v
. Let
w
be the right hand side of (
12
). We just need to verify that
w\in{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
and
u{-}w\perp w{-}v
for all
v\in{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
. It is first clear that
w\in{\mathscr{A}}
. Then
\langle w,h_{k}\rangle=\langle w,\widetilde{h}_{k}\rangle=\langle\widetilde{u},\widetilde{h}_{k}\rangle+(\mathrm{s}_{k}-\langle\widetilde{u},h_{k}\rangle)=\mathrm{s}_{k}
as a result of (
14
) again. So
w\in{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
. Next, for any
v\in{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
,
\langle u{-}w,w{-}v\rangle=\langle\widetilde{u}{-}w,w{-}v\rangle=-\alpha_{k}\langle\widetilde{h}_{k},w{-}v\rangle
where
\alpha_{k}
is the coefficient of
\widetilde{h}_{k}
in (
12
). But
\langle w{-}v,\widetilde{h}_{k}\rangle=\langle w{-}v,h_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}-\mathrm{s}_{k}=0
.
∎

Note that in certain cases such as in (
6
),
h_{k}
is by construction in
{\mathscr{A}}
, so that
\widetilde{h}_{k}=h_{k}
. This is however not the case of (
8
).

P_{\mathscr{V}}u
and
P_{\mathscr{V}}^{\lambda}u
for
\lambda\in[1,2]

III-B

Alternating projections

A single projection
P_{k}u
is only able to fix the
k
th sample of
u
. To obtain an estimate in
{\mathscr{S}}=\cap_{k\in{\sf Z}}{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
, a natural attempt is to perform an iteration of the type

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=P_{k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

(15)

where
(k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
is some sequence of indices in
{\sf Z}
. As
P_{\mathscr{V}}u=u
when
u\in{\mathscr{V}}
, what can be claimed from (
11
) is that the estimation error
\|u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x\|
will at least monotonically decrease with
n
. If one makes sure that the sets

I_{k}:=\{n\geq 0:k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=k\}

are infinite for every
k\in{\sf Z}
, then one can further guarantee that
\|u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x\|
will stop decreasing only when
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
is effectively in
{\mathscr{S}}
.

When
{\sf Z}
is finite, it is known that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
does eventually converge to an element of
{\mathscr{S}}

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}\in{\mathscr{S}}

(16)

under the stronger condition that
(k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
is “almost cyclic”, which means that the distance between every consecutive elements of
I_{k}
is bounded for each
k\in{\sf Z}
. This results from the general theory of projections onto convex sets (POCS)
[
16
,
17
]
, affine sets being a particular case of convex sets.
Of course,
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}=x
when
x
is the only element of
{\mathscr{S}}
. In other words, when the samples
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
uniquely characterize the input
x
, the POCS iteration of (
15
) leads to perfect reconstruction. When
{\mathscr{S}}
is not a singleton, it is actually known that

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}=P_{\mathscr{S}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}

(17)

because the subspaces
{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
are affine.
Qualitatively,
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
is the element of
{\mathscr{S}}
that is closest to the initial estimate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
with respect to the norm of
{\mathscr{H}}
. If one chooses
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}=0
, then
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
is the minimal norm element of
{\mathscr{S}}
.

Because
{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
is more specifically a hyperplane of
{\mathscr{A}}
, the iteration of (
15
) with a finite set
{\sf Z}
also falls in the description of the Kaczmarz method within the space
{\mathscr{A}}
(note that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in{\mathscr{A}}
for all
n\geq 1
regardless of
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
). The original version of this method is the case where
(k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
is “cyclic”
[
19
]
, which amounts to saying that the elements of
I_{k}
are exactly equidistant of period
\mathrm{card}\,{\sf Z}
for each
k\in{\sf Z}
. This was first applied in the basic non-uniform sampling case of (
2
) in
[
14
]
. Because of the slow convergence, some other sequences
(k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
were discussed in
[
4
]
in this specific application. Randomly generated sequences
(k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
were also proposed in
[
20
]
to accelerate the convergence. But (
16
) was shown only in a probabilistic sense and under uniqueness of reconstruction.

When
{\sf Z}
is infinite, there is no general result of convergence in the sense of (
16
). However, the following weaker convergence

\forall k\in{\sf Z},\qquad\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\langle u,h_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}

is obtained under an extended version of the “almost cyclic” condition (where the bound on the distance between every consecutive elements of
I_{k}
may depend on
k\in{\sf Z}
)
[
18
]
.

III-C

Relaxed projections

The estimation error reduction of (
11
) is in fact more generally realized by a relaxed version of
P_{\mathscr{V}}
. For any transformation
P
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
, let

\displaystyle P^{\lambda}u

\displaystyle:=\lambda Pu+(1-\lambda)u

\displaystyle=u+\lambda(Pu-u).

(18)

Then, a more general version of (
11
) is

x\in{\mathscr{V}}~\mbox{and}~u\notin{\mathscr{V}}~~\Rightarrow~~\forall\lambda\in(0,2),~\|P^{\lambda}_{\mathscr{V}}u-x\|<\|u-x\|.

(19)

This can be seen in Fig.
2
and is easy to prove from the Pythagorean theorem. Meanwhile,
P^{\lambda}u
is still equal to
u
when
u\in{\mathscr{V}}
for any
\lambda
. The iteration of (
15
) is then naturally generalized to

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=P^{\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}_{k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

(20)

where
(\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq}
is a sequence of relaxation coefficients in
(0,2)
. When these coefficients are more strictly in an interval of the type
[\epsilon,2{-}\epsilon]
for some constant
\epsilon\in(0,1]
, then all the convergence results of Section
III-B
are known to remain valid. This additional relaxation freedom permits in practice an acceleration of the convergence. There is however little analytical guideline on how to optimally adjust
(\lambda^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})_{n\geq 0}
.

III-D

More sophisticated POCS methods

There exists a large number of more sophisticated POCS methods aiming at accelerating the convergence. A generic technique of interest is the parallel use of multiple projections at each iteration. This involves transformations of the type

P_{K}^{\bm{\mu}}u=u+{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in K}}\mu_{k}(P_{k}u-u)

(21)

where
K
is some subset of
{\sf Z}
and
{\bm{\mu}}=(\mu_{k})_{k\in K}
is some sequence of coefficients. A basic version is to have positive coefficients
\mu_{k}
such that
\sum_{k\in{\sf Z}}\mu_{k}\in[\epsilon,2{-}\epsilon]
. This amounts to having
P_{K}^{\bm{\mu}}
equal to a convex combination of individual relaxed projections
P_{k}^{\lambda}

[
17
]
.

Whenever possible, a technique to further accelerate the convergence is to decide at each iteration what transformation to apply on
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
depending on its position with respect to the sets
({\mathscr{S}}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. In the serial case of (
15
), a greedy approach is to choose for
k^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
at each iteration
n
the index
k
that minimizes
\|P_{k}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x\|
. By the Pythagorean theorem, this is equivalent to maximizing
\|P_{k}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\|
, which amounts to choosing the set
{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
that is “most remote” from
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
. With the parallel scheme of (
21
), an adaptive approach allows to choose coefficients
\mu_{k}
of substantially larger magnitudes and leading to dramatic convergence accelerations
[
18
]
. But adaptive schemes inherently imply an overhead of computation complexity which may not be compatible with certain conditions of real-time signal processing.

IV
Orthogonal sampling kernel functions

In the example of integrate-and-fire sampling in Section
II-B2
, we saw that the samples
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
yield the generic form of (
1
) with functions
(h_{k}(t))_{k\in{\sf Z}}
of non-overlapping time supports as seen in (
8
). Thus,
(h_{k}(t))_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is orthogonal in
L^{2}({\mathbb{R}})
. Back in the general space setting of this paper, we show in this section that having an orthogonal family of sampling kernel functions
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
in
{\mathscr{H}}
allows a simpler version of POCS iteration for signal reconstruction, with a number of outstanding properties concerning convergence and implementation. All our derivations in this section allow an infinite set
{\sf Z}
.

IV-A

Special POCS configuration

One can always rewrite the set
{\mathscr{S}}=\cap_{k\in{\sf Z}}{\mathscr{S}}_{k}
of (
10
) as

{\mathscr{S}}={\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}

(22)

where

{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}:=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{H}}:\forall k\in{\sf Z},~\langle v,h_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}\big\}

(23)

and
{\mathbf{s}}
symbolizes the sequence
(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. Therefore, an alternative POCS method would be the iteration of

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)},\qquad n\geq 0.

(24)

But like
P_{\mathscr{S}}
, one has in general difficult access to the projection
P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
. Now, the outstanding contribution of having an orthogonal family of sampling kernels
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is that
P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
becomes directly accessible with the following closed form expression.

Proposition IV.1

Assuming that
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is orthogonal in
{\mathscr{H}}
,

\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},\qquad P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=u+{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\frac{\mathrm{s}_{k}-\langle u,h_{k}\rangle}{\|h_{k}\|^{2}}\,h_{k}.

(25)

Proof:

For any given
j\in{\sf Z}
,
\langle h_{j},h_{k}\rangle
is equal to
\|h_{j}\|^{2}
when
k=j
and 0 otherwise. Calling
w
be the right hand side of (
25
), we then obtain

\langle w,h_{j}\rangle=\langle u,h_{j}\rangle+(\mathrm{s}_{j}-\langle u,h_{j}\rangle)=\mathrm{s}_{j}
for any
j\in{\sf Z}
. So
w\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
. Let
v\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
. For any
k\in{\sf Z}
,
\langle w{-}v,h_{k}\rangle=\mathrm{s}_{k}-\mathrm{s}_{k}=0
. Meanwhile, it is clear that
u-w
is a linear combination of
(h_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. So
\langle u{-}w,w{-}v\rangle=0
for any
v\in{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
. Thus,
w=P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u
.
∎

The above proof was mainly an algebraic verification. Here are some additional explanations on the convergence and meaning of the sum in (
25
). This result is equivalent to

\forall u\in{\mathscr{H}},\qquad P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=u+{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\big(\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}-\langle u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle\big)\,\hat{h}_{k}.

(26)

where

\hat{h}_{k}:=h_{k}/\|h_{k}\|\quad\mbox{and}\quad\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}:=\mathrm{s}_{k}/\|h_{k}\|,\quad k\in{\sf Z}.

(27)

But as
\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}=\langle x,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle
, then

{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\big(\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}-\langle u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle\big)\,\hat{h}_{k}={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\langle x{-}u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle\,\hat{h}_{k}.

Since
(\hat{h}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is
orthonormal
in
{\mathscr{H}}
, not only this sum is convergent, but it is also precisely the orthogonal projection of
x-u
onto the closed space linearly spanned by
(\hat{h}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
.

The estimation error
\|u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\!-x\|
is strictly decreasing as long as
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\notin{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
. In all cases,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}=P_{{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}\,u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}.

(28)

Contrary to the iteration of (
15
), this convergence is
unconditional
when
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}\neq\emptyset
even when
{\sf Z}
is infinite.
Assuming that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is chosen in
{\mathscr{A}}
, the iterates
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
of (
24
) remain in
{\mathscr{A}}
for all
n\geq 0
. It then follows from (
26
) and (
24
) that

\forall u\in{\mathscr{A}},\quad P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=u+{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\big(\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}-\langle u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle\big)\,\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}

(29)

where
\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}:=P_{\mathscr{A}}\hat{h}_{k}
according to the notation of (
13
).

Remark:
Using (
14
) and (
27
), the projection
P_{k}u
of (
12
) can be put for all
u\in{\mathscr{A}}
in the form

P_{k}u=u+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{s}_{k}-\langle u,h_{k}\rangle}{\|\widetilde{h}_{k}\|^{2}}$}\,\widetilde{h}_{k}=u+\mbox{\small$\displaystyle\frac{\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}-\langle u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle}{\mu_{k}}$}\,\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}

where
\mu_{k}:=\|\widetilde{h}_{k}\|^{2}/\|h_{k}\|^{2}
. Thus,
P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
yields the equivalent expression

\forall u\in{\mathscr{A}},\qquad P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=u+{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mu_{k}(P_{k}u-u).

(30)

This is the parallel-projection form of (
21
) with
K={\sf Z}
and coefficients
\mu_{k}
that are in
(0,1]
by Bessel’s inequality.

IV-B

Linear operator presentation

The POCS iteration limit of (
28
) is guaranteed when
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
is non-empty. In practice however, the sampling sequence
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})\in{\sf Z}
is not exactly obtained by (
1
) due to inherent noise. The problem is that the iteration (
24
) is consistent with (
1
). As a result,
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
might be empty and it is no longer clear what is the behavior of
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
at the limit. As
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
from (
24
) can be presented as

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}=(P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}})^{n}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)},\qquad n\geq 0,

the goal is to study the dependence of
\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}})^{n}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
with both the initial estimate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
and the sequence
{\mathbf{s}}
, regardless of how
{\mathbf{s}}
has been produced.
This analysis is facilitated as
P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
is affine, i.e., linear plus a fixed translation. Its linear part is extracted by defining the following linear operators

\begin{array}[t]{rcl}{\hat{S}}:~\,{\mathscr{A}}&\rightarrow&\ell^{2}({\sf Z})\\
u&\mapsto&\big(\langle u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle\big)_{k\in{\sf Z}}\end{array}\!\!\mbox{and}~~\begin{array}[t]{rcl}{\hat{S}}^{*}:\,\ell^{2}({\sf Z})&\rightarrow&{\mathscr{A}}\\
{\mathbf{c}}&\mapsto&{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mathrm{c}_{k}\,\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}\end{array}

(31)

where
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
is the space of square-summable sequences
{\mathbf{c}}=(\mathrm{c}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
. By Bessel’s inequality, we have not only the guarantee that the range of
{\hat{S}}
is in
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
, but also that
{\hat{S}}
is bounded of norm
\|{\hat{S}}\|\leq 1
. Then (
29
) can be presented as

\forall u\in{\mathscr{A}},\quad P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=u+{\hat{S}}^{*}(\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}u)

(32)

where

\mathbf{\hat{s}}:=(\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k}/\|h_{k}\|)_{k\in{\sf Z}}.

(33)

We call
\mathbf{\hat{s}}
the normalized version of the sampling sequence
{\mathbf{s}}
.
The notation
{\hat{S}}^{*}
has been used because it is exactly the adjoint of
{\hat{S}}
. To see this, note first that
\langle u,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle=\langle u,\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}\rangle
for all
u\in{\mathscr{A}}
from (
14
). Then, denoting the canonical inner product of
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{2}
, we have for any
u\in{\mathscr{A}}
and
{\mathbf{c}}\in\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
,

\langle{\hat{S}}u,{\bm{c}}\rangle_{2}={\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\langle u,\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}\rangle\mathrm{c}_{k}=\Big\langle u,{\textstyle\sum\limits_{k\in{\sf Z}}}\mathrm{c}_{k}\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k}\Big\rangle=\langle u,{\hat{S}}^{*}{\mathbf{c}}\rangle.

(34)

Next, as (
57
) can be equivalently presented as
P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}u=(I-{\hat{S}}^{*}{\hat{S}})u+{\hat{S}}^{*}\mathbf{\hat{s}}
for all
u\in{\mathscr{A}}
, where
I
is the identity operator, the linear part of
P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}}
is then
I-{\hat{S}}^{*}{\hat{S}}
, which is self-adjoint.

IV-C

Theorem of POCS iteration limit

Before finding the limit of
(P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}})^{n}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, it is first interesting to characterize the emptiness condition of
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
in terms of the operator
{\hat{S}}
. As the set
{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
from (
23
) can be equivalently described as

{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{H}}:\forall k\in{\sf Z},~\langle v,\hat{h}_{k}\rangle=\hat{\mathrm{s}}_{k}\big\},

one sees that

{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{A}}:{\hat{S}}v=\mathbf{\hat{s}}\big\}.

(35)

Thus,

{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}=\emptyset\qquad\Leftrightarrow\qquad\mathbf{\hat{s}}\notin\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})

where
\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
designates the range of
{\hat{S}}
. The next theorem gives conditions under which the convergence of
(P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}})^{n}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is maintained and gives two characterizations of its limit.

Theorem IV.2

Assume that
\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
is closed. Then, for any
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\in{\mathscr{A}}
and any sequence
{\mathbf{s}}
such that
\mathbf{\hat{s}}\in\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}(P_{\mathscr{A}}P_{{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}})^{n}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}

\displaystyle=P_{{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{\bar{s}}}}\,u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}

(36)

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}(\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)})

(37)

where

•

\mathbf{\bar{s}}
is the sequence whose normalized version
\mathbf{\hat{\bar{s}}}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathbf{\hat{s}}
onto
\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
,

•

{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}
is the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse of
{\hat{S}}

[
28
]
.

By definition,

\displaystyle{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}{\mathbf{c}}:=\argmin\limits_{v\in{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}}\|v\|,\qquad{\mathbf{c}}\in\ell^{2}({\sf Z})

(38)

where

(39)

\displaystyle{\mathscr{M}}_{\mathbf{c}}:=\big\{v\in{\mathscr{A}}:\|{\hat{S}}v-{\mathbf{c}}\|_{2}\mbox{ is minimized}\big\}

and
\|\cdot\|_{2}
is the canonical norm of
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
. We prove this theorem in Appendix
-A
. When
\mathbf{\hat{s}}\in\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
, then
\mathbf{\bar{s}}={\mathbf{s}}
, so (
36
) is just the same as (
28
) and the proof focuses on (
37
). When
\mathbf{\hat{s}}\notin\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
, it then shows that (
36
) and (
37
) hold based on the fundamental property of adjoint operators

\mathrm{null}({\hat{S}}^{*})=\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})^{\perp}

(40)

where
\mathrm{null}({\hat{S}}^{*})
is the null space of
{\hat{S}}^{*}
. This in fact directly results from (
34
).
The characterization (
37
) was previously proved in
[
15
,
13
]
, but only for
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}=0
and with operators of different normalization settings.

The closed property of
\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
is necessary for
\mathbf{\bar{s}}
to be defined. In the pseudo-inversion interpretation, this property is necessary for
\|{\hat{S}}v-{\mathbf{c}}\|_{2}
to have a minimum with respect to
v\in{\mathscr{A}}
. It is by default realized when
{\sf Z}
is finite, which is always the case in practice. In this case also,
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
is just
{\mathbb{R}}^{\sf Z}
equipped with its Euclidean norm, so any sequence
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is allowed by the theorem.

IV-D

Dual interpretation of POCS iteration limit and impact

Theorem
IV.2
gives two interpretations of the POCS iteration limit
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
.

IV-D
1
Set theoretic interpretation

When
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
is non-empty, we know from (
28
) that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
is the element of this set that is closest to
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
. When
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{s}}
is empty, the POCS iteration behaves as if this set has been replaced by
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{\bar{s}}}
where
\mathbf{\bar{s}}
is the closest possible sequence to
{\mathbf{s}}
after normalization while ensuring a non-empty set
{\mathscr{A}}\cap{\mathscr{C}}_{\mathbf{\bar{s}}}
. Due to the normalization, the distance between
\mathbf{\bar{s}}
and
{\mathbf{s}}
that is minimized is the square root of

\sum_{k\in{\sf Z}}|\bar{\mathrm{s}}_{k}-\mathrm{s}_{k}|^{2}/\|h_{k}\|^{2}
.

IV-D
2
Operator theoretic interpretation

From an operator-theoretic viewpoint, (
1
) tells us that
\mathbf{\hat{s}}={\hat{S}}x
. When
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is chosen to be 0, it follows from (
37
) that
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}={\hat{S}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{s}}
. Thus, the POCS method coincides with the standard procedure of solving a linear equation by pseudo-inversion. A consequence of interest is the dependence of
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}
with sampling noise. In practice, the samples are more generally of the form
\mathrm{s}_{k}=\langle x,h_{k}\rangle+\mathrm{e}_{k}
where
{\mathbf{e}}=(\mathrm{e}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
is some unknown error sequence. In this case,

\mathbf{\hat{s}}={\hat{S}}x+\mathbf{\hat{e}}\qquad\mbox{and}\qquad u^{\scriptscriptstyle(\infty)}={\hat{S}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{s}}={\hat{S}}^{\dagger}{\hat{S}}x+{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{e}}

by linearity of
S^{\dagger}
, where
\mathbf{\hat{e}}
is the normalized version of
{\mathbf{e}}
. While
{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}{\hat{S}}x
is the error-free reconstruction (equal to
x
when
{\hat{S}}
is injective),
{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{e}}
is the error contribution of the sampling noise to the reconstruction. Now, as shown in Appendix
-A
,
{\hat{S}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{e}}={\hat{S}}^{\dagger}\mathbf{\hat{\bar{e}}}
, where
\mathbf{\hat{\bar{e}}}
is the orthogonal projection of
\mathbf{\hat{e}}
onto
\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
. By Bessel’s inequality,
\|\mathbf{\hat{\bar{e}}}\|_{2}\leq\|\mathbf{\hat{e}}\|_{2}
(with a strict inequality when
\mathbf{\hat{e}}\notin\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
). Thus, pseudo-inversion has a sampling-noise filtering effect. Meanwhile, as
\mathbf{\hat{\bar{e}}}\in\mathrm{ran}({\hat{S}})
,
\mathbf{\hat{\bar{e}}}
cannot be distinguished from the normalized sampling sequence of an actual signal in
{\mathscr{A}}
. So the error component
\mathbf{\hat{\bar{e}}}
is irreversible.

IV-E

Discrete-time implementation of iteration

As a result of (
32
), the iteration of (
24
) is explicitly

u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+{\hat{S}}^{*}(\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}),\qquad n\geq 0

(41)

assuming
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}\in{\mathscr{A}}
. In practice, this is an iteration of continuous-time functions. Now, whether the space
{\mathscr{A}}
has a countable dimension
or not
, there is a way to obtain
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
by a pure discrete-time iteration in
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
. Let
{\mathbf{s}}=(\mathrm{s}_{k})_{k\in{\sf Z}}
be the given sequence of samples and
\mathbf{\hat{s}}
be its normalized version as defined by (
33
). For any given initial estimate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
, consider the system iteration

\displaystyle{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle={\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+(\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)})-{\hat{S}}{\hat{S}}^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

(42a)

\displaystyle u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+{\hat{S}}^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}

(42b)

for
n\geq 0
with
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}:=0
, the zero vector of
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
. We have

\displaystyle u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+{\hat{S}}^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n+1)}

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}+{\hat{S}}^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+{\hat{S}}^{*}\big(\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}(u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}{+}{\hat{S}}^{*}{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)})\big)

\displaystyle=u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}+{\hat{S}}^{*}(\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}).

Thus
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}
reproduces the recursion of (
41
) for the given initial estimate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
. The outstanding contribution of (
42
) is that
{\mathbf{c}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(n)}\in\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
for all
n\geq 0
. So (
42a
) is a pure discrete-time iteration. In it,
\mathbf{\hat{s}}-{\hat{S}}u^{\scriptscriptstyle(0)}
is a fixed sequence in
\ell^{2}({\sf Z})
which just needs to be computed once. Meanwhile,
{\hat{S}}^{*}{\hat{S}}
can be seen as a square matrix of coefficients

{\hat{S}}{\hat{S}}^{*}=\begin{bmatrix}\langle\widetilde{\hat{h}}_{k^{\prime}},\hat{h}_{k}\rangle\end{bmatrix}_{(k,k^{\prime})\in{\sf Z}\times{\sf Z}}

(43)

which also needs to be determined once.
In practice, if one aims at the estimate
u^{\scriptscriptstyle(m)}
, then one only needs to iterate (
42a
) alone
m
times, and then perform the conversion of (
42b
) from discrete time to Hilbert space function, only
once
at
n=m
.

V-B

Signal and system formalization

The POCS method of

{\mathscr{H}}:=(L^{2}({\mathbb{R}}))^{M}.

Each element
{\bm{u}}\in{\mathscr{H}}

{\mathscr{H}}

\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\rangle:={\textstyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}u^{i}(t)v^{i}(t){\mathrm{d}}t,\qquad{\bm{u}},{\bm{v}}\in{\mathscr{H}}.

(45)

We will simply denote by
\|\cdot\|

{\mathscr{H}}

\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
,

Given their degree of freedom, the diagonal matrices

VII
Conclusion

We have formalized the use of the POCS method for signal reconstruction from non-uniform samples by describing the most general and abstract context where this is applicable, and giving the available properties of reconstruction from this approach. On the first aspect, this method is applicable in any Hilbert space, separable or not, with samples that can be images of the input by any linear functionals. On the second aspect, the iteration of the POCS method
unconditional
converges to the signal that yields the same samples as the input while being closest to the initial iterate. This is true no matter how heterogenous the samples are (for example by mixing samples of different filtered versions of the input) and whether they are uniquely characteristic of the input or not (in the first case, perfect reconstruction is automatic). Before convergence, each single iteration of the POCS method has its own contribution as it guarantees an error reduction of the current estimate in the metric sense of the Hilbert space. In the second part of the paper, we have substantially pushed the analysis of the POCS method when the kernel functions of the linear functionals are orthogonal to each other. This is a case of high interest as it covers the increasingly popular time encoding by integration. In this case, one obtains additionally a parallelized version of the iteration, the exact pseudo-inversion of the linear operator that formalizes the sampling operation (including in the presence of noise), a rigorous time discretization of the iteration (applicable even in non-separable spaces) with a multiplierless implementation option that paradoxically accelerates the convergence. For demonstration, we have applied our theory to the multi-channel time-encoding system of
[
23
,
9
]
, thus reformalizing this system while uncovering new consequences on it.
