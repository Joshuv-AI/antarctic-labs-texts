ARCS: Accurate Rotation and Correspondence Search
Liangzu Peng, Manolis C. Tsakiris, René Vidal

Abstract
This paper is about the old Wahba problem in its more general form, which we call “simultaneous rotation and correspondence search”. In this generalization we need to find a rotation that best aligns two partially overlapping 3 3 D point sets, of sizes m m and n n respectively with m ≥ n m\geq n . We first propose a solver, ARCS , that i) assumes noiseless point sets in general position, ii) requires only 2 2 inliers, iii) uses O ⁡ ( m ​ log ⁡ m ) O(m\log m) time and O ⁡ ( m ) O(m) space, and iv) can successfully solve the problem even with, e.g . , m , n ≈ 10 6 m,n\approx 10^{6} in about 0.1 0.1 seconds. We next robustify ARCS to noise, for which we approximately solve consensus maximization problems using ideas from robust subspace learning and interval stabbing. Thirdly, we refine the approximately found consensus set by a Riemannian subgradient descent approach over the space of unit quaternions, which we show converges globally to an ε \varepsilon -stationary point in O ⁡ ( ε − 4 ) O(\varepsilon^{-4}) iterations, or locally to the ground-truth at a linear rate in the absence of noise. We combine these algorithms into ARCS+ , to simultaneously search for rotations and correspondences. Experiments show that ARCS+ achieves state-of-the-art performance on large-scale datasets with more than 10 6 10^{6} points with a 10 4 10^{4} time-speedup over alternative methods. https://github.com/liangzu/ARCS

Prior Art: Accuracy Versus Scalability

Early efforts on Problem
1

\ICP

Besl-PAMI1992

Chetverikov-ICPR2002

Rusinkiewicz-2001
;
Chui-CVIU03
;
Myronenko-PAMI10
;
Jian-PAMI11

\GOICP

of the branch
\&

;

;

The above

2

correspondences
\hat{\mathcal{C}}:=[m]\times[n]

This justifies why researchers have recently focused on designing robust rotation search algorithms for extreme outlier rates,
e.g

,
\geq 90

4.2.2

We now consider the following consensus maximization:

\begin{split}&\max_{\mathcal{I}\subset[\ell],\bm{R}\in\SO(3)}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ |\mathcal{I}|\\

Algorithm.
We start with the following equivalence.

Proposition 4
.

We have
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{w}=\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}

Robust Rotation Search.
From

we randomly sampled point pairs

with

We compared
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{O}}

Discussion and Future Work

Despite of the progress that we made for robust rotation search and simultaneous rotation and correspondence search on large-scale point clouds, our
\ARCSplus
pipeline has a few limitations, and we discuss them next.

For small datasets (
e.g
.
,
\ell\leq 500
), as in homography fitting
Bustos-ICCV2015
, other methods,
e.g
.
,
MAGSAC++

Barath-CVPR2019
;
Barath-CVPR2020
;
Barath-TPAMI2021
,
VSAC

Ivashechkin-ICCV2021
,
\TEASER

Yang-T-R2021
, and
\GORE

Bustos-ICCV2015
might be considered with higher priority; they come with efficient C++ implementations. For more points,
e.g
.
,
\ell\geq 10^{4}
, but with higher inlier rates than in Table
4
(
e.g
.
,
\geq 15\%
),
\GNCTLS

Yang-RA-L2020
and
\RANSAC
are our recommendations for what to use.

Modern point clouds have more than
10^{5}
points, and are naturally correspondences-less (cf.
Cai-ISPRS-J-PRS2019
).
\ARCS
operates at that scale in the absence of noise (Table
1
), while
\ARCSplus
can handle
m,n\approx 10000
(Figure
2
) and
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{OR}}
can handle
\ell\approx 10^{7}
correspondences (Table
4
); all these are limited to the rotation-only case. To find rotation (and translation) from such point sets “in the wild”, it seems inevitable to downsample them. An interesting future work is to theoretically quantify the tradeoff between downsampling factors and the final registration performance. Another tradeoff to quantify, as implied by Remark
2
, is this: Can we design a correspondence matching algorithm that better balances the number of remaining points and the number of remaining inliers? In particular, such matching should take specific pose into consideration (cf.
\ARCS
); many methods did not.

Like
\TEASER
,
\GORE
,
\GNCTLS
,
\RANSAC
, our algorithm relies on an inlier threshold
c
. While how to set this hyper-parameter suitably is known for Gaussian noise with given variance,
in practice the distance threshold is usually chosen empirically
, as Hartley & Zisserman wrote
Hartley-2004
. While mis-specification of
c
could fail the registration, certain heuristics have been developed to alleviate the sensitivity to such mis-specification; see
Barath-CVPR2019
;
Barath-CVPR2020
;
Barath-TPAMI2021
;
Antonante-TRO2021
. Finally, our experience is to set
c
based on the scale of the point clouds.

Our outlier removal component
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{O}}
presented good performance (Table
3
), yet with no optimality guarantees. Note that, with
s=90
we have
|\phi_{j}-\phi^{*}|\leq 1
for some
\phi_{j}
, while Figure
1(a)
shows that
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{O}}
gave roughly
1
degree error at
s=90
. Theoretically justifying this is left as future work. Without guarantees, registration could fail, which might lead to undesired consequences in safety-critical applications. On the other hand, we believe that
\ARCSplus
is a good demonstration of trading optimality guarantees for accuracy and scalability; enforcing all of the three properties amounts to requiring solving NP hard problems efficiently at large scale! In fact, since any solutions might get certified for optimality (Remark
3
), bold algorithmic design ideas can be taken towards improving accuracy and scalability, while relying on other tools for optimality certification.

Appendix A
Proof of Proposition
4

We consider a stronger version of Proposition
4
:

Proposition 6
.

We have
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{w}=\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}
, where
\bm{w}
is a quaternion representation of
\bm{R}
of
(
8
)
, and
\bm{D}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
is a positive semi-definite matrix whose entries depend on
\bm{x}_{i}
,
\bm{y}_{i}
. So Problem (
8
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}}h(\bm{w}),\ \ \ \ h(\bm{w})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\sqrt{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{w}}.

(15)

Moreover,
\bm{D}_{i}
has eigenvalues
4,4,0,0
if
\bm{x}_{i}
and
\bm{y}_{i}
are normalized (that is
\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}=\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}=1
).

We first recall some basics about unit quaternions, an algebraic construction invented by Hamilton in the
1840
’s, when the notion of
vector
does not exist; see the beautiful account of
Altmann-1989
. In our current notation, each element
\bm{w}
of
\mathbb{S}^{3}
is called a unit quaternion. The most crucial fact is that
\SO(3)
is isomorphic to the
3
-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{3}

up to sign
, that is
\SO(3)\equiv\mathbb{S}^{3}/\{\pm 1\}
. This implies a two-to-one correspondence between unit quaternions and
3
D rotations. Algebraically, any
\bm{R}\in\SO(3)
can be written as a
3\times 3
matrix

\displaystyle\footnotesize{\begin{matrix}w_{1}^{2}+w_{2}^{2}-w_{3}^{2}-w_{4}^{2}&2(w_{2}w_{3}-w_{1}w_{4})&2(w_{2}w_{4}+w_{1}w_{3})\\
2(w_{2}w_{3}+w_{1}w_{4})&w_{1}^{2}+w_{3}^{2}-w_{2}^{2}-w_{4}^{2}&2(w_{3}w_{4}-w_{1}w_{2})\\
2(w_{2}w_{4}-w_{1}w_{3})&2(w_{3}w_{4}+w_{1}w_{2})&w_{1}^{2}+w_{4}^{2}-w_{2}^{2}-w_{3}^{2}\\
\end{matrix}}

where
\bm{w}=[w_{1},w_{2},w_{3},w_{4}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
. We can now write the three entries of
\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}
as quadratic forms
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i,1}\bm{w}
,
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i,2}\bm{w}
, and
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{X}_{i,3}\bm{w}
, respectively. Here
\bm{X}_{i,1}
,
\bm{X}_{i,2}
, and
\bm{X}_{i,3}
are
4\times 4
symmetric matrices, defined as

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i,1}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}\\
0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}\\
[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&0\\
-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&0&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}\end{bmatrix}

(16)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i,2}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}\\
-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&0\\
0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}\\
[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}\end{bmatrix}

(17)

\displaystyle\bm{X}_{i,3}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&0\\
[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}\\
-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&0&-[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}\\
0&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{1}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{2}&[\bm{x}_{i}]_{3}\end{bmatrix}

(18)

Defining
\bm{C}_{i}:=[\bm{y}_{i}]_{1}\bm{X}_{i,1}+[\bm{y}_{i}]_{2}\bm{X}_{i,2}+[\bm{y}_{i}]_{3}\bm{X}_{i,3}
, we get that
\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{C}_{i}\bm{w}
. And defining

\displaystyle\bm{D}_{i}=(\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2})\bm{I}_{4}-2\bm{C}_{i}

(19)

with
\bm{I}_{4}
the
4\times 4
identity matrix, we obtain the equality

\displaystyle\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}-2\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}

(20)

\displaystyle=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{w}.

(21)

Since
\bm{D}_{i}
is symmetric and
\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{w}\geq 0
for any
\bm{w}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}
, we know that
\bm{D}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 4}
is positive semi-definite.

Suppose
\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}=\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}=1
. Then there is at least two different
3
D rotations
\bm{R}_{1}
and
\bm{R}_{2}
satisfying
\bm{y}_{i}=\bm{R}_{1}\bm{x}_{i}=\bm{R}_{2}\bm{x}_{i}
. Thus, with the factorization
\bm{D}_{i}=\bm{Z}_{i}\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}
, there are at least two quaternions
\bm{w}_{1}
and
\bm{w}_{2}
with
\bm{w}_{1}\neq\pm\bm{w}_{2}
satisfying that
\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}_{1}=\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}_{2}=0
. So
\rank(\bm{D}_{i})=\rank(\bm{Z}_{i})\leq 2
. Recalling
\bm{D}_{i}=2\bm{I}_{4}-2\bm{C}_{i}
, we see that
1
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{C}_{i}
that has multiplicity at least
2
. Similarly, we can derive that
\big\|\bm{y}_{i}+\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}=\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}^{\prime}\bm{w}
where
\bm{D}_{i}^{\prime}=(\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2})\bm{I}_{4}+2\bm{C}_{i}=2\bm{I}_{4}+2\bm{C}_{i}
is positive semi-definite of rank at most
2
. That is,
-1
is an eigenvalue of
\bm{C}_{i}
of multiplicity at least
2
. Concluding,
\bm{C}_{i}
has eigenvalues
1,1,-1,-1
and
\bm{D}_{i}
has eigenvalues
4,4,0,0
.

Appendix B
Proposition
5
: Proof and Interpretation

Here we provide a proof (Appendix
B.1
) and probabilistic interpretation (Appendix
B.2
) for Proposition
5
. In this section, we use the notation
\bm{D}_{i}=\bm{Z}_{i}\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}
from Appendix
A
where we decomposed every positive semidefinite matrix
\bm{D}_{i}
into the product of its root
\bm{Z}_{i}
. Since we could always normalize the point sets
\bm{y}_{i}
and
\bm{x}_{i}
, and then normalize
\bm{D}_{i}
, we assume without loss of generality that
\bm{D}_{i}
has eigenvalues
1,1,0,0
(cf. Proposition
6
). In this situation, we can now specify that
\bm{Z}_{i}
is a matrix of size
4\times 2
and it has orthonormal columns, i.e.,
\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{I}_{2}
. Also, we see that the objective function (
9
) of interest can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}}h(\bm{w}),\ \ \ \ h(\bm{w})=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}\big\|_{2}.

(22)

Note that, if
\bm{Z}_{i}
had a single column, then (
22
) is exactly the problem of
dual principal component pursuit
(DPCP)
Tsakiris-JMLR2018
. On the other hand, one could think of (
22
) as a
group
version of DPCP, as
\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}\big\|_{2}
here promotes group sparsity. A similar group version of DPCP was considered by
Ding-CVPR2020
in the context of homography estimation. In
Ding-CVPR2020
, the authors provided conditions under which any global minimizer of (
22
) coincides with the ground-truth normal vector, or, in our context, the ground-truth unit quaternion
\pm\bm{w}^{*}
. Thus, our contribution here, if viewed from the angle of group-DPCP, is to show that, there is actually an efficient algorithm that exactly reaches the guaranteed ground-truth normal. We present our contribution next.

B.1
Proof of Proposition
5

The proof follows from Proposition 4 of
Li-SIAM-J-O2021
with some simplification for specializing arbitrary Stiefel manifolds to
\mathbb{S}^{3}
, and with some modification to tighten a constant factor (from
2
to
\sqrt{2}
). We also note that
\eta_{\min}
and
\eta_{\max}
are motivated from their corresponding definitions.

Write
\bm{w}:=c_{0}\bm{w}_{0}+c^{*}\bm{w}^{*}
with
c_{0}^{2}+(c^{*})^{2}=1
and
\bm{w}_{0}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}
. Without loss of generality assume
c^{*}\geq 0
. Then

\displaystyle\dist(\bm{w},\pm\bm{w}^{*})

\displaystyle=\min\big\{\sqrt{2+2c^{*}},\sqrt{2-2c^{*}}\big\}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{2-2c^{*}}

(23)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2-2(c^{*})^{2}}=\sqrt{2}c_{0}.

If
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
then by Proposition
4
we have

\displaystyle\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}^{*}\big\|_{2}=\sqrt{\bm{w}^{\top}\bm{D}_{i}\bm{w}}=\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{R}^{*}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}=0.

(24)

Hence the difference
h(\bm{w})-h(\bm{w}^{*})
is equal to

\displaystyle c_{0}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}_{0}\big\|_{2}+\sum_{i\in[\ell]\backslash\mathcal{I}^{*}}\Big(\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}\big\|_{2}-\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}^{*}\big\|_{2}\Big).

By (
B.1
) and the definition of
\eta_{\min}
(
12
), we know that

\displaystyle c_{0}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}_{0}\big\|_{2}\geq\frac{k^{*}\eta_{\min}\dist(\bm{w},\pm\bm{w}^{*})}{\sqrt{2}}.

(25)

By triangle inequality the second summation in the above the difference
h(\bm{w})-h(\bm{w}^{*})
is smaller than or equal to
\sum_{i\in[\ell]\backslash\mathcal{I}^{*}}\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{*})\big\|_{2}
, but this bound satisfies

\displaystyle\sum_{i\in[\ell]\backslash\mathcal{I}^{*}}\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{*})\big\|_{2}\leq(\ell-k^{*})\eta_{\max}\dist(\bm{w},\pm\bm{w}^{*}),

where we used
\dist(\bm{w},\pm\bm{w}^{*})=\sqrt{2-2c^{*}}=\big\|\bm{w}-\bm{w}^{*}\big\|_{2}
(
B.1
) and the definition of (
13
). We finished the proof.

B.2
Probabilistic Interpretation of Proposition
5

B.2.1
Technical Assumptions

We assume there is no noise for two reasons. First, analysis for noisy data is more challenging and requires a full different chapter to penetrate. Second, analysis in the noiseless case typically serves as a starting point for and sheds enough light on analysis for noise. For example, see the trajectory of the development from the noiseless case
Tsakiris-JMLR2018
to the noisy case
Ding-ICML2019
in the context of DPCP.

Next, we discuss probabilistic assumptions on inliers. For an inlier index
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
, each column of
\bm{Z}_{i}
lies in the ground-truth hyperplane
\mathcal{S}^{*}\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
that is perpendicular to the ground-truth unit quaternion
\pm\bm{w}^{*}
, and the two columns of
\bm{Z}_{i}
span a subspace
\mathcal{S}_{i}
of dimension
2
that is contained in
\mathcal{S}^{*}
. Note that any
\bm{Z}_{i}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{R}^{4\times 2}
whose columns are in
\mathcal{S}_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}
are equivalent to
\bm{Z}_{i}
in the sense that
\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}^{*}\big\|_{2}=\big\|(\bm{Z}_{i}^{\prime})^{\top}\bm{w}^{*}\big\|_{2}=0
. To impose randomness assumptions on
\bm{Z}_{i}
, one could simply replace
\bm{Z}_{i}
by a
4\times 2
random matrix whose columns are independently sampled uniformly at random from the intersection
\mathcal{S}_{i}\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}
. In fact, we need a slightly stronger assumption:

Assumption 1

(
randomness on inliers
)
.

For each
i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}
, every column of
\bm{Z}_{i}
is independently sampled uniformly at random from the intersection
\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

This assumption destroys some good property of
\bm{Z}_{i}
: it might not be orthonormal in general. However, it is orthonormal in expectation, i.e., it satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{Z}_{i}]=\bm{I}_{2}
. This will suffice for our later analysis.

On the other hand, Assumption
1
simplifies matters by a lot. This can be appreciated in comparison with a “common” approach, where one makes assumptions on the “source data”, which are point pairs
(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
’s in our case. Let us first recall the “data flow” from
(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
to
\bm{Z}_{i}
:

\displaystyle(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\xmapsto{\text{Proposition \ref{prop:SO3toDPCP1}}}\bm{D}_{i}\xmapsto{\text{factorizing $\bm{D}_{i}$}}\bm{Z}_{i}

(26)

In view of the above flow (or graphical model), one intuitively (not very rigorously) feels that, if
(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
’s are independent, then
\bm{Z}_{i}
’s are independent; the latter is implied by Assumption
1
. On the other hand, it seems hard to know the distribution of
\bm{Z}_{i}
’s, even if the distribution of
(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
’s is given or assumed. It is via Assumption
1
that this challenge is circumvented and that our theorems are developed.

Finally, we need randomness on outliers:

Assumption 2

(
randomness on outliers
)
.

Each column of any outlier
\bm{Z}_{j}
, where
j\in[\ell]\backslash\mathcal{I}^{*}
, is independently sampled uniformly at random from
\mathbb{S}^{3}
.

Since an outlier
\bm{Z}_{j}
could be distributed arbitrarily, this assumption is the most natural, if not the most challenging, as the outliers try their best to mimic the distribution of inliers. Assumptions
1
and
2
(together with the noiseless assumption) are all we need for the next section.

B.2.2
Probabilistic Interpretation

Recall that the quantities
\eta_{\min}
,
\eta_{\max}
of interest are equal to

\displaystyle\eta_{\min}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{*}}\min_{\bm{w}\in\mathcal{S}^{*}\cap\mathbb{S}^{3}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{*}}\big\|\bm{Z}_{i}^{\top}\bm{w}\big\|_{2},\text{\ \ and }

(27)

\displaystyle\eta_{\max}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\ell-k^{*}}\max_{\bm{w}\in\mathbb{S}^{3}}\sum_{j\in[\ell]\backslash\mathcal{I}^{*}}\big\|\bm{Z}_{j}^{\top}\bm{w}\big\|_{2}.

(28)

The following proposition gives probabilistic upper and lower bounds for
\eta_{\max}
and
\eta_{\min}
respectively.

Proposition 7
.

Under the assumptions of §
B.2.1
, we have

(i)

With probability at least
1-\exp(-\zeta^{2}/2)
it holds that

\displaystyle\eta_{\max}\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}+\frac{(4+\zeta)}{\sqrt{\ell-k^{*}}}.

(29)

(ii)

With probability at least
1-\exp(-\zeta^{2}/2)
it holds that

\displaystyle\eta_{\min}\geq\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}-\frac{(4+\zeta)}{\sqrt{k^{*}}}

(30)

To prove Proposition
7
(cf. Appendix
B.2.3
), we combine the proof strategies of
Li-SIAM-J-O2021
and
Zhu-NeurIPS2018
, where both sets of the authors found inspirations from
Lerman-FoCM2015
.
We can now see that the condition of Proposition
5
,
\alpha^{*}:=k^{*}\eta_{\min}/\sqrt{2}-(\ell-k^{*})\eta_{\max}>0
, holds with high probability as long as

\displaystyle\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}k^{*}-(4+\zeta)\sqrt{k^{*}}\geq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}(\ell-k^{*})+(4+\zeta)\sqrt{\ell-k^{*}}.

Ignoring lower-order terms we get the condition

\displaystyle k^{*}\gtrsim\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(\ell-k^{*})\Leftrightarrow\frac{k^{*}}{\ell}\gtrsim\frac{\sqrt{3}}{\sqrt{3}+2},

(31)

which holds true whenever there are sufficiently many inliers. This condition ensures the
\alpha^{*}
-sharpness, from which local linear convergence to
\pm\bm{w}^{*}
from a good enough initialization with proper stepsize ensues.

Appendix D
Interval Stabbing

Here we provide proofs for Propositions
2
and
3
. Along the way we will need multiple temporary variables to illustrate the idea; we use
a_{i,j}
’s to denote those variables. Here,
i
denotes the
i
-th point pair, and
j
denotes the order in which
a_{i,j}
appears for the first time. In §
4.2.1
we reviewed interval stabbing for closed intervals
\mathcal{J}_{i}
of the form
[a,b]
. One should note and verify that this can be easily extended to the case where
\mathcal{J}_{i}
is a finite (disjoint) union of closed intervals.

D.1
Proof of Proposition
2

Recall
\bm{b}=[\sin(\theta)\cos(\phi),\ \sin(\theta)\sin(\phi),\ \cos(\theta)]^{\top}
with
\theta\in[0,\pi]
,
\phi\in[0,\pi]
. Denote by
a_{i,1}:=[\bm{v}_{i}]_{1}\cos(\phi)+[\bm{v}_{i}]_{2}\sin(\phi)
, then
|\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b}|\leq c
is equivalent to

\displaystyle|a_{i,1}\sin(\theta)+[\bm{v}_{i}]_{3}\cos(\theta)|\leq c.

(50)

Without loss of generality we can assume that
a_{i,1}\geq 0
. So there is a unique
a_{i,2}\in[0,\pi]
which satisfies

\displaystyle\cos(a_{i,2})=\frac{[\bm{v}_{i}]_{3}}{\sqrt{[\bm{v}_{i}]_{3}^{2}+a_{i,1}^{2}}},\ \ \ \sin(a_{i,2})=\frac{a_{i,1}}{\sqrt{[\bm{v}_{i}]_{3}^{2}+a_{i,1}^{2}}}.

Hence (
50
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle|\cos(\theta-a_{i,2})|\leq c_{i},\ \ \ c_{i}:=\min\Bigg\{1,\frac{c}{\sqrt{[\bm{v}_{i}]_{3}^{2}+a_{i,1}^{2}}}\Bigg\}

Since the trigonometric function
\arccos:[0,\pi]\to[-1,1]
is decreasing and
|\theta-a_{i,2}|\leq\pi
, the above is equivalent to

\displaystyle a_{i,3}:=\arccos(-c_{i})\geq|\theta-a_{i,2}|\geq\arccos(c_{i})=:a_{i,4}.

Define
a_{i,5}=a_{i,2}-a_{i,3},\ a_{i,6}=a_{i,2}-a_{i,4},\ a_{i,7}=a_{i,2}+a_{i,4},
and
a_{i,8}=a_{i,2}+a_{i,3}
. Then
|\bm{v}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b}|\leq c
is the same as

\displaystyle\theta\in\big([a_{i,5},a_{i,6}]\cup[a_{i,7},a_{i,8}]\big)\cap[0,\pi]

(51)

To summarize, given
\phi\in[0,\pi]
, the
i
-th constraint of (
5
) requires
\theta
to lie in the union of some disjoint intervals defined in (
51
). So maximizing (
6
) amounts to finding a maximal set of intervals of the form (
51
) that overlap a point
\theta
, and can be solved by interval stabbing in
O(\ell\log\ell)
time.

D.2
Proof of Proposition
3

Assume that the rotation axis
\bm{b}
of the
3
D rotation

\displaystyle\bm{R}=\bm{b}\bm{b}^{\top}+[\bm{b}]_{\times}\sin(\omega)+(\bm{I}_{3}-\bm{b}\bm{b}^{\top})\cos(\omega)

(52)

is given, and we now solve (
7
). Let
a_{i,9}=\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{b}\bm{b}^{\top}\bm{x}_{i}
,
a_{i,10}=\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}[\bm{b}]_{\times}\bm{x}_{i}
and
a_{i,11}=\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}(\bm{I}_{3}-\bm{b}\bm{b}^{\top})\bm{x}_{i}
. Then

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}=a_{i,9}+a_{i,10}\sin(\omega)+a_{i,11}\cos(\omega).

(53)

Hence the constraint of (
4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}-c^{2}\leq 2\bm{y}_{i}^{\top}\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}

(54)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow

\displaystyle\ \ a_{i,10}\sin(\omega)+a_{i,11}\cos(\omega)\geq a_{i,12}

(55)

where we defined
a_{i,12}=(\big\|\bm{y}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}+\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}^{2}-c^{2})/2-a_{i,9}
. There is a unique angle
a_{i,13}\in[0,2\pi)
satisfying

\displaystyle\cos(a_{i,13})=\frac{a_{i,11}}{\sqrt{a_{i,10}^{2}+a_{i,11}^{2}}},\ \sin(a_{i,13})=\frac{a_{i,10}}{\sqrt{a_{i,10}^{2}+a_{i,11}^{2}}}

Thus, the constraint
\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}\leq c
of (
4
) is the same as

\displaystyle\cos(\omega-a_{i,13})\geq\max\Bigg\{\frac{a_{i,12}}{\sqrt{a_{i,10}^{2}+a_{i,11}^{2}}},-1\Bigg\}=:a_{i,14}.

Without loss of generality assume
a_{i,14}\leq 1
, for otherwise we could simply ignore this constraint. Define
a_{i,15}=\arccos(a_{i,14})\in[0,\pi]
. Since
|\omega-a_{i,13}|\in[0,2\pi]
, we consider two cases, namely
|\omega-a_{i,13}|\leq\pi
and
|\omega-a_{i,13}|>\pi
. In the former case, since
\arccos
is a decreasing function, the above constraint is equivalent to
|\omega-a_{i,13}|\leq a_{i,15}
. In the later case the above constraint is equivalent to

\displaystyle\cos(2\pi-|\omega-a_{i,13}|)\geq a_{i,14}\Leftrightarrow 2\pi-|\omega-a_{i,13}|\leq a_{i,15}.

Thus, the constraint
\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{R}\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}\leq c
of (
4
) requires
\omega\in[0,2\pi]
to lie in the union of the following intervals.

\displaystyle[a_{i,13}-a_{i,15},\ a_{i,13}+a_{i,15}]\cap[0,2\pi]

(56)

\displaystyle[a_{i,13}-a_{i,15}+2\pi,\ 2\pi]

(57)

\displaystyle[0,\ a_{i,13}+a_{i,15}-2\pi].

(58)

In the above, the invalid interval where the right endpoint is smaller than its left endpoint, if any, should be discarded. To conclude, (
7
) can be solved via interval stabbing.

Appendix F
Handling The Translation Case

Utilizing ideas that have been known in prior works, it is easy to extend our algorithms to the situation where there is an extra unknown translation. As we did for Problems
1
and
2
, we first define the two problems that we will discuss:

Problem 3

(
simultaneous pose and correspondences
)
.

Let the two point sets
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{P}
of Problem
1
instead satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{q}_{i}=\bm{R}^{*}\bm{p}_{j}+\bm{t}^{*}+\bm{o}_{i,j}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i,j},

(59)

where
\bm{t}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an extra unknown translation vector.
The task is to simultaneously estimate the rotation
\bm{R}^{*}
, translation
\bm{t}^{*}
, and correspondences
\mathcal{C}^{*}
from
\mathcal{Q}
and
\mathcal{P}
.

Problem 4

(
robust registration
)
.

Let the
\ell
pairs of
3
D points
\{(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
of Problem
2
instead satisfy

\displaystyle\bm{y}_{i}=\bm{R}^{*}\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{t}^{*}+\bm{o}_{i}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i}.

(60)

The task is to find
\bm{R}^{*}
,
\bm{t}^{*}
, and correspondences
\mathcal{I}^{*}
.

We will discuss more about Problem
3
in our future work; here we focus on the its special case, Problem
4
. Specifically, we next extend our
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{OR}}
algorithm to handle Problem
4
(Appendix
F.1
), and present its performance on the
3
DMatch dataset
Zeng-CVPR2017
(Appendix
F.2
).

F.1
Extension for Robust Registration

Here we present an extension of our
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{OR}}
algorithm for solving Problem
4
. In this extension, we essentially combine
\ARCSplus_{\texttt{OR}}
with
known
techniques. Thus, the presentation here serves more as an useful demonstration of concepts, and less as an entirely novel insight into, or the most efficient method for, solving Problem
4
. Nevertheless, we will show in Appendix
F.2
that our extension does enjoy state-of-the-art performance on the
3
DMatch dataset
Zeng-CVPR2017
.

We first review three crucial ingredients that are useful for solving Problem
4
:
translation elimination (
TE
), rotation elimination (
RE
), and
outlier removal
.

Translation Elimination (
TE
).
For each
i,j\in[\ell]
,
i>j
, define
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij}=\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{y}_{j}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij}=\bm{x}_{i}-\bm{x}_{j}
, then

\displaystyle\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij}=\bm{R}^{*}\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij}+(\bm{o}_{i}-\bm{o}_{j})+(\bm{\epsilon}_{i}-\bm{\epsilon}_{j}).

(61)

Here
(\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij},\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij})
is referred to in the literature as
translation invariant measurements
, as (
61
) no longer involves translation. As a consequence, robust rotation search might be performed over
\{(\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij},\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij})\}_{i>j}
, yielding an estimate of rotation and correspondences. After this, the translation can be easily computed. A disadvantage here is that computing all
(\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij},\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij})
’s needs
O(\ell^{2})
time; also note though that this computation can be implemented in parallel and thus can be efficient for medium-size datasets (
e.g
.
,
\ell\leq 3\times 10^{4}
).

Rotation Elimination (
RE
).
Every inlier
(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})
satisfying (
60
) with
\bm{o}_{i}=0
also necessarily satisfies

\displaystyle\big\|\bm{x}_{i}+\bm{\epsilon}_{i}\big\|_{2}=\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{t}^{*}\big\|_{2}\Leftrightarrow\big\|\bm{x}_{i}\big\|_{2}\approx\big\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{t}^{*}\big\|_{2}.

(62)

If there were no outliers, estimating
t^{*}
from relation (
62
) is the problem of
source localization
that appears in signal processing applications
Beck-TSP2008
. Estimating translation from (
62
) in the presence of outliers is more challenging. A possible algorithm is combining the least-squares solvers of
Beck-TSP2008
with an iterative reweighting strategy, but this does not have global optimality guarantee. The other approach, which we employ, is to estimate
t^{*}
via branch & bound, solving the following optimization problem:

\displaystyle\max_{\mathcal{I}\subset[\ell],\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}}\ \ \ \ \ \ \ |\mathcal{I}|

(63)

s.t.

\displaystyle\ \ \big|\|\bm{y}_{i}-\bm{t}\|_{2}-\|\bm{x}_{i}\|_{2}\big|\leq c,\ \ \forall i\in\mathcal{I}

If directly applying branch & bound to (
63
), one would branch over
\mathbb{R}^{3}
(cf.
Liu-ECCV18
). On the other hand, our development in §
4.2
implies that branching over
\mathbb{R}^{2}
, where the first two coordinates of
\bm{t}
lie, suffices, as the third coordinate can be determined by interval stabbing. In short, we solve (
63
) via branching over the two-dimensional space
\mathbb{R}^{2}
if needed. As a matter of fact, branch & bound runs much faster even if the parameter space has smaller dimension.

Remark 4

(
TE
versus
RE
)
.

Translation elimination (
TE
) yields
O(\ell^{2})
measurements, leads to the problem of robust rotation search, and is also used in the
2
D-
3
D
perspective-three-point
problem (see,
e.g
.
,
Persson-ECCV2018
); many recent papers on
3
D-
3
D registration used
TE
(see,
e.g
.
,
Yang-T-R2021
and its follow-up works).
RE
yields
O(\ell)
measurements, leads to a less familiar problem, and receives fewer attention;
Liu-ECCV18
is the only paper, which we know, that uses
RE
(for Problem
3
).

Outlier Removal.
Even though rotation or translation can be estimated independently of each other (using
TE
or
RE
respectively), they might not be able to handle the case of extreme outlier rates. In particular, if using
TE
then the inlier ratio decreases from
k^{*}/\ell
to
O\big((k^{*}/\ell)^{2}\big)
. This is why an outlier removal procedure is needed prior to estimation. For this, create (in mind) a graph
\mathcal{G}
with
\ell
vertices representing the
\ell
point pairs
\{(\bm{y}_{i},\bm{x}_{i})\}_{i=1}^{\ell}
. Moreover, create an edge between two vertices
i
and
j
, if
|\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij}-\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij}|\leq 2c
, where
\mathrm{\mathbf{y}}_{ij}
and
\mathrm{\mathbf{x}}_{ij}
are defined in (
61
). Then, find a maximum clique of
\mathcal{G}
, and remove all point pairs whose corresponding vertices are not contained in the maximum clique. See
Parra-arXiv2020
;
Yang-T-R2021
;
Shi-arXiv2020v2
for more transparent discussion on this idea.

For implementation, we use the code of
Parra-arXiv2020
to create
\mathcal{G}
and compute a maximum clique of it.

\ARCSplus_{\texttt{OR}}
