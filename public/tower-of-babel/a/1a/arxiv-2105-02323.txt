Degeneration of the spectral gap with negative Robin parameter
Derek Kielty

Abstract
. The spectral gap of the Neumann and Dirichlet Laplacians are each known to have a sharp positive lower bound among convex domains of a given diameter. Between these cases, for each positive value of the Robin parameter an analogous sharp lower bound on the spectral gap is conjectured. In this paper we show the extension of this conjecture to negative Robin parameters fails by proving that the spectral gap of double cone domains are exponentially small, for each fixed parameter value.



1.
Introduction

The Robin Laplacian eigenvalue problem with parameter
\alpha
on a bounded Lipschitz domain
\mathcal{D}
is

\begin{cases}-\Delta u=\lambda u\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ \mathcal{D},\\
\partial_{\nu}u+\alpha u=0\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ \partial\mathcal{D}.\end{cases}

It has a discrete spectrum of eigenvalues for each
\alpha\in(-\infty,\infty)
:

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D})<\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D})\leq\lambda_{3}(\mathcal{D})\leq\dots\rightarrow\infty.

Recall that when
\alpha=0
this is the Neumann eigenvalue problem. The limiting case
\alpha=\infty
corresponds to Dirichlet boundary conditions. See
[
9
, Chapter 4]
for foundational material on the Robin problem.

In this paper we prove that when
\alpha<0
the spectral gap

(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D})

can be arbitrarily small among domains
\mathcal{D}
of a given diameter. Investigation of this result for
\alpha<0
was motivated by the following conjecture of Andrews, Clutterbuck, and Hauer
[
3
, §10]
for
\alpha\geq 0
:

Conjecture 1

(Robin gap)
.

If
\alpha\geq 0
and
\mathcal{D}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a bounded convex domain then

(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D})\geq(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(I),

where
I\subset\mathbb{R}
is an open interval of length equal to the diameter of
\mathcal{D}
.

The Robin gap conjecture is known to hold in the Neumann case (
\alpha=0
) due to Payne and Weinberger
[
18
]
. A new proof was given by Andrews and Clutterbuck
[
2
, pp. 901]
. In the Dirichlet case (
\alpha=\infty
) it is known, not only for the Laplacian, but also for Schrödinger operators with a convex potential, thanks to Andrews and Clutterbuck
[
2
, Corollary 1.4]
.

Figure 1
.
The
double cone
domain
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
in 3-dimensions with
\theta=\pi/5
.

To state our main result, define the family of convex
double cone
domains of angle
\theta
by

\mathcal{D}_{\theta}=\{(x,y)\in(-1,1)\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:|y|<\tan(\theta/2)(1-|x|)\},\quad\text{for each}\penalty\ \theta\in(0,\pi),

as shown in Figure
1
.

Theorem 2

(Degeneration of the Robin gap)
.

If
\alpha<0
then
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\rightarrow 0
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. In fact, if
\alpha<0
then for each
\epsilon>0
there exists a constant
C>0
such that

(1)

(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\leq C\exp\{-4(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|/\theta\},\quad\text{for all }\theta\text{ sufficiently small}.

The scaling relation
\lambda_{j}(t\mathcal{D};\alpha)=t^{-2}\lambda_{j}(\mathcal{D};t\alpha)
then shows that
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(t\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\rightarrow 0
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, for each
t>0
. Since
t\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
has diameter
2t
when
\theta\in(0,\pi/2]
, Theorem
2
implies that among convex
\mathcal{D}
of any given diameter, the spectral gap
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D})
can be made arbitrarily small when
\alpha<0
. In particular, since
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(I)>0
there exist convex domains
\mathcal{D}
such that
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D})<(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(I)
, and so the Robin gap conjecture fails to extend to
\alpha<0
.

We believe the upper bound in Theorem
2
is sharp in the sense that the estimate (
1
) fails when
\epsilon=0
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. Although we do not prove this the proof of our upper bound on
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
and Lemma
3
suggests it is true.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows: in Section 2 we discuss a heuristic explaining Theorem
2
, in Section 3 we present a brief overview of relevant literature, in Section 4 we prove a lemma about convergence of Schrödinger eigenvalues, and in Section 5 we prove Theorem
2
. Finally, in Section 6 we suggest a variant of Theorem
2
to a modification of the domains
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
and discuss the possibility of extending Conjecture
1
for
\alpha<0
to a restricted class of domains.

2.
A heuristic for small gaps in Theorem
2
: disjoint union of cones

The Robin eigenvalue problem with
\alpha<0
on the infinite cone of opening angle
\theta<\pi

(2)

\mathcal{C}_{\theta}=\{(x,y)\in(0,\infty)\times\mathbb{R}^{n-1}:|y|<\tan(\theta/2)x\},

has at least one negative eigenvalue and a corresponding ground state (see equation (
16
)) that concentrates exponentially fast (in
L^{2}
-norm), as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. In the 2-dimensional case, it is known that
all
the eigenfunctions concentrate in this fashion (see
[
12
, Theorem 5.1]
).

Because of this concentration we expect that the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
concentrate at each vertex
(\pm 1,0)
like the ground state of
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
concentrates at the origin. Consequently, for small
\theta
the Robin problem on
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
should be approximated by the Robin problem on two copies of
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. This approximation suggests
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\rightarrow 0
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, because the first eigenvalue on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\sqcup\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
has multiplicity two and hence the spectral gap equals zero.

Proving the gap tends to zero is a difficult task because
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})=-\alpha^{2}/\sin^{2}(\theta/2)\sim\theta^{-2}
(see equation (
16
)). Thus, Theorem
2
says that even though
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
and
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
have size
\theta^{-2}
, they are still exponentially close as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. Indeed, since
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\leq\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
, estimate (
15
) in the proof of Theorem
2
actually shows that for each
\epsilon>0

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta}),\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})=-\frac{\alpha^{2}}{\sin^{2}(\theta/2)}+O(\exp\{-4(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|/\theta\}),\quad\text{ as }\theta\rightarrow 0.

3.
Literature

For an overview of the Robin Laplacian and related shape optimization problems see the article by Bucur, Freitas, and Kennedy
[
9
, Chapter 4]
. In the study of superconductors, Robin boundary conditions with negative parameter are also known for their connection to Ginzburg-Landau theory
[
10
, §1.4]
.

Recently, Ashbaugh and the current author
[
5
]
, as well as Andrews, Clutterbuck, and Hauer
[
4
]
, proved sharp lower bounds on the Robin spectral gap for 1-dimensional Schrödinger operators with various classes of single-well potentials. In contrast with Theorem
2
, some of these lower bounds continue to hold for some
\alpha<0
.

The Robin Laplacian on the infinite cone with small aperture will play a crucial role throughout this paper. Khalile and Pankrashkin
[
12
]
computed eigenvalue asymptotics for the small aperture limit of 2-dimensional infinite cones. Recently, this was also done in higher dimensions by Pankrashkin and Vogel
[
16
]
. This geometric limit is related to the
\alpha\rightarrow-\infty
limit, for which Khalile
[
11
]
proved that the first
N=N(\mathcal{D})
Robin eigenvalues of a fixed curvilinear polygon
\mathcal{D}
are determined (to leading order in
\alpha
) by the angles of the vertices. For smooth domains the second term in the
\alpha\rightarrow-\infty
asymptotic is determined by the mean curvature; see work of Pankrashkin and Popoff
[
17
]
. In related work, Bögli, Kennedy, and Lang
[
6
]
proved convergence of certain eigenvalues of the Robin Laplacian to those of the Dirichlet Laplacian for certain limits
\alpha\rightarrow\infty
, for complex
\alpha
.

4.
Convergence of Schrödinger eigenvalues on expanding balls

In this section we prove the convergence and calculate the convergence rate as
R\rightarrow\infty
of the first eigenvalue
E=E(R)
of the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem

(3)

\begin{cases}(-\Delta-\frac{\kappa}{r})\varphi=E\varphi\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ B(R),\\
(\partial_{r}+\gamma)\varphi=0\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ \partial B(R),\end{cases}

where
B(R)
is the ball of radius
R
centered at the origin,
\kappa>0
is constant, and
\gamma\in(-\kappa/(n-1),\infty]
. When
\gamma=\infty
we impose Dirichlet boundary conditions on
\partial B(R)
. Since the potential in (
3
) is radial, (
3
) reduces to an ODE eigenvalue problem that we analyze using special functions. In the proof of Theorem
2
in a later section we will get a lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
by proving a lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})-\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
in terms of
E(R)
, where
R=\cot(\theta/2)\rightarrow\infty
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
.

The aforementioned ODE eigenvalue problem gives rise to the
confluent hypergeometric equation

(4)

\rho\frac{d^{2}F}{d\rho^{2}}+(b-\rho)\frac{dF}{d\rho}-aF=0,

for certain
a\in\mathbb{R}
and
b\notin\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
, as we shall see in the proof of the next lemma. The solution
F=F(a,b;\rho)
is a linear combination of the confluent hypergeometric functions of the first and second kind,
M(a,b;\rho)
and
U(a,b;\rho)
, respectively. For more information on this ODE see Chapter 13 by Slater of the book edited by Abramowitz and Stegun
[
1
]
or Chapter 13 of the NIST Digital Library of Mathematical Functions
[
7
]
.

In Lemma
3
below, we show that the first eigenvalue of (
3
) is determined by a positive solution
z
of the transcendental equation

(5)

2\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}M(a_{0}+1,b_{0}+1;\rho_{0})=(1-2\gamma z/\kappa)M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0}),

where

m=(n-3)/2,\penalty\ a_{0}=a_{0}(z)=m+1-z,\penalty\ b_{0}=2m+2,\penalty\ \rho_{0}=\rho_{0}(z)=\kappa R/z.

When
\gamma=\infty
the transcendental equation is
M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})=0
. To see this case formally, divide by
\gamma
in (
5
) and let
\gamma\rightarrow\infty
.

Lemma 3
.

Assume that
\gamma\in(-\infty,\infty]
and
\kappa>0
are fixed.

(i) If
R<\infty
then the radial eigenfunctions of (
3
) with
E<0
are multiples of

(6)

M(a_{0},b_{0};2\sqrt{|E|}\penalty\ r)e^{-\sqrt{|E|}\penalty\ r}

and have eigenvalues
E=-\kappa^{2}/4z^{2}
, where
z
is a positive solution of (
5
).

(ii) If
R=\infty
(i.e. the domain is
\mathbb{R}^{n}
) then the radial eigenfunctions (that decay as
r\rightarrow\infty
) of (
3
) are multiples of (
6
) with eigenvalues
E=-\kappa^{2}/4(j+(n-3)/2)^{2}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
. Furthermore,
M(a_{0},b_{0};2\sqrt{|E|}r)
is a polynomial in
r
of degree
j-1
.

(iii) If
\gamma\in(-\kappa/(n-1),\infty]
then for each
\epsilon>0
, there is a solution
z_{*}
of (
5
) that corresponds to the first eigenvalue
E
of (
3
) and satisfies
z_{*}(R)=(n-1)/2+O(\exp\{-2(1-\epsilon)\kappa R/(n-1)\})
, as
R\rightarrow\infty
. Consequently,

E(R)=-\kappa^{2}/(n-1)^{2}+O\bigg(\exp\bigg\{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{2\kappa}{n-1}R\bigg\}\bigg),\quad\text{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

Part (iii) of the lemma shows that the first eigenvalue of (
3
) converges to
-\kappa^{2}/(n-1)^{2}
as
R\rightarrow\infty
, which is the first eigenvalue of
-\Delta-\kappa/r
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Computing the spectrum of the Schrödinger operator
-\Delta-\kappa/r
on
B(R)
and
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is closely related to computing the spectrum of the hydrogen atom from quantum mechanics. In fact, on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
this calculation is precisely the hydrogen atom calculation (see the book by Landau and Lifschitz
[
13
, Chapter V]
, for example). For general
n\geq 3
, the ODE in (
7
) below is the radial part of the Schrödinger operator for the 3-D hydrogen atom with angular momentum
m
(after changing variables). This physical interpretation breaks down when
n=2
since in this case
m<0
. The operator in (
7
) also appears in work of Khalile and Pankrashkin
[
12
]
as an effective operator for the Robin Laplacian with negative Robin parameter on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, when
n=2
.

Proof of Lemma
3
.

Part (i):

Let
\varphi=\varphi(r,\xi)
be an arbitrary radial eigenfunction of (
3
) with eigenvalue
E=E(R)
written in
(r,\xi)
-spherical coordinates. Thus,
\varphi(r,\xi)
is a constant times a function
u(r)
. In this case,
-\Delta-\kappa/r
reduces to the radial part of the Laplacian plus the potential
-\kappa/r
so that

\begin{cases}-u^{\prime\prime}-\frac{n-1}{r}u^{\prime}-\frac{\kappa}{r}u=Eu\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ (0,R),\\
u^{\prime}+\gamma u=0\quad&\text{at}\penalty\ r=R.\end{cases}

In the above problem and the ones below we impose Dirichlet boundary conditions when
\gamma=\infty
. Let
m=(n-3)/2
and
v(r)=r^{m+1}u(r)
so that

(7)

\begin{cases}-v^{\prime\prime}+\frac{m(m+1)}{r^{2}}v-\frac{\kappa}{r}v=Ev\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ (0,R),\\
v^{\prime}+(\gamma-\frac{m+1}{r})v=0\quad&\text{at}\penalty\ r=R.\end{cases}

Since
E<0
we can define
z=\kappa(2\sqrt{|E|})^{-1}>0
,
\rho=\kappa r/z
, and
w(\rho)=v(z\rho/\kappa)
. It follows that
w
satisfies

\begin{cases}-w^{\prime\prime}+\frac{m(m+1)}{\rho^{2}}w-(\frac{z}{\rho}-\frac{1}{4})w=0\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ (0,\rho_{0}),\\
w^{\prime}+(\frac{z\gamma}{\kappa}-\frac{m+1}{\rho})w=0\quad&\text{at}\penalty\ \rho=\rho_{0},\end{cases}

where
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}(z)=\kappa R/z
.

Guessing a solution of the form
w(\rho)=\rho^{m+1}e^{-\rho/2}F(\rho)
we find that
F(\rho)=F(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)
is a solution of (
4
) on the interval
(0,\rho_{0})
with
a_{0}(z)=m+1-z
,
b_{0}=2m+2
, and boundary condition
2F^{\prime}(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})=(1-2z\gamma/\kappa)F(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})
. When
\gamma=\infty
the boundary condition
F(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})=0
is immediate from the form of the ansatz. Thus, the original radial eigenfunctions
\varphi
are multiples of
F(a_{0},b_{0};2\sqrt{|E|}r)e^{-\sqrt{|E|}r}
.

The general solution of
(
4
) is a linear combination of the confluent hypergeometric functions of the first and second kind,
M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)
and
U(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)
, respectively. It follows from formulas 13.4.21, 13.5.5, and 13.5.7 in
[
1
]
that

U^{\prime}(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)=-a_{0}\frac{\Gamma(b_{0})}{\Gamma(a_{0}+1)}\rho^{-b_{0}}+o(\rho^{-b_{0}}),\quad\text{as }\rho\rightarrow 0,

so that
U^{\prime}(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)\not\in L^{2}(B(R))
since
b_{0}=n-1
. Thus,
F(\rho)
must have no
U(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)
component to guarantee that
\varphi\in H^{1}(B(R))
. This shows that
F(\rho)=M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho)
.

Finally, using the boundary condition we computed for
F
and the formula
M^{\prime}(a,b;\rho)=\frac{a}{b}M(a+1,b+1;\rho)

[
1
, formula 13.4.8]
we deduce that
z
must satisfy the transcendental equation (
5
). The definition of
z
shows that a negative eigenvalue
E
satisfies
E=-\kappa^{2}/4z^{2}
for some solution
z
.

Part (ii):
The same calculation shows that on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
the eigenfunctions
\varphi
are multiples of (
6
) with eigenvalue
-\kappa^{2}/4z^{2}
. Note that if
a
and
b
are fixed and
a\notin\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
then
M(a,b;\rho)\sim\rho^{a-b}e^{\rho}
as
\rho\rightarrow\infty
(see
[
1
, 13.1.4]
). If
a\in\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
then
M(a,b;\rho)
is a polynomial of degree
-a
(see (
8
)). Thus, we must have
a_{0}\in\{0,-1,-2,\dots\}
to ensure
\varphi
tends to zero as
r\rightarrow\infty
. It follows from the definition of
a_{0}=m+1-z
that
z=\kappa(2\sqrt{|E|})^{-1}
and
E=-\kappa^{2}/4(j+m)^{2}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}
, where
m=(n-3)/2
.

Part (iii):
We split the the proof into two cases depending on the size of
|\gamma|
. Recall that
\kappa>0
and
m=(n-3)/2
. Let
0<\epsilon<1
.

Case 1:
|\gamma|<\kappa/(2(m+1))
:
Put

z_{-}(R)=m+1-e^{-(1-\epsilon)\kappa R/(m+1)}\quad\text{and}\quad z_{+}=m+1.

We show that there exists a
z_{*}=z_{*}(R)\in(z_{-}(R),z_{+})
that solves (
5
) for all
R
sufficiently large, and
z_{*}(R)\nearrow m+1
exponentially fast, as
R\rightarrow\infty
. We do this by applying the intermediate value theorem to the function

\mathcal{M}(z)=2\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}M(a_{0}+1,b_{0}+1;\rho_{0})-(1-2\gamma z/\kappa)M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0}),

where recall that
a_{0}=a_{0}(z)=m+1-z
,
b_{0}=2m+2
, and
\rho_{0}=\rho_{0}(z)=\kappa R/z
.
(Note that
\mathcal{M}(z)=0
if and only if
z
satisfies the transcendental equation (
5
).) The desired convergence for
E(R)=-\kappa^{2}/4z_{*}(R)^{2}
as
R\rightarrow\infty
then follows immediately.

We show that
\mathcal{M}(z_{+})<0
and that
\mathcal{M}(z)>0
for
z\leq z_{-}(R)
in order to apply the intermediate value theorem on the interval
[z_{-},z_{+}]
for each fixed
R
sufficiently large. To prove these estimates on
\mathcal{M}
we use the power series representation

(8)

M(a,b;\rho)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{a^{(k)}}{b^{(k)}}\frac{\rho^{k}}{k!},\quad\text{for }a\in\mathbb{R},\penalty\ b\notin\{0,-1,-2,\dots\},

where
a^{(k)}
is the rising factorial. That is,
a^{(0)}=1
and
a^{(k)}=a(a+1)\cdots(a+k-1)
for
k\geq 1
. Observe that
z\mapstochar\rightarrow M(a_{0}(z),b;\rho_{0}(z))
is continuous for each fixed
R
, since the series converges uniformly in
z
on compact subsets of
(0,\infty)
by the Weierstrass M-test.

Note that
M(a_{0},b_{0};\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ )
is positive by (
8
) when
0<z<m+1
because
a_{0}(z)>0
. Thus, as soon as we prove that a solution
0<z_{*}<m+1
exists we know that it corresponds to the first eigenvalue
E
of (
3
) since the ground state is the unique positive eigenfunction.

First we prove
\mathcal{M}(z_{+})<0
for each
R>0
. Indeed, since
a_{0}(m+1)=0
we know

\mathcal{M}(z_{+})=\mathcal{M}(m+1)=-(1-2\gamma(m+1)/\kappa)M(0,b_{0};\rho_{0})<0

because
\gamma<\kappa/(2(m+1))
and
M(0,b_{0};\penalty\ \cdot\penalty\ )
is identically 1.

Now we prove
\mathcal{M}(z)>0
when
z\leq z_{-}(R)
and
R
is sufficiently large. First choose
\delta=\delta(\gamma)\in(0,1)
large enough that
z\mapstochar\rightarrow(\delta+2\gamma z/\kappa)
is uniformly positive on
[0,m+1]
, which is possible since
\gamma>-\kappa/(2(m+1))
. Next, observe that there is a
K=K(\gamma,m)
independent of

such that

(9)

2\frac{k+m+1-z}{k+2m+2}\geq 1+\delta,\quad\text{for each }k\geq K.

The
k^{\text{th}}
coefficient of the power series of the first term of
\mathcal{M}
satisfies the following identity, by considering the first and last factors in the rising factorials:

2\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}\frac{(a_{0}+1)^{(k)}}{(b_{0}+1)^{(k)}}=2\frac{m+1-z}{2m+2}\frac{(m+2-z)^{(k)}}{(2m+3)^{(k)}}=2\frac{k+m+1-z}{k+2m+2}\frac{(m+1-z)^{(k)}}{(2m+2)^{(k)}},

for each

. Applying the lower bound (
9
) we have

(10)

2\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}\frac{(a_{0}+1)^{(k)}}{(b_{0}+1)^{(k)}}\geq(1+\delta)\frac{a_{0}^{(k)}}{b_{0}^{(k)}},\quad\text{for each }k\geq K.

Dropping the first
K
terms in the power series of
M(a_{0}+1,b_{0}+1;\rho_{0})
, applying (
10
), and adding and subtracting
1+\delta
times the first
K

shows

\displaystyle 2\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}M(a_{0}+1,b_{0}+1;\rho_{0})\geq(1+\delta)\sum_{k=K}^{\infty}\frac{a_{0}^{(k)}}{b_{0}^{(k)}}\frac{\rho_{0}^{k}}{k!}=(1+\delta)M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})-(1+\delta)\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{a_{0}^{(k)}}{b_{0}^{(k)}}\frac{\rho_{0}^{k}}{k!}.

Subtracting and adding
(2\gamma z/\kappa)M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})
to the right side shows

(11)

2\frac{a_{0}}{b_{0}}M(a_{0}+1,b_{0}+1;\rho_{0})\geq(1-2\gamma z/\kappa)M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})+\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0},z),

where

\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0},z)=(\delta+2\gamma z/\kappa)M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})-(1+\delta)\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{(m+1-z)^{(k)}}{(2m+2)^{(k)}}\frac{\rho_{0}^{k}}{k!}.

To conclude that
\mathcal{M}(z)>0
from (
11
) we will show that
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0},z)>0
for

, when

is sufficiently large. In what follows

is a positive constant that is independent of both

and

and may change from line to line. Since
z\mapstochar\rightarrow(\delta+2\gamma z/\kappa)
is uniformly positive on
[0,m+1]
by our choice of
\delta
and the second term of
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0},z)
satisfies

(12)

(1+\delta)\sum_{k=0}^{K-1}\frac{(m+1-z)^{(k)}}{(2m+2)^{(k)}}\frac{\rho_{0}^{k}}{k!}\leq C(1+\rho_{0}^{K}),

to obtain
\mathcal{E}(\rho_{0},z)>0
it suffices to show that

M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})\geq Ce^{(\epsilon/2)\rho_{0}}.

(Note
\rho_{0}=\kappa R/z\geq\kappa R/m\rightarrow\infty
, as
R\rightarrow\infty

dominates
\rho_{0}^{K}

We estimate the factor
a_{0}^{(k)}/b_{0}^{(k)}
from below using that

and converting the rising factorials to ordinary factorials so that for
k\geq 1
,

\frac{a_{0}^{(k)}}{b_{0}^{(k)}}=\frac{(m+1-z)^{(k)}}{(2m+2)^{(k)}}\geq(m+1-z)\frac{(k-1)!}{(2m+2)^{(k)}}=(m+1-z)\frac{(k-1)!(2m+1)!}{(2m+2+(k-1))!}.

Noting that

only depends on the dimension

\frac{a_{0}^{(k)}}{b_{0}^{(k)}}\geq(m+1-z)\frac{C}{(k+2m+1)^{2m+2}},\quad\text{for }k\geq 0.

Using this lower bound, observing
(k+2m+1)^{-(2m+2)}\geq C(1-\epsilon/2)^{k}
, and using the Taylor series for

, we have

\displaystyle M(a_{0},b_{0};\rho_{0})\geq

\displaystyle C(m+1-z)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(k+2m+1)^{2m+2}}\frac{\rho_{0}^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle C(m+1-z)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{((1-\epsilon/2)\rho_{0})^{k}}{k!}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle C(m+1-z)e^{(1-\epsilon/2)\rho_{0}}.

Finally, using that
z<z_{-}(R)\leq m+1-e^{-(1-\epsilon)\rho_{0}}
shows that

(m+1-z)e^{(1-\epsilon/2)\rho_{0}}\geq e^{(\epsilon/2)\rho_{0}},

and hence (
12
) holds.

Since
z\mapstochar\rightarrow\mathcal{M}(a_{0}(z),b_{0};\rho_{0}(z))
is continuous the intermediate value theorem shows that there exists a solution
z_{*}
to (
5
) that satisfies
z_{-}(R)<z_{*}<z_{+}=m+1
. Using that
m+1=(n-1)/2
we have that

\frac{n-1}{2}-\exp\bigg\{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{2\kappa}{n-1}R\bigg\}\leq z_{*}\leq\frac{n-1}{2},

for all
R
sufficiently large. This shows the first estimate in part (iii) of the lemma for
|\gamma|<\kappa/(2(m+1))
. Hence,

(13)

E(R)=-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{4z_{*}(R)^{2}}=-\frac{\kappa^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}}+O\bigg(\exp\bigg\{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{2\kappa}{n-1}R\bigg\}\bigg),\quad\text{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

This completes the proof of part (iii) of the lemma for
|\gamma|<\kappa/(2(m+1))
.

Case 2:
\gamma\in[\kappa/(2(m+1)),\infty]
:
In the remainder of the proof let
E(R,\gamma)
denote the first eigenvalue of the Schrödinger problem (
3
). To prove the asymptotic for
E(R,\gamma)
when
\gamma\in[\kappa/(2(m+1)),\infty]
notice that
\gamma\mapstochar\rightarrow E(R,\gamma)
is increasing for
\gamma\in(-\infty,\infty]
by the min-max principle. In particular,
E(R,0)\leq E(R,\gamma)\leq E(R,\infty)
for each
\gamma>0
. Thus, applying formula (
13
) with
\gamma=0
gives the desired lower bound for
E(R,\gamma)
and it suffices to prove the right side of (
13
) is also an upper bound for the first Dirichlet eigenvalue
E(R,\infty)
.

By part (ii) of the lemma, the ground state of the
-\Delta-\kappa/r
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
\exp\{-\sqrt{|E(\mathbb{R}^{n})|}\penalty\ r\}
with eigenvalue

. Let
\chi
be a smooth radial cutoff function that is 1 for

, and is zero for

defined by
\chi(r/R)\exp\{-\sqrt{|E(\mathbb{R}^{n})|}\penalty\ r\}

is a valid trial function for the first eigenvalue

of (

\displaystyle E(R,\infty)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\int_{B(R)}f(-\Delta-\kappa/r)f\,dV}{\int_{B(R)}f^{2}\,dV}

\displaystyle=\frac{E(\mathbb{R}^{n})\int_{B((1-\epsilon)R)}\exp\{-2\sqrt{|E(\mathbb{R}^{n})|}\penalty\ r\}\,dV+\int_{B(R)\setminus B((1-\epsilon)R)}f(-\Delta-\kappa/r)f\,dV}{\int_{B((1-\epsilon)R)}\exp\{-2\sqrt{|E(\mathbb{R}^{n})|}\penalty\ r\}\,dV+\int_{B(R)\setminus B((1-\epsilon)R)}f^{2}\,dV}

\displaystyle=E(\mathbb{R}^{n})+O\bigg(\exp\{-(1-\epsilon)\frac{2\kappa}{n-1}R\}\bigg),\quad\text{as }R\rightarrow\infty.

The last equality follows from using the decay of

.

This completes the proof of the asymptotic for

for all

. Note that the asymptotic for

5.
Proof of Theorem
2

We prove Theorem
2
by getting an upper bound on
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
and a lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
in terms of the first eigenvalue of the infinite cone
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
(defined in (
2
)). Specifically, for each
\epsilon>0
we show that there exists a
C>0
such that

(14)

\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})+C\exp\{2(1-\epsilon)\alpha/\sin(\theta/2)\},\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0,

and

(15)

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})=\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})+O(\exp\{2(1-\epsilon)\alpha/\sin(\theta/2)\}),\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0.

Combining these bounds and using that
\sin(\theta/2)\leq\theta/2
we conclude that there is a
C>0
such that
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\leq C\exp\{-4(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|/\theta\}
for all
\theta
sufficiently small. This concludes the proof of Theorem
2
.

To prove both inequality (
14
) and (
15
) we will need to use the ground state of the infinite cone
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
to construct trial functions. A direct calculation shows the
L^{2}
-normalized ground state of the Robin Laplacian on
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
is

(16)

\phi_{\theta}(x,y)=A_{\theta}e^{\alpha x/\sin(\theta/2)},\quad\text{when }\alpha<0\text{ and }\theta<\pi,

with eigenvalue

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})=-\alpha^{2}/\sin^{2}(\theta/2),

where the constant in (
16
) satisfies

(17)

A_{\theta}^{-2}=2^{-n}|\alpha|^{-n}\omega_{n-1}\Gamma(n)\tan^{n-1}(\theta/2)\sin^{n}(\theta/2).

Here
\omega_{n-1}
is the volume of the
(n-1)
-dimensional unit sphere and
\Gamma
is the Gamma function.

5.1.
Upper bound on
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
:

In order to get an upper bound on
\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
we transplant two copies of
\phi_{\theta}
to construct a trial function. Fix
0<\epsilon<1/2
and let
\chi=\chi(x,y)
denote a smooth nonnegative cutoff function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is independent of
y
, equals 1 for
x\leq 1-\epsilon
, 0 for
x\geq 1
, and is less than or equal to 1 everywhere. Let
\psi_{\theta}=\chi\phi_{\theta}
be a cut-off cone ground state and

F(x,y)=(x+1,y)\quad\text{and}\quad G(x,y)=(1-x,y)

be rigid motions. Define the trial function
\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}:\mathcal{D}_{\theta}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
by putting a cut-off cone ground state at each vertex of
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
:

\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}=\frac{(\psi_{\theta}\circ F)-(\psi_{\theta}\circ G)}{\sqrt{2}}.

Notice that
\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}
is odd with respect to
x
, by construction.

Since
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
is reflection symmetric in
x
each eigenfunction with simple eigenvalue is either even or odd in
x
. The ground state must be even since it is non-negative. Thus, the odd function
\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}
is orthogonal to the ground state, and so it is a valid trial function for

\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
. By the min-max principle,

(18)

\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}|\nabla\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}|^{2}\,dV+\alpha\int_{\partial\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}^{2}\,dS}{\int_{\mathcal{D}_{\theta}}\widetilde{\phi}_{\theta}^{2}\,dV}=\frac{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}|\nabla\psi_{\theta}|^{2}\,dV+\alpha\int_{\Sigma_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dS}{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV},

where we define

\mathcal{T}_{\theta}=\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\cap\{x<1\}\quad\text{and}\quad\Sigma_{\theta}=\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta}\cap\{0\leq x<1\}

to be the truncated cone and the curved part of its boundary.

A straightforward calculation (see below) will show there are remainder terms

\operatorname{Rem}_{i}(\theta)=O(\exp\{2(1-2\epsilon)\alpha/\sin(\theta/2)\}),\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{for }i=1,2,3,

such that

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}|\nabla\psi_{\theta}|^{2}\,dV=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}|\nabla\phi_{\theta}|^{2}\,dV+\operatorname{Rem}_{1}(\theta),\quad\int_{\Sigma_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dS=\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dS+\operatorname{Rem}_{2}(\theta),

and

\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV+\operatorname{Rem}_{3}(\theta)=1+\operatorname{Rem}_{3}(\theta),

as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, where the final equality uses the
L^{2}
-normalization of
\phi_{\theta}
. Combining these estimates with the upper bound (
18
) shows we have a remainder term
\operatorname{Rem}(\theta)=(\operatorname{Rem}_{1}(\theta)+\alpha\operatorname{Rem}_{2}(\theta))/(1+\operatorname{Rem}_{3}(\theta))=O(\exp\{2(1-2\epsilon)\alpha/\sin(\theta/2)\})
such that

\lambda_{2}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}|\nabla\phi_{\theta}|^{2}\,dV+\alpha\int_{\partial\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dS}{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV}+\operatorname{Rem}(\theta)=\frac{\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})}{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV}+\operatorname{Rem}(\theta)\leq\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})+\operatorname{Rem}(\theta),

as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, since
\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV\leq 1
and
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})<0
. This proves (
14
) since
\epsilon
is arbitrary.

We estimate
\operatorname{Rem}_{3}(\theta)
; the other remainder terms are similar. The inequality
\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV\leq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV
follows from
\chi
being less than 1. For an inequality in the opposite direction, since

\int_{T_{\theta}}\psi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV\geq\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\cap\{x<1-\epsilon\}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV-\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\cap\{x\geq 1-\epsilon\}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV,

it suffices to show the second term on the right is exponentially small. Since
\phi_{\theta}
is constant in
y
we have that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\cap\{x\geq 1-\epsilon\}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV

\displaystyle=A_{\theta}^{2}\int_{1-\epsilon}^{\infty}\exp\{2\alpha x/\sin(\theta/2)\}\text{Vol}_{n-1}(B(\tan(\theta/2)x))\,dx

\displaystyle=\Gamma(n)^{-1}\int_{2(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|/\sin(\theta/2)}^{\infty}\exp\{-z\}z^{n-1}\,dz,

where we have made the change of variables
z=2|\alpha|x/\sin(\theta/2)
and used the definition of
A_{\theta}
in (
17
). Since
\exp\{-z\}z^{n-1}\lesssim\exp\{-\frac{1-2\epsilon}{1-\epsilon}z\}
for all sufficiently large
z
, by integrating we have

\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\cap\{x\geq 1-\epsilon\}}\phi_{\theta}^{2}\,dV\lesssim\exp\{2(1-2\epsilon)\alpha/\sin(\theta/2)\},\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0,

which shows that
\operatorname{Rem}_{3}(\theta)=O(\exp\{2(1-2\epsilon)\alpha/\sin(\theta/2)\})
.

5.2.
Lower bound on
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
:

We prove the lower bound by viewing
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})-\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
as the first eigenvalue of a problem on the truncated cone
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
. Then we “push out” the problem on
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
to a radial problem on a spherical sector
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
. Finally, we transform the problem on
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
into the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem (
3
) whose solutions were analyzed in Lemma
3
. We break up the proof into four steps.

Step 1:
Let
u
denote the
L^{2}
-normalized ground state of
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
restricted and translated to
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}=\mathcal{C}_{\theta}\cap\{x<1\}
. Since the ground state of
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
is even in
x
, we know

has Neumann boundary conditions on the flat part of the boundary defined by

\Gamma_{\theta}=\overline{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\cap\{x=1\}

and retains its Robin boundary conditions on the complement
\Sigma_{\theta}=\partial\mathcal{T}_{\theta}\cap\{0\leq x<1\}
.

For notational convenience write
\phi
instead of
\phi_{\theta}
for the Robin ground state of
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}
. A direct calculation shows that the ratio
v=u/\phi
is the first eigenfunction of the
\tau
-Laplacian operator
\Delta_{\tau}(\cdot)=\tau^{-1}\text{div}(\tau\nabla(\cdot))
with weight
\tau=\phi^{2}
. More specifically,
v
satisfies

(19)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\tau}v=\mu_{1}v&\text{on }\mathcal{T}_{\theta},\\
\partial_{\nu}v+(\alpha/\sin(\theta/2))v=0&\text{on }\Gamma_{\theta},\\
\partial_{\nu}v=0&\text{on }\Sigma_{\theta},\end{cases}

where
\mu_{1}=\mu_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta})=\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})-\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})
is the first eigenvalue of (
19

is positive.

Since the weight
\phi^{2}
is uniformly positive on
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}
, the above eigenvalue problem has a discrete spectrum with Rayleigh quotient

\frac{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}|\nabla f|\phi^{2}\,dx+(\alpha/\sin(\theta/2))\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}f^{2}\phi^{2}\,dS}{\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}f^{2}\phi^{2}\,dx},\quad\text{for }f\in H^{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta})\setminus\{0\}.

Note that
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta})<0
by using the min-max principle with a constant trial function.

Step 2:
Consider the spherical coordinates
(r,\xi)
, where
\xi=(\xi_{1},\dots,\xi_{n-1})
is the vector of angle coordinates and
\xi_{1}
is the angle between the point
(x,y)
and the
x
-axis. We will show
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})\leq(1+o(1))\mu_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta})
, as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, where
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})
is the first eigenvalue of a certain radial problem on the spherical sector

\mathcal{S}_{\theta}=\{(r,\xi):0<r<\cos(\theta/2)^{-1},\penalty\ |\xi_{1}|<\theta/2\}.

Note that this sector is obtained by “pushing out” the flat part of the boundary of
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
. To define this problem on
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
let the “push in” diffeomorphism
P:\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}\rightarrow\overline{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}
be defined in spherical coordinates by

P(r,\xi)=(pr,\xi),

where
p=p(\xi_{1})=\cos(\theta/2)\sec(\xi_{1})
. Also define the Rayleigh quotient as

\frac{\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}|\nabla f|^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV+(\beta/\sin(\theta/2))\int_{P^{-1}(\Gamma_{\theta})}f^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dS}{\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}f^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV},\quad\text{for }f\in H^{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})\setminus\{0\}.

The parameter
\beta=\beta(\theta)
is equal to
(1+o(1))\alpha
and will be defined precisely at the end of Step 2. The above Rayleigh quotient corresponds to the weighted
\sigma
-Laplacian eigenvalue problem on
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
with
\sigma=\phi^{2}\circ P
, with Robin conditions on the spherical part of the boundary
P^{-1}(\Gamma_{\theta})=\partial\mathcal{S}_{\theta}\cap\partial B(\cos(\theta/2)^{-1})
, and Neumann conditions on
\Sigma_{\theta}
.

We take
f=v\circ P
as a trial function for
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})
so that

(20)

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})\leq\frac{\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}|\nabla[v\circ P]|^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV+(\beta/\sin(\theta/2))\int_{P^{-1}(\Gamma_{\theta})}[v\circ P]^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dS}{\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}[v\circ P]^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV}.

Let
J_{P}
and
J_{P^{-1}}
be the Jacobian matrices of
P
and
P^{-1}
,
I_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}
and
I_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}
be the identities on
\overline{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}
and
\overline{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}
, and
I
be the identity matrix. After writing
P
and
P^{-1}
in rectangular coordinates a calculation shows that

(21)

\lVert P-I_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}\rVert_{\infty},\penalty\ \lVert J_{P}-I\rVert_{op,\infty},\penalty\ \lVert P^{-1}-I_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}\rVert_{\infty},\penalty\ \lVert J_{P^{-1}}-I\rVert_{op,\infty}\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0.

Here
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{\infty}
is the supremum norm and
\lVert\cdot\rVert_{op,\infty}
is the supremum norm of the (pointwise) operator norm of a matrix valued function.

We calculate the denominator of (
20
) by changing variables and using (
21
) so that

\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}[v\circ P]^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV=\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}|\det(J_{P^{-1}})|\,dV=(1+o(1))\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}\,dV,\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0.

Now we prove an upper bound on the gradient term in (
20
): the chain rule gives

\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}|\nabla[v\circ P]|^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV\leq\lVert J_{P}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\int_{\mathcal{S}_{\theta}}|(\nabla v)\circ P|^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dV\leq M(\theta)\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}|\nabla v|^{2}\phi^{2}\,dV,

where
M(\theta)=\lVert J_{P}\rVert_{\infty}^{2}\lVert\det(J_{P^{-1}})\rVert_{\infty}=1+o(1)
, as
\theta\rightarrow 0
by applying the estimates in (
21
).

Finally, to handle the boundary term we prove the lower bound

(22)

\int_{P^{-1}(\Gamma_{\theta})}[v\circ P]^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dS\geq m(\theta)\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}\,dS,

where
m(\theta)=1+o(1)
as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. By parametrizing the spherical cap
P^{-1}(\Gamma_{\theta})
with the map
P^{-1}|_{\Gamma_{\theta}}
we have

\displaystyle\int_{P^{-1}(\Gamma_{\theta})}[v\circ P]^{2}(\phi^{2}\circ P)\,dS=\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}\sqrt{\det(G)}\,dy\geq m(\theta)\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}\,dy,

where
G=G(y)
is the Gramian matrix with entries
G_{ij}=\langle\partial_{i}P^{-1}|_{\Gamma_{\theta}},\partial_{j}P^{-1}|_{\Gamma_{\theta}}\rangle
with
P^{-1}|_{\Gamma_{\theta}}
written in rectangular coordinates
y_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n-1
and
m(\theta)=\min\{\sqrt{\det(G)}\}
. The estimates in (
21
) imply that
\partial_{i}P^{-1}|_{\Gamma_{\theta}}=e_{i}+o(1)
, where
e_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
standard basis vector, so that
G_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+o(1)
uniformly as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. Thus,
m(\theta)=1+o(1)
and (
22
) holds.

Applying the estimates on each part of the Rayleigh quotient to (
20

\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})\leq(1+o(1))\bigg[M(\theta)\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}|\nabla v|^{2}\phi^{2}\,dV+(\beta(\theta)m(\theta)/\sin(\theta/2))\int_{\Gamma_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}\,dS\bigg]\bigg/\int_{\mathcal{T}_{\theta}}v^{2}\phi^{2}\,dV,

as
\theta\rightarrow 0
. Choosing
\beta(\theta)=M(\theta)m(\theta)^{-1}\alpha=(1+o(1))\alpha
and factoring out the

from the numerator shows that
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})\leq(1+o(1))\mu_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta})
, as
\theta\rightarrow 0
, completing the proof of Step 2.

Step 3:
We claim that
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})
is the first eigenvalue of the problem

(23)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\sigma}w=\mu w\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ B(\cos(\theta/2)^{-1}),\\
\partial_{r}w+(\beta/\sin(\theta/2))w=0\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ \partial B(\cos(\theta/2)^{-1}),\end{cases}

where
\sigma=\phi^{2}\circ P
and
\partial_{r}
is the radial derivative (i.e. the normal derivative). Indeed,
\sigma=\exp\{2\alpha\cot(\theta/2)r\}
is radial, and so the first eigenfunction

is also radial (by uniqueness), and therefore the restriction

has Neumann boundary conditions on
\Sigma_{\theta}
. The restriction is still an eigenfunction, and since it has the correct boundary conditions and is non-negative it must be a ground state of the problem on
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
and so
\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})
is the first eigenvalue of (
23
).

Now we transfer the
\theta
dependence of
\sigma
to the domain by rescaling the radial coordinate by
r\mapstochar\rightarrow\cot(\theta/2)r
and letting
R=\sin(\theta/2)^{-1}
and
\tilde{w}(r)=w(\tan(\theta/2)r)
so that

(24)

\begin{cases}-\Delta_{\tilde{\sigma}}\tilde{w}=\nu_{1}\tilde{w}\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ B(R),\\
\partial_{r}\tilde{w}+\tilde{\beta}\tilde{w}=0\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ \partial B(R),\end{cases}

where
\tilde{\beta}=\tilde{\beta}(\theta)=\cos(\theta/2)^{-1}\beta(\theta)
,
\tilde{\sigma}(r)=\sigma(\tan(\theta/2)r)=e^{2\alpha r}
, and
\nu_{1}(R)=\tan^{2}(\theta/2)\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})
.

Step 4:
We make a final change of variables to eliminate the weight
\tilde{\sigma}
and Robin parameter
\alpha
so that (
24
) becomes the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem (
3
) with a shifted potential. A direct calculation shows that the radial function
\varphi(r)=\tilde{w}(r)/e^{-\alpha r}
satisfies the Schrödinger eigenvalue problem

\begin{cases}(-\Delta-\frac{\kappa}{r})\varphi=(\nu_{1}-\alpha^{2})\varphi\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ B(R),\\
\partial_{r}\varphi+(\tilde{\beta}-\alpha)\varphi=0\quad&\text{on}\penalty\ \partial B(R),\end{cases}

where
\kappa=(n-1)|\alpha|
. Since
\varphi\geq 0
we know that
\nu_{1}-\alpha^{2}
is the first eigenvalue of this problem.

The definition of
\tilde{\beta}
(at the end of Step 3) shows that
\tilde{\beta}-\alpha=o(1)
as
R\rightarrow\infty
. Thus,
\gamma<\tilde{\beta}-\alpha
for all
R
sufficiently large, where
\gamma\in(-|\alpha|,0)
is the fixed Robin parameter in the Schrödinger problem (
3
) with eigenvalue
E(R)
. Because the eigenvalues of Schrödinger operators are increasing functions of the Robin parameter we have that
E(R)\leq\nu_{1}(R)-\alpha^{2}
for
R
sufficiently large. Using that
R=\sin(\theta/2)^{-1}

and combining this inequality with those from Steps 1, 2, and 3, there is a constant
C>0
such that for all small

,

C(R^{2}-1)(E(R)+\alpha^{2})\leq C(R^{2}-1)\nu_{1}(R)=C\mu_{1}(\mathcal{S}_{\theta})\leq\mu_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{\theta})=\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})-\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})<0.

Fix an
\epsilon>0
. Using part (iii) of Lemma
3
, we have that
E(R)+\alpha^{2}=O(\exp\{-2(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|R\})
as
R\rightarrow\infty
. Hence

\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})-\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{C}_{\theta})=O(\exp\{-2(1-\epsilon)|\alpha|/\sin(\theta/2)\})\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0,

where this last estimate follows by using that
R=\sin(\theta/2)^{-1}
and that the factor of

multiplying
E(R)+\alpha^{2}
can be absorbed into the error term since
\epsilon
is arbitrary. Thus, (
15
) is proven.

Remark
.

To prove the lower bound for
\lambda_{1}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta})
we used the map
P^{-1}
to “push out” the truncated cone
\mathcal{T}_{\theta}
to the spherical sector
\mathcal{S}_{\theta}
. This idea was used to compute bounds on eigenvalues of thin isosceles triangles by Freitas in
[
8
, §3]
for Dirichlet boundary conditions and by Laugesen and Suideja in
[
15
, proof of Lemma 5.2]
for Neumann boundary conditions. Here we adapt the technique to Robin boundary conditions.

6.
Questions

Theorem
2
shows that Conjecture
1
fails to extend to
\alpha<0
for general convex
\mathcal{D}
. In a positive direction Laugesen
[
14
, Theorem III.8]
recently showed that Conjecture
1
holds and is sharp for all
\alpha\in(-\infty,\infty]
for rectangular boxes (i.e. products of intervals). This inspires the question:
Is there a general class of convex domains such that the gap inequality in Conjecture
1
holds when
\alpha<0
?
Since the proof of Theorem
2
appears to rely on the fact that the domain
\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
has vertices with small opening angle, we might consider minimizing
(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\cdot)
over convex domains with a uniformly bounded Lipschitz constant.

Surprisingly, it seems that the gap still fails to have a positive lower bound over this restricted class of domains. To see this we sketch an approximation argument. Define the
truncated double cone
domains

\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon}=t_{\epsilon}\{(x,y)\in\mathcal{D}_{\theta}:|x|<1-\epsilon\},\quad\text{for }\epsilon\in[0,1),

where the rescaling
t_{\epsilon}
is such that
\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon}
has diameter 2 for each
\epsilon
sufficiently small. (Note that
t_{\epsilon}=1+o(1)
, as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
.) For
\theta
fixed,
\{\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon}\}_{\epsilon\in[0,1)}
has a Lipschitz constant that is uniform in
\epsilon\in[0,1)
since the angle between the vertical faces at
x=\pm t_{\epsilon}(1-\epsilon)
of
\partial\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon}
and the remainder of the boundary does not depend on
\epsilon
. In addition,
\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon}\rightarrow\mathcal{D}_{\theta}
in the Hausdorff distance and
|\partial\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon}|\rightarrow|\partial\mathcal{D}_{\theta}|
as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
so we expect that

\lambda_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon})\rightarrow\lambda_{j}(\mathcal{D}_{\theta}),\quad\text{as }\epsilon\rightarrow 0,

for each
j\in\mathbb{N}
; see
[
9
, Remark 4.33]
for a discussion of Robin continuity results.

Assuming the above limit, we can use Theorem
2
to find a “diagonal sequence”
\epsilon(\theta)
converging to zero such that

(\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1})(\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon(\theta)})\rightarrow 0,\quad\text{as }\theta\rightarrow 0,

and
\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon(\theta)}
has diameter 2 for each
\theta
. Observe that the truncated domains
\{\mathcal{D}_{\theta,\epsilon(\theta)}\}_{\theta\in(0,\pi/2)}
also have uniformly bounded Lipschitz constants. This shows that
\lambda_{2}-\lambda_{1}
can be made arbitrarily small among convex domains of a given diameter and with a uniformly bounded Lipschitz constant. This example demonstrates the difficulty of determining a general class of domains where Conjecture
1
holds when
\alpha<0
, even with a weaker lower bound.
