Canonical extensions via fitted sublocales
Tomáš Jakl, Anna Laura Suarez

Abstract
Abstract. We build on a result stating that the frame 𝖲𝖤 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{SE}(L) of strongly exact filters for a frame L L is anti-isomorphic to the coframe 𝒮 o ​ ( L ) \mathcal{S}_{o}(L) of fitted sublocales. The collection 𝖤 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{E}(L) of exact filters of L L is known to be a sublocale of this frame. We consider several other subcollections of 𝖲𝖤 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{SE}(L) : the collections 𝒥 ⁡ ( 𝖢𝖯 ⁡ ( L ) ) \mathcal{J}(\mathsf{CP}(L)) and 𝒥 ⁡ ( 𝖲𝖮 ⁡ ( L ) ) \mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L)) of intersections of completely prime and Scott-open filters, respectively, and the collection 𝖱 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{R}(L) of regular elements of the frame of filters. We show that all of these are sublocales of 𝖲𝖤 ⁡ ( L ) \mathsf{SE}(L) , and as such they correspond to subcolocales of 𝒮 o ​ ( L ) \mathcal{S}_{o}(L) , which all turn out to have a concise description. By using the theory of polarities of Birkhoff, one can show that all of the structures mentioned above enjoy universal properties which are variations of that of the canonical extension. We also show how some of these subcollections can be described as polarities and give three new equivalent definitions of subfitness in terms of the lattice of filters.

Canonical extension is a type of completion of ordered structures, such as Boolean algebras, distributive lattices, and posets which retains many properties of the original structure. Canonical extensions were originally constructed by Jonssón and Tarski for Boolean algebras

Canonical extensions are invaluable when extending Stone duality (and its variant) to algebras with additional operators, such as modalities.

Our main reference is the book

f^{-1}[B]=\{x\in X\mid f(x)\in B\}
for the preimage of

.

Further, by a
filter

F
of a lattice
L
we mean a non-empty upwards closed subset
F\subseteq L
which is closed under finite meets. We say that
F

2.1
Frames

A
frame
is a complete lattice
L
satisfying the following distributivity law.

(\forall A\subseteq L,b\in L)\qquad(\bigvee A)\wedge b=\bigvee\{a\wedge b\mid a\in A\}

This law implies that every frame is also a Heyting algebra, with the Heyting implication obtained as the right adjoint to meets, i.e.
a\wedge b\leq c
iff
a\leq b\to c
for any
a,b,c\in L
.
However,
frame homomorphisms
are only required to preserve infinite suprema and finite infima, including
0
and
1
, respectively. Heyting implication is typically not preserved.

Points
of a frame
L
can be defined in three equivalent ways. They are given as

•

frame homomorphisms
L\to\mathbf{2}
, where
\mathbf{2}=(0<1)
is the two-element frame,

•

completely prime filters
P\subseteq L
, that is, proper filters on
L
which satisfy, for any
A\subseteq L
, that
\bigvee A\in P
implies that
a\in P
for some
a\in A
, or

•

prime elements
p\in L
, that is, elements such that
x\wedge y\leq p
implies
x\leq p
or
y\leq p
.

Every completely prime filter
P
uniquely determines a frame homomorphism
L\to\mathbf{2}
as the characteristic function of
P
and, on the other hand, the prime element
p
corresponding to
P
is obtained by the join
\bigvee\{a\in L\mid a\notin P\}
.

The set of points
\mathsf{pt}(L)
of
L
can be endowed with a topology consisting of opens of the form
\{P\mid a\in P\}
for some
a\in L
. Conversely, given a topological space
X=(X,\tau)
, its lattice of opens
\Omega(X)=\tau
ordered by set inclusion is a frame.
Frames for which
\Omega(\mathsf{pt}(L))\cong L
are called
spatial
. Conversely, spaces for which
\mathsf{pt}(\Omega(X))
is homeomorphic to
X
are called
sober
.

2.2
Locales and sublocales

The category of frames and frame homomorphisms is dual to the category of topological spaces in the sense that the operations
\Omega(\,\text{-}\,)
and
\mathsf{pt}(\,\text{-}\,)
extend to contravariant adjoint functors.
The geometric point of view is retained if, instead, we work with the category of
locales
. Objects of this category are still frames (usually referred to as locales) and morphisms are
localic maps
, that is, maps
f\colon L\to M
such that their (unique) left adjoint is a frame homomorphism
h\colon M\to L
: we have
h(a)\leq b
iff
a\leq f(b)
for
a\in M,b\in L
.

The generalisation of the notion of a subspace of a space is that of a
sublocale
. Similarly to subspaces, sublocales correspond to images of injective localic maps. Equivalently, these are subsets
S\subseteq L
such that

(S1)

S
is closed under all meets in
L
, and

(S2)

for every
s\in S
and
a\in L
,
a\to s\in S
.

The collection
\mathcal{S}(L)
of all sublocales of
L
is a complete lattice, with meets
\bigwedge_{i}S_{i}
given by intersections
\bigcap_{i}S_{i}
and joins
\bigvee_{i}S_{i}
given by
\{\bigwedge A\mid A\subseteq\bigcup_{i}S_{i}\}
.

Every
a\in L
, induces the
open sublocale

\mathfrak{o}(a)
corresponding to
a
and, its complement in
\mathcal{S}(L)
, the
closed sublocale

\mathfrak{c}(a)
. Being defined by

\mathfrak{o}(a)=\{a\to b\mid b\ \in L\}=\{x\in L\mid a\to x=x\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathfrak{c}(a)=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua,

they exhibit a lot of expected properties of open and closed subspaces, for example:

\displaystyle\mathfrak{o}(1)=L

\displaystyle\qquad\mathfrak{o}(0)=\mathsf{O}

\displaystyle\textstyle\bigvee_{i}\mathfrak{o}(a_{i})=\mathfrak{o}(\bigvee_{i}a_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\mathfrak{o}(a)\cap\mathfrak{o}(b)=\mathfrak{o}(a\wedge b)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}(1)=\mathsf{O}

\displaystyle\qquad\mathfrak{c}(0)=L

\displaystyle\textstyle\bigcap_{i}\mathfrak{c}(a_{i})=\mathfrak{c}(\bigvee_{i}a_{i})

\displaystyle\qquad\mathfrak{c}(a)\vee\mathfrak{c}(b)=\mathfrak{c}(a\wedge b)

Here
\mathsf{O}=\{1\}
and

L

represent the smallest and largest sublocales of
L
, respectively. This means that
\mathfrak{c}(a)\cap\mathfrak{o}(a)=\mathsf{O}
and
\mathfrak{c}(a)\vee\mathfrak{o}(a)=L
.

2.3
Coframes, subcolocales, and supplements

We also make use of notions dual to those introduced above. For example, we say that a poset

is a

The coHeyting implication in a coframe

Then, similarly to the

An important example of a coframe is

In this text we aim to prioritise the geometric point of view on filters and we think of a filter

H\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}G=\{a\in L\mid\forall b\in G.\ b\vee a\in H\}

3
Canonical extensions and polarities

3.1
Canonical extensions

The motivation for our work comes from canonical extensions of (bounded) distributive lattices. Given such lattice
D
, its canonical extension is an embedding
e\colon D\hookrightarrow D^{\delta}
into a complete lattice
D^{\delta}
such that:

(D)

Every element of
D^{\delta}
is a join of meets and a meet of joins of elements in the image
e[D]
.

(C)

If
\bigwedge e[F]\leq\bigvee e[I]
for some filter
F\subseteq D
and ideal
I\subseteq D
, then
F\cap I\not=\emptyset
.

The existence, unicity and universal properties of
D\hookrightarrow D^{\delta}
follows from the theory of polarities which we discuss in Section
3.2
below.

3.1.1
Canonical extensions of frames.

The elements of the form
\bigwedge e[F]
and
\bigvee e[I]
, for a filter
F\subseteq D
and ideal
I\subseteq D
, play an important role in the definition above. In fact, axiom (D) equivalently says that every element of
D^{\delta}
is both a join of elements of the form
\bigwedge e[F]
and a meet elements of the form
\bigvee e[I]
.

Recall, from Stone duality for distributive lattices, that ideals correspond to opens of the spectral space
X
dual to
D
, i.e.
\mathsf{Idl}(D)\cong\Omega(X)

[
Pri84
]
. Similarly, filters correspond to compact saturated subsets of
X
. However, by the Hofmann–Mislove Theorem
[
HM81
]
, compact saturated subsets are in a correspondence with Scott-open filters on the frame
\Omega(X)
, that is, filters
F
such that for any directed family
\{a_{i}\}_{i}
such that
\bigvee_{i}^{\uparrow}a_{i}\in F
there is some

such that
a_{i}\in F
.

This inspired the frame-theoretic definition of canonical extensions of frames in
[
Jak20
]
. Given a frame
L
, we define its canonical extension to be a monotone mapping
e\colon L\to L^{\mathsf{SO}}
such that:

(D
SO
)

Every element of
L^{\mathsf{SO}}
is a join of elements of the form
\bigwedge e[F]
for some Scott-open filter
F\subseteq L
and a meet of elements of the form
e(a)
for some
a\in L
.

(C
SO
)

If
\bigwedge e[F]\leq e(a)
for some Scott-open filter
F\subseteq L
and
a\in L
, then
a\in F
.

Observe that by the above discussion we have that
D^{\delta}\cong\mathsf{Idl}(D)^{\mathsf{SO}}
and so canonical extensions of frames generalises canonical extensions for distributive lattices. Similarly to distributive lattices, the canonical extension
L\to L^{\mathsf{SO}}
always exists and is unique up to isomorphism. In case when
L=\Omega(X)
for some space

,

is simply the embedding
\Omega(X)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Up}(X)
where
\mathsf{Up}(X)
is the poset of saturated subsets, that is, upsets in the specialization order (cf. Example 3.5 in
[
Jak20
]
).

Remark 3.1
.

In the above discussion we omitted an important part of the theory which concerns with extensions of (not necessarily monotone) maps between distributive lattices

to maps between their canonical extensions

3.2
Polarities

The theory of polarities goes back all the way to Birkhoff
[
Bir48
, Chapter V]
. It can be used for presenting different types of completions of posets known from the literature. We use polarities for generalisations of canonical extensions, and showing their existence, uniqueness and universal properties.

3.2.1

A

is a tuple
P=(X,Y,Z)
where

is a relation between sets

Y

\displaystyle p\colon\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)\to\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y),\quad M\mapsto\{y\in Y~|~\forall x\in M.\ xZy\}

\displaystyle q\colon\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y)\to\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X),\quad N\mapsto\{x\in X~|~\forall y\in N.\ xZy\}

Note that these maps are adjoint to each other, since

3.2.4
Computing inside complete lattices.

We finish this overview of the general theory of polarities by a result that allows us to give concrete description of
\mathcal{G}(P)
by computing it as a certain closure within a complete lattice.
To this end, given a subset
S
of a complete lattice
C
, define the following closure and interior operators:

\displaystyle\mathsf{cl}_{S}:

\displaystyle\ C\to C

\displaystyle\mathsf{int}_{S}:

\displaystyle\ C\to C

\displaystyle c\mapsto\bigwedge\{s\in S\mid c\leq s\}

\displaystyle c\mapsto\bigvee\{s\in S\mid s\leq c\}

Furthermore, write

\mathcal{J}(S)\subseteq C\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}(S)\subseteq C

(3.2.2)

for the closure of
S
under all joins and meets in
C
, respectively.

Proposition 3.10
.

For
X,Y\subseteq C
where
C
is a complete lattice and
\leq
is the order of
C
,

\mathcal{G}(X,Y,\leq)\cong\mathsf{int}_{X}[\mathcal{M}(Y)]\cong\mathsf{cl}_{Y}[\mathcal{J}(X)]

and, consequently, also

\mathcal{G}(X,Y,\leq)\cong\mathcal{G}(X,\mathcal{M}(Y),\leq)\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{J}(X),Y,\leq)\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{J}(X),\mathcal{M}(Y),\leq).

Proof.

Recall from §
3.2.2
that
\mathcal{G}(X,Y,\leq)
can be concretely computed as the image
q[\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y)]
for
p
and
q
as in §
3.2.1
, induced by
Z
set to
{\leq}
. Therefore,
q[\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y)]
consists of the sets of the form

\{x\in X\mid x\leq\bigwedge N\}\qquad\kern 5.0pt\text{for some}\kern 5.0ptN\subseteq Y.

Define
\alpha\colon q[\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(Y)]\to\mathsf{int}_{X}[\mathcal{M}(Y)]
by sending
S
to
\bigvee S
. Observe that this is a well-defined map. Indeed, for any
N\subseteq Y
, we have
\alpha(q(N))=\mathsf{int}_{X}(\bigwedge N)
and, for
N,N^{\prime}\subseteq Y
, we have that
q(N)=q(N^{\prime})
if and only if, for any
x\in X
,
x\leq\bigwedge N
iff
x\leq\bigwedge N^{\prime}
.

Next we show that
\alpha
is an isomorphism of posets. Since
\alpha
is clearly monotone, it is enough to show that it is onto and order-reflective. For the latter, assume
\alpha(q(N))\leq\alpha(q(N^{\prime})
for some
N,N^{\prime}\subseteq Y
and let
x\in q(N)
. Then, by definition,
x\leq\alpha(q(N))\leq\alpha(q(N^{\prime}))\leq\bigwedge N^{\prime}
, giving us
x\in q(N^{\prime})
. Therefore
q(N)\subseteq q(N^{\prime})
. For surjectivity, it is enough to observe that a typical element of
\mathsf{int}_{X}[\mathcal{M}(Y)]
is of the form
\mathsf{int}_{X}(\bigwedge N)=\alpha(q(N))
for some
N\subseteq Y
. We have shown that
\mathcal{G}(X,Y,\leq)
is isomorphic to
\mathsf{int}_{X}[\mathcal{M}(Y)]
.

From this it follows that
\mathcal{G}(X,Y,\leq)\cong\mathcal{G}(X,\mathcal{M}(Y),\leq)
.
By Corollary
3.5
we also obtain the dual statement as
\mathcal{G}(X,Y,\leq)^{op}\cong\mathcal{G}(Y,X,\geq)\cong\mathsf{cl}_{Y}[\mathcal{J}(X)]
, viewed as a sub-poset of
C^{op}
.
∎

3.2.5
The isomorphism concretely.

Observe that the proof gives us that the formula for the isomorphism is given by the closure operation

\mathsf{cl}_{Y}:\mathsf{int}_{X}[\mathcal{M}(Y)]\xrightarrow{\kern 4.09723pt\cong\kern 4.09723pt}\mathsf{cl}_{Y}[\mathcal{J}(X)].

Indeed, for
N=p(q(N))
i.e.
N=\{y\in Y\mid\mathsf{int}_{X}(\bigwedge N)\leq y\}
and for the mapping
\beta\colon p[\raisebox{2.03998pt}{\Large$\wp$}(X)]\to\mathsf{cl}_{Y}[\mathcal{J}(X)]
,
S\mapsto\bigwedge S
(the map defined dually to
\alpha
), we have that
\mathsf{cl}_{Y}(\alpha(q(N)))=\mathsf{cl}_{Y}(\mathsf{int}_{X}(\bigwedge N))=\bigwedge N=\beta(N)
.

By a dual argument, the inverse map in the opposite direction is given by the interior operation

\mathsf{int}_{X}:\mathsf{cl}_{Y}[\mathcal{J}(X)]\xrightarrow{\kern 4.09723pt\cong\kern 4.09723pt}\mathsf{int}_{X}[\mathcal{M}(Y)].

), Corollary

below shows that these are in fact subcolocale inclusions.

5.1
Scott-open filters

We start by remarking that joins of Scott-open filters are strongly exact. The base case of this fact is due to Johnstone (Lemma 3.4 in
[
Joh85
]
, see also
[
Esc03
]
and
[
Vic97
]
) who showed that Scott-open filters are strongly exact.

Johnstone’s proof uses a transfinite induction on nuclei, whereas the proof in
[
Esc03
]
uses Pataraia’s fixed-point theorem for pre-nuclei and
[
Vic97
]
is written in terms of generalised points of powerlocales. We report here a simple explicit proof of the result which, on the other hand, uses Zorn’s Lemma.

Lemma 5.1
.

In a frame
L
and for
x,y,z\in L
we have
x\to y=y
if and only if
z>y
implies
z\wedge x\nleq y
.

Proof.

Note that
x\to y=y
if and only if
z\nleq y
implies that
z\wedge x\nleq y
. We claim that this is equivalent to having
z>y
implying
z\wedge x\nleq y
. It is clear that the first condition implies the second. For the converse, if
z\nleq y
, then we have
z\vee y>y
. Since the second condition holds, we have
(z\vee y)\wedge x=(z\wedge x)\vee(y\wedge x)\nleq y
, and so we must have
z\wedge x\nleq y
.
∎

Proposition 5.2
.

Scott-open filters are strongly exact.

Proof.

Suppose that
F
is a Scott-open filter of a frame
L
, and that
x\notin F
. Our argument will show that
x
cannot be a strongly exact meet of elements in F. First, we claim that there must be some
y\in L
which is maximal among the elements of
{\uparrow}x
which are not in
F
. If this were
not the case, then by Zorn’s Lemma we could find an infinite ascending chain
x\leq x_{1}<...<x_{n}<...
, outside of
F
but with supremum in
F
. This would contradict Scott-openness of
F
. Then, let
m\in L
be such an element, and in particular, observe
\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mum\cap F=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mum\setminus\{m\}
. Suppose that
x=\bigwedge_{i}x_{i}
with
\{x_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq F
, we show that the meet cannot be strongly exact. Observe that, for each
i\in I
, and for
z>m
, we must have
z\wedge x_{i}\nleq m
, because
z\wedge x_{i}\in F
and
m\notin F
. Then, by Lemma
5.1
,
x_{i}\to m=m
and, consequently,
m\in\bigcap_{i}\mathfrak{o}(x_{i})
. On the other hand, since
x\leq m
, we have
x\to m=1\neq m
and
m\notin\mathfrak{o}(x)
. Then,
x
can never be an exact meet of elements in
F
.
∎

Because strongly exact filters are closed under intersection (Lemma
4.4
), Proposition
5.2
entails the desired inclusion from (
1.0.1
).

Corollary 5.3
.

\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L))\subseteq\mathsf{SE}(L)
.

5.2
Completely prime filters

The class
\mathsf{CP}(L)
of completely prime filters is not, in general, closed under intersections. It is clear that we have
\mathsf{CP}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{SO}(L)
. In this special case we can actually describe the filter extension in terms of the dual space
\mathsf{pt}(L)
of the frame
L
. In the following, we regard the points of
\mathsf{pt}(L)
as completely prime filters
\mathsf{CP}(L)
.

Lemma 5.4
.

\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{CP}(L))\cong\mathcal{U}(\mathsf{pt}(L))
where, for a topological space
X
,
\mathcal{U}(X)
is the lattice of upsets in the specialisation order of
X
. The isomorphism is given by

P\mapsto\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muP=\{Q\in\mathsf{CP}(L)\mid P\subseteq Q\}.

Proof.

First, we observe that every
Q\in\mathsf{CP}(L)
is completely join prime in
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
, that is,
Q\sqsubseteq\bigsqcup_{i}F_{i}
(i.e.
\bigcap_{i}F_{i}\subseteq Q
) implies
Q\sqsubseteq F_{i}
(i.e.
F_{i}\subseteq Q
) for some
i
. Assume by contradiction that, for every
i
,
Q\not\sqsubseteq F_{i}
i.e. there is some
a_{i}\in F_{i}\setminus Q
. Then,
\bigvee_{i}a_{i}\in\bigcap_{i}F_{i}\subseteq Q
and so
a_{i}\in Q
for some
i
, a contradiction.

Observe that this shows that the map described in the statement is a surjection: any upset
\{P_{i}\mid i\in I\}
of completely prime filters is equal to
\{Q\in\mathsf{CP}(L)\mid\bigcap_{i}P_{i}\subseteq Q\}
. From the definition, it is also clear that it is an injection which preserves and reflects order. Therefore, it is an isomorphism.
∎

5.3
Exact filters

The fact that exact filters are a special kind of strongly exact filters

\mathsf{E}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{SE}(L)

has been observed earlier (cf. Remark 3.5 in
[
MPS20
]
).
We also make comparison of exact filters with regular filters, which we introduce later on. For that we need the following new characterisation of exact filters.

Proposition 5.5
.

For a filter
F\subseteq L
is exact if and only if it is an intersection of filters of the form

\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muy\mathbin{\raisebox{0.81778pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux=\{a\in L\mid y\leq a\vee x\}

for some
x,y\in L
. In fact,
F=\bigcap\{\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muy\mathbin{\raisebox{0.81778pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux\mid\forall f\in F\ y\leq f\vee x\}
which rewrites as

F=\{a\in L\mid\forall x,y\in L\ (\forall f\in F\ y\leq f\vee x)\Rightarrow y\leq a\vee x\}.

(ex)

Proof.

From the right-to-left implication assume
\bigwedge_{i}a_{i}
is an exact meet with
\{a_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq F
. Let
x,y
be such that for every
f
in
F
,
y\leq f\vee x
. Then, in particular, for every
i
,
y\leq a_{i}\vee x
. Therefore,
y\leq\bigwedge_{i}(a_{i}\vee x)=(\bigwedge_{i}a_{i})\vee x
and so
\bigwedge_{i}a_{i}\in F
.

Conversely, denote by
G
the right-hand side of (
ex
) above. We wish to prove
F=G
. Observe that
F\subseteq G
is immediate from the definition. For the converse inclusion, let
a\in G
. In order to show
a\in F
, it is enough to prove that

(a)

a=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a)
, and

(b)

the meet in (a) above is exact.

For (a) we only need to show
\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a)\leq a
. Taking
y=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a)
and
x=a
, observe that we have
y\leq f\vee a
for every
f\in F
. Therefore, since
a\in G
, we have the desired
y=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a)\leq a\vee x=a
.

For (b) let
b\in L
be arbitrary. We need to show that

a\vee b=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a)\vee b\geq\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a\vee b)

where the first equality holds by (1). Set
x=a\vee b
and
y=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a\vee b)
. Observe that trivially, for every
f^{\prime}\in F
,
y\leq f^{\prime}\vee x
. Therefore, since
a\in G
, the desired inequality follows:
y=\bigwedge_{f\in F}(f\vee a\vee b)\leq a\vee x=a\vee b
.
∎

5.4
Closed and regular filters

Finally, we introduce a class of filters that (as we explain in Section
9
) corresponds to fittings of closed sublocales. Define

•

closed filters

\mathsf{C}(L)
as the filters of the form
\{x\in L\mid a\vee x=1\}
for some
a\in L
.

As usual, we order
\mathsf{C}(L)
by the reverse inclusion order, denoted by
\sqsubseteq
. It turns out that closed filters are the supplements of principal filters in
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
:

\displaystyle(\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua)^{\#}=\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua=\{x\in L\mid\forall a^{\prime}\geq a.\ x\vee a^{\prime}=1\}=\{x\in L\mid x\vee a=1\}.

(5.4.1)

The class of intersections of closed filters has a very natural description. Let

•

regular filters

\mathsf{R}(L)
be the filters of the form
\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}F
for some filter
F\subseteq L
.

Lemma 5.6
.

Regular filters coincide with intersections of closed filters:

\mathsf{R}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L)).

Proof.

Recall from (
5.4.1
) that the closed filters are of the form
\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua
for some
a\in L
. Then, for a subset
A\subseteq L
, since difference reverts meets in the second coordinate into joins, we obtain that
\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}(\bigsqcap_{a\in A}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua)=\bigsqcup_{a\in A}(\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua)
. The result follows from the fact that every filter
G\in\mathsf{Filt}(L)
is equal to the meet
\bigsqcap_{b\in G}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mub
in
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
.
∎

The
Booleanization

\mathfrak{B}(L)
of a frame
L
is a standard construction in frame theory. It is the sublocale
\mathfrak{B}(L)\subseteq L
consisting of all regular elements of
L
, that is, the elements of the form
a\to 0
.
When we dualise the situation to coframes, the (co)Booleanization
\mathfrak{B}(C)
of a coframe
C
consists of all supplements
a^{\#}
, i.e. elements of the form
1\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}a
.

In case when the coframe is
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
, by definition
\mathsf{R}(L)
is defined precisely as
\mathfrak{B}(\mathsf{Filt}(L))
.
Therefore, from being a subcolocale,
\mathsf{R}(L)
is closed under joins (intersections) in
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
and
\mathsf{R}(L)\cong L^{\mathsf{R}}
.
Consequently, we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.7
.

For a frame
L
,
L^{\mathsf{C}}
and
L^{\mathsf{R}}
is isomorphic to the Booleanization of
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
.

Lastly, observe that, as a consequence of Proposition
5.5
and the fact that exact filters are closed under intersections, by Lemma
4.4
, we have the following inclusions.

\mathsf{C}(L)\kern 5.0pt\subseteq\kern 5.0pt\mathsf{R}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L))\kern 5.0pt\subseteq\kern 5.0pt\mathsf{E}(L)

5.5
Subcolocale inclusions of filters

Recall that we view classes of filters in (
1.0.1
) as certain filter extensions. Consequently, we give a general condition for a filter extension
L^{\mathcal{F}}
, viewed concretely as
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{F})
(cf. Proposition
4.3
), to be a subcolocale of
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
. Unlike in
[
Jak20
]
we do not have to impose any requirements on
L
.

We show that, for any class
\mathcal{F}
of filters, in order for
L^{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathsf{Filt}(L)
to be a subcolocale inclusion it is enough for the following following simple property to hold.

\displaystyle\forall F\in\mathcal{F}\ \ \forall a\in L,\ F\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua=\{x\mid x\vee a\in F\}\in\mathcal{F}.

(scl)

Note that all classes of filters that we’ve looked at so far satisfy this property.

Lemma 5.8
.

\mathcal{F}
satisfies (
scl
) if it is the class of

1.

closed filters
\mathsf{C}(L)
,

2.

Scott-open filters
\mathsf{SO}(L)
,

3.

completely prime filters
\mathsf{CP}(L)
,

4.

exact filters
\mathsf{E}(L)
, or

5.

strongly exact filters
\mathsf{SE}(L)
.

Proof.

(1) If
F_{d}=\{x\mid x\vee d=1\}
is a closed filter, then
F_{d}\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muc=\{x\mid x\vee c\in F\}=\{x\mid x\vee c\vee d=1\}=F_{c\vee d}
is a closed filter too.
(2) If
F
is Scott-open and a directed join
\bigvee_{i}a_{i}
is in
F\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muc
then the directed join
\bigvee_{i}(a_{i}\vee c)=(\bigvee_{i}a_{i})\vee c
is in
F
. Hence, for some
i
,
a_{i}\vee c
is in
F
, giving that
a_{i}\in F\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muc
.
(3) is proved analogously to (2).

(4) Let
F
be an exact filter and assume that
\{x_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq F\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muc
has an exact meet. Observe that
\bigwedge_{i}x_{i}\in F\setminus\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muc
because, for every
i
,
x_{i}\vee c\in F
and so
(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})\vee c=\bigwedge_{i}(x_{i}\vee c)\in F
since
F
is exact and
\bigwedge_{i}(x_{i}\vee c)
is an exact meet.

(5) Proposition 3.4 in
[
MPS20
]
says that if
\{x_{i}\}_{i}
has a strongly exact meet then so has
\{x_{i}\vee c\}_{i}
, for any
c
, and also
\bigwedge_{i}(x_{i}\vee c)=(\bigwedge_{i}x_{i})\vee c
. The rest of the proof goes as for (4).
∎

The following fact about the closure of a subset of a coframe under joins is needed in the theorem below.

Lemma 5.9
.

Assume that
C
is a coframe and
S\subseteq C
satisfies

\displaystyle\forall c\in C\ \forall s\in S,\ s\mathbin{\raisebox{0.81778pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}c\in\mathcal{J}(S)

(5.5.1)

then
\mathcal{J}(S)\subseteq C
is a subcolocale inclusion.

Proof.

Recall that coHeyting difference
\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}
preserves joins in the first component. Hence,
\mathcal{J}(S)
is stable under
(\,\text{-}\,)\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}c
since, for any
A\subseteq S
,

(\bigvee A)\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}c=\bigvee\{a\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}c\mid a\in A\}

and this is in
\mathcal{J}(S)
by (
5.5.1
).
∎

With this we easily see that (
scl
) suffices for
L^{\mathcal{F}}
to be a coframe. In fact we have more, it is a subcolocale of
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
.

Theorem 5.10
.

For a frame
L
, if a collection
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathsf{Filt}(L)
satisfies (
scl
) then the inclusion
L^{\mathcal{F}}\subseteq\mathsf{Filt}(L)
is a subcolocale inclusion.

Proof.

Recall that coHeyting difference reverses meets into joins in the second component. Then, since joins of filters are intersections, for any
G\in\mathsf{Filt}(L)
and
F\in\mathcal{F}
,

F\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}G=\bigcap\{F\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua\mid a\in G\}.

Therefore, by (
scl
) the assumptions of Lemma
5.9
are met for joins of
\mathcal{F}
in
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
, i.e. for
L^{\mathcal{F}}=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{F})
.
∎

Now by Lemma
5.8
and Theorem
5.10
we see that all classes of filters appearing in the diagram in (
1.0.1
) are subcolocales of
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
. Furthermore, upon recalling that if
S,T\subseteq C
are subcolocales of a coframe
C
and
S\subseteq T
then the inclusion
S\subseteq T
is a subcolocale inclusion as well, we obtain the following.

Corollary 5.11
.

All classes of filters in (
1.0.1

5.6
Topological properties and classes of filters

We now show some characterizations of frame theoretical properties in terms of the collections of filters we have discussed so far.

Recall that a frame
L
is
subfit
if
a\leq b
whenever, for every
c\in L
,
a\vee c=1
implies
b\vee c=1
.

Proposition 5.12
.

For a frame
L
we have the following.

•

L
is pre-spatial (i.e.
\mathsf{SO}(L)
-separable) iff
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L))
contains all principal filters.

•

L
is spatial (i.e.
\mathsf{CP}(L)
-separable) iff
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{CP}(L))
contains all principal filters.

•

L
is subfit iff
\mathsf{R}(L)
contains all principal filters.

Proof.

Notice that all three statements are special cases of Proposition
4.5
, with
\mathcal{F}
chosen to be the collection
\mathsf{SO}(L)
in the first case, the collection
\mathsf{CP}(L)
in the second, and the collection of filters of the form
\{x\in L:x\vee a=1\}
in the third. Indeed, we have
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L))=\mathsf{R}(L)
by Proposition
5.6
.
∎

Proposition 5.13
.

A frame is subfit if and only if all exact filters are regular.

Proof.

First, suppose that
L
is a subfit frame. By the characterization in Proposition
5.5
, it suffices to show that filters of the form

\{x\in L\mid b\leq x\vee a\}

are equal to the intersection of the closed filters above them. Consider, then, a filter
F
of the form above. Suppose that we have
z\in L
such that
z\in C
whenever
C
is a closed filter such that
F\subseteq C
. We show that
z\in F
. In order to show
b\leq z\vee a
, by subfitness, it suffices to show that
b\vee u=1
implies that
z\vee a\vee u=1
for all
u\in L
. Suppose, then, that the antecedent holds. Consider the closed filter
C=\{x\in L:x\vee a\vee u=1\}
. We claim that
F\subseteq C
. If
b\leq x\vee a
, then because we assumed the antecedent above we have
x\vee a\vee u=1
and so
x\in C
. So, indeed
F\subseteq C
. By our assumptions, this means
z\in C
or, in other words,
z\vee a\vee u=1
.

If, conversely, all exact filters are regular, in particular all principal filters are, and the frame is subfit by item (3) of Proposition
5.12
.
∎

We obtain a proof of Theorem 3.2 of
[
BMP20
]
and its converse, which can be thought of as the filter-only version of Theorem 3.5 in
[
PPT19
]
, without the detour to sublocales.

Corollary 5.14
.

A frame is subfit if and only if
\mathsf{E}(L)
is Boolean.

Proof.

We have seen above that all regular filters are exact for any frame. If
L
is subfit, by Proposition
5.13
we also have
\mathsf{E}(L)=\mathsf{R}(L)
, and so
\mathsf{E}(L)
is Boolean. Conversely, if
\mathsf{E}(L)
is Boolean,
\mathsf{R}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{E}(L)
implies that
\mathsf{R}(L)=\mathsf{E}(L)
, as the Booleanization is maximal among the Boolean sub(co)locales.
∎

6
Relating fitted sublocales and filters

In this section we connect the constructions of Galois closed sets arising from polarities of filters with recent important sublocale-based constructions of discretisations of a frame.
A common way (see e.g. in Section 4.2 of

First we connect our constructions with a prominent recent result representing fitted sublocales as strongly exact filters. In

Fitted joins.

\textstyle\bigwedge^{\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)}A\kern 5.0pt=\kern 5.0pt\bigwedge^{\mathcal{S}(L)}A\kern 5.0pt=\kern 5.0pt\bigcap A\quad\text{and}\quad{\textstyle\bigvee^{\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)}\,}A\kern 5.0pt=\kern 5.0pt\mathtt{fit}(\bigvee^{\mathcal{S}(L)}A).

(6.1.1)

To distinguish the joins in
\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
from those in
\mathcal{S}(L)
, we call the former ones
fitted joins
.
Then, for a set
M\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
, we write

\mathcal{FJ}(M)=\{{\textstyle\bigvee^{\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)}\,}A\mid A\subseteq M\}

for the closure of
M
under fitted joins in
\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
.

6.2
Smooth sublocales

In
[
PPT19
]
, the authors introduced the lattice

\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(L)

consisting
joins of closed sublocales
, i.e. sublocales of the form
\bigvee_{a\in M}\mathfrak{c}(a)
for some
M\subseteq L
. Among others,
[
PPT19
]
shows that that strongly exact filters correspond to
\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)
. In fact, Theorem
6.2
above is inspired by this fact. However, the isomorphism
\mathsf{E}(L)\cong\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)
takes
\mathsf{E}(L)
in the usual subset order
\subseteq
.

Therefore, the isomorphism from
[
PPT19
]
cannot be obtained as a restriction of Theorem
6.2
along the inclusion
\mathsf{E}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{SE}(L)
.
In fact, in
[
MPS20
]
it is proven that this restriction gives us fittings of supplements of joins of closed sublocales on the other side, i.e. Theorem
6.2
restricts to
\mathsf{E}(L)\cong\mathtt{fit}[(\mathcal{S}_{c}(L))^{\#}]
.
In the following lines we show that sublocales in
\mathtt{fit}[(\mathcal{S}_{c}(L))^{\#}]
are precisely fittings of smooth sublocales, from
[
Arr21
]
.

Observe that the filters arising in Proposition
5.5
are the filters associated with
locally closed sublocales
, i.e. sublocales of the form
\mathfrak{c}(x)\cap\mathfrak{o}(y)
. Indeed,

\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(x)\cap\mathfrak{o}(y))=\{a\mid y\leq a\vee x\}=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muy\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux

(6.2.1)

since
\mathfrak{c}(x)\cap\mathfrak{o}(y)=\mathfrak{o}(y)\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathfrak{o}(x)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)
iff
\mathfrak{o}(y)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)\vee\mathfrak{o}(x)=\mathfrak{o}(a\vee x)
. Therefore, it is justified calling filters of the form
\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0muy\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mux

locally closed filters
. We denote by

{\mathsf{L}\mathsf{C}}(L)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{S}_{lc}(L)

the class of locally closed filters and locally closed sublocales, respectively. By the same proof as in the first paragraph of Proposition
5.5
we see that
{\mathsf{L}\mathsf{C}}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{E}(L)
. Therefore, recalling (
4.1.1
) Proposition
5.5
immediately yields.

Lemma 6.3
.

Exact filters are the joins of locally closed filters, i.e.
\mathsf{E}(L)=\mathcal{J}({\mathsf{L}\mathsf{C}}(L))
.

On the other hand, since locally closed filters are in particular strongly exact filters and since the adjunction
\mathsf{fi}\dashv\mathsf{su}
restricts to an isomorphism on strongly exact filters, the description of locally closed filters from (
6.2.1
) immediately yields the following.

Lemma 6.4
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to
{\mathsf{L}\mathsf{C}}(L)\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{lc}(L)]
.

Observe that the isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
translates joins of strongly exact filters to fitted joins of the fitted sublocales. Therefore, by the previous two lemmas, exact filters correspond precisely to fitted joins of the fittings of locally closed sublocales. The following significantly simplifies the description of sublocales arising this way.

Lemma 6.5
.

Given a class of sublocales
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(L)
, fitted joins of fittings of sublocales in
\mathcal{A}
coincide with fittings of joins of sublocales in
\mathcal{A}
, i.e.
\mathcal{FJ}(\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{A}])=\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{A})]
.

Proof.

Given
\{S_{i}\}_{i}\subseteq\mathcal{A}
, the fitted join
{\textstyle\bigvee^{\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)}_{i}\,}\mathtt{fit}(S_{i})
is just the meet of sublocales
\mathtt{fit}(\bigvee\nolimits_{i}\,\mathtt{fit}(S_{i}))=\bigcap\{\mathfrak{o}(a)\mid\bigvee\nolimits_{i}\,\mathtt{fit}(S_{i})\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)\}
and we have that

\displaystyle\bigvee\nolimits_{i}\,\mathtt{fit}(S_{i})\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)

\displaystyle\iff\forall i\ S_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)

\displaystyle\iff\bigvee\nolimits_{i}\,S_{i}\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)\iff\mathtt{fit}(\bigvee\nolimits_{i}\,S_{i})\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a).\qed

Combining all the above we obtain that exact filters precisely correspond to fittings of joins of locally closed sublocales. The latter class of sublocales has been studied in
[
Arr21
]
. In fact, the sublocales of the form
\textstyle S=\bigvee_{i}\,(\mathfrak{c}(x_{i})\cap\mathfrak{o}(y_{i}))
for some set of
x_{i}
’s and
y_{i}
’s, are called
smooth sublocales
and correspond precisely to joins of complemented sublocales.
Upon denoting the collection of smooth sublocales by
\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)
, we can conclude the following.

Theorem 6.6
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to
\mathsf{E}(L)\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)]
.

6.3
Joins of closed sublocales

The results from the previous section, especially Theorem
6.6
, naturally lead to further questions about the nature of the frame
\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)
of joins of closed sublocales. Observe that we have
\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)
and so there must be a restriction of
\mathsf{E}(L)
that corresponds to
\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)]
. To this end, we appeal to the notion of closed filters which we have introduced in Section
5.4
. The reason why we call these filter closed is because they are of the form
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(a))
:

\displaystyle\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(a))=\{x\in L\mid\mathfrak{c}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(x)\}=\{x\in L\mid L\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathfrak{o}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(x)\}=\{x\in L\mid x\vee a=1\}

(6.3.1)

Wee see that closed filters are a special kind of locally closed filters, which in turn are a subclass of exact filters

\mathsf{C}(L)\subseteq{\mathsf{L}\mathsf{C}}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{E}(L).

Consequently, the following theorem is a genuine refinement of Theorem
6.6
.

Proposition 6.7
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L))\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)]
.

Proof.

By (
6.3.1
) we know that a filter is closed if and only if it is the
\mathsf{fi}(\,\text{-}\,)
image of a closed sublocale. Therefore, intersections of closed filters
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L))
are isomorphic to fitted joins of fittings of closed sublocales by Theorem
6.6
.

What is left to show is that these are precisely fittings of joins of closed sublocales. This follows from Lemma
6.5
.
∎

Furthermore, Lemma
5.6
gives the following rephrasing of Proposition
6.7
.

Theorem 6.8
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to
\mathsf{R}(L)\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)]
.

6.4
Joins of compact sublocales

The celebrated Hofmann-Mislove Theorem establishes a correspondence between compact saturated subsets of a sober space and Scott-open filters on the frame of open of the space. Johnstone gave a pointfree analogue of this result.

Proposition 6.9
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to the isomorphism between
\mathsf{SO}(L)
and
compact fitted sublocales
of
L
, that is, sublocales
S\in\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
such that
S\subseteq\bigvee_{a\in A}\mathfrak{o}(a)
implies
S\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a_{1})\vee\dots\vee\mathfrak{o}(a_{n})
for some
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}\in A
..

Proof.

This is Lemma 3.4 in
[
Joh85
]
, see also
[
Esc03
]
.
∎

This theorem gives an alternative (and constructive) proof of Lemma
5.2
that
\mathsf{SO}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{SE}(L)
. Indeed, since
\mathsf{SE}(L)
are the fixpoints of the adjunction (
6.0.1
), by Proposition
6.9
,
\mathsf{SO}(L)
is in the image of the mapping
\mathsf{fi}
.

However, unlike exact and strongly exact filters, Scott-open filters are not necessarily closed under intersection. Therefore, the arising filter extension
L^{\mathsf{SO}}=\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L))
induces a non-trivial extension of Johnstone’s theorem.

Proposition 6.10
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to an isomorphism between
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L))
and fitted joins of compact fitted sublocales.

This proposition already gives an already quite intuitive topological description of the sublocales corresponding to filters in
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L))
. However, we can specify these sublocales in another slightly simpler way. To this end, set

•

\mathcal{S}_{k}(L)
to be the poset of joins of compact sublocales in
\mathcal{S}(L)
.

Theorem 6.11
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{SO}(L))\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{k}(L)]
.

Proof.

First, observe that compact fitted sublocales are exactly the fittings of compact sublocales.
The inclusion from left to right is immediate. For the other direction, observe that
S\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)
if and only if
\mathtt{fit}(S)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)
for any sublocale
S
and
a\in L
. Therefore, if a sublocale is compact then its fitting is compact too.

From this and Lemma
6.5
we see that every fitted join of compact fitted sublocale is in fact of the form
\mathtt{fit}(\bigvee\nolimits_{i}K_{i})
for some compact
K_{i}
’s.
∎

6.5
Spatial sublocales

In our final refinement of the adjunction (
6.0.1
) we further restrict Theorem
6.11
along the inclusion
\mathsf{CP}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{SO}(L)
and describe the sublocales corresponding to the class of completely prime filters.

To this end recall, e.g. from
[
APP23
]
or IX.3.3 in
[
PP21b
]
, that an
p\in L
is
prime
iff
\{p,1\}
is a sublocale of
L
. When this is the case, we call
\{p,1\}
a
one-point sublocale
and denote it by
\mathfrak{b}(p)
.

Moreover, we can define the
spatialization operation
on sublocales:

\mathsf{sp}:\mathcal{S}(L)\to\mathcal{S}(L),\qquad S\subseteq L\kern 5.0pt\mapsto\kern 5.0pt\bigvee\{\mathfrak{b}(p)\mid p\in\mathsf{pt}(L),\ \mathfrak{b}(p)\subseteq S\}

With this, define

•

\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)
as the poset of
spatial sublocales
of
L
, that is, sublocales
S\subseteq L
such that
S=\mathsf{sp}(S)
.

Observe that
\mathsf{sp}
is an interior operator which preserves finite joins. As a consequence,
\mathsf{sp}
is a coframe map
\mathcal{S}(L)\to\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)
and therefore
\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)\subseteq\mathcal{S}(L)
is a subcolocale inclusion. On the other hand, the following theorem is crucial in establishing that
\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)]\subseteq\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
is a subcolocale of
\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
in Section
6.6
below.

Theorem 6.12
.

The isomorphism in Theorem
6.2
restricts to
\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{CP}(L))\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)]
.

Proof.

First, observe that for a prime
p\in L
,

\mathfrak{b}(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)\kern 5.0pt\iff\kern 5.0pta\nleq p.

(6.5.1)

Indeed,
\mathfrak{b}(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)
iff
a\to p=p
iff
a\nleq p
where the last equivalence holds because
a\to b=1
iff
a\leq b
and because
p\not=1
since
p
is prime.

With this, we show that a filter
F
is completely prime if and only if it is
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{b}(p))
for some prime
p\in L
. For the left-to-right direction, suppose that we have a prime
p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)
and that
\bigvee_{i}x_{i}\in\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{b}(p))
, that is,
\mathfrak{b}(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(\bigvee_{i}x_{i})
.
Then, by (
6.5.1
),
\bigvee_{i}x_{i}\nleq p
, which implies that
x_{j}\nleq p
for some
j\in I
, that is,
\mathfrak{b}(p)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(x_{j})
. Therefore the filter
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{b}(p))
is completely prime.

For the converse, suppose that
F\subseteq L
is a completely prime filter.
Consider the prime element
p_{F}=\bigvee(L{\setminus}F)
.
We clearly have that
f\nleq p_{F}
for all
f\in F
which, by (
6.5.1
), implies
F\subseteq\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{b}(p_{F}))
. Finally, notice that, by definition of
p_{F}
,
f\nleq p_{F}
implies
f\in F
.

This gives us a poset isomorphism
\mathsf{CP}(L)\cong\{\mathfrak{b}(p)\mid p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)\}
. Because
\mathsf{su}
restricted to
\mathsf{SE}(L)
is an isomorphism of complete lattices (by Theorem
6.2
) and because
\mathsf{CP}(L)\subseteq\mathsf{SE}(L)
(by Lemma
5.2
), intersections of completely prime filters correspond to fitted joins of one-point sublocales. Furthermore, since joins of one-point sublocales are precisely spatial sublocales, we obtain that
\mathcal{FJ}(\{\mathfrak{b}(p)\mid p\in\mathsf{pt}(L)\})=\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)]
as required.
∎

7
Immediate consequences of the theory of polarities

In Section
6
, we established a number of correspondences between classes of filters closed under intersections and classes of sublocales. In fact, since
L^{\mathcal{F}}\cong\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{F})
(cf. Proposition
4.3
), the main theorems in the previous section can be rephrased as

L^{\mathcal{F}}\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{C}]

(7.0.1)

for some class of filters
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathsf{Filt}(L)
and a class of sublocales
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}(L)
. Recalling that the filter extension
L^{\mathcal{F}}
is given as
\mathcal{G}(P)
for the polarity of the form
P=(\mathcal{F},L,\ni)
(cf. Section
4
), in this section we make use of the connections with the theory of polarities to obtain new facts about filters and sublocales.

7.1
Isomorphisms with interiors of fitted sublocales

Observe that the operator
\mathtt{fit}(\,\text{-}\,)
on sublocales is simply the closure operator
\mathsf{cl}_{\mathfrak{o}[L]}(\,\text{-}\,)
for the class
\mathfrak{o}[L]
of open sublocales, in sense of §
3.2.4
. Furthermore, the classes of sublocales in (
7.0.1
) in our examples are closed under joins in
\mathcal{S}(L)
. In fact they are of the form
\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{D})
for some smaller subclass of sublocales
\mathcal{D}
of
\mathcal{S}(L)
. Namely,

•

\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}_{lc}(L))
where
\mathcal{S}_{lc}(L)
is the class of locally closed sublocales,

•

\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathfrak{c}[L])
where
\mathfrak{c}[L]=\{\mathfrak{c}(a)\mid a\in L\}
is the class of closed sublocales,

•

\mathcal{S}_{k}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}_{co}(C))
where
\mathcal{S}_{co}(L)
is the class of compact sublocales, and

•

\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathcal{S}_{*}(L))
where
\mathcal{S}_{*}(L)
is the class of one-point sublocales, i.e. sublocales
\mathfrak{b}(p)
for some prime
p\in L
.

This immediately puts us in the scope of Proposition
3.10
about polarities, which reveals interesting new correspondences with the classes of sublocales defined dually.

Theorem 7.1
.

For a frame
L
,

1.

fittings of smooth sublocales correspond to locally closed interiors of fitted sublocales,

i.e.
\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)]\cong\mathsf{int}_{\mathcal{S}_{lc}(L)}[\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)]
,

2.

fittings of joins of closed sublocales correspond to closed interiors of fitted sublocales,

i.e.
\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)]\cong\mathsf{int}_{\mathfrak{c}[L]}[\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)]
,

3.

fittings of joins of compact sublocales correspond to compact interiors of fitted sublocales, i.e.
\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{k}(L)]\cong\mathsf{int}_{\mathcal{S}_{co}(L)}[\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)]
, and

4.

fittings of spatial sublocales correspond to spatializations of fitted sublocales,

i.e.
\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{sp}(L)]\cong\mathsf{int}_{\mathcal{S}_{*}(L)}[\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)]=\mathsf{sp}[\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)]
.

The isomorphisms in each of the cases are described as in $
3.2.5
.

As we show in Section
6.6
, all these classes of sublocales form a coframe. Before we get to that we explore the universal properties that these classes admit, thanks to the theory of polarities.

7.2
Universal properties

Coming back to (
7.0.1
), we see that Theorem
7.1
tells us that our example filter extensions can be equivalently specified by a class of sublocales instead.
Indeed, for a strongly exact filter

and

,
\mathsf{su}(F)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(a)
iff

. This gives us that the mapping

Therefore, by Propositions

,

and Theorems

,
6.8

\displaystyle L^{\mathsf{E}}\cong L^{\mathsf{L}\mathsf{C}}

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{S}_{lc}(L),\mathfrak{o}[L],\subseteq)

\displaystyle L^{\mathsf{C}}\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{c}[L],\mathfrak{o}[L],\subseteq)

\displaystyle L^{\mathsf{SO}}

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{S}_{co}(L),\mathfrak{o}[L],\subseteq)

\displaystyle L^{\mathsf{CP}}

\displaystyle\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathcal{S}_{*}(L),\mathfrak{o}[L],\subseteq)

Note that the first isomorphism

follows from Lemma

.

As a result, we can reason about these classes of sublocales in terms of filters and vice versa. Take for example the filter extension

. The induced mapping

(7.2.1)

satisfies the universal properties
(DF)
and

Now, by Propostion

We can now look at the concrete description of

, and compose it with the isomorphism from the proof of Proposition

and also §

and

.

7.3
Exact filters in the subset order

Recall that

\displaystyle(\mathsf{E}(L),\subseteq)\cong(\mathsf{E}(L),\sqsubseteq)^{op}\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathsf{E}(L),L,\ni)^{op}\cong\mathcal{G}(L,\mathsf{E}(L),\in)

Note that the natural order of

Consequently, consider the following pair of maps

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces L^{op}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces(\mathsf{E}(L),\subseteq)}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{c}}

(\mathsf{E}(L),\subseteq)
from
[
BMP20
]
.
Since
\mathfrak{c}(a)\subseteq J(F)
iff
a\in F
(cf.
[
BMP20
]
) it is immediate that these maps play the role of the mapping of
i_{L}
and
i_{\mathsf{E}(L)}
into
\mathcal{G}(L,\mathsf{E}(L),\in)
and satisfy the universal properties of Theorem
3.2
.

As a consequence of this we see that, by Lemma
3.7
,
\mathfrak{c}(\,\text{-}\,)
transforms exact meets into joins, i.e.
\bigvee_{a\in A}\mathfrak{c}(a)=\mathfrak{c}(\bigwedge A)
for any exact meet
\bigwedge A
.

8
The Booleanization as a polarity

Recall from Corollary
5.11
that

\displaystyle\mathsf{R}(L)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{E}(L)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{SE}(L)\hookrightarrow\mathsf{Filt}(L)

(8.0.1)

and also

\displaystyle\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}]\hookrightarrow\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)]\hookrightarrow\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)

(8.0.2)

are all subcolocale inclusions. Furthermore, Lemma
5.6
establishes that
\mathsf{R}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L))
is the Booleanization of
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
.
From these fact we immediately obtain the following.

Theorem 8.1
.

We have

\mathsf{R}(L)=\mathcal{J}(\mathsf{C}(L))=\mathfrak{B}(\mathsf{E}(L))=\mathfrak{B}(\mathsf{SE}(L))=\mathfrak{B}(\mathsf{Filt}(L))

and therefore also

\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)]=\mathfrak{B}(\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{b}(L)])=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)).

Proof.

Recall that for any dense sublocale inclusion
S\subseteq L
it is the case that
\mathfrak{B}(S)\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(L)
. If, furthermore,
\mathfrak{B}(L)\subseteq S
then also
\mathfrak{B}(S)=\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{B}(S))\subseteq\mathfrak{B}(L)
. Consequently, from the subcolocale inclusions in (
8.0.1
) we obtain the first set of equalities since
\mathsf{R}(L)=\mathfrak{B}(\mathsf{Filt}(L))
, which holds by definition of
\mathsf{R}(L)
.
The second line of equalities is just a translation of the first line along the adjunction (
6.0.1
).
∎

We also use the closure operator
\mathsf{cl}(\,\text{-}\,)
on the lattice of all sublocales. For a sublocale
S
it is defined as
\mathsf{cl}(S)=\bigcap\{\mathfrak{c}(x):S\subseteq\mathfrak{c}(x)\}
, i.e. in our notation
\mathsf{cl}(S)=\mathsf{cl}_{\mathfrak{c}[L]}(S)
. The following is a standard result of pointfree topology.

Lemma 8.2
.

For a sublocale
S\subseteq L
of a frame
L
, we have
\mathsf{cl}(S)=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mu\bigwedge S
.

Now we rephrase Booleanization of frames in terms of polarities.
The following result highlighting a symmetry between the Booleanization of
L
and that of
\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
. In the following,
\mathsf{tot}=\{(x,y)\in L\times L\mid x\vee y=1\}
and
\mathsf{con}=\{(x,y)\in L\times L\mid x\wedge y=0\}
.

Corollary 8.3
.

Let
L
be a frame. We have

1.

\mathcal{G}(L^{op},L,\mathsf{tot})\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{c}[L],\mathfrak{o}[L],\subseteq)\cong\mathtt{fit}[\mathcal{S}_{c}(L)]=\mathfrak{B}(\mathcal{S}_{o}(L))
.

2.

\mathcal{G}(L,L^{op},\mathsf{con})\cong\mathcal{G}(\mathfrak{o}[L],\mathfrak{c}[L],\subseteq)\cong\mathsf{cl}[\mathfrak{o}[L]]=\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{c}[L]^{op})\cong\mathfrak{B}(L)
.

Proof.

For the first item, the first isomorphism holds because it is a basic fact that the collection of open sublocales is isomorphic to
L
whereas that of closed sublocales are anti-isomorphic to it and
(a,b)\in\mathsf{tot}
iff
\mathfrak{c}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak{o}(b)
. The second isomorphism holds by Proposition
3.10
. Finally, the last equality holds by Theorem
8.1
.

For the second item, the first two isomorphisms are shown similarly as in the first except that we needed that
(a,b)\in\mathsf{con}
iff
\mathfrak{o}(a)\subseteq\mathfrak{c}(b)
. For the equality
\mathsf{cl}[\mathfrak{o}[L]]=\mathfrak{B}(\mathfrak{c}[L]^{op})
, we note that the regular elements of
\mathfrak{c}[L]^{op}
, i.e. the elements of its Booleanization, are the closed sublocales of the form
\mathfrak{c}(x^{*})
for some
x\in L
, as
x\mapsto\mathfrak{c}(x)
determines an isomorphism
L\cong\mathfrak{c}[L]^{op}
. But these are precisely the closures of open sublocales as for each
x\in L
we have
\mathsf{cl}(\mathfrak{o}(x))=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mu\bigwedge\mathfrak{o}(x)=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mu(x^{*})=\mathfrak{c}(x^{*})
, where we have used Lemma
8.2
for the first equality. The last isomorphism is once again from
L\cong\mathfrak{c}[L]^{op}
.
∎

9
Closed filters, open filters, sublocales and related facts

In this final section we would like to argue that the coframe
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
, importantly in the
\sqsubseteq
order, in some ways resembles the coframe
\mathcal{S}(L)
and is an interesting discretisation of
L
as well.

Recall from (
6.3.1
) that closed filters arise from closed sublocales, i.e. they are of the form
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(a))=\{x\mid x\vee a=1\}
. Similarly, we define
open filters
to be the principal filters since we have that
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{o}(a))=\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua
. To make reasoning with filters more natural we write
\mathfrak{cf}(a)
and
\mathfrak{of}(a)
for
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(a))
and
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{o}(a))
and, furthermore,
\mathsf{O}=L
and
\{1\}
for the smallest and largest filters, respectively.

Perhaps surprisingly, we can transfer some facts about open and closed sublocales to the setting of filters. For example, recall that a frame is subfit iff its open sublocales are joins of closed sublocales (see e.g. Proposition V.1.4 in
[
PP12
]
). Analogously, we obtain a new characterisation of subfitness in terms of open and closed filters.

Proposition 9.1
.

A frame
L
is subfit if and only if, for any
a\in L
,

\mathfrak{of}(a)=\bigsqcup\{\mathfrak{cf}(x)\mid\mathfrak{cf}(x)\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{of}(a)\}.

Proof.

First, observe that from the definitions
\mathfrak{cf}(x)\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{of}(a)
iff
\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{o}(a))\subseteq\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(x))
iff
a\in\mathsf{fi}(\mathfrak{c}(x))
iff
a\vee x=1
.
Since the
\sqsupseteq
inequality in the above expression always holds, the right-hand side of the equivalence can be rewritten in the subset order as follows.

\bigcap\{\mathfrak{cf}(x)\mid a\vee x=1\}\subseteq\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua

This inclusion can be further simplified to the first-order expression:

\forall b\kern 5.0pt(\forall x.\kern 5.0pta\vee x=1\implies b\vee x=1)\implies a\leq b

We see that this is precisely the axiom for subfitness.
∎

Since
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
is morally closer to
\mathcal{S}_{o}(L)
than to
\mathcal{S}(L)
, closed filters could be rather thought of as fittings of closed sublocales. Therefore, we do not expect closed and open sublocales and closed and open behave exactly alike. Some usual properties still hold.

Lemma 9.2
.

For any
a\in L
, we have

\mathfrak{of}(a)^{\#}=\mathfrak{cf}(a)
and
\mathfrak{of}(a)\sqcup\mathfrak{cf}(a)=\{1\}
.

Proof.

The first equality follows directly from definitions and (
5.4.1
). The second is a consequence of the first as
\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathfrak{of}(a)=\mathfrak{cf}(a)
implies
\{1\}\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{of}(a)\sqcup\mathfrak{cf}(a)
.
∎

On the other hand, some of the expected facts about open and closed sublocales do not transfer. For example, we do not in general have that supplements of closed filters are open. On the other hand, requiring this property yields a new (and perhaps surprising) characterisation of subfitness.

Proposition 9.3
.

For a frame
L
,

1.

L
is subfit iff, for every
a\in L
,
\mathfrak{cf}(a)^{\#}=\mathfrak{of}(a)
; and

2.

L
is a Boolean algebra iff, for every
a\in L
,
\mathfrak{of}(a)\sqcap\mathfrak{cf}(a)=\mathsf{O}
.

Proof.

For (1), as in Proposition 2.9.2 of
[
BMP20
]
, observe that the supplement
F^{\#}
of any filter
F
in
\mathsf{Filt}(L)
is the filter
\{b\mid\forall x\in F.\ b\vee x=1\}
since
F^{\#}=\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}F
computed in the subset order is precisely as
\bigvee\{\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mub\mid\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mub\cap F\subseteq\{1\}\}
. Furthermore,
\mathfrak{cf}(a)^{\#}=\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathfrak{cf}(a)\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{of}(a)
holds automatically since, from adjointness with
\sqcup
, it is equivalent to
\{1\}\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{cf}(a)\sqcup\mathfrak{of}(a)
which in turn is equivalent to
\mathfrak{cf}(a)=\{1\}\mathbin{\raisebox{0.80002pt}{$\smallsetminus$}}\mathfrak{of}(a)\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{cf}(a)
.

Finally, the non-automatic inequality
\mathfrak{of}(a)\sqsubseteq\mathfrak{cf}(a)^{\#}
translates as

\{b\mid\forall x.\ x\vee a=1\implies b\vee x=1\}=\{b\mid\forall x\in\mathfrak{cf}(a).\ b\vee x=1\}\subseteq\mathord{\uparrow}\mkern 1.0mua.

Clearly, requiring this to hold for every
a
is precisely subfitness.

For (2), observe that
\mathfrak{of}(a)\sqcap\mathfrak{cf}(a)\sqsubseteq\mathsf{O}
iff
0\in\mathfrak{of}(a)\cap\mathfrak{cf}(a)
iff there are some
x,a^{\prime}
such that
x\vee a=1
and
a^{\prime}\geq a
and we have
x\wedge a^{\prime}=0
. Clearly, the last statement is equivalent to
a
being complemented.
∎
