Gradient-based optimisation of the conditional-value-at-risk using the multi-level Monte Carlo method
Sundar Ganesh, Fabio Nobile

Abstract
In this work, we tackle the problem of minimising the Conditional-Value-at-Risk (CVaR) of output quantities of complex differential models with random input data, using gradient-based approaches in combination with the Multi-Level Monte Carlo (MLMC) method. In particular, we consider the framework of multi-level Monte Carlo for parametric expectations introduced in [ 24 ] and propose modifications of the MLMC estimator, error estimation procedure, and adaptive MLMC parameter selection to ensure the estimation of the CVaR and sensitivities for a given design with a prescribed accuracy. We then propose combining the MLMC framework with an alternating inexact minimisation-gradient descent algorithm, for which we prove Q-linear convergence in the optimisation iterations under the assumptions of strong convexity and Lipschitz continuity of the gradient of the objective function. We demonstrate the performance of our approach on two numerical examples of practical relevance, which evidence the same optimal asymptotic cost-tolerance behaviour as standard MLMC methods for fixed design computations of output expectations.



Keywords —
Multilevel Monte Carlo Methods, VaR, CVaR, Uncertainty Quantification, Optimisation Under Uncertainty, Gradient Descent.

Introduction

Optimisation algorithms play an important role across various scientific and engineering fields as valuable design tools.
The key goal of optimisation is to find the best values of certain parameters (design variables) of a model, typically a differential model such as a
Partial Differential Equation (PDE)
, used to predict the behaviour of a certain system, such that a desired output
Quantity of Interest (QoI)
of the model is optimised.
Such differential models usually also include various other input parameters besides the design variable, which may or may not be fully characterised.
There is an increasing interest in the computational science and engineering community to treat such parameters as random variables to reflect their uncertainty, either due to a lack of knowledge or to some intrinsic variability.
As a result, the output
QoI
being optimised also becomes a random variable.
Naively optimising the system for only one particular value of the inputs (e.g., the nominal value) can lead to a design that is not robust enough to the uncertainties in the system.
A classical example is of civil engineering structures designed to minimise structural loads for moderate wind conditions, which are then unable to withstand local wind gusts or storms
[
22
]
.

The field of
PDE
-constrained
Optimization Under Uncertainty (OUU)
seeks to characterise the randomness of the output
QoI
of the
PDE
using summary statistics such as moments, quantiles, etc., and optimise the summary statistic instead of the
QoI
directly.
In particular, in risk-averse
PDE
-constrained optimisation, one aims at favouring designs with acceptable performance also in extreme conditions.
In this case, the summary statistic, often called a risk-measure, should quantify the importance that is given in the design process to unfavourable scenarios.
The reader is referred to
[
38
]
for a comprehensive review of several risk-measures and their main properties.
An important class of risk-measures is that of coherent risk-measures
[
36
,
4
]
, which exhibit favourable properties such as monotonicity and convexity.

In this work, we focus on the so-called
Conditional-Value-at-Risk (CVaR)

[
35
]
, which corresponds to the expectation of the output
QoI
conditional on being above a given quantile (referred to as the
Value-at-Risk (VaR)
in the finance literature), and is a widely used coherent risk-measure.
To be more specific, let
Q(z)\in\mathbb{R}
denote the random
QoI
, which depends on the design parameter
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We denote by
\mathbb{E}[X]
the expected value of a random variable
X
, and by
c_{\tau}(z)
the
CVaR
of
Q(z)
of significance
\tau\in[0,1]
, i.e.,
c_{\tau}(z)\coloneqq\expectation\left[Q(z)|Q(z)\geq q_{\tau}\right]
, where
q_{\tau}
is the
\tau
-quantile of
Q(z)
.
It was demonstrated in
[
35
]
that
c_{\tau}(z)
could be written in the following form under certain conditions on the distribution of
Q(z)
:

\displaystyle c_{\tau}(z)=\min_{\theta\in\mathbb{R}}\big\{\Phi(\theta;z)\coloneqq\expectation\left[\phi(\theta,Q(z))\right]\big\},\hskip 9.24994pt\phi(\theta,Q)\coloneqq\theta+\frac{(Q-\theta)^{+}}{1-\tau},

(1)

where we denote with
X^{+}
the positive part of
X
; namely
X^{+}\coloneqq\max(0,X)
.
In this work, we consider the following problem of penalised
CVaR
minimisation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{*}

\displaystyle=\min_{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left\{c_{\tau}(z)+\kappa\left\|z-z_{ref}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right\},

(2)

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\
\theta\in\mathbb{R}\end{subarray}}\bigg\{\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)\coloneqq\Phi(\theta;z)+\kappa\left\|z-z_{ref}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\bigg\},

(3)

where we have added a term penalising deviation of the design
z
from a preferred design
z_{ref}
.
In Eq. (
3
), the parameter
\kappa
controls the strength of the penalisation, and
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{l^{2}}
denotes the Euclidean norm.
In particular, we will consider Monte Carlo type approximations of problem (
3
), and since evaluating the objective function and its sensitivities at a given design
z
requires the solution of a costly
PDE
many times, we will accelerate the Monte Carlo estimation by multilevel strategies following the well established
Multi-Level Monte Carlo (MLMC)
paradigm
[
15
]
, which has been shown to provide significant performance improvements in comparison to classical Monte Carlo methods for estimating various summary statistics of output
QoI
of differential models
[
16
,
15
,
19
,
25
,
12
]
.

Two broad approaches can be used to solve problem (
3
); namely, gradient-free and gradient-based methods.
Evolutionary algorithms, a type of gradient-free method, were used in combination with Monte Carlo estimators for
PDE
-constrained
CVaR
minimisation in
[
34
,
33
]
.
A genetic algorithm was also used in combination with
MLMC
estimators in
[
32
]
.
Multiple different risk-measures, including the
CVaR
, were estimated, and the framework was applied to aerodynamic shape optimisation problems.
The authors of
[
11
]
proposed a multifidelity Monte Carlo estimator for the
CVaR
based on cross-entropy methods combined with importance sampling.
This approach was used in
[
31
]
in combination with gradient-free optimisation algorithms to minimise the
CVaR
.
However, gradient-free algorithms typically have slower rates of convergence in comparison to gradient-based methods and involve multiple expensive evaluations of the objective function.
We propose instead the use of gradient based algorithms, combined with
MLMC
estimators, to compute sensitivities of the objective function in problem (
3
).
In particular, the
MLMC
estimators developed in this work rely on the framework of parametric expectations
[
24
]
and extend the work in
[
5
]
to the computation of
CVaR
sensitivities, addressing the corresponding challenges as outlined hereafter.
We mention that gradient-based methods have also been used for other risk-measures and sampling strategies in the context of
PDE
-constrained
OUU

[
17
,
40
,
29
]
.

The computation of the sensitivities of the
CVaR

c_{\tau}(z)
with respect to the extended design variables
z
and
\theta
typically requires the estimation of expectations of the form
\expectation\left[(Q(z)-\theta)^{+}\right]
and
\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q(z)\geq\theta}f(z)\right]
for suitable design-dependent random variables
f(z)
.
Although
\expectation\left[(Q(z)-\theta)^{+}\right]
and
\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q(z)\geq\theta}f(z)\right]
can be shown to be differentiable in
\theta
and
z

[
21
,
20
]
under some conditions on the distribution of
Q(z)
, sample- or quadrature-based approximations of these expectations are typically not differentiable and may require some additional treatment.
One possibility is to directly use the non-differentiable estimations in combination with non-smooth optimisation techniques that use sub-gradient information.
For example, the work in
[
26
]
uses a combination of smooth and non-smooth optimisation techniques, using sub-gradients computed using Monte Carlo estimators, to minimise the
CVaR
.
Alternatively, one could construct smoothed versions of the maximum/indicator functions, with sufficient regularity such that sample-based approximations are still differentiable.
For example, a regularised version of the
CVaR
was constructed in
[
23
]
, with second order differentiability, and optimised successfully using a trust-region method.
However, although regularised or smoothed versions of the
CVaR
can be constructed with adequate differentiability, this property is lost in the limit of vanishing smoothing, as is required when the algorithm is close to the optimum.
The method proposed in
[
24
,
5
]
offers an alternative to
CVaR
regularisation.
In these works, the quantity
\expectation\left[(Q(z)-\theta)^{+}\right]
is estimated directly using an
MLMC
estimator at a set of points in
\theta
, all sharing the same realisations of
Q(z)
, followed by a cubic spline interpolation over the pointwise evaluations thus obtained.
Derivatives such as
\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q(z)\geq\theta}f(z)\right]
are then approximated using derivatives of the cubic spline.
We propose to follow the above path in this work.
As was discussed in
[
5
]
, directly using a naive
MLMC
estimator to estimate
\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q(z)\geq\theta}f(z)\right]
causes non-optimal
MLMC
complexity behaviour.
By constructing an
MLMC
estimator of
\expectation\left[(Q(z)-\theta)^{+}f(z)\right]
and numerically differentiating in
\theta
instead, the approach in
[
5
]
ameliorates this issue and preserves the same optimal complexity behaviour of the
MLMC
method as predicted for estimating
\expectation\left[Q(z)\right]
.
Lastly, since the
MLMC
estimator proposed in
[
24
,
5
]
automatically provides an approximation
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\cdot,z)
of the function
\theta\mapsto\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
at a given design
z
, we propose in this work to use an optimisation algorithm in which, at each iteration, gradient steps are taken only in the design variable
z
, whereas exact optimisation in
\theta
is performed using the surrogate
\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\cdot,z)
.
Such an algorithm, introduced in
[
6
]
, was applied in combination with the Monte Carlo estimation of a regularised version of the
CVaR
in
[
7
]
.

The main contributions of this work are as follows.
We propose novel expressions for the sensitivity of the objective function defined in Eq. (
3
) in terms of parametric expectations, thus allowing us to use and extend the framework in
[
5
]
to build cost optimal adaptive
MLMC
estimators for those sensitivities with error control.
We then propose to use
MLMC
sensitivity estimators within an
Alternating Minimisation-Gradient Descent (AMGD)
algorithm, analogous to the one proposed in
[
6
,
7
]
, where gradient steps are taken in the design variable
z
whereas exact optimisation is performed in
\theta
using an
MLMC
-constructed surrogate
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
of
\mathcal{J}
.
The accuracy of the surrogate and sensitivity estimation is increased over the optimisation iterations and is set proportional to the gradient norm.
Following closely the analysis in
[
7
]
, we propose a convergence result for our algorithm under the assumption that the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
is strongly convex with Lipschitz continuous gradients.

The structure of this paper is as follows.
We present the problem formulation in Section
2
, for a problem of penalised
CVaR
minimisation of the form in Eq. (
3
).
The novel expression for the gradients in terms of the parametric expectations is also presented in Section
2
.
In Section
3
, we propose the
AMGD
algorithm with inexact gradient and objective function estimation and demonstrate its convergence.
Section
4
discusses the novel
MLMC
estimators, error estimation procedure, and adaptive
Continuation MLMC (CMLMC)
-type hierarchy selection for the gradients of
\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
.
In addition, it presents a final
CMLMC
-
AMGD
algorithm.
Lastly, in Section
5
, we demonstrate the above optimisation algorithm and
MLMC
procedure on two problems of interest.
The first is a two-dimensional oscillator, typically used to model oscillatory phenomena in excitable media.
The second is a more applied problem of pollutant transport modelling.
We demonstrate that the procedure proposed in this work performs well and reflects the theoretical results presented in Sections
2
and
3
.

Problem formulation

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
denote a complete probability space,
\omega\in\Omega
an elementary random event and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the vector of design variables.
We denote by

the random

, typically a functional of the solution to an underlying differential model with random input
\omega
and design

.
We are interested in minimising the

, as indicated in Eq. (
2
), following the formulation presented in
[

To this end, we first introduce the following assumptions on the random variable

Assumption 1

For any

(i)

\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})

.

(ii)

The measure of

We denote by

.

(iii)

There exists a positive random variable

, such that
\expectation\left[K\right]<\infty

\displaystyle\left|Q(z+\Delta z,\cdot)-Q(z,\cdot)\right|\leq K(\cdot)\left\|\Delta z\right\|_{l^{2}},

(4)

for any
\Delta z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
close enough to

(restated here from
[

20
]
).

(iv)

For almost every

.

.
(ii)
.

In this work, we extensively use the concept of parametric expectations.
In particular, let us introduce the function (parametric expectation)

\displaystyle\Phi(\theta;z)\coloneqq\expectation\left[\phi(\theta,Q(z,\cdot))\right],\hskip 9.24994pt\theta\in\mathbb{R},\;z\in\mathbb{R}^{d},

(7)

with

\displaystyle\phi(\theta,Q)

The introduction of the parametric expectation

The problem is restated below for reference:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}^{*}

\displaystyle=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\\

Let
\linop(X,Y)

We define the function
\mathcal{R}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}(\theta,Q)\coloneqq\theta+\frac{\expectation\left[(Q-\theta)^{+}\right]}{1-\tau}=\expectation\left[\phi(\theta,Q)\right].

Then,
\mathcal{R}(\theta,Q)
is jointly Fréchet differentiable in

(\theta,Q)\in\mathbb{R}\times\Gamma

(\delta\theta,\delta Q)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})

\displaystyle D\mathcal{R}(\theta,Q)(\delta\theta,\delta Q)=\left(1-\frac{\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q\geq\theta}\right]}{1-\tau}\right)\delta\theta+\frac{\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Q\geq\theta\}}\delta Q\right]}{1-\tau}.

(12)

Proof.

∎

This result, combined with Assumption

\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)

Corollary 2.1
.

The objective function
\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
is jointly Fréchet differentiable in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with Fréchet derivative
D\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)\in\linop(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{R})
at the point
(\theta,z)
in the direction
(\delta\theta,\delta z)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
given by:

\displaystyle D\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)(\delta\theta,\delta z)=\left(1-\frac{\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q\geq\theta}\right]}{1-\tau}\right)\delta\theta+\frac{\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Q\geq\theta\}}Q_{z}^{T}\delta z\right]}{1-\tau}+2\kappa(z-z_{ref})^{T}\delta z.

(13)

A direct implication of Corollary
2.1
is that the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{J}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and the partial derivatives
\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta,z)=\left[\mathcal{J}_{z^{1}}(\theta,z),...,\mathcal{J}_{z^{d}}(\theta,z)\right]^{T}

\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\theta,z)
exist and are given by the following expressions:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=1-\frac{\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q(z,\cdot)\geq\theta}\right]}{1-\tau},

(14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=\frac{\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{Q(z,\cdot)\geq\theta}Q_{z}(z,\cdot)\right]}{1-\tau}+2\kappa(z-z_{ref}).

(15)

One of the main contributions of this work is the estimation of the sensitivities in Eqs. (
14
) and (
15
) using
MLMC
estimators.
However, as discussed in Section
1
, using
MLMC
to directly estimate the expectations in Eqs. (
14
) and (

) may result in compromised or non-optimal

performance.
The reader is referred to
[
5
,
24
]
for a detailed discussion on the topic.
To ameliorate this issue, we propose the following alternative formulation of the gradients in terms of parametric expectations:

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}(\theta;z),\hskip 9.24994pt\text{with $\Phi$ as in Eqs.\penalty\ \eqref{eq:parest_form}-\eqref{eq:phi_form}},

(16)

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta,z)

\displaystyle=\Psi^{\prime}(\theta;z)+2\kappa(z-z_{ref}),

(17)

\displaystyle\text{where }\Psi(\theta;z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\expectation\left[-\frac{(Q(z,\cdot)-\theta)^{+}Q_{z}(z,\cdot)}{1-\tau}\right]\eqqcolon\left[\expectation\left[\psi(\theta,Q,Q_{z^{1}})\right],...,\expectation\left[\psi(\theta,Q,Q_{z^{d}})\right]\right]^{T}.

(18)

The superscript prime of the parametric expectations in Eq. (
16
) and Eq. (
17
) denotes the derivative computed with respect to
\theta
.
In addition to
\Phi(\theta;z)
, we have introduced the parametric expectation
\Psi(\theta;z)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and the function
\psi(\theta,Q,Q_{z^{k}})\in\mathbb{R}

and

denote the

components of

and

respectively,

.
The differentiability of
\Psi(\theta;z)
in

follows by the same arguments of Theorem

and Corollary
2.1
, under Assumption

.

]
that since
\phi

are Lipschitz continuous in their arguments, the corresponding

The idea is then to build

\hat{\Phi}(\cdot,z)
and
\hat{\Psi}(\cdot,z)
for the whole functions

, and then approximate

As a by-product of this approach for estimating sensitivities, we construct an approximation
\theta\in\Theta\mapsto\hat{\mathcal{J}}(\theta,z)=\hat{\Phi}(\theta;z)+\kappa\left\|z-z_{ref}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}
of the objective function itself for all
\theta\in\Theta
, at a given design

.
This allows us to consider an optimisation problem in which exact minimisation in
\theta
is performed at each iteration using the surrogate
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
, and gradient steps are performed only in

using the approximate gradient
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{z}
.
Notice that the gradient approximation in
z
is inconsistent with the surrogate model
\hat{\mathcal{J}}
, i.e.,
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}_{z}\neq\partial_{z}\hat{\mathcal{J}}
, in contrast to
\hat{\mathcal{J}}_{\theta}
.
We will detail this approach in the next section.

3
Gradient based optimisation algorithm

In this section, we present a gradient-based iterative procedure to find a local minimiser
(\theta^{*},z^{*})
of the
OUU
problem in Eq. (
10
), should it exist.
The broad goal of a gradient based algorithm is to define the iterates
(\theta_{j},z_{j}),j\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{j\to\infty}(\theta_{j},z_{j})=(\theta^{*},z^{*}),

(19)

where the iterates are computed using gradient information.
Motivated by our interest in using
MLMC
estimators based on parametric expectations to estimate the objective function and its sensitivities, we consider in this section the general situation in which, at each iteration
j
of the gradient based algorithm, we build an approximation
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta,z),\theta\in\Theta
of the objective function at the design
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
on a suitably chosen interval
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
(which may depend on
j
, although to ease the notation, we do not highlight such dependence), as well as approximations
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}(\theta,z)
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta,z)
,
\theta\in\Theta
, where the approximation
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}
may not coincide with the
z
-derivative of
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}
.
The approximations
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}
,
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}
may be random, as will be the case for
MLMC
estimators.
We then propose the following variation of the standard gradient descent algorithm, starting from an initial design
z_{0}
:

\displaystyle\theta_{j}\in\argmin_{\theta\in\Theta}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta,z_{j}),

(20)

\displaystyle z_{j+1}=z_{j}-\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j}),

(21)

where
\alpha>0
denotes a step size parameter.

3.1
Convergence analysis

For the interested reader, we present a self-contained convergence analysis of the iterates
(\theta_{j},z_{j})
in Theorem
3.1
, under additional assumptions on
\mathcal{J}
and
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}
, based on the analysis presented in
[

]
.
The key differences in the two analyses are related to the fact that the algorithm studied here is an
AMGD

We first note that the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
is convex under the additional assumption that
Q(z,\cdot)
is almost surely convex in
z

[
35
, Theorem 10]
.
When combined with the assumption that
\mathcal{J}\to\infty
when
\left\|z\right\|_{l^{2}},\left|\theta\right|\to\infty
, this ensures that a minimiser of
\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
exists in
\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
However, we require additional assumptions on the objective function
\mathcal{J}
to prove Q-linear convergence of the iterates
\theta_{j}
and

towards such a minimiser; namely Assumptions
2
and
3
below on strong convexity and on the Lipschitz continuity of the gradients, respectively.
An immediate implication of Assumption
2
is that there exists a unique minimiser
(\theta^{*},z^{*})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
for the
OUU
problem in Eq. (
10
) such that
\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta^{*},z^{*})=\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\theta^{*},z^{*})=0
.

In what follows, we denote by
\expectationc\left[\cdot\right]
the expectation conditional on all of the random variables used to define

(i.e., conditioned on the past up to iteration

), and by
\left\langle\cdot,\cdot\right\rangle

Readers interested in the implementation details of Algorithm

and its relation to the

Assumption 2
.

The objective function
\mathcal{J}
is
\mu
-strongly convex, i.e., there exists
\mu>0
such that, for all
w_{a},w_{b}\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
, equivalently:

(i)

\mathcal{J}(w_{b})\geq\mathcal{J}(w_{a})+\left\langle w_{b}-w_{a},\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{a})\right\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|w_{b}-w_{a}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}
,

(ii)

\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{b})-\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{a}),w_{b}-w_{a}\right\rangle\geq\mu\left\|w_{b}-w_{a}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}
.

Assumption 3
.

The objective function
\mathcal{J}
has Lipschitz continuous gradients, i.e., there exists
L>0
such that, for all
w_{a},w_{b}\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{b})-\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{a})\right\|_{l^{2}}\leq L\left\|w_{b}-w_{a}\right\|_{l^{2}}.

(22)

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
\mathcal{J}
satisfy Assumptions
2
and Assumptions
3
.
Then we have that, for
0<\alpha\leq 1/L
,

\displaystyle\frac{\mu}{2}\left\|w-w^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+\frac{\alpha}{2}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(w)\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}\leq\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}(w),w-w^{*}\right\rangle.

(23)

The above result is restated here from
[
7
, Lemma 2.1]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Theta\subset\mathbb{R}
be a convex set.
Let
\mathcal{J}:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy Assumptions
2
and
3
, and
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}:\Theta\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
satisfy the following condition:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\cdot,z_{j})\right)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\expectationc\left[\left\|\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}(\cdot,z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\cdot,z_{j})\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}\right]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\expectationc\left[\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z,k}(\cdot,z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{z,k}(\cdot,z_{j})\right\|^{2}_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}\right]\leq\eta^{2}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}},

(24)

for some
\eta>0
, where
(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})
is the
j^{\text{th}}
iterate produced by Algorithm
1
with step size
\alpha
satisfying
0<\alpha\leq 1/L
and
\alpha\mu\leq 1
.
Then, the following result holds true:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\left\|z_{j+1}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+C_{1}(\theta_{j}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\xi\expectation\left[\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+C_{1}(\theta_{j-1}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right],

(25)

for some constants
C_{1}>0
and
0<\xi<1
.

Proof.

From the definition of the iterate
z_{j+1}
in Eq. (
21
), we have:

\displaystyle\left\|z_{j+1}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}-\alpha\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}

(26)

\displaystyle=\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|^{2}-2\alpha\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j}),z_{j}-z^{*}\right\rangle

(27)

\displaystyle=\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+\underbrace{\alpha^{2}\left(\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|^{2}+\left(\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right)^{2}\right)}_{\eqqcolon\hat{T}_{1}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{-2\alpha\left(\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j}),z_{j}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\left\langle\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\theta_{j},z_{j}),\theta_{j}-\theta^{*}\right\rangle\right)}_{\eqqcolon\hat{T}_{2}}

\displaystyle\underbrace{-2\alpha\left(\left\langle\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta_{j},z_{j}),z_{j}-z^{*}\right\rangle+\left\langle\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}(\theta_{j},z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\theta_{j},z_{j}),\theta_{j}-\theta^{*}\right\rangle\right)}_{\eqqcolon\hat{T}_{3}}.

(28)

The term
\hat{T}_{1}=\alpha^{2}\left\|\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(w_{j})\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationc\left[\hat{T}_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha^{2}\expectationc\left[\left\|\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]

(29)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}\expectationc\left[\left\|\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\pm\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]

(30)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}\left[\expectationc\left[\left\|\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta_{j},z_{j})-\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}+\expectationc\left[\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right]^{2}

(31)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}\left[\eta\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}+\expectationc\left[\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}\right]^{2}

(32)

\displaystyle\leq\alpha^{2}\left[(\eta^{2}+\eta)\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+(1+\eta)\expectationc\left[\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]\right],

(33)

The term
\hat{T}_{2}=-2\alpha\left\langle\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{j}),w_{j}-w^{*}\right\rangle
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationc\left[\hat{T}_{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq-\alpha\mu\left(\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+\expectationc\left[(\theta_{j}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right]\right)-\alpha^{2}\expectationc\left[\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right],

(34)

where we have used Lemma
3.1
.
Finally, the term
\hat{T}_{3}=-2\alpha\left\langle\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(w_{j})-\nabla\mathcal{J}(w_{j}),w_{j}-w^{*}\right\rangle
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\expectationc\left[\hat{T}_{3}\right]

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha\expectationc\left[\left\|\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta_{j},z_{j})-\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}\left\|w_{j}-w^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}\right]

(35)

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha\expectationc\left[\left\|\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\theta_{j},z_{j})-\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\expectationc\left[\left\|w_{j}-w^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]^{1/2}

(36)

\displaystyle\leq 2\alpha\eta\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}\expectationc\left[\left\|w_{j}-w^{*}\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(37)

Combining the bounds for
\hat{T}_{1}
,
\hat{T}_{2}
and
\hat{T}_{3}
, we have the following:

\displaystyle\expectationc\left[\left\|z_{j+1}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha\mu)\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}-\alpha\mu\expectationc\left[(\theta_{j}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\alpha^{2}(\eta^{2}+\eta)\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+\alpha^{2}\eta\expectationc\left[\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+2\alpha\eta\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}\expectationc\left[\left\|w_{j}-w^{*}\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}\right]^{1/2}.

(38)

We now utilise Lemma
3.1
once again, from which we have the following result:

\displaystyle\alpha\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(w)\right\|_{l^{2}}\leq(1+\sqrt{1-\alpha\mu})\left\|w-w^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}\eqqcolon\tilde{L}\left\|w-w^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}},

(39)

for
0<\alpha\leq 1/L
and
\alpha\mu\leq 1
.
In addition, the last term of Eq. (
38
) can be rewritten as follows:

\displaystyle 2\alpha\eta\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|_{l^{2}}\expectationc\left[\left\|w_{j}-w^{*}\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}\right]^{1/2}\leq\eta\left(\frac{\alpha^{2}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{J}(\theta_{j-1},z_{j})\right\|^{2}_{l^{2}}}{\tilde{L}}+\tilde{L}\expectationc\left[\left\|w_{j}-w^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]\right)

(40)

Applying Eqs. (
39
) and (
40
) to Eq. (
38
), we then have the following simplified bound:

\displaystyle\expectationc\left[\left\|z_{j+1}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(1-\alpha\mu+(\eta^{2}+2\eta)\tilde{L}^{2}+2\eta\tilde{L}\right)\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\left(-\alpha\mu+\eta\tilde{L}^{2}+\eta\tilde{L}\right)\expectationc\left[(\theta_{j}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle+\left((\eta^{2}+\eta)\tilde{L}^{2}+\eta\tilde{L}\right)(\theta_{j-1}-\theta^{*})^{2},

(41)

\displaystyle=(1-C_{1}+C_{2})\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}-C_{1}\expectationc\left[(\theta_{j}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right]+C_{2}(\theta_{j-1}-\theta^{*})^{2},

(42)

where we have defined the constants
C_{1}=\alpha\mu-\eta\tilde{L}^{2}+\eta\tilde{L}
and
C_{2}=(\eta^{2}+\eta)\tilde{L}^{2}+\eta\tilde{L}
.
We then have the following:

\displaystyle\expectationc\left[\left\|z_{j+1}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}\right]+C_{1}\expectationc\left[(\theta_{j}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq(1-C_{1}+C_{2})\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+C_{2}(\theta_{j-1}-\theta^{*})^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\max\left(1-C_{1}+C_{2},\frac{C_{2}}{C_{1}}\right)\left(\left\|z_{j}-z^{*}\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+C_{1}(\theta_{j-1}-\theta^{*})^{2}\right).

(43)

We note that the leading constant on the right hand side is less than
1
as long as
C_{1}>C_{2}
, which holds true for
\eta<\sqrt{1+\alpha\mu/\tilde{L}^{2}}-1
.
This in turn ensures contraction in the norm
\left\|z\right\|_{l^{2}}^{2}+C_{1}\theta^{2}
on the space
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.
This completes the proof.

4
Gradient estimation and error control using
MLMC
methods

We note that the key assumption in the proof of Theorem
3.1
is Eq. (
24
); namely, that the gradient approximation is accurate up to a tolerance that is proportional to the magnitude of the true gradient.
As stated earlier in Section
1
, we are interested in utilising the framework of
MLMC
estimators for parametric expectations developed in
[
5
]
for the accurate estimation of the objective function
\mathcal{J}
(risk-measure
CVaR
) and its gradient.

Expressing the gradients
\mathcal{J}_{z}
and
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}
in terms of the first derivatives of the parametric expectations
\Phi(\theta;z)
and
\Psi(\theta;z)
as in Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
) and estimating the latter using
MLMC
estimators poses many key advantages.
The first advantage was already seen earlier in Section
3
; namely that
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}
and
\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}
can be estimated for all
\theta
for a given design
z
in one shot.
Secondly, as was demonstrated in
[
5
]
, the level-wise differences for the
MLMC
estimator of
\Phi(\theta;z)
, analogous to the level-wise differences corresponding to the classical
MLMC
estimator of
\expectation\left[Q\right]
, decay at the same rate in the levels
l
as the differences
Q_{l}-Q_{l-1}
, in an appropriately selected norm over
\theta\in\mathbb{R}
.
This ensures that if cost-optimal
MLMC
behaviour can be achieved for estimating
\expectation\left[Q\right]
, then it can be achieved also for
MLMC
estimators of
\Phi(\theta;z)
and
\Psi(\theta;z)
, using a practically computable number of samples.
The last key advantage is that, using the mechanism in
[
5
]
, one can select the parameters of the
MLMC
estimator such that a prescribed tolerance can be attained on the
MLMC
approximation error on
\Phi
and
\Psi
.
By prescribing a tolerance proportional to the gradient magnitude, one can estimate the gradient using
MLMC
estimators that respect the condition in Eq. (
24
) as required by Algorithm
1
.

Although the procedure used in this work to estimate
\Phi
accurately is identical to the one described in
[
5
]
, some important modifications are required to use the same procedure for accurately estimating
\Psi
.
We present in this section the modifications of the work developed in
[
5
]
that are required for the accurate estimation of
\Psi
, and consequently the gradients
\mathcal{J}_{\theta}
and
\mathcal{J}_{z}
, using the
MLMC
method.

4.1
MLMC
estimator for the gradients

We begin by recalling that the parametric expectation
\Psi
is defined as in Eq. (
18
).
The proposed
MLMC
method relies on a sequence of approximations
\{Q_{l}(z)\}_{l=0}^{L}
to
Q(z)
on a sequence of
L+1
discretisations with, for example, different mesh sizes
h_{0}>h_{1}>...>h_{L}
, typically a geometric sequence
h_{l-1}=sh_{l}
with
s>1
.
The
MLMC
estimator for the
k^{\text{th}}
component
\Psi_{k}(\cdot;z)\coloneqq\expectation\left[\psi(\cdot,Q(z),Q_{z^{k}}(z))\right]
of
\Psi
on
\Theta
,
k\in\{1,...,d\}
follows the same construction as that for
\Phi
in
[
5
]
.
The first step is to estimate
\Psi_{k}(\theta_{r},z),r\in\{1,...,n\}
, on a set of
n
equidistant points
\bm{\theta}=\{\theta_{1},...,\theta_{n}\}
such that
\Theta=[\theta_{1},\theta_{n}]
, by a standard
MLMC
estimator
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\theta_{r};z)
, which reads:

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\theta_{r};z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{0}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{0}}\psi\left(\theta_{r},Q_{0}^{(i,0)}(z),Q_{z^{k},0}^{(i,0)}(z)\right)

\displaystyle+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}\left[\psi\left(\theta_{r},Q_{l}^{(i,l)}(z),Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)}(z)\right)-\psi\left(\theta_{r},Q_{{l-1}}^{(i,l)}(z),Q_{{z^{k},l-1}}^{(i,l)}(z)\right)\right],

(44)

where
Q_{l}^{(i,l)}(z)\equiv Q_{l}(z;\omega^{(i,l)})
and
Q_{{l-1}}^{(i,l)}(z)\equiv Q_{l-1}(z;\omega^{(i,l)})
are correlated realisations of
Q_{l}(z)
and
Q_{l-1}(z)
, respectively, typically obtained by solving the underlying differential problem on meshes with discretisation parameters
h_{l}
and
h_{l-1}
, driven by the same realisation
\omega^{(i,l)}
of the random parameters for the fixed design
z
.
On the other hand,
Q_{l}^{(i,l)}
and
Q_{k}^{(j,k)}
are independent if
i\neq j
or
l\neq k
.
Finally,
Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)}
and
Q_{z^{k},l-1}^{(i,l)}
are the sensitivities of the realisations
Q_{l}^{(i,l)}
and
Q_{l-1}^{(i,l)}
respectively with respect to
z^{k}
.

\{N_{l}\}_{l=0}^{L}
is a decreasing sequence of sample sizes.
The
MLMC
hierarchy is hence defined by three parameters; namely the number of interpolation points
n
, the number of levels
L
and the level-wise sample sizes
N_{l}
.

We finally construct a
MLMC
estimator
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}
of the whole function
\Psi_{k}(\cdot;z):\Theta\to\mathbb{R}
by interpolating over the pointwise estimates as below:

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\cdot;z)=\mathcal{S}_{n}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\bm{\theta};z)\right),

(45)

where
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denotes a uniform cubic spline interpolation operator and
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\bm{\theta};z)
denotes the set of pointwise
MLMC
estimates in Eq. (
44
), that is
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\bm{\theta};z)=\{\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\theta_{1};z),\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\theta_{2};z),\dots,\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\theta_{n};z)\}
.
An estimate of the first derivative
\Psi_{k}^{\prime}
in
\theta
is then obtained by computing the derivative of the resultant interpolated function, for each component
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}
:

\displaystyle\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}(\cdot;z)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\prime}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\bm{\theta};z)\right)\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\mathcal{S}_{n}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}(\bm{\theta};z)\right).

(46)

4.2
Estimation of the
Mean Squared Error (MSE)
of the gradient

Since we have assumed that the gradient estimate
\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}
is a random vector in
\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
with
p\geq 2
, we propose to quantify the error on the gradient in an
MSE
sense as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\cdot,z_{j})\right)\coloneqq\expectation\left[\left\|\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}(\cdot,z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\cdot,z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}\right]+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\expectation\left[\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z,k}(\cdot,z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{z,k}(\cdot,z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}\right],

(47)

where
\mathcal{J}_{z,k}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z,k}
denote the
k^{\text{th}}
components of
\mathcal{J}_{z}
and
\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z}
.

We now present a result relating
\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\cdot,z_{j})\right)
to the
MSE
of the
MLMC
estimators
\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L}^{\prime}
.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\hat{\Phi}_{L}(\cdot;z_{j})
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L}(\cdot;z_{j})
denote the
MLMC
estimators of
\Phi(\cdot;z_{j})
and
\Psi(\cdot;z_{j})
as defined in
[
5
]
and Eq. (
44
) respectively.
Let
\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\cdot,z_{j})
be the approximation to the true gradient
\nabla\mathcal{J}(\cdot,z_{j})
computed using the estimates
\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})
at the
j^{\text{th}}
optimisation iteration.
Let
\Psi_{k}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}
denote the
k^{th}
component of
\Psi
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L}
respectively, for
k\in\{1,...,d\}
.
Let the
MSEs
on
\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}
be defined as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)(z_{j})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\expectation\left[\left\|\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})-\Phi^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}\right],

(48)

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}\right)(z_{j})

\displaystyle\coloneqq\expectation\left[\left\|\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})-\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}\right],

(49)

for the design
z_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then, we have that:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\cdot,z_{j})\right)=\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)(z_{j})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}\right)(z_{j}).

(50)

Proof.

We first note that:

\displaystyle\left\|\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{\theta}(\cdot,z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{\theta}(\cdot,z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\hat{\Phi}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})-\Phi^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}

(51)

\displaystyle\left\|\widetilde{\mathcal{J}}^{j}_{z,k}(\cdot,z_{j})-\mathcal{J}_{z,k}(\cdot,z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\|\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})-\Psi_{k}^{\prime}(\cdot;z_{j})\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}^{2}

(52)

Adding together each of the contribuitons and taking the expectation on both sides, we have that:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(\cdot,z_{j})\right)=\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)(z_{j})+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}\right)(z_{j}).

(53)

∎

As was described earlier in this section, we seek to use the error estimation and adaptivity procedure described in
[
5
]
to accurately estimate
\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Psi_{k}^{\prime}
, and consequently, to accurately estimate the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{J}
.
From Eq. (

), it is evident that if one can control the

of
\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}
in an

sense, one can control the
MSE
on the gradient
\nabla\mathcal{J}
as defined in Eq. (

).
Specifically, the

of the gradient is equal to a simple sum of the
MSEs
of the parametric expectations.
Eq. (
50
) hence allows us to use the work of
[
5
]
to accurately calibrate
MLMC

\Phi^{\prime}
and
\Psi^{\prime}
such that the resultant gradient estimate is accurate up to a prescribed tolerance.

4.3
Modified error estimation procedure

Since the error estimation procedure is independent of the design
z
, in the following, we drop the explicit dependence of
\Phi
and
\Psi
on
z
, with the dependence being implied.
We recall here that the error estimation procedure for estimating
\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)
is identical to that presented in
[
5
]
.
The procedure for estimating
\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)
however has several modifications from the procedure for
\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}
, that we detail in this section.
We recall that
\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)
was defined in Eq. (
49
).
Proceeding similarly as in
[
5
]
, we can bound
\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)
as follows:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}\right)\leq(\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{i})^{2}+(\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{b})^{2}+(\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{s})^{2},

(54)

where
\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{i}
,
\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{b}
and
\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{s}
denote error estimators that estimate the error due to interpolation, the error due to approximation of the
QoI
(i.e. bias error), and the error due to finite sampling (i.e. statistical error) respectively on
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}
.
The reader is referred to
[
5
]
for a detailed discussion of each of the three errors components, as well as their corresponding estimators.

The procedure for estimating the interpolation and bias errors requires the accurate estimation of
\theta
-derivatives of the function
\Psi_{l,k}(\theta)=\expectation\left[\psi(\theta,Q_{l},Q_{z^{k},l})\right]
.
Although the true function
\Psi_{l,k}
is smooth, replacing the true probability density with an empirical probability density corresponding to a Monte Carlo estimator implies that the right-hand side would be a linear combination of piecewise linear functions.
The first derivative of such a function would be piecewise constant, and high order derivatives would not exist in the discontinuity points, and would be zero otherwise.
A
MLMC
hierarchy designed based on estimates obtained in this manner would lead to non-optimal complexity behaviour.
In
[
5
, Section 3.2]
, a
Kernel Density Estimation (KDE)
based procedure was described for ameliorating this issue.
Although the error estimation procedure is broadly the same for estimating
\Psi
as for
\Phi
, an important distinction arises with respect to this
KDE
procedure, which we detail in this section.

Since the issue chiefly relates to the regularity of the empirical Monte Carlo probability density, we propose the use a
KDE
based smoothing technique; namely, we replace the true joint density
p_{l}
of
(Q_{l},Q_{z^{k},l})
with a
KDE
smoothed joint probability density
p_{l}^{kde}
, which consists of a linear combination of two-dimensional kernels composed of products of two one-dimensional Gaussian kernels centred on each of the
N_{l}
fine samples
\{(Q^{(i,l)}_{l},Q^{(i,l)}_{z^{k},l})\}_{i=1}^{N_{l}}
:

\displaystyle\Psi_{l,k}(\theta)

\displaystyle=\int\int\psi(\theta,q,q_{z^{k}})p_{l}(q,q_{z^{k}})dqdq_{z^{k}}

(55)

\displaystyle\approx\int\int\psi(\theta,q,q_{z^{k}})p^{kde}_{l}(q,q_{z^{k}})dqdq_{z^{k}}

(56)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}\int\int\psi(\theta,q,q_{z^{k}})K_{\delta_{l}}(q,Q_{l}^{(i,l)})K_{\delta_{z^{k},l}}(q_{z^{k}},Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)})dqdq_{z^{k}}

(57)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}\int q_{z^{k}}K_{\delta_{z^{k},l}}(q_{z^{k}},Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)})dq_{z^{k}}\int\frac{(q-\theta)^{+}}{1-\tau}K_{\delta_{l}}(q,Q_{l}^{(i,l)})dq

(58)

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)}\int\frac{(q-\theta)^{+}}{1-\tau}K_{\delta_{l}}(q,Q_{l}^{(i,l)})dq\eqqcolon\mathbb{E}^{kde}_{l,k}\left[\psi(\theta,\cdot,\cdot)\right].

(59)

Here,
K_{\delta_{l}}(\cdot,\mu)
denotes a Gaussian kernel with mean
\mu
and bandwidth parameter
\delta_{l}>0
, which is selected according to Scott’s rule
[
37
]
for the realisations
\{Q_{l}^{(i,l)}\}_{i=1}^{N_{l}}
and controls the ‘‘width’’ of the kernel.
A closed form expression can be computed for the integral in Eq. (
59
), leading to the
KDE
smoothened approximation
\mathbb{E}^{kde}_{l,k}\left[\psi(\theta,\cdot,\cdot)\right]
for
\Psi_{k}
.

According to the procedure in
[
5
]
, the interpolation error requires the estimation of the quantity
\left\|\Psi_{k}^{(4)}\right\|
, for which we use the
KDE
estimator described above.
To this end, we first select a level
\lceil L/2\rceil
from the
MLMC
hierarchy; this choice of level is to ensure that
\hat{\Psi}_{\lceil L/2\rceil,k}
is sufficiently close to
\Psi_{k}
, and
N_{\lceil L/2\rceil}
is large enough for the
KDE
procedure to produce accurate estimates.
We then construct the
KDE
approximation
\Upsilon_{\lceil L/2\rceil,k}(\theta)\coloneqq\mathbb{E}^{kde}_{\lceil L/2\rceil,k}\left[\psi(\theta,\cdot,\cdot)\right]
.
The fourth derivative
\Upsilon^{(4)}_{k}
is then constructed using a second order central finite difference scheme on a uniform grid on
\Theta
with
n^{\prime}\gg n
points.
The norm is evaluated on the same grid as follows:

\displaystyle\left\|\Psi_{k}^{(4)}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}\approx\max_{i\in\{1,...,n^{\prime}\}}\left|\Upsilon^{(4)}_{\lfloor L/2\rfloor,k}(\theta_{i})\right|.

(60)

For the bias error on
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}
, we are required to estimate the quantity

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{S}_{n}^{\prime}\left(\expectation\left[\psi(\theta,Q_{l},Q_{z^{k},l})-\psi(\theta,Q_{l-1},Q_{z^{k},l-1})\right]\right)\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}(\Theta)}.

(61)

Replacing the expectation by a Monte Carlo estimator leads to the same regularity issue as described earlier in this section.
To smooth the empirical Monte Carlo density, we propose the use of a
KDE
smoothed approximation
p_{l,l-1}^{kde}
to the true density
p_{l,l-1}
of
(Q_{l},Q_{z^{k},l},Q_{l-1},Q_{z^{k},l-1})
, consisting of products of four one-dimensional Gaussian kernels:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\psi(\theta,Q_{l},Q_{z^{k},l})-\psi(\theta,Q_{l-1},Q_{z^{k},l-1})\right]

(62)

\displaystyle=\int\int\int\int\left[\psi(\theta,q^{f},q^{f}_{z^{k}})-\psi(\theta,q^{c},q_{z^{k}}^{c})\right]p_{l,l-1}(q^{f},q^{f}_{z^{k}},q^{c},q^{c}_{z^{k}})dq^{f}dq^{f}_{z^{k}}dq^{c}dq_{z^{k}}^{c}

(63)

\displaystyle\approx\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}\int\int\int\int\left[\psi(\theta,q^{f},q^{f}_{z^{k}})-\psi(\theta,q^{c},q_{z^{k}}^{c})\right]

\displaystyle\times K_{\delta_{l}}(q^{f},Q_{l}^{(i,l)})K_{\delta_{z^{k},l}}(q^{f}_{z^{k}},Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)})K_{\delta_{l-1}}(q^{c},Q_{l-1}^{(i,l)})K_{\delta_{z^{k},l-1}}(q^{c}_{z^{k}},Q_{z^{k},l-1}^{(i,l)})dq^{f}dq^{f}_{z^{k}}dq^{c}dq_{z^{k}}^{c}

(64)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{N_{l}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{l}}Q_{z^{k},l-1}^{(i,l)}\int\frac{(q^{c}-\theta)^{+}}{1-\tau}K_{\delta_{l-1}}(q^{c},Q_{l-1}^{(i,l)})dq^{c}-Q_{z^{k},l}^{(i,l)}\int\frac{(q^{f}-\theta)^{+}}{1-\tau}K_{\delta_{l}}(q^{f},Q_{l}^{(i,l)})dq^{f}

(65)

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\mathbb{E}^{kde}_{l,l-1,k}\left[\psi(\theta,Q_{l},Q_{z^{k},l})-\psi(\theta,Q_{l-1},Q_{z^{k},l-1})\right].

(66)

The expectation in Eq. (
61
) can be replaced by the
KDE
smoothened expectation in Eq. (
66
), which can then be used in the bias error estimation procedure outlined in
[
5
]
.
Lastly, the procedure for the statistical error follows the idea of bootstrapping developed in
[
5
]
identically without modification.

4.4
Adaptive hierarchy selection procedure and
CMLMC
-gradient descent algorithm

We discuss in this section how to select the parameters of the
MLMC
hierarchy; namely the number of interpolation points
n
, the level-wise sample sizes
N_{l}
and the number of levels
L
.
The aim is to select these parameters such that a prescribed tolerance can be obtained on the gradient estimate
\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}
.
In what follows, we drop the dependence on
z
for notational simplicity, with the dependence being implied.
We propose here a minor variation of the framework presented in
[
5
, Section 5]
.
An adaptive strategy was proposed therein for the selection of the hierarchy parameters
n
,
L
and
N_{l}
for any statistic
s_{\tau}
, the
MSE
of whose estimator
\hat{s}_{\tau}
could be bounded by a linear combination of
MSEs
on
\hat{\Phi}_{L}
and its derivatives:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{s}_{\tau}\right)\leq c_{0}\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}\right)+c_{1}\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)+c_{2}\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime\prime}\right),\hskip 9.24994ptc_{0},c_{1},c_{2}>0.

(67)

We first note that the same hierarchy adaptivity procedure extends trivially to any linear combination of
MSEs
of
\hat{\Phi}_{L}
,
\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}
, and their derivatives.
Specifically, this includes the case of the
MSE
on the gradient
\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}
in Eq. (
50
).
In addition, each of the
MSEs
on the parametric expectations in Eq. (
50
) can be split into its three error contributions, similar to Eq. (
54
), leading to the following error estimator for
\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}(w)\right)
:

\displaystyle\textrm{MSE}\left(\tilde{\nabla}\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{d}\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Psi}_{L,k}^{\prime}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\underbrace{\left((\hat{\text{e}}^{\Phi}_{i})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{i})^{2}\right)}_{\text{Squared interpolation error}}+\underbrace{\left((\hat{\text{e}}^{\Phi}_{b})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{b})^{2}\right)}_{\text{Squared bias error}}+\underbrace{\left((\hat{\text{e}}^{\Phi}_{s})^{2}+\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\hat{\text{e}}^{\Psi_{k}}_{s})^{2}\right)}_{\text{Squared statistical error}}.

(68)

Here,
\hat{\text{e}}^{\Phi}_{i}
,
\hat{\text{e}}^{\Phi}_{b}
and
\hat{\text{e}}^{\Phi}_{s}
denote the interpolation, bias and statistical error estimators corresponding to
\textrm{MSE}\left(\hat{\Phi}_{L}^{\prime}\right)
.
Once in the above form, the procedure described in
[
5
]
for adapting the hierarchy parameters
n
,
L
and
N_{l}
for linear combinations of
MSEs
can be extended trivially to the current case when combined with the modifications proposed in Section
4.3
.

Lastly, we comment that the above adaptive procedure is carried out within the framework of the
CMLMC
algorithm presented in
[
5
]
.
The
CMLMC
algorithm works by first simulating a small ‘‘screening’’ hierarchy with relatively few samples and levels.
The algorithm then adapts the hierarchy parameters with respect to a decreasing set of tolerances, of which the target tolerance is the final one.
The optimal parameters for a given tolerance in the sequence are computed based on estimates obtained from the optimal hierarchy for the previous tolerance, or the initial ‘‘screening’’ hierarchy.
In this way, the
MLMC
estimator becomes robust to large variations in the estimates produced by an initial screening hierarchy.

We now possess all the ingredients required to tailor Algorithm

to the specific case in which an
MLMC

CMLMC

\hat{\mathcal{J}}^{j-1}_{w}(w_{j-1})
.
Another key difference to note is that in contrast to

5.1
FitzHugh Nagumo oscillator

To demonstrate the optimisation framework, we use the FitzHugh–Nagumo system described in
[
14
]
and
[
30
]
.
The FitzHugh–Nagumo model is a two dimensional simplification of the Hodgkin-Huxley model introduced by
[
18
]
, which was originally proposed in the field of neuroscience to model the phenomenon of spiking neurons.
The dynamical equations read as follows:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\dot{v}\\
\dot{w}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}v-\frac{v^{3}}{3}-w+I\\
\zeta\left(v+a-bw\right)\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 9.24994pt\left[\begin{matrix}v(t=0)\\
w(t=0)\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}v^{0}\\
w^{0}\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 9.24994ptt\in[0,T],

(69)

where
[v(t),w(t)]^{T}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
denotes the state variables and
a
,
b
,
\zeta
and
I
denote system parameters.
Fig.
1
shows a phase-space plot containing the
v
and
w
-nullclines for a nominal value of the system parameters.
The oscillator enters a limit cycle for parameter values such that the intersection of the two nullclines lies in the interval
v\in[-1,1]
, indicated by the black lines.
If the intersection lies exterior to this interval, then the oscillator eventually reaches the intersection and remains at a constant value of
v
and
w
.
Although initially proposed to model neuron behaviour, the FitzHugh–Nagumo model has seen widespread use in modelling wave phenomena in excitable media.
Examples include blood coagulation
[
13
,
27
]
and cardio-electrophysiological phenomena
[
9
]
, wherein the optimal control of the model plays an important role in the application.
The reader is referred to
[
39
]
for an overview of existing work on the modelling applications and optimal control of the FitzHugh–Nagumo system.

Figure 1:
FitzHugh–Nagumo oscillator dynamics

In this work, we study the forced FitzHugh–Nagumo system:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\dot{v}\\
\dot{w}\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}v-\frac{v^{3}}{3}-w+I+\sigma\dot{W}_{1}\\
\zeta\left(v+a-bw\right)+\sigma\dot{W}_{2}\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 9.24994pt\left[\begin{matrix}v(t=0)\\
w(t=0)\end{matrix}\right]=\left[\begin{matrix}v^{0}\\
w^{0}\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 9.24994ptt\in[0,T],

(70)

where
\dot{W}_{1}
and
\dot{W}_{2}
are ‘‘formal’’ derivatives of standard Brownian paths and
\sigma=0.01
controls the noise strength.
To study the behaviour of the system, we propose the following
QoI
:

\displaystyle Q=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}v^{2}(t)dt.

(71)

We are interested in minimising an objective function of the form in Eq. (
10
), where we seek to minimise the
CVaR
with significance
\tau=0.7
.
We denote by
z=[a,b,\zeta,I]^{T}
the vector of design parameters with respect to which we want to carry out the optimisation, and seek to penalise deviations from the design
z_{ref}=[0.8,0.7,0.08,1.0]
.

We discretise the interval
[0,T]
using a hierarchy of uniform grids
t_{j}=j\Delta t_{l},j\in\{0,1,...,N_{T,l}\}
, with
\Delta t_{l}=T/N_{T,l}
and
N_{T,l}=N_{T,0}2^{l}
.
We set
T=10
and
N_{T,0}=20
, and consider an Euler-Maruyama discretisation of Eq. (
70
).
Using the notation
v^{l}_{n}
to denote the approximation of
v(t_{n})
at level
l
, the discretised system then reads:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}v^{l}_{n+1}\\
w^{l}_{n+1}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}v^{l}_{n}\\
w^{l}_{n}\end{matrix}\right]+\Delta t_{l}\left[\begin{matrix}v^{l}_{n}-\frac{(v^{l}_{n})^{3}}{3}-w^{l}_{n}+I\\
\zeta\left(v^{l}_{n}+a-bw^{l}_{n}\right)\end{matrix}\right]+\sigma\sqrt{\Delta t_{l}}\left[\begin{matrix}\xi^{l}_{1,n}\\
\xi^{l}_{2,n}\end{matrix}\right],

(72)

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}v^{l}_{0}\\
w^{l}_{0}\end{matrix}\right]

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{matrix}v^{0}\\
w^{0}\end{matrix}\right],\hskip 9.24994ptn\in\{0,...,N_{T,l}-1\},

(73)

where
\xi^{l}_{1,n}
and
\xi^{l}_{2,n}
are independently drawn realisations of standard normal random variables.
The quantity of interest that we study is the following time average:

\displaystyle Q=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}v^{2}(t)dt\approx\sum_{n=0}^{N_{T,l}-1}\left(\frac{(v^{l}_{n})^{2}+(v^{l}_{n+1})^{2}}{2}\right)\frac{\Delta t_{l}}{T}\eqqcolon Q_{l}.

(74)

To compute the sensitivities
Q_{z,l}
, we utilize the method of adjoints.
We consider the corresponding adjoint variables
\lambda^{l}_{n}
and
\nu^{l}_{n}
corresponding to
v^{l}_{n}
and
w^{l}_{n}
,
n\in\{1,...,N_{T,l}\}
respectively.
The adjoint equation reads as follows:

\displaystyle\left[\begin{matrix}\lambda^{l}_{n}\\

Conclusions

The aim of this work was to tackle the challenge of minimising the
CVaR
of a random
QoI
, typically the output of a differential model with random inputs, over a suitable design space, using gradient-based optimisation techniques.
A main challenge in utilising gradient-based techniques was the differentiability of the
CVaR
in terms of the design variables.
A differentiability result was presented in Section
2
, which was a generalisation of the one presented in
[
21
]
, showing that gradient-based algorithms could still be used to directly minimise the
CVaR
without requiring smoothing.

The expression for the sensitivities of the
CVaR
with respect to design parameters required the computation of expectations of discontinuous functions of the
QoI
; namely, the indicator function.
Estimating this expectation naively using
MLMC
estimators could become impractically expensive, and possibly result in non-optimal complexity behaviour of the corresponding
MLMC
estimator.
A similar issue was discussed and tackled in
[
5
]
, and an alternative was proposed using the framework of parametric expectations.
We presented a modified expression for the sensitivities of the
CVaR
, based on derivatives of parametric expectations, thereby allowing us to use the work in
[
5
]
.
Based on this modification, we also presented a novel optimisation algorithm consisting of an alternating minimisation-gradient procedure.
We demonstrated a theoretical result that, under additional assumptions on the combined objective function in Eq. (
10
), the novel algorithm would achieve Q-linear convergence of the design iterates towards the optimal design in the optimisation iterations.

To enable the use of the work in
[
5
]
, we presented modifications of the
MLMC
estimator, the error estimation procedure and adaptive hierarchy selection procedure specific to computing the sensitivities of the
CVaR
.
Namely, a relation was derived between the
MSE
of the sensitivities and the
MSE
of the parametric expectations in Section
4
.
In addition, a modification of the
KDE
smoothing procedure presented in
[
5
]
was presented, specific to
CVaR
minimisation.
The combination of the
MSE
relation and
KDE
modification allowed us to trivially extend the error estimation and hierarchy adaptivity procedure of
[
5
]
to the current application.
Lastly, a minor modification of the
CMLMC
procedure of
[
5
]
was presented in Algorithm
2
, wherein the
CMLMC
was restarted from the optimal hierarchy of the previous design iterate.

The combination of gradient-based optimisation and
MLMC
estimation of the sensitivities of the
CVaR
was tested on two problems of practical relevance; namely the FitzHugh–Nagumo oscillator and a more applied problem of advection-reaction-diffusion problem used to model pollutant transport.
In both cases, it was observed that the novel error estimation procedure provided tight bounds on the
MSE
of the gradient as defined in Eq. (
47
).
In addition, the
CMLMC
algorithm was shown to produce the best-case complexity behaviour for the
MLMC
estimators of the sensitivities.
The
OUU
algorithm was shown to converge Q-linearly in the optimisation iterations, while also preserving the best case
MLMC
cost complexity.

The numerical examples considered in this work demonstrated that the
AMGD
procedure performs well for the cases presented here.
However, one may wish to improve on the performance of the algorithm by considering alternatives to the
AMGD
algorithm.
Such variations could, for example, include higher-order optimisation methods such as the Newton method.
It still remains to be seen whether higher-order methods can directly be used with objective functions of the type in problem (
10
), as well as whether the framework of parametric expectations can be combined with such an algorithm.
We plan to explore such questions in future works.

Proof of Theorem
2.1

To prove Theorem
2.1
on the Fréchet differentiability of the objective function
\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)
, we first prove an important result in Lemma
A.1
.
We recall that
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
is the set of
\mathrm{L}^{p}
-integrable random variables whose measures are atom-free.

Lemma A.1
.

Consider random variables
Y\in\Gamma\subset\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
and
\delta Y\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
.
We then have the following:

\displaystyle\lim_{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\to 0}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\right]

\displaystyle=0,

(85)

\displaystyle\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\lim_{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{lp}\to 0}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{-\delta Y\leq Y\leq 0\}}\right]

\displaystyle=0.

(86)

Proof.

We begin with the proof for Eq. (
85
), since the proof for Eq. (
86
) follows from identical arguments.
We make use of the following result; for any
X\in\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
, the following holds for any
\epsilon>0
and
p\geq 0
:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|X\right|\geq\epsilon\}}\right]\leq\expectation\left[\frac{\left|X\right|^{p}}{\epsilon^{p}}\right]=\left\|X\right\|^{p}_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\epsilon^{-p}.

(87)

Setting
\epsilon=\left\|X\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}
for some
\beta\in[0,1)
, we have that

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|X\right|\geq\left\|X\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]\leq\left\|X\right\|^{p-\beta p}_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}=\left\|X\right\|^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{L}^{p}},

(88)

where
\gamma\coloneqq p(1-\beta)
.
We rewrite the term within the limit in Eq. (
85
) as follows:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\left(\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|<\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|\geq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right)\right]

(89)

\displaystyle=\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|<\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]+\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|\geq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right].

(90)

The first term can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|<\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]

(91)

Due to dominated convergence, we can pass the limit into the expectation, resulting in the following:

\displaystyle\lim_{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\to 0}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\expectation\left[\lim_{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\to 0}\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]=\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y=0\}}\right]=0,

(92)

since
Y\in\Gamma
is atom-free.
The second term can be bounded as follows, where we use a Hölder inequality:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|\geq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{\infty}}\left\|\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|\geq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right\|_{\lebesgue^{1}}

(93)

\displaystyle\leq\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\left|\delta Y\right|\geq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}^{\beta}\}}\right]

(94)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|^{\gamma}_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}.

(95)

Hence, we have that the second term in Eq. (
90
) goes to zero as well with the application of the limit, thus concluding the proof for Eq. (
85
).
The proof for Eq. (
86
) follows from identical arguments.
∎

We now use the above result and present a proof of Theorem
2.1
.
We note that the function
\mathcal{R}(\theta,Q)
is a composition of two functions.
We define the functions
l_{1}:\Gamma\to\mathbb{R}
and
l_{2}:\mathbb{R}\times\Gamma\to\Gamma
as follows:

\displaystyle l_{1}(Y)

\displaystyle\coloneq\expectation\left[Y^{+}\right]

(96)

\displaystyle l_{2}(\theta,Q)

\displaystyle\coloneqq Q-\theta,

(97)

\displaystyle\implies\mathcal{R}(\theta,Q)

\displaystyle=\theta+\frac{l_{1}\circ l_{2}(\theta,Q)}{1-\tau}.

(98)

Hence, to show that
\mathcal{R}
is Fréchet differentiable, it suffices to show that each of the functions
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
are Fréchet differentiable.

It is straightforward to see that
l_{2}
is Fréchet differentiable (being linear and bounded) with Fréchet derivative
Dl_{2}(\theta,Q)
in the direction
(\delta\theta,\delta Q)\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathrm{L}^{p}(\Omega,\mathbb{R})
given by:

\displaystyle Dl_{2}(\theta,Q)(\delta\theta,\delta Q)=\delta Q-\delta\theta.

(99)

The Fréchet derivative of
l_{1}
however, requires some consideration.
We argue that the Fréchet derivative of
l_{1}
exists at any point
Y\in\Gamma
and is given by
Dl_{1}(Y)(\delta Y)=\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\delta Y\right]
.
To prove this statement, we must verify the following limit:

\displaystyle\lim_{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\to 0}\frac{\left|\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)^{+}\right]-\expectation\left[Y^{+}\right]-\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\delta Y\right]\right|}{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}=0

(100)

To show the above, we begin by re-writing the numerator as follows:

\displaystyle\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)^{+}-Y^{+}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\delta Y\right]

\displaystyle=\expectation\left[\delta Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y\geq 0,Y\geq 0\}}-\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\delta Y\right]

\displaystyle+\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y\geq 0,Y<0\}}\right]

\displaystyle-\expectation\left[Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y<0,Y\geq 0\}}\right].

(101)

Inserting Eq. (
101
) into Eq. (
100
), we have the following:

\displaystyle\frac{\left|\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)^{+}\right]-\expectation\left[Y^{+}\right]-\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0}\delta Y\}\right]\right|}{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}\leq\frac{T_{1}+T_{2}+T_{3}}{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}},

(102)

with the terms
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
and
T_{3}
given by:

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|\expectation\left[\delta Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y\geq 0,Y\geq 0\}}-\delta Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\right]\right|,

(103)

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y\geq 0,Y<0\}}\right]\right|,

(104)

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left|\expectation\left[Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y<0,Y\geq 0\}}\right]\right|.

(105)

We then begin with the term
T_{1}
.
We first note that
T_{1}
can be rewritten in the following manner:

\displaystyle T_{1}

\displaystyle=\left|-\expectation\left[\delta Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y<-\delta Y\}}\right]\right|\leq\expectation\left[\left|\delta Y\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y<-\delta Y\}}\right]

(106)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\left\|\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y<-\delta Y\}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}=\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y<-\delta Y\}}\right]^{1/q},

(107)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\right]^{1/q}.

(108)

The term
T_{2}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle T_{2}

\displaystyle=\left|\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y\geq 0\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y<0\}}\right]\right|\leq\expectation\left[|\delta Y|\mathbbm{1}_{\{-\delta Y\leq Y<0\}}\right],

(109)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\left\|\mathbbm{1}_{\{-\delta Y\leq Y<0\}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}=\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{-\delta Y\leq Y\leq 0\}}\right]^{1/q}.

(110)

Similarly, the term
T_{3}
can be bounded as follows:

\displaystyle T_{3}

\displaystyle=\left|\expectation\left[Y\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y+\delta Y<0\}}\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\right]\right|\leq\expectation\left[\left|\delta Y\right|\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y<-\delta Y\}}\right]

(111)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\left\|\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{q}}=\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{0\leq Y\leq-\delta Y\}}\right]^{1/q}.

(112)

Inserting Eqs. (
108
), (
110
) and (
112
) into Eq. (
102
), and applying the limit using Lemma
A.1
, we have that:

\displaystyle\lim_{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}\to 0}\frac{\left|\expectation\left[(Y+\delta Y)^{+}\right]-\expectation\left[Y^{+}\right]-\expectation\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{Y\geq 0\}}\delta Y\right]\right|}{\left\|\delta Y\right\|_{\mathrm{L}^{p}}}=0.

(113)

This concludes the proof.

Appendix B
Adjoint of first-order
ODE
with additive noise

We present here the derivation of the adjoints for a first-order
Ordinary Differential Equation (ODE)
with white noise forcing for an objective function containing the
CVaR
of a time-averaged quantity of the trajectory.
Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P})
be a complete probability space,
\omega\in\Omega
denote an elementary random event, and
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the set of design variables.
Let
u(t,z,\omega)\in U\subset\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
be the state vector at time
t\in[0,T]
for a given random input
\omega
and design
z
. The state vector
u
is governed by the following
ODE
with additive noise.

\displaystyle\dot{u}(t,z,\omega)

\displaystyle=g(u,z)+\tau\dot{W}(t,\omega)\hskip 9.24994pt\text{over }(0,T],

(114)

\displaystyle u(0,z,\omega)

\displaystyle=u^{0},

(115)

where
g:U\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
, and
W:[0,T]\times\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
is a
N_{u}
-dimensional standard Wiener process.

We discretise the problem on a uniform temporal grid
\mathbb{T}
where the interval
[0,T]
is divided into
N\in\mathbb{N}
segments of step size
\Delta t=T/N
,
\mathbb{T}\coloneqq\{t_{n}\coloneqq n\Delta t:n\in\llbracket 0,N_{l}\rrbracket\}
.
The
ODE
is discretised using the Euler–Maruyama scheme, which reads as follows:

\displaystyle u^{n+1}

\displaystyle=u^{n}+\Delta tg(u^{n},z)+\tau\sqrt{\Delta t}\xi^{n},

\displaystyle u^{0}

\displaystyle=u_{0},

where
u^{n}
denotes the approximation to
u(t_{n},z,\omega)
,
\xi^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
are
N_{u}
-dimensional random vectors whose components are independent identically distributed standard normal variables.
We are interested in computing the statistics of time-averages of functions of the trajectory.

\displaystyle Q=\langle f(u)\rangle_{T}.

(116)

We approximate the time integral using the trapezoid rule on the aforementioned temporal grid, leading to

\displaystyle Q(z,\omega)\approx Q_{h}(z,\omega)\coloneqq\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{f(u^{n})+f(u^{n+1})}{2}\right)\frac{\Delta t}{T}.

(117)

We are interested in minimising the
CVaR
of this quantity over the parameters
z
but use the combined formulation in Eq. (
10
).
The corresponding Lagrangian for the problem reads

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\theta,z,\{u^{n}\},\{\lambda^{n}\})=\theta+\frac{\expectation\left[(Q(z,\cdot)-\theta)^{+}\right]}{1-\tau}+\expectation\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda^{n+1}\left(u^{n}+\Delta tg^{n}+\tau\sqrt{\Delta t}\xi^{n}-u^{n+1}\right)-\lambda^{0}(u^{0}-u_{0})\right],

(118)

where we use
g^{n}\coloneqq g(u^{n},z)
, and
\lambda^{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{u}}
,
n\in\llbracket 0,N\rrbracket
denote the Lagrange multipliers for the initial condition and the steps of the discretised equations.

Differentiating with respect to
z
gives

\displaystyle\frac{\dif\mathcal{L}}{\dif{}z}

\displaystyle=\begin{multlined}\expectation\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\left(\frac{f^{n}_{u}u^{n}_{z}+f^{n+1}_{u}u^{n+1}_{z}}{2}\right)\Delta t\right]\\
+\expectation\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\lambda^{n+1}\left(u_{z}^{n}+\Delta t(g_{u}^{n}u_{z}^{n}+g_{z}^{n})-u_{z}^{n+1}\right)\right]\end{multlined}

\displaystyle\eqqcolon\expectation\left[\hat{\mathcal{L}}\right].

(121)

Re-arranging the terms leads to

\hat{\mathcal{L}}=u_{z}^{0}\left[\lambda^{1}(1+\Delta tg_{u}^{0})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\frac{f_{u}^{0}\Delta t}{2}\right]+\Delta t\lambda^{1}g_{z}^{0}+u_{z}^{N}\left[\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\frac{f_{u}^{N}\Delta t}{2}-\lambda^{N}\right]\\
+\sum_{n=1}^{N-1}u_{z}^{n}\left[\lambda^{n+1}(1+\Delta tg_{u}^{n})-\lambda^{n}+\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\Delta tf_{u}^{n}\right]+\Delta t\lambda^{n+1}g_{z}^{n},

(122)

where we have used the subscript notation for partial derivatives.

We have in our case that
u_{z}^{0}=0
.
To remove terms dependent on
u_{z}^{n}
, we set

\displaystyle\lambda^{n}

\displaystyle=\lambda^{n+1}(1+\Delta tg_{u}^{n})+\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\Delta tf_{u}^{n},\hskip 9.24994ptn=1,...,N-1

(123)

\displaystyle\lambda^{N}

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\frac{f_{u}^{N}\Delta t}{2}.

(124)

This gives us the adjoint equations which are solved backwards in time.
It is noteworthy to mention that since Eq. (
123
) is linear, that it can be solved for
\{\lambda^{n}\}
without the factor
\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{h}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}
, and equivalently, the sensitivities can be computed as:

\displaystyle\frac{\dif\mathcal{L}}{\dif{}z}=\frac{\mathbbm{1}_{Q_{l}\geq\theta}}{(1-\tau)T}\expectation\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Delta t\lambda^{n+1}g_{z}^{n}\right].

(125)

That is, setting

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\theta,z)=\theta+\frac{\expectation\left[(Q_{h}(z,\cdot)-\theta)^{+}\right]}{1-\tau},

(126)

we have that
\mathcal{J}_{z}(\theta,z)=\expectation\left[\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}\Delta t\lambda^{n+1}g_{z}^{n}\right]
.
