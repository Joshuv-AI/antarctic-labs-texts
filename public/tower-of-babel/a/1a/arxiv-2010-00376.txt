Abstract
. We extend the classical Bernstein technique to the setting of integro-differential operators. As a consequence, we provide first and one-sided second derivative estimates for solutions to fractional equations, including some convex fully nonlinear equations of order smaller than two —for which we prove uniform estimates as their order approaches two. Our method is robust enough to be applied to some Pucci-type extremal equations and to obstacle problems for fractional operators, although several of the results are new even in the linear case. We also raise some intriguing open questions, one of them concerning the “pure” linear fractional Laplacian, another one being the validity of one-sided second derivative estimates for Pucci-type convex equations associated to linear operators with general kernels.

1.
Introduction

The Bernstein technique is a powerful tool to establish derivative estimates, through the use of auxiliary functions and the maximum principle, for solutions of elliptic equations. The goal of this paper is to extend this method to the setting of fractional equations. Up to our knowledge, this is done in the current work for the first time in a systematic way,
even for the fractional Laplacian.
The technique will allow us to establish first and one-sided second derivative estimates for a large class of integro-differential equations, including some fully nonlinear equations of order smaller than two.

Fractional diffusions arise in classical models, notably in the description of water waves, of atom dislocations in crystals, and in the displacement of
an elastic membrane on a thin obstacle. These problems can be efficiently attacked
by transforming them into a fractional setting on a lower dimensional (or boundary) object.
More recent models include energy transfer in nanotubes,
plasma physics, price oscillations in stock markets, and biological dispersals
in sparse environments —see e.g.
[
MR3469920
]
and references
therein.

The Bernstein technique —as introduced in the local case by Bernstein
himself
[
MR1511375
,
MR1511579
]
—
relies on considering some auxiliary functions
which involve the solution, its derivatives, and suitable cutoff functions. In view of certain equations (inequalities, rather) satisfied by the auxiliary functions and thanks to the maximum principle, they allow to estimate higher derivatives of the solution in terms of lower order ones,
by paying a price in the size of the reference domain.

Let us recall this procedure with the simplest example in local equations, the Laplace operator. This will serve as a preparation for the nonlocal framework.
Given two functions
u
and
\eta
(
u
must be thought as the solution of an equation, while
\eta
will be a cutoff), both smooth enough, consider the auxiliary function

(1.1)

\varphi:=\eta^{2}(\partial_{e}u)^{2}+\sigma u^{2},

where
e\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a unit vector,
|e|=1
, and
\sigma>0
is a constant. We have that

(1.2)

\begin{split}-\Delta\varphi\;&=\;-2|\nabla\eta|^{2}(\partial_{e}u)^{2}-2\eta\Delta\eta(\partial_{e}u)^{2}-8\eta\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla(\partial_{e}u)\partial_{e}u\\
&\qquad-2\eta^{2}|\nabla\partial_{e}u|^{2}-2\eta^{2}\partial_{e}u\,\partial_{e}\Delta u-2\sigma|\nabla u|^{2}-2\sigma u\Delta u.\end{split}

Since, by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality

\big|8\eta\nabla\eta\cdot\nabla(\partial_{e}u)\partial_{e}u\big|\leqslant 2\eta^{2}\,|\nabla\partial_{e}u|^{2}+8|\nabla\eta|^{2}(\partial_{e}u)^{2},

equation (
1.2
) yields

\begin{split}-\Delta\varphi\;&\leqslant\;6|\nabla\eta|^{2}(\partial_{e}u)^{2}-2\eta\Delta\eta(\partial_{e}u)^{2}-2\sigma|\nabla u|^{2}+2\eta^{2}\partial_{e}u\,(-\Delta)\partial_{e}u+2\sigma u(-\Delta u).\end{split}

In particular, by choosing
\sigma\geqslant C_{n}\|\eta\|^{2}_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
for an appropriate constant
C_{n}
depending on
n
(more specifically, on the precise way in which the
C^{2}
-norm of a function in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is defined),
we obtain that

(1.3)

\begin{split}-\Delta\left(\eta^{2}(\partial_{e}u)^{2}+\sigma u^{2}\right)\;\leqslant\;2\eta^{2}\partial_{e}u\,(-\Delta)\partial_{e}u+2\sigma u\,(-\Delta u)\qquad\text{ if }\sigma\geqslant C_{n}\|\eta\|^{2}_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}.\end{split}

This is a “clean”, key inequality satisfied by any function
u
(not necessarily a solution of an equation).

Now, if we assume the function
u

to be harmonic, say
-\Delta u=0
in
B_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we deduce from (
1.3
)
that
-\Delta\varphi\leqslant 0
in
B_{1}
. If, in addition, we take the function
\eta\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1})
to have compact support in
B_{1}
and to satisfy
\eta=1
in
B_{1/2}
,
the maximum principle for subharmonic functions ensures that
\varphi

attains its maximum along
\partial B_{1}
. As a consequence,

\sup_{B_{1/2}}(\partial_{e}u)^{2}\leqslant\sup_{B_{1/2}}\varphi\leqslant\sup_{B_{1}}\varphi=\sup_{\partial B_{1}}\varphi=\sigma\sup_{\partial B_{1}}u^{2}\leqslant\sigma\|u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}^{2},

thus yielding an explicit interior gradient estimate for the solution
u
. As we will see, simple variations
of this method, in which higher derivatives are taken into account
within the auxiliary function, lead to higher order estimates as well.

In spite of its rather elementary flavor, the Bernstein method is a powerful nonvariational tool that finds applications in several contexts and
for a large number of equations. The quadratic auxiliary function above (which is the one that we will consider within the nonlocal setting) finds applications even for second order fully nonlinear uniformly elliptic equations; see the monograph
[
CC
, Chapter 9]
.
More sophisticated auxiliary functions (with other nonlinear dependences on
u
and
\partial_{e}u
) lead to gradient estimates for the prescribed mean curvature equation; see e.g.
[
MR1617971
]

and references therein.

Instead, to the best of our knowledge, the Bernstein method for the quadratic auxiliary function (
1.1
) has not been yet studied in relation with
fractional and integro-differential equations, not even for the fractional Laplacian. In this respect, the closest work that we could find is
[
MR2405856
]
, by Biswas, Jakobsen, and Karlsen, which concerns an integro-differential equation of parabolic type posed in the whole space. Here the Bernstein technique is applied to a quadratic auxiliary function depending
on incremental quotients (but not containing the cut-off function
\eta
) to obtain suitable
Lipschitz bounds, which are then exploited to deal with the convergence
of the discrete scheme under consideration.

In accordance with our proofs and results, we must merge
the operators that we treat into two
categories. The first one consists of
equations built from
operators that admit a local extension in one more
dimension
, as it is the case of the fractional Laplacian.
Our second category of equations consists of linear
integro-differential
operators with general kernels
, as well as fully nonlinear operators
built from them.

In the next subsections, we describe in detail the
framework and results of our work.

1.1.
Pucci-type equations in the presence of extensions

We start dealing with the case of Pucci-type equations associated to affine transformations of the fractional Laplacian with elliptic matrices.
To built them, given constants
0<\lambda\leqslant\Lambda

(1.4)

{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{A}}_{\lambda,\Lambda}\text{ be the set of $n$-dimensional symmetric

Now, for a given

\begin{split}{\mathcal{L}}_{A}u(x)\,&:=c_{n,s}\,{\rm P.V.}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\frac{u(x)-u(y)}{|A(x-y)|^{n+2s}}\,dy\\

1.2.
Pucci-type equations for general integro-differential operators

In this paper we also take into account the case of operators with more
general kernels, more precisely, kernels which are not pure powers, neither
rotationally invariant. This setting

is pointwise equivalent to

\begin{split}&2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(x)\,\big(\eta(x)-\eta(y)\big)\,{\mathcal{G}}\big(\partial_{e}u(x)\big)\,{\mathcal{G}}\big(\partial_{e}u(y)\big)\,K(x-y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big|\eta(x)\,{\mathcal{G}}\big(\partial_{e}u(x)\big)-\eta(y)\,{\mathcal{G}}\big(\partial_{e}u(y)\big)\Big|^{2}\,K(x-y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad+\sigma\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(x)-u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(x-y)\,dy+E.\end{split}

From this, as explained above, one deduces Propositions
1.4
and
2.1
.
∎

2.2.
Proof of the first key inequality with a remainder

This subsection contains the proof
of our main inequality for the auxiliary function in the case of general
integro-differential operators.

Proof of Theorem
1.5
.

By the translation invariance of the problem, we see that, to establish (
1.22
)
in
B_{2}
,
it suffices to prove that

(2.5)

\begin{split}&2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad\leqslant\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)-\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\sigma_{\varepsilon}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(0)-u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+\varepsilon^{2}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}.\end{split}

Once this is proved, and using Proposition
1.4
at
x=0

with
E:=\varepsilon^{2}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}
,
the right-hand side of inequality (
1.22
)
in
B_{2}
will become
\varepsilon^{2}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|^{2}_{L^{\infty}(B_{3})}
.

To prove (
2.5
), we exploit an appropriate cutoff procedure
on the gradient of
u
, to suitably remove the singularity
of the integrand near the origin in the left-hand side of (
2.5
), (without spoiling the estimates
at infinity).
Namely,
we consider an odd function
\xi\in C^{\infty}_{c}((-2,2))

such that
\xi(t)=t
if
t\in(-1,1)
, and

|\xi(t)|\leqslant 2
for every
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
We also consider
\delta\in\left(0,\frac{1}{2}\right]
, and set
\xi_{\delta}(t):=\delta\xi(t/\delta)
.
We observe that

(2.6)

\xi_{\delta}(0)=0,\quad\xi_{\delta}^{\prime}(0)=1,\quad{\mbox{ and }}\quad\|\xi_{\delta}\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R})}\leqslant\frac{C}{\delta},

for some universal constant
C
.

We also introduce the map
\mathbb{R}^{n}\ni y=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})\mapsto{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y):=\big(\xi_{\delta}(y_{1}),\dots,\xi_{\delta}(y_{n})\big)
.
Since
\xi_{\delta}
is odd, we have that

(2.7)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\,K(y)\,dy=0,

in the principal value sense, thanks to the symmetry of the kernel (
1.12
).
Consequently, we have that

(2.8)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big(\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)+\eta(0)|\partial_{e}u(0)|^{2}\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\Big)\,K(y)\,dy.\end{split}

Furthermore, we set

(2.9)

I_{1}(y)\,:=\,\eta(0)\,\Big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)+\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\Big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y),

(2.10)

I_{2}(y)\,:=\,\partial_{e}u(0)\,\big(\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)-\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)\big)\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y),

and

(2.11)

I_{3}(y)\,:=\,\big(\eta(y)-\eta(0)\big)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y),

and we
point out that

(2.12)

\begin{split}&\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)+\eta(0)|\partial_{e}u(0)|^{2}\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\\
&\qquad\qquad=\;\eta(0)\,\Big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)+\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\Big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\big(\partial_{e}u(0)-\partial_{e}u(y)\big)\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\\
&\qquad\qquad=\;I_{1}(y)+\partial_{e}u(0)\,\big(\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)-\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)\big)\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad+\partial_{e}u(0)\,\big(\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(y)\big)\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)\\
&\qquad\qquad=\;I_{1}(y)+I_{2}(y)+I_{3}(y).\end{split}

Hence, substituting into (
2.8
), we obtain

(2.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\,K(y)\,dy=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(I_{1}(y)+I_{2}(y)+I_{3}(y)\big)\,K(y)\,dy.

Now, we set

(2.14)

\varphi_{\delta}(y):=\eta(0)-\eta(y)+\nabla\eta(0)\cdot{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y),

and we observe that

(2.15)

\varphi_{\delta}(0)=0,\quad\nabla\varphi_{\delta}(0)=0,\quad{\mbox{ and }}\quad\|\varphi_{\delta}\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}\leqslant C_{\delta}\,\|\eta\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})},

for some constant
C_{\delta}>0
, depending only on
n
and
\delta
,
thanks to the properties (
2.6
) of
\xi_{\delta}
.

We also set

(2.16)

{J_{1}}\,:=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\,\big(\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,dy

and

(2.17)

{J_{2}}\,:=\,\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{e}\,\Big(\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\eta(y)\Big)\,\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,dy.

We now perform some integration by parts in
B_{R}
.
To this end, we use that the integrals involved in the computation are finite and that the boundary terms
on
\partial B_{R}
converge to zero as
R\to+\infty
,
thanks to
the decay of the kernel and of its derivatives assumed in (
1.13
) and (
1.14
).
More precisely, from (
2.9
) and (
2.14
), and integrating by parts
twice, we find that

(2.18)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{1}(y)\,K(y)\,dy\,&=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\varphi_{\delta}(y)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi_{\delta}(y)\,\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&=\,-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\,\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,dy\\
&=\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\big(\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,dy\\
&\qquad\qquad-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,dy\\
&=\,{J_{1}}-\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\,\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,dy\\
&=\,{J_{1}}+\frac{1}{2}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}{\partial_{e}}\,\Big(\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\eta(y)\Big)\,\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,dy\\
&=\,{J_{1}}+{J_{2}}.\end{split}

Furthermore, recalling again
the bound (
1.14
) on the first and second derivatives
of the kernel and (
2.15
),
we point out that

(2.19)

\begin{split}&\Big|\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\Big|\leqslant\big|\partial_{e}\varphi_{\delta}(y)\big|\,K(y)+\big|\varphi_{\delta}(y)\big|\,\big|\partial_{e}K(y)\big|\\
&\qquad\leqslant C_{\delta,\eta}\big(K(y)+|y|\,\big|\partial_{e}K(y)\big|\big)\leqslant C_{\delta,\eta}\,K(y),\end{split}

for some constant
C_{\delta,\eta}>0
, possibly varying from line to line,
and depending
only on
n
,
\delta
,

\|\eta\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
,
and on the structural constant
C_{3}

in (
1.14
)
(for convenience, in what follows, we will rename
C_{\delta,\eta}

allowing dependences also
on
s
and on the constants
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in (
1.13
)).

In addition, using again (
1.14
) and (
2.15
),

\displaystyle\Big|\partial^{2}_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\eta(y)\Big|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{\delta,\eta}\,\left(\Big|\partial_{e}^{2}\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\Big|+\Big|\partial_{e}\varphi_{\delta}(y)\,\partial_{e}K(y)\Big|+\Big|\varphi_{\delta}(y)\,\partial_{e}^{2}K(y)\Big|\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{\delta,\eta}\,\Big(K(y)+|y|\,\big|\partial_{e}K(y)\big|+|y|^{2}\big|D^{2}K(y)\big|\Big)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C_{\delta,\eta}\,K(y).

Hence, from the latter estimate and (
2.19
),

(2.20)

\Big|\partial_{e}\,\Big(\partial_{e}\big(\varphi_{\delta}(y)K(y)\big)\eta(y)\Big)\Big|\leqslant C_{\delta,\eta}\,K(y).

Thus, by the definitions (
2.16
) and (
2.17
)
of
J_{1}
and
J_{2}
, and the estimates (
2.19
) and (
2.20
),
using an appropriate Cauchy-Schwarz inequality
we get that

(2.21)

\begin{split}&|{J_{1}}|+|{J_{2}}|\\
&\qquad\leqslant\,C_{\delta,\eta}\,\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big|\,\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|\,K(y)\,dy\right.\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\left.+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy\right)\\
&\qquad\leqslant\,\frac{1}{8}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+C_{\delta,\eta}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy.\end{split}

This and (
2.18
) give that

(2.22)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{1}(y)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\;\frac{1}{8}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+C_{\delta,\eta}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy.\end{split}

Next, we notice that
|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|\leqslant C\,|y|
, for every
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, for some constant
C
depending only
on
n
.
Thus, we have that

(2.23)

J_{3}:=\int_{B_{1}}|y|\,|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|\,K(y)\,dy<+\infty.

In view of the definition (
2.10
) of
I_{2}
,
noticing that
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}
is supported in
B_{1}
,
and using a suitable Cauchy-Schwarz inequality,
we have that

(2.24)

\begin{split}&\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{2}(y)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\,C_{\eta}\,\int_{B_{1}}\big|\partial_{e}u(0)\big|\big|\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)-\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)\big|\;|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\,\frac{1}{8}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)-\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+C_{\eta}\,\int_{B_{1}}\big|\partial_{e}u(0)\big|^{2}\;|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\leqslant\,\frac{1}{8}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(0)\partial_{e}u(0)-\eta(y)\partial_{e}u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+C_{\eta}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}^{2}\,J_{3}\end{split}

for some constant
C_{\eta}>0
depending only
on
n
and
\|\eta\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
.
Similarly, recalling the definition (
2.11
)
of
I_{3}
,

(2.25)

\begin{split}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}I_{3}(y)\,K(y)\,dy\,\leqslant\,&C_{\eta}\,\int_{B_{1}}\big|\eta(y)-\eta(0)\big|\,|\partial_{e}u(0)|\,|\partial_{e}u(y)|\;|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|\,K(y)\,dy\\
\leqslant\,&C_{\eta}\,\int_{B_{1}}|y|\,|\partial_{e}u(0)|\,|\partial_{e}u(y)|\;|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|\,K(y)\,dy\\
\leqslant\,&C_{\eta}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}^{2}\,J_{3}.\end{split}

Now we provide a bound on
J_{3}
. To this aim, we split
the integral computation inside
B_{\delta}
, where
{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)=y
,
and in
B_{1}\setminus B_{\delta}
, where
|{\mathcal{Z}}_{\delta}(y)|\leqslant 2\delta\,n^{1/2}
.
In this way, recalling the definition (
2.23
)
of
J_{3}
, we get that

\displaystyle|J_{3}|

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle C\,\left(\int_{B_{\delta}}|y|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+\delta\int_{B_{1}\setminus B_{\delta}}|y|\,K(y)\,dy\right),

for some constant
C
depending only on
n
.
The latter quantity tends to zero as
\delta\searrow 0
,
thanks to the bounds (
1.13
) on the kernel.
Given
\varepsilon>0
, we can therefore
take
\delta\in(0,1)
sufficiently small,
in such a way that

|J_{3}|\leqslant\varepsilon^{2}.

After this choice of
\delta
,
the constants
C_{\delta,\eta}
above
will be written accordingly,
with a slight abuse of notation, as
C_{\varepsilon,\eta}
.
As a consequence, collecting the estimates in (
2.22
),
(
2.24
), and (
2.25
), we conclude that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(I_{1}(y)+I_{2}(y)+I_{3}(y)\big)\,K(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+C_{\varepsilon,\eta}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+C_{\eta}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}^{2}.

Therefore, recalling (
2.13
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\partial_{e}u(0)\,\partial_{e}u(y)\,K(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(y)\,\partial_{e}u(y)-\eta(0)\,\partial_{e}u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+C_{\varepsilon,\eta}\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+C_{\eta}\,\varepsilon^{2}\,\|\partial_{e}u\|_{L^{\infty}(B_{1})}^{2}.

This establishes (
2.5
), up to renaming
\varepsilon
and the constants,
by choosing
\sigma_{\varepsilon}
large enough.
As a consequence, also (
1.22
) follows.
∎

2.3.
“Downstairs”
proof of the first key inequality for
s
-harmonic functions

The next result is the particular
case of Proposition
1.2
where
u
is
s
-harmonic.
While the proof of Proposition
1.2

will rely on extension methods, we provide here a
proof of this particular case
without using the extension. Note that, as in Proposition
1.2
,
\sigma_{0}
is independent of
s
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\eta\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})\cap W^{2,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
,
and assume that
u\in W^{1,\infty}(\mathbb{R}^{n})

is a (weak) solution of

(2.26)

(-\Delta)^{s}u=0\quad{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}.

Then, there exists
\sigma_{0}>0
,
depending only on
n
and
\|\eta\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}
, such that

(2.27)

(-\Delta)^{s}\big(\eta^{2}|\nabla u|^{2}+\sigma u^{2}\big)\leqslant 0\quad{\mbox{ in }}B_{1}\quad\text{ if }\sigma\geqslant\sigma_{0}.

Proof
(
without using the extension problem
)
.

Though the statement
of Lemma
2.2
is specific for the fractional Laplacian,
we perform the initial part of the proof arguing for a general kernel
K
,
to isolate the only point where we will use that
K(z)=c_{n,s}|z|^{-n-2s}
.

The proof relies on several integrations by
parts, which carefully take into account oscillations
and compensations inside the integrals.

We observe that, by regularity results for (
2.26
),
u\in C^{\infty}(B_{1})
.
Exploiting Proposition
, in order
to prove (
2.27
),
it suffices to show, by translation invariance, that

(2.28)

\begin{split}&2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\nabla u(0)\cdot\nabla u(y)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\quad\leqslant\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(0)\nabla u(0)-\eta(y)\nabla u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy+\sigma\,\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(0)-u(y)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy\end{split}

knowing that

(2.29)

(-\Delta)^{s}u(0)=0.

To prove this, we call
I_{1}
the left-hand side of (
2.28
).
Integrating by parts, we have

(2.30)

\begin{split}I_{1}\,&=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\nabla u(0)\cdot\nabla\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,K(y)\,dy\\
&=-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(0)\,\nabla u(0)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\cdot\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy.\end{split}

We remark that, to obtain this integration by parts identity,
one must argue in balls
B_{R}
and use that the boundary terms
on
\partial B_{R}
go to zero as
R\to+\infty
(as well as the integrability in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
of the
above functions), thanks to
the decay of the kernel
and of its derivatives assumed in (
1.13
) and (
1.14
).

Moreover, from the bound (
1.14
)
on the first derivative of the kernel,

(2.31)

\Big|\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\Big|\leqslant\big|\nabla\eta(y)\big|\,K(y)+\big|\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big|\,\big|\nabla K(y)\big|\leqslant CK(y),

where, from now on,

C
denotes different constants depending only on
n
and
\|\eta\|_{C^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})}

(in particular, independent of
s
in our case, that is, when
the kernel
K
is that of the fractional Laplacian).
Therefore, we can bound (
2.30
) as

\begin{split}I_{1}\,&=2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(\eta(y)\,\nabla u(y)-\eta(0)\,\nabla u(0)\big)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\cdot\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy\\
&\quad\quad-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(y)\,\nabla u(y)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\cdot\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy\\
&\leqslant CI_{2}-2\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(y)\,\nabla u(y)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\cdot\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy,\end{split}

with

I_{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|\eta(y)\,\nabla u(y)-\eta(0)\,\nabla u(0)\big|\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|K(y)\,dy.

Now, integrating by parts and using again (
2.31
), we have

\begin{split}&2\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(y)\,\nabla u(y)\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\cdot\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy\right|\\
&\qquad\quad=\;\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\eta(y)\,\nabla\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\cdot\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy\right|\\
&\qquad\quad=\;\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,{\rm div}\Big(\eta(y)\nabla\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\Big)\,dy\right|\\
&\qquad\quad\leqslant\;C\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,|\nabla\eta(y)|\,K(y)\,dy\\
&\qquad\quad\qquad\quad+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\,\Delta\Big(\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,K(y)\Big)\,dy\right|\\
&\qquad\quad\leqslant\;CI_{3}+2|T_{1}|+|T_{2}|,\end{split}

with

I_{3}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,K(y)\,dy,

T_{1}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\,\nabla\eta(y)\cdot\nabla K(y)\,dy,

and

(2.32)

T_{2}:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\,\big(\eta(0)-\eta(y)\big)\,\Delta K(y)\,dy.

Clearly,
I_{2}
and
I_{3}
are “good terms” which are controlled by the
right-hand side of (
2.28
). Hence, to bound
I_{1}
it remains to control
|T_{1}|
and
|T_{2}|
.

To estimate
T_{1}
, we observe that

\big|\nabla\eta(y)-\nabla\eta(0)\big|\,|\nabla K(y)|\leqslant C\,|y|\,|\nabla K(y)|\leqslant CK(y),

thanks to the bound (
1.14
)
on the first derivative of the kernel, and therefore, integrating by parts,

\begin{split}|T_{1}|\;&\leqslant\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\,\nabla\eta(0)\cdot\nabla K(y)\,dy\right|\\
&\qquad+\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\,\big(\nabla\eta(y)-\nabla\eta(0)\big)\cdot\nabla K(y)\,dy\right|\\
&\leqslant\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\Big(\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\Big)\cdot\nabla\eta(0)\,K(y)\,dy\right|+CI_{3}\\
&\leqslant\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\nabla\big|u(y)-u(0)\big|^{2}\,\eta(y)\cdot\nabla\eta(0)\,K(y)\,dy\right|+CI_{3}\\
&\leqslant 2\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,\eta(y)\,\nabla u(y)\cdot\nabla\eta(0)\,K(y)\,dy\right|+CI_{3}\\
&\leqslant 2\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(u(y)-u(0)\big)\,\eta(0)\,\nabla u(0)\cdot\nabla\eta(0)\,K(y)\,dy\right|+C(I_{2}+I_{3})\\
&=C(I_{2}+I_{3}),\end{split}

where (
2.29
