Calculating spatiotemporally modulated surfaces: a dynamical differential formalism
Daigo Oue, Kun Ding, J. B. Pendry

Abstract
Electromagnetic waves in a system with a space and time dependent boundary experience both diffraction and Doppler-like frequency conversion. In order to analyse such situations, conventional methods call for either the eigenmodes or the dyadic Green’s function in space and time dependent media. Here, we propose a dynamical differential method which does not require either of them. Our method utilises a dynamical coordinate transformation in order to simplify the calculation of the optical response of the space and time dependent system. We reveal that the diffraction symmetry is broken in the presence of traveling-wave type spatiotemporal modulation.



I
Introduction

Recently,
spatiotemporal modulation of bulky media has been studied both theoretically and experimentally

Sounas and Alù 2017
;
Caloz et al. 2018
;
Shaltout et al. 2019
;
Galiffi et al. 2019
;
Huidobro et al. 2019
.
The modulation breaks reciprocity in the media and brings about unique phenomena such as light amplification and the Fresnel drag.
Keeping these in mind,
in this work,
we analyse the light scattering by spatiotemporally modulated surfaces.

One way to calculate the light scattering is to use the dyadic Green’s function in the system in question.
Once we obtain the dyadic Green’s function in the system,
we can perturbatively calculate the scattered field from incident field and thus the scattering matrix,

Goedecke and O’Brien 1988
;
Yurkin and Hoekstra 2007
.

Another approach is the boundary matching method.
In this approach,
the incident and scattered fields are expanded in series of the eigenmodes in the system,
and the field continuity conditions are imposed at the boundaries,
which lead to simultaneous equations.
The inversion of the equation system yields the scattering matrix.
Calculation of the Mie coefficients
Mie 1908
is one of the popular examples where boundary matching approach is taken.

Here, we consider the boundary matching at a dynamically deformed boundary with the help of a differential formalism.
The differential formalism is originally proposed by Chandezon et al. to calculate dielectric gratings

Chandezon et al. 1980
;
Chandezon et al. 1982
;
Li 1994
.
Since this method allows to directly match the boundary conditions at structured interfaces,
the geometry is properly captured.
One significance of the differential formalism is its convergence.
When the formalism is numerically implemented,
it shows steady convergence even in the presence of sharp edges

Li and Chandezon 1996
;
Li 1996
.
This is why it has been used to calculate not only dielectric gratings but also dispersive, lossy, anisotropic gratings

Barnes et al. 1995
;
Harris et al. 1995
;
Barnes et al. 1996
;
Harris et al. 1996
;
Kitamura and Murakami 2013
;
Murtaza et al. 2017
.
In the current work,
we extend the formalism to dynamical systems so that we can take the motion of the interface as well as the geometry.

There is a related approach based on conformal transformations proposed by Ward and Pendry

Ward and Pendry 1996
,
in which a surface structure is encoded into a conformal mappings.
This approach is also widely used in optics and plasmonics communities

Leonhardt 2006
;
Liu et al. 2010
;
Vakil and Engheta 2011
;
Xu and Chen 2015
;
Pendry et al. 2015
;
Pendry et al. 2019
.
Compared to the conformal approach,
the method utilised in this paper is more straightforward when it comes to analysing systems with time dependent boundaries because we can directly use time-dependent surface profile instead of finding corresponding conformal transformation.

This paper is organised as following.
In Sec.
II
,
we extend a differential formalism so as to enable the electromagnetic field analysis in the presence of a dynamically modulated boundary.
In Sec.
III
,
we numerically implement the dynamical differential formalism.
We will also see the calculation is consistent with effective medium description.
The conclusion is drawn in Sec.
IV
.

II
Dynamical differential formalism

As a simple example of the dynamically modulated surfaces,
we consider a sinusoidally modulated boundary shown in Figure
1
.

Spatiotemporally modulated surface.
The surface is weakly modulated in space and time.
The corrugation function is sinusoidal and given by Eq. (
1
).
We consider in-plane incidence of electromagnetic field and analyse the reflection and the transmission at the surface.
The red and blue arrows
\vec{t}_{1,2}
are the tangential vectors of the surface at each point.
The permittivities of medium and lower media are
\epsilon^{{\raisebox{1.19579pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{>}$}\hskip-4.26115pt\raisebox{-1.19579pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{<}$}}}
,
respectively.
The permeability is assumed to be unity in both media,
\mu^{{\raisebox{1.19579pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{>}$}\hskip-4.26115pt\raisebox{-1.19579pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{<}$}}}=1

The profile of the boundary is given by

\displaystyle a_{\mathbf{x}}=A\sin(\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x})=A\sin\left[g\left(x-\frac{\Omega}{g}t\right)\right]

(1)

Here, we have introduced three-component vectors
\mathbf{q}=\{g,0,-i\Omega/c\}
and
\mathbf{x}=\{x,y,-ict\}
,
where
A
,
g
and
\Omega
are the strength of the modulation, the spatial and temporal frequencies of the modulation, respectively.
The surface is not physically moving in the
x
direction,
but its profile is shifting at the phase velocity
\Omega/g
that can exceed the speed of light.

The permittivity distribution is given by means of the boundary profile,

\displaystyle\epsilon_{\mathbf{x},z}

\displaystyle=\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}\Theta(a_{\mathbf{x}}-z)+\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}\Theta(z-a_{\mathbf{x}})

(2)

\displaystyle=\alpha\Theta(a_{\mathbf{x}}-z)+\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}},

(3)

where

\Theta
represents the Heaviside unit step function,
and

\alpha\equiv(\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}-\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}})

corresponds to the permittivity difference.

We can calculate tangential and normal vectors of the surface,

\vec{t}_{1,2}
and
\vec{n}
,
by taking the partial derivatives of the boundary profile (
1
),

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}\vec{t}_{1}=\cfrac{\vec{u}_{x}+a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\vec{u}_{z}}{\sqrt{1+{a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}^{2}}},\quad\vec{t}_{2}=\vec{u}_{y}.\\
\vec{n}=\vec{t}_{1}\times\vec{t}_{2}=\cfrac{-a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}\vec{u}_{x}+\vec{u}_{z}}{\sqrt{1+{a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}^{2}}},\end{cases}

(4)

where
a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}=(\partial/\partial x)a_{\mathbf{x}}
is the partial derivative of the boundary profile in the
x
direction,
and
\vec{u}_{x,y,z}
are the unit vectors in the
x,y
and
z
directions.
We use those tangential and normal vectors when considering the field continuity conditions or surface integrations over the modulated interface.

II.1
Fourier expansion of fields

Since the upper and lower spaces of the boundary are homogeneous dielectrics,
we can unambiguously expand the incident, reflected and transmitted fields in a series of plane waves,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}},&\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{inc}}=\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}},\\
\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{ref}}=\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}},&\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{ref}}=\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}},\\
\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{tra}}=\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}},&\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{tra}}=\displaystyle{\int_{\mathbf{k}}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}.\end{cases}

(5)

The superscripts on the left hand side
(
\Lambda=\mathrm{inc},\ \mathrm{ref},\ \mathrm{tra}
)
are labels which identify modes in the real space and the time domain.
The two superscripts on the right hand side
(
\sigma=\pm,\ \tau={\raisebox{1.49994pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{>}$}\hskip-5.20004pt\raisebox{-1.49994pt}[0.0pt][0.0pt]{$\scriptscriptstyle{<}$}}
)
are corresponding labels in the reciprocal space.
While
\sigma
specifies in which direction the mode propagates,

\tau
specifies in which medium the mode lives.
The fields in the real space,

\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\Lambda}

and

\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\Lambda}
,
are given by means of complex-valued Fourier components,

\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}

and

\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}
.
Note that we have introduced a reciprocal vector

\mathbf{k}=\{k_{x},k_{y},-ik_{0}=-i\omega/c\}
.
Each Fourier component satisfies

\displaystyle\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle=\vec{E}{}_{-\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau\raisebox{2.3917pt}{$\scriptstyle{*}$}},\quad\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}=\vec{H}{}_{-\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau\raisebox{2.3917pt}{$\scriptstyle{*}$}}.

(6)

These conditions are required because

\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{{\sigma\tau}}

and

\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{{\sigma\tau}}
,
are real-valued.
We employ a shorthand notation for an integral operation,

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbf{k}}=\iint_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}k_{x}\mathrm{d}k_{y}}{(2\pi)^{2}}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\mathrm{d}\omega}{2\pi}.

(7)

We substitute Eq. (
5
) into Maxwell’s equations to get the Helmholtz equation,

\displaystyle\left[-\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial z^{2}}-\left(\epsilon^{\tau}{k_{0}}^{2}-{k_{\parallel}}^{2}\right)\right]\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}=0,

(8)

where we have defined

k_{\parallel}=\sqrt{{k_{x}}^{2}+{k_{y}}^{2}}
.
This differential equation can be easily solved,

\displaystyle\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle=e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}z}\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau}.

(9)

Here,

\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau}
is a complex-valued vector at
z=0
,
which determines the amplitude and the polarisation,
and we define the wavenumber in the
z
direction,

\displaystyle K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\operatorname{Re}\sqrt{\epsilon^{\tau}{k_{0}}^{2}-{k_{\parallel}}^{2}}+i\operatorname{Im}\sqrt{\epsilon^{\tau}{k_{0}}^{2}-{k_{\parallel}}^{2}}.

(10)

We have added the prefactor
\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)
at the real part in order to take into account that the propagation direction of a wave reverses when the sign of the frequency is flipped
Pendry 2008
.
Remind that the wavenumber satisfies

K_{-\mathbf{k}}^{\tau\raisebox{2.3917pt}{$\scriptstyle{*}$}}=-K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}
.

In our dielectric medium,
the field is divergenceless
(i.e.
\nabla\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\Lambda}=0
)
so that we have a transversality condition,

\displaystyle i(k_{x}\vec{u}_{x}+k_{y}\vec{u}_{y}+\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}\vec{u}_{z})\cdot\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau}=0.

(11)

We can choose two orthonormal basis vectors satisfying this condition,

\displaystyle\vec{e}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}{}\cfrac{\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\vec{k}\times\vec{u}_{z}}{|\vec{k}\times\vec{u}_{z}|}&(\lambda=s),\\
\cfrac{\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\vec{k}\times\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\vec{k}\times\vec{u}_{z}}{|\vec{k}\times\vec{k}\times\vec{u}_{z}|}&(\lambda=p),\end{cases}

(12)

where
\vec{k}=k_{x}\vec{u}_{x}+k_{y}\vec{u}_{y}+\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}\vec{u}_{z}
is the wavevector of a mode labeled by

\sigma
,

\tau

and

\mathbf{k}=\{k_{x},k_{y},-ik_{0}\}
.

The complex-valued vector is expressed by the linear combination of the basis vectors,

\displaystyle\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\lambda=s,p}E_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\vec{e}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau}.

(13)

Since the basis vectors satisfies

\vec{e}_{\lambda,-\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau\raisebox{2.3917pt}{$\scriptstyle{*}$}}=\vec{e}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau}
,
the constraint on the scalar coefficient is

\displaystyle E_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}=E_{\lambda,-\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau\raisebox{2.3917pt}{$\scriptstyle{*}$}}.

(14)

We can perform a similar calculation for the magnetic field.
Let us summarise the Fourier components of electric and magnetic fields,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}=e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}z}\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau},&\vec{E}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau}=\displaystyle{\sum_{\lambda}}E_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\vec{e}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau},\\
\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},z}^{\sigma\tau}=e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}z}\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau},&\vec{H}{}_{\mathbf{k},0}^{\sigma\tau}=\displaystyle{\sum_{\lambda}}H_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\vec{h}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau},\end{cases}

(15)

where we have introduced another basis for the magnetic field for convenience,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\vec{h}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\\
\vec{h}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\
-1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\vec{e}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau}\\
\vec{e}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau}\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

II.2
Boundary matching equations

Let us derive boundary matching equations,
which are used to calculate the reflection and transmission matrices.

Figure 2:

Path of integration to derive a boundary matching equation.
The contour
\Gamma
encloses and is curved along the boundary.

We consider the surface integral over the area enclosed by a closed curve
\Gamma
in the
xz
plane as shown in Figure
2
so that the integral catches the geometry of the boundary and reflects it on the boundary matching equation.
In the limit that the enclosed area vanishes (
\delta h\rightarrow 0
),
from the Faraday’s law,
we have

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta h\rightarrow 0}\oiint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\vec{S}\cdot\nabla\times\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}

\displaystyle=-\lim_{\delta h\rightarrow 0}\oiint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\vec{S}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mu_{0}\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},z},

(17)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta h\rightarrow 0}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}

\displaystyle=0,

(18)

where we have used the Stokes’ theorem.
The vectorial line element
\mathrm{d}\vec{r}
is given by means of the tangential vector
\vec{t}_{1}
and a scalar element
\sqrt{1+{a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}^{2}}\mathrm{d}x
,
and we can obtain an integral equation,

\displaystyle\int\mathrm{d}x\sqrt{1+{a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}^{2}}\vec{t}_{1}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}+0}-\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}-0})=0,

(19)

where

\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}+0}=\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{ref}}

and

\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}-0}=\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\mathrm{tra}}

are the electric fields evaluated above and below the boundary,
respectively.
Since Eq. (
19
) is independent of the choice of the integration interval,
we can obtain a boundary matching equation for the electric field,

\displaystyle\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=0.

(20)

This equation means that the tangential component of the field should be matched at each point of the boundary at each time.
Note that we set
\eta=\sqrt{1+{a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}}^{2}}
for simplicity.

Let us consider the matching equation for the magnetic field.
Integrating the Ampère-Maxwell equation,

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta h\rightarrow 0}\oiint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\vec{S}\cdot\nabla\times\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},z}

\displaystyle=\lim_{\delta h\rightarrow 0}\oiint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\vec{S}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\epsilon_{\mathbf{x},z}\epsilon_{0}\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z},

(21)

\displaystyle\lim_{\delta h\rightarrow 0}\oint_{\Gamma}\mathrm{d}\vec{r}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},z}

\displaystyle=\frac{\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}}{c}\alpha\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\frac{\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}}}{Z_{0}},

(22)

where we have used the Stokes’ theorem and the time derivative of the permittivity distribution,

\partial\epsilon_{\mathbf{x},z}/\partial t=\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}\alpha\delta(a_{\mathbf{x}}-z)
.
Note that there remains a surface electric current on the right hand side of Eq. (
22
).
This is because we induced electric polarisation on the boundary due to the permittivity difference
\alpha
.
We can write the matching equation for the magnetic field,

\displaystyle\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=\frac{\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}}{c}\alpha\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\frac{\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}}}{Z_{0}}.

(23)

Following the same procedure,
we can write the boundary matching equations in the
y
direction,

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{2}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=0,\vskip 5.0pt

(24)

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{2}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=-\frac{\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}}{c}\alpha\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot\frac{\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}}}{Z_{0}}.

(25)

Since our modulation is invariant with respect to the translation in the
y
direction,
the problem can be regarded as a two-dimensional one.
Here, we investigate two fundamental cases where the incident field is either
s
- or
p
-polarised.

There is no polarisation rotation as we stick to the in-plane calculation.
This is because our ‘optical axis’ induced by the surface structure of grating type is in the
y
direction,
which is parallel or perpendicular to the electric and magnetic fields.
In the
s
polarisation case,
the electric field oscillates perpendicularly to the
xz
plane while the magnetic field oscillates in the
xz
plane.
Therefore,
Eqs. (
20
,
25
) are automatically satisfied in the
s
polarisation case,
and we can focus on

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}\vec{t}_{2}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=0,\vskip 5.0pt\\
\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot Z_{0}(\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=\displaystyle{\frac{\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}}{c}}\alpha\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}}.\end{cases}

(26)

On the other hand,
the magnetic field oscillates in the
y
direction in the
p
polarisation case,
and the electric field lies in the
xz
plane.
Thus, we can focus on

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot(\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=0,\vskip 5.0pt\\
\vec{t}_{2}\cdot Z_{0}(\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{inc}}+\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{ref}}-\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}})=-\displaystyle{\frac{\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}}{c}}\alpha\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\mathrm{tra}}.\end{cases}

(27)

These simultaneous equations depend on space and time.
Thanks to the time derivative of the surface profile
\dot{a}_{\mathbf{x}}
and the tangential vector
\eta\vec{t}_{1}
in Eqs. (
26
,
27
),
we can properly take both temporal and spatial modulations into consideration.

We apply the Fourier transform

\mathscr{F}[g_{\mathbf{x}}]_{\mathbf{k}}\equiv\int g_{\mathbf{x}}e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}d\mathbf{x}

to Eqs. (
26
,
27
) to obtain equation systems determining the reflection and transmission matrices in the frequency domain.

Let take the
s
polarisation as an example.
We use the Fourier expansion of the electric field (
5
,
15
) to get

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\frac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}e^{i\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\sin\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}}E_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau},

(28)

where we have defined the propagating phase factor

\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}=\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}A
.
We use the Jacobi-Anger identity to expand the exponential of the trigonometric function

Cuyt et al. 2008
,

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\SumInt_{m,\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\frac{k_{x,m}}{k_{\parallel,m}}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega_{m})J_{-m}(\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau})E_{s,\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau},

where
J_{m}
is the
m^{\mathrm{th}}
order Bessel function of the first kind.
Note that the integration variables are relevantly shifted,

\mathbf{k}\mapsto\mathbf{k}_{m}=\mathbf{k}+m\mathbf{q}
,
after the expansion.
Note also that we have introduced a shorthand notation,

\displaystyle\SumInt_{m,\mathbf{k}}=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{+\infty}\int_{\mathbf{k}}.

Applying the Fourier transform,
we can obtain the
l
th order quantity,

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\left[\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}\right]_{\mathbf{k}_{l}}=\left[\mathsf{M}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\right]_{l}.

(45)

Here, we have collected the modal amplitude in each diffraction order into one column,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\vdots\\
E_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}_{-1}}^{\sigma\tau}\\
E_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}_{0}}^{\sigma\tau}\\
E_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}_{+1}}^{\sigma\tau}\\
\vdots\end{pmatrix},

(46)

and introduced a matrix-vector representation with the coefficient matrix that has the geometric information of the boundary,

\displaystyle[\mathsf{M}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}]_{lm}

\displaystyle=\frac{k_{x,m}}{k_{\parallel,m}}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega_{m})J_{l-m}(\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau}).

(47)

Here, the Bessel function is responsible for the correlation between the
l
th order and the
m
th order diffraction.
The diffraction is stronger as the surface corrugation depth increases with respect to the wavenumber in the
z
direction.
This is why we provide
\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau}=\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau}A
in the argument.

Finally,
applying the Fourier transform to Eq. (
24
),
we can obtain

\displaystyle\mathsf{M}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}+\mathsf{M}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}-\mathsf{M}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}

\displaystyle=0.

(48)

Similarly,
we can perform the Fourier transform for the boundary condition of the magnetic field.
The tangential component of the magnetic field is evaluated as below:

\displaystyle\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot Z_{0}\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}=\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}a_{\mathbf{x}}}\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot\vec{h}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}Z_{0}H_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau},

(49)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\frac{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}k_{x}-a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}{k_{\parallel}}^{2}}{k_{\parallel}|k_{0}|}e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}a_{\mathbf{x}}}E_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau},

(50)

\displaystyle=\SumInt_{m,\mathbf{k}}e^{i(\mathbf{k}-m\mathbf{q})\cdot\mathbf{x}}\left(\frac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}\frac{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}}{|k_{0}|}-\frac{-mg}{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}}\frac{k_{\parallel}}{|k_{0}|}\right)J_{-m}(\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau})E_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau},

where we expanded the exponential of the trigonometric function by using the Bessel functions.
Applying the Fourier transform to Eq. (

\displaystyle\mathsf{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}+\mathsf{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}-(\mathsf{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}+\mathsf{L}_{\mathbf{k}})\mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}=0.

\displaystyle=\frac{\alpha}{c}\frac{(l-m)\Omega}{-K_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}}\times\operatorname{sgn}(\omega_{m})\frac{k_{x,m}}{k_{\parallel,m}}J_{l-m}(\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}),

(60)

\displaystyle[\mathsf{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}]_{lm}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{k_{x,m}}{k_{\parallel,m}}\frac{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\tau}}{|k_{0,m}|}-\frac{(l-m)g}{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\tau}}\frac{k_{\parallel,m}}{|k_{0,m}|}\right)J_{l-m}(\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau}).

(61)

IV
Conclusions

In this study,
we proposed a dynamical differential formalism which enables analytical calculation of the scattering by a surface modulated both in space and time.
Using dynamical coordinate transformation generated by the boundary profile,
we can directly impose the boundary conditions at the dynamically deformed interface for both electric and magnetic fields to properly capture the geometry and motion.
In the numerical calculation,
we confirmed our formalism is consistent with the effective medium description.
The diffraction spectra of the surface become asymmetric in the presence temporal modulation.

In the
p

the tangential component of the electric field is

\displaystyle\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}a_{\mathbf{x}}}Z_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}H_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot\vec{e}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\hskip 1.63885pt\sigma\tau}

(70)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\frac{Z_{0}}{\epsilon^{\tau}}e^{i\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}a_{\mathbf{x}}}\frac{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}k_{x}-a_{\mathbf{x}}^{\prime}{k_{\parallel}}^{2}}{k_{\parallel}|k_{0}|}H_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau},

(71)

where we have used the fact that the electric and magnetic amplitude can be associated with each other,

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}E_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}=Z_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}H_{s,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau},\vskip 5.0pt\\

via the characteristic impedance,

\displaystyle Z_{\lambda,\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}=\begin{cases}{}\cfrac{Z_{0}}{\sqrt{\epsilon^{\tau}}}\sqrt{\cfrac{\omega^{2}\epsilon^{\tau}/c^{2}}{|K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\tau}|^{2}+{k_{\parallel}}^{2}}}&(\lambda=s),\\

(73)

where
Z_{0}=\sqrt{\mu_{0}/\epsilon_{0}}
is the impedance of free space.
We use the Jacobi-Anger identity and perform the Fourier transform,

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\left[\eta\vec{t}_{1}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{E}}_{\mathbf{x},z}^{\Lambda}\right]_{\mathbf{k}_{l}}

\displaystyle=Z_{0}\left[\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\mathbb{H}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\right]_{l},

(74)

where we have defined

\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}=\mathsf{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}/\epsilon^{\tau}

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\mathbb{H}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}+\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\mathbb{H}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{+}}{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}-\widetilde{\mathsf{N}}_{\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\mathbb{H}_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{-}}{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}

\displaystyle=0.

(75)

Note that we have divide the equation by the impedance of free space
Z_{0}
to make the coefficient matrix dimensionless.

\displaystyle\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{H}}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}

\displaystyle=-\int_{\mathbf{k}}e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{x}}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega)\frac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}e^{i\phi_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}\sin\mathbf{q}\cdot\mathbf{x}}H_{p,\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}.

\displaystyle\mathscr{F}\left[\vec{t}_{2}\cdot\vec{\mathcal{H}}{}_{\mathbf{x},a_{\mathbf{x}}}^{\Lambda}\right]_{\mathbf{k}_{l}}

Recover the Fresnel equations

In the flat boundary limit (
A\rightarrow 0
),
all the Bessel functions except the 0th order vanish,

\displaystyle\lim_{A\rightarrow 0}J_{l-m}(\phi_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\sigma\tau})

\displaystyle=\delta_{l,m},

(86)

and the matrices become diagonal

\displaystyle\begin{cases}{}[\mathsf{M}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}]_{lm}&\longrightarrow\cfrac{k_{x,m}}{k_{\parallel,m}}\operatorname{sgn}(\omega_{m})\delta_{l,m}\\
[\mathsf{N}_{\mathbf{k}}^{\sigma\tau}]_{lm}&\longrightarrow\cfrac{k_{x,m}}{k_{\parallel,m}}\cfrac{\sigma K_{\mathbf{k}_{m}}^{\tau}}{|k_{0,m}|}\delta_{l,m}\end{cases}\quad(A\longrightarrow 0).

(87)

Therefore,
the matrix equations (
66
,
67
) are reduced and give the conventional Fresnel coefficients,

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}R_{s,\mathbf{k}}\\
T_{s,\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\cfrac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}&-\cfrac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}\\
\cfrac{k_{x}K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}{k_{\parallel}k_{0}}&\cfrac{k_{x}K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}{k_{\parallel}k_{0}}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\cfrac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}\\
\cfrac{k_{x}K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}{k_{\parallel}k_{0}}\end{pmatrix}

(88)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cfrac{K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}-K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}{K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}+K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\\
\cfrac{2K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}{K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}+K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\end{pmatrix},

(89)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}R_{p,\mathbf{k}}\\
T_{p,\mathbf{k}}\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\begin{pmatrix}\cfrac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}&-\cfrac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}\\
\cfrac{k_{x}K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}{k_{\parallel}k_{0}\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}&\cfrac{k_{x}K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}{k_{\parallel}k_{0}\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\end{pmatrix}^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}-\cfrac{k_{x}}{k_{\parallel}}\\
\cfrac{k_{x}K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}{k_{\parallel}k_{0}\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}\end{pmatrix}

(90)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}\cfrac{K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}-K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}{K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}+K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\\
\cfrac{2K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}}{K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{>}}+K_{\mathbf{k}}^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}/\epsilon^{\scriptscriptstyle{<}}}\end{pmatrix}.

(91)
