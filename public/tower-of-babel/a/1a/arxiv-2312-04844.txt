Tied–boxed algebras
Diego Arcis Jorge Espinoza

Abstract
We introduce two new algebras that we call tied–boxed Hecke algebra and tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra . The first one is a subalgebra of the algebra of braids and ties introduced by Aicardi and Juyumaya, and the second one is a tied–version of the well known Temperley–Lieb algebra. We study their representation theory and give cellular bases for them. Furthermore, we explore a strong connection between the tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra and the so–called partition Temperley–Lieb algebra given by Juyumaya. Also, we show that both structures inherit diagrammatic interpretations from a new class of monoids that we call boxed ramified monoids . Additionally, we give presentations for the singular part of the ramified symmetric monoid and for the boxed ramified monoid associated to the Brauer monoid.

Introduction

The
algebra of braids and ties
or simply the
bt–algebra

\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
was introduced by Aicardi and Juyumaya in
[
34
,
3
]
by means an abstracting procedure of the so–called
Yokonuma–Hecke algebra

[
56
,
37
]
. This algebra can be regarded as a generalization of the well known
Iwahori–Hecke algebra

\mathcal{H}_{n}(q)

[
30
]
, and has a rich interpretation as a diagram algebra involving both the classic
braid generators
and a new family of generators, called
ties
, which arise from the combinatorics of set partitions
[
3
,
52
]
. The bt–algebra was introduced with the purpose of constructing new representations of the braid group, however, in recent years, this algebra and its derivatives have been studied from various perspectives.

The representation theory of the algebra of braids and ties was initiated by Ryom-Hansen in
[
52
]
. Specifically, he defined a tensor representation of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
which used to classify the simple modules of this algebra. As a consequence, he obtained a linear basis for the bt–algebra and proved that its dimension is
n!\mathfrak{b}_{n}
, where
\mathfrak{b}_{n}
denotes the
n
th
Bell number

[
29
, A000110]
. These results where used by Aicardi and Juyumaya in
[
4
]
to show that
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
supports a
Markov trace
and so constructed polynomial invariants for classical and singular knots in the sense of Jones
[
31
]
. Aicardi and Juyumaya observed that their invariants can be recovered by a new class of knotted objects that called
tied links
, which in turn can be obtained by closing a new class of braided object that called
tied braids

[
5
]
. Tied braids (of
n
strands) form a monoid
T\mathcal{B}_{n}
called the
tied braid monoid
, which play the role of algebraic counterpart for tied links just as the braid group is the algebraic counterpart of classical links by means the
Alexander theorem

[
10
]
. Furthermore, this monoid of tied braids was shown to be a semidirect product
T\mathcal{B}_{n}=\mathcal{P}_{n}\rtimes\mathcal{B}_{n}
, where
\mathcal{P}_{n}
denotes the monoid of set partitions of
[n]=\{1,\ldots,n\}
together with the classic product given by refinement
[
8
]
. More results related to knot theory via the bt–algebra and its derivatives can be found in the literature, such as other invariants for tied and singular links
[
6
,
8
]
, generalizations of the bt–algebra
[
7
,
21
,
8
,
11
]
, tied links in other topological settings
[
22
,
18
]
, among others.

The complex generic representation theory of the algebra of braids and ties was studied by Banjo in
[
13
]
. For this purpose, she showed that the algebra
\mathcal{E}_{n}(1)
is isomorphic to the so–called
small ramified partition algebra

[
45
]
, which is generated by some pairs of set partitions of
[n]
called
ramified partitions

[
46
]
. On the other hand, cellularity of the algebra of braids and ties was studied by the second author and Ryom-Hansen in
[
19
]
. Specifically, they constructed an explicit cellular basis for
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
, whose elements are indexed by a special type of pairs of multitableaux. This construction allowed them to define an explicit isomorphism between the bt–algebra and a direct sum of matrix algebras over certain wreath product algebras indexed by partitions of numbers, whose cellularity was studied by Geetha and Goodman in
[
24
]
. This last result was later generalized by Marin in
[
42
]
by defining bt–algebra versions for arbitrary Coxeter groups. See Subsection
3.1
, Subsection
3.1.1
and Subsection
3.1.2
.

A first generalization of the tied braid monoid was given by the first author and Juyumaya in
[
11
]
by replacing the respective factors of the semidirect product decomposition of
T\mathcal{B}_{n}
with a monoid of set partitions
[
50
]
and a monoid acting in some Coxeter group. Another generalization was given by the same authors together with Aicardi in
[
2
,
1
]
. Specifically, inspired by the work of Banjo
[
13
]
, they introduced a new class of monoids, formed by ramified partitions, that called
ramified monoids
, which are defined for every submonoid of the partition monoid. See Subsection
2.3
. Particularly, the algebra of braids and ties
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
can be regarded as a
q
–deformation of the monoid algebra of the ramified monoid
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
associated to the symmetric group
\mathcal{S}_{n}
. See Subsection
3.2
. Also, due to their combinatorial background, ramified monoids have a rich interpretation in terms of diagrams, and thus any algebra obtained by deformation of their monoid algebras inherit this diagrammatic interpretation. The ramified monoid
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{B}r_{n})
associated with the
Brauer monoid

\mathcal{B}r_{n}
was studied in
[
2
]
and the ramified monoid associated with the
symmetric inverse monoid
was studied in
[
1
]
. At the time of writing this paper, the structure of the ramified monoid
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
associated to the Jones monoid
\mathcal{J}_{n}
is still unknown.

A tied version of the well known
BMW algebra
is the
t–BMW algebra
introduced by Aicardi and Juyumaya in
[
6
]
, which can be regarded as deformation of the monoid algebra of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{B}r_{n})
. Just as there is no known structure for the monoid
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
, there also no a tied version of the
Temperley–Lieb algebra
that is obtained as a deformation of the monoid algebra of this monoid. However, there are two tied versions of this algebra that are not directly related to the monoid
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
. The first one is the
partition Temperley–Lieb algebra

\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
introduced by Juyumaya in
[
36
]
in a purely algebraic manner. The second one is the
tied Temperley–Lieb algebra
introduced by Aicardi, Juyumaya and Papi in
[
9
]
, which is related to a submonoid of
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
called the
planar ramified monoid
of
\mathcal{J}_{n}

[
1
]
. A generalization of the partition Temperley–Lieb algebra was studied by Ryom-Hansen in
[
53
]
. Specifically, inspired by the construction of
generalized Temperley–Lieb algebras
given in
[
28
]
, he introduced certain
permutation modules
, which can be regarded as
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
–submodules of a tensor space module introduced in
[
52
]
. To be precise, Ryom–Hansen proved that the annihilator ideal
\mathcal{I}
of the action of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
on this tensor space module is a free
\Z[q,q^{-1}]
–module with a basis given by a dual cellular basis of the one in
[
19
]
. Thus, he obtained that the quotient
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)/\mathcal{I}
is a simultaneous generalization of the genelized Temperley–Lieb algebra and the partition Temperley–Lieb algebra. In particular, for
N=2
, it is obtained that
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)/\mathcal{I}\simeq\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
, and thus
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
is cellular as well.

In the present paper, we introduce two tied–like algebras that inherit the diagrammatic interpretation from a new class of submonoids of the ramified ones, called
boxed ramified monoids
, which generalize a monoid in
[
2
, Section 7]
. We study the representation theory of these algebras and give cellular bases for them. The first one is a subalgebra of the algebra of braids and ties, which we call the
tied–boxed Hecke algebra
. The second one is a new tied version of the Temperley–Lieb algebra, which we call the
tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra
. Additionally, we determine presentations for the singular part of the ramified monoid associated to the symmetric group and for the boxed ramified monoid associated to the Brauer monoid.

The paper is organized as follows.

In Section
2
, we give the main combinatorial and algebraic ingredients that we will use along the paper. Specifically, we recall compositions and multicompositions (Subsection
2.1
), set partitions and related monoids such as partition monoids and ramified monoids (Subsection
2.2
and Subsection
2.3
), and also some cellular algebras such as the Iwahori–Hecke algebra and the Temperley–Lieb algebra (Subsection
2.4
). Section
3
is devoted to recall the definition of algebra of braids and ties and some important results mainly coming from representation theory (Subsection
3.1
). We also recall the ramified symmetric monoid, from which the bt–algebra inherits its diagram structure, and study its center (Subsection
3.2
). In Section
4
, we introduce a new family of monoids that we call
boxed ramified monoids
, which will give diagrammatic structures to the algebras that we will study in the following sections. These monoids are submonoids of the ramified ones, which are defined by imposing a linearity condition on the right components of ramified partitions.

In Section
5
, we introduce the
tied-boxed Hecke algebra

bH_{n}(q)
by generators and relations and study its representation theory (Subsection
5.1
). Specifically, we establish its connection with the algebra of braids and ties (Proposition
5.1
), enabling us to compute its dimension (Theorem
5.4
) and thereby obtain a faithful representation of
bH_{n}(q)
in a tensor space module. Subsequently, we construct a complete set of orthogonal idempotents for
bH_{n}(q)
(Proposition
5.8
), leading to a decomposition of it into a direct sum of two–sided ideals. This allows us to provide an explicit cellular basis for
bH_{n}(q)
indexed by linear set partitions and standard Young tableaux of the initial kind (Theorem
5.10
). Furthermore, we present a diagrammatic representation of
bH_{n}(q)
inherited from the boxed ramified monoid of the symmetric group
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
(Subsection
5.2
). Specifically, we give a presentation for
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
(Theorem
5.13
), concluding that the tied-boxed Hecke algebra is a
q
–deformation of the monoid algebra of
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
. We also prove that the center of
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
coincides with the monoid of compositions of integers (Proposition
5.16
). Additionally, we delve into the study of the singular part of
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
(Subsection
5.3
), providing a presentation for it (Theorem
5.20
).

Finally, in Section
6
, we introduce the
tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra

bTL_{n}(q)
as a quotient of the tied–boxed Hecke algebra by a two–sided ideal generated by
Steinberg elements
. We give a presentation for
bTL_{n}(q)
(Proposition
6.1
) and show that it can be decomposed as a direct sum of two–sided ideals, obtaining an explicit cellular basis for
bTL_{n}(q)
, which is indexed by multipartitions whose components have Young diagrams with at most two columns and standard multitableaux of the initial kind (Theorem
6.2
). Then, we explore a strong connection between
bTL_{n}(q)
and the partition Temperley–Lieb algebra
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
(Subsection
6.1
). Specifically, we prove that
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
is isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix algebras over certain wreath product algebras, obtaining a formula for the dimension of
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
, which is confirmed in
[
53
]
by considering the case
N=2
of the
generalized partition Temperley–Lieb
. We next observe that the natural embedding of
bH_{n}(q)
in
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
induces an embedding of
bTL_{n}(q)
in
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
(Corollary
6.6
). Furthermore, we present a diagrammatic realization of
bTL_{n}(q)
inherited from the boxed ramified monoid of the Jones monoid (Subsection
6.2
). Specifically, we recall the presentation for
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
given in
[
2
, Theorem 56]
and conclude that the tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra is a
q
–deformation of the monoid algebra of
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
. (Theorem
6.7
). Additionally, we study the boxed ramified monoid of the Brauer monoid (Subsection
6.3
), providing a presentation for it (Theorem
6.12
).

Preliminaries

For every pair of integers
m,n
with
m\leq n
we will denote by
[m,n]
the discrete interval
\{m,\ldots,n\}
. Also, if
n
is positive we set
[n]=[1,n]
and
[n]_{0}=[0,n]
.

In what follows
n
denotes a positive integer, and
\mathbb{S}
denotes the ring
\mathbb{C}[q,q^{-1}]
, where
q
is an indeterminate. Here, an
\mathbb{S}
–algebra
means an associative
\mathbb{S}
–algebra with unit.

2.1
Compositions and multicompositions

A
composition
of
size

n
is a finite sequence
\mu
formed by
\ell(\mu)

[n]_{0}
, called
parts
, such that their sum is
n

\mathcal{C}_{n}
the collection of compositions of

[
29
, A000079]
. We say that a composition of
n
is a
partition
if each pair of consecutive parts of it is nonincreasing. The collection of set partitions of
n
will be denoted by
\operatorname{Par}_{n}

The
Young diagram
or simply the

\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})

[\mu]=\{(i,j)\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\mid 1\leq j\leq\mu_{i}\}.

Elements of

2.2
Set partitions

In what follows
A
denotes a nonempty set.

A set partition of
A
is a collection of pairwise disjoint nonempty subsets of it, called
blocks
, such that their union is
A
. The collection of set partitions of
A
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}(A)
, and
\mathcal{P}([n])
is denoted by
\mathcal{P}_{n}
instead. The cardinality of
\mathcal{P}_{n}
is the
n
th
Bell number

\mathfrak{b}_{n}

[
29
, A000110]
.

Set partitions
\mathbf{I}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}
of
[n]
are denoted by
\mathbf{I}=(I_{a_{1}},\ldots,I_{a_{k}})
, where
\min(I_{a_{1}})<\cdots<\min(I_{a_{k}})
. These set partitions are usually represented by
arc diagrams
, which connect, in a transitive manner, the elements of
[n]
that belong to the same block. See Figure
7
. Also, we set
|\mathbf{I}|=k
and
\|\mathbf{I}\|=(|I_{1}|,\ldots,|I_{k}|)
. For
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
and
i,j\in[n]
, we will denote by
i\sim_{\mathbf{I}}j
the fact that
i,j
belong to the same block of
\mathbf{I}
.

Figure 7
:
Set partition
(\{1,3,8\},\{2,5,7\},\{4,6\})
of
[8]
.

Given
\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{P}(A)
, we say that
\mathbf{I}
is
finer
than
\mathbf{J}
, or that
\mathbf{J}
is
coarser
than
\mathbf{I}
, denoted by
\mathbf{I}\preceq\mathbf{J}
, if each block of
\mathbf{J}
is a union of blocks of
\mathbf{I}
. This relation gives to
\mathcal{P}(A)
a structure of lattice with supremum operation
\vee
. The pair
(\mathcal{P}_{n},\vee)
is called
the monoid of set partitions
of
[n]
. It was shown in
[
20
, Theorem 2]
that
\mathcal{P}_{n}
can be presented by
\frac{n(n-1)}{2}

[
29
, A000217]
generators
e_{i,j}
with
i<j
, subject to the following relations:

(2.2)

e_{i,j}^{2}=e_{i,j},\quad e_{i,j}e_{r,s}=e_{r,s}e_{i,j},\quad e_{i,j}e_{i,k}=e_{i,j}e_{j,k}=e_{i,k}e_{j,k},

where each
e_{i,j}
is the set partition of
[n]
whose unique block that is not a singleton is
\{i,j\}
. See Figure
8
.

Figure 8
:
Generators
e_{i,j}
of
\mathcal{P}_{4}
.

Given
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}(A)
and a subset
X
of
A
, we set
\mathbf{I}\cap X=\{B\cap X\mid B\in\mathbf{I}\}\backslash\{\emptyset\}
in
\mathcal{P}(X)
.

Observe that if
X
is a subset of
A
and
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
, then
\mathbf{I}
can be naturally regarded as a set partition of
A
by inserting to it the singletons
\{a\}
with
a\in A\backslash X
. Now, if
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}(A)
and
\mathbf{J}\in\mathcal{P}(B)
for some set
B
, we will simply denote by
\mathbf{I}\vee\mathbf{J}
the product of
\mathbf{I}
with
\mathbf{J}
regarded as elements of
\mathcal{P}(A\cup B)
.

A
ramified partition
of
A
is a pair
(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})
of set partitions of
A
such that
\mathbf{I}\preceq\mathbf{J}
. Observe that
(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})
can be regarded as a set partition of
\mathbf{I}
, in which two blocks of
\mathbf{I}
belong to the same block of
(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})
whenever both are contained in the same block of
\mathbf{J}
. The collection of ramified partitions of
A
is denoted by
\mathcal{RP}(A)
, and
\mathcal{RP}([n])
is denoted by
\mathcal{RP}_{n}
. We call
\mathcal{RP}(A)
, together with the product
(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})\vee(\mathbf{H},\mathbf{K})=(\mathbf{I}\vee\mathbf{H},\mathbf{J}\vee\mathbf{K})
, the
monoid of ramified partitions
of
A
.

[

, A000108]

2.4
Cellular algebras

In this subsection we recall briefly the concept of a
cellular algebra
, introduced by Graham and Lerher in
[
26
]
. Later, we recall the
Murphy’s standard basis
of the Iwahori–Hecke algebra, which results to be a cellular basis. We will use this basis as an inspiration to construct cellular bases for the two algebras that we study in this paper: the
tied–boxed Hecke algebra
and the
tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra
.

Let
R
be an integral domain, and let
A
to be an
R
–algebra which is free as an
R
–module. Consider
(\Lambda,\geq)
to be a poset in which for each
\lambda\in\Lambda
there is a finite indexing set
T(\lambda)
and elements
c_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}}^{\lambda}\in A
such that
B:=\{c_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}}^{\lambda}\mid\lambda\in\Lambda\text{ and }\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\in T(\lambda)\}
is an
R
–basis of
A
. The pair
(B,\Lambda)
is said to be a
cellular basis
of
A
if:

(i)

The
R
–linear map
*:A\to A
given by
(\mathop{c_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}}^{\lambda}})^{*}=c_{\mathfrak{t}\mathfrak{s}}^{\lambda}
is an algebra
antiautomorphism of
A
.

(ii)

For any
\lambda\in\Lambda,\;\mathfrak{t}\in T(\lambda)
and
a\in A
there is
r_{\mathfrak{v}}\in R
such that for all
\mathfrak{s}\in T(\lambda)

c_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}}^{\lambda}a\equiv\sum_{\mathfrak{v}\in T(\lambda)}r_{\mathfrak{v}}c_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{v}}^{\lambda}\mod A^{\lambda},

where the coefficient
r_{\mathfrak{v}}\in R
do not depend on
\mathfrak{t}
, and
A^{\lambda}
is the
R
–submodule of
A
with basis:

\{c_{\mathfrak{u}\mathfrak{v}}^{\mu}\mid\mu\in\Lambda\text{ such that }\mu>\lambda\text{ and }\mathfrak{u},\mathfrak{v}\in T(\mu)\}.

In this case,
A
is said to be a
cellular algebra
, and the tuple
(\Lambda,T,B,\ast)
is called the
cell datum
of it.

2.4.1
The Iwahori–Hecke algebra

Recall that the
braid group

\mathcal{B}_{n}
on
n
strands
[
12
]
is the one presented by generators
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}
subject to the well known
braid relations
, that is:

(2.6)

\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;\qquad\sigma_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}\sigma_{i},\quad|i-j|>1.

The
braid monoid

\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+}

[
23
]
is the submonoid of the braid group defined with the same presentation of
\mathcal{B}_{n}
.

The
Iwahori–Hecke algebra

H_{n}(q)
is the
\mathbb{S}
–algebra presented by generators
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n-1}
subject to the braid relations together with a
quadratic relation
:

(2.7)

\displaystyle h_{i}h_{j}h_{i}=h_{j}h_{i}h_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;\qquad h_{i}h_{j}=h_{j}h_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;\qquad h_{i}^{2}=1+(q-q^{-1})h_{i}.

Observe that
H_{n}(q)
can be concerned as the quotient of the monoid algebra of
\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+}
over
\mathbb{S}
, by the two–sided ideal defined by the quadratic relation in (
2.7
). Also, if
q=1
it coincides with the group algebra of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
.

Due to the Matsumoto’s theorem
[
48
]
, for each
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and each reduced expression
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{k}}
of it, the element
h_{w}:=h_{i_{1}}\cdots h_{i_{k}}
is well defined in
H_{n}(q)
. Moreover, it is well known that
H_{n}(q)
is a free
\mathbb{S}
–module with
\mathbb{S}
–basis
\{h_{w}\mid w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\}
. Hence
\dim(H_{n}(q))=n!
. Another important
\mathbb{S}
–basis for
H_{n}(q)
is the
Murphy’s standard basis

B_{H_{n}}

[
49
]
, which is indexed by pairs of standard tableaux:

(2.8)

B_{H_{n}}=\{m_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}}^{\lambda}:=h_{d(\mathfrak{s})}^{\ast}m_{\lambda}h_{d(\mathfrak{t})}\mid\lambda\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}\text{ and }\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\in\operatorname{Std}(\lambda)\}.

where
\ast:H_{n}(q)\to H_{n}(q)
is the antiautomorphism given by
h_{w}^{*}=h_{w^{-1}}
and
m_{\lambda}:=\sum_{w\in\mathcal{S}_{\lambda}}q^{\ell(w)}h_{w}
.

In
[
47
]
, it was studied in detail the representation theory of
H_{n}(q)
through its cellularity. Here, the Murphy’s standard basis
B_{H_{n}}
is used as a cellular basis for the Iwahori–Hecke algebra.

2.4.2
The Temperley–Lieb algebra

For every integer
n\geq 3
, the
Temperley–Lieb algebra

TL_{n}(q)
is the
\mathbb{S}
–algebra presented by generators
h_{1},\ldots,h_{n-1}
satisfying (
2.7
), subject to the following additional relations:

(2.9)

\displaystyle h_{i,j}=0;\quad\text{where}\quad h_{i,j}=1+qh_{i}+qh_{j}+q^{2}h_{i}h_{j}+q^{2}h_{j}h_{i}+q^{3}h_{i}h_{j}h_{i},\qquad|i-j|=1.

The elements
h_{i,j}
are called the
Steinberg elements
. Observe that
TL_{n}(q)
is the quotient of
H_{n}(q)
by the two–sided ideal generated by these elements. It is well know that
\dim(TL_{n}(q))=\mathfrak{c}_{n}
, the
m
th
Catalan number

[
29
, A000108]
.

It is worth to mention that there are several definitions of the Temperley–Lieb algebra
[
55
,
38
,
31
]
. In our definition, we consider the following changes with respect to the
Härterich’s presentation

[
28
]
.

\begin{array}[]{c|c}\text{H\"{a}rterich}&\text{Actual}\\
\hline\cr q&{q^{2}}^{{\color[rgb]{1,1,1}1}}\\
T_{s_{i}}&qh_{i}\end{array}

In
[
28
]
, Härterich established a relation between the diagrammatical cellular basis of
TL_{n}(q)
and the Murphy’s cellular basis of
H_{n}(q)
given in (
2.8
). Indeed, from this basis he obtained a new cellular basis
B_{TL_{n}}
for
TL_{n}(q)
by considering only the standard tableaux with at most two columns. More precisely,

(2.10)

B_{TL_{n}}=\{m_{\mathfrak{s}\mathfrak{t}}\mid\lambda\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}\text{ and }\mathfrak{s},\mathfrak{t}\in\operatorname{Std}(\lambda)\},

where
\operatorname{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}
is the subset formed by the partitions in
\operatorname{Par}_{n}
whose Young diagrams have at most two columns.

2.4.3
The Young Hecke algebra and the Young Temperley–Lieb algebra

A
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
–multitableau
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}
is called
of the initial kind
if the
i
th component of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}
contains exactly the numbers of the
i
th component of
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. For every
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}
, we set
T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in\operatorname{Std}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\text{ is of the initial kind}\}
.

For every
\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, the
Young Hecke algebra

H_{\mu}
(resp.
Young Temperley–Lieb algebra

TL_{\mu}
) of
\mu
, is the subalgebra of
H_{n}(q)
(resp.
TL_{n}(q)
) generated by the simple transpositions of
\mathcal{S}_{\mu}
, that is

H_{\mu}\simeq H_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{\mu_{k}},\qquad TL_{\mu}\simeq TL_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes TL_{\mu_{k}}.

It is well known that the tensorial product of cellular algebras is cellular, see for example
[
24
, Section 3.2]
. Indeed, by using a similar argument as the one used in
[
24
, Section 3.4]
, we obtain that the elements of the Murphy’s basis of
H_{\mu}
are indexed by pairs of standard
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
–multitableaux of the initial kind, say
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}=(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}=(\mathfrak{t}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{t}_{k})
, with the condition that
\operatorname{\textbf{comp}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\mu
. More precisely, if
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
, then

B_{H_{\mu}}=\{m_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}\text{ and }\operatorname{\textbf{comp}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\mu\},\quad\text{where}\quad m_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=m_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}^{\lambda^{(1)}}\cdots m_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}^{\lambda^{(k)}}.

Similarly, the Murphy’s basis of
TL_{\mu}
is the set

B_{TL_{\mu}}=\{m_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}\text{ and }\operatorname{\textbf{comp}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\mu\},

where
\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}=\{(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}\mid\lambda^{(i)}\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}\text{ for all }i\in[k]\}
.

3
The algebra of braids and ties

The
algebra of braids and ties

[
3
]
, or simply the
bt–algebra
, is the
\mathbb{S}
–algebra
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
presented by generators
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
and
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1}
, subject to the following relations:

(3.1)

\displaystyle e_{i}^{2}=e_{i};\qquad e_{i}e_{j}=e_{j}e_{i};

(3.2)

\displaystyle g_{i}g_{j}g_{i}=g_{j}g_{i}g_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;\qquad g_{i}g_{j}=g_{j}g_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;

(3.3)

\displaystyle g_{i}e_{i}=e_{i}g_{i};\qquad g_{i}e_{j}=e_{j}g_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;

(3.4)

\displaystyle e_{i}g_{j}g_{i}=g_{j}g_{i}e_{j},\quad e_{i}e_{j}g_{j}=e_{i}g_{j}e_{i}=g_{j}e_{i}e_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;

(3.5)

\displaystyle g_{i}^{2}=1+(q-q^{-1})e_{i}g_{i}.

Observe that, due to (
3.5
), each
g_{i}
is invertible with inverse
g_{i}^{-1}=g_{i}-(q-q^{-1})e_{i}
.

3.1
Representation theory

For
n,r
positive integers satisfying
n\leq r
and
V
to be the free
\mathbb{S}
–module with basis
\{v_{i}^{a}\mid i\in[n],a\in[r]\}
, we define the operators
\mathbf{E},\mathbf{G}\in\operatorname{End}_{S}(V^{\otimes 2})
as follows:

(3.6)

(v_{i}^{a}\otimes v_{j}^{b})\mathbf{E}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }a\neq b,\\
v_{i}^{a}\otimes v_{j}^{b}&\text{if }a=b,\end{cases}

and

(3.7)

(v_{i}^{a}\otimes v_{j}^{b})\mathbf{G}=\begin{cases}v_{i}^{a}\otimes v_{j}^{b}&\text{if }a\neq b,\\
qv_{i}^{a}\otimes v_{j}^{b}&\text{if }a=b,\;i=j,\\
v_{j}^{a}\otimes v_{i}^{b}&\text{if }a=b,\;i>j,\\
(q-q^{-1})v_{i}^{a}\otimes v_{j}^{b}+v_{j}^{b}\otimes v_{i}^{a}&\text{if }a=b,\;i<j.\end{cases}

These ones can be extended to operators
\mathbf{E}_{i}
and
\mathbf{G}_{i}
acting on the tensor space
V^{\otimes n}
by letting
\mathbf{E}
and
\mathbf{G}
act on the
i
th and
(i+1)
th factors.

Theorem 3.1

(
[
52
, Corollary 4]
)
.

The map
\rho:\mathcal{E}_{n}\to\operatorname{End}_{S}(V^{\otimes n})
given by
g_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{G}_{i}
and
e_{i}\mapsto\mathbf{E}_{i}
defines a faithful representation of
\mathcal{E}_{n}
.

Now, we recall some important elements of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
.

Given
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
and
s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{k}}
a reduced expression of it, we set
g_{w}=g_{i_{1}}\cdots g_{i_{k}}
, and put
g_{\operatorname{id}}=1\in\mathbb{S}
. From Matsumoto’s theorem
[
48
]
, the element
g_{w}
is independent on the choice of the reduced expression.

For
i,j\in[n-1]
, define
e_{i,j}
, recursively, as follows:

(3.8)

e_{i,i+1}=e_{i}\quad\text{and}\quad e_{i,j}=g_{i}\cdots g_{j-1}e_{j}g_{j-1}^{-1}\cdots g_{i}^{-1}\quad\text{whenever}\quad j>i+1.

For every subset
B=\{i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\}
of
[n]
, define

(3.9)

E_{B}=\prod_{j=1}^{r-1}e_{i_{j},\,i_{j+1}},\quad\text{where}\quad E_{B}=1\quad\text{if}\quad|B|=1.

Given a set partition
\mathbf{I}=\{I_{1},\ldots,I_{k}\}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we set

(3.10)

E_{\mathbf{I}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}E_{I_{i}}.

The elements
E_{B}
and
E_{\mathbf{I}}
were originally introduced by Ryom–Hansen
[
52
]
in a slightly different manner, however, as it was mentioned in
[
8
]
, both definitions are equivalent. We will use the definitions in (
3.9
) and (
3.10
) because they are more useful for our purposes.

With the above notations it was shown in
[
52
, Theorem 3]
that the bt–algebra is a free
\mathbb{S}
–module with basis
\{E_{\bf I}g_{w}\mid\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\,w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}\}
. Thus, the dimension of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
is
\mathfrak{b}_{n}n!
. Furthermore, the elements
E_{\mathbf{I}}
and
g_{w}
satisfy the relation
E_{\mathbf{I}}g_{w}=g_{w}E_{\mathbf{I}w}
, where
w
acts on
\mathbf{I}
by permuting the elements of its blocks.

3.1.1
Ideal decomposition of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)

Here, we recall some results from
[
19
, Subsection 6.1]
.

Let
\mu:\mathcal{P}_{n}\times\mathcal{P}_{n}\to\Z
to be the
Möebius function
over the lattice
(\mathcal{P}_{n},\preceq)
, defined in
[
19
, Section 3.2]
.

Now, for every
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
, we define

(3.11)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}=\sum_{\mathbf{J}\succeq\mathbf{I}}\mu(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})E_{\mathbf{J}}.

From this standard construction, due to Solomon
[
54
]
, we obtain a set of orthogonal idempotents
\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}\mid\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
over
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
. Moreover, the elements
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}
satisfy the following relations with the basis elements of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
.

(3.12)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}g_{w}=g_{w}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}w}\qquad\text{ and }\qquad\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{J}}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}&\text{if }\mathbf{J}\preceq\mathbf{I},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

In particular, we observe that
\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}g_{w}\mid w\in\mathcal{S}_{n},\;\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{n}\}
is another
\mathbb{S}
–basis for
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
.

For every
\alpha\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}
, we say that
\mathbf{I}=(I_{a_{1}},\ldots,I_{a_{k}})\in\mathcal{P}_{n}
is
of type
\alpha
, denoted by
|\mathbf{I}|=\alpha
, if there is a permutation
w\in\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that
(|I_{a_{1}w}|,\ldots,|I_{a_{k}w}|)=\alpha
. Thus, we define the following element of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
:

(3.13)

\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\,=\sum_{\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{P}_{n},\,|\mathbf{I}|=\alpha}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}.

The set
\{\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}\}
is a family of central orthogonal idempotents of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
which is also
complete
, that is:

\sum_{\alpha\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}}\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}=1.

As a consequence we obtain that
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
can be decomposed as a direct sum of two–side ideals:

(3.14)

\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}}\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q),\quad\text{where}\quad\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{n}(q).

Moreover, observe that the ideals
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)
are also
\mathbb{S}
–algebras with identities
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
and
\mathbb{S}
–bases:

\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}g_{w}\mid w\in\mathcal{S}_{n},\,|\mathbf{I}|=\alpha\}

In particular,
\dim\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)=\mathfrak{b}_{n}(\alpha)n!
, where
\mathfrak{b}_{n}(\alpha)
is the number of set partition having type
\alpha

[
29
, A036040]
.

3.1.2
Isomorphism theorem for
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)

It was shown in
[
19
, Theorem 64]
that
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
is isomorphic to a direct sum of matrix algebras with coefficients over certain wreath algebras introduced in
[
24
]
. The key point to establish this isomorphism is the construction of a cellular basis
\{m_{\mathbbm{s}\mathbbm{t}}^{\Lambda}\}
, which is indexed by a

5
The tied–boxed Hecke algebra

In this section we introduce the
tied–boxed Hecke algebra
by means generators and relations. Then, we study its representation theory and give a cellular basis for it (Subsection
5.1
). Further, we show a diagrammatic realization through the boxed ramified monoid of the symmetric group (Subsection
5.2
). Additionally, we study the singular part of the ramified symmetric monoid (Subsection
5.3
).

For every positive integer
n
, the
tied–boxed Hecke algebra

bH_{n}(q)
is the
\mathbb{S}
–algebra presented by generators
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
satisfying (
3.1
), and generators
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
, subject to the following relations:

(5.1)

\displaystyle z_{i}z_{j}z_{i}=z_{j}z_{i}z_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;\qquad z_{i}z_{j}=z_{j}z_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;

(5.2)

\displaystyle e_{i}z_{i}=z_{i};\qquad e_{i}z_{j}=z_{j}e_{i};

(5.3)

\displaystyle z_{i}^{2}=e_{i}+(q-q^{-1})z_{i}.

5.1
Representation theory

Here we study the representation theory of the tied–boxed Hecke algebra. In particular, we get the dimension of
bH_{n}(q)
(Theorem
5.4
) and show that it can be embedded into the algebra of braids and ties (Corollary
5.5
). Furthermore, we give a cell datum for
bH_{n}(q)
and show that it is cellular (Theorem
5.10
).

Proposition 5.1
.

There is a homomorphism of
\mathbb{S}
–algebras
\iota_{1}:bH_{n}(q)\to\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
satisfying
\iota_{1}(e_{i})=e_{i}
and
\iota_{1}(z_{i})=e_{i}g_{i}
.

Proof.

We need to show that
\iota_{1}(e_{i})
and
\iota_{1}(z_{i})
satisfy the defining relations of
bH_{n}(q)
. Relations in (
3.1
) are directly verified from the definition. The second relation in (
5.1
) and the first one in (
5.2
) are consequences of (
3.1
) and (
3.2
). To prove the first relation in (
5.1
) and the second one in (
5.2
) we will use many times (
3.4
) and the commutativity relations:

(5.4)

z_{i}z_{j}z_{i}=e_{i}g_{i}e_{j}g_{j}e_{i}g_{i}=g_{i}(e_{i}e_{j}g_{j})e_{i}g_{i}=g_{i}g_{j}e_{i}e_{j}e_{i}g_{i}=g_{i}g_{j}e_{i}(e_{j}e_{i}g_{i})=g_{i}g_{j}e_{i}g_{i}e_{j}e_{i}=g_{i}g_{j}g_{i}e_{i}e_{j}e_{i}.

The first relation in (
5.1

3.2
) and the inverse process of (
5.4
). The second relation in (
5.2

e_{i}z_{j}=e_{i}e_{j}g_{j}=g_{j}e_{i}e_{j}=g_{j}e_{j}e_{i}=e_{j}g_{j}e_{i}=z_{j}e_{i}.

Finally, the quadratic relation in (

z_{i}^{2}=(e_{i}g_{i})^{2}=e_{i}g_{i}^{2}=e_{i}(1+(q-q^{-1})e_{i}g_{i})=e_{i}+(q-q^{-1})e_{i}g_{i}=e_{i}+(q-q^{-1})z_{i}.

given by

Proof.

This representation is immediately given by the composition

Remark 5.7
.

It is known that the algebra of braids and ties
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
can be regarded as a subalgebra of the
d
–modular Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
Y_{n,d}(q)

[
56
,
33
,
37
,
35
]
. Indeed,
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
is the subalgebra of
Y_{n,d}(q)
generated by the elements
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1}
together with the idempotents
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
defined as follows:

(5.7)

e_{i}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{d-1}t_{i}^{s}t_{i+1}^{-s},

where
Y_{n,d}(q)
is the algebra presented by generators
g_{1},\ldots,g_{n-1}
satisfying (
3.2
), and generators
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
subject to the following relations
[
33
,
37
,
35
,
16
]
:

(5.8)

\displaystyle t_{i}^{\,d}=1;\qquad t_{i}t_{j}=t_{j}t_{i};\qquad g_{i}t_{j}=t_{s_{i}(j)}g_{i};\qquad g_{i}^{2}=1+(q-q^{-1})e_{i}g_{i}.

Thus, due to Corollary
5.5
, the tied–boxed Hecke algebra
bH_{n}(q)
is the subalgebra of
Y_{n,d}(q)
generated by the idempotents in (
5.7
), together with the elements
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
defined as follows:

z_{i}=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{s=0}^{d-1}t_{i}^{s}t_{i+1}^{-s}g_{i}.

In what follows we will show that the tied–boxed Hecke algebra
bH_{n}(q)
is cellular in the sense of Graham–Lerher
[
25
]
. Similarly to the
bt
–algebra
[
19
]
, we first construct an ideal decomposition of
bH_{n}(q)
.

First, we define a
Moëbius function
on the lattice
(\mathcal{LP}_{n},\preceq)
as the map
\mu:\mathcal{LP}_{n}\times\mathcal{LP}_{n}\to\Z
such that

\mu(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})=\begin{cases}(-1)^{|\mathbf{I}|-|\mathbf{J}|}&\text{if }\mathbf{I}\preceq\mathbf{J},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

This Moëbius function will allows us to construct a set of orthogonal idempotents on
bH_{n}(q)
, which are a special case of the general construction given in
[
54
,
27
]
.

For each
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{LP}_{n}
, we define

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}=\sum_{\mathbf{J}\supseteq\mathbf{I}}\mu(\mathbf{I},\mathbf{J})E_{\mathbf{J}}.

For example, for
n=3
, we have the following idempotents:

\begin{array}[]{lcl}\mathbb{E}_{\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}\}}&=&\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1\},\{2\},\{3\}\}}-\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}}-\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1\},\{2,3\}\}}+\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1,2,3\}\}}=1-e_{1}-e_{2}+e_{1}e_{2},\\
\mathbb{E}_{\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}}&=&\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1,2\},\{3\}\}}-\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1,2,3\}\}}=e_{1}-e_{1}e_{2},\\
\mathbb{E}_{\{\{1\},\{2,3\}\}}&=&\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1\},\{2,3\}\}}-\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1,2,3\}\}}=e_{2}-e_{1}e_{2},\\
\mathbb{E}_{\{\{1,2,3\}\}}&=&\mathbf{E}_{\{\{1,2,3\}\}}=e_{1}e_{2}.\end{array}

Proposition 5.8
.

The following properties hold.

1.

\mathcal{I}_{\mathbb{E}}:=\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}\mid\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{LP}_{n}\}
is a complete set of central orthogonal idempotent elements of
bH_{n}
.

2.

For all
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{LP}_{n}
we have
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{J}}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}&\text{if }\mathbf{J}\preceq\mathbf{I},\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}

Proof.

The proof is similar to the one of
[
19
, Proposition 39]
.
∎

As an immediate consequence of Proposition
5.8
we obtain that
bH_{n}(q)
can be decomposed as a direct sum of two–side ideals.

(5.9)

bH_{n}(q)\simeq\displaystyle\bigoplus_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}bH_{n}(q)^{\mu},

where
bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}bH_{n}(q)
and
\mathbf{I}
is the unique linear set partition satisfying
\|\mathbf{I}\|=\mu
. Moreover, every two–side ideal
bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}
is also a subalgebra of
bH_{n}(q)
.

Corollary 5.9
.

Let
\mathbf{I}\in\mathcal{LP}_{n}
and suppose that
\|\mathbf{I}\|=\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
. Then, the
\mathbb{S}
–linear map

\begin{array}[]{ccccc}bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}&\to&H_{\mu}&\simeq&H_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{\mu_{k}}\\[8.53581pt]
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}z_{w}&\mapsto&z_{w}=z_{w_{1}}\cdots z_{w_{k}}&\mapsto&z_{w_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes z_{w_{k}}\end{array}

is an isomorphism of
\mathbb{S}
–algebras. In particular
\dim bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}=\mu_{1}!\cdots\mu_{k}!
.

Proof.

It is enough to note that
bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}
is a free
\mathbb{S}
–module with
\mathbb{S}
–basis
\{\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}z_{w}\mid w\in\mathcal{S}_{\mu}\}
, which is obtained by using the linear basis
\{\mathbf{E}_{\mathbf{I}}z_{w}\}
of
bH_{n}(q)
together with Proposition
5.8
(
2
).
∎

In virtue of Corollary
5.9
, the decomposition in (
5.9
) can be rewritten as follows:

(5.10)

bH_{n}(q)\,\,\,\simeq\displaystyle\bigoplus_{(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}H_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes H_{\mu_{k}}.

Due to this isomorphism, the representation theory of the algebra
bH_{n}(q)
is well known. In fact, we can construct a cell datum for
bH_{n}(q)
as follows:

1.

The poset is the set
\mathcal{LP}_{n}
of linear multipartitions of
n
together with the usual
dominance order
of multipartitions, which we will denote by
\triangleright
.

2.

For each
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{LP}_{n}
we consider
T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in\operatorname{Std}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\mid\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\text{ of the initial kind}\}
.

3.

The antiautomorphism
*:bH_{n}(q)\to bH_{n}(q)
is given by
e_{i}^{*}=e_{i}
and
z_{i}^{*}=z_{i}
.

4.

For each pair
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
, we define

\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=z_{d({\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}})}^{\ast}\mathbf{m}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}z_{d(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}})},\quad\text{where}\quad\mathbf{m}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}q^{\ell(w)}z_{w}.

Theorem 5.10
.

The tied–boxed Hecke algebra
bH_{n}(q)
is cellular with cellular basis:

B=\left\{\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{LP}_{n}\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\right\}.

Proof.

Since the Young Hecke algebras
H_{\mu}
are cellular, see Subsection
2.4.3
, the isomorphism in Corollary
5.9
implies that the algebras
bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}
are cellular as well. Moreover, a cellular basis for
bH_{n}(q)^{\mu}
is given by

B_{\mu}=\left\{\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid\operatorname{\textbf{comp}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\mu\text{ and }{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\right\}.

Then, from (
5.10
), it is immediate that
bH_{n}(q)
is a free
\mathbb{S}
–module with basis
B=\coprod_{\mu}B_{\mu}
. Before proving that
B
satisfies the defining conditions of a cellular basis, observe that the elements
\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
can be decomposed as a product of classical Murphy’s elements. Indeed, if
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}=(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}=(\mathfrak{t}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{t}_{k})
are standard
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})
tableaux of the initial kind, then

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}&=&z_{d({\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}})}^{\ast}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\sum_{w\in\mathcal{S}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}q^{\ell(w)}z_{w}\right)z_{d(\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}})},\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{1})}\cdots z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{k})})^{\ast}\cdot(m_{\lambda^{(1)}}\cdots m_{\lambda^{(k)}})\cdot z_{d(\mathfrak{t}_{1})}\cdots z_{d(\mathfrak{t}_{k})},\\[5.69054pt]
&=&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{k})}^{\ast}\cdots z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{1})}^{*}\cdot(m_{\lambda^{(1)}}\cdots m_{\lambda^{(k)}})\cdot z_{d(\mathfrak{t}_{1})}\cdots z_{d(\mathfrak{t}_{k})}.\end{array}

Now, as
z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{i})}
and
m_{\lambda^{(j)}}
belong to different components of the decomposition of
bH_{n}(q)
in (
5.10
), we have

(5.11)

\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}(z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{l})}^{\ast}m_{\lambda^{(1)}}z_{d(\mathfrak{t}_{1})})\cdots(z_{d(\mathfrak{s}_{k})}^{\ast}m_{\lambda^{(k)}}z_{d(\mathfrak{t}_{k})})=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}.

Since the factors in the above decomposition commute with each other and
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
is invariant under
\ast
(it is immediate from the definitions), we have

\left(\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)^{\ast}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}^{\ast}\left(m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\right)^{\ast}\cdots\left(m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}\right)^{\ast}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{t}_{1}\mathfrak{s}_{1}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{t}_{k}\mathfrak{s}_{k}}=\mathbf{m}_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}.

To prove Condition
(ii)
it is enough to show that it holds for the cases when
a=e_{i}
and
a=z_{j}
. The case
a=e_{i}
is trivial because
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}e_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
or
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}e_{i}=0
, see Proposition
5.8
. The same occurs for the case when
a=z_{i}
, with
i
and
i+1
belong to different blocks of the set partition
\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, because
z_{i}=e_{i}z_{i}
. Finally, suppose that
a=z_{i}
, with
i
and
i+1
belonging to the block
I_{j}
of
\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Then,

\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}z_{i}=\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(j)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{j}\mathfrak{t}_{j}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}\right)z_{i}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\cdots\left(m^{\lambda^{(j)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{j}\mathfrak{t}_{j}}z_{i}\right)\cdots m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}.

By applying Condition
(ii)
in the factor
m^{\lambda^{(j)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{j}\mathfrak{t}_{j}}h_{i}
, we obtain

(5.12)

\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}z_{i}=\sum_{\mathfrak{v}_{j}\in\operatorname{Std}(\lambda^{(j)})}r_{\mathfrak{v}_{j}}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(j)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{j}\mathfrak{v}_{j}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}\quad\mod{H_{n_{j}}^{\lambda^{(j)}}}

where
n_{j}
is the size of
\lambda^{(j)}
. Note that if
\mu\triangleright\lambda^{(j)}
, then
{\boldsymbol{\mu}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\mu,\ldots\lambda^{(k)})\triangleright(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(j)},\lambda^{(k)})={\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. Thus, by using the inverse process of the decomposition in (
5.11
), the equality (
5.12
) can be rewritten as

\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}z_{i}=\sum_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{j}\in\operatorname{Std}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}r_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{j}}\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{j}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mod{bH_{n}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}},

where
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{v}}_{j}=(\mathfrak{t}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{v}_{j},\ldots,\mathfrak{t}_{k})
. This concludes the proof.
∎

5.2
Diagrammatic realization of
bH_{n}(q)

Here we study the boxed ramified monoid associated with the symmetric group,
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
, which can be regarded as a basis for the algebra
bH_{n}(q)
. Specifically, we get that
bH_{n}(q)
is a
q
–deformation of the monoid algebra generated by
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
(Theorem
5.13
), providing
bH_{n}(q)
with a structure of diagram algebra. Also, we show that the center of
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
coincides with the monoid of compositions of
n
(Proposition
5.16
).

It is well known that
|\mathcal{S}_{m}|=m!
for all integer
m\geq 1

[
29
, A356634]
. So, similarly as it was done in Proposition
4.1
and Remark
4.3
, we have

|\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})|=\sum_{(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})}\mu_{1}!\cdots\mu_{k}!=(1,3,11,47,231,1303,8431,62391,524495,4960775,\ldots),

where
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})
is a composition of
n
. See
[
29
, A051296]
.

For each
i\in[n-1]
, define
z_{i}
to be the ramified partition
e_{i}s_{i}
in
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
, which is represented as in Figure
26
. Due to the relations that define
\mathcal{R}(S_{n})
, the elements
z_{i}
satisfy the following relations:

(5.13)

\displaystyle z_{i}z_{j}z_{i}=z_{j}z_{i}z_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;\qquad z_{i}z_{j}=z_{j}z_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;\qquad e_{i}z_{j}=z_{j}e_{i};

(5.14)

\displaystyle z_{i}^{2}=e_{i};\qquad e_{i}z_{i}=z_{i}.

Proposition 5.12
.

The monoid
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
is generated by
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
(I,J)\in\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
. We have
(I,J)=(\tilde{I}_{1},\tilde{J}_{1})/\cdots/(\tilde{I}_{k},\tilde{J}_{k})
with
(\tilde{I}_{i},\tilde{J}_{i})\in\mathcal{R}(\mathfrak{C}_{m_{i}})
for all
i\in[k]
, where
I=\tilde{I}_{1}/\cdots/\tilde{I}_{k}
, and
J=\tilde{J}_{1}/\cdots/\tilde{J}_{k}
is the boxed decompositions of
J
. Thus, for each
i\in[k]
, we have
(\tilde{I}_{i},\tilde{J}_{i})=(e_{1}\cdots e_{m_{i}})p_{i}
, where
p_{i}\in\mathcal{S}_{m_{i}}
. In general, if
s=s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{q}}
is a permutation in
\mathcal{S}_{m}
, we have
(e_{1}\cdots e_{m})s=(e_{1}\cdots e_{m})s_{i_{1}}\cdots s_{i_{q}}=(e_{1}\cdots e_{m})(e_{i_{1}}s_{i_{1}})\cdots(e_{i_{q}}s_{i_{q}})=(e_{1}\cdots e_{m})z_{i_{1}}\cdots z_{i_{q}}
because of (
5.14
). Therefore
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
is generated by
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
,
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
.
∎

Theorem 5.13
.

The monoid
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
is presented by generators
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
satisfying (
3.1
), and generators
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
subject to (
5.13
) and (
5.14
).

Proof.

Observe that
\mathcal{C}_{m}\times\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+}
is presented by generators
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
and
\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{n-1}
satisfying (
3.1
) and (
2.6
) together with the relation
e_{i}\sigma_{j}=\sigma_{j}e_{i}
. So, Proposition
5.12
and (
5.13
) imply that
\varphi:\mathcal{C}_{n}\times\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+}\to\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
defined by
e_{i}\mapsto e_{i}
and
\sigma_{i}\mapsto z_{i}
is an epimorphism. Now, let
K
be the congruence closure generated by the pairs
(\sigma_{i}^{2},e_{i})
and
(e_{i}\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i})
for all
i\in[n-1]
. Observe that
K\subseteq\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)
because of (
5.14
).

Consider
e,e^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
and
\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+}
such that
\sigma=\sigma_{a_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{a_{p}}
,
\sigma^{\prime}=\sigma_{b_{1}}\cdots\sigma_{b_{q}}
and
\varphi(e\sigma)=\varphi(e^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})
. Then, because of (
5.14
), we have

(5.15)

\varphi(e\sigma)=ee_{a_{1}}\cdots e_{a_{p}}s_{a_{1}}\cdots s_{a_{p}},\qquad\varphi(e^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})=e^{\prime}e_{b_{1}}\cdots e_{b_{q}}s_{b_{1}}\cdots s_{b_{p}}.

Observe that
e\sigma\equiv_{K}ee_{a_{1}}\cdots e_{a_{p}}\sigma
and
e^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}\equiv_{K}e^{\prime}e_{b_{1}}\cdots e_{b_{q}}\sigma^{\prime}
because
(\sigma_{i}^{2},e_{i})\in K
. On the other hand, since
\mathcal{R}(\mathcal{S}_{n})=\mathcal{P}_{n}\rtimes\mathcal{S}_{n}
, the decompositions of
\varphi(e\sigma)
and
\varphi(e^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime})
in (
5.15
) are unique, hence

ee_{a_{1}}\cdots e_{a_{p}}=e^{\prime}e_{b_{1}}\cdots e_{b_{q}},\qquad s_{a_{1}}\cdots s_{a_{p}}=s_{b_{1}}\cdots s_{b_{q}}.

Note that each relation of
\mathcal{S}_{n}
used to get
s_{b_{1}}\cdots s_{b_{q}}
from
s_{a_{1}}\cdots s_{a_{p}}
can be translated into a relation of
z_{i}
’s because if
s_{i}
occurs above then
e_{i}
occurs as well. Thus
e\sigma\equiv_{K}ee_{a_{1}}\cdots e_{a_{p}}\sigma\equiv_{K}e^{\prime}e_{b_{1}}\cdots e_{b_{q}}\sigma^{\prime}\equiv_{K}e^{\prime}\sigma^{\prime}
, and then
\operatorname{Ker}(\varphi)\subseteq K
. Therefore
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})\simeq(\mathcal{C}_{n}\times\mathcal{B}_{n}^{+})/K
, which proves the theorem.
∎

It is immediate from Theorem
5.13
that
bH_{n}(q)
is a
q
–deformation of the monoid algebra generated by
\mathcal{BR}(S_{n})
. Thus, the algebra

6
The tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra

In this section, inspired by the quotient of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
in
[
53
]
, which in turn was inspired by
[
28
]
, we introduce the
tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra
. Then, we give a cellular basis for it (Theorem
6.2
) and study its connection with the
partition Temperley–Lieb algebra
introduced by Juyumaya
[
36
]
(Subsection
6.1
). Further, we show a diagrammatic realization through the boxed ramified monoid of the Jones monoid (Subsection
6.2
). Additionally, we study the boxed ramified monoid of the Brauer monoid (Subsection
6.3
).

The
tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra

bTL_{n}(q)
is the quotient of
bH_{n}(q)
by the two–sided ideal generated by the following
Steinberg elements
:

(6.1)

z_{i,\,j}=e_{i}e_{j}(1+qz_{i}+qz_{j}+q^{2}z_{i}z_{j}+q^{2}z_{j}z_{i}+q^{3}z_{i}z_{j}z_{i}),\qquad|i-j|=1.

Proposition 6.1
.

The tied–boxed Temperley–Lieb algebra
bTL_{n}(q)
is presented by generators
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
satisfying (
3.1
), and generators
d_{i}=q^{-1}e_{i}+z_{i}
for all
i\in[n-1]
, subject to the following relations:

(6.2)

\displaystyle d_{i}^{2}=(q+q^{-1})d_{i};\qquad d_{i}d_{j}d_{i}=e_{j}d_{i}e_{j},\quad|i-j|=1;

(6.3)

\displaystyle d_{i}d_{j}=d_{j}d_{i},\quad|i-j|>1;\qquad d_{i}e_{i}=d_{i};\qquad d_{i}e_{j}=e_{j}d_{i};

Proof.

By definition, the algebra
bTL_{n}(q)
is presented by the same generators and relations than
bH_{n}(q)
, together with the additional relation
z_{i,\,j}=0
for each Steinberg element in (
6.1
). Observe that
z_{i}=d_{i}-q^{-1}e_{i}
for all
i\in[i-1]
, then, in order to get a presentation for
bTL_{n}(q)
by generators
d_{i}
and
e_{i}
, it is enough to replace each
z_{i}
with
d_{i}-q^{-1}e_{i}
in the relations of this algebra.

Relations in (
6.3
) are directly equivalent to the second relation in (
5.1
) and the ones in (
5.2
), respectively. By using (
6.3
) we easily show that (
5.3
) is equivalent to the quadratic relation in (
6.2
). Thus, we get

z_{i,\,j}=e_{i}e_{j}+qe_{j}z_{i}+qe_{i}z_{j}+q^{2}z_{i}z_{j}+q^{2}z_{j}z_{i}+q^{3}z_{i}z_{j}z_{i}=q^{3}d_{i}d_{j}d_{i}-q^{2}e_{j}d_{i}^{2}+qe_{j}d_{i}=q^{3}(d_{i}d_{j}d_{i}-e_{j}d_{i}).

This implies that
z_{i,\,j}=0
is equivalent to the second relation in (
6.2
). Finally, observe that

z_{i}z_{j}z_{i}=-q^{-1}d_{j}d_{i}-q^{-1}d_{i}d_{j}+q^{-2}e_{i}d_{j}+q^{-2}e_{j}d_{i}-q^{-3}e_{i}e_{j}\quad\text{for all}\quad|i-j|=1.

Thus, by using (
6.2
) and (
6.3
), the first relation in (
5.1
) is superfluous. This concludes the proof.
∎

As with the tied–boxed Hecke algebra, for each
\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, we can define subalgebras
bTL_{n}^{\mu}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}bTL_{n}^{\mu}
of
bTL_{n}
, where
\mathbf{I}
is the unique linear set partition satisfying
\|\mathbf{I}\|=\mu
. Moreover, there is a two–side ideal decomposition of
bTL_{n}
given as follows:

(6.4)

bTL_{n}=\bigoplus_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}bTL_{n}^{\mu}

A similar argue to the one used in the proof of Corollary
5.9
implies that

bTL_{n}^{\mu}\simeq TL_{\mu}=TL_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes TL_{\mu_{k}},\qquad\mu=(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})\in\mathcal{C}_{n}.

Indeed, from the decomposition in factors given in (
5.11
), we get the following isomorphism of
\mathbb{S}
–algebras in terms of the cellular basis:

\begin{array}[]{ccc}TL_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes TL_{\mu_{k}}&\to&bTL_{n}^{\mu}\\[7.11317pt]
m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}&\mapsto&\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}m^{\lambda^{(1)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{1}\mathfrak{t}_{1}}\cdots m^{\lambda^{(k)}}_{\mathfrak{s}_{k}\mathfrak{t}_{k}}=\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\end{array}

where
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}=(\lambda^{(1)},\ldots,\lambda^{(k)})\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}
, and
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}=(\mathfrak{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{s}_{k})
and
\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}=(\mathfrak{t}_{1},\ldots,\mathfrak{t}_{k})
are standard
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
–multitableaux of the initial kind. Thus, due to (
6.4
), we obtain the following isomorphism of
\mathbb{S}
–algebras:

bTL_{n}\,\simeq\,\bigoplus_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}TL_{\mu_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes TL_{\mu_{k}}.

In particular,
\dim bTL_{n}(q)=\sum_{\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{n}}\mathfrak{c}_{\mu_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{c}_{\mu_{k}}
.

The following theorem is immediate from the previous results.

Theorem 6.2
.

For each
\mu\in\mathcal{C}_{n}
, the algebra
bTL_{n}^{\mu}
is cellular with cellular basis

B_{bTL_{n}^{\mu}}=\{\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}),\,{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2}\text{ and }\operatorname{\textbf{comp}}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})=\mu\}.

In consequence, the algebra
bTL_{n}(q)
is cellular with cellular basis
B_{bTL_{n}}=\{\mathbf{m}_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}^{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\in\mathcal{M}\mathrm{Par}_{n}^{\leq 2},\,{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}},\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}\in T({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})\}
.

6.1
Connection with the partition Temperley–Lieb algebra

Here we explore a strong connection between
bTL_{n}(q)
and the
partition Temperley–Lieb algebra

\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)

[
36
]
. It was shown in
[
53
]
that a generalized version of
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
is cellular by finding a concrete cellular basis for it. Here we rediscover this result for the particular case of the
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
. Further, we establish an explicit isomorphism between
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
and a direct sum of matrix algebras (Theorem
6.3
).

For every integer
n\geq 3
,
the partition Temperley–Lieb algebra

\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
is the quotient
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)/J_{n}
, where
J_{n}
is the two–sided ideal of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
generated by the elements
e_{i}e_{j}g_{i,j}
, where
g_{i,j}
are the
Steinberg elements
:

g_{i,j}=1+qg_{i}+qg_{j}+q^{2}g_{i}g_{j}+q^{2}g_{j}g_{i}+q^{3}g_{i}g_{j}g_{i},\qquad|i-j|=1.

Due to the decomposition of
\mathcal{E}_{n}(q)
as a direct sum of ideals
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)
in (
3.14
), we get the following induced decomposition of
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
:

(6.5)

\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)\,=\bigoplus_{\alpha\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}}\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}(q),\quad\text{where}\quad\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q).

Now, for each
\alpha=(k_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,k_{r}^{m_{r}})\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}
such that
k_{1}>\cdots>k_{r}
, we define a Temperley–Lieb version of the algebra
H^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)
, as follows:

TL_{\alpha}^{wr}(q)=TL_{k_{1}}(q)\wr\mathcal{S}_{m_{1}}\otimes\cdots\otimes TL_{k_{r}}(q)\wr\mathcal{S}_{m_{r}}.

The following result is partially inspired by
[
17
, Theorem 4.7]
, which establishes an isomorphism between a Temperley–Lieb–type quotient of the Yokonuma–Hecke algebra
\operatorname{FLT}_{r,n}(q)
and a direct sum of matrix algebras over certain Hecke algebras.

Theorem 6.3
.

For each partition
\alpha=(n_{1},\ldots,n_{r})
of
n
, the isomorphism
\Psi_{\alpha}
sending
m_{\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{s}}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{t}}}\mapsto x_{\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{s}}_{0}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{t}}_{0}}M_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{t}}}
in Theorem
3.3
, induces an isomorphism

\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)\simeq\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}\big(TL^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)\big).

Taking into account the cellular basis of
TL_{n}(q)
in (
2.10
), we obtain the following results as immediate consequences of Theorem
6.3
.

Corollary 6.4
.

The following is a cellular basis for
\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)
:

B_{TL^{\leq 2}_{n}}=\left\{m_{\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{s}}\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{t}}}\mid\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{s}},\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{t}}\in\operatorname{Std}(\Lambda)\text{ and }\Lambda\in\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\leq 2}\right\},

where
\mathcal{L}_{n}^{\leq 2}
denotes the collection of pairs
({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\mu}})
in
\mathcal{L}_{n}
such that each component of the Young diagram of
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
has at most two columns.

Corollary 6.5
.

The dimension of
\operatorname{PTL}(q)
is given by the following formula:

\dim\operatorname{PTL}_{n}(q)\,\,\,=\sum_{(k_{1}^{m_{1}},\ldots,k_{r}^{m_{r}})\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}}\!b_{n}(\alpha)^{2}(\mathfrak{c}_{k_{1}})^{m_{1}}m_{1}!\cdots(\mathfrak{c}_{k_{r}})^{m_{r}}m_{r}!,

where
\mathfrak{c}_{k}
denotes the
k
th
Catalan number
.

Proof of Theorem
6.3
.

Let
\pi_{1}^{\alpha}:\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}\big(H^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)\big)\to\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}\big(TL^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)\big)
be the natural surjection induced by the projection
H^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)\twoheadrightarrow TL_{\alpha}^{wr}(q)
. We will first prove that
\pi_{1}^{\alpha}\circ\Psi_{\alpha}
factors through
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}\mathcal{E}_{n}/\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}J_{n}\simeq\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}
. To show this, it is enough to check that

(6.6)

\pi_{1}^{\alpha}\circ\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}e_{1}e_{2}g_{1,2})=0,

because the Steinberg elements are all conjugate to each other in
\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)

[
36
, Proposition 4.5]
. For short, we set
\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}=\{\{1,2,3\},\{4\},\ldots,\{n\}\}
. In particular, we note that
E_{\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}}=e_{1}e_{2}
. Then, for the case when
n_{i}\leq 2
for all
i
, (
6.6
) holds trivially since
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}E_{\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}}=0
for all
\mathbf{I}
of type
\alpha
. Suppose now that
\alpha\in\operatorname{Par}_{n}
such that
n_{j}\geq 3
for some
j
. Directly from the definition
3.13
of
\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}
together with (
3.12
) we have that

\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}e_{1}e_{2}g_{1,2})=\mathop{\sum_{\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}\subseteq\mathbf{I}}}_{|\mathbf{I}|=\alpha}\Psi_{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}}g_{1,2}\right)=\sum_{w\in\mathbf{D}_{\{1,2,3\}}^{\alpha}}\Psi_{\alpha}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w}g_{1,2}\right),

where
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
is any multipartition of type
\alpha
and
\mathbf{D}_{\{1,2,3\}}^{\alpha}
is the set of distinguished right coset representatives
w
of
\mathcal{S}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}
in
\mathcal{S}_{n}
such that
\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w
. In particular, we can consider
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
as the increasing multipartition obtained by reordering the components of the multipartition
((1^{n_{1}}),\ldots,(1^{n_{i-1}}),(3,1^{n_{i}-3}),(1^{n_{i+1}}),\ldots,(1^{n_{r}}))
, where
i
is the index of the component of
\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w
containing the set
\{1,2,3\}
. Thus, we have

(6.7)

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w}g_{1,2}=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}g_{1,2}=g_{w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}^{\ast}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}g_{w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}g_{1,2}=g_{w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}^{\ast}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}g_{k,k+1}g_{w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}},

where
k=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{i-1}+1
and
{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}
is the standard
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
-multitableau associated with the distinguished right coset representative
w
, that is,
w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}=w
. From the definition of
\Psi_{\alpha}
and (
6.7
) it now follows that

\Psi_{\alpha}(\mathbb{E}_{\alpha}e_{1}e_{2}g_{1,2})=\mathop{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\in\operatorname{Std}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}}_{\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}\Psi_{\alpha}\left(g_{w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}^{\ast}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}g_{k,k+1}g_{w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}\right)=\mathop{\sum_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}\in\operatorname{Std}({\boldsymbol{\lambda}})}}_{\mathbf{I}_{\{1,2,3\}}\subseteq\mathbf{I}_{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}w_{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}g_{k,k+1}M_{{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{s}}}}

which is indeed equal to zero in
\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}(TL^{wr}_{\alpha})
since each
g_{k,k+1}
is equal to zero in
TL^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)
. Thus we get the following commutative diagram of
\mathbb{S}
–homomorphisms

\textstyle{\mathcal{E}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\Psi_{\alpha}}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{2}^{\alpha}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}\big(H^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)\big)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}
\scriptstyle{\pi_{1}^{\alpha}}
\textstyle{\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces\ignorespaces}

\textstyle{\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}\big(TL^{wr}_{\alpha}(q)\big)}

where
\pi_{1}^{\alpha}
and
\pi_{2}^{\alpha}
are the natural projections. Similarly, by using the inverse map of
\Psi_{\alpha}
, we have that there is a unique
\mathbb{S}
–homomorphism
\overline{\theta_{\alpha}}:\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}(TL^{wr}_{\alpha}(q))\to\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}(q)
such that
\overline{\theta_{\alpha}}\circ\pi_{1}^{\alpha}=\pi_{2}^{\alpha}\circ\Psi_{\alpha}^{-1}
. Combining these two facts we have that

(6.8)

(\overline{\theta_{\alpha}}\circ\theta_{\alpha})\circ\pi_{2}^{\alpha}=\pi_{2}^{\alpha}\quad\text{ and }\quad(\theta_{\alpha}\circ\overline{\theta_{\alpha}})\circ\pi_{1}^{\alpha}=\pi_{1}^{\alpha}

Since
\pi_{1}^{\alpha}
and
\pi_{2}^{\alpha}
are both surjective, the relations in (

\overline{\theta_{\alpha}}\circ\theta_{\alpha}={\operatorname{id}}_{\operatorname{PTL}_{n}^{\alpha}}
and
\theta_{\alpha}\circ\overline{\theta_{\alpha}}={\operatorname{id}}_{\operatorname{Mat}_{b_{n}(\alpha)}(TL^{wr}_{\alpha}(q))}
. This concludes the proof.
∎

6.3
The boxed ramified Brauer monoid

As both the symmetric group and the Jones monoid are submonoids of
\mathcal{B}r_{n}
, the boxed ramified monoid of the Brauer monoid
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{B}r_{n})
is an extension of
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{S}_{n})
and
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{J}_{n})
at once. Here we study this monoid, in particular we give a presentation for it (Theorem
6.12
).

It is well known that
|\mathcal{B}r_{m}|=(2m-1)!!
for all
m\geq 1

[
29
, A001147]
. So, similarly as it was done in Proposition
4.1
and Remark
4.3
, we have

|\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{B}r_{n})|=\sum_{(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})}(2\mu_{1}-1)!!\cdots(2\mu_{k}-1)!!=(1,4,22,154,1330,13882,171802,2474098,\ldots),

where
(\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{k})
is a composition of
n
. See
[
29
, A295553]
.

Proposition 6.10
.

The monoid
\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{B}r_{n})
is generated by
d_{1},\ldots,d_{n-1}
,
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n-1}
and
z_{1},\ldots,z_{n-1}
.

Proof.

Let
(I,J)\in\mathcal{BR}(\mathcal{B}r_{n})
. Since
I\in\mathcal{B}r_{n}
,
