Bounds for the Competition-Independence game on trees
Jan Petr, Julien Portier

Abstract
In this paper we prove that Sweller has a strategy so that the Sweller-Start Competition-Independence game lasts at least ( 5 ​ n + 3 ) / 13 (5n+3)/13 moves for every tree. Moreover, we show that there exist arbitrarily large trees such that the Sweller-Start Competition-Independence game lasts at most ( 5 ​ n + 26 ) / 12 (5n+26)/12 moves, disproving a conjecture by Henning.

Competition-independence games

The upper bound

This section is devoted to proving the following result, which indeed immediately implies

Theorem 2.1
.

For every

Proof.

Let
S_{k}
be the graph formed by
k
paths on
3

is formed by joining two copies of

Figure 1:
The tree

We describe here a strategy for Diminisher such that the Sweller-Start competition-independence game on

finishes after at most
3+5k/2
moves. If Sweller plays for its first move one of the central vertices, then Diminisher plays any legal move for its first move. Otherwise, one of the central vertices is a legal move, which Diminisher plays for its first move. Let
S^{1}_{k}
be the copy of
S_{k}
where the central vertex has been played and
S^{2}_{k}
be the copy of
S_{k}
where the central vertex has not been played. For the next moves, we instruct Diminisher to play the middle vertex on each of the paths of

where no player has played yet, thus making sure only one move is played on these paths throughout the whole game. It is clear that Diminisher is able to play at least

at most two moves will be played throughout the game, and on each path of

exactly one move will be played throughout the game.

In total, we have

∎

3.1
The strategy and notation

The competition-independence game can be rephrased as follows. Starting with a graph
G
, the players take turns, alternatively removing vertices together with their neighbourhoods. The game ends when the resulting graph is empty. Diminisher’s goal is to make the game as short as possible, whereas Sweller’s goal is to make it last as long as possible.

Considering global properties of graphs, a candidate for a heuristically good move for Sweller

•

removes only few vertices, and also, with the aim to make future moves remove fewer vertices,

•

removes many edges (so that neighbourhoods get smaller), and

•

creates many isolated vertices (as they will need to be played at some point, not removing any more vertices).

The following paragraphs are devoted to introducing Sweller’s strategy for Sweller-start competition game on a forest
F
on
C
components, together with notation we shall use. Let
K(G)
stand for the number of isolated vertices in a graph
G
. With the convention of
F_{D}^{0}=F
, let
F_{i}^{D}
be the forest after Diminisher’s
i
-th move. For any
u\in V(F_{i-1}^{D})
, we set:

•

v_{i}^{S}(u)=|N[u]|
, that is, the number of vertices that get removed if Sweller chooses
u
for its move,

•

e_{i}^{S}(u)=|E(F_{i-1}^{D})|-|E(F_{i-1}^{D}-N[u])|
, that is, the number of edges that get removed if Sweller chooses
u
for its move, and

•

k_{i}^{S}(u)=K(F_{i-1}^{D}-N[u])-K(F_{i-1}^{D})
, that is, the difference between the number of isolated vertices in the resulting forest if Sweller chooses
u
and in
F_{i-1}^{D}
.

We instruct Sweller to follow a simple greedy strategy:

Strategy 3.2
.

Sweller picks any vertex

m_{i}^{S}:V(F_{i-1}^{D})\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
, with

, where
\alpha=3/8
and
\beta=13/8

Suppose Sweller follows this greedy strategy and consider any sequence of Diminisher’s replies. Let
N
be the number of moves the game lasts. We will ultimately show

. Note that if
N
is even, Diminisher plays the last move, whereas if
N
is odd, the last move is played by Sweller. We set

, the number of moves played by Diminisher.

Let

be the vertex Sweller chooses for its
i
-th move and let

be the forest after Sweller’s
i
-th move, that is,

be the

-th move of Diminisher, then

•

and

It will be convenient for us to consider changes to the forest after both players make their move. In accordance with this, for all

Finally, we set

3.3
Bounding the number of moves

We now prove
Theorem

1.3
using
Lemma

3.3
. Recall that

is the total number of moves played by both players. Since the number of vertices removed throughout the game is exactly the number

of vertices in

\displaystyle n=\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil N/2\rceil}v_{i}^{S}(w_{i}^{S})+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}v_{i}^{D}(w_{i}^{D})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}v_{i}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{2\nmid N\}}\cdot v_{r+1}^{S}(w_{r+1}^{S}).

(1)

Similarly, the number of edges removed throughout the game is exactly the number of edges in
F
, hence we have

\displaystyle n-C=\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil N/2\rceil}e_{i}^{S}(w_{i}^{S})+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}e_{i}^{D}(w_{i}^{D})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{i}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{2\nmid N\}}\cdot e_{r+1}^{S}(w_{r+1}^{S}).

(2)

Moreover, as
k_{i}
is the increment of the number of isolated vertices, and that there are no isolated vertices once the game ends, we have

\displaystyle 0\geq\sum_{i=1}^{\lceil N/2\rceil}k_{i}^{S}(w_{i}^{S})+\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}k_{i}^{D}(w_{i}^{D})=\sum_{i=1}^{r}k_{i}+\mathbbm{1}_{\{2\nmid N\}}\cdot k_{r+1}^{S}(w_{r+1}^{S}).

(3)

Using
Lemma

3.3
and then plugging (
1
), (
2
) and (
3
) gives

\displaystyle N

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\lfloor N/2\rfloor}2+\mathbbm{1}_{\{2\nmid N\}}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{\beta}(\sum_{i=1}^{r}(1-\beta)k_{i}+v_{i}-\alpha e_{i})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathbbm{1}_{\{2\nmid N\}}\cdot((1-\beta)k_{r+1}^{S}(w_{i}^{S})+v_{r+1}^{S}(w_{i}^{S})-\alpha e_{r+1}^{S}(w_{i}^{S}))

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1-\alpha}{\beta}n+\frac{\alpha C}{\beta},

hence,
N\geq\frac{5n+3C}{13}
as wanted.∎

4
Concluding remarks and further directions

The following question remains unsolved.

Question 4.1
.

What is the value of
c=\inf\{\dfrac{I_{s}(T)}{|V(T)|}:\text{T is a tree}\}
?

In this paper, we showed that we have
5/13\leq c\leq 5/12
. The values of the constants
\alpha
and
\beta
have been optimised but the authors believe that with the same method and a more careful case analysis, it is likely that one can improve
Theorem

1.3
to a lower bound
c^{\prime}n
for some
c^{\prime}>5/13
. However, it does not seem that the technique developped here could lead to anything better than a lower bound of
2n/5
for the following reason. With the notations from the previous section, if Diminisher’s first move has
l=p=n/5
, then the best we can ensure with the reasoning we had above is that Sweller’s previous move removed constantly many vertices and roughly
n/5
edges from the graph. Then our technique cannot rule out that the resulting graph is now roughly a disjoint union of
2n/5

K_{2}
’s, and consequently that the game will last roughly
2n/5
more moves. Hence, if it turns out that
c>2/5
it seems necessary that a proof of such a lower bound will require analysis of the general structure of the graph and not just of the local structure around Diminisher’s moves.

Note that one can ask
4.1
for forests instead of trees as it is not clear whether the two problems have the same answer.

The authors would also like to point out that the following conjecture posed by Goddard and Henning
[
6
]
is yet to be settled.

Conjecture 4.2
.

Let
T
be a tree of order
n\geq 2
, then
I_{d}(T)\leq\frac{3}{4}n
.
