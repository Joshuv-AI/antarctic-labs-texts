Modulation of chiral anomaly and bilinear magnetoconductivity in Weyl semimetals by impurity-resonance states
Mei-Wei Hu 1, Zhuo-Yan Fang 1, Hou-Jian Duan 1,2, Mou Yang 1,2, Ming-Xun Deng 1,2, Rui-Qiang Wang 1,2

Abstract
The phenomenon of nonlinear transport has attracted tremendous interest within the condensed matter community. We present a theoretical framework for nonlinear transport based on the nonequilibrium retarded Green’s function, and examine the impact of disorder on nonlinear magnetotransport in Weyl semimetals (WSMs). It is demonstrated that bilinear magnetoconductivity can be induced in disordered WSMs by several mechanisms, including impurity-induced tilting of the Weyl cones, Lorentz-force-induced normal orbital magnetic moment, and chiral anomaly arising from the Berry-curvature-induced anomalous orbital magnetic moment. Additionally, we observe that the localization of Weyl fermions by impurity scattering will lead to resonant dips in both the chiral chemical potential and magnetoconductivity when the Fermi energy approaches the impurity resonance states. Our findings offer a theoretical proposition for modulating nonreciprocal transport in topological semimetals.

Introduction

Weyl semimetals (WSMs) are members of the topological semimetal family,
well-known for hosting low-energy Weyl fermion excitation with well-defined chirality near the Weyl

;

;
Lv et al. 2021

nonclosed Fermi arc surface states in momentum space, acting as sources and sinks of the Berry curvature. WSMs can be classified into two categories: inversion symmetry-broken WSMs and time-reversal symmetry-broken WSMs, with the Weyl nodes separated in momentum and energy space,
respectively. One of the most distinctive features of WSMs is the chiral
anomaly, i.e., a population difference between opposite-chirality Weyl valleys can be induced by nonorthogonal electric and magnetic
fields
Burkov 2014
;
Son and Spivak 2013
;
Zyuzin 2017
;
Nandy et al. 2017
. The chiral anomaly can give rise to various observable phenomena, such as
the negative magnetoresistance
Xiong et al. 2015
;
Li et al. 2015
;
Deng et al. 2019a
;
Deng et al. 2019b
, quantum oscillations
Potter et al. 2014
;
Moll et al. 2016
and topological pumping
effects
Deng et al. 2022
.

In recent studies, there have been proposals for chiral-anomaly-induced nonlinear transport phenomena in WSMs, such as the nonlinear Hall effect
Li et al. 2021
;
Mandal et al. 2022
;
Zeng et al. 2021
. The nonlinear Hall effect, initially introduced by Sodemann and Fu

years

. Additionally, the nonlinear anomalous Hall effect induced by the Berry curvature dipole has also been realized in
WSMs

. More interestingly, when the nonlinear

The previous theoretical studies were mostly based on the Boltzmann theory,
where the electric and magnetic fields are treated perturbatively and incorporated into transport via the semiclassical equations of

Xiao et al. 2010

;

;

;
Deng et al. 2019a
;

A unified theory across the weak and strong magnetic field limits is still lacking for nonlinear transport. Besides, the Boltzmann equation relies on phenomenological relaxation processes to determine the distribution function, where the constant relaxation time approximation is often employed

;
Son and Spivak 2013
;
Zyuzin 2017
;
Nandy et al. 2017
;

;
Matsyshyn and Sodemann 2019
;

;

;

In this work, we develop a unified theory based on the retarded Green’s
function and explore the nonlinear magnetotransport in disordered
WSMs, where the impurity-resonance states are taken into account. We find that
bilinear magnetoconductivity that is linearly dependent on both electric and magnetic fields can be induced by several mechanisms in disordered WSMs,
including impurity-induced tilting of the Weyl cones, Lorentz-force induced orbital magnetic moment, and the Berry-curvature induced anomalous orbital magnetic moment. We identify three key features in the second-order nonlinear conductivity: (
\mathrm{i}
) The nonlinear anomalous Hall conductivity and orbital-magnetic-moment induced nonlinear Hall conductivity exhibit a linear dependence on lifetime, while other nonlinear conductivities, such as the Drude conductivity, show a quadratic dependence.
(
\mathrm{ii}
) A finite quadratic-in-lifetime nonlinear conductivity requires at least two identical subscripts, whereas linear-in-lifetime nonlinear conductivities require at least two different subscripts. (
\mathrm{iii}
) The chiral anomaly can induce both the linear- and quadratic-in-lifetime nonlinear magnetoconductivity, with the former
(latter) being a constant (vanishing) for
E_{F}\rightarrow 0
and becoming

B/E_{F}^{2}
(
B/E_{F}
) dependent for
|E_{F}|\gg 1
. This contrasts with the

B^{2}/E_{F}^{2}
dependence in the linear case, where
E_{F}
is the Fermi
energy. Additionally, due to the impurity-induced resonance states, resonant dips are observed in both chiral anomaly and nonlinear conductivities with variations in the Fermi energy.

VII

II
Theory based on the retarded Green’s function

We start from a general system subjected to external fields, which
can be described by the Hamiltonian

H=\int d^{3}\bm{r}\Psi^{{\dagger}}(\bm{r})\mathcal{H}(\bm{r})\Psi(\bm{r}),

(1)

where

\bm{r}

governs the energy-band quasiparticles.

local charge
q

\hat{\bm{M}}_{s}
and current
density

to external fields. Here, the
external electric and magnetic fields can be denoted by

and
\bm{B}=\bm{\nabla}\times\bm{A}

\Phi
and
\bm{A}
are the electromagnetic scalar and vector potentials, respectively.

Provided the external fields are homogeneous in real space, we can rewrite

\mathcal{\hat{H}}_{\mathrm{ex}}\left(\bm{r}\right)=-\bm{r}\cdot\hat{\bm{F}}-\hat{\bm{M}}_{s}\cdot\bm{B}
with the generalized force defined
as

;
Deng et al. 2022

(2)

Although the translational symmetry is broken by the external fields,
the plane waves, as eigenstates of the momentum operator, can form a complete

\Psi(\bm{r})=\sum_{\bm{k}}c_{\bm{k}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}
and project Eq.
(

(3)

where
\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k}}=e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\mathcal{\hat{H}}_{0}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}_{\bm{kk}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}V_{n}e^{-i\left(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}\right)\cdot\bm{R}_{n}},

(4)

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\bm{kk}^{\prime}}

\displaystyle=\hat{U}_{\bm{kk}^{\prime}}-\hat{\bm{M}}_{s}\cdot\bm{B}\delta_{\bm{kk}^{\prime}}.

(5)

The driving potential in Eq. (
5
) is given by

\hat{U}_{\bm{kk}^{\prime}}=-i\frac{\partial\delta\left(\bm{k}-\bm{k}^{\prime}\right)}{\partial\bm{k}}\cdot\hat{\bm{F}}_{\bm{k}^{\prime}}

(6)

with
\hat{\bm{F}}_{\bm{k}}=e^{-i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}\hat{\bm{F}}e^{i\bm{k}\cdot\bm{r}}
. The derivation of the current density operator is provided in the next section.

In momentum space, the retarded Green’s function is defined as

IV
Nonequilibrium retarded Green’s function for disordered WSMs

We consider a WSM with a pair of Weyl nodes separated in the
z
axis. Around
the Weyl nodes, the low-energy quasiparticle excitation can be described by

\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}=\lambda k^{2}+\hbar\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\bm{k}\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\chi},

(22)

where
\chi=\pm 1
denotes the chirality,
\bm{\sigma}^{\chi}=\left(\sigma_{x},\sigma_{y},\chi\sigma_{z}\right)
is the spin Pauli matrix and

\lambda
characterizes the particle-hole asymmetry. Without external fields,
the impurity-free retarded Green’s function takes the form

\displaystyle g_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}(\epsilon)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta}\frac{|\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\rangle\langle\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}|}{\epsilon^{+}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\eta}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{+}-\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}\left(1+\eta\frac{\bm{k}}{k}\cdot\bm{\sigma}^{\chi}\right),

(23)

where
\eta=\pm 1
is the helicity of the Weyl fermions and
|\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\rangle=\left(\chi\cos\theta_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi},\sin\theta_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}e^{i\phi_{\bm{k}}}\right)^{T}
is the wavefuntion for the dispersion
\varepsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}=\lambda k^{2}+\eta\hbar\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}k
.
The related angles are defined as
\phi_{\bm{k}}=\tan^{-1}\left(k_{y}/k_{x}\right)
and
\theta_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}=\frac{\theta_{\bm{k}}}{2}+\frac{1-\eta\chi}{4}\pi
with
\theta_{\bm{k}}=\cos^{-1}\left(k_{z}/k\right)
. Upon summing over
\bm{k}
, the last term in the
second line of Eq. (
23
) vanishes, resulting in

g(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\bm{k}}g_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}(\epsilon)=\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}^{2}}{2\pi^{2}\hbar^{3}\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}^{3}}\left(\frac{1}{2}\ln\frac{\tilde{\epsilon}+\Lambda}{\tilde{\epsilon}-\Lambda}-\frac{\Lambda}{\tilde{\epsilon}}\right)

(24)

which is chirality-independent. Here, we define
\tilde{\epsilon}=\epsilon^{+}-\lambda\left(\epsilon/\hbar\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\right)^{2}
and introduce a higher-energy cutoff denoted by
\Lambda

for the dispersion. The unperturbed dispersion is illustrated by the dashed lines in Fig.

The disordered effect is modeled by a set of randomly-distributed
scattering potentials, consisting of a charge potential with strength

V_{0}
and a spin-dependent potential of strength

V_{n}=V_{0}\sigma_{0}+V_{M}\bm{s}_{n}\cdot\bm{\sigma}

is the direction vector of the local magnetic moment. The
Zeeman effect of the magnetic field can be incorporated into the impurity potential by
replacing
V_{M}\bm{s}_{n}\rightarrow V_{M}\bm{s}_{n}-g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\bm{B}

denotes the Lande factor, and
\mu_{\mathrm{B}}

\Sigma_{\bm{k}}(\epsilon)=\Gamma_{+}\left(\epsilon\right)-\Gamma_{-}\left(\epsilon\right)\bm{s}\cdot\bm{\sigma},

(25)

where
\bm{s}
is the mean direction of
g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\bm{B}-V_{M}\bm{s}_{n}
and

\Gamma_{\pm}\left(\epsilon\right)=-\frac{1}{2}\left[\frac{1}{g(\epsilon)-1/V_{+}}\pm\frac{1}{g(\epsilon)-1/V_{-}}\right]

(26)

with
V_{\pm}=n_{\mathrm{i}}V_{0}\pm|g\mu_{\mathrm{B}}\bm{B}-n_{\mathrm{i}}V_{M}\langle\bm{s}_{n}\rangle_{\mathrm{dis}}|
and

n_{\mathrm{i}}
representing the impurity concentration.

As a result, the impurity-perturbed equilibrium retarded Green’s function
is given by

\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\epsilon^{+}-\Gamma_{+}\left(\epsilon\right)-\mathcal{H}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}+\Gamma_{-}\left(\epsilon\right)\bm{s}\cdot\bm{\sigma}}.

(27)

Upon application of driving fields, the nonequilibrium retarded Green’s

14

G_{\bm{k}}^{r}(\epsilon)=\frac{1}{\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{-1}(\epsilon)-\hat{\bm{M}}_{J}\cdot\bm{B}+e\bm{E}\cdot i\tilde{\partial}_{\bm{k}}},

(28)

where
\tilde{\partial}_{\bm{k}}=\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}^{-1}(\epsilon)\partial_{\bm{k}}\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}(\epsilon)
and
\hat{\bm{M}}_{J}=i\tilde{\partial}_{\bm{k}}\times\hat{\bm{J}}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}
is the magnetic moment

Current-induced anomalous orbital magnetic moment

From the nonequilibrium retarded Green’s function, we can obtain the effective

\mathcal{H}_{\mathrm{eff}}\left(\bm{k}\right)=\epsilon_{R}^{+}-\left[G_{\bm{k}}^{r}(\epsilon)\right]^{-1}
,

Around the shifted Weyl nodes, the retarded Green’s function becomes

\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}(\epsilon)=\sum_{\eta}\frac{|\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\rangle\langle\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}|}{\epsilon^{+}-\Gamma_{+}\left(\epsilon\right)-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}

(32)

where
\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}=\eta\hbar\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}k+2\lambda\bm{k}_{\Gamma}^{\chi}\cdot\bm{k}
and the

\bm{k}_{\Gamma}^{\chi}

VI
Chiral anomaly originating from the anomalous orbital magnetic
moment

The chiral anomaly manifests itself through the change in the chemical potential for the Weyl valleys, quantified by the averaging of

\Delta\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}=\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}=\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\cdot\bm{B}-e\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\tau_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}

(40)

over the states on the Fermi surface, i.e.,

\Delta\mu_{\chi}=\frac{\sum_{\bm{k}\eta}\Delta\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\delta\left(E_{F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)}{\sum_{\bm{k}\eta}\delta\left(E_{F}-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)}.

(41)

The orbital magnetic moments can be derived as

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(0)}

\displaystyle=\chi\frac{2\ell_{B}^{2}e\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}}{1+2\ell_{B}^{2}k^{2}}\bm{k},

(42)

\displaystyle\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(E)}

\displaystyle=\chi\eta\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\tau_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}{1+2\ell_{B}^{2}k^{2}}\times\left(\bm{E}\times\frac{\bm{k}}{k}\right),

(43)

where the band group velocity is given by

\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\equiv\frac{\partial\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}{\hbar\partial\bm{k}}=\left(\frac{2\lambda\bm{k}_{\Gamma}^{\chi}}{\hbar\upsilon_{F}}+\eta\frac{\bm{k}}{k}\right)\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}.

(44)

While the Berry curvature is divergent at the Weyl nodes,
\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(0)}
vanishes and
\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(E)}
becomes a constant for
k=0
. Therefore, the nonequilibrium
spectrum is convergent in the whole Brillouin zone.

As specified by Eq. (
42
), the Lorentz force can not induce the
chiral anomaly, because
\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(0)}=-\bm{M}_{J,-\bm{k}\eta}^{(0)}
and
\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(0)}\cdot\bm{B}
vanishes after averaging
over the Fermi surface. In contrast,
\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(E)}
contains a component even in
\bm{k}
and when coupled with

\bm{B}
, will alter the population in the Weyl valleys. In Fig.

2
, we plot the nonequilibrium population and Fermi surfaces by
inspecting the poles of the nonequilibrium retarded Green’s function. As observed
in Figs.
2
(a) and (b), the Fermi surfaces will be drifted by the
electric field (normal orbital magnetic moment induced by the Lorentz force) toward
the same (opposite) direction, while the chiral anomaly will change the size
of the Fermi surfaces, as depicted by Fig.
2
(c). Concretely, the
nonequilibrium Fermi energy in the
\chi
Weyl valley is
E_{F}^{\chi}=E_{F}+\chi\Delta\mu
, where the chiral chemical potential is given by

\Delta\mu=\frac{e^{2}}{\hbar}\frac{\ell_{B}^{2}\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{1+2\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}}\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}

(45)

with
\tau_{\mathrm{F}}=\hbar/\operatorname{Im}\left(\epsilon_{R}^{+}\right)|_{\epsilon\rightarrow E_{F}}
and
k_{F}=E_{F}/\left(\hbar\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
. Equation (
45
) is consistent with previous derivations in both the
ultraquantum
Deng et al. 2019a
;
Deng et al. 2019b
;
Zeng et al. 2023

and semiclassical limits
Li et al. 2021
;
Mandal et al. 2022
.

As shown by Eq. (
45
),
\Delta\mu\propto\bm{E}\cdot\bm{B}/E_{F}^{2}
for
\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\gg 1
. In the
opposite regime, it converges to a constant, as indicated by the dark solid
line in Fig.
3
(a). When taking the Landau level quantization into
account
Deng et al. 2022

\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\rightarrow\sum_{n=1}^{\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}/2}\left[1-2n/\left(\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\right)\right]^{-1/2}
, the chiral chemical potential will exhibit a
periodic-in-
1/B
quantum oscillation, as illustrated by the blue dashed lines in
Fig.
3

g(\epsilon)V_{\pm}=1

V_{+}=V_{-}

3

and

Nonlinear magnetoconductivity in disordered WSMs

In this section, we estimate the conductivity based on the
nonequilibrium retarded Green’s function. By substituting Eqs. (
28
)
and (
29
) into Eq. (
21
), the current density can be expressed as

\bm{J}^{\chi}=-\frac{1}{\pi}\operatorname{Im}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\sum_{\bm{k}\eta}\frac{\langle\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}|\hat{\bm{J}}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}+\Delta\hat{\bm{J}}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}|\psi_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\rangle}{\epsilon^{+}-\Gamma_{+}\left(\epsilon\right)-\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}f(\epsilon)d\epsilon

(46)

with
\Delta\hat{\bm{J}}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}=i\partial_{\bm{k}}\hat{\bm{J}}_{\bm{k}}^{\chi}\cdot\mathcal{G}_{\bm{k}}(\epsilon)\hat{\bm{F}}_{\bm{k}}
.
In the weak impurity scattering regime, i.e.,
n_{\mathrm{i}}\ll 1
, we can
approximate

\operatorname{Im}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{+}-\Gamma_{+}\left(\epsilon\right)-\tilde{\epsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}=-\pi\delta\left(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}-\Delta\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right),

(47)

such that Eq. (
46
) can be expanded in series of
\Delta\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}
as

\bm{J}^{\chi}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\left[\bm{J}_{n}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)+\Delta\bm{J}_{n}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)\right]\frac{\partial^{n}f(\epsilon)}{\partial\epsilon^{n}}d\epsilon

(48)

with

\bm{J}_{n}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)=\sum_{\eta}\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\delta\left(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\left(\Delta\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)^{n}\bm{J}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}

(49)

and

\displaystyle\Delta\bm{J}_{n}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\eta}\int\frac{d^{3}\bm{k}}{\left(2\pi\right)^{3}}\delta\left(\epsilon-\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\left(\Delta\epsilon_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)^{n}

\displaystyle\times\frac{\partial\bm{J}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}}{\hbar\partial\bm{k}}\tau_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\cdot\left(e\bm{E}-\bm{J}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\times\bm{B}\right).

(50)

For brevity, we adopted the abbreviation

\bm{J}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}=-e\left[\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}+\frac{e}{\hbar}\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}+\frac{e\bm{B}}{\hbar}\left(\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\right].

(51)

As we concentrate on the second-order nonlinear magnetotransport, the
current density can be truncated as

J_{\mu}^{\chi}=\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\chi}E_{\nu}+\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi}E_{\nu}E_{\rho}+\mathcal{O}(\bm{E}^{3}),

(52)

where
\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\chi}
and
\sigma_{\mu\nu\gamma}^{\chi}
are the
linear and second-order nonlinear conductivities. Apparently, the current
density resulting from the second-order conductivity is invariant when
reversing the direction of the electric field and thus leads to the
nonreciprocal transport.

VII.1
Linear conductivity

Before delving into the analysis of nonlinear conductivity, we first present the results for linear conductivity. Following a detailed algebraic procedure, we obtain a unified analytical expression for the linear conductivity tensor, which can be partitioned into three distinct components:

\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\chi}=\sigma_{D}\delta_{\mu\nu}+\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{ch}}(B)+\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{tilt}}(B),

(53)

where
\sigma_{D}=n_{e}e^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{F}}/\left(\hbar k_{F}\right)
is
the Drude conductivity and

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{ch}}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{2}}{h}\frac{2\ell_{B}^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{F}}}{1+2\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}}\frac{e^{2}B_{\mu}B_{\nu}}{2\pi\hbar^{2}}\left(1-|\bm{\alpha}|^{2}\right)^{2}

(54)

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{tilt}}(B)

\displaystyle=\frac{\Delta n_{e}e^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{F}}}{\hbar k_{F}}-\frac{2e^{2}\ell_{\mathrm{F}}k_{F}^{2}}{1+2\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}}\frac{\alpha_{\mu}B_{\nu}+\alpha_{\nu}B_{\mu}}{\pi hB}

(55)

are the linear magnetoconductivities from the chiral anomaly and tilting of
the Weyl cones, respectively. The related factors are defined as

\bm{\alpha}=2\lambda\bm{k}_{\Gamma}^{\chi}/\left(\hbar\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\right)
and
\Delta n_{e}=\tilde{n}_{e}-n_{e}\delta_{\mu\nu}
, in which
n_{e}=\left(1/3\pi^{2}\right)k_{F}^{3}

is the carrier density,
\ell_{\mathrm{F}}=\upsilon_{\mathrm{F}}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}
denotes the mean free-path of the Weyl fermions and

\tilde{n}_{e}=\frac{3n_{e}}{2\left(1-|\bm{\alpha}|^{2}\right)^{2}}\left[\left(\frac{2-3\zeta}{|\bm{\alpha}|^{2}}-2\right)\alpha_{\mu}\alpha_{\nu}+\zeta\delta_{\mu\nu}\right]

(56)

with
\zeta=\frac{1-|\bm{\alpha}|^{2}}{|\bm{\alpha}|^{2}}\left(1-\frac{1-|\bm{\alpha}|^{2}}{2|\bm{\alpha}|}\ln\frac{1+|\bm{\alpha}|}{1-|\bm{\alpha}|}\right)
.

The linear magnetoconductivities are displayed in Figs.
3
(d) and
(e). From Eq. (
54
), we see that the linear magnetoconductivity
induced by the chiral anomaly scales with
B^{2}/k_{F}^{2}
when
\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\gg 1
. Conversely, for
\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\ll 1
,
\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu}^{\mathrm{ch}}(B)
becomes independent of
k_{F}
and
linear in
B
. These outcomes align with predictions from both semiclassical and quantum Boltzmann theories
Deng et al. 2019a
;
Deng et al. 2019b
;
Zeng et al. 2023
;
Li et al. 2021
;
Mandal et al. 2022
. Moreover, the Zeeman field induced tilting of the Weyl cones can
contribute a magnetoconductivity
\propto B^{2}k_{F}^{2}
, as demonstrated by
the first term of Eq. (
55
). The combined effect of the tilting and the
Berry curvature results in the second term of Eq. (
55
), which has
been reported to be
k_{F}
-independent
Ma et al. 2019b
;
Lei et al. 2021
. Here, we find this
conclusion holds true only for large Fermi levels. In the limit
k_{F}\rightarrow 0
,
this term vanishes due to the diminishing density of states. All the linear
magnetoconductivities exhibit a linear dependence on the lifetime of the Weyl fermions, leading to significant suppression around the impurity resonance states.

VII.2
Impurity-scattering modulated bilinear magnetoconductivity

For the sake of discussion, we refer to
e\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}/\hbar
and
e\bm{B}\left(\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)/\hbar
in Eq.
(
51
) as the anomalous velocity and pumping velocity, respectively.
Here, the nonvanishing second-order conductivity can can arise from several mechanisms, which to linear order in
B
can be expressed as

\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi}=\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{A}}+\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{D}}+\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{L}}+\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{P}}+\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{M}}.

(57)

To elaborate, the term
\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{A}}\sim\left(\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\left(\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)_{\mu}
represents the nonlinear anomalous Hall conductivity originating from
\bm{J}_{1}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)
in Eq. (
48
). The nonlinear
Drude conductivity
\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{D}}
comprises two
contributions:
\left(\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)^{2}\upsilon_{\mu,\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}
arising from
\bm{J}_{2}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)
and

\left(\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\bm{E}\cdot\partial_{\bm{k}}\upsilon_{\mu,\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}
arising from
\Delta\bm{J}_{1}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)
. Both the nonlinear anomalous Hall and Drude conductivities are not explicit functions of magnetic field.
The remaining terms represent the nonlinear magnetoconductivities explicitly
involving both electric and magnetic fields. Specifically,

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\left[\bm{E}\cdot\partial_{\bm{k}}\left(\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\cdot\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)B_{\mu}\right]

(58)

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle\sim\left(\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\left(\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\times\bm{B}\right)\cdot\partial_{\bm{k}}\left(\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)_{\mu}

(59)

arise from the pumping velocity and the combined effect of the anomalous
velocity and Lorentz force in
\Delta\bm{J}_{1}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)
, respectively. Additionally,
\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{M}}=\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{NM}}+\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{AM}}
, where
\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{NM}}\sim\left(\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(0)}\cdot\bm{B}\right)\left(\bm{E}\cdot\bm{\upsilon}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)\left(\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)_{\mu}
is attributable to the joint effect of the normal orbital magnetic moment
and anomalous velocity in
\bm{J}_{2}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)
, and
\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{AM}}
arises from the anomalous
orbital magnetic moment consisting of a term
\sim\left(\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(E)}\cdot\bm{B}\right)\left(\bm{E}\times\bm{\Omega}_{\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}\right)_{\mu}
from
\bm{J}_{1}^{\chi}
and a term
\sim

\left(\bm{M}_{J,\bm{k}\eta}^{(E)}\cdot\bm{B}\right)\bm{E}\cdot\partial_{\bm{k}}\upsilon_{\mu,\bm{k}\eta}^{\chi}
from
\Delta\bm{J}_{1}^{\chi}\left(\epsilon\right)
. Consequently, the chiral anomaly contributes to the nonlinear transport by interacting either with the anomalous velocity or the derivative of the band
group velocity.

In particular, upon integrating the above expressions over
\bm{k}
and

\epsilon
, the nonlinear anomalous Hall conductivity can be expressed as

\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{A}}=\chi\sigma_{2}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\rho}/2
and the nonlinear Drude conductivity, to leading order in

\bm{\alpha}
, takes the form

\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{D}}=-\sigma_{2}\frac{E_{F}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{\hbar}\left(\alpha_{\mu}\delta_{\nu\rho}+\alpha_{\nu}\delta_{\rho\mu}+3\alpha_{\rho}\delta_{\mu\nu}\right),

(60)

where
\sigma_{2}=\frac{e^{3}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{6\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}|_{n_{\mathrm{i}}\rightarrow 0}
is set as the unit of the second-order
conductivity. Similarly, the nonlinear magnetoconductivities originating from
the pumping velocity and Lorentz force can be summarized as

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{P}}

\displaystyle=-\chi\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{E_{F}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{\hbar}\frac{2B_{\mu}\delta_{\nu\rho}}{B}

(61)

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{L}}

\displaystyle=-\chi\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{E_{F}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{\hbar}\frac{B_{\mu}\delta_{\nu\rho}-B_{\nu}\delta_{\mu\rho}}{B}

(62)

with
\sigma_{2}^{\prime}=\sigma_{2}/\left(1+2\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\right)
. The nonlinear magnetoconductivities contributed by the normal and anomalous
orbital magnetic moments can be expressed as

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{NM}}

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{\alpha_{\rho}B_{\gamma}}{B}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma},

(63)

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{AM}}

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{\alpha_{\gamma}B_{\rho}-\alpha_{\rho}B_{\gamma}}{2B}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma}

\displaystyle+\chi\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{2E_{F}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{\hbar}\frac{B_{\mu}\delta_{\nu\rho}+B_{\nu}\delta_{\rho\mu}+B_{\rho}\delta_{\mu\nu}}{5B}.

(64)

Combining Eqs. (
61
)-(
64
), the total
nonlinear magnetoconductivity is given by

\displaystyle\Delta\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi}

\displaystyle=-\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{\alpha_{\rho}B_{\gamma}+\alpha_{\gamma}B_{\rho}}{2B}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma}

\displaystyle-\chi\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{E_{F}\tau_{\mathrm{F}}}{\hbar}\frac{13B_{\mu}\delta_{\nu\rho}-7B_{\nu}\delta_{\mu\rho}-2B_{\rho}\delta_{\mu\nu}}{5B}.

(65)

In the context of a single Weyl cone, we observe three primary characteristics in the second-order nonlinear conductivity. (
\mathrm{i}
) The nonlinear anomalous Hall conductivity and the nonlinear Hall conductivity induced by the orbital magnetic moment exhibit a linear scaling with the Fermi lifetime, while the nonlinear Drude conductivity and other magnetoconductivities are quadratic in
\tau_{\mathrm{F}}
. (
\mathrm{ii}
) Non-vanishing quadratic-in-
\tau_{\mathrm{F}}
nonlinear magnetoconductivity necessitates at least two identical subscripts, while linear-in-
\tau_{\mathrm{F}}
magnetoconductivities require at least two different subscripts. (
\mathrm{iii}
) The chiral anomaly due
to the Berry-curvature induced anomalous magnetic moment contributes both
the linear- and quadratic-in-
\tau_{\mathrm{F}}
nonlinear
magnetoconductivity, as demonstrated by Eq. (
64

former (latter), the contribution is a constant (vanishing) as
E_{F}\rightarrow 0
, evolving into a

(
B/E_{F}
) dependency for
\ell_{B}^{2}k_{F}^{2}\gg 1
. This is in contrast to the
B^{2}/E_{F}^{2}
dependence observed in the linear case. These features align with the results obtained from the semiclassical Boltzmann theory
Li et al. 2021
;
Mandal et al. 2022
.

All components of the nonlinear conductivities that are independent of
\bm{\alpha}

\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}(\bm{B})=\sum_{\chi}\left(\sigma_{\mu\nu\rho}^{\chi,\mathrm{D}}-\sigma_{2}^{\prime}\frac{\alpha_{\rho}B_{\gamma}+\alpha_{\gamma}B_{\rho}}{2B}\varepsilon_{\mu\nu\gamma}\right).

(66)

VIII
Summary

In summary, we have developed a nonlinear transport theory based on nonequilibrium retarded Green’s functions, allowing us to investigate the nonlinear magnetotransport in disordered WSMs. Our study revealed that, for a single Weyl valley, the bilinear magnetoconductivity can be attributed to various mechanisms, including the normal orbital magnetic moment induced by the Lorentz force, the anomalous orbital magnetic moment induced by Berry curvature, and the impurity-induced tilting of the Weyl cones. Due to impurity scattering, the chiral anomaly experiences significant suppression around impurity-induced resonance states, leading to resonant dips in the magnetoconductivity when the Fermi energy approaches these states. Our results are consistent with the predictions from both the semiclassical and quantum Boltzmann theories.
