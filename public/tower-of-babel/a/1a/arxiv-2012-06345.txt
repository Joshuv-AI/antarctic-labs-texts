Y.T. Albert Law


Abstract




A Compendium of Sphere Path Integrals

Abstract:
We study the manifestly covariant and local 1-loop path integrals on
S^{d+1}
for general massive, shift-symmetric and (partially) massless totally symmetric tensor fields of arbitrary spin
s\geq 0
in any dimensions
d\geq 2
. After reviewing the cases of massless fields with spin
s=1,2
, we provide a detailed derivation for path integrals of massless fields of arbitrary integer spins
s\geq 1
. Following the standard procedure of Wick-rotating the negative conformal modes, we find a higher spin analog of Polchinski’s phase for any integer spin
s\geq 2
. The derivations for low-spin (
s=0,1,2
) massive, shift-symmetric and partially massless fields are also carried out explicitly. Finally, we provide general prescriptions for general massive and shift-symmetric fields of arbitrary integer spins and partially massless fields of arbitrary integer spins and depths.

2
Review of massless vectors

We start with a pedagogical review of the case of massless vectors. The object of interest is the 1-loop approximation to the full Euclidean path integral

\displaystyle Z_{\text{PI}}=\frac{1}{\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})}\int\mathcal{D}A^{a}\mathcal{D}\Phi\,e^{-S_{E}[A^{a},\Phi]}

(2.1)

for a theory that involves a collection of massless vector (for example U(1) or Yang-Mills) gauge fields interacting with some matter fields, denoted as
A^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.95248pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.10892pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\mu}}}
and collectively as
\Phi
respectively, living on
S^{d+1}
.

U(1)
with a complex scalar

The simplest example involves a single U(1) gauge field
A_{\mu}
interacting with a complex scalar
\phi
(studied in
[
5
]
):

\displaystyle S_{E}[A,\phi]=\int_{S^{d+1}}\bigg[\frac{1}{4\mathrm{g}^{2}}F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu}+D_{\mu}\phi(D^{\mu}\phi)^{*}+m^{2}\phi\phi^{*}\bigg],

(2.2)

where

\displaystyle F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu},\qquad D_{\mu}\phi\equiv(\partial_{\mu}-iA_{\mu})\phi

(2.3)

are the field strength and the covariant derivative of the scalar. This action is invariant under the local U(1) gauge transformations

\displaystyle\phi(x)\to e^{i\alpha(x)}\phi(x),\quad A_{\mu}(x)\to A_{\mu}(x)+\partial_{\mu}\alpha(x).

(2.4)

The normalization adopted here is to emphasize the presence of the coupling constant
\mathrm{g}
. In this convention
\mathrm{g}
does not show up in the gauge transformation.

Yang-Mills

Another example is Yang-Mills (YM) theory with a Lie algebra

\displaystyle[L^{a},L^{b}]=f^{abc}L^{c}

(2.5)

generated by some standard basis of anti-hermitian matrices and
f^{abc}
is real and totally antisymmetric. The YM action is

\displaystyle S_{E}[A,\phi]=\frac{1}{4\mathrm{g}^{2}}\int_{S^{d+1}}\text{Tr}F^{2}=\frac{1}{4\mathrm{g}^{2}}\int_{S^{d+1}}F_{\mu\nu}^{a}F^{a,\mu\nu},

(2.6)

where the curvature is
F_{\mu\nu}\equiv\partial_{\mu}A_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A_{\mu}+[A_{\mu},A_{\nu}]
with
A_{\mu}=A_{\mu}^{a}L^{a}
. Here the overall normalization for the trace (or Killing form) is
defined
such that the generators
L^{a}
are unit normalized:

\displaystyle\text{Tr}(L^{a}L^{b})\equiv\delta^{ab}.

(2.7)

For SU(2) YM,
L^{a}=-\frac{i\sigma^{a}}{2}
satisfying
[L^{a},L^{b}]=\epsilon^{abc}L^{c}
, and the trace (
2.7
) would be
\text{Tr}\equiv-2\text{tr}
with tr being the matrix trace. The YM action is invariant under the non-linear gauge transformations
\alpha=\alpha^{a}L^{a}

\displaystyle A_{\mu}\to A_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\alpha+[A_{\mu},\alpha].

(2.8)

In both the
U(1)
and YM examples, the corresponding path integral in (
2.1
) clearly overcounts gauge equivalent configurations. A factor
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})
is thus inserted in (
2.1
) to quotient out configurations connected by gauge transformations. This factor is formally the volume of the space of gauge transformations
\mathcal{G}
(the measure with respect to which the volume is defined will be discussed in later subsection) and is theory dependent. For the U(1) example,
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})
is simply a path integral over a single local scalar field

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})_{\text{U(1)}}=\int\mathcal{D}\alpha,

(2.9)

while for SU(2) YM it would be a path integral over
3
local scalar fields

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})_{\text{SU(2)}}=\int\mathcal{D}\alpha_{1}\mathcal{D}\alpha_{2}\mathcal{D}\alpha_{3}.

(2.10)

More generally,
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})
is an integral over
N=\text{dim}\,G
local scalar fields for a gauge group
G
.

1-loop approximation

Now, suppose the equation of motion admits the trivial solution
A^{{{a}\mathchoice{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.86232pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.95248pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[2.10892pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}}}_{{\mathchoice{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\displaystyle$}}{\makebox[4.33765pt][c]{$\textstyle$}}{\makebox[2.59009pt][c]{$\scriptstyle$}}{\makebox[1.85005pt][c]{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}{\mu}}}=0=\Phi
, around which we perform a saddle point approximation for (
2.1
). Then at the quadratic (1 loop) level the vector and matter fields decouple:

\displaystyle Z^{\text{1-loop}}_{\text{PI}}=Z^{\delta A}_{\text{PI}}Z^{\delta\Phi}_{\text{PI}}.

(2.11)

In the following, we focus on the vector part of the 1-loop path integral (with
A^{a}
understood as the fluctuations around the background)

\displaystyle Z^{\delta A}_{\text{PI}}=\frac{1}{\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})}\int\prod_{a=1}^{\text{dim}\,G}\mathcal{D}A^{a}\,e^{-\sum_{a=1}^{\text{dim}\,G}S_{E}[A^{a}]}.

(2.12)

where
S_{E}[A^{a}]
is simply a Maxwell action

\displaystyle S_{E}[A^{a}]=\frac{1}{4\mathrm{g}^{2}}\int_{S^{d+1}}F^{a}_{\mu\nu}F^{a,\mu\nu},\quad F_{\mu\nu}=\partial_{\mu}A^{a}_{\nu}-\partial_{\nu}A^{a}_{\mu}\,.

(2.13)

A careful analysis of the Euclidean path integral for the U(1) theory on arbitrary manifolds has been presented in
[
13
]
, where the authors point out the importance of taking care of zero modes, large gauge transformations and non-trivial bundles for consistency with locality and unitarity.

In the following we will express
Z^{0}_{\text{PI}}
in terms of functional determinants and highlight the relevant subtleties in our case of
S^{d+1}
along the way. Before doing so we would like to make a last comment. Even though the problem is largely reduced to that of a free Maxwell theory on
S^{d+1}
, the knowledge of the parent theory (
2.1
) to which we performed the 1-loop approximation is required for the full determination of the (
2.12
). In particular, the group volume factor
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})
will eventually lead to a residual group volume factor in the final result, whose value depends on the specific gauge group (e.g.
U(1)
or
SU(N)
) and couplings of the gauge field to the matter fields.

2.1
Transverse vector determinant and Jacobian

Geometric approach and change of variables

Since the path integrations over
A^{a}
in (
2.12
) are decoupled, we can focus on one of the factors, and we will suppress the index
a
. Traditional ways to proceed include Faddeev-Popov or BRST gauge fixing (as done in
[
13
]
for example). Here instead we take the “geometric approach”
[
15
,
16
,
17
]
, which manifests its advantages when we deal with massless higher spin fields later. In this approach one changes the field variables by decomposing

\displaystyle A_{\mu}=A^{T}_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\chi

(2.14)

where
A^{T}_{\mu}
is the transverse or on-shell part of
A_{\mu}
satisfying
\nabla^{\mu}A^{T}_{\mu}=0
, and
\chi
is the longitudinal or pure gauge part of
A_{\mu}
. Since
S^{d+1}
is compact, the scalar Laplacian has a normalizable constant
(0,0)
mode, which must be excluded from the path integration for the change of variables (
2.14
) to be unique

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}A=J\,\mathcal{D}A^{T}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi

(2.15)

where prime denotes the exclusion of the
(0,0)
mode. We will find the Jacobian
J
for the change of variables (
2.14
) below.

Action for
A^{T}_{\mu}

Because the gauge invariance of the action,
\chi
simply drops out upon substituting (
2.14
)

\displaystyle S_{E}[A^{T},\chi]=\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}\int_{S^{d+1}}[A^{T}_{\mu}(-\nabla_{(1)}^{2}+d)A_{T}^{\mu}]

(2.16)

where
-\nabla_{(1)}^{2}
is the trasnverse Laplacian on
S^{d+1}
. Now we expand
A^{T}_{\mu}
in terms of spin-1 transverse spherical harmonics (see appendix
B
for their basic properties):

\displaystyle A^{T}_{\mu}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n,1}f_{n,\mu}

(2.17)

and the integration measure in our convention is

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}A^{T}=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{dc_{n,1}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{g}}.

(2.18)

Performing the path integration over these modes we have

\displaystyle\int\mathcal{D}Ae^{-S_{E}[A]}=J\,\det(-\nabla_{(1)}^2 +d)^{-1/2}\int\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi

(2.19)

Jacobian

We find the Jacobian
J
by requiring consistency with the normalization condition

\displaystyle 1=\int\mathcal{D}A\,e^{-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}(A,A)}=\int J\,\mathcal{D}A^{T}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi\,e^{-\frac{1}{2\mathrm{g}^{2}}(A^{T}+\nabla\chi,A^{T}+\nabla\chi)}.

(2.20)

Since
A^{T}
is transverse, we have

\displaystyle(A^{T}+\nabla\chi,A^{T}+\nabla\chi)=(A^{T},A^{T})+(\nabla\chi,\nabla\chi).

(2.21)

We can then path integrate
A^{T}
trivially. We expand
\chi
in terms of scalar spherical harmonics:

\displaystyle\chi=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n,0}f_{n}

(2.22)

with path integration measure

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi=\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{dc_{n,0}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{g}}.

(2.23)

Plugging this into (
2.20
) results in

\displaystyle J=\det\nolimits^{\prime}(-\nabla_{(0)}^{2})^{1/2},

(2.24)

where the prime denotes the omission of the constant
(0,0)
mode.

2.2
Residual group volume

Let us go back to the full 1-loop path integral (
2.12
). So far we have

\displaystyle Z^{\delta A}_{\text{PI}}=\frac{\int\prod_{a=1}^{\text{dim}G}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi^{a}}{\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})}\left(\frac{\det^{\prime}(-\nabla_{(0)}^{2})^{1/2}}{\det(-\nabla_{(1)}^2 +d)^{1/2}}\right)^{\text{dim}G}

(2.25)

where we have restored the color index
a
. Now we focus on the factor

\displaystyle\frac{\int\prod_{a=1}^{\text{dim}G}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi^{a}}{\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})}.

(2.26)

As explained above, the factor
\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})
is theory dependent and is formally an integral over
N={\text{dim}G}

local
scalar fields

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})=\int\prod_{n=1}^{\text{dim}G}\mathcal{D}\alpha_{n}.

(2.27)

In particular, the integral includes integrations over constant scalar modes. As explained in
[
13
]
, the inclusion of zero modes is crucial for consistency with locality and unitarity. Thus, this factor does not cancel completely with the integrations over
\chi
, leaving a factor

\displaystyle\frac{\int\prod_{a=1}^{\text{dim}G}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi^{a}}{\text{Vol}(\mathcal{G})}=\frac{1}{\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}},\quad\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}\equiv\int\prod_{a=1}^{\text{dim}\,G}\frac{d\alpha^{a}_{0}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\mathrm{g}}.

(2.28)

where
\alpha^{a}_{0}
is the expansion coefficient of the
(0,0)
mode of
\alpha^{a}
(
a
is the color index)

\displaystyle\alpha^{a}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\alpha^{a}_{n}f_{n}.

(2.29)

These constant scalar modes correspond to the gauge transformations that leave the background
A^{\mu}=0
invariant, or equivalently whose linear part is trivial. If the original full theory contains matter fields such as (
2.2
), these act non-trivially on the latter.
G
is therefore the group of
global
symmetries of the theory and
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}
is the volume of
G
. Note that the precise value of
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}
depends on the metric on
G
. We have been using a specific choice of metric

\displaystyle ds_{\text{PI}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{g}^{2}}\int_{S^{d+1}}\text{Tr}(\delta\alpha\delta\alpha)

(2.30)

induced by our convention for the path integral measure. Note that had we normalized the generators
L^{a}
in a different way:
L^{a}\to\lambda L^{a}
(or equivalently choosing a different overall normalization for the trace in the action (
2.6
):
\text{Tr}\to\lambda^{2}\text{Tr}
), the path integral describes the same physics if we rescale
\mathrm{g}\to\lambda\mathrm{g}
. In particular, the metric (
2.30
) remains the same. We want to relate the volume
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}
measured in this metric to a “canonical volume”
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}
, defined as follows. A general group element in
G
takes the form

\displaystyle e^{\theta\cdot\hat{L}}=e^{\theta^{a}\hat{L}^{a}}

(2.31)

where
\hat{L}^{a}
are unit-normalized. We define
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}
to be the volume of the space spanned by
\theta
. In our convention,
L^{a}
are unit-normalized, and therefore the relation between the metric (
2.30
) (restricted to the subspace of trivial gauge transformations) and the canonical metric is simply

\displaystyle ds_{\text{PI}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{g}^{2}}\sum_{a}(d\alpha_{0}^{a})^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{g}^{2}}\sum_{a}\left(\frac{d\theta^{a}}{f_{0}}\right)^{2}=\frac{\text{Vol}(S^{d+1})}{2\pi\mathrm{g}^{2}}ds_{\text{can}}^{2},\quad ds_{\text{can}}^{2}\equiv d\theta\cdot d\theta.

(2.32)

Thus we can express the group volume as

\displaystyle\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}=\left(\frac{\text{Vol}(S^{d+1})}{2\pi\mathrm{g}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{\text{dim}(G)}{2}}\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}=\bigg(\frac{\text{Vol}(S^{d-1})}{d\mathrm{g}^{2}}\bigg)^{\frac{\text{dim}(G)}{2}}\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}},

(2.33)

where we have used
\text{Vol}(S^{d+1})=\frac{2\pi}{d}\text{Vol}(S^{d-1})
in the last step. The canonical volume
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}
so defined is evidently independent of the coupling. To summarize, the full 1-loop path integral is

\displaystyle Z^{\delta A}_{\text{PI}}=

\displaystyle Z_{\text{G}}Z_{\text{Char}}

\displaystyle Z_{\text{G}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\gamma^{\text{dim}G}}{\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}},\quad\gamma=\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\sqrt{(d-2)\text{Vol}(S^{d-1})}}

\displaystyle Z_{\text{Char}}=

\displaystyle\left(d(d-2)\right)^{\frac{1}{2}{\text{dim}G}}\left(\frac{\det^{\prime}(-\nabla_{(0)}^{2})}{\det(-\nabla_{(1)}^2 +d)}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}{\text{dim}G}}

(2.34)

The notation
Z_{\text{Char}}
emphasizes that this part can be re-written in terms of the
SO(1,d+1)
characters as explained in
[
11
]
. In retrospect, the coupling dependence of the result is precisely encoded in the group volume factor
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{PI}}
. In the
G=U(1)
example,
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}=\text{Vol}(U(1))_{c}=2\pi
, and the full 1 loop vector path integral is therefore

\displaystyle Z^{U(1)}_{\text{PI}}=\frac{\mathrm{g}}{\sqrt{2\pi\text{Vol}(S^{d+1})}}\frac{\det^{\prime}(-\nabla_{(0)}^{2})^{1/2}}{\det(-\nabla_{(1)}^2 +d)^{1/2}},

(2.35)

which reproduces eq.(2.6) in
[
18
]
. For
G=SU(2)
,
\text{dim}\,G=3
and
\text{Vol}(G)_{\text{can}}=16\pi^{2}
, and thus

\displaystyle Z^{SU(N)}_{\text{PI}}=\frac{1}{16\pi^{2}}\bigg(\frac{2\pi\mathrm{g}^{2}}{\text{Vol}(S^{d+1})}\bigg)^{\frac{3}{2}}\left(\frac{\det^{\prime}(-\nabla_{(0)}^{2})}{\det(-\nabla_{(1)}^2 +d)}\right)^{3/2}.

(2.36)

As a concrete example for an exact evaluation of such a formal expression, with the recipe in
[
11
]
one can find for the 1-loop path integral for
SU(4)
Yang-Mills on
S^{5}

\displaystyle\log Z_{\rm PI}^{SU(4),\,S^{5}}=15\left(\frac{9\pi}{8}\frac{\ell^{5}}{\epsilon^{5}}-\frac{5\pi}{8}\frac{\ell^{3}}{\epsilon^{3}}-\frac{7\pi}{16}\frac{\ell}{\epsilon}\right)+\log\frac{\left(\mathrm{g}/\sqrt{\ell}\right)^{15}}{{\frac{1}{6}(2\pi)^{9}}}+15\left(\,\frac{5\,\zeta(3)}{16\,\pi^{2}}+\frac{3\,\zeta(5)}{16\,\pi^{4}}\,\right)

(2.37)

where we have restored the sphere radius
\ell
and
\epsilon
is the UV regulator in heat kernel regularization.

For the later discussions on spin 2 and massless higher spin fields, and to make connection with the work in

Local gauge algebra

Recall that the original Yang-Mills action (
2.6

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}A_{\mu}=\delta^{(0)}_{\alpha}A_{\mu}+\delta^{(1)}_{\alpha}A_{\mu}

\displaystyle\delta^{(0)}_{\alpha}A_{\mu}=\partial_{\mu}\alpha,\qquad\delta^{(1)}_{\alpha}A_{\mu}=[A_{\mu},\alpha].

(2.38)

Here the superscript
(n)
denotes the power in fields. This generates an algebra

\displaystyle\delta_{\alpha}\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}}A_{\mu}-\delta_{\alpha^{\prime}}\delta_{\alpha}A_{\mu}=\delta_{[[\alpha,\alpha^{\prime}]]}A_{\mu}

(2.39)

where we have defined a bracket
[[\cdot,\cdot]]
on the space of gauge parameters, which in our convention is equal to the negative of the matrix commutator
1
1

1

One should keep in mind that
[[\cdot,\cdot]]
is defined using the gauge transformations of
A_{\mu}
, whose precise form depends on the normalization conventions, while the commutator on the right hand side is the matrix commutator
[A,B]=AB-BA

4.2
Fronsdal action on
S^{d+1}

The 1-loop partition function for a free bosonic spin-

\displaystyle Z^{(s)}_{\text{PI}}=\frac{1}{\text{Vol($\mathcal{G}_{s}$)}}\int\mathcal{D}\phi_{(s)}\,e^{-S[\phi_{(s)}]}

(4.17)

where the quadratic Fronsdal action
[
36
]
in the operator language is given by

\displaystyle S[\phi_{(s)}]=\frac{s!}{2\mathrm{g}_{s}^{2}}\int_{S^{d+1}}\phi_{s}(\partial_{u})\Big(1-\frac{1}{4}u^{2}\partial_{u}^{2}\Big)\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(\nabla,u,\partial_{u})\phi_{s}(u)

(4.18)

with
\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(\nabla,u,\partial_{u})
is the Fronsdal operator

\displaystyle\hat{\mathcal{F}}_{s}(\nabla,u,\partial_{u})=

\displaystyle-\nabla^{2}+M_{s}^{2}-u^{2}\partial_{u}^{2}+u\cdot\nabla\hat{D}(\nabla,u,\partial_{u})

(4.19)

\displaystyle\hat{D}(\nabla,u,\partial_{u})=

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\partial_{u}-\frac{1}{2}(u\cdot\nabla)(\partial_{u}^{2}),

(4.20)

where

\displaystyle M_{s}^{2}=s-(s-2)(s+d-2)

(4.21)

and
\hat{D}
is the de Donder operator. An
s

is inserted as an overall factor. Canonical normalization corresponds to setting
\mathrm{g}_{s}=1
. We will choose a particular value for
\mathrm{g}_{s}
when we discuss the issue of group volume. (

) is invariant under the gauge transformations

\displaystyle\phi_{s}(u)\mapsto\phi_{s}(u)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}u\cdot\nabla\Lambda_{s-1}(u).

(4.22)

In this off-shell formalism
\phi_{s}(u)
satisfies a double-tracelessness condition (trivial for

)

\displaystyle(\partial_{u}^{2})^{2}\phi_{s}(u)=0,

(4.23)

which implies that the gauge parameter
\Lambda_{(s-1)}
must be traceless (imposed even for

)

\displaystyle\partial_{u}^{2}\Lambda_{s-1}(u)=0.

(4.24)

The division by the gauge group volume

)
in (

Change of variables

To proceed, we change field variables

\displaystyle\phi_{s}(u)=\phi^{\text{TT}}_{s}(u)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{s}}u\cdot\nabla\xi_{s-1}(u)+\frac{1}{\sqrt{2s(s-1)(d+2s-3)}}u^{2}\chi_{s-2}(u).

Here
\phi_{(s)}^{\text{TT}}
is the transverse traceless piece of
\phi_{(s)}
for which

\displaystyle\nabla\cdot\partial_{u}\phi^{\text{TT}}_{s}(u)=

\displaystyle 0=\partial_{u}^{2}\phi^{\text{TT}}_{s}(u).

(4.26)

Next,
\xi_{(s-1)}
is the symmetric traceless spin-(
s-1
) gauge parameters which are required to be orthogonal to all spin-(
s-1
) Killing tensors
\epsilon_{(s-1)}^{KT}
(which generate trivial gauge transformations) so that it is uniquely fixed:

\displaystyle\partial_{u}^{2}\xi_{s-1}(u)=

\displaystyle 0,\quad(\xi_{(s-1)},\epsilon_{(s-1)}^{KT})=0

(4.27)

Finally,
\chi_{(s-2)}
is the spin-(
s-2
) piece which carries all the trace information of
\phi_{(s)}
. The double-tracelessness condition (

) implies that

is traceless:

\displaystyle\partial_{u}^{2}\chi_{s-2}(u)=

\displaystyle 0.

(4.28)

Note that if

is a conformal Killing tensor (CKT) satisfying

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{s}(\nabla,u,\partial_{u})\epsilon^{CKT}_{s-1}(u)\equiv u\cdot\nabla\epsilon^{CKT}_{s-1}(u)-\frac{u^{2}}{d+2s-3}(\nabla\cdot\partial_{u})\epsilon^{CKT}_{s-1}(u)=0,

(4.29)

then any new set of variables related by the transformation

\displaystyle\xi_{s-1}(u)

\displaystyle\to\xi_{s-1}(u)+\epsilon^{CKT}_{s-1}(u)

(4.30)

\displaystyle\chi_{s-2}(u)

\displaystyle\to\chi_{s-2}(u)-\frac{u^{2}}{d+2s-3}(\nabla\cdot\partial_{u})\epsilon^{CKT}_{s-1}(u)

will result in the same
\phi_{(s)}
. To uniquely fix
\chi_{(s-2)}
, we thus impose

\displaystyle(\chi_{(s-2)},(\nabla\cdot\epsilon^{CKT})_{(s-2)})=0

(4.32)

for all the spin-(
s-1
) CKTs
\epsilon^{CKT}_{(s-1)}
. The path integral measure then becomes

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}\phi_{s}=J_{(s)}\mathcal{D}\phi^{\text{TT}}_{(s)}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\xi_{(s-1)}\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\chi_{(s-2)}

where the Jacobian

will be found below. The primes indicate that we exclude the

HS symmetries typically form infinite dimensional groups. Therefore there is an issue of making sense of (

), which we are not going to attempt in this paper.

HS invariant bilinear form

Instead, we are going to do a more modest task. As in the warm-up examples, the volume
\text{Vol($G$)}_{\text{PI}}
is defined with a particular metric, namely

\displaystyle ds_{\text{PI}}^{2}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sum_{s}\frac{1}{\mathrm{g}_{s}^{2}}d\alpha_{s-1,s-1}^{2}.

(4.74)

Again we want to express this in terms of a canonical metric with respect to which we define a canonical volume
\text{Vol($G$)}_{\text{can}}
. There are however complications compared to the massless spin-1 and spin-2 cases:

1.

As opposed to the case for Yang-Mills or Einstein gravity, we do not know the full nonlinear actions for Vasiliev theories that give rise to the interacting equations of motion and the full nonlinear gauge transformations in the metric-like formalism. This implies that we do not know the full local HS gauge algebra. Fortunately, the global part of the algebra does not require this knowledge, but only the lowest order ones, which only requires the information of the cubic couplings.

2.

Another complication is that since HS symmetries mix different spins, the HS invariant bilinear form depends on the relative normalizations of fields withe different spins in the action. Once this is fixed, the bilinear form is uniquely determined up to an overall normalization.

All of these have been worked out in the case of a negative cosmological constant
[
19
]
. To go to the case of a positive cosmological constant is a simple matter of analytic continuation. In appendix
C
, we translate the relevant results from
[
19
]
to the case of

\displaystyle\mathrm{g}_{s}^{2}=s!,

(4.75)

the HS invariant bilinear form is determined to be

\displaystyle\bra{\bar{\alpha}_1}\ket{\bar{\alpha}_2}_{\text{can}}=\frac{8\pi G_{N}}{\text{Vol}(S^{d-1})}\sum_{s}(d+2s-2)(d+2s-4)\bra{\bar{\alpha}_{1,(s-1)}}\ket{\bar{\alpha}_{2,(s-1)}}_{\text{PI}}

(4.76)

where the overall normalization is again fixed by requiring the canonical spin-2 generators to be unit-normalized with respect to (

(4.77)

\displaystyle\begin{split}Z^{\text{TT}}_{h}=&\det(-\nabla_{(2)}^2+m^2+2)^{-1/2}\\
\frac{Z^{\text{T}}_{\xi}}{Y^{\text{T}}_{\xi}}=&\int\mathcal{D}^{\prime}\xi^{T}\,e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2}(\xi^{T},\xi^{T})}\\
\frac{Z^{+}_{\sigma^{\prime}}Z^{+}_{\tilde{h}}}{Y^{+}_{\sigma}}=&\int\mathcal{D}^{+}\sigma^{\prime}\,e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})}\int\mathcal{D}^{+}\tilde{h}\,e^{-\frac{d(m^{2}-(d-1))}{2}(\tilde{h},\tilde{h})}\\
\frac{Z^{0}_{\sigma^{\prime}}}{Y^{0}_{\sigma}}=&\int\mathcal{D}^{0}\sigma^{\prime}\,e^{-\frac{dm^{2}}{2}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})}\\
Z^{-}_{\tilde{h}}=&\int\mathcal{D}^{-}\tilde{h}\,e^{-\frac{d(m^{2}-(d-1))}{2}(\tilde{h},\tilde{h})}.\end{split}

(5.16)

Observe that all factors but

can be combined in the following way:

\displaystyle\bigg(\frac{Z^{\text{T}}_{\xi}}{Y^{\text{T}}_{\xi}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{Z^{+}_{\sigma^{\prime}}Z^{+}_{\tilde{h}}}{Y^{+}_{\sigma}}\bigg)\bigg(\frac{Z^{0}_{\sigma^{\prime}}}{Y^{\text{CKV}}_{\xi}}\bigg)Z^{-}_{\tilde{h}}=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\mathcal{D}\xi\,e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2}(\xi,\xi)}\int\mathcal{D}\tilde{h}\,e^{-\frac{d(m^{2}-(d-1))}{2}(\tilde{h},\tilde{h})}}{\int\mathcal{D}^{0}\sigma^{\prime}\,e^{-\frac{(m^{2}-(d-1))}{2}(\sigma^{\prime},\sigma^{\prime})}\int\mathcal{D}^{0}\xi^{T}\,e^{-\frac{m^{2}}{2}(\xi^{T},\xi^{T})}}.

(5.17)

In the numerator, the path integrations are over local unconstrained fields and thus can be absorbed into bare couplings. In the denominator
\mathcal{D}^{0}\xi^{T}
denotes integration over the modes
f_{1,\mu}
. The integrals in the denominator can be easily evaluated. To conclude, we have

\displaystyle Z^{(s=2,m^{2})}_{\text{PI}}=

\displaystyle\det(-\nabla_{(2)}^2+m^2+M_2^2)^{-1/2}(m^{2}-m_{2,0}^{2})^{\frac{D^{d+2}_{1,0}}{2}}(m^{2}-m_{2,1}^{2})^{\frac{D^{d+2}_{1,1}}{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\det\nolimits_{-1}(-\nabla_{(2)}^{2}+m^{2}+M_{2}^{2})^{-1/2}

(5.18)

where we recall that
m_{s,t}^{2}
is defined in (
4.56
).

8
Discussion and outlooks

In this work, we derive the determinant expressions of the 1-loop path integrals for massive, shift-symmetric and (partially) massless symmetric tensor fields on
S^{d+1}
.

At the technical level, we have clarified subtleties arising from normalizable zero modes or negative modes on the sphere, and have made broad generalizations of all known results. One natural generalization of this work is to study path integrals involving fermionic massive, shift-symmetric and (partially) massless gauge fields. For instance, the free actions for massless fermionic fields are presented in
[
59
]
. Since fermionic fields are Grassman-valued, no Wick rotation is needed to make the path integral convergent. However, there is still a group volume factor corresponding to trivial fermionic gauge transformations, whose physical interpretations are more obscure than their bosonic counterparts, because the Grassman integrals are formally zero. Perhaps we need to combine bosonic and fermionic higher spin fields into a supersymmetric HS theory
[
60
]
so that we can make sense of the super-higher-spin group volume.

At the conceptual level, our results and their implications on de Sitter thermodynamics were discussed extensively in
[
11
]
. However, the interpretations of a number of features of 1-loop sphere partition functions require further investigations: First is the Polchinski’s phase. While we generalize the original massless spin-2 result to other classes of fields, their physical interpretations remain elusive. One is tempted to say perhaps these phases indicate non-unitarity. While this seems to be natural for massive fields with masses outside the unitary bounds (including shift-symmetric fields), the phases are present for PM fields which are perfectly unitary irreducible representations. Without other physical inputs, it is not clear whether we should ignore or retain these phases. However, we stress that these phases deserve our attentions. One reason is that perhaps a better understanding of these phases will lead us to the correct statistical interpretation of the Euclidean path integral.
16
16

16

For example, one might guess that these
i
’s are precisely the
i
’s present in the inverse Laplace transform to extract microcanonical entropies from the partition function.
Another reason is that
S^{d+1}
is only one of the many saddle points of the Euclidean gravitational path integral with a positive cosmological constant. If one considers other saddle points such as
S^{2}\times S^{d-1}
, since they have different amount of symmetries, after Wick rotating the conformal modes there will be
relative
phases between different saddle points.

The second remaining mystery is the residual group volume factor present for PM gauge fields and shift-symmetric fields. Such a factor is present for a manifestly local path integral and depends on the non-linear completion of the theory. Higher spin groups are typically infinite-dimensional and there is an issue of making sense of the group volume. The group volume may be more well-defined in theories gauging finite dimensional higher spin algebras studied in
[
57
]
.

The context in which both subtleties of phases and group volume are sharpest is in
d+1=3
dimension
[
11
]
. In this case one can check that for any PM fields, the determinants for the on-shell kinetic operator and the ghost operator cancel completely, so that the group volume and phases are the
only
non-trivial contributions to the 1-loop path integral. Also, on
S^{3}
there is an alternative formulation of massless HS gravity as a
SU(N)\times SU(N)
Chern-Simons theory. As noted in
[
11
]
, one finds that their 1-loop results agree only if we identify the residual group volume with the

Finally, as mentioned at the end of the introduction, it would be interesting to apply our results to other contexts such as such as string theory, Chern-Simons theory, supersymmetry, AdS/CFT correspondence, conformal field theory, as well as entanglement entropy in quantum field theories. We leave these to future work.

The Noether approach

Suppose we have a quadratic action

\displaystyle\delta_{\xi}S=0,

\displaystyle\delta_{\xi}^{(0)}S^{(2)}=0
