Multi-View Diffusion Maps
Ofir Lindenbaum, Arie Yeredor, Moshe Salhov, Amir Averbuch

Abstract
In this paper, we address the challenging task of achieving multi-view dimensionality reduction. The goal is to effectively use the availability of multiple views for extracting a coherent low-dimensional representation of the data. The proposed method exploits the intrinsic relation within each view, as well as the mutual relations between views. The multi-view dimensionality reduction is achieved by defining a cross-view model in which an implied random walk process is restrained to hop between objects in the different views. The method is robust to scaling and insensitive to small structural changes in the data. We define new diffusion distances and analyze the spectra of the proposed kernel. We show that the proposed framework is useful for various machine learning applications such as clustering, classification, and manifold learning. Finally, by fusing multi-sensor seismic data we present a method for automatic identification of seismic events.

An additional view can provide meaningful insights regarding the dynamical process behind the data, whenever the data is indeed generated or governed by such a latent process.

This study is devoted to the development of a framework for obtaining a low-dimensional parametrization from multiple measurements organized as “views”. Our approach essentially relies on quantifying of the speed of a random walk restricted to “hop” between views. The analysis of such a random walk provides a natural representation of the data using joint l organization of the multiple measurements.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows.

1.

We present a natural generalization of the DM method
[
5
]
for handling multiple views. The generalization is attained by combining the intrinsic relations
within each view with the mutual relations between views, so as to construct a multi-view kernel matrix. The proposed kernel defines a cross-view diffusion process, and related diffusion distances, which impose a structured random walk between the various views. In addition, we show that the spectral decomposition of the proposed kernel can be used to find an embedding of the data that offers improved exploitation of the information encapsulated in the multiple views.

2.

For the coupled views setting, we analyze theoretical properties of the proposed method. The associated parametrization is justified by being recast as a minimizer of a kernel-based objective function. Then, we relate the spectrum of the proposed kernel to the spectrum of a single-view kernel which is based on naïve concatenation of the views. Finally, under some simplification, we find the infinitesimal generator of the proposed kernel.

3.

For practical use, we present an automated method for setting the kernel bandwidth parameter and a proposed procedure for recursively augmenting the representation with each new data point.

4.

We demonstrate the applicability of our multi-view method to classification, clustering and manifold learning. Furthermore, by fusing data from multiple seismic sensors we demonstrate an automatic extraction of latent seismic parameters using real-world data.

2
Background

2.1
General dimensionality reduction

Consider a high-dimensional dataset
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}=\{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{M}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times M}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D},i=1,2,\ldots,M
. The goal is to find a low-dimensional representation
{\mbox{\boldmath$Z$}}=\{{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}_{2},\ldots,{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}_{M}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times M}
,
{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r},i=1,2,\ldots,M
, such that
r\ll D
, while latent “inner relations" (if any) among the multidimensional data points are preserved as closely as possible, in some sense. This problem setup is based on the assumption that the data is represented (viewed) in a single vector space (single view).

2.2
Diffusion Maps (DM)

DM
[
5
]
is a dimensionality reduction method which aims at extracting the intrinsic geometry of the data. The DM framework is highly effective when the data is densely sampled from some low-dimensional manifold, so that its “inner relations" are the local connectivities (or proximities) on the manifold. Given a high-dimensional dataset
X
, the DM framework consists of the following steps:

1.

A kernel function
\mathcal{K}:{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}\times{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is chosen, so as to construct a matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
with elements
K_{ij}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathcal{K}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})
, satisfying the following properties: (i) Symmetry:
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{\text{T}}
; (ii) Positive semi-defeniteness:
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}\succeq{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
, namely
\forall{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M}:\;\;{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}^{\text{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}\geq 0
; and (iii) Non-negativity:
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}\geq{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}
, namely
K_{i,j}\geq 0

\forall i,j\in[1,M]
. These properties guarantee that the matrix
K
has real-valued eigenvectors and non-negative eigenvalues. A
Gaussian kernel
is a common example, in which
\mathcal{K}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\exp\left\{-\frac{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}\right\}
, where
\|\cdot\|
denotes the
L_{2}
norm and
\sigma_{x}^{2}
is a user-selected width (scale) parameter.

2.

By normalizing the rows of
K
, the matrix

{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}

(1)

is obtained, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
is a diagonal matrix with
D_{i,i}=\sum_{j}K_{i,j}
.
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x}
can be interpreted as the transition probabilities of a (fictitious) Markov chain on
X
, such that
\left[({\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x})^{t}\right]_{i,j}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}p_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})
(where
t
is an integer power) describes the implied probability of transition from point
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}
to point
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}
in
t
steps.

3.

Spectral decomposition is applied to
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x}
, obtaining a set of
M
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{m}\}
(in descending order) and associated normalized eigenvectors
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{m}\}
satisfying
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x}{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{m}=\lambda_{m}{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{m}
,
m=0,\ldots,M-1
, or
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}
, where
\Psi
and
\Lambda
are (resp.) the eigenvectors and diagonal eigenvalues matrices.

4.

A new representation is defined for the dataset
X
, representing each
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}
by the
i
-th row of
({\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x})^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}^{t}
, namely:

{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}):\;\;{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\mapsto\left[\lambda_{1}^{t}\psi_{1}[i],\ldots,\lambda_{M-1}^{t}\psi_{M-1}[i]\right]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{M-1},\;\;i\in[1,M]

(2)

where
t
is a selected number of steps and
\psi_{m}[i]
denotes the
i
-th element of
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{m}
. Note that the trivial eigenvector
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}
(with corresponding eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}=1
) was omitted from the representation as it does not carry information about the data. The main idea behind this representation is that the Euclidean distance between two data points in the new representation equals a weighted
L_{2}
distance between the conditional probability vectors
p_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},:)
and
p_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j},:)
,
i,j\in[1,M]
(the
i
-th and
j
-th rows of
({\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{x})^{t}
). This weighted Euclidean distance is referred to as the
diffusion distance
, denoted

\mathcal{D}_{t}^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\|{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})-{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})\|^{2}\\
=\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\lambda_{m}^{2t}(\psi_{m}[i]-\psi_{m}[j])^{2}=\|p_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},:)-p_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j},:)\|_{{\boldmath W}^{-1}}^{2},

(3)

where
{\mbox{\boldmath$W$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}/\text{Trace}\{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}\}
and where
\|{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}^{T}\|_{\boldmath Q}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}^{\text{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$Q$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}
denotes a
Q
-weighted Euclidean norm (the last equality is shown in
[
5
]
).

5.

A desired accuracy level
\delta\geq 0
is chosen for the diffusion distance defined by Eq. (
3
), such that
r(\delta,t)=\max\{\ell:|\lambda_{\ell}|^{t}>\delta|\lambda_{1}|^{t}\}
. The new (truncated)
r(\delta,t)
-dimensional mapping, which leads to the desired representation
Z
, is then defined as

{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}_{t}^{\delta}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}):\;\;{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\mapsto\left[\lambda_{1}^{t}\psi_{1}[i],\lambda_{2}^{t}\psi_{2}[i].\ldots,\lambda_{r}^{t}\psi_{r}[i]\right]^{\text{T}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{r(\delta,t)}.

(4)

This dimensionality reduction approach was found to be useful in various applications in diverse fields. However, as previously noted, it is limited to a single-view representation.

3
Multi-view dimensionality reduction - Problem formulation and prior work

Assume now that we are given multiple sets (views) of observations
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\ell}\text{ },\ell=1,...,L
. Each
view is a high-dimensional dataset
{{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\ell}=\{{{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}^{\ell},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2}^{\ell},...,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{M}^{\ell}}}\}\in{\mathbb{R}^{D_{\ell}\times M}}}
, where
D_{\ell}
is the dimension of each feature space. Note that a bijective correspondence between views is assumed. These views can be a result of different measurement devices or different types of features extracted from raw data. For each view
\ell=1,...,L
, we seek a lower-dimensional representation that preserves the “inner relations" between multidimensional data points within each view
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\ell}
, as well as among all views
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{1},...,{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{L}\}
. Our goal is to find
L
representations
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{\ell}({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{1},...,{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{L}):\mathbb{R}^{D_{\ell}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}^{r}}
, such that
r\ll D_{\ell},\;\;\ell=1,...,L
.

Before turning to present our proposed approach, we describe existing alternative approaches for incorporating multiple views. In particular, we detail here the methods which we would later use as benchmarks for comparison to our proposed framework. All of these methods begin by computing the kernel matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{\ell}
for each (
\ell
-th) view (
\ell=1,\ldots L)
, and differ in the way of combining these kernels for generating the DM.

3.1
Kernel Product DM (KP)

A naïve generalization of DM
[
5
]
may be computed using an element-wise kernel product, namely by constructing
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{{\circ}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}\circ{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}\circ...\circ{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
, where
\circ
denotes Hadamard’s (element-wise) matrix product, such that
{K}_{i,j}^{\circ}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{K}_{i,j}^{1}\cdot{K}_{i,j}^{2}\cdot...\cdot{K}_{ij}^{L}
, followed by row-normalization:
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}=\left({\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{\circ}\right)^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{\circ}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
,
where
{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{\circ}
is diagonal, with
D_{i,i}^{\circ}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\underset{j}{\sum}{K_{i,j}^{\circ}}
.

Note that in the special case of using Gaussian kernels with equal width parameters
\sigma
, the resulting matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{\circ}
is
equal to the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{w}
constructed from the concatenated observations vector

{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{i}=\left[\left({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{1}_{i}\right)^{\text{T}},...,\left({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{L}_{i}\right)^{\text{T}}\right]^{\text{T}}

(5)

such that

K_{i,j}^{w}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\left\|{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}_{j}\right\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}.

(6)

3.2
Kernel Sum DM (KS)

An average diffusion process was used in
[
23
]
, where the
sum kernel
is defined as

{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{+}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum^{L}_{\ell=1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{\ell},

(7)

followed by row-normalization:
{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}^{+}=\left({\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{+}\right)^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{+}
, where
D^{+}_{i,i}=\underset{j}{\sum}{K_{i,j}^{+}}
.
The implied random walk sums up (and normalizes) the step probabilities from each view.

3.3
Kernel Canonical Correlation Analysis (KCCA)

The frameworks
[
15
,
16
]
extend the well-known Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) by applying a kernel function prior to the application of CCA. Kernels
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}
are constructed for each view, and the canonical vectors
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2}
are obtained by solving the following generalized eigenvalue problem

\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}}\\
{{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1}}\\
{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2}}\end{pmatrix}=\rho\cdot\begin{bmatrix}({\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}+\gamma{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}})^{2}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}}\\
{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}}&({\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}+\gamma{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}})^{2}\end{bmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{1}}\\
{{\mbox{\boldmath$v$}}_{2}}\end{pmatrix},

(8)

where
\gamma{\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}
are regularization terms added to prevent overfitting and thus improve generalization of the method. Usually the Incomplete Cholesky Decomposition (ICD)
[
15
,
16
,
30
]
is used to reduce the run time required for solving (
8
). For clustering tasks,
K
-means clustering is applied to the set of generalized eigenvectors.

3.4
Spectral clustering with two views

The approach in
[
19
]
generalizes the traditional normalized graph Laplacian for two views. Kernels
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}
are computed in each view and multiplied, yielding
{\mbox{\boldmath$W$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}
, from which

{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$W$}}}\\
{{\mbox{\boldmath$W$}}^{T}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{2M\times 2M}

(9)

is obtained. Symmetric normalization is applied by using the diagonal matrix
\bar{D}
with diagonal elements

{\bar{D}}_{i,i}=\underset{j}{\sum}{{{W}}_{i,j}}
, such that the normalized fused kernel is defined as

{\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{A}$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{D}$}}^{-0.5}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\bar{D}$}}^{-0.5}.

(10)

Assuming that the data can be clustered into
N_{C}
clusters, and denoting the
2M
eigenvectors of
\bar{A}
as
{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}_{i},i=1,...,2M
(in descending order of the corresponding eigenvalues), the mapping

{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}[i]\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\frac{1}{s[i]}\left[\phi_{1}[i],...,\phi_{N_{C}}[i]\right]^{\text{T}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{C}}

(11)

(where
s[i]=\sum^{N_{C}}_{j=1}(\phi^{2}_{j}[i])
) is useful for applying
K
-means clustering.
The work in
[
19
]
is focused on spectral clustering, however, a similar version of the kernel from Eq. (
10
) is suitable for manifold learning, as we demonstrate in the sequel. In section
6
this approach is referred to as de Sa’s.

As We show in the next section, our proposed approach essentially uses a stochastic matrix version of this kernel, and extends the construction to multiple views. We shall show that our stochastic matrix version is useful, as it provides various theoretical justifications for the implied multi-view diffusion process.

4
Our Proposed Approach for Multi-view Diffusion Maps

Here we propose our generalization of the DM framework for handling
a multi-view scenario. This is done by imposing an implied (fictitious) random walk model
using the local connectivities between data points within all
views. Our way to generalize the DM framework is by restraining
the random walker to “hop” between different views in each step. The construction requires to choose a set of
L
symmetrical positive
semi-definite, non-negative kernels, one for each view

{{\cal{K}}^{l}:{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{l}\times{{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{l}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}\text{ },\;\;l=1,...,L}
. We use the Gaussian kernel function, so that the
(i,j)
-th element of each
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
is given by

{K^{l}_{i,j}=\exp\left\{-\frac{||{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j}||^{2}}{2\sigma_{l}^{2}}\right\}},\;\;\;i,j=1,\ldots,M,\;\;l=1,\ldots,L,

(12)

where
\left\{\sigma_{l}^{2}\right\}_{l=1}^{L}
are a set of selected parameters.
The decaying property of the Gaussian is useful, as it removes the influence of large Euclidean distances.
The
multi-view kernel
is formed by constructing the following matrix

{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{K}$}}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{3}}&...&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{3}}&...&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{3}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{3}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&...&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{3}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}}\\
:&:&:&...&:\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{1}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{2}}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{L}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{3}}&...&{{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}}.\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{LM\times LM}.

(13)

Finally, using the diagonal row-normalization matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{D}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{LM\times LM}
with

{\widehat{D}}_{i,i}=\underset{j}{\sum}{{\widehat{K}}_{i,j}}
, the normalized row-stochastic matrix is defined as

{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{P}$}}={{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{D}$}}}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{K}$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{LM\times LM}.

(14)

We refer to the
(l,m)
-th block of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
as the square
M\times M
matrix starting at

[1+(l-1)M,1+(m-1)M],\;\;l,m=1,...,L
. Thus, the
(i,j)
-th element of the
(l,m)
-th block describes a (fictitious) probability of
transition from
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}^{l}
to
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}^{m}
. This construction takes into account all possibilities to “hop” between views, under the constraint that staying in the same view (namely, a transition from
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}^{l}
to
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}^{l}
) is forbidden.

4.1
Probabilistic interpretation of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}

Subsequent to our proposed construction (Eqs. (
12
), (
13
) and
(
14
)), each element of the power-
t
matrix
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}
,

\left[\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right]_{i+(l-1)M,j+(m-1)M}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\widehat{p}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{j})

(15)

denotes the probability of transition from
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}^{l}
to
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}^{m}
in
t
time-steps. Note that, as mentioned above, due to the block-off-diagonal structure of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
, which forbids a transition into the same view within a single time-step, we have
\widehat{p}_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j})=0,\;\;l=1,\ldots,L
, although for
t>1

\widehat{p}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j})
may be non-zero.

4.1.1
Smoothing effect
t=1

For simplicity let us examine the term
\widehat{p}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{j}),l\neq m
for
t=1
. The transition probability for
t=1
is

{{\widehat{p}}}_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{j})=\frac{\sum_{s}{K^{l}_{i,s}}K^{m}_{s,j}}{{\widehat{D}_{i,i}}}.

This probability takes into consideration all the various connectivities of node
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}
to node
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{s}
and the connectivities of the corresponding node
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{s}
to the destination node
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{j}
. The proposed multi-view approach has a “smoothing effect" in terms of the transition probabilities, meaning that the probability of transitioning from
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}
to
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{j}
could be positive even if
K^{l}_{i,j}=0
and
K^{m}_{i,j}=0
: Assume that a non-empty subset
{\cal{S}}=\{s_{1},...,s_{F}\}\subseteq[1,M]
exists, such that
K^{l}_{i,s_{f}}>0
and
K^{m}_{s_{f},j}>0,f=1,..,F
. Then by definition of the multi-view probability we have
{\widehat{p}}_{1}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{m}_{j})>0

,

can become relatively high.

Under the stochastic Markov model assumption, raising
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
to a higher power (by increasing the diffusion step

) spreads the probability mass function along its rows based on the connectivities in all views. As described in

[
5
]
. In Fig.

\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}
with different values of

. For this experiment we generated
L=3
Swiss Rolls with
M=1,\!200
data points each. It is evident that the numerical rank of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}
decreases for higher values of
t
.

4.2
Multi-view diffusion distance

In a variety of real-world data
types, the Euclidean distance between observed data points (vectors) does not provide sufficient
information about the intrinsic relations between these vectors, and is highly sensitive to non-unitary transformations. Common tasks such as classification, clustering or system
identification often require a measure for the intrinsic connectivity
between data points, which is only locally expressed
by the Euclidean distance in the high-dimensional ambient space. The
multi-view diffusion kernel (defined in section
4
)
describes all the small local connections between data points.
The row stochastic matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{P}$}}^{t}
(Eq. (
14
))
accounts for all possible transitions between data points in
t
time steps while hopping between the views. For a fixed value
t>0
, two
data points are
intrinsically similar
if the conditional
distributions

{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},:)={\mbox{\boldmath${[\widehat{P}^{t}]}$}}_{i,:}

and

{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$p$}}}}_{t}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j},:)={\mbox{\boldmath${[\widehat{P}^{t}]}$}}_{j,:}

are similar. This type of similarity measure indicates that the
points
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
are similarly connected (in the sense of similar probabilities of transitions) to several
mutual points. Thus, they are connected by some geometrical path. In
many cases, a small Euclidean distance can be misleading due to
the fact that two data points can be “close” without having any
geodesic path that connects them. Comparing the
transition probabilities is more robust, as it takes into consideration all of the local connectivities between the compared points. Therefore, even if two points seem far from one another in the Euclidean sense, they may still share many common neighbors and thus be “close" in the sense of having a small diffusion distance.

Based on this observation, by expanding the single-view
construction given in
[
5
]
, we define the weighted inner view
diffusion distances for the first view as

{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}^{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}^{1})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{L\cdot M}\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{0}(k)}\left(\left[\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right]_{i,k}-\left[\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right]_{j,k}\right)^{2}=\left\|({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{j})^{\text{T}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right\|^{2}_{\scriptsize{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}}},

(16)

where
1\leq i,j\leq M
,
{\mbox{\boldmath${e}$}}_{i}
is the
i
-th column of an
L\cdot M\times L\cdot M
identity matrix,
\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$\phi$}}}_{0}
is the (non-normalized) first left eigenvector of
\widehat{P}
, whose
k
-th element is
\tilde{\phi}_{0}(k)={\widehat{D}_{k,k}}
.
A similarly weighted norm is defined for the
l
-th view

{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}^{l},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}^{l})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{k=1}^{L\cdot M}\frac{1}{\tilde{\phi}_{0}(k)}\left(\left[\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right]_{i+\tilde{l},k}-\left[\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right]_{j+\tilde{l},k}\right)^{2}=\left\|({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+j})^{\text{T}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right\|^{2}_{\scriptsize{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}}},

(17)

where
\tilde{l}=(l-1)\cdot M
. The main advantage of these distances (Eqs. (
16
)
and (
17
)) is that they can be expressed in terms of the
eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the matrix

\widehat{P}
. This insight allows us to use a
representation (defined in section
4.3
below) where
the induced Euclidean distance is proportional to the diffusion
distances defined in Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
). Indeed, let
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{\text{T}}
denote the eigenvalues decomposition of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}=\left[\psi_{0},\ldots,\psi_{L\cdot M-1}\right]
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}=\left[\phi_{0},\ldots,\phi_{L\cdot M-1}\right]
denote the matrices of (normalized) right- and left-eigenvectors (resp.), and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\mathop{\rm Diag}\nolimits(\lambda_{0},\ldots,\lambda_{L\cdot M-1})
denotes the diagonal matrix of respective eigenvalues. Then

Theorem 1
.

The inner view diffusion distance defined by Eqs. (
16
) and
(
17
) can also be expresses as

{{{{{\cal{D}}_{t}^{2}}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j})=\sum_{{k}=1}^{L\cdot M-1}\lambda_{k}^{2t}\left(\psi_{k}\left[i+\tilde{l}\right]-\psi_{k}\left[j+\tilde{l}\right]\right)^{2}},i,j=1,...,M},

(18)

where
\tilde{l}=(l-1)\cdot M
.

Proof.

Using the eigenvalues decomposition of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
we have

\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}\left(\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right)^{\text{T}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}^{t}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{\text{T}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}^{t}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}}^{\text{T}}.

(19)

Define the symmetric matrix
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}_{s}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1/2}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1/2}=\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{1/2}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1/2}
, and note that this matrix is algebraically similar to
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
. Therefore, both matrices share the same set of eigenvalues
\Lambda
. Additionally, let
\Pi
denote the (left- and right-) orthonormal eigenvectors matrix of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}_{s}
, namely
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}_{s}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Pi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Pi$}}^{\text{T}}
. The left- and right-eigenvectors matrices of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}=\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1/2}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}_{s}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{1/2}
are then easily identified as
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}=\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{1/2}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Pi$}}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}=\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1/2}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}
(resp.), so that
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}^{\text{T}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Pi$}}^{\text{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Pi$}}={\mbox{\boldmath$I$}}
. Therefore,

{\mathcal{D}}_{t}^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}^{l},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}^{l})=\left\|({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+j})^{\text{T}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right\|^{2}_{\scriptsize{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}}}=({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+j})^{\text{T}}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{-1}\left(\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}^{t}\right)^{\text{T}}({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+j})\\
=({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+j})^{\text{T}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Lambda$}}^{2t}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}^{\text{T}}({\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{\tilde{l}+j})=\sum_{{k}=1}^{L\cdot M-1}\lambda_{k}^{2t}\left(\psi_{k}\left[i+\tilde{l}\right]-\psi_{k}\left[j+\tilde{l}\right]\right)^{2}.

(20)

The term with
k=0
can be excluded from the sum since
\psi_{0}={\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}
(an all-ones vector) is always the first right-eigenvector (with eigenvalue
1
) for any stochastic matrix, so the term corresponding to
k=0
in the sum would vanish anyway.
∎

4.3
Multi-view Data Parametrization

Tasks such as classification, clustering
or regression in a high-dimensional feature space are
considered to be computationally expensive. In addition, the
performance in such tasks is highly dependent on the distance
measure used. As explained in section
4.2
, distance measures in the original
ambient space are often meaningless in many real life situations.
Interpreting Theorem
1
in terms of Euclidean distance enables
us to define mappings for every view
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{l},l=1,...,L
, using
the right eigenvectors of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
(Eq. (
14
))
weighted by
\lambda^{t}_{i}
. The representation for instances in
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{l}
is given
by

{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i})}:{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}\longmapsto\begin{bmatrix}{\lambda_{1}^{t}\psi_{1}[i+\tilde{l}]},{.}{.}{.},\par{\lambda_{M-1}^{t}\psi_{M-1}[i+\tilde{l}]}\par\end{bmatrix}^{T}\in{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}},

(21)

where
\tilde{l}=(l-1)\cdot M
.
These
L
mappings capture the intrinsic geometry of the views as well
as the mutual relations between them. As shown in
[
31
]
, the set of eigenvalues
{\lambda_{m}}
has a decaying property
such that

{1=\lvert{\lambda_{0}}\rvert\geq\lvert{\lambda_{1}}\rvert\geq\text{...}\geq\lvert{\lambda_{M-1}}\rvert}
. Exploiting the decaying property enables us to represent data up to a dimension
r
where
r\ll D_{1},...,D_{l}
. The dimension
r\equiv r(\delta)
is
determined by approximating the diffusion distance (Eq. (
18
))
up to a desired accuracy
\delta
(we elaborate on this issue in
section
5.4
). The reduced dimension version of
{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}})}}
is denoted by
{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}^{r}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}})}}
.

Using the inner view diffusion distances defined in Eqs. (
16
) and (
17
), we define a multi-view
diffusion distance as the sum of inner views
distances,

{{{{{\cal{D}}_{t}}^{(MV)}}^{2}(i,j)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\left|{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}^{r}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i})}}-{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}^{r}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j})}}\right|^{2}}}.

(22)

This distance is the induced Euclidean distance in a space constructed from the concatenation of all low-dimensional multi-view mappings

\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}}_{t}({{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}})=\left[{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}^{r}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{1})};{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}^{r}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{2})};...;{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}^{r}_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{L})}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{L\cdot r\times M}.

(23)

This mapping is used in section
7.1
for the experimental evaluation of clustering.

4.4
Multi-view kernel bandwidth

When constructing the Gaussian kernels
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l},l=1,...,L
, in Eq. (
12
), the values of the scale (width) parameter
\sigma^{2}_{l}
have to be set. Setting these values to be too small
may result in very small local
neighborhoods that are unable to capture the local structures around the data points.
Conversely, setting the values to be too large
may result in a fully connected graph that may generate
a too coarse description of
the data.
Several approaches have been proposed in the literature for determining the kernel width (scale):

In
[
32
]
, a max-min measure is suggested such that the scale becomes

\sigma_{{l}}^{2}={{C}}\cdot\underset{j}{\max}\left[\underset{i,i\neq j}{\min}(||{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j}||^{2})\right].

(24)

Based on empirical results, the authors in
[
32
]
suggest to set
{{C}}
within the range
[1,1.5]
. The specific choice of
C
should be sufficient to capture all local connectivities. This single-view approach could be relaxed in the context of our multi-view scenario. The multi-view kernel
\widehat{K}
(Eq.
13
) consists of products of single-view kernel matrices
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l},l=1,...,L
(Eq.
12
). The diagonal values of each kernel matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}
are all
1
’s, therefore, in order to have connectivity in all rows of
\widehat{K}
a connectivity in only one of the views is sufficient. By connectivity, we mean that there is at least one nonzero value in the affinity kernel. This insight suggests that a smaller value for the parameter
{{C}}
could be used in the multi view setting. In
[
33
]
, the authors provide a probabilistic interpretation of the choice of
C
, and present a grid-search algorithm for optimizing the choice of
C
such that each point in the dataset is connected to at least one other point.

Another scheme, proposed in
[
34
]
, aims to find a range of values for
\sigma_{l}
. The idea is to compute the kernel
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}
(Eq. (
12
)) for various values of
\sigma
and search for the range of values where the Gaussian bell shape is more pronounced. To find such range of valid values for
\sigma
, first a logarithmic function is applied to the sum of all elements of the kernel matrix, next the range is identified as the maximal range where the logarithmic plot is linear. We expand this idea for a multi-view scenario based on the following algorithm:

Algorithm 1

Input:
Multiple sets of observations (views)
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{l},l=1,...,L
.

Output:
Scale parameters
\{\sigma_{1},...,\sigma_{L}\}
.

1:

Compute Gaussian kernels
{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}(\sigma_{l})},l=1,...,L
for several values of
{\sigma}_{l}\in[10^{-5},10^{5}]
.

2:

Compute for all pairs
l\neq m
:
{S^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})=\underset{i}{{\sum}}\underset{j}{{\sum}}K^{lm}_{i,j}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})}
, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})={\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}(\sigma_{l})\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{m}(\sigma_{m})
.

3:

for

l=1:L

do

4:

Find the minimal value for
\sigma_{l}
such that
{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}}^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})
is linear for all
m\neq l
.

5:

end

for

Note that the two dimensional function
S^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})
consists of two asymptotes,
S^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})\overset{\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m}\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}\text{log}(N)
, and
S^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})\overset{\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m}\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}\text{log}(N^{3})=3\text{log}(N)
, since for
\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m}\rightarrow 0
, both
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{m}
approach the Identity matrix, and for
\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m}\rightarrow\infty
, both
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{l}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{m}
approach all-ones matrices.
An example of the plot
S^{lm}(\sigma_{l},\sigma_{m})
for two views (
L=2
) is presented in Fig.
3
. The range of each
\sigma_{l}
should reflect the asymptotic behaviour, and may be determined empirically by choosing
\min(\sigma_{l})\ll median\{||{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j}||\}
and
\max(\sigma_{l})\gg\text{median}\{||{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{l}_{j}||\}
. In practice the range

is sufficient.

4.5
Computational Complexity

Let us now consider the computational complexity required for extracting
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Phi$}}_{l}({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{1},...,{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{L})\in{\mathbb{R}^{r\times M}}
from

{{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{l}=\{{{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}^{l},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2}^{l},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{3}^{l},...,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{M}^{l}}}\}\in{\mathbb{R}^{D_{l}\times M}}}
.
The computational complexity of computing
L
kernels is
{\cal{O}}(\sum_{l}M^{2}D_{l})
. Computing the proposed normalized kernel
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
(Eq.
14
) adds a complexity of
{\cal{O}}(\frac{L(L-1)}{4}M^{3})
. The final step requires the spectral decomposition of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
, thus it is the most computationally expensive step and adds a complexity of
{\cal{O}}(L^{3}M^{3})
. However, due to the low rank, sparse nature of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
, this spectral decomposition could be approximated using random projection methods such as in
[
35
,
36
,
37
]
. Using random projection the complexity of the spectral decomposition is improved to
{\cal{O}}(L^{2}M^{2}\log{r})
. For the optional step proposed in Algorithm
1
, if
n_{0}
values are chosen for
\sigma
, the additional complexity cost is of
\mathcal{O}(MLn_{0})
.

5
Coupled views
L=2

In this section we provide analytical results for the special case of a coupled data set (i.e
L=2
). Some of the results could be expanded to a larger number of views but not in a straightforward manner.
To simplify the notation in the rest of this section we denote
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{1}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{2}
.

5.1
Coupled mapping

The mappings provided by our approach (Eq. (
21
)) are justified by the relations given by Eq. (
18
). In this subsection we provide some intuition on the relation between the mappings of
X
and
Y
. We focus on the
analysis of a
1
-dimensional mapping for each view. Let

{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}})=[\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}),\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2}),...,\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{M})]^{\text{T}}

and

{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}})=[\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{1}),\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{2}),...,\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{M})]^{\text{T}}

denote such
1
-dimensional mappings (one for each view)
and define
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x},{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
are computed from
X
and
Y
(resp.) based on Eq. (
12
).
The following theorem characterizes the desirable
1
-dimensional mappings
{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y}
.

Theorem 2
.

A
1
-dimensional zero-mean representation which minimizes the following objective function

\min_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x},{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y}}\sum_{i,j}(\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})-\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}))^{2}{K}_{i,j}^{z}\\
{\rm s.t.}\;\;\|{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x}\|^{2}+\|{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y}\|^{2}=1,\;\sum_{i}\left(\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})+\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i})\right)=0,

(25)

is obtained by setting
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}[{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x\text{T}}\;{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y\text{T}}]^{\text{T}}={\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{1}
, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$\psi$}}_{1}
is the first non-trivial normalized eigenvector of the graph Laplacian
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}-\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}
. The scaling constraint avoids arbitrary scaling of the representation, while the zero-mean constraint (implying orthogonality of
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}}
to the constant all-ones vector
1
) eliminates any presence of a trivial (constant) component in the representations.

Note that
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}
is an affinity measure of points based on
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
. This means that a small value of
{K}_{i,j}^{z}
indicates weak
connectivity between data points
i
and
j
(possibly through an intermediate point
\ell\in[1,M]
), implying that the distance between

\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})
and
\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})
can be
large. Conversely, if
{K}_{i,j}^{z}
is large, indicating
strong connectivity (possibly through an intermediate point) between points
i
and
j
, the distance between

\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})
and
\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})
should be
small when aiming to minimize the objective function.

Proof.

By expanding the objective function in Eq. (
25
) we get

\sum_{i,j}(\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})-\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}))^{2}{K}_{i,j}^{z}=\sum_{i,j}\rho^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})K_{i,j}^{z}+\sum_{i,j}\rho^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})K_{i,j}^{z}-2\sum_{i,j}\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}){K}_{i,j}^{z}\\
=\sum_{i}\rho^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})\sum_{j}K^{z}_{i,j}+\sum_{j}\rho^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})\sum_{i}K^{z}_{i,j}-\sum_{i,j}\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}){K}_{i,j}^{z}-\sum_{i,j}\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}){K}_{j,i}^{z}\\
=\sum_{i}\rho^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})D_{i,i}^{\rm rows}+\sum_{j}\rho^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})D_{j,j}^{\rm cols}-\sum_{i,j}\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}){K}_{i,j}^{z}-\sum_{j,i}\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})\rho({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}){K}_{j,i}^{z}\\
=\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x\text{T}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y\text{T}}\end{bmatrix}\Bigg[\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{\rm rows}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{\rm cols}\end{bmatrix}-\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}&{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}}\\
({{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}})^{\text{T}}&{\mbox{\boldmath$0$}}_{M\times M}\end{bmatrix}\Bigg]\begin{bmatrix}{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{x}\\
{\mbox{\boldmath$\rho$}}^{y}\end{bmatrix},

(26)

where
{D_{i,i}^{\rm rows}}=\sum_{j=1}^{M}{{K^{z}_{i,j}}}
and
{D_{j,j}^{\rm cols}}=\sum_{i=1}^{M}{{K^{z}_{i,j}}}

(27)

5.3
Cross-view (CV) diffusion distance

In some physical systems the observed dataset
X
may depend on some underlying parameter, say

\alpha
. Under this model, multiple snapshots (views) can typically be obtained for
various values of
\alpha
, each denoted
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha}
. An example of such a scenario
occurs in hyper-spectral images that change over time.
Quantifying the amount of change in the datasets due to a change in
\alpha
in such models is often desired, but if the datasets are high-dimensional, this can be quite a challenging
task. This scenario was recently studied in
[
38
]
, where the DM
framework was applied to each fixed value of
\alpha
.
Then, by using the extracted low-dimensional mappings, the Euclidean
distance enables to quantify the extent of changes due to
\alpha
.
However, this approach may be rather unstable, since every small change in the data can
result in different mappings and the mappings are extracted
independently of any mutual influence.

Our multiview approach, on the other hand, incorporates the mutual relations of data
within each view, as well as the relations between views. This point of view
facilitates a more robust measure of the extent of variation
between two datasets that correspond to a small variation in

\alpha
. To this end, we define a new diffusion distance, which
measures the relation between two views, i.e. between all the data
points obtained for different values of
\alpha
. We measure the distance between all the coupled data points
among the mappings of the snapshots
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha_{l}}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha_{m}}
by using the expression

{{{{{\cal{D}}_{t}}^{\rm(CV)}}^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha_{l}},{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha_{m}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\sum\limits_{i=1}^{M}\left\|{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}_{t}{\left({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{l}}_{i}\right)}}-{{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\Psi}$}}_{t}{\left({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{m}}_{i}\right)}}\right\|^{2}}}.

(30)

Our kernel matrix is a product of the Gaussian kernel matrices in
each view. If these values of the kernel matrices (
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x^{\alpha_{l}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x^{\alpha_{m}}}
) are similar, this corresponds to similarity between
the views’ inner geometry. The right- and left-singular vectors of the
matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x^{\alpha_{l}}}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x^{\alpha_{m}}}
will be similar, thus,

{{{\cal{D}}_{t}}^{\rm(CV)}}
will be small.

Theorem 4
.

Using Gaussian kernels with identical
\sigma
values, the cross manifold distance (defined in Eq. (
30
)) is
invariant to orthonormal transformations between the ambient spaces

{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{{\alpha_{l}}}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{{\alpha_{m}}}
.

Proof.

Denote an orthonormal transformation matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}:{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{{\alpha_{l}}}\rightarrow{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{{\alpha_{m}}}
, w.l.o.g. by
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{{\alpha_{m}}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{{\alpha_{l}}}_{i}
. Then

K^{x^{\alpha_{m}}}_{i,j}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{m}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{{\alpha_{m}}}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{{{m}}}}\right\}=\exp\left\{-\frac{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{l}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$R$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{l}}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{m}}\right\}\\
=\exp\left\{-\frac{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{l}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{l}}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{l}^{2}}\right\}={K^{x^{\alpha_{l}}}_{i,j}}.

(31)

The penultimate transition is due to the orthonomality of
R
and to the identical
\sigma_{l}=\sigma_{m}
values.
Therefore, the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}=({\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x^{\alpha_{m}}})^{2}
from Eq. (
12
) is symmetric and its right- and left-singular vectors are
equal, i.e.

U=V
in Eq. (
28
). This induces a repetitive form
in
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Psi$}}=\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}^{{-1}/{2}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Pi$}}\rightarrow\psi_{l}[i]=\psi_{l}[M+i],\text{ }1\leq i,l\leq M-1\rightarrow{\Psi_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{l}}_{i})}}={\Psi_{t}{({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}^{\alpha_{m}}_{i})}}
,
thus,
{{{\cal{D}}_{t}}^{(CM)}}^{2}({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha_{l}},{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{\alpha_{m}})=0
.

∎

5.4.1
Intrinsic Dimension and Numerical Rank

In
[
41
]
Bermanis
et al.
prove that the numerical rank of a Gaussian kernel matrix is typically independent of its size. Their results state that the numerical rank is proportional to the volume of the data. By using boxes with side-length of
\epsilon^{D/2}=\sigma^{D}
(
D
is the dimension of the data), it is shown that the numerical rank is bounded from above by the minimal number of such cubes required to cover the data. As the number of eigenvalues
|\lambda_{\ell}|>\delta
is proportional to the numerical rank, this means that for a prescribed
\delta
, the number of coordinates
r_{\delta}
required is independent of the number of samples
N
.

Practically speaking, this result is useful if the numerical rank is small. Furthermore, as there are several methods for estimating the numerical rank, using the numerical rank to choose
r_{\delta}
is not very expensive. Another practical method, which we found useful for setting
r
without directly choosing
\delta
, is based on estimating the intrinsic dimension of the data
d
. Various methods have been proposed for estimating the intrinsic dimension of high-dimensional data. Here, we provide a brief description of a few. Popular methods, such as
[
42
,
43
]
apply local PCA to the data, and estimate the intrinsic dimension as the median of the number of principal components corresponding to singular values larger than some threshold. Based on our experience, we found that a method called Dimensionality from Angle and Norm Concentration (DANCo)
[
44
]
provides a robust estimation of the intrinsic dimension
d
. DANCo generates artificial data of dimension
d
and attempts to minimize the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the estimated probability distribution functions (pdf-s) of the observed data and the artificially-generated samples.

5.5

The Nyström extension is is similarly obtained by computing a weighted sum of the original eigenvectors. The weights are computed by applying the kernel
\cal{K}
to the extended data points, followed by row-normalization. For the proposed mapping, the extension is defined by

\hat{\psi}_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\underset{{j}}{\sum}{\hat{p}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})\psi^{y}_{k}[j]}

(39)

\hat{\psi}_{k}({\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{y}$}})=\frac{1}{\lambda_{k}}\underset{{j}}{\sum}{\hat{P}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{y}$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})\psi^{x}_{k}[j]}

(40)

where the “missing" probabilities
\hat{p}(\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})
and
\hat{p}(\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})
are approximated using the original kernel function as

\hat{p}(\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j})={\sum_{s}{\exp\bigg\{-\frac{||\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{s}||^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{x}}\bigg\}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{||{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{s}-{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}||^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{y}}\bigg\}}\frac{1}{\widehat{D}_{j+M,j+M}}

(41)

\hat{p}(\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})={\sum_{s}{\exp\bigg\{-\frac{||\tilde{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{s}||^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{y}}\bigg\}}}\exp\bigg\{-\frac{||{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{s}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}||^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}_{x}}\bigg\}\frac{1}{\widehat{D}_{j+M,j+M}}

(42)

and where
\psi_{k}^{x}[j]=\psi_{k}[j]
and
\psi_{k}^{y}[j]=\psi_{k}[m+j]
.

The new mapping vector for the new data point is then given by

{{\hat{{{{\Psi}}}}}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}})=\begin{bmatrix}{\lambda_{1}\hat{\psi}_{1}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}}})},{\lambda_{2}\hat{\psi}_{2}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}}})},{\lambda_{3}\hat{\psi}_{3}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}}})},{.}{.}{.},{\lambda_{M-1}\hat{\psi}_{M-1}({{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}}})}\par\end{bmatrix}}\in{\mathbb{R}^{M-1}}}.

(43)

The new coordinates in the diffusion space are approximated and the new data points
{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{x}$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$\tilde{y}$}}
have no effect on the original map’s structure.

5.6
Infinitesimal generator

In the subsequent analysis we only consider kernel functions operating on the scaled difference between their two arguments, namely kernel functions which can be written as

{\mbox{\boldmath$\cal{K}$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})=K\left(\frac{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right),

(44)

where
\epsilon
is a positive constant, and
K({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}):\mathbb{R}^{D}\mapsto\mathbb{R}
is a non-negative symmetric function, namely
R({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})\geq 0
and
R({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})=R(-{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})

\forall{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. For example, the Gaussian kernel (see subsection
2.2
)
\mathcal{K}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j})=\exp\left\{-\frac{\|{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{j}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{x}^{2}}\right\}
satisfies this property with
\epsilon=\sigma^{2}
.

A family of differently normalized diffusion operators was introduced in

[
5
]
. If
appropriate limits are taken such that
M\rightarrow\infty
,

\epsilon\rightarrow 0
, then from

[
5
]
it follows that the DM kernel operator will converge to one of the
following differential operators: 1. Normalized graph Laplacian; 2. Laplace-Beltrami diffusion; or 3. Heat kernel equation. These are
proved in
[
5
]
. The operators are all special cases of the
diffusion equation. This convergence provides not only a physical justification
for the DM framework, but allows in some cases to distinguish
between the geometry and the density of the data points. In this
subsection we study the asymptotic properties of the proposed kernel

\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}
(Eq. (
13
)), limiting the discussion to only two views, i.e.
L=2
.

We are interested in understanding the properties of the eigenfunctions of the proposed multi-view kernel
{\widehat{P}}
(Eqs. (
13
), (
14
)) for two views. We assume that there is some unknown mapping
{\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
from view
X
to view
Y
that satisfies
{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$\beta$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}),i=1,...,M
. Each view-specific kernel applies the same function, namely
{K}^{x}({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})={K}^{y}({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})=K({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})
, and
K({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}})
is normalized such that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}K({\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}){\rm d}{\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}=1
. Note that the Gaussian kernel can be propely normalized to satisfy this requirement. The analysis relates to data points
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},...,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{M}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
sampled from a uniform distribution over a bounded domain in
{\mathbb{R}^{d}}
. The image of the function
\beta
is a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with distribution
{\alpha}({\mbox{\boldmath${\mbox{\boldmath$z$}}$}})
.

Theorem 7
.

The infinitesimal generator induced by the proposed kernel matrix
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}
(Eq. (
13
)) after row-normalization, denoted in here as
\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}
, converges when

M\rightarrow\infty,\epsilon\rightarrow 0
(with
\epsilon=\sigma_{x}^{2}=\sigma_{y}^{2}
) to a “cross domain Laplacian operator”. The functions
f({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})
and
g({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}})
converge to eigenfunctions of
{\widehat{P}}
. These functions
are the solutions of the following diffusion-like equations:

\displaystyle(\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}f)({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})

\displaystyle=g({\mbox{\boldmath$\beta(x_{i})$}})+{{\epsilon}}\triangle\gamma({\mbox{\boldmath$\beta({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})$}})/\alpha({\mbox{\boldmath$\beta(x_{i})$}})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2}),

(45)

\displaystyle(\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$P$}}}g)({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i})

\displaystyle=f({\mbox{\boldmath$\beta^{-1}$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}))+{{\epsilon}}\triangle\eta({\mbox{\boldmath$\beta^{-1}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i})$}})/\alpha({\mbox{\boldmath$\beta(y_{i})$}}+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3/2}),

(46)

where the functions

are defined as

.

The proof of theorem

is deferred to the Appendix. Although the interpretation of the result is hardly intuitive, it provides evidence that the mapping

and

are indeed coupled and are related to the second derivative on the manifold. However, the distribution of the points
\alpha
has an impact on the mapping, which we hope to reduce in future research.

5.7
The convergence rate

In Theorem
7
, we assume the number of data points
M\rightarrow\infty
while the scale parameter
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. In practice we cannot expect to have an infinite number of data points. It was shown in
[
5
,
46
]
(and elsewehere) that a single-view graph Laplacian converges to the laplacian operator on a manifold. It is demonstrated in
[
47
,
48
]
that the variance of the error for such an operator decreases as
M\rightarrow\infty
, but increases as
\epsilon\rightarrow 0
. The study in
[
47
]
proves that for a uniform distribution of data points, the variance of the error is bounded by
\mathcal{O}(\frac{1}{M^{1/2}\epsilon^{1+d/4}},\epsilon^{1/2})
. This bound was improved in
[
48
]
by an asymptotic factor of
\sqrt{\epsilon}
based on the correlation between
{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}^{-1}
and
K
.

We now turn our attention to the variance of the multi-view kernel for a finite number of points. Given
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},....,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{M}
independent uniformly distributed data points sampled from a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, define the multi-view Parzen Window density estimator by

\dot{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}_{M,\epsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\triangle}}{{=}}\frac{1}{M^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{M}\sum^{M}_{j=1}\frac{1}{\epsilon^{d}}K\left(\frac{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{\ell}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)K\left(\frac{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{\ell}-{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right).

(47)

We are interested in finding a bound for the variance of
\dot{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}_{M,\epsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})
for a finite number of data points:

\mathop{\rm var}\nolimits(\dot{{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}}_{M,\epsilon}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}))=\frac{1}{{M}^{4}\epsilon^{2d}}\cdot M\cdot\mathop{\rm var}\nolimits\left(\sum^{M}_{\ell}K\left(\frac{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}-{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{\ell}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)K\left(\frac{{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{\ell}-{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{j}}{\sqrt{\epsilon}}\right)\right)\\
\leq\frac{1}{{M}^{4}\epsilon^{2d}}\cdot M^{3}\cdot\mathop{\rm var}\nolimits\left(K^{x}_{\epsilon}K^{y}_{\epsilon}\right)\leq\frac{1}{{M}\epsilon^{2d}}\big[\mathop{\rm var}\nolimits\big(K^{x}_{\epsilon}\big)\cdot||K^{y}_{\epsilon}\left|{}_{\infty}+\mathop{\rm var}\nolimits\big(K^{y}_{\epsilon}\big)\cdot||K^{x}_{\epsilon}\right|_{\infty}\big]\\
\leq\frac{1}{{M}\epsilon^{2d}}\cdot[{\epsilon^{d/2}}\cdot m_{1}\cdot 1+\epsilon^{d/2}\cdot m_{2}\cdot\alpha({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})\big]\leq\frac{m_{1}+m_{2}\cdot\alpha({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}})}{M\cdot\epsilon^{1.5d}},

(48)

where the constants
m_{1}
and
m_{2}
are functions of the chosen kernels and of the pdf
\alpha(\cdot)
of the points
{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i},i=1,...,M
. This bound helps to choose an optimal value for the scaling factor
\epsilon
given the number of data points
M
and the intrinsic dimension
d
.

5.8
Generalized multi-view kernel

One can consider a more general multi-view kernel which does not preclude a transition within views
X
and
Y
in each time step. Such a kernel will take the form

{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\widehat{K}}$}}=\begin{bmatrix}\eta\cdot({\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x})^{2}&(1-\eta)\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}\\
(1-\eta)\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}&\eta\cdot({\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y})^{2}\end{bmatrix},

(49)

where the parameter
\eta\in[0,1]
prescribes the (implied) probability of within-view transitions. This kernel is normalized using the sum of rows diagonal matrix
\widehat{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}}
, such that
{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\widehat{P}}$}}=\widehat{\widehat{{\mbox{\boldmath$D$}}}}^{-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$\widehat{\widehat{K}}$}}
.
For large values of
\eta
, the kernel favors the within-view transitions, thereby sharing characteristics with the single-view diffusion process. For small values of
\eta
, the kernel tends to behave like our multi-view kernel
\widehat{K}
(Eq. (
13
)).

5.9
Additive noise

In this sub section we explore the effect of noise by following the analysis presented in
[
18
]
: Assuming that the noise is additive, we evaluate how the proposed method preserves the statistics of the latent model parameters
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Theta$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times M}
. We begin by considering the case of a single-view linear model, then consider a multi-view (linear model) and finally use the kernel trick to relax the linearity assumption.

The single-view linear model is defined by
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{i},i=1,...,M
, where the vectors
{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},i=1,...,M
(columns of
\Theta
) describe latent parameters, modeled as i.i.d., zero-mean random vectors with a finite covariance matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}}
, and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
are i.i.d., zero-mean random “noise" vectors with a finite covariance matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{\xi}
. The observations are
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
and the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
has full column rank.

A standard approach for reducing the dimension is to apply PCA. However, in the presence of noise, even if the covariance of
\Theta
is full rank PCA will provide a biased representation of
\Theta
. In applying PCA to the set
X
, one first computes the sample covariance
\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}^{T}
, which at the limit
M\rightarrow\infty
converges to
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}}{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}^{T}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}}
. This means that using the top principal components of
X
embeds the data into a biased representation of
\Theta
. Nonetheless, by introducing a second set of measurements
{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}+{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i},i=1,...,M
, where
{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}\in\mathbb{R}^{D\times d}
is another full-rank matrix and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}
are i.i.d. zero-mean noise vectors which are all uncorrelated with the respective
{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{i}
(namely,
E\left[{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{i}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}^{\text{T}}\right]={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},i=1,...,M
), one can remove the bias term. Essentially, the corresponding additional measurements, along with the noise decorrelation assumption, provide the information required for retrieving an unbiased representation of
\Theta
. Removing the bias term is possible by applying CCA
[
49
]
to
X
and
Y
. CCA not only removes the bias, but enables to uncover a reduced representation that preserves the statistics of
\theta
. This is because the sample cross covariance of
X
and
Y
is an unbiased estimate of
{\mbox{\boldmath$A$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}}{\mbox{\boldmath$B$}}^{T}
. The top
d
right- and left-singular vectors of
\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$XY$}}^{T}
are used to embed the data into a
d
dimensional space, such that the statistics of
\Theta
are preserved. By denoting the top
d
singular vectors as
(\hat{{\mbox{\boldmath$U$}}},{\mbox{\boldmath$S$}},\hat{{\mbox{\boldmath$V$}}}^{T})=\text{SVD}_{d}(\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$XY$}}^{T})
, the reduced representations are defined as
{\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{U}$}}^{T}{\mbox{\boldmath$X$}}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{V}$}}^{T}{\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}}
, respectively.

Although CCA is an effective, powerful tool in this context, it is based on a linear model, and thus limited to linear transformations of the data. A widely used solution to capture non-linear relations in the data are Kernel matrices
[
50
,
51
,
52
]
. Using a kernel matrix in the ambient space is a natural way to capture the sample-covariance in some unknown high-dimensional feature space
[
53
]
. Therefore, using a kernel generalizes the results provided by CCA to the nonlinear case. We now demonstrate how under mild assumptions the proposed kernel
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}={\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
mitigates the bias effect of the additive uncorrelated noise.

Assume that
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i},{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i},i=1,...,M
are noisy observations of the same low-dimensional latent variable
{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}
, such that
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$r$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i})+{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{i}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$h$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i})+{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}
. The functions
{\mbox{\boldmath$r,h$}}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The affinity values of
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x},{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
are the sample covariance in two high-dimensional feature spaces
\Gamma,\Delta
. The inaccesible feature maps for both views are defined by
{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i})
. This means that by computing the SVD of
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}={\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
, we would essentially be applying CCA in the inaccessible features spaces
\Gamma,\Delta
. This implies that if the following conditions hold

1.

The functions
{\mbox{\boldmath$r,h$}}\in\mathbb{C}^{\infty}
are invertible.

2.

The feature representations
\Gamma({\mbox{\boldmath$X$}})\text{ and }\Delta({\mbox{\boldmath$Y$}})
are centered.

3.

The noise terms are uncorrelated:
E\left[{\mbox{\boldmath$\xi$}}_{i}\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$\eta$}}_{i}^{\text{T}}\right]={\mbox{\boldmath$0$}},i=1,...,M
.

4.

E[{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i})|{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}]=a_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}+a_{1}
, with some real valued constants
a_{0}
and
a_{1}
.

5.

E[{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}({\mbox{\boldmath$y$}}_{i})|{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}]=b_{0}{\mbox{\boldmath$\theta$}}_{i}+b_{1}
, with some real valued constants
b_{0}
and
b_{1}
.

then, applying SVD to
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}
enables to cancel out the view-specific noise terms. Condition (1) implies that the latent parameters
\Theta
lie on a noisy manifold in both observed spaces. Based on condition (2) and on the choice of kernels, the empirical covariance matrices in the feature space are
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}=\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma\Gamma$}}^{T}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}=\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta\Delta$}}^{T}
. We remind that
\Gamma
and
\Delta
are inaccessible and the kernels are computed based on
X
and
Y
. As in the linear case, using
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}
or
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}
alone is insufficient for obtaining an unbiased estimate of the representation of
\Theta
.

We now turn our attention to demonstrate that the matrix
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}={\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{x}{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{y}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma\Gamma^{T}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta\Delta^{T}$}}
enables to remove the uncorrelated additive noise. A right eigenvector of
{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}
, denoted by
{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i}
, satisfies

{\mbox{\boldmath$K$}}^{z}{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i}=\frac{1}{M^{2}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma\Gamma^{T}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta\Delta^{T}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i}=\lambda_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i},

(50)

by multiplying Eq.
50
on the left by
{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}}^{T}
we get

\frac{1}{M^{2}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}}^{T}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma\Gamma^{T}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta\Delta^{T}$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i}=\lambda_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}}^{T}{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i}.

(51)

Substituting
{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{i}={\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta$}}^{T}{\mbox{\boldmath$\tau$}}_{i}
,
\bar{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}}=\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Gamma^{T}\Delta$}}
and
\bar{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}}=\frac{1}{M}{\mbox{\boldmath$\Delta^{T}\Gamma$}}
yields

\bar{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}}\bar{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{i}=\lambda_{i}{\mbox{\boldmath$u$}}_{i},

(52)

so up to scaling the left- and right-singular vectors of
\bar{{\mbox{\boldmath$\Sigma$}}}_{{\mbox{\boldmath$\gamma$}}{\mbox{\boldmath$\delta$}}}
provide a low-dimensional representation that captures the statistics of
\Theta
. Thus, based on conditions (3-5), by taking the number of points to infinity, one can extract an unbiased estimate of the representation of
\Theta
.

7.2
Learning from multi sensor seismic data

Automatic detection and identification of seismic events is an important task, it is carried out constantly for seismic and nuclear monitoring. The monitoring process results in a seismic event bulletin that contains information about the detected events, their locations, magnitudes and type (natural or man made event). Seismic stations usually consist of multiple sensors recording continuously at a low frequency. The amount of available data is huge and only a fraction of the recordings contains the signal of interest. Thus, automatic tools for monitoring are of great interest. A suspect event is usually identified based on the energy of the signal, then, typical discrimination algorithms extract seismic parameters. A simple seismic parameter is the focal depth. Its drawback is that its estimation is usually inaccurate without the depth phases. Other widely used seismic discrimination methods are Ms:mb (surface wave magnitude versus body wave magnitude) and spectral amplitude ratios of different seismic phases
[
67
]

[
68
]
. Current automatic seismic bulletins comprise a large number of false alarms, which have to be manually corrected by an analyst.

In this subsection we apply the proposed method to extract essential latent seismic parameters. A suspected event is identified based on a short and long time average ratio (STA/LTA)
[
69
]
. Then, a time-frequency representation is computed to which multi-view is applied to fuse the data from multiple seismic sensors. Using the multi-view low-dimensional embedding and simple classifiers, we demonstrate capabilities classification of event type (earthquakes vs. explosions) and quarry source for explosions.

7.2.1

The dataset consists of recordings from two different broad band seismic stations MMLI (Malkishua) and HRFI (Harif). Both stations are operated by the Geophysical Institute of Israel (GII) and they are part of the Israel National Seismic Network
[

]
. MMLI and HRFI stations are located to the north and to the south of the analyzed region, respectively. Each station is equipped with a three component STS-2 seismometer, thus the total number of views is
L=6
. All recording are sampled at

Hz. The HRFI data includes
1654

7.2.2
Feature Extraction by Normalized Sonograms

A seismic event typically generates two underground traveling waves. A primary wave (P) and secondary wave (S). The two waves (P-S) arrive at the recording station with some time delay. A time frequency representation of the recording captures the spectral properties of the event while maintaining the P-S time gap. Here we use a time frequency representation termed sonogram
[
71
]
with some modifications. Each single-trace seismic waveform, denoted by
y[n]\in\mathbb{R}^{{N}}
is a time series signal sampled at the rate of
F_{s}=40
Hz. The waveform
y[n]
is decomposed into a set of overlapping windows of length

Discussion

We presented a multi-view based framework for
dimensionality reduction. The framework enables
to extract simultaneous embeddings from coupled embeddings. Our approach is based on imposing an implied cross-domain transition in each single time-step.
The transition probabilities depend on the connectivities in both
views. We reviewed various theoretical aspects of the proposed method
and demonstrated their applicability to both synthetic and real
data. The experimental results demonstrate the strength of the
proposed framework in cases where data is missing in each view or
each of the manifolds is deformed by an unknown function. The
framework is applicable to various real life machine learning tasks
that consist of multiple views or multiple modalities.
