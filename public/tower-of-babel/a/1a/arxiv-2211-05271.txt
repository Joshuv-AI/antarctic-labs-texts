Quantum circuits for discrete-time quantum walks with position-dependent coin operator
Ugo Nzongani, Julien Zylberman, Carlo-Elia Doncecchi, Armando Pérez, Fabrice Debbasch, Pablo Arnault

Abstract
The aim of this paper is to build quantum circuits that implement discrete-time quantum walks having an arbitrary position-dependent coin operator. The position of the walker is encoded in base 2 2 : with n n wires, each corresponding to one qubit, we encode 2 n 2^{n} position states. The data necessary to define an arbitrary position-dependent coin operator is therefore exponential in n n . Hence, the exponentiality will necessarily appear somewhere in our circuits. We first propose a circuit implementing the position-dependent coin operator, that is naive, in the sense that it has exponential depth and implements sequentially all appropriate position-dependent coin operators. We then propose a circuit that “transfers” all the depth into ancillae, yielding a final depth that is linear in n n at the cost of an exponential number of ancillae. The main idea of this linear-depth circuit is to implement in parallel all coin operators at the different positions. Reducing the depth exponentially at the cost of having an exponential number of ancillae is a goal which has already been achieved for the problem of loading classical data on a quantum circuit [ 1 ] (notice that such a circuit can be used to load the initial state of the walker). Here, we achieve this goal for the problem of applying a position-dependent coin operator in a discrete-time quantum walk. Finally, we extend the result of Ref. [ 2 ] from position-dependent unitaries which are diagonal in the position basis to position-dependent 2 × 2 2\times 2 -block-diagonal unitaries: indeed, we show that for a position dependence of the coin operator (the block-diagonal unitary) which is smooth enough, one can find an efficient quantum-circuit implementation approximating the coin operator up to an error ϵ \epsilon (in terms of the spectral norm), the depth and size of which scale as O ⁡ ( 1 / ϵ ) O(1/\epsilon) . A typical application of the efficient implementation would be the quantum simulation of a relativistic spin-1/2 particle on a lattice, coupled to a smooth external gauge field; notice that recently, quantum spatial-search schemes have been developed which use gauge fields as the oracle, to mark the vertex to be found [ 3 , 4 ] . A typical application of the linear-depth circuit would be when there is spatial noise on the coin operator (and hence a non-smooth dependence in the position).

There are two reasons why we focus on DQWs and do not treat CQWs in this work.
First, DQWs can be used to simulate the Dirac equation
while preserving the strict locality of the transport
, and it is often in that perspective that we are interested in DQWs.
Indeed, from one instant to the next one, a DQW transports the probability amplitude at some position towards adjacent positions,
within a bounded neighborhood around the original position
, and this creates, at the discrete level, an equivalent of the continuum lightcone of relativistic physics, which is exhibited in particular by the Dirac equation.
In contrast, CQWs are nothing but tight-binding-like Hamiltonians on lattices, in continuous time, and as such they do not exhibit a strict lightcone as DQWs, they only have an approximate lightcone via Lieb-Robinson bounds.
An example of such a CQW is the Kogut-Susskind Hamiltonian, which is a discretization of the Dirac Hamiltonian.
Again, such CQW discretizations are structurally fundamentally different from DQW discretizations, since they do not respect the strict locality of the continuum Dirac equation.
The second reason why we focus on DQWs and do not treat CQWs in this work is the following.
DQWs are governed (in addition to the kind of lattice on which they are evolving), by a fundamental and versatile piece: the coin operator.
This coin operator can be tailored at will, at each time step, thus allowing the DQW to reproduce a large variety of phenomena, such as couplings of the matter field (the walker) to electromagnetic fields, gravitational fields, or such as localization effects arising from an external potential.
In particular, in the case of gravitational couplings, the point is that the spatial and temporal variations of the metric are encoded in the coin operator solely, on a fixed regular lattice, i.e., with no need of considering a curved lattice.
For CQWs, simulating such a phenomenon may imply a specific design of the graph geometry, which, again, is not needed with DQWs.
We do not exclude the possibility of engineering appropriate CQWs but, again, the general point we want to make is that, to the best of our knowledge, such a degree of plasticity in simulations has not been achieved by CQWs.

In order to realize DQWs on quantum computers, the first task is to efficiently translate the DQW into a quantum circuit; indeed, the programs of quantum computers are designed within the quantum circuit model, so that the Hilbert space is that of
n

With

The DQW is made of two operations: one operation implements the coin-dependent shift, and the other implements the coin rotation; we recall these facts in Sec.

For the quantum-circuit implementation of the coin-dependent shift operation, see Sec.

Now, to date, all works proposing quantum circuits implementing DQWs, choose a uniform coin operator, i.e., a coin operator which does not depend on the position of the walker.
However, many applications of DQWs to physical simulations need to introduce a position-dependent coin operator, see above and in Sec.
VIII

The purpose of this work is to design quantum circuits for the implementation of coin operators depending arbitrarily on the position of the walker.
Since there is one different coin operator per lattice position, the number of parameters of a position-dependent coin operator is linear in

III.2
Comments on other existing schemes

On the one hand, one needs the circuit depth to be as small as possible,
as we are limited by the decoherence time of the qubits. On the other
hand, we also want to reduce the number of 2-qubit gates (and of course, more generally, of multi-qubit gates) because:
i) these gates have higher error rates, and ii) more importantly,
because if the qubits that are involved in a 2-qubit gate are not
directly linked in the chip, they must be moved to adjacent positions (usually
using SWAPs) which increases the number of gates and the total error
rate.
More generally, we want to decompose any multi-qubit unitary operation into a minimum number of one-qubit and two-qubit gates.

Many circuit implementations of the shift operator
S^{(n)}
make use of the
increment and decrement (ID) scheme introduced in Ref.
[
80
]
,
which is explained in Appendix
A
.
Following Ref.
[
70
]
, one can show that for the ID scheme one needs a circuit of size
n(2n^{2}-6n+7)/3
and
depth
2(2n^{2}-8n+9)
.
Alternatively,
n-3
extra ancillary qubits
can be used, so that the circuit size becomes
10n^{2}-50n+67
and the depth
2(4n^{2}-20n+27)
.
As compared to these quantities, the QFT scheme (which we have described above in Sec.
III.1
) makes use of a circuit of size
n^{2}+4n+1
and depth

2n+3
for the case of
N=2^{n}
lattice sites.
Thus, the use of
the QFT scheme represents a clear advantage.

In Ref.
[
71
]
, a different scheme is introduced that
uses rotations around the basis states.
They conclude that the number
of gates needed by their scheme increases exponentially
faster than for the generalized ID scheme (the one which
includes ancillary qubits) – although they also point out that the latter scheme anyways quickly demands a number of qubits that surpasses that available in near-term quantum machines.
Let us also mention that just modifying the digital mapping of the position space might be used to reduce the required gate count for implementing DQWs, as illustrated by some examples in Ref.
[
83
]
.

V
Linear-depth quantum circuit for position-dependent coin operator

The depth of the naive circuit presented Sec.
IV
above is exponential in the number of position wires.
Since the coherence time of the qubits is a high limiting factor in actual implementations of quantum circuits, it is known that whenever possible it is preferable to reduce the depth of a circuit at the cost of adding ancillary qubits
4
4

4

That being said, such a way to go implies that we need to handle many more entangled qubits; ultimately, this is only completely guaranteed if error correction is available.
.
In this section, we describe a quantum circuit

implementing the position-dependent coin operator

, which has a linear depth in the number

of position wires, at the cost of introducing an exponential number of ancillary wires and, correspondingly, an exponential number of gates.

Adding the ancillary wires

V.2
Main idea and definition of

The main idea of the circuit we want to build is to apply all the coin operators

This is why we have added

Now, the application of

\mathinner{|{k}\rangle}

This is why we have added

on the coin wire number

All these 1-controlled coin operations can thus be applied in parallel, and they form the central operation of our circuit, which we call

The explicit definition of

Q_{0}\vcentcolon=I_{2^{n}}\otimes\left(\sideset{}{{}^{L}}{\bigotimes}_{k=0}^{2^{n}-1}K_{b^{\prime}_{k},s_{k}}(C_{k})\right)\,,

\mathinner{|{b^{\prime}_{k}}\rangle}

\mathinner{|{s_{k}}\rangle}

5

5

We give this precision to keep coherence with how we handle the Kronecker product in this paper, but actually here in Eq. (

The big tensor product of Eq. (

In Eq. (

V.3
Initialization of the ancillae via

In order to apply

is the number of position qubits, and

Such coin operators with smooth position dependence are particularly relevant for both (i) DQWs coupled to gauge fields
[

,

,

,

,

[

]
.

VI.1
Introduction: decomposition of an arbitrary position-dependent coin operator

As above, the position-dependent coin operator is given by Eq. (
8
), and it is known that one can parametrize the generic position-dependent
2\times 2
unitary matrix
\tilde{C}_{k_{2}}
with the Euler angles and a global phase as

\displaystyle\tilde{C}_{k_{2}}

\displaystyle\equiv e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha(k_{2})}\begin{bmatrix}e^{\mathrm{i}\xi(k_{2})}\cos\theta(k_{2})&e^{\mathrm{i}\zeta(k_{2})}\sin\theta(k_{2})\\
-e^{-\mathrm{i}\zeta(k_{2})}\sin\theta(k_{2})&e^{-\mathrm{i}\xi(k_{2})}\cos\theta(k_{2})\end{bmatrix}

(34a)

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}\alpha(k_{2})}e^{\mathrm{i}(\xi(k_{2})+\zeta(k_{2}))\sigma^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}\theta(k_{2})\sigma^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}(\xi(k_{2})-\zeta(k_{2}))\sigma^{3}}

(34b)

\displaystyle=e^{\mathrm{i}F_{0}(k_{2})}e^{\mathrm{i}F_{1}(k_{2})\sigma^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}F_{2}(k_{2})\sigma^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}F_{3}(k_{2})\sigma^{3}}\,,

(34c)

where
\sigma^{2}\equiv Y

\sigma^{3}\equiv Z
is the third Pauli matrix, and
F_{0}\vcentcolon=\alpha
,
F_{1}\vcentcolon=\xi+\zeta
,
F_{2}\vcentcolon=\theta
, and
F_{3}\vcentcolon=\xi-\zeta
.

For technical reasons, we assume that we have first encoded
k_{2}
,
k=0,...,2^{n}-1

, via a dyadic expansion, that is,

x_{k}\vcentcolon=g(k_{2})\vcentcolon=\sum_{p=0}^{n-1}b_{p}/2^{p+1}\,,

(35)

where we recall that
\mathinner{|{k_{2}}\rangle}\equiv\mathinner{|{b_{n-1}...b_{0}}\rangle}

[0,1]

F_{i}(k_{2})\equiv f_{i}(g(k_{2}))\equiv f_{i}(x_{k})\,.

(36)

We can hence write
\tilde{C}_{k_{2}}
in Eq. (

\tilde{C}_{k_{2}}=e^{\mathrm{i}f_{0}(x_{k})}e^{\mathrm{i}f_{1}(x_{k})\sigma^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}f_{2}(x_{k})\sigma^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}f_{3}(x_{k})\sigma^{3}}

and the total coin operator

C^{(n)}=e^{\mathrm{i}\hat{f}_{0}\otimes I_{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\hat{f}_{1}\otimes\sigma^{3}}e^{\mathrm{i}\hat{f}_{2}\otimes\sigma^{2}}e^{\mathrm{i}\hat{f}_{3}\otimes\sigma^{3}}\,,

(37)

where the

are operators acting on the position Hilbert space which are diagonal in the position basis, such that

\hat{f}_{i}\mathinner{|{k_{2}}\rangle}=f_{i}(x_{k})\mathinner{|{k_{2}}\rangle}\,.

(38)

Let us now define

C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}\vcentcolon=e^{\mathrm{i}\hat{f}\otimes\sigma}\equiv\begin{bmatrix}e^{\mathrm{i}f(x_{0})\sigma}&0&\cdots&0\\
0&e^{\mathrm{i}f(x_{2})\sigma}&\cdots&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&e^{\mathrm{i}f(x_{2^{n}-1})\sigma}\end{bmatrix}\,,

(39)

where
\hat{f}
is, as above, a diagonal operator in position space associated to the function
f
, and
\sigma

2\times 2

One can rewrite
C^{(n)}
in Eq. (
37

C^{(n)}=C^{(n)}_{f_{0},I_{2}}C^{(n)}_{f_{1},\sigma^{3}}C^{(n)}_{f_{2},\sigma^{2}}C^{(n)}_{f_{3},\sigma^{3}}

We thus focus on constructing an efficient quantum circuit for

.

VI.2
Approximate efficient quantum circuit for block-diagonal unitaries of the form
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}

The case
\sigma=I_{2}
, and, more generally, the case of diagonal unitaries with smooth variations, has already been derived by J. Welch et al.
[
2
]
.
Following their method, we generalize their result to block-diagonal unitaries of the form of
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
in Eq. (
39
), with a smooth-enough
f
, by using the development of
f
into a so-called Walsh series.

The proof holds in Appendices
F.1
and
F.2
.
In Appendix
F.1
we first provide an exact circuit for
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
as the product
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}U_{j}
, depending on the coefficients
a_{j}
of the decomposition of
f
as a Walsh series.
The depth of such a circuit is, as the previous product formula reminds, exponential in
n
.
Now, in Appendix
F.2
we explain that we can truncate the previous product at some
j=2^{m}
such that the resulting size and depth of the approximating circuit
C^{(n)}_{f_{m},\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}U_{j}
scale as
O(1/\epsilon)
, where
\epsilon
depends on
m
and is, in terms of the spectral norm, the error committed when approximating
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
by
C^{(n)}_{f_{m},\sigma}
, and where we have introduced the truncation
f_{m}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}a_{j}w_{j}
of the development of
f
into a Walsh series.
This implementation is efficient according to the standard definition,
[
82
]
since the scaling of the circuit,
O(1/\epsilon)
, satisfies the condition
1/\epsilon=O(\text{poly}(n,1/\epsilon))
.
Now, it is trivial to realize from the above explanations that the condition for the approximating circuit to be efficient is
m\ll n
, which implies (see Appendix
F.2
) a smoothness condition on
f
, namely,

\text{sup}_{x\in[0,1]}f^{\prime}(x)\ll\epsilon 2^{n}\,.

(40)

Notice that
\epsilon
depends on
m
and so is independent of
n
only up to a certain extent, namely, we do need the condition
m\ll n
to be satisfied.

A final comment is in order here.
If we do not truncate the Walsh series, the full Walsh decomposition of the coin operator, namely,
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}U_{j}
, provides a quantum-circuit implementation of the coin operator the depth and size of which scale as
O(2^{n})
.
A priori, this implementation actually outperforms polynomially the naive implementation that we have presented earlier in Sec.
IV
, since the depth and size of the latter scale as
O(\text{poly}(n)\times 2^{n})
, where the
\text{poly}(n)
comes from the fact that we have to decompose the multiply-controlled gates into one- and two-qubit gates.
That being said, the naive circuit is still interesting because it is more intuitive than the Walsh-decomposition circuit, since it makes no use of the Walsh series, which is a rather technical tool.

VIII
Conclusions and discussion

In this paper we have reported on the quantum-circuit implementation of position-dependent coin operators in DQWs.
In Sec.
II
, we have recalled the definition of the DQW, made of the concatenation of a coin-dependent shift operator and a coin operator.
In Sec.
III
, we have recalled the QFT scheme quantum-circuit implementation of the coin-dependent shift operator, presented in Ref.
[
70
]
, and demanding less resources than the ID scheme of Ref.
[
80
]
.
Then we have proceeded to the presentation of the results of this work.
In Sec.
IV
, we have presented a certain quantum circuit implementing a position-dependent coin operator, the depth of which is exponential in the number of position wires
n
, but which uses no ancillary qubits.
This is because in this circuit we apply sequentially the coin operators at each position, which is why we call this the naive circuit.
In Sec.
V
, we present a new quantum circuit implementing a position-dependent coin operator, the depth of which is this time linear in
n
, at the cost of introducing an exponential number of ancillary wires.
The main idea of this linear-depth circuit is to implement in parallel (rather than sequentially) all coin operators at the different positions.
The linear-depth circuit is made of three blocks: we first initialize ancillary wires with the information about the position of the walker, we then initialize other ancillary wires with the information about the coin state at each position, and we finally apply all coin operators in parallel, after which we undo both initializations.
Finally, in Sec.
VI
, we have extended the result of Ref.
[
2
]
from position-dependent unitaries which are diagonal in the position basis to position-dependent
block-diagonal
unitaries.
Indeed, we have shown that for a position dependence of the coin operator (the block-diagonal unitary) which is smooth enough, one can find an efficient quantum-circuit implementation approximating the coin operator up to an error
\epsilon
(in terms of the spectral norm), the depth and size of which scale as
O(1/\epsilon)
.
In Sec.
VII
, we have implemented the naive, the linear-depth, and the Walsh circuits, on the classical simulator of IBM’s quantum processors, to give an idea of the results one obtains for a small number of position qubits.

We are currently working on a circuit the depth of which is tunable in an exponential manner, in between an exponential depth and a linear depth: that is, if the depth of the naive circuit is proportional to
2^{n}
where
n
is the number of position wires, then we want that a depth with any power
2^{p}
of
2
be achievable by our new circuit, at the cost of introducing the right amount of ancillae, which would grow exponentially as the depth diminishes exponentially.
This is reminiscent of Ref.
[
81
]
for quantum state preparation, where a trade-off between the depth and the ancillae is proposed.

Let us now mention some possible applications of our findings.
During the NISQ era, an efficient design of the quantum circuit implementing the desired DQWs is crucial in order to make use of their potential as quantum simulators.
Our proposed
linear-depth implementation
of the position-dependent coin operator, as well as the
efficient implementation
of the smooth position-dependent coin operator, constitute a step forward in this direction, and pave the way to simulating many interesting physical scenarios.
A first example of application of our circuits is the implementation of DQWs coupled to various gauge fields, either electromagnetic
[
29
,
30
,
31
,
47
,
48
]
, non-Abelian Yang-Mills
[
32
]
, or gravitational
[
33
,
34
,
35
,
37
]
, for which the efficient implementation is likely to be possible.
Notice that recently, quantum spatial-search schemes have been developed which use gauge fields as the oracle, to mark the vertex to be found
[
3
,
4
]
.
A second example is the implementation of DQWs with certain random coin operators, which leads to diffusion
[
74
,
75
]
.
A third example is that of DQWs leading to localization, either when spatial disorder is included
[
84
,
85
,
64
]
, or due to non-linear effects
[
86
]
(where soliton-like structures appear), or by the use of a spatially periodic coin operator
[
87
]
.
Notice that in the case of spatial disorder, i.e., spatial noise, on the coin operator, the efficient implementation will not be possible anymore, but we can use the linear-depth one.
Interestingly enough, localization can also appear as a consequence of the interaction with a smooth external potential, instead of a random, or even periodic perturbation: indeed, such a localization has been evidenced in the DQW simulation of a spin-1/2 particle in extra physical dimensions
[
40
,
88
]
.
Finally, neutrino oscillations in matter can also be simulated with the help of DQWs with a position-dependent coin operator
[
38
]
.
Most probably, the above examples do not cover all the possibilities that will appear in the future.

Finally, let us comment on the experimental requirements of the circuits we propose in this article.
Let us start with general comments.
In order to realize many steps of the DQW, one needs a large scalability of the experimental platform, i.e., one needs the capacity to conceive an experimental setup that allows for exploring a large number of steps, i.e., a graph with many nodes (in this work, the graph is the 1D line, with a given number of nodes on this line).
Moreover, for the experimental final probability distribution to match the theoretical one, one needs noise to be as reduced as possible.
Let us now give more precision on the noise requirements.
Let us consider that we want to implement experimentally the DQW of this paper over 8 nodes on the line.
What is the number of one- and two-qubit gates that one needs in order to implement an arbitrary position-dependent coin operator over 8 nodes (for an arbitrary initial state)?
We recall that this is the number of gates after the compilation, which has been done with the gates
R_{X}
,
R_{Y}
,
R_{Z}
,
P
and
CNOT
.
For the linear-depth circuit, this number of gates is
N_{\text{L}}^{\text{coin}}(8)=591

this gate count has been obtained for a position-dependent coin operator as arbitrary as possible, that is, of the form of Eq. (
42

9
.
For the Walsh circuit, this number of gates is, for a null error between the circuit we implement and the exact position-dependent coin operator,
N_{\text{W}}^{\text{coin}}(8)=103

34

7

For

Moreover, let

We have that

Let
\varepsilon

Since the total error of a circuit is the addition of the errors of each one- and two-qubit gate

Let us prove by induction on

Let us start by

69a

Let us also start with a state
\mathinner{|{K}\rangle}
of the form of Eq. (
65

After the

Now, if

Let us now assume that Eq. (

Let us consider situation (i).
We have that

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(n+1)}_{1}\mathinner{|{K}\rangle}

\displaystyle\equiv B^{(n+1)}\mathbf{Q}^{(n)}_{1}\mathinner{|{K}\rangle}

(71a)

\displaystyle=B^{(n+1)}\left(\mathinner{|{k_{2}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{s_{0}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{s^{\prime}=0}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(2^{k})_{2}}\rangle}\right)\,,

where the first equality (
71a
) holds by definition of
\mathbf{Q}^{(n+1)}_{1}
in Eq. (
69b
), and the second equality (
71b
) holds by induction assumption (it is trivial that this assumption holds when augmenting the Hilbert space from

We thus now have to prove that

We have that

k=\underbrace{1}_{=b_{n}}\times 2^{n}+l\,,

(73)

Now,

\mathinner{|{l}\rangle}
on the ancillary positions, that is,

\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{(n+1)}_{1}\mathinner{|{K}\rangle}

\displaystyle\equiv B^{(n+1)}\mathbf{Q}^{(n)}_{1}\left(\mathinner{|{(2^{n}+l)_{2}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{s_{0}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{s^{\prime}=0}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(b^{\prime}=0}\rangle}\right)

\displaystyle=B^{(n+1)}\left(\mathinner{|{(2^{n}+l)_{2}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{s_{0}}\rangle}\mathinner{|{s^{\prime}=0}\rangle}\mathinner{|{(2^{l})_{2}}\rangle}\right)\,,

(74b)

where the first equality (
74a
) holds by definition of
\mathbf{Q}^{(n+1)}_{1}
in Eq. (
69b
), and the second equality (
74b
) holds by induction assumption as we have just explained above.
We now have to prove that

swaps the

\displaystyle\ \ \,J_{m}^{(0)}\vcentcolon=K_{b_{m},s_{l_{m}}}(X)

(79a)

\displaystyle J_{m}^{(i\geq 1)}\vcentcolon=

\displaystyle\left(\sideset{}{{}^{L}}{\prod}_{l^{\prime}=0}^{2^{i}-2}K_{s_{l_{m}+l^{\prime}},s_{l_{m}+l^{\prime}+2^{i}}}(X)\right)K_{b_{m},s_{l_{m}+\sum_{u=1}^{i}2^{u-1}}}(X)\,,

(79b)

and where we have defined

\displaystyle l_{0}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=1

(80a)

\displaystyle l_{m}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=2^{m-1}-1+l_{m-1}\ \ \text{for}\ m\geq 1\,.

(80b)

One can convince oneself that the copies operation (i) copies as many position qubits as needed on the ancillary coins, and (ii) is linear in

F.1

We follow the same computational steps as in Ref.
[

]
.
In this subappendix, we first derive an exact quantum circuit for

using the development of

F.1.1
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
as a product of Walsh terms
U_{j}

First, let us define the Walsh functions: for any natural number
j
and real number
x\in[0,1]
, the
j
th Walsh function at point
x
is

w_{j}(x)\vcentcolon=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}x_{i-1}}\,,

(90)

where
j_{i}
is the
i
th bit in the binary expansion
j=\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}2^{i-1}
, with
n
the most significant non-vanishing bit of
j
, and
x_{i}
the
i
th bit in the dyadic expansion
8
8

8

Here the sum of the dyadic expansion is up to
\infty
because we expand
any
real number in
[0,1]
; the sum is finite only when we encode powers of
2
.

x=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}x_{i}/2^{i+1}
.
The Walsh functions form a complete set of orthonormal functions.
For a finite number of points
x_{p}
one can define the discrete Walsh functions
w_{jp}\vcentcolon=w_{j}(x_{p})
.
The set of discrete Walsh functions is still complete and orthormal:
\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{p=0}^{2^{n}-1}w_{jp}w_{lp}=\delta_{jl}
and
\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}w_{jp}w_{jl}=\delta_{pl}
.
Thus, one can expand the
f(x_{p})
’s in terms of discrete Walsh functions as

f(x_{p})=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}a_{j}w_{jp}\,,

(91)

where the
a_{j}
are real numbers such that

a_{j}\vcentcolon=\frac{1}{2^{n}}\sum_{p=0}^{2^{n}-1}f(x_{p})w_{jp}\,.

(92)

The Walsh functions at a given position can only take the value
+1
or
-1
, making their associated Walsh operators a tensor product of
Z
Pauli operators.
Indeed, we define the Walsh operators as

\hat{w}_{j}=(Z_{n-1})^{j_{n}}\otimes\cdots\otimes(Z_{0})^{j_{1}}\,,

(93)

where
Z_{i}
is a third Pauli matrix applied on qubit
i
, and
j=0,\cdots,2^{n}-1
.
Therefore, for each real number
x_{p}=\sum_{i=0}^{n-1}b_{i}/2^{i+1}
, with
b_{i}\in\{0,1\}
, one has

\displaystyle\hat{w}_{j}\mathinner{|{k_{2}}\rangle}

\displaystyle\equiv\hat{w}_{j}\mathinner{|{b_{n-1}\cdots b_{0}}\rangle}

(94a)

\displaystyle=(-1)^{\sum_{i=1}^{n}j_{i}b_{i-1}}\mathinner{|{b_{n-1}\cdots b_{0}}\rangle}

(94b)

\displaystyle=w_{jp}\mathinner{|{k_{2}}\rangle}\,.

(94c)

The operator
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
, defined in Eq. (

\displaystyle C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}

\displaystyle\vcentcolon=e^{i\hat{f}\otimes\sigma}

(95a)

\displaystyle=e^{i\sum_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}a_{j}\hat{w}_{j}\otimes\sigma}

(95b)

\displaystyle=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}U_{j}\,,

(95c)

where

(96)

\forall j,j^{\prime}

F.1.2
Quantum circuits for the Walsh terms
U_{j}

Let us now derive the quantum circuits for the operators
U_{j}
.
Let us consider, in the expression the Walsh operators
\hat{w}_{j}
, Eq. (
93
), only the
Z
Pauli matrices on the relevant qubits (i.e., one for each
j_{i}=1
), and omit the other,
I_{2}
tensor factors (i.e., one for each
j_{i}=0
).
Let
r
be the number of such
Z
Pauli matrices.
Now, we first remark that every tensor product of a number
r
of
Z
Pauli matrices can be rewritten using CNOT matrices:

Z_{r-1}\otimes\cdots\otimes Z_{1}\otimes Z_{0}=A_{r}(I_{2^{(r-1)}}\otimes...\otimes I_{2^{(1)}}\otimes Z_{0})A_{r}^{-1}\,,

(97)

where
A_{r}\vcentcolon=CNOT_{1}^{0}\cdot CNOT_{2}^{0}...CNOT_{r-1}^{0}
and
CNOT_{i}^{j}
is the CNOT quantum gate controlled by qubit
i
and applied on qubit
j
.
Therefore, the operator
U_{j}
written as acting on the
r
relevant position qubits and the coin qubit, can be written in terms of quantum gates as

U_{j}=(A_{r}\otimes I_{2})(I_{2^{(r-1)}}\otimes...\otimes I_{2^{(1)}}\otimes e^{ia_{j}Z\otimes\sigma})(A_{r}^{-1}\otimes I_{2})\,.

(98)

F.1.3
Quantum circuits for the terms
e^{ia_{j}Z\otimes\sigma}

Let us finally derive a quantum circuit for each
e^{ia_{j}Z\otimes\sigma}
, for
\sigma=X,Y,Z
.
For
\sigma=Z
, the quantum circuit is simply

\displaystyle e^{ia_{j}Z\otimes Z}

\displaystyle=CNOT(I_{2}\otimes e^{ia_{j}Z})CNOT

(99a)

\displaystyle=CNOT(I_{2}\otimes R_{Z}(-2a_{j}))CNOT\,,

(99b)

where
R_{Z}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}e^{-i\frac{\theta}{2}}&0\\
0&e^{i\frac{\theta}{2}}\end{bmatrix}
.

For
\sigma=Y
:

\displaystyle e^{ia_{j}Z\otimes Y}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ia_{j}Y}&0\\
0&e^{-ia_{j}Y}\end{bmatrix}

(100a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ia_{j}Y}&0\\
0&e^{ia_{j}XYX}\end{bmatrix}

(100b)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ia_{j}Y}&0\\
0&Xe^{ia_{j}Y}X\end{bmatrix}

(100c)

\displaystyle=CNOT(I_{2}\otimes e^{ia_{j}Y})CNOT

(100d)

\displaystyle=CNOT(I_{2}\otimes R_{Y}(-2a_{j}))CNOT\,,

(100e)

where
R_{Y}(\theta)=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})&-\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\\
\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})&\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\end{bmatrix}
.

For
\sigma=X
:

\displaystyle e^{ia_{j}Z\otimes X}

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ia_{j}X}&0\\
0&e^{-ia_{j}X}\end{bmatrix}

(101a)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ia_{j}Y}&0\\
0&e^{ia_{j}ZXZ}\end{bmatrix}

(101b)

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}e^{ia_{j}X}&0\\
0&Ze^{ia_{j}X}Z\end{bmatrix}

(101c)

\displaystyle=\widehat{CZ}(I_{2}\otimes e^{ia_{j}X})\widehat{CZ}

(101d)

\displaystyle=\widehat{CZ}(I_{2}\otimes R_{X}(-2a_{j}))\widehat{CZ}\,,

(101e)

where
R_{X}(\theta)\vcentcolon=\begin{bmatrix}\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})&-i\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})\\
-i\sin(\frac{\theta}{2})&\cos(\frac{\theta}{2})\end{bmatrix}
and
\widehat{CZ}\vcentcolon=\text{diag}(1,1,1,-1)
is the controlled-
Z
gate.

Since the operators
U_{j}
commute with each other, the implementation of
C^{(n)}_{f_{m},\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}U_{j}
can be optimized by choosing the right sequence that cancels a maximum number of CNOT gates.
This can be done by using the Gray code
[
90
]
, resulting into only one CNOT gate per Walsh operator implemented and improving polynomialy the complexity of the scheme.
Such an optimization could perhaps also be done by the transpiler of the used quantum computer.

We have finally obtained an exact quantum circuit
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}U_{j}
, where the order of the terms in the product is determined by the previous optimization.

F.2
Approximation by truncation of the complete circuit, and related efficiency

F.2.1
Idea

The idea here is the following.
Instead of implementing the exact, complete decomposition
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}U_{j}
, the depth of which scales exponentially with
n
, we truncate the circuit up to some
j=2^{m}
, i.e., we approximate
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
by some truncation
C^{(n)}_{f_{m},\sigma}=\prod_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}U_{j}
, where we have introduced the truncation
f_{m}\vcentcolon=\sum_{j=0}^{2^{m}-1}a_{j}w_{j}
of the Walsh development of a function
f
up to the term
2^{m}
.
This is interesting only if
m
does not scale exponentially with
n
.
Notice that even if we truncate the
quantum circuit

C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
into
C^{(n)}_{f_{m},\sigma}
, we still need the full decomposition of the
function

f
in order to compute accurately the coefficients
a_{j}
of the decomposition.

F.2.2
Approximation error and smoothness condition

Let
\epsilon
be a positive real number.
One can show that
\sup_{x}|f(x)-f_{m}(x)|<\frac{\sup_{x\in[0,1]}f^{\prime}(x)}{2^{m}}

[
91
,
92
]
, where
x
is a continuous or discrete variable in
[0,1]
.
This implies that for
m
such that
\frac{\sup_{x\in[0,1]}f^{\prime}(x)}{2^{m}}=\epsilon
, the approximating coin operator
C^{(n)}_{f_{m},\sigma}
is close to the real coin operator
C^{(n)}_{f,\sigma}
up to
\epsilon
in terms of the spectral norm.
Therefore, only
2^{m}\propto 1/\epsilon
Walsh operators have to be implemented to approximate the coin operator up to
\epsilon
, resulting in a quantum circuit with a total number of one-qubit and two-qubit quantum gates of
O(1/\epsilon)
.
The condition for the quantum circuit to be efficient is
m\ll n
, i.e.,
2^{m}\ll 2^{n}
, i.e.,
\epsilon 2^{m}\ll\epsilon 2^{n}
, which, using the fact that
\sup_{x\in[0,1]}f^{\prime}(x)=\epsilon 2^{m}
, yields Eq. (
40
), which is a smoothness condition on
f
.
