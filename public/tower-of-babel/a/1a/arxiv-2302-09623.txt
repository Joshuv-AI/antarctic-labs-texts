1.
Preliminaries

We denote by
{\mathbb{D}}

the unit disk and
{\mathbb{T}}:=\partial{\mathbb{D}}
the
unit circle.
For
z=x+iy\in\mathbb{C}
, the two complex
differential operators are defined by

\partial_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}-i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right)~\mbox{and}~\overline{\partial}_{z}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+i\frac{\partial}{\partial y}\right).

Also, we denote by
h({\mathbb{D}})
(resp.
H({\mathbb{D}})
the set of harmonic (resp. analytic) functions on
{\mathbb{D}}
.

1.1.
Hardy and Bergman spaces

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
harmonic Hardy space

{h}^{p}(\mathbb{D})
consists of all
harmonic functions from
\mathbb{D}
to
\mathbb{C}
such that

M_{p}(r,f)
exists for all
r\in(0,1)
, and
\|f\|_{p}<\infty
,
where

M_{p}(r,f)=\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^{p}\,d\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

and

\|f\|_{p}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sup\{M_{p}(r,f):\;0<r<1\}&\mbox{if }p\in[1,\infty),\\
\displaystyle\sup\{|f(z)|:\;z\in\mathbb{D}\}&\mbox{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

The
analytic Hardy space

H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
is the set of all elements of
h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
which are analytic. (cf.
[
2
,
3
]
).

Denote by
L^{p}(\mathbb{T})~(p\in[1,\infty])
the space of all measurable functions
F

of
\mathbb{T}
into
\mathbb{C}
with

\|F\|_{L^{p}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|F(e^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\mbox{if }p\in[1,\infty),\\
\displaystyle\sup\{|F(e^{i\theta})|:\;\theta\in[0,2\pi)\}&\mbox{if }p=\infty.\end{cases}

For
z\in\mathbb{D}
, let

P(z)=\frac{1-|z|^{2}}{|1-z|^{2}}

be the
Poisson kernel
.
For a mapping
F\in L^{1}(\mathbb{T})
, the
Poisson integral
of
F

is defined by

f(z)=P[F](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}P(ze^{-i\theta})F(e^{i\theta})d\theta.

It is well known that a function
f
belongs to
h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
(
1<p\leq\infty
) if and only if
f=P[\phi]
, with
\phi\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
. And if
f=P[\phi]
,
\phi\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, then

\|f\|_{p}=\|\phi\|_{p},\quad(1\leq p\leq\infty),

and

f_{*}(e^{i\theta}):=\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}f(re^{i\theta})=\phi(e^{i\theta})\quad a.e.

Thus for
p\in(1,\infty]
,
P:L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})\to h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
is an isometry, see
[
2
,
4
,
7
]
.

For
p\in[1,\infty]
, the
Bergman space

B^{p}(\mathbb{D})
consists of all analytic functions
f:\;\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
such that

\|f\|_{b^{p}}=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\left(\int_{\mathbb{D}}|f(z)|^{p}d\sigma(z)\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}&\mbox{if }p\in[1,\infty),\\
\displaystyle\sup\{|f(z)|:\;z\in\mathbb{D}\}&\mbox{if }p=\infty,\end{cases}

where
d\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{\pi}dxdy
denotes the normalized Lebesgue area measure on
{\mathbb{D}}
. Obviously,
H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})\subset B^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
for each
p\in[1,\infty]
, (cf.
[
8
]
).

1.2.
Harmonic conjugates and M. Riesz theorem

If
f
is a harmonic function on
{\mathbb{D}}
given by

f(re^{i\theta})=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}c_{n}r^{|n|}e^{in\theta},

then its harmonic conjugate is defined by

\tilde{f}(re^{i\theta}):=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}m_{n}c_{n}r^{|n|}e^{in\theta},

where
m_{n}=-i\,{\rm sign}\,n
; in particular
\tilde{f}(0)=0
. In addition, we have

(1.1)

f(z)+i\tilde{f}(z)=-f(0)+2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}c_{n}z^{n}.

The expression
\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}z^{n}
is called the
Riesz projection
of
f
, and denoted by
P_{+}f
. i.e.,

P_{+}:h({\mathbb{D}})\to H({\mathbb{D}})

is defined by

P_{+}(f)(z):=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}z^{n}.

Thus Eq. (
1.1
) can be rewritten as

(1.2)

f+i\tilde{f}=-f(0)+2P_{+}(f).

Next, assume that
f=P[\phi],\phi\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
, the function conjugate to
P[\phi]
, equals

\tilde{P}[\phi](z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\tilde{P}(ze^{-i\theta})\phi(e^{i\theta})d\theta,

here
\tilde{P}
denotes the conjugate Poisson kernel,

\tilde{P}(z)={\operatorname{Im}\,}\frac{1+z}{1-z}=\frac{2r\sin\theta}{1+r^{2}-2r\cos(\theta)},\quad z=re^{i\theta}.

The Riesz projection
P_{+}
may also be written as a Cauchy type integral,

(1.3)

P_{+}(P[\phi])(z)=C(\phi)(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{\phi(w)}{w-z}\,dw,\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Remark that in the case
f=P[\phi]
, the Eq. (
1.2
) can be rewritten as

(1.4)

P[\phi]+i\tilde{P}[\phi]=P[\phi](0)+2P_{+}(\phi).

Moreover, if
\phi\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
, then
\tilde{P}[\phi]
has radial limits almost everywhere and there holds the relation

\lim_{r\to 1^{-}}\tilde{P}[\phi](re^{i\theta})=\tilde{\phi}(e^{i\theta}),

where
\tilde{\phi}
is the
Hilbert transform
of
\phi
given by

H(\phi)(e^{i\theta})=\tilde{\phi}(e^{i\theta}):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\,{\rm p.v.}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{\phi(e^{i\theta})}{\tan(\frac{\theta-t}{2})}\,dt.

The Hilbert operator maps

L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
into
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, for every
p<1
, but not into
L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
, so in the general case the Poisson integral of
\tilde{\phi}
has no sense. However we have

Theorem A.

[
10
]

If
\phi\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
and
\tilde{\phi}\in L^{1}({\mathbb{T}})
, then

P[\tilde{\phi}]=\tilde{P}[\phi].

It is natural to consider the following question: if
f
is harmonic in
{\mathbb{D}}
and
f\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
,
1\leq p\leq\infty
, does
\tilde{f}\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
(or
P_{+}(f)\in H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
)?

The question has an affirmative answer for all
1<p<\infty
. This is the content of the famous theorem proved by M. Riesz
[
12
]
, see also Rudin
[
13
, Theorem 17.26]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

(M. Riesz) For
1<p<\infty
, the Riesz projection
P_{+}

P_{+}:L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})\to H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})

is a continuous operator on
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, i.e.,
there is a constant
A_{p}
such that

(1.5)

\|P_{+}(f)\|_{p}\leq A_{p}\|f\|_{p}

holds for
\ f\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Equivalently, for every
p\in(1,\infty)
there exists a constant
B_{p}
such that

\|u+i\tilde{u}\|_{p}\leq B_{p}\|u\|_{p},

holds for any harmonic function
u\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
, and
\tilde{u}
is its harmonic conjugate normalized by
\tilde{u}(0)=0
.

Recently Hollenbeck and Verbitsky
[
9
]
proved that the best possible constant in the Riesz’s inequality (
1.5
) is
A_{p}=\csc(\frac{\pi}{p})
.

It turns out that the Riesz theorem is not true for
p=1
and
p=\infty
. Indeed, if

f_{1}(z)=\frac{1+z}{1-z}.\quad f_{2}(z)=i\log(1-z),\quad z\in{\mathbb{D}}.

Then
{\operatorname{Re}\,}f_{1}=P
, the Poisson kernel, so
{\operatorname{Re}\,}f_{1}\in h^{1},
and
{\operatorname{Re}\,}f_{2}\in h^{\infty}
. However
f_{1}\not\in H^{1}
, because its boundary function is not integrable, and
f_{2}
is obviously unbounded.

As a consequence of M. Riesz’s theorem, we deduce that the Hilbert transform sends
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
into
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
for
p\in(1,\infty)
.

2.
Main results

In
[
15
]
, Zhu considered the following question :

Under what conditions on the boundary function
F
ensure that the
partial derivatives of its harmonic extension
f=P[F]
, i.e.,
f_{z}
and
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
, are in the space
\mathcal{B}^{p}(\mathbb{D})
(or
H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
), where
p\in[1,\infty]
?

The author proved Theorem B only for
p\in[1,2)
and then extended to
[1,\infty)
by Chen et al. in
[
1
]
.

Theorem B.
(
[
15
, Theorem 1.2]
),
[
1
, Theorem 1.1]
)

Suppose that
f=P[F]
is a harmonic mapping in
\mathbb{D}
and

\dot{F}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
, where
F
is an absolutely continuous function.

(1)

If
p\in[1,\infty)
, then both
f_{z}
and
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
are in
\mathcal{B}^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

(2)

If
p=\infty
, then there exists a harmonic
mapping
f=P[F]
, with
\dot{F}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{T})
, such that
neither
f_{z}
nor
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
is in
\mathcal{B}^{\infty}(\mathbb{D}).

Furthermore, under some additional conditions of
f
, they proved that the partial derivatives are in
H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
, for
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Theorem C.
(
[
15
, Theorem 1.3]
,
[
1
, Theorem 1.2]
)

Suppose that
p\in[1,\infty]
and
f=P[F]
is a
(K,K^{\prime})
-elliptic mapping in
\mathbb{D}
with

\dot{F}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
, where
F
is an absolute continuous function,
K\geq 1
and
K^{\prime}\geq 0
. Then both

f_{z}
and
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
are in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

In
[
1
]
, the authors showed that Theorem C also holds true for harmonic elliptic mappings, which are more general than harmonic quasiregular mapping.

Our first main result is a refinement of the two previous theorems, we prove that for
1<p<\infty
, both

f_{z}
and
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
are in
H^{p}(\mathbb{D})
without any extra conditions on

f
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Suppose that
F
is an absolute continuous function on
{\mathbb{T}}
and
f=P[F]
is a harmonic mapping in
\mathbb{D}
and

\dot{F}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
.

(1)

If
p\in(1,\infty)
, then both
f_{z}
and
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
are in
\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{D}).
Moreover, there exits a constant
A_{p}
such that

\max(\|f_{z}\|_{p},\|\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}\|_{p})\leq A_{p}\|\dot{F}\|_{p}.

(2)

If
p=1
or
p=\infty
, then both

f_{z}
and
\overline{f_{\overline{z}}}
are in
\mathcal{H}^{p}(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
H(\dot{F})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
. Moreover

2izf_{z}(z)=P[\dot{F}+iH(\dot{F})](z).

Let
z=re^{i\theta}\in{\mathbb{D}}
. The polar derivatives of
a complex valued function
f
are given as follows

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{array}[]{lr}\displaystyle f_{\theta}(z):=\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial\theta}=i\big(zf_{z}(z)-\overline{z}f_{\overline{z}}(z)\big)\\
\displaystyle f_{r}(z):=\frac{\partial f(z)}{\partial r}=f_{z}(z)e^{i\theta}+f_{\overline{z}}(z)e^{-i\theta}.\end{array}\right.

Thus, if
f
is harmonic, then
f_{\theta}
and
rf_{r}
are harmonic too and

(2.1)

2izf_{z}=f_{\theta}+irf_{r}.

Here, we should mention the following fundamental observation that
rf_{r}
is a harmonic conjugate to
f_{\theta}
.

The next lemma shows that
f_{\theta}\in h^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
for
f=P[F]
and
\dot{F}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, for
p\in[1,\infty]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

[
15
, Lemma 2.3]

Suppose
1\leq p\leq\infty
,
f=P[F]
is a harmonic mapping of
{\mathbb{D}}
with an absolutely continuous boundary function
F
satisfying
\dot{F}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
. Then

f_{\theta}=P[\dot{F}].

In particular,
f_{\theta}\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
and

\|f_{\theta}\|_{p}\leq\|\dot{F}\|_{L^{p}}
.

As a corollary, we obtain some information regarding the harmonic function
rf_{r}
, which is a harmonic conjugate to
f_{\theta}
.

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose that
F
is an absolutely continuous function and
f=P[F]
is a harmonic mapping in
\mathbb{D}
and

\dot{F}\in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})
.

(1)

If
p\in(1,\infty)
, then
rf_{r}\in h^{p}(\mathbb{D}).

(2)

If
p=1
or
p=\infty
, then
rf_{r}\in h^{p}(\mathbb{D})
if and only if
H(\dot{F})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
.

3.
Proofs

The next result provides an integral representation of
f_{z}
and
f_{\bar{z}}
in terms of the Riesz projection of
\dot{F}
.

Proposition 3.1
.

Let
f=P[F]
where
F
is an absolute continuous function on
{\mathbb{T}}
.
Then

f_{z}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(w)}{w-z}\,|dw|,

and

f_{\bar{z}}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(w)}{\bar{w}-\bar{z}}\,|dw|.

Thus

(3.1)

izf_{z}=P_{+}[\dot{F}](z),\quad and\quad iz\overline{f_{\bar{z}}}(z)=P_{+}[\overline{\dot{F}}](z).

Proof.

Let
P
be the Poisson kernel. Then

P_{z}=\partial P(z)=\frac{1}{(1-z)^{2}}.

Thus

f_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\partial P(ze^{-it})e^{-it}F(e^{it})dt=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{e^{-it}}{(1-ze^{-it})^{2}}F(e^{it})dt.

As

\frac{d}{dt}\left(\frac{1}{1-ze^{-it}}\right)=\frac{-ize^{-it}}{(1-ze^{-it})^{2}},

and
F
is absolutely continuous, using integration by parts, we obtain

izf_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(e^{it})}{1-ze^{-it}}dt=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{{\mathbb{T}}}\frac{\dot{F}(w)}{w-z}\,dw=C[\dot{F}]=P_{+}[\dot{F}].

Hence,

f_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{e^{-it}}{1-ze^{-it}}\dot{F}(e^{it})\,dt=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(w)}{w-z}\,|dw|.

Similar computations provide the expression of
f_{\bar{z}}
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

(1) Let
p\in(1,\infty)
and
f=P[F]
with
\dot{F}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
. Combining Proposition
3.1
and Riesz’s theorem, we conclude that
P_{+}[\dot{F}]=izf_{z}
is an element of
H^{p}
and there exists a constant
A_{p}
such that

\|zf_{z}\|_{p}\leq A_{p}\|\dot{F}\|_{L^{p}}.

Since
\|zf_{z}\|_{p}=\|f_{z}\|_{p}
, we deduce that
f_{z}\in H^{p}
and
\|f_{z}\|_{p}\leq A_{p}\|\dot{F}\|_{L^{p}}.

(2) Let
p=1
or
p=\infty
. Let
h=P[\dot{F}]
. Clearly
h
is harmonic and
h(0)=0
. Thus by (
1.2
) we get

h+i\tilde{h}=2P_{+}(h)
, that is

(3.2)

P[\dot{F}]+i\tilde{P}[\dot{F}]=2P_{+}(\dot{F})=2izf_{z}.

Assume that
f_{z}\in H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
, then its boundary
(f_{z})_{*}
belongs to
L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
obtained by taking radial limits, that is,
(f_{z})_{*}(\xi)=\lim_{r\to 1}f_{z}(r\xi)
a.e. As
h_{*}=\dot{F}
, by (
3.2
), we deduce that
(\tilde{h})_{*}=(\tilde{P}[\dot{F}])_{*}=H(\dot{F})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
.

Conversely, assume that
H(\dot{F})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, then

\tilde{P}[\dot{F}]=P[H(\dot{F})].
Therefore,

2izf_{z}=P[\dot{F}+iH(\dot{F})]
belongs to
H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
, as
\dot{F}+iH(\dot{F})\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
.
\square

Proof of Corollary
2.2
.

1) Let
p\in(1,\infty)
. As
rf_{r}
is the harmonic conjugate of
f_{\theta}=P[\dot{F}]\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
, by Riesz theorem, we deduce that
rf_{r}\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
.

2) In the case
p=1
or
p=\infty
. Using the identity

irf_{r}(z)=2izf_{z}(z)-P[\dot{F}](z),

we deduce that
rf_{r}\in h^{p}
if and only if
f_{z}\in H^{p}
, since
P[\dot{F}]\in h^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
. The conclusion follows from Theorem
2.1
.

Example 3.2
.

We provide an example of a harmonic mapping
f=P[F]
, where
F
is an absolutely continuous function with
\dot{F}\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}})
, such that neither
f_{z}
nor
f_{\overline{z}}
is in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{D}})
.

Let
F(t):=|t|
on
[-\pi,\pi]
.
F
is an absolute continuous function on
{\mathbb{T}}
and

\dot{F}=-1
on
(-\pi,0)
and
\dot{F}=1
on
(0,\pi)
. By Proposition
3.1
,
f_{z}
has the following integral representation

izf_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(e^{it})}{1-ze^{-it}}dt=\sum_{n\geq 0}\widehat{\dot{F}}(n)z^{n},

where
\widehat{\dot{F}}(n)
denotes the Fourier coefficient of
\dot{F}
of order
n
.
Elementary computations guarantee that for
z\in{\mathbb{D}}
,

izf_{z}(z)=-\frac{2i}{\pi}\sum_{n\geq 0}\frac{z^{2n+1}}{2n+1}=-\frac{2i}{\pi}{\operatorname{arctanh}}(z)=-\frac{2i}{\pi}\log\left(\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right).

Thus
|f_{z}(r)|
goes to
\infty
as
r\to 1
. In addition, one can compute the Hilbert transform of
\dot{F}
. Indeed, the boundary function of
izf_{z}
is given by
-\frac{2i}{\pi}\log\left(\frac{1+e^{i\theta}}{1-e^{i\theta}}\right)=-\frac{2i}{\pi}\log(i\cot\frac{\theta}{2})=\dot{F}+iH(\dot{F}),
and
izf_{z}=P[\dot{F}+iH(\dot{F})]
, as
f_{z}\in H^{2}
.
This shows that

H(\dot{F})=\frac{2}{\pi}\ln|\tan\frac{\theta}{2}|\not\in L^{\infty}({\mathbb{T}}).

\square

We close this paper by providing an elementary proof of Theorem B using only Proposition
3.1
and without switching to polar coordinates as in
[
15
,
1
]
.

Let
p\in[1,\infty)
and
f=P[F]
, where
\dot{F}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
, we will prove that
f_{z}
is in
L^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
. By Proposition
3.1
,
f_{z}
has the following integral representation

f_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(w)}{w-z}\,|dw|.

According to Jensen’s inequality, we have

|f_{z}(z)|^{p}\leq\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{|\dot{F}(e^{it})|^{p}}{|1-ze^{-it}|}dt\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{dt}{|1-ze^{-it}|}\right)^{p-1}.

Next, by Fubini’s theorem, we obtain that

(3.3)

\int_{0}^{2\pi}|f_{z}(re^{i\theta})|^{p}d\theta\leq|\dot{F}|_{p}^{p}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{dt}{|1-ze^{-it}|}\right)^{p}.

When
r
goes to
1
, we have, see Rudin
[
14
, Prop 1.4.10]

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{dt}{|1-ze^{-it}|}\simeq\ln\frac{1}{1-r^{2}},\quad r=|z|.

As
\left(\ln\frac{1}{1-r^{2}}\right)^{p}
is integrable near
1
, we deduce that
f_{z}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
. We remark that the identity (
3.3
) is not enough to prove that
f_{z}
is in the Hardy space
H^{p}({\mathbb{D}})
and the use of M. Riesz’s theorem is essential to reach our conclusion.

In addition, using Proposition
3.1
, we provide pointwise estimates of
|f_{z}|
and
|f_{\overline{z}}|
in terms of
\|\dot{F}\|_{p}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Let
f=P[F]
and
\dot{F}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
with
p\in(1,\infty)
, then there exists
C_{p}>0
such that

\max(|f_{z}(z)|,\,|f_{{\overline{z}}}(z)|)\leq\frac{C_{p}\|\dot{F}\|_{p}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{1/p}}.

Thus

|D_{f}(z)|\leq\frac{2C_{p}\|\dot{F}\|_{p}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{1/p}},

with

C_{p}=\left(\frac{\Gamma(q-1)}{\Gamma^{2}(q/2)}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}},

where
q
is the conjugate of
p
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.1
,
f_{z}
has the following integral representation

f_{z}(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{\dot{F}(w)}{w-z}\,|dw|.

Hence,
by Hölder inequality, we deduce that

|f_{z}(z)|\leq\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\mathbb{T}}\frac{dt}{|1-ze^{-it}|^{q}}\right)^{\frac{1}{q}}||\dot{F}||_{p}.

Let us denote

I_{a}(z):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{dt}{|1-ze^{-it}|^{a+1}}.

In
[
6
, Proposition 1.1]
, we proved that if
a>0
, then

I_{a}(z)\leq\frac{\Gamma(a)}{\Gamma^{2}(a/2+1/2)}\frac{1}{(1-|z|^{2})^{a}}.

Thus

|f_{z}(z)|\leq\left(I_{q-1}(z)\right)^{1/q}\|\dot{F}\|_{p}\leq\frac{C_{p}\|\dot{F}\|_{p}}{(1-|z|^{2})^{1/p}}.

∎

As a consequence, we obtain the following about the Lipschitz continuity of harmonic functions on the unit disc
{\mathbb{D}}
. In
[
11
]
, the authors provided a characterizations when a Poisson transformation of a Lipschitz function on the circle is Lipschitz on the disc.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
f=P[F]
and
\dot{F}\in L^{p}({\mathbb{T}})
with
p\in(1,\infty)
. Then
f
is
\frac{1}{q}
-Hölder continuous on
{\mathbb{D}}
, where
q
is the conjugate of
p
.

The proof is an immediate consequence of the following proposition essentially due to Gehring and Martio.

Proposition 3.5
.

[
5
]

Let
f\in\mathcal{C}^{1}({\mathbb{D}})
be such that

|Df(z)|\leq\frac{C}{(1-|z|^{2})^{1-\alpha}},\ \ \ \ \ z\in{\mathbb{D}},

for
0<\alpha\leq 1
and
C>0
. Then
f
is
\alpha
-Hölder continuous on
{\mathbb{D}}
.
