Resonant diffusion of a gravitactic circle swimmer
Oleksandr Chepizhko, Thomas Franosch

Abstract
We investigate the dynamics of a single chiral active particle subject to an external torque due to the presence of a gravitational field. Our computer simulations reveal an arbitrarily strong increase of the long-time diffusivity of the gravitactic agent when the external torque approaches the intrinsic angular drift. We provide analytic expressions for the mean-square displacement in terms of eigenfunctions and eigenvalues of the noisy-driven-pendulum problem. The pronounced maximum in the diffusivity is then rationalized by the vanishing of the lowest eigenvalues of the Fokker-Planck equation for the angular motion as the rotational diffusion decreases and the underlying classical bifurcation is approached. A simple harmonic-oscillator picture for the barrier-dominated motion provides a quantitative description for the onset of the resonance while its range of validity is determined by the crossover to a critical-fluctuation-dominated regime.

Theory.–

The goal of this part is to provide a theoretical explanation for the found resonance and to derive scaling laws in the vicinity of the classical bifurcation.
The dynamical properties are encoded in the propagator
\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})
, i.e. the
conditional probability distribution that the particle has displaced by
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
and its instantaneous speed exhibits an orientation
\vartheta
at lag time
t
given it started with orientation
\vartheta_{0}
. Angles are considered to
be
2\pi
-periodic.
We focus on its spatial Fourier transform
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}:=\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})=\int d\mathbf{r}\exp(-\mathrm{i}\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}})\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})

and derive by standard methods
Risken 1989
the (Fourier transformed) Fokker-Planck equation (see also SM
sup
)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\vartheta}[(\omega-\gamma\sin\vartheta)\tilde{\mathbb{P}}]+D_{\text{rot}}\partial_{\vartheta}^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}-iv\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{u}}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=:(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})\tilde{\mathbb{P}}.

(3)

Here the operator

describes the coupling to the translational dynamics.
The formal solution

From this quantity the intermediate scattering function (ISF)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm{d}\vartheta\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm{d}\vartheta_{0}\,\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta_{0})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm{d}\vartheta\exp[(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t]p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta).

(4)

Moments of the displacement can be extracted by a series expansion in the wave vector
\bm{\mathrm{k}}

F(\bm{\mathrm{k}},t)=1-\mathrm{i}\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\langle\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\langle[\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]^{2}\rangle+\ldots\,.

Here we follow the strategy of Ref.

, and apply time-dependent perturbation theory for

is represented in terms of its matrix elements

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{mn}

\displaystyle=\langle m|\mathcal{L}n\rangle:=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\vartheta}{2\pi}e^{-im\vartheta}\mathcal{L}e^{in\vartheta}

\displaystyle=(-D_{\text{rot}}m^{2}-im\omega)\delta_{mn}+\frac{\gamma}{2}m(\delta_{m,n+1}-\delta_{m,n-1}).

(6)

In particular, the matrix representation is non-Hermitian and tridiagonal due to the torque.
Right and left eigenstates
\mathcal{L}|r_{\lambda}\rangle=-\lambda|r_{\lambda}\rangle,\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|l_{\lambda}\rangle=-\lambda^{*}|l_{\lambda}\rangle

SM16

The corresponding real-space representation is obtained by expansion

By conservation of probability

Summary and Conclusion.–

We have demonstrated that the long-time translational diffusivity of a chiral active Brownian particle displays a resonance for the external torque approaching the intrinsic angular drift. The resonance originates from an underlying bifurcation of the classical driven pendulum. There are certain similarities
with the giant diffusion in tilted washboard potentials
Reimann et al. 2001
;
*Reimann2002
, yet our approach
of decomposition into eigenfunctions is rather complementary and allows calculating the entire time-dependence of low-order moments and is in spirit closer to Ref.
López-Alamilla et al. 2020
. The connection of the resonance to the vanishing low-lying eigenvalues is uncovered and an intuitive picture in terms of a competition between barrier-dominated and critical-fluctuation-dominated regime is developed.

The harmonic approximation is surprisingly accurate for the variance as well as for the diffusivity, despite ignoring the rare activation processes over the barrier. We conclude that far above the bifurcation, the orientation performs only small fluctuations most of the time close to the minimum of the effective potential. Then the diffusion coefficient for the translational diffusion remains also small. Yet, approaching the bifurcation these fluctuations become more significant as the confining angular potentials becomes softer yielding a significant enhancement of the diffusivity. For too large angular fluctuations the harmonic approximation breaks down as barrier-crossing events become important. After such barrier crossings the orientation quickly completes a full turn until getting stuck again. From the accurateness of the harmonic oscillator description we conclude that these fast events do not significantly contribute to the diffusivity. The essence of the resonance is thus due to the enhancement of small fluctuations as provided by the orientational diffusion coefficient exploring a softening potential as the bifurcation is approached.

Our theoretical work makes detailed predictions for active motion of chiral agents in external fields that can be tested in experiments, both for artificial and biological asymmetric microswimmers.
The method is readily extended to calculate higher moments such as the skewness, the non-Gaussian parameter, or the complete intermediate scattering function.
While we explicitly considered gravitaxis, the underlying equations are rather generic and the analysis and methods presented should transfer with suitable adjustments to other forms of
taxis
at the microscale such as durotaxis
Lo et al. 2000
, chemotaxis
Vuijk et al. 2021
, thermotaxis
Auschra et al. 2021
, or topotaxis
Schakenraad et al. 2020
. Also, the results could be valuable for the transport rigid polymer solutions in a flow near a wall
Park et al. 2007
.

The resonance is of interest not only for single-particle transport in external fields, but also has implications for the collective motion of chiral active particles with alignment interactions
Liebchen and Levis 2017
;
Levis and Liebchen 2018
;
Ventejou et al. 2021
;
Lei et al. 2019
provided effective mean-field equations can be derived
Chepizhko and Kulinskii 2010
;
Peruani et al. 2008
;
Pimentel et al. 2008
;
Bolley et al. 2012
which would be similar in structure to the equations of motion studied here.

Last our work has theoretical ramifications on the interplay of the critical slowing down of classical transport close to bifurcations and their smearing by random fluctuations. The evolution of the eigenspectrum also for other bifurcations, including the pitchfork or transcritical bifurcations, should be relevant for various branches of science, such as the physics of the Josephson junction
Strogatz 2018
or collective (Kuramoto) synchronization
Acebrón et al. 2005
.

II
Fokker-Planck equation, intermediate scattering function and perturbation theory

II.1
Fokker-Planck equation

By standard techniques [3] one derives the Fokker-Planck equation for the conditional probability density
\mathbb{P}=\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})
to find the particle at speed orientation
\vartheta
and displaced by
\bm{\mathrm{r}}
at time
t
given the orientation at time
t=0
is prescribed to
\vartheta_{0}
:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\mathbb{P}=-\partial_{\vartheta}[(\omega-\gamma\sin\vartheta)\mathbb{P}]+D_{\text{rot}}\partial_{\vartheta}^{2}\mathbb{P}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\cdot(\bm{\mathrm{V}}\mathbb{P})+\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\cdot\left(\textsf{D}_{T}\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial\bm{\mathrm{r}}}\mathbb{P}\right).

(SM8)

The initial condition is provided by

\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\vartheta,t=0|\vartheta_{0})=\delta(\bm{\mathrm{r}})\delta(\vartheta-\vartheta_{0})
.

We also introduce the spatial Fourier transform

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})=\int\mathrm{d}^{2}r\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{r}})\mathbb{P}(\bm{\mathrm{r}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0}),

(SM9)

with corresponding e.o.m.

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\vartheta}[(\omega-\gamma\sin\vartheta)\tilde{\mathbb{P}}]+D_{\text{rot}}\partial_{\vartheta}^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{V}}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}-D_{\perp}k^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}-(D_{\parallel}-D_{\perp})(\bm{\mathrm{u}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{k}})^{2}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}

\displaystyle=:(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})\tilde{\mathbb{P}}.

(SM10)

Here the operator
\mathcal{L}
encodes the motion of the angle, while
\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}=-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{V}}-D_{\perp}k^{2}-(D_{\parallel}-D_{\perp})(\bm{\mathrm{u}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{k}})^{2}
describes the coupling to the translational dynamics.
The formal solution
is thus
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\vartheta,t)|\vartheta_{0})=\exp[(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t]\delta(\vartheta-\vartheta_{0})
.
From this quantity the intermediate scattering function (ISF)
F(\mathbf{k},t)=\langle\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t))\rangle
is obtained in the stationary state by averaging over the initial angle and summing over the final one

\displaystyle F(\bm{\mathrm{k}},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm{d}\vartheta\!\!\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm{d}\vartheta_{0}\,\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}},\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta_{0})=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\!\!\mathrm{d}\vartheta\exp[(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t]p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta),

(SM11)

where
p^{\text{st}}(.)
is the non-trivial distribution of the orientation in the steady state. We will derive low-order spatial moments
by series expansion in powers of the wavevector
\bm{\mathrm{k}}
.

It is favorable to rely on the isomorphism of periodic square-integrable functions
f(\vartheta)\in L^{2}[0,2\pi]
and abstract states
|f\rangle\in\mathcal{H}
[4].
The relation is made explicit by introducing generalized eigenstates
|\vartheta\rangle
such that
f(\vartheta)=\langle\vartheta|f\rangle
. For this to hold we require

\displaystyle\langle f|g\rangle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta\,f(\vartheta)^{*}g(\vartheta)=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta\,\langle f|\vartheta\rangle\langle\vartheta|g\rangle,

(SM12)

for all
f,g\in L^{2}[0,2\pi]
. We therefore infer the completeness relation

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta|\vartheta\rangle\langle\vartheta|=\mathbbm{1}.

(SM13)

Furthermore from

\displaystyle\mathbbm{1}^{2}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta_{0}\,|\vartheta\rangle\langle\vartheta|\vartheta_{0}\rangle\langle\vartheta_{0}|\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}\mathbbm{1},

(SM14)

we conclude the generalized orthogonality

\displaystyle\langle\vartheta|\vartheta_{0}\rangle=\delta(\vartheta-\vartheta_{0}).

(SM15)

We write
\{|n\rangle:n\in\mathbb{Z}\}
for the standard orthonormal basis (ONB) in
\mathcal{H}
with real-space representation
\langle\vartheta|n\rangle=\exp(in\vartheta)/\sqrt{2\pi}
. We define the (non-Hermitian) operator
\mathcal{L}
in
\mathcal{H}
via its matrix elements
\langle m|\mathcal{L}n\rangle

5
5

5

Since
\mathcal{L}
is non-Hermitian one has to carefully indicate whether operators act to the right or the left.

of the Fokker-Planck operator [3]

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{mn}=\langle m|\mathcal{L}n\rangle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{\mathrm{d}\vartheta}{2\pi}e^{-im\vartheta}\mathcal{L}e^{in\vartheta}=(-D_{\text{rot}}m^{2}-im\omega)\delta_{mn}+\frac{\gamma}{2}m(\delta_{m,n+1}-\delta_{m,n-1}).

(SM16)

In particular the matrix representation becomes tridiagonal.
We solve for right and left eigenstates of the Fokker-Planck operator

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}|r_{\lambda}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\lambda|r_{\lambda}\rangle,\qquad\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}|l_{\lambda}\rangle=-\lambda^{*}|l_{\lambda}\rangle,

(SM17)

by expansion in the ONB

\displaystyle|r_{\lambda}\rangle=\sum_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|r_{\lambda}\rangle,\qquad|l_{\lambda}\rangle=\sum_{n}|n\rangle\langle n|l_{\lambda}\rangle.

(SM18)

Then the expansion coefficients
\langle n|r_{\lambda}\rangle
and
\langle n|l_{\lambda}\rangle
diagonalize the matrix
\langle m|\mathcal{L}n\rangle

\displaystyle\sum_{n}\langle m|\mathcal{L}n\rangle\langle n|r_{\lambda}\rangle=-\lambda\langle m|r_{\lambda}\rangle,\qquad\sum_{n}\langle m|\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}n\rangle\langle n|l_{\lambda}\rangle=-\lambda\langle m|l_{\lambda}\rangle.

(SM19)

In particular, the left and right eigenstates can be chosen to be orthonormal and we assume that they span the entire Hilbert space

\displaystyle\langle l_{\mu}|r_{\lambda}\rangle

\displaystyle=\delta_{\mu\lambda},\qquad\sum_{\lambda}|r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|=\mathbbm{1},

(SM20)

where
\sum_{\lambda}
means that we are summing over all eigenspaces. By conservation of probability the left eigenstate to eigenvalue
0
is trivial:
\langle l_{0}|=\langle 0|
, see Eq. (
SM16
). The corresponding right eigenstate
|r_{0}\rangle
is the stationary state with real space representation
\langle\vartheta|r_{0}\rangle=p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta)
, where the normalizing factor is chosen such that
\langle l_{0}|r_{0}\rangle=1
.

The angular propagator
P(\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})
encoding the probability to find an angle
\vartheta
at lag time
t
given the initial orientation at time
t=0
was
\vartheta_{0}
can then be written conveniently in abstract form as

\displaystyle P(\vartheta,t|\vartheta_{0})

\displaystyle=e^{\mathcal{L}t}\delta(\vartheta-\vartheta_{0})=\sum_{\lambda}e^{-\lambda t}\langle\vartheta|r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\vartheta_{0}\rangle=\langle\vartheta|\sum_{\lambda}e^{\mathcal{L}t}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\vartheta_{0}\rangle=\langle\vartheta|e^{\mathcal{L}t}\vartheta_{0}\rangle.

(SM21)

II.2
Perturbation theory

The full propagator
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}\vartheta t|\vartheta_{0})
solves the Fokker-Planck equation

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\tilde{\mathbb{P}}=(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})\tilde{\mathbb{P}},

(SM22)

with
the perturbation

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}=

\displaystyle-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{V}}-D_{\perp}k^{2}-(D_{\parallel}-D_{\perp})(\bm{\mathrm{u}}^{\parallel}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{k}})^{2}.

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-ik_{x}v\cos\vartheta-ik_{y}v\sin\vartheta+ik_{x}v_{1}\sin(\varphi_{H}+\vartheta)-ik_{y}v_{1}\cos(\vartheta_{H}+\vartheta)+ik_{x}v_{2}\sin[2(\varphi_{H}+\vartheta)]+ik_{y}v_{2}\cos[2(\varphi_{H}+\vartheta)]

\displaystyle+iv_{3}k_{y}-\frac{D_{\parallel}-D_{\perp}}{2}[(k_{x}^{2}-k_{y}^{2})\cos(2(\varphi_{H}+\vartheta))+2k_{x}k_{y}\sin(2(\varphi_{H}+\vartheta))]-\frac{D_{\parallel}+D_{\perp}}{2}k^{2}.

(SM23)

In the following we discard all terms due to translational motion and keep only

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}=-iv\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\bm{\mathrm{u}}=-ik_{x}v\cos\vartheta-ik_{y}v\sin\vartheta,

(SM24)

with corresponding matrix elements

\displaystyle(\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})_{mn}=\langle m|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}n\rangle=-\frac{ik_{x}v}{2}(\delta_{m,n+1}+\delta_{m,n-1})-\frac{k_{y}v}{2}(\delta_{m,n+1}-\delta_{m,n-1}).

(SM25)

The formal solution is then given by

\displaystyle\tilde{\mathbb{P}}(\bm{\mathrm{k}}\vartheta t|\vartheta_{0})=e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t}\delta(\vartheta-\vartheta_{0})=\langle\vartheta|e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t}\vartheta_{0}\rangle.

(SM26)

Then the intermediate scattering function in the stationary state is obtained as

\displaystyle F(\bm{\mathrm{k}},t)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta\int_{0}^{2\pi}\mathrm{d}\vartheta_{0}\langle l_{0}|\vartheta\rangle\langle\vartheta|e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t}\vartheta_{0}\rangle\langle\vartheta_{0}|r_{0}\rangle=\langle l_{0}|e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t}r_{0}\rangle.

(SM27)

We use the Dyson representation [4] for the time evolution operator

\displaystyle e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{\mathcal{L}(t-s)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})s},

(SM28)

and expand to second order in the perturbation

\displaystyle e^{(\mathcal{L}+\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})t}=e^{\mathcal{L}t}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\,e^{\mathcal{L}(t-s)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}s}+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{d}u\,e^{\mathcal{L}(t-s)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}(s-u)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}u}+O(\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}})^{3},

(SM29)

and sandwich it between the states
\langle l_{0}|
and
|r_{0}\rangle
. Note further that
\langle l_{0}|e^{\mathcal{L}t}=\langle e^{\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}t}l_{0}|=\langle l_{0}|
by conservation of probability and
e^{\mathcal{L}t}|r_{0}\rangle=|r_{0}\rangle
by the definition of the stationary state. This yields

\displaystyle F(\bm{\mathrm{k}},t)

\displaystyle=1+\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\,\langle l_{0}|e^{\mathcal{L}(t-s)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}s}r_{0}\rangle+\int_{0}^{t}\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{s}\!\mathrm{d}u\langle l_{0}|e^{\mathcal{L}(t-s)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}(s-u)}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}u}r_{0}\rangle+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3})

\displaystyle=1+t\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+\sum_{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{s}\!\mathrm{d}u\,\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}e^{\mathcal{L}(s-u)}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3})

\displaystyle=1+t\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+\sum_{\lambda}\int_{0}^{t}\!\mathrm{d}s\int_{0}^{s}\!\mathrm{d}u\,e^{-\lambda(s-u)}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3})

\displaystyle=1+t\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+\sum_{\lambda}\frac{e^{-\lambda t}+\lambda t-1}{\lambda^{2}}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3}).

(SM30)

For the cumulant-generating function this implies

\displaystyle\ln F(\bm{\mathrm{k}},t)

\displaystyle=t\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+\sum_{\lambda}\frac{e^{-\lambda t}+\lambda t-1}{\lambda^{2}}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle-\frac{t^{2}}{2}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3})

\displaystyle=t\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\frac{e^{-\lambda t}+\lambda t-1}{\lambda^{2}}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3}).

(SM31)

On the other hand the cumulant expansion yields

\displaystyle\ln F(\bm{\mathrm{k}},t)

\displaystyle=\ln\langle\exp(-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t))\rangle=\ln\left\langle 1-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)-\frac{1}{2}[\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]^{2}+O(|\bm{\mathrm{k}}|^{3})\right\rangle

\displaystyle=-i\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\langle\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)\rangle-\frac{1}{2}\left\{\langle[\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]^{2}\rangle-[\langle\bm{\mathrm{k}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)\rangle]^{2}\right\}.

(SM32)

In particular, we read off the mean drift velocity along the direction
\bm{\mathrm{n}}:=\bm{\mathrm{k}}/k

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)\rangle=\frac{i}{k}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle,

(SM33)

as well as the variance

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}[\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]

\displaystyle=\langle[\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]^{2}\rangle-[\langle\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)\rangle]^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k^{2}}\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\frac{1-\lambda t-e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda^{2}}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle.

(SM34)

In particular for the corresponding diffusion coefficient

\displaystyle D_{\bm{\mathrm{n}}}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{1}{2}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\textsf{Var}[\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]=\frac{-1}{k^{2}}\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\frac{1}{\lambda}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle.

(SM35)

For short times the variance grows as
t^{2}
since

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}[\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}t^{2}\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(t^{3})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k^{2}}t^{2}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle-\frac{1}{k^{2}}t^{2}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle\langle l_{0}\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle+O(t^{3}).

(SM36)

D_{\text{rot}}/k_{B}T
is then the mobility for the overdamped motion of the angle and the potential reads

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\text{rot}}}{k_{B}T}U(\vartheta)=-\omega\vartheta-\gamma\cos\vartheta.

(SM40)

For
\gamma\geq\omega
the
potential displays a minimum at
\vartheta_{*}\in[0,\pi/2]
with
\sin\gamma_{*}=\omega/\gamma

while a maximum is displayed at
\pi-\vartheta_{*}\in[\pi/2,\pi]
, see Fig.
SM3
for illustration.
Let’s make a harmonic approximation for the potential close to the minimum,

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\text{rot}}}{k_{B}T}U(\vartheta)

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{\text{rot}}}{k_{B}T}U(\vartheta_{*})+\frac{(\vartheta-\vartheta_{*})^{2}}{2}\gamma\cos\vartheta_{*}+O(\vartheta-\vartheta_{*})^{3}.

(SM41)

For the stochastic e.o.m. this results in a linear Langevin equation

\displaystyle\dot{\vartheta}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\tau}(\vartheta-\vartheta_{*})+\zeta(t),

(SM42)

with relaxation rate

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\tau}=\gamma\cos\vartheta_{*}=\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2}}.

(SM43)

The eigenvalues of the overdamped harmonic oscillator are simply

\displaystyle\lambda_{n}=\frac{1}{\tau}n.

(SM44)

The picture of the harmonic oscillator is expected to be valid for the low eigenvalues if the escape rate for diffusing over the barrier is small compared to the relaxation rate. For large barriers
\Delta U:=U(\pi-\vartheta_{*})-U(\vartheta_{*})
the escape is exponentially suppressed.
\propto\exp(-\Delta U/k_{B}T)
, by Kramers’ law, correspondingly we require that the barrier is high with respect to the thermal energy

\displaystyle 1\ll\frac{\Delta U}{k_{B}T}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k_{B}T}[U(\pi-\vartheta_{*})-U(\vartheta_{*})]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{D_{\text{rot}}}[2\omega\arcsin(\omega/\gamma)-\pi\omega+2\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2}}].

(SM45)

The condition is certainly fulfilled for
D_{\text{rot}}\to 0
for fixed
\gamma>\omega
. Here we want to investigate the scaling limit of
D_{\text{rot}}/\omega\to 0
and
\epsilon:=(\gamma-\omega)/\omega\to 0
. Then upon series expansion the condition of high barrier translates to

\displaystyle 1\ll\frac{\Delta U}{k_{B}T}=\frac{4\sqrt{2}}{3}\frac{\omega}{D_{\text{rot}}}\epsilon^{3/2}+O(\ldots).

(SM46)

This is the condition, Eq. (14), of the main text.

Matrix elements of the harmonic oscillator

For small
D_{\text{rot}}
and
\gamma>\omega
we perform the harmonic oscillator approximation around the minimum
\vartheta_{*}
,
x:=\vartheta-\vartheta_{*}

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}

\displaystyle=D_{\text{rot}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\vartheta^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial\vartheta}(\omega-\gamma\sin\vartheta)=D_{\text{rot}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}[\omega-\gamma\sin(\vartheta_{*}+x)]

\displaystyle\approx D_{\text{rot}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(\omega-\gamma\sin\vartheta_{*}-x\gamma\cos\vartheta_{*}).

(SM47)

Since
\gamma\sin\vartheta_{*}=\omega
and
\gamma\cos\vartheta_{*}=\sqrt{\gamma^{2}-\omega^{2}}=:1/\tau
this simplifies to

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}=D_{\text{rot}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{x}{\tau}\right).

(SM48)

This Fokker-Planck operator corresponds to the effective potential

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\text{rot}}U(x)}{k_{B}T}=\frac{x^{2}}{2\tau}.

(SM49)

The angular oscillator width
\langle(\vartheta-\vartheta_{*})^{2}\rangle^{1/2}=\sqrt{\langle x^{2}\rangle}=\sqrt{D_{\text{rot}}\tau}
quantifies the mean-square fluctuations. For
D_{\text{rot}}\to 0
the eigenfunctions become more and more localized.

For the perturbation we expand also to linear order

\displaystyle\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}

\displaystyle=-ik_{x}v\cos\vartheta-ik_{y}\sin\vartheta=-ik_{x}v\cos(\vartheta_{*}+x)-ik_{y}v\sin(\vartheta_{*}+x)

\displaystyle=-ik_{x}v\cos\vartheta_{*}-ik_{y}v\sin\vartheta_{*}+(ik_{x}v\sin\vartheta_{*}-ik_{y}v\cos\vartheta_{*})x+O(x^{2}).

(SM50)

Thus to leading order only the diagonal components are non-vanishing

\displaystyle\langle l_{\mu}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}|r_{\lambda}\rangle=(-ik_{x}v\cos\vartheta_{*}-ik_{y}v\sin\vartheta_{*})\delta_{\mu\lambda}+O(.).

(SM51)

This yields for the mean drift

\displaystyle\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\langle\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)\rangle=\frac{i}{k}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}|r_{0}\rangle\to(n_{x}v\cos\vartheta_{*}+n_{y}v\sin\vartheta_{*}),

(SM52)

with
\bm{\mathrm{n}}=\bm{\mathrm{k}}/k
. This reproduces the classical result.

The variance
\textsf{Var}_{\bm{\mathrm{n}}}[\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)
vanishes as
D_{\text{rot}}\to 0
. To obtain the leading order we need to evaluate the matrix elements
\langle l_{\mu}|x|r_{\lambda}\rangle
for
\mu\neq\lambda
.
The fastest way is to use the gauge transformation to map the Fokker-Planck operator to a Schrödinger Hamiltonian [3]
Define the Hamiltonian by the gauge transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-e^{U(x)/2k_{B}T}\mathcal{L}e^{-U(x)/2k_{B}T}=-e^{x^{2}/4D_{\text{rot}}\tau}\mathcal{L}e^{-x^{2}/4D_{\text{rot}}\tau}

\displaystyle=-D_{\text{rot}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{x^{2}}{4D_{\text{rot}}\tau^{2}}-\frac{1}{2\tau},

(SM53)

which is Hermitian in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
. Adjoining yields

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle=-e^{-U(x)/2k_{B}T}\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}e^{U(x)/2k_{B}T}.

(SM54)

We substitute
D_{\text{rot}}\mapsto\hbar^{2}/2m,1/2D_{\text{rot}}\tau^{2}\mapsto m\omega^{2}
and find the shifted harmonic oscillator

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}=\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}+\frac{m\omega^{2}}{2}x^{2}-\frac{\hbar\omega}{2},

(SM55)

with eigenvalues
\lambda_{n}=n\hbar\omega=n/\tau
. In particular the quantum oscillator length
\sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}
maps to the angular oscillator width
\sqrt{D_{\text{rot}}\tau}
. The matrix elements of
x
are thus expected to be of order
\sqrt{D_{\text{rot}}\tau}

The corresponding mapping for the eigenfunctions is then

\displaystyle r_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{-U(x)/2k_{B}T}\psi_{n}(x),

(SM56)

\displaystyle l_{n}(x)

\displaystyle=e^{U(x)/2k_{B}T}\psi_{n}(x),

(SM57)

with
\psi_{n}(x)
the eigenfunctions of
\mathcal{H}
. The matrix elements of an operator
\mathcal{V}
are obtained as

\displaystyle\langle l_{m}|\mathcal{V}r_{n}\rangle=\int\mathrm{d}x\,l_{m}(x)^{*}\,\mathcal{V}\,r_{n}(x)=\int\mathrm{d}x\,\psi_{m}(x)^{*}[e^{U(x)/2k_{B}T}\mathcal{V}e^{-U(x)/2k_{B}T}]\psi_{n}(x).

(SM58)

For the case of
\mathcal{V}=x
the exponentials cancel, and we read off (with harmonic oscillator creation and annihilation operators
a,a^{\dagger}
)

\displaystyle\langle l_{m}|x|r_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle\psi_{m}|x|\psi_{n}\rangle=\langle\psi_{m}|\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(a^{\dagger}+a\right)|\psi_{n}\rangle

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(\delta_{m,n+1}\sqrt{n+1}+\delta_{m,n-1}\sqrt{n}\right)\mapsto\sqrt{D_{\text{rot}}\tau}\left(\delta_{m,n+1}\sqrt{n+1}+\delta_{m,n-1}\sqrt{n}\right).

(SM59)

We thus read off the relevant matrix elements for the variance/diffusion coefficient

\displaystyle\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle=iv\sqrt{D_{\text{rot}}\tau}(k_{x}\sin\vartheta_{*}-k_{y}\cos\vartheta_{*})\delta_{\lambda,1}.

(SM60)

The picture of the harmonic oscillator is valid only for
\hat{D}_{\text{rot}}\ll 1
. In this regime the formulas for the variance and diffusion simplify since only a single term contributes

\displaystyle\textsf{Var}[\bm{\mathrm{n}}\cdot\Delta\bm{\mathrm{r}}(t)]

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{k^{2}}\sum_{\lambda\neq 0}\frac{1-\lambda t-e^{-\lambda t}}{\lambda^{2}}\langle l_{0}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{\lambda}\rangle\langle l_{\lambda}|\delta\mathcal{L}_{\bm{\mathrm{k}}}r_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=2\left(\frac{t}{\tau}-1+e^{-t/\tau}\right)(v\tau)^{2}D_{\text{rot}}\tau(n_{x}\sin\vartheta_{*}-n_{y}\cos\vartheta_{*})^{2},

(SM61)

and infer the associated long-time diffusion coefficient

\displaystyle D_{\bm{\mathrm{n}}}

\displaystyle=(v\tau)^{2}D_{\text{rot}}(n_{x}\sin\vartheta_{*}-n_{y}\cos\vartheta_{*})^{2}.

(SM62)

In particular, for small
D_{\text{rot}}\ll\omega
,
D_{0}\approx v^{2}D_{\text{rot}}/2\omega^{2}
in the harmonic oscillator approximation

\displaystyle D_{\bm{\mathrm{n}}}/D_{0}=2(\omega\tau)^{2}(n_{x}\sin\vartheta_{*}-n_{y}\cos\vartheta_{*})^{2}\approx\frac{1}{\epsilon}(n_{x}\sin\vartheta_{*}-n_{y}\cos\vartheta_{*})^{2}.

This result yields prefactors to the scaling law derived in the main text.

Supplementary figure on mean drift velocity

The mean drift velocity
is evaluated directly from the stationary distribution

\displaystyle v\begin{pmatrix}\langle\cos\vartheta\rangle\\
\langle\sin\vartheta\rangle\end{pmatrix}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}v\begin{pmatrix}\cos\vartheta\\
\sin\vartheta\end{pmatrix}p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta.

(SM63)

Mean drift velocity in the stationary state along the horizontal (left) and vertically against the field (right) as a function of the torque
\gamma
for various noise strength
D_{\text{rot}}
. Symbols correspond to simulation, full lines to numerical results.

The stationary distribution is known explicitly [3]

\displaystyle p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta)=

\displaystyle\mathcal{N}e^{-U(\vartheta)/k_{B}T}\left[\int_{\vartheta}^{2\pi}e^{U(\bar{\vartheta})/k_{B}T}\mathrm{d}\bar{\vartheta}+e^{[U(0)-U(2\pi)]/k_{B}T}\int_{0}^{\vartheta}e^{U(\bar{\vartheta})/k_{B}T}\mathrm{d}\bar{\vartheta}\right],

(SM64)

with the potential

\displaystyle\frac{D_{\text{rot}}U(\vartheta)}{k_{B}T}=-\omega\vartheta-\gamma\cos\vartheta.

(SM65)

The normalization factor
\mathcal{N}
is fixed by imposing
\int_{0}^{2\pi}p^{\text{st}}(\vartheta)\mathrm{d}\vartheta=1
.
