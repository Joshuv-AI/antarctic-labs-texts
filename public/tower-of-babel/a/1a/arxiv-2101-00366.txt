Geometric ergodicity of Gibbs samplers for the Horseshoe and its regularized variants
Suman K. Bhattacharya, Kshitij Khare, Subhadip Pal

Abstract
The Horseshoe is a widely used and popular continuous shrinkage prior for high-dimensional Bayesian linear regression. Recently, regularized versions of the Horseshoe prior have also been introduced in the literature. Various Gibbs sampling Markov chains have been developed in the literature to generate approximate samples from the corresponding intractable posterior densities. Establishing geometric ergodicity of these Markov chains provides crucial technical justification for the accuracy of asymptotic standard errors for Markov chain based estimates of posterior quantities. In this paper, we establish geometric ergodicity for various Gibbs samplers corresponding to the Horseshoe prior and its regularized variants in the context of linear regression. First, we establish geometric ergodicity of a Gibbs sampler for the original Horseshoe posterior under strictly weaker conditions than existing analyses in the literature. Second, we consider the regularized Horseshoe prior introduced in [ 17 ] , and prove geometric ergodicity for a Gibbs sampling Markov chain to sample from the corresponding posterior without any truncation constraint on the global and local shrinkage parameters. Finally, we consider a variant of this regularized Horseshoe prior introduced in [ 14 ] , and again establish geometric ergodicity for a Gibbs sampling Markov chain to sample from the corresponding posterior.

Introduction

Consider the linear model
\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{X}\boldsymbol{\beta}+\sigma\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
, where
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the response vector,
\mathbf{X}
is the
n\times p
design matrix,
\boldsymbol{\beta}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
is the vector of regression coefficients,
\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}
is the error vector with i.i.d. standard normal components, and
\sigma^{2}
is the error variance. The goal is to estimate the unknown parameters
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})
. In modern applications, datasets where the number of predictors
p
is much larger than the sample size
n
are commonly encountered. A standard approach for meaningful statistical estimation in these over-parametrized settings is to assume that only a few of the signals are prominent (the others are small/insignificant). This is mathematically formalized by assuming that the underlying regression coefficient vector is sparse. In the Bayesian paradigm, this assumption of sparsity is accommodated either by choosing spike-and-slab priors (mixture of point mass at zero and an absolutely continuous density) or absolutely continuous shrinkage priors which selectively shrink the small/insignificant signals.

A variety of useful shrinkage priors have been proposed in the literature (see
[
2
,
4
,
18
]
and the references therein), and the Horseshoe prior (
[
4
]
) is a widely used and highly popular choice. The Horseshoe prior for linear regression is specified as follows.

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right.\mid\boldsymbol{\lambda},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{i}\right.\sim C^{+}(0,1)\text{ independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\sim\pi_{\tau}(\cdot)\hskip 14.45377pt\sigma^{2}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}(a,b)

(1.1)

where
\mathcal{N}_{d}
denotes the
d-
variate normal density,
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries given by the entries
\left\{\lambda_{j}^{2}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
, and
\text{Inverse-Gamma}(a,b)
denotes the Inverse-Gamma density with shape parameter
a
and rate parameter
b
. The vector
\boldsymbol{\lambda}=(\lambda_{j}^{2})_{j=1}^{p}
is referred to as the vector of local (component-wise) shrinkage parameters, while
\tau^{2}
is referred to as the global shrinkage parameter.

The resulting posterior distribution for
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2})
is intractable in the sense that closed form computations or i.i.d. sampling from this distribution are not feasible. Several Gibbs sampling Markov chains have been proposed in the literature to generate approximate samples from the Horseshoe posterior, see for example (
[
1
,
7
,
8
,
12
]
).

The fact that parameter values which are far away from zero are not regularized at all due to the heavy tails is considered to be a key strength of the Horseshoe prior. However, as pointed out in
Piironen, Vehtari 2017

[
17
]
, this can be undesirable when the parameters are only weakly identified. To address this issue,
[
17
]
introduced the regularized Horseshoe prior, given by

\displaystyle\left.\beta_{i}\right.\mid\boldsymbol{\lambda},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(0,\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sigma^{2}\right)\text{ independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{i}\right.\sim C^{+}(0,1)\text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\sim\pi_{\tau}(\cdot)\hskip 14.45377pt\sigma^{2}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}(a,b)

Here
c
is a finite constant which controls additional regularization of all regression parameters (large and small). The original Horseshoe prior can be recovered by letting
c\rightarrow\infty
.
Piironen, Vehtari 2017

[
17
]
use a Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) based approach to generate approximate samples from the corresponding regularized Horseshoe posterior distribution. Also, any Gibbs sampler
for the Horseshoe posterior can be suitably adapted in the regularized setting.

For any practitioner using Markov chain Monte Carlo, it is crucial to understand the accuracy of the resulting MCMC based estimates by obtaining valid standard errors for these estimates. The notion of geometric ergodicity plays an important role in this endeavor, as explained below. Let
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{m},\sigma^{2}_{m})_{m\geq 0}
denote a Harris ergodic Markov chain with the Horseshoe or regularized Horseshoe posterior density, denoted by
\pi_{H}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})
, as its stationary density. The Markov chain is said to be geometrically ergodic if

\left\|K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0}}^{m}-\Pi_{H}\right\|_{\mbox{\tiny{TV}}}\leq C\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0}\right)\gamma^{m}

where
K_{\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0}}^{m}
denotes the distribution of the Markov chain started at
(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
after
m
steps,
\Pi_{H}
denotes the stationary distribution, and
\|\cdot\|_{\mbox{\tiny{TV}}}
denotes the total variation norm. Suppose we wish to evaluate the posterior expectation

E_{\pi_{H}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}g=\int\int g\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\right)\pi_{H}\left(\boldsymbol{\beta},\sigma^{2}\mid\mathbf{y}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}

for a real-valued measurable function
g
of interest. Harris ergodicity guarantees that the Markov chain based estimator

\bar{g}_{m}:=\frac{1}{m+1}\sum_{i=0}^{m}g\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}_{i},\sigma^{2}_{i}\right)

is strongly consistent for
E_{\pi(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}g
. An estimate by itself, however, is not quite useful without an associated standard error. All known methods to compute consistent estimates (see for example
[
6
]
,
[
4
]
) of the standard error for
\bar{g}_{m}
require the existence of a Markov chain CLT which establishes

\sqrt{m}\left(\bar{g}_{m}-E_{\pi_{H}(\cdot\mid\mathbf{y})}g\right)\rightarrow\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{g}^{2}),

for
\sigma_{g}^{2}\in(0,\infty)
. In turn, the standard approach for
establishing a Markov chain CLT requires proving geometric ergodicity of the underlying Markov chain. To summarize, proving geometric ergodicity helps
rigorously establish the asymptotic validity of
CLT based standard error estimates used by MCMC practitioners.

Establishing geometric ergodicity for continuous state space Markov chains encountered in most statistical applications is in general a very challenging task. For a significant majority of Markov chains in statistical applications, the question of whether they are geometrically ergodic or not has not been resolved, although there have been some success stories. In the context of Markov chains arising in Bayesian shrinkage, geometric ergodicity of Gibbs samplers corresponding to various shrinkage priors such as the Bayesian lasso, Normal-Gamma, Dirichlet-Laplace and double Pareto priors has been recently established in (
[
9
,
15
,
16
]
).

Results for the Horseshoe prior remained elusive until very recently. The marginal Horseshoe prior on entries of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
(integrating out
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
, given
\tau^{2}
) has an infinite spike near zero and significantly heavier tails than the shrinkage priors mentioned above. This structure, while making it very attractive for sparsity selection, implicitly creates a lot of complications and challenges in the geometric ergodicity analysis using drift and minorization techniques. Recently, the authors
in
Johndrow et al. 2020

[
8
]
derived a two-block Gibbs sampler for the Horseshoe posterior (the ‘exact algorithm’ in
[
8
, Section 2.1]
, henceforth referred to as the JOB Gibbs sampler), and established geometric ergodicity
(
[
8
, Theorem 14]
). However, the truncation assumptions needed for this result are rather restrictive, requiring all the local shrinkage parameters
\lambda_{i}^{2}
to be bounded above by a finite constant, and also requiring the global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
to be bounded above and below by finite positive constants. In parallel work (
Biswas et al. 2020

[
3
]
, uploaded on arxiv a few days prior to our submission) geometric ergodicity for the JOB Gibbs sampler has now been established without requiring truncation of the local shrinkage parameters. However, the requirement of the global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
to be bounded above
and
below remains.

Geometric ergodicity of a Horseshoe Gibbs sampler

2.1
A modified version of the JOB Gibbs sampler

In this section, we describe in detail the Horseshoe Gibbs sampler that will be analyzed in subsequent sections. As pointed out in
Makalic, Schmidt 2016

[
12
]
, if
\lambda_{j}^{2}\mid\nu_{j}\sim\mbox{Inverse-Gamma}(1/2,1/\nu_{j})
and

\nu_{j}\sim\mbox{Inverse-Gamma}(1/2,1)
, then
\lambda_{j}\sim C^{+}(0,1)
. Using this
fact, with
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}=(\nu_{1},\nu_{2},\cdots,\nu_{p})
, the Horseshoe
prior in
(
1.1
) can be alternatively written as

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right.\mid\boldsymbol{\lambda},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(0,\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda})

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{i}^{2}\right.\mid{\boldsymbol{\nu}}\sim\mbox{Inverse-Gamma}(1/2,1/\nu_{i})\;\text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\nu_{i}\sim\mbox{Inverse-Gamma}(1/2,1)\;\text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\sim\pi_{\tau}(\cdot),\hskip 14.45377pt\sigma^{2}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}(a,b)

(2.1)

Using the prior above and after straightforward calculations, various conditional
posterior distributions can be derived as follows.

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda,\nu,y}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}(A^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},\sigma^{2}A^{-1})

\displaystyle\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda,\nu,y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(a+\frac{n}{2},\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{j}^{2}\right|\nu_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\beta_{j},{\bf y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\ \text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\left.\nu_{j}\right|\lambda_{j}^{2},\tau^{2},{\bf y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)\ \text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda,y}\sim\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda,y}\right)\propto\frac{\left(\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}}{\sqrt{\left|I_{p}+\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}.\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{*}\right|}}\cdot\pi_{\tau}(\tau^{2})

(2.2)

where
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{*}=\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda};\ A=\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{*}^{-1}
and
\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}=\mathbf{X}A^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}
.

Consider a two-block Gibbs sampling Markov chain with transition kernel

K_{aug}
(with blocks
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2})
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
)
whose one-step transition from
({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0},\tau^{2}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0})
to

({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is given as follows.

1.

Draw
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2})
from
\pi({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2}\mid{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},{\bf y})
. This can be
done by sequentially drawing
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}
, then
\sigma^{2}
,
then
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, and then
\tau^{2}
from appropriate conditional posterior densities in (
2.1
).

2.

Draw
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
from
\pi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}})
. This can be done by
independently drawing the components of
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
from the
appropriate full conditional posterior density in (
2.1
).

The JOB Gibbs sampler from
Johndrow et al. 2020

[
8
]
is very similar to the above two-block Gibbs sampler
K_{aug}
. The difference is that the latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
are not used, and the two blocks used in the JOB Gibbs sampler are
({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2})
and
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
. While the sampling steps for
{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}

are exactly the same as above, the components of
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
are sampled differently. In particular, each
\lambda_{j}
is sampled from the conditional density given
\beta_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},{\bf y}
(no conditioning on
\nu_{j}
). This conditional density is not a standard density, and draws are made using a rejection sampler. To summarize, by considering the latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, we replace the
p
rejection sampler based
draws from a non-standard density in the JOB Gibbs sampler (for components of
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
) with
2p
draws from standard Inverse-Gamma densities (for components of

{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
).

The Gibbs sampler
K_{aug}
can essentially be considered a hybrid of the JOB Gibbs sampler and the Gibbs sampler in
Makalic, Schmidt 2016

[
12
]
, which uses a latent variable
\xi
(in addition to
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
) to replace the draws from the non-standard
\pi(\tau^{2}\mid{\boldsymbol{\lambda}},{\bf y})
density with two draws from standard Inverse-Gamma densities. As mentioned in the introduction, the geometric ergodicity result for the JOB Gibbs sampler in

[
8
, Theorem 14]
has been established by assuming that the local shrinkage parameters in
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
are all bounded above, and the global
shrinakge parameter
\tau^{2}
is bounded above and below. In very recent follow-up work
[
3
]
, the authors establish geoemtric ergodcity for a class of Half-
t
Gibbs samplers of which the JOB Gibbs sampler is a member. In this work, the truncation assumption on the local shrinkage parameters has been removed, but the global shrinkage parameter is still
assumed to be truncated above
and
below. However, we show below that geometric ergodicity for the hybrid Gibbs sampler
K_{aug}
can be established with no truncation at all on the local shrinkage parameters in
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
, and only assuming that the global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
is truncated below.

The reasons for this improved analysis of the hybrid chain
K_{aug}
lie in the intricacies of drift and minorization approach (
[
20
]
), which is the state of the art technique for proving geometric ergodicity for general state space Markov chains. The introduction of the latent variables
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, the resulting Inverse-Gamma posterior conditionals for entries of
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\nu}}
, and avoiding the latent variable
\xi
for the global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
provide just the right ingredients for establishing a geometric drift condition in Section
2.2
which is then leveraged to establish geometric ergodicity. Even a minor deviation in the structure of the Markov chain (such as in the JOB Gibbs sampler or the Gibbs sampler of
[
12
]
) leads to a breakdown of the intricate argument.

Before proceeding further, we note that geometric ergodicity of a two-block Gibbs sampler can be established by showing that any of its two marginal chains is geometrically ergodic (see for example
[
19
]
). Hence, we focus on the marginal
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
-chain corresponding to
K_{aug}
. The one-step transition dynamics of this Markov chain from
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m}
to
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m+1}
is given as follows:

1.

Draw
\tau^{2}
from
\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)

2.

Draw
\boldsymbol{\nu}
from
\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j;m}^{2}}\right)

3.

Draw
\sigma^{2}
from
\pi\left(\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(a+\frac{n}{2},\frac{\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\tilde{P}_{\mathbf{X}}\right)\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}}{2}+b\right)

4.

Draw
\boldsymbol{\beta}
from
\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\mathcal{N}_{p}(A^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},\sigma^{2}A^{-1})

5.

Finally draw
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m+1}
from
\pi\left(\left.\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)

-chain is given by

\displaystyle k\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}}\pi\left(\left.\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

\displaystyle\times\pi\left(\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

2.2
A drift condition for the
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
-chain

Consider the function
V:\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}\mapsto[0,\infty)
given by

V\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{\frac{\delta_{0}}{2}}+\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}},

(2.4)

where
\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
are some constants. The next result establishes a geometric drift condition for the marginal
{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}
-chain using the function
V
with appropriately small values of
\delta_{0}
and
\delta_{1}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Suppose the prior density
\pi_{\tau}
for the global shrinkage parameter is
truncated below i.e.,
\pi_{\tau}(u)=0
for

for some
T>0
and satisfies

\int_{T}^{\infty}u^{\delta/2}\pi_{\tau}(u)du<\infty

\delta\in(0.00162,0.22176)
.
Then, there exist
\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\in(0,1)
such that for every

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
we have

\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.V\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right]=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}}k\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)V(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})d{\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}}\leq\gamma^{*}V\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right)+b^{*}

(2.5)

with
0<\gamma^{*}=\gamma^{*}\left(\delta_{0},\delta_{1}\right)<1
and

.

3
Geometric ergodicity for regularized Horseshoe Gibbs samplers

3.1
A Gibbs sampler for the regularized Horseshoe

Recall from the introduction that the regularized Horseshoe prior
developed in
Piironen, Vehtari 2017

[
17
]
is given by

\displaystyle\left.\beta_{i}\right.\mid\lambda_{i}^{2},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}\sim\mathcal{N}_{p}\left(0,\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{i}^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)^{-1}\sigma^{2}\right)\text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{i}\right.\sim C^{+}(0,1)\text{independently for}\ i=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\tau^{2}\sim\pi_{\tau}(\cdot)\hskip 14.45377pt\sigma^{2}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}(a,b)

(3.1)

The only difference between this prior and the original Horseshoe prior
in (
1.1
) is the additional regularization introduced in
the the prior conditional variance of the
\beta_{i}
s through the constant
c
. As
c\rightarrow\infty
in (
3.1
), then one reverts back to the
original Horseshoe specification in (
1.1
).

Note that one of the salient features of the Horseshoe prior is the lack of shrinkage/regularization of parameter values that are far away from zero. The authors in
[
17
]
argue that while this feature is one of the key strengths of the Horseshoe prior in many situations, it can be a drawback in settings where the parameters are weakly identified. We refer the reader to
[
17
]
for a thorough motivation and discussion of the properties and performance of this prior vis-a-vis the Horseshoe prior. Our focus in this paper is to look at Markov chains to sample from the resulting intractable regularized Horseshoe posterior, and investigate properties such as geometric ergodicity.

The authors in
[
17
]
use Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) to generate samples from the posterior distribution. Geometric ergodicity of this HMC chain, however, is not established. In recent work
[
10
]
, sufficient conditions for geometric ergodicity (or lack thereof) for general HMC chains have been provided. However, these conditions, namely Assumptions A1, A2, A3 in
[
10
]
, are rather complex and intricate, and at least to the best of our understanding it is unclear and hard to verify if these conditions are satisfied by the HMC chain in
[
17
]
.

Given the host of Gibbs samplers available in the literature for the original Horseshoe posterior, it is natural to consider a Gibbs sampler to sample from the regularized Horseshoe posterior as well. In fact, after introducing the augmented variables
\left\{\nu_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
, the following conditional posterior distributions can be obtained after straightforward computations:

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},\sigma^{2}A_{c}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(a+\frac{n}{2},\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\mathbf{X}A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)

\displaystyle\left.\nu_{j}\right|\lambda_{j}^{2},\mathbf{y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right),\ \text{independently for}\ j=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}g\left(\left.\lambda_{j}^{2}\right|\nu_{j},\beta_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\sim\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\right)

(3.2)

where

g\left(\left.\lambda_{j}^{2}\right|\nu_{j},\beta_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right)\propto\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}

for
j=1,2,\cdots,p
,

\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\right)\propto\left|A_{c}\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\left(\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\mathbf{X}A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)

and
A_{c}=\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\left(\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)^{-1}+c^{-2}I_{p}
. Most of the above densities are standard and can be easily sampled from. Efficient rejection/Metropolis samplers can be used to sample from the one-dimensional non-standard densities
g\left(\left.\lambda_{j}^{2}\right|\nu_{j},\beta_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right)
and
\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\right)
(see in Appendix
D
). Hence, a two-block Gibbs sampler, whose one step-transition from
({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0},\tau^{2}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0})
to

({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})
is given by sampling sequentially from
\pi({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2}\mid{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{y})
and

\pi({\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\mid{\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y})
, can be used to generate approximate samples from the regularized Horseshoe posterior. We will denote the transition kernel of this two-block Gibbs sampler by
K_{aug,reg}
(analogous to
K_{aug}
in the original Horseshoe setting).

Our goal now is to establish geometric ergodicity for
K_{aug,reg}
. We
will achieve this by focusing on the marginal
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-chain corresponding to

K_{aug,reg}
. The one-step transition dynamics of this Markov chain from

\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m}
to
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{m+1}
is given as follows:

1.

Draw
\tau^{2}
from
\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)

2.

Draw
\boldsymbol{\nu}
from
\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j;m}^{2}}\right)

3.

Draw
\sigma^{2}
from
\pi\left(\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(a+\frac{n}{2},\frac{\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\mathbf{X}A_{c}^{-1}X^{T}\right)\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}}{2}+b\right)

4.

Draw
\boldsymbol{\beta}
from
\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\mathcal{N}_{p}(A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},\sigma^{2}A_{c}^{-1})

5.

Finally draw
\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{m+1}
from
\pi\left(\left.\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)=\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}g\left(\left.\lambda_{j}^{2}\right|\nu_{j},\beta_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right)
.

The Markov transition density (MTD) corresponding to the marginal
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-chain is given by

\displaystyle k\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}}\pi\left(\left.\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

(3.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}_{+}}\pi\left(\left.\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\nu,\mathbf{y}}\right)

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\times

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\nu,\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right)d\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

3.2
Drift and minorization analysis for the regularized Horseshoe

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-chain

The geometric ergodicity of the
\boldsymbol{\lambda}
-chain will be established using a
drift and minorization analysis. However, given the modifications in the
regularized Horseshoe posterior, the drift function
V(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
(see
(
2.4
)) used for the original Horseshoe does not work in
this case. We will instead use another drift function
\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})

defined by

\tilde{V}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)=\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}};\ \text{for some constant}\ \delta\in(0,1).

(3.4)

As discussed previously, the function
V(\boldsymbol{\lambda})
is unbounded off petite sets and the
V
-based drift condition in Lemma
2.1
is enough to guarantee geometric ergodicity for the original Horseshoe Gibbs sampler

K_{aug}
. A minorization condition is only needed if one also wants to get quantitative convergence bounds for distance to stationarity. The function

\tilde{V}
however, is
not
unbounded off petite sets since

B\left(\tilde{V},d\right)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}:\tilde{V}(\boldsymbol{\lambda})\leq d\right\}

is not a compact subset of
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
for
d>0
. Hence, a drift
condition with
\tilde{V}
needs to be complemented with a minorization condition in order to establish geometric ergodicity (Theorem
3.1
). We establish these
two conditions respectively in Sections
3.2.1
and
3.2.2
below. As opposed to the original Horseshoe setting,
we do not require that the prior density
\pi_{\tau}
is truncated below away from zero. Only the existence of the
\delta/2^{th}
-moment is assumed for some

\delta\in(0.00162,0.22176)
: a very mild condition, satisfied for example by the commonly used half-Cauchy density.

3.2.1
Drift condition

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}u^{\delta/2}\pi_{\tau}(u)du<\infty
for some
\delta\in(0.00162,0.22176)
. Then, there exist constants
0<\gamma^{*}=\gamma^{*}\left(\delta\right)<1
and
b^{*}<\infty
such that

\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\tilde{V}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right]\leq\gamma^{*}\tilde{V}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right)+b^{*}

(3.5)

for every
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
.

Proof.

Note that by linearity

\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\tilde{V}\left(\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}\right)\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right]=\sum_{j=1}^{p}\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right]

(3.6)

Fix
j\in\left\{1,2,\cdots,p\right\}
arbitrarily. It follows from the definition of the MTD (
3.3
) that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\right.\mid\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right]\right.\mid\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\nu,\mathbf{y}}\right]\right.\mid\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{\nu},\mathbf{y}\right]\right.\mid\tau^{2}\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}\right]\right.\mid\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{y}\right].

We begin by evaluating the innermost expectation. It follows by using
\sqrt{u+v}\leq\sqrt{u}+\sqrt{v}
for
u,v\geq 0
that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\right|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right]

(3.8)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\left(\frac{1}{|c|}+\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}}\right)\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\frac{\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}{|c|}\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad+

\displaystyle\frac{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}.

The first term in the last inequality of (
3.8
) can be expressed as

\frac{\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}{|c|}\frac{\boldsymbol{E}\left[X^{\delta}\right]}{\boldsymbol{E}\left[\sqrt{\frac{1}{c^{2}}+X^{2}}\right]}

where
\tau^{2}X^{2}\sim\text{Gamma}\left(\frac{1}{2},\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)
. Using Young’s inequality, it follows that the first term is bounded above by

\frac{\text{max}\left\{1,|c|\right\}}{|c|}\sqrt{\delta}\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}.

The second term in the last inequality of (
3.8
) is basically an Inverse-Gamma expectation, and is exactly equal to

\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}{\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}.

Hence, we get

\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}\right|\boldsymbol{\boldsymbol{\beta},\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right]\leq\frac{\text{max}\left\{1,|c|\right\}}{|c|}\sqrt{\delta}\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}+\frac{\Gamma\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)}{\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)^{\frac{\delta}{2}}}.

Note that the conditional distribution of
\beta_{j}
given
\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\mathbf{\mathbf{y}}
is a Gaussian distribution with variance
\sigma_{j}^{2}\overset{\text{def}}{=}\sigma^{2}\boldsymbol{e}_{J}^{T}A_{c}^{-1}\boldsymbol{e}_{j}\geq\sigma^{2}\left(\bar{\omega}+\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j;0}^{2}}\right)^{-1}
. Here
\bar{\omega}
is the maximum eigenvalue of
\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
. Now, proceeding exactly with the analysis
from (
2.9
) to (
2.13
) in the proof of
Lemma
2.1
with
\bar{\omega}
replaced by
\bar{\omega}+c^{-2}
and using Proposition
B.3
instead of
Proposition
B.1
yields

\boldsymbol{E}\left[\left.\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}}\right|\mathbf{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0}\right]\leq\gamma^{*}\left(\delta\right)\sum_{j=1}^{p}\left(\lambda_{j;0}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{\delta_{1}}{2}}+b^{*}

with

\gamma^{*}\left(\delta\right)=\Gamma\left(1+\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\left(\frac{1}{\left\{\Gamma\left(1-\frac{\delta}{2}\right)\right\}^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}+\frac{\sqrt{\pi}^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}{\left\{\Gamma\left(\frac{1-\delta}{2}\right)\right\}^{\frac{2}{\delta}}}\right)^{-\frac{\delta}{2}}

and

b^{*}=p\frac{\text{max}\left\{1,|c|\right\}}{|c|}\sqrt{\delta}C_{2}+p\cdot\text{max}\left\{1,(\bar{\omega}+c^{-2})^{\frac{\delta}{2}}C_{2}\right\}\cdot\gamma^{*}\left(\delta\right).

Here
C_{2}
is as in Proposition
B.3
. It can be shown that
\gamma^{*}\left(\delta\right)<1
for
\delta\in(0.00162,0.22176)
.

Hence, the required geometric drift condition has been established. ∎

3.2.2
Minorization condition

As discussed previously, the drift function
\tilde{V}
is not unbounded off compact sets, and the drift condition in Lemma
3.1
needs to be complemented by an associated minorization condition to establish geometric ergodicity. Fix a
d>0
. Define

B\left(\tilde{V},d\right)=\left\{\boldsymbol{\lambda}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}:\tilde{V}\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)\leq d\right\}

(3.9)

We now establish the following minorization condition associated to the geometric drift condition in Lemma
3.1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

There exists a constant
\epsilon^{*}=\epsilon^{*}\left(\tilde{V},d\right)>0
and a density function
h
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}
such that

k\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)\geq\epsilon^{*}h\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

(3.10)

for every
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\in B\left(\tilde{V},d\right)
.

Proof.

Fix a
\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0}\in B\left(\tilde{V},d\right)
arbitrarily. In order to prove (
3.10
) we will demonstrate appropriate lower bounds for the conditional densities appearing in (
3.3
). From (
3.1
) we have the following:

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle b^{a+\frac{n}{2}}\omega_{*}^{-\frac{p}{2}}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}|c|^{-p}\left(\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+b\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right);

where
\omega_{*}=\text{max}\left\{\bar{\omega}+c^{-2},d^{\frac{2}{\delta}}\right\}
; (recall that
\bar{\omega}
denotes the maximum eigenvalue of
\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}
),

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\left|c\right|^{-p}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp{\left[-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\Omega^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)^{T}\Omega\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-\Omega^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{X}\left(c^{2}I_{p}-\Omega^{-1}\right)\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]};

where
\Omega=\omega_{*}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)I_{p}
,

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right|\boldsymbol{\beta,\nu},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}}{\int\limits_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}d\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right\}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}}{k^{*}\left(\sqrt{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}\sqrt{\tau^{2}}}{\beta_{j}^{2}}\right)}\right\};

where
k^{*}=\text{max}\left\{\sqrt{\pi}|c|^{-1},2\right\}
, and

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\boldsymbol{\nu}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\nu_{j}^{-2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta}}\right)\right]}\right\}

\displaystyle\pi\left(\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

The following variant of regularized Horseshoe shrinkage prior has been introduced in

[

\displaystyle\pi\left(\beta_{j},\lambda_{j}\mid\tau^{2},\sigma^{2}\right)\propto\frac{1}{\sqrt{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}}\exp{\left[-\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)\right]}\pi_{\ell}\left(\lambda_{j}\right)

\displaystyle\text{independently for}\ j=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\sigma^{2}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(a,b\right);\ \tau^{2}\sim\pi_{\tau}\left(\cdot\right)

(3.12)

where
\pi_{\ell}

above specification

identical to the specification in

\boldsymbol{\lambda},\tau^{2}

(3.13)

where

(3.14)

The principal motivation for the algebraic modification of the prior as compared to
that of
[
17
]
is the resulting simplification of the posterior
computation, although an alternative interpretation using fictitious data is also
discussed in
[
14
]
. In fact, using
\pi_{\ell}
to be the half-Cauchy density
and using its representation in terms of a mixture of Inverse-Gamma densities (
[
12
]
) the following conditional
posterior distributions can be obtained from straightforward computations after augmenting the latent

\displaystyle\left.\boldsymbol{\beta}\right|\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},\sigma^{2}A_{c}^{-1}\right)

\displaystyle\left.\sigma^{2}\right|\tau^{2},\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(a+\frac{n}{2},\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\mathbf{X}A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)

\displaystyle\left.\nu_{j}\right|\lambda_{j}^{2},\mathbf{y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right),\ \text{independently for}\ j=1,2,\cdots,p

\displaystyle\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\sim\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\right)

\displaystyle\left.\lambda_{j}^{2}\right|\nu_{j},\beta_{j},\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\sim\text{Inverse-Gamma}\left(1,\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}+\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right),\ \text{independently for}\ j=1,2,\cdots,p

\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\right)\propto\left|\tau^{2}A_{c}\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\left(\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\mathbf{X}A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)c(\tau^{2})^{p}

are standard probability distributions (as opposed to the non-standard ones in the regularized Horseshoe posterior in (

can be constructed similar to the one provided in Appendix
D
. Hence, a two-block Gibbs sampler, whose one step-transition from
({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{0},\sigma^{2}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\nu}}_{0},\tau^{2}_{0},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0})

({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2},{\boldsymbol{\lambda}})

\pi({\boldsymbol{\beta}},\sigma^{2},{\boldsymbol{\nu}},\tau^{2}\mid{\boldsymbol{\lambda}}_{0},\mathbf{y})
and

, can be used to generate approximate samples from the regularized Horseshoe posterior in (
3.3
). We will denote the Markov transition kernel of this two-block Gibbs sampler by
\tilde{K}_{aug,reg}
(analogous to
K_{aug,reg}
in the regularized Horseshoe setting). The transition
density can be obtained by substituting the appropriate conditional posterior densities in the expression (
3.3
).

Note that the above conditional posterior distributions are very similar to that for the original Horseshoe Gibbs sampler
K_{aug}
given by (

) in Section

. The only differences are

1.

the matrix

appearing in (

(which is the matrix

\boldsymbol{\beta}

), and

Theorem 3.2
.

Suppose the prior density of the global shrinkage parameter is truncated below away
from zero; that is,
\pi_{\tau}(u)=0
for

\int_{T}^{\infty}u^{\frac{p+\delta}{2}}\pi_{\tau}(u)du<\infty

for some

The above theorem can be proved by essentially following verbatim the proof
of Lemma
2.1
(which establishes geometric ergodicity for

) with the same geometric drift function as in
Lemma
2.1
, and replacing the matrix
A

relevant places. However, appropriate modifications are needed using the following two facts.

1.

In the original Horseshoe setting, a uniform upper bound for the conditional posterior means of
\beta_{j}^{\prime}

Remark 3.1
.

In
[
14
]
, the authors focus on Bayesian logistic regression for their geometric ergodicity analysis. They use the regularized Horseshoe prior in (
3.3
) without the parameter
\sigma^{2}
as their is no need for an error variance parameter for the Binomial likelihood. However, for computational purposes, additional parameters
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}=\left\{\omega_{j}\right\}_{j=1}^{p}
with Polya-Gamma prior distributions are introduced. A two-block Gibbs sampler with blocks
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and
({\boldsymbol{\omega}},\tau^{2})
is then constructed and its geometric ergodicity is then established assuming that the global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
is bounded away from zero and infinity

[
14
, Theorem 4.6]
.

Many details of this analysis break down when translating to the Bayesian linear
regression framework considered in our paper. The parameters

{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
are now replaced by the error variance parameter
\sigma^{2}
.
One can still construct a two-block Gibbs sampler with blocks
(\boldsymbol{\beta},\boldsymbol{\lambda})
and

(\sigma^{2},\tau^{2})
, but many conditional independence and other algebraic niceties
involving
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
which are crucial in establishing the
minorization condition in the logistic regression context, do not hold analogously
with
\sigma^{2}
in the linear regression context. The structural differences also
imply that the drift condition with the function
\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}|\beta_{j}|^{-\delta}

does not work out in the linear regression setting.

Remark 3.2
.

The geometric ergodicity result (Theorem
3.2
)
corresponding to the regularized Horseshoe variant in
[
14
]

requires truncation of the global shrinkage parameter
\tau^{2}
below away from zero.
Such an assumption is not required for the geometric ergodicity result
(Theorem
3.1
) corresponding to the regularized Horseshoe of

[
17
]
. Also, due to the presence of the additional term

(c(\tau^{2}))^{p}
in
\pi\left(\left.\tau^{2}\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\mathbf{y}\right)
, a stronger moment assumption is required for Theorem
3.2
as compared to Theorem
3.1
.

Again, both algorithms were implemented in

2500

Proposition A.1
.

Let
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be any diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p}
. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be any matrix with rank
r
. Let the singular value decomposition of
X
is
X=UDV^{T}
where
D\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
is diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
d_{1},\ldots d_{r}
while

and

are such that
U^{T}U=I
and
V^{T}V=I

component of the vector
\tilde{u}=U^{T}y
and
P_{U^{\perp}}
is the orthogonal projection matrix for the orthogonal complement of the column space of

Proof.

Without loss of generality we assume that the matrix
\Lambda
is diagonal matrix with diagonal elements
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p}
where
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\ldots\leq\lambda_{p}.
According to the condition of the result
\lambda\leq\lambda_{1}
. Now we define a set of diagonal matrices
\{\Lambda_{(1)},\ldots,\Lambda_{(j)},\ldots,\Lambda_{(p)}\}
in the following manner. For
j=1,\ldots(p-1)
, the matrix
\Lambda_{(j)}
has first
j
diagonal elements to be identical and equal to
\lambda
while rest of the
(p-j)
diagonal elements are identical as that of the matrix
\Lambda
. Also let
\Lambda_{(p)}=\lambda I_{p\times p}
and
\Lambda_{(0)}=\Lambda
. The above set of matrices satisfy the following relation

\Lambda_{(j-1)}=\Lambda_{(j)}+(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}e_{j}^{T}\text{ for }j=1,\ldots,p

where
e_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denotes the
j^{\text{th}}
elementary vector. Now using the Sherman-Woodbury formula for inverting matrices we get that

\displaystyle\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j-1)}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}+(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}e_{j}^{T}\right)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}-\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}}{1-(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j-1)}\right)^{-1}-\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}=-\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}}{1-(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}},

(A.1)

Aggregating the equations
A.1
over
j=1,\ldots p
, we get that

\displaystyle\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda\right)^{-1}=\left(X^{T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}}{1-(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}},

(A.2)

where we have used the fact that
\Lambda_{(0)}=\Lambda
and
\Lambda_{(p)}=\lambda I
. If
y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
be arbitrary vector then it follows from
A.2
that

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda\right)^{-1}X^{T}y

(A.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}X^{T}y-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}X^{T}y}{1-(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}X^{T}y-\sum_{j=1}^{p}\frac{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)\|e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}X^{T}y\|^{2}}{1-(\lambda_{j}-\lambda)e_{j}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda_{(j)}\right)^{-1}e_{j}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}X^{T}y,

because
\lambda_{j}\geq\lambda
.
Now consider the singular value decomposition of the matrix
X=UDV^{T}
where
D
is a diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
d_{1},\ldots,d_{r}
,
U\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r},V\in R^{p\times r}
such that
U^{T}U=V^{T}V=I
. Also let
V^{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times(p-r)}
be such that the matrix
\left[V,V^{\perp}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
is a orthogonal matrix, i.e. the columns of
V^{\perp}
constitutes a orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement of the column space of
V
. Now consider the fact that

\displaystyle(X^{T}X+\lambda I)^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[VDU^{T}UDV^{T}+\lambda I\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[VD^{2}V^{T}+\lambda VV^{T}+\lambda V^{\perp}(V^{\perp})^{T}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[V(D^{2}+\lambda I)V^{T}+\lambda V^{\perp}(V^{\perp})^{T}\right]^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle V(D^{2}+\lambda I)^{-1}V^{T}+\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\perp}(V^{\perp})^{T}.

Note that
(V^{\perp})^{T}V=0
. Thus

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\lambda I\right)^{-1}X^{T}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left[V(D^{2}+\lambda I)^{-1}V^{T}+\frac{1}{\lambda}V^{\perp}(V^{\perp})^{T}\right]VDU^{T}y

(A.4)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{T}(UDV^{T})V(D^{2}+\lambda I)^{-1}V^{T}VDU^{T}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle y^{T}UD(D^{2}+\lambda I)^{-1}DU^{T}y

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{d_{i}^{2}\tilde{u}^{2}_{i}}{d_{i}^{2}+\lambda},

where
d_{1},\ldots d_{r}>0
are the diagonal elements of the matrix
D
and
\tilde{u}_{i}
is the
i^{\text{th}}
entry of the vector
U^{T}y
. Let
U^{\perp}
refers to the orthogonal completion of the matrix
U
then

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}=y^{T}UU^{T}y=y^{T}y-y^{T}U^{\perp}(U^{\perp})^{T}y=y^{T}y-\|P_{U^{\perp}}y\|^{2},

(A.5)

where
P_{U^{\perp}}
denotes the orthogonal projection for the column space of
{U^{\perp}}
. Finally, it follows from
A.3
,
A.4
and
A.5
that

\displaystyle y^{T}X\left(X^{T}X+\Lambda\right)^{-1}X^{T}y\leq\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda\tilde{u}^{2}_{i}}{d_{i}^{2}+\lambda}=y^{T}y-\|P_{U^{\perp}}y\|^{2}-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda\tilde{u}^{2}_{i}}{d_{i}^{2}+\lambda}.

Note that
\|P_{U^{\perp}}y\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda\tilde{u}^{2}_{i}}{d_{i}^{2}+\lambda}>0
beacuse
\sum_{i=1}^{r}\tilde{u}_{i}^{2}+\|P_{U^{\perp}}y\|^{2}=y^{T}y>0
.
∎

Proposition A.3
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be arbitrary matrix and
\Delta_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be any diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}
. Consider the following partition of the matrix

X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\|{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}+\delta_{1}&{\bf x}_{1}^{T}X_{2}\\
\hline\cr X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}&X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1}\end{array}\right],

where
\Delta_{p-1}
is the diagonal matrix with diagonal elements
\delta_{2},\ldots,\delta_{p}
. If
(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}\Delta_{p-1}
is uniformly bounded, then the first column of the matrix

\left(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{p}

is uniformly bounded. The notations
{\bf x}_{1}
and
X_{2}
are as they are defined in the Result
A.2
.

Proof.

If we consider the partition of

X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\|{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}+\delta_{1}&{\bf x}_{1}^{T}X_{2}\\
\hline\cr X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}&X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1}\end{array}\right],

then the Schur complement of the first block of the matrix is given as

T=(\|{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}+\delta_{1})-{\bf x}_{1}^{T}X_{2}(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}.

Employing the inversion formula of the block matrices
[
11
]
, we get that

\displaystyle(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\frac{1}{T}&\frac{{\bf x}_{1}^{T}X_{2}(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}}{T}\\
\hline\cr\frac{(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}}{T}&(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}+\frac{(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}{\bf x}_{1}^{T}X_{2}(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}}{T}\end{array}\right].

In the next two bullet points, we are going to show if
(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}\Delta_{p-1}
is uniformly bounded then so is all the entries of the vector

\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{\delta_{1}}{T}&\frac{\delta_{1}(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}}{T}\end{array}\right]^{T},

which is the first column of the matrix
(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}
.

•

To show
0<\frac{\delta_{1}}{T}\leq 1:

It is evident from the Result
A.2
that
T>0
. Therefore
\frac{\delta_{1}}{T}>0
as
\delta_{1}>0
as well. On the other hand, a direct implication of Result
A.2
is that for
0<\delta\leq\min\{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}\}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{1}}{T}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{\delta_{1}}{\delta_{1}+\|P_{U^{\perp}}{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\delta\tilde{u}^{2}_{i}}{d_{i}^{2}+\delta}}\leq 1.

(A.8)

where the details about the notations
\tilde{u}_{i}
,
P_{U^{\perp}},d_{i}
can be found in Result
A.2
.

•

To show
\frac{\delta_{1}(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}}{T}
uniformly bounded:

Let
{\bf v}_{1},{\bf v}_{2}
be such that
{\bf x}_{1}={\bf v}_{1}+{\bf v}_{2}
where
{\bf v}_{1}
belongs to the column space of
X_{2}
and
{\bf v}_{2}
belongs to the orthogonal complement of the column space of
X_{2}
. Therefore
{\bf v}_{1}=X_{2}l
for some vector
l\in\mathbb{R}^{p-1}
. Consequently,

\displaystyle(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}(X_{2}l+{\bf v}_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}X_{2}l

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left[I-(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}\Delta_{p-1}\right]l,

is uniformly bounded as we are assuming that the matrix
(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}\Delta_{p-1}
is uniformly bounded. Combining this fact along with
A.8
, we conclude that all the entries of the vector
\frac{\delta_{1}(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}}{T}
are also uniformly bounded.

Proposition A.4
.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be arbitrary matrix and
\Delta_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be any diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}
. Then for arbitrary
p
and
n
the matrix
\left(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{p}
is uniformly bounded. Specifically

\sup_{\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}>0}\left|e_{i}^{T}\left(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{p}e_{j}\right|<C

where
C
is a finite constant that does not depend on
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}
.

Proof.

We will show the result by induction on the integer
k
where the hypothesis of induction is as follows,

\mathcal{H}(k):
Let
n
be arbitrary positive integer. Then for any positive integer
k
, the matrix
\left(X^{T}X+\Delta_{k}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{k}
is uniformly bounded for all
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times k}
and arbitrary diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
\Delta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{k\times k}
.

Initial step:
The hypothesis trivially holds for
k=1
. We will show that
\mathcal{H}(k)
is true for the case
k=2.
Let
X=\left[{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}\right]\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times 2}
and
\Delta_{2}=\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{1}&0\\
0&\delta_{2}\end{bmatrix}
,
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0
be arbitrary. Define

A:=\begin{bmatrix}a_{1,1}&a_{1,2}\\
a_{2,1}&a_{2,2}\end{bmatrix}:=X^{T}X\text{ and }\text{ then }

\displaystyle\left(A+\Delta_{2}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{2}=\frac{1}{(a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{1,2}a_{2,1})+\delta_{1}a_{2,2}+\delta_{2}a_{1,1}+\delta_{1}\delta_{2}}\begin{bmatrix}\delta_{1}(a_{2,2}+\delta_{2})&-\delta_{2}a_{2,1}\\
-\delta_{1}a_{1,2}&\delta_{2}(a_{1,1}+\delta_{1})\end{bmatrix}

•

Note that

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0}\left|e_{1}^{T}\left(A+\Delta_{2}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{2}e_{1}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0}\frac{\delta_{1}(a_{2,2}+\delta_{2})}{(a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{1,2}a_{2,1})+\delta_{1}(a_{2,2}+\delta_{2})+\delta_{2}a_{1,1}}\leq 1

because
(a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{1,2}a_{2,1})\geq 0
as
X^{T}X
is nonnegative definte matrix. Additionally
a_{1,1}=\|{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}\geq 0
and
a_{2,2}=\|{\bf x}_{2}\|^{2}\geq 0
, where
X=[{\bf x}_{1},{\bf x}_{2}].

•

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0}\left|e_{1}^{T}\left(A+\Delta_{2}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{2}e_{2}\right|

(A.9)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0}\frac{|a_{2,1}\delta_{2}|}{(a_{1,1}a_{2,2}-a_{1,2}a_{2,1})+\delta_{1}(a_{2,2}+\delta_{2})+\delta_{2}a_{1,1}}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{|a_{2,1}|}{a_{1,1}}

for the case when
a_{1,1}\neq 0
. On the contrary, if
a_{1,1}=0
then it follows from
A.9
that

\displaystyle\sup_{\delta_{1},\delta_{2}>0}\left|e_{1}^{T}\left(A+\Delta_{2}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{2}e_{2}\right|

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

(A.10)

because
a_{1,1}=\|{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}=0
implies that
a_{2,1}={\bf x}_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}=0
.

In a similar fashion we can show that the absolute value of the other two entries of the matrix
\left(X^{T}X+\Delta_{2}\right)^{-1}\Delta_{2}
can be bounded above by numbers that does not depend on
\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
. Consequently
\mathcal{H}(k)
holds for
k=2.

Induction step:

Let
\mathcal{H}(k)
holds for
k=1,2,\ldots,(p-1)
. We will show that the result holds for
k=p
as well. Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be arbitrary matrix and
\Delta_{p}
for diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}
. Consider the partition of the matrices
X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}
as follows

\displaystyle X^{T}X+\Delta_{p}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\|{\bf x}_{1}\|^{2}+\delta_{1}&{\bf x}_{1}^{T}X_{2}\\
\hline\cr X_{2}^{T}{\bf x}_{1}&X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1}\end{array}\right],

where
X_{2},{\bf x}_{1},\Delta_{p-1}
are as it is in Result
A.2
. As it satisfies the conditions of the induction hypothesis
\mathcal{H}{(p-1)}
, the matrix
(X_{2}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta_{p-1})^{-1}\Delta_{p-1}
is uniformly bounded. Therefore using Result
A.3
, the first column of the matrix
(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}
is uniformly bounded.

In remaining of the proof, we show that the
m^{\text{th}}
column of
(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}
is uniformly bounded for any
m>1
. Consider the permutation matrix
P_{1,m}=\left[e_{m},e_{2}\ldots,e_{m-1},e_{1}\ldots,e_{p}\right]
. Note that
P_{1,m}
can be generated by exchanging the
1^{\text{st}}
and
m^{\text{th}}
columns of an identity matrix.
P_{1,m}
is a symmetric and orthogonal matrix, i.e.
P_{1,m}^{T}=P_{1,m}
and
P_{1,m}^{T}P_{1,m}=P_{1,m}^{2}=I
. Now consider

\displaystyle P_{1,m}(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}P_{1,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{1,m}(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}P_{1,m}P_{1,m}\Delta_{p}P_{1,m}

(A.14)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(P_{1,m}^{T}X^{T}XP_{1,m}+P_{1,m}\Delta_{p}P_{1,m})^{-1}P_{1,m}\Delta_{p}P_{1,m}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle({X^{*}}^{T}X^{*}+\Delta_{p}^{*})^{-1}\Delta_{p}^{*},

where the
X^{*}:=XP_{1,m}
is obtained by exchanging the first and
m^{\text{th}}
columns of
X
while
\Delta_{p}^{*}:=P_{1,m}\Delta_{p}P_{1,m}
is the diagonal matrix where the first and the
m^{\text{th}}
diagonal elements of
\Delta_{p}
are exchanged. We can represent
{X^{*}}^{T}X^{*}+\Delta^{*}_{p}
as

\left[\begin{array}[]{c|c}\|{\bf x}^{*}_{1}\|^{2}+\delta^{*}_{1}&{{\bf x}^{*}_{1}}^{T}X^{*}_{2}\\
\hline\cr{X_{2}^{*}}^{T}{\bf x}^{*}_{1}&{X^{*}_{2}}^{T}X_{2}+\Delta^{*}_{p-1}\end{array}\right],

where the notations are equivalent to that of the ones in
A
. The matrix
({X^{*}_{2}}^{T}X^{*}_{2}+\Delta^{*}_{p-1})^{-1}\Delta^{*}_{p-1}
satisfies the conditions of the induction hypothesis
\mathcal{H}{(p-1)}
, thus it is uniformly bounded. Therefore using Result
A.3
, the first column of the matrix
({X^{*}}^{T}X^{*}+\Delta^{*}_{p})^{-1}\Delta^{*}_{p}
is uniformly bounded as well.
It follows from
A.14
that the permuted version of the first column of
({X^{*}}^{T}X^{*}+\Delta_{p}^{*})^{-1}\Delta_{p}^{*}
is

\displaystyle P_{1,m}\left[({X^{*}}^{T}X^{*}+\Delta_{p}^{*})^{-1}\Delta_{p}^{*}e_{1}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle P_{1,m}P_{1,m}(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}P_{1,m}e_{1}=\left[(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}\right]e_{m},

which is the
m^{\text{th}}
column of
(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}
.
Therefore, we infer that all the columns of the matrix
(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}\Delta_{p}
are uniformly bounded and conclude that
\mathcal{H}(k)
holds for the case
k=p
.

∎

Proposition A.5
.

X\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times p}
be arbitrary matrix and
\Delta_{p}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
be any diagonal matrix with positive diagonal elements
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}

p
and

1.

is a finite constant that does not depend on
\delta_{1},\ldots,\delta_{p}
.

2.

The vector

Proof.

part(1):
Note that

part(2):

where
{\bf v}_{1}

and
{\bf v}_{2}
belongs to the perpendicular to the column space of

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(X^{T}X+\Delta_{p})^{-1}X^{T}(Xl+{\bf v}_{2})

\displaystyle=

Therefore part(a) of the result ensures that the

Proposition B.1

\displaystyle\left(2\pi\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\left(\omega_{*}\right)^{-p/2}d^{-p/\delta_{0}}\left(\frac{b}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}/2+b}\right)^{a+\frac{n}{2}}\frac{b^{a+\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{[T,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}^{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\nu_{j}^{-2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\nu_{j}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)\right]}\right\}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp{\left[-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)^{T}M\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\left(\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\exp{\left[-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I-\mathbf{X}M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+2b}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n+p}{2}\right)-1}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right\}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-p/2}\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-p/2}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)d\boldsymbol{\nu}d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

Next we perform the inner integral wrt
\boldsymbol{\nu}
and noting that
1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\leq\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)
we have:

\displaystyle k\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(2\pi\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\left(\omega_{*}\right)^{-p/2}d^{-p/\delta_{0}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}\right)^{-p}\left(\frac{b}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}/2+b}\right)^{a+\frac{n}{2}}\frac{b^{a+\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{[T,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\exp{\left[-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)^{T}M\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\left(\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n+p}{2}\right)-1}\exp{\left[-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I-\mathbf{X}M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+2b}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right\}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-p/2}\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-3p/2}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

Now recall that
M=\omega^{*}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)I_{p}
. Hence

\displaystyle\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)^{T}M\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)+\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\left(\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)^{-1}\boldsymbol{\beta}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\left(M+\left(\tau^{2}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\beta}-2\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{X}M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle\leq\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}Q\boldsymbol{\beta}-2\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{X}M^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}

\displaystyle=\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)^{T}Q\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{X}\left(M^{-1}-Q^{-1}\right)\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}

where
Q=\omega^{*}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)\left(I_{p}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}^{-1}\right).
Hence it follows that

\displaystyle k\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(\omega_{*}\right)^{-p/2}d^{-p/\delta_{0}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}\right)^{-p}\left(\frac{b}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}/2+b}\right)^{a+\frac{n}{2}}\frac{b^{a+\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{[T,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{p}}\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\left(2\pi\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\frac{p}{2}}\mid Q\mid^{1/2}\exp{\left[-\frac{\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)^{T}Q\left(\boldsymbol{\beta}-Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y}\right)}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\mid Q\mid^{-1/2}\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)-1}\exp{\left[-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I-\mathbf{X}Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+2b}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right\}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-p/2}\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-3p/2}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)d\boldsymbol{\beta}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

Note that if
\boldsymbol{\beta}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{y},\sigma^{2}Q^{-1}\right)
then the inner most integral wrt
\boldsymbol{\beta}
is equal to

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{E}\left[\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\beta_{j}^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2\sigma^{2}\right)^{-p}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{E\left[\beta_{j}^{2}\right]\right\};\ \text{since $Q$ is a diagonal matrix, $\beta_{j}^{\prime}$s are indep.}

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\left(2\sigma^{2}\right)^{-p}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\text{Var}\left[\beta_{j}\right]\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(2\omega^{*}\right)^{-p}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)^{-p}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{-1}\right\}

Also, noting that
\mid Q\mid=(\omega^{*})^{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}}\right)^{p}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)
we have the following lower bound:

\displaystyle k\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{-p}\left(\omega_{*}\right)^{-p/2}\left(\omega^{*}\right)^{-3p/2}d^{-p/\delta_{0}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}\right)^{-p}\left(\frac{b}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}/2+b}\right)^{a+\frac{n}{2}}\frac{b^{a+\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{[T,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)-1}\exp{\left[-\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I-\mathbf{X}Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}+\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+2b}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{-5/2}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right\}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-2p}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

Further noting that
\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I-\mathbf{X}Q^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}\leq\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}
we have:

\displaystyle k\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{-p}\left(\omega_{*}\right)^{-p/2}\left(\omega^{*}\right)^{-3p/2}d^{-p/\delta_{0}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}\right)^{-p}\left(\frac{b}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}/2+b}\right)^{a+\frac{n}{2}}\frac{b^{a+\frac{n}{2}}}{\Gamma\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\int_{[T,\infty)}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left(\sigma^{2}\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)-1}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}(\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+b)\right]}

\displaystyle\times

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(1+\frac{1}{\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{-5/2}\left(\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{-2}\right\}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-2p}d\sigma^{2}d\tau^{2}

Integrating wrt
\sigma^{2}
we have:

\displaystyle k\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}_{0},\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle 2^{-p}\left(\omega_{*}\right)^{-p/2}\left(\omega^{*}\right)^{-3p/2}d^{-p/\delta_{0}}\left(1+d^{\frac{2}{\delta_{1}}}\right)^{-p}\left(\frac{b}{\mathbf{y}^{T}\mathbf{y}+b}\right)^{2a+n}

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\frac{\sqrt{\lambda_{j}^{2}}}{\left(1+\lambda_{j}^{2}\right)^{5/2}}\right\}\int_{T}^{\infty}\left(1+\tau^{2}\right)^{-2p}\pi_{\tau}\left(\tau^{2}\right)d\tau^{2}

\displaystyle\epsilon^{*}h\left(\boldsymbol{\lambda}\right)

Hence, the minorization condition for the MTD (

\boldsymbol{\lambda}
and
\tau^{2}
for
K_{aug,reg}

D.1
Rejection sampler for
\boldsymbol{\lambda}

Recall that the target distribution
g\left(\left.\cdot\right|\nu,\beta,\sigma^{2},\tau^{2},\mathbf{y}\right)
has density proportion to the function
\phi(\cdot)
where

\phi(x)=\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{\tau^{2}x}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{-\frac{3}{2}}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}

Consider a probability density function
\psi
on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
as follows:

\psi(x)=\frac{\mid c\mid^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}}{\sqrt{\pi}\left(\mid c\mid^{-1}+\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\right)}x^{-3/2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}+\frac{\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)}{\left(\mid c\mid^{-1}+\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-1/2}\right)}x^{-2}\exp{\left[-\frac{1}{x}\left(\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}\right)\right]}

Note that the above is a convex combination of two Inverse-Gamma densities and is easy to sample from.
After simple algebraic manipulation, one can show that

\sup_{x\in(0,\infty)}\frac{\phi(x)}{\psi(x)}\leq M,

where

M=\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\mid c\mid^{-1}+\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-1/2}}{\sqrt{\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}}+\frac{\mid c\mid^{-1}+\left(\tau^{2}\right)^{-1/2}}{\frac{1}{\nu}+\frac{\beta^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}\tau^{2}}}

We apply the following algorithm:

For
i=1,2,\cdots

1.

sample
X_{i}
from
\psi(\cdot)

2.

sample
U_{i}
from the uniform distribution over
(0,1)

3.

Accept
X_{i}
if

U_{i}\leq\frac{\phi\left(X_{i}\right)}{M\psi\left(X_{i}\right)}

for all
i
; otherwise, we reject
X_{i}
.

4.

Repeat the above three steps until we reach a sample of a predetermined size, say,
p
.

D.2
Metropolis sampler for
\tau^{2}

Recall that the target distribution
\pi\left(\left.\cdot\right|\boldsymbol{\lambda},\mathbf{y}\right)
has density proportion to the function
\phi(\cdot)
where

\phi(x)=\left|A_{c}\right|^{-\frac{1}{2}}\prod\limits_{j=1}^{p}\left\{\left(\frac{1}{c^{2}}+\frac{1}{x\lambda_{j}^{2}}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\right\}\left(\frac{\mathbf{y}^{T}\left(I_{n}-\mathbf{X}A_{c}^{-1}\mathbf{X}^{T}\right)\mathbf{y}}{2}+b\right)^{-\left(a+\frac{n}{2}\right)}\pi_{\tau}\left(x\right),

A_{c}=\mathbf{X}^{T}\mathbf{X}+\left(x\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\right)^{-1}+c^{-2}I_{p}
and
\pi_{\tau}(\cdot)
is a probability density function supported on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
We will also need to pick what is called a “proposal distribution” that changes location at each iteration in the algorithm. We will call this
q\left(u\mid x\right)
.
Then the algorithm is:

1.

Choose some initial value
x_{0}
.

2.

For
i=1,\cdots,p

(a)

sample
x_{i}^{*}
from
q\left(u\mid x_{i-1}\right)
.

(b)

Set
x_{i}=x_{i}^{*}
with probalility

\alpha=\text{min}\left(\frac{\phi(x_{i}^{*})q\left(x_{i-1}\mid x_{i}^{*}\right)}{\phi(x_{i-1})q\left(x_{i}^{*}\mid x_{i-1}\right)},1\right)

otherwise set
x_{i}=x_{i-1}
.

Often times we choose
q
to be a
\mathcal{N}(x,1)
distribution. This has the convenient property of symmetry. Which means that
q\left(u\mid x\right)=q\left(x\mid u\right)
, so the quantity
\alpha
can be simplified to

\alpha=\text{min}\left(\frac{\phi(x_{i}^{*})}{\phi(x_{i-1})},1\right)

which is much easier to calculate. This variant is a called a
Metropolis sampler
.
