Projective Rectangles: Harmonic Conjugation Thanks: Flórez’s research was partially supported by a grant from The Citadel Foundation.
Rigoberto Flórez, Thomas Zaslavsky

Abstract
. A projective rectangle is like a projective plane that has different lengths in two directions. We develop harmonic conjugation in projective rectangles. We construct projective rectangles in some harmonic matroids (matroids where harmonic conjugation is defined on every collinear point triple), such as Desarguesian projective planes of finite characteristic, by harmonic conjugation from extended lift matroids based on finite fields. Similar results follow for countable fields with characteristic 0 0 . We also show that projective rectangles are almost harmonic matroids.

1.
Introduction

We generalize the concept of a projective plane to a projective rectangle, which is like a projective plane that has different sizes in two directions. Projective rectangles include projective planes as trivial examples; more broadly they are substructures found in harmonic matroids (matroids in which harmonic conjugation is well defined), in particular in full algebraic matroids, which is where we first encountered them.

Nontrivial projective rectangles have many properties not generally found in projective planes. For example, they have remarkable internal structure, as we showed in
[
3
]
and summarize in Section
2
, and they are almost, but apparently not quite, harmonic matroids (see Theorem
3.2
).
In this paper we develop the line of thought that originally inspired projective rectangles, namely the first author’s exploration of harmonic conjugation as applied to a certain matroid we call
L_{p}
(a type of complete lift matroid to be defined later in this article, closely related to the well-known Reid cycle matroid). We develop a theory of harmonic conjugation in harmonic matroids (which include Desarguesian and Moufang projective planes as well as full algebraic matroids) and show that multidimensional generalizations of
L_{p}
, generate projective rectangles by means of iterated harmonic conjugation. Our main conclusion is the existence of nontrivial projective rectangles in some harmonic matroids. We also show that a multidimensional generalization of the Reid cycle matroid is a small matroid such that, when embedded in a harmonic matroid, it generates a projective rectangle.

We wish to acknowledge the deep influence of Bernt Lindström’s ground-breaking work on abstract harmonic conjugation
[
7
,
8
]
, which, as extended by the first author in
[
2
]
, inspired this whole project.

2.
Background about projective rectangles

An
incidence structure
is a triple
({\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
of sets with
\mathcal{I}\subseteq{\mathcal{P}}\times\mathcal{L}
. The elements of
{\mathcal{P}}

are

A point

. A set

are in the same line. We say that two

intersect in a point
if they are incident with the same point.

A

is an incidence structure
({\mathcal{P}},\mathcal{L},\mathcal{I})
that satisfies the following axioms:

(A1)

Every two distinct points are incident with exactly one line.

(A2)

There exist four points with no three of them collinear.

(A3)

Every line is incident with at least three distinct points.

(A4)

There is a
special point

D
.
A line incident with

is called

. A line that is not incident with

is called

, and a point that is not

is called

.

(A5)

Each special line intersects every other line in exactly one point.

(A6)

If two ordinary lines

and
l_{2}
intersect
in a point, then every two lines that intersect both
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
in four
distinct points, intersect in a point.

Notation
: We write
\mathbb{PR}
for a projective rectangle. We write
\overline{pq}
for the unique line that contains two points

and

. After we establish the existence of projective planes in

, we use the notation

.

A projective rectangle is a combination of projective planes, in the strong sense that every two intersecting ordinary lines are lines of a substructure that is a projective plane.
For this, we clarify the notion of substructure of an incidence structure
({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{I}})
.
An
incidence substructure
of
({\mathcal{P}},{\mathcal{L}},{\mathcal{I}})
is an incidence structure
({\mathcal{P}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime})
in which

, and the incidence relation is the restriction of that in the superstructure. If
({\mathcal{P}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{L}}^{\prime},{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime})
is a projective plane, it may contain an ordinary line and all its points; we call that kind

. A subplane need not be full; it also need not be a maximal subplane. However:

Proposition 2.3
.

In a projective rectangle, every maximal projective subplane is full.

From now on, by a

, we mean there is a plane

\pi

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathbb{PR}
be a projective rectangle. The special point

is a point of every plane.
If

is a special line in the projective rectangle
\mathbb{PR}
and

is a plane in
\mathbb{PR}

is a line of
\pi

If two ordinary lines in
\mathbb{PR}
intersect in a point, then both lines are lines of a unique plane in
\mathbb{PR}
.

Given three noncollinear ordinary points in a projective rectangle

that contains all three points.
Given an ordinary line

that contains both.

A projective rectangle is nontrivial if and only if it contains more than one plane.

Corollary 2.5
.

Let
\mathbb{PR}
be a projective rectangle. Every ordinary line in
\mathbb{PR}
is a line of a plane in
\mathbb{PR}
. If

is nontrivial, then every ordinary line

Proposition 2.6
.

Suppose three lines in a projective rectangle
\mathbb{PR}
intersect pairwise in three different points. Then they are a coplanar triple.

Equivalently, if three lines intersect pairwise (i.e., are pairwise coplanar) but are not a coplanar triple, then they all intersect in the same point.

3.
Harmonic conjugation

The first key concept of this paper was that of a projective rectangle.
We now define the second: abstract harmonic conjugation (following Lindström
[

The main result in this section, Theorem

Lemma 3.1
.

A harmonic configuration in a projective rectangle is contained in a unique plane.

Proof.

At least two lines

At most three lines of

4.
Harmonic matroids

We now come to the third of our three key concepts: that of harmonic matroids (introduced in
[
1
]
).
A matroid
M
is
harmonic
if a harmonic conjugate exists
for every harmonic configuration in
M
.
Familiar examples of harmonic matroids include Desarguesian planes
and all projective spaces of rank four or more. Also, a

little Desargues plane
(also called a
Moufang
or

alternative
plane) is a harmonic matroid;
in fact, a projective plane is a harmonic matroid if and only if it is a little Desargues plane
[
10
, page 202]
. It follows that any projective plane contained in a harmonic matroid is a little Desargues plane.

Full algebraic matroids are harmonic; Lindström
[
8
]
defined them and proved this. We repeat the definition here.
Let
\mathbf{F}
and
\mathbf{K}
be two algebraically closed fields such that
\mathbf{K}
is an extension of
\mathbf{F}
that has finite
transcendence degree over
\mathbf{F}
. The
full algebraic
matroid

A(\mathbf{K}/\mathbf{F})
has ground set
\mathbf{K}
; a set
S\subseteq\mathbf{K}
is
independent if and only if its elements are algebraically independent over
\mathbf{F}
.
A full algebraic matroid
A(\mathbf{K}/\mathbf{F})
of rank 3 has the following flats:
the field
\mathbf{F}
(the flat of rank 0, i.e., transcendence degree zero over
\mathbf{F}
),
atoms or points (algebraically closed exensions of transcendence degree one over
\mathbf{F}
), lines
(algebraically closed exensions of transcendence degree two), and one plane, the field
\mathbf{K}
.

We shall discuss
algebraic embedding
of a matroid
M
over a
field
\mathbf{F}
; that means a mapping
f
from the elements of
M

into an extension field of
\mathbf{F}
, such that a subset
S
is
independent in
M
if and only if
f|_{S}
is injective and
f(S)
is
algebraically independent over
\mathbf{F}
. (Algebraic closure is not required for this concept.)

Theorem
3.2
says a nontrivial projective rectangle is almost an example of a harmonic matroid, but the theorem leaves open the question of harmonic conjugates for triples that do not include
D
but lie on a special line. Does the theorem extend to all coplanar collinear triples?
If so, all planes in the projective rectangle are little Desargues planes, even if the projective rectangle is not algebraically embeddable. This is an open question.

The purpose of this section is to develop the machinery of modular sequences, which are a kind of finite or infinite sequence generated by repeated harmonic conjugation.

We now introduce conjugate sequences in a harmonic matroid

A

is a sequence of points

such that

We observe that the points of a conjugate sequence are collinear.
A conjugate sequence is

We say that

(Note that we use the word “order” for both the “order” of a modular sequence and the “order” of a projective plane.)
For a modular sequence of order

we use the short notation
A,a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}
, but the sequence is implicitly infinite, so we still have all harmonic conjugacies
\mathrm{H}(A,a_{i};a_{i-1},a_{i+1})
for
i=m,m+1,\dots
.
In Proposition
4.3
Part (
ii
) we prove that if
A,a_{0},a_{1},\dots,a_{m-1}
is a modular sequence of order
m
, then
a_{m}=a_{0}
,
a_{m+1}=a_{1}
, …, i.e., a modular sequence is periodic.

For instance, consider
m=2
. Then
a_{2}=a_{0}
and the modular sequence is
A,a_{0},a_{1},a_{0},a_{1},\dots
. The harmonic conjugacy property
\mathrm{H}(A,a_{i};a_{i-1},a_{i+1})
becomes
\mathrm{H}(A,a_{1};a_{0},a_{0})
if
i
is odd and
\mathrm{H}(A,a_{0};a_{1},a_{1})
if
i
is even. An example is harmonic conjugation in the Fano plane.

Another example is illustrated by Figure
5.1
(in different notation). The modular sequence is
a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},x_{3},x_{4},\dots
(with
a_{0}
in the role of
A
). In this example
m=5
and
x_{5}=b_{0}
,
x_{6}=c_{0}
,
x_{7}=x_{2}
, etc.

Lemma 4.2

(Flórez
[
2
]
)
.

A modular sequence in a harmonic matroid
M
satisfies
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(S)=S
.

Figure 4.1.
The harmonic configuration
T
in Proposition
4.3
for
m=3
(solid lines) along with the additional points
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
, and
c_{i}
(where
i=2=m-1
).

Proposition 4.3
.

Suppose that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d,b_{0},b_{1})=(d,b_{0},\dots,b_{m-1})

is a modular sequence of order
m
in a harmonic matroid
M
.
If
c_{1},c_{0},a_{1},a_{0}
are points of
M
with
T:=\mathrm{HP}(d,b_{0},b_{1};c_{1},c_{0},a_{1},a_{0})
,
then the following properties hold:

(i)

m
is a prime number.

(ii)

We have
b_{m+i}=b_{i}
for all
i\geq 0
.

(iii)

\pi:=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(T)
is a Desarguesian projective plane
of order
m
in
M
.

(iv)

There are points
a_{0},\dots,a_{m-1}
in
M
such that

d,a_{0},\dots,a_{m-1}
is a modular sequence of order
m
and
\{a_{i},b_{i},c_{0}\}
is a
collinear set.

(v)

\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d,b_{0},b_{1})
is a line of
\pi
.

(vi)

If
l
is a line in
T
, then
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(l)
is a line of
\pi=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(T)
.

Proof.

Much of the proposition can easily be proven by modifying the hypothesis in the proof of Proposition 16 in
[
2
]
, but since it plays an important role in this paper we include a complete proof.

Let
d,b_{0},\dots,b_{m-1}
be all the points of the modular sequence

\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d,b_{0},b_{1})
, so
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{1};b_{0},b_{2})
,
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{2};b_{1},b_{3})
,
\dots,\mathrm{H}(d,b_{m-1};b_{m-2},b_{m})

and
b_{m}
is the first point that equals one of its preceding points.

Most of the proof is based upon a property
P(t)
, proved using mathematical induction. For
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, let
P(t)

be the statement that there is a point
a_{t}
in
M
such that
\{a_{t},b_{t},c_{0}\}
,
\{a_{t},b_{t+1},c_{1}\}
and

\{d,a_{0},\dots,a_{t}\}
are collinear sets.
P(0)
is the statement that
T
exists.
P(1)
is the statement that
b_{2}
is the harmonic conjugate of
b_{0}
with respect to
d
and
b_{1}
, obtained by the configuration
T
, which follows from the assumption that
d,b_{0},b_{1},\dots
is a conjugate sequence.

We now suppose that
t\geq 2
and that
P(t-2)
and
P(t-1)
hold, and we deduce
P(t)
.
From the modular sequence
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d,b_{0},b_{1})
, the configuration
\mathrm{HP}(d,b_{0},b_{1};c_{1},c_{0},a_{1},a_{0})
,
P(t-2)
, and
P(t-1)

we deduce that

\mathrm{HP}(d,b_{t-2},b_{t-1};c_{1},c_{0},a_{t-1},a_{t-2}).

From
P(t-1)
we deduce that
\{c_{1},a_{t-1},b_{t}\}
is a collinear set. Thus,
b_{t}
is the intersection of the line
\overline{c_{1}a_{t-1}b_{t}}
with the line
\overline{\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d,b_{0},b_{1})}
, which implies
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{t-1};b_{t-2},b_{t})
.
Therefore, by
Lemma
4.1
Part (
iii
) there is a point
a_{t}
such that
\{a_{t},b_{t},c_{0}\}
and

\{d,a_{t-2},a_{t-1},a_{t}\}
are collinear sets. Thus, this configuration holds:

\mathrm{HP}(d,b_{t-1},b_{t};c_{1},c_{0},a_{t},a_{t-1}).

It follows that there is a point
b_{t+1}
such that
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{t};b_{t-1},b_{t+1})
and
\{c_{1},a_{t},b_{t+1}\}
is a
collinear set. These prove
P(t)
.

Most of Part (
iv
) follows because we have the configuration
\mathrm{HP}(d,a_{t-2},a_{t-1};c_{1},c_{0},b_{t-1},b_{t})
and collinearity of
\{c_{1},b_{t-1},a_{t}\}
and of
\{d,a_{t-2},a_{t-1},a_{t}\}
, which imply the harmonic conjugacy
\mathrm{H}(d,a_{t-1};a_{t-2},a_{t})
. We note that
b_{t}=b_{i}
if and only if
a_{t}=a_{i}
because of the collinear sets
\{c_{0},a_{t},b_{t}\}
and
\{c_{0},a_{i},b_{i}\}
(from
P(t)
and
P(i)
, respectively) and the fact that the lines
\overline{da_{0}a_{1}\dots}
and
\overline{db_{0}b_{1}\dots}
are distinct.

We now prove Parts (
i
) and (
ii
) and that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(\mathrm{HP}(d,b_{0},b_{1};c_{1},c_{0},a_{1},a_{0}))
is a projective plane of prime order.

By the definition of harmonic conjugation,
b_{m}\neq d
. Suppose that
b_{m}=b_{t}
for some

t\in\{1,\dots,m-1\}
. Since
\{c_{1},a_{t-1},b_{t}\}
and
\{c_{1},a_{m-1},b_{m}\}=\{c_{1},a_{m-1},b_{t}\}
are collinear (by
P(t-1)
and
P(m-1)
, respectively), the set
\{c_{1},a_{t-1},a_{m-1}\}
is collinear.
Since
a_{t-1}\neq a_{m-1}
(because
b_{t-1}\neq b_{m-1}
, because
t-1<m-1<m
), we conclude that
c_{1}
is in the line
\overline{a_{t-1}a_{m-1}}=\overline{a_{0}a_{1}}
.
That is a contradiction because
\{c_{1},a_{0},a_{1}\}
is not collinear in
\mathrm{HP}(d,b_{0},b_{1};c_{1},c_{0},a_{1},a_{0})
. It follows that
b_{m}\neq b_{t}
for
0<t<m
, so
b_{m}=b_{0}
. We conclude that
a_{m}=a_{0}\neq a_{t}
for
0<t<m
; thus,
d,a_{0},a_{1},\dots
has order
m
.

That proves the first step of Part (
ii
). For the rest, for
t\geq 0
let
Q(t)
be the statement that
b_{t+m}=b_{t+j}
where
0\leq j<m
if and only if
j=0
. We apply induction on
t\geq 0
.
Q(0)
was just proved. Let
t>0
and consider the harmonic relations
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{t-1};b_{t-2},b_{t})
and
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{m+t-1};b_{m+t-2},b_{m+t})
, which is the same as
\mathrm{H}(d,b_{t-1};b_{t-2},b_{m+t})
by the induction assumption. Thus,
b_{t}=b_{m+t}
are equal by uniqueness of the harmonic conjugate. Now, compare
b_{t+m}=b_{t}
to
b_{t+j}=b_{t-m+j}
; they are unequal by induction. That completes the proof of Part (
ii
). Since
d,b_{0},b_{1},\dots
is any modular sequence of order
m
, the same conclusion applies to the sequence
d,a_{0},a_{1},\dots
.

Note that
M|E
where
E=\{d,a_{0},\dots,a_{m-1},b_{0},\dots,b_{m-1},c_{0},c_{1}\}
is the matroid
R_{\text{cycle}}[m]
. Thus

R_{\text{cycle}}[m]
is embedded in a harmonic matroid. By

[
1
, Theorem 10]
,
m
must be a prime number (this proves Part (
i
)).
By the definition of
E
we deduce that

\pi:=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(\{d,b_{0},b_{1};c_{1},c_{0},a_{1},a_{0}\})=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(M|E)=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R_{\text{cycle}}[m]).

Therefore, by

[
2
, Proposition 16]
,
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R_{\text{cycle}}[m])
is a projective plane of order
m
.
The plane is a little Desargues plane because it is contained in the harmonic matroid
M
, and since it is finite, it is a Desarguesian plane coordinatized by a finite field
[
5
, Theorem 6.20]
, specifically the prime field
\mathbb{Z}_{m}
.

Part (
v
) follows from the fact that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d,b_{0},b_{1})
is a collinear set in
\pi
of
m+1
points, the size of a line in
\pi
by Part (
iii
).

We prove Part (
vi
).
From Parts (
i
) and (
iii
) we know that
l
is a set of collinear points in the Desarguesian plane
\pi
of prime order
m
. Let
\bar{(}l)
denote the line of
\pi
that contains
l
. If
l
does not have three points, add one point of
\bar{(}l)
so we have three collinear points. Let those points be called
d^{\prime},b_{0}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime}
. Extend
d^{\prime},b_{0}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime}
to a configuration
T^{\prime}
isomorphic (as a matroid) to
T
with the correspondence
d^{\prime},b_{0}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime}\leftrightarrow d,b_{0},b_{1}
. Extend these three points to a conjugate sequence in
\pi
, which is necessarily finite, hence modular of order
m^{\prime}
. By Part (
iii
),
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(T^{\prime})
is a projective plane
\pi^{\prime}
of order
m^{\prime}
and is contained in
\pi
. By Part (
v
),
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d^{\prime},b_{0}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime})
is a line of
\pi^{\prime}
. By projective geometry,
\pi^{\prime}=\pi
; thus,
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(d^{\prime},b_{0}^{\prime},b_{1}^{\prime})
is a line of
\pi
.
∎

The proof of Part (
vi
) establishes a stronger statement, which may be a known theorem, but we have not seen it.

Corollary 4.4
.

Any conjugate sequence in a Desarguesian plane of prime order
p
is modular of order
p
.

5.
A projective rectangle in a harmonic matroid

In this section we show that if
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
(where
p
is prime) is embedded in a harmonic matroid, its harmonic closure is a projective rectangle. Thus, if a harmonic matroid
M
contains
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
, then it contains a projective rectangle.
(From
[
12
, Section 4]
it is known that
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
does embed in some harmonic matroids, if
\mathbf{V}
is the additive group of a field of characteristic
p
; namely, the projective plane over that field.)
In particular, if
L_{p}
embeds in
M
, then
M
contains the Desarguesian projective plane of order
p
.
We state the main theorem, whose proof appears at the conclusion of the section.

Theorem 5.1
.

For each embedding of
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
in a harmonic matroid,
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}})
is a projective rectangle of order
(p,|\mathbf{V}|)
in which every plane is Desarguesian of order
p
.

We assume throughout this section that
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
is embedded in a harmonic matroid
M
.

We often write
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(a_{g},c_{h})
with only two points instead of
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(a_{g},b_{g+h},c_{h})
, because the point
b_{g+h}
is predictable.
For convenience in formulas we employ the function
\phi_{\epsilon}:\mathbb{Z}_{p}\to\mathbf{V}
, which is an injection defined by
\phi_{\epsilon}(t)=t\epsilon-tg+h
where
g,h,
and
\epsilon
are fixed elements of

\mathbf{V}
such that
\epsilon-g\neq 0
. For the sake of simplicity, and if there is not ambiguity, we write
\phi(t)
instead of
\phi_{\epsilon}(t)
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
g
and
h
be elements of
\mathbf{V}
. For every
t\in\{1,\dots,p-1\}
there is a point
x_{t+1}
in
M
such that

(i)

for every
\epsilon\in\mathbf{V}
,

\{a_{\epsilon},b_{\epsilon+\phi(t)},c_{\phi(t)},x_{t+1}\}
is collinear;

(ii)

\mathbf{h}^{\infty}\{a_{g},b_{g+h},c_{h}\}=\{a_{g},b_{g+h},c_{h},x_{2},\dots,x_{p-1}\}
is a modular sequence of order
p
.

Recall that
x_{p}=b_{g+h}
by the definition of a modular sequence.

Figure 5.1.
The construction of
x_{t}
’s in Lemma
5.2
. In this example
k=1
,
p=5
,
g=h=0
, and the configuration of the points
D,a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
is the matroid
L_{5}
. The
a_{i\epsilon},b_{i\epsilon},c_{i\epsilon}
in the lemma’s proof are simplified to
a_{i},b_{i},c_{i}
.

We simplify the proof by applying an automorphism.

Lemma 5.3
.

For any
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbf{V}
, the mapping
a_{g},b_{g+h},c_{h}\mapsto a_{g+\alpha},b_{g+h+\alpha+\beta},c_{h+\beta}
is an automorphism of
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
.

Proof.

A line
a_{g}b_{g+h}c_{h}
becomes a line
a_{g+\alpha}b_{(g+\alpha)+(h+\beta)}c_{h+\beta}
. ∎

Take
\alpha=-g
and
\beta=-h
; this converts the general case of Lemma
5.2
to the case
g=h=0
and gives
\phi(t)=t+\epsilon
. (We think of the automorphism as relabelling
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
, not moving points, so there is no need for it to extend to an automorphism of
M
.) We restate Lemma
5.2
:

(i)

For every
\epsilon\in\mathbf{V}
,
\{a_{\epsilon},b_{(t+1)\epsilon},c_{t\epsilon},x_{t+1}\}
is collinear;

(ii)

\mathbf{h}^{\infty}\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\}=\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{p-1}\}
is a modular sequence of order
p
.

Now we are in a copy of
L_{p}
with
A_{\epsilon}=\{a_{i\epsilon}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}
,
B_{\epsilon}=\{b_{i\epsilon}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}
,
C_{\epsilon}=\{c_{i\epsilon}:i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}
. That reduces the proof to
L_{p}
.

Proof of Lemma
5.2
.

It is convenient to define
x_{0}=b_{0}
and
x_{1}=c_{0}
.

First of all, we prove both parts of the lemma for
p=2
. By the definition of
L_{2}^{k}
,
\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\}
and
\{a_{\epsilon},b_{2\epsilon},c_{\epsilon}\}

are collinear sets. Since
2\epsilon=0
in
\mathbf{V}
over
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, taking
x_{2}=b_{0}=x_{0}
and
x_{3}=c_{0}=x_{1}
completes the proof for
p=2
.

We now prove the lemma for
p>2
. We use mathematical induction on
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, where here we treat
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
as a field and let
t=1,2,\dots,p
so that
(t-1)^{-1}
is meaningful for
t>1
. To simplify some formulas we define
x_{0}=b_{0}
and
x_{1}=c_{0}
.

Suppose that
t=1
. From the definition of
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
we deduce that the configuration

\mathrm{HP}(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0};a_{\epsilon},D,c_{\epsilon},b_{\epsilon})

(5.1)

holds in
M
. This implies that there is a point
x_{2}\in M
with

\mathrm{H}(a_{0},c_{0};b_{0},x_{2})
such that
\{a_{\epsilon},b_{2\epsilon},c_{\epsilon},x_{2}\}

and
\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2}\}
are collinear sets. We observe that
x_{2}
depends only on
a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}
and is independent of
\epsilon
, because
M
is a harmonic matroid.
This proves the proposition for
t=1
.

We now fix
t\in\{2,\dots,p\}
and suppose that there are points
x_{t-1}
and
x_{t}
in
M
such that

\{a_{\epsilon},b_{(t-1)\epsilon},c_{(t-2)\epsilon},x_{{t-1}}\},

(5.2)

\{a_{\epsilon},b_{t\epsilon},c_{(t-1)\epsilon},x_{t}\},\text{ and }

(5.3)

\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{t}\}

(5.4)

are collinear sets.
From the definition of the matroid
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
we know that the sets

\{a_{0},c_{t\epsilon},b_{t\epsilon}\}
and
\{a_{0},a_{\epsilon\cdot((t-1)^{-1}+1)},a_{\epsilon}\}
are collinear.

Taking
\epsilon\cdot{(t-1)^{-1}(t)}
instead of
\epsilon
in (
5.2
) and (
5.3
), we deduce that

\{a_{\epsilon\cdot{(t-1)^{-1}(t)}},b_{t\epsilon},x_{t-1}\}
and

\{a_{\epsilon\cdot{(t-1)^{-1}(t)}},c_{t\epsilon},x_{t}\}
are collinear sets. These two sets are respectively equal to

\{a_{\epsilon\cdot((t-1)^{-1}+1)},b_{t\epsilon},x_{t-1}\}
and
\{a_{\epsilon\cdot((t-1)^{-1}+1)},c_{t\epsilon},x_{t}\}
.
These, (
5.3
), and (
5.4
) imply that

\mathrm{HP}(a_{0},x_{t-1},x_{t};c_{t\epsilon},b_{t\epsilon},a_{\epsilon},a_{\epsilon\cdot((t-1)^{-1}+1)})

holds in
M
. Therefore, there is a point
x_{t+1}\in M
with

\mathrm{H}(a_{0},x_{t};x_{t-1},x_{t+1})
such that
\{a_{\epsilon},c_{t\epsilon},x_{t+1}\}
, hence
\{a_{\epsilon},b_{(t+1)\epsilon},c_{t\epsilon},x_{t+1}\}
, and
\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{t+1}\}

are collinear sets.

We have proved
\mathrm{H}(a_{0},x_{t};x_{t-1},x_{t+1})
for
t=1,\dots,p
and that

\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{p}\}\subseteq\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\}),

hence
\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{p},x_{p+1}\}
is a collinear set and
a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{p+1}
is a conjugate sequence.

Since
p\epsilon=0
, we have
b_{p\epsilon}=b_{0}
.
We deduced at
t=p-1
that the set
\{a_{\epsilon},b_{p\epsilon},c_{(p-1)\epsilon},x_{p}\}

is collinear and lies in a distinct line from
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\})
.
Therefore,

b_{0},x_{p}\in\{a_{\epsilon},b_{p\epsilon},c_{(p-1)\epsilon},x_{p}\}\cap\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\}).

Since both
b_{0}
and
x_{p}
are in the intersection of distinct lines,
x_{p}=b_{0}
.
This proves that
a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{p-1}
is a modular sequence of order at most
p
. By Proposition
4.3
Part (
ii
), the order divides
p
, which is prime; thus the sequence has order
p
.
Since by Lemma
4.2
a modular sequence is harmonically closed,

\mathbf{h}^{\infty}\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0}\}\subseteq\{a_{0},b_{0},c_{0},x_{2},\dots,x_{p-1}\}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

The following construction of a projective rectangle in
M
is similar to that used by Flórez in
[
2
]
, where from an embedding of
L_{p}
into a harmonic matroid he produced an embedding of the Desarguesian plane of order
p
. (His analog of the vector space
\mathbf{V}
was simply
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.)

Construction 5.4

(Construction of the Harmonic Closure)
.

We construct the harmonic closure
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}})
, step by step, by applications of Lemma
4.1
. We begin with
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}=A\cup B\cup C
and we define
R_{\infty}=A
,
R_{0}=B
, and
R_{1}=C
. Given
R_{0},\ldots,R_{t}
for a particular
t=1,2,\dots,p-2
, we define
R_{t+1}
by harmonic conjugation from
R_{\infty},R_{t-1},R_{t}
. The conjugate sequence
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})=(a_{0},x_{0}=b_{0},x_{1}=c_{0},\ldots,x_{t-1},x_{t},x_{t+1},\dots)
gives us
x_{t+1}
as the first element of
R_{t+1}
.

When
t=1
,
R_{\infty}\cup R_{t-1}\cup R_{t}
is
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
. We assume in the induction that each
R_{i}\smallsetminus D
for
i\leq t
is labeled by
\mathbf{V}
, the labels being
x_{t,h}
, in such a way that
R_{\infty}\cup R_{t-1}\cup R_{t}
is isomorphic to
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
by a mapping that is the identity on
R_{\infty}
and maps
b_{g},c_{h}
respectively to
x_{t-1,g},x_{t,h}
. This isomorphism means that the construction of
R_{t}
is identical to that of
R_{2}
, only beginning with a different isomorph of
L_{p}^{\mathbf{V}}
. Thus, we only need to construct
R_{2}
.

There were several ways to construct
x_{2}
, the harmonic conjugate of
b_{0}
. We formed a harmonic configuration
\mathrm{HP}(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0};a_{g},D,c_{h},b_{k})
for some
g,h,k\neq 0
and observed that the line
\overline{a_{g}c_{h}}=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(a_{g},b_{g+h},c_{h})
intersects
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(a_{0},b_{0},c_{0})
at the harmonic conjugate of
b_{0}
with respect to
a_{0}
and
c_{0}
, because we are in the harmonic matroid
M

Lemma 5.5

7.
A submatroid that generates a projective rectangle

In this section we prove that a generalization of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]
is
a matroid that extends by harmonic conjugation to the projective rectangle

\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
, where
p
is a prime number and
k
is a positive integer.
We define the generalization in terms of any positive integer
m
.
The matroid
L_{m}^{k}
, where
k
and
m
are positive integers, is defined exactly like
L_{p}^{k}
in Section
4
.
Let
\{g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
be a minimal set of
generators of
\mathfrak{G}:=(\mathbb{Z}_{m})^{k}
.
We set
G=\{0,g_{1},\dots,g_{k}\}
.
We write
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[m]
to mean any matroid isomorphic to the restriction

L_{m}^{k}|(A\cup B\cup\{c_{f}\mid f\in G\}).

We call this the
higher Reid cycle matroid
.

Figure 7.1.
The higher Reid cycle matroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{2}[3]
with group
\mathbb{Z}_{3}\times\mathbb{Z}_{3}
; the identity element is
e=(0,0)
.

Gordon
[
4
]
, for
m=p
(a prime) and Flórez
[
1
]
(for all
m
) found the algebraic representability of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[m]
.

Lemma 7.1

(
[
4
, Theorem 2]
and
[
1
, Theorem 2]
)
.

The Reid cycle matroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[m]
is algebraically representable, and is linearly representable, if and only if
m
is a prime number.

Flórez
[
1
]
also found that
L_{p}^{k}
(recall that
p
is prime) embeds in some harmonic matroid for every
k>0
. More precisely, harmonic conjugation is involved when
k=1
:

Lemma 7.2

(
[
1
, Lemma 9]
)
.

Any embedding of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]

in a harmonic matroid
M
extends uniquely by harmonic conjugation to an embedding of
L_{p}
in
M
.

The proof of Lemma
7.2
shows that there are points

c_{2},c_{3},\dots,c_{p-1}
that extend
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]
to
L_{p}
.
Those points satisfy harmonicity,
H(c_{j},D;c_{j-1},c_{j+1})
, and that

\{a_{i},b_{i+j},c_{j}\}
is a collinear triple for
i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
.

We wish to extend these results to
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
. Our objectives are the following two theorems as well as Proposition
7.8
.

Theorem 7.3
.

For every
k>0
, the higher Reid cycle matroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[m]
is linearly representable, and is algebraically representable, if and only if
m
is a prime number. The same holds true for
L_{m}^{k}
.

Theorem 7.4
.

Suppose that
L_{p}^{k}
is embedded in a harmonic matroid
M
. Then each
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]\subseteq L_{p}^{k}
is a submatroid of
L_{p}^{k}
for which
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p])=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
. When
k=1
,
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]
is a minimal such matroid.

Question 7.5
.

What are the minimal submatroids
N
of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
such that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(N)=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
? It seems improbable that
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
is minimal for
k>1
.

We begin the proofs with the following lemma.

Lemma 7.6
.

The higher Reid cycle matroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
is linearly and algebraically representable in characteristic
p
, and therefore embeds in some harmonic matroid, for every
k>0
.

Proof.

The matroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
is linearly representable in the vector space
\mathbf{F}^{3}
over any field
\mathbf{F}
that contains
\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}
, because
\mathfrak{G}\cong\mathbb{F}_{p^{k}}^{+}\subseteq\mathbf{F}^{+}
, the additive group of the field
[
12
, Theorem 4.1(a)]
.
Therefore, it embeds in two kinds of harmonic matroid: every projective space of dimension
2
or greater over such a field, and consequently also in every full algebraic matroid of characteristic
p
and transcendence degree at least equal to
3=\text{rank}(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p])

[
9
, Proposition 6.7.10]
.
∎

For use in the next proposition we identify the Reid cycle matroids contained in
L_{p}^{k}
.

Construction 7.7

(Reid Cycle Submatroids)
.

A Reid cycle submatroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]\subseteq L_{p}^{k}
must have the following form: a point set
A_{1}\cup B_{1}\cup C_{1}
where
A_{1}=\{a_{\alpha_{i}}\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cup\{D\}
,
B_{1}=\{b_{\beta_{j}}\mid j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cup\{D\}
, and
C_{1}=\{c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma^{\prime}},D\}
, where
\alpha_{i},\beta_{j},\gamma,\gamma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{G}
; and lines
\{a_{\alpha_{i}},b_{\beta_{i}},c_{\gamma}\}
and
\{a_{\alpha_{i}},b_{\beta_{i+1}},c_{\gamma^{\prime}}\}
as well as
A_{1}
,
B_{1}
, and
C_{1}
. (The point indexing can always be chosen this way because the index set
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is prime cyclic; we omit the details.) The 3-point lines mean that
\beta_{i}=\alpha_{i}+\gamma
and
\beta_{i+1}=\alpha_{i}+\gamma^{\prime}
. It follows that
\alpha_{i}+\gamma=\alpha_{i-1}+\gamma^{\prime}
, so
\alpha_{i}=\alpha_{i-1}+\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma=\alpha_{0}+i(\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma)
. Let
\delta=\gamma^{\prime}-\gamma
, so that
\gamma^{\prime}=\delta+\gamma
. Then
\alpha_{i}=\alpha+i\delta
(where
\alpha
is short for
\alpha_{0}
) and
\beta_{i}=\alpha+i\delta+\gamma
.
We denote this submatroid by
R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
; see Figure
7.2
.

Figure 7.2.
The submatroid
R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
(solid dots) and its harmonic extension
\bar{R}_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
(with the hollow dots).

Our description means there is a cyclic subgroup
\langle\delta\rangle=\{i\delta\mid i\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, such that
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i}=\alpha+\langle\delta\rangle
and
\{\beta_{i}\}_{i}=\gamma+\alpha+\langle\delta\rangle
; that is, the group elements that label
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
are cosets of
\langle\delta\rangle
. The choice of the parameters
\alpha,\gamma,\delta\in\mathfrak{G}
is arbitrary as long as
\delta\neq 0
. Every Reid cycle submatroid of
L_{p}^{k}
has this form (up to changes of notation) if
p^{k}>2
because the lines
A_{1}
and
B_{1}
, having more than 3 points, must be contained in long lines of
L_{p}^{k}
. (We note that the same Reid cycle submatroid may be constructed from different sets of parameters.)

Proposition 7.8
.

Any embedding of

R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
in a harmonic matroid
M
extends uniquely by
harmonic conjugation to an embedding of
L_{p}^{k}
in
M
.

Proof.

By Lemma
7.2
we may assume
k>1
. Let
\alpha,\gamma,\delta
be three fixed elements of
\mathfrak{G}
with
\delta\neq 0
.

We consider the simple matroid
R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)\cong R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]
of rank 3 on the point set

E(R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma))=\{a_{\alpha+t\delta},b_{\gamma+\alpha+t\delta}\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cup\{c_{\gamma},c_{\gamma+\delta},D\}.

Because
R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
is a submatroid of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
, it embeds in
M
.
By Lemma
7.2
,
R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
extends by harmonic conjugation to an
embedding
\bar{R}_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
of
L_{p}
in
M
, where the new points are denoted by

c_{\gamma+t\delta}
for
t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\smallsetminus\{0,1\}
.
The existence of these new points does not depend on the choice of
\alpha
, but only on
\gamma
and
\delta
, as we can see from their subscripts.
Moreover, the points satisfy
\mathrm{HP}(c_{\gamma\delta^{j}},D;\ c_{\gamma+(j-1)\delta},c_{\gamma+(j+1)\delta})

for every
j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
(as one can see in Figure
7.2
), which shows directly that
\bar{R}_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
extends
R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
by harmonic conjugation.

Identifying each point
a_{i}
,
b_{i}
and
c_{i}
in
L_{p}
with

a_{\alpha+i\delta}
,
b_{\gamma+\alpha+i\delta}
,
c_{\gamma+i\delta}
,
respectively, we can verify that the set of nontrivial flats of this
extension
\bar{R}_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)
is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)=\big\{

\displaystyle\{a_{\alpha+t\delta}\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cup\{D\},\{c_{\gamma+t\delta}\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cup\{D\},

\displaystyle\quad\{b_{\gamma+\alpha+t\delta}\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}\cup\{D\},\{a_{\alpha+i\delta},b_{\gamma+\alpha+(i+j)\delta},c_{\gamma+j\delta}\}\text{ for }i,j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\big\}.

Varying
\alpha
in
\mathfrak{G}
we obtain a family of matroids,
\{R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)\}_{\alpha}
.
The matroid
M_{p}^{k}(\gamma,\delta)
defined on ground set

\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}}E(\bar{R}_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma))=E(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p])\cup\{c_{\gamma+t\delta}\mid t\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\}

with the set of nontrivial flats

\displaystyle{\mathcal{F}}^{k}(\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=\bigcup_{\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}}{\mathcal{F}}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)=\{F\mid F\text{ is a flat of }R_{p}(\alpha,\delta,\gamma)\text{ for some }\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}\}

is a (harmonic) extension of the matroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
and a submatroid of
L_{p}^{k}
.

The matroid
M_{p}^{k}
with the ground set

\bigcup_{\gamma,\delta\in G}E\big(M_{p}^{k}(\gamma,\delta)\big)=E\big(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]\big)\cup\{c_{\omega}\mid\omega\in\mathfrak{G}\smallsetminus G\}=A\cup B\cup C

in the harmonic matroid
M
, defined by the set of nontrivial flats

\displaystyle\bigcup_{\gamma,\delta\in G}{\mathcal{F}}^{k}(\gamma,\delta)

\displaystyle=\{F\mid F\text{ is
a flat of }M_{p}^{k}(\gamma,\delta)\text{ for some }\gamma,\delta\in G\}

\displaystyle=\big\{\{a_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}\}\cup\{D\},\{c_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}\}\cup\{D\},\{b_{\alpha}\mid\alpha\in\mathfrak{G}\}\cup\{D\},

is a (harmonic) extension of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]
in

. Its points and flats are the points and flats of

. Thus,

, which proves the proposition.
∎

If
L_{p}^{k}
is embedded in a harmonic matroid, then any submatroid
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
naturally is also embedded. The following corollary follows from Proposition
7.8
and the fact that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}
is a closure operator.

Corollary 7.9
.

Suppose that
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]
is embedded in a harmonic matroid

M
. Then
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p])=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
.

Proof of Theorem
7.3
.

Because
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[m]
contains a copy of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[m]
, it can only be linearly or algebraically representable when
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[m]
is. By Lemma
7.1
, the latter is not embeddable when
m
is a composite number. That rules out embedding of
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[m]
unless
m
is prime. When
m
is prime we call upon Lemma
7.6
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
7.4
.

From Proposition
7.8
, Corollary
7.9
, and Theorem
5.1
we conclude that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p])=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
.

Regarding
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]
, Gordon
[
4
, Proof of Proposition 12]
proved that any proper minor embeds in a full algebraic matroid over the rational numbers
\mathbb{Q}
. (He derived this from the similar result of Lindström for linear representability.) Since a full algebraic matroid is harmonic,
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R)
is algebraically representable over
\mathbb{Q}
. If
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R)=\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p])
, then it equals
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p})
, which contains
L_{p}
, so
L_{p}
would be algebraically representable over
\mathbb{Q}
. However,
L_{p}
is algebraically representable only over fields of characteristic
p

[
1
, Theorem 7]
. This contradiction proves that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(R)
is smaller than
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p})
.
∎

Define the
algebraic characteristic set

\chi_{A}(M)
to be the set of field characteristics over which the matroid
M
is algebraic.

Proposition 7.10
.

The algebraic characteristic sets
\chi_{A}(\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k}))=R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]=\{p\}
.

Proof.

From
[
12
]
we know that
L_{p}^{k}
is linearly representable over a field of characteristic
p
. Therefore, by
[
9
, Proposition 6.7.10]
,
L_{p}^{k}
is algebraically representable over a field of characteristic
p
. Consequently, it embeds in a full algebraic matroid
M
over a field of characteristic
p
, which is a harmonic matroid by Lindström
[
8
]
. We deduce that
\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
is algebraically representable over a field of characteristic
p
. This proves that
p\in\chi_{A}(\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k}))
.

On the other hand, from
[
4
, Theorem 2]
we know that
\chi_{A}(R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p])=\{p\}
. Since
R_{\mathrm{cycle}}[p]\subseteq R_{\mathrm{cycle}}^{k}[p]\subseteq\mathbf{h}^{\infty}(L_{p}^{k})
, we obtain the desired conclusion.
∎
