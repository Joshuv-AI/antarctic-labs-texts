Cohen-Macaulay test ideals over rings of finite and countable Cohen-Macaulay type
Julian Benali, Shrunal Pothagoni, and Rebecca R.G

Abstract
. The third named author and Pérez proved that under certain conditions the test ideal of a module closure agrees with the trace ideal of the module closure. We use this fact to compute the test ideals of various rings with respect to the closures coming from their indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules. We also give an easier way to compute the test ideal of a hypersurface ring in 3 variables coming from a module with a particular type of matrix factorization.



1.
Introduction

The test ideal of a noetherian local ring is an invariant that helps us determine how singular the ring is. It was originally defined in terms of tight closure for rings of characteristic
p>0

[
HH90
,
HH94
,
ST12
]
. Later it was proved that the test ideal agrees with a characteristic 0 invariant from algebraic geometry, the multiplier ideal, via reduction to characteristic
p>0

[
Smi00
,
Har01
]
. The third named author studied test ideals coming from arbitrary closure operations in
[
PR21
]
, and proved that the test ideals coming from big Cohen-Macaulay module closures give information on the singularities of the ring.

One of the main results of
[
PR21
]
is that the test ideal of a module closure agrees with the trace ideal of the module when the ring is local or the module is finitely-presented. This enables us to study the singularities of the ring by computing trace ideals. In this paper, we compute trace ideals of finitely generated indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules over rings of finite or countable Cohen-Macaulay type, providing examples for some of the results of
[
PR21
]
.

The following result inspired the computations in this paper:

Proposition 1.1

(
[
PR21
, Proposition 4.13]
)
.

Let
(R,m)
be a Cohen-Macaulay ring with finite Cohen-Macaulay type. If
R
is not regular, then

\sqrt{\tau_{MCM}(R)}=m.

This leads to two questions:

(1)

Which examples give
\tau_{MCM}(R)=m
?

(2)

When
R
does not have finite Cohen-Macaulay type, does this still hold?

The second question was answered negatively in
[
PR21
]
, in the case of the Whitney Umbrella. In this paper, we give many more examples, fleshing out the details of this result.

Another question in
[
PR21
]
is whether
\tau_{MCM}(R)
is always nonzero. Proposition 4.18 of that paper indicates that
\tau_{MCM}(R)
is nonzero when
R
is a local domain of dimension 1. In this paper, we give examples where
R
is not a domain and
\tau_{MCM}(R)=0
(see Proposition
5.2
).

In Section
3
, we compute trace ideals of finitely generated Cohen-Macaulay modules over Veronese subrings via direct computation. In Section
4
, we prove a result on trace ideals of modules whose resolutions take the form of matrix factorizations. This is a special case of the result stated by Faber in
[
Fab20
, Section 2.3]
, based on
previous work of Vasconcelos
[
Vas98
, Proposition 6.2.3]
.
In Sections
5
and
6
, we apply this result to compute trace ideals of finitely generated Cohen-Macaulay modules over dimension 1 and 2 ADE singularities, including some that are not domains or have countable Cohen-Macaulay type.

2.
Background/Previous Results

Remark 2.1
.

All rings are assumed to be commutative, unital, Noetherian, and local. We will use
k
to denote a field,
R
to denote a ring, and
M
to denote an
R
-module. The notation
(R,m,k)
indicates that
R
is a local ring with maximal ideal
m
and residual field
k=R/m
.

Definition 2.2
.

The
Krull dimension
of
R
is the length of the longest proper chain of prime ideals
p_{0}\subsetneq p_{1}\subsetneq\cdots\subsetneq p_{n}
of
R
.

Definition 2.3
.

A sequence
x_{1},...,x_{n}
of elements in
R
is said to be a
regular sequence
on
M
if
(x_{1},...,x_{n})M\neq M
,
x_{1}
is a non-zerodivisor on
M
, and
x_{i}
is a non-zerodivisor on
M/(x_{1},...,x_{i-1})M
for all
i\in\{2,...,n\}
.

Definition 2.4
.

We say
M
is a
Cohen-Macaulay module
over
R
if the length of the longest regular sequence on
M
is the same as the Krull dimension of
R
. When
M
is finitely generated, it is often called a
maximal Cohen-Macaulay module
, and we will use this term throughout the paper.

Remark 2.5
.

The reason for the name maximal Cohen-Macaulay module is that sometimes Cohen-Macaulay modules are defined to be modules
M
for which the length of the longest regular sequence on
M
is the same as the Krull dimenson of
M
. In this case the Cohen-Macaulay modules whose Krull dimension is maximal (i.e. equal to
\dim R
) are called maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules.

Next we give the definition of the test ideal, and describe its relationship to the trace ideal.

Definition 2.6

(
[
R.G16
, Definition 2.3]
)
.

Let
M
be an
R
-module. We define the
module closure
{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}
to be the operation that takes an
R
-module
A
contained in another
R
-module
B
to the
R
-module

A_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}}:=\{b\in B:x\otimes b\in\mathrm{im}\,(M\otimes_{R}A\to M\otimes_{R}B)\text{ for all }x\in M\}.

By
[
R.G16
]
this is a closure operation, i.e.:

(1)

A\subseteq A_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}}\subseteq B
,

(2)

(A_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}})_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}}=A_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}},

(3)

and if
A\subseteq C\subseteq B
, then
A_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}}\subseteq C_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}}.

Definition 2.7

(
[
RG18
, Definition 4.7]
)
.

Let
M
be an
R
-module. The
test ideal of
R
associated to
M
is the ideal

\tau_{M}(R):=\bigcap_{\begin{subarray}{c}A\subseteq B\\
\textit{ $R$-modules}\end{subarray}}(A:_{R}A_{B}^{{\mathrm{cl}}_{M}}.)

Definition 2.8
.

If
R
is a complete local domain and
M
an
R
-module, the
trace ideal of
M
is

\tr_{M}(R)=\sum_{f\in\Hom(M,R)}f(M).

That is, we find the images of the
R
-module homomorphisms from
M
to
R
and take their sum.

Theorem 2.9

(
[
PR21
, Theorem 3.12]
)
.

Let
R
be local and
M
be an
R
-module. If
R
is complete or
M
is finitely generated  then
\tau_{M}(R)=\tr_{M}(R)
.

We will use
\tau_{M}(R)
to denote this ideal in the rest of the paper.

This implies that to compute test ideals of maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules, we can compute the trace ideals of these modules, which is a much more doable task. We will in fact go a step further and compute the maximal Cohen-Macaulay module test ideal of
R
, defined as follows:

Definition 2.10

(
[
PR21
, Section 4]
)
.

Let
MCM(R)
denote the set of all isomorphism classes of maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules, i.e. finitely generated Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules.
The
maximal Cohen-Macaulay module test ideal
of
R
is

\tau_{MCM}(R):=\bigcap_{M\in MCM(R)}\tau_{M}(R).

Remark 2.11
.

For further discussion of how to reduce classes of Cohen-Macaulay modules to sets, see
[
PR21
, Remark 4.6]
.

Remark 2.12
.

Our goal is to compute
\tau_{MCM}(R)
. In practice, we do not need to compute the test ideal of every maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-module in order to compute
\tau_{MCM}(R)
. The following are two shortcuts that make this task more doable:

(1)

Let
M
be a nonzero free
R
-module. Then any projection map from
M
to
R
is a surjective
R
-module homomorphism and thus,
\tau_{M}(R)=R
.

(2)

Every maximal Cohen-Macaulay module is a direct sum of indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules. Furthermore, it follows from the definition that for any Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules,
N
and
L
,
\tau_{N\oplus L}(R)=\tau_{N}(R)+\tau_{L}(R)
. Hence to compute
\tau_{MCM}(R)
, it suffices to compute the intersection over the indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules.

From these facts, we conclude that
\tau_{MCM}(R)
is the intersection of the test ideals of the non-free indecomposable MCM
R
-modules.

3.
First Example: Veronese subrings

In this section, we compute the test ideal of the (complete versions of the) Veronese subrings of the polynomial ring in two variables. This example is explained in detail to give a framework for how these computations are done.

Proposition 3.1
.

If
R=k\llbracket x^{d},x^{d-1}y,...,xy^{d-1},y^{d}\rrbracket
where
k
is a field, then
\tau_{MCM}(R)=m
. Furthermore,
\tau_{M}(R)=m
for all non-free indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules
M
.

Proof.

Up to isomorphism, the set of non-free indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules is

\mathcal{M}=\{R,xR+yR,x^{2}R+xyR+y^{2}R,\ldots,x^{d-1}R+x^{d-2}yR+\cdots+xy^{d-2}R+y^{d-1}R\}.

This follows from
[
LW12
, Corollary 6.4]
, which states that the indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules are isomorphic to the direct summands of
S=k\llbracket x,y\rrbracket
as an
R
-module.
We compute the trace ideals
\tau_{M}(R)
for
M\in\mathcal{M}
and then compute their intersection
\tau_{MCM}(R).
We claim that
\tau_{R}(R)=R
and if
M\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{R\}
, then
\tau_{M}(R)=m
where
m
is the maximal ideal.

Let
S=k\llbracket x,y\rrbracket
. We will view
R
as a subring of
S
. Since
\Hom_{R}(R,R)
contains the identity map,
\tau_{R}(R)=R
. This will be true for any ring as previously stated in Remark
2.12
.

Now let

M_{n}=x^{n}R+x^{n-1}yR+\cdots+xy^{n-1}R+y^{n}R=\sum_{i=0}^{n}x^{n-i}y^{i}R\in\mathcal{M},

where
1\leq n\leq d-1
.

Claim:

\Hom(M_{n},R)
is generated by the maps
f_{i}
for
i\in\{0,...,d-n\}
defined by
f_{i}(p)=x^{d-n-i}y^{i}p
for all
p\in M_{n}
.

Proof:
Let
f\in\Hom(M_{n},R)
. For each
j\in\{0,...,n\}
, we see that

\displaystyle x^{j}y^{d-j}f(x^{n-j}y^{j})

\displaystyle=f(x^{n}y^{d})=x^{n}y^{d-n}f(y^{n})

\displaystyle x^{d-n+j}y^{n-j}f(x^{n-j}y^{j})

\displaystyle=f(x^{d}y^{n})=x^{d-n}y^{n}f(x^{n}).

Since
x
and
y
are non-zerodivisors in
S
, this implies

\displaystyle y^{d-j}f(x^{n-j}y^{j})

\displaystyle=x^{n-j}y^{d-n}f(y^{n})\in(x^{n-j})S

\displaystyle x^{d-n+j}f(x^{n-j}y^{j})

\displaystyle=y^{j}f(x^{n})\in(y^{j})S.

As
x^{n-j},y^{d-j}
and
y^{j},x^{d-n+j}
are both regular sequences in
S
, we now have

f(x^{n-j}y^{j})\in(x^{n-j})S\cap(y^{j})S=(x^{n-j}y^{j})S.

Finally, we obtain

f(x^{n-j}y^{j})\in(x^{n-j}y^{j})S\cap R=(x^{d-j}y^{j},x^{d-j-1}y^{j+1},...,x^{n-j}y^{d-n+j})R.

Now, since
f(x^{n})\in(x^{d},x^{d-1}y,...,x^{n}y^{d-n})R
there exist
p_{0},...,p_{d-n}\in R
such that

\displaystyle f(x^{n})

\displaystyle=p_{0}x^{d}+p_{1}x^{d-1}y+\cdots+p_{d-n}x^{n}y^{d-n}

\displaystyle=p_{0}f_{0}(x^{n})+p_{1}f_{1}(x^{n})+\cdots+p_{d-n}f_{d-n}(x^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}p_{i}f_{i}(x^{n})

Then for each
j\in\{0,...,n\}
, we have

\displaystyle x^{j}y^{d-j}f(x^{n-j}y^{j})

\displaystyle=f(x^{n}y^{d})=y^{d}f(x^{n})

\displaystyle=y^{d}\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}p_{i}x^{d-i}y^{i}

\displaystyle=x^{j}y^{d-j}\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}p_{i}x^{d-i-j}y^{i+j}

\displaystyle=x^{j}y^{d-j}\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}p_{i}f_{i}(x^{n-j}y^{j}).

Since
x^{j}x^{d-j}
is a non-zerodivisor in
R
, this yields
f(x^{n-j}y^{j})=\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}p_{i}f_{i}(x^{n-j}y^{j})
. Thus
f=\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}p_{i}f_{i}
, so that
f_{0},...,f_{d-n}
generate
\Hom(M_{n},R)
, as claimed.

\blacksquare

We can see that
f_{i}(M_{n})=(x^{d-i}y^{i},x^{d-i-1}y^{i+1},...,x^{d-n-i}y^{n+i})
for each
i
so that

\displaystyle\tau_{M_{n}}(R)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}f_{i}(M_{n})=\sum_{i=0}^{d-n}(x^{d-i}y^{i},x^{d-i-1}y^{i+1},...,x^{d-n-i}y^{n+i})

\displaystyle=(x^{d},x^{d-1}y,...,y^{d})=m

as desired.

Therefore, we conclude that
\tau_{MCM}(R)=m
.
∎

4.
Test Ideals of Modules with Matrix Factorizations

Although it is possible to manually compute the test ideal in many more cases, it will become apparent in later sections that this approach is not always optimal. The results of this section allows us to easily compute the test ideals of any module of the form stated in Corollary
4.7
. This will reduce the length and complexity of the computations for examples like the Kleinian singularities.

Theorem
4.6
and Corollary
4.7
are a special case of the result stated by Faber in
[
Fab20
]
, based on Proposition 6.2.3 of
[
Vas98
]
. We give a detailed proof in the special case that the ring is of the form
R=S\llbracket z\rrbracket/(z^{2}+g)
and the module has a matrix factorization, relying as much as possible on linear algebra and properties of
\Hom
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
S
be a Noetherian ring and let
\varphi
be an
n\times n
matrix over
S\llbracket z\rrbracket
whose entries are all in
S
. Then
\ker(z\id_{n}-\varphi)
is trivial.

Proof.

Let
R=S[z]
and
\widehat{R}=S\llbracket z\rrbracket
, the
(z)
-adic completion of
R
. Let
\Phi=z\id_{n}-\varphi:(\widehat{R})^{n}\to(\widehat{R})^{n}
.
Then
\Phi
is the unique
\hat{R}
-linear extension of the
R
-linear map
\Psi:R^{n}\to R^{n}
which, as a matrix, has the same entries as
\Phi
. Let
v\in\ker(\Psi)
and assume
v\neq 0
. Let
v_{i}
be a nonzero component of
v
with maximal degree in
z
. We will use
\deg
to refer to degree in
z
. Then we have that

0=(\Psi v)_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\Psi_{ij}v_{j}=(z-\varphi_{ii})v_{i}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Psi_{ij}v_{j}.

We see that
\deg((z-\varphi_{ii})v_{i}))=\deg(v_{i})+1
, but because the off-diagonal entries of
\Psi
do not have any
z
’s in them,

\deg\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}\Psi_{ij}v_{j}\right)\leq\max_{j\neq i}(\deg(\Psi_{ij}v_{j}))=

\max_{j\neq i}(\deg(v_{j}))\leq\deg(v_{i}).

This implies
(\Psi v)_{i}
is a polynomial in
z
of degree
\deg(v_{i})+1
and is therefore nonzero, a contradiction. Hence
\ker(\Psi)
is trivial.

Now, because
S
, and therefore
R
, is Noetherian,
\widehat{R}
is a flat
R
-module. Thus, since
\Psi
is injective, the induced map

\Psi\otimes id_{\widehat{R}}:R^{n}\otimes_{R}\widehat{R}\to R^{n}\otimes_{R}\widehat{R}

is also injective. However,
R^{n}\otimes_{R}\widehat{R}\cong(\widehat{R})^{n}
, so
\Psi\otimes id_{\widehat{R}}
corresponds to the unique extension
\Phi
of
\Psi
to
(\widehat{R})^{n}
. Thus,
\Phi
is injective as well.
∎

Definition 4.2
.

Let
S
be a ring and
x\in S
. A
matrix factorization
for
x
is a pair of maps of free
S
-modules (
\varphi:F\to G
,
\psi:G\to F
) such that
\varphi\psi=x\cdot 1_{G}
and
\psi\varphi=x\cdot 1_{F}
.
[
Eis80
]

Notation 4.3
.

Let
S
be a ring and let
R
be a quotient ring of
S
. Then given a matrix
\Phi
over
S
we will use
\overline{\Phi}
to denote the same matrix over the quotient
R
.

Remark 4.4
.

All matrix factorizations that appear in this paper are square. That is to say, all matrix factorizations which appear will be maps between free modules of the same rank.

Lemma 4.5
.

Let
S
be a Noetherian ring and
g\in S
. If
\varphi
is an
n\times n
matrix over
S\llbracket z\rrbracket
whose entries lie in
S
such that
(z\id_{n}-\varphi,z\id_{n}+\varphi)
is a matrix factorization for
z^{2}+g
in
S\llbracket z\rrbracket

(
or equivalently,
\varphi^{2}=-g\id_{n})
, then
\ker(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})=\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi})
and
\ker(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi^{\intercal}})=\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}})
in
R=S\llbracket z\rrbracket/(z^{2}+g)
.

Proof.

Since
(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi})=0
over
R
,
\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi})\subset\ker(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})
. Let
\overline{v}\in\ker(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})
for some
v\in(S\llbracket x\rrbracket)^{n}
so that
(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})\overline{v}=0
. Then

(z\id_{n}-\varphi)v\in\textim((z^{2}+g)\id_{n}).

So, there exists
w\in(S\llbracket z\rrbracket)^{n}
such that

(z\id_{n}-\varphi)v=(z^{2}+g)w.

This can be rewritten as

(z\id_{n}-\varphi)v=(z\id_{n}-\varphi)(z\id_{n}+\varphi)w.

Since
\ker(z\id_{n}-\varphi)
is trivial in
S\llbracket z\rrbracket
by Lemma
4.1
, this implies

v=(z\id_{n}+\varphi)w\in\textim(z\id_{n}+\varphi)

and hence
\overline{v}\in\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi})
over
R
. Thus,
\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi})=\ker(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi}).

Now note that

\displaystyle(z\id_{n}-\varphi^{\intercal})(z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal})

\displaystyle=((z\id_{n}+\varphi)(z\id_{n}-\varphi))^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=((z^{2}+g)\id_{n})^{\intercal}

\displaystyle=(z^{2}+g)\id_{n}

so that
(z\id_{n}-\varphi^{\intercal},z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal})
is also a matrix factorization for
z^{2}+g
. So, by what was already shown,
\ker(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi^{\intercal}})=\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}})
. ∎

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
S
be a Noetherian ring and
R=S\llbracket z\rrbracket/(z^{2}+g)
. Let
\varphi
be an
n\times n
matrix over
S\llbracket z\rrbracket
whose entries lie in
S
such that
(z\id_{n}-\varphi,z\id_{n}+\varphi)
is a matrix factorization for
z^{2}+g
. If
M=\cok(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})
, then

\textim(\Hom_{R}(M,R)\hookrightarrow\Hom_{R}(R^{n},R)\xrightarrow{\cong}R^{n})=\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal})}.

Proof.

Let
\Phi=\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi}
. Consider the exact sequence

(1)

0\to\textim(\Phi)\xrightarrow{\iota}R^{n}\xrightarrow{p}M\to 0.

Since
\Hom_{R}(\cdot,R)
is left exact, this yields an exact sequence

(2)

0\to\Hom_{R}(M,R)\xrightarrow{p^{*}}\Hom_{R}(R^{n},R)\xrightarrow{\iota^{*}}\Hom_{R}(\textim(\Phi),R).

Note that there is a standard
R
-module isomorphism
\alpha:R^{n}\to\Hom_{R}(R^{n},R)
which sends a vector
v
to the dot product map
\langle v,\underline{\ \ }\rangle

\alpha^{-1}
sends a map to the vector whose entries are the evaluations of the map at the standard basis vectors. That is, for any
f:R^{n}\to R

\alpha^{-1}(f)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}f(e_{1})\\
f(e_{2})\\
\vdots\\
\\
f(e_{n})\\
\end{smallmatrix}\right)

where
e_{i}
is the
i
th standard basis vector. This isomorphism, combined with the exact sequence in (
2
), yields a new exact sequence

(3)

0\to\Hom_{R}(M,R)\xrightarrow{\alpha^{-1}\circ p^{*}}R^{n}\xrightarrow{\iota^{*}\circ\alpha}\Hom_{R}(\textim(\Phi),R).

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
S
be a Noetherian ring and
R=S\llbracket z\rrbracket/(z^{2}+g)
. Let
\varphi
be an
n\times n
matrix over
S\llbracket z\rrbracket
whose entries lie in
S
such that
(z\id_{n}-\varphi,z\id_{n}+\varphi)
is a matrix factorization for
z^{2}+g
. If
M=\cok(\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi})
, then the trace ideal of
M
is generated by the entries of
\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi}
.

Proof.

By the previous theorem, the image of the injective map

\Hom_{R}(M,R)\hookrightarrow\Hom_{R}(R^{n},R)

is isomorphic to
\mathrm{im}\,(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}})
and thus
\Hom_{R}(M,R)\cong\mathrm{im}\,(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}})
. The generators of
\textim(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}})
are the columns of
\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}}
and the corresponding generators of
\Hom_{R}(M,R)
under the isomorphism are the maps
f_{i}:M\to R^{n}
for
1\leq i\leq n
defined by
f_{i}(e_{j})=(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi^{\intercal}})_{ji}=(\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi})_{ij}
. Then,
f_{i}(M)
is generated by the entries of the
i
th row of
\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi}
. Since
\Hom_{R}(M,R)
is generated by the maps
f_{1},f_{2},\ldots,f_{n}
, the trace ideal is generated by the image of these maps, and hence, the trace ideal is generated by the entries of
\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi}
. ∎

Remark 4.8
.

In the case that
\varphi
has
0

\overline{z\id_{n}+\varphi}
is the same as the ideal generated by the entries of
\overline{z\id_{n}-\varphi}
.

5.
Dimension One ADE Singularities

Remark 5.1
.

The following two examples are not integral domains. For this reason, while we can compute
\tau_{MCM}(R)
, it may not give us helpful information about the singularities of the ring. This will be apparent for Proposition
5.2
.

Proposition 5.2
.

(
D_{\infty}
)
Let
R=k\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(x^{2}y)
where
k
is a field of some arbitrary characteristic. Then
\tau_{MCM}(R)=(0)
.

Proof.

Two of the indecomposable Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules are the cokernels of the
1\times 1
matrices
(y)
and
(x^{2})

[
LW12
, Example 14.23]
.

Let

M_{1}=\cok(y)=R/(y)

M_{2}=\cok(x^{2})=R/(x^{2}).

We claim that
\tau_{M_{1}}(R)=(x^{2})
.
First, the map
1\mapsto x^{2}
is a well-defined element of
\Hom(M_{1},R)
since
1\mapsto x^{2}
is a homomorphism from
R\to R
such that
y\mapsto x^{2}y=0
. Now, let
f\in\Hom(M_{1},R)
. Then
yf(1)=f(y)=f(0)=0
which implies
f(1)\in\ann_{R}(y)=(x^{2})R
. Thus,
1\mapsto x^{2}
generates
\Hom(M_{1},R
) and therefore,
\tau_{M_{1}}=(x^{2})
.

Next, we claim that
\tau_{M_{2}}(R)=(y)
. The map
1\mapsto y
is a well-defined element of
\Hom(M_{2},R)
since the map gives
x^{2}\mapsto yx^{2}=0
. Now, let
f\in\Hom(M_{2},R)
. Then
x^{2}f(1)=f(x^{2})=f(0)=0
which implies
f(1)\in\ann_{R}(x^{2})=(y)R
. Thus,
1\mapsto y
generates
\Hom(M_{2},R
) and therefore,
\tau_{M_{2}}=(y)
.

Thus, we see

\tau_{MCM}(R)\subset(x^{2})\cap(y)=(x^{2}y)=(0).\qed

In consequence, the singular test ideal
\tau_{sing}(R)
(obtained by intersecting
\tau_{M}(R)
for
all
big Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules
M
, see
[
PR21
]
) is equal to zero in this example as well. This result very much justifies the hypothesis that
R
is a domain in the results of
[
PR21
]
that describe how
\tau_{sing}(R)
is connected to the singularities of the ring.

In proving the next result, we see our first application of Corollary
4.7
.

Proposition 5.3
.

Let R be the ring
R=k\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(y^{2})
where
k
is a perfect field. Then
\tau_{MCM}(R)=(y)
.

Proof.

Let
I_{n}=(x^{n},y)
for
0\leq n<\infty
, and
I_{\infty}=(y)
. Up to isomorphism, the set of indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules is

\mathcal{M}=\{I_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\}

[
Yos90
, Example 6.5]
. We claim that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\tau_{(x^{n},y)}(R)=(x^{n},y)
and
\tau_{(y)}(R)=(y)
.
For each
n
, let

\varphi_{n}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
x^{n}&0\end{pmatrix}

and let
M_{n}=\mathrm{coker}\,(y\id_{2}-\varphi_{n})
. Similarly, let
\varphi_{\infty}
be the
1\times 1
matrix
(0)
and
M_{\infty}=\mathrm{coker}\,(y\id_{1}-\varphi_{\infty})=R/(y)
. Observe that the
R
-linear map
R^{2}\to(x^{n},y)
which sends
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\
0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
to
x^{n}
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\
1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
to
y
is a surjective map whose kernel is the image of
y\id_{2}-\varphi_{n}
and therefore
M_{n}\cong(x^{n},y)=I_{n}
as
R
-modules. Similarly,
M_{\infty}\cong(y)=I_{\infty}
. Furthermore, we see
\varphi_{n}^{2}=0
and
\varphi_{\infty}^{2}=0
which implies that
(y\id-\varphi_{n},y\id+\varphi_{n})
is a matrix factorization for
y^{2}
for all

. So, by Corollary
4.7
, the trace ideal of
I_{n}
is generated by the entries of
y\id+\varphi_{n}
. Thus,
\tau_{I_{n}}=(x^{n},y)
for all

and
\tau_{I_{\infty}}(R)=(y)

\tau_{MCM}(R)=(y)
.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

Let
R=k\llbracket x,y\rrbracket/(y^{2}+x^{n})
where
k
is an algebraically closed field and
n
is an odd positive integer. Then for each

\tau_{(x^{j},y)}(R)=(x^{j},y)

\tau_{MCM}(R)=(x^{\frac{n-1}{2}},y)
.

Proof.

Up to isomorphism, the set of indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules is
\mathcal{M}=\{R,(x,y),(x^{2},y),...,(x^{\frac{n-1}{2}},y)\}

[
Yos90
, Proposition 5.11]
. For

\varphi_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-x^{n-j}\\

. Observe that the
R
-linear map
R^{2}\to(x^{j},y)

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1\\

\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0\\

y

y\id_{2}-\varphi_{j}

\varphi_{j}^{2}=\begin{pmatrix}-x^{n}&0\\

Remark 5.5
.

Note that

Proposition 6.7
.

(Two-Dimensional
A_{n}
)
Let
R=k\llbracket x,y,z\rrbracket/(z^{2}+x^{2}+y^{n+1})
where
k
is a field (which is algebraically closed and not of characteristic not equal to
2
,
3
, or
5
). Then
\tau_{MCM}(R)=(x,y^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor})
.

Proof.

Up to isomorphism, the non-free indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay
R
-modules are
M_{j}=\mathrm{coker}\,(z\id_{2}-\varphi_{j})
where

\varphi_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}ix&y^{n+1-j}\\
-y^{j}&-ix\\
\end{pmatrix}

for
j\in\{0,...,n\}

[
LW12
, 9.20]
. So by Corollary
4.7
the trace ideal of
M_{j}=\mathrm{coker}\,(z\id_{2}-\varphi_{j})
is generated by the entries of

. Thus,

\tau_{M_{j}}(R)=(x,y^{\min\{j,n+1-j\}}).

Consequently, the intersection is
\tau_{MCM}(R)=(x,y^{\lfloor\frac{n+1}{2}\rfloor})

∎

Proposition 6.8
.

(Two-Dimensional
D_{n}
)

Let
k
be a field and let
R=k\llbracket x,y,z\rrbracket/(z^{2}+x^{2}y+y^{n-1})
for some
n\geq 4
. The maximal Cohen-Macaulay module test ideal of
R
is

\tau_{MCM}(R)=(x^{2},y^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor},z)
.

Proof.

The indecomposable maximal Cohen-Macaulay modules of
R
are given by
M_{j}=\cok(z\id-\varphi_{j})
where
\varphi_{j}
is the following:

For
j=1
, let

\varphi_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-x^{2}-y^{n-2}\\
y&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

For
j\in\{2,3,\ldots,n-2\}
, if
j
is even, let

\varphi_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-xy&-y^{n-1-j/2}\\
0&0&-y^{j/2}&x\\
x&y^{n-1-j/2}&0&0\\
y^{j/2}&-xy&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

and if
j
is odd, let

\varphi_{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-xy&-y^{n-1-(j-1)/2}\\
0&0&-y^{(j+1)/2}&xy\\
x&y^{n-2-(j-1)/2}&0&0\\
y^{(j-1)/2}&-x&0&0\\
\end{pmatrix}.

If
n
is odd, let

\displaystyle\varphi_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}iy^{(n-1)/2}&-x\\
xy&-iy^{(n-1)/2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}iy^{(n-1)/2}&-xy\\
x&-iy^{(n-1)/2}\\
\end{pmatrix}

and if
n
is even, let

\displaystyle\varphi_{n-1}

\displaystyle=\cok\begin{pmatrix}0&-x-iy^{(n-2)/2}\\
xy-iy^{n/2}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle\varphi_{n}

\displaystyle=\cok\begin{pmatrix}0&-x+iy^{(n-2)/2}\\
xy+iy^{n/2}&0\\
\end{pmatrix}

[
LW12
, 9.21]
.

So by Corollary
4.7
the trace ideal of
M_{j}=\cok(z\id-\varphi_{j})
is generated by the entries of
z\id+\varphi_{j}
. Consequently,

\tau_{M_{1}}(R)=(x^{2},y,z).

Given
j\in\{2,3,\ldots,n-2\}
, we have
j<n-1
so that
j/2<n-1-j/2
and thus

\tau_{M_{j}}(R)=(x,y^{j/2},z)

for even values of
j
and

\tau_{M_{j}}(R)=(x,y^{(j-1)/2},z)

for odd values of
j
.

If
n
is odd, we see

\displaystyle\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)

\displaystyle=(x,z+iy^{(n-1)/2},z-iy^{(n-1)/2})=(x,y^{(n-1)/2},z),

\displaystyle\tau_{M_{n}}(R)

\displaystyle=(x,z-iy^{(n-1)/2},z+iy^{(n-1)/2})=(x,y^{(n-1)/2},z).

If
n
is even, we see

\displaystyle\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)

\displaystyle=(x+iy^{(n-2)/2},xy-iy^{n/2},z),

\displaystyle\tau_{M_{n}}(R)

\displaystyle=(x-iy^{(n-2)/2},xy+iy^{n/2},z).

Hence if
n
is odd,
\tau_{MCM}(R)=(x^{2},y^{(n-1)/2},z)
.

If
n
is even, we first claim that

\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)\cap\tau_{M_{n}}(R)=(x^{2},xy,y^{n/2},z).

Indeed,

\displaystyle x^{2}

\displaystyle=(x+\frac{1}{2i}y^{(n-2)/2})(x+iy^{(n-2)/2})+\frac{1}{2i}y^{(n-4)/2}(xy-iy^{n/2})\in\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}y(x+iy^{(n-2)/2})+\frac{1}{2}(xy-iy^{n/2})\in\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)

\displaystyle y^{n/2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2i}y(x+iy^{(n-2)/2})-\frac{1}{2i}(xy-iy^{n/2})\in\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)

\displaystyle x^{2}

\displaystyle=(x-\frac{1}{2i}y^{(n-2)/2})(x-iy^{(n-2)/2})-\frac{1}{2i}y^{(n-4)/2}(xy+iy^{n/2})\in\tau_{M_{n}}(R)

\displaystyle xy

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}y(x-iy^{(n-2)/2})+\frac{1}{2}(xy+iy^{n/2})\in\tau_{M_{n}}(R)

\displaystyle y^{n/2}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2i}y(x-iy^{(n-2)/2})+\frac{1}{2i}(xy+iy^{n/2})\in\tau_{M_{n}}(R)

so that

\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)\cap\tau_{M_{n}}(R)\supset(x^{2},xy,y^{n/2},z).

Conversely, any element
p
of

(\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)\cap\tau_{M_{n}}(R))/(x^{2},xy,y^{n/2},z)

must simultaneously be of the form
q_{1}(x+iy^{(n-2)/2})
and
q_{2}(x-iy^{(n-2)/2})
for
q_{1},q_{2}\in R/(x^{2},xy,y^{n/2},z)
. However, because
x
,
y
, and
z
annihilate
x\pm iy^{(n-2)/2}
, we may suppose that
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are constants. However,

q_{1}(x+iy^{(n-2)/2})=q_{2}(x-iy^{(n-2)/2})

implies

(q_{2}-q_{1})x=(q_{2}+q_{1})iy^{(n-2)/2}.

Since
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
are constants, this is only possible if
q_{2}-q_{1}=0
and
q_{2}+q_{1}=0
so that
q_{1}=q_{2}=0
and thus
p=0
. Hence,
\tau_{M_{n-1}}(R)\cap\tau_{M_{n}}(R)=(x^{2},xy,y^{n/2},z)
as claimed. Since
\tau_{M_{j}}(R)\supset(x^{2},y^{n/2},z)
for
j<n-1
, we conclude
\tau_{MCM}(R)=(x^{2},y^{n/2},z)
when
n
is even as well.
∎
