Quantum vs Classical Birth and Death Processes; Exactly Solvable Examples


Abstract
A coinless quantisation procedure of continuous and discrete time Birth and Death (BD) processes is presented. The quantum Hamiltonian ℋ \mathcal{H} is derived by similarity transforming the matrix L L describing the BD equation in terms of the square root of the stationary (reversible) distribution. The quantum and classical systems share the entire eigenvalues and the eigenvectors are related one to one. When the birth rate B ⁡ ( x ) B(x) and the death rate D ⁡ ( x ) D(x) are chosen to be the coefficients of the difference equation governing the orthogonal polynomials of Askey scheme, the quantum system is exactly solvable. The eigenvectors are the orthogonal polynomials themselves and the eigenvalues are given analytically. Many examples are periodic since their eigenvalues are all integers, or all integers for integer parameters. The situation is very similar to the exactly solvable one dimensional quantum mechanical systems. These exactly solvable Markov chains contain many adjustable free parameters which could be helpful for various simulation purposes.

Constructing the quantum counterparts of various classical stochastic processes, in particular,
random walks on graphs, has been an exciting hot topic in computer science,

2
Continuous time birth and death processes

Birth and Death (BD) processes are a special branch of the general stationary Markov processes (chains) on graphs,
having the nearest neighbour interactions only.
Let us denote the set of vertices of the graph
G
by
V
, either finite or semi-infinite:

V=\{0,1,\ldots,N\}:\quad\text{finite},\qquad V=\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}:\quad\text{semi-infinite}.

(2.1)

For analytic treatments I use
x,y,..
as representing the the vertices of V,
x,y\in{V}

rather than the conventional
n
and
m
etc.
Let us denote the birth rate at population
x
by
B(x)>0
going from
x
to
x+1
and the death rate by
D(x)>0

going from
x
to
x-1
.
In order to keep non-negative populations and the maximal population
N
in the semi-infinite case, the following boundary conditions are imposed,

D(0)=0,\quad B(N)=0:\ (\text{only for a finite case}),

(2.2)

which are called the reflecting boundary conditions.
Let
\mathcal{P}(x;t)\geq 0
be the probability distribution over
V
at time
t
.
The continuous time evolution of the probability distribution is governed by the following differential equation:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\mathcal{P}(x;t)

\displaystyle=-(B(x)+D(x))\mathcal{P}(x;t)+B(x-1)\mathcal{P}(x-1;t)+D(x+1)\mathcal{P}(x+1;t),

(2.3)

\displaystyle=(L\mathcal{P})(x;t)=\sum_{y\in{V}}L_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}(y;t),\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=1,

(2.4)

Here
L
is a
tri-diagonal
matrix on
\ell^{2}(V)
,

\displaystyle{L}_{x+1\,x}=B(x),\ \ {L}_{x-1\,x}=D(x),\ {L}_{x\,x}=-B(x)-D(x),\ \ L_{x\,y}=0,\quad|x-y|\geq 2,

(2.5)

satisfying the condition

\sum_{x\in{V}}{L}_{x\,y}=0.

(2.6)

This ensures the conservation of probability, that is, the condition

\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=1
is preserved by the time evolution (
2.4
).
It should be stressed that in the continuous time formulation,
the
time scale is completely arbitrary
. In other words, the overall scale of
the birth/death ratio
B(x)
and
D(x)
can be multiplied by any factor depending on various parameters.

Let us introduce a positive function

\phi_{0}(x)
on
{V}
and a
diagonal matrix

\Phi
consisting of
\phi_{0}(x)

by the ratios of
B(x)
and
D(x+1)
,

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(0)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}1,\quad\phi_{0}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}\frac{B(y)}{D(y+1)}}\Leftrightarrow\frac{\phi_{0}(x+1)}{\phi_{0}(x)}=\frac{\sqrt{B(x)}}{\sqrt{D(x+1)}},\quad x\in{V}.

(2.7)

\displaystyle\Phi_{x\,x}=\phi_{0}(x),\quad\Phi_{x\,y}=0,\quad x\neq y.

(2.8)

For semi-infinite cases,
B(x)
and
D(x)
must be restricted so that
\phi_{0}(x)
is square summable,

\sum_{x\in{V}}\phi_{0}(x)^{2}<\infty,\qquad\phi_{0}\in\ell^{2}(V).

(2.9)

By a similarity transformation
in terms of
\Phi
, let us introduce another
tri-diagonal
matrix
{\mathcal{H}}
on
\ell^{2}(V)
,

\displaystyle\hskip 142.26378pt\mathcal{H}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}-\Phi^{-1}{L}\Phi,

(2.10)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x\,x}=-{L}_{x\,x}=B(x)+D(x),\qquad\mathcal{H}_{x\,y}=0,\quad|x-y|\geq 2,

(2.11)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x+1\,x}=-\phi_{0}(x+1)^{-1}{L}_{x+1\,x}\phi_{0}(x)=-\phi_{0}(x+1)^{-1}B(x)\,\phi_{0}(x)=-\sqrt{B(x)D(x+1)},

(2.12)

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}_{x-1\,x}=-\phi_{0}(x-1)^{-1}{L}_{x-1\,x}\phi_{0}(x)=-\phi_{0}(x-1)^{-1}\,D(x)\phi_{0}(x)=-\sqrt{B(x-1)D(x)}.

(2.13)

The matrix
\mathcal{H}
is the
quantum Hamiltonian
corresponding to the classical continuous time
birth and death process (
2.3
) as it is

real symmetric
and
positive semi-definite
as can be seen clearly
by the following factorisation in terms of an upper triangular matrix
\mathcal{A}
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle={}^{t}\!\!\mathcal{A}\mathcal{A}\ \Rightarrow\mathcal{H}={}^{t}\mathcal{H},

(2.14)

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{x\,x}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{B(x)},\quad\mathcal{A}_{x\,x+1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}-\sqrt{D(x+1)},\quad\mathcal{A}_{x\,y}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}0,\ \text{otherwise},\quad x,y\in{V},

(2.15)

in which
{}^{t}\!\!\mathcal{A}
is the transposed matrix of
\mathcal{A}
.
The zero mode (eigenvector) of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{H}

is
\phi_{0}(x)

0=(\mathcal{A}\phi_{0})(x)=\sqrt{B(x)}\phi_{0}(x)-\sqrt{D(x+1)}\phi_{0}(x+1)\ \Rightarrow(\mathcal{H}\phi_{0})(x)=0.

(2.16)

The factorisation guarantees the reality and non-negativeness of the eigenvalues of
\mathcal{H}
,

\mathcal{E}(0)=0,\quad\mathcal{E}(n)>0,\qquad n\in{V}\backslash\{0\},

(2.17)

and the orthogonality of the eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}
, since the simpleness of
the spectrum is due to its tri-diagonality.
The positive semi-definiteness of
\mathcal{H}
(
2.17
) means
negative semi-definiteness

of
L
(
2.4
), which is essential for the stability of the system.

Next let us introduce another tri-diagonal matrix
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
by the similarity transformation
in terms of
\Phi
,

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\Phi^{-1}\mathcal{H}\Phi\ \Leftrightarrow\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,y}=\phi_{0}(x)^{-1}{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,y}\phi_{0}(y),

(2.18)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,x+1}=-B(x),\ \widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,x-1}=-D(x),\ \widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,x}=B(x)+D(x),\ \widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,y}=0,|x-y|\geq 2.

(2.19)

\displaystyle\hskip 85.35826pt\Longrightarrow\sum_{y\in{V}}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,y}=0.

(2.20)

The eigenvalue problem of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}

\displaystyle(\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}v_{n})(x)=\sum_{y\in{V}}\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}_{x\,y}v_{n}(y)=\mathcal{E}(n)v_{n}(x),\quad x,n\in{V}.

(2.21)

takes a simple form of a second order difference equation for
v_{n}(x)
,

\displaystyle B(x)\bigl(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(x+1)\bigr)+D(x)\bigl(v_{n}(x)-v_{n}(x-1)\bigr)=\mathcal{E}(n)v_{n}(x),\quad x,n\in{V}.

(2.22)

It should be stressed that for finite BD processes,
\{v_{n}(x)\}
are eigenvectors.
But for infinite systems, they might not be eigenvectors as such. For examplre

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt\mathcal{E}(0)=0,\quad v_{0}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}1.

(2.23)

is a solution but it is definitely not square summable
\sum_{x\in{V}}v_{0}(x)^{2}=\infty
.
Since the first component of the eigenvector of a tri-diagopnal matrix can never vanish,
it is natural to adopt the following universal normalisation of the solutions
\{v_{n}(x)\}
,

v_{n}(0)=1,\quad n\in{V}.

(2.24)

This assertion is valid irrespective of the square summability of
\{v_{n}(x)\}
.
The eigenvectors of
\mathcal{H}
and
L
are

\displaystyle(\mathcal{H}\phi_{n})(x)

\displaystyle=\mathcal{E}(n)\phi_{n}(x),\qquad\phi_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\phi_{0}(x)v_{n}(x),\quad n\in{V},

(2.25)

\displaystyle(L\phi_{0}\phi_{n})(x)

\displaystyle=-\mathcal{E}(n)\phi_{0}(x)\phi_{n}(x),\hskip 105.2751ptn\in{V},

(2.26)

so long as
\{\phi_{n}\}
are square summable,
\phi_{n}\in\ell^{2}(V)
,

\displaystyle(\phi_{n},\phi_{m})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in{V}}\phi_{n}(x)\phi_{m}(x)=\sum_{x\in{V}}\phi_{0}(x)^{2}v_{n}(x)v_{m}(x)=\frac{1}{d_{n}^{2}}\delta_{n\,m},\quad n,m\in{V}.

(2.27)

Here
\phi_{0}(x)^{2}
provides the orthogonality measure of the vectors
\{v_{n}(x)\}

of
\widetilde{\mathcal{H}}
.
The normalisation constant is positive
d_{n}>0
.

Let us define orthonormal vectors
\{\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
in
\ell^{2}(V)
,

\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}d_{n}\phi_{n}(x)=d_{n}\phi_{0}(x)v_{n}(x),\quad(\hat{\phi}_{n},\hat{\phi}_{m})=\delta_{n\,m},\quad n,m\in{V},

(2.28)

and the square of the normalised zero mode

\displaystyle\pi(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)^{2}=d_{0}^{2}\phi_{0}(x)^{2}=d_{0}^{2}\,\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}\frac{B(y)}{D(y+1)},

(2.29)

\displaystyle\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi(x)=1\Leftarrow\frac{1}{d_{0}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in{V}}\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}\frac{B(y)}{D(y+1)},

(2.30)

which will turn out to be the
stationary distribution
of the classical birth and death process.

The solution of the general classical continuous time BD problem (
2.3
) with the birth rate
B(x)

and death rate
D(x)
is reduced to the eigenvalue problem of the corresponding quantum
Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}
(
2.10
)–(
2.13
). It should be stressed that
\mathcal{H}

has a very special form corresponding to the nearest neighbour interactions
with non-vanishing elements on the diagonal, super and sub-diagonals only.
It is summarised by the following

Theorem 2.1

By using the complete set of eigensystem
\{\mathcal{E}(n),\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\}
,
x,n\in{V}
,
of the quantum Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}}

(
2.10
)–(
2.13
), (
2.25
),
the solution of the initial value problem of the classical continuous time BD (
2.5
)
is given by

\mathcal{P}(x;t)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\sum_{n\in{V}}c_{n}e^{-\mathcal{E}(n)t}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x),

(2.31)

in which
\{c_{n}\}
are determined as the expansion coefficients of the initial distribution
\mathcal{P}(x;0)
,

\mathcal{P}(x;0)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\sum_{n\in{V}}c_{n}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\Rightarrow c_{0}=1,\ c_{n}=\sum_{x\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)^{-1}\mathcal{P}(x;0),\quad n=1,\ldots.

(2.32)

The transition matrix from
y
to
x
after time
t
is

\mathcal{P}(x,y;t)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)\hat{\phi}_{0}(y)^{-1}\sum_{n\in{V}}e^{-\mathcal{E}(n)t}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y).

(2.33)

It is straightforward to verify the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation

\mathcal{P}(x,y;t+s)=\sum_{z\in{V}}\mathcal{P}(x,z;t)\mathcal{P}(z,y;s),

(2.34)

by the orthogonality relation (
2.28
) and the completeness relation

\sum_{n\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\delta_{x\,y},\quad x,y\in{V}.

(2.35)

The approach to the stationary (or terminal) distribution
\pi(x)
(
2.29
) is guaranteed by the positivity of
\mathcal{E}(n)>0

for the non-zero modes, irrespective of the initial distribution,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(x;t)=\pi(x),\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\mathcal{P}(x,y;t)=\pi(x).

(2.36)

Various stochastic quantities can be derived from these expressions.

This was reported as
Theorem 3.2
in

3
Discrete time birth and death processes

The formulation of classical discrete time BD processes goes almost parallel with the continuous time case,
with the same notation of G, V, and
x,y\in{V}
as in (
2.1
).
The birth and death probability at
x
per unit time interval is denoted by
0<b(x)<1
and
0<d(x)<1

satisfying the same boundary condition as (
2.2
)

d(0)=0,\quad b(N)=0:\ (\text{only for a finite case}),\quad 0<b(x)+d(x)<1.

(3.1)

Let
\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;\ell)\geq 0
(
\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;\ell)=1
)
be the probability distribution at population
x
over
V
at
\ell
-th time step.
The next step distribution is given by

\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;\ell+1)=\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}^{d}(y;\ell).

(3.2)

The initial value problem is to determine
\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;\ell)
for given
\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;0)
,

\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;\ell)=\sum_{y\in{V}}(K)^{\ell}_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}^{d}(y;0),\quad\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;0)=1,\quad\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;0)\geq 0.

(3.3)

Here
K
is a non-negative
tri-diagonal
matrix on
\ell^{2}(V)
,

\displaystyle{K}_{x+1\,x}=b(x),\ \ {K}_{x-1\,x}=d(x),\ {K}_{x\,x}=1-b(x)-d(x),\ \ K_{x\,y}=0,\quad|x-y|\geq 2,

(3.4)

satisfying the condition

\sum_{x\in{V}}{K}_{x\,y}=1\ \Rightarrow\sum_{x\in{V}}\mathcal{P}^{d}(x;\ell+1)=\sum_{y\in{V}}\sum_{x\in{V}}K_{x\,y}\mathcal{P}^{d}(y;\ell)=\sum_{y\in{V}}\mathcal{P}^{d}(y;\ell)=1.

(3.5)

In view of the quantum version, which is time reversal invariant, the following condition is imposed.

Condition 3.1

The system has a reversible (detail balanced) distribution, satisfying

K_{x\,y}\pi(y)=K_{y\,x}\pi(x),\qquad\sum_{x\in{V}}\pi(x)=1,\quad x,y\in{V}.

(3.6)

It is clear that
\pi(x)
is the eigenvector of
K
with eigenvalue 1,

\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{x\,y}\pi(y)=\sum_{y\in{V}}K_{y\,x}\pi(x)=\pi(x).

(3.7)

The reversibility condition (
3.6
) connects
\pi(x)
with
\pi(x\pm 1)
,

\displaystyle K_{x\,x+1}\pi(x+1)=K_{x+1\,x}\pi(x)\Rightarrow d(x+1)\pi(x+1)=b(x)\pi(x),

(3.8)

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\pi(x)\propto\phi_{0}(x)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}\frac{b(y)}{d(y+1)},\quad\frac{1}{d_{0}^{2}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sum_{x\in{V}}\prod_{y=0}^{x-1}\frac{b(y)}{d(y+1)},\quad\phi_{0}(0)=1,\ \phi_{0}(x)>0,

\displaystyle\ \quad\pi(x)=\hat{\phi}_{0}(x)^{2}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\phi_{0}(x)^{2}d_{0}^{2}>0.

(3.9)

Here the same notation as in (
2.29
) is used purposefully.
Since the eigenvector
\pi(x)
is all positive, it belongs to the maximal eigenvalue.
Perron-Frobenius theorem tells that

K
’s spectrum is bounded by
1
and
-1
:

-1\leq\text{Eigenvalues}(K)\leq 1.

(3.10)

Since
K
is tri-diagonal all the eigenvalues are non-degenerate.

By dividing the reversibility condition (
3.6
) by
\sqrt{\pi(x)\pi(y)}
, a non-negative real symmetric
tri-diagonal matrix
\mathcal{T}
is obtained,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{x\,y}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(x)}}K_{x\,y}\sqrt{\pi(y)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi(y)}}K_{y\,x}\sqrt{\pi(x)}

\displaystyle=\phi_{0}(x)^{-1}K_{x\,y}\phi_{0}(y)=\phi_{0}(y)^{-1}K_{y\,x}\phi_{0}(x)={\mathcal{T}}_{y\,x},\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{x\,y}=0,\ |x-y)\geq 2,

(3.11)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{T}}_{x+1\,x}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{b(x)d(x+1)},\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{x-1\,x}=\sqrt{b(x-1)d(x)},\quad{\mathcal{T}}_{x\,x}=1-b(x)-d(x).

(3.12)

This is equivalent to the similarity transformation of
K
in terms of
\Phi
,

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}=\Phi^{-1}K\Phi,\qquad\Phi_{x\,x}=\phi_{0}(x),\quad\Phi_{x\,y}=0,\quad x\neq y.

(3.13)

The Hamiltonian of the discrete time BD process
\mathcal{H}^{d}
is defined by

Quantum birth and death processes

The quantum BD process, continuous and discrete time, is described in the Hilbert space

{}^{t}(&\!\!0&\cdots&1&0&\cdots&)\\

\displaystyle\hskip 227.62204pt\Longrightarrow\sum_{n\in{V}}\|n\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle n\|=\boldsymbol{1}.

\displaystyle B(x)

\displaystyle=p(N-x),\quad D(x)=(1-p)x,\quad 0<p<1,\quad\mathcal{E}(n)=n,

\displaystyle\phi_{0}(x)^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{N!}{x!\,(N-x)!}\Bigl(\frac{p}{1-p}\Bigr)^{x},\quad d_{n}^{2}=\frac{N!}{n!\,(N-n)!}\Bigl(\frac{p}{1-p}\Bigr)^{n}\times(1-p)^{N},

\displaystyle={\phi}_{0}(x){\phi}_{0}(y)\sum_{n\in{V}}d_{n}^{2}\check{P}_{n}(x)\check{P}_{n}(y)=\sum_{n\in{V}}\hat{\phi}_{n}(x)\hat{\phi}_{n}(y)=\delta_{x\,y}.

6
Comments

A few comments are in order.

•

I follow the standard convention of quantum mechanics,

\langle\rm{final\ state}|e^{-i\mathcal{H}t}|{\rm initial\ state}\rangle
.
The corresponding probability
\mathcal{P}(x,y;t)
also means the probability of starting at the initial state
y
at time 0
and arriving at the final state
x
after time
t
. In probability community, the opposite convention is dominant.

•

The Karlin-McGregor representation
[
20
]
is a well-known solution method of BD processes.
A brief comparison/contrast with my approach would be helpful.
The orthogonal polynomials play important roles in both approaches.
In
[
20
]
, the population is denoted by
n,m\in{V}
. The birth rate
\lambda_{n}
and
death rate
\mu_{n}
define orthogonal polynomials
\{Q_{n}(x)\}
through three term recurrence relation

-xQ_{n}(x)=\mu_{n}Q_{n-1}(x)-(\lambda_{n}+\mu_{n})Q_{n}(x)+\lambda_{n}Q_{n+1}(x),

which is dual to the difference equation (
5.1
).
Here
x
is the spectral parameter. The positive orthogonality measure
\psi
on
0\leq x<\infty
,

\int_{0}^{\infty}Q_{i}(x)Q_{j}(x)d\psi(x)=0,\quad i\neq j,

(
[
20
]
.2.4)

is determined through a certain moment problem. The probability from
j
to
i
after time
t
is

\mathcal{P}(i,j;t)=\pi_{i}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-xt}Q_{i}(x)Q_{j}(x)d\psi(x),

(
[
20
]
.1.7)

in which
\pi_{i}
is the same as
\pi(i)
defined in (
2.29
).
In the approach no (symmetric) matrix
appears explicitly whose spectrum is related with
x
and the measure
\psi
.
Such matrix would be a candidate for the quantum Hamiltonian.
I presume this is one of the reasons why there are relatively few papers discussing quantum BD processes.

•

Grünbaum, Vinet and Zhedanov
[
16
]
, formulated continuous time quantum BD processes following
Karlin-McGregor approach. They adopted a Hamiltonian
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
which is related to
\mathcal{H}

(
2.10
)–(
2.13
) by a similarity transformation

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}_{x\,y}=(-1)^{x}\mathcal{H}_{x\,y}(-1)^{y},

which has all positive matrix elements. They also showed an interesting connection between a
continuous time quantum BD process

\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
and a continuous time spin chain with single excitation state,
whose Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}^{spin}
reads in the notation of this paper

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}^{\prime}}^{spin}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in{V}}\sqrt{B(x)D(x+1)}\bigl((\sigma^{1})_{x}(\sigma^{1})_{x+1}+(\sigma^{2})_{x}(\sigma^{2})_{x+1}\bigr)

\displaystyle\ +\frac{1}{2}\sum_{x\in{V}}\bigl(B(x)+D(x)\bigr)\bigl((\sigma^{3})_{x}+1).

(
[
16
]
.3.2)

Here
(\sigma^{j})_{x}

j=1,2,3
is the Pauli sigma matrix at vertex
x
.
The solution of the spin problem is obtained by the solution of the eigenvalue problem of the matrix
\mathcal{H}^{\prime}
.
This means all exactly solvable BD processes reported in the present paper and
[
35
]
provide as many exactly
solvable spin chains both continuous and discrete time. And many of them are periodic.

•

Some exact analytical results of continuous time quantum walks on graphs are worth mentioning.
They are on a cycle graph
[
38
]
, on a Cayley tree
[
27
]
, on a ring with
N
vertices and each vertex is connected with
m

nearest neighbours on the right and left
[
43
]
and on a star graph
[
44
]
.

•

Time averaged distributions of quantum walks were reported in many papers.
It should be stressed that these results are initial state specific and not universal, cf (
4.19
).

•

The present solution method does not apply to
q
-Meixner and
q
-Charlier, although they are listed in
§4 of
[
35
]
. This is because these polynomials fail to satisfy the completeness relation (
2.35
)
as proven in
[
6
]
. These two polynomials and others which have Jackson integral measures
provide another type of exactly solvable classical BD processes
[
32
]
.
It is a good challenge to formulate their quantum versions.

•

The same solution techniques apply to quantum BD processes related to
various new orthogonal polynomials
[
31
]
.
They are deformations of the classical orthogonal polynomials,
e.g.
the Racah and
q
-Racah, etc
obtained by multiple applications of the discrete analogue of the Darboux transformations or
the Krein-Adler transformations. These new polynomials offer virtually infinite examples of
exactly solvable quantum BD processes.
