Mamba Hawkes Process for Event Sequence Modeling
Shan Dai, Yuyang Shen, Yuyang Liang, Chenhao Ma, Anningzhe Gao

Abstract
. Modeling asynchronous event sequences is crucial in numerous real-world applications such as healthcare monitoring, financial transaction analysis, and so on. Traditional temporal point processes, including Hawkes Processes, often fail to capture complex dependencies due to their parametric limitations. While neural approaches like RNNs and Transformers have improved flexibility, they struggle with computational inefficiency, and attention saturation. In this paper, we introduce the Mamba Hawkes Process (MHP), the first framework to integrate selective state space model (Mamba) with temporal point processes. MHP leverages time-varying state transitions and input-dependent gating to efficiently encode event history and capture long-term dependencies with linear complexity. Importantly, we provide theoretical guarantees showing that MHP generalizes both classical multi-exponential Hawkes processes and exponential-decay gated RNNs, underscoring its expressive power and theoretical soundness. To address the inherent constraints of pure state space models in handling heterogeneous event interactions, we further develop Adaptive Mamba Hawkes Process (A-MHP) that incorporates two novel mechanisms: a Time-Scaling Mechanism that adaptively weights time intervals based on event type and history, and a Dual-Channel State Transition that adaptively processes event content and temporal dynamics for more refined state updates. Extensive experiments on synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that MHP and A-MHP consistently outperform state-of-the-art baselines in event prediction tasks, particularly in long-sequence scenarios. Our work establishes a scalable and theoretically grounded paradigm for event sequence modeling, with practical implications for predictive maintenance, anomaly detection, and dynamic system analysis. The code is available at https://github.com/Ethan-Shen-Individual-Lab/Mamba-Hawkes-Process.

1.
Introduction

Large volumes of irregular and asynchronous event sequences are generated by both human activities and natural phenomena. Examples include user interactions on social media platforms
(
Zhang et al., 2023
;
Fang et al., 2023
)
, transaction histories in financial markets
(
Bacry et al., 2015
;
Hawkes, 2018
)
, electronic health records
(
Wang et al., 2018
)
, and earthquake occurrences with aftershocks in geophysics
(
Ogata, 1998
)
. Unlike traditional sequential data such as time series, these asynchronous event sequences are characterized by irregular intervals between events, where the timing of events is as essential as their sequence order in describing underlying dynamics.

Temporal point processes (TPPs), defined by the intensity functions, are widely used for modeling asynchronous event sequences
(
Cox and Isham, 1980
;
Brillinger et al., 2002
)
. Among TPPs, Hawkes Processes are particularly noteworthy for their non-negative intensity functions that capture the triggering effects of previous events. However, traditional Hawkes Processes have significant limitations. They do not account for mutual inhibition between events, a crucial factor in many real-world scenarios, and they lack robust nonlinear fitting capabilities, thereby limiting their expressive power. To address these limitations, researchers have introduced likelihood-free methods
(
Xiao et al., 2017a
;
Li et al., 2018
)
and also non-parametric approaches like kernel methods and splines
(
Zhou et al., 2013
)
.

Figure 1
.
SSM is naturally aligned with the Hawkes Process

The advancement of neural networks and deep learning has revolutionized sequence modeling.
(
Du et al., 2016
)
introduced RNN-based Hawkes process models, enabling neurally self-modulating point processes and capturing complex memory effects. However, RNNs, even with LSTM
(
Hochreiter and Schmidhuber, 1997
)
and GRU
(
Chung et al., 2014
)
, struggle with long-term dependencies due to vanishing and exploding gradients
(
Pascanu et al., 2013
)
. To overcome this,
(
Zuo et al., 2020
)
proposed the Transformer Hawkes Process (THP), which avoids RNN/CNN structures and achieves better performance. Yet, attention-based transformers also face challenges in modeling very long sequences, as their finite attention windows limit long-range dependency capture
(
Gao and Dai, 2024
)
.

Recently, structured state space sequence models (SSMs)
(
Gu et al., 2021a
)
have emerged as a powerful paradigm for modeling long sequences with continuous dynamics. The Mamba model
(
Gu and Dao, 2023
)
, a selective SSM, introduces data-dependent context compression by updating hidden states through a continuous transition kernel that controls how past information decays or persists over time. This mechanism closely parallels temporal point processes (TPPs), where each event modulates a continuous conditional intensity function. In both frameworks, the latent dynamics evolve between events and are updated upon event occurrences. Consequently, selective SSMs like Mamba offer a natural neural parameterization for temporal point processes (Figure
1
).

However, adapting Mamba to model Hawkes Processes introduces several theoretical and practical challenges.
First, Mamba and other SSMs are typically designed for regularly sampled sequences, whereas Hawkes Processes operate on irregular, event-driven timelines, thereby requiring the state transitions of SSMs to be redefined to adapt to non-uniform event arrivals.
Second, Mamba’s linear state space formulation, while computationally efficient, limits its ability to represent nonlinear interactions common in real-world event dynamics. Third, while Mamba excels at capturing long-range dependencies, its selective scanning mechanism may overlook sparse but critical long-term excitatory or inhibitory effects in TPPs, such as delayed reactions in social contagion
(
Ding et al., 2021
)
.
To address these challenges, we propose a novel Mamba Hawkes Process (MHP) model that integrates Mamba’s selective state space framework with Hawkes Processes.
Beyond this intuitive alignment, we provide theoretical guarantees that substantiate the connection between Mamba and temporal point processes. Specifically, we prove that the proposed MHP can be degraded into a multi-exponential Hawkes process as well as an exponential-decay gated RNN in Section
4.3
.

While MHP establishes a principled foundation, its state transition nature and fixed temporal scaling limit its expressive power in capturing heterogeneous event interactions in sequences. To overcome these limitations, we further propose the Adaptive Mamba Hawkes Process (A-MHP), an extended framework that enhances the expressiveness of MHP by introducing adaptive temporal processing and dual embedding transitions to better capture heterogeneous event dynamics.

Contributions.
Building on these innovations, the main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

•

Constructive Integration of Mamba into Temporal Point Processes.
To the best of our knowledge, this is the inaugural work to adapt the Mamba state space model for temporal point processes, enabling efficient event history encoding and improved long-term dependency modeling beyond RNNs and Transformers. Importantly, we provide theoretical foundations proving that MHP can be reduced to multi-exponential Hawkes Processes and exponential-decay gated RNNs under specific configurations. This alignment not only validates the design of MHP but also highlights its generality and expressive capacity in capturing complex temporal dynamics.

•

Adaptive Temporal Modeling with Dual Innovations.
To address the inherent constraints of a state space model in handling heterogeneous event interactions, we develop A-MHP, which introduces two key mechanisms-
Time-Scaling
for adaptive temporal processing across event types, and
Dual-Channel State Transition
for content-dependent and time-dependent embedding computation, addressing limitations of MHP and enhancing event prediction accuracy.

•

Comprehensive Experiment Validation and Practical Impact.
We conducted extensive experiments on both synthetic and real-world datasets to assess the effectiveness of MHP and A-MHP , which consistently outperform state-of-the-art baselines in both event type and timing and enable more accurate event prediction in practical applications.

2.
Related Work

2.1.
Neural Hawkes Process

Hawkes processes are widely used for temporal prediction in various fields. To enhance their performance, many deep learning approaches have been applied.
The Recurrent Marked Temporal Point Process (RMTPP)
(
Du et al., 2016
)
employs RNNs to model the influence of event history on intensity functions. RMTPP inspired subsequent models that use dual RNNs, one for background intensity and another for the impact of historical events, enabling end-to-end sequence modeling
(
Xiao et al., 2017b
)
. Continuous-time LSTM models
(
Mei and Eisner, 2017
)
extend RMTPP by capturing self-modulating effects, addressing both excitation and inhibition from previous events.

With the development of self-attention mechanisms, self-attention-based neural Hawkes processes were proposed. SAHP
(
Zhang et al., 2020
)
utilized self-attention to enhance these processes, while THP
(
Zuo et al., 2020
)
used the transformer encoder to convert sequence data into continuous conditional intensity functions. UTHP
(
Zhang et al., 2021
)
incorporated RNNs and CNNs to address issues in THP, such as parallel processing and recursive learning. TAA-THP
(
Zhang et al., 2022
)
improved attention structures by incorporating temporal encoding. Lastly,
(
Yu et al., 2022
)
proposed Hawkesformer, linking hierarchical attention mechanisms to Hawkes processes for information cascade prediction.

2.2.
State Space Model

State space models (SSMs) were initially developed as mathematical tools to describe dynamic systems in modern control theory. With the introduction of HiPPO
(
Gu et al., 2020
)
initialization, the Linear State-Space Layer (LSSL)
(
Gu et al., 2021b
)
demonstrated the capability to handle long-range dependencies in sequential data. However, LSSL’s computational and memory overhead make it impractical for large-scale applications. Structured State Space models (S4)
(
Gu et al., 2021a
)
address these issues by using reparameterization to enhance computational efficiency, providing an effective alternative to traditional attention mechanisms.

Several recent variants of S4 have been proposed to achieve linear time attention, including H3
(
Fu et al., 2022
)
, Gated State Space
(
Mehta et al., 2022
)
, Hyena
(
Nguyen et al., 2024
)
, and RWKV
(
Peng et al., 2023
)
. Mamba
(
Gu and Dao, 2023
)
introduces a data-dependent selection mechanism to S4, improving the capture of long-range context as sequence length increases. Mamba not only achieves linear time efficiency in long-sequence modeling but also outperforms Transformers across various benchmarks. Recently, Jamba
(
Lieber et al., 2024
)
has been introduced as a novel hybrid model that combines Transformer and Mamba layers in a mixture-of-experts (MoE) architecture. Jamba interleaves blocks of Transformer and Mamba layers, harnessing the strengths of both model families.

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Hawkes Processes

A TPP
(
Daley et al., 2003
;
Daley and Vere-Jones, 2007
)
is a stochastic process that defines a probability distribution over event sequences. In this process, the number of points (events)
K
and their locations (arrival times)
t_{i}
are random. The realization of a TPP can be represented as a sequence of discrete events with
\{t_{i}\}\in\mathbb{R}^{+}
and
i\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
abstracted as points on a timeline. We can represent a TPP realization by a counting measure
N(t)=\sum_{i}^{n}\mathbb{I}(t_{i}<t)
, for
t\in[0,T]
. The conditional intensity characterizing a TPP can be interpreted as the expected number of events per unit of time and is defined as:

(1)

\lambda(t)=\lim\limits_{\Delta t\downarrow 0}\frac{\mathbb{E}[N(t+\Delta t)-N(t)|\mathcal{H}_{t}]}{\Delta t},

where
\mathcal{H}_{t}=\{t_{i}:t_{i}<t\}
is the event history until time
t
, which acts as a filtration to the process. Given
\lambda(t)
, the probability that no new event occurs up to time
t
, after the last event occurblue at
t_{n}
, can be expressed as
\exp\left(-\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\lambda(\tau)\,d\tau\right)
. Then, the conditional density function can be formulated by

(2)

f(t)=\lambda(t)\exp\left(-\int_{t_{n}}^{t}\lambda(\tau)\,d\tau\right).

The Hawkes process
(
Hawkes, 1971
;
Laub et al., 2015
)
is a typical temporal point process, and it models past events and predicts the timestamp of the following event by its conditional intensity function, which is defined as:

(3)

\lambda(t)=\mu+\sum\limits_{j:t_{i}<t}\psi(t-t_{i}),

where
\mu\geq 0
, named base intensity, is an exogenous component of the intensity function independent of the history, while
\psi(t)>0
is an endogenous component dependent on the history. Besides,
\psi(t)
is a triggering kernel containing the peer influence of past events. Due to the self-exciting charities, the Hawkes process has received wide attention and applications in event sequence modeling.

3.2.
Mamba

The structublue state space sequence models (S4)
(
Gu et al., 2021a
)
is defined by the equation (
4
), which maps a 1-dimensional function or sequence
x(t)\in\mathbb{R}\mapsto y(t)\in\mathbb{R}
through an implicit latent state
h(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
:

(4)

\displaystyle h^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{A}h(t)+\mathbf{B}x(t),

\displaystyle y(t)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}h(t),

where
\mathbf{A}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times N},\mathbf{B}\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times 1},\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times N}
are parameters of neural networks in deep learning. To deal with the discrete input sequence
\boldsymbol{x}=(x_{0},x_{1},...)\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
, S4 discretizes these parameters in equation (
4
) using a step size
\Delta
, where the continuous parameters
\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}
are converted into discrete parameters
\overline{\mathbf{A}}=f_{A}(\Delta,\mathbf{A}),\overline{\mathbf{B}}=f_{B}(\Delta,\mathbf{B})
, where the pair
(f_{A},f_{B})
is called a discretization rule
(
Gu et al., 2021a
)
. As a result, a recurrent formula is derived to iteratively compute the state and output of the system for discrete time steps:

(5)

\displaystyle h_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{\overline{A}}h_{t-1}+\mathbf{\overline{B}}x_{t},

\displaystyle y_{t}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}h_{t}.

Various rules can be used such as the zero-order hold (ZOH) defined as follows:

(6)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}

\displaystyle=\exp(\Delta\mathbf{A}),

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{B}}

\displaystyle=(\Delta\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\exp(\Delta\mathbf{A})-\mathbf{I})\cdot\Delta\mathbf{B}.

In addition to recurrent mode in equation (
5
), the model can also be computed in convolution mode, as expanded below:

(7)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{K}}

\displaystyle=(\mathbf{C}\overline{\mathbf{B}},\mathbf{C}\overline{\mathbf{AB}},\ldots,\mathbf{C}\overline{\mathbf{A}}^{L-1}\overline{\mathbf{B}}),

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{y}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{x}*\overline{\mathbf{K}},

where
L
denotes the length of the input sequence
\boldsymbol{x}
and
\overline{\mathbf{K}}\in\mathbb{R}^{L}
is the convolution kernel.

By introducing a data-dependent selection mechanism to S4, the Mamba models
(
Gu and Dao, 2023
)
utilize selective SSMs with a time-variant machanism, where the discrete matrices
\bar{A},\bar{B},
and
C
of each channel are modified over
L
time steps depending on the input sequence. Unlike traditional state-space layers, which operate individually on each channel, selective state-space layers compute the SSM matrices
\bar{A}_{i},\bar{B}_{i},C_{i}
for all
i\leq L
based on all the channels, and then apply the time-variant recurrent rule individually for each channel.

Thus, we denote the entire input sequence by
\hat{x}:=(\hat{x}_{1},\cdots,\hat{x}_{L})\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
where
\hat{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
. The discrete matrices
\mathbf{B}_{i},\mathbf{C}_{i},
and
\Delta_{i}
are defined as follows:

(8)

\mathbf{B}_{i}=S_{B}(\hat{x}_{i}),\quad\mathbf{C}_{i}=S_{C}(\hat{x}_{i}),\quad\Delta_{i}=\text{Softplus}(S_{\Delta}(\hat{x}_{i})).

By defining

(9)

\displaystyle f_{A}(\Delta_{i},\mathbf{A})

\displaystyle=\exp(\Delta_{i}\mathbf{A}),

\displaystyle f_{B}(\Delta_{i},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{i})

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{A}})^{-1}(\exp(\Delta_{i}{\mathbf{A}})-\mathbf{I})\cdot\Delta_{i}\mathbf{B}_{i},

where
f_{A},f_{B}
represents the discretization rule,
S_{B},S_{C},S_{\Delta}
are linear projection layers, and the Softplus is an elementwise function that is a smooth approximation of ReLU. Then we get:

(10)

\overline{\mathbf{A}}_{i}=f_{A}(\Delta_{i},\mathbf{A}),\quad\overline{\mathbf{B}}_{i}=f_{B}(\Delta_{i},\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B}_{i}).

4.
Mamba Hawkes Process

The primary challenge in modeling event sequence data revolves around several aspects. Firstly, the discontinuous nature of TPPs requires redefining state transitions to dynamically adapt to irregular event arrivals, which involves designing input-dependent gating mechanisms to reset or propagate hidden states only at event timestamps rather than fixed intervals, and poses a non-trivial adaptation problem in standard SSMs.
Secondly, there is the question of how to efficiently handle extended event sequences while effectively capturing the complex and nonlinear evolving dynamics of the intensity function that drives the sequence. Additionally, it is crucial to capture long-range event transition dependencies within the sequence, particularly considering the self-exciting properties inherent to a large class of point processes that may exhibit interactions between events located far apart in the temporal domain. To address these challenges, we propose the Mamba Hawkes Process (MHP) model.

Denote the sequence
\mathcal{S}=\{(t_{j},k_{j})\}_{j=1}^{L}
as the temporal event sequence of
L
events, where each events has type
k_{j}\in\{1,2,...,K\}
with a total number of
K
types. Each pair
(t_{j},k_{j})
corresponds to an event of type
k_{j}
occurring at time
t_{j}
. Different from (
8
), let
\Delta_{j}=t_{j}-t_{j-1}
represent the temporal differences, with
\Delta_{1}=t_{1}
for convention, hence the temporal sequence can be represented by

\mathbf{\Delta}=(\Delta_{1},\Delta_{2},...,\Delta_{L})
.

4.1.
Model Formulation

Let
W^{e}
be the event embedding matrix with dimensions
D\times K
, where the
k
-th column of
W^{e}
is a
D
-dimensional embedding for event type
k
. For any event of type
k_{j}
, let
\mathbf{k}_{j}
be its one-hot encoding, then the event embedding at corresponding timestamp
t_{j}
is calculated as
x_{t_{j}}=\mathbf{k}_{i}(W^{e})^{T}
. Thus, for the event sequence
\mathcal{S}
, the embedding is computed by

(11)

(x_{t_{1}},x_{t_{2}},...,x_{t_{L}})=(\mathbf{k}_{1},\mathbf{k}_{2},...,\mathbf{k}_{L})(W^{e})^{T}.

The key idea is to enable the Mamba model to handle the temporal dependency over both the event types and the timestamps from past events. Equivalent to equation (
5
), we have discrete recurrence:

(12)

h(t+\Delta)=\overline{\mathbf{A}}h(t)+\overline{\mathbf{B}}x(t),

where the hidden state at
(t+\Delta)
is built on previous state at
t
. In the Mamba architecture, the parameters
(\Delta,\mathbf{B},\mathbf{C})
are time-dependent and are obtained by linear projection from the input, thus resulting in a time-variant SSM. To model the event sequence
\mathcal{S}
, we adopt the time-variant version of equation (
12
) and construct Mamba Hawkes Process as Algorithm
1
. For the event of type
k_{j}
occurring at time
t_{j}
, the event embedding
x_{t_{j}}
is fed as input into the state space model, and temporal difference between this event and its previous event
\Delta_{j}=t_{j}-t_{j-1}
is assigned as the value of
\Delta
.

The temporal sequence

\mathbf{h}

In particular, We define

\mathbf{B}(t_{j})=\text{Linear}_{\mathbf{B}}(x_{t_{j}}),\mathbf{C}(t_{j})=\text{Linear}_{\mathbf{C}}(x_{t_{j}}),

which are obtained by a linear transformation of the vector
x_{t_{j}}
. We have the transition formulas for the Mamba Hawkes process:

(13)

\displaystyle h_{t_{j}}

\displaystyle=\overline{\mathbf{A}}(t_{j})h_{t_{j-1}}+\overline{\mathbf{B}}(t_{j})x_{t_{j}},

\displaystyle y_{t_{j}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{C}(t_{j})h_{t_{j}},

where

(14)

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}(t_{j})

\displaystyle=\exp(\Delta_{j}\mathbf{A}),

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{B}}(t_{j})

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{j}\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\exp(\Delta_{j}\mathbf{A})-\mathbf{I})\cdot\Delta_{j}\mathbf{B}(t_{j}),

are the temporal-dependent coefficients. As shown in equation (
13
), the hidden state
h_{t_{j-1}}
represents the memory of the influence from the timings and the types of past events up to timestamp
t_{j-1}
. The time-variant SSM updates
h_{t_{j-1}}
to
h_{t_{j}}
by taking into account the effect of the current event
(t_{j},k_{j})
in a selective way. Specifically, the model modulate the recurrent dynamics based on temporal difference
\Delta_{j}
and current input
x_{t_{j-1}}
, determining how much to forget or persist the current state
h_{t_{j-1}}
and whether to focus or ignore the current input.

Once the hidden representation of the event sequence is generated, we can further interpolate the continuous intensity function. Given
h_{t_{j}}
, the conditional intensity function of Neural Hawkes process is given by

(15)

\lambda(t)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\lambda_{k}(t),

where
\lambda_{k}
is the conditional intensity function of the
k
-th event, and

(16)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}

\displaystyle=f_{k}(\alpha_{k}(t-t_{j})+\mathbf{w}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{h}(t_{j})+b_{k}),

\displaystyle f_{k}(x)

\displaystyle=\beta_{k}\log\left(1+\exp\left(x/\beta_{k}\right)\right),

where
t
is defined on interval
t\in\left[t_{j},t_{j+1}\right)
, and
f_{k}(x)
is the Softplus function.
The Softplus function is chosen for the intensity function in Hawkes process models for two primary reasons. First, it ensures that the intensity remains non-negative. Second, it is smooth and differentiable everywhere, which facilitates training with gradient-based optimization.
The first term
\alpha_{k}(t-t_{j})
in equation (
16
) models the temporal decaying effect of past events of type
k
in Hawkes process. Specifically,
t-t_{j}
represents the time elapsed since timestamp
t_{j}
, and
\alpha_{k}
controls how quickly the influence of past events of type
k
diminishes over time.
The second term
\mathbf{w}_{k}^{T}\mathbf{h}(t_{j})
represents the cumulative influence of all past events of type
k
up until timestamp
t
, reflecting the self-modulating feature of the Hawkes process.
The third term
b_{k}
indicates the baseline level of intensity for the event type
k
, independent of the history and elapsed time.

4.2.
Model Training

Given the conditional intensity function, the log-likelihood
\mathcal{\ell}
of the event sequence
\mathcal{S}^{i}
can be derived based on equation (
2
), and is expressed as

(17)

\mathcal{\ell}(\mathcal{S}^{i})=\sum_{i=1}^{L}\log\lambda\left(t_{i}\right)-\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{L}}\lambda(t)dt.

Based on this, we can further obtain the log-likelihood for a collection of sequences
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{\{S}^{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
, where
\mathcal{S}^{i}=\left((t_{j}^{i},k_{j}^{i})\right)_{j=1}^{L_{i}}
, which is given by

(18)

\mathcal{\ell(\mathcal{S})}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{\ell}(\mathcal{S}^{i}).

The model parameters are learned by maximizing the log-likelihood across all sequences
\mathcal{\ell(\mathcal{S})}
. This optimization problem can be efficiently solved by stochastic gradient type algorithms like ADAM
(
Kingma and Ba, 2014
)
,
except one challenge in computing
\int_{t_{1}}^{t_{L}}\lambda(t)dt
, the log-likelihood for the non-event part. Since it involves the softplus function, there is no closed-form computation for this integral, and we adopt a similar approximation computing method
(
Zuo et al., 2020
)
for the non-event log-likelihood by using Monte Carlo integration.

To facilitate the prediction of the next event’s type and timestamp, we train two linear layers that operate on the hidden state representation
\mathbf{h}(t_{j})\in\mathbb{R}^{D}
, which encodes historical information up to the
j
-th event. Specifically, the layer for predicting next event type is denoted as
\mathbf{P}^{e}\in\mathbb{R}^{K\times D}
, and the layer for predicting next event timestamp is denoted as
\mathbf{P}^{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{1\times D}
. Building on this, the next event type
\hat{k}_{j+1}
and the timestamp
\hat{t}_{j+1}
can be predicted as follows:

(19)

\displaystyle\hat{k}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\text{argmax}(\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{P}^{e}\mathbf{h}(t_{j})),

\displaystyle\hat{t}_{j+1}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{P}^{t}\mathbf{h}(t_{j}).

Given the previously defined collection of sequences
\mathbf{S}
, we define two primary loss function to learn
\mathbf{P}^{e}
and
\mathbf{P}^{t}
, specified by

(20)

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{event}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}-\log(\text{Softmax}(P^{e}\mathbf{h}(t_{j}))_{k_{j+1}}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{time}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{n-1}((t_{j+1}-t_{j})-(\hat{t}_{j+1}-\hat{t}_{j}))^{2},

where
{t}_{j}
is the true timestamp of event
j
.
\mathcal{L}_{event}
is the cross-entropy loss that measures the accuracy of the event type prediction, and
\mathcal{L}_{time}
is the MSE loss that measures the accuracy of the time prediction. Finally, the joint training loss can then be defined as

(21)

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{S})=-\mathcal{\ell}(\mathcal{S})+\beta\mathcal{L}_{event}(\mathcal{S})+\gamma\mathcal{L}_{time}(\mathcal{S}),

where
\beta,\gamma
are hyperparameters to control the range of event and time losses.

4.3.
Model Properties

4.3.1.
Relations to Hawkes processes

We show MHP contains classical multi-exponential Hawkes processes as a special case.

Proposition 0

(Recovery of multi-exponential Hawkes)
.

Consider a
R
-dimensional state with
A=\mathrm{diag}(-\omega_{1},\dots,-\omega_{R})
,
\omega_{r}>0
and
B\in\mathbb{R}^{R\times K}
as a learnable matrix in (
14
). Let
x(t)
be
\sum_{j=1}^{n}\delta(t-t_{j})\,\mathbf{k}_{j}
where
\mathbf{k}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
is the one-hot event type vector encoding type

5.
Adaptive Mamba Hawkes Process

5.1.
Time-Scaling Mechanism

While the foundational MHP framework provides significant advantages, our analysis revealed an important limitation: conventional temporal discretization assumes uniform decay rates across event types. This contradicts empirical evidence showing varying temporal dynamics—financial transactions exhibit persistent liquidity impacts while social media interactions demonstrate rapid decay. To address this, we developed the time-scaling mechanism, which introduces adaptive temporal processing.

Time-Scaling design is based on the recognition that different time intervals within a temporal point process should be weighted distinctly. For example, the weights should be influenced by the type of events, which is natural in real temporal processes such as earthquakes and financial orderings, where high-magnitude earthquakes or large-volume orders should be given special attention, as they exert considerably larger impact on the fitting of point processes. We also propose that these weights be determined not only by the type of event at the current time
t_{j}
, but also by the historical experience observed by the model, which dictates whether the weights should be amplified or attenuated. Therefore, we designed the Time-Scaling mechanism as follows:

(23)

\displaystyle\Delta_{j}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\Delta_{j}\cdot\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{L}_{j}),

\displaystyle\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{L}_{j})

\displaystyle=\text{Softplus}(W_{l}^{T}l_{j}+b_{l}),

\displaystyle l_{j}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{GRU}(\mathbf{x}_{t_{j}},l_{j-1}),

where
\Delta_{j}^{\prime}

\mathbf{s}(\mathbf{L}_{j})
is the scaling factor, which is calculated based on the hidden layer
L_{j}

(24)

\displaystyle r_{j}

\displaystyle=\sigma(W_{r}x_{t_{j}}+U_{r}l_{j-1}+b_{r}),

\displaystyle z_{j}

\displaystyle\tilde{l}_{j}

\displaystyle=\tanh(W_{l}x_{t_{j}}+U_{l}(r_{j}\odot l_{j-1})+b_{l}),

\displaystyle l_{j}

\displaystyle=(1-z_{j})\odot l_{j-1}+z_{j}\odot\tilde{l}_{j},

where

Dual-Channel State Transition

Dual-Channel modifies
\mathbf{B}(t_{j})
to improve the selection mechanism by involving more information in data-dependent state transitions. This is realized by concatenating embedded events and time intervals:

(25)

\displaystyle\widetilde{\mathbf{B}}(t_{j})

7.
Conclusion

In this work, we proposed the Mamba Hawkes Process (MHP), a novel model that integrates the selective state space framework of Mamba with temporal point processes to efficiently capture long-range dependencies in asynchronous event sequences with linear complexity. Theoretically, MHP generalizes classical Hawkes processes and certain neural point processes, providing a unified modeling perspective. We further introduced A-MHP, which enhances adaptability through a Time-Scaling Mechanism and Dual-Channel State Transition, enabling more nuanced handling of heterogeneous event dynamics. Extensive experiments across synthetic and real-world datasets demonstrate that MHP and A-MHP consistently outperform state-of-the-art baselines in both event type and time prediction, with particularly strong performance on long sequences. This work establishes a foundational integration of state space models into TPPs and opens avenues for future extensions, such as spatiotemporal modeling, causal interpretability, and resource-efficient deployment.

Remark 1
.

If one uses ZOH with piecewise constant
x(t)\equiv\tilde{x}_{i}
on
[t_{i},t_{i+1})
, the discrete update carries a factor
A^{-1}(e^{A\Delta_{i}}-I)
, which depends on
\Delta_{i}
. Unrolling through time,
h_{r}(t)
becomes a weighted sum of exponentials with interval-dependent coefficients, not the fixed amplitudes required by Hawkes. In practice, MHP can be implemented event-driven with instantaneous jumps (impulses) plus continuous decay between events.

When using a monotone link
f_{k}
(e.g., Softplus) instead of the linear readout, the model becomes a neural Hawkes process generalization. Proposition
1
shows the reduction of MHP to classical Hawkes process by selecting a linear, nonnegative readout; with Softplus, one obtains a flexible nonlinearity around the linear regime.

Proof.

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{A}}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=e^{A\Delta_{i}}=e^{-\omega\Delta_{i}}I.

\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{B}}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=(\Delta_{i}\mathbf{A})^{-1}(\exp(\Delta_{i}\mathbf{A})-\mathbf{I})\cdot\Delta_{i}\mathbf{B}(t_{i})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{-\omega}(e^{-\omega\Delta_{i}}-1)\mathbf{B}(t_{i})

Remark 2

RMTPP
(

)
uses a nonlinear discrete recurrence
h_{i}=\tanh(W_{h}h_{i-1}+W_{x}x_{i}+W_{\Delta}\Delta_{i}+b)
and a softplus intensity head of the form
\lambda_{k}(t)=\mathrm{Softplus}(\alpha_{k}(t-t_{i})+w_{k}^{\top}h_{i}+b_{k})
