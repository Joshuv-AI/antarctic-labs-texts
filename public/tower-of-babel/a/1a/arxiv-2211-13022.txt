Molecular Berry curvatures and the adiabatic response tensors
Raffaele Resta

Abstract
Adiabatic transport in a many-electron system is expressed in terms of the appropriate Berry curvature, owing to the Niu-Thouless theory [J. Phys A 17 , 2453 (1984)]; the main equation is very compact and very general. I address here three paradigmatic adiabatic response tensors—-the atomic polar tensor, the atomic axial tensor, and the rotational g g factor—and I show that, for all of them, the known formulas do not need an independent proof. They are just case studies of the general expression, for different choices the curvature’s two arguments.



I
Introduction

The quantity which today is commonly addressed as a Berry curvature was introduced in a milestone 1984 paper by Michael Berry;
Berry84
at about the same time, Niu and Thouless (NT) established the theory of adiabatic charge transport in a many-electron system in terms of the same quantity.
Niu84

The curvature is a quasi-static quantity, in the sense that its definition requires solely the instantaneous ground eigenstate; yet it encodes the lowest-order effect of the excited states on the adiabatic evolution.

While the notion of Berry curvature has permeated several areas of condensed matter physics,
rap_a20
;
Xiao10
;
Vanderbilt
its role in molecular physics is not generally appreciated. I am going to show here that the known expressions for some molecular adiabatic response tensors are indeed paradigmatic manifestations of the Berry curvature. When addressed by means of the NT theory of adiabatic evolution, such tensors become simple case studies. Actual derivation of the known formulas requires no independent proof: it is enough to plug the definition of each tensor into the general NT expression for the adiabatic evolution, based on the appropriate Berry curvature. I am going to show how this works in three cases: the atomic polar tensor, the atomic axial tensor, and the gyromagnetic factor. All three of these tensors are a measure of how much electronic charge is “dragged” by the nuclear adiabatic motion, and this is precisely what the Berry curvature allows casting in a very compact formalism. It is also worth remarking that, when the nuclear motion is harmonic at frequency
\omega
, the NT time-evolution of the many-electron system is exact to linear order in the adiabaticity parameter
\omega
.

In Sec.
II
I give the general definition of a Berry curvature and I detail its most basic features. In Sec.
III
I consider a generic time-independent operator and the evolution of its expectation value over a given state when such state is adiabatically varied in time: the NT theory—rooted in a Berry curvature—provides a universal formula for such evolution. In Sec.
IV
I work out in detail the three case studies mentioned above. A Conclusions Section is not contemplated; in the Appendix I deal with the case where the ground eigenstate is a Slater determinant of doubly occupied one-electron orbitals.

II
The Berry curvature: fundamentals

Let
\hat{H}
be a time-independent Hamiltonian and let
|\Psi_{n}\rangle
its eigenstates with eigenvalues
E_{n}
;
\hat{H}
depends on a couple of generic real parameters
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
: the eigenstates and eigenvalues are parameter-dependent as well. We assume the ground state to be nondegenerate for all
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
.
The Berry curvature is by definition
rap_a20
;
Xiao10
;
Vanderbilt

\displaystyle\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle i(\langle\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi_{0}|\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\Psi_{0}\rangle-\langle\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\Psi_{0}|\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi_{0}\rangle)

(1)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2\,\mbox{Im }\langle\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi_{0}|\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\Psi_{0}\rangle.

The parameters may have various physical interpretations and different dimensions; the curvature has the inverse dimensions of the product
\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2}
. For a macroscopic homogeneous system
\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
is an extensive quantity.

The curvature admits a sum-over-states formula, first displayed in the original Berry’s paper;
Berry84

\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=-2\mbox{Im}\sum_{n\neq 0}\frac{\langle\Psi_{0}|\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\hat{H}|\Psi_{n}\rangle\langle\Psi_{n}|\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\hat{H}|\Psi_{0}\rangle}{(E_{0}-E_{n})^{2}}.

(2)

The formula perspicuously shows that the Berry curvature encodes—to lowest order—the effect of the excited states on the ground state when the quantum system is transported in the parameter space. Eq. (
2
)
has also the virtue of showing that the curvature becomes ill defined whenever the ground state becomes degenerate with the first excited state.

In the special case where
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
is the Slater determinant of doubly occupied single-particle orbitals—either Hartree-Fock or Kohn-Sham—
\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
becomes a sum of single-particle curvatures: the expression is presented in Appendix
A
.

The curvature is a geometrical quantity defined by the evolution of the ground-state projector

{\hat{\rho}}=|\Psi_{0}\rangle\langle\Psi_{0}|

(3)

in the parameter space, and can equivalently be expressed directly in terms of
{\hat{\rho}}
:

\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=-i\,\mbox{Tr }\{{\hat{\rho}}\,[\,\partial_{\lambda_{1}}{\hat{\rho}},\partial_{\lambda_{2}}{\hat{\rho}}\,]\},

(4)

where “Tr” indicates the trace over the Hilbert space. The form of Eq. (
4
) explicitly displays the gauge-invariance of
\Omega
.
A gauge-transformation modifies
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
by a phase factor, ergo leaves
{\hat{\rho}}
and the curvature unchanged.

An important caveat is in order: the curvature is
not
gauge-invariant when the parameter
\lambda_{1}
is identified with a macroscopic magnetic field
{\bf B}
, which is not a “normal” parameter on which the Hamiltonian explicitly depends. When in Eq. (
4
)
\lambda_{1}
is identified with
{\bf B}
, the relevant entry is
\partial_{{\bf B}}{\hat{\rho}}
, which actually is the functional derivative of
{\hat{\rho}}
with respect to the vector potential
{\bf A}({\bf r})
, times the derivative of
{\bf A}({\bf r})
with respect to
{\bf B}
: the latter factor is manifestly gauge-dependent.

Practical calculations are performed by projecting the Hamiltonian on a finite basis set, and are notoriously plagued by gauge-dependence problems. I am not discussing the issue in this work, ideally assuming a complete Hilbert space.

III
Adiabatic transport

Let us suppose here that
\hat{H_{t}}
is instead a time-dependent Hamiltonian, and
|\Psi\rangle
one of its solutions:

\hat{H}_{t}|\Psi\rangle=i\hbar|\dot{\Psi}\rangle;

(5)

the time-dependent energy is

E(t)=\langle\Psi|\,\hat{H}_{t}\,|\Psi\rangle.

(6)

Let us also assume that a generic observable
\hat{O}
can be written as the derivative of the Hamiltonian with respect to some parameter
\lambda_{1}
:

\hat{O}=\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\hat{H}_{t},\qquad\mbox{time independent}.

(7)

The
\lambda_{1}
-derivative of Eq. (
6
) yields

\partial_{\lambda_{1}}E(t)=\langle\Psi|\,\hat{O}\,|\Psi\rangle+i\hbar\,(\,\langle\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi|\dot{\Psi}\rangle-\langle\dot{\Psi}|\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi\rangle\,).

(8)

Next suppose that the time-dependence of
\hat{H_{t}}
owes to the time dependence of a parameter
\lambda_{2}
entering it: a nuclear coordinate in the cases of interest here. Upon replacing the exact time-dependent energy and state vector in Eq. (
8
) with the instantaneous ground-state eigenvalue and eigenvector one gets the adiabatic evolution of the ground-state observable in terms of the Berry curvature as

\langle O(t)\rangle=\langle\Psi_{0}|\,\hat{O}\,|\Psi_{0}\rangle+\hbar\,\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})\,\dot{\lambda}_{2}(t),

(9)

where
|\Psi_{0}\rangle
and the curvature depend implicitly on time.
To the best of the author’s knowledge, a similar formulation first appeared in the milestone NT paper.
Niu84
;
Xiao10
In the following, for the sake of simplicity, I am going to call Eq. (
9
) the “NT formula”.

When the Hamiltonian is time-reversal (T) invariant the ground state can be taken as real in the Schrödinger representation; its derivative with respect to a nuclear coordinate
\lambda_{2}
is real as well. The observables of interest in the following are purely imaginary. Therefore the first term in Eq. (
9
) vanishes and the curvature, Eq. (
1
), admits a simplified expression; the NT formula becomes in this special case

\langle O(t)\rangle=2i\hbar\,\langle\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi_{0}|\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\Psi_{0}\rangle\,\dot{\lambda}_{2}(t),\qquad\mbox{T-invariant}.

(10)

In view of the rest of this paper, it is important to notice that Eq. (
10
) becomes exact in the adiabatic limit, i.e.. when
\dot{\lambda}_{2}
is infinitesimal. Ergo the expression

\frac{\partial\langle O(t)\rangle}{\partial\dot{\lambda}_{2}}=2i\hbar\,\langle\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\Psi_{0}|\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\Psi_{0}\rangle=\hbar\,\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})

(11)

is also exact.

IV
The adiabatic response tensors

IV.1
The electronic Hamiltonian

Within the Born-Oppenheimer (also called Born-Huang) approximation one considers a bounded system of
N
interacting electrons. The many-body Hamiltonian is

\hat{H}=\frac{1}{2m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left({\bf p}_{i}+\frac{e}{c}{\bf A}({\bf r}_{i})\right)^{2}+\hat{V},

(12)

where the potential
\hat{V}
comprises one-body and two-body terms; it is a multiplicative operator in the Schrödinger representation, and is a function of the instantaneous nuclear positions
\{{\bf R}_{s}\}
. We assume a singlet ground state and we neglect irrelevant spin variables. The Hamiltonian is T-invariant at
{\bf A}=0
.

It is also expedient to define the two operators:

\hat{\bf v}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf p}_{i},\qquad\hat{{\bf L}}=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{N}{\bf r}_{i}\times{\bf p}_{i}:

(13)

velocity and angular momentum of the many-body system, respectively (at
{\bf A}=0
). In the following of this work Greek subscripts are Cartesian indices, and the sum over repeated indices is implicitly understood.

IV.2
Atomic polar tensor

When a given ion is transported with velocity
{\bf v}_{s}=\dot{\bf R}_{s}
, the induced electrical current is

{\bf J}_{s}=eZ_{s}{\bf v}_{s}-e\langle{\bf v}(t)\rangle;

(14)

the dimensionless atomic polar tensor (Born charge in solids and liquids) is then defined as

Z^{*}_{s,\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{e}\partial_{v_{s\beta}}J_{s\alpha}=Z_{s}\delta_{\alpha\beta}-\partial_{v_{s\beta}}\langle{v}_{\alpha}(t)\rangle.

(15)

Once this definition adopted, its microscopic expression immediately follows. In the NT formula it is enough to identify
\lambda_{1}
with
A_{\alpha}
and
\lambda_{2}
with
R_{s\beta}
; the velocity is

\hat{{\bf v}}=\frac{c}{e}\partial_{{\bf A}}\hat{H}.

(16)

The atomic polar tensor is then

Z^{*}_{s,\alpha\beta}=Z_{s}\delta_{\alpha\beta}+\frac{\hbar c}{e}\Omega(A_{\alpha},R_{s\beta})..

(17)

This equation is well known in molecular physics since the 1980s.
Stephens85
;
Stephens87
;
Buckingham87
;
Barron

What I deem new here is the very concise derivation, as well as the realization that Eq. (
17
) is arguably the most straightforward case study of the very general NT formula, Eq. (
11
). Eq. (
17
) is gauge-invariant and invariant by translation of the coordinate origin.

IV.3
Atomic axial tensor

Let us suppose again that the nuclei are transported with velocities
{\bf v}_{s}
. The magnetic moment of the molecule is, in Gaussian units,

{\bf m}=\frac{e}{2c}\left(\sum_{s}Z_{s}{\bf R}_{s}\times{\bf v}_{s}-\langle{{\bf L}}(t)\rangle\right).

(18)

A common coordinate origin must be set for the two terms in parenthesis, and—for an arbitrary velocity distribution—the value of
{\bf m}
depends on the coordinate origin.

The atomic axial tensor of nucleus
s
is defined as

M_{s,\alpha\beta}=\frac{\partial m_{\beta}}{\partial v_{s\alpha}}=\frac{e}{2c}[\,\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}Z_{s}R_{s\gamma}-\partial_{v_{s\alpha}}\langle{L}_{\beta}(t)\rangle\,].

(19)

Next, in order to exploit the NT formula, Eq. (
11
), all what is needed is recognizing the operator
\hat{L}_{\beta}
as the Hamiltonian derivative with respect to some parameter. By adopting the central gauge centered on the momentum origin the parameter is clearly the
{\bf B}
field:

\partial_{B_{\beta}}\hat{H}=\frac{e}{2c}\hat{L}_{\beta}.

(20)

Upon identifying
\lambda_{1}
with
B_{\beta}
and
\lambda_{2}
with
R_{s\alpha}
, the NT formula of Eq. (
11
) immediately yields the well known expression:
Stephens85
;
Stephens87
;
Buckingham87
;
Barron

\displaystyle M_{s,\alpha\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{2c}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}Z_{s}R_{s\gamma}+\hbar\,\Omega(B_{\beta},R_{s\alpha})

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{e}{2c}\varepsilon_{\alpha\beta\gamma}Z_{s}R_{s\gamma}+2i\hbar\langle\partial_{B_{\beta}}\Psi_{0}|\partial_{R_{s\alpha}}\Psi_{0}\rangle.

In the present straightforward derivation it appears as one of the numerous manifestations of the Berry curvature in adiabatic phenomena.

In order to arrive at the usual compact notation for the Berry curvature
rap_a20
;
Xiao10
;
Vanderbilt

one casts the antisymmetric tensor
M_{s,\alpha\beta}
into its equivalent vector form

\tilde{M}_{s\gamma}=\frac{1}{2}\varepsilon_{\gamma\alpha\beta}M_{s,\alpha\beta}:

(22)

\tilde{{\bf M}}_{s}=\frac{e}{2c}Z_{s}{\bf R}_{s}+i\hbar\,\langle\partial_{{\bf B}}\Psi_{0}|\times|\partial_{{\bf R}_{s}}\Psi_{0}\rangle.

(23)

The magnetic moment linearly induced by the displacement of the
s
-th nucleus is then

{\bf m}_{s}=\tilde{{\bf M}}_{s}\times{\bf v}_{s}.

(24)

Eqs. (
21
) and (
23
) are not invariant by translation of the coordinate origin; it was early recognized that such ambiguity does not affect the measurable properties. They are also gauge-dependent; it is nonetheless known how to express the physical observables in gauge-invariant form.
Stephens87

IV.4
Rotational
\bf g
factor

In order to simplify the algebra, we fix here the rotation axis, taken as the
z
-axis. Suppose a molecule is rigidly rotating around its center of mass; the rotational
g
factor is defined as the ratio between the total magnetic moment of the molecule and its mechanical angular moment, expressed in dimensionless units.

If
\dot{\vartheta}
is the angular velocity, the mechanical moment is

{\cal M}_{z}=\sum_{s}M_{s}R_{s}^{2}\dot{\vartheta},

(25)

where
R_{s}
is the distance of the
s
-th nucleus from the axis and
M_{s}
is its mass. The magnetic moment, analogously to Eq. (
18
), is

m_{z}=\frac{e}{2c}\left(\sum_{s}Z_{s}R^{2}_{s}\dot{\vartheta}-\langle L_{z}(t)\rangle\right).

(26)

The
g
factor can be cast as

g=\frac{1}{\mu_{\rm B}}\frac{m_{z}}{{\cal M}_{z}}=\frac{1+\sigma}{\mu_{\rm B}}\;\frac{\sum_{s}Z_{s}R^{2}_{s}}{\sum_{s}m_{s}R^{2}_{s}},

(27)

where
\mu_{\rm B}
is the Bohr magneton

\mu_{\rm B}=\frac{e\hbar}{2mc}.

(28)

The magnetic screening
\sigma
measures how much the electrons are “left behind” by the rotation of the nuclei. For
\sigma=0
the electrons do not contribute and the nuclei rotate as naked, while for
\sigma=-1
the electronic screening is complete and the molecule rotates as an uncharged rigid body.

From Eq. (
26
) the screening factor is

\sigma=-\frac{\partial_{\dot{\theta}}\langle L_{z}(t)\rangle}{\sum_{s}Z_{s}R^{2}_{s}}.

(29)

By adopting a gauge centered on the rotation axis, and exploiting Eq. (
20
), the NT formula yields

\sigma=-\frac{2\hbar c}{e}\frac{\Omega(B_{z},\vartheta)}{\sum_{s}Z_{s}R^{2}_{s}},

(30)

where the Berry curvature
\Omega(B_{z},\vartheta)
is indeed a constant (evaluated at
{\bf B}=0
, and
\vartheta
-independent).

To the best of the author’s knowledge,
\sigma
was first recognized as a Berry curvature in Ref.
Ceresoli02
; the form of Eq. (
30
) owes to Ref.
Stengel18
. Eq. (
30
) can be cast into a more familiar sum over states by means of Eq. (
2
).

Appendix A
Independent-electron Berry curvature

In the mean-field case (either Hartree-Fock or Kohn-Sham) the singlet ground state is a Slater determinant of doubly occupied single-particle orbitals
|\psi_{j}\rangle
with eigenvalues
\epsilon_{j}
. We assume that the parameters do not affect the two body terms in the Hamiltonian, ergo the
\lambda
-derivatives entering Eq. (
2
) are one-body operators:

\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\hat{H}=\sum_{i=1}^{N}O_{1}({\bf r}_{i}),

(31)

and analogously for
\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\hat{H}
. By the Slater-Condon rules the matrix elements in Eq. (
2
) are converted into one-body elements of
O_{1}
and
O_{2}
between occupied and unoccupied orbitals; the energy differences are easily expressed as well, and Eq. (
2
) becomes

\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=2\,i\hskip-14.22636pt\sum_{\stackrel{{\scriptstyle j=\mbox{occupied}}}{{j^{\prime}=\mbox{unoccupied}}}}\hskip-14.22636pt\frac{\langle\psi_{j}|O_{1}|\psi_{j^{\prime}}\rangle\langle\psi_{j^{\prime}}|O_{2}|\psi_{j}\rangle}{(\epsilon_{j}-\epsilon_{j^{\prime}})^{2}}+\mbox{cc},

(32)

where the factor of two accounts for spin, and “cc’ stays for complex conjugate. Straightforward manipulations yield the independent-electron version of Eq. (
1
) as

\Omega(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=2\,\tilde{\Omega}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}),

(33)

\tilde{\Omega}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=-2\,\mbox{Im }\sum_{j}\theta(\mu-\epsilon_{j})(\,\langle\partial_{\lambda_{1}}\psi_{j}|\partial_{\lambda_{2}}\psi_{j}\rangle\,),

(34)

where
\mu
is the Fermi level.

\tilde{\Omega}
can be expressed in terms of the one-body ground-state projector

P=\sum_{j}\theta(\mu-\epsilon_{j})|\psi_{j}\rangle\langle\psi_{j}|:

(35)

\tilde{\Omega}(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})=-i\,\mbox{Tr }\{P\,[\,\partial_{\lambda_{1}}P,\partial_{\lambda_{2}}P\,]\}.

(36)

This shows that
\tilde{\Omega}
enjoys a generalized form of gauge invariance: it is in fact invariant by any unitary mixing of the occupied orbitals between themselves.
