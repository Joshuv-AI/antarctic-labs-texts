Abstract
. Let G G be one of the finite general linear, unitary, symplectic or orthogonal groups over finite fields of odd order. We find the cardinality of the fibers of the square map at a given arbitrary element of G G . Using this we find the number of real conjugacy classes of G G . This is primarily achieved by leveraging a recent solution to Brauer’s problem 14.

1.
Introduction

In recent decades, there has been increasing interest and progress in the theory of word maps in groups, driven by various motivations and applications.
By a
word
, we refer to an element
\omega
in the free group on
t
generators, denoted
\mathscr{F}_{t}
.
For any group
G
and a word
\omega
, there exists a map
\widetilde{\omega}:G^{t}\longrightarrow G
through evaluation, which is known as
word map
.
Some of the important questions studied extensively are:
(a) analyzing the image of the word map,
(b) determining the width of the word, defined as the smallest
\ell>0
such that
\omega(G)^{\ell}=\langle\omega(G)\rangle
,
(c) investigating the fibers of the map,
(d) identifying the kernel of the map, i.e., the inverse image of the identity under the word map.
A particularly intriguing result emerges for the second question when considering finite nonabelian simple groups. Refer to
[
19
]
and
[
9
]
for further details, where the width of non-trivial words in nonabelian finite simple groups of large orders was ultimately demonstrated to be two by Larsen, Shalev, and Tiep.
Refer to the following articles, namely
[
17
]
,
[
8
]
,
[
6
]
, and
[
18
]
, to explore the nature of results and the state of available methodologies in the area of word maps.
Even more captivating results exist for certain specific words.
For instance, if
\omega
represents the commutator word
[x_{1},x_{2}]
, it turns out that
\omega(G)=G
for a nonabelian finite simple group
G
, meaning that every element of a nonabelian finite simple group is a commutator.
This significant achievement was established by Liebeck, O’Brien, Shalev, and Tiep
[
10
]
, thereby resolving a long-standing conjecture posed by Ore in 1951
[
13
]
.
It is worth noting that, generally, the width of a word cannot be
1
. This observation comes from examining power maps, which take the form
x\mapsto x^{M}
for some
M\geq 2
.
The focus of our investigation in this article is the square map associated with
M=2
, applied to certain finite groups of Lie type, namely the general linear group, unitary group, symplectic group, and orthogonal group over finite fields of odd order.
The analysis of the image of the power maps for these groups has been explored in the following articles:
[
7
]
(for the general linear group),
[
15
]
(for the symplectic and orthogonal groups), and
[
16
]
(for the unitary groups). The findings in these works are articulated in terms of generating functions and categorize the conjugacy classes (and elements) of distinct types, namely separable, cyclic, regular, and semisimple.
In a recent study, we determined the kernel of the general power maps within these groups, see
[
14
]
.
The main goal of this article is to determine the fiber of the square map at any element of the given group.
We revisit the groups introduced in
Section

2
. In
Section

3
, we identify the elements possessing a square root. In
Proposition

3.4
, we present the result for the general linear group, specifying the count of square roots for an arbitrary element. This result can be readily extended to the other groups discussed in the article. In
Section

4
, we employ
Proposition

3.4
to determine the number of real conjugacy classes of the finite general linear group.

2.
Preliminaries

While the groups discussed in this article are widely recognized, we briefly introduce them to establish our notation.
We work with a finite field of odd order
q
, where
q
is a power of a prime.
By
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
we denote the group of all invertible matrices with entries in the finite field
\mathbb{F}_{q}
.
The subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})
, fixing a non-degenerate Hermitian form is called a unitary group and will be denoted by
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q^{2})
.
Since all non-degenerate Hermitian forms on a
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}^{n}
are unitarily congruent to the standard form, one concludes that all the unitary groups arising are conjugate with each other inside
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q^{2})
.
The symplectic group is the subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(q)
consisting of those elements which preserve a non-degenerate alternating bilinear form on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{2n}
.
The symplectic group will be denoted by
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
.
Lastly, the orthogonal group consists of elements of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
which preserve a non-degenerate quadratic form
Q
on
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
.
There are two non-isomorphic orthogonal groups when
n
is even and one orthogonal group up to isomorphism when
n
is odd.
As is customary, we denote all them by
\mathrm{O}^{\epsilon}_{n}(q)
for
\epsilon\in\{\pm,0\}
.
We will require the parametrization of conjugacy classes and the determination of centralizer sizes for arbitrary elements in all the groups mentioned above.
These are widely known results, and we will refrain from restating them here.
Nonetheless, we provide references for interested readers.
The conjugacy classes of
{\mathrm{GL}_{n}}(q)
are established in
[
11
]
by Macdonald (also refer to
[
5
]
by Green).
For
{\mathrm{U}_{n}}(q^{2})
, the conjugacy classes are detailed in
[
3
]
by Ennola and
[
20
]
by Wall.
The conjugacy classes for
{\mathrm{Sp}}_{2n}(q)
and
\mathrm{O}^{\epsilon}_{n}(q)
are determined in
[
20
]
by Wall.
They also mention the centralizer sizes in the respective groups.
To determine the classes which have a square root we need a result of Butler.
The result for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
was determined in
[
7
]
in a more general context, although we won’t need them.
We will reference the result in
Section

3
for convenient access.
For an integer
s

q
, let

denote the multiplicative order of
q
in

.

[

Suppose that the polynomial
f(x)
of degree

has exactly

3.
Number of square roots in some finite groups of Lie type

In this section, we will identify the elements of
G_{n}(q)
that possess a square root within
G_{n}(q)
, where
G_{n}(q)
represents one of the finite general, unitary, symplectic, or orthogonal groups.
This is accomplished by analyzing the conjugacy classes of
G_{n}(q)
, as any element with a square root will have all of its conjugates having square roots as well.
By
G_{0}(q)
we mean the trivial group and
\mathscr{Z}_{G_{n}}(\alpha)
will be used to denote the centralizer of an element
\alpha
in
G_{n}(q)
.
We start with a corollary to
Lemma

2.1
.

Corollary 3.1
.

Let
f
be an irreducible polynomial of degree
d
over a finite field

\mathbb{F}_{q}
of odd order and all irreducible factors of
f(x^{2})
are of degree greater than or equal to
d
.
Then either
f(x^{2})
is irreducible or
f(x^{2})
has exactly two
distinct irreducible factors of degree
d
. If
f(x^{2})
is irreducible the matrix
\textup{diag}(C_{f},C_{f})
has a square root and otherwise

C_{f}
has a square root.

A monic irreducible polynomial
f
is called a
2
-power
polynomial if
f(x^{2})
has a monic irreducible factor of degree as
\deg f
.
The set of all
2
-power polynomials will be denoted as
\Phi_{2}
.
If
f
is a monic irreducible polynomial such that
f(x^{2})
is irreducible, we call it to be a
skew
2
-power
polynomial.
The set of all monic irreducible skew
2
-power polynomials will be denoted by
\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
.
We note down some easy observations.
Let
\mathscr{C}
be a conjugacy class of
\mathrm{G}_{n}
and
g_{1}\sim g_{2}
be two elements of
\mathscr{C}
. Then if
g_{1}
has
m
square roots, so does
g_{2}
.
Thus it is enough to find the number of square roots for a particular element in
\mathscr{C}
.
It is well known that the centralizers of two conjugate elements are conjugate to each other and thus have the same number of elements.
Let
g\in\mathrm{G}_{n}(q)
be a solution of the equation
X^{2}=\alpha
. Then
\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{G}_{n}}(\alpha)
-conjugates of
g
are also solutions of
X^{2}=\alpha
.
If
g_{1}
and
g_{2}
are conjugate to each other and both are solutions of
X^{2}=\alpha
, it follows that they are conjugate by an element of
\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{G}_{n}}(\alpha)
.
Because of the containment
\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{G}_{n}}(g)\subseteq\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{G}_{n}}(\alpha)
, these conjugates of
g
amount to
[\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{G}_{n}}(\alpha):\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{G}_{n}}(g)]
terms.

Lemma 3.2
.

[
4
, Theorem 2.3.14]

Let
x\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
has combinatorial data
(f_{i},\lambda_{f_{i}})_{i=1}^{h}

with
\lambda_{f_{i}}=\lambda_{i,1}^{l_{i},1}\lambda_{i,2}^{l_{i},2}\cdots\lambda_{i,k_{i}}^{l_{i},k_{i}}
and
\deg f_{i}=d_{i}
. Then

|\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}(x)|=q^{\gamma}\cdot\left|\prod\limits_{i=1}^{h}\left(\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k_{i}}\mathrm{GL}_{l_{i,j}}(q^{d_{i}})\right)\right|,

where
\gamma=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{h}d_{i}\left(2\sum\limits_{u<v}\lambda_{i,u}l_{i,u}l_{i,v}+\sum\limits_{j}(\lambda_{i,j}-1)l_{i,j}^{2}\right)
.

Corollary 3.3
.

(See also
[
7
, Proposition 4.6]
)
Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
be a matrix with combinatorial data consisting of polynomials
f
and partitions
\lambda_{f}=1^{m_{1}(\lambda_{f})}2^{m_{2}(\lambda_{f})}\cdots
. Then
\alpha
has a square root if and only if for all
f
,

(1)

either
f
is a
2
-power polynomial

(2)

or
f(x^{2})
is irreducible and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{f})
for all
j
.

To determine the number of square roots of a arbitrary element, we first start with the Jordan blocks of a single polynomial.
We divide this into two cases; first when
f
is a skew
2
-power polynomial and next when
f
is a
2
-power polynomial.
When
f
is a skew
2
-power polynomial,
F(x)=f(x^{2})
is an irreducible polynomial and
J_{F,b}^{2}
is conjugate to
\textup{diag}(J_{f,b},J_{f,b})
.
Note that any square root of
\textup{diag}(J_{f,b},J_{f,b})
is conjugate to
J_{F,b}
(consider them as elements in
\mathrm{GL}_{b\cdot\deg F}(q^{\deg F})
).
Hence in this case any matrix with Jordan data
(f,\lambda_{f}=(1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\cdots k^{m_{k}}))
has

|\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}(J_{f,\lambda_{f}})|/|\mathscr{Z}_{\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)}(J_{F,\lambda_{F}})|

square roots, where
\lambda_{F}
is given by
1^{m_{1}/2}2^{m_{2}/2}\cdots k^{m_{k}/2}
. Using
Lemma

3.2
, this number is

q^{\gamma(\lambda_{f})}\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}|\mathrm{GL}_{m_{j}}(q^{\deg f})|}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k}|\mathrm{GL}_{m_{j}/2}(q^{2\deg f})|},

where
\gamma(\lambda_{f})=\sum\limits_{1\leq u<v\leq k}\frac{3um_{u}m_{v}}{4}+\sum\limits_{u=2}^{k}\frac{3(u-1)m_{u}^{2}}{4}
.
Now we consider the full Jordan block of a
2
-power polynomial.
Note that this matrix has two different square roots up to conjugacy, as
f(x^{2})
has exactly two distinct irreducible monic factors of degree
\deg f
, say
F_{1}
and
F_{2}
.
In this case
J_{F_{i},b}^{2}
is conjugate to
J_{f,b}
for
i=1,2
.
Unlike the previous case, not any two square roots of
J_{f,b}
are conjugate to each other.
However, they are of the same type (see
[
5
, pp. 407]
).
Even
J_{F_{i},b}
and
J_{f,b}
are of the same type, implying that their centralizer has the same number of elements.
This implies that any matrix with Jordan data
(f,\lambda_{f}=(1^{m_{1}}2^{m_{2}}\cdots k^{m_{k}}))
has

2^{\ell(\lambda_{f})}-1

many square roots, where
\ell(\lambda_{f})
is number of
j
’s such that
m_{j}\neq 0
.
Since for an arbitrary element with combinatorial data
\{(f,\lambda_{f}):f\in\Phi\}
the size of the centralizer is the product of the size of the centralizers of the individual Jordan blocks, the following proposition follows from the above discussion.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
has a square root in
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
.
Let the combinatorial data attached to
\alpha
be given by

\displaystyle\left\{(f_{i},(1^{m_{1}(\lambda_{f_{i}})},2^{m_{2}(\lambda_{f_{i}})},\ldots)):1\leq i\leq r,f_{i}\in\Phi_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\bigcup

\displaystyle\left\{(g_{j},(1^{m_{1}(\lambda_{g_{j}})},2^{m_{2}(\lambda_{g_{j}})},\ldots)):1\leq j\leq s,g_{j}\in\Phi_{2}^{\prime}\right\}.

Then the number of square roots of
\alpha
is given by

\displaystyle\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{GL}}(\alpha,2)=\prod\limits_{i=1}^{r}\left(q^{\gamma(\lambda_{f_{i}})}\dfrac{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k_{i}}|\mathrm{GL}_{m_{j}(\lambda_{f_{i}})}(q^{\deg f_{i}})|}{\prod\limits_{j=1}^{k_{i}}|\mathrm{GL}_{m_{j}(\lambda_{f_{i}})/2}(q^{2\deg f_{i}})|}\right)\cdot\prod\limits_{j=1}^{s}\left(2^{\ell(\lambda_{g_{j}})}-1\right),

where
\gamma(\lambda_{f_{i}})=\sum\limits_{1\leq u<v\leq k_{i}}\frac{3um_{u}(\lambda_{f_{i}})m_{v}(\lambda_{f_{i}})}{4}+\sum\limits_{u=2}^{k}\frac{3(u-1)m_{u}(\lambda_{f_{i}})^{2}}{4}
and
\ell(\lambda_{g_{j}})
is the number of
t
’s such that
m_{t}(\lambda_{g_{j}})\neq 0
.

We document the outcomes analogous to
Corollary

3.3
specifically for finite unitary, symplectic, and orthogonal groups. The proofs of these results involve essential yet straightforward adjustments, which we opt to exclude here. They represent appropriately adapted versions of the results presented in
[
15
]
and
[
16
]
.
We present a few necessary definitions now. A
self-reciprocal polynomial
of degree
d
is a polynomial
f
satisfying
f(0)\neq 0
and
f(x)=f^{*}(x)=f(0)^{-1}x^{d}f(x^{-1})
.
A self-reciprocal irreducible monic polynomial
f
of degree

is called a
2^{*}
-power
polynomial if it has an irreducible self-reciprocal monic factor of degree
r
.
If
f
is a self-reciprocal irreducible monic and
f(x^{2})
is irreducible, call
f
to be a
skew
2^{*}
power polynomial
.
The set of all
2^{*}
-power (resp. skew
2^{*}
-power) polynomials will be denoted by
{\Phi_{2}^{*}}
(resp.
{\Phi_{2}^{*}}^{\prime}
).
Consider the field
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
, and the map
\sigma\colon\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
defined as
\sigma(a):=\overline{a}=a^{q}
which is an order two automorphism of the field.
This further induces an automorphism of
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t]
, the polynomial ring over
\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}
.
The image of
f\in\mathbb{F}_{q^{2}}[t]
will be denoted by
\overline{f}
.
A polynomial
f
of degree
d
is called
self-conjugate
if
\widetilde{f}=f
where
\widetilde{f}(t)=\overline{f(0)}^{-1}t^{d}\bar{f}(t^{-1})
.
A self-conjugate irreducible monic polynomial
f
of degree
d
will be called an
\widetilde{2}
-power polynomial
if
f(x^{2})
has a self-conjugate irreducible monic factor
g
of degree
d
.
The set of all irreducible
\widetilde{2}
-power polynomials will be denoted as
\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}
.
A self-conjugate monic irreducible polynomial
f
will be called a
skew
\widetilde{2}
-power polynomial
is
f(x^{2})
is irreducible.
The set of all irreducible
\widetilde{2}
-power (skew
\widetilde{2}
-power) polynomials will be denoted as
\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}
(resp.
{\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}}^{\prime}
).
The conjugacy classes of finite unitary groups, finite symplectic and orthogonal groups can be found in
[
3
]
and
[

]
.
The determination of semisimple classes for which square-root exists, has been carried out for symplectic and orthogonal groups in

For the unitary groups we know that two elements in
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q^{2})
are conjugate if and only if they are

Also for each possible Jordan form of unipotent matrices, there is exactly one conjugacy class of it in

Combining all of these together we have the following

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{U}_{n}(q^{2})
be a matrix with combinatorial data consisting of polynomials
f
and partitions
\lambda_{f}=1^{m_{1}(\lambda_{f})}2^{m_{2}(\lambda_{f})}\cdots
. Then
\alpha
has a square root in
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q^{2})
if and only if for all
f
one of the following holds

(1)

f=\widetilde{f}
and
f\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}
,

(2)

f=\widetilde{f}
,
f\in\widetilde{\Phi}_{2}^{\prime}
and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{f})
for all
j
,

(3)

f\neq\widetilde{f}
and
f\in\Phi_{2}
(and
\widetilde{f}\in\Phi_{2}
),

(4)

f\neq\widetilde{f}
,
f\in\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
(and
\widetilde{f}\in\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
) and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{f})
(and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{\widetilde{f}})
) for all
j
.

A unipotent element of
\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(q)
belongs to
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
precisely when all Jordan block of odd dimension occur with even multiplicity.
There are
2^{r}
many
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
-conjugacy class for some

(see

) with same Jordan data, each corresponding to a
type
.
Although they split into several types, using the tensor product construction (see

).
To describe the conjugacy classes of matrices of
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
with eigenvalues
\pm 1
, we need the concept of signed partition.
A

is a partition of a number

, such that the odd parts have even multiplicity and even parts have a sign associated with it.
They actually correspond to the splitting of conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{2n}(q)
inside
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
. We note that the combinatorial data corresponding to a conjugacy class of an element in

has the form

\displaystyle\left\{(f,\lambda_{f}=1^{m_{1}(\lambda_{f})},2^{m_{2}(\lambda_{f})},\ldots):f\in\Phi,f\neq x,x\pm 1\right\}

\displaystyle\bigcup

\displaystyle\left\{(h,\lambda_{h}=1^{\pm m_{1}(\lambda_{h})},2^{m_{2}(\lambda_{h})},3^{\pm m_{3}(\lambda_{h})},\ldots),h=x\pm 1\right\}.

Noting that all the unipotent conjugacy classes have a square root in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
, we have the following result concerning the elements which has a square root in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
, in view of
[

, Corollary 7.4]
.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
\alpha\in\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
be a matrix with combinatorial data consisting of polynomials
f
and partitions
\lambda_{f}
. Then
\alpha
has a square root in
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
if and only if for all
f\neq x\pm 1
one of the following holds

(1)

f=f^{*}
and
f\in{\Phi^{*}}_{2}
,

(2)

f=f^{*}
,
f\in{\Phi^{*}}_{2}^{\prime}
and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{f})
for all
j
,

(3)

f\neq{f^{*}}
and
f\in\Phi_{2}
(and
{f^{*}}\in\Phi_{2}
),

(4)

f\neq{f^{*}}
,
f\in\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
(and
{f^{*}}\in\Phi_{2}^{\prime}
) and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{f})
(and
2|m_{j}(\lambda_{{f^{*}}})
) for all

.

Furthermore, all unipotent element has a square root and no element with combinatorial data
(x+1,\lambda_{x+1})
has a square root.

A similar result holds for the orthogonal groups as well; however, we opt not to present the result here.

4.
Number of real conjugacy classes

Recall that for a group
G
, a conjugacy class
\mathscr{C}
is called real if
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}^{-1}=\{g^{-1}:g\in\mathscr{C}\}
.
It is a well-established fact that the count of real irreducible characters aligns with the tally of real conjugacy classes.
Brauer’s Problem
14
asks whether the number of characters which arise from real irreducible representations (i.e. irreducible characters with Frobenius-Schur indicator
1
) can be described group theoretically.
Murray and Sambale have affirmed this in their work
[
12
, Theorem A]
.
In particular they have shown that for a finite group
G
, the number of real conjugacy classes is given by
|s(2)|/|G|
, where
s(2)=\{(g,h)\in G^{2}:g^{2}h^{2}=1\}
.
We apply the results of the previous section to find the cardinality of
s(2)
in the case of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
, thereby finding the number of real conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
.
The same technique can be used to find the number of real conjugacy classes of the unitary group
\mathrm{U}_{n}(q)
, the symplectic group
\mathrm{Sp}_{2n}(q)
, and the orthogonal groups
\mathrm{O}^{\epsilon}_{n}(q)
for
\epsilon\in\{0,\pm\}
.
We refrain from mentioning them. Here is the result for
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
.

Theorem 4.1
.

The number of real conjugacy classes of
\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)
is given by

\displaystyle 1+\dfrac{1}{|\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)|}\left(c_{4}+{c_{2}(c_{2}-1)}+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)^{2}\\
\alpha\neq 1,\,{\alpha^{2}\neq 1}\end{subarray}}{\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{GL}}(\alpha,2)(\mathrm{R}_{\mathrm{GL}}(\alpha,2)-1)}\right),

where
c_{t}
is the number of elements of order
t
for
t=2,4
,

Proof.

We count the number of elements in
s(2)
. Note that
s(2)
is a disjoint union of the sets
\{(g,g^{-1}):g\in\mathrm{GL}_{n}(q)\}
,
\{(g,g):o(g)=4\}
,
\{(g,h):g\neq h,g^{2}=h^{2}=1\}
and
\{(g,h):g^{2}h^{2}=1,g^{2}\neq 1\neq h^{2},g^{2}\neq g^{-2}\}
.
The last set has cardinality as same as
\{(g,h)|g^{2}=h^{2}\neq 1,g^{2}\neq g^{-2}\}
. This finishes the proof.
∎
