Abstract
. Program equivalence is the fulcrum for reasoning about and proving properties of programs. For noninterference, for example, program equivalence up to the secrecy level of an observer is shown. A powerful enabler for such proofs are logical relations . Logical relations only recently were adopted for session types—but exclusively for terminating languages. This paper scales logical relations to general recursive session types . It develops a logical relation for progress-sensitive noninterference (PSNI) for intuitionistic linear logic session types (ILLST), tackling the challenges non-termination and concurrency pose, and shows that logical equivalence is sound and complete with regard to closure of weak bisimilarity under parallel composition, using a biorthogonality argument. A distinguishing feature of the logical relation is its stratification with an observation index (as opposed to a step or unfolding index), a crucial shift to make the logical relation closed under parallel composition in a concurrent setting. To demonstrate practicality of the logical relation, the paper develops an information flow control (IFC) refinement type system for ILLST, with support of secrecy-polymorphic processes, and shows that well-typed programs are self-related by the logical relation and thus enjoy PSNI. The refinement type system has been implemented in a type checker, featuring local security theories to support secrecy-polymorphic processes.



1.
Introduction

Whether two programs are
equivalent
is at the heart of many program verification problems,
such as proofs of parametricity, compiler correctness, and noninterference.
Noninterference is a security property that guarantees that a program’s confidential data cannot be leaked to a (malicious) observer.
This paper develops a
logical relation
to reason about program equivalence of
session-typed processes
,
proves soundness and completeness of the relation via a
biorthogonality
argument, and
instantiates it on
noninterference
.

Message-passing concurrency

Message-passing is a prevalent concurrency paradigm,
rooted in process calculi
(
Hoare 1985
;
Milner 1980
;
Milner 1989
)
and
adopted by practical languages such as Erlang, Go, and Rust.
A program in this setting amounts to a number of
processes
connected by
channels
,
which compute by exchanging
messages
along these channels.
The
\pi
-calculus
(
Milner 1999
;
Sangiorgi and Walker 2001
)
, among these calculi,
sets itself apart in its support of
higher-order
channels,
admitting channels themselves to be sent as messages over channels.
This feature is equally empowering as daunting because it allows the process topology to be changed dynamically.
Conceived as an untyped calculus
(
Milner 1999
)
, the
\pi
-caluclus has gradually been enriched with types
(
Sangiorgi and Walker 2001
)

to prescribe the kinds of messages that can be exchanged over a channel.
More advanced type systems for the
\pi
-caluclus additionally assert correctness properties, such as deadlock freedom and data race freedom

(
Kobayashi 1997
;
Igarashi and Kobayashi 2001
;
Igarashi and Kobayashi 2004
;
Kobayashi 2006
)
.

To prescribe not only the types of exchanged messages but also the
protocol
underlying the exchange,

session types

(
Honda 1993
;
Honda et al. 1998
)
were introduced.
Session types rely on a
linear
treatment of channels to model the state transitions induced by a protocol.
This foundation is substantiated by a Curry-Howard correspondence between linear logic and the session-typed
\pi
-calculus

(
Caires and Pfenning 2010
;
Wadler 2012
)
,
resulting in two families of session type languages:

intuitionistic linear logic session types (ILLST)

(
Caires and Pfenning 2010
;
Toninho et al. 2013
;
Caires et al. 2016
)

and
classical linear logic session types (CLLST)

(
Wadler 2012
;
Lindley and Morris 2015
;
Lindley and Morris 2016
)
.
Due to their logical foundation both ILLST and CLLST guarantee that well-typed processes
are free of data races and deadlocks (a.k.a., progress) and protocol-compliant (a.k.a., preservation).

This paper studies logical relations for ILLST-typed processes.
ILLST reject linear negation and distinguish the provider side from the client side of a channel.
Run-time configurations of processes will thus give raise to a
rooted tree
,
with a child node as the provider and a parent node the client.

Program equivalence

Today’s predominant techniques for reasoning about program equivalence of stateful programs are
Kripke logical relations (KLRs)
(
Pitts and Stark 1998
;
Ahmed et al. 2009
;
Dreyer et al. 2010
;
Neis et al. 2011
;
Hur and Dreyer 2011
;
Thamsborg and Birkedal 2011
)

and bisimulations
(
Koutavas and Wand 2006
;
Sumii and Pierce 2007
;
Støvring and Lassen 2007
;
Sangiorgi et al. 2007
)
.
KLRs tend to be used for sequential, ML-like languages,
bisimulations for process calculi, i.e., concurrent languages.
KLRs are naturally phrased by structural induction on the types of the underlying language,
bisimulations by coinduction in terms of the functional associated to the bisimulation
(
Sangiorgi 2012
)
.

Hur et al. 2012
note that KLRs and bisimulations offer / suffer from complementary advantages / limitations.
Whereas KLRs easily support higher-order features thanks to extensionality, but struggle with recursive types,
bisimulations easily support recursive features thanks to coinduction, but struggle with higher-order features.
Support of recursive types in KLRS necessitates step-indexing
(
Appel and McAllester 2001
;
Ahmed 2004
;
Ahmed 2006
)

to stratify the logical relation.
Step-indexed KLRs, however, challenge transitivity of the logical relation
(
Hur et al. 2012
)
.

This paper scales logical relations to session-typed concurrency in the presence of general recursive types,
contributing a
recursive session logical relation (RSLR)
.
In contrast to KLRs with recursive types, RSLRs use an
observation index
to stratify the logical relation.
An observation index is associated with the free channels connecting a program with the outside world and
bounds the number of messages exchanged along these channels.
Associating the index with an externally observable event, i.e., a message being sent, rather than an internal computation step,
naturally ensures
transitivity
of RSLRs and
closure
under parallel composition.
We show that RSLRs are
sound and complete
with regard to closure of weak
bisimilarity
under parallel composition,
using a
biorthogonality
argument à la
Pitts 2000
.
The inherent
duality
of session types establishes a natural connection to biorthogonality.

Noninterference

A property naturally phrased as a program equivalence is
noninterference
.
Noninterference states that an observer cannot infer any secrets from made observations.
To prove noninterference one has to show that a program
P
is related to itself,
P=_{\xi}P
,
up to the secrecy level of the observer
\xi
.
The "up to" part of the equality means that only outcomes of level
\sqsubseteq\xi
can be discriminated,
whereas outcomes of level
\not\sqsubseteq\xi
are trivially related.
It may be helpful to pause a moment to understand why noninterference is stated as a program equivalence.
After all, observations can only be made about the execution of
one
program.
What is being related are different executions of a program,
accounting for the possibility that an observer can make different observations when a program branches on a secret.
For example, consider a scenario in which, when branching on a secret bit,
the message “left” is sent along an observable channel
x
when the bit is true,
and the message “right” otherwise.
Assuming that an observer of
x
has access to the source code of the program,
seeing either “left” or “right” in this scenario is sufficient to deduce that the secret bit is true or false, resp.

The question of equivalence becomes subtle in the presence of divergence,
leading to different definitions of noninterference.
If divergence is a possible outcome,
progress-sensitive
noninterference (PSNI)
only allows equating a divergent program run with another diverging one,
whereas
progress-insensitive
noninterference (PINI) allows equating a divergent program run with any other outcome
(
Heidin and Sabelfeld 2011
)
.
PSNI provides a stronger guarantee, especially in a concurrent setting,
where the
termination channel

(
Sabelfeld and Myers 2003
)
can be scaled to many parallel computations,
each leaking “just” one bit
(
Stefan et al. 2012
;
Alpernas et al. 2018
)
.

This paper instantiates the developed logical relation on noninterference, yielding a RSLR for PSNI.
Moreover, the paper develops an information flow control refinement type system for ILLST,
facilitating proof of PSNI by type checking.

Information flow control

Type systems for
information flow control (IFC)
assert noninterference by type checking

(
Volpano et al. 1996
;
Smith and Volpano 1998
;
Sabelfeld and Myers 2003
)
.
Given a lattice over security levels and labeling of observables (e.g., output, locations, channels) with these levels,
typing prevents "flows from high to low".

Flow-sensitive
IFC type system additionally track the current level of information obtained up to the current program point,
accounting for a program’s control flow and yielding a more fine-grained analysis.

This paper contributes a flow-sensitive IFC type system as a
refinement
of ILLST,
extended with general recursive types.
Using the RSLR developed in this paper,
we prove that well-typed processes in this refinement type system enjoy PSNI.
The refinement type system has been implemented in a type checker,
featuring local security theories to support secrecy-polymorphic processes.

2.1.
Running example

The session types in our banking example dictate the following protocol:
When a customer opens an account with the bank, the bank sends them a PIN and authorization process (
\mathsf{customer}
).
A customer can now repeatedly query their account’s balance (
\mathit{high}
,
\mathit{med}
,
\mathit{low}
), preceding authentication.
To authenticate themselves, a customer sends their PIN to the authorization process.
The authorization process goes ahead and sends the received PIN to the verifier.
If the authentication by the verifier is successful, the verifier sends the message
\mathit{succ}
to the authorization process, otherwise the message
\mathit{fail}
.
In either case, the verifier returns the PIN to the authorization process.
The latter relays the outcome of the authentication to the customer, yielding the bank account in case of
\mathit{succ}
and the PIN in case of
\mathit{fail}
.
Once done, a customer returns the account to the authorization process and receives their PIN, allowing them to start over.

Terms are typed using the sequent

\Omega\vdash_{\Sigma}P::x{:}A

“process

\Omega

Bound channel variables are subject to

providing

which, conversely, is the

\Omega

and

Right rules define a communication from the point of view of the provider, left rules from the point of view of the client.
In case of a positive connective, the provider sends, and the client receives,
in case of a negative connective, the provider receives, and the client sends.

We briefly comment on the rules.
Cut reduction in the sequent calculus invites a computational, bottom-up reading of the rules,

\mathbf{proc}(y_{\alpha+1},([y_{\alpha+1}/y_{\alpha}]P))

generating a new

Like bound variables, bound channels are subject to

and capture-avoiding substitution, as usual

The configuration typing rules (see

\Delta_{0}\Vdash\mathcal{C}::\Delta

We allow configurations to have

,
which amount to
\Delta_{0}
and

The channels in

comprise the channels for which there exist providing processes in the configuration

\Delta_{0}
comprise the channels for which there exist client processes in the configuration
\mathcal{C}
.
It may be surprising that we refer to these channels as free,
given that they occur in
C

Would linearity not imply that they occur “exactly once”, and thus must be bound?
It is more fruitful to think of channels as shared resources,
like memory locations,
that are
shared
among processes.
ILLST then ensures that at most two processes, a provider and a client,
share a channel at any point in time, where each "owns" one endpoint of (i.e., a reference to) the channel.
The careful accounting of “resources” is visible in the configuration typing rules.
For example, rule

3.
Recursive session logical relation (RSLR)

This section develops a logical relation for ILLST with general recursive types (
Section

3.1
),
shows that the logical relation is closed under parallel composition (
Section

3.2
),
amounts to an equivalence relation (
Section

3.3
),
and is sound and complete with regard to an asynchronous bisimilarity (
Section

3.4
).
The main result obtained via a biorthogonality argument,
soundness and completeness of the logical relation with regard to closure of weak bisimilarity under parallel composition,
is presented in
Section

3.5
.

3.1.
Logical relation

Logical relations have only recently been developed for session types

(
Pérez et al. 2012
;
Caires et al. 2013
;
Pérez et al. 2014
;
DeYoung et al. 2020
;
Derakhshan et al. 2021
)
,
with an emphasis on unary logical relations
and exclusively for ILLST
without
recursion (neither term-level nor type-level).
We contribute a logical relation for ILLST with support of
general recursive types
.
In devising the resulting
recursive session logical relation (RSLR)
, we had to tackle the following challenges:

•

Well-foundedness:
Recursive types mandate use of a measure like step-indexing or later modalities

(
Appel and McAllester 2001
;
Ahmed 2006
;
Dreyer et al. 2009
)
to keep the logical relation well-founded.
Our logical relation makes use of a more
extensional
notion, an
observation index
,
which bounds the number of messages exchanged with the outside world along the free channels of a configuration.
The observation index facilitates proofs of various results shown in this section.

•

Non-termination:
In the presence of general recursive types, divergence is a possible outcome.
Our logical relation is
progress-sensitive
with regard to divergence in that
it equates a divergent program only with another diverging one.

•

Nondeterminism:
Although ILLST enjoy confluence, processes run
concurrently
.
As a result, the logical relation has to account for relatedness of messages that are possibly not being sent simultaneously
due to nondeterminism of scheduling.

3.1.1.
A logical relation for message-passing concurrency

It may be helpful to pause a moment and ask oneself what a logical relation for session-typed processes should amount to.
Logical relations for functional languages
relate terms by defining their equality at ground type when evaluated to values.
This can be phrased in terms of two mutually recursive relations, a value interpretation

\mathcal{V}\llbracket\tau\rrbracket
and
a term interpretation

\mathcal{E}\llbracket\tau\rrbracket
,
defined by structural induction on the type
\tau
.
The former defines equality of values at ground type, by inducting over the type,
and the latter steps the terms until they reach a value,
demanding that these must be related by the value interpretation.
In imperative languages, logical relations are typically enriched with Kripke possible worlds
to semantically type the store

(
Pitts and Stark 1998
;
Ahmed et al. 2009
;
Dreyer et al. 2010
;
Neis et al. 2011
;
Hur and Dreyer 2011
;
Thamsborg and Birkedal 2011
)
.

To answer our question,
it is helpful to remind ourselves that logical relations are a means to prove observational equivalence

(
Morris 1968
)
,
which equates two programs, if the same observations can be made about them.
In a pure functional setting, the observables are the values to which the terms evaluate.
In an imperative setting, the observables can additionally comprise the values of locations in the store.
The observables in a message-passing concurrent setting
are the messages that a configuration of processes is willing to exchange with the outside world.

A logical relation for session-typed processes thus relates pairs of process configurations
(\mathcal{D}_{1};\mathcal{D}_{2})
,
such that

\Delta\Vdash\mathcal{D}_{1}::x_{\alpha}{:}A
and

\Delta\Vdash\mathcal{D}_{2}::x_{\alpha}{:}A
,
demanding that the same messages can be observed along the
free channels
\Delta
and
x_{\alpha}{:}A
.

Figure

6
shows the resulting logical relation for well-typed process configurations in
SESSION
.
We find it convenient to distinguish a value interpretation
\mathcal{V}

from a term interpretation
\mathcal{E}
.
As expected, the term interpretation steps the configurations until they reach a “value”.
But what does it mean for a configuration to be a value in a message-passing concurrent setting?
It means that a configuration is
ready to

send
or
receive
along any of
the free channels in
\Delta\cup\{x_{\alpha}{:}A\}
.
For any such channel,
the term interpretation invokes the value interpretation,
which demands that the exchanged messages be related,
and then invokes the term interpretation for the configurations resulting after the message exchange.
The mutually recursive relations are thus defined by

)

3.1.4.
Non-termination and nondeterminism

It is now time to take a closer look at the definition of the term interpretation of our logical relation.
Its definition is challenged by the possibility of divergence and nondeterminism of scheduling.
For the former, a progress-sensitive statement of equivalence demands that the relation
relates a divergent program only with another diverging one.
For the latter, the term interpretation has to account for the possibility that
two configurations may not
simultaneously
be ready to send or receive
along a channel in the interface of the logical relation.
To address these challenges, the term interpretation is phrased as follows (we are repeating clause (11) in
Figure

6
):

\begin{array}[]{@{}lcl@{}}({\mathcal{D}_{1}};{\mathcal{D}_{2}})\in\mathcal{E}\llbracket\Delta\Vdash K\rrbracket^{m+1}&\mathsf{iff}&({\mathcal{D}_{1}};{\mathcal{D}_{2}})\in\mathsf{Tree}(\Delta\Vdash K)\,\mathsf{and}\,\forall\,\Upsilon_{1},\Theta_{1},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}.\\[2.0pt]
&&\mathsf{if}\;{\mathcal{D}_{1}}\;\mapsto^{{*}_{\Upsilon_{1};\Theta_{1}}}\;{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}}\,\mathsf{then}\,\exists\Upsilon_{2},\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}\mathsf{\,such\,that\,}\,{\mathcal{D}_{2}}\mapsto^{{*}_{\Upsilon_{2}}}{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}}\,\mathsf{and}\,\Upsilon_{1}\subseteq\Upsilon_{2}\;\mathsf{and}\\[2.0pt]
&&\forall\,y_{\alpha}\in\mathbf{Out}(\Delta\Vdash K).\,\mathbf{if}\,y_{\alpha}\in\Upsilon_{1}.\,\mathbf{then}\,({\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}};{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}})\in\mathcal{V}\llbracket\Delta\Vdash K\rrbracket_{\cdot;y_{\alpha}}^{m+1}\,\mathsf{and}\\[2.0pt]
&&\forall\,y_{\alpha}\in\mathbf{In}(\Delta\Vdash K).\mathbf{if}\,y_{\alpha}\in\Theta_{1}.\,\mathbf{then}\,({\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{1}};{\mathcal{D}^{\prime}_{2}})\in\mathcal{V}\llbracket\Delta\Vdash K\rrbracket_{y_{\alpha};\cdot}^{m+1}\end{array}

The existentially quantified

but restricts observations to those free channels that are observable.
The remaining free channels will be composed in parallel with arbitrary ILLST-typed other configurations.

Definition 0 (Equivalence of trees by the logical relation upto the observer level).

We define the relation

CONSESSION
-typed configurations are related by the term interpretation of the RSLR,

To strengthen the inductive hypothesis,
Theorem

4

relies on the notion of
relevant nodes
of a configuration
\mathbb{D}{\Downarrow}\xi
, introduced by
Derakhshan et al. 2021
,
and the invariant that the relevant nodes in both configurations execute the same code

\mathbb{D}_{1}{\Downarrow}\xi=_{\xi}\mathbb{D}_{2}{\Downarrow}\xi
.

Definition

5
gives the formal definition of a relevant node,
but intuitively we can think of the relevant nodes as those processes in a configuration
who can either directly or indirectly cause a message to be sent along a free channel in the interface of the logical relation.
This invariant is maintained in the proof and crucial to keep the two configurations in sync.

The definition of relevant nodes relies on quasi-running secrecy
(
Derakhshan et al. 2021
)
,
which provides a more accurate handling of receives.
We refer the interested reader to Def.
on page
in the Appendix for its definition.

Definition 0 (Relevant node).

Consider a configuration
\Gamma\Vdash\mathbb{D}::K^{s}
and observer level
\xi
.
A channel is relevant in
\mathbb{D}
if
(1) it has a maximal secrecy level less than or equal to
\xi
, and
(2) it is either an observable channel or it shares a process or message with quasi-running secrecy less than or equal to
\xi
with a relevant channel.
(A channel shares a process with another channel if they are siblings or one is the parent of another.)
A process or message is relevant if
(1) it has quasi-running secrecy less than or equal to
\xi
, and
(2) it has at least one relevant channel.
We denote the relevant processes and messages (i.e., nodes) in
\mathbb{D}
by
\mathbb{D}{\Downarrow}\xi
.
We write
\mathbb{D}_{1}{\Downarrow}\xi=_{\xi}\mathbb{D}_{2}{\Downarrow}\xi
if the relevant nodes in
\mathbb{D}_{1}
are identical to those in
\mathbb{D}_{2}
up to renaming of channels with higher or incomparable secrecy than the observer.

We provide the security theory of each process variable consisting of its essential constraints next to its definition. We leave it to the reader to check that in each spawn, the caller can assert the security theory of their callee.

In contrast to Alice’s verifier, her PIN, and her account, the customer and authorization processes do not have any specific information about Alice, e.g., her correct PIN, hard-coded in their code. Thus, we can define them as generic processes that can be spawned for any customer. Their caller only needs to instantiate their secrecy variables with the customer’s correct secrecy level when spawned for a specific customer.

5.2.
Implementation

We implemented a
type checker
for
CONSESSION
, whose features we now briefly describe.
The type checker is written in OCaml.
It offers a list of the existing examples, including the ones discussed throughout this paper, and produces type checking outputs.
The type checker uses a different syntax from the more formal one used throughout this paper.
An example input source file is provided and can be used as a syntax guide.
The implementation works with a standard
forward
processes.

User-specified security semilattice.
Our type checker allows the user to specify a
concrete security join-semilattice
\Psi_{0}
(see Def.
on page
in the Appendix) and verify its validity.
It will reject security levels that
fail the semilattice axioms. To specify
\Psi_{0}
, the user must prefix the source code with a

secrecy
section, where the user must list the elements of the lattice in decreasing order of secrecy.

Lattice theories for polymorphic process definitions

Our type checker allows the programmer
to specify any number of
lattice theories
, as defined in Def.
.
A lattice theory postulates a series of security terms
c,d\in\mathcal{S}
,
ranging over the elements of the concrete security lattice as well as a set of security variables
\psi\in\mathcal{V}
, along with a list of
relations
E\in\mathcal{E}
that the variables must satisfy.
As defined in Def.
,
E
may only be of the form
c\sqsubseteq d
,
but the implementation also allows programmers to specify
equality between formulas (e.g.,
c\sqcup d=e\sqcup\texttt{Alice}
),
which is desugared into two
\sqsubseteq
relations
(
c\sqcup d\sqsubseteq e\sqcup\texttt{Alice}
, and vice versa). Lattice theories are named.
A process is then parametrized over a lattice theory of the programmer’s choice.

Mutual recursive definition
The type checker also implements limited support for
equi-recurisve definitions, in line with the rules laid out in the main body of the paper.
In particular, for (recursive) type definitions, although definitions can refer to each other, they must
all be individually
contractive
.

6.
Related work and discussion

Logical relations for session types and noninterference in general:

The application of logical relations to session types has focused predominantly on unary logical relations
for proving termination
(
Pérez et al. 2012
;
Pérez et al. 2014
;
DeYoung et al. 2020
)
,
except for a binary logical relation for parametricity
(
Caires et al. 2013
)
and
noninterference
(
Derakhshan et al. 2021
)
.
Our RSLR shares its foundation in ILLST with this line of work.
However, it departs significantly through its support of general recursive types.

Among prior work on logical relations for ILLST, the work by
Derakhshan et al. 2021
is most closely related to ours.
The authors contribute an IFC session type system and develop a logical relation for it to show noninterference.
Our refinement IFC type system extends the authors’ system with support of
general recursive session types and secrecy-polymorphic processes.
Besides the authors’ confinement to a terminating language,
sidestepping the intricacies of non-termination and nondeterminism,
the authors’ metatheoretic results are significantly more limited than ours.
In particular, the logical relation is dependent on IFC typing,
and thus only amounts to a syntactic logical relation for noninterference.
Moreover, the development lacks metatheoretic results about the entailed program equivalence.

(
Gregersen et al. 2021
)
recently contributed a logical relation for a higher-order functional language with higher-order store, recursive types, existential types, impredicative type polymorphism, and label polymorphism.
They use a semantic approach to proving noninterference, allowing integration of syntactically well-typed and ill-typed components, if the latter are shown to be semantically sound.
Besides the difference in language, the authors consider termination-
insensitive
noninterference,
whereas we consider progress-
sensitive
noninterference.

Relationship to logical relations for stateful languages:

Logical relations have been scaled to accommodate state using Kripke logical relations (KLRs)
(
Pitts and Stark 1998
)
.
KLRs are indexed by a
possible world W
, providing a semantic model of the heap.
Invariants can then be imposed that must be preserved by any future worlds W’.
KRLs can be combined with step indexing
(
Appel and McAllester 2001
;
Ahmed 2006
)

to address circularity arising in higher-order stores
(
Ahmed et al. 2009
;
Dreyer et al. 2010
;
Dreyer et al. 2012
)
and to express state transition systems.
Our RSLR, like KLRs, is situated in a stateful setting because channels, like locations,
are subject to concurrent mutation.
However, our RSLR is rooted in ILLST, which guarantees race freedom, stratifies the store (the configuration of process),
and prescribe state transitions.
When squinting one’s eyes, it seems that one can view the interface
\Delta\Vdash K
of the logical relation
as a possible world, since it provides the semantic typing of the free channels.
However, linearity would not comport well with the usual monotonicity requirement of logical relations.
We would like to investigate this connection as part of future work.
We also find it intriguing to explore connections to game semantics,
for which the connection to session types and process calculi has been established
(
Castellan and Yoshida 2019
;
Jaber and Sangiorgi 2022
)
.

IFC type systems for multiparty session types:

IFC type systems have also been explored for multiparty session types
(
Capecchi et al. 2010
;
Capecchi et al. 2014
;
Castellani et al. 2016
;
Capecchi et al. 2016
)
.
These works explore declassification
(
Capecchi et al. 2010
;
Capecchi et al. 2014
)
and flexible run-time monitoring techniques
(
Castellani et al. 2016
;
Capecchi et al. 2016
)
.
Our work not only differs in use of session type paradigm (i.e., binary vs. multiparty) but also in use of a logical relation for showing noninterference.

IFC type systems for process calculi:

Our work is more distantly related with IFC type systems developed for process calculi

(
Honda et al. 2000
;
Honda and Yoshida 2002
;
Crafa et al. 2002
;
Crafa and Rossi 2005
;
Crafa and Rossi 2006
;
Honda and Yoshida 2007
;
Crafa and Rossi 2007
;
Hennessy and Riely 2002
;
Hennessy 2005
;
Kobayashi 2005
;
Zdancewic and Myers 2003
;
Pottier 2002
)
by virtue of its underlying concurrent, message-passing computation model.
These works prevent information leakage by associating a security label with channels/types/actions
(
Honda and Yoshida 2002
;
Honda and Yoshida 2007
)
, read/write policies with channels
(
Hennessy and Riely 2002
;
Hennessy 2005
)
, or capabilities with expressions
(
Crafa et al. 2002
)
.

(
Honda and Yoshida 2002
;
Honda and Yoshida 2007
)
moreover also use a substructural type system and prove a sound embedding of Dependency Core Calculus (DCC)
(
Abadi et al. 1999
)
into their calculus.
Of interest is Kobayashi’s information flow analysis
(
Kobayashi 2005
)
,
which also proves progress-sensitive noninterference,
where message transmission can be unsuccessful due to deadlocks.
To guarantee successful transmission, the author annotates channel types with usage information
(
Kobayashi 2002
)
.
Our work sets itself apart from these works in its use of session types and meta theoretic developments based on a novel recursive logical relation.

7.
Concluding remarks

We have contributed a recursive session logical relation (RSLR)
for progress-sensitive equivalence of programs.
The RSLR is rooted in intuitionistic linear logic session types,
inheriting from it a strong foundation in linear logic,
ensuring that channel endpoints are treated as resources.
A novel aspect of the RSLR is its use of an
observation index

to keep the logical relation well-defined in the presence of general recursive types.
This shift, from a step index / unfolding index associated with
internal
computation steps
to an index only associated with
external
observations,
facilitates statement and proof of metatheoretic properties of the logical relation:
closure under parallel composition,
soundness and completeness with regard to weak asynchronous bisimilarity,
and biorthogonal closure of the logical equivalence relation induced by the RSLR.
A biorthogonality argument arises naturally from the duality inherent to session types.
In future work, we would like to explore these connections more deeply,
possibly connecting to Kripke logical relations and game semantics.
