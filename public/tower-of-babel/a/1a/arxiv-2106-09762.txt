Causal Bias Quantification for Continuous Treatments
Gianluca Detommaso, Michael Brückner, Philip Schulz, Victor Chernozhukov

Abstract
We extend the definition of the marginal causal effect to the continuous treatment setting and develop a novel characterization of causal bias in the framework of structural causal models. We prove that our derived bias expression is zero if, and only if, the causal effect is identifiable via covariate adjustment. We show that under some restrictions on the structural equations, the causal bias can be estimated efficiently and allows for causal regularization of predictive probabilistic models. We demonstrate the effectiveness of our method for causal bias quantification in various settings where (not) controlling for certain covariates would introduce causal bias.

1
Introduction

In probabilistic modeling we are often concerned with estimating the posterior predictive distribution
p(Y|X=x,\mathcal{D})
from observed data
\mathcal{D}
, generated by some unknown data generating mechanism
(
Bishop 2006
;
Koller & Friedman 2009
)
. The posterior predictive captures our beliefs about the association of some input variable
X
and the corresponding outcome variable
Y
when both were generated by the same mechanism as the training data. However, in many practical applications, new inputs might not be generated by the exact same mechanism; we may face selection bias; or we aim for a fair model explaining outcomes primarily based on cause. In these cases, we may consider a causal model and estimate the counterfactual distribution (posterior interventional distribution),
p(Y|X\leftarrow x,\mathcal{D})

(
Lauritzen 2001
;
Pearl 2009a
;
Pearl 2009b
)
, which states the posterior probability of an outcome if we intervene in the data generating mechanism and set treatment variable (input)
X
to
x
, also written
do(X=x)
.

To illustrate the difference, consider watching people (not) wearing a raincoat and we are interested in predicting street conditions. In a purely predictive setting, we would learn from past data that observing raincoats is correlated with wet streets and predict street conditions accordingly. However, such a model may fail when applied to a different population of people. For instance, in windy regions, people may wear raincoats even on sunny days; in warm regions, people may prefer umbrellas. In contrast, the posterior interventional distribution would tell us that putting on a coat does not affect street conditions and our prediction would purely rely on the marginal probability of observing wet streets.

In many applications, the situation is more nuanced and we are facing the problem whether to include certain covariates in the model to improve predictive performance or whether to exclude them to not incur into bias. The problem is well-understood if we aim to avoid any causal bias; however, what if we are willing to accept some bias in order to improve predictive performance?

Starting from a given causal probabilistic model defined in terms of structural equations, we are interested in quantifying the causal bias of a predictive model which, together with its posterior probability, allows us to balance predictive performance with causal alignment. We focus on the setting of continuous treatment variables, which will enable us to state an analytic expression of the causal bias.

1.1
Related Work

Large parts of literature on causal inference focus on
causal identifiability
, that is, identifying conditions under which the causal relationship can be unbiasedly estimated from the observed data
(
Spirtes et al. 2000
;
Shpitser & Pearl 2006
;
Pearl 2009c
)
, and
causal estimation
of models when these conditions are satisfied.

If we are not willing to make any additional (parametric) modeling assumptions, causal identifiability requires treatments from a finite domain
(
Angrist & Pischke 2008
;
Hernán & Robins 2020
)
. In particular, for binary treatments, we might only be interested in the causal effect expressed in terms of the average treatment effect of the treated (ATET) or the average treatment effect (ATE)
(
Imbens & Rubin 2015
)
, which are the expected difference between the (potential) outcomes under the two alternative treatments for the treated and the whole population, respectively.

In situations where different posterior interventional distributions are compatible with the observed data and the assumed causal relations, the causal effect is
non-identifiable
without making additional modeling assumptions, for instance, on the functional relationship of treatments and outcomes; this is in particular true for continuous treatments
(
Rosenbaum et al. 2010
;
Silva 2016
;
Hines et al. 2021
)
. Modeling the treatment assignment mechanism allows for
partial identification
of the causal effect through inverse propensity weighting
(
Imbens 2000
;
Hirano & Imbens 2004
;
Imai & Van Dyk 2004
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Austin 2019
)
, modeling the outcome mechanism allows for regression methods
(
Chipman et al. 2010
;
Hill 2011
)
, and doubly robust estimation can be used to combine both
(
Kennedy et al. 2017
;
Chernozhukov et al. 2018
)
. More generally, structural causal models
(
Pearl 2009c
)
allow us to describe the full data generating mechanism.

Similarly, estimating the average treatment effect for continuous treatments either requires some discretization or assuming a certain functional form called dose-response function in case of bounded univariate treatments
(
Altshuler 1981
;
Gill & Robins 2001
;
Wang 2015
;
Galagate 2016
)
.

However, most existing works on continuous treatments assume that the causal effect is identifiable and, hence, the causal bias is zero. In contrast, we consider the scenario where the causal effect might be non-identifiable and develop a characterization of the bias that can be used to evaluate how far a model is from being causal. While different in methodology, a similar purpose can be found in
(
Tran et al. 2016
;
Gain & Shpitser 2018
)
. In addition, it can be used to perform sensitivity analysis in the presence of unobserved confounders in a completely non-linear framework, similarly to what is proposed in
(
Cinelli & Hazlett 2020
)
for the linear case. Our proposed criterion for identifiability via covariate adjustment is also related to the adjustment criterion in
(
Shpitser et al. 2010
)
, which was shown by the authors to imply and be implied by conditional ignorability
(
Rosenbaum & Rubin 1983
;
Little & Rubin 2019
)
. Our criterion is useful to build the link between identifiability and causal bias being zero.

Preliminaries

We assume a probabilistic model of the data generating mechanism, that is, a joint distribution over some random variables
\mathcal{V}=\{V_{1},\ldots,V_{n}\}
and a causal directed acyclic graph (DAG)

We further assume that the probabilistic model and causal graph are stated in terms of a canonical structural causal model (SCM)

consisting of a set of independent random variables

. The
exogenous variables

Technically, an SCM defines a joint probability distribution over

,
\text{An}^{+}(\cdot)\subset\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{U}
, and
\text{De}^{+}(\cdot)\subset\mathcal{V}\cup\mathcal{U}
. Figure

shows an example of a causal graph

is a scalar function,
\nabla_{x}h
denotes a transposed gradient. We use
\mathbb{E}
,

for expectation, covariance, and variance. We may use subscripts to specify the underlying probability density, e.g.

2
Identification, quantification and estimation

In this section we (i) introduce a novel criterion for identifiability of causal effects via covariate adjusts; (ii) we derive closed-form expressions for causal effect and causal bias that can be computed in closed-form; and (iii) show how the latter can be estimated via standard probabilistic inference methods.

2.1
A characterization of identifiability via covariate adjustment

This section offers a complete criterion for identifiability of causal effects
(
Pearl 2009b
)
via covariate adjustment
(
Shpitser et al. 2010
)
in the framework of structural causal models. The underlying idea is to first construct a minimal set of exogenous variables,
\mathcal{U}^{C}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, such that, together with variables in
\mathcal{X}
and
\underline{\mathcal{O}}
, this set fully characterizes the randomness of outcome variable
Y
.
To this end, let us again consider the causal graph in Figure
1(a)
as a running example.

Example 1
.

We assume the causal graph in Figure
1(a)
and
X=V_{2}
,
Y=V_{4}
,
\mathcal{O}=\{V_{3},V_{5}\}
, and
\mathcal{L}=\{V_{1}\}
. In this case, it is well understood that the unobserved variable
V_{1}
and the observed variables
V_{3}
and
V_{5}
each compromise identifiability of the causal effect from
X
to
Y
. Latent variable
V_{1}
is a hidden
confounder
introducing confounding bias, observing
mediator

V_{3}
introduces overcontrol bias, and observing
collider

V_{5}
introduces (endogenous) selection bias
(
Elwert 2013
)
. Figure
1(b)
is the causal graph
\mathcal{G}^{+}
of an SCM aligning with
\mathcal{G}
. We notice that

U_{V_{1}}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp X,\quad U_{V_{3}}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp X|V_{3},\quad U_{Y}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp X|V_{5},

which we will see in Theorem
2.4
to be the very reasons why the causal effect is not identifiable through covariate adjustment.

Definition 2.1
.

For any set of treatment variables
\mathcal{X}
and outcome variable
Y
, the set of exogenous random variables which are ancestors of
Y
in
\mathcal{G}^{+}
and that are not independent of
Y
given
\mathcal{X}
and
\underline{\mathcal{O}}
is called the
causal exogenous set
:

\mathcal{U}^{C}:=\{U_{V_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}\cap\text{An}^{+}(Y):U_{V_{i}}\not\perp\!\!\!\perp Y|\mathcal{X},\underline{\mathcal{O}}\}.

(1)

Intuitively, this set contains all exogenous variables which we would need to control for to translate the observational setting into an experimental setting. In Example
1
this set includes random variables
U_{V_{1}}
,
U_{V_{3}}
, and
U_{Y}
. While we can never observe exogenous variables, we might be able to obtain a posterior distribution over them by making (weak) prior distributional assumptions. If this posterior distribution is insensitive to the treatment variable, the causal effect can be (partially) identified. In the following, we state this observation more formally.

Assumption 2.2
.

We assume that the given SCM is defined through continuous exogenous variables
U_{V_{i}}
where the probability density exists and is strictly positive for all possible values of
U_{V_{i}}
.

Assumption 2.3
.

We assume that the given SCM is faithful to the causal graph
\mathcal{G}^{+}
, that is, any independences that hold in the model must hold in the causal graph.

Theorem 2.4
.

Given an SCM that satisfies Assumption
2.2
with causal exogenous set
\mathcal{U}^{C}
for treatment variable
X
, outcome variable
Y
, and a set of observations
\mathcal{O}
, the causal effect of
X
on
Y
can be identified by covariate adjustment if, and only if,

U_{V_{i}}\perp\!\!\!\perp X|\mathcal{O}\ \ \ \forall\ U_{V_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}^{C}

(2)

Proof.

See Appendix
A.1
.
∎

Corollary 2.5
.

Given an SCM that satisfies Assumptions
2.2
-
2.3
where the causal effect of
X
on
Y
can be identified by covariate adjustment and which is differentiable in
X
, then

\left.\nabla_{X}\log p(U^{C}|X,O)\right|_{X=x}=0\ \ \ \forall\ x,

(3)

where
U^{C}
denotes the vector of all endogenous variables in
\mathcal{U}^{C}
and
O
denotes the vector of observations.

Proof.

According to Theorem
2.4
, treatment variable
X
is independent of all elements of the causal exogenous set given the observed variables,
p(U^{C}|X,O)=p(U^{C}|O)
. If the density defined through the SCM exists and is differentiable w.r.t.
X
, the partial gradient
\nabla_{X}\log p(U^{C}|X,O)
is zero everywhere.
∎

2.2
Quantification of average partial effect and bias

Corollary
2.5
gives rise to a measure of causal bias which is zero if the causal bias is zero. To formalize this, let us introduce the notation of
partial evaluation
, which is related to the potential outcome notation and interventions in structural causal models.

Definition 2.6
.

Given a function
f
with arguments
\mathcal{A}=\{V_{i_{1}},\ldots,V_{i_{m}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
and some set
\mathcal{B}=\{V_{j_{1}},\ldots,V_{j_{k}}\}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, we define
f^{b}
to be the
partial evaluation
of
f
where all arguments
\mathcal{A}\cap\mathcal{B}
have been assigned to the corresponding value
b=[b_{1},\ldots,b_{k}]
, that is

f^{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow b}(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{B}):=f(V_{i_{1}},\dots,V_{i_{m}})\big|_{V_{j_{1}}=b_{1},\ldots,V_{j_{k}}=b_{k}}.

(4)

We may use the short form
f^{b}
for
f^{\mathcal{B}\leftarrow b}(\mathcal{A}\setminus\mathcal{B})
if the corresponding set of assigned random variables
\mathcal{B}
is clear from the context, for example, all observed variables
\mathcal{O}
. Likewise, if sets
\mathcal{B}
and
\mathcal{C}
are disjoint sets of random variables, we use
f^{b,c}
to denote simultaneously assignments
\mathcal{B}\leftarrow b
and
\mathcal{C}\leftarrow c
.

.

Consider again the causal graph in Figure

If derivatives are well-defined, this for example implies
\nabla_{U_{V_{3}}}f_{V_{5}}^{x,o}=0
, while in general

.

Given an SCM that satisfies Assumptions

\displaystyle\underbrace{\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]}_{\mathcal{A}(x,o)}

\displaystyle\!\!=\!\!

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{U^{C}|x,o}\left[\nabla_{x}f_{Y}^{x,\underline{o}}\right]}_{\mathcal{C}(x,o)}\ +

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{U^{C}|x,o}\left[f_{Y}^{x,\underline{o}}\nabla_{x}\!\log p(U^{C}|x,o)\right]}_{\mathcal{B}(x,o)}\!.\ \ \ \

In the above proposition, expression

\mathcal{B}(x,o)

Remark 2.8
.

The decomposition in (
5
) has a well-known discrete counterpart for some reference treatment
x
(control) and proposal
x^{\prime}:=x+h
(treated),

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y|x^{\prime}]-\mathbb{E}[Y|x]}_{\text{association}}

\displaystyle\!\!=\!\!

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y^{x^{\prime}}-Y^{x}|x^{\prime}]}_{\text{ATET}}\ +

\displaystyle\underbrace{\mathbb{E}[Y^{x}|x^{\prime}]-\mathbb{E}[Y^{x}|x]}_{\text{bias}}\,,\

where association is defined as the contrast in average outcomes by observed treatment value, and ATET refers to the average treatment effect on the treated. If we condition on
O=o
, divide both sides by
h
and take the limit for
h\to 0
, we recover (
5

The expressions for
\mathcal{C}(x,o)
and
\mathcal{B}(x,o)
in (

in order to compute them. Indeed, because
\nabla_{x}f_{Y}^{x,\underline{o}}
does not depend on

by definition of
\mathcal{U}^{C}
, via marginalization we can write

\mathcal{C}(x,o)=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}\left[\nabla_{x}f_{Y}^{x}\right],

(6)

where

provides an elegant formulation of the bias that is easier to compute in practice. Under some additional assumptions Theorem

Under the assumptions of Proposition

, the bias in (

\mathcal{B}(x,o)=\text{Cov}_{U^{C}|x,o}\left(f_{Y}^{x,\underline{o}},\nabla_{x}\log p(U^{C},x,o)\right),

(7)

where

denotes the joint density over

,

, and

(partially) evaluated for

and

.

See Appendix
A.3
.
∎

The expression of the bias in Theorem

is a clear improvement over (

p(U^{C},X,O)
does not include a normalization constant involving

. If no mediators between

For instance, consider Example

Suppose that

\displaystyle\ (\nabla_{U_{V_{i}}}f_{V_{i}}^{x,o})^{-1}\,\nabla_{x}(f_{V_{i}}^{x,o}-v_{i})\Big]

with potential outcome

Remark 2.11
.

Note that (i) all terms in the expectation of (
2.10
) can be efficiently computed via automatic differentiation. (ii) Bias
\mathcal{B}(x,o)
can be decomposed in the contributions given by

(potential confounding bias) and by each variable

(potential overcontrol or selection bias). (iii) If

is modeled as a (multivariate) standard Gaussian, then

. Finally, (iv) the inverse Jacobian

can be efficiently computed if

is low-dimensional or if

has, for example, a normalizing flow structure
(
Papamakarios et al. 2019
)
.

2.3
Estimation of average partial effect and bias

Sections
2.1
and
2.2
provide closed-form expressions for average partial effect on the treated and the causal bias, namely (
6
) and (
2.10
). Their numerical estimation reduces to a mere statistical inference task: the approximation of an expectation with respect to the probability density
p(U|x,o)
. We further notice that

p(U|x,o)=p(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)\prod_{V_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{O}}\delta(U_{V_{i}}-u_{V_{i}}^{*}),

(9)

where
u_{V_{i}}^{*}
are uniquely determined by the assumptions in Theorem
2.10
that
f_{V_{i}}
are invertible in
U_{V_{i}}
for all
V_{i}\in\mathcal{X}\cup\mathcal{O}
given
U_{\mathcal{L}}
. Hence, we can restrict our attention to infer
p(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)
. In some cases, such a posterior might be available in closed-form. Otherwise we may resort to sampling methods such as MCMC
(
Brooks et al. 2011
)
, SMC
(
Doucet et al. 2001
)
, and importance sampling
(
Neal 2001
)
, or variational methods like Laplace approximation
(
Shun & McCullagh 1995
)
, variational Bayes
(
Jordan et al. 1999
;
Blei et al. 2017
)
, and normalizing flows
(
Rezende & Mohamed 2015
;
Papamakarios et al. 2019
)
. Let us quickly review Laplace approximation and importance sampling in our framework, as we will use them in Sections
3.2
and
3.3
, respectively.

Laplace approximation.
The Laplace approximation is a Gaussian approximation of
p(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)
around its mode. In order to compute the mode, we first notice that via automatic differentiation we have immediate access to
\nabla_{U_{V_{i}}}\log p(U_{\mathcal{L}},x,o)
for all
U_{V_{i}}\in\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{L}}
, which we can employ in any gradient-based optimization to compute a maximum-a-posteriori (MAP) estimate
(
Murphy 2012
)
. Let us denote the latter by
u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\scriptsize\text{MAP}}
. Furthermore, automatic differentiation also gives access to the Hessian with elements (sub-matrices)
\nabla_{U_{V_{i}}}\nabla_{U_{V_{j}}}\log p(U_{\mathcal{L}},x,o)
or to any scalable approximation of it (e.g. diagonal approximation). Denote this Hessian evaluated at the MAP estimate by
H(u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\scriptsize\text{MAP}})
. Whenever
p(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)
has a well-defined MAP estimate,
-H(u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\scriptsize\text{MAP}})
is positive-definite and we can approximate the posterior by a (multivariate) Gaussian,

p(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)\approx\mathcal{N}\!\left(u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\scriptsize\text{MAP}},-H^{-1}(u_{\mathcal{L}}^{\scriptsize\text{MAP}})\right)\!(U_{\mathcal{L}}).

(10)

We can now generate a sample

from the approximated posterior, compute the remaining exogenous variables via the respective inverse maps of
f_{V_{i}}^{x,o}
, and produce a Monte Carlo estimate of
\mathcal{C}(x,o)
and
\mathcal{B}(x,o)
using (
6
) and (
2.10
), respectively.

Importance sampling.
Importance sampling is a way to modify the density underlying an expectation. In causal inference, it is used for inverse propensity weighting (IPW)
(
Imbens 2000
;
Hirano & Imbens 2004
;
Imai & Van Dyk 2004
;
Wu et al. 2018
;
Austin 2019
)
. Consider any treatable density
q(U_{\mathcal{L}})>0
of a sampling distribution, and some function
h(u_{\mathcal{L}},u_{X},u_{\mathcal{O}})
, which is integrable in all arguments. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[h(U_{\mathcal{L}},\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\!\!\!\!U_{X},U_{\mathcal{O}})]

(11)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o}\left[h(U_{\mathcal{L}},u_{X}^{*},u_{\mathcal{O}}^{*})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U_{\mathcal{L}}}\left[h(U_{\mathcal{L}},u_{X}^{*},u_{\mathcal{O}}^{*})w(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)\right],\ \ \ \ \ \

where
\mathbb{E}_{U_{\mathcal{L}}}
denotes the expectation with respect to density
q(\mathcal{U}_{\mathcal{L}})
and normalized importance weights

w(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)\propto\frac{p(U_{\mathcal{L}},x,o)}{q(U_{\mathcal{L}})},

(12)

with
\mathbb{E}_{U_{\mathcal{L}}}\left[w(U_{\mathcal{L}}|x,o)\right]=1
. Then we can first sample
u_{\mathcal{L}}^{(i)}
from
q(U_{\mathcal{L}})
for
i=1,\ldots,N
, compute the unnormalized weights
\tilde{w}^{(i)}=p(u_{\mathcal{L}}^{(i)},x,o)\,/\,q(u_{\mathcal{L}}^{(i)})
, and normalize the importance weights to sum up to
N
. Finally, using these same samples, we can compose a Monte Carlo estimator using (
11
), and estimate
\mathcal{C}(x,o)
and
\mathcal{B}(x,o)
by applying this technique for
h
being the respective expressions in (
6
) and (
2.10
). Notice that if we take
q(U_{\mathcal{L}})
to be the prior
p(U_{\mathcal{L}})
, the unnormalized weights simplify to be the likelihood
p(x,o|u_{\mathcal{L}}^{(i)})
.

3.1
Confounding, overcontrol and selection bias

First, we study simple linear models exhibiting the three possible types of bias: confounding, overcontrol and (endogenous) selection
(

)
. Studying linear models is useful because we can compute
\mathcal{A}
,
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{B}
in closed-form, checking our results and building up intuition. For all models, we will consider
\alpha,\beta,\gamma
and
\delta
to be scalar parameters and
U_{V_{1}},U_{X},U_{Y}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
. For detailed proofs of the following results, see Appendix
B
.

Confounding bias.

Given the model in Figure
4
, we can compute

\mathcal{A}(x,o)\,=\,\beta+\frac{\gamma\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}},\quad\mathcal{C}(x,o)\,=\,\beta,\quad\mathcal{B}(x,o)\,=\,\frac{\gamma\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

Notice that because the model is linear,
\mathcal{C}(x,o)
is independent of
p(U|x,o)
. If

then

. Thus (
2
) is violated and
\mathcal{B}(x,o)\neq 0
.

Overcontrol bias.

Given the model in Figure
4
, we can compute

\mathcal{A}(x,o)\,=\,\beta,\quad\mathcal{C}(x,o)\,=\,\beta+\gamma\alpha,\quad\mathcal{B}(x,o)\,=\,-\gamma\alpha.

Again, because the model is linear,
\mathcal{C}(x,o)

p(U|x,o)

then

\mathcal{B}(x,o)\neq 0
.

Selection bias.

Given the model in Figure
4

\mathcal{A}(x,o)\,=\,\frac{\alpha-\gamma\beta}{1+\gamma^{2}},\quad\mathcal{C}(x,o)\,=\,\alpha,\quad\mathcal{B}(x,o)\,=\,-\frac{\gamma(\beta+\gamma\alpha)}{1+\gamma^{2}}.

4
Conclusion and future direction

In this work we developed a new complete criterion for identifiability of causal effects via covariate adjustment. We provided a mathematical relation between association, causal effect, and bias, showing the bias is zero if such criterion holds. We developed a formulation of the bias where all terms can be estimated from data using automatic differentiation. We demonstrated the usefulness of our results over several experiments. A natural future work direction is to exploit the bias characterization as a causal regularizer for the training of highly non-linear machine learning models where we cannot easily control for causal bias.

Definition A.1
.

Appendix B
Confounding, overcontrol and endogenous selection bias

B.1
Confounding bias

Let us consider the model in Figure
4
. Following (
6
), the average partial effect on the treated can be expressed as

\mathcal{C}(x,o)=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\nabla_{x}f_{Y}^{x}]=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta]=\beta.

For the marginal causal bias, we have
\nabla_{u_{X}}f_{Y}^{x,o}=0
,
\nabla_{u_{X}}\log p(U_{X})=-U_{X}
,
\nabla_{u_{X}}f_{X}^{x,o}=1
and
\nabla_{x}(f_{X}^{x,o}-x)=-1
. Then (
2.10
) gives

\mathcal{B}(x,o)=-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\left(f_{Y}^{x}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}]\right)U_{X}].

Notice that
p(u_{V_{1}},u_{X},u_{Y}|x)=p(u_{V_{1}},u_{X}|x)p(u_{Y})
, which implies that
U_{Y}|X=x
and
U_{X}|X=x
are independent. This yields

\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\left(f_{Y}^{x}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}]\right)U_{X}]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[(\beta x+U_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+U_{Y}])U_{X}]-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[(\gamma U_{V_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\gamma U_{V_{1}}])U_{X}]

\displaystyle=-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+U_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+U_{Y}]]\,\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{X}]-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[(U_{V_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}])U_{X}]

\displaystyle=-\gamma\,\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[(U_{V_{1}}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}])U_{X}]

\displaystyle=-\gamma\,\text{Cov}_{U|x,o}(U_{V_{1}},U_{X}),

since
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+U_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+U_{Y}]]=0
. With some simple calculation, one can show that
p(u_{X}|x)=N(\tfrac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}},\frac{\alpha^{2}}{1+\alpha^{2}})(u_{X})
and
p(u_{V_{1}}|x)=N(\tfrac{\alpha x}{1+\alpha^{2}},\tfrac{1}{1+\alpha^{2}})(u_{V_{1}})
, where
N(\mu,\sigma^{2})(a)
generally denotes a Gaussian probability density with mean
\mu
, variance
\sigma^{2}
, evaluated at
a
. We then have that
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{X}]=\tfrac{x}{1+\alpha^{2}}
,
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]=\tfrac{\alpha x}{1+\alpha^{2}}
and
\text{Var}_{U|x,o}(U_{V_{1}})=\tfrac{1}{1+\alpha^{2}}
. Furthermore, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}U_{X}]

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}u_{X}p(u_{V_{1}},u_{X}|x)\,du_{X}\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}\int u_{X}p(u_{X}|u_{V_{1}},x)\,du_{X}\,p(u_{V_{1}}|x)\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}\int u_{X}\delta(u_{X}+\alpha u_{V_{1}}-x)\,du_{X}\,p(u_{V_{1}}|x)\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}(x-\alpha u_{V_{1}})\,p(u_{V_{1}}|x)du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=x\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]-\alpha\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=x\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]-\alpha(\text{Var}_{U|x,o}(U_{V_{1}})+\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]^{2}).

Then we have all the elements to compute

\mathcal{B}(x,o)=-\gamma\left(\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}U_{X}]-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{X}]\right)=\frac{\gamma\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

Thus

\mathcal{C}(x,o)+\mathcal{B}(x,o)=\beta+\frac{\gamma\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}.

Let us check that the latter matches the association
\mathcal{A}(x,o)
when computed directly. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]

\displaystyle=\beta x+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{V_{1}|x,o}[V_{1}]+\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{Y}]

\displaystyle=\beta x+\gamma\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]

\displaystyle=\beta x+\frac{\gamma\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}x.

Then
\mathcal{A}(x,o)=\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]=\beta+\tfrac{\gamma\alpha}{1+\alpha^{2}}
, which verifies the statement.

B.2
Overcontrol bias

Let us consider the model in Figure
4
. Following (
6
), we have

\mathcal{C}(x,o)=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta+\gamma\alpha]=\beta+\gamma\alpha.

For the bias, we have
\nabla_{u_{X}}f_{Y}^{x,o}=0
,
\nabla_{u_{X}}\log p(U_{X})=-U_{X}
,
\nabla_{u_{X}}f_{X}^{x,o}=1
and
\nabla_{x}(f_{X}^{x,o}-x)=-1
. Then the contribution given by
X
to the bias is null, since

\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\left(f_{Y}^{x}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}]\right)U_{X}]=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}]]\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{X}]=0,

where we used that
f_{Y}^{x}
is independent of
U_{X}
given
X=x
.

Regarding the contribution to the bias given by
V_{1}
, we have
\nabla_{u_{V_{1}}}f_{Y}^{x}=\gamma
,
\nabla_{x}f_{V_{1}}^{x,o}=\alpha
and
\nabla_{u_{V_{1}}}f_{V_{1}}^{x,o}=1
. Moreover,
U_{Y}|V_{1}
is independent of
X
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x,o)

\displaystyle=-\gamma\alpha+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\left(f_{Y}^{x}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}]\right)U_{V_{1}}]

\displaystyle=-\gamma\alpha+\alpha\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+\gamma v_{1}+U_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+\gamma v_{1}+U_{Y}]]\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]

\displaystyle=-\gamma\alpha

since
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+\gamma v_{1}+U_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\beta x+\gamma v_{1}+U_{Y}]]=0
. Then

\mathcal{C}(x,o)+\mathcal{B}(x,o)=\beta+\gamma\alpha-\gamma\alpha=\beta.

Let us check the result by computing the marginal association directly. We have
p(y|x,v_{1})=N(\beta x+\gamma v_{1},1)(y)
, whence
\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]=\beta x+\gamma v_{1}
and
\mathcal{A}(x,o)=\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]=\beta
.

B.3
Endogenous selection bias

Let us consider the model in Figure
4
. Following (
6
), we have

\mathcal{C}(x,o)=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\nabla_{x}f_{Y}^{x}]=\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\alpha]=\alpha.

For the bias, we can argue that the contribution of
X
is null for exactly the same reason as in the overcontrol bias case (see Appendix
B.2
). Regarding the contribution of
V_{1}
, we have
\nabla_{u_{V_{1}}}f_{Y}^{x}=0
,
\nabla_{u_{V_{1}}}\log p(U_{V_{1}})=-U_{V_{1}}
,
\nabla_{u_{V_{1}}}f_{V_{1}}^{x,o}=1
and
\nabla_{x}(f_{V_{1}}^{x,o}-{v_{1}})=\beta+\gamma\alpha
. Then

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}(x,o)

\displaystyle=(\beta+\gamma\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\left(f_{Y}^{x}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[f_{Y}^{x}]\right)U_{V_{1}}]

\displaystyle=(\beta+\gamma\alpha)\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[\left(U_{Y}-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{Y}]\right)U_{V_{1}}]

\displaystyle=(\beta+\gamma\alpha)\text{Cov}_{U|x,o}(U_{Y},U_{V_{1}}).

One can compute
p(u_{Y}|x,v_{1})=N(\tfrac{\gamma}{1+\gamma^{2}}(v_{1}-(\beta+\gamma\alpha)x),\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma^{2}})(u_{Y})
and

, which respectively imply
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{Y}]=\tfrac{\gamma}{1+\gamma^{2}}(v_{1}-\beta-\gamma\alpha)x
,
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]=\tfrac{x}{1+\gamma^{2}}(v_{1}-\beta-\gamma\alpha)
and
\text{Var}_{U|x,o}(U_{V_{1}})=\tfrac{\gamma^{2}}{1+\gamma^{2}}
. If
\gamma=0
, then
\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}U_{Y}]=0
. Otherwise

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}U_{Y}]

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}u_{Y}p(u_{V_{1}},u_{Y}|x,v_{1})\,du_{Y}\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}\int u_{Y}p(u_{Y}|u_{V_{1}},x,v_{1})\,du_{Y}\,p(u_{V_{1}}|x,v_{1})\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}\int u_{Y}\delta((\beta+\gamma\alpha)x+\gamma u_{Y}+u_{V_{1}}-v_{1})\,du_{Y}\,p(u_{V_{1}}|x,v_{1})\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\int u_{V_{1}}\left(\frac{1}{\gamma}(v_{1}-(\beta+\gamma\alpha)x)-\frac{1}{\gamma}u_{V_{1}}\right)\,du_{V_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}(v_{1}-(\beta+\gamma\alpha)x)\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]-\frac{1}{\gamma}\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\gamma}(v_{1}-(\beta+\gamma\alpha)x)\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]-\frac{1}{\gamma}(\text{Var}_{U|x,o}(U_{V_{1}})+\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]^{2}).

Then we have all the elements to compute

\mathcal{B}(x,o)=(\beta+\gamma\alpha)\left(\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}U_{Y}]-\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{V_{1}}]\mathbb{E}_{U|x,o}[U_{Y}]\right)=-\frac{\gamma(\beta+\gamma\alpha)}{1+\gamma^{2}}.

Thus

\mathcal{C}(x,o)+\mathcal{B}(x,o)=\frac{\alpha-\gamma\beta}{1+\gamma^{2}}.

Once more, we check that this is the same as computing the association directly. Since
p(y|x,v_{1})=N(\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma^{2}}\left(\gamma v_{1}+(\alpha-\beta\gamma)x\right),\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma^{2}})(y)
, we have
\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]=\tfrac{1}{1+\gamma^{2}}\left(\gamma v_{1}+(\alpha-\beta\gamma)x\right)
, whence
\mathcal{A}(x,o)=\nabla_{x}\mathbb{E}_{Y|x,o}[Y]=\tfrac{\alpha-\beta\gamma}{1+\gamma^{2}}
.
