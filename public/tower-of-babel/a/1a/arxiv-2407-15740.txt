The syzygy distinguisher Thanks: This is an updated and expanded version of the work with the same title presented at Eurocrypt 2025 [ 37 ] . Details about the new material can be found in the Introduction.
Hugues Randriambololona

Abstract
We present a new distinguisher for alternant and Goppa codes, whose complexity is subexponential in the error-correcting capability, hence better than that of generic decoding algorithms. Moreover it does not suffer from the strong regime limitations of the previous distinguishers or structure recovery algorithms: in particular, it applies to the codes used in the Classic McEliece candidate for postquantum cryptography standardization. The invariants that allow us to distinguish are graded Betti numbers of the homogeneous coordinate ring of a shortening of the dual code. Since its introduction in 1978, this is the first time an analysis (in the CPA model) of the McEliece cryptosystem breaks the exponential barrier.

Introduction

In the McEliece cryptosystem
[
29
]
, a private message is encoded
as a codeword in a public binary Goppa code
[
17
]
, with some noise added. Knowing the secret algebraic
data that served to construct the public code, the legitimate recipient has an efficient
decoding algorithm and can recover the message. However, to an attacker, the public code
looks like a random code, and removing the noise is untractable.

At the time of publication of
[
29
]
it was not customary to fully formalize the
expected security properties of the scheme. In modern language we would say that it aims
at one-wayness against chosen plaintext attacks (OW-CPA). There are now variants of the McEliece scheme
that claim stronger security properties (e.g. IND-CCA2), but for this they rely on the hypothesis
that the original McEliece scheme is OW-CPA. This makes it even more relevant today to scrutinize whether
this hypothesis can be reduced to more fundamental theoretical assumptions.

And indeed, as the informal description of the system naturally suggests, a traditional security proof
reduces this hypothesis to the following two assumptions:

1.

Goppa codes are computationally indistinguishable from generic linear codes
(say, when described by generator matrices in reduced row echelon form).

2.

Decoding a generic linear code is difficult.

Cryptanalytic attempts can be classified depending on whether they target assumption
1

or
2
.

First, those aiming at assumption
1
themselves come in two flavours:

•

Distinguishers
address the decisional version of the problem: given a generator matrix,
decide whether it is that of a Goppa code or a generic code.

•

Key recovery attacks
address the computational version:
recover the Goppa structure of the code, or at least an equivalent one
[
16
]
, if it exists.

Assumption
1
was first introduced formally in
[
9
]
.
Although certain arguments, such as the fact that the class of Goppa codes is very large,
make this assumption plausible, it remains quite ad hoc from a theoretical
point of view. Its sole virtue is that it passed the test of time.
There seems to be something special with Goppa codes happening there: indeed, variants of the
McEliece system were proposed, with Goppa codes replaced with other types of codes allowing
more manageable parameters; however, most of these propositions were eventually broken, as the
hidden structure of the codes could be recovered.
Also, although the McEliece cryptosystem marked the birth of code-based cryptography,
the idea of having an object (such as a Goppa code) constructed from data defined over an extension
field and then masked by considering it over a small subfield, was then found in other branches
of cryptography: for instance it is at the basis of the HFE cryptosystem
[
32
]

in multivariate cryptography.
Such systems can often be attacked by algebraic methods
(including, but not limited to, the use of Gröbner basis algorithms).
This suggests that if a weakness in assumption
1
were to be uncovered one day,
then algebraic methods should be a tool of choice.
However, up to now, the best distinguishers and key recovery algorithms such as those
in
[
15
]
[
11
]
[
10
]
[
2
]
[
24
]
[
23
]

only apply to alternant or Goppa codes with very degraded parameters.
Against McEliece with cryptographically relevant parameters, either they have exponential complexity with large
constants, which makes them useless, or worse: they simply cease to work.

Assumption
2
, on the other hand, stands on a firm theoretical ground:
the decoding problem for generic linear codes is known to be NP-hard
[
3
]
.
As such, it is believed to resist the advent of quantum computers, which made
the McEliece system a good candidate for postquantum cryptography.
However, starting with Prange’s information set decoding algorithm
[
33
]
,
generic decoding methods saw continuous incremental improvements over time.
Joint with technological progress in computational power, this eventually led
to practical
message recovery attacks
against the McEliece system with its
initially proposed parameter set.
However, it appears that this weakness was only the result of a too optimistic
choice of parameters.
With the need for new standards for postquantum cryptography,
an updated version named
Classic McEliece
was proposed, still relying on binary Goppa codes,
but with more conservative parameter sets
adapted to resist the best generic decoding attacks with some margin of safety.
In the design rationale of this new system
[
5
]
one can find
an impressive list of several dozens of papers on generic decoding algorithms,
ranging over the last five decades. It is then observed that all these algorithms
have complexity exponential in the error-correcting capability of the code. Better, the constant in this
exponential is still the same as in Prange’s original result: all improvements
remain confined in terms of lesser order!
This might give a feeling that we could possibly have reached the intrinsic
complexity of cryptanalysis of this system.

Preventing the spread of this belief, we present a new distinguisher for alternant and Goppa codes,
i.e. a basic structural analysis of the McEliece cryptosystem,
whose asymptotic complexity is subexponential in the error-correcting capability,
hence better than that of generic decoding algorithms.
Moreover, for given finite parameters, it is more efficient than state-of-the-art distinguishers or key recovery
methods, while not suffering from their strong regime limitations:
in particular, it applies to the codes used in
Classic McEliece
.

Principles and organization

A natural strategy to build a distinguisher is to design code invariants — quantities
that intrinsically depend only on the code, not on the choice of a generator matrix — that
behave differently for the classes of codes we want to distinguish.
The invariants we use here are graded Betti numbers of the homogeneous
coordinate ring of a shortening of the dual code.
Generators of the dual of an alternant or Goppa code, after extension of scalars,
satisfy quadratic relations of a special form: they can be expressed as
2\times 2
minors
of a matrix. As such, we can find relations between these quadratic relations, called
syzygies
.
Then these syzygies also satisfy relations, and iterating this process we get higher syzygies up to
some order that we can estimate. However, for generic codes, we do not expect this to
happen in the same magnitude. This directly gives a distinguisher, at least in theory.
In practice, computing these spaces of syzygies only involves basic linear algebra:
they can be constructed iteratively, as the kernel of some generalized Macaulay matrices.
This can be done efficiently, except for the fact that the dimension of the spaces involved grows
exponentially.
Our last ingredient is then
shortening
, which allows us to work with syzygies of a lesser order,
and keep these dimensions more under control.

Let us quickly illustrate our result with two basic examples.

First, our distinguisher can be seen as a generalization of the so-called
square code
distinguisher,
first presented in
[
15
]
, reinterpreted in
[
26
]
, and fully analyzed in
[
30
]
.
Let
{\mathsf{C}}
be a
[n,k]
-code, and
S_{2}
the space of quadratic forms in
k
indeterminates.
Let

\operatorname{ev}_{2}:S_{2}\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}^{n}

(1)

be the evaluation map at the columns of a given generator matrix of
{\mathsf{C}}
.
Then the image of
\operatorname{ev}_{2}
is the square code
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}
, and its kernel is the space of quadratic relations
I_{2}({\mathsf{C}})
.
The dimensions of these spaces are related, and can be expressed as a Betti number:

\beta_{1,2}({\mathsf{C}})=\dim(I_{2}({\mathsf{C}}))=\binom{k+1}{2}-\dim({\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}).

(2)

Now
[
15
]
gives a lower bound on this
\beta_{1,2}({\mathsf{C}})
when
{\mathsf{C}}
is the dual of an alternant or (binary) Goppa code.
On the other hand,
[
4
]
shows
\beta_{1,2}({\mathsf{C}})=\left(\binom{k+1}{2}-n\right)^{+}
with high probability when
{\mathsf{C}}
is random.
If this quantity is smaller than the said lower bound, we can distinguish.

Likewise we claim that Theorem 2.8 of
[
13
]
provides a
\beta_{2,3}
-based distinguisher for
\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}
codes
among MDS
[7,4]
-codes.
Indeed, for such codes, the square always fills the whole space — so the square distinguisher does not apply —
and we have
\dim(I_{2}({\mathsf{C}}))=\binom{4+1}{2}-7=3
.
Let
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
be a basis of
I_{2}({\mathsf{C}})
.
By definition,
Q_{1},Q_{2},Q_{3}
do not satisfy linear relations with coefficients in
\mathbb{F}
, however they can satisfy
relations whose coefficients are forms of degree
1
.
Such relations are also called degree
3
syzygies.
They live in the kernel of the degree
3
Macaulay matrix

\mathbf{M}_{3}:I_{2}({\mathsf{C}})\otimes S_{1}\longrightarrow S_{3}

(3)

where
S_{1}
(resp.
S_{3}
) is the space of homogeneous linear (resp. cubic) forms in
k=4
variables.
Now Theorem 2.8 of
[
13
]
shows that for a non-
\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}
code the map
\mathbf{M}_{3}
is injective.
On the other hand, if
{\mathsf{C}}
is
\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}
with standard basis
\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{2},\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{3}

then we can take

Q_{1}=X_{1}X_{3}-X_{2}^{2},\quad Q_{2}=X_{1}X_{4}-X_{2}X_{3},\quad Q_{3}=X_{2}X_{4}-X_{3}^{2}

(4)

and these satisfy the syzygies

X_{1}Q_{3}-X_{2}Q_{2}+X_{3}Q_{1}\;=\;X_{2}Q_{3}-X_{3}Q_{2}+X_{4}Q_{1}\;=\;0.

(5)

Thus we can distinguish by computing
\beta_{2,3}({\mathsf{C}})=\dim\ker(\mathbf{M}_{3})
,
which will yield
2
for
\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}
and
0
for non-
\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}
MDS
[7,4]
-codes.

We generalize these examples to higher Betti numbers, following the exact same pattern:

•

On one hand, we give lower bounds on the Betti numbers of algebraic codes (dual alternant, dual Goppa, and their shortened subcodes).
This is done in section
3
, using the Eagon-Northcott complex, a tool precisely crafted to detect long conjugate
\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}
subcodes.

•

On the other hand, we estimate the Betti numbers of random codes in terms of raw code parameters (length, dimension, distance).
This is done in section
4
, partially relying on a natural heuristic: random codes are not expected to admit more syzygies than those forced by these parameters.
We do not have full proofs for this fact, but we provide experimental evidence and partial theoretical arguments that support it.

Prior to that, section
2
explains how these invariants can be effectively computed.
Last, section
5
combines everything and chooses parameters in order to optimize asymptotic complexity.
Of special importance in this regard is Proposition
4
from section
3
,
which shows that the property that the ideal of a code contains minors of a matrix of linear forms passes to its shortened subcodes.

Related (and unrelated) works

As already noted, our distinguisher can be seen as a generalization of the square distinguisher of
[
15
]
.
Using an approach similar to ours, the work
[
10
]
also extends this square distinguisher
by exploiting special properties of the space of quadratic relations, but in a different direction:
the authors detect the existence of short relations by computing the Hilbert series of a convenient Pfaffian modeling
(and in some cases they even manage to recover the key).
Last, the key recovery attack in
[
2
]
combines shortening of the dual code
with ideas from the square distinguisher to reduce parameters, and then performs a careful algebraic modeling exploiting
the symmetries of a system, that can finally be solved with Gröbner basis algorithms.
All these results have limited range of applicability, but they introduced numerous techniques
that influenced the present work.

After the first version of this text appeared, other works continued this series of results.
A refined analysis of the Pfaffian distinguisher can now be found in
[
24
]
.
Using purely geometric methods,
[
23
]
presents a new key recovery attack; but quite
surprisingly, this attack works precisely in the regimes where those of
[
10
]
and
[
2
]
apply!
Last, the recent work
[
41
]
proposes a distinguisher based on higher degree relations vanishing with
higher multiplicities.

From a geometric point of view, the Betti numbers and the syzygies we consider are those
of a set of points in projective space (namely, defined by the columns of a parity check matrix of the code).
As such, they have been already extensively studied. Of notable importance to us are the works

[
19
]
[
25
]
[
21
]
[
14
]
,
in relation with the so-called
minimal resolution conjecture
— regardless of it being false in general:
we just request it being “true enough”.
Initially, syzygies of sets of points were considered as a mere tool in the study of syzygies of curves.
They were then studied for themselves, but the focus was mostly on points in generic position,
over an algebraically closed field.
Keeping applications to coding theory and cryptography in mind, we will have more interest
in finite field effects.

Syzygies sometimes appear as a tool in cryptanalytic works, or in the study of Gröbner basis algorithms;
however in general only the first module of syzygies is considered, not those of higher order.
Likewise, a few works in coding theory (such as
[
38
]
or
[
20
]
) use homological
properties of finite sets of points; but the applications differ from ours.

Last, note that our approach is apparently unrelated to the series of works initiated with
[
22
]
:
while these authors also define Betti numbers for codes, these are constructed
from the Stanley-Reisner ring of the code matroid, not the homogeneous coordinate ring.
This leads to different theories, although seeking links between the two could be an interesting project.

The book

For codes, especially the link between powers of codes and the geometric view on coding theory, we will follow

]

Given a field
\mathbb{F}
, we see
\mathbb{F}^{n}
as the standard product algebra of dimension

.
Thus
\mathbb{F}^{n}
is not a mere vector space, it comes canonically equipped with componentwise
multiplication: for
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
and
\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\dots,y_{n})
in

,

\mathbf{x}\mathbf{y}=(x_{1}y_{1},\dots,x_{n}y_{n}).

(6)

(Some authors call this the Schur product of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
;
how the name of this great mathematician ended associated with this trivial operation
is quite
convoluted
.)

In any algebra, we can define a trace bilinear form. In the case of
\mathbb{F}^{n}
, this trace
bilinear form is the standard scalar product:

\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle=x_{1}y_{1}+\cdots+x_{n}y_{n}.

(7)

A
k
-dimensional subspace
{\mathsf{C}}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
is called a
[n,k]
-code (and a
[n,k]_{q}
-code in case
\mathbb{F}=\mathbb{F}_{q}
).
The orthogonal space
{\mathsf{C}}^{\perp}
is called the
dual code
of
{\mathsf{C}}
.

Componentwise multiplication extends to codes, taking the linear span:
for
{\mathsf{C}},{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}
,

{\mathsf{C}}{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime}=\langle\mathbf{c}\mathbf{c^{\prime}}:\>\mathbf{c}\in{\mathsf{C}},\,\mathbf{c^{\prime}}\in{\mathsf{C}}^{\prime}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}}.

(8)

Powers
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r\rangle}
of a code are defined inductively:
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 0\rangle}=\mathbb{F}\cdot\mathbf{1}

is the
1
-dimensional repetition code, and
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r+1\rangle}={\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r\rangle}{\mathsf{C}}
.

If
{\mathsf{C}}
is a
[n,k]
-code, a generator matrix for
{\mathsf{C}}
is a
k\times n
matrix
\mathbf{G}

whose rows
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k}
form a basis of
{\mathsf{C}}
.
A parity check matrix
\mathbf{H}
for
{\mathsf{C}}
is a generator matrix for
{\mathsf{C}}^{\perp}
.

Thanks to the algebra structure, polynomials in one or several variables can be
evaluated in
\mathbb{F}^{n}
. In particular, let

S=\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\dots,X_{k}]

(9)

be the algebra of polynomials in
k

\mathbb{F}
, graded by total degree.
Evaluation at the rows
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k}
of
\mathbf{G}
then gives linear map

\operatorname{ev}_{\mathbf{G}}:S\longrightarrow\mathbb{F}^{n}.

(10)

Observe that if
\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{n}
are the columns of
\mathbf{G}
,
then for
f(X_{1},\dots,X_{k})\in S
we have

\begin{split}\operatorname{ev}_{\mathbf{G}}(f)&=f(\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k})\\
&=(f(\mathbf{p}_{1}),\dots,f(\mathbf{p}_{n}))\end{split}

(11)

where in the first line we have one evaluation of
f
at a
k
-tuple of vectors,
while in the second line we have a vector of evaluations of
f
at
k
-tuples of scalars.

A code is projective if it has dual minimum distance
\operatorname{d}_{\min}({\mathsf{C}}^{\perp})\geq 3
,
or equivalently if no two of the
\mathbf{p}_{i}
are proportional. Any code can be “projectivized”
by discarding (puncturing) coordinates, keeping only one
\mathbf{p}_{i}

Restricting to homogeneous polynomials of degree

, we have a
surjective
map
S_{r}\longrightarrow{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r\rangle}
,
whose kernel we denote

.
We then define the homogeneous coordinate ring of

as the formal direct sum

{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}=\bigoplus_{r\geq 0}{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r\rangle}

(12)

and likewise its homogeneous ideal

I({\mathsf{C}})=\bigoplus_{r\geq 0}I_{r}({\mathsf{C}}).

(13)

It turns out these are also the homogeneous coordinate ring and the homogeneous ideal of the finite set of points

\{\mathbf{p}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{p}_{n}\}\subseteq\mathbf{P}^{k-1}.

(14)

The short exact sequence

0\longrightarrow I({\mathsf{C}})\longrightarrow S\longrightarrow{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}\longrightarrow 0

(15)

makes
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}
a homogeneous quotient ring of
S
.
In this work we will use coordinates, but identifying
S
with the symmetric algebra of
{\mathsf{C}}

would allow to make all this coordinatefree.

Given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}^{n}
,

\mathbf{x}
distinct, all entries of
\mathbf{y}

the

with support vector
\mathbf{x}

and multiplier

is

\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\mathbf{y},\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x},\dots,\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^{k-1}\rangle_{\mathbb{F}}=\{\mathbf{y}f(\mathbf{x}):\>f(X)\in\mathbb{F}[X]_{<k}\}\subseteq\mathbb{F}^{n}.

(16)

It is a
[n,k]
-code if

.

Now let
\mathbb{F}_{q}\subseteq\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}
be an extension of finite fields.
Given
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in(\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}})^{n}

(17)

with parameters
[n,(\geq)n-mt]_{q}
.

Last, given a polynomial

(18)

We will work mostly in the class

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Alt}}}^{\perp}_{q,m,n,t}

of

-ary alternant codes of extension degree

(20)

In this case, after extension of scalars, we have

{\mathsf{C}}_{\mathbb{F}_{q^{m}}}=\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}_{t}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\oplus\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{q},\mathbf{y}^{q})\oplus\cdots\oplus\operatorname{{\mathsf{GRS}}}_{t}(\mathbf{x}^{q^{m-1}},\mathbf{y}^{q^{m-1}}).

Likewise we define the corresponding class

\operatorname{{\mathsf{Gop}}}^{\perp}_{q,m,n,t}

(22)

Also we define subclasses
\operatorname{{\mathsf{Gop}}}^{\operatorname{irr},\perp}_{q,m,n,t}\subseteq\operatorname{{\mathsf{Gop}}}^{\operatorname{sqfr},\perp}_{q,m,n,t}\subseteq\operatorname{{\mathsf{Gop}}}^{\perp}_{q,m,n,t}

2
Minimal resolutions and graded Betti numbers

Generalities

We freely borrow results and terminology from
[
13
]
, and then elaborate on the parts of the theory that we will need.

Let
S=\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\dots,X_{k}]
be the
k
-dimensional polynomial ring over
\mathbb{F}
, graded by total degree.
If
M_{0}
is a finitely generated graded
S
-module, and
F_{0}
is the free module on a minimal system
of homogeneous generators of
M_{0}
, then the (first) syzygy module of
M_{0}
is
M_{1}=\ker(\;F_{0}\longrightarrow M_{0}\;)
.
Concretely, if
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
are minimal generators of
M_{0}
, elements of
F_{0}
can be seen as formal
sums
\sum_{u}f_{u}[g_{u}]
with
f_{u}\in S
, where the
[g_{u}]
are just formal symbols;
such a formal sum then lies in
M_{1}
when the actual sum evaluated in
M_{0}
satisfies
\sum_{u}f_{u}g_{u}=0
.

Iterating this construction, we obtain a minimal resolution

\cdots\longrightarrow F_{2}\longrightarrow F_{1}\longrightarrow F_{0}

(23)

of
M_{0}
,
where the graded free modules
F_{i}
, together with the iterated syzygy modules
M_{i}
, are constructed inductively:

•

F_{i}
is the free module on a minimal system of homogeneous generators of
M_{i}

•

M_{i+1}=\ker(\;F_{i}\longrightarrow M_{i}\;)
.

The number

of degree

does not depend on

.
Keeping track of the grading, we have

F_{i}=\bigoplus_{j\geq 0}S(-j)^{\beta_{i,j}}

where

is the free rank
1
module generated in degree

S(-j)_{d}=S_{d-j}
.

It is customary to display the graded Betti numbers in the form of a Betti diagram, as follows:

\begin{array}[]{c|ccccc}&0&1&\dots&i&\dots\\
\hline\cr\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\\
r&\beta_{0,r}&\beta_{1,r+1}&\dots&\beta_{i,r+i}&\dots\\
r+1&\beta_{0,r+1}&\beta_{1,r+2}&\dots&\beta_{i,r+i+1}&\dots\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&&\vdots&\\
\end{array}

with null entries marked as “

Lemma 1

Assume
M_{i}
is generated in degrees
\geq D
, i.e.
\beta_{i,j}=0
for all
j\leq D-1
.
Then
M_{i+1}
is generated in degrees
\geq D+1
,
and by induction all the upper-right quadrant of the Betti diagram defined by
\beta_{i,D-1}
vanishes.

Proof

Let
g_{1},\dots,g_{N}
form a minimal system of generators of
M_{i}
.
By hypothesis all
g_{u}
have degree
\geq D
.
Then, in a homogeneous relation
\sum_{u}f_{u}g_{u}=0
, no
f_{u}
can be a nonzero constant:
otherwise the corresponding
g_{u}
could be expressed as a linear combination of the others,
hence could be removed from the system of generators, contradicting minimality.
Thus all nonzero
f_{u}
have degree
\geq 1
,
and the element of
M_{i+1}
defined by this homogeneous relation has degree
\geq D+1
.

As
M_{i}
and

are graded

-modules, we will also write

and

for their degree

From now on let

be a

,

So we have an exact sequence

and the Betti diagram has

As

Likewise

,

is the number of homogeneous polynomials of degree

As the evaluation map

Effective computation of linear syzygies

There are several algorithms to compute minimal resolutions and graded Betti numbers in general.
Many of them rely first on a Gröbner basis computation.
However in this work we will only be interested in computing the first (nontrivial) row of the Betti diagram,
or equivalently, the so-called
linear strand
of the resolution.
This easier computation can be described in elementary terms.
First, for any
r\geq 3
, consider the natural multiplication map

\varphi_{r}:M_{r-2,r-1}\otimes S_{1}\longrightarrow M_{r-2,r}.

(27)

Lemma 4

We have:

\displaystyle\ker(\varphi_{r})=M_{r-1,r}

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{F}^{\beta_{r-1,r}}

(28)

\displaystyle\coker(\varphi_{r})

\displaystyle\simeq\mathbb{F}^{\beta_{r-2,r}}.

(29)

Proof

We prove (
29
) first.
Let
\mathcal{G}
be a minimal system of homogeneous generators of
M_{r-2}
, and
for each
j

B_{r-2,j}\subseteq M_{r-2,j}
be the linear subspace generated by the degree
j

\mathcal{G}
.
Then, as
M_{r-2}
is generated in degrees
\geq r-1
, we have
B_{r-2,r-1}=M_{r-2,r-1}
,
and
B_{r-2,r}
is a complementary subspace to
S_{1}\cdot B_{r-2,r-1}=\im(\varphi_{r})
in

. This proves (
29
).

Now let
F_{r-2}
be the free graded module on
\mathcal{G}
.
Then
M_{r-1,r}
is the kernel of the natural map
F_{r-2,r}\longrightarrow M_{r-2,r}
.
However, under the decompositions

and

,

\varphi_{r}\oplus\operatorname{id}_{B_{r-2,r}}

This readily gives a coarse upper bound on the

Lemma 5

We have
\beta_{1,2}\leq\frac{k(k-1)}{2}

\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})\leq\frac{k}{2}(k-1)^{r-1}

Proof

We have

.
Now let

, multiplication by

\beta_{r-1,r}=k\beta_{r-2,r-1}-\dim\im(\varphi_{r})\leq(k-1)\beta_{r-2,r-1}

Further properties

We will have a particular interest in the length of the linear strand, or equivalently, in the following quantity:

Definition 4

For a linear code
{\mathsf{C}}
, we set

r_{\max}({\mathsf{C}})=\max\{r:\>\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})>0\}.

(36)

As the minimal resolution of a
[n,k]
-code
{\mathsf{C}}
has length
k-1
, we always have
r_{\max}({\mathsf{C}})\leq k
.

Sometimes we only control the syzygies of certain subideals of
I({\mathsf{C}})
, and from
these we want to deduce information on the syzygies of the whole ideal.
Intuitively, we expect that syzygies between elements of the subideals could be seen as syzygies between elements of the ideal.
Of course this fails in general, but it remains true if we restrict to the linear strand:

Proposition 2

Let
M
be a finite
S
-module generated in degrees
\geq d_{0}
, and
suppose its degree
d_{0}
part
M_{d_{0}}
decomposes as a direct sum

M_{d_{0}}=A_{d_{0}}\oplus B_{d_{0}}.

(37)

Let
A=\langle A_{d_{0}}\rangle_{S}
and
B=\langle B_{d_{0}}\rangle_{S}
be the sub-
S
-modules of
M
generated by
A_{d_{0}}
and
B_{d_{0}}
.
Let
\widehat{M}
be the (first) syzygy module of
M
, and
\widehat{A}
and
\widehat{B}
those of
A
and
B
.
Then we have a natural inclusion

\widehat{A}_{d_{0}+1}\oplus\widehat{B}_{d_{0}+1}\;\subseteq\;\widehat{M}_{d_{0}+1}

(38)

the cokernel of which identifies with
A_{d_{0}+1}\cap B_{d_{0}+1}
, hence a (non-canonical) isomorphism

\widehat{M}_{d_{0}+1}\;\simeq\;\widehat{A}_{d_{0}+1}\oplus\widehat{B}_{d_{0}+1}\oplus(A_{d_{0}+1}\cap B_{d_{0}+1}).

(39)

Proof

We have surjective maps

(A_{d_{0}}\oplus B_{d_{0}})\otimes S_{1}\;\twoheadrightarrow\;A_{d_{0}+1}\oplus B_{d_{0}+1}\;\twoheadrightarrow\;A_{d_{0}+1}+B_{d_{0}+1}

(40)

with
\widehat{A}_{d_{0}+1}\oplus\widehat{B}_{d_{0}+1}
the kernel of the leftmost map,

A_{d_{0}+1}\cap B_{d_{0}+1}
the kernel of the rightmost map,
and
\widehat{M}_{d_{0}+1}
the kernel of the composite map.
We conclude with the associated kernel-cokernel exact sequence.

Suppose

contains a certain direct sum of

I_{2}({\mathsf{C}})\supseteq V^{(1)}\oplus\cdots\oplus V^{(l)}.

(41)

Then the minimal resolution of
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}
canonically admits the direct sum
of the linear strands of the minimal resolutions of
S/\langle V^{(1)}\rangle
, …,
S/\langle V^{(l)}\rangle

as a subcomplex.
In particular for all

we have

\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})\;\geq\;\beta_{r-1,r}(S/\langle V^{(1)}\rangle)+\cdots+\beta_{r-1,r}(S/\langle V^{(l)}\rangle).

(42)

Proof

Apply Proposition
2

is a punctured code of
{\mathsf{C}}
if it is obtained from
{\mathsf{C}}

Generalities

Let
R
be a ring, and for integers

let
\mathbf{\Phi}\in R^{g\times f}
define a linear map

\mathbf{\Phi}:F=R^{f}\longrightarrow G=R^{g}.

The Eagon-Northcott complex
[

]
of

is the following complex of

defined in terms of the exterior and (dual of) symmetric powers of

and

0\to(\Sym^{f-g}G)^{\vee}\otimes\bigwedge^{f}F\to\cdots\to G^{\vee}\otimes\bigwedge^{g+1}F\to\bigwedge^{g}F\overset{\bigwedge^{g}\mathbf{\Phi}}{\longrightarrow}\bigwedge^{g}G\simeq R.

(46)

Under mild hypotheses, this complex is exact
[
13
, Th. A.2.60 & Th. 6.4]
,
so it defines a resolution of the quotient
R/\im(\bigwedge^{g}\mathbf{\Phi})

defined by the ideal generated by the maximal minors of
\mathbf{\Phi}
.

In this work we will only need the case

. In this case the complex has length

,
and for

its

-th module is free of rank

\rk\left((\Sym^{r-2}G)^{\vee}\otimes\bigwedge^{r}F\right)\;=\;(r-1)\binom{f}{r}.

(47)

Let

\mathbf{\Phi}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}x_{1}&x_{2}&\dots&x_{f}\\

. Then:

•

\im(\bigwedge^{2}\mathbf{\Phi})
is generated by the
\binom{f}{2}

q_{i,j}=x_{i}x^{\prime}_{j}-x_{j}x^{\prime}_{i}

(49)

for
1\leq i<j\leq f
,

•

these

are annihilated by the

\begin{split}r_{ijk}&=x_{i}q_{jk}-x_{j}q_{ik}+x_{k}q_{ij}\\
r^{\prime}_{ijk}&=x^{\prime}_{i}q_{jk}-x^{\prime}_{j}q_{ik}+x^{\prime}_{k}q_{ij}\end{split}

(50)

for
1\leq i<j<k\leq f
,

•

these
r_{ijk}

Shortening (or projection from a point)

We have interest in how this Eagon-Northcott complex interacts with reduction to a subcode,
and in particular with shortening. By induction it suffices to consider the case of
a codimension
1
subcode, or a
1
-shortening respectively.

So let
{\mathsf{C}}
be a
[n,k]
-code over
\mathbb{F}
, and
{\mathsf{C}}_{\mathcal{H}}

We will assume

projective, but then

Choose a basis

Let

Recall from

1.

1

Further results on syzygies of dual alternant codes

It is possible to improve the lower bound on
\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}}_{s})
, using not only
\mathbf{\Phi}=\mathbf{\Phi}^{(0)}
, but also
its conjugates
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(1)},\dots,\mathbf{\Phi}^{(m-1)}
.
Unfortunately we only have very partial results in this direction.
These partial results will not be used in the remainder of the text, but we present them nevertheless in the hope that
they could motivate further study.

Let us first consider the simpler case
s=0
, i.e. without shortening.
Let then
{\mathsf{C}}
be a proper dual alternant code as in Theorem
3.1
.

The Eagon-Northcott complex of each conjugate
\mathbf{\Phi}^{(u)}
is a subcomplex of the minimal resolution of
{\mathsf{C}}
,
so that in each degree
r
we get a subspace

V_{r}=M_{r-1,r}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{(0)})+\cdots+M_{r-1,r}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{(m-1)})\subseteq M_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}}),

(69)

hence
\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})\geq\dim V_{r}
. Two questions then arise naturally:

(Q1)

Is the sum in (
69

M_{r-1,r}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{(u)})
in
M_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})
?

(Q2)

Is the inclusion
V_{r}\subseteq M_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})
an equality, i.e. do the
M_{r-1,r}(\mathbf{\Phi}^{(u)})
together generate
M_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})

We can answer these questions in degree

.

Definition 5

For any

we let

I_{2}^{(u,v)}\subseteq I_{2}({\mathsf{C}})

Observe that

. Thus, in order to avoid these

appearing twice, we will impose some conditions
on the pairs

we consider. Say, we consider only those where

0\leq w<m/2
,
and also in case

However this monomial does not appear in the

Regularity
2
and consequences

If
{\mathsf{C}}
is a
[n,k]
-code, we let

B_{j}=\sum_{i\geq 0}(-1)^{i}\beta_{i,j}

(96)

be the alternating sum of its Betti numbers degree
j
,
and
B(z)=\sum_{j\geq 0}B_{j}z^{j}
their generating polynomial (it is indeed a finite sum).

Let also
H_{{\mathsf{C}}}(z)=\sum_{r\geq 0}z^{r}\dim{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r\rangle}
be the Hilbert series of
{\mathsf{C}}
.

Proposition 8

We have

B(z)=(1-z)^{k}H_{{\mathsf{C}}}(z).

(97)

Proof

Generating series reformulation of
[
13
, Cor. 1.10]
.

Definition 6
(
[
36
, Def. 1.5 & Th. 2.35]
)

The Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of a projective
[n,k]
-code
{\mathsf{C}}
is the smallest integer
r

such that
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle r\rangle}=\mathbb{F}^{n}
.

Definition 7
(cf.
[
13
, §4A]
, after
[
31
, Lect. 14]
)

The Castelnuovo-Mumford regularity of
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}
is
\max\{r:\>\exists i,\,\beta_{i,i+r}({\mathsf{C}})>0\}
.

Proposition 9
(
[
13
, Th. 4.2]
)

These two definitions coincide.

From now on we will just say “regularity” for short.

The square code distinguisher, and the filtration attack from
[
2
]
that extends it,
work for codes
{\mathsf{C}}
with
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}\varsubsetneq\mathbb{F}^{n}
, i.e. of regularity
>2
.
This means that codes of regularity
2
are hard to deal with under this approach.
On the opposite, for us, codes of regularity
2
are nice because Definition
7

means their Betti diagram is simple: it has only two nontrivial rows.
Observe that most codes of interest have regularity
2
(with the notable exception of self-dual codes).

Definition 8

If
f:U\longrightarrow V
is a linear map between finite dimensional
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces, we define its index

\begin{split}\operatorname{ind}(f)&=\dim(U)-\dim(V)\\
&=\dim\ker(f)-\dim\coker(f)\end{split}

(98)

and its rank defect

\begin{split}\operatorname{def}(f)&=\min(\dim(U),\dim(V))-\rk(f)\\
&=\min(\dim\ker(f),\dim\coker(f)).\end{split}

(99)

For any real
x
we set
x^{+}=\max(x,0)
and
x^{-}=(-x)^{+}
, so
x=x^{+}-x^{-}
.
Then we always have

\displaystyle\dim\ker(f)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{ind}(f)^{+}

(100)

\displaystyle\dim\coker(f)

\displaystyle\geq\operatorname{ind}(f)^{-}

(101)

and then

\operatorname{def}(f)=\dim\ker(f)-\operatorname{ind}(f)^{+}=\dim\coker(f)-\operatorname{ind}(f)^{-}

(102)

measures the distance to equality in these two inequalities.

For
r\geq 3
, recall the linear map
\varphi_{r}:M_{r-2,r-1}\otimes S_{1}\longrightarrow M_{r-2,r}
from (
27
).
It will also be handy to let
\varphi_{2}=\operatorname{ev}_{\mathbf{G},2}:S_{2}\longrightarrow{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}
be the evaluation map.

Theorem 4.1

For all
r\geq 2
we have

\displaystyle\dim\ker(\varphi_{r})

\displaystyle=\beta_{r-1,r}

(103)

\displaystyle\dim\coker(\varphi_{r})

\displaystyle=\beta_{r-2,r}

(104)

and moreover, if
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}=\mathbb{F}^{n}
, then

\operatorname{ind}(\varphi_{r})=\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{r}-n\right)\binom{k-1}{r-2}

(105)

Proof

For
r=2
this is proved directly. Now assume
r\geq 3
.
Then the first two equalities are reformulations of Lemma
4
.
Now assume moreover
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}=\mathbb{F}^{n}
.
Then
H_{{\mathsf{C}}}(z)=1+kz+n\frac{z^{2}}{1-z}
,
so
B(z)=(1+kz)(1-z)^{k}+nz^{2}(1-z)^{k-1}
by Proposition
8
,
thus
B_{r}=(-1)^{r-1}(r-1)\binom{k+1}{r}+(-1)^{r}n\binom{k-1}{r-2}=(-1)^{r-1}\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{r}-n\right)\binom{k-1}{r-2}
.
On the other hand,
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}=\mathbb{F}^{n}
also means
{\mathsf{C}}
has regularity
2
in the sense of Definition
7
,
so (
96
) reduces to
B_{r}=(-1)^{r-1}\beta_{r-1,r}+(-1)^{r-2}\beta_{r-2,r}=(-1)^{r-1}\operatorname{ind}(\varphi_{r})

and we conclude.

Corollary 6

If
{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}=\mathbb{F}^{n}
, then the bottom right entry of its Betti diagram is

\beta_{k-1,k+1}=n-k.

(106)

Proof

The minimal resolution of
{\mathsf{C}}
has length
k-1
, so
\beta_{i,j}=0
for

,
hence
\beta_{k-1,k+1}=-\operatorname{ind}(\varphi_{k+1})=n-k
.

[n,k]
-code

(
[

Let

The small defect heuristic

Because of (
103
), a necessary condition for a
[n,k]
-code
{\mathsf{C}}
of regularity
2

to have
\beta_{r-1,r}=0
is that
\operatorname{ind}(\varphi_{r})\leq 0
.
This immediately yields

r_{\max}({\mathsf{C}})\geq\lceil r_{\operatorname{crit}}({\mathsf{C}})\rceil-1

(108)

where we define the
critical degree
of
{\mathsf{C}}

r_{\operatorname{crit}}({\mathsf{C}})=\frac{k(k+1)}{n}

(109)

as the value of
r
at which the formula (
105
) for
\operatorname{ind}(\varphi_{r})
changes sign.

More precisely, (
100
) and Theorem
4.1
give the lower bound

\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})\geq\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{r}-n\right)^{+}\binom{k-1}{r-2}

(110)

that can be interpreted as the number of “contingent” syzygies that are forced to exist,
solely because of the dimension difference between the source and target spaces of the map
\varphi_{r}
.
Then, when
{\mathsf{C}}
is random, it is quite natural to expect that no other
syzygy will appear, or equivalently that (
110
) will be an equality, i.e.
\operatorname{def}(\varphi_{r})=0
.

This is compatible with results such as those in
[
7
]
and the references within,
that give estimates on the distribution of the rank, hence the defect, of random linear maps,
under various probability laws.
Unfortunately, even if the probability distribution of
{\mathsf{C}}
is nice
(say, uniform among codes of given
[n,k]
), it is not easy to control the distribution of
\varphi_{r}
,
so we will not be able to give proofs.
Moreover it could be that the iterative algebraic process in the construction of
\varphi_{r}

would lead to some unexpected constraints on its rank.
And indeed, in this section we will identify some parameter ranges for which the defect
cannot
be small;
but conversely, we will also give arguments that support the validity of this small defect heuristic

at least under some proper conditions
.

A first argument in support of the heuristic is that it is unconditionally true when
r=2
.
Indeed, in this special case,
[
4
]
manages to give lower bounds, exponentially close to
1
,
on the probability that
\operatorname{def}(\varphi_{2})=0
.

Another argument is the
minimal resolution conjecture
of
[
25
]
.
It postulates that, over an infinite field, a Zariski-generic code satisfies

\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})=0
for
r\geq r_{\operatorname{crit}}({\mathsf{C}})

and
\beta_{r-2,r}({\mathsf{C}})=0
for
r\leq r_{\operatorname{crit}}({\mathsf{C}})
,
or equivalently,
\operatorname{def}(\varphi_{r})=0
for all
r\geq 2
.
However, two points require our attention:

1.

This conjecture is now known to be false in general
[
14
]
.

2.

We work over a finite field, not an infinite one.

Concerning point
1
, we will argue that the conjecture is still “true enough” for our use.
First, a nonzero defect might not be a problem in our Betti number estimates, as long as it remains small.
Moreover, as noted in the introduction of
[
14
]
, the conjecture has been proved for a large range
of values of
n
and
k
. In fact, although
[
14
]
provides an infinity of counterexamples,
these remain limited to very specific parameters, namely of the form
n=k+O(\sqrt{k})
.
And indeed, perhaps the most valuable result for us is
[
21
]
, which proves that the conjecture is true
when
n
is large enough with respect to
k
.

Concerning point
2
, clearly, codes behave quite differently over a finite field
and over an infinite field.
For instance, generic codes over an infinite field are MDS for any parameter set, while over a finite field
they clearly are not.
This makes it desirable to investigate links between Betti numbers and distance properties of a code.
At this moment the author mostly has only experimental results in this direction:
Actually, experiments suggest much stronger results hold.
For any code
{\mathsf{C}}
, let us denote by
A_{i}({\mathsf{C}})
the number of codewords of Hamming weight
i
in
{\mathsf{C}}
.
Also recall
r_{\operatorname{crit}}=\frac{k(k+1)}{n}
.

Experimental fact 1

Let
{\mathsf{C}}
be a
[n,k]_{q}
-code of regularity
2
, with minimum distance
d=\operatorname{d}_{\min}({\mathsf{C}})
and dual minimum distance
d^{\perp}=\operatorname{d}_{\min}({\mathsf{C}}^{\perp})
.

1.

For all
r\geq d^{\perp}
we have
\beta_{r-2,r}({\mathsf{C}})>0
.

Moreover if
d^{\perp}\leq r_{\operatorname{crit}}
we have
\beta_{d^{\perp}-2,d^{\perp}}({\mathsf{C}})\geq A_{d^{\perp}}({\mathsf{C}}^{\perp})/(q-1)
(and there are numerous examples of
{\mathsf{C}}
where this inequality is an equality).

2.

Dually, for all
r\leq k+1-d
we have
\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})>0
.

Moreover if
k+1-d\geq r_{\operatorname{crit}}
we have
\beta_{k-d,k+1-d}({\mathsf{C}})\geq A_{d}({\mathsf{C}})/(q-1)
(and there are numerous examples of
{\mathsf{C}}
where this inequality is an equality).

It follows that
\operatorname{def}(\varphi_{r})>0
for
d^{\perp}\leq r\leq r_{\operatorname{crit}}
and for
r_{\operatorname{crit}}\leq\ r\leq k+1-d
, when applicable (i.e. when these intervals are nonempty).

Experimental fact 2

Conversely, for a
random
code
{\mathsf{C}}
among codes of given parameters
[n,k,d,d^{\perp}]_{q}
,
the probability that
\operatorname{def}(\varphi_{r})=0
tends quickly to
1

as
r\ll\min(d^{\perp},r_{\operatorname{crit}})
and as
r\gg\max(r_{\operatorname{crit}},k+1-d)
.

In particular
r_{\max}({\mathsf{C}})=\max(\lfloor r_{\operatorname{crit}}\rfloor,k+1-d)
, or is very close to this value, with high probability.

Actually, as a modest first step, the author is able to prove the first assertion in point 2. of Experimental fact
1
:

Proposition 12

Any
[n,k,d]
code
{\mathsf{C}}
has
r_{\max}({\mathsf{C}})\geq k+1-d
,
i.e.
\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})>0
for all
r\leq k+1-d
.

Proof

Let
\mathbf{c}_{1}
be a codeword of minimum weight
\operatorname{w}(\mathbf{c}_{1})=d
.
Let
S
be the support of
\mathbf{c}_{1}
, and let
{\mathsf{C}}_{S}\subseteq{\mathsf{C}}
be the subcode made
of all codewords that vanish over
S
. Then
\dim({\mathsf{C}}_{S})\geq k-\#{S}=k-d
, so we can find
k-d
linearly
independent codewords
\mathbf{c}_{2},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k+1-d}
in
{\mathsf{C}}_{S}
.
Let then complete
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k-d+1}
into a basis
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k}
of
{\mathsf{C}}
,
and use this basis to define the graded evaluation map
S=\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\dots,X_{k}]\longrightarrow{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}
.
By construction we have
\mathbf{c}_{1}\mathbf{c}_{2}=\cdots=\mathbf{c}_{1}\mathbf{c}_{k-d+1}=\mathbf{0}
,
which means
\langle X_{1}X_{2},\dots,X_{1}X_{k+1-d}\rangle\subseteq I_{2}({\mathsf{C}})
.
By Corollary
2
we deduce
\beta_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})\geq\beta_{r-1,r}(S/\langle X_{1}X_{2},\dots,X_{1}X_{k+1-d}\rangle)
for all

,
hence in particular
r_{\max}({\mathsf{C}})\geq r_{\max}(S/\langle X_{1}X_{2},\dots,X_{1}X_{k+1-d}\rangle)
.

However we have
\langle X_{1}X_{2},\dots,X_{1}X_{k+1-d}\rangle=X_{1}\langle X_{2},\dots,X_{k+1-d}\rangle
, so the
minimal resolution of
\langle X_{1}X_{2},\dots,X_{1}X_{k+1-d}\rangle
can be identified with the Koszul complex
on
X_{2},\dots,X_{k+1-d}
(with degree shifted by
1
), so its

is
k+1-d
.

Consequently, we postulate the following relaxation of the minimal resolution conjecture:

Heuristic 1

Fix a field cardinality
q
,
assume
n
is not too close to
k
in order to stay away from the counterexamples to the minimal resolution conjecture (e.g.
n>k+\sqrt{2k}+2
should suffice),
and
n<\binom{k+1}{2}
in order to ensure regularity
2
.
Then for random
[n,k]_{q}
-codes, with high probability:

1.

if
d>k+1-r_{\operatorname{crit}}
we expect
\beta_{r-1,r}=0
for
r>r_{\operatorname{crit}}

2.

if
d^{\perp}>r_{\operatorname{crit}}
we expect
\beta_{r-1,r}=\left(\frac{k(k+1)}{r}-n\right)\binom{k-1}{r-2}
for
r<r_{\operatorname{crit}}

where
r_{\operatorname{crit}}=\frac{k(k+1)}{n}
.

Remark 3

Consider this Heuristic in the asymptotic regime.
Setting
R=k/n
,
we can take
d=\operatorname{d}_{GV}(q,n,k)\approx H_{q}^{-1}(1-R)n
and
d^{\perp}=\operatorname{d}_{GV}(q,n,n-k)\approx H_{q}^{-1}(R)n
the corresponding Gilbert-Varshamov distances,
where
H_{q}
is the
q
-ary entropy function.
Then the condition in 1. translates as
H_{q}^{-1}(1-R)>R(1-R)
, and the condition in 2. translates as
H_{q}^{-1}(R)>R^{2}
,
both of which are satisfied when
R
is small enough.
In particular for
q=2
, we find that 1. is satisfied for
R<0.277
and 2. is satisfied for
R<0.141
.

5
The distinguisher

Basic version (without shortening)

Distinguishers typically work by computing certain code invariants.
We might have theoretical bounds on the values of these invariants, that are essential for an asymptotic analysis.
However these bounds need not be tight. Hence for a given set of finite parameters, we can also adopt a more empirical
approach: sample a certain number of codes, compute their invariants, and observe when the distinguisher “just works”.

So for given
q,m,t
and a type of codes (alternant or Goppa),
we set
n=q^{m}
and we compute the Betti numbers of a certain number of dual codes.
Most of the time, it turns out that these numbers are the same for all samples. We will denote
by
\beta_{r-1,r}^{*}
these common values.
By Corollary
3
, these
\beta_{r-1,r}^{*}
still provide lower bounds on
\beta_{r-1,r}
for smaller
n
.

For a given
r
with
\beta_{r-1,r}^{*}>0
, our distinguisher then works as follows.
Taking as input a (dual) code

.

is random.

Observe that for random codes of dimension

or equivalently when

When

[
15
]
.
Using syzygies of higher degree

In the following paragraphs we compare our new distinguisher (in this basic version) with the one from

, on the very same examples

On the other hand, for

A general description of the set of distinguishable parameters is complicated, as it has to encompass

However, a very interesting phenomenon will appear in the asymptotic analysis: all these constraints will be

Remark 4

The sheer fact that the minimum distance is easily computable for codes with such small parameters actually

An alternative approach through Koszul cohomology and the (block) Wiedemann algorithm

Koszul cohomology
[
18
]
allows to directly compute one syzygy space, with no need to iteratively
compute all the intermediate steps in the resolution.

Let
{\mathsf{C}}
be a
[n,k]
-code,
S=\mathbb{F}[X_{1},\dots,X_{k}]

the polynomial ring in
k
indeterminates,
and
\operatorname{ev}:S\longrightarrow{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}
the graded evaluation map associated
to some basis
\mathbf{c}_{1},\dots,\mathbf{c}_{k}
of
{\mathsf{C}}
.

Proposition 14

For any
r
, the syzygy space
M_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})
can be computed as the cohomology of the sequence

\bigwedge\nolimits^{r}S_{1}\overset{\partial}{\longrightarrow}\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{r-1}S_{1}\right)\otimes{\mathsf{C}}\overset{\partial^{\prime}}{\longrightarrow}\left(\bigwedge\nolimits^{r-2}S_{1}\right)\otimes{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}

(119)

(120)

where the linear maps
\partial,\partial^{\prime}
are defined on the standard basis elements as

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\partial:&\bigwedge^{r}S_{1}&\longrightarrow&(\bigwedge^{r-1}S_{1})\otimes{\mathsf{C}}\\
&X_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{i_{r}}&\mapsto&\sum_{j=1}^{r}(-1)^{j}(X_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\widehat{X}_{i_{j}}\cdots\wedge X_{i_{r}})\otimes\mathbf{c}_{i_{j}}\end{array}

(121)

and

\begin{array}[]{cccc}\partial^{\prime}:&(\bigwedge^{r-1}S_{1})\otimes{\mathsf{C}}&\longrightarrow&(\bigwedge^{r-2}S_{1})\otimes{\mathsf{C}}^{\langle 2\rangle}\\
&(X_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{i_{r-1}})\otimes\mathbf{c}_{l}&\mapsto&\sum_{j=1}^{r-1}(-1)^{j}(X_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\widehat{X}_{i_{j}}\cdots\wedge X_{i_{r-1}})\otimes(\mathbf{c}_{i_{j}}\mathbf{c}_{l})\end{array}

(122)

where notation
\widehat{X}_{i_{j}}
means that
X_{i_{j}}
is omitted.

Proof

Apply
[
18
, Th.(1.b.4)]
with
B={\mathsf{C}}^{\langle\cdot\rangle}
,
p=r-1
,
q=1

(or
[
13
, Prop. 2.7]
with

,

,

).

By construction
\partial,\partial^{\prime}
satisfy
\im(\partial)\subseteq\ker(\partial^{\prime})
,
and elementary linear algebra allow to rephrase (
120

M_{r-1,r}({\mathsf{C}})\simeq\ker(\partial^{\prime}|_{T})

(123)

where

is any complementary subspace to
\im(\partial)
in
(\bigwedge^{r-1}S_{1})\otimes{\mathsf{C}}
.

Let us denote the standard basis of

\mathbf{e}_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r-1};l}=(X_{i_{1}}\wedge\cdots\wedge X_{i_{r-1}})\otimes\mathbf{c}_{l}

Lemma 14

The subfamily of the basis (

Proof

First, a subfamily of a basis is linearly independent, hence a basis of its linear span

Next we show
\im(\partial)\cap T=0
. An element of

\sum_{1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{r}\leq k}\lambda_{i_{1},\dots,i_{r}}\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}(-1)^{j}\mathbf{e}_{i_{1},\dots\widehat{i_{j}}\dots,i_{r};i_{j}}.

If such an element is in

To conclude, we show that

On the other hand, if

Lemma 15

We have

\dim(T)=(r-1)\binom{k+1}{r}=\frac{k(k+1)}{r}\binom{k-1}{r-2}.

Proof

We give two proofs.
For the first proof, we count the number of indices

On the other hand, if we compute the index of

[

, in its basic version, probabilistically finds a preimage of a nonsingular linear map

with only black-box queries to

This means that the overall cost of the algorithm does not involve the cost of general linear algebra,
but only the cost

Several reductions provide variants of the algorithm that find a nonzero element in the kernel of a singular

at essentially the same cost.

This allows us to propose a “Koszul-Wiedemann” (KW) alternative implementation of the distinguisher.
We keep the setting of part 1 of Proposition

.
Then we apply Wiedemann’s algorithm to find a nonzero element in the kernel
of the linear map
\partial^{\prime}|_{T}
associated with
{\mathsf{C}}_{s}
and

:

(a)

if Wiedemann’s algorithm succeeds, we declare
{\mathsf{C}}
is in
\mathcal{T}_{q,m,n,t}

(b)

otherwise, we declare
{\mathsf{C}}
is probably random.

in case (b), the code
{\mathsf{C}}
is random with nonnegligible probability (and we can then iterate the procedure in order to make this probability become very close to
1
).

Each application of this procedure has complexity

\kappa_{KW}=O\left(n_{s}^{2}\binom{k_{s}}{r-2}\left((r-1)^{2}\binom{k_{s}+1}{r}+\binom{k_{s}}{r-2}\right)\right).

(131)

Proof

Correctness follows from the discussion just above, and part 1 of Proposition
13
.

Now consider the map
\partial^{\prime}|_{T}
associated with
{\mathsf{C}}_{s}
and
r=r^{*}-s
:

\partial^{\prime}|_{T}:T\longrightarrow(\bigwedge\nolimits^{r-2}S_{1})\otimes\mathbb{F}^{n_{s}}

(132)

where
S
now is the polynomial ring in
k_{s}
indeterminates.
By (
126
) the source space of this map has dimension

M=\dim(T)=(r-1)\binom{k_{s}+1}{r}

(133)

while on the other end the target space has dimension

N=n_{s}\binom{k_{s}}{r-2}.

(134)

Moreover, by formula (
122
), this map sends each basis vector of
T

to a vector of weight at most
(r-1)n_{s}
in the standard basis of the target space.
Thus the cost of evaluation of
\partial^{\prime}|_{T}
satisfies

c(\partial^{\prime}|_{T})\leq M(r-1)n_{s}.

(135)

Now the estimate for the complexity of Wiedemann’s algorithm is

O(N(c(\partial^{\prime}|_{T})+N))

(136)

and we conclude.

It is also possible to use the block version of Wiedemann’s algorithm
[
8
]
.
This definitely improves the complexity, but does not qualitatively change the theoretical
analysis (
131
) as this improvement can be incorporated in the constant in the big O notation.

Two tentative implementations of the KW version of the distinguisher can be found in
[
28
]
,
one with the original Wiedemann algorithm, one with the block Wiedemann algorithm.
It was especially tempting to leverage the very optimized implementation of block Wiedemann
that can be extracted from
[
39
]
. However several technical difficulties appear,
such as the fact that this implementation of block Wiedemann was not designed to make black-box queries to the
linear map, or also the fact that it uses heuristics adapted to the specifics of the linear
systems appearing in the number field sieve, while these heuristics can lead to an unexpected
behaviour in our use case.

Asymptotics

Fix a base field cardinality
q
, for instance
q=2
, and a (dual) rate
R
.
In
[
5
]
it is suggested to take a primal code of rate between
0.7
and
0.8
,
so passing to the dual gives
0.2\leq R\leq 0.3
. However here we allow
any

R
.
Then for
n\to\infty
set:

•

m=\lceil\log_{q}(n)\rceil=\log_{q}(n)+O(1)

•

k\approx Rn\;
such that:

•

t=\frac{k}{m}\;
is an integer.

A key observation then is that the lower bound
f
on
r_{\max}
of dual alternant codes in Theorem
3.1
,
or equivalently, the number
f
of columns of the matrix
\mathbf{\Phi}
in (
65
),
is very close to
k
:

Lemma 16

We have

f=\left(1-\frac{\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)}{\log_{q}(n)}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log_{q}(n)}\right)\right)k.

(137)

Proof

Direct consequence of:

•

e\;=\;\lfloor\log_{q}(t-1)\rfloor\;=\;\log_{q}(n)-\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)+O(1)

•

f\;=\;(e+1)t-\frac{q^{e+1}-1}{q-1}\;=\;et+O(t)
.

In the case of binary Goppa codes, the
\widehat{f}
from Corollary
5

and the
r^{*}
from Remark
1
improve
the
f
from Theorem
3.1
.
However one could show that they all have the same asymptotics. So, in order to distinguish these codes
from random codes, we will not care about the extra structure: treating Goppa codes as alternant codes will suffice.
(Still, this could be used to distinguish Goppa from “general” alternant codes.)

Now, under Heuristic
1
, we have:

Theorem 5.1

Asymptotically,
q
-ary alternant (including Goppa) codes of dual rate
R
can be distinguished from random codes,
with complexity at most

\kappa=q^{\left(\frac{2R^{2}}{1-R}+o(1)\right)\frac{(\log_{q}\log_{q}(n))^{3}}{(\log_{q}(n))^{2}}n}.

(138)

Proof

We use part 1 of Proposition
13
with
\mathcal{T}=\operatorname{{\mathsf{Alt}}}^{\perp}
, and
r^{*}=f
as provided by Theorem
3.1
.
The analysis will be easier if rephrased in terms of
k_{r^{*}}=k-r^{*}

and
r=r^{*}-s
.
First, Lemma
16
gives

k_{r^{*}}\;=\;k-f\;\sim\;R\frac{\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)}{\log_{q}(n)}n.

(139)

Let us then turn to condition (
115
). Fix an
\varepsilon>0
,
and set

r=\left\lceil(1+\varepsilon)\frac{R^{2}}{1-R}\left(\frac{\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)}{\log_{q}(n)}\right)^{2}n\right\rceil\ll k_{r^{*}}

(140)

and
s=r^{*}-r
. Then
k_{s}=k_{r^{*}}+r
and
n_{s}=n-k+k_{r^{*}}+r
, so for
n
large enough we get

\textstyle\frac{k_{s}(k_{s}+1)}{n_{s}}=\frac{(k_{r^{*}}+r)(k_{r^{*}}+r+1)}{n-k+k_{r^{*}}+r}\approx\frac{k_{r^{*}}^{2}}{n-k}\approx\frac{R^{2}}{1-R}\left(\frac{\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)}{\log_{q}(n)}\right)^{2}n<r=r^{*}-s.

(141)

Thus, for any
\varepsilon>0
, condition (
115
) is satisfied with this value of
r
as soon as
n
is large enough, say larger than some
n(\varepsilon)
.
But this means precisely that as
n\to\infty
, we can let
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(n)\to 0
while still satisfying the condition.
We then get

r\;\sim\;\frac{R^{2}}{1-R}\left(\frac{\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)}{\log_{q}(n)}\right)^{2}n.

(142)

Moreover the shortened code
{\mathsf{C}}_{s}
has rate
\frac{k_{s}}{n_{s}}=\frac{k_{r^{*}}+r}{n-k+k_{r^{*}}+r}=o(1)
,
so by Remark
3
both conditions in Heuristic
1
are satisfied.
Thus the hypotheses of Proposition
13
are satisfied, and both the iterative and the KW version of the distinguisher
work with these parameters. It turns out the KW approach gives a slightly better asymptotic complexity.

Now in
(
131
)
we see that, up to factors of lesser order, this complexity is essentially
\binom{k_{s}}{r}^{2}=\binom{k_{r^{*}}+r}{r}^{2}
.
Using
r\ll k_{r^{*}}
and Stirling’s formula we find
\log_{q}\binom{k_{r^{*}}+r}{r}\sim\log_{q}\binom{k_{r^{*}}}{r}\sim r\log_{q}\left(\frac{k_{r^{*}}}{r}\right)\sim r\log_{q}\log_{q}(n)
, and we conclude.

In
[
6
, §3.4]
, the security level
2^{b}

— or less formally, the number
b
of targeted “bits of security” —
of the
Classic McEliece
system, essentially defined as the complexity of the currently best attack (namely, by information set decoding),
is shown asymptotically to be linear in
t\propto n/\log(n)
.
Then in (
138
) we have

\frac{\log(\kappa)}{n/\log(n)}\propto\frac{(\log\log(n))^{3}}{\log(n)}\to 0

(143)

which means that the complexity of our distinguisher is subexponential in
b

(although, admittedly, only very slightly so).

As already mentioned, the existence of our subexponential distinguisher does not logically result
in a downgrade of the security of the McEliece cryptosystem and its variants.
All we can conclude is that the assumption that the original McEliece cryptosystem is OW-CPA
is an ad hoc assumption, certainly backed by decades of practical experience, but that cannot
easily be reduced to more fundamental theoretical justifications.

It also leaves open the question of whether some of the ideas introduced here,
possibly combined with other arguments, could lead to a subexponential key recovery algorithm.
