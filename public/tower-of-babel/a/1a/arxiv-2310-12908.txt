The von Neumann inequality for 3
×

Abstract




ABSTRACT

It is shown that the constant
c_{d,3}
in von Neumann’s inequality for
d
-tuples of commutative and row contractive
3\times 3
matrices, as proved by Hartz, Richter, and Shalit in
[
2
]
, is independent of the size of the
d
-tuple. A numerical estimation of the constant is provided.

1.
Introduction

The von Neumann inequality is a classical result in operator theory which states that, for any bounded and contractive operator
T\in B(H)
acting on a Hilbert space
H
and any polynomial
p\in\mathbb{C}[z]
, the following inequality holds:

(1.1)

\displaystyle\|p(T)\|\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|p(z)|.

It is a natural question to ask whether this inequality can be extended to a
d
-tuple of operators. For
d=2,
And
\hat{\text{o}}
answered to this question positively proving that
if
T=(T_{1},T_{2})
is a pair of commuting, contractive, bounded operators on a Hilbert space
H
and
p\in\mathbb{C}[z,w]
is a polynomial, then

\|p(T)\|\leq\sup_{(z,w)\in\mathbb{D}^{2}}|p(z,w)|.

However, a full generalization of this result is not possible. Davie and Crabb proved that in a 8-dimensional Hilbert space there exist a triple
T
of pairwise commuting, contractive operators, and a homogeneous polynomial
p
of degree 3 such that

\|p(T)\|>1.

Further details on the proof of And
\hat{\text{o}}
’s inequality and the Davie-Crabb example can be found in Chapter 1 of
[
8
]
.

In
[
5
]
, Knese used And
\hat{\text{o}}
’s inequality and Kosiński’s result on a solution of the 3-point Pick interpolation problem in the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
to show that the von Neumann inequality holds for
d
-tuples of
3\times 3
commuting and contractive matrices in the polydisc
\mathbb{D}^{d}
.

Recall that a Pick interpolation problem, for a domain
D\subset\mathbb{C}^{n}
, can be formulated as follows: given distinct points
z_{1},\ldots,z_{N}
in
D
and points
\zeta_{1},\ldots,\zeta_{N}
in the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
, decide if there exists a holomorphic function
F:D\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that
F(z_{j})=\zeta_{j},\ j=1,\ldots,N
.

Additionally, the Pick interpolation is extremal if it is solvable, and there is no holomorphic function
G:D\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that
G(z_{j})=\zeta_{j},\ j=1,\ldots,N
and
G(D)
is a relatively compact subset of
\mathbb{D}.

In the work of Hartz, Richter and Shalit
[
2
]
the contractivity property was replaced by the row contractivity condition, which means that a
d
-tuple of operators
T
defined on a Hilbert space satisfies the condition

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{d}T_{j}T^{*}_{j}\leq I,

where
I
represents the identity operator, and
T^{*}
denotes the adjoint operator of
T
. They proved:

Theorem 1.1
.

There exists the smallest constant
c_{d,n}\geq 0,
such that
for any
d-
tuple of commuting, row contractive,
n\times n
matrices and for any polynomial
p\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},...,z_{d}],
one has

(1.3)

\displaystyle\|p(T)\|\leq c_{d,n}\sup_{z\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}|p(z)|,

where
d,n\geq 2.
Additionally,
c_{d,n}>1
if
n\geq 3,\ d\geq 2
and
c_{d,2}=1,
if
d\geq 2.

Following Knese’s idea we use the solution to the Pick interpolation problem in the unit Euclidean ball, developed by Kosiński and Zwonek in
[
4
]
, to prove Theorem
1.1
in the particular case:

Theorem 1.2
.

The constant
c_{d,3}
, described in Theorem
1.1
, is independent of
d
, i.e.,

c_{d,3}=c_{2,3},\ d\geq 2.

Moreover, one has the following numerical estimation:
1.11767\leq c_{d,3}\leq 3.14626.

2.
Case of a
d
-tuple of
2\times 2
commuting, row contractive matrices

To present our proof idea for Theorem
1.2
, we show a special case of Theorem
1.1
, which was proved by Hartz, Richter, and Shalit in
[
2
]
:

Proposition 2.1
.

The von Neumann inequality (
1.3
) holds for any
d
-tuple of commuting, row contractive
2\times 2
matrices, and for any polynomial
p\in\mathbb{C}[z_{1},\dots,z_{d}],
where
d\geq 2
with a constant
c_{d,2}=1
.

The following lemma is crucial in the proof of Proposition
2.1
:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

(2.1)

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{d}\ni z\mapsto\zeta\in\mathbb{D},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{d}\ni w\mapsto\eta\in\mathbb{D},

be a 2-point extremal Pick interpolation problem in the unit Euclidean ball. Then, up to composition with automorphisms of the unit Euclidean ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
and the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, it can be solved by the projection onto the first coordinate,
\pi(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})=z_{1}
.

Proof of Lemma
2.2
.

Let
F
be a solution of (
2.1
). It is known that automorphisms of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
are finite compositions of transformations of the form

A_{t}(z_{1},...,z_{d})=\left(\sqrt{1-|t|^{2}}\frac{z_{1}}{1+\overline{t}z_{d}},....,\sqrt{1-|t|^{2}}\frac{z_{d-1}}{1+\overline{t}z_{d-1}},\frac{z_{d}+t}{1+\overline{t}z_{d}}\right),\ t\in\mathbb{D}

as well as unitary mappings. Using automorphisms of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{d}
and the unit disk
\mathbb{D}
, we can assume that
w=(0,\dots,0)
,
\eta=0
, and
\zeta>0
. Using additionally a unitary mapping, we can assume that
z=(x,0,\dots,0)
, where
x>0
. Consider a function
G:\mathbb{D}\rightarrow\mathbb{D},
given by
G(\mu):=F(\mu,0,\dots,0)
. It follows from the Schwarz lemma that
|G(\mu)|\leq|\mu|.
Therefore, one has
\zeta\leq x
. Extremality means in fact that
\zeta=x
. The Schwarz lemma yields that the projection
\pi:\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
given by
\pi(z_{1},\dots,z_{d})=z_{1}
is the desired solution.
∎

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.

The set of diagonalizable
d
-tuples of
2\times 2
matrices is dense in the set of all
d
-tuples of square
2\times 2
matrices
[
1
, Paragraph 4, p.21]
, so we can assume that the
d
-tuple
T
of pairwise commuting, row contractions consists of diagonalizable matrices. In
[
7
, Theorem 2.21.]
, it was shown that any family of diagonalizable and commuting matrices is simultaneously diagonalizable. Hence, we can express
T
in the form

T=P\text{diag}(\alpha,\beta)P^{-1},

where
\text{diag}(\alpha,\beta)
is defined as the
d
-tuple of diagonal matrices

\displaystyle\text{diag}(\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle:=\left(\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{1}&0\\
0&\beta_{1}\end{pmatrix},...,\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_{d}&0\\
0&\beta_{d}\end{pmatrix}\right),

where
\alpha=(\alpha_{1},...,\alpha_{d})\in\mathbb{B}^{d},\ \beta=(\beta_{1},...,\beta_{d})\in\mathbb{B}^{d}

and
P
is a transformation matrix.
Consequently, for any holomorphic function
f:\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{C}
, we obtain

f(T)=P\text{diag}(f(\alpha),f(\beta))P^{-1}.

In particular, this holds for polynomials.

Consider a polynomial
p
and a
d
-tuple
T
of commutative, row-contractions with a joint spectrum
\{\alpha,\beta\}
in
\mathbb{B}^{d}
. We can assume that
p(\alpha)\neq p(\beta).
Otherwise
T=p(\alpha)I,
where
I
is the identity matrix. One has in this case
\|p(T)\|=|p(\alpha)|\leq\sup_{\lambda\in\mathbb{B}^{d}}|p(\lambda)|
and the assertion follows. Next, let the interpolation problem

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto p(\alpha),

(2.2)

\displaystyle\beta\mapsto p(\beta),

have an analytic solution
f
. Consider the modified problem,

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto tp(\alpha),

(2.3)

\displaystyle\beta\mapsto tp(\beta),

where
t\geq 1
. We are interested in finding the greatest
t
such that there exists a solution of (
2
). Note that the set
\tau
of all such
t
is bounded from above as the image of any solution of (
2
) is a subset of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
.
Let
(t_{i})_{i}
be a sequence converging to the supremum
t_{0}
of
\tau.
For any
i
, there exists a holomorphic function
f_{i}:\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that

(2.4)

\displaystyle f_{i}(\alpha)=t_{i}p(\alpha),\ f_{i}(\beta)=t_{i}p(\beta).

Using Montel’s theorem, we can pass to a convergent subsequence. Therefore, there exists a holomorphic function
F:\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
such that

F(\alpha)=t_{0}p(\alpha),\ F(\beta)=t_{0}p(\beta).

Since
t_{0}
is the greatest possible, the problem is extremal. Due to Lemma
2.2
, the function
F
can be expressed in the form
F=m\circ\pi\circ A,
where
m\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),\ A\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{d})
and
\pi
is the natural projection onto the first coordinate. Put
S=A(T)
. Since a composition of the unit ball automorphism with a
d
-tuple of commutative, row contractions is a commutative, row contraction, we infer that
S
is a
d
-tuple of commutative, row contractions. It follows that

F(T)=m(\pi(A(T)))=m(S_{1}).

We have

(2.5)

\displaystyle\|p(T)\|\leq\|F(T)\|=\|m(S_{1})\|\leq\sup_{z\in\mathbb{D}}|m(z)|=1.

In the last inequality we applied inequality (
1.1
) which holds for all holomorphic functions in the neighbourhood of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
due to uniform approximation by complex polynomials on compact subsets of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
. Thus, we have
c_{d,2}=c_{1,2}
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
: Constant
c_{d,3}=c_{2,3}

In
[
4
]
Kosiński and Zwonek found a solution to the 3-point Pick interpolation problem in the unit Euclidean ball.

Theorem 3.1

(Kosiński, Zwonek)
.

If the
3-
point Pick interpolation problem

\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{D},\ w_{j}\mapsto\lambda_{j},\ j=1,2,3,

is extremal, then, up to a composition with automorphisms of
\mathbb{B}^{d}
and
\mathbb{D}
, it is
interpolated by a function which belongs to one of the classes

\mathcal{F}_{D}=\{(z_{1},...,z_{n})\mapsto\frac{2z_{1}(1-\tau z_{1})-\overline{\tau}\omega^{2}z_{2}^{2}}{2(1-\tau z_{1})-\omega^{2}z_{2}^{2}}:\ |\tau|=1,|\omega|\leq 1\},

\mathcal{F}_{ND}=\{(z_{1},...,z_{n})\mapsto\frac{z_{1}^{2}+2\sqrt{1-a^{2}}z_{2}}{2-a^{2}}:\ a\in[0,1)\}.

We begin with showing that for every integer
d\geq 2
, it is true that
c_{d,3}=c_{2,3}
. To prove it, we can adopt the same reasoning employed in the
2\times 2
matrix case, but instead consider
d
-tuples of diagonalizable
3\times 3
matrices (the fact that the set of
d-
tuples of
3\times 3,
diagonalizable and commuting matrices is dense in set of all
d-
tuples of commuting square matrices can be found in
[
6
, Lemma 10]
). Assuming that
T
consists of a
d
-tuple of pairwise commuting, diagonalizable, row contractive matrices, we can represent
T
as

T=P\text{diag}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)P^{-1},

where
P
is a transformation matrix and
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\mathbb{B}^{d}.
As before, let
p
be a polynomial. First, consider the case where
p(\alpha),p(\beta),p(\gamma)
are distinct. The interpolation problem

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto p(\alpha),

\displaystyle\beta\mapsto p(\beta),

\displaystyle\gamma\mapsto p(\gamma),

possesses an analytic solution
f
. Next, we consider the modified problem,

\displaystyle\alpha\mapsto tp(\alpha),

(3.1)

\displaystyle\beta\mapsto tp(\beta),

\displaystyle\gamma\mapsto tp(\gamma).

Using the same Montel-type argument as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
, there exists a holomorphic function
F:\mathbb{B}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{D}
that is an extremal solution of the interpolation problem (
3
).
Theorem
3.1
allows us to represent
F
in the form
F=m\circ h\circ A,
where
m\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),\ A\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{d})
and
h
is a function belonging either to
\mathcal{F}_{D},
or
\mathcal{F}_{ND}.
Put
S=A(T).
Then

F(T)=m(h(A(T)))=m(h(S_{1},S_{2})),

due to the special forms of functions from classes
\mathcal{F}_{D},\mathcal{F}_{ND}.

As in the case of
2\times 2
matrices, if
p(\alpha)=p(\beta)=p(\gamma),
then we can proceed as in the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

Next, consider the case where only 2 of 3 points
p(\alpha),p(\beta),p(\gamma)
are equal, say
p(\alpha)=p(\beta).
Then, we can modify the polynomial
p
replacing
p(\beta)
by
p(\beta)-\varepsilon\eta,
where
\varepsilon>0
is arbitrarily small number and
\eta\in\partial\mathbb{D}
are such that
p(\beta)-\varepsilon\eta\in\mathbb{D},
for all such
\varepsilon>0
. Denote the modified polynomial by
p_{\varepsilon}.
Points of spectrum for
p_{\varepsilon}(T)
are distinct, hence we can argue as above constructing a holomorphic function
F_{\varepsilon}
such that
F_{\varepsilon}(T)=m(h_{\varepsilon}(A(T)))=m(h_{\varepsilon}(S_{1},S_{2})).
Since this is true for all such
\varepsilon>0
suitably small, by the continuity of the norm and the continuity of holomorphic functions we can pass with
\varepsilon
to
0
preserving required properties. To get back to the von Neumann’s inequality we argue similarly as in the end of the proof of the Proposition
2.1
. Therefore, we infer that
c_{d,3}=c_{2,3}.

4.
Proof of Theorem
1.2
: Estimation of the
c_{2,3}
constant

According to the previous section, we can assume that
d=2
. Furthermore, we have a pair
T=(T_{1},T_{2})
consisting of diagonalizable
3\times 3
commuting and row contractive matrices. We can assume that the joint spectrum of
T
is a set
\{(a_{1},a_{2}),(0,d_{2}),(0,0)\}\subset\mathbb{B}^{2}
with
d_{2}>0
.
Indeed, the composition of an automorphism of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{2}
with a commutative row contraction is itself a commutative row contraction. Therefore, as shown in the proof of Lemma
2.2
, we can compose
T
with appropriate automorphisms of the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{2}.

Using
[
7
, Theorem 2.23.]
, we can assume that
T_{i}
’s are upper triangular matrices, that is,

T_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}&c_{1}\\
0&0&e_{1}\\
0&0&0\ \end{pmatrix},\ T_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}&c_{2}\\
0&d_{2}&e_{2}\\
0&0&0\ \end{pmatrix}.

We can represent the matrices
T_{i}
using a block matrix notation

(4.1)

\displaystyle T_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}A_{i}&\beta_{i}\\
0&0\ \end{pmatrix},

where

A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&b_{1}\\
0&0\ \end{pmatrix},\ A_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{2}&b_{2}\\
0&d_{2}\ \end{pmatrix},\ \beta_{i}=\begin{pmatrix}c_{i}\\
e_{i}\ \end{pmatrix}\ i=1,2.

As in the preceding sections, in order to estimate
c_{2,3}
, it is sufficient to appraise the norm of
(m\circ h\circ A)(T)
, where
m\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D})
,
A\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{2})
, and
h\in\mathcal{F}_{ND}
or
h\in\mathcal{F}_{D}
.

We split the estimation with respect to the function type.

Lemma 4.1
.

If
h\in\mathcal{F}_{ND},
then
\|h(T)\|\leq 2/\sqrt{3}.

Proof.

Consider a function
F_{\alpha}
from the class
\mathcal{F}_{ND}.
It is worth noting that in this case, the inequality given by
(\ref{rc:1})
can be restated in an equivalent form as
\|T_{1}z\|^{2}+\|T_{2}z\|^{2}\leq 1,
where
z\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{3}.
This can also be expressed using the block form as follows:

(4.2)

\displaystyle\|A_{1}x+\beta_{1}y\|^{2}+\|A_{2}x+\beta_{2}y\|^{2}\leq 1,\ z=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\ \end{pmatrix}\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{3},\ x\in\mathbb{B}^{2},\ y\in\mathbb{D}.

We get

F_{\alpha}(T_{1},T_{2})=\begin{pmatrix}F_{\alpha}(A_{1},A_{2})&\frac{1}{2-\alpha^{2}}(A_{1}\beta_{1}+2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\beta_{2})\\
0&0\ \end{pmatrix}.

Using the triangle inequality and boundedness of
A_{1}
we get

(4.3)

\displaystyle\|F_{\alpha}(T_{1},T_{2})\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\ \end{pmatrix}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(2-\alpha^{2})^{2}}\left[\|A_{1}x+\beta_{1}y\|+2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\|A_{2}x+\beta_{2}y\|\right]^{2}.

We can simplify the estimation of (
4.3
) by making the substitution
u=\|A_{1}x+\beta_{1}y\|
and
v=\|A_{1}x+\beta_{1}y\|
. Then, the estimation reduces to finding the supremum

\sup\{\frac{1}{2-\alpha^{2}}(u+2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}v):\ 0\geq u,v,\ u^{2}+v^{2}\leq 1,\ 0\leq\alpha<1\}.

This supremum equals
2/\sqrt{3}
.

∎

Next, we prove

Lemma 4.2
.

If
h\in\mathcal{F}_{ND},
and
m\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),
then
\|(m\circ h)(T)\|\leq 3.14626.

Proof.

Let
m_{t}\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),\ F_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{F}_{ND},
where
t\in\mathbb{D},\ \alpha\in[0,1).

We compute

(m_{t}\circ F_{\alpha})(T)=\begin{pmatrix}m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(A))&\frac{1}{2-\alpha^{2}}(\overline{t}m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(A))+I)(A_{1}\beta_{1}+2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\beta_{2})\\
0&-t\ \end{pmatrix}.

Express
z\in\partial\mathbb{B}^{3}
in the block form
z=\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\ \end{pmatrix},
where
x\in\mathbb{B}^{2},\ y\in\mathbb{D}.
Then, by the boundedness property of linear operators

(4.4)

\displaystyle\|m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(T_{1},T_{2}))\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\ \end{pmatrix}\|^{2}

\displaystyle\leq(\|m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(A_{1},A_{2}))\|\|x\|

\displaystyle+\frac{|y|}{2-\alpha^{2}}\|\overline{t}m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(A_{1},A_{2}))+I\|\|A_{1}\beta_{1}

\displaystyle+2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\beta_{2}\|)^{2}+|ty|^{2}.

According to Proposition
2.1
, we have that

\|m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(A_{1},A_{2}))\|\leq 1.

By applying the triangle inequality, we can estimate the right-hand side of inequality (
4.4
) by

(4.5)

\displaystyle(\|x\|+\frac{2|y|}{2-\alpha^{2}}\|A_{1}\beta_{1}+2\sqrt{1-\alpha^{2}}\beta_{2}\|)^{2}+|y|^{2}.

We may utilize numerical computations to obtain an upper bound for the expression (
4.5
). Because of the maximum modulus principle, we may also assume that
\|x\|^{2}+|y|^{2}=1
. Hence, the right-hand side of inequality (
4.4
) may be estimated from above by a function that is dependent on the coefficients of matrices
T_{1}
and
T_{2}
, as well as the parameters
y
and
\alpha
. All variables and parameters satisfy the constraints given by the equations
|a_{1}|^{2}+|b_{1}|^{2}+|c_{1}|^{2}+|c_{2}|^{2}\leq 1,\ |e_{1}|^{2}+|e_{2}|^{2}\leq 1,\ |y|\leq 1,\ 0\leq\alpha<1.

By imposing these constraints on our function, we obtain

\|m_{t}(F_{\alpha}(T_{1},T_{2}))\|\leq 3.14626.

∎

Finally, we consider the composition of a unit disc automorphism with a function from the class
\mathcal{F}_{D}
. We prove

Lemma 4.3
.

If
h\in\mathcal{F}_{D}
and
m\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),
then
\|(m\circ h)(T)\|\leq 3.14626.

Proof.

Consider a function
F_{\tau,\omega}\in\mathcal{F}_{D}
. Note that it can be expressed as

F_{\tau,\omega}(z,w)=\overline{\tau}F_{1,1}(\tau z,\omega w),

where
|\tau|\leq 1,\ |\omega|=1.

The function
F_{1,1}
can be obtained as a limit of functions from the class
\mathcal{F}_{ND}
, composed with appropriate automorphisms of the unit disc
\mathbb{D}
and the unit ball
\mathbb{B}^{2}
. In particular, we have

F_{1,1}(z,w)=\lim_{\mathbb{R}\cap\mathbb{D}\ni t\rightarrow 1^{-}}(m_{t}\circ F_{0}\circ A_{t}\circ u)(z,w),

where

•

m_{t}(z)=-\frac{z-t}{1-tz}\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),\ t\in(0,1),

•

A_{t}(z,w)=\large(\sqrt{1-t^{2}}\frac{z}{1+tw},\frac{w+t}{1+tw}\large)\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{2}),\ t\in(0,1),

•

u(z,w)=(z,-w)
is an unitary map,

•

F_{0}\in\mathcal{F}_{ND}.

Consider a function
F_{\tau,\omega}\in\mathcal{F}_{D}
, where
\tau,\omega\in\partial\mathbb{D}
. Let
m_{s}(z)=\frac{z-s}{1-\overline{s}z}\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{D}),
for some
s\in\mathbb{D},
and
A\in\text{Aut}(\mathbb{B}^{2}).
Then, the mapping
U_{\tau,\omega}:\mathbb{B}^{2}\ni(z,w)\mapsto(\tau z,\omega w)\in\mathbb{B}^{2}
is unitary.

Recall that the composition of a commutative, row contraction with a unitary map or a unit Euclidean ball automorphism is itself a commutative, row contraction. Therefore, if
T
is a commutative, row contraction, then so is
U_{\tau,\omega}(T)
. In estimating
\|(m_{s}\circ F_{\tau,\omega}\circ A)(T)\|
we are considering all pairs
T
of commuting, row contractions. Hence, we can replace any composition of a unitary map or a unit ball automorphism with
T
by
T
itself. Thus, we have

\|(m_{s}\circ F_{\tau,\omega}\circ A)(T)\|=\|(m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}F_{1,1}\circ U_{\tau,\omega}\circ A)(T)\|=\|(m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}F_{1,1})(T)\|,

where
s\in\mathbb{D},
and
\omega,\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}.
Hence, using the continuity of a norm, we can estimate the norm of
(m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}F_{1,1})(T)
as follows.

(4.6)

\displaystyle\|(m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}F_{1,1})(T)\|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathbb{R}\cap\mathbb{D}\ni t\rightarrow 1^{-}}\|(m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}m_{t}\circ F_{0}\circ A_{t}\circ u)(T)\|

\displaystyle=\lim_{\mathbb{R}\cap\mathbb{D}\ni t\rightarrow 1^{-}}\|(m_{\tilde{s}}(F_{0}(T)))\|\leq 3.14626,

where
\tilde{s}\in\mathbb{D}
is such that
m_{\tilde{s}}=m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}m_{t}.

Thus we have the upper bound on the constant
c_{2,3}
for
\tau,\omega\in\partial\mathbb{D}
.

Consider the mapping
\mathbb{D}\ni\omega\mapsto(m_{s}\circ F_{\tau,\omega})(T)
. Using the estimation (
4.6
) and the maximum modulus principle, we obtain

\|(m_{s}\circ\overline{\tau}F_{1,1})(T)\|\leq 3.14626,

for
\omega\in\overline{\mathbb{D}}
and
\tau\in\partial\mathbb{D}.
The proof of the Lemma
4.3
is completed.
∎

Moreover, by computational simulations, one can obtain a lower bound estimate. The sample considered for estimation of the lower bound had a size
\sim 5\cdot 10^{6}
per each of classes
\mathcal{F}_{ND}
and
\mathcal{F}_{D}.

Example 4.4
.

Let
\alpha=0.707107
and

D_{1}=\textup{diag}(0.289+0.31i,0.02-0.08i,-0.22-0.12i),
