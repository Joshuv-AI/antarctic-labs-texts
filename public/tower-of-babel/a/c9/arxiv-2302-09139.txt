A Class of Algorithms for Quadratic Minimization
Marc Stromberg email: mstromberg@psmfc.org

Abstract
Certain problems in quadratic minimization can be reduced to finding the point x x of a polyhedron 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} that minimizes the distance ‖ x − p ‖ \|x-p\| for some p ∉ 𝒫 p\notin{\mathcal{P}} . This amounts to a search for the appropriate face F F of 𝒫 {\mathcal{P}} for which the minimizing point is the projection of p p onto F F . We present a class of algorithms for finding the face F F and the corresponding minimizing point x ∈ 𝒫 x\in{\mathcal{P}} , then a number of examples using those methods.

elements of
F^{\circ}
by ascent into
G
or descent from
G^{\circ}
, where the number by descent excludes
\pi_{G}
because that point will never descend to a proper face of
G
, and ascent from a proper face of
G
containing
F
is also excluded, where
\sigma_{F,G}=|\{G_{0}<G\mid F\leq G_{0}\}|
. We can get elements from below by ascent from a proper face of
F
into
F^{\circ}
but as we have noted, any of these will result in
\pi_{F}
, which has been accounted for. Therefore we have the bound

i_{F}\leq 2+\sum_{j<k,G\in\Gamma_{j}\atop{F<G}}(f_{G}-\sigma_{F,G}+i_{G}-1)

(1.7)

on the number of elements of the sequence in
F^{\circ}
, so inductively
i_{F}
is finite for each
F\in\Gamma_{k}
for each
k=1,\ldots,n=\hbox{Dim}({\mathcal{P}})
, and of course for
F\in\Gamma_{n}
we have the better bound
i_{F}=1
. In any case since
{\mathcal{P}}
has finitely many faces, this shows that
\{e^{(i)}\}
is finite.
∎

Remark 2.
The starting point for Algorithm
1.1
can be any
p
-visible point of
{\mathcal{P}}
, so we can get a starting point
e_{0}
from any point of
x\in{\mathcal{P}}
by taking
e_{0}
as the only
p
-visible point on the segment
[x,p]
. Starting at a vertex of
{\mathcal{P}}
is less desirable, since it immediately forces an ascent unless the vertex is actually the solution.

2
Experiments

In this section we demonstrate some properties of Algorithm
1.1
with simple low-dimensional examples and then some experimental trials in higher dimensions. These methods are easily scaled for real-world problems.

For the singular problems below, we require a brief digression into the construction of constraints on the linear image of a polyhedron. Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be a polyhedron and let
\Lambda:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a linear transformation with restriction
\Lambda:{\mathcal{P}}\rightarrow\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
. We will confuse
\Lambda
and its matrix representation
\Lambda_{n\times m}
as required. Let
{\mathcal{P}}
be defined by constraints
{\mathcal{P}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\mid Bx\leq c\}
for some matrix
B
and vector
c
. If
\Lambda
is invertible, one can easily construct constraints on
\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
as
\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mid B\Lambda^{-1}x\leq c\}
. If
\Lambda
is not invertible, the construction is not quite as straightforward. We will show that constraints can be constructed with varying degrees of ‘easily’, i.e., computational complexity, the indicator of this being substantially more a function of the right corank
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)
of
\Lambda
than the values of
m
or
n
, where
\Lambda
is
n\times m
and where this quantity is defined as
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)=m-r
where
r
is the rank of
\Lambda
, and is the dimension of the null space of
\Lambda
. We will also use the idea of the usual (left) corank
\rho_{n}(\Lambda)=n-r
.

Let
\Lambda=P^{*}LU
where
L_{n\times r}
and
U_{r\times m}
are truncated lower triangular and upper echelon form matrices of rank
r
where
\Lambda
has rank
r
and where
P
is a permutation matrix, as in
[
2
]
. The leading nonzero entry in each row of
U
is
1
and in particular there is an integer array
\gamma
of length
r
such that

U_{ij}=\begin{cases}1&\hbox{ if }j=\gamma_{i},\cr 0&\hbox{ if }j<\gamma_{i},\cr u_{ij}&\hbox{ if }j>\gamma_{i}\end{cases}

for each
i=0,\ldots,r-1
,
j=0,\ldots,m-1
with elements
u_{ij}
determined by the factorization of
\Lambda
. For the transformation
U:{\mathcal{P}}\rightarrow U{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
a preimage
x
of
y\in{\mathcal{P}}^{\prime}
can be constructed by back substitution, with

\quad x_{\gamma_{i}}\leftarrow y_{i}-\sum_{m-1\geq k>\gamma_{i}}U_{ik}x_{k}

(2.1)

for
r-1\geq i\geq 0
in that order of
i
. The calculation (
2.1
) does not determine
x_{j}
for
j\notin\gamma
. These quantities can be selected arbitrarily, and taken together with the components determined by (
2.1
) define a preimage
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
of
y
under
U
. Let
\nu
be the complement (if any) of the set
\gamma
in the set of integers
0,\ldots,m-1
, and define the
suspension
of
U
as the matrix
{\tilde{U}}
given by

{\tilde{U}}_{ij}=\begin{cases}U_{kj}&\hbox{ if }i=\gamma_{k}\in\gamma\cr\delta_{ij}&\hbox{ if }i\in\nu\cr\end{cases}

for
i,j=0,\ldots,m-1
where
\delta
is the Kronecker delta. Note that the suspension
{\tilde{U}}:\mathbb{R}^{m}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{m}
and that
{\tilde{U}}
is invertible. For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
we can write
{\tilde{U}}x={\tilde{x}}
where

{\tilde{x}}_{i}=\begin{cases}y_{k}&\hbox{ if }i=\gamma_{k}\in\gamma,\cr x_{i}&\hbox{ if }i\in\nu\cr\end{cases}

(2.2)

where
y_{k}
are components of
Ux
. The constraints on
{\tilde{U}}{\mathcal{P}}={\mathcal{P}}^{\prime\prime}
are of the form

B^{\prime}{\tilde{x}}\leq c

(2.3)

where
B^{\prime}=B{\tilde{U}}^{-1}
. There are clearly
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)
free parameters in any preimage
x
of
y\in U{\mathcal{P}}
and these are unaffected under transformation by
{\tilde{U}}
, simply being reproduced in the components
x_{i}
for
i\in\nu
. Any of the constraints of (
2.3
) can be rearranged into an expression involving the free parameters
x_{i}
for
i\in\nu
and the components
y_{k}
of
y\in U{\mathcal{P}}
, and the columns of
B^{\prime}
can be (or be imagined to be) arranged so that the free parameters come first. Doing this, we apply just
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)
steps of Fourier-Motzkin elimination (aka FME, see
[
1
]
) to eliminate these free parameters, to obtain ‘corank reduced constraints’ of the form
B^{\prime\prime}y\leq c^{\prime}
involving only the components of
y
, or no constraints at all (in the case that
B^{\prime\prime}y\leq c^{\prime}
is an empty set of inequalities, satisfied by any
y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
) if at some point of the FME reduction all coefficients of one of the free variables are strictly positive or strictly negative. While the bad news is that Fourier-Motzkin is a doubly exponential time algorithm, the good news is that we can ignore the bad news if the corank is sufficiently small. If
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)=0
we take (
2.3
) as the definition of
B^{\prime\prime}y\leq c^{\prime}
, applying no steps of FME. The following argument applies for the reduced constraints, whether empty or not.

Theorem 2.1
.

The corank reduced constraints constitute a complete set of constraints for
U{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

If
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)=0
there is nothing to show, so assume
\rho_{m}(\Lambda)>0
. Let
y\in U{\mathcal{P}}
and let
x\in{\mathcal{P}}
be a preimage of
y
, constructed as in (
2.1
) for some choice of free parameters. Then
{\tilde{x}}={\tilde{U}}x
satisfies the constraints (
2.3
). But then
y
satisfies the reduced constraints
B^{\prime\prime}y\leq c^{\prime}
since FME produces equivalent sets of constraints at each step. On the other hand, suppose
y\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
satisfies the reduced constraints. If
y\notin U{\mathcal{P}}
then no preimage
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
of
y
is an element of
{\mathcal{P}}
, so
{\tilde{x}}
violates at least one of the constraints (
2.3
). But since
y
satisfies the reduced constraints produced by FME, it is possible to work backward to find values of the eliminated variables for which we do have a solution of (
2.3
). This is a contradiction, so in fact
y\in U{\mathcal{P}}
.
∎

To complete the construction we define constraints
B^{\prime\prime\prime}z\leq c^{\prime\prime}
as the set of constraints
B^{\prime\prime}(L^{*}L)^{-1}L^{*}Pz\leq c^{\prime}
(if
B^{\prime\prime}y\leq c^{\prime}
is nonempty) together with the auxilliary constraints
\zeta_{i}^{*}z\leq 0,\enskip-\zeta_{i}^{*}z\leq 0
where
\{\zeta_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\rho_{n}(\Lambda)}
is a basis of the null space of
L^{*}P
.

Theorem 2.2
.

The constraints
B^{\prime\prime\prime}z\leq c^{\prime\prime}
are a complete set of constraints for
\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
.

Proof.

If
B^{\prime\prime}y\leq c^{\prime}
is nonempty and
z
satisfies
B^{\prime\prime}(L^{*}L)^{-1}L^{*}Pz\leq c^{\prime}
then we have
(L^{*}L)^{-1}L^{*}Pz=Ux
for some
x\in{\mathcal{P}}
by Theorem
2.1
. If
z
also satisfies the auxilliary constraints then
z=P^{*}Lw
for some
w\in\mathbb{R}^{r}
from which
z=P^{*}LUx\in\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
. If the reduced constraints are empty then
U{\mathcal{P}}=\mathbb{R}^{r}
, in which case
\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
is simply the range of
P^{*}L
and we still have
z=P^{*}LUx\in\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
for some
x\in{\mathcal{P}}
. Finally, it is clear that points of
\Lambda{\mathcal{P}}
satisfy the constraints
B^{\prime\prime\prime}z\leq c^{\prime\prime}
. ∎
