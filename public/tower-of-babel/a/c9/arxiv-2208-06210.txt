Measuring incompatibility and clustering quantum observables with a quantum switch
Ning Gao, Dantong Li, Anchit Mishra, Junchen Yan, Kyrylo Simonov, Giulio Chiribella

Abstract
The existence of incompatible observables is a cornerstone of quantum mechanics and a valuable resource in quantum technologies. Here we introduce a measure of incompatibility, called the mutual eigenspace disturbance (MED), which quantifies the amount of disturbance induced by the measurement of a sharp observable on the eigenspaces of another. The MED provides a metric on the space of von Neumann measurements, and can be efficiently estimated by letting the measurement processes act in an indefinite order, using a setup known as the quantum switch, which also allows one to quantify the noncommutativity of arbitrary quantum processes. Thanks to these features, the MED can be used in quantum machine learning tasks. We demonstrate this application by providing an unsupervised algorithm that clusters unknown von Neumann measurements. Our algorithm is robust to noise can be used to identify groups of observers that share approximately the same measurement context.



Introduction.
— One of the most striking features of quantum mechanics is the existence of incompatible observables.
Incompatible observables are at the heart of Bohr’s notion of complementarity
Bohr 1928
and of Heisenberg’s uncertainty principle
Heisenberg 1927
, and have non-trivial relations with Bell nonlocality
Wolf et al. 2009
;
Quintino et al. 2014
and other forms of nonclassicality
Uola et al. 2014
;
Tavakoli and Uola 2020
;
De Bièvre 2021
;
De Bièvre 2023
;
Xu 2022
. In addition to their foundational relevance, they play center stage in quantum information technologies
Quintino et al. 2014
;
Skrzypczyk et al. 2019
;
Heinosaari et al. 2016
,
where quantum incompatibility serves as a resource
Buscemi et al. 2020
;
D’Ariano et al. 2022
;
Buscemi et al. 2022
, in a similar way as quantum entanglement and coherence.

Several measures of incompatibility have been proposed in the past years, including robustness to noise (defined as the minimum amount of noise that has to be added to a set of incompatible observables in order to make them compatible)
Busch et al. 2013
;
Heinosaari et al. 2014
, sensitivity to eavesdropping (defined as the minimum amount of disturbance that an arbitrary entanglement-breaking channel would induce on a quantum system prepared in an unknown eigenstate of the given observables)
Bandyopadhyay and Mandayam 2013
, and disturbance on the measurement statistics (defined as the maximum distance between the probability distribution of observable
A
on a given input state and the probability distribution of
A
after a measurement of observable
B
has been performed on the same input state, with the maximum evaluated over all possible input states)
Mandayam and Srinivas 2014
. Since all these measures are defined in terms of optimization problems, they are often hard to compute analytically, and numerical evaluation becomes unfeasible for systems of high dimension. In addition,
there is generally no direct way to estimate these measures from experimental data: in most cases, the best known way to infer the incompatibility of two unknown observables is to perform a full tomography, which is unfeasible for quantum systems consisting of many particles.

In this paper we introduce a measure of incompatibility for sharp observables
Busch et al. 1996
,
called the Mutual Eigenspace Disturbance (MED).
The MED quantifies the noncommutativity of the spectral resolutions associated to the two observables, and can be naturally extended to a larger class of noncommutativity measures for unsharp measurements and general quantum processes. It has a simple closed-form analytical expression and,
unlike other incompatibility measures, it constitutes a metric on the space of von Neumann measurements, a property that makes it suitable for machine learning applications. The MED and its generalizations to measures of noncommutativity can be directly estimated using the quantum switch
Chiribella et al. 2009a
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
, an operation that combines quantum processes in a coherently-controlled order. Estimation of the MED via the quantum switch can be realized with existing technology
Procopio et al. 2015
;
Rubino et al. 2017
;
Goswami et al. 2018
;
Wei et al. 2019
;
Guo et al. 2020
;
Goswami et al. 2020
;
Rubino et al. 2021
;
Goswami and Romero 2020
and its sample complexity
is independent of the dimension of the system, meaning that the number of experiments needed to estimate the MED remains small even for multiparticle systems.

The experimental accessibility of the MED and its metric properties make it suitable for applications in quantum machine learning. To illustrate the idea, we provide a quantum algorithm that clusters noisy von Neumann measurements based on their mutual compatibility. This algorithm can be used to identify clusters of observers who share approximately the same measurement context
Specker 1960
;
Kochen and Specker 1967
;
Budroni et al. 2022
;
Abramsky and Brandenburger 2011
, and thereby could share the same notion of an emergent classical reality
d’Espagnat 2018
;
Schlosshauer 2005
;
Grangier 2002
;
Zurek 2009
;
Horodecki et al. 2015
. Notably, the observers could be localized in distant laboratories, and the algorithm does not require access to their measurement outcomes, but only to the average evolution associated to their measurement devices.

MED.
—For sharp observables, compatibility is equivalent to commutativity
Lahti 2003
.
Let
A
and
B
be two sharp observables on a
d
-dimensional quantum system, and let
\mathbf{P}=(P_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}}
and
\mathbf{Q}=(Q_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}
be the projectors on the eigenspaces of
A
and
B
, respectively.
We now introduce a measure of noncommutativity between
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
.
Imagine that the system is initially in an eigenstate of
A
, say
|\alpha_{i}\rangle
, picked uniformly at random from the
i
-th eigenspace, with
i
distributed according to the probability distribution
p_{i}:=d_{A,i}/d
, where
d_{A,i}
is the eigenspace’s dimension. Then, the system undergoes the canonical (Lüders) measurement process associated to the observable
B
: with probability
p_{B}(j)=\langle\alpha_{i}|Q_{j}|\alpha_{i}\rangle
the measurement yields outcome
j
, leaving the system in the post-measurement state
Q_{j}|\alpha_{i}\rangle/\|Q_{j}|\alpha_{i}\rangle\|
. On average over all outcomes, the density matrix of the system is
\sum_{j}p_{B}(j)~Q_{j}|\alpha_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|Q_{j}/\|Q_{j}|\alpha_{i}\rangle\|^{2}=\mathscr{B}(|\alpha_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|)
, where
\mathscr{B}
is the dephasing channel defined by the relation
\mathscr{B}(\rho):=\sum_{j}\,Q_{j}\rho Q_{j}
for arbitrary density matrices
\rho
. Finally, a measurement of the observable
A
is performed. The probability to find the outcome
i
, associated to the original subspace, is
\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}\,\mathscr{B}(|\alpha_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|)]
. On average, the probability that the system is still found in the original eigenspace is

\displaystyle{\sf Prob}(A,\mathscr{B}):=\sum_{i}\,p_{i}\,\int\pi_{i}({\rm d}\alpha_{i})\,\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}\,\mathscr{B}(|\alpha_{i}\rangle\langle\alpha_{i}|)]\,,

(1)

where
\pi_{i}({\rm d}\,\alpha_{i})
is the uniform probability distribution over the pure states in the
i
-th subspace.
Explicit calculation yields the expression

\displaystyle{\sf Prob}(A,\mathscr{B}):=\frac{1}{d}\,\sum_{ij}\,\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]\,,

(2)

which is related to an extension of the Kirkwood-Dirac quasiprobability distribution
Yunger Halpern et al. 2018
;
Arvidsson-Shukur et al. 2021
.

Note that the role of the projectors
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
is completely symmetric. Operationally, this symmetry implies to the relation

\displaystyle{\sf Prob}(A,\mathscr{B})={\sf Prob}(B,\mathscr{A})\,,

(3)

where
{\sf Prob}(B,\mathscr{A})
is the average probability that a randomly chosen state
|\beta_{j}\rangle
from the
j
-th eigenspace of
B
, drawn with probability
q_{j}:=d_{B,j}/d
(where
d_{B,j}
is the eigenspace’s dimension), is still found in the same eigenspace after the action of the dephasing channel
\mathscr{A}(\rho):=\sum_{i}P_{i}\rho P_{i}
.

Note also that the probabilities in Eq. (
3
) depend only on the dephasing channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
. Accordingly, we will denote by
D(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}):=1-{\sf Prob}(A,\mathscr{B})\equiv 1-{\sf Prob}(B,\mathscr{A})
the average probability of eigenstate disturbance. We then define the MED of the two observables
A
and
B
as

\displaystyle{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}):=\sqrt{D(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})}=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}\,.

(4)

The MED exhibits several appealing properties for a measure of incompatibility:

1.

it is
symmetric and nonnegative
, namely
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})={\rm MED}(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A})\geq 0
for every pair of dephasing channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
,

2.

it is
faithful
, namely
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})>0
if and only if
A
and
B
are incompatible,

3.

it is
decreasing under coarse-graining
,

4.

it is a
a metric on von Neumann measurements
, corresponding to observables with non-degenerate spectrum.

5.

it is
robust to noise
: it remains faithful even if one of the channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
is replaced by the evolution resulting from a noisy measurement of the corresponding observable,

6.

it is
maximal for maximally complementary observables

Kraus 1987
, that is, observables such that their eigenstates form mutually unbiased bases
Ivonovic 1981
;
Wootters and Fields 1989
. In general, one has the bound
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-1/\min\{k_{A},k_{B}\}}
, and the maximum value
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=\sqrt{1-1/d}
and is attained if and only if
A
and
B
are maximally complementary.

The proof of the above properties is provided in Appendix
A
. There, we also extend the MED to a broader class of incompatibility measures, given by the expression

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}):=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}\,,

(5)

where
\rho
is a density matrix. The original MED, defined above, corresponds to the case where
\rho
is the maximally mixed state
I/d
.
Notably, the generalized MED (
5
) is also a metric on von Neumann measurements whenever the density matrix
\rho
is non-singular, including
e.g.
the case where
\rho
is a thermal state.

Experimental setup.
—We now provide an experimental setup that can be used to estimate the MED of two observables and, more generally, the amount of noncommutativity between two arbitrary quantum processes.

The setup is based on the quantum switch
Chiribella et al. 2009a
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
, an operation that combines two unknown processes in a coherent superposition of two alternative orders. Previously, the quantum switch was shown to be able to distinguish between pairs of quantum channels with commuting or anti-commuting Kraus operators
Chiribella 2012
;
Swingle et al. 2016
, a task that can be practically achieved with photonic systems
Andersson et al. 2005
;
Procopio et al. 2015
. We now show that the quantum switch can be used to quantify the amount of noncommutativity of quantum meausurements and, more generally, of arbitrary quantum processes.

Suppose that an experimenter is given access to two black boxes, acting on a
d
-dimensional quantum system. The two black boxes implement two quantum processes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
with Kraus representations
\mathscr{C}(\rho)=\sum_{i}C_{i}\rho C_{i}^{\dagger}
and
\mathscr{D}(\rho)=\sum_{j}D_{j}\rho D_{j}^{\dagger}
, respectively. The goal of the experimenter is to estimate the noncommutativity of the Kraus operators
(C_{i})_{i}
and
(D_{j})_{j}
. To this purpose, one can combine the two boxes in the quantum switch
Chiribella et al. 2009a
;
Chiribella et al. 2013
, generating a new quantum process
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}}
with Kraus operators

S_{ij}=C_{i}D_{j}\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|+D_{j}C_{i}\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|

(6)

where
\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}
are basis states of a control qubit.
The action of the channel
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}}
on a generic product state
\rho\otimes\omega
is

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}}(\rho\otimes\omega)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ij}\Bigl(\{C_{i},D_{j}\}\rho\{C_{i},D_{j}\}^{\dagger}\otimes\omega

\displaystyle+\{C_{i},D_{j}\}\rho[C_{i},D_{j}]^{\dagger}\otimes\omega Z

\displaystyle+[C_{i},D_{j}]\rho\{C_{i},D_{j}\}^{\dagger}\otimes Z\omega

\displaystyle+[C_{i},D_{j}]\rho[C_{i},D_{j}]^{\dagger}\otimes Z\omega Z\Bigr),

(7)

where
[C_{i},D_{j}]:=C_{i}D_{j}-D_{j}C_{i}
denotes the commutator,
\{C_{i},D_{j}\}:=C_{i}D_{j}+D_{j}C_{i}
denotes the anti-commutator, and
Z:=|0\rangle\langle 0|-|1\rangle\langle 1|
.

To estimate the noncommutativity of
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
, the experimenter can initialize the control qubit in the maximally coherent state
\omega=|+\rangle\langle+|
, apply the quantum channel
\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})
, and measure the control system in the Fourier basis
\{|+\rangle,|-\rangle\}
, with
|\pm\rangle:=(|0\rangle\pm|1\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. Using Eq. (
Measuring incompatibility and clustering quantum observables with a quantum switch
), one can see that the probability of the outcome “
-
” is

\displaystyle p_{-}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_{ij}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho~\Big|[C_{i},D_{j}]\Big|^{2}\right)\,,

(8)

where
|O|^{2}:=O^{\dagger}O
denotes the modulus square of an arbitrary operator
O
.

We define the
non-commutativity
of two generic quantum processes
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
relative to the state
\rho
as

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}):=\sqrt{2p_{-}}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{ij}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho~\Big|[C_{i},D_{j}]\Big|^{2}\right)}{2}}

(9)

(in the special case
\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{D}
and
\rho=I/d
, a related definition was used in
Loizeau and Grinbaum 2020
to quantify the degree of non-commutativity of the Kraus operators of a given channel). It is evident from the definition that
{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})
is symmetric and non-negative. When the matrix
\rho
is invertible,
{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})
is a faithful measure of non-commutativity,
i.e.

{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})=0
if and only if every Kraus operator
\mathscr{C}
commutes with every Kraus operator of
\mathscr{D}
. For composite systems, it is possible to show that the noncommutativity between a maximally entangled measurement and product measurement is at least
\sqrt{1-1/d_{\min}}
,
d_{\min}
being the dimension of the smallest subsystem (see Appendix
B
).

In the special case where
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
are two dephasing channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, explicit calculation yields the relation

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})={\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\,.

(10)

Hence, the MED of two unknown observables can be directly estimated from experimental data.
Crucially, the sample complexity of the estimation procedure is independent of the dimension of the system: for a fixed error threshold
\epsilon
and for every state
\rho
, the estimate of
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
can be guaranteed to have error at most
\epsilon
with probability at least
1-\delta
by repeating the experiment for
n=\log{\frac{2}{\delta}}/(2\epsilon^{2})
times (see Appendix
C
).

The experimental estimation of the MED is feasible with photonic systems, in particular in the case where the state
\rho
is maximally mixed, and its preparation can be achieved by generation two-photon Bell states.
The preparation of the maximally mixed state is also standard in the DQC1 model of quantum computing
Knill and Laflamme 1998
and can be well approximated in other models of quantum computing with highly mixed states
Ambainis et al. 2000
.
In Appendix
D
we also discuss the advantages of the quantum switch set up with respect to other ways to estimating the MED.

Besides providing direct way to the experimental estimation of the MED, the relation with the noncommutativity also provides an alternative route to its analytical/numerical evaluation. In Appendix
E
we show that the noncommutativity can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}[D\,\check{C}\,(I\otimes\rho^{T})]}\,,

(11)

where
\check{C}
and
D
are two operators associated to the maps
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
, respectively. When the operators
\check{C}
,
D
and
\rho
have a suitable tensor network structure, Eq. (
11
) provides a way to efficiently evaluate the noncommutativity, avoiding the sums in Eqs. (
5
) and (
9
) which may contain an exponentially large number of terms when the system has exponentially large dimension. In Appendix
F
we also show another equivalent expression that reduces the noncommutativity to the overlap between two pure states, a task that can be carried out efficiently in a variety of physically relevant cases, including
e.g.
matrix product
Fannes et al. 1992
;
Verstraete and Cirac 2006
;
Perez-Garcia et al. 2007
and MERA states
Vidal 2008
.

Clustering algorithm for quantum observables.
—We now provide a machine learning algorithm for identifying clusters of observables that are approximately compatible with one another. The algorithm is unsupervised: the learner does not need to be trained with labelled examples of observables belonging to different clusters.

The input of the algorithm is the access to
m
black boxes, implementing
m
unknown dephasing channels
\mathscr{A}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{A}_{m}
associated to non-degenerate quantum observables
A_{1},\dots,A_{m}
. The quantum part of the algorithm is the estimation of the MED for every pair of observables. Then, the estimated values of the MED are fed into a classical clustering algorithm. Here we choose
k
-medoids clustering with
k
-means++ style initial seeding
Pedregosa et al. 2011
;
Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2007
. Compared to the popular
k
-means method,
k
-medoids works better (in terms of convergence) with arbitrary dissimilarity measures.

To illustrate the algorithm, we generate
m=100
random qubit observables, of the form
A_{l}=b^{(l)}_{x}\,X+b^{(l)}_{y}\,Y+b^{(l)}_{z}\,Z
,
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
, where
X,Y,Z
are the three Pauli matrices and
\mathbf{b}_{l}=(b^{l}_{x},b^{l}_{y},b^{l}_{z})\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a unit vector (the Bloch vector of the
l
-th observable). The vectors are generated in the following way: for 50 observables, we start from the Bloch vector
(1,0,0)
and apply a rotation by a random angle
\theta
with
|\theta|\leq 22.5\degree
about a random rotation axis. For the remaining 50 observables, we start from the Bloch vector
(0,0,1)
and apply the same procedure. In this way, the 100 observables are naturally divided into two clusters, as in Figure
1
.

We performed a numerical experiment on the classical part of the algorithm, feeding the values of the MED into the
k
-medoids algorithm. For improved reliability, we repeated the experiment for 50 times, finding that in each repetition all the
100
observables are correctly clustered.
Note that, while we fed the algorithm with the exact values of the MED, the robustness of the
k
-medoids
Pedregosa et al. 2011
;
Arthur and Vassilvitskii 2007
algorithm implies that the results are robust to errors in the estimation of the MED from actual experimental data.

Clustering with noisy observables.
—Our clustering algorithm can also be extended to noisy measurements. Following
Heinosaari et al. 2016
, the noise is modelled by randomizing the measurement of each ideal observable with a trivial measurement, which produces the same outcome statistics for every possible input state. Mathematically, this means that the projective measurement
\mathbf{P}^{(l)}
, associated to the
l
-th observable, is replaced by a non-projective measurement
\mathbf{N}^{(l)}=(1-\lambda_{l})\,\mathbf{P}^{(l)}+\lambda_{l}\,\mathbf{T}^{(l)}
, where
\lambda_{l}
is the noise probability, and
\mathbf{T}^{(l)}
is a trivial measurement, with POVM operators
T^{(l)}_{i}=p_{i}^{(l)}\,I
, for a fixed probability distribution
\mathbf{p}^{(l)}=(p^{(l)}_{i})
. For the measurement process associated to the noisy measurement
\mathbf{N}^{(l)}
, we take quantum instruments with Kraus operators of the form
N^{(l)}_{ij}=\sqrt{a^{(l)}_{ij}\,P_{i}^{(l)}+b^{(l)}_{ij}\,I}
, where
a^{(l)}_{ij}
and
b^{(l)}_{ij}
are arbitrary nonnegative coefficients subject to the constraints
\sum_{j}a_{ij}^{(l)}=1-\lambda_{l}
and
\sum_{j}b_{ij}^{(l)}=\lambda_{l}\,p_{i}^{(l)}
for every
i
and
l
. The
k
-medoid algorithm can then be applied, using the noncommutativity (
9
) of the noisy channels
\mathscr{N}_{1},\dots,\mathscr{N}_{m}
defined by
\mathscr{N}_{l}(\rho):=\sum_{ij}N_{ij}^{(l)}\rho N^{(l){\dagger}}_{ij}
for
l\in\{1,\dots,m\}
.

To test the algorithm, we performed a numerical experiment on 100 randomly generated noisy qubit observables.
For simplicity, we chose isotropic noise
Dall’Arno et al. 2010
and set the noise randomly following a uniform distribution, picking each probability
p_{i}^{(l)}
uniformly at random in the interval
(0,1)
, subject to the constraint
\sum_{i}p_{i}^{(l)}=1
,
\forall l
.
For the original observables, we generated the Bloch vectors as in the noiseless case. For the noise, we first defined a maximum noise level
\eta
and then we picked a random noise probability
\lambda_{l}=\eta\,R_{l}
where each
R_{l}
is chosen independently, uniformly at random in the interval
[0,1]
. The coefficients
a_{ij}^{(l)}
and
b_{ij}^{(l)}
are then chosen uniformly at random, subject to the constraints. The experiment has been performed for
\eta=0.25
,
0.5
, and
0.75
, and for each setting the
k
-medoids algorithm has been run 50 times.
The results of the experiment, plotted on Fig.
2
, show that perfect clustering is still achieved in the presence of noise.

Conclusions and outlook.
—In this paper we introduced the MED, an experimentally accessible measure of incompatibility for sharp observables. The MED quantifies the noncommutativity of the projectors associated to a pair of observables, and can be directly measured without access to the measurement outcomes, by letting the two measurement processes act in an indefinite order. Thanks to its properties, the MED can be used in quantum machine learning tasks, such as clustering unknown observables based on their degree of compatibility.

An interesting direction of future research is to extend the results of this paper to infinite dimensional systems, unsharp observables, and general quantum channels. For sharp observables with discrete spectrum, our approach can be easily extended by taking a limit of finite dimensional subspaces. For observables with continuous spectrum, however, the situation is more complex, due to the fact that no repeatable measurement exists
Ozawa 1984
. Regarding the extension to unsharp observables and general channels, one approach is to focus on the noncommutativity, which can be measured with the quantum switch. On the other hand, commutativity is not a necessary condition, and an open question is to determine whether there exists a measure of incompatibility that can be measured experimentally like the MED.

Another direction is the extension of the MED from pairs to arbitrary numbers of observables.
One option would be to generalize our definition, examining the amount of disturbance on the eigenspace of one observable induced by measurements of the other observables. An appealing feature is that the resulting quantity could be estimated by placing the measurements in a superposition of orders, in a similar way as it was done in our paper for the case of two observables.

Appendix A
Properties of the MED and its generalization

Here we establish the main properties of the MED, including symmetry, nonnegativity, faithfullness, robustness to noise, being a metric for von Neumann measurements, and reaching its maximum value for mutually unbiased bases. Some of the properties will be established for a generalized version of the MED, which can also be experimentally accessed using the quantum switch.

In the following, we will often consider the
generalized MED

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B}):=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i,j}{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}\,.

(12)

Note that, by definition, one has

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\frac{I}{d}}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}\equiv{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\,,

(13)

for every pair of observables
A
and
B
. In the following we establish a number of properties of
{\rm MED}_{\rho}
and
{\rm MED}
. As it turns out, some of the properties of
{\rm MED}_{\rho}
require the density matrix
\rho
to have some properties, such as being invertible, or commuting with the projectors onto the eigenspaces of
A
and
B
. All these properties are automatically satisfied by the choice
\rho=I/d
.

Symmetry.
By definition, one has

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i,j}{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i,j}{\sf Re}\overline{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i,j}{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}\left[(\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j})^{\dagger}\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i,j}{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}\rho]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\sum_{i,j}{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}]}

\displaystyle={\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A})\,.

(14)

Here the fourth equality follows from the property
(XY)^{\dagger}=Y^{\dagger}X^{\dagger}
, valid for arbitrary operators
X
and
Y
, along with the fact that the operators
\rho,P_{i},
and
Q_{j}
are self-adjoint. The fifth equality follows from the cyclic property of the trace.

Nonnegativity.
The generalized MED can be equivalently expressed as

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}\Big\{[P_{i},Q_{j}]\rho\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}\Big\}}{2}}

(15)

The right-hand side is non-negative, because each of the operators
[P_{i},Q_{j}]\rho\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}
is non-negative, and therefore has a non-negative trace.

Faithfulness.
We now show that the generalized MED is faithful for every invertible state
\rho
: for two arbitrary dephasing channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
,
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})>0
if and only if the observables
A
and
B
are incompatible.

The proof is based on Eq. (
15
), which shows that
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is non-negative and equals to zero if and only if
\operatorname{Tr}\big\{[P_{i},Q_{j}]\rho\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}\big\}=0
for every
i
and every
j
. By the cyclicity of the trace, this condition holds if and only if

\operatorname{Tr}\big\{\sqrt{\rho}\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}[P_{i},Q_{j}]\sqrt{\rho}\big\}=0
for every
i
and every
j
. Moreover, since each operator
\sqrt{\rho}\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}[P_{i},Q_{j}]\sqrt{\rho}
is positive, its trace is zero if and only if the operator itself is zero. Finally, since
\rho
is invertible, the condition
\sqrt{\rho}\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}[P_{i},Q_{j}]\sqrt{\rho}=0
holds if and only if
[P_{i},Q_{j}]^{\dagger}[P_{i},Q_{j}]=0
, or equivalently, if and only if
[P_{i},Q_{j}]=0
. Summarizing, the condition
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=0
is equivalent to the condition
[P_{i},Q_{j}]=0\,,\forall i,j
, which is equivalent to the compatibility of the observables
A
and
B
.

Monotonicity under coarse-graining.
Here we show that
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
is non-increasing under coarse-graining, provided that the density matrix
\rho
commutes with the measurement that is being coarse-grained:

Proposition 1

If the state
\rho
commutes with the observable
A
, then one has
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\geq{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B})
whenever
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is the dephasing channel associated to a coarse-grained measurement of
A
.

Proof.
Let
\mathbf{P}^{\prime}:=(P^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{o^{\prime}}
be a coarse-graining of the measurement
\mathbf{P}=(P_{i})_{i=1}^{o}
, meaning that there is a surjective function
f:\{1,\dots,o\}\to\{1,\dots,o^{\prime}\}
such that

\displaystyle P_{k}^{\prime}=\sum_{i:f(i)=k}\,P_{i}\,.

(16)

Note that, by definition, one has the operator inequality

\displaystyle P_{i}\leq P_{f(i)}^{\prime}\,.

(17)

Now, suppose that the density matrix
\rho
commutes with the projectors
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{o}
. In this case, one has the relation

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i^{\prime}}Q_{j}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\sqrt{\rho}P_{i}\sqrt{\rho})\,(Q_{j}P_{i^{\prime}}Q_{j})]

\displaystyle\geq 0\,,

(18)

valid for every pair of indices
i
and
i^{\prime}
. Here, the last inequality follows from the fact that the operators
\sqrt{\rho}P_{i}\sqrt{\rho}
and
Q_{j}P_{i^{\prime}}Q_{j}
are positive semidefinite, and therefore the trace of their product is non-negative.

In particular, Eq. (
A
) implies that
\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]
is a real number. Hence, the real part in the definition of the generalized MED can be dropped, and one has

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=1-\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]\,.

(19)

Since
\rho
commmutes also with the projectors
(P^{\prime}_{k})_{k=1}^{o^{\prime}}
, we also have the relation

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B})=1-\sum_{k,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{k}^{\prime}Q_{j}P_{k}^{\prime}Q_{j}]\,,

(20)

where
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is the dephasing channel associated to the coarse-grained measurement
(P_{k}^{\prime})_{k=1}^{o^{\prime}}

At this point, it is easy to see that
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B})\leq{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
. Indeed, one has the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{k,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{k}^{\prime}\,Q_{j}P_{k}^{\prime}Q_{j}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{k,j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:f(i)=k\\
i^{\prime}:f(i^{\prime})=k\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}\,Q_{j}P_{i^{\prime}}Q_{j}]

(21)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}\,Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]

\displaystyle+\sum_{k,j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:f(i)=k\\
i^{\prime}:f(i^{\prime})=k\\
i\neq i^{\prime}\end{subarray}}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}\,Q_{j}P_{i^{\prime}}Q_{j}]

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}\,Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}],

where the last inequality follows from Eq. (
A
). The inequality
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B})\leq{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
then follows by combining Eq. (
21
) with Eqs. (
19
) and (
20
).
\blacksquare

Metric on von Neumann measurements.
We now show that the generalized MED based on an invertible state is a metric on the set of rank-one projective measurements.

Lemma 1

For every pair of dephasing channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
associated to rank-one measurements, one has the expression

\displaystyle{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\,\|({\tau_{\mathscr{A}}-\tau_{\mathscr{B}}})\,\,(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes I)\|_{2}\,,

(22)

where
\|X\|_{2}=\sqrt{\operatorname{Tr}[X^{\dagger}X]}
is the Hilbert-Schmidt norm, and
\tau_{\mathscr{A}}
and
\tau_{\mathscr{B}}
are the Choi operators of
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, given by

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathscr{A}}:=\sum_{m,n}\mathscr{A}(|m\rangle\langle n|)\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|=\sum_{i}P_{i}\otimes\overline{P}_{i},

(23)

\displaystyle\tau_{\mathscr{B}}:=\sum_{m,n}\mathscr{B}(|m\rangle\langle n|)\otimes|m\rangle\langle n|=\sum_{j}Q_{j}\otimes\overline{Q}_{j}\,,

(24)

with
\overline{X}
denoting the complex conjugate of a matrix
X
.

Proof.
Let us define the product

\displaystyle\langle X,Y\rangle_{\rho}:=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,X^{\dagger}Y]\,.

(25)

For every self-adjoint operator
\tau
, we have the relation

\displaystyle\|\tau\,(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes I)\,\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes I)\tau^{2}(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes I)]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\tau^{2}]

\displaystyle=\langle\tau,\tau\rangle_{\rho}\,.

(26)

Using the two equations above, we obtain

\displaystyle||({\tau_{\mathscr{A}}-\tau_{\mathscr{B}}})\,(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes I)\,||_{2}^{2}=

\displaystyle\langle(\tau_{\mathscr{A}}-\tau_{\mathscr{B}}),(\tau_{\mathscr{A}}-\tau_{\mathscr{B}})\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{\mathscr{A}},\tau_{\mathscr{A}}\rangle_{\rho}+\langle\tau_{\mathscr{B}},\tau_{\mathscr{B}}\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle\quad-\langle\tau_{\mathscr{A}},\tau_{\mathscr{B}}\rangle_{\rho}-\langle\tau_{\mathscr{B}},\tau_{\mathscr{A}}\rangle_{\rho}\,.

(27)

Note that one has

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{\mathscr{A}},\tau_{\mathscr{A}}\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,\tau_{\mathscr{A}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,\tau_{\mathscr{A}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}]\,\times\,\operatorname{Tr}[\overline{P}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}]\,

\displaystyle=1\,,

(28)

where the second equality follows from the fact that
\tau_{\mathscr{A}}
is a projector, the third equality follows from Eq. (
23
), the fourth equality follows from the fact that the measurement is rank-one (and therefore
\operatorname{Tr}[\overline{P}_{i}]=1
for every
i
), and the fifth equality follows from the normalization condition
\sum_{i}P_{i}=I
.
Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{\mathscr{B}},\tau_{\mathscr{B}}\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,\tau_{\mathscr{B}}^{2}]

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,\tau_{\mathscr{B}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,Q_{j}]\,\times\,\operatorname{Tr}[\overline{Q}_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,Q_{j}]

\displaystyle=1\,.

(29)

For the remaining terms, we have

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{\mathscr{A}},\tau_{\mathscr{B}}\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,{\tau_{\mathscr{A}}^{\dagger}\tau_{\mathscr{B}}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}Q_{j}]\,\times\,\operatorname{Tr}[\overline{P}_{i}\,\overline{Q}_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}Q_{j}]\,\times\,\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}\,Q_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]\,,

(30)

where the last equality follows from the fact that the operators
P_{i}
and
Q_{j}
are rank-one.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\langle\tau_{\mathscr{B}},\tau_{\mathscr{A}}\rangle_{\rho}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[(\rho\otimes I)\,{\tau_{\mathscr{B}}^{\dagger}\tau_{\mathscr{A}}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,Q_{j}P_{i}]\,\times\,\operatorname{Tr}[\overline{Q}_{j}\overline{P}_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,Q_{j}P_{i}]\,\times\,\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{j}P_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\,\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\,\overline{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho\,P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}.

(31)

Hence,

\displaystyle\|{\tau_{\mathscr{A}}-\tau_{\mathscr{B}}}\|^{2}=

\displaystyle 2-2{\sf Re}\,\sum_{i,j}{\operatorname{Tr}[\rho P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\left[{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\right]^{2}.

(32)

Eq. (
A
) shows that
{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})
coincides with the Hilbert-Schmidt norm
||({\tau_{\mathscr{A}}-\tau_{\mathscr{B}}})\,(\sqrt{\rho}\otimes I)||_{2}
up to a constant factor.

\blacksquare

Lemma 2

For every invertible density matrix
\rho
,
{\rm MED}_{\rho}
is a metric on the space of von Neumann measurements.

Proof.
To prove that
{\rm MED}_{\rho}
is a metric, we need to show that

1.

{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})={\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A})
for every
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
(symmetry)

2.

{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\geq 0
for every
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
, with the equality if and only if
\mathscr{A}=\mathscr{B}
(nonnegativity and identity of indiscernibles)

3.

{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{C})\leq{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})+{\rm MED}_{\rho}(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{C})
(triangle inequality).

Symmetry was established at the beginning of this Supplemental Material, in Eq. (
14
).

Nonegativity of the generalized MED was also established earlier in this Supplemental Material in the demonstration of faithfulness of
{\rm MED}_{\rho}
. When
\rho
is invertible, we also showed that
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=0
implies
[P{{}_{i},Q_{j}}]=0
for every
i
and
j
.
For von Neumann measurements,
(P_{i})_{i}
and
(Q_{j})_{j}
are two maximal sets of rank-one projectors, and the commutation condition means that there exists a permutation
\pi:\{1,\dots,d\}\to\{1,\dots,d\}
such that
P_{i}=Q_{\pi(i)}
. In this case, one has
\mathscr{A}(X)=\sum_{i}P_{i}XP_{i}=\sum_{i}Q_{\pi(i)}XQ_{\pi(i)}=\sum_{j}Q_{j}XQ_{j}=\mathscr{B}(X)
for every
d\times d
matrix
X
.

Finally, the triangle inequality can be deduced from Eq. (
22
) of Lemma
1
and from the triangle inequality of the Hilbert-Schmidt norm.
\blacksquare

Robustness to noise.
We have seen that the generalized MED based on an invertible state
\rho
is a faithful measure of noncommutativity. We now show that this faithfulness property is preserved even when the ideal projective measurements of
A
and
B
are replaced by noisy measurements. Precisely, we consider the noisy scenario where the
canonical channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
are replaced by quantum channels
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
of the form
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}=\sum_{i,k}A_{i,k}\rho A_{i,k}^{\dagger}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}(\rho)=\sum_{j,l}B_{j,l}\rho B_{j,l}^{\dagger}
, with

\displaystyle\sum_{k}A_{i,k}^{\dagger}A_{i,k}=(1-\lambda)\,P_{i}+\lambda p_{i}\,I

(33)

and

\displaystyle\sum_{l}B_{j,l}^{\dagger}B_{j,l}=(1-\mu)\,Q_{j}+\mu\,q_{j}\,I,

(34)

with suitable probabilities
\lambda,\mu\in[0,1]
and suitable probability distributions
\mathbf{p}
and
\mathbf{q}
.
In the following, we show robustness under the assumption that at least one of the two channels
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
is self-adjoint (recall that a linear map
\mathscr{M}
is self-adjoint if, for every pair of
d\times d
matrices
X
and
Y
, one has
\operatorname{Tr}[X\mathscr{M}(Y)]=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathscr{M}(X)Y]
).

In the noisy case, we consider the noncommutativity

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{A}^{\prime},\mathscr{B}^{\prime})=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i,j,k,l}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\rho~\Big|[A_{i,k},B_{j,l}]\Big|^{2}\right)}{2}}\,.

(35)

If this quantity is zero, then each of the commutators
[A_{i,k},B_{j,l}]
must vanish, i.e. one must have the relations

\displaystyle[A_{i,k},B_{j,l}]=0\qquad\forall i,j,k,l

(36)

and

\displaystyle[A_{i,k}^{\dagger},B_{j,l}^{\dagger}]=0\qquad\forall i,j,k,l\,,

(37)

where the second relation is obtained from the first by taking the adjoint on both sides of the equality sign.

Note that the above relations must hold for every possible Kraus decomposition of the channels
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
. If channel
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
is self-adjoint, the operators
(A_{i,k})_{i,k}
also form a Kraus representation, and therefore one must have the relation

\displaystyle[A_{i,k}^{\dagger},B_{j,l}]=0\qquad\forall i,j,k,l\,,

(38)

and, taking the adjoint on both sides of the equality

\displaystyle[A_{i,k},B_{j,l}^{\dagger}]=0\qquad\forall i,j,k,l\,.

(39)

Similarly, if channel
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
is self-adjoint, the above relations must hold. Using Eqs. (
33
), (
34
, (
36
), (
37
), (
38
), and (
39
), we then obtain

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)(1-\mu)\,[P_{i},Q_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k,l}[A_{i,k}^{\dagger}A_{i,k},B_{j,l}^{\dagger}B_{j,l}]=0\qquad\forall i,j\,.

(40)

Hence, if the noncommutativity of the channels
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
and
\mathscr{B}^{\prime}
is zero, then the ideal measurements
(P_{i})_{i}
and
(Q_{j})_{j}
must be compatible, for every value of the noise parameters
\lambda
and
\mu
except in the trivial case
\lambda=1
or
\mu=1
, in which the original measurements are replaced by white noise.

Maximality for maximally complementary observables.
We now show the inequality
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-1/\min\{k_{A},k_{B}\}}
, where
k_{A}
(
k_{B}
) is the number of projectors in the spectral decomposition of the observable
A
(
B
). The maximum value is given by
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=\sqrt{1-1/d}
and attained if and only if
A
and
B
are maximally complementary
Kraus 1987
or in other words, their POVM operators are rank-one projectors onto the basis vectors of two mutually unbiased bases.

The proof uses a series of lemmas.

Lemma 3

Let
A,B
be
n\times n
Hermitian matrices: If
A
and
B
are positive semi-definite, then
AB
is diagonalizable and has non-negative eigenvalues.

Proof.
The proof can be found in Corollary 7.6.2(b) of Ref.
Horn and Johnson 2013
.
\blacksquare

Lemma 4

For every pair of observables
A
and
B
, one has the bound
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{k_{B}}}
where
k_{B}
is the number of distinct eigenvalues of
B
. The equality holds only if
\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]
is independent of
j
.

Proof.
Using the definitions (
12
) and (
13
), we focus on a term
\sum_{j}{\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}
, fixing thereby the index
i
. If
P_{i}
is a projector of rank
r_{i}
, then

\operatorname{rank}(P_{i}Q_{j})\leq r_{i}.

(41)

Both
P_{i}
and
Q_{j}
are orthogonal projectors, they are Hermitian and positive semi-definite operators. Using Lemma
3
, we can diagonalize the operator
P_{i}Q_{j}
as

\displaystyle P_{i}Q_{j}=X_{ij}\Delta_{ij}X_{ij}^{-1},

where
\Delta_{ij}
is a diagonal matrix with non-negative entries. Let
r_{ij}
be the rank of
\Delta_{ij}
and assume that the first
r_{ij}
diagonal entries of
\Delta_{ij}
, denoted by
\lambda_{ij,1},\cdots\lambda_{ij,r_{i}}
, are non-zero. Then, we can write

\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]=\sum_{s=1}^{r_{ij}}{\lambda_{ij,s}}.

(42)

Taking into account the relation
\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]=\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}]=:r_{i}
, we find

\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\sum_{s=1}^{r_{ij}}{\lambda_{ij,s}}=r_{i}.

(43)

On the other hand,

\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\sum_{s=1}^{r_{ij}}{(\lambda_{ij,s})^{2}}.

(44)

The minimum of (
44
) under the constraint (
43
) can be computed with the method of Lagrange multipliers.
The coefficients that minimize (
44
) are given by

\displaystyle\lambda^{\min}_{ij,s}=\frac{r_{i}}{\sum_{l=1}^{k_{B}}r_{il}}\qquad\forall j\in\{1,\dots,k_{B}\},\,\forall s\in\{1\dots,r_{ij}\}\,.

(45)

Plugging the optimal coefficients into the right hand side of Eq. (
44
) we then obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]\geq\frac{r_{i}^{2}}{\sum_{l=1}^{k_{B}}r_{il}}\,.

(46)

Now, recall that
r_{il}=\operatorname{rank}(P_{i}Q_{l})\leq\operatorname{rank}(P_{i})=r_{i}
. Plugging this relation into the previous inequality, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]\geq\frac{r_{i}}{k_{B}}\,.

(47)

Finally, summing over
i
yields the lower bound

\sum_{i=1}^{k_{A}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]\geq\frac{d}{k_{B}}\,.

(48)

Hence, we obtained

\displaystyle{\rm{MED}}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}{d}}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{k_{B}}}\,.

(49)

A necessary condition for achieving the equality is that the eigenv0alues
\lambda_{ij,s}
depend only on
i
(and not on
j
and
s
), cf. Eq. (
45
). This condition implies in particular that the sum
\sum_{s}\,\lambda_{ij,s}=\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]
is independent of
j
.
\blacksquare

Lemma 5

For every two observables
A
and
B
, one has the bound
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\min\{k_{A},k_{B}\}}}
. The equality is achieved only if

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]=\frac{d}{k_{A}k_{B}}\qquad\forall i\in\{1,\dots,k_{A}\}\,,\forall j\in\{1,\dots,k_{B}\}\,.

(50)

Proof.
Lemma
4
implies the upper bound
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{k_{B}}}
. Moreover, the symmetry of the MED yields the condition
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})={\rm MED}(\mathscr{B},\mathscr{A})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{k_{A}}}
(the inequality following again from Lemma
4
). Hence, one has the bound
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{\min\{k_{A},k_{B}\}}}
. By Lemma
4
, the equality holds only if
\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]
is independent of
j
, and only
\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{j}P_{i}]\equiv\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]
is independent of
i
. In summary, it is necessary that
\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]
is constant, say
\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]=c
for some constant
c
and for every
i
and
j
. The value of the constant can be obtained from the condition

\displaystyle d

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[I]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{A}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\,\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{A}}\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\,c

\displaystyle=k_{A}\,k_{B}\,c\,,

(51)

which implies
c=d/(k_{A}k_{B})
.

\blacksquare

Lemma 6

For every two observables
A
and
B
, one has the bound
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}}
and the equality holds if and only if
A
and
B
are non-degenerate and their eigenvectors form two mutually unbiased bases.

Proof.
The inequality
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})\leq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}}
is immediate from Lemma
5
and from the fact that
\min\{k_{A},k_{B}\}
is at most
d
. Let us now determine when the equality sign is attained. A first necessary condition is that
\min\{k_{A},k_{B}\}=d
, which implies
k_{A}=k_{B}=d
, that is, both observables
A
and
B
are nondegenerate. The necessary condition in Lemma
5
then becomes
\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}]=1/d\,,\forall i,j
. Hence,
P_{i}
and
Q_{j}
must be projectors on two vectors from two mutually unbiased bases.

Conversely, suppose that
(|\alpha_{i}\rangle)_{i=1}^{d}

and
(|\beta_{j}\rangle)_{j=1}^{d}
are two mutually unbiased bases, and that
(P_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
and
(Q_{j})_{j=1}^{d}
are the corresponding rank-1 projectors. Then, we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]=\left|\langle\alpha_{i}|\beta_{j}\rangle\right|^{4}=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\qquad\forall i,j\,.

(52)

Hence, the MED of the corresponding observables is

\displaystyle{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}\,\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[P_{i}Q_{j}P_{i}Q_{j}]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}}\,.

(53)

In summary, the MED reaches the maximum value
{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})=\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d}}
if and only if
A
and
B
are nondegenerate observables associated to mutually unbiased bases.

\blacksquare

Appendix B
Noncommutativity between entangled and product measurements

The mathematical description of a measurement process, including both the measurement statistics and the post-measurement states, is provided by a quantum instrument, that is, a collection of completely positive, trace non-increasing maps
(\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k}
such that the sum
\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathscr{C}_{i}
is trace-preserving. Here the index
i
labels the possible measurement outcomes, which occur on an input state
\rho
with probability
p(i|\rho)=\operatorname{Tr}[\mathscr{C}_{i}(\rho)]
, leaving the system in the post-measurement state
\rho_{i}=\mathscr{C}_{i}(\rho)/\operatorname{Tr}[\mathscr{C}_{i}(\rho)]
. The average evolution due to the measurement is then given by the quantum channel
\mathscr{C}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathscr{C}_{i}
.

We define the noncommutativity between two instruments as the noncommutativity of the corresponding average evolutions:

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}\left((\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}}\,,\,(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\right):={\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})\,,

(54)

with
\mathscr{C}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k_{A}}\mathscr{C}_{i}
and
\mathscr{D}:=\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\mathscr{D}_{j}
.

We now provide a lower bound on the noncommutativity between a maximally entangled measurement and a product measurement. Consider the case of a bipartite system, consisting of two subsystems
S_{1}
and
S_{2}
, of dimensions
2\leq d_{1}\leq d_{2}
. By “maximally entangled measurement" we mean a quantum instrument
(\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}}
of the form
\mathscr{C}_{i}(\rho)=C_{i}\rho C_{i}^{\dagger}
with
C_{i}=\lambda_{i}|\Psi_{i}\rangle\langle\Phi_{i}|
where
|\Phi_{i}\rangle
and
|\Psi_{i}\rangle
are maximally entangled states, and
0\leq\lambda_{i}\leq 1
is a suitable coefficient. By a “product measurement”, we mean an instrument
(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}
of the form
\mathscr{D}_{j}(\rho)=D_{j}\rho D_{j}^{\dagger}
with
D_{j}=\mu_{j}\,|\gamma_{j}\rangle\langle\alpha_{j}|\otimes|\delta_{j}\rangle\langle\beta_{j}|
, where
|\alpha_{j}\rangle
and
|\gamma_{j}\rangle
(
|\beta\rangle
and
|\delta_{j}\rangle
) are pure states of system
S_{1}
(
S_{2}
), and
0\leq\mu_{j}\leq 1
is a suitable coefficient. (Note that we are not assuming that the instrument
(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}
can be realized by local operations and classical communication. In other words, our notion of product measurement corresponds to fine-grained separable instruments
Chitambar et al. 2014
, which are not necessarily realizable through local operations and classical communication).

The noncommutativity of the instruments
(\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}}
and
(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}
is given by

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}\Big((\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}},(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\Big)

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\rho\,\big|[C_{i},D_{j}]\big|^{2}\Big)}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[C_{i}^{\dagger}D_{j}^{\dagger}C_{i}D_{j}\rho]\right]}\,.

(55)

Here we consider the case where the state
\rho
is maximally mixed, namely
\rho=I_{1}/d_{1}\otimes I_{2}/d_{2}
. In this case, one has the bound

\displaystyle{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[C_{i}^{\dagger}D_{j}^{\dagger}C_{i}D_{j}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i,j}\left|\operatorname{Tr}[C_{i}^{\dagger}D_{j}^{\dagger}C_{i}D_{j}]\right|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\big|\langle\Phi_{i}|D_{j}|\Phi_{i}\rangle\,\langle\Psi_{i}|D_{j}^{\dagger}|\Psi_{i}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sum_{i,j}\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\mu_{j}\,\big|\langle\Phi_{i}|D_{j}|\Phi_{i}\rangle\,\big|\,,

(56)

the last bound following form the expression
D_{j}=\mu_{j}\,|\gamma_{j}\rangle\langle\alpha_{j}|\otimes|\delta_{j}\rangle\langle\beta_{j}|
and from the fact that the state
|\Psi_{i}\rangle
is maximally entangled.

Using the polar decomposition
D_{j}=U|D_{j}|
, we then have the bound

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\big|\langle\Phi_{i}|D_{j}|\Phi_{i}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\big|\langle\Phi_{i}|~U|D_{j}|~|\Phi_{i}\rangle\big|

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\langle\Phi_{i}|~|D_{j}|~|\Phi_{i}\rangle}

\displaystyle\quad\times\sqrt{\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\,\langle\Phi_{i}|U|D_{j}|U^{\dagger}|\Phi_{i}\rangle}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}[|D_{j}|]

\displaystyle=\mu_{j}\,,

(57)

the second to last equality following from the completeness relation
\sum_{i=1}^{k_{A}}\lambda_{i}^{2}|\Phi_{i}\rangle\langle\Phi_{i}|=I_{1}\otimes I_{2}
, implied by the normalization of the instrument
(\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}}
.

Summarizing, we obtained the bound

\displaystyle{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}[C_{i}^{\dagger}D_{j}^{\dagger}C_{i}D_{j}]\right]

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{d_{1}}\sum_{j}\mu_{j}^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d_{1}}~(d_{1}d_{2})\,,

(58)

where the last equality follows by taking the trace on both sides of the completeness relation
\sum_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\mu_{j}^{2}|\alpha_{j}\rangle\langle\alpha_{j}|\otimes|\beta_{j}\rangle\langle\beta_{j}|=I_{1}\otimes I_{2}
, implied by the normalization of the instrument
(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}
.

Hence, the noncommutativity is lower bounded as

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\frac{I_{1}\otimes I_{2}}{d_{1}d_{2}}}\Big((\mathscr{C}_{i})_{i=1}^{k_{A}},(\mathscr{D}_{j})_{j=1}^{k_{B}}\Big)

\displaystyle\quad=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{i,j}\frac{1}{d_{1}d_{2}}\operatorname{Tr}[C_{i}^{\dagger}D_{j}^{\dagger}C_{i}D_{j}]\right]}

\displaystyle\quad\geq\sqrt{1-\frac{1}{d_{1}}}\,.

(59)

The above bound can be immediately extended to the multipartite case, by considering a bipartition of the system into the lowest dimensional sybsystem and all the remaining ones.

Appendix C
Sample complexity of (generalized) MED estimation

Our protocol provides a direct estimate of the MED in terms of the probability that a Fourier basis measurement on the control system yields outcome “
-
”. The probability can be estimated from the frequency of the outcome “
-
” in a number of repetitions of the experiment. We now show that the number of repetitions is independent of the system’s dimension and scales inverse polynomially with the desired level of accuracy.

The proof is standard, and is provided here just for completeness. The result of the Fourier basis measurement defines a Bernoulli variable with outcomes
k\in\{0,1\}
. Here, outcome 1 corresponds to the outcome corresponding to the basis vector
|-\rangle
, while outcome 0 corresponds to the basis vector
|0\rangle
. The probability mass function of this Bernoulli variable is
f(k;p_{-})
defined by

\displaystyle f(-;p_{-})=p_{-}\qquad{\rm and}\qquad f(+;p_{-})=1-p_{-}\,.

(60)

If the experiment is repeated for
n
times, the outcomes are a sequence of independent Bernoulli variables
X_{1},X_{2},...,X_{n}
, with each variable distributed according to the probability mass function
f(k;p_{-})
.
The sum of these variables, denoted by
S=\sum_{i}X_{i}
, is distributed according to the binomial distribution
B(s;n,p_{-}):=p_{-}^{s}(1-p_{-})^{n-s}\begin{pmatrix}n\\
s\end{pmatrix}
. Then, Hoeffding’s inequality
Hoeffding 2021
implies that the empirical frequency
S/n
is close to
p_{-}
with high probability, namely

\displaystyle\forall\epsilon>0,P\left(\left|\frac{S}{n}-p\right|<\epsilon\right)\geq 1-2e^{-2\epsilon^{2}n},

Hence, the minimum number of repetitions of the experiment needed to guarantee that the empirical frequency has probability at most
\delta
to deviate from the frequency by at most
\epsilon
is

\displaystyle n(\epsilon,\delta)=\left\lceil-\frac{1}{2\epsilon^{2}}\log{\frac{\delta}{2}}\right\rceil\,.

(61)

This expression shows that the sample complexity is independent of the dimension of the system under consideration. In particular, the sample complexity does not increase when the MED is measured on multiparticle systems, in contrast with the sample complexity of process tomography which increases exponentially with the number of particles.

Appendix D
Comparison with other experimental schemes

A naïve way to estimate the MED is to characterize the channels
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
via process tomography
Chuang and Nielsen 1997
;
Poyatos et al. 1997
;
Leung 2003
;
D’Ariano and Presti 2001
;
Dür and Cirac 2001
;
Altepeter et al. 2003
, or the projective measurements
\mathbf{P}
and
\mathbf{Q}
via measurement tomography
D’Ariano et al. 2004
, and then use Eq. (
4
). However, process and measurement tomography requires a number of experimental settings that grows polynomially with the dimension, and therefore exponentially in the number of particles for multiparticle quantum systems.

To avoid the exponential complexity of tomography, one needs a direct measurement protocol whose sample complexity is independent of the system’s dimension, and, therefore, also on the number of particles. A possible approach is to use the operational scheme that motivated the definition of the MED. This scheme, described in the main text, involves the initialization of the system in a random eigenstate of observable
A
. Practically, this can be achieved by preparing the maximally mixed state, and then applying a measurement of the observable
A
. The estimation of the MED would then proceed by performing
a measurement of the observable
B
, and finally, another measurement of the observable
A
. The total probability that the two measurements of
A
give equal outcomes is equal to
{\sf Prob}(A,\mathscr{B})=1-{\rm MED}(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{B})^{2}
. In this case, the complexity of estimating the MED does not grow exponentially with the system’s size.

The main difference between the above scheme and the scheme using the quantum switch is that the above scheme requires access to the outcomes of two measurements of the observable
A
. The ability to estimate the incompatibility without access to the outcomes offers an advantage in situations where the experimenter wants to discover the incompatibility of two observables measured by two parties in their local laboratories. In this case, the quantum switch scheme allows the experimenter to estimate the incompatibility/noncommutativity in a black box fashion, by sending input states to the two laboratories and observing their outputs states, without any access to the outcomes generated inside the laboratories.

Appendix E
Proof of Eq. (11) in the main text

From Eq. (9) in the main text, we have

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}\Big(\rho\,\big|[C_{i},D_{j}]\big|^{2}\Big)}{2}}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{i,j}\operatorname{Tr}\left(C_{i}D_{j}\rho C_{i}^{\dagger}D_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{j}\operatorname{Tr}\left(\mathscr{C}(D_{j}\rho)~D_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{j}\langle\!\langle\,D_{j}\,|\,\mathscr{C}(D_{j}\rho)\,\rangle\!\rangle\right]}\,,

(62)

where we used the double-ket notation
|X\rangle\!\rangle:=\sum_{m,n}\langle m|X|n\rangle\,|m\rangle\otimes|n\rangle
for an arbitrary operator
X
, and the property
\langle\!\langle X|Y\rangle\!\rangle=\operatorname{Tr}[X^{\dagger}Y]=\operatorname{Tr}[YX^{\dagger}]
, valid for arbitrary
X
and
Y
(in our case,
X=D_{j}
and
Y=\mathscr{C}(D_{j}\rho)
).

Now, consider the operator
\check{C}
defined through the relation
Chiribella et al. 2009b

\displaystyle\check{C}|X\rangle\!\rangle:=|\mathscr{C}(X)\rangle\!\rangle\,,\qquad\forall X\,.

(63)

Using this definition, the noncommutativity can be expressed as

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{j}\langle\!\langle\,D_{j}\,|\,\check{C}\,|D_{j}\rho\,\rangle\!\rangle\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\left[\sum_{j}\langle\!\langle\,D_{j}\,|\,\check{C}\,(I\otimes\rho^{T})\,|D_{j}\,\rangle\!\rangle\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}\left[\left(\sum_{j}|D_{j}\,\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle\,D_{j}\,|\right)\,\check{C}\,(I\otimes\rho^{T})\,\right]}

\displaystyle=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}\left[D\,\check{C}\,(I\otimes\rho^{T})\,\right]}\,,

(64)

where
D:=\sum_{j}|D_{j}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle D_{j}|=(\mathscr{D}\otimes\mathscr{I})(|I\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle I|)
is the Choi operator of
\mathscr{D}
, with
\mathscr{I}
being identity channel.

In particular, when the state
\rho
is maximally mixed, the noncommutativity takes the simple expression

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\frac{I}{d}}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})=\sqrt{1-\frac{{\sf Re}\operatorname{Tr}\left[D\,\check{C}\right]}{d}}\,.

(65)

The operator
\check{C}
is in one-to-one correspondence with the map
\mathscr{C}
. Explicitly, Eq. (
63
) implies the explicit expression

\displaystyle\check{C}=\sum_{i}C_{i}\otimes\overline{C}_{i}\,,

(66)

where
\overline{C}_{i}
is the complex conjugate of the matrix
C_{i}
.
The operator
\check{C}
can be equivalently expressed in terms of its Choi operator via the relation

\displaystyle\check{C}=\Big(I_{\mathrm{out},\overline{\mathrm{out}}}\otimes\langle\!\langle I_{\mathrm{in},\overline{\mathrm{in}}}|\Big)(C_{\mathrm{out,in}}\otimes I_{\overline{\mathrm{out}},\overline{\mathrm{in}}})~\Big(|I_{\mathrm{out},\overline{\mathrm{out}}}\rangle\!\rangle\otimes\mathtt{SWAP}_{\mathrm{in},\overline{\mathrm{in}}}\Big)\,,

(67)

where “
\rm in
” and “
\rm out
” label the input and output systems of channel
\mathscr{C}
, respectively,
\overline{\rm in}
and
\overline{\rm out}
are auxiliary systems of the same dimensions of
\rm in
and
\rm out
, respectively, and
{\tt SWAP}_{{\rm in},\overline{\rm in}}
is the swap operator, defined by the relation
{\tt SWAP}_{{\rm in},\overline{\rm in}}|\varphi\rangle|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle|\varphi\rangle
for every pair of vectors
|\varphi\rangle
and
|\psi\rangle
. Eq. (
67
) can be verified explicitly using the expressions
\check{C}=\sum_{i}C_{i}\otimes\overline{C}_{i}
and
C=\sum_{i}|C_{i}\rangle\!\rangle\langle\!\langle C_{i}|
.

Appendix F
Expression of the noncommutativity of two channels in terms of their unitary realizations

Here we provide an alternative expression of the noncommutativity (and therefore of the MED) in terms of the unitary realizations of the two channels
\mathscr{C}
and
\mathscr{D}
. Consider two unitary realizations, of the form

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{E}\left[U_{SE}\,(\rho_{S}\otimes|\eta\rangle\langle\eta|_{E})\,U_{SE}^{\dagger}\right]

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{F}\left[V_{SF}\,(\rho_{S}\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|_{F})\,V_{SF}^{\dagger}\right]\,,

(68)

where
E
and
F
are two suitable quantum systems, serving as the environments,
|\eta\rangle
and
|\phi\rangle
are pure states of
E
and
F
, respectively, and
U_{SE}
and
V_{SF}
are two unitary evolutions between the target system (denoted by
S
) and the environments
E
and
F
, respectively.

To compute the action of the channel
\mathscr{S}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})
, we consider the quantum switch of the unitary gates
U_{SE}\otimes I_{F}
and
V_{SF}\otimes I_{E}
, and then take the partial trace over the environments. The quantum switch of the unitary gates
U_{SE}\otimes I_{F}
and
V_{SF}\otimes I_{E}
yields the controlled unitary gate

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=(U_{SE}\otimes I_{F})(V_{SF}\otimes I_{E})\otimes|0\rangle\langle 0|_{C}

\displaystyle\quad+(V_{SF}\otimes I_{E})(U_{SE}\otimes I_{F})\otimes|1\rangle\langle 1|_{C}\,,

(69)

where the subscript
C
denotes the control system, and it is implicitly understood that the Hilbert spaces in the tensor product are suitably arranged according to the subscripts of the corresponding systems.

Hence, the action of the channel
\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}}
on the state
\rho\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|
is given by

\displaystyle\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}}\,\big(\rho_{S}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|_{C}\big)

\displaystyle=\operatorname{Tr}_{EF}[W\,(\rho_{S}\otimes|\eta\rangle\langle\eta|_{E}

\displaystyle\quad\otimes|\phi\rangle\langle\phi|_{F}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|_{C})\,W^{\dagger}]\,.

(70)

To reduce the above expression to the case where the input state is pure, we take a purification of
\rho
, given by a pure state
|\Psi\rangle\in\mathcal{H}_{S}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{R}
, where
R
is a suitable purifying system.
Let us define the state

\displaystyle|\Gamma\rangle_{SREFC}

\displaystyle:=(W\otimes I_{R})\,|\Psi\rangle_{SR}\otimes|\eta\rangle_{E}\otimes|\phi\rangle_{F}\otimes|+\rangle_{C}

\displaystyle=\frac{|\Lambda_{0}\rangle_{SREF}\otimes|0\rangle_{C}+|\Lambda_{1}\rangle_{SREF}\otimes|1\rangle_{C}}{\sqrt{2}}\,,

(71)

with

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{0}\rangle_{SREF}:=

\displaystyle\Big[(U_{SE}\otimes I_{F})(V_{SF}\otimes I_{E})\otimes I_{R}\Big]\,|\Psi\rangle_{SR}\otimes|\eta\rangle_{E}\otimes|\phi\rangle_{F}\,,

(72)

\displaystyle|\Lambda_{1}\rangle_{SREF}:=

\displaystyle\Big[(V_{SF}\otimes I_{E})(U_{SE}\otimes I_{F})\otimes I_{R}\Big]\,|\Psi\rangle_{SR}\otimes|\eta\rangle_{E}\otimes|\phi\rangle_{F}\,.

(73)

The probability of the outcome - when the output state is measured on the Fourier basis is then given by

\displaystyle p_{-}

\displaystyle=\langle-|\,\operatorname{Tr}_{S}[\mathscr{S}_{\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D}}\,\big(\rho_{S}\otimes|+\rangle\langle+|_{C}\big)]\,|-\rangle

\displaystyle=\langle-|\,\operatorname{Tr}_{SREF}[|\Gamma\rangle\langle\Gamma]\,|-\rangle

\displaystyle=\left\|(I_{SREF}\otimes\langle-|_{C})\,|\Gamma\rangle_{SREFC}\,\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\left\||\Lambda_{0}\rangle-|\Lambda_{1}\rangle\,\right\|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1-{\sf Re}\langle\Lambda_{0}|\Lambda_{1}\rangle}{2}\,.

(74)

Using the relation,
{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})=\sqrt{2p_{-}}
, we finally obtain

\displaystyle{\rm NCOM}_{\rho}(\mathscr{C},\mathscr{D})=\sqrt{1-{\sf Re}\langle\Lambda_{0}|\Lambda_{1}\rangle}\,.

(75)

The evaluation of the noncommutativity is reduced to the evaluation of the overlap between the states
|\Lambda_{0}\rangle
and
|\Lambda_{1}\rangle
. When the system
S
consists of a large number
N
of particles, its Hilbert space has exponentially large dimension in
N
, and therefore the evaluation of the overlap may be computationally demanding. However, there exist many relevant situations in which the overlap between two states of exponentially large dimension can nevertheless be computed efficiently,
i.e.
in a polynomial number of steps. This is the case, for example, if the states
|\Lambda_{0}\rangle
and
|\Lambda_{1}\rangle
are matrix product states
Fannes et al. 1992
;
Verstraete and Cirac 2006
;
Perez-Garcia et al. 2007
or MERA states. Physically, these cases correspond to the situation in which the pure state
|\Psi\rangle
and the unitary evolutions
U_{SE}
and
V_{SF}
have an appropriate tensor network structure, generated by sequences of local interactions.
