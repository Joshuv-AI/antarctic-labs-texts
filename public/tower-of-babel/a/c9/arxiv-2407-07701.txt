Completing the enumeration of inversion sequences avoiding one or two patterns of length 3
Benjamin Testart

Abstract
We present four constructions of inversion sequences, and use them to compute the enumeration sequences of 24 classes of pattern-avoiding inversion sequences. This completes the enumeration of inversion sequences avoiding one or two patterns of length 3. Some of our constructions are based on generating trees. Others involve pattern-avoiding words, which we also count using generating trees. To solve some of these cases, we introduce a generalization of inversion sequences, which we call shifted inversion sequences. Lastly, we briefly discuss the asymptotics of pattern-avoiding inversion sequences, focusing on their exponential or super-exponential behavior.

Method

A

A combinatorial generating tree can be defined using a map, called

\ell

Theorem 2
.

The enumeration of inversion sequences avoiding the patterns 000 and 100 is given by the succession rule

\Omega_{\{000,100\}}=\begin{cases}(1,0)\\

For all

and
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(000,100)

•

\sigma

\sigma

Let

We begin by establishing some general properties of integer sequences avoiding 102 and 201. For any

Proposition 3

Let
\sigma

\forall i\in[\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma),\mathbf{lastmax}(\sigma)],\,\sigma_{i}\in\{m,s\}

First, we show that each condition is necessary:

1.

\sigma_{j}<m

\sigma_{i}<\sigma_{j}

\sigma_{i}<m

3.

\sigma_{i}<s
, let

be an integer such that
\sigma_{j}=s
. If

, then

is an occurrence of 102. If

(m,\sigma_{i},\sigma_{j})

3
Removing the maximum

3.1
Method

Generally speaking, removing a term from an inversion sequence does not always create another inversion sequence. In fact, removing the
i
-th term
\sigma_{i}
from an inversion sequence
\sigma\in\mathbb{\mathcal{I}}_{n}
for some
i\in[1,n]
yields an inversion sequence if and only if
\sigma_{j}<j-1
for all
j\in[i+1,n]
. In particular if
\sigma_{i}=\mathbf{max}(\sigma)
, then for all
j\in[i+1,n]
we have
\sigma_{j}\leqslant\sigma_{i}<i\leqslant j-1
, and the above condition is satisfied. Hence, removing a maximum from an inversion sequence always yields an inversion sequence.

Following this idea, we can decompose inversion sequences by removing their maxima one by one. For this decomposition to be unambiguous, we must choose the order of removal of the different occurrences of the maximum (this order is not always trivial). Observe that this decomposition defines a combinatorial generating tree
\mathcal{T}
for inversion sequences. In that tree, the parent of each nonempty inversion sequence is obtained by removing its “next” maximum in the chosen order.

For any set of patterns
P
, a combinatorial generating tree for
P
-avoiding inversion sequences can be obtained by removing all nodes of
\mathcal{T}
whose label contains a pattern in
P
. Indeed, this procedure does not disconnect
\mathcal{T}
since the parent (in
\mathcal{T}
) of any nonempty
P
-avoiding inversion sequence also avoids
P
.

Let us quickly go over each pattern that appears in this section, and establish necessary and sufficient conditions for an integer sequence to avoid any occurrence of the given pattern which involves the maximum of the sequence.

•

000: The maximum does not appear more than twice.

•

100: All values appearing after the first maximum are distinct, or equal to the maximum.

•

101: All occurrences of the maximum are consecutive.

•

110: All maxima after the first one appear in a single factor, at the end of the sequence.

•

102: The subsequence to the left of the last maximum is nondecreasing, ignoring other occurrences of the maximum.

•

201: The subsequence to the right of the first maximum is nonincreasing, ignoring other occurrences of the maximum.

•

210: The subsequence to the right of the first maximum is nondecreasing, ignoring other occurrences of the maximum.

Since our generating tree construction inserts a maximum, the children of a node labelled
\sigma
in
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
are the children of
\sigma
in
\mathcal{T}
which satisfy the above conditions (for the patterns in
P
).
Such facts will be used in proofs throughout this section without explicit reference.

The pair {000, 102}

, introduced in

or
n+2
, denoted by
\overset{1}{\leadsto}
and
\overset{2}{\leadsto}
in the succession rule. We find a doubled succession rule describing a generating tree for

Remark 15
.

Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}(000,102)\backslash\{\varepsilon\}
be a nonempty inversion sequence. Since

avoids the pattern 102, the factor

.

is positive.

Given

and

the sequence obtained from

by inserting the value

at position

[\sigma]_{p}^{v}=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,p-1]}\cdot v\cdot(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[p,n]}

Lemma 16
.

Let

,
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(000,102)
,

,

, and

Proof.

Since
\sigma
does not contain the value

is nondecreasing, hence inserting

∎

It follows from Lemma

can be interpreted either as the number of values (denoted

Theorem 17
.

The enumeration of inversion sequences avoiding the patterns 000 and 102 is given by the doubled succession rule

\Omega_{\{000,102\}}=\begin{cases}(1)\\

Proof.

We consider the combinatorial generating tree (with jumps) for

In the rest of this proof, let

Let

The pair {102, 210}

In this section, we present a generating tree for the class

For any

Proposition 18
.

Let
\sigma
be a
\{102,210\}
-avoiding integer sequence. Then any descent top of
\sigma
is the position of a maximum of

Proof.

Assume, for the sake of contradiction, that

and
\sigma_{i}<\mathbf{max}(\sigma)
. Since
\mathbf{Des}(\sigma)
is nonempty,
|\sigma|\geqslant 2
. Let

be such that
\sigma_{j}=\mathbf{max}(\sigma)
. In particular,
j\notin\{i,i+1\}
. If

, then
(\sigma_{j},\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1})
is an occurrence of 210. If
j>i+1
, then
(\sigma_{i},\sigma_{i+1},\sigma_{j})
is an occurrence of 102.
∎

Proposition 19
.

Let
\sigma
be a
\{102,210\}
-avoiding integer sequence, and let

.

•

If
\sigma
is nondecreasing, then inserting the value

anywhere in

does not create any occurrence of 102 or 210.

•

If
\sigma
has exactly one descent

, then inserting the value

at some position

yields a

.

•

\sigma

We go case-by-case.

•

Since

This order of insertion may not be intuitive, but the resulting generating tree construction has some advantages compared to a simpler order:

•

most importantly, it only requires one parameter (the value of the
\mathbf{bounce}
statistic),

•

it does not involve “phantom objects” as in Section
2.3
,

•

the resulting succession rule does not involve the size
n
of the sequences.

We now turn to a step-by-step description of the generating tree, which matches the succession rule
\Omega_{\{102,210\}}
. Let
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}(102,210)
,
n=|\sigma|
,
k=\mathbf{bounce}(\sigma)
, and
{m=\mathbf{max}(\sigma)}
.

•

Suppose
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n,k}
. In particular, if
\sigma
is nonempty then
\mathbf{max}(\sigma)=\sigma_{n}=n-k
.

–

Inserting any value
v\in[n-k,n]
at the end of
\sigma
yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{A}_{n+1,n+1-v}
, where
n+1-v\in[1,k+1]
. This production alone generates all nondecreasing inversion sequences.

–

If
\sigma
is nonempty, inserting any value
v\in[n-k+1,n-1]
at any position
{p\in[v+1,n]}
creates a sequence in
\mathfrak{B}^{(1)}_{n+1,p-v}
. For each
i=p-v\in[1,k-1]
, there are
k-i
sequences in
\mathfrak{B}^{(1)}_{n+1,i}
generated by
\sigma
(one sequence for each value
v\in[n-k+1,n-i]
, for
p=v+i
).

•

Suppose
\sigma\in\mathfrak{B}^{(1)}_{n,k}
. In particular,
\mathbf{Des}(\sigma)=\{k+m\}
.

–

Inserting the value
m
at the end of
\sigma
yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{B}^{(1)}_{n+1,k}
.

–

Inserting any value
v\in[m,m+k]
between the top and bottom of the only descent of
\sigma
(i.e. at position
k+m+1
) yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{B}^{(2)}_{n+1,k-v+m+1}
.

–

Inserting the value
m
at any position
p\in[m+1,k+m-1]
yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{C}^{(1)}_{n+1,p-m}
.

•

Suppose
\sigma\in\mathfrak{B}^{(2)}_{n,k}
. In particular,
\mathbf{Des}(\sigma)=\{k+m\}
. As before, inserting any value
v\in[m,m+k]
between the top and bottom of the only descent of
\sigma
(i.e. at position
k+m+1
) yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{B}^{(2)}_{n+1,k-v+m+1}
.

•

Suppose
\sigma\in\mathfrak{C}^{(1)}_{n,k}
. In particular,
\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)=k+m
.

–

Inserting the value
m
right-adjacent to the rightmost descent top of
\sigma
(i.e. at position
\mathbf{max}(\mathbf{Des}(\sigma))+1)
yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{C}^{(1)}_{n+1,k}
.

–

Inserting the value
m
at any position
p\in[m+1,\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)]
yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{C}^{(2)}_{n+1,p-m}
.

•

Suppose
\sigma\in\mathfrak{C}^{(2)}_{n,k}
. In particular,
\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)=k+m
. As before, inserting the value
m
at any position
p\in[m+1,\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)]
yields a sequence in
\mathfrak{C}^{(2)}_{n+1,p-m}
.

Notice that inserting some value
v\geqslant m
at any other position would either yield a sequence which already appears in this construction, or create an occurrence of a pattern 102 or 210.
∎

3.4
The pairs {000, 201} or {000, 210}

A bijection between
\mathcal{I}(000,201)
and
\mathcal{I}(000,210)
was established in
[
YL20
, Theorem 8.1]
. In this section we enumerate
\mathcal{I}(000,201)
, although the same construction can also be applied to
\mathcal{I}(000,210)
with a little more work, and results in the same equations.
Let
\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(000,201)\;:\;m=\mathbf{max}(\sigma),\;p=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)=\mathbf{lastmax}(\sigma)\}
be the set of
\{000,201\}
-avoiding inversion sequences of size
n
, maximum
m
, and such that
m
appears only once and at position
p
. Let
\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=|\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}|
. By definition,
\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=0
if
n<p
or
p\leqslant m
. When
m=0
,
\mathfrak{a}_{n,0,p}=\delta_{n,1}\delta_{p,1}
.
The numbers
(\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p})_{n,m,p\in\mathbb{N}}
can then be calculated using the following lemma.

Lemma 21
.

For all
1\leqslant m<p\leqslant n
,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sum_{q=s+1}^{p}\mathfrak{a}_{n-1,s,q}+(p-q+\delta_{p,q}-\delta_{p,n})\mathfrak{a}_{n-2,s,q}

Proof.

Let
m\geqslant 1,p>m,n\geqslant p
be three integers, and let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}
. Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be the sequence obtained by removing the value
m
from
\sigma
. Let
s=\mathbf{max}(\sigma^{\prime})
be the second largest value in
\sigma
, and
q=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma^{\prime})
. Note that if
q<p
then
\sigma_{q}=s
, and if
q\geqslant p
then
\sigma_{q+1}=s
. In particular, if
q>p
then the subsequence
(\sigma_{p},\sigma_{p+1},\sigma_{q+1})
is an occurrence of the pattern 201. Hence
q
is less than or equal to
p
.

Since
\sigma^{\prime}
avoids the pattern 000, its maximum
s
appears either once or twice.

•

If
s
appears only once in
\sigma^{\prime}
, then
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,q}
. Note that inserting the value
m
at position
p
in any sequence in
\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,q}
for
s<m
and
q\leqslant p
does not create an occurrence of 000 or 201, so this is a bijection.

•

If
s
appears twice, then removing the second occurrence yields an inversion sequence
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-2,s,q}
. The possible positions for the second
s
in
\sigma^{\prime}
are:

–

Before
p
:

\begin{cases}[q+1,p-1]&\text{if}\quad q<p\\
\emptyset&\text{if}\quad q=p\end{cases}
, so there are
p-q-1+\delta_{p,q}
choices.

–

After
p
:

\begin{cases}\{p+1\}&\text{if}\quad p<n\\
\emptyset&\text{if}\quad p=n\end{cases}
, so there are
1-\delta_{p,n}
choices.

This adds up to
p-q+\delta_{p,q}-\delta_{p,n}
possible positions for the second
s
.

Summing over all possible values of
m
and
p
completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 22
.

For all
n\geqslant 2
,

|\mathcal{I}_{n}(000,201)|=\sum_{m=0}^{n-1}\sum_{p=m+1}^{n}\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}+(n-p)\mathfrak{a}_{n-1,m,p}.

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(000,201)
,
m=\mathbf{max}(\sigma)
, and
p=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)
. Since
\sigma
avoids 000, the maximum
m
appears either once or twice in
\sigma
.

•

If
m
appears only once, then
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}
.

•

If
m
appears twice, then removing the second
m
yields a sequence
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,m,p}
. There are
n-p
possible positions for the second
m
, hence
n-p
different sequences
\sigma
correspond to the same
\sigma^{\prime}
.

Summing over all possible values of
m
and
p
completes the proof.
∎

Remark 23
.

The pairs of patterns studied in the next four sections contain either 101 or 110. In a sequence that avoids 101 (resp. 110), all occurrences of the maximum after the leftmost one must appear in a single factor at the end of the sequence (resp. consecutive to the leftmost maximum).
This allows us to restrict the enumeration to inversion sequences which contain only one occurrence of the maximum without loss of generality, since each repetition of that maximum can only be placed at a single position.

3.5
The pair {100, 110}

Let
\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}=\{\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(100,110)\;:\;m=\mathbf{max}(\sigma),\;p=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)=\mathbf{lastmax}(\sigma)\}
be the set of
\{100,110\}
-avoiding inversion sequences of size
n
, maximum
m
, and such that
m
appears only once and at position
p
. Let
\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=|\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}|
. In particular,
\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=0
if
n<p
or
p\leqslant m
, and
\mathfrak{a}_{n,0,p}=\delta_{n,1}\delta_{p,1}
.

Lemma 24
.

Let
1\leqslant m<p\leqslant n
. If
p\in\{n-1,n\}
,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sum_{q=s+1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=q}^{n-1}\mathfrak{a}_{\ell,s,q}.

If
p<n-1
,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}(n-p)\cdot\mathfrak{a}_{n-1,s,p}+\sum_{q=s+1}^{p-1}\mathfrak{a}_{n-1,s,q}+\mathfrak{a}_{n-2,s,q}.

Proof.

Let
1\leqslant m<p\leqslant n
, and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,p}
. Let
\sigma^{\prime}=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,n]\backslash\{p\}}
be the sequence obtained by removing the value
m
from
\sigma
. Let
s=\mathbf{max}(\sigma^{\prime})
and
q=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma^{\prime})
. Let
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
be the sequence obtained by removing all terms of value
s
except the first one from
\sigma^{\prime}
, so that
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{\ell,s,q}
for some
\ell\in[q,n-1]
.

Since
\sigma
avoids 100, all terms of
\sigma
after position
p
must have distinct values. In particular,
s
appears at most once after
p
. Since
\sigma^{\prime}
avoids 110, every
s
after the first one must appear in a single factor at the end of
\sigma^{\prime}
. At this point, there are two cases:

1.

If
p\in\{n-1,n\}
, then the value
s
could appear any number of times without creating an occurrence of 100 in
\sigma
. In that case,
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
can be any sequence in
\mathfrak{A}_{\ell,s,q}
for any
\ell\in[q,n-1]
: starting from any sequence in
\mathfrak{A}_{\ell,s,q}
for any
s<m
,
q>s
, and
\ell<n
, we can append
n-\ell-1
terms of value
s
, then insert
m
at position
p\in\{n-1,n\}
without creating an occurrence of 100 or 110, and this is a bijection. Thus, we obtain the first equation of the lemma.

2.

If
p<n-1
:

•

If
q<p
:

–

If
\sigma
contains a single term of value
s
, then
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,q}
.

–

If
\sigma
contains two terms of value
s
, then the second one is
\sigma_{n}
due to the avoidance of 110. In that case,
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-2,s,q}
.

In either case, this is a bijection for the same reason as before. Note that
s
cannot appear more than twice in
\sigma
, otherwise
(\sigma_{p},\sigma_{n-1},\sigma_{n})=(m,s,s)
would be an occurrence of 100.

•

If
q\geqslant p
, then
s
appears only once in
\sigma
in order to avoid 100.
In that case,
\sigma^{\prime\prime}=\sigma^{\prime}
can be any sequence in
\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,q}
such that all values
(\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{i})_{i\in[p,n-1]}
are distinct. Moving the value
s
in
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
from position
q
to position
p
yields a sequence
\tau\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,p}
. Conversely, starting from any sequence
\tau\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,p}
and moving the value
s
from position
p
to position
q\in[p,n-1]
yields a sequence
\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,q}
such that all values
(\sigma^{\prime\prime}_{i})_{i\in[p,n-1]}
are distinct. Since there are
n-p
possible values of
q
, each sequence
\tau\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-1,s,p}
is obtained from
n-p
different sequences
\sigma^{\prime\prime}
. ∎

Theorem 25
.

For all
n\geqslant 2
,

|\mathcal{I}_{n}(100,110)|=\sum_{\ell=1}^{n}\sum_{m=0}^{\ell-1}\sum_{p=m+1}^{\ell}\mathfrak{a}_{\ell,m,p}.

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant 2
,
\sigma\in\mathcal{I}_{n}(100,110)
,
m=\mathbf{max}(\sigma)
, and
p=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)
. Since
\sigma
avoids
110
, all terms of value
m
after the first one
\sigma_{p}
are in a factor
(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[\ell+1,n]}
for some
\ell\in[p,n]
. Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be the sequence obtained by removing all terms of value
m
from
\sigma
, except the first one
\sigma_{p}
. By definition,
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{\ell,m,p}
. Since we can rebuild
\sigma
by appending
n-\ell
terms of value
m
to
\sigma^{\prime}
, this is a bijection between
\mathcal{I}_{n}(100,110)
and
\coprod_{0\leqslant m<p\leqslant\ell\leqslant n}\mathfrak{A}_{\ell,m,p}
.
∎

Lemma 26
.

Let
1\leqslant m<p\leqslant n
. If
p\in\{n-1,n\}
,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,p}=\sum_{s=0}^{m-1}\sum_{q=s+1}^{n-1}\sum_{\ell=q}^{n-1}\mathfrak{a}_{\ell,s,q}.

This is quite similar to the proof of Lemma

, except that now the terms of value

in
\sigma^{\prime}

3.9
A generating tree perspective on the previous constructions

The inversion sequences studied in Sections
3.4
to
3.8
could also be generated by inserting their values one by one, in increasing order (and inserting any repetitions of a value from left to right). This yields generating trees which differ only slightly from our construction. We chose not to present them that way, since it would require distinguishing several subsets of inversion sequences, as we did in Sections
2.3
and
3.3
.
In all five cases, the resulting succession rules would have two integer parameters, which always record the number of positions where a new maximum may be inserted, and the number of possible values of a new maximum. The first parameter is influenced by pattern-avoidance, and the second one is used to maintain the “subdiagonal” property characterizing inversion sequences.

Note that Sections
3.2
and
3.3
use such generating tree constructions, but only have one integer parameter.

•

For the pair of patterns
\{000,102\}
, the two parameters are one and the same, and correspond to the statistic we denoted
\mathbf{sites}
, as explained in the paragraph just before Theorem
17
.

•

For the pair
\{102,210\}
:

–

a new maximum cannot be inserted in a sequence with several descents; it follows that such sequences only require one parameter to record the number of positions where repetitions of the current maximum could be inserted.

–

in a sequence which has 0 or 1 descents, the positions where a new maximum may be inserted are trivial (for 0 descents any position is fine, and for 1 descent the only available position is between the top and the bottom of the descent), so only the number of possible values of a new maximum is required.

Lemma 39

For all

\mathfrak{c}_{n,k}=2\mathfrak{c}_{n,k-1}+\mathfrak{c}_{n-1,k}-\mathfrak{c}_{n-1,k-1}-\frac{\binom{k-2}{n}\binom{2n}{n}}{n+1}.

be the position of the first letter

in
\omega

–

\omega_{1}=k
and
(\omega_{i})_{i\in[2,n]}
can be any word of
\mathfrak{B}_{n-1,k}

–

If

, let

and
\gamma=(\omega_{i})_{i\in[p+1,n]}
. In particular,
\alpha
is a nonincreasing word of length

over the alphabet
[0,k-1]
. By Remark

, there are
\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}
possible words

. Note that any letter

such that
v\neq k
must satisfy both
v\leqslant\mathbf{min}(\alpha)
(by the nonincreasing property) and
v\geqslant\mathbf{max}(\alpha)
(to avoid 120). We distinguish two cases:

*

If
\alpha
is constant, then
\alpha=(v)^{p-1}
for some
v\in[0,k-1]
, and
\gamma\in\{v,k\}^{n-p}
. In that case, there are
k
possible choices for
\alpha
, and for each
\alpha
there are
2^{n-p}
possible choices for
\gamma
.

*

Otherwise, there are
\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}-k
remaining choices for
\alpha

\alpha
is not constant,
\mathbf{min}(\alpha)<\mathbf{max}(\alpha)
, hence no letter in
[0,k-1]
may appear in
\gamma
, so

.

We obtain the following equation for all

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\binom{k+n-1}{n}+\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}+\sum_{p=2}^{n}\left(k\cdot 2^{n-p}+\binom{k+p-2}{p-1}-k\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{k+n-1}{n}+\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}+k(2^{n-1}-1)+\binom{k+n-1}{n-1}-1-k(n-1)

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}+\binom{k+n}{n}+k(2^{n-1}-n)-1.

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}_{0,k}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\mathfrak{b}_{i,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{i-1,k}\right)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\binom{k+i}{i}+k(2^{i-1}-i)-1\right)

\displaystyle=\binom{k+n+1}{n}+k\left(2^{n}-1-\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)-n.\qed

Lemma 47
.

For all
1\leqslant k\leqslant n
,

\mathfrak{c}_{n,k}=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\binom{n+k}{k-1}.

Proof.

For all
0\leqslant k\leqslant n
and
\omega\in\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}
, let
\mathbf{dec}(\omega)
be the size of the longest strictly decreasing factor at the beginning of
\omega
. In particular, if
n>0
then
\omega_{\mathbf{dec}(\omega)}
is the first letter 0 in
\omega
(otherwise the first 0 would appear after a weak increase, creating an occurrence of 110 or 120). Also note that
\mathbf{dec}(\omega)\leqslant k
since all letters of a strictly decreasing factor are distinct. Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n,k,s}=\{\omega\in\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}\;:\;s=\mathbf{dec}(\omega)\}
, and
\mathfrak{c}_{n,k,s}=|\mathfrak{C}_{n,k,s}|
.

We consider the combinatorial generating tree for
\coprod_{n\geqslant k\geqslant 0}\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}
obtained by inserting letters in decreasing order of value, and inserting repeats of a letter from right to left. This amounts to inserting the letter 0 before the first 0 in a word
\omega\in\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}
, or inserting the letter 0 in
\omega+1
.
This indeed defines a generating tree since any nonempty word
\omega
has a unique parent of size
|\omega|-1
, obtained by removing the first 0 from
\omega
, and subtracting 1 from every letter if the resulting word does not contain the letter 0.

For any word
\omega\in\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}
, the positions where the letter 0 may be inserted before the first 0 in
\omega
are
[1,\mathbf{dec}(\omega)]
, and this clearly cannot create an occurrence of 110 or 120.
The positions where the letter 0 may be inserted in
\omega+1
without creating an occurrence of 110 or 120 are
[1,\mathbf{dec}(\omega)+1]
.
This implies that each word of
\mathfrak{C}_{n,k,s}
has one child in
\mathfrak{C}_{n+1,k,i}
for all

, and one child in
\mathfrak{C}_{n+1,k+1,i}
for all
i\in[1,s+1]
. We obtain the following equation for all
2\leqslant i\leqslant k\leqslant n
:

\mathfrak{c}_{n,k,i}=\sum_{s=i}^{n-1}\mathfrak{c}_{n-1,k,s}+\sum_{s=i-1}^{n-1}\mathfrak{c}_{n-1,k-1,s}.

In particular,

, so a simpler recurrence relation holds: for all

\mathfrak{c}_{n,k,s}=\mathfrak{c}_{n,k,s+1}+\mathfrak{c}_{n-1,k,s}+\mathfrak{c}_{n-1,k-1,s-1}.

From this equation, we can easily prove by induction that for all

\mathfrak{c}_{n,k,s}=\frac{s(n+k-s-1)!}{(n-k)!k!(k-s)!}.

Finally, the expression of

is obtained by summing over

. For all

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{k}\mathfrak{c}_{n,k,s}

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=1}^{k}\frac{s(n+k-s-1)!}{(n-k)!k!(k-s)!}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\sum_{s=1}^{k}s\binom{n+k-s-1}{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\sum_{s=i}^{k}\binom{n+k-s-1}{n-1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\binom{n+k-i}{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{k}\binom{n-1}{k-1}\binom{n+k}{k-1}.\qed

Lemma 55
.

The following recurrence relation holds for all
k\geqslant 1,n\in[k+1,2k]
:

\mathfrak{f}_{n,k}=\mathfrak{f}_{n,k+1}-\mathfrak{f}_{n-1,k},

with initial conditions
\mathfrak{f}_{n,n}=\frac{1}{n+1}\binom{2n}{n}
for all
n\geqslant 0
.

Proof.

Let
k\geqslant 2,n\in[k,2k-2]
, and
\omega\in\mathfrak{F}_{n,k}
. In particular,
\omega
contains a single letter
k-1
and a single letter
k-2
.

•

If the letter
k-2
is on the left of
k-1
, then
k-1
is the last letter of
\omega
(because
\omega
avoids the pattern 120). In that case, removing the letter
k-1
yields a word in
\mathfrak{F}_{n-1,k-1}
, and this is a bijection.

•

Otherwise, the letter
k-2
is on the right of
k-1
. In that case, replacing the letter
k-1
by

yields a word in
\mathcal{W}_{(2)^{n-k}\cdot(1)^{2k-n-2}\cdot(2)}(120)
, and this is a bijection. By Theorem

, this set is equinumerous with
\mathcal{W}_{(2)^{n-k+1}\cdot(1)^{2k-n-2}}(120)=\mathfrak{F}_{n,k-1}
.

This shows that for all

and

,
\mathfrak{f}_{n,k}=\mathfrak{f}_{n,k-1}+\mathfrak{f}_{n-1,k-1}
, which is equivalent to the recurrence relation of the lemma.

For all

,
\mathfrak{F}_{n,n}
is trivially in bijection with the set of 120-avoiding permutations of size

.
∎

4.10
The pattern 010

We denote by
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}
the unsigned Stirling numbers of the first kind, which count the number of permutations of size
n
with

cycles (among other combinatorial interpretations, cf. entry A132393 of the OEIS).

Let

be the set of
010
-avoiding inversion sequences of size
n
, maximum
m
, and having

distinct values. Let

-avoiding words of length

\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}=|\mathfrak{B}_{n,k}|

Theorem 56
.

For all
2\leqslant d\leqslant m+1\leqslant n
,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,d}=\sum_{i=0}^{d-1}\binom{m-i}{d-i-1}\sum_{p=m+1}^{n}\mathfrak{b}_{n-p+1,d-i}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathfrak{a}_{p-1,j,i}.

Let

,
p=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)
,
\alpha=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,p-1]}
, and
\beta=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[p,n]}
.

Since
\sigma
avoids the pattern 010,
\alpha
and
\beta
avoid 010, and
\mathbf{Vals}(\alpha)\cap\mathbf{Vals}(\beta)=\emptyset
by Proposition
35
. In particular,
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}_{p-1,j,i}
for some

and

, and
\beta
is a 010-avoiding word of length

, which contains exactly
d-i
distinct values chosen from the remaining

(that is, all values in
[0,m]
except for the

\alpha
), and such that
\beta_{1}=m
. Since

is always in
\beta
, there are
\binom{m-i}{d-i-1}

\mathbf{Vals}(\beta)
.
Once the values of
\beta

\mathfrak{b}_{n-p+1,d-i}

∎

Lemma 57
.

For all
n,k\geqslant 1
,

\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}=\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n+1-k}.

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant k\geqslant 2
, and
\omega\in\mathfrak{B}_{n,k}
. Since
\omega
avoids the pattern
010
, all letters 0 in
\omega
are consecutive.

•

If
\omega
contains several letters 0, then removing one of them yields a word
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{n-1,k}
, and this is clearly a bijection.

•

If
\omega
contains a single letter 0, then removing it and subtracting 1 from all other letters yields a word
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{B}_{n-1,k-1}
. Since
\omega_{1}=k-1>0
, there are
n-1
possible positions for the letter 0 in
\omega
, so exactly
n-1
words
\omega\in\mathfrak{B}_{n,k}
yield the same

.

Hence the following recurrence relation holds for all

\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}=\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}+(n-1)\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k-1}.

This recurrence relation is also satisfied by the Stirling numbers of the first kind
\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{n+1-k}
, and we can easily verify that initial conditions also match.
∎

4.12
Forbidden values

In the remainder of Section
4
, if
\alpha
is a sequence avoiding a set of patterns
P
, we say that a value
v\in\{0,\dots,\mathbf{max}(\alpha)\}
is
forbidden
by
\alpha
and
P
if
\alpha\cdot mv
contains a pattern in
P
when
m>\mathbf{max}(\alpha)
. We denote by
\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha,P)
the set of values forbidden by
\alpha
and
P
, or simply
\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha)
when there is no ambiguity.

In our decomposition around the first maximum of an inversion sequence
\sigma=\alpha\cdot m\cdot\gamma
, if
\gamma
contains a value in
\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha,\tau)
, then
\sigma
contains
\tau
. This means that in order for
\sigma
to avoid
\tau
,
\gamma
must be a word over the alphabet
[0,m]\backslash\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha)
. This is in fact a weaker version of condition 3 from Remark
33
, since it only ensures that
\sigma
does not contain any occurrence of
\tau
whose last entry only is in
\gamma
.

Note that Propositions
34
,
35
can be expressed in terms of forbidden values:

\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha,120)=[0,\mathbf{max}(\alpha)-1],\qquad\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha,010)=\mathbf{Vals}(\alpha),

and these identities hold for any integer sequence
\alpha
(not only for inversion sequences). In particular, if
\alpha
is an inversion sequence such that
\mathbf{max}(\alpha)>0
, then
|\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha,010)|\geqslant 2
.

Earlier, we refined the enumeration of pattern-avoiding inversion sequences
\sigma
according to one or two parameters (in addition to their size). The first one was the maximum
m
of
\sigma
, and is required for our decomposition around the first maximum (so that the maximum of the left part
\alpha
is less than
m
). For every pair of pattern which contained 120, the number of forbidden values was redundant with the maximum of
\sigma
, and therefore unnecessary.
In the previous two cases (the patterns 010 and {000, 010}), the second parameter counting the number of distinct values
d
of
\sigma
was in fact the number of forbidden values
|\mathbf{Forb}(\sigma,010)|
.

For each pair of patterns
P
which follows, there is no simple equivalent description of
|\mathbf{Forb}(\sigma,P)|
, and introducing this parameter allows us to solve the enumeration of
P
-avoiding inversion sequences.

4.13

A bijection between

Remark 60

Let
\sigma

Let

be the set of
\{010,210\}
-avoiding inversion sequences of size

, maximum

, and having

forbidden values. Let
\mathfrak{B}_{n,k}=\{\omega\in\mathcal{W}_{n,k+1}(010)\;:\;\omega_{i}<\omega_{j}<k\implies i<j\;\text{ and }\;k-1\in\mathbf{Vals}(\omega)\}

, or

if

, be the set of

-avoiding words
\omega
of length

over the alphabet
[0,k]
such that the subword
\omega^{\prime}
obtained by removing all letters

from
\omega
is nondecreasing, and

is the maximum of
\omega^{\prime}
.
Let
\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,f}=|\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,f}|

\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}=|\mathfrak{B}_{n,k}|

Theorem 61
.

For all
2\leqslant f\leqslant m+1\leqslant n
,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,m,f}=\sum_{p=m+1}^{n}\sum_{i=0}^{f-1}\mathfrak{b}_{n-p,f-i-1}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\mathfrak{a}_{p-1,j,i}.

Proof.

Let
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n,m,f}
,
p=\mathbf{firstmax}(\sigma)
,
\alpha=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[1,p-1]}
, and
\gamma=(\sigma_{i})_{i\in[p+1,n]}
. Let
\gamma^{\prime}
be the subsequence of
\gamma
obtained by removing all values
m
from
\gamma
.
Since
\sigma
avoids the pattern 010,
\alpha
and
\gamma
avoid 010, and
\mathbf{Vals}(\alpha)\cap\mathbf{Vals}(\gamma)=\emptyset
by Proposition
35
. Since
\sigma
avoids the pattern 210,
\alpha
avoids 210, and
\gamma^{\prime}
is nondecreasing.

The subsequence
\alpha
is in
\mathfrak{A}_{p-1,j,i}
for some
j<m
and

. Notice that we actually have

since
m
is a forbidden value for
\sigma
(because of the avoidance of 010), but not for
\alpha
(since
m>\mathbf{max}(\alpha))
.
The subsequence
\gamma
is a 010-avoiding word of length

over the alphabet
\Sigma=[0,m]\backslash\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha)
of size

\gamma^{\prime}
is nondecreasing.

The maximum of
\gamma^{\prime}
is the largest letter of

such that a larger letter appears to its left, and

is the only value in

which is greater than

. Hence, by Remark

,
\mathbf{Forb}(\sigma)=\mathbf{Forb}(\alpha)\sqcup\{\ell\in\Sigma\;:\>\ell\leqslant\mathbf{max}(\gamma^{\prime})\}\sqcup\{m\}

and the letter

by

yields a bijection between the words
\gamma
and the words of
\mathfrak{B}_{n-p,f-i-1}

Let
\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}
be the set of

-avoiding words
\omega

is the maximum of

. Clearly, taking any word

Lemma 62
.

For all
n\geqslant 2,k\geqslant 1

\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}=\mathfrak{b}_{n,k-1}+2\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k-1}+\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k-1}.

Let
n\geqslant 1,k\geqslant 0
, and
\omega\in\mathfrak{C}_{n,k}
.

•

If
\omega\in\mathfrak{C}^{(1)}_{n,k}
, then
\omega_{n}=\infty
. Removing
\omega_{n}
yields a word in
\mathfrak{C}_{n-1,k}
.

•

If
\omega\in\mathfrak{C}^{(2)}_{n,k}

\omega_{n}=k-1
.

–

If
\omega_{n-1}=k-1
, then removing
\omega_{n}
yields a word in
\mathfrak{C}^{(2)}_{n-1,k}

–

If
\omega_{n-1}=\infty

yields a word in

\omega_{n}
yields a word in
\mathfrak{C}^{(2)}_{n-1,i}

\mathfrak{c}^{(1)}_{n,k}=\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k},

and for all

,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}^{(2)}_{n,k}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{c}^{(2)}_{n-1,k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathfrak{c}^{(1)}_{n-1,i}+\mathfrak{c}^{(2)}_{n-1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}-\mathfrak{c}^{(1)}_{n-1,k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,i}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,i}.

By summing

and

We conclude by telescoping the sum over

. For all

,

\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}_{n,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n,k-1}

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,i}-(2\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k-1}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k-1}+\sum_{i=0}^{k-2}\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,i})

\displaystyle=2\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k}-\mathfrak{b}_{n-1,k-1}+\mathfrak{b}_{n-2,k-1}.\qed

By definition,

Lemma 65
.

For all
n,k,s\geqslant 1
,

\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s}^{(1)}=(s-1)\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k-1,s-1}.

For all

\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s}^{(2)}=\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s+1}^{(2)}+\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k,s}^{(2)}-\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k,s+1}^{(2)}+\mathfrak{d}_{n-2,k-1,s-1}.

Proof.

Let

,
\omega\in\mathfrak{D}_{n,k,s}
, and let
(p_{1},\dots,p_{s})
be the values of
\mathbf{Sites}(\omega)
in increasing order (in particular,

and
p_{s}=n+1
). We consider three different ways in which the word
\omega
can “grow”.

1.

Inserting a letter
k
. Let

, and let
\omega^{\prime}
be the word obtained by inserting the letter

at position

in

2.

Inserting two occurrences of the letter
k
. This is similar to the previous case, since the rightmost letter

can only be inserted at the end of the word in order to avoid the pattern 110. Let

, and let

be the word obtained by inserting one letter

at position

in

3.

Inserting a repeat of letter

, and only if

already contains at least two occurrences of the letter

(i.e.

). In that case, every letter

It can be seen that any word in a set

. In other words, we described a combinatorial generating tree for the class

.

From item 1, we have for all

\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s}^{(1)}=(s-1)\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k-1,s-1}.

From items 2 and 3, we have for all

\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s}^{(2)}=\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k,s}^{(2)}+\sum_{j=s-1}^{n-1}\mathfrak{d}_{n-2,k-1,j},

which may be rewritten

\displaystyle\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s}^{(2)}-\mathfrak{d}_{n,k,s+1}^{(2)}

\displaystyle=\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k,s}^{(2)}-\mathfrak{d}_{n-1,k,s+1}^{(2)}+\mathfrak{d}_{n-2,k-1,s-1}.\qed

For any integer sequence

Lemma 66
.

For all
n,k,f\geqslant 0
,

\mathfrak{b}_{n,k,f}=\sum_{t=0}^{f}\binom{t+k-f}{k-f}\sum_{b=0}^{f-t}\binom{f-t-1}{b}\sum_{s=1}^{n-t+1}\frac{(s+t-1)!}{(s-1)!}\mathfrak{d}_{n-t,f-b-t,s}^{(2)}.

Proof.

Let
\omega\in\mathfrak{B}_{n,k,f}
. In particular, by Remark
63
,
f=\mathbf{rep}(\omega)+\mathbf{top}(\omega)+1
, and
k-f
is the number of unused letters of
\omega
greater than
\mathbf{rep}(\omega)
.
Let
t=\mathbf{top}(\omega)
,

, and

be the number of unused letters in
\omega
less than
\mathbf{rep}(\omega)
. To summarize, over the alphabet
[0,k-1]
,
\omega
has:

•

t
letters greater than
\mathbf{rep}(\omega)
,

•

unused letters greater than
\mathbf{rep}(\omega)
,

•

b
unused letters less than
\mathbf{rep}(\omega)
,

•

\mathbf{rep}(\omega)-b=f-t-1-b

\mathbf{rep}(\omega)
, if

Replacing the

yields a word

For any

whose image under the above construction is

•

5
Shifted inversion sequences

5.1
Method

For all
n,s\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}=\{\sigma\in\mathbb{N}^{n}\;:\;\sigma_{i}<i+s\quad\forall i\in[1,n]\}
be the set of
s
-shifted
inversion sequences of size
n
.
In particular,
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}\subseteq\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s+1}
, and
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{0}=\mathcal{I}_{n}
. An
s
-shifted inversion sequence of size
n
can be seen as an inversion sequence of size
n+s
whose first
s
entries were removed. More precisely, for all
n,s\geqslant 0
,
\mathcal{I}_{n+s}=\{\sigma\cdot\tau\;:\;(\sigma,\tau)\in\mathcal{I}_{s}\times\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}\}
. For any set of patterns
P
, we denote by
\mathcal{I}_{n}^{s}(P)
the set of
P
-avoiding
s
-shifted inversion sequences of size
n
.

In this section, we study some patterns for which it is easier to split sequences around their minimum. We use a decomposition of sequences around their first minimum, similar to that of Section
4
, although a shifted inversion sequence now naturally appears on the right side when this decomposition is applied to an inversion sequence.

In Section
4
, the right side of the decomposition was a word, since the value of the maximum was fixed. A similar event occurs in the upcoming cases, when decomposing sequences around their first minimum: the sequences of Sections
5.2
and
5.3
avoid the pattern 102, so when the left side of the decomposition is nonempty (i.e. the first value of the sequence is not the minimum), all values of the right side must be less than or equal to all values of the left side, hence the right side is a word on a fixed alphabet, once again. It follows that shifted inversion sequences only appear on the right side of this decomposition when the left side is empty. Naturally, the left side of the decomposition around the first minimum of an inversion sequence is always empty, since the leftmost value of a nonempty inversion sequence is always 0. It follows that words only appear when this decomposition is recursively applied to a shifted inversion sequence.

For instance, applying this decomposition to
(0,0,0,0,4,4,5,0,3,4,2,3,0)\in\mathcal{I}_{13}(102)
will first remove each leading zero, yielding the sequence
(4,4,5,0,3,4,2,3,0)\in\mathcal{I}_{9}^{4}(102)
, then split it into a shifted inversion sequence
(4,4,5)
and a word
(3,4,2,3,0)
.

Theorem 67
.

For all

,

\mathfrak{a}_{n,s}=(\sum_{z=0}^{n}\mathfrak{a}_{n-z,s+z-1})+(\sum_{z=1}^{n-1}\mathfrak{a}_{n-z,s-1})+\sum_{r=1}^{n-2}\sum_{m=1}^{s}\mathfrak{b}_{r,m}\sum_{\ell=0}^{n-r-1}(n-r-\ell-\delta_{\ell,0})\mathfrak{a}_{\ell,s-m-1}.

Proof.

Let
n\geqslant 1,s\geqslant 0
, and
\sigma\in\mathfrak{A}_{n,s}
. Since
\sigma
avoids the pattern 010, all occurrences of the value 0 in
\sigma
must be consecutive. Let
z=|\{i\in[1,n]\;:\;\sigma_{i}=0\}|
be the number of occurrences of the value 0 in
\sigma
. Let
\sigma^{\prime}
be the sequence obtained by removing every occurrence of the value 0 from
\sigma
, and subtracting 1 from all remaining values.

•

If

or
\sigma_{1}=0
, then

•

If
\sigma_{1}\neq 0
and
\sigma_{n}=0
, then
\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{A}_{n-z,s-1}

•

If

,
\sigma_{0}\neq 0
, and
\sigma_{n}\neq 0

\alpha
and

are both nonempty, and their values are positive. Let

be the size of
\beta

be the maximum of

\sigma
avoids 102, every value of

[

]
