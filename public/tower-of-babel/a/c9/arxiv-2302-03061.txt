Energy measurements remain thermometrically optimal beyond weak coupling
Jonas Glatthard, Karen V. Hovhannisyan, Martí Perarnau-Llobet, Luis A. Correa, Harry J. D. Miller

Abstract
We develop a general perturbative theory of finite-coupling quantum thermometry up to second order in probe–sample interaction. By assumption, the probe and sample are in thermal equilibrium, so the probe is described by the mean-force Gibbs state. We prove that the ultimate thermometric precision can be achieved—to second order in the coupling—solely by means of local energy measurements on the probe. Hence, seeking to extract temperature information from coherences or devising adaptive schemes confers no practical advantage in this regime. Additionally, we provide a closed-form expression for the quantum Fisher information, which captures the probe’s sensitivity to temperature variations. Finally, we benchmark and illustrate the ease of use of our formulas with two simple examples. Our formalism makes no assumptions about separation of dynamical timescales or the nature of either the probe or the sample. Therefore, by providing analytical insight into both the thermal sensitivity and the optimal measurement for achieving it, our results pave the way for quantum thermometry in setups where finite-coupling effects cannot be ignored.

Finite-coupling quantum thermometry

2.1
Framework

Let us consider a probe
S

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{int}}.

The total system is in a Gibbs state at the inverse temperature

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}=\frac{e^{-\beta\boldsymbol{H}}}{\tilde{Z}},

with partition function

, and the goal is to determine the inverse temperature

. In this paper, we work in the units where

, the latter being the Boltzmann constant. In all what follows, operators are denoted with boldface symbols. Note that, since only the probe is measured, for our forthcoming results to hold it is only required that the probe’s state be given by

—the actual global state may differ from

and be microcanonical
[

,
75
,
76
,
77
,
78
,
79
]
, pure
[

,
81
,
77
]
, or neither
[

,
82
]
.

To control the magnitude of the probe–sample interaction, we will henceforth write the interaction term as
\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{int}}=\gamma\boldsymbol{V}
, where the dimensionless parameter
\gamma
is the coupling strength. The equilibrium state of the probe, from which the inverse temperature
\beta
is to be inferred,

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\frac{\text{tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\left[e^{-\beta\boldsymbol{H}}\right]}{\tilde{Z}},

(3)

is the so-called mean-force Gibbs state
[
64
,
69
]
, and it will only coincide with the bare thermal state of the probe,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\frac{e^{-\beta\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}{Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}},

(4)

in the limit of vanishing coupling
(\gamma\rightarrow 0)
. Here the partition function is
Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\tr{e^{-\beta\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}}
.

Estimates for
\beta
, drawn from a measurement record of length
N
via an unbiased estimator, carry an uncertainty
\delta\beta
such that the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) is upper-bounded through the Cramér–Rao inequality
[
38
,
39
,
83
,
84
,
37
]
as

\frac{\beta}{\delta\beta}\leq\sqrt{N\beta^{2}\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)}.

(5)

Here,
\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)
is the quantum Fisher information (QFI), which is given by
[
83
,
84
,
37
]

\displaystyle\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)=\text{tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\left[\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}^{2}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\right],

(6)

where the symmetric logarithmic derivative
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
(SLD) is implicitly defined by

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\frac{1}{2}\{\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S},\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\},

(7)

with
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
denoting the anticommutator. Equation (

) is a Lyapunov equation with respect to
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
and, since
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
is a bounded positive operator, it has a unique solution, given by
[
85
]

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=2\int^{\infty}_{0}d\lambda\ e^{-\lambda\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\partial_{\beta}\,\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})e^{-\lambda\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}.

(8)

The optimal thermometric measurement—namely, the one that minimises
\delta\beta
—is the projection onto the eigenbasis of
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}

[
83
,
84
,
37
]
. This saturates the Cramér–Rao bound (
5
) in the
N\to\infty
limit
[
37
,
48
]
. For any other measurement, the best-case scaling of the SNR is given by the ‘classical’ Cramér–Rao bound
[
38
,
39
]
. For example, when one projects onto the eigenbasis of
\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, then

\frac{\beta}{\delta\beta}\leq\sqrt{N\beta^{2}\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)},

(9)

where
\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)\leq\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)
is the (classical) Fisher information associated with this specific measurement. Here, the bound also becomes tight in the

limit
[
86
]
. By definition,

\displaystyle\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)=\sum\nolimits_{n}\tilde{p}_{n}\,(\partial_{\beta}\ln\tilde{p}_{n})^{2},

where

of

\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)
can be written as

\displaystyle\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)=\text{tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\left[\mathscr{D}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})^{2}\mathscr{D}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})\right],

where

is the dephasing operation in the eigenbasis of
\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}

\displaystyle\mathscr{D}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}(\boldsymbol{Q})\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{n}\ket{\epsilon_{n}}\bra{\epsilon_{n}}\boldsymbol{Q}\ket{\epsilon_{n}}\bra{\epsilon_{n}}.

(12)

The bare energy measurement defined by
\{\ket{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n}
is known to be optimal in the limit
\gamma\rightarrow 0
, where
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\to\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
and, therefore,
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\to\left\langle\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}-\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}

[
24
]
; here and throughout,
\left\langle\cdot\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\text{tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\left[\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\,\cdot\right]
. Furthermore, the bare energy measurement is optimal at any

as long as

, because then
[\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S},\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}]=0
, and therefore
[\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S},\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}]=0
, which means that
\{\ket{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n}
is an eigenbasis for
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
. In general, however,
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, and hence
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, will not commute with
\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, so the optimal thermometric basis will differ from
\{\ket{\epsilon_{n}}\}_{n}
. Nonetheless, as we prove below, the bare energy measurement remains as informative as the truly optimal measurement up to the first two leading orders in the perturbative expansion in the probe–sample coupling strength
\gamma
.

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}&=\int^{\beta}_{0}du\ (\beta-u)\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\big(\sum\nolimits_{k}\phi_{nk}(-iu)-\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu)\big),\end{split}

(24a)

\displaystyle\begin{split}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{n\neq m}&=\frac{1}{\Delta_{mn}}\sum\nolimits_{k}(\boldsymbol{S})_{nk}(\boldsymbol{S})_{km}\,\int_{0}^{\beta}du\,\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\,\left(e^{u\Delta_{km}}\,e^{\beta\Delta_{mn}}-e^{u\Delta_{kn}}\right).\end{split}

(24b)

We have defined

\displaystyle\phi_{nk}(x)=|(\boldsymbol{S})_{nk}|^{2}e^{-i\,x\,\Delta_{nk}}.

(25)

with
\Delta_{nk}=\epsilon_{k}-\epsilon_{n}
and
(\boldsymbol{S})_{nk}=\bra{\epsilon_{n}}\boldsymbol{S}\ket{\epsilon_{k}}
. We have also introduced here the probe and sample auto-correlation functions, defined as

\displaystyle\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle e^{ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}\boldsymbol{S}\,e^{-ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}\boldsymbol{S}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\text{tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\left[\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\,e^{ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}\boldsymbol{S}\,e^{-ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}\boldsymbol{S}\right]

(26a)

\displaystyle\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\langle e^{ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}\boldsymbol{B}\,e^{-ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}\boldsymbol{B}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=\text{tr}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\left[\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\,e^{ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}\boldsymbol{B}\,e^{-ix\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}}\boldsymbol{B}\right].

(26b)

From these formulas, we see that the condition for the validity of Eq. (
23
),
\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\ll\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
, is satisfied if
\gamma^{2}\langle\boldsymbol{S}^{2}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\langle\boldsymbol{B}^{2}\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\ll T^{2}
. This shows that, the smaller the temperature
T
, the weaker the coupling needs to be in order for the perturbative calculations to make sense. An analogue of Eq. (
24
) was derived in Ref.
[
61
]
and for the special case of linear coupling to a bosonic bath in Ref.
[
65
]
.

Thus equipped with Eqs. (
23
) and (
24
), we can turn to Eq. (
8
) and write the SLD as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)=-\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\gamma^{2}\sum\nolimits_{nm}\alpha_{nm}(\beta)\ket{\epsilon_{n}}\bra{\epsilon_{m}}+O(\gamma^{4})

(27a)

with
\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\coloneqq\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
and coefficients

\displaystyle\alpha_{nm}(\beta)=\frac{2\,p_{n}}{p_{n}+p_{m}}\,\partial_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nm}+\frac{p_{n}\Delta_{nm}}{p_{n}+p_{m}}\,(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nm}.

(27b)

Here,
\Delta_{n}\coloneqq\epsilon_{n}-\left\langle\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
. Also note that the populations appearing above are

p_{n}=\bra{\epsilon_{n}}\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\ket{\epsilon_{n}}=e^{-\beta\epsilon_{n}}/Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}

(28)

and should not be confused with the populations of the mean-force Gibbs state

\tilde{p}_{n}=\bra{\epsilon_{n}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\ket{\epsilon_{n}}=p_{n}+\gamma^{2}p_{n}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}+O(\gamma^{4}).

(29)

We now have the two ingredients needed to compute the QFI from Eq. (
6
). After some algebra, this can be compactly written as

\displaystyle\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)=\partial^{2}_{\beta}\ln Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\gamma^{2}\int^{\beta}_{0}du\ \left[\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\,\partial^{2}_{\beta}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu)+2\,\partial_{\beta}\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu)\right]+O(\gamma^{4}),

(30)

with the modified auto-correlation function
\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(x)\coloneqq(\beta-ix)\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(x)
. To second order in
\gamma
the QFI can thus be expressed solely in terms of the probe and sample correlation functions. We emphasise that the validity of Eqs. (
27
) and (
30
) is not, in any way, underpinned by the Born–Markov approximation.

Equations (
27
) and (
30
) are our second main result. They can be applied directly to
any
open quantum system under finite coupling, provided that Assumptions I and II are met. And since the systems satisfying these assumptions span an exceptionally wide range, our closed-form expressions for
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
and
\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)
open up new avenues in quantum thermometry. Note that, when Assumptions I and II are violated, the QFI will in general pick up a first-order term, as can be anticipated from Eq. (
22
). We provide illustrative examples of that in Appendix
A
.

Finally, it is often useful to talk in terms of
T
rather than inverse temperature
\beta
. Namely, from Eq. (
5
) we see that the best-case signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for estimates of
T
can be cast as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\left(\frac{T}{\delta T}\right)^{2}\leq\beta^{2}\,\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)\coloneqq C_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(T)+\gamma^{2}\xi(T),

(31)

with
C_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(T)=\beta^{2}\partial^{2}_{\beta}\ln Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\partial_{T}\left\langle\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
being the heat capacity of the probe when in local thermal equilibrium
[
24
]
, and where
\xi(T)
follows from the second-order term in Eq. (
30
).

\displaystyle=-\partial_{\beta}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu)

(57a)

\displaystyle\sum\nolimits_{n}p_{n}\Delta_{n}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\beta}^{2}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu).

(57b)

Furthermore, since
\sum\nolimits_{n}p_{n}\,\Delta_{n}=0
, we see that
\partial_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}
reduces to

\displaystyle\partial_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}=-\partial_{\beta}\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu),

(58)

where, recall that
\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)=(\beta-u)\,\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)
. Hence, Eq. (
56
) can be recast as desired:

\displaystyle\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)=\partial^{2}_{\beta}\ln Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\gamma^{2}\int^{\beta}_{0}du\ \left[\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\,\partial^{2}_{\beta}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu)+2\,\partial_{\beta}\tilde{\Phi}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}(-iu)\,\partial_{\beta}\Phi_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(-iu)\right]+O(\gamma^{4}).

4.4
Classical Fisher information for energy measurements

Finally, we connect with our general result in Eq. (
18
). To that end, we use the definition (
10
) of the (classical) Fisher information associated to energy measurements of
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
on the basis of the bare Hamiltonian
\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
. Namely,

\displaystyle\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)=\sum\nolimits_{n}\tilde{p}_{n}\,(\partial_{\beta}\ln\tilde{p}_{n})^{2}.

(59)

Plugging in the mean-force Gibbs state populations from Eq. (
29
) gives

\displaystyle\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)

\displaystyle=\sum\nolimits_{n}\tilde{p}_{n}(\partial_{\beta}\ln\tilde{p}_{n})^{2}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\beta}^{2}\ln{Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}}+\gamma^{2}\sum\nolimits_{n}\left[p_{n}\Delta_{n}^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}-2\Delta_{n}\partial_{\beta}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}\right]+O(\gamma^{4}).

Here, we have used the identities
\ln\tilde{p}_{n}=p_{n}+\gamma^{2}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\scriptscriptstyle S})_{nn}+O(\gamma^{4})
,
\partial_{\beta}\ln p_{n}=-\Delta_{n}
, and the fact that
\sum\nolimits_{n}p_{n}(\partial_{\beta}\ln p_{n})^{2}=\partial_{\beta}^{2}\ln Z_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
. As expected, we thus recover Eq. (
56
), meaning that local energy measurements on the probe are as efficient as the truly optimal measurements in finite-coupling quantum thermometry. This statement holds generally to second order in the coupling for any thermometric setup. The only underpinning assumption is that probe and sample have a separable coupling, and that the sample operator averages to zero when in a local thermal state. This choice for the dissipative interaction is indeed widespread in open quantum systems.

5
Conclusions

We have developed a theory of finite-coupling quantum thermometry, focusing on the second-order correction terms. On the one hand, we have obtained closed-form expressions for the symmetric logarithmic derivative, which fixes the optimal basis in which to measure the probe; and the quantum Fisher information, which gauges the responsiveness of the probe to temperature fluctuations on the sample. Our formulas make minimal assumptions on the probe–bath coupling and can be readily applied to any setup by simply computing the probe and sample auto-correlation functions. In particular, we make no assumptions on the nature of the sample, so that our framework is applicable even when standard open-system weak-coupling tools—based on the Born–Markov approximation—break down.

On the other hand, we have proved that
the sensitivity of
projective measurements in the local energy basis of the probe is always optimal to the first two leading orders in the probe–sample coupling. That is, even if temperature information may be encoded in the off-diagonal elements of the probe’s marginal state, the advantage in extracting and processing such information would only show up in higher order terms. Hence, measurement optimisation is unnecessary for quantum thermometry at moderate couplings.

Finally, we have illustrated the use of our approach with two relevant examples—the exactly solvable Caldeira–Legett model (which served as benchmark) and the spin–boson model. Specifically, we have derived an explicit expression for the second-order correction to the signal-to-noise ratio for temperature estimation, suitable for any open-system model which uses a canonical linear bath with quasi-continuum spectrum; regardless of the specific spectral density. Such formula can thus be of independent interest.

Our results open up new directions in finite-coupling thermometry. Namely, our formulas can easily deal with thermometry on more realistic non-linear sample models, which remain virtually unexplored to date. They can also facilitate the treatment of complex probe–sample interactions, such as the ones appearing when modelling an atomic impurity in a Bose–Einstein condensate
[
34
]
, without the need for
linearisation
. Crucially, having established the optimality of
the sensitivity of
energy measurements up to second order in the coupling strength highlights the importance of directing practical efforts towards measuring the probe in its local basis. Furthermore, guaranteeing that optimal
thermometric sensitivity
may be attained using a fixed
temperature-independent
basis also eliminates the need for more complex adaptive schemes.

Interestingly, lifting our Assumption II on the vanishing expectation value of the interaction in the stationary state of the sample would result in inhomogeneous terms, which might introduce a thermometric advantage in terms of additional temperature dependence. Studying how this may be exploited in practice will be the subject of future study.

Lastly, we emphasise that equilibrium thermometry assumes that the probe and sample are (jointly) thermalised. In practice, however, this assumption can break down whenever the thermalisation time is comparable to the reset time of the experiment. In those cases, the transient probe–sample dynamics becomes important. Such non-equilibrium thermometry has been studied
[
40
,
27
]
, often in the framework of weak-coupling master equations
[
94
,
95
,
33
]
. An alternative approach to dynamical thermometry is the use of collisional ancilla-systems as probe
[
30
,
96
,
97
,
44
,
98
]
. In presence of periodic driving even the long time behaviour becomes time dependent, as the probe settles into a limit cycle instead of a steady state
[
26
,
28
]
. Examining the dynamical implications of the finite probe–sample coupling scenario studied here remains an open problem that could be tackled using methods developed in the context of quantum control
[
99
,
100
,
101
,
102
]
.

Appendix A
Examples with
\left\langle\boldsymbol{B}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle B}\neq 0

In this short Appendix, we give explicit examples in which Assumption II in Eq. (
20
)—namely
\left\langle\boldsymbol{B}\right\rangle_{\scriptscriptstyle B}=0
—is not satisfied. Specifically, we
illustrate
how, in this case, the QFI and SLD may pick up terms of
O(\gamma)
.

A.1
Non-dissipative coupling

First, let us consider the two-qubit model

\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{int}}=\omega\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}_{2}+\omega\,\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,2}+\gamma\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,2},

where the sub-indices label the qubits, ‘1’ being the probe (
S
) and ‘2’ being the sample (
B
). Also, in all the expressions that follow, we shall set
\omega=1
for simplicity.

The corresponding mean-force Gibbs state can be expanded as

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\gamma\,p_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}+\gamma^{2}p_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}+O(\gamma^{3}),

with
p_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\,\beta\,\sech^{2}{\beta}\,\tanh{\beta}
and
p_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\,\beta^{2}\,\sech^{2}{\beta}\,\tanh^{3}{\beta}
. As we anticipated, in this case the QFI does feature a linear term, namely

\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)=\partial_{\beta}^{2}\ln{Z_{S}}+\sech^{4}{\beta}\left[\beta(\cosh{2\beta}-3)-\sinh{2\beta}\right]\,\gamma+O(\gamma^{2}),

and so does the symmetric logarithmic derivative

\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=-\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+l_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}+O(\gamma^{2}),

with
l_{1}=1+\tanh{\beta}+\sech^{2}{\beta}\,(\beta+2\beta\,\tanh{\beta}-1)
. We know from Sec.
2.2.1
that the classical Fisher information for measurements in the local energy basis and the QFI must agree in the zeroth and first order in
\gamma
. However, as noted in Sec.
2.1
, due to the coupling in this toy model being ’non-dissipative’, i.e.,
[\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{int}},\boldsymbol{H}]=0
,
\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
is diagonal in the
\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}
basis at all orders. Hence, energy measurements are always optimal in this particular case.

A.2
Dissipative coupling

Let us now turn to a more general scenario. Namely,

\boldsymbol{H}=\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle B}+\boldsymbol{H}_{\text{int}}=\omega\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}_{2}+\omega\,\boldsymbol{\mathbbm{1}}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,2}+\gamma\,\boldsymbol{\Pi}_{1}\otimes\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x,2},

where
\boldsymbol{\Pi}=\ket{1}\bra{1}
. In this case, we can see that

\displaystyle\tilde{\boldsymbol{\pi}}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=\boldsymbol{\pi}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\gamma\,p_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x,1}+\gamma^{2}\,p_{2}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{z,1}+O(\gamma^{3}),

with coefficients

\displaystyle p_{1}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\,\tanh{\beta}\,(\tanh{\beta}-1)

\displaystyle p_{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{8}\,\sech{\beta}\,(\tanh{\beta}-1)\,(\beta\,\sech{\beta}-\sinh{\beta}).

In particular, note how the mean-force Gibbs state does feature
\beta
-dependent coherences.

The corresponding symmetric logarithmic derivative is

\boldsymbol{L}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}=-\Delta\boldsymbol{H}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}+\gamma\,l_{1}\,\boldsymbol{\sigma}_{x,1}+O(\gamma^{2}),

with
l_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\,\sech^{2}{\beta}\,(2\tanh{\beta}-1)
, meaning that the optimal measurement is non-diagonal in the energy basis already to first order in the coupling strength. And yet, as we proved in full generality in Sec.
2.2.1
, the classical Fisher information of local energy measurements and the quantum Fisher information must agree to
second
leading order. Indeed, direct calculation shows that

\pazocal{F}_{\scriptscriptstyle S}(\beta)-\pazocal{I}_{\boldsymbol{H}_{S}}(\beta)=\frac{1}{4}\,\sech^{4}{\beta}\,(1-2\tanh{\beta})^{2}\,\gamma^{2}+O(\gamma^{3}).
