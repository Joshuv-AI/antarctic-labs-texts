Blaschke-Santaló type inequalities and quermassintegral inequalities in space forms
Yingxiang Hu, Haizhong Li

Abstract
. In this paper, we prove a family of identities for closed and strictly convex hypersurfaces in the sphere and hyperbolic/de Sitter space. As applications, we prove Blaschke-Santaló type inequalities in the sphere and hyperbolic/de Sitter space, which generalizes the previous work of Gao, Hug and Schneider [ 16 ] . We also prove the quermassintegral inequalities in hyperbolic/de Sitter space.

1.
Introduction

A convex body
K
in Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is a compact convex set with non-empty interior. For any interior point
z\in\operatorname{int}K
, the dual body of
K
with respect to
z
is defined by

\displaystyle K_{z}^{\ast}=\{y+z|y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:y\cdot(x-z)\leq 1,\forall~x\in K\}.

As noted by Santaló
[
39
]
, there exists a unique point
s=s(K)\in\operatorname{int}K
such that
\operatorname{Vol}(K_{s}^{\ast})\leq\operatorname{Vol}(K_{z}^{\ast})
for all
z\in\operatorname{int}K
. The famous Blaschke-Santaló inequality says that

(1.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Vol}(K)\cdot\operatorname{Vol}(K_{s}^{\ast})\leq|\mathbb{B}^{n+1}|^{2},

where
|\mathbb{B}^{n+1}|
is the volume of the unit ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Equality holds if and only if
K
is an ellipsoid. This inequality in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
was first proved by Blaschke
[
7
]
, and later it is generalized to higher dimensions by Santaló
[
39
]
with the equality case characterized by Petty
[
37
]
. For sake of its importance, more proofs of the Blaschke-Santaló inequality were given by Hug
[
27
]
, Meyer and Pajor
[
35
]
. Andrews
[
5
]
studied the affine normal flow, which also provides a proof of the Blaschke-Santaló inequality for smooth convex hypersurfaces. By using Steiner symmetrization, Lutwak and Zhang
[
30
]
extended the Blaschke-Santaló inequality to the
L_{p}
Blaschke-Santaló inequality for star bodies.

The volume functional can be considered as one of the quermassintegrals, which are the coefficients in the
Steiner formula
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Vol}(K+tB)=\sum_{i=0}^{n+1}\binom{n+1}{i}W_{i}(K)t^{i},

where
K+tB=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1}:d(K,x)\leq t\}
is the outer parallel body of
K
at distance
t\geq 0
. If
K
is smooth, then the quermassintegrals can be also expressed in terms of curvature integrals of its boundary and the enclosed volume as follows:

(1.2)

\begin{split}&W_{0}(K)=\operatorname{Vol}(K),\quad W_{k+1}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K}E_{k}d\mu,\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n,\end{split}

where
E_{k}
is the normalized
k
th mean curvature and
d\mu
is the area element of
\partial K
, respectively. For simplicity, we define the
k
th
mean radius
of the smooth bounded domain
K
as follows:

(1.3)

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(K)=f_{k}^{-1}(W_{k}(K)),

where
f_{k}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a monotone function defined by

f_{k}(r)=W_{k}(B_{r})=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial B_{r}}E_{k-1}d\mu=\frac{\omega_{n}}{n+1}r^{n+1-k},

the
k
th quermassintegral for the ball of radius
r
,
\omega_{n}
is the area of the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{n}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
f_{k}^{-1}
is the inverse of
f_{k}
. In terms of the mean radius, the Blaschke-Santaló inequality (
1.1
) becomes

(1.4)

\displaystyle\zeta_{0}(K)\cdot\zeta_{0}(K_{s}^{\ast})\leq 1.

The following inequalities can be considered as a natural extension of the Blaschke-Santaló inequality (
1.4
) in Euclidean space.

Theorem A
.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and
K_{z}^{\ast}
is its dual body of
K
with respect to any point
z\in\operatorname{int}K
. Then for
0\leq i,j\leq n
satisfying
i+j\geq n
, there holds

(1.5)

\displaystyle W_{i}(K)^{n+1-j}W_{j}(K_{z}^{\ast})^{n+1-i}\geq|\mathbb{B}^{n+1}|^{2n+2-i-j},

or equivalently,

(1.6)

\displaystyle\zeta_{i}(K)\cdot\zeta_{j}(K_{z}^{\ast})\geq 1.

Equality holds if and only if
K
is a ball centered at
z
.

For
i+j=n
, this result is due to Firey
[
15
]
and for
i=j=n
to Lutwak
[
31
]
. The general result was deduced from Firey’s case, independently by Heil
[
23
]
and Lutwak
[
32
]
. Further inequalities of this type were proved by Ghandehari
[
20
]
, see also Schneider’s book
[
42
, P. 393]
.

In the sphere or hyperbolic space, the Steiner formula also makes sense, but leads to different functionals. An investigation of such functionals within integral geometry can be found in Santaló’s book
[
40
]
. In the smooth category, convex bodies and their dual bodies in the sphere, as well as the counterparts in hyperbolic/de Sitter space, are well-defined in terms of the Gauss map (see §
2.2
for details). The motivation of this paper is to extend Blaschke-Santaló’s inequality (
1.4
) and the inequalities (
1.6
) in the sphere and hyperbolic/de Sitter space. The quermassintegrals
W_{k}
in Riemannian space forms and the upper branch of de Sitter space are introduced in (
3.1
), (
3.2
) respectively, and the mean radius
\zeta_{k}
can be similarly defined by (
3.3
).

The first result of this paper is a complete family of identities for convex bodies and their dual bodies in the sphere, where the dual body of a convex body in the sphere is defined by (
2.14
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
K
be a smooth bounded and strictly convex domain in
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
and
K^{\ast}
the dual body of
K
. Then the following identities hold:

(1.7)

\displaystyle\tan\left(\zeta_{n-k}(K)\right)\cdot\tan\left(\zeta_{k}(K^{\ast})\right)=1,\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n.

We also prove a complete family of identities for convex bodies in hyperbolic space and their dual bodies in de Sitter space, where the dual body in de Sitter space is defined by (
2.19
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
K
be a smooth bounded and strictly convex domain in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
and
K^{\ast}
the dual body of
K
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
. Then the following identities hold:

(1.8)

\displaystyle\coth(\zeta_{k}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{n-k}(K^{\ast}))=1,\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n.

As a direct application of Theorem

(1.10)

\displaystyle\tan\left(\zeta_{n-k}(K)\right)\cdot\tan\left(\zeta_{0}(K^{\ast})\right)\leq

\displaystyle\tan\left(\zeta_{n-l+2i}(K)\right)\cdot\tan\left(\zeta_{l}(K^{\ast})\right)\geq

\displaystyle\tan\left(\zeta_{n-l-2j}(K)\right)\cdot\tan\left(\zeta_{l}(K^{\ast})\right)\leq

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Hypersurfaces in semi-Riemannian manifolds

Let
(N,\overline{g})
be an
(n+1)
-dimensional semi-Riemannian manifold. The coordinates
(x^{\alpha})_{0\leq\alpha\leq n}
in
N
are labelled from
0
to
n
. In general, the coordinate
x^{0}
refers to the radial distance to a fixed point or the time function. Let
(\xi^{i})
be the local coordinates system of an open neighbourhood
U
in
M
, where
M
is a smooth hypersurface in
N
. Locally,
M
can be represented by a map
X=X(\xi)=(x^{\alpha}(\xi))
, and the induced metric is given by

\displaystyle g_{ij}=\overline{g}(X_{i},X_{j}),\quad\text{where}~X_{i}=\frac{\partial X}{\partial\xi^{i}}.

Since
M
has codimension
1
, its normal space is spanned by a single vector
\nu
at each point
x\in M
. We can always define a continuous normal vector field
\nu
by requiring that

\displaystyle\det\left(X_{1},\cdots,X_{n},\nu\right)>0,\quad\forall\xi\in U.

Moreover, the normal vector
\nu
is normalized such that

\displaystyle\overline{g}(\nu,\nu)=\sigma=\pm 1.

The geometry of a hypersurface
M
in a semi-Riemannian manifold
N
is governed by the following basic equations (see
[
18
, (1.1.6),(1.1.21),(1.1.36),(1.1.37)]
):

(i)

The Gauss formula is

(2.1)

\displaystyle X_{ij}=-\sigma h_{ij}\nu,

where
X_{ij}
is the second covariant derivatives of
X
with respect to the induced metric
g
, and
h_{ij}
is called the
second fundamental form
of
M
with respect to
-\sigma\nu
.

(ii)

The Weingarten equation is

(2.2)

\displaystyle\nu_{i}=h_{i}^{k}X_{k},

where
h_{i}^{k}=g^{kl}h_{il}
is the Weingarten matrix, with its eigenvalues
\kappa=(\kappa_{1},\cdots,\kappa_{n})
being called the
principal curvatures
of
M
in
N
.

(iii)

The Codazzi equation is

(2.3)

\displaystyle h_{ij;k}-h_{ik;j}=\overline{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\nu^{\alpha}X_{i}^{\beta}X_{j}^{\gamma}X_{k}^{\delta},

where
h_{ij;k}=\nabla_{k}h_{ij}
and
\overline{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
is the components of Riemann curvature tensor.

(iv)

The Gauss equation is

(2.4)

\displaystyle R_{ijkl}=\sigma(h_{ik}h_{jl}-h_{jk}h_{il})+\overline{R}_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}X_{i}^{\alpha}X_{j}^{\beta}X_{k}^{\gamma}X_{l}^{\delta}.

Remark 6
.

The choice of the normal
\nu
in the Gaussian formula (
2.1
) is free, i.e., we could just as well have replaced
\nu
by
-\nu
, then the principal curvatures
\kappa_{i}
would have been replaced by
-\kappa_{i}
. We make the following convention on the choice of
\nu
:

(1)

If
M
is a closed oriented hypersurface and the ambient space is Riemannian, then in this case we always choose
\nu
as the outward normal to
M
. Then the starshapedness of
M
is equivalent to
\overline{g}(\partial_{r},\nu)>0
.

(2)

If
M
is spacelike (i.e., the induced metric
g
of
M
is Riemannian) and the ambient space is Lorentzian, and the coordinate system
(x^{\alpha})
is supposed to be
future directed
, i.e., the time function
x^{0}
is increasing on future directed curves. Then in this case we always choose
\nu
to be also future directed (timelike) normal, which is equivalent to
\overline{g}(\partial_{r},\nu)<0
.

In the sequel, the Euclidean space
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
is the
(n+2)
-dimensional vector space equipped with the bilinear form

\displaystyle\langle v,w\rangle=\sum_{\alpha=0}^{n+1}v^{\alpha}w^{\alpha},

while the Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
is the
(n+2)

\displaystyle\langle v,w\rangle=-v^{0}w^{0}+\sum_{\alpha=1}^{n+1}v^{\alpha}w^{\alpha}.

The Riemannian space form
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
is the Riemannian manifold with constant curvature
\epsilon

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{if $\epsilon=1$},\quad\mathbb{S}^{n+1}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}:~\langle y,y\rangle=1\};\\
&\text{if $\epsilon=0$},\quad\mathbb{R}^{n+1};\\
&\text{if $\epsilon=-1$},\quad\mathbb{H}^{n+1}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}:~\langle y,y\rangle=-1,y^{0}>0\}.\end{aligned}\right.

Similarly, let
\mathbb{N}^{n,1}(\epsilon)
be the following Lorentzian manifold of constant curvature

[

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\text{if $\epsilon=1$},\quad\mathbb{S}^{n,1}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}:~\langle y,y\rangle=1\};\\

\mathbb{S}^{n}
. Here we restrict the de Sitter space
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
to be its upper branch
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
and hence
\rho>0
, which guarantees that a bounded domain can be enclosed by a spacelike hypersurface and
\{y^{0}=0\}
. Along the hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
, the future directed unit timelike normal is given by

(2.7)

\displaystyle\nu=\frac{1}{v}\left(1,-\frac{1}{\cosh^{2}\rho}\sigma^{ik}\rho_{k}\right),

where
\rho_{k}=\partial_{\theta^{k}}\rho
is the covariant derivative on
\mathbb{S}^{n}
and

(2.8)

\displaystyle v^{2}=1-\cosh^{-2}\rho\sigma^{ij}\rho_{i}\rho_{j}=1-\cosh^{-2}\rho|D\rho|^{2}.

The induced metric and the area element of
M
are given by

\displaystyle g_{ij}=-\rho_{i}\rho_{j}+\cosh^{2}\rho\sigma_{ij},

and

(2.9)

\displaystyle d\mu=\sqrt{\det(g_{ij})}d\theta=\cosh^{n}\rho v\sqrt{\det(\sigma_{ij})}d\theta.

Along the variational vector field
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X
, the spacelike hypersurfaces
M_{t}
can be parametrized by

\displaystyle M_{t}=\{(\rho(t,\theta),\theta):\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\},

where
\rho(t,\cdot)
is a smooth function defined on the unit sphere. Hence the evolution of the spacelike hypersurfaces
M_{t}
can be reduced to a parabolic equation for the radial function. As long as the evolving hypersurface exists and remains spacelike, the radial function
\rho
satisfies

(2.10)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}=\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X,\nu\rangle v,

where
v
is given by (
2.8
). Notice that the velocity vector of (
2.10
) is in the direction of
\partial_{r}
. A direct calculation yields

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\partial_{r}=

\displaystyle(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X)^{\top}+\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X,\nu\rangle(\nu-v\partial_{r}),

and
\langle\nu-v\partial_{r},\nu\rangle=0
. Therefore, the difference
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X-\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t}\partial_{r}
is a time-dependent tangential vector field, and (
2.10
) follows by composing the original flow with the reparametrization associated with this tangential vector field.

2.2.
Duality

In this subsection, we recall the duality relations in the sphere and in hyperbolic/de Sitter space via the Gauss map. For more details, we refer the readers to Gerhardt’s book
[
18
, Chapters 9&10]
.

Duality in the sphere
.
Let
X:M_{0}\rightarrow M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
be an embedding of a closed, connected, strictly convex hypersurface. Then by the Hadamard theorem in the sphere
[
14
]
,
M
is embedded, homeomorphic to
\mathbb{S}^{n}
, contained in an open hemisphere and it is the boundary of a convex body
\hat{M}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
. Viewing
M
as a submanifold of codimension
2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, its Gaussian formula is

\displaystyle X_{ij}=-g_{ij}X-h_{ij}X^{\ast},

where
X^{\ast}\in T_{X}\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
represents the unit outward normal
\nu\in T_{X}\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
. Then the map

(2.11)

\displaystyle X^{\ast}:M_{0}\rightarrow M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}

is an embedding of a closed and strictly convex hypersurface
M^{\ast}
. We call this map the
Gauss map
of
M
.

The duality in the sphere is characterized by the following theorem.

Theorem 2.1
.

[
18
, Theorem 9.2.5, 9.2.9]

Let
X:M_{0}\rightarrow M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
be a closed, connected, strictly convex hypersurface. Then the Gauss map
X^{\ast}
given by (
2.11
) is the embedding of a closed, connected, strictly convex hypersurface
M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
. Viewing
M^{\ast}
as a submanifold of codimension
2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
, its Gaussian formula is

(2.12)

\displaystyle X^{\ast}_{ij}=-g^{\ast}_{ij}X^{\ast}-h^{\ast}_{ij}X,

where
g^{\ast}_{ij}
and
h^{\ast}_{ij}
are the metric and the second fundamental form of the hypersurface
M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
and
X=X(\xi)
is the embedding of
M
which also represents the unit outward normal of
M
. The second fundamental form
h^{\ast}_{ij}
is defined with respect to the unit inner normal vector.

The second fundamental forms of
M
,
M^{\ast}
and the corresponding principal curvatures
\kappa_{i},\kappa_{i}^{\ast}
satisfy

(2.13)

\displaystyle h_{ij}=h^{\ast}_{ij}=\langle X^{\ast}_{i},X_{j}\rangle,\quad\kappa_{i}^{\ast}=\kappa_{i}^{-1},

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n+2}
.

The hypersurface
M^{\ast}
is also called the
polar hypersurface
of
M
, which can be equivalently defined by

M^{\ast}=\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}:\sup_{x\in M}\langle x,y\rangle=0\}.

Let
\hat{M}
be the (closed) convex body of
M\subset\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
. Then the
dual body
of
\hat{M}
is defined by

(2.14)

\displaystyle\hat{M}^{\ast}=\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq 0,~\forall~x\in\hat{M}\}.

By the Hadamard theorem, there exists a point
z\in\hat{M}
such that
\hat{M}\subset\mathcal{H}(z)
, where
\mathcal{H}(z)
is the open hemisphere centered at
z\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
. Then it follows from
[
18
, Corollary 9.2.10]
that the dual body
\hat{M}^{\ast}
of
\hat{M}
is a convex body in
\mathcal{H}(-z)
, where
-z\in\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
is the antipodel point of
z
. Moreover, the dual body
\hat{M}^{\ast}
is the convex body of
M^{\ast}
. Furthermore, we have
(M^{\ast})^{\ast}=M
and
(\hat{M}^{\ast})^{\ast}=\hat{M}
.

Duality between hyperbolic/de Sitter space
.
Similar as the duality in the sphere, let
X:M_{0}\rightarrow M\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
be an embedding of a closed, connected, strictly convex hypersurface. Then the representation
X^{\ast}\in T_{X}\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
of the unit outward normal
\nu\in T_{X}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
is also an embedding

(2.15)

\displaystyle X^{\ast}:M_{0}\rightarrow M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n,1}

of a strictly convex, closed and spacelike hypersurface
M^{\ast}
. We also call
X^{\ast}
the
Gauss map
of
M
. The duality in hyperbolic/de Sitter space is characterized by the following two theorems.

Theorem 2.2
.

[
18
, Theorem 10.4.4]

Let
X:M_{0}\rightarrow M\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
be a closed, connected, strictly convex hypersurface. Then the Gauss map
X^{\ast}
given by (
2.15
) is an embedding of a closed, spacelike, strictly convex hypersurface
M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
. Viewing
M^{\ast}
as a submanifold of codimension
2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
, its Gaussian formula is

(2.16)

\displaystyle X^{\ast}_{ij}=-g^{\ast}_{ij}X^{\ast}-h^{\ast}_{ij}X,

where
g^{\ast}_{ij}
and
h^{\ast}_{ij}
are the metric and the second fundamental form of the hypersurface
M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
and
X=X(\xi)
is the embedding of
M
which also represents the future directed normal vector of
M^{\ast}
. The second fundamental form
h^{\ast}_{ij}
is defined with respect to the future directed unit normal, where the time orientation is inherited from
\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
.

The second fundamental forms of
M
,
M^{\ast}
and the corresponding principal curvatures
\kappa_{i},\kappa_{i}^{\ast}
satisfy

(2.17)

\displaystyle h_{ij}=h^{\ast}_{ij}=\langle X^{\ast}_{i},X_{j}\rangle,\quad\kappa_{i}^{\ast}=\kappa_{i}^{-1}.

where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
is the inner product in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
.

The hypersurface
M^{\ast}
is also called the
polar hypersurface
of
M
, which can be also equivalently defined by

M^{\ast}=\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n,1}:\sup_{x\in M}\langle x,y\rangle=0\}.

Let
\hat{M}
be the (closed) convex body of
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, which is characterized by (see
[
18
, Lemma 10.4.1]
)

(2.18)

\displaystyle\hat{M}=\{y\in\mathbb{H}^{n+1}:\langle y,X^{\ast}\rangle\leq 0,~\forall X\in M\}.

In the hyperboloid model of the hyperbolic space, the point
e_{0}=(1,0,\cdots,0)\in\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
is called the
Beltrami point
. The Hadamard theorem in hyperbolic space (
[
18
, Theorem 10.3.1]
) states that any strictly convex hypersurface
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
bounds a strictly convex body
\hat{M}
of the hyperbolic space. Due to the homogeneity of the hyperbolic space, any interior point
z
in
\hat{M}
may act as a Beltrami point by using a Lorenz boost
\mathbb{O}_{z}\in O(n+1,1)
to bring
z
to
e_{0}
. By
[
18
, Theorem 10.4.9]
, the Gauss maps provide a bijection between the closed, strictly convex and connected hypersurfaces
M\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
having the Beltrami point in the interior of their convex bodies and the spacelike, closed, connected and strictly convex hypersurfaces
M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
, where
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
is the upper branch of the de Sitter space. Therefore, the polar hypersurface
M^{\ast}
of
M
is contained in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
. Moreover, the dual body of
\hat{M}
can be defined by

(2.19)

\displaystyle\hat{M}^{\ast}=\{y\in\mathbb{S}^{n,1}:\langle x,y\rangle\leq 0,~\forall x\in\hat{M}\}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+},

which is the bounded domain enclosed by the slice
\{y^{0}=0\}
and the hypersurface
M^{\ast}
.

The reverse direction starting from a closed, spacelike, strictly convex hypersurface in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
will be stated as follows:

Theorem 2.3
.

[
18
, Theorem 10.4.5]

Let
X^{\ast}:M_{0}\rightarrow M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n,1}
be a closed, connected, spacelike and strictly convex hypersurface. Viewing
M^{\ast}
as a submanifold of codimension
2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
, its Gaussian formula is

(2.20)

\displaystyle X^{\ast}_{ij}=-g^{\ast}_{ij}X^{\ast}+h^{\ast}_{ij}X,

where
X^{\ast}=X^{\ast}(\xi)
is the embedding,
X
the future directed unit normal, and
g^{\ast}_{ij}
and
h^{\ast}_{ij}
the induced metric and the second fundamental form of the hypersurface
M^{\ast}\subset\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
. We define the Gauss map as
X=X(\xi)

\displaystyle X:M_{0}\rightarrow M\subset\mathbb{H}^{n+1}.

Then the Gauss map is the embedding of a closed, connected, strictly convex hypersurface
M
in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. Viewing
M
as a submanifold of codimension
2
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1,1}
, then its Gaussian formula is

\displaystyle X_{ij}=g_{ij}X-h_{ij}X^{\ast}.

where
g_{ij}
,
h_{ij}
be the induced metric and the second fundamental form of
M
. The second fundamental forms of
M
,
M^{\ast}
and the corresponding principal curvatures
\kappa_{i},\kappa_{i}^{\ast}
satisfy

(2.21)

\displaystyle h_{ij}=h^{\ast}_{ij}=\langle X_{i},X^{\ast}_{j}\rangle,\quad\kappa^{\ast}_{i}=\kappa_{i}^{-1},

Combining Theorems
2.2
with
2.3
, we conclude that
(M^{\ast})^{\ast}=M
and
(\hat{M}^{\ast})^{\ast}=\hat{M}
.

Remark 7
.

For a convex body
K
in Euclidean space, its dual body
K_{z}^{\ast}
depends on the choice of the interior point
z
in
K
. For a strictly convex body
K
in
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, or a spacelike and strictly convex domain
K
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
, its dual body
K^{\ast}
in
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
of
K
is defined by (
2.14
), (
2.19
) and (
2.18
), respectively. This definition is independent of the choice of the interior point in
K
.

2.3.
Dual flows

The dual flows for (pure) curvature flows in the sphere was first introduced by Gerhardt
[
19
]
, and later it was also used by Yu
[
48
]
for curvature flows in hyperbolic space. For general curvature flows, the deduction of the dual flows can be found in
[
9
, (4.3),(4.4)]
.

Let
X_{0}:M_{0}^{n}\rightarrow(N^{n+1},\overline{g})
be a smooth embedding such that
M=X_{0}(M_{0})
is a closed, strictly convex hypersurface, where
N
is one of
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
and
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
. We consider the smooth family of strictly convex embeddings
X:M_{0}\times[0,T)\rightarrow N
satisfying

(2.22)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}X=&-\sigma\mathcal{F}\nu,\\
X(\cdot,0)=&X_{0}(\cdot).\end{aligned}\right.

where
\nu
is the unit normal of
M_{t}=X(M_{0},t)
and
\mathcal{F}
is a smooth and strictly monotone function of the principal curvatures of
M_{t}
. Here the signature
\sigma=\overline{g}(\nu,\nu)=\langle\nu,\nu\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
represents the Euclidean or the Minkowski inner product for the flows in
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
, respectively. In all three cases, the pair
X
,
X^{\ast}=\nu
satisfies
\langle X,X^{\ast}\rangle=0
. It follows that

\displaystyle\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X^{\ast},X\rangle=-\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X,X^{\ast}\rangle=-\langle-\sigma\mathcal{F}X^{\ast},X^{\ast}\rangle=\mathcal{F}.

Due to
\langle X^{\ast},X^{\ast}_{i}\rangle=0
, the Weingarten equation
X^{\ast}_{i}=h_{i}^{k}X_{k}
(see also
[
18
, Lemma 9.2.4, Lemma 10.4.3]
) imply that

\displaystyle 0=\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X^{\ast},X^{\ast}_{i}\rangle=\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X^{\ast},h_{i}^{k}X_{k}\rangle=-h_{i}^{k}\langle X^{\ast},(-\sigma\mathcal{F}X^{\ast})_{k}\rangle=\sigma h_{i}^{k}\langle X^{\ast},\mathcal{F}_{k}X^{\ast}\rangle=h_{i}^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k},

where
\mathcal{F}_{k}=\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\xi^{k}}
. Note that
X=(X^{\ast})^{\ast}=\nu^{\ast}
and
X^{\ast}_{i}=h_{i}^{k}X_{k}
span
T_{X^{\ast}}\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
T_{X^{\ast}}\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
or
T_{X^{\ast}}\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X^{\ast}=\langle X,X\rangle\mathcal{F}X+h_{i}^{k}\mathcal{F}_{k}(g^{\ast})^{il}X^{\ast}_{l}=\sigma^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\nu^{\ast}+h_{i}^{k}(g^{\ast})^{il}\mathcal{F}_{k}X^{\ast}_{l}=\sigma^{\ast}\mathcal{F}\nu^{\ast}+(b^{\ast})^{kl}\mathcal{F}_{k}X^{\ast}_{l},

where
\sigma^{\ast}=\langle X,X\rangle
,
((b^{\ast})_{i}^{j})
is the inverse matrix of the Weingarten matrix
\mathcal{W}^{\ast}=((h^{\ast})_{i}^{j})
of
M^{\ast}
and
h_{i}^{k}=(b^{\ast})_{i}^{k}
due to (
2.13
), (
2.17
) and (
2.21
). Let us define

(2.23)

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}^{\ast}(\mathcal{W}^{\ast}):=-\mathcal{F}(\mathcal{W}).

Let
N^{\ast}
be dual ambient space of
N
, that is,
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
and
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, respectively. Then the flow of the polar hypersurfaces is a smooth family of strictly convex embeddings
X^{\ast}:M_{0}\times[0,T)\rightarrow N^{\ast}
satisfying

(2.24)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t}X^{\ast}=&-\sigma^{\ast}\mathcal{F}^{\ast}\nu^{\ast}-(b^{\ast})^{kl}\mathcal{F}^{\ast}_{k}X^{\ast}_{l},\\
X^{\ast}(\cdot,0)=&X^{\ast}_{0}(\cdot).\end{aligned}\right.

where
\mathcal{F}^{\ast}
is evaluated at the Weingarten matrix
\mathcal{W}^{\ast}
of
M_{t}^{\ast}=X^{\ast}(M_{0},t)
, and
X^{\ast}_{0}:M_{0}\rightarrow N^{\ast}
is the smooth embedding of
M^{\ast}=X_{0}^{\ast}(M_{0})
which is determined by the Gauss map of the hypersurface
M
. Therefore, a flow of the form (
2.22
) in the ambient spaces
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
has a dual flow of the form (
2.24
) in the ambient spaces
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}
,
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, respectively.

3.
Quermassintegrals

Let
K
be a bounded domain with smooth boundary
\partial K
in Riemannian space forms
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
with constant curvature
\epsilon\in\{-1,0,1\}
. Then the quermassintegrals
W_{k}(K)
can be expressed by a linear combination of the curvature integrals and of the enclosed volume as follows:

(3.1)

\begin{split}&W_{0}(K)=\operatorname{Vol}(K),\quad W_{1}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\operatorname{Area}(\partial K),\\
&W_{k+1}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K}E_{k}d\mu+\epsilon\frac{k}{n+2-k}W_{k-1}(K),\quad k=1,\cdots,n,\end{split}

where
E_{k}
is the normalized
k
th mean curvature and
d\mu
is the area element of
\partial K
.

When the ambient space is the upper branch of the de Sitter space, then the volume of the region
K
enclosed by the slice
\{y^{0}=0\}
and a spacelike graph
\partial K=\{(\rho(\theta),\theta):\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{n}\}
can be expressed by (see
[
34
, Section 4]
)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Vol}(K)=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\rho(\theta)}\sqrt{|\det(\overline{g}_{ij}(r,\theta))|}drd\theta=\int_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}\int_{0}^{\rho(\theta)}\cosh^{n}r\sqrt{\det(\sigma_{ij})}drd\theta,

where
\overline{g}=-dr^{2}+\cosh^{2}rg_{\mathbb{S}^{n}}
. As the hypersurface
\partial K
is spacelike, the surface area of
\partial K
is defined as usual by its induced Riemannian metric. For simplicity, we also call
K
to be a
spacelike domain
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
if
\partial K
is a spacelike hypersurface. It is natural to define the quermassintegrals for a smooth bounded domain
K
with spacelike boundary
\partial K
in the upper branch of the de Sitter space. In this direction, Scheuer
[
41
]
defined the second quermassintegral as

\displaystyle W_{2}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K}E_{1}d\mu-\frac{1}{n+1}\operatorname{Vol}(K).

Inspired by the expression (
3.1
) of quermassintegrals in the Riemannian space forms, the quermassintegrals for a spacelike domain
K
in
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n,1}
can be defined inductively by

(3.2)

\begin{split}&W_{0}(K)=\operatorname{Vol}(K),\quad W_{1}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\operatorname{Area}(\partial K),\\
&W_{k+1}(K)=\frac{1}{n+1}\int_{\partial K}E_{k}d\mu-\frac{k}{n+2-k}W_{k-1}(K),\quad k=1,\cdots,n.\end{split}

In Riemannian space forms
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
, the definition (
3.1
) coincides with the measure of totally geodesic
k
-dimensional subspaces in
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
which intersect
K
, which is the definition of quermassintegrals from integral geometry, see
[
40
]
or
[
44
, Proposition 7]
. It would be an interesting question whether one can establish the integral geometric theory for smooth spacelike domains in
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n,1}
. For example, the Crofton formulas and Cauchy formulas have been extended to Lorentzian constant curvature space, see
[
43
]
.

Remark 8
.

It is tempting to ask that whether or not there is a similar definition for the quermassintegrals in the other Lorentzian space forms, i.e., the Minkowski space
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
or the anti de Sitter space
\mathbb{H}^{n,1}
. However, we are not able to define the surface area of the complete spacelike hypersurfaces in
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
and
\mathbb{H}^{n,1}
. For example, the slice
\{y^{0}=0\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n,1}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n,1}
is a complete noncompact spacelike hypersurface, which it is isometric to
\mathbb{R}^{n}
or
\mathbb{H}^{n}
, respectively.

The
k
th
mean radius
of the smooth bounded domain
K
in
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
or a spacelike domain
K
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
can be similarly defined by

(3.3)

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(K)=f_{k}^{-1}(W_{k}(K)),

where
f_{k}:[0,\infty)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}_{+}
is a smooth function determined by
f_{k}(s)=W_{k}(B_{s})
in
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
, where
B_{s}
is the geodesic ball of radius
s
; or
f_{k}(s)=W_{k}(\{0\leq r\leq s\})
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
, where
\{0\leq r\leq s\}
denotes the region enclosed by the slice
\{y^{0}=0\}
and the slice
\{r=s\}
in
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n,1}
. In all above cases, it is easy to check that
f_{k}
is monotone increasing, and
f_{k}^{-1}
is the inverse of
f_{k}
.

The key feature of quermassintegrals is the following elegant variational formulas in Riemannian space forms and in de Sitter space.

Lemma 3.1
.

Assume that one of the following holds:

(i)

Let
K_{t}
be a family of smooth bounded domains in
\mathbb{N}^{n+1}(\epsilon)
such that
\partial K_{t}
is a family of smooth closed hypersurfaces.

(ii)

Let
K_{t}
be a family of smooth bounded domains in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
such that
\partial K_{t}
is a family of smooth closed spacelike hypersurfaces.

Let
\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X
be the variational vector field of
\partial K_{t}
. Then

(3.4)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{k}(K_{t})=\frac{n+1-k}{n+1}\int_{\partial K_{t}}\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X,\nu\rangle E_{k}d\mu_{t},\quad k=0,1,\cdots,n.

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote by
f=\langle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}X,\nu\rangle
, where
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the Euclidean inner product for
\mathbb{S}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and the Minkowski inner product for
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
,
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
, respectively.

(i) By
[
38
, Theorem A]
, the curvature integral of
\partial K_{t}
evolves by

(3.5)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\partial K_{t}}E_{k}d\mu_{t}=\int_{\partial K_{t}}\left((n-k)E_{k+1}-\epsilon kE_{k-1}\right)fd\mu_{t},\quad k=1,\cdots,n.

The volume of
K_{t}
evolves by (see e.g.
[
6
]
)

(3.6)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\operatorname{Vol}(K_{t})=\int_{\partial K_{t}}fd\mu_{t}.

Now the variational formula (
3.4
) can be proved by induction (see
[
46
, Proposition 3.1]
). In view of (
3.5
) and (
3.6
), it is true for
k=0,1
. Assume that it is true for
k-1
, that is,

(3.7)

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{k-1}(K_{t})=\frac{n+1-(k-1)}{n+1}\int_{\partial K_{t}}E_{k-1}fd\mu_{t},

then by using (
3.1
), (
3.5
) and the inductive assumption (

), we can compute that

\displaystyle W_{n-2k}(K)\leq f_{n-2k}\circ f^{-1}_{n-1}(W_{n-1}(K)),\quad 0<2k\leq n.

We prove (
5.15
) by induction. For
k=1
, by taking an interior point of
K
as the Beltrami point, its dual
K^{\ast}
is a strictly convex and spacelike domain in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
. Then the quermassintegral inequality (
5.10
) in
\mathbb{S}_{+}^{n,1}
implies that

\tanh(\zeta_{2}(K^{\ast}))\leq\tanh(\zeta_{1}(K^{\ast})).

Combining this with the identities (
5.13
), i.e.,

\coth(\zeta_{n-2}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{2}(K^{\ast}))=\coth(\zeta_{n-1}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{1}(K^{\ast})),

we obtain

\displaystyle\zeta_{n-2}(K)\leq\zeta_{n-1}(K),

in view of the monotonicity of
\coth s
. This verifies (
5.15
) for
k=1
.

Assume that (
5.15
) is true for
k-1
, i.e.,

(5.16)

\displaystyle W_{n-2k+2}(K)\leq f_{n-2k+2}\circ f^{-1}_{n-1}(W_{n-1}(K)),

we show that (
5.15
) also holds for
k
. Along the harmonic mean curvature flow starting from
\partial K
in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
, we know that the flow hypersurface
M_{t}=\partial K_{t}
is strictly convex and it shrinks to a round point as
t\rightarrow T^{\ast}
. Then by (
3.4
), we have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{n-1}(K_{t})=

\displaystyle-\frac{2}{n+1}\int_{M_{t}}E_{n}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-2W_{n+1}(K_{t})-nW_{n-1}(K_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\left(-2W_{n+1}(B_{r})-nW_{n-1}(B_{r})\right)\right|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{2}{n+1}\int_{\partial B_{r}}E_{n}d\mu\right|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))}

(5.17)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{2}{n+1}\omega_{n}\cosh^{n}r\right|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))},

where we used (
3.1
) and

W_{n+1}(K_{t})=W_{n+1}(B_{r})|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))}=\frac{\omega_{n}}{n+1}

due to Gauss-Bonnet-Chern theorem (see
[
12
,
13
]
, see also
[
44
]
).
For
k\geq 2
, we also have

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}W_{n-2k}(K_{t})=

\displaystyle-\frac{2k+1}{n+1}\int_{M_{t}}E_{n-2k}\frac{E_{n}}{E_{n-1}}d\mu

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-\frac{2k+1}{n+1}\int_{M_{t}}E_{n-2k+1}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(2k+1)W_{n-2k+2}(K_{t})-(n+1-2k)W_{n-2k}(K_{t})

(5.18)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle-(2k+1)f_{n-2k+2}\circ f^{-1}_{n-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))-(n+1-2k)W_{n-2k}(K_{t}),

where we used the Newton-MacLaurin inequality, (
3.1
) and the induction assumption (
5.16
). Note that
f_{m}(r)=W_{m}(B_{r})
, we have

\displaystyle f^{\prime}_{m}(r)=\frac{d}{dr}W_{m}(B_{r})=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1-m}{n+1}\int_{\partial B_{r}}E_{m}d\mu

(5.19)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n+1-m}{n+1}\omega_{n}\sinh^{n-m}r\cosh^{m}r,\quad 0\leq m\leq n.

Using (
5
) and (
5
), we get

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left.\frac{f^{\prime}_{n-2k}(r)}{f^{\prime}_{n-1}(r)}\right|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))}\cdot\frac{d}{dt}W_{n-1}(K_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2k+1}{2}\left.\frac{\sinh^{2k-1}r}{\cosh^{2k-1}r}\cdot\left(-\frac{2}{n+1}\omega_{n}\cosh^{n}r\right)\right|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\left.\frac{2k+1}{n+1}\int_{\partial B_{r}}E_{n+1-2k}d\mu\right|_{r=f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-(2k+1)f_{n+2-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))-(n+1-2k)f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t})),

where we also used (
3.1
) in the last equality. Combining this with (
5
), we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left(f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))-W_{n-2k}(K_{t})\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle-(n+1-2k)\left(f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))-W_{n-2k}(K_{t})\right),

and hence

\displaystyle\frac{d}{dt}\left[e^{(n+1-2k)t}\left(f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))-W_{n-2k}(K_{t})\right)\right]\leq 0.

As the flow hypersurface
M_{t}
shrinks to a round point as
t\rightarrow T^{\ast}
, and the quermassintegral is monotone under the set inclusion, we have (see
[
4
, Lemma 3.3]
)

\displaystyle\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{\ast}}W_{m}(K_{t})=0,\quad 0\leq m\leq n.

It follows that

\displaystyle f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K))-W_{n-2k}(K)

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle e^{(n+1-2k)T^{\ast}}\lim_{t\rightarrow T^{\ast}}\left[f_{n-2k}\circ f_{n-1}^{-1}(W_{n-1}(K_{t}))-W_{n-2k}(K_{t})\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0,

which verifies (
5.15
) for
k\geq 2
. The equality case can be similarly characterized. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.5
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.6
.

The quermassintegral inequalities (
1.15
) in Theorem
1.5
and (
5.5
) in Lemma
5.2
for strictly convex domains in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
imply that

\displaystyle\zeta_{l}(K)\leq

\displaystyle\zeta_{n-1}(K),\quad 0\leq l<n-1,

and

\displaystyle\zeta_{k}(K)\leq

\displaystyle\zeta_{n}(K),\quad 0\leq k<n.

In view of (
5.13
) and the monotonicity of
\cosh s
, we get

\displaystyle\coth(\zeta_{l}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{1}(K^{\ast}))\geq\coth(\zeta_{n-1}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{1}(K^{\ast}))=1,\quad 0\leq l<n-1,

and

\displaystyle\coth(\zeta_{k}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{0}(K^{\ast}))\geq\coth(\zeta_{n}(K))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{0}(K^{\ast}))=1,\quad 0\leq k<n,

which are the desired inequalities (
1.17
) and (
1.18
), respectively. If the equality holds in (
1.17
) or (
1.18
), then the equality also holds in (
1.15
) or (
5.5
), which implies that
K
is a geodesic ball in
\mathbb{H}^{n+1}
. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.6
. ∎

Proof of Theorem
1.7
.

For any smooth bounded spacelike domain
K
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
with its principal curvatures satisfy
0<\kappa_{i}\leq 1
, then its dual body
K^{\ast}
is a smooth bounded and h-convex domain in hyperbolic space. In view of duality in hyperbolic/de Sitter space, we have
(K^{\ast})\ast=K
. The quermassintegral inequality (
5.4
) for smooth bounded and h-convex domains in hyperbolic space implies that

\displaystyle\zeta_{l}(K^{\ast})\leq\zeta_{k}(K^{\ast}),\quad 0\leq l<k\leq n.

Then by the monotonicity of
\coth s
, we get

\coth(\zeta_{l}(K^{\ast}))\geq\coth(\zeta_{k}(K^{\ast})),\quad 0\leq l<k\leq n.

In view of the identities (
1.8
), i.e.,

\coth(\zeta_{l}(K^{\ast}))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{n-l}(K))=\coth(\zeta_{k}(K^{\ast}))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{n-k}(K)),\quad 0\leq l<k\leq n,

we obtain

\displaystyle\zeta_{n-l}(K)\leq\zeta_{n-k}(K),\quad 0\leq l<k\leq n,

which is equivalent to (
1.20
). The equality case can be similarly characterized. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.7
.
∎

Proof of Theorem
1.8
.

For any smooth bounded spacelike and strictly convex domain
K
in
\mathbb{S}^{n,1}_{+}
, its dual body
K^{\ast}
is a smooth bounded and strictly convex domain in hyperbolic space. Then by the monotonicity of
\coth s
and the quermassintegral inequalities (
1.15
), we obtain

\displaystyle\coth(\zeta_{n-1}(K^{\ast}))\leq\coth(\zeta_{k}(K^{\ast})),\quad 0\leq k<n-1.

In view of the identities (
1.8
), we have

\displaystyle\coth(\zeta_{n-1}(K^{\ast}))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{1}(K))=\coth(\zeta_{k}(K^{\ast}))\cdot\tanh(\zeta_{n-k}(K)),\quad 0\leq k<n-1.

It follows that

\displaystyle\tanh(\zeta_{1}(K))\geq\tanh(\zeta_{n-k}(K)),\quad 0\leq k<n-1,

which is equivalent to (
1.21
) due to the monotonicity of
\tanh s
.
Applying the quermassintegral inequalities (
5.7
) and (
5.8
) to strictly convex hypersurfaces in hyperbolic space, the inequalities (
1.22
) and (
1.23
) can be proved similarly. Moreover, the equality case in (
1.21
)–(
1.23
) can be similarly characterized. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.8
. ∎
