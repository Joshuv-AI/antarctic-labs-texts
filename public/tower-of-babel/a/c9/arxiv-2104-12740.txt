Bubbles in discrete time models Thanks: We thank Johannes Ruf as well as Paolo Guasoni, the co-editor, and two anonymous referees for helpful comments on previous versions.
Martin Herdegen, Dörte Kreher

Abstract
. We introduce a new definition of bubbles in discrete-time models based on the discounted stock price losing mass at some finite drawdown under an equivalent martingale measure. We provide equivalent probabilistic characterisations of this definition and give examples of discrete-time martingales that are bubbles and those that are not. In the Markovian case, we provide sufficient analytic conditions for the presence of bubbles. We also show that the existence of bubbles is directly linked to the existence of a non-trivial solution to a linear Volterra integral equation of the second kind involving the Markov kernel. Finally, we show that our definition of bubbles in discrete time is consistent with the strict local martingale definition of bubbles in continuous time in the sense that a properly discretised strict local martingale in continuous time is a bubble in discrete time.

Over the last 20 years, a mathematical theory of bubbles for continuous time models has been developed based on the concept of strict local martingales, cf. the seminal papers by Loewenstein and Willard
[
12
]
, Cox and Hobson
[
4
]
, Jarrrow, Protter and Shimbo
[
9
,
10
]
as well as the survey article by Protter
[
17
]
and the references therein. In economic terms, an asset price bubble exists if the fundamental value of the asset deviates from its current price. If the fundamental value is understood to be the expectation of the (discounted) price process
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
under the equivalent local martingale measure
\mathbb{P}
, then the asset
S
has a bubble if
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{T}]<\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{0}]
for some fixed time
T>0
, i.e., if
S
is a strict local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale, that is a local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale that fails to be a
\mathbb{P}
-martingale.

While many implications and extensions of this definition have been discussed in the literature (see e.g. Ekström and Tysk
[
6
]
, Bayraktar, Kardaras and Xing
[
1
]
, Biagini, Föllmer and Nedelcu
[
2
]
, Herdegen and Schweizer
[
7
]
), the strict local martingale definition of bubbles has no direct analogue in discrete time models. The reason for this is that a nonnegative local martingale
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
in discrete time with
S_{0}\in L^{1}
is automatically a (true) martingale. Hence, a definition of bubbles based on strict local martingales is void. Also trying to define a bubble in discrete time as a martingale
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
that is not uniformly integrable does not lead to a meaningful concept as this would imply that virtually all relevant models such as the standard binomial model (considered on an unbounded time horizon) are bubbles, which seems absurd.

Despite the above negative results, the goal of this paper is to introduce a new definition of bubbles for discrete-time models on an unbounded time horizon – keeping the standard assumption that the discounted stock price is a martingale. This definition has to satisfy at least two conditions.

(I)

It should
split
martingales that are not uniformly integrable into two sufficiently rich classes: those that are bubbles and those that are not. In particular, standard discrete-time models with i.i.d. returns like the binomial model should not be bubbles.

(II)

It should be
consistent
with the strict local martingale definition in continuous times in the sense that a properly discretised strict local martingale in continuous time is a bubble in discrete time, and conversely, that a local martingale in continuous time is a strict local martingale if all properly discretised martingales are bubbles in discrete time.

To the best of our knowledge, there has been no attempt in the extant literature to extend the martingale theory of bubbles to discrete time. The only slight exception is Roch
[
18
]
who introduced the notion of asymptotic asset price bubbles using the concept of weakly convergent discrete time models (“large financial market”). More precisely, he showed that even if the price process is a martingale in a sequence of weakly convergent discrete time models, it can have properties similar to a bubble in that the fundamental value in the asymptotic market can be lower than the current price in the asymptotic market. In contrast to
[
18
]
, our approach is non-asymptotic.

To motivate our definition of a bubble in a discrete time model, consider a non-sophisticated investor who follows a simple buy-and-hold strategy to invest into an asset with (discounted) price process
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
. The investor buys the asset at time
k=0
and hopes that it will rise and rise. When the (discounted) asset price drops for the first time, the investor fears to lose money and sells the asset.
Denoting by
\tau_{1}:=\inf\{j>0:\ S_{j}<S_{j-1}\}
the time of the first drawdown of the asset, the fundamental value of
S
at time
0
(viewed with regard to the first drawdown) is
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{\tau_{1}}]
, where
\mathbb{P}
denotes an equivalent martingale measure. As the process
S
is a nonnegative supermartingale, we always have
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{\tau_{1}}]\leq S_{0}
. If
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{\tau_{1}}]<S_{0}
, the fundamental value of the asset (viewed with regard to the first drawdown) is lower than its initial price and hence
S
might be considered a bubble. Indeed, if the market is complete, the predictable representation theorem implies that a sophisticated investor might choose a dynamic trading strategy
\vartheta
whose (discounted) value process
V(\vartheta)=(V_{k}(\vartheta))_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
satisfies
V_{0}(\vartheta)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[S_{\tau_{1}}]<S_{0}
and
V_{\tau}(\vartheta)=S_{\tau}
.

Of course, the requirement that
S
loses mass at the
first
drawdown is somewhat arbitrary and unrealistic. For this reason, our precise definition of a bubble in discrete time is more general and only requires
S
to lose mass at the
k
-th drawdown for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. While this definition is very simple, it leads to a rich theory.

In Section
1
, we provide several equivalent probabilistic characterisations for a nonnegative discrete-time martingale to be a bubble. We also provide necessary and sufficient characterisations for a discrete-time martingale with independent increments to have a bubble. In particular, we show that the standard binomial model does not have a bubble, which implies that Condition (I) above is satisfied.

In Sections
2
, we look at the special case that
S
is a Markov martingale. We provide characterisations for the presence or absence of bubbles, depending on the
probability
of going down,
a(x)=\mathbb{P}_{x}[S_{1}<x]
, and the
relative recovery
when going down,
b(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[\tfrac{S_{1}}{x}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}<x\}}]
. Loosely speaking, it turns out that
S
is a bubble if and only if
b(x)
converges to
0
fast enough as
x\to\infty
. To make this precise, however, is quite involved. While we are able to give sufficient conditions in the general case, we provide necessary and sufficient conditions in the case of complete markets.

In Section
3
, we continue our study of Markov martingales by looking more closely at the underlying Markov kernel. We show that the existence of bubbles for
S
is directly linked to the existence of a non-trivial nonnegative solution to a linear Volterra integral equation of the second kind involving the Markov kernel. Among others, this allows us to give some additional sufficient conditions for the existence of bubbles that cannot be covered with the results from Section
2
.

Finally, in Section
4
, we discuss how our definition of a bubble in discrete time relates to the strict local martingale definition in continuous time. We show that when discretising a positive continuous strict local martingale along sequences of stopping times in a certain somewhat canonical class, one obtains a bubble in discrete time. Conversely, we show that a positive continuous local martingale is a strict local martingale if for all localising sequences in the same class, the corresponding discretised martingales are bubbles. This shows that Condition (II) above is also satisfied.

Hence, by showing that appropriately discretised strict local martingales yield discrete-time bubbles, we provide additional and new support to modelling continuous-time bubbles by strict local martingales.

[

1.
Definition and characterisation of bubbles

In this section, we introduce our definition of a bubble in discrete time and provide equivalent probabilistic characterisations of this concept.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}},\mathbb{P})
be a filtered probability space. A nonnegative
(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{F})
-martingale
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is called a
bubble
if

(1.1)

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau_{k}}\right]<\mathbb{E}\left[S_{0}\right]

for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, where
\tau_{0}:=0
and
\tau_{k}:=\inf\{j>\tau_{k-1}:\ S_{j}<S_{j-1}\},\ k\in\mathbb{N}
, denotes the
k
-th
drawdown
of
S
. We also call
\mathbb{P}
a
bubble measure
for
S
.

Some comments on the above definition are in order.

Remark 1.2
.

(a) We include the possibility that
\mathbb{P}[\tau_{k}=\infty]>0
with positive probability. Since
S
is a nonnegative martingale, it converges
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
to some integrable random variable
S_{\infty}
by Doob’s supermartingale convergence theorem. Hence,
S_{\tau_{k}}
is well defined in any case.

(b) If
S
is a Markov process,
S
will be a bubble if and only if
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{1}}]<\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
, cf. Section
2
below. In general, however, the above definition does not contain any redundancy, thinking for example of dynamics with a change point.

(c) By the stopping theorem for uniformly integrable martingales,
S
can only be a bubble if it is
not
uniformly integrable. Example
1.9
, however, shows that not every martingale that fails to be uniformly integrable is a bubble. This can be compared to the strict local martingale definition in continuous time. Also, it emphasizes that the precise definition of the stopping time
\tau_{k}
in (
1.1
) is important and cannot naively be replaced by an arbitrary stopping time.

(d) Our bubbles are strictly speaking
\mathbb{P}
-bubbles
since the definition depends on the choice of the (equivalent) martingale measure
\mathbb{P}
. In incomplete markets, it is possible to have a
\mathbb{P}
-bubble
under some equivalent martingale measure (EMM)
\mathbb{P}
but not a
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
-bubble
under a different EMM
\tilde{\mathbb{P}}
. In this sense, our definition is not robust with respect to the choice of EMM. Note that exactly the same issue arises in the strict local martingale definition of bubbles in continuous time. Using similar ideas as in
[
7
]
, one could introduce the notion of a
strong bubble
in discrete time. We leave the details of this to future work.

(e) Definition
1.1
can be reformulated in the following way: For some
k\in\mathbb{N}
, the stopped processes
S^{\tau_{k}}
fails to be uniformly integrable. This reformulation allows to apply results from the literature that are proven for general càdlàg (local) martingales. For example, Hulley and Ruf
[
8
]
provide necessary and sufficient characterizations for a càdlàg (local) martingale to be uniformly integrable. However, the conditions given in
[
8
]
require to calculate the distribution of the supremum of
S^{\tau_{k}}
and the distribution of the jumps of
S^{\tau_{k}}
at certain hitting times, both of which are difficult to get hold of. For this reason, they are not very useful in our discrete time setup.

We proceed to give a first simple example of a bubble in a complete market model.

Example 1.3
.

Define the process
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
and the probability measure
\mathbb{P}
recursively by
S_{0}=s_{0}>\tfrac{1}{2}
, and for
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[S_{k}=2S_{k-1}-\frac{1}{2}\;\middle|\;S_{k-1}>\frac{1}{2}\right]={\mathbb{P}}\left[S_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\;\middle|\;S_{k-1}>\frac{1}{2}\right]=\frac{1}{2}

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left[S_{k}=\frac{1}{2}\;\middle|\;S_{k-1}=\frac{1}{2}\right]=1.

Then
S
is a
\mathbb{P}
-martingale for its natural filtration,
\tau_{1}<\infty

\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
, and
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{1}}]=\frac{1}{2}<s_{0}=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
,
i.e.,
S
is a bubble.

The following result provides two equivalent characterisations of bubbles. The first one shows that a nonnegative martingale
S
is a bubble if and only if there exists a deterministic time
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that
S
loses mass at the first drawdown after
k
. The second one provides a limit characterisation. This second characterisation is particularly useful for checking whether or not a martingale
S
is a bubble.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a nonnegative martingale. For
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, define the stopping time
\tilde{\tau}_{k}
by

\tilde{\tau}_{k}:=\inf\{j>k:S_{j}<S_{j-1}\}.

Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

S
is a bubble.

(b)

There exists
k\geq 0
such that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}]<\mathbb{E}[S_{k}]
.

(c)

There exists
k\geq 0
such that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]>0
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b). Suppose that (b) is not true, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{k}]
for all
k\geq 0
. Then for all
k\geq 0
, the stopped process
S^{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}
is a right-closed supermartingale which does not lose mass at
\infty
and hence is uniformly integrable. We proceed by induction to show that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{\ell}}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
for all
\ell\geq 0
, whence (a) is not true. The induction basis
\ell=0
is trivial. For the induction step, suppose that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{\ell-1}}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
for some
\ell\geq 1
. Then using the stopping theorem for the uniformly integrable martingale
S^{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}
in the third equality, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{\ell}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\ell-1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau_{\ell}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell-1}=k\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}[S_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell-1}=\infty\}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\ell-1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell-1}=k\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell-1}=\infty\}}]

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=\ell-1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau_{\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell-1}=k\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{\ell-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\tau_{\ell-1}=\infty\}}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau_{\ell-1}}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}].

(b)
\Rightarrow
(a). This follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}[S_{k}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
by the martingale property of
S
together with the fact that
\tilde{\tau}_{k}\leq\tau_{k+1}
.

(b)
\Leftrightarrow
(c). The equivalence follows from the following calculation, which uses the martingale property of
S
in the third equality:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tilde{\tau}_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{\ell-1}>S_{\ell}\}}\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq\dots\leq S_{\ell-1}\}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq\dots\leq S_{\ell}\}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{\ell=k+1}^{n}\left(\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\ell-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq\dots\leq S_{\ell-1}\}}\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\ell}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq\dots\leq S_{\ell}\}}\right]\right)

\displaystyle\qquad+\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\}}\right]

(1.2)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{k}\right]+\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(S_{\infty}-S_{n})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}].

This finishes the proof.
∎

The characterisation (c) in Theorem
1.4
is generally the most useful one to decide whether or not
S
is a bubble. The following corollary strengthens this characterisation. It shows directly that bounded martingales fail to be bubbles. (Of course, this follows directly from the fact that a bounded martingale is uniformly integrable.)

Corollary 1.5
.

Let
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a nonnegative martingale.
Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

S
is a bubble.

(b)

There exist
x\geq 0
and
k\geq 0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]>0.

(c)

For all
x\geq 0
, there exists
k\geq 0
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]>0.

Proof.

It is clear that (c) implies (b), and (b) a fortiori implies condition (c) of Theorem
1.4
, which by Theorem
1.4
gives (a). So it remains to prove (a)
\Rightarrow
(c).
So fix
x\geq 0
. Since
S
is a bubble, by Theorem
1.4
(b) and the martingale property of
S
, there exists
\ell\geq 0
such that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\tau^{\ell}_{1}}]<\mathbb{E}[S_{\ell}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
.

Define the stopping times
\sigma_{\ell,x}
and
\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}
by

\displaystyle\sigma_{\ell,x}:=\inf\{k\geq\ell:S_{k}\geq x\}\quad\text{and}\quad\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}:=\inf\{j>\sigma_{\ell,x}:S_{j}<S_{j-1}\}.

Then
\sigma_{\ell,x}
is the first hitting time of
[x,\infty)
after
\ell
and
\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}
the first drawdown of
S
after
\sigma_{\ell,x}
. Since
\sigma_{\ell,x}\geq\ell
, it follows that
\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}\geq\tau^{\ell}_{1}
.

By the definition of
\sigma_{\ell,x}
, the stopped process
S^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}
is uniformly integrable because

S^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{k}\leq\max(S_{0},\ldots,S_{\ell},S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}},x)\in L^{1}.

This implies that
\mathbb{E}[S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}}]=\mathbb{E}[S_{0}]
.
Since
\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}\geq\tau_{1}^{\ell}
, this in turn implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}}\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\tau^{\ell}_{1}}\right]<\mathbb{E}\left[S_{0}\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}}\right].

A similar calculation as in (
1.2
) shows that

\mathbb{E}\big[S_{\tau^{\sigma_{\ell,x}}_{1}}\big]=\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}}\right]+\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\Big[(S_{\infty}-S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}+n})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}}\leq\dots\leq S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}+n}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma_{\ell,x}<\infty\}}\Big].

This together with the tower property of conditional expectations and dominated convergence yields

\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}+n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}}\leq S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{\sigma_{\ell,x}+n}\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{\sigma_{\ell,x}}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma_{\ell,x}<\infty\}}\right]>0.

We may deduce that there is
k\geq\ell
such that

\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_{k+n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{k+n}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\sigma_{\ell,x}=k\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right]>0.

Using that
S_{k}\geq x
on
\{\sigma_{\ell,x}=k\}
, this implies that

\mathbb{E}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[(S_{k+n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{k+n}\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\right]>0.

Now dominated convergence and the tower property of conditional expectations give (c).
∎

While characterisations (b) or (c) in Corollary
1.5
are an improvement of Theorem
1.4
(c), they still depend on
S_{\infty}
, of which we generally do not have a good knowledge. The following corollary provides a mild condition on
S
under which the bubble behaviour of
S
can be characterised without involving
S_{\infty}
.

Proposition 1.6
.

Let
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
be a nonnegative martingale and
x>0
. Suppose that

(1.3)

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left[S_{k}<x\text{ or }S_{k}>S_{k+1}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=\infty\;\;\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}

Then for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(1.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[S_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}\right]=0.

In particular,
S
is a bubble if and only if there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]>0.

Proof.

The conditional Lemma of Borel–Cantelli implies that

{\mathbb{P}}\left[\liminf_{k\to\infty}\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\}\right]=1-{\mathbb{P}}\left[\limsup_{k\to\infty}\left(\{S_{k}<x\}\cup\{S_{k}>S_{k+1}\}\right)\right]=0.

This implies a fortiori that
\{x\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\}
is a
\mathbb{P}
-null set for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. This gives (
1.4
). The final claim now follows from Corollary
1.5
.
∎

The following result gives simple necessary and sufficient conditions for a nonnegative martingales with independent increments to be a bubble.

Theorem 1.7
.

Let
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be an independent sequence of nonnegative random variables with
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=1
for
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Define the process
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
S_{k}=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}X_{\ell}
and the filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(S_{0},\ldots,S_{k})
. Moreover, for
k\in\mathbb{N}
set

a_{k}:=\mathbb{P}[X_{k}<1]\in[0,1),\quad b_{k}:=\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}<1\}}\right]\in[0,a_{k}].

Then
S
is a positive
(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{F})
-martingale. It is a bubble if and only if

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty\quad\text{and}\quad\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}<\infty.

Proof.

It is clear by construction that
S
is a nonnegative
(\mathbb{P},\mathbb{F})
-martingale.

First, we argue that if
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}<\infty
, then
S
is uniformly integrable and hence cannot be a bubble. To this end, note that

\infty>\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}[X_{k}<1]\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[(1-\sqrt{X_{k}})\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}<1\}}\big]\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\big[(1-\sqrt{X_{k}})\big].

By Kakutani’s theorem (see, e.g.
[
19
, Theorem 14.12(v)]
) this implies that
S
is uniformly integrable.

Next, if
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty
, by independence of the
X_{k}
,

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left[S_{k}<1\text{ or }S_{k}>S_{k+1}\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]\geq\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{k+1}<1\;\middle|\;\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty.

This together with Corollary
1.5
implies that
S
is a bubble if and only if there exists
k\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{1\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}[S_{k}\geq 1]\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[X_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}\geq 1\}}\right]

\displaystyle=P[S_{k}\geq\varepsilon]\prod_{\ell=k+1}^{\infty}(1-b_{\ell})>0.

Since
\mathbb{P}[S_{k}\geq 1]>0
and
(1-b_{\ell})>0
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
, this is equivalent to
\prod_{\ell=1}^{\infty}(1-b_{\ell})>0
, which in turn is equivalent to
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}<\infty
.
∎

We illustrate the above theorem by two examples. The first one gives bubble in a time-dependent binomial-type model, where the downward jumps get more and more severe.

Example 1.8
.

Let
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of independent random variables with
P[X_{k}=\frac{1}{k}]=\frac{1}{k}
and
P[X_{k}=1+\frac{1}{k}]=1-\frac{1}{k}
. Define the process
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
S_{k}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}X_{\ell}
and the filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
{\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(S_{0},\ldots,S_{k})}
. Then
a_{k}=\frac{1}{k}
and
b_{k}=\frac{1}{k^{2}}
. Hence,
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}a_{k}=\infty
and
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}<\infty
, whence
S
is a bubble.

The second example shows that a martingale with i.i.d. returns is never a bubble. In particular an standard binomial model is never a bubble, which coincides with our intuition.

Example 1.9
.

Let
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables that are nonnegative and satisfy
\mathbb{E}[X_{k}]=1
and
\mathbb{P}[X_{k}\neq 1]>0
. Define the process
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
S_{k}:=\prod_{\ell=1}^{k}X_{\ell}
and the filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
by
{\mathcal{F}_{k}:=\sigma(S_{0},\ldots,S_{k})}
. Then
S
is a martingale but fails to be uniformly integrable because
S_{n}=\exp\left(\sum_{k=1}^{n}\log(X_{k})\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle a.s.}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
by the strong law of large numbers since
\mathbb{E}[\log(X_{k})]<1
. However, setting
b_{k}:=\mathbb{E}[X_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{X_{k}<1\}}]=b>0
, we obtain

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}b_{k}=\infty.

Thus,
S
fails to be a bubble.

2.
Characterization of bubble measures for Markov chains

Throughout this section, we suppose that
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a positive Markov martingale with transition kernel
K:(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{B}_{(0,\infty)}\to[0,\infty)
, starting from
S_{0}=x>0
. Our goal is to determine under which conditions on the kernel
K(x,\,\mathrm{d}y)
the measure
\mathbb{P}_{x}
is a bubble measure. To this end, we define the functions
a,b:(0,\infty)\to[0,1)
by

(2.1)

\displaystyle a(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[S_{1}<x\right]=\int_{[0,x)}K(x,\mathrm{d}y),

(2.2)

\displaystyle b(x)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\frac{S_{1}}{x}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}<x\}}\right]=\int_{[0,x)}\frac{y}{x}K(x,\mathrm{d}y).

Here,
a(x)
denotes the
probability
of a downward jump and
b(x)
the
relative recovery
in case of a downward jump.

First, we show we show that
S
cannot be a bubble unless the relative recovery function
b
converges to zero at infinity.

Proposition 2.1
.

Assume that
\liminf_{x\to\infty}b(x)>0
. Then
S
fails to be a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for any
x\in(0,\infty)
.

Proof.

There exists
x_{0}>0
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that
b(x)\geq\varepsilon
for all
x\geq x_{0}
. Pick
x\in(0,\infty)
. By Corollary
1.5
and using that
S_{\infty}\geq 0
, it suffices to show that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{0}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{n}\}}\right]=0.

So let
k<n
. Then by the Markov property and the definition of
b
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{0}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\frac{S_{n}}{S_{n-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\}}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{n-1}\right]S_{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{0}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{n-1}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[(1-b(S_{n-1}))S_{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{0}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{n-1}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1-\varepsilon)\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n-1}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{0}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{n-1}\}}\right]

(2.3)

\displaystyle\quad\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{n-k}\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{0}\leq S_{k}\}}\big]\leq(1-\varepsilon)^{n-k}x.

Now the claim follows by letting
n\to\infty
.
∎

We proceed to formulate a mild condition on the function
a
, which allows us to characterize the bubble behaviour of
S
without involving
S_{\infty}
.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exists
x_{a}>0
such that
\inf_{x\in[x_{a},y]}a(x)>0
for all
y>x_{a}
.

Remark 2.3
.

Assumption
2.2
is in particular fulfilled if
a
is positive and lower semi-continuous.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose Assumption
2.2
is satisfied for some
x_{a}>0
. Let
x^{\prime}\geq x_{a}
. Then for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

(2.4)

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{x^{\prime}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}\right]=0.

Moreover,
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
if and only if there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x^{\prime}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{n}\}}\right]>0.

Proof.

By Proposition
1.6
, it suffices to check that
\mathbb{P}_{x}\text{-a.s.}

\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[S_{k}<x^{\prime}\text{ or }S_{k}>S_{k+1}\,\middle|\,\mathcal{F}_{k}\right]=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}<x^{\prime}\}}+a(S_{k})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}\}})=\infty.

We now distinguish two cases: If
\omega\in\limsup_{k\to\infty}\{S_{k}<x^{\prime}\}
, it follows that
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}(\omega)<x^{\prime}\}}=\infty
. If
\omega\in\liminf_{k\to\infty}\{S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}\}\cap\{\sup_{k\geq 0}S_{k}<\infty\}
, then
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a(S_{k}(\omega))\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}(\omega)\geq x\}})=\infty
by the assumption on
a
. Since
\sup_{k\geq 0}S_{k}<\infty

\mathbb{P}_{x}\text{-a.s.}
by Doob’s martingale convergence theorem, the claim follows.
∎

We next aim to give a sufficient characterisation for
S
being a bubble. To this end, we need to slightly relax the definition of the function
b
from (
2.2
):
For
\varepsilon>0
, define the function
b_{\varepsilon}:(0,\infty)\to(0,1]
by

b_{\varepsilon}(x):=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\frac{S_{1}}{x}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}<x(1+\varepsilon)\}}\right]=\int_{[0,x(1+\varepsilon))}\frac{y}{x}K(x,\mathrm{d}y).

Theorem 2.5
.

Suppose Assumption
2.2
is satisfied and there exists
\varepsilon>0
and
x_{b}>0
such that the function
b_{\varepsilon}
is nonincreasing for
x\geq x_{b}
and satisfies
\int_{\log(x_{b})}^{\infty}b_{\varepsilon}(\exp(x))\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
. Then
S
is a bubble under each
\mathbb{P}_{x}
, for which
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded.

Proof.

Suppose that
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded. Let
x_{a}
be the constant in Assumption
2.2
. We may assume without loss of generality that
x_{b}\geq x_{a}
. By Proposition
2.4
, it suffices to check that there is
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{b}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{k+n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\geq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x0}\bigg[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{S_{k+j}}{S_{k+j-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k+j}\geq S_{k+j-1}(1+\varepsilon)\}}\bigg]>0.

Since
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded, there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
with
\mathbb{E}_{x}[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}]>0
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the definition of
b_{\varepsilon}
, we obtain for
j\in\{1,\ldots n\}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[\frac{S_{k+j}}{S_{k+j-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{j+k-1}(1+\varepsilon)\leq S_{j+k}\}}\,\bigg|\,\mathcal{F}_{k+j-1}\bigg]=(1-b_{\varepsilon}(S_{k+j-1}))\;\;\mathbb{P}_{x}\text{-a.s.}

This together with the tower property of conditional expectations and the fact that
b_{\varepsilon}
is nonincreasing for
x\geq x_{b}
gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{S_{k+j}}{S_{k+j-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k+j}\geq S_{k+j-1}(1+\varepsilon)\}}\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{S_{k+j}}{S_{k+j-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k+j}\geq S_{k+j-1}(1+\varepsilon)\}}\bigg)(1-b_{\varepsilon}(S_{k+n-1}))\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\bigg(\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}\frac{S_{k+j}}{S_{k+j-1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k+j}\geq S_{k+j-1}(1+\varepsilon)\}}\bigg)(1-b_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}(1+\varepsilon)^{n-1})\bigg]

\displaystyle\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\bigg[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-b_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}(1+\varepsilon)^{j}))\bigg]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\right]\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-b_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}(1+\varepsilon)^{j})).

Thus, it remains to show that
\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-b_{\varepsilon}(x_{b}(1+\varepsilon)^{j}))>0
. The latter condition is equivalent to
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b_{\varepsilon}(\exp(\log(x_{b})+\log(1+\varepsilon)j))<\infty
, which is equivalent to
\int_{\log(x_{b})}^{\infty}b_{\varepsilon}(\exp(x))\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.
∎

We illustrate the above result first by two examples. The first one is a “smooth” version of Example
1.3
; the second one is an example of a “discrete diffusion” for the log price.

Example 2.6
.

Assume that the Markov kernel is given by

K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{(0,1)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y+\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{1}_{(2x-1,2x)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y&\text{if }x>1,\\
\frac{1}{2x}\mathbf{1}_{(0,2x)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y&\text{if }x\leq 1.\end{cases}

Then
a(x)=\frac{1}{2}
and for
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
,

b_{\varepsilon}(x)=\tfrac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}\mathbf{1}_{\{x\leq 1\}}+(1-x(1-\tfrac{(1+\varepsilon)^{2}}{4}))\mathbf{1}_{\{1\leq x\leq\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\}}+\tfrac{1}{4x}\mathbf{1}_{\{x>\tfrac{1}{1-\varepsilon}\}}.

By Theorem
2.5
,
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for all
x>0
.

Example 2.7
.

Let
(Z_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of i.i.d. standard normal random variables and
\sigma:\mathbb{R}\to(0,\infty)
a measurable function such that
\sigma(x)
is nondecreasing for large values of
x
. Define the process
(X_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
recursively by
X_{0}:=0
and

X_{k+1}:=X_{k}+\sigma(X_{k})Z_{k}-\frac{\sigma^{2}(X_{k})}{2},\quad k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Then the process
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
defined by
S_{k}:=\exp(X_{k})
for
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
is a Markov martingale.

Denoting by
\Phi
the cdf of a standard normal random variable, it is not difficult to check that for
x>0
and
\varepsilon\geq 0
,

a(x)=\Phi\left(\frac{\sigma(\log(x))}{2}\right),\qquad b_{\varepsilon}(x)=\Phi\left(\frac{\log(1+\varepsilon)}{\sigma(\log(x))}-\frac{\sigma(\log(x))}{2}\right)

where
b_{0}=b
.
Thus, by Proposition
2.1
, for
S
to be a bubble it is necessary that
\sigma(x)\to\infty
as
x\to\infty
.
Moreover, by Theorem
2.5
, a sufficient condition for
S
to be a bubble is given by

(2.5)

\int^{\infty}_{x_{0}}\Phi\left(\frac{\log(2)}{\sigma(x)}-\frac{\sigma(x)}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty\quad\text{for some }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Denoting the pdf of a standard normal random variable by
\varphi
, using Mill’s ratio and the fact that
|(\frac{\log(2)}{\sigma(x)}-\frac{\sigma(x)}{2})^{2}-(\frac{\sigma(x)}{2})^{2}|\leq(\frac{\log(2)}{\sigma(x)})^{2}+\log(2)
is uniformly bounded for all
x
sufficiently large as
\sigma
is nondecreasing, it is not difficult to check that (
2.5
) is equivalent to

(2.6)

\int^{\infty}_{x_{0}}\frac{1}{\sigma(x)}\varphi\left(-\frac{\sigma(x)}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty\quad\text{for some }x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}.

Remark 2.8
.

It is insightful to compare Example
2.7
to the continuous-time theory of bubbles. To this end,
recall that in continuous time, the process
S=(S_{t})_{t\geq 0}
given by
S_{t}:=\exp(X_{t})
, where
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
solves the SDE

dX_{t}=\sigma(X_{t})dW_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma^{2}(X_{t})dt,

is a strict local martingale and hence a bubble if and only if for some
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,

(2.7)

\int^{\infty}_{x_{0}}\frac{dx}{\sigma^{2}(x)}<\infty,

cf.
[
14
, Corollary 4.3]
. While (
2.6
) and (
2.7
) both say that
S
is a bubble if and only if
\sigma(x)\to\infty
fast enough as
x\to\infty
, the exact rate of increase of
\sigma
required for a bubble is quite different as (
2.7
) is a much stronger requirement than (
2.6
) on the growth of
\sigma
. The reason for this is that the discretisation of the diffusion model in continuous time should not be done along a deterministic time grid, but along certain sequences of stopping times; see Section
4
below.

While Proposition
2.1
and Theorem
2.5
give useful general sufficient conditions for the absence or presence of a bubble, respectively, these conditions are not necessary. In the complete Markov case, we can give a necessary and sufficient characterisation of bubbles under mild assumptions on the functions
a
and
b
.

Theorem 2.9
.

Suppose the Markov kernel is given by

K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=a(x)\delta_{\frac{b(x)x}{a(x)}}(\mathrm{d}y)+(1-a(x))\delta_{\frac{(1-b(x))x}{1-a(x)}}(\mathrm{d}y),

where
0\leq b(x)<a(x)<1
and
0<\liminf_{x\to\infty}a(x)\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}a(x)<1
. Moreover, suppose that there exists
x_{b}>0
such that the function
b
is nonincreasing for
x\geq x_{b}
. Then
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
if and only if
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded and
\int_{\log(x_{b})}^{\infty}b(\exp(x))\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
.

Proof.

Suppose that
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded. We may focus on the case that
\lim_{x\to\infty}b(x)=0
. Indeed, otherwise it follows that
\lim_{x\to\infty}b(x)>0

and hence
\int_{\log(x_{b})}^{\infty}b(\exp(x))\,\mathrm{d}x=\infty
and
S
fails to be a bubble by Proposition
2.1
.

Since
\lim_{x\to\infty}b(x)=0
and
0<\liminf_{x\to\infty}a(x)\leq\limsup_{x\to\infty}a(x)<1
, after potentially enlarging
x_{b}
, we may assume that there exists
0<c<C
such that
1+c\leq\frac{1-b(x)}{1-a(x)}\leq 1+C
for all
x\geq x_{b}
. By Proposition
2.4
,
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
if and only if there exists
k\geq 0
such that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{b}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{k+n}\}}\right]>0.

Using that
\{S_{j}\leq S_{j+1}\}=\{S_{j+1}=\frac{1-b(S_{j})}{1-a(S_{j})}S_{j}\}
for
j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
and arguing as in the proof of Theorem
2.5
gives for each
0\leq k\leq n
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\right]\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-b(x_{b}(1+C)^{j}))\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{x_{b}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S_{k+n}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\geq\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{k}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x_{b}\}}\right]\prod_{j=0}^{n-1}(1-b(x_{b}(1+c)^{j})).

Now using that for
\gamma\in\{c,C\}
,
\prod_{j=0}^{\infty}(1-b(x_{b}(1+\gamma)^{j})>0
if and only if
\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}b(\exp(\log(x_{b})+\log(1+\gamma)j))<\infty
, which in turn is equivalent to
\int_{\log(x_{b})}^{\infty}b(\exp(x))\,\mathrm{d}x<\infty
, the claim follows.
∎

3.
A fixed point equation associated to a Markovian bubble

In this section, we continue our study of Markov martingales, taking a more analytic perspective. We assume throughout that
S=(S_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is a positive Markov martingale with kernel
K:(0,\infty)\times\mathcal{B}_{(0,\infty)}\to[0,\infty)
, starting from
S_{0}=x>0
. The key object of this section is the
default function
of
S
.

Definition 3.1
.

The Borel-measurable function
M_{S}:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
, defined by

(3.1)

M_{S}(x):=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq x\}}\right],

is called the
default function
of
S
.

It follows from (
1.2
) (with
k=0
) that
M_{S}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}[S_{\tau_{1}}]
, so that
M_{S}
measures the loss of mass at the first drawdown of
S
. It is clear that
\mathbb{P}_{x}
is a bubble measure for
S
if
M_{S}(x)>0
. The following two results show that
M
essentially fully characterises the bubble behaviour of
S
under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for all
x>0
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Suppose
M_{S}(x)=0
for all
x\geq x^{\prime}>0
. Then
M_{S}(x)=0
for all
x>0
, and
\mathbb{P}_{x}
fails to be a bubble measure for
S
for any
x>0
.

Proof.

Fix
x>0
. Then for all
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, the Markov property of
S
, the choice of
x^{\prime}
and dominated convergence give

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{x^{\prime}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{k}}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[M_{S}(S_{k})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}\}}\right]=0.

Thus, Corollary
1.5
implies that
S
is not a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
and so
M_{S}(x)=0
.
∎

Proposition 3.3
.

Suppose that
\liminf_{x\to\infty}M_{S}(x)>0
. Then
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for all
x>0
for which
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded.

Proof.

Fix
x>0
and suppose that
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded. By hypothesis, there exists
x^{\prime}\geq x
such that
M_{S}(y)>0
for all
y\geq x^{\prime}
. Since
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded, there is
k\geq 0
such that
\mathbb{P}_{x}[S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}]>0
. Then by the Markov property of
S
, the choice of
x^{\prime}
and dominated convergence,

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{x^{\prime}\leq S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{k}}[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\leq S_{k+1}\leq\dots\leq S_{n}\}}]\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}\}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[M_{S}(S_{k})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{k}\geq x^{\prime}\}}\right]>0.

Thus,
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
by Corollary
1.5
.
∎

In the remainder of this section, we seek to characterise the function
M_{S}
in an analytic way and provide conditions for it to be non-zero.

First, we show that
M_{S}
solves a fixed point equation, more precisely a homogeneous
Volterra integral equation of the second kind
, cf. Brunner
[
3
]
for a textbook treatment.

Lemma 3.4
.

The default function
M_{S}
is a solution to the Volterra integral equation

(3.2)

M_{S}(x)=\int_{[x,\infty)}M_{S}(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y),\quad x>0.

Proof.

Fix
x>0
. Using the definition of
M_{S}
, dominated convergence and the Markov property of
S
, we obtain

\displaystyle\int_{[x,\infty)}M_{S}(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[M_{S}(S_{1})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}\geq x\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{S_{1}}\left[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq x\}}\right]\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq x\}}\right]=M_{S}(x).

∎

Note that (
3.2
) is non-standard in that the domain is non-compact. Therefore, we cannot apply standard existence and uniqueness results for Volterra integral equations, cf.
[
3
, Chapter 8]
. In fact, existence is anyway not an issue since the zero function always solves (
3.2
). Since the bubble case corresponds to (
3.2
) having a non-zero (nonnegative) solution, we are actually interested in
non-uniqueness
, i.e., the case that (
3.2
) has multiple nonnegative solutions. By homogeneity of (
3.2
), we then always have infinitely many solutions and so it is clear that we need an additional condition to pin down the default function
M_{S}
.

It follows from the definition of
M_{S}
, that
M_{S}(x)\leq x
for all
x>0
. So we consider nonnegative solutions to (
3.2
) that are dominated by the identity. To this end, denote by
\mathcal{I}
all Borel-measurable functions
M:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
satisfying
M(x)\leq x
for all
x>0
. Using that

\displaystyle 0\leq\int_{[x,\infty)}M(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\leq\int_{(0,\infty)}M(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\leq\int_{(0,\infty)}yK(x,\mathrm{d}y)=x

for all
M\in\mathcal{I}
and
x>0
, we can define the map

\mathcal{K}:\mathcal{I}\to\mathcal{I}
by

\mathcal{K}(M)(x)=\int_{[x,\infty)}M(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y),\quad x>0.

Then the nonnegative solutions to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity are precisely given by fixed points of
\mathcal{K}
.

While the map
\mathcal{K}
is in general not a contraction (and therefore (
3.2
) may have multiple solutions on
\mathcal{I}
), it is
monotone
, and this property will prove crucial for our subsequent analysis.

Proposition 3.5
.

The map
\mathcal{K}
is monotone on
\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{I}
with
M_{1}\leq M_{2}
. Then monotonicity of the integral gives for
x>0
,

\mathcal{K}(M_{1})(x)=\int_{[x,\infty)}M_{1}(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\leq\int_{[x,\infty)}M_{2}(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\mathcal{K}(M_{2})(x).

∎

Due to monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
, it is very useful to consider
subsolution
and
supersolutions
to (
3.2
) on
\mathcal{I}
.

Definition 3.6
.

A function
M\in\mathcal{I}
is called a
subsolution
to (
3.2
) if

M(x)\leq\int_{[x,\infty)}M(y)K(x,dy),\quad x>0.

It is called a
supersolution
to (
3.2
) if

M(x)\geq\int_{[x,\infty)}M(y)K(x,dy),\quad x>0.

The following result shows that we can construct from each sub- or supersolution a solution to (
3.2
) by Picard iteration. To this end, for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, define
\mathcal{K}^{n}(M)
recursively by
\mathcal{K}^{0}(M):=M
and
\mathcal{K}^{n}(M):=\mathcal{K}(\mathcal{K}^{n-1}(M))
for
n\geq 1
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
M\in\mathcal{I}
be a sub- or supersolution to (
3.2
). Then the limit
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)=\lim_{n\to\infty}K^{n}(M)
exists and is a solution to (
3.2
). Moreover,

•

if
M
is a subsolution, then the sequence
(\mathcal{K}^{n}(M))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is nondecreasing and
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)
is the smallest solution dominating
M
;

•

if
M
is a supersolution, then the sequence
(\mathcal{K}^{n}(M))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is nonincreasing and
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)
is the largest solution dominated by
M
.

Proof.

We only consider the case that
M
is a subsolution; the proof for the case that
M
is a supersolution is analogous. If
M
is a subsolution, the sequence
(\mathcal{K}^{n}(M))_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
is nondecreasing in
M
by monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
. Hence the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}K^{n}(M)
exists and is in
\mathcal{I}
since each
\mathcal{K}^{n}(M)
is in
\mathcal{I}
. Moreover, it follows from monotone convergence that

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathcal{K}^{n}(M)(x)=\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_{[x,\infty)}\mathcal{K}^{n-1}(M)(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\int_{[x,\infty)}\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y),\quad x>0,

whence
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)
is a solution to (
3.2
).

Moreover, let
\tilde{M}\in\mathcal{I}
be any solution to (
3.2
) dominating
M
. It suffices to show that
\tilde{M}\geq\mathcal{K}^{n}(M)
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. We argue by induction. The induction basis is trivial. For the induction step, suppose that
n\geq 1
and
\tilde{M}\geq\mathcal{K}^{n-1}(M)
. Then by the induction hypothesis and the definition of
\mathcal{K}^{n}(M)
, for
x>0
,

\tilde{M}(x)=\int_{[x,\infty)}\tilde{M}(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq\int_{[x,\infty)}\mathcal{K}^{n-1}(M)(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\mathcal{K}^{n}(M)(x).

∎

We note the following important corollary.

Corollary 3.8
.

The largest solution to (
3.2
) on
\mathcal{I}
is given by
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)
, where
\id
denotes the identity function.

It follows from Lemma
3.4
and Corollary
3.8
that the default function
M_{S}
is dominated by
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)
. Under a mild assumption on the kernel
K
, we can assert that
M_{S}
coincides with
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)
. Thus, in this case, we can characterise the default function
M_{s}
as the maximal solution to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity.

Theorem 3.9
.

Suppose that Assumption
2.2
is satisfied for any
x_{a}>0
. Then
M_{S}
is the maximal solution to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity. It is given by
M_{S}=\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)
. Moreover,
M_{S}(x)<x
for all
x>0
and if
M_{S}\neq 0
, then

(3.3)

\limsup_{x\to\infty}\frac{M_{S}(x)}{x}=1.

Proof.

We first show by induction that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathcal{K}^{n}(\id)(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq S_{0}\}}\right],\quad x>0.

The induction basis
n=0
follows from the martingale property of
S
. For the induction step, let
n\geq 1
. By the definition of
\mathcal{K}^{n}(\id)
and the Markov property of
S
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{n}(\id)(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{[x,\infty)}\mathcal{K}^{n-1}(\id)(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathcal{K}^{n-1}(\id)(S_{1})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}\geq x\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[\mathbb{E}_{S_{1}}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\}}\right]\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}\geq x\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq S_{0}\}}\right].

Hence, the definitions of
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)
and
M_{S}
together with (
2.4
) give

\displaystyle\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)(x)

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[S_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq S_{0}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}_{x}\left[(S_{n}-S_{\infty})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{n}\geq S_{n-1}\geq\dots\geq S_{1}\geq S_{0}\}}\right]=M_{S}(x),\quad x>0.

It follows from Corollary
3.8
that
M_{S}
is the maximal solution to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity.

Moreover,
M_{S}\leq\id
, the fact that
\mathbb{P}_{x}[S_{1}<x]=a(x)>0
for all
x>0
and the martingale property of
S
give

\displaystyle M_{S}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[M_{S}(S_{1})\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}\geq x\}}\big]\leq\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[S_{1}\mathbf{1}_{\{S_{1}\geq x\}}\big]<\mathbb{E}_{x}\big[S_{1}\big]=x.

Finally, to establish (
3.3
), denote the function
H_{S}:(0,\infty)\to[0,1]
by
H_{S}(x)=\sup_{y\geq x}\tfrac{M_{S}(x)}{x}
. If
M_{S}\neq 0
, it follows from Proposition
3.2
that
H_{S}(x)>0
for all
x>0
. It suffices to show that
H_{S}(x)=1
for all
x>0
. Seeking a contradiction, suppose there exists
x^{\prime}>0
such that
H_{S}(x^{\prime})<1
. Define the function
M:(0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
by

M(y)=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }x<x^{\prime},\\
\frac{M_{S}(x)}{H_{S}(x^{\prime})}&\text{if }x\geq x^{\prime}.\end{cases}

Then
M\in\mathcal{I}
by the fact that
H_{S}(x^{\prime})\geq\tfrac{M_{S}(x)}{x}
for
x\geq x^{\prime}
. Moreover,
M
is a subsolution to (
3.2
) and
M(x)>M_{S}(x)
for
x\geq x^{\prime}
. This together with Proposition
3.7
implies that
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)
is a solution to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity. Since
\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)(x)\geq M(x)>M_{S}(x)
for
x>x^{\prime}
, this is in contradiction to
M_{S}
being the maximal solution to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity.
∎

The following corollary shows that if we can find a non-trivial subsolution to (
3.2
), then
S
is a bubble.

Corollary 3.10
.

Suppose Assumption
2.2
is satisfied for any
x_{a}>0
. If
M\in\mathcal{I}
is a subsolution to (
3.2
), then
M\leq M_{S}
. If in addition
\liminf_{x\to\infty}M(x)>0
, then
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for all
x>0
for which
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded.

Proof.

Proposition
3.7
and Theorem
3.9
give
M\leq\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(M)\leq M_{S}
. The additional claim then follows from Proposition
3.3
.
∎

A typical candidate for a subsolution in Corollary
3.10
is the call function
M(x)=(x-L)^{+}
for some
L>0
. This is illustrated by the following example. Note that this example cannot be addressed with the results from Section
2
.

Example 3.11
.

Suppose that for all
x>0
,
K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y
, where the density
k
satisfies

k(x,y)=\frac{2}{3(x+1)}\mathbf{1}_{[x,2x]}(y)\quad\text{for }y\geq x>0,

and
k(x,y)
on
\{(x,y):0<y<x\}
is chosen such that
K
is a martingale kernel.
Then the function
a
from (
2.1
) satisfies
a(x)=1-\int_{[x,\infty)}k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y=\tfrac{3+x}{3+3x}\geq\frac{1}{3}
so that Assumption
2.2
is satisfied for any
x_{a}>0
.
Consider
M(x):=(x-3)^{+}
. Then trivially
\int_{x}^{\infty}M(y)k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y\geq 0=M(x)
for
x\leq 3
and

\int_{x}^{\infty}M(y)k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y=x-3\frac{x}{x+1}\geq x-3=M(x)\text{ for }x>3.

It follows that
M
is subsolution to (
3.2
) and we may deduce that
S
has a bubble under
P_{x}
for all
x>0
by Corollary
3.10
since
S
is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded for any
x>0
.

While Theorem
3.9
provides a characterisation of the default function
M
, it does not provide a criterion to decide whether (
3.2
) has a non-trivial, i.e., a non-zero nonnegative solution dominated by the identity. Moreover, it does not provide a criterion to decide whether a given candidate solution
M
to (
3.2
) is indeed maximal. Under a stronger assumption on the kernel
K
, we can provide a sufficient criterion for the existence of non-trivial solutions to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity. Moreover, we obtain a local uniqueness result in this case. To this end, recall the definitions of the functions
a
and
b
from (
2.1
) and (
2.2
), respectively. Moreover, denote by
\|\cdot\|_{\sup}
the supremum norm.

Theorem 3.12
.

Suppose
\inf_{x>0}a(x)>0
. Then the following are equivalent:

(a)

\sup_{x>0}xb(x)<\infty
.

(b)

For all
L>0
sufficiently large, the call function
M(x)=(x-L)^{+}
is a subsolution to (
3.2
).

(c)

The default function
M_{S}
is non-trivial and satisfies
\|\id-M_{S}\|_{\sup}<\infty
.

Moreover, if one of the above conditions is satisfied,
M_{S}
is the unique solution to (
3.2
) among all solutions
M\in\mathcal{I}
satisfying
\|\id-M\|_{\sup}<\infty
.

Proof.

(a)
\Rightarrow
(b). Set
\alpha:=\inf_{x>0}a(x)>0
,
\beta:=\sup_{x>0}xb(x)
and
L\geq\frac{\beta}{\alpha}
. Then the call function
M(x):=(x-L)^{+}
satisfies
\int_{[x,\infty)}M(y)K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\geq 0=M(x)
for
x\leq L
and

\displaystyle\int_{[x,\infty)}M(y)K(x,\,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=x(1-b(x))-L(1-a(x))=M(x)-\beta(x)x+\alpha(x)L

\displaystyle\geq M(x)-\beta+\alpha L\geq M(x),\quad x>L.

Hence,
M
is a subsolution to (
3.2
).

(b)
\Rightarrow
(c). Let
L>0
such that
M(x)=(x-L)^{+}
is a subsolution to (
3.2
). Then
M_{S}\geq M
by Corollary
3.10
, whence
M_{S}
is non-trivial and satisfies
\|\id-M_{S}\|_{\sup}<\|\id-M\|_{\sup}=L
.

(c)
\Rightarrow
(a). Since
\mathcal{K}(\id)\geq\mathcal{K}^{\infty}(\id)=M_{S}
by Proposition
3.7
and Theorem
3.9
, it follows that
\|\id-\mathcal{K}(\id)\|_{\sup}\leq\|\id-M_{S}\|_{\sup}<\infty
. Now the claim follows from the fact that
xb(x)=\int_{(0,x)}yK(x,\mathrm{d}y)=x-\mathcal{K}(\id)(x)=\id(x)-\mathcal{K}(\id)(x)
for
x>0
.

For the additional claim, set
\mathcal{I}_{\sup}:=\{M\in\mathcal{I}:\|\id-M\|_{\sup}<\infty\}
. Then
\mathcal{I}_{\sup}
is a complete metric space for the metric generated by the supremum norm. Moreover,
\mathcal{K}
maps
\mathcal{I}_{\sup}
to itself. Indeed, for each
M\in\mathcal{I}_{\sup}
, by (b), there exists
L\geq\|\id-M\|_{\sup}
such that
\tilde{M}(x):=(x-L)^{+}
is a subsolution to (
3.2
). Hence by monotonicity of
\mathcal{K}
and the fact that
\tilde{M}\leq M
is a subsolution,

\|\id-\mathcal{K}(M)\|_{\sup}\leq\|\id-\mathcal{K}(\tilde{M})\|_{\sup}\leq\|\id-\tilde{M}\|_{\sup}=L<\infty.

Finally, we show that
\mathcal{K}
is a contraction on
\mathcal{I}_{\sup}
. Let
M_{1},M_{2}\in\mathcal{I}_{\sup}
. Then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{K}(M_{1})(x)-\mathcal{K}(M_{2})(x)|

\displaystyle=\bigg|\int_{[x,\infty)}(M_{1}(y)-M_{2}(y))K(x,\mathrm{d}y)\bigg|

\displaystyle\leq\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\sup}\int_{[x,\infty]}K(x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle\leq(1-\alpha)\|M_{1}-M_{2}\|_{\sup}.

Taking the supremum over
x
shows that
\mathcal{K}
is indeed a contraction since
\alpha>0
. Now Banach’s fixed point theorem implies that
\mathcal{K}
contains a unique fixed point, and by (c), this fixed point is
M_{S}
.
∎

We proceed to illustrate Theorem
3.12
by an example.

Example 3.13
.

Suppose that for all
x>0
,
K(x,\mathrm{d}y)=k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y
, where the density satisfies

k(x,y)=\frac{e}{2}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-x}}{1-e^{-y}}\frac{1}{x}e^{-y/x}\quad\text{for }y\geq x>0,

and
k(x,y)
on
\{(x,y):0<y<x\}
is chosen such that
K
is a martingale kernel.
Note that

\int_{x}^{\infty}k(x,y)\,\mathrm{d}y<\frac{e}{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{1}{x}e^{-y/x}\,\mathrm{d}y=\frac{1}{2}.

This implies in particular that
a(x)\geq 1/2
for all
x>0
. In this case, the fixed point equation (
3.2
) is given by

\displaystyle M(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{x}^{\infty}M(y)\cdot\frac{e}{2}\cdot\frac{1-e^{-x}}{1-e^{-y}}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\cdot e^{-y/x}\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\quad\frac{M(x)x}{1-e^{-x}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e}{2}\int_{x}^{\infty}\frac{M(y)}{1-e^{-y}}\cdot e^{-y/x}\,\mathrm{d}y.

As can easily be checked, the function
M_{\lambda}(x):=\lambda x(1-e^{-x})
,
x>0
, is a solution in
\mathcal{I}
to (
3.2
) for any
\lambda\in[0,1]
. As
M_{S}
is the largest solution to (
3.2
) dominated by the identity, this yields the candidate
M_{1}(x)=x(1-e^{-x})
. Since
\|\id-M_{1}\|_{\sup}=e^{-1}<\infty
, it follows from Theorem
3.12
that
M_{S}=M_{1}
.

Combing Theorem
3.12
with Proposition
3.3
, we get the following existence results for bubbles. Note that this result covers cases that cannot be treated with the theory of Section

.

Corollary 3.14
.

Suppose that
\inf_{x>0}a(x)>0
and
\sup_{x>0}xb(x)<\infty
. Then
S
is a bubble under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
for all

for which

is not
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-a.s. bounded.

4.
Relation to the strict local martingale definition of asset price bubbles in continuous time models

In this final section, we discuss how our definition of bubbles in discrete time relates to the strict local martingale definition of bubbles in continuous time. To approach this question, one first has to discretise a positive continuous local martingale
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
in continuous time in such a way that it becomes a discrete time martingale. Of course, there are many ways to do this and we choose a somewhat canonical construction. To wit, we consider localising sequences
(\tau_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of stopping times with
\tau_{n}\to\infty

\mathbb{P}
-a.s. such that for each
n
,
\tau_{n}
and the stopped process
X^{\tau_{n}}
are uniformly bounded. We then define the discrete time process
S=(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by
S_{n}:=X_{\tau_{n}}
. Then
S
is a martingale by the stopping theorem and satisfies
S_{\infty}=X_{\infty}

\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
, which implies that
S
is uniformly integrable if and only if
X
is uniformly integrable.

The simplest way to get localising sequences as above is to choose two increasing sequences of positive real numbers
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to infinity and to define the sequence
(\tau^{a,b}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of stopping times by
\tau^{a,b}_{0}:=0
and

(4.1)

\tau^{a,b}_{n}:=\inf\{t\geq 0:\ X_{t}\geq b_{n}\}\wedge a_{n},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

Then
(\tau^{a,b}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a localizing sequence of stopping times for
X
with
\tau^{a,b}_{n}\leq a_{n}
and
\sup_{t\geq 0}X^{\tau^{a,b}_{n}}_{t}\leq b_{n}
.

In the special case that
X
is a Markov process, we would like to stop in such a way that the discrete time process
S
is again a Markov process. In this case, the simplest way to get localising sequences as above is to choose two constants
\alpha,\beta>0
and to define the sequence of stopping times
(\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by
\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{0}:=0
and

(4.2)

\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n}:=\inf\left\{t\geq\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n-1}:\ X_{t}\geq(1+\beta)X_{\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n-1}}\right\}\wedge\left(\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n-1}+\alpha\right),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

In this case it is still true that
(\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is a localizing sequence of stopping times for
X
and
\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n}
and
X^{\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n}}
are uniformly bounded.

Our first goal in this section is to show that if
X
is a continuous positive strict local martingale, then the discrete time process
S
is a bubble for either choice of stopping times.

The proof of this result relies on the following deep characterisation of strict local martingales in continuous time; cf.
[
13
,
5
,
11
]
. Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a positive càdlàg local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale with
X_{0}=x
. Then under some technical assumptions on the probability space and the underlying filtration, there exists a probability measure
\mathbb{Q}
with
\mathbb{Q}|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\gg\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
for all
t\geq 0
such that
Y:=1/X
is a nonnegative true
\mathbb{Q}
-martingale, and for all bounded stopping times
\tau
and
A\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
,

\mathbb{P}[A]=x\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[Y_{\tau}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right].

Especially, we have the identity

\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}[X_{0}-X_{t}]=x\cdot\mathbb{Q}[Y_{t}=0],\quad t\geq 0,

i.e.,
X
is a strict local martingale on
[0,t]
if and only if
\mathbb{Q}[Y_{t}=0]>0
.

With this, we have the following two results.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous positive strict local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale. Let
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be increasing sequences of positive real numbers converging to
\infty
. Define the sequence of stopping times
(\tau^{a,b}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by (
4.1
) and set
S^{a,b}_{n}=X_{\tau^{a,b}_{n}}
for
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
. Then the measure
\mathbb{P}
is a bubble measure for the discrete time martingale
S^{a,b}=(S^{a,b}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Proposition 4.2
.

Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous-time positive strict local Markov martingale under the measure
\mathbb{P}_{x}
. Let
\alpha,\beta>0
and define the sequence of stopping times
(\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
by (
4.2
) and set

We proceed to illustrate Proposition
4.2

Example 4.3
.

Let
(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be the three-dimensional inverse Bessel process, i.e.,
X
is the unique strong solution to the SDE
\,\mathrm{d}X_{t}=-X_{t}^{2}\mathrm{d}B_{t}
, where
B=(B_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a
\mathbb{P}_{x}
-Brownian motion. The process
Y:=1/X
is
\mathbb{Q}_{1/x}
-Brownian motion, stopped when it reaches zero.

Fix
\alpha,\beta>0
. We proceed to calculate the Markov kernel
K(x,\,\mathrm{d}y)
for
S^{\alpha,\beta}
under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
. Denote by
W
a standard Brownian motion starting at zero and by
\Phi
the cdf of standard normal random variable. Using the reflection principle of Brownian motion and denoting the running supremum and the running infimum of a process
Z
by
\overline{Z}
and
\underline{Z}
, respectively, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[S^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}=(1+\beta)x\right]=\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}}=(1+\beta)x\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=x\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{1/x}\left[Y_{\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}}=\frac{1}{(1+\beta)x}\}}\right]=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\mathbb{Q}_{1/x}\left[\underline{Y}_{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{(1+\beta)x}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\frac{1}{1+\beta}\mathbb{Q}\left[\overline{W}_{\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{(1+\beta)x}\right]=\frac{2}{1+\beta}\Phi\left(-\frac{\beta}{(1+\beta)x\sqrt{\alpha}}\right).

Moreover, for
z\in(0,(1+\beta)x)
, using the joint density of Brownian motion and its running supremum, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[S^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}\leq z\right]=\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{\tau^{\alpha,\beta}_{1}}\leq z\right]=\mathbb{P}_{x}\left[X_{\alpha}\leq z,\overline{X}_{\alpha}<(1+\beta)x\right]

\displaystyle=x\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}_{1/x}\left[Y_{\alpha}\mathbf{1}_{\{Y_{\alpha}\geq\frac{1}{z}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\underline{Y}_{\alpha}>\frac{1}{(1+\beta)x}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=x\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\left[\left(\frac{1}{x}-W_{\alpha}\right)\mathbf{1}_{\{W_{\alpha}\leq\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{z}\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{\overline{W}_{\alpha}<\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{(1+\beta)x}\}}\right]

\displaystyle=x\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{z}}\left(\frac{1}{x}-y\right)\int_{0}^{\frac{\beta}{(1+\beta)x}}\frac{2(2u-y)}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha^{3}}}\exp\left(-\frac{(2u-y)^{2}}{2\alpha}\right)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=x\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{z}}\left(\frac{1}{x}-y\right)\int_{\frac{-y}{\sqrt{\alpha}}}^{\frac{\frac{2\beta}{(1+\beta)x}-y}{\sqrt{\alpha}}}\frac{v}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}}\exp\left(-\frac{v^{2}}{2}\right)\,\mathrm{d}v\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=x\int_{-\infty}^{\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{z}}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}}\left(\frac{1}{x}-y\right)\bigg(\exp\left(-\frac{y^{2}}{2\alpha}\right)-\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\frac{2\beta}{(1+\beta)x}-y)^{2}}{2\alpha}\bigg)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}y

\displaystyle=x\int_{0}^{z}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}w^{3}}\bigg(\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\frac{1}{x}-\frac{1}{w})^{2}}{2\alpha}\bigg)-\exp\bigg(-\frac{(\frac{2\beta}{(1+\beta)x}-\frac{1}{x}+\frac{1}{w})^{2}}{2\alpha}\bigg)\bigg)\,\mathrm{d}w

Thus, the Markov kernel
K(x,\mathrm{d}y)
for
S^{\alpha,\beta}
under
\mathbb{P}_{x}
is given by

\displaystyle K(x,\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{1+\beta}\Phi\left(-\frac{\beta}{(1+\beta)x\sqrt{\alpha}}\right)\delta_{(1+\beta)x}(\mathrm{d}y)

\displaystyle+\frac{x}{\sqrt{2\pi\alpha}y^{3}}\bigg(e^{-\frac{(\frac{1}{y}-\frac{1}{x})^{2}}{2\alpha}}-e^{-\frac{(\frac{1}{y}+\frac{\beta-1}{(1+\beta)x})^{2}}{2\alpha}}\bigg)\mathbf{1}_{(0,(1+\beta)x)}(y)\,\mathrm{d}y.

This ends the example.

We finish this section by providing a converse to Proposition
4.1
.

Theorem 4.4
.

Let
X=(X_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a continuous positive local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale that converges to zero
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
Then
X
is a strict local
\mathbb{P}
-martingale if and only if for all sequences
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and
b=(b_{n})_{n\mathbb{N}}
converging to infinity, the measure
\mathbb{P}
is a bubble measure for the discrete time martingale
S^{a,b}=(S^{a,b}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}
.

Proof.

First, assume that
X
is a strict local martingale. The the result follows from Proposition
4.1

Conversely, suppose that
X
is a true
\mathbb{P}
-martingale. As in the proof of Proposition
4.1
, we may assume without loss of generality that
X
is the canonical process on
C([0,\infty),(0,\infty])
with
X_{0}=1
. Then there exists a measure
\mathbb{Q}
on
C([0,\infty),(0,\infty])
, with
\mathbb{Q}|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}\gg\mathbb{P}|_{\mathcal{F}_{t}}
for all
t\geq 0
, such that
Y:=1/X
is a positive
\mathbb{Q}
-martingale that converges to
0

\mathbb{Q}
-almost surely and
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[X_{\tau}\mathbf{1}_{A}\right]=\mathbb{Q}[A]
for each bounded stopping time
\tau<\tau_{\infty}
and
A\in\mathcal{F}_{\tau}
.
By Proposition
A.1
, there exists an increasing sequence
a=(a_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to infinity such that
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

(4.3)

\mathbb{Q}[Y_{a_{k}}\geq Y_{a_{k+1}}\geq\cdots]=0.

Since
Y
is positive
\mathbb{Q}\text{-a.s.}
, we can find an increasing sequence
b=(b_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to infinity such that
\mathbb{Q}[\underline{Y}_{a_{n}}\leq 1/b_{n}]<2^{-n}
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
. By the Borel–Cantelli lemma, this implies that

(4.4)

\mathbb{Q}\left[\underline{Y}_{a_{k}}\leq\frac{1}{b_{k}}\text{ for infinitely many }k\right]=0.

Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, using (
4.3
) and (
4.4
),and recalling that each
\tau^{a,b}_{n}
is bounded by
a_{n}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{P}}\left[S^{a,b}_{n}\mathbf{1}_{\{S^{a,b}_{k}\leq S^{a,b}_{k+1}\leq\cdots\leq S^{a,b}_{n}\}}\right]=\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{Q}\left[Y_{\tau^{a,b}_{n}}\leq Y_{\tau^{a,b}_{n-1}}\leq\cdots\leq Y_{\tau^{a,b}_{k}}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad=\mathbb{Q}\left[Y_{\tau^{a,b}_{k}}\geq Y_{\tau^{a,b}_{k+1}}\geq\cdots\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\mathbb{Q}\left[\underline{Y}_{a_{\ell}}\leq\frac{1}{b_{\ell}}\text{ for infinitely many }\ell\right]+\mathbb{Q}\left[Y_{a_{\ell}}\leq Y_{a_{\ell-1}}\text{ eventually}\right]=0.

By Theorem
1.4
, this shows that
\mathbb{P}
is not a bubble measure for
S^{a,b}
.
∎

Appendix A
Auxiliary results

Proposition A.1
.

Let
(M_{t})_{t\geq 0}
be a positive continuous local martingale that
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
never becomes constant, i.e.
\mathbb{P}(M_{t}=M_{\infty}\text{ for all }t\geq s)=0
for all
s\geq 0
. Then there exists an increasing sequence of nonnegative real numbers
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{k\to\infty}a_{k}=\infty
such that for each
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

P[M_{a_{k}}\geq M_{a_{k+1}}\geq\cdots]=0.

Proof.

For
n,m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, set
D^{n}_{m}:=\{n+j2^{-m}:j\in\mathbb{N}_{0}\}
. Then for each fixed
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,
D^{n}_{m}
is increasing in
m
. Set
D^{n}_{\infty}:=\bigcup_{m\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}D^{n}_{m}
, which is dense in
[n,\infty)
. Since
M
is a continuous local martingale that
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
never becomes constant, its paths
\mathbb{P}\text{-a.s.}
never become monotone. By continuity of the paths of
M
, this implies that for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}[M_{s}\geq M_{t}\text{ for all }s,t\in D^{n}_{\infty}\text{ with }s<t]=0.

By
\sigma
-continuity of
\mathbb{P}
, for each
n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
, there exists
m_{n}\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
such that

\mathbb{P}[M_{s}\geq M_{t}\text{ for all }s,t\in D^{n}_{m_{n}}\text{ with }s<t]\leq 2^{-n}.

We may assume without loss of generality that the sequence
(m_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
is nondecreasing. Define the set
D
by

D:=\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\left(D^{n}_{m_{m}}\cap[n,n+1)\right).

Then
D\cap[n,\infty)\supset D^{n^{\prime}}_{m_{n^{\prime}}}
for all
n^{\prime}\geq n
. This implies that
for each
k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}
,

\mathbb{P}\left[M_{s}\geq M_{t}\text{ for all }t,s\in D\cap[n,\infty)\text{ with }s<t\right]\leq\lim_{n^{\prime}\to\infty}2^{-n^{\prime}}=0.

If
(a_{k})_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
is an enumeration of
D
in increasing order, the result follows.
∎

Remark A.2
