With ChatGPT, do we have to rewrite our learning objectives - CASE study in Cybersecurity
Peter Jamieson, Suman Bhunia, Dhananjai M. Rao

Abstract
With the emergence of Artificial Intelligent chatbot tools such as ChatGPT and code writing AI tools such as GitHub Copilot, educators need to question what and how we should teach our courses and curricula in the future. In reality, automated tools may result in certain academic fields being deeply reduced in the number of employable people. In this work, we make a case study of cybersecurity undergrad education by using the lens of “Understanding by Design” (UbD). First, we provide a broad understanding of learning objectives (LOs) in cybersecurity from a computer science perspective. Next, we dig a little deeper into a curriculum with an undergraduate emphasis on cybersecurity and examine the major courses and their LOs for our cybersecurity program at Miami University. With these details, we perform a thought experiment on how attainable the LOs are with the above-described tools, asking the key question “what needs to be enduring concepts?” learned in this process. If an LO becomes something that the existence of automation tools might be able to do, we then ask “what level is attainable for the LO that is not a simple query to the tools?”. With this exercise, we hope to establish an example of how to prompt ChatGPT to accelerate students in their achievements of LOs given the existence of these new AI tools, and our goal is to push all of us to leverage and teach these tools as powerful allies in our quest to improve human existence and knowledge. Our general hypothesis is that the LOs of early first- and second-year courses will need to remain as they are in the revised Bloom’s Taxonomy verbiage, but the rate at which we can go deeper in the LO can be aided with ChatGPT tools. Additionally, in our use of UbD to create courses, we believe the exercise will need to be done at all levels of the undergraduate curriculum such that from the LOs and the respective levels we can then devise valuable activities and assessments that will help students develop into valuable thinkers as they navigate their future careers. We will argue that similar to mathematics where the 3 steps in mathematics (1. Devise 2. Compute 3. Evaluate Correctness of Result) that just like computers have severely reduced the needs of step 2, the same model can be thought of for computing education where ideas and skills such as syntax and algorithmic implementation might be shifted more to the AI-based assistive tools, and the learner/user needs to be able to create the design (analogous to step 1 in mathematics) and evaluate (analogous to step 2 in mathematics) the results produced by the tools.



I
Introduction

Technological advancements can have an influence on how we approach education, and the emergence of Artificially Intelligent (AI) chatbot tools such as ChatGPT will result in the next wave of debates and questions on how the existence of these tools will change our educational approaches. ChatGPT is a natural language chatbot tool (where a chatbot is an autonomous robot that will respond to user queries) that has been trained on large language models such that the tool can respond to questions and generate user-requested solutions. For example, you can ask ChatGPT to write a story in the voice of your favorite author and define a simple plot and characters. ChatGPT will create that story, and in many cases, the story will be reasonably written. Additionally, ChatGPT and other tools such as Github’s Copilot provide users with the ability to help them design and code in a variety of programming and design languages to build various user-requested functionality. For example, we have used ChatGPT to design Verilog HDL modules of a Radix-2 SRT divider with reasonable success (noting that the tool is not perfect and expertise is needed for a well-formed design).

These later capabilities have caused those of us who sit in the computing education space into debates about how the existence of these tools changes what and how we teach. In this work, we propose to approach this debate from the perspective of student learning outcomes (LOs) as defined using verbiage from Bloom’s Taxonomy
[
1
]
. With LOs for a particular curriculum (our example case will focus on cybersecurity), we can pose the simple question of how these LOs might change, and what additional LOs should be added to the curriculum. To aid in this case study, we also will guide our discussion with the help of the useful concept filter described by Wiggins and McTyghe in “Understanding by Design” to classify the importance of LOs. By approaching a curriculum and the courses it comprises with these perspectives, our goal is to demonstrate how we might approach integrating AI tools into our curricula and education.

For this case study, we use existing cybersecurity organizational schemes to extract the “enduring understanding” concepts. This organizational scheme will then look at some of the LOs asking if the LO can be “accelerated” given the available AI tools. Next, we will look at our existing cybersecurity curricula with courses and look at each LO mapping and where it sits in Bloom’s Taxonomy. This will allow us to see what are the LOs for cybersecurity education and ask the question, “Should they be leveled up?” We will provide ChatGPT example usages on how students can be taught to use the tool to deepen their understanding of a knowledge unit. These examples show that much of what we do as teachers may be offset to ChatGPT given that they learn to use the tool.

A
Learning Outcomes
(LO) is an educational goal for a learner such that they will be able to cognitively perform the objective once they have learned it. Typically, an LO is described by a cognitive process taken from Bloom’s Taxonomy
[
1
]
and applying the process to some content as related to the field of study. Bloom’s taxonomy provides a hierarchy of cognitive processes as defined from “lower-order thinking skills”, such as recall and classification, to “higher-order thinking skills” such as creating or planning. The “higher-order” thinking skills tend to be what society wants of our workers, but these higher-order skills are much more challenging to develop and take significantly more time than the lower-order thinking skills to teach and learn because of the profession nature of learning. In this work, we are asking this question in terms of accelerating an LO to a higher Bloom’s level, but we are aware of this pitfall, and will hopefully provide insight into how the new technology modifies the space.

IV

As noted in the last section’s analysis, the demands of remembering, understanding, and applying new knowledge and skills are overwhelming for the cybersecurity curriculum. This section provides a few examples of how an LO can be linked with ChatGPT to accelerate and potentially raise the skill and knowledge to a higher Bloom’s taxonomy level. As stated earlier, the goal is not to simply move up in terms of Bloom’s Taxonomy levels as this is not a feasible approach to learning and becoming an effective practitioner. Instead, our goal is to provide examples of how ChatGPT can be used to extend what the learner achieves in terms of the knowledge unit. In each example, we will show the knowledge unit in the title, show the prompt and response and then discuss how this allows the learner to push their knowledge further.

What linux commands can help a sys admin to evaluate their network security?

Here are some Linux commands that can help a system administrator evaluate their network security:

1.

nmap: nmap is a powerful network scanner that can be used to identify open ports, running services, and detect vulnerabilities in a network. It can be used to scan specific hosts or entire networks.

2.

netstat: netstat is a command-line tool used for monitoring network connections. It can show open network sockets, their associated processes, and network interface statistics.

3.

tcpdump: tcpdump is a packet analyzer that can capture and display network traffic in real-time. It can be used to inspect packets for potential security issues and can also be used to identify suspicious network activity.

4.

5.

fail2ban: fail2ban is a log analysis and intrusion prevention tool. It can be used to monitor log files for suspicious activity and automatically block IP addresses that show signs of malicious activity.

6.

chkrootkit: chkrootkit is a tool that can be used to scan for rootkits on a Linux system. It checks for common signs of a rootkit infection, such as hidden files and processes.

7.

lynis: lynis is a security auditing tool that can be used to scan Linux systems for security vulnerabilities. It can perform a variety of checks, including system and file permissions, firewall configuration, and malware scanning.

These commands can be combined with other security tools and practices to help ensure the security of a Linux network. However, it’s important to note that evaluating network security is a complex and ongoing process that requires a comprehensive approach.

V
Conclusion

In this work, we looked at our existing cybersecurity curriculum as a case study of how well AI-driven natural language processing tools such as ChatGPT change the LOs of a curriculum. To do this we used the lense of LOs and their respective Bloom’s taxonomy verbiage and mapped out our cybersecurity curriculum. We noticed that there are a large number of LOs in Cybersecurity and most of those LOs sit in the “low-order” thinking skills. Based on this, we hypothesize that ChatGPT will allow us to accelerate some of our existing LOs, given the tool’s capabilities. To show this we provide five examples of how an existing LO can be accelerated with ChatGPT, given the capabilities of the tool.

From this exercise, we have learned two things in particular that we believe we will need to be further examined by all educators. First, our experiences with ChatGPT suggest that the tool can provide a powerful means to allow learners to generate pieces of their work quickly. However, learners will need to be taught how to create prompts, and how to validate the responses and designs provided by the tool, and just like using the browser to find things, the learner needs to be aware of the vocabulary used to get acceptable responses. Second, we will need to consider how to teach concepts that need to be experienced from “first-principle” learning approaches and learn how to motivate students to perform some rudimentary exercises that “the tool” can easily do for me. We will argue, that some basic experiments need to be done that provide us with evidence of why certain concepts still need to be done to help us prove to our students the importance of early learning exercises.

The experiment we suggest going forward is to use the cybersecurity concept inventory for a control class that is taught various cyber concepts in a traditional method and compares a group that is taught similar ideas but is also prompted to use ChatGPT to explore the ideas more deeply. This depth could be facilitated by designing assignments that ask for more detailed challenges within the cybersecurity space.
