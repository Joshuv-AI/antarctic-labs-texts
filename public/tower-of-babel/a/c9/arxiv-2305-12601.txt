Abstract
. We consider the following decision problem: given two simply typed λ \lambda -terms, are they β \beta -convertible? Equivalently, do they have the same normal form? It is famously non-elementary, but the precise complexity – namely Tower -complete – is lesser known. One goal of this short paper is to popularize this fact. Our original contribution is to show that the problem stays Tower -complete when the two input terms belong to Blum and Ong’s safe λ \lambda -calculus, a fragment of the simply typed λ \lambda -calculus arising from the study of higher-order recursion schemes. Previously, the best known lower bound for this safe β \beta -convertibility problem was PSpace -hardness. Our proof proceeds by reduction from the star-free expression equivalence problem, taking inspiration from the author’s work with Pradic on “implicit automata in typed λ \lambda -calculi” . These results also hold for β ​ η \beta\eta -convertibility.

1.3.
Some pedantic points

Traditionally, the simply typed

-convertible.
1
1

1

A Church-style simply typed
\lambda

2
2

2

2.1.
A simple
Tower
normalization algorithm using the degree

Let us first define the
type of a redex

\big(\lambda x^{A}.\,t\big)\,u
in a Church-style simply typed
\lambda
-term as
A\to B
where
B
is the type of
t
(so
A\to B
is also the type of
\lambda x^{A}.\,t
). The
degree of a redex
is the degree of its type.
The idea of using redex degrees to get a weak normalization proof for the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus goes back at least to Turing, see
[
BS23
, Section 1]
for an explanation and historical discussion. Here, we use a variation on this idea based on the notion of

, which comes from the Tait/Martin-Löf proof of confluence for untyped
\beta
-reduction.

{defiC}

[
[
Tak95
, p. 121]
]
The parallel reduction

of a
\lambda
-term

is, inductively:

x^{\star}=x\qquad(\lambda x.\,t)^{\star}=\lambda x.\,t^{\star}\qquad(t\,u)^{\star}=\begin{cases}(t^{\prime})^{\star}\{x:=u^{\star}\}&\text{when}\ t=\lambda x.\,t^{\prime}\ \text{for some}\ t^{\prime}\\
t^{\star}\,u^{\star}&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}

This definition on untyped
\lambda
-terms can be extended to Church-style simply typed
\lambda
-terms in the obvious way.

It is immediate that
t\longrightarrow_{\beta}^{*}t^{\star}
. Furthermore:

{propC}
[
[
AL13
, Appendix]
]
Let
t
be a simply typed
\lambda
-term whose redexes have degree at most
d\in\mathbb{N}
. Then:

•

The redexes of
t^{\star}
have degree at most
d-1
.

•

As a consequence, the normal form of
t
can be reached in
d
parallel reductions. In other words, let
t^{\star 0}=t
and
t^{\star(n+1)}=(t^{\star n})^{\star}
; then
t^{\star d}
is the normal form of
t
.

This gives us a simple algorithm to compute the normal form of a simply typed
\lambda
-term. In fact, since parallel reduction does not inspect types, we may also apply it to simply
typable
terms, to solve the Curry-style version of the
\beta
-convertibility problem (cf. §
1.3
).

We consider the following measures on an untyped
\lambda
-term
t
to get a complexity bound:

•

\mathrm{height}(t)
denotes the height of its syntax tree;

•

its
size

|t|
is the number of nodes of its syntax tree, including the leaves (variables):

|x|=1\qquad|\lambda x.\,t|=1+|t|\qquad|t\,u|=1+|t|+|u|

Fact 4
.

For any untyped
\lambda
-term
t
, we have
\mathrm{height}(t^{\star})\leqslant|t|\leqslant 2^{\mathrm{height}(t)}
.

(We have not managed to find this statement in the literature, but it is probably not new.)

Proof 2.1
.

The first inequality can be proved by structural induction on Definition
2.1
, using

\mathrm{height}((t^{\prime})^{\star}\{x:=u^{\star}\})\leqslant\mathrm{height}((t^{\prime})^{\star})+\mathrm{height}(u^{\star})

while the second inequality is due to the fact that each node in the syntax tree of a
\lambda
-term has at most two children.

As a consequence,
|t^{\star}|\leqslant 2^{|t|}
for any
\lambda
-term
t
. Putting all this together, we get:

Theorem 5
.

There is an algorithm that takes as input a simply typable
\lambda
-term
t
and returns its normal form in time at most
2_{d}^{P(|t|)}
, where
d
is the maximum redex degree in some typing of
t
(not given as input) and

is some polynomial independent from

.

Proof 2.2
.

The algorithm iterates parallel reductions until it reaches a normal form. This takes at most
d

, computing

can be done by feeding the input
t^{\star(n-1)}
– which has size at most
2_{n-1}^{|t|}

FExpTime
procedure.

Given two simply typable
\lambda
-terms, we can compute their normal forms and compare them to decide whether they are
\beta
-convertible. Together with the considerations from §
1.3

“membership in
Tower
”
part of Theorem
1
. (Again, we do not make any claim to originality concerning the material in this section; it has been included for expository purposes.)

Finer bounds with the order

Actually, a

Remark 6
.

The paper
[

]

]

For any simple type

\deg(\underbrace{o\to\dots\to o}_{n\ \text{times}}\to o)=n\qquad\mathrm{ord}(\underbrace{o\to\dots\to o}_{n\ \text{times}}\to o)=1

While using the degree seems to be the easier way to devise a normalization algorithm together with a proof of a

•

;

•

in fact,

•

the

3.1.
Homogeneity and long-safety

Before proceeding with the proof of Theorem
7
, let us explain the additional restrictions – beyond the safety condition recalled in Section
1.2
– imposed on the input terms. We chose to prove hardness in presence of these restrictions because they are natural in the context of higher-order recursion schemes (cf. §
3.5.2
).

First,
homogeneity
is a simple syntactic criterion on types.

{defi}

A simple type
A_{1}\to\dots\to A_{n}\to o
(for
n\geq 0
) is homogeneous when each of the
A_{i}
is itself homogeneous (
i\in\{1,\dots,n\}
) and
\mathrm{ord}(A_{1})\geq\dots\geq\mathrm{ord}(A_{n})
.

The second restriction is
long-safety
. In Church style, it is the same as the notion of safety in §
1.2
except more subterms of a simply typed
\lambda
-term are considered to be in
“unsafe position”
: if
u
is not a
\lambda
-abstraction, then it is in unsafe position in
t=C[\lambda x.\,u]
. Equivalently, in Curry style:

{defiC}
[
[
BO09
, Def. 1.21]
]
The typing rules of the long-safe
\lambda
-calculus are

\frac{}{x:A\vdash x:A}\qquad\frac{\Theta\vdash t:A}{\Theta^{\prime}\vdash t:A}\ (\Theta\subset\Theta^{\prime})

\frac{\Theta\vdash t:A_{1}\to\ldots\to A_{n}\to B\quad\Theta\vdash u_{1}:A_{1}\;\ldots\;\Theta\vdash u_{n}:A_{n}}{\Theta\vdash t\,u_{1}\,\ldots\,u_{n}:B}\ \text{when}\ \mathrm{ord}(B)\leqslant\mathrm{inford}(\Theta)

\frac{\Theta,\,x_{1}:A_{1},\,\ldots,\,x_{n}:A_{n}\vdash t:B}{\Theta\vdash\lambda x_{1}\dots x_{n}.\,t:A_{1}\to\dots\to A_{n}\to B}\ \text{when}\ \mathrm{ord}(A_{1}\to\dots\to A_{n}\to B)\leqslant\mathrm{inford}(\Theta)

\text{where}\ \mathrm{inford}(\Theta)=\displaystyle\;\inf_{\mathclap{(y:C)\in\Theta}}\;\mathrm{ord}(C)\ \text{with the usual convention}\ \inf(\varnothing)=+\infty.

For the rest of Section
3
, we only work with Curry-style typed
\lambda
-terms. This is because it allows our constructions to be carried out in polynomial time. If we had to build Church-style terms, writing out the type annotations would take exponential time, as explained in §

(though this is harmless in a
Tower
-hardness proof; the point is just to have a more precise statement).

{defi}

For a
\lambda
-term
t

A
and a context

\Gamma\vdash_{\mathsf{hls}}t:A
when there is a derivation
using only homogeneous types

in the above type system. In that case we say that

.

3.2.
Star-free expressions

As we said in the introduction, our

{defi}

A
star-free expression
over a finite alphabet
\Sigma
is a regular expression without the Kleene star, but with complementation, defining a language
\llbracket E\rrbracket\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
:

E,E^{\prime}\mathrel{::=}\varnothing\mid\overbrace{\varepsilon}^{\mathclap{\text{empty string}}}\mid\underbrace{a}_{\mathclap{\text{letter}\ a\in\Sigma}}\mid E\cup E^{\prime}\mid\overbrace{E\cdot E^{\prime}}^{\mathclap{\text{concatenation}}}\mid\underbrace{\lnot E}_{\mathclap{\text{complement}}}

The
star-free equivalence problem
consists in deciding whether two star-free expressions (over the same alphabet) denote the same language: given
E
and
E^{\prime}
, does
\llbracket E\rrbracket=\llbracket E^{\prime}\rrbracket
hold?

For instance, over the alphabet
\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}
, we have the equivalence

\llbracket a\cdot\lnot\varnothing\cup b\cdot\lnot\varnothing\rrbracket=\llbracket\lnot(\varepsilon\cup c\cdot\lnot\varnothing)\rrbracket=\{w\in\Sigma^{*}\mid\text{$w$ starts with an $a$ or a $b$}\}

Schmitz’s paper introducing
Tower
presents star-free equivalence as a typical example of a
Tower
-complete problem
[
Sch16
, §3.1]
(rephrasing a hardness result proved by Meyer and Stockmeyer, cf.
[
Sto74
, §4.2]
). Furthermore, equivalence reduces to emptiness: given two expressions
E
and
F
, the language denoted by
\lnot(\lnot E\cup F)\cup\lnot(E\cup\lnot F)
is empty if and only if
E
and
F
are equivalent. And to test whether
\llbracket E\rrbracket=\varnothing
, it is well known that it suffices to examine words of length at most
\mathrm{tower}(|E|-1)
:

Proposition 8

(cf. e.g.
[
Sto74
, proof of Prop. 4.25]
)
.

If a star-free expression of size
n+1
denotes a non-empty language
L
, then
L
contains a word of length at most
\mathrm{tower}(n)
.

(Further remarks on the above proposition and on the complexity of star-free equivalence are given at the end in Section
3.5.1
.) Altogether, this discussion leads us to conclude that:

Lemma 9
.

The following problem is
Tower
-hard:

•

Input:
a star-free expression
E
and a natural number
n
given in unary.

•

Output:
whether or not
\llbracket E\rrbracket
contains a word of length at most
\mathrm{tower}(n)
.

3.3.
From star-free expressions to safe
\lambda
-terms

We shall represent (star-free) languages as functions from strings to booleans. We already have a coding of booleans, that is involved in the statement of Theorem
7
. For strings, we use the standard
Church encodings
.

{defiC}

[
[
BB85

]
The type
\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}=\overbrace{(o\to o)\to\dots\to(o\to o)}^{|\Sigma|\ \text{times}}\to o\to o

The Church encoding of a word

(resp. of a number

For any finite alphabet
\Sigma

•

\quad\mathtt{and}=\lambda b_{1}b_{2}xy.\;b_{1}\;(b_{2}\;x\;y)\;y\qquad\mathtt{or}=\lambda b_{1}b_{2}xy.\;b_{1}\;x\;(b_{2}\;x\;y)\qquad\mathtt{not}=\lambda bxy.\;b\;y\;x

\vdash_{\mathsf{hls}}\mathtt{not}:\mathtt{Bool}\to\mathtt{Bool}

) with

\mathtt{cat}=\lambda s_{1}s_{2}f_{1}\dots f_{|\Sigma|}x.\;s_{1}\;f_{1}\;\dots\;f_{|\Sigma|}\;(s_{2}\;f_{1}\;\dots\;f_{|\Sigma|}\;x)

\lambda

The next step in our proof is the following lemma, which is reused in Section

Lemma 11

.

Let
\Sigma=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{|\Sigma|}\}

Explanation: computing

\varepsilon\in L(w_{0}\#\dots\#w_{n})
.

Proof 3.2

(Proof of Lemma
10
)
.

By induction on the expression, leading to a recursive algorithm. We also want the algorithm to return

on the input

O(|E|)
.

•

We take
A_{\varnothing}=o
and
t_{\varnothing}=\lambda s.\;\mathtt{false}:\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}\to\mathtt{Bool}
.

•

In the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus, testing for the empty word can be
done with type
\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}\to\mathtt{Bool}
, but this is not possible with safe
\lambda
-terms
as discussed in
[

, Section 2]
. We use instead
A_{\varepsilon}=\mathtt{Bool}
and

t_{\varepsilon}=\lambda s.\;s\;(\lambda x.\;\mathtt{false})\;\dots\;(\lambda x.\;\mathtt{false})\;\mathtt{true}

•

To test whether the word consists of a single letter, say, the first one in
the alphabet
\Sigma

\lambda

\lambda sxy.\;s\;(\overbrace{\lambda qz_{0}z_{1}z_{2}.\;q\;z_{1}\;z_{2}\;z_{2}}^{\mathclap{\hskip 16.38895pt\hskip 16.38895pt\text{represents the transition}\ \delta(c_{1},q_{0})=q_{1},\;\delta(c_{1},q_{1})=\delta(c_{1},q_{2})=q_{2}}})\;\underbrace{(\lambda q.\;q_{2})\;\dots\;(\lambda q.\;q_{2})}_{|\Sigma|-1\ \text{times}}\;q_{0}\;\overbrace{y\;x\;y}^{\mathclap{\hskip 16.38895pt\text{accepting states}\;=\;\{q_{1}\}}}\quad\text{where}\ \underbrace{q_{i}=\lambda z_{0}z_{1}z_{2}.\;z_{i}}_{\mathclap{\text{the 3 states of the DFA (of type $A_{c_{1}}$)}\hskip 16.38895pt\hskip 16.38895pt}}

•

Complementation is trivially implemented by post-composing with
\mathtt{not}
.

•

To handle a union

, we first check whether
\mathrm{ord}(A_{E})\geq\mathrm{ord}(A_{F})
. If that is the case, we take
A_{E\cup F}=\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}[A_{E}]\to\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}[A_{F}]\to\mathtt{Bool}
and

t_{E\cup F}=\lambda s.\;s\;\underbrace{\dots\;(\lambda kxy.\;k\;(\mathtt{cat}\;x\;\overline{c_{i}})\;(\mathtt{cat}\;y\;\overline{c_{i}}))\;\dots}_{\text{for each $c_{i}\in\Sigma$}}\;(\lambda xy.\;\mathtt{or}\;(t_{E}\;x)\;(t_{F}\;y))\;{\overline{\varepsilon}}\;{\overline{\varepsilon}}

with

,

and

in the typing derivation; the

at the end must be given
the different types
\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}[A_{E}]
and

respectively.

This can be seen as a continuation-passing-style transformation of the following procedure using pair types (which we do not have here): perform a
“left fold”
over the input string with an accumulator of type
\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}[A_{E}]\times\mathtt{Str}_{\Sigma}[A_{F}]

.

When

– this is true in

.

First, we introduce a new letter

As in the case of unions in the proof of Lemma

\displaystyle X_{p}\colon\mathbb{N}

\displaystyle\to\Sigma^{*}

3.5.
Final remarks

To conclude this paper, we discuss some topics related to Theorem
7
and its proof.

3.5.1.
On the complexity of star-free expression equivalence

The equivalence problem for usual regular expressions, with Kleene star but no complementation, is
“merely”

PSpace
-complete
[
Sto74
, Theorem 4.13]
– see also
[
HK11
, Theorem 15]
. One could therefore think that the main source of complexity in the case of star-free expressions is
complementation
.

This can be witnessed in the proof idea for Proposition
8
. Translate
E
to an equivalent nondeterministic finite automaton (NFA), whose number of states bounds the length of a shortest word in the language (since the shortest accepting runs visit each state at most once). This can be done by induction on
E
, using any standard construction on NFA for union and concatenation; the costliest operation is complementation, which uses determinization, inducing a single exponential state blowup.

But in our translation from star-free expressions to
\lambda
-terms (Lemma
10
), it turns out that complementation is trivial while concatenation increases the order of the types. Relatedly, in
deterministic
finite automata, complementation can be done without changing the set of states, while concatenation may need an exponential blowup of the number of states. So the complexity of the problem is in fact rooted in the
alternation
between complementation and concatenation – which leads to the
“dot-depth hierarchy”

[
Pin17
]
.

3.5.2.
Safety in higher-order recursion schemes

Blum and Ong’s main motivation for introducing the safe
\lambda
-calculus
[
BO09
]
was unrelated to complexity: they wanted to transpose to the
\lambda
-calculus the notion of safety on higher-order recursion schemes (HORS)
[
KNU02
]
– and indeed, the correspondence between HORS and
\lambda\mathbf{Y}
-terms (i.e. terms in the simply typed
\lambda
-calculus
extended with fixed-point combinators
\mathbf{Y}_{A}:(A\to A)\to A
) relates safe HORS with safe
\lambda\mathbf{Y}
-terms
[
