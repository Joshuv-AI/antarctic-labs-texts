A new approach to the S-functional calculus
El Hassan Benabdi and Mohamed Barraa

Abstract
. In this paper, we first prove that the S-spectrum of a bounded right quaternionic linear operator on a two-sided quaternionic Banach space is a union of the spectrum of some bounded linear operators on a complex Banach space. Furthermore, we show that the S-functional calculus is obtained by the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus for complex linear operators. We also give simple proofs of some already existing results.

47A10; 47A60; 46S10; 47S10

1.
Introduction

Let
X
be a complex Banach space, we denote by
\mathcal{B}(X)
the set of all bounded linear operators on
X
. For
T\in\mathcal{B}(X)
,
\rho(T)
,
\sigma(T)
and
r(T)
shall denote its resolvent set, its spectrum set and its spectral radius, respectively. Let
U
be an open set in the plane and let
\mathcal{H}(U)
be the set of all complex valued functions defined and holomorphic on
U
. Suppose that
D
is a bounded Cauchy domain such that
\sigma(T)\subset D\subset\overline{D}\subset U
, the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus for
T
is defined for any function
f\in\mathcal{H}(U)
by

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi\mathrm{i}}\int_{\partial D}f(\lambda)(\lambda I-T)^{-1}d\lambda,

where
\partial D
is the (oriented) boundary of
D
. Let
f,g\in\mathcal{H}(U)
, it is well-known that

•

\sigma(f(T))=f(\sigma(T))
;

•

(fg)(T)=f(T)g(T)=g(T)f(T)
;

•

let
V
be an open set containing
f(U)
and
h\in\mathcal{H}(V)
, then
(h\circ f)(T)=h(f(T))
;

•

if
(f_{n})
converges to
f
uniformly on compact subsets of
U
, then
f(T)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}(T)
.

The aim of this paper is to show that almost every results of quaternionic spectral theory can be obtained by the classical spectral theory.

The article is organized as follows. In Section 2 we introduce the set of quaternions, slice hyperholomorphic functions, two-sided quaternionic Banach space and S-spectrum as needed for the development of this article. In Section 3 we give an equivalent definition of the S-spectrum and we give alternative proof of some already existing results. In Section 4 we show that the S-functional calculus can be obtained by the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus for complex linear operators. We also show that most of the properties that hold for the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus can be extended to the S-functional calculus.

2.
Preliminary results

We denote by
\mathbb{H}
the algebra of quaternions, introduced by Hamilton in 1843. An element
q
of
\mathbb{H}
is of the form

q=a+b\mathrm{i}+c\mathrm{j}+d\mathrm{k};\;a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{R}

where
\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j}
and
\mathrm{k}
are imaginary units. By definition, they satisfy

\mathrm{i}^{2}=\mathrm{j}^{2}=\mathrm{k}^{2}=\mathrm{i}\mathrm{j}\mathrm{k}=-1.

Given
q=a+b\mathrm{i}+c\mathrm{j}+d\mathrm{k}
, then the conjugate quaternion, the norm, the real and the imaginary parts of
q
are respectively defined by
\bar{q}=a-b\mathrm{i}-c\mathrm{j}-d\mathrm{k}
,
|q|=\sqrt{q\bar{q}}=\sqrt{a^{2}+b^{2}+c^{2}+d^{2}}
,
\text{Re}(q):=\frac{1}{2}(q+\bar{q})=a
and
\text{Im}(q):=\frac{1}{2}(q-\bar{q})=b\mathrm{i}+c\mathrm{j}+d\mathrm{k}
.

The unit sphere of imaginary quaternions is given by

\mathbb{S}=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:q^{2}=-1\}.

Let
p
and
q
be two quaternions.
p
and
q
are said to be conjugated, if there is
s\in\mathbb{H}\setminus\{0\}
such that
p=sqs^{-1}
. The set of all quaternions conjugated with
q
, is equal to the 2-sphere

[q]=\{\text{Re}(q)+|\text{Im}(q)|j:j\in\mathbb{S}\}=\text{Re}(q)+|\text{Im}(q)|\mathbb{S}.

For every
j\in\mathbb{S}
, denote by
\mathbb{C}_{j}
the real subalgebra of
\mathbb{H}
generated by
j
; that is,

\mathbb{C}_{j}:=\{\alpha+\beta j\in\mathbb{H}:\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}\}.

We say that
U\subseteq\mathbb{H}
is axially symmetric if
[q]\subset U
for every
q\in U
.

For a thorough treatment of the algebra of quaternions
\mathbb{H}
, the reader is referred, for instance, to
[
3
]
.

Definition 2.1

(
[
1

.

Let

be an axially symmetric open set and let
\mathcal{U}=\{(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\alpha+\beta\mathbb{S}\subset U\}
.

A function

and if

(2.1)

f_{0}(\alpha,-\beta)=f_{0}(\alpha,\beta),\;\;\;\;\;\;f_{1}(\alpha,-\beta)=-f_{1}(\alpha,\beta).

If in addition

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha}f_{0}(\alpha,\beta)-\frac{\partial}{\partial\beta}f_{1}(\alpha,\beta)=0,\\

is called left slice hyperholomorphic. We denote the set of all left slice hyperholomorphic functions on

A function

such that:

f(q)=f_{0}(\alpha,\beta)+f_{1}(\alpha,\beta)i\;\;\;\text{ for every }q=\alpha+\beta i\in U

and if

satisfy the compatibility conditions
(\ref{5eq6})
. If in addition

and

satisfy
the Cauchy-Riemann equations

is called right slice hyperholomorphic. We denote the set of all right slice hyperholomorphic functions on

.

If

is a left (or right) slice hyperholomorphic function such that

and

are real-valued, then

is called intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic function. The set of all intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions on

will be denoted by
\mathcal{N}(U)
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
(X,+)
be an abelian group.

•

X
is a right quaternionic vector space denoted by
X_{R}
if it is endowed with a right quaternionic multiplication
(X,\mathbb{H})\rightarrow X
,
(x,q)\mapsto xq
such that for all

x(p+q)=xp+xq,\;\;(x+y)q=xq+yq,\;\;(xp)q=x(pq)\;\text{ and }\;x1=x.

•

X
is a left quaternionic vector space denoted by
X_{L}
if it is endowed with a left quaternionic multiplication
(\mathbb{H},X)\rightarrow X
,
(q,x)\mapsto qx
such that for all
x,y\in X
and all

(p+q)x=px+qx,\;\;q(x+y)=qx+qy,\;\;q(px)=(qp)x\;\text{ and }\;1x=x.

•

X
is a two-sided quaternionic vector space if it is endowed with a left and a right quaternionic multiplication such that
X
is both a left and a right quaternionic vector space and such that
rx=xr
for all
r\in\mathbb{R}
, and
(px)q=p(xq)
for all

and all

.

Definition 2.5
.

Let
X_{R}
be a right quaternionic vector space. A function
\|\cdot\|:X_{R}\rightarrow[0;+\infty)
is called a norm on
X_{R}
, if it satisfies

(i)

\|x\|=0
if and only if
x=0
;

(ii)

\|xq\|=\|x\||q|
for all
x\in X_{R}
and all
q\in\mathbb{H}
;

(iii)

\|x+y\|\leq\|x\|+\|y\|
for all
x,y\in X_{R}
.

If
X_{R}
is complete with respect to the metric induced by
\|\cdot\|
, we call

a right quaternionic Banach space.

Let
X_{L}
be a left quaternionic vector space. A function
\|\cdot\|:X_{L}\rightarrow[0;+\infty)
is called a norm on
X_{L}
, if it satisfies
(\mathrm{i})
,
(\mathrm{iii})
and

(ii’)

\|qx\|=|q|\|x\|
for all
x\in X_{L}
and all
q\in\mathbb{H}
.

If
X_{L}
is complete with respect to the metric induced by
\|\cdot\|
, we call
X_{L}
a left quaternionic Banach space.

Finally, a two-sided quaternionic vector space
X
is called a two-sided quaternionic Banach space if it is endowed with a norm
\|\cdot\|
such that it is both left and right quaternionic Banach space.

Remark 2.6
.

If
X
is a two-sided quaternionic Banach space, then
\|qx\|=\|xq\|=|q|\|x\|
for all
x\in X
and all
q\in\mathbb{H}
.

Definition 2.7
.

Let
X
be a two-sided quaternionic Banach space and let
\mathbb{K}\in\{\mathbb{R},\mathbb{H},\mathbb{C}_{i}:i\in\mathbb{S}\}
. A
\mathbb{K}
-right linear operator on
X
is a map
T:X\rightarrow X
such that:

T(xq+y)=(Tx)q+Ty\;\;\text{ for all }\;\;x,y\in X\;\;\text{ and all }\;\;q\in\mathbb{K}.

A
\mathbb{K}
-right linear operator
T
on
X
is called bounded if

\|T\|:=\sup\{\|Tx\|:x\in X,\|x\|=1\}<\infty.

We say that
T
is a right linear operator if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{H}
and we say that
T
is a linear operator if
\mathbb{K}=\mathbb{C}_{i}
for some
i\in\mathbb{S}
.

The set of all right linear bounded operators on
X
is denoted by
\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. By
\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
we denote the set of all
\mathbb{R}
-right linear bounded operators on
X
. For
T\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
, let
\mathcal{N}(T)
denote the null space of
T
, and let
\mathcal{R}(T)
denote the range of
T
.

In a two-sided quaternionic Banach space
X
, we can define a left and a right quaternionic multiplication on
\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
by

\displaystyle(Tq)x=T(qx)\text{ and }(qT)(x)=q(Tx)\text{ for all }q\in\mathbb{H},\;x\in X\text{ and all }T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X).

So
\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
is a two-sided quaternionic Banach space equipped with the metric
\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)\times\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)\ni(A,B)\mapsto\|A-B\|
.

Definition 2.8
.

Let
X
be a two-sided quaternionic Banach space and let
T\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
. For
q\in\mathbb{H}
, we set

Q_{q}(T):=T^{2}-2\text{Re}(q)T+|q|^{2}I,

where
I
is the identity operator on
X
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. We define the spherical resolvent set
\rho_{S}(T)
of
T
as

\rho_{S}(T):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:Q_{q}(T)\text{ is invertible in }\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)\},

and we define the spherical spectrum
\sigma_{S}(T)
of
T
as

\sigma_{S}(T):=\mathbb{H}\setminus\rho_{S}(T).

The spherical approximate point spectrum of
T
is defined by

\sigma_{aS}(T):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\text{there is a sequence }\{x_{n}\}\text{ of unit vectors in }X\text{ such that }\|Q_{q}(T)x_{n}\|\rightarrow 0\}.

The surjectivity spherical spectrum of
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
is defined by

\sigma_{sS}(T):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{R}(Q_{q}(T))\neq X\}.

The spherical spectrum
\sigma_{S}(T)
decomposes into three disjoint subsets as follows:

(i)

The spherical point spectrum of
T
:

\sigma_{pS}(T):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{N}(Q_{q}(T))\neq\{0\}\}.

(ii)

The spherical residual spectrum of
T
:

\sigma_{rS}(T):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{N}(Q_{q}(T))=\{0\}\text{ and }\overline{\mathcal{R}(Q_{q}(T))}\neq X\}.

(iii)

The spherical continuous spectrum of
T
:

\sigma_{cS}(T):=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{N}(Q_{q}(T))=\{0\},\;\overline{\mathcal{R}(Q_{q}(T))}=X\text{ and }\mathcal{R}(Q_{q}(T))\neq X\}.

Let
p
and
q
be two quaternions such that
p\in[q]
, then
\text{Re}(p)=\text{Re}(q)
and
|p|=|q|
. Hence for any
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
,
Q_{p}(T)=Q_{q}(T)
. It follows that

Proposition 2.9
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. The sets
\rho_{S}(T)
,
\sigma_{S}(T)
,
\sigma_{aS}(T)
,
\sigma_{sS}(T)
,
\sigma_{pS}(T)
,
\sigma_{rS}(T)
and

are axially symmetric.

3.
Quaternionic spectrum

In the following
X
will be a fixed two-sided quaternionic Banach space. For convenience, we define the mapping:

\begin{array}[]{cccc}R_{q}:&X&\rightarrow&X\\
&x&\mapsto&xq.\end{array}

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
and let
q\in\mathbb{H}
. Define
\Delta_{q}(T):=R_{q}-T
. If no confusion can arise, then we write simply
\Delta_{q}
instead of
\Delta_{q}(T)
. Note that
\Delta_{q}\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
and let
q\in\mathbb{H}
. Then
\Delta_{q}
is injective (resp. surjective, bijective, has closed range, has dense range) if and only if
\Delta_{\bar{q}}
is.

Proof.

Since
\bar{q}\in[q]
, there exists
j\in\mathbb{S}
such that
qj=j\bar{q}
. Then for all
x\in X
,
(\Delta_{q}x)j=\Delta_{\bar{q}}(xj)
, and the result follows immediately.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
and let
q\in\mathbb{H}
. Then

Q_{q}(T)=\Delta_{q}\Delta_{\bar{q}}=\Delta_{\bar{q}}\Delta_{q}.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
. Then

(3.1)

\displaystyle\Delta_{q}\Delta_{\bar{q}}x=(\Delta_{\bar{q}}x)q-T\Delta_{\bar{q}}x=(x\bar{q}-Tx)q-T(x\bar{q}-Tx)=T^{2}x-(\bar{q}+q)Tx+q\bar{q}x=Q_{q}(T)x.

Hence
Q_{q}(T)=\Delta_{q}\Delta_{\bar{q}}
.

Since
Q_{\bar{q}}(T)=Q_{q}(T)
, the remaining equality
Q_{q}(T)=\Delta_{\bar{q}}\Delta_{q}
will be proved by interchanging
q
and
\bar{q}
in (
3.1
).
∎

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}^{\mathbb{R}}(X)
and let
q\in\mathbb{H}
. Then
Q_{q}(T)
is injective (resp. surjective, bijective, has dense range) if and only if
\Delta_{q}
is.

Proof.

By Lemmas
3.1
and
3.2
, it is clear that
Q_{q}(T)
is injective (resp. surjective, bijective, has dense range) if and only if
\Delta_{q}
is.
∎

The following proposition is an easy consequence of Lemmas
3.2
and
3.3
.

Proposition 3.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. Then

(i)

\sigma_{S}(T)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\Delta_{q}\text{ is not bijective}\};

(ii)

\sigma_{pS}(T)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{q})\neq\{0\}\};

(iii)

\sigma_{rS}(T)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{q})=\{0\}\text{ and }\overline{\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{q})}\neq X\};

(iv)

\sigma_{cS}(T)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{N}(\Delta_{q})=\{0\},\;\overline{\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{q})}=X\text{ and }\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{q})\neq X\};

(v)

\sigma_{aS}(T)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\text{there is a sequence }\{x_{n}\}\text{ of unit vectors in }X\text{ such that }\|\Delta_{q}x_{n}\|\rightarrow 0\};

(vi)

\sigma_{sS}(T)=\{q\in\mathbb{H}:\mathcal{R}(\Delta_{q})\neq X\}.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. If
Tx=xq
for some
q\in\mathbb{H}
and
x\in X\setminus\{0\}
, then
x
is called right eigenvector of
T
with right eigenvalue
q
. The assertion (ii) in the above proposition is well-known (see, for instance,
[
1
, Proposition 3.1.9]
or
[
3

).

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
i
be a fixed element of
\mathbb{S}
. Identify
\mathbb{C}_{i}
with
\mathbb{C}
in the natural way, then
X
is a complex Banach space with respect to the structure induced by

: its sum is the sum of
X
, its complex scalar multiplication is the right scalar multiplication of
X
restricted to
\mathbb{C}_{i}
. For short, we denote this space by

and

the linear operator on

defined by

.

Note that for all
q\in\mathbb{C}_{i}
and all

(T_{i}q)x=T_{i}(x)q
. We also use the notation

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
q\in\mathbb{C}_{i}
then
\Delta_{q}=I_{i}q-T_{i}
. Moreover, if
\Delta_{q}
is bijective, then
\Delta_{q}^{-1}
is the resolvent of
T_{i}
at

.

Proof.

Let
x\in X
, then
(I_{i}q-T_{i})x=(I_{i}q)x-T_{i}x=xq-Tx=\Delta_{q}x
. Hence
\Delta_{q}=I_{i}q-T_{i}
. If
\Delta_{q}
is bijective, then so is

. Thus
\Delta_{q}^{-1}=(I_{i}q-T_{i})^{-1}
is the resolvent of
T_{i}
at

.
∎

Theorem 3.6
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
q\in\mathbb{H}
with
\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|T^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}<|q|
. Then

Q_{q}(T)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}\bar{q}^{\;-n+k-1}\big)T^{n},

where this series converges in the operator norm.

Proof.

Note that for all
p\in[q]
,
Q_{p}(T)=Q_{q}(T)
. Then we may assume that
q\in\mathbb{C}_{i}
. Since

\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|T_{i}^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}=\limsup_{n\rightarrow+\infty}\|T^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}<|q|,

I_{i}q-T_{i}=\Delta_{q}
and
I_{i}\bar{q}-T_{i}=\Delta_{\bar{q}}
are invertible in
\mathcal{B}(X_{i})
. Moreover,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\Delta_{q}^{-1}=(I_{i}q-T_{i})^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T_{i}^{n}q^{-n-1}\text{ and }\Delta_{\bar{q}}^{-1}=(I_{i}\bar{q}-T_{i})^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T_{i}^{n}\bar{q}^{-n-1}.

Then

Q_{q}(T)^{-1}=\Delta_{q}^{-1}\Delta_{\bar{q}}^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T_{i}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}.

Since
\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}=\overline{\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}}
,
\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}\in\mathbb{R}
. Hence

T_{i}^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}=T^{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}\;\;\text{ for all }\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus

Q_{q}(T)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}\bar{q}^{\;-n+k-1}\big)T^{n},

this series converges in the operator norm, because so are the series in (
3.2
). This completes the proof.
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Let
q\in\mathbb{H}
and let
a_{n}:=\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}{\bar{q}}^{\;-n+k-1}

, we can write

Q_{q}(T)^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T^{n}\big(\sum_{k=0}^{n}q^{-k-1}\bar{q}^{\;-n+k-1}\big).

Proposition 3.8
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. Then

\sigma_{S}(T)=[\sigma(T_{i})].

Proof.

By Proposition
3.4
,
\sigma(T_{i})\subseteq\sigma_{S}(T)
. Since

∎

Theorem 3.9

(Compactness of the S-spectrum)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. The S-spectrum
\sigma_{S}(T)
of
T
is a nonempty compact set contained in the closed ball
\{q\in\mathbb{H}:|q|\leq\|T\|\}
.

Proof.

It is well-known that
\sigma(T_{i})
is a nonempty compact subset of
\mathbb{C}_{i}
. Hence
[\sigma(T_{i})]
is compact in
\mathbb{H}
. Then by Proposition
3.8
,
\sigma_{S}(T)
is a nonempty compact set. That
\sigma_{S}(T)
contained in the closed ball
\{q\in\mathbb{H}:|q|\leq\|T\|\}
follows from Theorem
3.6
.
∎

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. Then the S-spectral radius of
T
is defined to be the nonnegative real number

r_{S}(T):=\sup\{|q|:q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}.

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. Then

r_{S}(T)=r(T_{i}).

In particular,

r_{S}(T)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|T^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.8
, we have
\sigma_{S}(T)=[\sigma(T_{i})]
. Clearly if
p,q\in\mathbb{H}
are conjugate, then
|p|=|q|
. Hence
\{|q|:q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}=\{|q|:q\in\sigma(T_{i})\}
. Thus

r_{S}(T):=\sup\{|q|:q\in\sigma(T_{i})\}.

And so

r_{S}(T)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|T_{i}^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\|T^{n}\|^{\frac{1}{n}}.

∎

Let
P(q)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}q^{i}
be a polynomial with coefficients
\alpha_{i}\in\mathbb{R}
. For
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
we set
P(T)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\alpha_{i}T^{i}
. The S-spectrum of
T
and
P(T)
and related in the following way:

Theorem 3.11

(The spectral mapping theorem for polynomials)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
P
be a polynomial with real coefficients. Then

\sigma_{S}(P(T))=P(\sigma_{S}(T)):=\{P(q):q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}.

Proof.

We have
\sigma(P(T_{i}))=P(\sigma(T_{i}))
. Then by Proposition
3.8
,
\sigma_{S}(P(T))=[P(\sigma(T_{i}))]=P([\sigma(T_{i})])=P(\sigma_{S}(T)).

∎

Remark 3.12
.

Note that Theorem
3.11
would be fail if the polynomial coefficients are not real. To see this, consider the polynomial
P(q)=\mathrm{i}q
and
I
the identity operator on
X
. Then
\sigma_{S}(P(I))=\sigma_{S}(\mathrm{i}I)=\mathbb{S}
and
P(\sigma_{S}(I))=\{\mathrm{i}\}
.

4.
S-functional calculus

In this section we will see that the S-functional calculus can be obtained by the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus for complex linear operators.

Definition 4.1

(
[
1
, Definition 2.1.30]
, Slice Cauchy domain)
.

An axially symmetric open set
D\subset\mathbb{H}
is called a slice Cauchy domain if
D\cap\mathbb{C}_{i}
is a Cauchy domain in
\mathbb{C}_{i}
for every
i\in\mathbb{S}
. More precisely,
D
is a slice Cauchy domain if for every
i\in\mathbb{S}
the boundary
\partial(D\cap\mathbb{C}_{i})
of
D\cap\mathbb{C}_{i}
is the union of a finite number of nonintersecting piecewise continuously differentiable Jordan curves in
\mathbb{C}_{i}
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
D
be a bounded slice Cauchy domain that contains
\sigma_{S}(T)
. Let
f
be an intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic function with
\overline{D}\subset\mathcal{D}(f)
, let
f_{i}:=f|_{\mathcal{D}(f)\cap\mathbb{C}_{i}}
and
D_{i}:=D\cap\mathbb{C}_{i}
(
D_{i}
is a bounded slice Cauchy domain in
\mathbb{C}_{i}
). Since
\sigma(T_{i})=\sigma_{S}(T)\cap\mathbb{C}_{i}
and
\Delta_{s}^{-1}

(
s\in\rho_{S}(T)\cap\mathbb{C}_{i}
)
is the resolvent of
T_{i}
,

(4.1)

\displaystyle f_{i}(T_{i})=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{i}(s)ds.

Definition 4.2

(
[
1
, Definition 3.1.10]
)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. For
s\in\sigma_{S}(T)
, we define the left S-resolvent operator as

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T):=-Q_{s}(T)^{-1}(T-\bar{s}I),

and the right S-resolvent operator as

S_{R}^{-1}(s,T):=-(T-\bar{s}I)Q_{s}(T)^{-1}.

Theorem 4.3

(
[
1
, Theorem 3.1.6]
)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
s\in\mathbb{H}
with
\|T\|<|s|
. Then

S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T^{n}s^{-n-1};

S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}s^{-n-1}T^{n}.

These series converge in the operator norm.

In
[
1
]
the authors defined the S-functional calculus as follows. For every right (resp. left) slice hyperholomorphic function
f
and every bounded slice Cauchy domain
D
such that
\sigma_{S}(T)\subset D\subset\overline{D}\subset\mathcal{D}(f)
,

f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)\;\;\;\;\left(\text{resp. }f(T):=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{i}f(s)\right).

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
D
be a bounded slice Cauchy domain such that
\sigma_{S}(T)\subset D
. Let
f
be an intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic function such that
\overline{D}\subset\mathcal{D}(f)
. Then

(4.2)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{i}f(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f(s)ds.

That is
f(T)=f_{i}(T_{i})
.

Proof.

The two integrals in (
4.2
) are independent of the choice of the bounded slice Cauchy domain
D
. Let
D
be the ball
B_{r}(0)
with
\|T\|<r
. Since
\Delta_{s}^{-1}=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T_{i}^{n}s^{-n-1}
converges uniformly on
\partial D_{i}
,

\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f(s)ds=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T_{i}^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds.

And
S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T^{n}s^{-n-1}
converges uniformly on
\partial D_{i}
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{i}f(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}ds_{i}f(s)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}T^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds.

Since
f
is intrinsic, by Lemma
2.2
(iii),

\overline{\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds.

Hence
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds\in\mathbb{R}
, and so

T^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds=T_{i}^{n}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}s^{-n-1}f(s)ds\;\;\text{ for all }\;\;n\in\mathbb{N}.

Thus

\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}S_{L}^{-1}(s,T)ds_{i}f(s)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f(s)\Delta_{s}^{-1}ds.

∎

Remark 4.5
.

It is easy to see that
f(T)_{i}=f_{i}(T_{i})
.

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an axially symmetric open set and let
f
be a right (resp. left) slice hyperholomorphic function on
U
. Then there exist intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
and
f_{4}
on
U
such that

f=f_{1}+\mathrm{i}f_{2}+\mathrm{j}f_{3}+\mathrm{k}f_{4}\;\;\;\;\left(\text{resp. }f=f_{1}+f_{2}\mathrm{i}+f_{3}\mathrm{j}+f_{4}\mathrm{k}\right).

Proof.

Let
j\in\mathbb{S}
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha+\beta j\in U
. Write

f(\alpha+\beta j)=f_{0}(\alpha,\beta)+f_{1}(\alpha,\beta)j
. Let
f_{n,m}
where
n=0,1
and
m=1,2,3,4
be real functions such that

f_{0}(\alpha,\beta)=f_{0,1}(\alpha,\beta)+f_{0,2}(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{i}+f_{0,3}(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{j}+f_{0,4}(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{k};

f_{1}(\alpha,\beta)=f_{1,1}(\alpha,\beta)+f_{1,2}(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{i}+f_{1,3}(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{j}+f_{1,4}(\alpha,\beta)\mathrm{k}.

Let

f_{m}(\alpha+\beta j):=f_{0,m}(\alpha,\beta)+f_{1,m}(\alpha,\beta)j\;\;\;\;\;\;\alpha+\beta j\in U,\;\;m=1,2,3,4.

Since
\{1,\mathrm{i},\mathrm{j},\mathrm{k}\}
is a basis of
\mathbb{H}
,
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
and
f_{4}
are intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions on
U
.

The left slice hyperholomorphic case can be proved similarly.
∎

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
D
be a bounded slice Cauchy domain such that
\sigma_{S}(T)\subset D
. Let
f
be a right slice hyperholomorphic function such that
\overline{D}\subset\mathcal{D}(f)
. By Lemma
4.6
, there exist intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}
such that
f=f_{1}+\mathrm{i}f_{2}+\mathrm{j}f_{3}+\mathrm{k}f_{4}
. Then

\displaystyle f(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f_{1}(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)+\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f_{2}(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)

\displaystyle+\mathrm{j}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f_{3}(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)+\mathrm{k}\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f_{4}(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T).

By Proposition
4.4
, for all
m=1,2,3,4
, the integral
\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{\partial D_{i}}f_{m}(s)ds_{i}S_{R}^{-1}(s,T)
can be written as
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{m}(s)ds
. Hence

\displaystyle f(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{1}(s)ds+\mathrm{i}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{2}(s)ds

\displaystyle+\mathrm{j}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{3}(s)ds+\mathrm{k}\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{4}(s)ds.

Similarly, for any left slice hyperholomorphic function such that
\overline{U}\subset\mathcal{D}(f)
, there exist intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3},f_{4}
such that

\displaystyle f(T)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{1}(s)ds+\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{2}(s)ds\right)\mathrm{i}

\displaystyle+\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{3}(s)ds\right)\mathrm{j}+\left(\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\partial D_{i}}\Delta_{s}^{-1}f_{4}(s)ds\right)\mathrm{k}.

Thus the S-functional calculus is obtained easily by the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus for complex bounded linear operators.

Now we are able to show that most of the properties that hold for the Riesz-Dunford functional calculus can be extended to the S-functional calculus. In
[
1
]
the authors extended these properties with several additional efforts.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
K
be a compact axially symmetric subset of
\mathbb{H}
. We denote by
\mathcal{SH}_{L}(K)
,
\mathcal{SH}_{R}(K)
and
\mathcal{N}(K)
the sets of all left, right and intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions
f
, where the domain of the function
f
contains the compact axially symmetric set
K
.

Theorem 4.7

(
[
1
, Theorem 4.1.3]
, Product rule)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
f\in\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
and
g\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
or let
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
and
g\in\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
. Then

\big(fg\big)\big(T\big)=f(T)g(T).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
and
g\in\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
. By Lemma
4.6
there exist intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions
f_{1},f_{2},f_{3}
and
f_{4}
such that

f=f_{1}+\mathrm{i}f_{2}+\mathrm{j}f_{3}+\mathrm{k}f_{4}.

Then
fg=f_{1}g+\mathrm{i}f_{2}g+\mathrm{j}f_{3}g+\mathrm{k}f_{4}g
. Hence

(fg)(T)=(f_{1}g)(T)+\mathrm{i}(f_{2}g)(T)+\mathrm{j}(f_{3}g)(T)+\mathrm{k}(f_{4}g)(T).

Note that
(f_{m}g)(T)=(f_{m}g)_{i}(T_{i})=(f_{m})_{i}(T_{i})g_{i}(T_{i})=f_{m}(T)g(T)

\;(m=1,2,3,4)
. Hence
(fg)(T)=f(T)g(T).

∎

Theorem 4.8

(
[
1
, Theorem 4.2.1]
, The spectral mapping theorem)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
f\in\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
. Then

\sigma_{S}\big(f(T)\big)=f\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big):=\{f(q):q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}.

Proof.

Note that
\sigma\big(f(T)_{i}\big)=\sigma\big(f_{i}(T_{i})\big)=f_{i}\big(\sigma(T_{i})\big)
. Thus by Proposition
3.8
,

\sigma_{S}\big(f(T)\big)=\big[\sigma\big(f(T)_{i}\big)\big]=\big[f_{i}\big(\sigma(T_{i})\big)\big]=f\big([\sigma(T_{i})]\big)=f\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big).

∎

Theorem 4.9

(
[
1
, Theorem 4.2.4]
, Composition rule)
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
f\in\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
. If
g\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}\big(\sigma_{S}\big(f(T)\big)\big)
, then
g\circ f\in\mathcal{SH}_{L}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
, and if
g\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}\big(f\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)\big)
, then
g\circ f\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
. In both cases,

g\big(f(T)\big)=\big(g\circ f\big)\big(T\big).

Proof.

Let
f\in\mathcal{N}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
and
g\in\mathcal{SH}_{R}\big(\sigma_{S}(T)\big)
. By lemma
4.6
there exist intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic functions
g_{1},g_{2},g_{3}
and
g_{4}
such that

g=g_{1}+\mathrm{i}g_{2}+\mathrm{j}g_{3}+\mathrm{k}g_{4}.

Then
g\circ f=g_{1}\circ f+\mathrm{i}g_{2}\circ f+\mathrm{j}g_{3}\circ f+\mathrm{k}g_{4}\circ f
. Hence

\big(g\circ f\big)\big(T\big)=\big(g_{1}\circ f\big)\big(T\big)+\mathrm{i}\big(g_{2}\circ f\big)\big(T\big)+\mathrm{j}\big(g_{3}\circ f\big)\big(T\big)+\mathrm{k}\big(g_{4}\circ f\big)\big(T\big).

We have
\big(g_{m}\circ f\big)\big(T\big)=\big(g_{m}\circ f\big)_{i}\big(T_{i}\big)=\big(g_{m}\big)_{i}\big(f_{i}(T_{i})\big)=g_{m}\big(f(T)\big)
. Hence
\big(g\circ f\big)\big(T\big)=g\big(f(T)\big).
The remaining case can be proved similarly.
∎

Theorem 4.10
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
and let
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\alpha\notin\sigma_{S}(T)
. Then we have

\mathrm{dist}\big(\alpha,\sigma_{S}(T)\big)=\frac{1}{r_{S}\big((\alpha I-T)^{-1}\big)}.

Proof.

Let
U\subseteq\mathbb{H}
be an axially symmetric open set containing
\sigma_{S}(T)
, but not
\alpha
. Then
f(q)=(\alpha-q)^{-1}
is an intrinsic slice hyperholomorphic function on
U
. So by Theorem
4.8
we have

\sigma_{S}\big((\alpha I-T)^{-1}\big)=\{(\alpha-q)^{-1}:q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}.

So in particular,

r_{S}\big((\alpha I-T)^{-1}\big)=\sup\{|(\alpha-q)^{-1}|:q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}=1/\inf\{|\alpha-q|:q\in\sigma_{S}(T)\}=1/\mathrm{dist}\big(\alpha,\sigma_{S}(T)\big).

∎

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
, we define

\exp(T)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}T^{n}

this series converges in the operator norm.

Theorem 4.11
.

Let
T\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
. Suppose that
0
is in the unbounded component of
\rho_{S}(T)
. Then there exists
S\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
such that
T=\exp(S)
. In particular, for every integer
n\geq 1
there exists
S\in\mathcal{B}_{R}(X)
such that
S^{n}=T
.

Proof.

Since
0
is in the unbounded component of
\rho_{S}(T)
,
0
is in the unbounded component of
\rho(T_{i})
. By Lemma
3.1
,
\sigma(T_{i})
is symmetric with respect to the real axis. Hence there exists a symmetric simply connected open set
O
of
\mathbb{C}_{i}
containing
\sigma(T_{i})
but not
0
. By
[
4
, Theorem 13.18]
there exists
f
holomorphic on
O
such that
s=\exp(f(s))
for all
s\in O
. The function
f
can be extended to a left slice hyperholomorphic function
\tilde{f}
on
[O]
(see,
[
1
, Lemma 2.1.11]
). Thus by
[
1
, Theorem 2.1.8]

s=\exp(\tilde{f}(s))
for all
s\in[O]
, and so
T=\exp(\tilde{f}(T))
, and
T=\exp(\frac{1}{n}\tilde{f}(T))^{n}
for all
n\geq 1
.
∎
