Abstract
. For r , n ≥ 2 r,n\geq 2 , an ordered r r -uniform matching of size n n is an r r -uniform hypergraph on a linearly ordered vertex set V V , with | V | = r ​ n |V|=rn , consisting of n n pairwise disjoint edges. There are 1 2 ​ ( 2 ​ r r ) \tfrac{1}{2}\binom{2r}{r} different ways two edges may intertwine, called here patterns. Among them we identify 3 r − 1 3^{r-1} collectable patterns P P , which have the potential of appearing in arbitrarily large quantities called P P -cliques. We prove an Erdős-Szekeres type result guaranteeing in every ordered r r -uniform matching the presence of a P P -clique of a prescribed size, for some collectable pattern P P . In particular, in the diagonal case, one of the P P -cliques must be of size Ω ⁡ ( n 3 1 − r ) \Omega\left(n^{3^{1-r}}\right) . In addition, for each collectable pattern P P we show that the largest size of a P P -clique in a random ordered r r -uniform matching of size n n is, with high probability, Θ ⁡ ( n 1 / r ) \Theta\left(n^{1/r}\right) .

1.1.
Ordered matchings, words, and patterns

A hypergraph
G

G
have the same size

Compared to ordinary isomorphism, one context in which order-isomorphism makes quite a difference is that of sub-hypergraph containment. If

-graph

of

-graph with the ordering of

.
Given two ordered

All kinds of questions concerning sub-hypergraphs in unordered hypergraphs can be posed for ordered hypergraphs as well (see, e.g.,

Throughout the paper

Typically, to represent a given ordered matching, we will choose a word whose letters occur in alphabetic order. 1.2.
Erdős-Szekers type results for ordered
2

3

A frequent theme in combinatorics and graph theory concerns unavoidable sub-structures that appear in every member of a prescribed family of structures. A flagship example is the famous theorem of Erdős and Szekeres
[
15
]
on monotone subsequences (see, e.g.,
[
3
,
5
,
6
,
14
,
16
,
24
,
27
,
10
]
for some recent extensions and generalizations). In its diagonal form it states that any sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}
of distinct real numbers contains a monotone subsequence of length at least
\sqrt{n}
. Our goal is to prove its analog for ordered
r
-matchings.

The reason why the original Erdős-Szekeres Theorem lists only two types of subsequences is, obviously, that for any two elements
x_{i}
and
x_{j}
with
i<j
there are just two possible relations:
x_{i}<x_{j}
or
x_{i}>x_{j}
. However, as noted above, for every two edges in an ordered
r
-matching, there are exactly
\tfrac{1}{2}\binom{2r}{r}
patterns in which they may intertwine. Therefore, more complex phenomena involving homogeneous sub-structures can be expected in this case.

In
[
13
]
(see also
[
12
]
) we proved the following analog of the Erdős-Szekeres Theorem for ordered 2-matchings.

Theorem 1.1

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let

be arbitrary positive integers and let

. Then, every matching

contains a line of size
\ell+1
, or a stack of size
s+1
, or a wave of size
w+1
. Moreover, this is not true for

.

In the symmetric case (

was deduced from Dilworth’s Theorem by Huynh, Joos and Wollan in
[

(though, a similar proof works in the general case too). For the sake of completeness, we give yet another proof
in the appendix.

1.3.
Collectable patterns and the main result

In this subsection we describe our new results about unavoidable sub-structures in
every
matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
. (For results about
random

r
-matchings see the next subsection.) The main goal of this paper is to generalize Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
to arbitrary
r\geq 2
.

In order to state our main result we need to introduce some terminology and notation. First we need to recognize patterns that allow for constructing arbitrarily large cliques. A pattern
P
is called
collectable
if for every
k\geq 2
it is possible to build a
P
-clique of size
k
. It turns out that collectable patterns are easily characterized in terms of their representing words.

A block
in a word is a segment of consecutive letters of any length. A block consisting of the same letter is called a
run
(it does not need to be maximal, though). A run
AA\cdots A
of length
k
is often denoted compactly by
A^{k}
and called an
A
-run
of
length

k
.

A pattern
P
is
splittable
if it can be split into a number of blocks each consisting of an
A
-run and a
B
-run of the same length. For example,
AABBABBA
is splittable, because it splits into three such blocks:
|AABB|AB|BA|
, while
AABABB
is not.

It is easy to see that any splittable pattern is collectable. For instance, if
P=|AABB|AB|BA|
, then for every
k\geq 2
, matching

|A_{1}A_{1}A_{2}A_{2}\cdots A_{k}A_{k}|A_{1}A_{2}\cdots A_{k}|A_{k}\cdots A_{2}A_{1}|

is a
P
-clique of size
k
.
Indeed, one can see that for every
1\leq i<j\leq k
, letters
A_{i}
and
A_{j}
do form pattern
P
.

In Subsection
2.1
we show that a pattern is collectable if and only if it is splittable. It follows, by means of some elementary enumeration, that there are precisely
3^{r-1}
collectable
r
-patterns for every
r\geq 2
.
In particular, for
r=2
all three patterns are collectable, for
r=3
all but one (
P_{1}^{*}
), while for
r=4
, out of 35 patterns, 27 are collectable (see Table
2
in Subsection
2.2
, where suitable partitions are shown).

While generalizing Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
one has to accommodate the deficiencies similar to the sole constant
1/2
in Theorem
1.2
. For this sake, we look at the very end of each pattern.
The
maturity
of a collectable pattern equals the length of its last
maximal
run minus
2
, unless this value is negative, in which case we set it to
0
. The maturity of a pattern
P
will be denoted by
m(P)
. For instance,
m(AAAABBBB)=4-2=2
, while
m(AABBBBAA)=m(ABABABAB)=0
.

We may now formulate our main result. An
r
-matching is called
clean
if every pair of its edges forms a collectable pattern.

Theorem 1.3
.

For
r\geq 2
, let
P_{1},\dots,P_{3^{r-1}}
be all collectable
r
-patterns and let positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{3^{r-1}}
be given. If
n\geq\prod_{i=1}^{3^{r-1}}a_{i}+1
, then

(a)

every matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size greater than
a_{i}2^{-m(P_{i})}
, for some
i\in[3^{r-1}]
;

(b)

every clean matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size at least
a_{i}+1
, for some
i\in[3^{r-1}]
.

Note that for
r=2
and
r=3
, we retain, respectively, Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
, the latter with an additional statement (b), where
clean
just means that no pair of edges forms pattern
P_{1}^{*}
. As shown in Section
4.1
, Theorem
1.3
is not optimal.

1.4.
Corollaries

In this subsection we gather some easy corollaries of Theorem
1.3
and prove them right away.

It can be routinely calculated (see Subsection
2.1
) that the total maturity of all
r
-patterns equals

(1.1)

\gamma_{r}:=\sum_{i=1}^{3^{r-1}}m(P_{i})=\tfrac{1}{2}\left(3^{r-2}-1\right).

Thus, one can evenly redistribute the deficiencies
2^{-m(P_{i})}
occurring in Theorem
1.3
(a) among
all
collectable patterns. To avoid minor technical issues with integrality, we state it in the following form.

Corollary 1.4
.

For
r\geq 2
, let
P_{1},\dots,P_{3^{r-1}}
be all collectable
r
-patterns and let positive integers
b_{1},\dots,b_{3^{r-1}}
be given. If
n>2^{\gamma_{r}}\prod_{i=1}^{3^{r-1}}b_{i}
, then
every matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size at least
b_{i}+1
, for some
i\in[3^{r-1}]
.

Proof.

Set
a_{i}:=2^{m(P_{i})}b_{i}
,
i=1,\dots,3^{r-1}
. Then

\prod_{i=1}^{3^{r-1}}a_{i}\overset{\eqref{summPi}}{=}2^{\gamma}\prod_{i=1}^{3^{r-1}}b_{i}<n

by assumption, and so, by Theorem
1.3
(a), every matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains, for some
i
, a
P_{i}
-clique of size greater than
a_{i}2^{-m(P_{i})}=b_{i}
. ∎

One context in which clean
r
-matchings emerge is that of
r
-partiteness. An ordered
r
-matching
M
of size
k
is
r
-partite
if, after splitting its vertex set into
r
consecutive blocks of size
k
,
every edge of
M
contains one vertex from each block.
Note that the only patterns possible in an
r
-partite
r
-matching are those which are themselves
r
-partite. These patterns can be characterized as splittable patterns with each block being either
AB
or
BA
. Consequently, there are exactly
2^{r-1}
of them.
For instance, for
r=2
, both nesting and crossing are bipartite, while
for
r=3
, only patterns
P_{5},P_{6},P_{8},P_{9}
(as listed in Table
1
) are tripartite.
Moreover, an
r
-partite
r
-matching is, indeed, clean.
Setting
a_{i}=1
for each non-
r
-partite pattern
P_{i}
, we thus obtain the following corollary of Theorem
1.3
(b). Its optimality follows by a construction given in Section
4.1
.

Corollary 1.5
.

For
r\geq 2
, let
Q_{1},\dots,Q_{2^{r-1}}
be all
r
-partite patterns and let positive integers
a_{1},\dots,a_{2^{r-1}}
be given. If
n\geq\prod_{i=1}^{2^{r-1}}a_{i}+1
, then every
r
-partite matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
Q_{i}
-clique of size at least
a_{i}+1
, for some
i\in[2^{r-1}]
. Moreover, this is not true for
n=\prod_{i=1}^{2^{r-1}}a_{i}
. ∎

The above result illustrates the following principle: setting
a_{i}=1
for any pattern absent from a matching results in more “prey” for the remaining patterns. One should also remark that Corollary
1.5
follows easily by
r-1
applications of the original Erdős-Szekeres Theorem on permutations. Indeed, an
r
-partite
r
-matching of size
n
can be viewed as an
(r-1)
-tuple of permutations of order
n
, while
P
-cliques, in this setting, correspond to
(r-1)
-tuples of monotone subsequences thereof.

Theorem
1.3
and its corollaries can be interpreted in terms of edge-colorings of a complete graph. As mentioned above, we may associate with every matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
an edge colored clique
K_{n}
, whose vertices are the edges of
M
and colors (of the edges of
K_{n}
) correspond to patterns occurring in
M
. It follows from known bounds on Ramsey numbers that a monochromatic clique of order
\Omega(\log n)
is guaranteed (see, e.g.,
[
8
]
), however, in this setting much larger cliques are present: of order
\Omega(n^{2^{1-r}})
in
r
-partite matchings and
\Omega(n^{3^{1-r}})
in general and clean matchings.

Corollary 1.6
.

For
r\geq 2
, let
P_{1},\dots,P_{3^{r-1}}
be all collectable patterns and
Q_{1},\ldots,Q_{2^{r-1}}
be the
r
-partite patterns among them.

(a)

There is a constant
C_{r}>0
such that every matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size at least
C_{r}n^{3^{1-r}}
, for some
i\in[3^{r-1}]
.

(b)

Every clean matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size at least
n^{3^{1-r}}
, for some
i\in[3^{r-1}]
.

(c)

Every
r
-partite matching
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains a
Q_{i}
-clique of size greater than
n^{2^{1-r}}
, for some
i\in[2^{r-1}]
.

Proof.

For (a), recall the definition of
\gamma_{r}
in (
1.1
) and set

C_{r}:=2^{\gamma_{r}/3^{r-1}}

and
b_{i}=\lceil C_{r}n^{3^{1-r}}\rceil-1
,
i=1,\dots,3^{r-1}
. Then,
n>2^{\gamma_{r}}\prod_{i=1}^{3^{r-1}}b_{i}
and, by Corollary
1.4
, every
M\in{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
contains, for some
i
, a
P_{i}
-clique of size greater than
b_{i}
, that is, at least
b_{i}+1\geq C_{r}n^{3^{1-r}}
.
For (b), setting
a_{i}=\lceil n^{3^{1-r}}\rceil-1
, we deduce the conclusion from Theorem
1.3
(b). Similarly, part (c) follows from Corollary
1.5
by setting
a_{i}=\lceil n^{2^{1-r}}\rceil-1
.
∎

1.5.
Random matchings

In the second part of this paper we consider a
random

r
-matching
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
, that is, a matching picked uniformly at random from the family
{\mathcal{M}}_{n}^{(r)}
of all
\frac{(rn)!}{(r!)^{n}\,n!}

r
-matchings on the same vertex set
[rn]
. We say that an event
{\mathcal{A}}_{n}
holds
asymptotically almost surely
, or shortly,
a.a.s.
, if
\Pr({\mathcal{A}}_{n})\to 1
, as
n\to\infty
.

With respect to the Erdős-Szekeres Theorem on monotone subsequences, it is well-known that a.a.s. a random permutation contains both, an increasing and a decreasing subsequence, of order
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
. This, roughly, matches the order of a monotone subsequence guaranteed in every permutation. In
[
13
, Theorem 12 and Proposition 11]
we gave a simple proof of a similar result about random 2-matchings. (For much sharper versions of this result see
[
2
]
and
[
20
]
.)

Theorem 1.7

(
[
13
]
)
.

A.a.s. the size of the largest line, stack, and wave in a random matching
\mathbb{RM}_{n}^{(2)}
is
\Theta(\sqrt{n})
.

Note that, this time, the size of the sub-structure expected in a random matching exceeds that guaranteed in every matching (c.f. Theorem
1.1
with

2.1.
Collectable patterns

In Section

we defined collectable and splittable patterns. Now we show that these two notions are equivalent. In fact, for any non-collectable pattern
P
, one cannot even construct a
P
-clique of order as small as three.

Proposition 2.1
.

A pattern
P
is collectable if and only if it is splittable. Moreover, if
P
is unsplittable, then every
P
-clique has size at most two.

Proof.

It is not hard to verify that every splittable pattern is collectable. Indeed, as demonstrated in Section
1
, it suffices to replace every block of the splitting, which is always of the form
A^{t}B^{t}
or
B^{t}A^{t}
, by, respectively,
A_{1}^{t}\cdots A_{k}^{t}
or
A_{k}^{t}\cdots A_{1}^{t}

Now, assume that
P
is an unsplittable pattern and let

be the longest splittable prefix of

, that is

, where

is a splittable word (possibly empty) of length

, and

begins, say, with a block
A^{s}B^{t}A
where

(2.1)

there are exactly
q+t

B
’s to the left of the
(q+s+1)
-st letter

Moreover, there are

A

q+\max(2t-s,0)

B

(2.2)

the numbers of

’s and
B

2q+2t
letters of

are
not
the same.

Suppose to the contrary that there is a
P
-clique

with three edges
e_{A}
,
e_{B}
and
e_{C}
(in lexicographic order) represented in the word notation, respectively, by letters

and

, both isomorphic to

In view of (

q+t

q+t

This implies that among the first

) of

In the symmetric case, when

∎

Theorem 2.5
.

For

, for some

,

Recall that

. There are two other equivalent ways of drawing

The formula for

indicates that each ordered

-matching by the following permutational scheme. Let

yielding the same matching. This scheme allows one to use concentration inequalities for random permutations in the context of random matchings (see Subsection
3.2
).

The online scheme of generating

goes as follows. Given an arbitrary ordering of the vertices
u_{1},\dots,u_{rn}
(not necessarily the same as the canonical ordering

) one selects uniformly at random an
(r-1)
-element set

. Then, after crossing out

from the list, one selects uniformly at random an
(r-1)
-element set (in
\binom{rn-r-1}{r-1}
ways) to be matched with the first uncrossed vertex, and so on, and so forth. This scheme comes in handy when estimating probabilities of events involving small sets of fixed vertices (see Subsection
3.2
and Appendix C).

3.1.

We begin with an upper bound. A slightly more general result was already stated in

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
P
be a collectable
r
-pattern. Then, a.a.s. the size of the largest
P
-clique in a random
r
-matching
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
is
O_{r}(n^{1/r})
.

Proof.

For every integer
k\geq 2
, let
X_{k}
be the number of
P
-cliques of size
k
in

\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
. In order to compute the expectation of
X_{k}
, one has to choose a set
S
of
rk
vertices (out of all
rn
vertices) on which a
P
-clique will be planted. Formally, for each
S\in\binom{[rn]}{rk}
define an indicator random variable
I_{S}=1
if there is a
P
-clique on
S
in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
, and
I_{S}=0
otherwise. As there is just one way to plant a
P
-clique on a given set

, we have
X_{k}=\sum_{S}I_{S}
and

{\mathds{E}}I_{S}=\Pr(I_{S}=1)=\frac{\alpha^{(r)}_{n-k}}{\alpha^{(r)}_{n}}.

Thus, by the linearity of expectation,

{\mathds{E}}X_{k}=\binom{rn}{rk}\cdot\frac{\alpha^{(r)}_{n-k}}{\alpha^{(r)}_{n}}=\frac{(r!)^{k}}{(rk)!}\cdot\frac{n!}{(n-k)!}\leq\frac{(r!)^{k}}{(rk)!}\cdot n^{k}\leq\frac{(r!)^{k}}{(rk/e)^{rk}}\cdot n^{k}=\left(\frac{e^{r}r!n}{(rk)^{r}}\right)^{k}.

Fixing a constant
c>\tfrac{e}{r}(r!)^{1/r}
and setting
k_{0}=\lceil cn^{1/r}\rceil
, we conclude, by Markov’s inequality, that

\Pr(X_{k_{0}}>0)\leq{\mathds{E}}X_{k_{0}}\leq\left(\frac{e^{r}r!}{(rc)^{r}}\right)^{k_{0}}=o(1).

This completes the proof.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Given integers

Proof.

Let

4.2.
Improvement for
r=3

We have recently learned a cute argument which, for
r=3
, improves Corollary
1.6
(a) from
\Omega(n^{1/9})
to
\Omega(n^{1/7})
, matching the upper bound
O(n^{1/7})
from Remark
4.1
. For a set of 3-patterns
\mathcal{P}
, a
\mathcal{P}
-clique
is a matching whose all pairs of edges form a pattern from
\mathcal{P}
. Let
M
be a 3-matching of size
n\geq a_{1}(a_{2}+a_{4})a_{6}a_{8}(a_{3}+a_{7})a_{5}a_{9}+1
. The claim is that for some
i\in[9]
,
M
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size
a_{i}+1
.
The starting point is, unlike in our inductive proof, to drop the middle vertex of each edge of
M
and apply to the 2-matching
M^{\prime}
obtained this way Theorem
1.1
with
\ell=a_{1}
,
s=(a_{2}+a_{4})a_{6}a_{8}
, and
w=(a_{3}+a_{7})a_{5}a_{9}
. It is easy to check that when two edges of
M^{\prime}
form a nesting, then the corresponding edges of
M
form one of the patterns in
\mathcal{P}=\{P_{2},P_{4},P_{6},P_{8}\}
, while if they form a crossing, then the corresponding edges of
M
form one of the patterns in
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}=\{P_{1}^{*},P_{3},P_{5},P_{7},P_{9}\}
. We thus obtain in
M
either a line of size
a_{1}+1
or a
\mathcal{P}
-clique
H
of size
h:=(a_{2}+a_{4})a_{6}a_{8}+1
, or a
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
-clique
H^{\prime}
of size
h^{\prime}:=(a_{3}+a_{7})a_{5}a_{9}+1
. Now our goal is to somehow separate patterns
P_{2}
and
P_{4}
in
H
, and patterns
P_{3}
and
P_{7}
in
H^{\prime}
.

To this end, let us say that a vertex
v
in a 3-matching is
covered
by an edge
e
of that matching if
v
is between the first and the last vertex of
e
(
v
does not need to be the middle vertex of
e
). Let
e_{1},\dots,e_{h}
be the edges of
H
ordered by their left ends. Notice, and this is absolutely crucial, that the set of edges of
H
which cover a fixed vertex
v
of
H
form a prefix of the sequence
e_{1},\dots,e_{h}
(possibly empty). This is because each pattern in
\mathcal{P}
begins and ends with the same letter. So, if an edge
e_{j}
covers a vertex, then so does each
e_{i}
for
i\leq j
.

If
v
itself is a midpoint of an edge
e_{j}
and the edges
e_{g},\dots,e_{h}
do
not
cover
v
, then
v
is either entirely to the left of all these edges or entirely to the right. This brings about a classification of all edges of
H
into two categories: the
left edges
– those whose midpoints are to the left of all edges not covering it, and the
right edges
. (If all edges of
H
cover
v
, then
e_{j}
can be classified either way.)
Here is an example (note that the extreme points of edges form mutually a nesting):

A\;A\;B\;C\;D\;C\;D\;E\;F\;F\;B\;F\;E\;E\;D\;C\;B\;A.

The midpoint of
e_{C}
is covered by edges
e_{A},e_{B},e_{C},e_{D}
and is to the left of edges
e_{E},e_{F}
, while the midpoint of
e_{E}
is not covered only by
e_{F}
and lies to the right of
e_{F}
. In this example, edges
e_{A},e_{C},e_{D}
are left,
e_{E}
is right, while
e_{B},e_{F}
can be both (their midpoints are covered by all six edges).

Another crucial observation is that no left edges form pattern
P_{4}
, while no right edges form pattern
P_{2}
. Indeed, if
e_{i}=XXX
and
e_{j}=YYY
,
i<j
, form pattern
P_{4}=XYYYXX
, then the midpoint of
e_{i}
is not covered by
e_{j}
, while it is to the right of
e_{j}
. Similarly, if
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
form
P_{2}=XXYYYX
, then the midpoint of
e_{i}
(the second
X
) is to the left of
e_{j}
(and
e_{j}
does not cover it).

So, if
h\geq a_{2}a_{6}a_{8}+1
, then, by Theorem
1.3
(b) applied to the set of left edges of
H
, for some
i\in\{2,6,8\}
there is a
P_{i}
-clique in
H
, and thus in
M
, of size
a_{i}+1
. On the other hand, if
h\geq a_{4}a_{6}a_{8}+1
, then, Theorem
1.3
(b) applied to the set of right edges of
H
, for some
i\in\{4,6,8\}
there is a
P_{i}
-clique in
H
of size
a_{i}+1
. And one of the two statements must hold, since
h:=(a_{2}+a_{4})a_{6}a_{8}+1
.

The situation is similar, though a bit more complicated, for
H^{\prime}
. Here the edges covering a fixed vertex may form a prefix or a suffix of the sequence of edges of
H^{\prime}
ordered by their left ends. This also yields a binary classification of the edges of
H^{\prime}
with a similar mutual exclusion of patterns
P_{3}
and
P_{7}
, and of pattern
P_{1}^{*}
whatsoever. We leave the details for the reader. In conclusion, if
n\geq a_{1}(a_{2}+a_{4})a_{6}a_{8}(a_{3}+a_{7})a_{5}a_{9}+1
, then for some
i\in[9]
,
M
contains a
P_{i}
-clique of size
a_{i}+1
, which, setting all
a_{i}
equal, yields the lower bound
\Omega(n^{1/7})
.

But in general, this statement
is still far from optimal. If all
a_{i}=1
except for
a_{2}
and
a_{7}
, then, according to the discussion in the previous subsection, the correct bound should be

\Theta(a_{2}+a_{7})
, not
\Theta(a_{2}a_{7})
given above. A similar situation holds for
a_{3}
and
a_{4}
.

4.3.
Open questions

Let us conclude the paper with some questions concerning possible future research. Firstly, in view of the discussion in the previous two subsections, one could try to strengthen Theorem
1.3
so that the new version would be indeed optimal.

Problem 4.2
.

Given
r\geq 3
, let
P_{1},\dots,P_{3^{r-1}}
be all collectable ordered
r
-matchings. Find a function
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{3^{r-1}})
such that every
r
-matching of size
n\geq f(a_{1},\dots,a_{3^{r-1}})+1
contains for some
i
a
P_{i}
-clique of size
a_{i}+1
and, on the other hand, there exists an
r
-matching with
f(a_{1},\dots,a_{3^{r-1}})
edges not containing a
P_{i}
-clique of size
a_{i}+1
for any
i
.

Another direction in which one could sail inspired by our results is a
cyclic
counterpart of ordered matchings.
A cyclic version of the original Erdős-Szekeres theorem for permutations was obtained recently by Czabarka and Wang
[
10
]
.
A
cyclic
r
-matching
of size
n
is just an
r
-uniform matching with
n
edges on a cyclically ordered set of vertices. Such matchings can be naturally represented by cyclic words in which every letter corresponding to an edge occurs exactly
r
times.
For instance, for
r=2
we have only two possible cyclic patterns, namely
Q_{1}=AABB
and
Q_{2}=ABAB
(since
ABBA
is equivalent to
AABB
), while for
r=3
there are just four of them:
AAABBB
,
AABABB
,
AABBAB
, and
ABABAB
.

It would be desirable to know whether in this setting a similar Erdős-Szekeres type phenomena occur. First, however, we should decide what is the appropriate analog of a
P
-clique. Take a look, for instance, at two cyclic matchings of size three,
AABBCC
and
AABCCB
. They both form a
Q_{1}
-clique in a sense that each pair of edges form a cyclic pattern
Q_{1}
, but they are not isomorphic. Perhaps, to remedy this problem, the right definition of a homogenous cyclic
r
-matching of size
n
should demand that, for each
k\leq n
, all sub-matchings of size
k
are mutually order-isomorphic.

Problem 4.3
.

For each
r\geq 2
define homogenous cyclic matchings “in a right way”. Then
identify and/or characterize all possible homogenous cyclic ordered
r
-matchings. Subsequently, prove an Erdős-Szekeres type theorem for them.

Our next question refers to the random setting and aims to pinpoint the multiplicative constants in Theorem
1.8
.

Conjecture 4.4
.

For every
r\geqslant 2
and every collectable
r
-pattern
P
, there exists a positive constant
b_{P}
such that the maximum size of a
P
-clique in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
is a.a.s. equal to
(1+o(1))b_{P}n^{1/r}
.

Problem 4.5
.

Assuming the conjecture is true, determine all constants
b_{P}
.

The conjecture is true (and the problem is solved) for
r=2
. Indeed, Stanley
[
26
]
deduced from a deep result of Baik and Rains
[
2
]
concerning monotone subsequences in random permutations that the maximum size of stacks and waves in
\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n}
is a.a.s
(1+o(1))\sqrt{2n}
. In turn, Justicz, Scheinerman, and Winkler in
[
20
]
showed, while studying a related notion of random interval graphs, that
the size of the largest line contained in
\mathbb{RM}_{n}
is a.a.s. equal to
(2+o(1))\sqrt{n/\pi}
.

Let us conclude with asking for the size of the largest twins in ordered
r
-matchings, that is, pairs of order-isomorphic, disjoint sub-matchings. Let
t^{(r)}(M)
denote the maximum size of twins in an
r
-matching
M
(measured by the size of just one of them) and
t^{(r)}(n)
– the minimum of
t^{(r)}(M)
over all
r
-matchings on
[rn]
. In
[
13
, Theorem 20]
we proved that a.a.s.
t^{(2)}(\mathbb{RM}^{(2)}_{n})=\Theta(n^{2/3})
and, by linking the presence of twins in 2-matchings with the presence of twins in permutations, we derived an estimate
t^{(2)}(n)=\Omega(n^{3/5})
. We conjectured in
[
13
, Conjecture 23]
(in the more general setting of multiple twins) that
t^{(2)}(n)=\Omega(n^{2/3})
.

In the
r
-uniform scenario, we proved in
[
11
, Theorem 1]
that a.a.s.
t^{(r)}(\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n})=\Theta_{r}(n^{2/(r+1)})
.
However, its deterministic, much harder counterpart remains wide open.

Problem 4.6
.

Determine the order of magnitude of
t^{(r)}(n)
.

Currently, we only know that
t^{(r)}(n)=\Omega_{r}\left(n^{\frac{3}{5(2^{r-1}-1)}}\right)
(see
[
11
, Theorem 2]
) and, as a consequence of the above average case result, that
t^{(r)}(n)=O_{r}\left(n^{2/(r+1)}\right)
.
Even a similar question for permutations is far from being settled (see, e.g.,
[
7
]
).

An alternative proof of Lemma

For simplicity we assume that
t\to\infty
together with

.
Let
\mathcal{E}
be the event that no edge in
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
spans the sets
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
. We will generate
\mathbb{RM}^{(r)}_{n}
using a random permutation
\pi
of
[rn]
such that the edges are defined as
e_{i}=\pi(\{(i-1)r+1,\dots,ir\})
for
1\leq i\leq n
.

Let
n^{\prime}=\lceil n/2\rceil
. First observe that
\left|\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}}e_{i}\cap A_{j}\right|
has a hypergeometric distribution,

\Pr\left(\left|\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}}e_{i}\cap A_{j}\right|=k\right)=\frac{\binom{|A_{j}|}{k}\binom{rn-|A_{j}|}{rn^{\prime}-k}}{\binom{rn}{rn^{\prime}}}=\frac{\binom{t}{k}\binom{rn-t}{rn^{\prime}-k}}{\binom{rn}{rn^{\prime}}}.

Thus, the expected size of the intersection is
trn^{\prime}/rn\geq t/2
and the Chernoff bound for the hypergeometric distribution (see, e.g.,
[
19
, Theorem 2.10]
) together with the union bound yields

\Pr\left(\exists_{1\leq j\leq r}:\left|\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}}e_{i}\cap A_{j}\right|\geq 0.99t\right)\leq\sum_{1\leq j\leq r}\Pr\left(\left|\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}}e_{i}\cap A_{j}\right|\geq 0.99t\right)\leq\exp(-\Omega(t)).

\mathcal{F}
be the event that “
\forall_{1\leq j\leq r}:\left|\bigcup_{1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}}e_{i}\cap A_{j}\right|<0.99t
”. Also, for
1\leq i\leq n^{\prime}
, let
{\mathcal{E}}_{i}
be the event that
e_{i}
does not span
A_{1},\dots,A_{r}
. Note that conditioning on
\mathcal{F}
implies that even after revealing the first
(i-1)r
positions of
\pi
(yielding

), we still have at least

uncovered points in each

\Pr(\bar{{\mathcal{E}}_{i}}|{\mathcal{E}}_{1},\dots,{\mathcal{E}}_{i-1},\mathcal{F})\geq\frac{(0.01t)^{r}}{\binom{rn-(i-1)r}{r}}\geq\frac{(0.01t)^{r}}{\binom{rn}{r}}\geq\left(\frac{0.01t}{en}\right)^{r}.

Then, by the chain formula,

\displaystyle\Pr(\mathcal{E}|\mathcal{F})

\displaystyle\leq\Pr\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}}{\mathcal{E}}_{i}|\mathcal{F}\right)=\Pr({\mathcal{E}}_{1}|\mathcal{F})\Pr({\mathcal{E}}_{2}|{\mathcal{E}}_{1},\mathcal{F})\cdots\Pr\left({\mathcal{E}}_{n^{\prime}}\Big|\bigcap_{i=1}^{n^{\prime}-1}{\mathcal{E}}_{i},\mathcal{F}\right)

. ∎

Lemma C.2
.

Let a sequence

be given such that

Proof.

We are going to apply the second moment method.
For each
r

be the indicator random variable such that
