Reflection positivity and infrared bounds for quantum spin systems


Abstract
Zusammenfassung The method of reflection positivity and infrared bounds allows to prove the occurrence of phase transitions in systems with continuous symmetries. We review the method in the context of quantum spin systems. Dedicated to Elliott Lieb on the occasion of his 90th birthday.

1
Introduction

1.1
The quest for understanding phase transitions

The description of phase transitions puzzled physicists at the dawn of
Statistical Physics. The first mathematical description goes back to
1924 with Einstein’s description of Bose–Einstein condensation, but
its physical relevance was not recognised then. Peierls’ argument for
the Ising model was published in 1936 but did not settle the
question either. Legend has it that in Amsterdam in 1937, during the
conference celebrating the centenary of the birth of van der Waals, a
spirited debate took place regarding the validity of the Gibbs
formalism, and of the thermodynamic limit. This was supported by Born
and Uhlenbeck but strongly contested by Sommerfeld. Kramers, who was
chairman, apparently called for the question to be settled by a
vote… and the outcome was positive, narrowly!

The setting has been clarified over the years and given precise
mathematical meaning. The main challenge now is to prove the occurrence
of phase transitions in specific systems.

The first results dealt with systems with discrete symmetries such as
the Ising model. Regarding systems with continuous symmetries, Mermin
and Wagner proved a negative result in 1966, namely that the
Heisenberg model does not display spontaneous magnetisation in two
dimensions, at any positive temperature
[
38
]
. It took another
decade for the first positive result to appear, due to Fröhlich,
Simon, and Spencer; they established in 1976 that the classical
Heisenberg model undergoes a phase transition in dimensions three and
higher
[
18
]
.
Their work was inspired by ideas from quantum field field theory, specifically by the Källén–Lehmann representation of two-point Green functions in relativistic quantum field theory,
which suggested the right form of infrared bounds, and by reflection positivity, as
formulated in the works of Jost
[
25
]
, Osterwalder and Schrader
[
40
]
, and Glaser
[
22
]
. (Furthermore, bounds in
[
23
,
13
]
inspired the exponential infrared bounds proved in
[
18
]
.)

The extension of these ideas to quantum spin systems was achieved in
another groundbreaking article, by Dyson, Lieb, and Simon in 1978

[
10
]
. The method was then further extended and streamlined in

[
15
]
and
[
16
]
. Further refinements include an
extension to the ground states in two dimensions
[
39
]
and
improved conditions that establish long-range order in the XY model in
two dimensions
[
26
,
27
,
29
]
.

It should be pointed out that the method does not apply to models where all coupling constants are positive
[
43
]
. An important problem, which remains open to this day, is to prove spontaneous magnetisation or long-range order in the Heisenberg ferromagnet.

Another extension of the method deals with "chessboard estimates",
proposed by Fröhlich and Lieb
[
17
]
(they were partly motivated by
[
24
]
). Among many interesting
works that use these ideas, let us mention the flux phase problem

[
32
,
37
]
; spin reflection positivity applied to Hubbard models

[
33
,
46
,
45
]
; itinerant electron models
[
34
,
35
,
36
]
;
high spin systems whose classical limit has long-range order

[
6
,
7
]
; spin nematic phases
[
4
,
44
]
; Néel order in the spin-1 model with biquadratic interactions
[
31
]
; hard-core bosons

[
27
,
1
]
; loop models associated with quantum spin systems
[
49
]
(motivated by
[
47
,
2
]
) and other loop models associated with classical spin systems
[
41
]
. Finally, let us mention an alternate extension of
[
18
]
to quantum systems by Albert, Ferrari, Fröhlich, and Schlein
[
3
]
.

There exist a few results about phase transitions in systems with continuous symmetry that were proved with different methods, see
[
8
,
20
]
. But reflection positivity and infrared bound remains the most prolific method and the only one that has been applied to quantum systems.

A beautiful account of the method of reflection positivity in statistical mechanics has been
written by Biskup
[
5
]
. It is restricted to classical
systems, so the present survey can be seen as a
complement, dealing with the quantum counterparts. Nevertheless, we
have attempted to write a self-contained
pedagogical account, that encompasses many of the results on
phase transitions for quantum
spin systems.

The handwritten notes of Tóth for his Prague lectures give a clear account of the method
[
48
]
. And
an extensive overview, which retraces the origin of the key ideas, can be found in the handwritten notes of Fröhlich for his Vienna lectures
[

1.2
Organisation of the survey

The setting for quantum spin systems is introduced in Section

2
. We recall the existence of the
infinite-volume limit of the free energy
(Theorem
2.1
), and discuss non-differentiability of
the free energy and how it relates to long-range order.
Subsequently, the chapter is organised as follows:

—

The existence of long-range order is stated in Section

3
.
For this we consider the case of positive temperature in dimensions 3
and higher as well as the ground state of
two-dimensional systems.

—

The case of nearest-neighbour interactions is covered in Theorem
3.2
. As discussed after the theorem, long-range order has been proved for all
d\geq 2
and all
S\in\frac{1}{2}{\mathbb{N}}
, except for the case
d=2
and
S=\frac{1}{2}
where it is restricted to models close to XY.

—

The proof of long-range order in turn relies on an "infrared
bound" on correlations which we state in Section
4
. This involves transferring the bound from the Duhamel correlation function to the normal correlation function.

—

We establish reflection positivity of our models in Section
5
and use it to prove the infrared bound on the Duhamel correlation function.

—

The appendix contains correlation inequalities for quantum spin systems that we need in various places.

2
Setting and results

The domain of the system is a finite subset
\Lambda\subset{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
. The state space is the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}=\otimes_{x\in\Lambda}{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
where
n=2S+1=2,3,4,\dots
. Equivalently,
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
can be defined as the
linear span of the space of classical configurations,
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}={\textrm{span}}(\Sigma^{\Lambda})
, where
\Sigma=\{-S,-S+1,\dots,S\}
. We
consider the spin operators
(S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)})_{x\in\Lambda}^{i=1,2,3}

that satisfy the relations:

\begin{split}&[S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}]={\textrm{i}}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\delta_{x,y},\quad[S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)},S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}]={\textrm{i}}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\delta_{x,y},\quad[S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)},S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}]={\textrm{i}}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\delta_{x,y},\\
&\bigl(S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\bigr)^{2}+\bigl(S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\bigr)^{2}=S(S+1){\textrm{Id}},\end{split}

(2.1)

for all
x,y\in\Lambda
. It can be shown that all these operators have eigenvalues
\{-S,\dots,S\}
. For
S=\frac{1}{2}
, these operators are given by (half) the Pauli matrices in the basis where
\{S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\}
are diagonal, namely

\begin{split}&S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\otimes{\textrm{Id}}_{\Lambda\setminus\{x\}},\\
&S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}0&-{\textrm{i}}\\
{\textrm{i}}&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\otimes{\textrm{Id}}_{\Lambda\setminus\{x\}},\\
&S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\otimes{\textrm{Id}}_{\Lambda\setminus\{x\}}.\end{split}

(2.2)

These expressions are adapted to the tensor product structure

\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}=\otimes_{x\in\Lambda}{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Using the
interpretation
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}={\textrm{span}}(\Sigma^{\Lambda})
instead, we can
define the spin operators as follows. Given
\sigma_{\Lambda}\in\Sigma^{\Lambda}
, let
\sigma_{\Lambda}^{(x)}

denote the configuration that is equal to
\sigma_{\Lambda}
, except at

x
where the value has been flipped; then

\begin{split}&S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}|\sigma_{\Lambda}\rangle=|\sigma_{\Lambda}^{(x)}\rangle,\\
&S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\sigma_{\Lambda}\rangle={\textrm{i}}\sigma_{x}|\sigma_{\Lambda}^{(x)}\rangle,\\
&S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}|\sigma_{\Lambda}\rangle=\sigma_{x}|\sigma_{\Lambda}\rangle.\end{split}

(2.3)

Here
|\sigma_{\Lambda}\rangle
is the vector in
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
that corresponds to
\sigma_{\Lambda}
.
The Hamiltonian of the system is the operator

H_{\Lambda,h}=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}J_{x-y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-h\sum_{x\in\Lambda}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}

(2.4)

and the partition function is

Z(\Lambda,\beta,h)={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,h}}\,.

(2.5)

The functions
J_{x-y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
are called coupling parameters or
coupling constants. We always assume that they are real and symmetric,
J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=J_{-x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\in{\mathbb{R}}
for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
and
x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
.

We define the finite-volume free energy by

f_{\Lambda}(\beta,h)=-\frac{1}{\beta|\Lambda|}\log Z(\Lambda,\beta,h),\qquad f_{\Lambda}(\infty,h)=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}f_{\Lambda}(\beta,h).

(2.6)

The limit
\beta\to\infty
exists and it corresponds to a trace in the eigenspace
for
H_{\Lambda,h}
with lowest eigenvalue (the ground-state energy).
As is well-known, we can take the limit of large volumes and we obtain
a thermodynamic potential. This is a pillar of Statistical
Mechanics. For this, we recall the notion of "van Hove sequences". We
say that the sequence of finite domains
(\Lambda_{n})
tends to

{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}

in the sense of van Hove
,
written
\Lambda_{n}\Uparrow\mathbb{Z}^{d}
, if

(i)

it is increasing, i.e.
\Lambda_{n}\subset\Lambda_{n+1}
;

(ii)

it invades
{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
, i.e.
\cup_{n}\Lambda_{n}={\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
;

(iii)

its ratio boundary/volume tends to zero,
i.e.
\frac{|\{x\in\Lambda_{n}:{\textrm{dist}}(x,\Lambda_{n}^{\textrm{c}})=1\}|}{|\Lambda_{n}|}\to 0
.

Theorem 2.1
.

Assume that

\sum_{x\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}|J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}|<\infty

for all
i\in\{1,2,3\}
. Then there exists a function

f(\beta,h)
where
0\leq\beta\leq\infty
and
h\in{\mathbb{R}}
, such that for
any van Hove sequence
(\Lambda_{n})
, we have

f(\beta,h)=\lim_{n\to\infty}f_{\Lambda_{n}}(\beta,h).

Convergence is locally uniform. Further, the function
f(\beta,h)
is
concave and even in
h
.

We refer to
[
42
,
12
]
for the proof of this theorem.
For finite
\beta
, the function
\beta f(\beta,h)
is
jointly concave in
(\beta,\beta h)
(but here we only use the
concavity in
h
).
It is also
well-known that
f(\beta,h)
is smooth when
\beta
is small — it is
actually analytic.

In what follows we need to consider periodic boundary
conditions. Given
\ell\in{\mathbb{N}}
, let
\Lambda_{\ell}=\{0,1,\dots,\ell-1\}^{d}
, and let the Hamiltonian

H_{\Lambda_{\ell},h}^{\textrm{per}}
be given by Eq. (
2.4
), but
with coupling parameters replaced by the following periodised ones:

J_{x,{\textrm{per}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=\sum_{z\in{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}J_{x+\ell z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}.

(2.7)

We can define the periodised partition function
Z^{\textrm{per}}(\Lambda_{\ell},\beta,h)
accordingly, and the free energy

f^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda_{\ell}}(\beta,h)=-\frac{1}{\beta\ell^{d}}\log Z^{\textrm{per}}(\Lambda_{\ell},\beta,h).

(2.8)

As
\ell\to\infty
, these free energies converge to the free
energies
f(\beta,h)
of Theorem
2.1
.

We introduce the finite-volume equilibrium states

\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,h}=\frac{{\operatorname{Tr\,}}[\cdot\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,h}}\,]}{Z_{\Lambda,\beta,h}},\qquad\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Lambda,\infty,h}=\lim_{\beta\to\infty}\langle\cdot\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,h}.

(2.9)

We also consider the states
\langle\cdot\rangle^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda_{\ell},\beta,h}
with periodic boundary conditions, where we use
H^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda_{\ell},h}
instead of
H_{\Lambda_{\ell},h}
.
In Section
3
we make several assumptions on the coupling
constants and we show that the system then exhibits
long-range
order
at low temperature, in the sense that there exists a lower
bound
c
such that

\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{2}}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}\langle S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,0}\geq c>0,

(2.10)

where
c
is positive uniformly in the volume
\Lambda
. As for the
domains
\Lambda
, the statement is relevant if it holds for all
domains in a van Hove sequence.

In order to motivate the importance of this property, we show that it implies the occurrence of a first-order phase transition as
h
crosses 0. This also implies that there exist many distinct Gibbs
states at
(\beta,0)
.

Theorem 2.2
.

We assume that the system displays long-range order in the form of Eq. (
2.10
).
Then

\frac{\partial}{\partial h}f(\beta,h)\Big|_{h=0-}\;>\;0\;>\;\frac{\partial}{\partial h}f(\beta,h)\Big|_{h=0+}.

Beweis.

We first give a simplified proof in the case where
[H_{\Lambda,h},M_{\Lambda}]=0
, where
M_{\Lambda}
is the magnetisation operator

M_{\Lambda}=\sum_{x\in\Lambda}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{x}.

(2.11)

For the general case, we will use a result of Koma and Tasaki
[
28
]
.

Let
|M_{\Lambda}|
be the unique positive semi-definite square root of
M_{\Lambda}^{2}
.
We have
|M_{\Lambda}|\leq|\Lambda|S\,{\textrm{Id}}
, so that
M_{\Lambda}^{2}\leq|\Lambda|S|M_{\Lambda}|
. Since Gibbs states are positive linear functionals, we get

\Bigl\langle\frac{M_{\Lambda}^{2}}{|\Lambda|^{2}}\Bigr\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,0}\leq S\Bigl\langle\frac{|M_{\Lambda}|}{|\Lambda|}\Bigr\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,0}.

(2.12)

Long-range order implies that
\frac{1}{|\Lambda|^{2}}\langle M_{\Lambda}^{2}\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,0}\geq c
, so the right side above is positive.

In order to get an inequality for the derivative of the free energy, let us introduce
\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,h)
to be the free energy of the model with Hamiltonian

\tilde{H}_{\Lambda,h}=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}J_{x-y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-h|M_{\Lambda}|.

(2.13)

We now check that
\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,h)

converges as
\Lambda\Uparrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}
to the free energy
f(\beta,h)

for
h\geq 0
. For this, notice that
M_{\Lambda}
(and
|M_{\Lambda}|
) commute with
H_{\Lambda,0}=\tilde{H}_{\Lambda,0}
. For
h\geq 0
we have the inequalities (for the second one, observe that the spectrum of
M_{\Lambda}
is symmetric around 0)

{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,\beta,0}+\beta hM_{\Lambda}}\,\leq{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,\beta,0}+\beta h|M_{\Lambda}|}\,\leq 2\,{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,\beta,0}+\beta hM_{\Lambda}}\,.

(2.14)

Taking the logarithm and dividing by
\beta|\Lambda|
, and
taking the relevant limits, we get that
f
and
\tilde{f}
are equal.

We now use the concavity in
h
of
\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}
and the fact
that

\sup_{n}(\liminf_{m}a_{m,n})\leq\liminf_{m}(\sup_{n}a_{m,n})
and we get

\begin{split}\frac{\partial}{\partial h}f(\beta,h)\Big|_{h=0+}\!\!&=\sup_{h>0}\frac{f(\beta,h)-f(\beta,0)}{h}=\sup_{h>0}\liminf_{\Lambda\Uparrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,h)-\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,0)}{h}\\
&\leq\liminf_{\Lambda\Uparrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\sup_{h>0}\frac{\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,h)-\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,0)}{h}=\liminf_{\Lambda\Uparrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\frac{\partial}{\partial h}\tilde{f}_{\Lambda}(\beta,h)\Big|_{h=0}\\
&=\liminf_{\Lambda\Uparrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Bigl\langle-\frac{|M_{\Lambda}|}{|\Lambda|}\Bigr\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,0}.\end{split}

(2.15)

The last expectation is with respect to the Gibbs state with Hamiltonian
\tilde{H}_{\Lambda,0}=H_{\Lambda,0}
.
The right side
is positive and
\frac{\partial}{\partial h}f(\beta,h)\big|_{h=0+}
is indeed negative. Since
f
is even in
h
we get the other inequality as well.

When
M_{\Lambda}
does not commute with the Hamiltonian, it does not seem possible to show that
f
and
\tilde{f}
are equal. But since right-derivatives of concave functions are right-continuous, we can proceed as above and get

\frac{\partial}{\partial h}f(\beta,h)\Big|_{h=0+}=\lim_{h^{\prime}\to 0+}\frac{\partial}{\partial h}f(\beta,h)\Big|_{h=h^{\prime}+}\leq\lim_{h^{\prime}\to 0+}\liminf_{\Lambda\Uparrow{\mathbb{Z}}^{d}}\Bigl\langle-\frac{M_{\Lambda}}{|\Lambda|}\Bigr\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,h^{\prime}}.

(2.16)

Koma and Tasaki
[
28
]
have proved that long-range order (in the sense of (
2.10
)) implies that the right side is strictly negative.
∎

We now discuss spin rotations. They show that Hamiltonians with different couplings are related by a unitary transformation, which allows to make assumptions on the couplings without loss of generality.
The following lemma applies to spin operators in
{\mathbb{C}}^{2S+1}
, and
immediately extends to tensor products.
Given
\vec{a}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, let

S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a})}=\vec{a}\cdot\vec{S}=a_{1}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+a_{2}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}+a_{3}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}.

(2.17)

By linearity, the commutation relations (
2.1
) generalise as

[S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a})},S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{b})}]={\textrm{i}}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})}.

(2.18)

Finally, let
R_{\vec{a}}\vec{b}
denote the vector
\vec{b}
rotated around
\vec{a}
by the angle
\|\vec{a}\|
.

Lemma 2.3
.

\,{\textrm{e}}^{-{\textrm{i}}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a})}}\,S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{b})}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a})}}\,=S^{\scriptscriptstyle(R_{\vec{a}}\vec{b})}.

Beweis.

We replace
\vec{a}
by
s\vec{a}
, and we check that both sides of the identity satisfy the same differential equation. We find

\frac{{\textrm{d}}}{{\textrm{d}}s}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-{\textrm{i}}S^{(s\vec{a})}}\,S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{b})}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}S^{(s\vec{a})}}\,=-{\textrm{i}}[S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a})},\,{\textrm{e}}^{-{\textrm{i}}S^{(s\vec{a})}}\,S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{b})}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}S^{(s\vec{a})}}\,],

(2.19)

and

\frac{{\textrm{d}}}{{\textrm{d}}s}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(R_{s\vec{a}}\vec{b})}=\Bigl(\frac{{\textrm{d}}}{{\textrm{d}}s}R_{s\vec{a}}\vec{b}\Bigr)\cdot\vec{S}=\Bigl(\vec{a}\times R_{s\vec{a}}\vec{b}\Bigr)\cdot\vec{S}=-{\textrm{i}}[S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\vec{a})},S^{\scriptscriptstyle(R_{s\vec{a}}\vec{b})}].

(2.20)

We used (
2.18
) for the last identity.

Here
|z|
denotes the sum of the coordinates of
z\in\mathbb{Z}^{d}
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\rho(1),\rho(2),\rho(3)
be a permutation of

and let

\sigma_{1},\sigma_{2},\sigma_{3}\in\{-1,+1\}
satisfy
\sigma_{1}\sigma_{2}\sigma_{3}=+1
. Let

H_{\Lambda,h}
be as in (

) and let

\tilde{H}_{\Lambda,h}=-\sum_{i=1}^{3}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}\tilde{J}_{x-y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}-\sum_{x\in\Lambda}\tilde{h}_{x}S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\rho^{-1}(3))}_{x}

(2.21)

where

\tilde{J}_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=(\sigma_{\rho(i)})^{|x|}J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(\rho(i))}

and
\tilde{h}_{x}=(\sigma_{\rho^{-1}(3)})^{|x|}h

Then for any finite

{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,h}}\,={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta\tilde{H}_{\Lambda,h}}\,.

(2.22)

Using Proposition
2.4

one may transfer results on non-differentiability of the

Beweis.

For any unitary matrix
U
acting on
\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}
, we have

{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,h}}\,={\operatorname{Tr\,}}[U^{\ast}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,h}}\,U]={\operatorname{Tr\,}}[\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta U^{\ast}H_{\Lambda,h}U}\,].

(2.23)

Consider

of the form

(2.24)

By combining rotations by an angle

, we can choose

such that

, where the

.

3
Long-range order

We state two results about long-range order. The first theorem holds
for a larger class of coupling constants and for
S
large enough. The
second theorem is restricted to nearest-neighbour interactions, but it
has the advantage of applying to more values of
S
and more dimensions.
To briefly summarise the consequences of those results,
we will see that long-range order (in
the form (
2.10
)) holds under the following conditions:

—

for certain long-range interactions (specified below)
if
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
for some
\beta_{0}<\infty

provided
d\geq 3
and
S
is large enough, or

—

for nearest-neighbour interactions
if
\beta\geq\beta_{0}
for some
\beta_{0}<\infty

provided
d\geq 3
and
S\geq\frac{1}{2}
, or

—

for nearest-neighbour interactions
in the ground-state
\beta=\infty

provided
d\geq 2
and either
S\geq 1
, or

S\geq\tfrac{1}{2}
and
-J^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}/J^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\leq 0.13
.

The original results dealt with nearest-neighbour interactions

[
18
,
10
]
. Then Fröhlich, Israel, Lieb, and Simon formulated a
more abstract notion of reflection positive interactions
[
15
]
. Here we consider two cases that fit the setting;
we provide explicit proofs for each, see Lemma
5.2
.
Explicitly, we consider interactions of the following forms:

—

Nearest neighbour:
J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=0
unless
\|x\|_{1}=1
, in
which case it equals some constant
J^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
;

—

Fourier transform:

J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}{\textrm{d}}\nu^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}(k)\,{\textrm{e}}^{ik\cdot x}\,

where
\nu^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
is a positive, finite measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

The latter case allows us to include these
examples:

—

J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}=a^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-b^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\|x\|_{p}^{p}}\,

for
p\in(0,2]
and
constants
a^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\in\mathbb{R}
,
b^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}>0
. Indeed,
this follows from the fact that the
characteristic function of a
stable distribution

in probability theory is of the form
\,{\textrm{e}}^{-c|t|^{p}}\,
.
(For
p>2
this is not possible
as the positivity of
\nu
would be violated.) See e.g.
[
9
]
.

—

4
Infrared bounds

This section explores estimates of the Fourier
transform of correlations and their
consequences. Such estimates are particularly relevant at small
Fourier parameters; this corresponds to large wavelengths, i.e. the
infrared spectrum for light, hence the name given by physicists.

We need to introduce the conventions about the Fourier transform used
in this survey.
Recall that
\Lambda_{\ell}^{*}=\tfrac{2\pi}{\ell}\bigl\{-\tfrac{\ell}{2}+1,\dots,\tfrac{\ell}{2}\bigr\}^{d}
.
The Fourier transform of a function
f:\Lambda_{\ell}\to{\mathbb{C}}
is

\widehat{f}(k)=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-{\textrm{i}}kx}\,f(x),\qquad k\in\Lambda_{\ell}^{*},

(4.1)

where we write
kx
for the usual inner product
\sum_{i=1}^{d}k_{i}x_{i}
.
One can check that the inverse relation is then

f(x)=\frac{1}{\ell^{d}}\sum_{k\in\Lambda_{\ell}^{*}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}kx}\,\widehat{f}(k).

(4.2)

Note that
{\varepsilon}(k)=\widehat{J}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}(0)-\widehat{J}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}(k)
.

The first infrared bound involves the Duhamel correlation function
\eta(x)
, defined by

\eta(x)=\frac{1}{\beta}\frac{1}{Z_{\textrm{per}}(\Lambda_{\ell},\beta,h)}\int_{0}^{\beta}{\textrm{d}}s\;{\operatorname{Tr\,}}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda,h}}\,S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(\beta-s)H^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda,h}}\,.

(4.3)

The method of reflection positivity allows us to establish the following
infrared bound.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
h=0
and
\ell
be even. Assume that the coupling constants
J^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
satisfy the
assumptions of Theorem
3.1
.
Then

\widehat{\eta}(k)\leq\frac{1}{2\beta{\varepsilon}(k)},\qquad\mbox{ for all }k\in\Lambda_{\ell}^{*}\setminus\{0\}.

The proof of this lemma can be found at the end of Section
5
.

4.1
Falk–Bruch inequality

We cannot use the infrared bound directly on the Duhamel function because of a lack of suitable lower bound for
\eta(0)
. The way out is to derive another bound on the ordinary correlation function. This can be done using the Falk–Bruch inequality, which was proposed independently in
[
11
]
and
[
10
]
.

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable Hilbert space,
H
a bounded hermitian operator such that
{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-H}\,<\infty
, and let
{\mathcal{B}}
denote the space of bounded operators on
\mathcal{H}
. We define the
Duhamel inner product
in
{\mathcal{B}}
by

(A,B)=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{0}^{1}{\textrm{d}}s\;{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,A^{*}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,B,\qquad A,B\in{\mathcal{B}},

(4.4)

with
Z={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-H}\,
. We have

\begin{split}\frac{{\textrm{d}}}{{\textrm{d}}s}{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,A^{*}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,B&={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,[H,A^{*}]\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,B\\
&={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,A^{*}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,[B,H],\end{split}

(4.5)

and we obtain the useful identity

\bigl([A,H],B\bigr)=\bigl(A,[B,H]\bigr).

(4.6)

Further,

\bigl(A,[B,H]\bigr)=\frac{1}{Z}\int_{0}^{1}{\textrm{d}}s\,\frac{{\textrm{d}}}{{\textrm{d}}s}{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,A^{*}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,B=\bigl\langle[B,A^{*}]\bigr\rangle

(4.7)

where

\langle\cdot\rangle=\frac{1}{Z}{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\cdot\,{\textrm{e}}^{-H}\,.

(4.8)

For a given
A\in{\mathcal{B}}
, let us introduce the function
F(s)={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,A^{*}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,A
. We have

\frac{{\textrm{d}}^{2}}{{\textrm{d}}s^{2}}F(s)={\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-(1-s)H}\,[A,H]^{*}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-sH}\,[A,H]\geq 0

(4.9)

(positivity can be shown by casting the right side in the form
{\operatorname{Tr\,}}B^{*}B
). The function
F(s)
is therefore convex. Then

\tfrac{1}{2}\langle A^{*}A+AA^{*}\rangle=\frac{1}{2Z}\bigl(F(0)+F(1)\bigr)\geq\frac{1}{Z}\int_{0}^{1}F(s){\textrm{d}}s=(A,A)

(4.10)

with equality if and only if
[A,H]=0
. The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality
of the Duhamel inner product (
4.4
) gives

\bigl|\bigl(A,[B,H]\bigr)\bigr|^{2}\leq(A,A)\;\bigl([B,H],[B,H]\bigr).

(4.11)

Using Eq. (
4.7
) to write the Duhamel inner product of
commutators as expectations in the state
\langle\cdot\rangle
, and
the inequalities (
4.10
) and (
4.11
) as
well as cyclicity of the trace, we get
Bogolubov’s inequality

\bigl|\bigl\langle[B,A^{*}]\bigr\rangle\bigr|^{2}\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\langle A^{*}A+AA^{*}\rangle\;\bigl\langle\bigl[[B,H],B^{*}\bigr]\bigr\rangle.

(4.12)

Inequality (
4.10
) gives an upper bound for the Duhamel inner product, but we actually need a lower bound. For this, we consider the function

\Phi(s)=\sqrt{s}\coth\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{s}}.

(4.13)

This function is increasing, concave, and is depicted in Fig.
1
. One can check that

\sqrt{s}\leq\Phi(s)\leq\sqrt{s}+s.

(4.14)

Abbildung 1:
The function
\Phi
of the Falk–Bruch inequality.

Lemma 4.2

(Falk–Bruch inequality)
.

For all
A\in{\mathcal{B}}
such that the denominators differ from zero, we have

\frac{2\langle A^{*}A+AA^{*}\rangle}{\bigl\langle\bigl[A^{*},[H,A]\bigr]\bigr\rangle}\leq\Phi\biggl(\frac{4(A,A)}{\bigl\langle\bigl[A^{*},[H,A]\bigr]\bigr\rangle}\biggr).

It is worth noting that the double commutator is nonnegative, as can be seen from Eq. (
4.7
). Indeed, taking
A\mapsto[A^{*},H]
and
B\mapsto A^{*}
, we can express it using the Duhamel inner product as

\bigl\langle\bigl[A^{*},[H,A]\bigr]\bigr\rangle=\bigl([A^{*},H],[A^{*},H]\bigr)\geq 0.

(4.15)

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

Recall the function
F(s)
defined before (
4.9
). The Falk–Bruch inequality can be written as

2\frac{F(0)+F(1)}{F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)}\leq\Phi\biggl(\frac{4\int_{0}^{1}F(s){\textrm{d}}s}{F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)}\biggr).

(4.16)

If
\{\varphi_{j}\}
is an orthonormal set of eigenvectors of
H
with eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}
, we can write

F(s)=\sum_{i,j}\bigl|(\varphi_{i},A\varphi_{j})\bigr|^{2}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\lambda_{j}}\,\,{\textrm{e}}^{(\lambda_{j}-\lambda_{i})s}\,=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\,{\textrm{e}}^{st}\,{\textrm{d}}\mu(t),

(4.17)

where
\mu
is a positive measure. We have

\begin{split}&F(0)+F(1)=\int(\,{\textrm{e}}^{t}\,+1){\textrm{d}}\mu(t),\\
&F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)=\int t(\,{\textrm{e}}^{t}\,-1){\textrm{d}}\mu(t),\\
&\int_{0}^{1}F(s){\textrm{d}}s=\int\frac{\,{\textrm{e}}^{t}\,-1}{t}{\textrm{d}}\mu(t).\end{split}

(4.18)

Let us consider the probability measure
{\textrm{d}}\nu(t)=(\int t(\,{\textrm{e}}^{t}\,-1){\textrm{d}}\mu(t))^{-1}t(\,{\textrm{e}}^{t}\,-1){\textrm{d}}\mu(t)
. We have

\begin{split}&\frac{F(0)+F(1)}{F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)}=\int\tfrac{1}{t}\coth\tfrac{t}{2}\;{\textrm{d}}\nu(t),\\
&\frac{\int F(s){\textrm{d}}s}{F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)}=\int\tfrac{1}{t^{2}}\,{\textrm{d}}\nu(t).\end{split}

(4.19)

Since
\Phi
is concave we can use Jensen’s inequality and we get (
4.16
):

\begin{split}\Phi\biggl(\frac{4\int_{0}^{1}F(s){\textrm{d}}s}{F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)}\biggr)&=\Phi\Bigl(\int\tfrac{4}{t^{2}}\,{\textrm{d}}\nu(t)\Bigr)\geq\int\Phi\bigl(\tfrac{4}{t^{2}}\bigr){\textrm{d}}\nu(t)\\
&=\int\tfrac{2}{t}\coth\tfrac{t}{2}\;{\textrm{d}}\nu(t)=2\frac{F(0)+F(1)}{F^{\prime}(1)-F^{\prime}(0)}.\end{split}

(4.20)

∎

The Falk–Bruch inequality is saturated when the measure
{\textrm{d}}\mu
is a
Dirac on a single value. This is the case if
H
is the Hamiltonian of
the harmonic oscillator, and
A
is the creation or annihilation
operator.

The following inequality follows from Lemma
4.2
and the upper bound in Eq. (

Corollary 4.3
.

We have

\tfrac{1}{2}\langle A^{*}A+AA^{*}\rangle\leq\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{(A,A)\;\bigl\langle\bigl[A^{*},[H,A]\bigr]\bigr\rangle}+(A,A).

\ell
and

One easily checks the inverse identity

\widehat{\eta}(k)=\widehat{(S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)},S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)})}(k)=\frac{1}{\ell^{d}}(\widehat{S}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)},\widehat{S}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}).

We take

(4.27)

We used the fact that operators at different sites commute, and also

\begin{split}\bigl[\widehat{S}_{-k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)},[H^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda,0},\widehat{S}_{k}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}]\bigr]&=-2{\textrm{i}}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-{\textrm{i}}kx}\,\Bigl[\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}kx}\,S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}+\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}ky}\,S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)},-J_{x-y,{\textrm{per}}}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\\

\bigl\langle\bigl[A^{*},[H,A]\bigr]\bigr\rangle=\bigl\langle\bigl[\hat{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{-k},[\beta H^{\textrm{per}}_{\Lambda,0},\hat{S}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}_{k}]\bigr]\bigr\rangle=4\beta\ell^{d}e(k).

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

We have the inequality (see Lemma

)

\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\rangle\geq\tfrac{1}{3}\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{3}\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{3}S(S+1).

(4.30)

This is where we use that

J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\geq J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\geq-J_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\geq 0
.

We now use the inverse Fourier transform on the two-point correlation
function, namely

\tfrac{1}{3}S(S+1)\leq\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\rangle=\frac{1}{\ell^{d}}\widehat{\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\rangle}(0)+\frac{1}{\ell^{d}}\sum_{k\in\Lambda_{\ell}^{*}\setminus\{0\}}\widehat{\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}\rangle}(k).

(4.31)

Notice that the first term of the right side is equal to the long-range order parameter. Then

(5.1)

Let us fix an orthonormal basis
\{e_{i}\}
on
\mathcal{H}

\overline{a}
of a bounded operator

\langle e_{i},\overline{a}e_{j}\rangle=\overline{\langle e_{i},ae_{j}\rangle}.

(5.2)

In matrix notation, that means taking the complex conjugate of its elements, without transposing as for hermitian adjoints. The reason to use the complex conjugate is that for all

\overline{a\,b}=\overline{a}\;\overline{b}.

(5.3)

Here is the key inequality that is closely related to reflection
positivity. Let
I
be an index set and
\mu
a positive, finite
measure on
I

A,C_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\textrm{left}}
and

B,D_{i}\in{\mathcal{B}}_{\textrm{right}}
for all

Lemma 5.1
.

We have

\Bigl|{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{A+B+\int C_{i}D_{i}{\textrm{d}}\mu(i)}\,\Bigr|^{2}\leq{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{A+{\mathcal{R}}\overline{A}+\int C_{i}{\mathcal{R}}\overline{C}_{i}{\textrm{d}}\mu(i)}\,\cdot{\operatorname{Tr\,}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\mathcal{R}}\overline{B}+B+\int{\mathcal{R}}\overline{D}_{i}\,D_{i}{\textrm{d}}\mu(i)}\,.

Beweis.

We use the Duhamel formula in the following form. If
A,B
are bounded operators, then

\,{\textrm{e}}^{A+B}\,=\sum_{n\geq 0}\int_{0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}<1}{\textrm{d}}t_{1}\dots{\textrm{d}}t_{n}\,{\textrm{e}}^{t_{1}A}\,B\,{\textrm{e}}^{(t_{2}-t_{1})A}\,B\dots B\,{\textrm{e}}^{(1-t_{n})A}\,.

(5.4)

In what follows, we use the shorthands

\int{\textrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{i}}\equiv\int{\textrm{d}}\mu(i_{1})\dots\int{\textrm{d}}\mu(i_{n})\qquad\text{and}\qquad\int{\textrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{t}}\equiv\int_{0<t_{1}<\dots<t_{n}<1}{\textrm{d}}t_{1}\dots{\textrm{d}}t_{n}.

(5.5)

We also write
A=a\otimes{\mathchoice{\textrm{1}\mskip-4.0mu\textrm{l}}{\textrm{1}\mskip-4.0mu\textrm{l}}{\textrm{1}\mskip-4.5mu\textrm{l}}{\textrm{1}\mskip-5.0mu\textrm{l}}}

\begin{split}&\Bigl|{\operatorname{Tr\,}}_{\mathcal{H}\otimes\mathcal{H}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{A+B+\int C_{i}D_{i}{\textrm{d}}\mu(i)}\,\Bigr|^{2}\\

&\qquad\cdot\sum_{n\geq 0}\int{\textrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{i}}\int{\textrm{d}}{\boldsymbol{t}}\;{\operatorname{Tr\,}}_{\mathcal{H}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{t_{1}\overline{b}}\,\overline{d}_{i_{1}}\dots\overline{d}_{i_{n}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{(1-t_{n})\overline{b}}\,{\operatorname{Tr\,}}_{\mathcal{H}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{t_{1}b}\,d_{i_{1}}\dots d_{i_{n}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{(1-t_{n})b}\,\\

(5.6)

We used the ordinary Cauchy–Schwarz inequality for functions, here

), we have the "Gaussian domination" bound

\frac{Z(sv)}{Z(0)}\leq\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\frac{1}{4}s^{2}\beta(v,\Delta v)}\,.

(5.20)

The derivative of
Z(sv)
with respect to
s
is equal to 0 at
s=0

because of symmetries (for instance, a rotation around the 3rd spin
axis by angle
\pi
, which takes
S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
to
-S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(i)}
,

i=1,2
, and leaves
S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
invariant). The second derivative can
be calculated e.g. using the Duhamel formula (
5.4
) and
translation-invariance.
Recalling the Duhamel correlation function
\eta
from
(
4.3
), we get

\frac{1}{Z(0)}\frac{{\textrm{d}}^{2}}{{\textrm{d}}s^{2}}Z(sv)\bigg|_{s=0}=\beta^{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda}h_{x}h_{y}\eta(x-y),

(5.21)

where we recall that
h_{x}=(\Delta v)_{x}
.
We now choose the field
v
to be

v_{x}=\cos(kx),\qquad k\in\Lambda_{\ell}^{*}.

(5.22)

Observe that
\Delta v_{x}=-{\varepsilon}(k)v_{x}
. The order
s^{2}
of the inequality (
5.20
) gives

\tfrac{1}{2}\beta^{2}{\varepsilon}(k)^{2}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)\cos(ky)\eta(x-y)\leq\tfrac{1}{4}\beta{\varepsilon}(k)\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)^{2}.

(5.23)

Since
\eta(x)
and
\widehat{\eta}(k)
are both real, the
left-hand-side satisfies

\begin{split}\sum_{x,y\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)\cos(ky)\eta(x-y)&=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)\sum_{y\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}ky}\,\eta(x-y)\\
&=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)\sum_{z\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}k(x-z)}\,\eta(z)\\
&=\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)\,{\textrm{e}}^{{\textrm{i}}kx}\,\widehat{\eta}(k)\\
&=\widehat{\eta}(k)\sum_{x\in\Lambda_{\ell}}\cos(kx)^{2}.\end{split}

(5.24)

Inserting this in Eq. (
5.23
) we obtain Lemma
4.1
.
∎

Appendix A
Correlation inequalities for quantum systems

We needed inequalities on correlation functions in different spin directions. Such inequalities go back at least to
[
29
]
. The present lemma appeared in this form in
[
19
]
. It also holds with periodic boundary conditions.

Lemma A.1
.

Assume that, for all
x,y\in\Lambda
, the coupling constants satisfy

|J_{x-y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\leq J_{x-y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}.

Then we have that

\bigl|\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,h}\bigr|\leq\langle S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\rangle_{\Lambda,\beta,h},

for all
x\in\Lambda
.

Beweis.

Let
|a\rangle
,
a\in\{-S,\dots,S\}
denote basis elements of
{\mathbb{C}}^{2S+1}
. Let the operators
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\pm)}
be defined by

\begin{split}&S^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}|a\rangle=\sqrt{S(S+1)-a(a+1)}\;|a+1\rangle,\\
&S^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)}|a\rangle=\sqrt{S(S+1)-(a-1)a}\;|a-1\rangle,\end{split}

(A.1)

with the understanding that
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}|S\rangle=S^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)}|-S\rangle=0
. Then let
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}=\frac{1}{2}(S^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}+S^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)})
,
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}=\frac{1}{2{\textrm{i}}}(S^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}-S^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)})
, and
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}|a\rangle=a|a\rangle
. It is well-known that these operators satisfy the spin commutation relations. Further, the matrix elements of
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)},S^{\scriptscriptstyle(\pm)}
are all nonnegative, and the matrix elements of
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
are all less than or equal to those of
S^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}
in absolute values. Using the Trotter formula and multiple resolutions of the identity, we have

\begin{split}&\bigl|{\operatorname{Tr\,}}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,0}}\,\bigr|\leq\lim_{N\to\infty}\sum_{\sigma_{0},\dots,\sigma_{N}\in\{-S,\dots,S\}^{\Lambda}}\biggl|\langle\sigma_{0}|S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\sigma_{1}\rangle\\
&\langle\sigma_{1}|\,{\textrm{e}}^{\frac{\beta}{N}\sum J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}\,|\sigma_{1}\rangle\langle\sigma_{1}|\Bigl(1\!+\!\tfrac{\beta}{N}\sum_{y,z\in\Lambda}(J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})\Bigr)|\sigma_{2}\rangle\\
\dots&\langle\sigma_{N}|\,{\textrm{e}}^{\frac{\beta}{N}\sum J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}}\,\!|\sigma_{N}\rangle\langle\sigma_{N}|\Bigl(1\!+\!\tfrac{\beta}{N}\!\!\sum_{y,z\in\Lambda}\!(J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\!+\!J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})\Bigr)|\sigma_{0}\rangle\biggr|.\end{split}

(A.2)

Observe that the matrix elements of all operators are nonnegative, except for
S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}
. Indeed, this follows from

\begin{split}&J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}+J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\\
&=\tfrac{1}{4}(J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\!-\!J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})(S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}\!+\!S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)})+\tfrac{1}{4}(J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\!+\!J_{y-z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)})(S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)}\!+\!S_{y}^{\scriptscriptstyle(-)}S_{z}^{\scriptscriptstyle(+)}).\end{split}

(A.3)

We get an upper bound for the right side of (
A.2
) by replacing
|\langle\sigma_{0}|S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}|\sigma_{1}\rangle|
with
\langle\sigma_{0}|S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}|\sigma_{1}\rangle
. We have obtained

\bigl|{\operatorname{Tr\,}}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(2)}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,0}}\,\bigr|\leq{\operatorname{Tr\,}}S_{0}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(1)}\,{\textrm{e}}^{-\beta H_{\Lambda,0}}\,,

(A.4)

which proves the claim. We actually set
h=0
in order to shorten the equations, but adding terms involving the
S_{x}^{\scriptscriptstyle(3)}
operators to the Hamiltonian is straightforward.
∎
