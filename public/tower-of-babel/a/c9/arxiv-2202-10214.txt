No-signalling constrains quantum computation with indefinite causal structure
Luca Apadula, Alessandro Bisio, Paolo Perinotti

Abstract
Quantum processes with indefinite causal structure emerge when we wonder which are the most general evolutions, allowed by quantum theory, of a set of local systems which are not assumed to be in any particular causal order. These processes can be described within the framework of higher-order quantum theory which, starting from considering maps from quantum transformations to quantum transformations, recursively constructs a hierarchy of quantum maps of increasingly higher order. In this work, we develop a formalism for quantum computation with indefinite causal structures; namely we characterize the computational structure of higher order quantum maps. Taking an axiomatic approach, the rules of this computation are identified as the most general compositions of higher order maps which are compatible with the mathematical structure of quantum theory. We provide a mathematical characterization of the admissible composition for arbitrary higher order quantum maps. We prove that these rules, which have a computational and information-theoretic nature, are determined by the more physical notion of the signalling relations between the quantum systems of the higher order quantum maps.

\displaystyle\overline{{\sf in}_{x}}\otimes{\sf out}_{x}\subseteq x\subseteq{\sf in}_{x}\to{\sf out}_{x}

Compositional structure of higher order
maps.

Applying a quantum channel to a state, is not the
only way to use a channel.
We can compose a channel from
A
to
B
with one from
B

to
C
to obtain a channel
from
A
to
C
. More generally, multipartite
channels can be connected only through
some of their inputs and outputs,

.

What does it mean to compose two higher order maps?
Any map of type
x\to y
can clearly be composed
with a map of type
y\to z
to obtain a map of
type
x\to z
but what about more general
composition schemes?
Let’s consider two maps

R=\begin{array}[]{l}\includegraphics[width]{Pictures_CCSHOM_mapR2.pdf}\end{array}

of type
x

they share, i.e.

.
In terms of the Choi isomorphism, this operation is
given by the so called

]

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &R*T=\operatorname{Tr}_{{\sf S}}[(R\otimes I_{{\sf B}\setminus{\sf S}})(I_{{\sf A}\setminus{\sf S}}\otimes T^{\theta_{{\sf S}}})]\\
&R\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{{\sf A}}),\;\;T\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{{\sf B}}),\end{aligned}

(8)

where
{\sf S}:={\sf A}\cup{\sf B}
is the set of systems that

is the
partial traspose of

on the systems of

{\sf S}
. Notice that for the case

R*T=R\otimes T

However, we cannot expect that any composition
between two arbitrary maps of type

and

the composition of two effects

7

7

Indeed if we

and

which has no physical interpretation
.
At the very least, we should require that
the composition between two types
x
and

is
admissible if, whenever we compose a map of type

x
with a map of type
y
we obtain something
that could be interpreted as an admissible higher
order map. This motivates the following
definition:

Definition 2
.

Let
x
and

We say that the composition

is

if

\displaystyle\begin{split}\forall R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x),\,\forall T\in{\sf T}_{1}(y),\;\exists z\mbox{ s.t. }R*T\in{\sf T}_{1}(z)\end{split}

(9)

too permissive since Equation (

)
implies that

R*T\in{\sf T}(z)
for any

and

.

Our next goal is to provide a
characterization of the composition of type that
are admissible according to Definition

2

we introduce the following slight variation of the
composition of maps.

Definition 3
.

Let

\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{H}_{A}}=\operatorname{dim}{\mathcal{H}_{B}}

8

8

The condition

We say that the

of

\displaystyle\forall R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x)\;\exists z\mbox{ s.t. }\mathcal{C}_{AB}(R):=R*\Phi_{AB}\in{\sf T}_{1}(z)

where
\Phi_{AB}:=\sum_{i,j}|ii\rangle\langle jj|\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})

Basically, this definition determines whether it

A
with system

for any map of type

, e.g.

\mathcal{C}_{AB}(R)=\begin{array}[]{l}\includegraphics[width]{Pictures_CCSHOM_contraction2.pdf}\end{array}

\displaystyle R*S=\mathcal{C}_{AA}(\mathcal{C}_{BB}(\dots(R\otimes S)\dots)

Lemma 1

The composition

Thanks to Lemma

the characaterization of

]
(see also Fig.

Proposition 3
.

Let
x
be a type and
A,B\in{\sf Ele}_{x}

If

or

\mathcal{C}_{AB}(x)

If
A\in{\sf in}_{x}
and

then

\mathcal{C}_{AB}(x)

we have that

\mathcal{C}_{AB}(R)\in{\sf T}_{1}(\widetilde{{\sf in}_{x}}\to\widetilde{{\sf out}_{x}})

where we defined
\widetilde{{\sf in}_{x}}:={\sf in}_{x}\setminus A

.

Thanks to Proposition
3
,
the admissibility of contractions (and therefore

elementary language of quantum channels: a
contraction between
A
and
B
is admissible if
and only if
A
and
B
can be connected in a
loop. This result dramatically reduces the
complexity of the problem, since we only need to
establish that the contracted maps belongs to the
type of quantum channels

\widetilde{{\sf in}_{x}}\to\widetilde{{\sf out}_{x}}
. It may seems that we
have to check this condition for all the

,
but this is not the case. Indeed, it is possible
to prove that the contraction transforms the set
of deterministic maps
{\sf T}_{1}(x)

a subspace of linear operators, to another set of
operators which is also characterized by a linear
subspace. As a consequence, a contraction is
admissible if and only if this subspace generated
by the contraction is a subspace of the one that
characterizes the type of quantum channels. Thanks
to this observation, the admissibility of a
contraction can be further simplified to a finite
combinatorial problem. The proof of these
statements requires several technical steps which
can be found in the supplemental material

]
.

One could easily extend the statement of

\mathcal{C}_{AA}(\mathcal{C}_{BB}(\dots x)\dots)

\mathcal{C}_{AA}(\mathcal{C}_{BB}(\dots R)\dots)\in{\sf T}_{1}(\tilde{{\sf in}}\to\tilde{{\sf out}})
.
As we prove in the supplemental material

]

the admissibility of a set of contraction

set of contractions is immaterial).
A necessary condition for a set of contraction to
be admissible is that each individual contraction
in the set is admissible. On the other hand,
the

5
Causal structure of higher order maps

We have seen that the admissibility condition of
the composition of higher order maps can be
traced back to the admissibility of closing
input and output of a channel
in a loop. We now wonder what is the physical
significance of the admissibility of these loops.
In particular,
we expect that this is related to how
information can flow between the quantum systems,
i.e. to the causal structure of the channel.

We remind
[
47
,
48
]
that a
multipartite quantum channel

R\in{\sf T}_{1}(A_{1}A_{2}\dots A_{n}\to B_{1}B_{2}\dots B_{m})
does not allow for signalling from
A_{i}
to

B_{j}
if and only if

\operatorname{Tr}_{\neg B_{j}}[R]=I_{A_{i}}\otimes R^{\prime}
, and we
write
A_{i}\not\rightsquigarrow_{R}B_{j}
,

9
9

9

We use the shortcut

\neg B_{j}:=B_{1}B_{2}\dots B_{j-1}B_{j+1}\dots B_{m}
. Otherwise, we say that

R\in{\sf T}_{1}(A_{1}A_{2}\dots A_{n}\to B_{1}B_{2}\dots B_{m})
does allow for signalling from
A_{i}
to
B_{j}

and we write
A_{i}\not\rightsquigarrow_{R}B_{j}
.

Intuitively, we would expect that if we have
signalling from

A
to
B
it would not be
possible to close this information flow in a loop
and therefore that the contraction of
A
with

B
is not admissible. On the other hand, this
would be possible if no information flows from

A
to
B
. As the following proposition shows, this
intuition is correct.

Proposition 4
.

Let
x
be a type,
A\in{\sf in}_{x}
, and
B\in{\sf out}_{x}
. Then we have that

\mathcal{C}_{AB}(x)\mbox{ is admissible}\iff A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B

where
A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B
means that

A\not\rightsquigarrow_{R}B
for any
R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x)
.

Proof.

First we prove that
\mathcal{C}_{AB}(x)
is
admissible if and only if

x\subseteq(B\to A)\to(\widetilde{{\sf in}}\to\widetilde{{\sf out}})
. Then, the realisation
theorem of supermaps
[
49
,
50
]
implies that

A\not\rightsquigarrow_{R}B\iff R\in{\sf T}_{1}((B\to A)\to(\widetilde{{\sf in}}\to\widetilde{{\sf out}}))
. The thesis
follows by combining these
results (see the supplemental material

[
42
]
for the details).

\,\blacksquare

Proposition
4
relates the causal and the
compositional structure of a type: the admissible
contractions are the ones that are allowed by the
causal structure.
The admissibility of a set of contractions (and
therefore of a composition)
is therefore equivalent to
verify a sequence of no-signaling
conditions.

Finally, we present a result that allows to
determine the signalling relations
between the elementary systems of type
x
from
the expression of the type itself. The proof
can be found in the supplemental material

[
42
]
.

Proposition 5
.

Let
x
be a type,
A\in{\sf in}_{x}
,

B\in{\sf out}_{x}
. Then, there exists a
unique type
y\prec x
such that

\{A,B\}\subseteq{\sf Ele}_{y}
and
y^{\prime}\prec y\implies\{A,B\}\not\subseteq{\sf Ele}_{y}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle A\in{\sf in}_{y}\implies A\rightsquigarrow_{x}B,\quad A\in{\sf out}_{y}\implies A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B.

(12)

Thanks to Proposition
5
we
have an efficient (i.e. polynomial in the size of the
type) algorithm for determining whether or not a
type
x
allows for signalling between

A
and
B
:
i)
find
y
by
computing the smallest substring of the type
x

which contains both
A
and
B
and has balanced
brackets;
ii)
Compute
K_{y}(A)
and
K_{x}(A)

(see Prop.
2
); then
x
allows
signalling form
A
to
B
if and only if

K_{y}(A)=K_{x}(A)
.

6
Discussion

We extended
higher order quantum theory making it into a
higher order
computation
in which the
object of the framework can be composed together.
The construction and the results that we derived
have been grounded on
assumptions which translate
the requirement that the probabilistic structure
of quantum theory should be preserved.

In particular, no notions of local interventions
or of causal
order between parties has been used. However, somewhat
surprinsingly, the
compositional
structure of higher order
maps is intimately related with the causal
relations among the systems that constitute a
given type, since it is the signalling structure
that dictates whether a given contraction is
admissible or not.

Our results focused on the
signalling relation between couples of systems,
but the compositional structure generally involves
more systems at the same time. It is therefore
interesting to deepen our understanding of the
relation between causal and compositional
structure in this more general case:
how does a contraction modify the causal structure
of a type? Is it possible to retrieve this
information only from the expression of the type itself?

A further line of development of this research is
the generalization of the framework beyond quantum
theory itself. Since the axioms of
higher order quantum theory do not rely on the
mathematical structure of quantum theory

10
10

10

The most important assumption is
the Choi isomorphism, that can be always
provided in theories where local
discriminability holds.
, one could wonder what
would emerge if we replaced the fundamental level
of hierarchy with a general operational
probabilistic theory. Since in a general
operational theory causality and non-signalling
exhibits a finer structure, we could expect that
these features propagates and maybe get emphasized
throughout the hierarchy. This could inform the
search for principles that single out quantum
mechanics and deepen our understanding of quantum
computing.

Every finite dimensional quantum system

A.2
Higher order maps

We now relate the hierarchy of types to the linear
maps on Hilbert spaces.
We start from the following preliminary
definition, which is still devoid of any physical
content but it is useful in order to develop the formalism

Definition 6

(Generalized map)
.

If
x
is a type, the set of generalized maps of type
x

{\sf T}_{\mathbb{R}}(x)

•

{\sf T}_{\mathbb{R}}(A):=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A})
.

•

if
x

, is a generalized map

M

(x\to y)
.

Since we are describing linear map in terms of
their Choi operator, the choice of an orthonormal
basis is understood.
The following characterization easily follows from the previous definition

Lemma 5

(Characterization of generalized maps)
.

Let
x
be a type. Then

{\sf T}_{\mathbb{R}}(x)=\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{x})

\mathcal{H}_{x}:=\bigotimes_{i}\mathcal{H}_{i}

\mathcal{H}_{i}

Proof.

See Ref.
[
12
]

\,\blacksquare

Clearly, given two quantum systems

Our goal is to generalise Kraus axiomatic
characterization of quantum operation to map of
arbitrary type, i.e. a map is admissible if it
preserves the probabilistic structure of quantum
theory. In Kraus’ axiomatic characterization the
requirements that translates the compatibility

When applying this idea to maps of arbitrary type, the non

Appendix D
Compositional structure of Higher order maps

In the following section we provide a notion of composition for types and we study
the relevant structure involved.
Let us consider two types
x
and
y
.
As we did in the previous section we will assume
that the same non-trivial elemntary system does not appear twice in the
expression of the same type.
However, the same elementary system can appear
both in the expressions of
x
and
y
, i.e. the intersection

{\sf Ele}_{x}\cap{\sf Ele}_{y}
can be non empty.
Then,
to compose a map of type
x
with a map of type

y
,
means to connect the systems of
x
and
y
which
have the same label.

Definition 16

(Admissible type composition)
.

Let
x
,
y
be two types and let us define the
set

{\sf H}:={\sf Ele}_{x}\cap{\sf Ele}_{y}
.
We say that the composition
x\ast y
is admissible if

\displaystyle\forall R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x),\;\forall S\in{\sf T}_{1}(y)\quad\exists z\;\textit{s.t.}\;R\ast S\in{\sf T}_{1}(z).

(55)

where

\displaystyle R\ast S=\operatorname{Tr}_{{\sf H}}[(R\otimes I_{{\sf Ele}_{y}\setminus{\sf H}})(S^{T_{{\sf H}}}\otimes I_{{\sf Ele}_{x}\setminus{\sf H}})],

(56)

\operatorname{Tr}_{{\sf H}}
denotes the partial trace on
the Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{{\sf H}}:=\bigotimes_{j\in{\sf H}}\mathcal{H}_{Aj}

and
S^{T_{{\sf H}}}
the partial trasposition
with respect to the basis which has been choosed in the
definition of the Choi operator on
the space
\mathcal{H}_{{\sf H}}
.

The operation defined in Equation (
56
)
is known as the
link product
[
11
]

of
R
and
S
. It can be shown that the following
properties hold

\displaystyle R*S=S*R\quad R*(S*T)=(R*S)*T

(57)

\displaystyle R\geq 0,S\geq 0\implies R*S\geq 0.

(58)

One could be tempted to modify Equation
(
55
)
as follows:

\displaystyle\forall R\in{\sf T}(x),\;\forall S\in{\sf T}(y)\quad\exists z\;\textit{s.t.}\;R\ast S\in{\sf T}(z).

(59)

However, Equation (
59
)
would allow for some non-physical composition.
For instance,
let us consider the elementary types
x={A}
and

y=AB
and two arbitrary
the deterministic states
\rho\in T_{1}(A)
and

\sigma\in T_{1}(AB)
.
Since
\sigma*\rho\geq 0
and

\operatorname{Tr}[\sigma*\rho]\leq 0

we have that
\sigma*\rho\in T(A)
for any

\rho\in T_{1}(A)
and

\sigma\in T_{1}(AB)
.
According to Equation (
59
)
that would mean that
A*AB
is an admissible
type composition which is clearly not the case.

According to Equation (
55
),
a composition is admissible if and only if it well
behaves on the set of
deterministic
maps.
The following lemma proves that this condition
implies that the set of probabilistic map is also
preserved.

Lemma 10
.

Let
x,y
be two types such that the composition

x*y
is admissible. Then we have

\forall R\in{\sf T}(x),\;\forall S\in{\sf T}(y)\quad\exists z\;\textit{s.t.}\;R\ast S\in{\sf T}(z),

(60)

Proof.

Let us consider
R\in{\sf T}(x)
and
S\in{\sf T}(y)
two generic
probabilistic maps. Then
\exists R^{\prime}\in{\sf T}_{1}(x),S^{\prime}\in{\sf T}_{1}(y)

such that
R^{\prime}\geq R
and
S^{\prime}\geq S
. Moreover, we can find
\widetilde{R}\in{\sf T}(x)
,
\widetilde{S}\in{\sf T}(y)
which satisfies
R+\widetilde{R}=R^{\prime}
and
S+\widetilde{S}=S^{\prime}
respectively.
According to Equation (
55
),
\exists z
such that
R^{\prime}\ast S^{\prime}\in{\sf T}_{1}(z)

R^{\prime}\ast S^{\prime}=R\ast S+R\ast\widetilde{S}+\widetilde{R}\ast S+\widetilde{R}\ast\widetilde{S}.

Therefor
R\ast S\leq R^{\prime}\ast S^{\prime}
and
R\ast S\in{\sf T}(z)
follows.

\,\blacksquare

We will now prove a collection of results which
provide a characterization of the admissible
compositions.
The first one shows that we can without loss of
generality assume that the type
z
in Equation
(
55
) is of the kind
{\sf in}_{z}\to{\sf out}_{z}
.

Lemma 11
.

Let
x
and
y
be two types. Then
the composition
x\ast y
is admissible
if and only if for any
R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x)
and
S\in{\sf T}_{1}(y)
there exist two disjoint set of
non-trivial elementary types
{\sf in}_{z},{\sf out}_{z}\subseteq({\sf Ele}_{x}\cup{\sf Ele}_{y})\setminus({\sf Ele}_{x}\cap{\sf Ele}_{y})
,

{\sf in}_{z}\cap{\sf out}_{z}=\emptyset

such that

R\ast S\in{\sf T}_{1}({\sf in}_{z}\to{\sf out}_{z})
.

Proof.

From Proposition
8
we
know that
z\subseteq{\sf in}_{z}\to{\sf out}_{z}
.
Therefore, we can replace
z
with
{\sf in}_{z}\to{\sf out}_{z}
in Equation (
55
).

\,\blacksquare

The following lemma shows that a necessary
condition for a composition to be admissible
is that we must connect either input systems of
x

with output systems of
y

or output systems of
x

with input systems of
y
.

Lemma 12
.

Let
x
and
y
be two types and let us denote
with
{\sf H}:={\sf Ele}_{x}\cap{\sf Ele}_{y}

the set of non-trivial elementary systems that

x
and
y
have in common.
If the composition
x\ast y
is admissible
then,
{\sf H}\cap({\sf out}_{x}\cap{\sf out}_{y})={\sf H}\cap({\sf in}_{x}\cap{\sf in}_{y})=\emptyset
.

Proof.

First, we will prove that

{\sf H}\cap({\sf out}_{x}\cap{\sf out}_{y})=\emptyset
.
By contradiction, let us assume that there exist
A\in{\sf H}\cap({\sf out}_{x}\cap{\sf out}_{y})
.
Let us define
{\sf out}^{\prime}_{x}:={\sf out}_{x}\setminus A
and

{\sf out}^{\prime}_{y}:={\sf out}_{y}\setminus A
and let us consider
the higher order maps

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} R=I_{{\sf in}_{x}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{A}\otimes\frac{1}{d_{{\sf out}^{\prime}_{x}}}I_{{\sf out}^{\prime}_{x}}\\
S=I_{{\sf in}_{y}}\otimes{|1\rangle\langle 1|}_{A}\otimes\frac{1}{d_{{\sf out}^{\prime}_{y}}}I_{{\sf out}^{\prime}_{y}}.\end{aligned}

(61)

where
I_{{\sf J}}
denotes the identity
operator
on
\mathcal{H}_{{\sf J}}
and
|0\rangle,|1\rangle

are two orthonormal states of system
A
.
It is straightforward to verify that

R\in{\sf T}_{1}(\overline{{\sf out}_{x}\to{\sf in}_{x}})\subseteq{\sf T}_{1}(x)

and

R\in{\sf T}_{1}(\overline{{\sf out}_{y}\to{\sf in}_{y}})\subseteq{\sf T}_{1}(y)
.
Since
x*y
is admissible there must exist a type

z
such that
R*S\in{\sf T}_{1}(z)
.
On the other hand, from a straightforward computation
we have that
R*S=0
.

Let us now prove

{\sf H}\cap({\sf in}_{x}\cap{\sf in}_{y})=\emptyset
.
By contradiction, let us assume that

{\sf K}:={\sf H}\cap({\sf in}_{x}\cap{\sf in}_{y})\neq\emptyset
.
Let us define
{\sf in}^{\prime}_{x}:={\sf in}_{x}\setminus{\sf K}
and

{\sf in}^{\prime}_{y}:={\sf in}_{y}\setminus{\sf K}
and let us consider
the maps

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &R=I_{{\sf in}^{\prime}_{x}}\otimes I_{{\sf K}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{{\sf out}_{x}}\\
&S=I_{{\sf in}^{\prime}_{y}}\otimes I_{{\sf K}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{{\sf out}_{y}}\end{aligned}

(62)

\displaystyle{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{{\sf A}}:=\bigotimes_{i\in{\sf A}}{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{i}.

It is straightforward to verify that

R\in{\sf T}_{1}(\overline{{\sf out}_{x}\to{\sf in}_{x}})\subseteq{\sf T}_{1}(x)
and

R\in{\sf T}_{1}(\overline{{\sf out}_{y}\to{\sf in}_{y}})\subseteq{\sf T}_{1}(y)
. Since

x*y
is admissible there must exist a type

z
such that

R*S\in{\sf T}_{1}(z)\subseteq{\sf T}_{1}({\sf in}_{z}\to{\sf out}_{z})
. Therefore, we must have

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{{\sf out}_{z}}R*S=I_{{\sf in}_{z}}.

(63)

By
a direct computation we have

\displaystyle R*S=d_{{\sf K}}\,I_{\alpha}\otimes I_{\beta}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\gamma}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\delta}

(64)

where we defined the sets

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \alpha&:=\Big(({\sf in}^{\prime}_{x}\cup{\sf in}^{\prime}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}\Big)\cap{\sf in}_{z}\\
\beta&:=\Big(({\sf in}^{\prime}_{x}\cup{\sf in}^{\prime}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}\Big)\cap{\sf out}_{z}\\
\gamma&:=\Big(({\sf out}_{x}\cup{\sf out}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}\Big)\cap{\sf in}_{z}\\
\delta&:=\Big(({\sf out}_{x}\cup{\sf out}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}\Big)\cap{\sf out}_{z}.\end{aligned}

(65)

Equation (
63
) then becomes

\displaystyle d_{{\sf K}}d_{\beta}I_{\alpha}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\gamma}=I_{\alpha}\otimes I_{\gamma}

(66)

which implies
I_{\gamma}\propto{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\gamma}
, i.e.
d_{\gamma}=1
. Howevere, since

\gamma
is a collection of non-trivial
elementary types it must be
\gamma=\emptyset
.
Then Equation (
66
) becomes

d_{{\sf K}}d_{\beta}I_{\alpha}=I_{\alpha}

which implies

d_{{\sf K}}d_{\beta}=1
. Since we assumed
that
{\sf K}

were a non empty collection of non-trivial
elementary types, we have a contradiction.

\,\blacksquare

We are now ready to prove the characterisation of the admissible
type compositions.
In order to make the derivation clearer, we will
first prove this
preliminary lemma.

Lemma 13
.

Let
x
and
y
be types and let us denote
with
{\sf H}:={\sf Ele}_{x}\cap{\sf Ele}_{y}

the set of non-trivial elementary types that

x
and
y
have in common.
If the composition
x\ast y
is admissible,
then

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\forall R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x),\,\forall S\in{\sf T}_{1}(y),\;\;\operatorname{Tr}[R*S]=d_{{\sf\widetilde{in}}}\\
&\widetilde{{\sf in}}:=({\sf in}_{x}\cup{\sf in}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}.\end{aligned}

(67)

Proof.

Let us fix some arbitrary
R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x)

and
S\in{\sf T}_{1}(y)
and let us define

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} Q_{x}:=I_{{\sf in}_{x}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{{\sf out}_{x}}\\
Q_{y}:=I_{{\sf in}_{y}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{{\sf out}_{y}}\\
{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{{\sf A}}:=\bigotimes_{i\in{\sf A}}{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{i}\end{aligned}

(68)

where
|0\rangle_{i}
is some fixed (normalized) state
on system
\mathcal{H}_{i}
and
I_{{\sf A}}

denotes the identity on the hilbert space

\mathcal{H}_{{\sf A}}:=\bigotimes_{i\in{\sf A}}\mathcal{H}_{i}
.
From Lemma
12
we know that

{\sf H}\subseteq({\sf in}_{x}\cap{\sf out}_{y})\cup({\sf out}_{x}\cap{\sf in}_{y})

and therefore we have that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{x}*Q_{y}]=\operatorname{Tr}[I_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\widetilde{{\sf out}}}]=d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}\\
&\widetilde{{\sf out}}:=({\sf out}_{x}\cup{\sf out}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}.\end{aligned}

(69)

Since
x*y
is admissible we have that for any

p\in[0,1]
there exists a type
z
such that

\displaystyle(pR+(1-p)Q_{x})*Q_{y}\in{\sf T}_{1}(z).

(70)

By taking the trace on both side we have that

\displaystyle p\operatorname{Tr}[R*Q_{y}]+(1-p)\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{x}*Q_{y}]\in\mathbb{N}\quad\forall p\in[0,1]

(71)

which, for
p

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[R*Q_{y}]=\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{x}*Q_{y}]=d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}.

(72)

On the other hand, if we consider

Q_{x}*(pS+(1-p)Q_{y})
we obtain

\operatorname{Tr}[Q_{x}*S]=d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}
.
Finally by considering
R*(pS+(1-p)Q_{y})

we obtain

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}[R*S]=d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}.

(73)

which is the thesis.

\,\blacksquare

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 9
.

Let
x
and
y
be types and let us denote
with
{\sf H}:={\sf Ele}_{x}\cap{\sf Ele}_{y}

the set of non-trivial elementary types that

x
and
y

x\ast y
is admissible
if and only

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &{\sf H}\subseteq({\sf in}_{x}\cap{\sf out}_{y})\cup({\sf out}_{x}\cap{\sf in}_{y})\\
&\forall R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x),\;\forall S\in{\sf T}_{1}(y),\;R\ast S\in T_{1}(\widetilde{{\sf in}}\to\widetilde{{\sf out}}),\end{aligned}

(74)

where
we define

\tilde{{\sf in}}:=({\sf in}_{x}\cup{\sf in}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}

and

\tilde{{\sf out}}:=({\sf out}_{x}\cup{\sf out}_{y})\setminus{\sf H}
.

Proof.

If Equation (
74
) is satisfied, then
the admissibility of the composition
x*y
is
trivially satisfied.

We now show that the admissibility of
x*y

implies Equation (
74
).
Let us fix some arbitrary
R\in{\sf T}_{1}(x)

and
S\in{\sf T}_{1}(y)
.
Since
x*y
is admissible, we have that there
exist some set
{\sf in}_{z}
and
{\sf out}_{z}
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &T:=(pR+(1-p)Q_{x})*Q_{y}\in{\sf T}_{1}({\sf in}_{z}\to{\sf out}_{z}),\\
&p<{d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}}^{-1}\end{aligned}

where
Q_{x}
have been defined in Equation
(
68
).
By direct computation we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &T=pR*Q_{y}+(1-p)I_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\widetilde{{\sf out}}}.\end{aligned}

(75)

Let us assume that there exists a nontrivial
elementary type
A
such that

A\in{{\sf in}_{z}}
and

A\not\in\widetilde{{\sf in}}
, i.e.

A\in{\sf in}_{z}\cap\widetilde{{\sf out}}
.
Consider now the state

{|1\rangle\langle 1|}_{{\sf in}_{z}}=\bigotimes_{i\in{\sf in}_{z}}{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{i}

where
|1\rangle_{i}
is a (normalized state) such that

\langle 0|1\rangle_{i}=0
.
Such a
|1\rangle_{i}
must exist for any
i

since we are considering non-elementary
types.
Since
T
is a channel from

\mathcal{H}_{{\sf in}_{z}}
to

\mathcal{H}_{{\sf out}_{z}}

we must have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} 1=&\operatorname{Tr}[T(I_{{\sf out}_{z}}\otimes{|1\rangle\langle 1|}_{{\sf in}_{z}})]=\\
=&p\operatorname{Tr}[R*Q_{y}(I_{{\sf out}_{z}}\otimes{|1\rangle\langle 1|}_{{\sf in}_{z}})]+\\
&+(1-p)\operatorname{Tr}[(I_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}\otimes{|0\rangle\langle 0|}_{\widetilde{{\sf out}}})(I_{{\sf out}_{z}}\otimes{|1\rangle\langle 1|}_{{\sf in}_{z}})]=\\
=&p\operatorname{Tr}[R*Q_{y}(I_{{\sf out}_{z}}\otimes{|1\rangle\langle 1|}_{{\sf in}_{z}})]\leq\\
\leq&p\operatorname{Tr}[R*Q_{y}]=pd_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}<1\end{aligned}

where we used Lemma
13

for the identity

\operatorname{Tr}[R*Q_{y}]=d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}
.
Therefore, it must be
{\sf in}_{z}\cap\widetilde{{\sf out}}=\emptyset
,
i.e.
{\sf in}_{z}\subseteq\widetilde{{\sf in}}
. However, since we
have

d_{{\sf in}_{z}}=d_{\widetilde{{\sf in}}}

from Lemma
13
,
it must be

(89)

Appendix E
Signalling and admissible composition

In this section we will show how the admissibility
of contractions is related to the signalling
structure between the input systems and the output
systems of a type.
Let us start by recalling the definition of a
no-signalling channel.

Definition 18
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a
bipartite quantum channel
\mathcal{E}:\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{A}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{B})\to\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{H}_{C}\otimes\mathcal{H}_{D})

A

\operatorname{Tr}_{D}[R]=I_{A}\otimes R^{\prime}_{BC}

Thanks to Proposition
8
,
the previous definition can be strightforwardly
generalized to maps of arbitrary type

Definition 19
.

Let
x
be a type,

,
B\in{\sf out}_{x}

and let

be a deterministic map of type

from

to

to

\mathcal{H}_{{\sf in}_{x}}

\mathcal{H}_{{\sf out}_{x}}

\operatorname{Tr}_{\widetilde{{\sf out}}}[R]=I_{A}\otimes R^{\prime}

where

We now can prove that the admissibility
of a contraction is equivalent to a no-signalling

Proposition 14
.

Let
x
be a type,

, and

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{AB}(x)\mbox{ is admissible}\iff A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B

Proof.

From Corollary
4

\mathcal{C}_{AB}(x)

50

\,\blacksquare

Corollary 5
.

Given a type
x
with

and

This result proves that to check the validity of a

valid channel to a valid channel at each step of

\displaystyle A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B\implies A\not\rightsquigarrow_{y\to x}B\mbox{ and }B\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{x\to y}A

(130)

Proof.

This result follows from
Lemmas
17
and

18
and the type equivalence

x\to y\equiv\overline{x\otimes\overline{y}}
.

\,\blacksquare

Lemma 20
.

Let us define the type
z=x\rightarrow y
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} A\in{\sf in}_{y},B\in{\sf in}_{x}\implies A\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{z}B\\
A\in{\sf out}_{y},B\in{\sf out}_{x}\implies B\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{z}A\end{aligned}

(131)

Proof.

Let us prove the first implication. Let us consider

\overline{z}=\overline{x\rightarrow y}=x\otimes\overline{y}
. we have

D_{x\otimes\overline{y}}=D_{x}\textbf{e}_{y}\cup\textbf{e}_{x}D_{\overline{y}}\cup D_{x}D_{\overline{y}}
and

S^{x\otimes\overline{y}}_{BA}=W_{{\sf in}_{x\otimes\overline{y}}\setminus B}0_{B}\textbf{e}_{{\sf out}_{x\otimes\overline{y}}\setminus A}0_{A}=W_{{\sf in}_{x}\setminus B}W_{{\sf out}_{y}}0_{B}\textbf{e}_{{\sf out}_{x}}\textbf{e}_{{\sf in}_{y}\setminus A}0_{A}
.
Since we have that

D_{x}\subseteq W_{{\sf in}_{x}}T_{{\sf out}_{x}}
and

D_{\overline{y}}\subseteq W_{{\sf out}_{y}}T_{{\sf in}_{y}}
, we can
verify that

D_{x\otimes\overline{y}}\cap S^{x\otimes\overline{y}}_{BA}=\emptyset
. This
means that
B\not\rightsquigarrow_{\overline{z}}A
which implies

A\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{z}B
.
The proof of the second implication
is analogous.

\,\blacksquare

We are now ready to prove the main result of this section.

Proposition 15
.

Let
x
be a type,
A\in{\sf in}_{x}
,

B\in{\sf out}_{x}
. Then, there exists a
unique type
y\prec x
such that

\{A,B\}\subseteq{\sf Ele}_{y}
and
y^{\prime}\prec y\implies\{A,B\}\not\subseteq{\sf Ele}_{y}
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle A\in{\sf in}_{y}

\displaystyle\implies A\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{x}B,

(132)

\displaystyle A\in{\sf out}_{y}

\displaystyle\implies A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B.

(133)

Proof.

The existence an uniqueness of such a type

y=y_{1}\to y_{2}
was proved in Lemma

4
. Let us now assume
A\in{\sf in}_{y}
.
Then
it must be
A\in{\sf out}_{y_{1}}
and

B\in{\sf out}_{y_{2}}
which from Lemma

20
implies

A\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{y}B
.
Let us now introduce the following notation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{0}^{z}(x):=z\rightarrow x,\quad\mathcal{C}_{1}^{z}(x):=x\rightarrow z.

(134)

Since
y\preceq x
, we have

x=\mathcal{C}^{z_{n}}_{s_{n}}(\mathcal{C}^{z_{n-1}}_{s_{n-1}}(\cdots\mathcal{C}^{z_{1}}_{s_{1}}(y)\cdots))

for some types
z_{i}
.
Since
A\in{\sf in}_{x}
, it implies that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}s_{i}(\text{mod 2})=0
which in turn
gives
A\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.00002pt\hskip-5.00002pt\hbox{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\rightsquigarrow$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-2.00522pt\raisebox{5.05103pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle f$}}\hskip-2.00522pt\hskip 5.0pt}}\hskip-5.00002pt\hskip 0.0pt\raisebox{-2.97221pt}{\hbox{$\scriptscriptstyle$}}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip 5.00002pt}}_{x}B
.

The proof of the implication
A\in{\sf out}_{y}\implies A\not\rightsquigarrow_{x}B
is analogous.

\,\blacksquare
