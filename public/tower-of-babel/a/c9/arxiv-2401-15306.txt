On 2 2 -integral Cayley graphs

Abstract
In this paper, we introduce the concept of k k -integral graphs. A graph Γ \Gamma is called k k -integral if the extension degree of the splitting field of the characteristic polynomial of Γ \Gamma over rational field ℚ \mathbb{Q} is equal to k k . We prove that for any positive integers k k and Δ \Delta , the set of all finite connected graphs with algebraic degree at most k k and maximum degree at most Δ \Delta is finite. We study 2 2 -integral Cayley graphs over finite groups G G with respect to Cayley sets which are a union of conjugacy classes of G G . Among other general results, we completely characterize all finite abelian groups having a connected 2 2 -integral Cayley graph with valency 2 , 3 , 4 2,3,4 and 5 5 . Furthermore, we classify the finite groups G G that all Cayley graphs over G G with bounded valency are 2 2 -integral.



Keywords
: Cayley graph, algebraic degree, characters of groups, integral eigenvalue.

Introduction and results

A graph is called
integral
if the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix are all integers.
This concept was first introduced by Harary and Schwenk
[
13
]
and they raised the question: which graphs are integral?
After this, classifications and construction of the integral graph has become an active topic.
The general way to study the integral graphs is to study some special classes of graphs, such as Cayley graphs.
A
Cayley digraph
over a group
G
with respect to a subset
S
of
G
, denoted by
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
, is a digraph with vertex set
G
such that
(g,h)
is an arc if and only if
hg^{-1}\in S
. If
S=\emptyset
then
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is the empty graph, and if

then the corresponding Cayley digraph has a loop at each vertex.
If
S
is inverse-closed, i.e
S=S^{-1}=\{s^{-1}\mid s\in S\}
, then
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is an undirected graph. Furthermore,
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is a connected digraph if and only if
G=\langle S\rangle
.
The Cayley graph over a cyclic group is also called
circulant
.
A subset
S
of a group
G
is called
normal
if for any
g\in G
, we have
g^{-1}Sg=S
. Clearly, a subset of any abelian group is a normal set.
The Cayley graph over a group
G
with respect to a normal subset
S
is also called
normal Cayley graph
.
A large number of results on the eigenvalues of Cayley graphs have been produced over the past more than four decades, for a survey on this topic see
[
17
]
.
All Cayley digraphs in this paper are loop-free and non-empty.

Mönius, Steuding and Stumpfm
[
21

introduced the concept of the splitting fileds and the algebraic degrees of graphs to investigate which graph properties prevent integral eigenvalues.
For a graph
\Gamma

\Gamma

The extension degree

Since eigenvalues of graphs are all algebraic integers, a graph

Some works have been done, very recently, on determining algebraic degrees of Cayley graphs and their generalization
[

Note that the question raised by Harary and Schwenk can be translated as: which graphs have algebraic degree 1?

Definition.
For a given positive integer

3
The number of
k
-integral graphs

One of the main questions to study
k
-integral graphs is to know that the number of such connected graphs are finite. In this section, we give a positive answer to this question.

Proposition 3.1
.

There exists a function
f:\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that the number of distinct eigenvalues of any graph with maximum degree
\Delta
and algebraic degree
k
is at most
f(k,\Delta)
.

Proof.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph with algebraic degree
k
and
p(x)
be the characteristic polynomial of
\Gamma
. Then we have
p(x)=p_{1}(x)^{t_{1}}\cdots p_{l}(x)^{t_{l}}
, where
p_{i}(x)
s are monic polynomials on
\mathbb{Q}[x]
, irreducible and pairwise relatively prime and
t_{i}
’s are positive integers. Since
p_{i}(x)
s are irreducible and pairwise relatively prime in
\mathbb{Q}
, then the roots of
p_{i}(x)
s are pairwise distinct and
p_{i}(x)
s has no repeated roots.

Let
p_{i}(x)=x^{k_{i}}+a_{1}x^{k_{i}-1}+\cdots+a_{k_{i}-1}x+a_{k_{i}}
, where
a_{i}
s are integers.
Since the roots of
p_{i}(x)
are also the roots of
p(x)
,
k_{i}
divides
k
. Furthermore, since the roots of
p_{i}(x)
are eigenvalues of
\Gamma
, the absolute value of any root of
p_{i}(x)
is less than or equal to
\Delta(\Gamma)
, the maximum degree of vertices of
\Gamma
. Now by Vieta’s formula and triangle inequality, for each
j
, we have

|a_{j}|\leqslant\binom{k_{i}}{j}\Delta(\Gamma)^{k_{i}-j}.

This means that the number of such polynomials and so the number of distinct eigenvalues
of
\Gamma
is less than or equal to a function of
k
and
\Delta(\Gamma)
, as desired.
∎

Theorem 3.2
.

For any positive integers
k
and
\Delta
, the set of all finite connected graphs with algebraic degree at most
k
and maximum degree at most
\Delta
is finite.

Proof.

Since for any connected graph with
s
distinct eigenvalues and diameter
D
, we have
D\leqslant s-1
, the result directly follows from Proposition
3.1
and boundlessness of the size of a connected graph with given diameter and maximum degree.
∎

4
k
-integral Cayley graphs

In this section, we are going to study the algebraic degrees of normal Cayley (di)graphs.
First let us recall two known results about the algebraic degrees of abelian Cayley (di)graphs and normal Cayley (di)graphs.

Lemma 4.1
.

[
18
, Theorem 1]

Let
G
be an abelian group of order
n
and
\Gamma=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
for some subset
S
of
G
. Then the algebraic degree of
\Gamma
is
\mathrm{deg}(\Gamma)=\frac{\varphi(n)}{|H|}
,
where
H=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{n}^{*}\mid S^{k}=S\}
.

Lemma 4.2
.

[
27
, Corollary 3.10]

Let
G
be a finite group with exponent
m
, that is the smallest positive integer such that
g^{m}=1
for all
g\in G
, and
\Gamma=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
for some normal subset
S
of
G
. Then the algebraic degree of
\Gamma
is

\mathrm{deg}(\Gamma)=\frac{\varphi(m)}{|H^{\prime}|}
,
where
H^{\prime}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}\mid S^{k}=S\}
.

Next let us start with some basic results.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
\Omega(G)=\{[g_{1}],\ldots,[g_{t}]\}
,
\emptyset\neq S
be a normal subset of
G
, and
\Gamma=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
. Then
S=\bigcup_{i=1}^{t}S_{i}
, where
S_{i}=[g_{i}]\cap S
for each
1\leqslant i\leqslant t
, and for
S_{i}\neq\emptyset
,
\deg(\Gamma_{i})
is a divisor of
\deg(\Gamma)
, where
\Gamma_{i}=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S_{i})
.

Proof.

For each
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
, put
S_{i}=[g_{i}]\cap S
.
Since
\Omega(G)
is a partition of
G
,
\{S_{i}\mid 1\leqslant i\leqslant n\}
is a partition of
S
.
Let
A=\mathrm{Aut}(G)
and
m
be the exponent of
G
.
By Lemma
4.2
,
\deg(\Gamma)=\frac{\varphi(m)}{|H|}
, where
H=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}\mid S^{k}=S\}
, and if
S_{i}\neq\emptyset
,
\deg(\Gamma_{i})=\frac{\varphi(m)}{|H_{i}|}
, where
H_{i}=\{k\in\mathbb{Z}_{m}^{*}\mid S_{i}^{k}=S_{i}\}
.
Note that to complete the proof, it is suffice to show that
H\leqslant H_{i}
.

Suppose
k\in H
and we have
S_{i}^{k}=([g_{i}]\cap S)^{k}=[g_{i}]^{k}\cap S^{k}
.
Note that
\langle g_{i}^{k}\rangle=\langle g_{i}\rangle
.
Then
[g_{i}]^{k}\cap S^{k}=[g_{i}^{k}]\cap S^{k}=[g_{i}]\cap S
, and so

which implies

.
∎

The above lemma reveals the relationship between the algebraic degree of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
and
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S_{i})
.
In the following we will prove that the splitting field of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S_{i})
is same as the splitting field of
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle g_{i}\rangle,S_{i})
.
And so, in short, it seems that to study

-integral normal Cayley graphs, the studying of

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
g\in G
and
\emptyset\neq S\subseteq[g]
. Then
\langle S\rangle=\langle g\rangle
and the splitting field of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
and
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle g\rangle,S)
are the same.

Proof.

Since
x\in[g]
if and only if
\langle x\rangle=\langle g\rangle
, the first part is clear. On the other hand,
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is isomorphic to
|G:\langle S\rangle|
copies of

\mathrm{Cay}(\langle S\rangle,S)
, where
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle S\rangle,S)
is a connected component of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
. Hence the sets of all distinct eigenvalues of
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
and
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle S\rangle,S)
are the same and so their splitting fields are the same, as desired.
∎

Let
G=\langle a\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and
\Gamma=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
.
By Lemma
4.1
, we know that
\deg(\Gamma)
is a divisor of
\varphi(n)
.
In what follows, we are going to give a way to find possible inverse-closed generating sets of

\deg(\mathrm{Cay}(G,S))=1

p

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
n\geqslant 3
,
d\neq n
,
d\mid n
,
G=\langle a\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{n}
and
A=\mathrm{Aut}(G)
. Let
T_{d}=\{\sigma\in A\mid g^{\sigma}=g,~\forall g\in[a^{d}]\}
and
K_{d}
be a subgroup of

containing

.
For a fixed integer

(k,n)=d
, put
S_{k,d}=\{(a^{k})^{\sigma}\mid\sigma\in K_{d}\}
and
\Gamma_{k,d}=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S_{k,d})
. Then

(1)

S_{k,d}
is inverse-closed if

contains the element
\tau
of

which maps

to

.

(2)

|S_{k,d}|=|K_{d}:T_{d}|\leqslant\varphi(\frac{n}{d})

(3)

if and only if

.

(4)

.

is integral if and only if

. In this case,

.

In particular, if

Proof.

Part

.
Note that

is the set of all elements of

\frac{n}{d}
.
Since the order of

Moreover,

.
Note that

. Hence we have

, which proves

If

[

is a regular permutation group on

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
G
be a finite group,
S_{1},\ldots,S_{k}
be normal subsets of
G
,
\langle S_{i}\rangle\cap\langle S_{j}\rangle=\{1\}
for all distinct
i,j
, and
S=S_{1}\cup\cdots\cup S_{k}
. Let
\Gamma=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
and
\Gamma_{i}=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,k
. Then

(1)

\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma_{i})\subseteq\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)
for all
i
,

(2)

\deg(\Gamma_{i})
divides
\deg(\Gamma)
for all
i
,

(3)

if
\deg(\Gamma_{i})=\deg(\Gamma)=2
for some
i
, then
\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma_{i})
,

(4)

if
\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma_{i})=\mathbb{F}
for all
i
, then
\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{F}
.

Proof.

Put
G_{i}:=\langle S_{i}\rangle
,
i=1,\ldots,k
. Since
S_{i}
is a normal subset of
G
,
G_{i}
is a normal subgroup of
G
. Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue
of
\Gamma_{i}
and
\mathrm{Irr}(G)=\{\chi_{1},\cdots,\chi_{k}\}
. Then
\lambda=\frac{\psi(S_{i})}{\psi(1)}
for some
\psi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G_{i})
. Let
\psi\uparrow G
be the induced character from
\psi
. Then
\psi\uparrow G=d_{1}\chi_{1}+\cdots+d_{k}\chi_{k}
for some integers
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
. Since
S_{i}
’s are normal subsets of
G
and
\langle S_{l}\rangle\cap\langle S_{l^{\prime}}\rangle=\{1\}
for distinct
l,l^{\prime}
, we have
(\psi\uparrow G)(S_{i})=\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\psi(S_{i})
and
(\psi\uparrow G)(S\setminus S_{i})=0
. Hence
\frac{1}{|G_{i}|}\psi(S_{i})=(\psi\uparrow G)(S)=d_{1}\chi_{1}(S)+\cdots+d_{k}\chi_{k}(S)
. Since the splitting fields contain the rational field and the characters degrees are positive integers, we conclude that
\lambda\in\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)
. Hence
\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma_{i})\subseteq\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)
. This proves
(1)
. Parts
(2)
and
(3)
are direct consequences of
(1)
.

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of
\Gamma
. Since
S_{i}
s are normal subsets of
G
,
S
is also a normal subset of
G
. Hence there exists
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
such that

\lambda=\frac{\chi(S)}{\chi(1)}
. On the other hand,
S_{i}
’s are pairwise disjoint and so
\chi(S)=\chi(S_{1})+\cdots+\chi(S_{k})
. Since for each

,
\chi(S_{i})\in\mathbb{F}
, we conclude
that
\lambda\in\mathbb{F}
. Thus
\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)\subseteq\mathbb{F}
. Now
(1)
implies that
\mathbb{SF}(\Gamma)=\mathbb{F}
as desired.
∎

Lemma 5.6
.

Let
G
be a finite group of even order,
S
be a normal subset of
G
and there exists an involution

\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)

(1)

\mathbb{F}

Proof.

Since
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is connected,
G=\langle S\rangle=\langle(S\setminus\{x\})\cup\{x\}\rangle

Since

is also conjugate-closed, and since

(1)

Let
\Gamma_{1}=\mathrm{Cay}(G,S\setminus\{x\})

is
\mathbb{F}
.
First we suppose that
G=\langle S\setminus\{x\}\rangle
.
Then Lemma
5.2
implies that

is
\mathbb{F}
.
Next we suppose that
G=\langle S\setminus\{x\}\rangle\times\langle x\rangle

Let
\lambda
be an eigenvalue of

.
Then

, for some
\chi\in\mathrm{Irr}(G)
. Since

, see
[

, Exrecise 5 of Chapter 13]

\lambda=\frac{\chi(S\setminus\{x\})}{\chi(1)}\pm 1
. Let

to

is an irreducible character of

, for some eigenvalue

of
\Gamma_{1}
. Now assume that
\mu
be an eigenvalue of
\Gamma_{1}

\mu=\frac{\eta(S\setminus\{x\})}{\eta(1)}

6
On groups all of whose undirected Cayley graphs of bounded valency are
2
-integral

In this section, we are going to classify the finite groups
G
that all Cayley graphs
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
are
2
-integral when
2\leqslant|S|\leqslant k
for each integer
k\geqslant 2
. This leads us to give the following definition.

Definition 6.1
.

For an integer
k\geqslant 2
, set

\mathcal{B}_{k}:=\{G\mid\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)\ \text{is $2$-integral whenever $S\subset G$ such that $1_{G}\notin S=S^{-1}$ and $2\leqslant|S|\leqslant k$}\}.

Clearly,
\mathcal{B}_{k+1}\subseteq\mathcal{B}_{k}
, and if
|G|
is odd, then
G\in\mathcal{B}_{2k-1}
if and only if
G\in\mathcal{B}_{2k-2}
.

To determine the sets
\mathcal{B}_{k}
, we start with some basic and useful results about
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
k\geqslant 2
be an integer.
Then the following holds for every
G\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

1)

Every proper subgroup
H<G
with
H\ncong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is also in
\mathcal{B}_{k}
.

2)

For every
g\in G
, the order of
g
is in
\{1,2,5,10\}
.

3)

The Sylow
5
-subgroup of
G
is a
5
-group of exponent
5
.

4)

If
|G|
is even, then the Sylow
2
-subgroup of
G
is isomorphic to
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.

Moreover,
|G|=2^{i}5^{j}
, where
i=0,1
and
j\geqslant 1
, and the Sylow
5
-subgroup of
G
is normal.

Proof.

Let
G\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
.
Suppose
H
is a proper subgroup of
G
and
H\ncong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
.
Since for a subset
S\subset H\leqslant G
, The Cayley graph
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
consists of
|G:H|
copies of
\mathrm{Cay}(H,S)
, we have
1)
.

Since
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is Cayley integral, we always assume that
|G|\geqslant 3
.
If all non-identity elements of
G
are involution, then we have
2)
.
Next suppose that
G
has at least one element with order great than
2
, say
g
.
Then
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{g,g^{-1}\})
is
2
-integral and so
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle g\rangle,\{g,g^{-1}\})
is
2
-integral by 1), which implies
o(g)
,the order of
g
, is in
\{5,8,10,12\}
by Theorem
5.1
. If
o(g)=8
or
12
, then
h:=g^{2}
or
h:=g^{3}
has order
4
, respectively. Now
\mathrm{Cay}(G,\{h,h^{-1}\})
must be
2
-integral, which means
o(h)\in\{5,8,10,12\}
, a contradiction. This proves
2)
.

By
2)
we can suppose that
|G|=2^{i}5^{j}
,
i,j\geqslant 0
. If
j=0
then, by
2)
,
G
is
2
-elementary abelian and so it is Cayley integral, a contradiction. Hence we may assume that
j\geqslant 1
and again by
2)
, a Sylow
5
-subgroup of
G
is a
5
-group of exponent
5
, which proves
3)
. Let
i\neq 0
and
P_{2}
be a Sylow
2
-subgroup of
G
. Clearly,
P_{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{i}
.
If
i\geqslant 2
, then
P_{2}\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{i}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
by
1)
.
However
\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{i}
is Cayley integral, a contradiction.
Thus
i=1
, which proves
4)
.

Furthermore,
G=P_{5}
if
i=0
and
|G:P_{5}|=2
if
i=1
, which implies that
P_{5}
is normal in
G
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 6.3
.

\mathcal{B}_{k}
is an empty set for any
k\geqslant 4
.

Proof.

Suppose
G\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
for a
k\geqslant 4
.
Then
G\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
.
By Lemma
6.2
,
G
has at least one subgroup
H=\langle h\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{5}
.
Note that
\mathrm{Cay}(H,H\setminus\{1\})
is integral, contradicting to Lemma
6.2
–1).
Thus
G\notin\mathcal{B}_{k}
for any
k\geqslant 4
.
This completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 6.4
.

D_{2n}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
for
k=2,3
if and only if
n=5
.

Proof.

If
D_{2n}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
, then by Lemma
6.2
we have
n=5
.
For the converse direction, it can be directly calculate that
D_{10}\in\mathcal{B}_{k}
for
k=2,3
.
∎

By Theorem
6.3
, it is enough to determine the sets
\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{B}_{3}
. In what follows, we will do this.

6.1
\mathcal{B}_{2}

In this part, we will classify the groups in
\mathcal{B}_{2}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

G\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
if and only if
G
is a
5
-group of exponent
5
or the Sylow
5
-subgroup of
G
is a group of exponent
5
and has index
2
.

Proof.

One direction is clear by Lemma
6.2
.

Next suppose
P_{5}
is a
5
-group of exponent
5
.
If
G=P_{5}
, then for any inverse-closed subset
S
of
G
,
S=\{g,g^{-1}\}
, where the order of
g
is
5
, and so
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle g\rangle,\{g,g^{-1}\})
is
2
-integral by Lemma
5.1
.
Hence
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is
2
-integral.

Suppose
P_{5}<G
and
|G:P_{5}|=2
.
Then there exist
a\in G
such that
G=P_{5}\cup aP_{5}=P_{5}\cup P_{5}a
. Furthermore, by Lemma
6.2
, we may assume that
a^{2}=1
.
Next we consider the form of inverse-closed subset
S
of
G
with
|S|=2
.

Case I:

S=\{x,x^{-1}\}
, where
x\in G
is not an involution.

If
x\in P_{5}
, then the order of
x
is
5
and so
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle x\rangle,\{x,x^{-1}\})
is
2
-integral by Lemma
5.1
.
Hence
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is
2
-integral.

Suppose that
x\notin P_{5}
, that is to say
x=ap=p^{{}^{\prime}}a
for some
p,p^{\prime}\in P_{5}
. Then
x^{2}=p^{\prime}aap=p^{\prime}p\in P_{5}
, which means that the order of
x
is
5
or
10
. In both cases
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle x\rangle,\{x,x^{-1}\})
is
2
-integral and so
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is
2
-integral.

Case II:

S=\{x,y\}
, where
x,y\in G
are both involution.

In this case,
x=ap=p^{-1}a
and
y=ap^{\prime-1}=p^{\prime}a
for some
p,p^{\prime}\in P_{5}
. Note that
xy\neq yx
, otherwise
\langle x,y\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}^{2}\leqslant G
which is impossible by Lemma
6.2
–3). Furthermore,
xy\in P_{5}
. Hence either
xy=1
or
xy
has order
5
. The first case is impossible, otherwise
x=y
which is a contradiction. Hence
xy
has order
5
and
\langle x,y\rangle\cong D_{10}
.
By Proposition
6.4
,
D_{10}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
.
Hence
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is
2
-integral, which completes the proof.

∎

6.2

In this part, we will classify the groups in
\mathcal{B}_{3}

Proposition 6.6
.

If

if and only if

Suppose

, then by Lemma
6.2
,
G\cong P_{5}
or

, where

is a

-group of exponent

.

Next suppose
G\cong P_{5}
or
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times P_{5}
, where
P_{5}
is a
5
-group of exponent
5
.
First assume that
G\cong P_{5}
.
Then by Theorem
6.5
,
P_{5}\in\mathcal{B}_{2}
.
Since
|P_{5}|
is odd, we have
G\in\mathcal{B}_{3}
.
Assume that
G\cong\mathbb{Z}_{2}\times P_{5}
, and
S
is an inverse-closed subset of
G
such that
1_{G}\notin S
where
|S|=2
or
3
.
Note that the order of any non-identity element of
G
is
2,5
or
10
.
If
|S|=2
, then
S=\{g,g^{-1}\}
, where the order of
g
is
5
or
10
.
Furthermore,
\langle S\rangle=\langle g\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{5}
or
\mathbb{Z}_{10}
.
By Lemma
5.1
,
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle S\rangle,S)
is
2
-integral.
Hence
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is
2
-integral.
If
|S|=3
, then
S=\{a,g,g^{-1}\}
, where
a^{2}=1_{G}
and the order of
g
is
5
or
10
. Furthermore,
\langle S\rangle=\langle a\rangle\times\langle g\rangle\cong\mathbb{Z}_{5}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2}=\mathbb{Z}_{10}
.
By Theorem
5.3
–
i)
,
\mathrm{Cay}(\langle S\rangle,S)
is

Hence
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is
2
-integral.
This completes the proof.
∎

In what follows, we consider the non-nilpotent groups in
\mathcal{B}_{3}
.

Proposition 6.7
.

Suppose
G=\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
is not nilpotent.
Then
G\notin\mathcal{B}_{3}
.
Moreover,
G=\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\notin\mathcal{B}_{3}
for any
n\geqslant 2
.

Proof.

Suppose
G=\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\cong(\langle a\rangle\times\langle b\rangle)\rtimes\langle x\rangle
, where
a^{5}=b^{5}=x^{2}=1_{G}
.
Write
P=\langle a\rangle\times\langle b\rangle
.
Actually,
G
is the generalized dihedral group over
P
, that is,
p^{x}=p^{-1}
for all
p\in P
.

Let
S=\{bx,b^{2}x,ax\}
.
Clearly
S
is inverse-closed.
Next we will show that
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is not
2
-integral.
Note that
\mathrm{Cay}(G,S)
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{BiCay}(P,\emptyset,\emptyset,T)
, where
T=\{b,b^{2},a\}

[
2
, Lemma 8]
.
By
[
28
, Theorem 3.2]
, the eigenvalues of
\mathrm{BiCay}(P,\emptyset,\emptyset,T)
are
\pm|\chi_{p}(b+b^{2}+a)|
, where

and
\chi_{p}
’s are irreducible characters of

, see also
[
1
, Section 3.]
.
However, one can calculate that
\pm|\chi_{b^{4}}(b+b^{2}+a)|=\sqrt{4+\sqrt{5}}
.
Thus
\mathrm{BiCay}(P,\emptyset,\emptyset,T)
is not

-integral.
Hence

.

On the other hand,
\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{2}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\leqslant\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}
for

.
By Lemma
6.2
–1), if

Thus
\mathbb{Z}_{5}^{n}\rtimes\mathbb{Z}_{2}\notin\mathcal{B}_{3}
for any

.
∎
