The dyadic Riesz vector II
Komla Domelevo, Stefanie Petermichl

Abstract
We derive a dyadic model operator for the Riesz vector. We show linear upper L p \displaystyle L^{p} bounds for 1 < p < ∞ \displaystyle 1<p<\infty between this model operator and the Riesz vector, when applied to functions with values in Banach spaces. By an upper bound we mean that the boundedness of the dyadic Riesz vector implies the boundedness of the Riesz vector. The same holds for single dyadic Riesz transforms and their continuous counterparts. The linear dependence is with constant one.

(1)

We are interested in comparing the
\displaystyle L^{p}

\displaystyle i\in[1,d]

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}f:=(\mathcal{R}_{1}f,\ldots,\mathcal{R}_{d}f)

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\mathcal{S}}f:=(\mathcal{S}_{1}f,\ldots,\mathcal{S}_{d}f)

\displaystyle|\vec{u}|_{\ell^{2}}:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|u_{i}|_{X}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
. In the companion paper [arxiv], the authors prove linear
lower bounds

\forall i\in[1,d],\quad\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\leqslant c_{0}\|\mathcal{R}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p},

as well as

\|\overrightarrow{\mathcal{S}}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\leqslant c_{0}\|\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}\|_{p\rightarrow p},

for a universal constant
\displaystyle c_{0}
. The main goal of the present paper is to prove
the dimensionless upper bounds

Theorem 1

(Upper bound for a single Riesz transform)

We have for all
\displaystyle i\in[1,d]
,

\|\mathcal{R}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}.

Theorem 2

(Upper bound for the Riesz vector)

We have

\|\overrightarrow{\mathcal{R}}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\leqslant\|\overrightarrow{\mathcal{S}}\|_{p\rightarrow p}.

As compared to the onedimensional case studied in
[
3
]
, several new difficulties arise, that we detail later. To summarize, (i) the
Riesz transforms are directly defined as stochastic integrals but as
conditional expectations upon arrival, (ii) convergence of weak formulations
are needed, (iii) the domain and its boundary are unbounded, (iv) the Riesz
vector estimate above requires a specific weak formulation, and finally (v)
the discrete random walks associated to the different dyadic Riesz transforms
are more intricate.

We introduce now the dyadic setting and define the dyadic Riesz transforms.

\displaystyle i

Inspired by the onedimensional case, we wish to approximate the Riesz
transforms in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d}
with the help of
\displaystyle(d+1)
–dimensional discrete

\displaystyle d=1

(3)

Discrete martingales and their transforms

Given
\displaystyle f\in L^{p}(\mathbb{R}^{d},X)
and its harmonic extension also noted
\displaystyle f

approximating both
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}^{f}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{i}_{f}
by discrete
martingales adapted to the action of the dyadic Riez transforms. An important
difference with the onedimensional case is that the discretization of not only

\displaystyle i\in[1,d]

and the approximate

M_{k}^{(i),i}:=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}A_{i}\nabla f(B_{k-1}^{(i)})\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i)}.

The superscript

by the
\displaystyle i

\displaystyle B^{(i)}
, whereas the superscript
\displaystyle i

refers to the fact that we are approximating the
\displaystyle i
–th martingale transform

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{i}
. Our first goal is achieved:

Lemma 1

For all
\displaystyle f
, all
\displaystyle i\in[1,d]
, we have

\displaystyle M_{k}^{(i),i}=\mathcal{S}_{i}M^{(i),f}_{k}
and therefore

\|M_{k}^{(i),i}\|_{p}\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\|M^{(i),f}_{k}\|_{p}.

(8)

Proof.

By construction of the discrete random walks and property (
7
),
we have immediately

\begin{array}[]{lll}\mathcal{S}_{i}M^{(i),f}_{k}&=&\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\nabla f(B_{k-1}^{(i)})\cdot\mathcal{S}_{i}\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i)}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\nabla f(B_{k-1}^{(i)})\cdot A_{i}^{\ast}\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i)}\\
&=&\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}A_{i}\nabla f(B_{k-1}^{(i)})\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i)}=M_{k}^{(i),i}\end{array}

and the result follows.
∎

Remark 1

If
\displaystyle X^{(i)}_{n}:=B^{(i)}_{nN}
is the coarse–grained random walk, we
could also define the stochastic integrals based on
\displaystyle X^{(i)}
instead of

\displaystyle B^{(i)}
, i.e.

M^{(i),f}_{n}:=\mathcal{M}_{0}^{f}+\sum_{m=1}^{n}\nabla f(X_{m-1}^{(i)})\cdot\mathrm{d}X_{m}^{(i)},\quad M_{n}^{(i),f_{i}}:=\sum_{m=1}^{n}A_{i}\nabla f(X_{m-1}^{(i)})\cdot\mathrm{d}X_{m}^{(i)}.

and we would still have by linearity of
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{i}
that
\displaystyle M_{n}^{(i),f_{i}}=\mathcal{S}_{i}M^{(i),f}_{n}
. It is not a big simplification to the proof
of convergence.

Strategy and plan

Our main goal is now to show that one can pass
to the limit in inequality (
8
) of
Lemma
1
. As compared to the onedimensional case
\displaystyle d=1
studied in
[
3
]
, a few new difficulties arise.

First, the Riesz transforms are not directly defined by a stochastic
integral, but rather through a projection of a stochastic integral. This
projection is the conditional expectation upon arrival as in
(
6
). This projection would not be easy to
handle as a limit of a discrete process. For this reason, part of our proof
relies on the weak formulation (
5
) and related
convergence results from discrete weak formulations to continuous weak
formulations.

Second, the estimate of the Riesz vector requires a specific weak formulation
that we can relate to the dyadic Riesz transforms.

Finally, the upper half space as well as its boundary are unbounded, and the
random processes involved in the stochastic representation need to start at
“infinity”, which involves a supplementary limiting process as compared to
the onedimensional case on the circle.

Lemma 5

(Weak convergence) Let
\displaystyle y>0
fixed. Let
\displaystyle i\in[1,d]
fixed. Let
\displaystyle T>0
. Let
\displaystyle\psi
smooth on
\displaystyle\Omega
. Assume weak
consistency. Then we have

\mathbb{E}\psi(X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})=\mathbb{E}\psi(W_{T}^{y,\tau})+c_{\psi,T}(\varepsilon).

Proof.

This is a consequence of the weak consistency from Lemma
2
. See e.g.
[
6
,
5
]
where a slightly
different definition of weak consistency is used. We sketch the proof
adapted to our situation and notations. The function defined for all
\displaystyle t\in[0,T]
,
\displaystyle x\in\Omega
as

u(t,x):=\mathbb{E}(\psi(W^{y,\tau}_{T})|W^{y,\tau}_{t}=x),

is a solution to the backward heat equation

\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u=0,\quad u(T,x)=\psi(x),

and Ito formula reads

\psi(W^{y,\tau}_{T})=u(T,W^{y,\tau}_{T})=u(0,y)+\int_{0}^{T}\nabla u(\psi(W^{y,\tau}_{s}))\cdot\mathrm{d}W^{y,\tau}_{s}+\int_{0}^{T}\left(\partial_{t}u+\frac{1}{2}\Delta u\right)(s,W^{y,\tau}_{s})\mathrm{d}s,

where the last term is null. Taking expectation allows to compare with the
discrete process

\mathbb{E}\psi(X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})-\mathbb{E}\psi(W_{T}^{y,\tau})=\mathbb{E}u(T,X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})-u(0,y)=\mathbb{E}[u(T,X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})-u(0,X_{0}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})].

The last term is written as a telescopic sum, with summands

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[u(t_{n+1},X_{t_{n+1}}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})-u(t_{n},X_{t_{n}}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}u(t_{n+1},X_{t_{n+1}}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})-\mathbb{E}u\left(t_{n+1},W^{t_{n},X_{t_{n}}}_{t_{n+1}}\right)

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\{u(t_{n+1},X_{t_{n+1}}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})-u(t_{n},X_{t_{n}})\}

\displaystyle\displaystyle-\mathbb{E}\left\{u\left(t_{n+1},W^{t_{n},X_{t_{n}}}_{t_{n+1}}\right)-u(t_{n},X_{t_{n}})\right\},

where we have used again Ito formula to replace
\displaystyle u(t_{n},X_{t_{n}}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})
by
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}u\left(t_{n+1},W^{t_{n},X_{t_{n}}}_{t_{n+1}}\right)
. A Taylor expansion of the last two terms
around the common point
\displaystyle(t_{n},X_{t_{n}})
exhibits the differences of
increments
\displaystyle\{X_{t_{n+1}}-X_{t_{n}}\}-\left\{W^{t_{n},X_{t_{n}}}_{t_{n+1}}-W^{t_{n},X_{t_{n}}}_{t_{n}}\right\}
and higher moments. Those are
estimated thanks to weak consistency as in the onedimensional case, yielding
the desired result.
∎

Lemma 6

(Weak convergence of
discrete martingale transforms) Let
\displaystyle y>0
fixed. Let
\displaystyle i\in[1,d]
fixed.
Let
\displaystyle T>0
. Let
\displaystyle f
harmonic as above.

\|f(X_{T}^{(i)})-M_{T}^{(i),f}\|_{p}=c_{T}(\varepsilon)

Proof.

The estimates are handeled slightly differently as in the onedimensional

\displaystyle f
.
Recalling that

\begin{array}[]{lll}M_{T}^{(i),f}-M_{0}^{(i),f}&:=&\sum_{k=1}^{N^{5}}\sum_{j=1,d}\partial_{j}f(B^{(i)}_{k-1})\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i),j},\end{array}

we compare with the Taylor expansion

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(X_{T}^{(i)})-f(X_{0}^{(i)})

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle f(B_{T}^{(i)})-f(B^{(i)}_{0})=\sum_{k=1}^{N^{5}}[f(B_{k}^{(i)})-f(B_{k-1}^{(i)})]

\displaystyle\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{N^{5}}\sum_{j=1,d}\partial_{j}f(B^{(i)}_{k-1})\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i),j}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=1}^{N^{5}}\sum_{j_{1},j_{2}=1,d}\partial^{2}_{j_{1}j_{2}}f(B^{(i)}_{k-1})\mathrm{d}B^{(i),j_{1}}_{k}\mathrm{d}B^{(i),j_{2}}_{k}

where the last terms are Taylor remainders. Recalling that
\displaystyle M_{0}^{(i),f}=f(B^{(i)}_{0})

\displaystyle j

. This is because the first limit involves the
discretisation of the harmonic function
\displaystyle f
. However the second limit
involves the martingale transform
\displaystyle M_{T}^{(i),i}
of
\displaystyle M_{T}^{(i),f}
, or in
the limit the martingale transform
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{y,i}_{\infty}
of

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{y,f}_{\infty}
. Those are not martingales directly associated
to
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}f
or its discretisation, since a projection
(
6
) is needed to recover
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{i}f
.

Indeed for the first limit, using Lemma
6
and recalling that all functions are smooth with
compact support, we have successively

\mathbb{E}\langle M_{T}^{(i),f},M_{T}^{(i),g}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle f(X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}}),M_{T}^{(i),g}\rangle+c_{T}(\varepsilon),

and similarly

\mathbb{E}\langle f(X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}}),M_{T}^{(i),g}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle f(X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}}),g(X_{T}^{(i),\tau_{\varepsilon}})\rangle+c_{T}(\varepsilon).

Applying the weak convergence of Lemma
5
to
\displaystyle\psi(x):=\langle f(x),g(x)\rangle
then letting
\displaystyle T
go to infinity
yields the first limit.

For the second limit, explicit functions of
\displaystyle X^{(i)}
are only accessible
after expanding the product martingales. Indeed, given
\displaystyle T>0
, we have

\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{M}^{y,i}_{T},\mathcal{M}^{y,g}_{T}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{M}^{y,i}_{0},\mathcal{M}^{y,g}_{0}\rangle+\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(W^{y,\tau}_{t}),\nabla g(W^{y,\tau}_{t})\rangle\mathrm{d}t,

and similarly

\mathbb{E}\langle M_{T}^{(i),i},M_{T}^{(i),g}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\langle M_{0}^{(i),i},M_{0}^{(i),g}\rangle+\mathbb{E}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{4}}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(X_{t_{n}}),\nabla g(X_{t_{n}})\rangle(t_{n+1}-t_{n}).

Notice that the first terms on the right hand sides are null. Choose now a
large integer
\displaystyle K
. We know that we can approximate the continuous stochastic
integral as

\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{T}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(W^{y,\tau}_{t}),\nabla g(W^{y,\tau}_{t})\rangle\mathrm{d}t=\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(W^{y,\tau}_{T_{k-1}}),\nabla g(W^{y,\tau}_{T_{k-1}})\rangle(T_{k}-T_{k-1})+c(K),

where
\displaystyle(T_{k})_{k\in[0,K]}
is a regular subdivision of
\displaystyle[0,T]
, i.e.
\displaystyle T_{k}=kT/K
, and
\displaystyle c(K)
is a function that goes to zero when
\displaystyle K
goes to
infinity. Similarly, it is now an exercise to show that we can approximate

\mathbb{E}\sum_{n=1}^{N^{4}}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(X_{t_{n}}),\nabla g(X_{t_{n}})\rangle(t_{n+1}-t_{n})=\mathbb{E}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(X_{T_{k-1}}),\nabla g(X_{T_{k-1}})\rangle(T_{k}-T_{k-1})+c(K),

where we assume without loss of generality that
\displaystyle N
is a multiple of
\displaystyle K
. We
can now pass to the limit for each
\displaystyle k\in[1,K]
so that
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(X_{T_{k-1}}),\nabla g(X_{T_{k-1}})\rangle\rightarrow\mathbb{E}\langle A_{i}\nabla f(W^{y,\tau}_{T_{k-1}}),\nabla g(W^{y,\tau}_{T_{k-1}})\rangle
when
\displaystyle\varepsilon
goes to zero,
using again weak convergence. This allows to prove the second limit,
therefore concludingg the proof of Lemma
8
.
∎

Finally, we prove our main result.

Proof of Theorem
1

(Upper bound for a single riesz transform)
Recall that we know from
Lemma
1
that

\|M_{k}^{(i),i}\|_{p}\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\|M^{(i),f}_{k}\|_{p}.

We have equivalently

\mathbb{E}\langle M_{k}^{(i),i},M^{(i),g}\rangle\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\|M^{(i),f}_{k}\|_{p}\|M^{(i),g}_{k}\|_{p}

for any test function
\displaystyle g\in L^{q}(\partial\Omega,X^{\ast})
. Using Lemmas

7
&
8
, we pass to
the limit
\displaystyle T\rightarrow\infty
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
and obtain,
for any fixed
\displaystyle y>0
,

\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{M}^{y,i}_{\infty},\mathcal{M}^{y,g}_{\infty}\rangle\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}(\mathbb{E}|f(W^{y,\tau}_{\infty})|^{p})^{1/p}(\mathbb{E}|g(W^{y,\tau}_{\infty})|^{q})^{1/q}

Notice that

\mathbb{E}|f(W^{y,\tau}_{\infty})|^{p}=\int_{\partial\Omega}|f(x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}\omega^{y}(x)

where
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{y}
is the harmonic measure associated to the Brownian

\displaystyle W^{y,\tau}
started at
\displaystyle y
and stopped upon hitting
\displaystyle\partial\Omega
. Due
to translation invariance of the domain, we have
\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{y}(x)=\mathrm{d}\omega^{0}(x-y)=G(x-y)\mathrm{d}x
, where
\displaystyle G
is the normalized
Gaussian. It follows that

\int_{y}\mathbb{E}|f(W^{y,\tau}_{\infty})|^{p}=\int_{y}\int_{\partial\Omega}|f(x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}\omega^{y}(x)=\left(\int_{\partial\Omega}|f(x)|^{p}\mathrm{d}x\right)\left(\int_{y}G(y)\mathrm{d}y\right)=\|f\|_{p},

hence

\int_{y}(\mathbb{E}|f(W^{y,\tau}_{\infty})|^{p})^{1/p}(\mathbb{E}|g(W^{y,\tau}_{\infty})|^{q})^{1/q}\leqslant\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{q}.

Following
[
4
]
, we observe that

\lim_{y\rightarrow\infty}\int_{y}\mathbb{E}\langle\mathcal{M}^{y,i}_{\infty},\mathcal{M}^{y,g}_{\infty}\rangle=\int_{\Omega}\langle A_{i}\nabla f,\nabla g\rangle 2y\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}x=\langle\mathcal{R}_{i}f,g\rangle,

yielding

\langle\mathcal{R}_{i}f,g\rangle\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}_{i}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{q},

hence the desired result.

\displaystyle\Box

5.2
Upper bound for the vector Riesz transform

Let
\displaystyle\mathcal{R}f:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\mathcal{R}_{i}f|_{X}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
and
\displaystyle\mathcal{S}f:=\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}|\mathcal{S}_{i}f|_{X}^{2}\right)^{1/2}
. Our goal is to prove Theorem
2
, namely

\|\mathcal{R}\|_{p}\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}\|_{p}.

Let
\displaystyle g=(g_{i})_{i=1,\ldots,d}
a vector consisting of
\displaystyle d
test functions.
The usual weak formulation for the estimate of the Riesz vector reads

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\|\mathcal{R}f\|_{p}&=&\sup_{\|g\|_{q}\leqslant 1}\langle\mathcal{R}f,g\rangle=\sup_{\|g\|_{q}\leqslant 1}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\mathcal{R}_{i}f,g_{i}\rangle=\sup_{\|g\|_{q}\leqslant 1}\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle A_{i}\nabla f,\nabla g_{i}\rangle\mathrm{d}t\end{array}

The last term is the stochastic representation involving the
\displaystyle(d+1)
–dimensional Brownian motion
\displaystyle W^{\tau}
, namely

\langle\mathcal{R}f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{\tau}\langle A_{i}\nabla f,\nabla g_{i}\rangle=\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{\tau}A_{i}\nabla f(W_{s}^{\tau})\cdot\nabla g_{i}(W_{s}^{\tau})\mathrm{d}t.

Notice that all summands involve the
same
realization of the
Brownian motion.

Adapted stochastic representation

In order for us to compare the
dyadic Riesz vector and the usual Riesz vector, we need to adapt the
stochastic representation in a simple (but effective!) way. Observing that

\langle\mathcal{R}f,g\rangle=\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{\tau}A_{i}\nabla f(W_{s}^{\tau})\cdot\nabla g_{i}(W_{s}^{\tau})\mathrm{d}t=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\int_{0}^{\tau}A_{i}\nabla f(W_{s}^{\tau})\cdot\nabla g_{i}(W_{s}^{\tau})\mathrm{d}t,

one realizes that the expectation operator
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}
is applied
\displaystyle d
times
in the last expression, and that the Brownian motion is a dummy variable. We
can therefore go further and reformulate

\begin{array}[]{lll}\langle\mathcal{R}f,g\rangle&=&\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{i}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}}A_{i}\nabla f(W_{s}^{(i),\tau_{i}})\cdot\nabla g_{i}(W_{s}^{(i),\tau_{i}})\mathrm{d}t,\\
&=&\mathbb{E}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\int_{0}^{\tau_{i}}A_{i}\nabla f(W_{s}^{(i),\tau_{i}})\cdot\nabla g_{i}(W_{s}^{(i),\tau_{i}})\mathrm{d}t,\end{array}

(9)

where the
\displaystyle W^{(i),\tau_{i}}
’s are
\displaystyle d
different realizations of Brownian
motions in
\displaystyle\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
,
\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{i}
the corresponding expectations
and
\displaystyle\tau_{i}
the corresponding stopping times. Those realizations do

not
need to be independent. This is reminiscent of the
\displaystyle d
discrete
random walks
\displaystyle B^{(i)}
, each stopped at different times but sharing the same
horizontal component.

Proof of Theorem
2

(Upper bound for the Riesz vector)
For all
\displaystyle i\in[1,d]
, we
approximate
\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}^{f}
as well as the test function

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}_{t}^{g_{i}}
, by

M^{(i),f}_{k}:=\mathcal{M}_{0}^{f}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\nabla f(B_{k-1}^{s(i)})\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i)},\quad M^{(i),g_{i}}_{k}:=\mathcal{M}_{0}^{g_{i}}+\sum_{\ell=1}^{k}\nabla g_{i}(B_{k-1}^{(i)})\cdot\mathrm{d}B_{k}^{(i)}.

We have by construction, for any fixed
\displaystyle y
, any fixed numerical parameters,
that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{i}M^{(i),\mathcal{S}_{i}f}_{T}M^{(i),g_{i}}_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\langle\mathcal{S}_{i}f,g_{i}\rangle\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{q}.

An immediate consequence of Lemma
8

reads

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{i}M^{(i),f}_{T}M^{(i),g_{i}}_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{i}\mathcal{M}^{(i),f}_{\infty}M^{(i),g_{i}}_{\infty}

\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\lim_{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{i}M^{(i),\mathcal{S}_{i}f}_{T}M^{(i),g_{i}}_{T}=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}^{i}\mathcal{M}^{(i),\mathcal{R}_{i}f}_{\infty}M^{(i),g_{i}}_{\infty}

Passing to the limit as for the proof of Theorem
1
yields

\langle\mathcal{R}f,g\rangle\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}\|_{p\rightarrow p}\|f\|_{p}\|g\|_{q},

hence the desired result
\displaystyle\|\mathcal{R}\|_{p}\leqslant\|\mathcal{S}\|_{p}
.

\displaystyle\Box
