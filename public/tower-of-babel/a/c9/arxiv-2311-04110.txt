Elliptic units for complex cubic fields (On Eisenstein’s Jugendtraum)
Nicolas Bergeron, Pierre Charollois, Luis E. García

Abstract
. We propose a conjecture extending the classical construction of elliptic units to complex cubic number fields K \displaystyle K . The conjecture concerns special values of the elliptic gamma function, a holomorphic function of three complex variables arising in mathematical physics whose transformation properties under SL 3 ​ ( 𝐙 ) \displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z}) were studied by Felder and Varchenko in the early 2000s. Using this function we construct complex numbers that we conjecture to be units in narrow ray class fields of K \displaystyle K . We also propose a reciprocity law for the action of the Galois group on these units in the style of Shimura. To support our conjecture we offer numerical evidence and also prove a new type of Kronecker limit formula relating the logarithm of the modulus of these complex numbers to the derivatives at s = 0 \displaystyle s=0 of partial zeta functions of K \displaystyle K . Our constructions unveil the role played by the elliptic gamma function in Hilbert’s twelfth problem for complex cubic fields.

1.
Introduction

Elliptic units are certain special units in abelian extensions of imaginary quadratic number fields. One key object of this theory is the theta function

\theta_{0}(z,\tau)=\prod_{n\geq 0}(1-e^{2\pi i(n\tau+z)})(1-e^{2\pi i((n+1)\tau-z)}).

The infinite product converges absolutely when
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\tau)>0
and defines a holomorphic function on the product of the complex plane by the complex upper half-plane
\displaystyle\mathscr{H}
. Up to a factor
\displaystyle ie^{\pi i(z-\tau/6)}\eta(\tau)
, where
\displaystyle\eta
is the Dedekind eta function, it is the first (odd) Jacobi theta function. The function
\displaystyle\theta_{0}
enjoys the periodicity properties

\begin{split}\theta_{0}(z+1,\tau)&=\theta_{0}(z,\tau)\\
\theta_{0}(z+\tau,\tau)&=-e^{-2\pi iz}\theta_{0}(z,\tau)\end{split}

in the variable
\displaystyle z
, as well as well-known transformation properties in the variable
\displaystyle\tau
under the usual action of the group
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
on
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\times\mathscr{H}
.

Taking quotients of values of
\displaystyle\theta_{0}
at special points
\displaystyle(v,\tau)
, where
\displaystyle v
is a rational number and
\displaystyle\tau
is a quadratic irrationality in
\displaystyle\mathscr{H}
, one obtains elliptic units. For example, let
\displaystyle v=1/3\in\mathbf{Q}
and
\displaystyle\tau=(2+i)/5\in\mathbf{Q}(i)
. Then

\theta_{0}(v,\tau)^{5}\cdot\theta_{0}(5v,5\tau)^{-1}

belongs to an abelian extension of
\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}(i)
ramified only above
\displaystyle 3
. This is a general phenomenon: similar expressions for other
\displaystyle v
and
\displaystyle\tau
allow for the analytic construction of units in abelian extensions of imaginary quadratic fields, encode special values of zeta functions through the Kronecker limit formula, and led Stark to his famous conjectures on the existence of special units related to Hecke L-functions. We refer to
[
32
,
11
,
18
,
33
]
for beautiful expositions of this subject.

Our article is motivated by the desire to transpose these classical results to the setting of complex cubic fields. We propose a conjecture that is very similar to the theory of elliptic units. The cornerstone analytic function becomes the elliptic gamma function. For complex numbers
\displaystyle z
,
\displaystyle\tau
,
\displaystyle\sigma
with
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\tau)>0
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(\sigma)>0
, it is defined by the absolutely convergent infinite product

(1.1)

\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma)=\prod_{j,k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1-e^{2\pi i((j+1)\tau+(k+1)\sigma-z)}}{1-e^{2\pi i(j\tau+k\sigma+z)}}.

Its domain is then extended to
\displaystyle(z,\tau,\sigma)\in\mathbf{C}\times(\mathbf{C}-\mathbf{R})^{2}
by the rules

\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(z-\tau,-\tau,\sigma)}=\frac{1}{\Gamma(z-\sigma,\tau,-\sigma)}.

Introduced by Ruijsenaars
[
34
]
, this function appears implicitly in Baxter’s work on the eight-vertex model in statistical physics. It is a solution of an elliptic version of Euler’s functional equation
\displaystyle\Gamma(z+1)=z\Gamma(z)
, in which the rational function
\displaystyle z
is replaced by the above theta function: we have

\begin{split}\Gamma(z+1,\tau,\sigma)&=\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma)\\
\Gamma(z+\tau,\tau,\sigma)&=\theta_{0}(z,\sigma)\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma),\end{split}

and a similar relation between
\displaystyle\Gamma(z+\sigma,\tau,\sigma)
and
\displaystyle\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma)
.
Now three periods
\displaystyle 1
,
\displaystyle\tau
,
\displaystyle\sigma
are involved, and one might expect the elliptic gamma function to have

\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z})
-modular properties. This was shown to be the case by Felder and Varchenko
[
25
]
, who studied this function in depth and established identities such as

\Gamma(z/\tau,-1/\tau,\sigma/\tau)=e^{i\pi P(z;\tau,\sigma)}\Gamma((z-\tau)/\sigma,-\tau/\sigma,-1/\sigma)\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma)

for an explicit polynomial
\displaystyle P\in\mathbf{Q}(\tau,\sigma)[z]
. The resulting transformation properties of
\displaystyle\Gamma(z,\tau,\sigma)
are higher analogues of the
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z})
-modular properties of the theta function
\displaystyle\theta_{0}(z,\tau)

[
25
,
24
]
.

Let
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbf{C}
be a complex cubic number field. Along the lines of Hilbert’s 12th problem we propose a way to produce complex numbers conjecturally lying in specific abelian extensions of
\displaystyle K
. These numbers are obtained by taking products and quotients of the elliptic gamma function evaluated at special points
\displaystyle(v,\tau,\sigma)
belonging to
\displaystyle K^{3}
. Let us give a first example before stating the general conjecture.

Example.
Consider the complex cubic field
\displaystyle K=\mathbf{Q}(\beta)
with
\displaystyle\beta=\sqrt[3]{7}\cdot e^{-\frac{2i\pi}{3}}
, so that
\displaystyle\beta^{3}=7
. Define the point

[\tau:\sigma:1]\in\mathbf{C}\mathrm{P}^{2}-\mathbf{R}\mathrm{P}^{2},\quad\tau=\frac{2\beta+\beta^{2}}{15}\in\overline{\mathscr{H}},\quad\sigma=-\frac{2+\beta}{15}\in\mathscr{H}.

The complex number

(1.2)

\Gamma\left(1/3,\tau,\sigma\right)^{5}\cdot\Gamma\left(5/3,5\tau,5\sigma\right)^{-1}\approx-4.024029545\ldots-i\cdot 41.85595177\ldots

coincides (to 1000 digits at least) with a complex root of the polynomial

(1.3)

Q=x^{6}+(6\beta^{2}-14\beta+2)x^{5}+(4\beta^{2}-6\beta+2)x^{4}\\
+(106\beta^{2}-152\beta-103)x^{3}+(4\beta^{2}-6\beta+2)x^{2}+(6\beta^{2}-14\beta+2)x+1.

This polynomial is irreducible over
\displaystyle K
and its splitting field
\displaystyle H
is a cyclic totally complex abelian extension of
\displaystyle K
of degree
\displaystyle 6
ramified only at
\displaystyle 3
. The roots of
\displaystyle Q
are units in
\displaystyle H
; the polynomial
\displaystyle Q
being palindromic, the inverse of (
1.2
) is also a root of
\displaystyle Q
. The other four roots are (numerically with similar precision) obtained again using the same formula (
1.2
) now with

(\tau,\sigma)=\left(\frac{\beta^{2}+2\beta+75}{345},-\frac{\beta+32}{345}\right)\quad\mbox{and}\quad\left(\frac{\beta^{2}+2\beta+15}{150},-\frac{\beta-43}{150}\right).

Returning to the general setting, let us consider an arbitary complex cubic field
\displaystyle K\subset\mathbf{C}
and fix a proper ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
of its ring of integers
\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{K}
. Class field theory shows that there exists a number field
\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})\subset\mathbf{C}
containing
\displaystyle K
that is maximal among abelian extensions of
\displaystyle K
ramified only at
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
. The field
\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})
is called the narrow ray class field of conductor
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
. In Section
2
, assuming that
\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})
is totally complex, we introduce complex numbers
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{fb}^{-1})
, depending on
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
as well as on some auxiliary data, obtained as a product of values of the elliptic gamma function (
1.1
) evaluated at elements of
\displaystyle K
.

Our main conjecture is as follows (see Conjecture
2.3
, we highlight here only two of the three claims):

Conjecture.

(1)

The complex numbers
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{fb}^{-1})
belong to
\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})
. More precisely, they are
units
in this number field, that is
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{fb}^{-1})\in\mathscr{O}_{K(\mathfrak{f})}^{\times}
.

(2)

The action of the Galois group
\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(K(\mathfrak{f})/K)
on the units
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{fb}^{-1})
can be described explicitly: if
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}\in\mathrm{Gal}(K(\mathfrak{f})/K)
is the element assigned to an ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
relatively prime to
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
by the Artin map, then we have the
reciprocity law

\sigma_{\mathfrak{b}}(\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{f}))=\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{fb}^{-1}).

These properties are very similar to the expected properties of units whose existence is predicted by celebrated conjectures of Stark, known as
Stark units
. In Section
3.3
we describe the precise relation between the two units. We show that our conjecture provides a formula for Stark units themselves rather than just their absolute values. This answers (conjecturally) a question plainly raised by Samit Dasgupta in
[
16
]
, and unexpectedly relates the elliptic gamma function to Hilbert’s 12th problem asking for transcendental functions that provide explicit constructions of class fields.

Throughout the paper we consider several examples and provide numerical evidence for the above properties. We pay special attention to an example studied by Samit Dasgupta
[
16
]
and we relate our conjectural units to the alleged Stark unit he found. Building on a subtle study of Shintani zeta functions associated to cones, Ren and Sczech
[
31
]
have proposed another conjectural formula for the Stark unit. We numerically check on their examples that our formulas give the same complex numbers.

Going beyond numerical evidence, our main result (Theorem
3.2
) relates our elliptic gamma values to the first derivative at
\displaystyle s=0
of partial zeta functions of
\displaystyle K
. For an ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
coprime to
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
, define the partial zeta function

\zeta_{\mathfrak{f}}(\mathfrak{b},s)=\sum_{\mathfrak{c}\in[\mathfrak{b}]}\frac{1}{\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{c})^{s}},\quad\mathrm{Re}(s)>1.

Here the sum runs over integral ideals
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}
defining the same class as
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
in the narrow ray class group of conductor
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
and
\displaystyle\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{c})
denotes the absolute norm of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}
. These functions admit meromorphic continuation to
\displaystyle s\in\mathbf{C}
that vanishes at
\displaystyle s=0
. For ideals
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
coprime with
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
we set

\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(\mathfrak{b},0)=\frac{d}{ds}\left(\zeta_{\mathfrak{f}}(\mathfrak{ab},s)-\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\zeta_{\mathfrak{f}}(\mathfrak{b},s)\right)|_{s=0}.

Theorem.

Assume that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
,
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
satisfy the conditions in Section
2.2
. Then

\zeta_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b},0)=\log|\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(\mathfrak{fb}^{-1})|^{2}.

This theorem is an analogue, in the setting of complex cubic fields, of the classical Kronecker limit formula. It will be used in conjunction with our main conjecture to relate our units to Stark units. We have also verified this formula numerically in the examples considered in the paper. Coming back to the example above, the field
\displaystyle H
equals
\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})
for
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
the unique ideal of norm
\displaystyle 3
in
\displaystyle K
, and for appropriate choices of
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
our Theorem shows that

\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(\mathfrak{b},0)=\log\left|\frac{\Gamma\left(1/3,\tau,\sigma\right)^{5}}{\Gamma\left(5/3,5\tau,5\sigma\right)}\right|^{2}.

For the proof of Theorem
3.2
we consider a fiber bundle over the locally symmetric space of a congruence subgroup of
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z})
, with fiber the period domain
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mathrm{P}^{2}-\mathbf{R}\mathrm{P}^{2}
. We then introduce Eisenstein series on this space and relate two periods for them. The first, along a closed geodesic, equals the derivative at
\displaystyle 0
of a partial zeta function. We prove that the second period, along a new kind of modular symbol, equals the logarithm of the absolute value of products and quotients of the elliptic gamma function. Our Eisenstein series arise naturally from the equivariant point of view developed in
[
4
]
; the period comparison is similar to the main argument in
[
5
]
.

Several authors have worked on generalising Kronecker’s limit formulas and the related question of finding the correct analog of
\displaystyle|\eta(\tau)|
, see e.g.
[
20
,
21
,
3
]
. The resulting formulas take a complicated form involving integration and are not simply obtained by taking
\displaystyle\log|\cdot|
of a function with modular properties. Note also that the elliptic gamma function is not just an automorphic form but part of an “automorphic
\displaystyle 1
-cocycle” for
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z})

[
25
]
.

The geometric picture underlying our main construction is analogous to that of the recent work of Darmon and Vonk
[
15
]
on explicit class field theory for real quadratic fields. In general other related works, like
[
12
,
9
,
13
,
14
,
17
]
, are either non-archimedean or restricted to other types of fields.

2.
Values of the elliptic gamma function – a conjecture

In this section we state our main conjecture. After reviewing the definition and main properties of the elliptic gamma function in §
2.1
, we proceed to define a variant of this function depending on some arithmetic data, involving a choice of complex cubic field and some ideals, in §
2.2
. We then state our conjecture regarding the algebraicity and integrality of certain values of these functions, as well as a reciprocity law for the Galois action, in §
2.3
. A first numerical example is discussed in detail in §
2.4
.

2.1.
The elliptic gamma function

Let
\displaystyle L
be a free abelian group of rank three and write
\displaystyle\Lambda
for its dual
\displaystyle\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbf{Z}}(L,\mathbf{Z})
. We fix an orientation of
\displaystyle L
that we denote by
\displaystyle\det
, i.e. an isomorphism
\displaystyle\det:\wedge^{3}_{\mathbf{Z}}L\to\mathbf{Z}
. Let
\displaystyle V=\Lambda\otimes_{\mathbf{Z}}\mathbf{Q}
and write similarly
\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{R}}
and
\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{C}}
for the three–dimensional vector spaces over
\displaystyle\mathbf{R}
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}
obtained from
\displaystyle V
by extending scalars; our fixed orientation on
\displaystyle L
induces orientations on all these vector spaces that we still denote by
\displaystyle\det
.

Primitive elements in
\displaystyle\Lambda
are in one-to-one correspondence with oriented planes in
\displaystyle L\otimes\mathbf{Q}
: the plane
\displaystyle H(a)
corresponding to a primitive element
\displaystyle a\in\Lambda
is the kernel of
\displaystyle a
, which divides
\displaystyle L
into two subsets

L_{+}(a)=\{\alpha\in L\;\big|\;a(\alpha)>0\}\quad\mbox{and}\quad L_{-}(a)=\{\alpha\in L\;\big|\;a(\alpha)\leq 0\}.

An ordered basis
\displaystyle\lambda,\mu
of
\displaystyle H(a)
is (positively) oriented if
\displaystyle\det(\lambda,\mu,\delta)>0
whenever
\displaystyle\delta(a)>0
. A primitive element
\displaystyle a\in\Lambda
also determines an open subset
\displaystyle U_{a}\subset V_{\mathbf{C}}
defined by

(2.1)

U_{a}=\left\{z\in V_{\mathbf{C}}\;\big|\;\mathrm{Im}\left(\lambda(z)\overline{\mu(z)}\right)>0\right\}

for any oriented basis
\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu)
of
\displaystyle H(a)
.

Fix an ordered pair
\displaystyle(a,b)
of linearly independent primitive elements of
\displaystyle\Lambda
. It determines oriented planes
\displaystyle H(a),H(b)\subset L\otimes\mathbf{Q}
that intersect in a line spanned by the element
\displaystyle\det(a,b,\cdot)\in\Lambda^{\vee}=L
. This element is not primitive in general, but we may write

(2.2)

\det(a,b,\cdot)=s\gamma

for a unique pair
\displaystyle(s,\gamma)
consisting of a positive integer
\displaystyle s
and a primitive element
\displaystyle\gamma
of
\displaystyle L
.

To the pair
\displaystyle(a,b)
we also associate the cones

(2.3)

\begin{split}C_{\texttt{+-}}(a,b)&=\{\delta\in L\;\big|\;\delta(a)>0,\ \delta(b)\leq 0\}\\
C_{\texttt{-+}}(a,b)&=\{\delta\in L\;\big|\;\delta(a)\leq 0,\ \delta(b)>0\}.\end{split}

Following first works of Ruijsenaars
[
34
]
and Felder and Varchenko
[
25
]
, Felder, Henriques, Rossi and Zhu
[
24
]
defined the
elliptic gamma function

(2.4)

\Gamma_{a,b}(w,z;L)=\frac{\prod_{\delta\in C_{\texttt{+-}}(a,b)/\mathbf{Z}\gamma}\left(1-e^{-2\pi i(\delta(z)-w)/\gamma(z)}\right)}{\prod_{\delta\in C_{\texttt{-+}}(a,b)/\mathbf{Z}\gamma}\left(1-e^{2\pi i(\delta(z)-w)/\gamma(z)}\right)}\cdot

The product (
2.4
) converges to a meromorphic function on
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\times(U_{a}\cap U_{b})
. It has simple zeros at
\displaystyle w=\delta(z)+n\gamma(z)
, with
\displaystyle\delta\in C_{\texttt{+-}}(a,b)
,
\displaystyle n\in\mathbf{Z}
, and simple poles at
\displaystyle w=\delta(z)+n\gamma(z)
, with
\displaystyle\delta\in C_{\texttt{-+}}(a,b)
,
\displaystyle n\in\mathbf{Z}
(
[
24
, Prop. 3.3]
).

It is also proved in
[
24
]
that the function (
2.4
) can be expressed in terms of the basic elliptic gamma function
\displaystyle\Gamma
defined in the introduction. For this, let
\displaystyle\alpha,\beta\in L
be such that

(2.5)

\alpha(b)=\beta(a)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad\alpha(a)>0,\quad\beta(b)>0.

Then
\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
is an oriented basis of
\displaystyle L\otimes\mathbf{Q}
, hence
\displaystyle(\beta,\gamma)
and
\displaystyle(\gamma,\alpha)
are oriented bases of
\displaystyle H(a)
and
\displaystyle H(b)
respectively and so

(2.6)

U_{a}\cap U_{b}=\{z\in V_{\mathbf{C}}\;\big|\;\mathrm{Im}(\alpha(z)/\gamma(z))<0\mbox{ and }\mathrm{Im}(\beta(z)/\gamma(z))>0\}.

Then Proposition 3.5 of loc. cit. shows that

(2.7)

\Gamma_{a,b}(w,z;L)=\prod_{\delta\in F/\mathbf{Z}\gamma}\Gamma\left(\frac{w+\delta(z)}{\gamma(z)},\frac{\alpha(z)}{\gamma(z)},\frac{\beta(z)}{\gamma(z)}\right).

Here the product is finite and
\displaystyle F
is the set of
\displaystyle\delta\in L
such that

0\leq\delta(a)<\alpha(a)\quad\mbox{and}\quad 0\leq\delta(b)<\beta(b).

The group
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(\Lambda)\cong\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z})
acts on
\displaystyle\Lambda
,
\displaystyle V
,
\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{C}}
. If
\displaystyle a
is a primitive vector in
\displaystyle\Lambda
and
\displaystyle g
an element in
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(\Lambda)
, then
\displaystyle ga
is primitive, we have
\displaystyle U_{ga}=gU_{a}
, and

(2.8)

\Gamma_{ga,gb}(w,gz;L)=\Gamma_{a,b}(w,z;L),\quad z\in U_{a}\cap U_{b}.

The group
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}(\Lambda)
also acts on
\displaystyle L
by duality.

2.2.
Complex cubic fields and values of the elliptic gamma function

Let
\displaystyle K
be a complex cubic number field over
\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}
and let
\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{K}
be its ring of integers. We denote by
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}:K\hookrightarrow\mathbf{R}
the unique real embedding of
\displaystyle K
and we fix once and for all a complex embedding
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}:K\hookrightarrow\mathbf{C}
of
\displaystyle K
. The map
\displaystyle x\mapsto(\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(x),\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(x))
induces an isomorphism
\displaystyle K\otimes\mathbf{R}\simeq\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{C}
and hence an orientation on
\displaystyle K
by pulling back the standard orientation on
\displaystyle\mathbf{R}\times\mathbf{C}
.

Let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{b}
be non-zero ideals of
\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{K}
with
\displaystyle(\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{b})=1
. Write
\displaystyle L=\mathfrak{fb}^{-1}
; it is a free
\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}
–module of rank three and a fractional ideal of
\displaystyle K
and the unit
\displaystyle 1_{K}\in K
is a primitive
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
-division point of
\displaystyle L
. We denote by
\displaystyle q
the smallest positive integer in
\displaystyle L\cap\mathbf{Z}
(equivalently, in
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}\cap\mathbf{Z}
), so that
\displaystyle q
is the order of
\displaystyle 1_{K}
in
\displaystyle L\otimes\mathbf{Q}/L
. The group of units

\mathscr{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{+,\times}=\{u\in\mathscr{O}_{K}^{\times}\;\big|\;u-1_{K}\in\mathfrak{f}\mbox{ and }\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(u)>0\}

is of rank
\displaystyle 1
, it acts by multiplication on the
\displaystyle 3
-dimensional
\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}
-vector space
\displaystyle K
and preserves the lattice
\displaystyle L
. We assume that this group equals
\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{K}^{\times}\cap(1+\mathfrak{f})
, i.e. that there are no units in
\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{K}^{\times}\cap(1+\mathfrak{f})
of negative norm (in particular, this implies that
\displaystyle-1\notin 1+\mathfrak{f}
and hence that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}\neq\mathscr{O}_{K}
and
\displaystyle q\geq 3
); equivalently, the real place of
\displaystyle K
ramifies in the narrow ray class field of
\displaystyle K
of conductor
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
. We fix the generator
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in\mathscr{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{+,\times}
such that
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}:=\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(\varepsilon)\in(0,1)
.

We will now define some complex numbers
\displaystyle\mathbf{\Gamma}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(L)
using the elliptic gamma function. As the notation indicates, these depend on
\displaystyle L
as well as on some auxiliary data consisting of an ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathscr{O}_{K}
and an element
\displaystyle h\in L\otimes\mathbf{Q}
. These data must satisfy the following conditions: for the ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
we require that

(2.9)

\mathfrak{a}\text{ is relatively prime to }\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{f})\text{ and }\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\text{ is an odd prime,}

while for
\displaystyle h
we require that

(2.10)

h=\lambda/q

for
\displaystyle\lambda
an
admissible
element of
\displaystyle L
. Here we say that
\displaystyle\lambda\in L
is admissible if it satisfies the following congruence conditions:

•

\displaystyle\lambda/q
is congruent to
\displaystyle 1_{K}
modulo
\displaystyle L
,

•

\displaystyle\lambda/\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})
belongs to
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L
and generates the quotient
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L/L
.

These conditions are equivalent to
\displaystyle\lambda\in\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L
with
\displaystyle\lambda\equiv q\mod qL
and
\displaystyle\lambda\not\equiv 0\mod\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})L
, and so the existence of admissible elements follows from the isomorphism

\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\mathfrak{a}^{-1}\mathfrak{f}/(q\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a}))\mathfrak{f}\simeq\mathfrak{f}/q\mathfrak{f}\times\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\mathfrak{a}^{-1}/(\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})).

We note that if
\displaystyle\lambda
is an admissible element of
\displaystyle L
and
\displaystyle u\in\mathscr{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{+,\times}
, then
\displaystyle u\lambda
is an admissible element of
\displaystyle L
. If
\displaystyle\lambda
is admissible for
\displaystyle L=\mathfrak{fb}^{-1}

\displaystyle\alpha\in 1+\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle\alpha^{-1}\lambda
is admissible for
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}((\alpha)\mathfrak{b})^{-1}
.

Let us now fix
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
and
\displaystyle h=\lambda/q
with
\displaystyle\lambda\in L
admissible as above. The restriction of our fixed complex embedding
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}:K\to\mathbf{C}
to
\displaystyle L
defines a non-zero element

(2.11)

{\bf x}\in V_{\mathbf{C}}=\mathrm{Hom}(L,\mathbf{C})

and therefore a point
\displaystyle\langle{\bf x}\rangle
in

(2.12)

\mathbf{X}=(V_{\mathbf{C}}-\mathbf{C}\cdot V_{\mathbf{R}})/\mathbf{C}^{\times}=\mathbf{C}\mathrm{P}^{2}-\mathbf{R}\mathrm{P}^{2}.

It will be convenient to write write
\displaystyle\det:\wedge_{\mathbf{Z}}^{3}L\to\mathbf{Z}
for the isomorphism induced by the orientation of
\displaystyle K
fixed above. The unit
\displaystyle\varepsilon
acts by multiplication on
\displaystyle K

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\displaystyle\lambda\in L
be admissible. There exists a unique primitive element

Lemma 2.2

(Distribution relation)
.

2.3.
The main conjecture

Let
\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})
be the narrow ray class field of
\displaystyle K
of conductor
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle K
that is maximal among abelian extensions ramified only at
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle\mathscr{H}

Denote by
\displaystyle I(\mathfrak{f})
the group of fractional ideals of
\displaystyle K
generated by ideals coprime to
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
, and by
\displaystyle P(\mathfrak{f})
and
\displaystyle P(\mathfrak{f})_{+}

\displaystyle(\alpha)
generated by elements
\displaystyle\alpha\in K^{\times}
such that
\displaystyle v_{\mathfrak{p}}(\alpha-1)\geq v_{\mathfrak{p}}(\mathfrak{f})

\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}
dividing
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(\alpha)>0
for
\displaystyle(\alpha)
in
\displaystyle P(\mathfrak{f})_{+}

According to class field theory, the narrow ray class group

\displaystyle\mathrm{Gal}(K(\mathfrak{f})/K)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{c}
in
\displaystyle I(\mathfrak{f})

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathfrak{p}}(x)\equiv x^{\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{p})}\mod\mathfrak{P}
when
\displaystyle\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{P}\cap\mathscr{O}_{K}

Conjecture.

Let
\displaystyle\mathfrak{f}
be an ideal of
\displaystyle\mathscr{O}_{K}

\displaystyle K(\mathfrak{f})
is totally complex and fix a complex embedding

\displaystyle 6\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{f})

(1)

defines a section of
\displaystyle\mathscr{V}^{\vee}
, and
\displaystyle\psi(v)
is a multiple of the pullback to
\displaystyle\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{X}
(via this section) of a double transgression of the canonical Thom form of
\displaystyle\mathscr{V}^{\vee}

[
29
]
.

For
\displaystyle t>0
, define

(4.8)

\Theta_{\psi_{t}}(h,L)=\sum_{\lambda\in h+L}\psi(t\lambda)\in\Omega^{1}(\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{X})

and set

(4.9)

E_{\psi}(h,L,s)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\Theta_{\psi_{t}}(h,L)t^{3s}\frac{dt}{t}.

The integral converges for any
\displaystyle s\in\mathbf{C}
; this follows from the standard argument splitting the integration domain into the intervals
\displaystyle(0,1)
and
\displaystyle(1,\infty)
and applying Poisson summation (note that
\displaystyle\psi
is odd and so both
\displaystyle\psi
and its Fourier transform vanish at the origin).

Note that for
\displaystyle g\in G
we have

g^{*}\psi(gv)=\psi(v).

In particular, the differential forms
\displaystyle\Theta_{\psi}(h,L)
and
\displaystyle E_{\psi}(h,L,s)
are invariant under

\Gamma_{1}(h)=\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(L)\;|\;gh\equiv h\mod L\},

and so we have

\Theta_{\psi}(h,L)\in\Omega^{1}(\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{X})^{\Gamma_{1}(h)}\simeq\Omega^{1}(\Gamma_{1}(h)\backslash(\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{X}))

and

E_{\psi}(h,L,0)\in\Omega^{1}(\overline{\mathbf{S}}\times\mathbf{X})^{\Gamma_{1}(h)}\simeq\Omega^{1}(\Gamma_{1}(h)\backslash(\overline{\mathbf{S}}\times\mathbf{X})).

4.2.2.
Generalized modular symbols

A point
\displaystyle x=\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle\in\mathbf{X}
determines a point
\displaystyle s_{x}\in\overline{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{Sat}}
corresponding to the family of degenerate (rank two) quadratic forms obtained by rescaling the quadratic form
\displaystyle v\mapsto|\mathbf{x}(v)|^{2}
for some non-zero generator
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
of
\displaystyle x
.

The vector
\displaystyle a\in\Lambda
spans a line
\displaystyle\langle a\rangle\subset V_{\mathbf{R}}
that also determines a point
\displaystyle s_{\langle a\rangle}
in
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{Sat}}
corresponding to the degenerate (rank one) quadratic forms obtained by rescaling the quadratic form
\displaystyle v\mapsto a(v)^{2}
.

The convex hull of
\displaystyle s_{\langle a\rangle}
and
\displaystyle s_{x}
is then a compact interval in
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{Sat}}
. We regard this interval as the image of a singular
\displaystyle 1
-chain

\Delta_{\langle a\rangle,x}:[0,1]\to\overline{\mathbf{S}}^{\mathrm{Sat}}.

Its interior
\displaystyle\Delta_{\langle a\rangle,x}^{0}
consists of the image in
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}
of all quadratic forms of the form
\displaystyle v\mapsto t_{1}a(v)^{2}+t_{2}|\mathbf{x}(v)|^{2}
. A choice of
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
gives a parametrisation

(4.10)

\mathbf{R}_{>0}\xrightarrow{\sim}\Delta_{\langle a\rangle,x}^{0},\quad t_{1}\mapsto(v\mapsto t_{1}^{-2}a(v)^{2}+t_{1}|\mathbf{x}(v)|^{2})\ \ \mathrm{mod}\ Z^{0}.

Let
\displaystyle x\in\mathbf{X}
. The
generalised modular symbol

\displaystyle[a,b]_{x}
is the
\displaystyle 1
-chain defined by

[a,b]_{x}=\Delta_{\langle a\rangle,x}-\Delta_{\langle b\rangle,x}.

The obvious properties of these chains justify our terminology (cf.
[
2
, Prop. 2.2]
).

4.2.3.
The closed geodesic

Let us write
\displaystyle\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle\in\mathbf{X}
for the complex line in
\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{C}}
spanned by the complex embedding
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}:=\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}
. Since
\displaystyle h\in\mathfrak{b}^{-1}
, we have
\displaystyle(\varepsilon-1)h\in\mathfrak{fb}^{-1}=L
and so
\displaystyle\varepsilon\in\Gamma_{1}(h)
. For any
\displaystyle t_{1}>0
, define a metric
\displaystyle H_{t_{1}}\in\mathbf{S}
by

(4.11)

H_{t_{1}}(v)=t_{1}^{-2}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}+t_{1}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2},\quad v\in V^{\vee}_{\mathbf{R}}.

Let us write
\displaystyle\overline{H}
for the point in
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}=\mathbf{S}/Z^{0}
defined by a positive definite quadratic form
\displaystyle H\in\mathbf{S}
. Since
\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}t_{1}}(\varepsilon v)=H_{t_{1}}(v)
, the assignment

\iota:\mathbf{R}_{>0}\to\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{X},\qquad t_{1}\mapsto(H_{t_{1}},\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle)

induces a map

\iota:\langle\varepsilon^{n}_{\mathbf{R}}\;|\;n\in\mathbf{Z}\rangle\backslash\mathbf{R}_{>0}\to\Gamma_{1}(h)\backslash(\overline{\mathbf{S}}\times\mathbf{X}).

Its image is a closed oriented geodesic in
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(h)\backslash(\mathbf{S}\times\mathbf{X})
that we denote by
\displaystyle\langle\varepsilon\rangle
.

Lemma 4.2
.

\int_{\langle\varepsilon\rangle}E_{\psi}(h,L,s)=\frac{1}{6}\Gamma(s)\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)\sum_{v\in(1+L)/\mathscr{O}_{\mathfrak{f}}^{+,\times}}\frac{\mathrm{sgn}(\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v))}{|\mathrm{N}(v)|^{s}}.

Proof.

Writing
\displaystyle e_{t_{1}}
for the positive generator of the fiber of
\displaystyle\mathscr{W}
over
\displaystyle\iota(t_{1})
, we have
\displaystyle(v,e_{t_{1}})=t_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)
and hence

\iota^{*}\psi(v)=e^{-t_{1}^{-2}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}-t_{1}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2}}t_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}.

This expression remains invariant upon replacing
\displaystyle(v,t_{1})
with
\displaystyle(\varepsilon v,\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}t_{1})
(use
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}|\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}|^{2}=1
). Since
\displaystyle h\equiv 1\mod L
, this gives

\begin{split}\int_{\langle\varepsilon\rangle}&E_{\psi}(h,L,s)\\
&=\int_{t_{0}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}}^{t_{0}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{v\in 1+L}e^{-t_{1}^{-2}t^{2}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}-t_{1}t^{2}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2}}tt_{1}^{-1}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}t^{3s}\frac{dt}{t}\\
&=\sum_{v\in(1+L)/\mathscr{O}^{+,\times}_{\mathfrak{f}}}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-t_{1}^{-2}t^{2}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}-t_{1}t^{2}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2}}t_{1}^{-1}t^{3s+1}\frac{dt_{1}dt}{t_{1}t}.\end{split}

The integral can be computed using the change of variables
\displaystyle u=t_{1}^{-1}t
,
\displaystyle w=t_{1}t^{2}
, which gives

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}&e^{-u^{2}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}-w|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2}}(u^{2}w^{-1})^{1/3}(uw)^{s+1/3}\frac{1}{3}\frac{dudw}{uw}\\
&=\frac{1}{3}\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-u^{2}\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}}u^{1+s}\frac{du}{u}\right)\left(\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-w|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2}}w^{s}\frac{dw}{w}\right)\\
&=\frac{1}{|\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)|^{1+s}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2s}}\frac{1}{6}\Gamma\left(\frac{s+1}{2}\right)\Gamma\left(s\right),\end{split}

proving the claim.
∎

For an auxiliary ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
as in Section
2.2
, define the “
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
–smoothed” forms

(4.12)

\begin{split}\Theta_{\psi_{t},\mathfrak{a}}(h,L)&=\Theta_{\psi_{t}}(h,\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L)-\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\Theta_{\psi_{t}}(h,L)\\
E_{\psi,\mathfrak{a}}(h,L,s)&=E_{\psi}(h,\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L,s)-\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})E_{\psi}(h,L,s).\end{split}

Then Lemmas
4.2.3
and
3.1
imply

(4.13)

\int_{\langle\varepsilon\rangle}E_{\psi,\mathfrak{a}}(h,\mathfrak{fb}^{-1},0)=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{3}\zeta_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b},0).

4.2.4.
Period on a generalized modular symbol

For
\displaystyle t_{1}>0
, define metrics
\displaystyle H_{a,t_{1}},H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}\in\mathbf{S}
by

(4.14)

\begin{split}H_{a,t_{1}}(v)&=t_{1}^{-2}a(v)^{2}+t_{1}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2},\\
H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}(v)&=t_{1}^{-2}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}^{2}b(v)^{2}+t_{1}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2},\quad v\in V^{\vee}_{\mathbf{R}}.\end{split}

We obtain a map

\iota_{\langle a\rangle}:\mathbf{R}_{>0}\to\overline{\mathbf{S}}\times\mathbf{X},\quad t_{1}\mapsto(\overline{H}_{a,t_{1}},\langle\mathbf{x}\rangle).

Contrary to the map
\displaystyle\iota
in the previous subsection, this map does not have compact image in
\displaystyle\Gamma_{1}(h)\backslash(\overline{\mathbf{S}}\times\mathbf{X})
. However, as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to\infty
the image of
\displaystyle\iota_{\langle a\rangle}
approaches the closed geodesic defined by
\displaystyle\iota
(see Figure
1
). More precisely, we have the following.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\displaystyle\alpha\in\Omega^{1}(\Gamma_{1}(h)\backslash(\overline{\mathbf{S}}\times\mathbf{X}))
. Then

\int_{\langle\varepsilon\rangle}\alpha=\lim_{t_{1}\to\infty}\int_{t_{1}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}}^{t_{1}}\iota_{\langle a\rangle}^{*}\alpha.

Proof.

For any
\displaystyle t_{1}>0
, we have

\int_{\langle\varepsilon\rangle}\alpha=\int_{t_{1}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}}^{t_{1}}\iota^{*}\alpha.

Since
\displaystyle H_{x^{3}a,x^{2}t_{1}}=x^{2}H_{a,t_{1}}
for any
\displaystyle x>0
, we may, rescaling
\displaystyle a
if necessary, assume that
\displaystyle H_{t_{1}}
and
\displaystyle H_{a,t_{1}}
have the same determinant. It then suffices to show that the distance
\displaystyle d(\overline{H}_{t_{1}},\overline{H}_{a,t_{1}})
(where
\displaystyle d
denotes a
\displaystyle G
–invariant Riemannian distance on
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}
) is rapidly decreasing as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to\infty
. Let
\displaystyle P
be the real parabolic of
\displaystyle G/Z^{0}
stabilizing the plane
\displaystyle\langle\mathrm{Re}(\mathbf{x}),\mathrm{Im}(\mathbf{x})\rangle
. Let
\displaystyle P=N_{P}A_{P}M_{P}
be its Langlands decomposition and let
\displaystyle X_{P}=M_{P}/(M_{P}\cap K)
. Consider the associated horospherical decomposition (
[
8
, §I.1]
)

N_{P}\times A_{P}\times X_{P}\simeq\overline{\mathbf{S}},\quad(n,\exp(t_{1}H),m(M_{P}\cap K))\mapsto n\exp(t_{1}H)mK.

In the above coordinates the points
\displaystyle\overline{H}_{t_{1}}
and
\displaystyle\overline{H}_{a,t_{1}}
of
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}
both have
\displaystyle A_{P}
-component
\displaystyle\exp(t_{1}H)
and differ only by multiplication by an element in
\displaystyle N_{P}
. The explicit formula for the distance in
[
7
, Prop. 4.3]
shows that
\displaystyle d(\overline{H}_{t_{1}},\overline{H}_{a,t_{1}})
decreases exponentially as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to\infty
.
∎

Consider the metric
\displaystyle H_{a,t_{1}}
for some
\displaystyle t_{1}>0
and the corresponding point
\displaystyle\iota_{\langle a\rangle}(t_{1})\in\mathbf{S}
. Under this metric the subspaces
\displaystyle\ker(\sigma_{\mathbf{C}})
and
\displaystyle\ker(a)
of
\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{R}}^{\vee}
are orthogonal; the subspace
\displaystyle\ker(\sigma_{\mathbf{C}})
is the fiber over
\displaystyle\iota_{\langle a\rangle}(t_{1})
of the bundle
\displaystyle\mathscr{W}
, and
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}
identifies
\displaystyle\ker(a)
with the fiber of
\displaystyle\pi^{*}\mathscr{L}^{\vee}
(cf. (
4.7
)). Note that we must have
\displaystyle e_{\mathscr{W}}(a)<0
; otherwise, if
\displaystyle e_{\mathscr{W}}(a)>0
, then a basis
\displaystyle(\lambda,\mu)
of
\displaystyle\ker(a)
is oriented if and only if
\displaystyle(\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(\lambda),\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(\mu))
is an oriented basis of
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}
, which contradicts the condition that
\displaystyle\mathbf{x}\in U_{a}
. It follows that
\displaystyle(v,e_{\mathscr{W}})=-t_{1}^{-1}a(v)
.

For positive real numbers
\displaystyle t_{1},t
, let us define
\displaystyle\Theta_{a}(t_{1},t,h,L)
via

\iota^{*}_{\langle a\rangle}\Theta_{\psi_{t}}(h,L)=\Theta_{a}(t_{1},t,h,L)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}.

We may then write

(4.15)

\Theta_{a}(t_{1},t,h,L)=\sum_{v\in h+L}\psi^{0}(tv)_{a,t_{1}}

with

(4.16)

\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}=-t_{1}^{-1}a(v)e^{-t_{1}^{-2}a(v)^{2}-t_{1}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2}}.

We also have

\iota^{*}_{\langle a\rangle}\Theta_{\psi_{t},\mathfrak{a}}(h,L)=\Theta_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}

with

\Theta_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L):=\Theta_{a}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L)-\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\Theta_{a}(t_{1},t,h,L).

Lemma 4.4
.

For any
\displaystyle s\in\mathbf{C}
, the Mellin transform

\int_{0}^{\infty}|\Theta_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L)|t^{s}\frac{dt}{t}

decreases rapidly as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to 0
. In particular, the form

\displaystyle\iota_{\langle a\rangle}^{*}E_{\psi,\mathfrak{a}}(h,L,s)\in\Omega^{1}(\mathbf{R}_{>0})

is rapidly decreasing as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to 0
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the statement for the integrals over the domains
\displaystyle t\in(1,\infty)
and
\displaystyle t\in(0,1)
separately. Consider first the domain
\displaystyle t>1
: we have

(4.17)

\int_{1}^{\infty}|\Theta_{a}(t_{1},t,h,L)|t^{s}\frac{dt}{t}\leq\sum_{\lambda\in h+L}\int_{1}^{\infty}|\psi^{0}(t\lambda)_{a,t_{1}}|t^{s}\frac{dt}{t}

and a similar inequality with
\displaystyle L
replaced by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L
. Hence it suffices to show that the right hand side of (
4.17
), and the same sum with
\displaystyle L
replaced by
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L
, both decrease rapidly as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to 0
. To see this, let us take
\displaystyle\lambda_{1}\in L
with
\displaystyle a(\lambda_{1})=1
(recall
\displaystyle a\in\mathrm{Hom}(L,\mathbf{Z})
is primitive). Then we may write
\displaystyle L=(L\cap H(a))+\mathbf{Z}\lambda_{1}
and, since
\displaystyle a(h)=0
, also
\displaystyle h+L=((h+L)\cap H(a))+\mathbf{Z}\lambda_{1}
. Also, the Schwartz function
\displaystyle\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}
is given by (
4.16
); in particular,
\displaystyle\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}=0
when
\displaystyle v
belongs to
\displaystyle\ker(a)
. Hence we have

\begin{split}\sum_{\lambda\in h+L}|\psi^{0}(t\lambda)_{a,t_{1}}|&=\sum_{n\in\mathbf{Z}}\sum_{v\in(h+L)\cap H(a)}|\psi^{0}(t(n\lambda_{1}+v))|\\
&=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbf{Z}\\
n\neq 0\end{subarray}}t_{1}^{-1}t|n|e^{-t_{1}^{-2}t^{2}n^{2}}\sum_{v\in(h+L)\cap H(a)}e^{-t_{1}t^{2}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(n\lambda_{1}+v)|^{2}}.\end{split}

The inner sum is over a (translate of a) rank two lattice and so as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to 0
it is
\displaystyle O(t_{1}^{-1}t^{-2})
, where the implicit constant is independent of
\displaystyle n
. The rapid decrease of the right hand side of (
4.17
) for the lattices
\displaystyle L
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L
follows.

Now consider the integration over
\displaystyle t\in(0,1)
. Here we will apply Poisson summation. Let us write
\displaystyle\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}
for the Schwartz form in
\displaystyle\mathscr{S}(V(\mathbf{A}_{f})^{\vee})
defined by

(4.18)

\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}(v)=1_{\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L\otimes\hat{\mathbf{Z}}}(v-h)-\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})1_{L\otimes\hat{\mathbf{Z}}}(v-h)

(where
\displaystyle 1_{X}
denotes the indicator function of
\displaystyle X
),
so that

\Theta_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L)=\sum_{v\in V_{\mathbf{Q}}^{\vee}}\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}(v)\psi^{0}(tv)_{a,t_{1}}.

Writing
\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}\in\mathscr{S}(V(\mathbf{A}_{f}))
and
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}^{0}_{a,t_{1}}\in\mathscr{S}(V_{\mathbf{R}})

for the Fourier transforms of
\displaystyle\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}
and
\displaystyle\psi^{0}_{a,t_{1}}
, Poisson summation gives

\Theta_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L)=t^{-3}\sum_{w\in V_{\mathbf{Q}}}\hat{\varphi}_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}(w)\hat{\psi}^{0}_{a,t_{1}}(t^{-1}w).

Note that the integral of
\displaystyle\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}
over the line
\displaystyle\mathbf{A}_{f}\gamma\subset V(\mathbf{A}_{f})^{\vee}
vanishes. This implies that the restriction of
\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}
to
\displaystyle\ker(\gamma)
is identically zero. The argument now proceeds as in the previous case; it suffices to observe that a direct computation of the Fourier transform
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}^{0}_{a,t_{1}}
shows that, for a fixed
\displaystyle w_{1}\in V_{\mathbf{Q}}
,
\displaystyle w_{1}\not\in\ker(\gamma)
, one has a bound similar to the above, namely

\sum_{w\in ker(\gamma)}\hat{\varphi}_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}(w+nw_{1})\hat{\psi}^{0}(t^{-1}(w+nw_{1}))_{a,t_{1}}=O(e^{-\alpha t_{1}^{-1}t^{-1}n^{2}}),\ n\in\mathbf{Z},\ n\neq 0,

for some
\displaystyle\alpha>0
.
∎

Using these lemmas we obtain the following explicit expression for
\displaystyle\zeta_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}^{\prime}(\mathfrak{b},0)
. Let us define

F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})=\Theta_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L)-\Theta_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,L).

Lemma 4.5
.

The function
\displaystyle F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(h,\mathfrak{b})
belongs to
\displaystyle L^{1}(\mathbf{R}_{>0}^{2},\tfrac{dt_{1}dt}{t_{1}t})
and

\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{3}\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(\mathfrak{b},0)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}.

The proof relies on the following bounds. Let us write
\displaystyle\hat{\psi}^{0}(\cdot)_{a,t_{1}}
for the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle\psi^{0}_{a,t_{1}}
, defined by

\hat{\psi}^{0}(w)_{a,t_{1}}=\int_{V_{\mathbf{R}}^{\vee}}\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}e^{-2\pi i(v,w)}dv,\quad w\in V_{\mathbf{R}},

with respect to some invariant measure
\displaystyle dv
. Recall the metrics
\displaystyle H_{a,t_{1}}
,
\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon b,t_{1}}
introduced in (
4.14
); part (i) of the next Lemma quantifies the statement that the distance in
\displaystyle\overline{\mathbf{S}}
between the points defined by these metrics tends to zero as
\displaystyle t_{1}
grows (cf. Figure
1
).

Lemma 4.6
.

(i)

There exists
\displaystyle A>0
such that

\left|H_{a,t_{1}}(v)-H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}(v)\right|<At_{1}^{-3/2}H_{a,t_{1}}(v)

for every
\displaystyle v\in V^{\vee}_{\mathbf{R}}
and
\displaystyle t_{1}>1
.

(ii)

There exists
\displaystyle A>0
such that

|\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}-\psi^{0}(v)_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}|<At_{1}^{-3/2}e^{-H_{a,t_{1}}(v)/2}

for every
\displaystyle v\in V^{\vee}_{\mathbf{R}}
and
\displaystyle t_{1}\gg 1
.

(iii)

There exists
\displaystyle A>0
such that

|\hat{\psi}^{0}(w)_{a,t_{1}}-\hat{\psi}^{0}(w)_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}|<At_{1}^{-3/2}e^{-H_{a,t_{1}}(w)/2}

for every
\displaystyle w\in V_{\mathbf{R}}
and
\displaystyle t_{1}\gg 1
.

Proof.

Let us first prove (i). We have

(4.19)

\left|H_{a,t_{1}}(v)-H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}(v)\right|=t_{1}^{-2}|a(v)^{2}-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}^{2}b(v)^{2}|.

Let
\displaystyle C_{0}>0
be such that

|a(v)|\leq C_{0}(\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v)^{2}+|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|^{2})^{1/2}

for all
\displaystyle v\in V^{\vee}_{\mathbf{R}}
.
Since
\displaystyle b(v)=(\varepsilon a)(v)=a(\varepsilon^{-1}v)
, we have

\begin{split}|a(v)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b(v)|&=|a(v-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}\varepsilon^{-1}v)|\\
&\leq C_{0}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}\varepsilon^{-1}v)|\\
&\leq C_{0}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|+C_{0}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(\varepsilon^{-1}v)|\\
&=C_{0}(1+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}|\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}|^{-1})|\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v)|\\
&\leq C_{0}(1+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}|\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}|^{-1})t_{1}^{-1/2}H_{a,t_{1}}(v)^{1/2},\end{split}

where the first inequality follows from the fact that
\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{R}}(v-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}\varepsilon^{-1}v)=0
. Similarly we have
\displaystyle a(v)^{2}\leq t_{1}^{2}H_{a,t_{1}}(v)
and hence

\begin{split}|a(v)+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b(v)|&\leq 2|a(v)|+|a(v)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b(v)|\\
&\leq(2t_{1}+C_{0}(1+\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}|\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}|^{-1})t_{1}^{-1/2})H_{t_{1}}(v)^{1/2}.\end{split}

The bound (i) follows from (
4.19
) by multiplying the above two inequalities.

Consider now (ii). Using the explicit expression (
4.16
), we find

\begin{split}|\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}-\psi^{0}(v)_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}|\leq\ &|e^{-H_{a,t_{1}}(v)}-e^{-H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}(v)}|\cdot t_{1}^{-1}|\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b(v)|\\
&+e^{-H_{a,t_{1}}(v)}\cdot t_{1}^{-1}|a(v)-\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b(v)|,\end{split}

and so (ii) follows from the inequalities above together with the elementary inequality
\displaystyle|e^{x}-1|\leq|x|e^{|x|}
valid for
\displaystyle x\in\mathbf{R}
.

To prove (iii), consider the Schwartz form
\displaystyle\varphi
on
\displaystyle V^{\vee}_{\mathbf{R}}
defined by
\displaystyle\varphi(v)=v_{1}e^{-\pi Q(v)}
with
\displaystyle Q
as in (
4.5
). Let
\displaystyle g_{a,t_{1}}\in G
be
such that
\displaystyle\pi^{1/2}(g_{a,t_{1}}v)_{1}=t_{1}^{-1}a(v)
,
\displaystyle\pi^{1/2}(g_{a,t_{1}}v)_{2}=t_{1}^{1/2}\mathrm{Re}(\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v))
and
\displaystyle\pi^{1/2}(g_{a,t_{1}}v)_{3}=t_{1}^{1/2}\mathrm{Im}(\sigma_{\mathbf{C}}(v))
.
Then

\psi^{0}(v)_{a,t_{1}}=\varphi(g_{a,t_{1}}v)

and hence

\hat{\psi}^{0}(w)_{a,t_{1}}=C\det(g_{a,t_{1}})^{-1}\varphi({{}^{t}}g_{a,t_{1}}^{-1}w)

for some

.
(Note that
\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}=C\varphi
and

is independent of

.) Now (iii) can be proved in the same way as (ii).
∎

Proof of Lemma

.

For the integrability claim, note that the Mellin transform

converges absolutely for every

by the standard argument using Poisson summation. By Lemma

\displaystyle(0,1)

\displaystyle(1,\infty)

for some
\displaystyle C>0
, and so it suffices to show that the integral on the right hand side is bounded for
\displaystyle t_{1}>1
. To see this, let
\displaystyle\alpha\neq 0
be such that the restriction of

vanishes on the plane

and consider the metric

By (

\begin{split}\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{3}\zeta^{\prime}_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(\mathfrak{b},0)&=\lim_{T_{1}\to\infty}\int_{T_{1}}^{T_{1}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}}\iota_{\langle a\rangle}^{*}E_{\psi,\mathfrak{a}}(h,\mathfrak{fb}^{-1},0)\\

4.2.5.
Relation with the elliptic gamma function

Let us rewrite the integrand
\displaystyle F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}
in Lemma
4.2.4
as follows: define

f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,v)=\sum_{j=0}^{\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})-1}\left(\psi^{0}(t(v+j\frac{\gamma}{\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})}))_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}-\psi^{0}(t(v+j\frac{\gamma}{\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})}))_{a,t_{1}}\right)\\
-\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\left(\psi^{0}(tv)_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}-\psi^{0}(tv)_{a,t_{1}}\right).

Then

\begin{split}F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})&=\sum_{v\in h+L}f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,v)\\
&=\sum_{w\in L/\mathbf{Z}\gamma}\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h+w+k\gamma).\end{split}

Lemma 4.7
.

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}&F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}\\
&=\sum_{w\in L/\mathbf{Z}\gamma}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h+w+k\gamma)\right)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}.\end{split}

Proof.

Let

G_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})=\sum_{w\in h+L}\left|f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,v)\right|.

The arguments in the proofs of Lemmas
4.2.4
and
4.2.4
show that the integral
\displaystyle\smallint_{1}^{\infty}G_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\tfrac{dt}{t}
is
\displaystyle O(t_{1}^{-3/2})
as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to\infty
and is rapidly decreasing as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to 0
. Hence

(4.21)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{1}^{\infty}G_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\frac{dt}{t}\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}<\infty,

which implies that

\begin{split}\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}&F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}\\
&=\sum_{w\in L/\mathbf{Z}\gamma}\int_{1}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h+w+k\gamma)\right)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}.\end{split}

To prove the same identity with
\displaystyle\smallint_{1}^{\infty}\tfrac{dt}{t}
replaced by
\displaystyle\smallint_{0}^{1}\tfrac{dt}{t}
, let us first rewrite
\displaystyle F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}
using Poisson summation. To this end, choose
\displaystyle\alpha
and
\displaystyle\beta
as in Section
2.1
and let
\displaystyle\tau=\alpha(\mathbf{x})/\gamma(\mathbf{x})
and
\displaystyle\sigma=\beta(\mathbf{x})/\gamma(\mathbf{x})
. First define a Schwartz function
\displaystyle\varphi
on
\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{3}
by

\varphi(x)=-\sqrt{\pi}x_{1}e^{-\pi(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2})},\quad x=(x_{1},x_{2},x_{3})\in\mathbf{R}^{3}

and let

A_{a}=\pi^{-1/2}|\gamma(\mathbf{x})|\begin{pmatrix}\alpha(a)/|\gamma(\mathbf{x})|&0&0\\
\mathrm{Im}(\tau)&\mathrm{Im}(\sigma)&0\\
\mathrm{Re}(\tau)&\mathrm{Re}(\sigma)&1\end{pmatrix},\quad m(t_{1})=\begin{pmatrix}t_{1}^{-1}&0&0\\
0&t_{1}^{1/2}&0\\
0&0&t_{1}^{1/2}\end{pmatrix}.

Note that
\displaystyle A_{a}
is invertible and
\displaystyle m(t_{1})
has unit determinant. Then we have

\psi^{0}(x_{1}\alpha+x_{2}\beta+x_{3}\gamma)_{a,t_{1}}=\varphi(m(t_{1})A_{a}x).

Since
\displaystyle\varphi
is (up to multiplication by constants) its own Fourier transform, this shows that the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle x\mapsto\psi^{0}(t(x_{1}\alpha+x_{2}\beta+x_{3}\gamma))_{a,t_{1}}
is

x\mapsto\frac{1}{\det(A_{a})t^{3}}\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{a}^{-1}x).

Similar considerations apply with
\displaystyle a
replaced by
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b
(note that, since the metrics
\displaystyle H_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}(\varepsilon v)=H_{a,t_{1}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}^{-1}}(v)
, we have
\displaystyle\det(A_{a})=\det(A_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b})
). Hence writing
\displaystyle(\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\alpha_{3})=(\alpha,\beta,\gamma)
and setting

\varphi_{\mathfrak{a},h}(m_{1},m_{2},m_{3})=\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}(\Sigma m_{i}\alpha_{i})\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{A}_{f}^{3})

with
\displaystyle\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}
defined by (
4.18
), Poisson summation reads

\begin{split}&-F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\\
&=\sum_{m\in\mathbf{Q}^{3}}\varphi_{\mathfrak{a},h}(m)\left(\psi^{0}\left(\sum tm_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)_{a,t_{1}}-\psi^{0}\left(\sum tm_{i}\alpha_{i}\right)_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b,t_{1}}\right)\\
&=\frac{\det(A_{a})^{-1}}{Ct^{3}}\sum_{m\in\mathbf{Q}^{3}}\hat{\varphi}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(m)\left(\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{a}^{-1}m)-\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b}^{-1}m)\right)\end{split}

for some constant
\displaystyle C\neq 0
. Now the Schwartz function
\displaystyle\varphi_{h,L,\mathfrak{a}}
is a linear combination of functions of the form
\displaystyle\varphi_{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}\otimes\varphi_{\langle\gamma\rangle}
, where
\displaystyle\varphi_{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}\in\mathscr{S}(\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle\otimes_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{A}_{f})
and
\displaystyle\varphi_{\langle\gamma\rangle}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{Q}\gamma\otimes_{\mathbf{Q}}\mathbf{A}_{f})
, and where the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle\varphi_{\langle\gamma\rangle}
satisfies
\displaystyle\hat{\varphi}_{\langle\gamma\rangle}(0)=0
. Using the bounds in Lemma
4.2.4
we obtain

\begin{split}\frac{1}{C\det(A_{a})t^{3}}\sum_{m\in\mathbf{Q}^{3}}&|\hat{\varphi}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(m)\left(\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{a}^{-1}m)-\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b}^{-1}m)\right)|\\
&\leq C_{1}\det(A_{a}^{-1})t^{-3}t_{1}^{-3/2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\in\mathbf{Z}^{3}\\
\mathrm{N}(\mathfrak{a})\nmid m_{3}\end{subarray}}\varphi^{0}(C_{2}t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{a}^{-1}m),\end{split}

where
\displaystyle C_{1},C_{2}>0
and
\displaystyle\varphi^{0}(x)=e^{-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}}
is the standard Gaussian. Since the sum contains no terms with
\displaystyle m_{3}=0
, as in the proof of Lemma
4.2.4
one shows that the integral
\displaystyle\smallint_{0}^{1}\tfrac{dt}{t}
of this expression is rapidly decreasing as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to 0
. Applying Poisson summation again to this last expression shows that it equals

C^{\prime}t_{1}^{-3/2}\sum_{m_{i}\in\mathbf{Q}^{3}}\varphi_{f}(m)\varphi^{0}(C_{2}^{-1}tm(t_{1})A_{a}m).

for an appropriate Schwartz function
\displaystyle\varphi_{f}\in\mathscr{S}(\mathbf{A}_{f}^{3})
. The argument in the proof of Lemma
4.2.4
shows that the integral
\displaystyle\smallint_{0}^{1}\tfrac{dt}{t}
of the sum in this expression is bounded for
\displaystyle t_{1}>1
, and so the same integral of the whole expression is
\displaystyle O(t_{1}^{-3/2})
as
\displaystyle t_{1}\to\infty
. Overall, these estimates imply that we can exchange sum and integral and so write

\begin{split}&-C\det(A_{a})\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{\mathfrak{f},\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h,\mathfrak{b})\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}\\
&=\sum_{m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{m_{3}\in\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\varphi}_{\mathfrak{a},h}(m)\\
&\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad\times\left(\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{a}^{-1}m)-\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1}){{}^{t}}A_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b}^{-1}m)\right)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t^{4}}.\end{split}

To reach the expression in the statement we apply Poisson summation again, this time to the right hand side of this equation. That is, define a function
\displaystyle g:\mathbf{R}^{2}\to\mathbf{C}
by

\begin{split}g(x_{1},x_{2})=&\frac{1}{C\det(A_{a})}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{m_{3}\in\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\varphi}_{\langle\gamma\rangle}(m_{3}\gamma)\\
&\times\left(\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1})\cdot{{}^{t}}A_{a}^{-1}\cdot{{}^{t}}(x_{1},x_{2},m_{3}))\right.\\
&\left.\qquad-\varphi(t^{-1}m(t_{1}^{-1})\cdot{{}^{t}}A_{\varepsilon_{\mathbf{R}}b}^{-1}\cdot{{}^{t}}(x_{1},x_{2},m_{3}))\right)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t^{4}}.\end{split}

Our goal is to show that Poisson summation applies to
\displaystyle\Sigma_{m_{1},m_{2}\in\mathbf{Q}}\hat{\varphi}_{\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle}(m_{1}\alpha+m_{2}\beta)g(m_{1},m_{2})
. To see this, note that the estimates above show that
\displaystyle g
is a continuous and integrable function on
\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{2}
and that for any
\displaystyle l\in\mathbf{Q}^{2}
and lattice
\displaystyle L_{1}
in
\displaystyle\mathbf{Q}^{2}
, the series
\displaystyle G(x)=\Sigma_{m\in l+L_{1}}g(x+m)
converges absolutely to a continuous function
\displaystyle G
on
\displaystyle\mathbf{R}^{2}/L_{1}
. The Fourier coefficients of
\displaystyle G
are
\displaystyle G(\lambda)=\hat{g}(\lambda)e^{2\pi i\lambda(l)}
, where
\displaystyle\lambda\in L_{1}^{\vee}=\mathrm{Hom}(L_{1},\mathbf{Z})
and
\displaystyle\hat{g}
denotes the Fourier transform of
\displaystyle g
. Since
\displaystyle G
is continuous, Poisson summation (which is equivalent to the pointwise convergence of the Fourier series of
\displaystyle G
at
\displaystyle x=0
) holds if we can show that
\displaystyle\Sigma_{\lambda\in L_{1}^{\vee}}|\hat{g}(\lambda)|<\infty
.
This last convergence follows from (
4.21
) and the explicit computation below of the right hand side of the Lemma’s statement (cf. sentence after (
4.25
)).
∎

We can now explain how this lemma implies Theorem
3.2
. By Lemma
4.2.4
, it suffices to compute the integrals on the right hand side; for this it will be convenient to change variables and set

(4.22)

u=\pi|\gamma(\mathbf{x})|^{-2}t_{1}^{-1}t^{-2},\quad v=t_{1}^{-1}t.

Then
\displaystyle(t_{1}t)^{-1}dt_{1}dt=3^{-1}(uv)^{-1}dvdu
and so for fixed

we have

\begin{split}\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}&\left(\sum_{k\in\mathbf{Z}}f_{a,\mathfrak{a}}(t_{1},t,h+w+k\gamma)\right)\frac{dt_{1}}{t_{1}}\frac{dt}{t}\\

Lemma 4.8
.

We have

\displaystyle\log\left|\theta_{0}^{(N)}(z_{0},\sigma)/\theta_{0}^{(N)}(z_{0},-\tau)\right|=0
.

Proof.

Since
\displaystyle b=\varepsilon a
, multiplication by
\displaystyle\varepsilon
induces an isomorphism
\displaystyle L\cap H(a)\simeq L\cap H(b)
. Hence we can write

\begin{split}\varepsilon\beta&=-a^{\prime}\alpha+b^{\prime}\gamma\\
\varepsilon\gamma&=-c\alpha+d\gamma\end{split}

for some integers
\displaystyle a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c,d
with
\displaystyle a^{\prime}d-b^{\prime}c=\pm 1

\displaystyle\mathbf{x}
and writing
\displaystyle\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}=\varepsilon(\mathbf{x})\in\mathbf{C}^{\times}

\begin{split}\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}\beta(\mathbf{x})&=(\varepsilon\beta)(\mathbf{x})=-a^{\prime}\alpha(\mathbf{x})+b^{\prime}\gamma(\mathbf{x})\\
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}\gamma(\mathbf{x})&=(\varepsilon\gamma)(\mathbf{x})=-c\alpha(\mathbf{x})+d\gamma(\mathbf{x}).\end{split}

Dividing by
\displaystyle\gamma(\mathbf{x})
this gives

\begin{split}\sigma&=\frac{a^{\prime}\cdot(-\tau)+b^{\prime}}{c\cdot(-\tau)+d}\\
\varepsilon_{\mathbf{C}}&=c\cdot(-\tau)+d.\end{split}

Since
\displaystyle\sigma
and
\displaystyle-\tau
have positive imaginary part, this implies that
\displaystyle a^{\prime}d-b^{\prime}c=1
. Note that
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L\cap H(a)=\mathbf{Z}\beta+\mathbf{Z}(\gamma/N)
and
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L\cap H(b)=\mathbf{Z}\alpha+\mathbf{Z}(\gamma/N)

\displaystyle\varepsilon
preserves
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}^{-1}L

\displaystyle N
divides
\displaystyle c
. We also have
\displaystyle\lambda/q\equiv 1\mod L
and
\displaystyle\varepsilon\equiv 1\mod\mathfrak{f}
and hence

\begin{split}\varepsilon\frac{\lambda}{q}=\varepsilon+\varepsilon\left(\frac{\lambda}{q}-1\right)\in\varepsilon+L=\frac{\lambda}{q}+L.\end{split}

Thus
\displaystyle n(\varepsilon\gamma-\gamma)/q=(\epsilon\lambda-\lambda)/q\in L\cap H(b)
, giving
\displaystyle nc\equiv 0\mod q
and
\displaystyle n(d-1)\equiv 0\mod q
.

The statement now follows from the uniqueness principle in
[
27
, Prop. 1.3]
. For
\displaystyle z\in\mathbf{C}
, the function

satisfies

is odd) and so it defines a meromorphic function on the elliptic curve

Consider now the function

4.2.6.
A geometric interpretation of the elliptic gamma function

Felder–Henriques–Rossi–Zhu
[
24
]
gave a geometric interpretation of the elliptic gamma function. A
triptic curve
is a pointed holomorphic stack of the the form
\displaystyle[\mathbf{C}/\mathbf{Z}^{3}]
where the action is induced by a homomorphism
\displaystyle\mathbf{Z}^{3}\to\mathbf{C}
of real rank
\displaystyle 2
. It plays a role analogous to that of the elliptic curve for the theta function. The moduli space of oriented triptic curves is the complex stack

(4.33)

\left[\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z})\backslash(\mathbf{C}\mathrm{P}^{2}-\mathbf{R}\mathrm{P}^{2})\right].

The elliptic gamma function is a section of a hermitian abelian gerbe over the universal oriented triptic curve. Our construction can be understood in terms of a modified version of this gerbe, defined using the ideal
\displaystyle\mathfrak{a}
. The stack (
4.33
) is however far from being algebraic, thus proving our conjecture certainly requires new ideas. We nevertheless want to point out that — up to replacing
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{3}(\mathbf{Z})
by
\displaystyle\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbf{Z}[1/p])
and
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}\mathrm{P}^{2}-\mathbf{R}\mathrm{P}^{2}
by the product of
\displaystyle\mathscr{H}
by the Drinfeld upper half-plane — the above geometric picture seems quite parallel to that of the recent work of Darmon and Vonk
[
15
]
on explicit class field theory for real quadratic fields.

Appendix: Eisenstein’s Jugendtraum

This section provides some historical background underpinning our work,
deeply rooted in Gotthold Eisenstein’s largely ignored paper
[
22
]
.

In 1844, three years before his masterpiece
[
23
]
laying alternative foundations for a theory of elliptic functions, Eisenstein published in Crelle’s journal a short prospective note
[
22
]
where he first sketches his own construction of classical trigonometric and elliptic functions by considering nested conditionally convergent infinite products.

In the last pages, he indicates how one could go beyond and investigate higher dimensional cases.
Aware of the fact that no meromorphic functions over
\displaystyle\mathbf{C}
can be triply periodic, he first states:
