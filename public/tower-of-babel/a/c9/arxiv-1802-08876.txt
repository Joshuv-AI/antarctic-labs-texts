Lovász Meets Weisfeiler and Leman
Holger Dell, Martin Grohe, Gaurav Rattan

Abstract
In this paper, we relate a beautiful theory by Lovász with a popular heuristic algorithm for the graph isomorphism problem, namely the color refinement algorithm and its k k -dimensional generalization known as the Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm. We prove that two graphs G G and H H are indistinguishable by the color refinement algorithm if and only if, for all trees T T , the number 𝖧𝗈𝗆 ⁡ ( T , G ) \mathsf{Hom}(T,G) of homomorphisms from T T to G G equals the corresponding number 𝖧𝗈𝗆 ⁡ ( T , H ) \mathsf{Hom}(T,H) for H H . There is a natural system of linear equations whose nonnegative integer solutions correspond to the isomorphisms between two graphs. The nonnegative real solutions to this system are called fractional isomorphisms, and two graphs are fractionally isomorphic if and only if the color refinement algorithm cannot distinguish them (Tinhofer 1986, 1991). We show that, if we drop the nonnegativity constraints, that is, if we look for arbitrary real solutions, then a solution to the linear system exists if and only if, for all t t , the two graphs have the same number of length- t t walks. We lift the results for trees to an equivalence between numbers of homomorphisms from graphs of tree width k k , the k k -dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm, and the level- k k Sherali-Adams relaxation of our linear program. We also obtain a partial result for graphs of bounded path width and solutions to our system where we drop the nonnegativity constraints. A consequence of our results is a quasi-linear time algorithm to decide whether, for two given graphs G G and H H , there is a tree T T with 𝖧𝗈𝗆 ⁡ ( T , G ) ≠ 𝖧𝗈𝗆 ⁡ ( T , H ) \mathsf{Hom}(T,G)\neq\mathsf{Hom}(T,H) .

1
Introduction

An old result due to Lovász
[
18
]
states a graph
G
can
be characterized by counting homomorphisms from all graphs
F
to

G
. That is, two graphs
G
and
H
are isomorphic if and only if, for all

F
, the number
\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)
of homomorphisms from
F
to
G
equals
the number
\mathsf{Hom}(F,H)
of homomorphism from
F
to
H
. This simple
result has far reaching consequences, because mapping graphs
G
to
their
homomorphism vectors

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}(G):=\big(\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)\big)_{F\text{ graph}}
(or suitably scaled
versions of these infinite vectors) allows us to apply
tools from functional analysis in graph theory. This is the foundation
of the beautiful theory of graph limits, developed by Lovász and
others over the last 15 years (see
[
19
]
).

However, from a computational perspective, representing graphs by
their homomorphism vectors has the disadvantage that the problem of
computing the entries of these vectors is NP-complete. To avoid this
difficulty, we may want to restrict the homomorphism vectors to
entries from a class of graphs for which counting homomorphisms is
tractable. That is, instead of considering the full homomorphism
vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}(G)
we consider the vector

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G):=\big(\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)\big)_{F\in\mathcal{F}}
for a class
\mathcal{F}
of
graphs such that the problem of computing
\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)
for given graphs

F\in\mathcal{F}
and
G
is in polynomial time. Arguably the most natural
example of such a class
\mathcal{F}
is the class of all trees. More
generally, computing
\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)
for given graphs
F\in\mathcal{F}
and
G
is
in polynomial time for all classes
\mathcal{F}
of bounded tree width, and
under a natural assumption from parameterized complexity theory, it is
not in polynomial time for any class
\mathcal{F}
of unbounded tree width

[
11
]
. This immediately raises the question what the vector

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
, for a class
\mathcal{F}
of bounded tree width, tells us
about the graph
G
.

A first nice example (Proposition
9
) is that the
vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(G)
for the class
\mathcal{C}
of all cycles
characterizes the spectrum of a graph, that is, for graphs
G,H
we
have
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(H)
if and only if the
adjacency matrices of
G
and
H
have the same eigenvalues with the
same multiplicities. This equivalence is a basic observation in spectral graph theory (see
[
25
, Lemma 1]
).
Before we state deeper results along these lines, let us describe a different (though related) motivation for this research.

Determining the similarity between two graphs is an important
problem with many applications, mainly in machine learning, where it
is known as “graph matching” (e.g.
[
9
]
).
But how can the similarity between graphs be measured?
An obvious idea is to use the
edit distance
, which simply counts how many edges and vertices have to be deleted from or added to one graph to obtain the other.
However, two graphs that have a small edit distance can nevertheless be structurally quite dissimilar (e.g.
[
19
, Section 1.5.1]
).
The edit distance is also very hard to compute as it is closely related to the
notoriously difficult quadratic assignment problem (e.g.
[
3
,
21
]
).

Homomorphism vectors offer an alternative, more structurally oriented approach to measuring graph similarity.
After suitably scaling the vectors, we can can
compare them using standard vector norms. This idea is reminiscent of the “graph kernels” used in machine learning (e.g.
[
26
]
). Like the homomorphism vectors, many graph
kernels are based on the idea of counting certain patterns in graphs,
such as paths, walks, cycles or subtrees, and in fact any inner
product on the homomorphism vectors yields a graph kernel.

A slightly different type of graph kernel is the so-called Weisfeiler-Leman
(subtree) kernel
[
22
]
.
This kernel is derived from the
color refinement
algorithm (a.k.a. the
1-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm
), which is a simple and efficient heuristic to test whether two graphs are isomorphic (e.g.
[
13
]
).
The algorithm computes a coloring of the vertices of a graph based on the iterated degree sequences, we give the details in Section
3
.
To use it as an isomorphism test, we
compare the color patterns of two graphs. If they are
different, we say that color refinement
distinguishes
the
graphs. If the color patterns of the two graphs turn out to be the same, the graphs may still be non-isomorphic, but the algorithm fails to detect this.

Whether color refinement is able to distinguish two graphs
G
and
H
has a very nice linear-algebraic characterization due to Tinhofer
[
23
,
24
]
.
Let
V
and
W
be the vertex sets and let
A\in\{0,1\}^{V\times V}
and
B\in\{0,1\}^{W\times W}
be the adjacency matrices of
G
and
H
, respectively.
Now consider the system
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
of linear equations:

\displaystyle AX

\displaystyle=XB

(F1)

\displaystyle X\boldsymbol{1}_{W}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}_{V}

(F2)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{1}_{V}^{T}X

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{1}_{W}^{T}

(F3)

In these equations,
X
denotes a
(V\times W)
-matrix of variables and
\boldsymbol{1}_{U}
denotes the all-1 vector over the index set
U
.
Equations (
F2
) and (
F3
) simply state that all row and column sums
of
X
are supposed to be
1
.
Thus the nonnegative integer solutions to
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
are permutation
matrices, which due to (
F1
) describe isomorphisms between
G
and
H
.
The nonnegative real solutions to
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
, which in fact are always rational, are called
fractional isomorphisms
between
G
and
H
.
Tinhofer proved that two graphs are fractionally isomorphic if and only if color refinement does not distinguish them.

For every
k\geq 2
, color refinement has a generalization, known as
the
k
-dimensional Weisfeiler-Leman algorithm (
k
-WL)
, which colors
not the vertices of the given graph but
k
-tuples of vertices.
Atserias and Maneva
[
4
]
(also see
[
20
]
) generalized
Tinhofer’s theorem by establishing a close correspondence between
k
-WL and
the level-
k
Sherali-Adams relaxation of
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
.

Our results

How expressive are homomorphism vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
for restricted graph classes
\mathcal{F}
?
We consider the class
\mathcal{T}
of trees first, where the answer is surprisingly clean.

Theorem 1
.

For all graphs
G
and
H
, the following are equivalent:

i

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(H)
.

ii

Color refinement does not distinguish
G
and
H
.

iii

G
and
H
are fractionally isomorphic, that is, the system
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
of linear equations has a nonnegative real solution.

As mentioned before, the equivalence between
ii
and

iii
is due to Tinhofer
[
23
,
24
]
.
An unexpected consequence of our theorem is that we can decide in
time
O((n+m)\log n)
whether
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(H)
holds for two
given graphs
G
and
H
with
n
vertices and
m
edges. (If two graphs have a
different number of vertices or edges, then their homomorphism counts
already differ on the 1-vertex or 2-vertex trees.) This is remarkable,
because every known algorithm for computing the entry
\mathsf{Hom}(T,G)
of the vector

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)
requires quadratic time when
T
and
G
are given as input.

It is a consequence of the proof of Theorem
1
that, in order
to characterize an
n
-vertex graph
G
up to fractional isomorphisms, it
suffices to restrict the homomorphism vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)
to
trees of height at most
n-1
. What happens if we restrict the structure
of trees even further?
In particular, let us restrict the homomorphism vector
to its path entries, that is, consider
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(G)
for the class
\mathcal{P}
of all
paths. Figure
1
shows an example of two graphs
G
and
H
with

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(H)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(G)\neq\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}}(H)
.

Figure 1
:
Two fractionally non-isomorphic graphs with the same path
homomorphism counts.

Despite their weaker distinguishing capabilities, the vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(G)
are quite
interesting. They are related to graph kernels based on counting walks,
and they have a clean algebraic description: it is easy to see that

\mathsf{Hom}(P_{k},G)
, the number of homomorphisms from the path
P_{k}
of
length
k
to
G
, is equal to the number of length-
k
walks in
G
, which in turn is equal to
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}A^{k}\boldsymbol{1}
, where
A
is
the adjacency matrix of
G
and
\boldsymbol{1}
is the all-
1
vector of
appropriate length.

Theorem 2
.

For all graphs
G
and
H
, the following are equivalent:

i

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(H)
.

ii

The system
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
of linear equations has a real solution.

While the proof of Theorem
1
is mainly graph-theoretic—we
establish the equivalence between the assertions
i
and
ii
by expressing
the “colors” of color refinement in terms of specific tree
homomorphisms—the proof of Theorem
2
is purely
algebraic. We use spectral techniques, but with a twist, because
neither does the spectrum of a graph
G
determine the
vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(G)
nor does the vector determine the spectrum.
This is in contrast with
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(G)
for the class
\mathcal{C}
of all cycles, which, as we already mentioned, distinguishes two graphs if and only if they have the same spectrum.

Let us now turn to homomorphism vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(G)
for the
class
\mathcal{T}_{k}
of all graphs of tree width at most
k
. We will relate these
to
k
-WL, the
k
-dimensional generalization of color refinement. We
also obtain a
corresponding system of linear equations. Let
G
and
H
be graphs
with vertex sets
V
and
W
, respectively. Instead of variables
X_{vw}

for vertex pairs
{(v,w)\in V\times W}
, as in the system
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
, the new system
has variables
X_{\pi}
for
\pi\subseteq V\times W
of size
|\pi|\leq k
. We call
\pi=\{(v_{1},w_{1}),\ldots,(v_{\ell},w_{\ell})\}\subseteq V\times W
a

partial bijection
if
v_{i}=v_{j}\iff w_{i}=w_{j}
holds for all
i,j
, and
we call it a
partial isomorphism
if in addition

{v_{i}v_{j}\in E(G)\iff w_{i}w_{j}\in E(H)}
holds for all
i,j
.
Now consider the following system
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k}}(G,H)
of linear equations:

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in V}X_{\pi\cup\{(v,w)\}}

\displaystyle=X_{\pi}

for all
\pi\subseteq V\times W
of size
|\pi|\leq k-1
and all
w\in W

(L1)

\displaystyle\sum_{w\in W}X_{\pi\cup\{(v,w)\}}

\displaystyle=X_{\pi}

for all
\pi\subseteq V\times W
of size
|\pi|\leq k-1
and all
v\in V

(L2)

\displaystyle X_{\pi}

\displaystyle=0

for all
\pi\subseteq V\times W
of size
|\pi|\leq k
such that
\pi
is not a partial isomorphism from
G
to
H

(L3)

\displaystyle X_{\emptyset}

\displaystyle=1

(L4)

This system is closely related to the Sherali-Adams relaxations of

{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
: Every solution for the level-
k
Sherali-Adams
relaxation of
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
yields a solution to
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k}}(G,H)
, and
every solution to
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k}}(G,H)
yields a solution to the level
k-1

Sherali-Adams relaxation of
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)

[
4
,
14
]
.
Our result is this:

Theorem 3
.

For all
k\geq 1
and for all graphs
G
and
H
, the following are equivalent:

i

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}_{k}}(H)
.

ii

k
-WL does not distinguish
G
and
H
.

iii

{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k+1}}(G,H)
has a nonnegative real solution.

The equivalence between
ii
and
iii
is implicit in previous work
[
16
,
4
,
14
]
.
The system
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k}}(G,H)
has another nice interpretation related to
the proof complexity of graph isomorphism: it is shown in

[
7
]
that
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k}}(G,H)
has a real solution if and only if a natural system of polynomial
equations encoding the isomorphisms between
G
and
H
has a
degree-
k
solution in the Hilbert Nullstellensatz proof system
[
6
,
8
]
. In view of Theorem
2
, it is tempting to
conjecture that the solvability of
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k+1}}(G,H)
characterizes the
expressiveness of the homomorphism vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(G)
for the
class
\mathcal{P}_{k}
of all graphs of path width
k
. Unfortunately, we
only prove one direction of this conjecture.

Theorem 4
.

Let
k
be an integer with
k\geq 2
and let
G,H
be graphs.
If
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k+1}}(G,H)
has a real solution, then
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(H)
.

Combining this theorem with a recent result from
[
15
]

separating the nonnegative from arbitrary real solutions of our
systems of equations, we obtain the following corollary.

Corollary 5
.

For every
k
, there are graphs
G
and
H
with

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(H)
and

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(G)\neq\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}(H)
.

2
Preliminaries

Basics.

Graphs in this paper are simple, undirected, and finite
(even though our results transfer to directed graphs and even to
weighted graphs).
For a graph
G
, we write
V(G)
for its vertex set and
E(G)
for its edge set.
For
v\in V(G)
, the set of neighbors of
v
are denoted with
N_{G}(v)
.
For
S\subseteq V(G)
, we denote with
G[S]
the subgraph of
G
induced by the vertices of
S
.
A
rooted graph
is a graph
G
together with a designated root vertex
r(G)\in V(G)
.
We write multisets using the notation
\{\!\!\{1,1,6,2\}\!\!\}
.

Matrices.

An
LU
-decomposition of a matrix
A
consists of a lower triangular matrix
L
and an upper triangular matrix
U
such that
A=LU
holds.
Every finite matrix
A
over
\mathbf{R}
has an
LU
-decomposition.
We also use
infinite matrices
over
\mathbf{R}
, which are functions
A:I\times J\to\mathbf{R}
where
I
and
J
are locally finite posets and countable.
The matrix product
AB
is defined in the natural way via
(AB)_{ij}=\sum_{k}A_{ik}B_{kj}
if all of these inner products are finite
sums, and otherwise we leave it undefined.
An
n\times n
real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and a corresponding set of orthogonal eigenspaces.
The spectral decomposition of a real symmetric matrix
M
is
of the form
M=\lambda_{1}P_{1}+\dots+\lambda_{l}P_{l}
where

\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{l}
are the eigenvalues of
M
with
corresponding eigenspaces
W_{1},\dots,W_{l}
. Moreover,
each
P_{j}
is the projection matrix corresponding to the projection
onto the eigenspace
W_{j}
. Usually,
P_{j}
is expressed
as
P_{j}=UU^{T}
for a matrix
U
whose columns form an orthonormal basis of
W_{j}
.

Homomorphism numbers.

Recall that a mapping
h:V(F)\to V(G)
is a homomorphism if
h(e)\in E(G)
holds for all
e\in E(F)
and that
\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)
is the number of homomorphisms from
F
to
G
.
Let
\mathsf{Surj}(F,G)
be the number of homomorphisms from
F
to
G
that are surjective on both the vertices and edges of
G
.
Let
\mathsf{Inj}(F,G)
be the number of injective homomorphisms from
F
to
G
.
Let
\mathsf{Sub}(F,G)=\mathsf{Inj}(F,G)/\mathsf{Aut}(F)
, where
\mathsf{Aut}(F)
is the number of
automorphisms of
F
. Observe that
\mathsf{Sub}(F,G)
is the number of
subgraphs of
G
that are isomorphic to
F
.
Where convenient, we view the objects
\mathsf{Hom}
,
\mathsf{Surj}
, and
\mathsf{Inj}
as
infinite matrices; the matrix indices are all unlabeled graphs, sorted
by their size. However, we only use one representative of each
isomorphism class, called the
isomorphism type
of the graphs in
the class, as an index in the matrix.
Then
\mathsf{Surj}
is lower triangular and
\mathsf{Inj}
is upper triangular,
so
\mathsf{Hom}=\mathsf{Surj}\cdot\mathsf{Sub}
is an LU-decomposition of
\mathsf{Hom}
.
Finally,
\mathsf{Ind}(F,G)
is the number of times
F
occurs as an induced
subgraph in
G
.
Similarly to the homomorphism vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
we define vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{INJ}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{IND}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
.
Finally, let
G,H
be rooted graphs. A homomorphism from
G
to
H
is a graph homomorphism that maps the root of
G
to the root of
H
.
Moreover, two rooted graphs are isomorphic if there is an isomorphism mapping the root to the root.

3
Homomorphisms from trees

3.1
Color refinement and tree unfolding

Color refinement iteratively colors the
vertices of a graph in a sequence of
refinement rounds
. Initially, all
vertices get the same color. In each refinement round, any two
vertices
v
and
w
that still have the same color get different colors if
there is some color
c
such that
v
and
w
have a different number
of neighbors of color
c
; otherwise they keep the same color.
We stop the refinement process if the vertex partition that is induced by the colors does not change anymore, that is, all pairs of
vertices that have the same color before the refinement round still
have the same color after the round.
More formally, we define the sequence
C^{G}_{0},C^{G}_{1},C^{G}_{2},\ldots
of colorings as follows.
We let
C^{G}_{0}(v)=1
for all
v\in V(G)
, and for
i\geq 0
we let

C^{G}_{i+1}(v)=\{\!\!\{\,C^{G}_{i}(u):u\in N_{G}(v)\,\}\!\!\}
.
We say that color refinement
distinguishes
two graphs
G
and
H
if there is an
i\geq 0
with

\{\!\!\{\,C_{i}^{G}(v):v\in V(G)\,\}\!\!\}\neq\{\!\!\{\,C_{i}^{H}(v):v\in V(H)\,\}\!\!\}\,.

(1)

We argue now that the color refinement algorithm implicitly constructs a tree at
v
obtained by simultaneously taking all possible walks starting at
v
(and not remembering nodes visited in the past).
For a rooted tree
T
with root
r
, a graph
G
, and a vertex
v\in V(G)
, we say
that
T
is
a tree at
v
if there is a homomorphism
f
from

T
to
G
such that

f(r)=v
and,
for all non-leaves
t\in V(T)
, the function
f
induces a
bijection between the set of children of
t
in in
T
and the set
of neighbors of
f(t)
in
G
.
In other words,
f
is a homomorphism from
T
to
G
that is
locally bijective
.
If
T
is an infinite tree at
v
and does not have any leaves,
then
T
is uniquely determined up to isomorphisms, and we call this

the infinite tree at
v
(or the
tree unfolding
of
G
at
v
), denoted with
T(G,v)
.
For an infinite rooted tree
T
, let
T_{\leq d}
be the finite rooted subtree of
T
where all leaves are at depth exactly
d
.
For all finite trees
T
of depth
d
, define
\mathsf{Cr}(T,G)\in\{0,\dots,\lvert V(G)\rvert\}
to be the number of vertices
v\in V(G)
for which
T
is isomorphic to
T(G,v)_{\leq d}
.
Note that this number is zero if not all leaves of
T
are at the same depth
d
or if some node of
T
has more than
n-1
children.
The
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}
-vector of
G
is the
vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)=(\mathsf{Cr}(T,G))_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{r}}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{r}
denotes the
family of all rooted trees.
The following connection between the color refinement algorithm and the
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}
-vector is known.

Lemma 6

(Angluin
[
2
]
, also see Krebs and Verbitsky
[
17
, Lemma 2.5]
)
.

For all graphs
G
and
H
, color refinement distinguishes
G
and
H

if and only if
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)\neq\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(H)
holds.

3.2
Proof of Theorem
1

Throughout this section, we work with rooted trees. For a
rooted tree
T
and an (unrooted) graph
G
, we simply let
\mathsf{Hom}(T,G)

be the number of homomorphisms of the plain tree underlying
T
to

G
, ignoring the root.

Let
T
and
T^{\prime}
be rooted trees. A homomorphism
h
from
T
to
T^{\prime}

is
depth-preserving
if, for all vertices
v\in V(T)
, the depth
of
v
in
T
is equal to the depth of
h(v)
in
T^{\prime}
. Moreover, a
homomorphism
h
from
T
to
T^{\prime}
is
depth-surjective
if the
image of
T
under
h
contains vertices at every depth present
in
T^{\prime}
. We define
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}(T,T^{\prime})
as the number of homomorphisms from
T
to
T^{\prime}
that are both depth-preserving and depth-surjective.
Note that
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}(T,T^{\prime})=0
holds if and only if
T
and
T^{\prime}
have different
depths.

Lemma 7
.

Let
T
be a rooted tree and let
G
be a graph.
We have

\mathsf{Hom}(T,G)=\sum_{T^{\prime}}\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}(T,T^{\prime})\cdot\mathsf{Cr}(T^{\prime},G)\,,

(2)

where the sum is over all unlabeled rooted trees
T^{\prime}
.
In other words, the matrix identity

\mathsf{Hom}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}\cdot\mathsf{Cr}
holds.

Proof.

Let
d
be the depth of
T
and let
r
be the root of
T
.
Every
T^{\prime}
with
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}(T,T^{\prime})\neq 0
has depth
d
too and there are at most
n
non-isomorphic rooted trees
T^{\prime}
of depth
d
with
\mathsf{Cr}(T^{\prime},G)\neq 0
.
Thus the sum in (
2
) has only finitely many non-zero terms and is well-defined.

For a rooted tree
T^{\prime}
and a vertex
v\in V(G)
, let
H(T^{\prime},v)
be the
set of all homomorphisms
h
from
T
to
G
such that
h(r)=v
holds and
the tree unfolding
T(G,v)_{\leq d}
is isomorphic to
T^{\prime}
.
Let
H(T^{\prime})=\bigcup_{v\in V(G)}H(T^{\prime},v)
and observe

|H(T^{\prime},v)|=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}(T,T^{\prime})
.
Since
\mathsf{Cr}(T^{\prime},G)
is the number of
v\in V(G)
with
T(G,v)_{\leq d}\cong T^{\prime}
, we thus have

|H(T^{\prime})|=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}(T,T^{\prime})\cdot\mathsf{Cr}(T^{\prime},G)
.
Since each homomorphism from
T
to
G
is contained in exactly one set
H(T^{\prime})
,
we obtain the desired equality (
2
).
∎

For rooted trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
, let
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}(T,T^{\prime})
be the number of
depth-preserving and surjective homomorphisms from
T
to
T^{\prime}
. In
particular, not only do these homomorphisms have to be
depth-surjective, but they should hit every vertex of
T^{\prime}
. For
rooted trees
T
and
T^{\prime}
of the same depth, let
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}(T,T^{\prime})
be
the number of subgraphs of
T^{\prime}
that are isomorphic to
T
(under an
isomorphism that maps the root to the root); if
T
and
T^{\prime}
have
different depths, we set
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}(T,T^{\prime})=0
.

Lemma 8
.

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}
is an
LU
-decomposition of
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}
, and
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}
and
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}
are invertible.

As is the case for finite matrices, the inverse of a lower
(upper) triangular matrix is lower (upper) triangular. As the matrix

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}
is lower triangular and the matrix
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}
is upper
triangular, their inverses are as well.
We are ready to prove our first main theorem.

Proof of Theorem
1
.

We only need to prove the equivalence between assertions
i
and
ii
.
For every graph
G
, let

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G):=\big(\mathsf{Hom}(T,G)\big)_{T\in\mathcal{T}_{r}}
.
By our convention that for a rooted tree
T
and an unrooted graph
G

we let
\mathsf{Hom}(T,G)
be the number of homomorphisms of the plain tree underlying
T
to

G
, for all
G
and
H
we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(H)\iff\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}(H)
.
By Lemma
6
, it suffices to prove for all graph

G,H
that

\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(H)\iff\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(H)\,.

(3)

We view the vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)
and
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)
as infinite column vectors.
By Lemma
7
, we have

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)\text{ and }\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(H)=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(H)\,.

(4)

The forward direction of (
3
) now follows immediately.

It remains to prove the backward direction.
Since
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}\cdot\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}
holds by Lemma
8
for two invertible matrices
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}
and
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}
, we can first left-multiply with
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}^{-1}
to obtain the equivalent identities

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)\text{ and }\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}^{-1}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(H)=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(H)\,.

(5)

Now suppose
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(H)
holds, and set
\boldsymbol{v}=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{r}(G)
.
Then
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{v}
is well-defined, because

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Surj}}
and its inverse are lower triangular. Thus we obtain

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}\cdot\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(H)
and set
\boldsymbol{w}=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)
. Unfortunately,

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}
may be undefined, since
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{-1}
is upper triangular.
While we can
still use a matrix inverse, the argument becomes a bit subtle. The
crucial observation is that
\mathsf{Cr}(T^{\prime},G)
is non-zero for at most
n

different trees
T^{\prime}
, and all such trees have maximum degree at most

n-1
.
Thus we do not need to look at
all
trees but only those with maximum degree
n
.
Let
\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
be the set of all unlabeled rooted trees of maximum degree at most
n
.
Let
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}}
,
let
\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}=\boldsymbol{w}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}}
, and let

\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime}=\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}|_{\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}\times\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}}
.
Then we still have the following for all
T\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
and
G
:

\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{T}=\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}}\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime}(T,T^{\prime})\cdot\mathsf{Cr}^{\prime}(T^{\prime},G)\,.

(6)

The new matrix
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime}
is a principal minor of
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}
and thus remains invertible.
Moreover,
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}
is well-defined, since

\sum_{T^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}}\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime-1}(T,T^{\prime})\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}_{T^{\prime}}

(7)

is a finite sum for each
T
: The number of (unlabeled) trees
T^{\prime}\in\widetilde{\mathcal{T}}
that have the same depth
d
as
T
is bounded by a function in
n
and
d
.
Thus
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}^{\prime}(G)
.
By a similar argument, we obtain
\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Sub}}^{\prime-1}\cdot\boldsymbol{w}^{\prime}=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}^{\prime}(H)
.
This implies
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}^{\prime}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}^{\prime}(H)
and thus
\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{CR}}(H)
.
∎

4
Homomorphisms from cycles and paths

While the arguments we saw in the proof of Theorem
1
are
mainly graph-theoretic, the proof of Theorem
2
uses
spectral techniques.
To introduce the techniques, we first prove a
simple, known result already mentioned in the introduction. We call two square
matrices
co-spectral
if they have the same
eigenvalues with the same multiplicities, and we call two graphs

co-spectral
if their adjacency matrices are co-spectral.

Proposition 9

(e.g.
[
25
, Lemma 1]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the class of all cycles (including the degenerate cycle of length

0
, which is just a single vertex).
For all graphs
G
and
H
, we have

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(H)

if and only if
G
and
H
are co-spectral.

For the proof, we review a few simple facts from linear algebra. The
trace
\operatorname{tr}(A)
of a square matrix
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
is the sum of the
diagonal entries. If the eigenvalues of

A
are
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}
, then

\operatorname{tr}(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}
. Moreover, for each
\ell\geq 0
the eigenvalues
of the matrix
A^{\ell}
are
\lambda_{1}^{\ell},\ldots,\lambda_{n}^{\ell}
, and thus

\operatorname{tr}(A^{\ell})=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}^{\ell}
.
The following technical lemma encapsulates the fact that the information
\operatorname{tr}(A^{\ell})
for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
suffices to reconstruct the spectrum of
A
with multiplicities.
We use the same lemma to prove Theorem
2
, but for Proposition
9
a less general version would suffice.

Lemma 10
.

Let
X,Y\subseteq\mathbf{R}
be two finite sets and let
c\in\mathbf{R}^{X}_{\neq 0}
and
d\in\mathbf{R}^{Y}_{\neq 0}
be two vectors.
If the equation

\sum_{x\in X}c_{x}x^{\ell}=\sum_{y\in Y}d_{y}y^{\ell}

(8)

holds for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
,
then
X=Y
and
c=d
.

Proof.

We prove the claim by induction on
k:=\lvert X\rvert+\lvert Y\rvert
.
For
k=0
, the claim is trivially true since both sums in (
8
) are equal to zero by convention.

Let
\hat{x}=\arg\max\{\,\lvert x\rvert:x\in X\cup Y\,\}
and let
\hat{x}\in X
without loss of generality.
If
\hat{x}=0
, then
X=\{0\}
and we claim that
Y=\{0\}
holds.
Clearly (
8
) for
\ell=0
yields

0\neq c_{0}=\sum_{y\in Y}d_{y}
.
In particular,
Y\neq\emptyset
holds. Since
\hat{x}=0
is the maximum of
X\cup Y
in absolute value, we have
Y=\{0\}
and thus also
c=d
.

Now suppose that
\hat{x}\neq 0
holds.
We consider the sequences
(a_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbf{N}}

and
(b_{\ell})_{\ell\in\mathbf{N}}
with

\displaystyle a_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\hat{x}^{\ell}}\cdot\sum_{x\in X}c_{x}x^{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad b_{\ell}=\frac{1}{\hat{x}^{\ell}}\cdot\sum_{y\in Y}d_{y}y^{\ell}\,.

(9)

Note that
a_{\ell}=b_{\ell}
holds for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
by assumption.
Observe the following simple facts:

1)

If
-\hat{x}\not\in X
, then

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}a_{\ell}=c_{\hat{x}}
.

2)

If
-\hat{x}\in X
, then

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}a_{2\ell}=c_{\hat{x}}+c_{-\hat{x}}
and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}a_{2\ell+1}=c_{\hat{x}}-c_{-\hat{x}}
.

As well as the following exhaustive case distinction for
Y
:

a)

If
\hat{x},-\hat{x}\not\in Y
, then

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}b_{\ell}=0
.

b)

If
\hat{x}\in Y
and
-\hat{x}\not\in Y
, then

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}b_{\ell}=d_{\hat{x}}
.

c)

If
\hat{x}\not\in Y
and
-\hat{x}\in Y
, then

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}b_{2\ell}=d_{-\hat{x}}
and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}b_{2\ell+1}=-d_{-\hat{x}}
.

d)

If
\hat{x},-\hat{x}\in Y
, then

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}b_{2\ell}=d_{\hat{x}}+d_{-\hat{x}}
and

\lim_{\ell\to\infty}b_{2\ell+1}=d_{\hat{x}}-d_{-\hat{x}}
.

If
-\hat{x}\not\in X
holds, we see from 1) that
a_{\ell}
converges to the non-zero value
c_{\hat{x}}
.
Since the two sequences are equal, the sequence
b_{\ell}
also converges to a non-zero value.
The only case for
Y
where this happens is b), and we get
\hat{x}\in Y
,
-\hat{x}\not\in Y
, and
c_{\hat{x}}=d_{\hat{x}}
.
On the other hand, if
-\hat{x}\in X
, we see from 2) that
a_{\ell}
does not converge, but the even and odd subsequences do.
The only cases for
Y
where this happens for
b_{\ell}
too are c) and d).
We cannot be in case c), since the two accumulation points of
b_{\ell}
just differ in their sign, while the two accumulation points of
a_{\ell}
do not have the same absolute value.
Thus we must be in case d) and obtain
x,\hat{x}\in Y
as well as

c_{\hat{x}}+c_{-\hat{x}}=d_{\hat{x}}+d_{-\hat{x}}\quad\text{and}\quad c_{\hat{x}}-c_{-\hat{x}}=d_{\hat{x}}-d_{-\hat{x}}\,.

This linear system has full rank and implies
c_{\hat{x}}=d_{\hat{x}}
and
c_{-\hat{x}}=d_{-\hat{x}}
.

Either way, we can remove
\{\hat{x}\}
or
\{\hat{x},-\hat{x}\}
from both
X
and
Y
and apply the induction hypothesis on the resulting instance
X^{\prime},Y^{\prime},c^{\prime},d^{\prime}
.
Then
(X,c)=(Y,d)
follows as claimed.
∎

Proof of Proposition
9
.

For all
\ell\geq 0
, the number of homomorphisms from the cycle
C_{\ell}
of length
\ell
to a graph
G
with adjacency matrix
A
is equal to the number of closed length-
\ell
walks in
G
, which in turn is equal to the trace of
A^{\ell}
.
Thus for graphs
G,H
with adjacency matrices
A,B
, we have
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(H)
if and only if
\operatorname{tr}(A^{\ell})=\operatorname{tr}(B^{\ell})
holds for all
\ell\geq 0
.

If
A
and
B
have the same spectrum
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
, then

\operatorname{tr}(A^{\ell})=\lambda_{1}^{\ell}+\dots+\lambda_{n}^{\ell}=\operatorname{tr}(B^{\ell})
holds for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
.
For the reverse direction, suppose
\operatorname{tr}(A^{\ell})=\operatorname{tr}(B^{\ell})
for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
.
Let
X\subseteq\mathbf{R}
be the set of eigenvalues of
A
and for each
\lambda\in X
, let
c_{\lambda}\in\{1,\dots,n\}
be the multiplicity of the eigenvalue
\lambda
.
Let
Y\subseteq\mathbf{R}
and
d_{\lambda}
for
\lambda\in Y
be the corresponding eigenvalues and multiplicities for
B
.
Then for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
, we have

\sum_{\lambda\in X}c_{\lambda}\lambda^{\ell}=\operatorname{tr}(A^{\ell})=\operatorname{tr}(B^{\ell})=\sum_{\lambda\in Y}d_{\lambda}\lambda^{\ell}\,.

By Lemma
10
, this implies
(X,c)=(Y,d)
, that is, the spectra of
A
and
B
are identical.
∎

for

of cycles and

.

Let us now turn to the proof of Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
2
.

Let
A
and
B
be the adjacency matrices of
G
and
H
, respectively.
Since
A
is a symmetric and real matrix, its eigenvalues are real and the corresponding eigenspaces are orthogonal and span
\mathbf{R}^{n}
.
Let
\boldsymbol{1}
be the
n
-dimensional all-
1
vector, and let
X=\{\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}\}
be the set of all eigenvalues of
A
whose corresponding eigenspaces are not orthogonal to
\boldsymbol{1}
.
We call these eigenvalues the
useful
eigenvalues of
A
and without loss of generality assume
\lambda_{1}>\dots>\lambda_{k}
.
The
n
-dimensional all-
1
vector
\boldsymbol{1}
can be expressed as a direct sum of eigenvectors of
A
corresponding to useful eigenvalues.
In particular, there is a unique decomposition

\boldsymbol{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}
such that
each
u_{i}
is a non-zero eigenvector in the eigenspace of
\lambda_{i}
.
Moreover, the vectors
u_{1},\dots,u_{k}
are orthogonal.
For the matrix
B
, we analogously define its set of useful eigenvalues
Y=\{\mu_{1},\dots,\mu_{k^{\prime}}\}
and the direct sum
\boldsymbol{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}v_{i}
.

We prove the equivalence of the following three assertions (of which the first and third appear in the statement of Theorem
2
).

1.

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}}(H)
.

2.

A
and
B
have the same set of useful eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}
and
\|u_{i}\|=\|v_{i}\|
holds for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
.
Here,
\|.\|
denotes the Euclidean norm with
\|x\|^{2}=\sum_{j}x_{j}^{2}
.

3.

The system
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}(G,H)
of linear equations has a
real solution.

Note that in 2, we do not require that the useful eigenvalues occur with the same multiplicities in
A
and
B
.
We show the implications (1
\Rightarrow
2), (2
\Rightarrow
3), and (3
\Rightarrow
1).

(1
\Rightarrow
2): Suppose that
\mathsf{Hom}(P_{\ell},G)=\mathsf{Hom}(P_{\ell},H)
holds for all paths
P_{\ell}
. Equivalently, this can be stated
in terms of the adjacency matrices
A
and
B
: for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
, we have
\boldsymbol{1}^{T}A^{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}^{T}B^{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}
.
We claim that
A
and
B
have the same useful eigenvalues, and that the projections of
\boldsymbol{1}
onto the corresponding eigenspaces have the same lengths.

Note that
A^{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\ell}_{i}u_{i}
holds.
Thus we have

\boldsymbol{1}^{T}A^{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}=\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{k}u_{i}^{T}\right\rparen\left\lparen\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda^{\ell}_{i}u_{i}\right\rparen=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|u_{i}\|^{2}\cdot\lambda^{\ell}_{i}\,.

(10)

The term

\boldsymbol{1}^{T}B^{\ell}\boldsymbol{1}
can be expanded analogously, which together yields

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\|u_{i}\|^{2}\cdot\lambda^{\ell}_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{k^{\prime}}\|v_{i}\|^{2}\cdot\mu^{\ell}_{i}\quad\text{for all $\ell\in\mathbf{N}$.}

(11)

Since all coefficients
c_{\lambda_{i}}=\|u_{i}\|^{2}
and
d_{\mu_{i}}=\|v_{i}\|^{2}
are non-zero, we are in the situation of Lemma
10
.
We obtain

and, for all
i\in\{1,\dots,k\}
, we obtain
\lambda_{i}=\mu_{i}
and
\|u_{i}\|=\|v_{i}\|
.
This is exactly the claim that we want to show.

(2
\Rightarrow
3): We claim that the
(n\times n
)-matrix
X
defined via

X=\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot u_{i}{v^{T}_{i}}

(12)

satisfies the
{{\mathsf{F}}_{\textup{iso}}}

AX=XB
and
X\boldsymbol{1}=\boldsymbol{1}=X^{T}\boldsymbol{1}
.
Indeed, we have

\displaystyle AX=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot Au_{i}{v^{T}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot u_{i}{v^{T}_{i}}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot u_{i}{v^{T}_{i}}B^{T}=XB^{T}=XB\,,

(13)

This follows, since
Au_{i}=\lambda_{i}u_{i}
,
Bv_{i}=\lambda_{i}v_{i}
, and
B
is symmetric.
Moreover, we have

\displaystyle X\boldsymbol{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot Au_{i}{v^{T}_{i}}\boldsymbol{1}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot u_{i}{v^{T}_{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{k}v_{j}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\frac{1}{\|u_{i}\|^{2}}\cdot u_{i}\cdot{v^{T}_{i}}v_{i}=\boldsymbol{1}\,.

(14)

This holds by definition of
u_{i}
and
v_{i}
and from
v_{i}^{T}v_{i}=\|v_{i}\|^{2}=\|u_{i}\|^{2}
.
The claim

follows analogously.

(3
\Rightarrow
1):
Suppose there is a matrix
X
with
X^{T}{\boldsymbol{1}}=X{\boldsymbol{1}}={\boldsymbol{1}}
and

.
We obtain
A^{\ell}X=XB^{\ell}
by induction for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}_{>0}
.
For
\ell=0
, this also holds since
A^{0}=I_{n}
by convention.
As a result, we have

{\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}A^{\ell}{\boldsymbol{1}}={\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}A^{\ell}X{\boldsymbol{1}}={\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}XB^{\ell}{\boldsymbol{1}}={\boldsymbol{1}}^{T}B^{\ell}{\boldsymbol{1}}
for all
\ell\in\mathbf{N}
.
Since these scalars count the length-
\ell
walks in
G
and
H
,
respectively, we obtain
\mathsf{Hom}(P_{\ell},G)=\mathsf{Hom}(P_{\ell},H)
for
all paths
P_{\ell}
as claimed.
∎

5
Homomorphisms from bounded tree width and path width

We briefly outline the main ideas of the proofs of Theorems
3
and
4
; the technical details are deferred to the appendix.
In Theorem
3
, the equivalence between
ii
and
iii
is essentially
known, so we focus on the equivalence between
i
and
ii
.
The proof is similar to the proof of Theorem
1
in Section
3
.

Let us fix
k\geq 2
.
The idea of the
k
-WL algorithm is to iteratively color
k
-tuples of
vertices. Initially, each
k
-tuple
(v_{1},\ldots,v_{k})
is colored by
its
atomic type
, that is, the isomorphism type of the labeled
graph
G[\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}\}]
. Then in the refinement step, to define
the new color of a
k
-tuple
\bar{v}
we look at the current color of
all
k
-tuples that can be reached from

by adding one vertex and
then removing one vertex.

Similar to the tree unfolding of a graph
G
at a vertex

6
Conclusions

We have studied the homomorphism vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
for
various graph classes
\mathcal{F}
, focusing on classes
\mathcal{F}
where it is
tractable to compute the entries
\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)
of the vector. Our main
interest was in the “expressiveness” of these vectors, that is, in
the question what
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
tells us about the graph

G
. For the classes
\mathcal{C}
of cycles,
\mathcal{T}
of trees,
\mathcal{T}_{k}
of graphs of tree width
at most
k
, and
\mathcal{P}
of paths, we have obtained surprisingly clean
answers to this question, relating the homomorphism vectors to various
other well studied formalisms that on the surface have nothing to do
with homomorphism counts.

Some interesting questions remain open. The most obvious is whether
the converse of Theorem
4
holds, that is, whether for two
graphs
G
,
H
with
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{P}_{k}}(H)
,
the system
{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k+1}}(G,H)
has a real solution (and hence the
Nullstellensatz propositional proof system has no degree-
(k+1)
refutation of

G
and
H
being isomorphic).

Another related open problem in spectral graph theory is to characterize graphs
which are identified by their spectrum,
up to isomorphism. In our framework, Proposition
9

ensures that we can equivalently
ask for the following characterization: for which graphs
G
does
the vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{C}}(G)
determine the entire
homomorphism vector
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}(G)
?

Despite the computational intractability, it is also interesting to
study the vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
for classes
\mathcal{F}
of unbounded
tree width. Are there natural classes
\mathcal{F}
(except of course the class
of all graphs) for which the vectors
\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)
characterize

G
up to isomorphism? For example, what about classes of bounded
degree or the class of planar graphs? And what is the complexity of deciding whether

\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G)=\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(H)
holds when
G
and

H
are given as input? Our results imply that this problem is in polynomial time for the
classes
\mathcal{T}
,
\mathcal{T}_{k}
, and
\mathcal{P}
.
For the class of all graphs, it is in quasi-polynomial time by Babai’s quasi-polynomial isomorphism test
[
5
]
.
Yet it seems plausible that there are classes
\mathcal{F}
(even natural classes decidable in polynomial time) for which the problem is co-NP-hard.

Maybe the most interesting direction for further research is to study
the graph similarity measures induced by homomorphism
vectors. A
simple way of defining an inner product on the homomorphism vectors is by letting

\Big\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G),\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(H)\Big\rangle:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\geq 1\\
\mathcal{F}_{k}\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{k^{k}|\mathcal{F}_{k}|}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}}\mathsf{Hom}(F,G)\mathsf{Hom}(F,H),

where
\mathcal{F}_{k}
denotes the class of all graph
F\in\mathcal{F}
with
k
vertices. The mapping
(G,H)\mapsto\langle\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(G),\operatorname{\mathsf{HOM}}_{\mathcal{F}}(H)\rangle
is what is known as a
graph
kernel
in machine learning. It induces a (pseudo)metric
d_{\mathcal{T}}
on the class
of graphs. It is an interesting question how it relates to other graph
similarity measures, for example, the metric induced by the
Weisfeiler-Leman graph kernel. Our Theorem
1
implies that
the metric
d_{\mathcal{T}}
for the class
\mathcal{T}
of trees and the metric
induced by the Weisfeiler-Leman graph kernel have the same graphs of distance zero.

are in one-to-one correspondence, the claimed identity follows.
The matrix
\mathsf{bIso}\overrightarrow{\mathsf{Hom}}
is invertible, for which reason the claimed equivalence “

” of the Theorem follows.

“
3
is equivalent to
4
”:
Let
F

We have the following identity:

(32)

Here,

Since the matrices

Let

,

|Y_{1}|=\dots=|Y_{\ell-1}|=k+1

Also,

We define a conditional variant of

,
respectively, such that the system

The following lemma shows how to “transfer” the conditional

Lemma 26

Let
F
be a finite undirected graph with a path decomposition

k
,
where

\displaystyle\mathsf{bIso}\left((F,P),G\,|\,\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}\dots u_{k}\\

\in W^{k}\end{subarray}}X_{\{(v_{1},w_{1}),\dots,(v_{k},w_{k})\}}\,\mathsf{bIso}\left((F,P),H\,|\,\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}\dots u_{k}\\
w_{1}\dots w_{k}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the length parameter
\ell

The base case
\ell=1
corresponds to the situation when

consists of a single bag

.
Denote
\tau=\operatorname{atp}(F,(u_{1},\dots,u_{k}))

{v}_{1}\dots{v}_{k}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=1

Likewise,
\mathsf{bIso}\left((F,P),H\,|\,\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}\dots u_{k}\\

Therefore, given a
k

for every

of type
\tau
. Otherwise, the
second possibility is that
\operatorname{atp}(G,(v_{1},\dots,v_{k}))=\tau
. Then, the LHS
\mathsf{bIso}\left((F,P),G\,|\,\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}\dots u_{k}\\
{v}_{1}\dots{v}_{k}\end{smallmatrix}\right)=1

The RHS sum can be taken over all
\bar{w}
such that
\operatorname{atp}(H,\bar{w})=\tau
. The RHS simplifies to
the sum of all

over all
\bar{w}
of atomic type
\tau
.
This sum is equal to

, a fact which is immediate from repeated application of equations

L4

{{\mathsf{L}}_{\textup{iso}}^{k+1}}(G,H)

This finishes the base case.

We proceed to the inductive case for

In the path decomposition

We can rewrite

\displaystyle\mathsf{bIso}\left((F,P),G\,|\,\begin{smallmatrix}u_{1}\dots u_{k}\\
{v}_{1}\dots{v}_{k}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

{v}_{0}\dots{v}_{k}\end{smallmatrix}\right)

{v}_{0}\dots{v}_{k-1}\end{smallmatrix}\right)\cdot I_{G,[k-1]}\cdot I_{G,\{0\}}
