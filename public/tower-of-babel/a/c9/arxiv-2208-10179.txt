On parametric resonance in the laser action 1 1 1 Preprint arXiv:2208.10179 [quant-ph]


Abstract
We consider the selfconsistent semiclassical Maxwell–Schrödinger system for the solid state laser. The system consists of the Maxwell equations coupled to N ∼ 10 20 N\sim 10^{20} Schrödinger equations for active molecules. The system includes time-periodic pumping and a weak dissipation. We introduce the corresponding Poincaré map 𝒫 {\cal P} and consider the differential D ​ 𝒫 ​ ( Y 0 ) D{\cal P}(Y^{0}) at suitable ground state Y 0 Y^{0} . We conjecture that the stable laser action is due to the parametric resonance (PR) which means that the maximal absolute value of the corresponding multipliers is sufficiently large. The multipliers are defined as eigenvalues of D ​ 𝒫 ​ ( Y 0 ) D{\cal P}(Y^{0}) . The PR makes the stationary state Y 0 Y^{0} highly unstable, and we suppose that this instability maintains the coherent laser radiation . We prove that the spectrum Spec ​ D ​ 𝒫 ​ ( Y 0 ) {\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}D{\cal P}(Y^{0}) is approximately symmetric with respect to the unit circle | μ | = 1 |\mu|=1 if the dissipation is sufficiently small. More detailed results are obtained for the Maxwell–Bloch system which is a finite-dimensional approximation of the Maxwell–Schrödinger system. The approximation consists of one-mode Maxwell field coupled to N N two-level molecules. We calculate the corresponding Poincaré map P P by successive approximations. The key role in calculation of the multipliers is played by the sum of N N positive terms arising in the second-order approximation for the total current. This fact can be interpreted as the synchronization of the main parts of molecular currents in all active molecules, which is provisionally in line with the superradiance and with the role of stimulated emission in the laser action. The calculation of the sum relies on probabilistic arguments which is one of main novelties of our approach. Other main novelties are i) the calculation of the differential D ​ P DP in the “Hopf representation” which corresponds to the factorization of the dynamics by the action of the symmetry gauge group, ii) the justification of this representation, iii) the block structure of the differential, and iv) the justification of the “rotating wave approximation” by a new estimate for the averaging of slow rotations. The main peculiarity of the eigenvalue problem is that D ​ P DP is a matrix of size ∼ N × N \sim N\times N which depends on random distribution of active molecules in the resonance cavity. The block structure of D ​ P DP allows us to reduce the eigenvalue problem to polynomial equation of degree four. This reduction relies on a novel probabilistic arguments.

Introduction

The article addresses the problems of laser and maser radiation.
In the existing approaches, time-periodic pumping is not included in the dynamical equations, so the corresponding resonance features are absent. This fact was one of our main motivations.

We consider the
selfconsistent
semiclassical Maxwell–Schrödinger system
for the solid state laser. The system consists of the Maxwell equations
coupled to
N\sim 10^{20}
Schrödinger equations for active molecules
with one active electron.
The system includes time-periodic pumping and a weak dissipation.
We introduce the corresponding Poincaré map
{\cal P}

and consider its differential
D{\cal P}(Y^{0})
at suitable ground state
Y^{0}
.
Let

\mu
denote
the multipliers which are eigenvalues of the
differential
D{\cal P}(Y^{0})
.
We conjecture that the laser action is due to the parametric resonance, when

\max_{\mu\in{\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}D{\cal P}(Y^{0})}|\mu|>1.

(1.1)

For solutions to the linearized dynamical equation at
Y^{0}
with the spectrum satisfying (
1.1
),
the components with
|\mu|\leq 1
are bounded, while
the ones with
|\mu|>1
grow exponentially for large times.
As a result,
almost all
solutions grow exponentially
which is in line with the conventional interpretation of the laser action as the appearance of exponentially growing solutions of the corresponding linearized dynamics, see (
[
28
, (6.5.12) and (6.6.23)]
.
This is why we expect that under the condition (
1.1
),
similar growth takes place in the case of sufficiently small nonlinear coupling (the growth only occurs
for limited time because of nonlinear effects and dissipation).

The conjecture is confirmed by the fact that laser action is possible only
for the pumping above a threshold that is quite similar to
the parametric resonance
[
2
]
.

Remark 1.1
.

We suppose that the overwhelming predominance of a single mode, corresponding to the maximal value of
|\mu|
, can be responsible for the
coherent monochromatic laser radiation
.

The main goal of the present article is an analysis of properties
of the multipliers and

Our main results are as follows.

2
Laser equations

We use the Heaviside–Lorentz units (
unrationalized Gaussian units
), in which the main physical constants
(electron charge and mass, Planck’s constant, and the speed of light in vacuum)
read
[
43
, p. 221]

e=-4.8\times 10^{-10}{\rm esu},\quad{\rm m}=9.1\times 10^{-28}{\rm g},\quad\hbar=1.055\times 10^{-27}\mbox{\rm erg$\cdot$ s},\qquad c=3.0\times 10^{10}{\rm cm/s}.

(2.1)

Laser cavity and active molecules.
The laser resonator is a (usually cylindrical)
cavity
V\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
with metallic walls (ideal conductor) connected with the output waveguide.
Active molecules occupy a subregion
V_{a}\subset V
.
Typical industrial laser consists of two cylindrical lamps
of identical size with glass walls
placed at the foci of a metallic cavity
V
with an elliptic cross-section.
The
active lamp

V_{a}
is filled with a dielectric medium doped with identical
active molecules. The other
pumping lamp
is the
discharge light source which produces the pumping field.
In particular, for the typical
cylindrical lamp of length
12\,cm
and diameter
0.6\,cm
we have

|V_{a}|=\frac{12\pi 0.36}{4}\approx 3.4\,cm^{3}.

(2.2)

The active region
V_{a}
is filled with a dielectric medium of electrical conductivity
\sigma
.
For example, the ruby laser is filled with corundum of electrical conductivity

\sigma=10^{-14}Om^{-1}\cdot cm^{-1}=10^{-2}s^{-1}.

(2.3)

The active region
V_{a}
is filled with active molecules located at the points

x_{n}
with numbers

n=1,\dots,N\sim 10^{20}
.
Suppose, for simplicity of notation, that in each active molecule only
one electron is involved in this interaction, and it is subject to an effective static molecular
potential

\Phi_{n}(x)=\Phi(R_{n}(x-x_{n})),\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^{3},\qquad R_{n}\in SO(3).

(2.4)

Here
\Phi
is the potential of the ion (or nucleus) with the total charge
|e|>0
.
Further we consider two different hypothesis on the distribution of
the
random values

x_{n}
and
R_{n}
with
n\in\overline{N}:=(1,\dots,N)
.

H1. Polycrystalline medium (or ”glass medium”
[
37
]
).

The random values
x_{n}\in V
and
R_{n}\in SO(3)

are uniformly distributed and independent, and
almost independent from
x_{n^{\prime}},R_{n^{\prime}}

with large
|x_{n}-x_{n^{\prime}}|
.

H2. Crystalline medium.

The values
x_{n}\in V

are uniformly distributed, while

R_{n}
do not depend on

n\in\overline{N}
.

Maxwell–Schrödinger equations.

Each active molecule is described by the corresponding wave function

\psi_{n}(x,t)
, and in Heaviside–Lorentz units.
Neglecting spin and scalar potential (both can be easily added),
the corresponding Maxwell–Schrödinger system
reads (see

[
4
,
5
,
7
,
13
,
22
,
23
,
24
,

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{1}{c^{2}}\ddot{\bf A}(x,t)&=&\Delta{\bf A}(x,t)-\frac{\sigma}{c^{2}}\dot{\bf A}(x,t)+\frac{1}{c}P_{s}{\bf j}(\cdot,t),\,\,x\in V\par\\
\\
i\hbar\dot{\psi}_{n}(t)&=&H_{n}(t)\psi_{n}(t),\,\,\,n=1,\dots,N\end{array}\right|,

where

We assume the Coulomb gauge

and we denote by

in the Hilbert space

Here

where

The

{\bf j}(x,t)=\frac{e}{{\rm m}}\sum_{n}{\rm Re{\hskip 1.42262pt}}[\overline{\psi}_{n}(x,t)D(t)\psi_{n}(x,t)].

(2.10)

This formula

Note that the pumping

Boundary conditions.

We choose the boundary conditions

More precisely, we assume that

{\bf n}(x)\times{\bf A}(x,t)=0,\quad{\bf n}(x)\cdot{\rm rot{\hskip 1.42262pt}}{\bf A}(x,t)=0;\quad\psi_{n}(x,t)=0,\,\,\,\,n\in\overline{N};\qquad x\in\Gamma,

where

The boundary conditions (

The system (

\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\frac{1}{c^{2}}\dot{\bf A}(t)=D_{\bm{\Pi}}{\cal H}({\bf A}(t),{\bm{\Pi}}(t),\psi(t),t),\,\,\,\frac{1}{c^{2}}\dot{\bm{\Pi}}(t)=-D_{\bf A}{\cal H}({\bf A}(t),{\bm{\Pi}}(t),\psi(t),t)-\frac{\sigma}{c^{2}}\dot{\bf A}(x,t)\\
\\
i\hbar\dot{\psi}_{n}(t)=\frac{1}{2}D_{\psi_{n}}{\cal H}({\bf A}(t),{\bm{\Pi}}(t),\psi(t),t)=H_{n}({\bf A}(t),\psi(t),t)\psi_{n}(t),\end{array}\right|.

In the last equation,

For any

Parametric resonance

Let us denote
L^{2}=L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{3})
.
The system (

{\mathbb Y}=[L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\otimes\mathbb{R}^{3}]^{2}\oplus[L^{2}]^{2N}

(3.1)

This system is

(3.2)

The corresponding

4
Symmetry of spectrum

In the

ideal case
, when
\sigma=0
, the dissipation is absent, and
the system (
3.1
) is Hamiltonian by (
2.15
),
so it can be written as

\dot{Y}(t)=JD_{Y}{\cal H}(Y(t)),t),

(4.1)

where

J
is a skewadjoint operator.
We can make

J^{2}=-1

choosing suitable units wiht
c=2\hbar=1

(note that the multipliers do not depend on the choice of units).
Denote by
{\cal P}:Y(0)\mapsto Y(T)
the corresponding Poincaré map.

Lemma 4.1
.

(Lyapunov–Poincaré theorem)

In the ideal case,
the symmetry holds

{\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}D{\cal P}(Y^{0})=R({\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}D{\cal P}(Y^{0})),

(4.2)

where
R\mu={\overline{\mu}}^{-1}
is the inversion, which is the reflection in the unit circle
|\mu|=1
.

Proof.

The differential
D{\cal P}(Y^{0})

admits the representation

D{\cal P}(Y^{0}){\bf Y}(0)={\bf Y}(T),

(4.3)

where

{\bf Y}(t)
is the solution
to the linearized equation

\dot{\bf Y}(t)=K(t){\bf Y}(t),\qquad K(t)=D_{Y}{\cal F}(Y^{0}_{*}(t),t),

(4.4)

and
Y^{0}_{*}(t)
is the solution to (
4.1
) with
initial condition
Y^{0}_{*}(0)=Y^{0}
.
However, (
4.1
) implies that

{\cal F}(Y,t)=JD_{Y}{\cal H}(Y,t),

and therefore,

K(t)=JD^{2}_{Y}{\cal H}(Y^{0}_{*}(t),t)=JA(t),

(4.5)

where
A(t)
is the selfadjoint operator.
Denote the map
U(t):{\bf Y}(0)\to{\bf Y}(t)
, so

D{\cal P}(Y^{0})=U(T)
by (
4.3
).
Finally,
the Lyapunov–Poincaré arguments
[
41
, (3.28)]

imply that
{\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}U^{*}={\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}U^{-1}
, where
U=U(T)
.
Hence,
the symmetry
(
4.2
) is proved.
∎

Recall the arguments
[
41
, (3.28)]
.
We have

\dot{U}(t)=JA(t)U(t)
, so
differentiating
W(t):=U^{*}(t)JU(t)
, we obtain

\dot{W}=\dot{U}^{*}JU+U^{*}J\dot{U}=U^{*}A(-J^{2})U+U^{*}J^{2}AU=0

(4.6)

since
J^{2}=-1
commutes with
A
.
Therefore,
U^{*}(t)JU(t)=J
, and hence
U^{*}(t)=JU^{-1}(t)J^{-1}
.
This similarity implies that the spectra of
U^{*}(t)
and
U^{-1}(t)

coincide.

Remark 4.2
.

The symmetry (
4.2
) can be considered as a nonlinear version of the
Lyapunov–Poincaré theorem, which plays the key role in M.G. Krein’s theory of parametric resonance
[

,

]
.
The ground for this extension is the Hamiltonian structure of the dynamics (

).

Thus, in the ideal case,
the set of multipliers is symmetric with respect to the unit circle.
In general case, when the dissipation is included, the symmetry (

) is broken, though
is holds approximately for sufficiently small

:

{\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}D{\cal P}(Y^{0})\approx R({\rm Spec{\hskip 1.42262pt}}D{\cal P}(Y^{0})).

(4.7)

Accordingly, the parametric resonance (
1.1
) is
very likely
in this case.
The required smallness defines
the
pumping/damping
threshold
.

Remark 4.3
.

Similar symmetry also holds for the case of the Maxwell–Bloch equations (

)
which makes the parametric resonance
(

)
very plausible
.

independently

Remark 16.1
.

This

is provisionally in line with the

and with

Local coordinates.

To calculate

The charge conservation (

Denote by

.
One branch of the inverse map
is a diffeomorphism of

onto the neighborhood

of the point

(where

), and another

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}|c_{1}|>|c_{2}|&{\rm for}&(c_{1},c_{2})_{*}\in S^{2}_{+}\\

Appendix A
On averaging of slow rotations

Here we justify the
approximation of the Schrödinger equations from (
13.3
)
by their averaged version (
14.6
) (“rotating wave approximation”

[
1
,
3
,
14
,
28
,
31
,
33
,
36
,
42
]
).
Let us show that
this approximation is very accurate for small matrices

\Omega_{n}(\tau)
. Namely, consider the system

\dot{c}(\tau)=-i\Omega(\tau)c(\tau),\qquad\tau\in[0,T],

(A.1)

where
\Omega(\tau)
is a
bounded measurable
2\times 2
complex matrix-function,

\varepsilon:=\sup_{\tau\in[0,T]}|\Omega(\tau)|<\infty.

(A.2)

Denote the averaged matrix

\tilde{\Omega}:=\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Omega(\tau)d\tau

(A.3)

and consider the corresponding averaged system

\dot{\hat{c}}(\tau)=-i\tilde{\Omega}\hat{c}(\tau),\qquad\tau\in[0,T].

(A.4)

Lemma A.1
.

For solutions of (
A.1
) and (
A.4
) with identical initial data

\hat{c}(0)=c(0)=c_{0}
,
their final values
c(T)
and

\hat{c}(T)
are very close for small
\varepsilon
:

|c(T)-\hat{c}(T)|=O(\varepsilon^{2}),\qquad\varepsilon\to 0.

(A.5)

Proof.

Rewrite (
A.1
) as

\dot{c}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)=-i\varepsilon\Omega_{1}(\tau)c_{\varepsilon}(\tau),\qquad\tau\in[0,T];\qquad c_{\varepsilon}(0)=c_{0},

(A.6)

where
\Omega_{1}(\tau):=\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\Omega(\tau)
.
The corresponding averaged system (
A.4
) now reads

\dot{\hat{c}}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)=-i\varepsilon\hat{\Omega}_{1}(\tau)\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(\tau),\qquad\tau\in[0,T];\qquad\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(0)=c_{0},

(A.7)

where
\tilde{\Omega}_{1}(\tau):=\displaystyle\frac{1}{T}\int_{0}^{T}\Omega_{1}(\tau)d\tau
.
It suffices to prove that

|c_{\varepsilon}(T)-\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(T)|=O(\varepsilon^{2}),\qquad\varepsilon\to 0.

(A.8)

The Taylor formula gives

c_{\varepsilon}(\tau)=c_{0}+\varepsilon[\partial_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}(\tau)]|_{\varepsilon=0}+O(\varepsilon^{2}),\qquad\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)=c_{0}+\varepsilon[\partial_{\varepsilon}\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)]|_{\varepsilon=0}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

(A.9)

Therefore,
(
A.8
) will follow from the identity

[\partial_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}(T)]|_{\varepsilon=0}=[\partial_{\varepsilon}\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(T)]|_{\varepsilon=0}.

(A.10)

Indeed,
\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(T)=e^{-i\varepsilon\tilde{\Omega}T}c_{0}
, so
the right-hand side of (
A.10
) is equal to

[\partial_{\varepsilon}\hat{c}_{\varepsilon}(T)]|_{\varepsilon=0}=-i\tilde{\Omega}_{1}Tc_{0}=-i\int_{0}^{T}\Omega_{1}(\tau)c_{0}d\tau.

(A.11)

It remains to calculate the left-hand side.
Denote

b_{\varepsilon}(\tau):=\partial_{\varepsilon}c_{\varepsilon}(\tau).

(A.12)

Differentiating equation (
A.6
) in
\varepsilon
,
we obtain

\dot{b}_{\varepsilon}(\tau)=-i\Omega_{1}(\tau)c_{\varepsilon}(\tau)-i\varepsilon\Omega_{1}(\tau)b_{\varepsilon}(\tau),\qquad\tau\in[0,T];\qquad b_{\varepsilon}(0)=0.

(A.13)

In particular, for
\varepsilon=0
we have

\dot{b}_{0}(\tau)=-i\Omega_{1}(\tau)c_{0},\qquad\tau\in[0,T];\qquad b_{0}(0)=0.

(A.14)

Hence,

b_{0}(T)=-i\int_{0}^{T}\Omega_{1}(\tau)c_{0}d\tau.

(A.15)

Now (
A.10
) is proved.
∎

Remark A.2
.

Equation (
A.6
)
can be rewritten for

(A.16)

where

Thus, Lemma
A.1
