How to play the Accordion: Uniformity and the (non-)conservativity of the linear approximation of the λ-calculus
Rémy Cerda, Lionel Vaux Auclair

Abstract
Twenty years after its introduction by Ehrhard and Regnier, differentiation in λ-calculus and in linear logic is now a celebrated tool. In particular, it allows to write the Taylor formula in various λ-calculi, hence providing a theory of linear approximations for these calculi. In the standard λ-calculus, this linear approximation is expressed by results stating that the (possibly) infinitary β-reduction of λ-terms is simulated by the reduction of their Taylor expansion: in terms of rewriting systems, the resource reduction (operating on Taylor approximants) is an extension of the β-reduction. In this paper, we address the converse property, conservativity: are there reductions of the Taylor approximants that do not arise from an actual β-reduction of the approximated term? We show that if we restrict the setting to finite terms and β-reduction sequences, then the linear approximation is conservative. However, as soon as one allows infinitary reduction sequences this property is broken. We design a counter-example, the Accordion. Then we show how restricting the reduction of the Taylor approximants allows to build a conservative extension of the β-reduction preserving good simulation properties. This restriction relies on uniformity, a property that was already at the core of Ehrhard and Regnier’s pioneering work.

Introduction

The traditional approach to program approximation
in a functional setting consists in describing the
total information that a (potentially non-terminating) program can produce
by the supremum of the finite pieces of information
it can produce in finite time.
This
continuous
approximation is tightly related to
the Scott semantics of λ-calculi
[
29
]
:
the Böhm tree of a term (or equivalently its semantics)
is the limit of the approximants produced by hereditary head reduction

[
21
,
32
,
3
]
.

More recently, Ehrhard and Regnier introduced the differential λ-calculus
and differential linear logic

[
16
,
17
]
,
following ideas rooted in the semantics of linear logic

[
20
,
13
,
14
]
.
This suggested the renewed approach of
linear
approximation
of functional programs.
In this setting,
a program (
i.e.
a λ-term) is approximated by multilinear
(or
“polynomial”
) programs,
obtained by iterated differentiation at zero.
Using this differential formalism, the Taylor formula yields
a weighted sum
\mathcal{T}(M)
of all multilinear approximants of a λ-term
M
,
producing the same total information as
M
,

via
normalization.
More precisely, Ehrhard and Regnier’s

“commutation”
theorem
[
19
,
18
]

ensure that the normal form of the Taylor expansion of
M

is the Taylor expansion of the Böhm tree of
M
:

\nf(\mathcal{T}(M))=\mathcal{T}(\BT(M))

(1)

(and a Böhm tree is uniquely determined by its Taylor expansion).
This approach subsumes the previous one

[
2
]
. In addition, it
allows for characterising quantitative properties of programs
(
e.g.
complexity
[
11
]
),
which is a key benefit of linearity.
This approximation technique
has been fruitfully applied to many languages,
richer than the plain λ-calculus:
nondeterministic
[
31
]
,
probabilistic
[
10
]
,
extensional
[
5
]
,
call-by-value
[
23
]
, and call-by-push-value

[
15
,
9
]
calculi,
as well as for Parigot’s λμ-calculus
[
1
]
.
The interplay of the operational and Taylor approximations
also suggests a broader notion of a approximation of
a computation process
[
26
,
12
]
.

Another benefit of linear approximation is that it
can approximate not only β-normalisation
(the information ultimately produced by a program)
but β-reduction
(the
“information flow”
along program execution).
In particular,
\cref
intro:commut can be refined into

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}N\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}(M)\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{T}(N),

(2)

where
\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}
denotes the so-called
“resource”
reduction
acting linearly on approximants.
As highlighted by our previous work

[
8
,
7
]
,
this can even be extended to

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{\infty}N\Rightarrow\mathcal{T}(M)\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}\mathcal{T}(N)

(3)

if one extends the λ-calculus with infinite λ-terms
and an infinitary closure of the β-reduction,
which is a way to encompass infinite computations and their limits

[
22
]
.

This paper is interested in the converse of

\cref
intro:sim-fin,intro:sim-infin:
is the linear approximation of the λ-calculus conservative?
In other terms, we ask whether every resource reduction
from
\mathcal{T}(M)
to
\mathcal{T}(N)
corresponds to a
β-reduction sequence from
M
to
N
.

We show that the finite β-reduction of finite λ-terms
is conservatively approximated (
\cref
fin),
but we are able to design a counter-example to conservativity
(the Accordion
\mathtt{A}
) as soon as we want to approximate
infinitary β-reductions (
\cref
infin).
However, we introduce a
uniform
linear approximation
allowing for the same good properties as the standard one,
while enjoying conservativity (
\cref
uniformity).

2
Preliminaries

In this section, we briefly recall the linear approximation
of the λ-calculus, following its refined presentation in

[
7
]
.
We first recall the definition of the λ-calculus,
as well as its
“001”
infinitary extension:
this is the version of the infinitary λ-calculus
that fits the formalism of both continuous and linear approximations
as they are usually presented (
\cref
ssec:lcalc).
Then we present the resource λ-calculus,

i.e.
a linear variant of the λ-calculus
(there are no duplications or erasures of subterms
during the reduction) enjoying strong confluence
and normalisation properties (
\cref
ssec:rcalc).
Finally, the linear approximation relies on the
Taylor expansion, that maps a λ-term
to a sum of resource terms,
in a way such that the reduction of λ-terms
is simulated by the reduction of the resource approximants
(
\cref
ssec:taylor).

2.1
Finite and infinitary λ-calculi

We give a brief presentation of the 001-infinitary λ-calculus.
A more detailed exposition and a general account of infinitary λ-calculi
can be found in
[
7
,
4
]
.
From now on, we fix a countable set
\mathcal{V}
of variables.

Definition 1
.

The set
\Lambda
of (finite)
λ-terms
is
the set
\mathcal{X}
defined
by the inductive rules:

(\mathcal{V})

\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}x\in\mathcal{V}
\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}x\in\mathcal{X}

(λ)

x\in\mathcal{V}
M\in\mathcal{X}
λx.M\in\mathcal{X}

(@)

M\in\mathcal{X}
N\in\mathcal{X}
(M)N\in\mathcal{X}.

The set
\Lambda^{001}
of
001-infinitary λ-terms
is
the set
\mathcal{X}
defined
by the rules
(\mathcal{V})
,
(λ)
, and:

(@^{001})

M\in\mathcal{X}
\mathrel{\triangleright}N\in\mathcal{X}
(M)N\in\mathcal{X}

(\mathrel{\triangleright})

\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}N\in\mathcal{X}
\mathrel{\triangleright}\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}N\in\mathcal{X}

where the rule
(\mathrel{\triangleright})
is treated coinductively:
infinite derivations are allowed provided each infinite branch
crosses infinitely often this coinductive rule.

This means that
\Lambda^{001}
contains the infinitary λ-terms whose syntax tree
contains only infinite branches entering infinitely often
the argument side of an application.

Notice that we use Krivine’s notation for applications

[
25
]
,
i.e.
we parenthesise functions instead of arguments.
We abbreviate the application of a term to successive arguments

(\cdots((M)N_{1})\cdots)N_{k}
as
(M)N_{1}\cdots N_{k}
,
which is obtained by nesting applications on the left:
this allows to use parentheses more sparingly,
which will be a great relief later on.
By contrast,
(M_{1})(M_{2})\cdots(M_{k})N
is obtained
by nesting applications on the right.
A typical example of a term in
\Lambda^{001}
is

(x)^{\omega}\coloneqq(x)(x)(x)\dots
.
Observe also that there is an immediate inclusion
\Lambda\subseteq\Lambda^{001}
.
On the contrary, neither
λx_{0}.λx_{1}.λx_{2}.\dots

nor
(((\dots)x_{2})x_{1})x_{0}
are allowed in
\Lambda^{001}
.

In practice we only consider infinitary terms having
finitely many free variables,
which allows us to consider them up to α-equivalence
(
i.e.
renaming of bound variables)
— as one usually does when dealing with λ-terms,
and as we will do implicitely in all this paper.
This enables us to define capture-avoiding substitution
in the usual way, and we denote by
M[N/x]
the term obtained
by substituting
N
to
x
in
M
.
We refer to
[
6
]
for a more careful and detailed presentation.
These sets come equipped with the following dynamics.

Definition 2
.

The relation
\mathord{\longrightarrow_{β}}\subset\Lambda^{001}\times\Lambda^{001}

of
β-reduction
is defined by the rules:

(β)

(λx.M)N\longrightarrow_{β}M[N/x]

(λ_{β})

P\longrightarrow_{β}P^{\prime}
λx.P\longrightarrow_{β}λx.P^{\prime}

(@l_{β})

P\longrightarrow_{β}P^{\prime}
(P)Q\longrightarrow_{β}(P^{\prime})Q

(@r_{β})

Q\longrightarrow_{β}Q^{\prime}
(P)Q\longrightarrow_{β}(P)Q^{\prime}.

Definition 3
.

The relation
\mathord{\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}}\subset\Lambda^{001}\times\Lambda^{001}

of
001-infinitary β-reduction
is defined by the rules:

(\mathcal{V}_{β}^{001})

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}x
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}x

(λ_{β}^{001})

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}λx.P
P\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}P^{\prime}
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}λx.P^{\prime}

(@_{β}^{001})

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}(P)Q
P\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}P^{\prime}
\mathrel{\triangleright}Q\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}Q^{\prime}
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}(P^{\prime})Q^{\prime}

(\mathrel{\triangleright})

\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}Q\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}Q^{\prime}
\mathrel{\triangleright}\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}Q\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}Q^{\prime}

where
\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}
denotes the reflexive-transitive closure of
\longrightarrow_{β}
.

Infinitary β-reduction can be understood as allowing an infinite number
of β-reduction steps, as long as the β-redexes are fired
inside increasingly nested arguments of applications.
This is formalised in the following result:

Theorem 4

(stratification)
.

Given
M,N\in\Lambda^{001}
, there is a reduction
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}N
iff
there exists a sequence of terms
(M_{d})\in(\Lambda^{001})^{\mathbb{N}}

such that for all
d\in\mathbb{N}
,

M=M_{0}\longrightarrow_{β\geq 0}^{*}M_{1}\longrightarrow_{β\geq 1}^{*}M_{2}\longrightarrow_{β\geq 2}^{*}\dots\longrightarrow_{β\geq d-1}^{*}M_{d}\longrightarrow_{β\geq d}^{001}N,

where
\longrightarrow_{β\geq d}^{*}
and
\longrightarrow_{β\geq d}^{001}
denote β-reductions
occurring inside (at least)
d
nested arguments of applications.
Formally,

β-reduction at minimum depth d

is defined by:

(\mathcal{V}_{β\geq 0})

M\longrightarrow_{β}M^{\prime}
M\longrightarrow_{β\geq 0}M^{\prime}

(λ_{β\geq d+1})

P\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}P^{\prime}
λx.P\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}λx.P^{\prime}

(@l_{β\geq d+1})

P\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}P^{\prime}
(P)Q\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}(P^{\prime})Q

(@r_{β\geq d+1})

Q\longrightarrow_{β\geq d}Q^{\prime}
(P)Q\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}(P)Q^{\prime}

and
001-infinitary β-reduction at minimum depth d
is
defined by:

(\mathcal{V}_{β\geq 0}^{001})

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}M^{\prime}
M\longrightarrow_{β\geq 0}^{001}M^{\prime}

(\mathcal{V}_{β\geq d+1}^{001})

x\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}^{001}x

(λ_{β\geq d+1}^{001})

P\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}^{001}P^{\prime}
λx.P\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}^{001}λx.P^{\prime}

(@_{β\geq d+1}^{001})

P\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}^{001}P^{\prime}
Q\longrightarrow_{β\geq d}^{001}Q^{\prime}
(P)Q\longrightarrow_{β\geq d+1}^{001}(P^{\prime})Q

.

A typical (and even motivating) example of an infinitary β-reduction
involves the fix-point combinator
\mathtt{Y}\coloneqq λf.(λx.(f)(x)x)λx.(f)(x)x
.
It consists in the reduction
(\mathtt{Y})M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}(M)^{\omega}

corresponding to the sequence

(\mathtt{Y})M\longrightarrow_{β\geq 0}^{*}(M)(\mathtt{Y})M\longrightarrow_{β\geq 1}^{*}(M)(M)(\mathtt{Y})M\longrightarrow_{β\geq 2}^{*}\dots

On the contrary, the infinite reduction sequence

\text{{Ω}}\longrightarrow_{β}\text{{Ω}}\longrightarrow_{β}\text{{Ω}}\longrightarrow_{β}\ldots
,
where
\text{{Ω}}\coloneqq(λx.(x)x)λx.(x)x
,
does not give rise to a
001
-infinitary reduction
because the redexes are fired at top-level all the way.
On the other hand, each finite reduction sequence

\text{{Ω}}\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}\text{{Ω}}
induces a reduction

\text{{Ω}}\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}\text{{Ω}}
, but only because
\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}
contains
\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}

(see
[
8
]
, Lemma 2.13).

2.2
The resource λ-calculus

The resource λ-calculus is the target language of the linear approximation
of the λ-calculus. We recall its construction, and we refer to

[
31
,
7
]
for more details.
The main intuition behind this calculus is that arguments become

finite multisets
, and that
(λx.s)[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]

will reduce to a term obtained by substituting
linearly

one
t_{i}
for each occurrence of
x
in
s
.
The different matchings of the
t_{i}
’s and the occurrences of
x

are superposed by a sum operator;
if a wrong number of
t_{i}
’s is provided,
the term collapses to the empty sum.

Given a set
\mathcal{X}
, we denote by
!\mathcal{X}

the set of finite multisets of elements of
\mathcal{X}
.
A multiset is denoted by
\bar{x}=[x_{1},\dots,x_{n}]
,
with its elements in an arbitrary order.
Multiset union is denoted multiplicatively, by
\bar{x}\cdot\bar{y}
.
Accordingly, the empty multiset is denoted by
1
.
We may also write
[x_{1}^{k_{1}},\ldots,x_{m}^{k_{m}}]

to indicate multiplicities:
this is the same as
[x_{1}]^{k_{1}}\cdot\ldots\cdot[x_{m}]^{k_{m}}
.

Definition 5
.

The set
\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
of
resource terms

is defined by the rules:

(\mathcal{V})

\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}x\in\mathcal{V}
\mathrel{}
\mathrel{}x\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}

(λ)

x\in\mathcal{V}
s\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
λx.s\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}

(@!)

s\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
\bar{t}\in\mathord{!\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
(s)\bar{t}\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}

and is implicitely quotiented by α-equivalence.
Multisets in
\mathord{!\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
are called
resource monomials
.
To denote indistinctly
\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
or
\mathord{!\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
, we write
\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
.

Given a semiring
\mathbb{S}
and a set
\mathcal{X}
, we denote by

\mathbb{S}^{\mathcal{X}}
the set of possibly infinite linear combinations
of elements of
\mathcal{X}
with coefficients in
\mathbb{S}
,
considered as formal weighted sums.
Given a sum
\mathbf{S}\in\mathbb{S}^{\mathcal{X}}
, its support
\left|\mathbf{S}\right|

is the set of all elements of
\mathcal{X}
bearing a non-null coefficient.
We also denote by
\mathbb{S}^{(\mathcal{X})}
the sub-semimodule
of
\mathbb{S}^{\mathcal{X}}
of all sums having a finite support.

We use the following syntactic sugar.
The empty sum
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}0\cdot x
is denoted by
0
.
The one-element sum
\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}\delta_{x,y}\cdot x

is assimilated to
y
, yielding an inclusion

\mathcal{X}\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{\mathcal{X}}
.
Sums can be summed,
i.e.

\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}a_{x}\cdot x+\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}b_{x}\cdot x=\sum_{x\in\mathcal{X}}(a_{x}+b_{x})\cdot x
.
It is also convenient to extend by linearity
all the constructors of the calculus
to sums of resource terms,
i.e.

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle λx.\left(\sum\limits_{i\in I}a_{i}\cdot s_{i}\right)\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i\in I}a_{i}\cdot λx.s_{i},

(4)

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\left(\sum\limits_{i\in I}a_{i}\cdot s_{i}\right)\sum\limits_{j\in J}b_{j}\cdot\bar{t}_{j}\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i\in I}\sum\limits_{j\in J}a_{i}b_{j}\cdot(s_{i})\bar{t}_{j},

\displaystyle\textstyle\displaystyle\left[\sum\limits_{i\in I}a_{i}\cdot s_{i}\right]\cdot\sum\limits_{j\in J}b_{j}\cdot\bar{t}\coloneqq\sum\limits_{i\in I}\sum\limits_{j\in J}a_{i}b_{j}\,\cdot\,[s_{i}]\cdot\bar{t}_{j}\,.

Definition 6
.

For all
u\in\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
,
\bar{t}=[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]\in\mathord{!\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}

and
x\in\mathcal{V}
, the
multilinear substitution

of
x
by
\bar{t}
in
u
is the finite sum

s\langle\bar{t}/x\rangle\in\mathbb{N}^{(\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}})}
defined by

s\langle\bar{t}/x\rangle\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\displaystyle\sum_{\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}(n)}u[t_{\sigma(1)}/x_{1},\dots,t_{\sigma(n)}/x_{n}]&\text{if $x$ occurs $n$ times in $u$}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{array}\right.

where
x_{1},\dots,x_{n}
is an arbitrary enumeration of the occurrences
of
x
in
u
, and
u[t_{\sigma(1)}/x_{1},\dots]

denotes the result of the (capture-avoiding) substitution
of each
x_{i}
by the corresponding
t_{\sigma(i)}
.

Definition 7
.

The relation
\mathord{\longrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}}\subset\mathbb{N}^{(\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}})}\times\mathbb{N}^{(\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}})}

of
resource β-reduction
is defined
using the auxiliary relation

\mathord{\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}}\subset\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}\times\mathbb{N}^{(\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}})}

generated by the rules

(β_{\mathrm{r}})

\left(λx.s\right)\bar{t}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}s\langle\bar{t}/x\rangle

(λ_{\mathrm{r}})

s\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}S^{\prime}
λx.s\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}λx.S^{\prime}

(@l_{\mathrm{r}})

s\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}S^{\prime}
\left(s\right)\bar{t}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}\left(S^{\prime}\right)\bar{t}

(@r_{\mathrm{r}})

\bar{t}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}\bar{T}^{\prime}
\left(s\right)\bar{t}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}\left(s\right)\bar{T}^{\prime}

(!_{\mathrm{r}})

s\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}S^{\prime}
[s]\cdot\bar{t}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}{[S^{\prime}]}\cdot\bar{t}

as well as the lifting rule

(\Sigma_{\mathrm{r}})

u_{1}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}U^{\prime}_{1}
\forall i\geq 2,\ u_{i}\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}^{?}U^{\prime}_{i}
\sum_{i=1}^{n}u_{i}\longrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}U^{\prime}_{i}

where
\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}^{?}
is the reflexive closure of
\relbar\joinrel\rightharpoonup_{\mathrm{r}}
.

From now on, we fix a semiring
\mathbb{S}
.
We consider
\mathbb{N}
as a subset of
\mathbb{S}
through the map

n\mapsto 1+\ldots+1
(notice however that it might not be an injection),
and we suppose that
\mathbb{S}

“has fractions”
,

i.e.
for all non-null
n\in\mathbb{N}
there is some
\frac{1}{n}\in\mathbb{S}

such that
n\times\frac{1}{n}=1
.
This is the case of the semirings
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
and
\mathbb{R}_{+}

of non-negative rational (resp. real) numbers,
but also of the semiring
\mathbb{B}
of boolean values
(equipped with the logical
“or”
and
“and”
operations).

Definition 8
.

Given a set
\mathcal{X}
and a semiring
\mathbb{S}
,
a family of sums
(\mathbf{S}_{i})_{i\in I}\in(\mathbb{S}^{\mathcal{X}})^{I}

is
summable
when each
x\in\mathcal{X}

bears a non-null coefficient in finitely many of the
\mathbf{S}_{i}
.
If this is the case then
\sum_{i\in I}\mathbf{S}_{i}
is a well-defined sum.

Definition 9
.

The relation
\mathord{\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}}\subset\mathbb{S}^{\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}}\times\mathbb{S}^{\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}}

of
pointwise resource reduction
is defined by saying that
there is a reduction
\mathbf{U}\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}\mathbf{V}

whenever there are summable families

(u_{i})_{i\in I}\in(\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}})^{I}
and

(V_{i})_{i\in I}\in(\mathbb{N}^{(\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}})})^{I}

such that

Theorem 17

4
Non-conservativity wrt. the infinitary λ-calculus

The previous theorem relied on the excellent properties
of the Taylor expansion of finite λ-terms:
a single (well-chosen) term
\lfloor M\rfloor_{\mathrm{r}}\in\left|\mathcal{T}(M)\right|
is enough to
characterise
M
,
and a single (again, well-chosen) sequence of resource reducts of some

s\in\left|\mathcal{T}(M)\right|
suffices to characterise any sequence
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}N
.
These properties are not true any more when considering more complicated
settings, like the 001-infinitary λ-calculus.
This does not only make the
“mashup”
proof technique fail,
but also enables us to give a negative answer to
\cref
problem:infinitary.

4.1
Failure of the
“mashup”
technique

Let us first describe where we hit an obstacle if we try to reproduce
the proof we have given in the finite setting,
which will make clearer the way we later build a counterexample.

First, it is not obvious what the mashup relation should be:
we could just use the relation
\vdash
defined on

\Lambda^{001}\times\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
by the same set of rules as in

\cref
conserv:defi:mashup,
or define an infinitary mashup
\vdash_{001}
by the rules

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}x
M\vdash_{001}x

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}λx.P
P\vdash_{001}s
M\vdash_{001}λx.s

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}(P)Q
P\vdash_{001}s
Q\vdash_{001}\bar{t}
M\vdash_{001}\left(s\right)\bar{t}

M\vdash_{001}t_{1}
\dots
M\vdash_{001}t_{n}
M\vdash_{001}[t_{1},\dots,t_{n}]

and extend it to
\mathbb{S}^{\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}}
accordingly.
In fact, this happens to define the same relation.

Lemma 24
.

For all
M\in\Lambda^{001}
and
s\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
,

M\vdash_{001}s
iff
M\vdash s
.

Proof.

The inclusion

\mathord{\vdash}\subseteq\mathord{\vdash_{001}}
is immediate.
Let us show the converse.
First, observe that the proof of
\cref
conserv:lem:mashup-2flb
can be easily extended in order to show that
for all
M,N\in\Lambda^{001}
and
s\in\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
,
if
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}N\vdash_{001}s
then
M\vdash_{001}s
.
Then we proceed by induction on
s
.

•

If
M\vdash_{001}x
, then
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}x
,
i.e.

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}x
,
and finally
M\vdash x
.

•

If
M\vdash_{001}λx.u
, then there is a derivation:

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}λx.P
P\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}P^{\prime}
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}λx.P^{\prime}

P^{\prime}\vdash_{001}u
M\vdash_{001}λx.u

Since
P\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}P^{\prime}\vdash_{001}u
,
we have
P\vdash_{001}u
,
and by induction on
u
we obtain
P\vdash u
.
With
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}λx.P
, this yields
M\vdash λx.u
.

•

The case of
M\vdash_{001}\left(u\right)\bar{v}
is similar. ∎

As a consequence,
\crefrange
conserv:lem:mashup-1refl
conserv:lem:mashup-4flr can be easily extended
to
\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}
and
\vdash_{001}
.
We have already explained how the proof of this can be done for

\cref
conserv:lem:mashup-2flb;
for the other ones, one just needs to observe that
the proofs are all by induction on
resource terms or on some inductively defined relation,
hence replacing
\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}
with
\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}

does not change anything
(and neither does replacing
\vdash
with
\vdash_{001}
,
thanks to
\cref
conserv:lem:mashup-001-is-mashup).

The failure of the infinitary
“mashup”
proof occurs
in the extension of
\cref
conserv:lem:mashup-5tay.
Indeed, this proof crucially relies on the existence of an injection

\lfloor-\rfloor_{\mathrm{r}}:\Lambda\to\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}
,
whereas for
\Lambda^{001}
there is only the counterpart

\lfloor-\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},-}:\Lambda^{001}\times\mathbb{N}\to\mathord{\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}

defined by

\displaystyle\lfloor x\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq x

\displaystyle\lfloor(P)Q\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},0}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\lfloor P\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},0}\right)1

\displaystyle\lfloor λx.P\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}

\displaystyle\coloneqq λx.\lfloor P\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}

\displaystyle\lfloor(P)Q\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d+1}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left(\lfloor P\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d+1}\right)\left[\lfloor Q\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}\right].

Now, if we suppose that
M\vdash\mathcal{T}(N)

and we want to show that
M\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}N
,
we cannot rely any more on the fact that
M\vdash\lfloor N\rfloor_{\mathrm{r}}
,
but only on the fact that

\forall d\in\mathbb{N},\ M\vdash\lfloor N\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}
.
This makes the induction fail.
For instance, for the case where

N
is an abstraction
λx.P^{\prime}
,
we obtain a
d
-indexed sequence of derivations

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}λx.P_{d}
P_{d}\vdash\lfloor P^{\prime}\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}
M\vdash\lfloor N\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}=\lfloor λx.P^{\prime}\rfloor_{\mathrm{r},d}

but nothing tells us that the terms
P_{d}
and reductions

M\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}λx.P_{d}
are coherent!
This failure is what enables us to design a counterexample.

4.2
The Accordion

In this section, we define 001-infinitary λ-terms
\mathtt{A}
and
\bar{\mathtt{A}}

and show that they form a counterexample not only to the
001-infinitary counterpart of
\cref
conserv:lem:mashup-5tay,
but also to the conservativity property in the infinitary setting.

Notation 25
.

We denote as follows the usual representation of booleans,
an
“applicator”

\langle-\rangle
,
and the Church encodings of integers and of the successor function:

\displaystyle\mathtt{T}\coloneqq λx.λy.x\qquad\mathtt{F}\coloneqq λx.λy.y\qquad\langle M\rangle\coloneqq λb.(b)M

\longrightarrow_{β}^{*}

•

5
The missing ingredient: Uniformity

The fact that the simulation of
\longrightarrow_{β}^{001}
by
\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}

via
the Taylor expansion is not conservative
confirms that the pointwise reduction
\relbar\joinrel\twoheadrightarrow_{\mathrm{r}}
,
even if needed in order to express the pointwise normal form
of a sum through the resource reduction,
weakens the dynamics of the β-reduction
by allowing to reduce resource approximants
along reductions paths that do not correspond
to an actual reduction of the approximated term.
As already underlined by Ehrhard and Regnier in their
seminal work
[
19
]
,

uniformity
is what gives the linear approximation
all its robustness; this will also be the case for our study.

Definition 32
.

The relation

\mathord{\mathrel{\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\hbox{$\hbox{\hskip 5.0pt\hskip 0.0pt\hbox{$$}\hskip 0.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\raisebox{0.0pt}{\hbox{$\frown$}}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip 5.0pt}$}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip-5.0pt\raisebox{-3.5764pt}{\hbox{$\smile$}}\hskip-5.0pt\hskip 5.0pt}}}\subset\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}\times\mathord{(!)\Lambda_{\mathrm{r}}}

of

Notation 35
.

The (applicative)

defined by

\displaystyle\rdepth(x)

\displaystyle\coloneqq 0

\displaystyle\rdepth(\left(s\right)\bar{t})
