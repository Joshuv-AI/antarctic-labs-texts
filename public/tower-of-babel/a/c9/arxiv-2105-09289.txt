Bicomplex Version of Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem and Hyperbolic Invariant Measure
Chinmay Ghosh, Soumen Mondal

Abstract
In this article we have studied bicomplex valued measurable functions on an arbitrary measurable space. We have established the bicomplex version of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem and some other results related to this theorem. Also we have proved the bicomplex version of Lebesgue-Radon-Nikodym theorem. Finally we have introduced the idea of hyperbolic version of invariant measure. AMS Subject Classification (2010) : 28E99, 30G35. Keywords and Phrases : Bicomplex measurable function; Bicomplex Lebesgue integrable function; Hyperbolic invariant measure. .

2
Basis definitions

We denote the set of real and complex numbers by
\mathbb{R}
and
\mathbb{C}
respectively. We may think three imaginary numbers
\mathbf{i}_{1},\mathbf{i}_{2}
and
\mathbf{j}
governed by the rules

\mathbf{i}_{1}^{2}=-1,\mathbf{i}_{2}^{2}=-1,\mathbf{j}^{2}=1

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}_{1}\mathbf{i}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}_{2}\mathbf{i}_{1}=\mathbf{j}

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}_{1}\mathbf{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{ji}_{1}=-\mathbf{i}_{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{i}_{2}\mathbf{j}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbf{ji}_{2}=-\mathbf{i}_{1}.

Then we have two complex planes
\mathbb{C}\left(\mathbf{i}_{1}\right)=\left\{x+\mathbf{i}_{1}y:x,y\in\mathbb{R}\right\}
and
\mathbb{C}\left(\mathbf{i}_{2}\right)=\left\{x+\mathbf{i}_{2}y:x,y\in\mathbb{R}\right\},

both of which are identical to
\mathbb{C}.
Bicomplex numbers are defined
as
\zeta=z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}z_{2}
for
z_{1},z_{2}\in\mathbb{C}\left(\mathbf{i}_{1}\right)
. The set of all bicomplex numbers is denoted by
\mathbb{T}
. In particular if
z_{1}=x,z_{2}=\mathbf{i}_{1}y
where
x,y\in\mathbb{R}
we get
\zeta=x+\mathbf{j}y
and these type of numbers are
called hyperbolic numbers or duplex numbers. The set of all hyperbolic
numbers is denoted by
\mathbb{D}
. For
\left(z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}z_{2}\right),\left(w_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}w_{2}\right)\in\mathbb{T},

the addition and multiplication are definde as

\displaystyle\left(z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}z_{2}\right)+\left(w_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}w_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(z_{1}+w_{1}\right)+\mathbf{i}_{2}\left(z_{2}+w_{2}\right)

\displaystyle\left(z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}z_{2}\right)\left(w_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}w_{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(z_{1}w_{1}-z_{2}w_{2}\right)+\mathbf{i}_{2}\left(z_{1}w_{2}+z_{2}w_{1}\right).

With these operations
\mathbb{T}
forms a commutative ring with zero
divisors. The elements
z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}z_{2}\in\mathbb{T}
such that
z_{1}^{2}+z_{2}^{2}=0
are the zero divisors. The interesting property of a
bicomplex number is its idempotent representation. Setting
\mathbf{e}_{1}=\frac{1+\mathbf{j}}{2}
and
\mathbf{e}_{2}=\frac{1-\mathbf{j}}{2},
we get

z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{2}z_{2}=\left(z_{1}-\mathbf{i}_{1}z_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(z_{1}+\mathbf{i}_{1}z_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Many calculations become easier for this representation.

Throughout this article we will consider
\mathfrak{M}
to be a
\sigma-
algebra in a set
X,
unless stated otherwise.

2.1
Partial order on
\mathbb{D}

The set of nonnegative hyperbolic numbers is

\mathbb{D}^{+}=\left\{\nu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\nu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}:\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\geq 0\right\}.

A hyperbolic number
\zeta
is said to be
\left(\text{strictly}\right)

positive if
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}^{+}\backslash\left\{0\right\}.
The set
of nonnegative hyperbolic numbers is also defined as

\mathbb{D}^{+}=\left\{x+y\mathbf{k}:x^{2}-y^{2}\geq 0,x\geq 0\right\}.

On the realization of
\mathbb{D}^{+},
M.E. Luna-Elizarraras et.al.
[
6
]
defined a partial order relation on
\mathbb{D}
. For two hyperbolic
numbers
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2}
the relation
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}
is
defined as

\zeta_{1}\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{2}\text{ if and only if }\zeta_{2}-\zeta_{1}\in\mathbb{D}^{+}.

One can check that this relation is reflexive, transitive and antisymmetric.
Therefore
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}
is a partial order relation on
\mathbb{D}
. This partial order relation
\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}
on
\mathbb{D}

is an extension of the total order relation
\leq
on
\mathbb{R}.
We say
\zeta_{1}\prec_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{2}
if
\zeta_{1}\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{2}
but
\zeta_{1}\neq\zeta_{2}.
Also we say
\zeta_{2}\succeq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{1}
if
\zeta_{1}\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{2}
and
\zeta_{2}\succ_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{1}
if
\zeta_{1}\prec_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\zeta_{2}.

Definition 1

For any hyperbolic number
\zeta=\nu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\nu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},
the
\mathbb{D}-
modulus of
\zeta
is defined by

\left|\zeta\right|_{\mathbb{D}}=\left|\nu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\nu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}\right|_{\mathbb{D}}=\left|\nu_{1}\right|\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left|\nu_{2}\right|\mathbf{e}_{2}\in\mathbb{D}^{+}

where
\left|\nu_{1}\right|
and
\left|\nu_{2}\right|

are the usual modulus of real numbers.

Definition 2

A subset
A
of
\mathbb{D}
is said to be
\mathbb{D}-
bounded if there
exists
M\in\mathbb{D}^{+}
such that
\left|\zeta\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\preceq_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}M
for any
\zeta\in A.

Set

\displaystyle A_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\text{ }y\in\mathbb{R},\text{ }x\mathbf{e}_{1}+y\mathbf{e}_{2}\in A\right\},

\displaystyle A_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}:\exists\text{ }x\in\mathbb{R},\text{ }x\mathbf{e}_{1}+y\mathbf{e}_{2}\in A\right\}.

If
A
is
\mathbb{D}-
bounded then
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are bounded subset
of
\mathbb{R}
.

Definition 3

For a
\mathbb{D}-
bounded subset
A
of
\mathbb{D},
the
supremum

of
A
with respect to the
\mathbb{D}-
modulus is defined by

\sup\nolimits_{\mathbb{D}}A=\sup A_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\sup A_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Definition 4

A sequence of hyperbolic numbers
\left\{\zeta_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is
said to be
convergent to
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}
if for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{D}^{+}\backslash\left\{0\right\}
there exists
k\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|\zeta_{n}-\zeta\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\prec_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\varepsilon.

Then we write

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\zeta_{n}=\zeta.

Definition 5

A sequence of hyperbolic numbers
\left\{\zeta_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is
said to be
\mathbb{D}-
Cauchy sequence
\zeta\in\mathbb{D}
if
for
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{D}^{+}\backslash\left\{0\right\}

\exists

N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|\zeta_{N+m}-\zeta_{N}\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\prec_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\varepsilon

for all
m=1,2,3,...
.

Note that a sequence of hyperbolic numbers
\left\{\zeta_{n}\right\}_{n\geq 1}
is
convergent
if and only if it is a
\mathbb{D}-
Cauchy sequence.

Definition 6

A hyperbolic series
\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{n}
is
convergent
if and only if its partial sums is a
\mathbb{D}-
Cauchy
sequence, i.e.
,
for any
\varepsilon\in\mathbb{D}^{+}\backslash\left\{0\right\}

\exists

N\in\mathbb{N}
such that

\left|\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{k=1}^{m}\zeta_{N+k}\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\prec_{{}_{\mathbb{D}}}\varepsilon

for any
m\in\mathbb{N}.

Definition 7

A hyperbolic series
\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\zeta_{n}
is
\mathbb{D}-
absolutely convergent
if the series
\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{n=1}^{\infty}\left|\zeta_{n}\right|_{\mathbb{D}}
is convergent.

Every
\mathbb{D}-
absolutely convergent series is convergent.

3
Main Results

In this section we have established our main results. We have arranged these
in three subsections. In the first subsection we have proved the bicomplex
version of Lebesgue’s dominated convergence theorem. In the second
subsection we have focussed on the bicomplex version of
Lebesgue-Radon-Nikodym theorem and also we have established bicomplex
version of Hahn decomposition theorem. Finally in the last subsection we
have introduced the idea of hyperbolic version of invariant measure.

3.1
Bicomplex Version of Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem

Definition 8

[
2
]
Let
X
be a measurable space then the bicomplex valued function
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
is called
\mathbb{T}-
measurable
on
X
if
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are complex measurable functions on
X.
In
particular if
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are real measurable functions on
X
then
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
is called
\mathbb{D}-
measurable
function on
X.

For a bicomplex measurable function
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
one can easily check that
\left|f\right|=\left|f_{1}\right|\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left|f_{2}\right|\mathbf{e}_{2}

is
\mathbb{D}-
measurable. Also for two
\mathbb{T}-
measurable functions
f
and
g
it is routine check up that
f+g
and
fg
are also
\mathbb{T}-
measurable functions.

Theorem 1

If
f
is a
\mathbb{T}-
measurable function on a measurable space
X
then
there is a
\mathbb{T}-
measurable function
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
such that
\left|\alpha_{1}\right|=1,\left|\alpha_{2}\right|=1
and
f=\alpha\left|f\right|.

Proof.
Let
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Since
f
is a
\mathbb{T}-
measurable function on a measurable space
X,

f_{1}
and
f_{2}
are complex measurable functions on
X.

So there exist complex measurable functions
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2}
such
that
\left|\alpha_{1}\right|=1,\left|\alpha_{2}\right|=1
and
f_{1}=\alpha_{1}\left|f_{1}\right|,f_{2}=\alpha_{2}\left|f_{2}\right|.

Set
\alpha=\alpha_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.
Obviously

\alpha
is a
\mathbb{T}-
measurable function on
X
and the result
follows.

Definition 9

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a
\sigma-
algebra in a set
X.
A bicomplex function

\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
defined on
X
is
called a
\mathbb{T}-
measure on
\mathfrak{M}
if
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
are complex measures on
\mathfrak{M.}
In particular if
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
are positive measures on
\mathfrak{M}
i.e range of
both
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
are
\left[0,\infty\right]
then
\mu

is called a
\mathbb{D}-
measure on
\mathfrak{M}
and if
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
are real measures on
\mathfrak{M}
i.e range of both
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
are
[0,\infty)
then
\mu
is called a
\mathbb{D}^{+}-
measure on
\mathfrak{M.}

Definition 10

Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
be a
\mathbb{D}-
measure on an arbitrary measurable space
X.
We say
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
to be bicomplex Lebesgue integrable function on
X

if

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{1}\right|d\mu_{1}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty,

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{2}\right|d\mu_{2}

\displaystyle<

\displaystyle\infty

i.e.,
f_{i}\in L^{1}\left(\mu_{i}\right),
the set of all complex
Lebesgue integrable functions with respect to
\mu_{i}
for
i=1,2.

In that case we write

\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}fd\mu=\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{1}d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{2}d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}.

We define
L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right)
to be the collection of
all bicomplex Lebesgue integrable functions on
X.

Theorem 2

Let
f,g\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right)
and
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{C}.
Then
\alpha f+\beta g\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right),
and

\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left(\alpha f+\beta g\right)d\mu=\alpha\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}fd\mu+\beta\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}gd\mu.

Proof.
Let
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},g=g_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+g_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},\alpha=\alpha_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\alpha_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},\beta=\beta_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\beta_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Then

\alpha f+\beta g=\left(\alpha_{1}f_{1}+\beta_{1}g_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\alpha_{2}f_{2}+\beta_{2}g_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Since
f,g\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right)
we have
\alpha_{1}f_{1}+\beta_{1}g_{1}\in L^{1}\left(\mu_{1}\right)
and
\alpha_{2}f_{2}+\beta_{2}g_{2}\in L^{1}\left(\mu_{2}\right)
and therefore
\alpha f+\beta g\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right).

The last part follows from the facts

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left(\alpha_{1}f_{1}+\beta_{1}g_{1}\right)d\mu_{1}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{1}d\mu_{1}+\beta_{1}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}g_{1}d\mu_{1},

\displaystyle\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left(\alpha_{2}f_{2}+\beta_{2}g_{2}\right)d\mu_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha_{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{2}d\mu_{2}+\beta_{2}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}g_{2}d\mu_{2},

and

\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left(\alpha f+\beta g\right)d\mu=\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left(\alpha_{1}f_{1}+\beta_{1}g_{1}\right)d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left(\alpha_{2}f_{2}+\beta_{2}g_{2}\right)d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Theorem 3

If
f\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right),
then

\left|\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}fd\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\preceq_{\mathbb{D}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f\right|_{\mathbb{D}}d\mu.

Proof.
Let
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Since
f\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right),
we have
f_{1}\in L^{1}\left(\mu_{1}\right)
and
f_{2}\in L^{1}\left(\mu_{2}\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}fd\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{1}d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{2}d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}\right|_{\mathbb{D}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left|\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{1}d\mu_{1}\right|\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left|\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{2}d\mu_{2}\right|\mathbf{e}_{2}\preceq_{\mathbb{D}}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{1}\right|d\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{2}\right|d\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}=\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f\right|_{\mathbb{D}}d\mu.

Theorem 4
(Lebesgue’s Dominated Convergence Theorem)

Let
\left\{f_{n}=f_{n1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{n2}\mathbf{e}_{2}\right\}
be a
sequence of
\mathbb{T}-
measurable functions on
X
such that

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{n}\left(x\right)=f\left(x\right)

exists for all
x\in X.
If there exists
g=g_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+g_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right)
such that

\left|f_{ni}\left(x\right)\right|\leq g_{i}\left(x\right)

for all
n=1,2,3,...

;

i=1,2;

x\in X,
then
f\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right),

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{n}\left(x\right)-f\left(x\right)\right|d\mu=0,

and

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{n}\left(x\right)d\mu=f\left(x\right).

Proof.
Since
\left\{f_{n}\right\}
is a sequence of
\mathbb{T}-
measurable
functions on
X,
both
\left\{f_{n1}\right\}

and
\left\{f_{n2}\right\}
are sequences of complex measurable functions on
X.

Let
f=f_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2},\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Thus

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}f_{ni}\left(x\right)=f_{i}\left(x\right)

for
i=1,2.

Now since

\left|f_{ni}\left(x\right)\right|\leq g_{i}\left(x\right)

for all
n=1,2,3,...

;

i=1,2;

x\in X,
we get
f_{i}\in L^{1}\left(\mu_{i}\right)
for
i=1,2
and therefore
f\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right).

Also,

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{ni}\left(x\right)-f_{i}\left(x\right)\right|d\mu_{i}=0,

and

\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{n_{i}}\left(x\right)d\mu_{i}=f_{i}\left(x\right)

for
i=1,2.
Hence

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{n}\left(x\right)-f\left(x\right)\right|_{\mathbb{D}}d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{n1}\left(x\right)-f_{1}\left(x\right)\right|d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}\left|f_{n2}\left(x\right)-f_{2}\left(x\right)\right|d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 0

and

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{n}\left(x\right)d\mu

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{n1}\left(x\right)d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left(\mathop{\displaystyle\int}\limits_{X}f_{n2}\left(x\right)d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{1}\left(x\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+f_{2}\left(x\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f\left(x\right).

3.2
Bicomplex Version of Lebesgue-Radon-Nikodym Theorem

Let
\mathfrak{M}
be a measure space and
E\in\mathfrak{M}.
Let
P=\left\{E_{k}\right\}
be a partition of
E.
Then for all
E\in\mathfrak{M}
the
\mathbb{T}-
measure
\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
on
\mathfrak{M}
satisfies

\mu\left(E\right)=\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\mu\left(E_{k}\right)

for every partition
\left\{E_{k}\right\}
of
E.

Let
\lambda=\lambda_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\lambda_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
be a
\mathbb{D}-
measure on
\mathfrak{M}.
We say that
\lambda
dominates
\mu
on
\mathfrak{M}
if
\left|\mu_{i}\left(E\right)\right|\leq\lambda_{i}\left(E\right)
for all
E\in\mathfrak{M}
and for
i=1,2.

The
\mathbb{D}-
modulus of
\mu,
denoted by
\left|\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}},
is defined on
\mathfrak{M}
by

\left|\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\left(E\right)=\sup_{P}\mathop{\displaystyle\sum}\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\left|\mu\left(E_{k}\right)\right|_{\mathbb{D}}

for all
E\in\mathfrak{M}.

Theorem 5

For a
\mathbb{T}-
measure
\mu
on
\mathfrak{M},

\left|\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}
is a
\mathbb{D}-
measure on
\mathfrak{M}.

Proof.
Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Since
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
being complex measures on
\mathfrak{M},

\left|\mu_{1}\right|

and
\left|\mu_{2}\right|
are positive measures on
\mathfrak{M}.

Hence
\left|\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}=\left|\mu_{1}\right|\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left|\mu_{2}\right|\mathbf{e}_{2}\,
is a
\mathbb{D}-
measure on
\mathfrak{M}.

Theorem 6

For a
\mathbb{T}-
measure
\mu
on
X,

\left|\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\left(X\right)\prec_{\mathbb{D}}\infty_{\mathbb{D}}.

Proof.
Let
\mu=\mu_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}.

Since
\mu_{1}\mathbf{,}\mu_{2}
being complex measures on
\mathfrak{M},

\left|\mu_{i}\right|\left(X\right)<\infty

for
i=1,2.

Hence

\left|\mu\right|_{\mathbb{D}}\left(X\right)=\left|\mu_{1}\right|\left(X\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left|\mu_{2}\right|\left(X\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}\prec_{\mathbb{D}}\infty_{\mathbb{D}}.

Let
\mu,\lambda
be two
\mathbb{T}-
measures on
\mathfrak{M}
and
c\in\mathbb{D}.
For all
E\in\mathfrak{M}
define

\displaystyle\left(\mu+\lambda\right)\left(E\right)

\displaystyle=

Definition 14
(Push-forward of measures)

Let

be the map from the space of
\mathbb{D}-
probability measures to
itself, defined by

For any

and any

We now prove some properties of the map

Lemma 1

For all
\varphi\in L_{\mathbb{T}}^{1}\left(\mu\right)
we
have
\int\varphi d(f_{\ast}\mu)=\int\varphi\circ fd\mu.

Proof.
First let
\varphi=\chi_{A}
be the characteristic function of some

Then

\displaystyle\int\chi_{A}d(f_{\ast}\mu)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle f_{\ast}\mu(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu\left(f^{-1}\left(A\right)\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu_{1}\left(f^{-1}\left(A\right)\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\mu_{2}\left(f^{-1}\left(A\right)\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\left(\int\chi_{f^{-1}(A)}d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\int\chi_{f^{-1}(A)}d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int(\chi_{A}\circ f)d\mu.

So, the statement is true for characteristic functions and thus for simple
functions. Hence the statement is true for general integrable functions by
standard approximation arguments.

Corollary 1

f_{\ast}:\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\longrightarrow\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}

is continuous.

Proof.
Consider a sequence
\mu_{n}\longrightarrow\mu
in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}
. Then, by Lemma
1
, for any continuous function
\varphi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}
we have

\int\varphi d(f_{\ast}\mu_{n})=\int\varphi\circ fd\mu_{n}\longrightarrow\int\varphi\circ fd\mu=\int\varphi d(f_{\ast}\mu)

which implies
f_{\ast}\mu_{n}\longrightarrow f_{\ast}\mu.
Hence
f_{\ast}
is continuous.

Corollary 2

\mu
is
\mathbb{D-}
invariant with respect to a measurable
function
f:X\longrightarrow X
if and only if
\int\varphi\circ fd\mu=\int\varphi d\mu
for all continuous function
\varphi:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{D}
.

Proof.

\varphi=\varphi_{1}\mathbf{e}_{1}+\varphi_{2}\mathbf{e}_{2}
is
continuous
\Longleftrightarrow\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
are
continuous.

Suppose first that
\mu
is
\mathbb{D-}
invariant. Then
f_{\ast}\mu=\mu.
Now using Lemma
\ref{L1},
we have

\int\varphi\circ fd\mu=\int\varphi df_{\ast}\mu=\int\varphi d\mu.

For the converse, we have

\int\varphi d\mu=\int\varphi\circ fd\mu=\int\varphi d(f_{\ast}\mu).

which implies

\left(\int\varphi_{1}d\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\int\varphi_{2}d\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}=\left(\int\varphi_{1}df_{\ast}\mu_{1}\right)\mathbf{e}_{1}+\left(\int\varphi_{2}df_{\ast}\mu_{2}\right)\mathbf{e}_{2}.

So,

\int\varphi_{1}d\mu_{1}=\int\varphi_{1}df_{\ast}\mu_{1}

and

\int\varphi_{2}d\mu_{2}=\int\varphi_{2}df_{\ast}\mu_{2}

for every continuous function
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}.

By the Riesz Representation Theorem, measures correspond to linear
functionals and therefore this can be restated as saying that

\mu_{1}(\varphi_{1})=f_{\ast}\mu_{1}(\varphi_{1})

and

\mu_{2}(\varphi_{2})=f_{\ast}\mu_{2}(\varphi_{2})

for all continuous function
\varphi_{1},\varphi_{2}:X\longrightarrow\mathbb{R}.

Hence

\mu_{1}=f_{\ast}\mu_{1}\text{ and }\mu_{2}=f_{\ast}\mu_{2}

and so

f_{\ast}\mu=\mu.

Therefore
\mu
is
\mathbb{D-}
invariant.

We now prove a general result which gives conditions to guarantee that
atleast some
\mathbb{D-}
invariant
\mathbb{D}-
probability measure exists.

Let

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right)=\{\mu\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\text{: }\mu\text{ is }\mathbb{D-}\text{invariant with respect to }f\text{\lx@text@rbrace.}

Theorem 13

Suppose
M
is compact metric space and
f:M\longrightarrow M
is
continuous. Then
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right)
is non-empty,
convex, compact.

Proof.
Krylov-Boguliobov Theorem
[
7
]
states that if
M
is compact metric
space and
f:M\longrightarrow M
is continuous, then the set
\{\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}{}^{\ast}
:
\mu^{\ast}
is
f-
invariant
}
is non-empty,
where
\mu^{\ast}
is probability measure and
\mathcal{M}{}^{\ast}
is
the space of probability measures.

It is clear that

\{\mu^{\ast}\in\mathcal{M}{}^{\ast}:\mu^{\ast}\text{ is }f-\text{
invariant}\}\subseteq\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right).

Hence
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right)
is non-empty.

Now let
\mu^{1},\mu^{2}\in\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right)
.
Then
\mu^{1}(f^{-1}(A))=\mu^{1}(A)
and
\mu^{2}(f^{-1}(A))=\mu^{2}(A),

for all
A\in\mathfrak{B}.

Now for
t\in[0,1],
let
\mu=t\mu^{1}+(1-t)\mu^{2}.

Then

\displaystyle\mu(f^{-1}(A))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\mu^{1}(f^{-1}(A))+(1-t)\mu^{2}(f^{-1}(A))

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle t\mu^{1}(A)+(1-t)\mu^{2}(A)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mu(A).

Hence
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right)
is convex.

To show compactness, suppose that
\mu_{n}
is a sequence in
\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}\left(f\right)
converging to some
\mu\in

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}
. Then by Lemma
1
we have, for any continuous function
\varphi,
that
\int\varphi\circ fd\mu=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi\circ fd\mu_{n}=\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}\int\varphi d\mu_{n}=\int\varphi d\mu.
Therefore by Corollary
2
,
\mu
is
\mathbb{D-}
invariant and so
\mu\in

\mathcal{M}_{\mathbb{D}}(f)
.
