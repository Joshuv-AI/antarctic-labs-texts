Flat Metrics with a Prescribed Derived Coframing
Robert L. BRYANT † and Jeanne N. CLELLAND ‡

Abstract
The following problem is addressed: A 3 3 -manifold M M is endowed with a triple Ω = ( Ω 1 , Ω 2 , Ω 3 ) \Omega=\big(\Omega^{1},\Omega^{2},\Omega^{3}\big) of closed 2 2 -forms. One wants to construct a coframing ω = ( ω 1 , ω 2 , ω 3 ) \omega=\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big) of M M such that, first, d ​ ω i = Ω i {\rm d}\omega^{i}=\Omega^{i} for i = 1 , 2 , 3 i=1,2,3 , and, second, the Riemannian metric g = ( ω 1 ) 2 + ( ω 2 ) 2 + ( ω 3 ) 2 g=\big(\omega^{1}\big)^{2}+\big(\omega^{2}\big)^{2}+\big(\omega^{3}\big)^{2} be flat. We show that, in the ‘nonsingular case’, i.e., when the three 2 2 -forms Ω p i \Omega^{i}_{p} span at least a 2 2 -dimensional subspace of Λ 2 ​ ( T p ∗ ​ M ) \Lambda^{2}(T^{*}_{p}M) and are real-analytic in some p p -centered coordinates, this problem is always solvable on a neighborhood of p ∈ M p\in M , with the general solution ω \omega depending on three arbitrary functions of two variables. Moreover, the characteristic variety of the generic solution ω \omega can be taken to be a nonsingular cubic. Some singular situations are considered as well. In particular, we show that the problem is solvable locally when Ω 1 \Omega^{1} , Ω 2 \Omega^{2} , Ω 3 \Omega^{3} are scalar multiples of a single 2-form that do not vanish simultaneously and satisfy a nondegeneracy condition. We also show by example that solutions may fail to exist when these conditions are not satisfied.

Introduction

1.1
The problem

Given a
3
-manifold
M

and a triple
\Omega=\big(\Omega^{1},\Omega^{2},\Omega^{3}\big)

of closed
2
-forms on
M
,
it is desired to find a coframing
\omega=\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big)

(i.e., a triple of linearly independent
1
-forms)
satisfying the first-order differential equations

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{i}=\Omega^{i}

(1.1)

and the second-order equations that ensure that the metric

\displaystyle g=\big(\omega^{1}\big)^{2}+\big(\omega^{2}\big)^{2}+\big(\omega^{3}\big)^{2}

(1.2)

be flat.

This question was originally posed in the context of a problem regarding ‘residual stress’ in elastic bodies due to defects, where the existence of solutions to equations (
1.1
) and (
1.2
) is related to the existence of residually stressed bodies that also satisfy a global energy minimization condition. (See
[
1
]
for more details.) However, we feel that the problem is of independent geometric interest.

1.2
Initial discussion

As posed, this problem becomes an overdetermined system
of equations for the coframing
\omega
,
which, in local coordinates
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)
, can be specified
by choosing the
9
coefficient functions
a^{i}_{j}(u)

in the expansion
\omega^{i}=a^{i}_{j}(u)\mathrm{d}u^{j}
.
Indeed, (
1.1
)
is a system of
9
first-order equations
while the flatness of the metric
g

as defined in (
1.2
)
is the system of
6
second-order equations
\operatorname{Ric}(g)=0
.
Together, these constitute a system of
15

partial differential equations on the coefficients
a^{i}_{j}

that are independent in the sense that no one of them is a combination of derivatives of the others.

However, the problem can be recast into a different form that makes it more tractable. For simplicity, we will assume that
M
is connected and simply-connected. The condition that the
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued
1
-form
\omega
define a flat metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is then well-known to be equivalent to the condition that
\omega

be representable as

\displaystyle\omega=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x},

where
\mathbf{x}\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
is an immersion and
\mathbf{a}\colon M\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
is a smooth mapping.
1
1

1

In this note, we regard
\mathbb{R}^{3}
as
columns

of real numbers of height
3
, though we will, from time to time, without comment, write them as row vectors in the text.

This representation is unique up to a replacement of the form

\displaystyle(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})\mapsto(\mathbf{x}^{\prime},\mathbf{a}^{\prime})=(R\mathbf{x}+T,R\mathbf{a}),

where
T\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
is a constant and
R\in\operatorname{SO}(3)
is a constant.

Since
\operatorname{SO}(3)
has dimension
3
, specifying a pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\operatorname{SO}(3)
is, locally, a choice of
6
arbitrary (smooth) functions on
M
.
The remaining conditions on
\omega
needed to solve our problem,

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\big(\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\big)=-\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{a}\wedge\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\Omega,

(1.3)

still constitute
9
independent first-order equations
for the ‘unknowns’
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})
(which are essentially
6
in number),
but these equations are not fully independent:

\mathrm{d}\Omega=0
by hypothesis,
and the exterior derivatives of the three
2
-forms
on the left hand side of (
1.3
)
also vanish identically for any pair
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})
,
which provides
3
‘compatibility conditions’ for the
9
equations,
thereby, at least formally,
restoring the ‘balance’ of
6
equations for
6
unknowns.
Thus, this rough count gives some indication that the problem might
be locally solvable.

However, caution is warranted.
Let
(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{a}})\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\operatorname{SO}(3)

be a smooth mapping and let
\bar{\Omega}=\mathrm{d}\big(\bar{\mathbf{a}}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{x}}\big)
.
Linearizing the equations (
1.3
)
at the ‘solution’
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})=(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{a}})

yields a system of differential equations of the form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\big(\bar{\mathbf{a}}^{-1}(\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}-\mathbf{b}\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{x}})\big)=\Psi,

(1.4)

where
(\mathbf{y},\mathbf{b})\colon M\to\mathbb{R}^{3}\oplus\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(3)
are unknowns
and
\Psi
is a closed
2
-form with values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
If one were expecting (
1.3
) to always be solvable,
one might naïvely expect (
1.4
) to always
be solvable as well, but this is not so:
When one linearizes at
(\bar{\mathbf{x}},\bar{\mathbf{a}})=(\bar{\mathbf{x}},I_{3})
,
the linearized system reduces to

\displaystyle-\mathrm{d}\mathbf{b}\wedge\mathrm{d}\bar{\mathbf{x}}=\Psi,

(1.5)

where
\mathbf{b}\colon M\to\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(3)\simeq\mathbb{R}^{3}

is essentially a set of
3
unknowns
and
\Psi
is a given closed
2
-form with values in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
However, as is easily seen, the solvability of (
1.5
)
for
\mathbf{b}
imposes a system of
9
independent first-order
linear equations on
\Psi
, while the closure of
\Psi

is only a subsystem of
3
independent first-order linear equations on
\Psi
.

Thus, some care needs to be taken in analyzing the system.
Indeed, as Example
4.1
in Section
4
shows,
there exists an
\Omega
defined on a neighborhood of the origin in
\mathbb{R}^{3}

for which there is no solution
\omega=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

to the system (
1.3
) on an open neighborhood of the origin.

1.3
An exterior differential system

The above observation suggests formulating the problem
as an exterior differential system
{\mathcal{I}}

on
X=M\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\operatorname{SO}(3)

that is generated by the three
2
-form components
of the closed
2
-form

\displaystyle\Theta=-\bm{\EuRoman{a}}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\EuRoman{a}}\wedge\bm{\EuRoman{a}}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\bm{\EuRoman{x}}-\Omega,

(1.6)

where now, one regards
\bm{\EuRoman{x}}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
and
\bm{\EuRoman{a}}\colon X\to\operatorname{SO}(3)

as projections on the second and third factors.
2
2

2

We use a different font in equation (
1.6
) to emphasize that
\bm{\EuRoman{a}}
,
\bm{\EuRoman{x}}
, etc., denote matrix- and vector-valued coordinate functions on
X
, while
\mathbf{a}
,
\mathbf{x}
, etc., denote matrix- and vector-valued functions on
M
. We use
\Omega
to denote both the 2-form on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
and its pullback to
X
via the projection map
\bm{\EuRoman{x}}\colon X\to\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

We will show that, when
\Omega
is suitably nondegenerate,
this exterior differential system is involutive, i.e.,
it possesses Cartan-regular integral flags at every point.
In particular, if
\Omega
is also real-analytic,
the Cartan–Kähler theorem will imply that the original problem
is locally solvable.

2.2
Formulation as an exterior differential systems problem

Now suppose that, on
M^{3}
, there is specified an
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued, closed
2
-form
\Omega=\big(\Omega^{i}\big)
. Choose an
\mathbb{R}^{3}
-valued coframing
\eta=(\eta^{i})\colon TM\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
.
Then one can write

\displaystyle\Omega=\tfrac{1}{2}Z[\eta]\wedge\eta,

where
Z
is a function on
M
with values in
3
-by-
3

Let
{\mathcal{I}}
be the exterior differential system on
X^{9}=M\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\operatorname{SO}(3)
that is generated by the three components of the closed
2
-form

\displaystyle\Theta=\mathrm{d}\xi-\Omega=-[\alpha]\wedge\xi-\tfrac{1}{2}Z[\eta]\wedge\eta.

Proposition 2.1
.

If
N^{3}\subset X
is an integral manifold of
{\mathcal{I}}
to which
\eta
and
\xi
pull back to be coframings, then each point of
N^{3}
has an open neighborhood that can be written as a graph

\displaystyle\bigl\{\bigl(p,\mathbf{x}(p),\mathbf{a}(p)\bigr)\,\vrule\,p\in U\bigr\}\subset X

(2.4)

for some open set
U\subset M
and smooth maps
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to\mathbb{R}^{3}

and
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
. Moreover, on
U
,
the coframing
\omega=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
satisfies
\mathrm{d}\omega=\Omega

and the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega={}^{t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\circ\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
is flat.

Conversely, if
U\subset M
is a simply-connected open subset on which there exists a coframing
\omega\colon TU\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
satisfying
(i)

\mathrm{d}\omega=\Omega
, and
(ii)
the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
be flat, then there exist mappings
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
such that
\omega=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
. Moreover, the immersion
\iota\colon U\to X
defined by
\iota(p)=\bigl(p,\mathbf{x}(p),\mathbf{a}(p)\bigr)
is an integral manifold of
{\mathcal{I}}
that pulls
\eta
and
\xi
back to be coframings of
U
.

Proof.

The statements in the first paragraph of the proposition are proved by simply unwinding the definitions and can be left to the reader.

For the converse statements (i.e., the second paragraph), suppose that a coframing
\omega\colon TU\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be given satisfying the two conditions. By the fundamental lemma of Riemannian geometry, there exists a unique
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued
1
-form
\phi\colon TU\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
such that

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega=-[\phi]\wedge\omega.

The condition that the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
be flat is then the condition that
\mathrm{d}\phi=-\tfrac{1}{2}[\phi]\wedge\phi
. These equations for the exterior derivatives of
\omega
and
\phi
, together with the simple-connectivity of
U
, imply that there exist maps
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
such that

\displaystyle\omega=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\qquad\text{and}\qquad[\phi]=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{a}.

(2.5)

Consequently,
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is equal to
{}^{t}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\circ\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
, which is flat, by definition. Finally, since
\mathrm{d}\omega=\Omega
, it follows that the graph manifold
N^{3}\subset X
defined by (
2.4
) is an integral manifold of
{\mathcal{I}}
. Moreover, since, by construction,

\displaystyle(\mathrm{id}_{U},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})^{*}(\xi)=\omega,

it follows that
\xi
and
\eta
pull back to
N^{3}
to be coframings on
N^{3}
.
∎

2.3
Integral elements

By Proposition
2.1
,
proving existence of local solutions of our problem
is equivalent to proving the existence of integral manifolds of
{\mathcal{I}}

to which
\xi
and
\eta
pull back to be coframings. (This latter
condition is usually referred to as an ‘independence condition’.)

The first step in this approach
is to understand the nature of the integral elements of
{\mathcal{I}}
,
i.e., the candidates for tangent spaces to the integral manifolds of
{\mathcal{I}}
.

A (necessarily
3
-dimensional) integral element
E\in\mathrm{Gr}(3,TX)

of
{\mathcal{I}}
will be said to be
admissible

if both
\xi\colon E\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\eta\colon E\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
are isomorphisms.

Proposition 2.3
.

All of the admissible integral elements of
{\mathcal{I}}
are Kähler-ordinary.
3
3

3

For definitions of Kähler-ordinary, Cartan-ordinary, etc.,
see
[
2
, Chapter III, Definition 1.7]
.

The set
{\mathcal{V}}_{3}\bigl({\mathcal{I}},(\xi,\eta)\bigr)

consisting of admissible integral elements of
{\mathcal{I}}

is a submanifold of
\mathrm{Gr}(3,TX)
,
and the basepoint projection
{\mathcal{V}}_{3}\bigl({\mathcal{I}},(\xi,\eta)\bigr)\to X

is a surjective submersion
with all fibers diffeomorphic to
\operatorname{GL}(3,{\mathbb{R}})
.

Proof.

Let
(p,\bm{\EuRoman{x}},\bm{\EuRoman{a}})\in X=M\times{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\times\operatorname{SO}(3)
, and
let
E\subset T_{(p,\bm{\EuRoman{x}},\bm{\EuRoman{a}})}X
be a

-dimensional integral element of
{\mathcal{I}}

to which both

and
\eta
pull back to give an isomorphism of

with

. Then there will exist a

and a
3
-by-
3

with real entries
such that

is defined as the kernel of the surjective linear mapping

\displaystyle(\xi{-}P\eta,\alpha{-}QP\eta)\colon\ T_{(p,\bm{\EuRoman{x}},\bm{\EuRoman{a}})}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\oplus{\mathbb{R}}^{3}.

(2.6)

To simplify the notation, set
\bar{\eta}=E^{*}\eta
.
Then,
E^{*}\xi=P\bar{\eta}
and

.
The
2
-form
\Theta
, which vanishes

\displaystyle 0=E^{*}\Theta=-[QP\bar{\eta}]\wedge P\bar{\eta}-\tfrac{1}{2}Z(p)[\bar{\eta}]\wedge\bar{\eta}

\displaystyle\hphantom{0 = E^*\Theta}{}=-\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(\bigl((\tr Q)I_{3}-{}^{t}\!Q\bigr)\det(P)\big({}^{t}\!P\big)^{-1}+Z(p)\bigr)[\bar{\eta}]\wedge\bar{\eta}.

2.4
Polar spaces and Cartan-regularity

In order to be able to apply the Cartan–Kähler theorem to prove existence of solutions in the real-analytic category, one needs a stronger result than Proposition
2.3
; one needs to show that there are
Cartan
-ordinary admissible integral elements, in other words, to establish the existence of ordinary
flags
terminating in elements of
{\mathcal{V}}_{3}\bigl({\mathcal{I}},(\xi,\eta)\bigr)

{\mathcal{I}}

Let

is given by

\displaystyle H(E_{i})=\big\{\mathbf{v}\in T_{(p,\bm{\EuRoman{x}},\bm{\EuRoman{a}})}X\,\vrule\,\big([\alpha_{E}(\mathbf{v})]+[\xi_{E}(\mathbf{v})]Q\big)w_{j}=0,\ j\leq i\big\}.

Consequently, the codimension
c_{i}
of this polar space
satisfies
c_{i}\leq 3i
for
0\leq i\leq 3
. Since the codimension
of
{\mathcal{V}}_{3}\bigl({\mathcal{I}},(\xi,\eta)\bigr)
in
\mathrm{Gr}(3,TX)
is
9
,
which is always greater than or equal to
c_{0}+c_{1}+c_{2}
,
it follows, by Cartan’s test, that the flag

(E_{0}\subset E_{1}\subset E_{2}\subset E_{3})

will be Cartan-ordinary if and only if
c_{0}+c_{1}+c_{2}=9
,
i.e.,
c_{i}=3i
for
i=0,1,2
.
Moreover, this holds if and only if
c_{2}=6
.

Whether or not there is a
2
-plane
E_{2}\subset E
with
c_{2}=6

evidently depends on
Q
(which is determined by
E
).

Example 2.4
.

Suppose that
E
satisfies
Q=0
, which, by (
2.7
),
is the case for all of the admissible integral elements based at
(p,\bm{\EuRoman{x}},\bm{\EuRoman{a}})

if
Z(p)=0
.
In this case, it is clear that
\left[\alpha_{E}\right]+\left[\xi_{E}\right]Q=\left[\alpha_{E}\right]
takes values in skew-symmetric
3
-by-
3

matrices and hence that, for every
2
-plane
E_{2}\subset E
,
one must have
H(E_{2})=\ker\alpha_{E}
, so that
c_{2}=3
.
Thus, Cartan’s inequality is strict,
and the integral element
E
is not Cartan-ordinary.

Note, though, that this does not imply that there are no solutions
to the original problem on domains containing
p
when
Z(p)=0
;
it’s just that Cartan–Kähler cannot immediately be applied
in such situations. For example, note that,
when
\Omega
vanishes identically (equivalently,
Z
vanishes identically),
then all of the admissible integral elements of
{\mathcal{I}}

are contained in the integrable
6
-plane field
\alpha=0
,
and, indeed, the general solution
\omega
is of the form
\omega=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

where
\mathbf{x}\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is any immersion.

For any
3
-by-
3
matrix
Q
, define
A_{Q}\subset\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,{\mathbb{R}})=\Hom\big({\mathbb{R}}^{3},{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\big)
, the
tableau
of
Q
,
to be the span of the
3
-by-
3
matrices

\displaystyle[\mathbf{x}]+[\mathbf{y}]Q

for
\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.
The dimension of the vector space
A_{Q}
lies between
3
and
6
.

It is evident that the polar equations of flags
in a given admissible integral element
E

defined by (
2.8
) are governed
by the properties of the tableau
A_{Q}
.

To simplify the study of
A_{Q}
, it is useful to note that
it has a built-in equivariance:
For
R\in\operatorname{SO}(3)
, one has

\displaystyle R\bigl([\mathbf{x}]+[\mathbf{y}]Q\bigr)R^{-1}=R[\mathbf{x}]R^{-1}+R[\mathbf{y}]R^{-1}\big(RQR^{-1}\big)=[R\mathbf{x}]+[R\mathbf{y}]RQR^{-1}.

Hence,

\displaystyle RA_{Q}R^{-1}=A_{RQR^{-1}}.

In particular, properties of
A_{Q}
such as its dimension, character sequence, and involutivity depend only on the equivalence class of the matrix
Q
under the action of conjugation by
\operatorname{SO}(3)
. Also, writing
Q=qI_{3}+Q_{0}
where
\tr(Q_{0})=0
, one has

\displaystyle[\mathbf{x}]+[\mathbf{y}]Q=[\mathbf{x}+q\mathbf{y}]+[\mathbf{y}]Q_{0}.

Thus,

\displaystyle A_{Q}=A_{Q_{0}}.

Proposition 2.5
.

The tableau
A_{Q}\subset\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,{\mathbb{R}})=\Hom\big({\mathbb{R}}^{3},{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\big)

has dimension
6
and is involutive
with characters
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=(3,3,0)
,
except when the trace-free part of
Q
is conjugate by
\operatorname{SO}(3)

to a matrix of the form

\displaystyle Q_{0}\simeq\begin{pmatrix}-2x&0&0\\
0&x+3r&3y\\
0&-3y&x-3r\end{pmatrix},

(2.9)

where
(x,y,r)
are real numbers satisfying either
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}

or
r=y=0
.

Proof.

The proof is basically a computation. The conjugation action of
\operatorname{SO}(3)

on
3
-by-
3
matrices preserves the splitting of
\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,{\mathbb{R}})

into three pieces: The multiples of the identity (of dimension
1
),
the subalgebra
\operatorname{\mathfrak{so}}(3)
(of dimension
3
),
and the traceless symmetric matrices (of dimension
5
).
Moreover, as is well-known, a symmetric
3
-by-
3
matrix can be
diagonalized by conjugating with an orthogonal matrix. Thus, one
is reduced to studying the case in which
Q_{0}
is written in the form

\displaystyle Q_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}q_{1}&\phantom{-}p_{3}&-p_{2}\\
-p_{3}&\phantom{-}q_{2}&\phantom{-}p_{1}\\
\phantom{-}p_{2}&-p_{1}&\phantom{-}q_{3}\end{pmatrix},

(2.10)

where
q_{1}+q_{2}+q_{3}=0
.

It is now a straightforward (if somewhat tedious) matter
(which can be eased by MAPLE) to check that, when
A_{Q_{0}}

has dimension less than
6
(the maximum possible),
two of the
p_{i}
must vanish. Thus, after conjugating by
a signed permutation matrix that lies in
\operatorname{SO}(3)
,
one can assume that
p_{2}=p_{3}=0
.
With this simplification,
A_{Q_{0}}
is seen to have dimension
less than
6
if and only if

\displaystyle p_{1}\big({p_{1}}^{2}+2{q_{2}}^{2}+5q_{2}q_{3}+2{q_{3}}^{2}\big)=(q_{2}-q_{3})\big({p_{1}}^{2}+2{q_{2}}^{2}+5q_{2}q_{3}+2{q_{3}}^{2}\big)=0.

Thus, either
{p_{1}}^{2}+2{q_{2}}^{2}+5q_{2}q_{3}+2{q_{3}}^{2}=0
or
p_{1}=q_{2}-q_{3}=0
.
Making the necessary changes of basis,
these two cases give the two non-involutive normal forms
in (
2.9
).

It remains to show that, when
A_{Q}
has dimension
6
,
it actually is involutive with the stated characters
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=(3,3,0)
.
To do this, return to the general normal form (
2.10
),
and assume that
A_{Q}
has dimension
6
.
Because
A_{Q}
has codimension
3
in
\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,{\mathbb{R}})
,
it will be involutive with characters
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=(3,3,0)

if and only if it has a non-characteristic covector.
Now, the condition that a covector
\mathbf{z}^{*}=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})\in\big({\mathbb{R}}^{3}\big)^{*}

be characteristic for
A_{Q}
is the condition
that the
3
-dimensional vector space of rank
1
matrices
of the form
\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{*}
(where
\mathbf{x}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\mathbf{z}^{*}=(z_{1},z_{2},z_{3})

is regarded as a row vector) have a nontrivial intersection
with
A_{Q}
in
\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,\mathbb{R})
.
The condition that a rank
1
matrix
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{x}\mathbf{z}^{*}

lie in the
6
-dimensional subspace
A_{Q}
of the
9
-dimensional space

\mathfrak{gl}(3,\mathbb{R})
can be expressed as 3 homogeneous linear equations in
\mathbf{r}
,
i.e.,
3
homogeneous equations bilinear in the components
of
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{z}^{*}
. Regarding
\mathbf{z}^{*}\not=0
as given,
this becomes a system of three linear equations for the components
of
\mathbf{x}
whose coefficient matrix
C_{Q}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
is
3
-by-
3

with entries that are linear in the components of
\mathbf{z}^{*}
.
This system will have a nonzero solution
\mathbf{x}

if and only if
\det\bigl(C_{Q}(\mathbf{z}^{*})\bigr)=0
.
In terms of the coefficients
p_{i}
and
q_{i}
of
Q_{0}
,
this determinant vanishing can be written
as a homogeneous cubic polynomial equation

\displaystyle 0=\sum_{ijk}c_{ijk}(p,q)z_{i}z_{j}z_{k}=c_{Q}(\mathbf{z}^{*}).

One then finds (again by a somewhat tedious calculation
that is eased by MAPLE) that this equation holds identically in
\mathbf{z}^{*}

(i.e., that all of the
c_{ijk}(p,q)
vanish) if and only if
Q_{0}

is equivalent to a matrix of the form (
2.9
)
subject to either of the two conditions
r=y=0
or
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
.

Thus, except when
Q_{0}
is orthogonally equivalent to such matrices,

A_{Q}
has dimension
6
and there exists a non-characteristic covector
\mathbf{z}^{*}
for
A_{Q}
. As already explained, this implies that
A_{Q}
is
involutive, with the claimed Cartan characters.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

The
\operatorname{SO}(3)
-orbits of the matrices

whose trace-free part

is of the form (
2.9
)
with

forms a closed cone of dimension
4

in the (
9
-dimensional) space
\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,\mathbb{R})

of
3
-by-

Meanwhile, the
\operatorname{SO}(3)
-orbits of the matrices

.

Consequently, the set consisting of those

\operatorname{\mathfrak{gl}}(3,\mathbb{R})
.

Remark 2.7
.

It does not appear to be easy to determine the condition on

that the real cubic curve

be a smooth,
irreducible cubic with two circuits. This is what one would need

Corollary 2.8
.

If

is defined by equations (

),
then

is Cartan-regular
if and only if
Q_{0}=Q-\tfrac{1}{3}\tr(Q)I_{3}

is not orthogonally equivalent
to a matrix of the form (

),
where either

or

.

Proof.

Everything is clear from Proposition
2.5
,
except possibly the assertion of Cartan-

.
However, because the characters are
(s_{1},s_{2},s_{3})=(3,3,0)
,
when

avoids the two ‘degenerate’ cones, it follows that,

has the property
that its

∎

3
Involutivity

Finally, we collect all of this information together,
yielding our main result:

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a real-analytic closed
2
-form on a
3
-manifold
M
with values in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and suppose that there is no nonzero vector
\mathbf{v}\in T_{p}M

such that
\mathbf{v}\mathbin{\hbox{\vrule height=1.4pt,width=4.0pt,depth=-1.0pt\vrule height=4.0pt,width=0.4pt,depth=-1.0pt}}\Omega=0
. Then there is an open
p
-neighborhood
U\subset M
on which there exists an
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued coframing
\omega\colon TU\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

such that
\mathrm{d}\omega=\Omega_{U}
and such that the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is flat. Moreover, the space of such coframings
\omega
depends locally on
3
functions of
2
variables.

Proof.

Keeping the established notation, it suffices to show that, if
Z(p)
has rank at least
2
, then there exists a
P\in\operatorname{GL}(3,{\mathbb{R}})
such that, when
Q
is defined by (
2.7
), the tableau
A_{Q}
is involutive.

Now, by the hypothesis that there is no nonzero vector
\mathbf{v}\in T_{p}M

such that
\mathbf{v}\mathbin{\hbox{\vrule height=1.4pt,width=4.0pt,depth=-1.0pt\vrule height=4.0pt,width=0.4pt,depth=-1.0pt}}\Omega=0
, the rank of
Z(p)
is either
2
or
3
.
When the rank of
Z(p)
is
3
,
as
P
varies over
\operatorname{GL}(3,{\mathbb{R}})
,
the matrix
Q
varies over an open subset of
\operatorname{GL}(3,{\mathbb{R}})
,
and it is clear that, for the generic choice of
P
,
the corresponding
Q_{0}
will not be
\operatorname{SO}(3)
-equivalent to anything
in the two ‘degenerate’ cones defined by (
2.9
)
with either
r=y=0
or
r^{2}=x^{2}+y^{2}
.

When the rank of
Z(p)
is
2
, we can assume, after an
\operatorname{SO}(3)
rotation,
that the bottom row of
Z(p)
vanishes and that the first two rows of
Z(p)

are linearly independent. It then follows that
P/(\det P)\,{}^{t}\!Z(p)

has its last column equal to zero, but that, as
P
varies, the first
two columns of
P/(\det P)\,{}^{t}\!Z(p)
range over all linearly independent
pairs of column vectors. Now explicitly computing the polynomial
c_{Q}(\mathbf{z}^{*})

for the corresponding matrix
Q
shows that
c_{Q}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
does not vanish
identically on the set of such matrices, hence it is possible to choose
P

so that
c_{Q}(\mathbf{z}^{*})
does not vanish identically, and the corresponding
A_{Q}

is then involutive, implying that the corresponding admissible integral
element
E
is Cartan-ordinary.

In either case, there exist Cartan-ordinary admissible integral elements
of
{\mathcal{I}}
based at
p
, so the Cartan–Kähler theorem applies, showing
that there exist admissible integral manifolds of
{\mathcal{I}}
passing through
any point
(p,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{a})\in X^{9}
, and hence,
by Proposition
2.1
,
the original problem is solvable in an open neighborhood of
p
.
Moreover, since the last nonzero Cartan character
of a generic integral flag is
s_{2}=3
,
the space of solutions
\omega

depends locally on
3
functions of
2
variables,
in the sense of Cartan.
∎

4
The rank 1 case

If the rank of
Z(p)
is either 0 or 1,
then, for all values of
Q
as defined in (
2.7
)
with
P
invertible, the tableau
A_{Q}
fails to be involutive,
so the Cartan–Kähler theorem cannot be applied to prove local solvability.

However, as noted in Example
2.4
,
this does not necessarily preclude
the existence of integral manifolds of
{\mathcal{I}}
in a neighborhood of
p
.
Indeed, when
Z
vanishes identically on a neighborhood of
p\in M
,
the general solution
\omega=\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
(where
\mathbf{x}\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}

is an arbitrary immersion)
depends locally on 3 functions of 3 variables;
so there are actually
more
integral manifolds
in this case than in the case in which
Z(p)
has rank
2
or
3
.

Nevertheless, as the following example demonstrates, even local solvability is not guaranteed in general.

Example 4.1
.

Set
\Omega=(\Omega^{i})=(\Upsilon,0,0)
, where

\displaystyle\Upsilon=u^{1}\mathrm{d}u^{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{3}+u^{2}\mathrm{d}u^{3}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{1}-2u^{3}\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}.

(4.1)

(Note that in this case, the matrix
Z
has rank
1

everywhere except at the origin, where the rank is
0
.)
We will show that there is no coframing
\omega=(\omega^{i})
on any neighborhood of
u=(u^{i})=(0,0,0)
such that the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is flat.
In fact, we will show, more generally, that if
\omega
is any coframing on
M
such that
\mathrm{d}\omega^{2}=\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}=0

and the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is flat,
then we must have
\omega^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}=0
.

Meanwhile,
\Upsilon
defined as in (
4.1
) has no nonvanishing factor on any neighborhood of
u=(u^{i})=(0,0,0)
.
In order to see this, suppose that
\Upsilon\wedge\beta=0
,
where
\beta=b_{1}\mathrm{d}u^{1}+b_{2}\mathrm{d}u^{2}+b_{3}\mathrm{d}u^{3}
.
Then

u^{1}b_{1}+u^{2}b_{2}-2u^{3}b_{3}=0.

This implies, for example,
that
u^{3}b_{3}
must vanish on the line
u^{1}=u^{2}=0
and hence
that
b_{3}
must also vanish there. In particular,
b_{3}

must vanish at the origin
u^{i}=0
. Similarly,
b_{1}
and
b_{2}

must also vanish at the origin. Thus,
\beta
must vanish
at the origin.

To establish the general claim, let
\omega
be a coframing on
M^{3}
such that
\mathrm{d}\omega^{2}=\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}=0

and the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is flat.
Writing

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{1}\\
\omega^{2}\\
\omega^{3}\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\phantom{-}0&\phantom{-}\phi^{3}&-\phi^{2}\\
-\phi^{3}&\phantom{-}0&\phantom{-}\phi^{1}\\
\phantom{-}\phi^{2}&-\phi^{1}&\phantom{-}0\end{pmatrix}\wedge\begin{pmatrix}\omega^{1}\\
\omega^{2}\\
\omega^{3}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},

we see, from the vanishing of
\mathrm{d}\omega^{2}
and
\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}
, that there must exist functions
a^{1}
,
a^{2}
, and
a^{3}

such that

\displaystyle\phi^{1}=a^{1}\omega^{1},\qquad\phi^{2}=a^{2}\omega^{1}-a^{1}\omega^{2},\qquad\phi^{3}=a^{3}\omega^{1}-a^{1}\omega^{3}.

Consequently, we must have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}=-2a^{1}\omega^{2}\wedge\omega^{3}-a^{2}\omega^{3}\wedge\omega^{1}-a^{3}\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}.

Now, the flatness of the metric
g
is equivalent to the equations

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\phi^{1}-\phi^{2}\wedge\phi^{3}=\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}-\phi^{3}\wedge\phi^{1}=\mathrm{d}\phi^{3}-\phi^{1}\wedge\phi^{2}=0.

However, from the above equations, we see that

\displaystyle 0=\mathrm{d}\phi^{1}-\phi^{2}\wedge\phi^{3}=\mathrm{d}a^{1}\wedge\omega^{1}-3\big(a^{1}\big)^{2}\omega^{2}\wedge\omega^{3}-2a^{1}a^{2}\omega^{3}\wedge\omega^{1}-2a^{1}a^{3}\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}.

Wedging both ends of this equation with
\omega^{1}
yields

-3\big(a^{1}\big)^{2}\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}\wedge\omega^{3}=0
. Hence
a^{1}=0
,
and we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}=\omega^{1}\wedge\big(a^{2}\omega^{3}-a^{3}\omega^{2}\big).

In particular,
\omega^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}=0
, as claimed.

It is worthwhile to carry these calculations with the
coframing
\omega
a little further. Since
a^{1}=0
,
we see that
\phi^{1}=0
, and the condition for flatness
reduces to
\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}=\mathrm{d}\phi^{3}=0
.

Let us assume that
M
is connected and simply-connected.
Fix a point
p\in M
and write
\omega^{2}=\mathrm{d}u^{2}
and
\omega^{3}=\mathrm{d}u^{3}

for unique functions
u^{2}
and
u^{3}
that vanish at
p
.
Since
\omega^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}=0
, it follows from the Frobenius Theorem that there exists
an open
p
-neighborhood
U\subset M
on which
there exists a function
u^{1}
vanishing at
p
such that

\omega^{1}=f\mathrm{d}u^{1}
for some nonvanishing function
f
on
U
.
Restricting to a smaller
p
-neighborhood if necessary, we can
arrange that
\mathbf{u}=\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
be a rectangular coordinate chart.
Now, computation yields

\displaystyle\phi^{1}=0,\qquad\phi^{2}=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial u^{3}}\mathrm{d}u^{1},\qquad\phi^{3}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial u^{2}}\mathrm{d}u^{1}.

The remaining flatness conditions
\mathrm{d}\phi^{2}=\mathrm{d}\phi^{3}=0

then are equivalent to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\big(\partial u^{2}\big)^{2}}=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\partial u^{2}\partial u^{3}}=\frac{\partial^{2}f}{\big(\partial u^{3}\big)^{2}}=0.

Consequently,
f=f\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)
is linear in
u^{2}
and
u^{3}
,
so it can be written in the form
f=g_{1}\big(u^{1}\big)+g_{2}\big(u^{1}\big)u^{2}+g_{3}\big(u^{1}\big)u^{3}

for some functions
g_{1}
,
g_{2}
,
g_{3}
. Since
f
does not vanish
on
u^{2}=u^{3}=0
, by changing coordinates in
u^{1}
, we can arrange
that
g_{1}\big(u^{1}\big)=1
. Thus, the coframing takes the form

\displaystyle\omega=\big(\big(1+g_{2}\big(u^{1}\big)u^{2}+g_{3}\big(u^{1}\big)u^{3}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{1},\mathrm{d}u^{2},\mathrm{d}u^{3}\big),

where the
p
-centered coordinates
u^{i}
are unique. Conversely,
for any two functions
g_{2}
and
g_{3}
on an interval containing
0\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
the above coframing has the property that
\mathrm{d}\omega^{2}=\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}=0

while the metric
g={}^{t}\omega\circ\omega
is flat. Finally,
note that
\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}
is nonvanishing at
\mathbf{u}=0
if and only if
g_{2}(0)

and
g_{3}(0)
are not both zero.

In light of Example
4.1
, it is clear that some assumptions will be required in order to ensure that local solutions exist. First, in order to avoid a singularity of the type in Example
4.1
, where
Z
vanishes at a single point, we will assume that
Z
has constant rank 1 in some neighborhood
U
of
p\in M
. This assumption is equivalent to the assumption that the 2-forms
\Omega^{1}
,
\Omega^{2}
,
\Omega^{3}
are scalar multiples of each other and do not simultaneously vanish.

4.1
Formulation as an exterior differential system

We will take the following approach: Rather than assuming that
Z
is specified in advance, we will seek to characterize functions
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
,
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
such that the components
\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big)
of the
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued 1-form
\omega=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
form a local coframing on
U
with the property that the 2-forms
\big(\mathrm{d}\omega^{1},\mathrm{d}\omega^{2},\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}\big)
are pairwise linearly dependent and do not vanish simultaneously. Since this property is invariant under reparametrizations of the domain

Theorem 4.4
.

The space of local orthonormal coframings
\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big)
on an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
whose exterior derivatives
\big(\mathrm{d}\omega^{1},\mathrm{d}\omega^{2},\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}\big)
are pairwise linearly dependent and do not simultaneously vanish and satisfy the additional property that

is locally parametrized by
1
function of
2
variables.

This function count suggests that, if the rank 1 matrix
Z
on
M
is specified in advance, local solutions are likely to exist for arbitrary, generic choices of
Z

1
condition implies that we can find local coordinates
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)
on some neighborhood
U
of any point

such that

\Omega=\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}

for some smooth, nonvanishing
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued function
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
. Moreover, by local coordinate transformations of the form
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\to\big(\tilde{u}^{1}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),\tilde{u}^{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),u^{3}\big)
, we might expect that we could normalize 2 of the 3 functions

. For example, if

, then we could choose the functions

in a neighborhod of

such that

and

. Then the vector

is characterized by the remaining single function of 2 variables

Now computing the 2-forms

Now computing the 2-forms
\big(\mathrm{d}\theta^{4},\ldots,\mathrm{d}\theta^{8}\big)

\left.\begin{array}[]{@{}l@{}}\pi_{14}\equiv\mathrm{d}w_{1}\\

and

\pi_{16}=\mu v_{5}\pi_{14}+4\lambda v_{4}\pi_{15},\qquad\pi_{17}=4\lambda v_{4}\pi_{14}-\mu v_{5}\pi_{15}.

First, consider the open set where

to this submanifold is, at best, a Frobenius system with a finite-dimensional space of integral manifolds. More likely, differentiating the equation that defines this submanifold will lead to additional relations that will further restrict the set that admits integral elements, thereby reducing the dimension of the space of integral manifolds, possibly to the point that there are no integral manifolds on which

Next, we consider the case where
v_{4}=v_{5}=0
. In order to characterize integral manifolds satisfying this condition, we must go back to the system
{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}
on the manifold

generated by
\big(\theta^{1},\ldots,\theta^{8}\big)

denote the restriction of
{\mathcal{I}}^{\prime}
to

1

\big(\theta^{1},\theta^{2},\theta^{3}\big)
, together with the

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \theta^{4}&=\mathrm{d}\lambda-2\lambda v_{2}\chi^{1}+\mu v_{2}\chi^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda\mu\chi^{3},\\

\big(\mathrm{d}\theta^{4},\ldots,\mathrm{d}\theta^{8}\big)

,

, but the space of integral elements is only 2-dimensional. Since

is the EDS on the manifold

, with coordinates
(q_{1},q_{2})
on the
{\mathbb{R}}^{2}
factor, generated by the 1-forms
\big(\theta^{1},\ldots,\theta^{8}\big)

1

\displaystyle\theta^{9}=\mathrm{d}v_{1}+\big(\tfrac{1}{2}\lambda v_{3}-v_{1}v_{2}\big)\chi^{1}+q_{1}\chi^{2}+q_{2}\chi^{3},

\displaystyle\theta^{10}=\mathrm{d}v_{2}+\big(\tfrac{1}{4}\lambda^{2}-v_{2}^{2}\big)\chi^{1}-\tfrac{1}{4}\lambda\mu\chi^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}\mu v_{2}\chi^{3},

\big(\mathrm{d}\theta^{9},\mathrm{d}\theta^{10},\mathrm{d}\theta^{11}\big)

As a result of this computation and Remark
4.2
, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 4.5
.

Aside from a possible finite-dimensional family of solutions
(
which may be empty
)
, the space of local orthonormal coframings
\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big)
on an open subset of
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
whose exterior derivatives
\big(\mathrm{d}\omega^{1},\mathrm{d}\omega^{2},\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}\big)
are pairwise linearly dependent and do not simultaneously vanish and satisfy the additional property that

\omega^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{1}+\omega^{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{2}+\omega^{3}\wedge\mathrm{d}\omega^{3}\neq 0

is locally parametrized by
2
functions of
1
variable.

One consequence of this result is that the space of integral manifolds with
\lambda\neq 0
is strictly smaller than the space of integral manifolds with
\lambda=0
, which we recall is locally parametrized by 1 function of 2 variables. In particular, if the function
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
is specified in advance, there will be no solutions with
\lambda\neq 0
for generic choices of
\mathbf{z}
. The question of precisely which choices for the function
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
do admit solutions is an interesting one, but we shall not attempt to address it here.

4.4
Explicit solutions with
\bm{\lambda=0}

We will conclude by showing how to construct explicit solutions with
\lambda=0
for arbitrary choices of the function
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
that satisfy a certain nondegeneracy condition, which will be described below. First, we will show how to construct local coordinates and a local normal form for a general integral manifold of the system
\bar{{\mathcal{I}}}
on the manifold
\bar{X}
. We will need the following well-known fact from linear algebra:

Lemma 4.6
.

Let
\mathbf{v}={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(v^{1},v^{2},v^{3}\big)
be a nonzero vector in
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, and let
[\mathbf{v}]
denote the skew-symmetric matrix

[\mathbf{v}]=\begin{pmatrix}0&v^{3}&-v^{2}\\
-v^{3}&0&v^{1}\\
v^{2}&-v^{1}&0\end{pmatrix}.

Then
[\mathbf{v}]
has rank
2
, and its kernel is spanned by
\mathbf{v}
. Specifically, for any vector
\mathbf{w}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
, we have

{}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\mathbf{w}[\mathbf{v}]=[\mathbf{v}]\mathbf{w}=0

if and only of
\mathbf{w}
is a scalar multiple of
\mathbf{v}
.

Let
N^{3}\subset\bar{X}
be any integral manifold of
\bar{{\mathcal{I}}}
; in keeping with our conventions, let
\omega
and
\phi
denote the pullbacks to
N
of
\xi
and
\alpha
, respectively. As noted above, the assumption that
\lambda=0
implies that the map
\mathbf{a}\colon V\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
whose graph determines the integral manifold
N
has rank 1. Therefore, there exists a local coordinate function
u^{1}
on
V
such that
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{a}\big(u^{1}\big)
, and we can write

\displaystyle[\phi]=\mathbf{a}^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}0&g^{3}\big(u^{1}\big)&-g^{2}\big(u^{1}\big)\\
-g^{3}\big(u^{1}\big)&0&g^{1}\big(u^{1}\big)\\
g^{2}\big(u^{1}\big)&-g^{1}\big(u^{1}\big)&0\end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}u^{1}

(4.13)

for some smooth functions
g^{i}\big(u^{1}\big)
on
V
that do not all vanish simultaneously.

Let
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
denote the
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued function
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(g^{1}\big(u^{1}\big),g^{2}\big(u^{1}\big),g^{3}\big(u^{1}\big)\big)
. From equation (
4.13
), the
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued
2
-form
\Omega
must satisfy

\displaystyle\Omega=\mathrm{d}\omega=-[\phi]\wedge\omega=-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\omega.

(4.14)

It follows that each of the 2-forms
\big(\Omega^{1},\Omega^{2},\Omega^{3}\big)
must have the 1-form
\mathrm{d}u^{1}
as a factor. By Darboux’s theorem, we can find another independent coordinate function
u^{2}
on
V
such that each of the 2-forms
\Omega^{i}
is a multiple of
\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}
.

Now let
u^{3}
be any coordinate function on
V
that is independent from
u^{1}
and
u^{2}
, so that
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)
form a local coordinate system on
V
. Let
\mathbf{u}=\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\colon V\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and let
U=\mathbf{u}(V)\subset{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
; then we may regard
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)
as local coordinates on
N
and
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{a}
as functions
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
and
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
.

Next, we can write

\omega=\mathbf{w}_{j}\mathrm{d}u^{j}

for some
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued functions
(\mathbf{w}_{1},\mathbf{w}_{2},\mathbf{w}_{3})
on
U
that are linearly independent at each point of
U
. Then we have

-[\phi]\wedge\omega=-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{w}_{3}\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{3}.

Since the left-hand side is a multiple of
\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}
, it follows that
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{w}_{3}=0
. Since the vector
\mathbf{w}_{3}
cannot vanish, it must lie in the kernel of the rank 2 matrix
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]
; therefore, Lemma
4.6
implies that

\mathbf{w}_{3}=\bar{\mu}\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)

for some smooth, nonvanishing function

Setting

\tilde{u}^{3}=\int\bar{\mu}\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{3},

we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}_{3}\mathrm{d}u^{3}=\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\bar{\mu}\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{3}\equiv\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\mathrm{d}\tilde{u}^{3}\mod{\mathrm{d}u^{1},\mathrm{d}u^{2}}.

So, via the local coordinate transformation

We now have

\displaystyle\omega=\mathbf{w}_{1}\mathrm{d}u^{1}+\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathrm{d}u^{2}+\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{3}.

(4.15)

Differentiating gives

\displaystyle\Omega=\mathrm{d}\omega=-(\mathbf{w}_{2})_{3}\mathrm{d}u^{2}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{3}+\big((\mathbf{w}_{1})_{3}-\mathbf{g}^{\prime}\big(u^{1}\big)\big)\mathrm{d}u^{3}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{1}

(4.16)

where subscripts outside parentheses indicate partial derivatives with respect to the coordinates

. On the other hand, substituting (

\displaystyle\Omega=-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{w}_{2}\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}.

(4.17)

Comparing (

) and (

\displaystyle(\mathbf{w}_{2})_{3}=0,\qquad(\mathbf{w}_{1})_{3}=\mathbf{g}^{\prime}\big(u^{1}\big),\qquad(\mathbf{w}_{2})_{1}-(\mathbf{w}_{1})_{2}=-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{w}_{2}.

The first two equations in (

\mathbf{w}_{1}
,
\mathbf{w}_{2}
have the form

\mathbf{w}_{1}=u^{3}\mathbf{g}^{\prime}\big(u^{1}\big)+\mathbf{h}_{1}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),\qquad\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{h}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)

-valued functions

of

The general solution to this equation is

\mathbf{h}_{1}=(\mathbf{k})_{1}+\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{k},\qquad\mathbf{h}_{2}=(\mathbf{k})_{2},

where
\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
is an arbitrary, smooth

-valued function of
\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
.

We now have

\displaystyle\omega=\big(\big(u^{3}\mathbf{g}^{\prime}\big(u^{1}\big)+\mathbf{k}_{1}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)+\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\big)\mathrm{d}u^{1}

\displaystyle\hphantom{\omega=}{}+\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{2}+\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{3},

(4.19)

where
\mathbf{k}_{i}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
denotes
\frac{\partial}{\partial u^{i}}\big(\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\big)
.
(Note that
\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
must be chosen so that the components of
\omega
are linearly independent at each point of
U

Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\Omega=-[\phi]\wedge\omega=-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}.

Now, suppose that we are given a vector
\Omega
of closed 2-forms on
U
whose components
\big(\Omega^{1},\Omega^{2},\Omega^{3}\big)
are all scalar multiples of a single 2-form and do not vanish simultaneously. What conditions must
\Omega
satisfy in order to guarantee the existence of a local coordinate system
\big(u^{i}\big)
on
U
and
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued functions
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
,
\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
so that the coframing

given by (

) satisfies the condition

?

First note that, by Darboux’s theorem, we can find local coordinates
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)
on
U
such that

\Omega=\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}

for some smooth, nonvanishing
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued function
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
. Moreover, under any change of coordinates of the form
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\to\big(\tilde{u}^{1}(u^{1},u^{2}\big),\tilde{u}^{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),u^{3}\big)
, each of the functions

is multiplied by the determinant of the Jacobian of the coordinate transformation. Thus, it is geometrically natural to regard
\mathbf{z}
as defining a map
[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
into
{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, and this map is unchanged by coordinate transformations of this form.

The following theorem shows that a mild nondegeneracy condition on the function
[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
is sufficient to guarantee the existence of solutions.

Theorem 4.7
.

Let
\Omega=\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{1}\wedge\mathrm{d}u^{2}
, where
\mathbf{z}\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
is a smooth, nonvanishing function. Let
[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}

\mathbf{z}\colon M\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}\setminus\{\mathbf{0}\}
with the standard projection

, and suppose that either,
(i)
the image of
[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
is contained in a line in
{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}

(ii)

d[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
is nonvanishing on

M

\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
,
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
such that the map
\mathbf{a}
has rank

and the components
\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big)
of the
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued

form a local coframing on
U
and
\mathrm{d}\omega=\Omega
.

Proof.

We will show that, possibly after a coordinate transformation of the form
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\to\big(\tilde{u}^{1}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),\tilde{u}^{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),u^{3}\big)
, we can find
{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
-valued functions
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
,
\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
such that

\displaystyle-\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)=\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big).

(4.20)

It is important to observe that the matrix
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]
necessarily
has rank 2, and equation (
4.20
) requires that, for any fixed value of
u^{1}
, the vector
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
be contained in the image of
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]
for all values of
u^{2}
. This, in turn, is true if and only if

\displaystyle{}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)=0.

(4.21)

There may not initially appear to exist such a function
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
depending on
u^{1}
alone, but under the hypotheses of the theorem, we can find refined local coordinates and a nonvanishing function
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
for which this condition holds. For instance:

•

If the image of
[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
is contained in a line in
{\mathbb{R}}{\mathbb{P}}^{2}
, then there exist constants
a_{1},a_{2},a_{3}\in{\mathbb{R}}
, not all zero, such that

a_{1}z^{1}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)+a_{2}z^{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)+a_{3}z^{3}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)=0.

In this case, let
\mathbf{g}={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt(a_{1},a_{2},a_{3})
.

•

If
\mathrm{d}[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
is nonvanishing on
U
, then in some neighorhood of every point, at least one of the ratios
z^{i}/z^{j}
has no critical points. If, say, the ratio
r\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)=z^{2}/z^{1}
is nonconstant and has no critical points, then in a neighborhood of any point we can make a change of coordinates of the form
\big(u^{1},u^{2},u^{3}\big)\to\big(\tilde{u}^{1}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),\tilde{u}^{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big),u^{3}\big)
with
\tilde{u}^{1}=r\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
, so that in the new coordinates we have
z^{2}=u^{1}z^{1}
. After performing this coordinate transformation, let
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(u^{1},-1,0\big)
.

Now, having constructed the desired local coordinate system and function
\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
, let
\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
be a smooth solution of the linear system of equations (
4.20
). As noted above, this equation can be solved for
\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
precisely because the condition (
4.21
) is exactly the condition required to ensure that for every
\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
, the vector
\mathbf{z}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
lies in the image of the rank 2 matrix
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]
.

Now let

\displaystyle\mathbf{k}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)=\int\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)\mathrm{d}u^{2}+\bar{\mathbf{k}}\big(u^{1}\big),

(4.22)

where the function
\bar{\mathbf{k}}\big(u^{1}\big)
may be chosen arbitrarily,
and define
\omega
by equation (
4.19
). By construction,
\omega
satisfies
\mathrm{d}\omega=\Omega
and so is the desired coframing.

The only detail remaining to check is that the components
\mathbf{w}_{j}
of
\omega
in (
4.19
) are linearly independent, so that
\big(\omega^{1},\omega^{2},\omega^{3}\big)
is a coframing on
U
. First, observe that
\mathbf{w}_{3}=\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)
lies in the kernel of
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]
. The vector
\mathbf{w}_{2}=\mathbf{k}_{2}\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
, however, must satisfy (
4.20
) and so cannot lie in the kernel of
\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big]
; hence the vectors
\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
\mathbf{w}_{3}
are linearly independent. And since the function
\bar{\mathbf{k}}\big(u^{1}\big)
in (
4.22
) may be chosen arbitrarily, we can arrange for
\mathbf{w}_{1}
to be linearly independent from
\mathbf{w}_{2}
and
\mathbf{w}_{3}
by choosing
\bar{\mathbf{k}}\big(u^{1}\big)
appropriately.

Finally, the functions
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
and
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
promised by the theorem may be constructed as follows. First, the function
\mathbf{a}\colon U\to\operatorname{SO}(3)
is given by the solution (unique up to multiplication by a constant matrix in
\operatorname{SO}(3)
) of the ODE

\displaystyle\mathbf{a}^{\prime}\big(u^{1}\big)=\mathbf{a}\big(u^{1}\big)\big[\mathbf{g}\big(u^{1}\big)\big].

(4.23)

Then the function
\mathbf{x}\colon U\to{\mathbb{R}}^{3}
is given by integrating the (necessarily closed) 1-form

\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{a}\omega.

Note that, while constructing these functions requires solving the ODE (
4.23
), the coframing
\omega
can be constructed from
\Omega
using only quadratures.
∎

The following example shows that the nondegeneracy assumptions of Theorem
4.7
are essential; specifically, it shows how the construction above can fail near a point where
\mathrm{d}[\![\mathbf{z}]\!]
vanishes.

Example 4.8
.

For ease of notation, we will use
(u,v)
in place of
\big(u^{1},u^{2}\big)
in this example.
Suppose that

\mathbf{z}(u,v)={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(1,\rho(u,v),\rho(u,v)^{2}\big),

where

\rho(u,v)=u^{2}+v^{2}.

Then
\mathrm{d}[\![\mathbf{z}]\!](0,0)=0
.

Suppose that there exists a
(0,0)
-centered local coordinate system
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
in some neighborhood
U
of
(0,0)
and a nonvanishing vector field
\mathbf{g}(\tilde{u})
on
U
such that

\displaystyle{}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\mathbf{g}(\tilde{u})\mathbf{z}(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})=0.

(4.24)

Because the function
\rho
has a critical point at
(0,0)
and is strictly convex, it has the property that for any nonvanishing vector field
\mathbf{v}
on
U
,

\mathbf{v}[\rho](0,0)=0,\qquad\mathbf{v}[\mathbf{v}[\rho]](0,0)>0.

In particular, we have

\rho_{\tilde{v}}(0,0)=0,\qquad\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}(0,0)=\kappa_{0}>0,

where subscripts denote partial derivatives with respect to
\tilde{v}
in the
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
coordinate system.
It follows that

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}(0,0)={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt(1,0,0),

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}(0,0)={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(0,\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}},2\big(\rho\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}+\rho_{\tilde{v}}^{2}\big)\big)\big|_{(0,0)}={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt(0,\kappa_{0},0),

\displaystyle\mathbf{z}_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}(0,0)={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(0,\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}},6\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}^{2}+8\rho_{\tilde{v}}\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}+2\rho\rho_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}\big)\big|_{(0,0)}={}^{t}\hskip-1.0pt\big(0,*,6\kappa_{0}^{2}\big),

where the second entry of
\mathbf{z}_{\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}\tilde{v}}(0,0)
is irrelevant.

Consequently, evaluating equation (
4.24
) together with its
2
nd and
4
th
\tilde{v}
-derivatives at
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})=(0,0)
yields three independent linear equations for the components of
\mathbf{g}(0)
. It follows that
\mathbf{g}(0)=\mathbf{0}
, and hence there is no nonvanishing vector field
\mathbf{g}(\tilde{u})
satisfying the condition (
4.24
) for any local coordinate system
(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
on any neighborhood of
(u,v)=(0,0)
.
