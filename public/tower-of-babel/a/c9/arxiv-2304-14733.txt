Consecutive Pattern Containment and c-Wilf Equivalence
Reza Rastegar

Abstract
We offer elementary proofs for several results in consecutive pattern containment that were previously demonstrated using ideas from cluster method and analytical combinatorics. Furthermore, we establish new general bounds on the growth rates of consecutive pattern avoidance in permutations.

Introduction

Consecutive pattern containment is a variation of the standard definition of pattern containment in permutations. In this containment, the entries of a pattern are required to appear in adjacent positions. This problem is significant in combinatorics, as it naturally arises in descents, peaks, and alternating permutations. The first systematic analysis of consecutive pattern containment is credited to Elizalde and Noy
[
10
]
, who defined it formally and established several fundamental results. For more information on the broader subject of pattern containment, we refer the reader to
[
1
]
and
[
13
]
. Additionally, for a more focused review of consecutive pattern containment, see
[
9
]
. In this paper, we revisit consecutive pattern containment in both permutations and words, and we present several new and previously known facts. Our proofs are based on elementary probabilistic arguments.

. The notation

with respect to the universe set. For a given

, we denote the set

, and the set of all
d

. Denote by

. A word

Let us define an occurrence of the pattern

(1)

An important area of study in pattern containment is to determine when two patterns are avoided by the same
number of permutations or words of the same length, or more generally when the distribution of occurrences of the two patterns is the same. We say that two patterns

Problem 1.1

Classify all patterns into (strongly) c-Wilf equivalence classes in permutations (in words).

2
Consecutive containment in permutations

In order to prove our results, we found it easier to reformulate the problem of consecutive pattern containment within a probabilistic framework. However, it is also possible to present combinatorial proofs of many of these results without relying on the language of probability. Let
(Y_{n})
be a sequence of independently and identically distributed random variables
(Y_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, uniformly sampled from
[0,1]
. We will denote by
{\mathbb{P}}
the probability law in a suitable probability
space that supports all random variables considered in this section. Consider a discrete-time Markov process
(X_{n})_{n\in{\mathbb{N}}}
on the space
[0,1]^{d}
, where
X_{n}:=Y_{n}\cdots Y_{n+d-1}
. The transition probability of
X_{n}
is defined as

\displaystyle P(x,{\mathcal{A}})={\mathbb{L}}(\{y\ |(x_{2},\cdots,x_{d},y)\in{\mathcal{A}}\}),\quad x:=x_{1}\cdots x_{d}\in[0,1]^{d},\quad{\mathcal{A}}\subset[0,1]^{d},

where
{\mathbb{L}}
represents the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]
. It is well-known that
\red(Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n})
follows the same distribution as a uniformly randomly sampled permutation from
{\mathcal{S}}_{n}
. This implies that

\displaystyle a_{n}^{(r)}(v)=\frac{g_{r-1}^{v}({\mathcal{S}}_{n+d-1})}{(n+d-1)!}={\mathbb{P}}(\con_{v}(\red(Y_{1}\cdots Y_{n+d-1}))=r-1).

In the rest of this section, we will use
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(v)
to denote the event
\{\red(X_{i})\neq v\}
and use
\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i}(v)
to refer to its complement. We define
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{j}(v)
as
\cap_{s=i}^{j}{\mathcal{B}}_{s}(v)
for
i\leq j
. When
i>j
, we define
{\mathcal{B}}_{i}^{j}(v)
as the universe set with probability measure of one. We will drop
v
whenever the context is clear. The following facts will be used frequently without explicit mention: For
i\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}(\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i})=\frac{1}{d!}\quad\text{and}\quad{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{i})=1-\frac{1}{d!},

and for
i\geq 2
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{i-1},\widetilde{\mathcal{B}}_{i})={\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{i-1})-{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{B}}_{1}^{i}).

We commence by presenting the following observation, which although not employed directly in our proofs, serves as a straightforward proof of the existence of the growth rate
\rho_{v}
and the inequality
\rho_{v}<1
. This outcome was originally demonstrated in
[
7
]
(see Theorem 4.1 herein). It is noteworthy to mention that Ehrenborg et al.
[
6
]
established a more refined outcome, as mentioned in the introduction.

Let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}^{v}(x)
be the exponential generating function of
g_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{S}}_{n})
; that is,

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{S}}_{r}^{v}(x)=\sum_{n=d}^{\infty}g_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{S}}_{n})\frac{x^{n}}{n!},\quad r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0}.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
v\in{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
be a given pattern. Then, there exists a constant
\kappa_{v}>1
such that
{\mathfrak{S}}_{0}^{v}(\kappa_{v})<\infty
. In particular, this implies that

exists and is at most

Proof.

Our initial task is to verify Doeblin’s condition for
X_{n}
(refer to
[
19
]
for a detailed discussion). To do so, let
P^{k}(.,.)
denote the
k

(X_{n})
. For every
a\in(0,1)
, for any
x\in[0,1]^{d}
, and any Borel measurable subset
{\mathcal{A}}
of
[0,1]^{d}
, we have the following inequality:

\displaystyle(1-a)\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a^{k}P^{k}(x,{\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle\geq

\displaystyle(1-a)\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}a^{k}P^{k}(x,{\mathcal{A}})\geq(1-a)\sum_{k=d}^{\infty}a^{k}{\mathbb{L}}({\mathcal{A}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle a^{d}{\mathbb{L}}({\mathcal{A}}).

By Theorem 16.0.2 of
[
19
]
, this implies that
X_{n}
is irreducible and has at most one invariant measure. In the next line, we show that this invariant measure is, in fact, the Lebesgue measure on

\displaystyle P{\mathbb{L}}({\mathcal{A}}):=\int_{[0,1]^{d}}P(x,{\mathcal{A}})dx=\int_{0}^{1}\cdots\int_{0}^{1}{\mathbb{L}}({(x_{2},\cdots,x_{d},y)\in{\mathcal{A}}})dx_{2}\cdots dx_{d}dy={\mathbb{L}}({\mathcal{A}}),

where
{\mathbb{L}}
is the Lebesgue measure on
[0,1]^{d}
.

Let
T
denote the first time that
(X_{n})
visits
{\mathcal{A}}_{v}:=\{x\in[0,1]^{d}\ |\red(x)=v\}.
Since Doeblin’s condition holds for the aperiodic chain

, Theorem 16.0.2 of
[
19
]
also implies the existence of
\kappa_{v}>1
such that

\sup_{x\in[0,1]^{d}}{\mathbb{E}}(\kappa_{v}^{T}|X_{1}=x)<\infty,

(11)

where
{\mathbb{E}}
denotes the expectation operator with respect to the probability measure
{\mathbb{P}}
. The inequality (

is finite. To proceed, we can write:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\kappa_{v}^{T})=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(T=n)\kappa_{v}^{n}=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left({\mathbb{P}}(T>n-1)-{\mathbb{P}}(T>n)\right)\kappa_{v}^{n}

\displaystyle\quad=1+(\kappa_{v}-1)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(T>n)\kappa_{v}^{n}=1+(\kappa_{v}-1){\mathfrak{S}}_{0}^{v}(\kappa_{v}).

is a
\binom{\malp}{d}\times\binom{\malp}{d}

({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})

, can be expressed as follows:

\displaystyle[R(\alpha,v,\malp)]_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(v_{\mathcal{A}}*v_{\mathcal{B}})_{\alpha,k}=\frac{1}{\alpha^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\malp^{i}\alpha^{d-i}\mbox{\bf 1}_{\{v_{\mathcal{A}}[d-i+1,d]=v_{\mathcal{B}}[1,i]\}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{\bf 1}_{\{{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}\}}\left(\frac{\malp}{\alpha}\right)^{d}+\sum_{i\in{\mathcal{O}}_{v}}\left(\frac{\malp}{\alpha}\right)^{i}\mbox{\bf 1}_{\{v_{\mathcal{A}}[d-i+1,d]=v_{\mathcal{B}}[1,i]\}}.

For example, if

is a non-overlapping pattern, then for
\malp\geq d
, the
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})

is given by (
34

\displaystyle[R(\alpha,v,\malp)]_{{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}}=\begin{cases}\left(\frac{\malp}{\alpha}\right)^{d}&\text{if }{\mathcal{A}}={\mathcal{B}}\\
\frac{\malp}{\alpha}&\text{if }(v_{\mathcal{A}})_{d}=(v_{\mathcal{B}})_{1}\\
0&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

(34)

Note that for
({\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}})
to satisfy the condition
(v_{\mathcal{A}})_{d}=(v_{\mathcal{B}})_{1}
, the
v_{d}
-th smallest element of the set
{\mathcal{A}}
must be the
v_{1}
-th smallest element of the set
{\mathcal{B}}

Recall equation (
32

{\mathcal{I}}
with
{\mathcal{I}}_{v}(\malp)
in (

) and denote the resulting functions and values by

and

, respectively. Using this notation, we can state that the equation
\red(W_{n-d+1}\cdots W_{n})=v

. Moreover, for any

we have

\displaystyle\cup_{j=0}^{r}\{\con_{v}(W_{1}\cdots W_{n})=j\}=\cup_{j=0}^{r}\{W_{1}\cdots W_{n}\in G_{j}^{v}([\malp]^{n})\}=\{T^{(r+1)}_{v}>n\}.

Let
f_{r}^{v}([\malp]^{n})
denote the number of words in
[\malp]^{n}
containing at most

v
. We can express this number as the sum of

. Hence, we will write

\displaystyle f_{r}^{v}([\malp]^{n})=\sum_{j=0}^{r}g_{j}^{v}([\malp]^{n})=\malp^{n}\sum_{j=0}^{r}{\mathbb{P}}(W_{1}\cdots W_{n}\in G_{j}^{v}([\malp]^{n}))=\malp^{n}{\mathbb{P}}(T^{(r+1)}_{v}>n).

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

Let us begin by recalling that it is well known that if
\malp\geq d
, then
T_{v}^{(r)}<\infty
almost surely and hence
{\mathbb{E}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}})<\infty
. This is due to the fact that
(W_{n-d+1}\cdots W_{n})_{n\geq d}
is an irreducible finite Markov chain on
[\malp]^{d}
, which means that the hitting time of any nonempty subset of
[\malp]^{d}
has an exponential tail and is almost surely finite.

Now, let us inspect the generating functions of the random variables
T_{r}(v,k)
. To that end, observe that Fubini’s theorem can be utilized to write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}{\mathbb{P}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}}\geq x)dx=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\int_{\alpha^{\ell+1}}^{\alpha^{\ell}}{\mathbb{P}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}}\geq x)dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}}\geq\alpha^{\ell+1})\int_{\alpha^{\ell+1}}^{\alpha^{\ell}}dx=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}}\geq\alpha^{\ell+1})(\alpha^{\ell}-\alpha^{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(T_{v}^{(r)}\leq\ell+1)(\alpha^{\ell}-\alpha^{\ell+1})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\left(1-{\mathbb{P}}(T_{v}^{(r)}>\ell+1)\right)(\alpha^{\ell}-\alpha^{\ell+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 1-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}(T_{v}^{(r)}>\ell)\alpha^{\ell}=1-\frac{1-\alpha}{\alpha}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}f_{r-1}^{v}([\malp]^{\ell})\left(\frac{\alpha}{\malp}\right)^{\ell}.

Let us continue by noting that for
\malp\geq d
, we can express

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{W}}_{r,k}^{v}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\malp}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}f_{r}^{v}([\malp]^{\ell})\left(\frac{\alpha}{\malp}\right)^{\ell}-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}f_{r-1}^{v}([\malp]^{\ell})\left(\frac{\alpha}{\malp}\right)^{\ell}

(35)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left({\mathbb{E}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}})-{\mathbb{E}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r+1)}})\right).

To complete the argument, we observe that Equation (

holds true, and this same fact also applies to all the modified matrices described in the paragraph following Equation (

\displaystyle{\mathfrak{W}}_{r,\malp}^{v}\left(\frac{\alpha}{\malp}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}\left({\mathbb{E}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r)}})-{\mathbb{E}}(\alpha^{T_{v}^{(r+1)}})\right)

3.2
From words to permutations

This section is devoted to proving that for any two patterns
v
and
w
in
{\mathcal{S}}_{d}
, (
1
) implies that
v
and
w
are strongly c-Wilf-equivalent in permutations. To accomplish this, we rely on a lemma that allows us to extend the result of Theorem
1.3
to permutations. This lemma was previously presented in
[
17
]
within the context of classical pattern containment. However, we include it here for completeness, even though the proof is identical.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
v
be any pattern. Then, for
r\in{\mathbb{N}}_{0},

\displaystyle g_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{S}}_{n})=\sum_{\malp=1}^{n}(-1)^{n-\malp}\binom{n}{\malp}g_{r}^{v}([\malp]^{n}).

(36)

Proof of Theorem
3.2
-(a).

We define
{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}({\mathcal{A}})
as the set of all words of length
n
whose distinct set of letters is exactly
{\mathcal{A}}
. Formally,

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}({\mathcal{A}}):={\mathcal{A}}^{n}\setminus\left(\cup_{e\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n}\right)\quad\mbox{where}\quad{\mathcal{A}}_{e}:={\mathcal{A}}\setminus\{e\}.

Recall equation (
1
) and express it using the notation introduced above as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{Y}}_{n}({\mathcal{A}}))={\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}^{n})\setminus\left(\cup_{e\in{\mathcal{A}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n})\right).

(37)

Furthermore, for any non-empty subset
{\mathcal{J}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}
, we have

\displaystyle\cup_{e\in{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n})={\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}(\cup_{e\in{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n}),\quad\mbox{and}\quad\cap_{e\in{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n})=\cap_{e\in{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathcal{G}}_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n}).

Using the inclusion-exclusion principle on equation (
37
), we get:

\displaystyle g_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{Y}}_{n}({\mathcal{A}}))=g_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}^{n})-\sum_{{\mathcal{J}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}}(-1)^{n-\#{\mathcal{J}}}g_{r}^{v}(\cap_{e\in{\mathcal{J}}}{\mathcal{A}}_{e}^{n})

\displaystyle=\sum_{{\mathcal{J}}\subset{\mathcal{A}}}(-1)^{\#{\mathcal{J}}}g_{r}^{v}(({\mathcal{A}}\setminus{\mathcal{J}})^{n}).

Since
g_{0}^{v}({\mathcal{A}}^{n})=g_{0}^{v}({\mathcal{B}}^{n})
for any
{\mathcal{A}},{\mathcal{B}}\subset[n]
where
{\mathcal{A}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
have the same size, we have

\displaystyle g_{r}^{v}({\mathcal{Y}}_{n}([a]))=g_{r}^{v}([a]^{n})+\sum_{\malp=1}^{a-1}(-1)^{\malp}\binom{a}{\malp}g_{r}^{v}([a-\malp]^{n}),

for each
a\in{\mathbb{N}}
. Finally, note that
{\mathcal{Y}}_{n}([n])={\mathcal{S}}_{n}
. This completes the proof of (
36
).
∎

An immediate consequence of this lemma is the following corollary.

Corollary 3.3
.

(strongly) c-Wilf-equivalence in words implies (strongly) c-Wilf-equivalence in permutations.

The result follows from combining Theorem
1.3
with Corollary
3.3
.
