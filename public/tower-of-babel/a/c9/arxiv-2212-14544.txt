Real roots of random orthogonal polynomials with exponential weights
Yen Do, Doron Lubinsky, Hoi H. Nguyen, Oanh Nguyen, and Igor Pritsker

Abstract
. We consider random orthonormal polynomials P n ​ ( x ) = ∑ i = 0 n ξ i ​ p i ​ ( x ) , P_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\xi_{i}p_{i}(x), where ξ 0 \xi_{0} , . . . , ξ n \xi_{n} are independent random variables with zero mean, unit variance and uniformly bounded ( 2 + ε 0 ) (2+\varepsilon_{0}) -moments, and { p n } n = 0 ∞ \{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty} is the system of orthonormal polynomials with respect to a general exponential weight W W on the real line. This class of orthogonal polynomials includes the popular Hermite and Freud polynomials. We establish universality for the leading asymptotics of the expected number of real roots of P n P_{n} , both globally and locally. In addition, we find an almost sure limit of the measures counting all roots of P n . P_{n}. This is accomplished by introducing new ideas on applications of the inverse Littlewood-Offord theory in the context of the classical three term recurrence relation for orthogonal polynomials to establish anti-concentration properties, and by adapting the universality methods to the weighted random orthogonal polynomials of the form W ​ P n . WP_{n}.

Introduction and main results

The study of roots of random polynomials has a long history which can traced back to the fundamental papers by Bloch and Polya in 1932
[

, consider random polynomials of the form

P_{n}(x)=\xi_{0}p_{0}(x)+\xi_{1}p_{1}(x)+....+\xi_{n}p_{n}(x)

•

Random algebraic polynomials, which correspond to

•

Random trigonometric polynomials, which correspond to
p_{n}=a_{n}\sin(nx)
and

,

•

Random orthogonal polynomials, which correspond to an orthonormal basis of polynomials

associated with a given measure.

In this paper, we focus on random orthogonal polynomials. Let
\mu
be a finite positive measure on the
real line
with finite moments of all orders, and with the associated orthonormal system of polynomials
\{p_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
. In other words, each
p_{n}
has degree
n
, real coefficients, and a positive leading coefficient, and furthermore

\int_{\mathbb{R}}p_{n}(x)p_{m}(x)d\mu(x)=\begin{cases}1,&m=n;\\
0,&m\neq n;\end{cases}

see, e.g., Freud
[
14
]
for details.
Assume furthermore that
\mu
is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure, that is
d\mu(x)=w(x)dx
, where
w
is referred to as the weight function. Some popular systems of orthogonal polynomials are

(E1)

Legendre polynomials: The weight
w(x)
is the indicator function of
[-1,1]
.

(E2)

Chebysev polynomials of type 1:
w(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^{2}}}
for
x\in(-1,1)
, and zero elsewhere.

(E3)

Chebysev polynomials of type 2:
w(x)=\sqrt{1-x^{2}}
for
x\in(-1,1)
, and zero elsewhere.

(E4)

Jacobi polynomials: These are generalizations of Chebyshev and Legendre polynomials, with
w(x)=(1-x)^{\alpha}(1+x)^{\beta}
for
x\in(-1,1)
and zero elsewhere, where
\alpha,\beta>-1
.

(E5)

Hermite polynomials:
w(x)=e^{-x^{2}}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
.

(E6)

Freud polynomials: These are generalizations of Hermite polynomials, with
w(x)=e^{-c|x|^{\lambda}}
for
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
c>0,\lambda>0
.

One of the most basic questions in the study of roots is the average number of real roots of
P_{n}
. In
[
19
]
, Kac derived the celebrated Kac-Rice formula that allows us to reduce this task to evaluating an integral, which is often most manageable when the random variables
\xi_{i}
are standard Gaussian.
Let
N_{n}(S)
be the number of roots of
P_{n}
in a set
S
. In this Gaussian setting, the average number of real roots of
P_{n}
has been known for the following models.

•

Random Legendre polynomials: Das
[
5
]
found that
\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(-1,1)]
is asymptotically equal to
n/\sqrt{3}
. Wilkins
[
39
]
improved the error term in this asymptotic relation by showing that
\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(-1,1)]=n/\sqrt{3}+o(n^{\varepsilon})
for any
\varepsilon>0
;

•

Random Jacobi polynomials: Das and Bhatt
[
6
]
concluded that
\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(-1,1)]
is also asymptotically equal to
n/\sqrt{3}
;

•

More generally, when
\mu
a finite Borel measure with
compact support
on the real line. Lubinsky–Pritsker–Xie
[
27
]
showed that under mild conditions on the weight
d\mu/dx
, the same asymptotic holds.

•

Beyond compactly supported measures, Pritsker–Xie
[
33
]
extended this asymptotic to the random Freud polynomials. Later, Lubinsky–Pritsker–Xie
[
28
]
showed that the same asymptotic holds for general exponential weights (see Theorem
1.2
below).

Little is known about the average number of roots when the coefficients are not Gaussian. In
[
10
]
, Do–Nguyen–Vu extended the asymptotics for the model in
[
27
]
to general random variables with mean 0, variance 1, and bounded
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moments for some
\varepsilon_{0}>0
. In other words, under some conditions, the asymptotic of
\mathbb{E}N_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is known when the measures
\mu
are compactly supported, such as the measures in Examples (E1)–(E4) above.

This paper is devoted to random orthogonal polynomials associated with exponential weights on the whole real line, such as random polynomials spanned by Hermite and Freud polynomials mentioned in (E5) and (E6). In particular, we show that the results of
[
28
]
hold for general random coefficients
\xi_{i}
. We shall assume throughout the paper that the measure
\mu
is absolutely continuous, with
d\mu(x)=W^{2}(x)dx
, where the weight
W
belongs to the class
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
of exponential weights considered in
[
22
]
and
[
28
]
.

Definition 1.1

(Weight class
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
)
.

Let
W=e^{-Q}
, where
Q:\mathbb{R}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
satisfies the following conditions:

(a)
Q^{\prime}
is continuous in
\mathbb{R}
and
Q(0)=0
.

(b)
Q^{\prime}
is non-decreasing in
\mathbb{R}
, and
Q^{\prime\prime}
exists in
\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
.

(c)

\displaystyle\lim_{|t|\rightarrow\infty}Q(t)=\infty.

(d) The function

T(t)=\frac{tQ^{\prime}(t)}{Q(t)},\ t\neq 0,

is quasi-increasing in
(0,\infty)
, in the sense that for some
C_{1}>0
,

0<x<y\Rightarrow T(x)\leq C_{1}T(y).

We assume an analogous restriction for
y<x<0
. In addition, we assume that for some
\Lambda>1
,

T(t)\geq\Lambda\text{ in }\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

(e) There exists
C_{2}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{Q^{\prime\prime}(x)}{|Q^{\prime}(x)|}\leq C_{2}\frac{|Q^{\prime}(x)|}{Q(x)},\ x\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}.

Then we write
W\in\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
.

It is straightforward to check that the Freud weights
w(x)=e^{-c|x|^{\lambda}}
of (E6) belong to
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
when
\lambda>1.

From the definition,
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
consists of smooth functions
e^{-Q}
, where
Q
is convex, and has regular growth behavior at infinity.
We note that
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
is one of the largest known classes of weights that allow for a very complete theory of the associated orthogonal polynomials,
see
[
22
]
for a comprehensive exposition.

We recall the result of
[
28
]
on the asymptotic for
\mathbb{E}N_{n}(\mathbb{R})
where
N_{n}(\mathbb{R})
is the number of real roots of the random polynomial

P_{n}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\xi_{i}p_{i}(x).

Theorem 1.2

(Gaussian setting, cf. Theorem 2.2 of
[
28
]
)
.

Let
W=e^{-Q}\in\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
, where
Q
is even. If the function
T
in the definition of
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
satisfies

(1.1)

\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}T(x)=\alpha,

where
\alpha\in(1,\infty]
, then the expected number of real zeros of
P_{n}
, with
\{\xi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{\infty}
being i.i.d. standard Gaussian, satisfies

(1.2)

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}[N_{n}(\mathbb{R})]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}.

To state the result on the local asymptotics for the expected number of real zeros, we first define the Ullman distribution that provides the limiting density for the number of real roots.
Following
[
22
]
and
[
35
]
, we define the Ullman distribution
\mu_{\alpha},\ \alpha>1,
by

(1.3)

d\mu_{\alpha}(x)=\left(\frac{\alpha}{\pi}\int_{|x|}^{1}\frac{t^{\alpha-1}}{\sqrt{t^{2}-x^{2}}}\,dt\right)dx,\quad x\in[-1,1],

and we consider the following contracted version of
P_{n}

(1.4)

\displaystyle P_{n}^{*}(s):=P_{n}(a_{n}s),\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

where
a_{n}
is the Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff number associated with the weight
W
, see
[
22
]
and
[
35
]
.

Roughly speaking, most of the real roots of
P_{n}^{*}
stay in
[-1,1]
. For any set
E\subset\mathbb{C}
,
N_{n}^{*}(E)
denotes the number of zeros of a random polynomial
P_{n}^{*}
located in
E
. We now state the local result on the asymptotic of
\mathbb{E}[N_{n}^{*}\left([a,b]\right)]
for intervals
[a,b]\subset(-1,1)
. It says that
\mu_{\alpha}
is the limiting measure, up to a constant.

Theorem 1.3

(Gaussian setting, see Theorem 2.3 in
[
28
]
)
.

Assume that
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
are i.i.d. standard Gaussian. Let
W=e^{-Q}\in\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
, where
Q
is even. Suppose that the function
T
in the definition of
\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
satisfies (
1.1
). For any
[a,b]\subset(-1,1)
, we have

(1.5)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{n}\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}^{*}\left([a,b]\right)\right]=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\mu_{\alpha}([a,b]).

It is worth noting that condition (
1.1
) plays a crucial role in the existence of the limit in (
1.5
). In fact, if the weight function
W
does not behave at infinity in a regular way,
by for example oscillating between the values of
e^{-c|x|^{\lambda_{1}}}
and
e^{-c|x|^{\lambda_{2}}}
for
x
near infinity, with
\lambda_{1}>\lambda_{2}>1,
then the limit in (

) may fail to exist. This indicates that the local asymptotic behavior of
\mathbb{E}\left[N_{n}^{*}\left([a,b]\right)\right]
may not abide a single limiting distribution, and may differ dramatically when the interval
[a,b]
is shifted.

(a)

(b)

2.
The method of proof and universality of the correlations

To establish Theorem
1.4
, we apply the universality framework of
[
31
]
. This approach was earlier developed in Tao–Vu
[
38
]
, Do–Nguyen–Vu
[
9
]
, and it now becomes one of the standard methods in the field. The global idea is to compare the distribution of roots of
P_{n}^{*}
with that of the Gaussian counterpart, namely

\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}(x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\tilde{\xi}_{k}p_{k}(a_{n}x)

where
\tilde{\xi}_{k}
are iid standard Gaussian. Generally speaking, if none of the
p_{k}
dominates the sum (see the Delocalization Condition C3 below), then the effect of changing the distribution of the coefficients is small and so the roots of
P_{n}^{*}
have roughly the same distribution as those of
\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}
. For technical reasons, and in particular to tame the growth of the orthogonal polynomials
p_{k}
, we shall work with the random functions

F_{n}(x)=P_{n}^{*}(x)W(a_{n}x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}p_{k}(a_{n}x)W(a_{n}x)=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\xi_{k}f_{k}(x),

where
f_{k}(x)=p_{k}(a_{n}x)W(a_{n}x),\ k=1,\ldots,n.
Since
W(x)\neq 0,\ x\in\mathbb{R},
the real zeros of
F_{n}
and
P_{n}^{*}
are clearly identical. Further, we need an extension of the weight
W
into the complex plane, which is convenient to obtain via the potential theoretic argument below. Following
[
22
]
and
[
35
]
, we introduce the Mhaskar-Rakhmanov-Saff interval
\Delta_{n}:=[-a_{n},a_{n}]
. The equilibrium density (or the density of the weighted equilibrium measure) is defined as

\sigma_{n}(x)=\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{(x-a_{-n})(a_{n}-x)}}{\pi^{2}}\int_{a_{-n}}^{a_{n}}\frac{Q^{\prime}(s)-Q^{\prime}(x)}{s-x}\frac{ds}{\sqrt{(s-a_{-n})(a_{n}-s)}},\,x\in\Delta_{n}.

It satisfies the equilibrium equation
[
22
, p. 41]
:

(2.1)

\displaystyle\int_{a_{-n}}^{a_{n}}\log\frac{1}{|x-t|}\sigma_{n}(t)\,dt+Q(x)=M,\quad x\in\Delta_{n}.

Note that the weighted equilibrium measure
\sigma_{n}(x)\,dx
is supported on
\Delta_{n}
, and has total mass
n
:

\int_{a_{-n}}^{a_{n}}\sigma_{n}(x)\,dx=n.

For details on
\sigma_{n}
, we refer to the book
[
22
]
. We now set

(2.2)

\displaystyle Q(z):=M-U^{\mu_{n}}(z)\ \mbox{and}\ W(z):=\exp\left(-Q(z)\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus[-a_{n},a_{n}],

where
U^{\mu_{n}}(x)
is the logarithmic potential of
\mu_{n}:

U^{\mu_{n}}(x):=-\int_{a_{-n}}^{a_{n}}\log|x-t|\sigma_{n}(t)\,dt.

This gives the desired extension of
W
into
\mathbb{C}
,
with
Q
being harmonic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus[-a_{n},a_{n}]
and continuous in
\mathbb{C},
cf.
[
22
]
and
[
35
]
.

The exact conditions for the applicability of the universality method are stated below. For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, we shall show that there exists a constant
b>0
such that for any positive constants
c_{1},A
, there exists
C_{1}>0
for which the following conditions hold for the interval
D(\varepsilon)=[-1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

(C1)

Boundedness:
For any
z\in D(\varepsilon)
, with probability at least
1-C_{1}n^{-A}
,
|F_{n}(w)|\leq\exp(n^{c_{1}})
for all
w\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(z,1/n)
.

(C2)

Delocalization:
For every
z\in D(\varepsilon)+B_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1/n)
, for every
k=0,\dots,n
,

\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(a_{n}z)|^{2}}}\leq C_{1}n^{-b}.

(C3)

Derivative growth:
For any
s\in D(\varepsilon)
,

\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}^{\prime}(a_{n}s)|^{2}a_{n}^{2}\leq C_{1}n^{2+c_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(a_{n}s)|^{2},

\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}^{\prime\prime}(a_{n}z)|^{2}a_{n}^{4}\leq C_{1}n^{4+c_{1}}\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(a_{n}s)|^{2}.

(C4)

Anti-concentration:
For every
z\in D(\varepsilon)
, with probability at least
1-C_{1}n^{-A}
, there exists
w\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(z,1/n)
for which
|F_{n}(w)|\geq\exp(-n^{c_{1}})
.

The following key lemma shows that these conditions are satisfied by our
F_{n}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Assume the hypothesis of Theorem
1.4
. For any constant
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a constant
b>0
such that for any positive constants
c_{1},A
, there exists
C_{1}>0
for which Conditions (C1)–(C4) hold for the interval
D(\varepsilon)=[-1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
.

Assuming Lemma
2.1
, we have the following universal result of the correlations of real roots.

Theorem 2.2
.

Assume the hypothesis of Theorem
1.4
. Let
k
be any positive integer. Then there exist positive constants
C
and
c
independent of
n
such that the following holds.
For any real numbers
x_{1},\dots,x_{k}
, all of which are in
D(\varepsilon)
, and any function
G:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
supported on
\prod_{i=1}^{k}[x_{i}-c/n,x_{i}+c/n]
with continuous derivatives up to order
2k+4
and
||\triangledown^{m}G||_{\infty}\leq n^{a}
for all
0\leq m\leq 2k+4
, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum G\left(\zeta_{i_{1}},\dots,\zeta_{i_{k}}\right)\bigg]-\mathbb{E}\bigg[\sum G\left(\tilde{\zeta}_{i_{1}},\dots,\tilde{\zeta}_{i_{k}}\right)\bigg]\right|\leq Cn^{-c},

where the first sum runs over all
k
-tuples
(\zeta_{i_{1}},\dots,\zeta_{i_{k}})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
of the roots
\zeta_{1},\zeta_{2},\dots
of
P^{*}_{n}
, and the second sum runs over all
k
-tuples
(\tilde{\zeta}_{i_{1}},\dots,\tilde{\zeta}_{i_{k}})\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
of the roots
\tilde{\zeta}_{1},\tilde{\zeta}_{2},\dots
of
\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}
.

This theorem is a generalization of
[
31
, Theorem 2.6]
. We provide a proof in Section
9
.

It is worth mentioning, by triangle inequality, that Theorem
2.2
also holds true if
\xi_{k}
and
\tilde{\xi}_{k}
are any random variables with mean 0, variance 1, and uniformly bounded
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
moments. In other words, it is not necessary that
\tilde{\xi}_{k}
are standard Gaussian. In Section
12
we will provide various formulas for the
k
-correlation function of the real roots of
P^{*}_{n}
(or of
P_{n}
) under the assumption that the random coefficients
\xi_{i}
have smooth density functions.

Using Theorem
2.2
, we derive an estimate for the number of real roots at a local scale, which immediately gives the second part of Theorem
1.4
by decomposing a big interval into intervals of length
O(1/n)
. It is convenient here to use the notation
N_{P_{n}^{*}}(E)
and
N_{\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}}(E)
for the number of roots of
P_{n}^{*}
and
\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}
respectively in a set
E
.

Theorem 2.3
.

Under the hypothesis of Theorem
1.4
, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there exists a positive constant
c
such that the following holds for any interval
[a,b]
of length
O(1/n)
inside
D(\varepsilon)
:

\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}^{*}}([a,b])]-\mathbb{E}[N_{\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}}([a,b])]\ll n^{-c}.

Outside of the set
D(\varepsilon)
, we show that the number of roots is negligible.

Theorem 2.4
.

Under the hypotheses of Theorem
1.4
, we have

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}^{*}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus D(\varepsilon))]}{n}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}}(\mathbb{R}\setminus D(\varepsilon))]}{n}=0.

Combining the last two theorems, we obtain that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{P_{n}^{*}}(\mathbb{R})]}{n}-\frac{\mathbb{E}[N_{\widetilde{P}_{n}^{*}}(\mathbb{R})]}{n}\right)=0.

This proves the first part of Theorem
1.4
, in view of Theorem
1.2
.

3.
Proof ideas and our contributions

As mentioned in the previous section, the global structure of our paper is to follow the universality framework that has been developed in
[
31
,
38
]
, and more recently in
[
10
]
for random orthogonal polynomials with compactly supported measures with a “nice” density function. However, handling exponential weights is a much more complicated task that requires new tools and ideas.

Firstly, the functions
F_{n}
whose universality property is of interest in previous papers were assumed to be analytic, which was important for several of their arguments such as the one using Halász’s inequality. Unfortunately, our function
F_{n}(x)=P_{n}(x)W(x)
is not analytic. We propose a novel way to circumvent this condition in Section
9
.

Secondly, the framework of universality requires to investigate the behavior of
F_{n}
in an open neighborhood of the real line in the complex plane. That means we need to extend and study the behavior of the weight function
W
on
\mathbb{C}
. This brings technical challenges that one did not have to deal with in the previous papers such as
[
28
]
.

Last but not least, one of the most challenging obstacles that we have to face is to show that the function
F_{n}(x)
cannot be too small on any interval
I
of length
1/n
inside the core interval
(-a_{n},a_{n})
; this property is known as the anti-concentration property. If
F_{n}(x)
were too small, the function would become sensitive to noise, and one would not expect universality. So, it is crucial to establish the anti-concentration. In particular, we want to show that with high probability, there exists
x\in I
such that

|F_{n}(x)|\geq\exp(-n^{\varepsilon})

for some small
\varepsilon>0
.

When
I
is very near the edge of
(-a_{n},a_{n})
(that is
|x|=\Theta(a_{n})
),
W(x)
can be as small as
\exp(-\Theta(n))
and hence the above inequality becomes

(3.1)

|P_{n}(x)|\geq\exp(\Theta(n^{1})).

This is in sharp contrast to the case when the measure
\mu
is compact in which one only needs to show

|P_{n}(x)|\geq\exp(-n^{\varepsilon}).

The difficulty here is that with the power
\varepsilon
for
n
, one can restrict to lower degree polynomials
p_{k}
for
k\ll n^{2\varepsilon}
and use classical tools like Remez inequality. This was one of the key ingredients in both
[
31
]
and
[
10
]
. Now, having the power 1 in (
3.1
), one can no longer disregard the high degree polynomials and use Remez inequality as before. It boils down to controlling
p_{k}(x)
for
k\approx n
and
|x|\approx a_{n}
. This is known to have erratic behavior and it may be possible that
p_{k}
is very tiny on the entire
I
which is a significant obstruction.

In this paper, we come up with a completely new argument that incorporates the inverse Littlewood-Offord theory, the polynomial recurrence structure of
p_{k}
, and Remez inequality. The general idea is that if
P_{n}
has a small anti-concentration probability, then
p_{k}
must exhibit an algebraic structure. We then show that it is impossible for
p_{k}
to have both algebraic structure and polynomial recurrence structure. We refer the details to Section
7
, where we also include a sketch of proof. We note that certain recurrence was also used in
[
38
, Section 13]
but our implementation here is completely orthogonal and different.

Finally, we obtain the
k
-correlation function formulas (in Section
12
) by adapting the method of
[
15
]
with some minimal modifications toward orthogonal polynomials.

Organization.
To prove Lemma
2.1
, in Sections
4
–
7
, we verify Conditions (C1)–(C4). In Section
8
, we prove Theorem
2.3
. Section
10
contains a proof of Theorem
1.6
. Theorem
2.4
is proven in Section
11
. We conclude with explicit formulas for
k
-correlation function in Section
12
.

Notations.
We use standard asymptotic notations under the assumption that
n
tends to infinity. For two positive sequences
(a_{n})
and
(b_{n})
, we say that
a_{n}\gg b_{n}
or
b_{n}\ll a_{n}
if there exists a constant
C
such that
b_{n}\leq Ca_{n}
. If
|c_{n}|\ll a_{n}
for some sequence
(c_{n})
, we also write
c_{n}\ll a_{n}
.

If
a_{n}\ll b_{n}\ll a_{n}
, we say that
b_{n}=\Theta(a_{n})
. If
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{b_{n}}=0
, we say that
a_{n}=o(b_{n})
. If
b_{n}\ll a_{n}
, we sometimes employ the notations
b_{n}=O(a_{n})
and
a_{n}=\Omega(b_{n})
to make the idea intuitively clearer or the writing less cumbersome; for example, if
A
is the quantity of interest, we may write
A=A^{\prime}+O(B)
instead of
A-A^{\prime}\ll B
, and
A=e^{O(B)}
instead of
\log A\ll B
. We also write that
a_{n}=O_{C}(b_{n})
if the implied constant depends on a given parameter
C
.

For the orthonormal polynomials
\{p_{j}(x)\}_{j=0}^{\infty}
, we define the
reproducing kernel
by

K_{n}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{j}(x)p_{j}(y),

and the differentiated kernels by

K_{n}^{(k,l)}(x,y)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{j}^{(k)}(x)p_{j}^{(l)}(y),\quad k,l\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{0\}.

It is apparent that the above Conditions (C1)–(C4) can be rephrased in term of reproducing kernels.

4.
Partial proof of Lemma
2.1
: Boundedness

In this section, we prove Condition (C1) as the first step in proving Lemma
2.1
.
Let

J_{n}(\varepsilon)=a_{n}D(\varepsilon)=[-(1-\varepsilon)a_{n},(1-\varepsilon)a_{n}].

Since
P_{n}
is spanned by the orthonormal basis
\{p_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
, we have that

\|WP_{n}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|P_{n}(x)|^{2}W^{2}(x)\,dx\right)^{1/2}=\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\xi_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

Applying the Nikolskii-type inequality (cf.
[
28
, p. 60]
), valid for any polynomial
Q_{n}
with complex coefficients of degree at most
n
,

\left\|WQ_{n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq Cn\left\|WQ_{n}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},

we obtain that

(4.1)

\displaystyle\left\|WP_{n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq Cn\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\xi_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2},\quad n\in\mathbb{N}.

It follows from Lemma
4.1
below (with
m=0
) that there is a constant
B>0
such that for all

n\in\mathbb{N},\ s\in D(\varepsilon)=[-1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon],
we have

(4.2)

\displaystyle W(a_{n}s)\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|P_{n}(a_{n}z)|\leq B\left\|WP_{n}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})}\leq Cn\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\xi_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}.

From Chebyshev’s inequality for random coefficients with finite
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moments,
we obtain that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{k}|>n^{(A+1)/2})=O\left(n^{-(A+1)}\right)
holds for any
A>0,\ k=0,1,\ldots,n.
Hence

\left(\sum_{k=0}^{n}|\xi_{k}|^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq n^{(A+1)/2}\sqrt{n+1}

and

W(a_{n}s)\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|P_{n}(a_{n}z)|\leq Cn^{2+A/2},\quad n\in\mathbb{N},\ s\in D(\varepsilon),

with probability at least
(1-cn^{-(A+1)})^{n+1}\geq 1-d/n^{A},\ d>0
. Since

\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|W(a_{n}z)|\leq CW(a_{n}s)

by our choice of the extension for
W
to the complex plane, we have that

\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|P_{n}(a_{n}z)W(a_{n}z)|\leq\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|P_{n}(a_{n}z)|\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|W(a_{n}z)|\leq Cn^{2+A/2}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N},\ s\in D(\varepsilon),
with probability at least
1-d/n^{A},\ d>0
. This verifies the boundedness condition (C1)
because
W(a_{n}z)P_{n}(a_{n}z)=F_{n}(z)
and
n^{2+A/2}=o\left(\exp(c_{1}n)\right)
for all
c_{1}>0.

We now state and prove the following lemma about growth of weighted polynomials and their derivatives in the complex plane. It was already used
in the above proof, namely in (
4.2
), and will also be very useful in the other sections.

Lemma 4.1
.

For all
n\in\mathbb{N},\ s\in D_{n}(\varepsilon),
and polynomials
R_{n},\ \deg(R_{n})\leq n,
there is a constant
B>0
such that

W(a_{n}s)\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|R_{n}^{(m)}(a_{n}z)|\leq B\left(\frac{n}{a_{n}}\right)^{m}\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|W(a_{n}t)R_{n}(a_{n}t)|,\quad m=0,1,2,\ldots.

Proof of Lemma
4.1
.

We begin with a general estimate for the growth of an arbitrary polynomial
R_{n},\ \deg(R_{n})\leq n,
in the complex plane in terms of its weighted supremum norm on
\Delta_{n}=[-a_{n},a_{n}].
Recall that the potential
U^{\mu_{n}}(x):=-\int_{-a_{n}}^{a_{n}}\log|x-t|\sigma_{n}(t)\,dt
of the weighted equilibrium measure
\mu_{n}(x):=\sigma_{n}(x)\,dx
satisfies the equilibrium equation from (
2.1
)

U^{\mu_{n}}(x)+Q(x)=M,\quad x\in\Delta_{n}.

It is known that
U^{\mu_{n}}(x)
is superharmonic and continuous in
\mathbb{C}
, harmonic in
\mathbb{C}\setminus\Delta_{n}
, and behaves like
-n\log|x|
near infinity. Therefore the function

h(z):=\log|R_{n}(z)|-\log\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}+U^{\mu_{n}}(z)-M

is subharmonic in the domain
\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{n}
, and has the following boundary values

\displaystyle h(x)

\displaystyle=\log|R_{n}(x)|-\log\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}-Q(x)

\displaystyle=\log|R_{n}(x)W(x)|-\log\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}\leq 0,\quad x\in\Delta_{n}.

Appying the Maximum Principle for subharmonic functions, we conclude that
h(z)\leq 0
for all
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\setminus\Delta_{n}
, which is equivalent to

(4.3)

\displaystyle|R_{n}(z)|\leq\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}\exp\left(M-U^{\mu_{n}}(z)\right),\quad z\in\mathbb{C}.

This upper bound allows us to estimate the growth of
R_{n}
, provided we have appropriate estimates on the potential
U^{\mu_{n}}(z).
Required estimates follow from Lemma 5.10(a) of
[
22
, p. 130]
: There is a constant
C>0
such that

(4.4)

\displaystyle U^{\mu_{n}}(x+iy)-U^{\mu_{n}}(x)=O(1)

holds uniformly for
n\geq n_{0},\ x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon),
and
|y|\leq Ca_{n}/n.
Furthermore, for any pair
x_{1},x_{2}\in\Delta_{n}
such that
|x_{1}-x_{2}|\leq ca_{n}/n
, we obtain that

(4.5)

|U^{\mu_{n}}(x_{1})-U^{\mu_{n}}(x_{2})|=|Q(x_{1})-Q(x_{2})|\leq|x_{1}-x_{2}|\,\max_{x\in\Delta_{n}}|Q^{\prime}(x)|\leq c\frac{a_{n}Q^{\prime}(a_{n})}{n}=O(1),

where we used that
a_{n}Q^{\prime}(a_{n})=O(n)
by (3.7) and (3.11) of
[
28
]
. The latter estimate together with (
4.4
) implies that

U^{\mu_{n}}(z)=U^{\mu_{n}}(x)+O(1)=-Q(x)+O(1),\quad z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(x,ca_{n}/n),\ x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon),

where
c>0
is independent of
n,x,z
. Hence (
4.3
) now gives

(4.6)

\displaystyle|R_{n}(z)|

\displaystyle\leq C\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}\exp\left(M-U^{\mu_{n}}(x)\right)

\displaystyle=C\frac{\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}}{W(x)},\quad z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(x,ca_{n}/n),\ x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon).

Differentiating Cauchy’s integral formula, we deduce from (
4.6
) that

(4.7)

\displaystyle|R_{n}^{(m)}(z)|

\displaystyle=\left|\frac{m!}{2\pi i}\int_{|w-x|=2a_{n}/n}\frac{R_{n}(w)\,dw}{(w-z)^{m+1}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\frac{n}{a_{n}}\right)^{m}\frac{\|R_{n}W\|_{L^{\infty}(\Delta_{n})}}{W(x)},\quad z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(x,a_{n}/n),\ x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon).

The statement of Lemma
4.1
now follows by changing variable
x=a_{n}s
, while passing from
\Delta_{n}
to
[-1,1]
and from
J_{n}(\varepsilon)
to
D_{n}(\varepsilon)
.
∎

5.
Partial proof of Lemma
2.1
: Delocalization

In this section, we prove Condition (C2) as the next step in proving Lemma
2.1
.

Recall that
K_{n}(x,x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}p_{j}^{2}(x),
so that
|K_{n}(x,x)|=K_{n}(x,x),\ x\in\mathbb{R},

and
|K_{n}(w,w)|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(w)|^{2},\ w\in\mathbb{C}.
We also use the notation

J_{n}(\varepsilon)=a_{n}D(\varepsilon)=[-(1-\varepsilon)a_{n},(1-\varepsilon)a_{n}].

In order to prove the delocalization condition, we write

(5.1)

\displaystyle\frac{|p_{k}(w)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(w)|^{2}}}\leq\frac{|p_{k}(w)|}{\sqrt{|K_{n}(w,w)|}}=\frac{|p_{k}(w)|W(x)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(x,x)W^{2}(x)}}\left(\frac{K_{n}(x,x)}{|K_{n}(w,w)|}\right)^{1/2},

where we assume that
x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon)
and
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Our first goal is to find upper bounds for the orthonormal polynomials
p_{k}.

Applying Theorem 1.18 of
[
22
, p. 22]
, we obtain the estimate

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|W(a_{n}t)p_{k}(a_{n}t)|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|W(x)p_{k}(x)|\leq Ck^{1/6}a_{k}^{-1/3}\left(T(a_{k})/a_{k}\right)^{1/6},

that holds uniformly for all
k\geq 1.
Since
T
is bounded as a continuous function on
\mathbb{R}
with finite limit at
\infty
, cf. (
1.1
),
we have that

(5.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{t\in[-1,1]}|W(a_{n}t)p_{k}(a_{n}t)|\leq\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}|W(x)p_{k}(x)|=O\left(k^{1/6}a_{k}^{-1/2}\right),\quad k\in\mathbb{N}.

Combining the above estimate with Lemma
4.1
(for
m=0
), we arrive at

(5.3)

\displaystyle W(a_{n}s)\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|=O\left(k^{1/6}a_{k}^{-1/2}\right),\quad k\in\mathbb{N},

which holds uniformly for
s\in D(\varepsilon).

We now recall some facts about the reproducing kernel
K_{n}
. Theorem 1.25 of
[
22
, p. 26]
states that

\lim_{n\to\infty}K_{n}(x,x)W^{2}(x)=\sigma_{n}(x),\quad x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon),

where convergence is uniform in
J_{n}(\varepsilon)
. Further, Lemma 5.1(a) of
[
20
, p. 87]
gives that uniformly for
x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon)
,

C_{1}\frac{n}{a_{n}}\leq\sigma_{n}(x)\leq C_{2}\frac{n}{a_{n}}.

Hence we have that

(5.4)

C_{1}\frac{n}{a_{n}}\leq K_{n}(x,x)W^{2}(x)\leq C_{2}\frac{n}{a_{n}},\quad x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon),

which implies (after changing variable
x=a_{n}s
) by (
5.3
) that

(5.5)

\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|W(a_{n}s)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(a_{n}s,a_{n}s)W^{2}(a_{n}s)}}\leq C\frac{k^{1/6}}{n^{1/2}}\left(\frac{a_{n}}{a_{k}}\right)^{1/2},\quad s\in D(\varepsilon),\ z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n),

where the estimate holds uniformly in
z,s
and
k\leq n\in\mathbb{N}.
Suppose first that
k\leq n^{\tau},\ \tau\in(0,1).
Recall that
a_{n}
is an increasing sequence of positive numbers, and that Lemma 3.5(c) of
[
22
, p. 72]
states for a constant
C>0
:

(5.6)

\displaystyle 1\leq\frac{a_{n}}{a_{1}}\leq Cn^{1/\Lambda}\text{ for all }n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\Lambda>1
is from the Definition
1.1
of
W
. Hence (
5.5
) gives that

(5.7)

\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|W(a_{n}s)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(a_{n}s,a_{n}s)W^{2}(a_{n}s)}}\leq Cn^{(1/\Lambda-1)/2+\tau/6},\quad s\in D(\varepsilon),\ z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n),

uniformly for
k\leq n^{\tau}.
If
n^{\tau}\leq k\leq n
then we use that
K_{n}(x,x)\geq K_{k}(x,x)
, together with (
5.3
) and (
5.4
), and write instead of (
5.5
) that

(5.8)

\displaystyle\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|W(a_{n}s)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(a_{n}s,a_{n}s)W^{2}(a_{n}s)}}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|W(a_{n}s)}{\sqrt{K_{k}(a_{n}s,a_{n}s)W^{2}(a_{n}s)}}

\displaystyle\leq C\frac{k^{1/6}}{k^{1/2}}=Ck^{-1/3}\leq Cn^{-\tau/3},\quad s\in D(\varepsilon),\ z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n).

Selecting the value of
\tau>0
sufficiently small, so that
b:=\min(\tau/3,(1-1/\Lambda)/2-\tau/6)>0,
we obtain from (
5.7
)-(
5.8
) that

(5.9)

\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|W(a_{n}s)}{\sqrt{K_{n}(a_{n}s,a_{n}s)W^{2}(a_{n}s)}}\leq Cn^{-b},\quad s\in D(\varepsilon),\ z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n),

holds uniformly for all
k\leq n.

Lemma 8.3(a) of
[
26
]
states that uniformly for
u,v
in compact subsets of the complex plane, and
x\in J_{n}\left(\varepsilon\right),

we have as
n\rightarrow\infty,

(5.10)

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\frac{K_{n}\left(x+\frac{u}{\tilde{K}_{n}(x,x)},x+\frac{v}{\tilde{K}_{n}(x,x)}\right)}{K_{n}(x,x)}e^{-\frac{Q^{\prime}(x)}{\tilde{K}_{n}(x,x)}(u+v)}=\frac{\sin{\pi(v-u)}}{\pi(v-u)},

where
\tilde{K}_{n}(x,x):=K_{n}(x,x)W^{2}(x).
The original result (
5.10
) for the real parameters
u,v
was established in Theorem 1.2 of
[
21
]
.
Recall that

|Q^{\prime}(x)|\leq C\frac{n}{a_{n}}

uniformly in
x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon),
by Lemma 3.8(a) of
[
22
, p. 77]
. Combining the latter fact with (

), we obtain that

(5.11)

0<C_{1}\leq\frac{Q^{\prime}(x)}{\tilde{K}_{n}(x,x)}\leq C_{2}

holds uniformly for
x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon).
Thus (
5.10
) now gives that

\frac{K_{n}(x,x)}{|K_{n}(w,w)|}\leq C

uniformly for
x\in J_{n}(\varepsilon)
and
w\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(x,a_{n}/n)
. If we change variables

and

in the above estimate, and use the result
together with (

) in (

), then we arrive at the estimate

(5.12)

\frac{|p_{k}(a_{n}z)|}{\sqrt{\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(a_{n}z)|^{2}}}\leq Cn^{-b},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n,

uniformly for

and

Partial proof of Lemma

|v|<1

x=a_{n}s

\frac{\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}^{\prime\prime}(a_{n}z)|^{2}a_{n}^{4}}{\sum_{j=0}^{n}|p_{j}(a_{n}s)|^{2}}=a_{n}^{4}\,\frac{\displaystyle\sup_{z\in B_{\mathbb{C}}(s,1/n)}K_{n}^{(2,2)}(a_{n}z,a_{n}\bar{z})}{K_{n}(a_{n}s,a_{n}s)}=O(n^{4}).

We also used that the coefficients of the orthogonal polynomials

are real, to express the numerator in the above equation via the kernel

This completes verification of the derivative growth conditions with

.

7.
Final piece of proof of Lemma
2.1
: Anti-concentration

In this section, we prove Condition (C4) as the last step of proving Lemma
2.1
. In particular, we show that the minimum of
P_{n}^{*}
in a local ball is bounded away from 0 with high probability.
The key result of this section is

Lemma 7.1

(Anti-concentration)
.

For any positive constants
A,c
and
c_{1}
, the following holds: for every
x_{0}\in[-1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, with probability at least
1-O(n^{-A})
, there exists
x_{1}\in[x_{0}-c/n,x_{0}+c/n]
for which
|W(a_{n}x_{1})P_{n}^{*}(x_{1})|\geq\exp(-n^{c_{1}})
.

We will prove an actually stronger result below

Lemma 7.2
.

For any positive constants
A,c
and
c_{1}
, the following holds: for every
x_{0}\in[-1+\varepsilon,1-\varepsilon]
, there exists
x_{1}\in[x_{0}-c/n,x_{0}+c/n]
such that

\mathbb{P}\left(|W(a_{n}x_{1})P_{n}^{*}(x_{1})|\leq\exp(-n^{c_{1}})\right)<n^{-A}.

The rest of this section is devoted to the proof of this lemma. Before going into the detail, let us first provide a road map.

(1)

Step 0: Assume by contradiction that Lemma
7.2
fails.

(2)

Step 1: We recall the classical three-term recurrence for three consecutive
p_{m}
. From that, we can write
p_{m+k}
in terms of
p_{m}
and
p_{m-1}
which we call a jumped three-term recurrence (see Lemma
7.4
).

(3)

Step 2: From the inverse Littlewood-Offord theory, we know that for a given
x
, for the function
W(a_{n}x)P_{n}^{*}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\xi_{i}W(a_{n}x)p_{i}(a_{n}x)
to satisfy

\mathbb{P}(|W(a_{n}x)P_{n}^{*}(x)|\leq\exp(-n^{c_{1}}))\geq n^{-A},

it is necessary that the components
W(a_{n}x)p_{i}(a_{n}x)
are close to a general arithmetic progression. Roughly speaking, this means that they have a low linear-dimension. See Lemma
7.5
.

(4)

Step 3: From Step 2, we know that there are many non-trivial linear combinations of the
(W(a_{n}x)p_{i}(a_{n}x))
that are very small

\sum_{j=1}^{O(1)}W(a_{n}x)p_{i_{j}}(a_{n}x)\approx 0\text{ (which precisely means $\exp(-n^{c})$-close to 0}).

Using the three-term recurrence in Step 1 to write all of these
p_{i_{j}}
as combination of
p_{m}
and
p_{m-1}
, we obtain a so-called two-term degeneracy

W(a_{n}x)p_{m}(a_{n}x)\approx\Gamma(x)W(a_{n}x)p_{m-1}(a_{n}x)

for some low-degree fractional polynomial
\Gamma
. See Lemma
7.7
.

(5)

Step 4: Adapting the same strategy from Step 3 (using two-term degeneracy in place of three-term recurrence), we can reduce two-term degeneracy to (one-term) degeneracy. See Lemma
7.9
.

(6)

Step 5: Show that degeneracy is impossible as it reduces to a polynomial of low degree and large leading term taking small value on a large interval.

We shall now go into the details.

7.1.

The following lemma covers a classic fact concerning the three-term recurrence for

The polynomials

As

), we can find rational coefficients

\displaystyle W(a_{n}x)p_{n_{3}+1}(a_{n}x)-\sum_{\ell=2}^{r_{0}-2k}d_{\ell}W(a_{n}x)p_{n_{3}+\ell}(a_{n}x)=O(n^{O(1)}\beta),\quad x\in{\mathcal{E}}.

\displaystyle\quad\ W(a_{n}x)p_{n_{1}+j+1}(a_{n}x)

\displaystyle-\frac{\frac{A_{n_{1}+j}}{\prod_{i=n_{1}+j+1}^{n_{1}+j+r-1}A_{i}}x^{r-2}+\dots}{\frac{1}{\prod_{i=n_{1}+j+1}^{n_{1}+j+r-1}A_{i}}x^{r-1}+\dots}W(a_{n}x)p_{n_{1}+j}(a_{n}x)+O(n^{O(1)}\beta^{1-\varepsilon_{1}}).

∎

We follow ideas from
[
9
]

From Jensen’s inequality for the number of roots of

10.
Almost sure zero distribution results and consequences

The main goal of this section is to prove Theorem
1.6
. We start with several auxiliary facts on the asymptotic properties of random variables
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}.

Lemma 10.1
.

If
\{\xi_{n}\}_{n=0}^{\infty}
are random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{n}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C
for some constants
C,\varepsilon_{0}>0
and all
n=0,1,\dots
, then

(10.1)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}|\xi_{n}|^{1/n}\leq 1\quad\mbox{ a.s.}

and

(10.2)

\displaystyle\limsup_{n\to\infty}\left(\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|\xi_{k}|\right)^{1/n}\leq 1\quad\mbox{ a.s.}

Proof of Lemma
10.1
.

It is immediate from our assumptions that

x^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}\,\mathbb{P}(\{|\xi_{n}|>x\})\leq\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{n}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C.

Using the distribution function of
|\xi_{n}|
defined by
H_{n}(x)=\mathbb{P}(\{|\xi_{n}|\leq x\}),\ x\in\mathbb{R},
we estimate

\displaystyle 1-H_{n}(x)=\mathbb{P}(\{|\xi_{n}|>x\})\leq C/x^{2+\varepsilon_{0}},\quad x>0,\ n=0,1,\ldots.

Now (
10.1
) follows directly from Lemma 4.1 of
[
32
]
, see (4.1) there.

Let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the event of probability one in (
10.1
). On
{\mathcal{A}}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there is
n_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{N}
such that
|\xi_{n}|^{1/n}\leq 1+\varepsilon

for all
n\geq n_{\varepsilon}
by (
10.1
). Hence

\max_{0\leq k\leq n}|\xi_{k}|^{1/n}\leq\max\left(\max_{0\leq k\leq n_{\varepsilon}}|\xi_{k}|^{1/n},1+\varepsilon\right)\to 1+\varepsilon\quad\mbox{ as }n\rightarrow\infty,

and (
10.2
) follows by letting
\varepsilon\to 0.
∎

In the next two auxiliary lemmas, we also show that under our standard assumptions on the random variables, they cannot be too concentrated.

Lemma 10.2
.

Let
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
be independent random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}]=0,\textup{Var}[\xi_{k}]=1,
and
\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C

for some constants
C,\varepsilon_{0}>0
and all
k=0,1,\dots
. There exists a constant
a>0
depending only on
C
and
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for all
k
,

(10.3)

\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{k}|\leq a)\leq 1-a.

Moreover, there exists a constant
a^{\prime}>0
depending only on
C
and
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for all
k
and for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
,

(10.4)

\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{k}-x|\leq a^{\prime})\leq 1-a^{\prime}.

Proof.

To see (
10.3
), assume that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{k}|\geq a)<a
, then we obtain that

\displaystyle 1

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|^{2}]=\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|^{2}\textbf{1}_{|\xi_{k}|<a}]+\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|^{2}\textbf{1}_{|\xi_{k}|\geq a}]

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a^{2}+\left(\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|]^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{2/(2+\varepsilon_{0})}\left(\mathbb{P}(|\xi|\geq a)\right)^{\varepsilon_{0}/(2+\varepsilon_{0})}\text{ by H\"{o}lder's inequality}

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle a^{2}+C^{2/(2+\varepsilon_{0})}a^{\varepsilon_{0}/(2+\varepsilon_{0})}.

Since the latter upper bound converges to

as

, there must exist some
a>0
(independent of
k
) for which it is smaller than 1. This
contradiction shows that there is a sufficiently small
a>0
that satisfies (
10.3
).

Next, for (
10.4
), assume that
\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{k}-x|\leq a^{\prime})>1-a^{\prime}
then letting
\xi_{k}^{\prime}
be an independent copy of
\xi_{k}
, we have

(10.5)

\mathbb{P}(|\xi_{k}-\xi_{k}^{\prime}|\leq 2a^{\prime})\geq(1-a^{\prime})^{2}\geq 1-2a^{\prime}.

By applying the first part to the random variables
\frac{\xi_{k}-\xi_{k}^{\prime}}{\sqrt{2}}
which have mean 0, variance 1, and uniformly bounded
(2+\varepsilon_{0})
-moments, we obtain an
a^{\prime}
that violates (
10.5
), and hence satisfies (
10.4
).
∎

Lemma 10.3
.

If
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
are independent random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}]=0,\textup{Var}[\xi_{k}]=1,
and
\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C

for some constants
C,\varepsilon_{0}>0
and all
k=0,1,\dots
, then there is
b>0
such that

(10.6)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\max_{n-b\log{n}<k\leq n}|\xi_{k}|\right)^{1/n}\geq 1\quad\mbox{ a.s.}

Proof.

Let
s_{n}\leq n,\ n\in\mathbb{N},
be a sequence of natural numbers that will be specified later. Consider

\displaystyle M_{n}:=\max_{n-s_{n}<k\leq n}|\xi_{k}|.

The statement

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(M_{n}\right)^{1/n}\geq 1\quad\mbox{ a.s.}

is equivalent to

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{M_{n}\leq\lambda^{n}\mbox{ i.o.}\})=0

for all positive
\lambda<1.
The latter would follow from the first Borel-Cantelli Lemma, if we show that

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\{M_{n}\leq\lambda^{n}\})<\infty

for all positive
\lambda<1.

Since the variables
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{\infty}
are independent, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(\{M_{n}\leq\lambda^{n}\})=\prod_{k=1}^{s_{n}}\mathbb{P}(\{|\xi_{n-k}|\leq\lambda^{n}\}).

Using (
10.3
), for any
\lambda<1
we find
N=N(a,\lambda)\in\mathbb{N}
such that
\mathbb{P}(\{|\xi_{k}|\leq\lambda^{n}\})\leq 1-a
for all
n\geq N
and all
k=0,1,\dots.
This gives

\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\{M_{n}\leq\lambda^{n}\})\leq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-a)^{s_{n}}<\infty,

provided
(1-a)^{s_{n}}\leq 1/n^{2}
for large
n
. It suffices to take
s_{n}\geq(-2/\log(1-a))\log{n}
to satisfy the latter condition.
∎

We now state a companion result for the coefficients of our contracted random orthogonal polynomials

see (

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
\{\xi_{k}\}_{k=0}^{n}
be independent random variables such that
\mathbb{E}[\xi_{k}]=0,\textup{Var}[\xi_{k}]=1,
and
\mathbb{E}[|\xi_{k}|^{2+\varepsilon_{0}}]<C

for some constants
C,\varepsilon_{0}>0
and all
k=0,1,\dots
. Assume that
W=e^{-Q}\in\mathcal{F}(C^{2})
, where

is even, and the function

in the definition of

satisfies (
1.1
) with
\alpha\in(1,\infty)
. For the orthogonal polynomials

that
correspond to the weight

p_{k}(a_{n}x)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}b_{j,k}x^{j}=b_{k,k}\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-x_{j,k}),\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n.

If the random variables
c_{k,n}
are defined by

c_{k,n}:=\sum_{i=k}^{n}\xi_{i}b_{k,i},\quad k=0,1,\ldots,n,

then there is

such that

(10.7)

\displaystyle\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(\max_{n-b\log{n}<k\leq n}|c_{k,n}|\right)^{1/n}\geq 2\,e^{1/\alpha}\quad\mbox{ a.s.}

We follow the idea in
[
7
, Lemma 4.2]
.
Note that
b_{k,k}=a_{n}^{k}\gamma_{k}
, where
\gamma_{k}
is the leading coefficient of

. We have

(10.8)

\lim_{n\to\infty}|b_{n,n}|^{1/n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_{n}\gamma_{n}^{1/n}=2\,e^{1/\alpha}.

For every
\varepsilon>0
, it suffices to show that

(10.9)

\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\max_{n-b\log{n}<k\leq n}|c_{k,n}|\right)^{1/n}\leq 2e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon\text{ infinitely often}\right)=0.

Note that
|c_{k,n}|\leq(2e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon)^{n}
implies that
\xi_{k}b_{k,k}
lies in an interval of radius
(2e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon)^{n}
centered around
\sum_{i=k+1}^{n}\xi_{i}b_{k,i}
. Thus, by independence, for any event
A_{k,n}\in\sigma(\xi_{k+1},\dots,\xi_{n})
– the
\sigma

, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|c_{k,n}|^{1/n}\leq 2\,e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon\big|A_{k,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\xi_{k}b_{k,k}-x|\leq\left(2\,e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon\right)^{n}\right).

By (
10.8
), this implies that for sufficiently large

and

,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|c_{k,n}|^{1/n}\leq 2\,e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon\big|A_{k,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}\left(|\xi_{k}-x|\leq\left(1-\delta\right)^{n}\right)

where
\delta=\frac{\varepsilon}{2\,e^{1/\alpha}}
is a constant.

By (
10.4
), the last quantity is smaller than
1-a^{\prime}
for the same constant
a^{\prime}
as in (
10.4
), again for all sufficiently large

A_{k,n}=\{|c_{\ell,n}|^{1/n}\leq 2\,e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon,\forall\ell\in(k,n]\cap\mathbb{N}\}\in\sigma(\xi_{k+1},\dots,\xi_{n})

and
A_{n,n}
be the whole sample space, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left(\max_{n-b\log{n}<k\leq n}|c_{k,n}|\right)^{1/n}\leq 2\,e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\prod_{k=\lfloor n-b\log n\rfloor+1}^{n}\mathbb{P}\left(|c_{k,n}|\leq(2\,e^{1/\alpha}-\varepsilon)^{n}\big|A_{k,n}\right)

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle(1-a^{\prime})^{b\log n}\leq n^{-a^{\prime}b}.

By choosing

which is summable over

By the first Borel-Cantelli Lemma, this implies (

∎

We next rely on the Coarea formula
[
12
]
.

Lemma 12.5
.

Let
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a region. Let
u:B\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
be a Lipschitz function and
h:\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}^{1}
be an
L^{1}
-function. Then

\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\#\{{\mathbf{x}}\in B\,:\,u({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{y}}\}\,h({\mathbf{y}})\,d{\mathbf{y}}=\int\limits_{B}|\det J_{u}({\mathbf{x}})|\,h(u({\mathbf{x}}))\,d{\mathbf{x}},

where
J_{u}({\mathbf{x}})
is the Jacobian matrix of
u({\mathbf{x}})
.

Proof of Theorem
12.1
.

Let
B_{1},\dots,B_{k}
be a family of mutually disjoint Borel subsets in
\mathbb{R}
and let
B=B_{1}\times\dots\times B_{k}
. By the Coarea formula and by Fubini theorem we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\,\left[\prod_{i=1}^{k}N_{n}(B_{i})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\,\#\Big\{{\mathbf{x}}\in B\,:\,\eta({\mathbf{x}})=(\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{k-1})\Big\}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\,\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{k}}\,\#\Big\{{\mathbf{x}}\in B\,:\,\eta({\mathbf{x}})={\mathbf{y}}\Big\}f_{0}(y_{0})\dots f_{k-1}(y_{k-1})\,d{\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\int\limits_{B}\mathbb{E}\,|\det(J_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}({\mathbf{x}}))|\,f_{0}(\eta_{0}({\mathbf{x}}))\dots f_{k-1}(\eta_{k-1}({\mathbf{x}}))\,d{\mathbf{x}},

where we recall that
f_{0},\dots,f_{k-1}
are the density functions of
\xi_{0},\dots,\xi_{k-1}
respectively.
∎

Proof of Theorem
12.2
.

Note that by Theorem
12.1
,

(12.3)

\displaystyle\rho_{k}({\mathbf{x}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{m=0}^{k-1}\gamma_{m}^{-1}\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}|x_{j}-x_{i}|^{-1}

\displaystyle\times\int\limits_{\mathbb{R}^{n-k+1}}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\left|\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}p_{j}^{\prime}(x_{i})\right|\prod_{i=0}^{n}f_{i}(a_{i})\,da_{k}da_{k+1}\dots da_{n},

where
(a_{0},\dots,a_{k-1})^{T}=-V^{-1}({\mathbf{x}})(\sum_{j=k}^{n}a_{j}p_{j}(x_{1}),\dots,\sum_{j=k}^{n}a_{j}p_{j}(x_{k}))^{T}
.

This means that
x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k}
are zeros of the polynomial
\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}p_{i}(x)
. Hence there exists a unique polynomial
\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}b_{j}x^{j}
such that

\displaystyle P(x)=\sum_{i=0}^{n}a_{i}p_{i}(x)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}(x-x_{i})\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}b_{j}x^{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{k-j}\sigma_{k-j}({\mathbf{x}})x^{j}\bigg)\bigg(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}b_{j}x^{j}\bigg)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n}\big(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(-1)^{k-i+j}\sigma_{k-i+j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j}\big)x^{i}.

The variables
a_{0},\dots,a_{n}
are uniquely defined by
{\mathbf{x}}
and
b_{0},\dots,b_{n-k}
from the above equation, that for any
0\leq l\leq n
we have

a_{l}=\langle P(x),p_{l}(x)\rangle_{\mu}=\sum_{i=l}^{n}(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(-1)^{k-i+j}\sigma_{k-i+j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{i},p_{l}(x)\rangle_{\mu}

where we note that
\langle x^{i},p_{l}(x)\rangle_{\mu}=0
if
i\leq l-1
.
In particularly,

\displaystyle a_{k}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k}^{n}(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(-1)^{k-i+j}\sigma_{k-i+j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{i},p_{k}(x)\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(-1)^{j}\sigma_{j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{k},p_{k}(x)\rangle_{\mu}+(\sum_{j=1}^{n-k}(-1)^{j-1}\sigma_{j-1}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{k+1},p_{k}(x)\rangle_{\mu}+\dots

\displaystyle+(\sum_{j=n-k}^{n-k}(-1)^{k-n+j}\sigma_{k-n+j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{n},p_{k}(x)\rangle_{\mu}

\displaystyle=b_{0}\langle x^{k},p_{k}(x)\rangle_{\mu}+b_{1}c_{k,1}+\dots+b_{n-k}c_{k,n-k},

for some numbers
c_{k,1},\dots,c_{k,n-k}
independently of the
b_{i}
.

More generally, for any
0\leq l\leq n-k

\displaystyle a_{k+l}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=k+l}^{n}(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}(-1)^{k-i+j}\sigma_{k-i+j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{i},p_{k+l}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=(\sum_{j=l}^{n-k}(-1)^{j-l}\sigma_{j-l}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{k+l},p_{k+l}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle+(\sum_{j=l+1}^{n-k}(-1)^{j-l-1}\sigma_{j-l-1}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{k+l+1},p_{k+l}(x)\rangle+\dots+

\displaystyle+(\sum_{j=n-k}^{n-k}(-1)^{k-n+j}\sigma_{k-n+j}({\mathbf{x}})b_{j})\langle x^{n},p_{k+l}(x)\rangle

\displaystyle=b_{l}\langle x^{k+l},p_{k+l}(x)\rangle+b_{l+1}c_{k+l,1}+\dots+b_{n-k}c_{k+l,n-k-l}

for some numbers
c_{k+l,1},\dots,c_{k,n-k-l}
independently of the
b_{i}
.

Thus the Jacobian of the substitution of
(a_{k},\dots,a_{n})
by
(b_{0},\dots,b_{n-k})
is a lower triangle matrix with diagonal
\diag(\langle x^{k},p_{k}(x)\rangle_{\mu},\dots,\langle x^{n},p_{n}(x)\rangle_{\mu})=\diag(\gamma_{k}^{-1},\dots,\gamma_{n}^{-1})
. Hence

(12.4)

da_{k}\dots da_{n}=\prod_{j=k}^{n}\gamma_{j}^{-1}db_{0}\dots db_{n-k}.

Finally, by differentiating
P(x)=\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}p_{j}(x)=\prod_{j=1}^{k}(x-x_{j})\left(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}b_{j}x^{j}\right)
at the point
x_{i}
we get

\sum_{j=0}^{n}a_{j}p^{\prime}(x_{i})=\prod_{j\in\{1,\dots,k\}\backslash\{i\}}(x_{j}-x_{i})(\sum_{j=0}^{n-k}b_{j}x_{i}^{j}),\quad i=1,\dots k.

Substitute the above and (
12.4
) into (
12.3
) we obtain the claim.
∎
