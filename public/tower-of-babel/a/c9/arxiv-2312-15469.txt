Efficient Estimation of the Central Mean Subspace via Smoothed Gradient Outer Products
Gan Yuan, Mingyue Xu, Samory Kpotufe, Daniel Hsu

Abstract
We consider the problem of sufficient dimension reduction (SDR) for multi-index models. The estimators of the central mean subspace in prior works either have slow (non-parametric) convergence rates, or rely on stringent distributional conditions (e.g., elliptical symmetric covariate distribution P 𝑿 P_{\boldsymbol{X}} ). In this paper, we show that a fast parametric convergence rate of form C d ⋅ n − 1 / 2 C_{d}\cdot n^{-1/2} is achievable via estimating the expected smoothed gradient outer product , for a general class of distribution P 𝑿 P_{\boldsymbol{X}} that admits Gaussian or heavier distributions. When the link function is a polynomial with a degree of at most r r and P 𝑿 P_{\boldsymbol{X}} is the standard Gaussian, we show that the prefactor depends on the ambient dimension d d as C d ∝ d r C_{d}\propto d^{r} .

Introduction

We consider a regression model for data
(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
in which the regression function
g(\boldsymbol{x})=\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}=\boldsymbol{x}]
has the form

g(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}\boldsymbol{x})

for some unknown matrix
\boldsymbol{U}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
of rank
k
and some unknown function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
.
The goal of this work is to provide computationally tractable methods for estimating the column space of
\boldsymbol{U}
, denoted
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
, from a finite sample of
(\boldsymbol{X},Y)
.

The model described above is called a
multi-index model
in the literature, and
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
is referred to as the
index space
.
The index space is also known as the
central mean subspace
in the context of sufficient dimension reduction
[
9
,
11
]
under mild identifiability conditions on
\supp(\boldsymbol{X})
1
1

1

See
Section

2.2
for a discussion of these conditions.
.
An accurate estimate of the index space has many potential applications, including the following:

•

The index space has, in the context of regression, an explanatory role analogous to that of the leading principal components of the data.
In particular, it reveals which (linear combinations of) covariates are able to best predict the response
Y
.

•

If
k\ll d
, then the achievable rates for estimating the regression function
g
may be greatly improved by first projecting the covariates
\boldsymbol{x}
to the index space before performing estimation.
For instance, if
g
is only known to be Lipschitz, then we may hope to improve the rate of estimation from
n^{-1/(d+2)}
to
n^{-1/(k+2)}
.

The problem of estimating the index space has received much attention, but important gaps remain in terms of what approximation error rates are achievable by computationally tractable estimators.
Results may be cataloged into two main directions, one emphasizing fast convergence rates
2

2

Under suitable subspace approximation measures, e.g., angles between spaces (see
Section

2.2
).
but requiring strict distributional conditions, the other preferring general distributional settings but admitting significantly worse convergence rates.
For example, on one hand, so-called
inverse regression
approaches—which estimate the index space via functionals of
\E[\boldsymbol{X}\mid Y]
—often admit fast convergence rates of the form
O(n^{-1/2})
however at the cost of restrictive conditions tying the marginal distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
to
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
; furthermore, outside of these conditions on
P_{X}
, only a strict subspace of
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
may be recovered. On the other hand,
forward regression
methods—which leverage functionals of the regression function
g:=\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
,
e.g., its derivatives—can ensure full recovery of the index space, importantly without un-naturally forcing a relation between
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
; however such milder distributional conditions often come at the cost of significantly slower estimation rates, often of a nonparametric form
n^{-1/\Omega(k)}\gg n^{-1/2}
.

Our main contribution is to elucidate the following middle-ground: we show that fast rates of the form
C_{d}\cdot n^{-1/2}
remain possible for a reweighted variant of forward methods, for a prefactor
C_{d}
depending on data distribution.
While our distributional conditions—knowledge of the marginal distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and certain moment assumptions—are stricter than for some forward regression methods, our rate guarantees are of a parametric form even for nonparametric link
g
, similar to usual convergence rates for inverse regression methods. Yet, unlike inverse regression methods, we guarantee full recovery of the index space
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
under qualitatively milder distributional conditions, where in particular, we impose no condition on the relation between
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and the unknown index space
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
as is common in that literature.

Our result relies on estimating outer products of a
smoothed
version of
\nabla f
, where the smoothing itself—induced by careful reweighting of the training sample—leverages a first-order Stein’s Lemma to guarantee full recovery of
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
.
Similar to other existing analyses, we use the knowledge of
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
to properly reweight the training sample.

To better situate our results, we expand next on the state-of-the-art on the subject, followed by a more detailed overview of the results.

For ease of comparison, we first note that a prevalent approach in the literature, also adopted here, is to proceed by defining and estimating a population object
\boldsymbol{M}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
, whose column space estimates (part of)

\col(\boldsymbol{U})

\boldsymbol{X}\mid Y

Inverse regression methods

As the name suggests, the class of inverse regression methods leverage functionals of the inverse relation
\boldsymbol{X}\mid Y
that may reveal the index space or a subspace thereof.
Although these methods are initially designed to estimate the
central subspace
, they can also serve the purpose of estimating the central mean subspace when the covariates
\boldsymbol{X}
and the error
\varepsilon=Y-g(\boldsymbol{X})
are independent, under which case these two subspaces are identical.
For instance, Sliced Inverse Regression (SIR) by
[
23
]
estimates
\boldsymbol{M}=\text{Cov}[\E[\boldsymbol{X}\mid Y]]
and Sliced Average Variance Estimator (SAVE) by
[
10
]
estimates
\boldsymbol{M}=\E[(\boldsymbol{I}_{d}-\text{Cov}[\boldsymbol{X}\mid Y])^{2}]
.
By carefully estimating means or covariances over
slices
, i.e., partitions of the
Y
space, parametric convergence rates of the form
O(n^{-1/2})
may be guaranteed, albeit under restrictive distributional conditions.

For instance, SIR imposes a so-called
linear conditional mean
(LCM) condition (where
\E[\boldsymbol{X}\mid\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X}]
is linear in
\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X}
) in order to ensure
\col(\boldsymbol{M})\subset\col(\boldsymbol{U})
, i.e., in order to guarantee
partial
recovery of
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
; if in addition we impose a so-called
random conditional projection
(RCP) condition (requiring for all
v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
that
\E[\boldsymbol{v}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X}\mid Y]
is not constant as a r.v.) then one can ensure
\col(\boldsymbol{M})=\col(\boldsymbol{U})
. These distributional conditions, by restricting the relation between
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
and the index space
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
, rule out some rather benign settings: for instance, for
k=1
, it is well known that under Gaussian
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
, these conditions cannot hold for
even
link functions such as
f(z)=z^{2}
(also referred as phase retrieval problem). Similar examples of failure modes are discussed for SAVE in
Appendix

A
. Additional discussions and examples for non-exhaustiveness issues can be found in
[
16
]
and
[
21
]
.

Forward regression methods

In contrast to inverse regression methods, the class of forward regression methods leverage functionals of the regression function
\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
.
An important line of work relies on
Stein’s Lemma
in constructing
\boldsymbol{M}
.
The earliest work along this line by
[
4
]
considered a single-index model (
k=1
)
estimates the index space direction, say
\boldsymbol{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, via
\boldsymbol{M}=\E[Y\cdot\boldsymbol{X}]
: suppose that
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
were standard Gaussian, and assuming an additive noise model, then Stein’s Lemma implies that
\E[Y\cdot\boldsymbol{X}]=\E[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})]\cdot\boldsymbol{u}
.
For the general case
k>1
,
[
32
]
proposed an estimator based on a second-order Stein’s Lemma that is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent. Similar to our work, they assume the setting on known
P_{X}
, and admit a broad class of distributions including Gaussian and more heavy-tailed distributions.
However, their theoretical results relies on the non-singularity of the expected Hessian
\E[\nabla^{2}g(\boldsymbol{X})]
, again ruling out some apparently benign settings: for instance for
k=1
, and
\E X=0
, simple link functions such as
f(z)=z^{3}
are not admitted. In contrast, the approach analyzed here relies on a first-order Stein’s Lemma (
Lemma

8
) and thus imposes no condition on the Hessian or any other higher order derivatives of
g
.

Another class of forward regression methods are those that rely on functionals of the gradient of
g
.
In the simplest case of single index models (
k=1
), we may take
\boldsymbol{M}=\E[\nabla g(\boldsymbol{X})]=\E[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})]\cdot\boldsymbol{u}
, for an index direction
\boldsymbol{u}
; thus, provided
\E[f^{\prime}(\boldsymbol{u}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})]
does not vanish (similar to
[
4
]
discussed above),
[
18
]
designs an estimator of
\boldsymbol{u}
with convergence rate
n^{-1/2}
.
In the more general case
k\geq 1
, one may consider a so-called

expected gradient outer product
(EGOP), defined as
\boldsymbol{M}=\E[\nabla g(\boldsymbol{X})\nabla g(\boldsymbol{X})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}]=\boldsymbol{U}\E[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}]\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}
.
This approach is quite appealing as it holds that, under quite mild conditions (see
Section

2.2
), one can show that
\col(\boldsymbol{M})=\col(\boldsymbol{U})
. Thus, a number of estimators of the EGOP
\boldsymbol{M}
have been proposed over the years, e.g.,
[
28
,
31
,
30
,
27
]
.
However, the convergence guarantees available for these methods are, at best, of the nonparametric form
n^{-1/\Omega(k)}
, without additional assumptions on
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
.
For the restricted case of
k\leq 3
,
[
17
,
12
]
shows that the parametric rate
n^{-1/2}
may be achieved under known Gaussian noise and in a fixed design setting (under some conditions on the observed
\boldsymbol{X}
data itself).
We have no such restriction on the index space dimension
k
, and operate in the more general random design setting, however while assuming known
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
.

Finally, the popular MAVE approach of
[
31
]
, while still a forward regression method, differs considerably from all the above in that it does not explicitly define a surrogate object
\boldsymbol{M}
for
\boldsymbol{U}
; instead, it works by optimizing a suitable objective over all possible projections
\boldsymbol{U}
. MAVE is appealing in that, similar to EGOP, it can guarantee full recovery of the index space under mild conditions.
However, this also comes at the cost of a nonparametric rate of the form
n^{-1/\Omega(k)}
.

1.2
Detailed Contributions

We introduce an object called the
Expected Smoothed Gradient Outer Product (ESGOP)

\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
, which can be viewed as a surrogate for the EGOP.
The ESGOP is based on a
smoothed
version of the gradient
\nabla g(\boldsymbol{X})
, as well as a subtle change of distribution (from
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
to a Gaussian) that enables application of Stein’s inequality.

We first show that
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
is
exhaustive
in that
\col(\overline{\boldsymbol{M}})=\col(\boldsymbol{U})
(
Proposition

4
). The surrogate
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
can then be estimated from a finite sample from
P_{\boldsymbol{X},Y}
via a reweighting scheme; in particular, the estimation of the
smoothed
gradients may be interpreted as local linear estimator with a data-dependent kernel (
Remark

12
).

Our main result states that, for a general class of distributions
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
admitting Gaussians and heavier tail distributions, our estimator admits a parametric error rate of the form
C_{d}\cdot n^{-1/2}
.
Finally, to elucidate the leading constant
C_{d}
, we consider the special case of polynomial links
g
—which has received much recent interest—and show that it behaves as
d^{O(r)}
for polynomials of degree at most
r
(
Corollary

22
), in line with recent lower-bounds of
[
13
]
for certain classes of computationally tractable procedures.

2.2
Central Mean Subspace

Let
(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\sim P_{\boldsymbol{X},Y}
, with
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
the marginal distribution assumed to be known, and let
g(\boldsymbol{X}):=\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
denote the regression function.

Definition 1
(Mean dimension-reduction subspaces and central mean subspace
[
8
]
).

A
mean dimension-reduction subspace
for
\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
is a subspace
W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
is a function only of
\boldsymbol{V}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}\boldsymbol{X}
for some matrix
\boldsymbol{V}
whose columns form a basis for
W
.
Define

\mathcal{U}:=\bigcap\{W\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{d}:\text{$W$ is a mean dimension-reduction subspace for $\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]$}\}.

We say
\mathcal{U}
is the
central mean subspace (CMS)
for
\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
if
\mathcal{U}
is a mean dimension-reduction subspace.
(If
\mathcal{U}
is not a mean dimension-reduction subspace, then the CMS does not exist.)

The CMS is known to exist under mild conditions on
\supp(\boldsymbol{X})
, as discussed by
[
8
]
; an example sufficient condition is that
\supp(\boldsymbol{X})
is open and convex.
Henceforth, we shall assume that
\supp(\boldsymbol{X})
satisfies a sufficient condition that guarantees the existence of the CMS.
(Such an assumption will be explicit given later in
1
.)

Let
k=\dim(\mathcal{U})
, and let the columns of
\boldsymbol{U}:=[\boldsymbol{u}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{u}_{k}]\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times k}
be an orthonormal basis for
\mathcal{U}
.
Because
\mathcal{U}
is the CMS for
\E[Y\mid\boldsymbol{X}]
, the regression function has the form of a
k
-index model
, specifically

\displaystyle g(\boldsymbol{X})=f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})

(1)

for some function
f\colon\mathbb{R}^{k}\to\mathbb{R}
, which we call the
link function
.
Our goal is to estimate
\mathcal{U}
(or equivalently,
\col(\boldsymbol{U})
) from an i.i.d. sample from
P_{\boldsymbol{X},Y}
.
We evaluate our estimate using the following measure of error.

Definition 2
(Distance with Optimal Rotation, Adapted from
[
7
]
).

Given an orthogonal matrix
\boldsymbol{U}
and its estimate
\widehat{}\boldsymbol{U}
(which is also orthogonal), the distance with optimal rotation between them defined as:

d(\widehat{}\boldsymbol{U},\boldsymbol{U}):=\min_{\boldsymbol{R}\in\mathcal{O}^{k\times k}}\lVert\widehat{}\boldsymbol{U}\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{U}\rVert_{\op}\,,

where
\mathcal{O}^{k\times k}
is the set of all
k\times k
orthogonal matrices.

The distance with optimal rotation in
Definition

2
turns out to be equivalent to the so-called
\mathbf{\sin}_{\Theta}
distance
, another popular notion of distance between subspaces given as follows. Let
\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d-k)}
denote a matrix whose columns form an orthonormal basis for the orthogonal complement
\mathcal{U}^{\perp}
of
\mathcal{U}
. Then, the
\sin_{\Theta}
distance between
\boldsymbol{U}
and
\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}
is defined as
\sin_{\Theta}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U}):=\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{U}_{\perp}\rVert_{\op}.

It can be shown (Lemma 2.6 from
[
7
]
) that there exists
c,C>0
such that

c\cdot d(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U})\leq\sin_{\Theta}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U})\leq C\cdot d(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U}).

The distance with optimal rotation is appealing in that it readily yields control on regression error when data is first projected to
\col(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}})
rather than
\mathcal{U}
. For instance, suppose
f
is
L
-Lipschitz (e.g, as in linear regression, or as in polynomial regression with a bounded domain), and let
\tilde{f}:=f\circ\boldsymbol{R}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}
, where
\boldsymbol{R}\in\mathcal{O}^{k\times k}
is such that
d(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U})=\lVert\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{R}-\boldsymbol{U}\rVert_{\op}
, then:

\lVert\tilde{f}(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})-f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})\rVert_{P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}:=\lVert f((\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}\boldsymbol{R})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})-f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})\rVert_{P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}\leq L\cdot\lVert\boldsymbol{X}\rVert_{P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}\cdot d(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},{\boldsymbol{U}})\,.

To estimate
\tilde{f}
, one can apply the classical Nadaraya-Watson estimator on the projected sample of
(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X},Y)
. If
d(\widehat{\boldsymbol{U}},\boldsymbol{U})\lesssim n^{-1/(k+2)}
, the regression error rate is the same as if
\boldsymbol{U}
is known.

3
Main Results

We first introduce a surrogate object
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
in Section
3.1
below, and show that it indeed is
exhaustive
, i.e., it recovers all of
\col({\bf U})
. Following this, we derive a separate surrogate object

\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
in Section
3.3
which approximates
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
and turns out to be simpler to estimate and retains the same exhaustiveness properties. Our main algorithm then estimates
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{M}}
, and returns the column space
\hat{}\boldsymbol{U}
of this estimate. Our main result of Theorem
16
which bounds the estimation error is presented in Section
3.4
, followed by an instantiation for polynomial link functions in Section
3.5
.

We will need the following few assumptions.

Assumption 1
.

The joint distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{X},Y}
satisfies the following basic conditions.

(1)

The link function
f
is differentiable, with gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{z}),\forall\boldsymbol{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
;

(2)

The marginal
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
admits a Lebesgue density
p
supported on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, i.e.,
p>0
everywhere.

The assumption of differentiability above implies that of the regression function
g
, and is needed as our approach relies on gradient estimates. The second assumption above will be required in designing the estimation procedure, and also ensures the existence of
\mathcal{U}
as previously remarked.

3.1
Expected Smoothed Gradient Outer Product

We now present a smoothed version of the expected gradient outer product
\boldsymbol{M}:=\E_{\boldsymbol{X}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}[\nabla g(\boldsymbol{X})\nabla g(\boldsymbol{X})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}]
, which was discussed in the introduction.

Definition 3
.

The Expected Smoothed Gradient Outer Product (ESGOP)
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
is defined as follows.
First, let
h>0
be given,
and define the
smoothed gradient
of
g
at
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\beta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=\boldsymbol{U}\cdot\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta}),\quad\text{where }\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}[\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}(\boldsymbol{Z}+\boldsymbol{\theta}))].

Let
\sigma_{\theta}>0
. We then have:

\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}:=\E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\boldsymbol{\beta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\right]=\boldsymbol{U}\left(\E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}\right]\right)\boldsymbol{U}^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}.

(2)

The ESGOP
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
differs from the EGOP
\boldsymbol{M}
in two important ways. First, the gradient
\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})
is replaced by a smoothed gradient
\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. Second, the expectation in the definition of
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}
is taken over a Gaussian distribution (i.e.,
\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})
), instead of the data distribution
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
as in the EGOP. As we will see later in
Section

3.3
, such a design will allow us to invoke Stein’s Lemma (
lemma

8
) for Gaussians.

3.1.1
Exhaustiveness of the ESGOP
\overline{\boldsymbol{M}}

From
eq.

2
it is clear that
\col(\overline{\boldsymbol{M}})
is contained in
\mathcal{U}:=\col(\boldsymbol{U})
. We argue here that the two column spaces are in fact equal. From the same equation, this boils down to arguing that

\E_{\boldsymbol{\theta}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\sigma_{\theta}^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})\overline{\nabla}_{h}f(\boldsymbol{\theta})^{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}\right]

is full-rank. To gain some intuition, let’s first revisit a similar argument for the EGOP

\boldsymbol{M}=\boldsymbol{U}\left(\E_{\boldsymbol{X}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\right)\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}.

Suppose
\boldsymbol{M}
were not exhaustive, then one can find a direction
\boldsymbol{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
with
\lVert\boldsymbol{v}\rVert=1
, such that
\E_{\boldsymbol{X}\sim P_{\boldsymbol{X}}}[(\boldsymbol{v}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\nabla f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X}))^{2}]=0

The exhaustiveness of

-a.s. since

Proposition 4

Suppose that

We note however that, outside of CSQ procedures, much more benign sample size requirements are possible, for instance

Appendix A
SAVE Is Not Exhaustive

In this section, we provide an example where the Sliced Average Variance Estimator (SAVE) proposed by
[
10
]
cannot extract all the relevant directions, and hence cannot recover the whole central mean subspace (CMS), let alone the central subspace (CS). In the setting of the multi-index model
eq.

1
, SAVE extracts the index space by estimating the SAVE matrix:

\boldsymbol{V}=\E\left[\left(\E\left[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{T}\mid\ Y\right]-\boldsymbol{I}\right)^{2}\right].

One critical condition needed for exhaustiveness here is that
\col(\boldsymbol{V})
should contain all the relevant directions in the index space. This condition is not always satisfied.

Consider a simple noiseless single-index model:

\displaystyle Y=f(\boldsymbol{u}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{X})\,,

(12)

where
\boldsymbol{X}=(X_{1},X_{2})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0},\boldsymbol{I}_{2})
, and
\boldsymbol{u}=(1,0)^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}
. Next, we show that there is a class of link functions with which
\boldsymbol{V}
is a zero matrix. Let
\xi(z_{1},z_{2}):=\E_{Z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}[Z^{2}|Z\in[z_{1},z_{2}]]
for
0\leq z_{1}\leq 1\leq z_{2}
. Note that
\xi
is continuous, and monotonically increasing in each of
z_{1}
and
z_{2}
,
\xi(1,1)=\E[Z^{2}|Z=1]=1
and
\xi(0,\infty)=\E[Z^{2}|Z\geq 0]=1
.

Therefore, there exists a continuous decreasing function

\nu:[1,\infty)\to(0,1]
, such that
\nu(1)=1,\lim_{z\to\infty}\nu(z)=0
and

\displaystyle\xi(\nu(z),z)=1\text{ for any }z\in[1,\infty)\,.

Let
f_{0}\colon[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
be any continuous, non-negative, and monotone increasing function defined on
[0,1]
with
f_{0}=0
. Then, we define
f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
in terms of
f_{0}
as follows:

\displaystyle f(z):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}0,&&z<0;\\
f_{0}(z),&&0\leq z\leq 1;\\
f_{0}(\nu(z)),&&1<z<\infty.\end{array}\right.\,

The following proposition shows that the SAVE matrix
\boldsymbol{V}
is a zero matrix with link function
f
.

Proposition 28
.

Suppose
(\boldsymbol{X},Y)
is an example generated from Model (
12
) with link function as defined in (
A
). Then, we have
\boldsymbol{V}=0
.

Proof.

It suffices to show that for any
y\geq 0
,
\E[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\mathbbm{1}(Y\geq y)]=\boldsymbol{I}_{2}\p(Y\geq y)
.
For
y>f_{0}(1)
or
y\leq 0
, this is vacuously true.
For
0<y\leq f_{0}(1)
, we can show from the definition of
f
that:

\displaystyle\{Y\geq y\}=\{X_{1}:y\leq f(X_{1})\leq f_{0}(1)\}=\{f_{0}^{-1}(y)\leq X_{1}\leq\nu^{-1}(f_{0}^{-1}(y))\}.

(16)

See also Figure
3
for a graphical illustration. Therefore,

\displaystyle\E\left[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\Big|Y\geq y\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\begin{bmatrix}X_{1}^{2}&X_{1}X_{2}\\
X_{1}X_{2}&X_{2}^{2}\end{bmatrix}\Bigg|Y\geq y\right]

\displaystyle=\begin{bmatrix}\E\left[X_{1}^{2}\Big|Y\geq y\right]&\E\left[X_{1}\Big|Y\geq y\right]\E[X_{2}]\\
\E\left[X_{1}\Big|Y\geq y\right]\E[X_{2}]&\E[X_{2}^{2}]\end{bmatrix}

\displaystyle\hskip-11.42018pt\overset{\eqref{eqn:xy}}{=}\begin{bmatrix}\xi(f_{0}^{-1}(y),\nu^{-1}(f_{0}^{-1}(y)))&0\\
0&1\end{bmatrix}=\boldsymbol{I}\,,

Finally, we have:

\displaystyle\E\left[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\mathbbm{1}(Y\geq y)\right]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\boldsymbol{X}\boldsymbol{X}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\mid Y\geq y\right]\p(Y\geq y)=\boldsymbol{I}\p(Y\geq y)\,.

The proof is completed.

Appendix B
Connection to Local Linear Regression

In this section, we will show that our estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta};\mathcal{D})
, which is defined similarly as in
eq.

5
but with a full sample
\mathcal{D}=\{(\boldsymbol{X}_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, is asymptotically equivalent to the following local linear estimator (LLE) of
\nabla g(\boldsymbol{\theta})
with a data-dependent kernel:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\LLE}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}):=\argmin_{(\alpha,\boldsymbol{\beta})\in\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(Y_{i}-\alpha-\boldsymbol{\beta}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\theta})\right)^{2}K^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i})\,,

where
K^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{x}):=\rho_{h}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta})
.

Proposition 29
.

For each
\boldsymbol{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\left\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})-\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\LLE}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right\rVert\overset{\p}{\rightarrow}{0}\,,

here, the notation
\overset{\p}{\rightarrow}
represents convergence in probability.

Proof.

By standard solution for the weighted least square error problems gives:

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\LLE}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\boldsymbol{I}_{d}^{\backslash 1}(\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{W}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}})^{-1}\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\boldsymbol{W}^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}\boldsymbol{Y}

(17)

where
\boldsymbol{I}_{d+1}^{\backslash 1}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(d+1)}
is obtained by removing the first row of a
(d+1)\times(d+1)
identity matrix
\boldsymbol{I}_{d+1}
,

\boldsymbol{X}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\begin{bmatrix}1&(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\\
\vdots&\vdots\\
1&(\boldsymbol{X}_{n}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\end{bmatrix},\quad\boldsymbol{W}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=\diag(K^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{1}),\ldots,K^{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{n})),\quad\boldsymbol{Y}=\begin{bmatrix}Y_{1}\\
\vdots\\
Y_{n}\end{bmatrix}.

One can show from
eq.

17
and the block matrix inversion formula (
)
that

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=h^{-2}\left(\hat{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\,\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{\LLE}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta})+\hat{\mu}_{y}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right),

where

\displaystyle\hat{\Sigma}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\theta})^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}};

\displaystyle\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})(\boldsymbol{X}_{i}-\boldsymbol{\theta});

\displaystyle\hat{\mu}_{y}(\boldsymbol{\theta})

\displaystyle:=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\rho_{h}(\boldsymbol{X}_{i};\boldsymbol{\theta})Y_{i}.

By the weak law of large numbers,

\hat{\Sigma}\overset{\p}{\rightarrow}h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d},

\hat{\boldsymbol{\mu}}_{\boldsymbol{x}}\overset{\p}{\rightarrow}0,

\hat{\mu}_{y}\overset{\p}{\rightarrow}\E_{\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}[f(\boldsymbol{U}^{{\scriptscriptstyle{\mathsf{T}}}}\tilde{\boldsymbol{X}})].
The proposition follows and the proof is completed.

Appendix C
Estimation Bias Under
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
with a Bounded Support

In this section, we discuss the performance of our estimator when
1
(2) is not satisfied and the Lebesgue density
p
of
P_{\boldsymbol{X}}
has a bounded support
\{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\lVert\boldsymbol{x}\rVert\leq K\}
for some
K<\infty
. In this case, we note that the smoothed gradient estimator
\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j};\mathcal{D}_{j,l})
as defined in
eq.

5
is biased for
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
. In particular, for a fixed
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
,

\displaystyle\E[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j};\mathcal{D}_{j,l})]

\displaystyle={h^{-2}}\E[\rho_{h}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta})\cdot Y\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})]

\displaystyle={h^{-2}}\int_{\lVert\boldsymbol{X}\rVert\leq K}\rho_{h}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\cdot g(\boldsymbol{X})\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})dP_{\boldsymbol{X}}

\displaystyle={h^{-2}}\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\mathbbm{1}(\lVert\boldsymbol{Z}\rVert\leq K)\cdot g(\boldsymbol{Z})\cdot(\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\right]\,.

Hence, the bias is given as:

\displaystyle B_{h,j}

\displaystyle:=\E[\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j};\mathcal{D}_{j,l})]-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})

\displaystyle=-{h^{-2}}\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\mathbbm{1}(\lVert\boldsymbol{Z}\rVert>K)\cdot g(\boldsymbol{Z})\cdot(\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\right]

(18)

Intuitively,
\lVert B_{h,j}\rVert
should shrink to zero as
h\rightarrow 0
when
K\gg\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert
, which holds with high probability if we choose the variance
\sigma_{\theta}^{2}
of
\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}
’s is small compared to
K
.

On the other hand, it is clear that if
\sup_{j\in[m]}\lVert B_{h,j}\rVert=o(n^{-1/2})
, then the bias will not affect the rate in the upper-bound for subspace estimation error. The following proposition shows how small
h
needs to be to guarantee such small biases.

Proposition 30
.

Suppose that
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert\leq h
. Let
h=K(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log n})^{-1}/4
, then we have the bias
\lVert\E\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\rVert=O(\log n\cdot n^{-2/3})
.

Proof.

By Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality and
appendix

C
, we have:

\displaystyle\lVert B_{h,j}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq{h^{-2}}\Bigg(\E_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(h^{-2}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\mathbbm{1}(\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}\rVert>Kh^{-1})\right]\cdot\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[g(\boldsymbol{Z})^{3}\right]

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\cdot\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\lVert\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert^{3}\right]\Bigg)^{1/3}\,.

We will upper-bound each of the terms in the parentheses.
By
4
and
5
, it can be verified that there exists some
C>0
, independent of
h
and
n
, such that:

\displaystyle\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[g(\boldsymbol{Z})^{3}\right]\leq C\cdot\mu_{\rho}\cdot\mu_{f}\,.

(19)

The condition
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert\leq h
implies that:

\displaystyle\E_{\boldsymbol{Z}\sim\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},h^{2}\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\lVert\boldsymbol{Z}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert^{3}\right]\leq C\cdot h^{3}\,.

(20)

Finally, we have under the assumption
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert<h<K/2
that:

\displaystyle\E_{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}\sim\mathcal{N}(h^{-2}\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})}\left[\mathbbm{1}(\lVert\widetilde{\boldsymbol{Z}}\rVert>Kh^{-1})\right]

\displaystyle\leq\p\left(\lVert\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{0}_{d},\boldsymbol{I}_{d})\rVert>\frac{Kh^{-1}}{2}\right)=\p\left(\chi^{2}_{d}>\frac{K^{2}h^{-2}}{4}\right).

(21)

Consider the following concentration inequality for the chi-squared distribution:

Lemma 31
(Concentration for
\chi^{2}_{d}
, adapted from Lemma 1 of
[
22
]
).

Let
W
be a random variable follows the centered chi-squared distribution with degree of freedom
d
, i.e.,
W\sim\chi^{2}_{d}
, then for any
t>0
:

\p(W>d+2\sqrt{dt}+2t)\leq\exp(-t)\,.

By
Lemma

31
, when
h=K(\sqrt{d}+\sqrt{\log n})^{-1}/4
, we have:

\displaystyle\p\left(\chi^{2}_{d}>\frac{K^{2}h^{-2}}{4}\right)\leq n^{-2}\,.

(22)

From
eq.

19
to
eq.

22
, we have:

\displaystyle\lVert B_{h,j}\rVert\leq Ch^{-2}(n^{-2}\cdot\mu_{\rho}\cdot\mu_{f}\cdot h^{3})^{1/3}\leq\tilde{C}\cdot\log n\cdot n^{-2/3}\,.

where
\tilde{C}
is independent of
n
.

Remark 32
.

The condition
\lVert\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert\leq h
in
Proposition

30
holds uniformly for
j\in[m]
, with probability at least
1-\delta
with the choice
\sigma_{\theta}\lesssim h\cdot\log(m/\delta)
.
This is consistent with our choice of
\sigma_{\theta}
in the main theorem.

Appendix D
The Case with Unknown Density Ratio

In this section, we consider the estimation of the smoothed gradient when the density ratio is unknown. Without loss of generality, we assume that the smoothing radius
h=1
throughout this section and omit all
h
in the subscript and simply write
\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}:=\boldsymbol{\beta}_{h}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
. Recall that for
j\in[m]
,
\ell=1,2
, we have the following smoothed gradient estimators in
Section

3.3
:

\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,l}=\frac{2m}{n}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}}\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\cdot Y\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}),\quad

One natural solution to resolve the issue of unknown
\rho
is to replace it by some estimates
\hat{\rho}_{N}
based on another sample
\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{X}_{N}
, and we get the following plug-in estimator:

\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}=\frac{2m}{n}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}}\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\cdot Y\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}).

The following proposition quantifies the effect of “plugging in” on the
L_{2}
error of estimating the smoothed gradients.

Proposition 33
.

Let
j\in[m]
,
\ell=1,2
, and
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that
\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
is an estimate of the density ratio
\rho(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
from a sample
\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{}\boldsymbol{X}_{N}
with:

\displaystyle\E\left[\left|\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\right|^{12}\right]<\infty\,,\E\left[\lVert\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert^{12}\right]<\infty\,,

\displaystyle a_{N,j}:=\E\left[\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}{\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}\right)^{4}\right]\overset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}1,

\displaystyle b_{N,j}:=\E\left[\left({\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})-\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}\right)^{2}\right]\overset{N\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

where the expectation is taken over the randomness of
\boldsymbol{X},\widetilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{1},\ldots,\widetilde{\boldsymbol{X}}_{N}
. Then, we have for some
C>0
independent of
n,m
and
N
such that:

\displaystyle\E\left[\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}-\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j}\rVert^{2}\right]\leq C\cdot\left(\frac{m}{n}\cdot a_{N,j}^{n/4m}+b_{N,j}\right).

Remark 34
.

The performance of the plug-in estimator
\tilde{}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,\ell}
depends on both the relative error
a_{N,j}
and the absolute error
b_{N,j}
of the density ratio estimator
\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{x};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})
. The same squared
L_{2}
error rate of
O(m/n)
as the oracle estimator
\hat{}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,\ell}
can be obtained when
a_{N,j}^{n/4m}
is bounded from above by a constant and
b_{N,j}
is of smaller order than
O(m/n)
.

Proof.

We first apply the standard
L_{2}
error decomposition:

\displaystyle\E[\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E[\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}-\E\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}\rVert^{2}]+\lVert\E{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\E[\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}\rVert^{2}]+\lVert\E{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}\rVert^{2}.

(23)

In what follows, we provides upper bound the two terms on the right-hand side.

\displaystyle\E[\lVert\tilde{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}\rVert^{2}]

\displaystyle=\E\left[\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}\rVert^{2}\cdot\prod_{\boldsymbol{X}:(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}}\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}{{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\left(\E[\lVert\hat{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j,\ell}\rVert^{4}]\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\E\left[\left(\frac{\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}{{\rho}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}\right)^{4}\right]\right)^{n/4m}

\displaystyle=\left(\E\left[\left\lVert\frac{2m}{n}\sum_{(\boldsymbol{X},Y)\in\mathcal{D}_{j,\ell}}\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\cdot Y\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\right\rVert^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}\cdot a_{N}^{n/4m}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2m}{n}\cdot\left(\E\left[\left\lVert\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\cdot Y\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\right\rVert^{4}\right]\right)^{1/2}\cdot a_{N}^{n/4m}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2m}{n}\cdot\left(\E\left[\left|\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\right|^{12}\right]\E\left[\lVert\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert^{12}\right]\E\left[Y^{12}\right]\right)^{1/6}\cdot a_{N}^{n/4m}

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{2m}{n}\cdot a_{N,j}^{n/4m}\,.

\displaystyle\lVert\E\tilde{}\boldsymbol{\beta}_{j,\ell}-{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{j}\rVert

\displaystyle=\lVert\E[\left({\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})-\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}\right)\cdot(\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})\cdot Y]\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left(\E[\left({\hat{\rho}_{N}(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})-\rho(\boldsymbol{X};\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j})}\right)^{2}]\right)^{1/2}\cdot\left(\E[Y^{4}]\right)^{1/4}\cdot\left(\E[\lVert\boldsymbol{X}-\boldsymbol{\theta}_{j}\rVert^{4}]\right)^{1/4}

\displaystyle\lesssim b_{N,j}^{1/2}\,.

The proof is completed by substituting these bounds in
Appendix

D
.
