Stirling numbers for complex reflection groups
Bruce E. Sagan, Joshua P. Swanson

Abstract
. In an earlier paper, we defined and studied q q -analogues of the Stirling numbers of both types for the Coxeter group of type B B . In the present work, we show how this approach can be extended to all irreducible complex reflection groups G G . The Stirling numbers of the first and second kind are defined via the Whitney numbers of the first and second kind, respectively, of the intersection lattice of G G . For the groups G ⁡ ( m , p , n ) G(m,p,n) , these numbers and polynomials can be given combinatorial interpretations in terms of various statistics. The ordered version of ths q q -Stirling numbers of the second kind also show up in conjectured Hilbert series for certain super coinvariant algebras.

1.
Introduction

The purpose of this work is to define and study Stirling numbers of both kinds for any irreducible complex reflection group
G
. In order to motivate our construction, we first examine the classic case in type
A
.

We will use the following notation for two subsets of the integers,
{\mathbb{Z}}
,

\displaystyle{\mathbb{N}}

\displaystyle=\{0,1,2,\ldots\},

\displaystyle[n]

\displaystyle=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}.

If
S
is a finite set then a
set partition
,
\sigma
, of
S
is an unordered collection of nonempty subsets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{k}
with disjoint union
\uplus_{i}S_{i}=S
. The
S_{i}
are called
blocks
and we write
\sigma=S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots/S_{k}\vdash S
where set braces and commas are often eliminated in the
S_{i}
. For example, if
S=[9]
then the partition
\sigma
with blocks
\{1,3,4\}
,
\{2,7\}
, and
\{5,6,8\}
would be written

\sigma=134/27/568
. If we let

S([n],k)=\{\sigma\vdash[n]\mid\text{$\sigma$ has $k$ blocks}\}

then the
Stirling numbers of the second kind
are

S(n,k)=\#S([n],k)

where the hash tag denotes cardinality and
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
. Note that this definition implies that
S(n,k)=0
for
k<0
or
k>n
.

Let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
denote the symmetric group of all permutations of
[n]
. Then every
\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
can be written as a disjoint product of cycles
\pi=c_{1}c_{2}\ldots c_{n}
.
Letting

c([n],k)=\{\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}\mid\text{$\pi$ has $k$ disjoint cycles}\},

the
Stirling numbers of the first kind
are

s(n,k)=(-1)^{n-k}\#c([n],k).

In order to explain the sign in the definition of
s(n,k)
as well as our generalization to complex reflection groups, we need the partition lattice. If
\sigma=S_{1}/\ldots/S_{k}
and
\tau=T_{1}/\ldots/T_{k}
are partitions of the same set
S
then
\sigma
is a
refinement
of
\tau
, written
\sigma\leq\tau
, if every
S_{i}
is contained in some
T_{j}
.
Refinement is a partial order on the partitions of
S
which is, in fact, a lattice in the order theoretic sense. Let
\Pi_{n}
denote the lattice of partitions of
[n]
.

Suppose that
P
is a finite poset (partially ordered set) with a unique minimum element
\hat{0}
. The
Möbius function
of
P
is
\mu:P\rightarrow{\mathbb{Z}}
defined inductively by the equation

\mu(x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}1&\mbox{if $x=\hat{0}$,}\\
-\displaystyle\sum_{y<x}\mu(y)&\mbox{if $x>\hat{0}$.}\end{array}\right.

Suppose that
P
is
ranked
, which means that for any
x\in P
, all maximal chains from
\hat{0}
to
x
have the same length which is denoted

\rk x
and called the
rank of
x
. Given
k\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, define the
Whitney numbers of the second and first kinds
for
P
as

W(P,k)=\#\{x\in P\mid\rk x=k\}

and

w(P,k)=\sum_{\rk x=k}\mu(x),

respectively. The following result is well known, for example see
[
Sag20
, Section 5.4]
.

Theorem 1.1
.

We have

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle\text{$W(\Pi_{n},k)=S(n,n-k)$ and $w(\Pi_{n},k)=s(n,n-k)$.}}\hfill\qed

To connect the previous theorem with complex reflection groups we will need intersection lattices. A set
{\mathcal{A}}=\{H_{1},H_{2},\ldots,H_{k}\}
of hyperplanes in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is called an
arrangement
. The
intersection lattice
of
{\mathcal{A}}
is the set
L({\mathcal{A}})
of all subspaces of
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
which can be formed by intersecting the
H_{i}
partially ordered by reverse inclusion. Note that
L({\mathcal{A}})
has a
\hat{0}
, namely
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
which is the empty intersection, and is ranked by codimension. As a Coxeter group
{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}=A_{n-1}
has reflecting hyperplanes

(1)

{\mathcal{A}}_{n-1}=\{X_{i}=X_{j}\mid 1\leq i<j\leq n\},

where
X_{i}
is the
i
th coordinate function on
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
. Note our unusual use of capital
X
to distinguish these functions from the variables we will use for symmetric polynomials later.
From this description it is easy to see that we have an isomorphism of posets
L({\mathcal{A}}_{n-1})\cong\Pi_{n}
. Thus
Theorem

1.1
can be viewed as a statement about
A_{n-1}
.

We are finally ready to define our generalizations of the Stirling numbers. A
pseudoreflection
is a linear map
\rho:{\mathbb{C}}^{n}\rightarrow{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
whose fixed point set is a hyperplane and which is of finite order. A
complex reflection group
is a group,
G
, generated by pseudoreflections. The group is
irreducible
if
the only
G
-invariant subspaces are
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
and the origin, and in this case
n
is called the
rank
of
G
.
The finite irreducible complex reflection groups were classified by Shephard and Todd
[
ST54
]
into essentially one infinite family
G(m,p,n)
and
34
exceptional groups.
Let
{\mathcal{A}}_{G}
be the set of hyperplanes for the pseudoreflections in
G
. We define the
Stirling numbers of the second and first kinds
for
G
by

(2)

S(G,k)=W(L({\mathcal{A}}_{G}),n-k)

and

(3)

s(G,k)=w(L({\mathcal{A}}_{G}),n-k),

respectively.

Of course, a definition is only as good as the theorems it can help prove. In fact, one result indicating that (
2
) and (
3
) are worthy of study is already in the literature. Let
{\bf x}_{n}=\{x_{1},x_{2},\ldots,x_{n}\}
be a set of variables.
The corresponding
complete homogeneous symmetric polynomial of degree
k
, denoted

h_{k}(n)=h_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, is the sum of all monomials in
{\bf x}_{n}
of degree
k
.
By contrast, the
elementary symmetric polynomial of degree
k
, written
e_{k}(n)=e_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, is the sum of all such square-free monomials. The ordinary Stirling numbers are just specializations of these polynomials, in particular

(4)

S(n,k)=h_{n-k}(1,2,\ldots,k)\text{ and }s(n,k)=(-1)^{n-k}e_{n-k}(1,2,\ldots,n-1).

The following theorem was proved by Orlik and Solomon, although it was stated in a different form. It shows how
G
’s Stirling numbers of the first kind can be computed using its coexponents, certain invariants of the group.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
OS80
]
)
.

If
G
is an irreducible complex reflection group with coexponents

e_{1}^{*},\ldots,e_{n}^{*}
then

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle s(G,k)=(-1)^{n-k}e_{n-k}(e_{1}^{*},\ldots,e_{n}^{*}).}\hfill\qed

We will see that a similar statement can be made for
S(G,k)
in some, but not all, irreducible complex reflection groups.

The rest of this paper is organized as follows.
In the next section we will see how one can give combinatorial interpretations to the Stirling numbers for the groups
G(m,p,n)
in terms of colored partitions and permutations. This will enable us to give an even more refined connection between the Möbius function of the intersection lattice and certain permutations indexed by partitions.
Section
3
will be devoted to studying an inversion-like statistic on colored partitions and permutations. The resulting
q
-analogues satisfy generalizations of the equalities (
4
). In Section
4
, we study two ordered analogues of the
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind. We show that they satisfy nice alternating sum identities using both properties of symmetric polynomials and sign-reversing involutions.
Section
5
explores a conjectured connection between the ordered Stirling numbers of the second kind and certain super coinvariant algebras. We end with a section devoted to miscellaneous identities and an open problem.

2.
A combinatorial interpretation

It turns out that one can give a combinatorial interpretation to the
S(G,k)
for certain complex reflection groups in terms of colored partitions. This generalizes work of Zaslavsky
[
Zas81
]
in types
A
,
B
, and
D
. Using this interpretation we will be able to derive formulas for these Stirling numbers in terms of complete homogeneous symmetric functions. We will also show how individual Möbius values for elements in the intersection lattice of
G
can be interpreted in terms of colored permutations.

2.1.
Hyperplanes for
G(m,p,n)

The groups we will be concerned with in this section are
G(m,p,n)
, where the three parameters are positive integers with
p\mid m
, defined as follows. Let

\mu_{m}=\{\zeta\in{\mathbb{C}}\mid\zeta^{m}=1\}

be the
m
th roots of unity. If
p
divides
m
then
G(m,p,n)
is the set of all
n\times n
matrices satisfying the following constraints.

(1)

Each row and column contains exactly one non-
0
entry. Let
\zeta_{i}
be the entry in row
i
.

(2)

We have
\zeta_{i}\in\mu_{m}
for all
i\in[n]
.

(3)

We have
(\zeta_{1}\zeta_{2}\cdots\zeta_{n})^{m/p}=1
.

The pseudoreflections and corresponding hyperplanes in
G(m,p,n)
are well-known; the following is included to keep our exposition self-contained. As usual, the standard basis vectors for
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
are denoted

{\bf e}_{1},\ldots,{\bf e}_{n}
.

Lemma 2.1
.

The pseudoreflections
\rho\in G(m,p,n)
are of two forms.

(a)

For some
i\in[n]
and
\zeta\in\mu_{m/p}\setminus\{1\}

\rho({\bf e}_{i})=\zeta{\bf e}_{i}
and

\rho({\bf e}_{k})={\bf e}_{k}
for
k\neq i
.

(b)

For some
i,j\in[n]
and
\zeta\in\mu_{m}

\rho({\bf e}_{i})=\zeta{\bf e}_{j}
,

\rho({\bf e}_{j})=\zeta^{-1}{\bf e}_{i}
and

\rho({\bf e}_{k})={\bf e}_{k}
for
k\neq i,j
.

The corresponding hyperplanes are as follows.

(a)

The hyperplanes
X_{i}=0
for some
i\in[n]
(provided
p<m
).

(b)

The hyperplanes
\zeta X_{i}=X_{j}
for some
i,j\in[n]
and
\zeta\in\mu_{m}
(for all
p
),

Proof.

We will verify that
\rho
is a pseudoreflection if and only if it has one of the two given forms. Once this is done, the equations of the reflecting hyperplanes are clear.

It is easy to see that (a) and (b) yield pseudoreflections. Conversely, suppose
\rho\in G(m,p,n)
is a pseudoreflection. Let
\pi\in{\mathfrak{S}}_{n}
be the permutation whose matrix has the same nonzero positions as
\rho
. Each
k
-cycle of
\pi
corresponds to a
k\times k
submatrix of
\rho
with non-zero entries
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
. Using Laplace expansion, we find the characteristic polynomial of this submatrix is

\pm a_{1}\cdots a_{k}-\lambda^{k},

which has distinct roots. The characteristic polynomial of
\rho
is the product of these and is by assumption divisible by
(1-\lambda)^{n-1}
. Hence we must have
k\leq 2
, and there may be at most one
2
-cycle of
\pi
.

If
\pi
is a transposition, then for some
i,j\in[n]
there are scalars such that

\rho({\bf e}_{i})=a{\bf e}_{j}
,

\rho({\bf e}_{j})=b{\bf e}_{i}
and

\rho({\bf e}_{k})=c_{k}{\bf e}_{k}
for
k\neq i,j
.

The characteristic polynomial is then

(ab-\lambda^{2})\prod_{k\neq i,j}(c_{k}-\lambda)^{n-2}.

The only way for
1-\lambda
to have multiplicity
n-1
is if
b=a^{-1}
which also forces
c_{k}=1
for all
k
, so
\rho
is of form (b). The case when
\pi
is the identity is similar and results in form (a). This completes the proof.
∎

2.2.
Colored partitions

It follows from the previous lemma that the intersection lattice for
G(m,p,n)
is the same for all
p<m
. We will denote this lattice by

L(m,n)
. And the intersection lattice for
G(m,m,n)
will be written
\overline{L}(m,n)
. To give a combinatorial model for these lattices we will used colored set partitions.
Fix
m
and let
\zeta_{m}=\exp(2\pi i/m)
.
For
c\in{\mathbb{N}}
, we will use the abbreviation

(5)

i^{c}=\zeta_{m}^{c}{\bf e}_{i},

We will sometimes refer to
i^{c}
as the
base
i
with color
c
. Let

[n^{m}]=\{0\}\uplus\{i^{c}\mid i\in[n],\ 0\leq c<m\}.

For any scalar
k
and subset
S\subseteq[n^{m}]
we let

(6)

kS=\{ks\mid s\in S\}.

For example, if
n=2
,
m=3
and
\zeta=\zeta_{3}
then

\zeta^{2}\{0,1^{0},1^{2},2^{1}\}=\{\zeta^{2}\cdot 0,\ \zeta^{2}\cdot{\bf e}_{1},\ \zeta^{2}\cdot\zeta^{2}{\bf e}_{1},\ \zeta^{2}\cdot\zeta{\bf e}_{2}\}=\{0,\ \zeta^{2}{\bf e}_{1},\ \zeta{\bf e}_{1},\ {\bf e}_{2}\}=\{0,1^{2},1^{1},2^{0}\}.

Say that set
T
is a
multiple
of set
S
if there is
\zeta\in\mu_{m}
with
T=\zeta S
. Note that this is an equivalence relation. Say that distinct sets
S_{1},S_{2},\ldots,S_{m}
are an
m
-tuple
if
S_{i}
is a multiple of
S_{j}
for all
i,j\in[m]
. This implies that, for a suitable reindexing, we have
S_{i}=\zeta_{m}^{i-1}S_{1}
for all
i\in[m]
. We are now able to state our two crucial colored partition definitions, the first for
G(m,p,n)
with
p<m
and the second for
G(m,m,n)
.

Definition 2.2
.

A
colored set partition of type
(m,n)

is a partition of
[n^{m}]
of the form

\sigma=S_{0}/S_{1}/S_{2}/\ldots/S_{km}

for some
k
which satisfies the following two conditions:

(i)

0\in S_{0}
and if
i^{c}\in S_{0}
for any
c
then
i^{d}\in S_{0}
for all
0\leq d<m
, and

(ii)

the blocks
S_{lm+1},S_{lm+2},\ldots,S_{(l+1)m}
form an
m
-tuple for each
0\leq l<k
.

Block
S_{0}
is called the
zero block
and, because of (i), it must have cardinality
lm+1
for some
l\in[k]
.
We write
\sigma\vdash_{m,n}[n^{m}]
and let

S([n^{m}],k)=\{\sigma\vdash_{m,n}[n^{m}]\mid\text{$\sigma$ has $km+1$ blocks}\}

as well as

S(m,n,k)=\#S([n^{m}],k)

Finally, we will consider the poset

\Pi_{m,n}=\uplus_{k\geq 0}S([n^{m}],k)

ordered by refinement.

Definition 2.3
.

A
colored set partition of type
\overline{(m,n)}
is a type
(m,n)
partition
\sigma
which also satisfies

(iii)

B_{0}\neq\{0,i^{0},i^{1},\ldots,i^{m-1}\}
for any
i
.

We similarly write
\sigma\vdash_{\hskip 3.0pt\overline{m,n}}[n^{m}]
and let
\overline{\Pi}_{m,n}
be the corresponding poset. We also use the notation

\overline{S}([n^{m}],k)
for the set of all
\overline{(m,n)}
partitions with
km+1
blocks and
\overline{S}(m,n,k)=\#\overline{S}([n^{m}],k)
.

When writing examples, we will separate the
m
-tuples of blocks by vertical bars for clarity. To illustrate, suppose
m=n=3
. Then

(7)

\sigma=0\mid 1^{0}2^{2}/1^{1}2^{0}/1^{2}2^{1}\mid 3^{0}/3^{1}/3^{2}\in S([3^{3}],2)

and
\sigma
is also a type
\overline{(3,3)}
partition. On the other hand

(8)

\sigma=04^{0}4^{1}4^{2}\mid 1^{0}3^{2}/1^{1}3^{0}/1^{2}3^{1}\mid 2^{0}/2^{1}/2^{2}\in S([4^{3}],2)

is not type
\overline{(3,4)}
.
It turns out that the two posets just defined are isomorphic to the intersection lattices for the
G(m,p,n)
.

Theorem 2.4
.

If
p<m
then

L(G(m,p,n))\cong\Pi_{m,n}

so that
S(G(m,p,n),k)=S(m,n,k)
for all
k\geq 0
.
Also

L(G(m,m,n))\cong\overline{\Pi}_{m,n}

which implies
S(G(m,m,n),k)=\overline{S}(m,n,k)
for all
k\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
\zeta=\zeta_{m}
.
For the first statement, we will define a map
f:\Pi_{m,n}\rightarrow L(G(m,p,n))
as follows. Suppose
\sigma\vdash_{m,n}[n^{m}]
. With each
i^{j}
in the zero block associate the hyperplane
X_{i}=0
. And for any pair of elements
i^{c},j^{d}
in a nonzero block associate the hyperplane
\zeta^{d}X_{i}=\zeta^{c}X_{j}
. By
Lemma

2.1
, these hyperplanes are all in
L(G(m,p,n))
. Letting
f(\sigma)
be the intersection of its associated hyperplanes thus gives an element of the intersection lattice. It is easy to see that
f
is order preserving.

To show that
f
is an isomorphism, we construct its inverse. Given a subspace
W
in
L(G(m,p,n))
, let
{\mathcal{A}}
be the arrangement of all hyperplanes
H
of
G(m,p,n)
containing
W
. We form
\sigma=f^{-1}(W)
as follows.
For each hyperplane in
{\mathcal{A}}
, if
H
is of the form
X_{i}=0
then put the elements
i^{c}
in the zero block of
\sigma
for all
0\leq c<m
. If
H
has equation

\zeta^{c}X_{i}=X_{j}
then put
i^{d}
in the same block with
j^{d+c}

for each
0\leq d<m
. This is a type
(m,n)
partition because the coordinate hyperplanes make sure that condition (i) is satisfied and the others guarantee condition (ii). Again, the fact that this map is order preserving is clear. Finally,
f
and
f^{-1}
are actual inverses of each other since the steps taken to do one are reversed to do the other.

The corresponding statement about the Stirling numbers is now immediate.

The construction of an isomorphism
\overline{f}:\overline{\Pi}_{m,n}\rightarrow L(G(m,m,n))
is similar with the following modifications. For
\overline{f}
, if the block
S_{0}
contains any colored elements it must contain at least two with different bases, say
i^{c}
and
j^{d}
with
i\neq j
. For every such pair, put the hyperplane
\zeta^{d}X_{i}=\zeta^{c}X_{j}
in the arrangement of associated hyperplanes. Since all colors of
i
and
j
are in
S_{0}
, their intersection will be contained in the hyperplanes
X_{i}=0
and
X_{j}=0
even though these are not elements of
L(G(m,m,n))
. Similar considerations take care of the inverse map.
∎

We can use the description of these lattices in terms of colored partitions to obtain formulas for
S(G,k)
when
G=G(m,p,n)
in terms of complete homogeoneous symmetric polynomials. We will need the following well-known recursion for the
h_{k}(n)=h_{k}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
:

(9)

h_{k}(n)=h_{k}(n-1)+x_{n}h_{k-1}(n)

for
n\geq 1
, with boundary condition
h_{k}(0)=\delta_{k,0}
(Kronecker delta).

Theorem 2.5
.

We have

(10)

S(m,n,k)=h_{n-k}(1,m+1,2m+1,\ldots,km+1),

and

(11)

\overline{S}(m,n,k)=h_{n-k}(1,m+1,2m+1,\ldots,km+1)-nh_{n-k-1}(m,2m,\ldots,km).

Proof.

For the first equality, it suffices to show that
S(m,n,k)
satisfies the same initial conditions and recursion as the homogeneous symmetric function on the right side of (
10
). Proving the former is simple, so we concentrate on the latter. If

\sigma\in S([n^{m}],k)
then let
\sigma^{\prime}
be the result of removing all elements of the form
n^{c}
from
\sigma
. There are two cases.

First suppose
n^{0}
is in a block of size one. This implies that the same is true of
n^{c}
for all
c
. So
\sigma^{\prime}\in S([(n-1)^{m}],k-1)
. And in this case the map
\sigma\mapsto\sigma^{\prime}
is a bijection so there are
S(m,n-1,k-1)
possible
\sigma
.

The other possibility is that
n^{0}
is in a block of size at least two. Now eliminating the
n^{c}
leaves the same number of blocks so that
\sigma^{\prime}\in S([(n-1)^{m}],k)
for which there are
S(m,n-1,k)
choices. We also need to count the number of
\sigma
which will result in a given
\sigma^{\prime}
. We can reinsert
n^{0}
into any of the
km+1
blocks of
\sigma^{\prime}
. And once
n^{0}
is placed, the two conditions for a colored partition force the blocks for the other
n^{c}
. So the total number of
\sigma
in this case is
(km+1)S(m,n-1,k)
.

From these two cases we get the recursion

(12)

S(m,n,k)=S(m,n-1,k-1)+(km+1)S(m,n-1,k).

It is now an easy matter of reindexing to show that this agrees with equation (
9
) applied to
h_{n-k}(1,m+1,2m+1,\ldots,km+1)
.

Now consider (
11
).
By definition, the set
\overline{S}([n^{m}],k)
is just

S([n^{m}],k)
with all
\sigma
having a zero block
S_{0}
of size
m+1
removed. So, by appealing to the first case, it suffices to show that the number of removed
\sigma
is

nh_{n-k-1}(m,2m,\ldots,km)
.

The
m
nonzero elements in
S_{0}
are all of the form
i^{c}
for some
i\in[n]
and all values of
c
. So there are
n
choices for
i
. There remains to show that the
h_{n-k-1}(m,2m,\ldots,km)
counts the number of possibilities for the rest of the blocks. This argument is much the same as the one given for
S(m,n,k)
and so is left to the reader.
∎

To make the connection of the previous result with
Theorem

1.2
even stronger we note that, for
p<m
, the coexponents of
G(m,p,n)
are

(13)

e_{k}^{*}=(k-1)m+1\text{ for }k\in[n].

See
[
SW23
, Table 1]
for the general case.

2.3.
Colored permutations

We will now show how the individual Möbius values in the lattices for
G(m,p,n)
, not just their rank sums, can be given a combinatorial interpretation in terms of colored permutations. This generalizes a well-known result in type

A

[
Sag20
, equation (5.9)]
as well as a recent result of Sagan and Swanson in type
B

[
SS24
]
.

Let
{\mathfrak{S}}_{[n^{m}]}
be the symmetric group of permutations of
[n^{m}]
.
Recalling our convention (
5
), we see that
G(m,p,n)
acts on
[n^{m}]
by left multiplication, where we interpret
0

We wish to relate colored permutations to colored partitions.

Theorem 2.6

3.
Inversions and a
q
-analogue

We now introduce a

-analogue of the Stirling numbers of the second kind for

using an inversion-like statistic on colored set partition. In type

-analogues and statistics have a substantial history which the reader will find in
[
SS24
]
. For type

[

,

,
KAAM24
,

,

,

]
.

We will need to write our partitions in a standard form. Define the
minimum base
of a nonempty subset

to be

\minb S=\min\{i\mid\text{$i^{c}\in S$ for some color $c$}\}

if

, or
\minb S=0
if

.
If
\sigma=S_{0}/\ldots/S_{km}\in S([n^{m}],k)

.
For example, in the partition in (
7

,

, and

.
Since

. Also note that if

is an

.
Say that

if its blocks are listed in such a way that

(1)

The statistic we will use on colored partitions is as follows. An

Note that the first entry of an inversion pair must have color zero. Also, because of the standard form, it is not possible to have

although equality will become possible when we define

-analogues of ordered partitions. We let

be the set of inversions of

.
Using the partition above as an example, we have

.

Define the

-Stirling numbers of the second kind

to be

[k]_{q}=1+q+q^{2}+\cdots+q^{k-1},

Note that this forces

since the sum is empty. Also, we will often drop the subscript

if no confusion will result with the set

.

Proposition 3.1
.

For

and

we have

and, for

S[m,n,k]=S[m,n-1,k-1]+[km+1]_{q}\ S[m,n-1,k].

Proof.

We follow the same line of reasoning as in the proof of (

)
when

except keeping track of inversions.
So consider
\sigma=S_{0}/\ldots/S_{km+1}\in S([m^{n}],k)
and remove all copies of

to obtain

.

In the case when

is in a block of size one then, by the standard form conditions,

. It follows that

and so such partitions contribute

to the polynomial for

When

∎

We have

4.
Ordered Stirling numbers

In this section we will study three ordered analogues of the Stirling numbers of the second kind. In particular, we will prove that for
G(m,1,n)
they satisfy an alternating sum identity. Our proofs are both algebraic and using sign-reversing involutions.

4.1.
Lattice ordered Stirling numbers

In type
A
, the
ordered Stirling numbers of the second kind
are

S^{o}(n,k)=k!\ S(n,k).

So
S^{o}(n,k)
counts the number of ways to partition
[n]
into
k
blocks and then order the blocks (as opposed to ordering the elements within each block). The following alternating sum identity is well known
[
Sag20
, Theorem 2.2.2]

(18)

\sum_{k\geq 0}(-1)^{n-k}S^{o}(n,k)=1.

One can prove this equation either algebraically or using a sign-reversing involution.

A
q
-analogue of (
18
) is as follows. Let
S[n,k]=S[1,n,k]
and

S^{o}[n,k]=[k]!\ S[n,k]

where

[k]!=[1][2]\cdots[k].

Then one can show
[
SS24
, Theorem 5.5]

(19)

\sum_{k\geq 0}(-q)^{n-k}S^{o}[n,k]=1.

In order to find a corresponding ordered analogue for
S[m,n,k]
, we will need an identity of Sagan and Swanson. Given a set of variables
{\bf x}=\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
and a variable
t
we define the
{\bf x}
-falling factorial
to be

t\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow_{k}^{\bf x}=(t-x_{1})(t-x_{2})\ldots(t-x_{k}).

Theorem 4.1

(
[
SS24
]
)
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle t^{n}=\sum_{k\geq 0}h_{n-k}(k+1)\ t\hskip-2.0pt\downarrow_{k}^{\bf x}.}\hfill\qed

Comparing Theorems
3.2
and
4.1
, we see that to make the complete homogeneous symmetric functions agree we must let

x_{k}=q\ [(k-1)m+1]
for all
k
.
Note that the factor of
q
is to give the power
q^{n-k}
as in (
19
).
Furthermore, to get the power
(-1)^{n-k}
, we must set
t=-1
. With these substitutions we obtain

t-x_{k}=-1-q\ [(k-1)m+1]=-[(k-1)m+2].

Thus we let the
lattice ordered
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind
be

S^{o}[m,n,k]=[(k-1)m+2]!_{m}\ S[m,n,k]

where
[l]!_{m}
is defined analogously to (
14
), replacing each factor by the corresponding polynomial.
The term “lattice” here comes from the fact that the unordered version comes from the intersection lattice of
G(m,1,n)
.
We have proved the following generalization of (
19
).

Theorem 4.2
.

For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m\geq 1

\rule{4.30554pt}{0.0pt}\hfill{\displaystyle\sum_{k\geq 0}(-q)^{n-k}S^{o}[m,n,k]=1.}\hfill\qed

4.2.
Super ordered Stirling numbers

There is another variety of ordered Stirling numbers which turns up in the Hilbert series of certain super coinvariant algebras. We will have more to say about these algebraic aspects in the next section. Here we will show how they satisfy an alternating sum identity using combinatorial means.

Define the
super
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind
as

\tilde{S}[m,n,k]=h_{n-k}([m-1],[2m-1],\ldots,[(k+1)m-1]).

It follows easily from recursion (
9
) that for
n\geq 1
we have

(20)

\tilde{S}[m,n,k]=\tilde{S}[m,n-1,k-1]+[(k+1)m-1]\tilde{S}[m,n-1,k]

and
\tilde{S}[m,0,k]=\delta_{k,0}
.

In order to develop a combinatorial model for the
\tilde{S}[m,n,k]
we must modify the rules of colored set partitions in
Definition

2.2
.
A
super set partition

\sigma=S_{0}/\ldots/S_{km}
is obtained from a colored set partition by ordering some of the elements of
S_{0}
as follows.
For each base
i
in
S_{0}
we totally order the colorings of this base as

i^{c}i^{c+1}i^{c+2}\ldots i^{c+m}

where the colors are taken modulo
m
and

. Note that we do not permit

(equivalently,
c=m
), so there are
m-1
possible orderings for a given base. For example, if
m=3
then the possible orders are

i^{1}i^{2}i^{0}
and
i^{2}i^{0}i^{1}

but not
i^{0}i^{1}i^{2}
. Different bases are ordered independently with no implied ordering between separate bases. In examples, we will put spaces between the various base orderings. For example, if
m=3
,
n=3
, and
k=1
then

0\ 1^{1}1^{2}1^{0}\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 2^{1}/2^{2}/2^{0}

and

0\ 1^{2}1^{0}1^{1}\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 2^{1}/2^{2}/2^{0}

are two different super colored set partitions. On the other hand

0\ 1^{1}1^{2}1^{0}\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 2^{2}/2^{0}/2^{1}

is the same as the first super colored set partition above since the order of the blocks
S_{1}.\ldots,S_{km}
is immaterial. Let
\tilde{S}([n^{m}],k)
be the set of super colored set partitions of
[n^{m}]
with
km+1
blocks.

We will also need to enhance the definition of inversion in the super case.
The definition of standard form for super colored set partitions is the same as the one for their ordinary cousins.
If
\sigma=S_{0}/\ldots/S_{km}
is a super colored set partition in standard form then the

of
\sigma
are of two types:

(1)

pairs
(i^{0},S_{l})
which are inversions of the corresponding ordinary colored set partition, and

(2)

pairs
(i^{0},i^{c})
where
i^{0},i^{c}\in S_{0}
and
i^{c}
is to the right of
i^{0}
is the ordering on base
i
.

We let
\Inv\sigma
be the set of inversions of
\sigma
and
\inv\sigma=\#\Inv\sigma
.
To illustrate, if

\sigma=0\ 1^{2}1^{0}1^{1}\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 2^{1}/2^{2}/2^{0}

then

\Inv\sigma=\{(1^{0},1^{1}),\ (3^{0},3^{1})\}\ \uplus\ \{(3^{0},S_{1}),\ (3^{0},S_{2}),\ (3^{0},S_{3})\}

so
\inv\sigma=5
. The following result should not be unexpected.

Theorem 4.3
.

We have

\tilde{S}[m,n,k]=\sum_{\sigma\in\tilde{S}([n^{m}],k)}q^{\inv\sigma}.

Proof.

It suffices to show that the sum satisfies the same boundary condition and recursion (
20
) as
\tilde{S}[m,n,k]
. This proof follows the same line of reasoning as that of Proposition
3.1
so we will only sketch the differences. We keep the same notation as in that demonstration.

If deleting copies of
n
results in
\sigma^{\prime}
having fewer blocks than
\sigma
, then this base was not in
S_{0}
. So exactly the same argument gives the first term of the recursion. Now assume the
n

\sigma^{\prime}
is
\tilde{S}[m,n-1,k]
as before. So consider the inversions caused by
n^{0}
. If it was in one of the nonzero blocks then, as previously, these contribute
[km]
. If
n^{0}\in S_{0}
then it is in inversion with all the nonzero blocks as well as any
n^{c}
coming after it in the ordering of this base. This gives an extra

Adding the two contributions results in the desired factor of
[(k+1)m-1]
.
∎

The
ordered super
q
-Stirling numbers of the second kind
are

\tilde{S}^{o}[m,n,k]=[km]!_{m}\ \tilde{S}[m,n,k].

An
ordered super set partition
is a sequence

\omega=(S_{0}/S_{1}/\ldots/S_{km})
such that
\sigma=S_{0}/S_{1}/\ldots/S_{km}

is a super set partition satisfying

for all

m
.
In this case we write
\omega\models[n^{m}]
.
Note the use of parentheses to indicate that the order of the blocks is important. For example, when
m=n=3
and

(22)

(0\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 1^{0}/1^{1}/1^{2}\mid 2^{1}/2^{2}/2^{0})

and

(0\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 1^{2}/1^{0}/1^{1}\mid 2^{1}/2^{2}/2^{0})

are two different ordered super partitions, while

(0\ 3^{2}3^{0}3^{1}\mid 1^{1}/1^{0}/1^{2}\mid 2^{1}/2^{2}/2^{0})

Theorem 4.4

Definition 4.7

Define

Theorem 4.8
.

We have

\sum_{k\geq 0}(-q)^{n-k}\tilde{S}^{o}[m,n,k]=1.

Proof.

Throughout the proof we keep the notation of

.
If

then define its

to be

\sgn\omega=(-1)^{n-k}

Note that both
\sigma_{M}
and

are sign-reversing since they change the number of

-tuples in

by one.

By the previous definition and

We claim that in any two-cycle of
\iota
, the terms for
\omega
and
\iota(\omega)
will cancel because they have opposite sign, but the same value of

will increase both

and

by one. Since

is the inverse, it will do the opposite. So in either case the exponent on

is preserved.

We will only check the claim for the first case in the definition of
\sigma_{M}
and leave the other two to the reader. The fact that

increases by one has already been noted. As far as the number of inversions, the only inversions which change are those caused by
M^{0}
.
It is in inversion with the elements after it in its sequence in

as well as with certain

for

. The latter inversions are carried over in

. However,

moves one place to the left in the blocks

of

thus causing one more inversion.

To finish the proof, it suffices to show that
\iota
has a unique fixed point

\omega_{0}=(0\mid 1^{1}/1^{2}/\ldots/1^{m-1}/1^{0}\mid\ldots\mid n^{1}/n^{2}/\ldots/n^{m-1}/n^{0})

since

\sgn\omega_{0}\ q^{n-k-\inv\omega}=(-1)^{n-n}\ q^{n-n-0}=1.

If
\iota(\omega)=\omega
then
\omega
can not have any blocks of size at least

since otherwise it would be splittable. Next, we claim that the

-tuples of singletons must be arranged in increasing order of base. If not, then there would have to be an index

with
\maxb S_{(j-1)m}<\maxb S_{jm}>\maxb S_{(j+1)m}
. But in this case the

-tuple containing

could be merged either to the left or right depending on the value of

. Finally, we have to rule out the possibility that, for some

. But if this were true, then that

is the only possible fixed point and it is easy to check that it is indeed left invariant by

.
∎

Here is the conjecture alluded to above.

Conjecture 5.6
.

The set
{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n}
is a basis for
\SRG_{m,n}
.

Proposition 5.7
.

Conjecture

5.6
implies
Conjecture

5.3
.

Proof.

Fix
T\subseteq[n]
with
\#T=n-k
and consider the monomials in
{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n}
whose theta-factor is
\theta_{T}
. The columns for
U
form an
(m,k)
-staircase and so will contribute a
q
-factor of
[km]!_{m}
. Similar reasoning and the definition of
\phi(T)
shows that the columns for
T
contribute a factor of

[m-1]^{\phi_{1}}[2m-1]^{\phi_{2}}\cdots[(k+1)m-1]^{\phi_{k+1}}.

So summing over all
T
with cardinality
n-k
results in a coefficient of

[km]!_{m}\ h_{n-k}([m-1],[2m-1],\ldots,[(k+1)m-1])=\tilde{S}^{o}[m,n,k]

for the power
t^{n-k}
by equation (

). Summing over
k
gives the desired Hilbert series.
∎

There is a way to index
{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n}
using ordered super set partitions. Suppose that we are given
\omega=(S_{0}/\ldots/S_{km})\models[n^{m}]
and
s\in[n]
. We let

\inv_{s}\omega=\text{number of pairs in $\Inv\omega$ with $s^{0}$ as first component}.

These are the parts of the
inversion composition

(30)

\eta(\omega)=(\inv_{1}\omega,\inv_{2}\omega,\ldots,\inv_{n}\omega).

For example, suppose

\omega=(0\ 4^{2}4^{0}4^{1}\mid 1^{0}3^{2}/1^{1}3^{0}/1^{2}3^{1}\mid 2^{0}/2^{1}/2^{2}).

Now
\inv_{3}\omega=4
because of
(3^{0},S_{i})
for
3\leq i\leq 6
. Also
\inv_{4}\omega=7
caused by
(4^{0},4^{1})
and
(4^{0},S_{i})
for
i\in[6]
. And
\inv_{s}\omega=0
for all other
s\in[4]
so that

\eta(\omega)=(0,0,4,7).

We also need the set

T(\omega)=\{t\in[n]\mid\text{$t>\minb S_{i}$ where $S_{i}$ is the block containing $t^{0}$}\}.

In the previous example,
T(\omega)=\{3,4\}
because
3^{0}
is in a block with
1^{1}
, and
4^{0}
is in a block with
0
.

Proposition 5.8
.

We have

{\mathcal{S}}{\mathcal{A}}_{m,n}=\{{\bf x}^{\eta(\omega)}\theta_{T(\omega)}\mid\omega\models[n^{m}]\}.

Proof.

It suffices to define a weight-preserving bijection from pairs
(T,\alpha)
appearing in
Definition

5.5
to the
\omega\models[n^{m}]
. Given
(T,\alpha)
we construct
\omega
inductively as follows. We start with
\omega=(0)
and insert all colorings of
1,2,\ldots,n
in order into blocks according to the following rules when it comes to inserting the colors of
k
. An example follows this demonstration. Note that for blocks
S_{i}
with
i\geq 1
it suffices to specify the block containing
k^{0}
since then the other colors are determined by (
21
). And for the zero block, one just needs to specify the position of
k^{0}
in its sequence to determine the sequence.

(1)

If
k\in T
then put
k^{0}
in the existing block of
\omega
, and in the correct position in its sequence if using the zero block, so that exactly
\alpha_{k}
new inversions will result.

(2)

If
k\in U
then make
k^{0}
a new block of
\omega
so that exactly
\alpha_{k}
new inversions result.

By the way we have constructed this map, it will be weight preserving as long as it is well defined in that there exists a block, and position in a sequence if necessary, so that
k^{0}
causes
\alpha_{k}
inversions. Since
\alpha\leq\beta(T)
, this will follow if the
k
th part of
\beta(T)
is the maximum number of inversions which can be caused by
k^{0}
at the
k
th step.
This is an easy induction on
k
using the definition of
\beta(T)
and the rules for when a new
m
-tuple of blocks is created by
k^{0}
. So we leave it to the reader.
It is also routine to describe the inverse map by reversing the above algorithm step-by-step, so these details are omitted as well.
∎

Example 5.9
.

To illustrate the procedure in the previous proof, suppose
n=m=3
,
T=\{2,3\}
, and
\alpha=(1,2,4)
. We start with
\omega=(0)
. Since
1\not\in T
, we must place
1^{0},1^{1},1^{2}
in their own blocks. And in order to obtain
\alpha_{1}=1
inversion caused by

, we let

\omega=(0\mid 1^{2}/1^{0}/1^{1}).

Now

so we will place the colors of
2
in blocks with other elements. If we wish to have
2^{0}
involved with

inversions then this forces

\omega=(0\mid 1^{2}2^{0}/1^{0}2^{1}/1^{1}2^{2}).

Finally we have

5.3.
The Chan-Rhoades generalized coinvariant algebra

We will use the abbreviation

S[n,k]=S[1,n,k]

for the
q
-Stirling numbers in type
A
and similarly for the ordered variant. For any polynomial in
q
we use the notation

f(q)|_{q^{m}}=f(q^{m}).

Haglund–Rhoades–Shimozono
[
HRS18
]
introduced
generalized coinvariant algebras

R_{n,k}={\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]/\langle x_{1}^{k},\ldots,x_{n}^{k},e_{n}(n),e_{n-1}(n),\ldots,e_{n-k+1}(n)\rangle.

The classical coinvariant algebra of
A_{n-1}
is
R_{n,n}=\RG_{1,n}
.
Chan–Rhoades
[
CR20
]
introduced generalized coinvariant algebras for
G(m,1,n)
with
m\geq 2
,

R_{n,k}^{(m)}={\mathbb{C}}[{\bf x}]/\langle x_{1}^{km+1},\ldots,x_{n}^{km+1},e_{n}({\bf x}^{m}),e_{n-1}({\bf x}^{m}),\ldots,e_{n-k+1}({\bf x}^{m})\rangle

where
{\bf x}^{m}
refers to
x_{1}^{m},\ldots,x_{n}^{m}
. Set
R_{n,k}^{(1)}=R_{n,k}
. Haglund–Rhoades–Shimozono
[
HRS18
]
proved

(31)

\rev_{q}\Hilb(R_{n,k}^{(1)};q)=S^{o}[n,k]=[k]!h_{n-k}([1],[2],\ldots,[k]),

where if
f(q)
is a polynomial of degree
d
then

\rev f(q)=q^{d}f(1/q)
is the polynomial with reversed coefficients.

Proposition 5.10
.

For
m\geq 2
, we have

\rev_{q}\Hilb(R_{n,k}^{(m)};q)=S_{\CR}^{o}[m,n,k]=[km]!_{m}\ h_{n-k}([1],[m+1],\ldots,[km+1]).

Proof.

After accounting for
q
-reversals,
[
CR20
, Cor. 4.11]
gives

\displaystyle\rev_{q}\Hilb(R_{n,k}^{(m)};q)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\binom{n}{i}q^{n-k-i}\ [m]_{q}^{n-i}\ ([k]!\ S[n-i,k])|_{q^{m}}

\displaystyle=[km]!_{m}\ \sum_{i=0}^{n-k}\binom{n}{i}\ (q[m])^{n-k-i}\ S[n-i,k]|_{q^{m}}

for
m\geq 2
. One may check directly that the final sum satisfies the same initial conditions and recurrence as
h_{n-k}([1],[m+1],\ldots,[km+1])
. So the theorem follows from (
28
) and Theorem
3.2
.
∎

Remark 5.11
.

We can unify the cases
m=1
in equation (
31
) and
m\geq 2
in Proposition
5.10
by noting that in both we have

\rev_{q}\Hilb(R_{n,k}^{(m)};q)=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}[d_{i}]\right)h_{n-k}([e_{1}^{*}],\ldots,[e_{k+1}^{*}])

where the product is over the
degrees
of
G(m,1,n)
and
e_{i}^{*}
are its coexponents (see
[
SW23
, Table 1]
), both listed in increasing order. Here we use the convention that, for
m=1
,
d_{i}=i
and
e_{i}^{*}=i-1
, so in particular
e_{1}^{*}=0
.

6.1.
Identities

Let us define
q
-Stirling numbers of the first kind for
G(m,1,n)
consistent with
Theorem

1.2
for

to be

s[m,n,k]=(-1)^{n-k}e_{n-k}([1],[m+1],\ldots[(n-1)m+1]).

The following results about

and

can be derived using standard techniques from their expressions in terms of elementary and complete homogeneous functions, from the associated recursions, or for part (a) from
Theorem

Theorem 6.1
.

We have the following for
m\geq 2
.

(a)

\displaystyle t^{n}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}S[m,n,k](t-[1])(t-[m+1])\cdots(t-[km-k+1])
,

(b)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{n}s[m,n,k]t^{k}=(t-[1])(t-[m+1])\cdots(t-[nm-m+1])
.

(c)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}S(m,n,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{e^{x}}{k!}\left(\frac{e^{mx}-1}{m}\right)^{k}
,

(d)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}S(m,n,k)t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\exp(1+t(e^{mx}-1)/m)
,

(e)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}s(m,n,k)\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=\frac{1}{k!m^{k}(1+mx)^{1/m}}\left(\log(1+mx)\right)^{k}
,

(f)

\displaystyle\sum_{n\geq 0}s(m,n,k)t^{k}\frac{x^{n}}{n!}=(1+mx)^{(1+t)/m}
. ∎

We also get the following result immediately from
[
SS24
, Theorem 2.6]
.

Theorem 6.2
.

Consider infinite matrices

s_{m}=[s[m,n,k]]_{n,k\geq 0}

and

S_{m}=[S[m,n,k]]_{n,k\geq 0}.
