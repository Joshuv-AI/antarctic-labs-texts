Explicit fixed points of the smoothing tranformation
Jacques Peyrière

Abstract
. We deal with the equation Y = 𝖽 1 b ​ ∑ 1 ≤ j ≤ N W j ​ Y j \displaystyle Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathsf{d}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{b}\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}W_{j}Y_{j} , where the unknown is the distribution of Y Y , the variables in the right hand side are independent, the Y j Y_{j} are equidistributed with Y Y , N N is an integer valued random variable, and the W j W_{j} are equidistributed, nonnegative and of expectation 1. Usually a solution is obtained as the limit of a martingale. In some cases we give an explicit formula for the law of Y Y .

Let
N
be a nonnegative integer valued random variable with finite
second moment. Its expectation
b
is assumed to be larger than 1.
Set
\varphi(x)=\esp x^{N}=\sum_{n\geq 0}\proba(N=n)x^{n}
. Then

b=\varphi^{\prime}(1)
. Let
\mu
be a probability measure on
[0,+\infty)
such that

(1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}x\,\mu({\mathsf{d}}x)=1\text{\quad and\quad}\int_{0}^{\infty}x^{2}\mu({\mathsf{d}}x)<b.

We consider the equation

(2)

Y\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\mathsf{d}}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{b}\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}W_{j}Y_{j}

whose unknown is the probability law
\nu
on
[0,+\infty)
common
to
Y
and all the
Y_{j}
, the variables on the right hand side are
independent and all the variables
W_{j}
are distributed
according to
\mu
.

The Mandelbrot martingales

To get a solution to Equation (
2
) one way is to use the
Mandelbrot construction
[
7
]
. We set

Y_{n}=b^{-n}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\leq N}W_{j_{1}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j_{i}\leq N_{j_{1},j_{2}\dots,j_{i-1}}\\
\text{for }1\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}W_{j_{1},j_{2}}W_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\dots W_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n}},

where all the variables in the right hand side are independent, the

W_{j_{1},j_{2}\cdots j_{n}}
are distributed according to
\mu
, and
N

and the variables
N_{j_{1},j_{2}\cdots j_{n}}
are equidistributed.

One has

Y_{n+1}=b^{-n}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\leq N}W_{j_{1}}\times\\
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j_{i}\leq N_{j_{1},j_{2}\dots,j_{i-1}}\\
\text{for
}1\leq i\leq n\end{subarray}}W_{j_{1},j_{2}}W_{j_{1},j_{2},j_{3}}\dots W_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n}}\ b^{-1}\hskip-15.00002pt\sum_{1\leq j_{n+1}\leq N_{n+1}}W_{j_{1},j_{2},\dots,j_{n+1}}.

Therefore
(Y_{n})_{n\geq 1}
is a martingale.

We also have

(3)

Y_{n+1}=b^{-1}\sum_{1\leq j_{1}\leq N_{1}}W_{j_{1}}Y_{n}(j_{1})

where all the variables are independent and the
Y_{n}(j)
are
equidistributed with
Y_{n}
.

So,

\esp Y_{n+1}^{2}=b^{-2}\esp W^{2}\esp Y_{n}^{2}\esp N+b^{-2}\esp N(N-1)
, i.e.,

\esp Y_{n+1}^{2}=b^{-1}\esp W^{2}\esp Y_{n}^{2}+b^{-2}\esp N(N-1)
.

We therefore see that if
\esp W^{2}<b
the martingale
(Y_{n})
is
bounded in
L^{2}
. Then it has a limit
Y
, almost surely and in
L^{2}
,

\esp Y=1
, and

\esp Y^{2}=\displaystyle\frac{\esp N(N-1)}{b(b-\esp W^{2})}.

Due to (
3
) we see that
Y
is a solution to
Equation (
2
).

B. Mandelbrot
[
7
,
8
]
introduced this construction (with
constant
N
) to give a simplified statistical description of the
dissipation of energy in a turbulent flow. Since then this model and
Equation (
2
) have been extensively studied and
generalized (for instance
[
5
,
3
,
4
,
6
]
). See
[
1
,
2
]
for
a survey.

As a matter of fact, the necessary and sufficient condition for the
uniform integrability of the martingale
Y_{n}
is

\esp W\log W<\log b
(see
[
5
]
when
N
is constant and

[
6
]
when
N
is not constant).

Let
\alpha=\proba(W\neq 0)
and
\beta=\proba(Y=0)
. It results
from Equation (
2
) that

\beta=\varphi(\alpha\beta+1-\alpha)
. Due to convexity, the
function
t\mapsto\varphi(\alpha t+1-\alpha)
has a fixed point less
than 1 if and only if its derivative at 1 is larger than 1, which
means
\alpha b>1
. Due to hypotheses
\esp W=1
and
\esp W^{2}<b

this condition is fulfilled.

Laplace transform

For
t\geq 0
set
g(t)=\esp\mathrm{e}^{-tY}
. Then

(4)

g(0)=-g^{\prime}(0)=1.

From (
2
) we get

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009pt\esp\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-tY}\mid N,\ (W_{j})_{j\geq 1}\bigr)=\prod_{1\leq j\leq N}g\bigl(tW_{j}/b\bigr)

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009pt\esp\bigl(\mathrm{e}^{-tY}\mid N\bigr)=\left(\int g\bigl(tx/b\bigr)\,{\mathsf{d}}\mu(x)\right)^{N}

(5)

\displaystyle\hskip-60.00009ptg(t)=\varphi\Bigl(\int g\bigl(tx/b\bigr)\,{\mathsf{d}}\mu(x)\Bigr).

Let
\displaystyle u(t)=\int g\bigl(tx/b\bigr)\,{\mathsf{d}}\mu(x)
. So,

g(t)=\varphi\bigl(u(t)\bigr)
.

Relations (
4
) become

(6)

u(0)=1\text{\quad and\quad}u^{\prime}(0)=-\frac{1}{b}.

Also
g
and
u
are decreasing,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}g(t)=\proba(Y=0)=\beta
,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to+\infty}u(t)=\varphi^{-1}(\beta)=\alpha\beta+1-\alpha
, and
g(0)=u(0)=1
.

A particular case

Now we consider the particular case when

{\mathsf{d}}\mu(x)=(1-\alpha)\delta({\mathsf{d}}x)+\alpha(1-\gamma)b^{\gamma-1}x^{-\gamma}{\large\bf 1}_{(0,b)}(x)\,{\mathsf{d}}x,

with
\gamma<1
,
0<\alpha\leq 1
, and where
\delta
stands for the
unit Dirac mass at 0. If
W
is distributed according to
\mu
, the
condition
\esp W=1
, means
\gamma=1-\frac{1}{\alpha b-1}
and

\esp W^{2}<b
means
\gamma>1-\frac{2}{\alpha b-1}
. So the only
constraints on the paramters are

\frac{1}{b}<\alpha\leq 1\ \text{ and}\quad\gamma=1-\frac{1}{\alpha b-1}.

We have

\displaystyle u(t)=1-\alpha+\alpha(1-\gamma)b^{\gamma-1}\int_{0}^{b}g\bigl(tx/b\bigr)x^{-\gamma}{\mathsf{d}}x
.

Then

\displaystyle u^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\alpha(1-\gamma)b^{\gamma-1}\int_{0}^{b}g^{\prime}(tx/b)b^{-1}x^{1-\gamma}{\mathsf{d}}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(1-\gamma)}{t}g(t)-\frac{(1-\gamma)^{2}b^{\gamma-1}}{t}\int_{0}^{b}g(tx/b)x^{-\gamma}{\mathsf{d}}x

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\alpha(1-\gamma)}{t}g(t)-\frac{1-\gamma}{t}\bigl(u(t)-(1-\alpha)\bigr).

We see that
u
satisfies the following differential equation

(7)

(\alpha b-1)u^{\prime}(t)=\frac{1}{t}\Bigl(\alpha\varphi\bigl(u(t)\bigr)-u(t)+1-\alpha\Bigr).

Let

\displaystyle\omega(x)=\frac{\alpha b-1}{\alpha\varphi(x)-x+1-\alpha}+\frac{1}{1-x}
. As

\varphi(x)=1+b(x-1)+\mathrm{O}\bigl((x-1)^{2}\bigr)
,
\omega
is
bounded in a neighborhood of 1. Indeed, by continuity, we have

\displaystyle\omega(1)=-\frac{\esp N(N-1)}{2(\alpha b-1)}
. Then (
7
) rewrites as

(8)

\left(\omega(u)-\frac{1}{1-u}\right){\mathsf{d}}u=\frac{{\mathsf{d}}t}{t}.

Let
\displaystyle\Omega(x)=\exp\int_{1}^{x}\omega(\tau)\,{\mathsf{d}}\tau
. The function

x\mapsto\alpha\varphi(x)-x+1-\alpha
is convex and vanishes for

x=1
and
x=\varphi^{-1}(\beta)
; so it is negative on the interval

\bigl(\varphi^{-1}(\beta),1\bigr)
. This means that, on this
interval, the derivative of
(1-x)\Omega(x)
is negative.

It follows that there is a constant
c
such that, for
t\geq 1
,

u(t)
is the unique solution to equation

\bigl(1-u(t)\bigr)\Omega\bigl(u(t)\bigr)=ct,\text{\quad with\quad}u(0)=1,

in the interval
\bigl(\varphi^{-1}(\beta),1\bigr)
.
By taking in account the initial conditions (
6
) we see that

c=1/b
. Finally,
u
is implicitly defined by

b(1-u)\Omega(u)=t.

Examples

We give six examples of computations. The fourth one is interesting
because it shows that the mapping
(N,\mu)\longmapsto\nu
is not
one-to-one.

Example 1

We take
\varphi(x)=x^{n+1}
(where
n\geq 1

is an integer) and
\alpha=1
. Then
b=n+1
,

\gamma=1-\frac{1}{n}
,
\beta=0
, and

\displaystyle(1-x)\Omega(x)=\frac{1-x^{n}}{nx^{n}},

\displaystyle u(t)=\Bigl(1+\frac{nt}{n+1}\Bigr)^{-1/n},

\displaystyle g(t)=\Bigl(1+\frac{nt}{n+1}\Bigr)^{-(n+1)/n}.

This means that in this case the variable
Y
follows the

\displaystyle\Gamma\biggl(\frac{n+1}{n},\frac{n+1}{n}\biggr)

distribution, i.e.,

\nu({\mathsf{d}}s)=\Gamma\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{-1}\left(\frac{n+1}{n}\right)^{\frac{n+1}{n}}s^{\frac{1}{n}}\exp{\displaystyle-\frac{(n+1)s}{n}}\ {\mathsf{d}}s.

This situation has been independently studied by G. Letac and the
author. It is mentioned in
[
4
]
(page 264), but up to now (
[
9
]
,
p.387–388) seems to be the only written trace of this formula.
