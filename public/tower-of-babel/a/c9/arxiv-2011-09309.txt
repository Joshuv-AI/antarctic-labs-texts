Regularity and convergence of local first integrals of analytic differential systems
Xiang Zhang

Abstract
. Poincaré proved nonexistence of formal first integrals near a nonresonant singularity of analytic autonomous differential systems. In the resonant case with one zero eigenvalue and others nonresonant, there remains an open problem on regularity and convergence of local first integrals. Here we provide an answer to this problem. The system has always a local C ∞ C^{\infty} first integral near the singularity when it is nonisolated. In any finite dimensional space formed by analytic differential systems having the same linear part at the singularity, either all the systems have local analytic first integrals or only the systems in a pluripolar subset have local analytic first integrals.

1.
Introduction and statement of the main results

For an analytic autonomous differential system defined in a connected open region
\Omega
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, it is a well known fact that the system always have
n-1
functionally independent analytic first integrals defined in a neighborhood of any regular point in
\Omega
. So the system is completely analytically integrable near any regular point. But near a singularity the analytic differential system may have no analytic even no smooth first integrals. The situation will be more intricate, see e.g.
[
1
,
2
,
12
,
13
,
16
,
22
]
. The existence and regularity of first integrals of an analytic differential system are related to
dynamics on algebraic, geometry and topology, see e.g.
[
3
,
20
]
.

An analytic autonomous differential system near a singularity can be written in

(1.1)

\dot{x}=Ax+f(x),\qquad x\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ 0\right),

where the dot denotes the derivative with respect to the time
t
, and
f(x)=o(|x|)
is an
n
–dimensional vector valued analytic function defined in a neighborhood of the origin. Let
\lambda=(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
be the
n
–tuple of eigenvalues of
A
. Hereafter,
\mathbb{R}
is the set of real numbers, and
\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and
\mathbb{Q}_{+}
are respectively the sets of nonnegative integers and nonnegative rational numbers.

The existence and regularity of first integrals of system (
1.1
) depend on both
f(x)
and the eigenvalues of
A
. In case the eigenvalues of
A
are not resonant, Poincaré
[
15
]
proved the next result.

Theorem A.

If the
n
–tuple of eigenvalues
\lambda
do not resonant, i.e.

\langle m,\lambda\rangle\neq 0,\quad m=(m_{1},\ldots,m_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n},\ \ |m|\geq 2,

where
|m|=m_{1}+\ldots+m_{n}
and
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
denotes the inner product of two vectors, then the analytic differential system (
1.1
) has neither analytic nor formal first integral in a neighborhood of the origin.

For a proof of Theorem A, see e.g.
[
7
,
18
]
, where Furta
[
7
]
and Shi and Li
[
18
]
also extended the Poincaré result to quasihomogeneous and semi–quasihomogeneous differential systems on their local nonintegrability in the analytic or formal sense.
Shi
[
17
]
provided a sufficient condition on the nonexistence of meromorphic or formal meromorphic first integrals of the analytic differential system (
1.1
) near a rational nonresonant singularity.

On existence of local analytic or meromorphic first integrals near a singularity, there appeared some necessary conditions on the eigenvalues of linearization of system (
1.1
) at the singularity. Chen et al
[
4
]
in 2008 obtained the optimal upper bound on the number of analytic or formal first integrals of the analytic system (
1.1
). Cong et al
[
5
]
in 2011 got the optimal upper bound on the number of meromorphic or formal meromorphic first integrals for system (
1.1
), for quasihomogeneous and semi–quasihomogeneous analytic differential systems near a singularity, and for analytic autonomous differential system near a periodic orbit.

On determination of analytic integrability of analytic differential systems, the pioneer work belongs to Poincaré, who proved that a planar analytic differential system having a singularity with a pair of imaginary eigenvalues is locally analytically integrable if and only if it is analytically orbitally equivalent to its linear part. This result was extended to general higher dimensional analytic integrable differential systems by Zung
[
24
]
in 2002, and to analytic integrable Hamiltonian systems in the Liouvillian sense by Zung
[
25
]
in 2005 via the method of the torus action, where he proved that any local analytically Liouvillian integrable Hamiltonian system near a singularity is analytically equivalent to its Birkhoff normal form. Zhang
[
23
]
in 2011 proved the existence of analytic normalization for both general analytic integrable differential system near a singularity, and diffeomorphism near a fixed point, and provided the concrete forms of their analytic integrable Poincaré–Dulac normal forms. This result was extended to partially analytic integrable differential systems by Du et al
[
6
]
in 2016 with an additional condition.

We note that all the above results provide either necessary conditions on the existence and number of analytic (meromorphic, formal or formal meromorphic) first integrals, or on equivalent characterization on existence of an analytic normalization for analytic integrable systems. Whereas on the existence of first integrals with suitable regularity, there are some general results for two dimensional differential systems, see e.g. the book
[
16
]
and the references therein. But for higher dimensional analytic differential systems (
1.1
) with the singularity having resonant eigenvalues, the results are very few, see e.g.
[
11
]
.

In this paper the blanket assumptions are the following:

(1.2)

\lambda_{1}=0,\quad\langle\widetilde{m},\widetilde{\lambda}\rangle\neq 0,\quad\widetilde{m}\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n-1},\ \ |\widetilde{m}|\geq 2,

where
\widetilde{m}=(m_{2},\ldots,m_{n})
and
\widetilde{\lambda}=(\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{n})
. Li
et al

[
11
]
provided the conditions on the existence of a formal or an analytic first integral of system (
1.1
) as stated in the following.

Theorem B.

Assume that system (
1.1
) is analytic and satisfies (
1.2
). Then system (
1.1
) has a formal first integral in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ 0)
if and only if the singularity
x=0
is not isolated. Moreover, if
n=2
the first integral could be analytic.

Zhang
[
21
]
pursued the study on analyticity of the local first integral, and got the next results.

Theorem C.

For system (
1.1
) with
n-1
nonresonant eigenvalues
\widetilde{\lambda}
, the following statements hold.

(a)

If all the real parts of
\widetilde{\lambda}
have the same sign, then system (
1.1
) has an analytic first integral in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ 0)
if and only if the singularity
x=0
is not isolated.

(b)

There exist analytic differential systems (
1.1
) with
\widetilde{\lambda}
having both positive and negative real parts, which have no analytic first integrals in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ 0)
.

According to the results in statement
(b)
of Theorem C, it is natural to appear the next open problems.

Problem 1:

Are there
C^{\infty}
first integrals in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ 0)
of system (
1.1
) under the conditions (
1.2
)?

Problem 2:

What is the measure of the set of analytic system (
1.1
) satisfying the conditions (
1.2
), which have analytic first integrals in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},\ 0)
?

We first answer Problem 1 on existence of
C^{\infty}
first integrals.

Theorem 1.1
.

Under the conditions (
1.2
), the analytic differential system (
1.1
) has a
C^{\infty}
first integral in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)
if and only if the singularity at the origin is not isolated.

The next one is an answer to Problem 2.
Let
\mathfrak{K}
be the set of analytic differential systems of form (
1.1
) with the same linear part, which satisfies the conditions (
1.2
).

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that the origin is a nonisolated singularity of all systems in
\mathfrak{K}
. Let
\mathcal{K}
be any finite dimensional subspace of
\mathfrak{K}
. The following statements hold.

(a)

If
\mathcal{K}
contains an element, which has only formal but not analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, then all elements in
\mathcal{K}
except perhaps a pluripolar subset has also this property.

(b)

If
\mathcal{K}
has a nonpluripolar subset whose any element has an analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, then all systems in
\mathcal{K}
have this property.

Recall that the condition on the nonisolated singularity at the origin is necessary for the existence of local analytic or formal first integrals.
A pluripolar set is a subset of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
, and it is of Lebesgue measure zero, see e.g.
[
9
]
or
[
14
]
. A detail definition on pluripolar set will be given in Section
3
, where we prove Theorem
1.2
.

Theorem
1.2
has the next consequence.

Corollary 1
.

Let
\mathfrak{P}
be the set of polynomial differential systems of form (
1.1
) with the same linear part and a uniformly bounded degree, which satisfy the conditions (
1.2
) and have the origin as a nonisolated singularity. Then either
\mathfrak{P}
has all its elements having an analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, or
\mathfrak{P}
has only a pluripolar subset whose elements can have analytic first integrals near the origin.

We remark that the set
\mathfrak{P}
is a finite dimensional space formed by the coefficients of the nonlinear monomials of the systems in
\mathfrak{P}
.

Corollary
1
solves the open problem 2 for polynomial differential systems (
1.1
). Since a pluripolar set has Lebesgue measure zero, if
\mathfrak{P}
has a positive Lebesgue measure subset, whose each element has an analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, then all systems in
\mathfrak{P}
have analytic first integrals near the origin. In other words, if
\mathfrak{P}
has an element which has only divergent formal first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, then generic systems in
\mathfrak{P}
have only divergent formal first integrals near the origin.

All analytic differential systems of type (
1.1
) form an infinitely dimensional space in the sense of Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1
. In this case, it is still an open problem
whether the generic systems have only divergent first integrals at the origin.

is given Section

and Corollary

will be presented in Section

.

Recall that a pluripolar set in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
has Lebesgue measure
0
, and that the countable
union of pluripolar subsets is also pluripolar.

Denote by
\mathcal{P}
the set of plurisubharmonic functions in

\mathbb{C}^{m}
which have minimal growth, i.e.
u(z)-\log\|z\|
is bounded above when
\|z\|\rightarrow\infty
.
For any subset
{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
, define

V_{\Lambda}(z)=\sup\{p(z)|\ \,p\in{\mathcal{P}},\ p|_{\Lambda}\leq 0\},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}^{m}.

Having the above preparation one can recall the Bernstein–Walsh Lemma, see e.g.
[
14
]
or
[
9
, p.156]
.

Bernstein–Walsh Lemma.

Assume that
{\Lambda}\subset\mathbb{C}^{m}
is not pluripolar, and that
P(z)
is a polynomial of
degree
k
. Then

|P(z)|\leq\|P\|_{\Lambda}\exp\left(kV_{\Lambda}(z)\right),\quad\mbox{for }\ \ z\in\mathbb{C}^{m}

where
\|P\|_{\Lambda}
is the supremum of
P
on
\Lambda
.

We now apply the Bernstein–Walsh lemma to prove the next result, which is the key point in the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Lemma 2
.

For the subset
\mathcal{S}
given in Lemma
1
, the following statements hold.

(a)

Either each vector field in
\mathcal{S}
has an analytic first integral in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)
,

(b)

or only the vector fields in an exceptional pluripolar subset of
\mathcal{S}
have analytic first integrals in
(\mathbb{R}^{n},0)
.

Proof.

If statement
(b)
holds, the proof is done. We now suppose that
\mathcal{S}
has a subset, saying
\mathcal{T}
, which is not pluripolar, and whose each element has an analytic first integral.

Since we are in the conditions of Lemma
1
, we will use the notations and conclusions provided in Lemma
1
and its proof. Recall from Lemma
1
that any vector field in
\mathcal{S}
has a formal first integral, whose homogeneous part of degree
k
is a polynomial in
t
of degree
k-1
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be the subset of
\mathbb{C}^{m}
such that each
t\in\mathcal{B}
corresponds to the vector field
\mathcal{X}_{t}
in
\mathcal{T}
. Set
{\mathcal{B}}=\cup_{r\geq 1}{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
, with
{\mathcal{B}}_{r}
the subset of
\mathcal{B}
such that for each
t\in\mathcal{B}_{r}
its associated vector field
{\mathcal{X}}_{t}
has a convergent first integral
H_{t}
in the ball
D_{r}
of radius
1/r
, which is bounded by
1
in
D_{r}
. Since
\mathcal{B}
is not pluripolar and a countable union of pluripolar sets is again pluripolar, there exists at least a
{\mathcal{B}}_{r_{0}}
which is non–pluripolar.

By the construction of
\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}
and the proof of Lemma
1
, it follows that for each
t\in\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}
the vector field
\mathcal{X}_{t}
, and its associated system (
3.1
), has an analytic first integral
H_{t}(y)
in
D_{r_{0}}
, which is bounded by
1
. Write
H_{t}(y)
in series in
y
with coefficients in
t
as

H_{t}(y)=\sum\limits_{j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}^{n}}H_{j}(t)y^{j},

where
y^{j}
is the multi–index as used previously, and
H_{j}(t)
’s are polynomials of degree at most
|j|-1
as obtained in the proof of Lemma
1
.
For each
t\in\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}
, since
H_{t}(y)
is analytic in
{\mathcal{D}}_{r_{0}}
and bounded by
1
, one gets from the Cauchy inequality that there exists a
\rho_{0}>0
for
which

(3.8)

R(t):=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}}|H_{j}(t)|\rho_{0}^{|j|}<\infty,\qquad t\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r_{0}}.

In fact,
\rho_{0}
can be taken to be
1/(r_{0}+1)
.

According to the values of
R(t)
in
\mathcal{B}_{r_{0}}
, we further decompose the non–pluripolar set
{\mathcal{B}}_{r_{0}}
in the union of
\mathcal{C}_{s}:=\{t\in{\mathcal{B}}_{r_{0}}|\,\,R(t)\leq s\},\ \ s\in\mathbb{N}
. Then there exists at least one non-pluripolar set among
\mathcal{C}_{s}
’s, saying
\mathcal{C}_{s_{0}}
.
Taking any fixed non–pluripolar compact subset of
\mathcal{C}_{s_{0}}
, denoted by
D_{0}
. Then
R(t)
is bounded on
D_{0}
. Moreover, it follows from (
3.8
) that there exists a
\rho_{1}>0
such that

(3.9)

|H_{j}(t)|\leq\rho_{1}^{|j|},\quad\mbox{ for all }\ t\in D_{0},\ \ j\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}^{n}.

Since
H_{j}(t)
is a polynomial of degree
|j|-1
, we get from the Bernstein–Walsh lemma together with (
3.9
) that for
t\in\mathbb{C}^{m}

(3.10)

|H_{j}(t)|\leq\|H_{j}\|_{D_{0}}\exp((|j|-1)V_{D_{0}}(t))\leq\rho_{1}^{|j|}\exp((|j|-1)V_{D_{0}}(t)),

where
\|H_{j}\|_{D_{0}}
represents the maximum value of
|H_{j}(t)|
on
D_{0}
.

Choose any fixed compact subset
\mathcal{E}\subset{\mathbb{C}}^{m}
. Then there
exists a
\rho_{2}>0
such that

(3.11)

\exp((|j|-1)V_{D_{0}}(t))\leq\exp((|j|-1)\|V_{D_{0}}\|_{\mathcal{E}})\leq\rho_{2}^{|j|},

where
\|V_{D_{0}}\|_{\mathcal{E}}
represents the maximum value of
|V_{D_{0}}(t)|
on
\mathcal{E}
. Note that for given
D_{0}
,
\rho_{2}
depends only on
\mathcal{E}
.
Combining the inequalities (
3.10
) and (
3.11
) one obtains that

\|H_{j}\|_{\mathcal{E}}\leq\rho_{1}^{|j|}\rho_{2}^{|j|}.

Choose any
\rho\in(0,1/(\rho_{1}\rho_{2}))
. Then for any
t\in\mathcal{E}
the formal first integral
H_{t}(y)
of the vector field
\mathcal{X}_{t}
is convergent in the polydisc

\left\{y=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{n})|\right.

\left.\,|y_{j}|\leq{\rho},i=1,\ldots,n\right\}
.

By arbitrariness of the choice of the compact subset
\mathcal{E}
in
\mathbb{C}^{m}
, it follows that for any
t\in\mathbb{C}^{m}
the vector field
\mathcal{X}_{t}
has an analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin.

It completes the proof of the lemma. ∎

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.
(a)
By contrary we assume that there exists a nonpluripolar subset of
\mathcal{K}
, whose any element has an analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin. Then by Lemma
2
any system in
\mathcal{K}
has an analytic first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, a contradiction with the assumption that
\mathcal{K}
contains an element, which has only formal first integrals in a neighborhood of the origin.

(b)
follows from the proof of statement
(a)
. This proves the theorem. ∎

Proof of Corollary
1
. As mentioned in the remark after Corollary
1
,
\mathfrak{P}
is a finite dimensional space. Let
m
be the dimension of this space. By the proofs of Theorem
1.1
and Lemma
1
it follows that any system in
\mathfrak{P}
has a formal first integral in a neighborhood of the origin, whose each monomial has its coefficient being a polynomial in
t
. We can write the nonlinear parts of the vector fields associated to the systems in
\mathfrak{P}
as
t_{1}\mathcal{N}_{1}+\ldots+t_{m}\mathcal{N}_{m}
, where
t=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, and
\mathcal{N}_{j}
for
j\in\{1,\ldots,m\}
is an
n
–dimensional vector field with one component a nonlinear monomial and others all vanishing. Then the next proof follows from the same arguments as those given in the proof of Lemma
1
. The details are omitted. ∎
