Automated generation of attack trees with optimal shape and labelling
Olga Gadyatskaya, Sjouke Mauw, Rolando Trujillo-Rasua, Tim A. C. Willemse

Abstract
The problem this article addresses is, given a formal specification of a system, how to produce an attack tree that correctly and clearly describes the ways the system can be attacked. Correctness means that the attacks displayed by the attack tree are indeed attacks in the system; clarity means that the tree is efficient in communicating the attack scenario. To pursue clarity, we introduce an attack-tree generation algorithm that minimises the tree size and the information length of its labels without sacrificing correctness. We achieve this by establishing a connection between the problem of factorising algebraic expressions and the problem of minimising the tree size. Notably, our generation algorithm can handle complex attacks that execute actions in parallel and sequentially. For completeness, we introduce a system model that integrates well with our generation approach, and validate the resulting framework via a running example.

Introduction

Attack trees were introduced by Bruce Schneier
[
1
]
in
1999 as a
graphical method to evaluate and analyse threats.
Like mind maps, they are meant to break down a goal into
smaller, more
manageable subgoals.
The root
node of an attack tree
denotes the attacker’s goal; the root node’s children establish how that
goal can be refined into a set of smaller subgoals; and so on.
Because of their potential to help a
security analyst to
obtain
an overview
of the
system vulnerabilities, facilitate
communications
across the board, and succinctly store very complex threat
scenarios
[
2
]
, they have gained widespread
recognition in
industry
and academia alike
[
3
]
.

There exist two fundamental
ways of refining a goal: by a
conjunctive refinement expressing that all subgoals are necessary to meet the
parent goal, or by a disjunctive refinement expressing that a
single subgoal is sufficient to meet the
parent goal.
Both types of refinement are illustrated in
Figure
1
, which displays an attack tree example with the
following high-level description. The
goal of the attacker (root node) is to gain unauthorized access to a server.
To do so, the
attacker must first get a suitable credential for the server,
and then use the credential to log in remotely. This is expressed as
a conjunctive
refinement, denoted graphically by an arrow crossing or connecting the edges
that link a
parent node with its children.
A suitable credential can
be
obtained by eavesdropping on communications of an honest user who knows
the server password. Alternatively, the attacker
can bruteforce the password on the server or use an exploit to create
a new password. This constitutes a disjunctive refinement, denoted
graphically by the absence of an arrow.

Notice how the attack tree example has been built over hidden or subjective
assumptions about the attacker capabilities and the attacked system, making it
impossible
to determine how faithfully the attack-tree model captures the most relevant
attack
scenarios. In practice, confidence in
the developed attack-tree models heavily depends on the expertise of the
security team who builds them. That makes the process of creating an
attack tree time-consuming, resource-intensive, tedious, and
error-prone
[
2
,

]
.

Their approach, in general,
consists of three steps:

(i)

specify the system under analysis,

(ii)

use model checking or other formal methods to find attacks on
the system, and

(iii)

use automatic means to represent those attacks within an
attack-tree model.

In this case,
assurances about the completeness and soundness of the attack tree
come with
mathematical rigour by resorting to
state-of-the-art model-checking techniques that
can systematically and exhaustively explore various combinations of attacks and
system behaviour. The first two steps in this approach benefit from existing
knowledge of system specification and verification. The last step, however,
that of generating an easy-to-comprehend attack tree out of a system
specification or set of
attacks, is still largely an open problem.

Indeed, because attack trees are meant to be used as part of a threat

[

]
or risk assessment

[

]
, their communication power (i.e. their
ability to be understood by
security analysts) is
as
important as their soundness and completeness (i.e. their ability to faithfully
capture the most
relevant threats to the system).
Notably, attack trees are expected to help the analyst in understanding the

levels of abstraction

the tree.
In Figure

, for example, the first level tells the
analyst that a credential and a terminal to login is sufficient to access the
server, but it doesn’t specify how to obtain such credential.
The deeper one goes, however, the more details one will find on the attack.

To make attack trees comprehensible, one has to (at least)
give meaning to their labels.
Earlier generation approaches that produce trees without labels for
intermediate-nodes, such as

[
8
]
, are clearly unsuitable for a top-down reading.
There exist two complementary approaches in the literature to produce trees
with
meaningful labels. The
first one consists of using
attack-tree templates
[
13
]
,
libraries
[
17
]
, or refinement
specifications
[
6
]
to guide the generation process. This can help to
improve both hand-made and computer-generated trees, but it faces the problem
of creating a comprehensive knowledge base.
The second approach
consists of
providing a formal language to express attack tree goals/labels

[
18
,
19
,
4
,
5
]
and mechanisms to check
whether
those goals are consistent with respect to a system specification.
We deem this necessary,
given the prominent role labels play in the comprehension of an
attack tree,
but we have found no
attack-tree generation framework in the literature capable of producing trees
with labels expressed within a formal language.
This article introduces the first such framework.

The attack-tree generation framework introduced in this article unifies various

appear scattered in the
literature, namely,

•

Flexibility and extensibility

[

]
:

By
defining an interface between system models and the attack generation

plugging in any
system model that implements such
interface. In our case, the interface considers attacks expressed as
series-parallel graphs
[
20
]
, which allows for attack steps to be
executed in parallel and sequentially.

•

Correctness checking
[
18
,

,
4
,
5
,
14
,
6
]
:
The framework
uses a formal language
to produce goals/labels for the tree, which aids their
comprehension and gives goals an unambiguous meaning, and establishes
whether an attack tree is sound and
correctly-labelled with respect to the
output of a
system specification.

•

Attacks have well-defined goals:

We require attacks to tell which goals they achieve. This
has been implicitly assumed in most previous work, but only recently
formalised in
[
18
]
.

In addition, our framework introduces the first
optimality condition

for attack tree labels, requiring generation approaches to produce labels that
convey the minimum necessary information for the analyst to
understand

the tree.
In a nutshell, we define the
meaning
of an attack goal or label as the
set of
attacks that achieve such goal. Such meaning is assumed to be prior knowledge
for the analyst and thus is independent from the attack-tree generation
process.
Then, given an attack tree

t
with root goal

g
, we require the meaning of
g
to be captured by
t
as

tightly
as possible.
We shall make this more precise in Section

3
.

Another unique characteristic of our framework is the use of graph properties
as optimisation criteria to shape the generated trees.
Recognising that the structure of the tree, i.e. its nodes and
connections, impacts the way it is traversed via a top-down reading, we find it
important that attack-tree generation approaches provide an optimality
criterion for
their shape. This gives an objective measure to compare different generation
approaches.
In our method, we seek
to produce trees with minimum size. Intuitively, the smaller the tree, the
fewer
nodes the
analyst needs to scrutinise to grasp the attack scenario described by the tree.

We show instances of the
attack-tree generation
problem that reduce to the set cover problem.
To minimise the size of attack trees, we formalise the algebraic properties
of
the
attacks used as input to the attack-tree generation problem, and derive an
algebraic factorisation procedure to guide the minimisation of the tree size.
This shows
an interesting
connection between the problem of minimising attack
trees and the problem of minimising algebraic expressions; the latter is often
done via algebraic factorisation.
The resulting attack-tree generation algorithm (described in detail in Section

4
) is, to the best of our
knowledge, the first approach capable of
synthesising attacks with actions executed in parallel and sequentially.

For completeness, we introduce in Section
5
a system model and
its
corresponding interface with the attack-tree generation problem. Notably, we
show how to obtain attack-tree labels out of the system model and how those
labels relate to attacks.

All in all, this article introduces the first attack-tree generation method
that optimises the labelling and shape of the generated trees, while
guaranteeing their soundness and correctness with respect to a system
specification.

2
Related work

System-based approaches towards attack-tree generation start from a
formal specification of the system under analysis,
typically in the form of a graph describing the system assets, actors,
access control rules, etc.,
and produces an attack tree describing how an attacker can lead the system
into an undesired state.
That is to say, these approaches translate a specification in a
system modelling language, such as a labelled transition system,
into a specification in the attack-tree model.

Earlier attack-tree generation approaches attempted to make a direct
model-to-model translation. For example,
Vigo et al.
[
8
]
generate trees from a process
calculus system model by translating algebraic specifications into
formulae and backward-chaining these formulae into a formula for the
attacker’s goal success. Reachability-based approaches, such as

[
9
,
11
,
10
]
,
transform system models
into attack trees using information about connected elements in the
model. In essence, these approaches reason that the attacker can reach
the desired location from any system location adjacent to it. This
reasoning is applied recursively to traverse complete attack paths. Dawkins and
Hale

[
21
]
have generated attack
trees from network attack graphs (a formalism different from attack
trees
[
22
]
) by finding minimum cut sets for successful
attack paths (traces).
The main drawback of these approaches is that the
connection between system
and attack-tree model is made only informally via the generation algorithm.

Later, a series of works on attack-tree generation

[
4
,
5
,
6
,
7
]

provided simple and elegant soundness and completeness properties for
the generation algorithm. Given an attack-tree semantics, such as the one
introduced by Mauw and Oostdijk
[
23
]
, an attack-tree generation
algorithm is sound if all attacks in its semantics are attacks in the system
specification. In other words, these works use
attack traces

as an intermediate representation or interface for both system and attack-tree
models. The
task for the system specification is to find attacks; the task for the
generation
algorithm is to represent or synthetise those attacks within an attack-tree
model. Such separation of duties is not only useful to define soundness, but
also
to compare and integrate
different generation approaches.

In practice, attack trees are seldom used in isolation, though, but as part of
a
risk assessment
methodology. They are meant to succinctly represent an attack scenario, notably
from the point of view of the attacker, and to aid the comprehension and
evaluation
of threats against a system.
This means that the communication power of an attack tree is as important as
its soundness. Interestingly, because most attack-tree semantics ignore the
labels of the intermediate nodes in the tree
[
24
,
23
]
,
soundness is insufficient to guarantee that attack trees are labelled in some
meaningful way.

An approach to produce attack trees with meaningful labels is by using
templates
or libraries obtained from threat databases

[
13
,
17
,
6
,
14
,
25
]
. Both
the attack tree
labelling and shape
are determined, or highly influenced, by a knowledge base on how threats to
systems are modelled as attack trees.
For example,
the ATSyRA approach
[
26
,
27
]

requires that the
analyst first defines a set of actions at several abstraction levels
in the system model, and a set of rules for refinement of higher-level
actions into combinations of lower-level ones. This action hierarchy
allows to transform successful attack paths into an attack tree, containing
precise
actions as leaf nodes, while intermediate nodes represent more
abstract actions. This tree enjoys a refinement structure that is more
familiar to the human analyst, but the analyst still has to define the
refinement relation herself.
In fact, there does
not exist a comprehensive knowledge base with libraries, templates or
refinement
specifications available for these approaches to build attack trees.

Another line of work starts from the observation that, to obtain meaningful
labels, one has to formally define what a label is and how it relates to the
system
specification. On that direction,
Audinot et al.
[
5
]

establish a robust link between the system
states and leaf nodes of the tree, with the goal of guiding the analyst on
refining
useful
tree nodes only.
Their
labels are not constrained to be system actions, but reachability
goals defined as words over a set of propositions, which has some similarities
to our work.
In
[
4
]
, labels of their trees are goals
formulated in terms of
pre-conditions and post-conditions over the possible states of the system.
These works, however, do not propose a generation algorithm.

Mantel and Probst
[
18
]
go one step further. They claim that
establishing whether an
attack trace satisfies a given goal is essential to producing trees with
meaning. We agree with that observation and build our framework around system
models that can link attack traces and goals precisely.
Mantel and Probst introduce a formal language for specifying traces and
attacker goals. They propose different ways to interpret a
satisfaction of a goal. For example, a goal may be satisfied by a trace if at
some point of the trace the goal is satisfied. Or one could use a stronger
variant: a goal is satisfied if after a certain point of the trace the goal is
always
satisfied. Their work focuses on providing a template for defining success
criteria of
different flavours, rather than on the generation of attack trees. We, instead,
focus
on the attack-tree
generation problem, which can be instantiated with any of the success
criteria mentioned in
[
18
]
.

We conclude by highlighting that there also exist approaches to the generation
of attack trees from informal
system models, such as
[
15
]
. This goes along the spirit of the
attack-tree methodology as suggested by Schneier
[
1
]

back in 1999. However, as a generation mechanism, it lacks formal guarantees of
correctness. For informal approaches to attack-tree generation we refer the
reader to
[
28
]
, our focus instead is on methodologies whose
properties can be rigorously analysed.

3
The attack-tree generation problem with optimal labelling and
shape

The goal of this section is to formalise the attack-tree generation problem as
an optimisation problem where the attack tree labelling and shape are the
objective functions. Hence we shall formally introduce the grammar and
semantics of attack trees,
their underlying graph structure, their labelling and, lastly, the attack-tree
generation problem.

3.1
\mathtt{SAND}
attack trees

An attack tree defines how higher (
parent
) nodes are
interpreted through lower (
child
) nodes. The interpretations are
defined by the refinement operators:
\mathtt{OR}
specifies that if any of
the child nodes is achieved, then the immediate parent node is also
achieved; while
\and
defines that all child nodes need to be achieved
to achieve the parent node’s goal
[
29
]
. We will consider also
the sequential
\and
operator, or
\mathtt{SAND}
, that demands that the goals
of the child nodes are to be achieved in a sequential order for achieving
the parent node
[
20
]
.

3.1.1
Syntax.

Formally, let
\mathbb{B}
denote a set of
node labels
. Let

\mathtt{OR}
and
\and
be unranked
associative and commutative
operators, and
\mathtt{SAND}
be an unranked
associative but non-commutative
operator.
A
\mathtt{SAND}
attack tree
t
is an expression over
the signature
\mathbb{B}\cup\{\mathtt{OR},\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
, generated by the
following grammar (for
b\in\mathbb{B}
):

t::=b\mid b\triangleleft\mathtt{OR}(t,\ldots,t)\mid b\triangleleft\and(t,\ldots,t)\mid b\triangleleft\mathtt{SAND}(t,\ldots,t)\text{.}

When drawing
\mathtt{SAND}
attack trees, like in Figure
1
, we will
represent
the
\and
operator by a
bar connecting the edges to the node’s children,
the
\mathtt{SAND}
operator by an arrow
instead of a bar, and the
\mathtt{OR}
operator by the absence of a
connector.
Due to the commutativity of the

\mathtt{OR}
operator, the order in which an
\mathtt{OR}
node is connected to its children
is immaterial;
likewise for the
\and
operator. Note that the order of children for a
\mathtt{SAND}

nodes remains relevant and given by the direction of the arrow.

We use

\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{B}}

to denote all
\mathtt{SAND}
attack trees generated by
the
grammar above, given a set of labels
\mathbb{B}
. Different from the
definition of
\mathtt{SAND}
trees given
in
[
20
]
, we require every node in the tree to be annotated
with a label. A label in a node typically provides a generic
(sometimes informal)
description of the type of attack, e.g.
get
a user’s credentials
or
impersonate a security guard
, which is
helpful to a top-down interpretation of the tree.
An expression like

b\triangleleft\mathtt{SAND}(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})

denotes an attack tree of which the top node is labelled with

b
, and which has
n
children
t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}
that have to be achieved sequentially.

Example 3.1
.

Figure
1
illustrates a
\mathtt{SAND}
attack tree in which
the goal of the attacker is to gain unauthorized access to a server.
Using shorthands for labels, this tree can be represented by
the following expression:

\mathit{a}\triangleleft\mathtt{SAND}(\mathit{c}\triangleleft\mathtt{OR}(\mathit{eu}\triangleleft\mathtt{SAND}(\mathit{w},\mathit{ec}),\mathit{b},\mathit{x}),\mathit{l})
.

We define the auxiliary function
top
to obtain the label at the root node
as follows (for
\Delta\in\{\mathtt{OR},\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
):

\mathit{top}(b)=\mathit{top}(b\triangleleft\Delta(t,\ldots,t))=b\text{.}

Next, we briefly introduce useful notations about the structure of attack
trees.
We say that
t^{\prime}
is a
subtree
of
t=b\triangleleft\Delta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
,
denoted
t^{\prime}\preccurlyeq t
, if
t^{\prime}=t
or

t^{\prime}\preccurlyeq t_{i}
for some
i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where
\Delta\in\{\mathtt{OR},\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
.
If
t
is a simple tree, i.e. a tree with a single node,
then
t
itself is a subtree of
t
.
When the subtree
t^{\prime}
is not equal to
t
, we say that
t^{\prime}
is a
proper
subtree
.
We use the auxiliary function
size(.)
to denote the size of an attack
tree
measured in terms of its number of nodes. A recursive definition of the

size
function is as follows:

size(b)=1
if
b\in\mathbb{B}
;
size(b\triangleleft\Delta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}))=size(t_{1})+\dots+size(t_{n})+1
when
\Delta\in\{\mathtt{OR},\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
.

3.2
The SP semantics

Because attack trees of different shape may well represent the same

attack scenario
, researchers have been providing attack trees with
precise and unambiguous interpretations
[
30
,
31
,
20
]
.
This makes it possible to guarantee consistency of analyses performed
on attack trees and to determine whether two apparently different trees do
cover the same attacks. In this article, we use the
semantics (a.k.a. interpretation) of
\mathtt{SAND}
attack trees
given in
[
20
]
, known as

the SP semantics
.

The SP semantics encodes an attack tree as a set of

Series-Parallel graphs
(SP
graphs). An SP graph is an edge-labelled directed graph with a
source

vertex and a
sink
vertex. The simplest SP graph has the form

u\xrightarrow{b}v
, where
b
is an edge label,
u
is the
source

vertex because it has no incoming edges, and
v
is the
sink
vertex
because it has no outgoing edges. Any other SP graph is obtained as the
composition of single-edge SP graphs.

Two composition operators are used to build SP graphs: the sequential
composition operator (
\cdot
) and the parallel composition operator
(
\parallel
). A sequential composition joins the sink vertex of a graph with the
source vertex of the other graph. For example, given
G=u\xrightarrow{b}v

and
G^{\prime}=x\xrightarrow{z}y
, we obtain that
G\cdot G^{\prime}=u\xrightarrow{b}v\xrightarrow{z}y
. Note that the source vertex of
G^{\prime}
has been replaced in

G\cdot G^{\prime}
by the sink vertex
v
of
G
.
A parallel composition, instead, joins the source vertices of both graphs and
joins the sink vertices of both graphs. For example, given
G=u\xrightarrow{b}v

and
G^{\prime}=x\xrightarrow{z}y
, the parallel composition
G\parallel G^{\prime}
gives
the
following SP graph. It is worth remarking that whether vertex
u
or
x
is
preserved in a parallel
composition is irrelevant as far as the SP semantics is concerned.

u
v
b
z

Both composition operators are extended to sets of SP graphs as follows:
given sets of SP graphs
d_{1},\ldots,d_{k}
,

\begin{array}[]{l}d_{1}\parallel\dots\parallel d_{k}=\{G_{1}\parallel\dots\parallel G_{k}\ \mid\ (G_{1},...,G_{k})\in d_{1}\times...\times d_{k}\}\\
d_{1}\cdot\dots\cdot d_{k}=\{G_{1}\cdot\dots\cdot G_{k}\ \mid\ (G_{1},...,G_{k})\in d_{1}\times...\times d_{k}\}.\\
\end{array}

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{B}}
denote the set of SP graphs labelled with the
elements of
\mathbb{B}
.
The
SP semantics
for
\mathtt{SAND}
attack trees is given by the function

[\hskip-1.42271pt[\cdot]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}:\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{B}}\to\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{B}})
,
which is defined recursively as follows:
for
b\in\mathbb{B}
,
t_{i}\in\mathbb{T}_{b}
,
1\leq i\leq k
,

\begin{array}[]{l}[\hskip-1.42271pt[b]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=\{\xrightarrow{b}\}\\
[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathtt{OR}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{k}{[\hskip-1.42271pt[t_{i}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}}\\
[\hskip-1.42271pt[\and(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=[\hskip-1.42271pt[t_{1}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}\parallel...\parallel[\hskip-1.42271pt[t_{k}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}\\
[\hskip-1.42271pt[\mathtt{SAND}(t_{1},\dots,t_{k})]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=[\hskip-1.42271pt[t_{1}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}\cdot...\cdot[\hskip-1.42271pt[t_{k}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}.\\
\end{array}

We use
t=_{S}t^{\prime}
to denote semantic equivalence of two
trees
t,t^{\prime}\in\mathbb{T}_{\mathbb{B}}
, which is defined by

t=_{S}t^{\prime}\iff[\hskip-1.42271pt[t]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=[\hskip-1.42271pt[t^{\prime}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}
.

Example 3.2
.

The
\mathtt{SAND}

\{\xrightarrow{w}\xrightarrow{ec}\xrightarrow{l},\xrightarrow{b}\xrightarrow{l},\xrightarrow{x}\xrightarrow{l}\}
.

3.3
The underlying graph structure of attack trees

Figure
3
shows an attack tree whose semantics is also

\{\xrightarrow{w}\xrightarrow{ec}\xrightarrow{l},\xrightarrow{b}\xrightarrow{l},\xrightarrow{x}\xrightarrow{l}\}
. That is,
the attack tree in Figure
3
is semantically equivalent to
the attack tree in Figure
1
. Their difference lies in their
underlying graph structure. One tree is flat and does not differ from a mere
list of
attacks. The other is deeper and provides potentially useful information in
the way its nodes are refined, such as the fact that a credential can be
obtained by three different means: brute-forcing, eavesdropping and
exploiting a vulnerability. This illustrates that generating
an attack tree with a given semantics can be trivial. The challenge is to
generate trees whose refinements contribute to clarity and comprehensibility.

Figure 3:
Another way to represent the attack scenario depicted in
Figure
1
.

Existing generation approaches, in general, are not explicit about the graph
properties of the attack tree they produce. We argue that this is problematic,
because it thwarts a much needed comparison based on objective measures amongst
existing
synthesising
procedures. While readability and comprehensibility
might be subjective, graph
properties are not. In this article we propose a generation approach that seeks
to minimise the size of the tree, as it represents a simple objective measure
on the
number of labels an analyst needs to parse to understand the whole attack
scenario.
Should future works consider other relevant structural measures
for the underlying graph
structure of the attack tree, such as their depth or branching factor,
they can be easily accommodated within our
formulation.

3.4
The meaning of attacks and goals

Like in
[
18
]
, we assume attacks are not
arbitrary, or, even if they are, they achieve some goal. Hence we assume the
existence of a total relation
\vdash\subseteq\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{B}}\times\mathbb{B}

expressing whether an attack satisfies a goal.
The domain of the goal relation follows from the
\mathtt{SAND}
-attack-tree grammar,
which
defines an attacker goal as a label in

\mathbb{B}
, and the SP semantics, which defines an attack as an SP graph over

\mathbb{B}
.
The need for building a language to precisely define attacks and goals is
discussed in

[
18
]
at length. Later in Section

we introduce one
such
language, together with a system specification to obtain attacks and their
goals.

Existing attack-tree generation procedures have silently
assumed a
relation between attacks and goals. The reason is that, given a tree

t
with root goal

top(t)
, it is expected that all attacks captured by
t
satisfy the
root goal
top(t)
.
We make that expectation explicit by characterising when an attack tree is

correctly-labelled
.

Definition 3.2

(Correctly-labelled tree)
.

We say that an attack tree
t
is
correctly-labelled
, with respect
to a goal relation
\vdash
, if every subtree
t^{\prime}

in
t
satisfies
that

a\in[\hskip-1.42271pt[t^{\prime}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}\implies a\vdash top(t^{\prime})
.

Our correctness property on labels is stronger than the one provided in

[
18
]
, as they only require the root node to be correctly labelled.
We
believe their choice follows from their attack tree grammar, which let
intermediate nodes to go unlabelled.
We point out, however, that labels are
as relevant for the root node as for any other node of the tree. Hence we
require all labels of the tree to be correct.

Now, in the same way as there are many trees with the same semantics but with
different shapes, there might be many correctly-labelled trees with the same
semantics but with different labels. It remains to decide
which, amongst all correctly-labelled trees with a given semantics,
are better suited to be the output of the attack-tree generation problem.
We do so by arguing that a root goal of a (sub-)tree
should give the minimum information necessary for the analyst to make sound
conclusions about the
attacks
the tree is representing.
Next we define what we mean by
minimum information
.

Given a goal
g\in\mathbb{B}
, let
\mathit{attacks}(g)

be the set of attacks satisfying
g
, i.e.,

\mathit{attacks}(g)=\{a\in\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{B}}|a\vdash g\}
. Observe that
\mathit{attacks}(g)
gives information about
g

that is independent from
the attack-tree model. The analyst, just by looking at the root goal
g

of a correctly-labelled tree
t
, can soundly conclude that all attacks in
t

belong to
\mathit{attacks}(g)
.
Thus, given an attack tree
t
with root goal
g
,
we interpret
\mathit{attacks}(g)
as
the prior knowledge of the analyst, which is independent from
t
, and

[\hskip-1.42271pt[t]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}
as the posterior knowledge, which the analyst learns by looking at
how
t
fully or partially breaks down

into smaller
components.
Under that interpretation,
we say that an attack
a
is missing if
g

indicates that
a
is present, i.e.
a\in\mathit{attacks}(g)
, yet
a
is not in
t
, i.e.

a\not\in[\hskip-1.42271pt[t]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}
.
In words, a missed attack is an attack hinting at a label that does not appear
in the
tree.
Therefore, we will be looking to label trees in such a way that the number of
missed attacks is minimised.

Definition 3.3

(Optimally-labelled tree)
.

Let
t
be a correctly-labelled attack tree. Let
\textbf{loss}(t)=|\mathit{attacks}(top(t))\setminus[\hskip-1.42271pt[t]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}|
, where

\setminus
is the set-difference operator and
top(t)
the root
goal of
t
, be
the
number of missed attacks caused by the label
top(t)
. We say
that
t
is

optimally-labelled
if the following two conditions hold:

1.

All subtrees of
t
are optimally labelled

2.

If
t\equiv g\triangleleft\Delta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with
\Delta\in\{\mathtt{OR},\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
, then every tree of the form
g^{\prime}\triangleleft\Delta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with

\textbf{loss}(g^{\prime}\triangleleft\Delta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}))<\textbf{loss}(t)
has to be
incorrectly-labelled.

In the definition above we are assuming that the set of attacks is finite,
which seems reasonable as we are building finite attack trees.

Definition 3.4

(The attack-tree generation problem with optimal labelling
and shape)
.

Given a finite set of attacks
\mathcal{A}\subseteq\mathbb{G}_{\mathbb{B}}
and
goal
relation
\vdash
,
the attack-tree
generation problem
consists in finding an
optimally-labelled tree
t
with semantics
\mathcal{A}
and optimal
shape (i.e. with minimum size).

That is, if
t
is the output of the
generation algorithm, then there does not exist another optimally-labelled
tree

t^{\prime}

with
[\hskip-1.42271pt[t^{\prime}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=[\hskip-1.42271pt[t]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}=\mathcal{A}
and
size(t^{\prime})<size(t)
.

We finish this section by remarking that other optimality criteria can be
considered in the attack-tree generation problem, yet insisting that making
those criteria
explicit is the only way to objectively compare automated
generation
approaches.

4
A heuristic based on factoring to minimise the attack tree size

In this section we introduce a heuristic to minimise the size of attack trees
based on factoring algebraic expressions, which has been
long studied in algebra and digital circuits.
We start the section by
motivating the use
of attack trees in the factored form, then reduce the problem of factorising
attack
trees to the problem of factorising algebraic expressions.

4.1
Attack trees in the factored form

Consider the following set of attacks

\{\xrightarrow{a}\xrightarrow{b},\xrightarrow{c}\xrightarrow{d}\}
and, for the moment, let’s ignore the
labels of intermediate nodes.
In this case, it is easy to prove that an attack tree with minimum size with
such
semantics is

t=\epsilon\triangleleft\mathtt{OR}(\epsilon\triangleleft\mathtt{SAND}(a,b),\epsilon\triangleleft\mathtt{SAND}(c,d))
, where
\epsilon
denotes a placeholder for labels.
The proof follows from the observation that
\{\xrightarrow{a}\xrightarrow{b},\xrightarrow{c}\xrightarrow{d}\}
is
irreducible
, in the sense that it
cannot be expressed by an attack tree of the form
b\triangleleft\Delta(t_{1},\ldots,t_{n})
with
\Delta\in\{\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
.
We borrow the notion of irreducibility from the area of polynomial
factorisation where, roughly speaking,
a polynomial is said to be irreducible if it cannot be expressed as the product
of two non-constant polynomials.

Definition 4.1

(Irreducibility and the factored form for attack trees)
.

We say that a set of SP graphs
\mathcal{G}
is

reducible
if there exists a
\mathtt{SAND}
tree of the form

with
\Delta\in\{\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}

such that

.
Moreover, we say that

and that

if

,
\ldots

[\hskip-1.42271pt[t_{n}]\hskip-1.42271pt]_{\mathcal{S\!P}}
are not reducible.

Factorisation is a popular technique for the simplification of algebraic
expressions and we consider it equally useful for the simplification
(a.k.a. minimisation) of attack trees. The reason is that,
in the same way that

x*y
in general has more terms than
x+y
in their sum-of-product
representation, where
x
and
y
are algebraic expressions,
b\triangleleft\Delta(t,t^{\prime})
with
\Delta\in\{\and,\mathtt{SAND}\}
in general
represents a larger set of SP graphs
than
b\triangleleft\mathtt{OR}(t,t^{\prime})
. That is to say, while in attack trees conjunctive
refinements have a multiplying effect in their semantics, disjunctive
refinements
do not.
Our heuristics to solve the optimal attack-tree generation problem thus
consists of
creating attack trees in the factored form whenever possible.
Understanding under which conditions a set of SP graphs is
reducible/irreducible is the first step in this direction, which we pursue
next.

A set of SP graphs

Proposition 4.2

Let

4.3
A factorisation heuristic for expressions in idempotent
semirings

Our goal next is to introduce a factorisation algorithm for
expressions in the idempotent semiring
(\mathcal{P}(X),\cdot,\cup)
, where

(X,\cdot)
is
a semigroup and

P\cdot Q=\{p\cdot q|p\in P,q\in Q\}
, which shall be used to factorise SP graphs
straightforwardly. For
simplicity, given that the reader might be familiar
with standard algebraic manipulations, we use

as a shorthand notation for
\{x_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
,

instead of
\underbracket{x\ldots x}_{n}
, and

for

with
a,b\in\mathcal{P}(X)
. This gives, for example,
a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
instead of
\{\{a\cdot a\},\{a\cdot b\},\{b\cdot b\}\}
. We
remark,
nonetheless, that the product operator here should not be assumed to be
commutative. For example,

is not equal to
a^{2}b
, unless
\cdot
is commutative.

The solution we propose is inspired by the factorisation
procedure for logic functions introduced in
[
32
]
.
Let
us start by introducing the necessary notations.

•

A
cube
is a set

\{x_{1}\cdot\ldots\cdot x_{n}\}\in\mathcal{P}(X)
of cardinality one denoting the product
x_{1}\cdots x_{n}
. It is the simplest algebraic
expression one can form, such as

and
ab
with
a,b\in X
.

•

An expression in the Sum of Product form (SoP) is an expression
of the
form

c_{1}+\ldots+c_{n}
where
c_{1},\ldots,c_{n}
are cubes.

•

The
length
of an expression
f
is the minimum positive
integer

such that there exists an SoP expression
c_{1}+\ldots+c_{n}
equal
to

f
.

•

x
is said to be a left-divisor of
f
if

y
is the largest expression, called the quotient, such that
f=x\cdot y+r
for some
r
called the remainder of the division.

Right-division is defined
analogously.
For example,
a
is a left-divisor of

a^{2}+ab+b^{2}
with quotient
a+b
and remainder
b^{2}
, while
b
is
a
right-divisor with quotient
a+b
and remainder
a^{2}
. If
\cdot
was
commutative, then both right-division and left-division yield the same
result.

Proposition 4.4
.

Let
f
be an expression in SoP. Let

be the set of all pairs
(x,y)

such that

with

a cube in
f
. Let

and

\pi_{2}(R,z)=\{x|(x,z)\in R\}
be
the
projections of the first and second component of

, respectively,
onto

.
If
\{x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\}
is a set of maximum cardinality such that

\pi_{1}(R,x_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\pi_{1}(R,x_{n})
is non-empty, then

is
the quotient of the right-division of

by
\pi_{1}(R,x_{1})\cap\cdots\cap\pi_{1}(R,x_{n})
. Likewise, if

maximum
cardinality such that

Proof.

We just need to prove one of the implications, since the other one is

Let

.

∎

5
A system model to efficiently reason about attacks and goals

Two main questions remain unanswered in our approach. First,
how can attacks and their relation to goals be obtained from a system model?
Second, what sort of goal relation would allow for an efficient implementation
of the procedure
FindOptimalLabel
?
This section gives answers to both questions by introducing a class of labelled
transition
systems that expresses attacks and goals in a way that
allows for implementing the procedure

FindOptimalLabel
efficiently.

5.1
Mixed Kripke Structure: the system model

A Kripke structure is a variation of the
Labelled
Transition Systems
(LTS), which are used to describe
the behaviour
of a system by defining the transitions that bring a system from one
state into another. Each state in a Kripke structure contains properties that
hold in the corresponding state,
such
as the knowledge of an attacker or the location of a secret document.
The transition system
progresses via application of transition rules until a final state is reached.

Formally, a
Mixed kripke Structure
is a quadruple

(\mathcal{S},\Lambda,\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\to}},s_{0},F)
, where
\mathcal{S}
is a set of
states
;
\Lambda
is a set of

labels
;
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\to}}\subseteq\mathcal{S}\times\Lambda\times\mathcal{S}
is a

transition relation
;

s_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
is
the
initial
state
and
F\subset\mathcal{S}
is a set of final states.
We usually write
s\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}
to say that
(s,a,s^{\prime})\in\stackrel{{\scriptstyle}}{{\to}}
. And we say that

s_{0}\xrightarrow{a_{0}}s_{1}\xrightarrow{a_{1}}s_{2}\cdots s_{n-1}\xrightarrow{a_{n-1}}s_{n}

is a path if
s_{n}\in F
and

s_{i}\xrightarrow{a_{i}}s_{i+1}
for
every

i\in\{0,\ldots,n-1\}
.
Any portion of the path formed by one or more
consecutive transitions is called a
subpath
.
When
s_{0}
is a final state, then
s_{0}
itself is
a path with no transition.
We use
\mathit{paths}(l)
to denote the set of all
paths
generated by a labelled transition system
l
.

States

As mentioned earlier, states contains system properties,
which we formalise as predicates constructed over a signature

\Sigma
(i.e. a
collection of function symbols). Function symbols of arity zero are called
constants and are written without parentheses, such the name of a system actor
(e.g.

\mathit{Bob}
) or the
name
of an item (e.g.
\mathit{pwd}
to refer to a password). Function symbols with non-zero
arity surround within parentheses their arguments, such as

\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Bob},\mathit{pwd})
to denote that
\mathit{Bob}
knows password

\mathit{pwd}
.

The transition relation

The transition relation is defined through

transition rules
.
Every transition rule is of the form
{[\mathit{name}]}\frac{C}{~s\xrightarrow{a}f(s)~}
, and
contains a condition
C
and a conclusion
s\xrightarrow{a}f(s)
. The name of
a transition rule
is given left of the line. The condition
C
consists of zero or more
predicates
p
that
must be present in state
s
to enable the transition rule. The conclusion
s\xrightarrow{a}f(s)

describes the state change when the transition occurs, with
f
a function
mapping old state
s
to a new state
f(s)
. The arrow is labelled with the
event that describes the transition. The predicates may contain variables which
are implicitly universally quantified.

5.2
A network security example

Next, we illustrate how the introduced system model can be used to provide a
small
network security scenario with a formal specification.

System description

The system example consists of a computer network where users log in to
password-protected servers.

We use
U
and
\mathit{P}
to denote
the set of users and passwords, respectively. Ownership of a password is
denoted by the predicate
\mathtt{knows}(u,\mathit{psw})
, where
u\in U
and
\mathit{psw}\in\mathit{P}
.
The predicate

\mathtt{loggedin}(u,c)
denotes that the user
u\in U
has
logged into the server
serv\in S
.

Let

\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw})
be a predicate denoting that server
serv\in S

accepts password
\mathit{psw}\in\mathit{P}
. Then, a user
u
can connect
to a server

serv
if the user knows a password that the server accepts. This is
expressed
by the following rule.

{[\mathit{\mathtt{loggingIn}}]}\frac{u\in U,serv\in S,\mathit{psw}\in\mathit{P},\mathtt{knows}(u,\mathit{psw}),\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw})}{s\xrightarrow{\mathtt{loggingIn}(u,serv)}{s}\cup\{\mathtt{loggedin}(u,serv)\}}

Up to here we have specified a system where regular users
can log into a server, provided they have the right credential.

The next step is to formalise possible actions that a malicious user can take.
We start specifying the

\mathtt{exploit}
rule, which allows a malicious user
to, via an exploit,
create a
new credential on a server.

{[\mathit{\mathtt{exploit}}]}\frac{u\in U,serv\in S,\mathit{psw}\in\mathit{P},\mathtt{knows}(u,\mathit{psw})}{s\xrightarrow{\mathtt{exploit}(u,serv)}{s}\cup\{\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw})\}}

The
rule
\mathtt{forcePsw}
specifies how an attacker can bruteforce a password and find
a
suitable credential on a server.

{[\mathit{\mathtt{forcePsw}}]}\frac{u\in U,\mathit{psw}\in\mathit{P},\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw})}{s\xrightarrow{\mathtt{forcePsw}(u,serv)}{s}\cup\{\mathtt{knows}(u,\mathit{psw})\}}

Lastly, the
rule
\mathtt{listen}
expresses that a malicious user can eavesdrop on a connection
of
another user in order to find a credential
for
the server.

{[\mathit{\mathtt{listen}}]}\frac{u,u^{\prime}\in U,serv\in S,\mathit{psw}\in\mathit{P},\mathtt{loggedin}(u,serv),\\
\mathtt{knows}(u,\mathit{psw}),\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw})}{s\xrightarrow{\mathtt{listen}(u^{\prime},u,serv,\mathit{psw})}{s}\cup\{\mathtt{knows}(u^{\prime},\mathit{psw})\}}

In this rule,
u^{\prime}
is the malicious user.

Initial state.

The initial state of our system consists of a user
\mathit{Bob}
with password

\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}
and a server
serv
that accepts passwords

\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}
. We consider another user
\mathit{Eve}
with password

\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}}
that is not accepted by
serv
. That is to say,
our
initial state
s_{0}
is
\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Bob},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}),\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}}),\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\}
.

Final states.

Lastly, we define the set of final states to be any state where
\mathit{Eve}
is
logged in the server, i.e.
F=\{s\in\mathcal{S}|\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},s)\in s\}
. Each of those final states represents
a security breach, one where
\mathit{Eve}
logged in to the server without being
authorised.

Paths.

By analysing the system transition rules, one can compute the following
three paths that lead to a successful attack, i.e. that allows the
system to move from the initial state to a final state. Note that, this is not
an exhaustive list of paths.

1.

s_{0}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{exploit}(\mathit{Eve},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}})\}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{loggingIn}(\mathit{Eve},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}}),\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},serv)\}

2.

s_{0}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{forcePsw}(\mathit{Eve},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{loggingIn}(\mathit{Eve},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}),\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},serv)\}

3.

s_{0}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{loggingIn}(\mathit{Bob},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Bob},serv)\}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{listen}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{Bob},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Bob},serv),\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\}

\xrightarrow{\mathtt{loggingIn}(\mathit{Eve},serv)}

s_{0}\cup\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Bob},serv),\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}),\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},serv)\}

5.3
From paths in an LTS to SP graphs

The generation algorithm requires attacks expressed as SP graphs and a goal
relation.
A straightforward transformation of an LTS path
into an SP graph is as follows.
A single transition
s\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}
results in the simple SP graph
(s,a,s^{\prime})
, i.e. in an SP
graph with a single vertex labelled
(s,a,s^{\prime})
. A subpath

s_{i}\xrightarrow{a_{i}}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-1}\xrightarrow{a_{j-1}}s_{j}

is transformed into the SP graph

(s_{i},a_{i},s_{i+1})\cdot\ldots\cdot(s_{j-1},a_{j-1},s_{j})
where

\cdot
is the sequential composition operator.
In this case, an SP graph label carries the same information as a
transition in an LTS path.

Because SP graph labels become attack tree labels in our attack-tree generation
approach, we argue for a transformation that produces smaller labels. In
particular,
we propose a transformation where each SP graph label focuses on what the
attacker
has accomplished by making a transition from a state
s
to another
state
s^{\prime}
, rather than on the entire system state. Given a transition

s\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}
, what the attacker has accomplished is the subtraction of the properties

s\setminus s^{\prime}
from the system, where
\setminus
is the set subtraction
operator, and the addition of the properties
s^{\prime}\setminus s
.
Therefore,
we transform a transition
s\xrightarrow{a}s^{\prime}
into a simple SP graph

(s\setminus s^{\prime},s^{\prime}\setminus s)
.
Take the following transition as an example.

\displaystyle\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Bob},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}),\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}}),\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\}\xrightarrow{\mathtt{exploit}(\mathit{Eve},serv)}

\displaystyle\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Bob},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}}),\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}}),\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}})\}

Rather than carrying all the information in the transition, we propose to
succinctly express this step by
(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}})\})
, which describes
an attack step
where the adversary manages to add the password
\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}}
to the
server
serv
by launching an exploit.
We extend this transformation to subpaths in the
straightforward way, which would give the following SP graphs
corresponding to the three LTS paths
obtained from our network example:

1.

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{accepts}(serv,\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Eve}})\})\cdot

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},serv)\})

2.

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\})\cdot

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},serv)\})

3.

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Bob},serv)\})\cdot

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{knows}(\mathit{Eve},\mathit{psw}_{\mathit{Bob}})\})\cdot

(\emptyset,\{\mathtt{loggedin}(\mathit{Eve},serv)\})

Definition 5.1

(Goal relation)
.

An attack goal is a pair of sets of predicates
(P^{-},P^{+})\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{P})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{P})
where

P^{-}
and
P^{+}
contains the system properties the attacker
wishes to remove and add, respectively. We say that an SP graph
G=(P^{-}_{i},P^{+}_{i})\cdot\ldots\cdot(P^{-}_{j},P^{+}_{j})
obtained from an LTS subpath,

s_{i}\xrightarrow{a_{i}}s_{i+1}\cdots s_{j-1}\xrightarrow{a_{j-1}}s_{j}
,
satisfies
a goal
(P^{-},P^{+})
, denoted
G\vdash(P^{-},P^{+})
, if
the following two conditions
hold:

1.

P^{-}\subseteq s_{i}\setminus s_{j}
, meaning that each property in
P^{-}
has been removed from the
system.

2.

P^{+}\subseteq s_{j}\setminus s_{i}
, meaning
that every
property in
P^{+}
has been added
to the system.

Note that an SP graph over
\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{P})\times\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{P})
may satisfy many goals. In particular, if

G\vdash(X,Y)
, then
G\vdash(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
for every
X^{\prime}\subseteq X,Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
. That is, the fewer predicates
in a goal, the more
achievable
it becomes.
In particular,
G\vdash(\emptyset,\emptyset)

for every SP graph
G
, which makes
\vdash
a total relation. Further note that none of the SP graphs that
we produce from an LTS specification contains
parallel composition. This means that attack trees whose semantics is

\mathit{attacks}(l)
may contain disjunctive (
\mathtt{OR}
) and
sequential conjunctive (
\mathtt{SAND}
) refinements, but not conjunctive
refinement (
\and
).

We are ready now to establish the main result of this section, which shows that
the goal relation above allows for an efficient implementation of the

FindOptimalLabel
.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\mathcal{G}=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
be a set of SP graphs obtained,
respectively, from the following LTS subpaths,

\displaystyle\tau_{1}=s_{i_{1}}^{1}\xrightarrow{a_{i_{1}}^{1}}s_{i_{1}+1}^{1}\cdots s_{j_{1}-1}^{1}\xrightarrow{a_{j_{1}-1}^{1}}s_{j_{1}}^{1}

\displaystyle\cdots

\displaystyle\tau_{n}=s_{i_{n}}^{n}\xrightarrow{a_{i_{n}}^{n}}s_{i_{n}+1}^{n}\cdots s_{j_{n}-1}^{n}\xrightarrow{a_{j_{n}-1}^{1}}s_{j_{n}}^{n}

A goal
b
satisfying that
\mathcal{G}\subseteq\mathit{attacks}(b)

with
|\mathit{attacks}(b)|
minimum, i.e. a solution to the

FindOptimalLabel
problem on input
(\vdash,\mathcal{G})
, can be
calculated by,

\displaystyle b=\left((s_{i_{1}}^{1}\setminus s_{j_{1}}^{1})\cap\ldots\cap(s_{i_{n}}^{n}\setminus s_{j_{n}}^{n}),(s_{j_{1}}^{1}\setminus s_{i_{1}}^{1})\cap\ldots\cap(s_{j_{n}}^{n}\setminus s_{i_{n}}^{n})\right)

Proof.

Recall that
\mathit{attacks}(b)
contains all SP graphs that satisfy
b
.
The proof is based on the following property of the goal relation: if
g\vdash(X,Y)
,
X^{\prime}\subseteq X
and
Y^{\prime}\subseteq Y
, then
g\vdash(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
. A corollary of this result is that
\mathit{attacks}((X,Y))\subseteq\mathit{attacks}((X^{\prime},Y^{\prime}))
.

Assume there exists another
common goal
b^{\prime}=(P^{-},P^{+})
with
|\mathit{attacks}(b^{\prime})|<|\mathit{attacks}(b)|
. Because
b^{\prime}
is satisfied by all SP graphs in

\mathcal{G}
, it must be
the case that
P^{-}\subseteq(s_{i_{k}}^{k}\setminus s_{j_{k}}^{k})
and
P^{+}\subseteq(s_{j_{k}}^{k}\setminus s_{i_{k}}^{k})
for every
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, which
gives
P^{-}\subseteq(s_{i_{1}}^{1}\setminus s_{j_{1}}^{1})\cap\ldots\cap(s_{i_{n}}^{n}\setminus s_{j_{n}}^{n})
and
P^{+}\subseteq(s_{j_{1}}^{1}\setminus s_{i_{1}}^{1})\cap\ldots\cap(s_{j_{n}}^{n}\setminus s_{i_{n}}^{n})
. This gives that

\mathit{attacks}(b)\subseteq\mathit{attacks}(b^{\prime})
, which contradicts

|\mathit{attacks}(b^{\prime})|<|\mathit{attacks}(b)|
.
∎

The
FindOptimalLabel
procedure derived in
Theorem
5.1
calculates
2n
set intersections. In general,
that would result in a worst-cased complexity
\mathcal{O}(n\cdot|\mathbb{P}|)
. In practice, however, we argue this algorithm is much more
efficient as labels only contains changes in the
system states, hence they should be relatively small in comparison to the
system states.

We finally have all the ingredients necessary to showcase our attack-tree
generation
approach by using the output of the LTS specification from our running example.
The result is displayed by Figure

4
on input the three SP
graphs listed above. When drawing the tree we
removed unnecessary
information to make the labels easier to read. We also added to the leaf nodes’
labels
the name of the action of the transition used to obtain the leaf node’s label.
Even though actions are not part of an attack goal, they can be carried as
metadata to improve the readability of the tree.

Figure 4:
A graphical representation of the tree generated by our approach
on input the three SP graphs produced from our network example.

The resulting tree expresses the following attack scenario. An attack occurs
when a malicious user
\mathit{Eve}
logged into the server.
\mathit{Eve}
can do so
in two ways. On the one hand,
\mathit{Eve}
can includes her own password in the
server, which she accomplishes via an exploit. On the other hand,
\mathit{Eve}

may
learn
\mathit{Bob}
’s
password, which she accomplishes in two ways: by brute-forcing
\mathit{Bob}
’s
password or eavesdropping
on the communication
between
\mathit{Bob}
and the server. The latter requires
\mathit{Bob}
to log in to the
server.

Notice how the produced tree provides
just
the necessary information
needed to understand the attacker’s goal at each level of the tree. For
example, the
root’s children on the right says that
\mathit{Eve}

needs to learn
\mathit{Bob}
’s password, but it does not tell how. The deeper the
analyst goes onto the tree, the more details about the attack it obtains.

6
Conclusions

We have introduced
the first attack-tree generation framework
that optimises the labelling and shape of the generated trees, while
guaranteeing their soundness and correctness with respect to a system
specification.
The core of the
article
is dedicated to describing and proving correct the various algorithms involved
in the generation approach. Particularly relevant is our reduction of the tree
minimisation problem to
the factorisation problem of algebraic
expressions, which calls for further research on the factorisation of
(commutative) semirings without multiplicative
identity.
We showed the feasibility of our approach by introducing a system model that
couples well with our generation framework, and validated the entire approach
via a small running example.
Future work will be directed towards
interfacing the generation framework with a model checker tool, such as
mCRL2
3
3

3

https://www.mcrl2.org/web/index.html
, to allow for a
large-scale validation of the approach.

Theorem
4.1
.

The set
cover problem is polynomial-time reducible to the
problem of finding the smallest partition
\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
of a set of graphs
\mathcal{G}
such that, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,

\mathcal{G}_{i}

is an homogeneous set of SP graphs and there exists a label

b
such that
\forall g\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\colon g\vdash b
.

Proof.

First, we describe the set cover problem.
Let
U
be a set
of
elements and

\mathcal{S}=\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}\}
subsets
of
U
. A set
\mathcal{C}\subseteq\mathcal{S}
is said to be a cover if

\bigcup_{S\in\mathcal{C}}S=U
. The set cover problem consists in finding a cover
\mathcal{C}

of minimum
cardinality.

Without loss of generality, assume
U=\{g_{1},\ldots,g_{n}\}
is a
homogeneous set of SP graphs. Likewise, we shall consider

\{S_{1},\ldots,S_{m}\}
to be labels of
\mathtt{SAND}
trees.
Build the goal relation
\vdash
as follows:

g_{i}\vdash S_{j}\iff g_{i}\in S_{j}

First, let us show how to obtain, given a cover

S_{i_{1}},\ldots,S_{i_{k}}
, a partition
\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
of
U
such that, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
there exists a label

S_{j}
such that
\forall g\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\colon g\vdash S_{j}
. The algorithm works as follows.
From
j=1
to
j=k-1
, let
\mathcal{G}_{j}
be the set containing all
elements in
S_{i_{j}}
that do not appear in the remaining sets

S_{i_{j+1}},\ldots,S_{i_{k}}
. Let
\mathcal{G}_{k}=S_{i_{k}}
.
It follows that
\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
is a partition of
U
. Now, because
\mathcal{G}_{j}\subseteq S_{i_{j}}
, we obtain that

\forall g\in\mathcal{G}_{j}\colon g\vdash S_{i_{j}}
.

Second, let us show how to obtain a solution to the set cover
problem,
given a
partition
\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
of minimum cardinality
of
U
such that, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
there exists a label

S_{j}
such that
\forall g\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\colon g\vdash S_{j}
.
Let

f\colon\{1,\ldots,k\}\rightarrow\{1,\ldots,m\}
be a total mapping
satisfying that, for
every
i\in\{1,\ldots,k\}
,
g\in\mathcal{G}_{i}\implies g\vdash S_{f(i)}
. Note that there exists at
least one such mapping.
Because
g\vdash S_{f(i)}\implies g\in S_{f(i)}
, we obtain that

\mathcal{G}_{i}\subseteq S_{f(i)}
. Therefore,
S_{f(1)},\ldots,S_{f(k)}

is a covering of
U
.

We conclude the proof by showing that
S_{f(1)},\ldots,S_{f(k)}
is a
minimum
cover.
This follows from our first proof step, which shows that a cover
S_{i_{1}},\ldots,S_{i_{k^{\prime}}}
of
U
leads to a partition of
cardinality
k^{\prime}
. That is, the existence of a smaller cover implies the
existence of a smaller partition, which contradicts the
premise that
\{\mathcal{G}_{1},\ldots,\mathcal{G}_{k}\}
has minimum
cardinality.
It is worth remarking that this generalises the proof provided in our
previous
work
[
6
]
, as it imposes no restriction on the attacks, nor
on
the goal
relation.
