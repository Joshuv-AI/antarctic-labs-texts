A stochastic use of the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz property: Investigation of optimization algorithms behaviours in a non-convex differentiable framework.
Jean-Baptiste Fest, Audrey Repetti, Emilie Chouzenoux

Abstract
Asymptotic analysis of generic stochastic algorithms often relies on descent conditions. In a convex setting, some technical shortcuts can be considered to establish asymptotic convergence guarantees of the associated scheme. However, in a non-convex setting, obtaining similar guarantees is usually more complicated, and relies on the use of the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (KŁ) property. While this tool has become popular in the field of deterministic optimisation, it is much less widespread in the stochastic context and the few works making use of it are essentially based on trajectory-by-trajectory approaches. In this paper, we propose a new framework for using the KŁ property in a non-convex stochastic setting based on conditioning theory. We show that this framework allows for deeper asymptotic investigations on stochastic schemes verifying some generic descent conditions. We further show that our methodology can be used to prove convergence of generic stochastic gradient descent (SGD) schemes, and unifies conditions investigated in multiple articles of the literature.

Introduction

The objective of this work is to approximate a solution of an unconstrained optimization problem of the form of

\underset{{\mathb{w}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}}{\text{minimize}}\quad F({\mathb{w}}),

(1.1)

where

Specifically, we will investigate the behaviour of a process
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
defined on a probabilistic space
\left(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}}\right)
and belonging to the finite dimensional space
{\mathbb{R}}^{N}
, that will aim to asymptotically approximate such a solution

[

,

]
.
Celebrated examples are often based on stochastic gradient descent (SGD) algorithms.

When (
1.1
) is solved using a deterministic scheme, a set of recent proofs of convergence results rely on the Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz (
KŁ
) property, that has the advantage of promoting interesting asymptotic behaviors when
F
is non-necessarily convex.
In this context,
KŁ
has been used to prove convergence of proximal point algorithms
[
2
,
3
]
, of simple splitting algorithms such as the forward-backward algorithm and its variants
[

,

,

,

,

, as well as other algorithms based on the majorization-minimization principle

,

A natural question is to investigate the transfer of such proof techniques from the deterministic setting, to the stochastic setting, for asymptotic analysis including a.s. convergence of stochastic processes.
Such an extension is quite challenging, mainly due to the dynamics of the functions involved in

In this work we will develop a new framework, based on

[

({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, for the resolution of (

) when

is not convex.

Stochastic approximation theory.

Following stochastic approximation theory introduced in
[
52
,
53
]
and similarly to the stabilisation theory for dynamic systems
[
57
]
, the asymptotic behaviour of
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
to approximate the minimum of
F
can be investigated by introducing an auxiliary function
V\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
acting as a Lyapunov function, with
{\mathcal{H}}
being a finite-dimensional Euclidean space.
To this aim, we introduce an auxiliary augmented process
({\mathbf{x}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
defined on
\left(\Omega,{\mathcal{F}},{\mathbb{P}}\right)
that will be used to study the behaviour of
V
, including process
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and any underlying parameters of the associated recursive scheme (e.g., step-sizes).
In particular, similarly to
[
59
]
, in this work we will focus on functions
V
such that
\left(V({\mathbf{x}}_{k})\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
follows a supermartingale behavior. Such an approach is instrumental to avoid the strong assumption that process
(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
verifies a supermartingale condition. In fact, stochastic approximation theory results highlight that
(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
usually only verifies almost-supermartingale condition
[
53
]
. In many cases, however, it is fairly easy to construct
V
that holds supermartingale condition and enabling to study the asymptotic behaviour of
(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

[
53
]
.

In this work, we assume that such a Lyapunov function
V
exists, and we aim to design a framework, with mild assumptions, that allows obtaining stronger asymptotic convergence results on
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, including its almost-sure convergence to a solution to problem (
1.1
).
Such a result is challenging to establish in a non-convex stochastic context, and has mainly been studied so far for particular cases.
For instance,
[
45
]
proved this result when the set of accumulation points of
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is included in a subset that is only made up of isolated points. Without this strong assumption, the most common approach to handle non-convex problems consists in adopting the ODE method
[
4
]
. This method uses the fact that the asymptotic behaviour of most stochastic schemes is very similar to the one of some differential equations.
Nevertheless the noise conditions considered in these works may be difficult to verify in practice, hence restricting their practical use.
Instead, in this work we aim to introduce a framework that allows the use of the
KŁ
property to investigate almost-sure convergence of
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
under mild assumptions that are generally assumed to be true in modern stochastic optimization (i.e., supermartingale like properties).

However, as explained earlier, although
KŁ
conditions have been widely used to investigate convergence of deterministic schemes in a non-convex context, their use in a stochastic framework is not straightforward.
In the next paragraphs we better explain the Łojasiewicz and
KŁ
properties, and give an overview of their use in the stochastic literature.

Non-convex optimization and Łojasiewicz theory.

Łojasiewicz condition
[
46
]
was originally introduced to study the behaviour of trajectories of some bounded gradient flow in a continuous setting, for the class of analytical functions, under the form of a local curvature relation.
The works of Łojasiewicz have then been transferred later to the discrete deterministic framework. In particular, it is shown in
[
1
]
that the Łojasiewicz condition can be used to establish the convergence of a deterministic iterative approximation scheme starting from a descent condition.

The Łojasiewicz condition being originally designed to study gradient flow trajectories, it naturally shares strong connections with the ODE method.
Indeed, combining this fact to works such as
[
4
,
21
]
, Benaim provided in
[
5
]
strong convergence guarantees of the SGD algorithm when applied to real analytic non-convex cost functions, without further assumption on the set of accumulating points of
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. Almost at the same time, Tadić published similar results in
[
56
]
, proving convergence of SGD iterations under the Łojasiewicz condition for real analytic non-convex cost functions, using a trajectory-based proof.
Although the works of Benaim and Tadić
[
4
,
5
,
56
]
based on Łojasiewicz condition made a breakthrough to investigating asymptotic behaviour of stochastic processes within a non-convex context, the “trajectory-by-trajectory approach” of their analysis restricts their framework to noise conditions that may be difficult to satisfy in practice.
In the recent work
[
31
]
, the noise conditions have been relaxed thanks to the use of a combination of conditioning theory with the use of Łojasiewicz condition in a non-convex setting.

Non-convex optimization and
KŁ
theory.

Kurdyka proposed a generalization of the Łojasiewicz condition in
[
43
]
that holds for differentiable functions definable an o-minimal structure. Similarly to the Łojasiewicz condition, the use of
KŁ
condition for discrete deterministic approximations is fairly recent, and its popularity is mainly due to the pioneering works
[
12
,
2
,
13
]
.
Nevertheless, the use of
KŁ
property in the stochastic framework remains rare and seems to be limited to a few articles.
In
[
35
]
, the authors used the
KŁ
condition to show
L^{1}
and
L^{2}
convergence of some stochastic schemes.
Further,
[
32
]
proposed a new version of the
KŁ
condition, in expectation, to study the a.s. convergence of their specific stochastic proximal-gradient algorithm leveraging a mini-batch structure, in a non-convex non-differentiable setting.
In
[
48
]
the authors considered a similar algorithm with more general noise conditions, but with a more restrictive
KŁ
formulation and using a “trajectory-by-trajectory” approach. Finally, the authors of
[
44
]
mention that a stochastic formulation of the
KŁ
inequality would enable to study the accumulation points of their process, without however providing any theoretical results.

Hence, to our knowledge, no asymptotic analysis including a.s. convergence of generic stochastic processes has been developed yet.

Proposition 2.4

Let

be a continuous coercive function and

be a non-empty subset of

such that
|f(C)|<+\infty
.
The closure of
C
, denoted

, can be written as a finite union of non-empty compact sets, such that

is constant on each of them.

Proof
. We first show that
C
is bounded. If not, there would exist a sequence
({\mathb{v}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
of vectors of
C
such that
\|{\mathb{v}}_{k}\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}\underset{k\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}+\infty
, which, by coercivity, conducts to
f({\mathb{v}}_{k})\underset{k\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}+\infty
and finally contradicts the finiteness of
f(C)
.

Since
|f(C)|<+\infty
, we can denote
I=|f(C)|
and
f(C)=\left\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{I}\right\}~(f_{1}\neq f_{2}\cdots\neq f_{I})
.
As
C
is bounded, it follows that
\overline{C}
is also bounded. Moreover, since
f(C)
is finite and

continuous, we also have
f\left(\overline{C}\right)=f(C)
.
So
\overline{C}\subset f^{-1}\left(f(C)\right)=\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}f^{-1}\left(\{f_{i}\}\right)
, and we can write
\overline{C}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}C_{i}
where, for all
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}

C_{i}=f^{-1}\left(\{f_{i}\}\right)\cap\overline{C}
.

Let us now fix
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
. On the one hand,
C_{i}
is closed has an intersection of two closed sets.
On the other hand, since
\overline{C}
is bounded, so is
C_{i}
.
We can hence deduce that
C_{i}
is compact (since
{\mathcal{H}}
is of finite dimension).
Moreover, due to
f(\overline{C})=f(C)(=\{f_{1},\ldots,f_{I}\})
, there exists
{\mathb{v}}\in\overline{C}
such that

and so

is not empty.
Finally, the fact that

ensures that

is constant on

(with

).

\chi_{\infty}
, the set of cluster points of
({\mathbf{x}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}
, the limit of

Proposition 2.5

Under Assumptions

(i)

({\mathbf{x}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

(ii)

\chi_{\infty}

(iii)

(iv)

.

According to Assumption
2.2
, there exists a set
\Lambda\subset\Omega
of probability one where, for all

,

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{k\rightarrow+\infty}V({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(\omega))=V_{\infty}(\omega)<+\infty,

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\Gamma\cap\chi_{\infty}(\omega)\neq\varnothing.

(2.1b)

Inequality (

(V({\mathbf{x}}_{k}(\omega)))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}

According to Assumption

It follows that the set of cluster points

(ii)

We now show that

By contradiction, if

\omega\in\Lambda

there exist

will be dedicated to examples where the proposed theoretical differentiable framework can be used.

Definition 3.3

[
KŁ
Property]

A differentiable function
f:{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies the
Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (
KŁ
) property
at
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
, if there exist a neighbourhood
{\mathcal{V}}
of
\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}
,
\zeta>0
and
\varphi\in\Phi_{\zeta}
such that

\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}~\varphi^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}}))\geqslant 1,

for every
{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{V}}
satisfying
0<f({\mathb{x}})-f(\widetilde{{\mathb{x}}})<\zeta
.
Furthermore,
f
is said to satisfy the
KŁ
property over
E\subset{\mathcal{H}}
if the latter is verified at every point of
E
.

The
KŁ
property given in Definition
3.3
is defined locally in the sense that parameter
\zeta
and function
\varphi
depend on the point we are located. As a consequence, this naturally tends to favour trajectory-by-trajectory strategies as investigated, e.g., in
[
48
]
. Instead, we aim to adopt a more global approach, as proposed in
[
13
, Lemma 6]
, leading to the “uniformized
KŁ
property”.

Theorem 3.4

[Uniformized
KŁ
property]

Let
C
be a compact subset of
{\mathcal{H}}
and
f\colon{\mathcal{H}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable function constant on
C
, satisfying the
KŁ
property over
C
. Then, there exist
(\varepsilon,\zeta)\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
and
\varphi\in\Phi_{\zeta}
such that

\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|_{{\mathcal{H}}}~\varphi^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f(\overline{{\mathb{x}}}))\geqslant 1,

for all
\overline{{\mathb{x}}}\in C
and
{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
verifying
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C)<\varepsilon
and
0<f({\mathb{x}})-f(\overline{{\mathb{x}}})<\zeta
.

We can notice that the sets
C
in Theorem
3.4
and
\Gamma
in Assumption
2.2
are both partially characterized by their image through
f
and
V
, respectively.
This similarity suggests that Theorem
3.4
can be applied to function
V
, assuming that it satisfies the
KŁ
property on
C=\Gamma
.
However, Theorem
3.4
might be over restrictive as it would require that
V(\Gamma)
is reduced to a singleton.
Since
\Gamma
links all trajectories
\big(\chi_{\infty}(\omega)\big)_{\omega\in\Omega}
, the singleton assumption would require that the image sets
\big(V(\chi_{\infty}(\omega))\big)_{\omega\in\Omega}
are themselves all reduced to a common singleton (i.e. independent from
\omega
-variable), that is not amenable to working in a non-convex context.
Hence, the uniformized
KŁ
property given in Theorem
3.4
can be applied to each
\chi_{\infty}(\omega)
, for
\omega\in\Omega
, but then the associated parameters
\zeta
and
\epsilon
, as well as the desingularizing function
\varphi
would all also depend on
\omega
. Such an approach would again require working using a trajectory-by-trajectory strategy.

As emphasized in the Introduction, in this work we aim to move away from such a strategy and develop a simplified framework to study a.s. convergence of stochastic processes.
Specifically, in the remainder of this section, we build a new version of Theorem
3.4
such that (i) the image of
C
by
f
shall not be constant anymore, and (ii)
\zeta,\epsilon,\varphi
shall remain deterministic (i.e., independent from
\omega
).
The
\omega
-dependency issue of these three last parameters has already been considered in
[
32
]
. In their work, the authors assume a mini-batch structure of the gradient-noise and give a version in expectation of Theorem
3.4
.
In contrast, we aim here to provide a version of Theorem
3.4
that is independent from the considered stochastic scheme.

3.3
Proposed extension of the uniformized
KŁ
theorem

In this section, we will give our extended version of Theorem
3.4
to the case where the set
C
can be taken as a finite union of compact sets on which
f
is constant.
This result can be seen as a combination of the uniform
KŁ
property and
[
10
, Corollary 11]
, for a more generic set
C
.
Such an extension will be necessary to provide a framework where the
KŁ
property can be used independently from the considered stochastic scheme.

Theorem 3.5

[Extended uniformized
KŁ
property]

Let
C=\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}C_{i}
be a union of
I\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
non-empty disjoint and compact subsets
(C_{i})_{1\leqslant i\leqslant I}
of
{\mathcal{H}}
, and
f\colon{\mathcal{H}}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
be a differentiable function satisfying the
KŁ
property on
C
.
We also suppose that
f
is constant on every
C_{i}
, for
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
, with respective values
f_{C_{1}},\ldots,f_{C_{I}}
. Then, there exist
(\varepsilon,\zeta)\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
and
\varphi\in\Phi_{\zeta}
such that for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
, and for every
{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
verifying
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C)<\varepsilon
and
0<f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}<\zeta
, we have

\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|\varphi^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}})\geqslant 1.

(3.2)

Proof
. Without loss of generality we consider that
f_{C_{1}}\neq\cdots\neq f_{C_{I}}
.

We start by applying the uniformized
KŁ
property
3.4
to
C_{1},\ldots,C_{I}
.
For every
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
, there exist
(\varepsilon_{i},\zeta_{i})\in(0,+\infty)^{2}
and
\varphi_{i}\in\Phi_{\zeta_{i}}
such that for every
{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
verifying
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C_{i})<\varepsilon_{i}
and
0<f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}<\zeta_{i}
we have

\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}})\geqslant 1.

(3.3)

Let
\widetilde{\zeta}=\upsilon\min_{j\neq i}|f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}}|
with
\upsilon\in(0,1/2)
. We have
\widetilde{\zeta}>0
.
In addition, using the continuity of
f
, for every
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
, there exists
\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\in(0,\varepsilon_{i})
such that, for every
{\mathb{x}}
satisfying
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C_{i})<\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}
, we have

|f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}|<\widetilde{\zeta}.

(3.4)

Let
\delta\in(0,1)
and
\varepsilon=\delta\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant I}\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}
. Then

\left\{{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\,\mid\,\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C)<\varepsilon\right\}\subset\bigcup_{i=1}^{I}\left\{{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}\,\mid\,\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C_{i})<\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}\right\}.

(3.5)

We now show that (
3.2
) is satisfied for
\varepsilon
as defined above,
\zeta=\min(\zeta_{1},\ldots\zeta_{I},\widetilde{\zeta})
and
\varphi=\sum_{i=1}^{I}\varphi_{i}\in\Phi_{\zeta}
.
Let
{\mathb{x}}\in{\mathcal{H}}
and
i\in\{1,\ldots,I\}
be such that
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C)<\varepsilon
and
0<f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}<\zeta
.
For every
j\in\{1,\ldots,I\}\setminus\{i\}
, since
\zeta\leqslant\widetilde{\zeta}
, using the definition of
\widetilde{\zeta}
, we have
0<|f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}|\leqslant v|f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}}|
.
Insofar
v\in(0,1/2)
, we obtain

\displaystyle|f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{j}}|

\displaystyle=|(f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}})+(f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}})|

\displaystyle\geqslant\left|~|f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}}|-|f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}|~\right|

\displaystyle=|f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}}|-|f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}|

\displaystyle\geqslant(1-v)|f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}}|

\displaystyle>v|f_{C_{i}}-f_{C_{j}}|

\displaystyle\geqslant\widetilde{\zeta}.

(3.6)

Then
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C_{j})\geqslant\widetilde{\varepsilon_{j}}
, otherwise this contradicts the continuity property of
f
in (
3.4
).
So, as
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C)<\varepsilon
, and due to (
3.5
), this necessarily implies that

\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C_{i})<\widetilde{\varepsilon}_{i}
and thus
\operatorname{dist}_{{\mathcal{H}}}({\mathb{x}},C_{i})<\varepsilon_{i}
(since
\widetilde{\varepsilon_{i}}\leqslant\varepsilon_{i})
.
In addition, since
\zeta\leqslant\zeta_{i}
, we have
0<f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}}<\zeta_{i}
, and we can apply once more the uniformized
KŁ
property at
C_{i}
(i.e., (
3.3
) is verified). Finally the positivity of
\varphi_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\varphi_{I}^{\prime}
ensure the desired relation as follows

\displaystyle\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|\varphi^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}})

\displaystyle=\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|\sum_{j=1}^{I}\varphi_{j}^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}})

\displaystyle\geqslant\|\nabla f({\mathb{x}})\|\varphi_{i}^{\prime}(f({\mathb{x}})-f_{C_{i}})\geqslant 1.

This completes the proof.

4.1

We introduce here a purely technical lemma which will be useful for establishing the central result of this section

and

may look technical.
On the one hand, the left hand-side inequalities (of the form
V({\mathbf{x}}_{k})\geqslant{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}]
) always hold in the context of supermartingales, as a direct consequence of Fatou’s lemma. However, on the other hand, the right hand-side inequalities (of the form
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}]\geqslant{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}
) are more technical, and indicates that the estimation of
{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}
with respect to
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
, i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}]
, must remain greater than the true limit value
{\mathrm{V}}_{\infty}
(in other word, we must “approach” this limit “from above”).

4.2
Main result

We are now ready for our main convergence theorem, that aims to provide a generic framework to show convergence of stochastic schemes in a differentiable non-convex context. To this aim, we need to combine Lemma

with Proposition

Remark 5.2

(i)

Assumption
5.1
(i)
and
(ii)
are directly related to the curvature of
F
, i.e. the function to minimize in (

). The first one is common and tends to promote the existence of a descent condition for any gradient-type schemes (both in deterministic and stochastic) via the usual descent lemma
[
7
]
. The second one is necessary to easily verify Assumption
2.2
.

(ii)

Assumption
5.1
(iii)
is very common, although implicit most of the time. It will ensure the integrability of some of almost-sure limits we will need to manipulate.

(iii)

Assumption
5.1
(iv)
ensures that
({\mathbf{U}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is
well-conditioned enough
and is not over restrictive.
First, since, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
{\mathbf{U}}_{k}
is generally constructed using the past information,
{\mathcal{F}}_{k}
-measurability condition is not too constraining.
Second, the uniformly bounded spectrum condition of sequence
({\mathbf{f}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
, which is essential to ensure the existence of a decreasing Lyapunov function associated with the problem, is directly satisfied in the convex case when
({\mathbf{U}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
approximates the second order information of
F
(if twice-differentiable) and includes regularity terms in its structure
[
18
,
60
]
. In the non-convex case, it generally requires specific update rules
[
58
,
20
]
. Such rules have been investigated extensively also in the deterministic case, in particular within majorization-minimization theory
[
36
,
26
]
.

(iv)

The first condition in Assumption
5.1
(v)
classically stipulates that, with respect to the past events, the preconditioned approximate gradient and the true one are oriented in relatively closed directions. This is typically verified in the situation where process
({\mathbf{f}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is unbiased regarding filtration
({\mathcal{F}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
i.e.
{\mathbb{E}}[{\mathbf{f}}_{k}~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}]=\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})~\mathrm{a.s.}
for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
.

The second condition, often called the

in the literature
[
42
]
, is linked to the conditional variance and indicates that it can be controlled by three constants:
A
controlling the distance between the evaluation of
F
and
F^{*}
,
B
controlling the multiplicative tolerable error with respect to the true gradient, and
C
controlling some additive error. This condition has been first introduced in
[
8
]
to investigate SGD convergence in the non-convex setting, with
A=0
.
It has then been used in
[
37
]
with
A=0
and
B=1
, and in
[
54
]
with
A=C=0
. The latter case is often called
Strong Growth Condition
.
Although this simple case allows to study SGD taking a permissive stepsize (typically a constant one), this situation is only encountered in practice when

(written in the form of an empirical risk) has all its stationary points following an interpolation condition
[
54
]
.
Examples of these
A,B,C

.

(v)

Finally, Assumption

is the standard Robbins-Monro condition
[
52
]
used to control the relative fluctuations of the variance terms, in particular generated by the additive term

.

5.2
Quasi super-martingale condition

Similarly to the historical approach of
[
53
]
, the first step to show the convergence of

as defined in (

Lemma 5.3

Under Assumption
5.1

(i)
,
(iv)
-
(vi)
, process
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
defined in (
5.1
) satisfies for all
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
:

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}[F({\mathbf{w}}_{k+1})-F^{*}|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}]

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(1+A\lambda\alpha_{k}^{2}\right)\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\right)-\alpha_{k}\left(\mu\underline{\nu}-B\lambda\alpha_{k}\right)\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}+C\lambda\alpha_{k}^{2}~\mathrm{a.s.,}

(5.2)

where
\lambda=(\beta\overline{\nu}^{2})/2

Proof
. The proof strategy for this result is very common to study stochastic optimization schemes in a differentiable setting. In our case, this can be seen as an easy generalization of the proof of
[
37
]
or
[
16
]
, that can typically be found in
[
58
]
.

A common approach to deduce asymptotic convergence results of SGD (in a non-convex setting) using (
5.3
) then consists in using the Robbins-Siegmund lemma
[
53
]
.
In a nutshell, this lemma almost surely ensures that
(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}))_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
converges to a finite limit, and that
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}<+\infty
(assuming that
(\alpha_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is small enough).
In this article, the framework we have developed aims to drop the square for the summability, i.e. we aim to obtain
\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|<+\infty
. For this we need to build a suitable Lyapunov function
V
to rely on Theorem
4.13
.
Before this, we need to reformulate the algorithm’s descent condition (

), obtained in Lemma

.

Corollary 5.4

Under Assumption
5.1

(i)
,
(iv)
-
(vi)
, the process
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
defined in (
5.1
) satisfies

(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}})\quad{\mathbb{E}}\Big[p_{k+1}^{-1}\big(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k+1})-F^{*}\big)+C\lambda r_{k+1}\big|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\Big]\\
\leqslant~p_{k}^{-1}\big(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\big)+C\lambda r_{k}-p_{k+1}^{-1}\alpha_{k}\big(\mu\underline{\nu}-\lambda B\alpha_{k}\big)\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}~\mathrm{a.s.},

(5.3)

where

(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}})\quad p_{k}:=\prod_{i=0}^{k}(1+A\lambda\alpha_{i}^{2})\quad\text{ and }\quad r_{k}:=\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}p_{i}^{-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}.

(5.4)

Proof
. We only need to show that (
5.3
) implies (
5.3
).

Since
\sum\limits_{k=0}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}^{2}<+\infty
and that, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,

, thus we have
\displaystyle\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}p_{k}^{-1}\alpha_{k}^{2}<+\infty
. This allows us to write a “difference trick” on

of the form of:

(\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}})~~p_{k}^{-1}\alpha_{k}^{2}=\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}p_{i}^{-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}-\sum_{i=k+1}^{+\infty}p_{i}^{-1}\alpha_{i}^{2}=r_{k}-r_{k+1}.

(5.5)

Multiplying, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
, (
5.3
) by

, and using the fact that the associated sequence
(p_{k}^{-1})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is non-increasing, we obtain

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[p_{k+1}^{-1}\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k+1})-F^{*}\right)|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{k+1}^{-1}\left(1+A\lambda\alpha_{k}^{2}\right)\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\right)-p_{k+1}^{-1}\alpha_{k}\left(\mu\underline{\nu}-B\lambda\alpha_{k}\right)\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}+C\lambda p_{k+1}^{-1}\alpha_{k}^{2}

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{k+1}^{-1}\left(1+A\lambda\alpha_{k}^{2}\right)\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\right)-p_{k+1}^{-1}\alpha_{k}\left(\mu\underline{\nu}-B\lambda\alpha_{k}\right)\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}+C\lambda p_{k}^{-1}\alpha_{k}^{2}.

Combining this inequality with (

) leads to the conclusion

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[p_{k+1}^{-1}\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k+1})-F^{*}\right)|{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{k}^{-1}\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\right)-p_{k+1}^{-1}\alpha_{k}\left(\mu\underline{\nu}-B\lambda\alpha_{k}\right)\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}+C\lambda(r_{k}-r_{k+1})~\mathrm{a.s.},

hence the result.

Proof

Due to assumption

(5.8)

Finally,

(iii)

Following from (ii),

(iv)

Then,

The previous proof relies on the convergence

6
Application to generic SGD scheme

In this section, we introduce our main asymptotical results related to scheme (
5.1

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{x}}_{k})\|

({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
. To our knowledge, this result is novel, and although applied here to scheme (

A simplified version of these results has been introduced by the authors in
[
25

6.1
Asymptotic behavior of generic SGD scheme in a non-convex setting

We first aim to study the asymptotic behavior of the SGD scheme given in (

Proposition 6.1

(Upper-bound in expectation for non-convex SGD)

Proof
. Let

Moreover, since

.
Then, according to Proposition
5.5

Hence there exists

Combining this inequality with (

6.2
Particular cases for stronger convergence results

The results in Propositions
6.1
and
6.2
give upper-bounds on the expectation of the summation of a power of the norm of the gradient of
F
.
In this section we will investigate particular cases allowing to deduce stronger convergence guarantees. In particular we first focus on the Strong Growth Condition, and then explore another type of assumption.

6.2.1
Almost-sure convergence of SDG under Strong Growth Condition

We consider the process generated in (
5.1
), and focus on the case when
A=C=0
in Assumption
5.1
(v)
, i.e., the usual Strong Growth Condition
[
54
]
.
In this case, it can be noticed that, for every
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
\check{{\mathrm{Y}}}_{m,k}=0
, hence the following result is a direct application of Proposition
6.2
.

Proposition 6.3

(Almost sure convergence of SGD under Strong Growth Condition)
Let
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
be the process generated by (
5.1
).
Under the same assumptions as in Proposition
6.2
, and assuming further that
C=0
in Assumption
5.1
(v)
, the process
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
almost surely converges to a stationary point of
F
.
Moreover, there exits
\varphi\in\Phi_{+\infty}
and an almost-surely finite random variable
{\mathrm{K}}>0
such that

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{k={\mathrm{K}}}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\big]\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{\overline{\nu}\sqrt{B}}\left(\underline{\nu}-\overline{\alpha}B\lambda\right)^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi\big(F({\mathbf{w}}_{0})-{\mathrm{F}}_{\infty}\big)\big],

(6.6)

where
{\mathrm{F}}_{\infty}
is the integrable limit of
\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
.

Proof
. Since
C=0
, we have, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
,
\check{{\mathrm{Y}}}_{m,k}=0
in Proposition
6.2
, and by definition
\check{V}_{m}({\mathbf{x}}_{m,k})=F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}
and
\check{{\mathrm{V}}}_{m,\infty}=F_{\infty}-F^{*}
(see (
5.9
) and (
5.10
), respectively). It follows that (
6.5
) can be simplified as:

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\sum_{k=\check{{\mathrm{K}}}_{m}}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|\right]\leqslant\left(\underline{\nu}-\overline{\alpha}B\lambda\right)^{-1}{\mathbb{E}}\big[\varphi_{m}\big(F({\mathbf{w}}_{0})-{\mathrm{F}}_{\infty}\big)\big].

(6.7)

Let
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
. According to (
5.1
), and using Assumption
5.1
(v)
, we have

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]=\alpha_{k}\left[\|{\mathbf{B}}_{k}{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\|~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]\leqslant\alpha_{k}\overline{\nu}~{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\mathbf{f}}_{k}\|~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right].

Then, applying Jensen’s inequality to the last inequality, combined with Assumption
5.1
(vi)
, yield

{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]\leqslant\alpha_{k}\overline{\nu}~\|{\mathbb{E}}\left[{\mathbf{f}}_{k}~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]\|\leqslant\alpha_{k}\overline{\nu}\sqrt{B}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|.

Combining the last result with (
6.7
), and taking
\varphi=\check{\varphi}_{m}
and
{\mathrm{K}}=\check{{\mathrm{K}}}_{m}
, we obtain (
6.6
).

Since
{\mathrm{K}}>0
is finite almost-surely and that all terms in (

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}{\mathbb{E}}\left[\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|~|~{\mathcal{F}}_{k}\right]<+\infty~~\mathrm{a.s..}

Since, fro every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|\geqslant 0
, we can use
Levy’s sharpening of Borel-Cantelli Lemma
[
47

leading to

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\|{\mathbf{w}}_{k+1}-{\mathbf{w}}_{k}\|<+\infty~~\mathrm{a.s.}.

Almost-surely
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is then of finite length (i.e. Cauchy) and so it converges. Finally, similarly to the proof of Proposition
5.5
,
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
also possesses (almost-surely) an accumulation point contained in

6.2.2
Convergence guarantees under particular convergence rate conditions

In Proposition
6.3
we shown that, under the assumption that
A=C=0
, the process
({\mathbf{w}}_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is of finite length almost surely.
In this section we aim to extend this result for a class of events even when
A
and
C
are non-zero.
Hence we introduce the two following events:

\displaystyle\Xi_{1}:=\bigcup\limits_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}\left\{\limsup\limits_{k\to+\infty}\left[\dfrac{\left(\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{m}}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}}\right]<+\infty\right\},

(6.8)

\displaystyle\Xi_{2}:=\left\{\limsup\limits_{k\to+\infty}\frac{\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\right)}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|}<+\infty\right\},

(6.9)

that will act as
control bounds
on the behaviour of the gradient and the step-size of SGD. Before giving further intuition on these bounds, we give the following convergence guarantees on these events.

Corollary 6.4

We consider the same assumptions as in Proposition
6.1
.
Then

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|<+\infty

almost-surely on the event

\Xi:=\begin{cases}\Xi_{1}~\text{if}~A=0,\\
\Xi_{2}~\text{if}~C=0,\\
\Xi_{1}\cap\Xi_{2}~\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(6.10)

Proof
. We first look at the case
\Xi=\Xi_{1}\cap\Xi_{2}~
.
We introduce

{\mathcal{A}}:=\bigcap_{m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}}\left\{\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|}{\sqrt{1+{\mathrm{Y}}_{m,k}}}<+\infty\right\}.

Let
\omega\in\Xi\cap{\mathcal{A}}
. By definition of
\Xi
and
\Xi_{1}
more specifically, there exits
m_{\omega}>0
s.t.

\dfrac{\left(\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{m_{\omega}}}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}(\omega))\|^{2}}<+\infty.

(6.11)

Then, reformulating (
6.3
) we have

{\mathrm{Y}}_{m_{\omega},k}(\omega)=p_{k}^{1/m_{\omega}}\dfrac{(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}(\omega))-F^{*})}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}(\omega))\|}+(C\lambda)^{2}p_{k}^{2}\dfrac{r_{k}^{2-1/m_{\omega}}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}(\omega))\|^{2}}.

On the one hand, since
(p_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is bounded (as it converges to
p_{\infty}
)
On the other hand, since, by definition of
(r_{k})_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
(see (
5.4
)), we have
r_{k}<\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}
then the second term in the above equation is also finite. It follows that
\left({\mathrm{Y}}_{m_{\omega},k}(\omega)\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
is bounded and so

\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+{\mathrm{Y}}_{m_{\omega},k}(\omega)}}\underset{k\to+\infty}{=}{\mathcal{O}}(1).

(6.12)

As
\omega\in{\mathcal{A}}

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\frac{\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}(\omega))\|}{\sqrt{1+{\mathrm{Y}}_{m_{\omega},k}(\omega)}}<+\infty,

(6.13)

and, from positivity
\left({\mathrm{Y}}_{m_{\omega},k}(\omega)\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
and (
6.12
), this leads to

\sum_{k=0}^{+\infty}\alpha_{k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k}(\omega))\|<+\infty.

(6.14)

Since Proposition
6.1
ensures that
{\mathbb{P}}({\mathcal{A}})=1
, (
6.14
) thus occurs for almost every
\omega
of
\Xi
, hence the conclusion.

Cases
\Xi=\Xi_{1}
and
\Xi=\Xi_{2}
can be treated in a similar way (consider

instead of
\left(Y_{m,k}\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
for the case

).

The practical use of Corollary

depends on the probabilities
{\mathbb{P}}(\Xi_{1}),{\mathbb{P}}(\Xi_{2})
, that should be close to

\Xi_{2}
first,
\Xi_{1}
then).

Example 6.5

If
F
is assumed to be
\varrho
-strongly convex (
\varrho>0
), then the following particular version
of Łojasiewicz inequality (see e.g.
[
41
]
) holds

\forall w\in{\mathbb{R}}^{N}~~\|\nabla F(w)\|^{2}\geqslant 2\varrho\left(F(w)-F^{*}\right)

(6.15)

which ensures that

\forall k\in{\mathbb{N}}\quad\frac{F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|}=\frac{\sqrt{F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|}\sqrt{F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varrho}}\sqrt{F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}}.

(6.16)

Since
\left(F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}\right)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
almost-surely converges to the almost-sure finite limit
F_{\infty}-F^{*}\geqslant 0
, (
6.16
) conducts to
{\mathbb{P}}(\Xi_{2})=1
with

\limsup_{k\to+\infty}\frac{F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})-F^{*}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|}\leqslant\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\varrho}}\sqrt{F_{\infty}-F^{*}}~~\mathrm{a.s..}

(6.17)

To investigate more general cases than the convex settings described in Example
6.5
, we need to take a closer look at the convergence rates of the SGD scheme.
Intuitively, as discussed in
[
55
]
, the gradient
(\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|)_{k\in{\mathbb{N}}}
should not converge too fast to
0
to enable the event
\Xi
to not be too small, as shown in the following example.

Example 6.6

Let, for every
k\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
\alpha_{k}=\frac{a}{k^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon}}
with
a>0
small enough and
\epsilon\in(0,\frac{1}{2})
. Then,
according to
[
55
, Corollary 18]
, we have

\min_{1\leqslant i\leqslant k}\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\|^{2}\underset{k\to+\infty}{=}o\left(\frac{1}{k^{\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon}}\right)~~\mathrm{a.s.}.

Hence, intuitively, we can postulate that
\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{i})\|^{2}
does not decrease faster than
\frac{1}{k^{1/2}}
.
As such, assuming that
\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}\underset{k\to+\infty}{\sim}\frac{1}{k^{1/2}}
almost surely, then for all
m\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
, we deduce that

\dfrac{\left(\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{m}}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}}\underset{k\to+\infty}{\sim}\left(\frac{a^{2}}{k^{2\epsilon}}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{m}}k^{\frac{1}{2}}\underset{k\to+\infty}{=}{\mathcal{O}}\left(k^{\left(\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon(2-\frac{1}{m})\right)}\right)~~\mathrm{a.s.}.

Since
\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon(2-\frac{1}{m})\underset{m\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\frac{1}{2}-4\epsilon
, we deduce that, if
\epsilon\in[\frac{1}{8},\frac{1}{2})
, there exists
m_{0}\in{\mathbb{N}}^{*}
for which
\frac{1}{2}-2\epsilon(2-\frac{1}{m_{0}})\leqslant 0
and

\dfrac{\left(\sum_{i=k}^{+\infty}\alpha_{i}^{2}\right)^{2-\frac{1}{m_{0}}}}{\|\nabla F({\mathbf{w}}_{k})\|^{2}}\underset{k\to+\infty}{=}{\mathcal{O}}(1)~~\mathrm{a.s.}

Hence we have
{\mathbb{P}}(\Xi_{1})=1
.

7
Discussion and conclusion

In this work, we presented a new framework to investigate convergence properties of stochastic schemes in a non-convex context.

In Sections
3
-
4
, we introduced a framework to apply the KL property in a stochastic setting.
In this context the notion of “decay”, that is usually key to study convergence on deterministic schemes in a non-convex setting, is associated with that of supermartingale. It then appears to be more difficult to satisfy in the stochastic than in the deterministic case. To circumvent this difficulty, we made the choice to add mild conditions as those described in Theorem
4.3
. This allowed us to derive new asymptotic results leveraging mathematical tools different from the standard ODE method and “trajectory-by-trajectory” strategy used in
[
4
,
31
,
56
]
.
In Sections
5
-
6
, we studied the case of the SGD scheme to show that our assumptions are reasonable in practice. In particular, they do not require specific curvature properties for the functions on interest.

Finally, we focused on the particular case of smooth functions. However, as highlighted in
[
12
,
13
]
, we are naturally led to consider a non-smooth extension of our framework.
Using non-smooth version of the
KŁ
property, Sections
3
-
4
could be straightforwardly extended to non-smooth coercive and continuous Lyapunov functions
V
.
The difference would only be of structural order, by replacing the gradient norm by the distance of the limiting subdifferential
[
50
]
to zero. Note that the stochastic non-smooth case has been investigated for particular schemes (see e.g.,
[
30
]
or
[
39
]
).
