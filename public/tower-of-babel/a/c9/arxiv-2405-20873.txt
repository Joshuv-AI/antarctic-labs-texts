Mutually unbiased bases via complex projective trigonometry
Mikhail G. Katz

Abstract
. We give a synthetic construction of a complete system of mutually unbiased bases in ℂ 3 {\mathbb{C}}^{3} .

1.
Synthetic description

In quantum mechanics, the concept of mutually unbiased bases

[
1
]
is of fundamental importance. Recall that orthonormal
bases
(e_{i})
and
(f_{j})
for
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
are called
mutually
unbiased
if the absolute value of the Hermitian inner product

\langle e_{i},f_{j}\rangle
for all
i,j
is independent of
i
and
j

(and therefore necessarily equals
\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
). No knowledge
of quantum theory is required for reading this article.

A
complete system
of mutually unbiased bases in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is
a system of
n+1
such bases. It is still unknown whether a
complete system of bases exists for
\mathbb{C}^{6}
i.e., in
\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^{5}
. A complete system of mutually unbiased bases is known
to exist when the vector space dimension is a prime power. We propose
a new approach to constructing mutually unbiased bases, and implement
it in the case of the complex projective plane.

Fix a complex projective line

\ell\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}.

(1.1)

Recall that
\ell
is isometric to a 2-sphere of constant
Gaussian curvature. Let

A,B,C,D\in\ell

be the vertices of a regular inscribed tetrahedron, i.e., an
equidistant 4-tuple of points on the 2-sphere. There is a
synthetic construction of a complete system of mutually unbiased bases
(MUBs), where each basis includes one of the 4 points
A,B,C,D
. Let

m\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}

(1.2)

be the complex projective line consisting of points at maximal
distance from
A\in{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. On the projective line
\ell
of
(
1.1
), let

E\in\ell

be the antipodal point of
A
, so that
E=\ell\cap m
, where
m
is
the complex projective line of (
1.2
). Then
\{A,E\}
is a
pair of points at maximal geodesic distance in
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. In
homogeneous coordinates, this corresponds to the fact that their
representing vectors in
{\mathbb{C}}^{3}
are orthonormal with respect to the
standard Hermitian inner product.

Choose a great circle (i.e., closed geodesic)

S^{1}\subseteq m

passing through
E
. We view
S^{1}
as the equator of
m
and denote
by

A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\in m

the corresponding north and south poles. Then
S^{1}\subseteq m

is the set of equidistant points from
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
. Let

\gamma_{ABE}

be the closed geodesic (great circle) passing through the three points

A,B,E\in\ell
. Consider the real projective
plane

{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{B}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}

that includes the great circles
\gamma_{ABE}
and
S^{1}
. Consider
also the real projective plane
{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{C}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
that includes
the great circles
\gamma_{ACE}
and
S^{1}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

A complete system of MUBs is constructed as follows. The first basis
is
\{A,A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. On the complex projective line
passing through
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
, let
S^{1}
be the equidistant circle
from
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
. Choose an equilateral triangle
EE^{\prime}E^{\prime\prime}

in
S^{1}
. Let
\ell
be the projective line through
A
and
E
.
We complete
A
to an equidistant
4
-tuple
A,B,C,D
on
\ell
.
Next, we complete
B
to a basis

\{B,B^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}\}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{B}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
by choosing
B^{\prime}

appropriately on the great circle
\gamma_{AE^{\prime}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}_{B}
, and
similarly for
B^{\prime\prime}\in\gamma_{AE^{\prime\prime}}
. Next, we complete
C
to a
basis
\{C,C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\}
as we did for
B
; the same for
D
.

The remainder of the paper presents a proof of Theorem
1.1
.

2.
Complex projective trigonometry

Consider a pair of unit vectors
v,w
in
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
endowed with its
Hermitian inner product
\langle\;,\;\rangle
. Associated with the
pair
v,w
, there is a pair of angles:

(1)

\alpha=\arccos\text{Re}\langle v,w\rangle
is the usual angle
between the vectors when
{\mathbb{C}}^{n}
is identified with
{\mathbb{R}}^{2n}
, and

(2)

\theta=\arccos|\langle v,w\rangle|
is the least angle between
vectors in the complex lines spanned by
v
and
w
.

Letting
Pw
be the orthogonal projection of
w
to the complex line
spanned by
v
, we denote by

\psi

(2.1)

the angle between
v
and
Pw
, so that by the spherical law of
cosines (see
[
2
, p. 17]
) we have

\cos\alpha=\cos\theta\cos\psi.

(2.2)

Given a pair of geodesics issuing from a point
A\in{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
, the
corresponding angle
\alpha
(resp.
\theta
) is defined similarly
using their unit tangent vectors at
A
. Consider a geodesic triangle
in
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
with sides of length
a,b,c
and angles
\alpha
and

\theta
at the point opposite the side
c
. We normalize the metric
on
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
so that the sectional curvature satisfies
\frac{1}{4}\leq K\leq 1
. Then each complex projective line
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{1}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{n}
is
isometric to a unit sphere. The following formula, generalizing the
law of cosines of spherical trigonometry, goes back to Shirokov

[
3
]
and was exploited in
[
4
]
in 1984 as well as in
1991 in
[
5
, p. 176]
:

\cos c=\cos a\cos b+\sin a\sin b\cos\alpha-2\sin^{2}\frac{a}{2}\sin^{2}\frac{b}{2}\sin^{2}\theta.

(2.3)

Let
A\in{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and let
m\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
be the complex projective
line consisting of points at maximal distance
\pi
from
A
(the
notation is consistent with that introduced in Section
1
).

Let
E,X\in m
and consider a geodesic triangle
AEX
contained in a
copy of a real projective plane (of curvature
\frac{1}{4}
according our
normalisation). As before,
\alpha
and
\theta
are the angles
between the tangent vectors at
A
to the chosen
geodesics
\gamma_{AE}
and
\gamma_{AX}
.

Consider the tangent vector of the geodesic
\gamma_{AX}
at the
point
X
. If we parallel translate this vector along the
geodesic
\gamma_{XE}
to a vector
u
at the point
E
, then

(1)

the angle at
E
between
u
and the tangent vector to the
geodesic
\gamma_{AC}
is the angle
\psi
of (
2.1
) by

[
5
, p. 177]
;

(2)

the distance
d(E,X)
between
E
and
X
is
d(E,X)=2\theta

(the factor of
2
is due to our normalisation of the metric).

We complete
A
to a basis
\{A,A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\}
by choosing a pair of
antipodal points
A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\in m
.

The following identity plays a key role in the construction. Relative
to the above normalisation of curvature, a totally geodesic
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{1}

has Gaussian curvature
1
whereas a totally geodesic
{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
has
Gaussian curvature
\frac{1}{4}
. Let
d
be the spherical sidelength of
a regular inscribed tetrahedron in
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{1}
. Then geodesic arcs of
length
d
,
\pi-d
, and
\frac{\pi}{2}
form a right-angle triangle in

{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
with hypotenuse
d
, so that

\cos\tfrac{d}{2}=\cos\big(\tfrac{\pi-d}{2}\big)\cos\tfrac{\pi}{4}.

3.
Mutually unbiased bases

By definition, another basis
\{B,B^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}\}
is unbiased with respect
to
\{A,A^{\prime},A^{\prime\prime}\}
if and only if the distance
d
between
A
and
B

is
d=2\arccos\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
(again the factor of
2
is due to our
normalisation), and similarly for all the other distances between a
member of the first basis and a member of the second basis. Note
that
\cos d=2\cos^{2}\frac{d}{2}-1=\frac{2}{3}-1=-\frac{1}{3}
, i.e.,

d=\arccos\big(\hskip-2.0pt-\frac{1}{3}\big)
is the spherical sidelength of a
regular inscribed tetrahedron.

Consider the equator
S^{1}\subseteq m
equidistant from
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
,
and choose an equilateral triangle
EE^{\prime}E^{\prime\prime}
on
S^{1}
, so that

d(E,E^{\prime})=\frac{2\pi}{3}
and similarly for the other two pairs. The
complex projective line through
A
and
E
is denoted
\ell
as in
Section
1
.

We choose a geodesic arc
\gamma_{AE}\subseteq\ell\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
and
a point
B\in\gamma_{AE}
so that
d(A,B)=d
. Next, we consider the
complex projective line through
A
and
E^{\prime}
. On this line, we choose
a geodesic
\gamma_{AE^{\prime}}\subseteq{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
so that the angle
\psi

between
\gamma_{AE}
and
\gamma_{AE^{\prime}}
is
\psi=\pi

(here
\alpha=\frac{2\pi}{3}
and
\theta=\frac{\pi}{3}
). Then the
geodesics
\gamma_{AE}
and
\gamma_{AE^{\prime}}
lie in a common real
projective plane
{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
which also includes the equator
S^{1}\subseteq m
. We choose the point
B^{\prime}\in\gamma_{AE^{\prime}}
so that
d(A,B^{\prime})=d
. We
choose the point
B^{\prime\prime}
above
E^{\prime\prime}
similarly. Note that the
angle
\psi
between
\gamma_{AE^{\prime}}
and
\gamma_{AE^{\prime\prime}}
is also
\pi
,
because parallel transport around
S^{1}
in
{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
of a vector
u

orthogonal to
S^{1}
gives
-u
.

By construction,
d(A,B)=d
. Let us calculate
d(A^{\prime},B)
. Consider
the right-angle triangle
A^{\prime}EB
in
{\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}}^{2}
. As

\cos\frac{d}{2}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}
by the unbiased condition, we have

\sin\frac{d}{2}=\sqrt{\frac{2}{3}}
. Hence by the spherical theorem of
cosines,

\cos(\tfrac{1}{2}d(A^{\prime},B))=\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{4})\cos(\tfrac{\pi}{2}-\tfrac{d}{2})=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sin\tfrac{d}{2}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt{3}},

as required. The equality
AB=A^{\prime}B
is equivalent to (
2.4
). A
similar calculation shows that
\cos d(A^{\prime\prime},B)=-\frac{1}{3}
, as well. By
symmetry, each of the points
B^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime\prime}
is also at geodesic
distance
d
from
A
,
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
. It remains to check that the
triple
\{B,B^{\prime},B^{\prime\prime}\}
corresponds to an orthonormal basis.
Indeed,
\psi=\pi
implies
\cos\alpha=\cos\psi\cos\theta=-\cos\theta

by (
2.2
). We apply (
2.3
) at the point
A
we obtain

\displaystyle\cos d(B,B^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\cos^{2}d+\sin^{2}d\cos\alpha-2\sin^{4}\tfrac{d}{2}\sin^{2}\theta

\displaystyle=\tfrac{1}{9}+\tfrac{8}{9}(-\tfrac{1}{2})-2\cdot\tfrac{4}{9}\cdot\tfrac{3}{4}=-1

and therefore
d(B,B^{\prime})=\pi
as required.

4.
Completing the MUBs

To specify a third basis, we choose
C\in\ell
so as to complete
A

and
B
to an equilateral triangle
ABC
with side
d
(and angle

\frac{2\pi}{3}
). Then the corresponding angle
\theta
vanishes, and
hence
\alpha=\psi=\frac{2\pi}{3}
. We complete
C
to a basis

\{C,C^{\prime},C^{\prime\prime}\}
as in Section
3
, using a real projective plane
which includes the geodesic
\gamma_{ACE}
and the
equator
S^{1}\subseteq m
(the same equator as before, equidistant
between
A^{\prime}
and
A^{\prime\prime}
). To check that the
C
-basis is unbiased
relative to the
B
-basis, note that
\psi(B,B^{\prime})=\pi
and

\psi(B,C)=\frac{2\pi}{3}
hence

\psi(B^{\prime},C)=\pi-\frac{2\pi}{3}=\frac{\pi}{3}
, while
\theta=\frac{\pi}{3}
as
before. Hence
\cos d(B,C^{\prime})=\frac{1}{9}+\frac{8}{9}\cos\psi\cos\theta-2\cdot\frac{4}{9}\sin^{2}\theta=-\frac{1}{3}
, as required. The other pairs
are checked similarly.

To specify a fourth basis, we choose
D\in\ell
to complete the
equilateral triangle
ABC
to a regular inscribed tetrahedron, and
proceed as before. This completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

Such a synthetic construction of MUBs involves less redundancy than
complex Hadamard matrix presentations, and may therefore be useful in
shedding light on MUBs in higher-dimensional complex projective
spaces, such as
{\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}}^{5}
where the maximal number of MUBs is unknown.
