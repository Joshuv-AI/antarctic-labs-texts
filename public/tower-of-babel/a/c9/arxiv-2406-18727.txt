Demonic variance and a non-determinism score for Markov decision processes
Jakob Piribauer

Abstract
This paper studies the influence of probabilism and non-determinism on some quantitative aspect X X of the execution of a system modeled as a Markov decision process (MDP). To this end, the novel notion of demonic variance is introduced: For a random variable X X in an MDP ℳ \mathcal{M} , it is defined as 1 / 2 1/2 times the maximal expected squared distance of the values of X X in two independent execution of ℳ \mathcal{M} in which also the non-deterministic choices are resolved independently by two distinct schedulers. It is shown that the demonic variance is between 1 1 and 2 2 times as large as the maximal variance of X X in ℳ \mathcal{M} that can be achieved by a single scheduler. This allows defining a non-determinism score for ℳ \mathcal{M} and X X measuring how strongly the difference of X X in two executions of ℳ \mathcal{M} can be influenced by the non-deterministic choices. Properties of MDPs ℳ \mathcal{M} with extremal values of the non-determinism score are established. Further, the algorithmic problems of computing the maximal variance and the demonic variance are investigated for two random variables, namely weighted reachability and accumulated rewards. In the process, also the structure of schedulers maximizing the variance and of scheduler pairs realizing the demonic variance is analyzed.

In software and hardware systems, uncertainty manifests in two distinct forms:
non-determinism
and
probabilism
. Non-determinism emerges from, e.g., unknown operating environments, user interactions, or concurrent processes. Probabilistic behavior arises through deliberate randomization in algorithms or can be inferred, e.g., from probabilities of component failures.
In this paper, we investigate the uncertainty in the value
X
of some quantitative aspect of a system whose behavior is subject to non-determinism
and
probabilism.
On the one hand, we aim to quantify this uncertainty. In the spirit of the variance that quantifies uncertainty in purely probabilistic settings, we introduce the notion of
demonic variance
that generalizes the variance in the presence of non-determinism.
On the other hand, we provide a
non-determinism score
(NDS) based on this demonic variance that measures the extent to which the uncertainty of
X
can be ascribed to the non-determinism.

As formal models, we use Markov decision processes (MDPs, see, e.g.,
[
30
]
), one of the most prominent models combining non-determinism and probabilism, heavily used in verification, operations research, and artificial intelligence.
The non-deterministic choices in
an MDP are resolved by a
scheduler
.
Once a scheduler is fixed, the system behaves purely probabilistically.

Demonic variance.

For a random variable
Y
, the variance is equal to half the expected squared deviation of two independent copies
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
of
Y
:

\mathbb{V}(Y)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}((Y-\mathbb{E}(Y))^{2})=\mathbb{E}(Y^{2})-\mathbb{E}(Y)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}(Y_{1}^{2}-2Y_{1}Y_{2}+Y_{2}^{2})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}((Y_{1}-Y_{2})^{2}).

For a quantity
X
in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
, we obtain a random variable
X_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}}
for each scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
.

The

\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{V}(X^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}})

can serve as a measure for the “amount of probabilistic uncertainty” regarding

present in the MDP.
However, in the presence of non-determinism, quantifying the spread of outcomes in terms of the squared deviation of two independent executions of a system
gives rise to a whole new meaning:
We can allow the non-determinism to be resolved independently as well.
To this end, we consider two different scheduler
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
in two independent copies

\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}((X^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}}_{\mathcal{M}_{1}}-X^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}_{2}})^{2}).

If we now allow for a

choice of the two schedulers making this uncertainty as large as possible, we arrive at the
demonic variance

\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)

of

in
\mathcal{M}
.

Non-determinism score (NDS).

By the definition of the demonic variance, it is clear that

Potential applications.

First of all, the demonic variance might serve as the basis for refined guarantees on the behavior of systems, in particular, when employed in different environments.
As a first result in this direction, we will prove an analogue to Chebyshev’s Inequality using the demonic variance.
Further, as illustrated in Example
1
,
achieving a low demonic variance or NDS can be desirable when designing systems.
Hence, a reasonable synthesis task could be to design a system ensuring a high expected value of a quantity
X
while keeping the demonic variance of
X
below a threshold.

Secondly, the demonic variance and the NDS can serve to enhance the
explainability of a system’s behavior, a topic of growing importance in the area of formal verification (see, e.g.,
[
5
]
for an overview).
More concretely, the NDS can be understood as a measure assigning
responsibility
for the scattering of a quantity
X
in different executions to the non-determinism and the probabilism present in the system, respectively.
Further, considering the NDS for different starting states makes it possible to pinpoint regions of the state space in which the non-determinism has a particularly high influence.
Notions of responsibility that quantify to which extent certain facets of the behavior of a system can be ascribed to certain components, states, or events
have been studied in various settings

[
11
,
34
,
7
,
27
,
6
]
.

Finally, the NDS can also be understood as a measure for the power of control when non-determinism models controllable aspects of a system. This interpretation could be useful, e.g., when designing exploration strategies in reinforcement learning.
Here, the task is to learn good strategies as fast as possible by interacting with a system. One of the main challenges is to decide which regions of the state space to explore (see
[
23
]
for a recent survey).
Estimations for the NDS starting from different states could be useful here:
States from which the NDS is high might be more promising to explore than
states from which the NDS is low as the difference in received rewards from such a state is largely subject to randomness.

Contributions.

Besides establishing general results for the demonic variance and the NDS, we investigate the two notions for weighted reachability and accumulated rewards.
For weighted reachability, terminal states of an MDP are equipped with a weight that is received if an execution ends in this state. For accumulated rewards, all states are assigned rewards that are summed up along an execution.
The main contributions of this paper are as follows.

•

We introduce the novel notions of demonic variance and non-determinism score.
For general random variables
X
, we prove that the demonic variance is at most twice as large as the maximal variance.
Furthermore, we prove an analogue of
Chebyshev’s inequality.
For the non-determinism score, we establish consequences of a score of
0
or
1
.

•

In the process, we prove a result of independent interest using a topology on the space of schedulers that states that convergence with respect to this topology implies convergence of the corresponding probability measures.

•

For weighted reachability, we show that the
maximal and the demonic variance can be computed via quadratic programs.
For the maximal variance, this results in a
polynomial-time algorithm; for the demonic variance, in a separable bilinear program of polynomial size yielding an exponential time upper bound.
Further, we establish that there is a memoryless scheduler maximizing the variance and
a pair of memoryless deterministic schedulers realizing the demonic variance.

•

For accumulated rewards, we prove that
the maximal variance and an optimal finite-memory scheduler can be computed in exponential time.
Further, we prove that the demonic variance is realized by a pair of deterministic finite-memory schedulers which can be computed via a bilinear program of exponential size.

Related work.

We are not aware of investigations of notions similar to the demonic variance for MDPs.
Previous work on the variance in MDPs usually focused on the minimization of the variance.
In
[
26
]
, the problem to find schedulers that ensure a certain expected value while keeping the variance below a threshold is investigated
for accumulated rewards in the finite horizon setting. It is shown that deciding whether there is a scheduler ensuring variance
0
is NP-hard.
In
[
24
]
, the minimization of the variance of accumulated rewards and of the mean payoff is addressed with a focus on optimality equations and no algorithmic results.
The variance of accumulated weights in Markov chains is shown to be computable in polynomial time in
[
33
]
.
For the mean payoff, algorithms were given to compute schedulers that achieve given bounds on the expectation and notions of variance and variability in
[
10
]
.

One objective incorporating the variance that has been studied on MDPs is the variance-penalized expectation (VPE)
[
17
,
14
,
29
]
. Here, the goal is to find a scheduler that maximizes the expected reward minus a penalty factor times the variance.
In
[
29
]
, the objective is studied for accumulated rewards.
Methodically, our results for the maximal and demonic variance of accumulated rewards share similarities with the techniques of
[
29
]
and we
make use of some results proved there, such as the result that among expectation-optimal schedulers a variance-optimal memoryless deterministic scheduler can be computed in polynomial time.
Nevertheless, the optimization of the VPE inherently requires the minimization of the variance. In particular, it is shown in
[
29
]
that deterministic schedulers are optimal for the VPE, while randomization is necessary for the maximization of the variance.

Besides the variance, several other notions that aim to bound the uncertainty of the outcome of some quantitative aspect in MDPs have been studied – in particular, in the context of risk-averse optimization:
Given a probability
p
, quantiles for a quantity
X
are the best bound
B
such that
X
exceeds
B
with probability at most
p
in the worst or best case. For accumulated rewards in MDPs, quantiles have been studied
in
[
32
,
4
,
18
,
31
]
.
The
conditional value-at-risk
is a more involved measures that quantifies how far the probability mass of the tail of the probability distribution lies above a quantile.
In
[
22
]
, this notion has been investigated for weighted reachability and mean payoff; in
[
28
]
for accumulated rewards.
A further measure incentivizing a high expected value while keeping the probability of low outcomes small is the entropic risk measure. For accumulated rewards, this measure has been studied in
[
3
]
in stochastic games that extend MDPs with an adversarial player.

Finally, as the demonic variance is a measure that looks at a system across different executions, there is a conceptual similarity to hyperproperties

[
13
,
12
]
. For probabilistic systems, logics expressing hyperproperties that allow to quantify over different executions or schedulers have been introduced in
[
1
,
15
]
.

Preliminaries

Notations for Markov decision processes.

A
Markov decision process
(MDP) is a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}})

where
S
is a finite set of states,

\mathit{Act}
a finite set of actions,

P\colon S\times\mathit{Act}\times S\to[0,1]\cap\mathbb{Q}
the
transition probability function, and

s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}\in S
the initial state.
We require that

\sum_{t\in S}P(s,\alpha,t)\in\{0,1\}

for all
(s,\alpha)\in S\times\mathit{Act}
.
We say that action
\alpha
is
enabled
in state
s
iff
\sum_{t\in S}P(s,\alpha,t)=1
and denote the set of all actions that are enabled in state
s
by
\mathit{Act}(s)
. We further require that
\mathit{Act}(s)\not=\emptyset
for all
s\in S
.
If for a state
s
and all actions
\alpha\in Act(s)
, we have
P(s,\alpha,s)=1
, we say that
s
is
absorbing
.
The paths of
\mathcal{M}
are finite or
infinite sequences
s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\ldots

where states and actions alternate such that

P(s_{i},\alpha_{i},s_{i+1})>0
for all
i\geq 0
.
For
\pi=s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\,\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\,s_{k}
,

P(\pi)=P(s_{0},\alpha_{0},s_{1})\cdot\ldots\cdot P(s_{k-1},\alpha_{k-1},s_{k})

denotes the probability of
\pi
and
\mathit{last}(\pi)=s_{k}
its last state.
Often, we equip MDPs with a reward function
\mathit{rew}\colon S\times\mathit{Act}\to\mathbb{N}
.
The
size
of
\mathcal{M}

is the sum of the number of states
plus the total sum of the encoding lengths in binary of the non-zero
probability values

P(s,\alpha,s^{\prime})
as fractions of co-prime integers as well as the encoding length in binary of the rewards if a reward function is used.
A
Markov chain
is an MDP in which the set of actions is a singleton. In this case, we can drop the set of actions and consider a Markov chain as a tuple
\mathcal{M}=(S,P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}},\mathit{rew})
where

P
now is a function from
S\times S
to
[0,1]
and
\mathit{rew}
a function from
S
to
\mathbb{N}
.

An
end component
of
\mathcal{M}
is a strongly connected sub-MDP formalized by a subset
S^{\prime}\subseteq S
of states and a non-empty subset
\mathfrak{A}(s)\subseteq\mathit{Act}(s)
for each state
s\in S^{\prime}
such that for each
s\in S^{\prime}
,
t\in S
and
\alpha\in\mathfrak{A}(s)
with
P(s,\alpha,t)>0
, we have
t\in S^{\prime}
and such that in the resulting sub-MDP all states are reachable from each other.
An end-component is a
0
-end-component if
it only contains state-action-pairs with reward
0
.
Given two MDPs
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}})
and
\mathcal{N}=(S^{\prime},\mathit{Act}^{\prime},P^{\prime},s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}^{\prime})
, we define the (synchronous) product
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
as the tuple

(S\times S^{\prime},\mathit{Act}\times\mathit{Act}^{\prime},P^{\otimes},(s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}},s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}^{\prime}))
where we define

P^{\otimes}((s,s^{\prime}),(\alpha,\beta),(t,t^{\prime}))=P(s,\alpha,t)\cdot P(s^{\prime},\beta,t^{\prime})

for all
(s,s^{\prime}),(t,t^{\prime})\in S\times S^{\prime}
and
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathit{Act}\times\mathit{Act}^{\prime}
.

Schedulers.

A
scheduler
(also called
policy
) for
\mathcal{M}

is a function
\mathfrak{S}
that assigns to each finite path
\pi

a probability distribution over
\mathit{Act}(\mathit{last}(\pi))
.
If
\mathfrak{S}(\pi)=\mathfrak{S}(\pi^{\prime})
for all finite paths
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
with
\mathit{last}(\pi)=\mathit{last}(\pi^{\prime})
,
we say that
\mathfrak{S}
is
memoryless
. In this case, we also view schedulers as functions mapping states
s\in S
to probability distributions over
\mathit{Act}(s)
.
A scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
is called deterministic if
\mathfrak{S}(\pi)
is a Dirac distribution
for each finite path
\pi
, in which case we also view the scheduler as a mapping to actions in
\mathit{Act}(\mathit{last}(\pi))
.
Given two MDPs
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}})
and
\mathcal{N}=(S^{\prime},\mathit{Act}^{\prime},P^{\prime},s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}^{\prime})
and two schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
for
\mathcal{M}
and
\mathcal{N}
, respectively,
we define the product scheduler
\mathfrak{S}\otimes\mathfrak{T}
for
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{N}
by defining for a finite path
\pi=(s_{0},t_{0})\,(\alpha_{0},\beta_{0})\,(s_{1},t_{1})\,\dots\,(s_{k},t_{k})
:

\mathfrak{S}\otimes\mathfrak{T}(\pi)(\alpha,\beta)=\mathfrak{S}(s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,\dots\,s_{k})(\alpha)\cdot\mathfrak{T}(t_{0}\,\beta_{0}\,\dots\,t_{k})(\beta)

for all
(\alpha,\beta)\in\mathit{Act}\times\mathit{Act}^{\prime}
.

Probability measure.

We write
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}

to denote the probability measure induced by a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
and a state
s
of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
It is defined on the
\sigma
-algebra generated by the cylinder sets
\mathit{Cyl}(\pi)
of all infinite extensions of a finite path
\pi=s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\,\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\,s_{k}
starting in state
s
, i.e.,
s_{0}=s
, by assigning to
\mathit{Cyl}(\pi)
the probability that
\pi
is realized under
\mathfrak{S}
, which is

P^{\mathfrak{S}}(\pi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\prod_{i=0}^{k-1}\mathfrak{S}(s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\dots\,s_{i})(\alpha_{i})\cdot P(s_{i},\alpha_{0},s_{i+1})
.
This can be extended to a unique probability measure on the mentioned
\sigma
-algebra. For details, see
[
30
]
.
For a random variable
X
, i.e., a measurable function defined on infinite paths in
\mathcal{M}
, we denote the expected value of
X
under a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
and state
s
by
\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)
.
We define

\mathbb{E}^{\min}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\inf_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)

and

\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)
.
The variance of
X
under the probability measure determined by
\mathfrak{S}
and
s
in
\mathcal{M}
is denoted by
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)
and defined by

\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}((X-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X))^{2})=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X^{2})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)^{2}
.
We define
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)
.
If
s=s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}
, we sometimes drop the subscript
s
in
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}
,
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}
and
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(X)
.

Mixing schedulers.

Intuitively, we often want to use a scheduler that initially decides to behave like a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
and then to stick to this scheduler with probability
p
and to behave like a scheduler
\mathfrak{T}
with probability
1-p
. As this intuitive description does not match the definition of schedulers as functions from finite paths
2
2

2

This description would be admissible if we allowed stochastic memory updates (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
).
, we provide a formal definition:
For two schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
and
p\in[0,1]
, we use
p\mathfrak{S}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{T}
to denote the following scheduler.
For a path
\pi=s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\,\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\,s_{k}
, we define for an action
\alpha
enabled in
s_{k}

\displaystyle(p\mathfrak{S}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{T})(\pi)(\alpha)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\,

\displaystyle\frac{p\cdot P^{\mathfrak{S}}(\pi)\cdot\mathfrak{S}(\pi)(\alpha)}{p\cdot P^{\mathfrak{S}}(\pi)+(1-p)\cdot P^{\mathfrak{T}}(\pi)}+\frac{(1-p)\cdot P^{\mathfrak{T}}(\pi)\cdot\mathfrak{T}(\pi)(\alpha)}{p\cdot P^{\mathfrak{S}}(\pi)+(1-p)\cdot P^{\mathfrak{T}}(\pi)}.

This is well-defined for any path that has positive probability under
\mathfrak{S}
or
\mathfrak{T}
.
The following result is folklore; a proof is included in Appendix

Proposition 3
.

Let the schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
and the value
p
be as above. Then, for any path
\pi=s_{0}\,\alpha_{0}\,s_{1}\,\alpha_{1}\,\ldots\alpha_{k-1}\,s_{k}

P^{p\mathfrak{S}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{T}(\pi)}(\pi)=pP^{\mathfrak{S}}(\pi)+(1-p)P^{\mathfrak{T}}(\pi)
.

We conclude

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{p\mathfrak{S}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{T}}(A)=p\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}(A)+(1-p)\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{T}}(A)

for any measurable set of paths
A
.
Hence, we can think of the scheduler
p\mathfrak{S}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{T}
as behaving like
\mathfrak{S}
with probability
p
and like
\mathfrak{T}
with probability
(1-p)
.
In particular, we can also conclude that for a random variable
X
, we have

\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{p\mathfrak{S}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{T}}(X)=p\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}(X)+(1-p)\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{T}}(X)
.
For the variance, we obtain the following as shown in Appendix

.

Lemma 4
.

Given
\mathcal{M}
,
X
, and two schedulers
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
, as well as

, let
\mathfrak{T}=p\mathfrak{S}_{1}\oplus(1-p)\mathfrak{S}_{2}
. Then,

\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=p\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}}(X)+(1-p)\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}(X)+p(1-p)(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}(X))^{2}
.

Topology and convergence of measures.

Given a family of topological spaces
((S_{i},\tau_{i}))_{i\in I}
, the product topology
\tau
on
\prod_{i\in I}S_{i}
is the coarsest topology such that the projections

p_{i}\colon\prod_{i\in I}S_{i}\to S_{i},\quad(s_{i})_{i\in I}\mapsto s_{i}

are continuous for all
i\in I
.
For measures
(\mu_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
and
\mu
on a measure space
(\Omega,\Sigma)
where
\Omega
is a metrizable topological space and
\Sigma
the Borel
\sigma
-algebra on
\Omega
, we say
that the sequence
(\mu_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}

weakly converges
to
\mu
if for all bounded continuous functions

, we have

\lim_{j\to\infty}\int f\mathrm{d}\mu_{j}=\int f\mathrm{d}\mu
.
The set of infinite paths
\Pi_{\mathcal{M}}
of an MDP
\mathcal{M}
with the topology generated by the cylinder sets is metrizable as we can define the metric
d(\pi,\pi^{\prime})=2^{-\ell}
where
\ell
is the length of the longest common prefix of
\pi
and
\pi^{\prime}
.

In this section, we formally define the demonic variance.
After proving first auxiliary results, we prove an analogue of Chebyshev’s Inequality using the demonic variance.
Then, we introduce the non-determinism score and investigate necessary and sufficient conditions for this score to be

.
Proofs omitted here can be found in Appendix

.

Throughout this section, let
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}})
be an MDP and let
X
be a random variable, i.e., a Borel measurable function on the infinite paths of
\mathcal{M}
.
We will work under two assumptions that ensure that all notions are well-defined:
First, note that
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=0
implies that there is a constant
c
such that under all schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
, we have
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}}(X=c)=1
– an uninteresting case.
Furthermore, for meaningful definitions of demonic variance and non-determinism score, we need that the expected value and the variance of
X
in
\mathcal{M}
are finite. Hence, we work under the following assumption:

Assumption 3.1
.

We assume that

0<\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)<\infty
and that

\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\left|\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\right|<\infty
.

3.1
Demonic variance

As described in the introduction, the idea behind the demonic variance is to quantify the expected squared deviation of
X
in two independent executions of
\mathcal{M}
, in which the non-determinism is resolved independently as well.
We use the following notation: Given a path in
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
consisting of a sequence of pairs of states and pairs of actions, we denote by
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
the function
X
applied to the projection of the path on the first component and on the second component, respectively.
Given two schedulers
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
for
\mathcal{M}
, we define

\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}((X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}).

Intuitively, in this definition two independent executions of
\mathcal{M}
are run in parallel while the non-determinism is resolved by
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
in the first execution and by
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
in the second component.
As the two components in the products
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{S}_{2}
are independent, the resulting distributions of
X
in the two components, i.e.,
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are independent as well.
The factor
\frac{1}{2}
is included as for a random variable
Y
, this factor also appears in the representation
\mathbb{V}(Y)=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}((Y_{1}-Y_{2})^{2})
for two independent copies
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
of
Y
.

The
demonic variance
is now the worst-case value when ranging over all pairs of schedulers:

\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sup_{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}\otimes\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}((X_{1}-X_{2})^{2}).

A first simple, but useful, result allows us to express
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
in terms of the expected values and variances of
X
under
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
.

Lemma 5
.

Given two schedulers
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
for
\mathcal{M}
, we have

\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1},\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}}(X)+\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}(X)+(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1}}(X)-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}(X))^{2}\right).

This lemma allows us to provide an insightful graphical interpretation of the demonic variance using
the standard deviation
\mathbb{SD}(X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{\mathbb{V}(X)}
of a random variable
X
:

Example 6
.

Suppose in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
, there are four deterministic scheduler
\mathfrak{S}_{1},\dots,\mathfrak{S}_{4}
with expected values
1
,
2
,
3
, and
4
and variances
1
,
8
,
8
, and
5
for a random variable
X
.
Lemma
4
allows us to compute the variances of schedulers obtained by randomization leading to parabolic line segments in the expectation-variance-plane as depicted in
Figure
3(a)
(see also
[
29
]
). Further randomizations also make it possible to realize any combination of expectation and variance in the interior of the resulting shape.
When looking for the maximal variance and the demonic variance, only the upper bound of this shape is relevant.

In Figure
3(b)
,
we now depict the standard deviations of schedulers on this upper bound over the expectation twice on two orthogonal planes.
Clearly, the highest standard deviation (and consequently variance) is obtained for the expected value
2.5
in this example.
The red dotted line of length
\sqrt{2\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}
connects the two points corresponding to this maximum on the two planes.
Considering
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
, we can also find the value

\sqrt{2\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{4}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}

:
The blue dashed line connects the point corresponding to
\mathfrak{S}_{2}
on one of the planes to the point corresponding to
\mathfrak{S}_{4}
on the other plane.
By the Pythagorean theorem, its length is

\displaystyle\sqrt{\sqrt{\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}^{2}+(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)-(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{4}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X))^{2}+\sqrt{\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{4}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}^{2}}=\sqrt{2\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{2},\mathfrak{S}_{4}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}.

So, finding
\sqrt{2}
times the “demonic standard deviation” and hence the demonic variance corresponds to finding two points on the two orthogonal graphs with maximal distance.

The relation between maximal and demonic variance is shown in the following proposition.

Proposition 7
.

We have

\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\leq\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\leq 2\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
.

By means of Chebyshev’s Inequality, the variance can be used to bound the probability that a random variable
Y
lies far from its expected value.
Using the demonic variance, we can prove an analogous result providing bounds on the probability that the outcomes of
X
in two independent executions of the MDP
\mathcal{M}
lie far apart.
This can be seen as a first step in the direction of using the demonic variance to provide guarantees on the behavior of a system.

Theorem 8
.

We have

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}\otimes\mathfrak{T}}\left(|X_{1}-X_{2}|\geq k\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}\right)\leq\frac{2}{k^{2}}

for any
k\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
for
\mathcal{M}
.

Using the result that
\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\leq 2\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
, we obtain the following variant of the inequality providing a weaker bound in terms of the maximal variance.

Corollary 9
.

We have
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}\otimes\mathfrak{T}}\left(|X_{1}-X_{2}|\geq k\cdot\sqrt{\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}\right)\leq\frac{4}{k^{2}}

for any
k\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}
and schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
for
\mathcal{M}
.

3.2
Non-determinism score

We have seen that the demonic variance is larger than the maximal variance by a factor between
1
and
2
. As described in the introduction, we use this insight as the basis for a score quantifying how much worse the “uncertainty” of
X
is when non-determinism can be resolved differently in two executions of an MDP compared to how bad it can be in a single execution. We define the non-determinism score (NDS)

\mathrm{NDS}(\mathcal{M},X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\frac{\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)-\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}{\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)}.

By Assumption
3.1
, the NDS is well-defined.
By Proposition
7
, the NDS always returns a value in
[0,1]
.
Clearly, in Markov chains, the NDS is
0
. A bit more general, we can show:

Proposition 10
.

If
\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
for all schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
, then
\mathrm{NDS}(\mathcal{M},X)=0
.

In transition systems viewed as MDPs in which all transition probabilities are
0
or
1
, the NDS is
1
: Under Assumption
3.1
in a transition system the value of
X
must be bounded, i.e.,
X\in[a,b]
for some
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
such that
\sup_{\pi}X(\pi)=b
and
\inf_{\pi}X(\pi)=a
where
\pi
ranges over all paths. Any path can be realized by a scheduler with probability
1
.
So, for any
\varepsilon>0
, there are schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
with
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X<a+\varepsilon)=1
and
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X>b-\varepsilon)=1
.
Then,
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\geq\frac{1}{2}(b-a-2\varepsilon)^{2}
. For
\varepsilon\to 0
, this converges to
\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{2}
.
It is well-known that the variance of random variables taking values in
[a,b]
is maximal for the random variable taking values
a
and
b
with probability
\frac{1}{2}
each.
The variance in this case is
\frac{(a-b)^{2}}{4}
. So, the maximal variance is (at most) half the demonic variance in this case. Consequently, the NDS is
1
.

Of course, a NDS of
1
does not imply that there are no probabilistic transitions in
\mathcal{M}
.
Nevertheless, a NDS of
1
has severe implications showing that the outcome of
X
can be heavily influenced by the non-determinism in this case as the following theorem shows:

Theorem 11
.

If
\mathrm{NDS}(\mathcal{M},X)=1
, the following statements hold:

1.

For every
\varepsilon>0
,
there are schedulers
\mathfrak{Min}_{\varepsilon}
and
\mathfrak{Max}_{\varepsilon}
with

\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{Min}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\leq\mathbb{E}^{\min}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)+\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{Min}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\leq\varepsilon
, and

\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{Max}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\geq\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)-\varepsilon
and
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{Max}_{\varepsilon}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)\leq\varepsilon
.

2.

If there are schedulers
\mathfrak{S}_{0}
and
\mathfrak{S}_{1}
, with
\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)=\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}_{0},\mathfrak{S}_{1}}_{\mathcal{M}}(X)
, then, for
i=0
or
i=1
,

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{i}}(X=\mathbb{E}^{\min}_{\mathcal{M}}(X))=1
and
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}_{1-i}}(X=\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X))=1
.

3.

If
X
is bounded and continuous wrt the topology generated by the cylinder sets, there are schedulers
\mathfrak{Min}
and
\mathfrak{Max}
with

\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{Min}}(X=\mathbb{E}^{\min}_{\mathcal{M}}(X))=1
and
\mathrm{Pr}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{Max}}(X=\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(X))=1
.

The first two statements can be shown by elementary calculations. For the third statement, we use topological arguments.
We view schedulers as elements of
\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Distr}(\mathit{Act})^{\mathrm{Paths}_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}}
where
\mathrm{Paths}_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}
is the set of paths of length
k
in
\mathcal{M}

and prove the following result:

Proposition 12
.

The space of schedulers

\mathrm{Sched}(\mathcal{M})=\prod_{k=0}^{\infty}\mathrm{Distr}(\mathit{Act})^{\mathrm{Paths}_{\mathcal{M}}^{k}}

with the product topology is compact. So, every sequence of schedulers has a converging subsequence in this space.
Further,
for a sequence
(\mathfrak{S}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
converging to a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
in this space, the sequence of probability measures

(\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}_{j}}_{\mathcal{M}})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
weakly converges to the probability measure
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}
.

An example for a random variable that is bounded and continuous wrt the topology generated by the cylinder sets is the discounted reward:
Given a reward function
\mathit{rew}\colon S\to\mathbb{R}
, the discounted reward of a path
\pi=s_{0}\alpha_{0}s_{1}\dots
is defined as
\mathit{DR}_{\lambda}(\pi)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\lambda^{j}\mathit{rew}(s_{j})

for some discount factor
\lambda\in(0,1)
. First,
|\mathit{DR}_{\lambda}|
is bounded by
\max_{s\in S}|\mathit{rew}(s)|\cdot\frac{1}{1-\lambda}
.
Further, for any
\varepsilon>0
, let
N
be a natural number such that
\max_{s\in S}|\mathit{rew}(s)|\cdot\frac{\lambda^{N}}{1-\lambda}<\varepsilon
. Then,
|\mathit{DR}_{\lambda}(\pi)-\mathit{DR}_{\lambda}(\rho)|<\varepsilon

for all paths
\pi
and
\rho
that share a prefix of length more than
N
.

4
Weighted reachability

We now address the problems to compute the demonic and the maximal variance for weighted reachability where a weight is collected on a run depending on which absorbing state is reached.
As the NDS is defined via these two quantities, we do not address it separately here.
Throughout this section, let
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}})
be an MDP with set of absorbing states
T\subseteq S
and let
\mathit{wgt}\colon T\to\mathbb{Q}
be a weight function.
We define the random variable
\mathrm{WR}
on infinite paths
\pi
by

\mathrm{WR}(\pi)=\mathit{wgt}(t)
if
\pi
reaches the absorbing state
t\in T
, and

\mathrm{WR}(\pi)=0
if
\pi
does not reach
T
.
The main result we are going to establish is the following:

Main result.

The maximal variance
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})
and an optimal memoryless randomized scheduler can be computed in polynomial time.

The demonic variance
\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})
can be computed as the solution to a bilinear program that can be constructed in polynomial time. Furthermore, there is a pair of memoryless deterministic schedulers realizing the demonic variance.

The following standard model transformation collapsing end components (see
[
2
]
) allows us to assume that
T
is reached almost surely under any scheduler:
We add a new absorbing state
t^{\ast}
and set
\mathit{wgt}(t^{\ast})=0
and collapse all maximal end components
\mathcal{E}
in
S\setminus T
to single states
s_{\mathcal{E}}
.
In
s_{\mathcal{E}}
, all actions that were enabled in some state in
\mathcal{E}
and that did not belong to
\mathcal{E}
as well as a new action
\tau
leading to
t^{\ast}
with probability
1
are enabled.
In the resulting MDP
\mathcal{N}
, the set of absorbing states
T\cup\{t^{\ast}\}
is reached almost surely under any scheduler. Further, for any scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
for
\mathcal{M}
, there is a scheduler
\mathfrak{T}
for
\mathcal{N}
such that the distribution of
\mathrm{WR}
is the same under
\mathfrak{S}
in
\mathcal{M}
and under
\mathfrak{T}
in
\mathcal{N}
, and vice versa. So, w.l.o.g., assume the following:

Assumption 4.1
.

The set
T
is reached almost surely under any scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
for
\mathcal{M}
.

In the sequel, we first address the computation of the maximal variance and afterwards of the demonic variance of
\mathrm{WR}
in
\mathcal{M}
.
Omitted proofs can be found in Appendix
C
.

Computation of the maximal variance.

It is well-known that the set of vectors

(\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lozenge q))_{q\in T}

of combinations of reachability probabilities for states in
T
that can be realized by a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
can be described by a system of linear inequalities (see, e.g.,
[
19
]
).
We provide such a system of inequalities below in equations (
1
) – (
3
). The equations use
variables
x_{s.\alpha}
for all state-action pairs
(s,\alpha)
encoding the expected number of times action
\alpha
is taken in state
s
.
Setting
\mathds{1}_{s=s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}}=1
if
s=s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}
and
\mathds{1}_{s=s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}}=0
otherwise, we require

\displaystyle x_{s,\alpha}

\displaystyle\geq 0

for all
(s,\alpha)
,

(1)

\displaystyle\sum_{\alpha\in\mathit{Act}(s)}x_{s,\alpha}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in S,\beta\in\mathit{Act}(t)}x_{t,\beta}\cdot P(t,\beta,s)+\mathds{1}_{s=s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}}}

for all
s\in S\setminus T
,

(2)

\displaystyle y_{q}

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in S,\beta\in\mathit{Act}(t)}x_{t,\beta}\cdot P(t,\beta,q)

\displaystyle\text{ for all $q\in T$}.

(3)

The variables
y_{q}
for
q\in T
represent the probabilities that state
q
is reached.
We can now express the expected value of
\mathrm{WR}
and
\mathrm{WR}^{2}
via variables
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
via the constraints:

e_{1}=\sum_{q\in T}y_{q}\cdot\mathit{wgt}(q)\quad\text{ and }\quad e_{2}=\sum_{q\in T}y_{q}\cdot\mathit{wgt}(q)^{2}.

(4)

The variance can now be written as a quadratic objective function in
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
:

\text{maximize }\quad e_{2}-e_{1}^{2}.

(5)

Theorem 13
.

The maximal value in objective (
5
) under constraints (
1
) – (
4
) is

\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})
.

Due to the concavity of the objective function, we conclude:

Corollary 14
.

The maximal variance
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})
can be computed in polynomial time. Furthermore, there is a memoryless randomized scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
with
\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\mathrm{WR})=\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\max}(\mathrm{WR})
, which can also be computed in polynomial time.

Computation of the demonic variance.

The demonic variance can also be expressed as the solution to a quadratic program.
To encode the reachability probabilities for states in
T
under two distinct schedulers, we
use variables
x_{s,\alpha}
for all state weight pairs
(s,\alpha)
and
y_{q}
for
q\in T
subject to constraints (
1
) – (
3
) as before.
Additionally, we use variables
x_{s,\alpha}^{\prime}
for all state weight pairs
(s,\alpha)
and
y_{q}^{\prime}
for
q\in T
subject to the analogue constraints (
1
′
) – (
3
′
) using these primed variables.
The maximization of the demonic variance can be expressed as

\text{maximize }\quad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{q,r\in T}y_{q}\cdot y^{\prime}_{r}\cdot(\mathit{wgt}(q)-\mathit{wgt}(r))^{2}.

(6)

Theorem 15
.

The maximum in (
6
) under constraints (
1
) – (
3
), (
1
′
) – (
3
′
) is

\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})
.

The quadratic objective function (
6
) is not concave. However, it is
bilinear
and
separable
.
This means that the variables can be split into two sets, the primed and the unprimed variables, such that the quadratic terms only contain products of variables from different sets and each constraint contains
only variables from the same set.
In general, checking whether the solution to a separable bilinear program exceeds a given threshold is NP-hard
[
25
]
.
Nevertheless, solution methods tailored for bilinear programs that perform well in practice have been developed (see, e.g.,
[
20
]
).
Further, bilinearity allows us to conclude:

Corollary 16
.

There is a pair of memoryless deterministic schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
for
\mathcal{M}
such that

\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathit{dem}}(\mathrm{WR})=\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})
.

For the complexity of the threshold problem, we can conclude an NP upper bound.
Whether the computation of the demonic variance is possible in polynomial time is left open.

Corollary 17
.

Given
\mathcal{M}
,
\mathit{wgt}
and
\vartheta\in\mathbb{Q}
, deciding whether
\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathrm{WR})\geq\vartheta
in in NP.

5
Accumulated rewards

One of the most important random variables studied on MDPs are accumulated rewards:
Let
\mathcal{M}=(S,\mathit{Act},P,s_{\mathit{\scriptscriptstyle init}})
be an MDP and let
\mathit{rew}\colon S\to\mathbb{N}
be a reward function.
We extend the reward function to paths
\pi=s_{0}\alpha_{0}s_{1}\dots
by

\mathit{rew}(\pi)=\sum_{i=0}^{\infty}\mathit{rew}(s_{i})
.
For this random variable, we prove the following result:

Main result.

The maximal variance
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
and an optimal randomized finite-memory scheduler can be computed in exponential time.

The demonic variance
\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
can be computed as the solution to a bilinear program that can be constructed in exponential time. Furthermore, there is a pair of deterministic finite-memory schedulers realizing the demonic variance.

We provide a sketch outlining the proof strategy. For a detailed exposition, see Appendix
D
.

Proof sketch for the main result.

It can be checked in polynomial time whether
\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})<\infty

[
2
]
.
If this is the case,
this allows us to perform the same preprocessing as in Section
4
that removes all end components without changing the possible distributions of
\mathit{rew}

[
2
]
.

Bounding expected values and expectation maximizing actions:

After the pre-processing, a terminal state is reached almost surely. As shown in
[
29
]
, this allows to obtain a bound
Q

on
\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})
in polynomial time.
Further, the maximal expectation
\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew})
from each state
s
can be computed in polynomial time
[
8
,
2
]
.
From these values, a set of
maximizing actions

\mathit{Act}^{\max}(s)
for each state
s
can be computed. After the preprocessing, a scheduler is expectation optimal iff it only chooses actions from these sets.
If a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
initially chooses a non-maximizing action in a state
s
, the expected value
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\mathit{rew})
is strictly smaller than
\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\max}(\mathit{rew})
. We define
\delta
to be the minimal difference between these values ranging over all starting states and non-maximizing actions.
So,
\delta
is the “minimal loss” in expected value of
\mathit{rew}
received by choosing a non-maximizing action.

Switching to expectation maximization:

Using the values
Q
and
\delta
, we provide a bound
B
such that any scheduler choosing a non-maximizing action with positive probability after a path
\pi
with
\mathit{rew}(\pi)\geq B
cannot realize the maximal variance.
The bound
B
can be computed in polynomial time and its numerical value is exponential in the size of the input.

It follows that variance maximizing schedulers have to maximize the future expected rewards after a reward of at least
B
has been accumulated. Furthermore, we can show that among all expectation maximizing schedulers, a variance maximizing scheduler has to be used above the reward bound
B
.
In
[
29
]
, it is shown that a memoryless deterministic expectation maximizing scheduler
\mathfrak{U}
that maximizes the variance among all expectation maximizing schedulers can be computed in polynomial time.
So, schedulers maximizing the variance of
\mathit{rew}
can be chosen to behave like
\mathfrak{U}
once a reward of at least
B
has been accumulated.

Quadratic program:

Now, we can unfold the MDP
\mathcal{M}
by storing in the state space how much reward has been accumulated up to the bound
B
. This results on an exponentially larger MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
. Using the expected values
\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew})
and the variances
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew})
under
\mathfrak{U}
from each state
s
, we can formulate a quadratic program similar to the one for weighted reachability in Section
4
for this unfolded MDP
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
.
From the solution to this quadratic program, the maximal variance and an optimal memoryless scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
for
\mathcal{M}^{\prime}
can be extracted. Transferred back to
\mathcal{M}
, the scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
corresponds to a reward-based finite-memory scheduler that keeps track of the accumulated reward up to bound
B
.
As the quadratic program is convex, these computations can be carried out in exponential time.

Demonic variance:

For the demonic variance, the overall proof follows the same steps.
Similar to the bound
B
above, a bound
B^{\prime}
can be provided such that in any pair of scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
realizing the demonic variance, both schedulers can be assumed to switch to the behavior of the memoryless deterministic scheduler
\mathfrak{U}
above the reward bound
B^{\prime}
.
Again by unfolding the state space up to this reward bound, the demonic variance can be computed via a bilinear program of exponential size similar to the one used in
Section
4
for weighted reachability. Furthermore, the pair of optimal memoryless deterministic schedulers in the unfolded MDP, which can be extracted from the solution, corresponds to a pair of deterministic reward-based finite-memory schedulers in the original MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
∎

6
Conclusion

We introduced the notion of demonic variance that quantifies the uncertainty under probabilism
and
non-determinism of a random variable
X
in an MDP
\mathcal{M}
.
As this demonic variance is at most twice as big as the maximal variance of
X
, we used it to define the NDS for MDPs.

The demonic variance can be used to provide new types of guarantees on the behavior of systems.
A first step in this direction is the variant of Chebyshev’s Inequality using the demonic variance proved in this paper. Furthermore, the demonic variance and the NDS can serve as the basis for notions of responsibility. On the one hand, such notions could ascribe responsibility for the uncertainty to non-determinism and probabilism.
On the other hand, comparing the NDS from different starting states can be used to identify regions of the state space in which the non-deterministic choices are of high importance.

For weighted reachability and accumulated rewards, we proved that randomized finite-memory schedulers are sufficient to maximize the variance. For the demonic variance,
even pairs of deterministic finite-memory schedulers are sufficient.
While we obtained upper bounds via the formulation of the computation problems as quadratic programs, determining the precise complexities is left as future work.
In the case of accumulated rewards, we restricted to non-negative rewards. When dropping this restriction, severe difficulties have to be expected
as several related problems on MDPs exhibit inherent number-theoretic difficulties rendering the decidability status of the corresponding decision problems open
[
28
]
.

Of course the investigation of the demonic variance and NDS for further random variables constitutes an interesting direction for future work.
For practical purposes, studying also the approximability of the maximal and demonic variance is important.

Finally, In the spirit of the demonic variance, further notions can be defined to quantify the uncertainty in
X
if the non-determinism in two executions of
\mathcal{M}
is not resolved independently, but information can be passed between the two executions. This could be useful, e.g., to analyze the potential power of coordinated attacks on a network.
Formally, such a notion could be defined as

\sup_{\mathfrak{S}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}}((X_{1}-X_{2})^{2})

where
\mathfrak{S}
ranges over all schedulers for
\mathcal{M}\otimes\mathcal{M}
.
In this context, also using an asynchronous product of
\mathcal{M}
with
\mathcal{M}
could be reasonable.

\mathfrak{S}
for

[

2
]
.
The actions such a scheduler chooses in a state

\mathit{Act}^{\max}(s)\\

Finally, we call a scheduler

D.1
Computing the maximal variance

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 19
.

The maximal variance
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
as well as an optimal randomized reward-based finite-memory scheduler can be computed in exponential time.

In this section, we will show that we can restrict the supremum in the definitions of the maximal variance

Notation

\mathfrak{S}
after
\pi

\mathfrak{S}

\mathfrak{S}_{\pi,\alpha}(\zeta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathfrak{S}(\pi\circ\zeta)
.
Further, given a scheduler

, we denote by

\mathfrak{S}

\mathfrak{S}\uparrow_{\pi,\alpha}\mathfrak{T}

Bound

We begin by defining the

This bound depends on the maximal expected value

. As

, we conclude
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}\geq B)\leq\frac{1}{2}
.

\mathfrak{U}
.

Now, we can derive a system of linear inequalities

Ax\leq b

(7)

for a rational matrix
A
and vector
b
computable in polynomial time from
\mathcal{N}
such that the variable vector
x

contains, among others, variables
y_{q}
for each
q\in T_{1}^{\prime}\cup T_{2}^{\prime}
such that these variables capture exactly the possible
combinations of reachability probabilities of the terminal states in
\mathcal{N}
. This system of constraints is the system given in Equations (
1
) – (
3
) in Section
4
transferred to the MDP
\mathcal{N}
.

As for weighted reachability, we introduce two new variables
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
that express the expected value of
\mathit{rew}
and of
\mathit{rew}^{2}
and that only depend on the variables
y_{q}
with
q\in T_{1}^{\prime}\cup T_{2}^{\prime}
.
For the expected value, this is straight-forward:

e_{1}=\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}y_{(s,w)}\cdot w+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}y_{(s,w)}\cdot(w+\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew}))

(8)

For the expected value of
\mathit{rew}^{2}
, we derive

\displaystyle e_{2}={}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}y_{(s,w)}\cdot w^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}y_{(s,w)}\cdot(w+2w\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew}^{2}))

(9)

The fact that this set of constraints works as intended is stated in the following lemma and proved in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 23
.

For each scheduler
\mathfrak{S}\in\Sigma^{\mathfrak{U}}_{B}
, there is a solution to (
7
) – (
9
) in which

e_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
and
e_{2}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})
,
and vice versa.

Proof.

Let
A_{s,w}
be the event that terminal state
(s,w)\in T_{1}\cup T_{2}
is reached in
\mathcal{N}
. One of the events
A_{s,w}
occurs with probability
1
under any scheduler.
In
\mathcal{M}
, the corresponding events are that either a absorbing state is reached after a path
\pi
with
\mathit{rew}(\pi)\leq B

or that the first prefix of a run with reward
w>B
ends in state
s
.

Given a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}\in\Sigma^{\mathfrak{U}}_{B}
viewed as a scheduler for
\mathcal{N}
, we know (see
[
19
, Theorem 9.16]
) that there is a solution to (
7
)
such that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\lozenge(s,w))=y_{(s,w)}
for all
(s,w)\in T_{1}\cup T_{2}
, and vice versa.
In
\mathcal{M}
, this corresponds to
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(A_{s,w})=y_{(s,w)}
.

For the expected value, it is then clear that

\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})=\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}y_{(s,w)}\cdot w+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}y_{(s,w)}\cdot(w+\mathbb{E}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew}))=e_{1}

as the scheduler switches to the behavior of
\mathfrak{U}
which maximizes the future expected rewards in case of event
A_{s,w}
for
(s,w)\in T_{2}
.

For the expected value of
\mathit{rew}^{2}
, we compute

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}\cup T_{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2}\mid A_{s,w})\cdot\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(A_{s,w})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}w^{2}\cdot y_{s,w}+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2}\mid A_{s,w})\cdot y_{s,w}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}w^{2}\cdot y_{s,w}+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}((w+\mathit{rew})^{2})\cdot y_{s,w}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}w^{2}\cdot y_{s,w}+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}((w+\mathit{rew})^{2})\cdot y_{s,w}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{1}}w^{2}\cdot y_{s,w}

\displaystyle+\sum_{(s,w)\in T_{2}}(w^{2}+2w\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{U}}_{\mathcal{M},s}(\mathit{rew}^{2}))\cdot y_{s,w}

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle e_{2}.\qed

Consequently, the maximal variance can be found via the following optimization objective subject to (
7
) – (
9
):

\text{maximize }\quad e_{2}-e_{1}^{2}.

(10)

As the quadratic program (
7
) – (
10
) is a concave maximization problem and can be constructed in polynomial time from
\mathcal{N}
, which is of size exponential in the size of
\mathcal{M}
,
we conclude that the optimal variance can be computed in exponential time.
Furthermore, from a solution to the quadratic program, we can extract a memoryless scheduler for
\mathcal{N}
as in Corollary
14
.
A memoryless scheduler for
\mathcal{N}
corresponds to a reward-based finite-memory scheduler for
\mathcal{M}
.
This is the case here as a scheduler only has to keep track of the reward accumulated so far up to the bound
B
. Hence, we conclude:

Theorem 24
.

The maximal variance
\mathbb{V}^{\max}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
as well as an optimal randomized reward-based finite-memory scheduler can be computed in exponential time.

D.2
Computation of the demonic variance

For the demonic variance, we follow a similar line of argumentation as we did for the maximal variance. First, we restrict the class of schedulers that we have to consider to obtain the demonic variance. Afterwards, we formulate the computation of the demonic variance as a quadratic program.

To investigate the structure of schedulers necessary to obtain the demonic variance, fix a scheduler
\mathfrak{T}
. We take a closer look at
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
for a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}){=}\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}){+}\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}){+}(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}){-}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}))^{2})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})+\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})^{2})

We can see that in order to maximize
\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
for fixed
\mathfrak{T}
, the scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
has to maximize

\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
.
For simplicity, abbreviate
C\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
.
Using the bound
M=\max_{s\in S}\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M},s}^{\max}(\mathit{rew})
, we know
0\leq C\leq M
and want to find a scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
maximizing

\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
.

We will establish results analogous to the results in Section
D.1
for the maximization of this expression.
The proofs follow the similar ideas with slightly different calculations.

Bound
B^{\prime}

To provide a bound
B^{\prime}
in analogy to Section
D.1
, we use the values
Q
,
M
, and
\delta
defined there again.
We set

B^{\prime}=\frac{Q}{2\delta}+2M+1.

As before, we know that
\mathrm{Pr}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}\geq B^{\prime})\leq\frac{1}{2}
.
The following result is shown following the same idea as for Theorem
20
and is proved in Appendix
D
.

Theorem 25
.

Let
0\leq C\leq M
.
Let
\mathfrak{S}
be a scheduler for
\mathcal{M}
and let
\pi
be a finite path with
\mathit{rew}(\pi)\geq B^{\prime}
. Assume that
\pi
has positive probability under
\mathfrak{S}
and that there is an action
\alpha\in\mathit{Act}(s)\setminus\mathit{Act}^{\max}(s)
such that

\mathfrak{S}(\pi)(\alpha)>0
.
Let
\mathfrak{T}\in\Sigma^{\max}
. Then, setting
\mathfrak{R}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{\tiny def}}}{{=}}\mathfrak{S}\uparrow_{\pi,\alpha}\mathfrak{T}
, we have

\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})<\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}).

Proof.

Let
A
be the event that scheduler
\mathfrak{S}
chooses action
\alpha
at the end of path
\pi
and let
p
be the probability of this event under
\mathfrak{S}
. Note that
\mathfrak{R}
switches to the behavior of
\mathfrak{T}
exactly in case
A
occurs. By the definition of
B^{\prime}
, we know that
p<\frac{1}{2}
.
Now, we aim to estimate the difference

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})-(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}))

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})

\displaystyle+2\cdot C\cdot p\cdot(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M},\mathit{last}(\pi)}(\mathit{rew})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{\pi,\alpha}}_{\mathcal{M},\mathit{last}(\pi)}(\mathit{rew}))

In the proof of Theorem
20
, we computed

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle p(Q-2\cdot W\cdot(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M},\mathit{last}(\pi)}(\mathit{rew})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{\pi,\alpha}}_{\mathcal{M},\mathit{last}(\pi)}(\mathit{rew})))

where
W=\mathit{rew}(\pi)
.
So, we conclude using
C\leq M

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})-(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\cdot C\cdot\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{R}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}))

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle p\cdot((2M-2W)(\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M},\mathit{last}(\pi)}(\mathit{rew})-\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}_{\pi,\alpha}}_{\mathcal{M},\mathit{last}(\pi)}(\mathit{rew}))+Q)

\displaystyle\leq{}

\displaystyle p\cdot(2(M-B^{\prime})\delta+Q).

By the choice of
B^{\prime}
this value is less than
0
.
∎

Again, we can observe in these calculations that an optimal scheduler should in fact switch to a scheduler maximizing the expected value of
\mathit{rew}^{2}
among the schedulers maximizing the expected value of
\mathit{rew}
above the bound
B^{\prime}
.

Theorem 26
.

The demonic variance can be expressed as

\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})=\sup_{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})

where

\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
range over all schedulers such that for all path
\pi
with
\mathit{rew}(\pi)\geq B
, we have
\mathfrak{S}_{\pi}=\mathfrak{U}
for the memoryless deterministic scheduler
\mathfrak{U}
given by Lemma
21
.

In order to compute the demonic variance, we use the constraints (
7
) – (
9
) with a vector of variables
x
containing the variables
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
as well as a copy
(
7
′
) – (
9
′
) using variables
x^{\prime}
containing
e_{1}^{\prime}
and
e_{2}^{\prime}
.
By Lemma
23
, for each pair of schedulers
\mathfrak{S}
and
\mathfrak{T}
, there is a solution to (
7
) – (
9
) and (
7
′
) – (
9

)
such that

\displaystyle e_{1}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}),\quad e_{2}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2}),

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}),\quad e_{2}^{\prime}=\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2}),

and vice versa. By Lemma
5

\displaystyle\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\mathit{rew})+\mathbb{V}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{T}}(\mathit{rew})+(\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\mathit{rew})-\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{T}}(\mathit{rew}))^{2}\right)

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{S}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2})-2\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{S}}(\mathit{rew})\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{M}}^{\mathfrak{T}}(\mathit{rew})+\mathbb{E}^{\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew}^{2}).

This means we can express the demonic variance via the objective function

\text{maximize}\quad e_{2}-2e_{1}e_{1}^{\prime}+e_{2}^{\prime}.

(11)

Together with
constraints (

) – (

Reasoning as in Corollary

.
We conlcude:

Theorem 27

The demonic variance
\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})
as well as a pair of deterministic reward-based finite-memory schedulers

and
\mathfrak{T}

\mathbb{V}^{\mathit{dem}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})=\mathbb{V}^{\mathfrak{S},\mathfrak{T}}_{\mathcal{M}}(\mathit{rew})

Determining the precise complexity is left as future work.
