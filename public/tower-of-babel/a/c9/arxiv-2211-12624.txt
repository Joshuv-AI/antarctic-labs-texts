Improving Robust Generalization by Direct PAC-Bayesian Bound Minimization
Zifan Wang, Nan Ding, Tomer Levinboim, Xi Chen, Radu Soricut, Carnegie Mellon University, Google Research zifan@cmu.edu, {dingnan, tomerl, chillxichen, rsoricut}@google.com

Abstract
Recent research in robust optimization has shown an overfitting-like phenomenon in which models trained against adversarial attacks exhibit higher robustness on the training set compared to the test set. Although previous work provided theoretical explanations for this phenomenon using a robust PAC-Bayesian bound over the adversarial test error, related algorithmic derivations are at best only loosely connected to this bound, which implies that there is still a gap between their empirical success and our understanding of adversarial robustness theory. To close this gap, in this paper we consider a different form of the robust PAC-Bayesian bound and directly minimize it with respect to the model posterior. The derivation of the optimal solution connects PAC-Bayesian learning to the geometry of the robust loss surface through a Trace of Hessian (TrH) regularizer that measures the surface flatness. In practice, we restrict the TrH regularizer to the top layer only, which results in an analytical solution to the bound whose computational cost does not depend on the network depth. Finally, we evaluate our TrH regularization approach over CIFAR-10/100 and ImageNet using Vision Transformers (ViT) and compare against baseline adversarial robustness algorithms. Experimental results show that TrH regularization leads to improved ViT robustness that either matches or surpasses previous state-of-the-art approaches while at the same time requires less memory and computational cost.

Let

.
We denote the predicted label by

To train the network, we sample a dataset

\displaystyle L(\theta,\mathcal{D})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\;l((x,y),g_{\theta}),

\displaystyle\hat{L}(\theta,D^{m})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}l((x,y),g_{\theta}),

2.1
Adversarial Robustness

The goal of
adversarial robustness
is to train a deep network that is robust against
\ell_{p}
norm-bounded noises. In this case, the test and training loss functions are replaced by their respective robust counterparts –

R(\theta,\mathcal{D})
on the test set and
\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})
on the training set, such that

\displaystyle R(\theta,\mathcal{D})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\max_{||{\epsilon}||_{p}\leq\delta}l((x+\epsilon,y),g_{\theta}),

and

\displaystyle\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum^{m}_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}l((x+\epsilon,y),g_{\theta}),

where
\delta
is the maximum noise budget. Various methods have been proposed to improve adversarial robustness. In this paper, we focus on the following two well-known defenses:
adversarial training

madry2017towards
, denote AT (Definition
1
), and TRADES
zhang2019theoretically
(Definition
2
).
AT is a classical method in enforcing robustness; while TRADES achieves state-of-the-art robust accuracy on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet

.

Definition 1
(Adversarial Training (AT)

)

Given a network with parameter
\theta
, a training set

, the bound of noise
\delta
and norm
||\cdot||_{p}
, AT minimizes the following objective w.r.t
\theta
,

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{AT}}(\theta,D^{m})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{CE}((x+\epsilon,y),g_{\theta}).

which considers the adversarial sample
x+\epsilon
in a
\delta
-ball around each input, instead of the input itself.

Definition 2
(TRADES

)

Given the same assumptions as in Definition
1
, TRADES minimizes the following objective w.r.t
\theta
:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{T}}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\Big[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})

\displaystyle\;\;+\lambda_{t}\cdot\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),g_{\theta})\Big]

\displaystyle\text{where }\mathrm{KLloss}((

\displaystyle x,x+\epsilon),g_{\theta})=\mathrm{KL}(s(g_{\theta}(x))||s(g_{\theta}(x+\epsilon)),

\lambda_{t}
is a penalty hyper-parameter, which balances the clean accuracy and the robustness.

Intuitively, TRADES minimizes the cross-entropy loss, while also regularizing for prediction smoothness in an
\delta
-ball around each input.

2.2
Generalizing Robustness

Rice et al.
Rice2020OverfittingIA
observe that the AT and TRADES methods may suffer from severe overfitting. Namely, the model exhibits higher robustness on the training data compared to the test data. A number of traditional strategies have been applied to alleviate the overfitting problem in robust training, such as
\ell_{2}
weight regularization, early stop
Rice2020OverfittingIA
, label smoothing, data augmentation
zhang2017mixup
;
yun2019cutmix
and using synthetic data
Gowal2021ImprovingRU
.
Below we describe popular and recent methods that were designed specifically for adversarial training.

Stochastic Weight Averaging (SWA).

Izmailov et al.
waIzmailov
introduces SWA for improved generalization performance in standard training. SWA maintains an exponential running average
\theta_{\texttt{avg}}
of the model parameters
\theta
at each training iteration and uses the running average
\theta_{\texttt{avg}}
for inference. Namely,

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}\leftarrow\texttt{SGD}(\theta^{(t)});\quad\theta_{\texttt{avg}}\leftarrow\alpha\theta_{\texttt{avg}}+(1-\alpha)\theta^{(t+1)}

where
\alpha
is commonly set to
0.995
.
Recent work has also shown that SWA helps generalization in the robust training
Gowal2021ImprovingRU
scenario. Note that maintaining the running average requires additional memory resources.

Adversarial Weight Perturbation (AWP).

Similar to Sharpness-Aware Minimization
foret2021sharpnessaware
, Wu et al.
wu2020adversarial
encourages model robustness by penalizing against the most offending local weight perturbation to the model.
Specifically, given a robust loss
\hat{R}_{\texttt{*}}(\theta,D^{m})
,
AWP minimizes the following objective w.r.t
\theta
:

\displaystyle\max_{\psi(\xi)\leq\delta_{awp}}\hat{R}_{\texttt{*}}(\theta+\xi,D^{m})

(1)

where * denotes either
AT
or
T
,
\psi
measures the amount of noise
\xi
(e.g.
\ell_{2}
norm) and
\delta_{awp}
denotes the noise total budget.
Note that solving the inner maximization problem requires at least one additional gradient ascent step in the parameter space.

Second-Order Statistic (S2O).

Jin et al.
Jin_2022_CVPR
improve the robustness generalization by regularizing the statistical correlations between the weights of the network. Furthermore, they provide a second-order and data-dependent approximation
A_{\theta}
of the weight correlation matrix.
During training, S2O minimizes the following objective w.r.t
\theta
:

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{*}}(\theta,D^{m})+\alpha||A_{\theta}||_{F}

(2)

where
\alpha>0
is a hyper-parameter. Computing
A_{\theta}
involves computing the per-instance self-Kronecker product of the logit outputs for the clean and adversarial inputs.

2.3
PAC-Bayesian Theory for Robust Training

PAC-Bayesian theory
10.1145/267460.267466
;
McAllester1999PACBayesianMA
;
Catoni2004APA
;
pac-bayesian-linear
;
PierrePAC
;
neyshabur2017exploring
provides a foundation for deriving an upper bound of the generalization gap between the training and test error.
Both AWP and S2O plug the robust loss into the classical bound
neyshabur2017exploring
to obtain the following form:

Theorem 1
(Square Root-Form PAC-Bayesian Bound
wu2020adversarial
)

Given a prior distribution
\mathcal{P}
on the weight
\theta
of a network, any
\tau\in(0,1]
, a robustness loss
R(\theta,\mathcal{D})
for a data distribution
\mathcal{D}
and its empirical version
\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})
, for any posterior distribution
\mathcal{Q}
of
\theta
, the following inequality holds with a probability at least
1-\tau
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle+4\sqrt{\frac{1}{m}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})+\log\frac{2m}{\tau}}.

(3)

Generally speaking, the goal of PAC-Bayesian learning is to optimize
\mathcal{Q}
on the RHS of Eq.
3
so as to obtain a tight upper bound on the test error (LHS).
However, directly optimizing Eq.
3
over
\mathcal{Q}
is difficult because of the square root term.
Instead of optimizing the RHS, AWP replaces it with

\max_{\xi}\hat{R}(\theta+\xi,D^{m})
plus a constant upper bound on the square root term, which is only loosely connected to the bound.
On the other hand, S2O assumes
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
as non-spherical Gaussian, i.e.
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{p}),\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{N}(\theta,\Sigma_{q})
and show that the square root bound increases as the Frobenius norm and singular value of
\Sigma_{q}
increase. To overcome the complexity of the square root term, they approximate it with
\|A_{\theta}\|_{F}
as in Eq.
2
.

3
Direct PAC-Bayesian Bound Minimization

As mentioned earlier, neither AWP
wu2020adversarial
nor S2O
Jin_2022_CVPR
minimize the PAC-Bayesian bound directly, likely due to the complicating square-root term in Eq.
3
.
However, Eq.
3
is only one instance out of
PAC-Bayesian bounds defined in Germain et al.
pac-bayesian-linear
.
According to their framework, there is also a
linear-form
of the bound related to Bayesian inference which can be analytically minimized with the aid of a Gibbs distribution
germain2016pac
;
rothfuss2021pacoh
;
pactran
.
We leverage this linear-form of the PAC-Bayesian bound, and adapt it for bounding the robustness gap:

Theorem 2
(Linear-Form PAC-Bayesian Bound
germain2016pac
)

Under the same assumptions as in Theorem
1
, for any
\beta>0
, with a probability at least
1-\tau
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})\leq

\displaystyle\;\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})+C(\tau,\beta,m),

(4)

where
C(\tau,\beta,m)
is a function independent of
\mathcal{Q}
.

Here
\beta
is a hyper-parameter that balances the three terms on the RHS. A common choice is
\beta\propto m
, i.e. the size of the dataset
germain2016pac
;
NEURIPS2021_f6b6d2a1
.
Recently,
pactran
proposed to set
\mathcal{P}
and
\mathcal{Q}
to univariate Gaussians and used a second-order approximation to estimate the PAC-Bayesian bound.
Applying a similar technique, we introduce Theorem
3
(see Appendix.
A
for the proof) which minimizes the RHS of Eq.
4
.

Theorem 3
(Minimization of PAC-Bayesian Bound)

If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
, and
\mathcal{Q}
is also a product of univariate Gaussian distributions, then the minimum of Eq.
4
w.r.t
\mathcal{Q}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})\}+C(\tau,\beta,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\{\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))\}

\displaystyle\quad+C(\tau,\beta,m)+O(\sigma^{4}_{0}).

(5)

Assuming that
\sigma_{0}^{2}=1/d
is the variance of the initial weight matrices where
d
is the input feature dimension, then
O(\sigma^{4}_{0})=O(d^{-2})
.
Theorem
3
considerably simplifies the optimization problem from one over the space of probability density functions
\mathcal{Q}
to one over model weights
\theta
.
Moreover, the resulting bound (RHS of Eq.
5
) contains the Trace of Hessian (TrH) term

\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))
of the robust loss, which sums the loss surface curvatures in all directions.
For a convex loss, the Hessian is positive semi-definite (PSD) in which case trace minimization leads
\theta
to a flatter region of the loss surface.
Although in general, our Hessian is not PSD, empirically we find that its largest eigenvalue has a much larger magnitude than the least ones
alain2018negative

and that trace minimization correlates with minimizing the standard deviation of the eigenvalues (see Figure
5
in the appendix), so that the overall curvature is effectively decreases.

The RHS of Eq.
5
in Theorem
3
provides a training objective function that bounds the optimal test error up to a constant. However, evaluating and minimizing the bound in Eq.
5
requires computing the Trace of Hessian (TrH) term.
Unfortunately, computing the Hessian directly by applying auto-differentiation twice is infeasible for a large deep network. Another solution is to apply Hutchinson’s method
10.1145/1944345.1944349
to randomly approximate the TrH,
however, the variance of the resulting estimator is too high and it ends up being too noisy for training in practice.

Instead, we propose to restrict TrH regularization to the top layer of the deep network only. Although this restriction brings approximation error to the bound, it has two benefits.
First, the TrH on the top layer has simple analytical expressions for (Section
3.1
). Second, we show that the top-layer TrH regularization effectively regularizes the TrH of the entire network as well (Section
3.2
).

3.2
The effect of Top-Layer TrH Regularization

Although the TrH regularization is restricted to the top layer only, the gradient back-propagates to the entire network through the penultimate layer features. This motivates the following research question:

What effect does top-layer TrH regularization have on the TrH of the entire network?

To answer this question, we take a two-step approach. First, we experiment with a small 3-layer network on the synthetic Two-Moon dataset (see Appendix
B
for a visualization of the dataset), provided that computing the full network Hessian is inexpensive. Subsequently, we provide a theorem that can be used to bound the Trace of Hessian of the full network with that of the top layer only.

Example 1
(Two Moons)

We use
500
training examples, where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and
y\in\{0,1\}
from the Two Moon dataset. We train a 3-layer fully-connected network with Dense(100)-ReLU-Dense(100)-ReLU-Dense(2) with the AT loss under three settings:

•

Standard: no regularization.

•

Top: Regularizing the TrH of the top layer only.

•

Full: Regularizing the TrH of the entire network.

Throughout the training process (x-axis), we evaluate the TrH of the
entire
network (y-axis) with a numerical approach (twice differentiation) and plot the results in Figure
2
.

Figure
2
empirically shows that regularizing the TrH of the top layer (orange) indirectly reduces the TrH of the entire network and is nearly as effective as regularizing the entire network (green) after the 60-th epoch.

Next, we provide a theoretical justification for the impact of top-layer TrH regularization on the layers below. Intuitively, our Theorem
4
, establishes an inductive relation between the TrH of the consecutive layers in a feedforward neural network with ReLU activation and CE loss, a common building block in AT and TRADES training.

W^{(i-1)}
, is equal to

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}^{\mathrm{CE}}_{x}(W^{(i-1)})=||\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}||^{2}_{2}\sum_{k,d\in P^{(i)}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d}

\displaystyle\text{where }\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot\{h(x,\theta)\}_{k}

\displaystyle P^{(i)}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\{d_{i}|\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0\}

and the following inequality holds for any
\mathcal{H}^{(i)},\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}
:

\displaystyle\max_{k,d_{i}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}\leq\max_{k,d_{i+1}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}\}_{k,d_{i+1}}\cdot||W^{(i)}||^{2}_{1}.

(7)

Put simply, the max-norm of the Hessian of each layer forms an upper bound for the max-norm of the layer below it (times a scalar). Therefore, regularizing the TrH of the top layer only implicitly regularizes the Hessian of the layers below it. This effect explains the phenomenon observed in Example
1
.

Furthermore, the operator norm
||W^{(i)}||^{2}_{1}
of weights in Eq.
7
makes an interesting connection between TrH minimization and robust training. Roth et al.
NEURIPS2020_ab731488
show that robust training is an implicit way of regularizing the weight operator norm. Moreover, directly minimizing the operator norm is a commonly used technique in training certifiable robust networks
leino2021globally
;
huang2021training
, a stronger version of the robustness guarantee targeted in this paper. Thus, the combination of robustness training and top-layer regularization results in an implicit regularization on TrH for internal layers.

5
Related Work and Discussion

In this section, we situate our contributions in the broader context of current literature, such as related work on PAC-Bayesian theory, flatness regularization, as well as other types of robust adversarial losses.

PAC-Bayesian Theory and Flatness.

Beyond the PAC-Bayesian inspired approaches for robust training that were already mentioned in the paper (such as Wu et al.
wu2020adversarial
), a different line of work focuses on adapting PAC-Bayesian bounds for an ensemble of models, to improve either adversarial robustness
NEURIPS2021_78e8dffe
or out-of-distribution (OOD) robustness data
Zecchin2022RobustPT
.
Several other works have focused on methods for finding flat local minima of the loss surface in order to improve (standard) model performance
foret2021sharpnessaware
;
Jia2020
;
pactran
.
Also outside the scope of adversarial robustness, Ju et al.
pmlr-v162-ju22a
find that trace of Hessian regularization leads to robustness against noise in the
labels
.
Their Hessian regularization is based on randomized estimation and is done for all layers, whereas we effectively apply TrH regularization on the top layer only and with Theorem
4
as theoretical justification.

Perhaps the closest approach to ours is the PACTran-Gaussian metric
pactran
, which uses a 2nd-order approximation of the PAC-Bayesian bound as the metric for evaluating the transferability of pretrained checkpoints. Similarly to our Eq.
5
, the PACTran metric is composed of two terms: an
\ell_{2}
regularized empirical risk (RER) and a flatness regularizer (FR). Our work was inspired by the PACTran metric, but also makes several improvements. Firstly, in PACTran a single posterior variance
\sigma_{q}^{2}
was shared for all parameters. On the contrary, we used different
\sigma_{q}^{2}
’s for different parameters. Secondly, in PACTran the optimization of the posterior mean
\theta_{q}
is over the RER term only, while our optimization is over both RER and FR. These two changes potentially make our bound tighter than the one in the PACTran paper. In addition, we also study and explicitly include the high-order terms in
O(\sigma_{0}^{4})
, which was neglected in PACTran.

Adversarial Surrogate Loss.

In this paper, we focused only on the AT and TRADES approaches for robust adversarial optimization as they achieve reasonable results on the datasets of choice.
In fact, recent work
Gowal2021ImprovingRU
on achieving state-of-the-art robustness on CIFAR-10 also exclusively focuses on AT or TRADES.
Beyond AT and TRADES, the works in
Wang2020Improving
;
Ding2020MMA
;
Pang2022RobustnessAA
explore other types of adversarial surrogate losses. Our method can be easily transferred to these losses and we include additional examples in Appendix
G
.

6
Conclusion

In summary, we alleviate overfitting in adversarial training with PAC-Bayesian theory.
Using a linear-form PAC-Bayesian bound overlooked by the prior work, we derive a 2nd-order estimation that includes the Trace of Hessian (TrH) of the model.
We then focus on the TrH of the top layer only, showing both empirically and theoretically (for ReLU networks) that its minimization effectively leads to the minimization of the TrH of the entire network, and thereby providing a sound flatness regularization technique for adversarial training which adds only little computational overhead.
Experimental results show that our TrH regularization method is consistently among the top performers on CIFAR-10/100 benchmark as well as achieves a significant improvement in robust test accuracy on ImageNet compared to the other baselines.

Appendix A
Theorems and Proofs

A.1
Proof of Theorem
3

Theorem
3

If
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}_{0})
, and
\mathcal{Q}
is also a product of univariate Gaussian distributions, then the minimum of Eq.
4
w.r.t
\mathcal{Q}
can be bounded by

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})\}+C(\tau,\beta,m)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\{\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))\}+C(\tau,\beta,m)+O(\sigma^{4}_{0}).

Proof.
Before we begin our proof, we emphasize that the posterior
\mathcal{Q}
that minimizes the test loss may not be the same posterior
\mathcal{Q}
that minimizes the training loss. Namely, define

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{test}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\arg\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle\mathcal{Q}_{train}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\arg\min_{\mathcal{Q}}\{\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})\},

then
\mathcal{Q}_{test}
may not be equal to
\mathcal{Q}_{train}
. However, the following inequality clearly holds based on their definitions,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{test}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{train}}R(\theta,\mathcal{D})\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}_{train}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})+C(\tau,\beta,m).

Generally speaking, it is impossible to directly find
\mathcal{Q}_{test}
without knowing the true data distribution
\mathcal{D}
.
Thus, Theorem
3
attempts to derive
\mathcal{Q}_{train}
to minimize the RHS of Eq.
4
. When it is clear which optimal posterior, i.e.
\mathcal{Q}_{train}
instead of
\mathcal{Q}_{test}
, we can derive in the theorem, we omit the subscription and directly write
\mathcal{Q}
.

Proof Overview.

Based on our assumptions, we write
\mathcal{Q}=\mathcal{N}(\theta,\Sigma)
where
\Sigma
is the (diagonal) covariance matrix.
The minimization of the bound w.r.t
\mathcal{Q}
is to minimize the bound w.r.t
\Sigma
and
\theta
. Our proof is therefore three-fold: (1) firstly, we write the expression of
\mathrm{KL}(Q||P)
using
\theta
and
\Sigma
; (2) secondly, we use a second-order Taylor expansion to decompose
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})
into terms as functions of
\Sigma
and
\theta
; and (3) finally, we minimize the bound w.r.t
\Sigma
for two cases: all weights have the same or different variances. Namely, the diagonal of
\Sigma
has the same or different elements. At the end of the minimization w.r.t
\Sigma
, we arrive at the bound that requires to minimize
\theta
to actually minimize the RHS. Our proof follows below.

Extra Notations.

Throughout the proof we will use
N
for the total number of weights, i.e. the cardinality of
\theta
. When indexing a particular weight, we write
\theta_{n}
. Let the diagonal of
\Sigma
be
\sigma^{2}=[\sigma^{2}_{1},\sigma^{2}_{2},\cdots,\sigma^{2}_{N}]^{\top}
, i.e. the variance of each weight. We use
\mathrm{Diag}(a)
to denote the diagonal matrix where the the diagonal is the elements from vector
a
.

Step I: the KL term
\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})
.

The expression of the KL term
\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})
when
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma^{2}_{0}I)
is as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\log\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\det(\Sigma)}-N+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left[\sum_{n}\log\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}-N+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sum_{n}\sigma^{2}_{n}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\right](\text{when $\Sigma$ is a diagonal matrix}).

(8)

Step II: Second-order Taylor’s Expansion for
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})
.

We expand
\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})
at the point
\theta
and use the re-parameterization trick,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}[\epsilon^{\top}\triangledown\hat{R}]}_{=0\text{ because $\epsilon$ has zero mean}}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}[\epsilon^{\top}\triangledown^{2}\hat{R}\epsilon]

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{1}{6}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma)}[\sum_{i,j,k}\frac{\partial^{3}\hat{R}}{\partial\theta_{i}\theta_{j}\theta_{k}}\epsilon_{i}\epsilon_{j}\epsilon_{k}]}_{=0\text{ because the mean and the skewness of a standard Gaussian are 0}}+O(\sigma^{4})

\displaystyle=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}[(\sigma\circ\epsilon)^{\top}\triangledown^{2}\hat{R}(\sigma\circ\epsilon)]+O(\sigma^{4})

(9)

where
\circ
is the element-wise product, a.k.a the Hadamard product. Notice the connection between a vector product and Hadamard product:

\displaystyle\text{for any vectors }a,b,\quad a\circ b=\mathrm{Diag}(a)b.

(10)

Using Eq.
10
, we simplify Eq.
9
as follows,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}[(\mathrm{Diag}(\sigma)\epsilon)^{\top}\triangledown^{2}\hat{R}(\mathrm{Diag}(\sigma)\epsilon)]+O(\sigma^{4})

\displaystyle=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma\circ\triangledown^{2}\hat{R})+O(\sigma^{4}).

(11)

Step III: Bound Minimization.

Using Eq.
8
and
11
, we re-write the bound in Theorem
2
as follows

\displaystyle E_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})+C(\tau,\beta,m)

\displaystyle=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma\circ\triangledown^{2}\hat{R})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\left[\sum_{n}\log\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}-N+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sum_{n}\sigma^{2}_{n}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\right]+O(\sigma^{4})+C(\tau,\beta,m).

(12)

There are two sets of parameters in Eq.
12
for bound minimization:
\Sigma
(i.e. the
\sigma^{2}
) and
\theta
.
To minimize w.r.t
\Sigma
, strictly speaking it requires minimizing all relevant terms and the higher-order terms in
O(\sigma^{4})
, which is clearly infeasible.
Instead, we apply the following approximation by neglecting the higher-order
O(\sigma^{4})
and solve

\displaystyle\sigma^{*2}=\arg\min_{\sigma^{2}}\Big\{\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma\circ\triangledown^{2}\hat{R})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\left[\sum_{n}\log\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2}_{n}}-N+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sum_{n}\sigma^{2}_{n}}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}\right]\Big\}.

In the following, we discuss two cases of
\sigma_{n}^{2}
.

Case I (Spherical Gaussian):
\forall n,\sigma^{2}_{n}=\sigma^{2}_{q}
.

In this case, the solution to the problem above is to take the derivative w.r.t.
\sigma^{2}_{q}
and set it to zero. That is, for the optimal
\sigma^{*2}_{q}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))+\frac{K}{\beta}(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{*2}_{q}})=0\implies\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{*2}_{q}}=1+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta}{K}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})).

Substituting each
\sigma^{2}_{n}
with
\sigma^{*2}_{q}
in Eq.
12
gives the solution to the following problem

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma^{2}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})\right]=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{K}{2\beta}\log(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta}{K}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})))+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}_{0}}+O(\sigma^{*4}_{q}).

With the Taylor expansion for
\log(1+x)=x+O(x^{2})
,

\displaystyle\frac{K}{2\beta}\log(1+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta}{K}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{2\beta}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))+O(\sigma^{4}_{0})+O(\sigma^{*4}_{q}).

We can actually merge
O(\sigma^{4}_{0})+O(\sigma^{*4}_{q})
because

\displaystyle\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2*}_{q}}=1+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta}{K}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))\implies\sigma^{2}_{0}\geq\sigma^{*2}_{q}

so
O(\sigma^{4}_{0})+O(\sigma^{*4}_{q})=O(\sigma^{4}_{0})
. Lastly, to minimize the bound, we optimize it w.r.t
\theta
, which leads us to land on:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\{\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))\}+C(\tau,\beta,m)+O(\sigma^{4}_{0}).

Case II (Diagonal):
\exists n_{1},n_{2},\sigma^{2}_{n_{1}}\neq\sigma^{2}_{n_{2}}
.

In this case, we take the derivative w.r.t each
\sigma^{2}_{n}
and set it to zero. That is, for each optimal
\sigma^{2*}_{n}
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\hat{R}^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}_{n}}+\frac{1}{\beta}(\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}_{0}}-\frac{1}{\sigma^{2*}_{n}})=0\implies\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{\sigma^{2*}_{n}}=1+\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta\frac{\partial^{2}\hat{R}}{\partial\theta^{2}_{n}}.

Substituting
\sigma^{2}_{n}
with
\sigma^{2*}_{n}
in Eq.
12
gives the solution to the following problem

\displaystyle\min_{\sigma^{2}_{n}}\left[\mathbb{E}_{\theta\in\mathcal{Q}}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{\beta}\mathrm{KL}(\mathcal{Q}||\mathcal{P})\right]=\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{n}\log(1+\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta\frac{\partial\hat{R}^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}_{n}})+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}_{0}}+O(\sigma^{*4}).

Similar to Case I, we take Taylor’s Expansion of
\log(1+x)
so

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\beta}\sum_{n}\log(1+\sigma^{2}_{0}\beta\frac{\partial\hat{R}^{2}}{\partial\theta^{2}_{n}})=\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))+O(\sigma^{4}_{0}).

Lastly, using
O(\sigma^{4}_{0})+O(\sigma^{*4})=O(\sigma^{4}_{0})
, we land on the same objective as derived in Case I:

\displaystyle\min_{\theta}\{\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m})+\frac{||\theta||^{2}_{2}}{2\beta\sigma^{2}_{0}}+\frac{\sigma^{2}_{0}}{2}\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta}\hat{R}(\theta,D^{m}))\}+C(\tau,\beta,m)+O(\sigma^{4}_{0}).

A.2
Proof of Theorem
4

Theorem
4
Suppose that
g_{\theta}
is a feed-forward network with ReLU activation. For the
i
-th layer, let
W^{(i)}
be its weight and
\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{D^{(i)}}
be its input evaluated at
x
. Thus,
\mathrm{TrH}^{\mathrm{CE}}_{x}(W^{(i-1)})
, i.e. TrH evaluated at
x
using a CE loss w.r.t
W^{(i-1)}
, is as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}^{\mathrm{CE}}_{x}(W^{(i-1)})=||\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}||^{2}_{2}\sum_{k,d\in P^{(i)}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d}

\displaystyle\text{where }\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle P^{(i)}

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\{d_{i}|\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0\}

and the following inequality holds for any
\mathcal{H}^{(i)},\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}
:

\displaystyle\max_{k,d_{i}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}\leq\max_{k,d_{i+1}}\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}\cdot||W^{(i)}||^{2}_{1}.

Proof.

We use
d_{i-1},d_{i}
and
d_{i+1}
to index the element of
\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}
,
\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}
and
\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}
. By the definition of trace, we need to compute the second-order derivative of the CE loss w.r.t to each entry in
W^{(i)}
. That is, we write

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}^{\mathrm{CE}}_{x}(W^{(i-1)})=\sum_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial W^{(i-1)2}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}(CE((x,y),g_{\theta})).

(13)

First, we derive the first-order derivative. The following steps are based on the chain rule:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}(CE((x,y),g_{\theta}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\frac{\partial}{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}(CE((x,y),g_{\theta}))\cdot\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}(\{s(g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}-y_{k})\cdot\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}.

The second-order derivative is therefore equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial W^{(i-1)2}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}(CE((x,y),g_{\theta}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Big(\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\left[\{s(g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}-y_{k})\right]\cdot\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}+h_{k}(x,\theta)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\left[\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\right]\Big)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\Big(h_{k}(x,\theta)\cdot\left[\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\right]^{2}+h_{k}(x,\theta)\cdot\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\left[\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\right]\Big).

Notice that the first-order derivative of ReLU is either 1 or 0, so we have plenty of identity functions in
\frac{\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}
. Rigorously, ReLU does not have a second-order derivative. In practice, one will return 0 when querying the second-order derivative of ReLU. Thus, the following holds in practice.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\right]=0.

(14)

Eq.
14
simplifies the expression of the second-order derivative w.r.t
W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}
so we have the following clean expression:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial W^{(i-1)2}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}(CE((x,y),g_{\theta}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}}\}_{d_{i}}}\cdot\frac{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}{\partial W^{(i-1)}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\cdot\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0]\cdot\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i-1}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0]\cdot\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}^{2}_{d_{i-1}}\cdot\sum_{k}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta).

(15)

By defining
\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)
, we further simplify Eq.
15
as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial W^{(i-1)2}_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}}(CE((x,y),g_{\theta}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0]\cdot\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}^{2}_{d_{i-1}}\cdot\sum_{k}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}.

(16)

We plug Eq.
16
back to Eq.
13
and arrive

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}^{\mathrm{CE}}_{x}(W^{(i-1)})

\displaystyle=\sum_{d_{i-1},d_{i}}\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0]\cdot\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}^{2}_{d_{i-1}}\cdot\sum_{k}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle=\Big(\sum_{d_{i-1}}\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}^{2}_{d_{i-1}}\Big)\cdot\sum_{k,d_{i}}\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0]\cdot\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle=||\{\mathcal{I}^{(i-1)}_{x}\}||^{2}_{2}\cdot\sum_{k,d_{i}}\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}>0]\cdot\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}.

By defining
P^{(i)}
as a set of indices of positive neurons in
\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}
, i.e,
\forall d_{2}\in P^{(i)},\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{2}}>0
, we simplify the equation above as follows

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}^{\mathrm{CE}}_{x}(W^{(i-1)})=||\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}||^{2}_{2}\cdot\sum_{k,d_{i}\in P^{(i)}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}.

Furthermore, by the definition of
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}
, we notice that

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle=\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)

\displaystyle=\Biggl\{\sum_{d_{i+1}}\left[\frac{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}}\cdot\frac{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]\Biggr\}^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta)

(17)

\displaystyle=\Biggl\{\sum_{d_{i+1}}\left[\Big(\frac{\{\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}\}_{k,d_{i+1}}}{h_{k}(x,\theta)}\Big)^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]\Biggr\}^{2}\cdot h_{k}(x,\theta).

(18)

Notice that the transition from Eq.
17
to Eq.
18
is because
\forall k,h_{k}(x,\theta)>0
. Thus, we find

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle=\Biggl\{\sum_{d_{i+1}}\left[\{\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}\}^{\frac{1}{2}}_{k,d_{i+1}}\cdot\frac{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]\Biggr\}^{2}.

(19)

For each layer
i
, we denote
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}=\max_{k,d_{i}}\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}
. Since
\forall i,d_{i},\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}>0
, we can take the square root for
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}
such that

\displaystyle\forall k,d_{i},(\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max})^{\frac{1}{2}}\geq(\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}})^{\frac{1}{2}}.

Thus, we can bound
\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}
as follows:

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\Biggl\{\sum_{d_{i+1}}\left[(\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}_{max})^{\frac{1}{2}}\cdot\frac{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]\Biggr\}^{2}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}\left[\sum_{d_{i+1}}\frac{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}}{\partial\{\mathcal{I}^{(i)}_{x}\}_{d_{i}}}\right]^{2}.

The squared term can be further bounded by using the chain rule, namely,

\displaystyle\{\mathcal{H}^{(i)}\}_{k,d_{i}}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}_{max}\left[\sum_{d_{i+1}}\mathbb{I}[\{\mathcal{I}^{(i+1)}_{x}\}_{d_{i+1}}>0]\cdot W^{(i)}_{d_{i},d_{i+1}}\right]^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}_{max}\left[\sum_{d_{i+1}}|W^{(i)}_{d_{i},d_{i+1}}|\right]^{2}.

Recall that our goal is to bound
\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}
. By definition, we take the max on both sides over
d_{i}
and
k
(although the class dimension is already gone). The direction of the inequality still holds because quantities on both sides are all non-negative.

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}_{max}\max_{d_{i}}\left[\sum_{d_{i+1}}|W^{(i)}_{d_{i},d_{i+1}}|\right]^{2}.

The order of max and square can be exchanged because
|W^{(i)}_{d_{i},d_{i+1}}|
is non-negative. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}

\displaystyle\leq\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}_{max}\left[\max_{d_{i}}\sum_{d_{i+1}}|W^{(i)}_{d_{i},d_{i+1}}|\right]^{2}.

The last term inside the square is the definition of the
\ell_{1}
operator norm so we directly write

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}^{(i)}_{max}\leq\mathcal{H}^{(i+1)}_{max}\cdot||W^{(i)}||^{2}_{1}.

A.3
Derivations of Propositions

Proposition
1

Given a training dataset
D^{m}
and the adversarial input example
x^{\prime}
for each example
x
, the top-layer TrH of the AT loss (Definition
1
) is equal to

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\hat{R}_{\texttt{AT}}(\theta,D^{m}))=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\mathrm{TrH}_{\texttt{AT}}(x^{\prime};\theta),

\displaystyle\text{where }\;\;\mathrm{TrH}_{\texttt{AT}}(x^{\prime};\theta)=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta).

Proof.
We firstly write the expression of
\hat{R}_{\texttt{AT}}(\theta,D^{m})
based on Definition
1
,

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{AT}}(\theta,D^{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{CE}((x+\epsilon,y),F_{\theta}).

Let
x^{\prime}
be an adversarial example, namely

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=\arg\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\left[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),F_{\theta})\right]+x.

AT loss can be simplified as

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{AT}}(\theta,D^{m})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\mathrm{CE}((x^{\prime},y),F_{\theta})=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\sum_{k}-y_{k}\log s(g^{\prime}_{k}),

where
g^{\prime}_{k}=\{\theta^{\top}_{t}f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{k}
is the logit score of class
k
at the adversarial example
x^{\prime}
and
s(\cdot)
is the softmax function. Let’s consider the loss at each adversarial point

\displaystyle R=\sum_{k}-y_{k}\log s(g^{\prime}_{k}).

We want to compute the derivatives of
R
with respect to the weight at the top layer
\{\theta_{t}\}_{jk}
. For the ease of the notation, let’s define
w=\theta_{t}
so
w_{jk}=\{\theta_{t}\}_{jk}
and
\triangledown_{w_{jk}}R
is equal to

\displaystyle\triangledown_{w_{jk}}R=\sum_{k}-y_{k}\triangledown_{w_{jk}}[\log s(g^{\prime}_{k})]=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-y_{k}).

(20)

The transition to Eq.
20
uses the standard form of the gradient of Cross Entropy loss with the softmax activation. To compute the trace of a Hessian, we can skip the cross terms in Hessian (e.g.
\triangledown_{w_{j_{1}k},w_{j_{2}k}}R
) because they are not on the diagonal of the matrix. Thus, we are only interested in
\triangledown^{2}_{w_{jk}}R
, which are

\displaystyle\triangledown^{2}_{w_{jk}}R

\displaystyle=\triangledown_{w_{jk}}\left[\triangledown_{w_{jk}}R\right]=\triangledown_{w_{jk}}\left[x^{\prime}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-y_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\cdot\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\cdot s(g^{\prime}_{k})(1-s(g^{\prime}_{k}))

\displaystyle=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-s^{2}(g^{\prime}_{k})).

Eventually, to compute the trace of Hessian matrix we sum all diagonal terms so that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{w_{jk}}R)=\sum_{j,k}\triangledown^{2}_{w_{jk}}R=\sum_{j,k}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-s^{2}(g^{\prime}_{k}))=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta)

and we denote
\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{w_{jk}}R)
as
\mathrm{TrH}_{\texttt{AT}}(x^{\prime};\theta)
to complete the proof.

Proposition

Under the same assumption as in Proposition
1
, the top-layer TrH of the TRADES loss (Def’

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\hat{R}_{\texttt{T}}(\theta,D^{m}))=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\mathrm{TrH}_{\texttt{T}}(x,x^{\prime};\lambda_{t},\theta),

Before we start our proof of this proposition, we introduce the following useful lemmas.

Lemma 1

Let
s(g)
be the softmax output of the logit output
g
computed at input x, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s(g)}{\partial g}=\Phi=diag[s(g)]-s(g)\cdot\{s(g)\}^{\top}

(21)

where
diag(v)
returns an identity matrix with its diagonal replaced by a vector
v

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}=s(g_{i})(\mathbb{I}\left[i=k\right]-s(g_{k})),\Phi_{ik}=s(g_{i})\cdot\mathbb{I}\left[i=k\right]-s(g_{i})s(g_{k})\implies\frac{\partial s(g)}{\partial g}=\Phi.

Lemma 2

Let
\log s(g(x))
be the log softmax output of the logit output g computed at input x, then

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log s(g(x))}{\partial g(x)}=\Psi=I-\mathbf{1}\cdot\{s(g(x))\}^{\top}

Proof.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\log s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}=\mathbb{I}\left[i=k\right]-s(g_{k}),\Psi_{ik}=\mathbb{I}\left[i=k\right]-s(g_{k})\implies\frac{\partial s(g)}{\partial g}=\Psi.

Now we are ready to present our proof of the Proposition

.

Proof.
We write the expression of
\hat{R}_{\texttt{T}}(\theta,D^{m})
based on as Definition

.

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{T}}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\Big[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})+\lambda_{t}\cdot\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),g_{\theta})\Big],

where

. Thus, we can compute the trace of Hessian on the top layer for the CE loss and the KL loss, respectively. Namely, we derive
\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),F_{\theta})]
and
\triangledown^{2}[\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),F_{\theta})]

First, we see that the expression of

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),F_{\theta})]\}=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x)||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x,\theta).

(22)

Second, by denoting

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=\arg\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\left[\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),F_{\theta})\right]+x,

\displaystyle\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x^{\prime}),F_{\theta})=-\sum_{i}s(g_{i})\log(s(g^{\prime}_{i}))+\left[\sum_{i}s(g_{i})\log(s(g_{i}))\right],\quad g_{i}=\{\theta^{\top}_{t}f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{i},g^{\prime}_{i}=\{\theta^{\top}_{t}f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{i}.

w\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\theta_{t}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial w_{jk}}=-\sum_{i}s(g_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial g^{\prime}_{k}}\left[\log(s(g^{\prime}_{i}))\right]\frac{\partial g^{\prime}_{k}}{\partial\partial w_{jk}}+\underbrace{\sum_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial s(g_{i})}\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial\partial w_{jk}}}_{\text{gradient through }g_{k}}.

(23)

g

In Case I where the gradient on
g

23

Case I: Stop Gradient on

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i}s(g_{i})\frac{\partial}{\partial g^{\prime}_{k}}\left[\log(s(g^{\prime}_{i}))\right]\frac{\partial g^{\prime}_{k}}{\partial\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-s(g_{k})).

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\{\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x^{\prime}),F_{\theta})]\}=\mathrm{Tr}\{\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}K\}=\sum_{jk}\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial w^{2}_{jk}}=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta).

(25)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\Big[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})+\lambda_{t}\cdot\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}

\displaystyle\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),g_{\theta})\Big]\Big\}=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x)||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x,\theta)+\lambda_{t}||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta),

\displaystyle\text{where }x^{\prime}=\arg\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\left[\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),F_{\theta})\right]+x

By denoting
\mathrm{TrH}_{\texttt{t}}(x,x^{\prime};\lambda_{t},\theta)\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\mathrm{Tr}\Big\{\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\Big[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})+\lambda_{t}\cdot\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{KLloss}((x,x+\epsilon),g_{\theta})\Big]\Big\}
, we complete the proof for this case and this is the statement shown in Proposition
2
.

.

We restart our derivation from Eq.

and expand the first term as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial w_{jk}}=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-s(g_{k}))+\sum_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial s(g_{i})}\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial\partial w_{jk}}.

Here

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial s(g_{i})}\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial\partial w_{jk}}=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})+\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\log s(g_{i})+s(g_{i})\frac{\partial\log s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\right]

Using Lemma

and
2
, we write

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}=\Phi_{ik},\quad\quad\frac{\partial\log s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}=\Psi_{ik},\quad\Phi_{ik}=s(g_{i})\Psi_{ik}

and

\displaystyle\sum_{i}\frac{\partial K}{\partial s(g_{i})}\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial\partial w_{jk}}=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\Phi_{ik}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})+\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\Phi_{ik}\log s(g_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\right].

Therefore, the first-order derivative of
K
with respect to
w_{jk}
is

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-s(g_{k}))-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\Phi_{ik}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})+\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\Phi_{ik}\log s(g_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}s(g^{\prime}_{k})}_{K^{\prime}}\underbrace{-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}s(g_{k})}_{K_{1}}\underbrace{-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\Phi_{ik}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})}_{K_{2}}+\underbrace{\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\Phi_{ik}\log s(g_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\right]}_{K_{3}}.

To calculate the seconder-order derivative of

w.r.t

w.r.t

(1) Derivative of
K^{\prime}

K^{\prime}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K^{\prime}}{\partial w_{jk}}=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}(s(g^{\prime}_{k})-s^{2}(g^{\prime}_{k})).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\frac{\partial K^{\prime}}{\partial w_{jk}})=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta).

(2) Derivative of
K_{1}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K_{1}}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\frac{\partial s(g_{k})}{\partial g_{k}}

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\Phi_{kk}

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}(s(g_{k})-s^{2}(g_{k})).

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\frac{\partial K_{1}}{\partial w_{jk}})

\displaystyle=-\sum_{jk}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}(s(g_{k})-s^{2}(g_{k}))

\displaystyle=-f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})^{\top}f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x,\theta)

(3) Derivative of
K_{2}
.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K_{2}}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial w_{jk}}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\frac{\partial\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial g^{\prime}_{k}}\frac{\partial g^{\prime}_{k}}{\partial w_{jk}}\right]

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\Psi^{\prime}_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\right]

Notice that

\displaystyle i=k\implies\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}=\frac{\partial\Phi_{kk}}{\partial g_{k}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{k}}(s(g_{k})-s^{2}(g_{k}))=\Phi_{kk}-2s(g_{k})\Phi_{kk};

and

\displaystyle i\neq k\implies\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}=\frac{\partial}{\partial g_{k}}(-s(g_{i})s(g_{k}))=-2s(g_{i})\Phi_{kk}.

Thus,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}=\Phi_{kk}(\mathbb{I}[k=i]-s(g_{i}))=\Phi_{kk}\{\Psi^{\top}\}_{ik}=\Phi_{kk}\Psi_{ki}.

As a result,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K_{2}}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\Phi_{kk}\Psi_{ki}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\Psi^{\prime}_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}^{2}_{j}\Phi_{kk}\sum_{i}\Psi_{ki}\log s(g^{\prime}_{i})-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\sum_{i}\Phi_{ik}\Psi^{\prime}_{ik}

\displaystyle=(-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}^{2}_{j}\Phi_{kk})(\Psi_{k}\cdot\log s(g^{\prime}))-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}(\{\Phi^{\top}\}_{k}\cdot\{\Psi^{\prime\top}\}_{k}).

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\frac{\partial K_{2}}{\partial w_{jk}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{jk}\left[(-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}^{2}_{j}\Phi_{kk})(\Psi_{k}\cdot\log s(g^{\prime}))-\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}(\{\Phi^{\top}\}_{k}\cdot\{\Psi^{\prime\top}\}_{k})\right]

\displaystyle=-||f_{\theta_{b}}(x)||^{2}_{2}\cdot\psi^{{}^{\prime}\top}h(x,\theta)-f^{\top}_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}\omega^{\prime},

where
\psi^{\prime},\omega^{\prime}
are vectors such that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K_{3}}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial w_{jk}}\log s(g_{i})+\Phi_{ik}\frac{\partial\log s(g_{i})}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial w_{jk}}+\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial g_{k}}\frac{\partial g_{k}}{\partial w_{jk}}\right]

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\frac{\partial K_{3}}{\partial w_{jk}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{jk}\left[\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}^{2}_{j}\left[\Phi_{kk}(\Psi_{k}\cdot\log s(g))+(\{\Phi^{\top}\}_{k}\cdot\{\Psi^{\top}\}_{k})\right]\right]

Appendix B
Impact of Top-Layer Regularization

In this section, we provide a full description of Example
1
, as well as some additional results and analysis.

•

Dataset.
We use the Two Moons Dataset where the input features
x_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
and the label
y_{i}\in\{0,1\}
(Figure
4
).

•

Network.
We use a dense network Dense(100)-ReLU-Dense(100)-ReLU-Dense(2).

•

Training without Regularization (Standard).
We train the network with AT(
base
), such that it is robust in an
\ell_{\infty}
ball of size 0.02 with 1 PGD step. We use a momentum-SGD with learning rate 0.1 for 100 epochs.

•

Training with Top TrH Regularization (Top).
We further add the TrH of the top layer as a regularization term in the loss. We compute the TrH at the top layer using Proposition
1
. The regularizing coefficient for the top-layer TrH is 0.5 (i.e.
loss=AT+0.5*\mathrm{TrH}_{top}
) as we find it needs a stronger penalty due to the lack of TrH from other layers.

•

Training with Full TrH Regularization (Full).
In additional to the Standard setup, we add the TrH of the full network as a regularization term in the loss. We numerically compute the Hessian and its trace. The regularizing coefficient for the full-network TrH is 0.05 (i.e.
loss=AT+0.05*\mathrm{TrH}_{full}
).

Figure
5
, shows pairs of plots of
the sum and standard deviation of the eigenvalues of the Hessian matrix
across all layers, as well as for each of the three layers separately.
We compare the three setting (1) no regularization (Standard, blue); (2) the top-layer TrH regularization (Top, orange); and (3) the full-network TrH regularization (Full, green).

Focusing on the top-left plot in Figure
5
, we see that the TrH decreases across time with standard training (blue), but at the same time, that full-network and top-layer TrH regularization decrease it even further.
In particular, top-layer TrH regularization is nearly as effective as directly regularizing the TrH for all layers.
The standard deviation plots (on the right side of each TrH plot) show a a decrease in the standard deviation, which implies there is a contraction effect on the eigenvalues of the Hessian, with both positive and negative values approaching towards 0. This rules out the possibility that the TrH reduction comes from an increase in the magnitude of the negative eigenvalues. In fact, TrH regularization effectively contracts the magnitude of all eigenvalues and leads to a smoother region in the loss surface.

We further plot the TrH and standard deviation of Hessian eigenvalues for each individual layer respectively (where Layer 3 is the top layer).
Except for the first layer, whose eigenvalues seem to be small from the onset, the eigenvalue contraction effect is evident as training progresses, with a stronger and similar effect for both TrH regularization settings.

Appendix C
Hyper-parameters Shared by All Methods

Pre-Tuning.

Training ViTs can sometimes be challenging due to a large amount of hyper-parameters. For the choice of the parameters that are shared across different defense methods, e.g. batch size, patch size, training iterations, and etc., we do a large grid search and choose the parameter setting that produce the best results on TRADES(
base
) and use it for all the methods. This step is referred to as pre-tuning and is done per-dataset.

Pre-selected Hyper-parameters.

There is a set of hyper-parameters requiring no tuning because they are commonly selected in the literature. In the top of Table
4
and
3
, we write down the these parameters and explain the reason for choosing a particular value.

Tunable Hyper-parameters.

In the bottom of Table
4
and
3
, we show our choice of hyper-parameters for ImageNet and CIFAR-10/100, respectively. This includes

•

optimizer.
We tested a momentum-SGD (
sgd+momentum
) and an Adam optimizer (
adam
). We found that momentum-SGD is more stable in fine-tuning the ViT from a pre-trained checkpoint.

•

base_lr
,
warm_up_iterations
and
decay_type
. We linearly increase the learning rate from 0 to the
base_lr
during
warm_up_iterations
. After warm-up, we gradually schedule the learning rate based on the
decay_type
. We experiment with a multi-step and a cosine decay and find no apparent difference between these two schedulers. In the end, we choose cosine because it has less hyper-parameters to choose compared to the multi-step one.

•

l2_reg.
On ImageNet, we find
\ell_{2}
regularization with a penalty of 0.0001 helps the baseline TRADES(
base
). On CIFAR-10/100, we find that
\ell_{2}
regularization may not be necessary when using DDPM data.

•

cutmix.
In both cases we do not find the cut mix augmentation
yun2019cutmix
help to improve the results.

•

data_range.
On CIFAR10/100, we find that centered data, i.e. normalizing the data to have 0 mean and (close to) 1 standard deviation, provides better results than scaling the images to [-1, 1] (the range of data used by the pre-trained checkpoint). We simply subtract the CIFAR images from the average of per-channel mean (0.47) and divide it with the average of per-channel standard deviation (0.25). For ImageNet, we still use [-1, 1] as the data range. Notice that
{\epsilon}
ball needs to be re-scaled for both data ranges describe above. For example, when reporting results on
\epsilon=0.031
and using centered data, we need to use
\epsilon/0.25
as the actual noise bound passed to the attacker. When normalizing the data to
[-1,1]
, we need to pass
\epsilon/0.5
to the attacker.

•

patch_size
. The size of the image patch of the input sequence to ViT.
16\times 16
is the standard size of pre-trained ViT models on ImageNet21K. Thus, when fine-tuning on ImageNet, we use
16\times 16
. CIFAR images are a lot smaller compared to ImageNet images. As a result, we use a smaller patch size of
4\times 4
to produce more patches. Using a smaller patch size requires some modifications to ViT architecture and we discuss this in detail in Appendix
F
.

•

downsample
and
patch_stride.
These are particular to CIFAR images. Please refer to Appendix
F
for detail.

Hyper-parameters for Specific Methods

We fine-tune ViTs after common hyper-parameters are locked after pre-tuning. For all methods, we take 10 PGD steps on CIFAR-10/100 and 7 steps for ImageNet during the training. We report the best results for each method after trying different sets of hyper-parameters. This usually involves method-specific parameters. We elaborate what hyper-paramter is tuned as follows and report the final values used in the experiments in Table

(CIFAR-10/100) and

•

base

•

SWA

Gowal2021ImprovingRU

Appendix F
ViT Architecture

We describe the architectures of Vision Transformers used in our experiments in Section
4
.

•

ViT-B16 includes 12 transformer layers with hidden size 768, MLP layer size 3072 and 12 heads in the multi-head attention.

•

ViT-L16 includes 24 transformer layers with hidden size 1024, MLP layer size 4096 and 16 heads in the multi-head attention.

•

Hybird-L16 is a hybrid model of a ResNet-50 and a ViT-L16 model. The patches fed into ViT are feature representations encoded by a ResNet-50 and projected to the Transformer dimensions
dosovitskiy2021an
. Dissimilar to a standard ResNet-50 feature encoder, Dosovitskiy et al.
dosovitskiy2021an
replaced Batch Normalization with Group Normalization
Wu2019GroupN
and use standardized Convolution
qiao2019micro
. Moreover, Dosovitskiy et al.
dosovitskiy2021an
removed stage 4, placed the same number of layers in stage 3 (keeping the total number of layers), and took the output of this extended stage 3 as the input of ViT.

The pretrained ViT-B16 and ViT-L16 checkpoints use patch size
16\times 16
. However, since the images in CIFAR10/100 are of size
32\times 32
, there will be only 4 patches when using the original patch size. Therefore, we downsample the kernel of the first convolution to produce image patches of
4\times 4
. Among different ways of downsampling we find the
cubic
interpolation gives the best results, which is the one chosen in pre-tuning as discussed in Appendix
C
.
Indeed, Mahmood et al.
Mahmood_2021_ICCV
empirically finds that such down-sampling provides better results for CIFAR10 images. Furthermore, in generating patches, we also use a stride of 2 instead of 4, because we find using overlapped patches increases the sequence length and results in better performance.

Appendix G
TrH Regularization for Other Adversarial Losses

In this section, we apply TrH regularization to some additional robust losses other than AT or TRADES.

G.1
ALP
Kannan2018AdversarialLP

Similar to TRADES, Adversarial Logit Pairing (ALP)
Kannan2018AdversarialLP
is another method that pushes points away from the decision boundary by regularizing the
\ell_{2}
difference between the clean and the adversarial softmax outputs. Formally, ALP minimizes the following loss during training,

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{A}}(\theta,D^{m})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\mathrm{CE}((x^{\prime},y),g_{\theta})+\lambda_{A}||s(g_{\theta}(x))-s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))||^{2}_{2}

\displaystyle\text{ where }x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=x+\arg\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{CE}((x+\epsilon,y),g_{\theta}).

We hereby derive TrH regularization for
\hat{R}_{\texttt{A}}(\theta,D^{m})
. Using Proposition
1
, we know that

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}(\mathrm{CE}((x^{\prime},y),g_{\theta}))=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta).

The rest of this section will focus on the
\ell_{2}
distance loss,

\displaystyle S\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}||s(g_{\theta}(x))-s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))||^{2}_{2}=\sum_{i}(s(g_{i})-s(g^{\prime}_{i}))^{2},

(27)

where
g=g_{\theta}(x),g^{\prime}=g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})
. To compute
\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}S)
, we need to re-use Lemma
1
and
2
to obtain the derivatives of the softmax and log-softmax outputs, i.e.

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s(g_{\theta}(x))}{g_{\theta}(x)}

\displaystyle=\Phi\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}diag(s(g_{\theta}(x)))-s(g_{\theta}(x))\cdot s(g_{\theta}(x))^{\top}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\;\;\frac{\partial\log s(g_{\theta}(x))}{g_{\theta}(x)}

\displaystyle=\Psi\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}I-\mathbf{1}\cdot s(g_{\theta}(x))^{\top},

as well as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x)\}_{k}}=\Phi_{kk}\Psi_{ki}.

For the ease of notation, we let
w\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\theta_{t}
be the weights of the top-layer. Now we are ready to write the first-order derivative of
S
w.r.t
w_{jk}
as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial S}{\partial w_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{i}2(s(g_{i})-s(g^{\prime}_{i}))\left[\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial w_{jk}}-\frac{\partial s(g^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial w_{jk}}\right]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i}(s(g_{i})-s(g^{\prime}_{i}))(\Phi_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}-\Phi^{\prime}_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}).

The second-order derivative is equal to

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial w^{2}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i}\left[\frac{\partial s(g_{i})}{\partial w_{jk}}-\frac{\partial s(g^{\prime}_{i})}{\partial w_{jk}}\right](\Phi_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}-\Phi^{\prime}_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j})+(s(g_{i})-s(g^{\prime}_{i}))\left[\frac{\partial\Phi_{ik}}{\partial w_{jk}}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}-\frac{\partial\Phi^{\prime}_{ik}}{\partial w_{jk}}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}\right]

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i}(\Phi_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}_{j}-\Phi^{\prime}_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j})^{2}+(s(g_{i})-s(g^{\prime}_{i}))(\Phi_{kk}\Psi_{ki}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x)\}^{2}_{j}-\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}\Psi^{\prime}_{ki}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}).

(28)

Similar to TRADES, if one stops gradients over the clean logit
g
, then Eq.
28
can be simplified as,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}S}{\partial w^{2}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{i}(\Phi^{\prime}_{ik}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j})^{2}+(s(g_{i})-s(g^{\prime}_{i}))(-\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}\Psi^{\prime}_{ki}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j})

\displaystyle=2\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}\Big(||\Phi_{k}||^{2}_{2}-\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}((s(g)-s(g^{\prime}))^{\top}\Psi^{\prime}_{k})\Big).

Therefore, the trace of Hessian is equal to,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}S)

\displaystyle=\sum_{jk}2\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}\Big(||\Phi_{k}||^{2}_{2}-\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}((s(g)-s(g^{\prime}))^{\top}\Psi^{\prime}_{k})

\displaystyle=2||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\sum_{k}\Big(||\Phi_{k}||^{2}_{2}-\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}((s(g)-s(g^{\prime}))^{\top}\Psi^{\prime}_{k})\Big).

If the gradient over
g
is kept, then one can sum over the feature dimension
j
and the class dimension
k
in Eq.
28
. To put together, the TrH regularization term for ALP is given as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta)+\lambda_{A}\cdot\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}S).

G.2
MART
Wang2020Improving

In
Wang2020Improving
, Wang et al. proposed MART as a robust training loss that focus more on points that are not classified correctly. Different from AT, TRADES and ALP, MART explicitly aims to increase the margin between the top prediction and the second best candidate. In what follows we provide the TrH regularization for MART. We denote the MART loss as
\hat{R}_{\texttt{M}}
, which contains two components: a boosted Cross-Entropy (BCE) loss and a weighted KL-Divergence (WKL),

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{\texttt{M}}(\theta,D^{m})\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\frac{1}{m}\sum_{(x,y)\in D^{m}}\mathrm{BCE}((x^{\prime},y),g_{\theta})+\lambda_{m}\mathrm{WKL}((x,x^{\prime}),g_{\theta}),

where

\displaystyle\mathrm{BCE}((x^{\prime},y),g_{\theta})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})+(-\log(1-\max_{\kappa\neq y}s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa})),

\displaystyle\text{ and, }\;\mathrm{WKL}((x,x^{\prime}),g_{\theta})

\displaystyle\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}\mathrm{KL}(s(g_{\theta}(x))||s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})))(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x))\}_{y}).

Here

\displaystyle x^{\prime}=x+\arg\max_{||\epsilon||_{p}\leq\delta}\mathrm{CE}((x,y),\theta).

(29)

First, we derive TrH of the BCE loss with respect to the top-layer weights
\theta_{t}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\mathrm{BCE})=\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})])+\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[-\log(1-\max_{\kappa\neq y}s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa})]).

Using intermediate steps from the proof of Proposition
2
in Appendix
A
, we have that

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[\mathrm{CE}((x,y),g_{\theta})])=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x)||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x,\theta).

In order to compute
\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}[-\log(1-\max_{\kappa\neq y}s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa})])
, we make use of Lemma
1
and
2
to obtain the derivatives of the softmax and log-softmax outputs,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))}{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\Phi^{\prime}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}diag(s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})))-s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))\cdot s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))^{\top}

\displaystyle\text{ and }\;\;\frac{\partial\log s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))}{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})}

\displaystyle=\Psi^{\prime}\overset{\text{\tiny def}}{=}I-\mathbf{1}\cdot s(g_{\theta}(x^{\prime}))^{\top},

as well as,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\Phi^{\prime}_{ik}}{\partial\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{k}}=\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}\Psi^{\prime}_{ki}.

Let us denote
K=-\log(1-\max_{\kappa\neq y}s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa})]
and
w=\theta_{t}
for the ease of notation. Then the first-order derivative of
K
w.r.t.
w_{jk}
is,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial K}{\partial w_{jk}}=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}})}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j},

where

\displaystyle\kappa^{*}=\arg\max_{\kappa\neq y}s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa}).

The second-order derivative is,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial w^{2}_{jk}}

\displaystyle=\frac{\frac{\partial\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{\partial w_{jk}}(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))+\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}}{(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))^{2}}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{kk}\Psi^{\prime}_{k\kappa^{*}}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{k}(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))+\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}}{(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))^{2}}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}_{j}

\displaystyle=\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}\left[\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}})}+\frac{\Phi^{{}^{\prime}2}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))^{2}}\right].

Thus,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\frac{\partial^{2}K}{\partial w^{2}_{jk}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{jk}\{f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})\}^{2}_{j}\left[\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}})}+\frac{\Phi^{{}^{\prime}2}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\sum_{k}\left[\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}})}+\frac{\Phi^{{}^{\prime}2}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))^{2}}\right],

and

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\mathrm{BCE})=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x)||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x,\theta)+||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\sum_{k}\left[\frac{\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}\Phi^{\prime}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}})}+\frac{\Phi^{{}^{\prime}2}_{\kappa^{*}k}}{(1-s(\{g_{\theta}(x^{\prime})\}_{\kappa^{*}}))^{2}}\right].

Next, we derive the trace of Hessian for WKL loss. Similarly to before, by stopping the gradient over the clean logit
g_{\theta}(x)
, the trace of Hessian of WKL will be the same as the KL loss used in TRADES, which has been shown in Proposition
2
.
Namely, in this case

\displaystyle\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\mathrm{WKL})=||f_{\theta_{b}}(x^{\prime})||^{2}_{2}\cdot\mathbf{1}^{\top}h(x^{\prime},\theta).

On the other hand, if the gradient on
g_{\theta}(x)
is computed, there is an additional term in
\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\mathrm{WKL})
(similar but more complicated than
G(x,x^{\prime};\theta)
derived in the proof of Proposition
2
in Appendix
A
), which we will leave as future work. Finally, we present the TrH regularization for MART as follows,

\displaystyle\mathrm{TrH}_{\texttt{M}}(x,x^{\prime};\lambda_{m},\theta)=\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\mathrm{BCE})+\lambda_{m}\mathrm{Tr}(\triangledown^{2}_{\theta_{t}}\mathrm{WKL}).

This concludes our derivations of the TrH regularization for the MART loss.
