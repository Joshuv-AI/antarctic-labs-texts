Superscreening by a Retroreflected Hole Backflow in Tomographic Electron Fluids
Qiantan Hong 1 , Margarita Davydova 1 , Patrick J Ledwith 2 , Leonid Levitov

Abstract
Electron hydrodynamics gives rise to surprising correlated behaviors in which electrons “cooperate” to quench dissipation and reduce the electric fields needed to sustain the flow. Such collective “free” flows are usually expected at the hydrodynamic lengthscales exceeding the electron-electron scattering mean free path ℓ ee \ell_{\rm ee} . Here we predict that in two-dimensional electron gases the collective free flows actually occur at the distances much smaller than ℓ ee \ell_{\rm ee} , in a nominally ballistic regime. The sub- ℓ ee \ell_{\rm ee} free flows arise due to retroreflected holes originating from head-on quasiparticle collisions; the holes retrace the paths of impinging electrons and cancel out their potential. An exact solution, obtained in Corbino geometry, predicts potential strongly screened by the hole backflow. Screened potential is described by a fractional power law r − 5 / 3 r^{-5/3} over a wide range of r r values, from macroscales down to deep sub- ℓ ee \ell_{\rm ee} scales, and a distinct non-Fermi-liquid temperature dependence.

Here we argue that the tomographic behavior also dominates at ultrashort distances

,

r\sim\xi_{\rm b}\ll\ell_{\rm ee},\quad\xi_{\rm b}/\ell_{\rm ee}\sim\sqrt{T/T_{F}}\ll 1.

This peculiar behavior can be understood as a nonclassical quasi-ballistic dynamics of compound objects, the particle-hole pairs composed of particles and holes propagating opposite to each other, as illustrated in Fig.
1

Such restructuring of the flow leads to a dramatic reduction (superscreening) of current-induced fields and of Joule dissipation

The extinction of electric fields due to hole counterflow resembles some aspects of Andreev hole retroreflection in superconducting NS systems. In contrast, the behavior considered here is neither a low-temperature nor a phase-coherent phenomenon, which superconductivity is. In electron fluids it arises at elevated temperatures, becoming prominent at the temperatures for which electron-electron collisions dominate over other collision types. Still, in strong resemblance to Andreev transport, superscreening arises from retroreflected holes which retrace the ballistic paths of impinging electrons.

becomes larger), the second (subleading) term in Eq.(
25
) cannot be
neglected anymore, producing a change in the apparent scaling exponent values,
\Gamma(k)\propto k^{\alpha}
and
\phi(r)\propto r^{-2+\alpha}
,
\alpha>1/3
.

Conveniently, the crossover to the ballistic dependence occurs at abnormally short sub-
\ell_{\rm ee}
distances, where the
r^{-5/3}
potential is strong, lending tomographic behavior amenable to state-of-the-art scanning probe techniques
baem2018
;
sulpizio2019
;
ku2019
;
jenkins2020
.
Besides the short lengthscales, detection of tomographic transport is facilitated by the unique temperature dependence. Indeed, the e-e collision rate responsible for the backflow of holes will grow with temperature and, accordingly, potential in the system will diminish; the temeprature-induced suppression will occur over a wide range of length scales, including sub-
\ell_{\rm ee}
length scales. Simultaneously the lengthscale
\xi_{\rm b}
where the tomographic behavior sets in will become shorter as temperature grows, providing clear a experimental signature of superscreening.

Appendix A
Continued fractions formalism for a point source

A.1
Transport equation

To model the behavior of space charge in the presence of quasiparticle scattering, we will assume a weak deviation from equilibrium and use the linearized transport equation with the collision term
I_{\rm ee}
describing two-body scattering:

(\partial_{t}+{\boldsymbol{v}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}_{r}-I_{\rm ee})\delta f({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}})+e{\boldsymbol{E}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}}{\partial\epsilon}=s_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}.

(26)

The source term
s_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}=s_{0}\delta({\boldsymbol{r}})(-\partial f_{\rm eq}/\partial\epsilon)
represents an injector, here without loss of generality placed at the origin. The electric field is due to the space charge induced by the flow,
{\boldsymbol{E}}({\boldsymbol{r}})=-{\boldsymbol{\nabla}}\delta\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})
, with the space-charge potential

\delta\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})=\int d^{2}r^{\prime}\frac{e\delta n({\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime})}{\kappa|{\boldsymbol{r}}-{\boldsymbol{r}}^{\prime}|},\quad\delta n({\boldsymbol{r}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}\delta f({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}}),

(27)

where
\kappa
is the effective dielectric constant.
The solution of this problem will describe restructuring of the flow, ballistic near the source and tomographic at larger distances (Fig.
1
). It will also account for the non-local field-charge response, Eq.
27
, through the change in
{\boldsymbol{E}}
due to a current-induced deviation in the carrier distribution from equilibrium, as well as the Thomas-Fermi screening.

It will be convenient to transform our problem to an auxiliary easier-to-solve problem for a fictitious free-particle distribution
\tilde{f}({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}})
obtained by selfconsistently shifting the local chemical potential by an amount that depends on the local carrier depletion. The new problem, described by Eq.(
26
) with
{\boldsymbol{E}}=0
, will then be solved in a closed form in the Fourier representation.

We first rewrite Eq.(
26
) by taking the perturbed distribution and its potential to be a plane wave

\delta f({\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}})=\delta f_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{p}})e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{r}}-i\omega t},\quad\delta\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})=\delta\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{r}}-i\omega t},

(28)

to obtain

(-i\omega+i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}-I_{\rm ee})\delta f_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{p}})-i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}e\delta\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}}{\partial\epsilon}=s_{{\boldsymbol{p}},{\boldsymbol{r}}}

(29)

where
e\delta\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=U(k)\delta n_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}
are harmonics of the current-induced space-charge potential, with
U(k)=\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{\kappa k}
the 2D Coulomb potential formfactor.

In the regime of interest,
T\ll E_{F}
, the perturbed distribution
\delta f
is concentrated near the Fermi level and can be represented by angular harmonics describing the Fermi surface modulation evolving in space and time,

\delta f_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{p}})=-\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}(p)}{\partial\epsilon}\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}\delta f_{m}e^{im\theta}

(30)

where
\theta
is the angle parameter on the Fermi surface. For conciseness, we will suppress the dependence of the harmonics
\delta f_{m}
on the wavenumber
{\boldsymbol{k}}
, Eq.(
28
), restoring it at the end.
The factorization into the radial and angular dependence described by
-\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}(p)}{\partial\epsilon}
and the sum of harmonics
\delta f_{m}e^{im\theta}
, respectively, is an approximation that captures the behavior of the low-lying excitations in a Fermi gas at
T\ll E_{F}
.

Because of the cylindrical symmetry, the collision operator is diagonal in the
e^{im\theta}
basis,

I_{\rm ee}e^{im\theta}=-\gamma_{m}e^{im\theta},

(31)

with the eigenvalues
\gamma_{m}

describing the relaxation rates for different angular harmonics of the perturbed distribution.
Different values
\gamma_{m}
account for different scattering processes in the system. Here we analyze the two-rate model
ledwith2019a
;
ledwith2019b
in which the odd-
m
rates
\gamma_{m}
are much smaller than the even-
m
rates at small enough
m
, and scale as
m^{4}
. As
m
grows the odd-
m
and even-
m
rates eventually become equal. The dependence
\gamma_{m}
vs.
m
can be described as

\gamma_{m\,{\rm even}}=\gamma,\quad\gamma_{m\,{\rm odd}}=\frac{1}{\frac{1}{\gamma^{\prime}m^{4}}+\frac{1}{\gamma}}.

(32)

The parameter values of interest at temperatures
T\ll\epsilon_{F}
correspond to
\gamma^{\prime}\sim T^{4}/\epsilon_{F}^{3}
much smaller than
\gamma\sim T^{2}/\epsilon_{F}
and, in addition,
\gamma_{m=0}=\gamma_{m=\pm 1}=0
for the zero-mode harmonics. The crossover value
m
above which the even-
m
and odd-
m
rates become approximately equal,

m=m_{\star}=(\gamma/\gamma^{\prime})^{1/4},

(33)

grows as
\sqrt{\epsilon_{F}/T}
with temperature decreasing,
T\ll\epsilon_{F}
.

A.2
Computing the response function

A transformation to an auxiliary problem for a fictitious free-particle distribution can now be achieved as follows.
We first note that the field term in Eq.(
29
) is a product of the
p
harmonic
i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{v}}
and an angle-independent function
\delta\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}}{\partial\epsilon}
that depends on the injected current. This structure can be exploited to absorb the field term into the streaming term
i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{v}}\delta f_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{p}})
by introducing an auxiliary distribution function
\delta\tilde{f}

for which the
m=0
harmonic is rescaled by the dielectric function

\epsilon_{k}=1+\nu U(k)

(34)

with
\nu
the density of states at the Fermi level, whereas other harmonics remain unchanged:

\delta\tilde{f}_{m=0}=\epsilon_{k}\delta f_{m=0},\quad\delta\tilde{f}_{m\neq 0}=\delta f_{m\neq 0}.

(35)

The new distribution
\delta\tilde{f}
obeys Eq.(
29
) with
\delta\phi_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=0
; the potential of the space charge is given in terms of
\delta\tilde{f}
by the relation

\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})=\sum_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}e^{i{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{r}}}\frac{U(k)}{\epsilon_{k}}\nu\delta\tilde{f}_{m=0}({\boldsymbol{k}}),

(36)

where the density of states arises in the usual manner by approximating the sum over the states near the Fermi level as
\sum_{{\boldsymbol{p}}}-\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}(p)}{\partial\epsilon}=\nu
.
The fictitious particle distribution
\delta{\tilde{f}}
obeys the transport equation, Eq.(
29
), in which the space charge potential is suppressed:

(-i\omega+i{\boldsymbol{k}}\cdot{\boldsymbol{v}}-I_{\rm ee})\delta\tilde{f}_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}({\boldsymbol{p}})=-s_{0}\frac{\partial f_{\rm eq}(p)}{\partial\epsilon}

(37)

A general solution of Eq.(
37
) can be given in terms of continued fractions. For that we exploit the structure of the streaming term
i{\boldsymbol{v}}{\boldsymbol{k}}\delta f
in Eq.(
37
) which, in the angular harmonics basis, represents a nearest-neighbor “hopping” that couples harmonics
m
and
m\pm 1
. This observation allows us to rewrite Eq.(
37
)
as system of coupled algebraic equations for the Fourier coefficients
\delta\tilde{f}_{m}
as follows:

(\gamma_{m}-i\omega)\delta\tilde{f}_{m}+iz\delta\tilde{f}_{m+1}+i\bar{z}\delta\tilde{f}_{m-1}=s_{0}\delta_{m,0}

(38)

Here we defined a complex parameter
z=\frac{v}{2}(k_{x}+ik_{y})
,
and used the identity

{\boldsymbol{k}}{\boldsymbol{v}}=ze^{-i\theta}+\bar{z}e^{i\theta}
.

The coupled equations in Eq.(
38
) can be solved recursively as follows.
For
m>0
we define the quantities
\alpha_{m}=i\delta\tilde{f}_{m+1}/\delta\tilde{f}_{m}
;
in terms of
\alpha_{m}
the
m>0
equations read

\gamma_{m}+z\alpha_{m}-\frac{\bar{z}}{\alpha_{m-1}}=0,

(39)

where from now on, for brevity, we suppress
i\omega
.
These relations can be transformed to a recursion relation
\alpha_{m-1}=\frac{\bar{z}}{\gamma_{m}+z\alpha_{m}}
and iterated over
m+1
,
m+2
, …, to obtain

\alpha_{m-1}=\frac{\bar{z}}{\gamma_{m}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\gamma_{m+1}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\gamma_{m+2}+...}}}

(40)

Similarly, for
m<0
we define the quantities
\beta_{m}=i\delta\tilde{f}_{m-1}/\delta\tilde{f}_{m}
; in terms of
\beta_{m}
the
m<0
equations read

\gamma_{m}-\frac{z}{\beta_{m+1}}+\bar{z}\beta_{m}=0,

(41)

In this case, expressing
\beta_{m+1}
through
\beta_{m}
as
\beta_{m+1}=\frac{z}{\gamma_{m}+\bar{z}\beta_{m}}
and iterating over
m-1
,
m-2
, …, yields

\beta_{m+1}=\frac{z}{\gamma_{m}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\gamma_{m-1}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\gamma_{m-2}+...}}}.

(42)

We can now find the harmonic
\delta\tilde{f}_{0}
from the
m=0
equation in which we set
\gamma_{0}=0
,

iz\delta\tilde{f}_{1}+i\bar{z}\delta\tilde{f}_{-1}=s_{0}.

(43)

Writing
\delta\tilde{f}_{1}=-i\delta\tilde{f}_{0}\alpha_{0}
and
\delta\tilde{f}_{-1}=-i\delta\tilde{f}_{0}\beta_{0}
,
substituting the continued fraction representation for
\alpha_{0}
and
\beta_{0}
and setting
\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{-1}=0
, yields a relation for the zeroth harmonic which describes the space charge density:

\delta\tilde{f}_{0}=\frac{s_{0}}{2\Gamma(k)},\quad\Gamma(k)=\gamma_{2}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\gamma_{3}+\frac{|z|^{2}}{\gamma_{4}+...}}

(44)

The continued fraction
\Gamma(k)
is well behaved at
\omega=0
, since the quantities
\gamma_{m}
are finite and positive at large
m
. The
\omega
dependence, which can be obtained by an analytic continuation from small
\omega
values, will be discussed elsewhere.

Using the relation between the physical and fictitious
m=0
harmonics,
\delta f_{0}=\frac{1}{\epsilon_{k}}\delta\tilde{f}_{0}=\frac{1}{1+\nu U(k)}\delta\tilde{f}_{0}
,
U(k)=\frac{2\pi e^{2}}{\kappa k}
, we can write a closed-form expression for the harmonics of the potential

\displaystyle\phi(k)=\frac{\nu U(k)}{1+\nu U(k)}\delta\tilde{f}_{0}

(45)

Combining this relation with the above result for
\delta\tilde{f}_{0}
and linking the source term value to the injected current,

s_{0}=\frac{I}{e\nu},

(46)

yields the representation of the current-induced potential in terms of the continued fraction
\Gamma(k)
, Eqs.(
3
),(
2
), which is exploited in the main text.

In agreement with the charge neutrality requirement,
this nonlocal relation turns into a local relation at distances larger than the Thomas-Fermi screening length,
r>\lambda_{\rm TF}=\kappa/2\pi e^{2}\nu
, giving

\phi({\boldsymbol{r}})=\delta\tilde{f}_{m=0}({\boldsymbol{r}}).

(47)

This transformation, which replaces the actual distribution
f
with a fictitious distribution
\tilde{f}
obeing the free-particle problem, provides a general recipe to analyze the space charge buildup induced by currents in a nonequilibrium system.
Indeed, the net space charge density can be expressed through the
m=0
harmonic as

\delta n_{{\boldsymbol{k}}}=\nu e\delta f_{m=0}=\frac{\nu e}{\epsilon_{k}}\delta\tilde{f}_{m=0}.

(48)

This relation between the actual current-induced density change and the fictitious free-particle density buildup can be viewed as an extension of the Thomas-Fermi mean-field screening theory to a non-equilibrium transport problem; as such it is valid at first order in current.

5\times 10^{-8}

\ell_{ee}
. The infinite continued fraction is computed by setting a large threshold value of
m
after which
\gamma_{m\,{\rm odd}}=\gamma_{m\,{\rm even}}=\gamma
