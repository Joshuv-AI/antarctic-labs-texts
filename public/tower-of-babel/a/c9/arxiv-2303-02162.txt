T-Cell Receptor Optimization with Reinforcement Learning and Mutation Polices for Precision Immunotherapy
Ziqi Chen, Martin Renqiang Min ( ✉ ) {}^{(\text{{\char 0\relax}})}, Hongyu Guo, Chao Cheng, Trevor Clancy, Xia Ning ( ✉ ) {}^{(\text{{\char 0\relax}})}

Abstract
T cells monitor the health status of cells by identifying foreign peptides displayed on their surface. T-cell receptors (TCRs), which are protein complexes found on the surface of T cells, are able to bind to these peptides. This process is known as TCR recognition and constitutes a key step for immune response. Optimizing TCR sequences for TCR recognition represents a fundamental step towards the development of personalized treatments to trigger immune responses killing cancerous or virus-infected cells. In this paper, we formulated the search for these optimized TCRs as a reinforcement learning ( 𝚁𝙻 \mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits ) problem, and presented a framework 𝚃𝙲𝚁𝙿𝙿𝙾 \mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits with a mutation policy using proximal policy optimization. 𝚃𝙲𝚁𝙿𝙿𝙾 \mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits mutates TCRs into effective ones that can recognize given peptides. 𝚃𝙲𝚁𝙿𝙿𝙾 \mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits leverages a reward function that combines the likelihoods of mutated sequences being valid TCRs measured by a new scoring function based on deep autoencoders, with the probabilities of mutated sequences recognizing peptides from a peptide-TCR interaction predictor. We compared 𝚃𝙲𝚁𝙿𝙿𝙾 \mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits with multiple baseline methods and demonstrated that 𝚃𝙲𝚁𝙿𝙿𝙾 \mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits significantly outperforms all the baseline methods to generate positive binding and valid TCRs. These results demonstrate the potential of 𝚃𝙲𝚁𝙿𝙿𝙾 \mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits for both precision immunotherapy and peptide-recognizing TCR motif discovery.

Introduction

Immunotherapy is a fundamental treatment for human diseases, which uses a person’s immune system to fight diseases
[
Verdegaal2016
,
Esfahani2020
,
Waldman2020
]
.
In the immune system,
immune response is triggered by cytotoxic T cells which are activated by the engagement of
the T cell receptors (TCRs) with immunogenic peptides
presented by Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) proteins on the surface of infected or cancerous cells.
The recognition of these foreign peptides is determined by the interactions between the peptides and
TCRs on the surface of T cells.
This process is known as TCR recognition and constitutes a key step for immune response
[
Craiu1997
,
glanville2017identifying
]
.
Adoptive T cell immunotherapy (ACT), which has been a promising cancer treatment, genetically modifies the autologous T cells taken from patients in laboratory experiments, after which the modified T cells are infused into patients’ bodies to fight cancer.
As one type of ACT therapies, TCR T cell (TCR-T) therapy directly modifies the TCRs of T cells to increase the binding affinities, which makes it possible to recognize and kill tumor cells effectively
[
NatureSurvey2017
]
.
TCR is a heterodimeric protein with an
\alpha
chain and a
\beta
chain.
Each chain has three loops as complementary determining regions (CDR): CDR1, CDR2 and CDR3. CDR1 and CDR2 are primarily responsible for interactions with MHC, and CDR3 interacts with peptides
[
rossjohn2015t
]
.
The CDR3 of the
\beta
chain has a higher degree of variations and is therefore arguably mainly responsible for the recognition of foreign peptides
[
la2018understanding
]
.
In this paper, we focused on the optimization of the CDR3 sequence of
\beta
chain in TCRs to enhance their binding affinities against
peptide antigens, and we conducted the optimization through novel reinforcement learning.
The success of our approach will have the potential to guide TCR-T therapy design.
For the sake of simplicity, when we refer to TCRs in the rest of the paper, we mean the CDR3 of
\beta
chain in TCRs.

Despite the significant promise of TCR-T therapy, optimizing TCRs for therapeutic purposes remains a time-consuming process,
which typically requires exhaustive screening for high-affinity TCRs, either
in vitro
or
in silico
.
To accelerate this process, computational methods have been developed recently to predict peptide-TCR interactions
[
Springer2020
]
,
leveraging the experimental peptide-TCR binding data
[
Shugay2017
,
Tickotsky2017
]
and TCR sequences
[
tcrdb2020
]
.
However, these peptide-TCR binding prediction tools cannot immediately direct the rational design of new high-affinity TCRs.
Existing computational methods for biological sequence design include search-based methods
[
Arnold1998
]
, generative methods
[
Killoran2017
,
Gupta2019
]
,
optimization-based methods
[
Gonzalez2015
]
and reinforcement learning (
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
)-based methods
[
angerm2020
,
marcin2020
]
.
However, all these methods generate sequences without considering additional conditions such as peptides,
and thus cannot optimize TCRs tailored to recognizing different
peptides.
In addition, these methods do not consider the validity of generated sequences, which is important for TCR optimization
as valid TCRs should follow specific characteristics
[
Hou2016
]
.

In this paper, we presented a new reinforcement-learning (
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
) framework based on proximal policy optimization (PPO)
[
schulman17ppo
]
, referred to as
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits

1
1

1

The code is available at https://github.com/ninglab/TCRPPO
, to computationally optimize
TCRs through a mutation policy.
In particular,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
learns a joint policy to optimize TCRs customized for any given peptides.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
, we designed a new reward function that measures both the likelihoods of the mutated sequences being valid TCRs,
and the probabilities of the TCRs recognizing peptides.
To measure TCR validity, we developed a TCR auto-encoder, referred to as
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
, and utilized reconstruction errors from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
and also its latent space distributions, quantified by a Gaussian Mixture Model, to calculate novel validity scores.
To measure peptide recognition, we leveraged a state-of-the-art peptide-TCR binding predictor
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits

[
Springer2020
]

to predict peptide-TCR binding.
Please note that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is a flexible framework, as
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
can be replaced by any other
binding predictors
[
Cai2022
,
Weber2021
]
.
In addition, we designed a novel buffering mechanism,
referred to as
\mathop{\mathtt{Buf\text{-}Opt}}\limits
,
to revise TCRs that are difficult to optimize.
We conducted extensive experiments using
7 million TCRs from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits

[
tcrdb2020
]
, 10 peptides from
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits

[
Tickotsky2017
]
and 15 peptides from
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits

[
Shugay2017
]
.
Our experimental results demonstrated that

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
can substantially outperform the best baselines with best improvement of 45.04% and 52.89%
in terms of generating qualified TCRs with high validity scores and high recognition probabilities, over
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
peptides, respectively.

2
Related Work

Existing methods developed for biological sequence design include search-based methods,
deep generative methods, optimization-based methods and
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
-based methods.
Among search-based methods, the classical evolutionary search
[
Arnold1998
]
uses an evolution strategy to
randomly mutate the sequences and select desired ones in an iterative way.
Among generative methods, Killoran
et al.

[
Killoran2017
]

optimized the latent embeddings of DNA sequences learned from a variational autoencoder towards better properties.
Gupta
et al.

[
Gupta2019
]
used generative adversarial networks (GANs) to generate DNA sequences and selected the generated ones
with desired properties to further optimize GANs.
Among optimization-based methods, Gonzalez
et al.

[
Gonzalez2015
]
used a Gaussian process model
to emulate the production rates of a certain protein across different gene designs in living cells, and then optimized the
gene designs to improve the production rates using Bayesian optimization.
Both the above generative methods and optimization methods aim at optimizing the biological sequences
without any additional conditions,
As a consequence, these methods are not applicable to our TCR optimization problem.
In our problem, the optimization of TCRs must be tailored to given peptides,
because TCRs binding to different peptides have different characteristics.

Despite the success of
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
on many applications, there remains limited work of
applying
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
to biological sequence design.
Angermueller
et al.

[
angerm2020
]
developed a model-based
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
method for biological sequence design using PPO to improve the sample efficiency,
where the policy is trained over a simulator model learned to approximate the reward function.
Skwark
et al.

[
marcin2020
]
then leveraged a
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
method based on PPO to discover a potential Covid-19 cure.
Their method aims at identifying the variants of human angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE2) protein sequence that have higher binding affinities
against the SARS-CoV-2 spike protein than the original ACE2 protein. Our
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
also applies PPO with a mutation policy to optimize TCR sequences.
However,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is fundamentally different from previous methods in three aspects.
First,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
learns a joint policy to optimize the TCRs customized for any given peptides.
In addition,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
employs a comprehensive reward function to simultaneously optimize the validity and
the recognition probability of the TCRs against the peptides.

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
also leverages a buffering mechanism to generalize the optimization capability of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
to TCRs that are hard to optimize,
or peptides with fewer positive binding TCRs.

3.1
Problem Definition

In this paper, the recognition ability of a TCR sequence against the given peptides is measured by a recognition probability, denoted as
\mathop{s_{r}}
.
The likelihood of a sequence being a valid TCR is measured by a score, denoted as
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
.
A
qualified
TCR is defined as a sequence with
\mbox{$\mathop{s_{r}}$}>\sigma_{\text{r}}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{s_{v}}\limits$}>\sigma_{c}
,
where
\sigma_{r}
and
\sigma_{c}

are pre-defined thresholds (
\sigma_{r}
=0.9 and
\sigma_{c}
=1.2577, as discussed in Appendix
A.2
and

A.4.4
, respectively).
The goal of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is to mutate the existing TCR sequences that have low recognition probability against the given peptide, into
qualified ones.
A peptide
\mathop{p}
or a TCR sequence
\mathop{c}
is represented as a sequence of its amino acids

\langle\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{1},\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{2},\cdots,\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i},\cdots,\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{l}\rangle
,
where
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i}
is one of the 20 types of natural amino acids at the position
i
in the sequence,
and
l
is the sequence length.
We formulated the TCR mutation process as a Markov Decision Process (MDP)
M=\{\mbox{$\mathcal{S}$},\mbox{$\mathcal{A}$},P,\mathcal{R}\}

containing the following components:

•

\mathcal{S}
: the state space, in which each state
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{S}$}
is a tuple of
a potential TCR sequence
\mathop{c}
and a peptide
\mathop{p}
, that is,
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}=(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
. Subscript
t
(
t=0,\cdots,T
) is used to index step of

\mathop{s}\limits
, that is,
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}=(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
.
Please note that
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
may not be a valid TCR.
A state
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}
is a terminal state, denoted as
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{T}
, if it contains a qualified
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
, or
t
reaches the maximum step limit
T
.
Please also note that
\mathop{p}
will be sampled at
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{0}
and will not change over time
t
,

•

\mathcal{A}
: the action space, in which each action
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}\in\mbox{$\mathcal{A}$}
is a tuple of a mutation site
i
and a mutant amino acid
\mathop{o}
, that is,
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}=(i,\mbox{$\mathop{o}$})
.
Thus, the action will mutate the amino acid at position
i
of a sequence
\mathop{c}
=
\langle\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{1},\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{2},\cdots,\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i},\cdots,\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{l}\rangle
into another amino acid
\mathop{o}
.
Note that
\mathop{o}
has to be different from
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i}
in
\mathop{c}
.

•

\mathcal{P}
: the state transition probabilities, in which
\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t+1}|\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t})
specifies
the probability of next state
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t+1}
at time
t+1
from state
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}
at time
t

with the action
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t}
.
In our problem, the transition to
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t+1}
is deterministic, that is
\mbox{$\mathcal{P}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t+1}|\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t})=1
.

•

\mathcal{R}
: the reward function at a state. In
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
, all the intermediate rewards at states
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}
(
t=0,\cdots,T-1
)
are 0; only the final reward at
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{T}
is used to guide the optimization.

3.2
Mutation Policy Network

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
mutates one amino acid in a sequence
\mathop{c}
at a step to modify
\mathop{c}
into a qualified TCR.
Specifically, at the initial step
t=0
, a peptide
\mathop{p}
is sampled as the target, and a valid TCR

\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{0}
is sampled to initialize
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{0}=(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{0},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
;
at a state
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}=(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
(
t>0
), the mutation policy network of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
predicts an action
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t}

that mutates one amino acid of
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
to modify it into
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t+1}
that is more likely to lead to a final, qualified TCR bound to
\mathop{p}
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
encodes the TCRs and peptides in a distributed embedding space.
It then learns
a mapping between the embedding space and the mutation policy, as discussed below.

Encoding of Amino Acids

Following the idea in Chen
et al.

[
Chen2021
]
,
we represented each amino acid
\mathop{o}
by concatenating three vectors: 1)
\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}^{b}
, the corresponding
row of
\mathop{o}
in
the BLOSUM matrix, 2)
\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}^{o}
, the one-hot encoding of
\mathop{o}
, and 3)
\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}^{d}
, the learnable embedding,
that is,
\mathop{o}
is encoded as
\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}=\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}^{b}\oplus\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}^{o}\oplus\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}^{d}
,
where
\oplus
represents the concatenation operation.
We used such a mixture of encoding methods to enrich the representations of amino acids within
\mathop{c}
and
\mathop{p}
.

Embedding of States

We embedded
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}=(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
via embedding its associated sequences
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
and
\mathop{p}
.
For each amino acid
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i,t}
in
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
,
we embedded
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i,t}
and its context information in
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
into a hidden vector
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{i,t}

using a one-layer bidirectional LSTM
[
Graves2005
]
as below,

\displaystyle\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i,t},\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\!\!\text{LSTM}(\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}_{i,t},\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i-1,t},\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{i-1,t};\overrightarrow{W});

(1)

\displaystyle\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i,t},\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\!\!\text{LSTM}(\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}_{i,t},\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i+1,t},\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{i+1,t};\overleftarrow{W});

\displaystyle\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{i,t}

\displaystyle=\!\!\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i,t}\oplus\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i,t}

where
\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i,t}
and
\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i,t}
are the hidden state vectors of the
i
-th amino acid in
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
;

\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{i,t}
and
\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{i,t}
are the memory cell states of
i
-th amino acid;

\overrightarrow{W}
and
\overleftarrow{W}
are the learnable parameters of the two LSTM directions, respectively; and

\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{0,t}
,
\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{l_{c},t}
,
\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{0,t}
and
\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}}_{l_{c},t}

(
l_{c}
is the length of
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
) are initialized with random vectors.
With the embeddings of all the amino acids, we defined the embedding of
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
as the concatenation of hidden vectors at the two ends, that is,

\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{t}=\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{l_{c},t}\oplus\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{0,t}
.
We embedded a peptide sequence into a hidden vector
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}}
using another bidirectional LSTM in the same way.

Action Prediction

To predict the action
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t}=(i,\mbox{$\mathop{o}$})
at time
t
,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
needs to make two predictions: 1) the position
i
of current

\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
where
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t}
needs to occur; 2) the new amino acid
\mathop{o}
that
\mbox{$\mathbf{a}$}_{t}
needs to place with at position
i
.
To measure “how likely" the position
i
in
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
is the action site,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
uses the following network:

f(i)=\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}(\text{ReLU}(W_{1}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{i,t}+W_{2}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}}))/(\sum\nolimits_{j=1}^{l_{c}}\mathbf{w}^{\mathsf{T}}(\text{ReLU}(W_{1}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{j,t}+W_{2}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}}))),

(2)

where
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{i,t}
is the latent vector of
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i,t}
in
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
(Equation
1
);

\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}}
is the latent vector of
\mathop{p}
;

\mathbf{w}
/
W_{j}
(
j
=1,2) are the learnable vector/matrices.
Thus,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
measures the probability of position
i
being the action site by
looking at its context encoded in
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{i,t}
and the peptide
\mathop{p}
.
The predicted position
i
is sampled from the probability distribution from Equation
2

to ensure necessary exploration.

Given the predicted position
i
,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
needs to predict the new amino acid that should replace
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i}
in
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
calculates the probability of each amino acid type being the new replacement as follows:

\vskip-2.0ptg(\mbox{$\mathop{o}$})=\text{softmax}(U_{1}\times\text{ReLU}(U_{2}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{i,t}+U_{3}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}})),

(3)

where
U_{j}
(
j
=1,2,3) are the learnable matrices;

\text{softmax}(\cdot)
converts a 20-dimensional vector into probabilities over the 20 amino acid types.
The replacement amino acid type is then determined by sampling from the distribution,
excluding the original type of
\mbox{$\mathop{o}$}_{i,t}
.

3.3
Potential TCR Validity Measurement

Leveraging the literature
[
zong2018deep
,
Abati2019
]
,
we designed a novel scoring function to quantitatively measure the likelihood of a given sequence
\mathop{c}
being a valid TCR
(i.e., to calculate
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
), which will be part of the reward of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
.
Specifically, we trained a novel auto-encoder
model, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
, from only valid TCRs.
We used the reconstruction accuracy of a
sequence in
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
to measure its TCR validity.
The intuition is that since
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
is trained from only valid TCRs, its encoding-decoding process will obey the “rules" of
true TCR sequences, and thus, a non-TCR sequence could not be well reproduced from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
.
However, it is still possible that a non-TCR sequence can receive a high reconstruction accuracy from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
,
if
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
learns some generic patterns shared by TCRs and non-TCRs and fails to detect irregularities,
or
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
has high model complexity
[
Pang2021
,
zong2018deep
]
.
To mitigate this, we additionally evaluated the latent space within
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
using a Gaussian Mixture Model (
\mathop{\mathtt{GMM}}\limits
), hypothesizing that
non-TCRs would deviate from the dense regions of TCRs in the latent space.

\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}

Figure
1
presents the auto-encoder
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
uses a bidirectional LSTM to encode an input sequence
\mathop{c}
into
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime}
by concatenating the last hidden vectors from the two LSTM directions (similarly as in Equation
1
).
Please note that this bidirectional LSTM is independent of the mutation policy network in
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
.

\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime}
is then mapped into a latent embedding
\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}
as follows,

\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}=W^{z}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime},\vskip-5.0pt

(4)

which will be decoded back to a sequence
\hat{\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}}
via a decoder.
The decoder has a single-directional LSTM that decodes
\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}
by generating one amino acid at a time as follows,

\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime}_{i},\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}^{\prime}_{i}=\text{LSTM}(\hat{\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}}_{{i-1}},\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime}_{i-1},\mbox{$\mathbf{c}$}^{\prime}_{i-1};W^{\prime});\ \ \hat{o}_{i}=\text{softmax}(U^{\prime}\times\text{ReLU}(U^{\prime}_{1}\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime}_{i}+U^{\prime}_{2}\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime})),\vskip-5.0pt

(5)

where
\hat{\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}}_{{i-1}}

is the encoding of the amino acid
\hat{o}_{i-1}
that is decoded from step
i-1
;

W^{\prime}
is the parameter.
The LSTM starts with a zero vector
\mbox{$\mathbf{o}$}_{0}=\mathbf{0}
and
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{0}=W^{h}\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}
.
The decoder infers the next amino acid
by looking at the previously decoded amino acids encoded in
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\prime}_{i}
and
the entire prospective sequence encoded in
\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}
.

Please note that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
is trained from TCRs, independently of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
and in an end-to-end fashion.
Teacher forcing
[
Williams1989
]
is applied during training to feed the ground truth amino acids as inputs to predict the next amino acid, and thus cross entropy loss is applied on each amino acid to optimize
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
.
As a stand-alone module,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
is used to calculate the score
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
.
The input sequence
\mathop{c}
to
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
is encoded using only BLOSUM matrix as
we found empirically that BLOSUM encoding can lead to a good reconstruction performance and a fast convergence compared to
other combinations of encoding methods.

Reconstruction-based score

With a well-trained
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
, we calculated the reconstruction-based TCR validity score of a sequence
\mathop{c}
as follows,

\mbox{$\mathop{r_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})=1-{\mathtt{lev}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$},\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}))}/{l_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}}}}\vskip-5.0pt

(6)

where
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
represents the reconstructed sequence of
\mathop{c}
from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
;

\mathtt{lev}(.)
is the Levenshtein distance, an edit-distance-based metric, between

\mathop{c}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
;
l_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}}}
is the length of
\mathop{c}
.
Higher
\mbox{$\mathop{r_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
indicates higher probability of
\mathop{c}
being a valid TCR.
Please note that when
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
is used in testing, the length of the reconstructed sequence might not be the same
as the input
\mathop{c}
, because

\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
could fail to accurately predict the end of the sequence, leading to either too short or too long reconstructed sequences.
Therefore, we normalized the Levenshtein distance using the length of input sequence
l_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}}}
similarly to Snover
et al.

[
Snover2006
]
.
Please note that
\mbox{$\mathop{r_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
could be negative when the distance is greater than the sequence length.
The negative values will not affect the use of the scores (i.e., negative
\mbox{$\mathop{r_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
indicates very different

\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
and
\mathop{c}
).

Density Estimation-based Score

To better distinguish valid TCRs from invalid ones,

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
also conducts a density estimation over the latent space of
\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}
(Equation
4
)
using
\mathop{\mathtt{GMM}}\limits
. For a given sequence
\mathop{c}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
calculates the likelihood score of
\mathop{c}
falling within
the Gaussian mixture region of training TCRs as follows,

\mbox{$\mathop{r_{d}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})=\exp(1+\frac{\log P(\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime})}{\tau})\vskip-5.0pt

(7)

where
\log P(\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime})
is the log-likelihood of the latent embedding

\mbox{$\mathbf{z}$}^{\prime}
;
\tau
is a constant used to rescale the log-likelihood value (
\tau=10
).
We carefully selected the parameter
\tau
such that 90% of TCRs can have
\mbox{$\mathop{r_{d}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})
above
0.5
.
As we do not have invalid TCRs, we cannot use classification-based scaling methods such as Platt scaling
[
Platt1999
]
to
calibrate the log likelihood values to probabilities.

TCR Validity Scoring

Combining the reconstruction-based scoring and density estimation-based scoring, we developed a new
scoring method
to measure TCR validity as follows:

\mbox{$\mathop{s_{v}}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})=r_{r}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})+r_{d}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}).\vskip-5.0pt

(8)

This method is used to evaluate if a sequence is likely to be a valid TCR and is used in the reward function.

3.4
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
Learning

Final Reward

We defined the final reward for
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
based on
\mathop{s_{r}}
and
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
scores as follows,

\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})=\mbox{$\mathop{s_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})+\alpha\min(0,\mbox{$\mathop{s_{v}}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T})-\mbox{$\mathop{\sigma_{c}}\limits$})\vskip-5.0pt

(9)

where
\mbox{$\mathop{s_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
is the predicted recognition probability by
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
,

\mathop{\sigma_{c}}\limits
is a threshold that
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T}
is very likely to be a valid TCR;
and
\alpha
is the hyperparameter used to control the tradeoff between
\mathop{s_{r}}
and
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
(
\alpha=0.5
).

Policy Learning

We adopted the proximal policy optimization (PPO)
[
schulman17ppo
]

to optimize the policy network as discussed in Section
3.2
.
The objective function of PPO is defined as follows:

\displaystyle\max\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}L^{\text{CLIP}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[\min(r_{t}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})\hat{A}_{t},\text{clip}(r_{t}(\boldsymbol{\Theta}),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_{t})],

(10)

\displaystyle\text{where}\ r_{t}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\frac{\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})}{\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{old}}}}(a_{t}|s_{t})},

where
\boldsymbol{\Theta}
is the set of learnable parameters of the policy network and
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
is the probability ratio
between the action under current policy
\pi_{{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}}}
and the action under previous policy
\pi_{{\boldsymbol{\Theta}_{\text{old}}}}
.
Here,
r_{t}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
is
clipped to avoid moving
r_{t}
outside of the interval
[1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon]
.

\hat{A}_{t}
is the advantage at timestep
t
computed with the generalized advantage estimator
[
Schulmanetal2016
]
, measuring how much better a selected action is than
others on average:

\hat{A}_{t}=\delta_{t}+(\gamma\lambda)\delta_{t+1}+...+(\gamma\lambda)^{T-t+1}\delta_{T-1},

(11)

where
\gamma\in(0,1)
is the discount factor determining the importance of future rewards;

\delta_{t}=r_{t}+\gamma V(s_{t+1})-V(s_{t})
is the temporal difference error in which
V(s_{t})
is a value function;

\lambda\in(0,1)
is a parameter used to balance the bias
and variance of
V(s_{t})
.
Here,
V(\cdot)
uses a multi-layer perceptron (MLP) to predict the future return of current state
s_{t}
from the peptide embedding
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}}
and the TCR embedding
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{t}
.
The objective function of
V(\cdot)
is as follows:

\min\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}L^{V}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=\hat{\mathbb{E}}_{t}[(V(\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}})-\hat{R}_{t})^{2}],

(12)

where
\hat{R}_{t}=\sum_{i=t+1}^{T}\gamma^{i-t}r_{i}
is the rewards-to-go.
Because we only used the final rewards, that is
r_{i}=0\text{ if }i\neq T
, we calculated
\hat{R}_{t}
with
\hat{R}_{t}=\gamma^{T-t}r_{\scriptsize{T}}
.
We also added the entropy regularization loss
H(\boldsymbol{\Theta})
,
a popular strategy used for policy gradient methods
[
mniha16
,
schulman17ppo
]
, to encourage the exploration of the policy.
The final objective function of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is defined as below,

\min\nolimits_{\boldsymbol{\Theta}}L(\boldsymbol{\Theta})=-L^{\text{CLIP}}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})+\alpha_{1}L^{V}(\boldsymbol{\Theta})-\alpha_{2}H(\boldsymbol{\Theta}),\vskip-5.0pt

(13)

where
\alpha_{1}
and
\alpha_{2}
are two hyperparameters controlling the tradeoff among the PPO objective, the value function and the entropy regularization term.

Reward-Informed Buffering and Re-optimization

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
implements a novel buffering and re-optimizing mechanism, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{Buf\text{-}Opt}}\limits
,
to deal with TCRs that are difficult to optimize,
and to generalize its optimization capacity to more, diverse TCRs.
To optimize TCRs, various number of mutations will be applied to get the binding TCRs.
For TCRs requiring more mutations, it could be more difficult for
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
to optimize; and thus re-optimizing these TCRs enables
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
to explore more actions for the optimization of difficult TCRs, instead of being overwhelmed by relatively simple cases.
This mechanism includes a buffer, which memorizes the TCRs that cannot be optimized to qualify.
These hard sequences and the corresponding peptides will be sampled from the buffer again following the probability distribution below, to be further optimized by

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
,

\vskip-5.0ptS(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})=\xi^{(1-\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})})}/\Sigma.

(14)

In Equation
14
,
S
measures how difficult to optimize
\mathop{c}
against
\mathop{p}
based on its final reward
\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})

in the previous optimization,

\xi
is hyper-parameter (
\xi=5
in our experiments), and
\Sigma
converts
S(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
as a probability.
It is expected that by doing the sampling and re-optimization,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is better trained to learn from hard sequences, and also the
hard sequences have the opportunity to be better optimized by
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
.
In case a hard sequence still cannot be optimized to qualify, it will
have 50% chance of being allocated back to the buffer.
In case the buffer is full (size 2,000 in our experiments), the sequences earliest allocated in the buffer
will be removed.
We referred to the
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
with
\mathop{\mathtt{Buf\text{-}Opt}}\limits
as
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}\text{+}\mathtt{b}}\limits
.

4
Experimental Settings

4.1
Datasets

We selected peptides and TCR sequences for the training and testing of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
.
Figure
2
summaries the peptides and TCRs used in our experiments.

Peptides

To test
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
, we first identified a set of peptides that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
needs to optimize TCR sequences for. We aimed at selecting the
peptides which are very likely to have reliable peptide-TCR binding predictions,
such that their binding predictions can serve to test
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
’s optimized TCRs against the respective peptides.
We identified such peptides from two databases:
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits

[
Tickotsky2017
]
and
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits

[
Shugay2017
]
,
which have experimentally validated TCR-peptide binding pairs.
We applied the autoencoder-based
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
models
[
Springer2020
]

on
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
(
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
have pre-specified training and testing sets for each peptide),
and selected the peptides which have AUC values above 0.9 on their respective testing sets.
This resulted in 10 peptides selected from
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
, denoted as
\mathop{\mathcal{P}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits$}}}}\limits
, and 15 peptides selected from
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
, denoted as
\mathop{\mathcal{P}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits$}}}}\limits
,
with lengths ranging from 8 to 21, as presented in Appendix Table
.
Additional discussion on peptides is available in Appendix
A.1
.

TCR sequences

We then selected TCR sequences that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
needs to optimize against each of the peptides selected as above.
We selected such sequences from the
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
database
[
tcrdb2020
]
, which contains
277 million human TCR sequences, each with a
TCR-
\beta
sequence.
Here, we used
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
, not
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
or
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
, because
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
’s sequences are valid TCRs and have no information on
their binding affinities with the selected peptides.
Therefore, these valid TCRs can be used to train
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
to calculate
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
.
Meanwhile, since
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
is much larger than
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
(4,528 TCRs) and
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
(50,049 TCRs), it is very likely that
the
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
calculated from the
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
, which is trained over
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
data,
will be independent of the
\mathop{s_{r}}
calculated from
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
,
which is trained on
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
or
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
, avoiding possible correlation between
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
and
\mathop{s_{r}}
as in the reward (Equation
9
).

We selected all the TCRs with lengths below 27 (
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
can only predict sequences of length 27 or shorter) from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
,
resulting in 7,331,105 unique TCR-
\beta
sequences.
Figure
A.1
presents the distribution of lengths of TCRs in
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
.
As shown in Figure
A.1
, the most common length of TCRs is 15.
Additional discussion on the length of TCRs is available in Appendix
A.1
.
Among these selected sequences, we sampled 50K sequences, denoted
as the validation set
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{v}}\limits
, to test and validate
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
;
within these 50K sequences, we again sampled 1K sequences, denoted as the testing set
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{tst}}\limits
, to test
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
performance once it is well trained.
The remaining selected sequences (i.e., not in the validation set), denoted as the training set
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{trn}}\limits
,
are used to train
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
;
they are also used to train
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
.

4.2
Experimental Setup

For all the selected peptides from a same database (i.e., 10 peptides from
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
, 15 peptides from
\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
), we trained one
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
agent, which
optimizes the training sequences (i.e., 7,281,105 TCRs in Figure
2
) to be qualified against one of the selected peptides.
The
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
model trained on the corresponding database (the same
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
model also used to select the peptides from the database
as in Section
4.1
) will be used to test recognition probabilities
\mathop{s_{r}}
for the
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
agent.
Please note that as in Springer
et al.

[
Springer2020
]
, one
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
model is trained for all the peptides in each database
(i.e., one
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
predicts TCR-peptide binding for multiple peptides). Thus, the
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
model is suitable to test
\mathop{s_{r}}
for multiple peptides in our setting.
Also note that we trained one
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
agent corresponding to each database, because peptides and TCRs in these two databases
are very different, demonstrated by the inferior performance of an
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
trained over the two databases together, and discussed in Springer

et al.

[
Springer2020
]
.

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
mutates each sequence up to 8 steps (i.e., T=8).
In
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
training, an initial TCR sequence (i.e.,
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{0}
in
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{0}
) is randomly sampled from
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{trn}}\limits
,
and will be mutated in the following states;
a peptide
\mathop{p}
is randomly sampled at
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{0}
, and remains the same in the following states (i.e.,
\mbox{$\mathop{s}\limits$}_{t}=(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t},\mbox{$\mathop{p}$})
).
Once a
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is well trained from
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{trn}}\limits
, it will be tested on
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{tst}}\limits
.
We set the dimensions of the hidden layers (e.g., hidden layers of action prediction networks) as 256, and
the dimensions of latent embeddings (e.g.,
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{p}$}}}
,
\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}_{t}
) as 128 (i.e., half of the hidden dimensions).
Other hyper-parameters and the details of hyper-parameter selection of the TCR mutation environment, the policy network and the
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits
agent are available in Appendix
A.2
.

4.3
Baseline Methods

We compared the
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
method and
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}\text{+}\mathtt{b}}\limits
with multiple baseline methods of two primary categories:
1) generative methods that generate a new TCR in its entirety, and
2) mutation-based methods that optimize TCRs via mutating amino acids of existing TCRs.
For generative methods, we used two baseline methods including Monte Carlo tree search (
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
)
[
Coulom2007
]
and
a variational autoencoder with backpropagation (
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
)
[
GomezBombarelli2018
]
.
For mutation-based methods, we used three baseline methods to mutate each TCR sequence up to 8 steps and stop the mutation once a qualified TCR is generated,
including random mutation (
\mathop{\mathtt{RM}}\limits
), greedy mutation (
\mathop{\mathtt{Greedy}}\limits
) and genetic mutation (
\mathop{\mathtt{Genetic}}\limits
)
[
Whitley1994
]
.
In addition, we used a random selection method (
\mathop{\mathtt{RS}}\limits
) as another baseline to randomly sample a TCR from
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
, which helps quantify the space of valid TCRs.
More details about baseline methods are available in Appendix
A.3
.

4.4
Evaluation Metrics

We evaluated all the methods using six metrics including:
(1) qualification rate
\mathop{\mathtt{q\%}}\limits
, which measures the percentage of qualified TCRs (Section
3.1
) among all the output TCRs;
(2) edit distance between
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{0}
and
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T}
(
\mathop{\mathtt{edist}}
), which measures sequence difference between

\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{0}
and qualified
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{T}
;
(3) average TCR validity score
\mathop{\overline{\mbox{$\mathop{s_{v}}\limits$}}}\limits
over valid TCRs or over qualified TCRs;
(4) average recognition probability
\mathop{\overline{\mbox{$\mathop{s_{r}}$}}}\limits
over valid TCRs or over qualified TCRs;
(5) validity rate
\mathop{\mathtt{v\%}}\limits
, which measures the percentage of valid TCRs (Section
3.1
) among all the output TCRs;
(6) average number of calls to
\mathcal{R}
calculation (
\mathop{\#\mbox{$\mathcal{R}$}}
) (Equation
9
) over all the generated TCRs, which estimates
the efficiency of methods.
We calculated the metrics over two different sets of output TCRs:
(1) the set of valid TCRs
\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{v}}\limits
:
\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{v}}\limits$}=\{\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}|\mbox{$\mathop{s_{v}}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})>\mbox{$\mathop{\sigma_{c}}\limits$}\}
;
(2) the set of qualified TCRs
\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\limits
:
{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\limits$}}=\{\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}|\mbox{$\mathop{s_{r}}$}(\mbox{$\mathop{c}$})>\mbox{$\mathop{\sigma_{c}}\limits$},\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}\in\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{v}}\limits$}\}
.

6
Conclusions and Outlook

In this paper, we presented a reinforcement learning framework to optimize TCRs for more effective TCR recognition,
which has the potential to guide TCR engineering therapy.
Our experimental results in comparison with generation-based methods and mutation-based methods on optimizing TCRs demonstrate
that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
outperforms the baseline methods.
Our analysis on the TCRs generated by
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
demonstrates that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
can successfully learn the conservation patterns of TCRs.
Our experiments on the comparison between the generated TCRs and existing TCRs demonstrate that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
can generate TCRs similar to
existing human TCRs, which can be used for further medical evaluation and investigation.
Our results in TCR detection comparison show that the
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
score in our framework can very effectively detect non-TCR sequences.
Our analysis on the distribution of
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
scores over mutations demonstrates that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
mutates sequences along the trajectories not
far away from valid TCRs.

Our proposed
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
is a modular and flexible framework. Thus,
the
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
and
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
scoring functions in the reward design can be replaced with other predictors trained on large-scale data when available.
Also,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
can be further improved from the following perspectives.
First, the recognition probabilities of TCRs considered in our paper are based on a peptide-TCR binding predictor (i.e.,
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
) rather than experimental validation.
Therefore, testing the generated qualified TCR candidates in a wet-lab will be needed ultimately to validate the interactions between TCRs and peptides.
Moreover, when the predicted recognition probabilities are not sufficiently accurate,

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
learned from unreliable rewards could be inaccurate, resulting in
generating
TCRs that could not recognize the given peptide.
Thus, it could be an interesting and challenging future work
to incorporate the reliabilities of predictions in the reward function of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
, so that the effect of unreliable recognition probabilities can be alleviated.
Finally,

\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
only considers the CDR3 of
\beta
chain in TCRs, while other regions of TCRs, though not contributing most to interactions between TCRs and peptides, are not considered.
In this sense, incorporating other regions of TCRs (e.g., CDR3 of alpha chains) could be an interesting future work.

Parameter setup

We listed the hyper-parameters of the TCR mutation environment, the policy network and the
\mathop{\mathtt{RL}}\limits

.
We selected an optimal set of hyperparameters including the discount factor,

Particularly, we set the maximum steps

as 8 steps, as we empirically found that it can lead to the
highest average final rewards.
We set the parameter
\alpha
as 0.5 for the tradeoff between validity scores and recognition probabilities, as we empirically found that different
\alpha

We also constructed a validation set by sampling 1,000 TCRs from the test set of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCR\text{-}AE}}
;
these TCRs are not included in the test set of
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
.
We then selected the hyperparameters including the hidden dimension of policy network and value networks
and the ratio of difficult initial state from
\mathop{\mathtt{Buf\text{-}Opt}}\limits
that the corresponding models achieve
the maximum qualified percentage
\mathop{\mathtt{q\%}}\limits
.
Among three options
[64,128,256]
, the optimal hidden dimensions of policy and value networks for
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits

\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits
are 128.
We set the latent dimension of
\overrightarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i}/\overleftarrow{\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}}_{i}/\mbox{$\mathbf{h}$}^{p}

In terms of encodings of amino acids, we set the dimension of learnable embeddings as 20, which is the same with the dimensions of BLOSUM embeddings and one-hot encodings; that is, the total dimension of amino acid encodings is 60.
In terms of ratios of difficult initial state, the optimal ratios for
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits

We set the threshold for recognition probability

.
In the implementation of

A.3
Baseline methods

We describe the baseline methods used in the main text as follows,

•

\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
: Monte Carlo Tree Search.
Given a peptide
\mathop{p}
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
generates TCR sequences by adding one amino acid step by step
until reaching the maximum length of 20 amino acids or with the recognition probability
\mathop{s_{r}}
greater than
\sigma_{r}
.
All the generated peptides with no less than 10 amino acids will be evaluated by
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
.
The approach of
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
is described in Algorithm
A.1
.
At each step, the amino acid to be added is determined by the Upper Confidence Bound algorithm (see equation in line 12 of Algorithm
A.1
) to balance exploration and exploitation
[
auer2002
]
.
The hyper-parameter
c_{puct}
is set as 0.5.
For each peptide, the number of rollout
N
is set as 1,000, as all the methods including
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
generate 1,000 TCRs for each peptide.
At the beginning of search, we initialize the sequence with “C”, as all the TCRs in
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
begin with “C”.
We collected all the generated TCRs during the search process, and
calculated the metrics listed in Table
over the 1,000
TCRs with the highest reward values for each peptide.

•

\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
: Back Propagation with the Variational Autoencoder.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
, a VAE model is first pre-trained to convert TCR sequences of variable lengths into continuous latent
embeddings of a fixed size with a single-layer LSTM.
Then, a student model with the pre-trained VAE is employed to
distill knowledge from
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
via learning the recognition probabilities produced by
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
from the latent embeddings
[
hinton2015
]
.
Note that during this fine-tuning process, the parameters of pre-trained VAE will also be updated.
With the well-trained student model,
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
can generate TCRs by optimizing the latent embeddings of TCRs through
gradient ascent to maximize the predicted recognition probabilities from the student model.
The decoded TCR sequences with the prediction from student model greater than
\sigma_{r}
will be evaluated by
\mathcal{R}
function.
We fine-tuned the hyper-parameters and set the dimension of hidden layers for VAE as 64, the dimension of latent embeddings as 8,
the batch size as 256, the initial coefficient for KL loss as 0.05 and the initial learning rate as 0.005.
We increased the coefficient by 0.05 every 1e4 steps until reaching the maximum coefficient limit 0.3; we also decreased the learning rate by 10 percent every 5,000 steps until reaching the minimum learning rate limit 0.0001.
We pre-trained the VAE model for 2e6 steps.
We then fine-tune the VAE model with the student model for 50,000 steps.
During the inference, we set the maximum step
t
for gradient ascent as 50 and the learning rate of gradient ascent as 0.05.
Similar to
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
, we collected all the generated TCRs and calculated the metrics over the 1,000 TCRs with the highest reward values for each peptides.

•

\mathop{\mathtt{RM}}\limits
: random mutation.
It randomly mutates a sequence one amino acid at a step up to 8 steps, each step leading to an intermediate mutated sequence;
it does so for each sequence
n
times,
generating 8
n
mutated sequences. It selects the mutated sequence with the highest
\mathcal{R}
as the final output.

•

\mathop{\mathtt{Greedy}}\limits
: greedy mutation.
It randomly mutates one amino acid of
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
at step
t
, and does this 10 times, generating 10 mutated sequences which are all
one-amino-acid different from
\mbox{$\mathop{c}$}_{t}
. It selects the one with best
\mathcal{R}
among the 10 sequences as the
next sequence to further mutate.

•

\mathop{\mathtt{Genetic}}\limits
: genetic mutation.
It randomly mutates each sequence in the population (size
n=5
)
five times, each at one site, generating five mutated sequences.
Among all the mutated sequences
for all the sequences in the population,
\mathop{\mathtt{Genetic}}\limits
selects the top-
n
sequences with the best
\mathcal{R}
as the next
population.

A.4.1
Discussion on Comparison among Generation-based Methods

Generation-based methods
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
on average very much underperform mutation-based methods.
Both
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
hardly generate any qualified TCRs (very low
\mathop{\mathtt{q\%}}\limits
:
0.01\!\pm\!0.03\%
for
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
and
0.04\!\pm\!0.09\%
for
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
in
\mathop{\mathcal{P}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits$}}}}\limits
).

\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
explores potentially the entire sequence space, including both valid TCRs and non-TCRs.
Although it uses
\mathcal{R}
to guide the search,
with substantially more calls to calculate
\mathcal{R}
than other methods,
due to the fact that valid TCRs may only occupy an extreme small portion of the entire sequence space, it is extremely challenging for
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
to
find qualified TCRs.
In
\mathop{\mathcal{P}_{\scriptsize{\mbox{$\mathop{\mathtt{VDJDB}}\limits$}}}}\limits
,
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
even cannot find any qualified TCR for all the peptides within 1,000 rollouts, and thus has zero values at
\mbox{$\mathop{\overline{\mbox{$\mathop{s_{v}}\limits$}}}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\limits$})
and
\mbox{$\mathop{\overline{\mbox{$\mathop{s_{r}}$}}}\limits$}(\mbox{$\mathop{\mathcal{C}_{q}}\limits$})
.
An estimation using
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRdb}}\limits
over all possible length-15 sequence space results at most
\frac{1,817,721}{20^{15}}=5.55\times 10^{-12}
% TCRs being valid in the sequence space.
Therefore, it is possible that
\mathop{\mathtt{MCTS}}\limits
ends at a region in the sequence space with
\mathop{\mathtt{q\%}}\limits
even worse than random selection method
\mathop{\mathtt{RS}}\limits
.

Similarly,
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
also has a minimum
\mathop{\mathtt{q\%}}\limits
(
0.04\!\pm\!0.09\%
).
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
encodes valid TCRs into its latent space, and uses a predictor in the latent space,
which is
trained to approximate
\mathop{\mathtt{ERGO}}\limits
, to guide the search of an optimal latent vector. This vector could correspond to a binding TCR and then is decoded
to a TCR sequence.
However, there is a phenomenon that is observed in other VAE-based generative approaches
[
GomezBombarelli2018
]
: while the latent vector is searched
under a guidance to maximize desired properties, its decoded instance does not always have the properties or are not even valid.
This phenomenon also appears in
\mathop{\mathtt{BP\text{-}VAE}}\limits
: the decoded TCRs are not qualified most of the times. This might be due to the propagation or
magnification of the errors from the predictor in the latent space, or the exploration in the latent space ends at a region far away from
that of valid TCRs. However, theoretical justification behind VAE-based generative approaches is out of the scope of this paper.

A.4.3
Amino Acid Distributions of Binding TCRs

Figure
A.3
presents the patterns among binding TCRs in
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
dataset (left) and the generated TCRs of different lengths for peptide “RFYKTLRAEQASQ” in
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
.
Particularly, as in Figure
A.3C
, the patterns among binding TCRs in
\mathop{\mathtt{McPAS}}\limits
are similar to those of generated TCRs.
For example, the most frequent amino acids at position 6 - 9
in generated TCRs (i.e., “R”, “A”, “R”, “G”) are also one of the most frequent amino acids at corresponding positions in known binding TCRs.
Similar observations are also for generated TCRs of different lengths.
This demonstrates that
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
can successfully identify the patterns of binding TCRs.
In addition to the similar patterns, we also observe the inconsistent patterns between known binding TCRs and generated TCRs.
For example, the generated TCRs are highly conserved on position 4, that is, most fourth amino acids are “H”; this
conservation pattern does not exist in known binding TCRs.
Please note that “H” also exists on position 4 among known binding TCRs as in Figure
A.3A
.
One reason for inconsistency between generated TCRs and known binding TCRs could be that only limited percentage of binding TCRs are with predicted recognition probabilities greater than 0.9, resulting in that generated TCRs are more conserved on
some patterns.

Figure
A.4
presents the distribution of action types produced by
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}\text{+}\mathtt{b}}\limits
for TCRs of length 15 binding to the peptide “RFYKTLRAEQASQ”.
As shown in Figure
A.4
, positions 5, 7, 9
in the TCRs prefer to be Histidine (“H”), Alanine (“A”), Arginine(“R”) and Glycine (“G”).
Since Alanine and Glycine are non-polar and hydrophobic,
the hydrophobic effect tends to be strong around these positions.
On the other hand, position 4 prefer to be Histidine,
which has a positive charge on its side chain.
Additionally, positions 5, 6, 8 in the TCRs prefer to be Arginine,
which is hydrophilic and has a positive charge on its side chain.
This shows that the binding pocket around these positions is more electrophilic which favors the interaction with amino acids with positive charges.

A.4.4
Comparison on TCR Detection

We compared our
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
scoring method (Equation
8
)
and the likelihood ratio method
[
ren2019
]
, denoted as
\mathop{\mathtt{lr}}\limits
, on distinguishing TCRs from non-TCRs.
The likelihood ratio method is one of the state-of-the-art methods on out-of-distribution (OOD) detection for genomic sequences.
To compare
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{lr}}\limits
,
we generated 50,000 sequences for each TCR in
\mathop{\mathcal{S}_{tst}}\limits
, with the two ends conserved to ‘C’ and ‘F’ respectively as in valid TCRs, and
all the other internal amino acids fully random.
These generated, random sequences will be considered as non-TCRs.
The conserved ‘C’ and ‘F’ ends among the non-TCRs ensure that they are not trivially separable from TCRs simply due to the ends.
We compared
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
and
\mathop{\mathtt{lr}}\limits
scores to detect TCRs from such non-TCRs, and present their distributions in Figure
A.5
.

This demonstrates that
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
is very effective in distinguishing TCRs.
Please note that the random sequences are not guaranteed to be true non-TCRs. However, given the random nature of these sequences, it is highly
likely that they are not valid TCRs.
In
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
, we used 1.2577 as the threshold to quantify if a sequence is a valid TCR (i.e.,
\sigma_{c}=1.2577
).

Figure
A.6
presents the change of
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
scores over mutations.
Figure
A.6
A shows that as TCRs have more random mutations, their
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
scores decrease dramatically.
This implies that the TCR optimization via mutation is not trivial, as a random/bad mutation can significantly decrease
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
score and easily
lead to an unqualified solution.
However, as Figure
A.6
B shows, the
\mathop{s_{v}}\limits
scores do not decrease very dramatically during
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
mutations,
consistently with a good portion of mutated sequences being valid TCRs.
This demonstrates that for this non-trivial optimization problem,
\mathop{\mathtt{TCRPPO}}\limits
succeeds in mutating sequences along the trajectories not far away from valid TCRs, toward final qualified sequences.
