Very weak solutions of the Dirichlet problem for 2-Hessian equation
Tongtong Li, Guohuan Qiu

Abstract
. For any α \alpha small, we construct infinitely many C 1 , α C^{1,\alpha} very weak solutions to the 2-Hessian equation with prescribed boundary value. The proof relies on the convex integration method and cut-off technique.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 35M10; Secondary 76B03

1.
Introduction

Given a smooth function
v:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
, the
2
-Hessian equation of
v
is defined by
1
1

1

Our definition of
\sigma_{2}
is different from the standard one by a factor
2
.

(1.1)

\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v):=\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}[\partial_{ii}v\partial_{jj}v-(\partial_{ij}v)^{2}]=f.

In the seminal work
[
5
]
, Caffarelli, Nirenberg and Spruck established the existence and uniqueness of a classical solution to the Dirichlet problem:

(1.2)

\begin{cases}\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)=f>0&\text{in }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},\ n\geq 2,\\
v=g&\text{on }\partial\Omega,\end{cases}

Furthermore, existence and uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem (
1.2
) also hold for 2-convex weak solutions in the viscosity sense, see
[
43
,
39
]
.

The 2-Hessian equation (
1.1
) is a simplified model of the scalar curvature equation for hypersurfaces. Motivated by the Weyl isometric embedding problem, E. Heinz
[
20
]
established the interior estimates when
n=2
. This is a fully nonlinear partial differential equation for which the regularity is a highly nontrivial problem. Pogorelov
[
33
]
constructed non-
C^{2}
convex solutions to the equation
\det D^{2}u=1
in
B_{r}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
when
n\geq 3
. Warren and Yuan
[
44
]
established the interior estimate for
\sigma_{2}(D^{2}u)=1
when
n=3
. For smooth
f>0
and
n=3
, the second named author
[
34
,
35
]
proved the interior estimates for 2-Hessian equations and scalar curvature equations.
Recently, Shankar and Yuan
[
38
]
derived a priori interior Hessian estimate and interior regularity for
\sigma_{2}(D^{2}u)=1
in dimension four. Combining the solvability and uniqueness of the Dirichlet problem (
1.2
) for viscosity weak solutions, the above a priori estimates imply that viscosity solutions become smooth when
n=2,3,4
(at least for
f=1
when
n=4
). In higher dimensions, additional convexity conditions are assumed to obtain the interior estimate and regularity of the 2-Hessian equation, as seen in papers like
[
18
,
27
,
36
,
28
,
37
]
. The question of whether viscosity solutions to (
1.1
) become smooth in dimensions
n\geq 5
remains a famous open problem in the field. The most crucial ingredient for obtaining the interior estimate is the validity of the Jacobi inequality when
n=3
and
4
, as demonstrated in
[
44
,
34
,
38
]
. But the crucial Jacobi inequality does not hold when
n\geq 5
, see
[
38
]
. Thus one may inquire whether it is possible to construct
C^{1,\alpha}
viscosity weak solutions by combining the convex integration method with other approaches. The motivation behind our study of
very weak solutions
, as defined in
1.1
, is to explore possible ways to construct singular
C^{1,\alpha}
viscosity weak solutions when
n\geq 5
. Although
very weak solutions
and viscosity solutions are entirely different types of weak solutions, it is still worth exploring possible connections between them.

In this paper, we focus on the non-uniqueness of
very weak solutions
, defined as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
\Omega
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
v\in W^{1,2}_{loc}(\Omega)
. We say
v
solves
\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)=f
in very weak sense if

-\sum^{n}_{i,j=1}\int_{\Omega}\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)\partial_{i}v\partial_{j}v=\int_{\Omega}f\phi,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega),

where

\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)=\Delta\phi\delta_{ij}-\partial_{ij}\phi.

And
v
is a very weak solution to (
1.2
) if further
v\in C(\bar{\Omega})
and
v=g
on
\partial\Omega
.

This definition is motivated by the double divergence structure of
\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)
(see Section
2
). For the Laplace equation
\Delta u=f
, the well-known Weyl lemma guarantees the smoothness of locally integrable weak solutions in the following sense:

\int_{\Omega}u\Delta\phi=\int_{\Omega}f\phi,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\Omega).

One interesting point compared with the Laplace equation is that after integrating by parts twice, there are quadratic gradient terms for 2-Hessian equation. For the Monge-Ampere equation, one well-known concept of weak solutions was introduced by Alexandrov
[
1
]
, now commonly referred to as Alexandrov solutions, which are equivalent to viscosity solutions, as shown in
[
19
]
. After Trudinger and Wang’s series of challenging works
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
, Alexandrov solutions have been successfully extended to weak solutions defined by the
2
-Hessian measure for the
\sigma_{2}
operator. It is important to note that the concept of the
very weak
solution is distinct from the weak solution defined in
[
40
,
41
,
42
]
.

In dimension two, (
1.1
) reduces to Monge-Ampère equation. In this case, the concept of distributional
\sigma_{2}
was introduced by
Iwaniec 2001
in the name of
weak Hessian
.
Lewicka and Pakzad 2017
(see also
[
13
,
10
]
) noticed that (
1.1
) is closely related to isometric immersion of surfaces into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. Using the convex integration method there, they showed that
C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
very weak solutions to (
1.1
) are dense in
C^{0}(\bar{\Omega})
for any
\alpha<1/7
and
f\in L^{7/6}(\Omega)
. This was improved by
Cao and Székelyhidi Jr. 2019
to
\alpha<1/7
and recently by
Cao et al. 2023
to
\alpha<1/3
. For the corresponding Dirichlet problem (
1.2
),
Cao 2023
proved Theorem
1.2
and
1.3
with
\alpha<1/5
.

In general dimensions
n\geq 2
,
Lewicka 2023a
considered the following Monge-Ampère system

\mathfrak{Det}\nabla^{2}v=F\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n},

where
F=(F_{ij,st})_{1\leq i,j,s,t\leq n}:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n^{4}}
is given,
v:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{k}
is unknown and

(\mathfrak{Det}\nabla^{2}v)_{ij,st}:=\langle\partial_{is}v,\partial_{jt}v\rangle-\langle\partial_{it}v,\partial_{js}v\rangle.

She derived the denseness result for
\alpha<\frac{1}{1+(n^{2}+n)/k}
(cf.
[
25
]
). When
k=1
, this covers the counterpart of Theorem
1.2
without boundary conditions, since
\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)=\sum_{i,j}(\mathfrak{Det}\nabla^{2}v)_{ij,ij}
.

In this paper, we prove the following theorems, generalizing the result of
Cao 2023
to higher dimensions.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\Omega
be a bounded domain in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
C^{2,\kappa}
boundary
\partial\Omega
,
f\in C^{0,\kappa}(\bar{\Omega})
,
\kappa\in(0,1)
. Then there exists some
g\in C^{2,\kappa}(\partial\Omega)
, such that: for any

\alpha<\frac{1}{1+n+n^{2}},

there exist infinitely many very weak solutions
v
to the Dirichlet problem (
1.2
) such that
v\in C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Omega,f,\kappa
be as in Theorem (
1.2
). Assume further
f>0
. Then for any
g\in C^{0,\kappa}(\partial\Omega)
and any

\alpha<\frac{1}{1+n+n^{2}},

there exist infinitely many very weak solutions
v
to the Dirichlet problem (
1.2
) such that
v\in C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
.

The proof is based on an iteration scheme called convex integration, which was originally introduced by Nash-Kuiper
[
29
,
22
]
to construct
C^{1}
isometric embedding of manifolds, and later generalized (and named) by
Gromov 1974
,
Gromov 1986
to more general setting to prove the abundance of (weak) solutions (Gromov’s
h
-principle). After Borisov’s earlier work
[
2
,
4
]
,
Conti et al. 2012b
,
Conti et al. 2012a
and
Cao and Székelyhidi 2022
showed that any
C^{1}
differentiable immersion (resp. embedding) of a smooth closed
n
-manifold into
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
can be deformed to an
C^{1,\alpha}
isometric immersion (resp. embedding) if
\alpha<1/(1+n+n^{2})
. For surfaces this was improved to
\alpha<1/5
in
[
14
,
7
]
.

These results asserting the
abundance
of (weak) solutions are often referred to as
flexibility
results. On the other hand, one obtains
rigidity
, namely
uniqueness
of solutions, if higher regularity is required. For instance,
Borisov 1959
showed that any
C^{1,\alpha},\alpha>2/3
isometric
embedding
of a smooth closed surface with positive Guass curvature into
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is unique up to rigid motion. A modern proof can be found in
[
12
,
11
]
. This result is based on the work of
Pogorelov 1959
,
Pogorelov 1973
.
Gromov 2017
conjectures the regularity threshold of rigidity vs. flexibility to be
\alpha=1/2
. See
[
23
,
30
]
and references therein for recent developments. In
[
30
]
, Pakzad also proved the rigidity of
C^{1,\alpha}
very weak solutions to (
1.2
) for
n=2,\alpha>2/3
and
\Omega=\mathbb{R}^{2}
. One interesting question regarding the rigidity aspect of the 2-Hessian equation is whether a
C^{1,\alpha}
(for
\alpha\approx 1
) very weak solution of (
1.2
) become the viscosity weak solution.

This article is organized as follows. Section
2
collects some basic facts, and in Remark
2.2
we explain the idea of the proof. In Section

we omit the boundary condition

and construct very weak solutions by convex integration. The main ingredient is the one stage induction Proposition
3.5
, relying on the decomposition Lemma

. In Section

we apply the cut-off technique in
[

]
such that the boundary condition is satisfied, thus finishing the proof of Theorem
1.2
and
1.3
.

2.
Preliminaries.

2.1.
Distributional
\sigma_{2}

We observe that
\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)
has the following divergence structure

(2.1)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{i}(\partial_{i}v\partial_{jj}v)-\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}v\partial_{ij}v)

(2.2)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}(\partial_{i}v\partial_{j}v)-\partial_{i}(\partial_{ij}v\partial_{j}v)-\partial_{j}(\partial_{i}v\partial_{ij}v)

(2.3)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ij}(\partial_{i}v\partial_{j}v)-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{ii}(\partial_{j}v)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{jj}(\partial_{i}v)^{2}.

By (
2.3
), there holds

\displaystyle\int\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)\phi

\displaystyle=\sum_{i,j}\int\partial_{ij}\phi\partial_{i}v\partial_{j}v-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{ii}\phi(\partial_{j}v)^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\partial_{jj}\phi(\partial_{i}v)^{2}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i,j}\int\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)\partial_{i}v\partial_{j}v,\hskip 10.00002pt\forall\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{n}),

where

\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)=\Delta\phi\delta_{ij}-\partial_{ij}\phi.

This allows us to define
\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)
in distributional sense for any
v\in W^{1,2}_{loc}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
. Accordingly, we can define
very weak solutions
to
\sigma_{2}
equation as in Definition
1.1
.

Remark 2.1
.

In fact, we can provide different distributional definitions of
\sigma_{2}
based on (
2.1
) (
2.2
) (
2.3
). Refer to
[
21
]
for a discussion in dimension two.

2.2.
Notations

Basically we follow the notations in
[
8
, Section 2]
. The set of
n\times n
symmetric matrices is denoted by
{\mathcal{S}_{n}}
. We use usual Hölder norms
\|\cdot\|_{k},\|\cdot\|_{k,\beta}
. Also we write
\|(v,w)\|_{k}=\|v\|_{k}+\|w\|_{k}
.

We fix a non-negative radial function
\phi\in C^{\infty}_{c}(B_{1}(0))
, such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\phi(x)dx=1.

This
\phi
will serve as a mollifier. Namely, let
\phi_{l}(x)=l^{-n}\phi(\frac{x}{l})
. For any function
h
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the mollification of
h
at length
l
is

h*\phi_{l}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h(x-y)\phi_{l}(y)dy.

A constant is called universal if it depends only on
n,\Omega,f,g
.
C>1
denotes a positive universal constant that may vary from line to line. Besides, we write
a\lesssim b
if
a\leq Cb
, and
a\sim b
if
\frac{b}{C}\leq a\leq Cb
, for some universal constant
C
.

2.3.
Corrugation functions

Let

\Gamma_{1}(s,t)=\frac{s}{\pi}\sin(2\pi t),\hskip 10.00002pt\Gamma_{2}(s,t)=-\frac{s^{2}}{4\pi}\sin(4\pi t)

which satisfy the important relation

(2.4)

\partial_{t}\Gamma_{2}(s,t)+\frac{1}{2}|\partial_{t}\Gamma_{1}(s,t)|^{2}=s^{2}.

It’s easy to get following bounds: for
0\leq k\leq 3
,

(2.5)

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{1}(s,t)|\leq Cs,\hskip 10.00002pt|\partial_{s}\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{1}(s,t)|\leq C,

\displaystyle|\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{2}(s,t)|\leq Cs^{2},\hskip 10.00002pt|\partial_{s}\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{2}(s,t)|\leq Cs,\hskip 10.00002pt|\partial_{s}^{2}\partial_{t}^{k}\Gamma_{2}(s,t)|\leq C.

Remark 2.2
.

Such periodic functions
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
satisfying (
2.4
) are crucial in convex integration, whose existence is guaranteed by the quadratic structure in distributional
\sigma_{2}
. We give some explanations below.

It turns out that (
1.2
) is equivalent to (
3.3
) (
3.4
) with
v=g
on
\partial\Omega
. (
3.3
) is easy to solve since the elements of
A
are independent. The idea of convex integration is, starting from arbitrary
(v^{b},w^{b})
, to construct inductively
\{(V_{q},W_{q})\}_{q=1}^{\infty}
that finally converges in
C^{1,\alpha}
sense to some solution
(v,w)
of (
3.4
).

By linearization of (
3.4
), the derivative
(\nabla(v-v^{b}),\nabla(w-w^{b})+\text{sym}\nabla(v-v^{b})\otimes\nabla v^{b}))
has to satisfy a relation similar to
y+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}=1
, cf. (
3.17
). Since the origin lies in the convex hull of the curve
y+\frac{1}{2}x^{2}=1
, we let the derivative move along the curve, such that its integration is small. Then
(v,w)
oscillates around
(v^{b},w^{b})
, leading to the
C^{0}
closeness
\|(v^{b}-v,w^{b}-w)\|_{0}\leq\epsilon
. This is where the name ”convex integration” comes from.

This is achieved by adding higher and higher frequency oscillatory functions (i.e. corrugation) produced by
\Gamma_{1},\Gamma_{2}
at each stage
(V_{q},W_{q})\to(V_{q+1},W_{q+1})
. Since (
3.4
) is actually a matrix equation, each stage has to be decomposed into
N_{*}=n(n+1)/2
steps, which determines
(v,w)\in C^{1,\alpha}
with
\alpha<1/(1+2N_{*})
, cf.
[
8
,
9
]
.

2.4.
Mollification estimates.

Recall that for any
r,s\geq 0

(2.6)

\displaystyle[h*\phi_{l}]_{r}\leq[h]_{r},

(2.7)

\displaystyle[h-h*\phi_{l}]_{r}\leq C[h]_{r+s}l^{s},\hskip 10.00002pt[h*\phi_{l}]_{r+s}\leq C[h]_{r}l^{-s},

(2.8)

\displaystyle[(h_{1}h_{2})*\phi_{l}-(h_{1}*\phi_{l})(h_{2}*\phi_{l})]_{r}\leq C\|h_{1}\|_{1}\|h_{2}\|_{1}l^{2-r},

where
[h]_{r}=\|\nabla^{r}h\|_{0}
. See
[
12
, Lemma 1]
for the proof. Mollification is important to control higher order derivatives, and the commutator inequality (
2.8
) is used to estimate deficit matrices.

3.
Convex integration.

In this section we temporarily omit the boundary condition and construct very weak solutions to

(3.1)

\sigma_{2}(\nabla^{2}v)=f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Omega.

We first introduce some notations. Let
L
be a linear differential operator acting on
{\mathcal{S}_{n}}
-valued functions defined by

(3.2)

L(A):=\sum_{i,j}\partial_{ii}A_{jj}+\partial_{jj}A_{ii}-2\partial_{ij}A_{ij},\hskip 10.00002ptA=(A_{ij})_{1\leq i,j\leq n}:\Omega\to{\mathcal{S}_{n}}.

Then equation (
1.2
) can be written as

-\frac{1}{2}L(\nabla v\otimes\nabla v)=f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\ \Omega.

The operator
L
generalizes the
curl curl
operator in dimension two. In particular, for
\forall

w=(w_{1},...,w_{n}):\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
, let

\text{sym}\nabla w:=\frac{1}{2}(\nabla w+\nabla w^{T})=(\frac{\partial_{i}w_{j}+\partial_{j}w_{i}}{2})_{1\leq i,j\leq n},

then by simple computation, we can check that

L(\text{sym}\nabla w)=0

in distributional sense.
In fact, due to
\partial_{i}\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)=\partial_{j}\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)=0
, there holds

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j}\int\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)(\frac{\partial_{i}w_{j}+\partial_{j}w_{i}}{2})=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i,j}\int\partial_{i}\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)w_{j}+\partial_{j}\sigma_{2}^{ij}(\nabla^{2}\phi)w_{i}=0

for any
\phi\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
.

So to solve (
3.1
) it suffices to find
(A,v,w)\in C^{0}(\bar{\Omega},{\mathcal{S}_{n}})\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})\times C^{1}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that

(3.3)

\displaystyle-L(A)=f\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\ \Omega,

(3.4)

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v+\text{sym}\nabla w\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in}\ \Omega.

Part one: solve (
3.3
).

Let
A=(u+\tau)Id
, where
\tau>0
is a constant and
u
solves the Dirichlet problem

-(2n-2)\Delta u=f\ \text{in}\ \Omega,\hskip 10.00002ptu=0\ \text{on}\ \partial\Omega.

Then

-L(A)=-(2n-2)\Delta u=f

and
A\in C^{2,\kappa}(\bar{\Omega},{\mathcal{S}_{n}})
by classical elliptic PDE theory.

Part two: solve (
3.4
).

Replacing
v^{b}
by its mollification, we may assume
v^{b}\in C^{\infty}(\bar{\Omega})
, and let
w^{b}=0
. Denote the initial deficit matrix by

(3.5)

D^{b}:=A-\frac{1}{2}\nabla v^{b}\otimes\nabla v^{b}-\text{sym}\nabla w^{b}.

Recall
A=(u+\tau)Id
. By letting
\tau
large enough, the problem boils down to the following:

Theorem 3.1

(
[
24
]
, Theorem 1.1,
(d,k)=(n,1)
)
.

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an open, bounded domain. Given a function
v^{b}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega})
a vector field
w^{b}\in C^{1}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
and a matrix field
A\in C^{0,\beta}(\bar{\Omega},{\mathcal{S}_{n}})
, assume that:

(3.6)

D^{b}:=A-\frac{1}{2}\nabla v^{b}\otimes\nabla v^{b}-\text{sym}\nabla w^{b}\hskip 10.00002pt\text{satisfies}\hskip 10.00002ptD^{b}>\bar{\tau}Id\ on\ \bar{\Omega},

for some
\bar{\tau}>0
, in the sense of matrix inequalities. Fix
\epsilon>0
and let:

0<\alpha<\min\{\frac{\beta}{2},\frac{1}{1+n+n^{2}}\}.

Then, there exists
v\in C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega})
and
w\in C^{1,\alpha}(\bar{\Omega},\mathbb{R}^{n})
such that the following holds:

(3.7)

\displaystyle\|v-v^{b}\|_{0}\leq\epsilon,\hskip 10.00002pt\|w-w^{b}\|_{0}\leq\epsilon,

(3.8)

\displaystyle A=\frac{1}{2}\nabla v\otimes\nabla v+\text{sym}\nabla w\hskip 10.00002pt\text{in }\Omega.

For our purpose we only prove for
A\in C^{2,\kappa}(\bar{\Omega},{\mathcal{S}_{n}})
, and w.l.o.g we assume
\bar{\tau}>1
. We divide its proof into four steps following
[
8
]
. The key is the one stage induction Proposition
3.5
in Step 3.

Remark 3.2
.

Note that by (

(3.12)

\bar{D}=(D^{b}-Id)+(\frac{1}{2}\nabla v^{b}\otimes\nabla v^{b}+\text{sym}\nabla w^{b})-(\frac{1}{2}\nabla\bar{v}_{N}\otimes\nabla\bar{v}_{N}+\text{sym}\nabla\bar{w}_{N})

Lemma 3.3

(

\Lambda>\lambda>0

Proof of (
3.9
)-(

).

Because of (
3.6
) and
\bar{\tau}>1
, we can apply Lemma
