Twisted-Austere Submanifolds in Euclidean Space
Thomas A. IVEY a and Spiro KARIGIANNIS b

Abstract
A twisted-austere k k -fold ( M , μ ) (M,\mu) in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} consists of a k k -dimensional submanifold M M of ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} together with a closed 1 1 -form μ \mu on M M , such that the second fundamental form A A of M M and the 1 1 -form μ \mu satisfy a particular system of coupled nonlinear second order PDE. Given such an object, the “twisted conormal bundle” N ∗ ​ M + μ N^{*}M+\mu is a special Lagrangian submanifold of ℂ n {\mathbb{C}}^{n} . We review the twisted-austere condition and give an explicit example. Then we focus on twisted-austere 3-folds. We give a geometric description of all solutions when the “base” M M is a cylinder, and when M M is austere. Finally, we prove that, other than the case of a generalized helicoid in ℝ 5 {\mathbb{R}}^{5} discovered by Bryant, there are no other possibilities for the base M M . This gives a complete classification of twisted-austere 3 3 -folds in ℝ n {\mathbb{R}}^{n} .

Introduction

Special Lagrangian submanifolds are a special class of
n
-dimensional submanifold in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
, and more generally in Calabi–Yau
n
-folds. They were introduced by Harvey–Lawson
[
5
]
and were the first modern example of
calibrated
submanifolds. They are a class of
minimal
(vanishing mean curvature) submanifolds characterized by a first order nonlinear PDE, and in fact are absolutely locally volume minimizing in their homology class. Special Lagrangian submanifolds also play a key role in
mirror symmetry
through the Strominger–Yau–Zaslow conjecture
[
12
]
. They have been extensively studied by many authors. An excellent reference summarizing much of the work on special Lagrangian geometry up to the time of its publication is the textbook
[
10
]
of Joyce on calibrated geometry.

One particular construction of special Lagrangian submanifolds in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
first appeared in
[
5
]
and is known as the
conormal bundle construction
. Given a
k
-dimensional submanifold
M
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Harvey–Lawson showed that its conormal bundle
N^{*}M
is special Lagrangian in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
if and only if
M
is
austere
, which means that all the odd degree elementary symmetric polynomials in the eigenvalues of the second fundamental form vanish. Note that this is in general a
fully nonlinear
second order PDE on the immersion of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The conormal bundle construction was later reviewed in detail, and generalized to the exceptional holonomies
\mathrm{G}_{2}
and
\mathrm{Spin}(7)
, by Ionel–Karigiannis–Min-Oo in
[
8
]
. Austere submanifolds in Euclidean space have been studied by several authors, including Bryant
[
2
]
and Ionel–Ivey
[
6
,
7
]
.

A generalization of the conormal bundle construction was introduced by Borisenko
[
1
]
and later significantly extended by Karigiannis–Leung
[
11
]
. The idea is as follows. Let
M
be a
k
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}|_{M}=N^{*}M\oplus T^{*}M
. Let
\mu
be a
closed

1
-form on
M
. Define the “twisted conormal bundle” to be the
n
-dimensional submanifold
N^{*}M+\mu=\{(\nu_{p},\mu_{p})\,|\,\nu_{p}\in N^{*}_{p}M\}
of
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
. This is the total space of an affine bundle over
M
whose fibres are affine translates of the conormal spaces, translated by the
1
-form
\mu
. In
[
11
]
it was proved that
N^{*}M+\mu
is special Lagrangian if and only if the second fundamental form
A
of
M
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and the
1
-form
\mu
satisfy a system of coupled second order fully nonlinear PDE, which we call the
twisted austere
equations. This result is stated explicitly in Theorem
2.2
. (Borisenko had only considered the case when
\mu
is exact,
n=3
, and
k=2
.)

Both the original construction of Harvey–Lawson and the “twisted version” of Borisenko and Karigiannis–Leung produce examples of
ruled
special Lagrangian submanifolds. Joyce
[
9
]
has also studied ruled special Lagrangian submanifolds in
\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

We consider the case of
twisted-austere pairs

\big(M^{k},\mu\big)
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
for
k=1,2,3
and any
n
. The cases
k=1,2
are trivial to classify completely. The case
k=3
is significantly more involved. We obtain a complete classification and give a geometric description of all possibilities.

Organization of the paper and summary of results.
In Section
2
we review the twisted-austere condition, and completely describe the cases
k=1
and
k=2
, as well as the case when
M
is totally geodesic. We also present an explicit nontrivial solution when
k=2
and
n=3
, giving a special Lagrangian submanifold in
\mathbb{C}^{3}
. The remainder of the paper is concerned with the non totally geodesic case when
k=3
.

Section
3
establishes some general results on twisted-austere pairs
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
where
M
is not totally geodesic. The main result is Theorem
3.1
, where we show that
M
is either a generalized helicoid swept out by planes in
\mathbb{R}^{5}
, or else
n
is arbitrary and
M
is ruled by lines. Section
4
is concerned with the particular case when
M
is a cylinder. The main result is Theorem
4.2
, where we give a geometric characterization of this case, in terms of a minimal surface
\Sigma
in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
and a closed
1
-form
\lambda
on
\Sigma
with prescribed codifferential.

Section
5
is the heart of the paper, where we comprehensively study the case in which the base
M
is austere. This study breaks up naturally into two cases, called the “split case” and the “non-split case”, characterized by algebraic properties on the covariant derivative
\nabla\mu
. Each case then breaks up into subcases. In the split case,
M
can be either a cylinder, a cone, or a “twisted cone”. The first two subcases also occur in the non-split case. The two cylinder subcases are related to the results of Section
4
. In all subcases the twisted-austere pairs
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
with austere base
M
are related to geometric data on a surface
\Sigma
, being the cross-section of the cylinder or the link of the (twisted) cone. Using this data, the
1
-form
\mu
is described explicitly.

Finally in Section
6
we outline the proof of our classification, which is Theorem
6.1
. We prove that the pairs
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
studied in the earlier sections are the only possibilities. Three appendices follow, collecting various technical results that are used in the main body of the paper.

2
Preliminaries

In this section we review the
twisted-austere
condition for a pair
\big(M^{k},\mu\big)
where
M^{k}
is a
k
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
\mu
is a smooth
1
-form on
M
. We also discuss the cases
k=1
and
k=2
in detail, as well as the case when
M^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is totally geodesic. The remainder of the paper is concerned with the case
k=3
for
M^{k}
not totally geodesic.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
M^{k}
be a
k
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a let
\mu
be a smooth
1
-form on
M
. Define
L=N^{*}M+\mu
to be the
n
-dimensional submanifold of
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
given by

\displaystyle N^{*}M+\mu=\big\{(x,\xi+\mu_{x})\in T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}|_{M}\,|\,x\in M,\,\xi\in N^{*}_{x}M\big\}.

(2.1)

We say that
(M,\mu)
is a
twisted-austere pair
if
L=N^{*}M+\mu
is a special Lagrangian submanifold inside
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
with respect to some phase. Following
[
11
]
, we refer to this as the
Borisenko construction
.

It is shown in
[
11
]
that
L
is Lagrangian if and only if
\nabla\mu
is a symmetric tensor on
M
, that is
{\rm d}\mu=0
. The conditions under which
L
is special Lagrangian are more involved. In what follows, let
A^{\nu}=\nu\cdot\operatorname{II}
denote the second fundamental form of
M
in the normal direction
\nu
, and let
B=\nabla\mu
. We use the same letters to denote the matrices that represent these covariant tensors with respect to a local orthonormal frame field
\mathsf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{k}
on
M
. (For example,
B_{ij}=B(\mathsf{e}_{i},\mathsf{e}_{j})
, and the Lagrangian condition
is equivalent to
B
being a symmetric matrix.)

Theorem 2.2

(Karigiannis–Leung
[
11
]
)
.

Fix a
phase angle

\theta\in[0,2\pi)
. Let
C=I+\mathrm{i}B
, and define the
cophase angle

\phi
by

\displaystyle\phi=\theta-(n-k)\dfrac{\pi}{2}.

(2.2)

Then
(M,\mu)
is a twisted-austere pair with phase
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
if and only if the following three conditions all hold:

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mu=0,

(2.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\big({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\det C\big)=0,

(2.4)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\big(\mathrm{i}^{j}\sigma_{j}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,\qquad\text{for all $\nu$ and all $j=1,\dots,k$}.

(2.5)

Here
\sigma_{j}
denotes the
j^{\text{th}}
elementary symmetric function of the eigenvalues of a matrix, so in particular
\sigma_{1}=\operatorname{tr}
and
\sigma_{k}=\det
.
(
See Appendix
A.1
for more details.
)

Remark 2.3
.

In
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
the definition of
\phi
is the negative of what we have in (
2.2
), because in
[
11
]
the definition of special Lagrangian with phase
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
meant calibrated with respect to
{\rm e}^{-\mathrm{i}\theta}{\rm d}z^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}z^{n}
, whereas we take it to mean calibrated with respect to
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}{\rm d}z^{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge{\rm d}z^{n}
, which is standard.

Note that condition (
2.5
) is really a sequence of conditions for each normal direction
\nu
, as follows:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\big(\sigma_{1}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,\qquad\operatorname{Im}\big(\sigma_{2}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,\qquad\operatorname{Re}\big(\sigma_{3}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,\qquad\dots.

(2.6)

It is useful to rewrite equation (
2.5
) in the extreme cases
j=1
and
j=k
as follows. By the linearity of
\sigma_{1}=\operatorname{tr}
, we have

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\big(\sigma_{1}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big(A^{\nu}\big((I+\mathrm{i}B)^{-1}+(I-\mathrm{i}B)^{-1}\big)\big).

But because we can diagonalize the symmetric matrix
B
, it is easy to see that
(I+\mathrm{i}B)^{-1}+

(I-\mathrm{i}B)^{-1}=2\big(I+B^{2}\big)^{-1}
. Thus we find that

\displaystyle 2\operatorname{Re}\big(\sigma_{1}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=2\operatorname{tr}\big(A^{\nu}\big(I+B^{2}\big)^{-1}\big).

Hence, the condition (
2.5
) in the
j=1
case can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\big(A^{\nu}\big(I+B^{2}\big)^{-1}\big)=0\qquad\text{for all $\nu$}.

(2.7)

Because
\sigma_{k}=\det
is multiplicative, we have
\sigma_{k}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)=\det A^{\nu}\det C^{-1}
. Hence, the condition (
2.5
) in the
j=k
case can be rewritten as

\displaystyle(\det A^{\nu})\operatorname{Im}\left(\frac{\mathrm{i}^{k}}{\det C}\right)=0\qquad\text{for all $\nu$}.

(2.8)

The simplest case of the twisted austere condition is when
M^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is totally geodesic.

Proposition 2.4
.

Suppose that
M^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is
totally geodesic
and complete. Without loss of generality we can take
M^{k}=\mathbb{R}^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then the Borisenko construction yields a product
K\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
, where
K\subset T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a special Lagrangian submanifold which is the graph of
\mu
.

Proof.

Since
\mathbb{R}^{k}
is totally geodesic, we have
A^{\nu}=0
for all
\nu
. Thus the sequence of conditions (
2.6
) are trivially satisfied. We have
N^{*}M=\mathbb{R}^{k}\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}=T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The closed
1
-form
\mu
is necessarily exact, so
\mu={\rm d}f
for some
f\in C^{\infty}\big(\mathbb{R}^{k}\big)
. Equation (
2.4
) becomes
\operatorname{Im}\big({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\det(I+{\rm i}\operatorname{Hess}f)\big)=0
for
\phi=\theta-(n-k)\frac{\pi}{2}
. Then by
[
5
, Theorem 2.3]
, the graph of
\mu
in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{k}
is a special Lagrangian submanifold
K
of
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}
with phase
{\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\theta}
. (See
[
11
, Theorem 2.3]
for discussion about the phase.) Hence
L=N^{*}\mathbb{R}^{k}+\mu=K\times\mathbb{R}^{n-k}
as claimed.
∎

A discussion of the cases
k=1,2
of the twisted-austere condition was given in
[
11
, Section 2]
, which included a classification for
k=1
and a partial result for
k=2
. Here we complete the classification for
k=2
. For completeness, we give the details for both cases.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
k=1
. If
\big(M^{1},\mu\big)
is a twisted-austere pair in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
M
complete, then
L=N^{*}M+\mu
is an
n
-plane in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{n}=\mathbb{C}^{n}
.

Proof.

In this case,
M^{1}
is a curve. Equation (
2.3
) is vacuous. The
1\times 1
matrix
C
is
1-\mathrm{i}{\rm d}^{*}\mu
. Hence equation (
2.4
) becomes

\displaystyle\sin\phi=\cos\phi\,{\rm d}^{*}\mu.

(2.9)

(There is a harmless sign error here in
[
11
]
.) Using (
2.7
) for (
2.5
) in the
j=1
case (which is the only allowed value of
j
here), and since
A^{\nu}
is a scalar, we get

\displaystyle A^{\nu}=0\qquad\text{for all $\nu$}.

Thus
M^{1}
is totally geodesic, hence a straight line. Without loss of generality, we take it to be the
x
-axis in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Since
M=\mathbb{R}
, we have
\mu={\rm d}f
for some
f\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
. Then equation (
2.9
) says that
f^{\prime\prime}(x)=-\tan\phi
. Hence
\mu=(ax+b){\rm d}x
for some constants
a
,
b
, and
N^{*}M+\mu
is an affine translation of
N^{*}M
in
\mathbb{C}^{n}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\oplus\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and is thus an
n
-plane.
∎

Remark 2.6
.

Proposition
2.5
is consistent with Proposition
2.4
, as a special Lagrangian graph in
T^{*}\mathbb{R}^{1}=\mathbb{C}^{2}
is straight line.

Proposition 2.7
.

Let
k=2
, and let
\big(M^{2},\mu\big)
be a twisted-austere pair in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, such that
M
is not totally geodesic. Then
\sin\phi=0
, and
M
is a minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu
a closed and coclosed
1
-form on
M
with respect to the induced metric
(
and hence harmonic
)
.

Proof.

In this case,
M^{2}
is a surface and now
\sigma_{2}=\det
. From
\det C=\det(I+\mathrm{i}B)=1+\mathrm{i}\operatorname{tr}B-\det B
, we find that (
2.4
) becomes

\displaystyle\sin\phi(1-\det B)+\cos\phi(\operatorname{tr}B)=0.

(2.10)

(There is again a harmless sign error in
[
11
, equation (2.15)]
.) Using (
2.7
) for (
2.5
) in the
j=1
case gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{tr}\big(A^{\nu}\big(I+B^{2}\big)^{-1}\big)=0\qquad\text{for all $\nu$}.

(2.11)

We also have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\det C}=\frac{1}{1+\mathrm{i}\operatorname{tr}B-\det B}=\frac{(1-\det B)-\mathrm{i}\operatorname{tr}B}{(1-\det B)^{2}+(\operatorname{tr}B)^{2}}.

From the above, using (
2.8
) for (
2.5
) in the
j=k=2
case gives

\displaystyle(\det A^{\nu})(\operatorname{tr}B)=0.

(2.12)

Suppose that
\operatorname{tr}B\neq 0
, so that
\det A^{\nu}=0
for all
\nu
. Fixing a particular normal direction
\nu
, we can choose an orthonormal frame at a point on
M
such that

\displaystyle A^{\nu}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&a_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}b_{11}&b_{12}\\
b_{12}&b_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

Then we have

\displaystyle\big(I+B^{2}\big)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det\big(I+B^{2}\big)}\begin{pmatrix}1+b_{22}^{2}+b_{12}^{2}&-b_{12}(b_{11}+b_{22})\\
-b_{12}(b_{11}+b_{22})&1+b_{11}^{2}+b_{12}^{2}\end{pmatrix},

and thus equation (
2.11
) for this
\nu
gives
a_{22}\big(1+b_{11}^{2}+b_{12}^{2}\big)=0
, hence
a_{22}=0
and
A^{\nu}=0
for this
\nu
.
Therefore whenever
\det A^{\nu}=0
, we have
A^{\nu}=0
. Since this holds for all
\nu
, we are in the totally geodesic case which is covered by Proposition
2.4
.

Therefore we can assume there exists at least one
\nu
such that
\det A^{\nu}\neq 0
. From (
2.12
) we deduce that
\operatorname{tr}B=0
, so
\mu
is coclosed with respect to the induced metric. Since
\mu
is also closed by (
2.3
), we conclude that
\mu
is harmonic. Now choose at a point an orthonormal frame in which
B
is diagonal. Since
\operatorname{tr}B=0
, in such a frame we have

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\
0&-\lambda\end{pmatrix}.

But then
I+B^{2}
is a positive scalar multiple of the identity, so (
2.11
) implies that
\operatorname{tr}A^{\nu}=0
for all
\nu
, so
M^{2}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a minimal surface. Finally, equation (
2.10
) becomes
(\sin\phi)\big(1+\lambda^{2}\big)=0
, so
\sin\phi=0
.
∎

Example 2.8
.

We illustrate Proposition
2.7
with an explicit example when
n=3
. Throughout this example we identify vector fields and
1
-forms on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
using the Euclidean metric. Let
M^{2}
be a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
which is given by the graph of a smooth function
h\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
of two variables, where
\Omega
is some open set in
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is well known that the minimal surface equation in this case is

\displaystyle\big(1+h_{v}^{2}\big)h_{uu}+\big(1+h_{u}^{2}\big)h_{vv}-2h_{u}h_{v}h_{uv}=0.

(2.13)

With respect to the global frame of tangent vector fields given by
\mathsf{v}_{1}=(1,0,h_{u})
and
\mathsf{v}_{2}=(0,1,h_{v})
, the induced metric on
M^{2}
from the Euclidean metric on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
is

\displaystyle g=\begin{pmatrix}1+h_{u}^{2}&h_{u}h_{v}\\
h_{u}h_{v}&1+h_{v}^{2}\end{pmatrix}

and one can compute that for a function
f\colon\Omega\to\mathbb{R}
, thought of as function on the Riemannian manifold
(M,g)
, and writing the coordinates on
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}
as
(u_{1},u_{2})=(u,v)
, its exterior derivative is

\displaystyle{\rm d}f=f_{u}\big(g^{11}\mathsf{v}_{1}+g^{12}\mathsf{v}_{2}\big)+f_{v}\big(g^{21}\mathsf{v}_{1}+g^{22}\mathsf{v}_{2}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{{\rm d} f}{}=\frac{1}{\det g}\big(\big(1+h_{v}^{2}\big)f_{u}-h_{u}h_{v}f_{v},-h_{u}h_{v}f_{u}+\big(1+h_{u}^{2}\big)f_{v},h_{u}f_{u}+h_{v}f_{v}\big),

(2.14)

and its Laplacian is

\displaystyle\Delta_{g}f=\frac{1}{\sqrt{\det g}}\frac{\partial}{\partial u_{i}}\bigg(g^{ij}\sqrt{\det g}\frac{\partial f}{\partial u_{j}}\bigg)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Delta_g f}{}=\frac{1}{\big(1+h_{u}^{2}+h_{v}^{2}\big)}\big(\big(1+h_{v}^{2}\big)f_{uu}+\big(1+h_{u}^{2}\big)f_{vv}-2h_{u}h_{v}f_{uv}\big)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\Delta_g f=}{}-\frac{1}{(\det g)^{2}}(h_{u}f_{u}+h_{v}f_{v})\big(\big(1+h_{v}^{2}\big)h_{uu}+\big(1+h_{u}^{2}\big)h_{vv}-2h_{u}h_{v}h_{uv}\big).

(2.15)

Substituting (
2.13
) into (
2.15
) eliminates the second term. We deduce that
f
is a harmonic function on the minimal surface
M
if and only if

\displaystyle\big(1+h_{v}^{2}\big)f_{uu}+\big(1+h_{u}^{2}\big)f_{vv}-2h_{u}h_{v}f_{uv}=0.

(2.16)

Using the Euclidean metric to identify covectors with tangent vectors, the conormal space is spanned by
\nu^{*}=(-h_{u},-h_{v},1)
, and we obtain from (
2.1
) and (
2.14
) that the twisted conormal bundle
N^{*}M+df
is identified with the submanifold

\displaystyle\{(x_{1}(t,u,v),x_{2}(t,u,v),x_{3}(t,u,v),y_{1}(t,u,v),y_{2}(t,u,v),y_{3}(t,u,v)):(u,v)\in\Omega,t\in\mathbb{R}\}

in
\mathbb{R}^{6}\cong\mathbb{C}^{3}
, with coordinate functions given by

\displaystyle x_{1}=u,\qquad x_{2}=v,\qquad x_{3}=h(u,v),

\displaystyle y_{1}=-th_{u}+\frac{1}{1+h_{u}^{2}+h_{v}^{2}}\big(\big(1+h_{v}^{2}\big)f_{u}-h_{u}h_{v}f_{v}\big),

\displaystyle y_{2}=-th_{v}+\frac{1}{1+h_{u}^{2}+h_{v}^{2}}\big({-}h_{u}h_{v}f_{u}+\big(1+h_{u}^{2}\big)f_{v}\big),

\displaystyle y_{3}=t+\frac{1}{1+h_{u}^{2}+h_{v}^{2}}(h_{u}f_{u}+h_{v}f_{v}).

Proposition
2.7
says that if the two functions
h
and
f
satisfy the pair of equations (
2.13
) and (
2.16
), then the immersion of the open set
\Omega\times\mathbb{R}
in
\mathbb{R}^{2}\times\mathbb{R}
is a special Lagrangian submanifold of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
with phase
{\rm e}^{{\rm i}\frac{\pi}{2}}
.

Note that in particular, if we choose
f=h
then the pair of equations (
2.13
) and (
2.16
) coincide. For example, we can take
h(u,v)=\arctan{\frac{v}{u}}
, so that
M
is a helicoid in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, which is a minimal surface. Then taking

, one can compute that
(y_{1},y_{2},y_{3})
is

\displaystyle\left(\frac{v\big(t\big(1+u^{2}+v^{2}\big)-\big(u^{2}+v^{2}\big)\big)}{\big(u^{2}+v^{2}\big)\big(1+u^{2}+v^{2}\big)},-\frac{u\big(t(1+u^{2}+v^{2})-\big(u^{2}+v^{2}\big)\big)}{\big(u^{2}+v^{2}\big)\big(1+u^{2}+v^{2}\big)},\frac{1+t\big(1+u^{2}+v^{2}\big)}{\big(1+u^{2}+v^{2}\big)}\right).

The authors verified directly that the above is a special Lagrangian submanifold of
\mathbb{C}^{3}
with phase

. Of course, even over the helicoid, there are infinitely many more solutions. Given

, a computation on Maple shows that the general solution to (

\displaystyle f=A_{1}\left(\arctan\frac{v}{u}+\frac{1}{2}\arcsin\big(1+2u^{2}+2v^{2}\big)\right)

\displaystyle\hphantom{f =}{}+A_{2}\left(\arctan\frac{v}{u}-\frac{1}{2}\arcsin\big(1+2u^{2}+2v^{2}\big)\right),

where
A_{1}
,
A_{2}
are arbitrary
C^{2}
functions of one variable. The solution

corresponds to

3
Twisted-austere 3-folds

Because the special Lagrangian
n
-folds for
M
totally geodesic arise by taking products of lower-dimensional examples with a flat factor, we generally exclude the case where

is totally geodesic from now on.

In this section we state and prove the first of our two main theorems, which characterizes a twisted-austere pair
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
when

is a
3
-dimensional submanifold of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that is not totally geodesic. There are only two possibilities.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(M,\mu)
be a twisted-austere pair where

is
not totally geodesic
, and let
\phi
be as in (

.
Then
\cos\phi\neq 0
, and either

(i)

n
is arbitrary and
M
is ruled by lines, or else

(ii)

n=5
and

is a generalized helicoid swept out by planes in

The proof of Theorem

takes up this entire section, and we break it up into a sequence of propositions, all of which share the assumptions of Theorem
3.1
.

Proposition 3.2
.

We have
\det(A^{\nu})=0
for all normal directions
\nu
, and moreover
\cos\phi\neq 0
.

Proof.

Recall from Theorem
2.2
that, in addition to
{\rm d}\mu=0
which just says that
B
is symmetric, the twisted-austere conditions for
3

are

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\big({\rm e}^{\mathrm{i}\phi}\det C\big)=0,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\big(\sigma_{1}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,

(3.2)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Im}\big(\sigma_{2}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,

(3.3)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\big(\sigma_{3}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)\big)=0,

(3.4)

where

Using

\displaystyle(1-\sigma_{2}(B))\sin\phi+(\sigma_{1}(B)-\sigma_{3}(B))\cos\phi=0.

(3.5)

Next, note that we can expand conditions (
3.2
) and (

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)=\dfrac{\sigma_{1}(A^{\nu}(I-\operatorname{adj}B))+2\mathrm{i}\{A^{\nu},B\}}{\det C},

(3.6)

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}\big(A^{\nu}C^{-1}\big)=\dfrac{\sigma_{2}(A^{\nu})+\mathrm{i}\sigma_{1}(B\operatorname{adj}A^{\nu})}{\det C},

(3.7)

where

denotes the symmetric bilinear form corresponding to
\sigma_{2}
on the space

of

symmetric matrices (that is,

for all

). A general version (for

and the identity (

Suppose that

. We will derive a contradiction. The last condition (

\displaystyle\operatorname{Re}\det C=1-\sigma_{2}(B)=0.

(3.8)

By (

)

is purely imaginary, thus substituting (

) into (

) implies that

, while substituting (

. Together with (

), these in turn imply that

parametrizes a line on the quadric hypersurface in

defined by

. However, by Remark

, the signature of

on

is
(1,5)
. It is well-known (and easy to check) that this implies that the hypersurface contains no lines. Hence

, which contradicts our assumption that

.

Now suppose that

. Then (

) implies again that
\operatorname{Re}\det C=1-\sigma_{2}(B)=0
, so as before we conclude that

, contradicting our assumption that

is not totally geodesic.
∎

For use below, we note that multiplying the numerator and denominator of the right-hand side of (

Similarly, assuming (

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(A^{\nu})\sin\phi+\sigma_{1}(B\operatorname{adj}A^{\nu})\cos\phi=0.

.

The second fundamental form

cannot have rank one for any normal direction

Proof.

Suppose

. We will obtain a contradiction. There is a frame with respect to which

\displaystyle A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\

Substituting

\displaystyle(B_{22}+B_{33})\sin\phi+\big(B_{22}B_{33}-B_{23}^{2}-1\big)\cos\phi=0.

\displaystyle\big(\big(1+B_{23}^{2}-B_{22}B_{33}\big)B_{11}+B_{13}^{2}B_{22}+B_{22}+B_{33}\big)\cos\phi

\displaystyle\qquad{}-\big((B_{22}+B_{33})B_{11}-B_{13}^{2}+B_{22}B_{33}-B_{23}^{2}-1\big)\sin\phi=0.

Multiplying (

Proposition 3.4
.

At each point

, there exists an orthonormal frame with respect to which the span

\displaystyle|\operatorname{II}_{p}|=\{\nu\cdot\operatorname{II}\,|\,\forall\,\nu\in N_{p}M\}\subseteq S^{2}T^{*}_{p}M

\displaystyle\text{$(i)$\ }\mathscr{W}_{1}=\left\{\!\!\begin{pmatrix}*&*&0\\
*&*&0\\

0&0&*\\
*&*&*\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right\}.

Moreover, if

Proof.

The two possible forms for

, and the final statement is established in the first paragraph of the proof of Proposition

∎

Proposition 3.5
.

If
M
falls into case
(i)
of Proposition

, then

with respect to the same orthonormal frame.

Suppose that we are in case
(i)
. Then one can compute that equation (

\displaystyle\big(A^{\nu}_{11}A^{\nu}_{22}-(A^{\nu}_{12})^{2}\big)(\sin\phi+B_{33}\cos\phi)=0.

Since

Now suppose that we are not in case

\displaystyle A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\
0&0&0\\

Substituting

and

. Using these values

,

Proposition 3.6
.

If
M
falls into case
(i)
of Proposition
3.4
, then
M
is ruled by lines.

The proof of this proposition is relatively simple, but uses the method of moving frames. Before giving the proof, we recall some details about the frame bundle which will be needed in the proof as well as in later sections.

Let
\mathfrak{F}
be the oriented orthonormal frame bundle of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, whose fiber at a point
p
consists of all oriented orthonormal bases of
T_{p}\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We may think of a point
u
in the fiber as a matrix
U\in{\rm SO}(n)
whose columns comprise the corresponding frame. The frame bundle carries a canonical
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued
1
-form
\boldsymbol{\omega}
such that

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\omega}_{u}(\mathsf{v})=U^{-1}\pi_{*}\mathsf{v},

(3.13)

where
\pi\colon\mathfrak{F}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
is the basepoint map and we identify
\pi_{*}\mathsf{v}\in T_{\pi(u)}\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a column vector in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
in the usual way. (In other words, the entries of
\boldsymbol{\omega}_{u}(\mathsf{v})
give the coefficients of the expansion of
\pi_{*}\mathsf{v}
in terms of the frame corresponding to
u
.) In what follows let
\omega^{r}
denote the components of
\boldsymbol{\omega}
, where
1\leq r,s,t\leq n
.

Suppose
M^{k}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a submanifold and
f
is a local section of
\mathfrak{F}|_{M}
, that is a local oriented orthonormal frame field with component vector fields
\mathsf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{n}
. Then it follows from (
3.13
) that the
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued function
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}
on
M
giving the position in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies

\displaystyle{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}=\mathsf{e}_{r}f^{*}\omega^{r}.

(3.14)

In particular, if the frame
f
is
adapted to
M
in the sense that
\mathsf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{k}
span the tangent space to
M
at each point, then
f^{*}\omega^{a}=0
for
k<a\leq n
.

The frame bundle also carries a matrix-valued connection form
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
, taking value in
\mathfrak{so}(n)
, which satisfies the structure equation

\displaystyle{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\omega}=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\wedge\boldsymbol{\omega},\qquad{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\Omega}=-\boldsymbol{\Omega}\wedge\boldsymbol{\Omega}.

In terms of components, these equations read

\displaystyle{\rm d}\omega^{r}=-\omega^{r}_{s}\wedge\omega^{s},\qquad{\rm d}\omega^{r}_{s}=-\omega^{r}_{t}\wedge\omega^{t}_{s}.

(3.15)

The existence (and uniqueness) of the connection form is a special case of the existence of the Levi-Civita connection on a Riemannian manifold
N
. However, when
N=\mathbb{R}^{n}
an easy way to obtain the connection form, in terms of its components
\omega^{r}_{s}
, is to regard the members

of the frame as
\mathbb{R}^{n}
-valued functions on
\mathfrak{F}
, and resolve their exterior derivatives in terms of the frame itself:

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathsf{e}_{r}=\mathsf{e}_{s}\omega^{s}_{r}.

(3.16)

Returning to the situation of an adapted frame field
f
along a submanifold
M^{k}
, it follows from (
3.16
) that the pullbacks of the
\omega^{a}_{j}
encode the second fundamental form of
M
:

\displaystyle\operatorname{II}(\mathsf{e}_{i},\mathsf{e}_{j})=\big(\mathsf{e}_{i}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\hbox{\vrule height=0.5pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}}}\tilde{\omega}^{a}_{j}\big)\mathsf{e}_{a},

(3.17)

where we use
\mathsf{e}_{i}
,
\mathsf{e}_{j}
for
1\leq i,j\leq k
to denote the frame vector fields tangent to
M
, and the tilde accent denotes pullback by
f
.

Proof of Proposition
3.6
.

Let
f=(\mathsf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{n})
be an adapted local frame along

such that with respect to the basis
\mathsf{e}_{1}
,
\mathsf{e}_{2}
,
\mathsf{e}_{3}

We will show that the frame vector

is tangent to a ruling along

.

By Proposition

\displaystyle 0={\rm d}\tilde{\omega}^{4}_{3}=-\tilde{\omega}^{4}_{1}\wedge\tilde{\omega}^{1}_{3}-\tilde{\omega}^{4}_{2}\wedge\tilde{\omega}^{2}_{3}=\tilde{\omega}^{1}_{3}\wedge\big(A_{1j}\tilde{\omega}^{j}\big)+\tilde{\omega}^{2}_{3}\wedge\big(A_{2j}\tilde{\omega}^{j}\big),

That is,

\tilde{\omega}^{2}

Cylindrical examples

is ruled by lines or is a generalized helicoid in
\mathbb{R}^{5}

The canonical forms and connection forms on

We now consider the

Since the right-hand side of the above equation is semibasic for the projection to

4.1
) then gives

\displaystyle{\rm d}\lambda_{i}-\lambda_{j}\tilde{\omega}^{j}_{i}=B_{ij}\tilde{\omega}^{j}-u\sec\phi\big({\rm d}k_{i}-k_{j}\tilde{\omega}^{j}_{i}\big).

Expanding both sides as polynomials in
u
and comparing coefficients, we obtain

\displaystyle B_{ij}=\lambda_{ij}+uk_{ij}\sec\phi,

where we have set

\displaystyle{\rm d}\lambda_{i}=\lambda_{j}\tilde{\omega}^{j}_{i}+\lambda_{ij}\tilde{\omega}^{j},\qquad{\rm d}k_{i}=k_{j}\tilde{\omega}^{j}_{i}+k_{ij}\tilde{\omega}^{j}.

The
k_{ij}
are the components of the Hessian
\nabla^{2}k
with respect to the coframe on
\Sigma_{0}
, and the
\lambda_{ij}
are also symmetric in
i
and
j
, indicating that
\lambda=\lambda_{i}\tilde{\omega}^{i}
(which is well-defined, independent of choice of coframe) is a closed
1
-form on
\Sigma_{0}
. In terms of these tensor components, we have

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}-\tan\phi&k_{i}\sec\phi\\
k_{i}\sec\phi&\lambda_{ij}+uk_{ij}\sec\phi\end{pmatrix}.

(4.4)

Substituting (
4.2
) and (
4.4
) into the two remaining twisted-austere conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.9
), and equating powers of
u
, gives

\displaystyle\big(1+k_{2}^{2}\big)\lambda_{11}-2k_{1}k_{2}\lambda_{12}+\big(1+k_{1}^{2}\big)\lambda_{22}=-\big(k_{1}^{2}+k_{2}^{2}\big)\tan\phi,

(4.5)

\displaystyle\big(1+k_{2}^{2}\big)k_{11}-2k_{1}k_{2}k_{12}+\big(1+k_{1}^{2}\big)k_{22}=0,

(4.6)

\displaystyle\big(1+k_{2}^{2}\big)h^{a}_{11}-2k_{1}k_{2}h^{a}_{12}+\big(1+k_{1}^{2}\big)h^{a}_{22}=0.

(4.7)

We now give a geometric interpretation of the last two equations.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
\Sigma_{0}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a surface and let
k
be a smooth function on
\Sigma_{0}
. We endow
\Sigma_{0}
with the metric
g_{0}
it inherits from
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
dk
and
\nabla^{2}k
have components
k_{i}
and
k_{ij}
respectively, and let
h^{a}_{ij}
be the components of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma_{0}
, relative to an adapted orthonormal frame
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
,
e_{a}
. Let
\Sigma=\big\{(p,k(p))\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}\,|\,p\in\Sigma_{0}\big\}
be the graph of
k
. Then
\Sigma
is a minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
if and only if
k
satisfies (
4.6
) and (
4.7
).

Proof.

Let
\widehat{g}
be the pullback to
\Sigma_{0}
of the ambient metric on
\Sigma
. Then

\displaystyle\widehat{g}_{ij}=\delta_{ij}+k_{i}k_{j}.

(4.8)

Let
\omega^{i}
be the dual
1
-forms to the
e_{i}
, let
\omega^{i}_{j}
be the connection forms for the metric
g_{0}
on
\Sigma_{0}
, and let
\varphi^{i}_{j}
denote the connection forms for the metric
\widehat{g}
with respect to the
same
coframe. Differentiating (
4.8
) yields that

\displaystyle\varphi^{i}_{j}-\omega^{i}_{j}=\big(\widehat{g}^{-1}\big)^{i\ell}k_{\ell}k_{jm}\omega^{m}.

(4.9)

Letting
\widehat{\nabla}
denote the covariant derivative with respect to
\widehat{g}
, we can compute that relative to the coframe
\omega^{1}
,
\omega^{2}
, we have

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{\nabla}^{2}k\big)_{ij}=\dfrac{1}{\det\widehat{g}}k_{ij}.

It follows that equation (
4.6
) is equivalent to
\Delta_{\widehat{g}}k=0
. On the other hand, equation (
4.7
) says that the trace with respect to
\widehat{g}
of the second fundamental form of
\Sigma_{0}
vanishes. That is, the projection
\pi\colon\Sigma\to\Sigma_{0}
is harmonic.

In summary, the equations (
4.6
), (
4.7
) hold if and only if the coordinate functions on
\Sigma
are harmonic (relative to
\widehat{g}
),
which in turn is equivalent to
\Sigma
being minimal.
∎

We now geometrically interpret the remaining equation (
4.5
). Recall that the
1
-form
\lambda=\lambda_{i}\tilde{\omega}^{i}
is closed. If we introduce a local potential function
\ell
on
\Sigma_{0}
such that
{\rm d}\ell=\lambda
, then using (
4.9
) one computes that

\displaystyle\big(\widehat{\nabla}^{2}\ell\big)_{ij}=\lambda_{ij}-\big(\widehat{g}^{\,-1}\big)^{lm}\lambda_{l}k_{m}k_{ij}.

In particular, assuming that
k
satisfies (
4.6
), then equation (
4.5
) is equivalent to

\displaystyle\Delta_{\widehat{g}}\ell=-\big|\widehat{\nabla}k\big|^{2}_{\widehat{g}}\tan\phi.

Below, we will also express this condition in terms of the codifferential of
\lambda
.

Gathering together all our conclusions in this section, we have established the following result. Here we drop the hats and just use the metric on the graph of
k
, referring to the graph of
k
as
\Sigma
and its induced metric from
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
as
g
.

Theorem 4.2
.

Assume that
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
is a twisted-austere pair, and that
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is ruled by parallel lines. Then
M
is the union of lines passing through a minimal surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
. Moreover, if we choose Euclidean coordinates
x^{0},x^{1},\dots,x^{n}
such that the rulings point in the
x^{0}
-direction, then

\displaystyle\mu=\pi^{*}\lambda\!+\!\sec\phi\,{\rm d}(u(\pi^{*}k))\!-\!\tan\phi\,u{\rm d}u=\pi^{*}\lambda\!+\!\sec\phi\,((\pi^{*}k){\rm d}u\!+\!u{\rm d}(\pi^{*}k))\!-\!\tan\phi\,u{\rm d}u,

(4.10)

where
u
is the restriction of the
x^{0}
coordinate to
M
,
k
is the restriction of
x^{0}
to
\Sigma
,

\pi\colon M\to\Sigma
is the projection along the rulings, and
\lambda
is a closed
1
-form on
\Sigma
satisfying

\displaystyle*{\rm d}\!*\!\lambda=|\nabla k|^{2}\tan\phi,

(4.11)

where the Hodge star and norms used are with respect to the metric on
\Sigma
. Conversely, given a minimal surface
\Sigma\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
which is everywhere transverse to a fixed coordinate direction
\partial/\partial x^{0}
, and a
1
-form
\lambda
satisfying (
4.11
) for

being the restriction of
x^{0}
to
\Sigma
, then the union of lines through
\Sigma
parallel to this direction gives a
3
-dimensional submanifold
M
which forms a twisted-austere pair with
\mu
given by (
4.10
).

5
Examples with austere bases

In this section we will determine all examples of twisted-austere pairs
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
where the “base”
M
is
austere
but is not totally geodesic, nor a generalized helicoid. By Proposition
3.6
and our assumption that
M
is not a generalized helicoid, we know that
M
is ruled by lines. As in the previous section we let
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
be the ambient Euclidean space, and we number the orthonormal frame vectors as
(\mathsf{e}_{0},\mathsf{e}_{1},\mathsf{e}_{2},\mathsf{e}_{3},\dots\mathsf{e}_{n})
, where
\mathsf{e}_{0}
,
\mathsf{e}_{1}
,
\mathsf{e}_{2}
are tangent to
M
with
\mathsf{e}_{0}
pointing along the rulings.

The fact that
M
is ruled also follows from Bryant’s classification of austere 3-folds in Euclidean space
[
2
]
, which asserts that
M
is either a product of a minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a line, or a (possibly twisted) cone over a minimal surface in the
S^{n}
. (The twisted cone construction will be reviewed below.)

For 3-dimensional submanifolds, the austere condition amounts to minimality and
\det A^{\nu}=0
for all normal directions
\nu
. By Proposition
3.2
the twisted-austere conditions imply the determinant condition. We will now see how the twisted-austere conditions simplify in the presence of the minimality condition. With respect to the moving frame (adapted as described at the start of this section), the matrices representing
\nabla\mu
and the second fundamental form in the direction of
\mathsf{e}_{r}
(for
3\leq r\leq n
) look like

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}-\tan\phi&B_{10}&B_{20}\\
B_{10}&B_{11}&B_{12}\\
B_{20}&B_{12}&B_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^{r}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&A^{r}_{11}&A^{r}_{12}\\
0&A^{r}_{12}&-A^{r}_{11}\end{pmatrix},

(5.1)

respectively. (Note that we have incorporated the minimality condition.) Recall from the proof of Proposition
3.5
that the twisted-austere condition (
3.10
) is satisfied by the constant value of the top-left entry of
B
. In terms of these matrix entries, it is straightforward to compute that the two remaining conditions (
3.5
) and (
3.9
) are equivalent respectively to the pair of equations

\displaystyle B_{11}+B_{22}+\sin\phi\cos\phi\big(B_{10}^{2}+B_{20}^{2}\big)+\cos^{2}\phi\big(B_{20}^{2}B_{11}-2B_{10}B_{20}B_{12}+B_{10}^{2}B_{22}\big)=0

(5.2)

and

\displaystyle\big(B_{20}^{2}-B_{10}^{2}\big)A^{r}_{11}-2B_{10}B_{20}A^{r}_{12}=0.

(5.3)

If
B_{10}=B_{20}=0
, then conditions (
5.2
), (
5.3
) greatly simplify: the first becomes
B_{11}+B_{22}=0
and the second condition becomes vacuous. In this case, the tensor
B=\nabla\mu
splits as
B=-\tan\phi\big(\omega^{0}\big)^{2}+B_{ij}\omega^{i}\omega^{j}
, which is the sum of a constant multiple of the square of the arclength element along the ruling plus a quadratic form which restricts to be zero along the rulings. We will refer to this as the
split case
, and the case where one of
B_{10}
,
B_{20}
is always nonzero as the
non-split case
.

5.1
The split case

We begin by defining an exterior differential system whose integral submanifolds correspond to the adapted frame described above. In what follows, we will use index ranges
0\leq a,b,c,e\leq 2
,
1\leq i,j,k\leq 2
and
3\leq r,s\leq n
.

To an adapted frame
f
along
M
we can associate a submanifold of the orthonormal frame bundle
\mathfrak{F}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
by simply taking the image of
f\colon M\to\mathfrak{F}|M
. However, if we want to characterize submanifolds satisfying the austere conditions, we must introduce the components
A^{r}_{ab}
of the second fundamental form as extra variables, and take the image of
(f,A)
which is a submanifold of
\mathfrak{F}\times\mathcal{T}_{1}
, where
\mathcal{T}_{1}=\mathcal{S}_{3}\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
is the space of
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
-valued symmetric bilinear forms on
\mathbb{R}^{3}
. For example, if we were investigating submanifolds
M^{3}
whose second fundamental form satisfies certain algebraic conditions that defined a smooth subvariety
N\subset\mathcal{T}_{1}
, then on
\mathfrak{F}\times N
we would define
1
-forms

\displaystyle\omega^{r}_{a}-A^{r}_{ab}\omega^{b}

(where the components
A^{r}_{ab}
are taken as coordinate functions on
\mathcal{T}_{1}
) which, due to equation (
3.20
), would pull back to be zero on the image of
(f,A)
when
M
satisfies the conditions.

In our situation we want to impose conditions which also involve
\mu
, so we need to introduce the components of
\mu
and
\nabla\mu
as additional variables. Accordingly, let
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\mathcal{T}_{1}\times\mathbb{R}^{3}\times\mathcal{S}_{3}
denote the space where the tensor components
(A^{r}_{ab},\mu_{a},B_{ab})
take values, and let
N\subset\mathcal{T}_{2}
be the affine subspace defined by

\displaystyle B_{11}+B_{22}=0,\qquad B_{00}=-\tan\phi,\qquad B_{0i}=0,\qquad A^{r}_{0a}=0,\qquad A^{r}_{11}+A^{r}_{22}=0.

(5.4)

(Thus,
N
has dimension
5+2(n-2)
.) On
\mathfrak{F}\times N
define
1
-forms
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}

(taking value in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
,
\mathbb{R}^{n-2}
, and the space of
(n-2)\times 3
matrices, respectively) as follows:

\displaystyle\beta_{a}:=-{\rm d}\mu_{a}+\mu_{b}\omega^{b}_{a}+B_{ab}\omega^{b},

(5.5a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\theta}:=\big(\omega^{3},\dots,\omega^{n}\big)^{\rm T},

(5.5b)

\displaystyle\Omega^{r}_{a}:=-\omega^{r}_{a}+A^{r}_{ab}\omega^{b}.

(5.5c)

Then if
\big(M^{3},\mu\big)
is a twisted-austere pair where the base is austere and of split type, the image of
(f,A,\mu,B)
is an integral submanifold of the Pfaffian system
\mathcal{I}
generated by
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
. Because this integral submanifold lies over
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, it satisfies the independence condition
\omega^{0}\wedge\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}\neq 0
, and we will refer to integral submanifolds satisfying this condition as
admissible
. Conversely, any admissible integral submanifold of
\mathcal{I}
is generated by a moving frame along an austere
M^{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
such that
(M,\mu)
is a twisted-austere pair of split type.

Lemma 5.1
.

On any admissible integral submanifold
\widehat{M}^{3}
of
\mathcal{I}
, there are functions
p
,
q
such that

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \omega^{1}_{0}&=p\omega^{1}+q\omega^{2},\\
\omega^{2}_{0}&=-q\omega^{1}+p\omega^{2}.\end{aligned}

(5.6)

Moreover, the corresponding submanifold
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is one of the following three possibilities:

(i)

a product of a line with a surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
when
p=q=0
,

(ii)

a cone over a minimal surface in
S^{n}
when
q=0
but
p\neq 0
, or

(iii)

a twisted cone when
q\neq 0
.

Moreover, in case (iii), integral submanifolds only exist if
\sin\phi=0
.

Proof.

The system
\mathcal{I}
is algebraically generated by the component
1
-forms of
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
,
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
and their exterior derivatives, and for
\widehat{M}
to be an integral submanifold it is necessary and sufficient that this finite list of
1
-forms and 2-forms pull back to be zero on
\widehat{M}
. Moreover, in computing the generator 2-forms, any terms which are wedge products with the generator
1
-forms may be omitted. (This is known as computing ‘modulo the system
1
-forms’, denoted by
\mathcal{I}_{1}
.) For example,
{\rm d}\omega^{r}=-\Omega^{r}_{a}\wedge\omega^{a}\equiv 0
modulo
\mathcal{I}_{1}
, and hence the exterior derivative of the components of
\boldsymbol{\theta}
do not contribute any additional generator 2-forms to
\mathcal{I}
. In the same way, using (
3.15
), (
5.5b
), and (
5.5c
) we compute

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Omega^{r}_{a}

\displaystyle=-{\rm d}\omega^{r}_{a}+{\rm d}A^{r}_{ab}\wedge\omega^{b}+A^{r}_{ab}{\rm d}\omega^{b}

\displaystyle=\omega^{r}_{c}\wedge\omega^{c}_{a}+\omega^{r}_{s}\wedge\omega^{s}_{a}+{\rm d}A^{r}_{ab}\wedge\omega^{b}-A^{r}_{ab}\big(\omega^{b}_{c}\wedge\omega^{c}+\omega^{b}_{s}\wedge\omega^{s}\big)

\displaystyle\equiv A^{r}_{cb}\omega^{b}\wedge\omega^{c}_{a}+\omega^{r}_{s}\wedge A^{s}_{ac}\omega^{c}+{\rm d}A^{r}_{ab}\wedge\omega^{b}-A^{r}_{ab}\omega^{b}_{c}\wedge\omega^{c}\mod\mathcal{I}_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\big({\rm d}A^{r}_{ab}-A^{r}_{ac}\omega^{c}_{b}-A^{r}_{cb}\omega^{c}_{a}+A^{s}_{ab}\omega^{r}_{s}\big)\wedge\omega^{b}\mod\mathcal{I}_{1}.

In particular, noting the zero entries in
A^{r}
from (
5.4
) gives
{\rm d}\Omega^{r}_{0}\equiv-A^{r}_{ij}\omega^{i}_{0}\wedge\omega^{j}
. Because
A^{r}
has rank two for at least one
r
, it follows from the Cartan Lemma that on any admissible integral submanifold we have
\omega^{i}_{0}=P^{i}_{j}\omega^{j}
for some functions
P^{i}_{j}
. Substituting this into the expression for
{\rm d}\Omega^{r}_{0}
, we then find that in order for the 2-form
{\rm d}\Omega^{r}_{0}
to vanish along
\widehat{M}
, we must have

\displaystyle A^{r}_{ik}P^{k}_{j}=A^{r}_{jk}P^{k}_{i}

(5.7)

for all
i
,
j
. In other words, if we think of the
P^{i}_{j}
as entries in a
2\times 2
matrix, then
AP
must be symmetric whenever
A
is the lower-right block of a matrix
A^{r}
.

Suppose first that
|\operatorname{II}|
is
2
-dimensional on an open subset of
M
. Then the span of the lower-right blocks of the
A^{r}
includes
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\right)
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\
1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
, and substituting these into (
5.7
) shows that
P
must have the form
P=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}p&q\\
-q&p\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some functions
p
,
q
, as claimed.

On the other hand, suppose that
|\operatorname{II}|
is
1
-dimensional on
M
. Then we may adapt the frame so that, say
A^{3}=\lambda\left(\begin{smallmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
for some
\lambda\neq 0
, and
A^{t}=0
for

. In this case, one can compute that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Omega^{3}_{1}\wedge\omega^{2}-{\rm d}\Omega^{3}_{2}\wedge\omega^{1}\equiv\lambda\big(\omega^{2}_{0}\wedge\omega^{2}-\omega^{1}_{0}\wedge\omega^{1}\big)\wedge\omega^{0}

modulo the 1-forms in
\mathcal{I}
. The left hand side of this expression is in
\mathcal{I}
, and thus the right hand side must vanish when pulled back to
\widehat{M}
. Substituting
\omega^{i}_{0}=P^{i}_{j}\omega^{j}
into the right hand side gives

. Using (
5.7
) shows that
P_{1}^{1}=P_{2}^{2}
. Thus

indeed has the desired form.

From (
3.16
), and using (
5.5c
) and (
5.4
), the differential of the unit vector parallel to the ruling on

is

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathsf{e}_{0}

\displaystyle\equiv\mathsf{e}_{1}\omega^{1}_{0}+\mathsf{e}_{2}\omega^{2}_{0}\mod\mathcal{I},

\displaystyle=p\big(\mathsf{e}_{1}\omega^{1}+\mathsf{e}_{2}\omega^{2}\big)+q\big(\mathsf{e}_{1}\omega^{2}-\mathsf{e}_{2}\omega^{1}\big)\quad\text{using~\eqref{austpq}.}

Comparing this with the differential of the position vector on

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathsf{e}_{0}-p\,{\rm d}\boldsymbol{\mathrm{x}}\equiv q\big(\mathsf{e}_{1}\omega^{2}-\mathsf{e}_{2}\omega^{1}\big)\mod\mathcal{I},\omega^{0}.

In other words, if we follow a curve on

orthogonal to the rulings, the differential of the ruling direction
\mathsf{e}_{0}
is proportional to the differential of the position
if and only if

is identically zero, and the ruling direction is constant if and only if

and

are both identically zero. Hence,

is a cylinder iff

and is a cone iff

and

. That the remaining case, where

[

]
.

Additional generator 2-forms for
\mathcal{I}
are obtained by differentiating (

\displaystyle{\rm d}\beta_{a}\equiv\big({\rm d}B_{ab}-B_{ac}\omega^{c}_{b}-B_{cb}\omega^{c}_{a}\big)\wedge\omega^{b}+\mu_{b}A^{r}_{bc}A^{r}_{ae}\omega^{c}\wedge\omega^{e}\quad\mod\mathcal{I}_{1}.

(5.8)

In particular, from (

) we obtain that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\beta_{0}\equiv\tan\phi\,\omega^{0}_{i}\wedge\omega^{i}-B_{ij}\omega^{i}_{0}\wedge\omega^{j}.

Substituting (

. Thus, admissible integral manifolds with

.
∎

We now consider the three sub-cases given by Lemma

. In what follows, we will let
m=\mathsf{e}_{0}\mathbin{\raisebox{1.72218pt}{\hbox{\vrule height=0.5pt,width=5.0pt,depth=0.0pt\vrule height=3.0pt,width=0.5pt,depth=0.0pt}}}\mu=\mu_{0}
denote the slope, so that

\displaystyle\mu=m\omega^{0}+\mu_{i}\omega^{i}.

Note that from the form of

\displaystyle{\rm d}m\equiv\mu_{i}\omega^{i}_{0}-\tan\phi\omega^{0}+B_{0i}\omega^{i}\quad\mod\mathcal{I}_{1}.

5.1.1
\boldsymbol{M}
is a cylinder

In this case, we may write
M=\Sigma\times\mathbb{R}
where
\Sigma
is a minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We let

be the coordinate on the
\mathbb{R}

to denote the pullback to

\omega^{0}
of the frame vector
\mathsf{e}_{0}
. Since the

on
\widehat{M}
, equation (

) gives
\beta_{0}=-({\rm d}m+\tan\phi\,{\rm d}t)
, and the vanishing of this

is constant. Furthermore, wedging (

) with

and summing over

{\rm d}\big(\mu_{1}\omega^{1}+\mu_{2}\omega^{2}\big)\equiv 0
modulo

,
\beta_{2}
, which shows that
\check{\mu}=\mu-m\,{\rm d}t
is a well-defined closed

, and it is easy to check that
\check{\mu}
is harmonic. The converse also holds:

Theorem 5.2
.

Let
\Sigma
is an arbitrary minimal surface in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
\check{\mu}
a harmonic
1
-form on
\Sigma
and let
m
be a linear function with derivative

, together with

Proof.

This is a special case of the construction in Theorem

with

∎

5.1.2
\boldsymbol{M}
is a cone

In this case we assume that

identically. We will show in Remark

,
\omega^{0}_{2}

To this end, let

, and on this space define

\displaystyle\alpha_{1}:=\omega^{0}_{1}+p\omega^{1},\qquad\alpha_{2}:=\omega^{0}_{2}+p\omega^{2}.

(5.9)

One can compute that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\alpha_{1}\equiv\big({\rm d}p+p^{2}\omega^{0}\big)\wedge\omega^{1},\qquad{\rm d}\alpha_{2}\equiv\big({\rm d}p+p^{2}\omega^{0}\big)\wedge\omega^{2}

modulo
\mathcal{I}
,
\alpha_{1}
,

\displaystyle S_{1}:=2B_{10}^{3}u_{2}-3B_{10}^{2}p(B_{22}+\tan\phi)+p\tan\phi\sec^{2}\phi.

We then differentiate the above expression and again use (
5.17
) to compute

\displaystyle{\rm d}S_{1}\wedge\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}+3\sec^{2}\phi\bigg(\Upsilon-\dfrac{B_{10}}{2h}{\rm d}\alpha_{5}\bigg)\wedge\omega^{2}+5B_{10}^{3}{\rm d}\alpha_{4}\wedge\omega^{1}

\displaystyle\qquad{}\equiv 4\big({-}11pB_{10}^{3}u_{2}+9B_{10}^{2}p^{2}(B_{22}+\tan\phi)+2p^{2}\tan\phi\sec^{2}\phi\big)\omega^{0}\wedge\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}.

This yields a second polynomial integrability condition, and eliminating
B_{22}
between the two polynomials shows that all integral submanifolds must lie in the zero locus of

\displaystyle S_{2}:=B_{10}^{3}u_{2}-p\tan\phi\sec^{2}\phi.

Differentiating the above expression and again using (
5.17
), we obtain that

\displaystyle{\rm d}S_{2}\wedge\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}+B_{10}^{3}{\rm d}\alpha_{4}\wedge\omega^{1}\equiv p\big({-}7B_{10}^{3}u_{2}+p\tan\phi\sec^{2}\phi\big)\omega^{0}\wedge\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2}.

This last polynomial cannot vanish at the same time as
S_{2}
unless
\tan\phi=0
.
∎

As in Section
5.1.2
, we conclude that the slope
m
is a well-defined function on the minimal surface
\Sigma
inside
S^{3}
, and satisfies
\Delta m=-2m
. However, in this case
m
and
\Sigma
turn out
not
to be arbitrary. This is because the computations in the proof of Lemma
5.11
imply that
u_{2}=0
and
B_{22}=0
identically on all such solutions. Consequently, the system
\mathcal{I}^{+}
simplifies. For example, equation (
5.17
) along with the vanishing of
\alpha_{3}
,
\alpha_{4}
,
\alpha_{5}
now implies that
B_{10}^{2}
is a constant multiple of
hp^{3}
.

After an additional prolongation step, we obtain a Frobenius system. This means that solutions of this type are determined by solving systems of ODE, and so depend on finitely many constants. While leaving the details for the interested reader, the end result is that, up to a rigid motion, the surface
\Sigma
is the torus in
S^{3}
that is parametrized by

\displaystyle(t,u)\mapsto[\cos(t)\cos(au),\cos(t)\sin(au),\sin(t)\cos(u/a),\sin(t)\sin(u/a)],

where
a
is a positive constant. (Notice that the surface is compact if
a^{2}
is rational.) The slope function is given by

\displaystyle m=(c_{1}\cos(au)+c_{2}\sin(au))\cos(t)+(c_{3}\cos(u/a)+c_{4}\sin(u/a))\sin(t),

while on
M
, the
1
-form is
\mu={\rm d}(ms)+c_{5}{\rm d}u
. (Here,
c_{1},\dots,c_{5}
are arbitrary constants. However, since
m
is a linear combination of the
\mathbb{R}^{4}
coordinates of
\Sigma
, the constant
c_{5}
should be chosen to be nonzero so that the resulting special Lagrangian submanifold is not just a translation of
N^{*}M
.)

6
Classification results

In this section, we prove that the only examples of twisted-austere 3-folds in Euclidean space are precisely those that we have already discussed. More precisely we have the following result.

Theorem 6.1
.

Let
(M,\mu)
be a twisted-austere pair, where
M^{3}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
is not totally geodesic. Then either
M
is austere or
M
is a cylinder.

Proof.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.11
, we describe all the steps and leave the details to the reader.

By Proposition
3.5
we can assume that
M
falls into case
(i)
of Proposition
3.4
, because otherwise
M
is an austere generalized helicoid. Thus, as we did in Section
5
, we may adapt a moving frame
(\mathsf{e}_{0},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{n})
along
M
so that
\nabla\mu
and the second fundamental form in the direction of
\mathsf{e}_{r}
(for
3\leq r\leq n)
are represented respectively by

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}-\tan\phi&v_{1}&v_{2}\\
v_{1}&B_{11}&B_{12}\\
v_{2}&B_{12}&B_{22}\end{pmatrix},\qquad A^{r}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&A^{r}_{11}&A^{r}_{12}\\
0&A^{r}_{12}&A^{r}_{22}\end{pmatrix}.

In terms of these, the twisted-austere condition (
3.9
) takes the form

\displaystyle\mathsf{v}^{\rm T}\operatorname{adj}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{r})\mathsf{v}+\sec^{2}\phi\operatorname{tr}(\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{r})=0,

(6.1)

where
\mathsf{v}^{\rm T}=\begin{bmatrix}v_{1}&v_{2}\end{bmatrix}
and
\boldsymbol{\mathrm{a}}^{r}
is the lower-right
2\times 2
block of
A^{r}
. If
v_{1}=v_{2}=0
identically then (
6.1
) implies that
M
is minimal, and hence austere since
\det(A^{r})=0
already. Thus, we will assume from now on that
M
is not minimal, and hence that one of
v_{1}
,
v_{2}
is nonzero at each point.

Because equation (
6.1
) is linear condition on
A^{r}
, we see that
\dim|\operatorname{II}|\leq 2
at each point. First, we will assume that
|\operatorname{II}|
is 2-dimensional on an open set in
M
, and we will adapt the frame, by rotating the normal vectors, so that
A^{r}=0
for
r>4
. (The case where
|\operatorname{II}|
is 1-dimensional, including the case where
M\subset\mathbb{R}^{4}
, will be discussed later.) By Lemma
3.3
we know that
A^{r}
has rank 2 for some
r
. We now further adapt the frame by rotating
\mathsf{e}_{1}
,
\mathsf{e}_{2}
so that
v_{2}=0
identically. With this adaptation, equation (
6.1
) now reads

\displaystyle v_{1}^{2}A^{r}_{22}+\sec^{2}\phi\big(A^{r}_{11}+A^{r}_{22}\big)=0,\qquad r=3,4.

Thus the vectors
\big[A^{3}_{11},A^{3}_{22}\big]
and
\big[A^{4}_{11},A^{4}_{22}\big]
are linearly dependent, and we may rotate frame vectors
\mathsf{e}_{3}
,
\mathsf{e}_{4}
so as to arrange that the diagonal entries of
A^{4}
are zero.

We now consider the Pfaffian system for which this moving frame corresponds to an admissible integral manifold. As in Section
5
we let
\mathfrak{F}
be the frame bundle of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
, and on
N=\mathbb{R}^{11}
we take coordinates
\mu_{a}
,
v_{1}>0
,
B_{ij}
,
A^{3}_{ij}
and
A^{4}_{12}
. (We will not yet impose conditions (
6.1
) on the components of
A^{3}
, or impose (
5.2
) on the components of
B
.) We define the
1
-forms
\beta_{a}
,
\theta^{r}
, and
\Omega^{r}_{a}
for
0\leq a\leq 2
and
3\leq r\leq n
as in (
5.5
), and let
\mathcal{I}
be the Pfaffian system on
\mathfrak{F}\times N
generated by
\boldsymbol{\beta}
,
\boldsymbol{\theta}
, and
\boldsymbol{\Omega}
.

As in the proof of Lemma
5.1
, we compute that
{\rm d}\Omega^{r}_{0}\equiv-A^{r}_{ij}\omega^{i}_{0}\wedge\omega^{j}
mod
\mathcal{I}_{1}
. Our assumption that
M
is not minimal implies that
A^{3}
has rank two, and thus by the Cartan Lemma on any admissible integral manifold there must be functions
P_{ij}=P_{ji}
such that
\omega^{i}_{0}=P_{ij}A^{3}_{jk}\omega^{k}
. (Note that these coefficients are
different
from the
P^{i}_{j}
introduced in the proof of Lemma
5.1
.) Using this, we can compute that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\Omega^{4}_{0}\equiv A^{4}_{12}\big(A^{3}_{22}P_{22}-A^{3}_{11}P_{11}\big)\omega^{1}\wedge\omega^{2},

and thus there must be a function
p
such that
P_{11}=pA^{3}_{22}
and
P_{22}=pA^{3}_{11}
.

As in Sections
5.1.2
and
5.1.3
, we construct a partial prolongation
\mathcal{I}^{+}
by adjoining the
1
-forms

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}:=\omega^{0}_{i}+P_{ij}A^{3}_{jk}\omega^{k},\qquad\text{with}\quad P_{11}=pA^{3}_{22},\quad P_{22}=pA^{3}_{11}

defined on
\mathfrak{F}\times N\times\mathbb{R}^{2}
, with
p
and
P_{12}
as additional variables. Computing the system 2-forms then uncovers the additional integrability condition
P_{12}=-pA^{3}_{12}
. Restricting the system
\mathcal{I}^{+}
to the submanifold where this condition holds, one computes that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathsf{e}_{0}\equiv p\big(A^{3}_{11}A^{3}_{22}-\big(A^{3}_{12}\big)^{2}\big)\big(\mathsf{e}_{1}\omega^{1}+\mathsf{e}_{2}\omega^{2}\big)\mod\mathcal{I}^{+},

indicating that
M
must be a cone over a surface in
S^{n}
if
p\neq 0
, or a cylinder if
p=0
identically.

Imposing the twisted-austere condition (
6.1
) amounts to restricting to the smooth submanifold where the polynomial
Q_{0}:=v_{1}^{2}A^{3}_{22}+\sec^{2}\phi\big(A^{3}_{11}+A^{3}_{22}\big)
vanishes. We can compute that

\displaystyle{\rm d}Q_{0}\equiv-p\big(A^{3}_{11}A^{3}_{22}-\big(A^{3}_{12}\big)^{2}\big)\big(Q_{0}+4v_{1}^{2}A^{3}_{22}\big)\omega^{0}\mod\mathcal{I}^{+},\,\omega^{1},\,\omega^{2}.

Thus, admissible integral submanifolds lying within this locus must also have
p=0
(in which case
M
is a cylinder) or
v_{1}A^{3}_{22}=0
(in which case
Q_{0}=0
implies that
M
is minimal).

Now consider the case where
\dim|\operatorname{II}|=1
at each point. We will not initially assume that
v_{2}=0
. We again define the partial prolongation
\mathcal{I}^{+}
by adjoining
1
-forms
\alpha_{i}:=\omega^{0}_{i}+P_{ij}A^{3}_{jk}\omega^{k}
, but now we cannot assume any relations among the
P_{ij}
other than
P_{ij}=P_{ji}
. Again, to impose (
6.1
) we must restrict to the zero locus of

\displaystyle Q_{0}:=v_{1}^{2}A^{3}_{22}+v_{2}^{2}A^{3}_{11}-2v_{1}v_{2}A^{3}_{12}+\sec^{2}\phi\big(A^{3}_{11}+A^{3}_{22}\big).

By computing
{\rm d}Q_{k}\equiv Q_{k+1}\omega^{0}\mod\mathcal{I}^{+}
,
\omega^{1}
,
\omega^{2}
, we obtain additional polynomials
Q_{1}
,
Q_{2}
,
Q_{3}
in whose zero locus any admissible integral manifold must lie. By rotating the frame we can arrange that
v_{2}=0
. This simplifies the polynomials, and we find that we must have
P_{11}=0
or
\det(P_{ij})=0
on the common zero locus.

Setting
P_{11}=0
and
v_{2}=0
in
Q_{1}
implies that
P_{12}=0
, and then substituting these in
Q_{2}
implies that
P_{22}=0
, and thus in this case
M
is a cylinder. Therefore if
M
is not a cylinder, we must have
\det(P_{ij})=0
. This condition is invariant under rotating the vectors
\mathsf{e}_{1}
,
\mathsf{e}_{2}
, so in this case we may arrange that
P_{12}=P_{22}=0
instead of
v_{2}=0
. Substituting in
Q_{2}
yields
A^{3}_{11}=0
, and substituting these values into
Q_{1}
gives either
A^{3}_{12}=0
or
3v_{1}^{2}=\sec^{2}\phi
. If
A^{3}_{12}=0
then substituting into
Q_{0}
yields that
M
is totally geodesic. In the remaining case we have
P_{12}=P_{22}=A^{3}_{11}=0
and
v_{1}
has a constant value. Computing the prolongation of
\mathcal{I}^{+}
in this case yields additional integrability conditions that imply

must be totally geodesic.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

In light of Proposition
3.5
and the classification theorem just proved, the only remaining possibility for twisted-austere 3-folds other than those discussed in Sections
4
and
5
is that
M
is a generalized helicoid in
\mathbb{R}^{5}
. However, in this case the only possible values for the
1
-form
\mu
produce, via the Borisenko construction, a special Lagrangian submanifold in
\mathbb{R}^{10}
that is a translation of the conormal bundle of

by a constant vector.

Appendix A
Appendix

In this appendix, we collect some linear algebraic results that are needed in the main body of the paper. These include two identities relating the elementary symmetric polynomials with the operation of taking the adjugate matrix, as well as some results on the spans of singular symmetric matrices.

A.1
Identities relating
\boldsymbol{\sigma_{j}}
and
\boldsymbol{\operatorname{adj}}

In this section we prove the two fundamental identities (
3.6
) and (
3.7
) that are crucially used in our classification. We prove a more general result than (
3.7
) valid for any
k
, whereas for (
3.6
) we restrict to the case
k=3
.

We first recall some basic facts about the elementary symmetric polynomials and the adjugate matrix, to fix notation. Let
A
be a
k\times k
matrix with complex entries. For
j=0,\dots,k
we define the
j^{\text{th}}
elementary symmetric polynomial
\sigma_{j}(A)
of
A
by the expression

\displaystyle\det(I+tA)=\sum_{j=0}^{k}t^{j}\sigma_{j}(A).

(A.1)

It is clear from (
A.1
) that
\sigma_{j}\big(P^{-1}AP\big)=\sigma_{j}(A)
for all
j
. In particular we have
\sigma_{0}(A)=1
,
\sigma_{1}(A)=\operatorname{tr}A
, and
\sigma_{k}(A)=\det A
. Moreover, each
\sigma_{j}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
j
in the entries of
A
, so
\sigma_{j}(\lambda A)=\lambda^{j}\sigma_{j}(A)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

Suppose that
A
is invertible. Then we can compute

\displaystyle\det\big(I+tA^{-1}\big)=\det\big(tA^{-1}\big(I+t^{-1}A\big)\big)=t^{k}(\det A)^{-1}\sum_{j=0}^{k}\big(t^{-1}\big)^{j}\sigma_{j}(A)

\displaystyle\hphantom{\det\big(I + t A^{-1}\big)}{}=\frac{1}{(\det A)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}t^{k-j}\sigma_{j}(A)=\frac{1}{(\det A)}\sum_{j=0}^{k}t^{j}\sigma_{k-j}(A).

We deduce from the above and (
A.1
) that

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\big(A^{-1}\big)=\frac{1}{\det A}\sigma_{k-j}(A).

(A.2)

The
adjugate matrix

\operatorname{adj}A
is the unique
k\times k
matrix satisfying

\displaystyle(\operatorname{adj}A)A=A(\operatorname{adj}A)=(\det A)I.

It is clear that
\operatorname{adj}\big(P^{-1}AP\big)=P^{-1}(\operatorname{adj}A)P
. Moreover,
\operatorname{adj}A
is homogeneous of order
k-1
, so
\operatorname{adj}(\lambda A)=\lambda^{k-1}\operatorname{adj}A
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{C}
.

If
A
is invertible then
\operatorname{adj}A=(\det A)A^{-1}
. We can use (
A.2
) and the homogeneity of
\sigma_{j}
to compute that
\sigma_{j}(\operatorname{adj}A)=\sigma_{j}\big((\det A)A^{-1}\big)=(\det A)^{j}\sigma_{j}\big(A^{-1}\big)=(\det A)^{j-1}\sigma_{k-j}(A)
. By the density of invertible matrices we conclude that

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}(\operatorname{adj}A)=(\det A)^{j-1}\sigma_{k-j}(A)\qquad\text{for all $A$.}

(A.3)

Note that the above is well-defined for all
A
even when
j=0
, in which case it just says
1=1
.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
A
and
C
be
k\times k
complex matrices with
C
invertible. Then we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\big(AC^{-1}\big)=(\det A)^{j+1-k}(\det C)^{-1}\sigma_{k-j}(C\operatorname{adj}A).

Proof.

Assume first that
A
is invertible. Using (
A.2
), we compute

\displaystyle\sigma_{j}\big(AC^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\sigma_{j}\big(\big(CA^{-1}\big)^{-1}\big)=\frac{1}{\det\big(CA^{-1}\big)}\sigma_{k-j}\big(CA^{-1}\big)

\displaystyle=\frac{\det A}{\det C}\sigma_{k-j}\big((\det A)^{-1}C\operatorname{adj}A\big)=\frac{\det A}{\det C}(\det A)^{-(k-j)}\sigma_{k-j}(C\operatorname{adj}A)

\displaystyle=(\det A)^{j+1-k}(\det C)^{-1}\sigma_{k-j}(C\operatorname{adj}A),

as claimed. The result follows for all matrices
A
by the density of invertible matrices.
∎

Proposition A.2
.

Let
A
and
B
be
k\times k

real
matrices, and assume that
B
is symmetric. Let
C=I+{\rm i}B
. Then
C
is invertible and we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{k-1}\big(AC^{-1}\big)=\frac{\sigma_{k-1}(A)+{\rm i}\sigma_{1}(B\operatorname{adj}A)}{\det C}.

(A.4)

Proof.

The result (
A.4
) we seek to prove is similarity invariant, so we can assume by the spectral theorem that
B
is diagonal with real eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{k}
. But then
C=I+{\rm i}B
is diagonal with nonzero eigenvalues
1+{\rm i}\lambda_{1},\dots,1+{\rm i}\lambda_{k}
, and hence invertible. Applying Lemma
A.1
with
j=k-1
gives

\displaystyle\sigma_{k-1}\big(AC^{-1}\big)=(\det C)^{-1}\sigma_{1}(C\operatorname{adj}A).

But
\sigma_{1}=\operatorname{tr}
is linear, so
\sigma_{1}(C\operatorname{adj}A)=\sigma_{1}((I+{\rm i}B)\operatorname{adj}A)=\sigma_{1}(\operatorname{adj}A)+{\rm i}\sigma_{1}(B\operatorname{adj}A)
. The proof is completed upon noting that
\sigma_{1}(\operatorname{adj}A)=\sigma_{k-1}(A)
from (
A.3
).
∎

The next result is used to establish the second fundamental identity of this section.

Lemma A.3
.

Let
B
be a symmetric
3\times 3
real matrix and let
z\in\mathbb{C}
. Then we have

\displaystyle\operatorname{adj}(I+zB)=I+z(\sigma_{1}(B)I-B)+z^{2}\operatorname{adj}B.

(A.5)

Proof.

By similarity invariance, we can assume that
B
is diagonal with real entries. We compute explicitly that

\displaystyle B=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0&0\\
0&\mu&0\\
0&0&\nu\end{pmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{adj}B=\begin{pmatrix}\mu\nu&0&0\\
0&\lambda\nu&0\\
0&0&\lambda\mu\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle I+zB=\begin{pmatrix}1+z\lambda&0&0\\
0&1+z\mu&0\\
0&0&1+z\nu\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle\operatorname{adj}(I+zB)=\begin{pmatrix}(1+z\mu)(1+z\nu)&0&0\\
0&(1+z\lambda)(1+z\nu)&0\\
0&0&(1+z\lambda)(1+z\mu)\end{pmatrix}.

Then (
A.5
) can be directly verified. For example, the
(1,1)
entry gives

\displaystyle(1+z\mu)(1+z\nu)=1+z((\lambda+\mu+\nu)-\lambda)+z^{2}\mu\nu,

which is clearly true.
∎

Before we can state the final result of this section, we need to introduce some more notation. It is well-known that

\displaystyle\sigma_{2}(A)=\frac{1}{2}(\sigma_{1}(A))^{2}-\frac{1}{2}\sigma_{1}\big(A^{2}\big).

This is the simplest of
Newton’s identities
. It can be verified directly for a diagonal matrix, which implies the general case because the diagonalizable matrices are dense. By homogeneity,
\sigma_{2}
is a quadratic form on the space of matrices, and by polarization we obtain an induced symmetric bilinear form, which we denote by
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
. Explicitly,

\displaystyle 2\{A,B\}=\sigma_{1}(A)\sigma_{1}(B)-\sigma_{1}(AB).

(A.6)

Remark A.4
.

The positive-definite Frobenius norm
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
on real matrices is given by
\langle A,B\rangle=\sigma_{1}\big(A^{\rm T}B\big)=\operatorname{tr}\big(A^{\rm T}B\big)=\sum_{i,j}A_{ij}B_{ij}
. Note that
\operatorname{tr}A=\langle A,I\rangle
. Thus the traceless symmetric matrices are orthogonal to the identity matrix
I
with respect to
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
. Let
A
,
B
be symmetric. We can write
A=\frac{1}{k}(\operatorname{tr}A)I+A_{0}
where
A_{0}
is traceless and similarly for
B
. Then using (
A.6
) we have

\displaystyle 2\{A,B\}

\displaystyle=(\operatorname{tr}A)(\operatorname{tr}B)-\langle A,B\rangle=(\operatorname{tr}A)(\operatorname{tr}B)-\frac{1}{k^{2}}(\operatorname{tr}A)(\operatorname{tr}B)\langle I,I\rangle-\langle A_{0},B_{0}\rangle

\displaystyle=\frac{k-1}{k}(\operatorname{tr}A)(\operatorname{tr}B)-\langle A_{0},B_{0}\rangle.

The above computation shows that for
k>1
the symmetric bilinear form
\{\cdot,\cdot\}
is a
Lorentzian
inner product on the space of symmetric
k\times k
real matrices, that is, with signature
\big(1,\frac{k(k+1)}{2}-1\big)
. This fact is used several times in this paper.

Proposition A.5
.

Let
A
and
B
be
3\times 3

real
matrices, and assume that
A
is invertible and that
B
is symmetric. Let
C=I+{\rm i}B
. Then
C
is invertible and we have

\displaystyle\sigma_{1}\big(AC^{-1}\big)=\frac{\sigma_{1}(A(I-\operatorname{adj}B))+2{\rm i}\{A,B\}}{\det C}.

Proof.

The invertibility of
C
was proved in Proposition
A.2
. Applying Lemma
A.3
with
z={\rm i}
gives

\displaystyle\operatorname{adj}C=\operatorname{adj}(I+{\rm i}B)=I+{\rm i}\sigma_{1}(B)I-{\rm i}B-\operatorname{adj}B.

Multiplying both sides on the left by
A
and writing
\operatorname{adj}C=(\det C)C^{-1}
gives

\displaystyle(\det C)AC^{-1}=A+{\rm i}\sigma_{1}(B)A-{\rm i}AB-A\operatorname{adj}B.

Taking
\sigma_{1}=\operatorname{tr}
of both sides and using linearity gives

\displaystyle(\det C)\sigma_{1}\big(AC^{-1}\big)=\sigma_{1}(A-A\operatorname{adj}B)+{\rm i}(\sigma_{1}(A)\sigma_{1}(B)-\sigma_{1}(AB)).

Using (
A.6
) completes the proof.
∎

A.2
Spans of singular symmetric matrices

Let
\mathcal{S}_{n}
denote the space of
n\times n
symmetric matrices with real entries, and let
\mathcal{D}_{n}\subset\mathcal{S}_{n}
be the affine variety of symmetric matrices with vanishing determinant. We determine the maximal linear subspaces of
\mathcal{D}_{n}
up to
O(n)
-conjugation, for
n=2
and
n=3
.

Proposition A.6
.

Let
\mathscr{W}\subset\mathcal{D}_{2}
be a maximal linear subspace. Then
\dim\mathscr{W}=1
and is
O(2)
-conjugate to the span of
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.

Proof.

On the space
\mathcal{S}_{2}
, the determinant is a quadratic form with signature
(1,2)
, and thus
\mathcal{D}_{2}
contains no linear subspaces of dimension greater than one. The result follows by diagonalization.
∎

Proposition A.7
.

Let
\mathscr{W}\subset\mathcal{D}_{3}
be a maximal linear subspace. Then
\mathscr{W}
is
3
-dimensional and is
O(3)
-conjugate to one of

\displaystyle\mathscr{W}_{1}=\left\{\!\!\begin{pmatrix}*&*&0\\
*&*&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right\},\qquad\mathscr{W}_{2}=\left\{\!\!\begin{pmatrix}*&*&*\\
*&0&0\\
*&0&0\end{pmatrix}\!\!\right\}.

Proof.

Let
\mathscr{V}\subset\mathcal{D}_{3}
be an arbitrary linear subspace. If
\dim\mathscr{V}=1
then by diagonalization
\mathscr{V}
is conjugate to a subspace of
\mathscr{W}_{1}
. Thus we can assume
\dim\mathscr{V}\geq 2
.

Case one:
Suppose
\mathscr{V}
contains a rank one matrix
A_{0}
. By
O(3)
-conjugation we can assume that

\displaystyle A_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}=\{B\in\mathscr{V}\,|\,B_{11}=0\}
. Then for any
B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
, by expansion along the top row, we have

\displaystyle 0=\det(B+tA_{0})=\big(B_{22}B_{33}-B_{23}^{2}\big)t+\det B\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(A.7)

Let
p\colon\mathscr{V}^{\prime}\to\mathcal{S}_{2}
denote the linear projection that gives the lower-right
2\times 2
block. Then from the vanishing of the leading coefficient in (
A.7
), we see that
p(\mathscr{V}^{\prime})\subset\mathcal{D}_{2}
. By Proposition
A.6
, we know
\dim p(\mathscr{V}^{\prime})\leq 1
. If
p(\mathscr{V}^{\prime})=\{0\}
then
\mathscr{V}\subseteq\mathscr{W}_{2}
. If not then we can assume by conjugation that
B_{23}=B_{33}=0
for all
B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
. Now equation (
A.7
) reads
0=B_{22}B_{13}^{2}
. If
B_{22}=0
for all
B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
then
\mathscr{V}=\{A_{0}\}_{\mathbb{R}}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{W}_{2}
. If not, then
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
contains a matrix with
B_{13}=0
and hence by scaling a matrix
B_{0}
of the form

\displaystyle B_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}0&\lambda&0\\
\lambda&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}.

In this case, let
\mathscr{V}^{\prime\prime}=\ker p=\{B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime}\,|\,B_{22}=0\}.
Then for any
B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime\prime}
we can compute that

\displaystyle 0=\det(B+tB_{0})=-B_{13}^{2}t,\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus,
B_{13}=0
for all
B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime\prime}
, so
\mathscr{V}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathscr{W}_{1}
and
\mathscr{V}=\{A_{0}\}_{\mathbb{R}}\oplus\{B_{0}\}_{\mathbb{R}}\oplus\mathscr{V}^{\prime\prime}\subset\mathscr{W}_{1}
.

Case two:
Suppose that
\mathscr{V}
contains no rank one matrices. Thus every nonzero matrix in
\mathscr{V}
has exactly two nonzero eigenvalues and one zero eigenvalue. Hence, the space
\mathscr{V}
does not intersect the cone defined by the equation
\sigma_{2}(A)=0
except at the origin in
\mathcal{S}_{3}
. Since
\sigma_{2}
is a quadratic form on
\mathcal{S}_{3}
with signature
(1,5)
, and
\mathscr{V}
has dimension at least two, the restriction of
\sigma_{2}
to
\mathscr{V}
will be negative on an open subset in
\mathscr{V}
. If
\sigma_{2}>0
anywhere in
\mathscr{V}
, then by continuity there would be a nonzero
A\in\mathscr{V}
such that
\sigma_{2}(A)=0
. But we have ruled that out in this case, so we conclude that
\sigma_{2}(A)<0
for all nonzero
A\in\mathscr{V}
.

Now fix a rank two matrix
A_{1}
in
\mathscr{V}
. Since
\sigma_{2}(A_{1})<0
, it has one positive and one negative eigenvalue. Thus we can assume using
O(3)
-conjugation that
A_{1}
takes the form

\displaystyle A_{1}=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&-\lambda^{2}&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad\lambda>0.

Let
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}=\{B\in\mathscr{V}\,|\,B_{11}=0\}
. Then for
B\in\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
we must have

\displaystyle 0=\det(B+tA_{1})=-\lambda^{2}B_{33}t^{2}+\big(B_{22}B_{33}-B_{23}^{2}+\lambda^{2}B_{13}^{2}\big)t+\det(B),\qquad\forall\,t\in\mathbb{R}.

(A.8)

Thus,
B_{33}=0
and
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
either lies in the subspace where
B_{23}=\lambda B_{13}
or in the subspace where
B_{23}=-\lambda B_{13}
; without loss of generality, we can assume the former.

Under the assumption
B_{23}=\lambda B_{13}
, we have
\det(B)=B_{13}^{2}(2\lambda B_{12}-B_{22})
. The function that takes a matrix in
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
to its
(1,3)
-entry is a linear functional on the vector space
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
, so its kernel is either all of
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
or a subspace of
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
of positive codimension. Hence, either
B_{13}=0
for all matrices
B
in
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
, or else
B_{13}
is nonzero on a dense open subset of
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
. If
B_{13}=0
for all matrices in
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
, then
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}\subset\mathscr{W}_{1}
and hence
\mathscr{V}\subset\mathscr{W}_{1}
. Otherwise,
B_{13}\neq 0
on a dense open subset of
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
, and then
\det(B)=0
from (
A.8
) implies that
B_{22}=2\lambda B_{12}
for all matrices in
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
. In that case, conjugating the matrices in
\mathscr{V}^{\prime}
by the rotation matrix

\displaystyle R=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&\sin\theta&0\\
-\sin\theta&\cos\theta&0\\
0&0&1\end{pmatrix},

where
\sin\theta=\lambda\cos\theta
, yields a matrix in
\mathscr{W}_{2}
. Since
RA_{1}R^{-1}\in\mathscr{W}_{2}
as well, we conclude that
\mathscr{V}
is conjugate to a subspace of
\mathscr{W}_{2}
.
∎

A.3
A result for codimension reduction

In this section we establish a technical result that is used in the proofs of Propositions
3.5
and
5.9
.

Let
V
and
W
be real vector spaces. Given a linear subspace
L\subset S^{k}V^{*}\otimes W
, the
prolongation
of
L
is defined to be

\displaystyle L^{(1)}=V^{*}\otimes L\cap S^{k+1}V^{*}\otimes W.

This definition arises in the study of the tableaux associated to systems of linear first-order PDE. See
[
3
, Chapter VIII]
for more details. For example, one can check that if
V=\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
W=\mathbb{R}
and
L
is the set of symmetric
n\times n
matrices in block form

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}0&B\\
B^{t}&0\end{pmatrix},

where
B
is an arbitrary
k\times(n-k)
matrix, then
L^{(1)}=0
.

We use the above definition (in the special case where
W=\mathbb{R}
) to formulate a codimension-reduction theorem for submanifolds of Euclidean space.

Theorem A.8
.

Let
M^{m}\subset\mathbb{R}^{N}
be a smooth connected submanifold with second fundamental form
\operatorname{II}
such that the first normal bundle
N^{1}M
has constant rank
\rho
.
(
Recall that the fiber at
p
of
N^{1}M
is the image of
\operatorname{II}_{p}\colon T_{p}M\otimes T_{p}M\to N_{p}M.)
If at each point
p
in
M
, the set
|\operatorname{II}|_{p}
as a subspace of
S^{2}T_{p}^{*}M
satisfies
|\operatorname{II}|_{p}^{(1)}=0
, then
M
is contained in a totally geodesic submanifold
R
of dimension
m+\rho
which is tangent to
T_{p}M\oplus N^{1}_{p}M
at each
p\in M
.

Proof.

Near any point of
M
, choose an orthonormal frame
\mathsf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{N}
such that
\mathsf{e}_{1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{m}
span
T_{p}M
and
\mathsf{e}_{m+1},\dots,\mathsf{e}_{m+\rho}
span
N^{1}_{p}M
. (In what follows, use index ranges
1\leq\alpha,\beta\leq N
,
1\leq i,j,k\leq m
,
m<a,b\leq m+\rho
and
r,s>m+\rho
.) Let
\omega^{i}
,
\omega^{\alpha}_{\beta}
be the canonical and connection
1
-forms associated to this moving frame along
M
. Then

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathsf{e}_{j}\equiv e_{a}h^{a}_{jk}\omega^{k}\mod T_{p}M,

(A.9)

where
|\operatorname{II}|_{p}
equals the span of the symmetric matrices
h^{a}_{jk}
. Suppose that

\displaystyle{\rm d}\mathsf{e}_{a}=e_{r}q^{r}_{ai}\omega^{i}\mod T_{p}M,N^{1}_{p}M.

Then differentiating (
A.9
) shows that

\displaystyle 0=e_{r}q^{r}_{ai}\omega^{i}\wedge h^{a}_{jk}\omega^{k}.

For each
r
, it follows that
S^{r}_{ijk}=q^{r}_{ai}h^{a}_{jk}
satisfies
S^{r}_{ijk}=S^{r}_{ikj}
, and hence belongs to the space
|\operatorname{II}|^{(1)}
, which is zero. Since the matrices
h^{a}_{jk}
are linearly independent, the
q^{r}_{ai}
vanish. Hence the span of
\{\mathsf{e}_{i},\mathsf{e}_{a}\}
is fixed. If we let
R
be the totally geodesic submanifold tangent to this space at one point
p\in M
, then connecting any other point
q\in M
to
p
with a smooth curve in
M
shows that all other points of
M
must lie inside
R
.
∎

A generalization of this result to submanifolds in a Riemannian manifold may be found in
[
4
, Section 4.2]
.
