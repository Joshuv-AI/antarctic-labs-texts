Abstract
. This paper studies obstructions to preservation of return sets by episturmian morphisms. We show, by way of an explicit construction, that infinitely many obstructions exist. This generalizes and improves an earlier result about Sturmian morphisms.



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification
Primary 68R15; Secondary 37B10.

1.
Introduction

Return words have widely proved their importance in word combinatorics and symbolic dynamics. Given a uniformly recurrent infinite word
x
over a finite alphabet (uniform recurrence means that factors occur with bounded gaps within
x
), a
return word
is a word which separates successive occurrences of factors in
x
. They are particularly useful when considering infinite words generated by iterating morphisms of the free monoid. As an example, let us quote the characterization of morphic words by Durand
[
10
]
in terms of so-called
derived sequences
– sequences obtained by recoding an infinite word with respect to some set of return words. Durand showed that having a finite number of derived sequences precisely characterizes primitive substitutive sequences.

With this general motivation in mind, we consider the question of whether or not a given primitive morphism preserves its return sets, i.e. its sets of return words (with return words being considered with respect to the infinite word generated by the morphism). Note that the primitivity assumption guarantees that the corresponding infinite words are uniformly recurrent, and thus all return sets are finite. The definition of return sets and the precise formulation of the preservation property are given in
Section

2
.

This paper is a continuation of
[
4
]
, where the preservation property is introduced and the following results are established.

1.

The preservation property holds for every primitive aperiodic bifix morphism, wherein images of letters form a bifix code, for all but finitely many words (
[
4
]
, Theorem 1).

2.

This result is applied to the case of the Thue–Morse substitution (which is bifix) in order to describe the subgroups generated by the return sets (
[
4
]
, Proposition 6). These subgroups form several strictly decreasing chains of subgroups, which contrasts with the dendric case discussed below.

3.

On the other hand, the preservation property fails for primitive Sturmian morphisms up to conjugacy. More precisely, every conjugacy class of a primitive Sturmian morphism contains a member for which the property fails infinitely often (
[
4
]
, Theorem 2).

We focus in the present paper on the study of the return preservation property for
episturmian morphisms
, a generalization of Sturmian morphisms. The notion of episturmian word was introduced by Droubay, Justin and Pirillo as a generalization of Sturmian and Arnoux–Rauzy words
[
9
]
. An
episturmian word
is an infinite word whose language is closed under mirror image and which admits at most one factor of every length having at least two extensions; see Section
3
for a precise definition, and the survey
[
13
]
for more on episturmian words.

Inspired by analogies with the already existing rich theory of Sturmian morphisms (see e.g.
[
20
,
12
]
), episturmian morphisms have attracted considerable attention
[
14
,
17
,
24
,
25
,
26
]
. In particular, they belong to the so-called family of
dendric words
, which have the striking property that their return sets form bases for the free group over the alphabet
[
5
]
. This result is known as the
Return Theorem
and holds even for dendric words that are not generated by morphisms
[
5
]
. Dendric words are a common generalization of episturmian words and codings of interval exchanges. This kind of stable behavior for subgroups generated by return sets was also observed in the more general class of suffix-connected shift spaces, introduced by the second author
[
15
]
. Moreover, the behavior of return sets is closely related to a profinite invariant of shift spaces called the
Schützenberger group
, as highlighted by Almeida and Costa
[
2
]
.

These algebraic considerations provide a further motivation to the present work. Indeed, when it holds, the preservation property provides a simple way to construct infinite chains of return groups (i.e. subgroups of the free group generated by return sets) which can be used to understand the evolution of these groups as the length of the factors goes to infinity. In some cases (for instance for the Thue-Morse morphism), this leads to a very detailed understanding of the general behavior of these subgroups.

It is with the general goal of understanding the precise limitations of methods based on the preservation property that we set out to identify large families for which the property fails or succeeds, with the present paper being focused on failure. Understanding the failure of the preservation property also allows one to take a closer look at the behaviour of return words, and in particular at
shattering
phenomena (see the discussion at the end of
Section

7
).

We now give some details about our main result. In essence, it states that every primitive episturmian morphism
\sigma
fails the preservation property for infinitely many words. In its more precise form stated below (
Theorem

1.1
), we explicitly give a sequence of words which fail the property. We recover, in the case of Sturmian morphisms, the second main result from
[
4
]
, and in fact improve it by showing how to handle the full conjugacy class.

Episturmian morphisms are sorted into
conjugacy classes
which have a strong structure, such as highlighted in
[
24
]
. This explains the important role played by the integer
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)
, called the
conjugacy index
of
\sigma
, for the description of words that fail the preservation property; its is introduced in
Section

4
. This index measures the distance to the unique so-called standard episturmian morphism in its class. In other words, the conjugacy index allows one to locate in a given conjugacy class the episturmian morphism under study. The standard episturmian morphisms also play a special role; they generate words such that all their prefixes are
left special
(i.e. have several left extensions).

Next is the precise statement of our main result. Given a primitive episturmian morphism
\sigma
, we let
L_{n}
,
R_{n}
be its unique
left and right special factors
of length
n
; these are the factors that have several extensions in the language of
\sigma
. We also denote by
\|\sigma\|
is the sum of the lengths of letter images under
\sigma
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive episturmian morphism over the alphabet
A
. Then there is a letter
a_{\min}
in
A
such that
\sigma
fails the return preservation property for all but finitely many words in:

(i)

\{a_{\min}L_{n}\mid L_{n}=R_{n}\}
if
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\geq|\sigma(a_{\min})|
;

(ii)

\{R_{n}a_{\min}\mid L_{n}=R_{n}\}
if
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\leq\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|\sigma(a_{\min})|
.

For context, the quantity
\frac{\|\sigma\|-1}{|A|-1}=1+\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}
is the cardinality of the conjugacy class of
\sigma

[
24
]
. The proof of the main result relies on a careful study of return sets and Rauzy graphs (see
Section

5
); see in particular
Theorem

5.4
where an explicit description of return words is given. The letter
a_{\min}
appearing in the theorem is called the
minimal letter
and is introduced in
Proposition

4.9
. As highlighted in
Corollary

4.10
, the inequality
|\sigma(a_{\min})|\leq\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|\sigma(a_{\min})|
holds, thus implying that every episturmian substitution falls into one of the two cases of
Theorem

1.1
.

Let us sketch the content of this paper. Basic definitions, and in particular the preservation property, are recalled in
Section

2
.
Section

3
is devoted to episturmian and standard morphisms.
Section

4
focuses on properties of their conjugacy classes, and Rauzy graphs are considered in
Section

5
. Lastly, the proof of
Theorem

1.1
is given in
Section

6
and a brief conclusion is provided in
Section

7
.

2.
Preliminaries

Let
A
be a finite alphabet and
A^{*}
be the set of finite words on
A
equipped with concatenation. We denote the empty word by
\varepsilon
and we let
A^{+}=A^{*}\setminus\{\varepsilon\}
.

Let
A^{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of (right-sided) infinite word over
A
. Recall that a (one-sided)
shift space
is a subset
X\subseteq A^{\mathbb{N}}
invariant under the shift map
(x_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mapsto(x_{n+1})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
and closed for the product topology of
A^{\mathbb{N}}
(
A
being discrete). The
language of
X
, denoted
\mathcal{L}(X)
, is the set of all finite words occurring as factors in the elements
x\in X
, i.e. words of the form

x_{[i,j)}=x_{i}\cdots x_{j-1}\quad\text{for}\ i\leq j\in\mathbb{N}.

A
left extension
of
v
in
X
is a word
u\in A^{*}
such that
uv\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. Likewise, a
right extension
of
v
is a word
w\in A^{*}
such that
vw\in\mathcal{L}(X)
. We say that
v
is
left special
if it has at least two left extensions of length 1, and
right special
if those are instead right extensions. A word is
bispecial
if it is simultaneously left and right special.

Given
u\in\mathcal{L}(X)
, the following sets are known respectively as the
left
and
right return sets
of
u
in
X
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{X}(u)=\{r\in A^{*}\mid ru\in\mathcal{L}(X)\cap uA^{+}\setminus A^{+}uA^{+}\},

\displaystyle\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(u)=\{r\in A^{*}\mid ur\in\mathcal{L}(X)\cap A^{+}u\setminus A^{+}uA^{+}\}.

Their elements are called
left
and
right return words
respectively. Observe that these two return sets are related by the following formula (
[
1
]
, p. 16):

(1)

\mathcal{R}_{X}(u)=u\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(u)u^{-1}.

Let
\sigma\colon A^{*}\to A^{*}
be a morphism. We say that an infinite word
\mathbf{x}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
is a
periodic point
for
\sigma
if there is an integer
k\geq 1
such that
\sigma^{k}(\mathbf{x})=\mathbf{x}
. The least such
k
is known as the
period
of
\mathbf{x}
under
\sigma
. When the period is 1 we say that
\mathbf{x}
is a
fixed point
. Any primitive morphism admits at least one periodic point.

Assume that
\sigma
is primitive and let
\mathbf{x}
be a periodic point of
\sigma
. We denote by
X_{\sigma}
the shift space of
A^{\mathbb{N}}
associated with
\sigma
, which is defined as the closure of the orbit of
\mathbf{x}
under the action of the shift map. This set does not depend on the choice of a periodic point when
\sigma
is primitive; see for instance
[
22
]
, § 5 or
[
11
]
, § 1.4. We also let
\mathcal{L}(\sigma)
be the language of
X_{\sigma}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(u)
,
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(u)
be the left and right return sets of a word
u\in\mathcal{L}(\sigma)
. Under the primitivity assumption, every periodic point of
\sigma
is uniformly recurrent, hence all the return sets
\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(u)
are finite and
X_{\sigma}
is minimal; see e.g.
[
10
]
.

Let
\sigma
be a primitive morphism over
A
. Given a word
u\in\mathcal{L}(\sigma)
, consider the following dual preservation properties:

(P)

\displaystyle\sigma(\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(u))=\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(\sigma(u)),

(P')

\displaystyle\sigma(\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(u))=\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(\sigma(u)).

Because of (
1
), these two properties turn out to be equivalent (
[
4
]
, Lemma 3). The property (
P
) is the main actor of the present paper.

Let
\sigma
and
\rho
be two morphisms. We say that
\rho
is a
right conjugate
of
\sigma
, or
\sigma
a
left conjugate
of
\rho
, if there is a word
w\in A^{*}
such that
\sigma(a)w=w\rho(a)
for all
a\in A
; this is written
\sigma=w\rho w^{-1}
or
\rho=w^{-1}\sigma w
. We say that two morphisms are conjugate if the first is a right conjugate of the second or vice-versa. This is an equivalence relation (
[
24
]
, Lemma 3.1), so we may speak of
conjugacy classes
.

3.
First properties of episturmian morphisms

In this section, we gather some basic properties of episturmian morphisms. For a more detailed account on the theory of episturmian words, see the survey
[
13
]
.
Let us first recall some key definitions.

1.

An
episturmian
word is an infinite word
\mathbf{x}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
whose language
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{x})
is closed under reversal (mirror) and which admits at most one left special factor of every length (this is
[
9
]
, Theorem 5, used here as a definition).

2.

An episturmian word is called
non-degenerate
if it admits one left special factor of every length.

3.

A
standard episturmian
word is a non-degenerate episturmian word
\mathbf{x}
whose left special factors occur as prefixes, that is they are all of the form
\mathbf{x}_{[0,n)}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

4.

A
strict episturmian
word is a non-degenerate episturmian word whose left special factors all have
|A|
left extensions.

Strict episturmian words are sometimes called
Arnoux–Rauzy
words. Because of closure under mirror, left special factors and left extensions can equivalently be replaced by their right counterparts in the above definitions. Episturmian words are
uniformly recurrent
(
[
9
]
, Theorem 2), hence they generate minimal shift spaces. A minimal shift space containing
one
episturmian word consists therefore
only
of episturmian words; we call those
episturmian shift spaces
. Likewise it makes sense to speak of
strict episturmian shift spaces
.

A morphism
\sigma\colon A^{*}\to A^{*}
is called
episturmian
if it preserves episturmian words. A
standard morphism
is an episturmian morphism which additionally preserves standard episturmian words. Let
\operatorname{Epi}(A)
be the set of all episturmian morphisms of
A^{*}
and
\operatorname{Std}(A)
be the set of all standard morphisms of
A^{*}
. Note that
\operatorname{Std}(A)
is a submonoid of
\operatorname{Epi}(A)
which is itself a submonoid of
\operatorname{End}(A^{*})
, the monoid of all endomorphisms of
A^{*}
.

For
a\in A
, consider the morphisms
\psi_{a},\overline{\psi}_{a}\colon A^{*}\to A^{*}
defined by

\psi_{a}(b)=\begin{cases}a&\text{if }b=a,\\
ab&\text{if }b\neq a;\end{cases}\qquad\overline{\psi}_{a}(b)=\begin{cases}a&\text{if }b=a,\\
ba&\text{if }b\neq a.\end{cases}

In other words, on an alphabet
A=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b},\mathtt{c},\cdots\}
, one has

\psi_{\mathtt{a}}(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{a},\quad\psi_{\mathtt{a}}(\mathtt{b})=\mathtt{ab},\quad\psi_{\mathtt{a}}(\mathtt{c})=\mathtt{ac},\quad\cdots.

Let
\operatorname{Perm}(A)
be the group of permutations of
A
, which we also view as automorphisms of
A^{*}
.

Example 3.1

(
d
-bonacci)
.

Let
d\geq 2
and
A=\{\mathtt{a}_{1},\cdots,\mathtt{a}_{d}\}
be a
d
-letter alphabet. Let
\sigma=\psi_{\mathtt{a}_{1}}\circ\pi
, where
\pi=(\mathtt{a}_{1}\ \mathtt{a}_{2}\ \cdots\ \mathtt{a}_{d})
, with the usual cycle notation for permutations. This is a standard episturmian morphism known as the
d
-bonacci morphism
. When
d=2
we get the Fibonacci morphism

\sigma(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{a}\mathtt{b},\quad\sigma(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{a},

and with
d=3
the
“tribonacci”
morphism, first studied by Rauzy in
[
23
]
,

\sigma(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{ab},\quad\sigma(\mathtt{b})=\mathtt{ac},\quad\sigma(\mathtt{c})=\mathtt{a}.

Observe that
\sigma^{d}=\psi_{\mathtt{a}_{1}}\circ\psi_{\mathtt{a}_{2}}\circ\cdots\circ\psi_{\mathtt{a}_{d}}
.

Let us mention the following important result of Justin and Pirillo.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
18
]
)
.

The monoid
\operatorname{Epi}(A)
is generated by the endomorphisms
\psi_{a}
,
\overline{\psi}_{a}
for
a\in A
together with the elements of
\operatorname{Perm}(A)
.

The monoid
\operatorname{Std}(A)
is generated by the endomorphisms
\psi_{a}
for
a\in A
together with the elements of
\operatorname{Perm}(A)
.

Let
\overline{A}=\{\overline{a}\mid a\in A\}
be a disjoint copy of
A
and extend
\psi
as a map on
(A\cup\overline{A})
to a map on
(A\cup\overline{A})^{*}
defined by
\psi_{\overline{a}}=\overline{\psi}_{a}
and
\psi_{uv}=\psi_{u}\circ\psi_{v}
. Note that for all
u\in(A\cup\overline{A})^{*}
and
\pi\in\operatorname{Perm}(A)

(2)

\psi_{\pi(u)}\circ\pi=\pi\circ\psi_{u}.

In particular, the monoids
\operatorname{Epi}(A)
and
\operatorname{Std}(A)
admit the following descriptions:

\displaystyle\operatorname{Epi}(A)=\{\psi_{v}\circ\pi\mid v\in(A\cup\overline{A})^{*},\pi\in\operatorname{Perm}(A)\},

\displaystyle\operatorname{Std}(A)=\{\psi_{u}\circ\pi\mid u\in A^{*},\pi\in\operatorname{Perm}(A)\}.

Thus the tuples of the form
(\psi_{u}(a))_{a\in A}
with

We finish this section with a discussion on periodic points of episturmian morphisms, the main conclusion being that for primitive episturmian morphisms, all of them are strict episturmian words. This is not necessarily the case for non-primitive episturmian morphisms, since for instance permutations admit all infinite words as periodic points. We also have the following less trivial example of this.

Example 3.3
.

Consider
\sigma=\overline{\psi}_{\mathtt{ab}}
on the alphabet
A=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b},\mathtt{c}\}
. Then
\sigma
has a fixed point of the form
\mathbf{x}=\mathtt{c}\mathbf{y}
where
\mathbf{y}
is the Sturmian word determined by the restriction of
\sigma
to
\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}^{*}
(in fact
\mathbf{y}
is the Fibonacci word). This fixed point is not uniformly recurrent, hence it cannot be episturmian. Notice also that it is not closed under reversal, although it retains the property of having exactly one left and one right special factor of every length.

Proposition 3.4
.

Every episturmian morphism
\sigma
admits an episturmian periodic point. If moreover
\sigma
is primitive, then all of its periodic points are strict episturmian words and
X_{\sigma}
is a strict episturmian shift space.

Proof.

By (
2
), every episturmian morphism has a power of the form
\psi_{w}
for some
w\in(A\cup\overline{A})^{*}
: for an episturmian morphism of the form
\psi_{u}\circ\pi
with
u\in(A\cup\overline{A})^{*}
and
\pi\in\operatorname{Perm}(A)
, take
w=u\pi(u)\cdots\pi^{n-1}(u)
where
n
is order of
\pi
; then
(\psi_{u}\circ\pi)^{n}=\psi_{w}
. By
[
17
]
, Proposition 3.11,
\psi_{w}
acts as a transformation on some finite set of episturmian words, thus it must have a power fixing one of them.

Next we let
\sigma
be a primitive episturmian morphism. By the first part of the statement,
\sigma
admits some episturmian periodic point, which generates
X_{\sigma}
; hence
X_{\sigma}
is an episturmian shift space. Since all periodic points of
\sigma
are contained in
X_{\sigma}
, they must be episturmian. It remains to show that they are strict.

Up to taking a power, we may assume that
\sigma=\psi_{w}
for some word
w\in(A\cup\overline{A})^{*}
. The fact that
\sigma
is primitive then implies that for every letter
a\in A
, either
a
or
\overline{a}
occurs in
u
. By
[
9
]
, Theorem 7, the periodic points of
\sigma
must be strict episturmian words.
∎

4.
Conjugacy classes of episurmian morphisms

We focus in this section on a description of conjugacy classes of episturmian morphisms. We first recall the following foundational result by Richomme; see also
[
25
]
for related algorithms.

Theorem 4.1

(
[
24
]
, Theorem 5.1)
.

A morphism is episturmian if and only if it is a right conjugate of a standard morphism, which is moreover unique.

Thanks to this theorem, the following notion is well-defined.

Definition 4.2
.

Let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Epi}(A)
be an episturmian morphism. The
conjugacy index
of
\sigma
, denoted

Recall Justin’s left and right formulas from

]
relating
\operatorname{Pal}
with the morphisms

and

:

(J)

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pal}(uv)=\psi_{u}(\operatorname{Pal}(v))\operatorname{Pal}(u),

(J')

\displaystyle\operatorname{Pal}(uv)=\operatorname{Pal}(u)\overline{\psi}_{u}(\operatorname{Pal}(v)).

Let
\operatorname{gcp}
denote the
greatest common prefix
operator, i.e.
\operatorname{gcp}(u,v)
is the longest word which is a prefix of both
u
and
v
. It is related with
\operatorname{Pal}
by the following.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
u\in A^{*}
and
a\neq b\in A
. Then
\operatorname{Pal}(u)a
is a prefix of
\psi_{u}(ab)
and
\operatorname{Pal}(u)=\operatorname{gcp}(\psi_{u}(ab),\psi_{u}(ba))
.

Proof.

The proof is done by induction on
|u|
. If
u=\varepsilon
the statement is trivial. Assume the result holds for
u
and let
c\in A
. Fix
b\in A
with
a\neq b
. The rest of the proof has two cases.

Case
a\neq c
. By induction, there is
v\in A^{*}
such that
\psi_{u}(ac)=\operatorname{Pal}(u)av
. Let
d=b
if
c=b
and
d=\varepsilon
otherwise. Using Justin’s formula (
J
) we get

\displaystyle\psi_{uc}(ab)

\displaystyle=\psi_{u}(cacd)=\psi_{u}(c)\psi_{u}(ac)\psi_{u}(d)

\displaystyle=\psi_{u}(c)\operatorname{Pal}(u)av\psi_{u}(d)=\operatorname{Pal}(uc)av\psi_{u}(d).

Case
a=c
. By induction, there is
v\in A^{*}
such that
\psi_{u}(ab)=\operatorname{Pal}(u)av
and, using Justin’s formula (
J
),

\psi_{ua}(ab)=\psi_{u}(aab)=\psi_{u}(a)\psi_{u}(ab)=\psi_{u}(a)\operatorname{Pal}(u)av=\operatorname{Pal}(ua)av.

The second part of the claim follows immediately.
∎

For a morphism
\sigma\colon A^{*}\to A^{*}
, we let
\|\sigma\|=\sum_{a\in A}|\sigma(a)|
. In
[
17
]
, Corollary 2.7, Justin derived the following formula as a consequence of (
J
):

(3)

|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|+1=\frac{\|\psi_{u}\|-1}{|A|-1}.

As a case study, consider the binary alphabet
A=\{\mathtt{a},\mathtt{b}\}
. Then the above formula simplifies to
|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|=|\psi_{u}(\mathtt{ab})|-2
. For instance, when
u=\mathtt{aba}
, we have
\psi_{u}(\mathtt{a})=\mathtt{aba}
,
\psi_{u}(\mathtt{b})=\mathtt{abaab}
, so the formula yields
|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|=6
. And indeed, one checks that
\operatorname{Pal}(u)=\mathtt{abaaba}
.

Comparing the equality
|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|=|\psi_{u}(\mathtt{ab})|-2
with
Lemma

4.3
gives a proof of the fact, dating back to a paper of Séébold, that for every standard Sturmian morphism
\sigma
, the two words
\sigma(\mathtt{ab})
and
\sigma(\mathtt{ba})
agree except for their two last letters (
[
26
]
, Property 4).

In general, the right-hand side of (
3
) is also, according to Richomme, the formula for the size of conjugacy classes of episturmian morphisms.

Proposition 4.4

(
[
24
]
, Property 4.1)
.

Let
\sigma
be an episturmian morphism. The cardinality of the conjugacy class of
\sigma
equals
\frac{\|\sigma\|-1}{|A|-1}
.

In light of (
3
), we conclude that the episturmian conjugacy class of
\psi_{u}
contains
|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|+1
morphisms. The next proposition provides a direct explanation of this fact.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\sigma\in\operatorname{Std}(A)
with
\sigma=\psi_{u}\circ\pi
for
u\in A^{*}
,
\pi\in\operatorname{Perm}(A)
. A morphism
\rho
is a right conjugate of
\sigma
if and only if
\rho=w^{-1}\sigma w
for some prefix
w
of
\operatorname{Pal}(u)
.

Proof.

It suffices to prove the result for
\sigma=\psi_{u}
. Let
w
be a prefix of
\operatorname{Pal}(u)
. By
Lemma

4.3
,
w
and
\sigma(a)w
are common prefixes of
\sigma(aba)
, hence
w
is a prefix of
\sigma(a)w
. In particular,
\sigma=w\rho w^{-1}
for some morphism
\rho
.

Conversely, let
\rho
be a right conjugate of
\sigma
with
w
such that
\rho=w^{-1}\sigma w
. Then, for every
a\neq b\in A
,

\sigma(ab)w=\sigma(a)w\rho(b)=w\rho(ab).

Moreover
|w|\leq|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|
, otherwise the prefix of
w
of length
|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|+1
would be a common prefix of
\sigma(ab)
and
\sigma(ba)
, contradicting the maximality of
\operatorname{Pal}(u)=\operatorname{gcp}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))
from
Lemma

4.3
. Hence
w
is a prefix of
u
, as required.
∎

We next give two corollaries of
Proposition

4.5
. In the first one, we express the conjugacy index in terms of both the
\operatorname{gcp}
operator and its dual, the
greatest common suffix
operator
\operatorname{gcs}
.

Corollary 4.6
.

Let
\sigma
be an episturmian morphism. For every
a\neq b\in A
, the conjugacy index of
\sigma
satisfies

\displaystyle\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|\operatorname{gcp}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{gcs}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))|.

Proof.

It suffices to treat the case where
\sigma=w^{-1}\psi_{u}w
with
u\in A^{*}
and
|w|=\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)
. Recall that by
Proposition

4.5
,
w
is a prefix of
\operatorname{Pal}(u)
. Fix two distinct letters
a\neq b\in A
. By
Lemma

4.3
,
\operatorname{Pal}(u)a
is a prefix of
\psi_{u}(ab)
, and therefore
w^{-1}\operatorname{Pal}(u)a
is a prefix of

w^{-1}\psi_{u}(ab)w=(w^{-1}\psi_{u}w)(ab)=\sigma(ab).

It follows that
w^{-1}\operatorname{Pal}(u)=\operatorname{gcp}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))
and using (
3
),

|w^{-1}\operatorname{Pal}(u)|=|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|-|w|=\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma),

which gives the first equality.

For the second equality, observe that
\sigma(ab)
is a suffix of
bw
or vice-versa. Since
|\sigma(ab)|\geq|\operatorname{Pal}(u)|\geq|w|
we conclude that the latter alternative holds.
∎

Corollary 4.7
.

Let
u\in A^{*}
. For every conjugate
\sigma
of
\psi_{u}
and every
a\neq b\in A
,

\operatorname{Pal}(u)=\operatorname{gcs}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))\operatorname{gcp}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba)).

Proof.

Let
w
be the prefix of
\operatorname{Pal}(u)
such that
\sigma=w^{-1}\psi_{u}w
(cf.
Proposition

4.5
). Observe that for every
x\in A^{*}
, either
\sigma(x)
is a suffix of
w
or vice-versa. But by
Corollary

4.6
, we have that

|\operatorname{gcs}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))|=|w|,

hence
\operatorname{gcs}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))=w
. A dual argument shows that
\operatorname{gcp}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))=w^{-1}\operatorname{Pal}(u)
.
∎

We introduce with the next lemma the notion of minimal letters, which is well-defined for all episturmian morphisms which are not permutations.

Lemma 4.8
.

Let
\sigma
be an episturmian morphism over
A
which is not a permutation. There exists a unique letter
a_{\min}\in A
such that
|\sigma(a_{\min})|<|\sigma(a)|
for every
a\in A
,
a\neq a_{\min}
. Moreover if
\sigma=\psi_{u}
for
u\in A^{+}
, then
a_{\min}
is the last letter of
u
.

Proof.

Observe that the property of the first claim is preversed by conjugacy. Since, by
Theorem

4.1
, every episturmian morphism has a (unique) standard conjugate, we may assume that
\sigma
is standard. Up to precomposing by an appropriate permutation, we may in fact suppose that
\sigma=\psi_{u}
where
u\in A^{+}
. Write
u=va
where
v\in A^{*}
,
a\in A
. For
b\in A\setminus\{a\}
we have

\psi_{u}(b)=\psi_{v}(\psi_{a}(b))=\psi_{v}(ab)=\psi_{v}(a)\psi_{v}(b)=\psi_{v}(\psi_{a}(a))\psi_{v}(b)=\psi_{u}(a)\psi_{v}(b).

It follows that
\psi_{u}(a)
is a proper prefix of every other
\psi_{u}(b)
, hence the result holds for
a_{\min}=a
.
∎

We call
a_{\min}
the
minimal letter
of
\sigma
. All episturmian morphisms in the same (non-trivial) conjugacy class share the same minimal letter. The following proposition highlights some of its key properties.

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
\sigma
be an episturmian morphism which is not a permutation. Then its minimal letter
a_{\min}
satisfies the following three properties:

(i)

\sigma(A)\subseteq A^{*}\sigma(a_{\min})\iff\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\geq|\sigma(a_{\min})|
.

(ii)

\sigma(A)\subseteq\sigma(a_{\min})A^{*}\iff\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\leq\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|\sigma(a_{\min})|
.

(iii)

2|\sigma(a_{\min})|\leq\frac{\|\sigma\|-1}{|A|-1}
.

Proof.

(i)
. Let
\sigma=w^{-1}\psi_{u}w
with
u\in A^{+}
and
w
the prefix of length
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)
of
\operatorname{Pal}(u)
. We assume that
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)=|w|\geq|\sigma(a_{\min})|
, and take
b\neq a_{\min}
in
A
. By
Corollary

4.6
,
w
is the greatest common suffix of
\sigma(ba_{\min})
and
\sigma(a_{\min}b)
, hence
\sigma(a_{\min})
is a suffix of
w
and in turn a suffix of
\sigma(a_{\min}b)
. Since
|\sigma(b)|>|\sigma(a_{\min})|
, we conclude that
\sigma(a_{\min})
is a suffix of
\sigma(b)
. Conversely assume that
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)=|w|<|\sigma(a_{\min})|
. Then
\sigma(a_{\min})
cannot be a suffix
\sigma(a_{\min}b)
, because this would contradict the maximality of
w
as a common suffix of
\sigma(ba_{\min})
and
\sigma(a_{\min}b)
.

(ii)
. The proof is dual.

(iii)
. For this part of the statement, we may assume that
\sigma=\psi_{u}
for some
u\in A^{+}
. First we claim that for every
v\in A^{*}
:

(4)

\max\{|\psi_{v}(a)|\mathrel{;}a\in A\}\leq|\operatorname{Pal}(v)|+1.

We proceed by induction on
|v|
. When
v=\varepsilon
, this is trivial. Assume that the result holds for
v\in A^{*}
and let
a\in A
. Using Justin’s dual formula (
J'
) and the induction hypothesis, we find

\displaystyle|\operatorname{Pal}(va)|+1

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{Pal}(v)|+1+|\overline{\psi}_{v}(a)|=|\operatorname{Pal}(v)|+1+|\psi_{v}(a)|

\displaystyle\geq|\psi_{v}(b)|+|\psi_{v}(a)|=|\psi_{v}(ab)|\geq|\psi_{va}(b)|.

This proves the claim.

We conclude the proof of
(iii)
by reducing it to the inequality (
4
), where
v\in A^{*}
is such that
u=va
,
a\in A
. According to
Lemma

4.8
,
a=a_{\min}
. Moreover, we have
\psi_{va}(a)=\psi_{v}(a)
. Combining (
4
) and (
3
), we get

\frac{\|\psi_{va}\|-1}{|A|-1}=|\psi_{v}(a)|+\frac{\|\psi_{v}\|-1}{|A|-1}=|\psi_{v}(a)|+|\operatorname{Pal}(v)|+1\geq 2|\psi_{v}(a)|.\qed

Corollary 4.10
.

Let
\sigma
be an episturmian morphism over
A
which is not a permutation. Then its minimal letter
a_{\min}
is such that either
\sigma(A)\subseteq A^{*}\sigma(a_{\min})
, or
\sigma(A)\subseteq\sigma(a_{\min})A^{*}
.

Proof.

If
(i)
from
Proposition

4.9
does not hold, then by
(iii)
the conjugacy index of
\sigma
satisfies

\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\leq|\sigma(a_{\min})|-1\leq\frac{\|\sigma\|-1}{|A|-1}-|\sigma(a_{\min})|-1=\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|\sigma(a_{\min})|,

which means that
(ii)
holds.
∎

.

For a standard episturmian morphism, the conjugacy index is

First, let us extend the notion of palindromic closure to infinite words. Let
\mathbf{d}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}

),

\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d})
, having
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)})
as prefix for every

. This naturally extends

to a map

.

By
[

]
, Theorem 1, this extension of
\operatorname{Pal}
gives a bijection from

onto the set of standard episturmian words. In particular

(

are exactly the prefixes of

which are bispecial whenever they are right special. All of these words are bispecial, or equivalently
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d})
is non-degenerate, precisely when
\mathbf{d}
has at least two letters occurring infinitely often (
[
9
]
, Theorem 3).

Since an episturmian shift space
X
contains
exactly one
standard episturmian word, we conclude that

is the orbit-closure of
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d})
for a unique
\mathbf{d}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
. We call
\mathbf{d}
the

of the shift

. Note that the shift

does not need here to be generated by an episturmian morphism. See
[

for more details on directive words of episturmian shift spaces.

Let

Given a word

\psi_{u}^{(i)}
be the

\overline{\psi}_{u}^{(i)}
be the

\overline{\psi}_{u}

\psi_{u}^{(i)}=\overline{\psi}_{u}^{(m-i)}
where

.

The next theorem combines two results from
[
19

. In particular, the first part of the proof is essentially
[

; we provide an argument for the sake of completeness. We also recall in passing that episturmian shifts are always minimal (

, Theorem 2).

Theorem 5.4
.

Let
X
be a non-degenerate episturmian shift space with directive word
\mathbf{d}\in A^{\mathbb{N}}
. Take
w\in\mathcal{L}(X)
and let
n=n(w)
be the least positive integer such that
w
occurs in
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)})
. Assume that
w
occurs at index
\ell
in
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)})
and let
\ell^{\prime}=|\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)})|-\ell-|w|
. Then the following equalities hold:

\mathcal{R}_{X}(w)=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}^{(\ell)}(A),\qquad\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(w)=\overline{\psi}_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}(A).

Proof.

Let
p=|w|
. First, assume that
w
is bispecial, which is the case precisely when
w=L_{p}=R_{p}=\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)})
. In this case we have
\mathcal{R}_{X}(w)=\{V_{a,p}\mid a\in A\}
and
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(w)=\{\overline{V}_{a,p}\mid a\in A\}
. Let us show by induction on
n
that
V_{a,p}=\psi_{d_{[0,n)}}(a)
for every
a\in A
; the dual of this argument gives
\overline{V}_{a,p}=\overline{\psi}_{d_{[0,n)}}(a)
. If
n=0
, then
w=\varepsilon
and
\psi_{\varepsilon}(a)=a=V_{a,0}
, as expected.

For the induction step assume that
n>0
, let
v=\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n-1)})
and
q=|v|
. Using Justin’s second formula (
J'
), we find that

w=\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)})=\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n-1)})\overline{\psi}_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n-1)}}(\mathbf{d}_{n})=v\overline{\psi}_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n-1)}}(\mathbf{d}_{n}).

But notice that
\overline{\psi}_{x}(a)
starts with
a
, for every
a\in A
and
x\in A^{*}
; in particular it follows that
v\mathbf{d}_{n}=L_{q}\mathbf{d}_{n}=L_{q+1}
. Since
v=L_{q}=R_{q}
, we have by Theorem
5.2

(iii)
:

\displaystyle U_{q+1}\mathbf{d}_{n}=V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q},\qquad V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q+1}=\mathbf{d}_{n},

\displaystyle V_{b,q+1}=\mathbf{d}_{n}V_{b,q}\quad\text{for}\ b\neq\mathbf{d}_{n}.

Since
L_{i}\neq R_{i}
for
q<i<p
, we can then apply
Theorem

5.2

(iii)

p-q
times to conclude that

V_{b,p}=U_{q+1}V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q+1}=\begin{cases}V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q}&\text{ if }b=\mathbf{d}_{n}\\
V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q}V_{b,q}&\text{ if }b\neq\mathbf{d}_{n}.\end{cases}

Finally, using the induction hypothesis, we have:

\displaystyle V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},p}=V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q}=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n-1)}}(\mathbf{d}_{n})=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}(\mathbf{d}_{n}),

\displaystyle V_{b,p}=V_{\mathbf{d}_{n},q}V_{b,q}=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n-1)}}(\mathbf{d}_{n}b)=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}(b)\quad\text{for}\ b\neq d_{n}.

It remains to handle the case where
w
is not bispecial. Let
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)]})=fwg
where
|f|=\ell
and
|g|=\ell^{\prime}
. Since
f
is a prefix of
\operatorname{Pal}(\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)]})
, it follows that
f^{-1}\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}f=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}^{(\ell)}
. Next since
fwg
is bispecial, we have by the first part of the proof that

\mathcal{R}_{X}(fwg)=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}(A).

Finally, by
[
19
]
, Corollary 4.1, we get

\mathcal{R}_{X}(w)=f^{-1}\mathcal{R}_{X}(fwg)f=f^{-1}\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}(A)f=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}^{(\ell)}(A).

The second formula is obtained in a similar way: observe that
\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(fwg)=\overline{\psi}_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}(A)
and
g\overline{\psi}_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}g^{-1}=\psi_{\mathbf{d}_{[0,n)}}^{(\ell^{\prime})}
, and then apply the dual form of
[
19
]
, Corollary 4.1.
∎

\sigma(\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(a_{\min}L_{n}))=\overline{\rho}(A)
where

\overline{\rho}=(y\overline{\psi}_{w}(z^{\prime}))\overline{\psi}_{w\pi(\operatorname{d})}(y\overline{\psi}_{w}(z^{\prime}))^{-1}.

The conjugacy index of
\overline{\rho}
is given by the slightly more involved formula:

\operatorname{ind}(\overline{\rho})=\frac{\|\overline{\rho}\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|y\overline{\psi}_{w}(z^{\prime})|=\frac{\|\overline{\rho}\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}+\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)-|\overline{\psi}_{w}(z^{\prime})|.

Then for distinct elements
r,s\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(u)
, we find
r=\overline{\rho}(a)
,
s=\overline{\rho}(b)
where
a\neq b\in A
, and applying
Corollary

4.6
, we get

\displaystyle|\operatorname{gcp}(rs,sr)|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{gcp}(\overline{\rho}(ab),\overline{\rho}(ba))|=\frac{\|\overline{\rho}\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-\operatorname{ind}(\overline{\rho})

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)+|\overline{\psi}_{w}(z^{\prime})|\geq\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma).

with equality when
z^{\prime}=\varepsilon
, which happens precisely when
u
is right special.
∎

We are now ready to put all the pieces together and finalize the proof of our main result.

Proof of
Theorem

1.1
.

(i)
. Let
\sigma
be a primitive episturmian morphism with minimal letter
a_{\min}
. Let
j=|\sigma(a_{\min})|
and assume that
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\geq|\sigma(a_{\min})|
. Fix
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
L_{n}=R_{n}
. By
Lemma

6.2
, we may assume, up to taking
n
large enough, that
\sigma(a_{\min}L_{n})
is the
(\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)-j)
-th inner word of its Rauzy graph. Then, choosing any two distinct return words
r\neq s\in\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(\sigma(a_{\min}L_{n}))
,
Lemma

5.8
gives

|\operatorname{gcs}(rs,sr)|=\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)-j,

while by
Lemma

6.3
, any two distinct elements
r^{\prime}\neq s^{\prime}\in\sigma(\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(a_{\min}L_{n}))
satisfy

|\operatorname{gcs}(r^{\prime}s^{\prime},s^{\prime}r^{\prime})|\geq\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)>\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)-j.

This shows that
\sigma(\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(a_{\min}L_{n}))\neq\mathcal{R}_{\sigma}(\sigma(a_{\min}L_{n}))
.

(ii)
. Now we suppose that
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)\leq m-j
, where
m=\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}
. In this case, by
Lemma

6.2
, we may suppose, up to taking
n
large enough, that
\sigma(R_{n}a_{\min})
is the
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)
-th inner word in the Rauzy graph
\overline{\Gamma}_{k}
, where
k=|\sigma(R_{n}a_{\min})|
; in fact, we have that the inner branch of
\overline{\Gamma}_{k}
has length
m-j
. Then, by
Lemma

5.8
, we have for
r\neq s\in\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(\sigma(R_{n}a_{\min}))
:

|\operatorname{gcp}(rs,sr)|=|U_{k}|-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)=m-j-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma).

On the other hand, by
Lemma

6.3
, any two distinct elements
r^{\prime}\neq s^{\prime}\in\sigma(\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(R_{n}a_{\min}))
satisfy:

|\operatorname{gcp}(r^{\prime}s^{\prime},s^{\prime}r^{\prime})|\geq m-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)>m-j-\operatorname{ind}(\sigma).

Therefore we conclude that
\sigma(\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(R_{n}a_{\min}))\neq\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{\sigma}(\sigma(R_{n}a_{\min}))
.
∎

7.
Conclusion

Let us briefly summarize the main ideas that appeared in the paper. We have established the existence of infinitely many obstructions to the preservation of return sets by episturmian morphisms. Two specific combinatorial notions were used in the description of these obstructions: the conjugacy index
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)
(
Definition

4.2
) and the minimal letter
a_{\min}
(
Lemma

4.8
). On our way to the proof of
Theorem

1.1
, we established a number of properties involving these notions which are of independant interest.

The conjugacy index measures the distance to the standard episturmian morphism in its conjugacy class. According to
Corollary

4.6
, one observes in particular that for every
a\neq b\in A

\displaystyle\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)

\displaystyle=\frac{\|\sigma\|-|A|}{|A|-1}-|\operatorname{gcp}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))|

\displaystyle=|\operatorname{gcs}(\sigma(ab),\sigma(ba))|,

with
\frac{\|\sigma\|-1}{|A|-1}
being equal to the cardinality of the conjugacy class of
\sigma
.

For an episturmian morphism
\sigma
which is not a permutation, the minimal letter is defined as the unique letter
a_{\min}
such that
|\sigma(a_{\min})|<|\sigma(a)|
for every
a\in A
,
a\neq a_{\min}
. In particular, when
\sigma
is a standard morphism, i.e. when
\sigma
is of the form
\sigma=\psi_{u}
with
u\in A^{+}
, then
a_{\min}
is equal to the last letter of
u
.
Proposition

4.9
highlights the relationship between
\operatorname{ind}(\sigma)
and
\sigma(a_{\min})
.

The proof of the main result also relies on the existence of convenient descriptions of return words and Rauzy graphs in episturmian shifts. Concerning return words, we establish in particular the following description for left and right sets of return words when
X
is a non-degenerate episturmian shift space:

\mathcal{R}_{X}(u)=\psi_{\operatorname{d}(u)}^{(\ell(u))}(A),\qquad\overline{\mathcal{R}}_{X}(u)=\overline{\psi}_{\operatorname{d}(u)}^{(\ell^{\prime}(u))}(A),

where

\displaystyle\operatorname{d}(u)=\operatorname{Pal}^{-1}(\operatorname{gcs}(rs,sr)\operatorname{gcp}(rs,sr)),

\displaystyle\ell(u)=|\operatorname{gcs}(rs,sr)|,\quad\ell^{\prime}(u)=|\operatorname{gcp}(rs,sr)|,

for any choice of
r\neq s\in\mathcal{R}_{X}(u)
(see
Notation

5.7
).

The failure of the preservation property also reveals a
shattering
of return words under the morphism. Consider as an illustration a primitive morphism
\sigma
and a return word
r
to the word
u
. The word
\sigma(r)
is in general a concatenation of return words to
\sigma(u)
. When the preservation property holds, the word
\sigma(r)
is in fact
exactly
one return word to
\sigma(u)
. On the other hand, it may happen that
\sigma(u)
also occurs as an internal factor in
\sigma(ru)
, even if
u
does not appear as an internal factor in
ru
. In other words, the morphism
\sigma
can create an occurrence of
\sigma(u)
even in the absence of an occurrence of
u
, which causes
\sigma(r)
to
shatter
, so to speak, into several return words. While failure of the preservation property can sometimes be the result of a cardinality obstruction (as in
[
4
]
, Example 3), this cannot be the case for episturmian shift spaces, since the number of return words is constant. In particular our main result indicates that this shattering of return words occurs infinitely often.

Many questions remain open with regards to the preservation property. It is still unclear what are the precise boundaries of this property: it includes the bifix case and excludes the episturmian case, but many substitutions do not belong to either of these two families. The methods employed for constructing obstructions in the present paper relied heavily on the very special structure of episturmian morphisms. Investigating wider families (for instance dendric morphisms) would most likely require a novel approach. Another related question, which seems difficult in general, is to obtain a precise description of the set of obstructions. In the bifix case, the set of obstructions is finite and can be effectively computed; however, outside of this, we do not know how to calculate this set, even in the episturmian case.
