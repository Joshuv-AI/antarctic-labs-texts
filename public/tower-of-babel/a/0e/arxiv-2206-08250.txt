Bayesian estimation of the low-energy constants up to fourth order in the nucleon-nucleon sector of chiral effective field theory
Isak Svensson, Andreas Ekström, Christian Forssén

Abstract
We use Bayesian methods and Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC) sampling to infer the posterior probability density function (PDF) for the low-energy constants (LECs) up to next-to-next-to-next-to-leading order ( N3LO) in a chiral effective field theory ( χ \chi EFT) description of the nucleon-nucleon interaction. In a first step, we condition the inference on neutron-proton and proton-proton scattering data and account for uncorrelated χ \chi EFT truncation errors. We demonstrate how to successfully sample the 31-dimensional space of LECs at N3LO using a revised HMC inference protocol. In a second step we extend the analysis by means of importance sampling and an empirical determination of the neutron-neutron scattering length to infer the posterior PDF for the leading charge-dependent contact LEC in the S 0 1 {}^{1}S_{0} neutron-neutron interaction channel. While doing so we account for the χ \chi EFT truncation error via a conjugate prior. We use the resulting posterior PDF to sample the posterior predictive distributions for the effective range parameters in the S 0 1 {}^{1}S_{0} wave as well as the strengths of charge-symmetry breaking and charge-independence breaking. We conclude that empirical point-estimate results of isospin breaking in the S 0 1 {}^{1}S_{0} channel are consistent with the PDFs obtained in our Bayesian analysis and that, when accounting for χ \chi EFT truncation errors, one must go to next-to-next-to-leading order to confidently detect isospin breaking effects.



I
Introduction

In chiral effective field theory (
\chi
EFT)
Weinberg 1990
;
Epelbaum et al. 2009
;
Machleidt and Entem 2011
;
Hammer et al. 2020
the nuclear interaction is parametrized in terms of low-energy constants (LECs) that capture unresolved physics and must be determined from data. The number of LECs grows with the order of the chiral expansion, and the parameter estimation becomes a challenging inference problem that is directly connected with the precision of the theory.
Moreover, in
\chi
EFT the long-range pion-nucleon (
\pi N
) interaction appears as a subprocess of the nuclear interaction. We can therefore constrain the long-range part of the nuclear interaction rather well using measured
\pi N
scattering data
Hoferichter et al. 2016
, albeit less so in the delta-full sector of
\chi
EFT
Siemens et al. 2017
. In a Bayesian context, this knowledge can be straightforwardly accounted for as a prior when learning more about the nuclear interaction from new data, as demonstrated in, e.g., Refs.
Wesolowski et al. 2021
;
Svensson et al. 2022
. In fact, Bayesian inference methods allow us to account for any prior beliefs about
\chi
EFT, most importantly its truncation error
Furnstahl et al. 2015
. This highlights some of the central synergies of
\chi
EFT and Bayesian methods. In this paper, we focus on two challenges tied to a Bayesian analysis of the nucleon-nucleon (
NN
) interaction:
(i) how to reliably sample the high-dimensional Bayesian posterior probability density functions (PDFs) of the LECs up to next-to-next-to-next-to-leading order (
N3LO) in
\chi
EFT,
and (ii) how to extend these posterior PDFs, here inferred from neutron-proton (
np
) and proton-proton (
pp
) scattering data, to account for the uncertainty in the isospin breaking (IB) and leading neutron-neutron (
nn
) short-range LEC acting in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial wave.
Throughout the paper we will often use the short-hand labels
posterior
and
prior
to indicate posterior and prior PDFs, respectively.

Sampling a high-dimensional PDF poses a significant challenge in any Bayesian analysis. In a previous paper
Svensson et al. 2022
we explicitly demonstrated the efficiency and manageable dimensional scaling of the Hamiltonian Monte Carlo (HMC)
Duane et al. 1987
algorithm applied to
\chi
EFT up to next-to-next-to-leading order (
NNLO). HMC exploits the geometry of the parameter space and Hamiltonian dynamics to draw virtually independent Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) samples concentrated to the bulk of the probability mass. In this paper, we revise our HMC protocol to sample the LEC posterior at the next chiral order, i.e.,
N3LO, where we encounter a significantly more challenging inferential problem in 31 dimensions, one per LEC.

Furthermore, the fundamental effect of IB leads to slight differences in the strong interaction between neutrons, protons, and between protons and neutrons. It originates from differences in the masses and electromagnetic charges of the up- and down-quarks
Miller et al. 1990
and is expected to be weaker in the irreducible interaction between three nucleons
Friar et al. 2004
;
Epelbaum et al. 2005
. Although weak, the IB of the strong nuclear interaction plays an important role in
ab initio
, and mean-field, analyses of infinite nuclear matter and finite nuclei, in particular towards the driplines with pronounced proton-to-neutron ratios (see, e.g., Refs.
Hagen et al. 2016
;
Garcia Ruiz et al. 2016
;
Brown 2017
;
Koszorús et al. 2021
;
Roca-Maza et al. 2018
;
Hu et al. 2022
;
Novario et al. 2023
;
Naito et al. 2022a
;
Naito et al. 2022b
;
Reinhard and Nazarewicz 2022
). It is therefore important to quantify the uncertainties pertaining to the LECs governing the strengths of the IB effects. Unfortunately, inferences conditioned solely on the world database of
NN
scattering data
Pérez et al. 2013a
;
Pérez et al. 2013b
, which does not contain
nn
cross sections, leaves the posterior of LECs acting (only) in the
nn
isospin channel unconstrained. To handle this, a point estimate of the leading charge-dependent LEC in the
nn
channel is typically obtained using the empirical value for the corresponding scattering length, primarily in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial wave. This latter quantity parametrizes the total
nn
cross section in the limit of zero scattering energy and its value is estimated from data on hadronic reactions that involve two neutrons in the initial and/or final state
Gardestig 2009
. A future prospect is to employ results from realistic lattice quantum chromodynamics to directly infer the values of the LECs
Chang et al. 2018
.

In
\chi
EFT, isospin is an exact symmetry at leading order (
LO)
van Kolck 1995
. At next-to-leading order (
NLO), one introduces realistic charged-to-neutral pion mass splittings in the one-pion exchange potential (OPEP). The
\approx 3\%
mass splitting of the rather light pions induces IB in the
S
waves and beyond. We also have charge-dependent and nonderivative LECs in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial wave. Higher-order IB effects can be accounted for systematically by introducing, e.g., charge-dependent
\pi N
LECs, (charged) pion-photon interactions, and charge-dependent LECs in partial waves with nonzero angular momentum. However, many of those IB effects are estimated to be negligible compared to the OPEP mass splitting and nonderivative
S
-wave LECs
Epelbaum et al. 2009
;
Machleidt and Entem 2011
;
Hammer et al. 2020
. Indeed, the leading IB
\pi N
LECs were recently
Reinert et al. 2021
inferred using
NN
scattering data and fifth-order
\chi
EFT, i.e., N4LO, and found to exhibit no significant charge dependence. In fact, higher-order IB effects are often neglected in quantitative chiral interactions
Piarulli et al. 2015
;
Ekström et al. 2015
;
Reinert et al. 2018
;
Epelbaum et al. 2015a
;
Entem et al. 2017
.

In this paper, we employ
NN
interactions from
\chi
EFT up to
N3LO in Weinberg power counting as defined in Ref.
Machleidt and Entem 2011
, and with the IB effects due to pion mass-splitting in the OPEP and charge-dependent leading
S
-wave contacts. We use nonlocal regulators in relative momenta
p
according to
f(p)=\exp(-p^{2n}/\Lambda^{2n})
with a cutoff
\Lambda=450
MeV and
n=3
. Two-pion exchanges are spectral-function regulated

;
Epelbaum et al. 2004b
with a cutoff of 700 MeV. At
N3LO, to complete the subleading two-pion exchange, we include all two-loop diagrams with some of them evaluated using numerical integration. To remedy the on-shell redundancy

;
Wesolowski et al. 2019
in the
S
-wave contact potential at
N3LO we set the contact fourth-order contact LECs
\widehat{D}_{{}^{1}S_{0}}
,
\widehat{D}_{{}^{3}S_{1}}
, and
\widehat{D}_{{}^{3}S_{1}-^{3}D_{1}}
(in the notation of Ref.
Machleidt and Entem 2011
) to zero. For describing
pp
and
np

Carlsson et al. 2016
, at all chiral orders.

Within this
\chi
EFT framework, we perform a Bayesian study of the
NN
interaction, up to N3LO, conditioned on
NN
scattering data and subsequently extend the LEC posterior using an empirical value for the
{}^{1}S_{0}

nn
scattering length
a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}
. This allows us to quantify the uncertainties of the IB effects due to the short-range LECs in
\chi
EFT. In the process of doing so, we also give an example of the flexibility of the Bayesian framework to straightforwardly expand existing results by introducing new parameters and conditioning on new data. We also test the robustness of a commonly employed model
Furnstahl et al. 2015
;
Wesolowski et al. 2019
for estimating truncation errors in
\chi
EFT.

This paper is organized as follows. In Sec.
II
, we outline the statistical model upon which we base all inferences and demonstrate how to draw samples from the posterior PDFs and analyze the consistency of our inferences. In Sec.
III
, we draw samples from the posterior predictive distributions (PPDs) for scattering lengths and effective ranges in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial wave. We summarize our findings in Sec.
IV
.

II
Statistical method

In this section we explain our method for inferring the joint posterior PDFs for
all
the LECs in the
NN
sector of
\chi
EFT up to N3LO. After the specification of the prior and likelihood, our inference is performed in two stages. In a first step, we apply the HMC algorithm from Ref.
Svensson et al. 2022
to quantify the posterior PDFs for the
NN
LECs in the
np
and
pp
sectors conditioned on
np
and
pp
scattering data. The posteriors presented in this paper account for an uncorrelated
\chi
EFT error model conditioned on the order-by-order convergence pattern up to N3LO. In a second step, we marginalize-in the
nn
non-derivative LEC

\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{nn}
and condition the inference on a single datum: the
nn
scattering length in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial wave,
a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}=-18.9\pm 0.4
fm
Machleidt and Slaus 2001
;
Gardestig 2009
. We note that this is one of the currently accepted values for this scattering length and that there are conflicting experimental values for which the experimental uncertainties are not fully understood. However, we do not account for this additional level of uncertainty. See, e.g., Ref.
Göbel et al. 2021
for a summary of the present status on this topic and the proposal of a novel method to measure the
nn
scattering length. In this paper we only operate with empirical scattering lengths and effective ranges for which electromagnetic (EM) effects have been removed by the originators of those values. Similarly, our theoretical predictions for effective range parameters do not include any EM effects either. We do however include EM effects when we compute scattering cross sections during the HMC sampling.

II.1
Finding an expression for the joint posterior

Using the notation
\textnormal{pr}(A|B)
for the conditional probability that proposition
A
is true given
B
, the posterior PDF of interest
\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|D,I)
can, according to Bayes’ theorem, be evaluated as

\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|D,I)\propto\textnormal{pr}(D|\vec{\alpha},I)\times\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|I),

(1)

i.e., as a product of the data likelihood
\textnormal{pr}(D|\vec{\alpha},I)
times the prior PDF
\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|I)
. Here,
\vec{\alpha}
denotes the vector of LECs to be inferred,
D
is employed data, and
I
encompasses all other assumptions and given information. The given information includes, for example, the chiral order, the regulator cutoff, masses, etc., and we make assumptions about the truncation error, data selection, and so on. Throughout this paper, we omit the overall normalization factor
\textnormal{pr}(D|I)
as it does not play a central role in parameter estimation.

In a first step we use the
np
and
pp
scattering data
D_{np,pp}
contained in the Granada database
Pérez et al. 2013a
;
Pérez et al. 2013b
. In doing so we obtained posteriors for the
\pi N
and contact
NN
LECs, excluding the

\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{nn}
LEC. To simplify the notation we often denote this latter, explicitly charge-dependent,
nn
LEC as
\alpha_{nn}
while all other LECs are collectively denoted
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
. In this notation, the main objective in this paper is to extract the joint posterior
\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|D,I)
of
\vec{\alpha}\equiv(\alpha_{nn},\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp})
conditional on
D=(a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn},D_{np,pp})
.

Using the product rule of probabilities we can write

\begin{split}\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|D,I)&\equiv\textnormal{pr}(\alpha_{nn},\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn},D_{np,pp},I)\textnormal{pr}\left(\alpha_{nn}|\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn},D_{np,pp},I\right)\times\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn},D_{np,pp},I)\\
&=\textnormal{pr}\left(\alpha_{nn}|\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn},I\right)\times\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},I\right),\end{split}

(2)

where we have assumed conditional independence in the final equality.
Thus far, no strong assumptions have been made regarding the relation between
\alpha_{nn}
and
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
, and the analysis is quite general.

We use HMC to sample
\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},I)
, and the strategy that we employ for this is explained in Ref.
Svensson et al. 2022
. In brief, we employ order-by-order differences up to
N3LO, omitting the
LO results, to estimate the variance of the (uncorrelated)
\chi
EFT truncation error for describing scattering data. We place a multivariate Gaussian prior on the subleading
\pi N
LECs at
NNLO and
N3LO using the results from a Roy-Steiner analysis of
\pi N
scattering amplitudes
Siemens et al. 2017
. Furthermore, we place a rather weak prior on the
NN
contact LECs at all orders using an uncorrelated Gaussian PDF with zero mean and standard deviation of
5\times 10^{4}
GeV
-(k+2)
for the LECs belonging to the
k=0,2,4
(
LO,
NLO,
N3LO) contact Lagrangian as defined in Weinberg power counting. The full prior factorizes into independent
NN
and
\pi N
PDFs since we assume no correlation between these sectors. In Sec.
II.2.1
we present further details about the sampling and how we revised the sampling protocol to reach
N3LO. In the remainder of this section we assume that
\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},I)
is known to us.

Next, we extend this posterior by incorporating
\alpha_{nn}
. Using Bayes’ theorem, we rewrite the first factor in the final row of (
2
) according to

\begin{split}\textnormal{pr}(\alpha_{nn}|\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn},I)\propto&\ \textnormal{pr}(a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}|\alpha_{nn},\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},I)\\
&\times\textnormal{pr}(\alpha_{nn}|I),\end{split}

(3)

where we again used that the prior for
\alpha_{nn}
is conditionally independent of
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
. To evaluate the likelihood
\textnormal{pr}(a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}|\alpha_{nn},\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},I)
we numerically compute the scattering length at the specified chiral order in

\begin{split}\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}|D,I)\propto&\ \textnormal{pr}(a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}|\alpha_{nn},\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},I)\\
&\times\textnormal{pr}(\alpha_{nn}|I)\times\textnormal{pr}(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},I).\end{split}

(4)

One could certainly argue, using previous knowledge of IB, that we are in the right to place a narrow prior for
\alpha_{nn}
based on the marginal PDFs for
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{np}
and
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{pp}

\textnormal{pr}(\alpha_{nn}|I)=\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{\alpha}_{nn}^{2})

(5)

We develop the likelihood in (

a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}=a_{\textnormal{th}}^{nn}+\delta a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}+\delta a_{\textnormal{th}}^{nn}.

(6)

This relation introduces the experimental error

\textnormal{pr}(a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}|\alpha_{nn},\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp},I)\propto\exp(-\frac{(\aexpnn- \athnn)^2}{2 (\sigma\subexp^2 + \sigma\subth^2)}),

(7)

where
\sigma_{\textnormal{exp}}^{2}
and

;
Gardestig 2009

;
Furnstahl et al. 2015
;

. We therefore assume that we can express the order-by-order predictions for

a_{\textnormal{th}}^{nn}=a_{\textnormal{ref}}\sum_{i=0}^{k}c_{i}Q^{i}

where

II.2
Evaluating posteriors

In this section we expound on our sampling of the posterior for
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
using HMC and how we combine this posterior with
\alpha_{nn}
to produce a joint posterior (
15
) for all LECs at a given order. For clarity, we now make explicit that the inference of
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
is conditional on a fixed variance
\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2}
of the
\chi
EFT expansion coefficients. A detailed account of how to efficiently sample
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)
using HMC was given in Ref.
Svensson et al. 2022
. Here, we will mainly remark on new developments and results. Our procedure for extracting the joint posterior can be considered a two-step process:

1.

Sample the posterior
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)
using HMC.

2.

Numerically evaluate the joint posterior
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha},\bar{c}^{2}|D,\vec{c},I\right)
in (
15
) using importance sampling.

II.2.1
Step 1: Sampling
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)
using HMC

We follow the steps laid out in Ref.
Svensson et al. 2022
, with two modifications. First, we consistently use all available chiral orders, i.e., up to
N3LO, to learn about the variance of the truncation error. Second, we employ a new method for rapidly tuning the parameters of the HMC algorithm such that we achieve sufficiently high sampling efficiency to perform reliable sampling at
N3LO. This yields an
N3LO posterior that passes all imposed MCMC convergence tests.

Let us first focus on how we learn about the truncation error. In our previous work
Svensson et al. 2022
we limited ourselves to only use information about the
\chi
EFT convergence pattern up to the order at which we were sampling to estimate the variance
\bar{c}^{2}
of the truncation error, and we included the zeroth-order coefficients
\vec{c}_{0}
in the estimation. We then concluded that this procedure typically leads to an underestimation of the size of the truncation error. Here, we follow our own advice and infer the variance
\bar{c}^{2}
from all orders available to us, and exclude the rather uninformative and biasing zeroth-order coefficients. We use the same grid of observables, laboratory energies, and scattering angles, as in our previous work and arrive at
\bar{c}_{np,pp}=4.1
. This value is somewhat greater than the nominally expected natural scale of
\bar{c}\approx 1
, yet nothing too alarming and certainly in line with what we have observed before. As discussed in Sec.
II.1
, this is also the basis for us shifting the prior for the
\chi
EFT truncation error for the effective range towards slightly greater values.

The importance of tuning the HMC parameters, along with various strategies for achieving efficient sampling, is detailed in Ref.
Svensson et al. 2022
. In particular, we stress the importance of a suitable so-called mass matrix: a matrix that accounts for differences in scale between parameters in the sampled PDF. Previously we extracted a performant mass matrix by executing a short preliminary sampling and inverting the covariance matrix of the samples. Here, we instead optimize the LECs with respect to the posterior, i.e., we solve for

\vec{\alpha}_{\star}=\argmax_{\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}}\,\,\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right),

(16)

and estimate the parameter covariance matrix at that optimum. We use the BFGS optimization algorithm
Broyden 1970
;
Fletcher 1970
;
Goldfarb 1970
;
Shanno 1970
which is a so-called quasi-Newton method that relies on first-order gradients to update approximations to the Hessian employed in the Newton algorithm. We employ automatic differentiation (AD)
Griewank 2003
;
Charpentier and Utke 2009
;
Carlsson et al. 2016
to obtain the first-order gradients. In the end, we find that the approximate Hessian at the optimum, as found by the BFGS algorithm, makes for a suitable mass matrix in our application. In our case we have access to higher-order gradients via AD and could, in principle, obtain
\vec{\alpha}_{\star}
using any expedient optimization method and compute an exact-to-machine-precision Hessian. However, we find that BFGS is sufficient for tuning the HMC sampler. Bypassing the need for the
\approx 20\,000
function evaluations required during a preliminary HMC sampling is certainly a great improvement, as the BFGS algorithm typically terminates after at most a few hundred function evaluations. Better yet, the optimization-based method is in our experience more reliable and ultimately yields slightly higher performance in the subsequent sampling of the posterior.

We gather
N\approx 10\,000
samples in each of the ten chains we run at each chiral order. We find integrated autocorrelation times
\tau
(
\tau_{\textnormal{
NLO}}=0.71
,
\tau_{\textnormal{
NNLO}}=0.93
, and
\tau_{\textnormal{
N3LO}}=3.33
) that reach a plateau within very few samples, thus indicating converged HMC chains. The
\tau
value should not be too great since it is inversely proportional to the effective sample size that measures the number of independent samples in the HMC chain. We note that the
NLO and
NNLO autocorrelation times are similar to our previous results
Svensson et al. 2022
, achieved with the more cumbersome method of preliminary samplings. We should reiterate the concerns that always surround the topic of convergence in MCMC. In real-world applications it is not possible for us to declare a chain “converged”. This is because we can never explore the entire parameter space in finite time, and we can miss non-negligible (or even dominating) probability regions. All we can do is to probe the MCMC chains for signs of non-convergence. This issue becomes progressively more pressing as the dimensionality and domain of the parameter space grows, and is especially concerning at
N3LO in our case. With that said, we have searched the parameter domains to the best of our abilities by initializing the BFGS optimization, and the HMC algorithm, at multiple locations and we did not find any signs of multimodality and non-convergence.

II.2.2
Step 2: Evaluating the joint posterior

Equipped with an MCMC chain to represent
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)
, we proceed to evaluate the posterior
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha},\bar{c}^{2}|D,\vec{c},I\right)
in Eq. (
15
). Due to the expected weakness of IB effects in our
\chi
EFT we can be fairly certain that the posterior probability mass of the latter PDF will not be very far from the one of the former. We take advantage of this expectation and proceed by using the principles of sampling/importance sampling
Smith and Gelfand 1992
where we first define a sampling distribution

\begin{split}g(\vec{\alpha},\bar{c}^{2})\equiv&\ \textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)\\
&\times\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|\vec{c},I)\times\pi(\alpha_{nn}),\end{split}

(17)

with
\pi(\alpha_{nn})
a simple, bounded uniform distribution. Since the likelihood for the single
nn
scattering length poses no significant computational challenge, for each chiral order we first explore the
\alpha_{nn}{}
space to identify an interval of values encompassing the bulk of the
probability mass. These intervals are (in units of
10^{4}\,\textnormal{GeV}^{-2}
):
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{nn}\in[-0.1495,-0.1445]
at
NLO,
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{nn}\in[-0.1535,-0.1510]
at
NNLO, and
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{nn}\in[-0.132,-0.125]
at
N3LO. We then sample the full posterior (
15
) using the following procedure:

1.

Pick a sample
(\vec{\alpha}{}_{i},\bar{c}^{2}_{i})
from the sampling distribution (
17
). In practice, we go through the entire MCMC chain of
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
sequentially since we know
Svensson et al. 2022
that these are independent and random samples from
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)
. For each
\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}
sample we draw a sample of
\bar{c}^{2}
from its marginal posterior
\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|\vec{c},I)
, see Eq. (
12
), and a sample of
\alpha_{nn}
from
\pi(\alpha_{nn})
.

2.

Evaluate the ratio
\omega_{i}=\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{i},\bar{c}^{2}_{i}|D,\vec{c},I\right)/g(\vec{\alpha}{}_{i},\bar{c}^{2}_{i})
. Due to the form of our sampling distribution (
17
) and the factorization of the joint posterior (
15
), this ratio is simply the product of the Gaussian likelihood for the scattering length (
7
) and the prior for
\alpha_{nn}
(
5
).

Once we have the lists of samples
(\vec{\alpha}_{i},\bar{c}^{2}_{i})
we compute the normalized (importance) weights
q_{i}=\omega_{i}/\sum_{j}\omega_{j}
. This provides us with a weighted chain of
(\vec{\alpha},\bar{c}^{2})
values distributed as the target posterior
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha},\bar{c}^{2}|D,\vec{c},I\right)
(
15
). The method described here is simple to apply but may not work well in all cases, e.g., if the marginal posterior of
\alpha_{nn}
is relatively unknown and/or defined for a high-dimensional parameter domain. We have validated our results obtained using importance sampling by approximating
\textnormal{pr}\left(\vec{\alpha}_{np,pp}|D_{np,pp},\bar{c}_{np,pp}^{2},I\right)
with a multivariate normal distribution and directly sampling the full distribution (
15
) using HMC. Multivariate normal approximations to all posteriors are provided in the Supplemental Material
sup
.

IV
Summary and Outlook

In this paper, we sampled high-dimensional posteriors up to
N3LO in
\chi
EFT and studied the effects of IB in the
NN
sector. We used Bayesian inference, conditioned on
np
and
pp
scattering data as well as the empirical value
a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}
for the
{}^{1}S_{0}
scattering length in the
nn
channel, to infer posterior PDFs for the LECs at
LO,
NLO,
NNLO, and
N3LO. We split the inference in two steps. First we employed HMC and conditioned the LEC posteriors on
np
and
pp
scattering data and accounted for uncorrelated
\chi
EFT truncation errors. A new approach to tuning the mass matrix, based on posterior optimization, enabled us to extract a converged 31-dimensional posterior also at
N3LO. We find such advancements pivotal for enabling robust MCMC sampling of the
N3LO posterior. In the next step of the inference, we included
a_{\textnormal{exp}}^{nn}
and employed importance sampling to marginalize in the
nn
contact LEC. For the variance of the truncation error in the ERE parameters in the
{}^{1}S_{0}
partial wave we employed a conjugate
\mathcal{IG}
prior. In the end, we find that the resulting LEC posteriors at
NLO and
NNLO match our previous
Svensson et al. 2022
posteriors in the
np
and
pp
isospin sectors and that the LEC
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{nn}
is roughly twice as broad but exhibits a correlation pattern similar to
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{np}
and
\widetilde{C}_{1S0}^{pp}
.

Next, we sampled the PPDs for the ERE parameters and found that our results are consistent with existing point estimates using well-known chiral potentials, with the exception of
a_{pp}
at
N3LO. This outlier might be traced back to insufficient information in the
NN
scattering data set we conditioned the first part of the inference on. When accounting for
\chi
EFT truncation errors, we find that one must go to
NNLO to confidently detect IB effects.

One way to improve a Bayesian analysis of the strong interaction in the low-energy region of relevance to the ERE might be to mix pionless EFT
Bedaque and van
Kolck 2002
;
Hammer et al. 2020
and
\chi
EFT. Indeed,
\chi
EFT harbors an intrinsic uncertainty due to the low-energy scale set by
m_{\pi}
. Since the exact form of the
\chi
EFT expansion parameter
Q
for scattering amplitudes is obscured by the non-perturbative resummation in the Lippmann-Schwinger equation its uncertainty
\textnormal{pr}(Q|I)
should be accounted for. It could then be interesting to apply Bayesian mixture models to combine pionless EFT and
\chi
EFT predictions.

When we condition the inference on low-energy
NN
scattering data with
T_{\textnormal{lab}}\in[0,40.5]
MeV we return the Roy-Steiner priors for the
\pi N
LECs at
NNLO and
N3LO. This is in accordance with
\chi
EFT being a low-energy theory with long-ranged physics governed by the
\pi N
interaction. When conditioning the inference on all
NN
data up to the pion-production threshold at 290 MeV, the marginal
\pi N
LEC posteriors are significantly shifted with respect to the priors. Still, the discrepancies are small on a naturalness scale.

Ab initio
studies
Ekström et al. 2018
;
Jiang et al. 2020
indicate that the inclusion of the
\Delta
isobar yields more realistic predictions for bulk properties of nuclei and nuclear matter. However, a Roy-Steiner prior for the
\pi N
LECs with explicit
\Delta
isobars is less precise
Siemens et al. 2017
. As such, it will also become more important to employ additional nuclear structure and reaction data in the inference and employ error models that account for correlations between the
\chi
EFT expansion coefficients. Although it rapidly becomes computationally challenging to evaluate likelihoods encompassing nuclear data for increasing mass numbers, the development of fast and accurate emulators
König et al. 2020
;
Ekström and Hagen 2019
;
Melendez et al. 2021
;
Zhang and Furnstahl 2022
appears to provide sufficient leverage.
In particular, the present work serves as an example how to sequentially incorporate multiple sets of low-energy nuclear structure and reaction data in a Bayesian framework to perform inference and quantify the theoretical precision.

Appendix A
The appearance of the Student’s
t
distribution

We assume that the
n_{c}
expansion coefficients
\vec{c}=(c_{1},\ldots,c_{n_{c}})
in (
8
) are independent and identically distributed, and drawn from a normal distribution with variance
\bar{c}^{2}
:

\textnormal{pr}(c_{i}|\bar{c}^{2})=\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{c}^{2}).

(22)

We then place an inverse-gamma (
\mathcal{IG}
) prior on
\bar{c}^{2}
, and consequently get an (
\mathcal{IG}
) posterior, (
12
), as well due to conjugacy with respect to the normal distribution:

\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|\vec{c})=\mathcal{IG}(\alpha,\beta),

(23)

where the parameters
\alpha
(shape) and
\beta
(scale) have been updated, via (
13
), by the exposure to
\vec{c}
. We will now show that these assumptions result in a PPD for an unseen coefficient
c_{+}
given by a Student’s
t
distribution, i.e.,

\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})=t_{\nu}(0,\tau^{2}),

(24)

where
\nu
and
\tau
are the degrees of freedom and scale, respectively, of the Student’s
t
distribution using a method of derivation that can be applied in many other situations where conjugate priors are employed.

In general, the PPD
\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})
for a new datum
c_{+}
given a parameter (or parameters)
\bar{c}^{2}
and observed data
\vec{c}
can be expressed as

\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})=\frac{\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\bar{c}^{2},\vec{c})\times\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|\vec{c})}{\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|c_{+},\vec{c})},

(25)

which is easily verified using the product rule of probabilities. We know that
\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|\vec{c})=\mathcal{IG}(\alpha,\beta)
, and
\textnormal{pr}(\bar{c}^{2}|c_{+},\vec{c})
is obviously also an inverse-gamma distribution with further updated parameters
(\alpha_{+},\beta_{+})

\begin{split}\alpha_{+}&=\alpha+\frac{1}{2}=\alpha_{0}+\frac{n_{c}}{2}+\frac{1}{2},\\
\beta_{+}&=\beta+\frac{c_{+}^{2}}{2}=\beta_{0}+\frac{\vec{c}^{\,2}}{2}+\frac{c_{+}^{2}}{2}.\end{split}

(26)

Since
\bar{c}^{2}
is given in
\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\bar{c}^{2},\vec{c})
, this is just a normal distribution as in (
22
). Hence

\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})=\frac{\mathcal{N}(0,\bar{c}^{2})\times\mathcal{IG}(\alpha,\beta)}{\mathcal{IG}(\alpha_{+},\beta_{+})}

(27)

and we obtain for the PPD

\displaystyle\begin{split}\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})={}&\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\bar{c}^{2}}}\exp\left(-\frac{c_{+}^{2}}{2\bar{c}^{2}}\right)\\
{}&\times\frac{\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\times\bar{c}^{2(-\alpha-1)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta}{\bar{c}^{2}}\right)}{\frac{(\beta_{+})^{\alpha_{+}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{+})}\times\bar{c}^{2(-\alpha_{+}-1)}\exp\left(-\frac{\beta_{+}}{\bar{c}^{2}}\right)}.\end{split}

(28)

This is can be written as

\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\frac{\beta^{\alpha}\Gamma(\alpha_{+})}{\beta_{+}^{\alpha_{+}}\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(29)

Using (
26
) we can rewrite this further as

\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\beta}}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+\frac{1}{2})}{\Gamma\left(\alpha\right)}\times\left(1+\frac{c_{+}^{2}}{2\beta}\right)^{-\alpha-\frac{1}{2}}.

(30)

This is the PDF for a Student’s
t
distribution with
\nu=2\alpha
degrees of freedom and scale
\tau=\sqrt{\beta/\alpha}
, i.e.,

\textnormal{pr}(c_{+}|\vec{c})=t_{\nu}\left(0,\tau^{2}\right)=t_{2\alpha}\left(0,\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right).

(31)

One can analogously show that the truncation error is given by

\textnormal{pr}(\delta a_{\textnormal{th}}|\vec{a})=t_{2\alpha}\left(0,a_{\textnormal{ref}}^{2}\frac{Q^{2(k+1)}}{1-Q^{2}}\frac{\beta}{\alpha}\right)

(32)

where
\vec{a}
is a set of observations of
a
that can be transformed into expansion coefficients
\vec{c}
, and
\alpha
and
\beta
are given by (
13
).

Appendix C
Multivariate normal approximations of the LEC posteriors

The LEC posteriors presented in this paper are stored as arrays of samples from which subsequent results are produced. The posteriors are, however, approximately Gaussian, and we therefore provide multivariate normal approximations for convenience. These approximations are available in text format in the supplemental material
sup
. The filenames reflect the chiral order, and the contents are structured as follows:

•

The first line shows the ordering of the LECs.

•

Then follows a vector
\vec{\mu}
of corresponding mean values.

•

Finally, a covariance matrix
\vec{\Sigma}
encodes the uncertainties.

Put together, these quantities define a multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\vec{\mu},\vec{\Sigma})
that approximates the LEC posterior.
