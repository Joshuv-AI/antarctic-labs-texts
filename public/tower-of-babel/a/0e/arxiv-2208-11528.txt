Convergences of looptrees coded by excursions
Robin Khanfir

Abstract
In order to study convergences of looptrees, we construct continuum trees and looptrees from real-valued càdlàg functions without negative jumps called excursions. We then provide a toolbox to manipulate the two resulting codings of metric spaces by excursions and we formalize the principle that jumps correspond to loops and that continuous growths correspond to branches. Combining these codings creates new metric spaces from excursions that we call vernation trees. They consist of a collection of loops and trees glued along a tree structure so that they unify trees and looptrees. We also propose a topological definition for vernation trees, which yields what we argue to be the right space to study convergences of looptrees. However, those first codings lack some functional continuity, so we adjust them. We thus obtain several limit theorems. Finally, we present some probabilistic applications, such as proving an invariance principle for random discrete looptrees. Introduction

Background

Informally, a looptree consists of a collection of loops tangently glued together along some genealogical structure. Perhaps more clearly, one can associate a looptree with a finite ordered tree by replacing each vertex with a circle of length proportional to its degree and each edge with a unique point of tangency between the corresponding circles, thereby preserving the ordered tree structure. An example will be given with Figure
3
in Section
6.3
. Ever since their introduction by Curien and Kortchemski in
[
18
]
, looptrees have generated a growing interest thanks to their natural appearances in some random geometric models and because they can provide useful tools or insights to study other objects. Let us mention some of them. To begin with, when a sequence of trees admits a scaling limit and if the maximum degree becomes negligible against the height, then the scaling limit is often a continuum tree whose all branch points are of infinite degree. In this situation, it is then difficult to recover the limiting joint distribution of the scaled degrees from that object. However, a scaling limit of the associated looptrees would not only give such information (merely via the lengths of the loops) but also the whole asymptotic degree structure. This is exactly what happens for large critical Galton-Watson trees whose offspring distribution belongs to the domain of attraction of an
\alpha
-stable law, with
\alpha\in(1,2)
. While Duquesne showed in
[
21
]
these random trees converge, after suitable scaling, towards the
\alpha
-stable tree introduced in
[
32
,
23
]
, Curien and Kortchemski proved the rescaled associated looptrees converge towards a random compact metric space they called the
\alpha
-stable looptree. There are even more striking cases where the associated looptrees admit a non-degenerate scaling limit but the trees themselves do not so they are an interesting means to give sense to scaling limits of highly dense trees. An instance of such a situation is studied in
[

by Curien, Duquesne, Kortchemski, and Manolescu with the model of random trees built by linear preferential attachment, which was introduced and popularized in
[

In addition to their help to study trees, looptrees spontaneously appear as scaling limits of other random structures. For example, random Boltzmann dissections of a regular polygon, that were introduced in
[
29

, have a similar geometry as looptrees in some regimes. Indeed, Curien and Kortchemski proved in
[

that their scaling limit as the number of sides of the polygon tends to infinity is the
\alpha
-stable looptree, depending on the regime. Let us mention links between looptrees and dissections were also detected in
[
17
]
. Several instances of looptrees can be found within random planar maps too. The goal of this active area of research is to understand universal large-scale properties of graphs or their proper embeddings in the two-dimensional sphere. We refer to
[

37

[
19

[

3/2

[

39

Nevertheless, the most important application of looptrees may be their substantial connections with scaling limits of random planar maps. The bijections of Bouttier, Di Fransesco, Guitter
[
12

and of Janson, Stefánsson
[

Looptrees seem to be the right objects to consider here because the scaling limits of random planar maps with large faces introduced by Le Gall and Miermont in

Motivations and main results

The purpose of the present work is to build a framework and to provide a toolbox to construct, manipulate, and demonstrate convergences involving looptrees under the most general setting possible. A first issue is how to define compact continuum fractal looptrees that may appear as scaling limits. Although compact continuum fractal trees are well-known since the introduction of the Brownian Continuum Random Tree (CRT) by Aldous in
[
3
]
, mimicking the discrete setting to define continuum looptrees associated with them is not so easy. On the one hand, it could be not clear how to choose the lengths of the loops while ensuring the compactness of the resulting space. On the other hand, when the branch points are dense in the tree, it might be possible that two different cycles in the associated looptree would never share a common point. It is indeed what happens for the
\alpha
-stable looptree

of Curien and Kortchemski, with any
\alpha\in(1,2)

[

from the excursion

-stable spectrally positive Lévy process. This process also codes the

(1)

with

Outline

In Section 2, we construct the mentioned pseudo-distances and the metric spaces they induce by quotient. Section 3 gives some tools to better understand the relations between trees, looptrees, and excursions, and shows vernation trees generalize both trees and looptrees. Section 4 studies convergences of vernation trees by first stating the functional continuity of

and then deducing other limit theorems under some particular cases. This results in a proof of Theorem

. Section 6 is devoted to the three probabilistic applications of our work.

Tree, looptree, and vernation tree coded by an excursion

2.1
Genealogy associated with an excursion

In order to encode a looptree by an excursion

, we begin to construct a genealogy on
[0,1]
from

, which will give the tree structure of the loops in the associated looptree. If we take as a principle that

codes a looptree
\mathscr{L}
with root

f(s-)\leq\inf_{[s,t]}f
,
s\leq t\leq r
, and
f(s-)=f(r)
, the excursion
u\mapsto f(s+u(r-s))-f(u-)
should encode a sub-looptree of
\mathscr{L}
, with root
s
, and containing

is an ancestor of

according to the genealogy of

. This motivates the following definition, which is a direct extension of the genealogy defined by Curien and Kortchemski in
[

]
for the stable looptrees. We set for all

s\preceq_{f}t\text{ when both }s\leq t\text{ and }f(s-)\leq\inf_{[s,t]}f.

We also write
s\prec_{f}t
when
s\preceq_{f}t
and
s<t
. If the excursion
f
is obvious according to context, we would just write

Proposition 1
.

The relation
\preceq
enjoys the following properties.

(i)

The relation
\preceq
is a partial order on
[0,1]
.

(ii)

The genealogy admits
0
as its root, namely
0\preceq t
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

(iii)

Two points have a most recent common ancestor. For any
s,t\in[0,1]
, there is a unique

such that for all

r\preceq u\Longleftrightarrow r\preceq s,t
. We denote it by
s\wedge_{f}t
, or just by
s\wedge t
if the excursion
f
is obvious according to context.

(iv)

The ancestral lineages are totally ordered. For any

, the relation
\preceq
induces a total order on
\{s\in[0,1]\ :\ s\preceq t\}
.

Proof.

Let

\preceq

s\preceq t

, so

, and
\preceq
is antisymmetric. To prove the transitivity, we suppose that
s\preceq t
and
t\preceq r
and we can assume
s<t<r
. We find
f(s-)\leq\inf_{[s,t]}f\leq f(t-)\leq\inf_{[t,r]}f
, which entails

and
(i)
. The point
(ii)
is simply deduced from the fact that
f
is an excursion. Let us prove
(iii)
. We set
u=\sup\{r\in[0,1]\ :\ r\preceq s,t\}
. By definition,
u\leq s
and
u\leq t
. We give ourselves a sequence
(r_{n})_{n\geq 1}
that converges towards

0\leq r_{n}\leq r_{n+1}\leq u
and

\displaystyle{f(r_{n}-)\longrightarrow f(u-)}
and thanks to the inequality

f(r_{n}-)\leq\inf_{[r_{n},t]}f\leq\inf_{[u,t]}f,

we obtain
u\preceq t
. Likewise,
u\preceq s
. Conversely, if
r\preceq t
and
r\preceq s
, then
r\leq u
by definition and
f(r-)\leq\inf_{[r,t]}f\leq\inf_{[r,u]}f
, so
r\preceq u
. The uniqueness of
s\wedge t
immediately follows from the antisymmetry of
\preceq
. Let us prove
(iv)
. We suppose
s,r\preceq t
and without loss of generality, we assume
r\leq s
. Then,
f(r-)\leq\inf_{[r,t]}f\leq\inf_{[r,s]}f
, so
r\preceq s
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 1
.

Let
s,t\in[0,1]
. If
s\leq t
, then it holds that
\inf_{[s\wedge t,t]}f=\inf_{[s,t]}f
and

s\wedge t=\sup\left\{r\leq s\ :\ r\preceq t\right\}=\sup\left\{r\leq s\ :\ f(r-)\leq\inf_{[s,t]}f\right\}.

Proof.

If
r\leq s
and
r\preceq t
, then
f(r-)\leq\inf_{[r,t]}f\leq\inf_{[r,s]}f
, and so
r\preceq s
. Thus,
r\preceq s\wedge t
. Conversely, if
r\preceq s\wedge t
, then
r\leq s
and
r\preceq t
. So, we have shown
s\wedge t=\sup\{r\leq s\ :\ r\preceq t\}
. In particular, if
s\wedge t<r\leq s
, then we cannot have
r\preceq t
. It follows that for all
r\in(s\wedge t,s]
, it holds

f(r)\geq f(r-)>\inf_{[r,t]}f

Since
f
is càdlàg, this leads to
\inf_{[r,s]}f>\inf_{[r,t]}f
. Hence,
\inf_{[r,t]}f=\inf_{[s,t]}f
for all
r\in(s\wedge t,s]
and we end the proof by making
r
tend towards
s\wedge t
.
∎

As same as in
[
18
]
, we define a quantity
x_{s}^{t}
which will represent the relative position of the ancestor of
t
on the loop containing
s
on the looptree associated with
f
, when
s\preceq t
. For all
s,t\in[0,1]
, we set

x_{s}^{t}(f)={\bf 1}_{s\leq t}\max\left(\inf_{[s,t]}f-f(s-),0\right).

When the excursion
f
is obvious according to context, we would just write
x_{s}^{t}
. Let us remark that
x_{s}^{t}\in[0,\Delta_{s}]
,
x_{s}^{s}=\Delta_{s}
, and
x_{s}^{t}>0\Longrightarrow s\preceq t
. We finish this first step by providing two convenient lemmas about
x_{s}^{t}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
u,s,t\in[0,1]
. If
u<t\wedge s
, then
x_{u}^{t}=x_{u}^{s}=x_{u}^{t\wedge s}
.

Proof.

We merely write
\inf_{[u,t\wedge s]}f\leq f(t\wedge s-)\leq\min\left(\inf_{[t\wedge s,t]}f,\inf_{[t\wedge s,s]}f\right)
.
∎

Lemma 3
.

Let
s,t\in[0,1]
. If
s<t
, then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}s<r\\
r\prec t\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{t}\leq f(t-)-\inf_{[s,t]}f.

(2)

Proof.

Let
\{r_{i}\ :\ 1\leq i\leq n\}
be a finite subset of
\{r\in[0,1]\ :\ r\prec t,s<r\}
such that
r_{i}<r_{i+1}
for any
1\leq i\leq n-1
. Let us also set
r_{n+1}=t
. With these notations, we have
r_{i+1}\in(r_{i},t]
for any
1\leq i\leq n
, so we can write

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{r_{i}}^{t}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(\inf_{[r_{i},t]}f-f(r_{i}-)\right)\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}f(r_{i+1}-)-f(r_{i}-)=f(t-)-f(r_{1}-).

Eventually,
f(r_{1}-)\geq\inf_{[s,t]}f
because
r_{1}\in(s,t]
. The lemma follows.
∎

2.2
Pseudo-distances coded by an excursion

We denote by
(\mathcal{C},\delta)
the metric circle of perimeter
1
, seen as
[0,1]
endowed with the pseudo-distance
\delta
given by
\delta(a,b)=\min(|a-b|,1-|a-b|)
so that
0
and
1
are identified. We say that a function is càglàd when it is left-continuous with right limits (
continue à gauche, limite à droite
in French). We denote by
\overleftarrow{D}([0,1],\mathcal{C})
the set of càglàd functions from
[0,1]
to
\mathcal{C}
and we endow it with the Skorokhod distance
\overleftarrow{\rho}
given by

\overleftarrow{\rho}(\phi_{1},\phi_{2})=\inf_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\max\left(||\lambda-\mathsf{id}||_{\infty},\sup_{x\in[0,1]}\delta(\phi_{1}(x),\phi_{2}\circ\lambda(x))\right)

for all
\phi_{1},\phi_{2}\in\overleftarrow{D}([0,1],\mathcal{C})
, where
\Lambda
is the set of increasing bijections from
[0,1]
into itself.

Definition 2
.

A
shuffle
is a Borel application
\Phi:\Delta>0\longmapsto\phi_{\Delta}\in\overleftarrow{D}([0,1],\mathcal{C})
such that for all
\Delta>0
,
\phi_{\Delta}
is surjective,

\sup_{u\in(0,\Delta]}\sup_{x\in(0,1]}\left|\frac{2}{x}\delta\left(\phi_{u}(0),\phi_{u}(x)\right)-1\right|<\infty,\text{ and }\sup_{x\in(0,1]}\left|\frac{2}{x}\delta\left(\phi_{\Delta}(0),\phi_{\Delta}(x)\right)-1\right|\underset{\Delta\rightarrow 0+}{\longrightarrow}0.

(3)

Remark 1
.

Our requirements for the
\phi_{\Delta}
to be surjective with the convergence in (
3
) lead to the fact that the
\phi_{\Delta}
have to oscillate more and more quickly near
x=0
when
\Delta
is small enough. Moreover, when
\Delta
gets smaller,
\phi_{\Delta}
needs to jump an infinite number of times near
0
. As a result, there is no simple or canonical choice for
\Phi
. Nevertheless, it is not too difficult to construct various examples of shuffles. For instance, we could choose
\phi_{\Delta}(x)=x
when
\Delta\geq 1
and

\phi_{1-\Delta}(x)=\begin{cases}x-\frac{\Delta^{k}}{2}&\text{ if }x\in\left(\frac{\Delta^{k}+\Delta^{k+1}}{2},\Delta^{k}\right]\text{ with an integer }k\\
1-x+\frac{\Delta^{k+1}}{2}&\text{ if }x\in\left(\Delta^{k+1},\frac{\Delta^{k}+\Delta^{k+1}}{2}\right]\text{ with an integer }k\\
0&\text{ if }x=0\end{cases}

when
\Delta\in(0,1)
, for all
x\in[0,1]
.

For the following, we fix a shuffle and let the dependence on it be implicit. As we said before, the loops of our space will match the jumps of
f
, and the lengths of the loops will be equal to the heights of the jumps. For
t\in[0,1]
, we provide the pseudo-distances

\displaystyle\delta_{t}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}\delta\left(\frac{a}{\Delta_{t}},\frac{b}{\Delta_{t}}\right)=\min\left(|a-b|,\Delta_{t}-|a-b|\right),

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}_{t}(a,b)

\displaystyle=\Delta_{t}\delta\left(\phi_{\Delta_{t}}\left(\frac{a}{\Delta_{t}}\right),\phi_{\Delta_{t}}\left(\frac{b}{\Delta_{t}}\right)\right)

to the segment
[0,\Delta_{t}]
when
\Delta_{t}>0
. When
\Delta_{t}=0
, we set
\delta_{t}(0,0)=\tilde{\delta}_{t}(0,0)=0
. If needed, we would precise the dependence on
f
by writing
\delta_{t}^{f}(\cdot,\cdot)
or
\tilde{\delta}_{t}^{f}(\cdot,\cdot)
. Let us notice that
([0,\Delta_{t}],\delta_{t})
and
([0,\Delta_{t}],\tilde{\delta}_{t})
are isometric to a circle of length
\Delta_{t}
.

We are now ready to define our pseudo-distances of interest. For
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s\preceq t
, we set

\displaystyle d^{\circ}_{f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\prec r\preceq t}\delta_{r}(0,x_{r}^{t}),

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}^{\circ}_{f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{s\prec r\preceq t}\tilde{\delta}_{r}(0,x_{r}^{t}).

It is the distance to run through between
t
and its ancestor on the loop of
s
. Indeed, each term of the sum corresponds to the length of the path getting through one of the loops between
s
and
t
. For
\widetilde{d}^{\circ}
, the points where two loops are glued were shuffled. We reach
t
from
s
by going through the loop of
t\wedge s
. Explicitly, we set

\displaystyle d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\delta_{s\wedge t}(x_{s\wedge t}^{s},x_{s\wedge t}^{t})+d^{\circ}_{f}(s\wedge t,s)+d^{\circ}_{f}(s\wedge t,t),

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\tilde{\delta}_{s\wedge t}(x_{s\wedge t}^{s},x_{s\wedge t}^{t})+\widetilde{d}^{\circ}_{f}(s\wedge t,s)+\widetilde{d}^{\circ}_{f}(s\wedge t,t),

for all
s,t\in[0,1]
. The pseudo-distance
d^{\mathsf{L}}
is exactly the one constructed by Curien and Kortchemski in
[
18
]
for the stable looptrees. Now, we want to define a pseudo-distance from
f
that should induce a tree. For
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s\preceq t
, we set

d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s,t)=f(t)-\inf_{[s,t]}f-\sum_{s\prec r\preceq t}x_{r}^{t}.

While the term
f(t)-\inf_{[s,t]}f
should correspond to the distance to travel if the metric space was a tree, the excursion has jumps we have to erase because we do not want to count the distance traveled on loops. Thus, we subtract the terms
x_{r}^{t}
that are already counted in the term
f(t)-\inf_{[s,t]}f
. More generally, if
s,t\in[0,1]
, we set

d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t,s)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t\wedge s,t)+d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t\wedge s,s).

Observe the definition of
d^{\mathsf{T}}
is consistent because
d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,s)=f(s)-f(s)=0
. In order to combine the paths on the loops and on the tree branches, we set

\displaystyle d^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}

\displaystyle=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}+2d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f},

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}

\displaystyle=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}+2\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}.

The constant
2
before
d^{\mathsf{L}}
(or
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
) could be replaced with another one but we will justify this choice by the fact that
f\longmapsto\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}
enjoys a functional continuity with the constant
2
. It would be not the case with another constant. Nevertheless, Marzouk showed in
[
35
]
that metrics
d^{\mathsf{L}}+ad^{\mathsf{T}}
with
a\neq 1/2
can naturally appear as scaling limits of uniformly random discrete looptrees with prescribed degrees. When the function
f
is obvious according to context, we will just write
d^{\circ}
,
\widetilde{d}^{\circ}
,
d^{\mathsf{L}}
,
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
,
d^{\mathsf{T}}
,
d^{\mathsf{V}}
, and
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}
. It is important to be aware that the shuffle
\Phi
was used to construct
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
and
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}
, but not for
d^{\mathsf{L}}
,
d^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
d^{\mathsf{V}}
.

We now show our quantities are finite. The functions
d^{\circ}
,
\widetilde{d}^{\circ}
,
d^{\mathsf{L}}
, and
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
obviously are non-negative because they are series of non-negative terms. Keeping only the term for
r=t
yields that if
s\prec t
then
d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,t)\leq f(t-)-\inf_{[s,t]}f
. Moreover, the bound (
2
) implies that if
s\prec t
then

\displaystyle 0\leq d^{\circ}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq f(t-)-\inf_{[s,t]}f,

(4)

\displaystyle 0\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq f(t-)-\inf_{[s,t]}f,

(5)

because
\delta_{t}(0,x_{t}^{t})=\delta_{t}(0,\Delta_{t})=0
and
\delta(0,x)\leq x
for all
x\in[0,1]
. If we only have
s\preceq t
, the upper bound given by
f(t)-\inf_{[s,t]}f
stays true. Now, we only assume
s<t
. We observe
t\wedge s\leq s<t
and
\delta_{t\wedge s}(x_{s\wedge t}^{s},x_{s\wedge t}^{t})\leq x_{t\wedge s}^{s}-x_{t\wedge s}^{t}
. Recall we know
\inf_{[s,t]}f=\inf_{[t\wedge s,t]}f
from Lemma
1
, so if
s<t
then

\displaystyle 0\leq d^{\mathsf{L}}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq f(s)+f(t-)-2\inf_{[s,t]}f,

(6)

\displaystyle 0\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,t)

\displaystyle\leq f(s)+f(t-)-2\inf_{[s,t]}f.

(7)

Remark we cannot find these bounds for
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
because
\tilde{\delta}_{t}(0,\Delta_{t})\neq 0
and
\tilde{\delta}_{t}(a,b)\nleq|a-b|
. Nethertheless, the bound in (
3
) ensures there exists a constant
K\in(0,\infty)
which only depends on
||f||_{\infty}
and
\Phi
such that
\tilde{\delta}_{t}(0,x)\leq Kx
for any
t\in[0,1]
and
x\in[0,\Delta_{t}]
. In particular, if
s\preceq t
, then the bound (
2
) implies

0\leq\widetilde{d}^{\circ}(s,t)\leq K\left(f(t)-\inf_{[s,t]}f\right).

(8)

While the definitions of these quantities are intuitive and natural, the fact that
s\wedge t
appears could make them a little troublesome to manipulate, from a computational point of view. To handle this issue, we verify that for all
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s\leq t
, it holds

\displaystyle d^{\mathsf{L}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in[0,1]}\delta_{r}(x_{r}^{s},x_{r}^{t}),

(9)

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{r\in[0,1]}\tilde{\delta}_{r}(x_{r}^{s},x_{r}^{t}),

(10)

\displaystyle d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,t)

\displaystyle=f(s)+f(t)-2\inf_{[s,t]}f-\sum_{r\in[0,1]}|x_{r}^{s}-x_{r}^{t}|.

(11)

Indeed, Lemma
2
ensures
x_{r}^{s}=x_{r}^{t}
when
r\prec t\wedge s
, which yields

\displaystyle\delta_{r}(x_{r}^{s},x_{r}^{t})

\displaystyle=\delta_{r}(0,x_{r}^{s}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t\prec r\preceq s}+\delta_{r}(0,x_{r}^{t}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t\prec r\preceq t}+\delta_{r}(x_{r}^{s},x_{r}^{t}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t=r}

\displaystyle\tilde{\delta}_{r}(x_{r}^{s},x_{r}^{t})

\displaystyle=\tilde{\delta}_{r}(0,x_{r}^{s}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t\prec r\preceq s}+\tilde{\delta}_{r}(0,x_{r}^{t}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t\prec r\preceq t}+\tilde{\delta}_{r}(x_{r}^{s},x_{r}^{t}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t=r},

\displaystyle|x_{r}^{s}-x_{r}^{t}|

\displaystyle=x_{r}^{s}{\bf 1}_{s\wedge t\prec r\preceq s}+x_{r}^{t}{\bf 1}_{s\wedge t\prec r\preceq t}+\left(\inf_{[r,s]}f-\inf_{[r,t]}f\right){\bf 1}_{s\wedge t=r}.

Thus, (
9
) and (
10
) follow and Lemma
1
leads to (
11
). Let us show our quantities are pseudo-distances.

Proposition 2
.

The functions
d^{\mathsf{L}}
,
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
, and
d^{\mathsf{T}}
are pseudo-distances on
[0,1]
. As linear combinations of pseudo-distances,
d^{\mathsf{V}}
and
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}
are also pseudo-distances on
[0,1]
.

Proof.

We already proved these functions are well-defined and non-negative. The symmetries are obvious. Let
t\in[0,1]
, it holds clearly
d^{\mathsf{L}}(t,t)=\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}(t,t)=0
and we already saw
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,t)=0
. Let
s,r\in[0,1]
. We want to prove the triangular inequalities on
d^{\mathsf{L}}(t,r)
,
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}(t,r)
, and
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,r)
. The formula (
9
) makes it easy for
d^{\mathsf{L}}
as a simple application of the triangular inequality of
\delta_{u}
:

d^{\mathsf{L}}(t,r)=\sum_{u\in[0,1]}\delta_{u}(x_{u}^{t},x_{u}^{r})\leq\sum_{u\in[0,1]}\left(\delta_{u}(x_{u}^{t},x_{u}^{s})+\delta_{u}(x_{u}^{s},x_{u}^{r})\right)=d^{\mathsf{L}}(t,s)+d^{\mathsf{L}}(s,t).

The proof for
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}
is identical with (
10
) but the proof for
d^{\mathsf{T}}
requires some more care. We know either
s\wedge r\preceq t\wedge s
or
t\wedge s\preceq s\wedge r
, because the ancestral lineage of
s
is totally ordered. By symmetry, we can assume
s\wedge r\preceq t\wedge s
without loss of generality. Then, we also have
s\wedge r\preceq t\wedge r
. Thanks to the total order of the ancestral lineage of
t
, it holds either
t\wedge r\preceq t\wedge s
or
t\wedge s\prec t\wedge r
. If
t\wedge r\preceq t\wedge s
, then
t\wedge r\preceq s\wedge r
, and by antisymmetry of
\preceq
, we have
t\wedge r=s\wedge r
. Likewise, if
t\wedge s\prec t\wedge r
then
t\wedge s\preceq s\wedge r
and
t\wedge s=s\wedge r
.
From here, we treat the two cases separately. Recall Lemma
2
which we will use several times.

•

If
t\wedge r=s\wedge r\preceq t\wedge s
: We can write
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,r)=d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,r)
. Let us begin by bounding the distance between
t\wedge r
and
t
. Because
t\wedge r\preceq t\wedge s\preceq t
, we can easily make the distance between
t\wedge s
and
t
appear, which gives

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+\left(\inf_{[t\wedge s,t]}f-\inf_{[t\wedge r,t]}f\right)-\sum_{t\wedge r\prec u\preceq t\wedge s}x_{u}^{t}.

Now, we use Lemma
2
to transform the sum indexed by
s\wedge r=t\wedge r\prec u\prec t\wedge s
. We point out that the lemma does not allow us to transform the term for
u=t\wedge s
, thus we isolate it. Then, we recognize some terms of the distance between
s\wedge r
and
s
, namely

\sum_{t\wedge r\prec u\preceq t\wedge s}x_{u}^{t}=\sum_{s\wedge r\prec u\prec t\wedge s}x_{u}^{s}+x_{t\wedge s}^{t}{\bf 1}_{s\wedge r<t\wedge s}.

We make
d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,s)
appear by adding and subtracting the
x_{u}^{s}
for
t\wedge s\preceq u\preceq s
, so that

-\sum_{t\wedge r\prec u\preceq t\wedge s}x_{u}^{t}=d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,s)+\inf_{[s\wedge r,s]}f-d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,s)-\inf_{[t\wedge s,s]}f-(x_{t\wedge s}^{t}-x_{t\wedge s}^{s}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge r<t\wedge s}.

Since
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,s)\geq 0
, it follows

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,s)-(x_{s\wedge r}^{t}-x_{s\wedge r}^{s})+(x_{t\wedge s}^{t}-x_{t\wedge s}^{s}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge r=t\wedge s}.

Then, we remark that
-(x_{s\wedge r}^{t}-x_{s\wedge r}^{s})+(x_{t\wedge s}^{t}-x_{t\wedge s}^{s}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge r=t\wedge s}=-(x_{s\wedge r}^{t}-x_{s\wedge r}^{s}){\bf 1}_{s\wedge r<t\wedge s}
, so Lemma
2
ensures
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,s)
. Eventually, we find

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,r)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,r)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,s)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,s)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,r).

•

If
t\wedge s=s\wedge r\prec t\wedge r
: In this case, the triangular inequality is rougher, so its proof is simpler. To bound
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)
, we first make
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)
appear by writing

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+\inf_{[t\wedge s,t]}f-\inf_{[t\wedge r,t]}f+\sum_{t\wedge s\prec u\preceq t\wedge r}x_{u}^{t}.

We transform the sum thanks to Lemma
2
once again and we see
\inf_{[t\wedge r,t]}f=x_{t\wedge r}^{t}-x_{t\wedge r}^{t\wedge r}+f(t\wedge r)
, so that we get

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+\inf_{[t\wedge s,t]}f-\inf_{[t\wedge s,t\wedge r]}f-d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t\wedge r).

Lemma
2
also implies
\inf_{[t\wedge s,t]}f=\inf_{[t\wedge s,t\wedge r]}f
. Plus,
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t\wedge r)
is non-negative, so it follows
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge r,t)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)
. Thanks to the symmetry between
t
and
r
in this case, we find

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,r)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(t\wedge s,t)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s\wedge r,r)\leq d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,s)+d^{\mathsf{T}}(s,r).

∎

Remark 2
.

The triangular inequality for
d^{\mathsf{T}}
will become clearer later. Indeed, we will prove with Theorem
4
that there exists a continuous excursion
g
with
g(0)=0
such that
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}=d^{\mathrm{clas}}_{g}
, where
d^{\mathrm{clas}}
is the classical tree pseudo-distance defined by (
1
). In fact, Marzouk defines the same pseudo-distance

as such in
[

]
.

2.3
Quotient metric spaces induced by those pseudo-distances

A
pointed metric space
is a triple
(X,d,a)
where
(X,d)
is a metric space equipped with a distinguished point, also called a root,
a\in X
. We say two pointed metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1},a_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2},a_{2})
are
pointed-isometric
when there exists an bijective isometry
\lambda
from
X_{1}
to
X_{2}
such that
\lambda(a_{1})=a_{2}
. A
pointed weighted metric space
is a quadruple
(X,d,a,\mu)
where
(X,d,a)
is a pointed metric space also equipped with a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
(X,d)
. We say two pointed weighted metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1},a_{1},\mu_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2},a_{2},\mu_{2})
are
GHP-isometric
when there exists an bijective isometry
\lambda
from
X_{1}
to
X_{2}
such that
\lambda(a_{1})=a_{2}
and
\lambda_{*}\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}
. When no confusion is possible, we will denote a metric space (possibly endowed with a root and/or a Borel probability measure) by its underlying set. Finally, when
c>0
, we write
c\cdot(X,d,a,\mu)=(X,cd,a,\mu)
for the pointed weighted metric space obtained after multiplying all distances by
c
. As same, we write
c\cdot(X,d,a)=(X,cd,a)
and
c\cdot(X,d)=(X,cd)

First properties and an example

.

continuous excursion
when
\Delta_{t}=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Notice that it do not only require
f
to be continuous on
[0,1]
but also that
f(0)=0
. We say
f
is a
pure jump growth (PJG) excursion
when
\displaystyle{f(t)=\sum_{r\preceq t}x_{r}^{t}}
for all
t\in[0,1]
.

The following lemma is practical to both wrap one’s head around the notion of PJG excursions and to identify them in simple cases.

Lemma 4
.

Let
f
be an excursion. If there exists a partition
0=r_{0}<r_{1}<...<r_{n}<r_{n+1}=1
such that
f
is non-increasing on
[r_{i},r_{i+1})
for all
0\leq i\leq n
, then
f
is PJG.

Proof.

First, observe that
f
is PJG if and only if
d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,1)=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
. Indeed,
x_{s}^{1}\leq f(1)=0
for all
s\in[0,1]
so the identity (
11
) becomes
\displaystyle{d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,1)=f(t)-\sum_{r\preceq t}x_{r}^{t}}
. We prove the lemma by induction on
n
. If
n=0
, then
f
is non-increasing on
[0,1]
so

d^{\mathsf{T}}(t,1)\leq f(t)-x_{0}^{t}=f(t)-\inf_{[0,t]}f=0,

and it follows that
f
is PJG. If
n\geq 1
, we set
\bar{r}=\inf\{r\geq r_{n}\ :\ f(r)=f(r_{n}-)\}
so that
r_{n}\preceq\bar{r}
and
f(r_{n}-)=f(\bar{r})=f(\bar{r}-)
. We can assume
r_{n}<\bar{r}
because otherwise,
f(r_{n})=f(r_{n}-)
which means that
f
is in fact non-increasing on
[r_{n-1},1]
and so
f
is PJG by induction. We apply the branching property as given by Proposition
6
with
u=r_{n}
and
v=\bar{r}
and we keep the same notations. It is easy to see that
h
is non-increasing on
[0,1]
and that
g
is non-increasing on
[r_{n-1},1]
and on
[r_{i},r_{i+1})
for all
0\leq i\leq n-2
. By induction,
g
and
h
are PJG, so
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{g}(t,1)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{h}(t,1)=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
. The branching property then ensures
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t,1)=0
for all
t\in[0,1]
, so
f
is PJG.
∎

For any
\varepsilon>0
, we define the two following operators.

\displaystyle J:\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle J^{\varepsilon}:\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\mathcal{H}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto Jf:t\mapsto\sum_{r\preceq t}x_{r}^{t}

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto J^{\varepsilon}f:t\mapsto\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\preceq t\\
\Delta_{r}\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{t}

The operator
J
is the same as in Theorem
1
and the operator
J^{\varepsilon}
will be necessary for some approximations later. We are now ready to formulate the decomposition of an excursion into a sum of a continuous excursion with a PJG excursion, and how this decomposition translates into the associated pseudo-distances.

Theorem 4
.

Let
f
be an excursion and let
\varepsilon>0
. The following points hold.

(i)

The function
f-Jf
is a continuous excursion and the functions
Jf
and
J^{\varepsilon}f
are PJG excursions. Moreover, for any
t\in[0,1]
,
\Delta_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)>0\Longrightarrow\Delta_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)\geq\varepsilon
.

(ii)

The excursion
f
is PJG if and only if
Jf=f
.

(iii)

The excursion
f
is a continuous excursion if and only if
Jf=0
.

(iv)

We have
Jf=J^{\varepsilon}f
if and only if for any
t\in[0,1]
,
\Delta_{t}(f)>0\Longrightarrow\Delta_{t}(f)\geq\varepsilon
.

(v)

The function
f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f
is an excursion and
J(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)=J^{\varepsilon}f
.

Proof.

For any
t\in[0,1]
, the quantities
Jf(t)
and
J^{\varepsilon}f(t)
are sums of non-negative terms, so
Jf(t)\geq 0
and
J^{\varepsilon}f(t)\geq 0
. Plus,
x_{t}^{1}(f)\leq f(1)=0
so
Jf(1)=J^{\varepsilon}f(1)=0=f(1)
. It is clear
f(0)-J(0)=0
. As seen in the proof of the above lemma, we have

d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t,1)=f(t)-Jf(t).

(12)

Since
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}
is a continuous pseudo-distance, this immediately gives that
f-Jf
is continuous and non-negative on
[0,1]
, that
Jf
is càdlàg, and that
\Delta_{t}(Jf)=\Delta_{t}(f)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. We remark the fact that
f
is an excursion implies that for any
r\in[0,1]
, the function
t\in[0,1]\longmapsto x_{r}^{t}(f)
is càdlàg with a unique jump at
r
, which has height
\Delta_{r}(f)
. Hence, the expression

J^{\varepsilon}f(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{t}(f)

shows
J^{\varepsilon}f
is càdlàg as a finite sum of càdlàg functions and
\Delta_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)=\Delta_{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{t}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
. Let us show that
Jf
and
J^{\varepsilon}f
are PJG to complete the proof of the point
(i)
. The set
\{s\in[0,1]\ :\ \Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon\}
is finite, so it provides a partition
0=s_{0}<s_{1}<...<s_{n-1}<s_{n}=1
such that
J^{\varepsilon}f
is non-increasing on
[s_{i},s_{i+1})
for all
0\leq i\leq n-1
, as a finite sum of non-increasing functions. Hence,
J^{\varepsilon}f
is PJG according to Lemma
4
. Let
s,t,u\in[0,1]
such that
s\leq u\leq t
. If
s\preceq_{f}t
then
s\preceq_{f}u
and

Jf(u)=Jf(s-)+f(u)-f(s-)-d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s,u)

thanks to (
12
) and Corollary
1
. The inequality (
5
) leads to

x_{s}^{u}\leq Jf(u)-Jf(s-)\leq f(u)-f(s-).

By taking the infimum over
u
, we can deduce that if
s\preceq_{f}t
then
x_{s}^{t}(Jf)=x_{s}^{t}(f)
. Now, we set
\bar{s}=\inf\{r\geq s\ :\ f(r)=f(s-)\}
, so that
s\preceq_{f}\bar{s}
but
x_{s}^{\bar{s}}(f)=0
. Then, if we do not have
s\preceq_{f}t
then
\bar{s}<t
, and
x_{s}^{t}(Jf)\leq x_{s}^{\bar{s}}(Jf)=x_{s}^{\bar{s}}(f)=0=x_{s}^{t}(f)
. Thus, we have proven that for all
s,t\in[0,1]
,

x_{s}^{t}(Jf)=x_{s}^{t}(f).

(13)

It follows that
Jf
is PJG because

Jf(t)=\sum_{r\in[0,1]}x_{r}^{t}(f)=\sum_{r\in[0,1]}x_{r}^{t}(Jf)=\sum_{r\preceq_{Jf}t}x_{r}^{t}(Jf).

The point
(ii)
is a rewording of the definition of PJG excursions. The point
(iii)
follows from the definition of
J
and from (
12
), by continuity of
d^{\mathsf{T}}
. Proving the point
(iv)
is easy by using the identities
\Delta_{t}(Jf)=\Delta_{t}(f)
and
\Delta_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)=\Delta_{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{t}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
.

It remains to prove the point
(v)
. We set
g=f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f
. First, it is easy to see that
g
is indeed an excursion with the expression
g(t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t,1)+J^{\varepsilon}f(t)
inherited from (
12
). Plus, it also gives
\Delta_{t}(g)=\Delta_{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)=\Delta_{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{t}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
. Also recall
\Delta_{t}(Jf)=\Delta_{t}(f)
. Let
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s\preceq_{f}t
and
u\in[s,t]
, so that
s\preceq_{f}u
. We obtain

Jf(u)-J^{\varepsilon}f(u)\geq\left(Jf(s)-\Delta_{s}(f)\right)-\left(J^{\varepsilon}f(s)-\Delta_{s}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}\right)

(14)

by expressing the left-hand-side as a series over
r\in[0,1]
, then by cutting the series at
s
, and by applying Lemma
2
. A similar trick together with Corollary
1
leads to

d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(u,1)+J^{\varepsilon}f(u)\geq d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s,1)+\left(J^{\varepsilon}f(s)-\Delta_{s}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}\right)+x_{s}^{u}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}.

(15)

The expressions for
\Delta_{s}(Jf)
,
\Delta_{s}(J^{\varepsilon}f)
, and
g
and the bounds (
14
) and (
15
) eventually get us

x_{s}^{u}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}\leq g(u)-g(s-)\leq f(u)-f(s-),

which yields
x_{s}^{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}\leq x_{s}^{t}(g)\leq x_{s}^{t}(f)
by taking the infimum over
u
. But as
x_{s}^{t}(g)\leq\Delta_{s}(g)=\Delta_{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
, we deduce that if
s\preceq_{f}t
then
x_{s}^{t}(g)=x_{s}^{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
. Conversely, the same argument as for
Jf
gives that if we do not have
s\preceq_{f}t
then
x_{s}^{t}(g)=x_{s}^{t}(f)=0
. Thus, we have shown that for all
s,t\in[0,1]
,

x_{s}^{t}(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)=x_{s}^{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s}(f)\geq\varepsilon}.

(16)

Finally, we end the proof by writing

J(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)(t)=\sum_{r\in[0,1]}x_{r}^{t}(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{t}(f)=J^{\varepsilon}f(t).

∎

Theorem 5
.

Let
f
be an excursion. It holds
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f-Jf}=d^{\mathrm{clas}}_{f-Jf}
,
d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}=d^{\mathsf{L}}_{Jf}
, and
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}=\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{Jf}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle d^{\mathsf{L}}_{J^{\varepsilon}f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{r}^{f}\left(x_{r}^{s}(f),x_{r}^{t}(f)\right),

(17)

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{J^{\varepsilon}f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}\left(x_{r}^{s}(f),x_{r}^{t}(f)\right),

(18)

for all
s,t\in[0,1]
.

Before proving the latter theorem, we sum up why vernation trees can be considered as a natural unification of trees and looptrees. On the one hand, if
f
is a continuous excursion then
d^{\mathsf{L}}=0
and
d^{\mathsf{V}}=d^{\mathsf{T}}=d^{\mathrm{clas}}
, so
f
naturally codes a real tree. On the other hand, if
f
is a PJG excursion then
d^{\mathsf{T}}=0
and
d^{\mathsf{V}}=2d^{\mathsf{L}}
, so
f
naturally codes a looptree. Conversely, each excursion has a natural continuous part, which codes its tree distance
d^{\mathsf{T}}
, and a natural PJG part, which codes its looptree distance
d^{\mathsf{L}}
. Observe Theorem
1
is a direct consequence of Theorems
4
and
5
.

Proof.

Recall (
1
) for the definition of
d^{\mathrm{clas}}
. Let
s,t\in[0,1]
with
s<t
. The identities (
12
) and (
11
) yield

d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f-Jf}(s,t)=d^{\mathrm{clas}}_{f-Jf}(s,t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s,1)+d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t,1)-2\inf_{u\in[s,t]}d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(u,1)

because
f-Jf
is a continuous excursion. If
u\in[s,t]
then
s\wedge_{f}t\preceq_{f}u
so
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(u,1)\geq d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s\wedge_{f}t,1)
by Corollary
1
. Moreover, Lemma
1
and the inequality (
5
) allow us to bound

\inf_{u\in[s,t]}d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}\left(s\wedge_{f}t,u\right)\leq\inf_{u\in[s,t]}f(u)-\inf_{[s,t]}f=0.

Hence, we conclude

d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f-Jf}(s,t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s,1)+d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(t,1)-2d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s\wedge_{f}t,1)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s\wedge_{f}t,s)+d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s\wedge_{f}t,t)=d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}(s,t)

with another application of Corollary
1
. Thanks to (
13
), it holds
\Delta_{r}(Jf)=\Delta_{r}(f)
and
x_{r}^{t}(Jf)=x_{r}^{t}(f)
for all
r\in[0,1]
. The formula (
9
) then implies
d^{\mathsf{L}}_{Jf}=d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}
, and the formula (
10
) implies
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{Jf}=\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}
. The same argument can be applied to
f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f
, so that
\Delta_{r}(J^{\varepsilon}f)=\Delta_{r}(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)
and
x_{r}^{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)=x_{r}^{t}(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)
thanks to the point
(v)
of Theorem
4
. Furthermore, the identity (
16
) ensures
\Delta_{r}(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)=\Delta_{r}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
and
x_{r}^{t}(f-Jf+J^{\varepsilon}f)=x_{r}^{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon}
. Eventually, the formulas (
9
) and (
10
) lead to

\displaystyle d^{\mathsf{L}}_{J^{\varepsilon}f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(J^{\varepsilon}f)>0\end{subarray}}\delta_{r}^{J^{\varepsilon}f}\left(x_{r}^{s}(J^{\varepsilon}f),x_{r}^{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{r}^{f}\left(x_{r}^{s}(f),x_{r}^{t}(t)\right),

\displaystyle\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{J^{\varepsilon}f}(s,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(J^{\varepsilon}f)>0\end{subarray}}\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{J^{\varepsilon}f}\left(x_{r}^{s}(J^{\varepsilon}f),x_{r}^{t}(J^{\varepsilon}f)\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}\left(x_{r}^{s}(f),x_{r}^{t}(t)\right).

∎

Corollary 2
.

For all excursion
f
and
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
, it holds

\displaystyle||d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}-d^{\mathsf{L}}_{J^{\varepsilon}f}||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2||Jf-J^{\varepsilon}f||_{\infty},

\displaystyle||\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{L}}_{J^{\varepsilon}f}||_{\infty}

\displaystyle\leq 2K||Jf-J^{\varepsilon}f||_{\infty},

where
K
is a finite constant which only depends on the shuffle
\Phi
, as

K=\sup_{\Delta\in(0,1]}\sup_{x\in(0,1]}\frac{1}{x}\delta\left(\phi_{\Delta}(0),\phi_{\Delta}(x)\right).

Proof.

From the definition of a shuffle, we know
K
is indeed finite. By definition, we have
\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}(0,x)\leq Kx
for any
r\in[0,1]
such that
\Delta_{r}(f)\leq 1
and for any
x\in[0,\Delta_{r}(f)]
. Plus, it is clear that
\delta_{r}^{f}(0,x)\leq x
. It follows

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\delta_{r}^{f}\left(0,x_{r}^{t}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{t}(f)=Jf(t)-J^{\varepsilon}f(t),

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}\left(0,x_{r}^{t}(f)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\varepsilon\end{subarray}}Kx_{r}^{t}(f)=K\left(Jf(t)-J^{\varepsilon}f(t)\right).

We conclude by applying the formulas (
9
), (
10
), (
17
), and (

), as well as the triangular inequalities of the
\delta_{r}^{f}
and the
\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}
.
∎

We finish this section by providing some tools to study convergences involving
J
or
J^{\varepsilon}
.

Proposition 7
.

Let
f
be an excursion,
\varepsilon>0
, and let
\lambda:[0,1]\longrightarrow[0,1]
be an increasing bijection. The following points hold.

(i)

J(\Theta f)=\Theta(Jf)
and
J^{\varepsilon}(\Theta f)=\Theta(J^{\varepsilon}f)
,

(ii)

J(f\circ\lambda)=(Jf)\circ\lambda
and
J^{\varepsilon}(f\circ\lambda)=(J^{\varepsilon}f)\circ\lambda
,

(iii)

If
\varepsilon\notin\{\Delta_{t}(f)\ :\ t\in[0,1]\}
, then
J^{\varepsilon}
is continuous at
f
with respect to the topology of uniform convergence,

(iv)

\left|\left|Jf-J^{\varepsilon}f\right|\right|_{\infty}\underset{\varepsilon\rightarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}0
.

Proof.

When
s,t\in[1/2,1]
, recall
\Theta f(s)=f(2s-1)
so
x_{s}^{t}(\Theta f)=x_{2s-1}^{2t-1}(f)
. Then, the point
(i)
comes from the fact that
\Delta_{r}(\Theta f)=0
when
r\in[0,1/2)
. As for the proof of the time-changing property, we deduce
(ii)
from the strict monotony of
\lambda
and from the equality
x_{s}^{t}(f\circ\lambda)=x_{\lambda(s)}^{\lambda(t)}(f)
for all
s,t\in[0,1]
. Let us prove
(iii)
. The excursion
f
is càdlàg so the set
\{t\in[0,1]\ :\ \Delta_{t}(f)>\varepsilon/2\}
is finite. Thus,

\displaystyle\eta_{1}

\displaystyle=\max\{\Delta_{t}(f)\ :\ t\in[0,1]\text{ such that }\varepsilon/2<\Delta_{t}(f)<\varepsilon\}\cup\{\varepsilon/2\},

\displaystyle\eta_{2}

\displaystyle=\min\{\Delta_{t}(f)\ :\ t\in[0,1]\text{ such that }\Delta_{t}(f)\geq\varepsilon\}\cup\{2\varepsilon\}

are well-defined and
\eta_{1}<\varepsilon<\eta_{2}
. Let
g
be an excursion such that
||f-g||_{\infty}<\min(\varepsilon-\eta_{1},\eta_{2}-\varepsilon)/2
. If
\Delta_{r}(g)\geq\varepsilon
then
\Delta_{r}(f)>\eta_{1}
and we obtain
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon
by definition of
\eta_{1}
. Likewise, if
\Delta_{r}(g)<\varepsilon
then
\Delta_{r}(f)<\eta_{2}
and we obtain
\Delta_{r}(f)<\varepsilon
by definition of
\eta_{2}
. Hence,

J^{\varepsilon}g(t)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)\geq\varepsilon\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{t}(g)

for all
t\in[0,1]
, so
\left|\left|J^{\varepsilon}f-J^{\varepsilon}g\right|\right|_{\infty}\leq 2N(f)\times||f-g||_{\infty}
where we denote by
N(f)
the number of jumps of
f
of height at least
\varepsilon
. The point
(iii)
follows. To show the point
(iv)
, we mimic the proof of Dini’s theorem. We fix
\nu>0
and we set

U_{n}=\{t\in[0,1]\ :\ Jf(t)-J^{1/n}f(t)<\nu\text{ and }Jf(t-)-J^{1/n}f(t-)<\nu\}

for all
n\geq 1
. We remark that
U_{n}\subset U_{n+1}
because
J^{1/n}f\leq J^{1/(n+1)}f\leq Jf
. The functions
Jf
and
J^{1/n}f
are càdlàg, so
U_{n}
is open for all
n\geq 1
. Furthermore, the monotone convergence theorem implies that
J^{1/n}f(t)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}Jf(t)
for all
t\in[0,1]
. When
n
is large enough (depending on
t
), we have

\Delta_{t}(J^{1/n}f)=\Delta_{t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{t}(f)\geq 1/n}=\Delta_{t}(f)=\Delta_{t}(Jf)

which yields
Jf(t-)-J^{1/n}f(t-)=Jf(t)-J^{1/n}f(t)
. Therefore, the family
(U_{n})_{n\geq 1}
covers
[0,1]
, so there exists
N\geq 1
such that
U_{N}=[0,1]
by compactness. As a result,
||Jf-J^{1/n}f||_{\infty}\leq\nu
when
n\geq N
.
∎

4
Limit theorems

The main flaw of the pseudo-distances
d^{\mathsf{L}}
,
d^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
d^{\mathsf{V}}
is that they do not enjoy any good property of functional continuity, unlike the classical pseudo-distance
d^{\mathrm{clas}}
which induces a real tree from a continuous excursion
f
. We have already explained why a PJG excursion should code a looptree. However, it is possible to show every encoding of a pseudo-distance from an excursion cannot be continuous if it is equal to
d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}
whenever
f
is PJG. For example, we observe the two sequences of functions defined by

\left(f_{n}(t),g_{n}(t)\right)=\begin{cases}\left(k/2n+1/n-(t-k/2n),(k+1)/2n\right)&\text{ if }\frac{k}{2n}\leq t<\frac{k+1}{2n}\text{ with }0\leq k\leq n-1\\
\left(1/2-(t-1/2),1/2-(t-1/2)\right)&\text{ if }1/2\leq t\leq 1\end{cases}

are PJG thanks to Lemma
4
. Both have the same asymptotic behavior because both uniformly converge to the same continuous excursion
f:t\in[0,1]\longmapsto 1/2-|t-1/2|
, while their associated looptrees have very different limits. Indeed, on the one hand,
\mathscr{L}_{f_{n}}
consists of a chain of
n
loops of length
1/n
put back to back. Intuitively, this sequence converges to a segment of length
1/2
, which is also
\mathscr{T}_{f}
. On the other hand,
\mathscr{L}_{g_{n}}
consists of a bouquet of
n
loops of length
1/2n
all glued at the same point. This sequence converges to a single point, which is also
\mathscr{L}_{f}
, as the sequence of the diameters tends to
0
.

4.1
Continuity for the shuffled vernation tree pseudo-distance

We fix a shuffle
\Phi
and we set
\mathbb{B}(\Phi)=\mathbb{B}^{1}(\Phi)\cup\mathbb{B}^{2}(\Phi)
, where

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{1}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\left\{\Delta>0\ :\ \Delta\text{ discontinuity point of }\Phi\text{ for the Skorokhod topology}\right\},

\displaystyle\mathbb{B}^{2}(\Phi)

\displaystyle=\left\{(\Delta,x)\ :\ \Delta>0,\ x\in[0,1],\ x\text{ discontinuity point of }\phi_{\Delta}\right\}.

We are going to show that
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}
enjoys a property of functional continuity, which justifies its construction. The set
\mathbb{B}(\Phi)
will represent the imperfections of that continuity. Let us remark the definition of a shuffling implies
\mathbb{B}(\Phi)\cap\left((0,\infty)\times\{0,1\}\right)=\emptyset
. For any excursion

, we also define

, where

\displaystyle B_{f}^{1}

\displaystyle=\left\{\Delta_{t}\ :\ t\in[0,1]\text{ such that }\Delta_{t}>0\right\},

\displaystyle B_{f}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\{\left(\Delta_{r},\frac{x_{r}^{t}}{\Delta_{r}}\right)\ :\ r,s,t\in[0,1]\text{ such that }s\neq t,\Delta_{r}>0,\text{ and }x_{r}^{t}=x_{r}^{s}>0\right\},

which will indicate if the functional

is continuous at

. We remind that the set of excursions
\mathcal{H}
is a closed subset of the Skorokhod space
\mathcal{D}([0,1])
, endowed with the Skorokhod distance
\rho
. Also recall from Definition

the Skorokhod topology on
\mathcal{D}([0,1]^{2})
induced by the distance

. Furthermore, we already know from Proposition

for any

.

Theorem 6
.

Let

be an excursion. If

, then the functional

for the Skorokhod topologies.

Proof.

The proof requires a lot of care and can be tedious sometimes. The main idea is to use the convergence in (

. Then, the hypothesis

Choice of some parameters.

Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)

\text{if }u\in(0,2\Delta)\text{ then }\sup_{x\in(0,1]}\left|\frac{2}{x}\delta\left(\phi_{u}(0),\phi_{u}(x)\right)-1\right|\leq\varepsilon

(19)

thanks to (

\displaystyle\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}y,y^{\prime}\in[0,y_{n,1}]\\

(21)

Eventually, we obtain the existence of some

We set

\omega=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq n\leq N\\
1\leq i\leq k_{n}\end{subarray}}\min(x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}-\gamma}-x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}},x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}}-x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}+\gamma}),

and we know

. Then, we choose

\left(\frac{8}{\Delta}+1\right)\xi<\frac{\omega}{2+||f||_{\infty}}.

(24)

Recall the definition of the Skorokhod distance

for

2
. Because

(25)

Correspondence between big discontinuities.

Let

such that

. We are going to construct an increasing bijection from

to itself which makes correspond the discontinuity points of

and
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{g}
. If

. In particular for

, so (
25

Let

, and

It is well-defined because

is an excursion. Observe that
x_{r_{n}}^{\lambda(t_{n,i})}(g)=\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)\times\mu_{n}^{-1}(y_{n,i})
. Let us prove that

is an increasing function, close to the identity. Let
1\leq n\leq N
and
1\leq i\leq k_{n}
. We use

and
\omega
to find

\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}-\gamma}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(g)\geq\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}-\gamma}(f)-2\xi}{\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)}\geq\frac{y_{n,i}\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)+\omega-2\xi}{\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)}.

Plus, we found
\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)\geq\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)-2\xi\geq\Delta-2\eta
, so
\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)\geq\Delta/2
according to (

\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)\leq\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)+2\xi\leq 2+||f||_{\infty}
. It follows

\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}-\gamma}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(g)\geq y_{n,i}+\frac{\omega}{2+||f||_{\infty}}-\frac{8\xi}{\Delta},

then successive applications of (

\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}-\gamma}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(g)\geq\mu_{n}^{-1}(y_{n,i})+\frac{\omega}{2+||f||_{\infty}}-\left(\frac{8}{\Delta}+1\right)\xi>\mu_{n}^{-1}(y_{n,i}).

We prove
x_{r_{n}}^{t_{n,i}+\gamma}(g)/\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)<\mu_{n}^{-1}(y_{n,i})
in the same way. Thus,
|\lambda(t_{n,i})-t_{n,i}|<\gamma
. We deduce that
\lambda
is strictly increasing and

||\lambda-\mathsf{id}||_{\infty}\leq\gamma\leq\varepsilon.

(27)

Moreover,
\lambda
is a bijection from
[0,1]
to itself because
\lambda(0)=0
,
\lambda(1)=1
, and
\lambda
is continuous.

Control on the big loops.

We easily see that if
t
is outside all the intervals
[t_{n,i}-\gamma,t_{n,i}+\gamma]
then
\lambda(t)=t
. But if
t\in[t_{n,i}-\gamma,t_{n,i}+\gamma]
then
\lambda(t)\in[t_{n,i}-\gamma,t_{n,i}+\gamma]
too. Therefore, (

) allows us to deduce

||f-g\circ\lambda||_{\infty}\leq||f-f\circ\lambda||_{\infty}+||f-g||_{\infty}\leq 2\eta/3+\eta/3=\eta.

Now, we set

so that
||f-h||_{\infty}\leq\eta
. Our goal is to show that
||\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{h}||_{\infty}
is small, but before that, we are going to bound the difference of the distances on the big loops. We begin by recalling that the point
r_{n}
is outside all the intervals
[t_{n,i}-\gamma,t_{n,i}+\gamma]
so
\lambda(r_{n})=r_{n}
, which implies
\Delta_{r_{n}}(h)=\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)
and
x_{r_{n}}^{t}(h)=x_{r_{n}}^{\lambda(t)}(g)
. Let
t\geq r_{n}
and
1\leq i\leq k_{n}
. Recall
(\Delta_{r_{n}}(f),y_{n,i})\notin B_{f}
because
(\Delta_{r_{n}}(f),y_{n,i})\in\mathbb{B}(\Phi)
, so

\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)\leq y_{n,i}\Longleftrightarrow t_{n,i}\leq t.

Then,
\lambda(r_{n})=r_{n}\leq\lambda(t)
because
\lambda
is increasing. By definition of
\lambda(t_{n,i})
together with the observation that
x_{r_{n}}^{\lambda(t_{n,i})}(g)=\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)\times\mu_{n}^{-1}(y_{n,i})
, we have
x_{r_{n}}^{\lambda(t)}(g)/\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)\leq\mu_{n}^{-1}(y_{n,i})\Longleftrightarrow\lambda(t_{n,i})\leq\lambda(t)
. The functions
\lambda
and
\mu_{n}
are strictly increasing so it follows

\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)\leq y_{n,i}\Longleftrightarrow\mu_{n}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(h)\right)\leq y_{n,i}.

Hence, the points
x_{r_{n}}^{t}(f)/\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)
and
\mu_{n}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{t}(h)/\Delta_{r_{n}}(h)\right)
are either both in the interval
[0,y_{n,1}]
or both in the same interval
(y_{n,i},y_{n,i+1}]
with

. Furthermore, we can bound

\left|\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)-\mu_{n}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(h)\right)\right|\leq||\mu_{n}-\mathsf{id}||_{\infty}+\frac{2||f-h||_{\infty}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)}+|x_{r_{n}}^{t}(f)|\frac{2||f-h||_{\infty}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)},

so that the inequalities

, (

), and (

) give

\left|\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)-\mu_{n}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(h)\right)\right|\leq\left(1+\frac{4}{\Delta}+\frac{4}{\Delta^{2}}||f||_{\infty}\right)\eta\leq\nu.

Therefore, it follows from (
20
) and (
21
) that

\delta\left(\phi_{\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)\right),\phi_{\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)}\left(\mu_{n}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(h)\right)\right)\right)\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{N+1}

(28)

for all
t\in[0,1]
, the case

being obvious. Now, let
s,t\in[0,1]
, the triangular inequality on
\delta
implies

\left|\tilde{\delta}_{r_{n}}^{f}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{s}(f),x_{r_{n}}^{t}(f)\right)-\tilde{\delta}_{r_{n}}^{h}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{s}(h),x_{r_{n}}^{t}(h)\right)\right|\leq|\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)-\Delta_{r_{n}}(h)|\\
+\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)\times\delta\left(\phi_{\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)\right),\phi_{\Delta_{r_{n}}(h)}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{t}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(h)\right)\right)\\
+\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)\times\delta\left(\phi_{\Delta_{r_{n}}(f)}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{s}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(f)\right),\phi_{\Delta_{r_{n}}(h)}\left(\frac{x_{r_{n}}^{s}}{\Delta_{r_{n}}}(h)\right)\right).

Recall that
\Delta_{r_{n}}(h)=\Delta_{r_{n}}(g)
,
||f-h||_{\infty}\leq\eta
, and
\xi\leq\eta
. We apply the inequalities (

\left|\tilde{\delta}_{r_{n}}^{f}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{s}(f),x_{r_{n}}^{t}(f)\right)-\tilde{\delta}_{r_{n}}^{h}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{s}(h),x_{r_{n}}^{t}(h)\right)\right|\leq(2+4||f||_{\infty})\frac{\varepsilon}{N+1}.

Uniform control.

We are now ready to bound
||\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{h}||_{\infty}
. Let

with

. With the help of Lemma

and of the identities (

\displaystyle\left|\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}(s,t)-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{h}(s,t)\right|\leq

\displaystyle|f(s)-h(s)|+|f(t)-h(t)|+2\left|\inf_{[s,t]}f-\inf_{[s,t]}h\right|

\displaystyle+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|x_{r_{n}}^{s}(f)-x_{r_{n}}^{s}(h)\right|+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|x_{r_{n}}^{t}(f)-x_{r_{n}}^{t}(h)\right|

\displaystyle+2\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|\tilde{\delta}_{r_{n}}^{f}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{s}(f),x_{r_{n}}^{t}(f)\right)-\tilde{\delta}_{r_{n}}^{h}\left(x_{r_{n}}^{s}(h),x_{r_{n}}^{t}(h)\right)\right|

\displaystyle+3\Delta_{s\wedge_{f}t}(f){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s\wedge_{f}t}(f)<\Delta}+3\Delta_{s\wedge_{h}t}(h){\bf 1}_{\Delta_{s\wedge_{h}t}(f)<\Delta}

\displaystyle+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\Delta\end{subarray}}\left|2\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}\left(0,x_{r}^{s}(f)\right)-x_{r}^{s}(f)\right|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\

The sum of all the terms in the first two rows of the right-hand side can be easily bounded by

because

). The term of the third row is smaller than

, the sum of the terms in the fourth row is bounded by

. Moreover, the fact (

) ensures that if
\Delta_{r}(f)<\Delta
, then it holds

\left|2\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}(0,x)-x\right|\leq\varepsilon x\text{ and }\left|2\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{h}(0,y)-y\right|\leq\varepsilon y

for all

and for all

. It allows us to control the last four terms of the right-hand side. For instance,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\Delta\end{subarray}}\left|2\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{f}\left(0,x_{r}^{s}(f)\right)-x_{r}^{s}(f)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\Delta\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{s}(f)\leq\varepsilon Jf(s)\leq||f||_{\infty}\varepsilon,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\Delta\end{subarray}}\left|2\tilde{\delta}_{r}^{h}\left(0,x_{r}^{s}(h)\right)-x_{r}^{s}(h)\right|

\displaystyle\leq\varepsilon\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in[0,1]\\
\Delta_{r}(f)<\Delta\end{subarray}}x_{r}^{s}(h)\leq\varepsilon Jh(s)\leq\left(1+||f||_{\infty}\right)\varepsilon,

because we know from Theorem

that

. Eventually, we find

\left|\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}(s,t)-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{h}(s,t)\right|\leq\left(23+12||f||_{\infty}\right)\varepsilon.

Conclusion.

To sum up, we have shown that for all
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
\eta>0
such that if
||f-g||_{\infty}\leq\eta
then there exists
\lambda
an increasing bijection from
[0,1]
to itself with
||\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{g\circ\lambda}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
and
||\lambda-\mathsf{id}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
. Indeed, recall (

). Now, if the Skorokhod distance between

and
g
is smaller than
\min(\eta,\varepsilon)/2
, then we have
\mu
an increasing bijection from
[0,1]
to itself such that
||\mu-\mathsf{id}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
and
||f-g\circ\mu||_{\infty}\leq\eta
. Hence, there exists
\lambda
an increasing bijection from
[0,1]
to itself such that
||\mu\circ\lambda-\mathsf{id}||_{\infty}\leq 2\varepsilon
and
||\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}-\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{g\circ\mu\circ\lambda}||_{\infty}\leq\varepsilon
. Eventually, thanks to the time-changing property,
\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{g\circ\mu\circ\lambda}=\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{g}\circ(\mu\circ\lambda,\mu\circ\lambda)
. Thereby, if
\rho(f,g)\leq\min(\eta,\varepsilon)/2
then
\rho_{2}\left(\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{f},\widetilde{d}^{\mathsf{V}}_{g}\right)\leq 2\varepsilon
, where we remind that
\rho
is the Skorokhod distance on
\mathcal{H}
and
\rho_{2}
is the Skorokhod distance for bivariate functions.

4.2
Two particular cases for convergences of unshuffled pseudo-distances

Even if
d^{\mathsf{L}}
,
d^{\mathsf{T}}
, and
d^{\mathsf{V}}
do not enjoy a property of functional continuity, we are still able to provide two particular cases where it is possible to automatically deduce a convergence for
(d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f_{n}})
,
(d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f_{n}})
, or
(d^{\mathsf{V}}_{f_{n}})
from the convergence of
(f_{n})
.

Theorem 7
.

If
f_{n}\longrightarrow f
for the Skorokhod topology on
\mathcal{H}
and if there exists
N\geq 1
such that all the
f_{n}
have at most
N
jumps, then
d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f_{n}}\longrightarrow d^{\mathsf{L}}_{f}
and
d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f_{n}}\longrightarrow d^{\mathsf{T}}_{f}
uniformly on
[0,1]^{2}
. It follows
d^{\mathsf{V}}_{f_{n}}\longrightarrow d^{\mathsf{V}}_{f}
uniformly on
[0,1]^{2}
.

Proof.

The limiting excursion
f
also has at most
N
jumps. Let
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
such that all the jumps of
f
are higher than
2\varepsilon
. Hence, we get
J^{\varepsilon}f_{n}\longrightarrow J^{\varepsilon}f=Jf
for the Skorokhod topology thanks to Theorem
4
and to Proposition
7
. Let
s\in[0,1]
, we know there is
t\in[0,1]
such that
|\Delta_{t}(f)-\Delta_{s}(f_{n})|\leq 2\rho(f_{n},f)
. If
\rho(f_{n},f)<\varepsilon/2
and
\Delta_{s}(f_{n})<\varepsilon
, then
\Delta_{t}(f)<2\varepsilon
, so that
\Delta_{t}(f)=0
and
\Delta_{s}(f_{n})\leq 2\rho(f_{n},f)
. Hence, when
n
is large enough, we find

||Jf_{n}-J^{\varepsilon}f_{n}||_{\infty}\leq\sum_{s\in[0,1]}\Delta_{s}(f_{n}){\bf 1}_{0<\Delta_{s}(f_{n})<\varepsilon}\leq 2N\rho(f_{n},f)\underset{n\rightarrow\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

In particular, the sequence of excursions
g_{n}:=f_{n}-Jf_{n}+J^{\varepsilon}f_{n}
converges to
f
for the Skorokhod topology. Let us fix

4.3
From convergences of pseudo-distances to convergences of quotient metric spaces

Recall the notions presented at the start of Section
2.3
. To see how Theorems
6
,
7
, and
8
can bring us some convergences of pointed weighted metric spaces under the form of Theorem
3
, we have to present the definition of the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov distance, which truly induces a topology on the set of the GHP-isometry classes of pointed weighted compact metric spaces. Recall that when no confusion is possible, we simply denote a metric space (possibly endowed with some additional structure) by its underlying set.

A
correspondence
between two compact metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2})
is a subset
\mathcal{R}
of
X_{1}\times X_{2}
such that for all
x_{1}\in X_{1}
and
y_{2}\in X_{2}
, there exists
x_{2}\in X_{2}
and
y_{1}\in X_{1}
such that
(x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
. We say a correspondence between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is compact if it is compact in the product space
X_{1}\times X_{2}
. The distortion of a correspondence
\mathcal{R}
is defined by

\mathrm{dis}(\mathcal{R})=\sup\left\{\left|d_{1}(x_{1},y_{1})-d_{2}(x_{2},y_{2})\right|\ :\ (x_{1},x_{2}),(y_{1},y_{2})\in\mathcal{R}\right\}.

The Gromov-Hausdorff distance between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is then expressed by the formula

\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{1},X_{2})=\frac{1}{2}\inf_{\mathcal{R}}\mathrm{dis}(\mathcal{R}),

where the infimum is taken over all correspondences
\mathcal{R}
between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
. Similarly, the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff distance
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}^{\bullet}(X_{1},X_{2})
between two pointed compact metric spaces
(X_{1},d_{1},a_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2},a_{2})
is expressed by the same formula up to the difference that the infimum is instead taken over all correspondences
\mathcal{R}
between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
such that
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
. For both distances, we may restrict the infimum to compact correspondences without modifying the value. Indeed, a correspondence and its closure have the same distortion, and the latter is a compact correspondence because
X_{1}\times X_{2}
is compact. Let
(X_{1},d_{1},a_{1},\mu_{1})
and
(X_{2},d_{2},a_{2},\mu_{2})
be two pointed weighted compact metric spaces. A
coupling
between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
is a Borel probability measure on
X_{1}\times X_{2}
whose marginals on
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
. The pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov distance between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
is then defined by the formula

\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}^{\bullet}(X_{1},X_{2})=\inf_{\mathcal{R},\nu}\max\left(\frac{1}{2}\mathrm{dis}(\mathcal{R}),1-\nu(\mathcal{R})\right)

where the infimum is taken over all couplings
\nu
between
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
and all compact correspondences
\mathcal{R}
between
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
such that
(a_{1},a_{2})\in\mathcal{R}
.

The functions
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}
,
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}^{\bullet}
, and
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}^{\bullet}
are only pseudo-distances but
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}(X_{1},X_{2})=0
if and only if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are isometric,
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}^{\bullet}(X_{1},X_{2})=0
if and only if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are pointed-isometric, and
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}^{\bullet}(X_{1},X_{2})=0
if and only if
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are GHP-isometric. Hence,
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}
defines a genuine distance on the space
\mathbb{K}
of isometry classes of compact metric spaces,
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GH}}^{\bullet}
defines a genuine distance on the space
\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}
of pointed-isometry classes of pointed compact metric spaces, and
\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}^{\bullet}
defines a genuine distance on the space
\mathbb{K}_{\mathrm{w}}^{\bullet}
of GHP-isometry classes of pointed weighted compact metric spaces. We call their respective topologies the Gromov-Hausdorff, the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff, and the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topologies. Furthermore, the metric spaces
\mathbb{K}
,
\mathbb{K}^{\bullet}
, and
\mathbb{K}_{\mathrm{w}}^{\bullet}
are separable and complete. See
[
28
]
and
[
2
]
for proof of the stated facts and for more information. Now, recall from Section
2.3
the definition of quotient metric spaces and Definition
3
.

5
Topological space of vernation trees

Here, we propose a topological notion of vernation trees that does not require coding by excursions. First, let us fix our framework by giving some definitions. Let
(X,d)
be a metric space and let
x,y\in X
. We say that
\gamma
is a
path
on
X
from
x
to
y
when
\gamma:[0,1]\longrightarrow X
is continuous such that
\gamma(0)=x
and
\gamma(1)=y
. In that case, we will denote by
\Ima\gamma=\gamma([0,1])
its image. If
\gamma
is also injective, we say that
\gamma
is an
arc
on
X
from
x
to
y
. By compactness of
[0,1]
, an arc provides a topological embedding of the segment
[0,1]
into
X
. Moreover, we say that
\gamma
is a
geodesic
on
X
from
x
to
y
when
d(\gamma(t),\gamma(s))=|t-s|d(x,y)
for all
t,s\in[0,1]
. A geodesic from
x
to
y
is an arc if and only if
x\neq y
. Finally, we say that
\gamma
is a
loop
on
X
based at
x
when
\gamma:[0,1]\longrightarrow X
is continuous, injective on
[0,1)
, and such that
\gamma(0)=\gamma(1)=x
. A loop provides a topological embedding of the circle
\mathcal{C}
into
X
.

Definition 6
.

A metric space
(X,d)
is
geodesic
when there exists a geodesic on
X
from
x
to
y
, for all
x,y\in X
. A metric space
(X,d)
is a
real tree
when it is geodesic and when all arcs on
X
from
x
to
y
have the same image, for all
x,y\in X
. We say a metric space
(X,d)
is a
vernation tree
when it is geodesic and when it satisfies that for any
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
, two loops on
X
based at
x
, then either
\Ima\gamma_{1}=\Ima\gamma_{2}
or
\Ima\gamma_{1}\cap\Ima\gamma_{2}=\{x\}
, for all
x\in X
.

We point out that any geodesic space is arc-connected by definition. For our work, we exclusively are interested in compact metric spaces. When a metric space is indeed compact, it is geodesic if and only if it is a length space. See
[
20
, Chapter 2]
for a definition of length spaces, a proof of the just mentioned fact, and an extensive study of the notion. For a detailed study of real trees, we refer to Evans
[
24
]
or to Paulin
[
38
]
. Observe a geodesic space is locally path-connected. Indeed, for any
x\in X
and
r>0
, if
d(x,y)<r
then a geodesic between
x
and
y
stays inside the ball of radius
r
and of center
x
. Moreover, it is well-known that a Hausdorff space, so a metric space, is pathwise connected if and only if it is arcwise connected, see
[
42
, Chapter 31]
or
[
13
]
for example. We deduce the following convenient lemma.

Lemma 6
.

Let
(X,d)
be a compact geodesic space. If
F
is a finite subset of
X
, then the connected components of
X\backslash F
are open and arcwise connected.

Proposition 8
.

When
(X,d)
is a compact geodesic space, the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

The space
(X,d)
is a real tree.

(ii)

For all
x,y\in X
, if
x\neq y
then there exists
u\in X\backslash\{x,y\}
such that
x
and
y
are in different connected components of

.

(iii)

There are no loops on

.

The implications

and

. We observe that the point

obviously ensures that a real tree is also a vernation tree, which might not be so clear by only looking at the definitions. The interest of the point

When

to

Recall from Section

Theorem 9
.

Let

be a compact vernation tree for all

and let

be a compact metric space. If

for the Gromov-Hausdorff topology then

First, the subspace
\mathbb{L}
is closed so

is a compact geodesic space. Let

with

, we only need to find

such that
x
and

are in different connected components of

. There exists a sequence

of correspondences respectively between

and

. For any

and

\lim d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n})=d(x,y)>0
so we can assume that

for all
n\geq 0
by only considering large enough
n
. Hence, there exists
u_{n},v_{n}\in X_{n}
such that in the point
(iii)
of Proposition
9
. Then, we have
u_{n}^{\prime},v_{n}^{\prime}\in X
such that
(u_{n},u_{n}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
and
(v_{n},v_{n}^{\prime})\in\mathcal{R}_{n}
for all
n\geq 0
. Since
X
is compact, we can assume that there exists
u,v\in X
such that
u_{n}^{\prime}\longrightarrow u
and
v_{n}^{\prime}\longrightarrow v
, by restricting ourselves on a subsequence. It follows that
2d(x,u)=\lim 2d_{n}(x_{n},u_{n})\geq\lim d_{n}(x_{n},y_{n})=d(x,y)
, so
x\neq u
. In the same way, we show that
u,v\in X\backslash\{x,y\}
. We are going to show that
x
and
y
are in different connected components of
X\backslash\{u,v\}
.

Let
\gamma:[0,1]\longrightarrow X
be a path on
X
from
x
to
y
. For all

and
1\leq i\leq n-1
, there exists

such that

to

for all

d_{n}\left(w_{n,\lfloor nt_{n}\rfloor},u_{n}\right)\leq 2\mathrm{dis}\left(\mathcal{R}_{n}\right)+\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}s,r\in[0,1]\\

Words and plane trees

Before presenting our three applications, we recall the formalism of plane trees, see
[

]
for example. Let

the generation or height of

for the concatenation of

and

is an ancestor of

, and we write

. If

, we say

is a child of

and

is the parent of

.

A

is a finite subset of

such that:

•

,

•

and

, then

,

•

(the number of children of

in
\tau

if and only if

.

We denote the total progeny of
\tau
, which is the total number of vertices of
\tau

and the height of
\tau

stemming from

gives another way to explore the tree. Informally,

, and

. Moreover, the exploration processes induce finite sequences of integers that characterize the plane tree. Namely, the

is defined by

, and the

. General arguments of

draw relations between the height process and the contour process via the two bounds

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq 2\#\tau-2}\left|\xi(i)-\frac{i}{2}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{|\tau|}{2}+1,

(34)

\displaystyle\max_{0\leq i\leq 2\#\tau-2}\left|C_{i}(\tau)-H_{\xi(i)}(\tau)\right|

\displaystyle\leq 1+\max_{0\leq j\leq\#\tau-1}\left|H_{i+1}(\tau)-H_{i}(\tau)\right|,

(35)

with the convention

Theorem 15
.

Let

be a sequence of plane trees, let

Recall the definitions of

presented in the previous subsection, as well as that

stating that if
|\tau_{n}|/a_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}0
and
\displaystyle{\left(a_{n}^{-1}L_{\lfloor t\#\tau_{n}\rfloor}(\tau_{n})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}X^{\mathsf{exc},(\alpha)}}
for the Skorokhod topology, with some

for the Gromov-Hausdorff topology. We point out again that our definition of
\mathsf{Loop}(\tau)
slightly differs from Curien and Kortchemski’s, but the two objects are at Gromov-Hausdorff distance at most

and

. In the light of the asymptotic behavior when

\displaystyle{\left(a_{n}^{-1}L_{\lfloor t\#\tau_{n}\rfloor}(\tau_{n})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\mathbf{e}}
for the relaxed Skorokhod topology then

for the Gromov-Hausdorff topology, where

is the standard Brownian excursion. Sadly, the reader could convince oneself this is false by approximating

by PJG excursions, in a similar fashion as the counter-example given at the beginning of Section

. Here, the problem comes from a lack of exchangeability in the order of siblings inside the plane trees. Nevertheless, by adding a hypothesis that expresses such exchangeability, we will be able to state a general result for convergences of random discrete looptrees.

Let
\Psi=\left(\psi_{k}\right)_{k\geq 1}
be a family of permutations
\psi_{k}
respectively of
\{1,...,k\}
. The family
\Psi
induces a bijection, also denoted by
\Psi
with a slight abuse of notations, from any plane tree
\tau
to another plane tree
\Psi(\tau)=\left\{\Psi(u)\ :\ u\in\tau\right\}
by inductively setting
\Psi(\varnothing)=\varnothing
and
\Psi\left(u*(j)\right)=\Psi\left(u\right)*\left(\psi_{k_{u}(\tau)}(j)\right)
if

and

. Observe that if

then
|\Psi(u)|=|u|
,

, and

if and only if
\Psi(u)\preceq\Psi(v)
. Basically,

is the plane tree recursively constructed by shuffling the order of siblings of the same parents, so

can be retrieved by shuffling the joint points between the loops of
\mathsf{Loop}(\tau)
. It is natural to try to compare the looptree shuffled by a family of permutations
\Psi
with the shuffled looptree
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{w(\tau)}
induced by a shuffle
\Phi
.

Lemma 12
.

We assume the shuffle
\Phi
is such that the Lebesgue measure on

is invariant by

and

\mathtt{d}_{\mathrm{GHP}}^{\bullet}\left(\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\frac{1}{a}w(\tau)},\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{a}w(\Psi(\tau))}\right)\\
\leq\max_{v\in\tau}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}u\in\tau,j\geq 1\\
u*(j)\preceq v\end{subarray}}\frac{k_{u}(\tau)+1}{a}\delta\left(\phi_{\frac{k_{u}(\tau)+1}{a}}\left(1-\frac{j}{k_{u}(\tau)+1}\right),1-\frac{\psi_{k_{u}(\tau)}(j)}{k_{u}(\tau)+1}\right).

We denote by
c_{1}
the exploration by contour of
\tau
and by
c_{2}
the exploration by contour of
\Psi(\tau)
. For all
u\in\tau\backslash\{\varnothing\}
, we define
(2\#\tau-1)r_{1}(u)
as the unique integer

and

. Similarly, we define
(2\#\tau-1)r_{2}(u)
as the unique integer

such that
c_{2}(i)=\Psi(u)
and
|c_{2}(i)|=|c_{2}(i-1)|+1
. We also set
r_{1}(\varnothing)=r_{2}(\varnothing)=0
. The
r_{1}(u)
and the
r_{2}(u)
for

are respectively all the jumps of
w(\tau)
and
w(\Psi(\tau))
. For all
u\in\tau
and

, the times

\displaystyle t_{1}(u,y)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq r_{1}(u)\ :\ w_{t}(\tau)=w_{r_{1}(u)-}(\tau)+y\Delta_{r_{1}(u)}(w(\tau))\right\}

\displaystyle t_{2}(u,y)

\displaystyle=\inf\left\{t\geq r_{2}(u)\ :\ w_{t}(\Psi(\tau))=w_{r_{2}(u)-}(\Psi(\tau))+y\Delta_{r_{2}(u)}\left(w(\Psi(\tau))\right)\right\}

are well-defined and the applications

are bijective. It is a simple consequence of the fact

is strictly decreasing on the intervals
[i,i+1)
. We use the conventions

when
\phi_{\Delta}(x)=0=1\in\mathcal{C}
and

\phi_{\Delta}(x)=y\in(0,1)\subset\mathcal{C}
. Let us denote by
[s]_{1}
and
[s]_{2}

respectively on
\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\frac{1}{a}w(\tau)}

\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{a}w(\Psi(\tau))}

\mathcal{R}=\left\{\left([1]_{1},[1]_{2}\right)\right\}\cup\left\{\left(\left[t_{1}(u,y)\right]_{1},\left[t_{2}(u,\phi_{\frac{k_{u}(\tau)+1}{a}}(y))\right]_{2}\right)\ :\ u\in\tau,y\in[0,1]\right\}

is a correspondence between

2
, basic observations about
\Psi

\phi_{\Delta}(0)=0

\int_{\widetilde{\mathscr{L}}_{\frac{1}{a}w(\tau)}\times\mathscr{L}_{\frac{1}{a}w(\Psi(\tau))}}g{\rm d}\nu=\frac{1}{2\#\tau-1}\sum_{u\in\tau}(k_{u}(\tau)+1)\int_{0}^{1}g\left(\left[t_{1}(u,y)\right]_{1},\left[t_{2}(u,\phi_{\frac{k_{u}(\tau)+1}{a}}(y))\right]_{2}\right){\rm d}y

takes integer values at integer times and is affine of slope

We say a random plane tree
\tau
is called

if for any deterministic family
\Psi
of permutations,
\Psi(\tau)
has the same distribution as
\tau
. This will be the main assumption of our invariance principle for random discrete looptrees. For example, it is satisfied by uniform random trees or Galton-Watson trees conditioned on an event or a quantity that does not depend on the plane order: the number of vertices, the number of leaves, the height, the degree sequence, the generation size sequence, the existence of a chain with prescribed degrees…

Theorem 16
.

Let
(\tau_{n})
be a sequence of random exchangeable plane trees, let
X
be a random excursion, and let
(a_{n})
be a sequence of positive real numbers with
a_{n}\rightarrow\infty
. If the convergence

\left(a_{n}^{-1}L_{\lfloor t\#\tau_{n}\rfloor}(\tau_{n})\right)_{t\in[0,1]}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}X
holds in distribution for the relaxed Skorokhod topology, then the convergence

\frac{1}{a_{n}}\cdot\mathsf{Loop}(\tau_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{\longrightarrow}}\frac{1}{2}\cdot\mathscr{V}_{X}

holds in distribution for the pointed Gromov-Hausdorff-Prokhorov topology.

In particular, this theorem gives scaling limits for
\text{Loop}(\tau_{n})
when
\tau_{n}
is a large critical Galton-Watson tree conditioned to have
n
vertices with offspring distribution in the domain of attraction of the Gaussian law but with infinite variance, because the scaling limit of the Lukasiewicz walk and
|\tau_{n}|
are known in this case, see
[
21
]
. This was conjectured by Curien and Kortchemski in
[
18
]
and later proved by Kortchemski and Richier
[
30
]
with a spinal decomposition argument. As scaling limits of Lukasiewicz walks of uniform random trees with prescribed degree sequence are given by
[

[

.

Proof.
