Dynamical symmetries of homogeneous minisuperspace models
Marc Geiller, Etera R. Livine, Francesco Sartini

Abstract
We investigate the phase space symmetries and conserved charges of homogeneous gravitational minisuperspaces. These (0 + 1)-dimensional reductions of general relativity are defined by spacetime metrics in which the dynamical variables depend only on a time coordinate, and are formulated as mechanical systems with a non-trivial field space metric (or supermetric) and effective potential. We show how to extract conserved charges for those minisuperspaces from the homothetic Killing vectors of the field space metric. Going lower in dimension, one finds (0 + 1)-dimensional models, in which case field theories reduces to mechanical systems where the dynamical variables evolve only in time. In general relativity, such models are called minisuperspaces and arise from the reduction to a finite number of homogeneous degrees of freedom

This article is devoted to the study of the symmetry properties of such minisuperspace models. As expected, they inherit from full general relativity a remnant of diffeomorphism-invariance, which is the freedom in performing redefinitions of the time coordinate. The generator of these reparametrizations is the Hamiltonian itself. Our goal is to explain and show that in most minisuperspace models the Hamiltonian can in fact be embedded in larger symmetry algebras, which moreover bear similarities with the boundary symmetry algebras arising in four-dimensional general relativity. Although the minisuperspace models are not gauge field theories and have no spatial boundaries, the symmetries which we unravel act as physical symmetries (changing e.g. the mass of a black hole), and also interplay with the spatial cut-offs required in order to meaningfully define the homogeneous reduction of the Einstein–Hilbert action (and which are therefore remnants of information about the boundary)

Although minisuperspace models seem to be very simple, they contain a rich and physically-useful symmetry structure whose origin is not yet understood in a systematic manner. This is what we aim at improving here. At first sight, one could think of approaching this problem using the Hamiltonian formulation, where roughly speaking the requirement of homogeneity amounts to solving the vector constraint. We are then left with the scalar constraint only, which furthermore splits as
{\mathcal{H}}={\mathcal{H}}_{\text{kin}}+{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{pot}}

When the minisuperspace model is such that the three-dimensional Ricci curvature vanishes, the Hamiltonian has only a kinetic term and we are therefore guaranteed that the CVH algebra exists. This is what happens for example in flat FLRW cosmology. In more general models, where a non-trivial potential is present, we are therefore left with the question of how to recover (if possible) a CVH-type algebra. It turns out that studying this problem frontally by computing Poisson brackets involving the three-dimensional Ricci scalar is too cumbersome. An alternative route, which is the one we set out to study here, is to use the superspace formulation.

Minisuperspaces

In this section, we present the formalism used to describe homogeneous minisuperspace models in general relativity and to study their symmetries. We start by reviewing the homogeneous reduction of gravity, and introduce a triad formulation which allows to separate the homogeneous dynamical fields from the background geometric structure of the various minisuperspace models. This enables us to identify an internal metric on field space (or superspace), whose geometry controls the dynamics of the homogeneous symmetry-reduced action. We then explain how the symmetries of this field space geometry can be translated into algebraic and symmetry structures on the phase space of the minisuperspace models.

Homogeneous reduction of gravity

In this work, we are interested in the study of homogeneous minisuperspace models, for which the Einstein–Hilbert action reduces to a mechanical action integrated over a single time variable. In order to set the stage, let us consider a four-dimensional spacetime equipped with a metric of the general form

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s_{4\mathrm{D}}^{2}=-N(t)^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+q_{\alpha\beta}(x,t)\mathrm{d}x^{\alpha}\mathrm{d}x^{\beta}\,,\qquad\qquad q_{\alpha\beta}(x,t)=e_{\alpha}^{i}(x)\,e_{\beta}^{j}(x)\,\gamma_{ij}(t)\,.

(2.1)

In this parametrization the spatial triad
e^{i}_{\alpha}
does not depend on the time coordinate, but only on the position on the spatial slice. All the dynamical information is therefore captured in the spatially homogeneous but
time-dependent
fields
\gamma_{ij}

D

q_{\alpha\beta}(t,x)

In minisuperspace models, a leftover of the ADM formulation is encoded in the fact that the Hamiltonian of the mechanical model is the scalar constraint of general relativity. Because the line element (
2.1

\displaystyle K_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2N}\dot{q}_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2N}e^{i}_{\alpha}e^{j}_{\beta}\,\dot{\gamma}_{ij}\,,\qquad\qquad K\coloneqq q^{\alpha\beta}K_{\alpha\beta}=\frac{1}{2N}\gamma^{ij}\,\dot{\gamma}_{ij}\,,

The lapse function
N
is a remnant of the diffeomorphism invariance inherited from the full theory. It plays the role of a Lagrange multiplier enforcing the Hamiltonian constraint, and it ensures that the model is invariant under time reparametrizations. The Lagrangian (
2.11
) is indeed invariant under the symmetry

\displaystyle\delta_{f}q^{\mu}=f\dot{q}^{\mu}\,,\qquad\qquad\delta_{f}N=f\dot{N}+\dot{f}N\,,

(2.13)

for a generic function
f
which can also be field-dependent. Performing a Legendre transform of
(
2.11
)
we obtain the Hamiltonian on the phase space
\{q^{\mu},p_{\nu}\}=\delta^{\mu}_{\nu}
, which is

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=p_{\mu}\dot{q}^{\mu}-N{\mathcal{H}}\,,\qquad\qquad p_{\mu}=\frac{1}{N}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\dot{q}^{\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad H\coloneqq N{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{2}N\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}+N\tilde{U}(q)\,,

(2.14)

with
{\mathcal{H}}\approx 0
on-shell. At the classical level any choice of lapse leads to an equivalent description of the dynamics. It is indeed common to simply view a choice of lapse, which can also be field-dependent, as a choice of clock. For example, working with the proper time
\tau
, defined by
\mathrm{d}\tau=N\mathrm{d}t
, is equivalent to setting
N=1
.

As we are about to see, in order to study the symmetries it will however be important to choose the lapse as a field-dependent function
N(q)
, and to keep track of its interplay with the field space metric and the potential. For this reason, we will stick to the time variable
t
and denote
\mathrm{d}/\mathrm{d}t
by a dot, and view the lapse as a conformal factor in front of the field space metric. We therefore introduce a rescaled metric such that

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad p^{\mu}=g^{\mu\nu}p_{\nu}=\dot{q}^{\mu}\,,\qquad\qquad\xi_{\mu}=g_{\mu\nu}\xi^{\mu}\,,\qquad\qquad\partial_{\mu}\coloneqq\frac{\partial}{\partial q^{\mu}}\,,

(2.15)

for any vector on the field space
\xi^{\mu}
. The evolution of a phase space observable
{\mathcal{O}}
with respect to
t
is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{O}}\coloneqq\frac{\mathrm{d}{\mathcal{O}}}{\mathrm{d}t}=\partial_{t}{\mathcal{O}}+\big\{{\mathcal{O}},H\big\}\,,\qquad\qquad H\coloneqq N{\mathcal{H}}=\frac{1}{2}p^{\mu}p_{\mu}+N\tilde{U}\,.

(2.16)

Below we will interpret a change of lapse as a change of field space metric instead of a change of time (although the two pictures are equivalent at the end of the day). We would like to remark that the phase space is actually independent on the choice of lapse, and hereafter we will think it as a function on phase space, instead of a free field.

When working with the rescaled metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, the equation of motion obtained by varying (
2.11
) with respect to
q^{\mu}
is

\displaystyle\ddot{q}^{\mu}+\Gamma^{\mu}_{\rho\sigma}\dot{q}^{\rho}\dot{q}^{\sigma}+g^{\mu\nu}\partial_{\nu}\tilde{U}=0\,,

(2.17)

where
\Gamma
is the Christoffel connection of the field space metric
g_{\mu\nu}
. In the
free
case, i.e. without potential, this is simply the geodesic equation, and the scalar constraint
H=p^{\mu}p_{\mu}/2\approx 0
restricts to the null geodesics. In this case the evolution of a phase space functions
{\mathcal{O}}
along any trajectory is given by

\displaystyle\dot{\mathcal{O}}=\partial_{t}{\mathcal{O}}+p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}{\mathcal{O}}\,,\qquad\qquad p^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}p^{\nu}=0\,.

(2.18)

In order to unravel the symmetries on the phase space of this system, we first need to study the Killing vectors of the field space metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, and to understand their relation with conserved quantities.

2.2.1
Killing vectors and conserved quantities

We now turn to the core of the discussion, and explain how the phase space symmetry algebra is related to the symmetries of the field space metric. We begin with the analysis of the
free case
, for a vanishing potential. Let us start by considering the initial field space metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
without conformal rescaling by the lapse. We are interested in its conformal Killing vectors
\xi^{\mu}
, which by definition are such that

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{\nabla}_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\tilde{\nabla}_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}\doteq\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad\pounds_{\xi}\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}=-\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}\,.

(2.19)

Here
\tilde{\nabla}
is the covariant derivative with respect to
\tilde{g}
. Whenever
\tilde{\lambda}
is constant and non-vanishing we refer to these
conformal
Killing vectors as
homothetic
Killing vectors, while in the vanishing case we have
true
Killing vectors. It is now easy to see that the presence of a conformal factor
N
does not change the fact that a given vector
\xi^{\mu}
is conformal, but changes however the value of
\lambda
. Indeed, we have

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}=\pounds_{\xi}\left(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\right)=\big(\tilde{\lambda}-\pounds_{\xi}\log N\big)\left(\frac{1}{N}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\right)\eqqcolon\lambda{g}_{\mu\nu}.

(2.20)

This means that a field-dependent change of lapse can change the nature of a conformal Killing vector, by making the weight non-constant, constant, or vanishing. This is a first indication of the importance of the lapse in this analysis.

In order to set the stage and continue the discussion, let us now assume that we have fixed the lapse once and for all, and that we have found the homothetic Killing vectors of the metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, i.e. the vectors such that
\lambda
is constant (and possibly vanishing). The whole set
\left\{\xi_{(i)}\right\}
of homothetic and true Killing vectors then forms an algebra with commutation relations

\displaystyle\big[\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)}\big]=\left(\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(j)}^{\nu}-\xi_{(j)}^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(i)}^{\nu}\right)\partial_{\nu}={c_{ij}}^{k}\xi_{(k)}\,,

(2.21)

where the coefficients
{c_{ij}}^{k}
are the structure constants. Furthermore, as shown in appendix
C
, these Killing vectors are also solutions of the geodesic deviation equation

\displaystyle p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\rho}=-R_{\rho\mu\sigma\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\xi^{\sigma}\,.

(2.22)

With these vectors at hand let us now consider the phase space functions

\displaystyle C_{(i)}\coloneqq p_{\mu}\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}\,.

(2.23)

By construction we then get that

\displaystyle\big\{C_{(i)},H\big\}=p^{\nu}\partial_{\nu}\big(p_{\mu}\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}\big)=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{(i)}g_{\mu\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}=\lambda_{(i)}H\,,

(2.24)

which immediately implies that we have conserved quantities defined as

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}\coloneqq C_{(i)}-t\lambda_{(i)}H\,,\qquad\qquad\dot{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}=\partial_{t}{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}+\big\{{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)},H\big\}=0\,.

(2.25)

These conserved charges are linear in time, they are evolving constants of motion, as uncovered for FLRW cosmology and black hole mechanics in the previous works
[
42
,
45
,
50
,
47
,
54
,
52
]
. The charge
{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}
can indeed be understood as the initial condition at
t=0
for the observable
C_{(i)}
. This set of conserved charges form part of the phase space symmetry algebra which we set out to unravel. One can already add to this set the Hamiltonian at this stage. Then the question which remains is whether we can extend the construction to include charges which are quadratic (or higher polynomials) in time. This will be the goal of the next section.

As conserved quantities, the charges (
2.25
) are generators of symmetries of (
2.11
) whose infinitesimal action is

\displaystyle\delta q^{\mu}=\{q^{\mu},{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}\}

\displaystyle=\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}-\lambda_{(i)}t\,p^{\mu}=\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}-t\lambda_{(i)}\dot{q}^{\mu}\,,

(2.26a)

\displaystyle\delta p_{\mu}=\{p_{\mu},{\mathcal{Q}}_{i}\}

\displaystyle=-p_{\nu}\partial_{\mu}\xi_{(i)}^{\nu}+\frac{1}{2}t\lambda_{(i)}p_{\nu}p_{\rho}\partial_{\mu}g^{\nu\rho}\,.

(2.26b)

The interpretation of this symmetry transformation is as follows. Let us start with a geodesic which is affinely parametrized. Since the conformal Killing vectors are solutions of the geodesic deviation equation, the result of the transformation is to first move the point
q^{\mu}
to a nearby geodesic. Then, is shifts along this new geodesic by
-t\lambda_{(i)}p^{\mu}
, in order to account for a potential dilatation of the spacetime generated by
\xi_{(i)}
, so as to obtain again an affine parametrization of the new solution.

Using the Poisson bracket on phase space, we can finally show that the conserved charges constructed from the
\xi_{(i)}
’s form an algebra which reproduces the Lie algebra of the Killing vectors, i.e.

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)},{\mathcal{Q}}_{(j)}\big\}=-{c_{ij}}^{k}{\mathcal{Q}}_{(k)}\,.

(2.27)

As we will illustrate below, it should be noted that the size of this algebra (or even its existence) depends on the choice of the lapse (or conformal factor for the metric)
N
. This doesn’t mean that we cannot recover the corresponding conserved charges in any time parametrization, but conversely that their form might depend on the history of the system. This happens because the factors of
t
that appear in the expressions of the conserved charges (
2.25
), when expressed in terms of another time
\tilde{t}
, would lead to non-local terms given by the integral of the ratio of the two lapses, associated
with the original and the new times:
N\mathrm{d}t=\widetilde{N}\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\Rightarrow t=\int\mathrm{d}\tilde{t}\,(\widetilde{N}/N)

[
41
]
.

Inspired by the symmetry structure which has been exhibited in previous work for cosmological minisuperspaces
[
47
,
46
,
45
,
43
,
53
,
49
]
and for the black hole interior
[
54
,
50
,
51
]
, we would like to extend the above construction to charges which are quadratic in time. This can be done in a systematic manner in the case of two-dimensional field spaces, which fortunately enough covers many homogeneous models of general relativity. The construction in the case of higher-dimensional field spaces has to be done on a case by case basis.

2.2.2
Inclusion of a potential

Before turning to the study of the two-dimensional field space geometries, we present a first brief discussion concerning the inclusion of a non-vanishing potential. Once again, this is an aspect which is heavily impacted by the choice of lapse, and which we illustrate below with many examples. Let us assume that the lapse has been chosen to be field-dependent.

If the product
N(q)\,\tilde{U}(q)
is equal to a constant
U_{0}
, then this constant does not play any fundamental role form the point of view of the symmetry algebra. All the above discussion is still valid, except for the fact that previously null trajectories now become massive or tachyonic. Indeed, such a constant potential is a boundary term for the point of view of the action. In the charge algebra, the effect of a constant term
U_{0}
is simply to shift the value of the Hamiltonian to obtain the charge

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\coloneqq H-U_{0}\,,\qquad\qquad\{{\mathcal{O}},H\}=\{{\mathcal{O}},{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\}\,.

(2.28)

This is exactly what happens in the case of KS cosmologies and the black hole interior
[
54
,
50
]
. We will review the explicit examples below.

Now, if the potential contains a non-constant piece, we use the following notation

\displaystyle N(q)\,\tilde{U}(q)=U(q)+U_{0}

(2.29)

to disentangle between a possibly constant term
U_{0}
and a field-dependent piece
U(q)
. This separation evidently depends on the choice of lapse, and the constant piece is not uniquely determined. However, we must add an extra condition on the Killing vectors in order to be able to define the charges (
2.25
). This condition is the requirement that the non constant piece gets rescaled similarly to the supermetric, i.e.

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}U=\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}U=-\lambda U\,.

(2.30)

Indeed, this is necessary in order to ensure that we still have

\displaystyle\big\{C_{(i)},{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\big\}=\big\{C_{(i)},\frac{1}{2}p^{\mu}p_{\mu}+U\big\}=\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{(i)}p^{\mu}p_{\mu}-\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}U=\lambda_{(i)}{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\,,

(2.31)

so that we can once again define the conserved quantities

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}\coloneqq C_{(i)}-t\lambda_{(i)}{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\,,\qquad\qquad\dot{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}=0\,,

(2.32)

which in turn generate symmetries and form an algebra. Moreover the condition (
2.30
), for
\lambda\neq 0
, uniquely fixes the constant piece
U_{0}
, so that the separation (
2.29
) actually depends both on the lapse and the vector
\xi
.

It is important to notice that under a conformal rescaling
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=Ng_{\mu\nu}
of the metric, corresponding to a change of lapse, the initial requirement
\pounds_{\xi}U=-\lambda U
implies

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\big(\lambda+\pounds_{\xi}\log N\big)\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\pounds_{\xi}\tilde{U}=-\big(\lambda+\pounds_{\xi}\log N\big)\tilde{U}+\lambda\frac{U_{0}}{N}=-\tilde{\lambda}\tilde{U}+\lambda\frac{U_{0}}{N}\,.

(2.33)

This means that in the presence of a non-trivial potential the desired properties on the transformation of the metric and the potential are not covariant under changes of the lapse. This makes the analysis of the symmetries and their algebra very involved, since both the number of homothetic Killing vectors (satisfying the homothetic conditions on both the metric and the potential) and the decomposition of the potential (
2.30
) depend on the lapse. This is why we will have to perform this analysis on a case by case basis.

Another method to deal with a non-trivial potential is to use the so-called Eisenhart lift
[
63
]
. This consists in extending the field space by the addition of a new field
y
, in order to map the
n
-dimensional problem with potential to an
(n+1)
-dimensional free system. The Eisenhart lift is explicitly given by an enlarged supermetric
G_{AB}
, with

\displaystyle G_{AB}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{\mu\nu}&0\\
0&1/(2U)\end{pmatrix}

(2.34)

and where the new coordinates (encoding the fields) are
x^{A}=(q^{\mu},y)
. The null geodesic equation of
G_{AB}
on the configuration space
(q^{\mu},y)
gives exactly the same equation as
H=p^{\mu}p_{\mu}+U
, as can easily be verified by computing the Hamiltonian equation for
H_{\mathrm{lift}}=G^{AB}p_{A}p_{B}
with
p_{A}=(p_{\mu},p_{y})
. In particular, the equation of motion for
p_{y}
states that it is a constant. Once we pull back this into the equations for
q^{\mu}
and
p_{\mu}
, these become equivalent to the original equations for the
n
-dimensional problem.

It is possible to show that searching for the homothetic Killing vectors of the supermetric
G_{AB}
of the enlarged space is the same as finding a simultaneous solution of the rescaling equations (
2.30
) and (
2.20
) for the supermetric and potential,
\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}={\lambda}g_{\mu\nu}
and
\pounds_{\xi}U=-{\lambda}U
. Although this apparently looks equivalent, it nevertheless allows for vector fields
\xi
which can depend on the new coordinate
y
and have non-trivial components along
\partial_{y}
. Unfortunately, this new coordinate does not have a physical meaning and there is no way to extract non-trivial Dirac observables from such a homothetic Killing vector on the Eisenhart lift metric.

Nevertheless, there exists an improved version of the lift, called the Eisenhart–Duval lift
[
64
,
65
]
, which adds two new coordinates
(u,w)
instead of the one
y
, and uses a Brinkmann supermetric

\displaystyle g_{\mu\nu}\text{d}x^{\mu}\text{d}x^{\nu}-2U\text{d}u^{2}+\text{d}u\,\text{d}w\,.

(2.35)

As shown in
[
66
]
, this improved lift identifies the new coordinate
u
to the time coordinate
t
along null geodesics, and its Killing vectors directly provide time-dependent conserved charges.

Finally, we would like to stress that the construction presented here strongly relies on the separation between configuration space and momenta. However, the classical description is of course invariant under any canonical transformation that could mix the two. In the case of a momentum-dependent potential, we could proceed as follows: find a canonical transformation that maps the Hamiltonian to a form like (
2.14
), then apply the construction above and finally go back to the original variables.

Two-dimensional field space geometries

We now discuss the extension of the algebra (
2.27
) to charges which are quadratic in time. We will focus initially on the case of field space geometries which are two-dimensional and flat. Interestingly this is sufficient to cover many minisuperspace models of general relativity, as we will detail in section
3.3
.

Free case

Let us consider the case of two-dimensional field spaces. Since every two-dimensional metric is conformally flat, we can use coordinates such that in Lorentzian signature (which is the field space signature of interest for us) we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}^{2}_{\mathrm{mini}}=-2e^{-\varphi}\mathrm{d}\tilde{u}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{v}\,.

(3.1)

Here all the dynamical information is encoded in the conformal factor
\varphi(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})
. The structure of the homothetic Killing vectors depends on the Ricci scalar curvature, which is given by

\displaystyle R=-2e^{\varphi}\partial_{\tilde{u}}\partial_{\tilde{v}}\varphi\,.

(3.2)

In the non-flat case
R\neq 0
the number of independent homothetic Killing vectors varies on a case by case basis. For example we have the following cases:

—

For (A)dS
2
, which corresponds to
\varphi=2\log(u\mp v)
, there are only the three Killing vectors

\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=\left({\mathfrak{c}}_{1}u^{2}+{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}u+{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}\right)\partial_{u}+\left(\pm{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}v^{2}+{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}v\pm{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}\right)\partial_{v}\,,\qquad\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=0\,,\qquad{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}=\text{const.}\,,

(3.3)

where the upper sign is for AdS and the lower one is for dS.

—

For
\varphi=u^{2}v
there is only one homothetic Killing vector given by

\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=-\lambda(u\partial_{u}-2v\partial_{v})\,,\qquad\qquad\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=\lambda g_{\mu\nu}\,.

(3.4)

—

For
\varphi=uv
, there is only one true Killing vector given by

\displaystyle\xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=-u\partial_{u}+v\partial_{v}\,,\qquad\qquad\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=0\,.

(3.5)

The case of interest for us is that of a flat field space geometry.

In the free case (i.e. with vanishing potential), it is always possible to reduce the problem to that in a flat field space by a suitable choice of lapse. Indeed, in the Lagrangian (
2.11
) with coordinates such that the metric is (
3.1
) we can simply choose the lapse to be
N=e^{-\varphi}
, so that
g_{\mu\nu}=\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}/N
is indeed flat. Alternatively, it might be the case that there are null coordinates such that
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
by itself is already flat. This is the case whenever we can separate
\varphi(\tilde{u},\tilde{v})=\varphi_{1}(\tilde{u})+\varphi_{2}(\tilde{v})
. In this case we can simply take e.g.
N=1
, or any lapse of the form
N=N_{1}(\tilde{u})N_{2}(\tilde{v})
. Let us now assume that we are in the flat case, and use standard null coordinates to write

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\mathrm{mini}}=-2\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v\,.

(3.6)

This metric has three true Killing vectors (two translation and one boost), and one homothetic vector corresponding to the dilatation of the plane. For each of these vectors we can compute the factor
\lambda_{(i)}
and the phase space function (
2.23
). In null coordinates we find

\displaystyle\xi_{\mathrm{t}}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{t}}

\displaystyle=p_{u}+p_{v}\,,

(3.7a)

space translation:

\displaystyle\xi_{\mathrm{x}}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=

\displaystyle\;\partial_{u}-\partial_{v}

\displaystyle\lambda_{\mathrm{x}}=

\displaystyle\;0

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{x}}

\displaystyle=p_{u}-p_{v}\,,

(3.7b)

boost:

\displaystyle\xi_{\mathrm{b}}^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}=

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(up_{u}+vp_{v})\,.

(3.7d)

Thanks to these Killing vectors, in addition to the Hamiltonian

In two dimensions, the vanishing of the Ricci scalar and of the Riemann tensor are equivalent. This has an immediate implication on the existence of another set of charges. One can indeed show (see appendix
C

\displaystyle V_{(ij)}\coloneqq g_{\mu\nu}\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}\xi_{(j)}^{\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad\dddot{V}_{(ij)}=0\,.

(3.8)

The observables

This can be checked using the fact that the brackets with the Hamiltonian are

This procedure, which we have outlined here in the two-dimensional case, can be extended to a field space of arbitrary dimension, although it is of course not guaranteed to then lead to the same results. If the field space is non-flat and higher-dimensional we are indeed not guaranteed that homothetic Killing vectors exist, nor that the observables

With the construction presented here we have however the tools to investigate this in a case by case basis.

3.1.1
Charge algebra

In order to study the charge algebra it is convenient to slightly rewrite the above generators and first introduce the four quantities

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{-1}\coloneqq-{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{L}}_{0}\coloneqq{\mathcal{Q}}_{\mathrm{d}}\,,

\displaystyle=t^{n+1}\,,

\displaystyle n

\displaystyle\in\{-1,0,1\}\,,

(3.14a)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}^{\pm}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\Big(\big(\dot{g}V_{2}+gC_{\mathrm{t}}\big)\pm\big(\dot{g}V_{1}+gC_{\mathrm{x}}\big)\Big)\,,

\displaystyle g(t)

\displaystyle=t^{s+1/2}\,,

\displaystyle s

(3.14b)

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{L}}_{n},{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\big\}

(3.15a)

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{L}}_{n},{\mathcal{D}}_{0}\big\}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(3.15b)

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{S}}_{s}^{\epsilon},{\mathcal{S}}_{s^{\prime}}^{\epsilon^{\prime}}\big\}

\displaystyle=2n\delta_{\epsilon+\epsilon^{\prime}}\delta_{s+s^{\prime}}\,,

(3.15c)

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{L}}_{n},{\mathcal{S}}_{s}^{\pm}\big\}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n}{2}-s\right){\mathcal{S}}_{n+s}^{\pm}\,,

(3.15d)

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{D}}_{0},{\mathcal{S}}_{s}^{\pm}\big\}

\displaystyle=\pm{\mathcal{S}}_{s}^{\pm}\,,

(3.15e)

with

and

\displaystyle{\mathcal{A}}=\big({\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\oplus{\mathbb{R}}\big)\mathbin{\mathchoice{\makebox[9.99997pt]{\raisebox{-0.83333pt}{\begin{picture}\displaystyle\displaystyle 3

Because the physical system which we are describing has a four-dimensional phase space spanned by the coordinates

, these charges cannot be all independent. Indeed, one can show that they are related by the quadratic construction

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{n}=\sum_{k=-1/2}^{1/2}k\left(\frac{n}{2}+k\right)\big({\mathcal{S}}^{+}_{k}{\mathcal{S}}^{-}_{n-k}+{\mathcal{S}}^{-}_{k}{\mathcal{S}}^{+}_{n-k}\big)\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{D}}_{0}=2\sum_{k=-1/2}^{1/2}k{\mathcal{S}}^{-}_{k}{\mathcal{S}}^{+}_{-k}\,.

From this point of view, one may see the four generators

of

as the fundamental building blocks of the symmetry algebra. They represent the four constants of the motion which are preserved in the evolution, and any (possibly non-linear) combination of them will again be conserved on trajectories and generate a symmetry. The relations (

In order to make contact with the symmetry algebra discussed in
[

50

to build two sets of generators as

Due to the Heisenberg central extension, these two abelian sets of generators do not commute and give

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{T}}^{\pm}_{n},{\mathcal{T}}^{\pm}_{m}\big\}=0\,,\qquad\qquad\big\{{\mathcal{T}}^{+}_{n},{\mathcal{T}}^{-}_{m}\big\}=\frac{1}{2}(n-m){\mathcal{L}}_{n+m}-\frac{1}{4}(n^{2}+m^{2}-nm-1){\mathcal{D}}_{0}\,.

(3.19)

Finally, these generators can be combined into

\displaystyle{\mathcal{P}}^{\sigma}_{n}\coloneqq{\mathcal{T}}^{-}_{n}+\sigma{\mathcal{T}}^{+}_{n}\,,

for which we find

\displaystyle\big\{{\mathcal{P}}^{\sigma}_{n},{\mathcal{P}}^{\sigma}_{m}\big\}=\sigma(n-m){\mathcal{L}}_{n+m}\,,\qquad\qquad\big\{{\mathcal{P}}^{\sigma}_{n},{\mathcal{L}}_{m}\big\}=(n-m){\mathcal{P}}^{\sigma}_{n+m}\,.

(3.21)

Together with the brackets (
3.15a
), these commutation relations show that

span an algebra

which, depending on the sign of

of dS, the isometry algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\sigma<0}={\mathfrak{so}}(2,2)
of AdS, or the isometry algebra
\mathfrak{g}_{\sigma=0}={\mathfrak{iso}}(2,1)
of Minskowski. This latter, which is the (2+1)-dimensional Poincaré algebra, is the algebra which was studied in
[
54
,
50
]

3.1.2
Symmetry transformations

Let us now focus on the 8-dimensional algebra (
3.16
) and investigate the corresponding symmetry group. Indeed we view the conserved charges as Noether charges, whose Poisson brackets have an exponentiated flow which generates finite symmetry transformations. Thus we start by computing their infinitesimal action given by their Poisson brackets with the field space coordinates
q^{\mu}=(u,v)
. The action on these null coordinates is found to be

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\delta u\\
\delta v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{1}{2}\dot{f}(t)u-f(t)\dot{u}+ku+g(t)\vskip 8.5359pt\\
\dfrac{1}{2}\dot{f}(t)v-f(t)\dot{v}-kv+h(t)\end{pmatrix}\,,

(3.22)

where
k\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the function
f(t)
is quadratic in
t
, and the functions
g(t)
and
h(t)
are linear in
t
. A straightforward calculation shows that these finite transformations are indeed symmetries of the action

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}=-\int\mathrm{d}t\,\dot{u}\dot{v}\,.

(3.23)

The algebra of these transformations is

\displaystyle\big[\delta(f_{1},g_{1},h_{1},k_{1}),\delta(f_{2},g_{2},h_{2},k_{2})\big]_{\mathrm{Lie}}=\delta(f_{12},g_{12},h_{12},k_{12})\,,

(3.24)

with

\displaystyle f_{12}

\displaystyle=\dot{f}_{1}f_{2}-(1\leftrightarrow 2)\,,

(3.25a)

\displaystyle k_{12}

\displaystyle=0\,,

(3.25b)

\displaystyle g_{12}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}g_{1}\dot{f}_{2}+f_{2}\dot{g}_{1}-k_{1}g_{1}-(1\leftrightarrow 2)\,,

(3.25c)

\displaystyle h_{12}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}h_{1}\dot{f}_{2}+f_{2}\dot{h}_{1}+k_{1}h_{1}-(1\leftrightarrow 2)\,.

(3.25d)

This is equivalent to the charge algebra (
3.15
), up to the Heisenberg central extension. Each
{\mathcal{L}}_{n}
generates the coefficient
t^{n+1}
in the function
f
, each
{\mathcal{S}}^{-}_{s}
generates the coefficient
t^{s+1/2}
of
h
, and each
{\mathcal{S}}^{+}_{s}
generates the coefficient
t^{s+1/2}
of
g
. Finally,
{\mathcal{D}}_{0}
corresponds to
k
.

These infinitesimal transformations can can be integrated to a finite group action. The exponentiated generators (
3.12
) give the scaled Möbius transformations, acting as the group
\mathrm{SL}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\times{\mathbb{R}}
on the null coordinates via

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\\
\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\big(\dot{f}\big)^{1/2}\begin{pmatrix}k\,u\\
k^{-1}v\\
\end{pmatrix}\circ f^{(-1)}=\frac{1}{ct+d}\begin{pmatrix}k\,u\\
k^{-1}v\\
\end{pmatrix}\circ f^{(-1)}\,,\qquad\qquad\text{where}\;\;f(t)=\frac{at+b}{ct+d}\,,

(3.26)

for constants coefficients
(k,a,b,c,d)
such that
ad-bc=1
. The action of the Heisenberg generators (
3.13
) exponentiates to that of the abelian group
{\mathbb{R}}^{4}
acting as

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\\
\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}u+\alpha t+\beta\\
v+\gamma t+\delta\\
\end{pmatrix}\,.

(3.27)

Again, one can check that these are symmetries of (
3.23
).

Inclusion of a potential

The algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
obtained above always exists in the free case. We now would like to tackle the case of a non-vanishing potential, and determine if this algebra (or a suitable deformation thereof) exists, or if a sub-algebra survives, or if there is no symmetry structure. In the previous section
2.2.2
, we have briefly discussed our strategy for the case with a non-vanishing potential, looking for Killing vectors along which the potential gets rescaled. With the explicit expression for the Killing vectors for the free theory at hand, we can now push this analysis further.

Considering an arbitrary minisuperspace model with two-dimensional field space, one can always pick null conformally-flat coordinates such that the metric on field space simply takes the form
\mathrm{d}\tilde{s}^{2}_{\mathrm{mini}}=-2e^{-\varphi}\mathrm{d}\tilde{u}\,\mathrm{d}\tilde{v}
, so that the Lagrangian reads

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=-\frac{1}{N}e^{-\varphi}\dot{\tilde{u}}\dot{\tilde{v}}-N\tilde{U}\,,

(3.28)

where the potential
\tilde{U}
and the conformal factor
\varphi
follow from the definition of the minisuperspace, while the lapse
N
can be chosen arbitrarily. We see two natural possibilities for studying this Lagrangian.

(1)

Choose
N=1/\tilde{U}
so as to trivialize the potential term.

(1.1)

If the resulting conformal factor
\tilde{U}e^{-\varphi}
is such that the supermetric is flat then the whole algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
survives by the above construction.

(1.2)

If the resulting supermetric is not flat, one should go back to the beginning of the analysis to study the conformal Killing vectors
\xi_{(i)}
, construct the objects
C_{(i)}
and
V_{(ij)}
, and compute their algebra.

(2)

Choose
N=e^{-\varphi}N_{1}(\tilde{u})N_{2}(\tilde{v})
such that the supermetric is flat, and then study whether the resulting potential
U=N\tilde{U}
satisfies
\pounds_{\xi}U=-\lambda U
for (
3.7
).

This procedure has to be implemented on a case by case basis, and we give the examples of physical interest in section
3.3
below. In general, we expect that demanding that the homothetic Killing vectors of the metric also be Killing vectors of the potential will reduce the number of admissible vectors, and therefore also reduce the size of the symmetry algebra.

In order to illustrate this, let us consider an example, relevant for the Bianchi models, where the shifted potential
U(q)=N(q)\tilde{U}(q)-U_{0}
, as defined earlier in (
2.29
), is after a suitable choice of lapse a monomial in the two null coordinates, i.e. of the form
U=u^{n}v^{m}
. This leads to three possibilities:

(1)

If
n=0
,
m\neq 0
, then there are two vectors satisfying (
2.30
), given by

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\xi_{\mathrm{t}}+\xi_{\mathrm{x}}\,,\qquad\quad\xi_{2}=(2+m)\xi_{\mathrm{b}}+2m\xi_{\mathrm{d}}\,,\qquad\quad\lambda_{1}=0\,,\qquad\quad\lambda_{2}=2m\,.

(3.29)

(2)

If
n\neq 0
,
m=0
, we have

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=\xi_{\mathrm{t}}-\xi_{\mathrm{x}}\,,\qquad\quad\xi_{2}=(2+n)\xi_{\mathrm{b}}-2n\xi_{\mathrm{d}}\,,\qquad\quad\lambda_{1}=0\,,\qquad\quad\lambda_{2}=-2n\,.

(3.30)

(3)

If
n,m\neq 0
, then there is a single admissible vector given by

\displaystyle\xi_{1}=(2+n+m)\xi_{\mathrm{b}}+2(m-n)\xi_{\mathrm{d}}\,,\qquad\qquad\lambda_{1}=2(m-n)\,.

(3.31)

This shows that the existence and the number of the linear charges
{\mathcal{Q}}_{(i)}
defined in (
2.25
) depends on the potential. Then, in order for the quadratic charges to exist and close the algebra, we need to have closed brackets as in (
3.11
) between the phase space functions
V_{(ij)}=g_{\mu\nu}\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}\xi_{(j)}^{\nu}
, the Hamiltonian
{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}
, and the functions
C_{(i)}
. For example in the third case above, with
n,m\neq 0
we compute

\displaystyle V_{11}=\xi^{2}_{1}

\displaystyle=(2+n+m)^{2}\xi_{\mathrm{b}}^{2}-4(m-n)^{2}\xi_{\mathrm{d}}^{2}=4(1+m)(1+n)V_{3}\,,

(3.32a)

\displaystyle C_{1}=p_{\mu}\xi^{\mu}_{1}

\displaystyle=(2+n+m)C_{\mathrm{b}}-2(m-n)C_{\mathrm{d}}\,,

(3.32b)

\displaystyle\{C_{1},V_{11}\}

\displaystyle=2(n-m)V_{11}\,,

(3.32c)

\displaystyle\{{\mathcal{Q}}_{0},V_{11}\}

\displaystyle=8(1+m)(1+n)C_{\mathrm{d}}\,.

(3.32d)

In order for the last bracket to close on
C_{1}
the potential must be such that
2+n+m\stackrel{{\scriptstyle!}}{{=}}0
. In this case the algebra of the charges reduces to
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\subset{\mathcal{A}}
. This situation, which corresponds to a potential
U\propto u^{n}v^{m}
with
(n+m)=-2
which is homogeneous of degree

. We shall remark that also for the cases

the algebra closes, but

is trivial and the algebra is two dimensional, containing only

and

.

We note in passing that the case of a model with one-dimensional field space is trivial and always admits an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra. Indeed, if the Lagrangian is
{\mathcal{L}}=\tilde{g}(q)\dot{q}^{2}/N-N\tilde{U}
one can choose

and work with the rescaled metric

. The resulting phase space with Hamiltonian

then supports an

with

\displaystyle{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}=H-1\,,\qquad\qquad V=\xi^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad C=g^{-1}p\,\xi\,,\qquad\qquad\xi={\mathfrak{c}}_{1}+\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}}{2}\int^{q}\mathrm{d}q^{\prime}\sqrt{g(q^{\prime})}\,,

(3.33)

where
{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}
and
{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}\neq 0
are otherwise arbitrary constants. This simple setup is here deliberately presented in an analogous way to the case of a higher-dimensional field space.

Applications

Second, it turns out that this non-flat supermetric does not admit any Killing vectors, so the method fails when the lapse is chosen as the inverse of the full potential. However, it turns out that for these models the potential is actually a sum of two or three monomials of the form
\tilde{u}^{n}\tilde{v}^{m}
. This opens the possibility of choosing the lapse so as to set one of these terms of the potential to a constant. By construction, for these models since
\varphi=0
such a choice of lapse will lead to a flat field space metric. One can then choose coordinates to put this metric in the form (
3.6
), and finally study the condition (
2.30
) for the remaining terms in the potential.

Let us illustrate this construction on the general action of the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}=\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{G}\int\mathrm{d}t\left[-\frac{1}{N}\dot{\tilde{u}}\dot{\tilde{v}}+N\Big({\mathfrak{c}}_{1}\tilde{u}^{n_{1}}\tilde{v}^{m_{1}}+{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}\tilde{u}^{n_{2}}\tilde{v}^{m_{2}}\Big)\right]\,.

(3.43)

Without loss of generality we can now choose the lapse and the coordinates (with
m_{i},n_{i}\neq-1
) as

\displaystyle N=\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{G}\frac{1}{\tilde{u}^{n_{1}}\tilde{v}^{m_{1}}}\,,\qquad\qquad u=\dfrac{\tilde{u}^{n_{1}+1}}{1+n_{1}}\,,\qquad\qquad v=\dfrac{\tilde{v}^{m_{1}+1}}{1+m_{1}}\,,

(3.44)

to obtain

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}=\int\mathrm{d}t\Big[-\dot{u}\dot{v}-(U+U_{0})\Big]\,,

(3.45)

with the potential

\displaystyle U_{0}=-\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}^{2}}{G^{2}}{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}\,,\qquad\qquad U=-\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}^{2}}{G^{2}}{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}\big((n_{1}+1)u\big)^{\frac{n_{2}-n_{1}}{n_{1}+1}}\big((m_{1}+1)v\big)^{\frac{m_{2}-m_{1}}{m_{1}+1}}\,.

(3.46)

We are now exactly in the case (
3.31
) discussed above. There is a single homothetic Killing vector, and an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
survives, spanned by the charges
(C_{1},V_{11},{\mathcal{Q}}_{0})
, if and only if

\displaystyle 2+n_{1}+n_{2}+m_{1}+m_{2}+n_{1}m_{2}+n_{2}m_{1}=0\,.

(3.47)

It turns out that this equation is satisfied for the potentials of the Bianchi models VIII and IX, but not for the Bianchi models IV and VII.

In summary, we have shown that the full algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
(
3.16
) can be obtained for the models (III, V, VI, KS), while for the models (VIII, IX) only the conformal subalgebra survives. The Bianchi models IV and VII are particular in the sense that they cannot be assigned an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra of phase space functions with the construction presented here. This deserves more investigation. A classical change of variable does not alter this conclusion, as our analysis is completely covariant on the field space, however a different choice of internal metric (e.g. non diagonal) might change the results.

3.3.2
FLRW cosmology with scalar field

In the case of FLRW cosmology with curvature
k
, we only have a single scale factor
a
, so the field space is one-dimensional and (
3.33
) easily applies. This also extends to the case of a non-vanishing cosmological constant, where it reproduces the results of

]
. On the other hand, without any matter content the dynamics itself is trivial.

Let us therefore consider the addition of a scalar field
\Phi
, and apply the above formalism to the two-dimensional field space parametrized by the coordinates
\Phi
and
a
. With the line element

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\mathrm{FLRW}}=-N^{2}\mathrm{d}t^{2}+a(t)^{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{d}r^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}(\mathrm{d}\theta^{2}+\sin^{2}\theta\,\mathrm{d}\phi^{2})\right)\,,

the symmetry-reduced action reads

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}_{\mathrm{FLRW}}={\mathcal{V}}_{0}\int\mathrm{d}t\left[\frac{1}{2N}\left(a^{3}\dot{\Phi}^{2}-\frac{3a\dot{a}^{2}}{4\pi G}\right)+N\frac{3ka}{8\pi G}\right]\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{V}}_{0}=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\frac{r^{2}\sin\theta}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}}\,.

This reduced action is of the same form as the others treated above in this article, the supermetric and the potential beeing

\displaystyle\mathrm{d}s^{2}_{\mathrm{mini}}=\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}a^{3}}{N}\mathrm{d}\Phi^{2}-\frac{3{\mathcal{V}}_{0}a}{4\pi GN}\mathrm{d}a^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad\tilde{U}=-\frac{3ka{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{8\pi G}\,.

(3.50)

Along the lines of the above discussion in the case of the Bianchi models, we choose the lapse which sets the potential to a constant
N\tilde{U}=U_{0}
, i.e.

\displaystyle N=-\frac{8\pi{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{3Ga}\,,\qquad\qquad U_{0}=-k\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}^{2}}{G^{2}}\,,

(3.51)

and find a map to the null coordinates (

\displaystyle\begin{array}[]{rlcrl}u&=\dfrac{3}{16\pi}a^{2}e^{2\kappa\Phi/3}\,,&&p_{u}&=\dfrac{4\pi}{3\kappa}\dfrac{ap_{a}\kappa+3p_{\Phi}}{a^{2}}e^{-2\kappa\Phi/3}\,,\\[9.0pt]

\end{array}

with

under this transformation.

Three-dimensional field space geometries

We now briefly discuss an application of the framework to three-dimensional field spaces, which appear e.g. in the Bianchi I and II models. Using the line element (
D.1b
), we find that the reduced action for these Bianchi models takes the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}=-\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{G}\int\mathrm{d}t\left[\frac{2}{N}\big(a\dot{b}\dot{c}+b\dot{a}\dot{c}+c\dot{a}\dot{b}\big)+\varepsilon\frac{N}{2}\frac{a^{2}}{bc}\right]\,,

(4.1)

where Bianchi I has a vanishing potential with
\varepsilon=0
, and Bianchi II has
\varepsilon=1
. The three-dimensional field space metric is diagonalized by the change of (field space) coordinates

\displaystyle a=e^{\sqrt{2}\,u-2w}\,,\qquad\qquad b=e^{v+w}\,,\qquad\qquad c=e^{\sqrt{2}\,u-2v}\,,

(4.2)

leading to

\displaystyle{\mathcal{S}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{G}\int\mathrm{d}t\left[\frac{4}{N}e^{2\sqrt{2}\,u-v-w}\big(\dot{u}^{2}-\dot{v}^{2}-\dot{w}^{2}\big)+\varepsilon\frac{N}{2}e^{2\sqrt{2}\,u+v-7w}\right]

(4.3)

\displaystyle=-\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{G}\int\mathrm{d}t\left[\frac{1}{2N}\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}\dot{q}^{\mu}\dot{q}^{\nu}+\varepsilon\frac{N}{2}e^{2\sqrt{2}\,u+v-7w}\right]\,,

(4.4)

where we denote the new coordinates by
q^{\mu}=(u,v,w)
even though these are not null coordinates as in the two-dimensional case. The field space metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
is not flat and, in the case of Bianchi II, we have moreover to take into account the presence of the potential.

Bianchi I

In the case of the Bianchi I model, we have a free theory with
\varepsilon=0
. We identify two interesting choices of lapse, which both lead to an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
symmetry algebra.

A first possibility is to choose
N=e^{2\sqrt{2}\,u-v-w}
, so that the conformally-rescaled metric
g_{\mu\nu}
is that of
(2+1)
-dimensional Minkowski space. The metric then admits six exact Killing vectors corresponding to the six Poincaré isometries. They are given by

\displaystyle\xi=t^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}+{\varepsilon^{\mu}}_{\nu\rho}\partial_{\mu}q^{\nu}\ell^{\rho}\,,

(4.5)

where
t=(t_{1},t_{2},t_{3})
are translations and
\ell=(\ell_{1},\ell_{2},\ell_{3})
Lorentz transformations. The metric also admits an homothetic Killing vector, corresponding to a dilatation with

\displaystyle\xi_{\mathrm{d}}=u\partial_{u}+v\partial_{v}+w\partial_{w}\,,\qquad\qquad\lambda_{\mathrm{d}}=2\,,\qquad\qquad C_{\mathrm{d}}=up_{u}+vp_{v}+wp_{w}\,,

(4.6)

which generalizes the scaling symmetry (
3.7d
) of the two-dimensional models to the three-dimensional case. Starting with these 7 conformal Killing vectors, we follow the same procedure as above and define the quantities
C_{(i)}=p_{\mu}\xi^{\mu}_{(i)}
and
V_{(ij)}=g_{\mu\nu}\xi^{\mu}_{(i)}\xi^{\nu}_{(j)}
. There are 7 non-trivial
C_{(i)}
’s and 10 non-trivial
V_{(ij)}
’s given by

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}p_{u}\\
p_{v}\\
p_{w}\\
vp_{w}-wp_{v}\\
up_{w}+wp_{u}\\
-up_{v}-vp_{u}\\
up_{u}+vp_{v}+wp_{w}\end{pmatrix}\in C_{(i)}\,,\qquad\qquad\begin{pmatrix}u\\
v\\
w\\
uv\\
uw\\
vw\\
u^{2}-v^{2}\\
u^{2}-w^{2}\\
v^{2}+w^{2}\\

(4.7)

Computing the triply-iterated bracket of these

reveals that
\dddot{V}_{ij}=0
, in agreement with equation (
C
) and the fact that the field space geometry is flat. In order to have phase space functions which form a closed algebra with
C_{(i)}

V_{(ij)}
and

\dot{V}_{(ij)}
, as well as another six new functions produced by
\ddot{V}_{(ij)}

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}up_{u}+vp_{v}\\
up_{u}+wp_{w}\\
vp_{v}+wp_{w}\\
up_{v}-vp_{u}\\
up_{w}-wp_{u}\\
vp_{w}+wp_{v}\\
\end{pmatrix}\in\dot{V}_{(ij)}\,,\qquad\qquad\begin{pmatrix}p_{u}p_{v}\\
p_{u}p_{w}\\
p_{v}p_{w}\\

\end{pmatrix}\in\ddot{V}_{(ij)}\,.

(4.8)

At the end of the day, we find
7+10+1+6+6=30
generators forming a closed algebra. There are in particular three generators forming an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra. This symmetry algebra is spanned by

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{d}}=up_{u}+vp_{v}+wp_{w}\,,\qquad\qquad V_{\mathrm{dd}}=u^{2}-v^{2}-w^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad H=p_{u}^{2}-p_{v}^{2}-p_{w}^{2}\,,

(4.9)

which is in agreement with the results of
[
43
]
concerning the existence of an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra in the case of the Bianchi I model. We should note that the 30 phase space functions given above are not all independent: they satisfy linear as well as quadratic dependency relations. For instance, one recovers
V_{\mathrm{dd}}
as a linear combination of three other elements of
V_{(ij)}
. Similarly, one recovers
C_{\mathrm{d}}
as a linear combination of three other elements of
\dot{V}_{(ij)}
, and finally one recovers
H
as a linear combination of three other elements of
\ddot{V}_{(ij)}
. These linear and quadratic dependency relations are obvious from the fact that
C_{(i)}
and
V_{(ij)}
contain the phase space variables
(u,v,w,p_{u},p_{v},p_{w})
, which can therefore be combined in order to reproduce all the other 24 generators. The fact that the above 30 functions have closed brackets is nonetheless a non-trivial statement.

Alternatively, one could choose the lapse
N=1
, and work with the non-flat field space metric
g_{\mu\nu}=e^{2\sqrt{2}\,u-v-w}\text{diag}(1,-1,-1)
. This metric admits three homothetic vectors given by

\displaystyle\xi=\frac{{\mathfrak{c}}_{1}}{2\sqrt{2}}\partial_{u}+{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}\partial_{v}+{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}\partial_{w}\,,\qquad\qquad\lambda={\mathfrak{c}}_{1}-{\mathfrak{c}}_{2}-{\mathfrak{c}}_{3}\,,

(4.10)

where the
{\mathfrak{c}}_{i}
’s are constants (which can evidently be chosen so as to obtain true Killing vectors). Out of the six possible observables
V_{(ij)}
obtained by contracting these homothetic vectors, we find that only three are non-trivial, and furthermore all proportional to
V=e^{2\sqrt{2}\,u-v-w}=abc
, which is simply the 3d volume of the spatial slices. This function forms an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra together with
H
and the function
C=2\sqrt{2}\,p_{u}+p_{v}+p_{w}
, which is the sum of the three
C_{(i)}
’s and defines the isotropic dilatation generator. As expected, this is simply a canonical transformation of the algebraic structure found with the previous choice of lapse. This
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra controls the evolution of the isotropic volume
V=abc
, whose speed is given by the dilatation generator
C
and acceleration by the Hamiltonian
H
.

Perspectives

In this paper we have studied the symmetry structure of gravitational minisuperspaces. For this, we have considered reductions of general relativity to one-dimensional models, for which the variable components of a given space-time metric ansatz depend on a single coordinate, chosen as the time coordinate. In that case, the Einstein–Hilbert action reduces to mechanical systems. Imposing that the metric ansatz satisfies the ADM vector constraint ensures that the equations of motion resulting from the reduced action are equivalent to the full Einstein equations for the original line element.

We have based our study of the symmetries on the observation that the dynamics is described by a Lagrangian of the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}=\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}\dot{q}^{\mu}\dot{q}^{\nu}-U(q)\,.

(
2.11
)

Here the variables
q_{\mu}
are components of the space-time line element, i.e. the dynamical fields of the minisuperspace models. They depend on the time coordinate
t
and this Lagrangian describes their evolution in time. It depends on a metric in field space, or
supermetric
,
g_{\mu\nu}
. The isometries of this supermetric and the scaling properties of the potential
U
are directly connected to the symmetries of the mechanical model. In particular, once we identify a homothetic vector field
\xi
satisfying the conditions

\displaystyle\pounds_{\xi}g_{\mu\nu}=\lambda g_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad\pounds_{\xi}U=-\lambda U\,,

(5.1)

then the quantity
{\mathcal{Q}}=\xi^{\mu}p_{\mu}-t\lambda H
is conserved and, in turn, it generates a symmetry of the system. However, the non-covariance of the conformal properties of the supermetric and the potential under (field-dependent) changes of lapse render a systematic analysis quite involved. This subtlety reflects the deeper fact that the conserved charges associated with these minisuperspace symmetries are local only with respect some particular choices of clocks, while they might depend on the history of the system through a non-local factor for a more general time coordinate
[
41
]
.

The majority of cases of interest (the black hole interior and some Bianchi models) fall in the class of two-dimensional field spaces, where we can always pick coordinates in which the supermetric is conformally flat. The advantage of this observation is twofold. On the one hand, we can easily handle the question of the existence of homothetic killing vectors, and the related choice of lapse, and on the other hand, it allows us to determine the presence of charges that are quadratic in time. These are built for flat supermetrics starting from the scalar products between vector fields,
V_{(ij)}=\xi_{(i)\mu}\xi^{\mu}_{(j)}
. Thanks to the study of two-dimensional superspaces, we were able to identify the existence of an 8-dimensional algebra
{\mathcal{A}}=\big({\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})\oplus{\mathbb{R}}\big)\mathbin{\mathchoice{\makebox[9.99997pt]{\raisebox{-0.83333pt}{\begin{picture}\displaystyle\displaystyle 3
\roundcap\put(0.5,0.5){\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\displaystyle+$}}}
\put(0.5,0.5){\arc[270,90]{0.5}}
\end{picture}}}}{\makebox[9.99997pt]{\raisebox{-0.83333pt}{\begin{picture}\textstyle\displaystyle 3
\roundcap\put(0.5,0.5){\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\raisebox{0.83333pt}{$\textstyle+$}}}
\put(0.5,0.5){\arc[270,90]{0.5}}
\end{picture}}}}{\makebox[8.36108pt]{\raisebox{-0.83334pt}{\begin{picture}\scriptstyle\displaystyle 3
\roundcap\put(0.5,0.5){\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\raisebox{0.83334pt}{$\scriptstyle+$}}}
\put(0.5,0.5){\arc[270,90]{0.5}}
\end{picture}}}}{\makebox[7.36115pt]{\raisebox{-0.83336pt}{\begin{picture}\scriptscriptstyle\displaystyle 3
\roundcap\put(0.5,0.5){\pic@makebox@{\makebox}{}(0.0,0.0){\raisebox{0.83336pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle+$}}}
\put(0.5,0.5){\arc[270,90]{0.5}}
\end{picture}}}}}\mathfrak{h}_{2}
for FLRW cosmology with a scalar field, Kantowski–Sachs cosmologies and the Bianchi models III, V and VI. Therein the
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
sector is a generalization of the CVH algebra originally found in flat cosmologies. For the Bianchi VIII and XI models, only the CVH sector survives, while for the models IV and VII we find that the non-trivial potential spoils the construction and that our procedure does not produce any algebra. The reason behind this peculiar property of the Bianchi models IV and VII is still unclear.

We have also included a brief study of the Bianchi I and II models, which represent two examples of three-dimensional superspaces. For the Bianchi I model, we find an algebra with 30 generators (
4.7
), while Bianchi II leads to a four-dimensional algebra, still containing an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
subalgebra.

Despite their apparent simplicity, the minisuperspaces contain a rich symmetry structure. However, a clear understanding of the origin and physical role of this structure is still needed. For black holes and cosmology, a subtle relationship with the spatial boundaries and the scaling properties of the model have already been unravelled and studied
[
41
,
50
,
45
]
, and a possible consequence on perturbation theory has been recently pointed out
[
67
]
. It would be interesting to see to what extent this feature generalizes to the other Bianchi models.

For flat cosmologies and black holes, the infinite-dimensional extension of the symmetry group gives a solution generating tool, that allows to turn on an effective cosmological constant or a scalar field
[
51
,
53
,
54
]
. The infinite-dimensional group acts as a rescaling of the coupling constants of the theory, in a manner reminiscent of a renormalization group flow. A generalization to the Bianchi models is an ongoing work and might help us obtain a better understanding of these symmetry structures.

Finally, the simplicity of the mechanical models allows for a straightforward quantization, where the conserved charges naturally provide a (full) set of Dirac observables
[
41
,
52
]
, that can be straightforwardly quantized by means of the representation theory of the symmetry group. The requirement of protection of the symmetry gives also a valuable tool to discriminate between different effective dynamics
[
44
,
43
,
50
,
52
]
. It also opens the door towards a possible emerging description of spacetime itself, out of the symmetry group
[
68
]
.

In the end, a deeper comprehension of the origin of the minisuperspace symmetries could enlighten many aspects of the holographic properties of general relativity, and hopefully help understand the proper notion of observables for a quantum theory of gravity.

Appendices

ADM approach to the CVH algebra

We have explained in the core of the article how homogeneous minisuperspace models lead to mechanical Lagrangians of the form (
2.11
), and used the field space geometry of these Lagrangians to characterize the presence of a CVH algebra and possible extensions thereof. A natural question is therefore that of the relationship between this approach and the standard ADM field theory approach, in which one deals directly with the geometry of the
3+1
foliation instead of considering the geometry of an auxiliary field space. The spacetime ADM approach enables to show that a CVH algebra always exists if the three-dimensional Ricci scalar of the spatial slice is vanishing, regardless of the value of the cosmological constant. Here we recall this calculation.

Let us denote the spatial indices by
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\dots
. We consider the standard ADM approach where the canonical variables are the spatial metric
q_{\alpha\beta}
and its momentum
p^{\alpha\beta}=\sqrt{q}(Kq^{\alpha\beta}-K^{\alpha\beta})
. We denote
q=\det(q_{\alpha\beta})
. The smeared Hamiltonian constraint is

\displaystyle H=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,N{\mathcal{H}}\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{H}}=-\sqrt{q}\big(R^{(3)}-2\Lambda\big)-\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}Q_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}p^{\alpha\beta}p^{\gamma\delta}\,,

(A.1)

where
R^{(3)}
is the Ricci scalar of the three-dimensional slice and

\displaystyle Q_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}q_{\alpha\beta}q_{\gamma\delta}-q_{\alpha\gamma}q_{\beta\delta}\,.

(A.2)

From this expression of the ADM Hamiltonian, it is immediate to identify which term gets mapped to the terms of the field space Hamiltonian derived from the Lagrangian (
2.11
). Under the symmetry reduction to homogeneous minisuperspaces we have indeed that

\displaystyle H=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,N{\mathcal{H}}\qquad\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{sym. red.}\vphantom{\frac{1}{2}}}}{{\Longrightarrow}}\qquad{\mathcal{V}}_{0}N\left(\frac{1}{2}\tilde{g}^{\mu\nu}p_{\mu}p_{\nu}+\tilde{U}\right)\,,

(A.3)

so
R^{(3)}-2\Lambda
in the ADM Hamiltonian becomes the potential
\tilde{U}
of the superspace Hamiltonian, while the deWitt supermetric
Q_{\alpha\beta\gamma\delta}
becomes the field space metric
\tilde{g}_{\mu\nu}
. It should be noted however that this identification, although unambiguous, cannot be made much more explicit. It is really just an identification. This is the reason for which exact calculations on the side of ADM or of the superspace formulation cannot easily be compared to one another.

An example illustrating this is the proof that the CVH algebra always exists when
R^{(3)}=0
. This proof is possible in the ADM language, but not in the superspace formulation. In order to see this, we assume that the vector constraint has been solved by the requirement of homogeneity, although here we keep working in a mixed framework where we keep all the spatial integrals explicit (this is because there might be a spatial dependency in the frames
e^{i}_{\alpha}(x)
introduced in (
2.1
), although these are not the dynamical variables of the homogeneous theory). The Poisson bracket is
\big\{q_{\alpha\beta}(x),p^{\gamma\delta}(y)\big\}=\delta^{\gamma}_{(\alpha}\delta^{\delta}_{\beta)}\delta^{d}(x,y)
. In order to obtain the CVH algebra and compute Poisson brackets, we define the smeared Hamiltonian and a volume variable by

\displaystyle H=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,N{\mathcal{H}}\,,\qquad\qquad V=\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,q\,,

(A.4)

and we choose the lapse to be
N=1/\sqrt{q}
. With this choice of lapse it is then immediate to compute

\displaystyle C\coloneqq\big\{V,H\big\}=-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x\,p^{\alpha\beta}q_{\alpha\beta}\,,

(A.5)

and to show that we have the closed brackets

\displaystyle\big\{V,C\big\}=-18V\,,\qquad\qquad\big\{C,H\big\}=-3H+6\Lambda\int_{\Sigma}\mathrm{d}^{3}x,

(A.6)

where in the last bracket the integral (over a finite region with fiducial cut-offs) produces a numerical factor
L_{0}
which plays the role of shift in the Hamiltonian when defining
{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}=H-L_{0}
.

As announced, this simple calculation shows that it is always possible to chose the lapse so as to obtain a closed algebra between
(C,V,{\mathcal{Q}}_{0})
when
R^{(3)}=0
. However, this calculation carried out in the ADM formulation cannot be reproduced in the field space formulation since there it is not possible to write an analogue of
q=\det(q_{\alpha\beta})
. Of course, for any minisuperspace model for which
R^{(3)}=0
the field space calculation will also lead to an
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
algebra formed by
(C,V,{\mathcal{Q}}_{0})
, and we are guaranteed by the above ADM calculation that this will
always
work, but this has to be computed on a case by case basis.

Triad decomposition

The viewpoint we have taken in section
2.1
is to define a minisuperspace as a manifold sliced in such a way that the line element separates into a temporal (i.e. orthogonal to the slice) and spatial (i.e. tangential to the slice) dependence as in (
2.1
). This implies that the trace of the extrinsic curvature and the ADM kinetic term depend only on the internal metric
\gamma_{ij}

\displaystyle{\mathcal{H}}^{\alpha}

\displaystyle=2D_{\beta}\left(Kq^{\alpha\beta}-K^{\alpha\beta}\right)

Properties of homothetic Killing vectors

In the main text we have used some properties of the conformal Killing vectors, such as the fact that they are solutions to the geodesic deviation equation. We give here a proof of this statement as well as other properties of the conformal Killing vectors.

Given an invertible metric
g_{\mu\nu}
, we recall that the homothetic Killing vectors are defined by the property

\displaystyle\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}+\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\mu}\coloneqq 2\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}=\lambda g_{\mu\nu}\,,\qquad\qquad\lambda=\mathrm{const}.

(C.1)

We then have the following result:

Theorem 1
.

Any homothetic killing vector
\xi
is a solution of the geodesic deviation equation
[
69
]
:

\displaystyle p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\rho}=-R_{\rho\mu\sigma\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\xi^{\sigma}\,,

(C.2)

where
p^{\mu}
is the tangent vector to a geodesic (i.e. a curve describing a solution of the equations of motion). This vector satisfies the property
p^{\mu}\nabla_{\mu}p^{\nu}=0
.

Proof.

We start from the definition of the conformal Killing vectors to get

\displaystyle p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\rho}

\displaystyle=-p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\rho}\xi_{\nu}+\lambda p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\cancel{\nabla_{\mu}g_{\nu\rho}}

(C.3)

\displaystyle=-p^{\mu}p^{\nu}R_{\nu\sigma\mu\rho}\xi^{\sigma}+p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}\,,

(C.4)

where the second line is obtained from the definition of the Riemann tensor as a commutator of covariant derivatives. The last term in this expression is now vanishing when
\xi
is conformal with constant
\lambda
since it gives

\displaystyle p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{\nu}=p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{(\mu}\xi_{\nu)}=\lambda p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\rho}g_{\mu\nu}=0\,.

(C.5)

This therefore proves the above statement using the following properties of the Riemann tensor:

\displaystyle p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{\rho}=-p^{\mu}p^{\nu}R_{\nu\sigma\mu\rho}\xi^{\sigma}=-R_{\rho\mu\sigma\nu}p^{\mu}p^{\nu}\xi^{\sigma}\,.

(C.6)

∎

We now give a few properties of the homothetic Killing vectors and of the phase space functions
C_{(i)}
and
V_{(ij)}
constructed out of them. First, they form an algebra under the Lie bracket

\displaystyle[\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)}]

\displaystyle={c_{ij}}^{k}\xi_{(k)}\,,\qquad\qquad[\xi_{(i)},\xi_{(j)}]^{\mu}\coloneqq\xi_{(i)}^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{(j)}^{\mu}-\xi_{(j)}^{\nu}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{(i)}^{\mu}\,,

(C.7)

and the commutator of two vectors is actually a Killing vector, as one can check explicitly by computing

\displaystyle{\mathcal{L}}_{[i,j]}g_{\mu\nu}

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mu}(\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(j)\,\nu}-\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(i)\,\nu})+\left(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(j)\,\nu}+\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(j)\,\nu}-\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(i)\,\nu}+\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(i)\,\nu}+\left(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu\right)

\displaystyle=\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(i)\,\nu}\lambda_{(j)}-\cancel{\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{(j)\,\sigma}}+\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(j)\,\nu}-\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(j)\,\nu}\lambda_{(i)}

(C.8)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\ }+\cancel{\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{(i)\,\sigma}}+\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{(i)\,\nu}+\left(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu\right)

\displaystyle=\cancel{g_{\mu\nu}\lambda_{(i)}\lambda_{(j)}}+\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}R_{\nu\rho\mu\sigma}\xi_{(j)}^{\rho}-\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(j)\,\nu}-\cancel{g_{\mu\nu}\lambda_{(j)}\lambda_{(i)}}

(C.9)

\displaystyle\phantom{=\ }-\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}R_{\nu\rho\mu\sigma}\xi_{(i)}^{\rho}+\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}\nabla_{\mu}\xi_{(i)\,\nu}+\left(\mu\leftrightarrow\nu\right)

\displaystyle=\xi_{(i)}^{\rho}\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}(R_{\nu\mu\sigma\rho}+R_{\mu\nu\sigma\rho})-\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}(g_{\mu\nu})\lambda_{(j)}+\xi_{(j)}^{\sigma}\nabla_{\sigma}(g_{\mu\nu})\lambda_{(i)}

(C.10)

\displaystyle=0\,.

(C.11)

Here we have used (
C.1
) when going from the second to the third line, and eliminated the antisymmetric terms in
\mu\,,\nu
. We have then used (
C.1
) once again as well as the definition of the Riemannn tensor, and finally concluded by using the antisymmetry of the Riemann tensor. This result implies that the structure constants of the algebra of homothetic Killing vectors satisfy

\displaystyle{c_{ij}}^{k}\lambda_{(k)}=0\,.

(C.12)

Let us now consider the phase space functions
V_{(ij)}=g_{\mu\nu}\xi^{\mu}_{(i)}\xi^{\nu}_{(j)}
and
C_{(i)}=p_{\mu}\xi^{\mu}_{(i)}

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}^{3}}{\mathrm{d}t^{3}}V_{(ij)}

\displaystyle=p^{\mu}p^{\nu}p^{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}\nabla_{\nu}\nabla_{\rho}\left(\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}\xi_{(j)\sigma}\right)

\displaystyle=2p^{\mu}p^{\nu}p^{\rho}\nabla_{\mu}\left((\nabla_{\nu}\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma})(\nabla_{\rho}\xi_{(j)\sigma})-\xi_{(i)}^{\sigma}R_{\sigma\nu\kappa\rho}\xi_{(j)}^{\kappa})\right)

We can then use these changes of coordinates in the expression of the generators to obtain the algebra
{\mathcal{A}}
in terms of the scale factors and their momenta.

For the other models it is not possible to obtain a flat field space geometry by choosing the lapse to be the inverse of the potential. However, looking at the potentials for the models VIII and IX, we see that the two monomials are the same and only differ by a sign. Furthermore, they satisfy the condition (
3.47
). In these two cases we have two possible choices of lapse which lead to the
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
subalgebra of
{\mathcal{A}}
. The first choice of lapse and coordinates is

\displaystyle N=\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}}{2Ga}\,,\qquad\qquad u=2a^{2}b\,,\qquad\qquad v=2b\,,

(D.24)

and it leads to the charges

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{d}}=\frac{bb_{p}}{2}\,,\qquad\qquad V_{3}=8a^{2}b^{2}\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}=\frac{p_{a}(ap_{a}-2bp_{b})}{16ab^{2}}+\frac{{\mathcal{V}}_{0}a^{2}}{4L_{s}^{2}G^{2}b^{2}}\,.

(D.25)

The second choice of lapse and coordinates is

\displaystyle N=-\frac{2{\mathcal{V}}_{0}b^{3}}{Ga^{3}}\,,\qquad\qquad u=\dfrac{a^{4}b^{2}}{2}\,,\qquad\qquad v=\dfrac{1}{2b^{2}}\,,

(D.26)

and it leads to the charges

\displaystyle C_{\mathrm{d}}=\frac{bb_{p}}{2}\,,\qquad\qquad V_{3}=\frac{a^{4}}{2}\,,\qquad\qquad{\mathcal{Q}}_{0}=-\frac{p_{a}(ap_{a}-2bp_{b})}{4a^{3}}\mp\frac{4{\mathcal{V}}_{0}b^{2}}{L_{s}^{2}G^{2}a^{2}}\,,

(D.27)

where the
(-)
sign is for Bianchi VIII and the sign
(+)

3.47
) is not satisfied for the Bianchi models IV and VII, meaning that they do not exhibit the
{\mathfrak{sl}}(2,{\mathbb{R}})
