†

Abstract




The upper-crossing/solution (US) algorithm for root-finding with strongly stable convergence

Abstract
. In this paper, we propose a new and broadly applicable root–finding method, called as the
upper-crossing/solution
(US) algorithm, which belongs to the category of non-bracketing (or open domain) methods. The US algorithm is a general principle for iteratively seeking the unique root
\theta^{*}
of a non-linear equation
g(\theta)=0
and its each iteration consists of two steps: an upper-crossing step (
U-step
) and a solution step (
S-step
), where the
U-step
finds an upper-crossing function or a
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
[whose form depends on
\theta^{(t)}
being the
t
-th iteration of
\theta^{*}
] based on a new notion of so-called changing direction inequality, and the
S-step
solves the simple
U
-equation
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
to obtain its explicit solution
\theta^{(t+1)}
. The US algorithm holds two major advantages: (i) It strongly stably converges to the root
\theta^{*}
; and (ii) it does not depend on any initial values, in contrast to Newton’s method. The key step for applying the US algorithm is to construct one simple
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
such that an explicit solution to the
U
-equation
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
is available. Based on the first–, second– and third–derivative of
g(\theta)
, three methods are given for constructing such
U
-functions. We show various applications of the US algorithm in such as calculating quantile in continuous distributions, calculating exact
p
-values for skew null distributions, and finding maximum likelihood estimates of parameters in a class of continuous/discrete distributions. The analysis of the convergence rate of the US algorithm and some numerical experiments are also provided. Especially, because of the property of strongly stable convergence, the US algorithm could be one of the powerful tools for solving an equation with multiple roots.

Keywords
: Changing direction inequality; Multiple roots; Non-bracketing methods; Strongly stable convergence; US algorithm.

1
. Introduction

Solving roots of an equation or zeros of a function is the most fundamental problem in computational mathematics. Many problems in science and engineering
can be formulated as: Given a continuous function
g(x)
of a single variable defined in the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
, find the value
x^{*}\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
g(x^{*})=0
. These problems are usually called root finding problems. Calculating the
maximum likelihood estimate
(MLE) of a parameter in classical statistics and computing the posterior mode in Bayesian statistics can be viewed as finding a solution to a non-linear equation. Let
g(\theta)
be a non-linear function of a single variable and there exist a unique root
\theta^{*}
to the following equation:

\displaystyle g(\theta)=0,\quad\theta\in\Theta\subseteq{\mathbb{R}},

(1.1)

where
\Theta
is the parameter space or an interval in the real line
{\mathbb{R}}
.

There exist many methods for seeking the root of (
1.1
) in numerical analysis and they are roughly categorized into two classes:

(i)

Closed domain (bracketing) methods, for example, the bisection method and the false position method (Wood 1992, Chabert 1999 p.83–112).

(ii)

Open domain (non-bracketing) methods such as Newton’s method, the secant method, and Muller’s method (Wolfe 1959, Boyd & Vandenberghe 2004 p.484–496, Costabile
et al
. 2006).

The bisection method (Wood 1992) can guarantee to obtain a solution since the root is trapped in the closed interval, while it converges slowly since the information about actual functions behavior is not utilized. By using information about the function
g(\theta)
, the false position method (Chabert 1999) generally converges more rapidly than the bisection method, however it does not give a bound on the error of the root. Non-bracketing methods are not as robust as bracketing methods and may actually diverge. However, they use information about the function itself to refine the approximations of the root. Thus, they are considerably more efficient than bracketing ones.

It is well known that Newton’s method or the
Newton–Raphson
(NR) algorithm is a golden standard for seeking the root of (
1.1
) with the following NR iteration:

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\frac{g(\theta^{(t)})}{g^{\prime}(\theta^{(t)})},

(1.2)

where
\theta^{(t)}
denotes the
t
-th iteration/approximation of the root
\theta^{*}
and
g^{\prime}(\theta)
is the first-order derivative of
g(\theta)
. Although the NR algorithm converges quadratically, it is sensitive to initial values (Lindstrom & Bates 1988). This property hinders its applications in repeated use of the NR algorithm such as in bootstrapping calculation of the standard deviation of an MLE. One advantage of Newton’s method is that it is an optimization technique as well as an root–finding technique. In computational statistics, we know that the
expectation–maximization
(EM) algorithm (Dempster
et al
. 1977) and
minorization–maximization
(MM) algorithm (Lange
et al
. 2000, Hunter & Lange 2004) are two popular optimization tools for calculating MLEs of parameters with monotonic convergence, rather than two root–finding tools.

To overcome the drawback of sensitiveness to initial values associated with the NR algorithm and to retain a similar property to the monotonic convergence associated with the EM/MM algorithms, we in this paper propose a new root–finding method, called as the
upper-crossing/solution
(US) algorithm, which belongs to the category of open domain methods. The US algorithm is a general principle for iteratively seeking the root
\theta^{*}
of equation (
1.1
) with strongly stable convergence (its definition will be given in the beginning of Section 2), and its each iteration consists of an upper-crossing step (
U-step
) and a solution step (
S-step
), where

U-step
:

Find a
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
satisfying (
2.5
);

S-step
:

Solve the
U
-equation to obtain its root
\theta^{(t+1)}
as showed by (
2.6
).

The two-step process is repeated until convergence occurs.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section 2, first we define two new relation symbols on the changing direction inequality, and the concept of weakly/strongly stable convergence. Next we introduce new notions such as upper-crossing function or
U
-function and
U
-equation. Finally each US iteration with two steps (i.e.,
U-step
and
S-step
) is formulated and the strongly stable convergence of the US algorithm is proved. Three methods for constructing
U
-functions are given in Section 3. Section 4 presents various applications such as calculating quantile in continuous distributions, calculating exact
p
-values for skew null distributions, and finding MLEs of parameters in a class of continuous/discrete distributions. Section 5 provides the analysis of the convergence rate of the US algorithm. Numerical experiments and some discussions are given in Sections 6–7. More technical details are put in three Appendices. Here we first present one simple example to give the flavour of the US algorithm.

2
. The US algorithm with strongly stable convergence

First, we define two new relation symbols on the
changing direction
(CD) inequalities, “
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(a)}}{{\geqslant}}
” and “
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(a)}}{{\leqslant}}
”, as follows: For two functions
h_{1}(x)
and
h_{2}(x)
with the same domain
{\mathbb{X}}
,

h_{1}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(a)}}{{\geqslant}}h_{2}(x)\mbox{ means}\left\{\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}h_{1}(x)\geqslant h_{2}(x),&\mbox{if }\ a>0,\\[5.69054pt]
h_{1}(x)=h_{2}(x),&\mbox{if }\ a=0,\\[5.69054pt]
h_{1}(x)\leqslant h_{2}(x),&\mbox{if }\ a<0,\end{array}\right.

and

h_{1}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(a)}}{{\leqslant}}h_{2}(x)\mbox{ means}\left\{\!\!\begin{array}[]{ll}h_{1}(x)\leqslant h_{2}(x),&\mbox{if }\ a>0,\\[5.69054pt]
h_{1}(x)=h_{2}(x),&\mbox{if }\ a=0,\\[5.69054pt]
h_{1}(x)\geqslant h_{2}(x),&\mbox{if }\ a<0.\end{array}\right.

For example,
x^{2n-1}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(x)}}{{\geqslant}}0
for any positive integer
n
;
\sin(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(x-\pi)}}{{\geqslant}}0
for any
x\in(0,2\pi)
; and
\cos(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(x-\pi/2)}}{{\leqslant}}0
for any
x\in(0,\pi)
are three illustrative examples.

Next, a sequence
\{\theta^{(t)}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is called to weakly stably converge to a fixed point
\theta^{*}
, if

|\theta^{(t+1)}-\theta^{*}|<|\theta^{(t)}-\theta^{*}|,\qquad\forall\;t=0,1,\ldots,\infty.

A sequence
\{\theta^{(t)}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
is called to strongly stably converge to a fixed point
\theta^{*}
, if

\theta^{(0)}<\theta^{(1)}<\cdots<\theta^{(t)}<\cdots\leqslant\theta^{*}\quad\quad\mbox{or}\quad\quad\theta^{*}\leqslant\cdots<\theta^{(t)}<\cdots<\theta^{(1)}<\theta^{(0)}.

An algorithm is said to possess
weakly (or strongly) stable convergence
if the sequence
\{\theta^{(t)}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
generated by this algorithm weakly (or strongly) stably converges to a fixed point
\theta^{*}
. It is clear that a strongly stable convergence implies a weakly stable convergence.

2.1
Definition of a
U
-function

Suppose that directly solving the unique root
\theta^{*}
of the non-linear equation (
1.1
) is very difficult. Without loss of generality, we assume that

\displaystyle g(\theta)>0,\quad\forall\;\theta<\theta^{*}\quad\mbox{and}\quad g(\theta)<0,\quad\forall\;\theta>\theta^{*}.

(2.1)

If
g(\theta)<0
when
\theta<\theta^{*}
, then we can multiplies
-1
on both sides of (
1.1
) and obtain a new equation
-g(\theta)=0
, which satisfies the assumption (
2.1
) and has the same root
\theta^{*}
.

The idea of the US algorithm is as follows. Let
\theta^{(t)}
be the
t
-th iteration of
\theta^{*}
and
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
be a real-valued function of
\theta
whose form depends on
\theta^{(t)}
. The function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
is called a
U
-function for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
if the following three conditions are satisfied:

\displaystyle g(\theta)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)}),\qquad\mbox{if }\ \;\theta<\theta^{(t)},

(2.2)

\displaystyle g(\theta^{(t)})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle U(\theta^{(t)}|\theta^{(t)})\hskip 18.49411pt\mbox{if }\ \;\theta=\theta^{(t)},

(2.3)

\displaystyle g(\theta)

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)}),\qquad\mbox{if }\ \;\theta>\theta^{(t)}.

(2.4)

The three conditions can be equivalently rewritten as

\displaystyle g(\theta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})}}{{\geqslant}}U(\theta|\theta^{(t)}),\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta.

(2.5)

The conditions (
2.2
)–(
2.3
) indicate that the
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
function majorizes the objective function
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
for all
\theta\leqslant\theta^{(t)}
, and the conditions
(
2.4
)–(
2.3
) show that the
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
function minorizes
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
for all
\theta\geqslant\theta^{(t)}
. The geometric interpretations of the objective function
g(\theta)
(solid curve) and the
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
(dotted curve) are showed in Figure 1.

The assumption (
2.1
) implies
0\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{*})}}{{\geqslant}}g(\theta)
for all
\theta,\theta^{*}\in\Theta
. By comparing this CD inequality with (
2.5
), we conclude that
g(\theta)
is a
U
-function for 0 at
\theta=\theta^{*}
. Other properties on
U
-functions are listed in Property 1 below.

Property 1

(Basic properties on
U
-functions)
.

(a)

Let
g(\theta)
be defined in
\Theta
, then
g(\theta)
is a
U
-function for itself at any inner point in
\Theta
;

(b)

If
U_{1}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
and
U_{2}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
are two
U
-functions for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
, then
[U_{1}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})+U_{2}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})]/2
is a
U
-function for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
;

(c)

For two functions
g_{1}(\theta)
and
g_{2}(\theta)
defined in
\Theta
, if
U_{1}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
is a
U
-function for
g_{1}(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
, then
U_{1}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})+g_{2}(\theta)
is a
U
-function for
g_{1}(\theta)+g_{2}(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
.
\|

2.2
The
U
-equation and the US algorithm

If a
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
function satisfying (
2.5
) could be found, then one need only solve the simple
U-equation
:
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
to obtain its solution
\theta^{(t+1)}
, denoted by

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}=\mbox{sol}\,\{U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta\},

(2.6)

which defines a
US algorithm
. Therefore, the US algorithm is a general principle for iteratively seeking the root
\theta^{*}
with strongly stable convergence, and its each iteration consists of an upper-crossing step (
U-step
) and a solution step (
S-step
), where

U-step
:

Find a
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
satisfying (
2.5
);

S-step
:

Solve the
U
-equation to obtain its root
\theta^{(t+1)}
as showed by (
2.6
).

The two-step process is repeated until convergence occurs.

In general, the solution
\theta^{(t+1)}
in (
2.6
) has an explicit expression. Usually, the
U
-equation
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
could be any equation with analytic solution, e.g., a quadratic equation, even a linear equation. Therefore, the US principle is iteratively solving a sequence of tractable surrogate equations
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
instead of solving the intractable original non-linear equation
g(\theta)=0
.

Theorem 1 below states that the US algorithm holds two characteristics: (i) It strongly stably converges to the root
\theta^{*}
; (ii) It does not depend on any initial values in
\Theta
, in contrast to Newton’s method. Its proof is put in Appendix A.

Theorem 1

(Strongly stable convergence)
. Let
\theta^{*}
be the unique root of the equation (
1.1
), and
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
be one
U
-function for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
in
\Theta
. Then, for any initial value
\theta^{(0)}
in
\Theta
, the sequence
\{\theta^{(t+1)}\}_{t=0}^{\infty}
specified by (
2.6
) strongly stably converges to
\theta^{*}
.
\|

3
. Three methods for constructing
U
-functions

For the same objective function
g(\theta)
, it is possible that there exist several
U
-functions. In other words, for the equation (
1.1
), it may exist more than one US algorithm. We know that the key for a US algorithm is how to find a
U
-function. In this section, we propose three general methods to construct
U
-functions based on the objective function
g(\theta)
.

3.1
The FLB function method

Suppose the first–order derivative
g^{\prime}(\cdot)
exists and it is bounded by some function
b(\cdot)
; i.e.,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\theta)\geqslant b(\theta),\quad\forall\;\theta\in\Theta,

(3.1)

where
b(\theta)
is called the
first-derivative lower bound
(FLB) function. It is easy to verify that

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z,\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta,

(3.2)

is a
U
-function for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
. In fact, we have

\displaystyle g(\theta)-U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle[g(\theta)-g(\theta^{(t)})]-\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z\;=\;\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}g^{\prime}(z)\,\mbox{d}z-\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}[g^{\prime}(z)-b(z)]\,\mbox{d}z\;\left\{\begin{array}[]{l}\leqslant 0,\quad\mbox{if }\ \theta<\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.2}),\\[11.38109pt]
=0,\quad\mbox{if }\ \theta=\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.3}),\\[11.38109pt]
\geqslant 0,\quad\mbox{if }\ \theta>\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.4})\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})}}{{\geqslant}}

\displaystyle 0,\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta,

where we have used the result
g^{\prime}(z)-b(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.1})}}{{\geqslant}}0
for all
z\in\Theta
in the above CD inequality.

3.1.1
Two special cases of the FLB function
b(\theta)

Let
b(\theta)=b_{1}+b_{2}\theta
be a linear function. Then the
U
-function defined by (
3.2
) becomes a quadratic function

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=\frac{1}{2}b_{2}\theta^{2}+b_{1}\theta+c^{(t)}_{1}\quad\mbox{with}\quad c^{(t)}_{1}\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})-\frac{1}{2}b_{2}\theta^{(t)2}-b_{1}\theta^{(t)},

(3.4)

if
b_{2}\neq 0
. From (
2.6
), the
U
-equation
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
has two solutions

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}=\frac{-b_{1}\pm\sqrt{b_{1}^{2}-2b_{2}c^{(t)}_{1}}}{b_{2}},

(3.5)

but only one is the desired US iteration.

In particular, if
b_{2}=0
(i.e.,
g^{\prime}(\theta)\geqslant b_{1}
for all
\theta\in\Theta
, where
b_{1}
is called the FLB constant), then (
3.4
) becomes to a linear function

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=g(\theta^{(t)})+b_{1}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})

(3.6)

and the corresponding US iteration is given by

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}=\theta^{(t)}-\frac{g(\theta^{(t)})}{b_{1}},\quad b_{1}\neq 0,

(3.7)

which can be viewed as the NR iteration (
1.2
) by replacing
g^{\prime}(\theta^{(t)})
with
b_{1}
.

3.1.2
Motivation for constructing the
U
-function (
3.2
)

Under the assumption (
3.1
), the key for constructing the US algorithm is how to obtain
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
function specified by (
3.2
). Our motivation is from the mean value theorem (i.e., the first–order Taylor expansion). It states that there exists a point
z^{*}
between
\theta
and
\theta^{(t)}
such that

\displaystyle g(\theta)-g(\theta^{(t)})=(\theta-\theta^{(t)})g^{\prime}(z^{*})=\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}g^{\prime}(z)\,\mbox{d}z.

(3.8)

Rewriting (
3.8
), we obtain

\displaystyle g(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}g^{\prime}(z)\,\mbox{d}z

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})}}{{\geqslant}}

\displaystyle g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z\;\triangleq\;U(\theta|\theta^{(t)}),

where the result
g^{\prime}(z)-b(z)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.1})}}{{\geqslant}}0
is used in the above CD inequality.

3.2
The SLUB constants method

Suppose that
g^{\prime\prime}(\cdot)
exists and there are two constants
\{b_{21},b_{22}\}
(which are called the
second-derivative lower–upper bound
(SLUB) constants) such that

\displaystyle b_{21}\leqslant g^{\prime\prime}(\theta)\leqslant b_{22},\quad\forall\;\theta\in\Theta.

(3.9)

Let
I(\cdot)
denote the indicator function and define a step constant

\displaystyle b_{2}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})\triangleq b_{22}\cdot I(\theta\leqslant\theta^{(t)})+b_{21}\cdot I(\theta>\theta^{(t)}).

(3.10)

It is easy to verify that the quadratic function

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})+g^{\prime}(\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})+\frac{1}{2}b_{2}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2},\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta,

(3.11)

is a
U
-function for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
.

Note that the second–order Taylor’s expansion

g(\theta)=g(\theta^{(t)})+g^{\prime}(\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2},\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta,

where
\tilde{\theta}
lies between
\theta
and
\theta^{(t)}
, we only need to prove that
b_{2}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2}
is a
U
-function for
g^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2}
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
. In fact, by using (
3.9
) and (
3.10
), we have

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\leqslant b_{22}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2},&\mbox{if }\ \theta<\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.2}),\\[5.69054pt]
=0,&\mbox{if }\ \theta=\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.3}),\\[5.69054pt]
\geqslant b_{21}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2},&\mbox{if }\ \theta>\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.4})\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})}}{{\geqslant}}

\displaystyle b_{2}(\theta|\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2},\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta.

3.3
The TLB constant method

Suppose that
g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta)
exists and is bounded by some constant
b_{3}
; i.e.,

\displaystyle g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta)\geqslant b_{3},\quad\forall\;\theta\in\Theta,

(3.13)

where
b_{3}
is called the
third-derivative lower bound
(TLB) constant. It is easy to verify that the cubic function (if
b_{3}\neq 0
)

\displaystyle U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle g(\theta^{(t)})+g^{\prime}(\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2}

(3.14)

\displaystyle+\;\frac{1}{6}b_{3}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{3},\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta,

is a
U
-function for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
. If
b_{3}=0
, then (
3.14
) becomes a quadratic function.

In fact, by applying the third–order Taylor expansion

\displaystyle g(\theta)=g(\theta^{(t)})+g^{\prime}(\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})+\frac{1}{2}g^{\prime\prime}(\theta^{(t)})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{2}+\frac{1}{6}g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{3},

(3.15)

where
\tilde{\theta}
lies between
\theta
and
\theta^{(t)}
, we obtain

\displaystyle g(\theta)-U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.12})\ \&\ (\ref{eqnfive3.13})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{(\theta-\theta^{(t)})^{3}}{6}[g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})-b_{3}]\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\leqslant 0,&\mbox{if }\ \theta<\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.2}),\\[5.69054pt]
=0,&\mbox{if }\ \theta=\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.3}),\\[5.69054pt]
\geqslant 0,&\mbox{if }\ \theta>\theta^{(t)},\mbox{ indicating }(\ref{eqnfive2.4})\end{array}\right.

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})}}{{\geqslant}}

\displaystyle 0,\quad\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}\in\Theta,

where we have used the result
g^{\prime\prime\prime}(\theta)-b_{3}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.11})}}{{\geqslant}}0
for all
\theta\in\Theta
in the above CD inequality.

4
. Applications

4.1
Calculation of quantile in continuous distributions

Let
f(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
and
F(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
respectively denote the pdf and cdf of a population distribution. Given
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and a real number
p\in(0,1)
, the
p
-th quantile
\xi_{p}
of the distribution can be calculated as

\xi_{p}=\mbox{sol}\,\Big\{g(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\triangleq p-F(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=0,\;\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{X}}\Big\},

where we need to solve an integral equation. In this subsection, we will demonstrate that the US algorithm can be employed to iteratively find the
p
-th quantile
\xi_{p}
.

Assume that
g^{\prime}(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\triangleq\,\mbox{d}g(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})/\,\mbox{d}x
is bounded by some function
b(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
; i.e.,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})=-f(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geqslant b(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}),\quad\forall\;x\in{\mathbb{X}}.

(4.1)

Then, from (
2.6
), the US iteration is to solve

\displaystyle x^{(t+1)}=\mbox{sol}\left\{U(x|x^{(t)},{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}g(x^{(t)}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})+\int_{x^{(t)}}^{x}b(z|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;x,x^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{X}}\right\}.

(4.2)

In this subsection, we will consider two cases: (i) The mode
x_{\rm mod}
of the density
f(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
exists; (ii) The mode
x_{\rm mod}
of
f(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
does not exist.

4.1.1
The mode of the density exists

Let
x_{\rm mod}
denote the mode of the density
f(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, then we have
-f(x|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})\geqslant-f(x_{\rm mod}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})
for all
x\in{\mathbb{X}}
. From
(
4.2
), the US iteration is to solve

\displaystyle x^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\Big\{g(x^{(t)}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-f(x_{\rm mod}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})(x-x^{(t)})=0,\;\forall\;x,x^{(t)}\in{\mathbb{X}}\Big\}

(4.3)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{(t)}-\frac{F(x^{(t)}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})-p}{f(x_{\rm mod}|{\boldsymbol{\theta}})}.

Example 2

(Quantile of the normal distribution)
. To find the
p
-th quantile of the normal distribution with mean
\mu
and variance
\sigma^{2}
, we denote its cdf and pdf by
\Phi(x|\mu,\sigma^{2})
and
\phi(x|\mu,\sigma^{2})=[1/(\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma)]\exp[-(x-\mu)^{2}/(2\sigma^{2})]
, respectively. Note that the mode of
\phi(x|\mu,\sigma^{2})
is given by
x_{\rm mod}=\mu
, then (
4.3
) becomes

\displaystyle x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\sqrt{2\pi}\sigma\Big[\Phi(x^{(t)}|\mu,\sigma^{2})-p\Big].

(4.4)

Example 3

(Quantile of the skew normal distribution)
. To find the
p
-th quantile of the skew normal distribution (Azzalini 1985) with pdf

\displaystyle f(x|\mu,\sigma^{2},\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{2}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp{\left[-\frac{(x-\mu)^{2}}{2\sigma^{2}}\right]}\Phi\left(\alpha\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right),

(4.5)

where
x,\mu\in{\mathbb{R}}
,
\sigma^{2}>0
,
\alpha\geqslant 0
and
\Phi(\cdot)
is the cdf of
N(0,1)
, in Appendix B, we proposed an MM algorithm to iteratively calculate the mode
x_{\rm mod}
of the skew normal density as shown in (
B.1
). From (
4.3
), the US iteration for calculating the
p
-th quantile
\xi_{p}
is

x^{(t+1)}=x^{(t)}-\frac{F(x^{(t)}|\mu,\sigma^{2},\alpha)-p}{f(x_{\rm mod}|\mu,\sigma^{2},\alpha)},

where
F(x|\mu,\sigma^{2},\alpha)
is the cdf of the skew normal distribution.

4.1.2
The mode of the density does not exist

Let
F(x|\alpha,\beta)
denote the cdf of the beta distribution
\mbox{Beta}(\alpha,\beta)
and its pdf is

\displaystyle f(x|\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B(\alpha,\beta)}x^{\alpha-1}(1-x)^{\beta-1},\quad x\in(0,1),\;\alpha>0,\;\beta>0.

(4.6)

Given
\alpha\in(0,1),\beta\in(0,1)
and
p\in(0,1)
, suppose that we want to calculate the
p
-th quantile of the distribution as
\xi_{p}=\mbox{sol}\,\big\{g(x|\alpha,\beta)\triangleq p-F(x|\alpha,\beta)=0,\;\forall\;x\in(0,1)\big\}
. We know that if at least one of
\alpha
and
\beta
is less than 1, the mode of the beta density
f(x|\alpha,\beta)
does not exist. Thus, the US iteration specified by (
4.3
) can not be used for such cases.

We employ (
4.1
)–(
4.2
) to calculate
\xi_{p}
. First, it is easy to verify the following inequality:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x(1-x)}\leqslant\frac{1}{2x^{2}}+\frac{1}{2(1-x)^{2}},\quad\forall\;x\in(0,1),

(4.7)

where the equality holds iff
x=1/2
. Next,

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(x|\alpha,\beta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-f(x|\alpha,\beta)=-f(x|\alpha+1,\beta+1)\frac{B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\frac{1}{x(1-x)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive4.7})}}{{\geqslant}}

\displaystyle-f(x|\alpha+1,\beta+1)\frac{B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}{B(\alpha,\beta)}\cdot\left[\frac{1}{2x^{2}}+\frac{1}{2(1-x)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle-f(x_{\rm mod,1}|\alpha+1,\beta+1)\frac{B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}{2B(\alpha,\beta)}\left[\frac{1}{x^{2}}+\frac{1}{(1-x)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive4.8})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle-\frac{a}{x^{2}}-\frac{a}{(1-x)^{2}}\;\triangleq\;b(x|\alpha,\beta),\quad\forall\;x\in(0,1),

where
x_{\rm mod,1}\triangleq\alpha/(\alpha+\beta)
denotes the mode of the beta density
f(x|\alpha+1,\beta+1)
and

\displaystyle a\triangleq f(x_{\rm mod,1}|\alpha+1,\beta+1)\frac{B(\alpha+1,\beta+1)}{2B(\alpha,\beta)}.

(4.8)

Finally, from (
4.2
), the US iteration for calculating the
p
-th quantile
\xi_{p}
is

\displaystyle x^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(x^{(t)}|\alpha,\beta)+\int_{x^{(t)}}^{x}b(z|\alpha,\beta)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;x,x^{(t)}\in(0,1)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(x^{(t)}|\alpha,\beta)+\frac{a}{x}-\frac{a}{1-x}-\frac{a}{x^{(t)}}+\frac{a}{1-x^{(t)}}=0,\;\forall\;x,x^{(t)}\in(0,1)\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{a_{1}^{(t)}x^{2}+a_{2}^{(t)}x+a=0,\;\forall\;x,x^{(t)}\in(0,1)\right\},

where
a_{1}^{(t)}\triangleq F(x^{(t)}|\alpha,\beta)-p+a/x^{(t)}-a/(1-x^{(t)})
and
a_{2}^{(t)}\triangleq-a_{1}^{(t)}-2a
.

4.2
Calculation of exact
p
-values for skew null distributions

4.2.1
The chi-squared null distribution

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}N(\mu,\sigma^{2})
with unknown
\{\mu,\sigma^{2}\}
and we want to test
H_{0}
:
\sigma^{2}=\sigma_{0}^{2}
against
H_{1}
:
\sigma^{2}\neq\sigma_{0}^{2}
. The test statistic is
\chi=\nu S^{2}/\sigma_{0}^{2}
with corresponding observed value
\chi_{\rm obs}\triangleq\nu s^{2}/\sigma_{0}^{2}
, where
S^{2}=(1/\nu)\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_{i}-\bar{X})^{2}
is the sample variance,
s^{2}
is its observed value and
\nu\;\hat{=}\;n-1
. Under
H_{0}
, we have
\chi\sim\chi^{2}(\nu)
, which is called chi-squared null distribution. We denote the pdf of
\chi^{2}(\nu)
by

h_{\nu}(x)=[2^{\nu/2}\Gamma(\nu/2)]^{-1}x^{\nu/2-1}\mbox{e}^{-x/2}
for
x>0
, whose mode is
\nu-2\triangleq m_{0}
.

(a) Case I:
\chi_{\rm obs}<m_{0}

The exact
p
-value can be calculated as
p\mbox{-value}=\Pr\{\chi^{2}(\nu)\leqslant\chi_{\rm obs}\}+\Pr\{\chi^{2}(\nu)\geqslant x_{u}\}
, where
x_{u}\,(>m_{0})
satisfies
h_{\nu}(x_{u})=h_{\nu}(\chi_{\rm obs})
; i.e.,
m_{0}\log(x_{u})-x_{u}=m_{0}\log(\chi_{\rm obs})-\chi_{\rm obs}\;\triangleq\;c_{0}
. Thus, finding
x_{u}
is equivalent to finding the root to the equation
g(x_{u})=c_{0}-m_{0}\log(x_{u})+x_{u}=0
. Since
g^{\prime}(x_{u})=-m_{0}/x_{u}+1>-m_{0}/x_{u}\;\triangleq\;b(x_{u})
, the US iteration for calculating
x_{u}
is

\displaystyle x_{u}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(x^{(t)}_{u})+\textstyle\int_{x^{(t)}_{u}}^{x_{u}}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;x_{u},x^{(t)}_{u}>m_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\Big\{g(x^{(t)}_{u})-m_{0}\log(x_{u})+m_{0}\log(x^{(t)}_{u})=0,\;\forall\;x_{u},x^{(t)}_{u}>m_{0}\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{(t)}_{u}\exp\left[g(x^{(t)}_{u})/m_{0}\right].

(b) Case II:
\chi_{\rm obs}>m_{0}

The exact
p
-value can be calculated as
p\mbox{-value}=\Pr\{\chi^{2}(\nu)\leqslant x_{l}\}+\Pr\{\chi^{2}(\nu)\geqslant\chi_{\rm obs}\}
, where
x_{l}\in(0,m_{0})
satisfies
h_{\nu}(x_{l})=h_{\nu}(\chi_{\rm obs})
; i.e.,
m_{0}\log(x_{l})-x_{l}=m_{0}\log(\chi_{\rm obs})-\chi_{\rm obs}\;\triangleq\;c_{0}
. Thus, finding
x_{l}
is equivalent to finding the root to the equation
g(x_{l})=m_{0}\log(x_{l})-x_{l}-c_{0}=0
. Since
g^{\prime}(x_{l})=m_{0}/x_{l}-1>-1\;\triangleq\;b(x_{l})
, the US iteration for calculating
x_{l}
is

\displaystyle x_{l}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(x^{(t)}_{l})+\textstyle\int_{x^{(t)}_{l}}^{x_{l}}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;x_{l},x^{(t)}_{l}\in(0,m_{0})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\Big\{g(x^{(t)}_{l})-x_{l}+x^{(t)}_{l}=0,\;\forall\;x_{l},x^{(t)}_{l}\in(0,m_{0})\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle g(x^{(t)}_{l})+x^{(t)}_{l}=m_{0}\log\big(x^{(t)}_{l}/\chi_{\rm obs}\big)+\chi_{\rm obs}.

4.2.2
The
F
null distribution

Let
\{X_{ij}\}_{j=1}^{n_{i}}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}N(\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2})
for
i=1,2
, and the two random samples be independent, where
\{\mu_{i},\sigma_{i}^{2}\}
are unknown. We want to test
H_{0}
:
\sigma_{1}^{2}=\sigma_{2}^{2}
against
H_{1}
:
\sigma_{1}^{2}\neq\sigma_{2}^{2}
, and the test statistic is
F=S_{1}^{2}/S_{2}^{2}
with corresponding observed value
F_{\rm obs}\triangleq s_{1}^{2}/s_{2}^{2}
, where
S_{i}^{2}
is the
i
-th sample variance,
s_{i}^{2}
is its observed value and
\nu_{i}\triangleq n_{i}-1
. Under
H_{0}
, we have
F\sim F(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
, which is called
F
null distribution. We denote the pdf of
F(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})
by

h_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)=\frac{\Gamma(\nu_{12}/2)\nu^{\nu_{1}/2}}{\Gamma(\nu_{1}/2)\Gamma(\nu_{2}/2)}x^{\nu_{1}/2-1}(1+\nu x)^{-\nu_{12}/2},\quad x>0,\;\;\nu\triangleq\frac{\nu_{1}}{\nu_{2}},\;\;\nu_{12}\triangleq\nu_{1}+\nu_{2}.

The mode of
h_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x)
is
m_{1}\nu_{2}/[\nu_{1}(\nu_{2}+2)]\triangleq m_{0}
, where
m_{1}\triangleq\nu_{1}-2
.

(a) Case I:
F_{\rm obs}<m_{0}

The exact
p
-value can be calculated as
p\mbox{-value}=\Pr\{F(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\leqslant F_{\rm obs}\}+\Pr\{F(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\geqslant x_{u}\}
, where
x_{u}\,(>m_{0})
satisfies
h_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x_{u})=h_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(F_{\rm obs})
; i.e.,

m_{1}\log(x_{u})-\nu_{12}\log(1+\nu x_{u})=m_{1}\log(F_{\rm obs})-\nu_{12}\log(1+\nu F_{\rm obs})\triangleq c_{0}
. Thus, finding
x_{u}
is equivalent to finding the root to the equation
g(x_{u})=c_{0}-m_{1}\log(x_{u})+\nu_{12}\log(1+\nu x_{u})=0
. Since
g^{\prime}(x_{u})=-m_{1}/x_{u}+\nu_{12}\nu/(1+\nu x_{u})>-m_{1}/x_{u}\;\triangleq\;b(x_{u})
, the US iteration for calculating
x_{u}
is

\displaystyle x_{u}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(x^{(t)}_{u})+\textstyle\int_{x^{(t)}_{u}}^{x_{u}}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;x_{u},x^{(t)}_{u}>m_{0}\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\Big\{g(x^{(t)}_{u})-m_{1}\log(x_{u})+m_{1}\log(x^{(t)}_{u})=0,\;\forall\;x_{u},x^{(t)}_{u}>m_{0}\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{(t)}_{u}\exp[g(x^{(t)}_{u})/m_{1}].

(b) Case II:
F_{\rm obs}>m_{0}

The exact
p
-value can be calculated as
p\mbox{-value}=\Pr\{F(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\leqslant x_{l}\}+\Pr\{F(\nu_{1},\nu_{2})\geqslant F_{\rm obs}\}
, where
x_{l}\in(0,m_{0})
satisfies
h_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(x_{l})=h_{\nu_{1},\nu_{2}}(F_{\rm obs})
; i.e.,

m_{1}\log(x_{l})-\nu_{12}\log(1+\nu x_{l})=m_{1}\log(F_{\rm obs})-\nu_{12}\log(1+\nu F_{\rm obs})\triangleq c_{0}
. Thus, finding
x_{l}
is equivalent to finding the root to the equation
g(x_{l})=m_{1}\log(x_{l})-\nu_{12}\log(1+\nu x_{l})-c_{0}=0
. Since
g^{\prime}(x_{l})=m_{1}/x_{l}-\nu_{12}\nu/(1+\nu x_{l})>m_{1}/x_{l}-\nu_{12}/x_{l}=-(2+\nu_{2})/x_{l}\;\triangleq\;b(x_{l})
, the US iteration for calculating
x_{l}
is

\displaystyle x_{l}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(x^{(t)}_{l})+\textstyle\int_{x^{(t)}_{l}}^{x_{l}}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;x_{l},x^{(t)}_{l}\in(0,m_{0})\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\Big\{g(x^{(t)}_{l})-(2+\nu_{2})\log(x_{l}/x^{(t)}_{l})=0,\;\forall\;x_{l},x^{(t)}_{l}\in(0,m_{0})\Big\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle x^{(t)}_{l}\exp[g(x^{(t)}_{l})/(2+\nu_{2})].

4.3
MLEs of parameters in a class of continuous distributions

4.3.1
MLE of
\alpha
in gamma distribution

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
with pdf
\beta^{\alpha}x^{\alpha-1}\mbox{e}^{-\beta x}/\Gamma(\alpha)
,
x>0
, where
\{\alpha,\beta\}
are two positive parameters. The log-likelihood function
\ell(\alpha,\beta)=nG({\boldsymbol{x}})(\alpha-1)-n\bar{x}\beta+n\left[\alpha\log\beta-\log\Gamma(\alpha)\right]
,
where
G({\boldsymbol{x}})=G(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\triangleq(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})
and
\bar{x}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
. Let

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial\ell(\alpha,\beta)}{\partial\alpha}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle nG({\boldsymbol{x}})+n\log(\beta)-n\psi(\alpha),

(4.9)

\displaystyle 0=\frac{\partial\ell(\alpha,\beta)}{\partial\beta}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-n\bar{x}+\frac{n\alpha}{\beta}.

(4.10)

Given
\alpha
, from (
4.10
), we have
\beta=\alpha/\bar{x}
. Given
\beta
, from (
4.9
), the MLE of
\alpha
is the root of the equation:
g(\alpha)=c_{0}-\psi(\alpha)=0
for
\alpha>0
, where
c_{0}\triangleq G({\boldsymbol{x}})+\log(\beta)
is a known constant,
\psi(\cdot)
is the digamma function defined as

\displaystyle\psi(\alpha)\triangleq\frac{\Gamma^{\prime}(\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=-\gamma+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{m+1}-\frac{1}{m+\alpha}\right)

(4.11)

here
\gamma=\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(\sum_{m=1}^{n}m^{-1}-\log n\right)\approx 0.5772
is the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Since

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\psi^{\prime}(\alpha)=-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m+\alpha)^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(m+\alpha)^{2}}

\displaystyle>

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m^{2}}=-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\;\triangleq\;b(\alpha),

the US iteration for calculating the MLE
\hat{\alpha}
is

\displaystyle\alpha^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\alpha^{(t)})+\int_{\alpha^{(t)}}^{\alpha}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;\alpha,\alpha^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\alpha^{(t)})+\frac{1}{\alpha}-\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\alpha-\frac{1}{\alpha^{(t)}}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}\alpha^{(t)}=0,\;\forall\;\alpha,\alpha^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{(\pi^{2}/6)\alpha^{2}-a_{3}^{(t)}\alpha-1=0,\;\forall\;\alpha,\alpha^{(t)}>0\right\},

where
a_{3}^{(t)}=c_{0}-\psi(\alpha^{(t)})+(\pi^{2}/6)\alpha^{(t)}-1/\alpha^{(t)}
.

4.3.2
MLE of
\theta
in Weibull distribution

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Weibull}(\theta,\lambda)
with pdf
(\theta/\lambda)(x/\lambda)^{\theta-1}\exp[-(x/\lambda)^{\theta}]
,
x>0
, where
\{\theta,\lambda\}
are two positive parameters (Hallinan 1993, Rinne 2008). The log-likelihood function is
\ell(\theta,\lambda)=n\log(\theta)-n\theta\log(\lambda)+nG({\boldsymbol{x}})(\theta-1)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}(x_{i}/\lambda)^{\theta}
, where
G({\boldsymbol{x}})\triangleq(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})
. Let
0=\partial\ell(\theta,\lambda)/\partial\lambda=-n\theta\lambda^{-1}+\theta\lambda^{-\theta-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}
. Given
\theta
, we obtain
\lambda=[(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}]^{1/\theta}
. Replacing
\lambda
in
\ell(\theta,\lambda)
by
[(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}]^{1/\theta}
, we know that
\ell(\theta,\lambda)
will reduce to
\ell(\theta)=\theta nG({\boldsymbol{x}})+n\log(\theta)-n\log(\textstyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta})+\mbox{ constant}
. Thus, the MLE of
\theta
is the root of
\ell^{\prime}(\theta)=0
or the root of the equation

g(\theta)=G({\boldsymbol{x}})+\frac{1}{\theta}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}\log(x_{i})}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}}=0,\quad\theta>0.

Since

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}-\frac{[\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}(\log x_{i})^{2}](\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta})-(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}\log x_{i})^{2}}{(\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta})^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}(\log x_{i})^{2}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}^{\theta}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle-\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}-\max_{1\leqslant i\leqslant n}(\log x_{i})^{2}\;\triangleq\;-\frac{1}{\theta^{2}}-T_{\max}\;\triangleq\;b(\theta),

the US iteration for calculating the MLE
\hat{\theta}
is

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+\frac{1}{\theta}-T_{\max}\theta-\frac{1}{\theta^{(t)}}+T_{\max}\theta^{(t)}=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{T_{\max}\theta^{2}-a_{4}^{(t)}\theta-1=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>0\right\},

where
a_{4}^{(t)}\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})+T_{\max}\theta^{(t)}-1/\theta^{(t)}
.

4.4
MLEs of parameters in a class of discrete distributions

4.4.1
MLE of
\theta
in zeta distribution

The zeta (or Zipf) distribution can be used to model the frequency of occurrence of a word randomly chosen from a text, or the population rank of a city randomly chosen from a country. Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{Zeta}(\theta)
with pmf
Z^{-1}(\theta)x^{-\theta}
,
x=1,2,\ldots,\infty
, where
\theta\,(>1)
is a parameter and
Z(\theta)=\sum_{x=1}^{\infty}x^{-\theta}
is called Riemann’s zeta function (Nair
et al
. 2018, p.131–134). The log-likelihood is
\ell(\theta)=-nG({\boldsymbol{x}})\theta-n\log Z(\theta)
, where
G({\boldsymbol{x}})\triangleq(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log(x_{i})
. Let
0=\ell^{\prime}(\theta)=-nG({\boldsymbol{x}})-nZ^{\prime}(\theta)/Z(\theta)
, then the MLE of
\theta
is the root of the equation:
g(\theta)=-G({\boldsymbol{x}})-Z^{\prime}(\theta)/Z(\theta)=0
for
\theta>1
. Note that

g^{\prime}(\theta)=-\;\frac{Z^{\prime\prime}(\theta)Z(\theta)-[Z^{\prime}(\theta)]^{2}}{Z^{2}(\theta)}\geqslant-\;\frac{Z^{\prime\prime}(\theta)}{Z(\theta)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\rm\ref{eqn4.2})\;\&\;(\rm\ref{eqnfiveC.3})}}{{\geqslant}}a_{5}-\frac{a_{6}}{(\theta-1)^{2}}\;\triangleq\;b(\theta),

where
a_{5}\triangleq-\log 2\cdot(\log 2+2)
and
a_{6}\triangleq 2\log 2+2
, then
the US iteration for calculating the MLE
\hat{\theta}
is given by

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>1\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+a_{5}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})+\frac{a_{6}}{\theta-1}-\frac{a_{6}}{\theta^{(t)}-1}=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>1\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{a_{5}\theta^{2}+a_{7}^{(t)}\theta+a_{8}^{(t)}=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>1\right\},

where
a_{7}^{(t)}\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})-a_{5}(\theta^{(t)}+1)-a_{6}/(\theta^{(t)}-1)
and
a_{8}^{(t)}\triangleq-g(\theta^{(t)})+a_{5}\theta^{(t)}+a_{6}\theta^{(t)}/(\theta^{(t)}-1)
.

4.4.2
MLE of
\theta
in Yule–Simon distribution

The Yule–Simon distribution (Yule 1925, Garcia 2011) can be employed to model species among genera, words frequencies in texts, numbers of papers published by researchers, cities by population, income by size (Leisen, Rossini & Villa 2017). Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{YS}(\theta)
with pmf
\theta\,\mbox{Beta}(x,\theta+1)
,
x=1,2,\ldots,\infty
, where
\theta\,(>0)
is the shape parameter. The log-likelihood function is
\ell(\theta)=n\log(\theta)+n\log\Gamma(\theta+1)-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Gamma(x_{i}+\theta+1)+\mbox{constant}
. Thus, the MLE of
\theta
is the root of the following equation:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}(\theta)=\frac{n}{\theta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}[\psi(\theta+1)-\psi(x_{i}+\theta+1)]

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive4.11})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\theta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+\theta+1}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+x_{i}+\theta+1}\right)\qquad[\mbox{Let }m+x_{i}=k]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\theta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+\theta+1}+\sum_{k=x_{i}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k+\theta+1}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{n}{\theta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left(-\sum_{m=0}^{x_{i}-1}\frac{1}{m+\theta+1}\right)\;\triangleq\;g(\theta).

Since

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{\theta^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\sum_{m=0}^{x_{i}-1}\frac{1}{(m+\theta+1)^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\frac{n}{\theta^{2}}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\frac{1}{(\theta+1)^{2}}+I(x_{i}\geqslant 2)\sum_{m=1}^{x_{i}-1}\frac{1}{(m+\theta+1)^{2}}\right]\geqslant-\frac{n}{\theta^{2}}+\frac{n}{(\theta+1)^{2}}\triangleq b(\theta),

the US iteration for calculating the MLE
\hat{\theta}
is

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+\frac{n}{\theta}-\frac{n}{\theta+1}-\frac{n}{\theta^{(t)}}+\frac{n}{\theta^{(t)}+1}=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{a_{9}^{(t)}\theta^{2}+a_{9}^{(t)}\theta+n=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}>0\right\},

where
a_{9}^{(t)}\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})-n/\theta^{(t)}+n/(\theta^{(t)}+1)
.

4.4.3
MLE of
\alpha
in Gamma–Poisson distribution

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{GPoisson}(\alpha,\beta)
with pmf
\Gamma(x+\alpha)\Gamma^{-1}(\alpha)\beta^{\alpha}(\beta+1)^{-x-\alpha}/x!
,
x=0,1,2,\ldots,\infty
, where
\alpha
and
\beta
are two positive parameters (Arbous & Kerrich 1951, Bates & Neyman 1952). Define
\bar{x}=(1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}
, the log-likelihood function is

\ell(\alpha,\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Gamma(x_{i}+\alpha)-n\log\Gamma(\alpha)+n\alpha\log(\beta)-n(\alpha+\bar{x})\log(\beta+1)+\mbox{constant}.

Let
0=\partial\ell(\alpha,\beta)/\partial\beta=n\alpha/\beta-n(\alpha+\bar{x})/(\beta+1)
, we obtain
\beta=\alpha/\bar{x}
. Replacing
\beta
in
\ell(\alpha,\beta)
by
\alpha/\bar{x}
, we know that
\ell(\alpha,\beta)
becomes

\ell(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\Gamma(x_{i}+\alpha)-n\log\Gamma(\alpha)+n\alpha\log\alpha-\ n(\alpha+\bar{x})\log(\alpha+\bar{x})+\mbox{constant}.

Thus, the MLE of
\alpha
is the root of the following equation:

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\ell^{\prime}(\alpha)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\psi(x_{i}+\alpha)-\psi(\alpha)\right]+n\log\alpha-n\log(\alpha+\bar{x})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive4.11})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+x_{i}+\alpha}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+\alpha}\right]+n\log\alpha-n\log(\alpha+\bar{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-\sum_{m=x_{i}}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+\alpha}+\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{m+\alpha}\right]+n\log\alpha-n\log(\alpha+\bar{x})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[I(x_{i}\geqslant 1)\sum_{m=0}^{x_{i}-1}\frac{1}{m+\alpha}\right]+n\log\alpha-n\log(\alpha+\bar{x})\triangleq g(\alpha).

Since

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\alpha)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[-I(x_{i}\geqslant 1)\sum_{m=0}^{x_{i}-1}\frac{1}{(m+\alpha)^{2}}\right]+\frac{n}{\alpha}-\frac{n}{\alpha+\bar{x}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[I(x_{i}\geqslant 2)\sum_{m=1}^{x_{i}-1}\frac{1}{m^{2}}\right]-\frac{1}{\alpha^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(x_{i}\geqslant 1)+\frac{n\bar{x}}{\alpha(\alpha+\bar{x})}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle a_{10}+a_{11}/\alpha^{2}\triangleq\;b(\alpha),

where
a_{10}\triangleq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[I(x_{i}\geqslant 2)\sum_{m=1}^{x_{i}-1}m^{-2}\right]
and
a_{11}\triangleq-\sum_{i=1}^{n}I(x_{i}\geqslant 1)
, the US iteration for calculating the MLE
\hat{\theta}
is

\displaystyle\alpha^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\alpha^{(t)})+\int_{\alpha^{(t)}}^{\alpha}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;\alpha,\alpha^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\alpha^{(t)})+a_{10}(\alpha-\alpha^{(t)})-a_{11}/\alpha+a_{11}/\alpha^{(t)}=0,\;\forall\;\alpha,\alpha^{(t)}>0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{a_{10}\alpha^{2}+a_{12}^{(t)}\alpha-a_{11}=0,\;\forall\;\alpha,\alpha^{(t)}>0\right\},

where
a_{12}^{(t)}\triangleq g(\alpha^{(t)})-a_{10}\alpha^{(t)}+a_{11}/\alpha^{(t)}
.

4.4.4
MLE of
\theta
in Generalized Poisson distribution

Let
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm iid}}}{{\sim}}\mbox{GP}(\lambda,\theta)
with pmf
\lambda(\lambda+\theta x)^{x-1}\mbox{e}^{-\lambda-\theta x}/x!
,
x=0,1,2,\ldots,\infty
, where
\lambda>0
and
\max(-1,-\lambda/r)<\theta\leqslant 1
are two parameters and
r\ (\geqslant 4)
is the largest positive integer for which
\lambda+\theta r>0
when
\theta<0
(Consul & Jain 1973, Consul & Famoye 1989). The log-likelihood is
\ell(\lambda,\theta)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left[\log\lambda+(x_{i}-1)\log(\lambda+\theta x_{i})-\lambda-\theta x_{i}\right]+\mbox{constant}
. There is an MM algorithm for calculating the MLE of
\theta
for the case of
\theta\in[0,1]
, while, to the best of our knowledge, there is no efficient algorithm to calculate
\hat{\theta}
for the case of
\theta<0
. In this subsection, we only consider the case of
\theta<0
. Let

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell(\lambda,\theta)}{\partial\lambda}=\frac{n}{\lambda}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}-1}{\lambda+\theta x_{i}}-n\quad\mbox{and}\quad

(4.12)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\ell(\lambda,\theta)}{\partial\theta}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(x_{i}-1)x_{i}}{\lambda+\theta x_{i}}-n\bar{x}.

(4.13)

By multiplying
\lambda
and
\theta
to the two partial differential equations (
4.12
) and (
4.13
), respectively, and then adding them together, we obtain
\lambda=(1-\theta)\bar{x}
. Replacing
\lambda
in
\ell(\lambda,\theta)
by
(1-\theta)\bar{x}
, we know that
\ell(\lambda,\theta)
will reduce to
\ell(\theta)=n\log(1-\theta)+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big\{(x_{i}-1)\log[\bar{x}+(x_{i}-\bar{x})\theta]\big\}+\mbox{constant}
. Thus, the MLE of
\theta
is the root of the following equation:

\displaystyle 0=\ell^{\prime}(\theta)=-\frac{n}{1-\theta}+\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(x_{i}-1)(x_{i}-\bar{x})}{\bar{x}+(x_{i}-\bar{x})\theta}\;\triangleq\;g(\theta).

Since

\displaystyle g^{\prime}(\theta)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\;\frac{n}{(1-\theta)^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{(x_{i}-1)(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}}{[\bar{x}+(x_{i}-\bar{x})\theta]^{2}}\geqslant-\;\frac{n}{(1-\theta)^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}}{[\bar{x}(1-\theta)+x_{i}\theta]^{2}}

\displaystyle\geqslant

\displaystyle-\;\frac{n}{(1-\theta)^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}}{[\bar{x}(1-\theta)+x_{(n)}\theta]^{2}}=-\;\frac{n}{(1-\theta)^{2}}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{x_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}}{[\bar{x}+(x_{(n)}-\bar{x})\theta]^{2}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\;\frac{n}{(1-\theta)^{2}}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}[x_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}/(x_{(n)}-\bar{x})^{2}]}{[\bar{x}/(x_{(n)}-\bar{x})+\theta]^{2}}

\displaystyle\triangleq

\displaystyle-\;\frac{n}{(1-\theta)^{2}}-\frac{a_{13}}{(a_{14}+\theta)^{2}}\triangleq b(\theta),

where
x_{(n)}\triangleq\max(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
,
a_{13}\triangleq\sum_{i=1}^{n}[x_{i}(x_{i}-\bar{x})^{2}/(x_{(n)}-\bar{x})^{2}]
, and
a_{14}\triangleq\bar{x}/(x_{(n)}-\bar{x})
, the US iteration for calculating the MLE
\hat{\theta}
is

\displaystyle\theta^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle(\ref{eqnfive3.2})}}{{=}}

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})+\int_{\theta^{(t)}}^{\theta}b(z)\,\mbox{d}z=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}<0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{g(\theta^{(t)})-\frac{n}{1-\theta}+\frac{a_{13}}{a_{14}+\theta}+\frac{n}{1-\theta^{(t)}}-\frac{a_{13}}{a_{14}+\theta^{(t)}}=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}<0\right\}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mbox{sol}\left\{a_{15}^{(t)}\theta^{2}-a_{16}^{(t)}\theta+(n-a_{15}^{(t)})a_{14}-a_{13}=0,\;\forall\;\theta,\theta^{(t)}<0\right\},

where
a_{15}^{(t)}\triangleq g(\theta^{(t)})+n/(1-\theta^{(t)})-a_{13}/(a_{14}+\theta^{(t)})
and
a_{16}^{(t)}\triangleq a_{15}^{(t)}(1-a_{14})-n-a_{13}
.

5
. Analysis of the convergence rate

Let
\theta^{*}
be the unique root of the equation
g(\theta)=0
for all
\theta\in\Theta
. To get the rate of convergence for the US iteration
\theta^{(t+1)}\triangleq h(\theta^{(t)})
, we consider the first–order Taylor expansion of
h(\theta^{(t)})
around
\theta^{*}
:
h(\theta^{(t)})=h(\theta^{*})+(\theta^{(t)}-\theta^{*})h^{\prime}(\theta^{*})+0.5(\theta^{(t)}-\theta^{*})^{2}h^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})
,
where
\tilde{\theta}
is a point between
\theta^{(t)}
and
\theta^{*}
. The convergence rate of the US algorithm is defined by

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{|\theta^{(t+1)}-\theta^{*}|}{|\theta^{(t)}-\theta^{*}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}\frac{|h(\theta^{(t)})-h(\theta^{*})|}{|\theta^{(t)}-\theta^{*}|}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to\infty}|h^{\prime}(\theta^{*})+0.5(\theta^{(t)}-\theta^{*})h^{\prime\prime}(\tilde{\theta})|=|h^{\prime}(\theta^{*})|.

If
|h^{\prime}(\theta^{*})|\in(0,1)
, then the US algorithm has a linear convergence rate.

In this section, we investigate the convergence rate of the US algorithm for the three methods of constructing
U
-functions presented in Section 3. Theorem 2 below states that the US algorithm holds a linear convergence rate, just like the EM and MM algorithms with monotonic convergence and linear convergence rate in maximization of an objective function. Theorems 3–4 below show that the US algorithm holds a quadratic and even a cubic convergence rates, corresponding to the
SLUB constants method and the TLB constant method.

7
. Discussions

To solve the root of a non-linear equation
g(\theta)=0
, in this paper, we have established a general framework of a new root–finding method, called as the US algorithm. Its each iteration consists of a
U-step
and an
S-step
, where the
U-step
is to construct one
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
based on a new notion of changing direction inequality for the original function
g(\theta)
, while the
S-step
solves the
U
-equation
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
with respect to the left argument to obtain the next iterate
\theta^{(t+1)}
. Unlike the NR algorithm, which is sensitive to initial values and may diverge if a poor initial value is chosen, from Theorem 1, we know that the US algorithm has the property of strongly stable convergence; that is, it does not depend on any initial values in
\Theta
and strongly stably converges to the root
\theta^{*}
. Especially, because of the property of strongly stable convergence, the US algorithm could be one of the powerful tools for solving an equation with multiple roots as shown in Section 6.3.

The critical requirement for applying the US algorithm is to construct one
U
-function
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})
for
g(\theta)
at
\theta=\theta^{(t)}
(i.e., to satisfy the CD inequality
g(\theta)-U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle{\rm sgn}(\theta-\theta^{(t)})}}{{\geqslant}}0
) such that an explicit solution to the surrogate equation
U(\theta|\theta^{(t)})=0
is available. By using Taylor expansion, we have developed three methods for constructing
U
-functions based on the FLB function, SLUB constants and TLB constant. The resulting US algorithms have linear/quadratic/cubic convergence rates as shown in Theorems 2–4, respectively. In the future studies, we will develop other methods for constructing
U
-functions. Although the US algorithm enjoys strongly stable convergence, we would like to accelerate the US algorithm with slow convergence rate if the bound functions/constants are not tight enough, particularly for the FLB function-based US algorithms. Some accelerating techniques for the proposed US algorithms may significantly spark the method’s potential.

In Section 4, we successfully applied the US algorithms to deal with a wide range of statistical issues that are not perfectly solved by the traditional numerical algorithms. Beyond those problems, we are trying to use the US algorithms to handle other attractive statistical models that are still remained to be conquered.
