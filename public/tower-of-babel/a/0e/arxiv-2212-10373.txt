Bateman–Horn, polynomial Chowla and the Hasse principle with probability 1
Tim Browning, Efthymios Sofos, Joni Teräväinen

Abstract
. With probability 1 1 , we assess the average behaviour of various arithmetic functions at the values of degree d d polynomials f ∈ ℤ ⁡ [ t ] f\in\mathbb{Z}[t] that are ordered by height. This allows us to establish averaged versions of the Bateman–Horn conjecture, the polynomial Chowla conjecture and to address a basic question about the integral Hasse principle for norm form equations. Moreover, we are able to quantify the error term in the asymptotics and the size of the exceptional set of f f , both with arbitrary logarithmic power savings.

1.
Introduction

The average behaviour of arithmetic functions evaluated at polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
has long been a central pursuit in analytic number theory. For the
von Mangoldt function
\Lambda
, this encodes the fundamental question of when polynomials capture their primes. While Dirichlet’s theorem allows us to handle linear polynomials,
polynomials such as

f(t)=t^{2}+1
remain out of reach.
For the Liouville function

\lambda
, cancellation is predicted by the Chowla conjecture precisely when the polynomial is not proportional to the square of a polynomial. This also seems out of reach for irreducible
polynomials of degree at least two.
For the arithmetic function
r
that counts representations of an integer as a sum of two squares,
this relates to deep questions about the solubility of
the equation

u^{2}+v^{2}=f(t)

in integers.
Current techniques prevent us from treating such equations when
\deg(f)\geqslant 3
.

The purpose of this paper is
to resolve all of these questions when averaging over the coefficients of polynomials of a given degree.

1.1.
Bateman–Horn for random polynomials

Let
\Lambda
denote the von Mangoldt function, which we extend to all integers by setting

\Lambda(0)=0
and
\Lambda(-n)=\Lambda(n)
for
n>0
. We say that a polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
is
an
admissible polynomial

if
f
is irreducible over
\mathbb{Q}
and it has no fixed prime divisor. The latter means there exists no prime
p
such that all of the integers
f(n)
are divisible by
p
, as
n
ranges over the integers. In 1854
Bunyakovsky conjectured that any
admissible polynomial takes on infinitely many (positive or negative) prime values. The Bateman–Horn conjecture is a far-reaching generalisation of Bunyakovsky’s conjecture. It predicts that, for any irreducible polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
of degree
d\geqslant 1
, we have

(1.1)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda(f(n))=\mathfrak{S}_{f}+o(1),

as
x\to\infty
,
where

\mathfrak{S}_{f}=\prod_{p}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:f(u)=0\}}{p}\right).

Note that
\mathfrak{S}_{f}>0
precisely when
f
is
an admissible
polynomial, and so this assertion
contains Bunyakovsky’s conjecture.

For
fixed
polynomials
f
of degree
\geqslant 2
, these conjectures seem very difficult. Our first result studies the asymptotic (
1.1
) for
almost all
polynomials, which involves fixing some of the coefficients of the polynomial and letting the others vary in a box. This leads to the following definition.

Definition 1.1
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and let
H\geqslant 1
. For any index set
\mathscr{E}\subseteq\{0,\dots,d\}
and any integers
\alpha_{e}\in[-H,H]
for
e\in\mathscr{E}
, we call the set

\displaystyle\mathscr{C}=\mathscr{C}(H)=\left\{(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})\in(\mathbb{Z}\cap[-H,H])^{d+1}:a_{e}=\alpha_{e}\,\,\forall\,\,e\in\mathscr{E}\right\}

a
combinatorial cube
of side length
H
and dimension
d+1-\#\mathscr{E}
. We call
a_{d}
the
constant coefficient
of an element
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{C}
.
Given a combinatorial cube
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
, we say that a polynomial
a_{0}t^{d}+a_{1}t^{d-1}+\cdots+a_{d}

has its coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
if
(a_{0},\ldots,a_{d})\in\mathscr{C}
.

We are now ready to reveal our first result. Under the assumption that the dimension of the combinatorial cube
\mathscr{C}
is at least two, this shows that almost all
admissible polynomials with coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
produce many prime values.

Theorem 1.2

(Bateman–Horn with probability
1
)
.

Let
d,A\geqslant 1
and
0<c<5/(19d)
be fixed. There exists a constant
H_{0}(d,A,c)
such that the following holds for any
H\geqslant H_{0}(d,A,c)
. Let
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be a combinatorial cube of side length
H
and dimension at least
2
, such that the constant coefficient is not fixed to be
0
. Then, for all but at most
\#\mathscr{C}/(\log H)^{A}
degree
d
polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
with coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
, we have

(1.2)

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[H^{c},2H^{c}]}\left|\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda(f(n))-\mathfrak{S}_{f}(x)\right|\leqslant(\log H)^{-A},

where

\mathfrak{S}_{f}(x)=\prod_{p\leqslant\exp(\sqrt{\log x})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}\left(1-\frac{\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:f(u)=0\}}{p}\right).

The hypothesis that the constant coefficient of
\mathscr{C}
is not fixed to be
0
is clearly necessary, since
the polynomials parameterised by
\mathscr{C}
will be reducible otherwise. We proceed by putting Theorem
1.2
into context.
First and foremost, Theorem
1.2
(and its
generalisation to tuples of polynomials in
Theorem
1.4
) strengthens work of Skorobogatov and Sofos
[
34
, Thm. 1.9]
, with
the main improvement being the increased allowable size of
x
.
In our work we can take the length of the sum to be of polynomial size
x\asymp H^{c}
in terms of the height, whereas only sums of logarithmic length
x\asymp(\log H)^{A}
are allowed in

[
34
]
. Moreover, since the dimension of
\mathscr{C}
is allowed to be
2
, we can average over only two coefficients, rather than the full set of coefficients needed in
[
34
]
.

Under the additional assumption of GRH, it would be possible to prove (
1.2
) for
c=1/d-\delta
, for any fixed
\delta>0
. Thus, apart from the value of
c
, the range
x\ll H^{c}
we obtain is as good as what GRH would give.
Moreover, the size of the exceptional set of polynomials cannot be further improved
1
1

1

Indeed, if the exceptional set in Theorem
1.2
was of size
\ll\#\mathscr{C}/(\log H)^{\psi(H)}
for some
\psi(H)\to\infty
, then (
1.2
) would be true for every polynomial
f(t)=qt+a
with
1\leqslant a,q\leqslant(\log H)^{\psi(H)/2}
without improving the Siegel–Walfisz theorem.
Finally, we mention some further results in the literature which use averaging over only one coefficient to capture prime values of polynomials. For polynomials of the shape
f(t)=t^{d}+c
, for varying
c
, the best results available are due to Balestrieri and Rome
[
2
]
and Zhou
[
40
]
(generalising earlier works of Baier and Zhao
[
1
]
, Foo and Zhao
[
15
]
, and Granville and Mollin
[
16
, Prop. 1]
).

As an immediate corollary to Theorem
1.2
, we obtain a polynomial lower bound on the number of prime values that a typical polynomial takes.

Corollary 1.3

(100% of
admissible polynomials take polynomially many prime values)
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. Let
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be a combinatorial cube of side length
H\geqslant H_{0}(d,\varepsilon)
and dimension at least
2
. Then, for 100% of degree
d

admissible polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
with coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
, we have

\#\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\text{$f(n)$ is prime}\right\}\geqslant H^{5/(19d)-\varepsilon}.

Theorem
1.2
is a special case of the following result that applies to the simultaneous primality of a tuple of polynomials.

Theorem 1.4

(Multidimensional Bateman–Horn with probability
1
)
.

Let
d,A,r\geqslant 1
and
0<c<5/(19d)
be fixed. There exists a constant
H_{0}(d,A,c,r)
such that the following holds for any
H\geqslant H_{0}(d,A,c)
. Let
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be a combinatorial cube of side length
H
and dimension at least
2
, such that the constant coefficient is not fixed to be
0
. Then, for all but at most
(\#\mathscr{C})^{r}/(\log H)^{A}

r
-tuples of polynomials
f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
having degrees
\leqslant d
and coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
, we have

\sup_{x\in[H^{c},2H^{c}]}\left|\frac{1}{x}\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda(f_{r}(n))-\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}}(x)\right|\leqslant(\log H)^{-A},

where

(1.3)

\displaystyle\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}}(x)=\prod_{p\leqslant\exp(\sqrt{\log x})}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-r}\left(1-\frac{\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:f_{1}(u)\cdots f_{r}(u)=0\}}{p}\right).

Applying this with
f_{1}(t)=t
and
f_{2}(t)=f(t)
, and using partial summation to replace
\Lambda(n)
with the indicator of the primes, we see that (under the same conditions as in Theorem
1.4
) for all but
\ll\#\mathscr{C}/(\log H)^{A}
polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
of degree
d
with coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[H^{c},2H^{c}]}\left|\sum_{p\leqslant x}\Lambda(f(p))-\mathfrak{S}_{t,f}(x)\int_{2}^{x}\frac{\textnormal{d}u}{\log u}\right|\ll x(\log H)^{-A}.

This can be seen as an almost-all version of the Bateman–Horn conjecture for polynomials evaluated at primes.

1.2.
Chowla’s conjecture for random polynomials

Let
\lambda
denote the Liouville function, which we extend to all integers by setting
\lambda(0)=0
and
\lambda(-n)=\lambda(n)
for
n>0
.
The polynomial Chowla conjecture
[
10
]
states that, for any
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
that is not of the form
cg(t)^{2}
with
c\in\mathbb{R}
and
g\in\mathbb{R}[t]
, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}\lambda(f(n))=o(x).

Despite recent progress due to Tao and
Teräväinen

[
36
,
37
,
38
]
,
concerning the case of polynomials that factor into linear factors,
this conjecture remains wide open for any
f
that is irreducible and of degree at least
2
.
In the spirit of Theorem
1.2
, we can prove the polynomial Chowla conjecture for almost all polynomials, improving on a qualitative result due to Teräväinen

[
39
, Thm. 2.12]
.

Theorem 1.5

(Polynomial Chowla conjecture with probability
1
)
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
,
A\geqslant 1
and
0<c<5/(19d)
be fixed. There exists a constant
H_{0}(d,A,c)
such that the following holds for any
H\geqslant H_{0}(d,A,c)
. Let
\mathscr{C}\subset\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
be a combinatorial cube of side length
H
and dimension at least
2
. Then, for all but at most
\#\mathscr{C}/(\log H)^{A}
degree
d
polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
with coefficients in
\mathscr{C}
, we have

\displaystyle\sup_{x\in[H^{c},2H^{c}]}\frac{1}{x}\left|\sum_{n\leqslant x}\lambda(f(n))\right|\leqslant(\log H)^{-A}.

1.3.
Norm forms at random polynomials

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a finite extension of number fields of degree
e\geqslant 2

and fix a
\mathbb{Z}
-basis
\{\omega_{1},\dots,\omega_{e}\}
for the ring of integers of
K
.
We will denote the corresponding
norm form
by

(1.4)

\displaystyle\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(x_{1}\omega_{1}+\dots+x_{e}\omega_{e}),

where

\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\dots,x_{e})
and

N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}
is the field norm.
This is a homogenous polynomial of degree
e
with integer coefficients.
Fix a positive integer
d
.
Our final result concerns the probability that the equation

(1.5)

\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f(t)

is soluble over
\mathbb{Z}
, for a randomly chosen degree
d
polynomial

f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
. These surfaces are sometimes called
generalised Châtelet surfaces
.

The equation (
1.5
) defines an affine variety
X\subset\mathbb{A}^{e+1}
and
is equipped with a dominant morphism
\pi:X\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
. We denote by
\mathscr{X}
its integral model over
\mathbb{Z}
, which is given by the same equation. Let
\mathscr{X}(\mathbb{Z})
denote the set of integer points on
\mathscr{X}
and let

\mathscr{X}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Z}})=X(\mathbb{R})\times\prod_{p}\mathscr{X}(\mathbb{Z}_{p})
denote the set of adèles on
\mathscr{X}
. Since
\mathscr{X}(\mathbb{Z})
embeds diagonally in

\mathscr{X}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Z}})
, an obvious
necessary condition for the solubility of (
1.5
) over
\mathbb{Z}
is the condition

\mathscr{X}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Z}})\neq\emptyset
. In other words,
the equation should be soluble over
\mathbb{R}
and over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for all primes
p
.
If
\mathscr{X}(\mathbb{Z})=\emptyset
but

\mathscr{X}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Z}})\neq\emptyset
, then we have a
counter-example to the integral Hasse principle
. Colliot-Thélène and Xu
[
13
]
have established that the
Brauer–Manin obstruction
plays an important role in the existence of integral points on
\mathscr{X}
, leading to an inclusion of sets

\mathscr{X}(\mathbb{Z})\hookrightarrow\mathscr{X}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Z}})^{\Br X}\subseteq\mathscr{X}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathbb{Z}}),

where
\Br X=\mathrm{H}_{\text{\'{e}t}}^{2}(X,\mathbb{G}_{m})
is the Brauer group of
X
.
It remains a substantial challenge to find classes of varieties for which the
Brauer–Manin obstruction can be shown to be the only obstruction.

In
[
14
, Thm. 1.1]
,
Derenthal and Wei have studied the arithmetic of
(
1.5
) when
e=4
and
f(t)=c(t^{2}-a)
, for non-zero
c\in\mathbb{Z}
and square-free
a\in\mathbb{Z}
.
Under the assumption that
\sqrt{a}\in K
and
\pi(X(\mathbb{R}))
is unbounded, it is shown that the Brauer–Manin obstruction controls the integral Hasse principle for
X
.
The equation (
1.5
) is most familiar in the case
e=2
of quadratic extensions
K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{a})
, for square-free
a\in\mathbb{Z}
.
As explained in
[
18
, §4]
,
Harari
[
18
, Thm. 4]
uses class field theory to prove that the Brauer–Manin obstruction
is the only obstruction to the existence of integral points
when
f
is the constant polynomial.
When
f(t)=bt^{2}+c
is quadratic such that
\nf_{K}(x_{1},x_{2})-bt^{2}
is an indefinite ternary quadratic form, then it follows from
the work of Colliot-Thélène and Xu
[
13
, Thm. 6.3]
that the Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only one.
In
[
11
, p. 175]
, Colliot-Thélène and Harari have highlighted the difficulty of tackling the arithmetic of
X
when
f
is a separable polynomial of degree
d\geqslant 3
, noting that the quotient
\Br X_{t}/\Br\mathbb{Q}
is infinite for any smooth
\mathbb{Q}
-fibre
X_{t}=\pi^{-1}(t)
of the morphism
\pi:X\to\mathbb{A}^{1}
.
In 1979, Colliot-Thélène and Sansuc
[
12
]
discovered
that Schinzel’s Hypothesis
[
32
]
,
which is a generalisation of Bunyakovsky’s conjecture to multiple polynomials,
has a key role to play in the study of local–global principles for rational points on
conic bundle surfaces.
This was adapted to integral points by
Gundlach
[
17
, Thm. 2]
, who assumed
Schinzel’s Hypothesis
to prove that the
Brauer–Manin obstruction is the only obstruction to the existence of integral points
on (
1.5
) when
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
and

f(t)=-t^{d}+c
for odd
d\geqslant 3
and
c\in\mathbb{Z}
.
This work has been generalised to other quadratic fields

K=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{a})
by Mitankin
[
27
]
.
Suppose that
f(t)=cf_{1}(t)\cdots f_{n}(t)
is non-constant and separable, and that the discriminant of the splitting field of
f_{1}
is coprime to the discriminant of
K
. Then, under
Schinzel’s Hypothesis and
certain further technical assumptions, Mitankin confirms
that the Brauer–Manin obstruction controls the integral Hasse principle for
X
.
Given Corollary
1.3
, it is not very surprising that our methods might have something to say about the integral Hasse principle on average
for equations of the form (
1.5
). However, as discussed below, using prime values of polynomials is not enough to generate solutions to
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f(t)
for almost all
polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
, so we offer a different approach to the problem, which measures the density of integer solutions directly, at least on average.

Problems can occur
when
\pi(X(\mathbb{R}))
is bounded, which can be viewed as an additional
obstruction at infinity
.
Consider the case
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
and
f(t)=-t^{4}+22
, for example, corresponding to the equation

x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+t^{4}=22.

This equation has no solution over
\mathbb{Z}
, despite being soluble over
\mathbb{Q}
and over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for every prime
p
.
Berg
[
3
]
has shown that there is no Brauer–Manin obstruction to the existence of integer points and so this counter-example to the integral Hasse principle comes purely from an obstruction at infinity.

For
H\geqslant 1
,
we shall parameterise degree
d
polynomials

f(t)=c_{0}t^{d}+\cdots+c_{d}\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
by coefficient vectors belonging to the set

S_{d}(H)=\left\{\mathbf{c}=(c_{0},\dots,c_{d})\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}:|\mathbf{c}|\leqslant H\right\},

where
|\cdot|
is the sup-norm.
As
H\to\infty
, we would like to understand solubility of

\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)

over
\mathbb{Z}

for a randomly chosen vector
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(H)
,
where we adopt the notation
f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)=c_{0}t^{d}+\cdots+c_{d}
. At the non-archimedean places we shall want our vector
\mathbf{c}
to be such that
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)

is soluble over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for all primes
p
. At the infinite place we wish to capture the information that the
obstruction at infinity is void, which we achieve by simply
demanding that the leading coefficient
c_{0}
of
f_{\mathbf{c}}
is positive. Henceforth,
a vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}
is said to be
admissible
if
c_{0}>0
and

\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)

is soluble over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
for all primes
p
.
Let

(1.6)

S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(H)=\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(H):\text{$\mathbf{c}$ is admissible}\right\}.

It is easily confirmed that

\#S_{d}(H)=2^{d+1}H^{d+1}+O_{d}(H^{d}).

Moreover,
it follows from
[
6
, Thm. 1.4]
that

(1.7)

\#S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(H)=\left(\frac{1}{2}+o(1)\right)\prod_{p}\sigma_{p}\cdot\#S_{d}(H),

as
H\to\infty
,
where
\sigma_{p}
is the probability that
the equation
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)
is soluble over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, for a
coefficient vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d+1}
.
In particular, the equation
(
1.5
) is everywhere locally soluble for a positive proportion of coefficient vectors.
We shall prove that, with probability 1,
the equation
(
1.5
)
admits a solution
(\mathbf{x},t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{e+1}
, for any degree
d

polynomial
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
with
admissible coefficient vector.
In fact we shall be able to do so with an exceptional set that saves an arbitrary power of
\log H
.

Theorem 1.6

(Hasse principle for 100% of generalised Châtelet surfaces)
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
A>0
. Then, for any finite extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
, we have

\frac{\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(H):\text{$\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)$ is soluble over $\mathbb{Z}$}\right\}}{\#S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(H)}=1+O_{d,K,A}\left(\frac{1}{(\log H)^{A}}\right),

where the implied constant depends only on
d
, on
K
and on the choice of
A
.

It is natural to compare this result with recent work of Skorobogatov and Sofos
[
34
]
,
who are also interested in the equation
(
1.5
) for random polynomials
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
, although
it should be emphasised that our results concern solubility over
\mathbb{Z}
, rather than over
\mathbb{Q}
. Putting this key difference to one side, the scope of Theorem
1.6

improves on that of
[
34
, Thm. 1.3]
in two major aspects. Firstly, we can handle arbitrary number fields
K/\mathbb{Q}
, rather than restricting to cyclic extensions. Secondly, we are able to prove that
100\%
of admissible polynomials
yield integer solutions (with an error term that saves an arbitrary power of
\log H
),
whereas only a positive proportion is achieved in
[
34
]
.
In the subsequent paragraph to
[
34
, Thm. 1.3]
, Skorobogatov and Sofos
indicate how one might extend their work to handle totally imaginary abelian extensions
K/\mathbb{Q}
of class number
1
. The first step
is an application of the
Kronecker–Weber theorem, which implies that
K\subset\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_{m})
,
for some
m\in\mathbb{N}
.
But then the rational primes
p\equiv 1\bmod m
split in
K/\mathbb{Q}
, and so, in order to find an integer solution to
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f(t)
it suffices to find
t\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f(t)
is a prime congruent to
1
modulo
m
. It is clear that this approach cannot be made to work
for 100% of polynomials, since
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f(t)
may be soluble even if
f
does not take prime values in a given residue class. In contrast to this, our proof of Theorem
1.6
is based on estimates for the average number of representations of
f(n)
as a value of the norm form, as described in Theorem
8.5
.

Combining Theorem
1.6
with (
1.7
) leads to the following immediate consequence.

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and let
S_{d}^{+}(H)=\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(H):c_{0}>0\}
.
Then, for any finite extension
K/\mathbb{Q}
, we have

\frac{1}{\#S_{d}^{+}(H)}\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{+}(H):\text{$\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)$ is soluble over $\mathbb{Z}$}\right\}=(1+o(1))\prod_{p}\sigma_{p},

as
H\to\infty
,
where
\sigma_{p}
is the probability that
the equation
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)
is soluble over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
, for a
coefficient vector
\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{d+1}
.

This directly improves on

[
34
, Cor. 7.2]
, which only deals with the special case
K=\mathbb{Q}(i)
and proves that the left-hand side exceeds

\frac{56}{100}
,
for sufficiently large
H
.

In Theorem
8.5
, we shall prove a stronger version of Theorem
1.6
, in which we can almost always produce at least
H^{\Delta}
integer solutions to the equation

\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)
, for an appropriate constant
\Delta>0
depending only on
d
and
e
.
This quantification will permit us to
establish a weak form of Zariski density, for
100\%
of admissible polynomials.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field of degree
e\geqslant 2

and let
D,L\geqslant 1
. Then,
as admissible degree
d
coefficient vectors are ordered by height,

2.
Reduction to equidistribution in progressions

This section contains the key new technical tool in this paper. It shows that
controlling averages of a general arithmetic function over values of random polynomials can be done successfully
if the function is well distributed in arithmetic progressions.
We begin by giving a simpler version of the main technical result, which better conveys the structure.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let
d\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed, let
x,H\geqslant 2
with
x\leqslant H^{1/(100d)}
, and let
F:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
be a
1
-bounded sequence satisfying

(2.1)

\displaystyle\max_{1\leqslant u\leqslant q\leqslant x^{d}}\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}I\subset[-2H^{1.01},2H^{1.01}]\textnormal{ interval }\\
|I|>H^{0.99}\end{subarray}}\frac{q}{|I|}\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}F(n)\right|\ll(\log H)^{-10000d}.

Then, one has

(2.2)

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{n\leqslant x}F(f(n))\right|\ll x(\log x)^{-1000}

for almost all
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
of degree
d
having coefficients bounded by
H
in modulus.

An assumption of the form (
2.1
) is very natural, since if
d=1
, the conclusion (
2.2
) implies that
F
is equidistributed in almost all arithmetic progressions
u\bmod q
with
q\leqslant x^{d}
.
It turns out that the the simple hypothesis (
2.1
) is too demanding to be useful in proving Theorems
1.2
,
1.5
and
1.6
, which will require
a stronger (but more technical) version, given in Theorem
2.2
. On the other hand,
Corollary
2.1
is a relatively simple application of the second moment method; see footnotes
3
,
4
,
5
for various simplifications compared to the proof of Theorem
2.2
.

In order to prove Theorems
1.5
and
1.2
, we wish to obtain (
2.2
) (with
1000
replaced by an arbitrary constant) for the functions
F(n)=\lambda(n)
and
F(n)=(\Lambda(n)-\widetilde{\Lambda}(n))/(\log x)
, where
\widetilde{\Lambda}
is a simpler model function for the von Mangoldt function. (The function

\widetilde{\Lambda}
shares a similar distribution to
\Lambda
on arithmetic progressions, so that
F
has mean
o(1)
.)
If
x=(\log H)^{A}
for some fixed
A>0
, then the hypothesis (
2.1
)
follows directly from the prime number theorem in short intervals and arithmetic progressions; in particular, this would already lead to a simpler
2
2

2

Andrew Granville recently informed us that he has developed a similar argument in an unpublished note.
proof of
[
34
, Thm. 1.9]
and
[
39
, Thm. 2.12]
. However, since we have
x=H^{c/d}
for some
c>0
in Theorems
1.5
and
1.2
, we cannot hope to prove (
2.1
); the hypothesis would actually be false if
L(s,\chi)
had a zero too close to the
1
-line for some character
\chi\bmod q
. We therefore need a refinement of Corollary
2.1
that only requires equidistribution for moduli
q
that lie outside a sparse set of integers. Moreover, the values of the residue class
u\bmod q
also need to be constrained somewhat in (
2.1
), since if
u
and
q
share a prime divisor larger than
\exp(\sqrt{\log x})
, then the mean of
\Lambda(n)-\widetilde{\Lambda}(n)
is not small in the progression
n\equiv u\bmod q
. To approach Theorem
1.6

F
to be divisor-bounded, since we will take

to be a suitably normalised version of the representation function of a norm form. Finally, for Theorems

Taken together,

Theorem 2.2

(Equidistribution controls sums for almost all polynomials)
.

Let
A\geqslant 1
,
\varepsilon>0
and
0\leqslant k<\ell\leqslant d
be fixed. Let
H\geqslant H_{0}(A,d)
and
(\log H)^{2A}\leqslant x\leqslant H^{1/(2d)}
. Let
F:\mathbb{Z}\to\mathbb{C}
be a sequence such that

(1)

|F(n)|\leqslant\tau_{B}(n)

for all
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and for
some
B\geqslant 1
satisfying

10dB^{4d}\leqslant A

(2)

There exists a set
\mathscr{Q}\subset[1,x^{d}]\cap\mathbb{Z}
such that
\sum_{q\in\mathscr{Q}}q^{-1/(8d)}\ll(\log x)^{-3A}

and such that for any

\displaystyle\max_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant u\leqslant q\\

n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}F(n)\right|\ll(\log H)^{-10Ad}.

Then, for any polynomial
g\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
of degree

with coefficients in

such that

, and for any coefficients

we have

(2.3)

\displaystyle\sup_{x^{\prime}\in[x/2,x]}\sum_{|a|,|b|\leqslant H}\left|\sum_{1\leqslant n\leqslant x^{\prime}}\alpha_{n}F(an^{k}+bn^{\ell}+g(n))\right|^{2}\ll H^{2}x^{2}(\log x)^{-A}.

Preparatory results on the anatomy of integers

Lemma 2.3
.

Fix any
b,c\in\mathbb{N}
and let
q_{1},q_{2}\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for all
x\geqslant 1
we have

\#\left\{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x])^{2}:\gcd(n_{1},n_{2})=1,q_{1}\mid n_{1}^{b}-n_{2}^{b},q_{2}\mid n_{1}^{c}\right\}\ll\frac{x^{2}}{(q_{1}q_{2})^{1/(2\max\{b,c\}})}.

Proof.

The coprimality of
n_{1},n_{2}
allows us to write
the congruence as
(n_{2}/n_{1})^{b}\equiv 1\bmod{q_{1}}
.
Thus, the cardinality is at most

\displaystyle\mathbf{1}_{q_{1}\leqslant x^{b}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\bmod{q_{1}}\\
\lambda^{b}\equiv 1\bmod{q_{1}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leqslant x\\
q_{2}\mid n_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{2}\leqslant x\\
n_{2}\equiv\lambda n_{1}\bmod{q_{1}}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle\ll\left(\frac{x}{q_{1}}+\mathbf{1}_{q_{1}\leqslant x^{b}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\lambda\bmod{q_{1}}\\
\lambda^{b}\equiv 1\bmod{q_{1}}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leqslant x\\
q_{2}\mid n_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}1

(2.4)

\displaystyle\ll(2b)^{\omega(q_{1})}\left(\frac{x}{q_{1}}+\mathbf{1}_{q_{1}\leqslant x^{b}}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n_{1}\leqslant x\\
q_{2}\mid n_{1}^{c}\end{subarray}}1,

since the congruence
\lambda^{b}\equiv 1\bmod{p^{\alpha}}
has at most
2b
solutions for any prime power
p^{\alpha}
.
Indeed, if
p
is odd, there are at most
b
solutions by the existence of primitive roots modulo
p^{\alpha}
,
and if
p=2
, then the claim follows from the fact that
(\mathbb{Z}/2^{\alpha}\mathbb{Z})^{\times}
is generated by
-1
and
5
.
The standard estimate
\omega(q)=o(\log q)
gives
(2b)^{\omega(q_{1})}\ll q_{1}^{0.01}
. If

q_{1}\leqslant x^{b}
, then it gives
(2b)^{\omega(q_{1})}\ll x^{0.009}
. Hence,

(2b)^{\omega(q_{1})}\left(\frac{x}{q_{1}}+\mathbf{1}_{q_{1}\leqslant x^{b}}\right)\ll\frac{x}{q_{1}^{0.99}}+x^{0.009}\left(\frac{x^{b}}{q_{1}}\right)^{0.99/b}\leqslant\frac{x}{q_{1}^{0.99}}+\frac{x^{0.999}}{q_{1}^{0.99/b}}\leqslant\frac{2x}{q_{1}^{1/(2b)}}.

It remains to bound the sum over
n_{1}
in (
2.1
). To do so
we let
q_{2}^{\prime}=\prod_{p\mid q_{2}}p^{\alpha_{p}}
, where
\alpha_{p}=\lceil v_{p}(q_{2})/c\rceil
.
This means that

q_{2}\mid n_{1}^{c}
if and only if
q_{2}^{\prime}\mid n_{1}
. Thus, the sum over
n_{1}
is at most
x/q_{2}^{\prime}
. By definition we have
q_{2}^{\prime}\geqslant q_{2}^{1/c}
,
which exceeds
q_{2}^{1/(2\max\{b,c\})}
. ∎

Lemma 2.4
.

Fix any
b,c\in\mathbb{N}
. Then for all
x\geqslant 1
and
q\in\mathbb{N}

we have

\#\left\{\mathbf{n}\in(\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x])^{2}:q\mid(n_{1}^{b}-n_{2}^{b})n_{1}^{c}\right\}\ll x^{2}q^{-\frac{1}{8\max\{b,c\}}}.

Proof.

We classify all
(n_{1},n_{2})
according to the value of
r=\gcd(n_{1},n_{2})
. Letting
m_{1}=n_{1}/r,m_{2}=n_{2}/r

we see that the divisibility condition is equivalent to
q(r)=q/\gcd(q,r^{b+c})
dividing
(m_{1}^{b}-m_{2}^{b})m_{1}^{c}
.
Let
\lambda=2\max\{b,c\}
.
The coprimality of
m_{1},m_{2}

implies that
m_{1}^{b}-m_{2}^{b}
and
m_{1}^{c}
are coprime. Hence, the count is

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{r\leqslant x}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q_{1},q_{2})\in\mathbb{N}^{2}\\
q_{1}q_{2}=q(r)\\
\gcd(q_{1},q_{2})=1\end{subarray}}\#\left\{\mathbf{m}\in(\mathbb{N}\cap[1,x/r])^{2}:\gcd(m_{1},m_{2})=1,q_{1}\mid m_{1}^{b}-m_{2}^{b},q_{2}\mid m_{1}^{c}\right\}.

It follows from Lemma
2.3
that the summand is

O((x/r)^{2}(q_{1}q_{2})^{-1/\lambda})
.
Thus the count is

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}\frac{x^{2}\tau(q(r))}{r^{2}q(r)^{1/\lambda}}\ll x^{2}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}r^{-2}q(r)^{-1/(2\lambda)},

by the divisor bound.

Now define the arithmetic function
f_{\lambda}
through
n^{1/(2\lambda)}=\sum_{k\mid n}f_{\lambda}(k).

In particular,

f_{\lambda}(n)=\sum_{k\mid n}\mu(k)(n/k)^{1/(2\lambda)}=n^{1/(2\lambda)}\prod_{p\mid n}(1-p^{-1/(2\lambda)})\in\left[0,n^{1/(2\lambda)}\right].

This gives

\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}r^{-2}q(r)^{-1/(2\lambda)}=q^{-1/(2\lambda)}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}r^{-2}\gcd(q,r^{b+c})^{1/(2\lambda)}=q^{-1/(2\lambda)}\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}r^{-2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\mid q\\
k\mid r^{b+c}\end{subarray}}f_{\lambda}(k).

Define
k^{\prime}=\prod_{p\mid k}p^{\beta_{p}}
, where

\beta_{p}=\lceil v_{p}(k)/(b+c)\rceil
. In particular,
k\mid r^{b+c}
if and only if

k^{\prime}\mid r
. Thus, the sum over
r
turns into

\sum_{k\mid q}f_{\lambda}(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}r\in\mathbb{N}\\
k^{\prime}\mid r\end{subarray}}r^{-2}\ll\sum_{k\mid q}\frac{f_{\lambda}(k)}{k^{\prime 2}}\leqslant\sum_{k\mid q}k^{\frac{1}{2\lambda}-\frac{2}{b+c}},

owing to the bound
k^{\prime}\geqslant k^{1/(b+c)}
. We clearly have
\frac{1}{2\lambda}\leqslant\frac{2}{b+c}
, whence

\sum_{r\in\mathbb{N}}r^{-2}q(r)^{-1/(2\lambda)}\ll q^{-1/(2\lambda)}\tau(q)\ll q^{-1/(4\lambda)},

which is sufficient.
∎

We will also need the following simple lemma on sums of reciprocals of large divisors of a given integer.

Lemma 2.5
.

For all
q\in\mathbb{N}
and
z\geqslant 1
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q\\
d\geqslant z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\ll z^{-1/2}\exp\left(\frac{4(\log(3q))^{1/2}}{(\log\log(3q))^{3/2}}\right).

Proof.

Using Rankin’s trick, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q\\
d\geqslant z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\leqslant z^{-1/2}\prod_{p\mid q}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{1/2}}+\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{p^{3/2}}+\cdots\right)=z^{-1/2}\prod_{p\mid q}\left(1-\frac{1}{p^{1/2}}\right)^{-1}.

The inequality
(1-t^{-1})^{-1}\leqslant 1+t+4t^{2}
holds for
0<t\leqslant 1/\sqrt{2}
.
Moreover, we have
1+t\leqslant\mathrm{e}^{t}
for any
t\geqslant 0
. Hence

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q\\
d\geqslant z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}

\displaystyle\leqslant z^{-1/2}\prod_{p\mid q}\left(1+\frac{1}{p^{1/2}}+\frac{4}{p}\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant z^{-1/2}\exp\left(\sum_{p\mid q}(p^{-1/2}+4p^{-1})\right)

\displaystyle\leqslant z^{-1/2}\exp\left(\sum_{p\leqslant\omega(q)}(p^{-1/2}+4p^{-1})\right).

By partial summation and the prime number theorem, there exists an absolute constant
C>2
such that

\sum_{p\leqslant u}(p^{-1/2}+4p^{-1})\leqslant\frac{3u^{1/2}}{\log u},

for all
u>C
.
Moreover, it follows from
[
28
, Thm. 2.10]
that

\omega(q)\leqslant 1.1(\log q)/(\log\log q)
whenever
\omega(q)\geqslant C^{\prime}
, provided
C^{\prime}
is a large enough constant. This concludes the proof when
\omega(q)>\max\{C,C^{\prime}\}
. In the opposite case, we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q\\
d\geqslant z\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{d}\leqslant z^{-1/2}\exp\left(\sum_{p\leqslant\omega(q)}(p^{-1/2}+4p^{-1})\right)=O_{C,C^{\prime}}\left(z^{-1/2}\right),

which is sufficient.
∎

Lemma 2.6
.

For all
a,c\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
,
b\in\mathbb{Z}
and
x_{1},x_{2}\geqslant 1
we have

\displaystyle\#\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-x_{1},x_{1}]:\gcd(an+b,c)>x_{2}\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip-142.26378pt\ll\frac{x_{1}\gcd(a,c)}{x_{2}^{1/2}}\exp\left(\frac{4(\log(3|c|))^{1/2}}{(\log\log(3|c|))^{3/2}}\right)+\tau(c).

Proof.

Letting

we can bound the cardinality in the lemma by

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2}<t\leqslant|a|x_{1}+|b|\\
t\mid c\end{subarray}}\#\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-x_{1},x_{1}]:t\mid an+b\}

\displaystyle\quad=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2}<t\leqslant|a|x_{1}+|b|\\
t\mid c\end{subarray}}\#\left\{n\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[-x_{1},x_{1}]:\frac{t}{\gcd(a,t)}\mid\frac{a}{\gcd(a,t)}n+\frac{b}{\gcd(a,t)}\right\}.

By the coprimality of

and

, we can bound this by

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x_{2}<t\leqslant|a|x_{1}+|b|\\

The proof follows on invoking Lemma
2.5
.

Lemma 2.7

Let

By the union bound it suffices to show that the set of

Dealing with the first property is trivial, since there are

Furthermore, there are
\ll x^{2}/(\log x)^{A}

Lemma 3.4
.

Proof.

We may assume that

in (

Proof of Lemma
3.4
.

Let
T=(\log x)^{2A}x/h

and

(3.7)

\beta=\frac{2A\log\log x}{\varepsilon\log x}.

Let
F(s)=\frac{L(2s,\chi)}{L(s,\chi)}
or

. Write

. Then

or

, respectively. By Perron’s formula, for

Recalling the choice of

, the error term here is
\ll h(\log x)^{-A}
.
The mean value theorem gives

. Thus

We now replace the contour of integration with a contour
C
, which we proceed to define, and which is based on the Hooley–Huxley contour from Ramachandra’s paper
[

The contour

consists of vertical lines

, and of horizontal lines that connect them together to form one polygonal line.
The

are determined as follows:

(i)

, for

where

is the largest integer

such that

.

(ii)

\sigma_{j}
is

plus the largest real part of those zeros of

that have imaginary part in

. If there are no such zeros, we put

.

Since

possesses the zero-free region

,

,
where

, for

.

We now shift the line of integration to

\displaystyle M=\begin{cases}h\mathbf{1}_{\chi=\chi_{0}}&\text{ if $a_{n}=\Lambda(n)\chi(n)$},\\
0&\text{ if $a_{n}=\lambda(n)\chi(n)$.}\end{cases}

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{x\leqslant n\leqslant x+h}a_{n}-M\right|

\displaystyle\ll h\int_{C}|F(s)|x^{\real(s)-1}\mathrm{d}s+h(\log x)^{-A}

\displaystyle\ll h(\log x)\int_{C}x^{\real(s)-1}\mathrm{d}s+h(\log x)^{-A},

where we have observed that

,
since

throughout

\displaystyle\frac{1}{|L(s,\chi)|}\ll\log x,\quad\frac{|L^{\prime}(s,\chi)|}{|L(s,\chi)|}\ll\log x

by

, since

to the right of any zeros of

.

Let us consider the contribution

to the first term in (

\ll hx^{\sigma_{j}-1}\log x,

so it now suffices to show that

for any fixed

If

.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\kappa>0
and
D>1
. There exist two sequences of real numbers
(\lambda_{k}^{+})
and
(\lambda_{k}^{-})
, supported on square-free numbers and depending
only on
\kappa
and
D
,
with the following properties:

\lambda^{\pm}_{1}=1,\ \ |\lambda^{\pm}_{k}|\leqslant 1\ \ \ \forall k<D,\ \ \lambda^{\pm}_{k}=0\ \ \ \forall k\geqslant D,

and for any integer
n\geqslant 1
,

\sum_{k\mid n}\lambda^{+}_{k}\leqslant\mathbf{1}_{n=1}\leqslant\sum_{k\mid n}\lambda^{-}_{k}.

Let
P(w)=\prod_{p\leqslant w}p
and let
s=\log D/\log w
.
Then,
for any multiplicative function
h(k)
with
0\leqslant h(k)<1
, satisfying

\prod_{a\leqslant p<b}(1-h(p))^{-1}\leqslant\left(\frac{\log b}{\log a}\right)^{\kappa}\left(1+\frac{K}{\log a}\right),

for all
2\leqslant a<b\leqslant D
, we have

\sum_{d\mid P(w)}\lambda^{\pm}_{k}h(k)=\left(1+O\left(\mathrm{e}^{-s}\left(1+\frac{K}{\log w}\right)^{10}\right)\right)\prod_{p\leqslant w}(1-h(p)),

the implied constant depending only on
\kappa
.

Proof of Lemma
5.1
.

Let

\displaystyle h(p)

\displaystyle=\mathbf{1}_{p\leqslant w}\frac{\#\{u\in\mathbb{F}_{p}:f_{1}(u)\cdots f_{r}(u)=0\}}{p},

and extend
h
multiplicatively to all square-free integers.
In proving (
5.2
) we can assume that

h(p)
is strictly less than
1
for all primes
p\leqslant w
.
This is because in the opposite case both
\Lambda_{w}(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))
and
\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}}(x)
would be zero,
for
n\leqslant x
.
This implies that
f_{1}\cdots f_{r}
does not have a fixed prime divisor
p\leqslant w
.
Hence, we have
h(p)\leqslant dr/p
whenever
p\leqslant w
, by Lagrange’s theorem. It follows from Mertens’ theorem that

\prod_{a\leqslant p<b}(1-h(p))^{-1}\leqslant\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}a\leqslant p<b\\
p\leqslant w\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{dr}{p}\right)^{-1}\leqslant\left(\frac{\log b}{\log a}\right)^{dr}\left(1+\frac{K_{d,r}}{\log a}\right),

where
K_{d,r}
depends at most on
d
and
r
.
Applying Lemma
5.2
with
\kappa=dr
yields

\displaystyle\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda_{w}(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))

\displaystyle\leqslant\left(\frac{W}{\varphi(W)}\right)^{r}\sum_{k\mid W}\lambda_{k}^{+}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\\
f_{1}\cdots f_{r}(n)\equiv 0\bmod k\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{W}{\varphi(W)}\right)^{r}\sum_{k\mid W}\lambda_{k}^{+}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\bmod k\\
f_{1}\cdots f_{r}(m)\equiv 0\bmod k\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\\
\ n\equiv m\bmod k\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{W}{\varphi(W)}\right)^{r}\sum_{k\mid W}\lambda_{k}^{+}\left(h(k)x+O(kh(k))\right)

\displaystyle=(1+O(\mathrm{e}^{-s}))\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}}(x)x+O\left(\left(\frac{W}{\varphi(W)}\right)^{r}D^{2}\right),

where
s=(\log D)/(\log w)
and we used the trivial bound
h(k)\leqslant 1
in the error term.
Taking
D=x^{1/3}
and using the trivial bound
\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}}(x)\ll(W/\varphi(W))^{r}\ll(\log x)^{r}
,
we deduce that

\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda_{w}(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))\leqslant\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\ldots,f_{r}}(x)x+O\left(\frac{x(\log x)^{r}}{\mathrm{e}^{s}}+x^{2/3}(\log x)^{r}\right).

Recalling that
w=\exp(\sqrt{\log x})
, we obtain

\mathrm{e}^{s}=\exp((1/3)\sqrt{\log x})\gg_{A}(\log x)^{A+r},
whence

\sum_{n\leqslant x}\Lambda_{w}(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))\leqslant\mathfrak{S}_{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}}(x)x+O\left(x(\log x)^{-A}\right).

A symmetric argument proves the corresponding lower bound, thereby concluding the proof of (
5.2
).
∎

5.2.
Concluding the proof

We are now ready to prove Theorem
1.4
using Lemma
5.1
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that the combinatorial cube
\mathscr{C}
has dimension two whose constant coefficient is not fixed to be
0
.

By Lemma
5.1
, the triangle inequality and Chebyshev’s inequality, it suffices to prove

(5.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{f_{1},\dots,f_{r}\in\mathscr{C}}\left|\sum_{n\leqslant x}\alpha_{n}\left(\Lambda(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda(f_{r}(n))-\Lambda_{w}(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))\right)\right|^{2}\ll\frac{x^{2}H^{2r}}{(\log x)^{A}},

uniformly for
x\in[H^{c},2H^{c}]
, for any
coefficients
|\alpha_{n}|\leqslant 1
. In our application we shall only be interested in the sequence
\alpha_{n}=1
, but the proof requires us to allow general coefficients.

We first claim that it suffices to prove (
5.3
) for
r=1
, that is, for any coefficients
|\alpha_{n}|\leqslant 1
, we have

(5.4)

\sum_{f\in\mathscr{C}}\left|\sum_{n\leqslant x}\alpha_{n}(\Lambda-\Lambda_{w})(f(n))\right|^{2}\ll\frac{x^{2}H^{2}}{(\log x)^{A}},

uniformly for
x\in[H^{c},2H^{c}]
. To check this, we write

S_{r}(x,H;\alpha_{n})
for the sum in (
5.3
) that is to be estimated, and we argue by induction on
r
.
When
r=1
the desired estimate (
5.3
) is just (
5.4
).
Suppose now that
r>1
and write

\Lambda(f_{r}(n))=\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))+(\Lambda-\Lambda_{w})(f_{r}(n))
.
Then, on applying the triangle inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle S_{r}(x,H;\alpha_{n})\leqslant~

\displaystyle(\log x)^{2}\sum_{f_{r}\in\mathscr{C}}S_{r-1}(x,H;\beta_{n})+(\log x)^{2(r-1)}\sum_{f_{1},\dots,f_{r-1}\in\mathscr{C}}S_{1}(x,H;\gamma_{n}),

where
\beta_{n}=\alpha_{n}\Lambda_{w}(f_{r}(n))/(\log x)
and
\gamma_{n}=\alpha_{n}\Lambda(f_{1}(n))\cdots\Lambda(f_{r-1}(n))/(\log x)^{r-1}
. The desired bound now follows from the induction hypothesis, on adjusting the choice of
A
.

It remains to prove (
5.4
).
Suppose
\mathscr{C}
has the
k
th and
\ell
th coordinate as its two free coordinates, with
k<\ell
. It then suffices to prove that

(5.5)

\sum_{|a|,|b|\leqslant H}\left|\sum_{n\leqslant x}\alpha_{n}(\Lambda-\Lambda_{w})(an^{k}+bn^{\ell}+g(n))\right|^{2}\ll\frac{x^{2}H^{2}}{(\log x)^{A}},

uniformly for
sequences
|\alpha_{n}|\leqslant 1
, and for
polynomials
g\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
of degree
\leqslant d
having coefficients in
[-H,H]
, and with
g(0)\neq 0
.
The condition
g(0)\neq 0
can indeed be imposed, since if
k=0
, we can change the value of
g(0)
by
O(1)
without changing the validity of (
5.5
). Alternatively, if
k\geqslant 1
and
g(0)=0
, then
\mathscr{C}
consists of polynomials whose constant coefficient is fixed to be
0
, a case that was excluded.

We apply Theorem
2.2
to the
1
-bounded function
F(n)=(\Lambda(n)-\Lambda_{w}(n))/(\log x)
. The desired conclusion now follows, provided that the hypotheses of that theorem hold. Assumption (1) clearly holds. For assumption (2), we apply Lemma
5.2
to obtain that, for any
h\geqslant x^{2/3}
and
1\leqslant a\leqslant q\leqslant x^{1/3}
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\leqslant n\leqslant x+h\\
n\equiv a\bmod q\end{subarray}}\Lambda_{w}(n)

\displaystyle=(1+O((\log x)^{-100Ad}))\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(a,q,W)=1}\frac{W}{\varphi(W)}\frac{h}{q}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid W\\
p\nmid q\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+O((\log x)^{-100Ad}))\mathbf{1}_{\gcd(a,q,W)=1}\frac{h}{\varphi(q)}.

Note also that
\gcd(a,q,W)=\gcd(a,q)
unless
\gcd(a,q)
has a prime factor
>w
, in which case
\gcd(a,q)>w=\exp(\sqrt{\log x})
.

We now conclude from Proposition
3.2
that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\leqslant n\leqslant x+h\\
n\equiv a\bmod q\end{subarray}}(\Lambda(n)-\Lambda_{w}(n))\right|\ll\frac{h}{q(\log x)^{A}}

provided that
x\geqslant h\geqslant x^{1-5/24+\varepsilon}
,
1\leqslant a\leqslant q\leqslant x^{5/24}
,
\gcd(a,q)\leqslant\exp(\sqrt{\log x})
and
q
is not a multiple of any element of the set
\mathscr{Q}
present in Proposition
3.2
. Assumption (2) of Theorem

2.2
directly follows, provided that
(2Hx^{d})^{1-5/24}\leqslant H^{1-\varepsilon}
for some small
\varepsilon>0
, which indeed holds by the assumption that
x<H^{c}
for some fixed
c<5/(19d)
. Now (
5.4
) follows.
∎

6.
Polynomials and norm forms modulo prime powers

6.1.
Polynomials in one variable

To begin with, let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a polynomial of degree
d
and let

(6.1)

\lambda_{f}(q)=\#\left\{u\in\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z}:f(u)\equiv 0\bmod{q}\right\},

for any integer
q\geqslant 1
.
The
content
of
f
is defined to be the greatest common divisor of the coefficients of
f
.
We shall need some upper bounds for
\lambda_{f}(p^{k})
for prime power moduli.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
k\in\mathbb{N}
. Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a polynomial of degree
d\geqslant 1
. Assume that
f
is separable and let
\sigma
be the
p
-adic valuation of the content of
f
.
If
\sigma\geqslant k
then
\lambda_{f}(p^{k})=p^{k}
. If
\sigma<k
then

\lambda_{f}(p^{k})\leqslant d\min\left\{p^{k(1-\frac{1}{d})+\frac{\sigma}{d}},p^{k-1}\right\}.

Additionally, if
p
does not divide the discriminant of
f
, then

\displaystyle\lambda_{f}(p^{k})\leqslant d.

Proof.

The final bound follows directly from Lagrange’s theorem and Hensel’s lemma.
For the remaining bounds, we first proceed under the assumption
\sigma=0
,
so that the content is coprime to
p
. But then the first bound follows from Corollary 2 and Equation (44) of Stewart
[
35
]
and the second bound is a consequence of Lagrange’s theorem. Now suppose that

\sigma\geqslant 1
. If
\sigma\geqslant k
then
\lambda_{f}(p^{k})=p^{k}
. If
\sigma<k
then
\lambda_{f}(p^{k})=p^{\sigma}\lambda_{g}(p^{k-\sigma})
, where
g=p^{-\sigma}f
has content coprime to
p
, and we can apply our existing bounds.
∎

Next, let
\tau
denote the divisor function.
We will make frequent use of the following bound, which concerns its average size at polynomial arguments.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
\delta>0
and
A,C>0
. Let
f\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
be a
separable
polynomial of degree
d\geqslant 1
. Let
\|f\|
be the maximum modulus of the coefficients of
f
and assume that the content of
f
is at most
(\log x)^{A}
. Then there exist a positive constant
K
, depending only on
\delta
,
d
and
C
, such that for
x\geqslant\|f\|^{\delta}
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\end{subarray}}\tau(|f(n)|)^{C}\ll x(\log x)^{K}.

Proof.

Let
h
be the content of
f
, so that
f(t)=hg(t)
, where
g\in\mathbb{Z}[t]
has content
1
.
Then
\tau(|f(n)|)^{C}\leqslant\tau(h)^{C}\tau(|g(n)|)^{C}\ll(\log x)\tau(|g(n)|)^{C}
,
since
h\leqslant(\log x)^{A}
,
on applying the trivial bound for the divisor function.
Landreau’s inequality
[
26
]
states that for any
C>0
and
\varepsilon>0
there exists
C^{\prime}>0
such that

\displaystyle\tau(n)^{C}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\mid n\\
m\leqslant n^{\varepsilon}\end{subarray}}\tau(m)^{C^{\prime}}.

Applying this together with the assumption that
\|g\|\leqslant\|f\|\leqslant x^{1/\delta}
, we have

\displaystyle\tau(|g(n)|)^{C}\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\mid g(n)\\
m\leqslant x^{1/2}\end{subarray}}\tau(m)^{C^{\prime}}

for some constant
C^{\prime}>0
depending only on
\delta
,
d
and
C
. Hence, for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\end{subarray}}\tau(|f(n)|)^{C}

\displaystyle\ll\log x\sum_{m\leqslant x^{1/2}}\tau(m)^{C^{\prime}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\\
g(n)\equiv 0\bmod m\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=x\log x\sum_{m\leqslant x^{1/2}}\frac{\tau(m)^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(m)}{m}+O(x^{1/2+\varepsilon}),

by the divisor bound and
where
\lambda_{g}(m)
is given by
(
6.1
).

We may write

\sum_{m\leqslant x^{1/2}}\frac{\tau(m)^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(m)}{m}\leqslant\sum_{\ell\mid D^{\infty}}\frac{\tau(\ell)^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(\ell)}{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m^{\prime}\leqslant x^{1/2}/\ell\\
\gcd(m^{\prime},D)=1\end{subarray}}\frac{\tau(m^{\prime})^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(m^{\prime})}{m^{\prime}},

where
D
is the
non-zero discriminant of
g
.
Lemma
6.1
implies that

\lambda_{g}(m^{\prime})\leqslant d^{\omega(m^{\prime})}\leqslant\tau(m^{\prime})^{d},

when
\gcd(m^{\prime},D)=1
.
We conclude that

\displaystyle\sum_{m\leqslant x^{1/2}}\frac{\tau(m)^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(m)}{m}

\displaystyle\ll(\log x)^{O_{C^{\prime},d}(1)}\sum_{\ell\mid D^{\infty}}\frac{\tau(\ell)^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(\ell)}{\ell}

\displaystyle=(\log x)^{O_{C^{\prime},d}(1)}\prod_{p\mid D}\left(1+\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{\tau(p^{j})^{C^{\prime}}\lambda_{g}(p^{j})}{p^{j}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll(\log x)^{O_{C^{\prime},d}(1)}\prod_{p\mid D}\left(1+O_{C^{\prime},d}\left(\frac{1}{p}\right)\right),

by Lemma
6.1
.
Since
|D|\ll\|g\|^{d(d-1)}\leqslant x^{d(d-1)/\delta}
, this leads to the claimed bound.
∎

6.2.
Norm forms modulo prime powers

In this section we summarise some facts about the function

(6.2)

\rho(p^{k},a)=\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{e}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\equiv a\bmod{p^{k}}\right\},

for
a\in\mathbb{Z}
and a prime power
p^{k}
,
a detailed study of which has been carried out in
[
8
, §4]
.
Intimately related to
\rho(p^{k},a)
are the coefficients of the Dedekind zeta function associated to
K
.
Let
\n\mathfrak{a}=\#\mathfrak{o}_{K}/\mathfrak{a}
be the norm of an integral ideal
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{K}
.
Then we define

\zeta_{K}(s)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{K}\\
\mathfrak{a}\neq(0)\end{subarray}}\frac{1}{(\n\mathfrak{a})^{s}}=\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\frac{r_{K}(m)}{m^{s}},

for
s\in\mathbb{C}
such that
\real(s)>1
, with

r_{K}(m)=\#\{\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{K}:\n\mathfrak{a}=m\}.

This function is multiplicative and
the Dedekind zeta function admits a meromorphic continuation to all of
\mathbb{C}
with a simple
pole at
s=1
.
To describe its behaviour at prime powers, let
p
be any rational prime
and recall that the principal ideal
(p)
factorises into a product of
prime ideals in
\mathfrak{o}_{K}
. That is,

(6.3)

(p)=\mathfrak{p}_{1}^{e_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r}^{e_{r}},

where
e_{i}=e_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(p),r=r(p)\in\mathbb{N}
and
each
\mathfrak{p}_{i}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{K}
is a prime ideal satisfying
\n\mathfrak{p}_{i}=p^{f_{i}}
,
for some
f_{i}=f_{\mathfrak{p}_{i}}(p)\in\mathbb{N}
.
The prime
p
ramifies if and only if
p
divides the discriminant
D_{K}
of
K
.
Hence
e_{1}=\cdots=e_{r}=1
unless
p\mid D_{K}
.
Moreover, on taking norms in (
6.3
), we have

\sum_{i=1}^{r}e_{i}f_{i}=\deg(K/\mathbb{Q})=e.

Thus

r_{K}(p^{k})=\#\left\{\mathfrak{p}_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots\mathfrak{p}_{r}^{m_{r}}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{K}:f_{1}m_{1}+\cdots+f_{r}m_{r}=k\right\},

for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
. In particular, we have

(6.4)

r_{K}(p^{k})\leqslant(1+k)^{e}.

We begin by studying the function (
6.2
) when
k=1
.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
p
be a prime and let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
, with
p\nmid a
.
Then

\rho(p,a)\leqslant p^{e-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}\left(1-\frac{1}{\n\mathfrak{p}}\right),

with equality if
p\nmid D_{K}
.

Proof.

We shall prove this result using basic properties of finite fields.
Let
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}
be the finite field with
p^{\ell}
elements and

\mathbb{F}_{p}
-basis
\{\tau_{1},\dots,\tau_{\ell}\}
.
Write

N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=N_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(y_{1}\tau_{1}+\cdots+y_{\ell}\tau_{\ell})

for the associated norm form.
Then, for any
a\in\mathbb{F}_{p}
, we claim that

(6.5)

\#\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ell}:N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=a\}=\begin{cases}1&\mbox{if $a=0$,}\\
\frac{p^{\ell}-1}{p-1}&\mbox{if $a\neq 0$.}\end{cases}

The case
a=0
follows since
N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=0
if and
only if
y_{1}=\cdots=y_{\ell}=0
. To handle the case
a\neq 0
we first prove that the norm form
N_{\ell}
assumes every non-zero value of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
.
To see this, let
\alpha
be a generator of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}^{*}
,
so that
\alpha
has order
p^{\ell}-1
.
The Galois group of
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}
is cyclic and generated by

x\mapsto x^{p}
.
Thus

N_{\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}/\mathbb{F}_{p}}(\alpha)=\alpha^{(p^{\ell}-1)/(p-1)}
.
This is a generator of
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
and therefore establishes the
claim.
Since multiplication by a fixed element in
\mathbb{F}_{p^{\ell}}
is injective,
this allows us to construct a bijection between any two sets

\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ell}:N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=b\}
and

\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ell}:N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=b^{\prime}\}
for
b,b^{\prime}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
.
Thus, we conclude the proof of (
6.5
) for
a\neq 0
by noting that

\displaystyle p^{\ell}-1

\displaystyle=\sum_{b\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}}\#\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ell}:N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=a\}=(p-1)\#\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{\ell}:N_{\ell}(\mathbf{y})=a\}.

Suppose that
p
factorises in
\mathfrak{o}_{K}
as
(
6.3
).
It follows from Dedekind’s theorem that the norm form
has a similar factorisation modulo
p
, and there exists a
non-singular
\mathbb{F}_{p}
-linear transformation

\mathbf{x}\mapsto(\mathbf{y}^{(1)},\cdots,\mathbf{y}^{(r)})
,
with
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=(y_{1}^{(i)},\dots,y_{f_{i}}^{(i)})
, such that

\nf(\mathbf{x})\equiv\prod_{i=1}^{r}N_{f_{i}}(\mathbf{y}^{(i)})^{e_{i}}\bmod{p}.

For any
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
let
G_{n}
denote the set of
n
th powers in
\mathbb{F}_{p}^{*}
.
Then

(6.6)

\displaystyle\#G_{n}=\frac{p-1}{\gcd(n,p-1)}.

Moreover, there are precisely
\gcd(n,p-1)
elements of
\mathbb{F}_{p}
with order dividing
n
.
Since
p\nmid a
, it follows that

\displaystyle\rho(p,a)

\displaystyle=\#\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{e}:\nf(\mathbf{x})=a\}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\in G_{e_{1}}\times\cdots\times G_{e_{r}}\\
a_{1}\cdots a_{r}=a\\
\end{subarray}}\prod_{i=1}^{r}\#\{\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{F}_{p}^{f_{i}}:N_{f_{i}}(\mathbf{y})^{e_{i}}=a_{i}\}.

On appealing to
(
6.5
), we deduce that

(6.7)

\rho(p,a)=M(a)\prod_{i=1}^{r}\gcd(e_{i},p-1)\left(\frac{p^{f_{i}}-1}{p-1}\right),

where
M(a)
is the number of

(a_{1},\dots,a_{r})\in G_{e_{1}}\times\cdots\times G_{e_{r}}
such that

a_{1}\cdots a_{r}=a.

Recalling (
6.6
), an argument involving
primitive roots readily reveals that

M(a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $a\not\in G_{d}$,}\\
\frac{\gcd(d,p-1)}{p-1}\prod_{1\leqslant i\leqslant r}\frac{p-1}{\gcd(e_{i},p-1)}&\text{ if $a\in G_{d}$,}\end{cases}

where
d=\gcd(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})
. Once inserted into (
6.7
), this
establishes that

\rho(p,a)=\begin{cases}0&\text{ if $a\not\in G_{\gcd(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})}$,}\\
\frac{\gcd(e_{1},\dots,e_{r},p-1)}{p-1}(p^{f_{1}}-1)\cdots(p^{f_{r}}-1)&\text{ if $a\in G_{\gcd(e_{1},\dots,e_{r})}$.}\end{cases}

If
p\nmid D_{K}
then
e_{1}=\cdots=e_{r}=1
and it follows that

\displaystyle\rho(p,a)

\displaystyle=\frac{(p^{f_{1}}-1)\cdots(p^{f_{r}}-1)}{(p-1)}=p^{e-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}(1-p^{-f_{1}})\cdots(1-p^{-f_{r}}),

as desired. On the other hand, if
p\mid D_{K}
then the upper bound follows on noting that by the above

(p-1)\rho(p,a)\leqslant p^{f_{1}+\cdots+f_{r}}\min\{e_{1},\dots,e_{r}\}\leqslant p^{e_{1}f_{1}+\cdots+e_{r}f_{r}}=p^{e}.

This completes the proof of the lemma.
∎

We claim that

(6.8)

\begin{split}\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}\left(1-\frac{1}{\n\mathfrak{p}}\right)&=1-\frac{r_{K}(p)-1}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\\
&\geqslant 1-\frac{e-1}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right)\end{split}

in the statement of the previous lemma. To see this, on multiplying out

Lemma 6.4

Let

\begin{split}\gamma(W_{1},n)&=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid P(w)\end{subarray}}\gamma(p^{k(x)},n)\\

7.3.
Some smooth number estimates

To begin with, let
\Psi(N,w)
be the number of
w
-smooth numbers
n\leqslant N
.

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
. For
(\log N)^{2}\leqslant w\leqslant N
, we have

\Psi(N,w)\ll Ns^{-(1+o_{s\to\infty}(1))s},

where
s=(\log N)/(\log w)
.

Proof.

This follows from

[
20
, Cor. 1.3]

in the survey of Hildebrand and Tenenbaum on smooth numbers.
∎

Using this we may deduce the following result, which deals with short intervals and also allows for a restriction to a given congruence class.

Lemma 7.4
.

Let
r\in\mathbb{N}
and let
a\in\mathbb{Z}
. Assume that

\sqrt{N-H}\leqslant H\leqslant N/2
and
r\leqslant\sqrt{H}
. Suppose, furthermore, that

\log N\leqslant w\leqslant\min\{\sqrt{H},\exp((\log N)/(\log\log N))\}
.
Then,

\max_{a\bmod r}\#\{N-H\leqslant n\leqslant N:n\equiv a\bmod{r},~p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant w\}\ll\frac{H}{r}\cdot s^{-s/5},

where
s=(\log N)/(\log w)
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi(N,H)
denote the quantity that is to be estimated.
We shall apply Harper’s Smooth Numbers Result 3 in
[
19
]
, with
y=w
,
X=N-H
,
Z=H
and
q=r
.
The hypotheses of this result are met if

\log(N-H)\leqslant w\leqslant N-H
and

rw\leqslant H\leqslant N-H
, all of which are implied by the hypotheses of the lemma.
Let
\tilde{s}=(\log(N-H))/(\log w)
.
Then, in the light of

[
19
, Eq. (2.1)]
, we deduce that

\varphi(N,H)\ll\frac{H}{r(N-H)}\cdot\Psi(N-H,w)\cdot\left(\frac{(N-H)r}{H}\right)^{1-\alpha}\log N,

with

\alpha=1-\frac{\log(\tilde{s}\log(\tilde{s}+1))}{\log w}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log w}\right)=1-\frac{(1+o_{\tilde{s}\to\infty}(1))\log\tilde{s}}{\log w}.

Moreover, Lemma
7.3
implies that

\Psi(N-H,w)\ll(N-H){\tilde{s}}^{-(1+o(1))\tilde{s}}.
Hence

\displaystyle\varphi(N,H)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{H}{r}\cdot{\tilde{s}}^{-(1+o_{\tilde{s}\to\infty}(1))\tilde{s}}\cdot\left(\frac{(N-H)r}{H}\right)^{\frac{(1+o_{\tilde{s}\to\infty}(1))\log\tilde{s}}{\log w}}\log N

\displaystyle\ll\frac{H}{r}\cdot{\tilde{s}}^{-(\frac{1}{4}+o_{\tilde{s}\to\infty}(1))\tilde{s}}\log N,

since the hypotheses of the lemma imply that

(N-H)r/H\leqslant(N-H)^{3/4}
.
Finally, let

s=(\log N)/(\log w)
.
Then

{\tilde{s}}^{-(\frac{1}{4}+o_{\tilde{s}\to\infty}(1))\tilde{s}}\log N\ll{s}^{-s/5},

since
N-H\geqslant N/2
and
\tilde{s}\geqslant(\log\log N)/2
.
The statement of the lemma follows.
∎

We may also use Lemma
7.3
to study the sum

(7.22)

\Sigma_{B}(z)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid k\Rightarrow p\leqslant w\\
k>z\end{subarray}}\frac{\tau(k)^{B}}{k},

for
B\geqslant 1
and
z>0
, which will appear later in the course of proving Proposition
7.2
.

Lemma 7.5
.

Let
B\geqslant 1
,
w\geqslant 2
and
z\geqslant w^{C_{B}}
, where
C_{B}
is a large enough constant. Then

\Sigma_{B}(z)\ll_{B}\exp\left(-\frac{\log z}{4\log w}\right).

Proof.

Let
s=(\log 2^{j})/(\log w)
.
If
(j\log 2)^{2}\leqslant w
then it follows from
Lemma

7.3
that

\Psi(2^{j},w)\ll 2^{j}s^{-9s/10}
.
If

w<(j\log 2)^{2}
, we can still crudely estimate

\Psi(2^{j},w)\leqslant\Psi(2^{j},(j\log 2)^{2})\ll 2^{9j/10}
. Hence it follows that

\Psi(2^{j},w)\ll 2^{9j/10}+2^{j}s^{-9s/10},

for any
j\geqslant 1
. Furthermore, we can write

s^{-s}=\exp\left(-s\log s\right)=\exp\left(-\frac{j\log 2(\log j+\log\log 2-\log\log w)}{\log w}\right).

Breaking into dyadic intervals and then applying the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, it follows that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{B}(z)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{Z}\\
2^{j}>z/2\end{subarray}}2^{-j}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2^{j}\leqslant n<2^{j+1}\\
p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant w\end{subarray}}\tau(n)^{B}

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{Z}\\
2^{j}>z/2\end{subarray}}2^{-j}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}2^{j}\leqslant n<2^{j+1}\\
p\mid n\Rightarrow p\leqslant w\end{subarray}}1\right)^{1/2}\left(\sum_{2^{j}\leqslant n<2^{j+1}}\tau(n)^{2B}\right)^{1/2}.

Now

\sum_{2^{j}\leqslant n<2^{j+1}}\tau(n)^{2B}\ll_{B}2^{j}j^{2^{2B-1}}.

Hence it follows that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{B}(z)

\displaystyle\ll_{B}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{Z}\\
2^{j}>z/2\end{subarray}}2^{-j/30}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{Z}\\
2^{j}>z/2\end{subarray}}j^{2^{2B-1}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\log 2)j\log j}{3\log w}\right)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\in\mathbb{Z}\\
2^{j}>z/2\end{subarray}}\exp\left(-\frac{(\log 2)j\log j}{4\log w}\right)+z^{-1/30},

on taking
C_{B}
large enough. The statement of the lemma easily follows.
∎

) and
(
7.15
).

Next,
it follows from multiplicativity that

c(w)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
p\mid k\Rightarrow p\mid P(w)\\
\gcd(k,q)\mid u\end{subarray}}\frac{g(k)\gcd(k,q)}{k}=\prod_{p\mid P(w)}\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p},

where
\alpha_{p}
is given by
(
7.14
) and

\lambda_{p}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geqslant 0\\
\min(j,v_{p}(q))\leqslant v_{p}(u)\end{subarray}}\frac{g(p^{j})p^{\min(j,v_{p}(q))}}{p^{j}}.

We proceed by studying
\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p}
for a given prime
p\mid P(w)
.

Note that
g(p^{j})=\tilde{r}_{K}(p^{j})-\tilde{r}_{K}(p^{j-1})
, if
j\geqslant 1
, where

\tilde{r}_{K}
is given by (
7.19
). In particular, it follows that
g(p^{j})=0
if
j>k(x)
.
Let
\ell=v_{p}(q)
, as before. Then we may write

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leqslant j\leqslant\min(\ell,v_{p}(u))\end{subarray}}g(p^{j})+p^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell<j\leqslant k(x)\\
\min(j,\ell)\leqslant v_{p}(u)\end{subarray}}\frac{g(p^{j})}{p^{j}}.

The first sum is
r_{K}(p^{\min(\ell,v_{p}(u))})
, since
\ell<k(x)
.
If
\ell>v_{p}(u)
then the second sum vanishes and it follows that

\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p}=\alpha_{p}r_{K}(p^{v_{p}(u)})=\gamma(p^{\ell},u),

by (
7.31
).
Alternatively, if
\ell\leqslant v_{p}(u)
then we deduce that

\displaystyle\lambda_{p}

\displaystyle=r_{K}(p^{\ell})+p^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell<j\leqslant k(x)\end{subarray}}\frac{\tilde{r}_{K}(p^{j})-\tilde{r}_{K}(p^{j-1})}{p^{j}}

\displaystyle=r_{K}(p^{\ell})+p^{\ell}\left(-\frac{r_{K}(p^{\ell})}{p^{\ell}}+\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leqslant j<k(x)\end{subarray}}\frac{r_{K}(p^{j})}{p^{j}}+\frac{\alpha_{p}^{-1}\beta_{p^{k(x)}}}{p^{k(x)}}\right).

But then, recalling (
7.14
) and (
7.15
), we deduce that

\displaystyle\alpha_{p}\lambda_{p}

\displaystyle=p^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\ell\leqslant j<k(x)\end{subarray}}\frac{r_{K}(p^{j})}{p^{j}}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}\left(1-\frac{1}{\n\mathfrak{p}}\right)+\frac{p^{\ell}\beta_{p^{k(x)}}}{p^{k(x)}}

\displaystyle=p^{\ell}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j\geqslant\ell\end{subarray}}\frac{r_{K}(p^{j})}{p^{j}}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}\left(1-\frac{1}{\n\mathfrak{p}}\right)

\displaystyle=\gamma(p^{\ell},u),

by (
7.31
).

Putting everything together we finally deduce that

\displaystyle c(w)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
p\mid k\Rightarrow p\mid P(w)\\
\gcd(k,q)\mid u\end{subarray}}\frac{g(k)\gcd(k,q)}{k}

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\mid P(w)}\gamma(p^{\ell},u)=\gamma(\gcd(q,W_{1}),u),

which thereby completes the proof.
∎

We now have everything in place to complete the proof of Proposition
7.2
.

Proof of Proposition
7.2
.

Combining Lemma
7.7
with (
7.30
), we obtain

T(I;\nu)=\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W_{1}),u)}{[q,W_{0}]}\cdot\sigma(I)+O\left(\frac{|I|}{q}\exp\left(-\frac{\log X}{22\sqrt{\log x}}\right)\right).

We now wish to insert this into (
7.13
).
In view of Lemma
6.6
and (
7.12
),
it easily follows that

\exp\left(-\frac{\log X}{22\sqrt{\log x}}\right)\sum_{\nu\bmod{W_{0}}}\gamma(W_{0},\nu)\ll\exp\left(-\frac{\log X}{23\sqrt{\log x}}\right).

Moreover, for any
d\mid W_{0}
we have

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\\
\nu\equiv u\bmod{d}\end{subarray}}\gamma(W_{0},\nu)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{0}^{e-1}}\hskip-5.69046pt\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{x}\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{0}\mathbb{Z})^{e}\\
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\equiv u\bmod{d}\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt\#\left\{\nu\bmod{W_{0}}:\begin{array}[]{l}\nu\equiv u\bmod{d}\\
\nu\equiv\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\bmod{W_{0}}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{W_{0}^{e-1}}\cdot\frac{W_{0}^{e}\rho(d,u)}{d^{e}}\cdot 1

\displaystyle=\frac{W_{0}\gamma(d,u)}{d}.

Taking

, we deduce that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\\
\nu\equiv u\bmod{\gcd(W_{0},q)}\end{subarray}}\gamma(W_{0},\nu)\cdot\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W_{1}),u)}{[q,W_{0}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{W_{0}\gamma(\gcd(q,W_{0}),u)}{\gcd(q,W_{0})}\cdot\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W_{1}),u)}{[q,W_{0}]}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u)}{q}.

Hence

S(I;q,u)=\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u)}{q}\cdot\sigma(I)+O\left(\frac{|I|}{q}\exp\left(-\frac{\log X}{23\sqrt{\log x}}\right)\right),

which completes the proof.
∎

8.
Norm forms: approximation by the localised counting function

8.1.
Checking the hypotheses

Our goal in this section is to apply
Theorem

2.2
to the sequence

F(n)=R_{K}(n;B)-\hat{R}_{K}(n;B).

We begin by checking that assumption (1) holds.
Any vector
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}\cap B\mathscr{B}
counted by

R_{K}(n;B)
produces an element
x=x_{1}\omega_{1}+\cdots+x_{e}\omega_{e}\in\mathfrak{o}_{K}
such that

N_{K/\mathbb{Q}}(x)=n
. On accounting for units, we find that

R_{K}(n;B)\ll r_{K}(n),

where the implied constant depends on
K
and

r_{K}(n)
is the number of integral ideals
\mathfrak{a}\subset\mathfrak{o}_{K}
of norm
n
. It follows from
(
6.4
) that
r_{K}(n)\leqslant\tau(n)^{e}
.
Hence

(8.1)

R_{K}(n;B)\ll\tau(n)^{e},

for an implied constant depending only on
K
.
Next, it follows from
(
7.6
), part (iii) of Lemma
7.1
and Lemma
6.6
that

\displaystyle\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)

\displaystyle=\gamma(W,n)\cdot\omega(n;B)\ll\gamma(W,n)\ll\varphi^{*}(n)\tau(n)^{e}\leqslant\tau(n)^{e+1}.

Hence

|F(n)|\leqslant R_{K}(n;B)+\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)\ll\tau(n)^{e+1},

so that
|F(n)|\leqslant\tau_{J}(n)
if
J
is large enough.
This establishes that assumption (1) holds in Theorem

2.2
for any large enough constant
A
.

Turning to assumption (2), having addressed the first moment of
\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)
in
Proposition
7.2
,
we must next examine the first moment of

R_{K}(n;B)
.

Lemma 8.1
.

Let
B\geqslant 1
, let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval, let
q\in\mathbb{N}
and let
1\leqslant u\leqslant q
. Then

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}R_{K}(n;B)=\frac{\gamma(q,u)}{q}\int_{I}\omega(t;B)\mathrm{d}t+O(q^{e+1}B^{e-1/2}),

where
\gamma(q,u)
is given by (
7.5
).

Proof.

Recalling
(
7.1
),
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}R_{K}(n;B)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{s}\in(\mathbb{Z}/q\mathbb{Z})^{e}\\
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{s})\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}N(q,\mathbf{s}),

where

N(q,\mathbf{s})=\#\left\{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}\cap B\mathscr{B}:\begin{array}[]{l}\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\in I\\
\mathbf{x}\equiv\mathbf{s}\bmod{q}\end{array}\right\}.

Note that the outer sum features at most
q^{e}
choices for
\mathbf{s}
.
We would like to approximate the inner sum by an integral.
Our goal is to prove that

(8.2)

N(q,\mathbf{s})=\frac{1}{q^{e}}\vol\left\{\mathbf{x}\in B\mathscr{B}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\in I\right\}+O_{\varepsilon}(qB^{e-1+\varepsilon}),

for any
\varepsilon>0
.
Note that

\vol\left\{\mathbf{x}\in B\mathscr{B}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\in I\right\}=\int_{I}\omega(t;B)\mathrm{d}t,

by part (ii) of Lemma
7.1
. Hence,
on taking
\varepsilon=1/2
, it follows from (
8.2
) that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}R_{K}(n;B)=\frac{\rho(q,u)}{q^{e}}\int_{I}\omega(t;B)\mathrm{d}t+O(q^{e+1}B^{e-1/2}).

The statement of the lemma follows, assuming (
8.2
), on recalling the definition
(
7.5
) of
\gamma(q,u)
.

To prove (
8.2
), we claim that

(8.3)

N(1,\mathbf{0})=\vol\left\{\mathbf{x}\in B\mathscr{B}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\in I\right\}+O(B^{e-1}).

But

\displaystyle\vol\left\{\mathbf{x}\in B\mathscr{B}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\in I\right\}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}}\vol\left\{\mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^{e}:\text{$\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u}\in B\mathscr{B}$ and $\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})\in I$}\right\}.

Now
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x}+\mathbf{u})=\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})+O(|\mathbf{x}|^{e-1})
for any
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{e}
and

\mathbf{u}\in[0,1]^{e}
. Hence there are
O(B^{e-1})
pairs of vectors
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{u})
that contribute to the right-hand side, but for which

\mathbf{x}\not\in B\mathscr{B}
or
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\not\in I
. The claim (
8.3
) follows.

Next, we turn to the estimation of
N(q,\mathbf{s})
, which we achieve by comparing it with
N(q,\mathbf{0})
.
For
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}
let
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}=(q\lfloor x_{1}/q\rfloor,\dots,q\lfloor x_{e}/q\rfloor)
.
Then if
\mathbf{x}
is counted by
N(q,\mathbf{s})
it will follow that

\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
is counted by
N(q,\mathbf{0})
unless either
\mathbf{x}
is within a distance
O(q)
of the boundary
of
B\mathscr{B}
, or unless
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})
is within a distance of
O(qB^{e-1})
of the end points of
I
.
A given integer arises at most
O_{\varepsilon}(B^{\varepsilon})
times as a value of
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})
with

\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{Z}^{e}\cap B\mathscr{B}
, by (
8.1
).
Hence

N(q,\mathbf{0})=N(q,\mathbf{s})+O_{\varepsilon}(qB^{e-1+\varepsilon}).

Summing over all
\mathbf{s}\bmod{q}
we deduce that

q^{e}N(q,\mathbf{0})=N(1,\mathbf{0})+O_{\varepsilon}(q^{e+1}B^{e-1+\varepsilon}).

The claimed bound (
8.2
) follows on
combining this with (
8.3
).
∎

The following result is the key to dealing with assumption (2) in Theorem

2.2
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed. Let
B\geqslant 1
. Define

F(n)=R_{K}(n;B)-\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)

and

(8.4)

\mathscr{Q}=\left\{p^{k(x)}:p\leqslant w\right\}.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval such that

I\subset[-2Hx^{d},2Hx^{d}]
,
|I|>H^{1-\varepsilon}
and
x\leqslant H^{\Delta_{d,e}}
with
\Delta_{d,e}
as in (
7.2
).
Let
q\in\mathbb{N}
and let
1\leqslant u\leqslant q
.
Assume that
q
is not divisible by any element of
\mathscr{Q}
, that

q\leqslant x^{d}
and that

(8.5)

\gcd(u,q)\leqslant\exp\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right).

Then

\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}F(n)\right|\ll\frac{|I|}{q}\left(\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{23}\sqrt{\log x}\right)+\frac{x^{d(e+2)}B^{e-1/2}}{|I|}\right).

Proof.

To begin with, we break the interval

into a non-negative piece and a negative piece. On
I_{+}

we shall apply
Proposition
7.2

with
H^{1-\varepsilon}\ll X\ll B^{e}
.
Since
H\geqslant x^{2}
it follows that
(
7.10
) holds with
X=2Hx^{d}
under the hypotheses of the lemma.
Moreover, since
x\leqslant H^{1/2}
, we can crudely estimate

q\leqslant x^{d}\leqslant H^{1/6}\ll X^{1/5}
.
Thus the hypotheses of
Proposition
7.2
are met and it follows that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I_{+}\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)=\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u)}{q}\int_{I_{+}}\omega(t;B)\mathrm{d}t+O\left(\frac{|I|}{q}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{23}\sqrt{\log x}\right)\right).

To handle
I_{-}
we make a change of
n\mapsto-n
and then reapply
Proposition
7.2
. This leads to the conclusion that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}\hskip-5.69046pt\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)=\frac{\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u)}{q}\int_{I}\omega(t;B)\mathrm{d}t+O\left(\frac{|I|}{q}\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{23}\sqrt{\log x}\right)\right).

It follows from part (iii) of Lemma
7.1
that

\int_{I}\omega(t;B)\mathrm{d}t\ll|I|.

Combining the previous first moment bound
with Lemma
8.1
, we deduce that

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv u\bmod q\end{subarray}}F(n)\right|\ll~

\displaystyle\frac{|I|}{q}\left|\gamma(q,u)-\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u)\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{|I|}{q}\left(\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{23}\sqrt{\log x}\right)+\frac{x^{d(e+2)}B^{e-1/2}}{|I|}\right),

since
q\leqslant x^{d}
.

Let us put

D_{q}=\gamma(q,u)-\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u),

for any
q\in\mathbb{N}
. Assuming that
q\leqslant x^{d}
and that
q
is not divisible by any element of
\mathscr{Q}
, in the notation of
(
8.4
), we shall prove that

(8.6)

D_{q}\ll\exp\left(-\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{\log x}\right).

This will suffice to complete the proof of the lemma.
In order to prove the claim,
we recall the definition (
7.4
) of
W
. We shall factorise
q
as
q_{0}q_{1}
, where

q_{0}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{j}\|q\\
p\leqslant w\end{subarray}}p^{j}\quad\text{ and }\quad q_{1}=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{j}\|q\\
p>w\end{subarray}}p^{j}.

Our assumption on
q
implies that
q_{0}\mid W
.
Then we have
\gamma(q,u)=\gamma(q_{0},u)\gamma(q_{1},u)
and

\gamma(\gcd(q,W),u)=\gamma(q_{0},u)
.
Hence it follows that

(8.7)

D_{q}=\gamma(q_{0},u)\left(\gamma(q_{1},u)-1\right).

We appeal to our work in Section
6.2

to estimate these quantities. On the one hand,
Lemma
6.6

yields

\gamma(q_{0},u)\ll\varphi^{*}(q_{0})\tau(\gcd(u,q_{0}))^{e}.

We now appeal to the bound

\tau(n)^{e}\ll n^{1/2}.

Since
\gcd(u,q_{0})\leqslant\gcd(u,q)
and

\gcd(u,q)
satisfies (
8.5
),
we deduce that

(8.8)

\gamma(q_{0},u)\ll\exp\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\log x}}{\log\log x}\right).

On the other hand, the Chinese remainder theorem yields

\displaystyle\gamma(q_{1},u)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{j}\|q\\
p>w\end{subarray}}p^{-j(e-1)}\rho(p^{j},u).

If
v_{p}(u)\geqslant 1
for some prime
p
in this product, then
there exists a prime
p>w
such that
p\mid u
. But this is impossible, since we are assuming that
\gcd(u,q)\leqslant w
, where
w
is given by (
7.4
). Hence we can assume that

for every

, whence

\displaystyle p^{-j(e-1)}\rho(p^{j},u)

\displaystyle=\left(1-\frac{1}{p}\right)^{-1}\prod_{\mathfrak{p}\mid p}\left(1-\frac{1}{\n\mathfrak{p}}\right)=1-\frac{r_{K}(p)-1}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{2}}\right),

by part (iii) of Lemma

and (

).
But then it follows that

\left|\gamma(q_{1},u)-1\right|=\left|1-\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{j}\|q\\
p>w\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{r_{K}(p)-1}{p}+\frac{\theta_{p}}{p^{2}}\right)\right|

for some

.
Let

and
extend
c_{d}
multiplicatively on square-free integers

via
c_{d}=\prod_{p\mid d}c_{p}
.
Then we have

\displaystyle\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p^{j}\|q\\
p>w\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{r_{K}(p)-1}{p}+\frac{\theta_{p}}{p^{2}}\right)

\displaystyle=\prod_{p\mid q_{1}}\left(1-\frac{c_{p}}{p}\right)=1+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q_{1}\\
d>1\end{subarray}}\frac{\mu(d)c_{d}}{d}.

Note that
d>w^{\omega(d)}
, for any

such that

.
Moreover, there is a constant

, depending only on

, such that

. Hence

\displaystyle\left|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}d\mid q_{1}\\

.

(8.9)

\left|\gamma(q_{1},u)-1\right|\ll\frac{\omega(q_{1})}{w}\leqslant\frac{\log q_{1}}{w}.

We now combine (

) and (

) in (

) to conclude

Finally, we recall that

and

in (

).
The stated bound (

) easily follows.
∎

8.2.
Next steps

We summarise the next stage in the proof of Theorem

.
Rather than working with

We are now in a position to complete the proof.
Define

E_{\delta}(x,H)=\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(\tilde{H},H):|N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)-\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)|>\delta x\right\},

for any
\delta>0
, where

B=\tilde{H}^{1/e}x^{d/e}
in the definitions of
N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)
and
\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)
, as above.
Then

\displaystyle E_{\delta}(x,H)

\displaystyle<\frac{1}{\delta^{2}x^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(\tilde{H},H)\end{subarray}}|N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)-\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)|^{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\delta^{2}x^{2}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(\tilde{H},H)\end{subarray}}\left|\sum_{n\leqslant x}F(c_{0}n^{d}+c_{1}n^{d-1}+\cdots+c_{d})\right|^{2},

where

F(n)=R_{K}(n;B)-\hat{R}_{K}(n;B)
.
We use Theorem

2.2

to estimate this sum, with
\alpha_{n}=1
and
2A
in place of
A
. It therefore follows from
(
2.3
) that

\displaystyle E_{\delta}(x,H)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{1}{\delta^{2}x^{2}}\cdot H^{d+1}x^{2}(\log x)^{-2A}\ll\frac{H^{d+1}}{\delta^{2}(\log x)^{2A}},

as required.
∎

8.3.
Final deduction of Theorem
1.6

Recall the definition of

S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
from
(
1.6
) and (
8.12
).
In the next section we shall prove the following result, which shows that the localised counting function is rarely smaller than it should be.

Proposition 8.4
.

Let
A\geqslant 1
be fixed. Take
B=\tilde{H}^{1/e}x^{d/e}
and assume that (
8.10
) holds.
Let
\delta>(\log x)^{-A}
.
Then

\displaystyle\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)<\delta x\right\}\ll\frac{H^{d+1}}{(\log x)^{dA}}+(\delta(\log x)^{c_{d,e}})^{\frac{1}{d^{2}e}}{\tilde{H}H^{d}},

where
c_{d,e}=e+d^{2}(d+1)^{e+1}
.

In the rest of this section, we apply Proposition
8.4
to finish the proof of Theorem
1.6
.
Let
A\geqslant 1
be fixed and
recall the definition

N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)=\sum_{n\leqslant x}R_{K}(f_{\mathbf{c}}(n);B),

where
R_{K}
is given by (
7.1
).
We now have all the ingredients with which to show that

N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)
almost always has the expected order of magnitude (up to powers of
\log x
), as one runs over coefficient vectors

\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(H)
. This is summarised in the following result, in the light of
which Theorem
1.6
is a trivial consequence.

Theorem 8.5
.

Let
d\geqslant 1
and
let
K/\mathbb{Q}
be a number field of degree
e
.
Let
A>0
, let
H\geqslant 1
and let
x
lie in the interval

\frac{1}{2}H^{\Delta_{d,e}}<x\leqslant H^{\Delta_{d,e}}

for the parameter

\Delta_{d,e}
given in (
7.2
).
Then there exists
B_{A}>0
such that

\frac{1}{\#S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(H)}\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(H):N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)>\frac{x}{(\log x)^{B_{A}}}\right\}=1+O_{d,K,A}\left(\frac{1}{(\log H)^{A}}\right),

where the implied constant depends only on
d
, on the number field
K
and on the choice of
A
.

Proof of Theorem
8.5
assuming Proposition
8.4
.

We will find it convenient to break the range of
c_{0}
into dyadic intervals.
Recall from (
8.12
) that

S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
is
the set of
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(H)
such that
\frac{1}{2}\tilde{H}\leqslant c_{0}\leqslant\tilde{H}
.
Let
A^{\prime}\geqslant 1
. We note that

\displaystyle\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(H):N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)>\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A^{\prime}}}\right\}=\#S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(H)-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\tilde{H}\leqslant H\\
\tilde{H}=2^{j}\end{subarray}}E(\tilde{H},H),

where

E(\tilde{H},H)=\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)\leqslant\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A^{\prime}}}\right\}.

Our aim is to show that the contribution from
E(\tilde{H},H)
is negligible.

It will be convenient to put

\delta=\frac{1}{(\log x)^{A^{\prime}}}.

Let
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
and
let
B=\tilde{H}^{1/e}x^{d/e}
. Suppose that
\mathbf{c}
is counted by

E(\tilde{H},H)
. We claim that
either

|N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)-\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)|>\delta x
or
\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)<2\delta x.

Indeed, if the opposite of both inequalities held, then it would follow that

N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)=N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)-\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)+\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)>\delta x=\frac{x}{(\log x)^{A^{\prime}}},

which is impossible. Propositions
8.3
and
8.4
can therefore be combined to show that

E(\tilde{H},H)\ll\frac{H^{d+1}}{(\log H)^{A+1}},

provided that
\tilde{H}\geqslant H\exp(-\sqrt{\log H})
and
A^{\prime}
is chosen to be sufficiently large in terms of
A
.
It now follows that

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leqslant\tilde{H}\leqslant 2H\\
\tilde{H}=2^{j}\end{subarray}}E(\tilde{H},H)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{H}\leqslant 2H\exp(-\sqrt{\log H})\\
\tilde{H}=2^{j}\end{subarray}}E(\tilde{H},H)+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}H\exp(-\sqrt{\log H})\leqslant\tilde{H}\leqslant H\\
\tilde{H}=2^{j}\end{subarray}}E(\tilde{H},H)

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\tilde{H}\leqslant H\exp(-\sqrt{\log H})\\
\tilde{H}=2^{j}\end{subarray}}\tilde{H}H^{d}+\frac{H^{d+1}}{(\log H)^{A}}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{H^{d+1}}{\exp(\sqrt{\log H})}+\frac{H^{d+1}}{(\log H)^{A}},

since there are
O(\log H)
choices for
\tilde{H}
in the second sum.
∎

8.4.
Proof of Corollary
1.8

Any
(\mathbf{x},t)\in\mathbb{Z}^{e+1}
counted by

N_{\mathbf{c}}(x)
is constrained to lie in the box
[-P,P]^{e+1}
for

P\ll B\leqslant H^{1/e}x^{d/e}\leqslant H^{1/e+d\Delta_{d,e}/e}.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be fixed.
According to
Theorem
8.5
, with probability 1,
as admissible coefficient vectors are ordered by height,
there are
\gg H^{\Delta_{d,e}-\varepsilon}
integer solutions to the equation

\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f(t)
.
We now appeal to a result of Bombieri and Pila
[
4
]
, which shows that an irreducible degree
k
curve in
\mathbb{A}^{e+1}
has
O_{e,k,\varepsilon}(P^{1/k+\varepsilon})
points in
(\mathbb{Z}\cap[-P,P])^{e+1}
, where the implied constant depends only on
e,k
and
\varepsilon
. Thus, if all the solutions were covered by
\leqslant L
irreducible curves of degree
k\leqslant D
we would have

H^{\Delta_{d,e}-\varepsilon}\ll_{d,D,L,\varepsilon}P^{1/k+\varepsilon}\ll H^{(1/e+d\Delta_{d,e}/e)/k+\varepsilon},

which is a contradiction for

k>\frac{1}{e\Delta_{d,e}}+\frac{d}{e}.

This is equivalent to
k>d(2e^{2}+6e+1)/e
, on
recalling
the definition (
7.2
) of

\Delta_{d,e}
. Noting that
2e^{2}+6e+1<2e(e+7/2)
for
e\geqslant 2
, the desired statement follows.

9.
Norm forms: the localised counting function is rarely small

The aim of this section is to prove
Proposition
8.4
.
Recall the definition of

S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
from
(
1.6
) and (
8.12
).
We will find it convenient to
write

h_{\mathbf{c}}=\gcd(c_{0},\dots,c_{d})

for the content of the polynomial

Our aim in this section is to show that

We assume throughout this section that
\tilde{H}

H\exp(-\sqrt{\log H})\leqslant\tilde{H}\leqslant H
, as in (
8.10
).
We need to study the size of

for

and
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
.

Our first task is to prove the following result,
which will be proved in the next subsection.

Proposition 9.1
.

Let
A\geqslant 1
be fixed. Take
B=\tilde{H}^{1/e}x^{d/e}
and assume that
\tilde{H}
lies in the interval (
8.10
).
Let
\delta>(\log x)^{-A}
.
Then there exists a constant

such that

\displaystyle\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)<\delta x\right\}

\displaystyle\ll\frac{H^{d+1}}{(\log x)^{dA}}

\displaystyle\quad+\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\textnormal{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})\leqslant M_{A}\delta(\log x)^{c_{d,e}}\right\},

where

(9.2)

\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})=W_{0}^{-e}\#\left\{(\mathbf{x},t)\in(\mathbb{Z}/W_{0}\mathbb{Z})^{e+1}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\equiv f_{\mathbf{c}}(t)\bmod{W_{0}}\right\}.

9.1.
A transition to non-archimedean densities

Recall the definition (
7.8
) of
\omega(f_{\mathbf{c}}(n);B)
. Then we have the following lower bound.

Lemma 9.2
.

Suppose that
x\geqslant H^{2}
and that
H
is large enough. There exists some small
0<\eta\ll 1
such that

\omega(f_{\mathbf{c}}(n);B)\gg 1,

if
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{+}(\tilde{H},H)
and

|n/x-(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0})^{1/d}|<\eta^{2}.

Moreover, the
implied constants depend only on
K
.

Proof.

According to part (iv) of Lemma
7.1
, there exists
0<\eta\ll 1
such that

\omega(y;B)\gg 1
if
|y/B^{e}-\frac{1}{2}|<\eta
. But if
|n/x-(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0})^{1/d}|<\eta^{2}
, then

(n/x)^{d}=\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0}+O(\eta^{2})

since
c_{0}>\frac{1}{2}\tilde{H}
. But then, since
f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)=c_{0}n^{d}+O(Hx^{d-1})
and
B^{e}=\tilde{H}x^{d}
, it follows that

|f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)/B^{e}-\tfrac{1}{2}|\ll\eta^{2}+\frac{H}{x\tilde{H}}.

The right-hand side is
\ll\eta
if
\tilde{H}
satisfies (
8.10
) and
H\leqslant x^{1/2}
.
The statement of the lemma follows by choosing
\eta>0
small enough.
∎

Define the interval

I=\left\{t\in\mathbb{R}:0<t\leqslant x,~|t/x-(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0})^{1/d}|<\eta^{2}\right\}.

Note that

1\geqslant(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0})^{1/d}\geqslant 2^{-1/d}

if
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
. Assuming that
\eta
is sufficiently small, it follows that

I=(\xi_{0},\xi_{1})
, with

\xi_{0}=(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0})^{1/d}x-\eta^{2}x\quad\text{ and }\quad\xi_{1}=\min\left\{x,(\tfrac{1}{2}\tilde{H}/c_{0})^{1/d}x+\eta^{2}x\right\}.

In particular,

x\ll\xi_{0}<\xi_{1}\leqslant x
and

\xi_{1}-\xi_{0}\gg x
, for absolute implied constants. In particular
x\ll|I|\leqslant x
.

Lemma
9.2
implies that

(9.3)

\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):\hat{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(x)<\delta x\right\}\leqslant\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):\tilde{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(I)\ll\delta x\right\},

where

\tilde{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(I)=\sum_{n\in I}\gamma(W,f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)).

Recall
the definition
(
9.2
) of

\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})
and put

P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}M_{d,K}<p\leqslant w\\
p\nmid h_{\mathbf{c}}\end{subarray}}p,

for a suitably large constant
M_{d,K}>0
that only depends on
d
and
K
.
The following result is the key step in the proof of Proposition
9.1
.

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
A\geqslant 1
.
Let
\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}(\tilde{H},H)
, in the notation of (
8.11
).
Recall the definition (
7.19
) of

\tilde{r}_{K}
.
Suppose that
f_{\mathbf{c}}
is separable, has no integer zero, and has content
h_{\mathbf{c}}\leqslant(\log x)^{A}
.
Then

\tilde{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(I)=\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})|I|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
p\mid k\Rightarrow p\mid P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)\end{subarray}}\frac{c_{\mathbf{c}}(w)g(k)\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)}{k}+O_{A}\left(x\exp\left(-(\log x)^{1/8}\right)\right).

where
g=\tilde{r}_{K}*\mu
,
\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}
is given by (
6.1
),

W_{0}
is given by (
9.1
) and

c_{\mathbf{c}}(w)=\prod_{p\mid P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)}\alpha_{p},

with
\alpha_{p}
given by (
7.14
).

Proof.

We shall use the similar techniques to those that we developed in the proof of
Proposition
7.2
, writing
W=W_{0}W_{1}
in the notation of (
9.1
).
It follows from (
7.12
) that

\prod_{p\leqslant M_{d,K}}p^{k(x)}\ll(\log x)^{C_{1}},

where
C_{1}
depends on the constant
C
in the definition of
k(x)
, and on both
d
and the number field
K
.
Similarly,

\prod_{p\mid h_{\mathbf{c}}}p^{k(x)}\leqslant h_{\mathbf{c}}^{k(x)\omega(h_{\mathbf{c}})}\leqslant\exp\left(k(x)(\log h_{\mathbf{c}})^{2}\right)\leqslant\exp\left(C_{2}(\log\log x)^{3}\right),

since
h_{\mathbf{c}}\leqslant(\log x)^{A}
,
for a constant
C_{2}
that depends on
A
and
on the constant
C
in the definition of
k(x)
. Hence it follows that

(9.4)

W_{0}\leqslant\exp\left(C^{\prime}(\log\log x)^{3}\right),

for a further constant

that depends on

and

.

Since
\gamma(W,f_{\mathbf{c}}(n))=\gamma(W_{0},f_{\mathbf{c}}(n))\gamma(W_{1},f_{\mathbf{c}}(n))
, we obtain

(9.5)

\tilde{N}_{\mathbf{c}}(I)=\sum_{\nu\bmod{W_{0}}}\gamma(W_{0},f_{\mathbf{c}}(\nu))T(I;\nu),

where now

n\equiv\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\end{subarray}}\gamma(W_{1},f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)).

It follows from (
7.17
)
that

\gamma(W_{1},f_{\mathbf{c}}(n))=\prod_{p\mid P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)}\alpha_{p}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)\\
p^{k(x)}\nmid f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)\end{subarray}}r_{K}(p^{v_{p}(f_{\mathbf{c}}(n))})\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\mid P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)\\
p^{k(x)}\mid f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)\end{subarray}}\alpha_{p}^{-1}\beta_{p^{k(x)}},

where

and

are given by (

),
respectively.
Hence

(9.6)

T(I;\nu)=U(I;\nu)\prod_{p\mid P_{\mathbf{c}}(w)}\alpha_{p}

where

n\equiv\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\end{subarray}}\tilde{r}_{K}(f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)_{W}),

with the notation (
7.19

\tilde{r}_{K}

Note that

Write

Let

Using Möbius inversion to detect the coprimality condition, we obtain

\displaystyle U(I;\nu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\end{subarray}}g(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\\
k\mid f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)\end{subarray}}1,

where

.
We will break the outer sum into two intervals, leading to

(9.7)

U(I;\nu)=\Sigma_{1}+\Sigma_{2},

where

(9.8)

\Sigma_{1}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k\leqslant x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}g(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\\
k\mid f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)\end{subarray}}1

and

\Sigma_{2}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k>x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}g(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\\
k\mid f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)\end{subarray}}1.

Beginning with the second sum, it follows from (
7.27
) and the inclusion
I\subset(0,x]
, that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{2}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k>x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}\tau(k)^{e+1}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\\
k\mid f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)\end{subarray}}1.

Suppose that
\omega(k)=r
. Then any

in the sum satisfies

whence
\omega(f_{\mathbf{c}}(n))\geqslant\omega(k)=r>K_{0}

But then it follows that

, whence

\Sigma_{2}\ll\frac{1}{2^{K_{0}}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\leqslant x\end{subarray}}\tau(|f_{\mathbf{c}}(n)|)^{e+2}.

Since

has content

,
it now follows from Lemma

that

(9.9)

\Sigma_{2}\ll_{A}\frac{x(\log x)^{K_{1}}}{2^{K_{0}}}\ll x\exp\left(-(\log x)^{1/4}\right),

for a suitable constant

only depending on

and
A
.

Turning to the sum (

),
it follows from (

) that

\gcd(k,W_{0})=1

and
v_{p}(k)\leqslant k(x)
,
for any

such that

.
Breaking into residue classes modulo

, we deduce that

\displaystyle\Sigma_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k\leqslant x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}g(k)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\alpha\bmod{k}\\
f_{\mathbf{c}}(\alpha)\equiv 0\bmod{k}\end{subarray}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in I\\
n\equiv\nu\bmod{W_{0}}\\
n\equiv\alpha\bmod{k}\end{subarray}}1

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k\leqslant x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}g(k)\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)\left(\frac{|I|}{W_{0}k}+O(1)\right),

where
\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}
is given by (

).
Since
f_{\mathbf{c}}
has content
h_{\mathbf{c}}\leqslant(\log x)^{A}
,
Lemma
6.1
implies that
\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)=O_{\varepsilon}(k^{1-1/d+\varepsilon}(\log x)^{A/d})
, for any
\varepsilon>0
.
We have already seen in (
7.27
) that
|g(k)|\leqslant\tau(k)^{e+1}=O_{\varepsilon}(k^{\varepsilon})
.
Hence

(9.10)

\Sigma_{1}=\frac{|I|}{W_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k\leqslant x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}\frac{g(k)\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)}{k}+O_{A,\varepsilon}\left(x^{1-1/(2d)+\varepsilon}\right).

Since
|I|\leqslant x
, we see that

\frac{|I|}{W_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k\leqslant x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}\frac{g(k)\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)}{k}=\frac{|I|}{W_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\end{subarray}}\frac{g(k)\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)}{k}+O\left(x\Upsilon(x)\right),

where

(9.11)

\Upsilon(x)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\\
k>x^{1/(2d)}\end{subarray}}\frac{|g(k)|\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)}{k}.

We will show momentarily that

(9.12)

\Upsilon(x)\ll_{A}\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{16d}\right).

From (
9.12
) it will follow that

\Sigma_{1}=\frac{|I|}{W_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathscr{C}_{w}\end{subarray}}\frac{g(k)\lambda_{f_{\mathbf{c}}}(k)}{k}+O_{A}\left(x\exp\left(-\frac{\sqrt{\log x}}{16d}\right)\right),

in (
9.10
). Combining this with (
9.9
) in (

U(I;\nu)=\frac{|I|}{W_{0}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\

Lemma 9.4
.

Let

be a
separable polynomial of degree

where
\alpha_{p}
is given by (
7.14
)
and

c_{d,e}=e+d^{2}(d+1)^{e+1}

Proof.

It follows from multiplicativity that

\prod_{p\mid P}\alpha_{p}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\mathbb{N}\\
p\mid k\Rightarrow p\mid P\end{subarray}}\frac{c(w)g(k)\lambda_{f}(k)}{k}=\prod_{p\mid P}\alpha_{p}\xi_{p},

where
\alpha_{p}
is given by (
7.14
) and

\displaystyle\xi_{p}

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{g(p^{j})\lambda_{f}(p^{j})}{p^{j}}

\displaystyle\geqslant 1-\sum_{j\geqslant 1}\frac{d\tau(p^{j})^{e+1}\min\{p^{j-1},p^{j(1-1/d)}\}}{p^{j}}

\displaystyle=1-\sum_{j=1}^{d}\frac{d(j+1)^{e+1}}{p}-\sum_{j\geqslant d+1}\frac{d(j+1)^{e+1}}{p^{j/d}},

by (
7.27
) and Lemma
6.1
.
But then, it follows from (
6.8
) that

\alpha_{p}\xi_{p}\geqslant 1-\frac{e+d^{2}(d+1)^{e+1}}{p}+O\left(\frac{1}{p^{1+1/d}}\right).

On assuming that
M_{d,K}
is sufficiently large, the statement of the lemma readily follows from Mertens’ theorem.

(9.13)

9.2.
The non-archimedean densities are rarely small

Let
\Delta>0
. (In our application we shall take

\Delta
to be of order
\delta(\log x)^{c_{d,e}}
.)
In this section we seek to provide an upper bound for

(9.14)

M(\tilde{H},H;\Delta)=\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H):\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})<\Delta\right\},

where
W_{0}
is given by (
7.11
),

S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
is defined in (
8.12
) and

\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})
is given by (
9.2
).
We shall assume that
\tilde{H}
satisfies (
8.10
).
Broadly speaking, our strategy for bounding (
9.14
) is inspired by the work in
[
9
, §5]
.

Since

\sigma_{\mathbf{c}}(W_{0})
only depends on the
residue class of
\mathbf{c}
modulo
W_{0}
, we may break the
\mathbf{c}
into residue classes modulo
W_{0}
.
Let
U(W_{0})\subset(\mathbb{Z}/W_{0}\mathbb{Z})^{d+1}
be the
image of the set
S_{d}^{\text{loc}}(\tilde{H},H)
under reduction modulo
W_{0}

Then

\displaystyle M(\tilde{H},H;\Delta)

\displaystyle\leqslant\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\in U(W_{0})\\
\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(W_{0})<\Delta\end{subarray}}\#\left\{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{Z}^{d+1}:|\mathbf{c}|\leqslant H,~\mathbf{c}\equiv\mathbf{u}\bmod{W_{0}}\right\}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\mathbf{u}\in U(W_{0})\\
\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(W_{0})<\Delta\end{subarray}}\frac{\tilde{H}}{W_{0}}\left(\frac{H}{W_{0}}\right)^{d},

since
(
7.12
)
and our assumption
(
8.10
)
together ensure that

H\geqslant\tilde{H}\gg W_{0}
.
It follows from Rankin’s trick that

\displaystyle M(\tilde{H},H;\Delta)

\displaystyle\ll\frac{\Delta^{\kappa}\tilde{H}H^{d}}{W_{0}^{d+1}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in U(W_{0})}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(W_{0})^{\kappa}},

for any
\kappa>0
.

Let

U(p^{k})=\left\{\mathbf{u}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{d+1}:\begin{array}[]{l}\exists(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{e+1}\text{ s.t.\ }\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\equiv f_{\mathbf{u}}(t_{0})\bmod{p^{k}}\end{array}\right\},

for any prime power

Then

(9.15)

M(\tilde{H},H;\Delta)\ll\frac{\Delta^{\kappa}\tilde{H}H^{d}}{W_{0}^{d+1}}\prod_{p\mid W_{0}}\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in U(p^{k(x)})}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k(x)})^{\kappa}},

Moreover,

\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})=p^{-ke}\#\left\{(\mathbf{x},t)\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{e+1}:\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\equiv f_{\mathbf{u}}(t)\bmod{p^{k}}\right\}.

Our next result provides a good lower bound for

\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})
whenever the equation
\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})=f_{\mathbf{u}}(t)
admits a solution over
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
which is not too singular.

Lemma 9.5
.

Let
p^{k}
be a prime power and let

\mathbf{u}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{d+1}
.
Assume that there exists

\alpha\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
and

(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}^{e+1}
such that

(9.16)

\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{0})\equiv f_{\mathbf{u}}(t_{0})\bmod{p^{k}}\quad\text{ and }\quad p^{\alpha}\|(\nabla\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{0}),f_{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}(t_{0})).

Then

\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})\geqslant p^{-(\alpha+1)e}.

Proof.

We take the lower bound

\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})\geqslant p^{-ke}\#\left\{(\mathbf{x},t)\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k}\mathbb{Z})^{e+1}:\begin{array}[]{l}\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\equiv f_{\mathbf{u}}(t)\bmod{p^{k}}\\
(\mathbf{x},t)\equiv(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})\bmod{p^{\alpha+1}}\end{array}\right\}.

If
k\leqslant\alpha
then
\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})\geqslant p^{-ke}\geqslant p^{-\alpha e}
, which is satisfactory.
If
\alpha<k\leqslant 2\alpha
then
we write
(\mathbf{x},t)=(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})+p^{\alpha+1}(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})
, where
(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})
runs modulo

p^{k-\alpha-1}
. An application of Taylor’s theorem implies that
any choice of
(\mathbf{x}_{1},t_{1})
contributes to the right-hand side, since

p^{\alpha}
divides
(\nabla\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x}_{0}),f_{\mathbf{u}}^{\prime}(t_{0}))
. Hence

\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})\geqslant p^{-ke}\cdot p^{(k-\alpha-1)(e+1)}\geqslant p^{-(\alpha+1)e},

since
k\geqslant\alpha+1
.
Finally, if
k\geqslant 2\alpha+1
then
we can estimate

\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})
from below using Hensel’s lemma, in the form of
[
8
, Lemma 3.3]
.
It easily follows that

\displaystyle\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k})

\displaystyle\geqslant p^{-(2\alpha+1)e}\#\left\{(\mathbf{x},t)\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{2\alpha+1}\mathbb{Z})^{e+1}:\begin{array}[]{l}\nf_{K}(\mathbf{x})\equiv f_{\mathbf{u}}(t)\bmod{p^{2\alpha+1}}\\
(\mathbf{x},t)\equiv(\mathbf{x}_{0},t_{0})\bmod{p^{\alpha+1}}\end{array}\right\}

\displaystyle\geqslant p^{-(2\alpha+1)e}\cdot p^{\alpha(e+1)}

\displaystyle\geqslant p^{-(\alpha+1)e}.

Taking these estimates together, the proof of the lemma is complete.

U_{\alpha}(p^{k(x)})

\mathbf{u}\in(\mathbb{Z}/p^{k(x)}\mathbb{Z})^{d+1}

9.5
that
that

\displaystyle\sum_{\mathbf{u}\in U(p^{k(x)})}\frac{1}{\sigma_{\mathbf{u}}(p^{k(x)})^{\kappa}}\leqslant\sum_{0\leqslant\alpha\leqslant{k(x)}}p^{(\alpha+1)e\kappa}\#U_{\alpha}(p^{k(x)}).
