Sectional Voronoi tessellations: Characterization and high-dimensional limits
Anna Gusakova, Zakhar Kabluchko, Christoph Thäle

Abstract
The intersections of beta-Voronoi, beta-prime-Voronoi and Gaussian-Voronoi tessellations in ℝ d \mathbb{R}^{d} with ℓ \ell -dimensional affine subspaces, 1 ≤ ℓ ≤ d − 1 1\leq\ell\leq d-1 , are shown to be random tessellations of the same type but with different model parameters. In particular, the intersection of a classical Poisson-Voronoi tessellation with an affine subspace is shown to have the same distribution as a certain beta-Voronoi tessellation. The geometric properties of the typical cell and, more generally, typical k k -faces, of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation are studied in detail. It is proved that in high dimensions, that is as d → ∞ d\to\infty , the intersection of the d d -dimensional Poison-Voronoi tessellation with an affine subspace of fixed dimension ℓ \ell converges to the ℓ \ell -dimensional Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation. Keywords: Beta-Voronoi tessellation, Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation, high-dimensional limit, Laguerre tessellation, Poisson point process, Poisson-Voronoi tessellation, sectional tessellation, stochastic geometry, typical cell MSC: 60D05, 60G55

Introduction

The present paper is devoted to the study of affine sections of Poisson-Voronoi tessellations. To define these,
let
\zeta\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be the set of atoms of a stationary point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
For each point
x\in\zeta
we construct the
Voronoi cell

V(x,\zeta)
of
x
as the set of all points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
which are closer to
x
than to any other point of
\zeta
:

V(x,\zeta):=\{z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:\|x-z\|\leq\|y-z\|\quad\text{for all }y\in\zeta\setminus\{x\}\},

where
\|\,\cdot\,\|
stands for the Euclidean norm. The Voronoi cell can be thought of as a zone of influence or attraction of the point
x
and it is known that each Voronoi cell is a convex polytope in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with probability
1
. The collection of all these polytopes is the
Voronoi tessellation
associated with
\zeta
. If
\zeta
is a homogeneous Poisson point process with constant intensity
\rho>0
, this construction yields the
Poisson-Voronoi tessellation
, denoted here by
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho}
, – one of the most classical models studied in stochastic geometry. We refer to the monographs
[
22
,
27
,
29
]
for more detailed information, applications and further references on Voronoi tessellations and in particular the Poisson-Voronoi tessellation.

A number of random tessellations studied in stochastic geometry, such as the Poisson hyperplane or the STIT tessellations, have the distinguished feature of being stable under intersections with lower-dimensional affine subspaces. By this we mean that the intersection with an affine subspace of one of these random tessellations is again a model of the same type within the intersecting subspace. For example, the intersection of a Poisson hyperplane tessellation with an affine subspace
L
is again a Poisson hyperplane tessellation within
L
. However, a similar property is not true for the Poisson-Voronoi tessellation. In fact, it has been shown by Chiu, Van De Weygaert and Stoyan
[
6
]
that the intersection of the Poisson-Voronoi tessellation with an affine subspace cannot be a Voronoi tessellation induced by any stationary point process within the subspace. In other words, the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation is necessarily a ‘non-Voronoi’ tessellation. However, besides a few mean values determined in
[
6
,
20
]
further probabilistic or geometric information about the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation seems not available in the existing literature, although they are of importance for stereological applications (see
[
29
, Chapter 11.5.4]
or
[
21
, Section 14.4.6]
as well as the references cited therein). It is one of the main purposes of this paper to derive a precise description of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation and to study its typical cell. We do this by establishing a connection with the so-called
\beta
-Voronoi tessellations, a random tessellation model we recently introduced and studied in the series of papers
[
10
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
. Their analysis in turn was based on the connection with the class of beta random polytopes, which has already seen a number of applications in stochastic geometry
[
11
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
.

We study the problem of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation just explained in a more general framework. In fact, the random tessellation we study is either

•

a
\beta
-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with parameters
\beta\geq-1
and
\gamma>0
,

•

a
\beta^{\prime}
-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}^{\prime}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with parameters
\beta>{d\over 2}+1
and
\gamma>0
,

•

or a Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{G}_{d,\lambda}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with parameter
\lambda>0
;

a description of all these models will be provided in Section
3
. We remark that the classical Poisson-Voronoi tessellation generated by a stationary Poisson point process in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with intensity
\rho>0
appears in this framework as the
\beta
-Voronoi tessellation corresponding to the parameters
\beta=-1
and
\gamma=\pi^{{d+1\over 2}}\rho/\Gamma({d+1\over 2})
.
Now, let
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an affine subspace of dimension
1\leq\ell\leq d-1
. We show in Theorem
4.1
below that

•

the sectional tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}\cap L
is a
(\beta+{d-\ell\over 2})
-Voronoi tessellation in
L
with the same
\gamma
,

•

the sectional tessellation
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}^{\prime}\cap L
is a
(\beta-{d-\ell\over 2})^{\prime}
-Voronoi tessellation in
L
with the same
\gamma
,

•

the sectional tessellation
\mathcal{G}_{d,\lambda}\cap L
is again a Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation in
L
with the same
\lambda
.

In particular, the intersection of the classical Poisson-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho}
with
L
turns out to be a
\beta
-Voronoi tessellation within
L
with
\beta={d-\ell\over 2}-1
and
\gamma=\pi^{{d+1\over 2}}\rho/\Gamma({d+1\over 2})
. For clarity we should remark that none of the random tessellations
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}
with
\beta>-1
are actually Voronoi tessellations. Our terminology is motivated by the fact that
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}
may be viewed as a
deformation
of the classical Poisson-Voronoi tessellation (which corresponds to
\beta=-1
).

With the identification of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation at hand, in the second part of this paper we study its geometric properties. More precisely, we determine in Theorem
5.1
its face intensities in terms of quantities which have already appeared in the study of beta random polytopes
[
11
,
15
]
. From here on, we determine the expected volume, the expected intrinsic volumes as well as the expected
f
-vector of the typical cell (and even more generally the typical
k
-face) of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation. Moreover, generalizing earlier results of Miles
[
20
]
we consider the asymptotics, as
d\to\infty
, of several characteristics (such as the volume of the typical cell) of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation and identify the limits with the corresponding characteristics of a suitable Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation. The weak convergence on the level of tessellations is discussed as well using a coupling construction similar to the one in
[
8
]
.

2
Preliminaries on random tessellations

In this section we collect some definitions and facts about general stationary random tessellations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For more detailed discussions we refer the reader to
[
27
, Chapters 4 and 10]
as well as
[
29
, Chapter 10]
.

A
tessellation

T
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a countable, locally finite collection of
d
-dimensional polytopes, which cover the space and have non-empty, disjoint interiors. The elements of
T
are called the
cells
of
T
. Given a polytope
c\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
we denote by
\mathcal{F}_{k}(c)
the set of its
k
-dimensional faces,
0\leq k\leq d
, where
\mathcal{F}_{d}(c)=\{c\}
, and let
\mathcal{F}(c):=\bigcup_{k=0}^{d}\mathcal{F}_{k}(c)
. A tessellation
T
is called
face-to-face
if for any two of its cells
c_{1},c_{2}\in T
one has that

c_{1}\cap c_{2}\in(\mathcal{F}(c_{1})\cap\mathcal{F}(c_{2}))\cup\{\varnothing\},

that is, the intersection of two cells is either empty or a common face of both cells.
For a face-to-face tessellation
T
one defines
\mathcal{F}_{k}(T)=\bigcup_{c\in T}\mathcal{F}_{k}(c)
and
\mathcal{F}(T)=\bigcup_{c\in T}\mathcal{F}(c)
.
A face-to-face tessellation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is called
normal
if each
k
-dimensional face of the tessellation is contained in the boundary of precisely
d+1-k
cells, for all
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
.

We denote by
\mathbb{T}
the set of all face-to-face tessellations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which is supplied with a measurable structure as in
[
27
, Chapter 10]
. By a
random tessellation
we understand a particle process
\mathcal{T}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
(in the usual sense of stochastic geometry, see
[
27
, Section 4.1]
) satisfying
\mathop{\mathrm{supp}}\nolimits\mathcal{T}\in\mathbb{T}
almost surely. It is convenient to identify the random point process
\mathcal{T}
with its support. A random tessellation is
stationary
, provided that its distribution is invariant under all shifts in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
isotropic
if its distribution is invariant under all rotations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. For a stationary random tessellation
\mathcal{T}
and
k\in\{0,1,\ldots,d-1\}
we define the stationary particle process
\mathcal{T}^{(k)}:=\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{T})}\delta_{F}
of
k
-dimensional polytopes, which is referred to as the
process of
k
-faces
.

Next, we recall the concept of a typical cell (and a typical
k
-face) of a stationary random tessellation
\mathcal{T}
; see
[
27
, Section 4.1,4.2]
,
[
27
, page 450]
,
[
26
, Section 4.3]
for more details. Let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the space of non-empty compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
endowed with the Hausdorff metric. A
centre function
is a Borel function
z:\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
z(C+m)=z(C)+m
for all
C\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
and
m\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The
intensity of
k
-faces
of
\mathcal{T}
is defined by

\gamma_{k}(\mathcal{T}):=\mathbb{E}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{T})}{\bf 1}(z(F)\in[0,1]^{d}),\qquad k=0,\ldots,d.

These quantities are known to be independent of the choice of the centre function
z
. Assuming that
\gamma_{k}(\mathcal{T})\in(0,\infty)
, the
typical
k
-face of
\mathcal{T}
with respect to the centre function
z
is the
k
-dimensional random polytope whose distribution is given by

\mathbb{P}^{z}_{\mathcal{T},k}(\,\cdot\,):={1\over\gamma_{k}(\mathcal{T})}\,\mathbb{E}\sum_{F\in\mathcal{F}_{k}(\mathcal{T})}{\bf 1}({F-z(F)}\in\,\cdot\,){\bf 1}(z(F)\in[0,1]^{d}).

In particular, for
k=d
we get the concept of the
typical cell
of
\mathcal{T}
. It should be noted that translation-invariant characteristics of the distribution of the typical
k
-face do not depend on the choice of
z
. More precisely, if
z
and
z^{\prime}
are two centre functions, then
\mathbb{P}^{z^{\prime}}_{\mathcal{T},k}
is the push-forward of
\mathbb{P}_{\mathcal{T},k}^{z}
under the map
F\mapsto F-z^{\prime}(F)
.

3
Construction of
\beta
-,
\beta^{\prime}
- and Gaussian-Voronoi tessellations

3.1
General Laguerre tessellations

In this section we only briefly recall some facts about Laguerre tessellations and refer the reader to
[
7
, Sections 3.2–3.4]
and
[
8
, Sections 2.3 and 3.1]
for further details.

We start by defining a general Laguerre tessellation. Given two points
v,w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and
h\in\mathbb{R}
we define the
power
of
w
with respect to the pair
(v,h)
as

\mathop{\mathrm{pow}}\nolimits(w,(v,h)):=\|w-v\|^{2}+h.

In this situation,
h
is referred to as the
weight
(or
height
) of the point
v
. Let
X
be a countable set of marked points of the form
(v,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
.
Then the
Laguerre cell
of
(v,h)\in X
is the set

C((v,h),X):=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\mathop{\mathrm{pow}}\nolimits(w,(v,h))\leq\mathop{\mathrm{pow}}\nolimits(w,(v^{\prime},h^{\prime}))\text{ for all }(v^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in X\}.

The point
v
is called the nucleus of the cell
C((v,h),X)
. Note that a Laguerre cell may be empty and even if it is non-empty, it does not need to contain its nucleus. The collection of all non-empty Laguerre cells of
X
is called the
Laguerre diagram
:

\mathcal{L}(X):=\{C((v,h),X)\colon(v,h)\in X,C((v,h),X)\neq\varnothing\}.

In the special case when the heights
h
of all points are the same (say,
h_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
) the above definition leads to the classical
Voronoi cell
. More precisely, let
Y
be a countable set of points in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
whose “marked” version
X
is obtained by attaching a fixed weight
h_{0}
to each point. Then the Voronoi cell of
v\in Y
is

V(v,Y)=C((v,h_{0}),X)=\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\|w-v\|\leq\|w-v^{\prime}\|\text{ for all }v^{\prime}\in Y\}.

The collection of the Voronoi cells of all
v\in Y
is called the
Voronoi diagram

\mathcal{V}(Y)
. It should be mentioned that a Laguerre diagram is not necessarily a tessellation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, at least as long as no additional assumptions on the geometric properties of the set
X
are imposed. Such assumptions have been described in detail in
[
18
,
19
,
25
]
. In the present article we are interested in random tessellations built on Poisson point processes. More precisely, we consider a Poisson point process
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times E
, where
E\subset\mathbb{R}
is a Borel set (an interval), and the corresponding Laguerre diagram
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
. Lemmas 1 and 2 in
[
7
]
(see also
[
8
, Lemma 2.1]
) provide sufficient conditions on
\xi
which ensure that, almost surely,
\mathcal{L}(\xi)
is a stationary random face-to-face normal tessellation in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. In the following we work under these conditions and remark that they are automatically satisfied in the three cases we consider from Section
3.3
on.

3.2
Laguerre tessellations via paraboloid growth processes

An alternative approach to the construction of Laguerre diagrams uses so-called paraboloid growth processes with overlaps (or simply paraboloid growth process), which were first introduced in
[
1
,
28
]
in order to study the asymptotic geometry of random polytopes; see also
[
2
,
3
,
4
,
5
]
. In this section we briefly describe this rather useful construction and refer for more details to
[
8
, Section 3.1]
.
Let

\Pi_{\pm,x}:=\{(v^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\colon h^{\prime}=\pm\|v^{\prime}-v\|^{2}+h\}

be the upward (
+
) and downward (
-
) standard
paraboloids
with apex
x:=(v,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
, denoted as
\mathop{\mathrm{apex}}\nolimits\Pi_{\pm,x}:=x
. In case
(v,h)=(0,0)
we simply write
\Pi_{\pm}=\Pi_{\pm,(0,0)}
. Given a set
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
we put

\displaystyle A^{\downarrow}:

\displaystyle=\{(v,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\colon(v,h)\in A\text{ for some }h\geq h^{\prime}\},

\displaystyle A^{\uparrow}:

\displaystyle=\{(v,h^{\prime})\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}\colon(v,h)\in A\text{ for some }h\leq h^{\prime}\}.

Following the definition from
[
1
]
, for a given Poisson point process
\xi
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
,
we introduce the
paraboloid growth process

\Psi(\xi)
:

\Psi(\xi):=\bigcup\limits_{x\in\xi}\Pi^{\uparrow}_{+,x}.

It should be noted that, in typical situations, the majority of paraboloids will be completely covered by other paraboloids, implying that they do not "contribute" to the model and can thus be omitted without loosing any information about the set
\Psi(\xi)
. This leads to the definition of extreme points. A point
x\in\xi
is called
extreme
in the paraboloid growth process
\Psi(\xi)
if and only if its associated paraboloid is not fully covered by the paraboloids associated with other points of
\xi
, i.e., if

\Pi^{\uparrow}_{+,x}\not\subset\bigcup\limits_{y\in\xi,y\neq x}\Pi^{\uparrow}_{+,y}.

We denote by
\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}\nolimits(\Psi(\xi))
the set of all extreme points of the paraboloid growth process
\Psi(\xi)
.
Using the paraboloid growth process we can construct a random diagram in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Given a point
x=(v,h)\in\xi
define the
\Psi
-cell
of
x
as

C_{\Psi}(x,\xi):=\begin{cases}\{w\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\colon\big[(w,0)^{\uparrow}\cup(w,0)^{\downarrow}\big]\cap\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi)\in\Pi_{+,x}\},\qquad&\text{if }x\in\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}\nolimits(\Psi(\xi)),\\
\varnothing,&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
\operatorname{bd}A
denotes the boundary of a set
A
.
In other words,
w
belongs to
C_{\Psi}(x,\xi)
if and only if
\|w-v\|^{2}+h\leq\|w-v^{\prime}\|+h^{\prime}
for all
(v^{\prime},h^{\prime})\in\xi
. Thus, the
\Psi
-cell of an extreme point
x
of the paraboloid growth process
\Psi(\xi)
is non-empty and coincides with the Laguerre cell
C(x,\xi)
. Next, we construct the diagram
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi)
as the collection of all non-empty
\Psi
-cells:

\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi):=\{C_{\Psi}(x,\xi)\colon C_{\Psi}(x,\xi)\neq\varnothing\}=\{C_{\Psi}(x,\xi)\colon x\in\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}\nolimits(\Psi(\xi))\}.

We directly have that
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi)=\mathcal{L}(\xi)
.

3.3
Three families of random tessellations

In this article we consider random tessellations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
build on the following three families of Poisson point processes. For
\beta>-1
and
0<\gamma<\infty
we consider a Poisson point process
\eta_{d,\beta,\gamma}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,+\infty)
whose intensity measure has density

(v,h)\mapsto\gamma\,c_{d+1,\beta}h^{\beta},\qquad c_{d+1,\beta}:={\Gamma\left({d+1\over 2}+\beta+1\right)\over\pi^{d+1\over 2}\Gamma(\beta+1)},

(3.1)

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,+\infty)
. Further, for
\beta>\frac{d}{2}+1
and
0<\gamma<\infty
we consider a Poisson point process
\eta^{\prime}_{d,\beta,\gamma}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(-\infty,0)
with intensity measure having density

(v,h)\mapsto\gamma\,c^{\prime}_{d+1,\beta}(-h)^{-\beta},\qquad c^{\prime}_{d+1,\beta}:={\Gamma\left(\beta\right)\over\pi^{d+1\over 2}\Gamma(\beta-{d+1\over 2})},

(3.2)

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times(-\infty,0)
. The constants
c_{d+1,\beta}
and
c^{\prime}_{d+1,\beta}
in the above definitions are introduced for convenience. For example, they make the statement of Theorem
4.1
below more transparent.
Finally, for
\lambda>0
and
0<\gamma<\infty
we consider a Poisson point process
\zeta_{d,\lambda,\gamma}
in
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
whose intensity measure has density

(v,h)\mapsto\gamma\,e^{\lambda h},

with respect to the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}
. It was shown in
[
7
, Lemma 3]
and in
[
8
, Section 3.3]
that the Poisson point processes
\eta_{d,\beta,\gamma}
and
\zeta_{d,\lambda,\gamma}
satisfy the sufficient conditions of Lemma 1 and Lemma 2 in
[
7
]
and, hence, the corresponding Laguerre diagrams
\mathcal{V}_{d,\beta,\gamma}:=\mathcal{L}(\eta_{d,\beta,\gamma})
,
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}_{d,\beta,\gamma}:=\mathcal{L}(\eta^{\prime}_{d,\beta,\gamma})
and
\mathcal{G}_{d,\lambda}:=\mathcal{L}(\zeta_{d,\lambda,\gamma})
are stationary random normal tessellations in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, which are called
\beta
-Voronoi
,
\beta^{\prime}
-Voronoi
and
Gaussian-Voronoi tessellations
, respectively. These tessellations have been studied in
[
10
,
7
,
8
,
9
]
, where they were considered in
\mathbb{R}^{d-1}
instead of
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Simulations of these tessellations in the plane are shown in Figure
3.1
. Note that although the point process
\eta^{\prime}_{d,\beta,\gamma}
is well-defined in the range
\beta>\frac{d+1}{2}
, the corresponding
\beta^{\prime}
-Voronoi tessellation exists in the smaller range
\beta>\frac{d}{2}+1
only, see
[
7
, Lemma 3]
.

Remark 3.1
.

Note that changing the parameter
\gamma
amounts to shifting the Poisson point process
\zeta_{d,\lambda,\gamma}
along the height coordinate
h
. In particular, the distribution of the point process
\mathcal{G}_{d,\lambda}
does not depend on the choice of
\gamma
, which is reflected in our notation.

Remark 3.2
.

It will be convenient to extend the above definition of the
\beta
-Voronoi tessellation to the case
\beta=-1
(with arbitrary
\gamma>0
) by
defining
\mathcal{V}_{d,-1,\gamma}:=\mathcal{W}_{d,r(d)\gamma}
to be the classical Poisson-Voronoi tessellation constructed on the homogeneous Poisson point process on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with constant intensity
r(d)\gamma
, where

r(d):=\Gamma\left({d+1\over 2}\right)\pi^{-{d+1\over 2}}.

A justification for this definition is given by the following proposition. For the necessary background on point processes and weak convergence we refer to
[
24
, Chapter 3]
.

Proposition 3.3
.

As
\beta\downarrow-1
, the Poisson process
\eta_{d,\beta,\gamma}
converges, weakly on the space of locally finite integer-valued measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,\infty)
, to the Poisson point process whose intensity measure is the Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\{0\}
times
r(d)\gamma
.

Proof.

Write
\beta=-1+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
. Then the constant appearing in (
3.1
) satisfies

c_{d+1,\beta}={\Gamma\left({d+1\over 2}+\varepsilon\right)\over\pi^{d+1\over 2}\Gamma(\varepsilon)}={\Gamma\left({d+1\over 2}\right)\over\pi^{d+1\over 2}}\varepsilon(1+o(1)),

as
\varepsilon\downarrow 0
.
It follows that for every
c>0
and every bounded Borel set
B\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
the number of points of the Poisson point process
\eta_{d,\beta,\gamma}
appearing in
B\times[0,c]
is Poisson-distributed with expectation

\gamma\lambda_{d}(B)c_{d+1,\beta}\int_{0}^{c}h^{-1+\varepsilon}{\rm d}h\overset{}{\underset{\varepsilon\downarrow 0}{\longrightarrow}}\Gamma\left({d+1\over 2}\right)\pi^{-{{d+1}\over 2}}\gamma\lambda_{d}(B)=r(d)\gamma\lambda_{d}(B),

where
\lambda_{d}
denotes the
d
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. Since the right-hand side does not depend on
c
, it follows that, for every
0<c_{1}<c_{2}
, the expected number of points of
\eta_{d,\beta,\gamma}
in
B\times[c_{1},c_{2}]
converges to
0
, as

5.2
.

Remark 5.6
.

For example, we may take
\rho_{d}=\rho>0
to be constant, in which case
\kappa=1
.

Proof of Proposition
5.5
.

By Theorem
5.1
,

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}\gamma_{j}(\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell})

\displaystyle=\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{2\mathbb{J}_{\ell+1,\ell-j+1}({d-\ell-1\over 2})\pi^{\ell\over 2}}{d(\ell+1)\rho_{d}^{-{\ell\over d}}}\frac{\Gamma({(\ell+1)(d-1)\over 2}+1)}{\Gamma({(\ell+1)(d-1)+1\over 2})}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+1-{\ell\over d})}{\Gamma({\ell+2\over 2})}\frac{\Gamma({d\over 2}+1)^{\ell+1-{\ell\over d}}}{\Gamma({d+1\over 2})^{\ell+1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{2\mathbb{J}_{\ell+1,\ell-j+1}(\infty)(\kappa^{2}\pi)^{\ell\over 2}}{\ell+1}\frac{2\Gamma(\ell)}{\Gamma({\ell\over 2})}\lim_{d\to\infty}\frac{1}{d}\frac{\Gamma({(\ell+1)(d-1)\over 2}+1)}{\Gamma({(\ell+1)(d-1)+1\over 2})}\frac{\Gamma({d\over 2}+1)^{\ell+1-{\ell\over d}}}{\Gamma({d+1\over 2})^{\ell+1}}.

By Stirling’s formula for the Gamma function,
\Gamma(z)=\sqrt{2\pi/z}(z/e)^{z}(1+O(z^{-1}))
. Since
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}{\Gamma(n)n^{z}\over\Gamma(n+z)}=1
we get

\lim_{d\to\infty}\gamma_{j}(\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell})=\frac{\mathbb{J}_{\ell+1,\ell-j+1}(\infty)(\kappa^{2}\pi e)^{\ell\over 2}}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\frac{2\Gamma(\ell)}{\Gamma({\ell\over 2})}\lim_{d\to\infty}\Big(\frac{d}{4\pi}\Big)^{\ell\over 2d}=\frac{\mathbb{J}_{\ell+1,\ell-j+1}(\infty)(\kappa^{2}\pi e)^{\ell\over 2}}{\sqrt{\ell+1}}\frac{2\Gamma(\ell)}{\Gamma({\ell\over 2})}.

This completes the argument.
∎

.

Let

\mathbb{J}_{\ell+1,1}(\infty)

\ell

\Sigma_{\ell}

Proof.

This follows from the fact that
\mathbb{E}\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}\nolimits(Z_{d,\ell,\rho_{d}})=\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell})^{-1}
(see

6
Convergence to the Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation in high dimensions

In Sections
5.1
and
5.2
we computed explicitly several characteristics of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and the limits of these characteristics in the regime when
d\to\infty
and
(\rho_{d})^{1/d}\to\kappa>0
, while
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
stays fixed. It turns out that these limits coincide with the corresponding characteristics of the tessellation
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\lambda}
with
\lambda=\kappa^{2}\pi e
.
For example, for the typical cell
Z(\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\lambda})
of the Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\lambda}
it is known from
[
8
, Section 5]
(where the special case
\lambda=1/2
has been considered) that

\mathbb{E}\mathop{\mathrm{vol}}\nolimits(Z(\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\lambda}))={\sqrt{\ell+1}\over\mathbb{J}_{\ell+1,1}(\infty)\lambda^{\ell\over 2}}{\Gamma({\ell\over 2})\over 2(\ell-1)!}.

This formula coincides with the one obtained in Corollary
5.7
if we choose
\lambda=\kappa^{2}\pi e
.
In the next two theorems we explain this and other similar coincidences by proving weak convergence of the corresponding tessellations and the typical cells.

Theorem 6.1
.

Take any positive sequence
(\rho_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{d\to\infty}(\rho_{d})^{1/d}=\kappa>0
and let
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
be fixed. Then, as
d\to\infty
, the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
converges to
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e}
in the following sense: It is possible to define all these random tessellations on the same probability space such that for every
\ell
-dimensional ball
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
of radius
R>0
centred at the origin the probability that the restrictions of
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e}
to
B_{R}
coincide, converges to
1
, as
d\to\infty
.

Remark 6.2
.

The skeleton of a (random) tessellation
\mathcal{T}
is the (random) closed set
\mathop{\mathrm{skel}}\nolimits(\mathcal{T})=\bigcup_{c\in\mathcal{T}}\operatorname{bd}c
, where
\operatorname{bd}c
denotes the topological boundary of the cell
c
. The mode of convergence appearing in Theorem
6.1
implies that the random closed set
\mathop{\mathrm{skel}}\nolimits(\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell})
converges to the random closed set
\mathop{\mathrm{skel}}\nolimits(\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e})
weakly as
d\to\infty
; see
[
27
, Chapter 2]
for this concept.

Proof of Theorem
6.1
.

The proof of this theorem basically follows the same route as the proof of Theorem 4.2 in
[
8
]
, which is the reason why we leave out some details here. By Corollary
4.2
, the sectional tessellation
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{V}_{\ell,\beta_{d},\gamma_{d}}
with

\beta_{d}=\frac{1}{2}(d-\ell)-1\qquad\text{and}\qquad\gamma_{d}=\pi^{d+1\over 2}\Gamma\left({d+1\over 2}\right)^{-1}\rho_{d}.

We will prove that, as
d\to\infty
, the Poisson point processes
\eta_{\ell,\beta_{d},\gamma_{d}}
converge, after an appropriate vertical shift, to
\zeta_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e,1}
; see Section
3.3
for their definitions. The vertical shift
Q_{d}:\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}
is given by

Q_{d}(v,h)=(v,h-a_{d})
with

a_{d}={1\over\pi}\Big({\pi\Gamma({d-\ell\over 2})\over\rho_{d}}\Big)^{2\over d-\ell-2}.

(6.1)

Note that applying such a vertical shift to a point process does not change the resulting Laguerre tessellation since it amounts to shifting all paraboloids along the height coordinate.

In the following, we show that the intensity function of the Poisson point process
\xi_{\ell,d}:=Q_{d}(\eta_{\ell,\beta_{d},\gamma_{d}})
converges, as
d\to\infty
, to the intensity function of
\xi_{\ell,\infty}:=\zeta_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e,1}
uniformly on every compact subset of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}
. Indeed, the intensity function of
Q_{d}(\eta_{\ell,\beta_{d},\gamma_{d}})
is given by

\displaystyle f_{d}(v,h)

\displaystyle={\rho_{d}\pi^{d-\ell\over 2}\over\Gamma({d-\ell\over 2})}(h+a_{d})^{{d-\ell-2\over 2}}{\bf 1}\big(h+a_{d}>0\big)

\displaystyle={\rho_{d}\pi^{d-\ell\over 2}\over\Gamma({d-\ell\over 2})}a_{d}^{d-\ell-2\over 2}\Big(1+{h\over a_{d}}\Big)^{{d-\ell-2\over 2}}{\bf 1}\big(h+a_{d}>0\big)

\displaystyle=\Big(1+{h\over a_{d}}\Big)^{a_{d}\cdot{d-\ell-2\over 2a_{d}}}{\bf 1}\big(h+a_{d}>0\big).

Stirling’s formula for the Gamma function and (
6.1
) yield

\displaystyle\lim_{d\to\infty}{d-\ell-2\over 2a_{d}}

\displaystyle={1\over\kappa^{2}\pi}\lim_{d\to\infty}{{d-\ell+2}\over 2\Gamma({d-\ell\over 2})^{2\over d-\ell+2}}=\kappa^{2}\pi e\lim_{d\to\infty}{{d-\ell+2}\over(d-\ell)^{d-\ell\over d-\ell+2}}=\kappa^{2}\pi e.

Note that, in particular,
a_{d}\to\infty
as
d\to\infty
. We conclude that
\lim_{d\to\infty}f_{d}(v,h)=e^{\kappa^{2}\pi eh}
uniformly as long as
h
stays bounded. By standard results
[
24
, Propositions 3.6 and 3.19]
, this also implies weak convergence of the corresponding Poisson point processes.

After we have shown the convergence of the point processes
\xi_{\ell,d}
to
\xi_{\ell,\infty}
, as
d\to\infty
, we explain the procedure allowing to transfer this result to the convergence of the corresponding tessellations
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,d})
to
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,\infty})
as
d\to\infty
. Note that
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,d})
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
and
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,\infty})
has the same distribution as
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e}
(see Corollary
4.2
and Section
3.2
).

We fix an
\ell
-dimensional ball
B_{R}\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
of radius
R>0
centred at the origin and for any
\varepsilon>0
we aim to find a region
K(R,\varepsilon)\subset\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}
, independent of
d
, such that with probability at least
1-\varepsilon
the restrictions of the tessellations
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,d})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,\infty})
to
B_{R}
are completely determined by the restrictions of the point processes
\xi_{\ell,d}
and
\xi_{\ell,\infty}
to
K(R,\varepsilon)
for any
d
. To this end, we note that
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,d})
may be regarded as a vertical projection along the
h
-axis of the boundary of the corresponding paraboloid growth process
\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d})
(see Section
3.2
). From this it follows that if the restrictions of the tessellations
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,d})
and
\mathcal{L}_{\Psi}(\xi_{\ell,\infty})
to
B_{R}
do not coincide, then the boundaries of the corresponding paraboloid hull processes
\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d})
and
\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi_{\ell,\infty})
restricted to the cylinder
B_{R}\times\mathbb{R}
do not coincide as well. The construction of the region
K(R,\varepsilon)
can be now performed as follows. First, we consider the event
E(T,r)
that
\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d})
restricted to the cylinder
B_{R}\times\mathbb{R}
is completely determined by the restriction of
\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d})
to the cylinder
B_{R+r}\times(-\infty,T]
for some
T,r>0
. By this we mean that for every paraboloid
\Pi_{+,x}
with
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}\nolimits(\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d}))
the set
\Pi_{+,x}\cap\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d})
either does not intersect
B_{R}\times\mathbb{R}
or is included in
B_{R+r}\times(-\infty,T]
. We have that

1-\mathbb{P}(E(T,r))\leq c_{1}(R+r)^{\ell}(e^{c_{2}(4T-r^{2})}+e^{-c_{3}T^{c_{4}}}),\qquad r,d>c_{5},

(6.2)

where all constants
c_{1},\ldots,c_{5}
are positive and independent of the parameters
d,r
and
T
. Since the proof of this estimate follows exactly the same route as the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 4.4]
(estimate for
T
) and
[
8
, Lemma 4.5]
(estimate for
r
), we decided to omit the technical details. In particular, (
6.2
) shows that for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a choice of
T_{0}:=T(\varepsilon)
and
r_{0}:=r(\varepsilon)
such that
\mathbb{P}(E(T_{0},r_{0}))\geq 1-\varepsilon
. The same holds for
\Psi(\xi_{\ell,\infty})
. Further, we note that if a paraboloid
\Pi_{+,x}
with
x\in\mathop{\mathrm{ext}}\nolimits(\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d}))
is such that
\Pi_{+,x}\cap\operatorname{bd}\Psi(\xi_{\ell,d})\subset B_{R+r_{0}}\times(-\infty,T_{0}]
, then

x\in\{(v,h)\in\mathbb{R}^{\ell+1}\colon h\leq T_{0},\|v\|\leq R+r_{0}+\sqrt{T_{0}-h}\}=:K(R,\varepsilon).

To complete the proof, it suffices to argue that there exists a coupling of
\xi_{\ell,d}
and
\xi_{\ell,\infty}
on a common probability space such that the probability that the restrictions of these processes to the region
K(R,\varepsilon)
do not coincide converges to
0
, as
d\to\infty
. In a suitable coupling, this probability is bounded above by a constant multiple of the
L^{1}
-norm of the difference of their intensity measures restricted to
K(R,\varepsilon)
, see
[
23
, Theorem 3.2.2]
. As we have shown above, the densities
f_{d}(v,h)
converge to
e^{\kappa^{2}\pi eh}
as
d\to\infty
uniformly on compact sets and hence pointwise. Also, by the inequality
1+x\leq e^{x}
, we have
f_{d}(v,h)\leq e^{c_{6}h}
for some absolute constant
c_{6}>0
. The fact that this upper bound is integrable over
K(r,\varepsilon)
has been shown in
[
8
, Equation (4.18)]
. Thus,
f_{d}(v,h)\to e^{\kappa^{2}\pi eh}
with respect to the
L^{1}
-norm on
K(R,\varepsilon)
, which ensures that the required coupling of the Poisson processes indeed exists. This completes the argument.
∎

Our aim is now to prove the weak convergence of the typical cell of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation to the typical cell of the Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation. Fix some
\ell\in\mathbb{N}
and let
\mathcal{C}
be the space of compact subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
endowed with the Hausdorff metric. Put
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}=\mathcal{C}\backslash\{\varnothing\}
. The typical cells considered below are defined with respect to some fixed centre function
z:\mathcal{C}^{\prime}\to\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
in the sense of Section
2
, additionally satisfying
z(C)\in C
for every
C\in\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
.

Theorem 6.3
.

Take any positive sequence
(\rho_{d})_{d\in\mathbb{N}}
with
\lim_{d\to\infty}(\rho_{d})^{1/d}=\kappa>0
. Then, as
d\to\infty
, the distribution of the typical cell of the sectional Poisson-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{W}_{d,\rho_{d}}\cap\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
converges to the distribution of the typical cell of the Gaussian-Voronoi tessellation
\mathcal{G}_{\ell,\kappa^{2}\pi e}
weakly on
\mathcal{C}
.

This theorem is a consequence of Theorem
6.1
and the following general result.

Proposition 6.4
.

Let
(\mathcal{T}_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of stationary random tessellations on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
converging to a stationary random tessellation
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
on
\mathbb{R}^{\ell}
in the following sense: All random tessellations are defined on a common probability space and
for every
R>0
we have
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[A_{n}(R)]=1
, where
A_{n}(R)
is the event that the restrictions of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
and
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
to
[-R,R]^{\ell}
coincide. More precisely,
A_{n}(R)=A_{n}^{\prime}(R)\cap A_{n}^{\prime\prime}(R)
with

\displaystyle A_{n}^{\prime}(R)

\displaystyle:=\{\text{for every }C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\text{ s.t. }C\cap[-R,R]^{\ell}\neq\varnothing\text{ there is }C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\text{ s.t. }C\cap[-R,R]^{\ell}=C^{\prime}\cap[-R,R]^{\ell}\},

\displaystyle A_{n}^{\prime\prime}(R)

\displaystyle:=\{\text{for every }C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}\text{ s.t. }C^{\prime}\cap[-R,R]^{\ell}\neq\varnothing\text{ there is }C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}\text{ s.t. }C\cap[-R,R]^{\ell}=C^{\prime}\cap[-R,R]^{\ell}\}.

Also, suppose that the cell intensity of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
converges to that of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
, that is
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{\infty})
, and that all these intensities are finite. Then, the distribution of the typical cell of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
converges to the distribution of the typical cell of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
weakly on
\mathcal{C}
:

\mathbb{P}^{z}_{\mathcal{T}_{n},\ell}{\overset{}{\underset{n\to\infty}{\longrightarrow}}}\mathbb{P}^{z}_{\mathcal{T}_{\infty},\ell},\qquad\text{ weakly on }\mathcal{C}.

(6.3)

Proof.

Let
f:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}
be a bounded continuous function and recall that
z(C)
is the centre of a cell
C
. Consider the random variables

\xi_{n}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\,z(C)\in[0,1]^{\ell}}f(C),\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

By definition, the distribution of the typical cell of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
satisfies

\int_{\mathcal{C}}f\,{\rm d}\mathbb{P}^{z}_{\mathcal{T}_{n},\ell}=\frac{\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}}{\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{n})},\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

Since
\lim\limits_{n\to\infty}\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{\infty})
, to prove (
6.3
) it suffices to check that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\xi_{n}=\mathbb{E}\xi_{\infty}.

(6.4)

In the following we shall define a “good” event
C_{n}(R)
on which
\xi_{n}
and
\xi_{\infty}
are equal. For
n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
and
R>3
consider the random event

B_{n}(R):=\{\nexists C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:C\cap[0,1]^{\ell}\neq\varnothing,C\not\subset[-R/2,R/2]^{\ell}\}.

A cell
C
with the properties listed in this definition is called a “long cell” in the tessellation
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. The event
B_{n}(R)
occurs if there is no long cell. On the event
A_{n}(R)
, each long cell
C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
corresponds to a long cell
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
with the same restriction to
[-R,R]^{\ell}
.
It follows that
A_{n}(R)\cap(B_{n}(R))^{c}\subset(B_{\infty}(R))^{c}
. Since all cells in
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
are bounded almost surely, the maximal diameter of a cell in
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
intersecting
[0,1]^{\ell}
is some almost surely finite random variable
M
. It follows that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\mathbb{P}[A_{n}(R)\cap(B_{n}(R))^{c}]\leq\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[(B_{\infty}(R))^{c}]\leq\lim_{R\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[M>(R/2)-1]=0.

Recall also that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[A_{n}(R)]=1
for every fixed
R>0
. Consider now the “good” event
C_{n}(R):=A_{n}(R)\cap B_{n}(R)
and the “bad” event
D_{n}(R):=(C_{n}(R))^{c}
. It follows from the above that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}[D_{n}(R)]=0.

(6.5)

Note that, on the event
C_{n}(R)
, the sets
\{C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}:z(C)\in[0,1]^{\ell}\}
and
\{C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}:z(C^{\prime})\in[0,1]^{\ell}\}
are equal, implying that
\xi_{n}=\xi_{\infty}
. Indeed, every cell
C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
with
z(C)\in[0,1]^{\ell}
is contained in
[-R/2,R/2]^{\ell}
(since
z(C)\in C
and there is no long cell) and, consequently,
C
is also a cell of
\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
(since
A_{n}(R)
occurs). Conversely, for every cell
C^{\prime}\in\mathcal{T}_{\infty}
with
z(C^{\prime})\in[0,1]^{\ell}
there is a cell
C\in\mathcal{T}_{n}
such that the restrictions of
C
and
C^{\prime}
to
[-R,R]^{\ell}
coincide (since
A_{n}(R)
occurs) and
C
is not long (since
B_{n}(R)
occurs), which implies that
C=C^{\prime}
.
It follows that

\mathbb{E}[{\bf 1}(C_{n}(R))\,\xi_{n}]=\mathbb{E}[{\bf 1}(C_{n}(R))\,\xi_{\infty}],\qquad n\in\mathbb{N},\,R>3.

To complete the proof of (
6.4
), it suffices to verify that

\lim_{R\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\bf 1}(D_{n}(R))\,\xi_{n}]=0,\qquad\lim_{R\to\infty}\limsup_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}[{\bf 1}(D_{n}(R))\,\xi_{\infty}]=0.

In view of (
6.5
), it suffices to check that the family
\{\xi_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\}
is uniformly integrable. Now,

|\xi_{n}|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\eta_{n}\quad\text{ with }\quad\eta_{n}:=\sum_{C\in\mathcal{T}_{n},\,z(C)\in[0,1]^{\ell}}1,\qquad n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}.

As we already observed above, outside the event
D_{n}(R)
we have
\eta_{n}=\eta_{\infty}
. From (
6.5
) it follows that
\eta_{n}\to\eta_{\infty}
almost surely. On the other hand, we have
\mathbb{E}\eta_{n}=\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\to\gamma_{\ell}(\mathcal{T}_{\infty})=\mathbb{E}\eta_{\infty}
by assumption of the proposition (and all these expectations are finite). These two properties together with
\eta_{n}\geq 0
imply that the family
\{\eta_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\}
is uniformly integrable. Indeed, if this were not the case, we could find
\varepsilon>0
such that, after passing to a subsequence,
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{n}{\bf 1}(Q_{n})]>\varepsilon
for some events
Q_{n}
with
\mathbb{P}[Q_{n}]<1/2^{n}
. Let
W_{k}:=\cup_{n=k}^{\infty}Q_{n}
. If
K
is sufficiently large, then
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{\infty}{\bf 1}(W_{K})]<\varepsilon/2
, while
\mathbb{E}[\eta_{n}{\bf 1}(W_{K})]>\varepsilon
for all
n\geq K
. Applying Fatou’s lemma to the variables
(\eta_{n}{\bf 1}((W_{K})^{c}))_{n\geq K}
leads to a contradiction with the assumption
\mathbb{E}\eta_{n}\to\mathbb{E}\eta_{\infty}
. Finally, the bound
|\xi_{n}|\leq\|f\|_{\infty}\eta_{n}
implies that the family
\{\xi_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}\}
is uniformly integrable as well, and the proof is complete.
∎
