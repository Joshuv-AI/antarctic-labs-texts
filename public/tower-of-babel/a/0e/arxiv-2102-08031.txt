An analytic characterization of the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function in several variables Thanks: Key words. Herglotz-Nevanlinna functions, Cauchy-type functions, symmetric extension.
Mitja Nedic

Abstract
. In this paper, we derive an analytic characterization of the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function in several variables. Here, the main tools used are the so-called variable non-dependence property and the symmetry formula satisfied by Herglotz-Nevanlinna and Cauchy-type functions. We also provide an extension of the Stieltjes inversion formula for Cauchy-type functions.



2010 Mathematics Subject Classification
32A36, 32A99.

1.
Introduction

On the upper half-plane
\mathbb{C}^{+}:=\{z\in\mathbb{C}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \mathrm{Im}[z]>0\}
, the class of holomorphic functions with non-negative imaginary part plays an important role in many areas of analysis and applications. These functions, called
Herglotz-Nevanlinna functions
, appear, to name but a few examples, in the theory of Sturm-Liouville operators and their perturbations
[
3
,
4
,
7
,
10
]
, when studying the classical moment problem
[
2
,
16
,
17
]
, when deriving physical bounds for passive systems
[
5
]
or as approximating functions in certain convex optimization problems
[
8
,
9
]
.

A classical integral representation theorem
[
6
,
16
]
states that any Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
can be written, for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, as

(1.1)

h(z)=a+bz+\frac{1}{\pi}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left(\frac{1}{t-z}-\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}\right)\mathrm{d}\mu(t),

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
b\geq 0
and
\mu
is a positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}
for which
\int_{\mathbb{R}}(1+t^{2})^{-1}\mathrm{d}\mu(t)<\infty
. Although this representation is
a prioir
established for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
, it is well-defined, as an algebraic expression, for any
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. Hence, for a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
, we define its
symmetric extension

h_{\mathrm{sym}}
as the right-hand side of representation (
1.1
) where we now take
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. It is now an easy consequence of the definitions that a holomorphic function
f\colon\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
equals the symmetric extension of some Herglotz-Nevanlinna function if and only if it holds that
\mathrm{Im}[f(z)]\geq 0
for
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
f(z)=\overline{f(\overline{z})}
for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
. In this way, we obtain an analytic characterzation of the symmetric extension.

When considering, instead, functions in the poly-upper half-plane

\mathbb{C}^{+n}:=(\mathbb{C}^{+})^{n}=\big\{\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\,\big|\,\forall j=1,2,\ldots,n:\mathrm{Im}[z_{j}]>0\big\},

the analogous situation becomes more involved. Herglotz-Nevanlinna functions in several variables,
cf.
Definition
2.2
, appear
e.g.
when considering operator monotone functions
[
1
]
or with representations of multidimensional passive systems
[
19
]
. Their corresponding integral representation is recalled in detail in Theorem
2.3
later on and leads, in an analogous way as in the one-variable case, to the definition of a
symmetric extension
, which is now a holomorphic function on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
. As such, the main goal of this paper is to give an analytic characterization of symmetric extensions of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function in several variables,
i.e.
we wish to be able to determine when a function
f\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is, in fact, equal to the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function. This is answered by Theorem
3.3
and Corollary
3.4
.

The structure of the paper is as follows. After the introduction in Section
1
we review the different classes of functions that will appear throughout the paper in Section
2
. Section
3
is then devoted to presenting the main result of the paper as well as some important examples. Finally, Section
4
discusses how the Stieltjes inversion formula can be extended to certain functions on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
.

2.
Classes of functions in the poly cut-plane

Throughout this paper, we will primarily consider two classes of holomorphic functions on the poly cut-plane
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
, both of which are intricately connected to a certain kernel function. These objects are defined as follows.

2.1.
The kernel
K_{n}
and Cauchy-type functions

We begin by introducing the kernel
K_{n}\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
as

(2.1)

K_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t}):=\mathfrak{i}\left(\frac{2}{(2\mathfrak{i})^{n}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{t_{\ell}-z_{\ell}}-\frac{1}{t_{\ell}+\mathfrak{i}}\right)-\frac{1}{(2\mathfrak{i})^{n}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\left(\frac{1}{t_{\ell}-\mathfrak{i}}-\frac{1}{t_{\ell}+\mathfrak{i}}\right)\right).

If the vector
\bm{z}
is restricted to
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
, then the kernel
K_{n}
is a complex-constant multiple of the Schwartz kernel of
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
viewed as a tubular domain over the cone
[0,\infty)^{n}

[
19
, Sec. 12.5]
.

When
n=1
, it holds that

K_{1}(z,t)=\frac{1}{t-z}-\frac{t}{1+t^{2}}.

As such, the kernel
K_{1}
satisfies, for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and all
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the symmetry property

K_{1}(z,t)=\overline{K_{1}(\overline{z},t)}.

When
n\geq 2
, the symmetry satisfied by the kernel becomes more involved and requires the introduction of some additional notation. First, given two numbers
z,w\in\mathbb{C}
, an indexing set
B\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
and an index
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, define

\psi_{B}^{j}(z,w):=\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}z&;&j\not\in B,\\
\overline{w}&;&j\in B.\end{array}\right.

Second, given an indexing set
B\subseteq\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
, define the map
\Psi_{B}\colon\mathbb{C}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}^{n}
as
\Psi_{B}(\bm{z},\bm{w}):=\bm{\zeta}
with
\zeta_{j}:=\psi_{B}^{j}(z_{j},w_{j})
. In other words, the map
\Psi_{B}
functions as a way of selectively combining two vectors into one where the set
B
determines which components of
\bm{z}
should be replaced by the conjugates of the components of
\bm{w}
. It now holds that

(2.2)

K_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{K_{n}(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}),\bm{t})}

for every
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
and every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
13
, Prop. 6.1]
.

Using the kernel
K_{n}
, the largest class of functions that will be considered is the following.

Definition 2.1
.

A function
g\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is called a
Cauchy-type function
if there exists a positive Borel measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the growth condition

(2.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{\ell=1}^{n}\frac{1}{1+t_{\ell}^{2}}\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})<\infty

such that

g(\bm{z})=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})

for every
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
.

Note that this definition is different from
[
13
, Def. 3.1]
in that it assumes from the beginning that a Cauchy-type function is defined on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
and not only on
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
. Furthermore, it would be possible to define an even larger class of functions using the same kernel, but general distributions instead of measures, see
[
14
, Ex. 7.7]
for an example. However, this extension will not be considered here. Moreover, Definition
2.1
allows, in principle, for two (or more) different measure to yield the same function
g
, though we will show that this is not the case later in Section
4
.

An immediate consequence of the symmetry formula (
2.2
) is an analogous symmetry formula for Cauchy-type functions. In particular, it holds, for any Cauchy-type function
g
, that

(2.4)

g(\bm{z})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{g(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}

for every
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
and every
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
13
, Prop. 6.5]
.

The growth of a Cauchy-type function along a coordinate parallel complex line can be described using non-tangential limits. These are taken in so-called
Stoltz domains
and are defined as follows. An
upper Stoltz domain
with centre
0\in\mathbb{R}
and angle
\theta\in(0,\frac{\pi}{2}]
is the set
\{z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}\penalty\ |\penalty\ \theta\leq\arg(z)\leq\pi-\theta\}
and the symbol
z\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\vee\>}}\>\infty
then denotes the limit
|z|\to\infty
in any upper Stoltz domain with centre
0
. A
lower Stoltz domain
and the symbol
z\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\wedge\>}}\>\infty
are defined analogously. Furthermore, we note that in the literature, slightly different notations are sometimes used to describe these limits. Two examples of Stlotz domains are visualized in Figure
1
below.

x
\mathfrak{i}\,y
\theta_{1}
x
\mathfrak{i}\,y
\theta_{2}

An upper Stoltz domain with centre
0
and angle
\theta_{1}
(left) and a lower Stoltz domain with centre
0
and angle
\theta_{2}
(right).

For any Cauchy-type function
g
it now holds, for any
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
and any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, that

\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\vee\>}}\>\infty}\frac{g(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\wedge\>}}\>\infty}\frac{g(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=0,

see
[
13
, Lem. 3.2]
.

2.2.
Herglotz-Nevanlina functions

These functions are defined as follows,
cf.

[
12
,
13
,
18
,
19
]
.

Definition 2.2
.

A holomorphic function
h\colon\mathbb{C}^{+n}\to\mathbb{C}
is called a
Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
if it is holomorphic with non-negative imaginary part.

In contrast to the definition of Cauchy-type function, the above definition is analytic in nature,
i.e.
it describes the function class in terms of conditions on the function itself. In order to be able to relate it to the kernel
K_{n}
, we introduce, given ambient numbers
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
, the expressions

\begin{array}[]{RCL}N_{-1}(z,t)&:=&\frac{1}{2\,\mathfrak{i}}\left(\frac{1}{t-z}-\frac{1}{t-\mathfrak{i}}\right),\\[9.95863pt]
N_{0}(z,t)&:=&\frac{1}{2\,\mathfrak{i}}\left(\frac{1}{t-\mathfrak{i}}-\frac{1}{t_{j}+\mathfrak{i}}\right),\\[9.95863pt]
N_{1}(z,t)&:=&\frac{1}{2\,\mathfrak{i}}\left(\frac{1}{t+\mathfrak{i}}-\frac{1}{t-\overline{z}}\right).\end{array}

Note that
N_{0}
is independent of
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and
N_{0}(z,t)\in\mathbb{R}
while

\overline{N_{-1}(z,t)}=N_{1}(z,t)

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R}
and
t\in\mathbb{R}
. Using these expression, one may give an integral representation formula for Herglotz-Nevanlinna functions involving the kernel
K_{n}

[
13
, Thm. 4.1]
.

Theorem 2.3
.

A function
h\colon\mathbb{C}^{+n}\to\mathbb{C}
is a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function if and only if
h
can be written as

(2.5)

h(\bm{z})=a+\sum_{j=1}^{n}b_{j}\,z_{j}+\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t}),

where
a\in\mathbb{R}
,
\bm{b}\in[0,\infty)^{n}
, the kernel
K_{n}
is as before and
\mu
is a positive Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the growth condition (
2.3
) and the Nevanlinna condition

(2.6)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}\bm{\rho}\in\{-1,0,1\}^{n}\\
-1\in\bm{\rho}\wedge 1\in\bm{\rho}\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\prod_{j=1}^{n}N_{\rho_{j}}(z_{j},t_{j})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})=0

for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
. Furthermore, for a given function
h
, the triple of representing parameters
(a,\bm{b},\mu)
is unique.

The integral representation in formula (
2.5
) is well-defined for any
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
, which may be used to extend any Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
from
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
to
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
. This extension is called the
symmetric extension
of the function
h
and is denoted as
h_{\mathrm{sym}}
. The symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
is different from its possible analytic extension as soon as
\mu\neq 0

[
13
, Prop. 6.10]
and satisfies the following variable-dependence property
[
13
, Prop. 6.9]
.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
n\geq 2
and let
h_{\mathrm{sym}}
be the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
in
n
variables for which
\bm{b}=\bm{0}
. Let
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
be such that
z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
for some index
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. Then, the value
h_{\mathrm{sym}}(\bm{z})
does not depend on the components of
\bm{z}
that lie in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

Furthermore, if
h
is a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function for which
\bm{b}=\bm{0}
, then its symmetric extension
h_{\mathrm{sym}}
will satisfy the symmetry formula

(2.7)

h_{\mathrm{sym}}(\bm{z})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{h_{\mathrm{sym}}(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))},

where
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}

[
13
, Prop. 6.7]
. When
n=1
, it is not necessary to assume that
b=0
for formula (
2.7
) to hold. However, when
n>1
, this is required.

The representing vector
\bm{b}
describes the growth of the function
h
along coordinate parallel complex lines in
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
. More precisely, we recall from
[
13
, Cor. 4.6(iv)]
that, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, we have

(2.8)

b_{j}=\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\vee\>}}\>\infty}\frac{h(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}.

In particular, the above limit is independent of the entries of the vector
\bm{z}
at the non-
j
-th positions. This result carries over to the symmetric extension, for which it holds, for any
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, that

b_{j}=\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\vee\>}}\>\infty}\frac{h_{\mathrm{sym}}(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\wedge\>}}\>\infty}\frac{h_{\mathrm{sym}}(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}.

Every Herglotz-Nevanlinna function that is represented by a data-triple of the form
(0,\bm{0},\mu)
in the sense of Theorem
2.3
is also a Cauchy-type function. The converse,
i.e.
that every Cauchy-type function equals a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function represented by a data-triple of the form
(0,\bm{0},\mu)
, is true only when
n=1
. This is due to the fact that when
n=1
, the Nevanlinna condition (
2.6
) becomes emptily fulfilled by every positive Borel measures
\mu
satisfying the growth condition (
2.3
).

3.
Symmetry and variable non-dependence

We begin by recalling that the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
in one variable is uniquely determined by its values in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
. Indeed, when
n=1
, the symmetry formula (
2.7
) takes the form
h_{\mathrm{sym}}(z)=\overline{h_{\mathrm{sym}}(\overline{z})}
, providing a way to recover the values of the function in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
using only the values of the function in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

For functions of several variables, the appropriate analogue involves the following definition.

Definition 3.1
.

A function
f\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
is said to satisfy the
variable non-dependence property
if for every vector
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
such that
z_{j}\in\mathbb{C}^{-}
for some index
j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
the value
f(\bm{z})
does not depend on the components of
\bm{z}
that lie in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
.

By Proposition
2.4
, the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function satisfies the variable non-dependence property
3.1
if
\bm{b}=\bm{0}
. In particular, the symmetric extension of any Herglotz-Nevanlinna function that is also a Cauchy-type function will always satisfy the variable non-dependence property
3.1
. However, a general Cauchy-type function need not satisfy it, as shown by the function
f_{2}
in Example
3.5
later on.

We may now describe the precise circumstances under which we can recover the values of a function defined on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
purely in terms of its values in
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function satisfying the symmetry formula (
2.7
) and the variable non-dependence property
3.1
. Then, the values of the function
f
on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
are uniquely determined by its values in
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
.

Proof.

Using the symmetry formula (
2.7
), let us investigate the values of the function
f
in a connected component of
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
where at least one of the coordinates has a negative sign of the imaginary part,
i.e.
we are investigating a connected component
X\subseteq(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
where exist at lest one index
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
such that the
j
-th coordinate lies in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
. For any such chosen connected component
X
, let
B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
be the set of those indices for which the corresponding variables lie in
\mathbb{C}^{-}
. In particular,
1\leq|B^{\prime}|\leq n
. For
\bm{z}\in X
, it holds, by the symmetry formula (
2.7
), that

f(\bm{z})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}\\
=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\neq\emptyset\wedge B\subseteq B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\not\subseteq B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}.

Due to the definition of the set
B^{\prime}
, it holds that
\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z})\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
for any
\bm{z}\in X
and any indexing set
B\subseteq B^{\prime}
. Furthermore, by the variable non-dependence property
3.1
, it holds that

\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z})=\Psi_{B\setminus B^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z})

for any
\bm{z}\in X
and any indexing set
B
where
B\not\subseteq B^{\prime}
. Hence,

f(\bm{z})=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\neq\emptyset\wedge B\subseteq B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}\\
B\not\subseteq B^{\prime}\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B\setminus B^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}.

We now claim that the second sum is always equal to zero. Indeed, if
|B^{\prime}|=n
, there is nothing left to prove. Otherwise, we may assume that
|B^{\prime}|<n
, where we claim that there is a way to "pair up" the indexing sets in the second sum in such a way that the two sets in each pair only differ by one element in
B^{\prime}
. We construct this pairing in the following way. Let
j_{1}
be the smallest index in
B^{\prime}
. Then, exactly half of the sets
B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}
that are not subsets of
B^{\prime}
contain the index
j_{1}
and exactly half of them do not contain the index
j_{1}
. This follows from the general observation that exactly half of the subsets of a given set contain a specific element of the set. An indexing set
B_{1}
is then paired with the indexing set
B_{1}\cup\{j_{1}\}
. In this case,
(B_{1}\cup\{j_{1}\})\setminus B^{\prime}=B_{1}\setminus B^{\prime}
and

(-1)^{|B_{1}|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B_{1}\setminus B^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}+(-1)^{|B_{1}\cup\{j_{1}\}|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{(B_{1}\cup\{j_{1}\})\setminus B^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}\\
=(-1)^{|B_{1}|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B_{1}\setminus B^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}-(-1)^{|B_{1}|+1}\overline{f(\Psi_{B_{1}\setminus B^{\prime}}(\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{1},\bm{z}))}=0,

yielding the desired result.
∎

Using Proposition
3.2
, we may now given an analytic characterization of the symmetric extension of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function.

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function such that

\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\vee\>}}\>\infty}\frac{f(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\wedge\>}}\>\infty}\frac{f(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=0

for all indices
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. Then
f=h_{\mathrm{sym}}
for some Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
if and only if

(i)

it holds that
\mathrm{Im}[f(\bm{z})]\geq 0
for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
,

(ii)

the function
f
satisfies the symmetry formula (
2.7
),

(iii)

the function
f
satisfies the variable non-dependence property
3.1
.

Proof.

If
f=h_{\mathrm{sym}}
for some Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
, then this function must have
\bm{b}=\bm{0}
due to the assumption on the growth of
f
. Then, properties (i) – (iii) are satisfied by the previously known results discussed in Section
2.2
. Conversely, if we are given the function
f
satisfies the properties (i) – (iii), we construct a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function out the function
f
by setting

h:=f|_{\mathbb{C}^{+n}}.

This function
h
may then be symmetrically extended to
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
. However,
f
and
h_{\mathrm{sym}}
are now two holomorphic functions on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
satisfying the symmetry formula (
2.7
) and the variable non-dependence property
3.1
which, furthermore, agree on
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
. Therefore, by Proposition
3.2
, they agree everywhere on
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
, as desired.
∎

The assumption on the growth of the function
f
may be slightly weakened, but, to compensate, conditions (ii) and (iii) need to be slightly modified.

Corollary 3.4
.

Let
f\colon(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}\to\mathbb{C}
be a holomorphic function such that

\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\vee\>}}\>\infty}\frac{f(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=\lim\limits_{z_{j}\>\scriptsize{\xrightarrow{\wedge\>}}\>\infty}\frac{f(\bm{z})}{z_{j}}=d_{j}\geq 0

for all indices
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. In particular, for a fixed
j\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, the above limits are assumed to be independent of the values of the vector
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
at the non-

f=h_{\mathrm{sym}}
for some Herglotz-Nevanlinna function

if and only if

(i)

it holds that
\mathrm{Im}[f(\bm{z})]\geq 0
for all
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
,

(ii’)

the function
\bm{z}\mapsto f(\bm{z})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}z_{j}
satisfies the symmetry formula (
2.7
),

(iii’)

the function
\bm{z}\mapsto f(\bm{z})-\sum_{j=1}^{n}d_{j}z_{j}
satisfies the variable non-dependence property
3.1
.

The three conditions on the function

are independent of each-other. To verify this, consider the following functions on

.

4.
The Stieltjes inversion formula for Cauchy-type functions

For Herglotz-Nevanlinna functions, the Stieltjes inversion formula describes how to reconstruct the representing measure
\mu
of a Herglotz-Nevanlinna function
h
from the values of the imaginary part of the function in
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
. More precisely, it holds that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})=\lim\limits_{\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})\mathrm{Im}[h(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y})]\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

for all
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functions
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which there exists a constant
D\geq 0
such that
|\varphi(\bm{x})|\leq D\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+x_{j}^{2})^{-1}
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, see
e.g.

[
10
]
or
[
5
, Lem. 4.1]
for the case
n=1
and
[
13
, Cor. 4.6(viii)]
for the general case.

As noted in Section
2.2
, Cauchy-type functions are a subclass of Herglotz-Nevanlinna functions when
n=1
and, hence, one only need the values of (the imaginary part of) a Cauchy-type function in
\mathbb{C}^{+}
to reconstruct its measure. However, in Example
3.5
, we have seen two different positive Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{2}
for which the corresponding Cauchy-type functions agree on
\mathbb{C}^{+2}
, but not on the remaining connected components of
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{2}
.

The crucial role in the proof of the Stieltjes inversion formula is held by the Poisson kernel of
\mathbb{C}^{+n}
, which, we recall, is defined for
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
and
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as

\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t}):=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\mathrm{Im}[z_{j}]}{|t_{j}-z_{j}|^{2}}.

Note that
\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})>0
for every
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
and
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The imaginary part of the kernel
K_{n}
is equal to the Poisson kernel
\mathcal{P}_{n}
plus a remainder term which can be expressed in terms of the
N_{j}
-factors
[
13
, Prop. 3.3]
and the integral of the remainder with respect to any Nevanlinna measure is zero.

The following lemma now shows how one can recover the value of the Poison kernel
\mathcal{P}_{n}
at some point
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
(and
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
) using the values of kernel
K_{n}
form all of the connected components of the poly cut-plane
(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
n\in\mathbb{N}
,
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+n}
and
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, it holds that

2\,\mathfrak{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})=\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|B|}K_{n}(\Psi_{B}(\bm{z},\bm{z}),\bm{t}),

where
\Psi_{B}
is the selective conjugation map from Section
2.1
.

Proof.

The proof is done by induction on the dimension
n
. If
n=1
, then

\sum_{B\subseteq\{1\}}(-1)^{|B|}K_{1}(\Psi_{B}(z,z),t)=K_{1}(\Psi_{\emptyset}(z,z),t)+(-1)K_{1}(\Psi_{\{1\}}(z,z),t)\\
=K_{1}(z,t)-K_{1}(\overline{z},t)=2\,\mathfrak{i}\,\mathrm{Im}[K_{1}(z,t)]=2\,\mathfrak{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{1}(z,t),

as desired.

Assume now that the statement of the lemma holds for all
n=1,2,\ldots,N-1
for some
N\in\mathbb{N}
. For
n=N
, take
\bm{z}\in\mathbb{C}^{+N}
and
\bm{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}
and let
\bm{z}^{\prime}
and
\bm{t}^{\prime}
denote the same vectors with the last component removed,
i.e.

\bm{z}^{\prime}:=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{N-1})
and
\bm{t}^{\prime}:=(t_{1},\ldots,t_{N-1})
. Furthermore, denote

A(z,t):=\frac{1}{2\,\mathfrak{i}}\left(\frac{1}{t-z}-\frac{1}{t+\mathfrak{i}}\right).

Then, we then calculate that

\displaystyle\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}}(-1)^{|B|}K_{N}(\Psi_{B}(\bm{z},\bm{z}),\bm{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}\\
N\not\in B\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|}K_{N}(\Psi_{B}(\bm{z},\bm{z}),\bm{t})+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}\\
N\in B\end{subarray}}(-1)^{|B|}K_{N}(\Psi_{B}(\bm{z},\bm{z}),\bm{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\sum_{B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N-1\}}(-1)^{|B^{\prime}|}\bigg[\mathfrak{i}\bigg(2\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}A(\psi_{B^{\prime}}^{j}(z_{j},z_{j}),t_{j})\cdot A(z_{N},t_{N})-\prod_{j=1}^{N}A(\mathfrak{i},t_{j})\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle+\sum_{B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N-1\}}(-1)^{|B^{\prime}|+1}\bigg[\mathfrak{i}\bigg(2\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}A(\psi_{B^{\prime}}^{j}(z_{j},z_{j}),t_{j})\cdot A(\overline{z}_{N},t_{N})-\prod_{j=1}^{N}A(\mathfrak{i},t_{j})\bigg)\bigg]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\mathfrak{i}\,A(z_{N},t_{N})\sum_{B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N-1\}}(-1)^{|B^{\prime}|}K_{N-1}(\Psi_{B^{\prime}}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{z}^{\prime}),\bm{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle+\,\mathfrak{i}\,\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}A(\mathfrak{i},t_{j})\cdot(A(z_{N},t_{N})-A(\mathfrak{i},t_{N}))\cdot\overbrace{\sum_{B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N-1\}}(-1)^{|B^{\prime}|}}^{=0}

\displaystyle-2\,\mathfrak{i}\,A(\overline{z}_{N},t_{N})\sum_{B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N-1\}}(-1)^{|B^{\prime}|}K_{N-1}(\Psi_{B^{\prime}}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{z}^{\prime}),\bm{t}^{\prime})

\displaystyle-\,\mathfrak{i}\,\prod_{j=1}^{N-1}A(\mathfrak{i},t_{j})\cdot(A(\overline{z}_{N},t_{N})-A(\mathfrak{i},t_{N}))\cdot\overbrace{\sum_{B^{\prime}\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N-1\}}(-1)^{|B^{\prime}|}}^{=0}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle 2\,\mathfrak{i}\,A(z_{N},t_{N})\,\mathcal{P}_{N-1}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{t}^{\prime})-2\,\mathfrak{i}\,A(\overline{z}_{N},t_{N})\,\mathcal{P}_{N-1}(\bm{z}^{\prime},\bm{t}^{\prime})=2\,\mathfrak{i}\,\mathcal{P}_{N}(\bm{z},\bm{t}),

finishing the proof.
∎

The Stieltjes inversion for Cauchy-type functions is, thus, the following.

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
g
be a Cauchy-type function given by a measure
\mu
. Then, it holds that

(4.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})\\
=\lim\limits_{\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}}\frac{1}{2\,\mathfrak{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})\bigg[\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|B|}g(\Psi_{B}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y}))\bigg]\mathrm{d}\bm{x}

for all
\mathcal{C}^{1}
-functions
\varphi\colon\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which there exists a constant
D\geq 0
such that
|\varphi(\bm{x})|\leq D\prod_{j=1}^{n}(1+x_{j}^{2})^{-1}
for all
\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Proof.

By the definition of Cauchy-type functions and Lemma
4.1
, it holds that

\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|B|}g(\Psi_{B}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y}))\\
=\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg[\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|B|}K_{n}(\Psi_{B}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y}),\bm{t})\bigg]\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})\\
=\frac{2\,\mathfrak{i}}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t}).

Hence,

\lim\limits_{\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}}\frac{1}{2\,\mathfrak{i}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})\bigg[\sum_{B\subseteq\{1,\ldots,n\}}(-1)^{|B|}g(\Psi_{B}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y}))\bigg]\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
=\lim\limits_{\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t})\bigg)\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\\
=\lim\limits_{\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}}\frac{1}{\pi^{n}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}\bigg)\mathrm{d}\mu(\bm{t}),

where the assumptions on the function
\varphi
and condition (
2.3
) for
\mu
justify the use of Fubini’s theorem to change the order of integration. The same assumptions permit for Lebesgue’s dominated convergence to be used, allowing us to take the limit as
\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}
before integrating with respect to the measure
\mu
. Noting that, by
e.g.

[
11
, pg. 111]
,

\lim\limits_{\bm{y}\to\bm{0}^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\varphi(\bm{x})\mathcal{P}_{n}(\bm{x}+\mathfrak{i}\,\bm{y},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\bm{x}=\pi^{n}\varphi(\bm{t})

finishes the proof.
∎

As an immediate corollary of the previous theorem, we may now establish that the correspondence between a Cauchy-type function and its defining measure
\mu
is, indeed, a bijection.

Corollary 4.3
.

Let
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
be two positive Borel measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfying the growth condition (
2.3
). Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu_{1}(\bm{t})=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}K_{n}(\bm{z},\bm{t})\mathrm{d}\mu_{2}(\bm{t})

for all
\bm{z}\in(\mathbb{C}\setminus\mathbb{R})^{n}
if and only if
\mu_{1}\equiv\mu_{2}
.
