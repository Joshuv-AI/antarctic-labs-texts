Kernel-based Impulse Response Identification with Side-Information on Steady-State Gain
M. Khosravi and R. S. Smith

Abstract
In this paper, we consider the problem of system identification when side-information is available on the steady-state (or DC) gain of the system. We formulate a general nonparametric identification method as an infinite-dimensional constrained convex program over the reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS) of stable impulse responses. The objective function of this optimization problem is the empirical loss regularized with the norm of RKHS, and the constraint is considered for enforcing the integration of the steady-state gain side-information. The proposed formulation addresses both the discrete-time and continuous-time cases. We show that this program has a unique solution obtained by solving an equivalent finite-dimensional convex optimization. This solution has a closed-form when the empirical loss and regularization functions are quadratic and exact side-information is considered. We perform extensive numerical comparisons to verify the efficiency of the proposed identification methodology.

The identification approaches discussed are only applicable when a large set of high-quality data is available.

Therefore, these estimation methodologies are not suitable for infinite impulse responses (IIR) and continuous-time systems, particularly when the dynamics have a very slowly decaying impulse response and considerably long memory.

In this paper, we develop a nonparametric identification approach where the side-information on the steady-state gain of the system is integrated into the proposed scheme. To leverage the powerful framework of kernel-based identification, we employ RKHS of stable impulse responses as the hypothesis space

54

For the initial infinite-dimensional formulation of the identification problem, we derive an equivalent finite-dimensional convex program with a unique solution.
This solution has a closed-form when exact side-information is considered, and the empirical loss and regularization functions used are quadratic. Furthermore, we provide results for improving the computational complexity of the presented approach by obtaining the closed form of quantities used in the algorithm. We perform extensive numerical simulations confirming the efficacy of the proposed identification method.

IV
The Estimation Problem: Existence and Uniqueness of the Solution

In this section, we formulate a constrained regularized empirical loss minimization to address estimation Problem
1
. For this purpose, in addition to an appropriate objective function, we introduce a suitable hypothesis space characterizing the feasible set of the optimization problem and a constraint encoding the side-information about the steady-state gain of the system. Furthermore, we study the existence and uniqueness property for the solution of the resulting problem.

IV-A

Stable Reproducing Kernel Hilbert Spaces

The hypothesis space taken for the estimation problem is a
reproducing kernel Hilbert space
(RKHS)
[
58
]
, which contains stable impulse responses.
Based on the structure of RKHS, we can investigate the problem and obtain a tractable approach for solving the estimation problem.
These features are provided by the
kernel function
, which characterizes the RKHS uniquely and completely.

Definition 1

(
[
58
]
)
.

Let
\mathds{k}:{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a non-zero symmetric measurable function. Then, we say
\mathds{k}
is a
positive-definite kernel
, or simply
kernel
, if
we have

\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}a_{i}\mathds{k}(t_{i},t_{j})a_{j}\geq 0,

(6)

for any
m\in{\mathbb{N}}
,
t_{1},\ldots,t_{m}\in{\mathbb{T}}
and
a_{1},\ldots,a_{m}\in{\mathbb{R}}
.
If
\mathds{k}
is assumed to be continuous when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, then it is called a Mercer kernel.
The section of kernel
\mathds{k}
at
t\in{\mathbb{T}}
, denoted by
\mathds{k}_{t}
,
is the function defined as
\mathds{k}(t,\cdot):{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.

Theorem 1

(
[
58
]
)
.

Given positive-definite kernel
\mathds{k}:{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
, there exists a unique Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\subseteq{\mathbb{R}}^{{\mathbb{T}}}
equipped with inner product
{\langle{\cdot,\cdot}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}
, called a
RKHS with kernel

\mathds{k}
, such that, for any
t\in{\mathbb{T}}
, we have

i)

\mathds{k}_{t}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
, and

ii)

{\langle{{\mathrm{g}},\mathds{k}_{t}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}=g_{t}
, for all
{\mathrm{g}}=(g_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
.

The second feature is called the reproducing property.

According to Theorem
1
, a RKHS is completely characterized by the corresponding kernel.
As we are interested in the stable impulse responses in the bounded-input-bounded-output (BIBO) sense, we need to employ a kernel such that we have
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\subseteq{\mathscr{L}}^{1}
.
The following theorem provides a necessary and sufficient condition for this feature.

Theorem 2

(
[
28
,
59
]
)
.

Let
\mathds{k}:{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be a positive-definite kernel. Then,
\mathds{k}
is stable if and only if, for any
{\mathrm{u}}=(u_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{T}}}\in{\mathscr{L}}^{\infty}
,
we have

\sum_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\bigg|\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}u_{s}\mathds{k}(t,s)\bigg|<\infty,

(7)

when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, and,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}u_{s}\mathds{k}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

(8)

when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
The kernel
\mathds{k}
is said to be

when it satisfies this property.

The following stable kernels are frequently used in the literature
[
28
]
:

•

diagonally/correlated
(DC) kernel:

\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}(s,t)=\alpha^{\max(s,t)}\gamma^{|s-t|},

(9)

•

(TC) kernel:

\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}(s,t)=\alpha^{\max(s,t)},

(10)

•

stable spline
(SS) kernel:

\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}(s,t)=\alpha^{\max(s,t)+s+t}-\frac{1}{3}\alpha^{3\max(s,t)},

where
\alpha,\gamma\in{\mathbb{R}}
are such that
\alpha\in(0,1)
,
|\gamma|\in(0,\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}})
, if
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, and,
\gamma\in(0,\sqrt{\alpha^{-1}})
, if
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Note that by setting
\rho=\sqrt{\alpha}\gamma
, the definition of DC kernel in
[

]
reduces to (

]

IV-B

Empirical Loss and Regularization Function

Given the set of data
{\mathscr{D}}
and the hypothesis space
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
, the
empirical loss function
,

{\mathcal{E}}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, can be defined as the sum of squared errors, i.e., for each
{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
, we have

{\mathcal{E}}({\mathrm{g}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\big({\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{g}})-y_{t_{i}}\big)^{2}.

(12)

We can consider a more general form for the empirical loss function.
More precisely, let
{\mathcal{I}}:=\{i_{k}|k=1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}\}
be a subset of
\{1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}\}
,
{\mathrm{y}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}
be the vector defined as
{\mathrm{y}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}=[y_{t_{i}}]_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}}
, and

\ell:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a given convex function.
Accordingly, we define the generalized
loss function
,
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
as follows

{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}({\mathrm{g}}):=\ell([{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{g}})]_{i\in{\mathcal{I}}},{\mathrm{y}}_{{\mathcal{I}}}),\qquad\forall{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}},

(13)

where subscript
\ell
is considered to highlight the role of function
\ell
in the definition of
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}
.
When
{\mathcal{I}}=\{1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}\}
and function
\ell:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is defined as
\ell({\mathrm{v}}_{1},{\mathrm{v}}_{2})=\|{\mathrm{v}}_{1}-{\mathrm{v}}_{2}\|^{2}
, for any
{\mathrm{v}}_{1},{\mathrm{v}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}
, the empirical loss
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}
reduces to the special case introduced in (
12
).
Also, to be robust with respect to outliers, one may take function
\ell:{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\times{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
as

\ell({\mathrm{v}}_{1},{\mathrm{v}}_{2})=\sum_{i=1}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}L_{\sigma}(\left[{\mathrm{v}}_{1}\right]_{(i)}-\left[{\mathrm{v}}_{2}\right]_{(i)}),\quad\forall{\mathrm{v}}_{1},{\mathrm{v}}_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}},

(14)

where, for given
\sigma\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, function
L_{\sigma}:{\mathbb{R}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is the
Huber loss
defined as follows

L_{\sigma}(e)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}e^{2},&\text{ if }|e|\leq\sigma,\\
\sigma(|e|-\frac{1}{2}\sigma),&\text{ otherwise, }\\
\end{cases}

(15)

or, it can be the smoothed version of (
15
), known as the
pseudo-Huber
function
[
60
]
, which is

L_{\sigma}(e)=(e^{2}+\sigma^{2})^{\frac{1}{2}}-\sigma^{2},\quad\forall e\in{\mathbb{R}}.

(16)

The resulting empirical loss function
{\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}
is more suitable to the cases where output measurements are subject to noise disturbances with large outliers.

Since the hypothesis space is a RKHS endowed with kernel
\mathds{k}
, we define a regularization term, enforcing desired attributes such as stability, based on the norm in
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
, i.e.,
\|\cdot\|_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}
. More precisely, let
\rho:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be a strictly increasing
convex function. Then, the regularization function,
{\mathcal{R}}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, is defined as
{\mathcal{R}}({\mathrm{g}})=\rho(\|{\mathrm{g}}\|_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}})
, .
Accordingly, the objective function for the estimation problem,
{\mathcal{J}}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, is defined as following

{\mathcal{J}}({\mathrm{g}}):={\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}({\mathrm{g}})+\lambda{\mathcal{R}}({\mathrm{g}}),\qquad\forall{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}},

(17)

where
\lambda>0
is the regularization weight.
Note that, in addition to enforcing desired attributes such as stability, the regularization term helps avoiding over-fitting phenomena, enhancing the numerical performance, and improving the bias-variance trade-off.

IV-C

Steady-State Gain Side-Information

Define the set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}])\subset{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
as follows

{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}]):=\Big\{{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\ \!\Big|\ \!\ell_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$0$}}}}({\mathrm{g}})\in[\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}]\Big\}.

(18)

The elements of
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}])
are exactly the ones satisfying the side-information on the steady-state gain of the system.
Therefore, the estimation Problem
1
is formulated as the following optimization problem

\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatornamewithlimits{min}\limits_{{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}&\ {\mathcal{E}}_{\ell}({\mathrm{g}})+\lambda{\mathcal{R}}({\mathrm{g}})\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\ {\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}]).\end{array}

(19)

The existence and uniqueness of the solution of optimization problem (
19
) depends on the topological properties of set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}])
which is characterized by operator
\ell_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$0$}}}}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
.
To study these properties, we need the notion of integrable kernels
[
28
]
.

Definition 2
.

The positive-definite kernel
\mathds{k}:{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is said to be
integrable

if

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}|\mathds{k}(s,t)|\ \!\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

(20)

when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, or, if

\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\sum_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}|\mathds{k}(s,t)|<\infty,

(21)

when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

One can easily see that each of the kernels
\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
,
\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
and
\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
is integrable (see Appendix
A-A
).
Moreover, for any integrable kernel
\mathds{k}
and any
{\mathrm{u}}=(u_{s})_{s\in{\mathbb{T}}}\in{\mathscr{L}}^{\infty}
,
we have

\sum_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\Big|\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}u_{s}\mathds{k}(t,s)\Big|\leq\|{\mathrm{u}}\|_{\infty}\sum_{t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}|\mathds{k}(t,s)|<\infty,

when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, and,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\!\bigg|\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\!u_{s}\mathds{k}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s\bigg|\mathrm{d}t\leq\|{\mathrm{u}}\|_{\infty}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\!\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\!|\mathds{k}(t,s)|\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t<\infty,

when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
. Accordingly, the integrable kernels are stable.
The main importance of integrable kernels in this paper is highlighted by the following theorems.
The next theorem is the cornerstone of this work.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\mathds{k}:{\mathbb{T}}\times{\mathbb{T}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
be an integrable Mercer kernel, and
\varphi_{0}=(\varphi_{0,t})_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}
be defined as following

\varphi_{0,t}=\begin{cases}\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(t,s),&\text{ if }{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\\
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s,&\text{ if }{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+},\\
\end{cases}

(22)

for any
t\in{\mathbb{T}}
.
Then,
\varphi_{0}
is well-defined and
\varphi_{0}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
. Moreover, we have

\ell_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$0$}}}}({\mathrm{g}})={\langle{\varphi_{0},{\mathrm{g}}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}},\qquad\forall{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}.

(23)

Furthermore, one can see

\|\varphi_{0}\|_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}^{2}=\begin{cases}\sum_{s,t\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(t,s),&\text{ if }{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+},\\
\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s\mathrm{d}t,&\text{ if }{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}.\\
\end{cases}

(24)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-B
.
∎

Theorem
3
says that integrability of kernel
\mathds{k}
implies that the steady-state operator
\ell_{0}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is a linear continuous functional.
Accordingly, throughout this paper, we assume
\mathds{k}
is an integrable kernel.
More precisely, we make the following assumption.

Assumption 1
.

The kernel
\mathds{k}
is an integrable Mercer kernel, for which there exists
\tau\in{\mathbb{T}}
such that

\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(\tau,s)\neq 0
, when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, or,

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(\tau,s)\mathrm{d}s\neq 0
, when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

The following theorem describes topological properties of set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}])
, and together with Theorem
3
, provides the necessary foundation to guarantee the introduced problem is well-defined.

Theorem 4
.

Let Assumption
1
hold.
Then, for any
\underline{\delta}
and
\overline{\delta}
such that
-\infty\leq\underline{\delta}\leq\overline{\delta}\leq\infty
, the set
{\mathscr{G}}_{\mathds{k}}([\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}])
is a non-empty, closed and convex subset of
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A-C
.
∎

IV-D

From Infinite to Finite Dimension

The optimization problem (
19
) is defined over the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
.
In the following, we show that (
19
) admits a unique solution in
{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
.
Furthermore, we introduce an equivalent convex
finite-dimensional program which provides a tractable approach to address (
19
).
To this end, we need to introduce additional definitions and mathematical properties for (
19
).
Theorem
4
has provided suitable properties for the feasible set of (
19
).
The next assumption and lemma provide foundations to show that the objective function in
(
19
) has desired features which are latter employed in the main theorem of this paper to show the existence and uniqueness for the solution of (
19
).

Assumption 2
.

The operator
{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{\tau}:{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}\to{\mathbb{R}}
is continuous for each
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}
.

When
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
and
{\mathrm{u}}
is a step function as in (
37
), one can show the continuity of
{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{\tau}
, for any
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, based on an argument similar to the proof of Theorem
3
.
Also, for the case of
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
, one can easily see that Assumption
2
holds if the system is initially at rest, or more generally, when
(u_{t})_{t\leq t_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}}
is finitely non-zero.
Given this assumption, we have the following theorem.

Lemma 5
.

Let Assumption
2
hold. Then, for each
\tau\in{\mathscr{T}}
,
there exists
\varphi_{\tau}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}}=(\varphi_{\tau,t}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}})_{t\in{\mathbb{T}}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}
such that

{\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{\tau}({\mathrm{g}})={\langle{\varphi_{\tau}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}},{\mathrm{g}}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}},\quad\forall{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}.

(25)

Furthermore, for any
t\in{\mathbb{T}}
,
we have

\varphi_{\tau,t}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}}=\begin{cases}\int_{{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(t,s)u_{\tau-s}\mathrm{d}s,&\text{ if }{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+},\\
\sum_{s\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}}\mathds{k}(t,s)u_{\tau-s},&\text{ if }{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}.\end{cases}

(26)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-D
.
∎

Recall the index set
{\mathcal{I}}=\{i_{k}|k=1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}\}
introduced in Section
IV-B
.
For
k=1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}
, let
\varphi_{k}
be defined as
\varphi_{t_{i}}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}}
with
i=i_{k}
.
Accordingly, we define matrices
\Phi
and
{\mathrm{A}}
respectively as

\Phi:=\big[{\langle{\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}\big]_{i=0,j=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}},n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{(n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}+1)\times(n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}+1)},

(27)

and

{\mathrm{A}}:=\big[{\langle{\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}\big]_{i=1,j=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}},n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}\times(n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}+1)}.

(28)

Note that
{\mathrm{A}}
is a sub-matrix of
\Phi
which contains the rows corresponding to the index set
{\mathcal{I}}
. Denote the columns of
\Phi
by
{\mathrm{a}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathrm{a}}_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}+1}
, i.e., we have
\Phi=[{\mathrm{a}}_{0},\ldots,{\mathrm{a}}_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}]
.
Since
\Phi
is a symmetric matrix, one can see that
{\mathrm{A}}=[{\mathrm{a}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{a}}_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}]^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\!
.

Given the above definitions and theorems, we can present our main theorem.

Theorem 6
.

Let Assumption
1
and
Assumption
2
hold. Then, the optimization problem (
19
) admits a
unique
solution
{\mathrm{g}}^{\star}
.
Moreover, there exists

{\mathrm{x}}^{\star}=[x_{0}^{\star},\ldots,x_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}^{\star}]^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}+1}
such that

{\mathrm{g}}^{\star}=x_{0}^{\star}\varphi_{0}+\ldots+x_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}^{\star}\varphi_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}}x_{i}^{\star}\varphi_{i}.

(29)

Furthermore,
{\mathrm{x}}^{\star}
is the solution of following convex program

\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatornamewithlimits{min}\limits_{{\mathrm{x}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{${\mathcal{I}}$}}}+1}}&\ \ell({\mathrm{A}}{\mathrm{x}},{\mathrm{y}}_{{\mathcal{I}}})+\lambda{\mathcal{R}}\big(({\mathrm{x}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\Phi{\mathrm{x}})^{\frac{1}{2}}\big)\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\ {\mathrm{a}}_{0}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{x}}\in[\underline{\delta},\overline{\delta}].\end{array}

(30)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-E
.
∎

In the literature, it is common to employ
the empirical loss (
12
) and the regularization function
{\mathcal{R}}({\mathrm{g}})=\|{\mathrm{g}}\|_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}^{2}
.
When the steady-state gain of the system is known to be
\delta\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the resulting impulse response estimation problem is as following

\begin{array}[]{cl}\operatornamewithlimits{min}\limits_{{\mathrm{g}}\in{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}&\ \operatornamewithlimits{\sum}\limits_{i=1}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\big({\mathrm{L}}^{\!{\mathrm{u}}}_{t_{i}}({\mathrm{g}})-y_{t_{i}}\big)^{2}+\lambda\big\|{\mathrm{g}}\big\|_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}^{2}\\
\mathrm{s.t.}&\ \ell_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$0$}}}}({\mathrm{g}})=\delta.\end{array}

(31)

The next corollary provides a closed-form solution for this optimization problem.

Corollary 7
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
6
, the convex program (
31
) has a
unique
solution
{\mathrm{g}}^{\star}
.
Moreover, there exist

{\mathrm{x}}^{\star}=[x_{0}^{\star},\ldots,x_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}^{\star}]^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}+1}
and
\lambda\in{\mathbb{R}}
such that
{\mathrm{g}}^{\star}
has the parametric form (
29
) and
[{\mathrm{x}}^{\star}{}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!,\gamma^{\star}]^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!
is a solution of the following system of linear equations

\begin{bmatrix}{\mathrm{Q}}&{\mathrm{a}}_{0}\\
{\mathrm{a}}_{0}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!&0\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}{\mathrm{x}}\\
\gamma\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}{\mathrm{A}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{y}}\\
\delta\end{bmatrix},

(32)

where
{\mathrm{Q}}={\mathrm{A}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{A}}+\lambda\Phi
and
{\mathrm{y}}=[y_{t_{i}}]_{i=1}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}
.
Furthermore, when
\varphi_{0},\ldots,\varphi_{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}
are linearly independent, we have

{\mathrm{x}}^{\star}={\mathrm{Q}}^{-1}{\mathrm{A}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{y}}+\frac{\delta-{\mathrm{a}}_{0}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{Q}}^{-1}{\mathrm{A}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{y}}}{{\mathrm{a}}_{0}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!{\mathrm{Q}}^{-1}{\mathrm{a}}_{0}}{\mathrm{Q}}{\mathrm{a}}_{0}.

(33)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-F
. ∎

V
The Optimization Problem: Settings and Algorithm

Based on Theorem
6
, addressing the estimation problem
1
, or equivalently, optimization problem (
19
), reduces to solving the convex program (
30
). In this section, we discuss how to configure this optimization problem.

The main elements of optimization problems (
30
) are
{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
,
{\mathrm{A}}
, and
\Phi
.
We know that
{\mathrm{A}}
is a sub-matrix of
\Phi
and
{\mathrm{a}}_{0}
is the first column of
\Phi
. Hence, it suffices to calculate the matrix
\Phi
. According to (
27
), the entries of
\Phi
are inner products
{\langle{\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}
, for
i,j\in\{0,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}\}
.
To obtain the value of these inner products, we need to calculate improper double integrals when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, or infinite double summations when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.
In general, these calculations can be performed using techniques such as numerical integration.
On the other hand, these values can be obtained analytically in certain but fairly general situations. For example,
when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and the system is initially at rest, or,
when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, the standard kernels are employed and the input of the system is a step function.
In the remainder of this section, the details of these calculations are discussed.

V-A

Optimization Problem Configuration: Discrete-Time Case

Let
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
and the system be initially at rest, i.e., we have
u_{t}=0
, for
t<0
.
Also, let the measurement time instants be
{\mathscr{T}}=\{0,1,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1\}
.
Given an integrable kernel
\mathds{k}:{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{Z}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
and an input
{\mathrm{u}}
, define matrices
{\mathrm{K}},{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{u}}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}\times n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}
such that

[{\mathrm{K}}]_{(i,j)}=\mathds{k}(i-1,j-1),\quad\forall i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}\},

(34)

and

[{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{u}}}]_{(i,j)}=u_{i-j},\quad\forall i,j\in\{1,2,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}\}.

(35)

Following these definitions, we have the next theorem.

Theorem 8
.

Let
\varphi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}
be the column vector defined as
\varphi:=[\varphi_{0,i}]_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}-1}
.
Then, we have

\Phi=\begin{bmatrix}\|\varphi_{0}\|^{2}&\varphi{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{u}}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\\
{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{u}}}\varphi&{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{u}}}{\mathrm{K}}{\mathrm{T}}_{{\mathrm{u}}}^{{\scalebox{.63}{$\mathsf{T}$ }}}\!\end{bmatrix}.

(36)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-G
.
∎

Remark 1
.

Appendix
A-H
provides
\varphi_{0}
and
\|\varphi_{0}\|^{2}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}
for the standard kernels introduced in (
9
), (
10
), and (
11
), when
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{Z}}_{+}
.

V-B

Optimization Problem Configuration: Continuous-Time Case

The set of step functions is dense in
L^{p}({\mathbb{R}})
, for
p\in[1,\infty)
, and also, any function in
L^{\infty}({\mathbb{R}})
is an almost everywhere the point-wise limit of a sequence of step functions
[
61
]
. In other words, any signal of interest can be approximated arbitrarily closely by step functions.
Accordingly, for the case of
{\mathbb{T}}={\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, one can assume that the input signal
{\mathrm{u}}=(u_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
is a step function. More precisely, there exist
n_{\text{s}}\in{\mathbb{N}}
real scalars
\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{n_{\text{s}}}

and a finite increasing sequence
(s_{0},s_{1},\ldots,s_{n_{\text{s}}})
in
{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that we have

u_{t}=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{\text{s}}-1}\xi_{i+1}\mathbf{1}_{[s_{i},s_{i+1})}(t),\quad\forall t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(37)

For
{\mathrm{u}}=(u_{t})_{t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}}
given in (
37
), a closed-form for
\varphi_{\tau}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}}
can be introduced.
To this end, we require the function
\psi:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
defined as

\psi(t,a,b):=\int_{a}^{b}\mathds{k}(t,s)\mathrm{d}s,

(38)

for any
a,b,t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.
This function is denoted by
\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
,
\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
or
\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
respectively when

\mathds{k}
is
\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
,
\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
or
\mathds{k}_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
.

Theorem 9
.

For any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we have

\varphi_{\tau,t}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}}=\sum_{i=0}^{n_{\text{s}}-1}\xi_{i+1}\psi(t,\bar{s}_{i+1}(\tau),\bar{s}_{i}(\tau)),

(39)

where, for
i=0,\ldots,n_{\text{s}}
, function
\bar{s}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is defined as
\bar{s}_{i}(\tau):=\max(\tau-s_{i},0)
, for any
\tau\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
.

Proof.

See Appendix
A-I
.
∎

For the standard kernels defined in (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
),
one can obtain the closed-form of
\varphi_{\tau}^{\text{\rm{(u)}}}
using (
39
).
To this end, we need
\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}
,
\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}
, and
\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}
which are provided by the next theorem.

Theorem 10
.

Define the function
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}
as

\eta(s,\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\min(\max(t,\tau_{1}),\tau_{2}),

(40)

for any
s,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, and let
t,a,b\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
such that
a\leq b
.
Then, we have

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}}}(t,a,b)

\displaystyle\!=\!\big(\eta(t,a,b)-a\big)\alpha^{t}+\frac{\alpha^{b}-\alpha^{\eta(t,a,b)}}{\ln(\alpha)},

(41)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}}}(t,a,b)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{\gamma^{-a}-\gamma^{-\eta(t,a,b)}}{\ln(\gamma)}(\alpha\gamma)^{t}

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{(\alpha\gamma)^{b}-(\alpha\gamma)^{\eta(t,a,b)}}{\ln(\alpha\gamma)}\gamma^{-t},

(42)

\displaystyle\!\!\!\!\psi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}}}(t,a,b)

\displaystyle\!=\!\frac{\alpha^{\eta(t,a,b)}-\alpha^{a}}{\ln(\alpha)}\alpha^{2t}+\frac{\alpha^{2b}-\alpha^{2\eta(t,a,b)}}{2\ln(\alpha)}\alpha^{t}

\displaystyle\ \ \ -\frac{1}{3}\big(\eta(t,a,b)-a\big)\alpha^{3t}\!-\!\frac{\alpha^{3b}-\alpha^{3\eta(t,a,b)}}{9\ln(\alpha)}.

(43)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-J
.
∎

Similar to the previous theorem, one can obtain the closed-form of
\varphi_{0}
for the standard kernels (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
).

Theorem 11
.

For any
t\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we have

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{TC}$}}},0,t}

\displaystyle=\left(t-\frac{1}{\ln(\alpha)}\right)\alpha^{t},

(44)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{DC}$}}},0,t}

\displaystyle=-\Big[\frac{(1-\gamma^{t})}{\ln(\gamma)}+\frac{1}{\ln(\alpha\gamma)}\Big]\alpha^{t},

(45)

\displaystyle\varphi_{\text{{\scalebox{.75}{$\mathrm{SS}$}}},0,t}

\displaystyle=\Big[\frac{11}{18\ln(\alpha)}\alpha^{t}-\frac{1}{\ln(\alpha)}-\frac{t\alpha^{t}}{3}\Big]\alpha^{2t}.

(46)

Proof.

See Appendix
A-K
. ∎

Given
\{\varphi_{i}\}_{i=0}^{n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}}
, we can obtain
{\langle{\varphi_{i},\varphi_{j}}\rangle}_{{\mathscr{H}}_{\mathds{k}}}
, for
i,j=0,\ldots,n_{{\scalebox{.5}{$\!{\mathscr{D}}$}}}
.
To this end, define functions

\nu:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}

and

\bar{\nu}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}

respectively as

\nu(x,y):=\int_{0}^{x}\!\int_{0}^{y}\!\mathds{k}(s,t)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\forall x,y\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+},

(47)

and

\bar{\nu}(x):=\int_{0}^{x}\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\mathds{k}(s,t)\mathrm{d}t\mathrm{d}s,\qquad\forall x\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}.

(48)

When
\mathds{k}
is one of the standard kernels (
9
), (
10
) and (
11
),
we include a suitable subscript in
\nu
and
\bar{\nu}
to indicate the corresponding kernel.
For each
i,j\in\{0,1,\ldots,n_{\text{s}}-1\}
, let functions
\kappa_{ij}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}

and

\bar{\kappa}_{i}:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\times{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\to{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
be defined
such that, for any
\tau,\tau_{1},\tau_{2}\in{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
, we have

\begin{split}&\kappa_{ij}(\tau_{1},\tau_{2})=\nu\big(\bar{s}_{i}(\tau_{1}),\bar{s}_{j}(\tau_{2})\big)-\nu\big(\bar{s}_{i+1}(\tau_{1}),\bar{s}_{j}(\tau_{2})\big)\\
&\quad-\nu\big(\bar{s}_{i}(\tau_{1}),\bar{s}_{j+1}(\tau_{2})\big)+\nu\big(\bar{s}_{i+1}(\tau_{1}),\bar{s}_{j+1}(\tau_{2})\big),\end{split}

(49)

and

\bar{\kappa}_{i}(\tau)=\bar{\nu}\big(\bar{s}_{i}(\tau)\big)-\bar{\nu}\big(\bar{s}_{i+1}(\tau)\big).

To employ Algorithm

{\mathscr{D}}
, an appropriate stable kernel

and the regularization weight
\lambda

\lambda

\mathds{k}

[

(57)

where

. One can see that the dependency of model evaluation metric

[

VII
Conclusion

In this work, we have addressed the impulse response identification problem when side-information on the steady-state gain of the system is provided. The problem is formulated as a generic nonparametric identification in the form of an infinite-dimensional constrained convex program over the RKHS of stable impulse responses. This optimization problem is designed such that the objective function corresponds to the regularized empirical loss, and the imposed linear constraints enforce the integration of the given side-information into the solution. We have shown that the steady-state gain is a bounded operator over the employed RKHS, which results in guaranteeing the existence and uniqueness of the solution. By using the representer theorem, the optimization problem is reduced to a finite-dimensional convex program, which can be solved efficiently. In the case of exact side-information, quadratic empirical loss, and quadratic regularization, the identification problem has a closed-form solution.
Compared to the existing methods, the proposed identification approach can utilize non-uniform measurement samples and integrate steady-state gain side-information into the direct approach of continuous-time system identification.
Moreover, through an extensive Monte Carlo numerical experiment, we have verified that the introduced methodology outperforms the benchmark approaches.
The proposed scheme has several features which have led to the observed superior performance, including direct estimation of the impulse response without any truncation and
without inexact numerical procedures such as discrete derivatives. The method uses
kernel-based regularization to enforce the BIBO stability of the estimated impulse response, and, effective model selection and complexity tuning through estimating continuous variables such as regularization weight and hyperparameters.
