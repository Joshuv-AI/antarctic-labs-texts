Accurate, scalable, and efficient Bayesian optimal experimental design with derivative-informed neural operators
Jinwoo Go, Peng Chen

Abstract
We consider optimal experimental design (OED) problems in selecting the most informative observation sensors to estimate model parameters in a Bayesian framework. Such problems are computationally prohibitive when the parameter-to-observable (PtO) map is expensive to evaluate, the parameters are high-dimensional, and the optimization for sensor selection is combinatorial and high-dimensional. To address these challenges, we develop an accurate, scalable, and efficient computational framework based on derivative-informed neural operators (DINO). We propose to use derivative-informed dimension reduction to reduce the parameter dimensions, based on which we train DINO with derivative information as an accurate and efficient surrogate for the PtO map and its derivative. Moreover, we derive DINO-enabled efficient formulations in computing the maximum a posteriori (MAP) point, the eigenvalues of approximate posterior covariance, and three commonly used optimality criteria for the OED problems. Furthermore, we provide detailed error analysis for the approximations of the MAP point, the eigenvalues, and the optimality criteria. We also propose a modified swapping greedy algorithm for the sensor selection optimization and demonstrate that the proposed computational framework is scalable to preserve the accuracy for increasing parameter dimensions and achieves high computational efficiency, with an over 1000 × \times speedup accounting for both offline construction and online evaluation costs, compared to high-fidelity Bayesian OED solutions for a three-dimensional nonlinear convection-diffusion-reaction example with tens of thousands of parameters.

Introduction

Mathematical modeling and computational simulation become increasingly crucial for understanding and optimizing complex systems in many scientific and engineering fields. In practical applications, various uncertainties arise in the modeling and simulation either because of the lack of knowledge of the systems (epistemic uncertainty) or the inherent randomness in the systems (aleatoric uncertainty) that cannot be reduced. To reduce the epistemic uncertainty commonly represented as uncertain model parameters, it is essential to acquire knowledge or data of the systems by proper experimental design and calibrate the model parameters by either deterministic or statistical/Bayesian inference from the experimental or observational data. However, these experiments may be costly or take an enormous time or space. This is where optimal experimental design (OED) comes into play. Contributions.
We propose addressing the computational challenges in Bayesian OED by developing an accurate, scalable, and efficient computational framework based on the derivative-informed neural operator (DINO)

Problem statement

We present Bayesian inverse problems constrained by PDEs with infinite-dimensional parameters, Bayesian OED problems in the context of optimal sensor placement, and the challenges for their solutions.

2.1
Bayesian inverse problem

We consider Bayesian inverse problems to infer uncertain parameters in mathematical models described by PDEs written in an abstract form as

{\mathcal{R}}(u,m)=0\text{ in }{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime},

(1)

where
u\in{\mathcal{V}}
is the state variable in a separable Banach space
{\mathcal{V}}
defined in physical domain
D\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
, e.g., in dimension
d=1,2,3
;
m
represents the uncertain parameter. Specifically, we consider
m
as a random field parameter in a Hilbert space
{\mathcal{M}}
defined in
D
, which is infinite-dimensional. The operator
{\mathcal{R}}:{\mathcal{V}}\times{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
represents the strong form of the PDE, where
{\mathcal{V}}^{\prime}
is the dual of
{\mathcal{V}}
.

Let
{\mathcal{B}}:{\mathcal{V}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
denote an observation operator that maps the state variable
u
to a vector of
d_{s}
-dimensional observation functionals, with
d_{s}\in{\mathbb{N}}
. We define a corresponding PtO map
{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}}
as
{\mathcal{F}}(m):={\mathcal{B}}(u(m))
.
In particular, we consider the observation made in
d_{s}
sensor locations
({\boldsymbol{x}}_{1},...,{\boldsymbol{x}}_{d_{s}})
with
{\boldsymbol{x}}_{i}\in D
indicating the
i
-th sensor location in domain
D
. In the OED problem, we can only choose
r_{s}\in{\mathbb{N}}
out of the
d_{s}
candidate sensors because of the budget constraint, where typically
d_{s}\gg r_{s}
. We use a design matrix
\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}\times r_{s}}
to represent the selection of
r_{s}
sensors out of the
d_{s}
candidates, with
\xi_{ij}
= 1 if the
j

i
-th location

, and
\xi_{ij}=0
if not. Moreover, we consider that one location can not be placed with more than one sensor. Mathematically, we define the admissible design matrix set
\Xi
as

\Xi=\left\{\xi\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{s}\times r_{s}}:\xi_{ij}\in\{0,1\},\ \sum_{i=1}^{d_{s}}\xi_{ij}=1,\ \sum_{j=1}^{r_{s}}\xi_{ij}\in\{0,1\}\right\}.

(2)

For a given design matrix
\xi\in\Xi
, we denote the corresponding PtO map as
{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}=\xi^{T}{\mathcal{F}}:{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{s}}
. We consider that the

{\boldsymbol{y}}
is corrupted with an additive observation noise
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}
, given as

{\boldsymbol{y}}={\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)+\boldsymbol{\epsilon},

(3)

where we assume the observation noise obeys Gaussian distribution
\boldsymbol{\epsilon}\sim{\mathcal{N}}({\boldsymbol{0}},\Gamma_{\text{noise}})
with zero mean and covariance
\Gamma_{\text{noise}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{s}\times r_{s}}
. Under this assumption, the likelihood of the data
{\boldsymbol{y}}
for a parameter
m

\pi_{\text{like}}({\boldsymbol{y}}|m,\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^{r}|\Gamma_{\text{noise}}|}}\exp\left(-\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},m,\xi)\right),

(4)

where
|\Gamma_{\text{noise}}|
denotes the determinant of
\Gamma_{\text{noise}}

represents a data-model misfit term given as

\Phi({\boldsymbol{y}},m,\xi)=\frac{1}{2}||{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)||^{2}_{\Gamma_{\text{noise}}^{-1}}=\frac{1}{2}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m))^{T}\Gamma_{\text{noise}}^{-1}({\boldsymbol{y}}-{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)).

(5)

Given data
{\boldsymbol{y}}
in (
3
), the inverse problem is to infer the parameter
m

1

\mu_{\text{post}}

We consider a Gaussian prior

In particular, we use a common choice of Matérn covariance operator

\frac{d\mu_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m)}{d\mu_{\text{prior}}(m)}=\frac{1}{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}\pi_{\text{like}}({\boldsymbol{y}}|m,\xi),

where

2.2
Bayesian optimal experimental design

The Bayesian OED problem in our context seeks the optimal placement of
r_{s}
sensors out of
d_{s}
candidate locations that provide maximum information to infer the parameter. For this task, we consider several widely used optimality criteria, including
the variance-based A-optimality criterion and D-optimality criterion, and the information theory-based EIG or mutual information.

The variance-based A-/D-optimality criteria are defined for a Laplace approximation of the posterior distribution
given by

\hat{\mu}_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}={\mathcal{N}}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi},{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})
. Here,
m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}
denotes the MAP point defined as

m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}:=\arg\min_{m\in{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}}\frac{1}{2}||{\boldsymbol{y}}-{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)||^{2}_{\Gamma_{\text{noise}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}||m-m_{\text{prior}}||^{2}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{prior}}^{-1}},

(8)

where
{\mathcal{C}^{\prime}}
= range
({\mathcal{C}}_{\text{prior}}^{1/2})
is the Cameron–Martin space
da2006introduction
of the Gaussian measure
\mu_{\text{prior}}
, which is a subspace of
{\mathcal{M}}
.
The covariance operator
{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{post}}
is given by

{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}=({\mathcal{H}}_{\text{misfit}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})+{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{prior}}^{-1})^{-1},

(9)

where
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{misfit}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})
is the Hessian of data-model misfit term
\Phi(m,{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi)
with respect to
m
evaluated at
m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}
. A common choice for
{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{misfit}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})
in infinite-/high-dimensional Bayesian OED is to use its Gauss–Newton approximation

{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{misfit}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})\approx{\mathcal{H}}_{\text{misfit}}^{\text{GN}}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}):=\nabla_{m}{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})^{T}\Gamma_{\text{noise}}^{-1}\nabla_{m}{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}),

(10)

with the Jacobian
\nabla_{m}{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}):{\mathcal{M}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{s}}
evaluated at the MAP point
m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}
. This corresponds to a Fisher information matrix-based optimality criterion
schraudolph2002fast
.
The Gauss–Newton approximation of the Hessian is exact if the PtO map
{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)
is linear with respect to
m
, or it can be considered as a linear approximation of
{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)
at the MAP point, i.e.,

{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m)\approx{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})+\nabla_{m}{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})(m-m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}).

(11)

We note that for the A-/D-optimality criteria, we need to obtain the MAP point
m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}
by solving the optimization problem (
8

\nabla_{m}{\mathcal{F}}_{\xi}(m_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})
at the MAP point, both of which are constrained by the PDE (

{\boldsymbol{y}}
and the design matrix
\xi

To this end, we consider the Bayesian OED problem with the A-optimality criterion defined as

\xi^{*}_{A}=\arg\min_{\xi\in\Xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}[\text{trace}({\mathcal{C}}_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}}))],

(12)

where trace
(\cdot)
denotes the trace of the covariance operator
{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}(m^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}})

9

is taken with respect to the distribution of the data
{\boldsymbol{y}}
conditioned on
\xi
.
This expectation can be evaluated by the double integral
{\mathbb{E}}_{m\sim\mu_{\text{prior}}}{\mathbb{E}}_{{\boldsymbol{y}}\sim\pi_{\text{like}}(\cdot|m,\xi)}[\cdot]
.

For the D-optimality criterion, we use the definition from

as

\xi^{*}_{D}=\arg\max_{\xi\in\Xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}[\log\text{det}(\mathcal{I}+\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}})],

(13)

with the prior-preconditioned Hessian of the misfit term
\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}={\mathcal{C}}_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}{\mathcal{H}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}(m^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}}){\mathcal{C}}_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}

\xi^{*}_{\text{EIG}}=\arg\max_{\xi\in\Xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}\left[D_{\text{KL}}(\mu_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}||\mu_{\text{prior}})\right],

where the information gain is measured by the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between the posterior distribution

\begin{split}D_{\text{KL}}(\hat{\mu}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{post}}||\mu_{\text{prior}})=\frac{1}{2}\log\text{det}(I+\tilde{{\mathcal{H}}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}})-\frac{1}{2}\text{trace}({\mathcal{H}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}{\mathcal{C}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{post}})+\frac{1}{2}||m^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}}-m_{\text{prior}}||^{2}_{{\mathcal{C}}_{\text{prior}}^{-1}}.\end{split}

(17)

Several computational challenges arise in solving the Bayesian OED problems with these optimality criteria, including: (1) the dimension of the uncertain parameter space and the design space can be infinite or very high, which brings the curse of dimensionality, i.e., the computational/sampling complexity increases exponentially with respect to the dimensions; (2) each evaluation of the optimality criteria (trace (

3.2
Laplace and low-rank approximations

By the high-dimensional discretization, we can write the Laplace approximation of the posterior distribution of
{\boldsymbol{m}}
as
\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi},\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{post}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}))
, where the MAP point
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}
is obtained from solving the following finite/high-dimensional optimization problem corresponding to (

) in the function space

{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}=\arg\min_{{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\boldsymbol{m}}}}\frac{1}{2}||{\boldsymbol{y}}-F_{\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}})||^{2}_{\Gamma_{\text{noise}}^{-1}}+\frac{1}{2}||{\boldsymbol{m}}-{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}}||^{2}_{\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}},

(21)

with

denoting a map from
{\boldsymbol{m}}
to the observables corresponding to a discrete version of the PtO map
\mathcal{F}_{\xi}
for a given sensor selection
\xi
. To solve this optimization problem, we can employ, e.g., an inexact Newton–CG algorithm, which requires the computation of the derivative of
F_{\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}})
with respect to
{\boldsymbol{m}}
.

The posterior covariance matrix
\Gamma_{\text{post}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})
evaluated at
{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}
is given by

\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{post}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})=({H}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})+\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1})^{-1},

(22)

where

is a discrete approximation of
{\mathcal{H}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}(m_{\text{MAP}})
in (
10

H^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})\approx H^{\text{GN}}_{\text{misfit}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})=(\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F_{\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}))^{T}\Gamma_{\text{noise}}^{-1}\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F_{\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}).

(23)

The posterior covariance matrix
\Gamma_{\text{post}}
is high-dimensional and involves the Jacobian
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F_{\xi}
and its transpose which requires solving the PDE and its adjoint. To efficiently compute the posterior covariance, we employ a low-rank decomposition by first solving a generalized eigenvalue problem

{H}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}){\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}=\lambda_{i}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i},\ \ \ i=1,...,r,\\

(24)

with eigenpairs
(\lambda_{i},{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,r
, corresponding to the largest
r
eigenvalues
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}
, and
{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i}^{T}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{w}}_{j}=\delta_{ij}
with
i,j=1,\dots,r
. It requires
O(r)
linearized PDE solves using a randomized SVD algorithm
VillaPetraGhattas21
, see
WuChenGhattas23a
for more details. Let
\Lambda_{r}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{r})
,
W_{r}=({\boldsymbol{w}}_{1},\dots,{\boldsymbol{w}}_{r})
, and
V_{r}=\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1/2}W_{r}
, we have the low-rank approximation from (

)

\tilde{H}_{\text{misfit}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})=\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}{H}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}\approx V_{r}\Lambda_{r}V_{r}^{T},

(25)

which is accurate for a relatively small

as long as the eigenvalues decay fast with
\lambda_{r+1}\ll 1
.
To this end, the posterior covariance can be approximated by

\begin{split}\Gamma^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{post}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})&=\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}(I+\tilde{H}_{\text{misfit}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}))^{-1}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}\\
&\approx\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}(I+V_{r}\Lambda_{r}V_{r}^{T})^{-1}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}\\
&=\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}(I-V_{r}D_{r}V_{r}^{T})\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}\\
&=\Gamma_{\text{prior}}-W_{r}D_{r}W_{r}^{T},\end{split}

(26)

where we factorized
\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}
in the first equality, used low-rank approximation (
25
) in the inequality, employed Sherman–Morrison–Woodbury formula for the second equality with
D_{r}=\text{diag}(\lambda_{1}/(1+\lambda_{1}),\dots,\lambda_{r}/(1+\lambda_{r}))
, and used the definition of

with
\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}V_{r}=W_{r}
in the last equality.

3.3
High-fidelity Bayesian OED

By applying the Laplace and low-rank approximations of the posterior, we can compute the approximate A-optimality optimal design corresponding to (

\begin{split}\xi_{A}^{*}=\arg\min_{\xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}\left[\text{trace}(\Gamma_{\text{prior}}-W_{r}D_{r}W_{r}^{T})\right]=\arg\max_{\xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}\left[\text{trace}(W_{r}D_{r}W_{r}^{T})\right],\end{split}

where the equality is due to that

does not depend on
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and

. For simplicity, we consider a simplified A-optimality criterion as

as

, so that

(28)

Note that this definition of the A-optimality is consistent with the definition of the D-optimality (

) as to minimize the trace and log determinant of the same quantity

and log determinant of

(29)

For the EIG criteria defined in (

\xi_{\text{EIG}}^{*}=\arg\max_{\xi}{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}\left[\sum_{i=1}^{r}\log(1+\lambda_{i})-\sum_{i=1}^{r}\frac{\lambda_{i}}{1+\lambda_{i}}+||{\boldsymbol{m}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}}-{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}}||^{2}_{\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}}\right].

Note that the first two terms correspond to the D-optimality and A-optimality criteria.

3.4
Computational challenges

We remark that both the eigenpairs
(\lambda_{i},{\boldsymbol{w}}_{i})
,
i=1,\dots,r
, and the MAP point
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}}
depend on the data sample
{\boldsymbol{y}}
and the design
\xi
. The conditional expectation
{\mathbb{E}}_{\pi({\boldsymbol{y}}|\xi)}
in the above three optimal design problems can be computed using sample average approximation (SAA) by drawing samples
{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(n)}=F_{\xi}({\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)})+{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{(n)}
,
n=1,\dots,N_{s}
, with
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)}
drawn from the prior distribution of
{\boldsymbol{m}}
and
\varepsilon^{n}
drawn from the observation noise distribution. Then we need to solve the optimization problem (
21
) for the MAP point and the generalized eigenvalue problem (
24
) for the eigenpairs at each of the
N_{s}
samples, which becomes computationally prohibitive for large
N_{s}
and large-scale PDEs to solve. We point out that the authors of
WuChenGhattas23a
replace the MAP point
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}}^{(n)},\xi}_{\text{MAP}}
by the prior sample
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)}
, which avoids solving the optimization problem (
21
). Moreover, they assume uncorrelated observation noise
{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}}^{(n)}
in each observation dimension, by which they only need to solve a generalized eigenvalue problem in high dimensions once and then solve
N_{s}
eigenvalue problems of size
r\times r
for each design
\xi
in the design optimization. However, the MAP point may differ from the prior sample, especially for a small number of observables, which can make their method less effective. The computational method in solving the generalized eigenvalue problem also becomes not applicable for correlated observation noise. In this work, we propose to efficiently compute both the MAP point and the eigenpairs by using derivative-enhanced surrogate models, which completely avoids solving the PDE models during the optimization for the optimal design once the surrogate models are constructed. Moreover, we do not need the observation noise (as required in
WuChenGhattas23a
) to be uncorrelated to use the surrogate models.

4
Bayesian OED with DINO

In this section, we present our approach for scalable and efficient computation of the optimality criteria based on DINO
OLeary-RoseberryChenVillEtAl24
and introduce a swapping greedy algorithm modified from
WuChenGhattas23a
to optimize the experimental design. To achieve the scalability of the DINO approximation with accurate derivative evaluation, we employ derivative-informed dimension reduction methods
OLeary-RoseberryVillaChenEtAl22
,
zahm2020gradient
to reduce the dimensions of both the input parameters and output observables. For the efficient computation of the optimality criteria, we formulate both the optimization of the MAP point problem (
21
) and the generalized eigenvalue problem (
24
) in the derivative-informed subspaces. Additionally, we present a complexity analysis of the high-fidelity and DINO approximations regarding the number of PDE solves for the full Bayesian OED optimization.

4.1
Derivative-informed dimension reduction

The random field parameter
m
defined in the function space
\mathcal{M}
is infinite-dimensional. By the high-fidelity discretization of
m
in Section
3.1
, the dimension
d_{\boldsymbol{m}}
of discrete parameters
{\boldsymbol{m}}
is often remarkably high. Meanwhile, the dimension
d_{F}
of candidate sensors or observables
F
is also set high to select the most informative sensors. The high dimensionality of input parameters and output observables makes building an accurate neural network surrogate of the PtO map challenging without a sufficiently large amount of training data and a significant training cost. To address this challenge, we present derivative-informed dimension reduction and a commonly used principal component analysis (PCA) for comparison.

In both the computation of the MAP point by a Newton-CG optimizer to solve (
21
) and the evaluation of the Gauss–Newton Hessian in (
23
), we need to compute the derivative
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F({\boldsymbol{m}})
. This motivates a derivative-informed dimension reduction for the input parameter
{\boldsymbol{m}}
. Specifically, let

{\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}})={\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}}+\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{\eta}}

(31)

with the whitened noise
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
. We can formally write the eigenvalue decomposition for input dimension reduction as

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}F^{T}({\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}))\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}F({\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}))\right]{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{(i)}=\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{(i)},\quad i=1,\dots,r,

(32)

with
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}
and
({\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{(i)})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{(j)}=\delta_{ij}
, which are equivalent to compute
OLeary-RoseberryVillaChenEtAl22
,
zahm2020gradient

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F^{T}({\boldsymbol{m}})\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F({\boldsymbol{m}})\right]{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}=\lambda_{i}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)},\quad i=1,\dots,r,

(33)

with
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}=\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{1/2}{\boldsymbol{\varphi}}^{(i)}
and
({\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)})^{T}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(j)}=\delta_{ij}
. The expectation can be computed by SAA. To this end, the input parameter dimension reduction can be computed by the linear projection

{\boldsymbol{m}}\approx{\boldsymbol{m}}_{r}:={\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}\quad\text{where}\quad\beta_{i}=({\boldsymbol{m}}-{\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}})^{T}\Gamma_{\text{prior}}^{-1}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}.

(34)

We also consider a derivative-informed output dimension reduction by writing

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}F({\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}))\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}F^{T}({\boldsymbol{m}}({\boldsymbol{\eta}}))\right]{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}=\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)},\quad i=1,\dots,r,

(35)

with
\lambda_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{r}
and
({\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(j)}=\delta_{ij}
, which are equivalent to compute

{\mathbb{E}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}\left[\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F({\boldsymbol{m}})\,\Gamma_{\text{prior}}\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F^{T}({\boldsymbol{m}})\right]{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}=\lambda_{i}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)},\quad i=1,\dots,r,

(36)

where the expectation is computed by SAA. Then, the projected output observables can be computed by

F\approx F_{r}:=\bar{F}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}\quad\text{where}\quad\beta_{i}=(F-\bar{F})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)},

(37)

where
\bar{F}
is a the sample mean of
F
. To distinguish the eigenvectors for the input and output dimension reduction, we use
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(i)}
for
{\boldsymbol{m}}
and
{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{F}^{(i)}
for
F
. We also name
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}=({\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(r)})
and
\Psi_{F}=({\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{F}^{(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}_{F}^{(r)})
as the derivative-informed input subspace (DIS) basis and derivative-informed output subspace (DOS) basis.

We present a commonly used PCA dimension reduction to compare the derivative-informed dimension reduction. Given
N
samples of the input-output pairs
({\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)},F^{(n)})
,
n=1,\dots,N
, where
F^{(n)}
is computed by solving the PDE model at
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)}
, we can perform dimension reduction of them by PCA or equivalently a truncated singular value decomposition (tSVD). Let matrix
B=(F^{(1)}-\bar{F},\dots,F^{(N)}-\bar{F})
with sample mean
\bar{F}
. The tSVD of
B
, which can be computed by a randomized SVD algorithm, is given by

B\approx B_{r}:=\Psi_{r}\Sigma_{r}\Phi_{r}^{T},

(38)

where
\Psi_{r}=({\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(r)})
and
\Phi_{r}=({\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(1)},\dots,{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^{(r)})
are the matrices with
r
columns of the left and right singular vectors respectively corresponding to the
r
largest singular values
\sigma_{1}\geq\dots\geq\sigma_{r}
with
\Sigma_{r}=\text{diag}(\sigma_{1},\dots,\sigma_{r})
. For any new
F
, we can perform the dimension reduction by the linear projection

F\approx F_{r}:=\bar{F}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}\quad\text{where }\quad\beta_{i}=(F-\bar{F})^{T}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}.

(39)

Note that instead of using the sample-based PCA dimension reduction for the input parameter, we can also compute a discrete version of a truncated Karhunen–Loève expansion (KLE) for
{\boldsymbol{m}}\sim\mathcal{N}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}},\Gamma_{\text{prior}})
as

{\boldsymbol{m}}\approx{\boldsymbol{m}}_{r}:={\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{prior}}+\sum_{i=1}^{r}\beta_{i}{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)}\quad\text{with}\quad\beta_{i}=\sqrt{\lambda_{i}}\eta_{i},

(40)

where
(\lambda_{i},{\boldsymbol{\psi}}^{(i)})
,
i=1,\dots,r
, are the eigenpairs of the covariance matrix
\Gamma_{\text{prior}}
, and
\eta_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
.
The sample-based PCA projection converges to the KLE projection with increasing samples.

4.2
Derivative-informed neural operators

Based on the dimension reduction for the input and output, we construct a neural network surrogate
F_{\text{NN}}({\boldsymbol{m}})
of the PtO map
F({\boldsymbol{m}})
, or the discrete observation operator (thus neural operator), as

F({\boldsymbol{m}})\approx F_{\text{NN}}({\boldsymbol{m}}):={\mathcal{D}}_{\Psi_{F}}\circ\Phi_{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}}\circ{\mathcal{E}}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}}({\boldsymbol{m}}),

(41)

where
{\mathcal{E}}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{\boldsymbol{m}}}
represents an encoder defined by the linear projection with basis
\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{\boldsymbol{m}}\times r_{\boldsymbol{m}}}
as in (
34
) by DIS or in (
40
) by KLE. The output of the encoder is the
r_{\boldsymbol{m}}
-dimensional coefficient vector
{\mathcal{E}}_{\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}}({\boldsymbol{m}})={\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}=(\beta_{1},\dots,\beta_{r_{\boldsymbol{m}}})^{T}
defined in the linear projections.
{\mathcal{D}}_{\Psi_{F}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{F}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{F}}
represents a decoder by the linear projection with basis
\Psi_{F}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d_{F}\times r_{F}}
as in (
37
) by DOS or in (
39
) by PCA. Note that the full dimension of the observable vector
F
is

, the same as the number of candidate sensors
d_{s}
.
The map
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{\boldsymbol{m}}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{F}}
is given by a neural network parametrized by parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
, which takes the
r_{\boldsymbol{m}}
-dimensional input
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}
and maps it to a
r_{F}
-dimensional output to approximate the coefficient vector
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{F}
of the linear projection of
F
as in (
37
) by DOS or in (
39
) by PCA.
One example is given by

\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{z}}):=Z_{L}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{L}+{\boldsymbol{b}}_{L},

(42)

where

{\boldsymbol{z}}_{l+1}={\boldsymbol{z}}_{l}+W_{l}h_{l}(Z_{l}{\boldsymbol{z}}_{l}+{\boldsymbol{b}}_{l}),\quad l=0,\dots,L-1,

(43)

is a sequence of ResNets
he2016deep
with input
{\boldsymbol{z}}_{0}={\boldsymbol{z}}
,

represents elementwise nonlinear activation functions,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}=(W_{0},Z_{0},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{0},\dots,W_{L-1},Z_{L-1},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{L-1},Z_{L},{\boldsymbol{b}}_{L})
collects all the neural network parameters. Note that the neural network size depends only on
r_{F}
and
r_{\boldsymbol{m}}
, which is small, so a relatively small number of data would be sufficient for the training of the neural network.

To generate the training data, we draw
N_{t}
random samples
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)}
and solve the PDE to obtain
F^{(n)}
,
n=1,\dots,N_{t}
. Then we project them to their corresponding reduced basis and obtain
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)}
and
{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{F}^{(n)}
. Note that the samples in computing the projection basis in the last section do not need the same samples drawn here. In practice, we take a subset of samples drawn here to compute the projection basis first. To construct the neural network surrogate that is accurate not only for the PtO map
F({\boldsymbol{m}})
but also for its Jacobian

, we also compute a reduced Jacobian
J_{r}^{(n)}=\Psi_{F}^{T}J^{(n)}\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{F}\times r_{\boldsymbol{m}}}
with the full Jacobian
J^{(n)}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F^{(n)}
at each of the
N
samples. This computation requires solving
\min(r_{F},r_{\boldsymbol{m}})
linearized PDEs corresponding to the PDE model (
1
). These linearized PDEs have the same linear operator
\partial_{u}\mathcal{R}
or its adjoint at each sample
{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)}
. The computational cost can be amortized by, e.g., one LU factorization of the linear operator and repeated use of this factorization as a direct solver.

To this end, we define the loss function for the training of the neural network with two terms as

\ell({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=\frac{1}{N_{t}}\sum_{n=1}^{N_{t}}||{\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{F}^{(n)}-\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)})||_{2}^{2}+\lambda||J_{r}^{(n)}-\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{(n)})||_{2}^{2},

(44)

where the first term measures the difference between the projected PtO map and its neural network approximation in Euclidean norm, and the second term is informed by the derivative (thus DINO
OLeary-RoseberryChenVillEtAl24
) and measures the difference of their Jacobians in Frobenius norm. Note that
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{r_{F}\times r_{\boldsymbol{m}}}
represents the Jacobian of the neural network output
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
with respect to its input, which can be computed by automatic differentiation. The evaluation of the loss function and its gradient with respect to the parameters
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is very efficient since all quantities depend only on the small reduced dimensions
r_{F}
and
r_{\boldsymbol{m}}
. We remark that the scaling parameter
\lambda
in the loss function (

is sufficient to improve the PtO map’s accuracy and its reduced Jacobian.

4.3
Efficient computation of the MAP point and the eigenpairs with DINO

Once the neural network
\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
defined in (

Specifically, we solve the following optimization problem to compute the MAP point,

{\boldsymbol{\beta}}

To compute the eigenpairs of (

\nabla_{\boldsymbol{m}}F_{\text{NN}}({\boldsymbol{m}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi})=\Psi_{F}\,\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\beta}}\Phi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{MAP}})\,\Psi_{\boldsymbol{m}}^{T}\Gamma^{-1}_{\text{prior}},

Second, by observing the similarity of the generalized eigenvalue problem (

\hat{H}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}_{\text{misfit}}({\boldsymbol{\beta}}_{\text{MAP}}^{{\boldsymbol{y}},\xi}){\boldsymbol{u}}_{i}=\hat{\lambda}_{i}{\boldsymbol{u}}_{i},\quad i=1,\dots,r_{\boldsymbol{m}},

(47)

where the reduced matrix

4.4
Swapping greedy algorithm

This section presents a swapping greedy algorithm as a modification developed in
WuChenGhattas23a
to optimize the experimental design for the optimal sensor placement problem, inspired by
lourencco2003iterated
,
zhang2002feature
. Recall that we need to choose
r_{s}
sensors out of
d_{s}
candidate sensor locations. We first apply a standard greedy algorithm to place the sensor one by one from
1
to
r_{s}
sensors according to one of the optimality criteria, and then employ a swapping greedy algorithm to swap the selected
r_{s}
sensors one by one with the rest of the sensors to improve the optimality criterion until a stopping condition is satisfied, see Algorithm
1
. The optimality can be the trace/determinant of the posterior covariance matrix and EIG. More specifically, we initialize an empty sensor set in line
3
and then select and add the sensors
s_{t}^{*}
,
t=1,\dots,r_{s}
, one by one by a greedy algorithm in line
4
to
10

3.3
, or by neural network acceleration as in Section
4.3

12
to
22

.

We remark that a leverage score in

r_{s}
sensors before starting the swapping loop since the optimality criteria can only be evaluated in an online optimization stage for a fixed number (
r_{s}
) of sensors in its algorithm. In contrast, we can efficiently assess the optimality criteria for an arbitrary number of sensors, which allows us to initialize the sensor set by a greedy algorithm before swapping. We observe fewer swapping loops to converge in our example (1 to 2 loops are sufficient).

4.5

The total computation for the swapping greedy algorithm requires

r_{s}

candidates with

O(r_{s}d_{s})
evaluations of the optimality criterion. Each of the optimality criteria takes
N_{s}
times of solving the optimization problem (
45
) to compute the MAP point and
N_{s}
times of solving the eigenvalue problem (
47
) to compute the eigenpairs, where
N_{s}
is the number of samples in the SAA of the expectation in the optimality criteria.

If we only use the high-fidelity approximation without neural network acceleration, by the same swapping greedy algorithm, we would have to solve
O(N_{s}k_{s}r_{s}d_{s})
times the optimization problem (
21
) to compute the high-fidelity MAP point and solve
O(N_{s}k_{s}r_{s}d_{s})
times the high-fidelity generalized eigenvalue problem (
24
) to compute the eigenpairs. By using an inexact Newton-CG algorithm to solve the optimization problem (
21
), we need to solve
N_{\text{nt}}
state PDEs and
N_{\text{nt}}N_{\text{cg}}
linearized PDEs (for Hessian-vector product), with
N_{\text{nt}}
Newton iterations and
N_{\text{cg}}
(in average) CG iterations per Newton iteration. For the solution of the generalized eigenvalue problem (
24
) by a randomized SVD algorithm, we need to solve one state PDE and
O(r)
linearized PDEs to compute
r
eigenpairs. We remark that if a direct solver is used, e.g., by first LU factorization and then its repeated use to solve the linearized PDEs, then the cost
C_{2}
of each linearized PDE solve becomes much smaller than the cost
C_{1}
to solve the state PDE. As a result, the additional computational cost for the Jacobian data generation is comparable or smaller (depending on the nonlinearity of the state PDEs) than that for the PtO map data generation, as reported in our examples. 1
.

O(N_{\boldsymbol{m}})

O(N_{\boldsymbol{m}})
samples for SAA in (
33

O(N_{\boldsymbol{m}}r_{\boldsymbol{m}})

r_{\boldsymbol{m}}
bases for input dimension reduction, and
O(N_{F})
state and
O(N_{F}r_{F})

r_{F}

N_{t}

5
Error analysis

This section provides detailed error analysis for the approximations of the MAP point, the eigenvalues of the approximate posterior covariance, and the optimality criteria under assumptions of the accuracy of the neural surrogate and the dimension reduction, and the smoothness of the objective function in computing the MAP point.
We validate these assumptions in the numerical results presented in Section
6
.

Approximation error of the MAP point

Assumption 1
.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}^{*}
be the MAP points of (
49
) and (
50
), respectively.

A1

We assume that the gradients
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}L
and
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\eta}}\hat{L}
allow convergent Taylor expansion around
{\boldsymbol{\eta}}^{*}
and
\hat{{\boldsymbol{\eta}}}^{*}
,

for

.

We remark that Assumption A1 is mild and satisfied under the stronger assumption that

6
Numerical result

This section demonstrates our proposed computational framework’s accuracy, scalability, and efficiency for two Bayesian OED problems. To evaluate the accuracy of neural network surrogates, we consider the approximation of the PtO map, its Jacobian, the MAP point, the eigenvalues, and three different optimality criteria for Bayesian OED. We also verify that the assumptions outlined in Assumption
1
align with our numerical results, validating the theoretical foundation of our approach. For scalability, we illustrate how accuracy is preserved with increasing parameter dimensions while quantifying the computational savings in terms of required PDE solves. To demonstrate efficiency, we compare the computational cost of the neural network surrogates with that of the high-fidelity approximation. This comparison includes both the cost of offline training and the surrogate’s online evaluation in selecting optimal sensors, providing a comprehensive assessment of the framework’s computational advantages.

6.1
Test problems

We consider two test problems, one of a linear diffusion problem and the other of a nonlinear convection-diffusion-reaction problem with a nonlinear reaction term, both with infinite-dimensional parameter fields and leading to nonlinear Bayesian inverse problems. We present the two examples in this subsection and the numerical results side by side in the following subsections for easy comparison.

7
Conclusion

In this work, we develop an accurate, scalable, and efficient computational framework based on DINO to solve Bayesian OED problems constrained by PDEs with infinite-dimensional parameter input. We consider the three optimality criteria of A-/D-optimality and EIG, which all require the computation of the Jacobian of the PtO map with Laplace and low-rank approximations of the posterior. We propose to use derivative-informed dimension reduction of the parameter and DINO surrogate of the PtO map to achieve high accuracy and scalability of the approximations for both the PtO map and especially its Jacobian. We derive efficient formulations for computing the MAP point and eigenvalue decomposition in the reduced dimension, which leads to efficient evaluation of the optimality criteria. We provide detailed error analysis for the approximations of the MAP point, the eigenvalues, and the optimality criteria under suitable assumptions of the PtO map and its Jacobian approximations and dimension reduction. To solve the optimization problem of the Bayesian OED, we present a modified swapping greedy algorithm initialized by a greedy algorithm, which achieves fast convergence.

With two numerical examples of linear diffusion and nonlinear CDR problems in both two- and three-dimensional physical domains, we demonstrate the accuracy, scalability, and efficiency of the proposed method and verify the assumptions and theoretical results. Specifically, we demonstrate that DINO provides more accurate approximations of the PtO map, the reduced Jacobian, and the MAP point than other neural networks trained without Jacobian. This results in significantly higher approximation accuracy for all optimality criteria in Bayesian OED. We demonstrate that the accuracy of DINO approximations with proper dimension reduction is scalable with respect to increasing parameter dimensions for the same number of training samples or the same number of PDE solves.
We also demonstrated that the proposed method achieved high efficiency with a speedup of 80
\times
in 2D and 1148
\times
in 3D, including both the offline data generation and online evaluation time, compared to the high-fidelity approximations for the CDR problems.

This work uses Laplace and low-rank approximations of the posterior distribution for Bayesian OED. It is limited to the condition that the Laplace approximation is a good approximation of the posterior, and the decay of the eigenvalues of the Hessian is fast. In future work, we plan to address these limitations by developing the DINO surrogates in the context of variational approximation of the posterior distribution that can be highly non-Gaussian, and by using nonlinear dimension reduction and hierarchical approximation of the Hessian. We also plan to extend the computational framework to solve sequential Bayesian OED problems with efficient construction of time-dependent DINO surrogates.
