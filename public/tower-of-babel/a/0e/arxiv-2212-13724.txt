1
Introduction

In this paper, we handle a simple, finite, and undirected graph.
To check how well a graph
G
is connected, we normally compute the
connectivity
of
G
, written
\kappa(G)
, which is the minimum number of vertices
S

such that
G-S
is disconnected or trivial.
However, since this value is based on a worst-case situation,
Beineke, Oellermann, and Pippert
[
1
]
introduced a parameter to reflect a global amount of connectivity. The
average connectivity
of
G
is
defined to be the number
\overline{\kappa}(G)=\sum_{u,v\in V(G)}\frac{\kappa(u,v)}{{n\choose 2}}
, where
\kappa(u,v)
is the minimum number of vertices whose deletion makes
v
unreachable from
u
. By Menger’s Theorem,
\kappa(u,v)
equals the maximum number of internally disjoint paths between
u
and
v
.
For convenience, we may assume that
\kappa(v,v)=0
for every vertex
v\in V(G)
.

The
average connectivity matrix
of a graph
G
with the vertex set
V(G)=\{v_{1},\ldots,v_{n}\}
, written
A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)
, is an
n\times n
matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry is
\kappa(v_{i},v_{j})/{n\choose 2}
. For a vertex
v\in V(G)
, let the
transmission of
v
, written
T(v)
, be
\frac{1}{{n\choose 2}}\sum_{w\in V(G)}\kappa(v,w)
and let the
transmission of
G
, written
T(G)
, be
\max_{v\in V(G)}T(v)
. Note that for an
n
-vertex tree
T_{n}
, since there is a unique path between any
v_{i}
and
v_{j}
, we have

{n\choose 2}A_{\overline{\kappa}}(T_{n})=A(K_{n})=J_{n}-I_{n}
,
where
A(G)
is the adjacency matrix of a graph
G
,
J_{n}
is the
n\times n
all-1 matrix, and
I_{n}
is the
n\times n
identity matrix.
Note that
2\overline{\kappa}(G)=\sum_{v\in V(G)}T(v)
.

Observation 1.1
.

If
G
is a connected graph with
n
vertices, then for
v\in V(G)
, we have
\frac{2\kappa(G)}{n}\leq T(v)\leq\frac{2d(v)}{n}.

Proof.

For any pair of distinct vertices
u
and
v
, we have
\kappa(G)\leq\kappa(v,u)\leq d(v)
, which implies
that
(n-1)\kappa(G)\leq{n\choose 2}T(v)\leq(n-1)d(v)
.
∎

Equalities in the bounds in Observation
1.1
hold for complete graphs. For an
n
-vertex tree, equality in the lower bound holds, but for the center vertex
v
in an
n
-vertex star, there is a big gap between
T(v)
and
2d(v)/n
.

A connected graph
G
is
uniformly
r
-connected
if for any pair of non-adjacent vertices
u
and
v
, we have
\kappa(u,v)=r
. Note that if
G
is uniformly
r
-connected, then the average connectivity matrix has constant row sum equal to
2r/n
.

Since
A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)
is real and symmetric, all of its eigenvalues are real. Thus we can index the eigenvalues, say
\lambda_{1}(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)),\ldots,\lambda_{n}(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))
, in non-increasing order. For simplicity, we let
\lambda_{i}(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))=\lambda_{i}
, and
let
\rho(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))=\max\{|\lambda_{i}|:1\leq i\leq n\}
. If we let
\rho(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))=\rho
, then by Theorem
2.2
, we have
\lambda_{1}=\rho
.
We prove some relationships between the spectral radius and some quantities of the graph.

Theorem 1.2
.

For an
n
-vertex connected graph
G
, we have
\frac{2\overline{\kappa}(G)}{n}\leq\rho\leq T(G)
.
Equalities hold only when
G
is uniformly connected.

Proof.

By Theorem
2.2
, there exists a unique positive eigenvector
\mathbb{x}
corresponding to
\rho
. Let
x_{i}=\max_{j=1,\ldots,n}x_{j}
. Then we have

\rho x_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))_{ij}x_{j}\leq x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{n}(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))_{ij}=T(v_{i})x_{i}\leq T(G)x_{i},

which implies that
\rho\leq T(G)
. Equality holds only when
G
is a uniformly connected graph.

For the lower bound, by Theorem
2.1
, we have

\frac{2\overline{\kappa}(G)}{n}=\frac{{\bf 1}^{T}A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G){\bf 1}}{{\bf 1}^{T}{\bf 1}}\leq\rho.

Equality holds only when
\mathbb{x}=c{\bf 1}
for some constant
c
, i.e.
G
is uniformly connected.
∎

A
matching
in a graph
G
is a set of disjoint edges in it.
The
matching number
of a graph
G
, written
\alpha^{\prime}(G)
,
is the maximum size of a matching in it.
Kim and O
[
6
]
gave a relation between the matching number and the average connectivity of
G
.

Theorem 1.3
.

[
6
]

For a connected graph
G
, we have

\overline{\kappa}(G)\leq 2\alpha^{\prime}(G).

In Section 3, we prove an upper bound for the spectral radius of
A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)
in terms of the matching number and the number of vertices.

Theorem 1.4
.

For an
n
-vertex connected graph
G
, we have

\rho(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))\leq\frac{4\alpha^{\prime}(G)}{n}.

Equality holds only when
G=K_{n}
and
n
is odd.

If Theorem
1.4
is true, then by Theorem
1.2
, we have
\frac{2\overline{\kappa}(G)}{n}\leq\rho\leq\frac{4\alpha^{\prime}(G)}{n}
, which implies Theorem
1.3
. To prove Theorem
1.4
, we find the maximum spectral radius among
n
-vertex connected graphs with a given matching number. However, this idea does not derive the relation between
\overline{\kappa}(G)
and matching number
\alpha^{\prime}(G)
in connected bipartite graphs by Kim and O (see theorem 2.3 in
[
6
]
). Nevertheless, we also prove an upper bound for the spectral radius of
A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)
in
n
-vertex connected bipartite graphs to guarantee a certain matching number in Section 4.

Theorem 1.5
.

For an
n
-vertex connected bipartite graph
G
, we have

\rho(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G))\leq\frac{(n-\alpha^{\prime}(G))(4\alpha^{\prime}(G)-2)}{n(n-1)}.

Equality holds only when
G=K_{n/2,n/2}
.

For undefined terms of graph theory, see West
[
11
]
. For basic properties of spectral graph theory, see Brouwer and Haemers
[
2
]
or Godsil and Royle
[

]
.

2
Tools

In this section, we provide the main tools to prove Theorem
1.4
and Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 2.1
.

(Rayleigh-Ritz Theorem; see
[
5
, Theorem 4.2.2]
)

If
A
is an
n\times n
Hermitian matrix, then

\rho(A)=\max_{x\neq{\bf 0}}\frac{x^{*}Ax}{x^{*}x}.

Theorem
2.1
is used to prove Theorem
1.2
.

The Perron-Frobenius Theorem is a very important theorem, implying that
\rho=\lambda_{1}
and also the Perron vector is positive.

Theorem 2.2
.

(Perron-Frobenius Theorem; see
[
5
, Theorem 8.4.4]
)

Let
A
be an
n\times n
matrix and suppose that
A
is irreducible and non-negative. Then

(a)
\rho(A)>0;

(b)
\rho(A)
is an eigenvalue of
A
;

(c) There is a positive vector
x
such that
Ax=\rho(A)x
; and

(d)
\rho(A)
is an algebraically (and hence geometrically) simple eigenvalue of
A
.

To compare the spectral radii of two average connectivity matrices, we often need Theorem
2.3
.

Theorem 2.3
.

See
[
5
, Theorem 8.4.5]
)
Let
A
be an
n\times n
non-negative and irreducible matrix. If
A\geq|B|
, then we have
\rho(A)\geq\rho(B)
.

If we encounter a problem when we compare the spectral radii of two matrices, then we normally compare the spectral radii of matrices (called quotient matrices) derived from vertex partitions.

Let
A
be a real symmetric matrix of order
n
, whose rows and columns are indexed by
P=\{1,\ldots,n\}
, and let
\{P_{1},\ldots,P_{q}\}
be a partition of
P
.
If for each
i\in\{1,\ldots,q\}
,
n_{i}=|P_{i}|
, then
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{q}
. Let
A_{i,j}
be the submatrix of
A
formed by rows in
P_{i}
and columns in
P_{j}
.
The
quotient matrix
of
A
with respect to the partition
P
is the
q\times q
matrix
Q=(q_{i,j})
, where
q_{i,j}
is the average row sum of
A_{i,j}
.
If the row sum of each matrix
A_{i,j}
is constant, then we call the partition
P

equitable
.

Theorem 2.4
.

[
12
]

If a partition
P
corresponding to the quotient matrix
Q
of
A
is equitable, then we have
\rho(Q)=\rho(A)
.

Theorem 2.5
.

[
5
, Theorem 6.1.1]
(Gershgorin circle theorem)
Let
A
be an
n
by
n
, and let

R^{\prime}_{i}(A)\equiv\sum_{j=1,j\neq i}^{n}|a_{ij}|,\quad 1\leq i\leq n

denote the deleted absolute row sums of
A
. Then all the eigenvalues of
A
are located in the union of
n
discs

\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}\{z\in\mathbb{C}:|z-a_{ii}|\leq R^{\prime}_{i}(A)\}.

Since the diagonal entries of
A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)
are all zeros, the Gershgorin circle theorem says that
\rho\left(A_{\overline{\kappa}}(G)\right)
is bounded by the maximum row sum. Theorem
2.5
is used to prove Theorem
1.5
.

Theorem 2.6
.

[
10
]
(Berge-Tutte formula)

For an
n
-vertex graph
G
, we have

\alpha^{\prime}(G)=\min\limits_{S\subseteq V(G)}\frac{1}{2}\left(n-o(G-S)+|S|\right),

where
o(H)
is the number of components with odd number of vertices in a graph
H
.
