On the diameter and zero forcing number of some graph classes in the Johnson, Grassmann and Hamming association scheme
Aida Abiad Robin Simoens Sjanne Zeijlemaker

Abstract
We determine the diameter of generalized Grassmann graphs and the zero forcing number of some generalized Johnson graphs, generalized Grassmann graphs and the Hamming graphs. Our work extends several previously known results.



1
Introduction

Graph classes in the Johnson, Grassmann and Hamming association scheme have received a considerable amount of attention over the last decades, and several graph properties and parameters have been investigated for these families. Examples are the work of Alspach, who showed that the Johnson graphs are Hamilton connected
[
6
]
, the results of Bailey and Meagher, who studied the metric dimension of Grassmann graphs
[
25
]
, and the coloring results on generalized Kneser graphs by Balogh, Cherkashin and Kiselev
[
7
]
. However, many parameters of these graphs are still unknown. In this paper, we focus on studying their diameter and zero forcing number.

Zero forcing is a propagation process on a graph where the vertices are initially partitioned into two sets of black and white vertices. A white vertex is colored black (
forced
) if it is the unique white neighbor of a black vertex. The minimum number of initial black vertices needed to force all vertices of a graph
G
is called the
zero forcing number
. Zero forcing was formally introduced in the AIM workshop
[
4
]
as an upper bound for the minimum rank, a connection which was also established by Alon
[
5
]
. The study of this parameter is motivated by the investigation of quantum networks,
influence in social networks and power dominating sets, see for example
[
15
]
and
[
19
]
.

In this paper, we calculate the girth and diameter of generalized Grassmann graphs, extending the known results for Kneser graphs
[
27
]
and generalized Johnson graphs
[
3
]
by Valencia-Pabon and Vera, and Agong, Amarra, Caughman, Herman and Terada respectively. Recently, these results have also been complemented by Caughman, Herman and Terada
[
16
]
, who studied the odd girth of generalized Johnson graphs. Moreover, we determine the zero forcing number of some generalized Johnson and generalized Grassmann graphs. As a corollary of our work, we obtain the known zero forcing results for Johnson graphs on
2
-sets by Fallat, Meagher, Soltani and Yang.
[
21
]
and for Kneser graphs by Bres̆ar, Kos, and Torres
[
11
]
. Aazami posed a conjecture on the zero forcing of hypercubes
[
1
, Conjecture 2.4.4]
which was proved by Alon
[
5
]
and, independently, by several authors in a AIM workshop
[
4
]
. Here we determine the zero forcing number of Hamming graphs, extending the mentioned known results.

2
Preliminaries

Let
G=(V,E)
be a graph with vertex set
V
and edge set
E
. Throughout this paper, we consider simple graphs, i.e. undirected, loopless graphs without multiple edges. Adjacency of vertices
v
and
w
will be denoted by
v\sim w
and the open and closed
neighborhood of a vertex
v
by
G(v)
and
G[v]
respectively. The
induced subgraph

G[S]
on a subset
S\subseteq V
is the graph with vertices
S
and edges
\{e\in E\mid e\subseteq S\}
. The
Cartesian product
of two graphs
G
and
H
, denoted by
G\square H
, is the graph on
V(G)\times V(H)
such that
(u,v)
is adjacent to
(u^{\prime},v^{\prime})
if and only if
u=v
and
u^{\prime}
is adjacent to
v^{\prime}
in
H
, or
u^{\prime}=v^{\prime}
and
u
is adjacent to
v
in
G
. If graphs
G
and
H
are isomorphic, we denote this by
G\cong H
.

The
distance

\mathrm{d}(v,w)
between two vertices
v
and
w
is the length of a shortest walk from
v
to
w
. The
diameter

\mathrm{diam}{(G)}
is the largest possible distance between two vertices of
G
. The
girth

\mathop{\rm g}\nolimits(G)
is the length of a shortest cycle.

Let
n
,
k
be positive integers with
n\geq k
and let
S\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
. The
generalized Johnson graph

J_{S}(n,k)
has as vertices the
k
-subsets of
\{1,2,\dots,n\}=:[n]
. Two vertices are adjacent if their intersection size is in
S
. Well-known subfamilies include the
Kneser graph

K(n,k)=J_{\{0\}}(n,k)
and the
Johnson graph

J(n,k)=J_{\{k-1\}}(n,k)
. Note that if
n\leq 2k
, the Kneser graph is either empty or a disjoint set of edges, hence we assume that
n\geq 2k+1
for these graphs.

The generalized Johnson graphs have the following
q
-analogue. Let
\mathbb{F}_{q}
be the finite field of order
q
, where
q
is a prime power. A subspace of
\mathbb{F}_{q}
of dimension
k
is called a
k
-subspace
for short. If we let
S\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
, the
generalized Grassmann graph
J_{q,S}(n,k)
has as vertices the
k
-subspaces of
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
and vertices are adjacent if the dimension of their intersection is in
S
. Particular examples include the
q-Kneser graph

K_{q}(n,k)=J_{q,\{0\}}(n,k)
and the
Grassmann graph

J_{q}(n,k)=J_{q,\{k-1\}}(n,k)
. The number of vertices of
J_{q,S}(n,k)
is the Gaussian binomial coefficient

\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}\coloneqq\frac{(1-q^{n})(1-q^{n-1})\dots(1-q^{n-r+1})}{(1-q)(1-q^{2})\dots(1-q^{k})}.

Note that we may assume
n\geq 2k
since
J_{S}(n,k)\cong J_{\{s+n-2k\mid s\in S\}}(n,n-k)
, and similarly,
J_{q,S}(n,k)\cong J_{q,\{s+n-2k\mid s\in S\}}(n,n-k)
.

In Johnson graphs, vertices are associated with sets and adjacency is determined by the cardinality of their intersection. If we replace sets by ordered tuples, we obtain the well-known Hamming graphs
H(n,q)
, where
n
denotes the tuple length and the entries range from
0
to
q-1
. Tuples are connected by an edge whenever they coincide in
n-1
coordinates, i.e., when their Hamming distance is
1
. Alternatively, one may view
H(n,q)
as the Cartesian product of
n
copies of
K_{q}
.

Zero forcing
is a graph coloring problem in which an initial subset of vertices is
colored black, while the others are colored white. A black vertex
v
colors a white neighbor
w
black if
G(v)\setminus w
is entirely black, i.e. if
w
is the unique white neighbor of
v
. A black vertex which initiates a color change is called a
pivot
and we refer to the initial set of black vertices as the
leader set
. If all white vertices can be colored black under the given coloring rule, we call the leader set a
zero forcing set
. The
zero forcing number
of a graph
G=(V,E)
, denoted by
Z(G)
, is the cardinality of a smallest subset of
V
which is a zero forcing set.

By modifying the color changing rule or adding restrictions to the zero forcing set, several interesting variations on zero forcing can be obtained. Below, we list the ones that are relevant for the results in this article. A more complete overview of these parameters and their mutual relations can be found in
[
8
,
24
]
.

The
connected zero forcing number

Z_{c}(G)
is the cardinality of the smallest zero forcing set
S
such that
G[S]
is connected
[
14
]
. The
total zero forcing number

Z_{t}(G)
is the cardinality of the smallest zero forcing set
S
such that
G[S]
has no isolated vertices
[
18
]
. Both
Z_{c}(G)
and
Z_{t}(G)
upper bound the zero forcing number. A single vertex can only be a zero forcing set if
G
is a path, hence if
G\neq P_{|V|}
, we have
2\leq Z(G)\leq Z_{t}(G)\leq Z_{c}(G)
.

For a graph
G
, let
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathbb{F}}(G)
be the set of symmetric matrices
M
over the field
\mathbb{F}
with
m_{ij}=0
if and only if vertices
i\neq j
are not adjacent and arbitrary entries on the diagonal. The
maximum nullity
of
G
over
\mathbb{F}
, denoted by
M^{\mathbb{F}}
, is the largest multiplicity of eigenvalue zero for any matrix in
{\mathcal{S}}^{\mathbb{F}}(G)
. It was shown in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
that the maximum nullity of a graph over any field lower bounds the zero forcing number.

Lemma 1

(
[
4
]
, Proposition 2.4 and
[
5
]
, Theorem 2.1)
.

For any graph
G
and field
\mathbb{F}
,
M^{\mathbb{F}}(G)\leq Z(G)
.

Besides maximum nullity, zero forcing is closely related to other graph parameters, such as metric dimension
[
20
]
, power domination
[
19
]
and Grundy domination
[
12
]
. A vertex
v

dominates

w
if
w\in G[v]
. A sequence
(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\ell})
of vertices in
V
is called a
dominating sequence
for
G
if each
w\in V
is dominated by some
v_{i}
. If additionally for each
i
it holds that

G[v_{i}]\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}G[v_{j}]\neq\emptyset,

we call
(v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{\ell})
a
Grundy dominating sequence
and if for each
i
we have

G(v_{i})\setminus\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}G[v_{j}]\neq\emptyset,

the sequence is called
Z-Grundy dominating
. The vertex
v_{i}
is said to
footprint
the vertices in
G[v_{i}]\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{i-1}G[v_{j}]
or
G(v_{i})\setminus\cup_{j=1}^{i-1}G[v_{j}]
, respectively. In other words, a vertex
w
is footprinted by
v_{i}
if
i
is the smallest integer such that
v_{i}
dominates
w
. Note that every vertex has a unique footprinter. The maximum length of a Grundy or Z-Grundy dominating sequence, denoted by
\gamma_{gr}(G)
or
\gamma_{gr}^{Z}(G)
respectively, is referred to as the
(Z-)Grundy domination number
of
G
.

Grundy domination is known to give a lower bound on the zero forcing number, a fact that we will use in some of our proofs.

Lemma 2

(
[
12
, Corollary 2.3]
)
.

Let
G
be a graph without isolated vertices, then

•

Z(G)\geq|V|-\gamma_{gr}(G)
;

•

Z(G)=|V|-\gamma^{Z}_{gr}(G)
.

For an overview of Grundy domination variants and their relation to zero forcing, we refer to
[
12
]
.

Diameter and girth of generalized Grassmann graphs

We extend the results of
[
3
]
on the girth and diameter of Johnson graphs to generalized Grassmann graphs. Our main tool will be the following lemma.

Lemma 3

(

Let

q^{n-k-m+1}

k

\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}

m
-space that intersects them trivially.

Proof.

Let
a\leq q^{n-k-m+1}
be the number of given
k
-spaces in
\mathbb{F}_{q}^{n}
. We find a suitable
m
-space by constructing a basis
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{m}\}
for it. For the first basis vector,
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}
, there are at least

(q^{n}-1)-a(q^{k}-1)

choices: the total number of nonzero vectors, minus those that lie in one (and possibly more) of the
a
given
k
-spaces.
For
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2}
, there are at least

(q^{n}-1)-(q-1)-a(q^{k+1}-q)

choices: the total number of nonzero vectors, minus those that span the same
1
-space as
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}
, minus those that lie in the span of
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}
and one of the given
k
-spaces, but are no multiple of
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}
(because we eliminated those already).
Continuing in this way, we find a decreasing number of available vectors, ending with at least

(q^{n}-1)-(q^{m-1}-1)-a(q^{k+m-1}-q^{m-1})

choices for
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{m}
. Since
a\leq q^{n-k-m+1}
, this number is at least
q^{n}-q^{m-1}-q^{n}+q^{n-k}=q^{n-k}-q^{m-1}
, which is strictly positive since
k+m\leq n
. We conclude that such a space exists.

Allowing
n
to be smaller than
2k
leads to the following, more general statement. Note that the condition

Corollary 5
.

Let
S
be a proper subset of
\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
with
s\coloneqq\min(S)
such that

. The generalized Grassmann graph
J_{q,S}(n,k)
has diameter

\mathrm{diam}\left(J_{q,S}(n,k)\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}2&\text{if }s\in\{0,2k-n\}\\
\left\lceil\frac{\min(k,n-k)}{k-s}\right\rceil&\text{if }s\notin\{0,2k-n\}.\end{array}\right.

We distinguish two cases.

Case 1:

We are done by Theorem

Case 2:

.

By definition,

. Applying the first case on the latter graph (since

and

), results in the diameter being

We end this section with a short proof of the girth of generalized Grassmann graphs.

Proposition 6
.

Every generalized Grassmann graph
J_{q,S}(n,k)
with
S\neq\emptyset
has girth
3
.

Proof.

Let
J_{q,S}(n,k)
be a nontrivial Grassmann graph and let
s\in S
. Recall that we may assume that
n\geq 2k
without loss of generality. Choose two
k
-spaces
v
and
w
that intersect in an
s
-space
\pi
. Since
2\leq q\leq q^{n-2k+1}\leq q^{(n-s)-2(k-s)+1}
, we can apply Lemma
3
to the residual projective space of
\pi
to find a third
k
-space
u
that intersects
v
and
w
in
\pi
. Then
u
,
v
and
w
are mutually adjacent, i.e. there is a triangle in the graph. We conclude that the girth must be
3
.
∎

4
Zero forcing number

Determining the zero forcing number is known to be an NP-complete problem. The proof of this result is often wrongly attributed to Aazami
[
1
,
2
]
(see, e.g.
[
13
,
14
,
24
,
26
]
), who showed the NP-completeness for weighted zero forcing. However, this is incorrect, since the unweighted version does not follow from the weighted
0,1
version that Aazami established in
[
1
, Theorem 2.3.1]
. The NP-completeness of the unweighted variant is proved in
[
28
]
, where it is shown that fast-mixed searching is NP-complete, and it is known that this problem is equivalent to zero forcing
[
22
]
.
Despite its NP-completeness, the exact zero forcing number is known for certain graph classes
[
4
,
10
,
14
]
. In this section, we determine the zero forcing number of several families of generalized Johnson graphs, generalized Grassmann graphs and Hamming graphs.

4.1
Generalized Johnson graphs

First, we investigate the zero forcing number of generalized Johnson graphs, generalizing known results on Johnson and Kneser graphs from
[
21
]
and
[
11
]
. A natural way to generalize the Johnson graphs, is to extend their connection set
\{k-1\}
to
S=\{s,s+1,\dots,k-1\}
. We determine the zero forcing number of these graphs by studying the more general case where
\min(S)=s
. Note that if
n<2k-s
, every two
k
-sets intersect in strictly more than
s
elements, so not all options in
S
can actually occur. We therefore assume that
n\geq 2k-s
. To prove a lower bound on the zero forcing number, we will make use of the following variant of a theorem of Bollobás.

Lemma 7

(
[
23
, Theorem 1 + Remark 3.2]
)
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\dots,X_{m}
be
r
-element sets, let
Y_{1},Y_{2},\dots,Y_{m}
be
s
-element sets and let
t\geq 0
such that

(i)

|X_{i}\cap Y_{i}|\leq t
for
i\in[m]
;

(ii)

|X_{i}\cap Y_{j}|\geq t+1
for
1\leq i<j\leq m
.

Then
m\leq\binom{r+s-2t}{r-t}
.

Theorem 8
.

Let
S\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
with
s\coloneqq\min(S)
and let
n\geq 2k-s
. Then

Z(J_{S}(n,k))\leq Z_{t}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq Z_{c}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-2(k-s)}{s}.

If
S=\{s,s+1,\dots,k-1\}
, equality holds throughout.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq\{v\in V\mid[k-s]\subset v,\ k-s+1,k-s+2,\dots,2(k-s)\notin v\}
and
{\mathcal{B}}\coloneqq V\setminus{\mathcal{W}}
. Note that
{\mathcal{B}}
is well-defined, since
n\geq 2(k-s)
. This set has size
\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-2(k-s)}{s}
and we will show that it is a zero forcing set.

Consider a white vertex
v\coloneqq\{1,2,\dots,k-s,v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{s}\}\in{\mathcal{W}}
, with
v_{i}\notin\{k-s+1,k-s+2,\dots,2(k-s)\}
. It is adjacent to
w\coloneqq\{k-s+1,k-s+2,\dots,2(k-s),v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{s}\}\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Every other white vertex has at most
s-1
elements in common with
w
, because their intersection must be a subset of
\{v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{s}\}
. Therefore,
v
is the unique white neighbor of
w
and is forced black. This holds for any
v\in{\mathcal{W}}
, so
{\mathcal{B}}
is a zero forcing set.

It is also a connected zero forcing set, since we can construct a black path between any two black vertices as follows.
Let
v,v^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}
. For any choice of
S
, vertices are adjacent if they differ in
k-s
elements. Using this property, we can replace one element
a\in v
at the time with a new element
b
to eventually arrive at
v^{\prime}
: choose a
(2k-s)
-set
X
containing
v\cup\{a,b\}
and a subset
Y\subset v
such that
|Y|=k-s
,
a\in Y
. Then there exists a path
v\rightarrow X\setminus Y\rightarrow(v\setminus a)\cup b
, where at each step, we replace exactly
k-s
elements. Moreover, by choosing
Y
appropriately we can ensure that all path vertices are black. Therefore, the leader set is connected.

Now assume that
S=\{s,s+1,\dots,k-1\}
. To prove that the upper bound is tight, we use the Grundy domination number of
J_{S}(n,k)
. Consider a Grundy dominating sequence
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{m}
of maximum length and pair each
v_{i}
with a vertex
w_{i}
that is footprinted by it. Define the sets
X_{i}=v_{i}
and
Y_{i}=[n]\setminus w_{i}
. Then
|X_{i}\cap Y_{i}|\leq k-s
. Moreover, if
j>i
, we know that
|v_{i}\cap w_{j}|\leq s-1
, otherwise
w_{j}
is either
v_{i}
or one of its neighbors, so it would have been dominated by
v_{i}
before
v_{j}
. Hence
|X_{i}\cap Y_{j}|\geq k-s+1
for
i<j
. Lemma
7
implies that
\gamma_{gr}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq\binom{n-2(k-s)}{s}
, hence

Z(J_{S}(n,k))\geq\binom{n}{k}-\gamma_{gr}(J_{S}(n,k))\geq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-2(k-s)}{s},

where the first inequality follows from Lemma
2
.
∎

If
s=k-1
, we obtain the zero forcing number of the classical Johnson graphs.

Corollary 9
.

For
n\geq 2k
,

Z(J(n,k))=Z_{t}(J(n,k))=Z_{c}(J(n,k))=\binom{n}{k}-\binom{n-2}{k-1}.

The Kneser graphs can be generalized in a similar way by extending the intersection set to
\{0,1,\dots,t\}
for some integer
t\leq k-1
. This generalization also appears in
[
7
]
, where the coloring number of generalized Kneser graphs is studied, and
[
17
]
, which shows that some of these graphs are not determined by their spectrum. As usual for Kneser graphs, we require
n\geq 2k+1
.

Theorem 10
.

Let
n\geq 2k+1
and
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
for some
t\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
. Then

Z(J_{S}(n,k))\geq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.

Proof.

Consider a Z-Grundy dominating sequence
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{t}
of maximum length and pair each
v_{i}
with a vertex
w_{i}
that is footprinted by it. Then
|v_{i}\cap w_{i}|\leq t
and if
j>i
,
|v_{i}\cap w_{j}|\geq t+1
. Lemma
7
implies that
\gamma^{Z}_{gr}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}
, hence

Z(J_{S}(n,k))\geq\binom{n}{k}-\gamma^{Z}_{gr}(J_{S}(n,k))\geq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.\qed

Note that in the proof of Theorem
10
we need a Z-Grundy dominating sequence specifically. In a normal Grundy dominating sequence, a vertex could footprint itself, in which case the intersection
|v_{i}\cap w_{i}|
would equal
k>t
.

Theorem 11
.

Let
S\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,k-2\}
and
n\geq\max(3k-2t+1,2k+1)
, where
t\coloneqq\max(S)
. Then

Z(J_{S}(n,k))\leq Z_{t}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq Z_{c}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.

If
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
, equality holds throughout.

Proof.

Define
T\coloneqq\{n-2k+2t+2,n-2k+2t+3,\dots,n\}
and
{\mathcal{W}}\coloneqq{\mathcal{W}}_{1}\cup{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
, where

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{1}

\displaystyle=\{v\in V\mid|v\cap T|=k-t,\ [t]\subset v\},

\displaystyle{\mathcal{W}}_{2}

\displaystyle=\{v\in V\mid v\subset[2k-t],\ [t+1]\subset v\},

and let
{\mathcal{B}}\coloneqq V\setminus{\mathcal{W}}
.
Since
n\geq 2k-t
, we know that
t+1\notin T
. Then
{\mathcal{W}}
contains

\binom{2k-2t-1}{k-t-1}+\binom{2k-2t-1}{k-t}=\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}

distinct vertices,
so
{\mathcal{B}}
has the desired cardinality. We will show that it is a connected zero forcing set by first forcing the vertices in
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
and then those in
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
.

Let
v\in{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
and let
w
be the vertex
(T\setminus v)\cup[t+1]
. Note that
w\sim v
, since their intersection is
[t]
. For any other
v^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
, we have
|w\cap v^{\prime}|=|[t]\cup((T\setminus v)\cap v^{\prime})|\geq t+1
, so
w\not\sim v^{\prime}
. Moreover,
|w\cap v^{\prime\prime}|\geq|[t+1]|=t+1
for any
v^{\prime\prime}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
, so
w
has no white neighbors besides
v
. If
w
is black, it will therefore force
v
. This is the case if
(T\setminus[2k-t])\not\subset v
, because then we cannot have
w\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
. If this should hold for any choice of
v
, we need
|T\setminus[2k-t]|=n-2k+t>k-t
, which is satisfied by assumption, as
n>3k-2t
. Therefore,
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
can be forced entirely.

Next, consider a vertex
v\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
and let
w=([2k-t]\setminus v)\cup[t]\in{\mathcal{B}}
. Then
w\sim v
, but
w
is not adjacent to any other
v^{\prime}\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
, because
|w\cap v^{\prime}|=|[t]\cup(([2k-t]\setminus v)\cap v^{\prime})|\geq t+1
. This means that
v
is the unique white neighbor of
w
, so it will be colored black.

The above construction also holds for the connected zero forcing number. The proof is analogous to that of Theorem
8
. If
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
, it follows from Theorem
10
that the upper bound is tight for the (connected) zero forcing number of
J_{S}(n,k)
.
∎

The construction in Theorem
11
no longer works if
n\leq 3k-2t
, as some pivots of
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
will be contained in the white set
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
, which is forced last. However, for the extremal case
n=3k-2t
, we can construct a different zero forcing set of the same cardinality.

Theorem 12
.

Let
S\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,k-2\}
with
t\coloneqq\max(S)
, and let
n=3k-2t\geq 2k+1
. Then

Z(J_{S}(n,k))\leq Z_{t}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq Z_{c}(J_{S}(n,k))\leq\binom{n}{k}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.

If
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
, equality holds throughout.

Proof.

Consider the sets
T
,
{\mathcal{W}}
and
{\mathcal{B}}
from Theorem
11
. The zero forcing process used in the construction in Theorem
11
is no longer applicable when
n=3k-2t
because the white vertex
v\coloneqq[t+1]\cup(T\cap[2k-t])\in{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
now acts as a pivot for
[t]\cup(T\setminus[2k-t])\in{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
. However, we want to force
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
before
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
. Therefore, the zero forcing process will no longer color the entire graph. We propose the following change.

Let
x\in T\setminus[2k-t]
,
y\in T\cap[2k-t]
. Define
X=(T\cap[2k-t])\setminus\{y\}
. We add the previously white vertex
v=[t+1]\cup(T\cap[2k-t])
to the leader set
{\mathcal{B}}
, and instead color
v^{\prime}=[t]\cup\{t+2\}\cup X\cup\{x\}
white (note that
|v^{\prime}|=t+1+(k-t-2)+1=k
, so this is indeed a
k
-set). We will show that this gives a zero forcing set.

Using the same pivots as in the proof of Theorem
11
, all vertices of
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
can be colored black, except those containing
X\cup\{x\}
. We will force those later and focus on
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
first.

Consider the pivots from Theorem
11
corresponding to
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
. A vertex cannot be forced if its pivot is adjacent to
v^{\prime}
or a white vertex of
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
. It meets at least one of these conditions whenever it contains
X
. Let
v^{\prime\prime}
be such a vertex. Its pivot contains
y
, as
v^{\prime\prime}\neq v
. Replace
y
by
x
, then the resulting vertex has no other white neighbors in
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
and is not adjacent to
v^{\prime}
or any white vertex from
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
(both contain
x
). Moreover, it is black, so it can force
v^{\prime\prime}
. Hence all of
{\mathcal{W}}_{2}
can be colored black.

Now consider the vertex
[t]\cup(T\setminus(\{i\}\cup[2k-t]))\cup\{y\}
. This vertex from
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
has been forced already and is not adjacent to any white vertices in
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
, which must contain at least one element from
(T\setminus(\{i\}\cup[2k-t]))\cup\{y\}
. It can therefore force
v^{\prime}
. The remainder of
{\mathcal{W}}_{1}
can then be forced by their usual pivots.

Once again, tightness of this construction for
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
follows from Theorem
10
. The connectivity argument is analogous to the one in the proof of Theorem
8
.
∎

Suppose
{\mathcal{B}}
is a zero forcing set for
J_{\{0,1,\dots,t\}}(n,k)
and
{\mathcal{W}}=V\setminus{\mathcal{B}}
. Then for any
n^{\prime}>n
,
V\setminus{\mathcal{W}}
is also a zero forcing set for
J_{\{0,1,\dots,t\}}(n^{\prime},k)
. This means that if we can find a tight construction for the smallest case
n=2k+1
, this gives a tight bound for all admissible values of
n
. Such a zero forcing set can be found computationally when
(n,k,t)\in\{(9,4,1),(11,5,1),(13,6,1)\}
, but not for
(7,3,1)
(see
[
29
]
for source code). This suggests that it may be possible to extend Theorem
11
to all triples
(n,k,t)
such that
n\geq 2k+1
and
t\leq k-3
.

4.2
Generalized Grassmann graphs

Lemma
7
has the following analogue for subspaces over a field.

Lemma 13

(
[
23
]
)
.

Let
U_{1},U_{2},\dots,U_{m}
be
r
-dimensional subspaces and let
W_{1},W_{2},\dots,W_{m}
be
s
-dimensional subspaces of a linear space over a field
\mathbb{F}
. Let
t\geq 0
such that

(i)

\dim(U_{i}\cap W_{i})\leq t
for
i\in[m]
;

(ii)

\dim(U_{i}\cap W_{j})\geq t+1
for
1\leq i<j\leq m
.

Then
m\leq\binom{r+s-2t}{r-t}
.

Using the above lemma, we obtain a lower bound on
Z(J_{q,\{0,1,\dots,t\}}(n,k))
, similar to the generalized Johnson case.

Theorem 14
.

Let
n\geq 2k+1
and
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
for some
t\in\{0,1,\dots,k-1\}
. Then

Z(J_{q,S}(n,k))\geq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.

Proof.

Consider a Z-Grundy dominating sequence
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{s}
of maximum length in
J_{q,S}(n,k)
and pair each
v_{i}
with a vertex
w_{i}
that is footprinted by it. Then
\dim(v_{i}\cap w_{i})\leq t
and if
j>i
,
\dim(v_{i}\cap w_{j})\geq t+1
. Lemma
13
implies that
\gamma^{Z}_{gr}(J_{q,S}(n,k))\leq\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}
, hence

Z(J_{q,S}(n,k))\geq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}-\gamma^{Z}_{gr}(J_{q,S}(n,k))\geq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.\qed

For a given vector space
\mathbb{F}^{n}_{q}
, let
{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$a$}}_{n}
be an orthogonal basis. Note that the proofs of Theorem
11
and
12
are still valid for
K_{q}(n,k)
if we replace each subset of
[n]
with the corresponding set of basis vectors. Therefore, we have the following analogous result for
J_{q,S}(n,k)
.

Corollary 15
.

Let
S\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,k-2\}
with
t\coloneqq\max(S)
, and
n\geq\max(3k-2t+1,2k+1)
. Then

Z(J_{q,S}(n,k))\leq Z_{t}(J_{q,S}(n,k))\leq Z_{c}(J_{q,S}(n,k))\leq\genfrac{[}{]}{0.0pt}{}{n}{k}_{q}-\binom{2k-2t}{k-t}.

If
S=\{0,1,\dots,t\}
, equality holds throughout.

Note that this strategy can not be used to extend Theorem
8
to generalized Grassmann graphs. As an example, consider the graph
J_{2,\{1\}}(4,2)
. Theorem
11
implies that the corresponding Johnson graph
J(4,2)
has minimum zero forcing set
|V|\setminus\{\{1,3\},\{1,4\}\}
. In
J_{2,\{1\}}(4,2)
, the corresponding set
V\setminus\{\{a_{1},a_{3}\},\{a_{1},a_{4}\}\}
is also zero forcing, but not of minimum cardinality; there exists a significantly smaller zero forcing set

V\setminus\{\langle a_{1},a_{2}\rangle,\langle a_{1},a_{3}\rangle,\langle a_{1},a_{4}\rangle,\langle a_{2},a_{3}\rangle,\langle a_{2},a_{4}\rangle,\langle a_{3},a_{4}\rangle,\langle a_{1}+a_{2},a_{3}+a_{4}\rangle\}.

4.3
Hamming graphs

In this section we show the exact zero forcing number of Hamming graphs, extending the results on
H(n,2)
and
H(2,q)
in
[
4
]
and
[
5
]
, respectively.

We will use the following elementary property, which can be proved using Newton’s binomial theorem.

Lemma 16
.

For any integers
n\geq 1
,
q\geq 2
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k\in\{0,1,\dots,n\}\\
k\text{ even}\end{subarray}}\binom{n}{2k}(q-1)^{n-2k}=\frac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}+(q-2)^{n}\right).

Theorem 17
.

For
n\geq 1,\ q\geq 2
,
Z(H(n,q))=\frac{1}{2}(q^{n}+(q-2)^{n})
.

Proof.

Define
z_{n,q}\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(q^{n}+(q-2)^{n}\right)
. First, we show that
Z(H(n,q))\leq z_{n,q}
.
Define the
core
of
H(n,q)
as the set
C_{n,q}\coloneqq\{(a_{1},a_{2},\dots,a_{n})\mid a_{i}\in\{1,2,\dots,q-2\}\}
. We prove by induction on
n
that there exists a zero forcing set of size
z_{n,q}
that contains
C_{n,q}
, but where no vertex of
C_{n,q}
acts as a pivot.

For
n=1
, the set
C_{1,q}\cup\{(0)\}
is a zero forcing set for
H(1,q)
of size
q-1
, and white vertex
(q-1)
can be forced with pivot
(0)\notin C_{1,q}
.

Suppose that we have a zero forcing set of
H(n-1,q)
of size
z_{n-1,q}
in which no vertex of
C_{n-1,q}
acts as a pivot, but all are in the leader set. As
H(n,q)=H(n-1,q)\square K_{q}
, the Hamming graph
H(n,q)
can be constructed by taking
q
copies of
H(n-1,q)
and connecting the corresponding vertices in each copy with an edge. The tuple corresponding to each vertex is then extended with a new entry denoting the copy it is in. In the first
q-1
copies, select the same zero forcing set of
H(n-1,q)
respecting the above conditions. In the last copy, choose the same set, excluding the core. The first
q-1
copies can be forced black by applying the zero forcing process of
H(n-1,q)
to each. Note that, by the induction hypothesis, the pivots that we use here are black in every copy, so they still have only one white neighbor. Now we can force the core of the last copy, using the corresponding vertices in the first copy as pivots. Finally, the zero forcing process of
H(n-1,q)
can be repeated for the last copy. Note that the core of
H(n,q)
is included in the leader set, while none of its vertices were used as a pivot.

\small(0,4,0)
\small(0,0,4)
\small(4,0,0)

:
The zero forcing process for
H(3,4)
. The Hamming graph is depicted as a cube, with each unit subcube representing a vertex. Vertices are adjacent whenever their cubes line up along one of the three main axes.

The constructed zero forcing set has size

qz_{n-1,q}-(q-2)^{n-1}=z_{n,q}

and therefore completes the induction. We conclude that
Z(H(n,q))\leq z_{n,q}
.

To show that this bound is tight, we now prove that
Z(H(n,q))\geq z_{n,q}
. Define the matrix
B_{n}
recursively as

\left\{\begin{matrix}[l]B_{1}=J\\
B_{n}=J\otimes I^{\otimes(n-1)}+I\otimes B_{n-1}\end{matrix}\right.

or, explicitly,

B_{n}=\underbrace{J\otimes I\otimes\cdots\otimes I}_{n}+\underbrace{I\otimes J\otimes\cdots\otimes I}_{n}+\cdots+\underbrace{I\otimes\cdots\otimes I\otimes J}_{n}

where
I
and
J
are the
q\times q
unit matrix and the
q\times q
all-one matrix respectively, and
\otimes
denotes the tensor product. Then
B_{n}
is in
{\mathcal{S}}(H(n,q))
.
We show that its nullity as a matrix over
\mathbb{F}_{2}
is at least
z_{n,q}
by constructing a set of
z_{n,q}
independent eigenvectors with eigenvalue zero. The result then follows from Lemma
1
. Let
{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}
be the
i
th standard basis vector of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{q}
and let
1
denote the all-one vector of
\mathbb{F}_{2}^{q}
. This vector is an eigenvector of
J
with eigenvalue one if
q
is odd and eigenvalue zero if
q
is even. Let
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{q-1}\}
be a set of independent eigenvectors of
J
with eigenvalue zero.

Case 1:
q
is odd.

Consider all elements of
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{q-1}\}^{\otimes n}
that contain an even number of
1
’s. In other words, all tensors of the form

\underbrace{{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}}_{k}\;\otimes\;{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i_{1}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i_{2}}\otimes\cdots\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i_{n-k}}

with
k
even, and permutations thereof. These are all elements of the kernel of
B_{n}
and, by Lemma
16
, there are
z_{n,q}
such vectors. Moreover, they are all linearly independent, because
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{q-1}
and
1
are linearly independent.

Case 2:
q
is even.

We construct
z_{n,q}
eigenvectors with eigenvalue zero by induction on
n
. If
n=1
, then we can choose the
z_{1,q}=q-1
eigenvectors
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{q-1}
of
J
. For the induction step, suppose that
X
is a set of
z_{n-1,q}
linearly independent vectors that nullify
B_{n-1}
.

Since
J{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}=0
, we can choose the set
\{{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{q-1}\}
in such a way that
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{1}={\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}
. Consider the
(q-2)z_{n-1,q}
vectors of the form

{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{v}

with
i\in\{2,3,\dots,q-1\}
and
\mathbf{v}\in X
. They are eigenvectors with eigenvalue zero.

Consider also the
q^{n-1}
vectors of the form

{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{1}\otimes(B_{n-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})

with
{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}\in\{{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{1},{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{2},\dots,{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{q}\}^{\otimes(n-1)}
. They are nullified by
B_{n}
since

\displaystyle B_{n}({\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\otimes(B_{n-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}))

\displaystyle=(J\otimes I+I\otimes B_{n-1})({\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\otimes(B_{n-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}))

\displaystyle=2\cdot{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes(B_{n-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})+{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{i}\otimes(B_{n-1}^{2}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})

\displaystyle=\mathbf{0},

where we used that
B_{n-1}^{2}=0
since
J^{2}=qJ=0
.
Moreover, the vectors
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}\otimes\mathbf{v}
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$1$}}\otimes{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}}+{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{1}\otimes(B_{n-1}{\mbox{\boldmath$w$}})
are all linearly independent because the
{\mbox{\boldmath$x$}}_{i}
,
1
and
{\mbox{\boldmath$e$}}_{1}
are linearly independent, and all
\mathbf{v}
are linearly independent by the induction hypothesis. In total, we obtain

(q-2)z_{n-1,q}+q^{n-1}=\frac{1}{2}(q^{n}+(q-2)^{n})=z_{n,q}

linearly independent eigenvectors with eigenvalue zero.
∎
