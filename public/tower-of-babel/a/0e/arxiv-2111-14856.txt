Baby universes, ensemble averages and factorizations with matters
Cheng Peng, Jia Tian and Jianghui Yu

Abstract
We investigate 2D topological gravity theories with matter fields turned on. We compute correlators of boundary creation operators with extra matter insertions. We provide a systematic procedure to determine a set of α \alpha -states on which the correlation functions of the different boundary operators factorize. Our results suggest that the boundary dual is an ensemble average of theories with different numbers of replicas of a seed matter contribution that is represented by excitations on top of the gravity sector. The construction of the α \alpha -states and the subsequent computations in the Hilbert space point of view are most conveniently done when the bulk couplings of the matter fields are expanded in a basis of vortices, which can be thought of as a 1-dimensional analogue of the SD-branes. We further demonstrate the existence of null states and the conditions imposed by reflection positivity. Moreover, in the CGS model with only disk and cylinder topologies we map the baby universe creation and annihilation operators to the coupling constants in the one time SYK model, which allows us to match the wormhole and half-wormhole contributions in the CGS model explicitly to those identified in the SYK model. This explicitly realizes Coleman’s initial idea. Some details of the computations in a variety of concrete models are presented.

2
Review of the topological gravity theory of surfaces

Following
Marolf:2020xie
, we use
Z
to denote the boundary condition of circular boundaries and
\hat{Z}
to denote the corresponding boundary creation operator. A general state of the theory is

\displaystyle|Z^{n}\rangle=\hat{Z}^{n}|\widetilde{\rm{NB}}\rangle\,,

(1)

here
|\widetilde{\rm{NB}}\rangle
is the no-boundary state. The inner product
\langle Z^{n}|Z^{m}\rangle
is computed by a path integral subject to the boundary condition

\displaystyle\langle Z^{n}\rangle=\sum_{\text{$M$ with $n$ boundaries}}\mu(M)e^{S_{0}\tilde{\chi}(M)}

(2)

where
\mu(M)
is some measure which can be understood as symmetry factors and
\tilde{\chi}
is the bulk Euler’s character that does not count the number of boundaries. For any (connected) closed surface
M
without boundaries, the summation equals to

\displaystyle\sum_{M}e^{S_{0}\chi(M)}=\sum_{g}e^{-S_{0}(2g-2)}\equiv\lambda.

(3)

Therefore the vacuum diagrams with different number of genera sum to

\displaystyle\langle 1\rangle=\langle\widetilde{\rm{NB}}|\widetilde{\rm{NB}}\rangle=e^{\lambda}\ .

(4)

It is more convenient to define the normalized vacuum state
|\text{NB}\rangle=\sqrt{e^{-\lambda}}|\widetilde{\text{NB}}\rangle
. In this note, without special notice, all the correlation functions are computed within the normalized vacuum state so that the results in
Marolf:2020xie
are simply

\displaystyle\langle{Z}^{n}\rangle=B_{n}(\lambda)\,,\qquad\langle e^{u{Z}}\rangle=\exp(\lambda(e^{u}-1)),

(5)

where
B_{n}(\lambda)
is the Bell polynomial.

In analogy with the AdS/CFT duality in higher dimensions, the bulk one-point function

\displaystyle\langle Z\rangle=\lambda\,,

(6)

is related to the partition function of the dual boundary theory. In the present case, the boundary theory should be a topological quantum mechanics with vanishing Hamiltonian therefore the value
\langle Z\rangle
simply counts the dimension of the Hilbert space

\displaystyle{\langle Z_{\partial}\rangle}=\text{Tr}(1)=\text{dim}\,\mathcal{H}_{CFT}\equiv d\in\mathbb{N}.

(7)

As discussed in
Marolf:2020xie
, we should not directly identify
\langle Z_{\partial}\rangle=\langle Z\rangle
since
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
; rather, the bulk results should be regarded as an ensemble average of different boundary values

\displaystyle\left\langle Z^{n}\right\rangle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}d^{n}=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\text{Pois}_{\lambda}(d)\left\langle Z_{\partial}^{n}\right\rangle=\sum_{d=1}^{\infty}\text{Pois}_{\lambda}(d)\left\langle Z_{\partial}\right\rangle^{n}\,,

(8)

where
\left\langle Z_{\partial}\right\rangle
is considered as a random variable drawn from a Poisson distribution with parameter
\lambda
, and
\text{Pois}_{\lambda}(d)
is the probability of
\left\langle Z_{\partial}\right\rangle=d
.
The natural interpretation of this result is that the boundary dual of this topological gravity theory is an ensemble average of different boundary theories with a Poisson probability measure. Indeed, this ensemble average interpretation maps the moment generating function (MGF) of the ensemble of boundary theories,
\left\langle e^{uZ_{\partial}}\right\rangle
, precisely to the bulk result
\left\langle e^{u{Z}}\right\rangle
in (
5
).

Furthermore, since
\hat{Z}
has the physical meaning of creating a boundary, the above result indicates that we can map the ground state of each individual boundary theory with a fixed
\left\langle Z_{\partial}\right\rangle=d
to the
\alpha
-states
\left|Z=d\right\rangle

\displaystyle|d\rangle\equiv|Z=d\rangle,\quad\hat{Z}|d\rangle=d|d\rangle\,,

(9)

of the bulk
Z
operator. The projection coefficient of this map, namely the probability distribution
\text{Pois}_{\lambda}(d)
, provide a geometric meaning to the
Z_{\partial}=d
theory and the bulk
\alpha
-state; the
\lambda^{d}
in the probability distribution means the corresponding bulk configuration has
d
connected components. Since these
\alpha
-states diagonalize the
Z
operator, then
Z
naturally counts the number of connected components of spacetime configuration.

Turning on topological matters

In practice, lots of interesting physics such as Hawking radiation, happen in the presence of matter fields.
Therefore it is interesting to turn on bulk matters in the topological gravity/surface theory in

3.1
Bulk computations

To set up our computation, we would like to introduce operators that create boundary excitations and shoot particles into the bulk. A naive choice is to introduce the set of matter operators
\hat{\Phi}_{a}
, and boundaries with matter insertions would be

\displaystyle|Z_{A}\rangle\equiv\hat{\Phi}_{A}\hat{Z}|{\rm NB}\rangle\,,

(10)

where the index
A
labels the particle states (or species) and we define this state by inserting the operator
\Phi_{A}
on the boundary created by the original boundary operator
\hat{Z}
.

However, we immediately notice a subtlety that

\displaystyle[\hat{Z}_{x},\hat{\Phi}_{y}]\propto\delta_{x,y}\neq 0\,,

(11)

where
\hat{\Phi}_{y}
is the matter operator that shoots a “particle” into the bulk from boundary
y
and the
\hat{Z}_{x}
operator creates a boundary of type
x
. This is true because only when
\Phi_{x}
acts after the
\hat{Z}
operator the matter can be created. This suggests that a more convenient way to deal with the boundary Hilbert space description is to not consider
\hat{Z}
and
\hat{\Phi}

\hat{Z}_{A}

\displaystyle[\hat{Z}_{x},\hat{\Phi}_{y,A}]=\hat{Z}_{x,A}\delta_{x,y}\ .

(12)

To clarify the notation, the
\hat{\Phi}_{A}

here is quite general, it can be a single particle state or other complicated many-particle states.

To complete this demonstration, we evaluate the matter contribution in the above expression. The simplest choice is to consider free matter theory

\displaystyle\langle\hat{\phi}_{a}\rangle_{M}=0,\quad\langle\hat{\phi_{a}}\hat{\phi_{b}}\rangle_{M}=\delta_{ab}.

(15)

In this theory, higher-order correlation functions of the matter fields all factorize into products of these 2-point functions.
The final 2-point correlation function thus reads

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}_{a}\hat{Z}_{b}\rangle=\lambda\delta_{ab}\ .

(16)

As can be seen from this explicit example, the matter fields not only contribute a multiplicative factor, but they could also significantly alter the structure of the gravitational path integral, even if we do not consider the non-perturbative corrections from the matter fields.

Next, we move on to compute the correlation functions in a generic theory, which can be done in a few steps as follows.

First, we compute the partition function
S(t)
of the matter theory on a single (connected) surface with an arbitrary number of boundaries

\displaystyle S(t)=\left\langle\exp\left(\sum_{a}^{h}t_{a}\hat{\phi}_{a}\right)\right\rangle_{s}=\sum_{n}\frac{\left\langle(\sum_{a}t_{a}\hat{\phi}_{a})^{n}\right\rangle_{s}}{(n)!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(t)}{n!}\,,

(17)

where
s_{n}(t)
is the
n
-point correlation function of the bulk matter theory on a connected surface, which is computed by summing over topological distinct Witten diagrams. The subscript
s
indicates that we consider all the matter contributions on a given connected surface. For later convenience, we call the function
S(t)
the surface connection polynomial (SCP) of the bulk matter field.

In the second step, we compute the path integral on all possible bulk topologies that end on a given number of, say
k
, boundaries
\left\langle\left(\sum_{a}t_{a}\hat{Z}_{a}\right)^{k}\right\rangle
. The result is a sum over configurations with different numbers of disconnected components, which is organized into the Bell polynomials. On each component the contribution from the matter fields can be computed from the known SCP function,
S(t)
or
s_{n}(t)
. Putting all these together, we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\sum_{a}t_{a}\hat{Z}_{a}\right)^{k}\right\rangle=\sum_{\{k_{1},\ldots k_{j}\}\in P(k)}\frac{k!}{\prod_{i}(k_{i}!)\prod(m_{k}!)}\left(\prod_{i}s_{k_{i}}(t)\right)\lambda^{j}

(18)

where
m_{r}
is the number of
r
appearing in the partition
\{k_{1},\ldots k_{j}\}
.

The last step is to sum over these correlation functions to get the generating function

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(\sum_{a}t_{a}\hat{Z}_{a}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\left\langle\left(\sum_{a}t_{a}\hat{Z}_{a}\right)^{k}\right\rangle

(19)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\{k_{1},\ldots k_{j}\}\in P(k)}\frac{k!}{\prod_{i}(k_{i}!)\prod(m_{k}!)}\left(\prod_{i}s_{k_{i}}(t)\right)\lambda^{j}

(20)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k!}\sum_{\{k_{1},\ldots k_{j}\}\in P(k)}\frac{k!}{\prod_{i}(k_{i}!)\prod(m_{k}!)}\left(\prod_{i}s_{k_{i}}(t)\lambda\right)

(21)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(t)}{n!}\right)=\exp\left(\lambda\left(S(t)-1\right)\right)\ .

(22)

Up to now, the derivation is fairly general and details of the matter theory are all encoded in the SCP function.
In the above derivation, there are no constraints imposed on
S(t)
.
In practice, since the
S(t)
function is defined on a single connected surface, it can also be formulated as the expectation value of some source term in the bulk matter field theory. We will present further details in later examples. Here we only present a most general schematic computation.

First, without the sources, we can compute

\displaystyle z_{g}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\phi e^{-\frac{\phi^{2}}{2p}-V(\phi)}\,,\qquad

(23)

where
p
is the “propagator” of the topological theory and
V(\phi)
describes the possible interactions of the theory.

Next, the expectation values of the matter fields with the sources are

\displaystyle S(t)=\left\langle e^{J(t,\phi)}\right\rangle=\frac{1}{z_{g}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\phi e^{-\frac{\phi^{2}}{2p}-V(\phi)+J(t,\phi)}\,,

(24)

where
t
represents chemical potentials sourcing the matter insertions.

It is clear that the function
S(t)
depends not only on the theory we consider, it also depends on what are the operators inserted on the boundaries.
So we denote by
S_{a}(t)
the SCP function for the special case where all the boundaries are
\hat{Z}_{a}
for any fixed
a
. To understand how this is reflected in the above general result, we consider the following special example as an illustration. Suppose we want to compute

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(t_{1}\hat{Z}_{a_{1}}+\ldots+t_{m}\hat{Z}_{a_{m}}\right)\right\rangle\ .

(25)

We assume
\hat{Z}_{a_{i}}
are only “gravitationally coupled” to each other, namely they can be connected by the same surface, but the matter lines do not go from one type to the other. In this case, the SCP has a special form

\displaystyle S(t)=S_{1}(t)S_{2}(t)\ldots S_{m}(t)\ .

(26)

This relation is easily derived from

\displaystyle S(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}s_{n}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}=\sum_{n}\sum_{i_{1},\ldots i_{m}}^{n}{n\choose i_{1},i_{2},\ldots i_{m}}s_{1,i_{1}}\ldots s_{m,i_{m}}\frac{t^{n}}{n!}

(27)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1}}\frac{s_{1,i_{1}}t^{i_{1}}}{i_{1}!}\ldots\frac{s_{m,i_{m}}t^{i_{m}}}{i_{m}!}=S_{1}(t)S_{2}(t)\ldots S_{m}(t)\ .

(28)

When the fugacity of the different boundaries are different, it is easy to modify the above result to

\displaystyle S(t_{1},\ldots,t_{m})

\displaystyle=\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}}s_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}}\prod_{j}\frac{t_{j}^{n_{j}}}{n_{j}!}=\sum_{n_{1},\ldots,n_{m}}s_{1,n_{1}}\ldots s_{m,n_{m}}\frac{t_{j}^{n_{j}}}{n_{j}!}=S_{1}(t_{1})\ldots S_{m}(t_{m})\ .

(29)

Therefore in this special case, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(\sum_{a}t_{a}\hat{Z}_{a}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\lambda\left(S_{1}(t_{1})S_{2}(t_{2})\ldots S_{m}(t_{m})-1\right)\right)\ .

(30)

In more general theories, the SCP does not factorize and we have to evaluate it as a whole.

\bullet

Secondly, this result can be understood as a low-dimensional analogue of the RG flows in more familiar QFTs.

\bullet

From the above general discussion, we could deduce

Then the moment generating function (MGF), denoted by

, of the compound distribution is

\displaystyle\text{MG}_{Y}(u)=\text{MG}_{M}(\log\text{MG}_{X}(u))\,,

(86)

where
u
is the fugacity that help organize all the higher order moments, and the moment generating function is defined to be

\displaystyle\text{MG}_{X}(u)=\mathbb{E}_{X}(e^{ux})=\int dxP_{X}(x)e^{ux}=\sum_{j}\frac{u^{j}}{j!}\mathbb{E}_{X}(x^{j})\equiv\sum_{j}\frac{u^{j}}{j!}M_{j}\,,

(87)

where
M_{j}
is the
j^{\text{th}}
moment.
In our computation we consider the
M
distribution to be a Poisson distribution with the Poisson parameter
\lambda
(hence the compound distribution is called a compound Poisson distribution), which has a moment generating function

\displaystyle\text{MG}_{M}(u)

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\left(e^{u}-1\right)}\ .

(88)

Given this, if we consider the
X
distribution that has a MGF

\displaystyle\text{MG}_{X}(u)

\displaystyle=S(u)\,,

(89)

we find their compound distribution have a MGF

\displaystyle\text{MG}_{Y}(u)=e^{\lambda\left(e^{\log\left(S(u)\right)}-1\right)}=e^{\lambda\left(S(u)-1\right)}\ .

(90)

This is precisely the general result (
22
) we obtained previously. In particular, the gravity/surface contribution leads to the Poisson distribution of the number of copies of the matter fields; and the matter contribution provides the details of each copy in this compound Poisson distribution interpretation. This interpretation is pictorially illustrated in Figure
7
.

). The left figure demonstrate the original results in

, the right figure demonstrate the result with matter coupled.

As a consistent check, we notice that if we choose the
X
distribution to be the constant distribution, which means we insert an identity operator and there is no extra matter excitations, whose probability distribution is a delta function

\displaystyle f(x,a)=\delta(x-a)\,,

(91)

with a MGF

\displaystyle\int dxe^{ux}\delta(x-a)=e^{au}\ .

(92)

Then the resulting composite distribution is

\displaystyle\text{MG}_{Y}^{\prime}(u)=e^{\lambda\left(e^{\log\left(e^{au}\right)}-1\right)}=e^{\lambda\left(e^{au}-1\right)}\,,

(93)

which is nothing but the result of
Marolf:2020xie
once we normalize to
a=1
.

This leads to a clear interpretation of our result (
22
). Recall that in the results of
Marolf:2020xie
the random variable
Z
counts the dimensions of the Hilbert space of the boundary theory which is drawn from an ensemble. It can be reinterpreted as a composite random variable

\displaystyle Z=x_{1}+\ldots x_{d}\,,

(94)

where each
x_{i}
is a random variable that generate a sub-Hilbert space, and the
x_{i}
are
d
identical copies of the same system. When this sub-Hilbert system contains a single state
|i\rangle
, their contribution to
Z
, in other words, the MGF
x
is always
e^{u}
. This corresponds to the
\delta(x-1)
distribution function in the above discussion.

While in our theory with matter fields, the effect of adding bulk matter does not change the fact that
Z
is still a trace of some sub-Hilbert spaces
x_{i}
and the number of such sub-Hilbert spaces is still from the same Poisson distribution. These are determined by the “surface/gravity” sector of the theory, and including different numbers of replicas of the sub-Hilbert spaces leads to the ensemble of theories. However, due to the existence of the matter fields in the correlators, the sub-Hilbert that is detected contains more states due to the matter excitations; in the compound distribution language, this means the
x_{i}
variables follow another distribution instead of the constant distribution.
Our computation provides another explicit example, with matter fields coupled, of the recent insight that gravitational path integral might be dual to the average of an ensemble of field theories. Notice that ideas similar to this compound Poisson distribution have previously been discussed in
Peng:2020rno
although the discussion there is mainly from the field theory considerations, the interpretation in this section is closely related and inspired by
Peng:2020rno
.

Also notice that there could be other variance of this boundary interpretation where again the matter theory and the gravity sector play different roles. We will provide some discussions about this in section
4.4
.

3.4

In the discussion of

In the presence of matter fields, they contribute to the entropy

In the following, we denote the boundary operator as

4.1
Free fields, singleton insertion
\hat{Z}_{1}\simeq Z\phi

In this case the boundary insertion is

4.1.1
Summing over diagrams

In the current case of free matter theory with singleton insertions, only even insertions lead to non-vanishing contributions. In the presence of
2n
insertions, the trajectories are simply a collection of
n
propagators. Since the total number of such diagrams is
\frac{(2n)!}{n!2^{n}}
and each diagram simply contributes a factor of
t^{2}
, we therefore have

\displaystyle s_{2n}(t)=\frac{(2n)!}{n!2^{n}}p^{n}(t^{2})^{n}\,,\quad s_{2n+1}=0\,,\qquad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}\ .

(98)

Plugging this into (
17
), we get

\displaystyle S(t)=\sum_{n}\frac{p^{n}(t^{2})^{n}}{(2n)!}\frac{(2n)!}{n!2^{n}}=e^{\frac{pt^{2}}{2}}\ .

(99)

As expected, in a free scalar theory, the SCP is Gaussian. We then have

\displaystyle\left\langle e^{tZ_{1}}\right\rangle=e^{\lambda\left(e^{\frac{pt^{2}}{2}}-1\right)}\ .

(100)

4.1.2
Bulk path integral

As discussed in general in the previous section, there is a systematic way to compute the SCP on a given surface. Since only a single surface is involved, this counting is the same as computing the partition function of the matter fields. Furthermore, since the matter field is topological, the path integral to get the partition function is simple to evaluate.

In the current free theory, the SCP is nothing but the expectation of the sources
e^{u\phi}
. For example, the insertion of
\hat{Z}_{1}
means we turn on the source
u\phi
and in the free theory the expectation value of this is

\displaystyle S(t)=\left\langle e^{t\phi}\right\rangle_{c}=\frac{1}{z_{0}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}d\phi e^{-\frac{\phi^{2}}{2p}}e^{t\phi}=e^{\frac{pt^{2}}{2}}\ .

(101)

This reproduces the previous result we got from counting all the diagrams.

4.1.3
Probability interpretation and
\alpha
-states

To look for the set of
\alpha
-states, we consider the rewriting

\displaystyle S(t)^{d}

\displaystyle=e^{\frac{pdt^{2}}{2}}=\frac{1}{z_{x}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxe^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2pd}}e^{tx}\,,\qquad z_{x}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dxe^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2pd}}=\sqrt{2\pi pd}\ .

(102)

Then we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(t\hat{Z}_{1}\right)\right\rangle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2pd}}}{\sqrt{2\pi pd}}e^{tx}\right)

\displaystyle\quad=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2pd}}}{\sqrt{2\pi pd}}\right)e^{tx}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2pd}-\frac{1}{2}\log d}}{\sqrt{2\pi p}}\right)e^{tx}\ .

(104)

We can further do a change of variable

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(t\hat{Z}_{1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\frac{e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{2p}}}{\sqrt{2\pi p}}\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}e^{t\sqrt{d}x}\right)\ .

(105)

Another useful change of variable is

and we get

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(t\hat{Z}_{1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx\,\left(\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\frac{e^{-\frac{dx^{2}}{2p}+\frac{1}{2}\log d}}{\sqrt{2\pi p}}\right)e^{tdx}\ .

(106)

Although the most conservative point of view on this result is that the integral over

should be interpreted as a probability distribution that we average over. However, if we take the point of view discussed in section

more seriously, we can regard the integral kernels, for example the

in the last line above, as an effective action of the matter fields, once we condition on the

state.
Adopting this point of view, the expression (

. However, we find there exist other rewriting (

and the average is on the operator inserted in the single theory, like the discussion in

Peng:2020rno

Summing over diagrams

(139)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle d-ci-c^{\star}i+cc^{\star}j,

whose minimal value is at

\displaystyle c=c^{\star}=i/j,

(140)

\displaystyle\langle\Delta|\Delta\rangle=d-\frac{i^{2}}{j}.

(141)

Therefore the state

\displaystyle\left|\Delta_{0}\right\rangle=|\text{HC}_{i^{2}/j},n_{ij}\rangle-i/j|\text{HC}_{i^{2}/j},\phi,n_{ij}\rangle\,,

(142)

is null. More generally when more quantum numbers are non-zero, the null states are

\displaystyle\left|\Delta,\{n_{ij}\}\right\rangle=\left|\text{HC}_{d},\{n_{ij}\}\right\rangle-\frac{\sum_{i,j}in_{ij}}{\sum_{i,j}jn_{ij}}\left|\text{HC}_{d},\phi,\{n_{ij}\}\right\rangle,\quad d=\frac{\left(\sum_{i,j}in_{ij}\right)^{2}}{\sum_{i,j}jn_{ij}}\ .

(143)

The null condition (
143
) could be put into a more instructive form

\displaystyle\hat{Z}\hat{Z}_{2}-\left(\hat{Z}_{1}\right)^{2}\sim 0\,,

(144)

where the notion “
\sim
” indicates that the above expression (
144
) is true when acting on simultaneous eigenstates of the different boundary creation operators.

More generally, the above null condition gives the border of the region allowed by reflection positivity. This bound imposed by reflection positivity

\displaystyle Z_{\alpha}Z_{2,\alpha}\geq Z_{1,\alpha}^{2}\,,

(145)

indicates that quantum numbers of the boundaries with a lower number of insertions lead to a lower bound on the quantum numbers of the boundaries with a larger number of insertions.

Clearly, similar null states exist once we turn on boundaries with higher numbers of matter insertions, and the half-circle Hilbert space is spanned by

\displaystyle\left|\text{HC}_{d},\alpha,\phi^{n}\right\rangle\ .

(146)

The reflection positivity also leads to relations among the quantum numbers in the set of
\alpha
-states that diagonalize the new set of boundary operators. In practice, the null states and the reflection positive region can be determined from the Kac determinant, or equivalent the determinant of the Hankel matrix whose elements are
H_{ij}=\langle\text{HC}_{d},\alpha,\phi^{i}|\text{HC}_{d},\alpha,\phi^{j}\rangle

\displaystyle H=\begin{bmatrix}Z_{0,\alpha}&Z_{1,\alpha}&Z_{2,\alpha}&Z_{3,\alpha}\dots\\
Z_{1,\alpha}&Z_{2,\alpha}&Z_{3,\alpha}&Z_{4,\alpha}\dots\\
Z_{2,\alpha}&Z_{3,\alpha}&Z_{4,\alpha}&Z_{5,\alpha}\dots\\
\dots\end{bmatrix}\ .

(147)

From the familiar Kac determinant argument, or equivalently from the Hamburger theorem,
the following two statements are equivalent

1.

The Hilbert space is positive semidefinite.

2.

All Hankel matrices are positive semidefinite.

Therefore vanishing of the determinants gives the null states and the positivity of minors of the above matrix gives the reflection positivity constraints. Notice that this is the same technique used in recent amplituhedron/positive geometry discussion
Arkani-Hamed:2020blm
.

4.4
Some further speculations about the boundary interpretation

Following the approach discussed in section
3.3.1
, we can focus on a single
\left|Z=d\,\right\rangle
state.
Each individual boundary theory is defined in one of such
\alpha
-states. The matter theory is treated perturbatively, which means the inclusion of the matter fields does not affect our choice of the
\alpha
-state. In this interpretation, the sum over
d
in (
148
)-(
151
) should be considered as a sum over different
\alpha
-states, i.e. averaging over different theories. The integrals in
x
or
y
are just the path integral of the matter fields within the individual theory.

To see this more clearly, we first rewrite the result more instructively as an integral weighted by an effective action. Immediately we find that the effective action does not only depend on the choice of the
\alpha
-states
\left|Z=d\,\right\rangle
, it also depends on the choice of the operator/boundary conditions.
As we have worked out in details in the previous sections, we have

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(t\hat{Z}_{1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\frac{d}{2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{dx}\,e^{-\frac{dx^{2}}{2p}}e^{tdx}

(148)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\frac{d}{2p}\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dye^{y-\frac{de^{2y}}{2p}}\left(e^{tde^{y}}+e^{-tde^{y}}\right)

(149)

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(t\hat{Z}_{2}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\frac{d}{2p}\right)^{d/2}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}{dx}e^{-\frac{dx^{2}}{2p}+(d-1)\log|x|}e^{tdx^{2}}

(150)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\frac{d}{2p}\right)^{d/2}\,\Gamma\left(\frac{d}{2}\right)^{-1}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dye^{dy-\frac{de^{2y}}{2p}}2e^{tde^{2y}}\,,

(151)

where we have defined
y=\log x
, and notice that in both the above expressions, the integration variable
x
and
e^{y}
play a similar role as the boundary dual of the matter theory we coupled in the bulk.

Therefore, the exponent of the integrand should be identified as the action with the specific source inserted. Indeed, in the
y
variable the action looks like the Liouville theory at
R=1
for (
149
) or
R=d
for (
151
) where
y
is identified as the Liouville field. The insertion is also in the form of (half of) the Liouville potential. Alternatively, in the
x
variable the action contains a logarithmic potential as in the Coulomb gas model.

One peculiar feature of this computation is that naively since the effective action is independent of the boundary insertion, it should be universal for all different types of boundary insertions.
However, from our result it appears that the effective action needed to reproduce the bulk computation for the different boundary insertions are slightly different. For example, the difference between (
149
) and (
151
) is just the choice of the boundary insertions
\hat{Z}_{1}
versus
\hat{Z}_{2}
, or equivalently
\phi
versus
\phi^{2}
. However, the effective actions for the boundary matter field are different in the same
\left|Z=d\right\rangle

\alpha
-state. This is slightly counter-intuitive but it could actually be possible, viewed from both the boundary and the bulk points of view. From the boundary point of view, the effective action of the correlators is the Legendre transformation of the original theory in the presence of the operator insertions where the latter is treated as a source. So if the sources are different, the resulting effective actions after the Legendre transformation are expected to be different. From the bulk point of view, changing the boundary operator insertion has the effect of both changing the matter fields shot into the bulk and possibly changing the bulk propagator due to different boundary conditions. Indeed, as we observed here that changing the boundary insertion from
\hat{Z}_{1}
to
\hat{Z}_{2}
, ie from (
148
) to (
150
), the change of the effective action is simply replacing
y
to
yd
or in the Liouville theory analogue changing
Ry
to
Ryd
, this is the low-dimensional analogue of reducing the propagator by a factor of
d
since the
Ry
term in Liouville gives precisely the propagator of the Liouville field
y
.

Let us reiterate here that the results on the RHS of (
148
)-(
151
) should be considered as boundary results, following the suggestion originated in
Marolf:2020xie
, although the LHS of the equation is computed from the bulk. This is thus a baby version of the holographic duality.

4.5.2
Interacting matter fields, singleton insertions, universal coupling constants, without internal propagators

First, we consider the diagrams with only contact interactions. This can be regarded as taking the limit

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{k}\sim g_{k}p^{-k}\,,\qquad p\to\infty\,,

In this limit the curve connection polynomial (CCP) is

\displaystyle c_{0}=1\,,\quad c_{1}=g_{1}\,,\qquad c_{2}=1+g_{2}\,,\qquad c_{n}=g_{n}\,,\quad\forall n>2\ .

(161)

To write down the generating function in a compact form, we can further assume some relations among the couplings, one choice is

\displaystyle g_{n}=g\,,~\forall n>2\,,

(162)

\displaystyle C_{1}(u,g)=ge^{u}+\frac{g_{2}-g+1}{2}u^{2}+(g_{1}-g)u+(1-g)\ .

\displaystyle S_{1}=e^{h\left(ge^{u}+\frac{g_{2}-g+1}{2}u^{2}+(g_{1}-g)u-g\right)}\,,

(164)

With these results, we get the generating function

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(u\hat{Z}_{1}\right)\right\rangle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(e^{h\left(ge^{u}+\frac{g_{2}-g+1}{2}u^{2}+(g_{1}-g)u-g\right)}-1\right)\right]\ .

(165)

Notice that in this analysis we have kept explicitly the

In addition, we can also probe the Gaussian limit, also known as the CGS limit. The crucial point is that we can use the free propagators between the boundaries to select only cylindrical background geometries and turn off the higher coupling by setting

\displaystyle S_{1}\sim 1+hg_{1}u+h\frac{1+g_{2}}{2}u^{2}+\mathcal{O}(h^{2})\ .

(167)

This is the generating function of the CGS model

Next, we look for the probability distribution interpretation. To achieve this, we consider the

(170)

4.5.3
Interacting matter fields, singletons, tree level with internal propagators

We can in fact relax the constraints in the previous section to allow internal propagators, and thus exchanged diagrams. This leads to the tree approximation of the matter theory.

We take the
n
-vertices to be diagonal in the flavor space
g\sum_{i=1}^{h}\phi_{i}^{n}
and set the fugacity
t_{a}=u
. As a result, matter with different species gets decoupled.
For each connected component of a tree level Witten diagram, we have

\displaystyle P-E+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}V_{k}=1\,,\quad 2E=P+\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}kV_{k}\,,

(197)

where
P
represents the number of external points and
V_{k}
represents the number of
k
-point vertices.

Notice that the number of 2-valent vertices can be shifted away by the redefinition of the edges

\displaystyle\tilde{E}=E-V_{2}\,,

(198)

which is another equivalent expression of the fact that a theory with only 2-pt vertices is essentially free. Therefore in the following, we restrict the sum to run from 3 to infinity.
The solution of this set of equations is

\displaystyle E=2P-3+\sum_{k=4}^{P}(3-k)V_{k}\,,\qquad V_{3}=P-2+\sum_{k=4}^{P}(2-k)V_{k}\ .

(199)

Namely, for a given set of external points and the number of internal vertices, the total number of edges and the number of one type of vertices are determined. Notice that we have also used the no-loop assumption to cutoff the sum; in the absence of loops, a connected diagram can involve only up to
p
-valence vertices.

Then each diagram gives a contribution to the partition function as

\displaystyle ht^{p}\prod_{k=3}^{P}g_{k}^{V_{k}}\,,

(200)

where we do not put in the information of
E
since it is totally determined by the powers of
t
and
g_{k}
.

In fact, for a diagram with a set of
[V_{3},V_{4},\ldots V_{k}]
labelled vertices, there are

\displaystyle M^{\prime}(V_{3},\ldots,V_{P})=\frac{\left(\sum(k-1)V_{k}\right)!}{\prod_{k}\left((k-1)!\right)^{V_{k}}}\ .

(201)

For our cases the internal vertices are not labelled, so the actual number is

\displaystyle M(V_{3},\ldots,V_{P})=\frac{\left(\sum(k-1)V_{k}\right)!}{\prod_{k}\left((k-1)!\right)^{V_{k}}V_{k}!}\ .

(202)

One can compute the total number of tree diagrams for a given number of external points, these numbers have a generating function

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\left(u-1-2W_{0}\left(-\frac{1}{2}e^{\frac{u-1}{2}}\right)\right)\,,

(203)

where
W_{0}
is the principal branch of the product logarithm function.

The curve connection polynomial which is associated with connected tree is a refinement of the above numbers

\displaystyle c_{2}=1\,,\quad c_{3}=g\,,\quad c_{4}=3g^{2}+g\,,\quad c_{5}=15g^{3}+10g^{2}+g

(204)

\displaystyle c_{6}=105g^{4}+105g^{3}+25g^{2}+g\,,\quad c_{7}=945g^{5}+1260g^{4}+490g^{3}+56g^{2}+g\ .

(205)

The generating function of this series of polynomials can be worked out

\displaystyle C^{\prime}(u,g)=\frac{u-g}{g+1}-W_{0}\left(-\frac{ge^{\frac{u-g}{g+1}}}{g+1}\right)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}c_{n+1}\frac{u^{n}}{n!}\,,

(206)

where a subtle issue is the shift by one in the expansion of the generating function.
To cure this shift, we can simply integrate the generating function to get

\displaystyle C(u,g)

4.5.4

We can turn on more general types of vertices that mixes the different flavors. As a warm up, let us first consider two-valent vertices, or 2-vertex for short. We consider 2-vertices of the form

5.1

We consider single-particle excitation that is described by the new boundary operators
\hat{\psi}_{a}\hat{Z}\equiv\hat{\Psi}_{a}

\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{a}\hat{Z}\equiv\hat{\Psi}_{a}^{\dagger}

(261)

One way to proceed is to do a formal power expansion

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(\sum_{a=1}^{h}(v_{a}\hat{\Psi}_{a}+v^{*}_{a}\hat{\Psi}_{a}^{\dagger})\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{n}\frac{1}{(2n)!}{2n\choose n}\left\langle(\sum_{a}v_{a}\hat{\psi}_{a}\sum_{b}v_{b}^{*}\hat{\psi}_{b}^{\dagger})^{n}\hat{Z}^{2n}\right\rangle

(263)

where we have defined

where

still satisfies Poisson distribution and
x_{i},x_{i}^{\dagger}
satisfy the complex Gaussian distribution.

5.2
Free fields, doubleton insertions

Let us denote the interesting boundary operator by
\hat{\Psi}_{i,j}=\hat{\psi}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{Z}
. To compute the generating function

\displaystyle\langle e^{\sum_{i,j=1}^{h}t_{ij}\Psi_{ij}}\rangle,

(273)

we first compute the curve connection polynomial
c_{n}(t)
. Given the rule (
260
) one can easily find

\displaystyle c_{n}(t)=(n-1)!\text{tr}[t^{n}]

(274)

whose generating function is

\displaystyle C(t)=\sum_{n=1}\frac{c_{n}}{n!}=-\log\left(\text{det}[1-t]\right).

(275)

Therefore the generating function of the surface connection polynomial is

\displaystyle S(t)=e^{C}=\text{det}(1-t)^{-1}.

(276)

We can also include the
\hat{Z}
operator into the computation

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(v\hat{Z}+u\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\hat{\Psi}_{ij}\right)\right\rangle=\exp\left[\lambda\frac{e^{v}}{\left(\text{det}(I-ut)\right)}\right]\,,

(277)

which is consistent with the generating function with EOW branes
Marolf:2020xie
once we set
u=1
.
The generating function (
277
) can be again obtained from exponentiate the connected surface connection generating function, the factorial structure of it makes sure that the result after the exponentiation gives the correct counting of the degeneracy. This means

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(v\hat{Z}+u\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\hat{\Psi}_{ij}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left(\lambda\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(t,u,v)}{n!}\right)

(278)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{s_{n}(t,u,v)}{n!}-1\right)\right]

(279)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n!}\sum_{k=0}^{n}{n\choose k}s_{k}(t)u^{k}v^{n-k}-1\right)\right]

(280)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0}^{n}\frac{s_{k}(t)u^{k}v^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!}-1\right)\right]\,,

(281)

where the
s_{n}(t,u,v)
represents the surface connection polynomial with fugacities
t_{ij},u,v
that correspond to the matter part, the
\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\hat{\Psi}_{ij}
and
\hat{Z}
boundaries respectively. In the third line we have expressed this polynomial as products of the “mattered” boundaries and the normal boundaries with the associated symmetry, together with the
s_{k}(t)
defined in the expansion of (
276
) with respect to
t
.

To evaluate (
281
), we change variable

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(v\hat{Z}+u\sum_{i,j}t_{ij}\hat{\Psi}_{ij}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=k}^{\infty}\frac{s_{k}(t)u^{k}v^{n-k}}{k!(n-k)!}-1\right)\right]

(282)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{p=0}^{\infty}\frac{s_{k}(t)u^{k}v^{p}}{k!p!}-1\right)\right]

(283)

\displaystyle=\exp\left[\lambda\left(e^{v}\text{det}(1-u)^{-1}-1\right)\right]\,,

(284)

where in the last step we have done the two sums separately since they decouple.

5.3
Interacting fields, singleton insertions, with internal propagators

To illustrate some subtleties about the interacting theories, let us study two-vertex first. The most general two-vertex is of the form

\displaystyle\sum_{i,j=1}^{h}\left(g_{ij}\hat{\psi}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{j}+g_{ij}^{*}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{j}^{\dagger}+d_{ij}\hat{\psi_{i}}\hat{\psi}_{j}^{\dagger}\right)\ .

(285)

Note that the first two terms are not common in a complex field theory since they break the
U(1)
symmetry. In our theory, they will introduce non-trivial propagators (two-point function)
\langle\hat{\psi}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{j}\rangle_{M}
and
\langle\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{j}\rangle_{M}
.
The last term can be absorbed into the kinetic term and renormalize the bare propagator.
So we will only consider the first two types of vertices, and do the integral to get the partition function

\displaystyle F(v)=

\displaystyle\int\left[\prod_{a=1}^{h}\frac{d\psi_{a}d\psi_{a}^{\dagger}}{2\pi}e^{-\psi_{a}\psi_{a}^{\dagger}}\right]e^{\sum_{a}v_{a}\psi_{a}+v_{a}^{*}\psi_{a}^{\dagger}-\sum_{i,j=1}^{h}\frac{1}{2}g_{ij}{\psi}_{i}{\psi}_{j}-\frac{1}{2}g^{*}_{ij}{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}{\psi}^{\dagger}_{j}}

(287)

\displaystyle\quad=\frac{\exp\frac{1}{2}\left(|v|^{2}+(v^{*}-vg^{*})(1-gg^{*})^{-1}(v-gv^{*})\right)}{\sqrt{\text{det}(gg^{\star}-1)}}\ .

Now we add in the interactions perturbatively. We focus on three-vertices of the following four types

\displaystyle g^{(1)}_{ijk}\hat{\psi}_{i}\hat{\psi}_{j}\hat{\psi}_{k},\quad g^{(2)}_{ijk}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{\dagger}\hat{\psi}_{j}\hat{\psi}_{k},\quad g^{(3)}_{ijk}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{\psi}_{k},\quad g^{(4)}_{ijk}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{j}\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{k}\ .

(288)

A closed-form expression for the partition function of this interacting theory is complicated. Here we only provide some perturbative analysis. For example, the leading order correction is simply given by

\displaystyle F(v)_{1}=\left(g^{(1)}_{ijk}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta v_{i}\delta v_{j}\delta v_{k}}+g^{(2)}_{ijk}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta v^{*}_{i}\delta v_{j}\delta v_{k}}+g^{(3)}_{ijk}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta v^{*}_{i}\delta v^{*}_{j}\delta v_{k}}+g^{(4)}_{ijk}\frac{\delta^{3}}{\delta v^{*}_{i}\delta v^{*}_{j}\delta v^{*}_{k}}\right)e^{\sum_{i}v_{i}v_{i}^{*}}|_{v=v^{*}=0}.

If we focus again on the tree-level diagrams, their contributions to the partition function can also be obtained from a saddle approximation of the integral

\displaystyle F(v)=\int\frac{d\psi d\psi^{\dagger}}{2\pi}e^{-\psi\psi^{\dagger}}e^{v\psi+v^{*}\psi^{\dagger}+g{\psi}^{3}+g^{*}{\psi^{\dagger}}^{3}}.

(290)

The saddle point equation can be solved exactly, but the expression of the solution is very complicated so we only present its series expansion with respect to the couplings

\displaystyle\psi=v^{*}+3g^{*}v^{2}+18gg^{*}v{v^{*}}^{2}+\dots,

(291)

\displaystyle\psi^{\dagger}=v+3g{v^{*}}^{2}+18gg^{*}v^{2}{v^{*}}+\dots.

(292)

Then the tree diagram contributions to
F(v)
are

\displaystyle\tilde{F}(v,v^{*})=e^{|v|^{2}}\left(1+g{v^{*}}^{3}+g^{*}v^{3}+\frac{1}{2}[(g{v^{*}}^{3}+g^{*}v^{3})^{2}+18gg^{*}|v|^{2}]\right)+\dots

(293)

Other
n
-vertex can be analyzed similarly. The results are not very illuminating so we skip all the details.

5.4
Interacting fields, singleton insertions, without internal propagators

Another limit that is under analytic control is to consider again only contact diagrams, as we did in section (
4.5.2
) for the real scalar theory. One choice is to assume all the couplings to be the same, i.e.

\displaystyle c_{n,m}=g,\quad\text{ such that }\langle\hat{\psi}^{n}(\hat{\psi}^{\dagger})^{m}\rangle_{c}=g\,,

(294)

whose generating function (CCP) is

\displaystyle C(v,v^{*})=\sum_{n,m=1}^{\infty}\frac{v^{n}}{n!}\frac{{v^{*}}^{m}}{m!}g=g(e^{v}-1)(e^{v^{*}}-1),

(295)

which factorizes and hence it means that
\psi
and
\psi^{\dagger}
can be counted independently.

Another choice is to consider only those preserving the
U(1)
symmetry

\displaystyle c_{n,m}=g\delta_{n,m},\quad\text{ such that }\langle\hat{\psi}^{n}(\hat{\psi}^{\dagger})^{m}\rangle_{c}=g\delta_{n,m}.

(296)

In this case the corresponding CCP generating function is

\displaystyle C(v,v^{*})=ge^{vv^{*}-1}\ .

(297)

5.5
Interacting fields, doubleton insertions
\hat{Z}_{1,1}

From the result in the previous section, it is clear that the operators
\hat{\Psi}_{a}
and
\hat{\Psi}_{a}^{\dagger}
have to come in pairs for the correlation functions to have more interesting results. So it is more natural to consider the insertion of the boundary operator
\hat{\Psi}_{i}\hat{\Psi}_{j}^{\dagger}\hat{Z}
.
In the simplest setting, let us fix the boundary operator to be diagonal
\hat{Z}_{1,1}=\sum_{i=1}^{h}\hat{\Psi}_{ii}

and consider the bulk vertices to be homogeneously
k
-valent

\displaystyle V=g_{3}\sum_{i}\hat{\psi}_{i}^{k}+\bar{g}_{3}\sum_{i}\left(\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}_{i}\right)^{k}\ .

(298)

For simplicity in counting the contributing Witten diagrams, we represent the two types of fields by white (
\hat{\psi}
) and black (
\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}
) dots. Next we count the connected diagrams. Denoting
P_{w}
,
P_{b}
the numbers of external white and black points, and
V_{w}
,
V_{b}
the numbers of internal vertices. Because the propagator connects
\hat{\psi}
with
\hat{\psi}^{\dagger}
, a simple counting leads to

\displaystyle P_{w}+P_{b}+V_{w}+V_{b}-E=1\,,\qquad 2E=P_{w}+P_{b}+k\left(V_{w}+V_{b}\right)\,,

(299)

where
E
is the total number of edges.
This gives

\displaystyle V_{w}+V_{b}=\frac{P_{w}+P_{b}-2}{k-2}\,,\quad\Rightarrow\quad E=P_{w}+P_{b}+V_{w}+V_{b}-1=\frac{(k-1)(P_{w}+P_{b})-k}{k-2}\ .

(300)

Furthermore, looking only at the black and white dots separately, each edge connects one and only one white/black dot, therefore we get the following refined conditions

\displaystyle E=P_{w}+kV_{w}=P_{b}+kV_{b}=\frac{(k-1)(P_{w}+P_{b})-k}{k-2}\ .

(301)

This enables us to solve
V_{w/b}
in terms of the external points

\displaystyle V_{w}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)(P_{w}+P_{b})-k-(k-2)P_{w}}{k(k-2)}=\frac{P_{w}+(k-1)P_{b}-k}{k(k-2)}

(302)

\displaystyle V_{b}

\displaystyle=\frac{(k-1)(P_{w}+P_{b})-k-(k-2)P_{b}}{k(k-2)}=\frac{P_{b}+(k-1)P_{w}-k}{k(k-2)}\ .

(303)

Moreover, since the number
V_{w}
and
V_{b}
are integers, we get the condition

\displaystyle|V_{w}-V_{b}|=\frac{(2-k)P_{b}+(k-2)P_{w}}{k(k-2)}=\frac{|P_{w}-P_{b}|}{k}\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

(304)

which requires

\displaystyle P_{w}-P_{b}\equiv 0\bmod k\ .

(305)

From this counting, it is clear that each diagram contributes

\displaystyle h^{\frac{(k-1)(P_{w}+P_{b})-k}{k-2}}g^{\frac{P_{w}+(k-1)P_{b}-k}{k(k-2)}}\bar{g}^{\frac{P_{b}+(k-1)P_{w}-k}{k(k-2)}}\,,

(306)

to the final partition function.
There are some further simplifications that we can take.

First, we can focus on tree amplitudes, since the effect of all the loop corrections is some extra renormalization factors.

Second, we do not need to consider all types of vertices; our theory is topological and all higher valence vertices can be resolved into 3-valent vertices connected by internal propagators. As a result, we only include 3-vertices (
298
).

It turns out that it is still complicated to discuss the most general case with these interactions. Therefore in the rest we consider a perturbative treatment that includes at most one
g
and one
\bar{g}
insertion. The cases without any insertion have been discussed previously, in the following we only sum over diagrams with one pair of insertions.

Again, we first consider the CCP in the presence of one pair of interactions, we get

\displaystyle c^{(1)}_{n}={n\choose 3}\frac{(n-1)!}{2}=\frac{n!}{6}{n-1\choose 2}\ .

(307)

Notice that comparing to the free piece,

\displaystyle c^{(0)}_{n}=(n-1)!\,,

(308)

we find
c^{(1)}/c^{(0)}=\frac{1}{2}{n\choose 3}
.
Then the SCP can be obtained as

\displaystyle s^{(1)}_{n}=\sum_{\{n_{1},\ldots n_{j}\}\in P(n)}\frac{n!}{\prod_{i}(n_{i}!)\prod(m_{n}!)}\left(\prod_{i^{\prime}}\left(c^{(0)}_{n_{i}}+|g_{3}|^{2}c^{(1)}_{n_{i}}\right)\right)h^{j}\,,

(309)

where the product over
i^{\prime}
means only one of the
c^{(1)}
in the series should be kept. Explicitly, this means in terms of
|g|^{2}
we have the following expansion

\displaystyle s_{n}=s^{(0)}_{n}+|g_{3}|^{2}s^{(3)}_{n}\,,

(310)

where

\displaystyle s^{(0)}_{0}=1\,,

\displaystyle\quad s^{(0)}_{n}(h)=h(h+1)\ldots(h+n-1)

(311)

\displaystyle s^{(3)}_{i=0,1,2}=0\,,

\displaystyle\quad s^{(3)}_{n}(h)={n\choose 3}h(h+3)\ldots(h+n-1)\,,\quad n>2

(312)

We can then compute their generating function

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}s_{m}(h)\frac{v^{m}}{m!}=\left(1-v\right)^{-h}+|g_{3}|^{2}\frac{h}{6}\left(\frac{v}{1-v}\right)^{3}(1-v)^{-h}\ .

(313)

Since this is a perturbative expansion, to the leading order we can express the above result in a more compact way

\displaystyle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}s_{m}(h)\frac{v^{m}}{m!}=\left(1-\frac{v}{1+\delta}\right)^{-h}\,,

(314)

where

\displaystyle\delta=-\frac{|g_{3}|^{2}v^{2}}{6(v-1)^{2}}\ll 1\ .

(315)

This means the probability distribution changes from
\Gamma(h,1)
to
\Gamma(h,1-\frac{|g_{3}|^{2}v^{2}}{6(v-1)^{2}})
. This is as we expected since the inclusion of the bulk interaction should alter the boundary theory as well as their probability interpretation.

The spectral partition function is now

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(u\hat{Z}_{1,1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=e^{\lambda\left({(1-\frac{u}{1+\delta})^{-h}}-1\right)}=e^{\lambda\left(\left(1-v\right)^{-h}+|g_{3}|^{2}\frac{h}{6}\left(\frac{v}{1-v}\right)^{3}(1-v)^{-h}-1\right)}\ .

(316)

More concretely, we can expand the above MGF of the compound distribution into

\displaystyle\left\langle\exp\left(u\hat{Z}_{1,1}\right)\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}P(d,\lambda)E_{x}\left(e^{u(x_{1}+\ldots+x_{d})}\right)

(317)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(E_{x}\left(e^{ux}\right)^{d}\right)

(318)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\left(1-\frac{u}{1+\delta}\right)^{-h}\right)^{d}

(319)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\left(\left(1-u\right)^{-h}+|g_{3}|^{2}\frac{h}{6}\left(\frac{u}{1-u}\right)^{3}(1-u)^{-h}\right)^{d}

(320)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^{d}}{d!}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}(dh)_{k}\left(1+\Theta(k-2.5)\frac{|g_{3}|^{2}\binom{k}{3}}{(dh+1)(dh+2)}\right)\frac{u^{k}}{k!}\,,

(321)

where
(a)_{n}
is the Pochhammer symbol.
This means

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\hat{Z}_{1,1}\right)^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}P(d,\lambda)\left(dh\right)_{k}\left(1+\Theta(k-2.5)\frac{|g_{3}|^{2}\binom{k}{3}}{(dh+1)(dh+2)}\right)

(322)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}P(d,\lambda)\left[\left({dh}\right)\left({dh+1}\right)\ldots\left({dh+k-1}\right)+\right.

(323)

\displaystyle\quad+\left.\Theta(k-2.5)|g_{3}|^{2}\binom{k}{3}\left({dh}\right)\left({dh+3}\right)\ldots\left({dh+k-1}\right)\right]\ .

(324)

In particular, for
k>2
the result can be written as

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\hat{Z}_{1,1}\right)^{k}\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}P(d,\lambda)\left({dh}\right)\left({dh+3}\right)\ldots\left({dh+k-1}\right)\left[\left({dh+1}\right)\left({dh+2}\right)+|g_{3}|^{2}\binom{k}{3}\right]\,,

(325)

Since this is only a perturbative result, we can reorganize the terms into a more suggestive manner

\displaystyle\left\langle\left(\hat{Z}_{1,1}\right)^{k}\right\rangle

(326)

\displaystyle=\sum_{d=0}^{\infty}P(d,\lambda)\left({dh}\right)\left({dh+3}\right)\ldots\left({dh+k-1}\right)\left({dh+1+\frac{|g_{3}|^{2}}{2}\binom{k}{3}}\right)\left({dh+2}-\frac{|g_{3}|^{2}}{2}\binom{k}{3}\right)\,,

(327)

which, to the leading order of
|g|_{3}^{2}
, is the same as the previous result but it is written in terms of
k
factors.

On the other hand, we know there is another presentation of the result following the general discussions in section
3.2
where on each
\alpha
-state the result becomes the
k^{\text{th}}
power of the eigenvalue. It is interesting to further understand the relation between the two presentations.

6
The operator approach in the CGS model

The theory discussed above involves surfaces with arbitrary genera. One can also study similar problems in the CGS model
Coleman:1988cy
;
Giddings:1988cx
that only picks up the contributions from the disk and cylinder contributions. Besides the apparent simplicity of the surfaces, another property that becomes quite handy is that there is a powerful operator approach of the theory which helps streamline many of the computations. In the following, we first discuss an attempt to understand the problems we discussed above in terms of operators in the surface gravity theory, and then introduce the CGS model with its operator presentation.

6.1
Factorization and normal ordering in the surface model

In the surface model
Marolf:2020xie
, the correlation function does not factorize

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}^{n}\rangle=B_{n}(\lambda),

(328)

in particular

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}^{2}\rangle=\lambda^{2}+\lambda\neq\langle\hat{Z}\rangle^{2}=\lambda^{2}\ .

(329)

From the bulk point of view, the non-factorization is due to the inclusion of wormhole contributions in the correlators. From the boundary point of view, it is because that the boundary theory is the average of an ensemble of theories.

We can also understand the non-factorization of the surface theory from a point of view of operators.
As suggested in
Marolf:2020xie
, one can think of that
\hat{Z}
acts as a number operator on the Hilbert space of a harmonic oscillator such that

\displaystyle\hat{Z}=N=a^{\dagger}a;

(330)

\displaystyle a\left|Z=0\right\rangle=0,\quad|Z=d\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{d!}}\left(a^{\dagger}\right)^{d}\left|Z=0\right\rangle\ .

(331)

The no-boundary state then can be identified with the coherent state

\displaystyle|{\rm NB}\rangle=e^{\alpha a^{\dagger}-\alpha^{*}a}|Z=0\rangle\ .

(332)

In this description, the non-factorization of
\hat{Z}^{n}
can be attributed to the fact that
Z^{n}
is not normal-ordered. Suppose alternatively if we define
:\hat{Z}^{n}:
as

\displaystyle\langle{\rm NB}|:\hat{Z}^{n}:|{\rm NB}\rangle=\langle{\rm NB}|a^{\dagger}\dots a^{\dagger}a\dots a|{\rm NB}\rangle

(333)

then the factorization is manifest

\displaystyle\langle{\rm NB}|:\hat{Z}^{n}:|{\rm{\rm NB}}\rangle=\langle{\rm NB}|:\hat{Z}:|{\rm NB}\rangle^{n}\ .

(334)

However, the correlation functions of the normal ordered operator are not directly computed by a Euclidean path integral. In addition, there is not a universal way to define “normal-ordering” in a classical gravity theory.

It is interesting to further explore the normal-ordering effect of the boundary creation operators. For example, an interesting question is if there is a way to compute quantities like
\langle{\rm NB}|:\hat{Z}^{n}:|{\rm NB}\rangle
directly from bulk gravity path integral. In the following, we discuss a similar question in the CGS model introduced in
Coleman:1988cy
;
Giddings:1988wv
and recently re-analyzed in
Saad:2021rcu
;
Saad:2021uzi
. We will defer more detailed discussions somewhere else.

6.2
Lightening review of the CGS model

Recall the moments
\langle\hat{Z}^{n}\rangle
in the surface theory in
Marolf:2020xie
is given by the Bell polynomial, whose generating function can be written as

\displaystyle\langle e^{u\hat{Z}}\rangle=e^{\lambda\left(e^{u}-1\right)}=e^{\lambda\left(u+\frac{1}{2}u^{2}+\ldots\right)}\ .

(335)

If we keep only the first two terms in the above sum and neglect all the other higher-order terms, then
\langle\hat{Z}^{n}\rangle
becomes approximately like the moments of a Gaussian distribution with mean value and variance being
\lambda
. Physically, this means we only include the spacetime with disk and cylinder topology in the theory. The model in this limit is precisely the CGS model.

In the CGS model,
\hat{Z}
has a continuous spectrum and it has a natural representation as (shifted) position operator in a harmonic oscillator Hilbert space

\displaystyle\hat{Z}-\text{Disk}=a+a^{\dagger}\equiv W,\quad[a,a^{\dagger}]=\text{Cyl},\quad|0\rangle\equiv|{\rm NB}\rangle,

(336)

\displaystyle a|{\rm NB}\rangle=0,\quad a^{\dagger}|{\rm NB}\rangle=|1\rangle\ .

(337)

The basis
\{n\},n=0,1,2,\dots
is orthogonal such that

\displaystyle\langle n|m\rangle=n!\delta_{nm}\,,

(338)

and state
|n\rangle
are interpreted as the “
n
-universe” states. Then the
|Z^{k}\rangle=\hat{Z}^{k}|{\rm NB}\rangle
states can be expended in terms of the
n
-universe states up to
n=k
. For example the state
\hat{Z}^{2}|{\rm NB}\rangle
is given by

\displaystyle\hat{Z}^{2}|{\rm NB}\rangle=(\text{Disk}^{2}+\text{Cyl})|{\rm NB}\rangle+2\text{Disk}|1\rangle+|2\rangle\ .

(339)

Let us consider the
n
-point function in an arbitrary state
|\psi\rangle=\psi(\hat{Z})|{\rm NB}\rangle

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}^{n}\rangle_{\psi}=\langle\psi|\hat{Z}^{n}|\psi\rangle=\langle{\rm NB}|\psi(\hat{Z})^{\star}\hat{Z}^{n}\psi(\hat{Z})|{\rm NB}\rangle=\langle{\rm NB}|\hat{Z}^{n}|\psi(\hat{Z})|^{2}|{\rm NB}\rangle\equiv\langle Z^{n}|\psi^{2}\rangle\,,

(340)

where it is written as an overlap between the
|Z^{n}\rangle
state and the unnormalized auxiliary state
|\psi^{2}\rangle
. The
n
-point function in general does not factorize into
\langle\hat{Z}\rangle^{n}_{\psi}
, but it does factorize in the so-called
\alpha
-states which are the eigenvectors of the operator
\hat{Z}
i.e.

\displaystyle\hat{Z}|\psi_{z}\rangle=z|\psi_{z}\rangle\,,\qquad\langle\hat{Z}^{n}\rangle_{\psi_{z}}=z^{n}.

(341)

Therefore the factorization equation of this correlation function

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}^{2}\rangle_{\psi}=\langle\hat{Z}\rangle_{\psi}^{2}

(342)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}^{2}\rangle_{\psi}=(\text{Disk}^{2}+\text{Cyl})\langle{\rm NB}|\psi^{2}_{z}\rangle+2\text{Disk}\langle 1|\psi^{2}_{z}\rangle+\langle 2|\psi^{2}_{z}\rangle

(343)

\displaystyle\langle\hat{Z}\rangle_{\psi}^{2}=\text{Disk}^{2}\langle{\rm NB}|\psi^{2}_{z}\rangle^{2}+\langle 1|\psi_{z}^{2}\rangle^{2}+2\text{Disk}\langle{\rm NB}|\psi_{z}\rangle\langle 1|\psi_{z}^{2}\rangle

(344)

leads to the identity dubbed as the “exclusion rule”
Saad:2021rcu

\displaystyle\text{Cyl}+\langle 2|\psi_{z}^{2}\rangle=\langle 1|\psi_{z}^{2}\rangle^{2}.

(345)

One can also introduce different species of boundaries
Z_{I}
,
i=1,\ldots,\mathcal{N}
into the CGS model. In the presence of multiple species, the above exclusion rule can be generalized to

\displaystyle\langle\{IJ\}|\psi^{2}_{\{z_{I}\}}\rangle+\delta_{IJ}=\langle I|\psi^{2}_{\{z_{I}\}}\rangle\langle J|\psi^{2}_{\{z_{I}\}}\rangle\,,

(346)

which is enough to guarantee the factorization of the two point function

\displaystyle\langle(\hat{Z}_{I}-\text{Disk}_{I})(\hat{Z}_{J}-\text{Disk}_{J})\rangle_{\{z_{I}\}}=\langle(\hat{Z}_{I}-\text{Disk}_{I})\rangle_{\{z_{I}\}}\langle(\hat{Z}_{J}-\text{Disk}_{J})\rangle_{\{z_{I}\}}\ .

(347)

6.3
Normal ordering in the CGS model

It is very suggestive to interpret
\langle 1|\psi^{2}_{z}\rangle=\langle\psi_{z}|\hat{Z}-\text{Disk}|\psi_{z}\rangle=z-\text{Disk}\equiv{w}
as a partition function of a theory without average and similarly
\langle{\rm NB}|\hat{Z}|{\rm NB}\rangle
is interpreted as the averaged partition function

\displaystyle\langle{\rm NB}|\hat{W}|{\rm NB}\rangle

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dz\langle{\rm NB}|\psi_{z}\rangle\langle\psi_{z}|\hat{Z}-\text{Disk}|\psi_{z}\rangle\langle\psi_{z}|{\rm NB}\rangle

(348)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int dz\,P^{{\rm NB}}(z)\langle\hat{W}(z)\rangle_{\psi_{z}}=\int dz\,P^{{\rm NB}}(z){w}(z)=\mathbb{E}[w]_{{\rm NB}}\ .

(349)

Defining the “normal-ordered” quantity

\displaystyle:{w^{2}}:

6.4
Matching (half-)wormholes in the CGS model and the SYK model

From the boundary point of view, the bulk wormhole saddle point is due to an average over an ensemble of boundary theories. A natural question is then what, if exists, is the bulk dual description of the individual theory in the ensemble. In

6.5
A vanishing limit of the CGS model

The CGS model is defined on surfaces. However, due to the relatively simple topologies allowed in the model, it is straightforward to define a 1D theory that consists of lines with no intersection or bifurcation. They can be thought of as the limit of the CGS model where all the circular boundaries, and possibly the disks attached ending on them, shrink to points and the cylinders shrink to lines. We can thus also consider this model as a “vanishing” limit of the CGS model.

We find all results of the CGS model carry through in this limit. Even in cases where the boundary circles are identified with some thermal cycles, we can still consider their infinite temperature limit and deduce the results for the 1D model.

Notice that this limit is not well-defined in the more general surface theory defined in
Marolf:2020xie
; the genera on those surfaces will all shrink to loops in this 1D theory, but the shrinking results are not unique even for the simplest genus 1 case. Therefore only for the CGS, namely the surfaces are all disks or cylinders so that there are no nontrivial cycles on the surface that are not homologous to the boundary of the surface, this shrinking process is well defined. That is why we consider this limit only for this CGS model.

It turns out that we can use this 1D theory as the lines and by introducing large numbers of the lines and knitting them together (so that the lines do not bifurcate and this theory remains free), leads to a discrete version of the 2D theory.

This provides an alternative, but speculative, interpretation of the computation in the previous section; the
\hat{Z}
boundary could be considered as creating a 1D boundary that is a wormhole with vanishing size while the
\widetilde{W}
creates the usual boundary.
