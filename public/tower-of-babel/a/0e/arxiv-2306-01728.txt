The diameter of randomly twisted hypercubes
Lucas Aragão and Maurício Collares and Gabriel Dahia and João Pedro Marciano

Abstract
. The n n -dimensional random twisted hypercube 𝐆 n \mathbf{G}_{n} is constructed recursively by taking two instances of 𝐆 n − 1 \mathbf{G}_{n-1} , with any joint distribution, and adding a random perfect matching between their vertex sets. Benjamini et al. 2022 showed that its diameter is O ⁡ ( n ​ log ⁡ log ​ log ⁡ n / log ⁡ log ⁡ n ) O(n\log\log\log n/\log\log n) with high probability and at least ( n − 1 ) / log 2 ⁡ n {(n-1)/\log_{2}n} . We improve their upper bound by showing that diam ⁡ ( 𝐆 n ) = ( 1 + o ⁡ ( 1 ) ) ​ n log 2 ⁡ n \operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})=\big(1+o(1)\big)\frac{n}{\log_{2}n} with high probability.



1.
Introduction

The random twisted hypercube
\mathbf{G}_{n}
is a model for generating
n
-regular graphs with
2^{n}
vertices.

Benjamini et al. 2022
introduced it by generalising a related model studied by
Dudek et al. 2018
.
It is defined recursively in a similar way as the hypercube
Q_{n}
:
\mathbf{G}_{1}
is a single edge and

\mathbf{G}_{n}
is defined by adding a perfect matching
M_{n}
between two copies of

\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
. However, rather than matching vertices according to a (fixed) vertex ordering, as in
Q_{n}
, or even deterministically,
the matching
M_{n}
is chosen uniformly at random and independently in each step.
Moreover, instead of using two equal copies of
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
in the inductive construction,
we allow the pair to be sampled with an arbitrary joint distribution. Two concrete
examples are the
random duplicube
and the aforementioned
independent twisted hypercube
studied
by
Dudek et al. 2018
1

[
3
]
use the terminology “random twisted hypercube” for the special case where the two copies of
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
are independent; however, we shall instead follow the terminology of
[
1
]
.
. In the former, we sample a single graph from
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
and use two copies of it to construct
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, where, in the latter, the two copies of
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
are chosen independently.

One of the motivations for studying the random twisted hypercube is that it can be understood as a middle
ground between the structure of
Q_{n}
, the
n
-dimensional hypercube, and
H_{n}
, the random
n
-regular graph on
2^{n}
vertices.
To support this intuition,
Benjamini et al. 2022
studied several different properties of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, including its eigenvalue distribution, vertex expansion and diameter; here we will focus on the diameter of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
.
Recall that the diameter of
H_{n}
is
(1+o(1))\frac{n}{\log_{2}n}
with high probability
[
2
]
and that the diameter of
Q_{n}
is
n
.
The same simple greedy argument that proves the upper bound for
Q_{n}
works for
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, but it
was shown in
[
1
]
that this trivial bound is typically far from
the truth, and in fact
\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})
is sublinear in
n
with high probability.
The authors of
[
1
]
also observed that the deterministic lower bound on
\operatorname{diam}(H_{n})
also holds for
\mathbf{G}_{n}
.
To be precise, they showed that the bounds

\frac{n-1}{\log_{2}n}\leqslant\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})\leqslant C\frac{n\log\log\log n}{\log\log n}

hold with high probability for some constant
C>0
.
In the particular case of the random duplicube, they showed that
\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})=(1+o(1))n/\log_{2}n
with high probability, matching a result of
[
3
]
for the independent twisted hypercube.
Moreover, they remarked that they “have no intuition to the correct diameter” in the general case where the two samples of
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
used to construct
\mathbf{G}_{n}
may have arbitrary joint distribution.
In this note, we asymptotically determine the diameter of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mathbf{G}_{n}
be the random twisted hypercube, with arbitrary joint distribution. Then

\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})=(1+o(1))\frac{n}{\log_{2}n}

with high probability as
n\to\infty
.

We now give a brief sketch of the proof of
1.1
, starting with a more naive
approach, and then explaining how to improve it. In what follows, all logarithms are base

2
. Identify the vertices of
\mathbf{G}_{n}
with
\{0,1\}^{n}
so that every induced subgraph of

\mathbf{G}_{n}
that is given by a set of vertices with the same final
n-k
coordinates is
distributed as
\mathbf{G}_{k}
. Let
u,v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
be distinct, arbitrary vertices that we want to
connect using as few edges as we can and define
\alpha(u,v)
to be the
largest coordinate in which they differ. Note first that
d(u,v)\leqslant\alpha(u,v)
by the
following greedy approach:
u
has a (unique) neighbour
w
with

\alpha(w,v)\leqslant\alpha(u,v)-1
, so we may add the edge
uw
to our path and repeat with

u
replaced by
w
to finish in at most
\alpha(u,v)
steps, i.e. we reach
v
when

\alpha(w,v)=0
.

The key idea to prove
1.1
is to use a less greedy approach.
Let
M_{k}(u)
denote
the random matching between the distinct copies of
\mathbf{G}_{k-1}
with final coordinates
(u_{k},\ldots,u_{n})
and
(1-u_{k},u_{k+1},\ldots,u_{n})
, and let
t=t(n)
be a slowly growing function of
n
.
In each step, we shall choose an edge
u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
of
M_{k}(u)
for
k=\alpha(u,v)
such that
d(u,u^{\prime})\leqslant t
and
\alpha(v^{\prime},v)
is as small as possible.
If
\alpha(v^{\prime},v)\geqslant n/(\log n)^{2}
, we repeat the process with
v^{\prime}
in place of
u
.
Otherwise, since
d(v^{\prime},v)\leqslant\alpha(v^{\prime},v)
by the previous naive approach, we may finish the process by connecting
v^{\prime}
to
v
.
The number of steps taken, and therefore the length of the constructed path, will depend on how much
\alpha(u,v)
decreases when we replace
u
by
v^{\prime}
.
Since
u^{\prime}v^{\prime}
is chosen to be an edge of
M_{k}(u)
, the existence of a “good” choice of
v^{\prime}
depends on the size of the set
B
of vertices of
\mathbf{G}_{n}[\{w:\alpha(w,u)<k\}]
within distance
t
of
u
.

This idea motivates the following “quasirandomness” property: for every
k\geqslant n/(\log n)^{2}
and every pair of vertices
u,v
such that
\alpha(u,v)=k
,
there is a vertex
v^{\prime}\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
at distance at most
t+1
from
u
such that

\alpha(v^{\prime},v)\leqslant k-(t-2)\log n
. To show that it holds for
\mathbf{G}_{n}
, we will use a union bound over pairs of vertices. Fix
u
and
v
with
\alpha(u,v)=k
, and reveal the two samples of
\mathbf{G}_{k-1}
that contain
u
and
v
. We then use the randomness of the matching
M_{k}(u)
(and its independence of the choice of each
\mathbf{G}_{k-1}
) to bound the probability that such a vertex
v^{\prime}
does not exist. To prove this, we first show (see
2.2
) that, deterministically,

|B|\geqslant(k/t)^{t}
. Since the neighbours of
B
in
M_{k}(u)
form a random subset of the sample of
\mathbf{G}_{k-1}
that contains
v
, we can deduce (see
2.1
) that a suitable
v^{\prime}
exists with probability at least
1-\exp(-n^{3/2})
.

The above quasirandomness property is useful because it implies that the claimed upper bound on the
diameter holds
deterministically
. As at each step we grow our path by at most

t+1
and
\alpha(u,v)\leqslant n
, we take at most
n/((t-2)\log n)
steps to reach a
vertex that can be greedily connected to
v
using at most
n/(\log n)^{2}
edges. Since

t=\omega(1)
, the resulting path has length at most

(t+1)\frac{n}{(t-2)\log n}+\frac{n}{(\log n)^{2}}=(1+o(1))\frac{n}{\log n}.

The rest of the note is dedicated to
formalising this proof.

2.
The proof

The
n
-dimensional random twisted hypercube

\mathbf{G}_{n}
is defined recursively by placing a random matching between two instances of
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
.
Formally, we set
\mathbf{G}_{1}=K_{2}
and
M_{1}=E(K_{2})
, and for every
n\geqslant 2
we define
\mathbf{G}_{n}
in the
n
-th recursive step as follows.
We fix a distribution on pairs of graphs with both marginal distributions equal to
\mathbf{G}_{n-1}
; that is, a pair
(G_{n-1}^{(0)},G_{n-1}^{(1)})
sampled according to this distribution satisfies
\mathbb{P}(G_{n-1}^{(i)}=G)=\mathbb{P}(\mathbf{G}_{n-1}=G)
for every graph
G
and every
i\in\{0,1\}
.
We then sample
(G_{n-1}^{(0)},G_{n-1}^{(1)})
according to this distribution, and choose a perfect matching
M_{n}
between
V(G_{n-1}^{(0)})
and
V(G_{n-1}^{(1)})
uniformly at random and independently of all other choices made so far.
Then,
\mathbf{G}_{n}
will be the disjoint union of
G_{n-1}^{(0)}
and
G_{n-1}^{(1)}
with the perfect matching
M_{n}
in between, i.e.,

V(\mathbf{G}_{n})=V(G_{n-1}^{(0)})\cup V(G_{n-1}^{(1)})\qquad\text{ and }\qquad E(\mathbf{G}_{n})=E(G_{n-1}^{(0)})\cup E(G_{n-1}^{(1)})\cup M_{n}.

We stress that the choice of this perfect matching at each step is independent of all earlier choices.
Observe we can assume that
V(\mathbf{G}_{n})=\{0,1\}^{n}
and that for
i=0,1
the set of vertices with final coordinate equal to
i
is the vertex set of
G_{n-1}^{(i)}
.
For each
u\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
and
1\leqslant k<n
, the subgraph induced by the set of vertices with the same
n-k
final coordinates as
u
is an instance of
\mathbf{G}_{k}
.
Therefore,
\mathbf{G}_{n}
contains exactly
2^{n-k}
(correlated) samples of
\mathbf{G}_{k}
.

Recall that, for each
v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
, we write
M_{k}(v)
to denote the (last to be generated) random matching that appears inside the copy of
\mathbf{G}_{k}
that contains
v
. It will also
be useful to index each neighbour of
v
by the recursive step that introduced it,
so
N(v)=\big\{\eta_{1}(v),\ldots,\eta_{n}(v)\big\}
where
\eta_{k}(v)
is the neighbour of
v

given by the matching
M_{k}(v)
. Therefore, by construction,
\eta_{k}(v)
differs from
v

in the
k
-th coordinate and coincides with
v
in all coordinates greater than
k
. More
precisely, letting
u=\eta_{k}(v)
, we have
u_{k}\neq v_{k}
and
u_{i}=v_{i}
for every
k<i\leqslant n
.

For
{u,v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})}
, define
\alpha(u,v)
to be the largest coordinate in which
u
and
v
differ, i.e.,

\alpha(u,v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{c@{\quad\textup{if} \quad}l}\max\big\{j\in[n]:u_{j}\neq v_{j}\big\}&u\neq v\\[4.30554pt]
0&u=v.\end{array}\right.

Given
u,v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
, recall that in the greedy approach we can construct a path from
u
to
v
of length at most
\alpha(u,v)\leqslant n
.
Using the new notation to describe it, we start from
u
and iteratively replace
u
by
\eta_{\alpha(u,v)}(u)
, that is, we use the matching
M_{\alpha(u,v)}(u)
to “flip” the largest coordinate of
u
in which it differs from
v
.

The crux of the proof of
1.1
is the following lemma, which shows that the
less greedy approach we outlined in the introduction is indeed more efficient. It implies
that
\mathbf{G}_{n}
has the quasirandomness property defined in the introduction with high
probability.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
t\geqslant 3
and let
n,k\in\mathbb{N}
be sufficiently large integers such that
4t^{2t}\leqslant k\leqslant n
and
k\geqslant n/(\log n)^{2}
.
Let
E_{k,t}
denote the event that for every
u,v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
such that
\alpha(u,v)=k
, some vertex
w
of the graph
G^{\prime}=\mathbf{G}_{n}\big[\big\{w:w_{i}=u_{i},\forall i\geqslant k\big\}\big]
satisfies

d_{G^{\prime}}(u,w)\leqslant t\quad\text{ and }\quad\alpha(\eta_{k}(w),v)\leqslant k-(t-2)\log k.

Then
\mathbb{P}(E_{k,t}^{c})\leqslant\exp(-k^{3/2})
.

Define for a graph
G
the closed ball of
radius
t\in\mathbb{N}
centred at
v\in V(G)
to be
B_{G}(v,t)=\big\{w\in V(G):d_{G}(v,w)\leqslant t\big\}
.
To prove
2.1
we shall need the following deterministic lemma, which says that
closed balls in
\mathbf{G}_{n}
are always large.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
t,n\in\mathbb{N}
and
v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
.
Then, deterministically,

|B_{\mathbf{G}_{n}}(v,t)|\geqslant\binom{n+1}{t}.

Proof.

We will show that the function
f\colon\binom{[n]}{\leqslant t}\to B_{\mathbf{G}_{n}}(v,t)
given by

\big\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{s}\big\}\mapsto\big(\eta_{a_{s}}\circ\cdots\circ\eta_{a_{1}}\big)(v),

where
a_{1}>\cdots>a_{s}
, is injective.
From this, we will conclude the proof, since

|B_{\mathbf{G}_{n}}(v,t)|\geqslant\sum_{i=0}^{t}\binom{n}{i}\geqslant\binom{n}{t-1}+\binom{n}{t}=\binom{n+1}{t}.

Recall that, for every
u\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
,
u
and
\eta_{k}(u)
differ on the
k
-th coordinate and agree on every coordinate of greater index.
We will show that every sequence
n\geqslant a_{1}>\cdots>a_{s}
can be uniquely recovered from the vertex
w=\big(\eta_{a_{s}}\circ\cdots\circ\eta_{a_{1}}\big)(v)
.

To do so, first observe that
w
is such that
v_{a_{1}}\neq w_{a_{1}}
and
v_{i}=w_{i}
for all
i>a_{1}
.
Indeed, every other
a_{i}
with
i>1
is strictly smaller than
a_{1}
and, as observed before,
\eta_{a_{i}}
does not change coordinates strictly greater than
a_{i}
.
By definition of
\alpha
, we therefore have
a_{1}=\alpha(v,w)
.
Replacing
v
by
\eta_{a_{1}}(v)
, we may find
a_{2}
and so on.
The procedure stops when
v=w
, at which point we recover uniquely the sequence
(a_{1},\ldots,a_{s})
.
∎

We now turn our attention to
2.1
.
Observe that for any
k\leqslant n
we have

(1)

\big|\big\{v^{\prime}\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n}):\alpha(v^{\prime},v)\leqslant k\big\}\big|=2^{k}.

In the proof of
2.1
, we will use this fact, together with the bound in
2.2
, to show that
B_{G^{\prime}}(u,t)
intersects
\big\{w\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n}):\alpha(\eta_{k}(w),v)\leqslant k-(t-2)\log k\big\}
with probability at least
1-\exp(-2k^{3/2})
.

Proof of
2.1
.

Let
k,t\in\mathbb{N}
, and define
G^{*}(z)=\mathbf{G}_{n}\big[\big\{w:w_{i}=z_{i},\forall i\geqslant k\big\}\big]
for
z\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
. Fix a pair
u,v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
such that
\alpha(u,v)=k
, and let
G^{\prime}=G^{*}(u)
and
G^{\prime\prime}=G^{*}(v)
be the instances of
\mathbf{G}_{k-1}
in
\mathbf{G}_{n}
containing
u
and
v
, respectively, and consider

B^{\prime}=B_{G^{\prime}}(u,t)\quad\text{and}\quad B^{\prime\prime}=\big\{w\in V(G^{\prime\prime}):\alpha(w,v)\leqslant k-(t-2)\log k\big\}.

By
2.2
, applied to
G^{\prime}
, and (
1
), respectively, we have

|B^{\prime}|\geqslant\binom{k}{t}\geqslant\left(\frac{k}{t}\right)^{t}\qquad\text{and}\qquad|B^{\prime\prime}|\geq 2^{k-1-(t-2)\log k}.

We emphasise that both of the above equations hold deterministically, and that
so far we have only revealed
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
.

Let
A=A(u,v)
be the event that
\eta_{k}(B^{\prime})\cap B^{\prime\prime}=\emptyset
. Since the matching connecting
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
is chosen independently of
G^{\prime}
and
G^{\prime\prime}
, it follows that (conditioned on the pair
G^{\prime},G^{\prime\prime}
)
\eta_{k}(B^{\prime})
is a uniformly-chosen random subset of
V(G^{\prime\prime})
of size
|B^{\prime}|\geqslant(k/t)^{t}
.
We then obtain
2
2

2

If
2^{k-1}-|B^{\prime\prime}|<|B^{\prime}|
, then the product is zero, and the inequality holds trivially.

\mathbb{P}(A)=\prod_{i=0}^{|B^{\prime}|-1}\frac{2^{k-1}-|B^{\prime\prime}|-i}{2^{k-1}-i}\leqslant\big(1-k^{-(t-2)}\big)^{|B^{\prime}|}\leqslant\exp\left(-\frac{k^{2}}{t^{t}}\right),

and therefore
\mathbb{P}(A)\leqslant\exp(-2k^{3/2})
by the hypothesis
k\geqslant 4t^{2t}
.
Since
E_{k,t}^{c}
implies that
A(u,v)
holds for some
(u,v)\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})^{2}
with

, we deduce by the union bound that

(2)

\mathbb{P}(E_{k,t}^{c})\leqslant 2^{2n}\cdot\exp\big(-2k^{3/2}\big)\leqslant\exp\big(-k^{3/2}\big),

where the last inequality uses the hypothesis

.
∎

\mathbf{G}_{k}
inside
\mathbf{G}_{n}
is not an issue, consider the following alternative way of understanding the union bound in (

).
The event whose probability we bound is the existence of a sample of
\mathbf{G}_{k}
inside
\mathbf{G}_{n}
with the following property: there are two vertices

inside this specific sample of
\mathbf{G}_{k}
such that

.

Proof of
1.1
.

As observed in
[
1
]
, the lower bound follows from observing that, in an
n
-regular graph, there are at most
2n^{d}
vertices within distance
d
from any given vertex.

To prove the upper bound, let
n_{0}=n/(\log n)^{2}
and
t=\frac{\log n_{0}}{4\log\log n_{0}}
, which satisfies
n_{0}\geqslant 4t^{4t}
if
n
is large enough.
Moreover, let
E_{k,t}
be the events defined in
2.1
. By the union bound, the event
E:=\bigwedge_{k=n_{0}}^{n}E_{k,t}
holds with probability at least
1-\sum_{k=n_{0}}^{n}\exp(-k^{3/2})=1-o(1)
.

We now assume that
E
holds and show that
\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})=\big(1+o(1)\big)n/\log n
. To do so, we consider arbitrary
u,v\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
and construct a sequence of vertices
u^{(0)},\ldots,u^{(L)}\in V(\mathbf{G}_{n})
satisfying

(3)

u^{(0)}=0,\qquad d(u^{(i)},u^{(i+1)})\leqslant t+1\quad\text{and}\quad\alpha(u^{(i+1)},v)\leqslant\alpha(u^{(i)},v)-(t-2)\log n_{0}

for every
0\leqslant i<L
, as well as satisfying
\alpha(u^{(L)},v)<n_{0}
.

To construct the desired sequence, we start by setting
u^{(0)}=u
. Assuming inductively that we have constructed
u^{(0)},\ldots,u^{(i)}
satisfying the above properties, we do the following. If
\alpha(u^{(i)},v)<n_{0}
, we set
L=i
and finish the process. Otherwise, set
k:=\alpha(u^{(i)},v)
; by the assumption that
E_{k,t}
holds, there exists a vertex
w
such that

d(u^{(i)},w)\leqslant t\qquad\text{and}\qquad\alpha(\eta_{k}(w),v)\leqslant\alpha(u^{(i)},v)-(t-2)\log n_{0}.

We may then set
u^{(i+1)}:=\eta_{k}(w)
, which satisfies (
3
), and continue inductively.
At the end of the iteration, we have
d(u^{(L)},v)\leqslant\alpha(u^{(L)},v)<n_{0}
. Therefore, by the second property in (
3
),

(4)

d(u^{(0)},v)\leqslant d(u^{(L)},v)+\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}d(u^{(i)},u^{(i+1)})\leqslant n_{0}+(t+1)L.

Moreover, by the third property,

L\cdot(t-2)\log n_{0}\leqslant\sum_{i=0}^{L-1}\big(\alpha(u^{(i)},v)-\alpha(u^{(i+1)},v)\big)\leqslant n,

since the sum telescopes. Rearranging to obtain an upper bound on
L
, and plugging it into (
4
), we deduce that

(5)

d(u,v)=d(u^{(0)},v)\leqslant n_{0}+\frac{t+1}{t-2}\cdot\frac{n}{\log n_{0}}=\big(1+o(1)\big)\frac{n}{\log n},

where the last equality follows from
n_{0}=o(n/\log n)
,
\log n_{0}=(1+o(1))\log n
and
t=\omega(1)
. Since
u
and
v
were arbitrary, the result follows.
∎

Remark 1
.

In fact, the last step in (
5
) can be refined to obtain

\operatorname{diam}(\mathbf{G}_{n})=\frac{n}{\log n}+O\left(\frac{n\log\log n}{(\log n)^{2}}\right)

with high probability.

3.
