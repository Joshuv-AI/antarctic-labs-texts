Abstract
. If a multiplicative function f f is commutable with a quadratic form x 2 + x ​ y + y 2 x^{2}+xy+y^{2} , i.e., f ⁡ ( x 2 + x ​ y + y 2 ) = f ​ ( x ) 2 + f ⁡ ( x ) ​ f ​ ( y ) + f ​ ( y ) 2 , f(x^{2}+xy+y^{2})=f(x)^{2}+f(x)\,f(y)+f(y)^{2}, then f f is the identity function. In other hand, if f f is commutable with a quadratic form x 2 − x ​ y + y 2 x^{2}-xy+y^{2} , then f f is one of three kinds of functions: the identity function, the constant function, and an indicator function for ℕ ∖ p ​ ℕ \mathbb{N}\setminus p\mathbb{N} with a prime p p .

1.
Introduction

In 2014, Bašić classified arithmetic functions
f
satisfying

f(m^{2}+n^{2})=f(m)^{2}+f(n)^{2}

for all positive integers
m
and
n
. His result was a variation of Chung’s work
[
3
]
, which was inspired from Claudia Spiro’s study about
additive uniqueness sets

[
6
]
. It is naturally generalized to studying arithmetic functions
f
satisfying

f\big(Q(x_{1},x_{2},\dots,x_{k})\big)=Q\big(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}),\dots,f(x_{k})\big)

for various quadratic forms
Q
. After Bašić’s work for
Q(x,y)=x^{2}+y^{2}
, You et al.
[
7
]
and Khanh
[
4
]
studied about
Q(x,y)=x^{2}+ky^{2}
.

The author extended Bašić’s work to multiplicative functions commutable with sums of more than
2
squares. That is, if a multiplicative function
f
satisfy

f(x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+\dots+x_{k}^{2})=f(x_{1})^{2}+f(x_{2})^{2}+\dots+f(x_{k})^{2}

for
k\geq 3
, then
f
is uniquely determined to be the identity function
[
5
]
.

Let
Q(x,y)=ax^{2}+bxy+cy^{2}
be a positive definite binary quadratic form with
a,b,c\in\mathbb{Z}
. The value
b^{2}-4ac
is called
discriminant
of
Q
. The discriminant of the smallest absolute value is
-3
for
x^{2}\pm xy+y^{2}
. So, it is a natural question to ask which multiplicative function
f
satisfies the condition

f(x^{2}\pm xy+y^{2})=f(x)^{2}\pm f(x)\,f(y)+f(y)^{2}.

In this article, we classify such multiplicative functions.

2.
Results

Theorem 2.1
.

If a multiplicative function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies

f(x^{2}+xy+y^{2})=f(x)^{2}+f(x)\,f(y)+f(y)^{2},

then
f
is the identity function.

Proof.

We will show that
f(n)=n
for
1\leq n\leq 28
and use induction.

Note that
f(1)=1
and
f(3)=3
with
x=y=1
. Since
f
is multiplicative, the values of
f
at powers of primes determine
f
.

If
n
is not divisible by
3
, then
f(n^{2})=f(n)^{2}
from

\displaystyle f(3n^{2})

\displaystyle=f(3)\,f(n^{2})=3f(n^{2})

\displaystyle=f(n^{2}+n\cdot n+n^{2})=3f(n)^{2}.

Thus,
f(4)=f(2)^{2}
,
f(16)=f(4)^{2}
, and
f(25)=f(5)^{2}
.

Since

\displaystyle f(7)

\displaystyle=f(1^{2}+1\cdot 2+2^{2})=1+f(2)+f(2)^{2},

\displaystyle f(13)

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}+f(1)\,f(3)+f(3)^{2}=13,

\displaystyle f(21)

\displaystyle=f(3)\,f(7)=3f(7)

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}+f(1)\,f(4)+f(4)^{2}=1+f(2)^{2}+f(2)^{4},

\displaystyle f(39)

\displaystyle=f(3)\,f(13)=39

\displaystyle=f(2)^{2}+f(2)\,f(5)+f(5)^{2},

\displaystyle f(91)

\displaystyle=f(7)\,f(13)=13\,f(7)

\displaystyle=f(5)^{2}+f(5)\,f(6)+f(6)^{2}=f(5)^{2}+3f(2)f(5)+9f(2)^{2},

we can conclude that
f(n)=n
for
n=2,4,16,5,7,13

Since

\displaystyle f(84)

\displaystyle=f(4)\,f(3)\,f(7)=4\cdot 3\cdot 7=84

\displaystyle=f(2)^{2}+f(2)\,f(8)+f(8)^{2}=4+2f(8)+f(8)^{2},

\displaystyle f(43)

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}+f(1)\,f(6)+f(6)^{2}=43,

\displaystyle f(129)

\displaystyle=f(3)\,f(43)=3\cdot 43=129

\displaystyle=f(5)^{2}+f(5)\,f(8)+f(8)^{2}=25+5f(8)+f(8)^{2},

we can find
f(8)=8
.

Since
f(7)=7
and

\displaystyle f\!\left(3^{2}+3\cdot(2\cdot 3)+(2\cdot 3)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(3)^{2}+f(3)\,f(2\cdot 3)+f(2\cdot 3)^{2}=7f(3)^{2}

\displaystyle=f(7\cdot 3^{2})=f(7)\,f(9),

we obtain that
f(9)=9
.

The next prime is
11
. But we need to find
f(19)
to determine
f(11)
. Note that
f(19)=f(2)^{2}+f(2)\,f(3)+f(3)^{2}=19
. Now, since

\displaystyle f(133)

\displaystyle=f(7)\,f(19)=7\cdot 19=133

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}+f(1)\,f(11)+f(11)^{2}=1+f(11)+f(11)^{2},

\displaystyle f(247)

\displaystyle=f(13)\,f(19)=13\cdot 19=247

\displaystyle=f(7)^{2}+f(7)\,f(11)+f(11)^{2}=49+7f(11)+f(11)^{2},

we can find
f(11)=11
.

Note that

\displaystyle f(399)

\displaystyle=f(3)\,f(7)\,f(19)=3\cdot 7\cdot 19=399

\displaystyle=f(5)^{2}+f(5)\,f(17)+f(17)^{2}=25+5f(17)+f(17)^{2},

\displaystyle f(427)

\displaystyle=f(3)^{2}+f(3)\,f(19)+f(19)^{2}=427

\displaystyle=f(6)^{2}+f(6)\,f(17)+f(17)^{2}=36+6f(17)+f(17)^{2}.

Thus,
f(17)=17
.

We have
f(23)=23
from

\displaystyle f(553)

\displaystyle=f(7)\,f(79)=7\left(f(3)^{2}+f(3)\,f(7)+f(7)^{2}\right)=7\cdot 79=553

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}+f(1)\,f(23)+f(23)^{2}=1+f(23)+f(23)^{2},

\displaystyle f(579)

\displaystyle=f(3)\,f(193)=3\left(f(7)^{2}+f(7)\,f(9)+f(9)^{2}\right)=3\cdot 193=579

\displaystyle=f(2)^{2}+f(2)\,f(23)+f(23)^{2}=4+2f(23)+f(23)^{2}.

Note that

\displaystyle f(27)

\displaystyle=f(3)^{2}+f(3)\,f(3)+f(3)^{2}=27.

From the above results, it appears that
f(n)=n
for
1\leq n\leq 28
.

Now, consider
f(n)
for
n\geq 29
. We divide two cases:
n=2k+1
and
n=2k
.

Note that

\displaystyle(2k+1)^{2}+(2k+1)(k-3)+(k-3)^{2}

\displaystyle=(2k-3)^{2}+(2k-3)(k+2)+(k+2)^{2}

when
k>3
. Thus, if we assume that
f(m)=m
for all
m<n=2k+1
, we can write a functional equation

\displaystyle f(2k+1)^{2}+f(2k+1)(k-3)+(k-3)^{2}

\displaystyle=(2k-3)^{2}+(2k-3)(k+2)+(k+2)^{2}

for
f(2k+1)
by induction hypothesis and we obtain

f(2k+1)=2k+1\qquad\text{ or }\qquad f(2k+1)=-3k+2.

In other hand, since

\displaystyle f\!\left((2k+1)^{2}+(2k+1)(k-10)+(k-10)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f\!\left((2k-11)^{2}+(2k-11)(k+5)+(k+5)^{2}\right)

when
k>10
, we obtain

f(2k+1)=2k+1\qquad\text{ or }\qquad f(2k+1)=-3k+9.

Therefore, the solution satisfying both equalities simultaneously is that
f(n)=f(2k+1)=2k+1
.

Similarly, from

\displaystyle(2k)^{2}+(2k)(k-7)+(k-7)^{2}

\displaystyle=(2k-8)^{2}+(2k-8)(k+3)+(k+3)^{2}

with
k>7
we obtain that

f(2k)=2k\qquad\text{ or }\qquad f(2k)=-3k+7

if we assume that
f(m)=m
for
m<n=2k
. Also, from

\displaystyle(2k)^{2}+(2k)(k-14)+(k-14)^{2}

\displaystyle=(2k-16)^{2}+(2k-16)(k+6)+(k+6)^{2}

with
k>14
we obtain that

f(2k)=2k\qquad\text{ or }\qquad f(2k)=-3k+14.

Therefore, we conclude that
f(n)=f(2k)=2k
.
∎

Theorem 2.2
.

A multiplicative function
f:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{C}
satisfies

f(x^{2}-xy+y^{2})=f(x)^{2}-f(x)\,f(y)+f(y)^{2}

if and only if
f
is one of the following:

(1)

the identity function
f(n)=n

(2)

the constant function
f(n)=1

(3)

function
f_{p}
defined by

f_{p}(n)=\begin{cases}0&p\mid n\\
1&p\nmid n\end{cases}

for some prime
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
.

Proof.

It is trivial that the identity function and the constant function satisfy the functional equation. Let us consider the third case
f_{p}
.

It is known that
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
if and only if
p
cannot be represented as
x^{2}-xy+y^{2}

[
2
]
. Assume that
n=x^{2}-xy+y^{2}
. Then
n=(x+\omega y)(x+\overline{\omega}y)
with
\omega=(-1+\sqrt{-3})/2
is the factorization in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
a PID. If
n
is divisible by
p
, both
x
and
y
are divisible by
p
, since
p
is an inert prime in
\mathbb{Z}[\omega]
. Thus, the function
f_{p}
works well.

Now let us prove “only if” part. Note that

f(n^{2})=f(n^{2}-n\cdot n+n^{2})=f(n)^{2}-f(n)\,f(n)+f(n)^{2}=f(n)^{2}.

We have that
f(1)=1
. From the equalities

\displaystyle f(3)

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}-f(1)\,f(2)+f(2)^{2}=1-f(2)+f(2)^{2}

\displaystyle f(7)

\displaystyle=f(1)^{2}-f(1)\,f(3)+f(3)^{2}=1-f(3)+f(3)^{2}

\displaystyle=f(2)^{2}-f(2)\,f(3)+f(3)^{2}=f(2)^{2}-f(2)\,f(3)+f(3)^{2},

there are three cases:

\begin{array}[]{llllll}f(1)=1,&f(2)=0,&f(3)=1,&f(4)=0,&f(6)=0,&f(7)=1;\\
f(1)=1,&f(2)=1,&f(3)=1,&f(4)=1,&f(6)=1,&f(7)=1;\\
f(1)=1,&f(2)=2,&f(3)=3,&f(4)=4,&f(6)=6,&f(7)=7.\end{array}

Since

\displaystyle f(1-n+n^{2})

\displaystyle=f(1^{2}-1\cdot n+n^{2})

\displaystyle=1-f(n)+f(n)^{2}

and

\displaystyle f(1-n+n^{2})

\displaystyle=f\!\left((n-1)^{2}-(n-1)n+n^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(n-1)^{2}-f(n-1)\,f(n)+f(n)^{2},

we have that

f(n-1)^{2}-f(n-1)\,f(n)=1-f(n)

or

\big(f(n-1)-f(n)+1\big)\big(f(n-1)-1\big)=0.

Thus, it yields a condition

f(n-1)=1\qquad\text{or}\qquad f(n)=f(n-1)+1.

So, if
f(2)=2
, then
f(3)=3
and thus
f(4)=4
, and so forth. We obtain the identity function
f(n)=n
when
f(2)=2
.

If
f(2)=0
, then we have
f(3)=f(5)=f(7)=1
and
f(4)=f(6)=0
. From

\displaystyle f\!\left(2^{2}-2\cdot(2k)+(2k)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(2)^{2}-f(2)\,f(2k)+f(2k)^{2}=f(2k)^{2}

\displaystyle=f(4-4k+4k^{2})=f(4)\,f(1-k+k^{2})=0

we deduce that
f(2k)=0
for
k\geq 1
. Since
f(2k+1)=1
by condition (
\ast
),
f(2)=0
yields a sequence alternating
1
and
0
. That is,
f=f_{2}
.

Now, the condition
f(1)=f(2)=f(3)=f(4)=f(6)=f(7)=1
remains. If
f(n)=a
for some
a\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{1,0,-1,-2,\dots\}
, then
f(m)\neq 1
for all
m>n
. But, since
1=f(2)=f(2^{2})=f(2^{4})=\dots=f(2^{2^{N}})
for sufficiently large
N
, it is a contradiction. So, we can deduce that
f(n)
can have only integers
\leq 1
.

Suppose that
s
is the smallest integer such that
f(s)=0
. If there exist no such
s
, then
f
is a constant function
f(n)=1
.

Since
f
is multiplicative and
f(n^{2})=f(n)^{2}
, we can say that
s=p^{2k-1}
with prime
p
and positive integer
k
. Note that

\displaystyle f\!\left((p^{2k})^{2}-p^{2k}p^{2k-1}+(p^{2k-1})^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(p^{2k})^{2}-f(p^{2k})\,f(p^{2k-1})+f(p^{2k-1})^{2}=f(p^{2k})^{2}

\displaystyle=f\!\left((p^{2k-1})^{2}(p^{2}-p+1)\right)=f(p^{2k-1})^{2}f(p^{2}-p+1)=0.

Thus,
f(p^{2k})=f(p^{k})^{2}=0
. By the minimality of
s=p^{2k-1}
, we can deduce that
k=1
. That is,
s
is the prime
p
itself.

Then, we obtain
f(p\ell)=0
for any positive integer
\ell
, since

\displaystyle f\!\left(p^{2}-p(p\ell)+(p\ell)^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=f(p)^{2}-f(p)\,f(p\ell)+f(p\ell)^{2}=f(p\ell)^{2}

\displaystyle=f\!\left(p^{2}(1-\ell+\ell^{2})\right)=f(p)^{2}f(1-\ell+\ell^{2})=0.

That is, we can conclude that

f(n)=f_{p}(n)=0\text{ when $n$ is a multiple of $p$}

and
f(p\ell+1)=1
by condition (
\ast
).

Now, let
n
be a positive integer with
p\nmid n
. Then, there exists an integer
m
such that
nm\equiv 1\pmod{p}
and
(n,m)=1
. Letting
nm=p\ell+1
, we obtain

1=f(p\ell+1)=f(nm)=f(n)\,f(m).

Since
f
can have only integers
\leq 1
, we can conclude that

f(n)=\pm 1\text{ if }p\nmid n.

Now let us characterize the prime
p
. If
p
can be represented as
x^{2}-xy+y^{2}
, then
0=f(p)=f(x)^{2}-f(x)\,f(y)+f(y)^{2}
. But, this never happen since
f(x)
and
f(y)
are
\pm 1
. Hence,
p\equiv 2\pmod{3}
.

If
f(n)=-1
for
n\leq p-2
, then
f(n+1)=0
with
n+1\leq p-1
by (
\ast
). But, this is impossible by (
2
). Thus, if
f(n)=-1
for
n\leq p-1
, then
n=p-1
. In this case,
f(d)=-1
for a proper divisor
d
of
p-1
unless
p
is a Fermat prime. Thus, it is a contradiction. If
p=2^{2^{r}}+1
, then
-1=f(p-1)=f(2^{2^{r}})=f(2^{2^{r-1}})^{2}
, which is impossible for
f(2^{2^{r-1}})=\pm 1
. So, we can conclude that

f(1)=f(2)=f(3)=\dots=f(p-1)=1\text{ and }f(p)=0.

Similarly, suppose that
f(n)=-1
for some
n
. If
p(\ell-1)+1\leq n\leq p\ell-2
with
\ell\geq 2
, then
f(n+1)=0
with
p(\ell-1)+2\leq n+1\leq p\ell-1
by (
2
). But, this is a contradiction by (
2
) since
n+1
is not a multiple of
p
. Hence, if
f(n)=-1
with
p(\ell-1)+1\leq n\leq p\ell-1
, then
n=p\ell-1
. Then, since
n-1
and
n
are relatively prime and
(n-1)n=(p\ell-2)(p\ell-1)\equiv 2\not\equiv-1\pmod{p}
, we can deduce a contradictary equality

\displaystyle f\big((n-1)n\big)

\displaystyle=f(n-1)f(n)=-1

\displaystyle=f\big((p\ell-2)(p\ell-1)\big)=1.

Therefore, we can conclude that
f(n)=f_{p}(n)=1
when
n
is not divisible by
p
.

∎
