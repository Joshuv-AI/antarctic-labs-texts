Hofer–Zehnder capacity of disc tangent bundles of projective spaces
Johanna Bimmermann

Abstract
We compute the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of disc tangent bundles of the complex and real projective spaces of any dimension. The disc bundle is taken with respect to the Fubini-Study resp. round metric, but we can obtain explicit bounds for any other metric. In the case of the complex projective space we also compute the Hofer–Zehnder capacity for the magnetically twisted case, where the twist is proportional to the Fubini-Study form. For arbitrary twists we can still give explicit upper bounds.



1
Introduction

The Hofer–Zehnder capacity
[
12
, Chpt. 3]
belongs to the class of symplectic capacities, i.e. numerical symplectic invariants measuring the symplectic size of a symplectic manifold. For a compact symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
possibly with boundary
\partial M
the Hofer–Zehnder capacity is defined as follows

c_{HZ}(M,\omega):=\sup\left\{\mathrm{max}(H)\ |\ H:M\to\mathbb{R}\ \ \text{smooth, admissible}\right\},

where admissible means:

•

0\leq H
and there exists an open set
U\subset M\setminus\partial M
such that
H|_{U}\equiv 0
,

•

there exists a compact set
K\subset M\setminus\partial M
such that
H|_{M\setminus K}\equiv\max(H)
,

•

all non-constant periodic solutions
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to M
of
\dot{\gamma}=X_{H}
have period
T>1
.

Here,
X_{H}
denotes the Hamiltonian vector field defined imposing the relation
\mathrm{d}H=-\iota_{X_{H}}\omega
. As all symplectic capacities, also the Hofer–Zehnder capacity is fairly hard to compute and unknown in many cases. A classic class of examples for symplectic manifolds are (co-) tangent bundles. Recall that the cotangent bundle of any smooth manifold
N
admits a canonical symplectic structure
\mathrm{d}\lambda
, where
\lambda\in\Omega^{1}(T^{*}N,\mathbb{R})
is pointwise defined as follows

\lambda_{(x,p)}:T_{(x,p)}TN\to\mathbb{R};\ \xi\mapsto p(\mathrm{d}\pi_{(x,p)}(\xi))\ \ \ \forall\ (x,p)\in T^{*}N,

where
\pi:TN\to N
denotes the footpoint projection.
Fixing a Riemannian metric
g
on
N
we can pull back
\lambda
to
TN
via the metric isomorphism. By abuse of notation this pullback will also be denoted by
\lambda
. We will now study the disc subbundles with respect to the already chosen metric
g
,

D_{\rho}N:=\{(x,v)\in TN\ |\ g_{x}(v,v)<\rho^{2}\}

and investigate the following question.

Question 1.1
.

What is the value of
c_{HZ}(D_{\rho}N,\mathrm{d}\lambda)
?

To the authors knowledge this question is still wildly open and most of the results in this direction concern finiteness. For example the Hofer–Zehnder capacity is finite, if the base manifold carries a circle action with non-contractible orbits
[
13
]
, if the Hurewicz map
\pi_{2}(N)\to H_{2}(N,\mathbb{Z})
is non zero
[
2
]
or if
N
is rationally inessential
[
10
]
. All these results rely on the observation by Irie
[
13
, Cor. 3.5]
that vanishing of symplectic homology implies finiteness of the Hofer–Zehnder capacity. Finiteness can also be shown by finding a Lagrangian embedding of
N
into a manifold with finite Hofer–Zehnder capacity, as the Lagrangian neighborhood theorem assures that then also a small disc bundle
(D_{\varepsilon}N,\mathrm{d}\lambda)\hookrightarrow(M,\omega)
embeds symplectically. This is also the easiest way to see that for the two examples we will treat
c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
and
c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
are finite. Indeed we have Lagrangian embeddings
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS}\ominus\omega_{FS})
and
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}\hookrightarrow(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS})
.

However explicit values of the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of disc tangent bundles are only given for certain flat tori
[
12
, Ch.4.4, Prop. 4]
and some convex subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{n}

[
3
]
, using that in these cases the tangent bundles can be trivialized and then identified with subsets of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

There is a variation of the Hofer–Zehnder capacity for which the analog of Question
1.1
is partially answered. For a general compact symplectic manifold
(M,\omega)
possibly with boundary
\partial M
consider a closed submanifold
\Sigma\subset M
not intersecting the boundary, i.e.
\Sigma\subset M\setminus\partial M
. Then the Hofer–Zehnder capacity relative to
\Sigma
is defined as

c_{HZ}(M,\Sigma,\omega):=\sup\left\{\mathrm{max}(H)\ |\ H:M\to\mathbb{R}\ \ \text{smooth, admissible and vanishing on }\Sigma\right\}.

For any homotopy class
\nu\in\pi_{1}(M)
we say
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
is
\nu
-admissible if
H
is smooth,
0\leq H
, vanishes on an open set
U\subset M\setminus\partial M
, constantly attains its maximum on the complement of a compact set
K\subset M\setminus\partial M
and all non-constant periodic solutions
\gamma:\mathbb{R}\to M
of
\dot{\gamma}=X_{H}
with
[\gamma]=\nu
have period
T>1
. One defines the
\pi_{1}
-sensitive Hofer–Zehnder capacity
c^{\nu}_{HZ}(M,\Sigma,\omega)
, replacing admissible with
\nu
-admissible. In general we have the following chain of inequalities

c_{HZ}(M,\Sigma,\omega)\leq c_{HZ}^{\nu}(M,\Sigma,\omega)\leq c_{HZ}^{\nu}(M,\omega)

and also

c_{HZ}(M,\Sigma,\omega)\leq c_{HZ}(M,\omega)\leq c_{HZ}^{\nu}(M,\omega),

simply because the class of admissible Hamiltonians is less and less constrained.
An important result by Weber
[
18
]
shows that for closed
N
and a non-zero class
\nu\in\pi_{1}(N)
, the Hofer-Zehnder capacity relative to the zero section is given by the length
l_{\nu}
of the shortest closed geodesic in the class
\nu\in\pi_{1}(M)
, i.e.

c_{HZ}^{\nu}(D_{1}N,N,\mathrm{d}\lambda)=l_{\nu}.

(1)

For non-aspherical, homogeneous spaces and positive curvature metrics on the 2-sphere the result was extended to the class of contractible loops (
\nu=0
) by Benedetti and Kang
[
4
, Cor. 2.8]
. Observe that restricting to the case
\rho=1
is fine as scaling the fibers yields a symplectomorphism identifying
(D_{\rho}N,\mathrm{d}\lambda)\cong(D_{1}N,\rho\cdot\mathrm{d}\lambda)
.

In this article we derive the precise value of the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of
(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
and
(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
with respect to the Fubini-Study metric respectively the round metric.

Theorem A
.

Equip
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
with the Fubini-Study metric and
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
with the round metric. Denote by
l
the length of the prime geodesics, then

c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)=l,

while

c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)=2l.

Observe that in the case of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
it coincides with the relative capacity, while in the case of
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
there is a factor of two. This factor of two is an example of the phenomenon of Lagrangian barriers first described by Biran
[
6
]
and recently demonstrated impressively by Brendel and Schlenk
[
7
]
.

Remark 1.2
.

We already made two choices here. First the metric and second the neighborhood of the zero section. Both are in some sense related. Any two metrics
g
and
h
are related by a symmetric positive definite bundle map
A:TN\to TN
such that for all
(x,v)\in TN
we have

h_{x}(v,v)=g_{x}(A_{x}v,v).

Further denote
\lambda_{g}
resp.
\lambda_{h}
the pullback of the canonical 1-form with respect to
g
resp.
h
. Then these two 1-forms can also be identified via the bundle map
A
, i.e.
A^{*}\lambda_{g}=\lambda_{h}
. In particular the unit disc subbundle
D_{1}N
is identified with some ellipsoid subbundle

E_{A}N:=\{(x,v)\in TN\ |\ g_{x}(v,A_{x}^{-1}v)\leq 1\}.

Therefore
E_{A}N\subset D_{a}N
where
a:N\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
is the maximal eigenvalue of
A_{x}^{-1}
. In particular if
N
is compact we can obtain the following upper bound for the Hofer–Zehnder capacity

c_{HZ}(D_{1}N,\mathrm{d}\lambda_{h})\leq\max_{x\in N}a(x)\cdot c_{HZ}(D_{1}N,\mathrm{d}\lambda_{g}).

We will also look at the magnetically twisted tangent bundle
(D_{\rho}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})
for arbitrary constants
\rho>0,s\in\mathbb{R}
. We again have a scaling property, in this case

(D_{\rho}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})\cong\left(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\rho\left(\mathrm{d}\lambda-\frac{s}{\rho}\pi^{*}\omega_{FS}\right)\right).

It is therefore enough to restrict to the case
\rho=1
and
s\in\mathbb{R}
arbitrary.

Theorem B
.

Denote
\omega_{FS}
the Fubini-Study form and
l
the length of the prime geodesics, then

c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})=l\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+1}-|s|\right).

Remark 1.3
.

We can also say something about the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of non-constant magnetic systems on
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
. Let
\nu\in\Omega^{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})
be an arbitrary closed 2-form. As
H_{dR}^{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})
is generated by
[\omega_{FS}]
we know that
[\nu]=[s\omega_{FS}]
in
H_{dR}^{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})
for some
s\in\mathbb{R}
. Thus we find an exact form
\mathrm{d}\theta
such that
\nu+\mathrm{d}\theta=s\omega_{FS}
. But this means
(T^{*}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-\pi^{*}\nu)
is symplectomorphic to
(T^{*}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})

via the map that shifts the zero-section

(x,p)\mapsto(x,p+\theta(x)).

Clearly this map does not map disc bundles to disc bundles but we can still get some inclusions depending on
\theta\in\Omega^{1}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})
. Denote

\theta_{\max}=\max_{x}|\theta(x)|,

then

(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-\pi^{*}\nu)\hookrightarrow(D_{1+\theta_{\max}}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS}).

There is a freedom of choosing the primitive
\theta
that we can use to optimize the bound. Set

\rho=1+\inf_{\theta}\max_{x}|\theta(x)|,

where the infimum is taken over all 1-forms
\theta\in\Omega^{1}(M)
satisfying
\nu+\mathrm{d}\theta=s\omega_{FS}
. Then

c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{\nu})\leq l\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+\rho^{2}}-|s|\right).

The proofs of Theorem
A
and Theorem
B
evolve in two steps: finding a lower bound and finding an upper bound that coincides with the lower bound. For the lower we will explicitly construct an admissible Hamiltonian using geodesic billiards and the magnetic geodesic flow respectively. For the upper bound we will need more abstract theory, the theory of pseudo-holomorphic curves. There are not many cases where this theory becomes explicit, but we will construct the following symplectomorphisms, realizing compactifications of the disc bundles, that will put us in the perfect set up to study (pseudo-)holomorphic curves. In this sense the following two theorems are maybe the key contribution of this paper.

Theorem C
.

Denote
\omega_{FS}\in\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})
the Fubini-Study form normalized such that the generator of
H_{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathbb{Z})
has area
4\pi
. Then there is a symplectomorphism, which is equivariant with respect to the Hamiltonian
\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
actions,

F:(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})\to(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\setminus\bar{\Delta},R_{1}\omega_{FS}\ominus R_{2}\omega_{FS}),

where
\bar{\Delta}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
denotes the anti-diagonal divisor

\bar{\Delta}=\{(p,q)\in\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\ |\ \mathrm{dist}(p,q)\ \text{maximal}\}

and

R_{1}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+1}+s\right),\ \ R_{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+1}-s\right).

Observe that the case
s=0
is included in the Theorem and recovers the untwisted case. For
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
we have a similar theorem.

Theorem D
.

Denote
\omega_{FS}\in\Omega_{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})
the Fubini-Study form normalized such that the generator of
H_{2}(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathbb{Z})
has area
4\pi
.
Then there is an
SO(n+1)
-equivariant symplectomorphism

F:(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)\to(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\setminus Q^{n-1},\omega_{FS}),

where
Q^{n-1}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
denotes the quadric

Q^{n-1}:=\{[z_{0}:\ldots:z_{n}]\ |\ z_{0}^{2}+\ldots+z_{n}^{2}=0\}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}.

Note that the 2-dimensional version of this theorem was already proven by Adaloglou in
[
1
]
, but using a different approach.

Outline.
In section
2
we construct the symplectic compactfications of
(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
and
(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
, i.e. we prove Theorem
C
in the case
s=0
and Theorem
D
. Section
3
contains a recap on pseudoholomorphic and uses them to obtain an upper bound on the Hofer–Zehnder capacity. Section
4
is devoted to constructing admissible Hamiltonians to get a lower bound. We will see that upper and lower bound agree and conclude that Theorem
A
holds. The last section (section
5
) contains the magnetic version, i.e. the proofs of Theorem
B
and Theorem
D
.

2
Compactifications

Let
N
denote either
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
. We equip
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
with the Fubini-Study metric and
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
with the round metric, both denoted by
g
. Recall that the kinetic Hamiltonian

E(x,v):=\frac{1}{2}g_{x}(v,v)\left(=\frac{1}{2}|v|_{x}^{2}\right)

generates geodesic flow and since we chose Zoll metrics it is totally periodic of period
l/|v|
, where
l
denotes the length of the prime geodesics. It follows that the Hamiltonian

H:=l\sqrt{2E}:TN\setminus 0_{TN}\to\mathbb{R};\ (x,v)\mapsto l|v|_{x}

generates a free circle action. One can now perform a Lerman cut (see
[
16
]
) at
H=l
to obtain symplectic compactifications

\overline{(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)}\ \text{resp.}\ \overline{(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)}.

These compactifications are per construction closed symplectic, but their symplectic geometry is not very clear from the Lerman cut construction. This section is concerned with identifying these ’abstract’ compactifications with the well studied symplectic manifolds

\left(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\frac{1}{2}(\omega_{FS}\ominus\omega_{FS})\right)\ \text{resp.}\ \left(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS}\right),

which puts us in the perfect set up to study (pseudo-) holomorphic spheres. To find the identifying symplectomorphism, we will make use of the fact that the action of the isometry groups
\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
resp.
\mathrm{SO}(n+1)
of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
resp.
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
lift to Hamiltonian actions on the tangent bundle. We will see that indeed also the spaces
\left(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},(\omega_{FS}\ominus\omega_{FS})\right)\ \text{resp.}\ \left(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS}\right)
admit Hamiltonian
\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
- resp.
\mathrm{SO}(n+1)
-actions. The map we construct will be an equivariant smooth bijection and we will use the following lemma to deduce it is a symplectomorphism.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
G
be a Lie group and
(M_{i},\omega_{i})
,
i=1,2
symplectic manifolds. Assume we have a Hamiltonian G-action on
(M_{i},\omega_{i})
with moment maps
\mu_{i}:M_{i}\to\mathfrak{g}^{*}
for
i=1,2
. If
\varphi:M_{1}\to M_{2}
is an equivariant smooth bijection such that

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{1}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces M_{2}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mathfrak{g}^{*}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{1}}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\mu_{2}}

commutes and the distribution
\mathcal{D}\subset TM_{1}
tangent to the
G
-orbits admits a complement
\Upsilon
that is isotropic for both symplectic forms
\omega_{1}
and
\varphi^{*}\omega_{2}
, then
\varphi
is actually a symplectomorphism i.e.
\varphi^{*}\omega_{2}=\omega_{1}
.

Proof.

We need to show that

\varphi^{*}\omega_{2}(v,w)=\omega_{1}(v,w)\ \ \forall v,w\in TM_{1}.

By non-degeneracy of
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
it will then follow, that also
\mathrm{d}\varphi
must be non-degenerate hence a diffeomorphism.
We can decompose the tangent bundle
TM_{1}=\mathcal{D}\oplus\Upsilon
. The distribution tangent to the
G
-orbits is given by
\mathcal{D}_{x}=\{(a_{1}^{\#})_{x}:=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}e^{ta}(x)\ |\ a\in\mathfrak{g}\}
. In particular
\mathrm{d}\varphi a_{1}^{\#}=a_{2}^{\#}
by equivariance, where the subscript indicates the manifold the vector field induced by
a\in\mathfrak{g}
lives on. So if either
v=a_{1}^{\#}\in\mathcal{D}
or
w=a_{1}^{\#}\in\mathcal{D}
we get the equality as follows

\omega_{1}(a^{\#}_{1},\cdot)=\mathrm{d}(\mu_{1},a)=\mathrm{d}(\mu_{2}\circ\varphi,a)=\omega_{2}(a^{\#}_{2},\mathrm{d}\varphi\ \cdot)=\omega_{2}(\mathrm{d}\varphi\ a_{1}^{\#},\mathrm{d}\varphi\ \cdot)=\varphi^{*}\omega_{2}(a^{\#}_{1},\cdot).

The second equality holds due to the moment map triangle and the fourth due to equivariance of
\varphi
. If
v,w\in\Upsilon
we get

\omega_{1}(v,w)=0=\omega_{2}(\mathrm{d}\varphi(v),\mathrm{d}\varphi(w))

as
\Upsilon
is isotropic with respect to both symplectic forms by assumption.
∎

2.1
Complex projective space

In order to give an accurate description of the Hamiltonian group actions, including formulas for the moment maps, we will consider
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
as (co-)adjoint orbit in
\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
and
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}\subset\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
as a suborbit. Observe that for compact Lie groups such as
\mathrm{SU}(n)
we can use the Killing form to identify
\mathfrak{g}^{*}\cong\mathfrak{g}
, which we will do without further notice from now on. We follow
[
9
]
for the presentation of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
as adjoint orbit. The complex projective space can be identified with the homogeneous space

\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}={\mathchoice{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathrm{SU}(n+1)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\displaystyle{\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))}$}}{\raisebox{3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathrm{SU}(n+1)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-3.75pt}{$\textstyle{\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))}$}}{\raisebox{2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathrm{SU}(n+1)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-2.625pt}{$\scriptstyle{\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))}$}}{\raisebox{1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{SU}(n+1)}$}\mkern-5.0mu\diagup\mkern-4.0mu\raisebox{-1.875pt}{$\scriptscriptstyle{\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))}$}}},

where
\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))
denotes the matrices in
\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1)
of determinant one. Fix

Z:=\frac{1}{n+1}\begin{pmatrix}-i_{n}&\quad\\
\quad&ni\end{pmatrix}\in\mathfrak{su}(n+1).

The stabilizer of
Z
under the adjoint action is precisely
\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))
, thus
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
can be realized as the adjoint orbit
O_{Z}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
.
The stabilizer group
\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1))
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{U}(n)
via the map

\mathrm{U}(n)\to\mathrm{S}(\mathrm{U}(n)\times\mathrm{U}(1));\ A\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}A&\quad\\
\quad&\det A^{-1}\end{pmatrix}.

On Lie algebra level the inclusion looks like

\mathfrak{u}(n)\hookrightarrow\mathfrak{su}(n+1);\ B\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}B&\quad\\
\quad&-\mathrm{tr}B\end{pmatrix}.

Thus the Lie-algebra splits as

\mathfrak{su}(n+1)=\mathfrak{p}\oplus\mathfrak{u}(n),

where

\mathfrak{p}=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0_{n}&-\bar{z}\\
z^{t}&0\end{pmatrix}\Big|\ z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}\right\}

is the subspace corresponding to the tangent space of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
at
Z
. Observe that for any

v=\begin{pmatrix}0_{n}&-\bar{z}\\
z^{t}&0\end{pmatrix}\in T_{Z}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}=\mathfrak{p}

we have that

j_{Z}v:=[Z,v]=\frac{1}{n+1}\left(\begin{pmatrix}-i_{n}&\quad\\
\quad&ni\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0_{n}&-\bar{z}\\
z^{t}&0\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}0_{n}&-\bar{z}\\
z^{t}&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}-i_{n}&\quad\\
\quad&ni\end{pmatrix}\right)=\begin{pmatrix}0_{n}&-(\overline{iz})\\
(iz)^{t}&0\end{pmatrix}

recovers the standard complex structure on
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
. Further the negative of the Killing form

(X,Y):=-2\mathrm{tr}(X\cdot Y),\ \ \ \forall\ X,Y\in\mathfrak{su}(n+1)

yields an
\mathrm{Ad}
-invariant scalar product on
\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
. Restricted to
\mathfrak{p}
it is compatible with
j_{Z}
and equivariantly extended it yields the standard invariant Kähler structure of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
, i.e.
g(j\cdot,\cdot)=\omega_{FS}(\cdot,\cdot)
.

Lemma 2.2
.

The induced resp. diagonal group action of
\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
on
(T\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
resp.
(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS}\ominus\omega_{FS})
is Hamiltonian. The moment maps are given by

\mu_{1}:T\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathfrak{su}(n+1);\ (x,v)\mapsto[x,v]

resp.

\mu_{2}:\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathfrak{su}(n+1);\ (a,b)\mapsto a-b.

Proof.

For the first formula we compute for an arbitrary element
a\in\mathfrak{su}(n+1)

\mathrm{d}(([x,v],a))=\mathrm{d}((v,[x,a]))=\mathrm{d}(g(v,\mathrm{d}\pi(a^{\#})))=\mathrm{d}(\lambda(a^{\#}))=\iota_{a^{\#}}\mathrm{d}\lambda

as
\lambda
is invariant under the flow of
a^{\#}
and as a consequence
\mathcal{L}_{a^{\#}}\lambda=0
.

The formula for
\mu_{2}
follows immediately from the fact that the inclusion as adjoint orbit is a moment map for the group action on an adjoint orbit
[
15
, Ch. 1]
.
∎

We can now prove the following theorem using the Lemmas above.

Theorem 2.3
.

There is a symplectomorphism, which is equivariant with respect to the Hamiltonian
\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
actions,

2.2
Real projective space

Using the description of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
as adjoint orbit
O_{Z}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
explained before, we can identify
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
as an
\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\subset\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
sub orbit

\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}\cong\left\{\mathrm{Ad}_{g}(Z)|\ \ g\in\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\right\}\subset O_{Z}\cong\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}.

It is fixed under the involution

I:O_{Z}\to O_{Z};\ p\mapsto p^{T},

as can be seen by direct computation

(gZg^{-1})^{T}=gZ^{T}g^{T}=gZg^{-1}\ \ \forall g\in\mathrm{SO}(n+1).

The involution
I
is antiholomorphic, because for all
v\in\mathfrak{p}
we find

\mathrm{d}I_{Z}(j_{Z}(v))=[Z,v]^{T}=(Zv-vZ)^{T}=-(Zv^{T}-v^{T}Z)=-[Z,v^{T}]=-j_{Z}(\mathrm{d}I_{Z}(v)).

Lemma 2.6
.

The induced resp. subgroup action of
\mathrm{SO}(n+1)\subset\mathrm{SU}(n+1)
on
(T\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
resp.
(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS})
is Hamiltonian. The moment maps are given by

\mu_{1}:T\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathfrak{so}(n+1);\ (x,v)\mapsto[x,v]

resp.

\mu_{2}:\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathfrak{so}(n+1);\ a\mapsto\mathrm{Re}(a).

Proof.

The map
\mu_{1}
is the restriction of the moment map for
(T\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
.
We therefore only need to check that the map actually takes values in
\mathfrak{so}(n+1)
. From the description as suborbit it is only clear that
[x,v]\in\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
. The action of
\mathrm{SO(n+1)}
on
T\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
is transitive on the sphere subbundle, thus it is fine to check this at some point

x=\frac{1}{n+1}\begin{pmatrix}-i_{n-1}&\quad&\quad\\
\quad&-i&0\\
\quad&0&ni\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\ \ v=\frac{r}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0_{n-1}&\quad&\quad\\
\quad&0&-i\\
\quad&-i&0\end{pmatrix}\in T_{x}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}\cong[x,\mathfrak{so}(n+1)].

Observe that
x=Z
and
r=|v|
.
Indeed for this choice we see

\overline{[x,v]}=[\bar{x},\bar{v}]=[-x,-v]=[x,v]

and therefore
[x,v]\in\mathfrak{so}(n+1)
.

Next we look at the map
\mu_{2}
. Equivariance is clear as conjugation with a real matrix commutes with taking the real part. The rest of the proof is the following straight forward computation. Any tangent vector
v\in T_{p}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
is of the form
v=\xi^{\#}_{p}
for some
\xi\in\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
, thus for every
a\in\mathfrak{so}(n+1)
we find

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{Ad}_{e^{t\xi}}(p),\mathrm{Ad}_{e^{t\xi}}(a))

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{Ad}_{e^{t\xi}}(p),a)+(p,[\xi,a])

\displaystyle=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Big|_{t=0}(\mathrm{Re}(\mathrm{Ad}_{e^{t\xi}}(p)),a)+(p,[\xi,a])

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{Re},a)(\xi^{\#})+\omega_{FS}(\xi^{\#},a^{\#})

\displaystyle=\mathrm{d}(\mathrm{Re},a)(v)+\omega_{FS}(v,a^{\#}).

For the third equality we used that real and imaginary part are orthogonal with respect to the Killing form, i.e. for two real matrices
a,b
such that
a,ib\in\mathfrak{su}(n+1)
we know that

(a,ib)=-2\mathrm{tr}(a\cdot ib)=-2i\mathrm{tr}(a\cdot b)\in\mathbb{R}\ \ \Rightarrow\ \ (a,ib)=0.

For the fourth equality we used that

\omega_{FS}(\xi^{\#},a^{\#})=g(j\xi^{\#},a^{\#})=([p,\xi],a)=(p,[\xi,a]).

∎

Theorem 2.7
.

There is an
SO(n+1)
-equivariant symplectomorphism

2.7
,

F:D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n};\ \ (x,v)\mapsto\exp_{x}(-f(|v|)j_{x}v)=e^{f(|v|)v}xe^{-f(|v|)v},

for some smooth, even function

depending on

. This can be visualized nicely in dimension

Remark 2.9
.

A closed almost complex manifold
(W,J)
is called (compact) complexification of a real manifold
M
if there exists an antiholomorphic involution

I:W\to W;\ \ \ \mathrm{d}I\circ J=-J\circ\mathrm{d}I

such that
M
is isomorphic to the fixed point set of
I
. The symplectomorphisms constructed above realizes these complexifications of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
resp.
\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n}
. The antiholomorphic involution on
(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},j\ominus j)
is given by

I:\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n};\ \ (a,b)\mapsto(b,a).

The antiholomorphic involution on
(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{FS})
respresented as adjoint orbit is given by

I:\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n+1)\to\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\subset\mathfrak{su}(n+1);\ \ p\mapsto p^{T}.

Observe that in both cases the symplectomorphism is of the form

DM\to W,(x,v)\mapsto\exp_{x}^{W}(-Jc(v)v),

where in our cases

is a scalar depending only on the norm of
v
. It seems likely that for general Kähler manifolds it is be possible to construct a similar map, maybe promoting
c
to be operator valued and depending also on the direction of

and possibly replacing the disc subbundle by a different neighborhood of the zero section.

3
Upper bound

To find an upper bound of the Hofer–Zehnder capacity, we will use a theorem by H. Hofer and C. Viterbo
[
11
, Thm. 1.16]
that shows existence of periodic orbits in the presence of holomorphic spheres. Let
(M,\omega)
be a closed symplectic manifold and
J:TM\to TM
a
\omega
-compatible almost complex structure. Consider the moduli space
\mathcal{M}
of
J
-holomorphic spheres
u:S^{2}\to M
satisfying:

\displaystyle\begin{split}u(0)&\in\Sigma_{0},\\
u(\infty)&\in\Sigma_{\infty},\\
[u]&=A\\
\frac{1}{2}\omega(A)&=\int_{|z|\leq 1}u^{*}\omega,\end{split}

(3)

for two closed disjoint submanifolds
\Sigma_{0}
and
\Sigma_{\infty}
and some homotopy class
A\in\pi_{2}(M)
.
Observe that these conditions fix the reparametrization symmetry up to an
S^{1}
(rotation of the complex plane). Further Hofer–Viterbo assume
A
to be
minimal
, i.e.

\omega(A)=\inf\{\omega(B)\ |B\in[S^{2},M],\ \omega(B)>0\},

which ensures that bubbling cannot occur. We replace this condition by requiring
A
to be
\omega
-
indecomposable
, i.e.
A
can not be written as sum
\sum_{i}B_{i}
for free homotopy classes
B_{i}\in\pi_{2}(M)
with
\omega(B_{i})>0
. The reader my check that the proof of Theorem 1.16 in
[
11
]
goes through without any changes also for
\omega
-indecomposable classes.

If
J
is regular the moduli space
\mathcal{M}
is a compact oriented manifold without boundary and thus defines an oriented cobordism class. Since the
S^{1}
reparametrization symmetry of pseudoholomorphic curves is not fixed, the moduli space inherits a free
S^{1}
-action and one considers cobordisms of compact, oriented, free
S^{1}
-manifolds.

Theorem 3.1

(Hofer–Viterbo, Thm. 1.16
[
11
]
)
.

Let
(M,\omega)
be a closed symplectic manifold,
A\in\pi_{2}(M)

\omega
-indecomposable,
J
a smooth, regular,
\omega
-compatible almost complex structure and
\Sigma_{0},\Sigma_{\infty}
two disjoint nonempty closed submanifolds of
M
. Suppose
H:M\to\mathbb{R}
is a Hamiltonian such that

H|_{\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{0})}=\min(H)\ \ \text{and}\ \ H|_{\mathcal{U}(\Sigma_{\infty})}=\max(H),

then, if the free
S^{1}
-cobordism class
[\mathcal{M}]\neq 0
, the Hamiltonian system
\dot{x}=X_{H}(x)
possesses a non constant contractible
T
-periodic solution with

0<T(\max(H)-\min(H))<\omega(A).

In particular

c_{HZ}(M\setminus\Sigma_{\infty},\Sigma_{0},\omega)\leq\omega(A)

and if
\Sigma_{0}
is a one point set we can conclude

c_{HZ}(M\setminus\Sigma_{\infty},\omega)\leq\omega(A).

We can now use this theorem and the compactifications constructed in the previous section to determine an upper bound of the Hofer–Zehnder capacity of
(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
resp.
(D_{1}\mathbb{R}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda)
.

For the case

we pick
\Sigma_{0}=\{(p,q)\}
a one point set and
\Sigma_{\infty}
the anti-diagonal divisor
\bar{\Delta}
. We fix

to be the class of a degree one curve in the first factor. Further by

the split complex structure

is regular. Now every holomorphic sphere through

in class

is of degree 1 in the first and constant in the second factor. Further

restricted to the fiber over

is a copy of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n-1}
, in particular any

-holomorphic sphere through

intersects

. This means the moduli space of holomorphic curves

through

in class

can be identified with the space of complex lines through

. Recall that we left the

-reparametrization symmetry, therefore

and not
\mathcal{M}\cong\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n-1}
and the free circle action given by rotating the holomorphic spheres parameterizes the Hopf fibration.

4
Lower bounds

Finding a lower bound is in some sense more elementary and much more hands on. Indeed we will simply construct explicit admissible Hamiltonians. We observed already that the Hamiltonian
H(x,v)=l|v|
generates a Hamiltonian circle action away from the zero-section. Composing
H
with a smooth even function
f:[-l,l]\to\mathbb{R}
that satisfies
0\leq f^{\prime}<1
,
f\equiv 0
near
0
,
f\equiv l-\varepsilon
near
\pm l
for an arbitrary small
\varepsilon>0
, yields an admissible function

-manifold not cobordant to the empty set. It follows by Hofer–Viterbo’s Theorem that
\omega(A)
bounds the Hofer–Zehnder capacity from above. Observe that in this case it matters which factor we choose, as both yield different upper bounds. We obtain

c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})\leq 4\pi\min\{R_{1},R_{2}\}=2\pi\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+1}-|s|\right).

(8)

For the lower bound we need to work a little harder, as we can not use the same Hamiltonian as in the untwisted case. Indeed the kinetic Hamiltonian
E(x,v):=\frac{1}{2}g_{x}(v,v)
does not generate geodesic flow anymore but rather its magnetic version.

Lemma 5.4
.

The magnetic geodesic flow (i.e. the flow generated by
X_{E}
) is totally periodic.

Proof.

As shown in
[
5
, Lemma 6.1]
the vector field
X_{E}
is pointwise given by

(X_{E})_{(x,v)}=X_{(x,v)}+s(jv)^{\mathcal{V}},

where
X
denotes the vector field generating geodesic flow and
(\cdot)^{\mathcal{V}}
the vertical lift as introduced in the proof of Lemma
4.1
. Now recall that for any point
(x,v)\in T\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
outside the zero section there is a unique totally geodesic embedded copy of
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{1}
through
x
tangent to
v
. In particular the vector fields are
X+s(jv)^{\mathcal{V}}
are tangent to these embedded copies of
T\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{1}
and their flow will restrict to
T\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{1}
. It is therefore enough to study the one dimensional case. So let
\gamma(t)=(x(t),v(t))
be a magnetic geodesic, i.e.
\dot{\gamma}=X+s(jv)^{\mathcal{V}}
. Projecting to the vertical distribution we obtain

\dot{x}(t)=v(t)
. Now projecting to the horizontal distribution we
obtain

\nabla_{\dot{x}}v=sjv.

It follows that
\gamma(t)=(x(t),\dot{x}(t))
, where
x(t)
is a curve of constant geodesic curvature
\kappa_{g}=|\frac{s}{v}|
, i.e. it parametrizes a geodesic circle and is therefore closed.
∎

If
R
denotes the radius (with respect to the Riemannian metric
g
) of a geodesic circle we know using normal polar coordinates that its circumference
C
and the geodesic curvature
\kappa_{g}
are

\displaystyle C

\displaystyle=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{\kappa}}\sin(\sqrt{\kappa}R)=\frac{2\pi\sqrt{\kappa}^{-1}\tan(\sqrt{\kappa}R)}{\sqrt{1+(\tan(\sqrt{\kappa}R))^{2}}},

(9)

\displaystyle\kappa_{g}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{\kappa}}{\tan(\sqrt{\kappa}R)}.

(10)

Inserting
\kappa_{g}
into (
9
) yields

C=\frac{2\pi}{\kappa_{g}\sqrt{1+\kappa/\kappa_{g}^{2}}}=\frac{2\pi|v|}{\sqrt{s^{2}+\kappa|v|^{2}}}.

Now, we conclude that the period is given by

T=\frac{C}{|v|}=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt{s^{2}+\kappa|v|^{2}}}.

In particular the reparametrization

H:D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\to\mathbb{R};\ \ (x,v)\mapsto 2\pi\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+|v|^{2}}-|s|\right)

is the moment map of a circle action on
(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\mathrm{d}\lambda-s\pi^{*}\omega_{FS})
.
Arguing as in the untwisted case at the beginning of section
4
we can modify
H

changing its oscillation only by an arbitrary small
\varepsilon>0
to make
H
admissible. In particular we obtain the following lower bound

c_{HZ}(D_{1}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n})\geq\max H-\min H=2\pi\left(\sqrt{s^{2}+1}-|s|\right).

Comparing this to equation (
8
) we see that upper and lower bound agree, further we choose the normalization of the metric such that
l=2\pi
, therefore we just finished the proof of Theorem
B
.
∎

Remark 5.5
.

We can interpret the symplectomorphism of Theorem
5.1
as a Lerman cut with respect to
H
, i.e.

\overline{\left(D_{\rho}\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},\omega_{s}\right)}\cong\left(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n},R_{1}\omega_{FS}\ominus R_{2}\omega_{FS}\right).

Proof.

As
H
generates a circle action also
H\circ F^{-1}
must generate a Hamiltonian circle action on
(\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\setminus\bar{\Delta},R_{1}\omega_{FS}\ominus R_{2}\omega_{FS})
. Denote
(x,v):=F^{-1}(N,S)
. By construction
\mu_{1}(x,v)=\mu_{2}(N,S)
and therefore

\displaystyle|\mu_{1}(x,v)|^{2}=|\mu_{2}(N,S)|^{2}\

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ |v|^{2}+(R_{2}-R_{1})^{2}=R_{1}^{2}+R_{2}^{2}-2R_{1}R_{2}(N,S)

\displaystyle\Leftrightarrow\ |v|^{2}=2R_{1}R_{2}(1-(N,S)).

Thus

H(F^{-1}(N,S))=2\pi\left(|R_{1}N-R_{2}S|-|R_{2}-R_{1}|\right),

in particular
H\circ F^{-1}
extends smoothly to
\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}\times\mathbb{C}\mathrm{P}^{n}
and is critical on the anti-diagonal divisor
\bar{\Delta}
.
∎
