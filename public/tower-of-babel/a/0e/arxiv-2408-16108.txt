Improving Lagarias-Odlyzko Algorithm For Average-Case Subset Sum: Modular Arithmetic Approach
Antoine Joux, Karol Węgrzycki

Abstract
Lagarias and Odlyzko (J. ACM 1985) proposed a polynomial time algorithm for solving “ almost all ” instances of the Subset Sum problem with n n integers of size Ω ⁡ ( Γ LO ) \Omega(\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}) , where log 2 ⁡ ( Γ LO ) > n 2 ​ log 2 ⁡ ( γ ) \log_{2}(\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}})>n^{2}\log_{2}(\gamma) and γ \gamma is a parameter of the lattice basis reduction ( γ > 4 / 3 \gamma>\sqrt{4/3} for LLL). The algorithm of Lagarias and Odlyzko is a cornerstone result in cryptography. However, the theoretical guarantee on the density of feasible instances has remained unimproved for almost 40 years. In this paper, we propose an algorithm to solve “ almost all ” instances of Subset Sum with integers of size Ω ⁡ ( Γ LO ) \Omega(\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}) after a single call to the lattice reduction. Additionally, our argument allows us to solve the Subset Sum problem for multiple targets while the previous approach could only answer one target per call to lattice basis reduction. We introduce a modular arithmetic approach to the Subset Sum problem. The idea is to use the lattice reduction to solve a linear system modulo a suitably large prime. We show that density guarantees can be improved, by analysing the lengths of the LLL reduced basis vectors, of both the primal and the dual lattices simultaneously.

The Subset Sum problem, a cornerstone of computational complexity and

instances. Indeed, since the problem is NP-hard, it is very unlikely that we
could propose an efficient algorithm to solve all instances of the problem. The
natural next step is to characterize which instances of the problem can be
solved efficiently. On that front, there is a classical result of
Bellman
[
9

that proposes a polynomial time algorithm for Subset Sum

This inspired a long line of pseudopolynomial-time algorithms (see

On the other end of the spectrum, there is a result by Lagarias and

” instances of Subset Sum when the size of the numbers is

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geqslant 0}
be integers, selected uniformly at
random from
\{1,\ldots,\left\lceil\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}\right\rceil\}
, where
\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}=(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n^{2}}\cdot 2^{\Theta(n\log{n})}
.
With overwhelming probability, after a single call to a basis reduction algorithm with
parameter
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}
, we obtain a polynomial time tester, that for every given

T\in\mathbb{Z}
decides whether

\text{there exist }e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in\{0,1\}\text{ such that }\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\cdot a_{i}=T.

Our tester and its creation method are both deterministic.
Their success probability only depends on the random choice of the integers

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and is at least
1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
.

The improvement offered by
Theorem

1.1
is two-fold. First, in terms
of admissible range, our algorithm provides improvement from
\Omega(\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}})

down to
\Omega(\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}})
on numbers in the instance. Second, our algorithm is

oblivious
to the target. Namely, after a single call to the lattice basis
reduction, with input numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
we can deterministically decide
on an answer in polynomial time. In that sense, our argument provides a succinct
certificate for “almost all” instances of sparse Subset Sum. Precisely this
question was already considered by Furst and Kannan
[

used lattice basis reduction to bound the proof complexity of Subset Sum. They
strengthened the Lagarias and Odlyzko algorithm and showed that “almost all”

have a polynomial size proof.
Our argument again reduces the range down to
\{1,\ldots,\left\lceil\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}\right\rceil\}

simpler. In fact, the data-structure behind
Theorem

1.1

invokes a single matrix-vector multiplication.

1.1
Our Technique: Modular Arithmetic Approach

Now, we elaborate on the techniques that we introduce. In this note, we let

\llbracket a\rrbracket_{p}
be a unique integer in
(-p/2,p/2)
such
that
a\equiv\llbracket a\rrbracket_{p}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)
(see
Section

2
for notation).
Consider vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
,
{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
and an integer
T
such that

\displaystyle e_{1}\cdot a_{1}+e_{2}\cdot a_{2}+\ldots+e_{n}\cdot a_{n}

\displaystyle=T.

Obviously, for any integers
\mu,p\in\mathbb{Z}
the following also holds

\displaystyle e_{1}\cdot\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}+e_{2}\cdot\llbracket\mu a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}+\ldots+e_{n}\cdot\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}

\displaystyle\equiv\llbracket\mu T\rrbracket_{p}\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p).

Next, assume, that we have managed to find
\mu
and
p
, such that:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}|\llbracket\mu a_{i}\rrbracket_{p}|<p/2.

Then there are no overflows modulo
p
and actually, we can write

e_{1}\cdot\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}+e_{2}\cdot\llbracket\mu a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}+\ldots+e_{n}\cdot\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}=\llbracket\mu T\rrbracket_{p}.

We will show that if the density is small, then there are integers
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that for each of them
(a)
the
\ell_{1}
norm is bounded, i.e.,
\left\lVert\llbracket\mu_{i}{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}}\rrbracket_{p}\right\rVert_{1}<p/2
, and

(b)
the vectors
\llbracket\mu_{i}{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}}\rrbracket_{p}
are linearly independent. More formally we
will prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1.2

(Modular Arithmetic Approach)
.

Let
\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}\coloneqq\Theta((\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n^{2}}\cdot 2^{4n\log_{2}{n}})
and let
p=\Theta((\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{0.5n^{2}})
be a prime.
There exists a polynomial time algorithm, that
given integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
selected uniformly at random from

\{1,\ldots,\left\lceil\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}\right\rceil\}
outputs, with overwhelming probability, integers

\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that the following
n\times n
matrix

\mathcal{M}_{p}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\llbracket\mu_{1}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}&\llbracket\mu_{1}a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}&\cdots&\llbracket\mu_{1}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}\\
\llbracket\mu_{2}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}&\llbracket\mu_{2}a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}&\cdots&\llbracket\mu_{2}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
\llbracket\mu_{n}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}&\llbracket\mu_{n}a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}&\cdots&\llbracket\mu_{n}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix}

has full rank. Moreover, for every
1\leqslant i\leqslant n
it holds that:

\left\lVert(\llbracket\mu_{i}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu_{i}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})\right\rVert_{1}<\frac{p}{2}.

More precisely, the procedure succeeds with probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
, which only depends on the
random choice of integers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
.

Observe, that the algorithm for
Theorem

1.1
follows easily with the matrix

\mathcal{M}_{p}
in hand. Namely, consider
\mathcal{M}_{p}\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}^{\intercal}={\mathrm{\textbf{t}}}
, where
{\mathrm{\textbf{t}}}\coloneqq(\llbracket\mu_{1}T\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu_{n}T\rrbracket_{p})^{\intercal}
. First, because the

\ell_{1}
norm of each row of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is bounded by
p/2
, vector
e
is also
a solution to
\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}},{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}\rangle=T
. Second, by the fact that
\mathcal{M}_{p}
has
full-rank, such a vector
e
is
unique
. Hence, it is enough to
compute the inverse of
\mathcal{M}_{p}
(which is possible because
\mathcal{M}_{p}
has full-rank)
and verify that the vector
(\mathcal{M}_{p})^{-1}\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{t}}}
is in
\{0,1\}^{n}

(see
Section

4
for the full proof).

Now, we briefly sketch the proof of
Theorem

1.2
. Our algorithm is
inspired by Howgrave-Graham’s revisit
[
25
]
of
Coppersmith’s method for finding short roots of univariate modular
equations
[
16
]
. In a nutshell, we construct
a lattice (very similar to the original lattice of Lagarias and Odlyzko) for
Subset Sum modulo a prime number
p
. Analogously to the original approach of
Lagarias and Odlyzko we bound the length of vectors in this lattice. The caveat
is that we can also bound the lengths of the vectors in the
dual
lattice.
This crucially allows us to double the density of the admissible instances.

There are two main differences between our proposal and
Howgrave-Graham’s algorithm
[
25
]
(and the
original Coppersmith’s method
[
16
]
) Firstly,
the number of variables to consider in our case is much larger. Secondly, we
only need to construct linear polynomials in these variables rather than
considering large degree polynomials.

1.2
Extension to Block Basis Reduction

We would like to comment on the possible improvements of using stronger lattice
reduction.
Specifically, the only aspect of the Lagarias-Odlyzko algorithm
that relies on the LLL algorithm is the fact that it provides a

(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n}
-approximate solution to the Shortest Vector Problem with

\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}>\sqrt{4/3}
. Instead of the LLL algorithm, it is natural to apply
block-basis reduction (e.g.,
[
43
]
) with parameter
\Upsilon
arbitrarily
close to
1
. This way, one expects a better approximation
of SVP, consequently improving the range
\Gamma
. The obvious drawback of this
is the increased running time.

Our approach, in principle, should allow us to reduce the bitlengths of the Subset Sum instances that
can be solved regardless of the concrete algorithm behind the lattice reduction.
Unfortunately, adapting our approach to work with every basis lattice reduction
requires a separate analysis which is much more technical than for LLL.
Nevertheless, the takeaway message is as follows:

The Modular Arithmetic Approach allows us to solve
almost all instances of Subset Sum with integers
\Omega(\sqrt{\Gamma})
with
a single call to lattice reduction, whereas the previous approach guaranteed to
solve only instances with integers
\Omega(\Gamma)
while using the same lattice
reduction algorithm.

Since the LLL algorithm is the most popular we decided first to present our
algorithms using this algorithm. In
Section

5
, we demonstrate that our
approach works for the textbook block-reduction presented by Gama and
Nguyen
[
22
,
38
]
.

Theorem 1.3
.

Let
\Upsilon>1
be a parameter of lattice reduction
in
[
22
]
.
Let
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
be positive integers, selected uniformly at
random from range
\{1,\ldots,\left\lceil\sqrt{\Gamma}\right\rceil\}
, where
\Gamma=\Upsilon^{n^{2}}\cdot 2^{\Theta(n\log{n})}
.
With overwhelming probability, after a single call to a basis reduction algorithm
in
[
22
]
with parameter
\Upsilon
, we obtain a polynomial time tester, that for every given

T\in\mathbb{Z}
decides whether

\text{there exist }e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in\{0,1\}\text{ such that }\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}\cdot a_{i}=T,

Our tester and its creation method are both deterministic.
Their success probability only depends on the random choice of the integers

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
and is at least
1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
.

In comparison, the standard analysis of Lagarias-Odlyzko algorithm requires the
range of integers to be of order
\Gamma=\Upsilon^{n^{2}}\cdot 2^{\Theta(n\log{n})}
when the lattice reduction
with parameter
\Upsilon>1
is used.

2.1
Background on Lattices

A
lattice
in
n
is a discrete subgroup of

(\real^{n},+)
. This implies that a lattice is a non-empty set
L\subseteq\real^{n}
such that for all
{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}},{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}\in L
it holds that
{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}-{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}\in L
.
Any subgroup of
(\mathbb{Z}^{n},+)
is a lattice, and it is called an

integral lattice
.

Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}
be vectors in
n
. Let

\mathcal{L}({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m})
be the set of integral combinations of these
vectors, namely:

\mathcal{L}({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m})\coloneqq\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}\ell_{i}\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\text{ such that }\ell_{1},\ldots,\ell_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}\right\}.

Then,
\mathcal{L}({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m})
is a lattice, specifically the lattice spanned
by
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}.
Furthermore, when the vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}

are linearly independent (over ), we say that they form a basis
of
\mathcal{L}({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}).

In this case, every vector
{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}\in L
uniquely
decomposes as an integral combination of the basis vectors , i.e., for every
{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}\in L

there exists unique
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\alpha_{i}\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}

[
38
, Definition 5,
p. 26]
. It is well-known that every lattice admits
basis. The cardinality of any basis is an invariant of the lattice and
is called its
dimension
(or sometimes referred to as its rank). This
quantity, denoted by
\mathrm{dim}(L)
is equal to the
dimension of its linear span (denoted
\mathrm{span}(L)
) as a vector
space over
.

We say that a lattice
L\subseteq\real^{n}
has full rank when

n=\mathrm{dim}(L)
. Since any lattice of
n
admits some
basis, we assume w.l.o.g. that every lattice we consider is given in
the form
\mathcal{L}({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m})
for some
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}
’s in

n
where
m=\mathrm{dim}(L)
.

Thus,
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}\in\real^{n}
are linearly independent vectors. Their
Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization (GSO) is the orthogonal family

({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}^{\ast}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}^{\ast}_{m})
defined inductively as follows:

\displaystyle{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}={\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}={\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}-\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j}^{\ast}\;\text{ where
}\;\mu_{i,j}\coloneqq\frac{\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i},{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j}^{\ast}\rangle}{\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}}\;\text{
for all }\;1\leqslant j<i\leqslant m.

We can define the volume of the lattice
L
spanned by
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}
as

\mathrm{Vol}(L)=\prod_{i=1}^{m}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert,

and remark that it is a lattice invariant and does not depend on the
choice of basis.

Other classical invariants of a lattice are its successive minima. By
definition, the
i
th successive
minimum, denoted as
\lambda_{i}(L)
is the smallest real number for which there exist
i

linearly independent vectors in
L
each of length at most
\lambda_{i}(L)
. In
particular,
\lambda_{1}(L)
is the length of the shortest nonzero vector in
L
. Note
that
\lambda_{1}(L)\leqslant\lambda_{2}(L)\leqslant\ldots\leqslant\lambda_{m}(L)
.

2.2
Dual Lattice

For a lattice
L\subset\real^{n}
the
dual
lattice of
L
is defined as:

L^{\dagger}\coloneqq\{{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}\in\mathrm{span}(L)\text{ such that }\langle{{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}},{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}}\rangle\in\mathbb{Z}\text{ for all }{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}\in L\}.

When
L
is an
m
-dimensional lattice of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
L^{\dagger}
is
also an
m
-dimensional lattice of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
More precisely, if
L
is
spanned by the rows of the basis matrix
B
, then
L^{\dagger}
is spanned by the
rows of:

B^{\dagger}=\left(BB^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}B

Furthermore, it holds that
\mathrm{Vol}(L)\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(L^{\dagger})=1
. Finally, if we
reverse the order of the rows of
B^{\dagger}
, we obtain the so-called dual
basis
D^{\dagger}
of
B
. The GSO of dual basis is related to the GSO
of the primal basis by the following relations:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{d}}}_{m-i+1}^{\ast}\right\rVert=1\quad\mbox{for every}\ i\in[m].

In order to make explicit that the order of the (row) basis vectors is reversed, we
write:

D^{\dagger}=\textbf{Rev}\left(\left(BB^{\intercal}\right)^{-1}B\right).

2.3
The LLL algorithm and its property

Lenstra, Lenstra and Lovász
[
33
]
presented a polynomial time procedure
that, given an arbitrary basis of a lattice computes a so-called
reduced basis with better properties. Extensions of LLL can also
compute a reduced basis from an arbitrary generating family.

The main parameter of LLL is a real
\delta\in(0.25,1)
. A secondary parameter
\mu\in[0.5,1)
is also useful
when considering implementations of LLL with finite precision on
orthogonalised vectors. The initial LLL with exact rational arithmetic
[
33
]

uses
\mu=1/2
. Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m}\in\real^{n}
be basis of lattice
L
and

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}^{\ast}_{m}\in\real^{n}
be its Gram-Schmidt orthogonalized basis of

L
. We say that
({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{m})
is
(\delta,\mu)
-LLL
reduced
if and only if the following conditions are satisfied:

Size Reduction:

\displaystyle|\mu_{i,k}|\leqslant\mu

\displaystyle\text{ for every }1\leqslant k<i\leqslant m

Lovász Condition:

\displaystyle\delta\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}\leqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i+1}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}+\mu_{i+1,i}^{2}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{2}

\displaystyle\text{ for every }1\leqslant i<m

Recall that
\mu_{i,k}=\frac{\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i},{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\rangle}{\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast},{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\rangle}
. Both of these conditions
yield an
implied parameter

\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{\delta-\mu^{2}}}
, such that:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i+1}^{\ast}\right\rVert\text{ for
every }1\leqslant i<m

(1)

Equation

1
is the strongest for
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}=\sqrt{4/3}+\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon>0
. This is achieved by taking
\mu
sufficiently close to
1/2
and
\delta

sufficiently close to
1
. In the sequel, we focus on the parameter
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}

and express our results in terms of it. Note, that the same inequality holds
for the dual lattice.

The running time of LLL algorithm
[
33
]
is
\mathcal{O}(m^{5}n\cdot\text{polylog}(B))
, where
B
is the Euclidean length of the longest
basis)
[
33
,
38
]
(see
[
42
]
for
recent development).

3
State-of-the-art: Lagarias-Odlyzko Algorithm

Assume that
{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}}=(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})
is a vector in
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
where each
a_{i}
is chosen independently uniformly at
random from
\{1,\ldots,\Gamma\}
and
{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{n}
is
chosen independently at random. Set
T=\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}},{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}\rangle
. Then, clearly

e
is a solution to the equation:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}x_{i}=T\text{ such that }x_{i}\in\{0,1\}\text{ for every }i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Here, we recall the argument of Lagarias and Odlyzko who show that if
\Gamma\geqslant\Omega((\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n^{2}+o(n^{2})})
and
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}>\sqrt{4/3}
, then the LLL
algorithm is sufficient to solve an instance
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
with target
T
of
Subset Sum in polynomial time and with probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
.

Note that
T\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}
, and we can assume that

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}\right\rVert_{1}\leqslant\frac{n}{2}
as otherwise, we can
consider the same instance with target
\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}-T\right)
. Let
K\coloneqq\left\lceil n(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n}\right\rceil
be a sufficiently large integer. Lagarias
and Odlyzko
[
31
]
construct the following row-generated lattice:

L\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0&\cdots&0&a_{1}\cdot K\\
0&1&\cdots&0&a_{2}\cdot K\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1&a_{n}\cdot K\\
0&0&\cdots&0&T\cdot K\\
\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}=(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n},0)
such that
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{i}=T
.
Observe that
\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}\in L
and
\left\lVert\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}\right\rVert\leqslant\sqrt{n/2}
.
Therefore, the vector
\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}
is a
short vector
of
L
.

The idea of Lagarias and Odlyzko is to use the algorithm of Lenstra, Lenstra
and Lovász
[
33
]
. Let
v
be a minimum length non-zero vector in

L
. The LLL algorithm guarantees finding a nonzero vector
{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}\in L
with:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}\right\rVert.

Here,
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}
is an implied parameter of the LLL algorithm, and we can guarantee
that when
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}>\sqrt{4/3}
, the LLL algorithm runs in polynomial time
(see
Section

2
). Note that the vector
x
found by the LLL satisfies

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n}\left\lVert\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n}\sqrt{n/2}<K

The strategy is, hence, to run the LLL algorithm and hope that the vector
x
returned by
LLL is either
\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}
or
-\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}
. There is of course the possibility
that there are other spurious vectors of length less than
K
. The analysis by
Lagarias and Odlyzko demonstrates that this is, however, highly unlikely.
More precisely, they show the following statement:

Claim 3.1

(cf.,
[
19
]
)
.

\mathbb{Pr}\left[\text{exists }{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}\in L\setminus\{k\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}\mid k\in\mathbb{Z}\}\text{ such
that }\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{x}}}\right\rVert<K\right]\leqslant\frac{(2K+1)^{n+1}}{\Gamma}.

Note that this is
2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
when
\Gamma\geqslant\Omega((\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n^{2}+o(n^{2})})
. Therefore, by considering the opposite event, we can guarantee
that the vector
\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}
is found with a probability at least
1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
. We
include the proof of
Claim

3.1
due to
[
19
]
as it serves as an
introduction to our technique.

Proof of
Claim

3.1
.

Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n+1})\in L
. Observe that if
w_{n+1}\neq 0

then already
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}\right\rVert\geqslant K
. Hence, it must hold that
w_{n+1}=0
. Let

\mathcal{W}\coloneqq\{{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}\in L\text{ such that }\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}\right\rVert<K\text{ and }w_{n+1}=0\text{ and }{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}\neq k\cdot\bar{{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}}\text{ for any }k\in\mathbb{Z}\}.

Now, it suffices to bound the probability that
\mathcal{W}
is empty.
If, however,
\mathcal{W}
is nonempty, there exists
{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}=(w_{1},\ldots,w_{n},0)\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}

and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
that satisfy:

\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\cdot w_{i}=\ell\cdot T.

(2)

Let us now fix this vector
w
and integer
\ell
. Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{z}}}=(z_{1},\ldots,z_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
be such
that
z_{i}=w_{i}-e_{i}\cdot\ell
.
Observe that
\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}},{\mathrm{\textbf{z}}}\rangle=0
. Without loss of generality, we
can assume that
z_{1}\neq 0
and we let
Z\coloneqq-(\sum_{i=2}^{n}a_{i}z_{i}/z_{1})
. Hence,

\mathbb{Pr}\left[\langle{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}},{\mathrm{\textbf{z}}}\rangle=0\right]=\mathbb{Pr}\left[a_{1}=Z\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}\mathbb{Pr}\left[a_{1}=i\;|\;Z=i\right]\cdot\mathbb{Pr}\left[Z=i\right].

As
a_{1}
and
Z
are independent, this is bounded by

\sum_{i=1}^{\Gamma}\frac{1}{\Gamma}\cdot\mathbb{Pr}\left[Z=i\right]\leqslant\frac{1}{\Gamma}.

Therefore, a fixed
w
and
\ell
satisfy Equation (
2
)
with probability at most
1/\Gamma
. Note that the number of
{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
and
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{w}}}\right\rVert<K
,

w_{n+1}=0
and
|\ell|\leqslant 2K
is
at most
(2K+1)^{n+1}
and the proof concludes.
∎

Note, that the proof of
Claim

3.1
uses lattice reduction as a blackbox.
If one were to use lattice reduction with parameter
\Upsilon>1
, then the
range guaranteed by
Claim

3.1
would be
\Gamma=\Theta((\Upsilon)^{n^{2}+o(n^{2})})
. In the next sections, we will show a method to improve the admissible
range down to
\Gamma=\Theta((\Upsilon)^{0.5n^{2}+o(n^{2})})
that uses more properties of lattice
reduction. In
Section

4
we analyse it with the standard LLL. Then
in section
Section

5
we analyse it with a textbook block lattice
reduction of Gama and Nguyen
[
22
]
.

4
Modular arithmetic approach

In this section, we focus on the proof of the following statement.

See
1.2

Before we do so, let us show how to use
Theorem

1.2
to solve the Subset
Sum formally.

Proof of Theorem
1.1
assuming
Theorem

1.2
.

We start by describing the algorithm. First, we use
Theorem

1.2

to compute the integers
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n},p
. This gives us the matrix

\mathcal{M}_{p}
. We know that this matrix has full rank so we
can compute its inverse with the Gaussian elimination algorithm. This concludes the
description of the preprocessing phase.

Now, given a target
T
we compute the vector
{\mathrm{\textbf{t}}}_{p}\coloneqq(\llbracket\mu_{1}T\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu_{n}T\rrbracket_{p})^{\intercal}
and compute a candidate solution

{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}^{\intercal}=\mathcal{M}_{p}^{-1}\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{t}}}_{p}
. Finally, we check that
e
is indeed correct. Namely, if
\sum_{i=1}^{n}e_{i}a_{j}=T
and
{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
we return

e
and
\bot
otherwise. This concludes the description of the algorithm.
Clearly, the algorithm runs in polynomial time. Moreover, a query takes only

\mathcal{O}(n)
arithmetic operations (on numbers from the range
[R]
). The
success probability of the algorithm comes exclusively from a single
application of
Theorem

1.2
. Therefore, it remains to argue about
the correctness.

Let
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}\in\{0,1\}^{n}
be a solution to
\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}e_{i}=T
and assume that our algorithm returns
\bot
.
Because the norm
\left\lVert\llbracket\mu_{i}{\mathrm{\textbf{a}}}\rrbracket_{p}\right\rVert_{1}
is
bounded by
p/2
it holds that:

\begin{pmatrix}\llbracket\mu_{1}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}&\llbracket\mu_{1}a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}&\cdots&\llbracket\mu_{1}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}\\
\llbracket\mu_{2}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}&\llbracket\mu_{2}a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}&\cdots&\llbracket\mu_{2}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}\\
\vdots&&&\vdots\\
\llbracket\mu_{n}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p}&\llbracket\mu_{n}a_{2}\rrbracket_{p}&\cdots&\llbracket\mu_{n}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}e_{1}\\
e_{2}\\
\vdots\\
e_{n}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\llbracket\mu_{1}T\rrbracket_{p}\\
\llbracket\mu_{2}T\rrbracket_{p}\\
\vdots\\
\llbracket\mu_{n}T\rrbracket_{p}\\
\end{pmatrix}

Since the matrix
\mathcal{M}_{p}
has full rank, there exists a unique solution to the above linear system. Hence

(e_{1},\ldots,e_{n})^{\intercal}=\mathcal{M}_{p}^{-1}\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{t}}}_{p}
. Finally,
note that we only return this solution if all its coordinates are in

\{0,1\}^{n}
, thus ensuring an incorrect solution is never returned.
∎

From now on we will focus on the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.

4.1
Generating Family

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
be the prime fixed in
Theorem

1.2
. In
particular,
p
is odd. Because the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are generated at
random in the interval
\{1,\ldots,\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}\}
which has length much
larger than
p
, the modular reductions
a_{i}\pmod{p}
are very close to
uniform modulo
p
. For simplicity of the analysis we want to assume that they
are uniform. This can be achieved by using the rejection sampling to discard any event
when at least one
a_{i}
is greater than
\sigma_{p}
, where
\sigma_{p}
is the
largest multiple of
p
such that
\sigma_{p}<\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}
. This is valid,
because the probability that specific
a_{i}
is discarded is:

\mathbb{P}\left[a_{i}>\sigma_{p}\right]\leqslant\frac{|\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}-\sigma_{p}|}{\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}}}\leqslant 2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}.

Therefore, by the union bound, probability that we do not discard any of

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
is at least
1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
. Similarly, we can
assume that
a_{1}
is not a multiple of
p
. Hence, there exists an integer

a_{1}^{-1}
such that
a_{1}\cdot a_{1}^{-1}\equiv_{p}1
.

Consider the lattice generated by the rows of the following matrix:

\mathcal{L}=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}&a_{2}&a_{3}&\cdots&a_{n}\\
p&0&0&\cdots&0\\
0&p&0&&0\\
0&0&p&&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&p\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{(n+1)\times n}

(3)

Now, let us elaborate on the connection between matrix
\mathcal{L}
and any vector

(\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})
from the statement
of
Theorem

1.2
.

Observation 4.1
.

For every integer
\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
it holds that:

(\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})\in\mathcal{L}.

Conversely, for every vector
{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}\in\mathcal{L}\cap\{-\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor\}^{n}
, there exists
\mu^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
such
that:

{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}=(\llbracket\mu^{\prime}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu^{\prime}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}).

Moreover,
\mu^{\prime}
is unique modulo
p
and its exact value can be determined with a constant number of
arithmetic operations.

Proof.

For the first property observe that trivially
\mu\cdot(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})\in\mathcal{L}
. Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}_{i}
be the vector with
1
at the
i
th coordinate and
0

at the remaining coordinates. Note that by definition
(p\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}_{i})\in\mathcal{L}
for every
i\in[n]
. Then we construct the desired vector by

(\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})=\mu\cdot(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left\lfloor\frac{\mu a_{i}}{p}\right\rfloor\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}_{i}\in\mathcal{L}.

For the converse property, by definition any vector
{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}\in\mathcal{L}
is
represented as:

{\mathrm{\textbf{v}}}=k_{0}\cdot(a_{1},\ldots,a_{n})+\sum_{i=1}^{n}(k_{i}p)\cdot{\mathrm{\textbf{e}}}_{i}.

for some integers
k_{0},\ldots,k_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Let us inspect the
i
th
coordinate of
v
. For
every
i\in[n]
it holds that
k_{0}\cdot a_{i}+k_{i}\cdot p\in\{-\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor\}
. Therefore, for every
i\in[n]
, the
i
th coordinate of

v
is
\llbracket k_{0}a_{i}\rrbracket_{p}
. We hence can set
\mu^{\prime}\coloneqq k_{0}
. Note, that given vector
v
, the integer
\mu^{\prime}
can be computed
efficiently because it is expressed as
\mu^{\prime}=v_{1}\cdot a_{1}^{-1}
, where

v_{1}
is the first coordinate of
v
.
∎

Now, let us further elaborate on the subsequent steps. We run the LLL algorithm on
\mathcal{L}
.
The LLL algorithm returns a basis
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
of
\mathcal{L}
. We show that,
with high probability,

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\leqslant p/2
for every
i\in[n]
. If that occurs we are nearly finished.
By the
Observation

4.1
each of the basis vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}
is equal to
(\llbracket\mu_{i}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu_{i}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})
for some integer
\mu_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Moreover,
because the vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
form a basis of
\mathcal{L}
it means that the matrix
formed by the vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\dots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
has a full-rank.

Therefore, to complete the proof of
Theorem

1.2
it suffices to show
that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\leqslant p/2
for every
i\in[n]
. By Cauchy-Schwartz
inequality it is actually sufficient to prove that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\right\rVert_{2}\leqslant\frac{p}{2\sqrt{n}}
.

4.2
Basis of Generating Family and Dual Basis

Lattice
\mathcal{L}
is given to us by a generating family in form of a
rectangular matrix. It is much more convenient to work with a basis,
especially when given by a square matrice. Therefore, we start by
determining a basis of
\mathcal{L}
. As we have already noticed, we can
assume without loss of generality that
a_{1}
is invertible in

\mathbb{Z}_{p}
. For every
i\in{2,\ldots,n}
, let

\alpha_{i}=a_{i}a^{-1}_{1}
. The rows of the following matrix form a
basis of
\mathcal{L}
:

\mathcal{B}_{0}=\begin{pmatrix}1&\alpha_{2}&\alpha_{3}&\cdots&\alpha_{n}\\
0&p&0&&0\\
0&0&p&&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&0&\ldots&p\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n\times n}

In particular, this implies that the volume of
\mathcal{L}
is
p^{n-1}
.
We can determine the dual lattice of
\mathcal{L}
, which in the
full-dimensional case is spanned by the rows of the transpose of the inverse matrix.
In our case, this is:

\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\dagger}=(\mathcal{B}_{0}^{-1})^{T}=\frac{1}{p}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}p&0&0&\cdots&0\\
-\alpha_{2}&1&0&\cdots&0\\
-\alpha_{3}&0&1&&0\\
\vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
-\alpha_{n}&0&0&\ldots&1\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{Q}^{n\times n}

Observe that
p\mathcal{B}_{0}^{\dagger}
generates the set of integral vectors

(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such that

\sum_{i=2}^{n}x_{i}\cdot\alpha_{i}\equiv_{p}-x_{1}
. Or equivalently such
that:

\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}a_{i}\equiv_{p}0.

4.3
Every vector of the LLL-reduced basis is probably short

Now, let
\mathcal{B}
be an LLL-reduced basis of the
lattice
\mathcal{L}
. We denote its row vectors by

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
and their GSO by

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}
.
Recall that the dual basis, given by

\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}=\textbf{Rev}((\mathcal{B}^{-1})^{T})
.

In this section, we prove the following probabilistic
property on a reduced basis:

Lemma 4.2
.

With probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
, it holds that:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\quad\text{for every}\ k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.

Lemma

4.2
can be proved as a consequence of the following
inequalities, which hold with overwhelming probability when the integers

a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are drawn uniformly at random.

Claim 4.3
.

With probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
it holds that:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\leqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert.

Proof of
Lemma

4.2
assuming
Claim

4.3
.

Let us assume that both inequalities in
Claim

4.3
hold. If
k\geqslant\frac{n+1}{2}
then by repeated application of Inequality (
1
) we have that

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n-k}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}
, which is the desired result. Hence we need to focus on the case
where
k<\frac{n+1}{2}
.
Observe that by repeated application of Inequality (
1
) and
Claim

4.3
we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k-1}^{\ast}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}\frac{\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert}{(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{i-1}}=(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{2}}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{k-1}\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{2}}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{\frac{k-1}{n}}.

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{i=0}^{n-k}\frac{\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert}{(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{i}}=(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-\frac{(n-k)(n-k+1)}{2}}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}.

Recall that the volume of the lattice
\mathcal{L}
is
\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})=\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert
.
Hence by multiplying the above inequalities we have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{\frac{k-1}{n}}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}\cdot(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-\frac{(n-k)(n-k+1)}{2}-\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{2}}.

Because
k<\frac{n+1}{2}
we have that
(n-k)(n-k+1)+(k-1)(k-2)\leqslant(n-1)(n-k+1)
. Hence

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{\frac{k-1}{n}}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}\cdot(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-\frac{(n-1)(n-k+1)}{2}}.

By rearranging the terms we have

\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{\frac{n-k+1}{n}}\cdot(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{\frac{(n-1)(n-k+1)}{2}}\geqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}.

After taking
(n-k+1)
th root, this yields the desired inequality.
∎

It remains to prove inequalities in
Claim

4.3
. We split this proof
into
Proposition

4.4
and
Proposition

4.5
. First, we focus on the right
side of the inequality.

Proposition 4.4
.

\mathbb{Pr}\left[\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert<\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\right]<2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}.

Proof.

Recall that
\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})=p^{n-1}
and
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}={\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}
. Let us bound the probability of

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}\right\rVert<p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}
. Note that in this case,
Observation

4.1
asserts that there exists

\mu\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

\left\lVert(\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})\right\rVert<p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}.

Moreover, integer
\mu
is determined exactly as
\mu\coloneqq({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1})_{1}\cdot a_{1}^{-1}
. Hence, for a fixed
{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}\in\{-\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor\}^{n}
the
numbers
a_{2},\ldots,a_{n}
via
\llbracket\cdot\rrbracket_{p}
operations are determined. This
means that for a given value
a_{1}
, the conditional probability for a fixed
{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}\in\{-\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor\}^{n}
is:

\mathbb{Pr}\left[{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}=(\llbracket\mu a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu a_{n}\rrbracket_{p})\right]=\frac{1}{p^{n-1}}.

which does not depend on
a_{1}
. Thus the unconditional probability is

1/p^{n-1}
as well. On the other hand, the number of vectors
y
such
that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{y}}}\right\rVert<p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}
is
\leqslant\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}+\sqrt{n}))
. Therefore by the union bound we
conclude:

\mathbb{Pr}\left[\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}\right\rVert\leqslant p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\right]<\frac{\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{Ball}_{n}(p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}+\sqrt{n}))}{p^{n-1}}<2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}.\qed

For the other part of the inequality in
Claim

4.3
, we need to prove that:

Proposition 4.5
.

\mathbb{Pr}\left[\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert>\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\right]<2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}.

Proof.

Again recall that
\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})=p^{n-1}
and our goal is to bound the
probability that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert>p^{1-1/n}
. Now, we inspect the dual
lattice
\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
and the dual basis
\mathcal{B}^{\dagger}
of the
reduced basis. Recall that
({\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger})^{\ast}={\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}
and

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert=\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}\rVert^{-1}
. Therefore, we aim to bound the
probability
\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}\rVert<\frac{p^{1/n}}{p}
.

Because
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}\in\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}
, there exists
{\mathrm{\textbf{s}}}=(s_{1},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
such
that:

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}=\frac{1}{p}\cdot\left(\left(p\cdot s_{1}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\alpha_{i}\cdot s_{i}\right),s_{2},s_{3},\ldots,s_{n}\right).

First, remark that when
the length of
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}
is bounded by
\frac{p^{1/n}}{p}
it holds that:

\left\lVert(s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})\right\rVert<p^{1/n}.

Observe that the number of
(s_{2},\ldots,s_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}
of
length
<p^{1/n}
is at most
\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{Ball}_{n-1}(p^{1/n}+\sqrt{n}))
. Hence, from now on, we
fix coordinates
s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}
and examine the probability that the
first coordinate of
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}
is bounded. In particular, when

\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}\rVert<\frac{p^{1/n}}{p}
it holds that:

\left|p\,s_{1}-\sum_{i=2}^{n}\alpha_{i}s_{i}\right|<p^{1/n}

Note that, if the values
s_{2},\ldots,s_{n}
are fixed then
s_{1}
is
determined. Namely,
s_{1}
is selected in such a way that:

\sum_{i=2}^{n}\alpha_{i}s_{i}\equiv k\ (\mathrm{mod}\ p)\text{ for some }k\in[p^{1/n}]

(4)

Recall that the numbers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}
are selected uniformly at random
from a range greater than
[p]
. Hence, for a fixed
s
the
probability that Equation
4
holds is

\frac{p^{1/n}}{p}
. Therefore, by union bound we have that

\mathbb{Pr}\left[\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\dagger}\rVert<\frac{p^{1/n}}{p}\right]\leqslant\frac{p^{1/n}}{p}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\mathrm{Ball}_{n-1}(p^{1/n}+\sqrt{n}))<2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}.\qed

Applying the union bounds on the two propositions gives an upper bound
on the negation of the event considered in
Claim

4.3
. This concludes the
proof of
Claim

4.3
.

4.4
Proof of
Theorem

1.2

By
Lemma

4.2
, with probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
, for
every
i\in[n]
we have:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{\frac{n-1}{2}}p^{\frac{n-1}{n}}.

We set
p=\Theta((\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{n^{2}/2}\cdot 2^{2n\log_{2}{n}})
. This means that for every
i\in[n]
it holds that:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant\frac{p}{n^{2}}.

The vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}
are the GSO basis of

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}\in\mathbb{Z}^{n}
and do not necessarily have integral
components. We want to bound the lengths of the
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
in the LLL-reduced basis.
By definition of GSO basis, we have

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}={\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}^{\ast}_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{i-1}\mu_{i,j}{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j}^{\ast}.

The size reduction condition of LLL guarantees that
|\mu_{i,j}|\leqslant\mu<1
.
Therefore, for every
i\in[n]
it holds that:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\right\rVert\leqslant n\cdot\max_{k\in[n]}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert<\frac{p}{n}.

By the Cauchy-Schwartz inequality, the fact that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\right\rVert<p/n

implies that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\right\rVert_{1}<p/\sqrt{n}
. In particular, it means that
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}\in\{-\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor,\ldots,\left\lfloor p/2\right\rfloor\}^{n}
. Therefore, by
Observation

4.1
we can find
integers
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
such that:

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}=(\llbracket\mu_{i}a_{1}\rrbracket_{p},\ldots,\llbracket\mu_{i}a_{n}\rrbracket_{p}).

for every
i\in[n]
. Note that these vectors are linearly independent because
the vectors
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}
form a basis.

For the running time, observe that we need a single call to the LLL algorithm to get the basis

{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
and a linear number of arithmetic operations to retrieve
the coefficients
\mu_{1},\ldots,\mu_{n}
.
This concludes the proof of
Theorem

1.2
.∎

5
Extension to the block reduction

In this Section, we prove
Theorem

1.3
. Note that the proof
of
Theorem

1.1
and the proof of
Theorem

1.2
only relied on the
specific lattice reduction via
Lemma

4.2
. Hence, in
order to establish
Theorem

1.3
, it is enough to
generalize
Lemma

4.2
to the case of block basis reduction and prove the
following statement.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
be the reduced basis obtained by running the
block lattice reduction algorithm of
[
22
]
with parameter

\Upsilon
on the lattice
\mathcal{L}
defined in (
3
).
With probability
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
, it holds that:

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant C_{\Upsilon}\cdot\Upsilon^{\frac{n-1}{2}}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\quad\text{for every}\ k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},

where
C_{\Upsilon}
is a constant that only depends on
\Upsilon
.

Before we prove
Lemma

5.1
, let us elaborate on the block basis
reduction from
[
22
]
. Here, we follow the description from the
textbook
[
38
, Chapter 2, Sliding Algorithm]
. The main parameter in the block-reduction is the
block size
w>2
. Gama and Nguyen
[
22
]
, actually
parameterize their reduction with respect to the block-size. For any integer
k

let
\gamma_{k}
be the Hermite constant (see Chapter 2
[
38
]
).
The blocksize
w
of the lattice reduction of Gama and Nguyen is the smallest
integer such that
\Upsilon\geqslant(\gamma_{w})^{1/(w-1)}
. Note that
\gamma_{n}=\Theta(n)
, so
w
is a properly defined constant that depends on the choice of

\Upsilon
.

The block-reduction of
[
22
]
has two important properties.
First, it returns a basis that is
block-Mordell-reduced
. The only
property about block-Mordell-reduced basis we need is the following
inequality:

Claim 5.2

(Primal-Dual inequality, Chapter 2, Lemma 11, Equality (2.48) in
[
38
]
)
.

Let
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
be a block-Mordell-reduced basis of the lattice

L
with blocksize
w\geqslant 2
, then:

\displaystyle\frac{\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j}^{\ast}\right\rVert}{\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j+w}^{\ast}\right\rVert}\leqslant(\gamma_{w})^{w/(w-1)}\leqslant\Upsilon^{w}

(5)

where
j\in\{1,\ldots,n-w\}
such that
j\equiv_{w}1
.

The second property of the algorithm from
[
22
]
is that its
output basis is also LLL-reduced (see
[
38
, Chapter 2, Sliding Algorithm,
Algorithm 6]
). As a consequence, we can also use inequality (
1
).

Claim 5.3
.

For all
1\leqslant j<i\leqslant n
, it holds that

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w}\cdot\Upsilon^{j-i}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{j}^{\ast}\right\rVert.

(6)

Proof.

Note that blocks in block-reduced algorithm overlap on the indices
\equiv_{w}1

[
22
]
. Hence, for the sake of clarity for
i\in[n]

let
q(i)\in\mathbb{N}
to be the index of the block that
i
is contained, i.e.,

i\in[q(i)w+1,(q(i)+1)w]
. Let
\ell(i)=q(i)w+1
be the index of the
first element in the
i
th block. Similarly let
r(i)=\ell(i)
when
i\equiv_{w}1
and
r(i)=\min\{n,\ell(i)+1\}
otherwise, be the index of the
last element of the block.

Because
{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1},\ldots,{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}
is also LLL reduced we know that that by
repeated application of inequality (
1
), for every
k\in[n]

it holds:

\displaystyle(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-w}\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{\ell(k)}^{\ast}\rVert\leqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{w}\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{r(k)}^{\ast}\rVert.

(7)

Next, we use
Equation

5
, that for
j<i
says:

\displaystyle\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{\ell(i)}^{\ast}\rVert\geqslant\Upsilon^{(\ell(i)-r(j))w}\cdot\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{r(j)}^{\ast}\rVert.

(8)

Hence, by combining above we get:

\displaystyle\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-w}\cdot\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}^{\ast}_{\ell(i)}\rVert

(by (
7
))

\displaystyle\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-w}\cdot\Upsilon^{(\ell(i)-r(j))w}\cdot\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{r(j)}^{\ast}\rVert

(by (
8
))

\displaystyle\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w}\cdot\Upsilon^{i-j}\cdot\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}^{\ast}_{j}\rVert,

(by (
7
))

where the last inequality follows because
\ell(i)w-r(j)w\geqslant i-j
.
∎

Proof of
Lemma

5.1
.

The probabilistic event in
Claim

4.3
is a function of the lattice
\mathcal{L}

itself and not of any specific basis of it. Thus, it remains valid for the basis
produced by the block reduction algorithm. Hence, with
\geqslant 1-2^{-\Omega(n\log{n})}
we have that
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\leqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert
. First, consider the case
k\geqslant\frac{n+1}{2}
.

\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{2w}\cdot\Upsilon^{n-k}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert.

This concludes the proof for the case
k\geqslant\frac{n+1}{2}
as
n-k\leqslant(n-1)/2
.
It remains to consider the case
k<\frac{n+1}{2}
. By repeated
application of (
6
) we have:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k-1}^{\ast}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{i=1}^{k-1}(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w}\cdot\Upsilon^{-(i-1)}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w(k-1)}\cdot\Upsilon^{-(k-2)(k-1)/2}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{k-1}.

Similarly:

\displaystyle\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert\cdots\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{n}^{\ast}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant\prod_{i=k}^{n}(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w}\cdot\Upsilon^{-(i-k)}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w(n-k)}\cdot\Upsilon^{-(n-k)(n-k+1)/2}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k}.

Hence, by multiplying these two inequalities and by
\prod_{i=1}^{n}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{i}^{\ast}\right\rVert=\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})
we conclude that:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w(n-1)}\cdot\Upsilon^{-(n-k)(n-k+1)/2-(k-2)(k-1)/2}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{k-1}.

Because
k<\frac{n+1}{2}
we have that
(n-k)(n-k+1)+(k-1)(k-2)\leqslant(n-1)(n-k+1)
. Hence,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w(n-1)}\cdot\Upsilon^{-(n-1)(n-k+1)/2}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{k-1}.

Next, we use
Claim

4.3
with
\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{1}^{\ast}\right\rVert\geqslant\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}
to have

\displaystyle\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{\frac{n-k+1}{n}}\geqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{-2w(n-1)}\cdot\Upsilon^{-((n-1)(n-k+1)/2)}\cdot\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert^{n-k+1}.

After taking
(n-k+1)
th root we conclude that:

\displaystyle(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{2w(n-1)/(n-k+1)}\cdot\Upsilon^{(n-1)/2}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})\geqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert.

Finally, observe that as
k<(n+1)/2
this means that

(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{2w(n-1)/(n-k+1)}\leqslant(\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}})^{4w}=C_{\Upsilon}
is a constant
that depends only on
\Upsilon
, since
\gamma_{\textsf{LLL}}
is a constant and
w
depends
only on
\Upsilon
. Hence:

\displaystyle C_{\Upsilon}\cdot\Upsilon^{(n-1)/2}\cdot\mathrm{Vol}(\mathcal{L})^{1/n}\geqslant\left\lVert{\mathrm{\textbf{b}}}_{k}^{\ast}\right\rVert.

6
Conclusion

Subset Sum is one of the most fundamental problems in theoretical computer
science. To this day, there are three methods to solve it:

•

Dense regime
: Dynamic programming in
\mathcal{O}^{\star}(T)
, where
T
is the target value,

•

Threshold regime
: Meet-in-the-middle algorithm that runs in

\mathcal{O}^{\star}(2^{n/2})
time,

•

Sparse regime
: Lattice-reduction, for numbers of size

\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}=\Omega(\gamma^{n^{2}})
.

After almost 40 years, we are aware only of improvements to the exponent in the
Subset Sum problem. The exact
\mathcal{O}^{\star}(2^{n/2})
algorithm was enhanced to

\mathcal{O}(2^{0.291n})
in the average case setting
[
8
]
. In the
dense regime, the potential improvements are even smaller, as we know that no

T^{1-\epsilon}\cdot\mathrm{poly}(n)
algorithm is possible for any constant
\varepsilon>0
assuming SETH
[
1
]
. Hence, researchers have focused on optimizing
polynomial factors in
n
(see
e.g.,
[
12
,
14
,
27
,
13
]
).

In this paper, we improved the Lagarias-Odlyzko algorithm for average-case
Subset Sum and provided a method that works for almost all instances with
numbers of size
\Omega(\sqrt{\Gamma_{\textsf{LO}}})
. In terms of the constant in the
exponent, this represents one of the most substantial improvements to the regime
of solvable instances since the 1980s.

We would like to conclude with potential improvements and applications. For
example, Schnorr
[
43
]
presented a basis reduction that guarantees a

2^{\mathcal{O}(n\log\log n/\log n)}
-approximation to CVP and theoretically improves
LLL. Our method incurs essentially no additional and can just be plugged in even
with these more computationally expensive lattice-reductions to improve the
density range (see
Section

5
where we combine it with textbook variant of
Schnorr
[
43
]
lattice-reduction). However, to achieve even higher improvements,
substantially new ideas would need to be presented as improving lattice
reduction of Schnorr
[
43
]
is a major open question (see,
e.g.,
[
2
]
for recent progress on CVP). We
believe that similarly to the Lagarias-Odlyzko algorithm, our methods have
potential to be applicable outside of the realm of Knapsack problems (see for
example,
[
46
,
35
,
35
]
).
