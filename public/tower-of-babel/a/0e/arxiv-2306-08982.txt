Optimal constants of smoothing estimates for Dirac equations with radial data Thanks: The second author was supported by Japan Society for the Promotion of Science (JSPS) KAKENHI Grant Number JP23KJ1939.
Makoto Ikoma, Soichiro Suzuki

Abstract
. Kato–Yajima smoothing estimates are one of the fundamental results in study of dispersive equations such as Schrödinger equations and Dirac equations. For d d -dimensional Schrödinger-type equations ( d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 ), optimal constants of smoothing estimates were obtained by Bez–Saito–Sugimoto (2017) via the so-called Funk–Hecke theorem. Recently Ikoma (2022) considered optimal constants for d d -dimensional Dirac equations using a similar method, and it was revealed that determining optimal constants for Dirac equations is much harder than the case of Schrödinger-type equations. Indeed, Ikoma obtained the optimal constant in the case d = 2 d=2 , but only upper bounds (which seem not optimal) were given in other dimensions. In this paper, we give optimal constants for d d -dimensional Schrödinger-type and Dirac equations with radial initial data for any d ≥ 2 d\geq 2 . In addition, we also give optimal constants for the one-dimensional Schrödinger-type and Dirac equations.

In 2015, Bez–Saito–Sugimoto

5

pointed out that the so-called Funk–Hecke theorem (see Theorem
3.2
) is quite useful to obtain optimal constants of smoothing estimates and to determine extremisers of the inequalities for the following equation:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=\phi(\lvert D\rvert)u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=f(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d},\end{cases}

where
\phi(\lvert D\rvert)
denotes the Fourier multiplier operator whose symbol is
\phi(\lvert\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}\rvert)
, that is,

(1.2)

\mathcal{F}\phi(\lvert D\rvert)f=\phi(\lvert\xi\rvert)\widehat{f}(\xi).

Particularly, (
1.1
) becomes the free Schrödinger equation if
\phi(r)=r^{2}
and the relativistic Schrödinger equation if
\phi(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}
, respectively.
For convenience, we refer the equation (
1.1
) as a Schrodinger-type equation.
The smoothing estimate of the Schrodinger-type equation is expressed as

(1.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{it\phi(\lvert D\rvert)}f(x)\rvert^{2}\mathit{w}(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})},

here functions
\mathit{w}
and
\psi
are called spatial weight and dispersion relation, respectively.
We write
C_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,\phi)
for the optimal constant for the inequality (
1.3
), in other words,

(1.4)

C_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,\phi)\coloneqq\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\\
f\not\equiv 0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{it\phi(\lvert D\rvert)}f(x)\rvert^{2}\mathit{w}(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt/\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}.

Under some reasonable assumptions for
\mathit{w}
,
\psi
and
\phi
(see Assumption
2.1
for details),
[
5
]
established the following result:

Theorem 1.1

(
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Let
d\geq 2
.
Then we have

(1.5)

C_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,\phi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}_{0}}\sup_{r>0}\lambda_{k}(r),

where

(1.6)

\lambda_{k}(r)\coloneqq\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-2}\rvert\frac{r^{d-1}\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\int_{-1}^{1}F_{\mathit{w}}(r^{2}(1-t))p_{d,k}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,dt.

Here
F_{\mathit{w}}
is defined by the relation

(1.7)

\widehat{\mathit{w}(\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert)}(\xi)=F_{\mathit{w}}(\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2})

and
p_{d,k}
is the Legendre polynomial of degree
k
in
d
dimensions, which may be defined in a number of ways, for example, via the Rodrigues formula,

(1.8)

(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}p_{d,k}(t)=(-1)^{k}\frac{\Gamma(\frac{d-1}{2})}{2^{k}\Gamma(k+\frac{d-1}{2})}\frac{d^{k}}{dt^{k}}(1-t^{2})^{k+\frac{d-3}{2}}.

Since we are interested in explicit constants, here we clarify that the Fourier transform in this paper is defined by

(1.9)

\hat{f}(\xi)\coloneqq\int_{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}f(x)e^{-ix\cdot\xi}\,dx.

In this case, the Plancherel theorem states that

(1.10)

\lVert\hat{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=(2\pi)^{d}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}.

Now we are going to discuss the free Dirac equation. Let
d\geq 1
and write
N\coloneqq 2^{\lfloor(d+1)/2\rfloor}
.
The
d
-dimensional free Dirac equation with mass
m\geq 0
is given by

(1.11)

\begin{cases}i\partial_{t}u(x,t)=H_{m}u(x,t),&(x,t)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R},\\
u(x,0)=f(x),&x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.\end{cases}

Here the Dirac operator
H_{m}
is defined by

(1.12)

H_{m}\coloneqq\alpha\cdot D+m\beta=\sum_{j=1}^{d}\alpha_{j}D_{j}+m\beta,

where
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ldots,\alpha_{d}
,
\alpha_{d+1}=\beta

are
N\times N
Hermitian matrices satisfying the anti-commutation relation
\alpha_{j}\alpha_{k}+\alpha_{k}\alpha_{j}=2\delta_{jk}I_{N}
.
In this case, the smoothing estimate is

(1.13)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-itH_{m}}f(x)\rvert^{2}\mathit{w}(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt\leq C\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})},

and let
\widetilde{C}_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,m)
be the optimal constant of the inequality (
1.13
):

(1.14)

\widetilde{C}_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,m)\coloneqq\hskip-12.0pt\sup_{\begin{subarray}{c}f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})\\
f\not\equiv 0\end{subarray}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{-itH_{m}}f(x)\rvert^{2}\mathit{w}(\lvert x\rvert)\,dx\,dt/\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d},\mathbb{C}^{N})}.

Recently, Ikoma
[

Theorem 1.2

(
[

Let
d=2

Then we have

(1.15)

\widetilde{C}_{2}(\mathit{w},\psi,m)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{r>0}\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r),

where

(1.16)

\widetilde{\lambda}_{k}(r)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\lambda_{k}(r)+\lambda_{k+1}(r)+\frac{m}{\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}}\lvert\lambda_{k}(r)-\lambda_{k+1}(r)\rvert\right)

and
\lambda_{k}(r)
is (
1.6

\phi(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}
.

Theorem 1.3

(
[
12

)
.

Let
d\geq 3
.
Then we have

(1.17)

\widetilde{C}_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,m)\leq C_{d}(\mathit{w},\psi,\phi)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{d-1}}\sup_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\sup_{r>0}\lambda_{k}(r),

where
\lambda_{k}(r)
is (
1.6
) with
\phi(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}
.
In other words, the optimal constant for the Dirac equation is less than or equal to that for the relativistic Schrödinger equation.

Unlike Schrödinger-type equations, the optimal constant is known only in the case
d=2
for Dirac equations.
In particular, the physically most important case
d=3
remains open.
Also, though Theorem
1.3
gives upper bounds, there are no known results on lower bounds.
The main difficulty here is that
the multiplier of Dirac operator
H_{m}
is not radial.
Indeed, it causes a large number (growing exponentially with respect to the dimension
d
) of cross terms to appear when computing the
L^{2}
norm, which does not exist at all in the case of the Schrödinger-type equation.

In this paper, we consider the smoothing estimate of
d
-dimensional Dirac equations with radial initial data.
In that case cross terms does not appear, and actually we can obtain optimal constants.
Note these optimal constants with the radial restriction serve as lower bounds of constants without the restriction.
Additionally, we also give optimal constants of the one-dimensional Schrödinger-type equation and Dirac equation.
You may see that in the case
d=1

\mathbb{S}^{d-2}
in the definition of
\lambda_{k}
given by (
1.6

(d-2)
-dimensional unit sphere, does not make sense anymore.
Nevertheless, by redefining it appropriately, we establish results in the case
d=1
using essentially the same argument as that of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
.

Main results

In this section, we discuss the main results.
Through out this paper, we always suppose that the following Assumption

holds:

Assumption 2.1
.

The spatial weight
\mathit{w}
, the smoothing function
\psi
, and the dispersion relation
\phi
satisfy the following conditions.

The spatial weight:

a

•:

In the case
d=1
,
the spatial weight
\mathit{w}(\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert)\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
is positive and even, in which case we write

(

)

\widehat{\mathit{w}(\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert)}(\xi)=F_{\mathit{w}}(\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2})

In the case

,
the spatial weight
\mathit{w}(\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert)
is positive, radial, and its Fourier transform (in the sense of tempered distributions) coincides some locally bounded function on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
, in which case we write

(
1.7
)

\widehat{\mathit{w}(\lvert\,\cdot\,\rvert)}(\xi)=F_{\mathit{w}}(\tfrac{1}{2}\lvert\xi\rvert^{2}).

Furthermore,

is sufficiently nice to guarantee the continuity of
\lambda_{k}\colon\mathbb{R}_{>0}\to\mathbb{R}_{>0}
for each

(

)

\lambda_{k}(r)\coloneqq\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-2}\rvert\frac{r^{d-1}\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\int_{-1}^{1}F_{\mathit{w}}(r^{2}(1-t))p_{d,k}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,dt.

The smoothing function:

a

•:

In the case
d=1
, the smoothing function
\psi\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is continuous and non-negative.

•:

In the case
d=2
, the smoothing function
\psi\colon(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
is infinitely differentiable and non-negative.

The dispersion relation:

a

•:

For Schödinger equations,
the dispersion relation

•:

For Dirac equations,

2.1.
Schrödinger-type equations

In this section, we discuss Schrödinger-type equations.
Define the linear operator
S\colon L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})
by

(2.1)

Sf(x,t)\coloneqq\mathit{w}(\lvert x\rvert)^{1/2}\int_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}e^{ix\cdot\xi}\psi(\lvert\xi\rvert)e^{it\phi(\lvert\xi\rvert)}f(\xi)\,d\xi,

in other words,

(2.2)

S\widehat{f}(x,t)=\lvert\psi(\lvert D\rvert)e^{it\phi(\lvert D\rvert)}f(x)\rvert\mathit{w}(\lvert x\rvert)^{1/2}.

Then it is obvious that the smoothing estimate (
1.3
) is equivalent to

(2.3)

\lVert S\hat{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}^{2}\leq C\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=(2\pi)^{d}C\lVert\hat{f}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2},

and therefore it is enough to consider the operator norm of
S
.

At first we discuss the one dimensional case.
For simplicity, let

(2.4)

\lVert S\rVert\coloneqq\lVert S\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}.

Theorem 2.2
.

Let
d=1
and write

(2.5)

\displaystyle\lambda_{k}(r)\coloneqq\begin{cases}\frac{\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}(\lVert\mathit{w}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+F_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})),&k=0,\\
\frac{\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}(\lVert\mathit{w}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}-F_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})),&k=1,\end{cases}

(2.6)

\displaystyle\lambda^{*}\coloneqq\sup_{r>0}\max_{k=0,1}\lambda_{k}(r)=\sup_{r>0}\frac{\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}(\lVert\mathit{w}\rVert_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R})}+\lvert F_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})\rvert),

(2.7)

\displaystyle E_{k}\coloneqq\{\,r>0:\lambda_{k}(r)=\lambda^{*}\,\}.

Then, under Assumption
2.1
, we have

(2.8)

\lVert S\rVert^{2}=2\pi\lambda^{*}.

Regarding extremisers, fix
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and decompose it as

(2.9)

f(\xi)=f_{0}(\lvert\xi\rvert)+\sgn(\xi)f_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert).

Then the following are equivalent:

(2.10)

The equality

(2.11)

\lVert Sf\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}=2\pi\lambda^{*}\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}

holds.

(2.12)

The functions
\{f_{k}\}_{k=0,1}
satisfy

(2.13)

\supp f_{k}\subset E_{k}

for each
k=0,1
.

As a consequence, extremisers exist if and only if there exists

k\in\{0,1\}
such that
\lvert E_{k}\rvert>0
.

Next we consider smoothing estimates with radial data.
For simplicity we write

(2.13)

\lVert S\rVert_{\textup{rad.}}\coloneqq\lVert S\rVert_{L^{2}_{\textup{rad.}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\to L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})},

here
L^{2}_{\textup{rad.}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
denotes the subspace of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined by

(2.14)

L^{2}_{\textup{rad.}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\{\,f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d}):\text{$f$ is radial}\,\}.

Theorem 2.3
.

Let
d\geq 1
and write

(2.15)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}(r)\coloneqq\lambda_{0}(r),

(2.16)

\displaystyle\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}^{*}\coloneqq\sup_{r>0}\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}(r),

(2.17)

\displaystyle E_{\textup{rad.}}\coloneqq\{\,r>0:\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}(r)=\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}^{*}\,\}.

Then, under Assumption
2.1
, we have

(2.18)

\lVert S\rVert_{\textup{rad.}}^{2}=2\pi\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}^{*}.

Regarding extremisers,
fix
f\in L^{2}_{\textup{rad.}}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and write

(2.19)

f(\xi)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{-(d-1)/2}f_{0}(\xi).

Then the following are equivalent:

(2.20)

The equality

(2.21)

\lVert Sf\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d+1})}^{2}=2\pi\lambda_{\textup{rad.}}^{*}\lVert f\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}

holds.

(2.22)

The function
f_{0}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfies

(2.23)

2.2.
Dirac equations

As in the case of Schrödinger-type equations, we define the linear operator

3.
Preliminaries

3.1.
Spherical harmonics decomposition of
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

For each
k\in\mathbb{N}
, let
\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\{P_{k}^{n}\}_{1\leq n\leq d_{k}}
be the space of homogeneous harmonic polynomials of degree
k
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and its orthonormal basis, respectively.
Furthermore, we write
\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\coloneqq\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
Here the inner product of
P,Q\in\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is defined by

(3.1)

\langle P,Q\rangle_{\mathcal{H}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}\coloneqq\langle{\left.\kern-1.2ptP\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}},{\left.\kern-1.2ptQ\vphantom{\big|}\right|_{\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}}\rangle_{L^{2}(\mathbb{S}^{d-1})}=\int_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}P(\theta)\overline{Q(\theta)}\,d\sigma(\theta),

as usual.
The spherical harmonics decomposition (Theorem
3.1
) and the Funk–Hecke theorem (Theorem
3.2
) are well-known and play essential roles in
[
5
]
and
[
12
]
.

Theorem 3.1
.

For any
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, there exists
\{f_{k}^{n}\}_{k\in\mathbb{N},1\leq n\leq d_{k}}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfying

(3.2)

\displaystyle f(\xi)=\lvert\xi\rvert^{-(d-1)/2}\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{d_{k}}P_{k}^{n}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}^{n}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

(3.3)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{d})}^{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\sum_{n=1}^{d_{k}}\lVert f_{k}^{n}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}^{2}.

Note that in the case
d=1
, we have

(3.4)

\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R})=\begin{cases}\{\,c:c\in\mathbb{C}\,\},&k=0,\\
\{\,c\xi:c\in\mathbb{C}\,\},&k=1,\\
\{0\},&k\geq 2\end{cases}

and therefore (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) are reduced to

(3.5)

\displaystyle f(\xi)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}f_{0}(\lvert\xi\rvert)+\frac{\sgn(\xi)}{\sqrt{2}}f_{1}(\lvert\xi\rvert),

(3.6)

\displaystyle\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\lVert f_{0}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}^{2}+\lVert f_{1}\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})}^{2}.

We remark that (
3.5
) is equivalent to the even–odd decomposition

(3.7)

f(\xi)=\frac{f(\xi)+f(-\xi)}{2}+\frac{f(\xi)+f(-\xi)}{2}.

3.2.
The Funk–Hecke theorem

The Funk–Hecke theorem is as follows:

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
d\geq 2
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
F\in L^{1}([-1,1],(1-t^{2})^{(d-3)/2}\,dt)
and write

(3.8)

\mu_{k}[F]\coloneqq\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-2}\rvert\int_{-1}^{1}F(t)p_{d,k}(t)(1-t^{2})^{\frac{d-3}{2}}\,dt,

here recall that
p_{d,k}
denotes the Legendre polynomial of degree
k
in
d
dimensions (see (
1.8
)).
Then, for any
P\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, we have

(3.9)

\int_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}F(\theta\cdot\omega)P(\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)=\mu_{k}[F]P(\omega).

Note that the function
\lambda_{k}
defined in (
1.6
) satisfies

(3.10)

\lambda_{k}(r)=\frac{r^{d-1}\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\mu_{k}[F_{\mathit{w}}(r^{2}(1-\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}))],

in other words, it satisfies

(3.11)

\frac{r^{d-1}\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\int_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}}F_{\mathit{w}}(r^{2}(1-\theta\cdot\omega))P(\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)=\lambda_{k}(r)P(\omega)

for each
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
,
P\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.

Now a little concern is, the factor
\lvert\mathbb{S}^{d-2}\rvert
in (
3.8
), the surface area of the
(d-2)
-dimensional unit sphere, does not make sense in the case
d=1
.
Nevertheless, it is quite easy to see that the following one-dimensional variant of Theorem
3.2
holds:

Theorem 3.3
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
F\colon\mathbb{S}^{0}\to\mathbb{C}
and write

(3.12)

\mu_{k}[F]\coloneqq\begin{cases}F(1)+F(-1),&k=0,\\
F(1)-F(-1),&k=1,\\
0,&k\geq 2.\end{cases}

Then, for any
P\in\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R})
and
\omega\in\mathbb{S}^{0}
, we have

(
3.9
)

\int_{\theta\in\mathbb{S}^{0}}F(\theta\omega)P(\theta)\,d\sigma(\theta)=\mu_{k}[F]P(\omega).

Notice that
\lambda_{k}
defined in (
2.5
) satisfies

(3.13)

\lambda_{k}(r)=\frac{\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\mu_{k}[F_{\mathit{w}}(r^{2}(1-\makebox[4.30554pt]{{$\cdot$}}))].

4.1.
Proof of Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
: smoothing in the case

In this section, we prove Theorems
2.2
and
2.4
.
Through out this section, we choose orthonormal bases
\{P_{k}\}\subset\mathcal{H}_{k}(\mathbb{R})
(
k=0,1
) given by

(4.1)

P_{0}(\xi)\coloneqq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}},\quad P_{1}(\xi)\coloneqq\frac{\xi}{\sqrt{2}}.

At first we prove Theorem
2.2
.
Though the proof is essentially the same as that of
[
5
, Theorem 1.1]
,
we give details here so that readers can compare it with the proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

Fix
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
and decompose it as

(4.2)

f(\xi)=\sum_{k=0,1}P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert).

Then Lemma
3.4
implies that

(4.3)

\displaystyle\lVert Sf\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}

(4.4)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{k=0,1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(r)\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}\,dr

(4.5)

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\lambda^{*}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

where

(4.6)

\lambda^{*}\coloneqq\sup_{r>0}\max_{k=0,1}\lambda_{k}(r).

Hence, we have

(4.7)

\lVert S\rVert^{2}\leq 2\pi\lambda^{*}.

To see the equality
\lVert S\rVert^{2}=2\pi\lambda^{*}
,
we will show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}

such that
\lVert Sf\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}\geq 2\pi(\lambda^{*}-\varepsilon)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
.
Then, by the definition of
\lambda^{*}
and the continuity of
\lambda_{k}
,
we can choose
k\in\{0,1\}
such that the Lebesgue measure of

(4.8)

E_{k}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\{\,r>0:\lambda_{k}(r)\geq\lambda^{*}-\varepsilon\,\}

is nonzero (possibly infinite).
Now let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})\setminus\{0\}
be

(4.9)

f(\xi)=\sum_{k=0,1}P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)

with
\{f_{k}\}_{k=0,1}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfying

(4.10)

\supp f_{k}(r)\subset E_{k}(\varepsilon).

Then we have

(4.11)

\displaystyle\lVert Sf\rVert^{2}_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(r)\lvert f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rvert^{2}\,dr

(4.12)

\displaystyle\geq 2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}(\lambda^{*}-\varepsilon)\lvert f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\rvert^{2}\,dr

(4.13)

\displaystyle=2\pi(\lambda^{*}-\varepsilon)\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2},

hence the equality
\lVert S\rVert^{2}=2\pi\lambda^{*}
holds.

By a similar argument, we can show that
\eqref{item:extremiser condition 1D Schrodinger}\implies\eqref{item:extremiser 1D Schrodinger}
.
Let
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be

(4.14)

f(\xi)=\sum_{k=0,1}P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)

and
\{f_{k}\}_{k=0,1}\subset L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{>0})
satisfy

(4.15)

\supp f_{k}(r)\subset E_{k}.

Then we have

(4.16)

\displaystyle\lVert Sf\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{k=0,1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda_{k}(r)\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}\,dr

(4.17)

\displaystyle=2\pi\sum_{k=0,1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\lambda^{*}\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}\,dr

(4.18)

\displaystyle=2\pi\lambda^{*}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

Finally, we show that
\eqref{item:extremiser 1D Schrodinger}\implies\eqref{item:extremiser condition 1D Schrodinger}
.
Suppose that the equality
\lVert Sf\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}=2\pi\lambda^{*}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
holds.
Then, since

(4.19)

2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{k=0,1}(\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{k}(r))\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}\,dr=2\pi\lambda^{*}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}-\lVert Sf\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2})}^{2}=0

and

(4.20)

\sum_{k=0,1}(\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{k}(r))\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}\geq 0,

we obtain

(4.21)

\sum_{k=0,1}(\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{k}(r))\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}=0

for almost every
r>0
.
On the other hand, for each
k=0,1
, the definition of
E_{k}
implies that

(4.22)

\lambda^{*}-\lambda_{k}(r)>0

for any
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus E_{k}
.
Therefore, we conclude that

(4.23)

\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}=0

holds for almost every
r\in\mathbb{R}_{>0}\setminus E_{k}
.
∎

Now we are going to prove Theorem
2.4
, the optimal smoothing estimate for the one dimensional Dirac equation.
You may find that the proof is much more complicated than the previous one for Schrödinger equations, even in the case
d=1
.

Proof of Theorem
2.4
.

Fix
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})
and decompose it as

(4.24)

f(\xi)=\sum_{k=0,1}P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert).

Then, since we have

(4.25)

\displaystyle f_{\pm}(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0,1}\left(P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\pm\frac{1}{\phi(\lvert\xi\rvert)}(m\beta P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)+\alpha\xi P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert))\right)

(4.26)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0,1}P_{k}(\xi/\lvert\xi\rvert)\left(f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)\pm\frac{1}{\phi(\lvert\xi\rvert)}(m\beta f_{k}(\lvert\xi\rvert)+\alpha\lvert\xi\rvert f_{1-k}(\lvert\xi\rvert))\right),

Lemma
3.5
implies that

(4.27)

\lVert\widetilde{S}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})}^{2}=2\pi\sum_{k=0,1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{k}(r)\left(\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lvert m\beta f_{k}(r)+\alpha rf_{1-k}(r)\rvert^{2}\right)\,dr.

Moreover, using
\alpha^{2}=\beta^{2}=I_{2}
,
\alpha^{*}=\alpha
,
\beta^{*}=\beta
and
\alpha\beta=-\beta\alpha
,
the integrand is written as follows:

(4.28)

\displaystyle\quad\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0,1}\lambda_{k}(r)\left(\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}+\frac{1}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lvert m\beta f_{k}(r)+\alpha rf_{1-k}(r)\rvert^{2}\right)

(4.29)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{k=0,1}\lambda_{k}(r)\left(\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\right)\lvert f_{k}(r)\rvert^{2}+\frac{2mr}{\phi(r)^{2}}\operatorname{Re}{\langle\beta f_{k}(r),\alpha f_{1-k}(r)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}+\frac{r^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lvert f_{1-k}(r)\rvert^{2}}\right)

(4.30)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\right)\lambda_{0}(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lambda_{1}(r)\right)\lvert\beta f_{0}(r)\rvert^{2}

(4.31)

\displaystyle+\frac{mr}{\phi(r)^{2}}(\lambda_{0}(r)-\lambda_{1}(r))(\langle\beta f_{0}(r),\alpha f_{1}(r)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}}+\langle\alpha f_{1}(r),\beta f_{0}(r)\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{2}})

(4.32)

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\right)\lambda_{1}(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lambda_{0}(r)\right)\lvert\alpha f_{1}(r)\rvert^{2}

(4.33)

\displaystyle=\left\langle\Lambda(r)\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\
\alpha f_{1}(r)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\
\alpha f_{1}(r)\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{4}},

where

(4.34)

\displaystyle\Lambda(r)\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}a(r)I_{2}&b(r)I_{2}/2\\
b(r)I_{2}/2&c(r)I_{2}\end{pmatrix},

(4.35)

\displaystyle a(r)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\right)\lambda_{0}(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lambda_{1}(r)\right),

(4.36)

\displaystyle b(r)\coloneqq\frac{mr}{\phi(r)^{2}}(\lambda_{0}(r)-\lambda_{1}(r)),

(4.37)

\displaystyle c(r)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(1+\frac{m^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\right)\lambda_{1}(r)+\frac{r^{2}}{\phi(r)^{2}}\lambda_{0}(r)\right).

Now we need to determine the maximal eigenvalue of
\Lambda(r)
and its associated eigenspace.
Note that
\phi(r)=\sqrt{r^{2}+m^{2}}
implies that

(4.38)

\displaystyle\Lambda(r)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}(\lambda_{0}(r)+\lambda_{1}(r))I_{4}+\frac{m}{2\phi(r)^{2}}(\lambda_{0}(r)-\lambda_{1}(r))\begin{pmatrix}mI_{2}&rI_{2}\\
rI_{2}&-mI_{2}\end{pmatrix}

(4.39)

\displaystyle=\frac{\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\left(F_{\mathit{w}}(0)I_{4}+\frac{m}{\phi(r)^{2}}F_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})\begin{pmatrix}mI_{2}&rI_{2}\\
rI_{2}&-mI_{2}\end{pmatrix}\right).

Then, since eigenvalues and associated eigenspaces of the matrix

(4.40)

\begin{pmatrix}mI_{2}&rI_{2}\\
rI_{2}&-mI_{2}\end{pmatrix}

are
\pm\phi(r)
and

(4.41)

\ker{\begin{pmatrix}(m\mp\phi(r))I_{2}&rI_{2}\\
rI_{2}&-(m\mp\phi(r))I_{2}\end{pmatrix}}=\left\{\,s\begin{pmatrix}m\pm\phi(r)\\
0\\
r\\
0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m\pm\phi(r)\\
0\\
r\end{pmatrix}:s,t\in\mathbb{C}\,\right\},

we conclude that the maximal eigenvalue of
\Lambda(r)
and its associated eigenspace are

(
2.27
)

\widetilde{\lambda}(r)=\frac{\psi(r)^{2}}{\lvert\phi^{\prime}(r)\rvert}\left(F_{\mathit{w}}(0)+\frac{m}{\phi(r)}\lvert F_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})\rvert\right)

and

(
2.37
)

W(r)=\begin{cases}\mathbb{C}^{4},&mF_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})=0,\\
\left\{\,s\begin{pmatrix}m+\phi(r)\\
0\\
r\\
0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m+\phi(r)\\
0\\
r\end{pmatrix}:s,t\in\mathbb{C}\,\right\},&mF_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})>0,\\
\left\{\,s\begin{pmatrix}m-\phi(r)\\
0\\
r\\
0\end{pmatrix}+t\begin{pmatrix}0\\
m-\phi(r)\\
0\\
r\end{pmatrix}:s,t\in\mathbb{C}\,\right\},&mF_{\mathit{w}}(2r^{2})<0,\end{cases}

respectively.
Therefore, we have

(4.42)

\displaystyle\lVert\widetilde{S}f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{2},\mathbb{C}^{2})}^{2}

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\Lambda(r)\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\
\alpha f_{1}(r)\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\
\alpha f_{1}(r)\end{pmatrix}\right\rangle_{\mathbb{C}^{4}}\,dr

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}\lVert f\rVert_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})}^{2}

and hence

(4.45)

\lVert\widetilde{S}\rVert^{2}\leq 2\pi\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}.

To see the equality
\lVert\widetilde{S}\rVert^{2}=2\pi\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}
,
we will show that for any
\varepsilon>0
,
there exists
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})\setminus\{0\}

\varepsilon>0

\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}

\widetilde{\lambda}

(4.46)

\widetilde{E}(\varepsilon)\coloneqq\{\,r>0:\widetilde{\lambda}(r)\geq\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}-\varepsilon\,\}

f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\mathbb{C}^{2})\setminus\{0\}

(4.47)

(4.48)

(4.49)

Then we have

(4.50)

\displaystyle=2\pi\int_{0}^{\infty}\left\langle\Lambda(r)\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\

By a similar argument, we can show that
\eqref{item:extremiser condition 1D Dirac}\implies\eqref{item:extremiser 1D Dirac}
.

(4.61)

\displaystyle=0

and

(4.62)

(4.63)

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\
\alpha f_{1}(r)\end{pmatrix}\in W(r),

(4.64)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}(\lvert f_{0}(r)\rvert^{2}+\lvert f_{1}(r)\rvert^{2})-\left\langle\Lambda(r)\begin{pmatrix}\beta f_{0}(r)\\

(4.65)

\widetilde{\lambda}^{*}-\widetilde{\lambda}(r)>0

Therefore, we conclude that

\lvert f_{0}(r)\rvert^{2}+\lvert f_{1}(r)\rvert^{2}=0

.
∎
