Abstract
. We establish a real version of Turrittin’s result on polynomial and formal normal forms of linear systems of ODEs with meromorphic coefficients. Both the normal forms or the transformations used have only real coefficients. In order to adapt the proofs to the real case, we make a review of the result in the complex case.



1.
Preliminaries and statements

Let
K
be a field of characteristic zero and let
L_{K}=K[[x]][x^{-1}]
be the field of formal meromorphic series with coefficients in
K
, endowed with the usual derivation with respect to
x
(denoted only by a prime), and the usual valuation
\nu:L_{K}\to\mathbb{Z}\cup\{\infty\}
defined as the minimum of the support of the series, also called the
order
.
As a matter of notation, if
R
is any ring and
n\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
,
\mathcal{M}_{n}(R)
denotes the ring of square matrices of size
n
with entries in
R
.

A matrix
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
is identified with the
formal meromorphic linear system of ODEs

[A]\;\;\;\;\;Y^{\prime}=AY,

where
Y=(Y_{1},...,Y_{n})^{t}
is a column vector of
n
variables. Define
the order of
A
to be
\nu(A):=\min\{\nu(a_{ij})\,:\,1\leq i,j\leq n\}
, where
A=(a_{ij})
. Sometimes we use the notation
A=A(x)
to make explicit that we are dealing with meromorphic series in the variable
x
. Correspondingly, we will usually write the system as a series of matrices in the form

(1)

A=x^{\nu(A)}(A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots),\mbox{ where }A_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K)\mbox{ for any }i\mbox{ and }A_{0}\neq 0.

Also, if
N
is a non-negative integer, the
truncated system
up to degree
N
is defined by

J_{N}A:=x^{\nu(A)}(A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots+x^{N}A_{N}).

The system
A
is called
singular
(at
x=0
) if
\nu(A)<0
. The
Poincaré rank
of the system is defined as the non-negative integer
q=q(A):=\max\{-\nu(A)-1,0\}
. We usually rewrite
A
as the system of formal linear ODEs

x^{q+1}Y^{\prime}=\widetilde{A}Y,\;\;\mbox{ where }\;\;\widetilde{A}=A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots.

A singular system with Poincaré rank
q=0
(resp.
q>0
) is usually referred to be of
first kind
(resp. of
second kind
).

Denote by
I_{n}
the identity matrix of size
n
. Define the
radiality index
of
A
as the non-negative integer

k=k(A):=\min(\{j\,:A_{j}\not\in KI_{n}\}\cup\{q\}).

The truncation
J_{k-1}A
is called the
radial part
of
A
.

We are interested in the problem of getting formal normal forms of a given singular system under transformations of one of the following types:

(i)

Given
P\in GL_{n}(L_{K})
,
the linear change of variables

Y=PZ
transforms the system
[A]:Y^{\prime}=AY
into the system
[B]:Z^{\prime}=BZ
where

B=P^{-1}AP-P^{-1}P^{\prime}.

The map
\Psi_{P}:\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})\to\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
sending
A
to
\Psi_{P}[A]:=P^{-1}AP-P^{-1}P^{\prime}
is bijective. It is called
the
gauge transformation associated to
P
.
A gauge transformation
\Psi_{P}
will be called:
regular
, if
\nu(P)=0
and
\det P(0)\neq 0
;
polynomial
, if each entry of
P
belongs to
K[x]
;
diagonal monomial
, if
P=\mbox{diag}(x^{k_{1}},\cdots,x^{k_{n}})
with
k_{j}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
for each
j
.

(ii)

Given
r\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, the change of the independent variable

x=z^{r},

transforms the system
\frac{dY}{dx}=A(x)Y
into a system
\frac{dY}{dz}=B(z)Y
, where

B(z)=rz^{r-1}A(z^{r}).

Re-written with the same letter
x
, we define the map
R_{r}:\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})\to\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
given by
R_{r}[A]:=rx^{r-1}A(x^{r})
, called the
ramification of order
r
. It is an injective map but not bijective for
r>1
.

In addition, we are interested in polynomial (truncated) normal forms obtained by means of polynomial gauge transformations and ramifications (so that, if the initial system is polynomial or convergent, we preserve this character).

The case
K=\mathbb{C}
(or more generally,
K
algebraically closed) is classical and treated with different approaches in the literature (Birkhoff
[
7
]
, Hukuhara
[
10
,
11
]
, Turrittin
[
17
]
, Wasow
[
19
]
, Moser
[
15
]
, Balser-Jurkart-Lutz
[
3
]
, Babbitt-Varadarajan
[
1
]
, Barkatou
[
4
,
5
]
, Barkatou-Pflügel
[
6
]
. The different avatars of the algorithms for obtaining normal forms are commonly referred (as we will do here) by the generic expression
Turrittin’s Theorem
.

Our objective in this paper is to extend Turrittin’s Theorem to the real case
K=\mathbb{R}
, or more generally to the case where
K
is a real closed field.
As far as we know, this case has not been treated yet (except, of course, in the situation of a constant system
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K)
for which the usual well known real Jordan canonical form of
A
was proposed by Turrittin himself in
[
18
]
). We present versions of real (formal and polynomial) normal forms for any system, in such a way that they can be obtained by transformations written in the base field
K
, without passing through the algebraic closure
\overline{K}=K(\sqrt{-1})
.

1.1.
The complex case

In order to make precise statements and expose some of the steps that are useful to treat the real case, we propose first a brief revision of the complex case. Despite of its prevalence in the literature, we are led ourselves to sketch the different steps of the corresponding proofs (in section
2
), instead of simply addressing the reader to the references. There are additional reasons to justify this revision:

- Although there are other proofs (even better ones from the point of view of computational effectiveness, see
[
5
]
), maybe the most commonly used reference for the complex case is Wasow’s book
[
19
]
. We decided to follow also this last reference here. However, in that proof, the final arguments concerning the induction on the Poincaré rank is perhaps not sufficiently clarified: it drops as long as we do not need to make a ramification, but it increases after ramifications, an operation which is unavoidable in general. The required modification, even its simplicity, is worth to be made, in any case.

- In existing proofs of Turrittin’s Theorem, it is frequently allowed the use of
exponential shiftings
when the leading matrix has a single eigenvalue. Such transformations have not an algebraic or formal nature and are “strange” to the initial setting of the systems. Although they commute with the whole matrix of the system so that the resulting system has also formal meromorphic coefficients, the exponential shiftings may behave very badly with respect to non-linear terms in general systems. Thus, for applications, it is better to avoid these operations.

- The search of precise statements for polynomial normal forms of Turrittin’s result make necessary to enter in some details of the proofs; such statements are not exactly pursued in the common references, mostly devoted to obtain expressions of a fundamental matrix of solutions (cf. Remark
5
, (b) below).

We start by defining the normal forms that we expect to get.

Definition 1

(Turrittin-Ramis-Sibuya form)
.

Let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
be a system with Poincaré rank
q=q(A)
and let
\mu\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
. We say that
A
is in Turrittin-Ramis-Sibuya form of degree
\mu
(and of rank
q
), or in
(TRS)_{\mu}^{q}
-form for short, if it is written as

A(x)=x^{-(q+1)}\left(D(x)+x^{q}C+O(x^{q+\mu+1})\right),

where
D(x)={\rm diag}(d_{1}(x),...,d_{n}(x))
is a diagonal matrix with polynomial entries
d_{j}(x)\in K[x]
of degree at most
q-1
(equal zero iff
q=0
) and
C\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K)
is a constant matrix commuting with
D(x)
. In this case, the truncated system
J_{q}(A)=x^{-(q+1)}(D(x)+x^{q}C)
is called the principal part of
A
, while
D(x)
, resp.
C
, is called the exponential part, resp. the residual matrix.

Notice that if the system
A
is singular of first kind (that is
q=0
) then
A
is already in
(TRS)_{0}^{0}
-form, with exponential part equal to
D(x)=0
and residual matrix
C=A_{0}
. On the other hand, if
A
is in
(TRS)_{\mu}^{q}
-form for some
\mu
and
q>0
then its exponential part
D(x)
is not zero; in fact, it satisfies
D(0)\neq 0
.

The names Ramis and Sibuya in the definition above come from those authors paper
[
16
]
, devoted to summability properties of formal solutions of systems of holomorphic ODEs where the linear part is in
(TRS)
-form of some degree. We have added the name Turrittin by obvious reasons. It is worth to notice that analogous expressions as the
(TRS)
-forms appear also in the context of germs of biholomorphisms in
[
12
,
13
]
(where the name of “Ramis-Sibuya form” is used).

Definition 2
.

Let
C\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K)
be a constant square matrix with entries in
K
. We say that
C
is non-resonant
1
1

1

Other authors, for instance Balser in his book
[
2
]
, use instead the terminology “
C
has good spectrum”.
if for any pair of distinct eigenvalues
\lambda,\lambda^{\prime}\in\overline{K}
of
C
we have
\lambda-\lambda^{\prime}\not\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Now, Turrittin’s results for the case where
K=\overline{K}
can be stated in the two following theorems.

Theorem 3

(Complex Polynomial Normal Form)
.

Suppose that
K
is an algebraically closed field and let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
be a singular system with Poincaré rank equal to
q=q(A)
.

(i)

There exist some
r\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
and finitely many polynomial gauge transformations
\varphi_{1},...,\varphi_{m}
, either regular or diagonal monomial, such that, denoting

\psi=\varphi_{m}\circ\cdots\circ\varphi_{1}\circ R_{r},

the transformed system
\widetilde{A}=\psi[A]
is in
(TRS)_{0}^{\tilde{q}}
-form, where
\tilde{q}=q(\widetilde{A})
. Moreover, if
B\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
is another singular system with
q(B)=q
and
J_{nq}A=J_{nq}B
then
\widetilde{B}=\psi[B]
is also in
(TRS)_{0}^{\tilde{q}}
-form with the same principal part as
\widetilde{A}
; i.e,
q(\widetilde{B})=\tilde{q}
and

J_{\tilde{q}}\psi[A]=J_{\tilde{q}}\psi[B].

(ii)

Assume that
A
is in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form and that its residual matrix is non-resonant. Then, for any given
\mu\geq 0
, there exists a regular polynomial gauge transformation
\phi_{\mu}=\Psi_{P^{\mu}}
, where
P^{\mu}(0)=I_{n}
, such that
\phi_{\mu}[\widetilde{A}]
is in
(TRS)_{\mu}^{\tilde{q}}
-form with the same principal part as the original system
A
. Moreover, the family
\{P^{\mu}\}_{\mu}
can be chosen such that
P^{\mu}
is of degree at most
q+\mu
and satisfying that
J_{q+\mu}P^{\mu^{\prime}}=J_{q+\mu}P^{\mu}
for any
\mu^{\prime}>\mu
.

(iii)

Assume that
A
is in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form. Then there exists a gauge transformation
\phi
, given by a finite composition of regular polynomial or diagonal monomial transformations, such that
\phi[A]
is in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form with non-resonant residual matrix (and the same exponential part as
A
).

As a consequence of the theorem above, one obtains the following version of Turrittin’s formal normal forms of complex meromorphic linear ODEs

Theorem 4

(Complex Formal Normal Form)
.

Suppose that
K
is an algebraically closed field and let
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
be a singular system with Poincaré rank equal to
q=q(A)
.
There exists a formal gauge transformation
\Psi_{P}
and a ramification
R_{r}
such that the transformed system
F=(\Psi_{P}\circ R_{r})[A]
has Poinaré rank equal to
\tilde{q}=q(F)
and can be written in a Formal Normal Form

\hskip-28.45274pt[F]\hskip 28.45274ptY^{\prime}=x^{-(\tilde{q}+1)}(D(x)+x^{\tilde{q}}C)Y,

where
D(x)
and
C
satisfy the requirements in Definition
1
; i.e.,
F
is in
(TRS)^{\tilde{q}}_{0}
-form and
J_{\tilde{q}}F=F
. Moreover, the transformation
\Psi_{P}
can be chosen to be equal to
\Psi_{P}=\Psi_{Q}\circ\psi
, where
Q\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K[[x]])
with
Q(0)=I_{n}
and
\psi
is a finite composition of regular polynomial or diagonal monomial gauge transformations.

Remarks 5
.

Concerning the statements in Theorem
3
and Theorem
4
, we have the following.

(a)

The sufficient truncation order
nq
in the second sentence of item (i) is already obtained for instance by Babbit-Varadarajan
[
1
]
or Lutz-Schäfke
[
14
]
. Below, we propose a proof with the slightly improved order
N:=n(q-k)+k
, where
k
is the radial index of the initial system
A
.

(b)

To obtain the formal normal form
[F]
for the system
A
in Theorem
4
is equivalent to say that there exists a matrix
P(t)\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K[[t]])
and some
r\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
such that

(2)

Z(x)=P(x^{1/r})\exp\left(\int\frac{D(x^{1/r})}{x^{(\tilde{q}+1))/r}}\right)x^{\frac{C}{r}}

is a fundamental matrix of formal solutions of the system
A
.

(c)

Another consequence of the expression (
2
) is that the ratio
\tilde{q}/r
and the exponential part
D(x)
, modulo ramification of
x
, are both invariant under formal meromorphic gauge transformations. More precisely, if we have two systems
A
and
B
such that
B=\Psi_{T}[A]
with
T\in GL_{n}(K[[x]][x^{-1}])
and we get (TRS)-normal forms of
A
and
B
as in item (i) of Theorem
3
with resulting Poincaré ranks
\tilde{q}_{A}
and
\tilde{q}_{B}
and exponential parts
D_{A}(x)
and
D_{B}(x)
, respectively, then there are integers
r_{1},r_{2}
such that
\tilde{q}_{A}/r_{1}=\tilde{q}_{B}/r_{2}
and
D_{A}(x^{1/r_{1}})=D_{B}(x^{1/r_{2}})
. In particular,
\tilde{q}=0
iff the system
A
is equivalent to a system of first kind under a formal gauge transformation.

(d)

In the proof proposed below, one could see that the sequence of gauge transformations used in items (i) or (iii) can be chosen so that any one of them, individually, do not increase the Poincaré rank of the system it applies to in the process. As we know, this observation only concerns the diagonal monomial gauge transformations, since a regular gauge transformations always preserves the Poincaré rank.

(e)

In the proof below, we propose a bound, in terms of the eigenvalues of the residual matrix
C
, for the degree of the transformation
\phi
in item (iii).

1.2.
The real case

Suppose that
K
is a real closed field, i.e.,
K\varsubsetneq K(i)=\overline{K}
, where
i=\sqrt{-1}
.
Given
\lambda=a+bi\in\overline{K}
, denote by

\Lambda_{\lambda}=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}a&-b\\
b&a\end{array}\right).

Recall that the characteristic polynomial of
\Lambda_{\lambda}
has roots
a\pm bi
and is irreducible if and only if
\lambda\not\in K
, i.e.,
b\neq 0
.
For any
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, define the monomorphism of
K

\Theta_{m}:\mathcal{M}_{m}(\overline{K})\to\mathcal{M}_{2m}(K),

sending a matrix
C=(c_{uv})\in\mathcal{M}_{m}(\overline{K})
to the
(2\times 2)
-block matrix
(\Lambda_{c_{uv}})\in\mathcal{M}_{2m}(K)
. A square matrix in the image of
\Theta_{m}
will be called a
complex matrix over
K
, or a
\mathbb{C}
-matrix
, for short.

We extend
\Theta_{m}
to a monomorphism of
K
-algebras, denoted with the same letter, from
\mathcal{M}_{m}(L_{\overline{K}})
into
\mathcal{M}_{2m}(L_{K})
; that is,
from formal meromorphic linear systems over
\overline{K}
to formal meromorphic linear systems over
K
of double dimension. A system in the image of this map will be called a
complex system (over
K
)
or a
\mathbb{C}
-system
.

In what follows, if
U,V
are two square matrices of sizes
k,l
, respectively, we denote by

the square matrix of size
k+l
given in blocks

U\oplus V:=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}U&0\\

2.
Proof of the Complex Turrittin’s Theorem

Let
A(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
by a system with Poincaré rank
q=q(A)
, written as in (
1
). Let
k=k(A)
be the radiality index.
Since the radial part
A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots+x^{k-1}A_{k-1}
is preserved by any gauge transformation, the coefficient
A_{k}
is considered as the
first significant
matrix of the system. This must be compared with the usual proofs of Turrittin’s theorem, where the radial part is ruled out by an
exponential shifting
so that
A_{k}
becomes the new leading coefficient (and
q
drops to
q-k
). In our approach, where we stress the finitely determined nature of the transformations, we do not allow the use of exponential shifting, so that the radial part is carried all along the procedure.

Denote
N(n,q,k)=n(q-k)+k
as in Remark
5
, (a) and consider the statement (i)’ to be the same as item (i) in Theorem
3
but substituting in the second part the truncation order
nq
by
N(n,q,k)
.

For the proof of items (i)’-(iii) of Theorem
3
, we perform, a priori in arbitrary ordering, several ramifications, or regular polynomial or diagonal monomial gauge transformations. The desired expression of the composition of those transformations required in the different items of the statement will be a consequence of the following lemma, whose proof is straightforward.

Lemma 9
.

Fix any field
\kappa
. Let
r\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
. Let
P(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(\kappa[x])
and let
\Psi_{P}
be the associated polynomial gauge transformation. Then there exists another polynomial gauge transformation
\Psi_{\tilde{P}}
satisfying
R_{r}\circ\Psi_{P}=\Psi_{\tilde{P}}\circ R_{r}
. In fact, we can take
\tilde{P}(x):=P(x^{r})
. In particular,
\Psi_{P}
is regular or diagonal monomial iff
\Psi_{\tilde{P}}
is so.

Another important tool is the following result, known with the name of
Splitting Lemma
, which is valid for any given base field, algebraically closed or not. It permits to reduce the dimension of the system when the first significant matrix has two disjoint subsets of eigenvalues. It is usually stated in the formal setting (see for instance
[
19
,
2
,
5
]
), but it has a finitely determined nature in terms of truncations of the system.

Lemma 10

(Splitting Lemma)
.

Let
K
be any field. With the same notations as above, if
k
is the radiality index of the system
A
, assume that
k<q
and that
A_{k}
is conjugated to
A^{11}_{k}\oplus A^{22}_{k}
, where the characteristic polynomials
\chi_{A^{11}_{k}}(\lambda)
and
\chi_{A^{22}_{k}}(\lambda)
are coprime, both of positive degrees, say
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
, respectively. Then there exists a formal regular gauge transformation
\Psi_{T}
, where
T\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K[[x]])
satisfies
T(0)=I_{n}
, such that the transformed system
B=\Psi_{T}[A]
writes as
B=B^{11}\oplus B^{22}
, where
B^{ii}\in\mathcal{M}_{n_{i}}(L_{K})
is a system of dimension
n_{i}
for
i=1,2
. Moreover,
q(B)=q
,
k(B)=k
and, writing
B(x)=x^{-(q+1)}(\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}B_{j})
, we have
A_{j}=B_{j}
for
j=0,1,...,k
and for any
m>k
, the truncation
J_{m}B
only depends on
J_{m-k}T
and
J_{m}A
. In other words, if
\widetilde{A}
is another system with the same Poincaré rank
q(\widetilde{A})=q
and satisfying
J_{m}\widetilde{A}=J_{m}A
then
J_{m}\Psi_{T^{(m-k)}}[\widetilde{A}]=J_{m}B
, where
T^{(m-k)}=J_{m-k}T
.

2.1.
Proof of Theorem
3
, (i)’. Getting a
(TRS)
-form of degree
0
and some rank
\tilde{q}

First, notice that the cases
q=0
and
q=k
(the former being a particular case of the later, by definition) are trivial: in these cases, the system is already in
(TRS)_{0}^{q}
-form.

We proceed by induction on the dimension
n
of the system. The starting case
n=1
is also trivial. Assume then that
n>1
.

2.1.1.
Case with different eigenvalues

Suppose that we are in the case where
A_{k}
has at least two different eigenvalues. Then we can reduce to a smaller dimension as follows. First, up to a constant regular gauge transformation we can assume that
A_{k}=A^{11}_{k}\oplus A^{22}_{k}
where
A^{11}_{k}
and
A^{22}_{k}
are matrices of respective sizes
n_{1},n_{2}
, both smaller than
n
, and having no common eigenvalue. Using Lemma
10
for
N=N(n,q,k)
, there exists a regular polynomial gauge transformation
\Psi_{P}
such that the
N
-truncation of
B=\Psi_{P}(A)
is written as

J_{N}B=B^{11}\oplus B^{22}
, where, for
i=1,2
,
B^{ii}
is a system of dimension
n_{i}
. Moreover,
J_{N}B
has the same Poincaré rank, the same radiality index and the same
k
-truncation than
A
. In particular, if
q_{i}=q(B^{ii})
and
k_{i}=k(B^{ii})
then
q_{i}\leq q
and
q_{i}-k_{i}\leq q-k
. Taking into account that
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are both positive and hence strictly smaller than
n
, we obtain for
i=1,2
that

N(n,q,k)\geq n_{i}(q-k)+q\geq n_{i}(q_{i}-k_{i})+q_{i}\geq n_{i}(q_{i}-k_{i})+k_{i}=N(n_{i},q_{i},k_{i}).

Using the induction hypothesis to system
B^{ii}
for
i=1,2
, there is a finite composition
\psi^{ii}
of transformations in dimension
n_{i}
(as in statement (i)) such that
\widetilde{B}^{ii}=\psi^{ii}(B^{ii})
is in
(TRS)_{0}^{q_{i}}
-form and such that the second part of statement (i)’ holds for the truncation order
N(n_{i},q_{i},k_{i})
in the place of
n_{i}q_{i}
. Moreover, in the composition
\psi^{ii}
there is but a single ramification
R_{r_{i}}
(with
r_{i}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
, including the case
R_{r_{i}}=id
(
r_{i}=1
). Now, for
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
write, using Lemma
9
,

R_{r_{j}}\circ\psi^{ii}=\widetilde{\varphi}^{ii}\circ R_{r_{1}r_{2}},

where
\widetilde{\varphi}^{ii}
is a composition of transformations in dimension
n_{i}
, either regular polynomial or diagonal monomial (that is, no ramification). Notice that the composition
R_{r_{j}}\circ\psi^{ii}
satisfies the requirements of Theorem
3
, (i)’ for the system
R_{r_{j}}(B^{ii})
, for which the Poincaré rank and radiality index are equal to
r_{j}q_{i}
and
r_{j}k_{i}
, respectively. Writting
\widetilde{\varphi}^{ii}=\Psi_{Q_{i}}
, where
Q_{i}\in\mathcal{M}_{n_{i}}(K[x])
, we put
r=r_{1}r_{2}
and define

\psi=\Psi_{Q_{1}\oplus Q_{2}}\circ R_{r}\circ\Psi_{P}=\varphi\circ R_{r},

where
\varphi=\Psi_{Q_{1}\oplus Q_{2}}\circ\Psi_{P(x^{r})}
is a composition of gauge transformations, either regular polynomial or diagonal monomial. We check that
\psi
satisfies the requirements of statement (i)’ for the initial system
A
. To be convinced, we need to observe two facts. In one hand, for
\{i,j\}=\{1,2\}
, the composition
R_{r_{j}}\circ\psi^{ii}
satisfies all the requirements of (i)’ for the system
B^{ii}
, since so does
\psi^{ii}
by construction (notice that if
B
is any system then we have
q(R_{r}(B))=rq(B)
,
k(R_{r}(B))=rk(B)
and for any
M\geq 0
, the truncation
J_{rM}R_{r}(B)
is univocally determined by
J_{M}B
). On the other hand, use the second part of Lemma
10
to conclude that
J_{N}\Psi_{P}(A)
only depends on
J_{N}A
for
N=N(n,q,k)
and the inequality
N(n_{i},q_{i},k_{i})\leq N
for
i=1,2
proved above.

2.1.2.
Case with a single eigenvalue

Suppose now that
A_{k}
has a single eigenvalue
\lambda_{k}\in\overline{K}
. Recall that we are assuming that
K=\overline{K}
. So, up to a constant gauge transformation, we may suppose that
A_{k}
is in Jordan normal form. Explicitly, there exists a (unique) sequence
1\leq n_{1}\leq n_{2}\leq\cdots\leq n_{\ell}\leq n
with
n=n_{1}+\cdots+n_{\ell}
such that

(3)

A_{k}=(\lambda_{k}I_{n_{1}}+H^{(n_{1})})\oplus\cdots\oplus(\lambda_{k}I_{n_{\ell}}+H^{(n_{\ell})}),

where

H^{(n_{j})}=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}0&1&&\cdots&0\\
0&0&1&\cdots&0\\
&&\vdots&&\\
0&0&\cdots&0&1\\
0&0&\cdots&0&0\end{array}\right)_{n_{j}\times n_{j}}

(each such matrix will be called in the sequel a
shifting matrix
). Notice also that, since
A_{k}
is not a radial matrix, we have
n_{j}>1
for at least one index
j
.

We divide the proof in different steps.

Step 1. The tuple
I(A)
.-
In the situation above, for
i=1,...,n
, denote by
\gamma_{i}(A_{k})\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
the degree, as a polynomial in
\lambda
, of the g.c.d. of the family of all
i\times i
minors of the characteristic matrix
A_{k}-\lambda I_{n}
. In particular
\gamma_{n}(A_{k})=n
.
For such a system
A
(when
A_{k}
has a single eigenvalue), we define the following tuple of non-negative integer numbers

I(A):=(\gamma_{1}(A_{k}),\cdots,\gamma_{n}(A_{k}),q-k).

Remark that each
\gamma(A_{k})
depends only on the conjugation class of
A_{k}
. We need to recall also the following result on Linear Algebra (see in Wasow
[
19
, Lemma 19.4]
for a proof) concerning the behaviour of the values
\gamma_{i}
for a perturbation of the matrix
A_{k}
in the case where
A_{k}
has at least two blocks.

Lemma 11
.

Consider
A_{k}
as a block-diagonal Jordan matrix
A_{k}=\left(A_{k}^{(ij)}\right)
, where the diagonal blocks are given by
A_{k}^{(ii)}=\lambda_{k}I_{n_{i}}+H^{(n_{i})}
. Assume that
\ell\geq 2
. With the same block structure, let
G=\left(G^{(ij)}\right)\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K)
be a block-lower-triangular matrix with the same diagonal blocks as
A_{k}
(that is,
G^{(ii)}=A_{k}^{(ii)}
and
G^{(ij)}=0
if
i<j
). Then we have

\gamma_{t}(G)\leq\gamma_{t}(A_{k})\;\;\;\mbox{ for any }t\in\{1,2,...,n\}

and the inequality is strict for at least one
t
if
G\neq A_{k}
(that is,
G^{(ij)}\neq 0
for at least a pair
(i,j)
with
i>j
).

Step 2. Special matrices and choice of
g\in\mathbb{Q}
.-

Given a tuple
\sigma=(n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{\ell})
of positive integers as above, denote
m_{j}:=\sum_{u=1}^{j}n_{u}
for
j=1,...,\ell
. A matrix
T=(t_{uv})\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K)
will be called a
special matrix of type
\sigma
if
t_{uv}=0
for every
(u,v)
such that
u\not\in\{m_{1},m_{2},...,m_{\ell}\}
.

A result in Wasow (
[
19
, Lemme 19.2]
) assures that for any
N\geq k+1
there exists a regular polynomial gauge transformation
\Psi_{P_{N}}
, where
P_{N}
is of degree
N
and
P_{N}(0)=I_{n}
, such that the transformed system
B=\Psi_{P_{N}}(A)
satisfies that all coefficients of the truncation
J_{N}(B)
are special matrices of type
\sigma=(n_{1},n_{2},\cdots,n_{\ell})
, where the
n_{j}
are the sizes of Jordan blocks of the matrix
A_{k}
. We will put
N=N(n,q,k)
and, renaming
B
again as the system
A
, we may assume the following assumption

(*)   For any
k+1\leq j\leq N
, the coefficient
A_{j}
is a special matrix of type
\sigma
.

Write now

x^{q+1}A(x)=A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots=\sum_{i=0}^{k-1}\lambda_{i}x^{i}I_{n}+x^{k}\overline{A},\;\mbox{ with }\overline{A}=A_{k}+O(x)=(\overline{a}_{uv}(x)).

Put
\alpha_{uv}:=\nu(\overline{a}_{uv}(x))
. Notice that
\alpha_{uv}\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 0}
for any
u,v
, that
\alpha_{uu}>0
for any
u
and that
\alpha_{u,u+1}=0
for at least one index
u\in\{1,...,n\}
.

Definition 12
.

With the conditions above, we define the shearing order (of
A
) as the rational number

g=g(A):=\min\left(\{\frac{\alpha_{uv}}{1+u-v}\,:\,u>v\}\cup\{\alpha_{uu}\,:\,1\leq u\leq n\}\cup\{q\}\right).

Geometrically, as discussed in Wasow
[
19
, pp.104-105]
, the shearing order is the smallest abscissa in which a line of the following family

\{y=(v-u)x+\alpha_{uv}\}_{v<u}\cup\{y=\alpha_{uu}\}_{u}\cup\{y=q-k\}

cuts the diagonal
y=x
.

Notice also that
g\in\mathbb{Q}
and that
0<g\leq q-k
. Write
g=\frac{h}{r}
where
h,r
are positive integers with no common factor.

The name “shearing” comes from Wasow’s name for the gauge “ramified” transformation with matrix
S_{g}=\text{diag}\,(1,x^{g},x^{2g},...,x^{(n-1)g})
. In our definitions, such a transformation has only sense if
g=h
is an integer. Otherwise, we perform instead the composition
\Psi_{S_{h}}\circ R_{r}(=:``\Psi_{S_{g}}")
.

Step 3. The case where
g=h
is integer
.
We consider the monomial diagonal gauge transformation
\Psi_{S_{h}}
where
S_{h}=\text{diag}\,(1,x^{h},x^{2h},...,x^{(n-1)h})
. If
A=(a_{uv}(x))
then the transformed system
B:=\Psi_{S_{h}}[A]=S_{h}^{-1}AS_{h}-S_{h}^{-1}S^{\prime}_{h}
writes as

(4)

B=\left(\begin{array}[]{cccc}a_{11}(x)&x^{h}a_{12}(x)&\cdots&x^{(n-1)h}a_{1n}(x)\\
x^{-h}a_{21}(x)&a_{22}(x)&\cdots&x^{(n-2)h}a_{2n}(x)\\
&\vdots&&\\
x^{-(n-1)h}a_{n1}(x)&x^{-(n-2)h}a_{12}(x)&\cdots&a_{nn}(x)\end{array}\right)+x^{-1}K,

where
K
is a constant diagonal matrix (in fact
K=\text{diag}(0,h,2h,\dots,(n-1)h)
).

By the choice of the shearing order
g=h
, one can see that we can write
B(x)=x^{-(q+1)}(\widetilde{B}_{0}+x\widetilde{B}_{1}+\cdots)
, where the
\widetilde{B}_{j}
are constant matrices satisfying

(a)

\widetilde{B}_{j}
is diagonal for
j\in\{0,1,...,k+h-1\}
, with
\widetilde{B}_{j}=A_{j}
if
j<k
,
\widetilde{B}_{k}=\lambda_{k}I_{n}
and
\widetilde{B}_{j}=0
if
k<j<k+h
.

(b)

The entries of
\widetilde{B}_{k+h}
above the principal diagonal coincide with those of the matrix
H^{(n_{1})}\oplus\cdots\oplus H^{(n_{\ell})}
. Moreover,
\widetilde{B}_{k+h}
has a non-zero entry on or above the principal diagonal, except, possibly, if
h=q-k
.

(c)

For any
M\geq k
, the matrix
\widetilde{B}_{M}
depends only on the truncation
J_{M+(n-1)h}(A)
.

As a consequence, the Poincaré rank after the shearing transformation does not increase. That is,

(5)

q(B)\leq q.

Moreover, using (a) and (b), and denoting
q^{\prime}=q(B)
,
k^{\prime}=k(B)
, we have that if
q^{\prime}=q
then
k^{\prime}=k+h
, while, if
q^{\prime}<q
then
k=0
,
q^{\prime}=q-h
and
k^{\prime}=0
. In any case, we deduce

(6)

q^{\prime}-k^{\prime}<q-k\;\;\mbox{ and }\;\;N(n,q^{\prime},k^{\prime})+(n-1)h\leq N(n,q,k).

Thus, using the property (c) and the second part of equation (
6
), it will suffice to prove item (i)’ of Theorem
3
for the system
B
.

Write, accordingly to our main notations,
B(x)=x^{-(q^{\prime}+1)}\left(B_{0}+xB_{1}+\cdots\right)
. We will be done if
B
is in the trivial case
q^{\prime}-k^{\prime}=0
or if
q^{\prime}-k^{\prime}>0
but
B_{k^{\prime}}
has at least two different eigenvalues. Thus, assume that
q^{\prime}-k^{\prime}>0
and that
B_{k^{\prime}}
has a single eigenvalue, say equal to
\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}
. It is enough, by recurrence, to prove in this case that the tuple
I(B)
satisfies
I(B)<I(A)
.

Notice that
B_{k^{\prime}}=\widetilde{B}_{k+h}
, after the discussion above concerning the value of
q^{\prime}
and
k^{\prime}
. Consider the matrix
B_{k^{\prime}}
written in blocks according to the Jordan structure of
A_{k}
:

B_{k^{\prime}}=\left(B_{k^{\prime}}^{(ij)}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq\ell},\;\;B_{k^{\prime}}^{(ij)}\in\mathcal{M}_{n_{j}\times n_{j}}(K).

Using the property (b), we have that
B_{k^{\prime}}=H^{(n_{1})}\oplus\cdots\oplus H^{(n_{1})}+T
where
T
is a lower triangular matrix. In particular, for
i\in\{1,...,\ell\}
, since
\mbox{Spec}(B_{k^{\prime}}^{(ii)})=\{\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}\}
, the matrix
B_{k^{\prime}}^{(ii)}-\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}I_{n_{i}}
is nilpotent and of rank
n_{i}-1
. This implies that the Jordan decomposition of
B_{k^{\prime}}^{(ii)}
has a single block and hence
B_{k^{\prime}}^{(ii)}
is conjugated to
H^{n_{i}}+\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}I_{n_{i}}
. Consequently,
B_{k^{\prime}}
is conjugated to a block-lower-triangular matrix with diagonal blocks equal to
H^{n_{i}}+\lambda_{k^{\prime}}^{\prime}I_{n_{i}}
, those of the matrix
A_{k}
. Thus, either
B_{k^{\prime}}
is conjugated to
A_{k}
(when
\ell=1
) or we are in the situation where we can apply Lemma
11
. From this lemma, and taking into account the first part of equation (
6
), we deduce that
I(B)<I(A)
, as wanted.

Step 4. The case where
g
is not an integer
. Assume now that the shearing order
g
is not an integer and put
g=\frac{h}{r}
where
h,r
are positive integers without common factor and such that
r\geq 2
. We remark that the condition that
g
is not an integer implies that
g
is given by one of the quotients
\alpha_{uv}/(1-u-v)
in Definition
12
, while
\alpha_{uu}>g
for any
u
, as well as
q-k>g
.

Consider the ramification
R_{r}
and put
\widetilde{A}=R_{r}(A)
. This system has Poincaré rank and radiality index equal to
q(\widetilde{A})=rq
and
k(\widetilde{A})=rk
, respectively. Moreover, if we denote
\widetilde{N}=N(n,rq,rk)
and
N=N(n,q,r)
, then
\widetilde{N}=rN
but the truncation
J_{\widetilde{N}}(\widetilde{A})
only depends on
J_{N}(A)
, showing that if item (i)’ of Theorem
3
holds for
\widetilde{A}
then it also holds for
A
.

We notice moreover that the first non-radial coefficient of
\widetilde{A}
is equal to
A_{k}
and that
\widetilde{A}
also satisfies assumption (*) with respect to the same type
\sigma=(n_{1},...,n_{\ell})
given by the Jordan structure of
A_{k}
. On the other hand, the shearing order of
\widetilde{A}
is given by
g(\widetilde{A})=rg=h
, a natural number (the new valuations
\alpha_{uv}
for
\widetilde{A}
are all multiplied by
r
). Hence we are, for
\widetilde{A}
, in the situation of step 3. However, the last component
q-k
of the tuple
I(\widetilde{A})
has increased and we can not conclude automatically.
To finish, we put
B=\Psi_{S_{h}}(\widetilde{A})
, we denote by
q^{\prime},r^{\prime}
the Poincaré rank and the radiality index of
B
, respectively, and, writing
B=x^{-(q^{\prime}+1)}\left(B_{0}+xB_{1}+\cdots\right)
, we show again that assuming that
q^{\prime}-k^{\prime}>0
and that
B_{k^{\prime}}
has a single eigenvalue, we have
I(B)<I(A)
.

Write
B_{k^{\prime}}=\left(B^{(ij)}_{k^{\prime}}\right)
as a block matrix in the same block structure as
A_{k}
. We have (cf. property (b) in step 3 above) that
B_{k^{\prime}}
is block-lower triangular with diagonal blocks given by
B^{(ii)}_{k^{\prime}}=H^{(n_{i})}+T_{i}
, where
T_{i}
is a lower triangular matrix of size
n_{i}
. Moreover, we must have that all elements in the diagonal of
T_{i}
are zero (since all values
\alpha_{uu}
for
\widetilde{A}
are greater than
h=rg
, as mentioned above). On the other hand, by assumption (*), the entries of
T_{i}
on any row except possibly the last one are also zero. On the other hand, since we have assumed that each block
B^{(ii)}_{k^{\prime}}
, as the entire matrix
B_{k^{\prime}}
, has a single eigenvalue, being the trace of
B^{(ii)}_{k^{\prime}}
equal to zero, such eigenvalue is equal to zero. That is, each
B^{(ii)}_{k^{\prime}}
is nilpotent. But this implies that
T_{i}=0
and we conclude that

(7)

B^{(ii)}_{k^{\prime}}=H^{(n_{i})},\;\;\mbox{ for any }i\in\{1,2,...,\ell\}.

Furthermore, by the definition of the shearing order of
\widetilde{A}
, we must have at least one non-zero entry below the principal diagonal of the matrix
B_{k^{\prime}}
. Together with the equation (
7
), this implies that
\ell>1
and that
B_{k^{\prime}}
is a matrix
G
in the situation of Lemma
11
with a non-diagonal block
B^{(ij)}_{k^{\prime}}\neq 0
for at least one pair
(i,j)
with
i>j
. We conclude that
\gamma_{t}(B_{k^{\prime}})<\gamma_{t}(A_{k})
for at least one index
t
and thus
I(B)<I(A)
, as wanted.

Let us show now the statement in Remark
5
, (c) concerning this item (i). In other words, we have to justify that all along the above process for obtaining a
TRS
-form of degree
0
, the Poincaré rank can only increase after a ramification and never after a shearing transformation
\Psi_{S_{g}}
with
g\in\mathbb{N}
.

First, notice that this property goes through the induction arguments discussed in paragraph
2.1.1
. Thus, we may assume that we are in the case where
A_{k}
has a unique eigenvalue. If the shearing order
g=g(A)
is an integer, the required property is already established by equation (
5
). On the contrary, if the shearing order is
g=h/r
, with
r>1
and
h
not divisible by
r
, the procedure consists in the shearing
\Psi_{S_{h}}
after the ramification
R_{r}
. We conclude using the same equation (
5
) once we observe that the system
\widetilde{A}:=R_{r}[A]
has as first non-radial term the same matrix
A_{k}
and satisfies
g(\widetilde{A})=h\in\mathbb{N}
(just check that
\widetilde{A}
satisfies already the property (*) and that the new values
\alpha_{uv}
in Definition
12
are the old ones multiplied by
r
).

2.2.
Proof of Theorem
3
, (ii). Getting
(TRS)
-form of higher degree
\mu
when
C
is non-resonant

A proof of this item (ii) when
q=0
can be found for instance in Wasow
[
19
, Thm. 5.1]
, Coddington-Levinson
[
9
, Thm. 4.1, Ch IV]
or Balser
[
2
, Thm. 5]
. The general case
q>0
is not really different from those references because the exponential part
D(x)
, being diagonal and commuting with
C
will play no essential role to achieve (ii). For the sake of completeness, let us indicate here the steps of the general proof.

Assume that the system
A
is already in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form with principal part
D(x)+x^{q}C
and write it in the form

x^{q+1}Y^{\prime}=\left(A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots\right)Y,

so that

x^{q+1}J_{q}A=A_{0}+\cdots+x^{q}A_{q}=D(x)+x^{q}C.

We will need the following result from Linear Algebra (which is actually the core of the proof of the Splitting Lemma
10
). See
[
19
, Thm. 4.1]
for a proof.

Lemma 13
.

Fix a field
K
and let
R,S
be two square matrices with coefficients in
K
and of sizes
n\times n
and
m\times m
, respectively. Assume that
R,S
have no common eigenvalue in the algebraic closure
\overline{K}
of
K
. Then the linear map

X\mapsto RX-XS
from
\mathcal{M}_{n\times m}(K)
to itself is an isomorphism.

Up to reorder the variables of
Y
, we write

(8)

D(x)=D^{11}(x)\oplus D^{22}(x)\oplus\cdots\oplus D^{\ell\ell}(x),

where
D^{jj}(x)
is a radial matrix of size
n_{j}
(that is
D^{jj}(x)=Q_{j}(x)I_{n_{j}}
, where
Q_{j}(x)\in K[x]_{q-1}
) for each
j=1,..,\ell
, and such that
D^{ii}(x)\neq D^{jj}(x)
if
i\neq j
. Write also the residual matrix
C
, as well as any coefficient matrix
A_{j}
with
j\geq q+1
, as block matrices
C=(C^{uv})_{1\leq u,v\leq\ell}
and
A_{j}=(A_{j}^{uv})_{1\leq u,v\leq\ell}
, where
C^{uv},A_{j}^{uv}\in\mathcal{M}_{n_{u}\times n_{v}}(K)
. Using the commutativity property
[D(x),C]=0
, the assumption
D^{ii}(x)\neq D^{jj}(x)
for
i\neq j
and Lemma
13
, we obtain that

C^{uv}=0\;\;\mbox{ if }u\neq v.

We eliminate all coefficients
A_{j}
for
j\geq q+1
by means of a regular formal transformation in two steps.

First step. We eliminate all non-diagonal blocks
\{A_{j}^{uv}\}_{u\neq v}
.

We proceed by induction with respect to
q=q(A)
. If
q=0
(that is,
D(x)=0
), the block structure is the trivial one and there are no non-diagonal blocks, so that there is nothing to prove.

Suppose that
q>0
. We consider a coarser block structure

(9)

D(x)=\overline{D}^{11}(x)\oplus\cdots\oplus\overline{D}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{1}}(x)

in such a way that
\overline{D}^{jj}(0)
is a radial constant matrix for every
j=1,..,\ell_{1}
and
\overline{D}^{jj}(0)\neq\overline{D}^{ii}(0)
if
i\neq j
. Notice that, up to reordering, each block
\overline{D}^{jj}(x)
is formed by several of the diagonal blocks of the decomposition (
8
).
We consider a matrix
\overline{T}(x)\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K[[x]])
of the form
\overline{T}(x)=I_{n}+x^{q+1}\overline{T}_{q+1}+x^{q+2}\overline{T}_{q+2}+\cdots
such that, writing each coefficient
\overline{T}_{j}=(\overline{T}_{j}^{uv})
in the same block structure as the one in (
9
), we have
\overline{T}_{j}^{uu}=0
for any
u\in\{1,...,\ell_{1}\}
. The regular gauge transformation
\psi_{\overline{T}}
transforms the system
A
into a system
\overline{B}:=\psi_{\overline{T}}[A]
which is also in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form with the same principal part
D(x)+x^{q}C
. Write
x^{q+1}\overline{B}=D(x)+x^{q}C+\sum_{j\geq q+1}x^{j}\overline{B}_{j}
, and, for any
j
, consider each coefficient of
A
or
\overline{B}
written in the block structure (
9
) as
A_{j}=(\overline{A}_{j}^{uv})
and
\overline{B}_{j}=(\overline{B}_{j}^{uv})
, respectively. We obtain recursively, for any couple of indices
u,v\in\{1,...,\ell_{1}\}
with
u\neq v
:

(10)

\begin{array}[]{c}\overline{B}_{q+1}^{uv}=\overline{A}_{q+1}^{uv}+\overline{D}_{0}^{uu}\overline{T}_{q+1}^{uv}-\overline{T}_{q+1}^{uv}\overline{D}_{0}^{vv}\\
\overline{B}_{q+2}^{uv}=\overline{A}_{q+2}^{uv}+\overline{D}_{0}^{uu}\overline{T}_{q+2}^{uv}-\overline{T}_{q+2}^{uv}\overline{D}_{0}^{vv}+\theta^{uv}_{2}(A_{0},A_{1},\overline{T}_{q+1})\\
\vdots\\
\overline{B}_{q+j}^{uv}=\overline{A}_{q+j}^{uv}+\overline{D}_{0}^{uu}\overline{T}_{q+j}^{uv}-\overline{T}_{q+j}^{uv}\overline{D}_{0}^{vv}+\theta^{uv}_{j}(\{A_{s},\overline{T}_{q+s}\}_{s<j})\\
\vdots\end{array}

where each
\theta^{uv}_{j}
is a polynomial matrix acting on the block entries of the explicitly indicated matrices. Using Lemma
13
with
R=\overline{D}^{uu}_{0}
and
S=\overline{D}^{vv}_{0}
, we obtain recursively blocks
\overline{T}^{uv}_{q+1},\overline{T}^{uv}_{q+2},\ldots
(i.e. the entire matrix
\overline{T}(x)
) so that
\overline{B}^{uv}_{j}=0
for every
j\geq q+1
and for every
u,v\in\{1,...,\ell_{1}\}
with
u\neq v
. In other words,
\overline{B}=\psi_{\overline{T}}[A]
is a block-diagonal formal system
\overline{B}=\overline{B}^{11}\oplus\cdots\oplus\overline{B}^{\ell_{1}\ell_{1}}
. Now, we consider separately each one of the subsystems
\overline{B}^{jj}
, for
j=1,...,\ell_{1}
. By construction, this system is in (TRS)-form of degree
0
and with exponential part equal to
\overline{D}^{jj}(x)=\overline{D}^{jj}_{0}+\widetilde{D}^{jj}(x)
. Put
\widetilde{B}^{jj}:=\overline{B}^{jj}-x^{-(q+1)}\overline{D}^{jj}_{0}
, a system with Poincaré rank equal to
q(\widetilde{B}^{jj})=q-\nu(\widetilde{D}^{jj}(x))<q
, and also in (TRS)-form of degree
0
and exponential part equal to
x^{-\nu_{j}}\widetilde{D}^{jj}(x)
, where
\nu_{j}:=\nu(\widetilde{D}^{jj}(x))
. Consider a block decomposition

(11)

\widetilde{D}^{jj}(x)=\widetilde{D}^{jj,1}(x)\oplus\cdots\oplus\widetilde{D}^{jj,\ell^{j}}(x)

analogous to that of
D(x)
in equation (
8
); that is, each block in (
11
) is a radial matrix and two such blocks are different. By recurrence on
q
, there is a formal regular gauge transformation with matrix
T^{j}(x)=I+\cdots
(with size equal to the size of the system
\overline{B}^{jj}
) such that
\Psi_{T^{j}}[\widetilde{B}^{jj}]
is block-diagonal in the same block structure as (
11
). Taking into account that
\overline{D}^{jj}_{0}
is a radial matrix, the same is true for
\Psi_{T^{j}}[\overline{B}^{jj}]
. Put

T(x)=(T^{1}(x)\oplus\cdots\oplus T^{\ell_{1}})\cdot\overline{T}(x)=I+\cdots\in\mathcal{M}_{n\times n}(K[[x]]).

Then,
B:=\Psi_{T}[A]
is a system in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form with the same principal part as
A
and block-diagonal with respect to the concatenation of the different block-structures given by (
11

D(x)=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell_{1}}\widetilde{D}^{jj}(x)=\bigoplus_{j=1}^{\ell_{1}}\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\ell^{j}}\widetilde{D}^{jj,i}.

We are done, since this last decomposition is, up to reordering, the same as the initial one (
8
).

Step 2. Eliminating the diagonal terms.

Consider the block-diagonal system
B=\Psi_{T}[A]=B^{11}\oplus\cdots\oplus B^{\ell\ell}
as in the step 1, in the block structure given by (
8
). We propose a second formal regular gauge transformation associated to

of the form

U=(I_{n_{1}}+U^{11})\oplus\cdots\oplus(I_{n_{\ell}}+U^{\ell\ell}),

where
U^{uu}
has size
n_{u}
and
U^{uu}(0)=0
for any
u
. Write
U^{uu}=xU^{uu}_{1}+x^{2}U^{uu}_{2}+\cdots
. The transformed system

is again block-diagonal and
x^{q+1}J_{q}E=D(x)+x^{q}C
. We write
E=E^{11}\oplus\cdots\oplus E^{\ell\ell}
and
x^{q+1}E^{uu}=D^{uu}+x^{q}C^{uu}+x^{q+1}E^{uu}_{q+1}+\cdots
for each block
E^{uu}
. Taking into account that each block
D^{uu}
is radial and hence commutes with any matrix, we obtain recursively for any
u\in\{1,...,\ell\}
and for any

(12)

E^{uu}_{q+j}=B^{uu}_{q+j}+C^{uu}U^{uu}_{j}-U^{uu}_{j}(C^{uu}+jI_{n_{u}})+\eta^{uu}_{j}(\{U^{uu}_{s},B^{uu}_{q+s}\}_{s<j}),

where, similarly as in equation (
10
),
\eta^{uu}_{j}
is a polynomial acting over the expressed matrices, with
\eta^{uu}_{1}=0
. Using again Lemma
13
with matrices
R=C^{uu}
and
S=C^{uu}+jI_{n_{u}}
(notice that these two matrices have no common eigenvalue by the non resonance condition for

), we can construct recursively from (
12
) the coefficients
U^{uu}_{q+1},U^{uu}_{q+2},\ldots
such that

E^{uu}_{q+j}=0

for any
u\in\{1,...,\ell\}
and for any
j\geq 1
.

The conclusion from the two steps above is that the composition
\Psi_{UT}=\Psi_{U}\circ\Psi_{T}
satisfies

\Psi_{UT}[A]=x^{-(q+1)}(D(x)+x^{q}C).

To finish, notice from the expression of the transform of a system by a regular gauge transformation that, for every
\mu\geq 0
,
the
(q+\mu)
-jet of
\Psi_{UT}[A]
only depends on the
(q+\mu)
-jet of the product
UT
(and on
J_{q+\mu}A
).
Thus, we put
P^{\mu}:=J_{q+\mu}(UT)
and Theorem
3
, (ii) follows.

2.3.
Proof of Theorem
3
, (iii). Getting a non-resonant residual matrix

A proof of this item, for

, can be found in the same references cited in paragraph
2.2
above.

We assume that
A
is in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
form with exponential part equal to
D(x)
and residual part equal to
C
.

Radial case.-
We consider first the case where
D(x)
is a radial matrix; i.e.,
D(x)=Q(x)I_{n}
, where
Q(x)
is a polynomial (including the case
q=0
for which
D(x)=0
).

Take the partition
{\rm Spec}(C)=\Omega_{1}\cup\cdots\cup\Omega_{r}
of the spectrum of
C
in such a way that two eigenvalues differ by a non-zero integer number if and only if they belong both to some
\Omega_{j}
. Denote
\Omega_{j}=\{\lambda^{j}_{1},...,\lambda^{j}_{s_{j}}\}
and assume that the indices are chosen so that

Re(\lambda^{j}_{1})>Re(\lambda^{j}_{2})>\cdots>Re(\lambda^{j}_{s_{j}})
in case
s_{j}>1
.
Let

(13)

m(C):=\sum_{\{j\,:\,s_{j}>1\}}\lambda_{1}^{j}-\lambda^{j}_{s_{j}}\in\mathbb{N}.

Notice that
m(C)>0
if and only if
C
is resonant. In order to prove item (iii) in this case, we show that, if
m(C)>0
, there is a constant regular transformation
\Psi_{P_{0}}
and a monomial diagonal transformation
\Psi_{S}
such that the transformed system
\Psi_{S}\circ\Psi_{P_{0}}[A]
has Poincaré rank equal to
q
, the same exponential part
D(x)
and a residual matrix

satisfying
m(C^{\prime})<m(C)
.

The matrix
P_{0}
is chosen so that
\overline{C}:=P_{0}^{-1}CP_{0}
is block diagonal of the form

(14)

\overline{C}=(C^{11}\oplus\cdots\oplus C^{1s_{1}})\oplus\cdots\oplus(C^{r1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C^{rs_{r}}),

where
{\rm Spec}(C^{ji})=\{\lambda^{j}_{i}\}
. The transformed system
\overline{A}=\Psi_{P_{0}}[A]=P_{0}^{-1}AP_{0}
has the same exponential part
D(x)
(since this is a radial matrix) and residual matrix equal to
\overline{C}
. Put
x^{q+1}\overline{A}=\sum_{l\geq 0}\overline{A}_{l}x^{l}
and use the block structure given by equation (
14
) for each coefficient
\overline{A}_{l}=(\overline{A}_{l}^{uv})
, where
u,v
run in the set
\{(ji)\,:\,j=1,..,r,i=1,...,s_{j}\}\cup\{\Lambda\}
. Assume for instance that
\Omega_{1}
has at least two elements. The we consider the diagonal monomial matrix

(15)

S:=xI_{t}\oplus I_{n-t},

where
t
is equal to the size of
C^{11}
. Consider
B:=\Psi_{S}[\overline{A}]
and write
x^{q+1}B=\sum_{l\geq 0}B_{l}x^{l}
and
B_{l}=(B_{l}^{uv})
with the same block structure as in (

). A calculation shows that

(16)

\begin{array}[]{ll}B_{l}^{uu}=\overline{A}_{l}^{uu},\mbox{ if }l\neq q,&B_{l}^{uv}=\overline{A}_{l}^{uv},\mbox{ if }u\neq(11)\mbox{ and }v\neq(11),\\
B_{l}^{(11)v}=x^{-1}\overline{A}_{l}^{(11)v},\mbox{ if }v\neq(11),&B_{l}^{u(11)}=x\overline{A}_{l}^{u(11)},\mbox{ if }u\neq(1,1),\\
B_{q}^{(11)(11)}=\overline{A}_{q}^{(11)(11)}-I_{t}.&\end{array}

In particular, we obtain
q(B)=q
,
B
is in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form with the same exponential part
D(x)
and the residual matrix
C^{\prime}=B_{q}
is upper triangular with respect to the block structure
(B_{q}^{uv})
and with diagonal equal to

{\rm diag}(C^{\prime})=((C^{11}-I_{t})\oplus\cdots\oplus C^{1s_{1}})\oplus\cdots\oplus(C^{r1}\oplus\cdots\oplus C^{rs_{r}})\oplus C^{\Lambda}.

We deduce that

and we are done.

General case.-
Notice that, in preceding case, the degree of a polynomial gauge transformation needed to obtain a non-resonant residual matrix can be bounded by
m(C)
(cf. Remark
5
, (e)). Moreover, such transformation depends only on the truncation
J_{m(C)+q}(A)
. We use this remark and Step 1 in paragraph
2.2
to reduce the general case to the precedent case. More precisely, consider the decomposition of the exponential part in radial matrices
D(x)=D^{11}(x)\oplus\cdots\oplus D^{\ell\ell}(x)
, as in equation (
8
). Consider also
C=C^{11}\oplus\cdots\oplus C^{\ell\ell}
, decomposed into the same block structure. Put

m:=\max\{m(C^{ii}),\>\,i=1,...,\ell\}.

Taking into account Step 1 in the proof of (ii), there is regular polynomial gauge transformation
\psi
such that the truncation
J_{m+q}(\psi[A])
decomposes into several systems of smaller size

J_{m+q}(\psi[A])=A^{11}(x)\oplus\cdots\oplus A^{\ell\ell}(x),

where the principal part of
A^{jj}(x)
is

. In particular,
A^{jj}(x)
is in the radial case treated above, so that there is a finite composition of constant regular and monomial diagonal transformations
\varphi_{j}
such that
\varphi_{j}[A^{jj}]
has the same exponential part as

and a non-resonant residual matrix. We conclude that the composition
\phi=(\varphi_{1}\oplus\cdots\oplus\varphi_{\ell})\circ\psi
transforms
A
into a system in
(TRS)^{q}_{0}
-form with non-resonant residual matrix. On the other hand, as we have noticed in the radial case, the degree of each
\varphi_{j}
is bounded by
m(C^{jj})
, and hence the degree of the polynomial gauge transformation
\phi
can be bounded by
2m
. This ends the proof of item (iii) of Theorem
3
and completes the statement in Remark
5
, (e).
\square

One final comment on how to conclude Remark
5
, (d) in what it concerns for this item (iii). We need to take into account that the diagonal monomial transformations used in the process are only those of the form
\Psi_{S}
, where

is as in equation (

). As we have already observed from equations (

), such transformations preserve the Poincaré rank.

3.
Proof of the Real Turrittin’s Theorem

In this section we fix a real closed field
K
and we prove Theorem
7
, the real version of Turrittin’s Theorem on polynomial normal forms. As mentioned, the formal statement Theorem
8
will be a consequence of it.

We make use of the monomorphism of
K
-algebras defined in paragraph
1.2
. That is, for any
m\in\mathbb{N}
, we consider

\Theta_{m}:\mathcal{M}_{m}(\overline{K})\to\mathcal{M}_{2m}(K),\;\;(c_{uv})\mapsto(\Lambda_{c_{uv}}).

and (with the same name), its extension to a morphism of
K
-algebras from
\mathcal{M}_{m}(L_{\overline{K}})
to
\mathcal{M}_{2m}(L_{K})
sending an
m
-dimensional system
B=x^{-(q+1)}\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}B_{j}
with coefficients in
\overline{K}
to the system
\Theta_{m}(B)=x^{-(q+1)}\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}\Theta_{m}(B_{j})
.
Notice that
\Theta_{m}
preserves the Poincaré rank but not necessarily the radiality index of the system. On the other hand, one can check easily that
\Theta_{m}
commutes with the gauge transformations and with ramifications. To be precise, if
B,P\in\mathcal{M}_{m}(L_{\overline{K}})
with
\text{det}(P)\neq 0
, we have

(17)

\Theta_{m}(\Psi_{P}[B])=\Psi_{\Theta_{m}(P)}[\Theta_{m}(B)],

and, if
r
is a natural non-zero number, then

(18)

\Theta_{m}\circ R_{r}=R_{r}\circ\Theta_{m}.

3.1.
Propagating a
\mathbb{C}
-matrix to higher order coefficients

The key result for the proof of Theorem
7
is the following one.

Proposition 14
.

Consider a system
A\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(L_{K})
with Poincaré rank equal to
q
and written as
A=x^{-(q+1)}\left(A_{0}+xA_{1}+\cdots\right)
. Let
k
be the radiality index of
A
and assume that
k<q
and that the spectrum of
A_{k}
in
\overline{K}
consists in a pair of conjugated values
a\pm ib
with
a,b\in K
and
b\neq 0
(thus in particular
n=2m
is even). Then there exists a formal regular gauge transformation
\Psi_{T}
, where
T\in\mathcal{M}_{n}(K[[x]])
, such that the transformed system
B=\Psi_{T}[A]
is a
\mathbb{C}
-system. Moreover, writing
B=x^{-(q+1)}(\sum_{j\geq 0}x^{j}B_{j})
, we have
A_{j}=B_{j}\in KI_{n}
for
j=0,1,...,k-1
and for any
\mu\geq k
, the truncation
J_{\mu}B
only depends on
J_{\mu-k}T
and
J_{\mu}A
. In other words, if
\widetilde{A}
is another system with
q(\widetilde{A})=q
and satisfying
J_{\mu}\widetilde{A}=J_{\mu}A
, then
J_{\mu}(\Psi_{J_{\mu-k}T}[\widetilde{A}])=J_{\mu}B
.

The proof of Proposition
14
has a big similarity with the one of the Splitting Lemma (cf. Lemma
10
) or of Theorem
3
, (ii). This time, it is based on the following result for
\mathbb{C}
-matrices of size two:

Lemma 15
.

Let
\lambda\in\overline{K}\setminus K
and let
\Lambda_{\lambda}=\Theta_{1}(\lambda)
be the corresponding
\mathbb{C}
-matrix of size
2
. Given
S\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(K)
an arbitrary matrix with coefficients in
K
, there exists a matrix
X\in\mathcal{M}_{2}(K)
such that

\Lambda_{\lambda}X-X\Lambda_{\lambda}+S

is a
\mathbb{C}
-matrix.

Proof.

Put
\lambda=a+ib
with
a,b\in K
and
b\neq 0
and write
S=(s_{ij})
and
X=(x_{ij})
with
1\leq i,j\leq 2
. Computing we have

(19)

\Lambda_{\lambda}X-X\Lambda_{\lambda}+S=\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}-u+s_{11}&v+s_{12}\\
v+s_{21}&u+s_{22}\end{array}\right),

where
u=b(x_{12}+x_{21})
and
v=b(x_{11}+x_{22})
. The matrix in (
19
) is a
\mathbb{C}
-matrix iff we have
-u+s_{11}=u+s_{22}
and
v+s_{12}=-(v+s_{21})
. These two last equations have solutions in
u,v
once we are given the entries
s_{ij}
of
S
and, taking into account that
b\neq 0
, we conclude the lemma.

\square

Proof of Proposition
14
.-
First, using a real canonical form of

, there is a non-singular matrix

with entries in

Up to replacing

, a

-matrix. The radial part

Assume that

, we can assume that

, we use this to conclude item (i’) for the original system

.

Suppose now that
\text{Spec}(A_{k})=\{\lambda_{k},\overline{\lambda_{k}}\}

\lambda_{k}\in\overline{K}

Case 1:
\lambda_{k}\neq\overline{\lambda_{k}}
.
Notice that

to the system

. Notably, let
\Psi_{T}

\mathbb{C}

. Apply Theorem

: we get a natural number

and gauge transformations

, either regular polynomial or monomial diagonal (with coefficients in

) such that, putting

, we have

(a)

The system

(TRS)^{\tilde{q}}_{0}
-form for some
\tilde{q}\geq 0
.

(b)

Being
N=N(n,q,k)
, if
\overline{E}
is another system with
q(\overline{E})=q

, the system

is also in
(TRS)^{\tilde{q}}_{0}

.

Now, for any

, we put

\widetilde{\varphi}_{i}
is a gauge transformation with coefficients in

, either regular polynomial or diagonal monomial. Denote by

, (i’). Using the property (b) above and equations (

) and (

\Theta_{m}(\psi[J_{N}(\overline{B})])=\widetilde{\psi}[J_{N}(\Theta_{m}(\overline{B}))]=\widetilde{\psi}[J_{N}(B)]

Claim.-
If

Applying this claim to

It remains to show the Claim. It is a consequence of the property (b) above (using (

) and (

)) in the case that

is a
\mathbb{C}
-system). To be convinced that it is true for any system

in the hypothesis of the statement, we notice, using the description of a general gauge transformation or a ramification, that there exists some integer

The proof can be done similarly to the case where

Suppose that the system
