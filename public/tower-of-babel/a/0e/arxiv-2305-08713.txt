Low-lying resonances for infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces with long closed geodesics
Louis Soares

Abstract
. We consider sequences ( X n ) n ∈ ℕ (X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}} of coverings of convex cocompact hyperbolic surfaces X X with Euler characterictic χ ⁡ ( X n ) \chi(X_{n}) tending to − ∞ -\infty as n → ∞ . n\to\infty. We prove that for n n large enough, each X n X_{n} has an abundance of “low-lying” resonances, provided the length of the shortest closed geodesic on X n X_{n} grows sufficiently fast. When applied to congruence covers we obtain a bound that improves upon a result of Jakobson, Naud, and the author in [ 16 ] . Our proof uses the wave 0-trace formula of Guillopé–Zworski [ 13 ] together with specifically tailored test-functions with rapidly decaying Fourier transform.

1.
Introduction and Statement of Results

In the last few decades, much effort has been dedicated to the study of quantum resonances, see the expository article of Zworski
[
33
]
for a general introduction. In this paper, we are interested in infinite-area, “convex cocompact” hyperbolic surfaces, that is, quotients
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
that can be decomposed as

(1)

X=K\cup F_{1}\cup\cdots\cup F_{m},

where
K
is a compact surface with geodesic boundary and
F_{1},\dots,F_{m}
are finitely many funnel ends glued to
K
. We refer to Borthwick’s book
[
3
]
for an introduction to the spectral theory of infinite-area hyperbolic surfaces. The classical
L^{2}
-spectrum for the Laplace–Beltrami operator
\Delta_{X}
on
X
is rather sparse. In particular,
\Delta_{X}
has only finitely many
L^{2}
-eigenvalues. The main spectral data of interest are the
resonances
of
X
, which are defined as the poles of the meromorphic continuation of the resolvent operator

(2)

R_{X}(s)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}(\Delta_{X}-s(1-s))^{-1}\colon C_{c}^{\infty}(X)\to C^{\infty}(X).

In the sequel, we denote by
\mathcal{R}(X)
the set of resonances for
X
, repeated according to multiplicities, see §
2
for details. We denote by
\delta\in(0,1)
the Hausdorff dimension of the limit set of
X
, which is equal to the
critical exponent
of the Poincaré series of
\Gamma
. There are infinitely many resonances, all of which are contained in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\delta
. The vertical line
\mathrm{Re}(s)=\delta
has exactly one simple resonance at
s=\delta.
In general, it is a notoriously difficult problem to precisely locate resonances.

From the point of view of physics, resonances determine the ways in which
X
can “vibrate”. Indeed, each
s\in\mathcal{R}(X)
corresponds to a stationary solution

(3)

\psi_{s}(x,t)=e^{(s-\frac{1}{2})t}\phi_{s}(x),\quad x\in X,\,t\in(0,\infty)

of the automorphic wave equation

(4)

\left(\Delta_{X}+\partial_{t}-\frac{1}{4}\right)\psi_{s}=0.

In this view, the real part of
s
determines the decay amplitude and the imaginary part determines the oscillation frequency of the solution
\psi_{s}.

Resonances not corresponding to
L^{2}
-eigenvalues are precisely those that lie in the half-plane
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.
We study the counting function

\mathsf{N}(\sigma,T;X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\left\{s\in\mathcal{R}(X):\text{$\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}$ and $|\mathrm{Im}(s)|<T$}\right\},

where resonances are counted with multiplicities. Guillopé–Lin–Zworski
[
9
]
proved the upper fractal Weyl bound

\mathsf{N}(\sigma,T;X)\ll T^{1+\delta},\quad T\to\infty.

The first estimate of this form was established by Sjöstrand in his pioneering work on semi-classical Schrödinger operators
[
31
]
. The following non-exhaustive list of papers
[
19
,
33
,
9
,
6
,
23
,
7
,
32
,
29
,
24
,
22
,
21
]
provides similar fractal Weyl bounds. All of these results support a well-known conjecture in open chaotic systems and quantum chaos, known as the
fractal Weyl law
. In our setting, this conjecture says that for all
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
negative enough we should have

\mathsf{N}(\sigma,T;X)\asymp T^{1+\delta},\quad T\to\infty.

For numerical evidence for this law we refer to
[
2
]
and the references therein. While upper bounds for counting functions of resonances have become well understood, lower bounds of the same strength have remained elusive. Guillopé–Zworski
[
13
]
proved that for any
0<\epsilon<\frac{1}{2}
and
\sigma<-\frac{1}{2\epsilon}
we have

\mathsf{N}(\sigma,T;X)\neq O(T^{1-\epsilon}).

Jakobson–Naud
[
15
]
proved a lower bound with an explicit dependence on
\delta
, but the growth rate is only logarithmic unless
\Gamma
is an arithmetic group.

The aim of this paper is to give lower bounds for
\mathsf{N}(\sigma,T;X)
for small values of
T
and
\sigma
in terms of geometric quantities of
X
. We denote by
\ell_{0}(X)
the length of the shortest closed geodesic on
X
and by
\vol_{0}(X)
the volume of the compact core of
X
. Using the Gauss-Bonet formula for the area of hyperbolic polygons we deduce that if
X
has a decomposition as in (
1
), then

\vol_{0}(X)=\vol(K)=-2\pi(m-1)=-2\pi\chi(X),

where
\chi(X)
is the Euler characteristic of
X
, see also
[
3
, Lemma 10.3]
. Our main result is the following:

Theorem 1.1

(Main theorem)
.

Let
X
be a non-elementary, infinite-area, convex cocompact hyperbolic surface with
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
. Let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of finite-degree coverings of
X
such that the following hold true as
n\to\infty
:

•

\vol_{0}(X_{n})\to\infty
and

•

there exists some
A>0
such that
\ell_{0}(X_{n})\geqslant(A-o(1))\log(\vol_{0}(X_{n})).

Then for all
\alpha>1
and
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
there are constants
c=c(\alpha,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})>0
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(\alpha,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})
such that for all
n\geqslant n_{0}
we have

\mathsf{N}\left(\delta-\frac{1}{A}-\epsilon^{\prime},(\log\log(\vol_{0}(X_{n})))^{\alpha};X_{n}\right)\geqslant c\vol_{0}(X_{n})^{A(\delta-\frac{1}{2}-\epsilon)}.

We remark that any finite-degree cover of
X
has the same critical exponent
\delta,
that is, the point
s=\delta
is a common leading resonance for all finite-degree covers of
X
.

Our next goal is to specialize the main result to congruence covers of arithmetic surfaces. When
\Gamma
a subgroup of
\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we define the principal congruence subgroup of
\Gamma
of level
n
by

\displaystyle\Gamma(n)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\left\{\text{$\gamma\in\Gamma$ : $\gamma\equiv I$ mod $n$}\right\},

and we write
X(n)=\Gamma(n)\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
for the associated covering. Infinite-area congruence covers have attracted a lot of attention due to their applications in number theory and graph theory, see
[
8
,
4
,
25
]
. In these papers, uniform spectral gaps (that is, uniform resonance-free strips) are established. Our next result is a somewhat complementary statement, stating that congruence covers have an abundance of low-lying resonances. More concretely, we have the following:

Theorem 1.2
.

Assume that
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is as in Theorem
1.1
and that
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then for all
\alpha>1
and
\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}>0
there are
c=c(\alpha,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})>0
and
n_{0}=n_{0}(\alpha,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime})\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all positive integers
n
with
\gcd(n,n_{0})=1
we have

(5)

\mathsf{N}\left(\delta-\frac{3}{4}-\epsilon^{\prime},(\log\log n)^{\alpha};X(n)\right)\geqslant cn^{4(\delta-\frac{1}{2})-\epsilon}.

This result strengthens Theorem 1.4 in the work of Jakobson, Naud, and the author
[
16
]
in several ways.

The proof of our main Theorem
1.1
uses the wave 0-trace formula of Guillopé–Zworski
[
13
]
together with specifically tailored test-functions
\varphi
with rapidly decaying Fourier transform
\widehat{\varphi}(z)
as
|\mathrm{Re}(z)|\to\infty
. The construction of these test-functions uses the same harmonic analysis ingredient as in
[
16
]
, which in the present paper corresponds to Lemma
2.1
below. Theorem
1.2
can be deduced from the main theorem in conjunction with lower bounds for the minimal length of closed geodesics on congruence covers. We conjecture that Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
hold true for
all
geometrically finite hyperbolic surfaces. The restriction to the convex cocompact case is needed because we also require the following bound for the global counting function in
[
28
]
:

\mathsf{N}(r;X_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathcal{R}(X):|s|\leqslant r\}\ll\vol_{0}(X_{n})r^{2},

where the implied constant depends only on the base surface
X
. This is currently only known for covers of convex cocompact surfaces.

The paper is organized as follows: in Section
2
we gather the necessary preliminaries needed for our main Theorem
1.1
, which is then proven in Section
3
. In Section
4
we prove Theorem
1.2
.

2.
Preliminaries

2.1.
Hyperbolic geometry

Let us recall some basic facts about hyperbolic surfaces, referring the reader to
[
3
]
for a comprehensive discussion. One of the standard models for the hyperbolic plane is the Poincaré half-plane

\mathbb{H}^{2}=\{x+iy\in\mathbb{C}\ :\ y>0\}

endowed with its standard metric of constant curvature
-1
,

ds^{2}=\frac{dx^{2}+dy^{2}}{y^{2}}.

The group of orientation-preserving isometries of
(\mathbb{H}^{2},ds)
is isomorphic to
\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It acts on the extended complex plane
\overline{\mathbb{C}}=\mathbb{C}\cup\{\infty\}
(and hence also on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
) by Möbius transformations

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R}),\quad z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}}\Longrightarrow\gamma(z)=\frac{az+b}{cz+d}.

An element
\gamma\in\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is either

•

hyperbolic
if
|\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)|>2
, which implies that
\gamma
has two distinct fixed points on the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
,

•

parabolic
if
|\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)|<2
, which implies that
\gamma
has precisely one fixed point on
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}
, or

•

elliptic
if
|\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)|=2
, which implies that
\gamma
has precisely one fixed point in the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
.

2.2.
Hyperbolic surfaces and Fuchsian groups

Every hyperbolic surface
X
is isometric to a quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
, where
\Gamma
is a
Fuchsian
group, that is, a discrete subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{PSL}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. A Fuchsian group
\Gamma
is called

•

torsion-free
if it contains no elliptic elements,

•

non-cofinite
if the quotient
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
has infinite-area,

•

non-elementary
if it is generated by more than one element, and

•

geometrically finite
if it is finitely generated, which is equivalent with
\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
being geometrically and topologically finite.

All the Fuchsian groups
\Gamma
considered in this paper satisfy all the above conditions. The
limit set

\Lambda
of
X
is defined as the set of accumulation points of all orbits of the action of
\Gamma
on
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. Under the above conditions, the limit set is a Cantor-like fractal subset of the boundary
\partial\mathbb{H}^{2}\cong\mathbb{R}\cup\{\infty\}
with Hausdorff dimension
\delta
lying strictly between
0
and
1
.

Furthermore,
\Gamma
is called
convex cocompact
if it is finitely generated and if it contains neither parabolic nor elliptic elements. This is equivalent with the
convex core
of
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
being compact, or with
X
being geometrically finite and having no cusps.

•

•

The pure point spectrum is finite and contained in the interval

If

In view of these facts, the resolvent operator

-eigenvalue of

. From Guillopé–Zworski

are called the

of
X
; the multiplicity

of a resonance

is defined as the rank of the residue operator of

at

m_{X}(\zeta)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\rank\Res_{s=\zeta}R_{X}(s).

We denote by

the multiset of resonances of

repeated according to multiplicities. Resonances are contained in the half-plane

Note that resonances

2.4.
The global resonance counting function

The distribution of resonances and eigenvalues of finite-area hyperbolic surfaces is fairly well-understood. In particular, the classical Selberg trace formula leads to a Weyl law for the global resonance counting function of the form

\mathsf{N}(r;X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathcal{R}(X):|s|\leqslant r\}\sim\frac{\vol(X)}{2\pi}r^{2},\quad r\to\infty,

proven in this case by Müller
[
20
]
and Parnovski
[
26
]
. Here and henceforth, resonances are counted with multiplicities. In the infinite-area case, much less is known. Guillopé and Zworski
[
10
,
12
]
proved that for any geometrically finite hyperbolic surface
X
there are constants
C_{i}=C_{i}(X)>0
,
i=1,2
, and
r_{0}=r_{0}(X)
such that for all
r>r_{0}
we have

(8)

C_{1}r^{2}\leqslant\mathsf{N}(r;X)\leqslant C_{2}r^{2}.

In this paper we need to understand the behavior of
\mathsf{N}(r;X_{n})
for families of coverings
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of
X
as
\vol_{0}(X_{n})\to\infty.
Unfortunately, the proof method in
[
10
,
12
]
yields only ineffective constant
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
in (
8
) with no clear dependence on the geometry of
X
. However, when
X
is a convex cocompact surface, it is known from Pohl–Soares
[
28
]
that there are constants
r_{0}>0
and
C>0
, depending only on
X
, such that for any family
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
of finite-degree coverings of
X
and for all
r\geqslant r_{0}
we have

(9)

C^{-1}\vol_{0}(X_{n})r^{2}\leqslant\mathsf{N}(r;X_{n})\leqslant C\vol_{0}(X_{n})r^{2}.

This result follows essentially from an upper bound for
twisted
Selberg zeta functions
Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)
(sometimes also known as
L
-functions): there exists some
C>0
, depending only on
X=\Gamma\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
, such that for all finite-dimensional, unitary representations
\rho\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{U}(V)
, we have

(10)

\log|Z_{\Gamma}(s,\rho)|\leq C\dim(\rho)\langle s\rangle^{2},\quad\langle s\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{1+|s|^{2}}.

We will actually only use the upper bound in (
9
) in the present paper. This result generalizes and improves upon an earlier result of Jakobson–Naud
[
14
]
who have studied the specific case of congruence covers. Unfortunately, the estimate (
10
), which relies on the thermodynamic formalism for Selberg zeta functions, has so far only been established for convex cocompact groups
\Gamma.
We should also mention the sharp upper bound for
\mathsf{N}(r;X)
in terms of geometric quantities of
X
due to Borthwick
[
1
]
.

2.5.
A harmonic analysis result

We now state and re-prove a harmonic analysis result from the paper
[
16
]
. Roughly speaking, it says that we can find a smooth and compactly supported function
\psi
whose Fourier transform
\widehat{\psi}(z)
has almost exponential decay as
|\mathrm{Re}(z)|\to\infty
. This can be seen as the complex analogue of the classical Beurling–Malliavin Multiplier theorem. Our proof is an almost verbatim repetition of the proof given in
[
16
]
which in turn closely follows the construction given in
[
17
, Chapter 5, Lemma 2.7]
.

Lemma 2.1

(Lemma 6.2 in
[
16
]
)
.

For all
\beta>1
there are constants
c_{1}>0
and
c_{2}>0
and a positive smooth function
\psi
supported in
[-1,1]
such that

|\widehat{\psi}(z)|\leqslant c_{1}e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}\exp\left(-c_{2}\frac{|\mathrm{Re}(z)|}{(\log|\mathrm{Re}(z)|)^{\beta}}\right)

for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|\mathrm{Re}(z)|\geqslant 2.

Proof.

Let
(\mu_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be a sequence of positive real numbers summing up to
1
:

(11)

\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\mu_{j}=1.

For
k\in\mathbb{Z}
and
n\in\mathbb{N}
set

\varphi_{n}(k)=\prod_{j=1}^{n}\frac{\sin(\mu_{j}k)}{\mu_{j}k},\quad\varphi(k)=\prod_{j=1}^{\infty}\frac{\sin(\mu_{j}k)}{\mu_{j}k}

and consider the corresponding Fourier series

\psi_{n}(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi_{n}(k)e^{ikx},\quad\psi(x)=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\varphi(k)e^{ikx}.

The rapid decay of
\varphi(k)
implies that
\psi
is a
C^{\infty}
-function on the interval
[-2\pi,2\pi].
On the other hand,
\psi_{n}
converges uniformly to
\psi
as
n\to\infty
. Moreover, we have

(12)

\psi_{n}=g_{1}\ast g_{2}\ast\ldots\ast g_{n},

where
\ast
denotes convolution operator and each
g_{j}
is defined as

g_{j}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{2\pi}{\mu_{j}}&\text{ if }|x|\leqslant\mu_{j},\\
0&\text{ else. }\end{cases}

From (
12
) and (
11
) we deduce that
\psi
is a positive function with support in
[-1,1]
. We extend
\psi
outside
[-1,1]
by zero.

In what follows, we write
x=\mathrm{Re}(z).
Integration by parts and Cauchy–Schwarz Inequality yield for all
z\in\mathbb{C}
with
|x|\geqslant 2
and all
m\geqslant 1
the bound

(13)

|\widehat{\psi}(z)|\leqslant\frac{e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}}{|x|^{m}}\|\psi^{(m)}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}.

Using Plancherel’s Theorem, we obtain

(14)

\|\psi^{(m)}\|_{L^{2}(-1,1)}^{2}=\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}k^{2m}\varphi(k)^{2}\leqslant C\prod_{j=1}^{m+1}\mu_{j}^{-2},

where
C>0
is some absolute constant. Combining (
13
) and (
14
) gives

(15)

|\widehat{\psi}(z)|\leqslant C\frac{e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}}{|x|^{m}}\prod_{j=1}^{m+1}\mu_{j}^{-1}.

Now fix
\kappa>0
and we let
(\widetilde{\mu}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
be the sequence defined by

(16)

\widetilde{\mu}_{1}=1,\quad\widetilde{\mu}_{j}=\frac{1}{j\log(j)^{1+\kappa}}\text{ for }j\geqslant 2.

Note that the sequence
(\widetilde{\mu}_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
converges for all
\kappa>0
, so we may consider the normalized sequence
\mu_{j}=c\widetilde{\mu}_{j}
, where
c=c(\kappa)
is chosen so that the
\mu_{j}
’s add up to one. Using (
15
) we get

\displaystyle|\widehat{\psi}(z)|

\displaystyle\leqslant C\cdot c^{m}\frac{e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}}{|x|^{m}}\prod_{j=2}^{m+1}j\log(j)^{1+\kappa}

\displaystyle\leqslant\frac{e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}}{|x|^{m}}m!e^{(1+\kappa)m\log\log(m)+O(m)}.

Using Stirling’s formula for the factorial
m!=(1+o(1))\sqrt{2\pi m}(\frac{m}{e})^{m}
and choosing

m=\left\lfloor\frac{|x|}{(\log|x|)^{1+2\kappa}}\right\rfloor

yields (after some calculations)

(17)

|\widehat{\psi}(z)|\leqslant e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}\exp\left(-\kappa\frac{(\log\log|x|)|x|}{(\log|x|)^{1+2\kappa}}(1+o(1))\right),\quad x\to\infty.

This completes the proof of Lemma
2.1
with
\beta=1+2\kappa
.
∎

3.
Proof of Theorem
1.1

We now give the proof of our main Theorem
1.1
. Let
X
be an non-elementary, infinite-area, convex cocompact hyperbolic surface with critical exponent
\delta>\frac{1}{2}
, and let
(X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
be a family of finite-degree coverings of
X
. Recall that we consider the resonance counting function

\mathsf{N}(\sigma,t;X)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\left\{s\in\mathcal{R}(X):\text{$\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}$ and $|\mathrm{Im}(s)|<t$}\right\}.

The starting point of our proof is the wave 0-trace formula of Guillopé–Zworski
[
13
]
(see also
[
3
, Corollary 11.5]
). When applied to non-elementary, convex cocompact, hyperbolic surfaces
Y
this formula takes the following form: for all test-functions
\varphi\in C_{c}^{\infty}((0,\infty))
we have

(18)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(Y)}\widehat{\varphi}\left(i\left(s-\frac{1}{2}\right)\right)=-\frac{\vol_{0}(Y)}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cosh(\frac{t}{2})}{\sinh(\frac{t}{2})^{2}}\varphi(t)dt+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(Y)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell\varphi(k\ell)}{2\sinh(\frac{k\ell}{2})},

where

•

\mathcal{R}(Y)
is the set of resonances of
Y
, repeated with multiplicities,

•

\mathcal{L}(Y)
is the length spectrum, that is, the set of lengths of the primitive periodic geodesics on
Y
repeated with multiplicities,

•

\vol_{0}(Y)
is the 0-volume of
Y
, which equals the volume of the compact core of
Y
, and

•

\widehat{\varphi}
is the Fourier transform of
\varphi
defined by (
6
).

By substituting
\varphi(t)
by
\varphi(t)e^{-t/2}
in (
18
) we obtain the following more convenient version of the trace formula:

(19)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(Y)}\widehat{\varphi}\left(is\right)=-\frac{\vol_{0}(Y)}{4\pi}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\cosh(\frac{t}{2})}{\sinh(\frac{t}{2})^{2}}e^{-\frac{t}{2}}\varphi(t)dt+\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(Y)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell\varphi(k\ell)}{1-e^{-k\ell}}.

Resonances always come in conjugate pairs: if
s\in\mathcal{R}(Y)
, then also
\overline{s}\in\mathcal{R}(Y)
. This implies that whenever
\varphi
is real-valued, then the sum over resonances on the left of (
19
) is real. If we assume further that
\varphi\geqslant 0
, then the integral on the right is positive, so we obtain the inequality

(20)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(Y)}\widehat{\varphi}\left(is\right)\leqslant\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{L}(Y)}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell\varphi(k\ell)}{1-e^{-k\ell}}.

Recall that Lemma
2.1
furnishes, for any
\beta>1
, a non-negative function
\varphi=\varphi_{\beta}\in C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathbb{R})
with
\supp(\varphi)=[-1,1]
and

(21)

|\widehat{\varphi}(z)|\ll_{\beta}e^{|\mathrm{Im}(z)|}\exp\left(-c\frac{|\mathrm{Re}(z)|}{(\log|\mathrm{Re}(z)|)^{\beta}}\right),\quad|\mathrm{Re}(z)|\geqslant 2.

For the rest of the proof we fix
\alpha>\beta>1
and we let
\varphi=\varphi_{\beta}
be as above. Now we apply (
20
) to
Y=X_{n}
and to the family of test functions

(22)

\varphi_{L,\eta}(x)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\varphi\left(\frac{x-L}{\eta L}\right),

where
L
and
\eta
are positive parameters that we will choose further below, depending on
n
. By construction,
\varphi_{L,\eta}
is a positive smooth function with support equal to

\supp(\varphi_{L,\eta})=[(1-\eta)L,(1+\eta)L].

For the rest of this proof we assume that
0<\eta<1
and that

(1+\eta)L<\ell_{0}(X_{n}).

This condition guarantees that the right of (
20
) equals zero, so

(23)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})}\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)\leqslant 0.

Now we focus on the sum on the left. Given parameters
\sigma\in\mathbb{R}
and
K>0
(to be chosen below) we partition the complex plane into the following four mutually disjoint regions:

•

R_{1}=\{\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}\},

•

R_{2}=\{\text{$\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}$ and $|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\leqslant K$}\},

•

R_{3}=\{\text{$\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}$ and $|\mathrm{Im}(s)|>K$}\},
and

•

R_{4}=\{\mathrm{Re}(s)<\sigma\}.

We divide the sum on the left of (
23
) accordingly, writing

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})}\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)=S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}+S_{4},

where

S_{j}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})\cap R_{j}}\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right),\quad j\in\{1,2,3,4\}.

Note that we have suppressed the dependence of the sums
S_{j}
on
n,\eta,L,
and
K
since we assume these parameters to be fixed until the end of the proof. From (
23
) we have

S_{1}+S_{2}+S_{3}+S_{4}\leqslant 0.

As we will see below,
S_{1}>0
, so applying the triangle inequality gives

S_{1}\leqslant|S_{2}|+|S_{3}|+|S_{4}|.

Our goal now is to give a lower bound for
S_{1}
and upper bounds for
S_{2},S_{3},
and
S_{4}
.

Note that by basic properties of the Fourier transform we have

\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)=\eta Le^{sL}\widehat{\varphi}(i\eta Ls),

which we will use repeatedly in the sequel.

Lower bound for
S_{1}

Recall from the discussion in §
2.3
that resonances in the half-plane
R_{1}=\{\mathrm{Re}(s)>\frac{1}{2}\}
are related to eigenvalues
\lambda
of the Laplacian via
\lambda=s(1-s)
:

\mathcal{R}(X_{n})\cap R_{1}=\Omega(X_{n})\subset[\frac{1}{2},\delta].

It is easy to check that for all real
\xi
we have
\widehat{\varphi}\left(i\xi\right)\gg_{\beta}e^{-\xi}
for some implied constant depending only on
\beta
. Hence, we have for all
s\in\Omega(X_{n})

\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)=\eta Le^{sL}\widehat{\varphi}(i\eta Ls)\gg_{\beta}\eta Le^{sL(1-\eta)},

and therefore,

(24)

S_{1}=\sum_{s\in\Omega(X_{n})}\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)\gg_{\beta}\eta L\sum_{s\in\Omega(X_{n})}e^{sL(1-\eta)}.

Note that
\lambda_{0}=\delta(1-\delta)
is the common base eigenvalue for all the surfaces
X_{n}
(which by assumption are finite degree coverings of
X
). Thus,
\delta\in\Omega(X_{n})
for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
, whence

S_{1}\gg_{\beta}\eta Le^{\delta L(1-\eta)}.

Upper bound for
S_{2}

It is easy to verify that for all
s\in\mathbb{C}

(25)

|\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)|=|\int_{L(1-\eta)}^{L(1+\eta)}\varphi_{L,\eta}(x)e^{sx}dx|\ll_{\beta}\eta Le^{L(1+\sign(\mathrm{Re}(s))\eta)\mathrm{Re}(s)}.

Since all
s\in R_{2}
have
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
we immediately obtain

|S_{2}|\ll_{\beta}\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}\mathsf{N}(\sigma,K;X_{n}).

Upper bound for
S_{3}

Here we use the decaying properties of the Fourier transform of
\varphi
. Assume furthermore that the parameters
\eta,L,K
are chosen so that

(26)

\eta LK\geqslant 2.

Then for all
s\in R_{3}
we have
\eta L|\mathrm{Im}(s)|\geqslant 2
, so we may use the bound (
21
) to obtain

|\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)|=\eta Le^{\mathrm{Re}(s)L}|\widehat{\varphi}(i\eta Ls)|\ll_{\beta}\eta Le^{\mathrm{Re}(s)L+\eta L|\mathrm{Re}(s)|}\exp\left(-c\frac{\eta L|\mathrm{Im}(s)|}{(\log(\eta L|\mathrm{Im}(s)|))^{\beta}}\right).

Since all
s\in R_{3}
have
\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
we get

(27)

|\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)|\ll_{\beta}\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}f_{\beta}(\eta L|\mathrm{Im}(s)|),

where
f_{\beta}
is the function

(28)

f_{\beta}\colon[2,\infty)\to\mathbb{R},\quad f_{\beta}(t)=\exp\left(-c\frac{x}{(\log x)^{\beta}}\right).

Therefore,

(29)

|S_{3}|\ll_{\beta}\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}\sum_{s\in R_{3}}f_{\beta}(\eta L|\mathrm{Im}(s)|).

To estimate the sum on the left, recall from (
9
) that we have the following bound for all
r\geqslant r_{0}(X)
:

\mathsf{N}(r;X_{n})\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\#\{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n}):|s|<r\}\ll\vol_{0}(X_{n})r^{2}.

Observe that
\sigma\leqslant\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
and
|\mathrm{Im}(s)|<t
together imply
|s|\leqslant\sqrt{t^{2}+\max\{|\sigma|,\frac{1}{2}\}^{2}}
, so we have the rather crude bound

(30)

\mathsf{N}(\sigma,t;X_{n})\leqslant\mathsf{N}\left(\sqrt{t^{2}+\max\{|\sigma|,\frac{1}{2}\}^{2}};X_{n}\right)\ll_{\sigma}\vol_{0}(X_{n})t^{2}.

The remaining sum on the right of (
29
) can be estimated with a Stieljes integral over the resonance counting function
\mathsf{N}(\sigma,t;X)
. Using integration by parts and the bound (
30
) yields

\displaystyle\sum_{s\in R_{3}}f_{\beta}(\eta L|\mathrm{Im}(s)|)

\displaystyle\ll\int_{K}^{\infty}f_{\beta}(\eta Lt)\,d\mathsf{N}(\sigma,t;X_{n})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{N}(\sigma,K;X_{n})f_{\beta}(\eta LK)-\eta T\int_{K}^{\infty}\mathsf{N}(\sigma,t;X_{n})f_{\beta}^{\prime}(\eta Lt)\mathrm{d}t

\displaystyle=\mathsf{N}(\sigma,K;X_{n})f_{\beta}(\eta LK)-\int_{K\eta T}^{\infty}\mathsf{N}(\sigma,\frac{u}{\eta L};X_{n})f_{\beta}^{\prime}(u)\mathrm{d}u

\displaystyle\ll_{\sigma}\vol_{0}(X_{n})\left(K^{2}f_{\beta}(\eta LK)-\frac{1}{\eta^{2}L^{2}}\int_{K\eta L}^{\infty}u^{2}f_{\beta}^{\prime}(u)\mathrm{d}u\right)

It is an exercise to verify that for all
x\geqslant 2

(31)

-\int_{x}^{\infty}u^{2}f_{\beta}^{\prime}(u)du\ll_{\beta}x^{3}f_{\beta}(x).

Thus, returning to (
29
), we obtain

\displaystyle|S_{3}|

\displaystyle\ll_{\beta,\sigma}\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}\sum_{s\in R_{3}}f_{\beta}(\eta L\,\mathrm{Im}(s))

\displaystyle\ll_{\beta,\sigma}\vol_{0}(X_{n})\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}\left(K^{2}+\eta LK^{3}\right)f_{\beta}(\eta LK).

Upper bound for
S_{4}

Using the definition of the Fourier transform and repeated integration by parts, we obtain for all
m\in\mathbb{N}

|\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)|\ll_{m}\eta L\frac{e^{\mathrm{Re}(s)L(1+\sign(\mathrm{Re}(s))\eta)}}{\langle s\rangle^{m}},\quad\langle s\rangle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{def}}}{{=}}\sqrt{1+|s|^{2}}.

Thus, since all
s\in R_{4}
have
\mathrm{Re}(s)\leqslant\sigma
we get

|S_{4}|\leqslant\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})\cap R_{4}}|\widehat{\varphi}_{L,\eta}\left(is\right)|\ll\eta Le^{\sigma L(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta)}\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})}\langle s\rangle^{-m}.

We now take
m=3
and we estimate the sum on the right with a Stieltjes integral

(32)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})}\langle s\rangle^{-3}=\int_{0}^{\infty}\langle s\rangle^{-3}d\mathsf{N}(r;X_{n})

over the global resonance counting function
\mathsf{N}(r;X_{n})
. Using the bound (
9
) yields

(33)

\sum_{s\in\mathcal{R}(X_{n})}\langle s\rangle^{-3}\ll\vol_{0}(X_{n})

and therefore,

(34)

|S_{4}|\ll\vol_{0}(X_{n})\eta Le^{\sigma L\left(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta\right)}.

Putting everything together

Recall that

S_{1}\leqslant|S_{2}|+|S_{3}|+|S_{4}|.

Combining the above estimates and writing
v_{n}=\vol_{0}(X_{n})
for notational convenience gives

(35)

\displaystyle\eta Le^{\delta L(1-\eta)}

\displaystyle\ll_{\beta,\sigma}\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}\mathsf{N}(\sigma,K;X_{n})+

\displaystyle\qquad+v_{n}\eta Le^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}\left(K^{2}+\eta LK^{3}\right)f_{\beta}(\eta LK)+v_{n}\eta Le^{\sigma L(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta)},

provided the parameters
\eta,L,
and
K
satisfy the conditions

0<\eta<1,\quad(1+\eta)L<\ell_{0}(X_{n}),\quad\eta LK\geqslant 2.

Dividing both sides of (
35
) by
\eta L
gives

(36)

\displaystyle e^{\delta L(1-\eta)}

\displaystyle\ll_{\beta,\sigma}e^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}\mathsf{N}(\sigma,K;X_{n})

\displaystyle\qquad+v_{n}e^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}\left(K^{2}+\eta LK^{3}\right)f_{\beta}(\eta LK)+v_{n}e^{\sigma L(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta)}.

Recall that by assumption we have as
n\to\infty
:

•

v_{n}\to\infty
and

•

\ell_{0}(X_{n})\geqslant(A-o(1))\log v_{n}
for some constant
A>0
.

Now we fix some constant
\nu>0
satisfying
\alpha>\beta+\nu
and we choose

\eta=\frac{1}{(\log\log v_{n})^{\nu}},\quad K=(\log\log v_{n})^{\alpha},

and

L=(A-w(n))\log v_{n}

for some suitable function
w(n)\to 0
that ensures
(1+\eta)L<\ell_{0}(X_{n})
. Clearly, we also have
0<\eta<1
and
\eta LK\geqslant 2
for all
n
large enough. Recall that we have defined
f_{\beta}(x)=\exp\left(-c\frac{x}{(\log x)^{\beta}}\right)
, so one can check that for some constant
c^{\prime}>0
we have

f_{\beta}(\eta LK)\leqslant\exp\left(-c^{\prime}(\log v_{n})(\log\log v_{n})^{\alpha-\beta-\nu}\right).

That is, our choices of
\nu,\eta,L,
and
K
imply that
f_{\beta}(\eta LK)
decays faster as any polynomial in
v_{n}
. In particular, for any fixed
\sigma
, the second summand on the right of (
36
) tends to zero as
n\to\infty
:

v_{n}e^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta\max\{2|\sigma|,1\})}\left(K^{2}+\eta LK^{3}\right)f_{\beta}(\eta LK)\to 0.

Therefore, for all
n
large enough, the bound (
36
) reduces to

(37)

e^{\delta L(1-\eta)}\ll_{\beta,\sigma}e^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}\mathsf{N}(\sigma,K;X_{n})+v_{n}e^{\sigma L(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta)}.

Now we fix some
\epsilon^{\prime}>0
. By the above choices, we obtain that once

\sigma\leqslant\delta-\frac{1}{A}-\epsilon^{\prime},

the term
v_{n}e^{\sigma L(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta)}
becomes smaller than
e^{\delta L(1-\eta)}
for all
n
sufficiently large. Thus, upon rearranging (
37
), we obtain with
\sigma=\delta-\frac{1}{A}-\epsilon^{\prime}
the following lower bound:

\mathsf{N}\left(\delta-\frac{1}{A}-\epsilon^{\prime},K;X_{n}\right)\gg\frac{e^{\delta L(1-\eta)}-v_{n}e^{\sigma L(1+\sign(\sigma)\eta)}}{e^{\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}}\gg e^{\delta L(1-\eta)-\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}.

It is now easy to check that for any fixed
\epsilon>0
and for all sufficiently large
n
we have

e^{\delta L(1-\eta)-\frac{1}{2}L(1+\eta)}=e^{(\delta-\frac{1}{2})L-(\delta+\frac{1}{2})\eta L}\gg v_{n}^{A(\delta-\frac{1}{2})-\epsilon},

which completes the proof of Theorem
1.1
.

4.
Applying the main theorem to congruence covers

We now give the proof of Theorem
1.2
. For the rest of this section, we fix a non-elementary, convex cocompact subgroup
\Gamma\subset\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Note that the (principal) congruence subgroup
\Gamma(n)
is nothing else but the kernel
\ker(\pi_{n})
of the reduction modulo
n
map

\pi_{n}\colon\Gamma\to\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}),\quad\gamma\mapsto\gamma\mod n.

By the result of Bourgain–Varjú
[
5
]
there exists an integer
n_{0}
such that for all
n
with
\gcd(n,n_{0})=1
the map
\pi_{n}
is surjective. In what follows, we assume that
\gcd(n,n_{0})=1
, so
\Gamma(n)
is a finite-index subgroup of
\Gamma
of index

[\Gamma:\Gamma(n)]=\#\mathrm{SL}_{2}(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})\asymp n^{3},

and therefore, the Euler-characteristic of
X(n)
is

|\chi(X(n))|=[\Gamma:\Gamma(n)]|\chi(X)|\asymp n^{3}|\chi(X)|.

Using the Gauss-Bonnet relation
\vol_{0}(X)=-2\pi\chi(X)
leads to

(38)

\vol_{0}(X(n))\asymp n^{3},

with implied constant depending only on the base surface
X.

Now let us give a lower bound for the length of the shortest geodesic on
X(n)
. It is well-known that the set of closed geodesics on
Y\cong\Pi\backslash\mathbb{H}^{2}
is bijective to the set
[\Pi]
of conjugacy classes
[\gamma]
of the primitive hyperbolic elements
\gamma\in\Pi
, and the length of the geodesic corresponding to the conjugacy class of
\gamma\in\Pi
equals the displacement length
\ell(\gamma)
, see for instance
[
3
]
. Thus we need to give a lower bound for
\ell(\gamma)
for all hyperbolic
\gamma\in\Gamma(n)
. To that effect, recall that the displacement length is given by the relation

(39)

|\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)|=2\cosh\left(\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{2}\right)<2e^{\frac{\ell(\gamma)}{2}},

so it suffices to give a lower bound for the trace. For the rest of this section we write

\gamma=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix},\quad a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z},\quad ad-bc=1.

We write
I=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&1\end{pmatrix}
for the identity, and we put
\Gamma^{\ast}=\Gamma\smallsetminus\{I\}
. Suppose
\gamma\in\Gamma(n)^{\ast}
for some
n\geqslant 2
, or equivalently, suppose that
\gamma
satisfies the congruence

(40)

\gamma\equiv I\mod n.

Then, using an argument of Sarnak–Xue
[
30
]
, we can show that the trace of
\gamma
satisfies

(41)

\tr(\gamma)=a+d\equiv 2\mod n^{2}.

To see this, note that the congruence (
40
) implies that there are integers
a^{\prime},b^{\prime},c^{\prime},d^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
such that

a=a^{\prime}n+1
,
b=b^{\prime}n
,
c=c^{\prime}n
, and
d=d^{\prime}n+1
.

Furthermore, the condition
ad-bc=1
gives

(42)

1=(a^{\prime}d^{\prime}-b^{\prime}c^{\prime})n^{2}+(a^{\prime}+d^{\prime})n+1,

which forces

a^{\prime}+d^{\prime}=0\mod n.

But this implies that

\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)=(a^{\prime}+d^{\prime})n+2=2\mod n^{2},

as claimed. Now since
\gamma
is a hyperbolic element we have
|\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)|>2
, which when combined with (
41
) implies that for all
n\geqslant 2

(43)

|\tr(\gamma)|\geqslant n^{2}-2\gg n^{2}.

Combining this with (
39
) we obtain that

\ell_{0}(X(n))=\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(n)^{\ast}}\ell(\gamma)\geqslant 2\min_{\gamma\in\Gamma(n)^{\ast}}\log\left(\frac{|\mathrm{tr}(\gamma)|}{2}\right)\geqslant(4-o(1))\log(n).

Invoking the bound (
38
), this gives

\ell_{0}(X(n))\geqslant\left(\frac{4}{3}-o(1)\right)\log(\vol_{0}(X(n))).

Thus, we can apply our main Theorem
1.1
with
A=\frac{4}{3}
: by increasing
n_{0}
if necessary, we obtain that for all
n
coprime to
n_{0}
we have

\mathsf{N}\left(\delta-\frac{3}{4}-\epsilon^{\prime},(\log\log n)^{\alpha};X(n)\right)\gg_{\alpha,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}\vol_{0}(X(n))^{\frac{4}{3}(\delta-\frac{1}{2})-\epsilon}\gg_{\alpha,\epsilon,\epsilon^{\prime}}n^{4(\delta-\frac{1}{2})-\epsilon}.

This completes the proof of Theorem
1.2
.
