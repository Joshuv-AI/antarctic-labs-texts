A Subquadratic Time Algorithm for the Weighted k k -Center Problem on Cactus Graphs
Binay Bhattacharya, Sandip Das, Subhadeep Ranjan Dev

Abstract
The weighted k k -center problem in graphs is a classical facility location problem where we place k k centers on the graph, which minimize the maximum weighted distance of a vertex to its nearest center. We study this problem when the underlying graph is a cactus with n n vertices and present an O ⁡ ( n ​ log 2 ​ n ) O(n\log^{2}n) time algorithm for the same. This time complexity improves upon the O ⁡ ( n 2 ) O(n^{2}) time algorithm by Ben-Moshe et al. [ 2 ] , which is the current state-of-the-art.



1
Introduction

The facility location problem is a classical problem in operations research which has been well studied in both the graph and geometry settings. In graph, the vertices have some kind of demand, and the edges have some associated length. The length on the edges correspond to the distance between its two end vertices. The objective is to identify a certain number of locations on the edges and vertices of the graph where we can place facilities. The facilities are placed such that they satisfy the demands on the vertices of the graph and also minimize a certain predefined objective function.

In the
weighted
k
-center problem
the objective is to place
k
facilities which minimize the maximum weighted distance between a vertex and its nearest facility. Kariv and Hakimi
[
12
]
introduced this problem in 1979 and showed it to be NP-hard for general graphs. In fact, they proved a much stronger statement where they showed that the problem is NP-hard on planar graphs of maximum degree 3. However, the problem is shown to have efficient polynomial runtime when either the graph class is restricted or there is some bound on
k
. For example, in trees, Wang and Zhang
[
13
]
recently showed that the weighted
k
-center problem can be solved in
O(n\log n)
time, where
n
is the number of vertices in the tree. If
k
is a constant, Bhattacharya et al.
[
3
]
presented an optimal
O(n)
time solution for the problem on trees. For cactus, the current state of the art is an
O(n^{2})
time algorithm by Ben-Moshe et al.
[
2
]
. When
k
is restricted to be 1 or 2 then the runtime for cactus comes down to
O(n\log n)
and
O(n\log^{3}n)
time respectively
[
4
]
. For partial
p
-trees, Bhattacharya and Shi
[
5
]
showed that the weighted
k
-center problem can be solved in
O(n^{k}\log^{2p}n)
time, when the facilities are restricted to be placed only on the vertices.

The algorithm of Wang and Zhang
[
13
]
for the weighted
k
-center problem on trees uses Frederickson’s parametric search technique
[
7
]
to bring down the runtime from
O(n\log n+k\log^{2}n\log(n/k))

[
1
]
to
O(n\log n)
. We generalize Frederickson’s technique to present an
O(n\log^{2}n)
time algorithm for the problem. This is the first time that such a generalization could be achieved for a graph class other than trees and is the first subquadratic time algorithm for the problem. The feasibility test is the decision version of the problem where given a cost
\lambda
, the test returns whether the optimal cost (not known) is at most
\lambda
or not. We have also proposed an algorithm for the feasibility test with respect to the weighted
k
-center problem, which runs in optimal
O(n)
time.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we provide a formal definition of the weighted
k
-center problem and the tree representation of a cactus. We present some results on circular arc graphs which we use in the later sections. In Section
3
, we present our algorithm for the feasibility test. For any input
\lambda
, the test determines whether
\lambda\geq\lambda^{*}
or not, where
\lambda^{*}
is the optimal cost of the weighted
k
-center problem. In Section
4
, we prove some properties on linear arrangements whose results we use in Section
5
. In Section
5
, we define a cactus stem which is basically a subgraph of the cactus and a generalization of the (path) stem as defined in Frederickson
[
7
]
. We then present an
O(n\log^{2}n)
time algorithm to solve the weighted
k
-center problem on the cactus stem. We also show another technique with which we can get an
O(n\log n+kn\log n)
time algorithm. In Section
6
, we present our
O(n\log^{2}n+kn\log n)
time algorithm for cacti. Finally we conclude in Section
7
.

2.1
Problem definition

Let
G
be a graph with vertex set
V(G)
and edge set
E(G)
with
|V(G)|=n
. We say

is a

if no two cycles of

and

. We omit the subscript

if it is evident. For a point

and a vertex

, the

between the point

and the vertex

is equal to

. Given a set of points

of covering the vertices of

\max_{v\in V(G)}\left\{\min_{x\in X}\left\{w(v)\cdot d(x,v)\right\}\right\}

The objective of the

-center
problem in

is to find a set of
k
points

such that the cost of covering

is minimized. This cost is also called the

of the weighted
k
-center problem in

We say a point (or a center)

covers

. Extending this definition we say a set of centers

with cost

its nearest center in

. We sometimes do not explicitly mention

if it is well understood.

Feasibility Test

In this section, we present an algorithm which solves the decision version of the weighted
k
-center problem in time linear in the size of the cactus. The problem is commonly referred to as the
feasibility test
in literature. Given a cactus network
G
, let
\lambda^{*}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
be the optimal cost of the weighted
k
-center problem in
G
. For an input
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
, the feasibility test asks whether
\lambda\geq\lambda^{*}
, or not. Note that the optimal cost
\lambda^{*}
is an unknown value. If
\lambda\geq\lambda^{*}
, then we say
\lambda
is feasible, otherwise,
\lambda<\lambda^{*}
and we say
\lambda
is infeasible.

For a given
\lambda
, our algorithm works by placing a minimum number of centers
X
, such that every vertex in
G
is covered by some center in
X
with cost
\lambda
. If
|X|\leq k
, then
\lambda
is feasible; otherwise
|X|>k
, and
\lambda
is infeasible. The algorithm uses the tree representation
T
of
G
in order to fix a processing order on the vertices of
G
. Recall that
T
is rooted at the node
t_{r}
. We process the nodes of
T
in a bottom-up order, visiting the leaves first, and then visiting the nodes whose children have been processed.

3.3
Time complexity

Theorem 1
.

The feasibility test for the weighted

-center problem on cactus takes

Proof.

We traverse each node of

\Phi(u)

\operatorname{l-cost}(u,u_{e}),\operatorname{l-cost}(u,v_{e})\leq\lambda^{*}
. Since,
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,u_{e})
,
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,v_{e})\notin(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
this implies
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,u_{e})
,
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,v_{e})\leq\lambda
, for any
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. Therefore
\alpha
is empty.

If
\alpha^{*}
is nonempty and contains
u_{e}
then
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,u_{e}),\operatorname{l-cost}(v,u_{e})\geq\lambda^{*}
. Since,
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,u_{e})
,
\operatorname{l-cost}(v,u_{e})\notin(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
this implies
\operatorname{l-cost}(u,u_{e})
,
\operatorname{l-cost}(v,u_{e})\geq\lambda
, for any
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. Therefore,
\alpha
is also nonempty and contains
u_{e}
. The same can be proved with respect to the vertex
v_{e}
.

If
\alpha^{*}
is nonempty and does not contain either
u_{e}
or
v_{e}
then, there exists a point
y_{e}\in\alpha^{*}
such that
w(u)\cdot d(u,y_{e})=w(v)\cdot d(y_{e},v)
and for any two shortest paths
\pi_{y_{e}u}
and
\pi_{y_{e}v}
in
S
,
\pi_{y_{e}u}\cap\pi_{y_{e}v}=\varnothing
. This implies that
\operatorname{cost}(u,v,y_{e})\in R(S)
. Since,
e
does not belong to the major hemisphere of any cycle of
S
in between
u
and
v
,
\operatorname{cost}(u,v,y_{e})
is not a type 3(c) candidate value, as defined earlier, and so does not lie in the range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
. Since
y_{e}\in\alpha^{*}
,
\operatorname{cost}(u,v,y_{e})\geq\lambda^{*}
, which in turn implies for any
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
,
\operatorname{cost}(u,v,y_{e})\geq\lambda
. Therefore,
\alpha
is also nonempty and contains
y_{e}
. ∎∎

5.2
Stage 2

Now we show how to find
\lambda^{*}
if it is a type 3(c) candidate value of
R_{S}
. First we present a straightforward algorithm with running time
O(n^{2}\log n)
. Later in Section
5.3
and
5.4
we present two techniques by which we can improve the running time of this algorithm further.

For each cycle
C_{i}
of
S
we construct a path
P_{C_{i}}
as follows. Recall that
g_{i}
and
h_{i}
are the left and right hinge vertices of
C_{i}
, respectively. For each vertex
u_{j}
to the left of
C_{j}
, we place a point
p_{j}
at the
x
-axis of an
xy
-coordinate system with
x
-coordinate
-d(u_{j},g_{i})
. Similarly, for each vertex
v_{j^{\prime}}
to the right of
C_{i}
, we place a point
q_{j^{\prime}}
on the
x
-axis at the
x
-coordinate
\Delta_{i}+d(h_{i},v_{j^{\prime}})
, where
\Delta_{i}
is the length of the major hemisphere of
C_{i}
. The left to right ordering of these points represent the path
P_{C_{i}}
.

Notice that the set
R^{\prime}=\bigcup_{i}R(P_{C_{i}})
contains all type 3(c) candidate values of
R_{S}
. Therefore we can use Lemma
7
to find
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
such that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}<\lambda^{*}\leq\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
and no value in
R^{\prime}
lies inside the range
(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})
. We update the previous
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
values to
\lambda_{1}\leftarrow\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\}
and
\lambda_{2}\leftarrow\min\{\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}\}
. Since now no value in
R_{S}
lies inside the range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, we have
\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{2}
.

Let
\hat{P}
represent the concatenation of the paths
P_{C_{i}}
, for all cycles
C_{i}
in
S
. Since each path
P_{C_{i}}
can be of size
O(n)
, the size of
\hat{P}
can be
O(n^{2})
, and the running time of our algorithm is
O(n^{2}\log n)
.

5.3
An Improvement to Stage 2

One idea to improve the running time of Stage 2 is to not search for
\lambda^{*}
over all possible type 3(c) candidate values but over the onces which are absolutely necessary. We achieve this by performing a feasibility test on
S
and placing the centers optimally. Whenever we are unsure of the placement of a new center, we perform an additional feasibility test.

We perform the feasibility test on
S
by first choosing a
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
(from Stage 1). The test processes the vertices of
S
from left to right. At any intermediate step, the feasibility test may place centers on
S
. If the centers are placed on edges which do not belong to the major hemisphere of any cycle of
S
then we continue to place those centers. This is because if any such center
x_{i}
determines the optimal cost
\lambda^{*}
, then that cost does not belong to the range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, since then
\lambda^{*}
is not a type 3(c) candidate value. Therefore, the vertices covered by
x_{i}
will remain the same in the optimal case.

If the centers are placed on edges which do belong to the major hemisphere of some cycle
C_{j}
of
S
, then we perform some extra steps. Let
x_{l}
be such a center. There is a possibility that
x_{l}
determines the optimal cost
\lambda^{*}
. Let
u^{*},v^{*}\in V(S)
be two critical vertices of
x_{l}
i.e.
\operatorname{cost}(u^{*},v^{*},x_{l})=\lambda^{*}
. The value
\operatorname{cost}(u^{*},v^{*},x_{l})
is a type 3(c) candidate value if neither
u^{*}
nor
v^{*}
belong to
V(\hat{C}_{j})
. This implies that
\operatorname{cost}(u^{*},v^{*},x_{l})
may lie inside the range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, and we cannot provide the same guarantees as we did for the previous case. We therefore try to further reduce the range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
as follows.

We generate the path
P_{C_{j}}
as mentioned earlier and use Lemma
7
to find
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
such that
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}<\lambda^{*}\leq\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
and no value in
R(P_{C_{j}})
lies inside the range
(\lambda_{1}^{\prime},\lambda_{2}^{\prime})
. We update the previous
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
values to
\lambda_{1}\leftarrow\max\{\lambda_{1},\lambda_{1}^{\prime}\}
and
\lambda_{2}\leftarrow\min\{\lambda_{2},\lambda_{2}^{\prime}\}
.

This ensures that
\operatorname{cost}(u,v,x_{l})\notin(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
for any
u,v\in V(S)
. We update our choice of
\lambda
to the new range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
and continue placing centers using our feasibility test algorithm. The center
x_{l}
placed after this step will ensure that the vertices which covered by it will remain same in the optimal case. We continue the process until all
k
centers have been placed. We make the following claims.

Lemma 11
.

After completion of Stage 2,
\lambda^{*}=\lambda_{2}
.

Proof.

The only type 3(c) candidate values of
R(S)
that we need to consider are
\operatorname{cost}(u,v,x)
, where
x
lies on the major hemisphere of a cycle where our feasibility test wants to place a center. Since after completion of Stage 2 no such value lies inside the range
(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, therefore
\lambda_{2}=\lambda^{*}
. ∎∎

Lemma 12
.

The time required by Stage 2 is at most
O(kn\log n)
.

Proof.

Whenever a center needs to be placed on the major hemisphere of some cycle
C_{j}
of
S
we generate the path
P_{C_{j}}
and compute appropriate
\lambda_{1}^{\prime}
and
\lambda_{2}^{\prime}
. Similar to Stage 1, this step takes
O(n\log n)
time since
|V(P_{C_{j}})|=O(n)
. Since we are placing
k
centers in
S
, such a step can repeat at most
k
time. Therefore, the running time is
O(kn\log n)
. ∎∎

5.4.2
Finding the first cycle.

We present an algorithm to find for each vertex
v\in V(C)
the first cycle to its right which satisfies Lemma
13
, if it exists. Let
\mathcal{C}
be the set of cycles in
S
ordered from left to right. We compute
\Theta_{\lambda_{2}}(C_{i})
for all cycle
C_{i}
in
\mathcal{C}
. We remove from
\mathcal{C}
any cycle
C_{j}
for which either
\Theta_{\lambda_{2}}(C_{j})=\varnothing
or
h_{j}\in\Theta_{\lambda_{2}}(C_{j})
. This is because for any point
x\in A(C_{j})
a type 3(c) candidate value

will never satisfy Lemma
13
.

We process the vertices in
V(S)
with increasing rank and perform the following step. Let
v_{i}
be the vertex we are currently processing. Find the leftmost cycle
C_{j}\in\mathcal{C}
which is to the right of
u_{i}
. If no cycle exists we skip

and continue to the next higher ranked vertex. This implies that no cycle exists to the right of
u_{i}
which satisfies Lemma
13
.

If cycle
C_{j}
exists, then we check if
\Phi_{\lambda_{2}}(u_{i})\cap\Theta_{\lambda_{2}}(C_{j})\neq\varnothing
. If the condition holds, we correctly identify
C_{j}
as the first vertex to the right of
u_{i}
which satisfies Lemma
13
. We continue to the next higher ranked vertex.

Otherwise, if the condition is not satisfied, we remove
C_{j}
from
\mathcal{C}
. This is because from Observation
2
, for any vertex
v_{j}
with
\operatorname{rank}(v_{j})\geq\operatorname{rank}(v_{i})
,
\Phi_{\lambda_{2}}(u_{i})\cap\Theta_{\lambda_{2}}(C_{j})=\varnothing
. We continue to the next cycle in
\mathcal{C}
to the right of
C_{j}
. We stop when either
\mathcal{C}
is empty or when all vertices in
V(S)
have been processed.

When a vertex is successfully mapped to a cycle we charge the operation on the vertex and when a cycle is unsuccessfully compared for mapping with a vertex we charge the operation on the cycle. Since, each vertex and cycle is charged exactly once the running time of this procedure is
O(n\log n)
. The
\log n
factor comes in since we have to identify the first cycle in
\mathcal{C}
to the right of a particular vertex which takes

time.

The improved algorithm.

Here is the final algorithm which improves upon the running time of Stage 2 considerably. Consider an

The weighted

to
d(h,h^{\prime})
. Let
X_{S_{i}}\subseteq X
be the set of centers placed in
S_{i}
. Since
\lambda\in(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
, the vertices in
V(S_{i})\setminus V^{\prime}
will also be covered by
X_{S_{i}}\setminus\{x_{h}\}
in the optimal case. Therefore, we can be certain that these centers should also be included in the optimal set.

We decrement from
k
the number of centers thus placed i.e.
|X_{S_{i}}\setminus\{x_{h}\}|
. We continue the process recursively in the new cactus until a single cactus stem remains. We can compute the weighted
k
-center in this last stem by using the algorithm in Section
5
. We have the following result.

Theorem 4
.

The weighted
k
-center problem on a cactus
G
can be solved in
O(n\log^{2}n)
time, where
n
is the number of vertices of
G
.

Proof.

In each iteration, performing the steps of Stage 1 and Stage 2 takes
O(n\log n)
time. Moreover, in every iteration, all leaf cactus stems of the graph get reduced to a pendant vertex. This may result in new cactus stems becoming leaf cactus stems. However, the number of such new cactus stems can be at most half of the number of previous leaf cactus stems. This results in at most
O(\log n)
many iterations. Therefore, the total time taken by our algorithm over all iterations is
O(n\log^{2}n)
. ∎∎

7
Conclusion

In this paper we presented an
O(n\log^{2}n)
time algorithm to solve the weighted
k
-center problem in a cactus of size
n
. We adapt and use Frederickson and Johnson’s
[
9
]
parametric search technique, generalized by Wang and Zhang
[
13
]
, to achieve our result. In their papers they had also shown how to iteratively optimize the feasibility test algorithm to achieve a sublinear running time. It is worth pursuing to adapt this idea to find a faster feasibility test algorithm for our problem. This has the potential of further reducing the running time of our algorithm by a factor of
\log n
which we believe will be optimal.
