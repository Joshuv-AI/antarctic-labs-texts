Introduction.

This article is a continuation of our previous ones
[

CT
]

\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}u_{t}\left(x,t\right)=\bigtriangleup u\left(x,t\right),\ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\ \ \ t>0,\\
u\left(x,0\right)=\varphi\left(x\right),\ \ \ x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\end{array}\right.

\mathbb{R}^{n}\times(0,\infty)\
with
\lim_{\left(x,t\right)\rightarrow\left(x_{0},0\right)}u\left(x,t\right)=\varphi\left(x_{0}\right)\

In this paper, we shall always assume that

\ \varphi\

if and only if

By analogy, can we also compare the oscillation of

In particular, we note the similarity between (

Main results and their proofs.

2.1
Prescribing
H\left(\tau\right)
and
u\left(0,t\right).

The discussion of prescribing
H\left(\tau\right)
and
u\left(0,t\right)\
is motivated by Theorem
1
. Denote the four limit
values in (
8
) as
p,\ \alpha,\ \beta,\ q
respectively. In

[
CT
]
, with the help of the formula (
9
), we have constructed two
bounded radial functions
\varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right),

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
so that the following are satisfied:

(a).

p=-1<\alpha=-\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}<\beta=\sqrt{A^{2}+B^{2}}<q=1.\
Here

are the values of certain convergent improper integrals
satisfying
0<A^{2}+B^{2}<1
.

(b).

p=\alpha<\beta=q\
for arbitrary two finite numbers
\alpha<\beta.

In the above two examples, we have the identity

p+q=\alpha+\beta,\ \ \ \text{where}\ \ \ \alpha<\beta.

(14)

Our new result in the following is that, as long as the symmetry condition
(
14
) is satisfied, we can prescribe them. For four numbers
p\leq\alpha<\beta\leq q
to satisfy (
14
), we must have either
p<\alpha<\beta<q\
or
p=\alpha<\beta=q.\
Since the second case has been done, it
suffices to look at the first case. We have:

Theorem 5

Let
p<\alpha<\beta<q\
be four
arbitrary
finite
numbers satisfying the identity
p+q=\alpha+\beta.\
One can find a

continuous bounded radial function
\ \varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right),

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\
so
that its average integral
H\left(\tau\right)\
and the convolution
solution (
2
) of the problem (

) satisfy

\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H\left(\tau\right)=p<\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}u\left(0,t\right)=\alpha<\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}u\left(0,t\right)=\beta<\limsup_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H\left(\tau\right)=q.

(15)

More precisely, we can choose the initial data
\varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right),

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
as

\varphi\left(\tau\right)=\frac{q-p}{2}\sin\left(m\log\left(\tau+1\right)\right)+\frac{q-p}{2}\frac{m\tau}{n\left(\tau+1\right)}\cos\left(m\log\left(\tau+1\right)\right)+\frac{q+p}{2},

(16)

where
\ \tau\in[0,\infty)
and
\ m\in\left(0,\infty\right)
is a
number satisfying the identity

\displaystyle\left(\frac{2\omega\left(n\right)}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}z^{n+1}\cos\left(m\log z\right)dz\right)^{2}+\left(\frac{2\omega\left(n\right)}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-z^{2}}z^{n+1}\sin\left(m\log z\right)dz\right)^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{\beta-\alpha}{q-p}\right)^{2}\in\left(0,1\right).

(17)

Remark 7

Note that if the initial data

2.2
Prescribing
\varphi\left(\tau\right)
and
u\left(0,t\right).

It is due to Theorem
1
that we study the oscillation behavior
of
\ H\left(\tau\right)
and its effect on
u\left(0,t\right).

Instead, since
\varphi\left(\tau\right)
is the initial data, it is
perhaps more natural to study the oscillation relation between
\ \varphi\left(\tau\right)
and
u\left(0,t\right).
However, there is a major
difference here. Since we always assume that
\varphi\left(\tau\right)\in C^{0}[0,\infty)\bigcap L^{\infty}[0,\infty)
is continuous and bounded, its
average integral function
H\left(\tau\right)
will also be continuous and
bounded and satisfy
H^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=O\left(1/\tau\right)

as
\tau\rightarrow\infty,
which we call it a

if we
have
p<q\
(see (
22
) and Remark

below). Any such
oscillation will cause
u\left(0,t\right)
to have a slow oscillation too
(by Theorem
1
we have
p<q\
if and only if
\alpha<\beta
).

On the other hand,
\varphi\left(\tau\right)\in C^{0}[0,\infty)\bigcap L^{\infty}[0,\infty)
may not have a slow oscillation (i.e. it may not satisfy

\varphi^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=O\left(1/\tau\right)\
as

\tau\rightarrow\infty\
even if it is differentiable) and so its oscillation
(with
r<s
) may not pass to
u\left(0,t\right)
. In fact, for any

2\pi
-periodic
regular oscillation function
\varphi\left(\tau\right),

\tau\in[0,\infty),
the Cauchy problem (
1
)
with
\ u\left(x,0\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right),

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\
will have the convergence
\ \lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}u\left(0,t\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\varphi\left(\tau\right)d\tau
(see Lemma
17
below). However, due to the
similarity between the two representation formulas (
9
) and
(
10
), if
\varphi\left(\tau\right)\
has a slow
oscillation

as
\tau\rightarrow\infty\
with
r<s,
then it has the
chance of passing to the function
u\left(0,t\right)\
as
t\rightarrow\infty
(see Lemma
18
below).

The major result for prescribing
\varphi\left(\tau\right)
and
u\left(0,t\right)\
is the following:

Theorem 8

Let
r<\alpha<\beta<s\
be four
arbitrary
finite
numbers. One can find a
continuous bounded radial function
\varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right),

x\in\mathbb{R}^{n},
so that it and the convolution solution (

) of
the problem (

\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\varphi\left(\tau\right)=r<\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}u\left(0,t\right)=\alpha<\limsup_{t\rightarrow\infty}u\left(0,t\right)=\beta<\limsup_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\varphi\left(\tau\right)=s.

More precisely, we can choose the initial data
\varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right),

as

\varphi\left(\tau\right)=\left[C_{1}\sin\left(m\log\left(\tau+1\right)\right)+C_{2}\right]+\psi\left(\tau\right),\ \ \ \tau\in[0,\infty),

2.2.1
Proof of Theorem
5
.

The proof is an interesting modification of the argument in Section 3.1.1 of

[
CT
]
, which can achieve much more general result. Some motivation should
be explained at the beginning. First, we choose
\varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right)
to be a radial function in
order to simplify the computation. We think a non-radial function can also be
found as long as we can overcome the computational complexity. Second, we
choose the average integral function
H\left(\tau\right)
first and then
go back to find its corresponding
\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right).\
The function
H\left(\tau\right)\
has to be

oscillatory
so that we have
\ \liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H\left(\tau\right)<\limsup_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H\left(\tau\right).

Moreover, since we require
\varphi
to bounded and continuous (in view of
Theorem
1
), its average integral
H\left(\tau\right)
will
satisfy the derivative estimate
\ H^{\prime}\left(\tau\right)=O\left(1/\tau\right)\
as
\ \tau\rightarrow\infty\
(see (
22
)
below). Therefore, a natural choice is roughly like the function
\sin\left(\log\tau\right),\ \tau\in\left(0,\infty\right).\
After suitable
modification of the function
\sin\left(\log\tau\right),
the proof can be achieved.

Given
\varphi\left(x\right)=\varphi\left(\left|x\right|\right)\in C^{0}[0,\infty)\bigcap L^{\infty}[0,\infty),
its average
integral function

\varphi\left(\tau\right)=\sin\left(\log\left(\tau+1\right)\right)+\frac{\tau}{n\left(\tau+1\right)}\cos\left(\log\left(\tau+1\right)\right),\ \ \ \tau\in[0,\infty).

(78)

Then for each fixed
t>0
we have

\lim_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}\left|u\left(x,t\right)-\left(\sin\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)+\frac{1}{n}\cos\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)\right)\right|=0.

(79)

Remark 25

Note that for fixed
t>0,\
we have

\tilde{\alpha}:=\liminf_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}u\left(x,t\right)=\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\varphi\left(\tau\right)=r=-\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}<\liminf_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H\left(\tau\right)=p=-1

and

\tilde{\beta}:=\limsup_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}u\left(x,t\right)=\limsup_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}\varphi\left(\tau\right)=s=\sqrt{1+\frac{1}{n^{2}}}>\limsup_{\tau\rightarrow\infty}H\left(\tau\right)=q=1.

Thus the
space oscillation
of
u\left(x,t\right)\
is, in
general, not necessarily bounded by the oscillation of
H\left(\tau\right)\
(however, it is always bounded by the oscillation of
\varphi\left(\tau\right)
due to the maximum principle). This is different from the
behavior for
time oscillation
, where we always have
\ r\leq p\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq q\leq s.

Proof.
By Remark
7
, we know that
u\left(x,t\right)\
is

radial
in
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\
for each
t>0.\
Therefore, it suffices to
look at the oscillation behavior of
u\left(x,t\right)
for
\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty.\
The representation formulas (
9
) and
(
10
) cannot be used here since they are valid only for
u\left(0,t\right).
Instead, we use the convolution formula (
2
) to get

u\left(x,t\right)=\dfrac{1}{\left(4\pi t\right)^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{\left|x-y\right|^{2}}{4t}}\varphi\left(\left|y\right|\right)dy=\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\left|z\right|^{2}}\varphi\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|\right)dz,

(80)

where by (
78
) we have

\varphi\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}\sin\left(\log\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)\right)\\
+\frac{\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|}{n\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)}\cos\left(\log\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)\right).\end{array}\right.

By the Lebesgue Dominated Convergence Theorem and the identity

\sin\left(\log\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)\right)=\left\{\begin{array}[c]{l}\sin\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)\cos\left(\log\left(\frac{\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1}{\left|x\right|}\right)\right)\\
+\cos\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)\sin\left(\log\left(\frac{\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1}{\left|x\right|}\right)\right),\end{array}\right.

where

\lim_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1}{\left|x\right|}=1,\ \ \ \text{for
fixed\ }t\ \text{and }z\text{,}

we have

\lim_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}\left|\sin\left(\log\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)\right)-\sin\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)\right|=0

and so

\lim_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}\left|\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\left|z\right|^{2}}\sin\left(\log\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)\right)dz-\sin\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)\right|=0.

(81)

Similarly, we have

\lim_{\left|x\right|\rightarrow\infty}\left|\dfrac{1}{\pi^{n/2}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\left|z\right|^{2}}\left(\tfrac{\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|}{n\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)}\cos\left(\log\left(\left|x+\sqrt{4t}z\right|+1\right)\right)\right)dz-\frac{1}{n}\cos\left(\log\left|x\right|\right)\right|=0

(82)

and (
79
) follows. The proof is done.
