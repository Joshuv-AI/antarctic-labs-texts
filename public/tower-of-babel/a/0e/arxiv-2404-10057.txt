Universal distributions of overlaps from generic dynamics in quantum many-body systems
Alexios Christopoulos, Amos Chan †, Andrea De Luca ††

Abstract
We study the distribution of overlaps with the computational basis of a quantum state generated under generic quantum many-body chaotic dynamics, without conserved quantities, for a finite time t t . We argue that, scaling time logarithmically with the system size t ∝ log ⁡ L t\propto\log L , the overlap distribution converges to a universal form in the thermodynamic limit, forming a one-parameter family that generalizes the celebrated Porter-Thomas distribution. The form of the overlap distribution only depends on the spatial dimensionality and, remarkably, on the boundary conditions. This picture is justified in general by a mapping to Ginibre ensemble of random matrices and corroborated by the exact solution of a random quantum circuit. Our results derive from an analysis of arbitrary overlap moments, enabling the reconstruction of the distribution. Our predictions also apply to Floquet circuits, i.e., in the presence of mild quenched disorder. Finally, numerical simulations of two distinct random circuits show excellent agreement, thereby demonstrating universality.

To corroborate the universality derived in Eq. (

[
32

or

2-

Constraining the gates

[

Here we first consider the case where all gates are drawn independently in space and time
postponing the discussion of the Floquet case to the end. Also, we consider the analytically tractable limit

at fixed coupling
\epsilon

\mathbb{E}[w^{k}_{\mathbf{a}}]
, we consider

\varphi^{(j)}
random phase deletions. Specifically, the moments of the overlap can be expressed as

\mathbb{E}[w^{k}_{\mathbf{a}}]_{\rm RPM}=\;\sum_{\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{L}\in S_{k}}\prod_{j=1}^{L}[T_{\rm RPM}]_{\sigma_{j},\sigma_{j+1}}\delta_{\sigma_{1},\sigma_{L}}^{(\mathrm{bc})}\,.

(8)

where the transfer matrix
T_{\rm RPM}
over a single site is obtained by averaging over all phases between two neighbouring sites. Using that the phases at different times are uncorrelated, we obtain
[T_{\rm RPM}]_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}=\mathbb{E}[m_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(k)}]^{t}
, where
the coefficient
m_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(k)}
is the contribution from the random phase gate in
u^{(2)}_{j,j+1}(t)
at a given time slice

\displaystyle m_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(k)}=\;q^{-2k}\sum_{\{a\},\{b\}}\prod_{j=1}^{k}e^{i\left[\phi_{a_{j},b_{j}}-\,\phi_{a_{\sigma(j)},b_{\sigma^{\prime}(j)}}\right]}\,,

(9)

where the summations runs over
a_{i},b_{i}=0,1,\dots,q-1
,
and contain repetitions when two or more indices
a_{i},b_{i}
have the same value. However, such coincidences can be ignored in the limit of large
q
, where we arrive at

\mathbb{E}[m_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}^{(k)}]_{\mathrm{RPM}}=e^{-\epsilon[k-n_{\mathrm{F}}(\sigma\sigma^{\prime-1})]}\;,

(10)

where
n_{\mathrm{F}}(\sigma)
counts the number of fixed points in permutation
\sigma
.
With these expressions, we can now evaluate the moments in Eq. (
8
) and the frame potential
F^{(k)}_{\rm RPM}=q^{-kL}\mathbb{E}[w^{k}]_{\rm RPM}
.
In the limit of large
t
at fixed
L
, the sum is dominated by the situation where all permutations are the same
\sigma_{i}=\sigma
independently of
i

and one recovers the PT distribution. Additionally, setting in this case
L_{\rm Th}(t)=e^{2\epsilon t}
, we can expand

[T_{\rm RPM}]_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}=\delta_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}+\frac{A_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}}{L_{\rm Th}(t)}+O(L_{\rm Th}(t))^{-2}

(11)

where
A
is once again the adjacency matrix of the transposition graph. Thus, introducing the scaling variable
x=L/L_{\rm Th}(t)
, from Eq. (
8
) we recover Eq. (
5
).
The RPM provides a useful framework for discussing the anticipated self-averaging property (as proven in Appendix
C
), demonstrating that the bitstring average of a single circuit realization coincides with the circuit average in the scaling limit
[
51
]
.

IV
Generalisation and discussion

Unitary state design deals with sampling random Haar states with finite-depth unitary circuits. One expects convengence to Haar increasing the depth
t
and the discrepancy can be estimated by looking at the overlaps between different realisations of circuits
W(t),W^{\prime}(t)
:
\tilde{w}=\mathcal{N}|\braket{\Psi_{0}|W^{\dagger}(t)\tilde{W}^{\prime}(t)|\Psi_{0}}|^{2}
. As the product
W^{\dagger}(t)W^{\prime}(t)
defines a circuit of depth
2t
, one has that
\tilde{w}
follows the same universal distributions (
1
) with
L_{\rm Th}(2t)
.

The large
q
analysis applied to RPM suggests that even for
d>1
the moment
\mathbb{E}[w^{k}]
is described by mapping to a model in
d
dimensions in which local degrees of freedom are permutations and the ferromagnetic Boltzmann weight
J(t)
between sites grows exponentially with
t
. At large times, there exist
k!
perfectly ordered ground states. The excitations on top of these correspond to isolated defects
[
46
]
, in which a site differs by a single transposition from its neighbours. Since a defect can be placed anywhere in volume
N=L^{d}
and its presence breaks
2d
ferromagnetic bonds, the scaling limit in this case corresponds to
x=N/N_{\rm th}(t)
, with
N_{\rm th}(t)=J(t)^{2d}
. There are
\binom{k}{2}
choices of transpositions for each defect and summing over their number, we arrive at
\mathbb{E}[w^{k}]_{d>1}=k!\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(k(k-1)/2)^{n}x^{n}/n!=k!e^{k(k-1)x/2}
. This result coincides with Eq. (
6
) and leads again to Eq. (
1
), although its origin is different in that at
d>1
the excitations are not domain walls but isolated defects. Numerical verification of this result is difficult, but recent quantum computing platforms offer a promising framework to observe our predictions, including higher
d>1
.

We consider 2 generic many-body quantum chaotic models below.
For the

Spectrum of

As mentioned in the main text, the diagonalisation of

. The eigenvalues

corresponding to all choices of

. This clarifies the expansion in Eq. (
7

Applying (
15

for any
\sigma\in\lambda
.
We recall that, via Young diagrams, both the irreducible representations

and the conjugacy classes
\operatorname{Cl}(S_{k})

k
.
We denote a conjugacy class

if any of its representatives is composed by
a_{j}

j
-cycles.
In general, one has the expression

which cannot be simplified further for a general

In the case of

and by

Appendix D
Diagonalisation of the generalised circulant Toeplitz matrix

An
n
-by-
n
matrix
M
is a Toeplitz matrix
[
59
]
if
M_{ij}=m(i-j)
with
i,j=1,2,\dots,n
for some function
m
. As explained in the main text, given any function
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
,
we can generalise the notion of the Toeplitz matrix to an arbitrary group
\mathcal{G}
, introducing a
|\mathcal{G}|\times|\mathcal{G}|
matrix (with
|\mathcal{G}|
the order of the group
\mathcal{G}
):

F_{\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}}=f(\sigma\sigma^{\prime-1})\;,\qquad\forall\;\sigma,\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}\;.

(20)

Similarly to the case of standard Toeplitz matrices, the spectrum can be investigated using a generalised notion of the Fourier transform
[
59
]
. Given a finite group
\mathcal{G}
, the group’s representations
\rho:G\to\mathrm{GL}(d_{\rho},\mathbb{C})
with dimension
d_{\rho}
, and a function
f:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
, we define its Fourier transform
\hat{f}(\rho)
as a function over the space of representations of
\mathcal{G}
which reads

\hat{f}(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma)\rho(\sigma)\;.

(21)

The inverse of this relation can be shown to be given by
[
60
]

f(g)=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\rho\in\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})}\operatorname{dim}(\rho)\operatorname{Tr}[\rho(g^{-1})\hat{f}(\rho)]\,,

(22)

where the sum is restricted to the irreducible representation
\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})
.
The nice property of this Fourier transform is that it converts convolutions into products. In other words for two functions
h,g:\mathcal{G}\to\mathbb{C}
, one gets

h(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma\sigma^{\prime-1})g(\sigma^{\prime})\quad\Rightarrow\quad\hat{h}(\rho)=\hat{f}(\rho)\hat{g}(\rho)\,.

(23)

Now let’s consider an eigenvector of the matrix
F
in Eq. (
20
). Labelling its components as
c(\sigma)
for any
\sigma\in\mathcal{G}
, it must satisfy

\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma\sigma^{\prime-1})c(\sigma^{\prime})=\lambda c(\sigma)\,.

(24)

Taking the Fourier transform of both side, this implies

\hat{f}(\rho)\hat{c}(\rho)=\lambda\hat{c}(\rho)\;,\qquad\forall\rho\in\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})

(25)

Note that each side of this equation is a matrix of size
\dim(\rho)\times\dim(\rho)
. To solve this equation, let’s write the spectral decomposition of the matrix
\hat{f}(\rho)
in braket notation:

\hat{f}(\rho)=\sum_{j=1}^{\dim(\rho)}\lambda_{j}(\rho)\ket{j}\bra{j}\,.

(26)

Then, we see that for any
\tilde{\rho}\in\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})
and any pair
i,j\in\{1,\ldots,\dim(\rho)\}
, the following choice of
\hat{c}(\rho)
provides a solution of Eq. (
25
)

\hat{c}(\rho)\equiv\hat{c}^{(i,j,\tilde{\rho})}(\rho)=\begin{cases}0\,,&\rho\neq\tilde{\rho}\,,\\
\ket{i}\bra{j}\,,&\rho=\tilde{\rho}\,,\end{cases}

(27)

where
\ket{i}
and
\bra{j}
refer respectively to the right and left eigenvectors of
\hat{f}(\rho)
.
Once plugged in Eq. (
25
), it leads to

\hat{f}(\rho)\hat{c}^{(i,j,\tilde{\rho})}(\rho)=\lambda_{i}(\tilde{\rho})\hat{c}^{(i,j,\tilde{\rho})}(\rho)\;.

(28)

This shows that the spectrum of the matrix
F
is given by the
\lambda_{i}(\rho)
for
\rho\in\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})
and
i=1,\ldots,\dim(\rho)
with a degeneracy
\dim(\rho)
, labeled by the index
j
. This provides a full spectral decomposition, since one has the known equality

\sum_{\rho\in\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})}\dim(\rho)^{2}=|\mathcal{G}|\,.

(29)

Now, let us consider the case where the function
f
is a class function,
i.e. it is invariant under the group conjugation

f(\omega\sigma\omega^{-1})=f(\sigma)\;,

(30)

for every
\omega,\sigma\in\mathcal{G}
.
In this case, one can see that

[\hat{f}(\rho),\rho(\sigma)]=0\;,\qquad\forall\;\sigma\in\mathcal{G}\,.

(31)

Indeed, by definition, we have

\hat{f}(\rho)\rho(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma^{\prime})\rho(\sigma^{\prime})\rho(\sigma)=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma^{\prime})\rho(\sigma^{\prime}\sigma)\sum_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma^{\prime\prime}\sigma^{-1})\rho(\sigma^{\prime\prime})\\
=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma^{-1}\sigma^{\prime\prime})\rho(\sigma^{\prime\prime})=\sum_{\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime})\rho(\sigma\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime})=\rho(\sigma)\sum_{\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime})\rho(\sigma^{\prime\prime\prime})=\rho(\sigma)\hat{f}(\rho)\,.

(32)

Because of Schur’s lemma, if
\rho\in\operatorname{{Irr}}(\mathcal{G})
,
\hat{f}(\rho)
must be a multiple of the identity

\hat{f}(\rho)=\lambda(\rho)\,\mathbb{1}\,,

(33)

and therefore in the spectral decomposition Eq. (
26
),
\lambda_{j}(\rho)=\lambda(\rho)
for all
j
’s.
For generalised Toeplitz matrices obtained by class functions, the eigenvalues are labelled by the irreducible representations
\rho
and each has a degeneracy given by
\dim(\rho)^{2}
. We can finally obtain an equation for
\lambda(\rho)
by taking the trace of both sides in Eq. (
21
) and using (
33
)

\operatorname{Tr}[\hat{f}(\rho)]=\dim(\rho)\lambda(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}f(\sigma)\chi_{\rho}(\sigma)\,,

(34)

where
\chi_{\rho}(\sigma)=\operatorname{Tr}[\rho(\sigma)]
is the character of the representation
\rho
. Since both the function
f
and the character are class functions, we can rewrite the sum as a sum over conjugacy classes
\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{G})

\lambda(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{f(\sigma)\chi_{\rho}(\sigma)}{\chi_{\rho}(1)}=\sum_{\mu\in\operatorname{Cl}(\mathcal{G})}\frac{f(\mu)\chi_{\rho}(\mu)\dim(\mu)}{\chi_{\rho}(1)}\,,

(35)

where we used that
\chi_{\rho}(1)=\dim(\rho)
, since the representation of the neutral element is the
dim(\rho)
dimensional identity and we denote as
\dim(\mu)
the size of the conjugacy class
\mu
.
As a consistency check, we can look at the trivial case where

f(\mu)=1
irrespectively of
\mu
. In this case, from Eq. (
35
), we have

\lambda(\rho)=\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\frac{\chi_{\rho}(\sigma)}{\chi_{\rho}(1)}=\delta_{\rho,1}|\mathcal{G}|\,,

(36)

where we indicate as
\rho=1
the trivial one-dimensional representation where all elements are sent to
1
. Eq. (
36
) from the orthogonality of the characters

\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{G}}\chi_{\rho}(\sigma)\chi_{\rho^{\prime}}(\sigma)=\delta_{\rho,\rho^{\prime}}\,,

(37)

choosing
\rho^{\prime}=1
. Eq. (
36
) is consistent with the fact that for
f=1
, the matrix
F
reduces to a matrix made of
1
’s, which thus has only one non-vanishing eigenvalue and which equals the size of the matrix itself, i.e.
|\mathcal{G}|
.
