Gaussian Approximation of Convex Sets by Intersections of Halfspaces
Anindya De, Shivam Nadimpalli, Rocco A. Servedio

Abstract
We study the approximability of general convex sets in ℝ n \mathbb{R}^{n} by intersections of halfspaces, where the approximation quality is measured with respect to the standard Gaussian distribution N ⁡ ( 0 , I n ) N(0,I_{n}) and the complexity of an approximation is the number of halfspaces used. While a large body of research has considered the approximation of convex sets by intersections of halfspaces under distance metrics such as the Lebesgue measure and Hausdorff distance, prior to our work there has not been a systematic study of convex approximation under the Gaussian distribution. We establish a range of upper and lower bounds, both for general convex sets and for specific natural convex sets that are of particular interest. Our results demonstrate that the landscape of approximation is intriguingly different under the Gaussian distribution versus previously studied distance measures. For example, we show that 2 Θ ⁡ ( n ) 2^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})} halfspaces are both necessary and sufficient to approximate the origin-centered ℓ 2 \ell_{2} ball of Gaussian volume 1/2 to any constant accuracy, and that for 1 ≤ p < 2 1\leq p<2 , the origin-centered ℓ p \ell_{p} ball of Gaussian volume 1/2 can be approximated to any constant accuracy as an intersection of 2 O ~ ​ ( n 3 / 4 ) 2^{\tilde{O}(n^{3/4})} many halfspaces. These bounds are quite different from known approximation results under more commonly studied distance measures. Our results are proved using techniques from many different areas. These include classical results on convex polyhedral approximation, Cramér-type bounds on large deviations from probability theory, and—perhaps surprisingly—a range of topics from computational complexity, including computational learning theory, unconditional pseudorandomness, and the study of influences and noise sensitivity in the analysis of Boolean functions.

Introduction

A long line of mathematical research has investigated
convex polyhedral approximation of convex bodies
, i.e. the broad question of how to best approximate general convex bodies using intersections of halfspaces (or equivalently, convex hulls of finite point sets).
Research on questions of this sort dates back at least to the first half of the twentieth century, see for example the early works of Sas
[
Sas39
]
, Fejes Tóth
[
T4́8
]
and Macbeath
[
Mac51
]
, among others.
Contemporary motivation for the study of polyhedral approximation of convex bodies arises from many areas including discrete and computational geometry, geometric convexity, the study of finite-dimensional normed spaces, and optimization.
Given this breadth of connections, it is not surprising that the existing body of work on the subject is vast, as witnessed by the hundreds of references that appear in multiple surveys (including the 1983
[
Gru83
]
and 1993
[
Gru93
]
surveys of Gruber and the 2008 survey of Bronstein
[
Bro08
]
)
on approximation of convex bodies by polyhedra.

In the polyhedral approximation literature to date, a number of different distance notions have been used to measure the accuracy of convex polyhedral approximations of convex sets.
These include the Hausdorff distance, the Nikodym metric (volume of the symmetric difference), the Banach-Mazur distance, distances generated by various
L_{p}
metrics, and more.
(See
[
Bro08
]
for descriptions of each of these metrics and for an extensive overview of approximability results under each of those distance measures.)

This paper adopts a new perspective, by considering approximation of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
using the
(standard) Gaussian volume of the symmetric difference
as the distance measure.
We refer to this distance measure simply as the
Gaussian distance
.
This is a natural and well-studied distance measure in theoretical computer science, as witnessed by the many works that have considered learning, testing, derandomization, and other problems using it (see e.g.
[
KOS08
,
Vem10
,
MORS10
,
Kan11
,
Kan12
,
Kan14a
,
KK14
,
KNOW14
,
Kan15
,
CDS19
,
DMN19
,
DKP+21
,
OSTK21
,
DMN21
,
HSSV22
,
DNS23
]
and references therein). Given this body of work, it is perhaps surprising that Gaussian distance does not appear to have been previously studied in a systematic way for the convex polyhedral approximation problem.

Let us make our notion of approximation precise:

Definition 1
.

Given (measurable) sets
K,L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the
Gaussian distance
between
K
and
L
to be

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\boldsymbol{x}\in K\,\triangle\,L\right]}

where
N(0,I_{n})
denotes the
n
-dimensional standard Gaussian distribution.

We consider approximators which are intersections of finitely many
n
-dimensional halfspaces, and we measure the complexity of such an approximator by the number of halfspaces (facets) that it contains; we refer to this as its
facet complexity
.
Thus, we are concerned with the following broad question:

Given a convex set
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a value
0<\varepsilon<1/2
, what is the minimum
facet complexity of an intersection of halfspaces
L

such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K,L)\leq\varepsilon
?

1.1
Motivation

The broad approximation question stated above arises naturally in the context of contemporary theoretical computer science. As alluded to earlier, an increasing number of results across different areas of TCS involve convex sets and the Gaussian distance; taking the next step on various natural problems in these areas would seem to require an understanding of how well intersections of halfspaces can approximate convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. We give two specific examples below:

•

Testing Convex Sets:
A number of researchers have considered the property testing problem of efficiently determining whether an unknown set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex versus far from convex
[
RV05
,
CFSS17
,
BBB20
,
BB20
,
BBH23
]
. The Gaussian distance provides a clean mathematical framework for this problem, and one in which a natural analogy emerges between the well-studied problem of monotonicity testing over the Boolean hypercube and convexity testing
[
CDN+23
]
. Understanding how convex sets can be approximated by “simpler objects” under the Gaussian distance seems likely to be helpful in developing algorithms for testing convexity. (In support of this thesis, we recall that the study of approximating linear threshold functions by “simpler objects”
[
Ser07
]
yielded insights and technical ingredients, namely the notion of the “critical index,” that played an essential role in the development of efficient algorithms for testing linear threshold functions
[
MORS10
]
.) We also recall that several papers have given testing algorithms for single halfspaces
[
MORS10
]
and intersections of
k
halfspaces
[
DMN19
,
DMN21
]
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the Gaussian distance; combining these results with results about approximating general convex sets by intersections of halfspaces offers a potential avenue to developing testers for general convex sets.

•

Properly Learning Convex Sets:
It has long been known
[
BT96
]
that monotone Boolean functions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
can be learned to any constant accuracy under the uniform distribution in time
2^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})}
, via the “low-degree” algorithm based on the Fourier decomposition. Similarly, it has been known for some time
[
KOS08
]
that convex sets can be learned to any constant accuracy under the Gaussian distance, via a “Hermite polynomial” variant of the low-degree algorithm. However, neither the algorithms of
[
BT96
]
nor
[
KOS08
]
are
proper
: in the monotone case the output hypothesis of the learning algorithm is not a monotone function, and in the convex setting the output hypothesis is not a convex set. Exciting recent work
[
LRV22
]
has given a
2^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})}
-time uniform-distribution learning algorithm for monotone Boolean functions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
that
is
proper (even in the demanding
agnostic learning
model, see
[
LV23
]
); given this, it is a natural goal to seek a
2^{\tilde{O}(\sqrt{n})}
-time proper learning algorithm for convex sets under the Gaussian distance. What would the output hypothesis of such a hoped-for proper learning algorithm look like? It seems quite plausible that it would be an intersection of halfspaces; this begs the question of understanding the capabilities and limitations of intersections of halfspaces as approximators for general convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

We further note that intersections of halfspaces have been intensively studied in many branches of “concrete complexity theory;” indeed, many of the TCS results alluded to earlier that involve Gaussian distance are more specifically about intersections of halfspaces, e.g.
[
KOS08
,
Vem10
,
CDS19
,
DMN19
,
DKP+21
,
DMN21
,
HSSV22
]
.

Finally, we note that the suite of tools which turn out to be relevant in our study link our investigations closely to theoretical computer science. Perhaps unexpectedly, we will see that there are close connections between the study of facet complexity of polyhedral convex approximators and a number of fundamental ingredients and results (such as noise sensitivity, random restrictions, influence of variables/directions, various extremal constructions, etc.) in the
analysis of Boolean functions
over the discrete domain
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. From this perspective, our study can be seen as a continuation of the broad theme of exploring the emerging analogy between convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under the Gaussian distribution and monotone Boolean functions over
\{-1,1\}^{n}
under the uniform distribution (see e.g.
[
DS21
,
DNS21b
,
DNS22
,
HSSV22
]

•

•

Given this, we can use a sampling-based approach: if we uniformly sample

x

(1)

2.2
Overview of Lower Bounds

We now turn to a technical overview of our lower bounds.

Non-Explicit Average-Case Lower Bounds.

It is well known that standard counting arguments easily yield strong (average-case) lower bounds on the complexity of approximating (non-explicit)
Boolean functions
that map
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
to
\{-1,+1\}
.
For a range of different computational models, these lower bounds show that even achieving approximation error
1/2-o_{n}(1)
requires any approximator to be exponentially large.

In our context of approximating convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
by intersections of halfspaces, counting arguments are not quite as straightforward because there are infinitely many distinct halfspaces over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Nevertheless, counting arguments can be brought to bear to prove lower bounds. In more detail,
[
KOS08
]
used a counting argument to establish the existence of
N=2^{2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}}
distinct convex sets
K_{1},\dots,K_{N}
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, each of which is an intersection of
2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
many halfspaces, such that
\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(K_{i},K_{j})\geq 1/44000
for each
1\leq i\neq j\leq N.
Using this result, it is possible to establish the existence of a convex set
K
(one of the sets
K_{1},\dots,K_{N}
) such that
\mathrm{FC}(K,c)\geq 2^{c\sqrt{n}}
, where
c>0
is a small absolute constant.

In
Section

7
we give a stronger (but still non-explicit) lower bound, which shows that many halfspaces are required even to achieve error
1/2-o_{n}(1)
; in other words, we give an
average-case
lower bound (also known as a
correlation bound
). Our lower bound trades off the accuracy of the approximator against the number of halfspaces:

Theorem 8

(Informal version of
Theorem

44
)
.

For any
\tau=\omega(n^{-1/2}\cdot\log n)
, there exists some convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
that has
\mathrm{FC}(K,\frac{1}{2}-\tau)=2^{\Omega(\tau\cdot\sqrt{n})}
.

For example, taking
\tau=n^{-1/4}
,
Theorem

8
implies the existence of an
n
-dimensional convex set
K
such that no intersection of
2^{cn^{1/4}}
many halfspaces can approximate
K
to accuracy even as large as
1/2+n^{-1/4}
; taking
\tau
to be a constant, we recover the non-explicit
2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
lower bound mentioned in the previous paragraph.

The proof of
Theorem

8
combines classical results from statistical learning theory with an information theoretic lower bound on
weak
learning convex sets under
N(0,I_{n})
that was established in recent work
[
DS21
]
.
A high-level sketch is as follows: If every convex set could be approximated to high accuracy (
1/2-\tau
) by an intersection of “few” halfspaces, then classical results from statistical learning theory would imply the existence of (computationally inefficient but sample-efficient) algorithms to “weakly” learn any convex set
K
to error
1/2-\tau/2
. On the other hand, a recent result of De and Servedio
[
DS21
]
gives a strong lower bound on the error that any sample-efficient weak learning algorithm for convex sets must incur. The tension between these two bounds can be shown to imply a lower bound on the number of halfspaces that any
(1/2-\tau)
-approximator must use; see
Section

7
for the detailed argument.
We remark that the proof of
Theorem

8
is the first result we know of in which an information-theoretic sample complexity lower bound for learning is used to establish an
inapproximability
result.

Average-Case Lower Bounds via Gaussian Noise Sensitivity.

One drawback of
Theorem

8
is that it is non-constructive: not only does it not exhibit any particular convex set which is hard to approximate, it does not establish any particular criterion that implies that a convex set is hard to approximate.

Section

8
gives such a criterion. We show that any set with very high
Gaussian noise sensitivity
(at least
1/2-1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
) at very low noise rates (at most
1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
) requires many halfspaces to approximate to accuracy even
1/2+1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
:

Theorem 9

(Informal version of
Theorem

49
)
.

If
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any convex set which satisfies
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-c}}(K)\geq{\frac{1}{2}}-n^{-c}
, then
\mathrm{FC}(K,{\frac{1}{2}}-n^{-c})\geq 2^{n^{\Omega(1)}},
where
c>0
is a suitable absolute constant.

Theorem

9
naturally raises the question of whether there in fact exist
n
-dimensional convex sets that have Gaussian noise sensitivity as high as
1/2-1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
at noise rates that are as low as
1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
. While natural, this question does not appear
[
O’D23
]
to have been asked or answered prior to the current work. In
Section

8.5
we combine the information-theoretic lower bounds of
[
DS21
]
on weak learning convex sets with the “low-degree algorithm” for learning bounded functions to show that there do exist
n
-dimensional convex sets
K
with
\mathbf{GNS}_{1/\mathrm{poly}(n)}(K)\geq 1/2-1/\mathrm{poly}(n)
(see
Theorem

50
). We believe that this result may be of independent interest.

The proof of
Theorem

9
combines results from learning theory
[
KOS08
]
and the study of unconditional pseudorandomness
[
OSTK21
]
with classical ideas from the study of correlation bounds for Boolean functions
[
Hås86b
,
Cai86
]
.
It employs a notion of a “random zoom” for a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
which is closely analogous to the well-studied notion of a “random restriction” of a function from
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
to
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
.

Analogous to the well-known phenomenon that low-depth Boolean circuits over
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
simplify under random restrictions
[
Hås86b
,
Cai86
]
, in the context of developing efficient pseudorandom generators for intersections of halfspaces it was shown in
[
OSTK21
]
that low-degree polynomials over
N(0,I_{n})
simplify under random zooms; more precisely, they are very likely to have very low “hypervariance” after a random zoom (see
Section

8.1
for detailed definitions). Moreover, it was shown in
[
KOS08
]
(in the context of developing efficient agnostic learning algorithms for intersections of halfspaces) that any intersection of “not too many” halfspaces is close (in a suitable sense) to a low-degree polynomial. Combining these results, we get that (1) applying a random zoom to any intersection of few halfspaces, we get a function that must have very low hypervariance, and hence must be very close to a constant. But on the other hand, it can be shown that (2) if a set
K
has very high Gaussian noise stability at very low noise rates, then after a random zoom it must be very
far
from a constant function. Similar to classical correlation bounds for shallow Boolean circuits (which use the fact that that the parity function is far from a constant function after random restriction, while shallow Boolean circuits become close-to-constant after random restrictions), the tension between (1) and (2) can be used to deduce that any intersection of “not too many” halfspaces must be very far from any set
K
that has high Gaussian noise stability at low noise rates.

Lower Bounds via Convex Influences.

Neither of the two lower bound techniques discussed thus far yield lower bounds for any explicit convex sets that we know of.
For our final lower bound, we use the recently introduced notion of convex influences
[
DNS21b
,
DNS22
]
to prove lower bounds for the
\ell_{1}
and
\ell_{2}
balls.
In more detail, in
Section

9
we give a general criterion on a convex set

which suffices to ensure that
\mathrm{FC}(K,\varepsilon)=2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
, where
\varepsilon
is a suitable constant.
This criterion is that
K
is symmetric

3

3

Recall that a set

is

-x\in K
.
and has maximal

Since both the

ball of Gaussian volume

and the

The notion of convex influence was recently introduced in the paper
[
DNS22
]
as an analogue of the classical notion of influence from Boolean function analysis over the discrete cube

]

to be the convex set of maximal total influence.

In this paper, we show that any symmetric set

with maximal total influence (up to a constant factor) requires any approximator to have high facet complexity. In particular, we prove the following theorem:

Theorem 10

(Informal version of
Theorem

.

If

, then for a suitable constant

The main idea in the proof of the above theorem is a new structural result establishing that if
L
is a convex set which is an intersection of
s

\mathbf{I}[L]=O(\log s)
(Proposition
69

can be expressed in terms of the degree-

Once again, we observe that there is an analogy here with the Boolean setting. Namely, Boppana

2.3
Discussion

The current paper takes the first steps in studying polytopal approximations of convex sets under Gaussian measure. Several tantalizing questions emerge from our work; we highlight a few of these now.

Perhaps the most natural question is to close the gap between the worst case upper and lower bounds on
\mathrm{FC}(K,\varepsilon)
in the constant-
\varepsilon
regime. In particular, both
Theorem

70
and
Theorem

44
guarantee the existence of convex sets
K
such that

\mathrm{FC}(K,\varepsilon)=2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
for some constant
\varepsilon>0
. On the other hand,
Theorems

19
and
20
show that for any constant
\varepsilon>0
,
\mathrm{FC}(K,\varepsilon)=n^{O(n)}
. Can we close this gap?

In a related vein, it would also be interesting to obtain tighter upper and lower bounds on
\mathrm{FC}(K,\varepsilon)
when
K
is the
\ell_{p}
ball of Gaussian volume
1/2
. In particular, for
p>2
, we do not have an upper bound on the facet complexity (when the error
\varepsilon
is a constant) that is better than
n^{O(n)}
. Is it possible to obtain polytopal approximations for the
\ell_{p}
ball with
2^{n^{1-c}}
facets for constant
c>0
in the constant error regime?

Finally, looking ahead, it would be interesting to study Gaussian-distance polytopal approximation of convex sets via approximators with small
vertex complexity
. Very little seems to be known here and indeed, the vertex complexity analogues of many questions considered in the current paper seem to be wide open. For example,
Section

5
gets a
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
-facet approximator for the
\ell_{2}
ball. Can we obtain a similar approximator for the
\ell_{2}
ball which has subexponential vertex complexity?

The primary distance metric we will use throughout this paper is the following: Given two measurable sets

We now establish a sharpening of

, recall that by Brahmagupta’s formula (cf.

\frac{\sin(\pi-\kappa)}{\|W\|}=\frac{\sin(\kappa-\theta)}{\|U\|}.

This can be rewritten as

\|W\|=\frac{\sin\kappa}{\sin(\kappa-\theta)}\cdot\|U\|\leq\frac{\sin\kappa}{\sin(\kappa-\theta)}\cdot\ell(k)_{\max}.

(14)

Recalling

, we have that

\displaystyle\tan\kappa=\frac{\mathrm{dist}{\left(O,\overleftrightarrow{UW}\right)}}{\mathrm{dist}{\left(U,O\perp\overleftrightarrow{UW}\right)}}\geq\frac{1}{R}

where we write

to mean the distance between the origin and the line
\overleftrightarrow{UW}

is the distance between

(since the hypotenuse is the longest side in a right triangle), which in turn is at most

Returning to

For

\sin\theta\leq\theta\qquad\text{and}\qquad\cos\theta\geq 1-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2},

we thus get that

\frac{\sin\kappa}{\sin(\kappa-\theta)}\leq\frac{1}{\cos\theta-R\sin\theta}\leq\frac{1}{1-R\theta-\frac{\theta^{2}}{2}}\leq 1+2R\theta\leq 1+2R\theta^{\ast}

where the penultimate inequality can be readily verified from the definitions of

and
\theta

since

and

Plugging the above bound back into

\|W\|\leq\ell(k)_{\max}{\left(1+2R\theta^{\ast}\right)}.

As

(cf.

\displaystyle m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}\right)}-m{\left(\|W\|\right)}

\displaystyle\leq m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}\right)}-m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}{\left(1+2R\theta^{\ast}\right)}\right)}

\displaystyle=m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}\right)}{\left(1-\frac{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}{\left(1+2R\theta^{\ast}\right)}\right)}}{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}\right)}}\right)}

\displaystyle\leq\delta\cdot{\left(1-\frac{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}{\left(1+2R\theta^{\ast}\right)}\right)}}{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}\right)}}\right)}

(15)

where

15

m(\ell(v))\leq\delta
for any direction

. To see this, note that if

Now, note that it suffices to show that

\frac{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}{\left(1+2R\theta^{\ast}\right)}\right)}}{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}\right)}}\geq 1-{\frac{\varepsilon}{8}}

in order to complete the proof.
The remainder of the proof establishes

. We split into two cases depending on

:

Case 1:

.

.
Let

be a parameter that we will set later. We have

\frac{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}(1+2R\theta^{*})\right)}}{m(\ell(k)_{\max})}\geq\frac{m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}(1+2R\theta^{*})\right)}-m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}(1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{*})\right)}}{m(\ell(k)_{\max})-m{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}(1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{*})\right)}}.

Note that the right hand side of the above equation is

R.H.S. of (

)

\displaystyle={\left(\int_{\ell(k)_{\max}(1+2R\theta^{\ast})}^{\ell(k)_{\max}(1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast})}\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}(x)\,dx\right)}{\left(\int_{\ell(k)_{\max}}^{\ell(k)_{\max}(1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast})}\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}(x)\,dx\right)}^{-1}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{\ell(k)_{\max}\Delta R\theta^{\ast}\cdot\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}(1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast})\right)}}{\ell(k)_{\max}(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast}\cdot\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}(\ell(k)_{\max})}

(18)

\displaystyle={\left(\frac{\Delta}{2+\Delta}\right)}\frac{\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}{\left(\ell(k)_{\max}(1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast})\right)}}{\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}(\ell(k)_{\max})}

\displaystyle\geq{\left(\frac{\Delta}{2+\Delta}\right)}\underbrace{{\left((1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast})^{n-1}\exp{\left(\frac{-R^{2}}{2}{\left(2(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast}+(2+\Delta)^{2}R^{2}{\theta^{\ast}}^{2}\right)}\right)}\right)}}_{=\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\Upsilon(n,R,\theta^{\ast})},

(19)

where (
18
) used the fact that the
\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}(x)
is decreasing in
x
for
x\geq\sqrt{n-1}

and (
19
) used the formula for the density function of the
\chi(n)
-distribution, which is given by

\mathrm{pdf}_{\chi(n)}(x)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2^{n/2-1}\Gamma(n/2)}x^{n-1}\exp{\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)}&\mathrm{if}~x\geq 0\\
0&\mathrm{if}~x<0\end{cases},

and the fact that
\ell(k)_{\max}\leq R
. (This is because we truncated

We have that

\displaystyle\geq{\left((1+(2+\Delta)R\theta^{\ast})^{n-1}\exp{\left(-2R^{3}\theta^{\ast}(2+\Delta)^{2}\right)}\right)}

5
Improved Approximation for the
\ell_{2}
Ball

For the Hausdorff and Lebesgue distance metrics (cf.
Section

3.4
), the
\ell_{2}
ball is often an extremal example for known upper bounds on the vertex or facet complexity of polyhedral approximators
[
Dud74
,
BI76
,
B+97
,
LSW06
]
. In this section, we show that—perhaps surprisingly—the
\sqrt{n}
-radius
\ell_{2}
ball
B(\sqrt{n})
, which has Gaussian volume
1/2+o_{n}(1)
, can be approximated to within any constant error by an intersection of only
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
many halfspaces. This is a substantial improvement on the exponential-in-
n
approximation upper bounds obtained in
Section

4
for general convex bodies.

Throughout this section, we will write
{B_{2}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=B(\sqrt{n})
for ease of notation (the “2” subscript is because we are dealing with the
\ell_{2}
ball).

Theorem 27
.

Let
0<\varepsilon<c
for some sufficiently small absolute constant
c
. Then there exists a polytope
K
which is the intersection of

s=\exp{\left(\Theta{\left(\sqrt{n}\cdot\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\log{\left(\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)}\right)}~\text{halfspaces}

such that

\mathrm{dist}_{\mathrm{G}}(B_{2},K)\leq\varepsilon.

Theorem

27
is inspired by Theorem 2.1 of O’Donnell and Wimmer
[
OW07
]
, which shows that the
n
-bit majority function
\mathrm{Maj}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{0,1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, defined as

\mathrm{Maj}_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathbf{1}{\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\geq n/2\right\}},

can be approximated by a monotone CNF formula of size
2^{O(\sqrt{n})}
. O’Donnell and Wimmer’s construction is probabilistic and bears a close resemblance to Talagrand’s random CNF
[
Tal96
]
. Our approach for approximating
B

employs a modification of a probabilistic construction of a convex body due to Nazarov
[
Naz03
]
.
Looking ahead, in
Section

9
we will show that
Theorem

27
is tight for constant
\varepsilon
; more precisely, we will show that any
\varepsilon
-approximation to
B_{2}

must have
2^{\Omega(\sqrt{n})}
facets for a suitable small constant
\varepsilon>0
.

To prove
Theorem

27
, we begin by defining a suitable distribution over intersections of randomly chosen halfspaces:

Definition 28
.

For
w,s>0
, we write
\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)
to be the distribution over
s
-facet polytopes in
\mathbb{R}^{n}

where draw from
\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)
is obtained as follows:

1.

For
i\in[s]
, draw i.i.d.
\boldsymbol{g}^{(i)}\sim N(0,I_{n})
and let
\boldsymbol{H}_{i}
denote the halfspace

\boldsymbol{H}_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\langle x,\boldsymbol{g}^{(i)}\rangle\leq w\right\}}.

2.

Output the convex set
\mathbf{K}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\bigcap_{i=1}^{s}\boldsymbol{H}_{i}
.

\sqrt{n}
\approx n^{1/4}

Figure 3:
A cartoon of how a polytope drawn from
\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)
, for suitable
s=2^{\Theta(\sqrt{n})}
,
w\approx n^{3/4}
, approximates the radius-
\sqrt{n}
ball in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Our depiction of
{B_{2}}=B(\sqrt{n})
is inspired by Milman’s “hyperbolic” drawings of high-dimensional convex sets
[
Mil98
]
.

With
Definition

28
in hand, we turn to the proof of
Theorem

27
:

Proof of
Theorem

27
.

We will show that there exists an outcome
K
in the support of
\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)
for an appropriate choice of parameters
w
and
s
that has the desired properties.

To show this, it suffices to show that

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)}{\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq B_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}\right]}\leq\varepsilon,

which, by commuting the order of integration, is equivalent to showing that

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)}{\left[\mathbf{K}(\boldsymbol{x})\neq B_{2}(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}\right]}\leq\varepsilon.

(21)

Equation

21
allows us to control the error on an “
x
-by-
x
basis.” We set parameters

d_{\mathrm{in}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{n}-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}\qquad\text{and}\qquad d_{\mathrm{out}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sqrt{n}+\frac{\varepsilon}{4}.

(22)

We will show that when
\|x\|\leq d_{\mathrm{in}}
or
\|x\|\geq d_{\mathrm{out}}
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)}{\left[\mathbf{K}(x)\neq B_{2}(x)\right]}\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.

(23)

Since the Gaussian volume of the annulus
\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}d_{\mathrm{in}}\leq\|y\|\leq d_{\mathrm{out}}\}
is at most
\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
(this is an easy consequence of the standard fact that for
n>1
, the pdf of the
\chi^{2}(n)
-distribution is everywhere at most 1), this establishes
Equation

21
which in turn completes the proof.

Note that by construction (
Definition

28
), for any fixed
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
we have that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)}{\left[x\in\mathbf{K}\right]}=\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{\|x\|}\right)}^{s}

where
\Phi\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}\to[0,1]
denotes the cumulative density function of the univariate Gaussian distribution

. To see this, note that

\langle x,\boldsymbol{g}^{(j)}\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}\boldsymbol{g}^{(j)}_{i}\sim N(0,\|x\|^{2})\quad\text{and so}\quad\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{g}^{(1)},\ldots,\boldsymbol{g}^{(s)}}{\left[\langle x,\boldsymbol{g}^{(j)}\rangle\leq w~\text{for all}~j\in[s]\right]}=\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{\|x\|}\right)}^{s}

due to independence.

This observation informs our setting of the parameters
w
and
s
. We take
w
to satisfy the equation

\frac{1-\Phi(w/d_{\mathrm{out}})}{1-\Phi(w/d_{\mathrm{in}})}=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\,{\ln}{\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right)}

(24)

and take
s
to be

s\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{\varepsilon}{4}{\left(1-\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)}\right)}^{-1}.

(25)

(We will argue that there is a valid solution to
Equation

24
later on in the proof.) Given
Equations

24
and
25
,

•

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

, we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)}{\left[x\in\mathbf{K}\right]}=\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{\|x\|}\right)}^{s}\geq\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)}^{s}={\left(1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4s}\right)}^{s}\geq 1-\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

where the first inequality relies on the fact that

is an increasing function, the following equality relies on our choice of

in
Equation

25
, and the final inequality makes use of the fact that

. As

implies that

, we pick up at most
\varepsilon/4
error on such points.

•

For
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
with

, we have

\displaystyle\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\mathbf{K}\sim\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)}{\left[x\in\mathbf{K}\right]}=\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{\|x\|}\right)}^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{d_{\mathrm{out}}}\right)}^{s}={\left(1-\frac{4}{\varepsilon}{\left(1-\Phi{\left(\frac{w}{d_{\mathrm{in}}}\right)}\right)}{\ln}{\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)}^{s}

\displaystyle={\left(1-\frac{1}{s}\,{\ln}{\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right)}\right)}^{s}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\varepsilon}{4}

where we once again used the fact that
\Phi(\cdot)
is increasing in the first inequality. The following equalities follow from rearranging
Equations

24
and
25
, and the final inequality is due to the fact that
(1+x)\leq\exp(x)
. As

implies that

, we pick up at most
\varepsilon/4
error on such points as well.

In particular, the above establishes
Equation

23
, which in turn establishes
Equation

21
and so there exists a set

in the support of
\mathrm{Naz}(w,s)
that
\varepsilon
-approximates
B_{2}
.

It remains to argue that there exists a valid solution to
Equation

24
, and to bound the number of facets of
K
(i.e. the parameter
s
); we start with the former. Using standard Gaussian tail bounds (
Proposition

12
),
provided that
w/{d_{\mathrm{in}}},w/{d_{\mathrm{out}}}=\Omega(1)
(which holds with room to spare; below we will see that these quantities are

), we can write

24

\exp{\left(\frac{w^{2}}{2}{\left(\frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{in}}^{2}}-\frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{out}}^{2}}\right)}\right)}\cdot\Theta(1)=\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\,{\ln}{\left(\frac{4}{\varepsilon}\right)}.

6.1
Cramér-Type Large Deviations Bounds and General Setup

Roughly speaking, “Cramér-type” tail bounds are results which state that under suitable conditions, a sum
\boldsymbol{S}
of random variables for which
\operatorname{{\bf E}}[\boldsymbol{S}]=0
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{S}]=1
will have tail probabilities
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{S}\geq x]
which are
multiplicatively
close to the corresponding Gaussian tail bound
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{g}\sim N(0,1)}[\boldsymbol{g}\geq x].

The most standard results of this type are for sums of
independent
random variables, as in the following theorem:

Theorem 30

(Theorem 1 from Chapter VIII of
[
Pet12
]
)
.

Let
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{n}
be i.i.d. random variables that satisfy Cramér’s condition,
i.e., there exists constant
H>0
such that
\mathbf{E}[e^{H\boldsymbol{X}_{1}}]<\infty
.

Let us assume that the variables
\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
are centered — i.e.
\mathbf{E}[\boldsymbol{X}_{1}]=0
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\boldsymbol{X}_{1}]=\sigma^{2}
. Let
\boldsymbol{S}_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\boldsymbol{X}_{i}
and define
F_{n}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}[\boldsymbol{S}_{n}\leq x\sigma\sqrt{n}]
. If
x>0
and
x=o(\sqrt{n})
, then

\frac{1-F_{n}(x)}{1-\Phi(x)}=\exp\bigg(\frac{x^{3}}{\sqrt{n}}\cdot\lambda\bigg(\frac{x}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)\bigg)\cdot\bigg(1+O\bigg(\frac{x+1}{\sqrt{n}}\bigg)\bigg).

Here
\lambda(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_{k}t^{k}
is a power series whose coefficients
\{a_{k}\}
depend only on the cumulants of the random variable
\boldsymbol{X}_{1}
. Furthermore, the disc of convergence of this series has a positive radius.

We will also require a Cramér-type bound, due to
[
HRW07
]
, for when the sum is a sum of randomly chosen values that are drawn
without
replacement from a finite population. In more detail, let
\boldsymbol{X}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{X}_{m}
be a random sample drawn without replacement from a finite population
\{a_{1},\ldots,a_{n}\}
with

\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}^{2}=n,

where
m<n
. We
will write

\boldsymbol{S}_{m}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\boldsymbol{X}_{i},\quad p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\frac{m}{n},\quad q\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=1-p,\quad\text{and}\quad\omega_{n}^{2}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=npq.

Theorem 31

(Theorem 1.2 of
[
HRW07
]
)
.

There exists an absolute constant
A>0
such that

{\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}{\left[\boldsymbol{S}_{m}\geq t\omega_{n}\right]}={\left(1-\Phi(t)\right)}{\left(1+O(1)(1+t)^{3}\frac{\beta_{3n}}{\omega_{n}}\right)}}

for

The

6.2
Proof of
Theorem

29

The proof will proceed in three steps:

•

First, we show (

) that
B_{p}
can be
\varepsilon
-approximated by an intersection of
m
-dimensional “juntas” that each have very large volume. As in our earlier construction of an approximator for the

ball, the construction will be probabilistic, but it is a very different probabilistic construction from the
\ell_{2}

27
(in particular, it makes essential use of the Cramér-type bound (
Theorem

31
) for sums of independent random variables drawn without replacement from a finite population).

•

Next (
Theorem

39
) we get a high-accuracy estimate of the Gaussian volume

of each of these juntas, by determining the value of

up to a small multiplicative factor. This argument
uses Petrov’s Cramér-type bound (

30
) for sums of i.i.d random variables.

•

Finally, we combine our high-accuracy estimate of

with our “relative-error” universal approximation bound (

) for sets of volume

to show that each of the juntas can be approximated by an intersection of not-too-many halfspaces.

The theorem then follows immediately.
We note that for the special case when

, the second and third steps are not required to obtain a

-facet approximator for the cross-polytope; this is because the juntas used in the approximator themselves are polytopes, and hence do not need to be approximated by a polytope using the universal “relative-error” bound.
(See
Remark

for more on this.)

7
Non-Explicit Average-Case Lower Bounds

The goal of this section is to prove the following theorem:

Theorem 44

(Non-explicit average-case lower bound.)
.

There is an absolute constant
C>0
such that for any sufficiently large
n
and any
\varepsilon=\omega(n^{-1/2}\cdot\log n)
, there exists some convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for any
L
which is an intersection of at most
M\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2^{C\cdot\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n}}
halfspaces,

\mathrm{Vol}(K\,\triangle\,L)>\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon.

We note that
Theorem

44
gives an
average-case
lower bound, i.e. it establishes “strong inapproximability” by intersections of not-too-many halfspaces. For example, taking
\varepsilon=n^{-1/4}
, it shows that there is an
n
-dimensional convex set
K
such that no intersection of
2^{cn^{1/4}}
many halfspaces can approximate
K
to accuracy even as large as
1/2+n^{-1/4}
.

The proof of this theorem will go via the notion of
VC
-dimension, a fundamental measure of complexity in statistical learning theory. Recall that given a set
{\cal F}
of Boolean functions over some domain
X
, the
VC
-dimension of
{\cal F}
is the largest size of any
S\subseteq X
which is
shattered
by
{\cal F}
, meaning that every possible
0/1
labeling of the points in
S
is achieved by some function in
{\cal F}.
(See the book
[
KV94
]
for more details.)

To use
VC
-dimension in our context, we will also need the notion of agnostic learning. We recall the following standard definition:

Definition 45
.

A class
{\cal F}
of Boolean functions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is said to be
agnostically PAC learnable
with sample complexity
m(\varepsilon,\delta)
if for every
\varepsilon,\delta>0
, the following holds. There is an algorithm
{\cal A}
which for any
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\{0,1\}
and any distribution
{\cal D}
over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, given
m(\varepsilon,\delta)
many i.i.d. labeled samples of the form
(\boldsymbol{x},f(\boldsymbol{x}))
(where each
\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal D}
), outputs a hypothesis
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
such that with probability
1-\delta
,

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal D}}{\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\not=f(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}\leq\varepsilon+\min_{h^{\ast}\in{\cal F}}\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim{\cal D}}{\left[h^{\ast}(\boldsymbol{x})\not=f(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}.

A central result of statistical learning theory is that any concept class
{\cal F}
of Boolean functions is agnostically PAC learnable where the sample complexity is proportional to the

VC
-dimension of
{\cal F}
. We state a sharp form of the bound below, which is due to Talagrand
[
Tal94
]
:

Theorem 46
.

Any concept class
{\cal F}
of Boolean functions over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is agnostically PAC learnable with sample complexity

m_{{\cal F}}(\varepsilon,\delta)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Theta\bigg(\frac{\text{VC}\text{-}\mathrm{dim}({\cal F})}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg),

where
\text{VC}\text{-}\mathrm{dim}({\cal F})
is the
VC
-dimension of
{\cal F}
.

Recall that
\mathrm{Facet}(n,M)
denotes the class of convex sets in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which are intersection of at most
M
halfspaces. The following is shown in
[
BEHW89
]
(and is now a standard fact, see e.g. Exercise 3.4 of
[
KV94
]
):

Fact 47
.

\text{VC}\text{-}\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Facet}(n,M))=O(nM\log M)
.

The final ingredient we will require is a recent result of De and Servedio (Theorem 2 in
[
DS21
]
) which establishes a lower bound on the query complexity of any algorithm which “weakly learns” (meaning that the output hypothesis has an error rate only slightly less than
1/2
) convex sets over the Gaussian space. (This lower bound of course also holds for the sample complexity of any algorithm which only receives independent labeled samples
(\boldsymbol{x},f(\boldsymbol{x}))
where each
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
.)

Theorem 48

(Theorem 2 of
[
DS21
]
)
.

For sufficiently large
n
, for any
s\geq n
, there is a distribution
{\cal D}
over centrally symmetric convex sets
\mathbf{K}\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
with the following property: for a target convex set
\mathbf{K}\sim{\cal D},
for any black box query algorithm
A
making at most
s
many queries to
\mathbf{K}
, the expected error of
A
(the probability over
\mathbf{K}\sim{\cal D}
, over any internal randomness of
A
, and over a random Gaussian
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,1^{n})
, that the output hypothesis
h
of
A
predicts incorrectly on
\boldsymbol{x}
) is at least
1/2-{\frac{O(\log s)}{n^{1/2}}}
.

We now combine these ingredients to establish
Theorem

44
:

First, let us assume that for every convex set
K
, there is a convex set
L
which is an intersection of at most
M
halfspaces such that

\mathrm{Vol}(K\,\triangle\,L)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon.

Now, consider the task of “weak learning” an unknown convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
given i.i.d. labeled samples of the form
(\boldsymbol{x},K(\boldsymbol{x}))
where
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
and
K(\cdot)
is identified with the indicator function of the convex set
K
. To do this, we run the agnostic PAC learning algorithm from
Theorem

46
for the class
\mathrm{Facet}(n,M)
with parameters
\varepsilon/2
and
\delta
on the samples.

Now, given that there is a convex set
L\in\mathrm{Facet}(n,M)
such that
\mathrm{Vol}(K\,\triangle\,L)\leq\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon
, it follows that
with probability
1-\delta
, the algorithm from
Theorem

46
outputs a hypothesis
h
such that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[h(\boldsymbol{x})\not=K(\boldsymbol{x})]\leq\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon+\varepsilon/2=\frac{1}{2}-\varepsilon/2.

Further, the sample complexity of this algorithm is given by
S(\varepsilon,\delta)
defined as

S(\varepsilon,\delta)=\Theta\bigg(\frac{\text{VC}\text{-}\mathrm{dim}(\mathrm{Facet}(n,M))}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg)

Applying
47
, we get that

S(\varepsilon,\delta)=O\bigg(\frac{nM\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\delta)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg)

In particular, if we set
\delta=\varepsilon/4
, then we get that there is a PAC learning algorithm for convex sets (where the data
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
) with sample complexity

S(\varepsilon,\varepsilon/4)=O\bigg(\frac{nM\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}\bigg)

which has expected error at most
1/2-3\varepsilon/4
(where the expectation is over the internal randomness as well as the randomness of the data points
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N^{n}(0,1)
). On the other hand, by
Theorem

48
, we get that the expected error must be at least
1/2-O(n^{-1/2}\cdot\log S(\varepsilon,\varepsilon/4))
. Combining these bounds, we get that

\frac{1}{2}-\frac{3\varepsilon}{4}\geq\frac{1}{2}-O(n^{-1/2}\cdot\log S(\varepsilon,\varepsilon/4)).

This implies that
\log S(\varepsilon,\varepsilon/4))=\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n})
. This implies that

\frac{nM\log M}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{\log(1/\varepsilon)}{\varepsilon^{2}}=2^{\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n})}.

Since
\varepsilon=\omega(n^{-1/2}\cdot\log n)
and
M\geq 1
, the first term on the left hand side is the dominant one, and thus we have

M\log M=\frac{\varepsilon^{2}}{n}\cdot 2^{\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n})}.

Again using
\varepsilon=\omega(n^{-1/2}\cdot\log n)
, we have that

M\log M=2^{\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n})}\quad\quad\textrm{and thus}\quad\quad M=2^{\Omega(\varepsilon\cdot\sqrt{n})}.

This finishes the proof of
Theorem

44
.
∎

8
Average-Case Lower Bounds via Gaussian Noise Sensitivity

One drawback of
Theorem

44
is that it is non-constructive: not only does it not exhibit any particular convex set which is hard to approximate, it does not give any criterion that suffices to make a convex set hard to approximate.

In this section we describe a general structural property — namely, having high
Gaussian noise sensitivity
(see
Definition

61
) at low noise rates — and show that if a convex set has this property, then any intersection-of-halfspaces approximator for it must use many halfspaces.
More precisely, we prove the following:

Theorem 49
.

Fix any constant
c_{1}>0
and let
0\leq\kappa=\kappa(n)<1/2
(note that
\kappa
may depend on
n
).
Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set such that
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-c_{1}}}(K)\geq 1/2-\kappa.

Then there is a constant
c_{2}
(depending on
c_{1}
) such that if
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any intersection of
2^{n^{c_{2}}}
many LTFs, we have

\mathrm{Vol}(K\ \triangle\ L)\geq{\frac{1}{2}}-{2}\kappa^{1/4}-\frac{3}{2}n^{-c_{2}}.

Theorem

49
raises the question of whether there exist convex sets that have very high Gaussian noise sensitivity at very low noise rates. This is a natural question, but it does not seem to have been explicitly considered in prior work
[
O’D23
]
. In
[
Naz03
]
Nazarov gave a randomized construction of an
n
-dimensional convex set that has Gaussian surface area
\Omega(n^{1/4})
, thus showing that Ball’s
O(n^{1/4})
upper bound
[
Bal93
]
on the Gaussian surface area of any
n
-dimensional convex sets is best possible up to the hidden constant. A straightforward geometric argument applied to Nazarov’s construction gives that there are
n
-dimensional convex sets
K
for which
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-1/4}}(K)
is at least some absolute constant, but these arguments do not seem to give a stronger lower bound of the form
1/2-o_{n}(1)
.

In
Section

8.5
we show how known results on the information-theoretic hardness of
weak learning
convex sets under the Gaussian distribution (the main lower bound result of
[
DS21
]
, which we stated as
Theorem

48
in
Section

7
) can be leveraged to establish the existence of convex sets with very high Gaussian noise sensitivity (inverse-polynomially close to
1/2
) at very low noise rates (inverse polynomial). This result, stated below, may be of independent interest:

Theorem 50
.

Fix any constant
\tau<1/4
. For sufficiently large
n
, there are convex bodies
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}

that have
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-\tau}}(K)\geq 1/2-n^{-\tau}.

Combining
Theorem

50
with
Theorem

49
we immediately get an average-case lower bound for intersections of
2^{n^{\Omega(1)}}
halfspaces, albeit one which is quantitatively not quite as strong as
Theorem

44
:

Corollary 51
.

There is an absolute constant
c>0
such that for sufficiently large
n
, there are convex sets
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that if
L\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is any intersection of
2^{n^{c}}
many LTFs, then
\mathrm{Vol}(K\ \triangle\ L)\geq 1/2-n^{-c}
.

8.1
Random Zooms, Hypervariance, and Local Hyperconcentration

[
OSTK21
]
introduced the notion of a “random zoom” of a function over Gaussian space and observed that it is a natural analogue of the well-studied notion of a random restriction of a function defined over the Boolean cube
\{-1,1\}^{n}
. Random zooms are defined as follows:

Definition 52
.

For a polynomial
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
,
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we define the function
p_{\lambda\mid x}
by

p_{\lambda\mid x}(y)=p{\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}x+\sqrt{\lambda}y\right)},

and we refer to the function
p_{\lambda\mid x}(\cdot)
as the
\lambda
-zoom of
p
at
x
.

For intuition, if
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
is fixed we view
\sqrt{1-\lambda}x+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}
as a “
\lambda
-noisy” version of
x
, and we view changing a function
p
’s input from
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
to
\sqrt{1-\lambda}x+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}
as “zooming into
p
at
x
with scale
\lambda
.”
We note that if
p
is a degree-
d
polynomial then so is
p_{\lambda\mid x}
, for any
\lambda<1
.

The following observation will be useful:

Observation 53
.

If
\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\sim N(0,I_{n})
are independent and
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, then
\sqrt{1-\lambda}\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}
is distributed identically to
\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{n})
. Thus the distribution of
f_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})
is identical to
f(\boldsymbol{z}),
for any function
f
and any
0\leq\lambda\leq 1.

To adopt the terminology of
[
RST15
]
,
53
says for a standard Gaussian
\boldsymbol{x}
, a random zoom of
f
at
\boldsymbol{x}
“completes to the Gaussian distribution”.

53
is similar to how, in the Boolean function context of
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\{-1,+1\}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
, evaluating a random restriction on
f
at a uniform random input is the same as evaluating
f
at a uniform random input; we mention that this simple observation plays an important role in a number of classical as well as more recent correlation bounds for small-depth circuits over
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
, see e.g.
[
Hås86b
,
Cai86
,
RST15
]
. See
[
OSTK21
]
for a more detailed discussion of how random zooms for functions over Gaussian space are analogous to random restrictions of functions over
\{-1,+1\}^{n}
.

The “Gaussian noise” (or “Ornstein–Uhlenbeck”) operator (see, e.g.,
[
O’D14
, Def. 11.12]
) corresponds to the expectation of the zoom of a function:

Definition 54
.

Given
0<\rho\leq 1
, the operator
\mathrm{U}_{\rho}
acts on polynomials
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
via

(\mathrm{U}_{\rho}p)(x)=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[p{\left(\rho x+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\boldsymbol{y})\right)}\right]}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[p_{(1-\rho^{2})\mid x}(\boldsymbol{y})\right]}.

The operator
\mathrm{U}_{\rho}
acts diagonally in the Hermite polynomial basis
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
(see
Appendix

A
for a brief overview of the basics of Hermite analysis):

\mathrm{U}_{\rho}p=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{N}}\rho^{|\alpha|}\widehat{p}(\alpha)h_{\alpha}.

(56)

In particular, if
p
is a degree-
d
polynomial, so too is
\mathrm{U}_{\rho}p
.

Following
[
OSTK21
]
, we will need to extend the definition of
\mathrm{U}_{\rho}
to
\rho>1
, which we can do via the formula
Equation

56
; equivalently, by stipulating that
\mathrm{U}_{\rho^{-1}}=\mathrm{U}_{\rho}^{-1}
. Now we can define the
hypervariance
of a function:

Definition 55
.

Let
g\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be a polynomial. For
R>1
, we define the
R
-hypervariance
of
g
to be

\mathbf{HyperVar}_{R}[g]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[\mathrm{U}_{R}g]=\sum_{\alpha\neq 0}R^{2|\alpha|}\widehat{g}(\alpha)^{2}.

(For
R=1
, this reduces to the usual variance of
g
.)
If
\mathbf{HyperVar}_{R}[g]\leq\varepsilon\|g\|^{2}_{2}
, then we say that
g
is
(R,\varepsilon)
-attenuated
.

The key structural result of
[
OSTK21
]
states, roughly speaking, that a random zoom of a low-degree polynomial is very likely to be “hyperconcentrated.” In more detail, Theorem 85 of
[
OSTK21
]
, specialized to the case that its “nice” distribution
(g_{{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}})_{{\boldsymbol{\upsilon}}\sim\Upsilon}
is a single degree-
d
polynomial
p
, gives us the following:

Theorem 56

(Local Hyperconcentration Theorem for a single polynomial, Theorem 85 of
[
OSTK21
]
)
.

There exists a constant
c>0
so that for any
1>\varepsilon,\beta>0
and
R\geq 1
, if
p(x_{1},\dots,x_{n})
is a degree-
d
polynomial, then taking

\lambda\leq\frac{c\varepsilon\beta}{Rd^{9/2}},

with probability at least
1-\beta
over
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
we have that

\mathbf{HyperVar}_{R}(p_{\lambda|\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\varepsilon^{2}\|p_{\lambda|\boldsymbol{x}}\|_{2}^{2}\quad\text{(i.e.~$p_{\lambda|\boldsymbol{x}}$ is $(R,\varepsilon)$-attenuated).}

We also recall the following result from
[
OSTK21
]
, which roughly speaking says that “attenuated polynomials are very likely to take values that are multiplicatively close to their means”:

Proposition 57

(Proposition 32 of
[
OSTK21
]
)
.

Let
g
be a polynomial that is
(R,\varepsilon)
-attenuated, with
R\geq\sqrt{2}
and
\varepsilon\leq 1
. Write
\mu=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[g]
and assume
\mu\neq 0
.
Then for
0<\gamma\leq 1
, for
\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{n})
we have
g(\boldsymbol{z})\approx_{\gamma}\mu
(and hence
\mathrm{sign}(g(\boldsymbol{z}))=\mathrm{sign}(\mu)
) except with probability at most
(2\sqrt{\varepsilon}/\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}R^{2}+1}
.

8.2
PTFs and Intersections of Halfspaces Collapse Under Random Zooms

Theorem

56
and
Proposition

57
together give us that a random zoom of any degree-
d
PTF will with high probability become a close-to-constant function:

Corollary 58

(Random zooms of PTFs are close-to-constant whp)
.

Let
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
,
f=\mathrm{sign}(p)
be a degree-
d
PTF, and let
0<\lambda<c^{\prime}/d^{9/2}
for a sufficiently small constant
c^{\prime}
. Then for any
0<\tau\leq 1
, we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(f_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}})\geq\tau\right]}\leq{\frac{Cd^{9/2}\lambda}{\tau}},

(57)

where
C>0
is a universal constant.

Proof.

We assume that
p
is a non-constant polynomial since otherwise the claim is trivially true.
Let
R=\sqrt{2}
, let
\varepsilon=\tau/64
, and let
\lambda=c\varepsilon\beta/(Rd^{9/2})
where
c
is the constant from
Theorem

56
; equivalently, let
\beta
be
16\sqrt{2}d^{9/2}\lambda/(c\tau)
(this is the RHS of
Equation

57
, taking
C=16\sqrt{2}/c
). By taking
c^{\prime}
suitably small we have
\beta<1
, and clearly
\varepsilon<1
, so by
Theorem

56
with probability at least
1-\beta
over
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
we have that
p_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
is
(R,\varepsilon)
-attenuated.

Fix any outcome
x
of
\boldsymbol{x}
such that
p_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
is
(R,\varepsilon)
-attenuated and
\mu_{\boldsymbol{x}}\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[p_{\lambda\mid x}]\neq 0
(note that the probability that
\mu_{\boldsymbol{x}}=0
is zero for
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
, so the total probability of such
x
-outcomes is at least
1-\beta
). Taking
\gamma=1/2
in
Proposition

57
, we get that
f_{\lambda\mid x}(\boldsymbol{z})
equals
\mathrm{sign}(\operatorname{{\bf E}}[p_{\lambda\mid x})
except with probability at most
(2\sqrt{\varepsilon}/\gamma)^{\frac{1}{2}R^{2}+1}=16\varepsilon=\tau/4
, and hence for such an
x
we have
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(f_{\lambda\mid x})\leq 4\cdot{\frac{\tau}{4}}\cdot(1-{\frac{\tau}{4}})\leq\tau.

∎

To connect
Corollary

58
with intersections of halfspaces, we recall the fact (from
[
KOS08
]
) that any intersection of not-too-many halfspaces is close to a low-degree PTF:

Theorem 59

(Low-degree PTF approximators for intersections of halfspaces, Theorems 15 and 20 of
[
KOS08
]
)
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be any convex set. Then for any
\varepsilon>0
there is a polynomial
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
of degree
d=O(\log(k)/\varepsilon^{2})
such that the PTF
f=\mathrm{sign}(p)
satisfies

\mathrm{Vol}(K\ \triangle\ f^{-1}(1))\leq\varepsilon.

Combining
Theorem

59
with
Corollary

58
, we get that an intersection of few halfspaces simplifies to a close-to-constant function with high probability under a random zoom:

Corollary 60

(Intersections of few halfspaces collapse to close-to-constant under random zooms)
.

Let
L\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an intersection of at most
k
halfspaces, and let
0<\delta<1.

For a suitable choice of
\lambda=\Theta(\delta^{20}/(\log k)^{9/2}),
we have

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left(L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\geq\delta\right]}\leq\delta.

Proof.

We argue by contradiction; so suppose that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left(L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\geq\delta\right]}>\delta.

(58)

Let
\varepsilon\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\delta^{2}/64
.
By
Theorem

59
, there is a PTF
f=\mathrm{sign}(p)
of degree
d\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=C_{1}\log(k)/\varepsilon^{2}
which is an
\varepsilon
-approximator for
L
, i.e.
\mathrm{Vol}(L\ \triangle\ f^{-1}(1))\leq\varepsilon.

For a suitable choice of the hidden constant in
\lambda=\Theta(\delta^{20}/(\log k)^{9/2})
(note that this choice of
\lambda
satisfies the conditions of
Corollary

58
) and
\tau=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
, by
Corollary

58
we have that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(f_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}})\geq\tau\right]}\leq{\frac{Cd^{9/2}\lambda}{\tau}}=\delta/2.

(59)

Combining
Equation

58
and
Equation

59
, we get that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left(L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\geq\delta\text{~and~}\operatorname{{\bf Var}}(f_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}})\leq\tau\right]}\geq\delta/2.

(60)

Now, if
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}[a(\boldsymbol{y})]\geq\delta
and
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}[b(\boldsymbol{y})]\leq\tau
for
\{-1,1\}
-valued functions
a,b
and
\delta>\tau
then it must be the case that
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}[a(\boldsymbol{y})\neq b(\boldsymbol{y})]\geq(\delta-\tau)/4.

Recalling that
\delta=8\sqrt{\varepsilon}
and
\tau=\sqrt{\varepsilon}
,
Equation

60
gives

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})\neq f_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})\right]}\geq 7\sqrt{\varepsilon}/4\right]}\geq 4\sqrt{\varepsilon},

from which
53
gives

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})\neq L(\boldsymbol{z})\right]}\geq{\frac{7\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{4}}\cdot{4\sqrt{\varepsilon}}=7\varepsilon.

This contradicts
\mathrm{Vol}(L\ \triangle\ f^{-1}(1))\leq\varepsilon
, and the proof is complete.
∎

8.3
Gaussian Noise Stability and Expected Variance After Random Zooms

We recall the definitions of
Gaussian noise stability
and
Gaussian noise sensitivity
:

Definition 61

(cf. Definition 11.18 of
[
O’D14
]
)
.

For
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
and
\rho\in[-1,1]
, the
Gaussian noise stability of
f
at
\rho
is defined to be

\mathbf{Stab}_{\rho}[f]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}}{\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})f(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\right]}={\left\langle f,\mathrm{U}_{\rho}f\right\rangle},

where
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}
are
\rho
-correlated
N(0,I_{n})
Gaussians (equivalently,
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{g}
are independent
N(0,I_{n})
Gaussians and
\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}=\rho\boldsymbol{z}+\sqrt{1-\rho^{2}}\boldsymbol{g}
).
For
\rho\in[0,1]
, the
Gaussian noise sensitivity of
f
at
\rho
is

\mathbf{GNS}_{\rho}[f]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\frac{1}{2}}-{\frac{1}{2}}\mathbf{Stab}_{1-2\rho}[f].

We observe that for
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
, we have

\mathbf{GNS}_{\rho}[f]=\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}}{\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})\neq f(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})\right]}=2\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}}{\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})=1,f(\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})=-1\right]},

where now
\boldsymbol{z},\boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}
are
(1-2\rho)
-correlated
N(0,I_{n})
Gaussians.

Gaussian noise sensitivity is important for us because the expected variance of
K_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
, over
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})
, is exactly captured by Gaussian noise sensitivity:

Lemma 62
.

Let
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set (viewed as a
\pm 1
-valued function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
). For
0\leq\lambda\leq 1
, we have that

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}_{\boldsymbol{y}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[K_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})]\right]}=2\mathbf{GNS}_{\lambda/2}[K].

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}[f(\boldsymbol{y})]={\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{{\bf E}}_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}}[(f(\boldsymbol{y})-f(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}))^{2}]
where
\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}
are i.i.d., the LHS above is equal to

\displaystyle{\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[(K_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})-K_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}))^{2}\right]}\right]}

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[{\left(K{\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}\right)}-K{\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right)}\right)}^{2}\right]}

\displaystyle={\frac{1}{2}}\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y},\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[2-2K{\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}\right)}K{\left(\sqrt{1-\lambda}\boldsymbol{x}+\sqrt{\lambda}\boldsymbol{y}^{\prime}\right)}\right]}

\displaystyle=1-\mathbf{Stab}_{1-\lambda}[K]=2\mathbf{GNS}_{\lambda/2}[K].\qed

8.4
Proof of
Theorem

49

All the pieces are now in place for us to prove
Theorem

49
. We first sketch the idea of the argument and then give quantitative details: Let
K
be a convex set whose Gaussian noise stability at some low noise rate
\lambda/2
is high, i.e.

\mathbf{GNS}_{\lambda/2}(K)\geq{\frac{1}{2}}-\kappa
where
\kappa
is small, and let
L
be any intersection of “not too many” halfspaces. Our goal is to show that
L
cannot be a good approximator for
K
; to do this, let us consider what happens if we apply a random zoom at noise rate

to both

and

•

By the results of the previous subsection, the expected variance of
K
after the random zoom is
2\mathbf{GNS}_{\lambda}[K]
, which is very close to 1. This means that almost every outcome
\boldsymbol{x}
of the random zoom must be such that the convex body
K_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
has Gaussian volume very close to
1/2
.

•

But by the results of
Section

, almost every outcome
\boldsymbol{x}
of the random zoom causes
L
to collapse to a function with very
low
variance, and hence almost every outcome of the convex body
L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
has Gaussian volume very close to either 0 or 1.

This means that “zoom outcome by zoom outcome”
L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
is typically a poor approximator for
K_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}
; it follows (formally, by
53
) that
L
must overall be a poor approximator for
K
.

We now proceed with the formal proof.

Proof of
Theorem

49
.

Let
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be a convex set with
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-c_{1}}}\geq{\frac{1}{2}}-\kappa.

By
Lemma

62
, we have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[K_{2n^{-c_{1}}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})\right]}\right]}\geq 1-2\kappa.

Since
\operatorname{{\bf Var}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[K_{2n^{-c_{1}}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})\right]}\in[0,1]
for all outcomes of
\boldsymbol{x}
, a standard reverse Markov argument gives that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[K_{2n^{-c_{1}}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})\right]}\geq 1-\sqrt{2\kappa}\right]}\geq 1-\sqrt{2\kappa}.

Consequently, for at least a
1-\sqrt{2\kappa}
fraction of outcomes of
\boldsymbol{x}
, we have that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[K_{2n^{-c_{1}}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1\right]}\in{\left[{\frac{1}{2}}-(2\kappa)^{1/4},{\frac{1}{2}}+(2\kappa)^{1/4}\right]}.

(61)

On the other hand let
L
be any intersection of at most
2^{\Theta(n^{c_{2}})}
many halfspaces over
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
c_{2}=2c_{1}/49.

By
Corollary

60
(taking its
\delta=1/\log k=n^{-c_{2}}
), we get that

\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[\operatorname{{\bf Var}}{\left(L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}\right)}\geq n^{-c_{2}}\right]}\leq n^{-c_{2}}.

So for a least a
1-n^{-c_{2}}
fraction of outcomes of
\boldsymbol{x}
, we have that

\min{\left\{\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1\right]},\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=-1\right]}\right\}}\leq n^{-c_{2}}.

(62)

Combining

and
Equation

62
, for at least a

fraction of outcomes of

{\left|\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[K_{2n^{-c_{1}}\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1\right]}-\mathop{{\bf Pr}\/}_{\boldsymbol{y}}{\left[L_{\lambda\mid\boldsymbol{x}}(\boldsymbol{y})=1\right]}\right|}\geq{\frac{1}{2}}-(2\kappa)^{1/4}-n^{-c_{2}}.

Applying

8.5
Existence of Convex Sets with High Gaussian Noise Sensitivity at Low Noise

We now turn to the proof of
Theorem

50
.
The proof is by contradiction; so fix a constant
0<\tau<1/4
, and suppose that every convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-\tau}}(K)<1/2-n^{-\tau}
.
Viewing
K
as a
\{-1,1\}
-valued indicator function (where
x\in K
iff
K(x)=1
) and recalling
Definition

61
, this is equivalent to the assertion that every convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
satisfies
\mathbf{Stab}_{1-2n^{-\tau}}(K)>2n^{-\tau}
.

We recall the well-known fact (see
[
O’D14
, Prop. 11.37]
) that the noise stability at noise rate
\rho
has an exact expression in terms of the amount of “Hermite weight” (in the Hermite expansion) at each weight level
0,1,\dots.
More precisely, we have that for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
and
\rho\in[-1,1]
,

\mathbf{Stab}_{\rho}[f]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\rho^{|\alpha|}\widetilde{f}(\alpha)^{2},

where
\widetilde{f}(\alpha)
is the
\alpha
-th Hermite coefficient in the Hermite expansion of
f
. In our context, this gives us that every convex set
K
satisfies

\mathbf{Stab}_{1-2n^{-\tau}}[K]=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}{\left(1-2n^{-\tau}\right)}^{|\alpha|}\widetilde{K}(\alpha)^{2}>2n^{-\tau}.

(63)

Fix
\ell\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=2\tau\ln(n)n^{\tau}
. Since, by Parseval’s identity, we have
\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\tilde{f}(\alpha)^{2}=\operatorname{{\bf E}}[K^{2}]=1,
we have that all of the Hermite coefficients at levels
\ell
and beyond can together only make a small contribution to
\mathbf{Stab}_{1-2n^{-\tau}}[K]
, i.e.

\sum_{|\alpha|\geq\ell}{\left(1-2n^{-\tau}\right)}^{|\alpha|}\widetilde{K}(\alpha)^{2}\leq\sum_{|\alpha|\geq\ell}e^{-\tau\ln(n)}\widetilde{K}(\alpha)^{2}\leq n^{-\tau}.

(64)

Equations

63
and
64
together give a lower bound on the Hermite weight of any convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
at levels
0
through
\ell
, i.e.

\sum_{|\alpha|\leq\ell}\widetilde{K}(\alpha)^{2}\geq\sum_{|\alpha|\geq\ell}{\left(1-2n^{-\tau}\right)}^{|\alpha|}\widetilde{K}(\alpha)^{2}>n^{-\tau}.

(65)

Since the Hermite polynomials form an orthonormal basis for
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
,
Equation

65
is equivalent to the existence of a degree-
\ell
polynomial
p
whose
L^{2}
-distance from
K
is bounded away from 1, i.e.

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,1)^{n}}{\left[(p(\boldsymbol{x})-K(\boldsymbol{x}))^{2}\right]}<1-n^{-\tau}.

Given the existence of such a polynomial
p
, it is well known that the “low-degree algorithm”, which uses calls to a black-box oracle for
K
to estimate each Hermite coefficient
\widetilde{K}(\alpha)
for
|\alpha|\leq\ell
, makes at most
n^{O(\ell)}
black-box oracle calls to
K
and constructs a degree-
\ell
polynomial
g
such that with probability at least
1-1/n
(over the internal randomness of the low-degree algorithm) the polynomial
g
satisfies

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}{\left[(g-K)^{2}\right]}<1-n^{-\tau}/2.

Given such a polynomial
g
, a standard approach (see Lemma 3 of
[
BFJ+94
]
) transforms
g
into a randomized hypothesis
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{0,1\}
which satisfies
\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,1)^{n}}{\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\neq K(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}\leq{\frac{1}{2}}\operatorname{{\bf E}}[(g-K)^{2}]<1/2-n^{-\tau}/4.

(The transformation is as follows: for each
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, have
h(x)=-1
with probability
p\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}={\frac{(1-g(x))^{2}}{2(1+g(x)^{2})}}
and
h(x)=1
with probability
1-p
.)

Summarizing the above, we have the following: Under the assumption that every convex set
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
has
\mathbf{GNS}_{n^{-\tau}}(K)<1/2-n^{-\tau}
, there is a “weak learning algorithm” with the following performance guarantee: given black-box oracle access to any unknown convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the algorithm makes at most
n^{O(\ln(n)n^{\tau})}
oracle calls and returns a randomized hypothesis function
h\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\{-1,1\}
which satisfies

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[h(\boldsymbol{x})\neq K(\boldsymbol{x})\right]}<1/2-n^{-\tau}/4+1/n<1/2-n^{-\tau}/8,

(66)

where
\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,1)^{n}
and the probability is over the randomness of
\boldsymbol{x}
, the internal randomness of the learning algorithm, and the internal randomness of the randomized hypothesis
h
. In other words, the expected error of the weak learning algorithm is at most
1/2-n^{-\tau}/8.

However, this is in tension with the main lower bound of
[
DS21
]
, which gives an information-theoretic lower bound on the minimum possible error that can be achieved by any weak learning algorithm for convex sets that does not make too many black-box queries, and was stated earlier as
Theorem

48
.
Taking
\tau^{\prime}={\frac{1}{2}}(\tau+1/4)<1/4
, and taking
s=n^{O(n^{\tau^{\prime}})}
,
Theorem

48
gives that any algorithm making at most
s
black-box oracle calls must have expected error at least
1/2-O(\ln(n)n^{\tau/2-3/8}).
Since
\tau
is strictly less than
1/4
this is in contradiction with
Equation

66
, and
Theorem

50
is proved.

9.1
Convex Influences

The following notion was introduced in
[
DNS21b
,
DNS22
]
as an analogue of the well-studied notion of
influence of a variable on a Boolean function
(cf. Chapter 2 of
[
O’D14
]
).

Definition 65
.

Given a convex set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0^{n}\in K
, the
convex influence of a direction
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
on
K
is defined as

\mathbf{Inf}_{v}[K]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[K(\boldsymbol{x}){\left({1-{\left\langle\boldsymbol{x},v\right\rangle}^{2}}\right)}\right]},

where
K(\cdot)
is the 0/1-valued indicator function of the convex set
K
.
We further define the
total convex influence of
K
as

\mathbf{I}[K]\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{Inf}_{e_{i}}[K]=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[K(\boldsymbol{x}){\left({n-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}}\right)}\right]}.

We note that the definitions of
\mathbf{Inf}_{v}[K]
and
\mathbf{I}[K]
as defined in
[
DNS22
]
include an additional multiplicative factor of
{1}/{\sqrt{2}}
that we omit here.
The total convex influence as defined above can be understood as capturing the rate of growth of the Gaussian measure of a convex set under dilations. More formally, we have the following:

Proposition 66

(Dilation formulation of convex influence)
.

Given
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0^{n}\in K
, we have

\mathbf{I}[K]=\lim_{\delta\to 0}\frac{\mathrm{Vol}{\left((1+\delta)K\right)}-\mathrm{Vol}(K)}{\delta}.

Note that if
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is convex with
0^{n}\in K
, then
\mathbf{I}[K]
is non-negative.
Proposition

66
is analogous to the well-known Margulis–Russo lemma
[
Mar74
,
Rus78
]
from the analysis of Boolean functions, and a proof of it can be found in Appendix A of
[
DNS21a
]
. We note that a similar “dilation formulation” holds for the convex influence of a single direction
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
on
K
, although we will not require it here.

We will use the following alternative formulation of the total convex influence of a convex set, which was communicated to us by Joe Neeman
[
Nee22
]
:

Lemma 67

(Influence via a surface integral)
.

Given a measurable set
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0^{n}\in K
and a direction
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
, we have

\mathbf{I}[K]=\int_{\partial K}{\left\langle x,\nu_{x}\right\rangle}\cdot\varphi(x)\,d\sigma(x)

where
\nu_{x}
denotes the unit normal to
\partial K
at
x
.

Proof.

The proof is a straightforward computation using integration by parts. Recall that via
Definition

65
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}[K]

\displaystyle=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{x}\sim N(0,I_{n})}{\left[K(\boldsymbol{x}){\left(n-\|\boldsymbol{x}\|^{2}\right)}\right]}

\displaystyle=\int_{K}(n-\|x\|^{2})\cdot\varphi(x)\,dx

\displaystyle=\int_{K}\mathcal{L}{\left(\frac{\|x\|^{2}}{2}\right)}\cdot\varphi(x)\,dx

where for a function
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
we define
\mathcal{L}(f)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\Delta f-{\left\langle\nabla f,\nabla f\right\rangle}
. Integrating by parts then gives

\displaystyle\mathbf{I}[K]

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial K}{\left\langle x,\nu_{x}\right\rangle}\cdot\varphi(x)\,d\sigma(x),

completing the proof.
∎

Remark 68
.

We note that the surface integral formulation of total convex influence can be viewed as analogous to the fact that the total influence of a Boolean function is equal to its average sensitivity (cf. Chapter 2 of
[
O’D14
]
). Although we will not require it for our purposes, we note that a similar formulation holds for the convex influence of a direction
v\in\mathbb{S}^{n-1}
on
K
:

\mathbf{Inf}_{v}[K]=\int_{\partial K}{\left\langle x,v\right\rangle}\cdot{\left\langle\nu_{x},v\right\rangle}\cdot\varphi(x)\,d\sigma(x)

where as before
\nu_{x}
denotes the unit normal to
\partial K
at
x
.

9.3
Lower Bounds for Approximating Convex Sets with Maximal Influence

We finally establish the following lower bound on approximating symmetric convex sets with close-to-maximal convex influence. (The specific constants in the theorem below were chosen mostly for concreteness; other constants could have been used instead.)

Theorem 70
.

Suppose
K\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a symmetric convex set with
\mathrm{Vol}(K)=1/2\pm o_{n}(1)
and
\mathbf{I}[K]\geq 0.1\sqrt{n}
. Then any convex polytope
L
that
6.25\times 10^{-7}
-approximates

must have at least

2^{5.1\times 10^{-6}\sqrt{n}}

Proof.

Theorem

70
can be inferred along the lines of the proof of Theorem A.1 from
[

]
, but we give a slightly simpler argument below.
Our proof will make use of the Hermite basis; we refer the reader to
Appendix

for a primer on Hermite analysis over the Gaussian measure.
Writing
e_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
for the standard basis vector along the
i^{\text{th}}
coordinate direction, we have that

\mathbf{Inf}_{e_{i}}[K]=\sqrt{2}\cdot\widetilde{K}(2e_{i}).

(This is an immediate consequence of the fact that the degree-2 univariate Hermite polynomial

is
{{\left(1-x^{2}\right)}/{\sqrt{2}}}.
)
Since

is symmetric, it follows from Proposition 9 of
[

]
that
\mathbf{Inf}_{e_{\boldsymbol{i}}}[K]\geq 0
.
We will use the following simple claim that relies on this fact:

Claim 71
.

Suppose

. Then at least

Proof.

Let

uniformly at random. We have

\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{i}\sim[n]}[\mathbf{Inf}_{e_{\boldsymbol{i}}}[K]]\geq\frac{0.1}{\sqrt{n}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\mathop{{\bf Var}\/}_{\boldsymbol{i}\sim[n]}{\left[\mathbf{Inf}_{e_{\boldsymbol{i}}}[K]\right]}\leq\frac{1}{n}

\boldsymbol{X}

\mu

\operatorname{{\bf Pr}}{\left[\boldsymbol{X}>\mu-\theta\sigma\right]}\geq\frac{\theta^{2}}{1+\theta^{2}}

),

Suppose that

Appendix A
Hermite Analysis over
N(0,I_{n})

Our notation and terminology follow Chapter 11 of
[
O’D14
]
. We say that an
n
-dimensional
multi-index
is a tuple
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, and we define

|\alpha|\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\alpha_{i}.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}_{>0}
, we write
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
to denote the space of functions
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
that have finite second moment under the Gaussian distribution, i.e.
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
if

\|f\|^{2}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{n})}\left[f(\boldsymbol{z})^{2}\right]^{1/2}<\infty.

We view
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
as an inner product space with

\langle f,g\rangle\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\mathop{{\bf E}\/}_{\boldsymbol{z}\sim N(0,I_{n})}[f(\boldsymbol{z})g(\boldsymbol{z})]

We recall the Hermite basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
:

Definition 74

(Hermite basis)
.

The
Hermite polynomials

(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
are the univariate polynomials defined as

h_{j}(x)=\frac{(-1)^{j}}{\sqrt{j!}}\exp\left(\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\cdot\frac{d^{j}}{dx^{j}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right).

The following fact is standard:

Fact 75

(Proposition 11.33 of
[
O’D14
]
)
.

The Hermite polynomials
(h_{j})_{j\in\mathbb{N}}
form a complete, orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R},\gamma)
. For
n>1
the collection of
n
-variate polynomials given by
(h_{\alpha})_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}
where

h_{\alpha}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}h_{\alpha_{i}}(x)

forms a complete, orthonormal basis for
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
.

Given a function
f\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
and
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}
, we define its
Hermite coefficient on

\alpha
as
\widetilde{f}(\alpha)=\langle f,h_{\alpha}\rangle
. It follows that
f\mathrel{\mathop{\mathchar 58\relax}}\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
can be uniquely expressed as

f=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widetilde{f}(\alpha)h_{\alpha}

with the equality holding in
L^{2}(\mathbb{R}^{n},\gamma)
; we will refer to this expansion as the
Hermite expansion
of
f
. One can check that Parseval’s and Plancharel’s identities hold in this setting:

{\left\langle f,f\right\rangle}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widetilde{f}(\alpha)^{2}\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\left\langle f,g\right\rangle}=\sum_{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}}\widetilde{f}(\alpha)\widetilde{g}(\alpha).
