Convergence of Batch Updating Methods with Approximate Gradients and/or Noisy Measurements: Theory and Computational Results
Tadipatri Uday Kiran Reddy, M. Vidyasagar

Abstract
In this paper, we present a unified and general framework for analyzing the batch updating approach to nonlinear, high-dimensional optimization. The framework encompasses all the currently used batch updating approaches, and is applicable to nonconvex as well as convex functions. Moreover, the framework permits the use of noise-corrupted gradients, as well as first-order approximations to the gradient (sometimes referred to as “gradient-free” approaches). By viewing the analysis of the iterations as a problem in the convergence of stochastic processes, we are able to establish a very general theorem, which includes most known convergence results for zeroth-order and first-order methods. The analysis of “second-order” or momentum-based methods is not a part of this paper, and will be studied elsewhere. However, numerical experiments indicate that momentum-based methods can fail if the true gradient is replaced by its first-order approximation. This requires further theoretical analysis.

Introduction

1.1
Scope of the Paper

In this paper we consider the general problem of minimizing a
possibly nonconvex
C^{1}
function
J:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
,
using batch updating of the argument.
In our nomenclature, batch updating refers to selecting
some

of the
d
components
of the current guess
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
at each time
t
, and then updating these
components along a randomly chosen “search direction”
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
.
Usually the search direction
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
is an approximation
to the negative gradient
-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
.
When the dimension
d
of the parameter
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
is very large,
computing the complete gradient

\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
and/or updating every component of
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}

at every iteration can be
extremely resource-intensive in both storage and CPU time.
For this reason, batch updating
(under whatever name) has become the
de facto

approach for large-scale minimization.
This approach goes under a variety of names in the
optimization and deep learning research communities,
including Coordinate Gradient Descent,
(Batch) Stochastic Gradient Descent, (Randomized) Block Coordinate Descent, etc.
Further, much of the theory is developed for convex optimization.
However, in problems of deep learning, the objective function
is definitely not convex.
Thus, ideally, any approach should also be applicable to nonconvex
optimization.
There are further considerations as well.
When methods such as back-propagation are used to compute
the gradient
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
, computing
only some components

of
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
does not necessarily result in significant savings
in computation.
On the other hand, function evaluation is much simpler than gradient
evaluation.
Therefore approximating some components of the gradient
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})

using first-order differences is a feasible approach.
(This approach also has a variety of names, such as zeroth-order
or derivative-free.)
While doing so, it would be realistic to permit some measurement
errors, that is, random disparities between the desired outputs of
the computation, and the actual computation.

In this paper, we propose a very general framework that encompasses
all the known batch updating paradigms.
Then we state and prove a very general result on the convergence of
batch updating, provided two specific conditions are satisfied.
It is shown that all widely used batch updating protocols do indeed
satisfy these conditions; therefore most available convergence results
for batch updating are included as special cases of our general result.
Moreover, the framework provides a readymade method of proving the
convergence of batch updating for protocols to be developed in future.

The analysis given here is applicable to methods that use true gradients
(possibly corrupted by noise) and approximate gradients (zeroth-order
or gradient-free methods).
Another popular approach to optimization is to use momentum-based methods,
wherein the current search direction is a linear combination of the
current gradient and the immediately preceding gradient (or noisy
versions thereof).
Methods such as Polyak’s Heavy Ball method
[
1
]
, Nesterov’s accelerated method

[
2
,
3
]
,
Sutskever’s method
[
4
]
,
Bengio’s method
[
5
]
,
ADAM
[
6
]
, NADAM
[
7
]
,
and so on.
A recent paper
[
8
]
establishes the convergence
of the ADAM algorithm using methods very similar to those used here.
However, that is for full updating and not batch updating.
Thus we plan to extend our approach to second-order or momentum-based
methods coupled with batch updating.

We then carry out a few numerical simulations to validate our analysis.
As expected, zeroth-order and first-order methods converge with
batch updating. Also, momentum-based methods with noise-corrupted gradients
and batch updating also converge. However, momentum-based methods that use
approximate gradients and batch updating fail to converge.
This shows that there is room for further theoretical analysis.

General Convergence Theorems for Batch Updating

In this section, we state
the main convergence theorem for batch updating.
The proof is given in the Appendix.

3.1
Various Assumptions

In this section, we state and interpret various assumptions about the
objective function and the search direction.
We begin with the objective function.

Suppose
J:{\mathbb{R}}^{d}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}
is a given
{\cal C}^{1}
function, and it is
desired to find a stationary point of
J(\cdot)
, that is,
a solution of
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})={\bf 0}
.
It is
not
assumed that the solution is unique.
Our objective is to ensure that the theory presented here embraces
nonconvex functions in addition to convex functions.
In presenting these assumptions, we make use of the following concept
from
[
54
, Definition 1]
:
A function
\eta:{\mathbb{R}}_{+}\rightarrow{\mathbb{R}}_{+}
is
said to
belong to Class
{\cal B}
if
\eta(0)=0
, and in addition

\inf_{\epsilon\leq r\leq M}\eta(r)>0,\;\forall 0<\epsilon<M<\infty.

The following is a list of various assumptions about the objective function.
Note that not every assumption is used in every theorem.

(J1).

J(.)
is
{\cal C}^{1}
and
\nabla J(\cdot)
is globally
Lipschtiz-continuous with constant
2L
.

(J2).

J(.)
has a global minimum
J^{*}
, which is attained.
Moreover,
J(\cdot)
has compact level sets, that is, for each constant

c\in{\mathbb{R}}
, the set
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}:J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq c\}
is compact.
1
1

1

Other
equivalent phrases are:
J(\cdot)
is radially unbounded, or

J(\cdot)
is coercive.

(J3).

Define

\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-J^{*}.

Then there exists a constant
C
such that

\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2}^{2}\leq C_{1}[J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-J^{*}],\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(J4).

There is a function
\mu(\cdot)
belonging to Class
{\cal B}
such that

\mu(\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}))\leq\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\|_{2},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(J5).

There is a function
\nu(\cdot)
belonging to Class
{\cal B}

such that

\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}},S(J))\leq\nu(\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}})),\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}.

(2)

where

\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}},S(J)):=\inf_{{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\in S(J)}\|{\boldsymbol{\theta}}-{\boldsymbol{\psi}}\|_{2}

is the distance between
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
and the set
S(J)
.

Now we interpret these assumptions.
In the scalar case
d=1
, if there is a unique minimum
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^{*}
,
Assumption (J3) is equivalent to
J(\theta)\leq(C_{1}/2)\theta^{2}
.
In higher dimensions, the assumption requires
J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})
to grow no
faster than quadratically in
\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}},S(J))
.
Assumptions (J4) and (J5) permit
J(\cdot)
to have multiple stationary points;
thus
S(J)
need not be a singleton set.
However, both imply that
J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})=J^{*}
for all
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in S(J)
;
thus every local minimum must also be a global minimum.
Assumption (J4) implies that, for any sequence
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
,
if
\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
is bounded away from zero, then so is
\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}
,
while Assumption (J5) implies that if
\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow 0
, then

\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},S(J))\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Now suppose

is
{\cal C}^{2}
is strictly convex,

0<\underline{c}\leq\bar{c}<\infty

\underline{c}I_{d}\leq\nabla^{2}J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})\leq\bar{c}I_{d},\;\forall{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d},

The algorithm for finding a stationary point of
J(\cdot)
is as follows:
Choose an initial guess
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
(usually deterministic).
At time
t+1\geq 1
, we choose a
random

“search direction”
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
, which satisfies certain
assumptions to be specified below.
Once
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
is chosen,
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
is updated according to

{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1}={\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\alpha_{t}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}.

(3)

Here,
\{\alpha_{t}\}
is a
deterministic
sequence of step sizes
where
\alpha_{t}\in(0,1)
for each
t
.
In batch updating we could, in principle, use “local clocks” to choose
a different step size for each updated component, as suggested in

[
55
]
.
However, in the interests of simplicity,
in this paper we stick to using a
global clock
,
so that every component that is updated uses the same step size.
Batch updating means that, at any time
t
, only some components of

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
could be nonzero, and the rest are set to zero.
Some possible options for selecting
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
are discussed in
Section

.

Let
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}^{t}
denote the tuple
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0},\cdots,{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}
,
and define
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{1}^{t}
analogously.
(Note that there is no
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{0}
.)
Let
\{{\cal F}_{t}\}
be a filtration (that is, an increasing sequence of

\sigma
-algebras) such that
{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{0}^{t},{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{1}^{t}
, and the random processes

that are involved in selecting the nonzero components of

.
For future use, let

denote the set of all functions

.

The general framework studied in

56
]
(which is our inspiration)
is that the search direction
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t}
is
random, but there is a fixed constant
\delta
such that

E(\langle\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}\rangle|{\cal F}_{t})\leq-\delta\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2},

where

denote the conditional expectation with

{\cal F}_{t}
.
Since the constant

can be absorbed into the step size

,
we choose
\delta=1
, and accordingly define:

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}:=E({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t})+\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}).

(4)

Thus
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}
is the difference between the conditional expectation
of the search direction, and the desired value
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
.
Observe that
\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}\in{\cal M}({\cal F}_{t})
.
Next, define
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}
as the “unpredictable part”
of the search direction, namely

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}:={\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}-E({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t}).

(5)

With this background, we make the following assumptions about the search
direction
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
:

(D1).

There is a sequence of deterministic constants
\{b_{t}\}
such
that

\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq b_{t}^{2},\;\forall t.

(6)

(D2).

There is a sequence of deterministic constants
\{\sigma_{t}\}
such
that

E(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})\leq\sigma_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}),\;\forall t.

(7)

3.2
Theorem Statement

With the above assumptions, we now state the convergence theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Define
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
via (
3
).
Then we can state the following conclusions.

1.

Suppose assumptions (J1) – (J3) and (D1) – (D2) are satisfied.
Suppose further that

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}<\infty,\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}b_{t}<\infty,\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}<\infty.

(8)

Then
\{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\}
,
\{\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\}
,
and
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
are bounded almost surely.

2.

Suppose that in addition to the assumptions in Item 1, we add

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty.

(9)

Then

\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}=0\mbox{ a.s.}

(10)

3.

Suppose that in addition to the assumptions in Item 2, we add Assumption
(J4).
Then
J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow J^{*}
almost surely, and

\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow{\bf 0}
almost surely, as

.

4.

Suppose that in addition to the assumptions in Item 3, we add Assumption

Then
\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},S(J))\rightarrow 0

(16)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}-2\langle\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+E(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})

(Proof of Theorem
1
:)

Item 1:

Recall the update rule (
3
) and apply Lemma
1
.
This gives

\displaystyle J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\alpha_{t}{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}\rangle+\alpha_{t}^{2}L\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Subtract
J^{*}
from both sides and define
\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}):=J({\boldsymbol{\theta}})-J^{*}
.
This gives

\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})\leq\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}\rangle+\alpha_{t}^{2}L\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now take conditional expectations and invoke (
4
), (
15
)
and (
16
).
This gives

\displaystyle E(\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})|{\cal F}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\alpha_{t}\langle\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}\rangle-\alpha_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}L[\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+E(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})].

Now apply Schwarz’s inquality, and assumptions (D1), (D2).
This gives

\displaystyle E(\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})|{\cal F}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\alpha_{t}b_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}-\alpha_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}L[b_{t}^{2}+2b_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}+\sigma_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2})].

Next, invoke (J3), and observe that

\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}\leq 2\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}\leq 1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\leq 1+C_{1}\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}).

This leads to

\displaystyle E(\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t+1})|{\cal F}_{t})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\alpha_{t}b_{t}(1+C_{1}\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))-\alpha_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\alpha_{t}^{2}L[b_{t}^{2}+b_{t}(1+C_{1}\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))+C_{1}\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+\sigma_{t}^{2}(1+C_{1}\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))].

After regrouping terms, the above bound is of the form (
11
) with

z_{t}=\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),\delta_{t}=\alpha_{t}b_{t}C_{1}+\alpha_{t}^{2}LC_{1}(b_{t}+1+\sigma_{t}^{2}),

\gamma_{t}=\alpha_{t}b_{t}+\alpha_{t}^{2}L(b_{t}^{2}+b_{t}+\sigma_{t}^{2}),\psi_{t}=\alpha_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

For Item 1, let us ignore
\psi_{t}
because it is non-negative.
Also, both
\{\delta_{t}\}
and
\{\gamma_{t}\}
are deterministic sequences.
Hence, if it can be shown that both sequences
\{\delta_{t}\}
and
\{\gamma_{t}\}

are summable, then it would follow from Lemma
1

that
\bar{J}({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
converges almost surely to some random
variable
\eta
, and is bounded along almost all sample paths.
Now (J3) implies that
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
is also bounded almost surely,
while the compactness of the level sets of
J(\cdot)
implies that

\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
is bounded almost surely.
Thus we focus on establishing the summability of these two sequences

\{\delta_{t}\}
and
\{\gamma_{t}\}
.

For this purpose, we recall the hypotheses in (
8
).
Using these, it is shown that each term in the definitions of
\delta_{t}

and
\gamma_{t}
is summable.
First,

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}b_{t}<\infty
, which is the first term (modulo
the constant
C_{1}
) in both
\delta_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}
.
Next,

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}b_{t}<\infty\;\Longrightarrow\;\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}b_{t}^{2}<\infty,

which is the second term in
\gamma_{t}
.
Next, because
\alpha_{t}
is bounded by one, we have

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}b_{t}<\infty\;\Longrightarrow\;\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}b_{t}<\infty.

which is the second term in
\delta_{t}
and the third term in
\gamma_{t}
.
Next, by assumption
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}<\infty
, which is the
third term in
\delta_{t}
.
Last, by assumption
\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}\sigma_{t}^{2}<\infty
,
which is the fourth and final term (modulo
C_{1}
) in both
\delta_{t}
and
\gamma_{t}
.
Hence both sequences are summable, which establishes Item 1.

Item 2:

Now we make use of the second part of Lemma
1
, namely

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\psi_{t}=\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}<\infty\mbox{ a.s.}

Suppose there exists an
\omega\in\Omega
such that

\liminf_{t\rightarrow\infty}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})(\omega)\|_{2}^{2}>0,\mbox{ say }2\epsilon.

Choose a time
T
such that

\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})(\omega)\|_{2}^{2}\geq\epsilon,\;\forall t\geq T,

and observe that

\sum_{t=T}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty.

Then

\sum_{t=T}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})(\omega)\|_{2}^{2}\geq\epsilon\sum_{t=T}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty,

(17)

which shows that the set of such
\omega\in\Omega
must have measure zero.
This is Item 2.

Item 3:

Now it is assumed that Assumption (J4) also holds in addition to (J3).
Define

\Omega_{0}
to be the set of
\omega\in\Omega
such that

\lim_{t\rightarrow\infty}J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})=\eta(\omega)<\infty
, and observe that
P(\Omega_{0})=1
.
Now suppose
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
, and define

M=M(\omega)=\sup_{t}J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})<\infty
.
Suppose by way of contradiction that
\eta(\omega)>0
, say

\eta(\omega)>2\epsilon
.
Choose
T
sufficiently large that
J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\geq\epsilon
for all
t\geq T
.
Then by (J4), it follows that, for all
t\geq T
, we have

\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}\geq\min_{\epsilon\leq r\leq M}\mu(r)=:c>0.

Now apply (
17
) with
\epsilon
replaced by
c
,
which leads to a contradiction.
Hence the set of
\omega\in\Omega_{0}
for which
\eta(\omega)>0

has measure zero, or
J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow J^{*}
almost surely, and

\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow{\bf 0}
almost surely.

Item 4:

We already know from Item 3 that
J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow J^{*}
almost surely.
Now apply Assumption (J5).

4
Various Batch Updating Options

In this section, we first propose some possible choices of search directions

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
for batch updating.
These include many commonly used choices.
Then we analyze each of these choices, and
show that they all satisfy the standard assumptions (D1) and (D2).
Usually (D1) is straight-forward and the main challenge is to establish (D2).
Once it is established that both (D1) and (D2) are satisfied, then one
can apply Theorem
1
to conclude that the iterative algorithm
(
3
) converges to a solution for any objective function
J(\cdot)

that satisfies Assumptions (J1) through (J5), or some subset thereof,
provided the step size conditions (
8
) and (
9
) are
satisfied.

4.1
Various Options for Batch Updating

In this subsection we study different options that are possible to
fit in our convergence theorem.

Option 1:

The first of these is the standard steepest descent with possibly noise-corrupted
gradient measurements.
Let

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}=-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1},

(18)

where
\{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
is a sequence of unstructured disturbances,
including measurement errors.
Following the usage in this area, we will refer to these as “measurement
noise.” The noise
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}
is assumed to satisfy

E({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t})={\bf 0}\;\forall t,

(19)

E(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})\leq M_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2})\;\forall t,

(20)

for a known set of constants
M_{t}
. These assumptions applies to all options
studied here.

Option 2:

The next option is “coordinate gradient descent” as defined in

[
60
]
and studied further in
[
61
]
.
At time
t
, choose an index
\kappa_{t+1}\in[d]
at random with a
uniform probability, and independently of previous choices.
Let
{\bf e}_{\kappa_{t+1}}
denote the elementary unit vector with a
1

as the
\kappa_{t+1}
-th component and zeros elsewhere,
and let
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}
denote the measurement noise.
Then define

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}=d{\bf e}_{\kappa_{t+1}}\circ[-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}].

(21)

The factor of
d
arises because the likelihood that
\kappa_{t+1}

equalling any one index
i\in[d]
is
1/d
.
Thus, with the introduction of the factor
d
, it follows that if
we ignore the noise term
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}
for the time being, then

E({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t})=-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}).

Note that, though
\kappa_{t+1}
is used at time
t+1
,
it is
chosen
at time
t
, that is,
prior

to the update at time
t+1
.
Hence
\kappa_{t+1}\in{\cal M}({\cal F}_{t})
.

Option 3:

This is option is just coordinate gradient descent along multiple
coordinates chosen at random.
At time
t
, choose
N
different indices
\kappa_{t+1,n}
from
[d]

with replacement
, with each choice being independent of the rest,
and also of past choices.
Moreover, each
\kappa_{t+1,n}
is chosen from
[d]
with uniform probability.
Then define

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}:=\frac{d}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}{\bf e}_{\kappa_{t+1}^{n}}\circ[-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1,n}],

(22)

where
\{\xi_{t,n},t\geq 1,n\in[N]\}
is the noise sequence.
In this option,
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
can have
up to

N
nonzero
components.
Because the sampling is
with replacement
, there might
be some duplicated samples.
In such a case, the corresponding component of
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
simply
gets counted multiple times in (
22
).

Option 4:

This is a variant of coordinate gradient descent.
At time
t+1
, let
\{B_{t+1,i},i\in[d]\}
be independent Bernoulli
processes with success rate
\rho_{t+1}
.
Thus

\Pr\{B_{t+1,i}=1\}=\rho_{t+1},\;\forall i\in[d].

(23)

It is permissible for the success probability
\rho_{t+1}
to vary with time.
However, at any one time, all components must have the same success
probability.
Define

{\bf v}_{t+1}:=\sum_{i=1}^{d}{\bf e}_{i}I_{\{B_{t+1,i}=1\}}\in\{0,1\}^{d}.

(24)

Thus
{\bf v}_{t+1,i}
equals
1
if
B_{t+1,i}=1
, and equals
0
otherwise.
Now define

{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}=\frac{1}{\rho_{t+1}}{\bf v}_{t+1}\circ[-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})+{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}].

(25)

In this option,
the search direction
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
can have up to
d
nonzero components.
However, the
expected
number of nonzero components is
\rho_{t+1}d
.

Options 1A through 4A:

In these options, we apply Options 1 through 4, after replacing
the actual gradient
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
,
by a first-order approximation, as suggested first in

[
41
]
.
Let
\{c_{t}\}
be a predetermined sequence of “increments” (not to be
confused with the step size sequence
\{\alpha_{t}\}
).
At each time
t+1
, define the approximate gradient
{\bf y}_{t+1}\in{\mathbb{R}}^{d}
by

y_{t+1,i}=\frac{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})+\xi_{t+1,i}^{-}-J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})-\xi_{t+1,i}^{+}}{2c_{t}},

(26)

where
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{-},{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{+}
are zero-mean measurement noises
satisfying
(
34
) that

E({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{-}|{\cal F}_{t})={\bf 0}\;\forall t,E({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{+}|{\cal F}_{t})={\bf 0}\;\forall t,

(27)

\displaystyle\max\{E(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t}),E(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{+}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})\}

\displaystyle\leq M_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2})\;\forall t,

(28)

for suitable constants
M_{t}
.

4.2
Convergence Theorems

Theorem 2
.

Suppose the objective function
J(\cdot)
satisfies assumptions (J1)–(J5),
and suppose
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
is chosen according to
any one of Options 1 through 4.

1.

Suppose the step size sequence
\{\alpha_{t}\}
satisfies

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}<\infty,\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}M_{t}^{2}<\infty.

(29)

Then
\{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\}
,
\{\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\}
and
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
are
all bounded almost surely.

2.

If, in addition to (
29
), the step size sequence
\{\alpha_{t}\}

also satisfies

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty,

(30)

then
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow{\bf 0}
and
\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},S(J))\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, almost
surely as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Note that if
M_{t}\leq M
, a fixed constant for all
t
, then the two conditions
in (
29
) are equivalent.
Moreover, taken together, (
29
) and (
30
) become
the well-known Robbins-Monro conditions
[
33
]
.

Theorem 3
.

Suppose the objective function
J(\cdot)
satisfies assumptions (J1)–(J5),
and suppose we apply batch updating with any one of Options 1A through 4A.
Under these conditions,

1.

Suppose that the step size sequence
\{\alpha_{t}\}
satisfies

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}^{2}<\infty,\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}c_{t}<\infty,\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}(\alpha_{t}M_{t}/c_{t})^{2}<\infty.

(31)

Then
\{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\}
,
\{\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\}
and
\{{\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}\}
are
all bounded almost surely.

2.

If, in addition to (
31
), we have that

\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\alpha_{t}=\infty,

(32)

then
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rightarrow{\bf 0}
and
\rho({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t},S(J))\rightarrow 0
as
t\rightarrow\infty
, almost
surely as
t\rightarrow\infty
.

Note that if
M_{t}\leq M
, a fixed constant for all
t
, then the conditions
in (
31
) become the so-called “Blum conditions,” first proposed
in
[
42
]
.

4.3
Analysis of Various Batch Updating Options

In this subsection, we analyze the various batch updating options
in Section
4.1
.
To keep the notation from getting overly cumbersome, it is assumed that in
Options 1 through 4, the noise
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}
satisfies

E({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t})={\bf 0}\;\forall t,

(33)

E(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})\leq M_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\;\forall t,

(34)

for a known set of constants
M_{t}
.
With these assumptions, each of Options 1 through 4, “on average”
the search direction
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
equals
-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
.
Therefore Assumption (D1) is satisfied with
b_{t}=0
for all
t
.
This is
not true
in Options 1A through 4A.
The discussion below will reveal that more general models can be
accommodated at the expense of more cumbersome notation.

In
Option 1
, it is easy to see that

E({\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t})=-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}={\bf 0},\;\forall t.

Therefore Assumption (D1) is satisfied with
b_{t}=0
and

M_{t}^{2}
as in (
34
).

In
Option 2
, there are two factors contributing to the error

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}
.
First, even if
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}={\bf 0}
, the search direction
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
equals

-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})
only “on average,” and there is a variance due to this.
Second, the variance due to the noise
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}
also needs to be
taken into account.

Let
i
denote
\kappa_{t+1}
.
Then

\phi_{t+1,j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}-(d-1)(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))_{j},&\mbox{if }j=i,\\
(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))_{j},&\mbox{if }j\neq i.\end{array}\right.

Therefore

\sum_{j=1}^{d}(\phi_{t+1,j}-(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}))_{j})^{2}=(d-1)^{2}(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{i})^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{j})^{2}.

\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}=\sum_{j=1}^{d}[(d-1)^{2}(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{i})^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{j})^{2}].

Now
\kappa_{t+1}=i
with probability
1/d
.
Therefore the conditional probability

\displaystyle E(\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{j=1}^{d}[(d-1)^{2}(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{i})^{2}+\sum_{j\neq i}(\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{j})^{2}]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}\left[\frac{(d-1)^{2}+(d-1)}{d}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(d-1)\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}.

Since

E(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})=E(\|{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t}),

if
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}\neq{\bf 0}
, its conditional variance simply adds to the above.
Therefore (D2) is satisfied with

\sigma_{t}^{2}=(d-1)+M_{t}^{2}.

In
Option 3
,
{\boldsymbol{\phi}}_{t+1}
is the average of
N
different quantities
wherein the error terms
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}^{n},n\in[N]
are independent.
Therefore their variances just add up, an again Assumption (D2)
holds with
\sigma_{t}^{2}=(d-1)+M_{t}^{2}
.

Next we come to
Option 4
.
For notational simplicity, we just use
\rho
in the place of
\rho_{t+1}
.
In this case, each component
\phi_{t+1,i}
equals
-(1/\rho)\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{i}

with probability
\rho
, and
0
with probability
1-\rho
.
Thus
\zeta_{t+1,i}
equals
1-(1/\rho)\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{i}
with probability
\rho
,
and
\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})_{i}
with probability
1-\rho
.
As can be easily verified, the variance is

(1-\rho)/\rho
for each component.
As the Bernoulli processes for each component are mutually independent,
the variances simply add up.
It follows that

E(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})=\frac{d(1-\rho)}{\rho}.

Hence Assumption (D2) holds with

\sigma_{t}^{2}=\frac{d(1-\rho)}{\rho}+M_{t}^{2}.

Finally we come to
Option 1A through 4A
, which is distinguished by the fact that
we cannot take
b_{t}=0
in general.
Define
{\bf z}_{t+1}
by

z_{t,i}=\frac{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})-J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})}{2c_{t}},\;\forall i\in[d].

(35)

Then it is clear that the search direction
{\bf y}_{t+1}
in (
26
)
is given by

{\bf y}_{t+1}=-{\bf z}_{t}+\frac{{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}^{-}-{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t}^{+}}{2c_{t}}.

(36)

Recall from (
27
) and (
4.1
) that

E({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{-}|{\cal F}_{t})={\bf 0}\;\forall t,E({\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{+}|{\cal F}_{t})={\bf 0}\;\forall t,

(37)

\max\{E(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{-}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t}),E(\|{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{+}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})\}\leq M_{t}^{2}(1+\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}^{2})\;\forall t,

(38)

for suitable constants
M_{t}
.

Lemma 3
.

With
{\bf y}_{t+1}
defined as in (
36
), define, in analogy with
(
4
) and (
5
),

\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}=E(\|{\bf y}_{t+1}\|_{2}|{\cal F}_{t})+\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}),

(39)

{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}={\bf y}_{t+1}-E(\|{\bf y}_{t+1}\|_{2}|{\cal F}_{t}).

(40)

Then

\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}\|_{2}^{2}\leq\sqrt{d}Lc_{t},

(41)

E(\|{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}\|_{2}^{2}|{\cal F}_{t})\leq\frac{M_{t}^{2}}{2c_{t}^{2}}

(42)

Proof.

By (
27
), it follows that

E({\bf y}_{t+1}|{\cal F}_{t})=-{\bf z}_{t},\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}=-{\bf z}_{t}+\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}).

Next, we adapt the proof of Lemma
2

taken from
[
59
]

to the problem at hand, and write

\displaystyle z_{t,i}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})-J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}-c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})}{2c_{t}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{d}{d\lambda}[J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})]\;d\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2c_{t}}\int_{-1}^{1}c_{t}\langle{\bf e}_{i},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})\rangle\;d\lambda

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}[\langle{\bf e}_{i},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle\langle{\bf e}_{i},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle]\;d\lambda.

Now apply Assumption (J1) and Schwarz’s inequality.
This gives

\displaystyle|\langle{\bf e}_{i},\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\rangle|

\displaystyle\leq\|\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t}+\lambda c_{t}{\bf e}_{i})-\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})\|_{2}\leq 2L\lambda c_{t}.

Therefore

z_{t+1,i}\leq[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{i}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{-1}^{1}2L\lambda c_{t}\;d\lambda=[\nabla J({\boldsymbol{\theta}}_{t})]_{i}+Lc_{t}.

Therefore

\|\tilde{{\boldsymbol{\phi}}}_{t}\|_{2}\leq\sqrt{d}Lc_{t}.

This proves (
41
).
To prove (
42
), observe that
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{-}
and
{\boldsymbol{\xi}}_{t+1}^{+}

are independent.
Therefore the conditional variance of
{\boldsymbol{\zeta}}_{t+1}
is the sum of the
conditional variances of these two terms, divided by
4c_{t}^{2}
,
which is bounded by
M_{t}^{2}/2c_{t}^{2}
.
∎

6
Conclusions

In this paper, we have presented a general and unified framework for
establishing the convergence of batch updating algorithms.
Then we have presented sufficient conditions for the convergence of
these batch updating algorithms.
Our method of proof is based on stochastic approximation theory,
specifically
[
14
]
.
The results are applicable to nonconvex as well as convex objective
functions.
In particular, the search direction can be a batch-updated version of
any of the following:

•

A noisy measurement of the exact gradient.

•

A first-order approximation of the gradient based on
noisy function measurements (also called zeroth-order or derivative-free
methods).

Numerical experiments show that when as little as 5% of the coordinates
are updated at each iteration, and approximate gradients are used,
batch updating
still converges, while batch momentum-based convex optimization methods
either diverge or fail to converge at quite low noise levels.
The existing convergence theory for
momentum-based algorithms does not apply to the
case of batch updating.
Extending the present theory to commonly used algorithms like ADAM/NADAM is a
current topic of investigation.
