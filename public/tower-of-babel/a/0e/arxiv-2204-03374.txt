Is the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat detectable?
Jonte R. Hance, James Ladyman, John Rarity

Abstract
We explore how one might detect the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat proposed by Aharonov et al. We show that, in practice, we need to bias the initial state by adding/subtracting a small probability amplitude (‘field’) of the orthogonal state, which travels with the disembodied property, to make the effect detectable (i.e. if our initial state is | ↑ z ⟩ \left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle , we need to bias this with some small amount δ \delta of state | ↓ z ⟩ \left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle ). This biasing, which can be done either directly or via weakly entangling the state with a pointer, effectively provides a phase reference with which we can measure the evolution of the state. The outcome can then be measured as a small probability difference in detections in a mutually unbiased basis, proportional to this biasing δ \delta . We show this is different from counterfactual communication, which provably does not require any probe field to travel between sender Bob and receiver Alice for communication. We further suggest an optical polarisation experiment where these phenomena might be demonstrated in a laboratory.

II
Dynamical quantum Cheshire cat

Figure 1:
Aharonov et al’s protocol for applying the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat. The yellow line is a semi-transparent partition with reflection-transmission ratio

In their paper
Aharonov2021Dynamical
, Aharonov et al propose a protocol consisting of two coupled cavities of equal length, connected via a semi-transparent partition with transmissivity parameter
\epsilon=\pi/2N
for large integer
N

A single-particle wave-packet is introduced into the left-hand cavity before its left-side wall is closed, and evolves to have small amounts of field cross the semi-transparent partition and probe the spin-dependent wall closing the right-hand side of the right-hand cavity. This wall transmits field with spin
\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle

\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle

The overall length of the two coupled cavities is distance
D

D/T
). As the two cavities are of equal length, it takes the wave-packet reflected by the semi-transparent partition time
T

The key times for us to consider are

, for integer

\left|L\right\rangle
at the leftmost wall, a wave-packet
\left|R\right\rangle
just reflected at the spin-dependent right wall, a wave-packet
\left|0\right\rangle
just transmitted at the spin-dependent right wall, and states
\left|k\right\rangle
for integer

0\leq k\leq n-1

from the spin-dependant right-hand wall.

Given we input the particle in spin state
\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle
, the evolution between time

and

is given by unitary operator
\hat{U}
where

\begin{split}\hat{U}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle&=\cos{\epsilon}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle+\sin{\epsilon}\left|0\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle,\\
\hat{U}\left|k\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle&=\left|(k+1)\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle\end{split}.

(1)

Therefore, given the state starts at
\left|\Psi(n=0)\right\rangle=\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle
, the overall state at

is

\begin{split}\left|\Psi(n)\right\rangle=\cos^{n}{\epsilon}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle+\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}\sin{\epsilon}\cos^{k}{\epsilon}\left|(n-1-k)\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle.\end{split}

(2)

We now want to see what happens after large integer

. Due to the interrupted evolution from leaks through the spin-dependant wall, it would take the wave-packet time of order

Ergo, the state at time

\begin{split}\left|\Psi(2NT)\right\rangle&=\hat{U}^{2N}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle\\
&=\cos^{2N}{(\pi/2N)}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle\\
&\approx\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle.\end{split}

(3)

So far, so simple.
However, something weird happens when we consider this
\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle
spin decomposed as
(\left|\uparrow_{x}\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow_{x}\right\rangle)/\sqrt{2}
. To see this, consider the effect of the same system on
\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle

\left|L\right\rangle

\left|R\right\rangle

Therefore, the evolution of

\begin{split}&\hat{U}^{n}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle=\cos{(n\epsilon)}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle+\sin{(n\epsilon)}\left|R\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle,\\
&\hat{U}^{n}\left|R\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle=\cos{(n\epsilon)}\left|R\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle-\sin{(n\epsilon)}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle,\end{split}

(4)

an so, after time
2NT

\hat{U}^{2N}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle=-\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle.

(5)

This means, if we start with state
\left|\uparrow_{x}\right\rangle
, after time
2NT

\begin{split}\hat{U}^{2N}\left|L\right\rangle\left|\uparrow_{x}\right\rangle&=\hat{U}^{2N}\left|L\right\rangle\frac{\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\\
&=\left|L\right\rangle\frac{\cos^{2N}{(\pi/2N)}\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle+\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}+\text{Loss}\\
&\approx\left|L\right\rangle\frac{\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle-\left|\downarrow_{z}\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}=\left|L\right\rangle\left|\downarrow_{x}\right\rangle.\end{split}

(6)

Similar evolution occurs when, starting with
\left|\downarrow_{x}\right\rangle

\hat{U}^{\dagger,2N}\left|L\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle L\right|\sigma_{x}\hat{U}^{2N}\approx\left|L\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle L\right|(-\sigma_{x}),

(7)

where

is the Pauli-
x
operator (for spin,
\left|\uparrow_{x}\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle\uparrow_{x}\right|-\left|\downarrow_{x}\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle\downarrow_{x}\right|
) This is different to if the right-hand-cavity’s right-hand-wall was just open, as that would cause no flipping of

This leads us to ask whether we can observe this evolution when no element of the wavepacket ever returns from the right-hand side (i.e. when the initial state is
\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle
)?

Detecting the effect directly

IV
Detecting via weak value of
\sigma_{x}

Given we just showed that the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat effect cannot be observed directly using homodyne measurement, we now see if it is observable theoretically using weak values. Note that, while calculating weak values theoretically doesn’t cause the same coupling errors as weak measurement, actually obtaining a weak value experimentally causes disturbances, due to the initial weak measurement required to entangle the system with the pointer
Kastner2017DemystifWeakVals
;
Ipsen2022Disturbance
. This can be seen in Vaidman’s nested interferometer
Vaidman2013Past
;
Hance2021WeakTrace
;
Vaidman2023CommWeakVals
;
Hance2023ReplyCommWeakVals
. Therefore, it is debatable whether this measurement actually observes the effect on an unperturbed system, or just the effect applied to some perturbation from our system of interest.

In their paper, Aharonov et al proposed demonstrating the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat effect
by weakly measuring
\sigma_{x}
at the start of the experiment, then strongly measuring it after time
2NT
, to get the weak value of
\sigma_{x}
as

\sigma_{x}^{w}\approx-1.

(9)

They obtain this from the weak value formula

\hat{A}^{w}=\frac{\left\langle\psi_{f}\right|\hat{A}\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle}{\langle\psi_{f}|\psi_{i}\rangle},

(10)

where in this case,
\hat{A}=\sigma_{x}
,
\left|\psi_{i}\right\rangle=\left|H\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle
, and

\begin{split}\left|\psi_{f}\right\rangle&=\hat{U}^{2N}\left|D\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle\\
&=\frac{\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\left|H\right\rangle-\left|V\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\left|L\right\rangle+\text{ Loss}\\
&\approx\left|A\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle.\end{split}

(11)

Therefore,

\begin{split}\sigma_{x}^{w}&=\frac{\left\langle L\right|\left\langle A\right|\Big(\left|D\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle D\right|-\left|A\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle A\right|\Big)\left|H\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle}{\left\langle L\right|\langle A|H\rangle\left|L\right\rangle}\\
&=\frac{-1/\sqrt{2}}{1/\sqrt{2}}=-1.\end{split}

(12)

Looking more generally, we see that if we give the postselected final state as

\left|\psi_{f}^{\beta}\right\rangle=\Big(\cos(\beta)\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\left|H\right\rangle-\sin(\beta)\left|V\right\rangle\Big)\left|L\right\rangle,

(13)

this gives the form

\begin{split}\sigma_{x}^{w}(\beta)&=\frac{\left\langle L\right|\Big(\cos(\beta)\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\left\langle H\right|-\sin(\beta)\left\langle V\right|\Big)\Big(\left|D\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle D\right|-\left|A\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle A\right|\Big)\left|H\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle}{\left\langle L\right|\Big(\cos(\beta)\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\left\langle H\right|-\sin(\beta)\left\langle V\right|\Big)\left|H\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle}\\
&=\frac{-\sin(\beta)}{\cos(\beta)\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)}\approx-\tan(\beta),\end{split}

(14)

which goes to 0 as
\beta\rightarrow 0
(or rather, as we postselect on
\left|H\right\rangle
), and becomes infinite as
\beta\rightarrow\pi/2
(as we postselect on
\left|V\right\rangle)
.

Compare this to the weak value of
\sigma_{x}
when the polarising mirror is removed - in this case
\left|\psi_{f}\right\rangle
remains
\left|D\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle
, and so both the numerator and denominator of
\sigma_{x}
are
1/\sqrt{2}
, and so
\sigma_{x}^{w}=1
. In the extended case, we similarly get
\sigma_{x}^{w}(\beta)=\tan(\beta)
- therefore, the two cases become indistinguishable as
\beta\rightarrow 0
(or rather, as we postselect on
\left|H\right\rangle
), and becomes infinitely distinguishable as
\beta\rightarrow\pi/2
(as we postselect on
\left|V\right\rangle)
.

Note though that to calculate these weak values in this way, we must already know whether or not the polarising mirror is present in the apparatus.

We now give an illustrative example to explain why we see a negative weak value when we perform a weak measurement, but cannot see a phase appearing when measured normally. To do this, we describe an experimental proposal to measure the effect indirectly, akin to weak measurement. (We first use a direct perturbation in this proposal for clarity and simplicity, but for those who are interested give a full treatment for measuring experimentally using weak measurement in Section
VI
, and show that this is equivalent to the experimental protocol we give here). We then show this only allows us to observe the effect when we perturb the initial state away from
H
-polarisation, meaning we never observe the effect experimentally in the exact scenario Aharonov et al want us to consider.

In this proposal, we use an altered version of Aharonov et al’s apparatus, which we give in Fig.
3
, where before the protocol we perturb the polarisation from
\left|H\right\rangle
towards
\left|V\right\rangle
by tiny angle
\delta
, giving us the state
\left|\delta\right\rangle=\cos{\delta}\left|H\right\rangle+\sin{\delta}\left|V\right\rangle
. We then insert this state into our optical analogue of Aharonov et al’s device, letting it evolve for
2N
cycles, to state

\hat{U}^{2N}\left|\delta\right\rangle=\cos^{2N}(\frac{\pi}{2N})\cos{\delta}\left|H\right\rangle-\sin{\delta}\left|V\right\rangle,

(15)

before allowing it to leave via the switchable mirror, and travel into the new lower arm via the optical circulator.

In this arm, the
\left|H\right\rangle
and
\left|V\right\rangle
components are split by a

\left|H\right\rangle
component is attenuated by attenuation coefficient

\mu\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\cos(\delta)\left|H\right\rangle
). These are recombined at a

and

This means our output state is

\begin{split}\left|\delta_{f}\right\rangle&=\frac{\mu\cos^{2N}(\frac{\pi}{2N})\cos(\delta)-\sin(\delta)}{\sqrt{2}}\left|D\right\rangle+\frac{\mu\cos^{2N}(\frac{\pi}{2N})\cos(\delta)+\sin(\delta)}{\sqrt{2}}\left|A\right\rangle.\end{split}

(16)

By comparing our detection rates
P(D)=\langle D|\delta_{f}\rangle
and
P(A)=\langle A|\delta_{f}\rangle
, we get

\frac{P(D)-P(A)}{P(D)+P(A)}=\frac{-\sin(\delta)}{\mu\cos^{2N}(\frac{\pi}{2N})\cos(\delta)}.

(17)

When we don’t attenuate (
\mu=1
), and set
\delta=\beta
, this is the same as the general form of the weak value, which we give above in Eq.
14
. Further, by setting our attenuation factor so
\mu=\tan(\delta)
, we can observe the weak value -1 for any value of
\delta

\delta=0
. This is as, when
\delta=0
, our attenuation cause our ‘reference’
\left|H\right\rangle
value to go to 0, giving us nothing to compare the phase of
\left|V\right\rangle
in our interferometer to and so meaning the relative proportion of intensity detected in

and

).

What does this have to do with the weak value? While more complex, the entangling of some pointer variable with the
D/A

VI
to see how this is done experimentally) is equivalent to putting some indeterminate perturbation on the state, taking it from
\left|H\right\rangle
to
\cos{\delta}\left|H\right\rangle+\sin{\delta}\left|V\right\rangle
for some indeterminate tiny perturbation
\delta
. By postselecting on some non-zero
\beta
, we force this
\delta
to be non-zero too, and so observe the effect of Aharonov et al’s protocol on some non-zero
\left|V\right\rangle
component (this is shown in more detail in Section
VI.4
). This is different from the
\left|H\right\rangle
input state they claim to be evaluating.

While the weak value formula in Eq.
12
gives a well-defined weak value even without perturbation, experimentally, we cannot see this weak value without perturbing the system - it becomes undefined, as both the difference in detector clicks (the numerator) and the weighting denominator goes to zero. Further, as it is stochastic whether the wavepacket in each case goes to
DA
or
DD
, and the difference in probabilities between them tends to 0 as
\delta
goes to 0, our signal-to-noise ratio for experimentally obtaining the weak value goes to 0, and the uncertainty for any finite number of detections becomes far larger than the difference between the weak value reading as -1 or as 1. Therefore, we cannot experimentally tell the difference between the apparatus given in Fig.
1
, and a setup only differing through the lack of the spin-dependant right wall, using a wavepacket entirely in state
\left|\uparrow_{z}\right\rangle
. This means we cannot experimentally detect the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat with an unperturbed state.

Measuring the weak value of
\sigma_{x}

If we weren’t to apply the protocol, but were instead to send the beam straight to the detector
DA

{I_{w}(y)=|\langle D|E_{w}\rangle|^{2}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{w_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}e^{-\frac{2x^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big(e^{-\frac{2(y-a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big),}

(20)

If instead we apply the system evolution

\begin{split}\left|E_{f}\right\rangle=\hat{U}^{2N}\left|E_{w}\right\rangle=&\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{w_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big(\cos(\alpha)e^{-\frac{(y-a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\frac{\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\left|H\right\rangle-\left|V\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}+\sin(\alpha)e^{-\frac{(y+a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\frac{\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\left|H\right\rangle+\left|V\right\rangle}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)\\

(22)

Postselecting on the polarisation state
\left\langle\beta\right|=\cos(\beta)\left\langle D\right|+\sin(\beta)\left\langle A\right|

\begin{split}\langle\beta|E_{f}\rangle&=\sqrt{\frac{2\sqrt{2}}{w_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big(\cos(\alpha)\sin(\beta)e^{-\frac{(y-a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}+\sin(\alpha)\cos(\beta)e^{-\frac{(y+a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big)\\

(23)

and so the normalised intensity (at

Treating this as a probability density function, we can get the average position of the centre of the beam on detector

VI.2
What if there is no polarising mirror?

If there were no polarising mirror in the apparatus, any light travelling to the right-hand cavity of the apparatus would be lost, regardless of polarisation. Therefore, both
H
and
V
components would just get an attenuation of
\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)
, rather than
V
-polarised components picking up a phase of
-1
. Because of this,
D
-polarised components wouldn’t flip to
A
-polarised, but would stay
D
-polarised (and vice-versa), so the beam centroid would stay at
\langle y\rangle=a/2
, and the weak value would be 1, rather than -1. This shows a clear experimentally-detectable difference between when there is and when there isn’t a polarising mirror in the right-hand cavity, when we perturb the system to detect this.

VI.3
Weak value of
\left|R\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle R\right|

Looking at the perturbed state we give above, when we want to find the weak value of the projector
\hat{P}_{R}=\left|R\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle R\right|
at some time during the protocol (again, with polarising mirror), we follow Aharonov et al by saying

(26)

but inserting our weakly perturbed state
\left|E_{w}(\alpha=\pi/4)\right\rangle
instead of the initial
\left|H\right\rangle
. For ease, we can say
\hat{U}(\tau)=\hat{\mathds{1}}
, and so
\hat{U}(T-\tau)=\hat{U}

To see the result we get from this, we must first evaluate the effect of both the
\left|D\right\rangle
and
\left|A\right\rangle

\begin{split}\left\langle L\right|\left\langle D\right|\hat{U}^{2N-n}\hat{P}_{R}\hat{U}^{n}\left|D\right\rangle\left|L\right\rangle=&-\sin(\frac{(2N-n)\pi}{2N})\sin(\frac{n\pi}{2N})/2\\

and so

VI.4
What if we put an
H

Elsewhere in the text, we claim that entangling the wavepacket’s polarisation with a pointer variable is equivalent to perturbing the polarisation from state
\left|H\right\rangle

H

To do this, we take the state in Eq.

{\left|H\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle H\right|\left|E_{w}\right\rangle=\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2w_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big(e^{-\frac{(y-a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}+e^{-\frac{(y+a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big)\left|H\right\rangle.}

We then apply the evolution
\hat{U}^{2N}

\begin{split}\hat{U}^{2N}\left|H\right\rangle\hskip-2.84526pt\left\langle H\right|\left|E_{w}\right\rangle&=\sqrt{\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2w_{0}\sqrt{\pi}}}e^{-\frac{x^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\cos^{2N}(\pi/2N)\Big(e^{-\frac{(y-a/2)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}+e^{-\frac{(y+a)^{2}}{w_{0}^{2}}}\Big)\left|H\right\rangle+\,\text{Loss}\\

This proves that, even in cases where it is caused by entanglement between state and pointer (rather than directly as in Section

VII
What does this have to do with counterfactual communication?

Aharonov et al finally claim in their paper that this dynamical quantum Cheshire cat effect is the effect underlying counterfactual communication, an interesting development of exchange-free measurement developed by Salih et al
Salih2013Protocol
. Given the recent interest in counterfactual communication, both in terms of practical applications of quantum computation
Salih2016Qubit
;
Salih2018Paradox
;
Salih2020DetTele
;
Salih2021EFQubit
and metrology
Hance2021CFGI
, and on deeper foundational levels
Salih2018Laws
;
Aharonov2019Modification
;
Aharonov2020Nonlocal
;
Hance2021Quantum
, it is worth examining how our analysis above affects this claim.

The term ‘counterfactual’ in the name ‘counterfactual communication’ comes from Alice (the receiver)’s photon never having travelled to/via Bob (the sender), when she receives a bit of information from Bob in the protocol (i.e. by her postselection on photon either going to her detector
D0
for a 0-bit, or her detector
D1
for a 1-bit). Rather than factually interacting with anything on Bob’s side, the photon is ‘counterfactually’ telling Alice whether Bob is blocking his channel or not (and so whether his bit-value is 1 or 0 respectively), without ever having been to his channel
Salih2018Laws
;
Hance2021Quantum
. Therefore, counterfactual communication seems superficially similar to the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat, where there is a difference in the (theoretically-calculated) weak value depending on whether or not a polarisation-dependent mirror is placed at the end of the right-hand cavity, despite the
H
-polarised photon not being able to travel to-and-back-from this mirror.

However, as we have shown above, experimentally determining whether or not the spin-dependent mirror is present requires some small amount of
V
-polarised light to travel to, and back from, the mirror. This
V
-polarised light is obviously not counterfactual. Unlike this, in counterfactual communication, we do not require any small amount of field to travel to-and-back-from Bob’s channel to allow Alice to receive information from Bob - anything which goes to Bob is lost (like the
H
-polarised light which travels to the right-hand cavity and is then lost in the unperturbed dynamical quantum Cheshire cat setup).

This means that the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat is not behind Alice being able to detect Bob’s blocker setting (and so bit-value) in counterfactual communication. However, counterfactual communication could allow us a way to adapt the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat to make it experimentally detectable without perturbing the initial state.

VIII
Conclusion

We have shown that the dynamical quantum Cheshire cat effect Aharonov et al describe cannot be observed directly via homodyne detection. It can however be detected by obtaining weak values through weak coupling and postselection. This necessarily requires some perturbation away from the initial state, and so requires something to travel through the second cavity, interact with the probed barrier, and return to the first cavity. This shows the protocol is different to counterfactual communication, which does not require any field to travel from Bob (the sender) to Alice (the receiver) for information to be transferred. Further work may try to link this phenomenon instead to contextuality, as has recently been done for the (standard) quantum Cheshire cat protocol
hance2023contextuality
.
