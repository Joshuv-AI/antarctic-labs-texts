Abstract
. Motivated by the theory of domination for types, we introduce a notion of domination for Keisler measures called extension domination. We argue that this variant of domination behaves similarly to its type setting counterpart. We prove that extension domination extends domination for types and that it forms a preorder on the space of global Keisler measures. We then explore some basic properties related to this notion (e.g. approximations by formulas, closure under localizations, convex combinations). We also prove a few preservation theorems and provide some explicit examples.



1.
Introduction

The notion of domination was introduced by Lascar in
[
11
]
in the context of stable theories. This notion again appears in
[
12
]
which has been studied in more detail by Pillay
[
15
]
.
Later, it was studied in the general setting of complete first order theories
[
13
]
and extensively studied in the specific context of valued fields
[
5
]
. Loosely speaking,
p(x)
dominates
q(y)
captures the idea that
p
, along with a small amount of auxiliary
xy
-data
, controls
q
. This relation forms a preorder on the space of global types. In practice, sometimes one can understand the space of invariant types by studying a collection of
nice
invariant types together with the domination relation. For example, the space of invariant types in o-minimal structures and henselian valued fields
[
14
,
6
]
has been studied in precisely this way. Therefore, the question of whether a notion of domination exists for Keisler measures naturally arises. Moreover, in the context of valued fields, a useful tool in studying the geometry of definable sets is the notion of
stable domination
. An extensive study on this subject has been made in
[
5
]
which contributed to the understanding of Berkovich analytification of varieties
[
7
]
. It is conceivable that understanding domination of measures in the valued field context may yield an understanding of certain associated geometric spaces. More specifically, as suggested by the previous works on types
[
7
,
4
]
, it would be nice to be able to internalize the space of generically stable measures. To put a more concrete question:

Question 1.1
.

Is every generically stable measure in ACVF dominated by a collection of stably dominated types?

A positive answer would suggest that generically stable measures can indeed be approached as small projective limits of definable sets with some small auxilary data, which would serve as the first step towards an analysis of generically stable measures following
[
7
]
.

The notion of extension domination is also conceptually related to compact domination (see
[
10
,
19
]
and
[
18
, Chapter 8]
). Compact domination expresses the idea that a Keisler measure can be
controlled
by an auxiliary measure over a compact space. Extension domination, on the other hand, attempts to capture the idea of when one Keisler measure controls another. With compact domination, a measure
h
on a compact space
K
lifts uniquely to an invariant Keisler measure. With extension domination, if
\mu_{x}
extension dominates
\nu_{y}
, then there exists a unique Keisler measure
\omega_{xy}
whose projection to
x
is
\mu_{x}
and projection to
y
is
\nu_{y}
.

In this paper, we introduce
extension domination
for Keisler measures as a natural generalization of domination for types. We show that this notion extends domination for types (Proposition
3.6
) and forms a preorder on the space of global Keisler measures (Theorem
3.13
). Additionally, any Keisler measure dominates (definable) pushforwards of itself (Proposition
3.10
). Extension domination also admits a natural approximation property: If
\mu
extension dominates
\nu
, then there is a collection of formulas which forces this to be true via
squeezing
(Proposition
3.17
and Theorem
3.18
). Moreover, several properties of measures such as invariance, smoothness, and finite satisfiability are preserved under extension domination (Section 4). Akin to the classical setting where realized types form the bottom tier of the domination relation, the collection of smooth measures forms the bottom tier of the extension domination relation (Proposition
4.6
and Proposition
4.7
).

If domination is witnessed by a separated amalgam (such as the restriction of a Morley product), then one can show that extension domination is closed under localizations (Proposition
3.20
). The role of the separated amalgam allows us more control over our computation and leads to the notion of
amalgam deciphering
. A measure
\mu
amalgam deciphers a measure
\nu
if
\mu
dominates
\nu
over the class of separated amalgams (instead of the class of all possible extensions). The general behavior of this notion remains somewhat mysterious, yet it interacts well with localizations (Proposition
5.7
) and convex combinations (Proposition
5.9
).

The paper is structured as follows: In Section
2
, we recall some preliminaries and basic facts about domination, finitely additive measures, and Keisler measures. In Section
3
, we introduce and study extension domination. In Section
4
, we prove some preservation theorems. In Section
5
, we study separated amalgams and amalgam deciphering. In Section 6, we provide some explicit examples of extension domination.

2.1.
Domination for types

We first recall the definition of domination for global types. We refer the reader to
[
13
]
for additional details.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
p\in S_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
q\in S_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
A
be a small subset of
\mathcal{U}
. We say that
p
dominates

(over
A
)
if there is some
r\in S_{xy}(A)
such that
p|_{A}\subseteq r
and
p\cup r\vdash q
. We write
p\geq_{D,A}q
to mean “
p
dominates
q
over
A
” and
p\geq_{D}q
to mean “there exists some small set

such that
p\geq_{D,A}q
”. Finally, if
r
is as above we say that the type
r

witnesses
p\geq_{D,A}q
or simply
witnesses domination
.

The next fact follows directly from compactness.

Fact 2.2
.

Let
p\in S_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
q\in S_{y}(\mathcal{U})
,
A
be a small subset of
\mathcal{U}
, and
r\in S_{xy}(A)
such that
r
witnesses
p\geq_{D,A}q
. Then for every
\psi(y)\in q
, there exists
\varphi(x)\in p
and
\theta(x,y)\in r
such that
\varphi(x)\wedge\theta(x,y)\subseteq\psi(y)\wedge x=x
.

Remark 2.3
.

We remark that in the definition of domination in
[
13
]
, there is an additional assumption that
r\in S_{pq}(A):=\{r^{\prime}\in S_{xy}(A):r^{\prime}\supseteq p|_{A}\cup q|_{A}\}.
The assumption that
r\supseteq q|_{A}
is not strictly necessary since this property is a consequence of domination as defined above.

Remark 2.4
.

By compactness, Definition
2.1
is equivalent to the following:
p\geq_{D,A}q
if and only if there is some
r\in S_{xy}(A)
, such that
r\supseteq p|_{A}
and for any
w\in S_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
such that
w
extends both
p
and
r
,
w
extends
q
. This rephrasing of the definition motivates our definition for extension domination (see Definition
3.3
).

2.3.
Keisler measures

Lastly we recall some basic definitions and standard observations involving Keisler measures.

Definition 2.9
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and
x
a tuple of variables. Then a
Keisler measure
(over
A
, in variable(s)
x
) is a finitely additive probability measure on
\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
. More explicitly, a Keisler measure
\mu
is a map from
\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
to
[0,1]
such that

(1)

\mu(x=x)=1
.

(2)

For any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
,
\mu(\neg\varphi(x))=1-\mu(\varphi(x))
.

(3)

For any
\varphi(x),\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
,

\mu(\varphi(x)\vee\psi(x))=\mu(\varphi(x))+\mu(\psi(x))-\mu(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(x)).

We denote the space of Keisler measures on
\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
as
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
and
p\in S_{x}(A)
. We say that
p

is in the support of
\mu
if for every
\varphi(x)\in p
,
\mu(\varphi(x))>0
. We let
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
be the collection of types in
S_{x}(A)
which are in the support of
\mu
. We remark that for any
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
,
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)\neq\emptyset
and
\operatorname{supp}(\mu)
is a closed subset of
S_{x}(A)
.

We now discuss the topological/geometric properties of the space of Keisler measures. The following observations are folklore and follow from basic results in classical analysis. We refer the reader to
[
18
, Chapter 7]
for more details. We first recall some definitions from functional analysis.

Definition 2.11
.

Let
(V,||\cdot||)
be a real Banach space and
V^{*}
be the dual of
V
. More explicitly,
V^{*}
is the collection of (norm)-continuous linear maps from
V
to
\mathbb{R}
. Then the weak
∗
topology on
V^{*}
is the coarsest topology on
V^{*}
such that for any
x\in V
, the map
f\mapsto f(x)
is continuous.

Fact 2.12
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.

(1)

\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
forms a compact Hausdorff space with the following topology: Let
\varphi_{1}(x),...,\varphi_{n}(x)
be formulas in
\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
and
r_{1},...,r_{n},s_{1},...,s_{n}
be real numbers. Then a basic open set
O
of
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
is of the form

O=\bigcap_{i=1}^{n}\{\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A):r_{i}<\nu(\varphi_{i}(x))<s_{i}\}.

(2)

For any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
, the map
E_{\varphi}:\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)\to[0,1]
via
E_{\varphi}(\mu)=\mu(\varphi(x))
is continuous.

(3)

Every Keisler measure
\mu
in
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
is in unique correspondence with a regular Borel probability measure on
S_{x}(A)
. We denote the space of regular probability measures on
S_{x}(A)
as
\mathcal{M}_{x}(A)
.

(4)

If we let
V=(C(S_{x}(A)),||\cdot||_{\infty})
be the Banach space of continuous functions from
S_{x}(A)
to
\mathbb{R}
with the supremum norm, then
\mathcal{M}_{x}(A)
is naturally a subset of
V^{*}
with the weak
∗
topology. By (3), the space
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
can be identified with a subset of
V^{*}
. The induced topology on
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
from
V^{*}
is the same as the topology described in (1). Moreover,
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
is a convex subset of
V^{*}
.

(5)

There is a topological embedding
\delta:S_{x}(A)\to\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
via
p\to\delta_{p}
where
\delta_{p}
is a the Dirac measure concentrating on
p
. In other words, for any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
,

\delta(p)(\varphi(x))=\delta_{p}(\varphi(x))=\begin{cases}\begin{array}[]{cc}1&\text{if }\varphi(x)\in p,\\
0&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\end{cases}

Let

be locally convex topological vector spaces. Let

(1)

(2)

Fact 2.15
.

Let
B\subseteq A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.

(1)

The natural restriction map
r_{A,B}:\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)\to\mathfrak{M}_{x}(B)
is continuous. If
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)

(2)

The natural projection map

Fact 2.16

Let

Finally, we recall the definition of a separated amalgam
[

Definition 2.17
.

Let
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
. We say that
\lambda
is a
separated amalgam
if for any pair of formulas
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
and
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
, we have that

\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y))=\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\varphi(x))\cdot\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\psi(y)).

We let
\mathfrak{M}^{\mathrm{Am}}_{xy}(A):=\{\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A):\lambda
is a separated amalgam
\}
.

Lemma 2.18
.

The following are equivalent:

(1)

\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\mathrm{Am}}_{xy}(A)
.

(2)

There exists measures
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
and
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(A)
such that for any
(\varphi(x),\psi(y))\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)\times\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
,

\mu(\varphi(x))\cdot\nu(\psi(y))=\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y))
.

Proof.

(1)\implies(2)
is trivial. Suppose
(2)
holds. Then

\mu(\varphi(x))=\mu(\varphi(x))\cdot\nu(y=y)=\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge y=y)=\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\varphi(x)).

Similarly,
\nu(\psi(y))=\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\psi(y))
. Hence
\lambda
is a separated amalgam.
∎

Remark 2.19
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
. For any pair of measures
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
and
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(A)
, there exists a measure
\omega\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(A)
such that
\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu
and
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
. Indeed, one can build a finitely additive product measure
\mu\times\nu
on
\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)\times\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
. The map
\alpha:\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)\times\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)\to\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
given by

is an injective homomorphism of Boolean algebras. Hence we can consider the measure
\alpha(\mu\times\nu)
as a finitely additive probability measure on the
subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
generated by
\{\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y):\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A),\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)\}
. Any
\omega
extending
\alpha(\mu\times\nu)
to the Boolean algebra
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
is a separated amalgam. Notice that
\omega
exists by an application of Corollary
2.6
with
\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{B}=\alpha(\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)\times\mathcal{L}_{y}(A))

\mu_{1}=\mu_{2}=\alpha(\mu\times\nu)

[
10
]
. We provide a proof.

Lemma 2.20
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
. Suppose either
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\delta_{p}
for some
p\in S_{x}(A)
or
\pi_{y}(\lambda)=\delta_{q}
for some
q\in S_{y}(A)
. Then
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(A)
.

Proof.

By symmetry, we assume that
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\delta_{p}
. Fix
(\varphi(x),\psi(y))\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)\times\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
. First suppose that
\varphi(x)\in p
. Then

\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\varphi(x))\cdot\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\psi(y))=\delta_{p}(\varphi(x))\cdot\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\psi(y))=\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\psi(y))

=\lambda(\psi(y)\wedge x=x)\overset{(*)}{=}\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y)).

Where equality
(*)
holds from (1) of Lemma
2.16
and
\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\varphi(x))=\delta_{p}(\varphi(x))=1
.

Now suppose that
\neg\varphi(x)\in p
. Clearly
\delta_{p}(\varphi(x))\cdot\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\psi(y))=0
. Also note,

0=\delta_{p}(\varphi(x))=\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\varphi(x))=\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge y=y)\geq\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y))

\implies\lambda(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y))=0.

Hence
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(A)
.
∎

3.
Extension Domination

In this section, we introduce the notion of
extension domination
which we denote as ‘
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
’. We then prove that extension domination
behaves
like a notion of domination. More explicitly, we show that

(1)

Extension domination extends the definition of domination for global types.

(2)

Any Keisler measure dominates its (definable) push-forwards.

(3)

Extension domination forms a preorder on global Keisler measures.

After proving these properties, we prove a general approximation theorem. This theorem essentially states that if a measure
\mu
extension dominates a measure
\nu
, then there is a collection of formulas which forces this to be true via
squeezing
. We also derive a uniform version of this theorem via compactness. We end this section by showing that under a moderately stronger assumption, extension domination interacts nicely with
localizations
, a phenomenon unique to measures. This occurs when domination is witnessed by a separated amalgam.

3.1.
Basic definitions and observations

Throughout this section,
\mathcal{U}
is a fixed monster model and
A
is a small subset of
\mathcal{U}
. In order to define extension domination, we must first define the notion of an
extension space
.

Definition 3.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
such that
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
. We define the corresponding
extension space
, denoted
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
, as follows:

\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu):=\{\omega\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U}):\omega|_{A}=\lambda,\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu\}.

Notice that in the definition of an extension space, we require a consistency condition, i.e.
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
. We will now argue that this condition is sufficient to ensure that
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is non-empty. We also observe that
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is a convex compact space.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
such that
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
. Then

(1)

\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)\neq\emptyset
.

(2)

\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is a compact Hausdorff space under the topology induced by
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
.

(3)

\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is convex.

Proof.

We prove the statements.

(1)

This follows from an application of Corollary
2.6
. Identify the Boolean algebras
\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
with their images under their natural embeddings into
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
as well as
\mu
and
\lambda
with the appropriate finitely additive measures on the corresponding images. Let
\theta(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
,
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
, and suppose that
\theta(x,y)\subseteq\varphi(x)\wedge y=y
as definable sets. Consider the formula
\gamma(x,y):=\exists y\theta(x,y)\wedge y=y
. Notice that
\theta(x,y)\subseteq\gamma(x,y)\subseteq\varphi(x)\wedge y=y
. Now

\lambda(\theta(x,y))\leq\lambda(\gamma(x,y))=\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\exists y\theta(x,y))

=\mu(\exists y\theta(x,y))\leq\mu(\varphi(x))=\mu(\varphi(x)\wedge y=y).

By Corollary
2.6
, there exists
\omega\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
which is a common extension of the
\lambda
and
\mu
. By construction,
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
.

(2)

Any subspace of a Hausdorff space is Hausdorff. Let
r_{A}:\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})\to\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
be the obvious restriction map. Then
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)=\pi_{x}^{-1}(\{\mu\})\cap(r_{A})^{-1}(\{\lambda\})
. Since both
r_{A}
and
\pi_{x}
are continuous,
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is the intersection of two compact sets (and thus compact).

(3)

Suppose that
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
and let
r,s\in[0,1]
such that
r+s=1
.
Then

\pi_{x}(r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2})=r\pi_{x}(\omega_{1})+s\pi_{x}(\omega_{1})=r\mu+s\mu=\mu,

and,

(r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{1})|_{A}=r\omega_{1}|_{A}+s\omega_{1}|_{A}=r\lambda+s\lambda=\lambda.

Hence
r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. ∎

We now define extension domination.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
A
be a small subset of
\mathcal{U}
. We say that
\mu

extension dominates
\nu
(over
A
)
(denoted by
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
) if there exists
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
such that

(1)

\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{A}
.

(2)

For any
\omega
in
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
.

If the above conditions are satisfied, then
\lambda
is said to
witness

\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
or
witness domination
. We write
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
if there exists some small set
A
such that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
.

Convention 3.4
.

Let
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{U})
be the space of global Keisler measures in finitely many variables. Suppose that
\mu
and
\nu
are in
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{U})
. We often consider
\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}
and
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
as binary relations on this space by saying that
\mu
dominates
\nu
(over
A
) if
\mu(x)
dominates
\nu(y)
(over
A
) where
x
and
y
are distinct tuples of variables.

Remark 3.5
.

We remark that extension domination is
non-vacuous
. More explicitly, if
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\lambda
witnesses domination, then
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is non-empty. This is precisely the conclusion of
(1)
of Lemma
3.2
. This will become an issue when we discuss
amalgam deciphering
in Section 5.

We now show that extension domination extends the classical definition of domination for types.

Proposition 3.6
.

Let
p\in S_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
q\in S_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
A
be a small subset of
\mathcal{U}
. If
p\geq_{D,A}q
, then
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\delta_{q}
.

Proof.

Suppose that
p\geq_{D,A}q
. Then there exists some complete type
r\in S_{xy}(A)
which witnesses
p\geq_{D,A}q
. Consider the measure
\lambda:=\delta_{r}
. It is clear from construction that
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
and
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\delta_{p}|_{A}
. Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\delta_{p})
. It suffices to show that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\delta_{q}
. Fix a formula
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\delta_{q}(\psi(y))=1
. By definition,
\psi(y)\in q
and since
p\geq_{D,A}q
, there exists
\varphi(x)\in p
and
\theta(x,y)\in r
such that
\varphi(x)\wedge\theta(x,y)\subseteq\psi(y)\wedge x=x
(see Fact
2.2
). Hence

\pi_{y}(\omega)(\psi(y))=\omega(\psi(y)\wedge x=x)\geq\omega(\varphi(x)\wedge\theta(x,y))

\overset{(*)}{=}\omega(\varphi(x)\wedge y=y)=\pi_{x}(\omega)(\varphi(x))=\delta_{p}(\varphi(x))=1.

\implies\pi_{y}(\omega)=1.

Equality
(*)
follows from the fact that
1=\lambda(\theta(x,y))=\omega(\theta(x,y))
. The case where
\delta_{q}(\psi(y))=0
is handled via negation.
∎

Question 3.7
.

Suppose that
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\delta_{q}
. Does it follow that
p\geq_{D}q
?

Our next lemma allows us to extend the space of parameters.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
A
and
B
be a small subsets of
\mathcal{U}
such that
A\subseteq B
. If
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
, then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},B}\nu
.

Proof.

Since
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
, there exists some
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
which witnesses domination. By Lemma
3.2
, there exists some
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Let
\lambda^{\prime}:=\omega|_{B}
. We claim that
\lambda^{\prime}
witnesses
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},B}\nu
.

(1)

Notice that

\pi_{x}(\lambda^{\prime})=\pi_{x}(\omega|_{B})=\pi_{x}(\omega)|_{B}=\mu|_{B}.

(2)

Let
\eta\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda^{\prime},\mu)
. It suffices to show that
\pi_{y}(\eta)=\nu
. Since
\eta\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda^{\prime},\mu)
, it follows that
\pi_{x}(\eta)=\mu
and
\eta|_{A}=(\eta|_{B})|_{A}=\lambda^{\prime}|_{A}=\lambda
. Hence
\eta\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
and since
\mu
dominates
\nu
over
A
(via
\lambda
), we conclude
\pi_{y}(\eta)=\nu
. ∎

We now argue that measures dominate push-forwards of themselves (under definable maps). We recall the definition of a push-forward.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
f:\mathcal{U}^{x}\to\mathcal{U}^{y}
be a definable function (possibly defined over parameters). We define the push-forward measure
f(\mu)\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
where for any
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
,

f(\mu)(\psi(y))=\mu(\psi(f(x)).

Proposition 3.10
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
f:\mathcal{U}^{x}\to\mathcal{U}^{y}
be an
A
-definable function. Then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}f(\mu)
.

Proof.

Consider the function
g:\mathcal{U}^{x}\to\mathcal{U}^{x}\times\mathcal{U}^{y}
via
g(a)=(a,f(a))
. The map
g
is an
A
-definable function and so we can consider the push-forward
g(\mu)
. Define
\lambda:=g(\mu)|_{A}
. Notice that

(1)

\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{A}
and
\pi_{y}(\lambda)=f(\mu)|_{A}
.

(2)

\lambda(f(x)=y)=1
.

Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Consider the following computation:

\pi_{y}(\omega)(\psi(y))=\omega(\psi(y)\wedge x=x)\overset{(*)}{=}\omega(\psi(y)\wedge f(x)=y)=\omega(\psi(f(x))\wedge f(x)=y)

\overset{(*)}{=}\omega(\psi(f(x))\wedge y=y)=\pi_{x}(\omega)(\psi(f(x))=\mu(\psi(f(x)))=f(\mu)(\psi(y)),

where the equations with
(*)
hold by Fact
2.16
and the observation that
\omega(f(x)=y)=\lambda(f(x)=y)=1
.
∎

3.2.
Extension domination forms a preorder

We show that
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
is a preorder on the collection of global measures,
\mathfrak{M}(\mathcal{U})
. More explicitly, we argue that the relation
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
is both transitive and reflexive. We begin with a measure existence lemma.

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
.

(1)

Let
\lambda_{1}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
and
\lambda_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}_{yz}(A)
such that
\pi_{y}(\lambda_{1})=\pi_{y}(\lambda_{2})
. Then there exists
\lambda_{3}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(A)
such that
\pi_{xy}(\lambda_{3})=\lambda_{1}
and
\pi_{yz}(\lambda_{3})=\lambda_{2}
.

(2)

Suppose that
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(A)
,
\omega\in\mathfrak{M}_{xz}(\mathcal{U})
, and
\omega|_{A}=\pi_{xz}(\lambda)
. Then there exists some measure
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime})=\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}|_{A}=\lambda
.

Proof.

The proofs of the claims are similar to one another and also to Lemma
3.2
. For clarity, we provide both proofs.

(1)

We apply Corollary
2.6
. Identify the Boolean algebras
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
and
\mathcal{L}_{yz}(A)
with their natural embeddings into
\mathcal{L}_{xyz}(A)
as well as
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
with the corresponding finitely additive measure on the corresponding image. Suppose that
\theta_{1}(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
,
\theta_{2}(y,z)\in\mathcal{L}_{yz}(A)
and
(\theta_{1}(x,y)\wedge z=z)\subseteq(\theta_{2}(y,z)\wedge x=x)
as definable sets. Then we set
\Gamma(x,y,z):=\exists x(\theta_{1}(x,y)\wedge z=z)\wedge x=x
. It follows that
(\theta_{1}(x,y)\wedge z=z)\subseteq\Gamma(x,y,z)
and
\Gamma(x,y,z)\subseteq(\theta_{2}(y,z)\wedge x=x)
. Now,

\lambda_{1}(\theta_{1}(x,y)\wedge z=z)\leq\lambda_{1}(\Gamma(x,y,z))=\pi_{y}(\lambda_{1})(\exists x\theta_{1}(x,y))

=\pi_{y}(\lambda_{2})(\exists x\theta_{1}(x,y))=\lambda_{2}(\Gamma(x,y,z))\leq\lambda_{2}(\theta_{2}(y,z)\wedge x=x).

By Corollary
2.6
, there exists
\lambda_{3}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(A)
with the desired properties.

(2)

Again we apply Corollary
2.6
. Consider the Boolean algebras
\mathcal{L}_{xyz}(A)
and
\mathcal{L}_{xz}(\mathcal{U})
embedded in
\mathcal{L}_{xyz}(\mathcal{U})
with the usual identifications on the measures. Suppose that
\theta(x,y,z)\in\mathcal{L}_{xyz}(A)
and
\psi(x,z)\in\mathcal{L}_{xz}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\theta(x,y,z)\subseteq(\psi(x,z)\wedge y=y)
. Let
\Gamma(x,y,z):=\exists y\theta(x,y,z)\wedge y=y
. Again, it follows that
\theta(x,y,z)\subseteq\Gamma(x,y,z)\subseteq(\psi(x,z)\wedge y=y)
. Hence

\lambda(\theta(x,y,z))\leq\lambda\left(\Gamma(x,y,z)\right)=\pi_{xz}(\lambda)\left(\exists y\theta(x,y,z)\right)

=\omega(\exists y(\theta(x,y,z))=\omega(\Gamma(x,y,z))\leq\omega\left(\psi(x,y)\wedge y=y\right).

By Corollary
2.6
, there exists
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(\mathcal{U})
with the desired properties. ∎

Proposition 3.12
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{z}(\mathcal{U})
. If
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\eta
, then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\eta
.

Proof.

Since
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
, there exists
\lambda_{1}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
which witnesses domination. Likewise, since
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\mu
, there exists
\lambda_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}_{yz}(A)
which witness domination. Observe that
\pi_{y}(\lambda_{1})=\pi_{y}(\lambda_{2})=\nu|_{A}
. By Lemma
3.11
(1) , there exists
\lambda_{3}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(A)
such that
\pi_{xy}(\lambda_{3})=\lambda_{1}
and
\pi_{yz}(\lambda_{3})=\lambda_{2}
.
Let
\lambda:=\pi_{xz}(\lambda_{3})
. We now show that
\lambda
witnesses
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\eta
. Observe that
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{A}
and fix
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. It suffices to show that
\pi_{z}(\omega)=\eta
.

(1)

By definition,
\omega|_{A}=\lambda=\pi_{xz}(\lambda_{3})
. By Lemma
3.11
(2), there exists
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xyz}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\pi_{xz}(\omega^{\prime})=\omega
and
\omega^{\prime}|_{A}=\lambda_{3}
.

(2)

Consider the measure
\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime})
. Notice that
\pi_{x}(\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime}))=\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu
and
\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime})|_{A}=\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime}|_{A})=\pi_{xy}(\lambda_{3})=\lambda_{1}
. Hence
\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime})\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{1},\mu)
and by domination,
\pi_{y}(\pi_{xy}(\omega^{\prime}))=\nu
. Therefore
\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})=\nu
.

(3)

Now consider the measure
\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime})
. By above,
\pi_{y}(\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime}))=\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})=\nu
. Also,
\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime})|_{A}=\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime}|_{A})=\pi_{yz}(\lambda_{3})=\lambda_{2}
. Hence
\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime})\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{2},\nu)
and by domination,
\pi_{z}(\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime}))=\eta
. Therefore
\pi_{z}(\omega)=\pi_{z}(\pi_{yz}(\omega^{\prime}))=\eta
.

Thus for any
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\pi_{z}(\omega)=\eta
and so
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\eta
.
∎

Theorem 3.13
.

The relation
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
is a preorder on global Keisler measures.

Proof.

We need to show that
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
is transitive and reflexive.

(1)

Transitive: Suppose that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\eta
. Then there exists small sets
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
such that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A_{1}}\nu
and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A_{2}}\eta
.
By Lemma
3.8
, we have that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A_{1}\cup A_{2}}\nu

and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A_{1}\cup A_{2}}\eta
. By Proposition
3.12
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A_{1}\cup A_{2}}\eta
and so
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\eta
.

(2)

Reflexive: Notice that
\mu
is a push-forward of itself by considering the identity map, i.e. the map
f:\mathcal{U}^{x}\to\mathcal{U}^{y}
via
f(a)=a
. By Proposition
3.10
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\emptyset}\mu
and so
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\mu
.

Hence
\geq_{\mathbb{E}}
is a preorder.
∎

Corollary 3.14
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
f:\mathcal{U}^{y}\to\mathcal{U}^{z}
be a definable map. If
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}f(\nu)
.

Proof.

The map
f
is
B
-definable for some finite set
B
and so
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},B}f(\nu)
by Proposition
3.10
. Since
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
, there exists a small set
A
such that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
. By Lemma
3.8
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A\cup B}\nu
and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A\cup B}f(\nu)
. By transitivity,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A\cup B}f(\nu)
.
∎

3.3.
Approximation results

As in the case of domination for types, if
\mu
dominates
\nu
and
\lambda
witnesses domination, then there is a collection of formulas which
forces
this to occur. This is made precise in terms of the approximation results in this section. Moreover, this collection of formulas forms a Boolean subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
which we denote as
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
. We show that for each
\omega
in
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists formulas
\gamma^{-}(x,y)
and
\gamma^{+}(x,y)
in
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
which
squeeze
the value of
\pi(\omega)(\varphi(y))
. An application of (topological) compactness gives us a uniform version of this result. We begin with defining the auxiliary Boolean algebra
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
.

Definition 3.15
.

Let
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
. We let
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
be the Boolean subalgebra of
\mathcal{L}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
generated by
\{\varphi(x)\wedge y=y:\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})\}\cup\{\theta(x,y):\theta(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)\}
.

Remark 3.16
.

The Boolean algebra
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
is generated by formulas of the form
\{\theta(x,y)\wedge\psi(x):\theta(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A),\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})\}
using only negation and intersection. Notice that
\theta(x,y)\vee\psi(x)\equiv\neg(\neg\theta(x,y)\wedge\neg\psi(x))
.

Proposition 3.17
.

Suppose that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and let
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
witness domination. Then for any
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\varphi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
\epsilon>0
, there exists formulas
\gamma^{-}(x,y),\gamma^{+}(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
such that

(1)

\gamma^{-}(x,y)\subseteq(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\subseteq\gamma^{+}(x,y)

(2)

\omega(\gamma^{+}(x,y))-\omega(\gamma^{-}(x,y))<\epsilon
.

(3)

|\omega(\gamma^{\dagger}(x,y))-\nu(\varphi(y))|<\epsilon
where
\dagger\in\{+,-\}
.

Proof.

Fix
\varphi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and let
r_{1}=\sup\{\omega(\gamma(x,y)):\gamma(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A),\gamma(x,y)\subseteq(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\}
and let
r_{2}=\inf\{\omega(\gamma(x,y)):\gamma(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A),(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\subseteq\gamma(x,y)\}
. Clearly
r_{1}\leq r_{2}
.

Now suppose that
r_{1}<r_{2}
. By Fact
2.7
, for any
r\in[r_{1},r_{2}]
, there exists a measure
\omega_{r}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\omega_{r}\supseteq\omega|_{\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)}
and
\omega_{r}(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)=r
. Choose
r_{*}
such that
\omega_{r_{*}}(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\neq\nu(\varphi(y))
. However,
\omega_{r_{*}}|_{\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)}=\omega|_{\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)}
which implies that
\omega_{r}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. By domination, it follows that
\pi_{y}(\omega_{r_{*}})(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)=\nu(\varphi(y))
, a contradiction. Therefore
r_{1}=r_{2}=\nu(\varphi(y))
.

Fix
\epsilon>0
. Since
r_{1}=r_{2}=\nu(\varphi(y))
, there exists formulas
\gamma^{-}(x,y)
and
\gamma^{+}(x,y)
in
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
such that

(i)

\gamma^{-}(x,y)\subseteq(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)
and
\mid\omega(\gamma^{-}(x,y))-\nu(\varphi(y))\mid<\epsilon/2
.

(ii)

(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\subseteq\gamma^{+}(x,y)
and
\mid\omega(\gamma^{+}(x,y))-\nu(\varphi(y))\mid<\epsilon/2
.

Statements (1) and (3) have been satisfied while (2) follows from a basic application of the triangle inequality.
∎

The following theorem is a uniform version of the previous proposition.

Theorem 3.18
.

Suppose that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\lambda
witnesses domination. Then for every formula
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
\epsilon>0
there exists formulas
\chi_{\epsilon}^{-}(x,y)
and
\chi_{\epsilon}^{+}(x,y)
in
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
such that

(1)

\chi_{\epsilon}^{-}(x,y)\subseteq(\psi(y)\wedge x=x)\subseteq\chi_{\epsilon}^{+}(x,y)
.

(2)

for any
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\omega(\chi_{\epsilon}^{+}(x,y))-\omega(\chi_{\epsilon}^{-}(x,y))<\epsilon
.

(3)

for any
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
|\omega(\chi_{\epsilon}^{\dagger}(x,y))-\nu(\varphi(y))|<\epsilon
where
\dagger\in\{+,-\}
.

Proof.

Fix
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
\epsilon>0
. By Lemma
3.2
, the space
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is a non-empty compact Hausdorff space. By Proposition
3.17
, for each
\omega
in
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
, there exists formulas
\gamma_{\omega}^{-}(x,y)
and
\gamma_{\omega}^{+}(x,y)
in
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(A)
such that

(1)

\gamma_{\omega}^{-}(x,y)\subseteq(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\subseteq\gamma_{\omega}^{+}(x,y)
.

(2)

\omega(\gamma_{\omega}^{+}(x,y))-\omega(\gamma_{\omega}^{-}(x,y))<\epsilon
.

Let
D_{\omega}:\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})\to[0,1]
,
D_{\omega}(\eta)=\eta(\gamma_{\omega}^{+}(x,y))-\eta(\gamma_{\omega}^{-}(x,y))
. Since evaluation maps are continuous,
D_{\omega}
is continuous. Let
A_{\omega}=D_{\omega}^{-1}([0,\epsilon))
. Now
\bigcup_{\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)}A_{\omega}
is an open cover of
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
since each
\omega
in
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is in
A_{\omega}
. Hence there is a finite subcover. Without loss of generality suppose that
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}A_{\omega_{i}}
is a finite subcover and consider the formula
\chi_{\epsilon}^{-}(x,y):=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{-}(x,y)
and
\chi_{\epsilon}^{+}(x,y):=\bigwedge_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}^{+}(x,y)
. Clearly,
\chi_{\epsilon}^{-}(x,y)\subseteq(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\subseteq\chi_{\epsilon}^{+}(x,y)
.

Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Then
\omega\in A_{\omega_{i}}
for some
i\leq n
and so we have that

|\omega(\chi_{\epsilon}^{+}(x,y))-\omega(\chi_{\epsilon}^{-}(x,y))|\leq|\omega(\gamma_{i}^{+}(x,y))-\omega(\gamma_{i}^{-}(x,y))|=D_{\omega_{i}}(\omega)<\epsilon.

Statement (3) follows from an application of the triangle inequality.
∎

3.4.
Localizations

Localizations are inherently measure theoretic constructions which are trivial for types. We show that if
\mu
dominates
\nu
and if this domination is witnessed by a separated amalgam (e.g. the restriction of a Morley product), then
\mu
dominates any localization of
\nu
. This phenomenon also occurs when
\mu=\delta_{p}
for some type
p
and
\nu
is any measure. We begin with the definition of localization of a measure
[
10
, Definition 1.5]
.

Definition 3.19
.

Let
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(A)
and
\varphi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
. Suppose that
0<\nu(\varphi(y))
. Let
\nu_{[\varphi]}
be the
localization of
\nu
to
\varphi(y)
, i.e. for any
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
,

\nu_{[\varphi]}(\psi(y))=\frac{\nu(\varphi(y)\wedge\psi(y))}{\nu(\varphi(y))}.

Proposition 3.20
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
\varphi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(A)
. Suppose that
0<\nu(\varphi(y))<1
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(A)
witnesses domination. Then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu_{[\varphi]}
.

Proof.

Let
r:=\nu(\varphi(y))
and
s:=\nu(\neg\varphi(y))
. Let
\Phi(x,y):=(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)
and consider the measures
\lambda_{[\Phi]}
and
\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]}
in
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
. We show
\lambda_{[\Phi]}
witness
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu_{[\varphi]}
.

(1)

We claim that
\pi_{x}(\lambda_{[\Phi]})=\mu|_{A}
. Fix
\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(A)
and notice

\pi_{x}(\lambda_{[\Phi]})(\psi(x))=\frac{\lambda(\psi(x)\wedge\varphi(y))}{\lambda(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)}=\frac{\pi_{x}(\lambda(\psi(x)))\cdot\pi_{y}(\lambda(\varphi(y)))}{\pi_{y}(\lambda)(\varphi(y))}

=\pi_{x}(\lambda)(\psi(x))=\mu(\psi(x)).

Notice that the second equality follows from the fact that
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(A)
and so this assumption is necessary in our proof.

(2)

By (1), we have some
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{[\Phi]},\mu)
. It suffices to prove that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu_{[\varphi]}
.

(3)

Similarly,
\pi_{x}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]})=\mu|_{A}
. Hence
\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]},\mu)\neq\emptyset
and let
\eta\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]},\mu)
.

(4)

Let
\omega^{\prime}=r\omega+s\eta
. We show that
\omega^{\prime}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
.

(a)

Claim:
\omega^{\prime}|_{A}=\lambda
. Let
\theta(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(A)
. Then

\omega^{\prime}(\theta(x,y))=(r\lambda_{[\Phi]}+s\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]})(\theta(x,y))

=\frac{r\lambda(\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y))}{r}+\frac{s\lambda(\theta(x,y)\wedge\neg\varphi(y))}{s}=\lambda(\theta(x,y)).

(b)

Claim:
\pi_{x}(\omega^{\prime})=\mu
. Since
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{[\Phi]},\mu)
and
\eta\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]},\mu)
,

\pi_{x}(\omega^{\prime})=\pi_{x}((r\omega+s\eta))=r\pi_{x}(\omega)+s\pi_{x}(\eta)=r\mu+s\mu=\mu.

(5)

Since
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\lambda
witnesses domination, we conclude that
\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})=\nu
.

(6)

Finally, we argue that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu_{[\varphi]}
. Fix
\rho(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and consider the following sequence of computations:

\pi_{y}(\omega)(\rho(y))=\omega(\rho(y)\wedge x=x)\overset{(a)}{=}\omega(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)

\overset{(b)}{=}\frac{r\omega(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)}{r\omega(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)}

\overset{(c)}{=}\frac{r\omega(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)+s\eta(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y)\wedge x=x))}{r\omega(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)+s\eta(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)}

=\frac{\omega^{\prime}(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)}{\omega^{\prime}(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)}

=\frac{\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y))}{\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})(\varphi(y))}\overset{(d)}{=}\frac{\nu(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y))}{\nu(\varphi(y))}=\nu_{[\varphi]}(\rho(y)).

We now provide the following justifications:

(a)

Follows from
1=\lambda_{[\varphi]}(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)=\omega(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)
.

(b)

Since
\omega(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)=1
, we are dividing the numerator and denominator by
r
.

(c)

Follows from
\eta(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)=0
.

(d)

Follows from domination, i.e
\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})=\nu
. ∎

Corollary 3.21
.

Suppose that
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
0<\nu(\varphi(y))<1
for some
\varphi(y)\in\mathcal{L}(A)
. Then
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu_{[\varphi]}
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
witness
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
. Then
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(A)
(see Lemma
2.20
) and so by Proposition
3.20
, the statement holds.

∎

4.
Basic Preservation Results

In this section, we discuss several preservation theorems. For classical domination, realized types form the bottom tier of the domination preorder, and notions like invariance, finite satisfiability, and definability are preserved under domination (See
[
13
]
). For extension domination, we show that the collection of smooth measures form the bottom tier of the extension domination preorder and if
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
and
\mu
is invariant, smooth, or finitely satisfiable, then
\nu
is respectively invariant, smooth, or finitely satisfiable. For definable measures, the preservation remains open.

4.1.
Invariance

We first show that invariance is preserved under extension domination. We recall the definition.

Definition 4.1
.

We say that
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
is
invariant over
A
or
A
-invariant
if for every formula
\varphi(x,z)\in\mathcal{L}_{xz}(\emptyset)
and
b,c\in\mathcal{U}^{z}
such that
\operatorname{tp}(b/A)=\operatorname{tp}(c/A)
, we have
\mu(\varphi(x,b))=\mu(\varphi(x,c))
. We use
\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{inv}}_{x}(\mathcal{U},A)
to denote the set of all
A
-invariant measures.

We recall that if
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U},A)
, then for any variable
x
,
\sigma
induces a map
\sigma_{*}:\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})\to\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
via
\sigma_{*}(\omega)=\omega_{\sigma}
where
\omega_{\sigma}(\theta(x,b))=\omega(\theta(x,\sigma^{-1}(b))
. We remark that
\omega
is
A
-invariant
if and only if
\omega=\omega_{\sigma}
for all
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U},A)
.

Lemma 4.2
.

Assume that
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{inv}}_{x}(\mathcal{U},A)
,
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
,
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
, and
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U},A)
. Then the map
\sigma_{*}|_{\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)}:\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)\to\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is an affine homeomorphism. Moreover, for every
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U},A)
, there exists a measure
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
such that
\omega_{\sigma}=\omega
.

Proof.

Fix
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U},A)
.

(1)

We first show that
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is invariant under
\sigma_{*}
. Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Notice that
\omega_{\sigma}|_{A}=\lambda
since
\sigma
fixes all formulas with parameters only from
A
. Also,
\pi_{x}(\omega_{\sigma})=\mu
by the invariance of
\mu
over
A
.

(2)

Injective: Suppose that
\omega\neq\eta
. Then there exists a formula
\varphi(x,y,b)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\omega(\varphi(x,y,b))\neq\eta(\varphi(x,y,b))
. Then
\omega_{\sigma}(\varphi(x,y,\sigma(b))\neq\eta_{\sigma}(\varphi(x,y,\sigma(b))
.

(3)

Surjective: Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. By (1),
\omega_{\sigma^{-1}}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Then
\sigma_{*}(\omega_{\sigma^{-1}})=\omega
.

(4)

Affine homeomorphism: Clearly
\sigma_{*}
preserves convex combinations and by above,
\sigma_{*}(\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu))=\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. It suffices to show that
\sigma_{*}
is a homeomorphism from
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
to
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
. The map
\sigma_{*}
is continuous since the inverse image of a basic open set is basic open (see Fact
2.12
). Hence
\sigma_{*}
is a continuous bijeciton between compact Hausdorff spaces and therefore a homeomorphism.

(5)

Moreover portion: By Lemma
3.2
,
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is a convex compact space. By above,
\sigma_{*}|_{\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)}:\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)\to\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
is an affine homeomorphism. Hence an application of Schauder fixed point theorem establishes the claim. ∎

Proposition 4.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
. Assume that
\mu
is invariant over
A
and
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
. Then
\nu
is invariant over
A
. Moreover, if
\mu
is invariant and
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
, then
\nu
is invariant.

Proof.

Fix
\varphi(y,z)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(\emptyset)
and suppose that
b,c\in\mathcal{U}^{z}
with
\operatorname{tp}(b/A)=\operatorname{tp}(c/A)
. Choose
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U}/A)
such that
\sigma(b)=c
. Since
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
, there exists some
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
witnessing domination. By Lemma
4.2
, take
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
such that
\omega_{\sigma}=\omega
. By domination,
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
. Thus
\nu(\varphi(y,c))=\omega(\varphi(y,c)\wedge x=x)=\omega(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)=\nu(\varphi(y,b))
, proving the
A
-invariance of
\nu
.

The moreover portion follows from the previous paragraph, the and an application of Lemma
3.8
.
∎

4.2.
Smoothness

We show that the collection of smooth measures form the bottom tier of the extension domination preorder and smoothness is preserved under extension domination. It is interesting to note that the proofs of these propositions follow from the usual definition of smoothness (i.e. unique extensions) and not the associated
squeeze property
. We recall the definition of smooth
[
8
, Definition 2.5]
,
[
10
, Definition 2.1]
.

Definition 4.4
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
. We say that
\mu
is
smooth
over
A
if for any
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\nu|_{A}=\mu|_{A}
, then
\nu=\mu
. We say
\mu
is
smooth
if it is smooth over some small
A
.

Moreover, if
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(A)
, we say that
\eta
is smooth over
A
if there exists a unique measure
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\mu|_{A}=\eta
. We call
\mu
the
unique extension
of
\eta
.

Note that for a global type
p
,
\delta_{p}
is smooth if and only if
p
is realized.

Proposition 4.5
.

Let
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
,
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
, and suppose that
\nu
is smooth over
A
. Then for any
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
.

Proof.

Choose any
\lambda
in
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
such that
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{A}
and
\pi_{y}(\lambda)=\nu|_{A}
. By Remark
2.19
, such
\lambda
exists. Let
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Notice that

\pi_{y}(\omega)|_{A}=\pi_{y}(\omega|_{A})=\pi_{y}(\lambda)=\nu|_{A}.

Hence
\pi_{y}(\omega)
extends
\nu|_{A}
. Since
\nu
is smooth over
A
, it follows that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
.
∎

Proposition 4.6
.

Suppose that
\mu
is smooth over
A
and
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
. Then
\nu
is smooth over
A
.

Proof.

Let
\lambda
witness
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
. For any
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
. Suppose that there exists
\nu_{1}
and
\nu_{2}
in
\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\nu_{1}\neq\nu_{2}
and
\nu_{1}|_{A}=\nu_{2}|_{A}=\nu|_{A}
. By Lemma
3.2
, the sets
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\nu_{1})
and
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\nu_{2})
are non-empty and so let
\omega_{i}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\nu_{i})
for
i\in\{1,2\}
.

We now argue that
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. By construction,
\omega_{i}|_{A}=\lambda
. Now notice that
\pi_{x}(\omega_{i})|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\omega_{i}|_{A})=\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{A}
. Since
\mu
is smooth over
A
, we have that
\pi_{x}(\omega_{i})=\mu
. Hence
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. By domination (i.e.
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu)
, we have that

\nu_{1}=\pi_{y}(\omega_{1})=\nu=\pi_{y}(\omega_{2})=\nu_{2},

which completes the proof.
∎

Proposition 4.7
.

Suppose that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
and
\mu
is smooth. Then
\nu
is smooth.

Proof.

Since
\mu
is smooth, there exists some small set
A
such that
\mu
is smooth over
A
. It is clear from the definition that if
C\supseteq A
, then
\mu
is also smooth over
C
. Since
\mu
dominates
\nu
, there exists some small set
B
such that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},B}\nu
. By Lemma
3.8
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A\cup B}\nu
. By the previous proposition,
\nu
is smooth over
A\cup B
.
∎

The next proposition shows that if the measure
witnessing domination
is smooth, then both measures involved are smooth.

Proposition 4.8
.

Suppose that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\lambda
witnesses domination. If
\lambda
is smooth over
A
then both
\mu
and
\nu
are smooth over
A
.

Proof.

Suppose that
\mu
is not smooth over
A
. Then there exists distinct
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\mu_{1}|_{A}=\mu_{2}|_{A}=\mu|_{A}
. By Lemma
3.2
, the spaces
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu_{1})
and
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu_{2})
are non-empty. Let
\omega_{1}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu_{1})
an
\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu_{2})
. Notice that
\omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}
but
\omega_{1}|_{A}=\omega_{2}|_{A}=\lambda
, a contradiction. A similar argument demonstrates that
\nu
is smooth over
A
.
∎

4.3.
Finite Satisfiability

In this section, we show that finitely satisfiability is preserved under extension domination. Unlike the proofs of the previous two preservation results, the proof in this case follows from the approximation lemma derived in the first section of the paper (i.e. Proposition
3.17
). We recall the definition
[
8
, Definition 2.5]
.

Definition 4.9
.

Suppose that
M\prec\mathcal{U}
and
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
. We say that
\mu
is
finitely satisfiable
in
M
if for any
\varphi(x)
in
\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
, if
\mu(\varphi(x))>0
then there exists some
a\in M
such that
\mathcal{U}\models\varphi(a)
. We say that
\mu
is
finitely satisfiable
if it is finitely satisfiable over some small
M\prec\mathcal{U}

Lemma 4.10
.

Let
M\prec\mathcal{U}
be a small submodel. Suppose that
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\mu
is finitely satisfiable in
M
,
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(M)
and
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{M}
. Let
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Then for any
\gamma(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{xy}(M)
, if
\omega(\gamma(x,y))>0
, then there exists
(a_{0},b_{0})\in M^{xy}
such that
\mathcal{U}\models\gamma(a_{0},b_{0})
.

Proof.

Let
\gamma(x,y)
be given such that
\omega(\gamma(x,y))>0
. Since
\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu
, we have that
\mu(\exists y\gamma(x,y))=\omega(\exists y\gamma(x,y)\wedge y=y)\geq\omega(\gamma(x,y))>0
. Since
\mu
is finitely satisfiable in
M
, there exists some
a_{0}\in M
such that
\mathcal{U}\models\exists y\gamma(a_{0},y)
. Consider the set
B=\{b\in\mathcal{U}^{y}:\models\gamma(a_{0},b)\}
. The set
B
is a non-empty
\mathcal{U}
-definable subset of
\mathcal{U}
. We will argue that
B
is
M
-invariant, hence
M
-definable, and thus
B\cap M^{y}\neq\emptyset
. By the definition of the Boolean algebra
\mathbb{B}_{xy}(M)
,

\gamma(a_{0},y)\equiv\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\bigwedge_{j_{i}=1}^{m_{i}}(\theta_{j_{i}}(a_{0},y)\wedge\psi_{j_{i}}(a_{0}))^{\varepsilon_{i}(j_{i})},

where
\varepsilon_{i}:\{1,...,m_{i}\}\to\{0,1\}
. For
b\in\mathcal{U}^{y}
and
\sigma\in\operatorname{Aut}(\mathcal{U},M)
,
\mathcal{U}\models\theta_{j_{i}}(a_{0},b)
if and only if
\mathcal{U}\models\theta_{i}(a_{0},\sigma(b))
since all the parameters which occur in
\theta_{i}(a_{0},y)
are in
M
. Moreover,
\sigma
does not affect
\mathcal{U}\models\psi_{i}(a_{0})
since
\sigma(a_{0})=a_{0}
. Thus
\mathcal{U}\models\gamma(a_{0},b)
if and only if
\mathcal{U}\models\gamma(a_{0},\sigma(b))
and so
B
is
M
-invariant. Choose
b_{0}\in B\cap M^{y}
and so
\mathcal{U}\models\gamma(a_{0},b_{0})
. This completes the proof.
∎

Theorem 4.11
.

Suppose that
M\prec\mathcal{U}
,
\mu
is finitely satisfiable in
M
, and
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},M}\nu
. Then
\nu
is finitely satisfiable in
M
.

Moreover, if
\mu
is finitely satisfiable and
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
, then
\nu
is finitely satisfiable.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda
in
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(M)
such that
\lambda
witnesses
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},M}\nu
. Fix a formula
\varphi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\nu(\varphi(y))>0
. Choose
\epsilon>0
such that
\nu(\varphi(y))>\epsilon
. Fix
\omega
in
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. By Proposition
3.17
, there exists a formula
\gamma(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{xy}(M)
such that

(1)

\gamma(x,y)\subseteq(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)

(2)

|\omega(\gamma(x,y))-\omega(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)|<\frac{\epsilon}{2}
.

Notice that

\nu(\varphi(y))=\pi_{y}(\omega)(\varphi(y))=\omega(\varphi(y)\wedge x=x)\approx_{\frac{\epsilon}{2}}\omega(\gamma(x,y))\implies\omega(\gamma(x,y))>\frac{\epsilon}{2}.

Since
\gamma(x,y)\in\mathbb{B}_{xy}(M)
, we can apply Lemma
4.10
and so there exists
(a,b)\in M^{xy}
such that
\mathcal{U}\models\gamma(a,b)
. By construction,
\mathcal{U}\models\varphi(b)
which completes the proof.

The moreover portion follows from the previous paragraph and an application of Lemma
3.8
.
∎

Definition 4.12
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
. Then we say that
\mu
is
definable
if there exists a small subset
A
of
\mathcal{U}
such that
\mu
is
A
-invariant and for any formula
\varphi(x,z)\in\mathcal{L}_{xz}(\emptyset)
the map
F_{\mu,A}^{\varphi}:S_{z}(A)\to[0,1]
via
F_{\mu,A}^{\varphi}(q)=\mu(\varphi(x,b))
where
b\models q
is continuous.

Question 4.13
.

Suppose that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\nu
and
\mu
is
A
-definable. Does this imply that
\nu
is
A
-definable? Even if
\mu
or
\nu
is a type?

5.
Separated Amalgams

In this section, we consider a variant of extension domination which we call
amalgam deciphering
. Instead of requiring
\mu
to dominate
\nu
over the class of all extension (i.e.
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
), we only require that
\mu
dominates
\nu
over the class of all separated amalgams, a space we call
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
. This variant is well-behaved with respect to localizations and certain kinds of convex combinations. It is thus tempting to call this property
amalgam domination
. However, fundamental properties about this definition remain open and it seems more than plausible to the authors that this variant fails to be both reflexive and transitive. Therefore, we have decided to use the term
deciphering
in place of
domination
.

Nevertheless, amalgam deciphering provides some new results about extension domination. We observe that
\delta_{p}
extension dominates a measure if and only if
\delta_{p}
amalgam deciphers a measure. From this observation, we show that extension domination is well-behaved with respect to certain convex combination if the measure on the left is a Dirac measure concentrating on a type.

5.1.
Basic definitions and observations

The difference between extension domination and amalgam deciphering is the collection of measures over which domination occurs. Similar to extension domination, we begin with the definition of an
amalgam space
.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(A)
such that
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
. We define the corresponding
amalgam space
as follows

\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu):=\{\omega\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(\mathcal{U}):\omega|_{A}=\lambda,\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu\}.

We remark that in the previous definition, we assume that
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(A)
. If we did not, then
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
would be empty automatically. However, we will see that this is not sufficient to guarantee that
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
is non-empty (see Example
5.4
).

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(A)
such that
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
. Then

(1)

\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)\subseteq\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
.

(2)

\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
is a compact Hausdorff space (under the induced topology).

(3)

\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
is convex.

Proof.

First note that if
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)=\emptyset
, then the statements are vaccuously true.

(1)

Immediate by definition.

(2)

By (1) and Lemma
3.2
, it suffices to show that
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
is a closed subset of
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. For any
\theta(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
, the map
D_{\theta}:\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})\to[0,1]
via
D_{\theta}(\omega)=\omega(\theta(x,y))
is continuous. For each
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, observe that

A_{\varphi,\psi}:=\{\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu):\omega(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y))=\omega(\varphi(x)\wedge y=y)\omega(\psi(y)\wedge x=x)\}

=(D_{\varphi\wedge\psi}-D_{\varphi}\cdot D_{\psi})^{-1}(\{0\}).

Hence
A_{\varphi,\psi}
is a closed subset of
\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. Notice that
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)=\bigcap_{\varphi,\psi}A_{\varphi,\psi}
, and so it is also closed.

(3)

Suppose that
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
and fix
r,s\in[0,1]
such that
r+s=1
. A straightforward computation shows
\pi_{x}(r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2})=\mu
and
(r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2})|_{A}=\lambda
. It remains to show that
r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(\mathcal{U})
. A direct computation shows that for any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\psi(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
,

(r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2})(\varphi(x)\wedge\psi(y))=\mu(\varphi(x))\pi_{y}(r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2})(\psi(y))

Hence
r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2}
is a separated amalgam. ∎

We now provide an example where
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(A)
and
\mu|_{A}=\pi_{x}(\lambda)
and yet
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)=\emptyset
. This is in contrast to (1) of Lemma
3.2
. In general, finding whether or not separated amalgams with certain properties exists (as in Lemma
3.11
) is the main obstruction to the general theory of amalgam deciphering.

Definition 5.3

(Diagonal measure construction)
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and suppose that
|x|=|y|
. We let

\Delta_{xy}(\mu)
denote the measure
\Delta(\mu)
, where
\Delta:\mathcal{U}^{x}\to\mathcal{U}^{x}\times\mathcal{U}^{y}
via
a\mapsto(a,a)
.

Example 5.4
.

Let
M=(\mathbb{R};<)
and
\mathcal{U}
a monster model. We define the following types and measures:

(1)

Let
p\in S_{x}(M)
given by
\{x>a:a\in M\}
.

(2)

Let
p_{+}
be the unique global coheir of
p
over
M
. More precisely,
p_{+}
is finitely satisfiable in
M
and given by
\{x>a:a\in M\}\cup\{x<b:M<b\}
.

(3)

Let
p_{+\infty}
be the unique global heir of
p
. In particular,
p_{+\infty}
is definable over
M
and given by
\{x>a:a\in\mathcal{U}\}
.

(4)

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
where
\mu:=\frac{1}{2}\delta_{p_{+}}+\frac{1}{2}\delta_{p_{+\infty}}
. Notice that
\mu|_{M}=\delta_{p}
. Note that that
\mu
is invariant over
M
, but neither definable or finitely satisfiable over
M
.

(5)

Let
\omega\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
where
\omega:=\Delta_{xy}(\mu)=\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{xy}(\delta_{p_{+\infty}})+\frac{1}{2}\Delta_{xy}(\delta_{p_{+}})
.

(a)

Notice that
\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu
.

(b)

Notice that the formula
x=y
is in both
\Delta_{xy}(\delta_{p_{+\infty}})
and
\Delta_{xy}(\delta_{p_{+}})
. Hence
\omega(x=y)=1
.

(c)

Notice that
\omega
is not a separated amalgam. Fix
b\in\mathcal{U}\backslash M
such that
M<b
. Consider the formula
\gamma(x,y):=x>b\wedge y<b
. In particular,
\omega(\gamma(x,y))=0
while
\pi_{x}(\omega)(x>b)\cdot\pi_{y}(\omega)(y<b)=\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{2}=\frac{1}{4}
.

(6)

Consider
\lambda:=\omega|_{M}
.

(a)

Notice that
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{M}=\delta_{p}
since
\pi_{x}(\omega)=\mu
.

(b)

By Lemma
2.20
,
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(M)
, since
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\delta_{p}(x)
.

(c)

In fact,
\lambda=\Delta_{xy}(\delta_{p})
.

(d)

Since
\lambda=\omega|_{M}
,
\lambda(x=y)=1
.

We now argue that
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)=\emptyset
. Suppose not and let
\omega^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
. Fix
b\in\mathcal{U}
such that
b>M
. Notice that
x=y\subseteq[x\leq b\vee y\geq b]
. Since
\omega^{\prime}
is a separated amalgam,

1=\lambda(x=y)=\omega^{\prime}(x=y)\leq\omega^{\prime}(x\leq b\vee y\geq b)=1-\omega^{\prime}(x>b\wedge y<b)

=1-\omega^{\prime}(x>b\wedge y=y)\omega^{\prime}(y<b\wedge x=x)=1-\mu(x>b)\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})(y<b)

=1-\frac{1}{2}\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})(y<b)\implies\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})(y<b)=0.

On the other hand, we have the following.

\frac{1}{2}=\mu(x<b)=\pi_{x}(\omega^{\prime})(x<b)=\omega^{\prime}(x<b\wedge y=y)=\omega^{\prime}(x<b\wedge x=y)

=\omega^{\prime}(y<b\wedge x=y)=\omega^{\prime}(y<b\wedge x=x)=\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})(y<b).

Hence we have a contradiction.

With the previous example in mind, we now give the definition of amalgam deciphering.

Definition 5.5
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
A
be a small subset of
\mathcal{U}
. We say that
\mu

amalgam deciphers
\nu
(over
A
)
if there exists
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(A)
such that

(1)

\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{A}
.

(2)

\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)\neq\emptyset
.

(3)

For every
\omega\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
.

We write
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu
to mean “
\mu
amalgam deciphers
\nu
over
A
” and
\mu\blacktriangleright\nu
to mean “there exists some small set
A
such that
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu
”.

The following proposition essentially demonstrates all the pure theory we know about amalgam deciphering.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let

,

,
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})

(1)

If

\mu\blacktriangleright_{B}\nu

(2)

\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},A}\mu

\delta_{p}\blacktriangleright_{A}\mu
.

(3)

If
\mu
is A-invariant and
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu
, then
\nu
is
A
-invariant.

(4)

If
\mu
is smooth and
\mu\blacktriangleright\nu
, then
\nu
is smooth.

(5)

If
\nu
is smooth over

, then for any
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
, it follows that
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu
.

Proof.

Most of the statements above of slight variations of the proof as in the corresponding statement for extension domination and the only additional information needed is that the amalgamation space is non-empty. Therefore, the proofs are sketched.

(1)

Identical to Lemma
3.8
.

(2)

Immediate from Lemma
2.20
.

(3)

Word for word as in the proofs of Lemma

and Proposition

.

(4)

By Remark

, fix

5.2.
Localizations and convex combinations

While the general theory of amalgam deciphering remains mysterious, as previously advertised, amalgam deciphering interacts well with localizations and convex combinations. The proof of the following result is similar to Proposition
3.20
, so we only highlight the differences.

Proposition 5.7
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
\varphi(x,b)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(b)
. Suppose that
0<\nu(\varphi(y,b))<1
. If
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu
, then
\mu\blacktriangleright_{Ab}\nu_{[\varphi]}
.

Proof.

Let
r:=\nu(\varphi(y,b))
and
s=\nu(\neg\varphi(y,b))
. By Proposition
5.6
,
choose
\lambda\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(Ab)
such that
\lambda
witnesses
\mu\blacktriangleright_{Ab}\nu
. Let
\Phi(x,y):=\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x
and consider the measures
\lambda_{[\Phi]}
,
\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(Ab)
. We argue that
\lambda_{[\Phi]}
witness
\mu\blacktriangleright_{Ab}\nu
.

(1)

Claim:
\pi_{x}(\lambda_{[\Phi]})=\mu|_{Ab}
. The proof is the same as Proposition
3.20
.

(2)

Claim:
\lambda_{[\Phi]}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(Ab)
. Let
\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(Ab)
and
\theta(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(Ab)
. Then

\lambda_{[\Phi]}(\psi(x)\wedge\theta(y))=\frac{\lambda(\psi(x)\wedge\theta(y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{\lambda(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}

\overset{(*)}{=}\frac{\mu(\varphi(x))\nu(\theta(y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{\nu(\varphi(y,b))}=\mu(\varphi(x))\cdot\nu_{[\varphi]}(\theta(y)),

where equality
(*)
holds since
\pi_{y}(\lambda)=\nu|_{Ab}
and
\lambda
is a separated amalgam. By Lemma
2.18
, the claim holds.

(3)

Claim:
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{[\Phi]},\mu)\neq\emptyset
and
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]},\mu)\neq\emptyset
. By assumption,
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)\neq\emptyset
. Let
\omega\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
and consider
\omega_{[\Phi]}
and
\omega_{[\neg\Phi]}
. A straightforward computation show that
\omega_{[\Phi]}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{[\Phi]},\mu)
and
\omega_{[\neg\Phi]}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]},\mu)
.

(4)

Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{[\Phi]},\mu)
. It suffices to show that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu_{[\varphi]}
.

(5)

Let
\eta\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{[\neg\Phi]},\mu)
. Consider
\omega^{\prime}=r\omega+s\eta
. A straightforward computation show that
\omega^{\prime}|_{A}=\lambda
and
\pi_{x}(\omega^{\prime})=\mu
.

(6)

Claim:
\omega^{\prime}\in\mathfrak{M}^{\operatorname{Am}}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
. Let
\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\theta(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
. Then a straightforward computation shows

\omega^{\prime}(\psi(x)\wedge\theta(y))=\mu(\psi(x))(r\pi_{y}(\omega)+s\pi_{y}(\eta))(\theta(y)).

So the claim holds by Lemma
2.18
.

(7)

Hence
\omega^{\prime}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
. By amalgam deciphering,
\pi_{y}(\omega^{\prime})=\nu
. A similar argument to Proposition
3.20
(6), shows
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu_{[\varphi]}
, completing the proof. ∎

Our next goal is to show that amalgam deciphering is well-behaved with respect to a certain kind of convex combinations. We begin with a lemma and advise the reader to recall Definition
2.10
.

Lemma 5.8
.

Suppose that
\nu_{1}\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
\nu_{2}\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
and
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})\cap\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})=\emptyset
. Then there exists a formula
\gamma(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})\subseteq[\gamma(y)]
and
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})\cap[\gamma(y)]=\emptyset
.

Proof.

Since
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})
is closed, we have that
S_{y}(\mathcal{U})\backslash\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})
is an open subset of
S_{y}(\mathcal{U})
. Then
S_{y}(\mathcal{U})\backslash\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})=\bigcup_{i\in I}[\gamma_{i}(y)]
and since
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})\subseteq S_{y}(\mathcal{U})\backslash\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})
, this collection of basic clopens is an open cover of
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})
. We have that
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})
is compact and so there is a finite subcover, say
\bigcup_{i=1}^{n}[\gamma_{i}(y)]
. Setting
\gamma:=\bigvee_{i=1}^{n}\gamma_{i}(y)
concludes the lemma.
∎

Proposition 5.9
.

Suppose that
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu_{1}
and
\mu\blacktriangleright_{A}\nu_{2}
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})\cap\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})=\emptyset

such that for any

with

\mu\blacktriangleright_{Ab}r\nu_{1}+s\nu_{2}
.

Proof.

Assume the conditions above. By Lemma
5.8
, there exists
\varphi(y,b)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{1})\subseteq[\varphi(x,b)]
and
[\varphi(x,b)]\cap\operatorname{supp}(\nu_{2})=\emptyset
. By Proposition
5.6
,
\mu\blacktriangleright_{Ab}\nu_{i}
. Let
\lambda_{i}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Am}}(Ab)

. We claim that
\lambda

\mu\geq_{Ab}r\nu_{1}+s\nu_{2}
.

(1)

Claim:
\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\mu|_{Ab}
. Notice that

\pi_{x}(\lambda)=\pi_{x}(r\lambda_{1}+s\lambda_{2})=r\pi_{x}(\lambda_{1})+s\pi_{x}(\lambda_{2})=r\mu|_{Ab}+s\mu|_{Ab}=\mu|_{Ab}.

(2)

Claim:
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)\neq\emptyset
. By assumption,
\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{1},\mu),\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{2},\mu)\neq\emptyset
. Take
\omega_{i}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{i},\mu)
. It follows immediately
r\omega_{1}+s\omega_{2}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
.

(3)

Fix
\omega\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda,\mu)
. It suffices to show that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
. Consider the formula
\Phi(x,y):=\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x
.

(4)

Claim:
\omega_{[\Phi]}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{1},\mu)
.

(
i
)

Subclaim:
\pi_{x}(\omega_{[\Phi]})=\mu
. Let
\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and notice

\pi_{x}(\omega_{[\Phi]})(\psi(x))=\omega_{[\Phi]}(\psi(x)\wedge y=y)=\frac{\omega(\psi(x)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{\omega(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}

\overset{(*)}{=}\frac{\pi_{x}(\omega)(\psi(x))\cdot\pi_{y}(\omega)(\varphi(y,b))}{\pi_{y}(\omega)(\varphi(y,b))}=\pi_{x}(\omega)(\psi(x))=\mu(\psi(x)).

The equation
(*)
follows from the fact that
\omega\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}^{\operatorname{Am}}(\mathcal{U})
.

(
ii
)

Subclaim:
\omega_{[\Phi]}|_{Ab}=\lambda_{1}
. Fix
\theta(x,y)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy}(Ab)
. Now consider

\omega_{[\Phi]}(\theta(x,y))=\frac{\omega(\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{\omega(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}

\overset{(a)}{=}\frac{(r\lambda_{1}+s\lambda_{2})(\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{(r\lambda_{1}+s\lambda_{2})(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}

=\frac{r\lambda_{1}(\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))+s\lambda_{2}(\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{r\lambda_{1}(\ \varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)+s\lambda_{2}(\ \varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}

\overset{(b)}{=}\frac{r\lambda_{1}(\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{r\lambda_{1}(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}\overset{(c)}{=}\lambda_{1}(\theta(x,y)).

Equation
(a)
is justified since
\theta(x,y)\wedge\varphi(y,b)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(Ab)
and
\omega|_{Ab}=\lambda
. Equation

is justified since
\lambda_{2}(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)=\nu_{2}(\varphi(y,b))=0
. Equation
(c)
is justified since
\lambda_{1}(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)=\nu(\varphi(y,b))=1
.

(
iii
)

Subclaim:
\omega_{[\Phi]}\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}^{\operatorname{Amal}}(\mathcal{U})
. Let
\chi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\psi(y))\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
. Then

\omega_{[\Phi]}(\chi(x)\wedge\psi(y))=\frac{\omega(\chi(x)\wedge\psi(y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{\omega(\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)}

=\pi_{x}(\omega)(\chi(x))\cdot\frac{\pi_{y}(\omega)(\psi(y)\wedge\varphi(y,b))}{\pi_{y}(\omega)(\varphi(y,b))}

=\pi_{x}(\omega)(\chi(x))\cdot(\pi_{y}(\omega))_{[\varphi]}(\psi(y))

By Lemma
2.18
, the subclaim holds.

(5)

Claim:
\omega_{[\neg\Phi]}\in\operatorname{Amal}(\lambda_{2},\mu)
. The proof is similar to (4).

(6)

By amalgam deciphering,
\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\Phi]})=\nu_{1}
and
\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\neg\Phi]})=\nu_{2}
.

(7)

Claim:
\pi_{y}(\omega)=r\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\Phi]})+s\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\neg\Phi]})
. Fix
\rho(y)\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
. Notice

\pi_{y}(\omega)(\rho(y))=\omega(\rho(y)\wedge x=x)

=\omega(\rho(y)\wedge\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)+\omega(\rho(y)\wedge\neg\varphi(y,b)\wedge x=x)

=r\omega_{[\Phi]}(\rho(y)\wedge x=x)+s\omega_{[\neg\Phi]}(\rho(y)\wedge x=x).

=r\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\Phi]})(\rho(y))+s\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\neg\Phi]})(\rho(y)).

(8)

We finally show that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=r\nu_{1}+s\nu_{2}

\pi_{y}(\omega)=r\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\Phi]})+s\pi_{y}(\omega_{[\neg\Phi]})=r\nu_{1}+s\nu_{2}.

where the second equation follows from

. ∎

6.
Examples

We construct some explicit examples of extension domination. These examples demonstrate that extension domination is a non-trivial relation. More importantly, these computations demonstrate how to effectively show when one measure extension dominates another. Our first examples occur in Presburger arithmetic. There, we use a restriction of the Morley product to construct a witness for extension domination. Later, we will work in the Frässé limit of ordered equivalence relations. For the measures we consider here, the restriction of the Morley product does not witness extension domination. However, a witness does exist and is constructed by considering
smooth extensions
.

We begin with a lemma which make our calculations easier. The proof is a straightforward computation and left to the readers.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
\mu,\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
. If
\mu
and
\nu
agree on a basis i.e. a collection of definable sets (in variable
x
)
\mathcal{B}
closed under finite intersections such that any definable subset
X
(in variable
x
) is a finite union of sets in
\mathcal{B}
, then
\mu=\nu
.

We also recall the definition of the
Morley product
for invariant measures. This is well-defined in the NIP setting since invariance is equivalent to Borel-definable.

Definition 6.2
.

Suppose that
T
is an NIP theory,
M\models T
,
\mathcal{U}
is a monster model of
T
,
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
,
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
, and
\mu
is invariant over a small submodel
M
. Then we can define a measure
\mu\otimes\nu
in
\mathfrak{M}_{xy}
, known as the Morley product, where for any formula
\varphi(x,y;z)\in\mathcal{L}_{xy,z}(\emptyset)
, we have that

\mu\otimes\nu(\varphi(x,y,b))=\int_{S_{x}(N)}F_{\mu,N}^{\varphi}d(\widehat{\nu|_{N}}).

where

(1)

N
is a small model containing
Mb
.

(2)

F_{\mu,N}^{\varphi}(q)=\mu(\varphi(x,c,b))
where
c\in\mathcal{U}
and
c\models q
.

(3)

\widehat{\nu|_{N}}
is the unique regular Borel probability measure on
S_{y}(N)
which extends
\nu|_{N}
on definable sets (See Fact
2.12
(3)). We will simply write
\widehat{\nu|_{N}}
simply as
\nu|_{N}
.

6.1.
Presburger Arithmetic

Let
M=(\mathbb{Z};+,<,0)
and
\mathcal{U}
be a monster model of
\mathcal{U}
. We recall that this theory is NIP. We give some examples of extension domination in this structure.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{y}(\mathcal{U})
.

(1)

Suppose that for every
b\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mu(x>b)=\nu(x>b)=1
. Then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
.

(2)

Suppose that for every
b\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mu(x<b)=\nu(x<b)=1
. Then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
.

Proof.

The proofs are similar and so we only prove
(1)
. It is clear that for each
p\in\operatorname{supp}(\nu)
,
p
is
\mathbb{Z}
-invariant (even
\mathbb{Z}
-definable). It follows from
[
2
, Lemma 2.10]
that
\nu
is
\mathbb{Z}
-invariant. Thus the Morley product
\nu\otimes\mu
is well-defined. Hence, consider the measure
\lambda:=\nu_{y}\otimes\mu_{x}|_{\mathbb{Z}}
. Let
\varphi(x,y):=y>x
and notice that

\lambda(y>x)=\nu_{y}\otimes\mu_{x}(y>x)=\int_{S_{x}(\mathbb{Z})}F_{\nu_{y},\mathbb{Z}}^{\varphi}d\mu_{x}=\int_{S_{x}(\mathbb{Z})}\mathbf{1}d\mu=1.

The second equality follows since
F_{\nu_{y},\mathbb{Z}}^{\varphi}(q)=\nu(y>b)=1
where
b\models q
.

Let
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
. We now show that for every
a\in\mathcal{U}
,
\pi_{y}(\omega)(a<y)=1
. Fix
a\in\mathcal{U}
and consider the following computation:

\pi_{y}(\omega)(a<y))=\omega(a<y\wedge x=x)\overset{(*)}{=}\omega(a<y\wedge x<y)

=\omega((a<x\wedge x<y)\vee(x\leq a<y))\geq\omega(a<x\wedge x<y)

\overset{(*)}{=}\omega(a<x\wedge y=y)=\pi_{x}(\omega)(a<x)=1.

The
(*)
equations are justified by the fact that
\omega(y>x)=\lambda(y>x)=1
.

We now argue that for every
\omega\in\operatorname{E}(\lambda,\mu)
,
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
. For each
n\geq 2
, let
D_{n}(x):=\exists z(n\cdot z=x)
. Every definable subset of
\mathcal{U}
is a Boolean combination of intervals and formulas of the form
D_{n}(x+k)
where
k\leq n
. We let
I(y)
be of the form
x<b,b<x,x=b,x\neq b
and consider the set
\mathcal{B}
where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}^{\varepsilon_{1}(j)}(y)\wedge\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l}):n,m,s_{l}\in\mathbb{N},k_{l}\leq s_{l},

\displaystyle\varepsilon_{1}:\{1,...,n\}\to\{0,1\},\varepsilon_{2}:\{1,...,m\}\to\{0,1\}\Bigg\}.

Any definable subset of
\mathcal{U}^{y}
can be written as a finite union of elements from
\mathcal{B}
. We now show that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
. By Lemma
6.1
, it suffices to show that for any
\theta(y)\in\mathcal{B}
,
\pi_{y}(\omega)(\theta(y))=\nu(\theta(y))
. Let
\theta(y)=\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}^{\varepsilon_{1}(j)}(y)\wedge\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l})
. Let
c\in\mathcal{U}^{y}
such that
c
is larger than all the parameters which occur in
\theta(y)
. Then either:

(1)

[\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}^{\varepsilon_{1}(j)}(y)\wedge y>c]=\emptyset
and so
[\theta(y)\wedge y>c]=\emptyset
. In this case, we have that

\pi_{y}(\omega)(\theta(y))\overset{(*)}{=}\pi_{y}(\omega)(\theta(y)\wedge y>c)=\pi_{y}(\omega)(\emptyset)=0

=\nu(\emptyset)=\nu(\theta(y)\wedge y>c)\overset{(*)}{=}\nu(\theta(y)).

The equality
(*)
follows from the fact that
\omega(y>c)=\nu(y>c)=1
.

(2)

Or
[\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}^{\varepsilon_{1}(j)}(y)\wedge y>c]=[y>c]
and so

\pi_{y}(\omega)(\theta(y))\overset{(*)}{=}\pi_{y}(\omega)(\theta(y)\wedge y>c)=\pi_{y}(\omega)\left(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l})\wedge y>c\right)

\overset{(*)}{=}\pi_{y}(\omega)\left(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l})\right)\overset{(\dagger)}{=}\pi_{y}(\lambda)\left(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l})\right)

=\nu\left(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l})\right)\overset{(*)}{=}\nu\left(\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D_{s_{l}}^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}(y+k_{l})\wedge y>c\right)=\nu(\theta(y)).

Again, equality
(*)
follows from the fact that
\omega(y>c)=\nu(y>c)=1
. Equality
(\dagger)
follows from the fact that the formula
\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m}D^{\varepsilon_{2}(l)}_{s_{l}}(y+k_{l})\in\mathcal{L}_{y}(\mathbb{Z})
.

Hence, we conclude that
\pi_{y}(\omega)=\nu
and so
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Suppose that for every
b\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mu(x>b)=\nu(x<b)=1
. Then

(1)

\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
.

(2)

\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\mu
.

Proof.

Again, the proofs are similar and so we only prove
(1)
. Consider the definable function
f:\mathcal{U}^{x}\to\mathcal{U}^{y}
via
f(a)=-a
. Then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}f(\mu)
by Proposition
3.10
and
f(\mu)(x<b)=1
for every
b\in\mathcal{U}
. By Proposition
6.3
, we conclude that
f(\mu)\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
. By transitivity of extension domination,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
.
∎

Corollary 6.5
.

Suppose that for every
b\in\mathcal{U}
,
\mu(x>b)=\eta(x>b)=\nu(x<b)=1
and
r,s\in[0,1]
such that
r+s=1
. Then
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}r\nu+s\eta
.

Proof.

Let
p\in S_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
x>b\in p
for every
b\in\mathcal{U}
.
By Proposition
6.3
,
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\delta_{p}
and
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\eta
.
By Corollary
6.4
,
\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}\nu
.
Since
\operatorname{supp}(\eta)\cap\operatorname{supp}(\nu)=\emptyset
,
\operatorname{supp}(\eta)\subset[x>0]
and
\operatorname{supp}(\nu)\cap[x>0]=\emptyset
, by Corollary
5.10

\delta_{p}\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}r\nu+s\eta
. By transitivity, we conclude that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},\mathbb{Z}}r\nu+s\eta
.
∎

6.2.
Ordered equivalence relation

The following example is slightly more complicated. We construct two global measures
\mu
and
\nu
which are generically stable and dominate one another.

Definition 6.6
.

Suppose that
T
is NIP and
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
for some monster model
\mathcal{U}
. We say that
\mu
is
generically stable
if
\mu
is definable and finitely satisfiable over some small model
M
.

Lemma 6.7
.

Let
T
be an NIP theory in the language
\mathcal{L}
and let
T^{\prime}
be the reduct of
T
to the language
\mathcal{L}^{\prime}
such that
T^{\prime}
is distal. Let
\mathcal{U}\models T
,
M
is a small submodel of
\mathcal{U}
, and
\mu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\mu
is generically stable over
M
. Let
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
and
M^{\prime}
be the associated models of the reduct. Then
\mu^{\prime}=\mu|_{\mathcal{L}^{\prime}(\mathcal{U^{\prime}})}
is smooth over
M^{\prime}
. In particular, for any
\theta(x)\in\mathcal{L^{\prime}}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
and
\nu\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
, if
\nu|_{M}=\mu|_{M}
then
\nu(\theta(x))=\mu(\theta(x))
.

Proof.

We first claim that
\mu^{\prime}
is generically stable over
M^{\prime}
. It suffices to show that
\mu^{\prime}
is both definable and finitely satisfiable over
M^{\prime}
.

(1)

Claim 1:
\mu^{\prime}
is finitely satifiable over
M^{\prime}
follows directly by definition.

(2)

Claim 2:
\mu^{\prime}
is definable over
M^{\prime}
. Note that by claim 1,
\mu^{\prime}
is invariant over
M^{\prime}
. Hence for any formula
\varphi(x,z)\in\mathcal{L^{\prime}}_{xz}(\emptyset)
, the map
F_{\mu^{\prime},M^{\prime}}^{\varphi^{\prime}}:S_{z}(M^{\prime})\to[0,1]
is well-defined. Let
s:S_{z}(M)\to S_{z}(M^{\prime})
be the obvious restriction map. This map is continuous. By
[
3
, Proposition 2.9]
, for any closed
C\subseteq[0,1]
,
(F_{\mu,M}^{\varphi})^{-1}(C)
is
\varphi^{*}
-type definable. Hence for any
q_{1},q_{2}\in S_{z}(M)
and
b_{1}\models q_{1}
,
b_{2}\models q_{2}
, if
\operatorname{tp}_{\varphi^{*}}(b_{1}/M)=\operatorname{tp}_{\varphi^{*}}(b_{2}/M)
, then
\mu(\varphi(x,b_{1}))=\mu(\varphi(x,b_{2}))
. From this observation, one can check that
s((F^{\varphi}_{\mu,M})^{-1}(C))=(F^{\varphi}_{\mu^{\prime},M^{\prime}})^{-1}(C)
. Notice that
s((F^{\varphi}_{\mu,M})^{-1}(C))
is closed since it is the image of a compact set (which is closed). Therefore,
(F_{\mu^{\prime},M^{\prime}}^{\varphi})^{-1}(C)
is closed and hence
F_{\mu^{\prime},M^{\prime}}^{\varphi}
is continuous.

Hence
\mu^{\prime}
is generically stable over
M^{\prime}
. Since
T^{\prime}
is distal, all generically stable measures are smooth
[
17
, Theorem 1.1]
and so
\mu^{\prime}
is smooth over
M^{\prime}

[
18
, Lemma 7.17]
. The
in particular
portion follows immediately since
\nu^{\prime}|_{M^{\prime}}=\mu^{\prime}|_{M^{\prime}}
and
\mu^{\prime}
is smooth over
M^{\prime}
.
∎

Example 6.8
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{<,E\}
be the language of an
ordered equivalence relation
. Then finite linear orders with an equivalence relation form a Fraïssé class and we let
T_{OE}
be the theory of the Fraïssé limit. Let
\mathcal{U}
be a monster model of
T_{OE}
and
M
a small elementary submodel. Notice the following:

(1)

T_{OE}
admits quantifier elimination.

(2)

T_{OE}
restricted to the language
\{<\}
is the theory of a dense linear order without endpoints.

(3)

T_{OE}
restricted to the language
\{E\}
is the theory of infinitely many equivalence classes all with infinitely many elements.

(4)

For any
a\in\mathcal{U}
, the
E(x,a)
is a dense-codense subset of
\mathcal{U}
.

(5)

T_{OE}
is NIP.

Let
p
be the unique type in
S_{x}(\mathcal{U})
extending the collection of formulas
\{\neg E(a,x):a\in\mathcal{U}\}\cup\{x>a:a\in\mathcal{U}\}
. Note that
p
is definable over any model by quantifier elimination and so
p
is definable over
M
. Let
I=(a_{i})_{i\in[0,\infty)}
be a Morley sequence in
p
over
M
. By construction, we notice that:

(1)

\mathcal{U}\models a_{i}<a_{j}
if and only if
i<j
.

(2)

\mathcal{U}\models E(a_{i},a_{j})
if and only if
i=j
.

We now construct generically stable (non-smooth) measures
\mu
and
\nu
in
\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\nu
and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E}}\mu
. Let
L
denote the usual Lebesgue measure on
\mathbb{R}
.

(1)

For any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
, we define
\mu(\varphi(x))=L(\{i\in[0,1]:\mathcal{U}\models\varphi(a_{i})\})
.

(2)

For any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
, we define
\nu(\varphi(x))=L(\{i\in[2,3]:\mathcal{U}\models\varphi(a_{i})\})
.

Since
T_{OE}
is NIP and our sequence
(a_{i})_{i\in[0,\infty)}
is indiscernible,
\mu
and
\nu
are well-defined and generically stable (see
[
18
, Example 7.32]
). More explicitly, if we fix a small model
N
such that
M\cup\{a_{i}:i\in[0,\infty)\}\subseteq N
and
M\prec N\prec\mathcal{U}
, then both
\mu
and
\nu
are generically stable over
N
. We now argue that
\mu
and
\nu
are not smooth over any subset of
\mathcal{U}
. Let
\mathcal{U}\prec\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
and consider
b\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
such that
b\models p
.

(1)

Since
\mu
is generically stable,
\mu
admits a definable extension to
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
, namely
\mu^{\prime}
. The measure
\mu^{\prime}
is still definable over
N
and hence
\mu(E(x,b))=0
.

(2)

Let
\varphi(x)=E(x,b)
. For any
\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\models\varphi(x)\to\psi(x)
, it follows that there exists a finite (possibly empty) set
A
such that
A\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and
\psi(x)\equiv\bigwedge_{a\in A}x\neq a
. Hence, if follows that
\inf\{\mu(\psi(x)):\psi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})\,\text{ and }\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\models\varphi(x)\to\psi(x)\}=1
.

By construction,
\mu(\varphi(x))=1
. Likewise for any
\varphi(x)\in\mathcal{L}_{x}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\models\psi(x)\to\varphi(x)
, we have that
\varphi(x)\equiv x\neq x
and so
\mu(\varphi(x))=0
. Therefore, for any
r\in[0,1]
, there exists a measure
\mu_{r}\in\mathfrak{M}_{x}(\mathcal{U}^{\prime})
such that
\mu_{r}|_{\mathcal{U}}=\mu
and
\mu_{r}(E(x,b))=r
by Fact
2.7
.

We claim that
\mu\geq_{\mathbb{E},N}\nu
(and
\nu\geq_{\mathbb{E},N}\mu
). Choose
b\in\mathcal{U}\backslash N
. Then there exists unique global measures
\mu_{b}
and
\nu_{b}
such that

(1)

\mu_{b}
and
\nu_{b}
are smooth over
Nb
.

(2)

\mu_{b}|_{\mathcal{L}_{<}}=\mu|_{\mathcal{L}_{<}}
and
\mu(E(x,b))=1
.

(3)

\nu_{b}|_{\mathcal{L}_{<}}=\nu|_{\mathcal{L}_{<}}
and
\nu(E(x,b))=1
.

(4)

\mu_{b}|_{N}=\mu|_{N}
and
\nu_{b}|_{N}=\nu|_{N}

The measures
\mu_{b}
and
\nu_{b}
are
smooth extensions
of
\mu|_{N}
and
\nu|_{N}
respectively. Since
\mu_{b}
and
\nu_{b}
are smooth, there exists a unique measure
\eta\in\mathfrak{M}_{xy}(\mathcal{U})
such that
\eta
is a separated amalgam of
\mu_{b}
and
\nu_{b}
by
[
10
, Corollary 2.5]
. Moreover,
\eta=\mu_{b}(x)\otimes\nu_{b}(y)
(by uniqueness). We let
\lambda:=\eta|_{N}
. We show that
\lambda(E(x,y))=1
. Notice that

\lambda(E(x,y))=\mu_{b}\otimes\nu_{b}(E(x,y))=\int_{S_{x}(Nb)}F_{\mu_{b}}^{E}d(\nu_{b}|_{N_{b}})

Now, let

We now show that

6.1

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}:=\Bigg\{

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}I_{j}^{\varepsilon_{1}(j)}(y)\wedge\bigwedge_{i=1}^{m}E^{\varepsilon_{2}(i)}(y,c_{i}):n,m\in\mathbb{N},c_{i}\in\mathcal{U},

.
