Sustained Oscillations in Hyperbolic-Parabolic Systems
Athanasios E. Tzavaras

Abstract
. We construct examples of oscillating solutions with persistent oscillations for various hyperbolic-parabolic systems with singular diffusion matrices that appear in mechanics. These include, an example for the equations of nonlinear viscoelasticity of Kelvin-Voigt type with stored energy that violates rank-one convexity, which amounts to a time-dependent variant of twinning solutions. An example pertaining to the system of gas dynamics with thermal effects for a viscous, adiabatic gas. Finally, an example for the compressible Navier-Stokes system in one-space dimension with nonmonotone pressure function. We also study the existence of oscillating solutions for linear hyperbolic-parabolic systems with singular diffusion matrices.



1.
Introduction

The effectiveness of dissipation on smoothing of discontinuities was proposed by Dafermos
[
2
]
as
a yardstick for classifying the hyperbolic-parabolic systems of thermomechanics.
The properties of the Green function for the linearized operator around a constant state capture the efficacy of dissipation, as exemplified
in the penetrating study of Liu and Zeng
[
12
]
.
The picture is less understood regarding propagation of oscillations. It is well-known that systems of hyperbolic conservation laws can propagate
oscillations from the initial-data to solutions, and propagation and/or cancellations of oscillations and its relation to entropy inequalities
has been an extensively studied subject. By contrast, fully-parabolic systems instantaneously smoothen initial oscillations.
Less is known for the intermediate case of hyperbolic-parabolic systems which is the objective of the present work.

This question was brought to focus by an example, exhibiting sustained oscillations induced by the initial data,
[
10
, sec 7]
,
for the hyperbolic-parabolic system

(1.1)

\displaystyle u_{t}

\displaystyle=v_{x}

\displaystyle v_{t}

\displaystyle=\sigma(u)_{x}+v_{xx}\,,

with
\sigma(u)
non-monotone. The example was produced to
highlight the gap among two available existence theories for motions
y:(0,T)\times\mathbb{T}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}

on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
d=2,3
, describing the Cauchy problem of nonlinear viscoelastic materials

(1.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}y-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\nabla y)\Big)=\Delta\partial_{t}y\,,

\displaystyle y|_{t=0}=y_{0},\quad\partial_{t}y|_{t=0}=v_{0}\,.

Introducing the velocity
v=\partial_{t}y
and the deformation gradient
F=\nabla y
, the second order evolution (
1.2
)
is expressed as a hyperbolic-parabolic system

(1.3)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(S(F))

\displaystyle=\Delta\,v

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F-\nabla\,v

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{curl}}\,F

\displaystyle=0\,.

There are two frameworks for global existence of weak solutions for (
1.3
):
(a) one with spatial regularity
v\in L^{2}
,
F\in L^{p}
in
[
6
,
4
]
; and (b) a second with spatial regularity

v\in L^{2}
,
F\in H^{1}
in
[
10
]
. A key and perhaps unexpected difference among the two
is the possibility of sustained oscillations in the framework
v\in L^{2}
,
F\in L^{p}
demonstrated by the example in
[
10
, sec 7]
.

A second motivation stems from the existence theory of the compressible Navier-Stokes system

(1.4)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho u+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\rho u\otimes u+\nabla p(\rho)

\displaystyle=\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\mu(\nabla u+\nabla u^{T})+\lambda(\mathop{\mathrm{div}}u)\,I\,\Big)

where
\rho
and
u
are the density and velocity of the fluid while
\mu
the shear viscosity and
\lambda
the second viscosity
(or Lame constants) satisfy
\mu>0
,
\lambda+\mu>0
.
The existence theory of weak solutions of Lions
[
11
]
and Feireisl
[
5
]
makes essential use of the propagation
of compactness property from the initial data. Lions conjectured that the compressible Navier-Stokes system may exhibit propagation of
oscillations and provided an example for a variant of (
1.4
) that includes a forcing term, see
[
11
, Rmk 5.8]
.
We will provide an example of propagation of oscillations for the (unforced) compressible Navier-Stokes system in one space dimension.
The question of deriving the effective equations of the associated homogenization problem is studied in
[
13
,
7
]
.

Our objective is to explore persistent oscillations in hyperbolic-parabolic systems. The oscillations
are related to the fact that systems of mechanics are second order evolutions for the motion
and, as the reader will observe, this plays a fundamental role in the examples that we provide. We explore the existence
of sustained oscillations first for linear systems and then for nonlinear systems.
We start in section
2
with linear hyperbolic-parabolic systems,

(1.5)

\partial_{t}U+A\partial_{x}U=B\partial_{xx}U\,,

in one or several space dimensions, where
B
is singular. We focus on systems originating in mechanics and explore a special structure of oscillations
natural to such problems, where oscillations of the deformation gradient balance with oscillations of the strain-rate.
Examples include the equations of linear thermoviscoelasticity in one dimension (
2.1
) or several space dimensions (
2.27
).

In section
3
, we consider the system (
1.3
) of nonlinear viscoelasticity with a stored energy violating rank-1 convexity;
such models are employed in the theory of phase transitions.
We provide a novel class of dynamic oscillating solutions where the deformation gradient oscillates between two time dependent homogeneous
states and exhibits jumps across steady interfaces. Such solutions propagate oscillations of initial data to solutions and lead to Young measures
that are convex combinations of Dirac masses. In section
3.2
we study the system of one-dimensional viscoelasticity
(
3.13
) (or one-dimensional gas dynamics for viscous gases) where the calculations can be made explicit. Similar behavior
appears in a class of one-dimensional models in viscoplasticity studied in section
3.3
.

The existence of oscillatory solutions is extended in section
4
to the system describing gas dynamics with thermal effects
for viscous but adiabatic gases,

(1.6)

\displaystyle u_{t}-v_{x}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle v_{t}-\sigma(u,\theta)_{x}

\displaystyle=(\frac{\mu}{u}v_{x})_{x}

\displaystyle\big(\tfrac{1}{2}v^{2}+e(u,\theta)\big)_{t}-\Big(\sigma(u,\theta)\,v\Big)_{x}

\displaystyle=\Big(\frac{\mu}{u}\,v\,v_{x}\Big)_{x}\,.

For a special constitutive class (
CF
) and a carefully selected nonmonotone pressure weak solutions are constructed
that exhibit persistent oscillations. The construction combines a class of universal solutions describing uniform extension (or compression) solutions alternating
via jumps across steady interfaces that satisfy the Rankine-Hugoniot jump condition.

Finally, in section
5
, we consider the one-dimensional compressible Navier-Stokes system

(1.7)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}+(\rho u)_{y}

\displaystyle=0

\displaystyle(\rho u_{t}+(\rho u^{2}+p(\rho))_{y}

\displaystyle=\mu u_{yy}\,,

By exploring the transformation between Lagrangian and Eulerian descriptions, we map the Lagrangian variable solutions obtained in section
3.2

to the Eulerian domain. This leads to a special solution of the compressible Navier-Stokes system (
1.7
),

(1.8)

\displaystyle u(t,y)=\frac{y}{t}\,,\qquad\rho(t,y)

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\;\frac{1}{ta}\quad&\quad kv_{0}(\theta)<\frac{y}{t}<kv_{0}(\theta)+a\theta\\[5.0pt]
\;\frac{1}{tb}\quad&\;\;kv_{0}(\theta)+a\theta<\frac{y}{t}<(k+1)v_{0}(\theta)\end{cases}\quad k\in\mathbb{Z}\,,

where
v_{0}(\theta)=\theta a+(1-\theta)b
,
\theta\in(0,1)
, with persistent oscillations.
The function
(\rho,u)
solves (
1.7
) provided the nonmonotone pressure satisfies

p(\frac{1}{ta})=p(\frac{1}{tb})
for
t\in[1.2]
. The solution suggests propagation of oscillations from the initial data at
t=1
to the domain
[1,2]\times\mathbb{R}
.

2.
Oscillatory Solutions in Linear Hyperbolic-Parabolic Systems

In this section we study linear systems that combine hyperbolic and parabolic characters first in one-space
dimension and then in several space dimensions. The goal is to build a class of solutions for which initial
oscillations propagate into solutions despite the smoothing effect of parabolic characters, exploiting
degeneracies of the parabolic characters. The form of solutions is motivated by examples that arise
in the theory of thermoviscoelasticity and relate to the mechanical interpretation of such models.

In the first two subsections we study the one-dimensional case, and then we extend the results to the
linear systems in several space dimensions.

2.1.
Oscillations in a 1-d linear thermoviscoelasticity

Consider the system

(2.1)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial t^{2}}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}+m\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x^{2}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\kappa\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}+m\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}

where

2.1

\displaystyle\frac{\partial w}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\;\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial v}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=\lambda\frac{\partial w}{\partial x}+m\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}v}{\partial x^{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial t}

\displaystyle=m\frac{\partial v}{\partial x}+\kappa\frac{\partial^{2}\theta}{\partial x^{2}}

The form (

(2.3)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\theta|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\big(\lambda\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+m\theta+\mu\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}\big)\right)

\displaystyle+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\kappa\theta\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\right)-\kappa\Big|\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}-\mu\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}\Big|^{2}

Under appropriate boundary conditions (either periodic or no mechanical work and adiabatic at the boundaries) we obtain the energy
dissipation identity

(2.4)

\frac{d}{dt}\int\Big(\tfrac{1}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{\lambda}{2}\Big|\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\tfrac{1}{2}|\theta|^{2}\Big)\,dx+\int\kappa\Big|\frac{\partial\theta}{\partial x}\Big|^{2}+\mu\Big|\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x\partial t}\Big|^{2}=0\,.

Next, consider the system (

\displaystyle u(t,x)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}a(t)e^{inx}

\displaystyle=-\kappa n^{2}b-m\dot{a}

In summary, given

The latter is an equation for the Fourier modes (

2.2.
An illustrating example

The special case of linear viscoelasticity (in one and several space dimensions) presents an example
that can be worked out explicitly. For the equation

(2.21)

where

.
(For the multi-dimensional case, see section
2.4
).
The amplitude
\alpha(t)
satisfies

(2.22)

\frac{d^{2}\alpha}{dt^{2}}+\mu n^{2}\frac{d\alpha}{dt}+n^{2}\lambda\alpha=0\,.

The associated characteristic polynomial
\rho^{2}+\mu n^{2}+\lambda n^{2}=0

\rho_{\pm}=\frac{\mu n^{2}}{2}\left(-1\pm\sqrt{1-\frac{4\lambda}{\mu^{2}n^{2}}}\right)

\displaystyle=-\mu n^{2}+\frac{\lambda}{\mu}+\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{3}n^{2}}+O\big(\tfrac{1}{n^{4}}\big)

\displaystyle=-\frac{\lambda}{\mu}-\frac{\lambda^{2}}{\mu^{3}n^{2}}+O\big(\tfrac{1}{n^{4}}\big)

for (

Of interest here is the solution associated to

2.3.
Oscillations in linear multi-dimensional systems

Consider next the multi-dimensional system

3.
Oscillations in quasi-linear dynamical models for phase transitions

We record some examples of solutions exhibiting sustained oscillations for various
quasi-linear hyperbolic-parabolic systems. The first class of models concerns viscoelastic systems of
the type used in modeling of phase transitions.

3.1.
Time-dependent twinning solutions in nonlinear viscoelasticity

In preparation, we mention a well known result from continuum mechanics. Let
\psi(x)
be a displacement field defined on
a reference configuration
\mathcal{R}
and suppose that
\mathcal{R}
is split into two subdomains,
\mathcal{R}=\mathcal{R}^{+}\cup\mathcal{R}^{-}
, by a smooth
nonsingular hypersurface
\mathcal{S}
defined by

f(x)=0\quad\mbox{with $\nabla f\neq 0$}.

Suppose that
\psi
enjoys the regularity
\psi
is continuous on
\mathcal{R}
,
\psi\in C^{1}\big(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{-}}\big)
,
\psi\in C^{1}\big(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{+}}\big)
,
and the limits

\lim_{\scriptsize\begin{matrix}x\to x_{0}\\
x>x_{0}\end{matrix}}\nabla\psi(x)=F^{+}\,,\quad\lim_{\scriptsize\begin{matrix}x\to x_{0}\\
x<x_{0}\end{matrix}}\nabla\psi(x)=F^{-}\,,

exist for
x_{0}\in\mathcal{S}
and are finite. Then the deformation gradients satisfy across
\mathcal{S}
the jump conditions

F^{+}-F^{-}=a\otimes N

where
N
is the normal to the surface and
a
is an amplitude capturing the jump of the gradient in the normal direction at
\mathcal{S}
. This result is the backbone
in the construction of twinning solutions in elasticity,
[
9
,
1
]
.

Consider now the system of viscoelasticity of Kelvin-Voigt type,

(3.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{tt}y-\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(\nabla y)+\nabla y_{t}\Big)=0\,.

It is written as a system of conservation laws expressed in coordinate form, for the quantities

v_{i}=\frac{\partial y_{i}}{\partial t}
,
F_{i\alpha}=\frac{\partial y_{i}}{\partial x_{\alpha}}
, by

(3.2)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}F_{i\alpha}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}

\displaystyle\partial_{t}v_{i}

\displaystyle=\partial_{\alpha}\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}}(F)+\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}\Big)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\partial_{\beta}F_{i\alpha}-\partial_{\alpha}F_{i\beta}\,.

The last equation constrains
F
to be a gradient. It is an involution, i.e. a constraint that propagates
from the initial data to solutions.

Our purpose is to construct oscillating solutions. This will result from two ingredients, a special class of solutions
combined with conditions that guarantee joining these solutions across interfaces. We start with the interface conditions.

Suppose that
\mathcal{S}=\{(t,x):f(x,t)=0\}
is a space-time hypersurface that splits a space time domain
Q_{T}=\mathcal{R}\times(0,T)
into two parts,

Q_{T}=\mathcal{R}^{+}\cup\mathcal{R}^{-}
. We assume that
\mathcal{S}
is smooth and depicts the motion of an interface
x=x(t)
which is moving in the direction of the normal

\nu=\frac{\nabla_{x}f}{|\nabla_{x}f|}
with speed
\dot{x}=s\nu
. Since the interface
x(t)
moves on the surface, we have
f(x(t),t)=0
, which easily
yields for the interfacial speed that
s=-\frac{f_{t}}{|\nabla_{x}f|}
. Suppose a solution
(v,F)
of (
3.1
)
that has smoothness
v\in C(Q_{T})
, and such that
F,\nabla v\in C\big(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{-}}\big)
,
F,\nabla v\in C\big(\overline{\mathcal{R}^{+}}\big)

and the right and left limits of
F,\nabla v
exist and are finite on both sides of the space-time surface
\mathcal{S}
. The limits will satisfy the Rankine-Hugoniot conditions

(3.3)

\displaystyle-s[F_{i\alpha}]

\displaystyle=\nu_{\alpha}[v_{i}]

(3.4)

\displaystyle-s[v_{i}]

\displaystyle=\nu_{\alpha}\left[\frac{\partial W}{\partial F_{i\alpha}}(F)+\partial_{\alpha}v_{i}\right]

(3.5)

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\nu_{\beta}[F_{i\alpha}]-\nu_{\alpha}[F_{i\beta}]

where
[\cdot]
denotes the jump across the interface.
Condition (

[F]\tau=0
for any vector

and thus
[F]=a\otimes\nu
. Together with (
3.3

We are here interested in
[F]\neq 0
and
[v]=0

The latter precludes

This suggests to consider standing shocks
s=0

In summary,

[F]=a\otimes\nu\,,\quad\big[T\nu\big]=\Big[\Big(\frac{\partial W}{\partial F}(F)+\nabla v\Big)\nu\Big]=0

is the total stress. Observe that (

The second ingredient is special solutions of (
