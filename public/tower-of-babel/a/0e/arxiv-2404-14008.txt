Carleman estimates for higher order partial differential operators and applications Thanks: This work is partially supported by the NSF of China under grants 11971333, 11931011, and by the Science Development Project of Sichuan University under grant 2020SCUNL201.
Xiaoyu Fu, Yuan Gao

Abstract
In this paper, we obtain a Carleman estimate for the higher order partial differential operator 𝒫 = △ α ∂ t + ∂ x n {\cal P}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\alpha\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{n} (with OPEN α ∈ ℝ , n ∈ ℤ ≥ 2 ) \alpha\in\mathbb{R},n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}) . In the process of establishing this estimate, we developed a new method, which is called the “back-propagation method” (the BPM, for short). This method can also be used to build up Carleman estimates for some other partial differential operators, and might provide assistance with corresponding numerical analyses. As an application of the above-mentioned Carleman estimate, we proved the conditional stability of a Cauchy problem for a time fractional diffusion equation with 1 3 \frac{1}{3} -order.

1
Introduction and main result

In 1939, Carleman
[
1
]
showed that a second order elliptic operator in dimensional two enjoys the unique continuation property. The technique used there is called “Carleman weight inequality” and has become one of the major tools in the study of the unique continuation property, control problems and inverse problems for partial differential equations.

The Carleman estimate can be regarded as a weighted energy inequality, which can be described as follows:

Let
\Omega
be a
connected open set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
P=P(x,D)
be a differential operator of order
n
in
\Omega
. Assume that there is a suitable function

\phi(\cdot)\in C^{\infty}({\overline{\Omega}};\mathbb{R})
satisfying
\displaystyle\nabla\phi(x)\neq 0
,
x\in\Omega
. Let
\theta=e^{\lambda\phi}
. We say that the Carleman type estimate holds for
P
if there exists a constant
C>0
such that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{0\leq m<n}\lambda^{2(n-m)-1}\int_{\Omega}\theta^{2}|D^{m}v|^{2}dx\leq C\int_{\Omega}|\theta P(x,D)v|^{2}dx,\end{array}

where
v\in C_{0}^{\infty}(\Omega)
and
\lambda>0
is a parameter.

Up to now, there are numerous results on Carleman estimates for the second order partial differential operators,
the corresponding applications are well understood (for example, see
[
3
]
and the references cited therein).
For Carleman estimates of higher order partial differential operators, we refer to
[
2
,
4
,
5
,
13
,
14
,
15
]
for the fourth order parabolic-type operators and fourth order Schrödinger operators,
[
11
]
for the sixth-order parabolic operators. Based on an identity of Treves (see
[
9
, Lemma 17.2.2]
),
[
6
,
7
]
obtained unique continuation properties for higher Order parabolic equations and Schrödinger Equations. Recently
[
10
]
established a Carleman estimate for high order equations of Korteweg-de Vries type with the weight function
\theta=e^{\lambda x}
in one dimensional case. Compared to Carleman estimates for second order partial differential operators, the computation of Carleman estimates for higher order partial differential operators is much more complicated.

In this paper, we aim at establishing a Carleman estimate for the operator

{\cal P}=\alpha\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{n},\ \alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\ n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2},

with an exponential-type weight function
\theta(t,x)
(which will be given later). More importantly, using
{\cal P}
as a carrier, we will introduce the “backpropagation method” (the BPM, for short) in the building of the Carleman estimate for
{\cal P}
.

Next, we will explain what role the BPM will play in the process of establishing the Carleman
estimate for
{\cal P}
. As we know, elementary calculus can be enough to grasp the main idea of Carleman estimate (See
[
3
, Chapter 1]
). To obtain the core, there is an important ingredient which should be emphasized, i.e.,
the principal part of our operator
\theta({\cal P}\theta^{-1})
. Noting that the principal part of our operator contains the
n
-th derivative with respect to the
x
variable,
hence the decomposition and the computation will cause many difficulties. For the reader’s convenience, we simply choose the following weight functions:

\psi(t,x)=(x-x_{0})^{2}-\beta(t-t_{0})^{2},\quad\ell(t,x)=\lambda\psi(t,x),\quad\theta(t,x)=e^{\ell(t,x)}

(1.1)

where
x_{0},t_{0}\in\mathbb{R}
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

For
v(t,x)\in C^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R};\mathbb{R})
, set

w=\theta v=e^{\ell}v
with
\ell
given by (
1.1
). Then

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\theta{\cal P}v&\displaystyle=\alpha\theta\partial_{t}v+\theta\partial^{n}_{x}v\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=\alpha w_{t}-\alpha\ell_{t}w+I_{1}(w)+I_{2}(w)+I_{3}(w).\end{array}

(1.2)

Here
I_{j}(w),j=1,2,3
are chosen in the following manner:

\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v=I_{1}(w)+I_{2}(w)+I_{3}(w).

Note that the order of
\lambda
equals to the sum of orders of
\partial_{x}^{j}\ell
,
j\geq 1
. Then we decompose
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v
as following:

•

The item
{I_{1}(w)}
is the sum of all such terms that are the highest order terms of
\lambda
with an odd
order derivative of
x
, and the second highest orders terms of
\lambda
with an even order derivative of
x
;

•

The item
{I_{2}(w)}
is the sum of all such terms that are the highest order terms of
\lambda
with an even
order derivative of
x
, and the second highest orders terms of
\lambda
with an odd order derivative of
x
;

•

The item
{I_{3}(w)}
is the sum of all other terms in
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v
.

It deserves mentioning that
{I_{3}(w)}
consists of only lower order terms, compared with
{I_{1}(w)}
and
{I_{2}(w)}
.

By (
1.2
), we have

\theta{\cal P}vI_{2}=\alpha w_{t}I_{2}+I_{1}I_{2}+|I_{2}|^{2}+I_{2}(I_{3}-\alpha\ell_{t}w).

(1.3)

From (
1.3
), it is easy to see that:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\theta^{2}|{\cal P}v|^{2}\geq 2I_{1}I_{2}+2\alpha w_{t}I_{2}-|{I_{3}(w)}-\alpha\ell_{t}w|^{2}.\end{array}

(1.4)

Comparing
{I_{3}(w)}-\alpha\ell_{t}w
with
I_{1},I_{2}
, we know
|{I_{3}(w)}-\alpha\ell_{t}w|^{2}
contains only lower order term.

Our keys to establishing the Carleman estimate for
{\cal P}
are as follows:

•

The decomposition of principal operator
\theta\partial^{n}_{x}v
(see Proposition
2.2
).

•

The choice of
I_{j}(w)(j=1,2,3)
(see Proposition
2.3
).

•

The estimates of
I_{1}(w)I_{2}(w)
and
w_{t}I_{2}(w)
(see Propositions
3.1
and
4.1
).

The BPM developed in this paper is used to solve the third key above. More precisely, it can help
us to simply prove that the highest order energy terms in
I_{1}(w)I_{2}(w)
have the positive sign, while the
“bad” terms yielded from
w_{t}I_{2}(w)
, such as
w_{t}w_{x}
and
w_{tx}w_{xx}
, are indeed lower order terms.
Our BPM is inspired by “back-propagation”, which is widely used in the field of machine learning, dating back to
[
12
]
published in 1986. In machine learning, it is difficult to calculate derivatives forward, so people adopt a backward method, which was proposed in the 1970s, to solve differential problems of nested functions. Now people working on machine learning will draw a network graph to calculate a derivative backward, which can be viewed as the embryo of our BPM.

Throughout of this paper, we use
C_{n}^{k}
to denote combinatorial numbers,
v_{t}
or
\partial_{t}v
to represent the derivative of
v
in the time variable, and
v_{x}
or
\partial_{x}v
to denote the derivative of
v
in the space variable. In what follows, we will use
C
to denote a generic positive constant which may vary from line to line. For
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
, we denote by
O(\lambda^{k})
a function of order
\lambda^{k}
for large
\lambda
. We use “
(\cdot)
” or “etc.” to denote such terms whose concrete forms does not have to be given.

The main result of this paper is as follows:

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
T>0
,
L>0
,
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}\backslash\{0\},\ n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 2}
. Let
\psi=(x-x_{0})^{2}-\beta(t-t_{0})^{2}
with
x_{0}>L
,
0<t_{0}<T
,
\beta\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. For any
v\in C_{0}^{\infty}([0,T]\times[0,L];\mathbb{R})
, and
w=e^{\lambda\psi}v
, the following inequality holds:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum^{n-1}_{m=0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\Big[n^{2}C_{n-1}^{m}\lambda^{2n-2m-1}\psi_{x}^{2n-2m-2}\psi_{xx}+O(\lambda^{2n-2m-3})\Big]|\partial_{x}^{m}w|^{2}dxdt\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}e^{2\lambda\psi}\big|\alpha\partial_{t}v+\theta\partial^{n}_{x}v\big|^{2}dxdt.\end{array}

(1.5)

Remark 1.1
.

The main innovation of this paper is the BPM. It is not only applicable to different
partial differential operators, but also to different forms of the weight function
\varphi
. In addition, it can be
used for the estimation of boundary terms. These will be given in our forthcoming work.

Remark 1.2
.

The inequality (
1.5
), with
\alpha=-1
and
n=4
, was built up in
[
13
]
by a different
way from ours. Based on it, one can obtain the conditional stability in a Cauchy problem for a half-order
fractional diffusion equation.

Remark 1.3
.

The reason why we do not convert the function
w
on the left side of (
1.5
) into
v
is as follows: This way not only gives us the explicit coefficients of all energy terms, but also simplifies
the computation. In fact, this transformation can be easily implemented in the following way: Since
w=e^{\lambda\psi}v
, we can find a constant
C_{1}>0
such that

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum^{n-1}_{m=0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\lambda^{2n-2m-1}e^{2\lambda\psi}|\partial_{x}^{m}v|^{2}dxdt\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\sum^{n-1}_{m=0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\lambda^{2n-2m-1}e^{2\lambda\psi}|\partial_{x}^{m}(e^{-\lambda\psi}w)|^{2}dxdt\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\leq C_{1}\sum^{n-1}_{m=0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\lambda^{2n-2m-1}|\partial_{x}^{m}w|^{2}dxdt.\end{array}

(1.6)

Then, the combination of (
1.5
) and (
1.6
) yields that there exists a
\lambda_{0}>0
, such that for any
\lambda>\lambda_{0}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum^{n-1}_{m=0}\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}\lambda^{2n-2m-1}e^{2\lambda\psi}|\partial_{x}^{m}v|^{2}dxdt\leq C\int_{0}^{T}\int_{0}^{L}e^{2\lambda\psi}|{\cal P}v|^{2}dxdt\end{array}

(1.7)

for some
C>0
.

I_{1}I_{2}
and
w_{t}I_{2}
are given in sections 3 and 4, respectively. In section 5, we give our proof of Theorem
1.1
. As its application, we consider an inverse problem for a time-fractional diffusion equation in section 6.

This section presents preliminaries. We start with introducing notation.

For

, denote

\sum_{j={m_{1}}}^{m_{2}}a_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\sum_{j\in[m_{1},m_{2}]\cap\mathbb{Z}}a_{j},\qquad\prod_{j={m_{1}}}^{m_{2}}a_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\prod_{j\in[m_{1},m_{m_{2}}]\cap\mathbb{Z}}a_{j},

with the convention that
\displaystyle\sum_{j\in\varnothing}a_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}0
and
\displaystyle\prod_{j\in\varnothing}a_{j}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}1
.

For
j,k\in\mathbb{Z}
, denote

C_{j}^{k}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\frac{j!}{k!(j-k)!},&\mbox{ if }0\leq k\leq j,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle 0,&\mbox{ else }.\end{array}\right.

2.1
An Identity about Combinatorial Numbers

Proposition 2.1
.

For
(n,m)\in\{\mathbb{Z}^{2}|n\geq 2,\ 0\leq m\leq n-1\}
, define

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\cal K}_{n,m}&\displaystyle\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}-C_{n}^{2}C_{n}^{m}C_{n-2}^{m}-C_{n}^{2}\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k}(C_{n}^{m+k}C_{n-2}^{m-k}+C_{n}^{m-k}C_{n-2}^{m+k})\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\quad+\frac{2n-2m-1}{2}\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}(1+2k)(C_{n}^{m+1+k}C_{n}^{m-k}).\end{array}

(2.1)

Then

{\cal K}_{n,m}=\frac{n^{2}}{2}C_{n-1}^{m}.

(2.2)

Remark 2.1
.

In (
2.1
), the form of
{\cal K}_{n,m}
is the highest order coefficients of energy terms involved in the estimation of
I_{1}I_{2}
. We set this Proposition because it is not an obvious result.

Proof of Proposition
2.1
.
For
(n,m)\in\{\mathbb{Z}^{2}|n\geq 2,\ 0\leq m\leq n-1\}
, we simply write

{\cal K}_{n,m}=C_{n}^{2}{\cal H}_{1}+\frac{2n-2m-1}{2}{\cal H}_{2},

(2.3)

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\cal H}_{1}=-C_{n}^{m}C_{n-2}^{m}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k}(C_{n}^{m+k}C_{n-2}^{m-k}+C_{n}^{m-k}C_{n-2}^{m+k}),\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle{\cal H}_{2}=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}(1+2k)(C_{n}^{m+1+k}C_{n}^{m-k}).\end{array}\right.

(2.4)

We first calculate
{\cal H}_{1}
. By replacing the index of summation, one can get:

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\cal H}_{1}&\displaystyle=-C_{n}^{m}C_{n-2}^{m}-\sum_{k=1}^{m}(-1)^{k}(C_{n}^{m+k}C_{n-2}^{m-k}+C_{n}^{m-k}C_{n-2}^{m+k})\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{q=m}^{m}(-1)^{q}C_{n}^{q}C_{n-2}^{2m-q}+(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{q=m+1}^{2m}(-1)^{q}C_{n}^{q}C_{n-2}^{2m-q}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\quad+(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{q=0}^{m-1}(-1)^{q}C_{n}^{q}C_{n-2}^{2m-q}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{q=0}^{2m}(-1)^{q}C_{n}^{q}C_{n-2}^{2m-q}.\end{array}

(2.5)

Meanwhile, we have the identity:

(1-x)^{n}(1+x)^{n-2}=(1-x^{2})^{n-2}(x^{2}-2x+1),\quad x\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace R}},\ n\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace N}}.

(2.6)

Comparing the coefficients of
x^{2m}
(with
m\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace N}}
) on the both sides of (
2.6
), we find

\displaystyle\sum_{q=0}^{2m}(-1)^{q}C_{n}^{q}C_{n-2}^{2m-q}=C_{n-2}^{m}(-1)^{m}+C_{n-2}^{m-1}(-1)^{m-1},\quad m\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace N}}.

(2.7)

Combining (
2.5
) and (
2.7
), we have

{\cal H}_{1}=C_{n-2}^{m-1}-C_{n-2}^{m}.

(2.8)

Next, let us calculate
{\cal H}_{2}
. By a similar way dealing with
{\cal H}_{1}
, we can obtain

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle{\cal H}_{2}&\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}(-1)^{k}(1+2k)(C_{n}^{m+1+k}C_{n}^{m-k})\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=\sum_{k=0}^{m}\Big[(-1)^{k}(m+1+k)+(-1)^{k+1}(m-k)\Big]C_{n}^{m+1+k}C_{n}^{m-k}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=\sum_{q=m+1}^{2m+1}(-1)^{q-m-1}qC_{n}^{q}C_{n}^{2m+1-q}+\sum_{q=0}^{m}(-1)^{m-q-1}qC_{n}^{2m+1-q}C_{n}^{q}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=(-1)^{m+1}\sum_{q=1}^{2m+1}(-1)^{q}qC_{n}^{q}C_{n}^{2m+1-q}.\end{array}

(2.9)

At the same time, we have the identity:

(1+x)^{n}\frac{d}{dx}(1-x)^{n}=-n(1+x)(1-x^{2})^{n-1},\quad x\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace R}},\ n\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace N}}.

(2.10)

Comparing the coefficient of
x^{2m}
(with
m\in{\mathop{\rm l\negthinspace N}}
) on the both sides of (
2.10
), we find:

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\quad\sum_{k=0}^{2m}C_{n}^{2m-k}(-1)^{k+1}(k+1)C_{n}^{k+1}=\sum_{q=1}^{2m+1}(-1)^{q}qC_{n}^{q}C_{n}^{2m+1-q}=-n(-1)^{m}C_{n-1}^{m}.\end{array}

(2.11)

Combining (
2.9
) and (
2.11
), we have

{\cal H}_{2}=nC_{n-1}^{m}.

(2.12)

Finally, it follows from (
2.3
), (
2.8
) and (
2.12
) that

\begin{array}[]{ll}{\cal K}_{n,m}&\displaystyle=C_{n}^{2}(C_{n-2}^{m-1}-C_{n-2}^{m})+\frac{n(2n-2m-1)}{2}C_{n-1}^{m}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=\frac{n^{2}}{2}C_{n-1}^{m}.\end{array}

(2.13)

This completes the proof of Proposition
2.1
.

2.2
Decomposition of
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v

In this subsection, we introduce a decomposition of
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v
, which will play an important role in the proof of our main result.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\theta
be given by (
1.1
). Set
w=\theta v
. Then

\displaystyle\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v=\sum_{r+2s+m=n}\Big[(-1)^{s}\frac{1}{s!}(\prod_{l=0}^{s-1}{C_{n-2l}^{2}})\ell_{xx}^{s}\Big]\Big[(-1)^{r}C_{r+m}^{m}\ell_{x}^{r}\partial_{x}^{m}v\Big],

(2.14)

where
r,s,m\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
.

Remark 2.2
.

For any fixed
n
, the decomposition of
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}w
can be obtained by virtue of an iteration. Indeed, it is clear that

\theta\partial_{x}^{0}v=w,\quad\theta\partial_{x}^{1}v=\theta\partial_{x}^{1}(\theta^{-1}w)=\partial_{x}w-\ell_{x}w.

Assuming
f=\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v
, noting that
\theta_{x}=\theta\ell_{x}
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\theta\partial_{x}^{n+1}v&\displaystyle=\theta\partial_{x}\Big(\frac{f}{\theta}\Big)=\theta\Big(\frac{f_{x}\theta-\theta_{x}f}{\theta^{2}}\Big)=\partial_{x}f-\ell_{x}f\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=\partial_{x}(\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v)-\ell_{x}(\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v).\end{array}

(2.15)

As we explained before, the order of
\lambda
equals to the sum of orders of
\partial_{x}^{j}\ell
,
j\geq 1
. In fact, we only care about terms with
s=0,1
in
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v
. The terms involved in (
2.14
) with
s\geq 2
only yield lower order terms. Truncating
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v
at
s=0,1
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v&\displaystyle=\sum_{r+m=n}(-1)^{r}C_{n}^{m}\ell_{x}^{r}\partial_{x}^{m}w-\sum_{r+m=n-2}(-1)^{r}C_{n}^{2}C_{n-2}^{m}\ell_{x}^{r}\ell_{xx}\partial_{x}^{m}w+\cdots.\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle=\sum_{m=0}^{n}(-1)^{n-m}C_{n}^{m}\ell_{x}^{n-m}\partial_{x}^{m}w\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\quad-C_{n}^{2}\ell_{xx}\sum_{m=0}^{n-2}(-1)^{n-2-m}C_{n-2}^{m}\ell_{x}^{n-2-m}\partial_{x}^{m}w+\cdots.\end{array}

(2.16)

Proposition 2.3
.

Under the assumption of Proposition
2.2
, we have

\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v=I_{1}(w)+I_{2}(w)+I_{3}(w),

(2.17)

where

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle I_{1}(w)=\sum_{odd\ j\in[0,n]}C_{n}^{j}(-1)^{n-j}\ell_{x}^{n-j}\partial_{x}^{j}w\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad-C_{n}^{2}\ell_{xx}\sum_{even\ k\in[0,n-2]}C_{n-2}^{k}(-1)^{n-2-k}\ell_{x}^{n-2-k}\partial_{x}^{k}w,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle I_{2}(w)=\sum_{even\ k\in[0,n]}C_{n}^{k}(-1)^{n-k}\ell_{x}^{n-k}\partial_{x}^{k}w\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\quad\quad\qquad-C_{n}^{2}\ell_{xx}\sum_{odd\ j\in[0,n-2]}C_{n-2}^{j}(-1)^{n-2-j}\ell_{x}^{n-2-j}\partial_{x}^{j}w,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle I_{3}(w)\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\theta\partial_{x}^{n}v-I_{1}(w)-I_{2}(w).\end{array}\right.

(2.18)

For concrete
n
, by Proposition
2.3
, we can obtain the decomposition of
\theta\partial_{x}^{n}w
immediately.

\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle I_{2}=\partial_{x}^{4}w+6\ell_{x}^{2}\partial_{x}^{2}w+\ell_{x}^{4}w+12\ell_{x}\ell_{xx}\partial_{x}w.\\
\end{array}\right.

(2.19)

Example 2.2
.

In the case that
n=5

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\partial_{t}^{2/3}\hat{u}(0,x)=\partial_{t}^{1/3}[\partial_{t}^{1/3}\hat{u}(t,x)]\big|_{t=0}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{1/3}\Big[\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)+f(t,x)-f(0,x)-\frac{3}{\Gamma(1/3)}t^{1/3}f_{1}(x)\Big]\Big|_{t=0}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{2}\Big[\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)+f(t,x)\Big]\Big|_{t=0}+\partial_{t}^{1/3}f(0,x)-f_{1}(x)\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{1/3})f(0,x)-f_{1}(x)=0.\end{array}

(6.8)

Next, by (
6.7
)–(
6.8
), we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\partial_{t}\hat{u}(t,x)=\partial_{t}^{1/3}[\partial_{t}^{2/3}\hat{u}(t,x)]\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\partial_{t}^{1/3}\Big[\partial_{x}^{4}u(t,x)+\partial_{x}^{2}f(t,x)+\partial_{t}^{1/3}f(t,x)-f_{1}(x)\Big]\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{4}[\partial_{x}^{2}u(t,x)+f(t,x)]+\partial_{t}^{1/3}(\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{1/3})f(t,x)\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\partial_{x}^{6}u(t,x)+(\partial_{x}^{4}+\partial_{t}^{1/3}\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{1/3}\partial_{t}^{1/3})f(t,x).\end{array}

(6.9)

Combining (
6.5
) and (
6.9
), we end up with

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\partial_{x}^{6}u&\displaystyle=\widetilde{f}(t,x),\end{array}

(6.10)

where

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\widetilde{f}(t,x)=&\displaystyle\frac{1}{\Gamma(1/3)}t^{-2/3}f(0,x)+\frac{3/2}{\Gamma(2/3)}t^{-1/3}f_{1}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle+(\partial_{x}^{4}+\partial_{t}^{1/3}\partial_{x}^{2}+\partial_{t}^{1/3}\partial_{t}^{1/3})f.\end{array}

(6.11)

By (
6.2
) and (
6.10
), we have

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\partial_{t}u-\partial_{x}^{6}u=\widetilde{f}(t,x),&\ (t,x)\in Q,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\partial_{x}^{j}u(t,x)|_{x=0}=h_{j}(t),\ j=0,1,...,5,&\ t\in(\delta_{0},T).\end{array}\right.

(6.12)

Step 2.

Let
\chi(t,x)\in C^{\infty}(Q;[0,1])
satisfy

\chi(t,x)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle 1,&\ (t,x)\in Q_{\varepsilon/2},\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle 0,&\ (t,x)\in Q\backslash Q_{\varepsilon/3}.\end{array}\right.

(6.13)

Then we set

\widetilde{u}=\chi\Big[u-\sum_{j=0}^{5}\frac{x^{j}}{j!}h_{j}(t)\Big]\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\chi u-\chi\widetilde{h}(t,x).

(6.14)

The boundary of
Q_{\varepsilon/4}
consists of two line segments: one is a subset of
\{(t,x)|\delta_{0}<t<T,\ x=0\}
, while another one is in
Q\backslash Q_{\varepsilon/3}
. One can easily check that
\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}(t,x)=\partial_{x}^{j}\widetilde{u}(t,x)=0,\ j=0,1,...,5
on
\partial Q_{\varepsilon/4}
, hence
\widetilde{u}\in H_{0}^{1,6}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})
. Moreover

\begin{array}[]{ll}\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle\partial_{t}\widetilde{u}+\partial_{x}^{6}\widetilde{u}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr\displaystyle=\chi\widetilde{f}+(\partial_{t}\chi)u+\sum_{j=0}^{5}C_{6}^{j}(\partial_{x}^{6-j}\chi)\partial_{x}^{j}u-(\partial_{t}\chi+\partial_{x}^{6}\chi)\widetilde{h}-\chi\partial_{t}\widetilde{h}.\\
\end{array}

(6.15)

Applying the Carleman estimate in Theorem
1.1
with
v=\widetilde{u}
,
w=e^{\lambda\psi}\widetilde{u}
, we conclude that there is a large
\lambda>\lambda_{0}>0
on
Q_{\varepsilon/4}
, such that

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}\partial_{x}^{j}\widetilde{u}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}\leq C||e^{\lambda\psi}(\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{6})\widetilde{u}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leq C||e^{\lambda\psi}\chi\widetilde{f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}+C||e^{\lambda\psi}(\partial_{t}\chi)u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\quad+C\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}(\partial_{x}^{6-j}\chi)\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}+C\sum_{j=0}^{5}||h_{j}||^{2}_{H^{1}(\delta_{0},T)}.\end{array}

(6.16)

By the definition of
\chi(t,x)
in (
6.14
), one knows that
\partial_{t}\chi,\partial_{x}\chi,...,\partial_{x}^{5}\chi
are supported on
\{x|\psi\leq\varepsilon/2\}
. Recall that
\displaystyle M=\sum_{j=0}^{5}||\partial_{x}^{j}u||_{L^{2}(Q)}
. Thus, we have

||e^{\lambda\psi}(\partial_{t}\chi)u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}+\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}(\partial_{x}^{6-j}\chi)\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}\leq Ce^{\varepsilon\lambda}M^{2}.

(6.17)

Therefore

\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}\partial_{x}^{j}\widetilde{u}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}\leq Ce^{C\lambda}(||\widetilde{f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}+\sum_{j=0}^{5}||h_{j}||^{2}_{H^{1}(\delta_{0},T)})+Ce^{\varepsilon\lambda}M^{2}.

(6.18)

Since
u=\widetilde{u}+\widetilde{h}
, (
6.18
) yields

\begin{array}[]{ll}&\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}\partial_{x}^{j}\widetilde{u}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}+C||e^{\lambda\psi}\widetilde{h}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leq C\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}\partial_{x}^{j}\widetilde{u}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon/4})}+Ce^{2\varepsilon\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{5}||h_{j}||^{2}_{L^{2}(\delta_{0},T)}\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leq Ce^{C\lambda}\Big[||\widetilde{f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}+\sum_{j=0}^{5}||h_{j}||^{2}_{H^{1}(\delta_{0},T)}\Big]+Ce^{\varepsilon\lambda}M^{2}.\end{array}

(6.19)

Meanwhile, it follows from (
6.14
) and (
6.4
) that

||\widetilde{f}||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q)}+\sum_{j=0}^{5}||h_{j}||^{2}_{H^{1}(\delta_{0},T)}\leq CF^{2}.

Since
\psi>\varepsilon
in
Q_{\varepsilon}
, the above, along with (
6.19
), implies

\sum_{j=0}^{5}||\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\leq e^{-2\varepsilon\lambda}\sum_{j=0}^{5}||e^{\lambda\psi}\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\leq Ce^{C\lambda}F^{2}+Ce^{-\varepsilon\lambda}M^{2},

(6.20)

for all
\lambda>\lambda_{1}
, where
\lambda_{1}
is a sufficiently large number. Replacing the constant
C
by
Ce^{C\lambda_{1}}
, setting
\widetilde{\lambda}\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}\lambda-\lambda_{1}>0
, we have

\begin{array}[]{ll}\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{5}||\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}&\displaystyle\leq Ce^{C\lambda_{1}}\Big[Ce^{C\widetilde{\lambda}}F^{2}+Ce^{-\varepsilon\lambda-C\lambda_{1}}M^{2}\Big]\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr&\displaystyle\leq Ce^{C\widetilde{\lambda}}F^{2}+Ce^{-\varepsilon\widetilde{\lambda}}M^{2}.\end{array}

(6.21)

Step 3.

In the case that
F<M
, we put
\displaystyle\widetilde{\lambda}=\frac{2}{\varepsilon+C}\ln{\frac{M}{F}}>0.

Then we have

\sum_{j=0}^{5}||\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\leq CM^{\frac{2C}{\varepsilon+C}}F^{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+C}},\mbox{ with }\tau\mathrel{\mathop{\kern 0.0pt=}\limits^{\triangle}}{\frac{2\varepsilon}{\varepsilon+C}}.

(6.22)

In the case that
F\geq M
, it follows from (
6.21
) that

\sum_{j=0}^{5}||\partial_{x}^{j}u||^{2}_{L^{2}(Q_{\varepsilon})}\leq C(e^{C\widetilde{\lambda}}+e^{-\varepsilon\widetilde{\lambda}})F^{2}\leq CF^{2}.

(6.23)

Combining (
6.22
) and (
6.23
), we get the desired result.
