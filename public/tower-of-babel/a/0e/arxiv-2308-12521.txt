The generalized Bernoulli numbers and its relation with the Riemann zeta function at odd-integer arguments
Yayun Wu

Abstract




Abstract:

By using the generalized Bernoulli numbers, we deduce new integral representations for the Riemann zeta function at positive odd-integer arguments. The explicit expressions enable us to obtain criteria for the dimension of the vector space spanned over the rational by the
\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n}
,
n\geq 1
.

Key words:
Riemann zeta function; Integral representation; Generalized Bernoulli numbers; Hyperbolic functions

Mathematics Subject Classification:
11M06; 11C08; 11J72; 30A05

1. Introduction

The Riemann zeta function
\zeta(s)
is defined for complex values of
s
,
\Re(s)>1
as
\zeta(s):=\sum_{v=1}^{\infty}v^{-s}
, and its meromorphic continuation over the whole complex
s-
plane, except for a simple pole at
s=1
. The research of the arithmetical properties of the Riemann zeta function at positive integers is one of the fascinating topics of the complex analysis and number theory. Finding recurrence formulas and integral representations of zeta function has become a significant issue. As is well known, for Riemann’s zeta function
\zeta(s)
at even points, we have the formula (found by Euler)

\displaystyle\zeta(2k)=\frac{(-1)^{k-1}(2k)^{2k}B_{2k}}{2(2k)!},~k\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.

Here the
B_{k}=B_{k}(0)
is the well-tabulated Bernoulli numbers defined by means of the generating function

\displaystyle\frac{ze^{zx}}{e^{z}-1}=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{B_{k}(x)}{n!}z^{k},~|z|<2\pi.

However, there is no analogous closed evaluation for the values of
\zeta(s)
at positive odd integers or fractional points up to the present time. Using the series of special functions and the multidimensional integral to investigate
\zeta(s)
are the main methods.
Connecting Euler polynomials and trigonometric functions, Srivastava and Choi[3] give the Riemann zeta function at positive odd integers

\displaystyle\zeta(2n+1)=\frac{(-1)^{n}\pi^{2n+1}}{4\delta(1-2^{-(2n+1)})(2n)!}\int_{0}^{\delta}E_{2n}(u)\csc(u\pi){\rm d}u,~n\in\mathbb{N}^{+},~\delta=1~{\rm or}~1/2,

where
E_{n}(u)
is the Euler polynomials. Zurab[1], Cvijovi
\acute{c}
and Klinowski[2] establish similar results by using Euler polynomials and the polylogarithm function[8]. Generalizing the trigonometric function to hyperbolic version, Lima[6], D’Avanzo and Krylov[5] research the integral result about
\zeta(n)
,

\displaystyle\zeta(n)=\frac{2^{n}}{(2^{n}-1)(n-1)!}\int_{0}^{\infty}\ln^{n-1}(\coth(x)){\rm d}x,~n\geq 2.

These integral representations about
\zeta(s)
hardly show the correlation with the irrational problem of the zeta function values at odd integers. Since the problem of the irrationality of the values of the zeta function at odd integers is one of the most attractive topics in modern number theory.
These problems motivate us to modify the integral representations about
\zeta(s)
.

The article is organized as follows. In
\lx@sectionsign
2, we reconsider the evaluation of
\zeta(3)
so that technical details of the general case do not obscure. In
\lx@sectionsign
3, we elaborate the general case by using the generalized Bernoulli polynomials and numbers.
In
\lx@sectionsign
4, we evaluate the numerical results about
\zeta(5)
and
\zeta(7)
. In
\lx@sectionsign
5, the formula for
\zeta(2n+1)
enables us to obtain linear forms with rational coefficients in the values of
\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
.
We also ponder over the arithmetical properties of the sequence
\zeta(3)/\pi^{2},\zeta(5)/\pi^{4},\zeta(7)/\pi^{6}
,
\cdots
.

2. Evaluation of
\zeta(3)

In this part, we rediscover an integral representation for
\zeta(3)
. Let us briefly describe the proof for a better exposition of the general case and its consequences.

Lemma 2.1

For Riemann zeta function value
\zeta(3)
, we have

\displaystyle\zeta(3)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{7}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{u}(e^{u}-1)}{(e^{u}+1)^{3}u}du.

(1)

Proof.
For the part sum of the series
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{3}}
, the following expression is valid

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{3}}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{4}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{e^{z}(e^{z}-1)}{(e^{z}+1)^{3}z}{\rm d}z,~n\in\mathbb{N}^{+},

(2)

where
\gamma_{n}=\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}+\Gamma_{2}^{(n)}+\Gamma_{3}^{(n)}+\Gamma_{4}^{(n)}+\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
represent the contour with vertices
\pm R_{n}
,
R_{n}+{\rm i}R_{n}
,
-R_{n}+{\rm i}R_{n}
(see Fig. 1).
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is a semicircle of radius
\varepsilon\in(0,\pi/2)
centered at the origin.
\Gamma_{1}^{(n)}
represent
(-R_{n},0)\rightarrow(-\varepsilon,0)
and
(\varepsilon,0)\rightarrow(R_{n},0)
.
R_{n}=2n\pi
. In fact,
since in the neighborhood of the point
z=0
and
z=z_{k}=(2k-1)\pi{\rm i}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,n
, the following results are valid:

\displaystyle\frac{e^{z}-1}{z}=O(1),\quad\frac{e^{z}}{(e^{z}+1)^{3}}=O((z-z_{k})^{-3}).

Figure 1
:
The contour
\gamma_{n}

Let
f(z)=\frac{e^{z}(e^{z}-1)}{(e^{z}+1)^{3}z}.
The function
f(z)
is bounded on the boundary of the square with poles at the points
z_{k}
.
It follows that the integral in
(2)
is equal to the sum of residues of the integrand at the points
z_{1}=\pi{\rm i},z_{2}=3\pi{\rm i},\cdots,z_{n}=(2n-1)\pi{\rm i}
,

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{n}}f(z){\rm d}z=2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\underset{z=z_{k}}{\text{Res}}f(z)=\frac{4}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{3}}.

(3)

Furthermore,
|f(z)|=O(\frac{1}{R_{n}e^{R_{n}}})
,
z\in\Gamma_{j}^{(n)},j=2,3,4
.
In view of
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{3}}=7\zeta(3)/8
, and let
R_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
,
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in
\gamma_{n}
, we obtain the required expression

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{e^{u}(e^{u}-1)}{(e^{u}+1)^{3}u}{\rm d}u=\frac{7}{2\pi^{2}}\zeta(3),

(4)

which leads to the desired result.

\hfill\square

Corollary 2.1
Remembering inverse hyperbolic secant function:

{\rm asech}(u)=\log(1/u+\sqrt{1/u^{2}-1})
,
u\in(0,1]
, then we obtain

\displaystyle\zeta(3)=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{14}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{1}{u}{\rm d}{\rm sech^{2}(u)}=\frac{\pi^{2}}{7}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u}{{\rm asech(u)}}{\rm d}u.

By using the integral transformation
\log u=x
and remembering that
\int_{0}^{\infty}2(1+x)^{-3}x^{t}{\rm d}x=\pi t(1-t)/\sin(\pi t)
,
t\in(0,1)
, we find
(4)
equals with
(22)
in [1]:

\displaystyle\zeta(3)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{7}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x-1}{(1+x)^{3}\log x}{\rm d}x=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{7}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{x^{t}}{(1+x)^{3}}{\rm d}x{\rm d}t=\frac{1}{7}\int_{0}^{\pi}\frac{x(\pi-x)}{\sin x}{\rm d}x,

which means the result
(4)
is a special case of the more general result in [2]. But for
\zeta(2n+1),n\geq 2
, we found no association between these representations.

However, let us expand the integral representation of
\zeta(3)
into the sum of partial fractions with respect to
\exp(u)
:

\displaystyle\zeta(3)=\frac{2\pi^{2}}{7}\sum_{l=1}^{3}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{w_{l}}{u(e^{u}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}u,

(5)

where
w_{1}=1,w_{2}=-3,w_{3}=2
.
Since
\sum\limits_{l=1}^{3}w_{l}=0
, the integrand
\sum\limits_{l=1}^{3}\frac{w_{l}}{u(e^{u}+1)^{l}}
is an even function in
\mathbb{R}
except a removable singularity at origin. We wonder whether the decomposition (5) is unique and can be generalized to the general
\zeta(2n+1),n\geq 2
.

3. Evaluation of
\zeta(m)
at positive odd integers

3.1 A formula for
\zeta(m),m\geq 2

The evaluation of
\zeta(3)
can be straightforwardly generalized to
\zeta(m)
,
m\geq 2
. Before considering the general case, we need the following useful lemma.

Lemma 3.1

Let
m\geq 2
be a positive integer, and
\gamma_{n}
represent the integral contour in Lemma 2.1. There exists unique
w_{l}\in\mathbb{Q}
, which satisfying
\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}=0
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{w_{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z=\frac{2S_{m}}{({\rm i}\pi)^{m-1}}\left(\zeta(m)\frac{2^{m}-1}{2^{m}}-\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{m}}\right),~n=1,2,\cdots,

wherein
S_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Proof.
Step
1
. Let
f_{m}(z)=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\frac{w_{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}
. Note that the integrand
f_{m}(z)
over the vertical and top side
\Gamma^{(n)}_{j}
,
j=2,3,4
tend to
0
, when
R_{n}\rightarrow\infty
, since it can be bounded by
Ce^{-R_{n}}/R_{n}
and constant
C>0
. Let
z_{k}=(2k-1)\pi{\rm i}
,
k=1,2,\cdots,n
. By means of the theorem of residues, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\gamma_{n}}f_{m}(z){\rm d}z

\displaystyle=2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\left(\frac{{\rm d}}{{\rm d}z}\right)^{m-1}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\frac{w_{l}(z-z_{k})^{m}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}

\displaystyle=2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\frac{1}{(m-1)!}\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}\sum_{j=0}^{m-1}\binom{m-1}{j}\left[\frac{(z-z_{k})^{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}\right]^{(j)}\left[(z-z_{k})^{m-l}\right]^{(m-j-1)}

\displaystyle=2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\frac{w_{l}}{(l-1)!}\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\left[\frac{(z-z_{k})^{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}\right]^{(l-1)}.

(6)

Step
2
. Let us denote

\displaystyle g_{l}(z)=\frac{(z-z_{k})^{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}},~D_{l}=\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\frac{g_{l}^{(l-1)}(z)}{(l-1)!},~l=1,2,\cdots,m.

(7)

The conclusions

g_{l}^{(l-1)}(z)=\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}\binom{l-1}{j}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)^{(l-1-j)}\left[\left(\frac{z-z_{k}}{e^{z}+1}\right)^{l}\right]^{(j)},

follow from the Leibniz rule for the differentiation of a product. For
j=0
in
g_{l}^{(l-1)}(z)
, we have

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\binom{l-1}{0}\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)^{(l-1)}\left(\frac{z-z_{k}}{e^{z}+1}\right)^{l}=-\frac{(l-1)!}{z_{k}^{l}}.

(8)

According with
\big[\frac{1}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}}\big]^{\prime}+\frac{l}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}}=\frac{l}{(e^{z}+1)^{l+1}}
, which means for all
l
, the residues
c_{-1}^{(l)}
of
(e^{z}+1)^{-l}
at
z=z_{k}
are equal:

c_{-1}^{(l)}=c_{-1}^{(1)}=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\int_{\Lambda_{k}}\frac{1}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z,~l=1,2,\cdots,m,

where
\Lambda_{k}
is the small circle centered at the poles
z_{k}
and of radius
\epsilon_{k}>0
. When
l=1
, we acquire
c_{-1}^{(l)}=c_{-1}^{(1)}=\lim\limits_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}(z-z_{k})/(e^{z}+1)=-1
. For
j=l-1
in
g_{l}^{(l-1)}(z)
, by using Laurent series of
(e^{z}+1)^{-l}
at
z=z_{k}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\binom{l-1}{l-1}\frac{1}{z}\left[\left(\frac{z-z_{k}}{e^{z}+1}\right)^{l}\right]^{(l-1)}=\lim_{z\rightarrow z_{k}}\frac{1}{z}[c_{-1}^{(l)}(z-z_{k})^{l-1}]^{(l-1)}=-\frac{(l-1)!}{z_{k}}.

(9)

As a result of the relations
(8)
and
(9)
, we can write out
D_{l}
:

\displaystyle D_{l}=-(\frac{b_{l}^{(l)}}{z_{k}^{l}}+\cdots+\frac{b_{j}^{(l)}}{z_{k}^{j}}+\cdots+\frac{b_{1}^{(l)}}{z_{k}}),~j=1,\cdots,l,

(10)

in which
b_{l}^{(l)}=b_{1}^{(l)}=1
,
b_{2}^{(l)},\cdots,b_{l-1}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{Q}
.

Step
3
. In this part, we will prove that there exists unique
w_{l}
, which satisfying

\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}D_{l}=\frac{S_{m}}{z_{k}^{m}},~S_{m}\in\mathbb{Q}.

In fact, it should be noted that, by applying
(10)
in Step
2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}D_{l}=-\sum_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}(\frac{1}{z_{k}^{l}}+\cdots+\frac{b_{j}^{(l)}}{z_{k}^{j}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{z_{k}})=\frac{S_{m}}{z_{k}^{m}}.

(11)

Comparing the coefficients on both sides of identity
(11)
with respect to
1/z^{l}_{k}
, we have the relation:

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}1&1&\cdots&1\\
0&1&\cdots&b_{2}^{(m)}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
0&0&\cdots&1\end{pmatrix}_{m\times m}\begin{pmatrix}w_{1}\\
w_{2}\\
\vdots\\
w_{m}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
\vdots\\
-S_{m}\end{pmatrix}.

(12)

Let
A_{m\times m}
represents the coefficient matrix of
(12)
. Since
A_{m\times m}
is an upper-triangular matrix, and
\|A_{m\times m}\|=1,
then by Cramer’s Rule, the existence and unique of
w_{l}
can be obtained. Set
C^{(m)}=[0,0,\cdots,-S_{m}]^{T}
,
W^{(m)}=[w_{1},w_{2},\cdots,w_{m}]^{T}
, then

\displaystyle w_{l}=[W^{(m)}]_{l},~l=1,\cdots,m,

(13)

in which
W^{(m)}=A_{m\times m}^{-1}C^{(m)}
and
[\cdot]_{l}
represents the
l-
th element in the column vector.

Given that
z_{k}=(2k-1)\pi{\rm i}
, and substitute
w_{l}
and
D_{l}
into
(6)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{w_{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z=2\pi{\rm i}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{S_{m}}{z_{k}^{m}}=\frac{2S_{m}}{({\rm i}\pi)^{m-1}}\Big[\zeta(m)(1-\frac{1}{2^{m}})-\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{m}}\Big],

which gives the required equivalence. Lemma 3.1 is thus proved.

\hfill\square

Corollary 3.1

Let

S_{m}=\left\{\begin{aligned} &(-1)^{\frac{m-1}{2}}\Gamma(m),~~m=3,5,\cdots,\\
&(-1)^{\frac{m+2}{2}}\Gamma(m),~~m=2,4,\cdots,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\Gamma(\cdot)
denotes the Gamma function and
\Gamma(m)=(m-1)!
. For
n=1,2,\cdots
, by using Lemma 3.1, there exists unique
w_{l}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
l=1,2,\cdots,m
, such that

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{w_{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z=\frac{2\Gamma(m)}{\pi^{m-1}{\rm i}^{\kappa}}\Big[\zeta(m)(1-\frac{1}{2^{m}})-\sum_{k=n+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{(2k-1)^{m}}\Big],

(14)

in which
\kappa=0
or
1
with respect to
m
is positive odd or even integer.

3.2 A formula for
\zeta(m),m\geq 3
at positive odd integers

The generalized Bernoulli polynomials[4]
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
of degree
n
in
x
are defined by the generating function:

\displaystyle\left(\frac{z}{e^{z}-1}\right)^{l}e^{xz}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}^{(l)}(x)\frac{z^{n}}{n!},~|z|<2\pi,1^{l}:=1,

(15)

for arbitrary (real or complex) parameter
l
and
B_{n}^{(l)}(0)=B_{n}^{(l)}
.
Let
m\geq 3
be a positive odd integer.
w_{l}
are solutions to
(12)
and
z_{k}=(2k-1)\pi{\rm i}
,
k\geq 1
. Set
x=z-z_{k}
and
0<|x|<2\pi
. Using the property (22) of
B_{n}^{(l)}(x)
in [4], we obtain

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{(-1)^{l}}{(e^{x}-1)^{l}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{l}B_{n}^{(l)}\frac{x^{n-l}}{n!},

(16)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(e^{-z}+1)^{l}}

\displaystyle=\frac{e^{xl}}{(e^{x}-1)^{l}}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}B_{n}^{(l)}(l)\frac{x^{n-l}}{n!}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(-1)^{n}B_{n}^{(l)}\frac{x^{n-l}}{n!}.

(17)

Since the Laurent series of
(e^{z}+1)^{-l}
centered at
z=z_{k}
have the form

\displaystyle\frac{1}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}}=\sum_{j=-l}^{\infty}c_{j}^{(l)}(z-z_{k})^{j},~l=1,2,\cdots,m,

(18)

where

\displaystyle c_{j}^{(l)}=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{\Lambda_{k}}\frac{{\rm d}z}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}(z-z_{k})^{j+1}},~j=-l,-(l-1),\cdots.

Comparing the coefficients of
1/(z-z_{k})^{l}
in
(16)
and
(18)
, we obtain

\displaystyle c_{j}^{(l)}=\frac{(-1)^{l}B_{l+j}^{(l)}}{\Gamma(l+j+1)},~j=-l,\cdots,-1.

(19)

Taking
(7)
and
(18)
into account, we obtain

\displaystyle D_{l}=\sum_{j=0}^{l-1}(-1)^{l-j-1}\binom{l-1}{j}\frac{\Gamma(l-j)\Gamma(j+1)c_{-(l-j)}^{(l)}}{\Gamma(l)z_{k}^{l-j}}=\sum_{j=1}^{l}(-1)^{j+1}\frac{c_{-j}^{(l)}}{z_{k}^{j}}.

Hence, it follows from
(10)
that

\displaystyle b_{j}^{(l)}=(-1)^{j}c_{-j}^{(l)}.

(20)

Combining with
(19)
, we deduce that

\displaystyle b_{j}^{(l)}=\frac{(-1)^{l-j}}{\Gamma(l-j+1)}B_{l-j}^{(l)},~j=1,2,\cdots,l.

(21)

With the help of
(17)
and
(12)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{w_{l}}{z(e^{-z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z&=\frac{1}{2\pi{\rm i}}\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{l=j}^{m}\int_{\Lambda_{k}}w_{l}(-1)^{l-j}\frac{B_{l-j}^{(l)}}{\Gamma(l-j+1)}\frac{1}{z(z-z_{k})^{j}}{\rm d}z\\
&=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{l=j}^{m}w_{l}b_{j}^{(l)}\frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{z_{k}^{j}}=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{(-1)^{m-1}w_{m}b_{m}^{(m)}}{z_{k}^{m}}\\
&=-\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{S_{m}}{z_{k}^{m}}.\end{aligned}

Hence, we have the relation

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{w_{l}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{w_{l}}{z}\left[\frac{1}{(e^{z}+1)^{l}}-\frac{1}{(e^{-z}+1)^{l}}\right]{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{l-1}\int_{\gamma_{n}}\frac{(1-e^{z})w_{l}e^{zp}}{z(e^{z}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}z.

(22)

Since
z=0
is a removable singularity pole for the integrand in
(22)
, let
R_{n}\rightarrow+\infty
and
\varepsilon\rightarrow 0
in
\gamma_{n}
, then we have

\displaystyle 2\Gamma(m)(1-\frac{1}{2^{m}})\frac{\zeta(m)}{\pi^{m-1}}=\sum_{l=1}^{m}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{w_{l}}{u(e^{u}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}u=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{l-1}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{w_{l}(1-e^{u})e^{up}}{u(e^{u}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}u.

Note that the integrand
\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}\sum\limits_{p=0}^{l-1}\frac{w_{l}(1-e^{u})e^{up}}{u(e^{u}+1)^{l}}
is an even function on
\mathbb{R}
except a removable singularity at origin, then we have

\displaystyle\zeta(m)=\frac{(2\pi)^{m-1}}{(2^{m}-1)\Gamma(m)}\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{l-1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{w_{l}(1-e^{u})e^{up}}{u(e^{u}+1)^{l}}{\rm d}u,~~m=3,5,\cdots,

(23)

in which
w_{l}
are determined by
(13)
.

4. Evaluation of
\zeta(5)
and
\zeta(7)
by using the generalized Bernoulli numbers

In this part, we calculate the numerical results of
\zeta(5)
and
\zeta(7)
. In view of the Srivastava-Todorov formula [8, p.510, Eq.(3)], we know the following representation for the generalized Bernoulli numbers:

\displaystyle B_{n}^{(l)}=\sum_{k=0}^{n}\binom{l+n}{n-k}\binom{l+k-1}{k}\frac{n!}{(n+k)!}\sum_{j=0}^{k}(-1)^{j}\binom{k}{j}j^{n+k}.

(24)

Using the formula
(21)
and
(24)
, it is verified by a straightforward calculation that

\displaystyle D_{1}=-\frac{1}{z_{k}},~D_{2}=-\frac{1}{z_{k}^{2}}-\frac{1}{z_{k}},~D_{3}=-\frac{1}{2!}(\frac{2}{z_{k}^{3}}+\frac{3}{z_{k}^{2}}+\frac{2}{z_{k}}),

\displaystyle D_{4}=-\frac{1}{3!}(\frac{3!}{z_{k}^{4}}+\frac{12}{z_{k}^{3}}+\frac{11}{z^{2}_{k}}+\frac{3!}{z_{k}}),~D_{5}=-\frac{1}{4!}(\frac{4!}{z^{5}_{k}}+\frac{60}{z_{k}^{4}}+\frac{70}{z_{k}^{3}}+\frac{50}{z_{k}^{2}}+\frac{4!}{z_{k}}),

\displaystyle D_{6}=-\frac{1}{5!}(\frac{5!}{z_{k}^{6}}+\frac{360}{z_{k}^{5}}+\frac{510}{z_{k}^{4}}+\frac{450}{z_{k}^{3}}+\frac{274}{z_{k}^{2}}+\frac{5!}{z_{k}}),

\displaystyle D_{7}=-\frac{1}{6!}(\frac{6!}{z_{k}^{7}}+\frac{2520}{z_{k}^{6}}+\frac{4200}{z_{k}^{5}}+\frac{4410}{z_{k}^{4}}+\frac{3248}{z_{k}^{3}}+\frac{1764}{z_{k}^{2}}+\frac{6!}{z_{k}}),

where
z_{k}=(2k-1)\pi{\rm i},1\leq k\leq n
.
According with
S_{m}
in Corollary
3.1
and solving linear equation
(12)
, we obtain
w_{l}
and the following result.

Theorem 4.1

(1):
For Riemann zeta function value
\zeta(5)

\displaystyle\zeta(5)=\frac{(2\pi)^{4}}{(2^{5}-1)\Gamma(5)}\sum_{l=1}^{5}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{w_{l}}{(e^{u}+1)^{l}u}{\rm d}z,

where
w_{1}=-1,w_{2}=15,w_{3}=-50,w_{4}=60,w_{5}=-24
.

(2):
For Riemann zeta function value
\zeta(7)

\displaystyle\zeta(7)=\frac{(2\pi)^{6}}{(2^{7}-1)\Gamma(7)}\sum_{l=1}^{7}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{w_{l}}{(e^{u}+1)^{l}u}{\rm d}z,

where
w_{1}=1,w_{2}=-63,w_{3}=602,w_{4}=-2100,w_{5}=3360,w_{6}=-2520,w_{7}=720
.

5. The linear combination of
\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n},n\geq 1

In this part, we investigate the integral representation for the linear combination of
\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n}
. For all positive odd integer
m\geq 3
, it follows from
(23)
that

\displaystyle 2\Gamma(m)(1-\frac{1}{2^{m}})\frac{\zeta(m)}{\pi^{m-1}}=-\sum_{l=1}^{m}\sum_{p=0}^{l-1}\int_{0}^{+\infty}\frac{w_{l}e^{(2p+1-l)u}}{u(e^{u}+e^{-u})^{l}}{\rm d}(e^{u}+e^{-u}).

(25)

Considering the symmetry of the power of
\exp(2p+1-l)
with
p
from
0
to
l-1
, we can draw the following conclusions:

\displaystyle\sum_{p=0}^{l-1}\frac{e^{(2p+1-l)u}}{(e^{u}+e^{-u})^{l}}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\frac{q_{j}^{(l)}}{(e^{u}+e^{-u})^{2j-1}},~~k=\lceil{l/2}\rceil,q_{j}^{(l)}\in\mathbb{Z},

(26)

in which
\lceil{\cdot}\rceil
means the ceiling function and
q_{j}^{(l)}=1-\sum\limits_{\kappa=1}^{j-1}\binom{l+1-2\kappa}{j-\kappa}q_{\kappa}^{(l)},
for
j\geq 2
,
q_{1}^{(l)}\equiv 1
.
Remembering that
\sum\limits_{l=1}^{m}w_{l}=0
in Lemma 3.1, then we obtain

\displaystyle 2\Gamma(m)(1-\frac{1}{2^{m}})\frac{\zeta(m)}{\pi^{m-1}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil{m/2}\rceil}[\sum_{l=2j-1}^{m}w_{l}q_{j}^{(l)}]\frac{{\rm d}(e^{u}+e^{-u})}{u(e^{u}+e^{-u})^{2j-1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\sum_{j=2}^{\lceil{m/2}\rceil}[\sum_{l=2j-1}^{m}w_{l}q_{j}^{(l)}]\frac{2^{-2j+1}}{(j-1)u}{\rm d}({\rm sech}{(u)})^{2j-2}

\displaystyle=-\int_{0}^{1}\sum_{j=2}^{\lceil{m/2}\rceil}\frac{1}{4^{j-1}}[\sum_{l=2j-1}^{m}w_{l}q_{j}^{(l)}]\frac{u^{2j-3}}{{\rm~asech}{(u)}}{\rm d}u.

(27)

Let us denote

\displaystyle\tau_{j}^{(m)}=-\frac{2^{m-1}}{\Gamma(m)(2^{m}-1)}\sum\limits_{l=2j-1}^{m}\frac{w_{l}q_{j}^{(l)}}{4^{j-1}},

(28)

then

\displaystyle\frac{\zeta(m)}{\pi^{m-1}}=\sum_{j=2}^{\lceil{m/2}\rceil}\tau_{j}^{(m)}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2j-3}}{{\rm~asech}{(u)}}{\rm d}u.

(29)

Through above discussion, we have the following theorem.

Theorem 5.1

For any positive integer
n
,
then there exists
\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
,
k=1,2\cdots,n
, so that

\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}\frac{\zeta(2k+1)}{\pi^{2k}}=\theta_{n+1}I_{n},~\theta_{n+1}=0~or~1,

where
I_{n}
satisfy

I_{n}=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2n-1}}{{\rm asech}(u)}du>0,~~\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{n}=0.

Proof.
Since
{\rm asech(u)}=\log(1/u+\sqrt{1/u^{2}-1})
, then for
\vartheta
near to
0
from right side

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm asech(u)}}=-\frac{1}{\log u}-\frac{\log 2}{\log^{2}u}+o(1/\log^{2}u),~0<u<\vartheta<1,

(30)

where
o(\cdot)
means higher-order infinitesimal. Hence, we obtain
1/{\rm asech}(u)\rightarrow 0
as
u\rightarrow 0^{+}
.
And for
\eta>0
near to
1
from left side, when
u\in(\eta,1)
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{{\rm asech(u)}}=\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2\sqrt{1/u-1}}+\frac{\sqrt{2}}{24}\sqrt{1/u-1}+o(\sqrt{1/u-1}),

(31)

then
{\rm asech(u)}/\sqrt{1/u-1}\rightarrow\sqrt{2}
as
u\rightarrow 1^{-}
. Since
\int_{0}^{1}(1/u-1)^{-1/2}du=\pi/2
,
so we can deduce that
\int_{0}^{1}1/{\rm asech(u)}{\rm du}
converges. According with
{\rm asech(u)}>0
, when
u\in(0,1)
, and
0<I_{n}\leq\int_{0}^{1}1/{\rm asech(u)}{\rm du}
, then
\{I_{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
is a monotonically decreasing positive sequence with a lower bound, so it must converge.

From
(31)
, when
u\in(\eta,1)
, we can find proper
M>0
, so that

{\rm 1/asech(u)}\leq\frac{M}{\sqrt{1/u-1}}
. As a result, we have

\displaystyle I_{n}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\frac{u^{2n-1}}{{\rm asech}(u)}du,

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\eta}\frac{u^{2n-1}}{{\rm asech}(u)}du+\int_{\eta}^{1}\frac{u^{2n-1}}{{\rm asech}(u)}du

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{2n-1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{{\rm asech}(u)}du+M\int_{0}^{1}u^{2n-1}(1/u-1)^{-1/2}du

\displaystyle\leq\eta^{2n-1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{{\rm asech}(u)}du+M\sqrt{\pi}\frac{\Gamma(2n+1/2)}{\Gamma(2n+1)},

since
\eta\in(0,1)
and using Stirling’s approximation:
\frac{\Gamma(2n+1/2)}{\Gamma(2n+1)}=O(1/\sqrt{2n+1})
as
n\rightarrow\infty
, so
\lim\limits_{n\rightarrow\infty}I_{n}=0
.

Using
(29)
, let
m=2n+1
, if for
\forall n\geq 1
, then
\tau_{n+1}^{(2n+1)}\neq 0
, we can choose proper
\theta_{k}
,
1\leq k\leq n
so that
\theta_{n+1}=1
. If there exists
n\geq 2
such that
\tau_{n+1}^{(2n+1)}=0
, then we can choose proper
\theta_{k}
(where
\theta_{k}
satisfying
\sum_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}^{2}\neq 0
) making
\theta_{n+1}=0
.
Therefore, there must exist proper
\theta_{k}\in\mathbb{Q}
which make
\sum\limits_{k=1}^{n}\theta_{k}\frac{\zeta(2k+1)}{\pi^{2k}}=\theta_{n+1}I_{n}
,
\theta_{n+1}=0
or
1
. Thus Theorem 5.1 is proved.
\hfill\square

According with Theorem
5.1
in this paper and Nesterenko’s theorem[9], we derive criteria for the dimension of the vector space spanned over the rational by
\zeta(2n+1)/\pi^{2n}
,
n\geq 1
.

Theorem 5.2

Let
m=2n+1
in
(29)
, if
\tau_{n+1}^{(2n+1)}\neq 0
in
(28)
for
n\geq 1
, then

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{Q}}(\mathbb{Q}\frac{\zeta(3)}{\pi^{2}}+\mathbb{Q}\frac{\zeta(5)}{\pi^{4}}+\cdots+\mathbb{Q}\frac{\zeta(2n+1)}{\pi^{2n}}+\cdots)=\infty;

(32)

If there exits
n\geq 2

6. Concluding remarks

Through numerical calculations of
\zeta(5)
,
\zeta(7)
and
