A first-order logic characterization of safety and co-safety languages
Alessandro Cimatti ORCID:0000-0002-1315-6990, Luca Geatti ORCID:0000-0002-7125-787X, Nicola Gigante ORCID:0000-0002-2254-4821, Angelo Montanari ORCID:0000-0002-4322-769X, Stefano Tonetta ORCID:0000-0001-9091-7899

Abstract
. Linear Temporal Logic ( 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} ) is one of the most popular temporal logics and comes into play in a variety of branches of computer science. Among the various reasons of its widespread use there are its strong foundational properties: 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} is equivalent to counter-free ω \omega -automata, to star-free ω \omega -regular expressions, and (by Kamp’s theorem) to the First-Order Theory of Linear Orders ( 𝖥𝖮 ​ - ​ 𝖳𝖫𝖮 \mathsf{FO{\text{-}}TLO} ). Safety and co-safety languages, where a finite prefix suffices to establish whether a word does not belong or belongs to the language, respectively, play a crucial role in lowering the complexity of problems like model checking and reactive synthesis for 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} . 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} (resp., 𝖼𝗈𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} ) is a fragment of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} where only the tomorrow , the weak tomorrow and the until temporal modalities (resp., the tomorrow , the weak tomorrow and the release temporal modalities) are allowed, that recognises safety (resp., co-safety) languages only. The main contribution of this paper is the introduction of a fragment of 𝖥𝖮 ​ - ​ 𝖳𝖫𝖮 \mathsf{FO{\text{-}}TLO} , called 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖥𝖮 \mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO} , and of its dual 𝖼𝗈𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖥𝖮 \mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO} , which are expressively complete with respect to the 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} -definable safety and co-safety languages. We prove that they exactly characterize 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} and 𝖼𝗈𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} , respectively, a result that joins Kamp’s theorem, and provides a clearer view of the characterization of (fragments of) 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} in terms of first-order languages. In addition, it gives a direct, compact, and self-contained proof that any safety language definable in 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} is definable in 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} as well. As a by-product, we obtain some interesting results on the expressive power of the weak tomorrow operator of 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} , interpreted over finite and infinite words. Moreover, we prove that, when interpreted over finite words, 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} (resp. 𝖼𝗈𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} ) devoid of the tomorrow (resp., weak tomorrow ) operator captures the safety (resp., co-safety) fragment of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} over finite words. We then investigate some formal properties of 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖥𝖮 \mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO} and 𝖼𝗈𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖥𝖮 \mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO} : {enumerate*} [label=()] we study their succinctness with respect to their modal counterparts, namely, 𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} and 𝖼𝗈𝖲𝖺𝖿𝖾𝗍𝗒 ​ - ​ 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}} ; we illustrate an important practical application of them in the context of reactive synthesis; we compare them with expressively equivalent first-order fragments. Last but not least, we provide different characterizations of the (co-)safety fragment of 𝖫𝖳𝖫 \mathsf{LTL} in terms of temporal logics, automata, and regular expressions.

1.
Introduction

Linear Temporal Logic
(
\mathsf{LTL}
) is the de-facto standard logic for
system specifications
[
Pnu77
]
. It is a modal logic that is usually
interpreted over infinite state sequences, but the finite-words semantics has
recently gained attention as well
[
DV13
,
DV15
]
.
The widespread use of
\mathsf{LTL}
is due to its simple syntax and semantics, and to
its strong foundational properties. Among them, we would like to mention the
seminal work by Kamp
[
Kam68
]
and Gabbay
et al
.
[
GPSS80
]

on its expressive completeness, that is,
\mathsf{LTL}
-definable languages
are exactly those definable in the first-order fragment of the monadic
second-order theory of linear orders
[
Büc90
]
(
\mathsf{FO{\text{-}}TLO}
for short).

In formal verification, an important class of specifications is
that of
safety languages
. They are languages of infinite words
where a finite prefix suffices to establish whether a word does not belong to
the language. As an example, the set of all and only those infinite sequences where some particular bad event never happens can be regarded as a safety language.
In the dual
co-safety
languages (sometimes called
guarantee
languages), a finite prefix is sufficient to tell whether a word
belongs
to the language,
e
.
g
., when some desired event is mandated to eventually happen.
Safety and co-safety languages are important for verification, model-checking,
monitoring, and automated synthesis, because they capture a variety of real-world
requirements while being much simpler to deal with
algorithmically
[
KV01
,
BAS02
,
ZTL+17
]
.

\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
is the fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
where only the
tomorrow
, the

weak tomorrow
and the
until
temporal modalities are allowed.
Similarly, its dual
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
is obtained by only allowing the

tomorrow
, the
weak tomorrow
and the
release

modalities. It has been proved by Chang
et al
.
[
CMP92
]
that

\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
and
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
define exactly the safety and co-safety
languages that are definable in
\mathsf{LTL}
, respectively.

The paper consists of four parts.

In the first part, we provide a novel characterization of
\mathsf{LTL}
-definable safety
languages, and of their duals, in terms of a fragment of
\mathsf{FO{\text{-}}TLO}
, called

\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
, and of its dual
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
. We argue that they have a very natural
syntax, and we prove that they are
expressively complete
with respect to

\mathsf{LTL}
-definable safety and co-safety languages.
We first prove the correspondence between
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
and
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
,
which extends naturally to their duals and can be viewed as a version
of Kamp’s theorem
[
Kam68
]
specialized for safety and co-safety
properties. Such a result provides a clearer picture of the correspondence
between (fragments of) temporal and first-order logics.
Then, we exploit it to prove the correspondence between co-safety languages
definable in
\mathsf{LTL}
and
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
, thus establishing also the equivalence
between the former and
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
.
This gives a new proof of the fact that
\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
captures exactly the set of

\mathsf{LTL}
-definable safety languages
[
CMP92
]
, which can be viewed as
another contribution of the paper.

The interest of the latter proof is twofold: on the one hand, the original proof by
Chang
et al.

[
CMP92
]
is only sketched and it relies on two
non-trivial translations scattered across different
sources
[
Zuc86
,
SPH84
]
; on the other hand, such an equivalence
result seems not to be very much known, as some authors presented the problem as
open as lately as 2021
[
ZTL+17
,
DGDST+21
]
. Thus, a compact and self-contained proof of the result seems to be a useful
contribution for the community. It is worth to note that both proofs build
on the fact that safety/co-safety languages can be captured by formulas of
the form
\mathsf{G}^{\prime}
/
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
with
\alpha
pure-past, but, after
that, the two proofs significantly diverge.
At the end of this part, as a by-product, we give some results that assess the expressive power of the
weak tomorrow

operator of
\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
when interpreted over finite
vs.
infinite
words.

The second part is devoted to the safety and co-safety
fragments of
\mathsf{LTL}
interpreted over finite words. We show that the logic
obtained from
\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
(resp.
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
) by forbidding the
tomorrow
(resp.,

weak-tomorrow
) operator captures the set of safety (resp., co-safety)
properties of
\mathsf{LTL}
over finite words. This provides a clearer view of
which fragments of
\mathsf{LTL}
over finite words characterize the safety and
co-safety fragments.

In the third part, we study some formal properties of
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
and
\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
. We begin by studying
the succinctness of
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
with respect to
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
. We first show
that there is a linear-size equivalence-preserving translation from
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
to

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
. Then, we show that the proposed translation from
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
to
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
that we exploit to prove the expressive equivalence between the two formalisms is nonelementary.
Next, we illustrate an interesting practical application of
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
to reactive synthesis from temporal specifications. Finally, we
compare
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
with another fragment of
\mathsf{FO{\text{-}}TLO}
that has been proved
to be expressively equivalent to the co-safety fragment of

\mathsf{LTL}

[
Tho88
]
. Naturally, all the above results can be
dualized for the case of
\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
.

In the fourth and last part, we summarize the other
characterizations of the (co)safety fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
that have been
proposed in the literature so far, that is, those in terms of

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

temporal logics,

automata, and

regular expressions.

The paper is organized as follows.
Section

2
provides some
background knowledge.

Section

3
introduces
\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
and
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
and proves their

\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
and
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}

Section

4

proves their correspondence with the set of safety and co-safety languages

\mathsf{LTL}
, thus providing a compact and self-contained proof of the

\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
and
\mathsf{LTL}
-definable safety languages. Some
properties of the
weak next
operator are outlined as well.

Section

5
proves the expressive completeness of the

\mathsf{LTL}
over finite words devoid of the
tomorrow

(resp.,
weak-tomorrow
) operator and the safety (resp., co-safety)
fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
over finite words.

Section

6
compares
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

formula of this form:

\displaystyle\phi(z_{0},\ldots,z_{m})\coloneqq\exists x_{0}

\displaystyle\ldots\exists x_{n}\big(

\displaystyle x_{0}<x_{1}<\dots<x_{n}

ordering constraints

\displaystyle{}\land{}

\displaystyle z_{0}=x_{0}\land\bigwedge_{k=1}^{m}(z_{k}=x_{i_{k}})

binding constraints

\displaystyle{}\land{}

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}(x_{j})

punctual constraints

\displaystyle{}\land{}

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}\forall y(x_{j-1}<y<x_{j}\to\beta_{j}(y))\big)

interval constraints

where
i_{k}\in\{0,\ldots,n\}
for each
0\leq k\leq m
, and
\alpha_{j}
and

\beta_{j}
, for each
1\leq j\leq n
, are quantifier-free formulas with exactly
one free variable.

Some explanations are due. Each
\exists\forall
-formula states a number of
requirements for its free variables and for its quantified variables. Through
the binding constraints, the free variables are identified with a subset of the
quantified variables in order to uniformly state the punctual and interval
constraints, and the ordering constraints which sort all the variable in a total
order. Note that there is no relationship between
n
and
m
: there might be
more quantified variables than free variables, or less. Note as well that the
binding constraint
z_{0}=x_{0}
is always present,
i
.
e
., at least one free variable
has to be the minimal element of the ordering. This ensures that

\exists\forall
-formulas constrain only positions of the word that are greater
than the value of
x_{0}
.

We say that a formula of
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
is in
normal form
if and only if it
is a disjunction of
\exists\forall
-formulas.
To see how formulas in normal form make sense, let us immediately show how to
translate them into
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
formulas.

Lemma 8
.

For any formula
\phi(z)\in\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
in normal form, with a single free
variable, there exists a formula
\psi\in\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
such that

\langfin(\phi(z))=\langfin(\psi)
.

Proof 3.4
.

We show how any
\exists\forall
-formula is equivalent to a

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
-formula, over finite words. Since each formula in normal
form is a disjunction of
\exists\forall
-formulas, and since
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
is
closed under disjunction, this implies the proposition.
Let
\phi(z)
be a
\exists\forall
-formula with a single free variable.
Having only one free variable,
\phi(z)
is of the form:

\displaystyle\exists x_{0}\dots\exists x_{n}\big(

\displaystyle x_{0}<\dots<x_{n}{}\land{}z=x_{0}

\displaystyle{}\land{}\bigwedge_{j=0}^{n}\alpha_{j}(x_{j}){}\land{}\bigwedge_{j=1}^{n}\forall y(x_{j-1}<y<x_{j}\to\beta_{j}(y))\big)

Now, let
A_{i}
be the temporal formulas corresponding to
\alpha_{i}
and
B_{i}

be the ones corresponding to
\beta_{i}
. Recall that
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}

are quantifier free with only one free variable, hence this correspondence is
trivial. Since
z
is the first time point of the ordering mandated by the
formula, we only need future temporal operators to encode
\phi
into a

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
formula
\psi
defined as follows:

\displaystyle\psi=A_{0}\land^{\prime}(B_{0}^{\prime}(A_{1}\land^{\prime}(B_{1}^{\prime}A_{2}\land\dots^{\prime}(B_{n-1}^{\prime}A_{n})\dots)))

It can be seen that
\sigma,k\models\psi
if and only if

(\sigma)^{s},k\models\phi(z)
, for each
\sigma\in(2^{\Sigma})^{+}
and each

k\geq 0
.
Thus,
\langfin(\phi(z))=\langfin(\psi)
.

Two differences between our
\exists\forall
-formulas and those used by
Rabinovich
[
Rab14
]
are crucial: first, we do not
have unbounded universal requirements, but all interval constraints use bounded
quantifications, hence we do not need the
always
operator to encode them;
second, our
\exists\forall
-formulas are
future
formulas, hence we only need
future operators to encode them.

We now show that any
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
formula can be translated into normal form,
that is, into a
disjunction
of
\exists\forall
-formulas.

Lemma 9
.

Any
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
formula is equivalent to a disjunction of
\exists\forall
-formulas.

Proof 3.5
.

Let
\phi
be a
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
formula. We proceed by structural induction on

\phi
. For the base case, for each atomic formula
\phi(z_{0},z_{1})
we provide
an equivalent
\exists\forall
-formula
\psi(z_{0},z_{1})

(1)

if
\phi=(z_{0}<z_{1})
then
\psi:=\exists x_{0}\exists x_{1}(z_{0}=x_{0}\land z_{1}=x_{1}\land x_{0}<x_{1})
;

(2)

if
\phi=(z_{0}=z_{1})
, then
\psi:=\exists x_{0}(z_{0}=x_{0}\land z_{1}=x_{0})
.

(3)

if
\phi=(z_{0}\neq z_{1})

\phi\equiv z_{0}<z_{1}\lor z_{1}<z_{0}

(4)

if
\phi=P(z_{0})

\psi:=\exists x_{0}(z_{0}=x_{0}\land P(x_{0}))
. Similarly if
\phi=\lnot P(z_{0})
.

For the inductive step:

(1)

The case of a disjunction is trivial.

(2)

If
\phi(z_{0},\dots,z_{k})
is a conjunction, by the inductive hypothesis
each conjunct is equivalent to a disjunction of
\exists\forall
-formulas. By
distributing the conjunction over the disjunction we can reduce ourselves to
the case of a conjunction

\psi_{1}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k})\land\psi_{2}(z_{0},\ldots,z_{k})

\exists\forall
-formulas

1

1

\psi_{1}

and
\psi_{2}
have the same free variables

. In the

), then its

z_{i}
.

. In this case we have that:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}

\displaystyle\equiv\exists x_{0}\ldots\exists x_{n}\big(x_{0}<\dots<x_{n}\land z_{0}=x_{0}\land\ldots\big)

\displaystyle\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv\exists x_{n+1}\ldots\exists x_{m}(x_{n+1}<\dots<x_{m}\land z_{0}=x_{n+1}\land\ldots)

Since the set of quantified variables in
\psi_{1}
is disjoint from the set
of quantified variables in
\psi_{2}
, we can distribute the existential
quantifiers over the conjunction
\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}
, obtaining:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv{}\exists x_{0}\ldots\exists x_{n}\exists x_{n+1}\ldots\exists x_{m}

\displaystyle\big(x_{0}<\dots<x_{n}\land x_{n+1}<\dots<x_{m}\land z_{0}=x_{0}\land z_{0}=x_{n+1}\land\ldots\big)

Note that we can identify
x_{0}
and
x_{n+1}
, obtaining:

\displaystyle\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}

\displaystyle\equiv{}\exists x_{0}\ldots\exists x_{n}\exists x_{n+2},\ldots\exists x_{m}

\displaystyle\big(x_{0}<\dots<x_{n}\land x_{0}<x_{n+2}<\dots<x_{m}\land{}

\displaystyle z_{0}=x_{0}\land\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}(z_{i}=x_{j_{i}})\land{}\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=0,i\neq n+1\end{subarray}}^{m}\alpha_{i}(x_{i})\land

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{\begin{subarray}{c}i=1,i\neq n+1\\
i\neq n+2\end{subarray}}^{m}\forall y(x_{i-1}<y<x_{i}\to\beta_{i}(y))\land\forall y(x_{0}<y<x_{n+2}\to\beta_{n+2})\big)

where
j_{i}\in\{0,\dots,k\}
, for each
0\leq i\leq k
.
Now, to turn this formula into a disjunction of
\exists\forall
-formulas, we
consider all the possible interleavings of the variables that respect the
two imposed orderings and explode the formula into a disjunction that
consider each such interleaving. Let

X=\{x_{0},\ldots,x_{n},x_{n+2},\ldots,x_{m}\}
and let
\Pi
be the set of
all the permutations of

x_{0}<x_{n+1}<\dots<x_{m}
. For any

\pi(0)=x_{0}
. Now,

\psi_{1}\land\psi_{2}

\exists\forall
-formulas

\psi_{\pi}

\displaystyle\psi_{\pi}\equiv{}

\displaystyle\exists x_{\pi(0)}\ldots\exists x_{\pi(m)}

\displaystyle\big(x_{\pi(0)}<\dots<x_{\pi(m)}\land{}

\displaystyle z_{0}=x_{0}\land\bigwedge_{i=1}^{k}(z_{i}=x_{\pi(j_{i})})\land\bigwedge_{i=0}^{m}\alpha_{i}(x_{i})\land

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{i=0}^{m}\forall y(x_{\pi(i-1)}<y<x_{\pi(i)}\to\beta^{*}_{i}(y))\big)

\beta^{*}_{i}=\begin{cases}\beta_{\pi(i)}&\text{if both $\pi(i),\pi(i-1)\leq n$ or both $\pi(i),\pi(i-1)>n$ }\\
\beta_{\pi(i)}\land\beta_{\pi(i-1)}&\text{if $\pi(i)\leq n$ and $\pi(i-1)>n$ or {\emph{vice versa}} }\end{cases}

Then we have that

-formulas.

(3)

Let
\phi(z_{0},\dots,z_{m})=\exists z_{m+1}\mathrel{.}(z_{i}<z_{m+1}\land\phi_{1}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m},z_{m+1}))

.

\exists z_{m+1}(z_{i}<z_{m+1}\land\bigvee_{k=0}^{j}\psi_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m},z_{m+1}))

\psi_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m},z_{m+1})

\displaystyle\exists z_{m+1}\mathrel{.}(z_{i}<z_{m+1}\land\bigvee_{k=0}^{j}(\exists x_{0}\dots

\displaystyle\exists x_{n_{k}}\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m+1},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}})))

By distributing the conjunction over the disjunction, we obtain:

\displaystyle\exists z_{m+1}\mathrel{.}(\bigvee_{k=0}^{j}((z_{i}<z_{m+1})\land\exists x_{0}\dots

\displaystyle\exists x_{n_{k}}\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m+1},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}})))

\displaystyle\bigvee_{k=0}^{j}(\exists z_{m+1}((z_{i}<z_{m+1})\land\exists x_{0}\dots

\displaystyle\exists x_{n_{k}}\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m+1},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}})))

\displaystyle\bigvee_{k=0}^{j}(\exists z_{m+1}\mathrel{.}\exists x_{0}\dots

\displaystyle\exists x_{n_{k}}\mathrel{.}((z_{i}<z_{m+1})\land\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m+1},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}})))

(a)

\displaystyle(z_{i}<z_{m+1})\land(z_{i}=x_{l_{i}})\land(z_{m+1}=x_{l_{m+1}})\equiv\bot

(b)

suppose that the formula

\displaystyle(z_{i}<z_{m+1})\land(z_{i}=x_{l_{i}})\land(z_{m+1}=x_{l_{m+1}})\land(x_{l_{m+1}}<\dots<x_{l_{i}})\equiv\bot

(c)

\psi^{\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m+1},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}})

z_{i}=x_{l_{i}}
,

-formula.

After the previous transformation, we obtain:

\displaystyle\bigvee_{k=0}^{j^{\prime}}(\exists z_{m+1}\mathrel{.}\exists x_{0}\dots

\displaystyle\exists x_{n_{k}}\mathrel{.}\psi^{\prime\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m+1},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}}))

\displaystyle\bigvee_{k=0}^{j^{\prime}}(\exists x_{0}\dots

\displaystyle\exists x_{n_{k}}\mathrel{.}\psi^{\prime\prime}_{k}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m},x_{0},\dots,x_{n_{k}}))

which is a disjunction of
\exists\forall
-formulas.

(4)

Let
\phi(z_{0},\dots,z_{m})=\forall z_{m+1}(z_{i}<z_{m+1}<z_{j}\to\phi_{1}(z_{0},\dots,z_{m},z_{m+1}))

. By the

\phi_{1}
is equivalent to a disjunction

\bigvee_{k}\psi_{k}
where
\psi_{k}

\exists\forall
-formulas, i.e., each

\displaystyle\psi_{k}\equiv\exists x_{0},\ldots,x_{n}\big(

\displaystyle x_{0}<\ldots<x_{n}\land z_{0}=x_{0}\land\bigwedge_{l=1}^{m+1}(z_{l}=x_{u_{l}})\land{}

\displaystyle\bigwedge_{l=0}^{n}\alpha_{l}(x_{l})\land\bigwedge_{l=1}^{n}\forall y(x_{l-1}<y<x_{l}\to\beta_{l}(y))\big)

Without loss of generality, we can suppose that
z_{i}

z_{m+1}

\displaystyle{}\land{}

\displaystyle\alpha(z_{m+1})\land\forall y(z_{i}<y<z_{m+1}\to\beta(y))\land\forall y(z_{m+1}<y<z_{i}\to\beta^{\prime}(y))

\phi\equiv\forall z_{m+1}(z_{i}<z_{m+1}<z_{j}\to\bigvee_{k}(\theta_{k}\land\eta_{k}))

\phi\equiv\left(\begin{aligned} &\forall z_{m+1}(z_{i}<z_{m+1}<z_{j}\rightarrow\theta_{1}\lor\theta_{2})\\

Now, note that

{}\land{}&\forall y(z_{m+1}<y<z_{j}\to\beta^{\prime}(y))\end{aligned}\right)

\displaystyle z_{i}=z_{j}

\displaystyle{}\lor{}

\displaystyle z_{j}=z_{i}+1

\displaystyle{}\lor{}

\displaystyle\exists x_{i+1}(z_{i}<x_{i+1}\land x_{i+1}=z_{i}+1\land z_{j}=x_{i+1}+1\land\alpha(x_{i+1}))

\displaystyle{}\lor{}

\displaystyle\exists x_{i}\exists x_{i+1}\exists x_{j-1}\exists x_{j}\left(\begin{aligned} &x_{i}<x_{i+1}<x_{j-1}<x_{j}\\
{}\land{}&z_{i}=x_{i}\land z_{j}=x_{j}\\
{}\land{}&\alpha(x_{i+1})\land\alpha(x_{j-1})\\
{}\land{}&\forall y(x_{i}<y<x_{i+1}\to\bot)\\
{}\land{}&\forall y(x_{j-1}<y<x_{j}\to\bot)\\

{}\land{}&\forall y(x_{i+1}<y<x_{j}\to\alpha(y)\land\beta^{\prime}(y))\end{aligned}\right)

\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}

We show that there exists an equivalence-preserving translation from

\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
into
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
that involves only a linear blowup.

Proposition 23
.

For all
\phi\in\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
, there exists
\phi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

such that:

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

\lang(\phi)=\lang(\phi^{\prime})
; and

|\phi^{\prime}|\in\mathcal{O}(|\phi|)
.

Proof 6.1
.

The transformation of
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
into
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
is the same as the
transformation of
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
into
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
maintaing the
equivalence over finite words (see
7
). For
sake of clarity, we report here the transformation. We inductively
define the formula

, where

is a variable, as

•

FO(p,x)=P(x)
, for each

•

FO(\lnot p,x)=\lnot P(x)
, for each

•

FO(\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2},x)=FO(\phi_{1},x)\land FO(\phi_{2},x)

•

FO(\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2},x)=FO(\phi_{1},x)\lor FO(\phi_{2},x)

•

FO(\mathsf{X}\phi_{^{\prime}},x)=\exists y(x<y\land y=x+1\land FO(\phi_{1},y))

where

\forall z(x<z<y\rightarrow\bot)

•

FO(\phi_{1}\penalty\mathbin{\resizebox{}{0}{$\mathcal{U}$}}\phi_{^{\prime}},x)={}\exists y(x\leq y\land FO(\phi_{2},y)\land\forall z(x\leq z<y\rightarrow FO(\phi_{1},z)))

For each
\phi\in\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
, the formula

has exactly
one free variable

.
By the semantics of the operators in
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
, it is immediate to see
that for all infinite state sequences
\sigma\in(2^{\Sigma})^{\omega}

\sigma\models\phi

(\sigma)^{s},0\models FO(\phi,x)
, and

\lang(FO(\phi,x))\in\llbracket\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}\rrbracket
.

Now, we study the size of
FO(\phi,x)
in terms of the size of
\phi
.
From now on, let
n=|\phi|
. If
\phi
is an atomic formula, then

FO(\phi,x)
is of constant size. If instead
\phi\equiv^{\prime}\phi_{1}

|FO(\phi,x)|=\mathcal{O}(1)+|\phi_{1}|
.
Otherwise, if
\phi\equiv\phi_{1}\lor\phi_{2}
or
\phi\equiv\phi_{1}\land\phi_{2}

\phi\equiv\phi_{1}^{\prime}\phi_{2}
, then without loss of
generality we can suppose that

|FO(\phi,x)|=\mathcal{O}(1)+2\cdot|FO(\phi_{1},x)|

Therefore, the size of

is described by the following

\displaystyle S(n)=\begin{cases}\mathcal{O}(1)&\mbox{if }n=1\\
\max\{\mathcal{O}(1)+2\cdot S(\frac{n}{2}),\mathcal{O}(1)+S(n-1)\}&\mbox{otherwise}\end{cases}

We have that:

\displaystyle S(n)\leq(2^{i}\cdot S(\frac{n}{2^{i}})+i\cdot\mathcal{O}(1))+(S(n-1-j)+j\cdot\mathcal{O}(1))

For

and for

\displaystyle S(n)

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\log_{2}(n)}\cdot S(\frac{n}{2^{\log_{2}(n)}})+\log_{2}(n)\cdot\mathcal{O}(1)+S(n-1-n+2)+(n-2)\cdot\mathcal{O}(1)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot S(1)+\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}(n))+S(1)+\mathcal{O}(n)

\displaystyle\leq n\cdot\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(\log_{2}(n))+\mathcal{O}(1)+\mathcal{O}(n)

\displaystyle\in\mathcal{O}(n)

.

Of course, also in this case, the result can be dualized, having that for

\phi\in\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
, there exists
\phi^{\prime}\in\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
such that:

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

\lang(\phi)=\lang(\phi^{\prime})
; and

|\phi^{\prime}|\in\mathcal{O}(|\phi|)

The other direction of

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

with

\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}

6.2.

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

Interestingly, the succinctness of

Given a formula in

In
[
ZTL+17
]
, Zhu

\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}

\mathsf{FO}

. never explicitly identified it as such.

6.4.

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
and
\mathsf{EB\text{-}FO}

Since both

\mathsf{EB\text{-}FO}

\mathsf{EB\text{-}FO}

Proposition 24
.

For any formula
\phi(x)\in\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

Proof 6.2
.

Let
\phi(x)\in\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}

]
.

We start with the co-safety fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
over infinite words. Recall
that, by
3
,
\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY}
is captured by

\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
, where
\alpha\in\mathsf{LTL_{P}}
. Moreover, by
1
,

\mathsf{LTL_{P}}
(
i
.
e
., pure past
\mathsf{LTL{+}P}
) is expressively equivalent to
\mathsf{LTL}
over finite
words,
i
.
e
.,
\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket^{<\omega}=\llbracket\mathsf{LTL_{P}}\rrbracket^{<\omega}
. Now, since
\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket^{<\omega}
is
captured by star-free regular expressions, by the semantics of the

eventually
(
′
) operator, we have that

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY}
is captured by
\mathsf{SF}\cdot(\Sigma)^{\omega}
. For
finite words, by the same kind of reasoning, it follows that

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket^{<\omega}\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY^{<\omega}}
is captured by
\mathsf{SF}\cdot(\Sigma)^{*}
. By
duality,
\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{SAFETY}
and
\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket^{<\omega}\cap\mathsf{SAFETY^{<\omega}}
are
captured by
\overline{\mathsf{SF}\cdot(\Sigma)^{\omega}}
and
\overline{\mathsf{SF}\cdot(\Sigma)^{*}}
, respectively.

7.3.
Automata

In this part, we give an overview of some automata-based characterizations
proposed in the literature for the safety and co-safety fragments of
\mathsf{LTL}
.
We first recall some basic notions of automata theory.

{defi}

[Semi-automata]

A
nondeterministic semi-automaton

\autom_{s}
is a tuple

(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta)
such that:

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

\Sigma
is a finite alphabet;

Q
is a set of states;

q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state;

\delta:\allowbreak Q\allowbreak\times\allowbreak\Sigma\allowbreak\to\allowbreak 2^{Q}
is the transition function.

Given a finite alphabet
\Sigma
and
\delta:Q\times\Sigma\to 2^{Q}
, we
can extend
\delta
to
\delta^{*}:Q\times\Sigma^{*}\to 2^{Q}
in the
natural way.
Given a semi-automaton
\autom_{s}=(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta)
, we say that
the word
\sigma\in\Sigma^{*}
defines a
nontrivial cycle
in
\autom

if and only if there exists a state
q\in Q
such that

q\not\in\delta^{*}(q,\sigma)
and
q\in\delta^{*}(q,\sigma^{i})
for some

i>1

[
MP71
,
ST96
]
.

A semi-automaton
\autom_{s}=(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta)
is said to be:

•

deterministic
if and only if
\delta(q,s)
is a singleton
set, for each
q\in Q
and each
s\in\Sigma
.

•

counter-free
if and only if it does
not
contain any nontrivial
cycle.

Given a semi-automaton
\autom_{s}=(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta)
and a (finite
or infinite) word

\sigma=\!\langle\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\dots\rangle\!\in\Sigma^{*}\cup\Sigma^{\omega}
, a run

\pi
over
\sigma
is a (finite or infinite) sequence of states

\langle q_{0},q_{1},\dots\rangle\in Q^{*}\cup Q^{\omega}
such that
q_{i+1}\in\delta(q_{i},\sigma_{i})
, for any
i\geq 0
. We denote with
\infpi(\pi)
the
set of states that occur infinitely often in
\pi
, and with
\occpi(\pi)

the set of states that occur at least once in
\pi
.

An automaton
\autom
is a tuple
(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta,\allowbreak\alpha)
such
that
(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta)
is a semi-automaton and
\alpha
is an

accepting condition
.
Starting from semi-automata, we can obtain many types of
automata
by
defining different accepting conditions.

•

In nondeterministic finite automata (
\mathsf{NFAs}
, for short),
\alpha
is
a subset of
Q
and is called the set of
final state
. A run

\pi
is accepting iff there exists an
i\geq 0
such that
\pi_{i}\in\alpha
.

•

In deterministic Streett automata (
\mathsf{DSAs}
, for short),

\alpha=\{(G_{1},R_{1}),\dots,(G_{n},R_{n})\}
where
G_{i},R_{i}\subseteq Q
,
for each
1\leq i\leq n
and some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. A run
\pi
is accepting iff,
for all
1\leq i\leq n
, either
\infpi(\pi)\cap G_{i}=\varnothing
or

\infpi(\pi)\cap R_{i}\neq\varnothing
.

•

A Büchi automaton is a Streett automaton in which

\alpha=\{(Q,R_{1})\}
. In this case,
R_{1}
is called the set
of
final states
of the automaton.

•

A co-Büchi automaton is a Streett automaton in which

\alpha=\{(G_{1},\varnothing)\}
. In this case,
G_{1}
is called the set
of
rejecting states
of the automaton.

•

An Occurrence Streett automaton is a Streett automaton with
accepting condition
\alpha=\{(G_{1},R_{1}),\dots,(G_{n},R_{n})\}
in which
a run
\pi
is accepting iff either
\occpi(\pi)\cap G_{i}=\varnothing

or
\occpi(\pi)\cap R_{i}\neq\varnothing
, for all
1\leq i\leq n
.

•

The definitions of Occurrence Büchi and Occurrence co-Büchi
follow from the definition of Occurrence Streett automaton.

For all types of automata, an automaton
\autom
accepts a word
\sigma
if
and only if there exists an accepting run induced by
\sigma
in
\autom
.
The language recognized by
\autom
is the set of words that are accepted
by
\autom
. It is known that each
\omega
-language definable in
\mathsf{LTL}
is
recognized by a counter-free Büchi automaton, and

vice versa

[
MP71
]
. Similary, a language of finite
words is definable in
\mathsf{LTL}
iff it is recognized by a counter-free

\mathsf{NFA}

[
MP71
]
.

In
[
MP90
]
, Manna and Pnueli characterize the set of all
co-safety regular properties in terms of
guarantee (deterministic)
Streett automata
.
A guarantee Streett automaton
\autom=(\Sigma,\allowbreak Q,\allowbreak q_{0},\allowbreak\delta,\allowbreak\alpha)

is a Streett automaton such that:

•

\alpha=\{(G_{1},R_{1})\}
;

•

\goodset=(Q\setminus G_{1})\cup R_{1}
and
\badset=Q\setminus\goodset
;

•

\forall q\in\goodset\mathrel{.}\forall q^{\prime}\in\badset\mathrel{.}\forall\sigma\in\Sigma\mathrel{.}(q^{\prime}\not\in\delta(q,\sigma))
.

Intuitively, any accepting run of a guarantee Streett automaton can visit
the states in
G_{1}
only a finite number of times, after which it is forced
to visit only states in the
\goodset
region. Crucially, once a run enters
the
\goodset
region, each extension of it will result into an accepting
run, since it will never visit the states in
G_{1}
. For this reason, guarantee
Street automata capture
\mathsf{REG}\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY}
. In order to characterize

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY}
, by exploiting the equivalence between
counter-free automata and
\mathsf{LTL}
, Manna and Pnueli
[
MP90
]

prove that
counter-free guarantee Streett automata
capture the
co-safety fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
over infinite words.
By a simple dualization, they define
safety Streett automata
as
Streett automata in which there is no transition from the
\badset
to the

\goodset
region,
i
.
e
.:

•

\alpha=\{(G_{1},R_{1})\}
;

•

\goodset=(Q\setminus G_{1})\cup R_{1}
and
\badset=Q\setminus\goodset
;

•

\forall q\in\badset\mathrel{.}\forall q^{\prime}\in\goodset\mathrel{.}\forall\sigma\in\Sigma\mathrel{.}(q^{\prime}\not\in\delta(q,\sigma))
.

It holds that
counter-free safety Streett automata
capture

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{SAFETY}
.

In
[
CP03
]
, Cerná and Pelánek prove that

deterministic Occurrence Büchi automata
are equivalent to
guarantee Streett automata, thus proving also that the formers characterize
the co-safety fragment of regular languages. The intuition behind this
characterization is simple. A run
\pi
of a deterministic Occurrence
Büchi automaton is accepting if and only if it reaches a final state (say
at position
i
). Now, by definition of
Occurrence Büchi
automaton
, every run that agrees with
\pi
from
0
to
i
and then goes
on arbitrarly is accepting as well. It is not difficult to see that, in
order to capture
\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY}
, it suffices to add the
counter-free condition to deterministic Occurrence Büchi automata. By
dualization, Cerná and Pelánek
[
CP03
]
obtain that
counter-free deterministic Occurrence co-Büchi automata capture

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{SAFETY}
. It is simple to see that this characterization
of both the co-safety and the safety fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
in terms of
counter-free deterministic Occurrence Büchi and co-Büchi automata holds
for finite words as well.

Last but not least, the co-safety fragment of
\mathsf{LTL}
can be captured by

counter-free terminal automata

[
BRS99
,
CP03
]
.
Terminal automata are nondeterministic automata such that each final state

q\in\alpha
is such that
\delta(q,\sigma)\subseteq\alpha
(for any

\sigma\in\Sigma
),
i
.
e
., any run, once reached a final state, cannot reach
a state which is not final. It holds that
[
CP03
]
:

{enumerate*}
[label=()]

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY}
is captured by counter-free terminal
Büchi automata;

\llbracket\mathsf{LTL}\rrbracket^{<\omega}\cap\mathsf{coSAFETY^{<\omega}}
is captured by counter-free
terminal
\mathsf{NFAs}
.

8.
Conclusions

In this paper, we gave a first-order characterization of safety and
co-safety languages, by means of two fragments of first-order logic,

\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
and
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
. These fragments of
\mathsf{FO{\text{-}}TLO}
provide a very
natural syntax and are
expressively complete
with regards to

\mathsf{LTL}
-definable safety and co-safety languages.

The core theorem establishes a correspondence between
\mathsf{Safety\text{-}FO}
(resp.,

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
) and
\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
(resp.,
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
), and thus it can be
viewed as a special version of Kamp’s theorem for safety (resp., co-safety)
properties.
Thanks to these new fragments, we were able to provide a novel, compact,
and self-contained proof of the fact that
\mathsf{Safety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
captures

\mathsf{LTL}
-definable safety languages. Such a result was previously proved by
Chang
et al
.
[
CMP92
]
, but in terms of the properties of
a non-trivial transformation from star-free languages to
\mathsf{LTL}
by
Zuck
[
Zuc86
]
. As a by-product, we provided a number of results
that relate the considered languages when interpreted over finite and
infinite words. In particular, we highlighted the expressive power of the

weak tomorrow
temporal modality, showing it to be essential in

\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
over finite words. Last but not least, we show that

\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
and
\mathsf{Safety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
capture the set of co-safety and
safety languages of finite words definable in
\mathsf{LTL}
, respectively.

The equivalence-preserving translation from
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}FO}
to
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
shown in this paper can, in the worst case, produce formulas of
nonelementary size. An interesting future direction is to investigate
whether more efficient (even polynomial) translations are possible.

As we have seen, different fragments of
\mathsf{LTL}
can capture the (co-)safety
fragment. It is interesting to study the succinctness of these fragments,
in particular of
\mathsf{coSafety{\text{-}}\mathsf{LTL}}
and
\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
, and to ask whether one
can be exponentially more succinct than the other, or whether they are
incomparable as far as succinctness is considered. Last but not least,
a natural related question is whether the previous results generalize to
the case of finite words as well,
i
.
e
., for the logics
\mathsf{coSafety\text{-}\mathsf{LTL}(-^{\prime})}
and

\mathsf{F}^{\prime}
.
