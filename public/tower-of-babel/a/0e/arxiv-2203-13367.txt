Efficiency of higher-order algorithms for minimizing composite functions
Yassine Nabou, Ion Necoara

Abstract
Composite minimization involves a collection of functions which are aggregated in a nonsmooth manner. It covers, as a particular case, smooth approximation of minimax games, minimization of max-type functions, and simple composite minimization problems, where the objective function has a nonsmooth component. We design a higher-order majorization algorithmic framework for fully composite problems (possibly nonconvex). Our framework replaces each component with a higher-order surrogate such that the corresponding error function has a higher-order Lipschitz continuous derivative. We present convergence guarantees for our method for composite optimization problems with (non)convex and (non)smooth objective function. In particular, we prove stationary point convergence guarantees for general nonconvex (possibly nonsmooth) problems and under Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property of the objective function we derive improved rates depending on the KL parameter. For convex (possibly nonsmooth) problems we also provide sublinear convergence rates.

Introduction

In this work, we consider the class of general composite optimization problems:

\min_{x\in\text{dom}f}f(x):=g\big(F(x)\big)+h(x),

(1)

. Note that

This formulation unifies many particular cases, such as smooth approximation of minimax games, max-type minimization problems or exact penalty formulations of nonlinear programs, while recent instances include robust phase retrieval and matrix factorization problems

Contributions.
In this paper, we provide an algorithmic framework based on the notion of higher-order upper bound approximation of the composite problem (
1
). Note that in this optimization formulation we consider general properties for our objects, e.g., the functions
F
and
h
can be smooth or nonsmooh, convex or nonconvex and
g

F
by a higher-order surrogate, leading to a
General Composite Higher-Order
minimization algorithm, which we call GCHO. This majorization minimization approach is relevant as it yields an array of algorithms, each of which is associated with the specific properties of
F
and the corresponding surrogate, and it provides a unified convergence analysis. Note that most of our variants of GCHO for
p>1
were not explicitly considered in the literature before (at least in the nonconvex settings).
Moreover, our new first-, second-, and third-order methods can be implemented in practice using existing efficient techniques from e.g.,
Nes:20Inxt
;
WacBie:06
.

We derive convergence guarantees for the GCHO algorithm when the upper bound approximate
F
from the objective function up to an error that is
p\geq 1
times differentiable and has a Lipschitz continuous
p
derivative; we call such upper bounds composite higher-order surrogate functions. More precisely, on composite (possibly nonsmooth) nonconvex problems we prove for GCHO, with the help of a new auxiliary sequence, convergence rates
\mathcal{O}\left(\frac{1}{k^{p/(p+1)}}\right)
in terms of first-order optimality conditions. We also characterize the convergence rate of GCHO algorithm locally, in terms of function values, under the Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property. Our result show that the convergence behavior of GCHO ranges from sublinear to linear depending on the parameter of the underlying KL geometry. Moreover, on general (possibly nonsmooth) composite convex problems (i.e.,
f
is convex function) our algorithm achieves global sublinear convergence rate of order
\mathcal{O}\left(1/k^{p}\right)
in function values. We summaries our convergence results in Table

,
g(\cdot)=\max(\cdot)
and

Besides providing a general framework for the design and analysis of composite higher-order methods, in special cases, where complexity bounds are known for some particular algorithms, our convergence results recover the existing bounds. For example, from our convergence analysis one can easily recover the convergence bounds of higher-order algorithms from
Nes:20
for unconstrained minimization and from
Nes:20
;
Nes:20Inxt

Notations and preliminaries

We use the standard notations from
Nes:20
;
Nes:20Inxt
. We denote a finite-dimensional real vector space with
\mathbb{E}
and
\mathbb{E}^{*}
its dual space composed of linear functions on
\mathbb{E}
. For any linear function

, the value of

at point

\langle s,x\rangle
. Using a self-adjoint positive-definite operator

\displaystyle\lVert x\rVert=\langle{B}x,x\rangle^{{\frac{1}{2}}},\quad x\in\mathbb{E},\qquad\lVert g\rVert_{*}=\langle g,{B}^{-1}g\rangle^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad g\in\mathbb{E}^{*}.

For a twice differentiable function
\phi

, we denote by
\nabla\phi(x)
and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)
its gradient and hessian evaluated at

, respectively. Then,
\nabla\phi(x)\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
and
\nabla^{2}\phi(x)h\in\mathbb{E}^{*}
for all
x\in\text{dom}\;\phi
,
h\in\mathbb{E}
. Throughout the paper, we consider
p
a positive integer. In what follows, we often work with directional derivatives of function
\phi
at
x
along directions
h_{i}\in\mathbb{E}
of order
p
,
{D^{p}}\phi(x)[h_{1},\cdots,h_{p}]
, with
i=1:p
. If all the directions
h_{1},\cdots,h_{p}
are the same, we use the notation

{D^{p}}\phi(x)[h]
, for

Note that
{D^{p}}\phi(x)
is a symmetric
p
-linear form. Then, its norm is defined as:

\displaystyle\lVert{D^{p}}\phi(x)\rVert=\max\limits_{h\in\mathbb{E}}\left\{{D^{p}}\phi(x)[h]^{p}:\lVert h\rVert\leq 1\right\}.

Further, the Taylor approximation of order
p
of
\phi
at

is denoted:

\displaystyle T_{p}^{\phi}(y;\!\!x)=\phi(x)+\sum_{i=1}^{p}\frac{1}{i!}{D^{i}}\phi(x)[y-x]^{i}\quad\forall y\in\mathbb{E}.

If
\phi:\mathbb{E}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
is

, then the

\|{D^{p}}\phi(x)-{D^{p}}\phi(y)\|\leq L_{p}^{\phi}\|x-y\|\quad\forall x,y\in\text{dom}\;\phi.

(2)

It is known that if (
2

|\phi(y)-T_{p}^{\phi}(y;\!\!x)|\leq\frac{L_{p}^{\phi}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x\rVert^{p+1}\quad\forall x,y\in\text{dom}\;\phi.

(3)

If

, we also have the following inequalities valid for all

:

\displaystyle\|\nabla\phi(y)-\nabla T_{p}^{\phi}(y;\!\!x)\|_{*}\leq\frac{L_{p}^{\phi}}{p!}\|y-x\|^{p},

(4)

\displaystyle\|\nabla^{2}\phi(y)-\nabla^{2}T_{p}^{\phi}(y;\!\!x)\|\leq\frac{L_{p}^{\phi}}{(p-1)!}\|y-x\|^{p-1}.

(5)

For the Hessian, the norm defined in (
5

B
).
A function

is said to be nondecreasing if for all

,

is nondecreasing in its

\displaystyle z\mapsto g(z_{1},\cdots,z_{i-1},z,z_{i+1},\cdots,z_{m}),

Definition 1
.

(Subdifferential)

\displaystyle\partial f(x):=\left\{g_{x}\in\mathbb{E}^{*}\!\!:\exists x^{k}\to x,f(x^{k})\to f(x)\;\text{and}\;\exists g_{x}^{k}\in\widehat{\partial}f(x^{k})\;\;\text{such that}\;\;g_{x}^{k}\to g_{x}\right\}.

Note that we have

. In the previous inclusion, the first set is closed and convex while the second one is closed, see e.g.,

\displaystyle\partial(g\circ F)(x)=co\left\{\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}v_{i}\mid u\in\partial g\big(F(x)\big),\;v_{i}\in\partial F_{i}(x),\;i=1:m\right\},

(6)

where

, which is valid provided that, e.g.,

and

As a consequence, if

\partial(F_{1}+F_{2})(x)=\partial F_{1}(x)+\partial F_{2}(x).

For any

\displaystyle S_{f}(x)=\text{dist}\big(0,\partial f(x)\big):=\inf\limits_{g_{x}\in\partial f(x)}\lVert g_{x}\rVert.

If
\partial f(x)=\emptyset

.

General composite higher-order algorithm

In this section, we propose a higher-order algorithm for solving the general composite problem (
1
) and analyse its convergence.

Assumption 1
.

We consider the following assumptions for optimization problem (
1
):

1.

The functions
F_{i}
, with
i=1\!:\!m
,
g
and
h
are proper lower semicontinuous on their domains, satisfy the chain rule (
6
) and
\text{dom}\;h\subseteq g(\text{dom}\;F)
.

2.

Additionally,

is convex nondecreasing and has full domain, satisfying the following subhomogeneity property:

g(\alpha x)\leq\alpha g(x)\quad\forall x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}\quad\forall\alpha\geq 1.

(7)

3.

Problem (
1

f^{*}:=\!\inf_{x\in\text{dom}\;f}\!f(x)\!>\!-\infty
.

From Assumption
1

\text{dom}\;f=\text{dom}\;h
. Moreover, if Assumption
1
.2 holds, then from

g(x+ty)\leq g(x)+tg(y)\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(8)

Next, we provide several examples of optimization problems that can be written as (

Example 1
.

(Minimax strategies for nonlinear games)
Let us consider the problem:

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\triangle_{n}}\left\{f(x):=\max_{u\in\triangle_{m}}\langle F(x),u\rangle\right\},

\displaystyle\min_{x\in\triangle_{n}}\left\{f_{\mu}(x):=\max_{u\in\triangle_{m}}\Big\{\langle F(x),u\rangle-\mu\sum_{j=1}^{m}u_{j}\ln(u_{j})-\mu\ln(m)\Big\}\right\},

\displaystyle f_{\mu}(x)=\mu\ln\left(\sum_{j=1}^{m}e^{\frac{F_{i}(x)}{\mu}}\right).

Definition 2
.

Let
\phi:\mathbb{E}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
be a proper lower semicontinuous function and
x\in\text{dom}\;\phi
. We call the function
s(\cdot\;;\!x):\mathbb{E}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}
, with
\text{dom}\;s(\cdot\;;\!x)=\text{dom}\;\phi
, a
p
higher-order surrogate of

at

if it has the following properties:

the error function

\displaystyle e(y;\!\!x)=s(y;\!\!x)-\phi(y),

(9)

with
\text{dom}\;e
open such that
\text{dom}\;\phi\subseteq\text{dom}\;e
, is
p
differentiable and the
p
derivative is smooth with Lipschitz constant
L_{p}^{e}
.

the derivatives of the error function
e
satisfy

\displaystyle\nabla^{i}e(x;\!\!x)=0\quad\forall i=0:p,\;x\in\text{dom}\;\phi,

(10)

and there exist a positive constant
R_{p}^{e}>0
such that

\displaystyle e(y;\!\!x)\geq\frac{R_{p}^{e}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x\rVert^{p+1}\quad\forall x,y\in\text{dom}\;\phi.

(11)

Note that

Example 4
.

(Composite functions)

Let
F_{1}:\mathbb{E}\to\mathbb{R}
be
p

p

L_{p}^{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}F_{1}}}

F_{2}:\mathbb{E}\to\bar{\mathbb{R}}

, where
\text{dom}\;F=\text{dom}\;F_{2}
, one can consider the following

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}s(y;\!\!x)}=T^{F_{1}}_{p}(y;\!\!x)+\frac{M_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-y\rVert^{p+1}+F_{2}(y)\;\;\forall\;x,y\in\text{dom}\;F,

where

. Indeed, from the definition of the error function, we get:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}e(y;\!\!x)}=T^{F_{1}}_{p}(y;\!\!x)-F_{1}(y)+\frac{M_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-y\rVert^{p+1}.

(12)

Thus

, with
\text{dom}\;e=\mathbb{E}
and
\text{dom}\;F\subseteq\text{dom}\;e
, has the

\displaystyle\nabla^{i}e(x;\!\!x)

\displaystyle=\nabla T^{F_{1}}_{p}(x;\!\!x)-\nabla^{i}F_{1}(x)=\nabla^{i}F_{1}(x)-\nabla^{i}F(x)=0\;\;\forall i=1:p.

Moreover, since
F_{1}
has the
p

3

\displaystyle T^{F_{1}}_{p}(y;\!\!x)-F_{1}(y)\geq\frac{-L_{p}^{F_{1}}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-y\rVert^{p+1}.

Combining this inequality with (

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}e(y;\!\!x)}\geq\frac{M_{p}-L_{p}^{F_{1}}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-y\rVert^{p+1}.

(13)

Hence, the error function

L_{p}^{e}=M_{p}+L^{F_{1}}_{p}
and
R_{p}^{e}=M_{p}-L^{F_{1}}_{p}

(proximal higher-order)

Algorithm GCHO

x_{0}\in\text{dom}\;f
. For
k\geq 1
do:

1.

Compute surrogate
s(x;\!\!x_{k})\!:=\big(s_{1}(x;\!\!x_{k}),\cdots,s_{m}(x;\!\!x_{k})\big)
of
F
near
x_{k}
.

2.

Compute
x_{k+1}
satisfying the following descent:

g\big(s(x_{k+1};\!x_{k})\big)+h(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k}).

(14)

Although our algorithm requires that the next iterate
x_{k+1}
only to satisfy the descent (
14
), we usually generate
x_{k+1}
by solving the following subproblem:

\displaystyle\min_{x}g\big(s(x;\!x_{k})\big)+h(x).

(15)

If
F
and
h
are convex functions, then the subproblem (
15
) can be also convex. Indeed, for Example
4
, if
M_{p}\geq pL_{p}^{F_{1}}
and
F_{2}
is convex, then the surrogate function
s
is convex and hence the problem (
15
) is convex (see Theorem 1
Nes:20
), while for Example (
5
), the surrogate is convex if
M_{p}\geq 0
. Hence, in the convex case we assume that
x_{k+1}
is the global optimum of the subproblem (
15
). However, in the nonconvex case, we cannot guarantee the convexity of the subproblem. In this case, we either assume that we can compute a stationary point of the subproblem (
15
) if
g
is the identity function or we can compute an inexact solution as defined in (
25
) if
g
is a general function. Note that our algorithmic framework is quite general and yields an array of algorithms, each of which is associated with the specific properties of
F
and the corresponding surrogate. For example, if
F
is a sum between a smooth term and a nonsmooth one we can use a surrogate as in Example
4
; if
F
is fully nonsmooth we can use a surrogate as in Example
5
. This is the first time such an analysis is performed, and most of our variants of GCHO were not explicitly considered in the literature before (especially in the nonconvex settings). Note that in both Examples
4
and
5
,
x_{k+1}
can be computed
inexactly
, as detailed in the next sections.

3.1
Nonconvex convergence analysis

In this section we consider that each
F_{i}
, with
i=1:m
, and
h
are nonconvex functions (possible nonsmooth). Then, problem (
1
) becomes a pure nonconvex optimization problem. Now we are ready to analyze the convergence behavior of GCHO algorithm under these general settings.
In the sequel, we assume that
g(-R_{p}^{e})<0
. Note that since the vector
R_{p}^{e}>0
, then for all the optimization problems considered in Examples
1
,
2
and
3
this assumption holds provided that
M_{p}
is large enough.

Theorem 1
.

Let
F
,
g
and
h
satisfy Assumption
1
and additionally each
F_{i}
admits a
p
higher-order surrogate
s_{i}
as in Definition
2
with the constants
L^{e}_{p}(i)
and
R_{p}^{e}(i)
, for
i=1:m
. Let
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm GCHO,
R_{p}^{e}=\big(R_{p}^{e}(1),\cdots,R_{p}^{e}(m)\big)
and
L^{e}_{p}=\big(L^{e}_{p}(1),\cdots,L^{e}_{p}(m)\big)
. Then, the sequence
\left(f(x_{k})\right)_{k\geq 0}
is nonincreasing and satisfies the following descent relation:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{k})+\frac{g(-R_{p}^{e})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\qquad\forall k\geq 0.

(16)

Proof.

Denote
e(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k})=\big(e_{1}(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k}),\cdots,e_{m}(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k})\big)
. Then, from the definition of the error function
e
and (
11
), we have:

\displaystyle\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R_{p}^{e}}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\leq e(x_{k+1};x_{k})=s(x_{k+1};x_{k})-F(x_{k+1}).

This implies that:

\displaystyle F(x_{k+1})\leq s(x_{k+1};x_{k})-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R_{p}^{e}}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}.

Since
g
is nondecreasing, we get:

\displaystyle g(F(x_{k+1}))

\displaystyle\leq g\left(s(x_{k+1};x_{k})-\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R_{p}^{e}}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\right)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Lemma:1}}}{{\leq}}g\big((s(x_{k+1};x_{k})\big)+\frac{g(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R_{p}^{e}})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}.

Finally, we obtain that:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq g\big(s(x_{k+1};x_{k}))+h(x_{k+1})+\frac{g(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R_{p}^{e}})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:loc}}}{{\leq}}f(x_{k})+\frac{g(-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}R_{p}^{e}})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1},

which yields our statement.
∎

Summing (
16
) from
j=0
to
k
, we get:

\displaystyle\sum_{j=0}^{k}-\frac{g(-R_{p}^{e})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{j+1}-x_{j}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{j=0}^{k}f(x_{j})-f(x_{j+1})

\displaystyle=f(x_{0})-f(x_{k+1})\leq f(x_{0})-f^{*}.

Taking the limit as
k\to+\infty
, we obtain:

\displaystyle\sum\limits_{k=0}^{+\infty}\lVert x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rVert^{p+1}<+\infty.

(17)

Hence
\lim_{k\mapsto+\infty}\lVert x_{k}-x_{k+1}\rVert=0
.
In our convergence analysis, we also consider the following additional assumption which requires the existence of some auxiliary sequence that must be closed to the sequence generated by GCHO algorithm and some first-order relation holds:

Assumption 2
.

Given the sequence
\big(x_{k}\big)_{k\geq 0}
generated by GCHO algorithm, there exist two constants
L^{1}_{p},L^{2}_{p}>0
and a sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
such that:

\displaystyle\lVert y_{k+1}\!-\!x_{k}\rVert\!\leq\!L^{1}_{p}\lVert x_{k+1}\!-\!x_{k}\rVert\;\text{and}\;\;S_{f}(y_{k+1})\!\leq\!L^{2}_{p}\lVert y_{k+1}\!-\!x_{k}\rVert^{p}\;\;\;\forall k\geq 0.

(18)

In the next section, we provide concrete examples for the sequence
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfying Assumption
2
, and the corresponding expressions for
L_{p}^{1}
and
L_{p}^{2}
.

3.2
Approaching the set of stationary points

Before continuing with the convergence analysis of GCHO algorithm, let us analyze the relation between
\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}
and
S_{f}(x_{k+1})
and also give examples when Assumption
2
is satisfied. For simplicity, consider the following simple composite minimization problem:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{x}f(x):=F(x)+h(x),

where
F
is
p
times differentiable function, having the
p
derivative
L_{p}^{F}
-Lipschitz and
h
is proper lower semicontinuous function. In this case
g
is the identity function and we can take as a surrogate
s(y;\!x)=T_{p}^{F}(y;\!x)+\frac{M_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-y\rVert^{p+1}+h(y)
, with the positive constant
M_{p}
satisfying
M_{p}>L_{p}^{F}
and
g(-R_{p}^{e})<0
. The following lemma gives an example when Assumption
2
holds.

Lemma 1
.

Assume
g
is the identity function,
F
has the
p
derivative Lipschitz and
x_{k+1}
is a stationary point of the following subproblem:

x_{k+1}\in\argmin\limits_{x}T_{p}^{F}(x;\!\!x_{k})+\dfrac{M_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}+h(x).

(19)

Then, Assumption
2
holds with
y_{k+1}=x_{k+1}
,
L^{1}_{p}=1
and
L^{2}_{p}=\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}^{F}}{p!}
.

Proof.

Since
x_{k+1}
is a stationary point of subproblem (
19
), using (
6
), we get:

\displaystyle{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\dfrac{M_{p}}{p!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p-1}B(x_{k}-x_{k+1})-\nabla T_{p}^{F}(x_{k+1};\!x_{k})\in\partial h(x_{k+1}),}

or equivalently

\displaystyle\dfrac{M_{p}}{p!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p-1}B(x_{k}-x_{k+1})+\Big(\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla T_{p}^{F}(x_{k+1};\!x_{k})\Big)

\displaystyle\in\nabla F(x_{k+1})+\partial h(x_{k+1})=\partial f(x_{k+1}).

Taking into account that
F
is
p
-smooth, we further get:

\displaystyle S_{f}(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq\dfrac{M_{p}}{p!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}+\lVert\nabla F(x_{k+1})-\nabla T_{p}^{F}(x_{k+1},x_{k})\rVert_{\ast}

(20)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:TayAppG2}}}{{\leq}}\dfrac{M_{p}+L_{p}^{F}}{p!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}.

Hence, Assumption
2
holds with
y_{k+1}=x_{k+1}
,
L^{1}_{p}=1
and
L^{2}_{p}=\frac{M_{p}+L_{p}^{F}}{p!}
.
∎

The algorithm GCHO which generates a sequence
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfying the descent (
14
) and the stationary condition (
19
) has been also considered e.g., in the recent papers
NecDan:20
;
Nes:20Inxt
, with
h
assumed to be a convex function. Here we remove this assumption on
h
.
Combining (
20
) and (
16
), we further obtain:

\displaystyle S_{f}(x_{k+1})^{\frac{p+1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{M_{p}+L_{p}^{F}}{p!}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}\dfrac{(p+1)!}{M_{p}-L_{p}^{F}}\Big(f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})\Big)

\displaystyle=C_{M_{p},L_{p}^{F}}\Big(f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})\Big),

where
C_{M_{p},L_{p}^{F}}=\left(\dfrac{M_{p}+L_{p}^{F}}{p!}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}\dfrac{(p+1)!}{M_{p}-L_{p}^{F}}
. Summing the last inequality from
j=0:k-1
, and using that
f
is bounded from bellow by
f^{*}
, we get:

\displaystyle\sum^{k-1}_{j=0}S_{f}(x_{j})^{\frac{p+1}{p}}

\displaystyle\leq C_{M_{p},L_{p}^{F}}\Big(f(x_{0})-f(x_{k})\Big)\leq C_{M_{p},L_{p}^{F}}\Big(f(x_{0})-f^{*}\Big).

Hence:

\displaystyle\min_{j=0:k-1}S_{f}(x_{j})\leq\dfrac{\left(C_{M_{p},L_{p}^{F}}(f(x_{0})-f^{*})\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}{k^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}.

Thus, we have proved convergence for the simple composite problem under slightly more general assumptions than in
NecDan:20
;
Nes:20Inxt
, i.e.,
F
and
h
are possibly nonconvex functions.
Finally, if we have
\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}\leq\frac{p!}{L^{F}_{p}+M_{p}}\epsilon
, then from (
20
) it follows that
S_{f}(x_{k+1})\leq\epsilon
, i.e.,
x_{k+1}
is nearly stationary for
f
.
Note that in the previous Lemma
1
, we assume
x_{k+1}
to be a stationary point of the following subproblem (see (
19
)):

\displaystyle x_{k+1}\in\argmin_{x}s(x;\!x_{k}).

(21)

However, our stationary condition for
x_{k+1}
can be relaxed to the
following inexact
optimality criterion (see also
BriGar:17
):

\displaystyle\lVert g_{x_{k+1}}\rVert\leq\theta\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p},

(22)

where
g_{x_{k+1}}\in\partial s(x_{k+1};\!x_{k})
and
\theta>0
. For simplicity of the exposition, in our convergence analysis below for this particular case (i.e.,
g
identity function) we assume however that
x_{k+1}
satisfies the exact stationary condition (
21
), although our results can be extended to the
inexact
stationary condition from above. The situation is dramatically different for the general composite problem (
1
). When
g
is nonsmooth, the distance
\text{dist}\big(0,\partial f(x_{k+1})\big)
will typically not even tend to zero in the limit, although we have seen that
\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}
converges to zero. Indeed, consider the minimization of the following function:

f(x)=\max\big(x^{2}-1,1-x^{2}\big).

For
p=1
, we have
L_{1}^{F}(1)=L_{1}^{F}(2)=2
. Taking
x_{0}>1
and
M_{1}=M_{2}=4
, GCHO algorithm becomes:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=\argmin\limits_{x}Q(x,x_{k})\;\Big(:=\max\big(Q_{1}(x,x_{k}),Q_{1}(x,x_{k})-4xx_{k}+2x_{k}^{2}+2\big)\Big),

where
Q_{1}(x,x_{k})=2x^{2}-2xx_{k}+x_{k}^{2}-1
. Let us prove by induction that
x_{k}>1
for all
k\geq 0
. Assume that
x_{k}>1
for some
k\geq 0
.
We notice that the polynomials
Q_{2}(x,x_{k}):=Q_{1}(x,x_{k})-4xx_{k}+2x_{k}^{2}+2
and
Q_{1}(x,x_{k})
are 2-strongly convex functions and they intersect in a unique point
\bar{x}=\frac{x_{k}^{2}+1}{2x_{k}}
. Also, the minimum of
Q_{2}
is
\bar{x}_{2}=\frac{3}{2}x_{k}
and the minimum of
Q_{1}
is
\bar{x}_{1}:=\frac{1}{2}x_{k}
, satisfying
\bar{x}_{1}\leq\bar{x}\leq\bar{x}_{2}
. Let us prove that
x_{k+1}=\bar{x}
. Indeed, if
x\leq\bar{x}
, then
Q(x,x_{k})=Q_{2}(x,x_{k})
and it is nonincreasing on
(-\infty,\bar{x}]
. Hence,
Q(x,x_{k})\geq Q(\bar{x},x_{k})
for all
x\leq\bar{x}
. Further, if
x\geq\bar{x}
, then
Q(x,x_{k})=Q_{1}(x,x_{k})
and it is nondecreasing on
[\bar{x},+\infty)
. In conclusion,
Q(x,x_{k})\geq Q(\bar{x},x_{k})
for all
x\leq\bar{x}
. Finally, we have that:
Q(x,x_{k})\geq Q(\bar{x},x_{k})
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
x_{k}>1
, we also get that
x_{k+1}=\frac{x_{k}^{2}+1}{2x_{k}}>1
. Since
x_{k}>1
, then
\partial f(x_{k})=2x_{k}>2
and
S_{f}(x_{k})\geq 2>0
. Moreover,
x_{k+1}<x_{k}
and bounded below by 1, thus
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is convergent and its limit is
1
. Indeed, assume that
x_{k}\to\hat{x}
as
k\to\infty
. Then, we get
\hat{x}=\frac{\hat{x}^{2}+1}{2\hat{x}}
and thus
\hat{x}=1
(recall that
\hat{x}\geq 1
). Consequently,
\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert
also converges to 0. Therefore, we must look elsewhere for a connection between
S_{f}(\cdot)
and
\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}
.

Let us now consider the following subproblem:

\displaystyle\mathcal{P}(x_{k})=\argmin\limits_{y}\mathcal{M}_{p}(y,x):=f(y)+\dfrac{\mu_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1},

(23)

where
\mu_{p}>g(L^{e}_{p})
. Since
f
is assumed bounded from bellow, then for any fixed
x
, the function
y\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}(y,x)
is coercive and hence the optimal value
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{*}=\inf\limits_{y}\mathcal{M}_{p}(y,x)
is finite. Then, the subproblem (
23
) is equivalent to:

\displaystyle\inf\limits_{y\in\mathcal{B}_{k}}f(y)+\dfrac{\mu_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1},

for some compact set
\mathcal{B}_{k}
. Since
\mathcal{M}_{p}
is proper lower semicontinuous function in the first argument and
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is compact set, then from Weierstrass theorem we have that the infimum
\mathcal{M}_{p}^{*}
is attained, i.e., there exists
\bar{y}_{k+1}\in\mathcal{P}(x_{k})
such that
\mathcal{M}_{p}(\bar{y}_{k+1},x_{k})=\mathcal{M}_{p}^{*}
. Since the level sets of
y\mapsto\mathcal{M}_{p}(x,y)
are compact, then
\mathcal{P}(x_{k})
is nonempty and compact and one can consider the following point:

\displaystyle y_{k+1}=\argmin_{y\in\mathcal{P}(x_{k})}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert.

(24)

Let us assume that
F_{i}
admits a higher-order surrogate as in Definition
2
, where the error functions
e_{i}
are
p
smooth with Lipschitz constants
L_{p}^{e}(i)
for all
i=1:m
. Denote
L_{p}^{e}=\big(L_{p}^{e}(1),\cdots,L_{p}^{e}(m)\big)
and define the following positive constant
C_{L^{e}_{p}}^{\mu_{p}}=\dfrac{\mu_{p}}{\mu_{p}-g(L^{e}_{p})}

(
recall that
\mu_{p}
is chosen such that
\mu_{p}>g(L^{e}_{p})
)
. Next lemma shows that Assumption
2
holds provided that we compute
x_{k+1}
as an approximate local solution of subproblem (
15
) (hence,
x_{k+1}
doesn’t need to be global optimum) and
y_{k+1}
as in (
24
).

Lemma 2
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold and additionally there exists
\delta>0
such that
x_{k+1}
satisfies the following inexact optimality condition:

\displaystyle g\big(s(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k})\big)+h(x_{k+1})-\!\!\!\min_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}{x:\;\|x-x_{k}\|\leq D_{k}}}}

\displaystyle\left(g\big(s(x;\!\!x_{k})\big)+h(x)\right)

(25)

\displaystyle\qquad\leq\delta\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1},

where

. Then, Assumption
2
holds with

given in (
24
),
L^{1}_{p}=\left(C_{L^{e}_{p}}^{\mu_{p}}+\frac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta(p+1)!}}{\mu_{p}-g(L^{e}_{p})}\right)^{1/(p+1)}
and
L^{2}_{p}=\frac{\mu_{p}}{p!}
.

Proof.

From the definition of
y_{k+1}
in (
24
), we have:

\displaystyle f(y_{k+1})+\frac{\mu_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle=\min\limits_{y}f(y)+\frac{\mu_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

(26)

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k+1})+\frac{\mu_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}.

Further, taking
y=x_{k}
in (

) we also have:

\displaystyle f(y_{k+1})+\frac{\mu_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\leq f(x_{k}),

which implies that:

\displaystyle\|y_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\leq\left(\frac{(p+1)!}{\mu_{p}}(f(x_{k})-f^{*})\right)^{\frac{1}{p+1}}=D_{k}.

(27)

Note that since the error functions
e_{i}
’s have the

), we get:

\displaystyle|e_{i}(y;\!\!x_{k})-T_{p}^{e_{i}}(y;\!\!x_{k})|\leq\frac{L_{p}^{e}(i)}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\quad\forall i=1:m,\quad\forall y\in\text{dom}\;e_{i}.

From (

), the Taylor approximations of
e_{i}
’s of order

at

,

, are zero. Hence we get:

\displaystyle|s_{i}(y;\!\!x_{k})-F_{i}(y)|=|e_{i}(y;\!\!x_{k})|\leq\frac{L_{p}^{e}(i)}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\quad\forall i=1:m.

(28)

Further, since
F(x_{k+1})\leq s(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k})
(see (

)) and

is a nondecreasing function, we have:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq g\Big(s(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k})\Big)+h(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:inx}}}{{\leq}}\min_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y:\;\|y-x_{k}\|\leq D_{k}}}g\Big(s(y;\!\!x_{k})\Big)+h(y)+\delta\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:qq1}}}{{\leq}}\min_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y:\;\|y-x_{k}\|\leq D_{k}}}g\left(F(y)+\frac{L_{p}^{e}}{{p+1}!}\lVert y\!-\!x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\right)\!+\!h(y)\!+\!\delta\lVert x_{k+\!1}\!-\!x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Lemma:1}}}{{\leq}}\min_{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}y:\;\|y-x_{k}\|\leq D_{k}}}f(y)+\frac{g(L^{e}_{p})}{(p+1)!}\lVert y-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}+\delta\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:bdy}}}{{\leq}}f(y_{k+1})+\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})}{(p+1)!}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}+\delta\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}.

Then, combining the last inequality with (

), we get:

\displaystyle\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\leq\dfrac{\mu_{p}+{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta(p+1)!}}{\mu_{p}-g(L^{e}_{p})}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1},

which is the first statement of Assumption
2
. Further, using (

) and optimality conditions for

, we obtain:

\displaystyle 0\in\partial f(y_{k+1})+\frac{\mu_{p}}{p!}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p-1}B(y_{k+1}-x_{k}).

It follows that:

\displaystyle S_{f}(y_{k+1})\leq\frac{\mu_{p}}{p!}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}.

Hence, Assumption
2
holds with

given in (

),
L^{1}_{p}=\left(C_{L^{e}_{p}}^{\mu_{p}}+\frac{\delta(p+1)!}{\mu_{p}-g(L^{e}_{p})}\right)^{1/(p+1)}
and
L^{2}_{p}=\frac{\mu_{p}}{p!}
.
∎

Lemma 3

Proof.

See appendix.

From the proof of Lemma
3
one can see that
the global minimum
of subproblem (

) can be computed as:

\displaystyle x_{k+1}=x_{k}-H_{k}(u,w)^{-1}g_{k}(u),

where
H_{k}(u,w)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\nabla^{2}F_{i}(x_{k})+\frac{w}{2}I
,
g_{k}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}\nabla F_{i}(x_{k})
and
l_{k}(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}F_{i}(x_{k})
, with
(u,w)
the solution of the following dual problem:

\displaystyle\max_{(u,w)\in D}\;l_{k}(u)-\frac{1}{2}\left\langle H_{k}(u,w)^{-1}g_{k}(u),g_{k}(u)\right\rangle-\frac{1}{12(\sum_{i=1}^{m}u_{i}M_{i})^{2}}w^{3},

(29)

with
D=\left\{(u,w)\in\Delta_{m}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}:\;\;\text{s.t.}\;H_{k}(u,w)\succ 0\right\}
,
i.e., a maximization of a concave function over a convex set
D
. Hence, if
m
is not too large, this convex dual problem can be solved efficiently by interior point methods
NesNem:94
. In conclusion, GCHO algorithm can be implementable for
p=2
even for nonconvex problems, since we can effectively compute the global minimum
x_{k+1}
of subproblem (
15
) using the powerful tools from convex optimization.

Define the following constant:
D_{R_{p}^{e},L^{1,2}_{p}}=\frac{\left(L_{p}^{1}\left(L_{p}^{2}\right)^{p}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}(p+1)!}{-g(-R_{p}^{e})}
. Then, we derive the following convergence result for GCHO algorithm in the nonconvex case.

Theorem 2
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. Additionally, Assumption
2
holds. Then, for the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
generated by Algorithm GCHO we have the following sublinear convergence rate:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{j=0:k-1}S_{f}(y_{j})\leq\frac{\left(D_{R_{p}^{e},L^{1,2}_{p}}(f(x_{0})-f^{*})\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}{k^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}.

Proof.

From Assumption
2
, we have:

\displaystyle S_{f}(y_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq L_{p}^{2}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}\leq L_{p}^{2}\left(L_{p}^{1}\right)^{p}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert^{p}.

Using the descent (
16
), we get:

\displaystyle S_{f}(y_{k+1})^{\frac{p+1}{p}}\leq\frac{\left(L_{p}^{2}\left(L_{p}^{1}\right)^{p}\right)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}(p+1)!}{-g(-R_{p}^{e})}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x_{k+1})\right).

Summing the last inequality from
j=0:k-1
and taking the minimum, we get:

\displaystyle\min\limits_{j=0:k-1}S_{f}(y_{j})\leq\frac{\left(D_{R_{p}^{e},L^{1,2}_{p}}(f(x_{0})-f^{*})\right)^{\frac{p}{p+1}}}{k^{\frac{p}{p+1}}},

which proves the statement of the theorem.
∎

Theorem

requires that

satisfies the descent (

. However Assumption

, according to Lemmas

and

is an (inexact) stationary point or an inexact solution of the subproblem (

Remark 1
.

To this end, Assumption

requires an auxiliary sequence

satisfying:

\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\lVert y_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert\leq L_{p}^{1}\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert\\

(30)

If

is small, the point

is near

(recall that

converges to

but for the auxiliary sequence

\displaystyle y_{k+1}

\displaystyle=\argmin\limits_{y}\max\big(y^{2}-1,1-y^{2}\big)+2(y-x_{k})^{2}.

Then, it follows immediately that:

y_{k+1}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}\frac{2}{3}x_{k}&\text{if}\;\;x_{k}>\frac{3}{2}\\

3.3
Better rates for GCHO under KL

In this section, we show that improved rates can be derived for GCHO algorithm if the objective function satisfies the KL property. This is the first time when such convergence analysis is derived for the GCHO algorithm on the composite problem (
1
). We believe that this lack of analysis comes from the fact that, when
g
is nonsmooth and different from the identity function, one can’t bound directly the distance
S_{f}(x_{k+1})
by
\lVert x_{k+1}-x_{k}\rVert
. However, using the newly introduced (artificial) point
y_{k+1}
, we can now overcome this difficulty. Let us recall the definition of a function satisfying the
Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL)
property (see
BolDan:07
for more details).

Definition 3
.

A proper lower semicontinuous function
f:\mathbb{E}\rightarrow\bar{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies Kurdyka-Lojasiewicz (KL) property on the compact set
\Omega\subseteq\text{dom}\;f
on which
f
takes a constant value
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}
if there exist
\delta,\epsilon>0
such that one has:

\kappa^{\prime}(f(x)-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}})\cdot S_{f}(x)\geq 1\quad\forall x\!:\text{dist}(x,\Omega)\leq\delta,\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}<f(x)<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}+\epsilon,

where
\kappa:[0,\epsilon]\to\mathbb{R}
is concave differentiable function satisfying
\kappa(0)=0
and
\kappa^{\prime}>0
.

When
\kappa
takes the form
\kappa(t)=\sigma_{q}^{\frac{1}{q}}\frac{q}{q-1}t^{\frac{q-1}{q}}
, with
q>1
and
\sigma_{q}>0
(which is our interest here), the KL property establishes the following local geometry of the nonconvex function
f
around a compact set
\Omega
:

f(x)-{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}\leq\sigma_{q}S_{f}(x)^{q}\quad\forall x\!:\;\text{dist}(x,\Omega)\leq\delta,\;{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}<f(x)<{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}+\epsilon.

(31)

Note that the relevant aspect of the KL property is when
\Omega
is a subset of stationary points for
f
, i.e.
\Omega\subseteq\{x:0\in\partial f(x)\}
, since it is easy to establish the KL property when
\Omega
is not related to stationary points. The KL property holds for a large class of functions including semi-algebraic functions (e.g., real polynomial functions), vector or matrix (semi)norms (e.g.,
\|\cdot\|_{p}
with
p\geq 0
rational number), logarithm functions, exponential functions and uniformly convex functions, see
BolDan:07
for a comprehensive list. In particular, the max (sup) of semi-algebraic functions is a semi-algebraic function, see
BolSab:14
(Example 2). Let us show that if
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded, then also
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded and they have the same limit points.

Lemma 4
.

Let
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
generated by Algorithm GCHO be bounded and
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfies Assumption
2
. Then,
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded and the set of limit points of the sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
coincides with the set of limit points of
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
.

Proof.

Indeed, since
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
is bounded, then it has limit points. Let
x_{*}
be a limit point of the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. Then, there exists a subsequence
(x_{k_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
such that
x_{k_{t}}\to x_{*}
for
t\to\infty
. We have:

\displaystyle\lVert y_{k_{t}}-x_{k_{t}}\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\lVert y_{k_{t}}-x_{k_{t}-1}\rVert+\lVert x_{k_{t}}-x_{k_{t}-1}\rVert

(32)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{stp:yk}}}{{\leq}}\left(L^{1}_{p}+1\right)\lVert x_{k_{t}}-x_{k_{t}-1}\rVert\qquad\forall k\geq 0,

Since
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is bounded and
\|x_{k+1}-x_{k}\|\to 0
, then
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
is also bounded. This implies that
y_{k_{t}}\to x_{*}
. Hence,
x_{*}
is also a limit point of the sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. Further, let
y_{*}
be a limit point of the bounded sequence
\left(y_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
. Then, there exists a subsequence
(y_{\bar{k}_{t}})_{t\geq 0}
such that
y_{\bar{k}_{t}}\to y_{*}
for
t\to\infty
. Taking
t\to\infty
in an inequality similar to (
32
) and using
\lim_{t\to\infty}\|x_{\bar{k}_{t}}-x_{\bar{k}_{t}-1}\|=0
and boundedness of
(x_{k})_{k\geq 0}
, we get that
x_{\bar{k}_{t}}\to y_{*}
, i.e.,
y_{*}
is also a limit point of the sequence
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
.
∎

Note that usually for deriving convergence rates under KL condition, we need to assume that the sequence generated by the algorithm is bounded (see e.g., Theorem 1 in
BolSab:14
). Let us denote the set of limit points of

by:

\displaystyle\Omega(x_{0})=

\displaystyle\{\bar{x}\in\mathbb{E}:\exists\text{ an increasing sequence of integers }(k_{t})_{t\geq 0},

\displaystyle\text{ such that }x_{k_{t}}\to\bar{x}\text{ as }t\to\infty\},

and the set of stationary points of problem (

\text{crit}f
.

Lemma 5
.

Let the assumptions of Theorem
1
hold. Additionally, assume that
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
is bounded,
(y_{k})_{k\geq 0}
satisfies Assumption
2
and

is continuous. Then, we have:
\emptyset\neq\Omega(x_{0})\subseteq\text{crit}f
,
\Omega(x_{0})
is compact and connected set, and

is constant on
\Omega(x_{0})
, i.e.,
f(\Omega(x_{0}))=f_{*}
.

Proof.

First let us show that

is constant. From (
16
) we have that
\left(f(x_{k})\right)_{k\geq 0}
is monotonically decreasing and since
f

{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}>-\infty

f(x_{k})\to{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}f_{*}}

. On the other hand let

In conclusion, we have

\displaystyle\Omega(x_{0})=\cap_{q\geq 0}\overline{\cup_{k\geq q}\{x_{k}\}},

∎

Lemma 6

3.4
Convex convergence analysis

In this section, we assume that the objective function
f
in (
1
) is convex. Since the problem (
1
) is convex, we assume that
x_{k+1}
is a global minimum of the subproblem (
15
), which is convex provided that
M_{p}
is sufficiently large (see Theorem 1 in
Nes:20
). Below, we also assume that the level sets of
f
are bounded. Since GCHO algorithm is a descent method, this implies that there exist a constant
R_{0}>0
such that
\lVert x_{k}-x^{*}\rVert\leq R_{0}
for all
k\geq 0
, where
x^{*}
is an optimal solution of (
1
). Then, we get the following sublinear rate for GCHO algorithm.

Theorem 4
.

Let
F
,
g
and
h
satisfy Assumption
1
and additionally each
F_{i}
admits a
p
higher-order surrogate
s_{i}
as in Definition
2
with the constants
L^{e}_{p}(i)
and
R_{p}^{e}(i)
, for
i=1:m
. Additionally,
f
is a convex function and has bounded level sets. Let
\left(x_{k}\right)_{k\geq 0}
be the sequence generated by Algorithm GCHO,
R_{p}^{e}=\big(R_{p}^{e}(1),\cdots,R_{p}^{e}(m)\big)
and
L^{e}_{p}=\big(L^{e}_{p}(1),\cdots,L^{e}_{p}(m)\big)
. Then, we have the following convergence rate:

\displaystyle f(x_{k})-f(x^{*})\leq\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}(p+1)^{p}}{p!k^{p}}.

Proof.

Since
F(x_{k+1})\leq S(x_{k+1};\!\!x_{k})
(see (
11
)) and
g
is nondecreasing, we have:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq g\big(s(x_{k+1};\!x_{k})\big)+h(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:op_cond}}}{{=}}\min\limits_{x}g\big(s(x;\!\!x_{k})\big)+h(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:qq1}}}{{\leq}}\min\limits_{x}g\left(F(x)+\dfrac{L^{e}_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}\right)+h(x).

Hence we get:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{Lemma:1}}}{{\leq}}\min\limits_{x}g\big(F(x)\big)+\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}+h(x)

\displaystyle=\min\limits_{x}f(x)+\frac{g(L_{p}^{e})}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-x_{k}\rVert^{p+1}

\displaystyle\leq\min\limits_{\alpha\in[0,1]}f(x_{k})+\alpha\big[(f(x^{*})-f(x_{k})\big]+\alpha^{p+1}\dfrac{R_{0}^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}g\left(L^{e}_{p}\right),

where the last inequality follows from the convexity of
f
and the boundness of the level sets of
f
.
The minimum in
\alpha\geq 0
is achieved at:

\displaystyle\alpha^{*}=\left(\dfrac{f(x_{k})-f(x^{*})p!}{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.

We have
0\leq\alpha^{*}<1
. Indeed, since
\big(f(x_{k})\big)_{k\geq 0}
is decreasing, we have:

\displaystyle f(x_{k})\leq f(x_{1})

\displaystyle\leq g\big(s(x_{1};\!\!x_{0})\big)+h(x_{1})

\displaystyle=\min\limits_{x}g\big(s(x;\!\!x_{0})\big)+h(x)

\displaystyle\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:qq1}}}{{\leq}}\min\limits_{x}g\left(F(x)+\frac{L^{e}_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x-x_{0}\rVert^{p+1}\right)+h(x)

\displaystyle\leq g\left(F(x^{*})+\frac{L^{e}_{p}}{(p+1)!}\lVert x^{*}-x_{0}\rVert^{p+1}\right)+h(x^{*})

\displaystyle\leq f(x^{*})+\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}.

Hence:

\displaystyle 0\leq\alpha^{*}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{\big(f(x_{1})-f(x^{*})\big)p!}{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\leq\left(\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}p!}{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}(p+1)!}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{p!}{(p+1)!}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}=\left(\dfrac{{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}1}}{p+1}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}<1.

Thus, we conclude:

\displaystyle f(x_{k+1})

\displaystyle\leq f(x_{k})-\alpha^{*}\left(f(x_{k})-f(x^{*})-\frac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{(p+1)!}(\alpha^{*})^{p}\right)

\displaystyle=f(x_{k})-\dfrac{p\alpha^{*}}{p+1}\big[f(x_{k})-f(x^{*})\big].

Denoting
\delta_{k}=f(x_{k})-f(x^{*})
, we get the following estimate:

\displaystyle\delta_{k}-\delta_{k+1}\geq C\delta_{k}^{\frac{p+1}{p}},

where
{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}C=\frac{p}{p+1}\left(\dfrac{p!}{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}}
​​. Thus, for
\mu_{k}\!=\!C^{p}\delta_{k}
we get the following recurence:

\displaystyle\mu_{k}-\mu_{k+1}\geq\mu_{k}^{\frac{p+1}{p}}.

Following the same proof as in
Nes:20
(Theorem 4), we get:

\displaystyle\dfrac{1}{\mu_{k}}\geq\left(\dfrac{1}{\mu_{1}^{\frac{1}{p}}}+\dfrac{k-1}{p}\right)^{p}.

Since

\dfrac{1}{\mu_{1}^{\frac{1}{p}}}=\dfrac{1}{C{\color[rgb]{0,0,0}\delta_{1}}^{\frac{1}{p}}}=\frac{p+1}{p}\left(\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{p!(f(x_{1})-f^{*})}\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}\geq\frac{1}{p}(p+1)^{\dfrac{p+1}{p}},

then

\displaystyle\delta_{k}=C^{-p}\mu_{k}

\displaystyle=\left(\dfrac{p+1}{p}\right)^{p}\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{p!}\mu_{k}

\displaystyle\leq\left(\dfrac{p+1}{p}\right)^{p}\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{p!}\left(\frac{1}{p}(p+1)^{\frac{p+1}{p}}+\dfrac{k-1}{p}\right)^{-p}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{p!}\left((p+1)^{\frac{1}{p}}+\dfrac{k-1}{p+1}\right)^{-p}\leq\dfrac{(p+1)^{p}g(L^{e}_{p})R_{0}^{p+1}}{p!k^{p}}.

This proves the statement of the theorem.
∎

Note that in the convex case the convergence results from
DruPaq:19
;

p

4
