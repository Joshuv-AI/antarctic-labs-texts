Genus two Goeritz equivalence in S 3 S^{3}
Brandy Doleshal, Matt Rathbun

Abstract
. The Goeritz group of a genus g g Heegaard splitting of a 3-manifold is the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving automorphisms of the manifold that preserve the Heegaard splitting. In the context of the standard genus 2 Heegaard splitting of S 3 S^{3} , we introduce the concept of Goeritz equivalence of curves, present two algebraic obstructions to Goeritz equivalence of simple closed curves that are straightforward to compute, and provide families of examples demonstrating how these obstructions may be used.



1.
Introduction

The Goeritz group of a genus
g
Heegaard splitting of a 3-manifold was defined by Goeritz
[
12
]
as the group of isotopy classes of orientation-preserving automorphisms of the manifold preserving the Heegaard splitting (set-wise). Scharlemann
[
16
]
, Akbas
[
1
]
, Cho
[
5
]
, and Powell
[
15
]
have done work to define generators for the Goeritz group in order to solve a foundational question of whether the Goeritz group for the genus
g
splitting of
S^{3}
is finitely generated.

We focus on the Goeritz group in the setting of a genus
2
Heegaard splitting,
F
, of
S^{3}
. In
[
4
]
, Berge finds distinct primitive/primitive knots that have the same lens space surgery at the same surface slope. It is unknown whether there exist pairs of distinct primitive/Seifert knots that have the same Seifert fibered space surgery at the same surface slope. However in
[
13
]
,
[
8
]
and
[
2
]
, the authors, along with Evan Amoranto, find infinite families of primitive/Seifert knots that have distinct positions on the genus 2 surface. Infinite families with such distinct positions were also found by Eudave-Muñoz, Miyazaki, and Motegi
[
10
]
. While this previous work was in the service of understanding Dehn surgery, we zoom out from the specialized primitive/primitive and primitive/Seifert constructions to consider any non-separating curve in the standard genus 2 Heegaard surface,
F
, for
S^{3}
. However, in light of this context, we will also allow for automorphisms that exchange the two handlebodies, considering the
extended Goeritz group
.

We seek to determine when there exist elements of the Goeritz group, or the extended Goeritz group, that send one given curve in the standard genus 2 Heegaard splitting of
S^{3}
to another. If such an element from the Goeritz group exists, we call the two curves
Goeritz equivalent
.

Observe that two curves that are Goeritz equivalent represent the same knot, but that the same knot may be represented by multiple Goeritz equivalence classes. In particular, the authors’ previous work with primitive/Seifert knots shows that primitive/Seifert knots can belong to two distinct Goeritz equivalence classes. Understanding Goeritz equivalence and the Goeritz equivalence classes can lead to understanding questions about distinct representatives of primitive/Seifert knots and much more. The goal of this work is to move toward a better understanding of Goeritz equivalence.

To that end, we establish a simple quantity called the
split product
, that computes the surface slope of a curve.

Theorem 1.1
.

Suppose
K\subset F
is a non-separating simple closed curve. The split product of
[K]\in H_{1}(F)
with respect to any slope basis is equal to the surface slope of
K
with respect to
F
.

We then provide two algebraic obstructions to Goeritz equivalence.

The first is homological and arithmetic. We will say that two curves are
homologically Goeritz equivalent
if there is an element of the Goeritz group whose induced action on homology takes the homology class of the first curve to that of the second. Equivalently,
K
is homologically Goeritz equivalent to
K^{\prime}
if there is a Goeritz group element that takes
K
to a curve homologous to
K^{\prime}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Suppose
K
and
K^{\prime}
are non-separating nontrivial simple closed curves in the standard genus two Heegaard surface,
F
, for
S^{3}
with non-zero surface slope, and suppose
[K]=(a,x,b,y)^{T}
and
[K^{\prime}]=(a^{\prime},x^{\prime},b^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{T}
with respect to any slope basis. Then
K
and
K^{\prime}
are homologically Goeritz equivalent if and only if:

(1)

gcd(a,x)=gcd(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})
,

(2)

gcd(b,y)=gcd(b^{\prime},y^{\prime})
, and

(3)

ab+xy=a^{\prime}b^{\prime}+x^{\prime}y^{\prime}
,

and either

(4)

a^{\prime}b+xy^{\prime}
,

(5)

a^{\prime}y-ay^{\prime}
,

(6)

bx^{\prime}-b^{\prime}x,
and

(7)

x^{\prime}y+ab^{\prime}

are all multiples of
ab+xy
, or

(4
′
)

a^{\prime}b-xy^{\prime}
,

(5
′
)

a^{\prime}y+ay^{\prime}
,

(6
′
)

bx^{\prime}+b^{\prime}x,
and

(7
′
)

x^{\prime}y-ab^{\prime}

are all multiples of
ab+xy
.

To determine Goeritz equivalence of two simple closed curves, then, it suffices to consider equivalence between homologous curves. The next obstruction is a matter of homotopy, relative to the two handlebodies bounded by
F
,
H
and
H^{\prime}
, separately.

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose
K
and
K^{\prime}
are homologous simple closed curves with non-zero surface slopes. If
K
is not freely homotopic to
K^{\prime}
in
H
or if
K
is not freely homotopic to
K^{\prime}
in
H^{\prime}
, then
K
and
K^{\prime}
are not Goeritz equivalent.

2.1

2.2

*:\mathcal{G}\to GL(4,\mathbb{Z}),

\alpha_{*}
,
\beta_{*}
,

Proposition 2.1
.

Two curves

are (homologically) related by an extended Goeritz group element if and only if they are (homologically) Goeritz equivalent, or if

As the actions of the (non-extended) Goeritz group on homology act separately (though not independently) on the first two and the last two factors of the homology vector, the following lemmas will be of great use.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\textbf{\emph{v}}=(a,x)^{T}
, and
\textbf{\emph{v}}^{\prime}=(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})^{T}
be non-zero vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. There exists a matrix
G\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
so that
G\textbf{\emph{v}}=\textbf{\emph{v}}^{\prime}
if and only if
\gcd(a,x)=\gcd(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})
.

Proof.

First, suppose that
G=\begin{pmatrix}s&t\\
u&v\end{pmatrix}\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
and
G\textbf{v}=\textbf{v}^{\prime}
. Call
d=\gcd(a,x)
and
d^{\prime}=\gcd(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})
. By Bezout’s Identity, there exist
e_{1},e_{2},e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
so that
e_{1}a+e_{2}x=d
, and
e_{1}^{\prime}a^{\prime}+e_{2}^{\prime}x^{\prime}=d^{\prime}
. Now,
G\textbf{v}=\textbf{v}^{\prime}
implies that
a^{\prime}=sa+tx
and
x^{\prime}=ua+vx
. Then,

\displaystyle d^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}a^{\prime}+e_{2}^{\prime}x^{\prime}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle e_{1}^{\prime}(sa+tx)+e_{2}^{\prime}(ua+vx)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle(e_{1}^{\prime}s+e_{2}^{\prime}u)a+(e_{1}^{\prime}t+e_{2}^{\prime}v)x.

Thus, by a corollary to Bezout’s Identity,
d
divides
d^{\prime}
. Since
G
is invertible, this argument can be reversed to show that
d^{\prime}
divides
d
, and hence
d=d^{\prime}
.

Conversely, suppose that
\gcd(a,x)=\gcd(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})=d
. Then there exist
k,l,k^{\prime},l^{\prime}\in\mathbb{N}
so that
a=kd
,
x=ld
,
a^{\prime}=k^{\prime}d
, and
x^{\prime}=l^{\prime}d
. Now, observe that
\gcd(k,l)=\gcd(k^{\prime},l^{\prime})=1
, and there exist
e_{1},e_{2},e_{1}^{\prime},e_{2}^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}
so that
e_{1}k+e_{2}l=1=e_{1}^{\prime}k^{\prime}+e_{2}^{\prime}l^{\prime}
.

Let
M=\begin{pmatrix}k&-e_{2}\\
l&e_{1}\end{pmatrix}
, which is evidently in
GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
. Then, note that

\displaystyle M\begin{pmatrix}d\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}k&-e_{2}\\
l&e_{1}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
0\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}kd\\
ld\end{pmatrix}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\begin{pmatrix}a\\
x\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly,
M^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}k^{\prime}&-e_{2}^{\prime}\\
l^{\prime}&e_{1}^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
and
M^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}d\\
0\end{pmatrix}=\textbf{v}^{\prime}
, so
G=M^{\prime}M^{-1}
is the desired matrix.
∎

Corollary 2.3

For any knot

[K]=(a,x,b,y)^{T}

Proof.

Let

, and
((C^{-1})^{T})(b,y)^{T}
, then by Lemma

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{1}=(a,x)^{T}
and
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}=(b,y)^{T}
are non-orthogonal vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, and
\textbf{\emph{v}}^{\prime}_{1}=(a^{\prime},x^{\prime})^{T}
and
\textbf{\emph{v}}_{2}^{\prime}=(b^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{T}
are any vectors in
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, there are at most
two
4\times 4
Goeritz matrices
M
over
\mathbb{Z}
so that
M\cdot(a,x,b,y)^{T}=(a^{\prime},x^{\prime},b^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{T}
.

Proof.

Suppose there is a matrix

A=\begin{pmatrix}s&t\\
u&v\end{pmatrix}
, so that

M=\begin{pmatrix}A&0\\
0&\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}\end{pmatrix}
has the property that

M\cdot(a,x,b,y)^{T}=(a^{\prime},x^{\prime},b^{\prime},y^{\prime})^{T}.

Since
\left(A^{T}\right)^{-1}=\frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{pmatrix}v&-u\\
-t&s\end{pmatrix}
, we have

\frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{pmatrix}v&-u\\
-t&s\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}b\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}b^{\prime}\\
y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}.

Together with the equations coming from
A\textbf{v}_{1}=\textbf{v}^{\prime}_{1}
, we have:

\displaystyle\frac{(vb-uy)}{d}=b^{\prime}

\displaystyle\frac{(-tb+sy)}{d}=y^{\prime}

\displaystyle sa+tx=a^{\prime}

\displaystyle ua+vx=x^{\prime},

where
d=\det(A)
. As we require that
A\in GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
,
d
must be
\pm 1
.

This is equivalent to the matrix equation

\begin{pmatrix}\frac{y}{d}&-\frac{b}{d}&0&0\\
a&x&0&0\\
0&0&\frac{-y}{d}&\frac{b}{d}\\
0&0&a&x\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}s\\
t\\
u\\
v\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}y^{\prime}\\
a^{\prime}\\
b^{\prime}\\
x^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}
.

As
(a,x)
and
(b,y)
are non-orthogonal, each of the
2\times 2
submatrices is invertible over
\mathbb{Q}
. So for each of
d=\pm 1
, there is a unique solution for
s,t,u,
and
v
over
\mathbb{Q}
.
In particular, for
d=1
and
d=-1
,

\begin{pmatrix}s&t\\
u&v\end{pmatrix}=\frac{1}{ab+xy}\begin{pmatrix}xy^{\prime}d+a^{\prime}b\ &-ay^{\prime}d+a^{\prime}y\\
-b^{\prime}xd+bx^{\prime}&ab^{\prime}d+x^{\prime}y\end{pmatrix}.

Note that while these matrices will never be equal, either of them may be in
GL(2,\mathbb{Q})
but not
GL(2,\mathbb{Z})
.
∎

In fact, the determinants of the matrices found in Lemma
2.4
can be computed, showing that

d=d\frac{a^{\prime}b^{\prime}+x^{\prime}y^{\prime}}{ab+xy}.

This hints at the importance of the quantity

Definition 2.5

Let

A symplectic form,

on

\omega(\textbf{v},\textbf{v}^{\prime})=\sum\limits_{i=1}^{n}(v_{i}w_{i}^{\prime}-v_{i}^{\prime}w_{i}),

for
\textbf{v}=(v_{1},w_{1},\dots,v_{n},w_{n})^{T}
,
\textbf{v}^{\prime}=(v_{1}^{\prime},w_{1}^{\prime},\dots,v_{n}^{\prime},w_{n}^{\prime})^{T}
, and it is unique up to change of basis of
\mathbb{R}^{2n}
.

Now, the algebraic intersection number,

\hat{\iota}:H_{1}(F;\mathbb{Z})\wedge H_{1}(F;\mathbb{Z})\to\mathbb{Z}

extends uniquely to a symplectic form on

is a collection of oriented simple closed curves that form a symplectic basis. Such a geometric symplectic basis is formed by re-ordering the curves of our standard basis from Figure

If an essential (separating) curve on the Heegaard surface

3.1.
The condition of non-zero surface slope

The hypothesis that

as well, but the infinite list exhibits a theoretical problem with any kind of systematic search.

Or slightly less trivially, let

\textbf{k}=[K]=(0,0,0,1)^{T}

be the curve resulting from the band sum of

Even when conditions (

For the curves

.

\displaystyle A\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-1}}A\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-2}}\cdots A\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{1}}A\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-1}-1}X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-2}-1}\cdots X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{1}-1}X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{0}-1}.

Observe that regardless of the values of
p(q,m)
, this will always be a word with a single
X
on the left, followed by an alternating pattern of
\overline{A}
and some powers of
X
, ending on
(\overline{A}X)^{p_{0}-1}
on the right. We will eventually simplify and standardize this word by cycling the right-most power of
X
to the left, and record the powers of
X
as a sequence
\left(\chi^{1}_{n}\right)
.

Lemma 3.7
.

\chi^{1}_{n}=\widetilde{p}_{n}
for all
0\leq n\leq q-m-1
.

Proof.

We will treat two cases separately. They are in some very strong sense dual cases, but the analysis required is slightly different in each case.

Case 1:
d>1

In this case, all of the
p_{n}\geq 2
, so the powers appearing in the word
[g(K_{1})]_{H}
are all at least 1, and
\chi^{1}_{n}\in\left\{1,2\right\}
for all
n
, and the word becomes,

\displaystyle X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-1}-1}X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-2}-1}\cdots X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{1}-1}X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{0}-1}

\displaystyle=X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-1}-2}\left(\overline{A}X\right)X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-2}-2}\left(\overline{A}X\right)\cdots X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{1}-2}\left(\overline{A}X\right)X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{0}-2}\left(\overline{A}X\right)

\displaystyle=X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-1}-2}\left(\overline{A}X^{2}\right)\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{m-2}-2}\left(\overline{A}X^{2}\right)\cdots\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{1}-2}\left(\overline{A}X^{2}\right)\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{p_{0}-2}\left(\overline{A}X\right).

Analyzing the word after the right-most
X
is cycled to the left, we can see that
\chi^{1}_{n}=2
exactly when
n
is equal to

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle p_{m-1}-1,

\displaystyle p_{m-1}+p_{m-2}-2,

\displaystyle p_{m-1}+p_{m-2}+p_{m-3}-3,

\displaystyle p_{m-1}+p_{m-2}+\cdots+p_{2}-(m-2),\mbox{ or}

\displaystyle p_{m-1}+p_{m-2}+\cdots+p_{3}+p_{1}-(m-1).

In light of Lemma
3.5
, this is the same as
n
equal to

\displaystyle 0,

\displaystyle p_{0}-2,

\displaystyle p_{0}+p_{1}-3,

\displaystyle p_{0}+p_{1}+p_{2}-4,

\displaystyle p_{0}+p_{1}+\cdots p_{j}-(j+2),

\displaystyle p_{0}+p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m-3}-(m-1),\mbox{ or}

\displaystyle p_{0}+p_{1}+\cdots+p_{m-2}+p_{m-2}-m.

This is precisely when
\widetilde{p}_{n}=2
according to Lemma
3.6
.

Case 2:
d=1

Now
p_{n}\in\left\{1,2\right\}
for all
0\leq n\leq m-1
. Suppose
\left(p_{n_{i}}\right)
for
0\leq i\leq q-m-1
is the subsequence of
2
’s. We note that
X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{0}=X
, while
X\left(\overline{A}X\right)^{1}=X\overline{A}X
, so for all
1\leq i\leq q-m-1
,

\chi^{1}_{0}=1+m-n_{q-m-1},

and

\chi^{1}_{q-m-i}=1+(n_{i}-n_{i-1}).

In other words, the sequence
\left(\chi^{1}_{n}\right)
is the reverse of the sequence whose terms are the sum of each 2 in
\left(p_{n}\right)
with all subsequent 1’s before the next 2.

This is precisely
\widetilde{p}_{n}
by the second statement from, and Remark
6
following, Lemma
3.6
.
∎

Turning now to the other curve, we have a much more straightforward relationship between the word and the sequence
\widetilde{p}_{n}(q,m)
. Each purple strand contributes an
X
, and each pink strand contributes an
\overline{A}
, so that the word
[\varepsilon(K_{2})]_{H}
will be

\overline{A}X^{\widetilde{p}_{q-m-1}}\overline{A}X^{\widetilde{p}_{q-m-2}}\cdots\overline{A}X^{\widetilde{p}_{1}}\overline{A}X^{\widetilde{p}_{0}}.

This will always be a word with a single
\overline{A}
on the left, followed by an alternating pattern of some powers of
X
and
\overline{A}
. Again, we will simplify and standardize this word, this time by cycling the left-most
\overline{A}
to the right, so as to match the form of
[g(K_{1})]_{H}
, and record the powers of
X
as a sequence
\left(\chi^{2}_{n}\right)
. It is apparent that
\left(\chi^{2}_{n}\right)
is precisely the reverse of
\left(\widetilde{p}_{n}\right)
.

Then Lemma
3.7
shows that the words
[g(K_{1})]_{H}
and
[\varepsilon(K_{2})]_{H}
are mirror images of each other, so by Lemma
3.4
,
g(K_{1})
and
\varepsilon(K_{2})
represent the same free homotopy class in
\pi_{1}(H)
.

Next, we consider the outside handlebody
H^{\prime}
. Here, each blue strand of
g(K_{1})
contributes an
BY^{2}
, and each red strand contributes a
\overline{Y}\overline{B}
, so that the word
[g(K_{1})]_{H^{\prime}}
will be

\displaystyle\overline{Y}\overline{B}(BY^{2})^{p_{m-1}}\overline{Y}\overline{B}(BY^{2})^{p_{m-2}}\cdots\overline{Y}\overline{B}(BY^{2})^{p_{1}}\overline{Y}\overline{B}(BY^{2})^{p_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle Y(BY^{2})^{p_{m-1}-1}Y(BY^{2})^{p_{m-2}-1}\cdots Y(BY^{2})^{p_{1}-1}Y(BY^{2})^{p_{0}-1}.

Observe that regardless of the values of
(p_{n})
, this will always be a word with a power of
Y
on the left, followed by an alternating pattern of
B
and some powers of
Y
, ending on
Y^{2}
on the right. We will simplify and standardize this word by cycling the right-most
Y^{2}
to the left, and record the powers of
Y
as a sequence
\left(\Upsilon^{1}_{n}\right)
.

Lemma 3.8
.

\Upsilon^{1}_{n}=\widetilde{p}_{n}+1
for all
0\leq n\leq q-m-1
.

Proof.

The word
[\varepsilon(K_{2})]_{H^{\prime}}
has exactly the same structure as
[g(K_{1})]_{H}
, with
Y^{3}
in place of
X^{2}
, and
B
in place of
\overline{A}
, so
\Upsilon^{1}_{n}=\widetilde{p}_{n}+1
for all
0\leq n\leq q-m-1
.
∎

And again, the word representing the second curve,
\varepsilon(K_{2})
, has a much more straightforward relationship with the sequence
\left(\widetilde{p}_{n}\right)
. Each purple strand contributes a
Y
, and each pink strand contributes a
YB
, so that the word
[\varepsilon(K_{2})]_{H^{\prime}}
will be

\displaystyle YBY^{\widetilde{p}_{q-m-1}}YBY^{\widetilde{p}_{q-m-2}}\cdots YBY^{\widetilde{p}_{1}}YBY^{\widetilde{p}_{0}}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle YBY^{\widetilde{p}_{q-m-1}+1}BY^{\widetilde{p}_{q-m-2}+1}\cdots BY^{\widetilde{p}_{1}+1}BY^{\widetilde{p}_{0}}.

Cycling the left-most
YB
to the right, we obtain an alternating pattern of
B
and powers of
Y
, starting with a power of
Y
on the left and ending in a
B
, matching the form of
[g(K_{1})]_{H^{\prime}}
, and we’ll record the powers of
Y
as
\left(\Upsilon^{2}_{n}\right)
. It is clear that
\left(\Upsilon^{2}_{n}\right)
is the reverse of the sequence
\left(\widetilde{p}_{n}+1\right)
. Then, by Lemmas
3.8
and
3.4
,
g(K_{1})
and
\varepsilon(K_{2})
represent the same free homotopy class in
\pi_{1}(H^{\prime})
.

3.3.3.
The homotopy obstruction is conclusive

It is worth noting that Dehn twisting
g(K_{1})
, say, around any separating curve that is
not
a reducing sphere curve will provide examples of curves that are still homologous, but are not Goeritz equivalent due to Theorem
1.3
’s obstruction.

It would be ideal if the homotopy conditions of Theorem
1.3
were sufficient to conclude Goeritz equivalence as well.
