Signature asymptotics, empirical processes, and optimal transport
Thomas Cass, Remy Messadene, William F. Turner

Abstract
Rough path theory [ 16 ] provides one with the notion of signature, a graded family of tensors which characterise, up to a negligible equivalence class, and ordered stream of vector-valued data. In this article, we lay down the theoretical foundations for a connection between signature asymptotics, the theory of empirical processes, and Wasserstein distances, opening up the landscape and toolkit of the second and third in the study of the first. Our main contribution is to show that the Hilbert-Schmidt norm of the signature can be reinterpreted as a statement about the asymptotic behaviour of Wasserstein distances between two independent empirical measures of samples from the same underlying distribution. In the setting studied here, these measures are derived from samples from a probability distribution which is directly determined by geometrical properties of the underlying path. The general question of rates of convergence for these objects has been studied in depth in the recent monograph of Bobkov and Ledoux [ 2 ] . To illustrate this new connection, we show how the above main result can be used to prove a more general version of the original asymptotic theorem of Hambly and Lyons [ 20 ] . We conclude by providing an explicit way to compute that limit in terms of a second-order differential equation.

1.2
Contributions

In this article, we contribute to the effort of recovering the original path from its signature by laying down a novel route. We do so by explicitly relating Hilbert-Schmidt norm of projected signatures with
p
-Wasserstein distances between discrete probability measures, allowing the study of the former using tools of the latter. These measures are characterised in terms of
\gamma
only through an integral equation, making the contribution of the geometrical properties of
\gamma
(such as its curvature) in the limit of the norm explicit. To ease notation, from this point forwards, we consider unit speed paths parameterised on
[0.1]
rather than
[0,l]
.

The core insight of this connection originated when realising that a theorem by del Barrio, Giné, and Utze (see
Theorem

2
below) can be recasted to re-express the Hambly-Lyons limit
c(\gamma)
as the limit of a
2
-Wasserstein distance between empirical measures (
Section

2
). Formally, for a twice-continuously-differentiable unit speed path
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
that is regular enough (see exact conditions in
Proposition

10
), we construct and show the existence of a measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}
, prescribed in terms of the following integral equation for its cumulative distribution function

,

\displaystyle F(t)=\int_{0}^{t}|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{F(s)}|^{-1}ds\geq ct,\quad\lim_{s\rightarrow 0^{+}}F(s)=0,\quad\lim_{s\rightarrow b^{-}}F(s)=1,

(10)

for some constants

. When coupled with the B-G-U Theorem, we show that

\displaystyle c(\gamma)=\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp(-nW_{2}^{2}(\mu_{n},\mu))\right],

(11)

i.e. the Hambly-Lyons limit

can be seen as the limit of
2
-Wasserstein distances between
\mu
and an empirical version
\mu_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{F^{-1}(U_{i})}
, where

denote the Dirac delta distribution and where

is a sample of

.

In

and a series of

-Wasserstein distances. As a first application, we re-derive a generalised version of the Hambly-Lyons Limit Theorem (

Theorem

(Generalised Hambly-Lyons Limit Theorem)
.

Let
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a twice-continuously- differentiable unit-speed path such that the map

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}(B_{0,s})^{2}\left|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}\right|^{2}ds\right)\right]^{1/2}.

\left|\gamma^{\prime\prime}\right|
is bounded from below (since it is continuous and non-vanishing) and
F^{\prime\prime}
is bounded from above, it follows that conditions
C
and
D
are both satisfied.
∎

The above result states that a twice-differentiable solution
F
prescribed by the integral equation
Eq.

25

F^{\prime\prime}
is bounded, is the cumulative distribution function of a measure
\mu
required for the application of
Corollary

5
. We now determine the conditions on
\gamma
for which the existence of such an

is guaranteed. Let
\gamma
be as in
Lemma

7
. Then the following observations can be made.

1.

Since
\gamma
has bounded curvature, i.e. there exists a constant
c>0
such that

for all
s\in[0,1]
(or equivalently said, if the map
s\mapsto|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}|^{-1}
is bounded by below by

\displaystyle F(t)\geq ct\implies F(b)=1

(27)

for a constant

as
F
is monotone increasing.

2.

Additionally, if the map
s\mapsto|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}|^{-1}
is Lipschitz continuous, the Picard-Lindelöf Theorem ensures the existence of a unique differentiable function
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[a,b]\rightarrow[0,1]
such that

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(t)=|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{F(t)}|^{-1}>c,\quad F(a)=0.

(28)

3.

To ensure that
F
is twice differentiable, requiring Lipschitz continuity on

is not enough. Indeed, the latter assumption only implies the differentiability of

almost everywhere
on any open subset of the definition domain by virtue of the Rademacher’s Theorem (which is not sufficient as it can lead to the breaking of condition

to be differentiable, then

is guaranteed to be twice-differentiable for all

by the quotient rule and non-vanishing property of

.

4.

As it is now assumed that

is differentiable, the fundamental theorem of calculus implies that the probability density function

can be exactly written as

\displaystyle f(s)=|\gamma_{F(s)}^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\overset{\eqref{eq:ODE_F}}{\geq}c_{+},\quad s\in(a,b).

(29)

Additionally, since
\gamma^{\prime\prime}
is non vanishing, the curvature
|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}|
is bounded from below, i.e.
|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}|\geq\frac{1}{c_{-}}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
for all
s\in[0,1]
(or equivalently said, if the map
s\mapsto|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}|^{-1}
is bounded above by
c_{-}
), then the ODE
Eq.

28
implies

\displaystyle F^{\prime}(t)\leq c_{-}.

(30)

Assuming further that the derivative of the map

is bounded implies the boundedness of
F^{\prime\prime}
by the chain and quotient rules.

These observations are collected and combined with the
Lemma

7
in the following lemma.

Lemma 8

(Condition on
\gamma
for existence of admissible
F
)
.

Let
\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a twice-continuously-differentiable unit-speed path such that the map

is non-vanishing, and is differentiable with bounded derivative. Then there exists constants
a,b\in\mathbb{R}
twice-differentiable function

such that the integral equation and boundary conditions

25

Corollary

5
are also satisfied.

Remark 9

(Boundedness of the derivative of

as a condition on the curvature)
.

Observe that the boundedness condition on

(which we recall is there to ensure the fulfillment of the boundary conditions in equation

) can be reformulated as a condition on the total curvature

of

as follows. For a
c_{2}\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,

\displaystyle c_{2}

\displaystyle\geq\left(|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{-1}\right)^{\prime}=-\frac{|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{\prime}}{|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}}=-\bigg[\log|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|\bigg]^{\prime}\cdot|\gamma^{\prime\prime}_{s}|^{-1}

(31)

\displaystyle\iff|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|

\displaystyle\geq-\frac{1}{c_{2}}\bigg[\log|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|\bigg]^{\prime}

(32)

Taking the integral from

to

gives

\displaystyle\frac{1}{c_{2}}\log\left(\frac{|\gamma_{0}^{\prime\prime}|}{|\gamma_{t}^{\prime\prime}|}\right)\leq T(t),\quad\forall t\in[0,1],

(33)

Finally, the direct application of the existence
Lemma

8
followed by
Theorem

2

Proposition 10

(Hambly-Lyons limit as the limit of a Wasserstein distance)
.

Let
\gamma
be as in

8
and
F

25
. Let

be the measure associated with

Then, the Hambly-Lyons limit in

3
Signature projections and Wasserstein distances

Because of the known connection between the Hambly-Lyons limit and the limit of the Hilbert-Schmidt tensor norm of projected signatures
[
20
]
, the insights developed in the previous section naturally lead one to ask whether the Hilbert-Schmidt tensor norm of projected signatures can be related to Wasserstein distances. This section answers this question positively. By using this relationship, we are able characterise a class of curves larger than the
C^{3}
one originally considered in
[
20
]
that satisfies

\displaystyle\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}=c(\gamma).

(36)

We proceed as follows.

1.

First, in
Section

3.1
, we prove a technical augmentation of a lemma in
[
20
]
and then derive a probabilistic representation for the inner product of two signature terms.

2.

Once this is done,
Section

3.2
will exploit the characterisation of the Wasserstein distances between empirical measures to relate the quantities derived in the first step to these Wasserstein distances and hence derive lower and upper bounds on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)\right\rVert}
in terms of the former.

3.

By leveraging the results of the previous section, we generalise the Hambly-Lyons limit
Eq.

8
in
Section

3.4
and present the proof of
Theorem

22
.

4.

Finally, we show a practical way to compute the limit in
Section

3.5
and illustrate it in a simple case.

3.1
A probabilistic expression for signature inner products

In this subsection, we generalise Lemma 3.9 in
[
20
]
to the inner product between signatures before presenting a probabilistic formula in terms of the angles between the derivatives of the two underlying curves.

Definition 11

(Uniform order statistics sample)
.

Let
\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
and
\{V_{i}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
be two independent collections of
n
i.i.d. uniform random variables in
[0,1]
. Consider the relabeling
\{U_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
where

0\leq U_{(1)}\leq U_{(2)}\leq...\leq U_{(n)}\leq 1.

(37)

and similarly for
\{V_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
.
In the rest of this article, we will denote by
\{U_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
and
\{V_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
two independent collections of
i.i.d. uniform order statistics
on
[0,1]
.

Lemma 12

(Generalisation of Lemma 3.9 in
[
20
]
)
.

Let
\gamma,\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,1]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
be two
C^{1}
unit-speed curves and let
\{U_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
and
\{V_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,2...,n\}}
be two i.i.d. uniform order statistics collections (
Definition

11
). Then,

\displaystyle\langle S(\gamma)^{n},S(\sigma)^{n}\rangle=\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle\bigg]

(38)

Proof.

It is known
[
20
]
that

\displaystyle n!S(\gamma)^{n}=\mathbb{E}\left[\bigotimes_{i=1}^{n}\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}\right]

(39)

Hence, for an orthonormal basis
\left\{e_{\rho}\right\}_{\rho\in\{1,..,n\}}
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\displaystyle(n)^{2}\langle S(\gamma)^{n},S(\sigma)^{n}\rangle

\displaystyle\overset{\eqref{eq:kth_order_sig_as_uniform}}{=}\mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle\right]

(40)

For more details, we invite the reader to follow the arguments in
[
20
]
.
∎

This result states that the inner product between signatures of deterministic paths can be represented statistically through the mean of the product of
\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle
. Observe that for unit-speed curves, the inner products
\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle
only encode the information on the angles
\Theta_{i}
between the vectors
\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}
and
\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}
, i.e.

\displaystyle\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle=\cos\left(\Theta_{i}\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\cos\left(\angle(\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}})\right).

(41)

In this case, also observe that the angles
\Theta_{i}
can be exactly recovered from the norm of the difference between the two above random variables,

\displaystyle\Theta_{i}=\cos^{-1}\left(\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle\right)=\cos^{-1}\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\left|\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}-\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\right|^{2}\right).

(42)

Proposition 13

(Inner product as a probabilistic expression)
.

Suppose that
\gamma,\sigma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{d}
are two
absolutely continuous curves such that
|\gamma_{t}^{\prime}|=\left|\sigma_{t}^{\prime}\right|=1
for almost every
t\in\left[0,1\right].
Then for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
we have

\begin{split}\langle S(\gamma)^{n},S(\sigma)^{n}\rangle=&\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k2^{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\gamma_{U_{\left(i\right)}}^{\prime}-\sigma_{V_{\left(i\right)}}^{\prime}\right|^{2k}\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\{\max\Theta_{i}<\frac{\pi}{2}\}}\right]\\
&+\frac{1}{(n!)^{2}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\{\max\Theta_{i}\geq\frac{\pi}{2}\}}\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle\right],\end{split}

(43)

where
\Theta_{i}
in
\left[0,\pi\right]
is defined by
\,\left\langle\gamma_{U_{\left(i\right)}}^{\prime},\sigma_{V_{\left(i\right)}}^{\prime}\right\rangle\,=\cos\Theta_{i},
for
i=1,\dots,n
, and
\mathbbm{1}_{A}
denotes the indicator function on a set
A
.

Proof.

One the set
\left\{\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\Theta_{i}<\frac{\pi}{2}\right\}
, it holds that

\frac{1}{2}\left|\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}-\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\right|^{2}<1,\quad\text{for }i=1,\dots,n.

Consequently, on this set, we have the expansion

\displaystyle\log\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}\langle\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}},\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\rangle\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\left|\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}-\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\right|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k}\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}-\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\right|^{2}\right)^{k}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k2^{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\gamma^{\prime}_{U_{(i)}}-\sigma^{\prime}_{V_{(i)}}\right|^{2k},

where the second equality utilises the standard Talyor expansion for
\log(1-x)
, which is valid for
|x|<1
. Combining this expansion with
Lemma

12
yields
Eq.

43
.
∎

The following two subsections leverage the preceding probabilistic expression to attain upper and lower bounds for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}
. We first collate the recurrent objects and assumptions that will be used in several subsequent arguments.

Assumptions 14

(Standing assumptions)
.

Let
(\Omega,\mathscr{F},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space. The

will refer to the following set of recurring assumptions and definitions,

(i)

Let

is non-vanishing, and is differentiable with bounded derivative. Let
F\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[a,b]\rightarrow[0,1]
be its associated cumulative distribution as prescribed in

7
.

(ii)

Let
\{U_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
and
\{V_{(i)}\}_{i\in\{1,\dots,n\}}
be two i.i.d. collections of uniform order statistics (
Definition

11
). Define the collections
X_{(i)}
and
Y_{(i)}
as

\displaystyle X_{(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=F^{-1}(U_{(i)}),\quad Y_{(i)}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=F^{-1}(V_{(i)}),\quad i=1,\dots,n.

(44)

(iii)

Let
\mu_{n}^{X}
and
\mu_{n}^{Y}
be their respective empirical distributions defined by

\displaystyle\mu_{n}^{X}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{X_{(i)}},\quad\mu_{n}^{Y}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{Y_{(i)}}.

(45)

(iv)

Let

.

To conclude this subsection we recall the definition of

Wasserstein distances and a characterisation of distances between empirical measures.

Definition 15

(Wasserstein Distance)
.

Let
\mu
and
\nu
be probability measures supported on
\mathbb{R}
. The
p
th
Wasserstein distance,
W_{p}(\mu,\nu)
, between

and

W_{p}^{p}(\mu,\nu)=\inf_{\pi\in\Gamma(\mu,\nu)}\int_{\mathbb{\mathbb{R}}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\left|x-y\right|^{p}d\pi(x,y),

(46)

where
\Gamma(\mu,\nu)
denotes the set of all couplings of
\mu
and
\nu

In the case that

and

are both discrete measures, then the infimum is explicit.

Lemma 16

(Discrete characterisation of

-Wasserstein distance; Lemma 4.2 in
[
2
]
)
.

Let
\{A_{j}\}_{j\in\{1,..,n\}}
and
\{B_{j}\}_{j\in\{1,..,n\}}
be two samples of i.i.d. random variables. Denote by
\{A_{(j)}\}_{j\in\{1,..,n\}}
and
\,\{B_{\left(j\right)}\}_{j\in\{1,..,n\}}

\mu_{n}^{A}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{A_{i}}

and
\mu_{n}^{B}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{B_{i}}
be their associated empirical
probability measures. Then we have

\sum_{j=1}^{n}\left(A_{\left(j\right)}-B_{\left(j\right)}\right)^{p}=nW_{p}^{p}\left(\mu_{n}^{A},\mu_{n}^{B}\right),

where

is the

3.2
Lower bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}
in terms of Wasserstein distances

We now use the probabilistic expression of the signature inner product (
Proposition

13
) when
\sigma=\gamma
, and derives a lower-bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)\right\rVert}
in terms of Wasserstein distances.

Proposition 17

(Lower bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)\right\rVert}
in terms of Wasserstein distances)
.

Let
\gamma,\;\mu_{n}^{X},\;\mu_{n}^{Y}
be as defined in
14
. Then

\begin{split}(n!)^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S\left(\gamma\right)^{n}\right\rVert}^{2}\geq&\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-n\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k2^{k}}W_{2k}^{2k}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}\right|<1\right\}}\right]\\
&-\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|\geq 1\right),\end{split}

(47)

for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

Proof.

We use the fact that

|\gamma_{U_{(i)}}^{\prime}-\gamma_{V_{(i)}}^{\prime}|=|g(X_{\left(i\right)})-g(Y_{\left(i\right)})|

(48)

where
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\gamma^{\prime}\circ F
. The assumptions on
\gamma
give that

is once continuously differentiable and so the mean value inequality may be employed to see that

|\gamma_{U_{(i)}}^{\prime}-\gamma_{V_{(i)}}^{\prime}|\leq|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}|\cdot|g^{\prime}(\xi_{i})|\text{ for some }\xi_{i}\in[X_{\left(i\right)},Y_{\left(i\right)}]\text{ for
}i=1,\dots,n.

(49)

Furthermore, as
F
satisfies the integral equation of
Lemma

7
we
have that

|g^{\prime}(s)|=|\gamma_{F(s)}^{\prime\prime}|\cdot|F^{\prime}(s)|=1,\;\forall s\in[a,b]

By applying

16
we learn that

\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|\gamma_{U_{\left(i\right)}}^{\prime}-\gamma_{V_{\left(i\right)}}^{\prime}\right|^{2k}\leq\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{2k}=nW_{2k}^{2k}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y}).

(50)

Observe that
\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\left|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}\right|<1
is a strictly stronger condition than
\max_{i=1,\dots,n}\Theta_{i}<\frac{\pi}{2}
, and that the product in the second term on the right-hand-side of

may be lower bounded by

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. The lower bound
Eq.

47
then follows by combining this observation,
Eq.

43
, and

Eq.

50
.
∎

.

3.3
Upper bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}

Similarly to

, we use

in terms of a series of Wasserstein distances.

Proposition 19

(Upper bound on
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)\right\rVert}
in terms of Wasserstein distances)
.

Let
\gamma,\;\mu_{n}^{X},\;\mu_{n}^{Y}
be as defined in
14
. Then there exists an
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}\leq 1
so that for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
and
0<\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}

\begin{split}(n!)^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S\left(\gamma\right)^{n}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq&\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-n\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{(1-\phi(\varepsilon))^{k}}{k2^{k}}W_{2k}^{2k}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\right)\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}\right|<\varepsilon\right\}}\right]\\
&+\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right),\end{split}

(51)

where
\phi\left(u\right)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\sup_{\left|s-t\right|<u}\left|g^{\prime}\left(t\right)-g^{\prime}\left(s\right)\right|
is the modulus of continuity of the derivative of
g\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\gamma^{\prime}\circ F
.

Proof.

An application of the fundamental theorem of calculus and
Eq.

49
gives

\displaystyle\left|\gamma_{U_{(i)}}^{\prime}-\gamma_{V_{(i)}}^{\prime}\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left|g(X_{\left(i\right)})-g(Y_{\left(i\right)})\right|^{2}

\displaystyle=\left\langle\int_{X_{\left(i\right)}}^{Y_{\left(i\right)}}g^{\prime}(r)dr,\int_{X_{\left(i\right)}}^{Y_{\left(i\right)}}g^{\prime}(s)ds\right\rangle

\displaystyle=\int_{\left[X_{\left(i\right)},Y_{\left(i\right)}\right]^{2}}\left\langle g^{\prime}(r),g^{\prime}(s)\right\rangle-\left|g^{\prime}(r)\right|^{2}+1drds

\displaystyle=\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{2}+\int_{\left[X_{\left(i\right)},Y_{\left(i\right)}\right]^{2}}\left\langle g^{\prime}(r),g^{\prime}(s)-g^{\prime}(r)\right\rangle drds.

And hence, by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality to the integrand in the last line

\left|\left|\gamma_{U_{(i)}}^{\prime}-\gamma_{V_{(i)}}^{\prime}\right|^{2}-\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{2}\right|\leq\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{2}\phi\left(\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|\right),

(52)

and so, in particular

\left|\gamma_{U_{(i)}}^{\prime}-\gamma_{V_{(i)}}^{\prime}\right|^{2}\geq\left(1-\phi\left(\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|\right)\right)\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{2}.

(53)

is a modulus of continuity, and so there exists some

for which

for any

. Now, the product in the second term on the right-hand-side of

may be upper bounded by

by the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, so it follows from

,

and

that

holds provided
\varepsilon<\varepsilon^{\prime}
.
∎

3.4
Generalising the Hambly-Lyons Limit Theorem

Combined,
Propositions

17
and
19
provide lower and upper bounds for
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}
in terms of a series of Wasserstein distances. What remains is to show that the lower bound converges to the square of the Hambly-Lyons limit as
n\to\infty
, and that the same applies to the upper bound when taking
n\to\infty
and then
\varepsilon\to 0
. The following pair of lemmas provide the necessary results for this conclusion.

Lemma 20
.

Let
\mu
,
\mu_{n}^{X}
, and
\mu_{n}^{Y}
be as in the standing assumptions, then for any
\varepsilon>0

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)=0.

(54)

Proof.

Using mean value and inverse function Theorems, we may deduce that

X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}=F^{-1}\left(U_{\left(i\right)}\right)-F^{-1}\left(V_{\left(i\right)}\right)=\left(F^{-1}\right)^{\prime}\left(\eta_{i}\right)\left(U_{\left(i\right)}-V_{\left(i\right)}\right)=\left|\gamma_{\eta_{i}}^{\prime\prime}\right|\left(U_{\left(i\right)}-V_{\left(i\right)}\right)

(55)

for some
\eta_{i}\in\left[U_{\left(i\right)},V_{\left(i\right)}\right]
. An application of Markov’s inequality gives that

\mathbb{P}\left(\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|\geq\varepsilon\right)\leq\varepsilon^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\gamma^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left(\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|U_{\left(i\right)}-V_{\left(i\right)}\right|\right)\leq 2C\varepsilon^{-1}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\gamma^{\prime\prime}\right\rVert}_{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}},

for some absolute constant
C>0
. The first inequality utilises
Eq.

55
, and the second is due to Theorem 4.9 of
[
2
]
. Taking the limit as
n\to\infty
in the above inequality concludes the proof.
∎

Lemma 21
.

Let
\mu
,
\mu_{n}^{X}
, and
\mu_{n}^{Y}
be as in the standing assumptions and
0<a,\varepsilon\leq 1
, then

\Delta_{n}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=n\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}\right|<\varepsilon\right\}}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}}{k2^{k}}W_{2k}^{2k}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\rightarrow 0

(56)

in
L_{1}
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

We have that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n}\right]

\displaystyle=n\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}\right|<\varepsilon\right\}}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}}{k2^{k}}W_{2k}^{2k}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbbm{1}_{\left\{\max_{i=1,...,n}\left|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}\right|<\varepsilon\right\}}\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}}{k2^{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{2k}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{k=2}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}}{k2^{k}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\left|X_{\left(i\right)}-Y_{\left(i\right)}\right|^{4}\right]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left[nW_{4}^{4}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{a^{k}}{k2^{k}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[nW_{4}^{4}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\right]\log\left(\frac{2}{2-a}\right).

Using the following inequality (Section 4.1,
[
2
]
) for
p\in\mathbb{N}\setminus\{0\}
,

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\right]\leq 2^{p}\mathbb{E}\left[W_{p}^{p}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu)\right],

together with the bounds from Theorem 5.3 in
[
2
]
gives

\mathbb{E}\left[W_{4}^{4}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\right]\leq 2^{4}\left(\frac{20}{\sqrt{n+2}}\right)^{4}J_{4}(\mu),

where
J_{4}(\mu)
is finite since
\mu
is compactly supported with density
f
bounded below by
Lemma

8
. It follows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\Delta_{n}\right]\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}40^{4}\log\left(\frac{2}{2-a}\right)\frac{n}{(n+2)^{2}}J_{4}(\mu)=0.

∎

We are now ready to prove our main result.

Theorem 22

(Generalisation of the Hambly-Lyons Limit Theorem)
.

Let
\gamma
,
\mu
,
\mu_{n}^{X}
, and
\mu_{n}^{Y}
be as in the standing
14
. Then,

\lim_{n\rightarrow\infty}n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}(B_{0,s})^{2}|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}ds\right)\right]^{1/2}.

(57)

Proof.

For
0<\varepsilon\leq 1
, define
A_{n}^{\varepsilon}
to be the set
\{\max_{i=1,\dots,n}|X_{(i)}-Y_{(i)}|<\varepsilon\}
. Then, by
Lemma

20
,
\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}^{\varepsilon}}
converges to
1
in probability, and that
\mathbb{P}\left((A_{n}^{\varepsilon})^{c}\right)
converges to
0
. It follows from
Corollaries

4
and
5
, and Slutsky’s Theorem that

\frac{n}{2}\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}^{\varepsilon}}W_{2}^{2}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\to\int_{0}^{1}(B_{0,s})^{2}|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}ds

(58)

in distribution as
n\to\infty
. As such, by
Eq.

58
,
Lemma

21
, and another application of Slutsky’s Theorem we obtain the following convergence in distribution

n\mathbbm{1}_{A_{n}^{\varepsilon}}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{k2^{k}}W_{2k}^{2k}(\mu_{n}^{X},\mu_{n}^{Y})\to\int_{0}^{1}(B_{0,s})^{2}|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}ds.

(59)

For the lower bound, the combining of
Eq.

59
with
Proposition

17
,
Remark

18
,
Lemma

20
, yet another application of Slutsky’s Theorem, and the Continuous Mapping Theorem, results in the lower bound

\lim_{n\to\infty}(n!)^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}^{2}\geq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}(B_{0,s})^{2}|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}ds\right)\right].

(60)

Similar analysis for the upper bound from
Proposition

19
shows that

\lim_{n\to\infty}(n!)^{2}\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}^{2}\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-(1-\phi(\varepsilon))^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(B_{0,s})^{2}|\gamma_{s}^{\prime\prime}|^{2}ds\right)\right],

(61)

for suitably small
\varepsilon
. Using the fact that
\phi
is continuous at zero, we may take the limit in the preceding as
\varepsilon\to 0
and combine it with the lower bound
Eq.

60
to conclude that
Eq.

57
holds.
∎

This generalisation allows us to compute the limit of the signature norm for curves that are not
C^{3}
.

Example 23

(Integral of semi-circular curves)
.

Let
R>\frac{1}{2\pi}
be a constant. Let
\gamma_{+},\gamma_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,\pi R]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
denote two planar semi-circular curves in the plane of radius
R
, rotating clockwise and anti-clockwise respectively, and defined as

\displaystyle\gamma_{\pm}(s)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=R\left(\cos\left[\mp\;\frac{s}{R}\right],\sin\left[\mp\;\frac{s}{R}\right]\right).

(62)

Consider a concatenation
\psi
of these two curves, say
\psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\gamma_{+}*\gamma_{-}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,2\pi R]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
. It is easy to see that
\psi
is differentiable everywhere and that its second-derivative exists everywhere except at point
t=\frac{\pi}{R}
. Define now the integral
\Psi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}[0,2\pi R]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
as

\displaystyle\Psi(t)\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{R}\int_{0}^{t}\psi(s)ds.

(63)

Then,
\Psi|_{[0,1]}
satisfies the condition for
Theorem

22
but is not
C^{3}
. Indeed,

(i)

Because
\psi
is continuously-differentiable,
\Psi
is twice-continuously-differentiable by the fundamental theorem of calculus.

(ii)

As
\Psi^{\prime\prime}(s)=\psi^{\prime}(s)
, the map
s\mapsto|\Psi^{\prime\prime}(s)|=\frac{1}{R}
is non-vanishing, and is differentiable with bounded derivative.

(iii)

Because
\psi
is not twice-differentiable everywhere,
\Psi
does not admit a third-derivative everywhere and is therefore not
C^{3}
.

\phi
and

3.5
Computing the Hambly-Lyons limit explicitly

Finally, we propose a way to practically compute the limit presented in
Theorem

22
. Using the work of Yor and Revuz on Bessel bridges
[
33
]
, we relate the computation of the expected integral
c(\gamma)
to the solving of a second order distributional differential equation.

Lemma 24

(High order term and curvature)
.

Let
V
be a finite dimensional inner product space. Suppose that

\gamma\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\left[0,l\right]\rightarrow V
is parameterised at unit speed and
is three-times continuously differentiable (under this parameterisation). Let

\mu
denote the finite Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}_{+}
which is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure

\lambda=\lambda_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}
with density given by
\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}\left(t\right)=l^{2}\left|\gamma_{tl}^{\prime\prime}\right|^{2}1_{[0,1]}\left(t\right)
a.e. Then,

(i)

There exists a unique continuous function
\phi\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}\left[0,1\right]\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
which solves, in the distributional sense,
the second-order differential equation

\psi^{\prime\prime}=2\psi\mu,\text{with }\psi_{0}=0,\psi_{0}^{\prime}=1.

(64)

(ii)

This solution satisfies

\psi_{1}^{-l/4}=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\left(B_{0,t}\right)^{2}\mu\left(dt\right)\right)\right]^{1/2}=c(\gamma).

Proof.

Define the unit-speed curve
\tilde{\gamma}_{t}\mathrel{\mathop{\ordinarycolon}}=\frac{1}{l}\gamma_{lt}
over

\left[0,1\right]
and let the terms in its signature be given by
\left(1,S(\tilde{\gamma})^{1},..,S(\tilde{\gamma})^{n},..\right).
It suffices to prove the result
under the assumption that
l=1
since the general result can then be recovered
by noticing

\left(n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}\right)^{1/n}=l\left(n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\tilde{\gamma})^{n}\right\rVert}\right)^{1/n}\sim l+\frac{l}{n}\log c\left(\tfrac{1}{l}\gamma\right)+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right),

so that
c\left(\gamma\right)=c\left(\tfrac{1}{l}\gamma\right)^{l}.
We therefore assume
that
l=1,
and seek to prove that
c\left(\gamma\right)=\psi_{1}^{-1/4}.
To
this end, we first observe that the unit-speed parameterisation of
\gamma

gives that
\left\langle\gamma_{t}^{\prime},\gamma_{t}^{\prime\prime\prime}\right\rangle=-\left|\gamma_{t}^{\prime\prime}\right|^{2}
for
every
t
in
\left[0,1\right].
When used together with

Eq.

8
this gives that

c\left(1\right)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\left(-\int_{0}^{1}\left(B_{0,t}\right)^{2}\mu\left(dt\right)\right)\right]^{1/2}.

By noticing that
\left(B_{\cdot}^{0}\right)^{2}
is a squared Bessel
bridge of dimension
1
which returns to zero at time
1
, we can then use
Theorem 3.2 of
[
33
]
to see that

c\left(\gamma\right)=\left(\phi_{1}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{1}{\phi_{t}^{2}}dt\right)^{-1/4},

(65)

where
\phi
is the unique continuous positive function solving the
distributional equation
\phi^{\prime\prime}=2\phi\mu,
with
\phi_{0}=1,\;\phi_{0}^{\prime}=0.
. Using
Exercise 1.34 in
[
33
]
, we know that the function
\psi
in the
statement and
\phi
are related by

\psi_{t}=\phi_{t}\int_{0}^{t}\frac{1}{\phi_{s}^{2}}ds,

and therefore
Eq.

65
becomes
c\left(\gamma\right)=\psi_{1}^{-1/4}.

∎

This result reduces the computation of
c(\gamma)
for a planar circle to the solving of a simple differential equation.

Example 25

(The planar circle)
.

Let
\gamma_{t}=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left(\sin(2\pi t),\cos(2\pi t)\right)
for
t
in
\left[0,1\right]
, then
\gamma
is a
smooth unit-speed curve of length
l=1
and
\left|\gamma_{t}^{\prime\prime}\right|=2\pi
so that
\mu\left(dt\right)=2\pi 1_{[0,1]}\left(t\right)dt
. By solving the differential equation

Eq.

64
we find that

\displaystyle c\left(\gamma\right)=\left(\frac{2\sqrt{\pi}}{\sinh 2\sqrt{\pi}}\right)^{1/4}.

(66)

Remark 26

(Role of
c(\gamma)
in the asymptotics of the norm)
.

By starting from
Eq.

7
and

Eq.

8
, we notice the latter statement can be rewritten as a
statement of asymptotic equivalence, namely

\left(n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}_{\text{HS}}\right)^{1/n}\sim l+\frac{1}{n}\log c\left(\gamma\right)+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty\text{ ,}

(67)

where we write
\mathinner{\!\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert}_{\text{HS
}}
for the moment to emphasise the dependence of this expansion on the choice
of tensor norm. By contrast for the projective tensor norm it follows from

Eq.

6
that we have

\left(n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}_{\text{proj}}\right)^{1/n}\sim l+o\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\text{ as }n\rightarrow\infty.

When written in this way, the
c(\gamma)
limit has the
interpretation of being the second term in the asymptotic expansion of

(n!\mathinner{\!\left\lVert S(\gamma)^{n}\right\rVert}_{\text{HS}})^{1/n}
as

n\rightarrow\infty.
Natural questions would be to explore the higher order terms in these asymptotic expansions, and to relate them to geometric features of the underlying curve.
