A Safe Approximation Based on Mixed-Integer Optimization for Non-Convex Distributional Robustness Governed by Univariate Indicator Functions
J. Dienstbier, F. Liers, J. Rolfes

Abstract
. In this work, we present algorithmically tractable safe approximations of distributionally robust optimization (DRO) problems. The considered ambiguity sets can exploit information on moments as well as confidence sets. Typically, reformulation approaches using duality theory need to make strong assumptions on the structure of the underlying constraints, such as convexity in the decisions or concavity in the uncertainty. In contrast, here we present a duality-based reformulation approach for DRO problems, where the objective of the adverserial is allowed to depend on univariate indicator functions. This renders the problem nonlinear and nonconvex. In order to be able to reformulate the semiinfinite constraints nevertheless, an exact reformulation is presented that is approximated by a discretized counterpart. The approximation is realized as a mixed-integer linear problem that yields sufficient conditions for distributional robustness of the original problem. Furthermore, it is proven that with increasingly fine discretizations, the discretized reformulation converges to the original distributionally robust problem. The approach is made concrete for a challenging, fundamental task in particle separation that appears in material design. Computational results for realistic settings show that the safe approximation yields robust solutions of high-quality and can be computed within short time.

1.
Introduction

Determining optimal solutions or optimizing processes has been studied
in applied mathematics for decades. Nowadays, deep structural
insights have led to many beautiful, practically efficient
methodologies and implementations.
Going a step further, in particular real-world applications are often
strongly affected by uncertainties. Since typically processes can
neither be fully controlled nor can parameters be measured exactly,
optimization under uncertainty is a research area that currently
receives increased attention. In this work, we consider a general
class of distributionally robust optimization models.

In more detail, let
b\in\mathbb{R}
be a scalar,
\mathcal{P}
a set of probability measures,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{k}
decision variables We model the uncertainty in our DRO with a random vector
t\in T
distributed according to an (uncertain) probability measure
\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
.
Let the latter be defined on a compact domain
T\subseteq\mathbb{R}
and distributed according to
\mathbbm{P}
. Here,
v:\mathbb{R}^{k}\times T\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
denotes a function that connects the decision variables with the random variable
t
, e.g.
v(x,t)=x_{1}\cdot t
if we want to depict an uncertain coefficient in a linear program. Then, a
distributionally robust constraint
or DRO constraint is defined by

(1)

b\leq\min_{\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbbm{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left(v(x,t)\right).

Constraints of this form have been intensively studied for decades, see e.g.
[
11
]
or
[
18
]
. In
particular, setting
\mathcal{P}=\{\mathbbm{P}\}

stochastic
constraints

b\leq\mathbbm{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left(v(x,t)\right)

are a central object of investigation in stochastic
optimization.

On the other hand, setting
\mathcal{P}=\{\delta_{t}:t\in T\}
, where
\delta_{t}
denotes the Dirac measures at
t\in T
, (
1
) provides a
robust constraint

b\leq\min_{t\in T}v(x,t).

This special case of (
1
) constitutes a
central object of interest in robust optimization, and its
understanding is crucial in order to make progress in the field. In
particular, challenges consist of deriving algorithmically tractable
solution approaches for solving the robust counterparts, where
reformulations, decomposition and approximation algorithms are in the focus. Next,
we briefly review some relevant literature in optimization under uncertainty, and distributional robustness in particular.

When the uncertainty is distributed by a given distribution or the
constraints need to be satisfied in a probabilistic sense, stochastic
optimization is useful (see e.g.
[
17
]
,

[
6
]
and
[
19
]
). However, often
the underlying distributions are unknown. Then, protecting problems against predefined
uncertainties is a natural approach which lies in the core of robust optimization. To this end,
uncertainty sets are defined and a robust problem then optimizes the
worst case cost and guarantees feasibility for all realizations of
uncertainty within these uncertainty sets. Duality-based
reformulation approaches, decomposition as well as approximation
approaches have been developed for achieving computational
tractability of the semi-infinite or exponentially large
robust counterparts for wide classes of
optimization problems (see
[
22
]
,

[
3
]
,
[
5
]
and
[
4
]
).
Recently, first a
novel decomposition algorithm based on an adaptive bundle method was
presented in
[
Kuchlbauer2022adaptive
]
for nonlinear robust optimization and extended to
integral decisions in
[
kuchlbauerOuter
]
. The approach has been used
[
kuchlbauer2023quality
]
for
robust optimization models governed by a partial differential
equation.
In recent years, models interpolating between these extreme
versions have been studied intensively, among them probabilistic-robust (probust) functions , which combine aspect from stochastic and robust optimization
[
1
]
.

If the distribution itself is uncertain, distributionally robust
optimization (
1
) is a natural modelling
approach.
For recent surveys of this area, we refer to the very detailed
overviews
[
11
]
or
[
18
]
and the
references therein.
To summarize,
there are two main approaches for deciding about the uncertain
distributions against which protection is sought. They are assumed to
reside in a so-called ambiguity set. In discrepancy-based ambiguity
sets, it is assumed that a ’typical’, nominal distribution is
given. Ambiguity sets contain distributions within an appropriately defined
distance from the nominal distribution. Natural distances are given
by Wasserstein-balls
[
12
]
).

Alternatively, another modelling approach consists in defining
moment-based ambiguity sets where
the moments of the considered distributions satisfy given
bounds. As argued later, we will follow this modeling approach here and hence review some related results:
[
10
]
studies mean-variance or
Value-at-Risk risk measures, where the ambiguity set has a bound mean and covariance matrix and
develops algorithmically tractable algorithms for the robust counterpart.
[
16
]
also considers moment information
where the problem is reduced to optimizing an univariate mean-variance robust objective.
In
[
26
]
Slater conditions are used to show correctness of a
duality-based reformulation of the robust counterpart. Additionally, two
discretization schemes are presented that provide convergent approximate solutions to the problem. Exact reformulations of DRO problems, that contain (
1
) as a constraint, are only known for specific ambiguity sets.
[
8
]
present such reformulations for
convex problems and ambiguity sets with moment bounds.

Incorporating additional information to moment-based ambiguity sets forms a challenging task. However, if the probability distribution is known to be unimodal, the authors of
[
14
]
determine a semidefinite program (SDP) reformulation for (
1
). The difference of
[
14
]
to the presented work mainly consists in addressing the interplay of an outer-level DRO with (
1
) as a constraint.
In contrast to adding unimodality information, the article
[
25
]
, under some convexity assumptions, presents a tractable duality-based reformulation of (
1
), that incorporates information on the confidence sets. Under the given assumptions, their approach can be applied to a DRO with (
1
) as a constraint.

We use ambiguity sets similar to
[
8
]
and
assume the mean and covariance matrix to be in a given range in
addition to confidence set information as illustrated in

[
25
]
. However, we are able to not only consider
convex models but address nonconvexities as we consider indicator
functions – the building blocks of any Lebesgues integral such as

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(v(x,t)).

For the semiinfinite problem we present a novel inner approximation
which reformulates the counterpart to a finite-dimensional
mixed-integer linear optimization model. A main result consists in the
proof that its feasible solutions are also feasible for the robust
counterpart. For discretization widths tending to zero the optima
converge. This means that asymptotically the safe approximation
converges to the original DRO problem so that obtained solutions are
asymptotically correct.

This work is structured as follows.
In Section
2
, the distributionally robust
problem formulation including indicator functions is introduced. Motivating
problem classes are presented that can be modeled by the DRO formulation
studied here. Section
3

introduces a novel
semiinfinite inner approximation of the corresponding robust
counterpart together with a suitable discretization approach that
leads to a finite-dimensional mixed-integer linear optimization
model. Additionally, it is shown that its feasible solutions are also
feasible for the original robust DRO model. It is proven that with discretization width tending to
zero, an optimum solution of the inner approximation converges to an
optimum of original robust counterpart. Computational results are
given in Section
4
. As motivating application, a
fundamental and difficult optimization task in material design is
studied. It is argued that particle separation under uncertainty
falls
into the class of problems studied here, whereas known approaches fall
short either in modelling capabilities or in quality of the obtained
solutions. We will clarify this in the problem setting section.
Using realistic settings, it
turns out that the robust counterpart can be solved practically
efficiently via the mixed-integer linear model from Section
3
.

2.
Problem Setting and Notation

2.1.
DRO Constraints Containing Indicator Functions

We consider optimization problems with DRO
constraints of the form (
1
), where the function
v
may consist of
indicator functions
, i.e.,

(2)

v(x,x^{-},x^{+},t)=x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}(t),\text{ where }\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}(t)\coloneqq\begin{cases}1&\text{ if }t\in[x^{-},x^{+}]\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases}

Note, that the decisions may influence the height of the indicator
function by
x
or the non-zero level-set by
x^{-},x^{+}]
. Although, we
focus on the univariate case, i.e.
x,x^{-},x^{+},t\in\mathbb{R}
and the main
results of the article consider only functions of type (
2
), we would like to add, that the results partially
extend to finite sums of indicator functions. In particular, for independent random variables
t_{i}
, we demonstrate
in Section
4
, see Corollary
1
, that our results can be applied to finite sums of indicator functions, so-called
simple functions
, i.e.

v(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k},x_{1}^{-},\ldots,x_{k}^{-},x_{1}^{+},\ldots,x_{k}^{+},t_{1},\ldots,t_{k})=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-}_{i},x^{+}_{i}]}(t_{i}).

One of the reasons for this, is that the independence allows us to treat every summand as a separate univariate indicator function. If this assumption is not satisfied, we refer to
[
Dienstbier2023b
]
for more details on multivariate simple functions.

The function
v
as in (
1
) can also be phrased as

\mathbbm{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(v(x,t))=\mathbbm{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}\left(x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}(t)\right)=x\mathbbm{P}(\{t\in[x^{-},x^{+}]\})

and thus (
1
) can be phrased similarly to a robust chance-constraint:

(3)

b\leq x\min_{\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbbm{P}([x^{-},x^{+}]).

We continue by illustrating the modeling power of (
3
) by one of the most prominent risk measures in risk theory - the so-called

value-at-risk
. It is often applied as a tool to aid both,
financial controlling and reporting
[
9
]
. We refer to
[
10
]
for further details on this topic. At a confidence level of
\alpha
, the value-at-risk

\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)
is defined as follows. Given a real-valued random variable
X
that measures the loss (e.g., of a market participant) and suppose
that this loss function is distributed by
F_{X}
. Then the value at risk at a confidence
level
\alpha
is defined as

\text{VaR}_{\alpha}(X)\coloneqq\inf\{x\in\mathbb{R}:F_{X}(x)\geq\alpha\}.

Hence, by assuming that
X
may be randomly distributed by one of the uncertain distributions
\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, we define a
robust value-at-risk
:

(4a)

\displaystyle\text{VaR}_{\alpha,\mathcal{P}}(X)\coloneqq-\max-x^{+}

(4b)

s.t.

\displaystyle\alpha\leq\min_{\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbbm{P}((-\infty,x^{+}]).

With our results in Section

3
, we provide a mixed-integer linear
programming (MIP) approach to compute lower bounds on

\text{VaR}_{\alpha,\mathcal{P}}
, thereby complementing the upper bounds
given in Section 4 of
[
10
]
. Moreover, we can even prove that these bounds converge to the true solution of (
4
).

In the remainder of this section, we introduce some basic notation and
fundamental concepts.
For further details we refer to the seminal book

[
2
]
and to
[
20
]
.
The major challenge to address DRO constraints like (
1
) lies in the fact, that we need to optimize over a set of probability measures
\mathcal{P}
. In Euclidean vector spaces, e.g.
f,\mathbbm{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, linear constraints
of the form
b\leq\min_{\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\langle x,\mathbbm{P}\rangle
with a set
of convex constraints
\mathcal{P}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
are well-investigated. In particular, one can apply standard reformulation arguments from robust optimization and replace the inner
adverserial optimization problem
\min_{\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\langle x,\mathbbm{P}\rangle
by the feasible region of its dual. This results in a finite-dimensional convex problem, which can be solved, e.g. with interior point methods. In the next paragraphs, we present an inner product in order to achieve a similar reformulation of the DRO constraints.

First, let
t
be a random variable on a compact domain
T
distributed according to a probability density
\rho({t})
. We denote the corresponding probability measure as
\mathbbm{P}
, i.e.
d\mathbbm{P}=\rho({t})dt
. Moreover, the Riesz-Markov-Kakutani representation theorem implies the uniqueness of
\mathbbm{P}
, i.e.
\mathbbm{P}
is the only solution that satisfies
\int_{T}f(t)\rho({t})dt=\int_{T}fd\mathbbm{P}
.
Let us now denote by
\mathcal{C}(T)
the set of continuous functions on
T
and by
\mathcal{M}(T)
the set of
signed Radon measures
. As illustrated in Section III.3.2 in
[
2
]
, the corresponding inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle:\mathcal{C}(T)\times\mathcal{M}(T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
defined by

\langle f,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\coloneqq\int_{T}fd\mathbbm{P}

constitutes a non-degenerate inner product or a
duality
. As probability measures are specific signed Radon measures, the above duality is well-defined. Subsequently, we can reformulate (
3
) as follows:

(5a)

\displaystyle b\leq\min_{\mathbbm{P}}\penalty\

\displaystyle\left\langle x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}(t),\mathbbm{P}\right\rangle

(5b)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{M}(T)_{\geq 0},

(5c)

\displaystyle\langle 1,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq 1,

(5d)

\displaystyle\langle-1,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq-1,

(5e)

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}^{\prime},

where
\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{M}(T)_{\geq 0}
indicates that
\mathbbm{P}
is
contained in the cone of nonnegative Radon measures. In addition, the
remaining constraints (
5b
) - (
5d
) ensure that
\mathbbm{P}
is a probability measure, whereas
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
contains the remaining constraints of the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
.
We note in passing that with this modelling approach, we will be able to
reformulate
nonconvex
DRO constraints that depend on indicator functions. Algorithmical tractability will be achieved as a
mixed-integer linear program (MIP) via a
discretization approach.

2.2.
DRO Models governed by Moment Control and Confidence Sets

Choosing the ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
in a way, that (
5
) is one the one hand algorithmically tractable, but on the other hand also contains every realistic uncertainty, while avoiding the inclusion of unrealistic ones is a crucial task in its own right. Incorporating additional constraints to
\mathcal{P}^{\prime}
can improve the decision making in (
5
) significantly, but may also slow down the computation of (
5
). To this end, we present three classes of constraints, presented among other sources in
[
8
]
and
[
25
]
, that maintain the algorithmic tractability of (
5
) while capturing important features of the probability measure
\mathbbm{P}
.

First, one may assume that the
first moment
, i.e. the expectation, of
\mathbbm{P}
is either known, see e.g.
[
14
]
or at least contained in an interval
[
8
]
. Here, we mainly follow
[
8
]
, i.e. we assume, that estimates of the correct expectation
\mu
and variance
\sigma
are known. In particular, as we consider one-dimensional random variables
t
we can assume that jointly with
\mu
and
\sigma
, a third parameter
\varepsilon_{\mu}>0
, shapes the interval as follows:

(6)

(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(t)-\mu)^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\mu}^{2}/\sigma^{2}.

In particular, this translates to
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(t)
being contained in
[\mu-\varepsilon_{\mu}/\sigma,\mu+\varepsilon_{\mu}/\sigma]
.

Second, in the computational results in Section
4
it is natural to assume that the underlying uncertain
probability distributions
\rho
are monomodal functions, see also e.g.
[
14
]
. However, this is a challenging property as "with the current state of literature, monomodality cannot be modeled in a tractable manner", see
[
18
]
.
An approach followed in
[
14
]
is to
reformulate (
5
) as a semidefinite program (SDP) under the assumption that
\mathcal{P}
contains monomodal distributions with fixed first and second moments. However, incorporating this SDP in a reformulation approach often leads to bivariate variables and is thereby intractable in general.
In order to avoid intractability, we aim to exploit the fact that the monomodal distributions considered in Section
4
tend to have a relatively small variance. Therefore, we again follow
[
8
]
and impose an upper bound on the
second moment
by

(7)

\text{Var}_{\mathbbm{P}}(t)\leq\varepsilon_{\sigma}\sigma^{2}.

Finally, we follow
[
25
]
and add
confidence set
constraints, where the probability of certain subsets
T_{i}\subseteq T
is restricted, i.e.,

(8)

\langle\text{sign}(\varepsilon_{i})\mathbbm{1}_{T_{i}}(t),\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq\varepsilon_{i}\text{ for every }i\in I.

Observe, that these constraints can model
\mathbbm{P}(T_{i})\geq\varepsilon_{i}
if we set
\varepsilon_{i}>0
and
\mathbbm{P}(T_{i})\leq-\varepsilon_{i}
if
\varepsilon_{i}<0
. Furthermore, we observe, that by setting
T_{i}=T
and
\varepsilon_{i}=\pm 1
, the normalization constraints (
5c
) and (
5d
) can also be modeled as confidence set constraints.

2.3.
Relation to the Literature

We note that
\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{i}\mathbbm{P}(X_{i})=\mathbbm{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(v(x,t))
encodes the expectation of a non-convex, in our case a piecewise-constant step-like ,function
v
in
t\sim\mathbbm{P}
. This is a crucial distinction from results presented in
[
25
]
and
[
8
]
. Here, the underlying function
v(x,t)
has to be both, convex and piecewise-affine in
x
and
t
, see Condition (C3) in
[
25
]
and Assumption 2 in

[
8
]
. In
[
25
]
and
[
26
]
, there are exceptions to these assumptions, however in these exceptions the number of confidence set
|I|
has to be very low (see Observation 1ff in the electronic compendium of
[
25
]
) or even
|I|=0
(
[
26
]
).
In the present article, we extend this
setting by considering indicator functions
\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}(t)
, that generally do not satisfy any of those assumptions.

Lastly, we comment on discrepancy-based DRO models that restrict

\mathcal{P}
, in particular Wasserstein balls. Given a nominal
probability distribution
\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
, these measures usually
limit the probability mass that needs to be transferred to reach
another
\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}
. For our computational results in Section
4
, we will use confidence set
constraints to achieve a similar effect. In particular, as we consider
an ambiguity set
\mathcal{P}
, that contains realistic uncertainties with a rather large Wasserstein distance between each other, an additional Wasserstein constraint does not strengthen the model.

3.
A MIP approximation for DRO on a one-dimensional domain
T
and envelope confidence sets

Let us first illustrate the value of our approach. To this end, let us briefly recall the robust value-at-risk example from Section
2
, where we consider the following DRO problem:

(9a)

\displaystyle\text{VaR}_{\alpha,\mathcal{P}}(X)\coloneqq-\max-x^{+}

(9b)

s.t.

\displaystyle\alpha\leq\min_{\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{P}}\mathbbm{P}((-\infty,x^{+}]).

Observe, that in this particular example, the function
\mathbbm{1}_{(-\infty,x^{+}]}(t)
is monotonously increasing in
x^{+}
and decreasing in
t
and thus fits in the framework in
[
25
]
and, given we indeed know
\mu,\sigma
and omit confidence set constraints, also fits
[
27
]
. However, in financial applications it is crucial to not be overly conservative, which motivates the current section, where we develop a sharper approximation to
\mathcal{P}
.

Moreover, note that due to rescaling of
\varepsilon_{\mu}
and
\varepsilon_{\sigma}
, we can w.l.o.g. set
\sigma=1
in (
6
) and (
7
). Observe, that (
6
) is now represented by

\langle t,\mathbbm{P}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(t)\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]

with the predefined bounds
\mu_{-}=\mu(1-\varepsilon_{\mu})
and
\mu_{+}=\mu(1+\varepsilon_{\mu})
. However, in the remainder of this section, we will consider general bounds
\mu_{-},\mu_{+}
as our results also hold in this more general case. For (
7
), we obtain by linearity of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}
, that

\langle t^{2}-2\mu t,\mathbbm{P}\rangle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(t^{2}-2\mu t)=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbbm{P}}(t-\mu)^{2}-\mu^{2}\leq\varepsilon_{\sigma}\sigma^{2}-\mu^{2}.

In order to improve Program (
5
), we further restrict the uncertain measure
\mathbbm{P}
as follows: Let
\rho
denote the probability density corresponding to
\mathbbm{P}
, then we define an
envelope constraint
as

(10)

0\leq\rho({t})\leq\bar{\rho}({t}).

The envelope function restricts the value of the uncertain measure. Thus it incorporates additional information into the ambiguity set and prevents unrealistic realizations (e.g. dirac measure).
Here, the nonnegativity constraint is redundant as
\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{M}(T)_{\geq 0}
and thus
\rho\geq 0
. The upper bound however can model important information. In particular, the bound can be used to exclude measures that concentrate all the probability mass around a single point and thus have a high density at this point, e.g. Dirac point measures. Moreover, if the uncertainty is parametrized, bounds for (
10
) may be fairly simple to obtain. This can be illustrated by the following example:

Example 1
.

Let
T=[t_{0},t_{\max}]
and
\rho_{s}({t})
be the probability density function of a normal distribution with expectation
\bar{\mu}
. Suppose
\bar{\mu}
varies between
\mu_{-}
and
\mu_{+}
and
\rho({t})
lies in the convex hull of
\{\rho_{\bar{\mu}}({t}):\bar{\mu}\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]\}
. Then, a valid definition of
\bar{\rho}({t})
would be

\bar{\rho}({t})\coloneqq\left\{\begin{array}[]{lll}\rho_{\mu_{-}}({t}),&t\in[t_{0},\mu_{-}]\\
\rho_{\mu}({\mu}),&t\in[\mu_{-},\mu_{+}]\\
\rho_{\mu_{+}}({t}),&t\in[\mu_{+},t_{\max}].\end{array}\right.

Although constraints of type (
10
) may be useful to refine a DRO constraint like (
5
), we observe that simply adding the semiinfinite constraint

\rho({t})\leq\bar{\rho}({t})\text{ for every }t\in T

adds another level of difficulty to (
5
). In order to avoid this, we only consider a finite sample
T_{N}\coloneqq\delta_{N}\mathbbm{Z}\cap T
of
T
with
\mathbbm{Z}
being the set of integers and approximate only finitely many of the constraints in (
10
). Here,
\delta_{N}>0
denotes the sample stepwidth. Now, we use this discretization to bound the mass under
\rho({t})
by using confidence set constraints as follows:

Remark 1
.

Consider intervals
[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N})
for all
\tau\in T_{N}
and define the corresponding confidence set constraints by

\mathbbm{P}([\tau,\tau+\delta_{N}])=\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\delta_{N}}1d\mathbbm{P}(t)=\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N})},\mathbbm{P}\rangle\leq\delta_{N}\cdot\max_{t\in[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N}]}\bar{\rho}({t}).

For
\delta_{N}\rightarrow 0
, we illustrate, that this converges against the constraint (
10
):
As
\rho
is the distribution, that corresponds to
\mathbbm{P}
, we have
d\mathbbm{P}=\rho(t)dt
. Let
R
denote the antiderivative of
\rho
, then

\frac{1}{\delta_{N}}\int_{\tau}^{\tau+\delta_{N}}1d\mathbbm{P}(t)=\frac{R(\tau+\delta_{N})-R(\tau)}{\delta_{N}}\rightarrow\rho(\tau)\qquad\qquad(\delta_{N}\rightarrow 0)

and since
\lim_{\delta_{N}\rightarrow 0}\max_{t\in[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N}]}\bar{\rho}({t})=\bar{\rho}({\tau})
the claim follows. Defining
\bar{\rho}_{+}({\tau})\coloneqq\max_{t\in[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N}]}\bar{\rho}({t})
leads to

(11)

\langle\mathbbm{1}_{[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N})},\mathbbm{P}\rangle\leq\delta_{N}\cdot\bar{\rho}_{+}({\tau}).

Combining the above moment and envelope constraints (
11
), the DRO constraint (
5
) - the main target of investigation in this section - simplifies to:

(12a)

\displaystyle b\leq\min\penalty\

\displaystyle\langle x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}(t),\mathbbm{P}\rangle

(12b)

s.t.

\displaystyle\mathbbm{P}\in\mathcal{M}(T)_{\geq 0}

(12c)

\displaystyle\langle 1,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq 1

(12d)

\displaystyle\langle-1,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq-1

(12e)

\displaystyle\langle-t,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq-\mu_{+},

(12f)

\displaystyle\langle t,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq\mu_{-},

(12g)

\displaystyle\langle-t^{2}+2\mu t,\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq-\sigma^{2}\varepsilon_{\sigma}+\mu^{2}

(12h)

\displaystyle\langle-\mathbbm{1}_{[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N})}(t),\mathbbm{P}\rangle\geq-\delta_{N}\cdot\bar{\rho}_{+}({\tau})

We observe, that Constraint (

. In order to apply conic duality to (

. From a game-theoretic perspective, a larger

would translate to a stronger adverserial player and consequently a more conservative solution to (

We note that in order to
dualize (
12
), we consider
continuous approximators
x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}^{c},\mathbbm{1}_{[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N}]}^{c}
of the indicator functions

x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]},\mathbbm{1}_{[\tau,\tau+\delta_{N}]}
. This is necessary at this point since the dual cone is the continuous nonnegative functions and therefore with the indicator function the dual problem will be infeasible.
The existence of approximators that are w.l.o.g. arbitrarily close to their respective indicator
functions is given by the seminal Lemma of Urysohn, see e.g.
[
13
]
. In particular, we choose an upper approximator
\mathbbm{1}^{c}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}\geq\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}

and a lower approximator whenever

. The opposite approximators are chosen for

, i.e., we choose

. This establishes the following key property:

(13)

In the following, we will define necessary ingredients for being
able to reformulate such a DRO constraint by dualizing
(
12
). Subsequently, a
tractable
and high-quality inner approximation of the resulting
constraint will be obtained.
We first employ duality theory using an adjoint operator. To this end, let us denote the dual variables originating from constraints (
12c
) – (

) by

, e.g. for (

), we have

. Additionally, we denote the dual variables that correspond to the envelope constraint (

) as

and consider the following adjoint operator:

Remark 2
.

It might not be immediately clear whether an adjoint operator with respect to the primal operator
\mathcal{A}:\mathcal{M}(T)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{5\times T_{N}}
of (

) exists.
However, the following operator

Consequently, as a dual program to (

\displaystyle\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{5}_{\geq 0},z\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{T_{N}}}

s.t.

\displaystyle x\mathbbm{1}_{[x^{-},x^{+}]}^{c}(t)-y_{1}+y_{2}+y_{3}t-y_{4}t+y_{5}(t^{2}-2\mu t)

(14b)

As typical in reformulation
approaches in robust optimization, we aim at using strong duality. Indeed, next we establish strong duality between (

Suppose that the strictly positive Radon measure

Proof.

We observe that

Observe, that we can neglect to explicitly demand continuity in (

4.
Computational Results

To evaluate the introduced reformulation approaches, we test them on a
prototypical application in the setup of material design processes. A
fundamental and simultaneously challenging task in this active research field consists in the separation of a mixture of substances into its individual components, characterized by different
criteria. In this process, the
particle mixture flows along a so-called chromatographic column with
material-dependent velocities. Loosely speaking, while flowing along the column,
different materials can be separated. At the end of the process the concentration over time of each particle is detected and documented in the
chromatoram
. Challenges then consist in
an optimized setup of particle separation, in particular the layout
of (one or more) such columns. A fundamental question consists in
determining points in time when to perform different ways to separate
the materials. In addition, the separated materials need to satisfy quality
requirements. Collection one or more separated materials is called
fractionation
.
In this application, we consider Polyethylenglykol
(PEG) molecules that shall be separated
with respect to their
degree of polymerization

s
, i.e., the (discrete) number
s
of
monomeric units. Quality requirement then state that at least a certain
fraction of the separated material needs to have the specified degree.

Chromatographic processes are prone to uncertainties that already in
very simplified settings
can impact the separation results negatively. In particular, the
residencetime distributions
(RTDs), which distribute the time a PEG needs to pass the column may be uncertain themselves. In order to maintain quality
requirements even under uncertainty, robust
protection is sought which is a current challenge.

In our example, we use realistic settings from
[
24
]
. The uncertain RTDs are assumed to be normal distributions

\rho_{\mu}({t,s})
which is a standard assumption in practice, see e.g.
[
23
]
. Each distribution describes the degree of polymerization for one
s
. We naturally assume that the mean
\mu_{s}
and the variance

\sigma_{s}
are uncertain.

We denote
S
as the set of all polymerization degrees within the injection and
S_{w}\subseteq S
as the set of the polymerization degrees of the desired particles.
Assuming a mixture of different PEGs, the aim is to set up
the
separation such that as much share as possible is collected from one
desired PEG size.

Thus, we need to find the time interval, i.e., a point in time
x^{-}
where
to start and a not earlier point in time
x^{+}
where to end
fractionation. On the one hand, we wish to collect as much of the
desired PEG. This amount is determined by the area under its
concentration distribution in the resulting chromatogramm. As this area is strongly correlated with the quantity

x^{+}-x^{-}
, we determine
x^{-},x^{+}
such that this difference is maximized.

On the other hand, quality requirements on the
endproduct need to be met. It is required that the percentage of the

we require a purity of at least some value of

We point out that we have shown here that robust fractionation times
are algorithmically tractable by solving the
mentioned mixed-integer linear optimization problem, even though
simple functions are involved. In addition, we next compare the
quality of robust optima, when the ambiguity sets are
constrained. To this end,
we compare the robust solution with moment
control obtained by solving (
24

solutions obtained without moment control, i.e. without constraints (

concentration. Each of the four gray sharp peaks correspond to the PSD of one
PEG while the narrow black peak is the PSD of the desired PEG.
Under the considered uncertainty sets, the
peaks can lie below the envelope function (black box). Depending on the realization of the uncertainty the peaks can overlap. The rectangular functions show the fractionation with (blue) and without (red) moment control. If the function is zero no fractionation takes place otherwise product is collected.

For the small uncertainty set, there is nearly no overlap of the
uncertainty sets given by the envelopes of the distributions (i.e. the black boxes). As can be seen in Figure

For the large uncertainty set, the overlap of the envelopes
is large such that the bound on the variance does no longer bring

Finally, we study the medium uncertainty set where the situation is different. For the resulting
chromatogram the envelopes overlap slightly. As a consequence, the maximum robust fractionation time without variance control is
0.12 minutes (red) but considerably larger if in addition bounds on

Thus, by bounding the variance in cases where
the overlap is not too large, a better robust solution is
obtained where fractionation is longer and still
quality requirements are met. We conclude that for medium-sized uncertainty sets, robust
reformulations should also enforce additional information on the
ambiguity sets.

5.
Conclusion

In this paper, we have presented a novel approach for safely approximating
distributionally robust optimization problems where one-dimensional indicator
functions are allowed. We have shown that a suitably discretized
formulation yields a mixed-integer linear optimization
model. Under some assumptions on the feasible and ambiguity sets, we have proven
that the MIP formulation converges to the true robust counterpart which shows the high quality of the reformulation. The
fact that indicator functions can be included in the model pushes the
applicability of duality-based reformulations of distributional
robustness significantly beyond convexity.
