Accessibility, planar graphs, and quasi-isometries
Joseph Paul MacManus

Abstract
. We prove that a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph which is quasi-isometric to a planar graph is necessarily accessible. This leads to a complete classification of the finitely generated groups which are quasi-isometric to planar graphs. In particular, such a group is virtually a free product of free and surface groups, and thus virtually admits a planar Cayley graph.



Introduction

The activity of seeking to deduce algebraic information about the structure of a finitely generated group from the geometric structure of its Cayley graphs is a classic and common pastime of geometric group theorists. Amongst the oldest examples of this is a paper of Maschke from 1896
[
33
]
. This work features a complete classification of those finite groups admitting a Cayley graph which is
planar
. That is, it admits some embedding into the plane.
In particular, such a group is one of

1,\ \ \mathbb{Z}_{n},\ \ \mathbb{Z}_{n}\times\mathbb{Z}_{2},\ \ D_{2n},\ A_{4},\ \ S_{4},\ \ A_{5}.

In other words, they are precisely the finite subgroups of the group of homeomorphisms of the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Of course, the aforementioned result is less about these groups themselves, and more a statement about finite, connected, planar, transitive graphs. Such graphs often form the skeleta of certain types of uniform polyhedra, which are famously well studied. A complete classification of those finite, connected, planar, transitive graphs can be found in
[
17
]
.

The study of infinite planar Cayley graphs began much later. Results due to Wilkie
[
51
]
and Zieschang–Vogt–Coldewey
[
52
]
classify those groups with Cayley graphs that can be embedded in the plane with no accumulation points of vertices. In particular, these results show that a one-ended group admits a planar Cayley graph only if it is either a wallpaper group or a non–Euclidean crystallographic group. See
[
31
, §3]
for a good discussion. In order to study those infinite-ended planar groups, the first barrier one needs to cross is that of
accessibility
.

A celebrated theorem of Stallings
[
44
,
45
]
says that a finitely generated group has more than one end if and only if it splits over a finite subgroup.
The definition of an accessible group is due to Wall
[
49
]
and says that a group is
accessible
if it splits as a graph of groups with finite edge groups, where each vertex group has at most one end. In other words, if the process of iteratively taking a finite splitting and passing to a vertex group necessarily terminates. It is a consequence of the Grushko–Neumann theorem
[
25
,
39
]
that all finitely generated torsion-free groups are accessible. It was conjectured by Wall in 1971 that all finitely generated groups are accessible. In 1985, Dunwoody demonstrated that every finitely presented group is accessible
[
12
]
. Eight years later, Dunwoody presented the first known example of an inaccessible finitely generated group
[
13
]
.
Returning to the plane, it was noted by Levinson–Maskit
[
30
]
that a consequence of Maskit’s planarity theorem
[
34
]
is that if a Cayley graph of a given group admits a ‘point-symmetric embedding’ in the plane then the aforementioned group is finitely presented (and thus accessible). This was later extended to all planar Cayley graphs by Droms in
[
8
]
. A recent account of Maskit’s theorem has been given by Bowditch in
[
3
]
. It is shown in
[
1
]
by Arzhantseva–Cherix that ‘most’ Cayley graphs of finitely presented groups are non-planar, in a certain statistical sense. They also prove that the admission of a planar Cayley graph is a property preserved by free products.
An enumeration of planar Cayley graphs is given by Georgakopoulos–Hamann in
[
20
,
21
]
. Several other results relating to planar Cayley graphs are given in
[
19
]
, including the observation that any group acting properly discontinuously on a planar manifold admits a planar Cayley graph.

The concept of accessibility was later reframed by Thomassen–Woess as a graph theoretical property. They say that a connected, locally finite graph is accessible if there exists some positive integer
k\geq 1
such that any pair of distinct ends can be separated by the removal of at most
k
vertices. It is shown in
[
47
]
that a finitely generated group is accessible if and only if its Cayley graphs are accessible as graphs, and thus the two definitions coincide.
When studying infinite connected graphs, it is common to assume that our graphs are
quasi-transitive
. That is, that the automorphism group acts with finitely many orbits on the vertex set. Dunwoody showed in
[
14
]
that any connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, planar graph is accessible. Another proof of this fact was later given by Hamann in
[
29
]
by studying the cycle space of such a graph (see also
[
28
]
).
Recently, this result was strengthened by Esperet–Giocanti–Legrand-Duchesne in
[
16
]
, where it is proven that any connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, minor-excluded graph is accessible.

It is interesting to note that accessibility is, in some sense, a ‘coarse’ property. More precisely, after Gromov we say that two metric spaces
X
and
Y
are
quasi-isometric
if there exists maps
f:X\to Y
,
g:Y\to X
such that both
f
and
g
preserve distances up to some additive and multiplicative error, and
f\circ g
lies a bounded distance from the identity map under the sup-norm (see Section
1.3
).
Accessibility in the above sense is readily seen to be a quasi-isometry invariant amongst locally finite graphs, and so we would expect coarse assumptions to have an effect on accessibility. For example, Dunwoody’s proof in
[
12
]
actually shows that ’coarsely simply connected’ quasi-transitive graphs are accessible. Also, Möller
[
38
]
gives a characterisation of inaccessible quasi-transitive graphs as those with uncountably many ‘thick’ ends, also a coarse property.
Little else is known about how coarse hypotheses affect accessibility. To this end, we now state our main theorem.

Theorem A
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph, and suppose
X
is quasi-isometric to a planar graph. Then
X
is accessible.

By
planar graph
we mean a connected, 1-dimensional cell complex which admits a topological embedding into the plane, metrised so that each edge is isometric to the unit interval. We do not require this planar graph to be locally finite, or 2-connected.
The proof of Theorem
A
proceeds in the spirit of Dunwoody’s proof in
[
14
]
, and makes use of Dunwoody’s theory of tracks on simplicial 2-complexes. Of course, it is not obvious at all where this complex should come from in our setting, and constructing a suitable home for our tracks is one of the main technical achievements of this paper.

In the presence of accessibility, most questions relating to the structure of a particular class of graphs can be essentially reduced to understanding those graphs with exactly one end. In our case, we have the following statement relating to one-ended graphs.

Theorem B
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive, one-ended graph, and suppose
X
is quasi-isometric to a planar graph. Then
X
is also quasi-isometric to some complete Riemannian plane.

Note that while many one-ended planar graphs arise as the 1-skeleta of complete Riemannian planes, it is not true that every such graph is quasi-isometric to such a plane. The above result relies in the presence of an appropriate quasi-action in order to tame the structure of our planar graph.

We note that Theorems
A
and
B
allow us to deduce the following structural characterisation of connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graphs which are quasi-isometric to planar graphs.

Corollary C
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph. Then
X
is quasi-isometric to a planar graph if and only if
X
admits a canonical tree decomposition with bounded adhesion and tight separators, where each torso is either finite or quasi-isometric to a complete Riemannian plane.

Canonical tree decompositions are defined and discussed in Section
3.3
, but should be interpreted as a graph-theoretical analogue to the toolbox of Bass–Serre theory. Note that it also follows immediately from Corollary
C
that a quasi-transitive graph
X
which is quasi-isometric to a planar graph is necessarily coarsely simply connected (see Definition
1.32
below).

We now discuss the group-theoretical implications of Theorems
A
and
B
.
Recall that ‘the’ Cayley graph of a finitely generated group is only well-defined up to quasi-isometry, and as such there is a rich literature on how coarse metric assumptions affect the structure of a group. For example, an important theorem of Gromov states that a Cayley graph of a finitely generated group has polynomial growth if and only if said group is virtually nilpotent
[
23
]
. Another key example, which will play a central role in this paper, is a deep theorem originating in the work of Mess
[
36
]
which provides a strong characterisation of those groups quasi-isometric to planes. The proof of this result also involves contributions from Casson–Jungreis
[
5
]
, Gabai
[
18
]
, and Tukia
[
48
]
.
Summarising, we state the following.

Theorem

(
[
36
,
48
,
18
,
5
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. Then
G
is quasi-isometric to a complete Riemannian plane if and only if
G
is a virtual surface group.

In the above, a
virtual surface group
is one containing a finite index subgroup isomorphic to the fundamental group of a closed surface.
Alternative proofs and extensions of this result have been given by Bowditch
[
2
]
and Maillot
[
32
]
. In particular, Maillot shows the following.

Theorem

(
[
32
]
)
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. Then
G
is quasi-isometric to a complete, simply connected, planar Riemannian surface with non-empty geodesic boundary if and only if
G
is virtually free.

These results illustrate the philosophy that ‘planarity’ appears to be an incredibly rigid property amongst finitely generated groups. That is, weak assumptions relating to planarity tend to imply rather strong properties. We compare the above theorems with our next application, which extends the above and further supports this philosophy.

Corollary D
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group. Then the following are equivalent.

(1)

G
is quasi-isometric to a planar graph,

(2)

G
is quasi-isometric to a planar
Cayley
graph,

(3)

Some finite index subgroup of
G
admits a planar Cayley graph,

(4)

G
is virtually a free product of free and surface groups.

It is clear that (4)
\implies
(3)
\implies
(2)
\implies
(1). Our contribution is showing the non-trivial implication that (1)
\implies
(4).
The idea is to apply Theorem
A
, which allows us to restrict our view to one-ended groups only. Combining this with Theorem
B
, the characterisation of virtual surface groups described above readily applies.

It is interesting to note that Corollary
D
together with a result of Papasoglu–Whyte
[
40
]
imply that there are precisely eight quasi-isometry classes of finitely generated groups quasi-isometric to planar graphs, since every surface group is quasi-isometric to either the Euclidean plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
or the hyperbolic plane
\mathbb{H}^{2}
. In particular, every such group is quasi-isometric to one of:

1,\ \ \mathbb{Z},\ F_{2},\ \ \mathbb{Z}^{2},\ \ \Sigma,\ \ \mathbb{Z}^{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}^{2},\ \ \Sigma\ast\Sigma,\ \ \mathbb{Z}^{2}\ast\Sigma,

where
F_{2}
is the free group of rank two, and
\Sigma
denotes the fundamental group of the closed orientable surface of genus 2.

Discussion of the proofs

Before diving into the technical details of this paper, it will be helpful to motivate what is to come.

Let’s begin with Theorem
B
.
If we want to show that a (2-connected, locally finite) one-ended planar graph
\Gamma
is quasi-isometric to a complete Riemannian plane, then our natural instinct is glue 2-cells into the faces and extend the graph metric on the graph to a Riemannian metric on the resulting plane. The following two pathologies could arise, which will halt this plan in its tracks.

(1)

There could be ‘infinite face paths’, so the resulting complex is not a plane.

(2)

The finite faces of
\Gamma
could be arbitrarily big, which will stop the inclusion of our graph into the constructed Riemannian surface from being a quasi-isometry.

Consider, for example, the one-ended planar graph depicted in Figure
1
. This graph is obtained by taking a half-grid and removing bigger and bigger ‘holes’. It is not immediately clear that this graph is not quasi-isometric to some finitely generated group.

Our strategy of proof for Theorem
B
is to prove that neither of the two pathologies described above can occur in a one-ended planar graph which is quasi-isometric to a quasi-transitive graph. We show this by studying the induced quasi-action on the planar graph, and using this to obtain some control over the local features of the graph.

\cdots

Figure 1
.
One-ended planar graph which is not quasi-isometric to a complete Riemannian plane. It is not immediately clear whether or not this graph is quasi-isometric to a quasi-transitive graph.

We now look towards the headline result, Theorem
A
. The following discussion will be mainly centered around finitely generated groups, but everything is equally applicable to quasi-transitive graphs.

It will be instructive to first consider the Maskit planarity theorem
[
34
]
.

Theorem 0.1

(Maskit planarity theorem)
.

Let
S
be a planar surface equipped with a proper cocompact action by a group
G
, such that
\Sigma=S/G
is a finite-type orbifold. Then
\Sigma
contains a collection
\mathcal{C}
of essential simple closed curves such that

(1)

Given
\gamma\in\mathcal{C}
, every component of the lift
\tilde{\gamma}
of
\gamma
in
S
is a simple closed curve, and

(2)

The normal subgroup of
\pi_{1}(S)
generated by the connected components of the lifts of curves in
\mathcal{C}
is equal to
\pi_{1}(S)
.

See
[
3
]
for a good discussion of the above theorem.
An immediate observation to be made is that if we glue a disk along each of the components of the lifts of curves in
\mathcal{C}
then the resulting cell complex is simply connected. In particular, we deduce the following.

Corollary 0.2
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group acting properly and cocompactly on a planar surface
S
. Then
G
is finitely presented.

This will serve as inspiration for us. Somehow, our goal should be to obtain a group action on some 2-complex which behaves a bit like a planar surface.
For an idea on how to construct such a complex, consider the following.

Theorem 0.3

(Whitney planar embedding theorem)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a 3-connected, locally finite, planar graph. Then
\Gamma
embeds uniquely into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
up to post-composition of homeomorphisms of
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

This result is due to Whitney
[
50
]
in the case of finite graphs, and was later extended to locally finite infinite graphs by Richter–Thomassen
[
42
]
. It has the following consequence, which was noticed by Dunwoody in
[
14
]
.

Theorem 0.4
.

Let
G
be a finitely generated group acting properly and cocompactly on a connected, locally finite, planar graph
\Gamma
. Then
G
is finitely presented (and thus accessible).

Sketch.

First, assume that
\Gamma
is 3-connected.
In this case, we can glue 2-cells along all finite faces of
\Gamma
, and attach copies of
\mathbb{R}\times[0,1]
along infinite faces. Since the drawing of
\Gamma
is unique by Theorem
0.3
, this construction is equivariant, and the resulting complex is a planar surface with a proper cocompact
G
-action. By Corollary
0.2
we have that
G
is finitely presented.

If
\Gamma
is not 3-connected then a result of Droms–Servatius–Servatius
[
9
]
implies that
G
splits as a graph of groups with finite edge groups, such that for every vertex group
G_{v}
there exists a
G_{v}
-invariant 3-connected subgraph
\Gamma_{v}\subset\Gamma
, such that
\Gamma_{v}/G_{v}
is compact. In particular, each
G_{v}
is finitely presented by the previous case, and thus so is
G
.
∎

We can now set out a plan of attack, modelled on the above sketch. Let
G
be a finitely generated group which is quasi-isometric to a planar graph
\Gamma
.

(I)

Pass to a finite splitting of
G
where each infinite-ended vertex group
G_{v}
is quasi-isometric to some ‘highly connected’ planar graph
\Gamma_{v}
.

(II)

Abuse this high connectivity, and show that the quasi-action of
G_{v}
upon
\Gamma_{v}
‘coarsely preserves’ the faces of
\Gamma_{v}
. This should been viewed as a coarse-ification of Whitney’s unique planar embedding theorem.

(III)

Make use of this control of the quasi-action and construct an action of
G_{v}
upon some 2-complex which cohomologically ‘looks a bit like a planar surface’. From this point on, we can apply the tools developed by Dicks–Dunwoody in
[
6
]
to show that
G
is accessible.

Life will not quite be as simple as described above, and we will need to come face to face with some delicate and subtle technicalities. These will be discussed in due course.

Organisation of this paper

Section
1
introduces the basic tools and definitions required for the rest of the paper, and standardises our terminology and notation.

In Section
2
we consider the particular case of one-ended graphs and prove Theorem
B
.

Next, Section
3
studies the geometry of cuts in graphs. This includes a short introduction to the Dicks–Dunwoody theory of cuts
[
6
]
, and also sets up the notation and terminology relating to canonical tree decompositions. Finally, we define the ‘inner/outer coboundary diameters’ of a subgraph.

Section
4
introduces a new theory of cuts ‘relative to a collection of peripheral subgraphs’. We also introduce a notion of (relative)
cohomological planarity
, which will play a key role throughout.

Section
5
briefly discusses the link between the aforementioned ‘cohomological planarity’ property and planar graphs.

Sections
6
and
7
are together the most technical part of this paper. Here we provide a detailed study of how quasi-actions affect planar graphs and their faces. The main result of these sections together is that if we begin ‘cutting-up’ our planar graph along small cuts then eventually the dynamics of this quasi-action become quite controlled and predictable.

In Section
8
we piece all of the above together and prove Theorem
A
. Following this, we deduce Corollaries
C
and
D
.

In Section
9
we state some natural conjectures which follow from this work.

Finally, in Appendix
A
we briefly introduce Dunwoody’s theory of tracks on simplicial complexes. We also discuss how one can modify a certain argument in
[
6
]
in order to weaken some hypotheses. The details here are technical and only fleetingly relevant, which is why they have been relegated to the appendix.

1.
Preliminaries

We begin by setting up some standard notation and terminology, which will follow us throughout.

1.1.
Graphs, ends, and accessibility

By a
graph
, we mean a 1-dimensional CW-complex.
We call a
0
-cell a
vertex
and a 1-cell an
edge
. If
X
is a graph, we denote by
VX
the set of vertices of
X
, and
EX
its set of undirected, closed edges. The vertices which
e\in EX
abuts are called the
endpoints of
e
. If the endpoints of
e
are
u,v\in VX
, then we may write
e=uv
.
We also denote by
e^{-1}
the opposite orientation of
e
. Given
v,u\in VX
, a
combinatorial path
, or just
path
between
v
and
u
is a sequence
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
of
e_{i}\in EX
such that
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
share an endpoint every for
1\leq i<n
. The
length
of this path is
n
. We say that a path is
simple
if any given vertex is visited at most once. If
p
is a path from
u
to
v
, and
q
is a path from
v
to
w
, then the concatenation
pq
is a path from
u
to
w
. Denote by
p^{-1}
the reversal of
p
. A
(closed) loop
is a path as above starting and ending at the same vertex. and we say that a loop is
simple
if every vertex is visited at most once, except the common initial/terminal vertex, which is visited exactly twice. We may parameterise loops with continuous maps
S^{1}\to X
.
The graph
X
is said to be
connected
if there exists a path connecting any two points.
If
X
is connected, we equip
VX
with a metric by defining
\dist_{X}(u,v)
as the length of shortest path connecting
u,v\in VX
. We may extend this metric to all of
X
by identifying each
e\in EX
with a copy of the unit interval
[0,1]
. Note that throughout this paper, a connected subgraph will almost always be considered together with its own intrinsic metric, rather than the metric induced by the ambient graph. Given
r\geq 0
and
S\subset\Gamma
, let
B_{\Gamma}(S;r)
denote the closed
r
-neighbourhood of
S
in
\Gamma
.

The
degree
or
valence
of a vertex
v\in VX
is the number of
e\in EX
which abut
v
. We call
X

locally finite
if every vertex has finite degree. We call
X

bounded valence
if there exists
N\geq 0
such that every vertex has degree at most
N
.

Given a subset
F\subset EX
, we denote by
X-F
the subgraph of
X
obtained by removing the edges in
F
, but no vertices. Given a subgraph
Y\subset X
, we denote by
X-Y
the subgraph of
X
obtained by removing all vertices of
X
contained in
Y
, as well as all incident edges. Given a subset
U\subset VX
, we denote by
X[U]
the
subgraph induced by
U
. That is, the subgraph of
X
with vertex set precisely
U
, where we include
e\in EX
if and only if both endpoints of
e
lie in
U
. An
induced subgraph
is a subgraph which is equal to the subgraph induced by its vertex set.

When we say a group
G
acts on a connected graph
X
, we mean by isometries with respect to the metric
\dist_{X}
defined above. We say that a group action
G\curvearrowright X
is
quasi-transitive
if there are only finitely many orbits of vertices. Note that if
X
is also locally finite, then there are only finitely many orbits of edges too, and
X
is necessarily bounded valence.

A
ray

r
in
X
is an infinite sequence of directed edges
e_{1},e_{2},\ldots
such that
e_{i}
and
e_{i+1}
share an endpoint for every
i\geq 1
. A ray is
simple
if any vertex of
X
is visited at most once. An infinite subpath of a simple ray
r
is called a
tail
. We call the initial vertex of
r
the
basepoint
. We say that two simple rays
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
are
end-equivalent
if for all compact subgraphs
K\subset X
, we have that there are tails of
r_{1}
and
r_{2}
which lie in the same connected component of
X-K
. This is clearly an equivalence relation on the set of simple rays. An equivalence class is called an
end
of
X
. The set of ends of
X
is denoted by
\Omega X
.
A
bi-infinite ray
r
is the union of two rays
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
which share a common basepoint. If
r_{i}
approaches
\omega_{i}\in\Omega X
, we may simply say that
r
is a path between
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
.

It is helpful to sometimes consider a graph together with its ends as a single topological object. For this purpose, we have the following definition.

Definition 1.1

(Freudenthal compactification)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph. Then, the
Freudenthal compactification
of
\Gamma
is defined to be the compact topological space
\overline{\Gamma}
with underlying set
\Gamma\sqcup\Omega\Gamma
, whose topology is given by the open sets

V_{K}(x)=\{y\in\Gamma\cup\Omega\Gamma:\textrm{there exists a path from $x$ to $y$ in $X-K$}\},

where
K
is any compact subgraph of
\Gamma
.

It is easy to check that
\overline{\Gamma}
is compact, Hausdorff, and locally path connected
[
7
, §8.6]
and that the natural inclusion
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}
is a topological embedding.
We quickly record the following important property of the Freudenthal compactification, which sets it apart from other compactifications.

Proposition 1.2

(
[
41
, Thm. 1.5(f)]
)
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite graph. Let
U\subset\overline{X}
be a connected open set. Then
U-\Omega X
is also connected.

Given
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega X
and a finite subset
F\subset EX
, we say that
F

separates

\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
if any bi-infinite path between these ends must cross some
e\in F
. If
K\subset\Gamma
is a compact subgraph and
U
is a connected component of
\Gamma-K
, we say that an end
\omega\in\Omega\Gamma

lies in
U
if every simple ray in
\omega
has infinite intersection with
U
.
We now have the following key definition.

Definition 1.3

(Accessibility for graphs)
.

Let
X
be a connected graph. We say that
X
is
accessible
if there exists
k\geq 1
such that for any pair of distinct ends
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega X
, we have that
\omega_{1}
,
\omega_{2}
can be separated by the removal of at most
k
edges.

Accessibility was originally a purely group theoretical idea, first considered by Wall
[
49
]
. A finitely generated group is said to be
accessible
if it is isomorphic to the fundamental group of a finite graph of groups with finite edge groups, where each vertex group has at most one end. It was shown by Thomassen–Woess that a finitely generated group is accessible if and only if its Cayley graphs are accessible
[
47
, Thm. 1.1]
.

1.2.
Menger’s theorem

We next state a classical result which will play a key role throughout this paper, known as Menger’s theorem.
We first need to introduce some notation.

Definition 1.4
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, and let
x,y\in V\Gamma\sqcup\Omega\Gamma
be distinct. Define the
vertex separation
of
x
and
y
, denoted
\vs(x,y)
, as

\vs(x,y)=\inf\{|S|:S\subset V\Gamma-\{x,y\},\ \text{$x$, $y$ lie in distinct components of $\Gamma-S$}\}.

Similarly, define their
edge separation
, denoted
\es(x,y)
, as

\es(x,y)=\inf\{|F|:F\subset E\Gamma,\ \text{$x$, $y$ lie in distinct components of $\Gamma-F$}\}.

We now define the
minimal vertex end-cut size of
\Gamma
as

\vs(\Gamma)=\min\{\vs(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega\Gamma,\ \omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}\}.

We similarly define the
minimal edge end-cut size of
\Gamma
as

\es(\Gamma)=\min\{\es(\omega_{1},\omega_{2}):\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega\Gamma,\ \omega_{1}\neq\omega_{2}\}.

If
\Gamma
has at most one end, we adopt the convention that
\es(\Gamma)=\vs(\Gamma)=\infty
.

The following is immediate, but useful to note.

Proposition 1.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, bounded valence graph where every vertex has degree at most
d\geq 0
. Then

\vs(\Gamma)\leq\es(\Gamma)\leq d\cdot\vs(\Gamma)
.

We may now state Menger’s theorem as follows:

Theorem 1.6

(Menger)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph, and let
x,y\in V\Gamma\sqcup\Omega\Gamma
. Fix
N\geq 1
. Then
\es(x,y)\geq N
if and only if there exists
N
pairwise edge-disjoint paths connecting
x
to
y
.
Similarly, we also have that
\vs(x,y)\geq N
if and only if there exists
N
pairwise internally vertex-disjoint paths connecting
x
to
y
.

This result was first shown to hold in finite graphs by Menger
[
35
]
. It was later extended to locally finite, infinite graphs by Halin
[
27
]
.

Remark 1.7
.

One cannot drop the locally finite hypothesis as counterexamples exist. Consider a bi-infinite path
L
plus one extra vertex
v
, and add an edge between
v
and every vertex in
L
. The resulting graph
\Gamma
is two-ended and
\vs(\Gamma)=2
, but
\Gamma
does not contain two disjoint bi-infinite paths.

Corollary 1.8
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph, and suppose that
\vs(\Gamma)\geq N
for some
N\geq 1
. Then between any two ends
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega\Gamma
there exists a collection of
N
pairwise disjoint bi-infinite rays connecting
\omega_{1}
to
\omega_{2}
.

1.3.
Coarse notions

We briefly introduce some terminology relating to coarse geometry, which will follow us throughout this paper.

Let
X
be a metric space. Let
x\in X
and
A\subset X
. We define the distance between
x
and
A
as

\dist_{X}(x,A):=\inf\{\dist_{X}(x,a):a\in A\}.

If
B\subset X
is another subset, the
Hausdorff distance between
A
and
B
is defined as

\operatorname{Haus}_{X}(A,B):=\max\left\{\sup_{a\in A}\dist_{X}(a,B)\ ,\ \sup_{b\in B}\dist_{X}(b,A)\right\}.

Note that
\operatorname{Haus}_{X}(A,B)
is finite if and only if
A
is contained in a finite neighbourhood of
B
and vice versa.

Next, we introduce one of the central objects of this paper.

Definition 1.9

(Quasi-isometry)
.

Let
X
,
Y
be metric spaces,
\lambda\geq 1
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
. Then a map
\psi:X\to Y
is a
(\lambda,\varepsilon)
-
quasi-isometric embedding
if

\tfrac{1}{\lambda}\dist_{X}(x,y)-\varepsilon\leq\dist_{Y}(\psi(x),\psi(y))\leq\lambda\dist_{X}(x,y)+\varepsilon,

for all
x,y\in X
. We call
\psi
a
(\lambda,\varepsilon)
-quasi-isometry
if, in addition to the above, we have that

\operatorname{Haus}_{Y}(Y,\psi(X))\leq\varepsilon.

A map satisfying this second condition is said to be
coarsely surjective
. If there exists a quasi-isometry
\psi:X\to Y
, we say that
X
and
Y
are
quasi-isometric
.

Finally, let
\eta\geq 0
. If
\varphi:X\to Y
is a quasi-isometry such that
\dist_{X}(\varphi\circ\psi(x),x)\leq\eta
for all
x\in X
, then we call
\varphi
a
\eta
-quasi-inverse to
\psi
. Note that every quasi-isometry has a quasi-inverse.

The next two results demonstrate that, among over things, we may assume without loss of generality that our quasi-isometries are continuous.

Proposition 1.10
.

Let
\Gamma
,
\Pi
be connected graphs, and
\psi:\Gamma\to\Pi
a quasi-isometric embedding. Then there exists a subgraph
\Lambda\subset\Pi
and a surjective, continuous quasi-isometry
\varphi:\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\Lambda
. The inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Pi
is also a quasi-isometric embedding. Moreover, if
\Gamma
is bounded valence then so is
\Lambda
.

Proof.

We construct
\varphi
as follows. For every
v\in V\Gamma
, define
\varphi(v)=\psi(v)
. For every edge
e\in E\Gamma
, with endpoints
a
,
b
, set
\varphi(e)
to a geodesic path between
\psi(a)
and
\psi(b)
. Let
\Lambda=\varphi(\Gamma)
. We have that
\varphi
is thus a continuous, surjective map
\varphi:\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\Lambda
. We claim that this is a quasi-isometry.
Indeed, note that for all
x,y\in V\Gamma
we have by construction that

\dist_{\Lambda}(\varphi(x),\psi(y))=\dist_{\Pi}(\varphi(x),\psi(y)).

It follows that
\varphi:\Gamma\twoheadrightarrow\Lambda
is a quasi-isometric embedding since
\psi
is, and thus
\varphi
is a quasi-isometry. It is also clear from this construction that the inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Pi
is also a quasi-isometric embedding.

Suppose now that
\Gamma
is bounded valence. We claim that
\Lambda
is also bounded valence. Let
d\geq 0
be such that every vertex in
\Gamma
has degree at most
d
. Fix
\lambda\geq 1
such that
\varphi
is a
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasi-isometry.
Fix
v\in V\Lambda
, and let
u\in\varphi^{-}1(v)
. Let
v_{1},v_{2}\ldots
be the neighbours of
v
in
\Lambda
, and for each
v_{i}
choose some point
u_{i}\in\varphi^{-1}(v_{i})
. Note that each
u_{i}
lies inside a bounded neighbourhood
N
of
u
. Since
\Gamma
is bounded valence,
N
intersects a bounded number of edges, say
M
.
Recall that
\varphi
maps edges to geodesics between the images of their endpoints. This implies that if
e
is a (closed) edge in
\Gamma
, then
\varphi(e)
intersects at most
2\lambda+1
distinct vertices in
\Lambda
. Thus, the image
N
in
\Lambda
can contain at most
k=M(2\lambda+1)
distinct vertices. Thus,
v
has at most
k
neighbours in
\Lambda
. Since
v
was arbitrary and all our bounds were uniform, it follows that
\Lambda
is bounded valence.
∎

A similar construction to that given in Proposition
1.10
also gives the following.

Proposition 1.11
.

Let
\Gamma
,
\Pi
be connected graphs and
\psi:\Gamma\to\Pi
a quasi-isometry. Then there exists a continuous quasi-inverse
\varphi:\Pi\to\Gamma
.

Continuity is important for us, as it ensures that suitable restrictions of quasi-isometries are also quasi-isometries.

Proposition 1.12
.

Let
\Gamma
,
\Pi
be bounded valence, connected graphs. Let
\psi:\Pi\to\Gamma
be a continuous quasi-isometry. Let
\Lambda\subset\Pi
be a connected subgraph such that the inclusion map
\iota:\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Pi
is a quasi-isometric embedding. Then the restriction
\psi|_{\Lambda}:\Lambda\twoheadrightarrow\psi(\Lambda)
is a quasi-isometry.

Proof.

Let
x,y\in\Lambda
, and let
p
be a geodesic in
\Lambda
connecting
x
to
y
of length
n
. Then
\psi(p)
contains at most at
cn
vertices, where
c>0
is some constant depending only on the quasi-isometry constants of
\psi
and the maximum degree of
\Pi
. As
\psi
is continuous, we deduce that
\psi(p)
contains a path of between
\psi(x)
and
\psi(y)
of bounded length. This gives the upper bound.
For the lower bound, simply note that

\dist_{\psi(\Lambda)}(\psi(x),\psi(y))\geq\dist_{\Gamma}(\psi(x),\psi(y))
, and apply the fact that
\iota
and
\psi
are quasi-isometric embeddings.
∎

Remark 1.13
.

In the statement of Proposition
1.12
it is important to note that we consider
\psi(\Lambda)
with its own intrinsic path metric, and not the metric induced by the ambient graph
\Pi
. This is why we require continuity of
\psi
, lest
\psi(\Lambda)
may not even be connected.

Finally, we introduce the notion of a quasi-action.

Definition 1.14

(Quasi-actions)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, and let
\QI(\Gamma)
denote the set of quasi-isometries
\Gamma\to\Gamma
. Let
G
be a group, then a
\lambda
-quasi-action
of
G
on
\Gamma
is a map

G\to\QI(\Gamma),\ \ g\mapsto\varphi_{g},

such that the following hold:

(1)

Each
\varphi_{g}
is a
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasi-isometry,

(2)

For every
g,h\in G
,
x\in\Gamma
, we have that

\dist_{\Gamma}(\varphi_{gh}(x),\varphi_{g}\circ\varphi_{h}(x))\leq\lambda,

(3)

For every
g\in G
,
\varphi_{g}
and
\varphi_{g^{-1}}
are
\lambda
-quasi-inverses.

We say that a quasi-action as above is
B
-cobounded, where
B\geq 0
is some constant, if for all
x,y\in\Gamma
there exists some
g\in G
such that

\dist_{\Gamma}(x,\varphi_{g}(y))\leq B
.

Let
X
be a connected graph equipped with an action
G\curvearrowright X
, and let
\Gamma
be another connected graph. If
\varphi:X\to\Gamma
is a quasi-isometry with quasi-inverse
\psi:\Gamma\to X
, then this induces a quasi-action of
G
on
\Gamma
via

\varphi_{g}:=\varphi\circ g\circ\psi
.
It is easy to check that if the original action is cobounded, then the resulting quasi-action is cobounded. Also, if both
\varphi
and
\psi
are continuous then clearly so is every
\varphi_{g}
.

1.4.
Cuts and quasi-isometries

Our interest in quasi-actions is mainly driven by how they translate cut-sets. To this end we prove a series of lemmas which will find use throughout this paper.

Lemma 1.15
.

Let
\psi:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}
be a quasi-isometry between connected, locally finite graphs. Then there exists
r,R\geq 0
such that the following holds:

Let
S\subset V\Gamma_{1}
, and let
x,y\in[\Gamma_{1}-B_{\Gamma_{1}}(S;R)]\cup\Omega\Gamma
be such that
x
and
y
lie in distinct components of
\Gamma_{1}-S
. Then for all
g\in G
we have that
\psi(x)
and
\psi(y)
lie in distinct components of
\Gamma_{2}-B_{\Gamma_{2}}(\psi(S);r)
.

Proof.

Let
\varphi
be some choice of quasi-inverse.
Fix
\lambda>1
sufficiently large such that both
\psi
and
\varphi
are
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasi-isometries and
\varphi
is a
\lambda
-quasi-inverse to
\psi
.
Fix
r,R\geq 0
at some large values we will specify later, and let
x,y\in[\Gamma_{1}-B_{\Gamma_{1}}(S;R)]\cup\Omega\Gamma_{1}
lie in distinct components of
\Gamma-S
. To ease notation, let
S^{\prime}=B_{\Gamma_{2}}(\psi(S);r)
,
x^{\prime}=\psi(x)
,
y^{\prime}=\psi(y)
.

Suppose that
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
lie in the same component of
\Gamma_{2}-S^{\prime}
. Then there is some path
p
connecting
x^{\prime}
to
y^{\prime}
.
Then the
\lambda^{2}
-neighbourhood of
\varphi(p)
contains a path
p^{\prime}
connecting
x
to
y
. Such a path must intersect
S
, and so we deduce that
p
must intersect a bounded neighbourhood of
\psi(S)
. Set
r
to be precisely this bound, which depends only on
\lambda
. We now just choose
R
large enough so that
x^{\prime}
and
y^{\prime}
necessarily lie outside of
S^{\prime}
.
∎

We now apply Lemma
1.15
to graphs equipped with quasi-actions. The following will be helpful.

Lemma 1.16
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph equipped with a cobounded quasi-action by a group
G
. Then there exists
C\geq 1
such that the following holds. If
S\subset V\Gamma
is a finite set of vertices, and
Z\subset\Gamma
is a finite connected component of
\Gamma-S
, then

\dist_{\Gamma}(z,S)<C\diam_{\Gamma}(S)+C,

for all
z\in Z
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda>1
such that the quasi-action of
G
on
\Gamma
is a
\lambda
-quasi-action, and
B
such that this quasi-action is
B
-cobounded. Fix
r,R\geq 0
as in Lemma
1.15
.
Let
S
be a finite set of vertices and
Z
a finite connected component of
\Gamma-S
. Since
Z
is finite, we may choose
z\in Z
as to maximise
D:=\dist_{\Gamma}(z,S)
. Assume without loss of generality that
D>R
.
Fix
s\in S
, and choose
g\in G
such that
\dist_{\Gamma}(z,\varphi_{g}(s))\leq B
. Let
\omega
be any end in
\Gamma
, and let
\xi=g\omega
. Let
y=\varphi_{g}(z)
. We have that
B_{\Gamma}(\varphi_{g}(S);r)
separates
y
from
\xi
. To ease notation, write
S^{\prime}=B_{\Gamma}(\varphi_{g}(S);r)
. Since
D
is assumed to be large, we have that
S^{\prime}
is contained entirely within
Z
. In fact,
S^{\prime}
lies outside the
\diam_{\Gamma}(S)
-neighbourhood of
S
, so every vertex in
S^{\prime}
lies in the same connected component of
\Gamma-S
, and so there is exactly one infinite connected component of
\Gamma-S^{\prime}
. Thus, for
S^{\prime}
to separate
y
from
\xi
we must have that
y
lies in
Z
, and
S^{\prime}
separates
y
from
S
.

Since
z
was chosen to lie a maximal distance from
S
, we see that
\dist_{\Gamma}(y,S)\leq\dist_{\Gamma}(z,S)
. Every path connecting
y
to
S
must pass through
S^{\prime}
, so we deduce that

\displaystyle\dist_{\Gamma}(y,S)

\displaystyle\geq\dist_{\Gamma}(y,S^{\prime})+\inf_{s\in S}\dist_{\Gamma}(s,S^{\prime})

\displaystyle\geq[\tfrac{1}{\lambda}D-\lambda-r]+[D-B-\lambda\diam_{\Gamma}(S)-\lambda-r].

Combining and simplifying the above, we deduce that

D\leq\lambda^{2}\diam_{\Gamma}(S)+\lambda(2\lambda+2r+B).

Thus, by setting
C=\lambda(2\lambda+2r+B)
we are done.
∎

If we assume our graph has more than one end then we can say even more.

Lemma 1.17
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph equipped with a cobounded quasi-action by a group
G
. Suppose further that
\Gamma
has more than one end. Then there exists
C\geq 1
such that the following holds. If
Z\subset\Gamma
is a finite (not necessarily connected) subgraph such that
\Gamma-Z
is connected, then

\dist_{\Gamma}(z,\delta Z)<C,

for all
z\in Z
.

Proof.

Fix
\lambda>1
such that the quasi-action of
G
on
\Gamma
is a
\lambda
-quasi-action, and
B
such that this quasi-action is
B
-cobounded. Fix
r\geq 0
as in Lemma
1.15
.

Since
\Gamma
is multi-ended, there exists a finite connected subgraph
K\subset\Gamma
such that
\Gamma-K
has at least two infinite components. By Lemma
1.15
we have that
N:=B_{\Gamma}(\varphi_{g}(K);r)
separates at least two infinite components of
\Gamma
, for all
g\in G
.
Now, let
Z
be a finite subgraph of
\Gamma
such that
\Gamma-Z
is connected. Suppose that there exists some
z\in Z
such that

\dist_{\Gamma}(z,\delta Z)\geq\lambda\diam_{\Gamma}(K)+\lambda+2r+B.

Then there exists some
g\in G
such that
N
lies entirely within
Z
. If
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
are any two infinite rays in
\Gamma-N
, then clearly both rays will leave
Z
at some point. In particular, since
\Gamma-Z
is connected we have that
r_{1}
,
r_{2}
lie in the same component of
\Gamma-N
. Thus, there is exactly one infinite connected component of
\Gamma-N
, a contradiction. The lemma therefore follows by setting
C=\lambda\diam_{\Gamma}(K)+\lambda+2r+B
.
∎

Finally we note application of Lemma
1.16
. Firstly, a definition which will be helpful for brevity later on.

Definition 1.18

(Almost 2-connected graph)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a infinite, connected, locally finite graph. We say that
\Gamma
is
almost 2-connected
if there exists a unique maximal 2-connected infinite subgraph
\Gamma_{0}\subset\Gamma
and the inclusion map
\Gamma_{0}\hookrightarrow\Gamma
is a quasi-isometry. The subgraph
\Gamma_{0}
is called the
2-connected core
of
\Gamma
.

Note that every maximal 2-connected subgraph is necessarily isometrically embedded. This means that checking the quasi-isometry condition in the above definition amounts to just checking that the inclusion is coarsely surjective. Intuitively, an almost 2-connected graph
\Gamma
is obtained from a 2-connected graph
\Gamma_{0}
by ‘attaching’ a selection of boundedly small finite graphs at cut vertices.

Lemma 1.19
.

Let
\Gamma
be a infinite, connected, locally finite graph equipped with a cobounded quasi-action. Suppose
\vs(\Gamma)>1
. Then
\Gamma
is almost 2-connected.

Proof.

Since
\vs(\Gamma)>1
, no cut vertex in
\Gamma
separates ends. It follows from Lemma
1.16
that there exists some uniform constant
C\geq 0
such that there is exactly one infinite, maximal, 2-connected subgraph
\Lambda
of
\Gamma
, and every other such subgraph has diameter at most
C
. The inclusion map
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma
is clearly coarsely surjective, and thus a quasi-isometry.
∎

1.5.
Planar graphs

Recall that a
topological embedding
between topological spaces
X
,
Y
is a continuous injection
f:X\hookrightarrow Y
which restricts to a homeomorphism between
X
and
f(X)
(with the subspace topology). We adopt the following convention.

Definition 1.20

(Planar graph)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a graph. We say that
\Gamma
is
planar
if there exists topological embedding
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Such an embedding is called a
drawing
.

The above leaves room for strange embeddings. An embedding
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
is called
pointed
if for any 1-way infinite simple ray
r
in
\Gamma
, the images of the vertices in
r
accumulate at exactly one point in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The closure of the image of a planar graph in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
can be viewed as an embedding of a particular
compactification
of
\Gamma
. In this paper, we will generally restrict ourselves to the case where this closure is precisely the Freudenthal compactification of
\Gamma
.
This can generally be ensured thanks to the following, due to Richter–Thomassen
[
42
]
.

Proposition 1.21

(
[
42
, Lem. 12]
)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a 2-connected locally finite planar graph. Then the Freudenthal compactification
\overline{\Gamma}
embeds into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

This result can be pushed further, and the 2-connected assumption may be dropped. The following argument was suggested by Bruce Richter.

Proposition 1.22
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, planar graph. Then the Freudenthal compactification
\overline{\Gamma}
embeds into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof.

Fix an embedding
\vartheta:\Gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
We will find a 2-connected, locally finite planar
\Pi
such that (some subdivision of)
\Gamma
is a subgraph of
\Pi
and this inclusion is a quasi-isometry. From there, the embedding of
\overline{\Pi}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
given by Proposition
1.21
will induce an embedding of
\overline{\Gamma}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
.
Intuitively, our plan is to add a small cycle surrounding each cut vertex.
In order to make this sketch formal, we will construct
\Pi
as an ascending union of finite planar graphs, which is necessarily planar. Indeed, if an ascending union of finite planar graphs contained some subdivision of
K_{5}
or
K_{3,3}
then certainly some finite graph in this chain would have to, which is contradicts Kuratowski’s theorem.

We first assume that
\Gamma
contains no cut edges. That is, for every
e\in E\Gamma
we have that
\Gamma-e
is connected.
Choose some root
v_{0}\in V\Gamma
. We call
u\in V\Gamma
a
marked vertex
if it is a cut vertex of
\Gamma
. Given
r\geq 0
we construct
\Pi_{i}
as follows. Consider the closed
r
-neighbourhood of
v_{0}
in
\Gamma
, which we will denote
N_{r}=B_{\Gamma}(v_{0};r)
. This is a finite subgraph of
\Gamma
. Let
N_{r}^{\prime}
denote the subdivision of
N_{r}
, where we subdivide every edge into three edges. Suppose
u\in VN_{r}
is a marked vertex of
\Gamma
, and
\dist_{\Gamma}(v_{0},u)<r
. Let
u^{\prime}
be the image of
u
in
N_{r}^{\prime}
. Let
u_{1},\ldots,u_{n}
be the neighbours of
u^{\prime}
in
VN_{r}^{\prime}
. Since
\Gamma
is locally finite,
n
is finite. Let
e_{i}
be the (necessarily unique) edge of
N_{r}^{\prime}
with endpoints
u^{\prime}
,
u_{i}
. The drawing
\vartheta
restricts to a drawing of
N_{r}
, and thus of
N_{r}^{\prime}
since these graphs are homeomorphic. This induces a cyclic ordering to
e_{1},\ldots,e_{n}
, particularly their clockwise ordering about
u^{\prime}
. We assume this is exactly how they are ordered. We add a path of length 2 joining every
u_{i}
to
u_{i+1}
, with indices taken modulo
n
. We repeat this for every such
u^{\prime}
, and call the resulting graph
\Pi_{r}
. By construction
\Pi_{r}
is a finite planar graph.
Repeating this construction for every
r
, it is clear that we have an ascending chain

\Pi_{0}\subset\Pi_{1}\subset\Pi_{2}\subset\cdots.

Let
\Pi=\bigcup_{r}\Pi_{r}
.
Let
\Gamma^{\prime}
denote the subdivision of
\Gamma
, where each edge is divided into three edges. Clearly
\Gamma
is homeomorphic to
\Gamma^{\prime}
, and the natural map
\Gamma\to\Gamma^{\prime}
is a
(3,0)
-quasi-isometry. We have a natural inclusion
\Gamma^{\prime}\hookrightarrow\Pi
which is an isometry onto its image (indeed, the new paths added to
\Pi
) create no new shortcuts. Every vertex of
\Pi
is adjacent to a vertex of
\Gamma^{\prime}
, so this map is a quasi-isometry.
Finally, note that
\Pi
is certainly 2-connected. For suppose
\Pi
contained a cut vertex
w
. This vertex certainly must lie in
\Gamma^{\prime}
, as every
u\in V\Pi-V\Gamma^{\prime}
has degree 2 and lies on a simple cycle so cannot be a cut vertex. Since
\Gamma
contains no cut edges this implies that
w
is induced by a cut vertex of
\Gamma
. But then by construction if
u_{1}
,
u_{2}
are neighbours of
w
in
\Pi
then there is a path connecting them which avoids
w
. Thus
\Pi
is 2-connected. Applying Proposition
1.21
we conclude that the Freudenthal compactification of any connected, locally finite, planar graph
without cut edges
embeds into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Finally, we deal with the case where
\Gamma
has cut edges. We replace
\Gamma
with
\Gamma^{\prime}
where
\Gamma^{\prime}
is obtained from
\Gamma
by ‘doubling’ every edge. That is,
V\Gamma^{\prime}=V\Gamma
, and if
u
,
v
in
\Gamma
are connected by
k
edges in
\Gamma
then they are connected by
2k
edges in
\Gamma
.
The inclusion
\Gamma\hookrightarrow\Gamma^{\prime}
is certainly a quasi-isometry, and
\Gamma^{\prime}
is clearly a locally finite. To see that
\Gamma^{\prime}
is planar, note that doubling edges clearly preserves planarity in finite graphs. We now repeat a similar construction to above, and write
\Gamma^{\prime}
as an ascending union of finite planar graphs, where the
r
-th element of this chain is obtained by doubling the edges in the
r
-ball about some root vertex
v_{0}
in
\Gamma
. By the previous case, the Freudenthal compactification of
\Gamma^{\prime}
is planar, and so the same can be said about
\Gamma
. A cartoon of this full construction can be found in Figure

.

In light of Proposition

Definition 1.23

(Good drawing)

Let

Some planar graphs admit even nicer drawings in the plane, in the following sense. We say that a planar graph
\Gamma
admits a
vertex accumulation point-free drawing
, or
VAP-free drawing
, if there exists a topological embedding
\vartheta:\Gamma\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{2}
such that
\vartheta(V\Gamma)
is a discrete subset of
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. The following is a standard fact and follows easily from Proposition
1.22
.

Corollary 1.24
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, one-ended, planar graph. Then
\Gamma
admits a VAP-free drawing.

Remark 1.25
.

It is important to note that not every planar graph admits a VAP-free drawing. For example, the standard Cayley graph of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}\ast\mathbb{Z}_{2}
(a.k.a. the ‘tree of flats’) is planar but admits no such drawing.

An important feature of planar graphs is their
faces
. Informally, it is clear what we mean by a ‘face’. However, this word could be referring to several different but closely related concepts, especially in the realm of infinite planar graphs. Is a ‘face’ a component of
\mathbb{S}^{2}-\vartheta(\overline{\Gamma})
, or actually a particular form of subgraph of
\Gamma
? For the sake of clarity, we now set up some notation to help us discuss faces without ambiguity.

Definition 1.26

(Faces and facial subgraphs)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite, planar graph with a fixed good drawing
\vartheta
. The connected components of
\mathbb{S}^{2}-\vartheta(\overline{\Gamma})
are referred to as the
faces
of
\Gamma
, with respect to the drawing
\vartheta
. Let
\mathcal{D}\Gamma
denote the set of faces.

Given
U\in\mathcal{D}\Gamma
, write

\mathcal{F}(U):=\vartheta^{-1}(\partial U)-\Omega\Gamma.

We call the subgraph
\mathcal{F}(U)
the
facial subgraph of
\Gamma
bordering
U
.
Let

\mathcal{F}\Gamma

denote the set of facial subgraphs of
\Gamma
.

\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\Gamma=\{f\in\mathcal{F}\Gamma:\text{$f$ is infinite}\},\ \ \mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{c}}\Gamma=\{f\in\mathcal{F}\Gamma:\text{$f$ is compact}\}.

Remark 1.27
.

Note that
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\Gamma
need not be connected in general. However, the closure of
f
in
\overline{\Gamma}
will always be connected.

Remark 1.28
.

Really, we should include mention of the drawing
\vartheta
in our notation above. However, for our purposes this drawing will always be fixed in advance and so there is no risk of confusion.

There is a key benefit to working with 2-connected planar graphs, which is that their faces are incredibly sensible. More generally, we have the following.

Proposition 1.29

(
[
42
, Prop. 3]
)
.

Let
K
be a compact, 2-connected, locally connected subset of the sphere. Then the boundary of every component of
\mathbb{S}^{2}-K
is a simple closed curve.

In particular, if
\Gamma
is a 2-connected locally finite planar graph then the closure of every
f\in\mathcal{F}\Gamma
in
\overline{\Gamma}
is a simple closed curve. More precisely, if
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{c}}\Gamma
then
f
is a simple loop, and if
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\Gamma
then
f
is a disjoint union of bi-infinite lines.

1.6.
Filling loops in graphs

We conclude this section by introducing some terminology relating to gluing 2-cells to graphs.

Definition 1.30
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph, and
\varepsilon\geq 0
. The
\varepsilon
-filling of
\Gamma
, denoted
K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)
, is the 2-dimensional polyhedral complex obtained from
\Gamma
by attaching a 2-cell along every closed loop of length at most
\varepsilon
.

The following is an easy exercise.

Proposition 1.31
.

Let
\Gamma
be a locally finite graph and
G\curvearrowright\Gamma
quasi-transitively. Then for all
\varepsilon\geq 0
, this action extends to a cocompact action
G\curvearrowright K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)
.

We now conclude Section
1
with one final definition.

Definition 1.32

(Coarsely simply connected)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\varepsilon>0
. We say a closed loop
\ell
in
\Gamma
is
\varepsilon
-fillable
if
\ell
is null-homotopic in
K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)
. We say that
\Gamma
is

Remark 1.33
.

It is a classical fact that a finitely generated group is finitely presented if and only if its Cayley graphs are coarsely simply connected.

3.
Cutting up graphs, and coboundary diameters

In this section we collect definitions and results relating to cutting up graphs into simpler pieces. We also discuss in what sense these cuts can be passed through quasi-isometries.

3.1.
Preliminaries

We open by standardising our terminology.
Let
X
be a connected graph. An
edge-cut
, or just
cut
, of
X
is a subset
F\subset EX
such that
X-F
is disconnected. We say that a cut
F
is
finite
if
|F|<\infty
.
A finite cut
F
is said to be
tight
if
X-F
contains exactly two connected components.
Given a subset
b\subset VX
, let
b^{\ast}
denote the complement
VX-b
. Let
\delta b
, called the
coboundary of
b
, denote the set of edges in
X
with exactly one endpoint in
b
.
The term ‘coboundary’ is suggestive of the cohomology at play here, since if we take a the 0-cochain in
C^{0}(X;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
supported by
U
, then
\delta U
is precisely the support of coboundary of this cochain.
Given any
b\subset VX
, clearly
\delta b
is a cut and
\delta b=\delta b^{\ast}
.
Let

{\mathscr{B}}X=\{b\subset VX:\text{$\delta b$ is finite}\}.

Clearly
{\mathscr{B}}X
is closed under the operations of union, intersection, and complementation. This makes
{\mathscr{B}}X
into a Boolean ring. That is, a commutative ring with unity such that every element
r
satisfies idempotent
r^{2}=r
. The multiplicative operation is intersection, while the additive operation is symmetric difference.

Given a group
G
acting on
X
, this induces an action of
G
upon
{\mathscr{B}}X
. Thus, we may view
{\mathscr{B}}X
as a
G
-module.
Let
{\mathscr{B}}_{n}X
denote the subring of
{\mathscr{B}}X
generated by elements
A
such that
|\delta b|<n
. We may abuse terminology and say that
b\in{\mathscr{B}}X
is
tight
if its coboundary
\delta b
is a tight cut.
Given
b_{1},b_{2}\in{\mathscr{B}}X
, we say that
b_{1}

crosses

b_{2}
if the intersections

b_{1}\cap b_{2},\ \ b_{1}\cap b_{2}^{\ast},\ \ b_{1}^{\ast}\cap b_{2},\ \ b_{1}^{\ast}\cap b_{2}^{\ast}

are all non-empty. If
b_{1}
and
b_{2}
do not cross, we say they are
nested
. We say that a subset
{\mathcal{E}}\subset{\mathscr{B}}X
is
nested
if it is closed under taking complements and any two
b_{1},b_{2}\in{\mathcal{E}}
are nested.
We now state the following key theorem.

Theorem 3.1

(Dicks–Dunwoody,
[
6
, II.2.20]
)
.

Let
X
be a connected graph and
G
a group acting on
X
. Then there is a sequence
{\mathcal{E}}_{1}\subset{\mathcal{E}}_{2}\subset\ldots
of
G
-invariant nested subsets of
{\mathscr{B}}X
consisting of tight elements, such that
{\mathcal{E}}_{n}
generates
{\mathscr{B}}_{n}X
as a Boolean ring.

The following characterisation of accessibility is helpful, due to Thomassen–Woess
[
47
]
.

Theorem 3.2

(
[
47
, Thm. 7.6]
)
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite graph equipped with a quasi-transitive action by a group
G
. Then
X
is accessible if and only if there exists
n\geq 1
such that
{\mathscr{B}}X={\mathscr{B}}_{n}X
. In other words,
X
is accessible if and only if there is a nested,
G
-invariant,
G
-finite generating set of
{\mathscr{B}}X
.

Recall that the set of directed edges of a tree has a natural ordering.
The structure of nested sets can be encoded similarly in a tree via the following theorem, originally due to Dunwoody
[
10
]
.

Theorem 3.3

(
[
10
, Thm. 2.1]
)
.

Let
({\mathcal{E}},\leq)
be a partially ordered set equipped with an involution
\ast:{\mathcal{E}}\to{\mathcal{E}}
satisfying the following:

(1)

For any
A,B\in{\mathcal{E}}
, at least one of
A\leq B
,
A\leq B^{\ast}
,
A^{\ast}\leq B
,
A^{\ast}\leq B^{\ast}
hold.

(2)

If

for

, then
B^{\ast}\leq A^{\ast}
.

(3)

Given
A,B\in{\mathcal{E}}
, at most one of

,

hold. Similarly, at most one of
A\leq B
,
A^{\ast}\leq B
hold.

(4)

If
A\leq B
for

, then there are at most finitely many

such that
A\leq C\leq B
.

Then there exists a tree

such that the set of oriented edges of
T
can be naturally identified with
{\mathcal{E}}
, and the order
\leq
on
{\mathcal{E}}
is precisely the order determined by edge-paths in

.

We will refrain from proving this, but it is worth stating where the vertices of

come from. Define a relation
\sim
on
{\mathcal{E}}
as follows. Given

, say that

if

or

It is an exercise to check that

. The vertex set of

[

We combine the above with the following result. This was first observed by Dunwoody in

Proposition 3.4

3.2.
Inner and outer coboundary diameters

Let
\Gamma
be a graph and
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
a subgraph. Define the
coboundary of
\Lambda
, denoted
\delta\Lambda
, as simply the coboundary of its vertex set
\delta(V\Lambda)
.

Definition 3.8

(Coboundary diameters)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
a connected subgraph. Define the
inner coboundary diameter
of
\Lambda
, denoted
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}
, as

\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}=\sup\{\diam_{\Lambda}(\delta U\cap\Lambda):\text{$U$ is a connected component of $\Gamma-\Lambda$}\}.

Similarly, we define the
outer coboundary diameter
of
\Lambda
as

\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{out}}=\sup\{\|U\|_{\mathrm{in}}:\text{$U$ is a connected component of $\Gamma-\Lambda$}\}.

We say that
\Lambda
has
uniform coboundary
if both the inner and outer coboundary diameters of
\Lambda
are finite. If
\Lambda=\Gamma
, we adopt the convention that
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}=\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{out}}=0
.

We may write
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}^{\Gamma}
or
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{out}}^{\Gamma}
if the intended super-graph
\Gamma
is ever unclear.

Intuitively, we imagine the coboundary of
\Lambda
as a selection of tight cuts, separating
\Lambda
from the components of its complement. The inner diameter measures the size of these cuts from ‘inside’
\Lambda
, whereas the outer diameter measures this diameter from the other side of this cut. Without placing further restrictions on
\Lambda
, there is no reason these values need to correlate. However, we can say something in the presence of an appropriate group action.

Definition 3.9

(Quasi-transitively stabilised subgraph)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected graph and
G
a group acting on
\Gamma
by isometries. We say that a connected subgraph
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
is
quasi-transitively stabilised
if the set-wise stabiliser
\Stab(\Lambda)\leq G
acts quasi-transitively on
\Lambda
.

Lemma 3.10
.

Let
G
be a group acting on a connected, bounded valence graph
\Gamma
. Let
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
be a connected, quasi-transitively stabilised subgraph. If
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}
is finite, then
\Lambda
has uniform coboundary.

Proof.

Since
\Stab(\Lambda)
acts on
\Lambda
quasi-transitively and
\Gamma
has bounded valence, we see that
H
acts with finite quotient on the set of connected components of
\Gamma-\Lambda
. If
U
is a connected component of
\Gamma-\Lambda
, then we have that
\delta U
is a finite set of edges, and so
\|U\|_{\mathrm{in}}
is finite. Since there are only finitely many orbits of these components, we deduce that
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{out}}
must be finite, and so
\Lambda
has uniform coboundary.
∎

Proposition 3.11
.

Let
\Gamma
be a locally finite graph and
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
a connected subgraph with
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}<\infty
. Then the natural map
\Omega\Lambda\to\Omega\Gamma
is injective.

Proof.

Let
\gamma_{1},\gamma_{2}
be rays in
\Lambda
. We need to show that if
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
approach distinct ends in
\Lambda
, then they approach distinct ends in
\Gamma
. Let
K\subset\Lambda
be a compact subgraph such that infinite subpaths of
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
lie in distinct components of
\Lambda-K
. Let
U_{i}
denote the component of
\Lambda-K
containing the tail of
\gamma_{i}
.
Any path in
\Gamma
connecting
U_{1}
to
U_{2}
must pass through some connected component
U
of
\Gamma-\Lambda
, where
\delta U
intersects both
U_{1}
and
U_{2}
. Only finitely many such
U
exist since
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}
is finite, and moreover
\delta U
can only contain finitely many edges. Thus, let

K^{\prime}=K\cup\bigcup_{U}\delta U,

where
U
ranges over the aforementioned components of
\Gamma-\Lambda
. Clearly
K^{\prime}
is compact, and the tails of
\gamma_{1}
,
\gamma_{2}
lie in distinct components of
\Gamma-K^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 3.12
.

With
\Gamma
,
\Lambda
as above, i.e.
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}<\infty
, it follows from Proposition
3.11
that the closure of
\Lambda
in the Freudenthal compactification
\overline{\Gamma}
of
\Gamma
is naturally homeomorphic to
\overline{\Lambda}
. In particular, if
\Gamma
is planar and
\vartheta:\overline{\Gamma}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a good drawing of
\Gamma
, then the restriction of
\vartheta
to (the closure of)
\Lambda
is a good drawing of
\Lambda
.

Proposition 3.13
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite graph, and let
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
be a connected subgraph with uniform coboundary. Then the inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma
is a quasi-isometric embedding.

Proof.

Take a geodesic in
\Gamma
connecting
x,y\in V\Lambda
. Any segment of this geodesic lying outside of
\Lambda
has length at most
2+\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{out}}
, and we also know that there exists a path inside of
\Lambda
of length at most
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}
, with precisely the same endpoints. Thus, such a geodesic can be transformed into a path contained in
\Lambda
whose length is proportional to the length of the original geodesic. It follows immediately that the inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma
is a quasi-isometric embedding.
∎

3.3.
Tree decompositions and ends

We introduce some terminology.
Let
X
be a connected graph.
A
separation
of
X
is a triple
(Y,S,Z)
where
Y,S,Z\subset VX
are pairwise disjoint subsets such that
VX=Y\cup S\cup Z
, and there is no edge with one endpoint in
Y
and the other in
Z
. Such a separation is said to be
tight
if there exists connected components
C_{Y}
,
C_{Z}
of
X[Y]
,
X[Z]
respectively such that every vertex in
S
is adjacent to some vertex of
C_{Y}
and some vertex in
C_{Z}
.

A
tree decomposition
of
X
is a pair
(T,\mathcal{V})
, where
T
is a connected simplicial tree and
\mathcal{V}=(V_{v})_{v\in VT}
is a collection of subsets
V_{v}\subset VX
indexed by
VT
, called
bags
, such that the following hold:

(1)

VX=\bigcup_{v\in VT}V_{v}
,

(2)

If
v\in VT
lies on the unique geodesic connecting
u,w\in VT
, then
V_{u}\cap V_{w}\subset V_{v}
.

We say that this tree decomposition has
bounded adhesion
if there exists
N\geq 1
such that
|V_{u}\cap V_{w}|\leq N
for all edges
uw\in ET
. Given an edge
uw\in ET
, let
T_{1}
,
T_{2}
denote the connected components of
T-\{uw\}
. Then the
edge-separation
corresponding to this edge is the separation
(Y_{1},S,Y_{2})
where
S=V_{u}\cap V_{w}
and
Y_{i}=\bigcup_{s\in VT_{i}}V_{s}-S
for
i=1,2
. We say that a tree decomposition has
tight separations
if every edge-separation is tight. We call
S
an
adhesion set
.

If
G\curvearrowright X
, then we call this tree decomposition
G
-canonical
, or just
canonical
, if for all
g\in G
,
u\in VT
we have that
gV_{u}\in\mathcal{V}
. Note that this induces an action
G\curvearrowright T
.

The induced subgraphs
X[V_{u}]
are called the
parts
of
(T,\mathcal{V})
. We construct the
torso

X\llbracket V_{u}\rrbracket
corresponding to
u\in VT
by taking the part
X[V_{u}]
and adding an edge between
x,y\in V_{u}
if these two vertices lie in the same adhesion set. These additional edges are sometimes known as
virtual edges
.

If the group
G
acting on
X
is clear from context, then we may suppress mention of
G
from our terminology and simply call a tree decomposition
canonical
.

Remark 3.14
.

Note that torsos are generally connected, but not necessarily subgraphs of
X
. On the other hand, while parts are always subgraphs, they are often disconnected.
For our purposes, it will be more convenient to work directly with parts rather than torsos. We will see shortly that it is not too difficult to ensure that parts are connected by ‘thickening’ them up.

The following is an easy exercise, but worth noting.

Proposition 3.15
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph. Let
(T,\mathcal{V})
be a canonical tree decomposition, where
\mathcal{V}=(V_{u})_{u\in VT}
and let
r\geq 0
. For each
u\in VT
, let
V^{\prime}_{u}
denote the set of vertices which lie in the closed
r
-neighbourhood of
V_{u}
, and let
\mathcal{V}^{\prime}=(V^{\prime}_{u})_{u\in VT}
. Then
(T,\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
is a canonical tree decomposition. Moreover, if
(T,\mathcal{V})
has bounded adhesion and/or connected parts, then so does
(T,\mathcal{V}^{\prime})
.

It is helpful to view tree decompositions as essentially the graph-theoretical analogue of Bass–Serre trees found in geometric group theory. Indeed, if
G\curvearrowright X
then canonical tree decompositions of
X
induce splittings of
G
as a graph of groups in the usual way. For this reason, canonical tree decompositions play a key role in the study of accessibility in graphs. This is essentially due to the following standard proposition.

Proposition 3.16
.

Let
X
be a locally finite, connected graph and let
G\curvearrowright X
quasi-transitively. Let
(T,\mathcal{V})
be a
G
-canonical tree decomposition with bounded adhesion and connected parts, where
\mathcal{V}=(V_{u})_{u\in VT}
and
T/G
is compact. Then the following hold.

(1)

For each
u\in VT
, the stabiliser
\Stab(u)\leq G
acts quasi-transitively on
X_{u}
,

(2)

Each
X_{u}
has uniform coboundary, in the sense of Definition
3.8
,

(3)

This tree decomposition induces a natural partition

\Omega X\cong\Omega T\sqcup\bigsqcup_{u\in VT}\Omega X[V_{u}],

(4)

If each part
X[V_{u}]
is accessible then
X
is accessible.

Proof.

Property
1
follows from the fact that
\Stab(u)
must act with finitely many orbits on the set of edges going into
u
, since the induced action on
T
is cocompact.
Therefore,
X_{u}:=X[V_{u}]
is a
quasi-transitively stabilised subgraph
in the sense of Definition
3.9
. Property
2
then follows from Lemma
3.10
.

To see Property
3
, note that by Proposition
3.11
we have that the induced map
\Omega X_{u}\to\Omega X
is an injection. We may therefore identify
\Omega X_{u}
as a subset of
\Omega X
. Clearly
\Omega X_{u}\cap\Omega X_{v}=\emptyset
if
u\neq v
. Now, let
r
be a simple ray in
X
. Either
r
has infinite intersection with some
X_{v}
, or not.
In the first case, we may transform some tail of
r
into a ray which lies in some
\Omega X_{v}
. In the second case, we see that
r
projects to an infinite (though possibly not simple) ray in
T
. Even though this ray is not simple in
T
, it because
r
is simple in
X
we have that the induced path in
T
crosses each edge in
ET
a bounded number of times, and so clearly approaches some end in
T
.

Finally, suppose that every
X_{u}
is accessible. Then there is some
N\geq 1
such that every pair of ends in
\Omega X_{u}
can be separated in
X
by the removal on
N
edges. Clearly any end in
\Omega T
can be separated from any other end by the removal of a bounded number of edges, as can any two ends which lie in distinct
\Omega X_{u}
,
\Omega X_{v}
. Since there are only finitely many orbits of vertices in
T
, we deduce that any pair of ends can be separated by the removal of a bounded number of edges, and so
X
is accessible.
∎

Let

3.4.
Pushing big cuts through quasi-isometries

We quickly record the following pigeonhole argument, which will allow us to push the increased cut-size created in Theorem
3.17
through a quasi-isometry.

Lemma 3.21
.

Let
\Gamma_{1}
,
\Gamma_{2}
be bounded valence connected graphs, and let
\varphi:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}
be a quasi-isometry. Let
m>0
and fix a subgraph
\Lambda\subset\Gamma_{1}
such that
\vs(\Lambda)\geq m
. Then

\vs(\varphi(\Lambda))\geq Cm,

where
C=C(\varphi)>0
is some constant depending only on
\varphi
and
\Gamma_{1}
.

Proof.

We assume without loss of generality that
\varphi
is continuous by Proposition
1.10
.

Fix
\lambda\geq 1
such that
\varphi
is a
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasi-isometry. To simplify notation, let
\Lambda=\Lambda_{1}
and write
\varphi(\Lambda)=\Lambda_{2}
.
Suppose that there are
k
vertices
v_{1},\ldots,v_{k}
in
\Lambda_{2}
whose removal separates distinct ends
\omega_{1},\omega_{2}\in\Omega\Lambda_{2}
. Let
\xi_{i}=\varphi^{-1}(\omega_{i})
for each
i=1,2
, recalling that quasi-isometries induce well-defined bijections on the corresponding sets of ends. Since
\vs(\Lambda)\geq m
and the
\Gamma_{i}
are locally finite, we have by Menger’s theorem (
1.6
) that there exists
m
pairwise disjoint bi-infinite paths
\alpha_{1},\ldots,\alpha_{m}
in
\Lambda
between
\xi_{1}
and
\xi_{2}
.

Thus each
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}:=\varphi(\alpha_{i})
is a bi-infinite path between the ends
\omega_{1}
and
\omega_{2}
. Each
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}
must pass through some
v_{j}
, so by the Pigeonhole Principle there exists some
v_{j}
such that at least
m/k
of the
\alpha_{i}^{\prime}
pass through
v_{j}
. By relabelling, we can assume without loss of generality that
\alpha_{1}^{\prime},\ldots,\alpha_{m/k}^{\prime}
pass through
v_{1}
.
Let
x\in\varphi^{-1}(v_{1})
. As
\varphi
is a quasi-isometry, we have that
\alpha_{1},\ldots\alpha_{m/k}
must intersect the closed
r
-neighbourhood of
x
, for some
r=r(\varphi)>0
depending only on
\varphi
. Combining this observation with the assumption that
\Gamma_{1}
is bounded valence together with the fact that the
\alpha_{i}
are disjoint, we deduce that

\frac{m}{k}\leq|B_{\Gamma_{1}}(x;r)|.

The right-hand side is bounded above by some uniform constant since
\Gamma_{1}
is bounded valence. The lemma follows.
∎

3.5.
The Boolean ring
{\mathscr{B}}X
and quasi-isometries

We finally look at how elements of the ring
{\mathscr{B}}X
interact with quasi-isometries.

.

Let
\psi:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}

\operatorname{Haus}_{\Gamma_{2}}(\psi(b),b^{\prime})

\delta b^{\prime}
is contained in a bounded neighbourhood of

. Moreover if
\Gamma_{1}[b]

\Gamma_{2}[b^{\prime}]

Proof.

Let
\varphi:\Gamma_{2}\to\Gamma_{1}
be some choice of quasi-inverse to
\psi
.
Let
\lambda\geq 1
be such that
\psi
and
\varphi
are
(\lambda,\lambda)
-quasi-isometries, and
\varphi
is a
\lambda
-quasi-inverse to
\psi
.

Let

for some large

. Clearly if
\Gamma_{1}[b]
is connected then so is
\Gamma_{2}[b^{\prime}]
.
Suppose

and

are adjacent vertices in

such that
u\in b^{\prime}
and

.
Since
R
is sufficiently large compared to the quasi-isometry constants, we see that

and

and

.
∎

In

4.2.
The CHomP property and accessibility

The CHomP property is very relevant to the question of accessibility. In particular, we note that the arguments of
[
6

can be slightly modified to prove the following variant of one of their main results. We postpone the details of this modification to Appendix

Theorem 4.3

(Dicks–Dunwoody)
.

Let
K
be a connected, 2-dimensional polyhedral complex which is locally finite away from the 0-skeleton, and equipped with a cocompact action

{\mathscr{B}}K
admits a
G

G
-finite generating set.

Recall that a 2-dimensional polyhedral complex is said to be

Combining Theorem
4.3
with Theorem

, the following corollary is immediate, which we state now for later reference.

Corollary 4.4

Let

be a connected, locally finite graph equipped with a quasi-transitive group action

Note that simply connected complexes are clearly CHomP. In particular, the above implies Dunwoody’s theorem that finitely presented groups are accessible

]
.

Sadly, the CHomP property is still too strong for our purposes. For example, even a planar surface with non-empty boundary need not be CHomP. Proposition 4.15
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite graph equipped with a group action
G\curvearrowright X

is a
G
-invariant subring with a
G
-invariant, nested,
G
-finite generating set. Then there exists another
G
-invariant subring

which contains

, such that
S
admits a
G
-invariant, nested,
G
-finite generating set consisting of tight elements.

Proof.

First, note that any
b\in{\mathscr{B}}X
can be expressed as a disjoint union
b=c_{1}\sqcup\ldots\sqcup c_{k}
, where each
c_{i}\in{\mathscr{B}}X
is such that
X[c_{i}]
is connected. Now, by an application of Proposition

, every

is equal to the complement of a disjoint union of finitely many tight elements of

be a finite nested set, where each

is a tight element such that

lies in the subring generated by

is a nested set, and every

satisfies

, we see that the
G

is also nested. We may now take

to be the subring

This generating set is

clearly contains

∎

Remark 4.16
.

For the deformation space aficionados, Theorem
4.13
should be compared with
[
26
, Prop. 2.24]
of the Guirardel–Levitt paper on JSJ decompositions, which is a relative version of Dunwoody’s accessibility theorem for (relatively) finitely presented groups. This result states that if a given group is finitely presented relative to a collection of peripheral subgroups, then the relative JSJ deformation space exists over any class of subgroups
\mathscr{A}
. We briefly describe how this result relates to our own.

Set
\mathscr{A}
to the class of finite groups, and let
X
be a Cayley graph of a finitely generated group

. Let
\mathscr{H}
be a set of peripheral subgroups, say finitely generated for the sake of simplicity. Take

to be the set of some connected tubular neighbourhoods of the cosets of the elements of

, viewed as subgraphs of
X
.
Then it is not too hard to see that

is a finitely generated
G
-module if and only if the aforementioned relative JSJ deformation space exists.
Indeed, if
\mathcal{E}
is a
G
-finite nested generating set of
{\mathscr{B}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}X
, then the structure tree of
\mathcal{E}
is seen to be
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{H})
-universally elliptic and so the relative JSJ deformation space over
\mathscr{A}
exists. Conversely, if
{\mathscr{B}}_{{\mathcal{H}}}X
is not finitely generated as a
G
-module then we can find reduced
(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{H})
-trees with arbitrarily large quotients, which will contradict relative accessibility in the sense of
[
26
, Prop. 2.24]
.

It is also worth noting that if
G
is finitely presented relative to
\mathscr{H}
then this implies that the Cayley graph of

is coarsely CHomP relative to
{\mathcal{H}}
. The converse is false, however.

extends to a map

, where

is the annulus and

is identified with

are freely homotopic.
∎

In other words, the above lemma tells us that if

is taken to be sufficiently large then the map

induces the identity map on the set of free homotopy classes in

.
In
[

it is shown that finite presentability is a quasi-isometry invariant amongst finitely generated groups. The actual meat of the proof is devoted to proving the following.

Proposition 4.21
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta>0
such that the following holds.
Let
\ell
be a closed combinatorial loop, and suppose that
\varphi\circ\ell
is null-homotopic in
K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{1})
. Then
\ell
is null-homotopic in
K_{\eta}(\Gamma_{2})
. In particular, if
\Gamma_{2}
is coarsely simply connected then so is
\Gamma_{1}
.

Applying this, we can prove the following.

Proposition 4.22
.

For every sufficiently large
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\eta>0
such that
\psi:\Gamma_{1}\to\Gamma_{2}
extends to a continuous map
\hat{\psi}:K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{1})\to K_{\eta}(\Gamma_{2})
. Moreover, we may assume that the induced map

\hat{\psi}_{\ast}:H_{1}(K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{1});\mathbb{Z}_{2})\to H_{1}(K_{\eta}(\Gamma_{2});\mathbb{Z}_{2})

on the first homology is a surjection.

Proof.

Fix
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently large so that the hypothesis of Lemma
4.20
is satisfied, and choose
\eta>0
as in Proposition
4.21
.
Let
\ell
be a null-homotopic loop in

. We need only show that
\ell^{\prime}=\psi\circ\ell
is null-homotopic in
K_{2}:=K_{\eta}(\Gamma_{2})
.
Let
\ell^{\prime\prime}=\varphi\circ\ell^{\prime}
. Then
\ell^{\prime\prime}
and
\ell
are freely homotopic in

, and so
\ell^{\prime\prime}
is null-homotopic. Applying Proposition

we deduce that
\ell^{\prime}
is null-homotopic. We now extend
\psi
to a map
\hat{\psi}:K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma_{1})\to K_{\eta}(\Gamma_{2})
by mapping every 2-cell
\sigma
with boundary loop
\ell
to a homotopy disc bounded by
\psi\circ\ell
.

We now argue that the induced map on the first homology can be taken to be surjective. We have that the following diagram commutes:

.

If

.

We thus have a continuous map

5.
Planar graphs and the CHomP property

We now wish to record the fact that locally finite, 2-connected planar graphs with ‘small faces’ are coarsely CHomP relative to their infinite faces. The main result of this section is Theorem
5.5
.

Let
K
be a locally finite, 2-connected, planar, polyhedral complex. Then the 1-skeleton
K^{1}
is a locally finite planar graph and thus admits a good drawing, in the sense of Definition
1.23
. With this in mind, an embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
is said to be a
good embedding
if it restricts to a good drawing of the 1-skeleton.
1
1

1

It is not immediately clear whether every such complex has a good embedding, but we will not need this. Every complex we consider will automatically have this property.

If we assume that
K
has a fixed good embedding, we may now define the set of
facial subgraphs
of
K
as

\mathcal{F}K=\{f\in\mathcal{F}K^{1}:\text{$f$ is not the boundary of a 2-cell in $K$}\}.

As we did with planar graphs, we define
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}K
and
\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{c}}K
as the subsets of
\mathcal{F}K
containing precisely the infinite and finite facial subgraphs, respectively.

Our immediate goal is to show that any
K
as above with a good embedding is CHomP relative to
\mathcal{F}K
.
We first note the following very easy fact, the proof of which is an exercise in pointset topology.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
X
be a topological space, and
A\subset X
a subspace. Then the embedding
A\hookrightarrow X
extends to a continuous injection of cones
CA\hookrightarrow CX
.
2
2

2

In general, this will not be an embedding. The canonical example is that
C[0,1)
does not embed into
C[0,1]
.

We may apply this to prove the following.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
K
be a locally finite, planar, 2-connected polyhedral complex equipped with a good embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
. Then the coned-off complex
K_{\mathcal{F}}
, where
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}K
, admits a continuous injection into the 2-sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}
.

Proof.

Let
\vartheta:K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
denote the given good embedding of
K
.
By Proposition
1.29
, we see that for every
f\in\mathcal{F}K
there exists an open disk
D_{f}
in the complement of
\mathbb{S}^{2}-\vartheta(K)
, such that
\partial D_{f}
is a simple closed curve and
\vartheta(f)
is contained in
\partial D_{f}
. We may assume that
f\neq f^{\prime}
implies
D_{f}
and
D_{f^{\prime}}
are disjoint. Note that the cone of the circle is precisely the closed disk. By Lemma
5.1
, there is a continuous injection from
Cf
into
D_{f}
. Repeating this for every
f\in\mathcal{F}K
, we may extend the embedding
K\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
to a continuous injection of
K_{\mathcal{F}}
into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, where
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}K
.
∎

Next, we have the following technical lemma. Our proof relies lightly on the link between Dunwoody tracks and cohomology. See Appendix
A
for definitions and statements relating to tracks.

Lemma 5.3
.

Let
K
be a connected 2-dimensional polyhedral complex. Suppose
K
admits a continuous injection into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, and every 1-cell is a face of exactly two 2-cells. Then
K
is CHomP.

Proof.

By passing to the second barycentric subdivision, we assume without loss of generality that
K
is simplicial. Suppose then that
K
is not CHomP. Then by Proposition
A.6
there exists a compact track
t
on
K
which is non-separating. Since every 1-simplex is a face of exactly two 2-simplices, we see that
K
is a surface away from the 0-skeleton and thus
t
is a simple closed curve. Let
B
be a small closed tubular neighbourhood of
t
, so
B
is either an annulus or a Möbius band, and
t
is the meridian of this band.

Since
t
is non-separating, these must exist a simple edge loop which interests
t
an odd number of times. Let
\ell
be this loop. Consider the compact subspace
A=B\cup\ell
. Since
A
is compact, the continuous injection from
K
into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
restricts to an embedding of
A
in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
. The 2-sphere contains no Möbius bands, so
B
must be an annulus. Identifying
t
and
\ell
with their image in
\mathbb{S}^{2}
, we see that
t
and
\ell
are two simple closed curves which intersect an odd number of times, and only intersect transversely (this can be seen locally in the band
B
). Such a figure cannot exist in the 2-sphere.
∎

Piecing the above together, we deduce the following.

Lemma 5.4
.

Let
K
be a locally finite, 2-connected, planar polyhedral complex equipped with a good embedding. Then
K
is CHomP relative to
\mathcal{F}K
.

We now present the main result of this section.

Theorem 5.5
.

Let
\Gamma
be a locally finite, 2-connected planar graph with a fixed drawing. Suppose that there exists
n>0
such that every
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{c}}\Gamma
is a cycle of length at most
n
. Then
\Gamma
is coarsely CHomP relative to
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\Gamma
.

Proof.

First, it is clear that a planar surface with empty boundary is CHomP. We will reduce the proposition to this observation.

To ease notation, write
\mathcal{F}=\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\Gamma
.
Fix
\varepsilon>0
such that every finite facial subgraph of
\Gamma
is a cycle of length at most
\varepsilon
. Let
K=K_{\varepsilon}(\Gamma)
, so every
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{c}}\Gamma
is null-homotopic in
K
. Next, form a second complex
L
by attaching a 2-cell along every
f\in\mathcal{F}^{\mathrm{c}}\Gamma
. Note that since
\Gamma
is 2-connected, every such face is a simple closed curve, and moreover we see that the resulting complex
L
is itself planar, and satisfies
\mathcal{F}L=\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\Gamma=\mathcal{F}
. Finally, it is clear by construction that
L
the natural embedding of
L
into
\mathbb{S}^{2}
is a good embedding. By Lemma
5.4
, we deduce that
L
is CHomP relative to
\mathcal{F}
.

Next, we deduce that
K
is also CHomP relative to
\mathcal{F}
.
As every finite face is null-homotopic in
K
, the identity map
\Gamma\to\Gamma
extends to a continuous map
\psi:L\to K
. As
K
and
L
are locally finite 2-complexes, it is easy to see that this map can be taken to be proper. Note that since
\psi
restricts to an homeomorphism between 1-skeletons, it induces a surjection between first homology groups.
By Lemma
4.17
,
\psi
induces a proper map
\Psi:L_{\mathcal{F}}\to K_{\mathcal{F}}
between coned-off complexes. By Lemma
4.18
,
\Psi
also induces a surjection between first homology groups. By Proposition
4.2
, we have that
K_{\mathcal{F}}
is CHomP, as required.
∎

6.
Friendly-faced subgraphs of planar graphs

We now give some consideration to the relationship between the faces of a planar graph and the faces of its subgraphs.
Now would be a good time for the reader to review our notation and terminology pertaining to the faces of a planar graph (see Definition
1.26
).

6.1.
Initial discussion

The faces of a subgraph of a planar graph can be wildly different from the faces of the super-graph. This has the potential to cause issues for us further down the line, so the goal of this section is to try and claw back some control here. With this in mind, we have the following definition.

Definition 6.1

(Friendly-faced subgraphs)
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, locally finite planar graph with fixed good drawing
\vartheta:\overline{\Gamma}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{S}^{2}
. We say that a connected subgraph
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
is
friendly-faced
if the following are satisfied:

(1)

The good drawing
\vartheta
of
\Gamma
restricts to a good drawing of
\Lambda
.

(2)

There exists
r>0
such that for every
U_{1}\in\mathcal{D}\Lambda
(with the aforementioned induced good drawing) there exists
U_{2}\in\mathcal{D}\Gamma
such that
U_{2}\subset U_{1}
and

f_{1}\subset B_{\Gamma}(f_{2};r),

where
f_{i}=\mathcal{F}(U_{i})
for
i=1,2
.

Intuitively, a subgraph is friendly-faced if we can approximate its own facial subgraphs with those of the super-graph. Condition (
1
) is just to ensure that the induced drawing of the subgraph is sensible enough for us to work with, and is equivalent to asking that the inclusion
\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Gamma
induces an inclusion
\Omega\Lambda\hookrightarrow\Omega\Gamma
on the sets of ends, and thus an embedding
\overline{\Lambda}\hookrightarrow\overline{\Gamma}
of Freudenthal compactifications.

Since

Theorem 6.7

.

6.2.
Coboundary diameters and quasi-isometries

The first step towards proving Theorem
6.7
is to study how coboundary diameters (see Definition
3.8
) interact with quasi-isometries.
In particular, we will show the following.

Theorem 6.8

(Neighbourhoods with uniform coboundary)
.

Let
X
be a connected, locally finite, quasi-transitive graph and
Y\subset X
a connected, quasi-transitively stabilised subgraph with uniform coboundary. Let
\Gamma
be a connected, bounded valence graph and
\varphi:X\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma
a continuous, surjective quasi-isometry.
Then there exists
\delta>0
such that

\varphi(B_{X}(Y;\delta))

has uniform coboundary in
\Gamma
.

We will prove this through a series of lemmas.

Lemma 6.9
.

Let
\Gamma
be a connected, bounded valence graph and
\Lambda\subset\Pi\subset\Gamma
be connected subgraphs. If both
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}^{\Gamma}
and
\operatorname{Haus}_{\Gamma}(\Lambda,\Pi)
are finite then
\|\Pi\|_{\mathrm{in}}
is finite too.

Proof.

Write
k=\|\Lambda\|_{\mathrm{in}}^{\Gamma}
,
r=\operatorname{Haus}_{\Gamma}(\Lambda,\Pi)
.

Fix a connected component
U
of
\Gamma-\Pi
. Then there exists a unique component
V
of
\Gamma-\Lambda
such that
U\subset V
. Let
x,y\in\delta U\cap\Pi
. Choose
x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\in\Lambda
such that

\dist_{\Gamma}(x,x^{\prime})=\dist_{\Gamma}(x,\Lambda),\ \ \dist_{\Gamma}(y,y^{\prime})\dist_{\Gamma}(x,\Lambda).

In particular,
x^{\prime}
,
y^{\prime}
lie adjacent in
\delta V
and a geodesic in connecting
x
to
x^{\prime}
or
y
to
y^{\prime}
is internally disjoint from
\Lambda
. Such a geodesic is contained in
B_{\Gamma}(\delta V;r)
and so has bounded length, say length at most
L\geq 0
, since
\delta V
contains boundedly many vertices and
\Gamma
is bounded valence. Note that
L
depends only on
r
,
\Gamma
and
\Lambda
.

There is a path through
\Lambda
of length at most
k
connecting
x^{\prime}
to
y^{\prime}
. It follows that there is a path through
\Pi
of length at most
k+2L
connecting
x
to

. Since
x
,
y
and
U
were arbitrary, it follows that
\|\Pi\|_{\mathrm{in}}\leq k+2L
.
∎

Lemma 6.10
.

Let
X
,
\Gamma
be connected bounded valence graphs, and let
\varphi:X\twoheadrightarrow\Gamma
be a surjective quasi-isometry. Let
Y\subset X
be a subgraph. Then for all
\varepsilon>0
there exists
\delta>0
such that

\varphi(B_{X}(Y;\delta))\supset B_{\Gamma}(\varphi(Y);\varepsilon).

Proof.

Choose
x\in B_{\Gamma}(\varphi(Y);\varepsilon)
. Since
\varphi
is surjective, let
z\in\varphi^{-1}(x)
. We have that
\dist_{X}(z,Y)
is uniformly bounded, and so the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 6.11
.

Let
X
,
\Gamma
be connected bounded valence graphs, and let
\varphi:X\to\Gamma
be a continuous quasi-isometry. Let

a subgraph such that

\varepsilon_{0}>0
such that for all
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{0}

\|B_{\Gamma}(\varphi(Y);\varepsilon)\|_{\mathrm{in}}

Let
\psi:\Gamma\to X

. Fix

so that
\varphi
and

are

Let
\varepsilon_{0}=\lambda^{2}+2\lambda
and fix
\varepsilon>\varepsilon_{0}
.
To ease notation, write

.

Let
U
be some connected component of
\Gamma-N
.
Since
\varepsilon>\lambda^{2}
, we have that
\psi(U)
will be contained entirely in some connected component of

. Call this connected component

.
Let
u,v\in\delta U\cap N
, and let
\gamma
be a path connecting
u
to

, such that
\gamma\cap N=\{u,v\}
.
We have that
\psi(\gamma)
is a path connecting
\psi(u)
to
\psi(v)
, contained entirely within

.

Let
p_{1}
be a geodesic of length
\varepsilon
from

to
\varphi(Y)

be a geodesic of length
\varepsilon

to some

\psi(a)

\psi(b)

\lambda
from

\psi(a)

\psi(b)
respectively. Let

\psi(a)
to

and
q_{2}

\psi(b)
.
Let

.

, we have that
\psi(\gamma)
is disjoint from

, and so
\psi(u)
and

7.
Quasi-actions on planar graphs

The next technical challenge is to study quasi-actions on planar graphs and their subgraphs. We open with a discussion of what is to come.

7.1.
Initial discussion

The following definition will be useful for brevity.

Definition 7.1

(Quasi-planar tuple)

A

\mathfrak{Q}=(G,X,\Gamma,\vartheta,\varphi,\psi),

(1)

(2)

(3)

(4)

is a continuous surjective quasi-isometry with continuous quasi-inverse

By Propositions
1.10

\Gamma
is bounded valence, or that the quasi-isometries are continuous. The reason we want
\varphi
to be surjective is so we can apply Lemma

Note that there is an induced quasi-action of

upon
\Gamma
(see Definition

Definition 7.2

(Well-behaved quasi-planar tuple)
.

Let
\mathfrak{Q}=(G,X,\Gamma,\vartheta,\varphi,\psi)
be a quasi-planar tuple. We then define two

properties as follows:

(GB1)

There exists
m,n>0
such that the following holds. Let

satisfy
\diam_{\Gamma}(f)>n
, then for all

there exists

(GB2)

\Gamma

The property

Unfortunately, an important subtlety arises here. When we pass to a part of some canonical tree decomposition, simply restricting our quasi-isometries to these parts will not quite produce a well-defined quasi-planar tuple. Instead, we must modify the restricted quasi-inverse slightly in order to ensure that the domains and codomains align. Roughly speaking, we will consider a tubular neighbourhood of our subgraph, and approximate the faces of the ‘outer’ subgraph with faces of the ‘inner’ subgraph. ∎

A.1.
Patterns and tracks

Throughout this section let
K
be a connected, 2-dimensional simplicial complex. We will often abuse notation and identify a simplicial complex with its geometric realisation as a topological space.
Let
K^{1}
denote the 1-skeleton of
K
. Let
EK
denote the set of 1-simplices, and

the set of 0-simplices.

Definition A.1

(Patterns and tracks)
.

A
pattern

is a subspace of
K
such that

(1)

P
is disjoint from the 0-skeleton of
K
,

(2)

If
e\in EK
, then
P\cap e
is finite,

(3)

For each 2-cell
\sigma
of
K
, we have that
P\cap\sigma
is a disjoint union of finitely many straight lines connecting distinct faces of
\sigma
.

A connected component of a pattern is called a
track
. We say that a track
t
is
separating
if
K-t
contains exactly two connected components.

Let

denote the set of non-negative integers.
Given a pattern
P
, let
j_{P}:EK\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
be the map

j_{P}:e\longmapsto|P\cap e|.

Two patterns
P
,
Q
are said to be
parallel

P\equiv Q
, if
j_{P}=j_{Q}
.
Given a track
t\subset K
, write

\|t\|=|K^{1}\cap t|
.
The track

defines a simplicial 1-cocycle with coefficients in
\mathbb{Z}_{2}
, denoted
z_{t}
, via the formula

z_{t}(e)=|e\cap t|\mod 2
.

Given a track
t
, it is clear that the 1-cocycle
z_{t}
has compact support if and only if
t
is itself compact. We also have the following result, which characterises when a track separates.

Proposition A.2

(
[
6
, Cor. VI.3.5]
)
.

Let
t\subset K
be a track. Then
t
separates
K
if and only if
z_{t}
is a coboundary.

If
z_{t}
is a coboundary then let
b_{t}\in C^{0}(K;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
denote an element such that
\delta b_{t}=z_{t}
. Note that
b_{t}
is well-defined up to replacement by
1-b_{t}
. We may identify
b_{t}
with a subset of
VK
, namely its support. If
t
is compact,
b_{t}
can naturally be viewed as an element of
{\mathscr{B}}K
.
This is a helpful bridge between tracks and the Boolean ring

we discussed in Section
3
.

We have the following standard result, which is helpful for constructing tracks.

Theorem A.3

(
[
6
, Thm. VI.3.2]
)
.

Let
j:EK\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
be any map such that for any 2-simplex
\sigma
in
K
with faces
e_{1}
,
e_{2}
,
e_{3}
, we have that

(1)

j(e_{1})+j(e_{2})+j(e_{3})=2k
for some
k\geq 0
, and

(2)

j(e_{i})\leq k
for all
i=1,2,3
.

Then there is a pattern
P
such that
j_{P}=j
. The pattern
P
is unique up to equivalence.

The following corollary of this result is helpful to note.

Corollary A.4
.

Let
K
be a connected, 2-dimensional, simplicial complex, and let
z\in Z^{1}(K;\mathbb{Z}_{2})
. Then there exists a pattern
P
on
K
such that
z=z_{P}
. Moreover, we can take
P
such that every edge in
K^{1}
intersects
P
at most once.

Proof.

Let
e\in EK
.
Set
j(e)=1
if

lies in the support of
z
and
0
otherwise. Since
z
is cocycle, every 2-simplex will have an even number of faces supported by
e
. Apply Theorem
A.3
.
∎

We now describe how to sum two patterns.
Let
P,Q\subset K
be patterns. Consider the function
j_{P+Q}:EK\to\mathbb{N}_{0}
given by

j_{P+Q}(e)=j_{P}(e)+j_{Q}(e).

It is easy to check that
j_{P+Q}
satisfies the hypothesis of Theorem
A.3
, and thus determines a pattern, unique up to equivalence. We call denote this pattern
P+Q
. We say that two patterns
P
,
Q
are

if there exists
P^{\prime}\equiv P
,
Q^{\prime}\equiv Q
such that
P^{\prime}\cap Q^{\prime}=\emptyset
. In this case, we write

.

A.2.
Cohomological planarity and tracks

Recall definition of the CHomP property (see Definition
4.1
). We note that for a 2-dimensional simplicial complex, this property is equivalent to every compact track separating.

Proposition A.5

(
[
24

)

Let

be a connected 2-dimensional simplicial complex. Suppose

Proof.

As noted above, every compact track

Proposition A.6
.

Let

be a connected 2-dimensional simplicialcomplex. Suppose

Proof.

Suppose that

there exists a (necessarily compact) pattern

such that
