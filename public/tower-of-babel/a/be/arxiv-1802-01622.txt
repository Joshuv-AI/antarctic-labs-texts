A Matrix Baker–Akhiezer Function Associated with the Maxwell–Bloch Equations and their Finite-Gap Solutions
Vladimir P. KOTLYAROV

Abstract
The Baker–Akhiezer (BA) function theory was successfully developed in the mid 1970s. This theory brought very interesting and important results in the spectral theory of almost periodic operators and theory of completely integrable nonlinear equations such as Korteweg–de Vries equation, nonlinear Schrödinger equation, sine-Gordon equation, Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation. Subsequently the theory was reproduced for the Ablowitz–Kaup–Newell–Segur (AKNS) hierarchies. However, extensions of the Baker–Akhiezer function for the Maxwell–Bloch (MB) system or for the Karpman–Kaup equations, which contain prescribed weight functions characterizing inhomogeneous broadening of the main frequency, are unknown. The main goal of the paper is to give a such of extension associated with the Maxwell–Bloch equations. Using different Riemann–Hilbert problems posed on the complex plane with a finite number of cuts we propose such a matrix function that has unit determinant and takes an explicit form through Cauchy integrals, hyperelliptic integrals and theta functions. The matrix BA function solves the AKNS equations (the Lax pair for MB system) and generates a quasi-periodic finite-gap solution to the Maxwell–Bloch equations. The suggested function will be useful in the study of the long time asymptotic behavior of solutions of different initial-boundary value problems for the MB equations using the Deift–Zhou method of steepest descent and for an investigation of rogue waves of the Maxwell–Bloch equations.

Introduction

We consider the Maxwell–Bloch (MB) equations written in the form

\displaystyle{\mathcal{E}}_{t}+{\mathcal{E}}_{x}=\langle\rho\rangle,\qquad\langle\rho\rangle=\Omega\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}n(\lambda)\rho(t,x,\lambda)\textup{d}\lambda,

(1.1)

\displaystyle\rho_{t}+2\textup{i}\lambda\rho={\mathcal{N}}{\mathcal{E}},

(1.2)

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}_{t}=-\frac{1}{2}({\mathcal{E}}^{*}\rho+{\mathcal{E}}\rho^{*}).

(1.3)

Here,
{\mathcal{E}}={\mathcal{E}}(t,x)
is a complex-valued function of the time
t
and the coordinate
x
, and
\rho=\rho(t,x,\lambda)
and
{\mathcal{N}}={\mathcal{N}}(t,x,\lambda)
are complex-valued and real functions of
t
,
x
, and the additional parameter
\lambda
. Subindices refer to partial derivatives in
t
and
x
, and
*
means a complex conjugation.

Equations (
1.1
)–(
1.3
) are used in many physical models which deal with a classical electromagnetic field that interacts resonantly with quantum two-level objects – two-level atoms, which have only two energy position: upper and lower level. In particular, there are models of the self-induced transparency
[
1
,
2
]

[

is the complex valued envelope of an electromagnetic wave of fixed polarization, so that the field in the resonant medium is

\displaystyle{\bf E}(t,x)={\mathcal{E}}(t,x)e^{\textup{i}\Omega(x-t)}+{\mathcal{E}}^{*}(t,x)e^{-\textup{i}\Omega(x-t)}.

{\cal N}(t,x,\lambda)
and
{\rho}(t,x,\lambda)
are entries of the density matrix
F(t,x,\lambda)=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}{\cal N}(t,x,\lambda)&\rho(t,x,\lambda)\\
\rho^{*}(t,x,\lambda)&-{\cal N}(t,x,\lambda)\end{smallmatrix}\right)
. It describes the atomic subsystem. The parameter
\lambda
is the deviation of transition frequency of given two-level atom from its mean frequency
\Omega
. The angular brackets in (
1.1
) mean averaging with given weight function
n(\lambda)>0
, such that

\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}n(\lambda)\textup{d}\lambda=1.

(1.4)

The weight function
n(\lambda)
characterizes inhomogeneous broadening. From (
1.2
) and (
1.3
) it follows that

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\big({\mathcal{N}}^{2}(t,x,\lambda)+|\rho(t,x,\lambda)|^{2}\big)=0.

We interest in solutions where initial data are subjected to the condition

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}^{2}(0,x,\lambda)+|\rho(0,x,\lambda)|^{2}\equiv 1.

Then

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}^{2}(t,x,\lambda)+|\rho(t,x,\lambda)|^{2}\equiv 1

for all

, which reflects the conservation of probability: the total probability that an atom can be found in the upper or lower level equals 1. We also put
\Omega=1
in (
1.1

\displaystyle{\mathcal{N}}(0,x,\lambda)=\pm\sqrt{1-|\rho(0,x,\lambda)|^{2}}.

If
{\mathcal{N}}(0,x,\lambda)>0
, then an unstable medium is considered (the so-called two-level laser amplifier). If
{\mathcal{N}}(0,x,\lambda)<0
, then a stable medium is considered (the so-called attenuator).

The main goal of the paper is to give a construction of the Baker–Akhiezer function
\Psi(t,x,z)
associated with the Maxwell–Bloch equations. Using different Riemann–Hilbert problems posed on the complex plane with a finite number of cuts we propose such a matrix function

\displaystyle w_{t}+\textup{i}z\sigma_{3}w=-H(t,x)w,

\displaystyle\Psi(t,x,z)=\sigma_{2}\Psi^{*}(t,x,z^{*})\sigma_{2},\qquad\sigma_{2}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\textup{i}\\
\textup{i}&0\end{pmatrix}.

(1.10)

As a result of our construction we obtain also a solution to the Maxwell–Bloch equations (

1.3

3
Proof of the Theorem
2.2
and RH problem for
\boldsymbol{M=M(t,x,z)}

Uniqueness.

The matrix
\Psi(t,x,z)
has unit determinant. Indeed, since
\Psi
is a matrix of the second order then, due to definition of
\Psi
,
\det\Psi
is analytic in
z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Sigma
, continuous up to the contour
\Sigma
, except for the endpoints
E_{j}
,
E_{j}^{*}
where it has weak singularities, and bounded at all self-intersection points
\operatorname{Re}E_{j}
. In view of (
2.1
),
\det J(x,z)\equiv 1
, hence

\displaystyle\det\Psi_{-}(t,x,z)=\det\Psi_{+}(t,x,z),\qquad z\in\Sigma,

i.e.,
\det\Psi
has no jump at the contour
\Sigma
. Therefore
\det\Psi
is analytic everywhere, except for a set of self-intersection points and endpoints of
\Sigma
where it has removable singularities. At infinity
\det\Psi(t,x,z)=1+\mathrm{O}\big(z^{-1}\big)
, hence
\det\Psi(t,x,z)\equiv 1
by Liouville theorem. In particular,
\Psi(t,x,z)
is invertible for any
z
outside the exceptional set. Suppose that
\tilde{\Psi}(t,x,z)
is another solution of the RH problem. Then
\Phi(z):=\tilde{\Psi}(t,x,z)\Psi^{-1}(t,x,z)

satisfies

\displaystyle\Phi_{-}(z)=\tilde{\Psi}_{-}(t,x,z)\Psi_{-}^{-1}(t,x,z)=\tilde{\Psi}_{+}(t,x,z)J(x,z)J^{-1}(x,z)\Psi_{+}^{-1}(t,x,z)=\Phi_{+}(z),

and it is continuous across
\Sigma
with exception of end points
E_{j}
,
E^{*}_{j}
and points of self-intersection
\operatorname{Re}E_{j}
. These points are removable singularities. Hence
\Phi(t,x,z)
has an analytic continuation for
z\in\mathbb{C}
and it tends to identity matrix as
z\to\infty
. By Liovilles’s theorem
\Phi(t,x,z)=\tilde{\Psi}(t,x,z)\Psi^{-1}(t,x,z)\equiv I
and therefore
\tilde{\Psi}(t,x,z)\equiv\Psi(t,x,z)
, i.e., the matrix
\Psi(t,x,z)
is unique.
∎

Existence.

\Psi
using different RH problems. To transform the initial RH problem to a form allowing an explicit solution, let us seek

in the form

(3.1)

where constants

and

, scalar functions

and

and matrix

and

and

Due to the definition of

In this section we give the construction of the phase functions

and

and

are jumps of type (
3.2
) and (
3.3
)

\displaystyle f_{+}(z)+f_{-}(z)=C^{f}_{0},\qquad g_{+}(z)+g_{-}(z)=C^{g}_{0}

(4.1)

across a single arc
\Sigma_{0}
.

Define

\displaystyle w(z):=\sqrt{(z-E_{0})(z-E^{*}_{0})}

such that
w(z)
is analytic outside the arc and

as

, and introduce

\displaystyle\tilde{f}:=\frac{f}{w},\qquad\tilde{g}:=\frac{g}{w}.

(4.2)

Then the jump conditions (
4.1
) reduce to

\displaystyle\tilde{f}_{+}(z)-\tilde{f}_{-}(z)=\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{w_{+}},\qquad\tilde{g}_{+}(z)-\tilde{g}_{-}(z)=\frac{C^{g}_{0}}{w_{+}},\qquad z\in\Sigma_{0},

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{+}(\lambda)-\tilde{g}_{-}(\lambda)=\frac{\textup{i}\pi n(\lambda)}{2w(\lambda)},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{\operatorname{Re}E_{0}\}.

Due to the asymptotic conditions (

is (uniquely) determined by

through Cauchy integral

\displaystyle\tilde{f}(z)=1+\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{w_{+}(\xi)(\xi-z)}\textup{d}\xi=1+\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{2w(z)}.

Consequently,

\displaystyle f(z)=w(z)\left(1+\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{w_{+}(\xi)(\xi-z)}\textup{d}\xi\right)=w(z)+\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{2}.

Particularly,

is determined by

\displaystyle f_{0}=-\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{w_{+}(\xi)}\textup{d}\xi-\frac{1}{2}(E_{0}+E^{*}_{0})=-\operatorname{Re}E_{0}+\frac{C^{f}_{0}}{2}.

Taking into account (

) it can be put

. In this case we have

, and thus

\displaystyle\tilde{g}(z)=-1+\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\int_{\Sigma_{0}}\frac{C^{g}_{0}}{w_{+}(\xi)(\xi-z)}\textup{d}\xi+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{n(\lambda)}{w(\lambda)(\lambda-z)}\textup{d}\lambda

\displaystyle\hphantom{\tilde{g}(z)}{}=-1+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{n(\lambda)}{w(\lambda)(\lambda-z)}\textup{d}\lambda+\frac{C^{g}_{0}}{2}.

Consequently, by the same reason as above with

\displaystyle w(z):=\sqrt{\prod_{j=0}^{N}(z-E_{j})(z-E^{*}_{j})}

(4.3)

\displaystyle\tilde{g}_{+}(z)-\tilde{g}_{-}(z)=\frac{\textup{i}\pi n(\lambda)}{2w(\lambda)},\qquad\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{j=0}^{N}\{\operatorname{Re}E_{j}\}.

and

\displaystyle\hphantom{f_+(z)-f_-(z)}{}=2\sum\limits_{l=1}^{j}\int_{E_{l}}^{E^{*}_{l}}\varphi_{+}(\lambda)\textup{d}\lambda=\sum\limits_{l=1}^{j}A^{f}_{l}=0,\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

•

•

h_{+}(z)-h_{-}(z)=\frac{C^{h}_{j}}{w(z)}
,
z\in\Sigma_{j}
,
j=0,1,\ldots,N
;

•

h_{+}(\lambda)-h_{-}(\lambda)=\frac{\pi\textup{i}}{2w(\lambda)}n(\lambda)
,
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{j=0}^{N}\operatorname{Re}E_{j}
,

where
C^{h}_{j}=C^{g}_{j}+C^{f}_{j}
are to be determined. Due to (

)

are already known:
C^{f}_{j}=B^{f}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
. Then

can be written as a sum of Cauchy integrals

\displaystyle h(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\sum_{j=0}^{N}\int_{\Sigma_{j}}\frac{C^{h}_{j}}{w_{+}(\xi)(\xi-z)}\textup{d}\xi+\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{n(\lambda)}{w(\lambda)(\lambda-z)}\textup{d}\lambda,

and hence (
5.2
) follows. The asymptotic condition (
3.3
) leads to a system of
N
linear equation for
C^{h}_{j}
,
j=1,\dots,N
provided that
C^{h}_{0}=C^{g}_{0}=0
. The system are

\displaystyle\sum_{j=1}^{N}C^{h}_{j}\int_{\Sigma_{j}}\frac{\xi^{k}\textup{d}\xi}{w_{+}(\xi)}=-\frac{\textup{i}\pi}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\lambda^{k}n(\lambda)}{w(\lambda)}\textup{d}\lambda,\qquad k=0,\dots,N-1.

Similarly to (
2.6
) this system has a unique solution. In this case the parameter

is equal to

\displaystyle g_{0}=-f_{0}-\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\Sigma_{j}}\frac{C^{h}_{j}\xi^{N}}{w_{+}(\xi)}\textup{d}\xi-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\lambda^{N}n(\lambda)}{w(\lambda)}\textup{d}\lambda.

(5.3)

Substituting (
2.7
) in (
5.3
) and using equality
C^{f}_{j}-C^{h}_{j}=-C^{g}_{j}
we obtain

\displaystyle g_{0}=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{N}\operatorname{Re}E_{j}-\frac{1}{2\pi\textup{i}}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{\Sigma_{j}}\frac{C^{g}_{j}\xi^{N}}{w_{+}(\xi)}\textup{d}\xi-\frac{1}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{\lambda^{N}n(\lambda)}{w(\lambda)}\textup{d}\lambda,

which coincides with (
2.8
) and thus (
5.2
) is proved. Besides, we found relations (
5.2
) and (
5.3
) between the phase functions
f(z)
and
g(z)
.

Explicit construction of the matrix

In this section we present an explicit construction of

First, define

where cuts are chosen along

,

with orientation from top to bottom. The branch of root is fixed by the condition

\displaystyle\varkappa_{-}(z)=\textup{i}\varkappa_{+}(z),\qquad z\in\Gamma.

Notice also that

•

as

•

Recall that RH problem for

•

,

•

and

•

,

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\textup{i}e^{-\textup{i}\phi_{0}}\\
\textup{i}e^{\textup{i}\phi_{0}}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\in(E_{0},E^{*}_{0}),

(6.2)

\displaystyle=\begin{pmatrix}0&\textup{i}e^{-\textup{i}xC^{f}_{j}-\textup{i}tC^{g}_{j}-\textup{i}\phi_{j}}\\
\textup{i}e^{\textup{i}xC^{f}_{j}+\textup{i}tC^{g}_{j}+\textup{i}\phi_{j}}&0\end{pmatrix},\qquad z\in(E_{j},E^{*}_{j})

for

,

•

,

.

First, consider the case

),

can be constructed using

\displaystyle M(z)=\begin{pmatrix}\dfrac{\varkappa(z)+\varkappa^{-1}(z)}{2}&\dfrac{\varkappa(z)-\varkappa^{-1}(z)}{2}e^{-\textup{i}\phi_{0}}\vskip 2.84526pt\\
\dfrac{\varkappa(z)-\varkappa^{-1}(z)}{2}e^{\textup{i}\phi_{0}}&\dfrac{\varkappa(z)+\varkappa^{-1}(z)}{2}\end{pmatrix}.

Expanding

as

,

\displaystyle M(z)=I+\frac{m}{z}+\mathrm{O}\big(z^{-2}\big),

we have

\displaystyle m=\frac{E_{0}-E^{*}_{0}}{4}\begin{pmatrix}0&e^{-\textup{i}\phi_{0}}\\
e^{\textup{i}\phi_{0}}&0\end{pmatrix}

and thus the simplest periodic solution of the Maxwell–Bloch equation associated with

(

)–(

) at the end of the paper).

In order to present an explicit solution of the RH problem in the general case (

), we introduce necessary facts from the theory of the Riemann manifolds by following closely to

3
,
36
,
45
]
. First, let

be the Riemann surface of genus

, where

\displaystyle P(z)=\prod\limits_{j=0}^{N}(z-E_{j})(z-E^{*}_{j}),

with cuts along
\Sigma_{j}=(E_{j},E^{*}_{j})
,

. The Riemann surface
\mathcal{X}
can be viewed as a double covering of the complex

- plane: two sheets of

-plane are glued along
\Sigma_{j}
. The upper and lower sheets of
\mathcal{X}
are denoted by
\mathcal{X}_{+}
and
\mathcal{X}_{-}
respectively; they are fixed by the relations

\displaystyle\sqrt{P(z)}=\pm z^{N+1}\big(1+\mathrm{O}\big(z^{-1}\big)\big),\qquad z=\pi(\mathcal{P})\to\infty,\qquad\mathcal{P}\in{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm},

where

is the standard projection of
\mathcal{P}=(w,z)\in{\mathcal{X}}
on the Riemann sphere
{\mathbb{CP}}^{1}
. Thus each point on the
z
-plane has two preimages
\mathcal{P}_{\pm}=\mathcal{X}_{\pm}
, except for the branch points. Denote the preimage of
z=\infty
on
{\mathcal{X}}_{\pm}
by, respectively,
\infty^{\pm}
. With the inclusion of two points (
\infty^{+},\infty^{-}
),
\mathcal{X}
becomes a compact Riemann surface of genus
N
. The square root
\sqrt{P(z)}
turns into a meromorphic function on its own compact Riemann surface
\mathcal{X}
, which have
2N+2
zeros at
E_{j}
and

,
j=0,1,2,\dots,N
, and two poles at
\infty^{+}
and
\infty^{-}
, each of multiplicity
N+1
.

Further, we introduce the Abelian integrals

\displaystyle\omega_{j}(z)=\int_{E^{*}_{0}}^{z}\psi_{j}(s)\textup{d}s,\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N,

where
\textup{d}\omega_{j}(\mathcal{P})
is a basis of holomorphic differentials on
\mathcal{X}

\displaystyle\psi_{j}(z)=\frac{\sum\limits_{i=1}^{N}c_{ji}z^{N-i}}{\sqrt{P(z)}}.

The coefficients

are uniquely determined by the normalization conditions

\displaystyle\int_{{\mathbf{a}}_{l}}\textup{d}\omega_{j}(\mathcal{P})=2\int^{E^{*}_{l}}_{E_{l}}\psi_{j+}(z)\textup{d}z=\delta_{jl},\qquad j,l=1,2,\ldots,N.

We have chosen
{\mathbf{a}}_{l}
-cycles as ovals on the upper sheet of
\mathcal{X}
around the intervals
\big(E_{l},\hat{E}_{l}\big)
,
\hat{E}_{l}:=E_{l}^{*}
,
l=0,1,2,\ldots,N
, see Fig.
2
.

Figure 2:
\bf a
- and
\bf b
-cycles.

The normalized holomorphic differentials define the
b
-period matrix as

\displaystyle B_{jl}=\int_{{\mathbf{b}}_{l}}\textup{d}\omega_{j}(\mathcal{P})=2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{l}\int^{E_{k}}_{E^{*}_{k-1}}\psi_{j}(z)\textup{d}z,

where
{\mathbf{b}}_{l}
-cycle starts from
({E_{0}},E^{*}_{0})
, goes on the upper sheet to
({E_{l}},E^{*}_{l})
, and returns on the lower sheet to the starting point. This is a symmetric matrix with positive definite imaginary part.

Let
\mathbf{e}_{j}=(0,\ldots,1,\ldots,0)
be the unit vector in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
and

the
j
-th column of the matrix
B
. Denote by
\Lambda\subset\mathbb{C}^{N}
the lattice generated by the linear combinations, with integer coefficients, of the vectors
\mathbf{e}_{j}
and

for

. Then, by the definition, Jacobian variety of

is the complex torus

. The Abel mapping
\mathbf{A}\colon\mathcal{X}\to\operatorname{Jac}\{\mathcal{X}\}
is defined as follows

\displaystyle A_{j}(\mathcal{P})=\int_{\mathcal{P}_{0}}^{\mathcal{P}}\textup{d}\omega_{j}(\mathcal{Q}),\qquad j=1,2,\ldots,N,

(6.4)

where the point
\mathcal{P}_{0}
is fixed by condition

and
\mathcal{Q}
is the integration variable. The Abel mapping is also defined for integral divisors

\displaystyle\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{D})=\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{P}_{1})+\dots+\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{P}_{m})

(6.5)

and is extended to non-integral divisors

(where

-\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{D}^{-})

is zero, then

is independent of the chosen point
\mathcal{P}_{0}
. The Abel theorem states that if

is the divisor of a meromorphic function on the compact Riemann surface

and
\mathcal{D}^{+}
,
\mathcal{D}^{-}
are integral divisors of zeros and poles, then

in the Jacobian (
{\rm mod}\ \Lambda
). Besides, for any non-special integral divisor

of degree

, there exists a vector
\mathbf{w(\mathcal{D})}
such that the Riemann theta function

\mathcal{X}
with cuts along of the cycles

and

has precisely

zeros at
\mathcal{P}_{j}
,

. The vector

is defined by

\displaystyle\mathbf{w}(\mathcal{D})=-\mathbf{A}(\mathcal{D})-\mathbf{K}.

In the hyperelliptic case, the Riemann constant vector

is defined by (cf.
[

]
)

\displaystyle K_{j}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\end{subarray}}^{N}B_{lj}-\frac{j}{2}\qquad\operatorname{mod}\ \Lambda.

(6.6)

Associated with the matrix

there is the Riemann theta function defined for

by the Fourier series

\displaystyle\Theta(u_{1},\ldots,u_{n})=\sum\limits_{\mathbf{l}\in\mathbb{Z}^{N}}\exp{\{\pi\textup{i}(B\mathbf{l},\mathbf{l})+2\pi\textup{i}(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{u})\}},

(6.7)

where
(\mathbf{l},\mathbf{u})=l_{1}u_{1}+\dots+l_{N}u_{N}
. It is an even function, i.e.,
\Theta(-{\bf u})=\Theta({\bf u})
, and has the following periodicity properties

\displaystyle\Theta({\bf u}\pm{\bf e}_{j})=\Theta({\bf u}),\qquad\Theta({\bf u}\pm B{\bf e_{j}})=e^{\mp 2\pi\textup{i}u_{j}-\pi\textup{i}B_{jj}}\Theta(\bf{u}),

where
\mathbf{e}_{j}=(0,\dots,0,1,0,\dots,0)
is the j-th basis vector in
\mathbb{C}^{N}
. This implies that the function

\displaystyle h({\bf u})=\frac{\Theta({\bf u}+{\bf c}+{\bf d})}{\Theta({\bf u}+{\bf d})},

\displaystyle h({\bf u}\pm{\bf e}_{j})=h({\bf u}),\qquad h({\bf u}\pm B{\bf e_{j}})=e^{\mp 2\pi\textup{i}c_{j}}h({\bf u}).

The Abelian integrals

(

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{-}(z)-{\bf A}_{+}(z)=0\qquad\big({\rm mod}\ \mathbb{Z}^{N}\big),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\cup_{j-0}^{N}\operatorname{Re}E_{j},

(6.8)

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{-}(z)+{\bf A}_{+}(z)=0,\qquad z\in(E_{0},E_{0}^{*}),

(6.9)

\displaystyle{\bf A}_{-}(z)+{\bf A}_{+}(z)=B{\bf e}_{j},\qquad z\in(E_{j},E_{j}^{*}),\qquad j=1,\dots,N.

(6.10)

Indeed, since
\psi_{l}(z_{+})+\psi_{l}(z_{-})=0

(E_{j},E^{*}_{j})
and

is continuous on

, it easy to see that for

,

\displaystyle A_{l}(z_{+})-A_{l}(z_{-})=\int_{E^{*}_{0}}^{z}(\psi_{l}(s)-\psi_{l}(s))\textup{d}s=0,\qquad z_{\pm}\in(-\infty,\operatorname{Re}E_{0})\cup(\operatorname{Re}E_{0},\operatorname{Re}E_{1})

and

\displaystyle A_{l}(z_{+})-A_{l}(z_{-})=2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j-1}\int_{E_{k}}^{E^{*}_{k}}\psi_{l}(s_{+})\textup{d}s=\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j-1}\delta_{kl}=0\qquad({\rm mod}\ \mathbb{Z})

for

,

and for

. On the other hand,

\displaystyle A_{l}(z_{+})+A_{l}(z_{-})=\int_{E^{*}_{0}}^{z}(\psi_{l}(s_{+})+\psi_{l}(s_{-}))\textup{d}s=0,\qquad z_{\pm}\in(E_{0},E^{*}_{0})

and

\displaystyle A_{l}(z_{+})+A_{l}(z_{-})=2\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j}\int^{E_{k}}_{E^{*}_{k-1}}\psi_{j}(s)\textup{d}s+\sum\limits_{k=1}^{j-1}\delta_{kl}=B_{jl},

\displaystyle z_{\pm}\in(E_{j},E^{*}_{j}),\qquad j,l=1,2,\ldots,N.

Now define (

\displaystyle F_{s}(z)=\frac{\Theta({\bf A}(z)+{\bf c}+{\bf d}_{s})}{\Theta({\bf A}(z)+{\bf d}_{s})},\qquad H_{s}(z)=\frac{\Theta(-{\bf A}(z)+{\bf c}+{\bf d}_{s})}{\Theta(-{\bf A}(z)+{\bf d}_{s})},\qquad z\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\Gamma,

(6.11)

where

\displaystyle F_{s-}(z)=F_{s+}(z),\qquad H_{s-}(z)=H_{s+}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{j=0}^{N}\operatorname{Re}E_{j}

\displaystyle F_{s-}(z)=e^{-2\pi\textup{i}c_{j}}H_{s+}(z),\qquad H_{s-}(z)=e^{2\pi\textup{i}c_{j}}F_{s+}(z),\qquad z\in(E_{j},E^{*}_{j})

for

, where

Next, define the matrix-valued function

\displaystyle\hat{M}(z):=\begin{pmatrix}F_{1}(z)&H_{1}(z)\\

Then we have

\displaystyle\hat{M}_{-}(z)=\hat{M}_{+}(z),\qquad z\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\bigcup\limits_{j=0}^{N}\operatorname{Re}E_{j},

with
