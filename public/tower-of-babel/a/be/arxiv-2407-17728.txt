A Boolean-valued space approach to separation axioms and sobriety of bitopological spaces
Jing He, Dexue Zhang

Abstract
This paper presents a study of separation axioms and sobriety of bitopological spaces from the point of view of fuzzy topology via identifying bitopological spaces with topological spaces valued in the Boolean algebra of four elements. A system of separation axioms is proposed making use of Boolean-valued specialization order of bitopological spaces; The relationship between d-sobriety of bitopological spaces proposed by Jung and Moshier and sobriety of fuzzy topological spaces is studied; A Hofmann-Mislove theorem for bitopological spaces is established. Keywords: Bitopological space; fuzzy topology; separation axiom; frame; specialization order; sober space MSC(2020): 54E55, 54A40, 54D10, 06D22

\mathbb{B}
-topological spaces

A bitopological space
[

]
is a triple
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
, where

is a set,
\tau[{t\!t}]

and
f\colon(X,\tau_{X}[{f\!\!f}])\longrightarrow(Y,\tau_{Y}[{f\!\!f}])
are continuous. The category of bitopological spaces and continuous maps is denoted by

\bf{BiTop}.

Let
\mathbb{B}
denote the Boolean algebra
\{0,1,{t\!t},{f\!\!f}\}
, with
0
being the bottom element,

being the top element,
{t\!t}
and

being complement of each other,
as visualized below:

\textstyle{1}
\textstyle{{t\!t}}
\textstyle{1}
\textstyle{{f\!\!f}}
\textstyle{{t\!t}}
\textstyle{0}
\textstyle{{f\!\!f}}
\textstyle{0}

As we shall see, the category of bitopological spaces is isomorphic to the category of
\mathbb{B}
-valued topological spaces. We introduce some notations first.

•

The arrow

denotes the implication operator of the Boolean algebra

, where

\neg{t\!t}={f\!\!f},~\neg{f\!\!f}={t\!t},~\neg 0=1,~\neg 1=0.

For each set

-valued subset

In particular, for each

For each

3
Separation axioms

In this section we use the
\mathbb{B}
-valued specialization order of
\mathbb{B}
-topological spaces to postulate lower separation axioms for bitopological spaces, including
R_{0},R_{1},T_{0},T_{1}
and Hausdorff.
\mathbb{B}
-valued order is a special instance of fuzzy order, related definitions are included here for sake of self-containment.

A
\mathbb{B}
-valued order
(
\mathbb{B}
-order for short) on a set
X
is a map
r\colon X\times X\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
such that for all
x,y,z\in X
,

\bullet

r(x,x)=1
;

\bullet

r(y,z)\wedge r(x,y)\leq r(x,z)
.

Suppose
(X,r_{X})
and
(Y,r_{Y})
are
\mathbb{B}
-ordered sets. A map
f\colon X\longrightarrow Y

preserves
\mathbb{B}
-order
if
r_{X}(x_{1},x_{2})\leq r_{Y}(f(x_{1}),f(x_{2}))
for all
x_{1},x_{2}\in X
. The category of
\mathbb{B}
-ordered sets and maps preserving
\mathbb{B}
-order is denoted by
\mathbb{B}\text{-}{\bf Ord}.

There is a general method to generate
\mathbb{B}
-orders. Suppose
X
is a set and
\Lambda\subseteq\mathbb{B}^{X}
. Then

\Omega(\Lambda)\colon X\times X\longrightarrow\mathbb{B},\quad\Omega(\Lambda)(x,y)=\bigwedge\limits_{\lambda\in\Lambda}(\lambda(x)\rightarrow\lambda(y))

is a
\mathbb{B}
-order on
X
. Interesting instances of this method include:

(i)

(Implication operator) If
X=\mathbb{B}
and
\Lambda=\{\rm id_{\mathbb{B}}\}
, then
\Omega(\Lambda)
is the implication operator

\rightarrow\colon\mathbb{B}\times\mathbb{B}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B},\quad(a,b)\mapsto\neg a\vee b.

(ii)

(Inclusion
\mathbb{B}
-order) For each set
X
, consider the family
\Lambda=\{[x]\colon\mathbb{B}^{X}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}\}_{x\in X}
of functions, where
[x](\lambda)=\lambda(x)
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
. Then
\Omega(\Lambda)
is the
inclusion
\mathbb{B}
-order

\sub_{X}
on
\mathbb{B}^{X}
:

\Omega(\Lambda)(\lambda,\mu)=\sub_{X}(\lambda,\mu)=\bigwedge\limits_{x\in X}(\lambda(x)\rightarrow\mu(x)).

Intuitively, the value
\sub_{X}(\lambda,\mu)
measures the truth degree that
\lambda
is contained in
\mu
, so the name.

Definition 3.1
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. The
\mathbb{B}
-order
\Omega(\tau)
on
X
generated by

is called the specialization
\mathbb{B}
-order of
(X,\tau)
. Explicitly, for all

,

\Omega(\tau)(x,y)=\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}(\lambda(x)\rightarrow\lambda(y)).

Some basic properties of the specialization

-order are given in the following proposition.

Proposition 3.2
.

Let
(X,\tau)
be a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. Let
\leq_{t\!t}
and
\leq_{f\!\!f}
be the specialization order of the topological spaces
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
, respectively. Then

(i)

For all

,

\Omega(\tau)(x,y)\geq{t\!t}\iff x\leq_{t\!t}y;\quad\Omega(\tau)(x,y)\geq{f\!\!f}\iff x\leq_{f\!\!f}y.

So the specialization
\mathbb{B}
-order of
(X,\tau)
determines and is determined by the specialization orders of the topological spaces
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
.

(ii)

\Omega(\tau)(x,y)=\overline{1_{y}}(x),
where
\overline{1_{y}}
is the closure of
1_{y}
in
(X,\tau)
.

(iii)

Each subbasis
\mathcal{B}
of

\Omega(\tau)
; that is,
\Omega(\tau)(x,y)=\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\mathcal{B}}(\lambda(x)\rightarrow\lambda(y)).

The assignment
(X,\tau)\mapsto(X,\Omega(\tau))
gives rise to a functor
\Omega\colon\mathbb{B}\text{-}{\bf Top}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}\text{-}{\bf Ord}
. It is known that

[

,

3.1
R_{0}
,
T_{0}
and
T_{1}

Suppose
X
is a topological space. By the
Kolmogorov equivalence relation
of
X
we mean the relation given by
x\sim y
if the closure of
\{x\}
is equal to that of
\{y\}
. It is well-known that the quotient space
X/\!\sim
(a.k.a Kolmogorov quotient) is a
T_{0}
space, it is the
T_{0}
-reflection of
X
.

Definition 3.4
.

(
[
6
]
) A topological space
X
is
R_{0}
provided that if
U
is an open set and
x\in U
, then the closure of
\{x\}
is contained in
U
.

A topological space is
R_{0}
if and only if its Kolmogorov quotient is
T_{1}
. The following fact should have been observed somewhere else, but we fail to locate where it is explicitly stated for the first time.

Lemma 3.5
.

A topological space
X
is
R_{0}
if and only if its specialization order is symmetric.

Proof.

Suppose
X
is
R_{0}
. If
x\not\leq y
, the open set
X\setminus\overline{\{y\}}
contains
x
, hence the closure of
\{x\}
, then
y\not\leq x
. This shows that the specialization order is symmetric. Conversely, suppose the specialization order of
X
is symmetric,
U
is an open set and
x\in U
. For each
y\notin U
, since the closure of
\{y\}
misses
U
, it follows that
x\not\leq y
, then
y\not\leq x
by symmetry of the specialization order, which means
y
is not in the closure of
\{x\}
, and consequently,
X
is
R_{0}
. ∎

Lemma
3.5
motivates the following postulation of
R_{0}
for
\mathbb{B}
-topological spaces.

Definition 3.6
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. We say that

(i)

(X,\tau)
is
R_{0}
if
\Omega(\tau)
is symmetric in the sense that
\Omega(\tau)(x,y)=\Omega(\tau)(y,x)
for all
x,y\in X
.

(ii)

(X,\tau)
is

if
\Omega(\tau)
is separated in the sense that
\Omega(\tau)(x,y)=1=\Omega(\tau)(y,x)\implies x=y
.

(iii)

(X,\tau)
is

if it is both
T_{0}
and

.

It is readily verified that a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
is
R_{0}
if, and only if, for each open set
\lambda
and each point

, it always holds that
\lambda(x)\leq\sub_{X}(\overline{1_{x}},\lambda).
This inequality says that if
\lambda
contains
x
, then it contains the closure of
1_{x}
.
We list below some basic properties of the axioms of

(i)

.

Given a

Proposition 3.10
.

A

-topological space is

if and only if its

-reflection is

Proposition 3.11
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space.

(i)

If
(X,\tau)
is

, then it is join

.

(ii)

∎

3.2
R_{1}
and Hausdorff

Definition 3.14
.

(
[
6
,
25
]
) A topological space
X
is
R_{1}
provided that if
x
and
y
are points of
X
such that
\overline{\{x\}}\not=\overline{\{y\}}
, then
x
and
y
have disjoint neighborhoods.

Like
R_{0}
,
R_{1}
can also be characterized in terms of specialization order.

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
X
be a topological space and
\leq
be its specialization order. The following are equivalent:

(1)

X
is
R_{1}
.

(2)

\leq
is a closed set of the product space
X\times X
.

(3)

\leq\cap\leq^{\rm op}
is a closed set of the product space
X\times X
.

Proof.

(1)\Rightarrow(2)
If
x\not\leq y
, then
\overline{\{x\}}\not=\overline{\{y\}}
, hence
x
and
y
have disjoint neighborhoods, say
U
and
V
. So
U\times V
is a neighborhood of
(x,y)
in the product space that misses
\leq
, then
\leq
is a closed set of
X\times X
.

(2)\Rightarrow(3)
Follows from the fact that
X\times X\longrightarrow X\times X,~(x,y)\mapsto(y,x)
is a homeomorphism.

(3)\Rightarrow(1)

If
\overline{\{x\}}\not=\overline{\{y\}}
, then either
x\not\leq y
or
y\not\leq x
, hence
(x,y)
is not in
\leq\cap\leq^{\rm op}
. Since
\leq\cap\leq^{\rm op}
is a closed set of
X\times X
, there exist open sets
U
and
V
of
X
such that
U\times V
contains
(x,y)
but misses
\leq\cap\leq^{\rm op}
; in particular
U\times V
misses the diagonal of
X\times X
. Thus,
U
and
V
are disjoint neighborhoods of
x
and
y
. ∎

It follows from the above lemma that (i) a topological space
X
is
R_{1}
if and only if its
T_{0}
-reflection is Hausdorff; in particular,
X
is Hausdorff if and only if it is both
T_{0}
and
R_{1}
; (ii) every regular space is
R_{1}
; and (iii) subspaces and products of
R_{1}
spaces are
R_{1}
. These conclusions are already known in the literature (e.g.
[
6
,
25
]
) and are easy to prove without resort to Lemma
3.15
. The benefit of Lemma
3.15
is that it leads to the following:

Definition 3.16
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. We say that

(i)

(X,\tau)
is
R_{1}
if
\Omega(\tau)
is a closed set of the product space
(X,\tau)\times(X,\tau)
.

(ii)

(X,\tau)
is Hausdorff if it is both
T_{0}
and
R_{1}
.

Proposition 3.17
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. Then
(X,\tau)
is
R_{1}
if and only if it is componentwise
R_{1}
; that is, both of the topological spaces
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
are
R_{1}
. Therefore,
(X,\tau)
is Hausdorff if and only if it is componentwise
R_{1}
and join
T_{0}
.

Proof.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is
R_{1}
.
Since
\Omega(\tau)
is a closed set of the product space
(X,\tau)\times(X,\tau)
, then
\Omega(\tau)[{t\!t}]=\{(x,y):\Omega(\tau)(x,y)\geq{t\!t}\}
is a closed set of
\iota_{t\!t}((X,\tau)\times(X,\tau))=(X,\tau[{t\!t}])\times(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
. Since
\Omega(\tau)[{t\!t}]
is the specialization order of the topological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
, it follows that
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
is
R_{1}
. Likewise,
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is
R_{1}
. This verifies the necessity. Sufficiency is verified in a similar way. ∎

Thus, a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is Hausdorff if and only if it is join
T_{0}
and componentwise
R_{1}
. In particular, for each topological space
X
, the space
\omega(X)
is Hausdorff if and only if
X
is Hausdorff.

Corollary 3.18
.

A
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
is
R_{1}
if and only if
\Omega(\tau)\wedge\Omega(\tau)^{\rm op}
is a closed set of the product space
(X,\tau)\times(X,\tau)
.

Proof.

By Proposition
3.2
(i),
\Omega(\tau)[{t\!t}]
and
\Omega(\tau)[{f\!\!f}]
are the specialization order of the topological spaces
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
, respectively. Then, the conclusion follows from Proposition
3.17
and Lemma
3.15
immediately. ∎

Corollary 3.19
.

A
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is
R_{1}
if and only if its
T_{0}
-reflection is Hausdorff.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that for each topological space
X
that is
R_{1}
, if
\sim
is an equivalence relation contained in the Kolmogorov equivalence relation, i.e.,
x\sim y
implies
\overline{\{x\}}=\overline{\{y\}}
,
then the quotient space

is
R_{1}
. ∎

Corollary 3.20
.

Subspaces and products of
R_{1}

\mathbb{B}
-topological spaces are
R_{1}
.

Proposition 3.21
.

Every
R_{1}

\mathbb{B}
-topological space is
R_{0}
; every Hausdorff
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is
T_{1}
.

Proof.

If a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is
R_{1}
, then by Proposition
3.17
it is componentwise

, hence componentwise

, therefore
R_{0}
by Proposition

. ∎

Proposition 3.22
.

The full subcategory of
\mathbb{B}\text{-}{\bf Top}
consisting of Hausdorff spaces is reflective.

Proof.

For each
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
, let
\hat{\tau}
be the initial
\mathbb{B}
-topology on

determined by the continuous maps
f_{i}\colon(X,\tau)\longrightarrow(Y_{i},\tau_{i})
with
(Y_{i},\tau_{i})
Hausdorff. Then,
(X,\hat{\tau})
is an

space and its

. ∎

The following example shows, out of expectation to some extent, that Hausdorff is not preserved under refinement of
\mathbb{B}
-topologies. The reason for this is that
R_{1}
is not preserved under refinement of topology. For topological spaces,
R_{1}
is preserved under refinement in the realm of
T_{0}
spaces. But, for
\mathbb{B}
-topological spaces, it need not be preserved even in the realm of
T_{0}
spaces. Thus, Hausdorff spaces might behave differently in the
\mathbb{B}
-valued setting.

Example 3.23
.

This example shows that, unlike the situation for topological spaces, Hausdorff is not preserved under refinement of
\mathbb{B}
-topologies.
Let
X
be an infinite set. Let
\mathcal{T}_{1}
be the discrete topology,
\mathcal{T}_{2}
be the indiscrete topology, and
\mathcal{T}_{3}
be the co-finite topology on
X
.

•

The specialization order of
(X,\mathcal{T}_{1})
is the identity relation, so
(X,\mathcal{T}_{1})
is
R_{1}
.

•

The specialization order of
(X,\mathcal{T}_{2})
is the trivial order (i.e.,
x\leq y
for all
x,y\in X
), so
(X,\mathcal{T}_{2})
is
R_{1}
.

•

Since
X
is infinite, for any nonempty open sets
U,V
of
(X,\mathcal{T}_{3})
, the set
U\times V
meets the diagonal
\{(x,x):x\in X\}
, thus the diagonal
\{(x,x):x\in X\}
, which is specialization order of
(X,\mathcal{T}_{3})
, is not closed in the product of
(X,\mathcal{T}_{3})
with itself. So
(X,\mathcal{T}_{3})
is not
R_{1}
.

The bitopological space
(X,\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2})
is join
T_{0}
and componentwise
R_{1}
, so it is Hausdorff. The bitopological space
(X,\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{3})
is not componentwise
R_{1}
, it is not Hausdorff, though it is finer than
(X,\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2})
.

A bitopological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is
pairwise Hausdorff

[
17
, Definition 2.5]
if, for each pair of distinct points
x
and
y
, there exist a
\tau[{t\!t}]
-neighborhood
U
of
x
and a
\tau[{f\!\!f}]
-neighborhood
V
of
y
such that
U\cap V=\emptyset
.
In
[
30
,
4
]
the axiom of pairwise Hausdorff is weakened to that for each pair of distinct points
x
and
y
, there exist disjoint
U\in\tau[{t\!t}]
and
V\in\tau[{f\!\!f}]
such that
x\in U,y\in V
or
x\in V,y\in U
.

It is clear that a bitopological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is pairwise Hausdorff in the sense of Kelly
[
17
]
if and only if the diagonal
\{(x,x):x\in X\}
is a closed set of the product space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])\times(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
.

Another generalization of the Hausdorff separation axiom to bitopological spaces is that of order-separatedness in
[
14
,
15
]
. A bitopological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is
order-separated

[
15
, Definition 3.7]
if

(i)

the binary relation
\leq\thinspace\coloneqq\thinspace\leq_{t\!t}\cap\geq_{f\!\!f}
is a partial order; and

(ii)

x\not\leq y
implies that there exist a
\tau[{t\!t}]
-neighborhood
U
of
x
and a
\tau[{f\!\!f}]
-neighborhood
V
of
y
such that
U\cap V=\emptyset
.

Example 3.24
.

Let
X,Y
be topological spaces. Consider the
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
X.Y=(X\times Y,\tau)
in Example
2.2
. Then

(i)

X.Y
is
R_{1}
(Hausdorff, resp.) if and only if so are the topological spaces

and

, respectively.

(ii)

is pairwise Hausdorff if and only if

3.3
Regularity and normality

Definition 3.25
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. We say that

(i)

(X,\tau)
is regular if for each
\lambda\in\tau
,
\lambda=\bigvee\{\mu\in\tau:\overline{\mu}\leq\lambda\}.

(ii)

(X,\tau)
is
T_{3}
if it is
T_{0}
and regular.

The above definition is a special case of regularity and
T_{3}
in
[
11
, Section 6]
for fuzzy topological spaces, it is motivated by the fact that in a regular topological space, every neighborhood of a point contains a closed neighborhood of the given point, see e.g.
[
32
, Theorem 14.3]
.

Proposition 3.26
.

A
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
is regular if and only if it is componentwise regular. In particular, for each topological space
X
, the
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
\omega(X)
is regular if and only if
X
, as a topological space, is regular.

Proof.

We prove the necessity first. Let
(X,\tau)
be a regular
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. Assume that
U
is an open set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and that
x\in U
. By definition of
\tau[{t\!t}]
, there is some
\lambda\in\tau
such that
U=\lambda[{t\!t}]
. Since
(X,\tau)
is regular, there is some
\mu\in\tau
such that
\mu(x)\geq{t\!t}
and
\overline{\mu}\leq\lambda
. Since
\mu[{t\!t}]
is an open set and
\overline{\mu}[{t\!t}]
is a closed set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
, it follows that
\mu[{t\!t}]
is an open neighborhood of
x
whose closure is contained in
U
, hence
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
is regular. Likewise,
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is regular.

For sufficiency, suppose that
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
are both regular. Let
\lambda\in\tau
. Since
\lambda[{t\!t}]
is open in
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
, there is a family
\mathcal{U}
of open sets of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
such that
\mathcal{U}
covers
\lambda[{t\!t}]
and the closure of each of its element in
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
is contained in
\lambda[{t\!t}]
. Similarly, there is a family
\mathcal{V}
of open sets of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
such that
\mathcal{V}
covers
\lambda[{f\!\!f}]
and the closure of each of its element in
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is contained in
\lambda[{f\!\!f}]
. Let

\Lambda=\{{t\!t}_{U}:U\in\mathcal{U}\}\cup\{{f\!\!f}_{V}:V\in\mathcal{V}\}.

Then
\Lambda
is a family of open sets of
(X,\tau)
such that
\lambda
is the join of
\Lambda
and that the closure of each element of
\Lambda
is contained in
\lambda
. This shows that
(X,\tau)
is regular.
∎

Corollary 3.27
.

(i)

A
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is
T_{3}
if and only if it is join
T_{0}
and componentwise regular.

(ii)

Every
\mathbb{B}
-topological space that is regular is
R_{1}
; every
\mathbb{B}
-topological space that is

is Hausdorff.

.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. We say that

(i)

(
[

]
)
(X,\tau)
is normal if for each open set
\lambda
and each closed set

of

is
T_{4}

.

(i)

A
\mathbb{B}

(ii)

A

Similar to Proposition

As an immediate corollary we obtain that for each topological space

There is a Urysohn lemma for normal
\mathbb{B}
-topological spaces, which is essentially a special case of that for normal fuzzy topological spaces in Hutton
[
10
]
. We formulate it in terms of bitopological spaces. We need an analog of the unit interval in the bitopological setting:

[0,1](\mathbb{B})=([0,1]\times[0,1],\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}]),

where,
\tau[{t\!t}]=\{U\times[0,1]:U\in\mathcal{O}\}
,
\tau[{f\!\!f}]=\{[0,1]\times V:V\in\mathcal{O}\}
,
\mathcal{O}
is the usual topology of
[0,1]
. In the notation of Example
2.2
,
[0,1](\mathbb{B})
is the space
[0,1].[0,1]
.
Now we state the Urysohn lemma, leaving the proof to the reader.

Lemma 3.30

(Urysohn lemma)
.

Suppose
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is a normal bitopological space. If

(i)

F_{t\!t}
is a closed set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
,
F_{f\!\!f}
is a closed set of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
,

(ii)

U_{t\!t}
is an open set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
,
U_{f\!\!f}
is an open set of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
, and

(iii)

F_{t\!t}\subseteq U_{t\!t}
,
F_{f\!\!f}\subseteq U_{f\!\!f}
,

then there exists a continuous map

Sobriety

Sobriety is an important notion in topology, as manifested by its role in the connection between topological spaces and frames, the algebraic dual of topological spaces
[

,
29
]

introduced biframes as algebraic duals of bitopological spaces. In 2006, Jung and Moshier

developed an algebraic realization of bitopological spaces, resulting in the theory of of d-frames and d-sober bitopological spaces. Since bitopological spaces are a special kind of fuzzy topological spaces,
\mathbb{B}
-valued topological spaces to be precise, the theory of sobriety developed for fuzzy topological spaces, see e.g.
[

34
,
35
]

[

]
and

-sobriety in

]

A

A

\displaystyle\mathbf{con}

\displaystyle=\{(U,V)\in\tau[{t\!t}]\times\tau[{f\!\!f}]:U\cap V=\emptyset\},

\displaystyle\mathbf{tot}

\displaystyle=\{(U,V)\in\tau[{t\!t}]\times\tau[{f\!\!f}]:U\cup V=X\}.

For each continuous map

As in the situation for topological spaces and frames, the functor

has a right adjoint.

(

Proposition 4.7
.

F\colon\mathbb{B}\downarrow\mathbf{Frm}\longrightarrow\mathbf{dFrm}
is left adjoint to
G\colon\mathbf{dFrm}\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}\downarrow\mathbf{Frm}

Proof.

Routine verification. ∎

The composite of the adjunctions

The assignment

where

to the constant open set

.

Suppose

\mathbb{B}

Definition 4.11
.

(
[
33
]
) Let
(X,\tau)
be a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. A closed set
\gamma
of
(X,\tau)
is irreducible if

(i)

\sub_{X}(\gamma,b_{X})=b
for all

;

(ii)

\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})=\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})\vee\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2}
) for all closed sets
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
of
(X,\tau)
.

Condition (i) is equivalent to requiring
\gamma
be
inhabited
in the sense that
\bigvee_{x\in X}\gamma(x)=1
.

Suppose
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
is a bitopological space. We say that
(K_{t\!t},K_{f\!\!f})
is a
pair of irreducible closed sets
of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
if,
K_{t\!t}
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])

K_{f\!\!f}
is an irreducible closed set of

. The following lemma says that irreducible closed sets of a

-topological space

correspond to pairs of irreducible closed sets of the bitopologial space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
.

Lemma 4.12
.

Suppose
\gamma
is a closed set of a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
. Then,
\gamma
is irreducible if and only if
(\gamma[{t\!t}],\gamma[{f\!\!f}])
is a pair of irreducible closed sets of the bitopological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
.

Proof.

For necessity, suppose
\gamma
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau)
. We wish to show that
\gamma[{t\!t}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
. That
\gamma[{t\!t}]\not=\emptyset
is obvious. Suppose on the contrary that
\gamma[{t\!t}]
is not an irreducible closed subset of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
. Then there are closed sets
A,B
of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
such that
\gamma[{t\!t}]\nsubseteq A
,
\gamma[{t\!t}]\nsubseteq B
, and
\gamma[{t\!t}]\subseteq A\cup B
. Let

\mu_{1}={t\!t}_{A}\vee({f\!\!f}\wedge\gamma)\quad\text{and}\quad\mu_{2}={t\!t}_{B}\vee({f\!\!f}\wedge\gamma).

Then
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})=1
, but
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})\vee\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})\leq{f\!\!f}
, a contradiction. Likewise,
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
.

For sufficiency, suppose
\gamma
is a closed set of
(X,\tau)
such that
\gamma[{t\!t}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
. Since both
\gamma[{t\!t}]
and
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]
are not empty, then
\bigvee_{x\in X}\gamma(x)=1
.
It remains to check that for all closed sets
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
of
(X,\tau)
,

\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})=\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})\vee\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2}).

We proceed with four cases.

Case 1.
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})=1
or
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})=1
.
In this case there is nothing to prove.

Case 2.
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})={t\!t}
or
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})={t\!t}
.
Without loss of generality assume that
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})={t\!t}
. In this case
\gamma[{t\!t}]\subseteq\mu_{1}[{t\!t}]
, but
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]\nsubseteq\mu_{1}[{f\!\!f}]
.
If
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})\geq{f\!\!f}
, then
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})\vee\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})=1
, the equality holds trivially. If
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})\leq{t\!t}
, then
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]\nsubseteq\mu_{2}[{f\!\!f}]
. Since
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
, it follows that
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]\nsubseteq(\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})[{f\!\!f}]
, and consequently,

\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})={t\!t}=\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})\vee\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2}).

Case 3.
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})={f\!\!f}
or
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})={f\!\!f}
.
Similar to Case 2.

Case 4.
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1})=0=\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{2})
.
We show that
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})=0
. Since
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{i})=0
, then
\gamma[{t\!t}]\nsubseteq\mu_{i}[{t\!t}]
and
\gamma[{f\!\!f}]\nsubseteq\mu_{i}[{f\!\!f}]
,
i=1,2
. Since
\gamma[{t\!t}]
is an irreducible closed set of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
, then
\gamma[{t\!t}]\nsubseteq(\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})[{t\!t}]
, hence
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})\leq{f\!\!f}
. Likewise,
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})\leq{t\!t}
. Therefore,
\sub_{X}(\gamma,\mu_{1}\vee\mu_{2})=0
. ∎

(i)

4.3
A Hofmann-Mislove theorem

The Hofmann-Mislove theorem
[
9
,
7
]
says that for a sober space
X
, the poset of compact saturated subsets of
X
(ordered by reverse inclusion) is isomorphic to the poset of Scott open filters of the open-set lattice of
X
(ordered by inclusion). In this subsection we establish a similar result for
\mathbb{B}
-sober spaces. In
[
14
,
15
]
, making use of the d-frame structure of open sets of a bitopological space, Jung and Moshier have established a Hofmann-Mislove theorem for bitopological spaces, see
[
15
, Theorem 6.6]
. The Hofmann-Mislove theorem established in this paper, see Theorem
4.25
below, only makes use of the fact that the open sets of a bitopological space form an object of the slice category
\mathbb{B}\downarrow\mathbf{Frm}
. While the result of Jung and Moshier establishes a connection between bitopological spaces and d-frames, the result presented here is about the connection between bitopological spaces and objects of a slice category of frames. So, the approaches and the results are quite different.

We need counterpart notions of compact sets, saturated sets, and Scott open filters in the bitopological context.

Definition 4.17
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space and
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
. We say that
\theta
is a compact (
\mathbb{B}
-valued) set of
(X,\tau)
if for each directed family
\Lambda
(w.r.t pointwise order) of open sets of
(X,\tau)
,

\sub_{X}\Big(\theta,\bigvee\Lambda\Big)=\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda).

Proposition 4.18
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space and
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
. Then
\theta
is compact in
(X,\tau)
if and only if
\theta[{t\!t}]
is compact in the topological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
\theta[{f\!\!f}]
is a compact in the topological space
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
.

Proof.

This follows from the fact that for each family
\Lambda
of open sets of
(X,\tau)
,

{t\!t}\leq\sub_{X}\Big(\theta,\bigvee\Lambda\Big)\iff\theta[{t\!t}]\subseteq\bigcup\{\lambda[{t\!t}]:\lambda\in\Lambda\}

and that

\displaystyle{f\!\!f}\leq\sub_{X}\Big(\theta,\bigvee\Lambda\Big)

\displaystyle\iff\theta[{f\!\!f}]\subseteq\bigcup\{\lambda[{f\!\!f}]:\lambda\in\Lambda\}.\qed

Corollary 4.19
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space and
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
. Then
\theta
is compact in
(X,\tau)
if and only if for each directed family
\Lambda
of open sets,
\theta\leq\bigvee\Lambda
implies that
\theta\leq\lambda
for some
\lambda\in\Lambda
.

A
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
is
compact
if the constant function
X\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
with value
1
is compact. It follows from Proposition
4.18
that
(X,\tau)
is compact if and only if it is componentwise compact; that is, both of topological spaces
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
are compact.

Proposition 4.20
.

Every Hausdorff and compact
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is normal; every regular and compact
\mathbb{B}
-topological space is normal.

Proof.

This follows from Proposition
3.29
and the fact that a compact
R_{1}
topological space is normal
[
25
, Theorem 8]
. ∎

Definition 4.21
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space and
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
. We say that
\theta
is a saturated (
\mathbb{B}
-valued) set of
(X,\tau)
if the map
\theta\colon(X,\Omega(\tau))\longrightarrow(\mathbb{B},\rightarrow)
preserves
\mathbb{B}
-order; i.e.,
\Omega(\tau)(x,y)\leq\theta(x)\rightarrow\theta(y)
for all
x,y\in X
.

The (pointwise) join and meet of any family of saturated sets of a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space are saturated.

Proposition 4.22
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space and
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
. Then
\theta
is a saturated set of
(X,\tau)
if and only if
\theta
is the intersection of a family of open sets of
(X,\tau)
.

Proof.

By definition we have

\Omega(\tau)(x,y)=\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}\lambda(x)\rightarrow\lambda(y),

so every open set is saturated, hence the intersection of any family of open sets is saturated. This proves the sufficiency.

For necessity, suppose
\theta\colon X\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a saturated set of
(X,\tau)
. Then for each
x
and each
y
,

\theta(x)\leq\Omega(\tau)(x,y)\rightarrow\theta(y)=\overline{1_{y}}(x)\rightarrow\theta(y)=(\overline{1_{y}}\rightarrow\theta(y))(x).

Since
\overline{1_{y}}\rightarrow\theta(y)=\theta(y)_{X}\vee\neg(\overline{1_{y}})
, then
\overline{1_{y}}\rightarrow\theta(y)
is open and
\theta
is contained in the intersection of the family of open sets
\{\overline{1_{y}}\rightarrow\theta(y):y\in X\}.
Finally, since
\theta(x)=\overline{1_{x}}(x)\rightarrow\theta(x)
for all
x\in X
,
\theta
equals the intersection of that family of open sets, as desired. ∎

Before presenting the next definition, we recall that for each
\mathbb{B}
-topological space
(X,\tau)
, the map
i_{X}\colon\mathbb{B}\longrightarrow\tau
assigns to each element
b
of
\mathbb{B}
the constant open set
X\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
with value
b
; i.e.,
i_{X}(b)=b_{X}
.

Definition 4.23
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space. A map
F\colon\tau\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a
\mathbb{B}
-filter of
\tau
if it satisfies

(F1)

F\circ i_{X}=\rm id_{\mathbb{B}}
, i.e.,
F(b_{X})=b
for all
b\in\mathbb{B}
;

(F2)

F(\lambda\wedge\mu)=F(\lambda)\wedge F(\mu)
for all
\lambda,\mu\in\tau
.

A
\mathbb{B}
-filter is Scott open if it satisfies moreover

(F3)

F\big(\bigvee\Lambda\big)=\bigvee_{\lambda\in\Lambda}F(\lambda)
for each directed subset
\Lambda
of
\tau
.

For each
\mathbb{B}
-filter
F
and each element
\lambda
of
\tau
, from (F1) and (F2) one verifies that
F({t\!t}\wedge\lambda)={t\!t}\wedge F(\lambda)
and
F({f\!\!f}\wedge\lambda)={f\!\!f}\wedge F(\lambda)
, hence
F(\lambda)=F({t\!t}\wedge\lambda)\vee F({f\!\!f}\wedge\lambda)
.

With the notions of compact sets, saturated sets, and Scott open
\mathbb{B}
-filters at hand, we are able to formulate and prove a Hofmann-Mislove theorem for bitopological spaces. We begin with a lemma.

Lemma 4.24
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space and
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
.

(i)

The map
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)\colon\tau\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a
\mathbb{B}
-filter if and only if
\theta
is inhabited; that is,
\bigvee_{x\in X}\theta(x)=1
.

(ii)

The map
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)\colon\tau\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a Scott open
\mathbb{B}
-filter if and only if
\theta
is inhabited and compact.

Proof.

(i) If
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)\colon\tau\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a
\mathbb{B}
-filter, then by (F1) we have

0=\sub_{X}(\theta,0_{X})=\bigwedge_{x\in X}(\theta(x)\rightarrow 0)=\Big(\bigvee_{x\in X}\theta(x)\Big)\rightarrow 0,

which shows that
\theta
is inhabited. Conversely, if
\theta
is inhabited, we show that
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-filter of
\tau
. It is trivial that
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)
satisfies (F2). For (F1) we calculate: for each
b\in\mathbb{B}
,

\sub_{X}(\theta,b_{X})=\bigwedge_{x\in X}(\theta(x)\rightarrow b)=\Big(\bigvee_{x\in X}\theta(x)\Big)\rightarrow b=b.

(ii) This follows from a combination of the following facts:
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)
satisfies (F1) if and only if
\theta
is inhabited;
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)
satisfies (F3) if and only if
\theta
is compact;
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)
always satisfies (F2). ∎

Theorem 4.25
.

Suppose
(X,\tau)
is a
\mathbb{B}
-topological space.

(i)

For each inhabited, saturated and compact set
\theta
of
(X,\tau)
, the map
\sub_{X}(\theta,-)\colon\tau\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a Scott open
\mathbb{B}
-filter of
\tau
.

(ii)

For all inhabited, saturated and compact sets
\theta_{1}
and
\theta_{2}
of
(X,\tau)
, it holds that

\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})=\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}(\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\lambda)\rightarrow\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\lambda)).

While the left side measures the truth degree that
\theta_{2}
is contained in
\theta_{1}
, the right side measures the truth degree that
\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},-)
is contained in
\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},-)
.

(iii)

If
(X,\tau)
is
\mathbb{B}
-sober, then for each Scott open
\mathbb{B}
-filter
F
of
\tau
, there is a unique inhabited, saturated and compact set
\theta
of
(X,\tau)
such that
F=\sub_{X}(\theta,-)
.

Proof.

(i) Lemma
4.24
.

(ii) First we show that if
\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})\leq{t\!t}
then

\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}(\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\lambda)\rightarrow\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\lambda))\leq{t\!t}.

Since
\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})\leq{t\!t}
, then
\theta_{2}(x)\rightarrow\theta_{1}(x)\leq{t\!t}
for some
x\in X
. Consider the open set
\mu
of
(X,\tau)
given by

\mu(y)=\overline{1_{x}}(y)\rightarrow\theta_{1}(x)=\neg(\overline{1_{x}}(y))\vee\theta_{1}(x).

Since
\theta_{1}
is saturated, it follows that
\overline{1_{x}}(y)=\Omega(\tau)(y,x)\leq\theta_{1}(y)\rightarrow\theta_{1}(x)
, then

\theta_{1}(y)\leq\overline{1_{x}}(y)\rightarrow\theta_{1}(x)=\mu(y),

which shows that
\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\mu)=1
. Since

\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\mu)\leq\theta_{2}(x)\rightarrow\mu(x)=\theta_{2}(x)\rightarrow\theta_{1}(x)\leq{t\!t},

then

\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}(\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\lambda)\rightarrow\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\lambda))\leq\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\mu)\rightarrow\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\mu)\leq{t\!t}.

Likewise, if
\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1})\leq{f\!\!f}
then

\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}(\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\lambda)\rightarrow\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\lambda))\leq{f\!\!f}.

Therefore,

\bigwedge_{\lambda\in\tau}(\sub_{X}(\theta_{1},\lambda)\rightarrow\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\lambda))\leq\sub_{X}(\theta_{2},\theta_{1}).

The converse inequality is trivial.

(iii) Suppose
(X,\tau)
is
\mathbb{B}
-sober and
F\colon\tau\longrightarrow\mathbb{B}
is a Scott open
\mathbb{B}
-filter of
\tau
. We wish to prove that there is a unique inhabited, saturated and compact set
\theta
of
(X,\tau)
such that
F(\lambda)=\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\tau
. The proof is an adaptation of that for the Hofmann-Mislove theorem in
[
16
]
to the bitopological context.

We prove the existence of
\theta
first.
Let

\mathcal{F}=\{U\in\tau[{t\!t}]:F({t\!t}_{U})={t\!t}\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{G}=\{V\in\tau[{f\!\!f}]:F({f\!\!f}_{V})={f\!\!f}\}.

Making use of the equality
F({t\!t}\wedge\lambda)={t\!t}\wedge F(\lambda)
one readily verifies that
\mathcal{F}
is a Scott open filter of the topology
\tau[{t\!t}]
. Likewise,
\mathcal{G}
is a Scott open filter of the topology
\tau[{f\!\!f}]
. Let
S_{t\!t}
be the intersection of all members of
\mathcal{F}
,
S_{f\!\!f}
be the intersection of all members of
\mathcal{G}
.
Put

\theta_{t\!t}={t\!t}_{S_{t\!t}},\quad\theta_{f\!\!f}={f\!\!f}_{S_{f\!\!f}},\quad\theta=\theta_{t\!t}\vee\theta_{f\!\!f}.

We show that
\theta
satisfies the requirement.
Since both
\theta_{t\!t}
and
\theta_{f\!\!f}
are saturated,
\theta
is saturated. If we could show that
F(\lambda)=\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
for all
\lambda\in\tau
, then
\theta
would be compact and inhabited by Lemma
4.24
. We do this in two steps.

Step 1
.
F(\lambda)\leq\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
. If
{t\!t}\leq F(\lambda)
, then
\theta_{t\!t}\leq\lambda
, hence

\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)=\sub_{X}(\theta_{t\!t},\lambda)\wedge\sub_{X}(\theta_{f\!\!f},\lambda)\geq 1\wedge{t\!t}={t\!t}.

Likewise, if
{f\!\!f}\leq F(\lambda)
, then
{f\!\!f}\leq\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
. Therefore,
F(\lambda)\leq\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
.

Step 2
.
F(\lambda)\geq\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
. We distinguish three cases.

Case 1.
\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)=1
. By definition of
\sub_{X}
we have

S_{t\!t}=\theta[{t\!t}]\subseteq\lambda[{t\!t}]\quad\text{and}\quad S_{f\!\!f}=\theta[{f\!\!f}]\subseteq\lambda[{f\!\!f}].

Suppose on the contrary that
F(\lambda)\not=1
. From
F(\lambda)=F({t\!t}\wedge\lambda)\vee F({f\!\!f}\wedge\lambda)
one deduces that either
\lambda[{t\!t}]\notin\mathcal{F}
or
\lambda[{f\!\!f}]\notin\mathcal{G}
. Without loss of generality we assume that
\lambda[{t\!t}]
is not in
\mathcal{F}
. Take an open set
U_{0}
of
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
that contains
\lambda[{t\!t}]
and is maximal with respect to not being in the filter
\mathcal{F}
; take an open set
V_{0}
of
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
that is maximal with respect to not being in the filter
\mathcal{G}
. Let
K_{t\!t}
be the complement of
U_{0}
and let
K_{f\!\!f}
be the complement of
V_{0}
. From maximality of
U_{0}
and
V_{0}
one verifies that
(K_{t\!t},K_{f\!\!f})
is a pair of irreducible closed sets of the bitopological space
(X,\tau[{t\!t}],\tau[{f\!\!f}])
. Since
(X,\tau)
is
\mathbb{B}
-sober, there is a unique point
x
of
X
such that
K_{t\!t}
is the closure of
\{x\}
in
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
and
K_{f\!\!f}
is the closure of
\{x\}
in
(X,\tau[{f\!\!f}])
. We claim that every member
U
of
\mathcal{F}
contains
x
, for otherwise
U
misses the closure of
\{x\}
in
(X,\tau[{t\!t}])
, and
hence
U\subseteq U_{0}
, which would imply
U_{0}\in\mathcal{F}
, a contradiction. Therefore,
x\in S_{t\!t}\subseteq\lambda[{t\!t}]
, contradicting that
\lambda[{t\!t}]\subseteq U_{0}
.

Case 2.
\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)={t\!t}
. By definition of
\sub_{X}
we have
\theta\leq{t\!t}\rightarrow\lambda
, then
F({t\!t}\rightarrow\lambda)=1
by Case 1. Therefore

F(\lambda)\geq F({t\!t}\wedge({t\!t}\rightarrow\lambda))={t\!t}\wedge 1={t\!t}.

Case 3.
\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)={f\!\!f}
. Similar to Case 2.

Now we prove the uniqueness of
\theta
. Suppose
\theta\in\mathbb{B}^{X}
satisfies the requirement. Since
\theta
is saturated, it is an intersection of a family of open sets by Proposition
4.22
. Since
F(\lambda)=\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)
and

\sub_{X}(\theta,\lambda)=1\iff\theta\leq\lambda

for each open set
\lambda
, it follows that
\theta=\bigwedge\{\lambda\in\tau:F(\lambda)=1\}
, which proves the uniqueness of
\theta
. ∎
