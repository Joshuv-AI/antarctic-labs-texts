Mathematical foundations of spectral methods for time-dependent PDEs
Arieh Iserles

Abstract
The contention of this paper is that a spectral method for time-dependent PDEs is basically no more than a choice of an orthonormal basis of the underlying Hilbert space. This choice is governed by a long list of considerations: stability, speed of convergence, geometric numerical integration, fast approximation and efficient linear algebra. We subject different choices of orthonormal bases, focussing on the real line, to these considerations. While nothing is likely to improve upon a Fourier basis in the presence of periodic boundary conditions, the situation is considerably more interesting in other settings. We introduce two kinds of orthonormal bases, T-systems and W-systems, and investigate in detail their features. T-systems are designed to work with Cauchy boundary conditions, while W-systems are suited to zero Dirichlet boundary conditions.

Introduction: what is a spectral method?

Spectral methods have been around since the pioneering book of
?
) and they have been the subject of several monographs
[
boyd01cfs
,
canuto06sm
,
funaro92pad
,
hesthaven07smt
,
trefethen00smm
]
. A natural setting for such methods are pure boundary-value PDEs and indeed most – but definitely not all – texts on spectral methods have been concerned with them. The purpose of the current paper is to revisit the theory of spectral methods for
time-dependent PDEs
and recast it in a form consistent with the emerging theory of
geometric numerical integration

[
blanes16cig
,
hairer06gni
,
iserles18wgn
]
.

Our starting point is a time-dependent problem

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\mathcal{L}u+f,\qquad t\geq 0,\quad x\in\Omega,

(1.1)

given in a separable Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, equipped with the inner product
\langle\,\cdot\,,\,\cdot\,\rangle
, with suitable initial and boundary conditions. Here
\mathcal{L}
is a well-posed differential operator.

A spectral method is determined by an orthonormal basis
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
of
\mathcal{H}
. The solution of (
1.1
) is expressed in the underlying basis,

u(x,t)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{n}(t)\varphi_{n}(x),

(1.2)

where the values of
\hat{u}_{n}(0)
are given by expanding the initial condition in the underlying basis, while the subsequent evolution of the coefficients
\hat{u}_{n}(t)
is given by the
Galerkin conditions

\left\langle\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}^{\prime}(t)\varphi_{m}-\mathcal{L}\!\left(\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\hat{u}_{m}(t)\varphi_{m}\!\right)-f,\varphi_{n}\!\right\rangle=0,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(1.3)

In particular, once
\mathcal{L}
is linear, (
1.3
) reduces to

\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}=\sum_{m=0}^{\infty}\langle\mathcal{L}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle\hat{u}_{m}+\langle f,\varphi_{n}\rangle,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(1.4)

by virtue of orthogonality. In practice, (
1.3
) or (
1.4
) are truncated and the subsequent ODE system is solved by a suitable method.

And that’s it! A spectral method is completely determined by an orthonormal basis of the underlying Hilbert space. In other words, the many features that determine the quality of a spectral method hinge on the choice of
\Phi
. All the rest are technicalities which, needless to say, are hugely important and nontrivial.

In the sequel we reduce the discussion for simplicity’s sake to the linear case. The attributes of a spectral method that govern the choice of
\Phi
are

Stability:

The truncated ODE systems

\hat{u}_{n}^{\prime}=\sum_{m=0}^{N}\langle\mathcal{L}\varphi_{m},\varphi_{n}\rangle\hat{u}_{m}+\langle f,\varphi_{n}\rangle,\qquad n=0,\ldots,N.

(1.5)

of (
1.4
) must be uniformly well posed as
N\rightarrow\infty
. This, by the agency of the Lax equivalence theorem, guarantees the convergence of finite-dimensional solutions to the solution of (
1.1
) and is a non-negotiable feature of a numerical method.

Speed of convergence:

The main reason for the efficiency – and, simultaneously, for the limitations – of spectral methods is the following classical result. Consider the Hilbert space
\mathrm{L}_{2}((a,b),\,\mathrm{d}\mu)
of functions defined in the real interval
(a,b)
and square-integrable there with a smooth Borel measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mu
. Let
\Omega
be an open domain such that
[a,b]\subset\Omega
and suppose that both
f
and the
\varphi_{n}
s are
analytic
in
\Omega
. Then the speed of convergence, once
f
is approximated by
\Phi
, is at least exponential, in the sense that

\limsup_{N\rightarrow\infty}\left\|f-\sum_{n=0}^{N}\hat{f}_{n}\varphi_{n}\right\|^{1/N}=\rho

for some
\rho\in[0,1)

[
trefethen13ata
,
walsh65iar
]
. This can be extended to multivariate domains. In other words, as long as both the solution of (
1.1
) and the orthonormal set
\Phi
are analytic (or, at least, very regular), spectral methods converge very rapidly. Moreover, we require fairly small
N
for suitably accurate solution.

In other words, under suitable conditions nothing can beat a spectral method but, once either the solution or the orthonormal basis exhibit poor regularity, we can expect very slow convergence, large
N
and a spectral method is no longer competitive.

Geometric numerical integration:

A fairly recent paradigm in numerical analysis is
Geometric Numerical Integration (GNI):
it is not enough to produce an approximate solution to given accuracy but also it is vital to preserve qualitative features of the solution
[
blanes16cig
,
hairer06gni
]
. In particular, two structural features lend themselves to appropriately designed spectral methods: preservation of dissipativity in the solution of the diffusion equation and preservation of mass (essentially, of the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm) in the solution of the linear Schrödinger equation. Naive discretisation methods may fail to respect these features, thereby mis-computing fundamental physical features.

Note incidentally that preservation of dissipativity or mass automatically (and trivially) implies stability.

Fast expansion:

In the context of spectral methods, a function
f\in\mathcal{H}
is rendered as a sequence
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}}=\{\hat{f}_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
of its expansion coefficients. In other words, we have a
fundamental map

\mathcal{F}
such that
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}}=\mathcal{F}f
. This map is invertible. The computation of
\mathcal{F}f
and
\mathcal{F}^{-1}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$f$\unboldmath}}
need be done at least once per time step and it is important to do so by fast algorithms. This is an important reason why spectral methods are so popular in the presence of periodic boundary conditions: it is then natural to choose
\Phi
as a Fourier basis, whereby
\mathcal{F}
and its inverse can be computed to exponential accuracy by Fast Fourier Transform.

Note that we can assume without loss of generality that
\mathcal{F}
is unitary,
\mathcal{F}^{-1}=\mathcal{F}^{*}
, and an isometry – all it takes is the right scaling of the
\ell_{2}
norm. This assumption, which means that the Plancherel theorem is valid in this setting, is henceforth taken for granted.

Linear algebra:

Ultimately, computer calculations (as distinct from the mathematical theory underpinning algorithms) reduce in the main to linear algebra. While finite differences and finite elements typically yield very large, yet very sparse linear systems, spectral methods lead to considerably smaller systems (in particular for highly regular solutions, cf. our comments on rapid convergence requiring small
N
to attain stipulated accuracy). In traditional spectral methods, though, these systems are dense (and often poorly conditioned). However, it turns out that in the context of time-dependent problems an appropriate choice of
\Phi
leads to ‘nice’ linear systems, which can be solved rapidly. Again, everything depends on the choice of an orthonormal basis!

To recap, these five features determine how good a spectral method is – and the only weapon in our arsenal is the choice of an orthonormal basis.

Before we proceed further, we need to introduce a fundamental concept.

Definition
Given an orthonormal basis
\Phi\subset\mathcal{H}\cap\mathrm{C}^{1}
, a
differentiation matrix

\mathcal{D}
is a linear map taking the vector
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
to its derivative
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}=\{\varphi_{n}^{\prime}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
. In other words,

\varphi_{m}^{\prime}=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{D}_{m,n}\varphi_{n},\qquad m\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

A differentiation matrix always exists and is unique because
\Phi
is a basis of
\mathcal{H}
. Using
\mathcal{D}
in tandem with the fundamental map
\mathcal{F}
allows us to assemble a spectral method. For example, consider (in a single space dimension) the
diffusion equation

\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}},\qquad x\in[-1,1],\quad t\geq 0,

(1.6)

with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions and the
linear Schrödinger equation

{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2}u}{\partial x^{2}}-V(x)u,\qquad x\in\mathbb{R},\quad t\geq 0.

(1.7)

For (
1.6
) we choose an orthonormal basis
\Phi
of

\circ
\mathrm{H}_{2}^{1}[0,1]
and represent the solution in the form (
1.2
), where
\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}_{n}(0)=\mathcal{F}u(\,\cdot\,,0)
. It follows from (
1.4
) that

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}^{2}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}},\qquad\mbox{hence}\qquad\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{t\mathcal{D}^{2}}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(0).

In particular, once
\mathcal{D}
is a skew-Hermitian matrix,
\mathcal{D}^{2}=-\mathcal{D}^{\top}\mathcal{D}
is a Hermitian semi-negative matrix and we conclude that
\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)\|_{2}\leq\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(0)\|_{2}
: this implies not just stability but also dissipativity, a major structural feature of the diffusion equation.

Likewise, for (
1.7
), we obtain

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}^{\prime}={\mathrm{i}}\left(-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{2}+\mathcal{F}V\mathcal{F}^{-1}\right)\!\mbox{\boldmath$\hat{u}$\unboldmath}

In particular, once
\mathcal{D}
is skew-Hermitian and recalling the unitarity of
\mathcal{F}
, the matrix
\mathcal{E}:=-\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{2}+\mathcal{F}V\mathcal{F}^{-1}=\frac{1}{2}\mathcal{D}^{\top}\mathcal{D}+\mathcal{F}V\mathcal{F}^{*}
is Hermitian and

\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)={\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}t\mathcal{E}}\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(0)\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(t)\|_{2}=\|\hat{\mbox{\boldmath$u$\unboldmath}}(0)\|_{2}.

Thus, we have both stability and unitarity of the solution operator: the latter is crucial to applications of (
1.7
) in quantum mechanics.

A common denominator to both examples is that we wish the differentiation matrix to be skew Hermitian (or skew symmetric if it is real). This should come as little surprise because the differentiation operator (subject to Cauchy, periodic or zero Dirichlet boundary conditions) is itself skew symmetric.

The two above examples illustrate the major requirement in the remainder of the paper:
the differentiation matrix
\mathcal{D}
is skew Hermitian.
This is a condition that will guide us in the choice of the orthonormal set
\Phi
.

We do not consider in this paper a different approach to the stability of spectral methods which, in effect, replaces the Galerkin conditions (
1.3
) by
Galerkin–Petrov conditions

[
townsend15asp
]
. This provides an alternative avenue toward stable spectral methods.

2
Orthogonal systems

2.1
Periodic boundary conditions

Once (
1.1
) is given in a finite interval with periodic boundary conditions, there is an obvious choice of an orthonormal system: the standard Fourier basis. In particular, in the interval
[-\pi,\pi]
the Fourier basis is
\varphi_{n}(x)={\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}nx}/\sqrt{2\pi}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
– note that
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}
is indexed over all of
\mathbb{Z}
but this should present no difficulties. The differentiation matrix is both diagonal and skew-Hermitian,

\mathcal{D}_{m,n}=\left\{\hskip-9.0pt\begin{array}[]{ll}{\mathrm{i}}n,&m=n,\\[4.0pt]
0,&\mbox{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.\hskip-9.0pt

An important advantage of a Fourier basis is that the first
N
expansion coefficients of
f\in\mathrm{L}_{2}[\pi,\pi]\cap\mathrm{C}^{\mathrm{per}}[\pi,\pi]
can be approximated to very high accuracy (exponential accuracy for analytic
f
) using the
Fast Fourier Transform (FFT)
in
{\cal O}\!\left(n\log_{2}n\right)
operations.

2.2
Cauchy boundary conditions

Once
\Omega=\mathbb{R}
in (
1.1
), we impose an additional condition on
\Phi
: the differentiation matrix should be both skew-Hermitian and tridiagonal. In other words,

\varphi_{n}^{\prime}=-b_{n-1}\varphi_{n-1}+{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\varphi_{n}+\bar{b}_{n}\varphi_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(2.1)

where
b_{n}\in\mathbb{C}\setminus\{0\}
,
c_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, and
b_{-1}=0
. However, letting
\theta_{0}=0
,
\theta_{n+1}=\theta_{n}-\arg b_{n}
and
\varphi_{n}={\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta_{n}}\tilde{\varphi}_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, we obtain

\tilde{\varphi}_{n}^{\prime}=-|b_{n-1}|\tilde{\varphi}_{n-1}+{\mathrm{i}}c_{n}\tilde{\varphi}_{n}+|b_{n}|\tilde{\varphi}_{n+1},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Therefore, we may assume without loss of generality that
b_{n}>0
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, in (
2.1
). Such an orthonormal basis
\Phi
of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is called a
T-system
and we henceforth proceed to characterise such systems. The theory of T-systems has been initiated in
[
iserles19oss
]
.

Let
\vartheta_{n}(\xi)=(-{\mathrm{i}})^{n}\hat{\varphi}_{n}(\xi)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where
\hat{\varphi}_{n}
is the Fourier transform of
\varphi_{n}
. Then it follows from (
2.1
) that the
\vartheta_{n}
s obey the three-term recurrence relation

b_{n}\vartheta(\xi)=(\xi-c_{n})\vartheta_{n}(\xi)-b_{n-1}\vartheta_{n-1}(\xi),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(2.2)

It is known even to casual students of mathematical analysis that any system of orthonormal polynomials obeys (
2.2
), but a much stronger result is the
Favard theorem:
Whenever
\vartheta_{0}
is a constant (and, consequently, each
\vartheta_{n}
a polynomial of degree
n
), there exists a Borel measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mu
such that
\{\vartheta_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is an orthonormal polynomial system with respect to the inner product

\langle f,g\rangle_{\mu}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(\xi)g(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)

[
chihara78iop
]
. We conclude that the general solution of (
2.2
) is
\vartheta_{n}=gp_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is an orthonormal polynomial system with respect to some Borel measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mu
, while
g\in\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a nonzero distribution. Applying inverse Fourier transform to
{\mathrm{i}}^{n}\vartheta_{n}
results in
\Phi=\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
which is guaranteed to obey (
2.1
) and this, indeed, characterises all its solutions.

Yet, there is absolutely no reason
per se
why
\Phi
should be orthonormal: for this we need to choose the function
g
judiciously. Because of the Plancherel theorem,

\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\varphi_{n}(x)\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\hat{\varphi}_{m}(\xi)\overline{\hat{\varphi}_{n}(\xi)}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{m}(\xi)q_{n}(\xi)|g(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi.

Therefore we have orthogonality if and only if
|g(\xi)|^{2}\,\mathrm{d}\xi=\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)
: an obvious choice is
g(\xi)=\sqrt{w(\xi)}
, where
w
is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of
\mu
,
\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)=w(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi
. This is a complete characterisation of all
\mathrm{L}_{2}
-orthonormal sequences
\Phi
that obey (
2.1
).

Finally, we need
\Phi
to be not just orthonormal with a tridiagonal, skew-Hermitian differentiation matrix: in addition it needs also to be a basis of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. In general, this need not be the case. It follows from our construction that the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
closure of
\Phi
is the
Paley–Wiener space

\mathcal{PW}_{\Xi}
of all
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})\cap\mathrm{C}(\mathbb{R})
functions whose Fourier transform lives in
\Xi=\mathrm{supp}\,\,\mathrm{d}\mu
. Thus, we need
\,\mathrm{d}\mu
to be supported in the entire real line except that, as we will see, there are ways round it.

Our first example of a T-system is the
Legendre measure

\chi_{(-1,1)}(\xi)\,\mathrm{d}\xi
, whereby
p_{n}(\xi)=\mathrm{P}_{n}(\xi)/\sqrt{n+\frac{1}{2}}
, where
\mathrm{P}_{n}
is the standard Legendre polynomial and
g(\xi)\equiv 1
. The outcome is

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{\sqrt{n+\frac{1}{2}}}{x}\mathrm{J}_{n+\frac{1}{2}}(x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(2.3)

spherical Bessel functions. Since the measure is supported in
(-1,1)
, they form an orthonormal basis of
\mathcal{PW}_{(-1,1)}
and are of little interest in the construction of spectral methods.

Next we consider the
Hermite measure

\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi^{2}}\,\mathrm{d}\xi
. The orthonormal polynomial system consists of scaled Hermite polynomials
\mathrm{H}_{n}(\xi)/\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}
,
g(\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi^{2}/2}
and

\varphi_{n}(x)=\frac{\mathrm{H}_{n}(x)}{\sqrt{2^{n}n!\sqrt{\pi}}}{\mathrm{e}}^{-x^{2}/2},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(2.4)

Since the Hermite measure is supported on the entire real line, (
2.4
) is indeed a basis of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
. It is well known in theoretical physics, since the
\varphi_{n}
s are eigenfunctions of the free Schrödinger operator. A T-system of the special form
\varphi_{n}(x)=G(x)q_{n}(x)
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
, where
G
is a function and
q_{n}
an
n
th degree polynomial, occurs exclusively with the Hermite measure
[
iserles19oss
]
.

Our final example are the
Malmquist–Takenaka functions

[
iserles20for
]
. We commence from the
Laguerre measure

\,\mathrm{d}\mu(\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi}\chi_{[0,\infty)}(\xi)
, associated with Laguerre polynomials
\mathrm{L}_{n}
, hence
g(\xi)={\mathrm{e}}^{-\xi/2}
. The closure of
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
, though, is
\mathcal{PW}_{[0,\infty)}
and, to extend to
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
, we combine it with its ‘mirror weight’
{\mathrm{e}}^{\xi}\chi_{(-\infty,0]}(\xi)
, which corresponds to
p_{n}(\xi)=\mathrm{L}_{n}(-\xi)
. It is convenient to index the ensuing functions by integers,

\varphi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}{\mathrm{i}}^{n}\frac{(1+2{\mathrm{i}}x)^{n}}{(1-2{\mathrm{i}}x)^{n+1}},\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}.

(2.5)

This is a complex-valued system and so is
\mathcal{D}
, with
b_{n}=n+1
and
c_{n}=2n+1
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}
.

A particularly appealing feature of the T-system (
2.5
) is that, changing the variable
x=\tan(\theta/2)
,

\hat{f}_{n}=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f(x)\overline{\varphi_{n}(x)}\,\mathrm{d}x=\frac{(-{\mathrm{i}})^{n}}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\!\!\left(1\!-\!{\mathrm{i}}\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\!f\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\tan\frac{\theta}{2}\right)\!{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}n\theta}\,\mathrm{d}\theta,\quad\!\!n\in\mathbb{Z},

a system of Fourier integrals which can be evaluated rapidly with FFT. A very minor generalisation of this setting leads to all possible T-systems that can be computed by FFT
[
iserles20for
]
, while all T-systems that can be evaluated by Fast Cosine and Sine Transforms have been characterised in
[
iserles21fco
]
.

2.3
Dirichlet boundary conditions

Can T-systems be extended to the setting of a compact interval
(a,b)
with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions? While their construction via Fourier transform no longer works, in a forthcoming publication we introduce an alternative, more algebraic means to ‘build’ a T-system inductively. Given
\varphi_{0}\in\mathrm{C}^{\infty}
, we construct
\{\varphi_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
, as well as
b_{n},c_{n}
by recursion for
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}

[
iserles24ost
]
, and this works in any interval with zero Dirichlet boundary conditions. However, the problem is that once
\varphi_{n}(a)=0
for all
n
at some point
a
then
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}^{\prime}=\mathcal{D}\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}
implies that
\varphi_{n}^{\prime}(a)=0
,
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
and, more generally,
\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}^{(\ell)}=\mathcal{D}^{\ell}\mbox{\boldmath$\varphi$\unboldmath}
for all
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. This and the fact that
\mathcal{D}^{\ell}
is bounded imply that
\varphi_{n}^{(\ell)}(a)=0
for all
n,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
. Therefore the
\varphi_{n}
s cannot be analytic at the endpoints.

In principle, this can be overcome by placing essential singularities at the endpoints while maintaining integrability (and analyticity) in
(a,b)
: for example, we might take
(a,b)=(-1,1)
and

\varphi_{0}(x)=a_{0}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{1-x^{2}}\right)\!,\qquad a_{0}=\left[\int_{-1}^{1}\exp\!\left(-\frac{2}{1-y^{2}}\right)\!\,\mathrm{d}y\right]^{-1/2},

This can be extended to a full T-system using the methodology of
[
iserles24ost
]
. Unfortunately, essential singularities at the endpoints strike back: as
n
grows, the
\varphi_{n}
s develop a boundary layer of increasing amplitude and the approximating power of
\Phi
is very poor indeed.

An alternative to T-systems, better suited to this setting, are W-systems
[
iserles24ssm
]
. Given
-\infty\leq a<b\leq\infty
and a weight function
w\in\mathrm{C}^{1}(a,b)
, we let

\varphi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{w(x)}p_{n}(x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+},

(2.6)

where
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
is an orthonormal polynomial system with respect to the Borel measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x)=w(x)\chi_{(a,b)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
. Trivially,
\Phi
is an orthonormal basis of
\mathrm{L}_{2}(a,b)
. Moreover, since

\mathcal{D}_{m,n}+\mathcal{D}_{n,m}=\int_{a}^{b}(\varphi_{m}^{\prime}\varphi_{n}+\varphi_{m}\varphi_{n}^{\prime})\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{a}^{b}\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}x}\varphi_{m}\varphi_{n}\,\mathrm{d}x=\varphi_{m}(x)\varphi_{n}(x)\,\rule[-5.0pt]{0.75pt}{18.0pt}_{\,x=a}^{\,x=b},

it follows from (
2.6
) that
\mathcal{D}
is skew symmetric if and only if
w(a)=w(b)=0
.

Unlike in the case of T-systems, the differentiation matrix is neither tridiagonal nor can we take for granted that all its powers are bounded – indeed, the entire purpose of the exercise is to construct systems so that
\mathcal{D}^{s+1}
blows up for some
s\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}
: provided that
\mathcal{D}^{\ell}
is bounded for
\ell\leq s
, we say that
s
is the
index
of
w
and denote it by
\mathrm{ind}\,w
. It is possible to prove that
\mathrm{ind}\,w\geq 2
if and only if
{w^{\prime}}^{2}/w
is a weight function (i.e. nonnegative, with all its moments bounded and the zeroth moment positive)
[
iserles24ssm
]
. Moreover, a necessary condition for
\mathrm{ind}\,w\geq s
in a finite interval
(a,b)
is that
w(x)=(x-a)^{\alpha}(b-n)^{\beta}\tilde{w}(x)
, where
\alpha,\beta>s-1
, with obvious extension to
(a,\infty)
and
(-\infty,b)

[
iserles24ssm
]
.

Our first example of a W-system is when
\mathcal{D}
is a banded matrix: in a sense, this is the least interesting case because all its powers are then bounded as well, which somewhat defeats the purpose of the exercise. This happens if and only if
w(x)={\mathrm{e}}^{-q(x)}
,
x\in\mathbb{R}
, where
q
is an even-degree polynomial,
q(x)=x^{2r}+\mbox{l.o.t.}
. This corresponds to a
Freud measure
and a special case,
q(x)=x^{2}
, yields Hermite functions – the only instance of a W-system being also a T-system (cf. (
2.4
)).

Without loss of generality, it is enough to consider the intervals
[0,\infty)
and
[-1,1]
. In
[0,\infty)
the obvious choice is the
Laguerre weight function

w(x)=x^{\alpha}{\mathrm{e}}^{-x}
,
\alpha>-1
, whereby

\varphi_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{n!}{\mathrm{\Gamma}(n+1+\alpha)}}x^{\alpha/2}{\mathrm{e}}^{-x/2}\mathrm{L}_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x),\qquad x>0,\quad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

(2.7)

It takes several pages of dense algebra to derive the explicit form of the differentiation matrix for
\alpha>1
,

\mathcal{D}_{m,n}=\left\{\hskip-9.0pt\begin{array}[]{ll}-\frac{1}{2}\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n},&m\geq n+1,\\[2.0pt]

2.4
Multivariate setting

It is straightforward to extend Fourier expansions to the torus
\mathbb{T}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
, while we can be generalise T-systems to
\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
d\geq 2
and W-systems to parallelepipeds using tensor products. Moreover, W-systems can be extended to different multivariate geometries using the theory of orthogonal polynomials in
d
dimensions, although each generalisation is nontrivial, not least the derivation of the differentiation matrix. In particular,
[
gao24fss
]
extends W-systems to
d
-dimensional balls using a generalisation of Zernike polynomials, while a generalisation to triangles and simplexes is subject to current active research.

3
Fast expansion and linear algebra

The following tasks are at the heart of a successful implementation of a spectral method:

•

The computation of
\mathcal{F}_{N}
(the fundamental map, truncated to its first
N+1
coefficients) and its inverse
\mathcal{F}^{*}_{N}
;

•

Formation of a product of the form
\mathcal{D}_{N}\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}
, where
\mathcal{D}_{N}
is an
(N+1)\times(N+1)
principal minor of
\mathcal{D}
and
\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}\in\mathbb{C}^{N+1}
;

•

The solution of linear algebraic systems of the form
(I-\kappa\mathcal{D}_{N})\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}
, where
\kappa\in\mathbb{C}
– note that a system of the form
q(\mathcal{D}_{N})\mbox{\boldmath$y$\unboldmath}=\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}
, where
q
is a polynomial of degree
k
, can be represented as a succession of
k
such problems. Note further that
\mathcal{D}^{*}=-\mathcal{D}
means that polynomials in both
\mathcal{D}_{N}
and
\mathcal{D}^{*}_{N}
fit into this formalism.

•

The computation of
f(t\mathcal{D}_{N})\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}
, where
f
is a rational function – in particular
f(z)={\mathrm{e}}^{z}
;

These four imperatives, combined with the speed of convergence – a subject relegated to the next section – are fundamental in guiding us in the choice of the underlying orthonormal basis
\Phi
.

3.2
W-systems

Insofar as W-systems are concerned, computing

A differentiation matrix of a W-system is dense (unless the weight function is a Freud weight) and we no longer enjoy the benefit of tridiagonality. However, linear algebra with both Laguerre and ultraspherical W-systems can be accomplished very effectively exploiting another feature of their differentiation matrices: they are
semiseparable

[
hackbusch15hma
,
vandebril08mc1
,
vandebril08mc2
]

r
if each finite-dimensional sub-matrix which lies entirely above or entirely underneath the main diagonal is at most of rank
r\in\mathbb{N}
and there exists at least one such matrix of rank exactly
r
. It follows at once from (
2.8
) and (
2.10
) that
r=1
for both Laguerre and ultraspherical W-systems.

As an example how semiseparability leads to fast linear algebra, we show how to compute rapidly the product
\mathcal{D}_{N}\mbox{\boldmath$x$\unboldmath}
for the Laguerre W-system, while noting that the solution of linear systems (and much more) can be also done in an economical manner using the material in
[
vandebril08mc1
]

y_{m}=\sum_{n=0}^{K}\mathcal{D}_{m,n}x_{n},\qquad m=0,\ldots,N,

where
N\leq K
, as this allows for far greater accuracy in truncating
\mathcal{D}
. To this end, recalling (

\sigma_{m}=\sum_{n=0}^{m-1}\mbox{\gothic b}_{n}x_{n},\quad\rho_{m}=\sum_{n=m+1}^{K}\mbox{\gothic a}_{n}f_{n},\qquad m=0,\ldots,N.

Then

y_{0}=\mbox{\gothic b}_{0}\rho_{0},\qquad y_{m}=-\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}\sigma_{m}+\mbox{\gothic b}_{m}\rho_{m},\quad m=1,\ldots,N.

Since

\sigma_{m}=\sigma_{m-1}+\mbox{\gothic b}_{m-1}x_{m-1},\qquad\rho_{m}=\rho_{m-1}-\mbox{\gothic a}_{m}x_{m},

once

and

are precomputed, it takes just

4
Speed of convergence

It is convenient to commence from W-systems in a compact interval, specifically considering the ultraspherical W-system in
(-1,1)
(although other W-systems in a compact interval are subject to the same ground rules). The speed of convergence is governed by standard theory of orthogonal polynomials. Thus, let
\rho>0
and consider the
Bernstein ellipse

\mathcal{B}_{\rho}=\{\rho{\mathrm{e}}^{{\mathrm{i}}\theta}+\rho^{-1}{\mathrm{e}}^{-{\mathrm{i}}\theta}\,:\,-\pi\leq\theta\leq\pi\}.

Suppose that
f
is analytic inside
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
and let
\{p_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}}
be an orthonormal polynomial system in
(-1,1)
with respect to the measure
\,\mathrm{d}\mu
. Then

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|f-\sum_{m=0}^{n}\check{f}_{n}p_{n}\right\|_{\mathrm{L}_{2}[(-1,1),\,\mathrm{d}\mu]}^{1/n}=\frac{1}{\rho},

(4.1)

where

\check{f}_{n}=\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)p_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}\mu(x),\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

In other words, the speed of convergence is exponential and governed by the ‘analyticity ellipse’
[
trefethen00smm
,
walsh65iar
]
.

Let
f
be analytic inside
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
and, in addition,
f^{(i)}(-1)=f^{(i)}(1)=0
,
i=0,\ldots,s-1
. Therefore,

f^{\langle s\rangle}(x):=\frac{f(x)}{(1-x^{2})^{s}}

is analytic inside
\mathcal{B}_{\rho}
. Recalling that
\varphi_{n}(x)=(1-x^{2})^{\alpha/2}\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha)}(x)
, where
\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha)}
is an orthonormal Jacobi polynomial, we note that it is analytic if and only if
\alpha
is an even nonnegative integer. Moreover, we have

\hat{f}_{n}=\int_{-1}^{1}f(x)\varphi_{n}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x=\int_{-1}^{1}(1-x^{2})^{\alpha/2+s}f^{\langle s\rangle}(x)\tilde{\mathrm{P}}_{n}^{(\alpha,\alpha)}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x,\qquad n\in\mathbb{Z}_{+}.

Therefore, once we choose
\alpha=2s
, it is true that
\hat{f}_{n}=\check{f}_{n}^{\langle s\rangle}
and (
4.1
) implies exponential convergence. Note in passing that any
\alpha=2q
, where
q\geq s
is an integer, guarantees exponential convergence in the
\mathrm{L}_{2}
norm but, once
q>s
,
\mathrm{H}_{\infty}^{s}
convergence is not possible because the
(s+1)
st derivative of the approximation – but not of
f
– vanishes at the endpoints. Thus, the choice
\alpha=2s
is the ‘sweet spot’, allowing for both exponential convergence in
\mathrm{L}_{2}
and for
\mathrm{H}_{\infty}^{s}
convergence.

The situation is, if at all, even more open-ended with regard to T-systems, because comprehensive convergence theory on the real line is not available either. There are few results available and they are intriguing, sometimes baffling. In one case, when
f\in\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
is a rational function, everything is known and can be derived with relative ease. Thus, suppose that the poles of
f
are
s_{1},\ldots,s_{N}
and that
\mathrm{Im}\,s_{k}<0
for
k=1,\ldots,K-1
and
\mathrm{Im}\,s_{k}>0
for
k=K,\ldots,N
– clearly, unless
\mathrm{Im}\,s_{k}\neq 0
,
k=1,\ldots,N
, the function
f
cannot be
\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})
! Set

\sigma_{k}=\frac{1-2{\mathrm{i}}s_{k}}{2s_{k}-{\mathrm{i}}},\qquad k=1,\ldots,N

and note that
|\sigma_{k}|<1
for
k=1,\ldots,K-1
and
|\sigma_{k}|>1
for
k=K,\ldots,N
. Then

\limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty}\left\|f-\sum_{k=-n}^{n}\hat{f}_{n}\varphi_{n}\right\|^{1/n}_{\mathrm{L}_{2}(\mathbb{R})}=\max\left\{\max_{k=1,\ldots,K-1}|\sigma_{k}|,\max_{k=K,\ldots,N}|\sigma_{k}|^{-1}\right\}<1.

(4.2)

We have exponential convergence! Unfortunately, all this goes away even upon a seemingly minor change of scenery. Thus, consider first
f(x)=1/(1+x+x^{2})
: we have
s_{1}=-\frac{1}{2}-{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
,
s_{2}=-\frac{1}{2}+{\mathrm{i}}\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}
, therefore
\sigma_{1}=-(2+3{\mathrm{i}})/(5+2\sqrt{3})
and
\sigma_{2}=-(2+3{\mathrm{i}})/(5-2\sqrt{3})
– note that
|\sigma_{1}|=|\sigma_{2}|^{-1}=\sqrt{(37-20\sqrt{3})/13}\approx 0.4260
and all is well. However, suppose that we replace
f
by
\cos x/(1+x+x^{2})
: on the face of it, not much has changed – the cosine is an entire function, bounded by 1 in modulus along the real line. However, it has an essential singularity at infinity (i.e. at the North Pole of the Riemann sphere) and this degrades the speed of convergence down to
{\cal O}\!\left(|n|^{-4/3}\right)
– from an exponential heaven to little better than linear convergence.

The situation is, if at all, worse for a Hermite T-system: now both
1/(1+x+x^{2})
and
\cos x/(1+x+x^{2})
exhibit
{\cal O}\!\left(n^{4/3}\right)
convergence.

The speed of convergence for different flavours of T-systems is an open problem. Asymptotic analysis in
[
weideman95cht
]
provides few clues, as does the analysis of Hermite and Malmquist–Takenaka systems in the case of wave packets in
[
iserles23awp
]
, but much is yet to be done to investigate how well T-systems approximate analytic (or rougher) functions.
