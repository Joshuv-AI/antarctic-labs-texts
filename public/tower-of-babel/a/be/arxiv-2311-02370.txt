FIRE 6.5: Feynman Integral Reduction with New Simplification Library Journal: Computer Physics Communications
Alexander V. Smirnov, Mao Zeng

Abstract
FIRE is a program which performs integration-by-parts (IBP) reduction of Feynman integrals. Originally, the C++ version of FIRE relies on the computer algebra system Fermat by Robert Lewis to simplify rational functions. We present an upgrade of FIRE which incorporates a new library FUEL initially described in a separate publication, which enables a flexible choice of third-party computer algebra systems as simplifiers, as well as efficient communications with some of the simplifiers as C++ libraries rather than through Unix pipes. We achieve significant speedups for IBP reduction of Feynman integrals involving many kinematic variables, when using an open source backend based on FLINT newly added in this work, or the Symbolica backend developed by Ben Ruijl as a potential successor of FORM.

Nature of problem:

Feynman integrals of a given family are reduced to a finite set of master integrals, by solving linear equations arising from integration by parts, using Gaussian elimination. The coefficient of the linear equations are generally rational functions in kinematic variables and the spacetime dimension, and the simplification of such rational functions during Gaussian elimination is a key task that is improved in this upgrade of
FIRE

Solution method:

Computer algebra systems with state-of-the-art capabilities for polynomial GCD computations are used as simplification backends, or simplifiers in short. Due to the design of
FIRE

C++
parser is written to parse strings into the internal format of an external simplifer,
FLINT
[3], with state-of-the-art performance for multivariate polynomial calculations. Similarly, the simplifier
Symbolica
[4] has high performance in both GCD computations and parsing, and has been integrated into FIRE.

Introduction

Evaluating Feynman integrals underlies perturbative calculations in quantum field theory. Integration-by-parts (IBP) reduction
[
1
]
is a standard step in evaluating Feynman integrals and expresses all integrals of a given family, with a common set of propagators, in terms of a finite number of master integrals. IBP reduction exploits the fact that total derivatives integrate to zero in dimensional regularization, which generates linear relations, referred to as
integration-by-parts equations
, between different integrals of a given family. Typically, master integrals are chosen to be “simple” integrals where propagators are not raised to high powers and numerators are low-degree monomials.

In all but the simplest applications, one must consider non-obvious linear sums of IBP equations to accomplish the reduction. One systematic approach is the Laporta algorithm
[
2
]
. Some publicly available implementations of the Laporta algorithm include
AIR

[
3
]
,
Reduze

[
4
]
,
LiteRed

[
5
]
,
FIRE

[
6
,
7
,
8
]
, and
Kira

[
9
,
10
,
11
]
. FIRE has been developed by one of the current authors and collaborators over the past decade and has found many applications in cutting-edge calculations in high energy physics. The Laporta algorithm generates all IBP equations under a certain cutoff of the most complicated integral involved, before solving the system of linear equations using Gaussian elimination. The linear equations have coefficients that are polynomials in the spacetime dimension
d
and kinematic variables (e.g. Mandelstam variables and mass parameters), and the coefficients become rational functions of the same set of variables during Gaussian elimination. These rational functions need to be simplified (usually into single fractions whose numerators and denominators have no nontrivial polynomial GCD) throughout the process of Gaussian elimination to prevent the expressions from exploding in size.

An alternative to the Laporta algorithm is deriving reduction rules with symbolic dependence on the powers of propagators and irreducible scalar products, through heuristic algorithms, e.g. implemented in
LiteRed

[
12
]
, or through Groebner bases
[
13
,
14
,
15
,
16
,
17
,
18
,
19
]
. The reduction rules express complicated integrals in terms of simpler integrals (according to some ordering), and the rules generally need to be applied repeatedly to express a given integral in terms of master integrals. Therefore the simplification of rational functions in intermediate steps is again an essential computational task. The approach of syzygy equations
[
20

21

reduces the size of the linear system, while the approach of block triangular forms
[
29

30
]
makes the linear system more structured, but the need to simplify rational functions in intermediate steps remains.

Simplification of rational functions is a functionality provided by
computer algebra systems
. Some IBP programs are written in a computer algebra system already, so this functionality can be accessed naturally. For example, AIR is written in Maple, and FIRE has an alternative
Wolfram Mathematica
variant besides the much faster
C++
variant. However, many of the high-performance IBP programs are written in a general-purpose programming language. This requires interfacing an external computer algebra system as either a separate program or as a library. For example,
Reduze
,
Kira
, and (the preferred variant of)
FIRE
are primarily written in C++. All these three programs (until the new version of FIRE presented here) use the Fermat computer algebra system by default for simplifying rational functions.

Performance considerations in rational function simplification

The complete list of supported simplifiers included in
FUEL
, and hence available in the new version of
FIRE
, can be found on the
README

https://gitlab.com/feynmanintegrals/fuel.git

In this section, we focus on
FLINT
and
Symbolica
, which achieve competitive performance across a wide range of IBP reduction problems when used with
FIRE
. In Ref.
[
33
]
which presented
FUEL
, the Nemo computer algebra system
[
49

was investigated as a simplifier, which provided much of the prototyping for our subsequent use of
FLINT
in
FUEL
1.0 described here. Nemo is a package for the Julia programming language, and the underlying engine for polynomial computations is the C library
FLINT
. By switching to using
FLINT
directly, we find it easier to gain control over performance. We use recent development versions of
FLINT
which contain an implementation of multivariate rational function arithmetic on top of its more established polynomial functionality.

[

, before being merged into the development repository of

fmpz_mpoly_q.h
.

Symbolica

[
36

is being developed by Ben Ruijl as a modernized alternative to FORM
[

, and the use of Symbolica in
FUEL
was already investigated in Ref.
[

. The author of
Symbolica
has further optimized the software for our purposes since then.

In the subsections below, we briefly describe some of the performance considerations that have been taken into account in the
FLINT
and
Symbolica
backends.

2.1
Overhead in string representations of expressions

In
FIRE
and
FUEL
, rational functions are stored as strings in the common mathematical notation, e.g.
(2*x-y)/(x+yˆ2)
. Though not without drawbacks, this is a convenient choice, since (1) this format is accepted by almost any third-party computer algebra system; (2) database systems, used to store intermediate results, commonly accept string data; (3) strings are used to exchange expressions between different worker processes running in parallel.

When
FIRE
needs to simplify an expression, it is sent as a string to the external simplifier, which parses the string into an internal representation, performs simplifications, and prints out the simplified expression. As pointed out in the initial paper for
FUEL

[
33
]
, an external simplifier must have excellent performance in not only the simplification itself but also parsing and printing to achieve competitive performance. For example, the aforementioned reference finds Maple to be among the fastest simplifiers in contrived tests that minimize the overhead of parsing and printing, but Maple performs poorly for realistic FIRE workloads due to the overhead.

FLINT
has a built-in parser for polynomials but not rational functions. We have written a fast
C++
parser to convert strings representing rational functions into
FLINT
’s internal rational function type. The parsing algorithm is a variant of the well-known shunting yard algorithm for parsing mathematical expressions while taking operator precedence into account.
Symbolica
also has a fast parser for rational function expressions. The good parsing performance is essential for the good performance of
FLINT
and
Symbolica
backends in the simpler IBP reduction problems tested in Section
3
, since the parsing overhead is usually significant in this situation
[
33
]
.

2.2
Re-evaluation cost

Since
FIRE
stores rational functions as strings, binary arithmetic operators are implemented by concatenating the operands with the appropriate operator characters. For example, to add two rational functions that were previously simplified, written schematically as
(poly1)/(poly2)
and
(poly3)/(poly4)
,
FIRE
forms the string

(poly1)/(poly2) + (poly3)/(poly4)

and sends it (via the
FUEL
library) to the simplifier. However, now the simplifier does not make assumptions about the input and attempts to simplify the two rational functions again before adding them. For example, the simplifier will attempt to find the polynomial GCD of
poly1
and poly2 and divide both polynomials by the GCD. This step can be avoided if it is known that the rational function is already simplified and the numerator and denominator have no nontrivial GCD.

To solve this problem, the
FLINT
and
Symbolica
backends have been programmed to print out simplified rational functions in the custom notation

[poly1,poly2]

instead of
(poly1)/(poly2)
, to indicate that the rational function has already been simplified. When such strings are inserted into further computations, the parser will directly produce a rational function given by the numerator
poly1
and denominator
poly2
, without attempting a redundant polynomial GCD computation. When a simplified expression is a single polynomial rather than a rational function, the custom notation

[poly]

is used.
3
3

3

When this notation is encountered in further computations, the
FLINT
backend uses a polynomial parser that does not handle rational functions, and the Symbolica backend uses a dedicated parser for polynomials that are known to have been expanded.

At the end of a FIRE run, all such non-conventional strings are converted back to the conventional mathematical format, for the results (IBP reduction tables) to be consumed by end users, e.g. using Wolfram Mathematica.

Installation and Usage

Most of the installation and usage instructions are unchanged from the original paper describing
FIRE

[

4.1
Installation

The installation instructions are similar to
FIRE
6.0
[
8
]
, except that the
configure
script allows additional optional arguments for installing third-party simplifiers to be used by
FIRE
through
FUEL
. For example, to compile
FLINT
and
Symbolica
backends, in addition to the bundled Fermat backend, run

./configure --enable_flint --enable_symbolica

As in
FIRE
6.0, the
ZStandard

./configure --enable_flint --enable_symbolica --enable_zstd --enable_tcmalloc

Then the dependencies can be compiled by

make dep

while
FIRE
itself can be compiled by

make

Since both the
FLINT

FIRE
depend on
GMP
and
MPFR
libraries for arbitrary-precision arithmeetic, the source code for the latter two libraries have been bundled and will be built during the
make dep
step. For convenience, we have optionally supplied prebuilt binaries of these two libraries which work for most current Linux distributions that use
glibc
as the C library, such as Ubuntu 22.04 and CentOS 7. The use of the prebuilt binaries can be enabled by the following extra options supplied to the command line of
configure
:

--enable_prebuilt_gmp --enable_prebuilt_mpfr

4.2
Usage

The only significant change in usage compared with
FIRE
6.0 is the possibility to specify the simplifier used. Currently,
Fermat
is still the default simplifier. To specify a different simplifer, e.g.
FLINT
, one can use the

--calc flint

command line option for
FIRE
or the finite-field variant

. Alternatively, one can add the line

#calc flint

to the configuration file. Similarly, one could replace
flint
by the name of another simplifier in lower-case letters, e.g.
symbolica
, if desired. Please note that the user is responsible for acquiring the licenses of any proprietary simplifiers, such as
Fermat
and

.

To run the benchmarks in Section

bin/FIRE6 -c examples/v2l --calc flint

bin/FIRE6 -c examples/v2l_high --calc flint

The necessary

5
Conclusion

We have presented the new version 6.5 of
FIRE
, a program for integration-by-parts reduction of Feynman integrals. The main change is the use of the
FUEL
library which provides a uniform interface for simplifying rational functions, with a flexible choice of third-party simplifiers as backends. We have presented benchmarks for a variety of IBP reduction problems, including both runs based on the Laporta algorithm and runs based on
LiteRed
reduction rules. The open source
FLINT
backend, newly added in this work, and the
Symbolica
backend both achieve performance that is comparable with, or moderately better than, the default
Fermat
backend for simple IBP reduction problems with a small number of kinematic scales and low-complexity integrals.
FLINT
and
Symbolica
start to significantly out-perform Fermat when the complexity of the integrals increases, as can be seen in the benchmark for the double box diagram with a rank-8 numerator and the four-loop diagram involved in classical two-body scattering in electrodynamics. The performance advantages of
FLINT
and
Symbolica
over
Fermat
become especially prominent for IBP problems with a large number of kinematic scales, with a speedup of up to a factor 9 seen in the benchmark for the 3-loop banana diagram with four unequal internal masses.

In the future, we would like to explore further ideas for optimizing the simplification of rational functions in
FIRE
, e.g. by exploring the freedom to reorder summation terms, as already implemented in
Kira

[
9
]
. More complicated IBP reduction tasks involving many kinematic scales will be tested, given the promising results already seen. We would also like to apply
FUEL
to the reconstruction of multivariate rational functions through the balanced reconstruction algorithm
[
37
]
.
