Finite element-based space-time total variation-type regularization of the inverse problem in electrocardiographic imaging
Manuel Haas, Thomas Grandits, Thomas Pinetz, Thomas Beiert, Simone Pezzuto, Alexander Effland

Abstract
Reconstructing cardiac electrical activity from body surface electric potential measurements results in the severely ill-posed inverse problem in electrocardiography. Many different regularization approaches have been proposed to improve numerical results and provide unique results. This work presents a novel approach for reconstructing the epicardial potential from body surface potential maps based on a space-time total variation-type regularization using finite elements, where a first-order primal-dual algorithm solves the underlying convex optimization problem. In several numerical experiments, the superior performance of this method and the benefit of space-time regularization for the reconstruction of epicardial potential on two-dimensional torso data and a three-dimensional rabbit heart compared to state-of-the-art methods are demonstrated.

Introduction

Deducing the electrical activity of the heart non-invasively has enormous potential to shorten clinical intervention times and improve patient outcomes.
For this purpose, specialized hardware was developed to capture the electrical activity on the torso and reconstruct the epicardial potential
[
13
,
42
]
in the form of body potential surface maps (BSPM).
Reconstructing the epicardial potential from BSPMs by exploiting further information about the smoothness and regularity of the potential functions is widely known as the inverse problem in electrocardiographic imaging (ECGI).
The resulting epicardial potential enables clinicians to understand and identify the origins of arrhythmias.
Previous studies
[
34
,
41
]
provide comprehensive overviews of inverse problems in cardiovascular modeling, including electrocardiographic imaging and blood flow modeling.
The mathematical formulation of the inverse problem in ECGI in terms of a linear forward operator is extensively studied in the literature (see e.g.
[
15
,
47
,
50
]
).
Computing the discretized forward operator numerically results in different choices like the boundary element method (BEM)
[
30
,
46
]
, the method of fundamental solutions (MFS)
[
55
]
, and the finite element methods (FEM)
[
9
,
44
,
52
,
53
]
.
In this work, we focus on the FEM, as it demonstrated improved performance for the solution of the inverse problem
[
6
]
.

The potential-based ECGI problem results in an ill-posed Cauchy problem in the sense of Hadamard
[
4
,
24
,
25
]
.
In particular, the inverse solution is extremely sensitive to small perturbations of the measurements.
To overcome these issues, regularization of the epicardial potential is required to solve the inverse problem robustly.
Different approaches were proposed including the well-known Tikhonov regularization
[
49
]
of different orders, model-based approaches
[
22
,
39
]
, and machine learning-driven methods
[
48
]
.
Despite the fact that machine learning and deep learning approaches lead to promising results in simulated models, the training imposes challenges.
Due to the absence of available human data sets containing BSPMs, thoracic CT scans, and corresponding invasive measurements of epicardial potentials as ground truth reference, well-established methods like Tikhonov regularization are still among the most commonly applied types of regularization.
Tikhonov regularization imposes a penalty on the electrical activity of the heart or its derivatives with the quadratic
L^{2}
-norm causing considerable smoothing of the epicardial potential.
In some approaches (see e.g.
[
12
,
27
]
) different regularization techniques and hyperparameter choices for optimization are systematically compared.
Optimizing inverse problems with non-quadratic
L^{1}
or
L^{2,1}
regularization with
\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{L^{2,1}(\Omega)}\coloneqq\int_{\Omega}\left\lVert f\right\rVert_{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}
for
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
open,
f\in(L^{2}(\Omega))^{d}
, and
d\in\mathbb{N}
is widely known to successfully reconstruct solutions in imaging and computing less smoothed potential functions (see e.g.
[
2
,
10
,
17
,
20
,
40
,
54
]
).

Further studies using temporal as well as spatial priors for spatiotemporal inverse problems, including Bayesian MAP-based regularization, show promising improvements (see e.g.
[
1
,
23
,
31
,
36
]
).

In this paper, we present a novel space-time total variation-type regularization in a finite element setting on unstructured grids that allows for sharp transitions of the electrical activity on the heart, which is crucial for accurately modeling rapid changes in cardiac electrical signals.
Unlike Tikhonov regularization, which primarily enforces smoothness and often struggles to represent sharp transitions effectively, total variation captures discontinuities and sharp gradients inherent in cardiac electrophysiology, making it a more suitable choice for this problem.
The novelty of our approach lies in integrating space-time total variation regularization with finite element methods, a concept that has been scarcely explored in the literature. To identify the most suitable method, we introduce various finite element discretizations and different formulations of total variation for evaluation.
Here, the quality of the solution depends on two hyperparameters representing anisotropic smoothing in spatial and temporal directions.
The proposed total variation-type regularizer is non-differentiable and therefore not amenable to classical derivative-based optimization algorithms.
Classically, this non-differentiability can be overcome by a smooth approximation
[
7
]
, which, however, neglects sharp interfaces promoted by total variation.
Thus, in this paper, we advocate a first-order primal-dual method
[
11
]
adapted for finite elements, thereby exploiting the convexity of the energy function, resulting in fast and accurate reconstruction.

Forward and Inverse Problem in Electrocardiographic Imaging

In the following section, we introduce the forward problem in electrocardiographic imaging and deduce the inverse problem.
Furthermore, we propose a novel space-time total variation-type regularizer and prove the existence and uniqueness of weak solutions.

2.1
Forward Problem

The well-posed forward problem in ECGI aims to compute the electrical activity on the torso domain from the given epicardial potential.
Let us denote the
torso domain
by
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
for
d\in\{2,3\}
with
body surface

\Gamma=\partial\Omega
and associated
unit outer normal vector

\mathbf{n}
, and the
heart
by
\Omega_{H}\subset\Omega
with
epicardium

\Gamma_{H}=\partial\Omega_{H}
.
Furthermore, we denote by
\Omega_{0}=\Omega\setminus\bar{\Omega}_{H}
the torso domain excluding the cardiac region.
The boundary
\partial\Omega_{0}=\Gamma\cup\Gamma_{H}
is assumed to be Lipschitz and bounded, and
\Gamma\cap\Gamma_{H}=\emptyset
.
In our work, we exclusively assume static domains that are invariant over time.
We also denote by
\sigma\in L^{\infty}(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^{+})
the
torso’s conductivity tensor (bulk conductivity)
, where we assume the space-dependent matrix
\sigma
to be symmetric satisfying the ellipticity condition
\zeta^{-1}|\mathbf{y}|^{2}\leq\sigma(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{y}\cdot\mathbf{y}\leq\zeta|\mathbf{y}|^{2}
for some
\zeta>0
and all
\mathbf{y}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Then, the
volumetric torso potential

v:\Omega_{0}\times T\to\mathbb{R}
in some finite time interval
T=(0,\bar{t})
for
\bar{t}>0
with given
epicardial potential

u:\Gamma_{H}\times T\to\mathbb{R}
solves the following (forward) problem

\begin{cases}\begin{aligned} -\operatorname{div}\left(\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v(\mathbf{x},t)\right)&=0,&&\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)\in\Omega_{0}\times T,\\
\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot\mathbf{n}&=0,&&\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)\in\Gamma\times T,\\
v(\mathbf{x},t)&=u(\mathbf{x},t),&&\left(\mathbf{x},t\right)\in\Gamma_{H}\times T.\end{aligned}\end{cases}

(1)

The subsequent theorem ensures the existence, uniqueness, and regularity of solutions of this elliptic PDE system.

Theorem 1
.

There exists a unique solution
v\in H^{1}(T,H^{1}(\Omega_{0}))
of (
1
) for

Proof.

The proof relies on standard arguments from elliptic PDE theory, regularity theory, and the trace theorem; we omit further details and refer the reader to
[
18
,
33
]
.
∎

We remark that, under suitable regularity assumptions on the temporal part of the function,
the existence and uniqueness of a solution to the space-time problem is ensured by pointwise evaluation in time for the boundary function

Let

be the solution associated with

.
Then, we can define the continuous

which “transfers” the epicardial potential to the torso.
Note that the inverse of the forward operator is, in general, unbounded.

In this subsection, we introduce the severely ill-posed inverse problem of reconstructing the epicardial potential using measurements

on the body surface.
In detail, we assume the

z\in L^{2}(\Sigma\times T)
at the electrodes represented by
\Sigma\subset\Gamma
for

electrodes to be known, and no further information about the potential on the heart is available.
Henceforth, we assume that the electrodes do not collapse to a single point, reflected by a positive
(d-1)

For the inverse problem, we assume Dirichlet boundary conditions on the torso domain instead of Dirichlet boundary conditions on the heart as in (

Consequently, we aim to minimize the cost function

\argmin_{u\in H^{1}\left(\Gamma_{H}\times T\right)}\left\{G(u)\coloneqq\frac{1}{2|\Sigma|N_{\Sigma}}\int_{\Sigma\times T}\left(A[u](\mathbf{x},t)-z(\mathbf{x},t)\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)\right\},

(2)

where
|\Sigma|
is the constant area of the electrodes
\Sigma
.
Driven by the ill-posedness, we follow the common paradigm in inverse problem theory by adding a regularization term.
Various methods for finding the best solution to this problem have been proposed such as zero- or first-order Tikhonov regularization methods promoting sparsity or, more recently, machine learning-based techniques.
It is well-known that
L^{2}
-norm-based regularizers exhibit smoothing effects while preventing sharp transitions of solutions.
In a seminal paper, Rudin, Osher, and Fatemi
[
43
]
advocated the total variation regularizer for applications in imaging to overcome the aforementioned issues.
Promising studies about total variation-type regularization (in space only) for the inverse problem in ECGI have already been conducted in a BEM setting (see e.g.
[
20
,
45
,
56
]
).
Here, the normal derivative of the epicardial potential is penalized in the
L^{1}
-norm.
To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to introduce a finite-element total variation-type approach in both space and time for the inverse problem in ECGI.
We here advocate the spatiotemporal total variation-type regularizer with
L^{1}
- or
L^{2,1}
-norm given by

F^{\alpha}(u)=\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times T}\mathrm{TV}_{\epsilon}^{\alpha}\left(Ku(\mathbf{x},t)\right)\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)

(3)

\alpha\in\{1,2\}
for each of the norms respectively, and the weighted total variation operator
K\coloneqq\Lambda\nabla_{(\mathbf{x},t)}
.
The classical total variation is extended by an anisotropy term represented by a matrix
\Lambda=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{\gamma},\ldots,\lambda_{\gamma},\lambda_{t})\in\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)\times(d+1)}_{+}
, where
\lambda_{\gamma}
and
\lambda_{t}
represent the spatial and temporal penalization parameters, respectively.
We consider the subsequent half-quadratic penalization functions for
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d+1}
and fixed
\epsilon>0

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}_{\epsilon}^{1}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d+1}\widetilde{\mathrm{TV}}_{\epsilon}^{1}(x_{i}),\quad\text{with}\quad\widetilde{\mathrm{TV}}_{\epsilon}^{1}(x_{i})=\begin{cases}|x_{i}|,&|x_{i}|\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon},\\
\epsilon x_{i}^{2},&\text{else},\end{cases}\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\mathrm{TV}_{\epsilon}^{2}(\mathbf{x})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2},&\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}\leq\frac{1}{\epsilon},\\
\epsilon\left\lVert\mathbf{x}\right\rVert_{2}^{2},&\text{else}.\end{cases}

By considering
\mathrm{TV}_{\epsilon}^{\alpha}
we can analyze the function in classical Sobolev spaces rather than in the space of functions of bounded variation, which facilitates the analytical treatment of the problem.
Then, we can define the
regularized energy function
for
u\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)\subset H^{1}(T,H^{1/2}(\Gamma_{H}))
as
J^{\alpha}(u)=F^{\alpha}(u)+G(u)
.

Theorem 2
.

J^{\alpha}+T
for a Tikhonov regularizer
T(u)=\frac{\eta}{2}\int_{\Gamma_{H}\times T}u(x,t)^{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)
with
\eta>0
admits a unique minimizer in
H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)
for
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
.

Proof.

We employ the direct method in the calculus of variations.
Note that the additional Tikhonov regularizer
T
is solely required to ensure coerciveness and dropped in all further considerations.
Let
\{u_{n}\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\subset H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)
be a minimizing sequence, i.e.

J^{\alpha}(u_{1})\geq J^{\alpha}(u_{n})\to\inf_{u\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)}J^{\alpha}(u)
.
For every thoracic potential
z\in L^{2}(\Gamma\times\mathbb{T})
, the associated sequence
J^{\alpha}(u_{n})
is constrained from below by
0
and from above due to the boundedness of the forward operator
A
.
By the definition of
\mathrm{TV}_{\epsilon}^{\alpha}
we directly obtain that there exists
C>0
such that

J^{\alpha}(u_{1})\geq J^{\alpha}(u_{n})\geq C\left\lVert u_{n}\right\rVert^{2}_{H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)}

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
Since
H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)
is Hilbert, there exists a subsequence converging weakly to
u^{\ast}\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times\mathbb{T})
.
Due to the linearity of
A
and the convexity of
J
we can deduce that
J^{\alpha}
is sequentially lower semi-continuous, which implies
J^{\alpha}(u^{\ast})\leq\inf_{u\in H^{1}(\Gamma_{H}\times T)}J^{\alpha}(u)
.
Since
A
is injective,
G
, and consequently
J^{\alpha}
, is strictly convex.
Hence, the minimizer is unique.
∎

\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}

\mathcal{V}_{h}

The respective spatial and temporal mass matrices

\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{P}_{0}}

The corresponding block diagonal matrices for the number of timesteps and different quadrature rules are defined with the help of the Kronecker product
\otimes_{K}
for
\mathcal{V}_{h}
and the gradient spaces
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}

\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}=\mathbf{I}_{S+1}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}},\quad\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}}=\mathbf{I}_{S}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}},

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}=\mathbf{I}_{S+1}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{0}},\quad\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}=\mathbf{I}_{(d+1)\frac{N_{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}}{N_{\mathcal{Q}}}}\otimes_{K}\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{0}}.

Since
\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}
is a tridiagonal matrix, the temporal block mass matrices induce weights in block tridiagonal matrices for
\mathcal{P}_{1}
finite elements and block diagonal matrices for
\mathcal{P}_{0}

\displaystyle\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}=\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{P}_{0}}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{I}_{N_{\mathcal{V}}},\quad\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{P}_{0}}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{I}_{(d+1)\frac{N_{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}}{S}}.

We want to point out that all mass matrices are symmetric positive definite by construction since the Kronecker product of two symmetric positive definite matrices is symmetric and positive definite.
Furthermore, the spatial and temporal mass matrices in the same spaces commute by the
mixed-product property
since

(\mathbf{X}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{I}_{N})(\mathbf{I}_{M}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{Y})=(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{I}_{M})\otimes_{K}(\mathbf{I}_{N}\mathbf{Y})=(\mathbf{Y}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{I}_{M})(\mathbf{I}_{N}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{X})

for
(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}\times\mathbb{R}^{N\times N}
and
N,M\in\mathbb{N}
.
The corresponding lumped mass matrices of
\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{X}}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{X}}
for a set
\mathcal{X}
are computed by

\widetilde{\mathbf{M}}^{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}\left(\sum_{j}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{X}}_{i,j}\right)_{i},\quad\text{and}\quad\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\mathcal{X}}\coloneqq\mathrm{diag}\left(\sum_{j}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{X}}_{i,j}\right)_{i}.

For the quadrature rules in
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}
in the regularizer function
F^{1}
, we define the vectors of nodal values in time
\mathbf{d}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{S}
and space
\mathbf{m}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
for the first-order quadrature rule as follows:

\displaystyle d^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}_{s}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}|J_{j}^{s}|,\quad s\in\llbracket S\rrbracket_{0}\quad\text{with}\quad t_{s}\in J_{j}^{s}\quad\text{for}\quad J_{j}^{s}\in\mathcal{S}_{h},

\displaystyle m^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}}}|L_{l}^{i}|,\quad i\in\llbracket N_{\mathcal{V}}\rrbracket\quad\text{with}\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in L_{l}^{i}\quad\text{for}\quad\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}\in\boldsymbol{\Xi},L_{l}^{i}\in\mathcal{T}_{h},

with
N_{\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}}
representing the number of elements for which
\boldsymbol{\xi}_{i}
is part of.
The induced
L^{2}
-inner product of
\mathcal{V}_{h}
results in

\displaystyle(u_{h},v_{h})_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{H}^{h}\times T^{h}}u_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)v_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{v}.

The standard
L^{2}
scalar product for the space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}
is split into the spatial and temporal gradient parts with different weights

\displaystyle\left(p_{h},q_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{H}^{h}\times T^{h}}p_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot q_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{p}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbf{q}_{k}+\mathbf{p}_{d+1}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbf{q}_{d+1}

and for
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{2}
as

\displaystyle\left(p_{h},q_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{2}}

\displaystyle=\int_{\Gamma_{H}^{h}\times T^{h}}p_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\cdot q_{h}(\mathbf{x},t)\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)=\mathbf{p}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}\mathbf{q}.

3.2
Total Variation Operator

We introduce the discretized weighted total variation operator
K_{h}
as a splitting of the gradient
\nabla_{(\mathbf{x},t)}
in the spatial and the temporal part weighted by the matrix
\Lambda
.
The spatial part computes
d
-dimensional directional derivatives
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
on the heart surface incorporating a
\mathcal{P}_{1}
element basis (see
[
10
]
).
For each time interval, a temporal derivative is computed in a
\mathcal{P}_{1}
finite element basis.
The weighted total variation operator
K_{h}
is defined as
K_{h}u_{h}\coloneqq(\lambda_{\gamma}\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u_{h},\lambda_{t}\nabla_{t}u_{h})^{\top}
.
Since the finite element spaces are fixed for each weighted total variation operator, the matrix representations
\mathbf{K}^{\alpha}
of
K_{h}
for
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
applied on the vector of nodal values
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}(S+1)}
of
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
is related to the corresponding gradient space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\alpha}
.
The matrix representation
\mathbf{K}^{1}=(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{1},\ldots,\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{d+1})^{\top}

induced by the gradient space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}
is divided into space gradient matrices
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{k}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}(S+1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{Q}}(S+1)}
with
k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
and a time gradient matrix
\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{d+1}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}(S+1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}S}
via

(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{k}\mathbf{u})_{l,j}=\lambda_{\gamma}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}K^{k}_{l,j,i,s}u_{i,s}=\lambda_{\gamma}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\nabla_{x_{k}}\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x})|_{L_{l}}u_{i,j}

for
L_{l}\in\mathcal{T}_{h}
and
(l,j)\in\llbracket N_{\mathcal{Q}}\rrbracket\times\llbracket S\rrbracket_{0}
.
The time gradient matrix is defined as

(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{d+1}\mathbf{u})_{l,j}=\lambda_{t}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}K^{d+1}_{l,j,i,s}u_{i,s}=\lambda_{t}\sum_{s=0}^{S}\nabla_{t}\rho_{s}(t)|_{J_{j}}u_{l,j}

for
J_{j}\in\mathcal{S}_{h}
and
(l,j)\in\llbracket N_{\mathcal{V}}\rrbracket\times\llbracket S\rrbracket
.

By definition of the gradient space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{2}
, the weighted total variation operator
\mathbf{K}^{2}
computes the gradients and interpolates them elementwise with a matrix
\mathbf{P}
to the nodes of each element such that
\mathbf{K}^{2}\coloneqq\mathbf{P}\cdot\mathbf{K}^{1}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}(S+1)}\to\mathbb{R}^{(d+1)N_{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}}
.
The adjoint
K_{h}^{*}
of the weighted total variation operator
K_{h}
is implicitly defined by

\left(K_{h}u_{h},p_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\alpha}}=\left(u_{h},K_{h}^{*}p_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}\quad\text{with}\quad\left(u_{h},K_{h}^{*}p_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}=\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}(\mathbf{K}^{\alpha})^{*}\mathbf{p}

for
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
.
By definition of the scalar product in
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}
, we can compute

\left(K_{h}u_{h},p_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}}=\sum_{k=1}^{d}(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{k}\mathbf{u})^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbf{p}_{k}+(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{d+1}\mathbf{u})^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbf{p}_{d+1}.

Thus, the adjoint weighted total variation operator results in

(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{k})^{*}=(\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{k})^{\top}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}\quad\text{and}\quad(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{d+1})^{*}=(\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}(\hat{\mathbf{K}}^{d+1})^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{1},\mathcal{P}_{0}}.

Likewise, the adjoint of
\mathbf{K}^{2}
in the gradient space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{2}
satisfies

\left(K_{h}u_{h},p_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{2}}=(\mathbf{K}^{2}\mathbf{u})^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}\mathbf{q},

resulting in

(\mathbf{K}^{2})^{*}=(\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}(\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}})^{-1}(\mathbf{K}^{2})^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{Q}^{2}}.

3.3
Forward Operator

Next, we discretize the forward operator
A
in the finite element setting following
[
53
]
.
Since the epicardium is assumed to be rigid over time, we can compute the forward operator for fixed time
\tilde{t}\in T
, i.e.
u_{h}(\mathbf{x},\tilde{t})=u_{h}(\mathbf{x})
for all

in this section.
We reformulate the weak formulation of (
1
) by Green’s identity with a test function

defined on the

\Gamma^{h}

0=\int_{\Omega_{0}^{h}}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}v_{h}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}-\int_{\Gamma_{H}^{h}}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u_{h}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\mathbf{n}\psi_{h}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Then, we evaluate the weak formulation for all basis functions
\left(\varphi_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}\right)_{k=1}^{N_{\mathcal{X}}}
on
\mathcal{X}\in\left\{\Omega_{0},\Gamma,\Gamma_{H}\right\}
with
\varphi_{k}^{\Gamma_{H}}\coloneqq\varphi_{k}
and
N_{\Gamma_{H}}\coloneqq N_{\mathcal{V}}
on

\displaystyle\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\Omega_{0}}}v_{l}\int_{\Omega_{0}^{h}}\sigma\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{l}^{\Omega_{0}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}+\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\Gamma}}\left(v|_{\Gamma}\right)_{j}\int_{\Gamma^{h}}\sigma\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{j}^{\Gamma}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle-\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\Gamma_{H}}}u_{i}\int_{\Gamma_{H}^{h}}\sigma\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{i}^{\Gamma_{H}}\cdot\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{k}^{\mathcal{X}}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

Rewriting the integrals with stiffness matrices results in

\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Omega_{0}}&\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Gamma}\\
\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Omega_{0}}&\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Gamma}\\
\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{v}\\
\mathbf{v}|_{\Gamma}\\
\end{pmatrix}=-\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Gamma_{H}}\\
\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Gamma_{H}}\end{pmatrix}\mathbf{u}

with matrices

\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{X}\mathcal{Y}}=\left(\int_{\Omega_{0}^{h}}\sigma(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{i}^{\mathcal{X}}(\mathbf{x})\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}\varphi_{j}^{\mathcal{Y}}(\mathbf{x})\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}\right)_{1\leq i\leq N_{\mathcal{X}},1\leq j\leq N_{\mathcal{Y}}}

and
\mathcal{X},\mathcal{Y}\in\left\{\Omega_{0},\Gamma,\Gamma_{H}\right\}
.
We explicitly compute the forward operator by taking advantage of
\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Gamma_{H}}=0
and the invertibility of the stiffness matrix
\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Omega_{0}}
.
Thus,

\mathbf{v}|_{\Gamma}=\mathbf{A}^{\Gamma}\mathbf{u},\quad\text{with}\quad\mathbf{A}^{\Gamma}=\left(\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Gamma}-\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Omega_{0}}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Omega_{0}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Gamma}\right)^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Gamma\Omega_{0}}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Omega_{0}}^{-1}\mathbf{A}_{\Omega_{0}\Gamma_{H}}.

The forward operator needs to be restricted on the set

depicting the nodes on the torso where the potential is measured with electrodes such that

Thus, for

we obtain
\mathbf{v}|_{\Sigma}=\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma}\mathbf{u}
.
Finally, we construct the block diagonal matrix for all timesteps
\llbracket S\rrbracket_{0}
via

\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{I}_{S+1}\otimes_{K}\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma}.

4
Primal-Dual Optimization Algorithm

This section presents details on the primal-dual algorithms to solve the inverse problem in a finite element setting.

4.1
Problem Formulation

In what follows, we consider the subsequent convex finite element-based variational problem

\min_{u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\frac{1}{2|\Sigma^{h}|N_{\Sigma}}\int_{\Sigma^{h}\times T^{h}}\left(A[u_{h}]-z_{h}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)+\int_{\Gamma_{H}^{h}\times T^{h}}\mathrm{TV}_{\epsilon}^{\alpha}\left(K_{h}u_{h}\right)\mathrm{d}(\mathbf{x},t)

for
\alpha\in\{1,2\}
.
Furthermore, we define the discretized functions of (
2
) and (
3
) as
G_{h}
and
F_{h}^{\alpha}
, respectively, which are proper, convex, and lower semi-continuous.
Exploiting the properties of the convex conjugate
[
8
]
, we transform this problem into a saddle point via

\min_{u\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\max_{p_{h}\in\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\alpha}}G_{h}(u_{h})+\left(K_{h}u_{h},p_{h}\right)_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\alpha}}-(F_{h}^{\alpha})^{*}(p_{h}),

(4)

with
(F_{h}^{\alpha})^{*}
being the convex conjugate of
F_{h}^{\alpha}
, which is proper, convex, and lower semi-continuous.
Since these functions can be fixed to a finite element discretization and we only optimize the real-valued vectors of nodal and element values, we define
\mathbf{G}_{h}
and
\mathbf{F}^{\alpha}_{h}
as representations of
G_{h}
and
F_{h}^{\alpha}
such that

\mathbf{G}_{h}(\mathbf{u})\coloneqq G_{h}(u_{h})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{F}_{h}^{\alpha}(\mathbf{p})\coloneqq F_{h}^{\alpha}(p_{h}).

(5)

Next, we compute the matrix-vector representation of the energy function.
All computations for the total variation-based methods are computed using lumped mass matrices in space and time to improve computational time.
As a result, lumped mass matrices are applied in the computations in this section to simplify the problem at hand.
On the discretized set
\Sigma^{h}
, we assume the spatial weights to vanish since the size of the electrodes used to measure the BSPM can be chosen arbitrarily small.
Therefore, the function
\mathbf{G}_{h}
can be expressed using matrix-vector notation as a time series without spatial weights

\mathbf{G}_{h}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{1}{2N_{\Sigma}}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z}\right)^{\top}\widetilde{\mathbf{D}}^{\Sigma}\left(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{z}\right).

In the numerical computation of the regularizer, we assume
0<\epsilon\ll 1
such that only the non-quadratic total variation regularization occurs to facilitate the presentation.
By assuming the boundedness of all involved functions and utilizing compactness arguments, it can be shown that there exists a mesh-dependent constant
\epsilon_{0}
such that, for all
\epsilon<\epsilon_{0}
, only the non-quadratic components of the regularization are active.

For the gradient space
\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{1}
, we compute the regularizer
\mathbf{F}_{h}^{1}(\mathbf{p})
by splitting the
L^{1}
norm into the sum over separate integrals
\mathbf{F}_{h,k}^{1}(\mathbf{p})
and
\mathbf{F}_{h,d+1}^{1}(\mathbf{p})
for each spatial gradient dimension
k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket
and the temporal gradient dimension, i.e.

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{h}^{1}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{F}_{h,k}^{1}(\mathbf{p})+\mathbf{F}_{h,d+1}^{1}(\mathbf{p})\quad\text{with}\quad

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{h,k}^{1}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{s=0}^{S}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\mathcal{Q}}}d^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}_{s}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{0}}_{l,l}|p_{k,l,s}|,\quad k\in\llbracket d\rrbracket,

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{h,d+1}^{1}(\mathbf{p})

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=1}^{S}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}\mathbf{D}^{0}_{j,j}m_{i}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}|p_{d+1,i,j}|

Next, we compute the regularizer
F_{h}^{2}

\displaystyle\mathbf{F}_{h}^{2}(\mathbf{p})=\sum_{j=1}^{S}\sum_{l=1}^{N_{\mathcal{Q}}}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{s=1}^{2}\frac{\mathbf{D}^{0}_{j,j}}{2}\frac{\mathbf{M}^{0}_{l,l}}{d}\left\lVert p_{i,l,s,j}\right\rVert_{2}

with the
\ell^{2}
norm over the gradient dimension
\left\lVert p_{i,l,s,j}\right\rVert_{2}=\sqrt{\sum_{k=1}^{d+1}p_{k,i,l,s,j}^{2}}
.
For optimization of the saddle point problem (
4

[

This method iteratively updates primal and dual variables according to the proximal mappings of

and

(\mathbf{F}_{h}^{2})^{*}
is also computed vector element-wise as

\left(\prox_{\sigma(\mathbf{F}_{h}^{2})^{*}}(\widetilde{\mathbf{p}})\right)_{k,i,l,s,j}=\widetilde{p}_{k,i,l,s,j}/\max\left(1,\left\lVert\widetilde{p}_{i,l,s,j}\right\rVert_{2}\right)

for
(k,i,l,s,j)\in\llbracket d+1\rrbracket\times\llbracket d\rrbracket\times\llbracket N_{\mathcal{Q}}\rrbracket\times\llbracket 2\rrbracket\times\llbracket S\rrbracket
.
The primal-dual algorithm
1
requires the Lipschitz constant of the weighted total variation operator
K_{h}
as it is essential for the convergence

\left\lVert K_{h}\right\rVert^{2}_{\left(\mathcal{V}_{h},\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\alpha}\right)}=\sup_{u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}\frac{\left\lVert K_{h}u_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{Q}_{h}^{\alpha}}^{2}}{\left\lVert u_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}^{2}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}((\mathbf{K}^{\alpha})^{*}\mathbf{K}^{\alpha}).

5
Numerical Results

This section introduces different state-of-the-art finite element regularization methods for solving the inverse problem.
Moreover, we describe the simulation of electrical activity on the epicardium, which serves as the ground truth function, and visualize the two- and three-dimensional reconstruction results for both the baseline methods and our novel total variation-based approach in the presence of noise.

5.1
Baseline Methods

The most common regularization approach applied in the inverse problem in electrocardiographic imaging is Tikhonov regularization
[
14
,
49
]
.
For the energy minimization problem incorporating Tikhonov regularization, we adopt the following model

\min_{u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}}G_{h}(u_{h})+\frac{\lambda_{\gamma}}{2}\left\lVert L_{h}u_{h}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}_{h}}^{2}

(6)

with
L_{h}
being the identity
I_{h}
for zero-order Tikhonov (
\mathbf{T0}
) or the spatial gradient
\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}
for first-order Tikhonov in space (
\mathbf{T1\;S}
).
Since the regularizer function of Tikhonov is differentiable, we compute the explicit solution for
\mathbf{T0}
and
\mathbf{T1\;S}
by rearranging the optimality condition of (
6
).
For zero-order Tikhonov with
L_{h}=I_{h}
differentiating with respect to
\mathbf{u}
results in

0=\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{A}^{\top}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{z})+\lambda_{\gamma}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{u}.

The weights of the mass matrices
\mathbf{D}^{\Sigma}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}
vanish after solving for
\mathbf{u}
since
\mathbf{D}^{\Sigma}\mathbf{A}=\mathbf{A}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}
and
\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}=\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}\mathbf{D}^{\mathcal{V}}
.
Due to the remaining matrices
\mathbf{A}
and
\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{V}}
being timestep-wise uniform, we can solve the linear system independently at each timestep

\mathbf{u}(t_{s})=(\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma}+\lambda_{\gamma}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}})^{-1}\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{z}(t_{s})

for each
t_{s}
with
\mathbf{u}(t_{s})\coloneqq(u_{1,s},\ldots,u_{N_{\mathcal{V}},s})^{\top}
and
s\in\llbracket S\rrbracket_{0}
.
Following the same steps for
\mathbf{T1\;S}
, we compute

\mathbf{u}(t_{s})=(\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma}-\lambda_{\gamma}\Delta_{\mathbf{x},h})^{-1}\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{z}(t_{s}),

with the discretized spatial Laplacian
\Delta_{\mathbf{x},h}
.
As introduced in
[
37
]
for BEM methods, we propose a method with a first-order Tikhonov term and additionally compare the reconstruction with an estimate of the epicardial potential
v_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
which is chosen as the potential of the previous timestep.
Since we need to solve this regularization iteratively, the optimization problem norms are solely spatial.
Let us define
\mathcal{V}_{h}^{t_{s}}
as the space of all functions
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
evaluated at time
t_{s}
.
With
\mathbf{u}(t_{-1})=(0,\ldots,0)^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}}
ensuring Dirichlet boundary conditions, the first-order Tikhonov method with additional time regularization (
\mathbf{T1\;S+T}
) is determined by minimizing for
u_{h}(t_{s})\in\mathcal{V}_{h}^{t_{s}}
time stepwise

\min_{u_{h}(t_{s})}\frac{1}{2N_{\Sigma}}\left\lVert(A[u_{h}]-z_{h})(t_{s})\right\rVert_{L^{2}(\Sigma^{h})}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{\gamma}}{2}\left\lVert\nabla_{\mathbf{x}}u_{h}(t_{s})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}_{h}^{t_{s}}}^{2}+\frac{\lambda_{t}}{2}\left\lVert u_{h}(t_{s})-u_{h}(t_{s-1})\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}_{h}^{t_{s}}}^{2}

for all
s\in\llbracket S\rrbracket_{0}
.
Solving the optimality condition for each timestep results in computing

\mathbf{u}(t_{s})=(\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma}-\lambda_{\gamma}\Delta_{\mathbf{x},h}+\lambda_{t}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}})^{-1}(\frac{1}{N_{\Sigma}}(\mathbf{A}^{\Sigma})^{\top}\mathbf{z}(t_{s})+\lambda_{t}\mathbf{M}^{\mathcal{P}_{1}}\mathbf{u}(t_{s-1})).

5.3
Results

The novel space-time total variation-based methods for the regularizer
F_{h}^{\alpha}
are subsequently defined as
\mathbf{TV\;ST}^{\alpha}
.
To highlight the benefit of time regularization, we also compare all results to the spatial total variation
\mathbf{TV\;S}^{\alpha}
induced by the regularizer
F_{h}^{\alpha}
with
\lambda_{t}=0
.
All total variation-based methods are computed with lumped mass matrices in contrast to the baseline methods.
To compare the results obtained by the different approaches, we define the frequently used
relative error
(RE) and
Pearson’s correlation coefficient
(CC) evaluating the vector of nodal values
\mathbf{u}
of a discretized finite element function
u_{h}\in\mathcal{V}_{h}
, as well as the
\mathcal{V}_{h}
error
for discretized functions taking the geometry of the problem in consideration:

\text{RE}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}-\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{g}}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{g}}\right\rVert_{2}},\quad\text{CC}(\mathbf{u})=\frac{(\mathbf{u}-\overline{\mathbf{u}},\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{g}}-\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{g}})_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{u}-\overline{\mathbf{u}}\right\rVert_{2}\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{g}}-\overline{\mathbf{u}}^{\mathrm{g}}\right\rVert_{2}},\quad\mathcal{V}_{h}^{\mathrm{err}}(u_{h})=\left\lVert u_{h}-u_{h}^{\mathrm{g}}\right\rVert_{\mathcal{V}_{h}},

where
\mathbf{u}^{\mathrm{g}}
represents the vector of nodal values for the ground truth function
u_{h}^{\mathrm{g}}
, and
\overline{\mathbf{u}}
is defined as the mean of
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{\mathcal{V}}(S+1)}
.
The relative error gain of our method, denoted as
\epsilon^{\text{our}}
, compared to the baseline method, denoted as
\epsilon^{\text{base}}
, is calculated using the formula
\frac{\lvert\epsilon^{\text{our}}-\epsilon^{\text{base}}\rvert}{\epsilon^{\text{base}}}
.
The introduced baseline and total variation-based methods are evaluated in two- and three-dimensional applications.
We remark that solving the inverse problem is performed using the same discretization as in the simulation of the ground truth, which may lead to more optimistic results compared to real-world scenarios.
All computations are performed using CuPy
[
35
]
and SciPy
[
51
]
on an NVIDIA A40 GPU and an AMD EPYC 7543 CPU.
All computations are performed in double precision.
The algorithm is terminated if the
l^{\infty}
-norm difference of two consecutive primal iterates, i.e.,
\left\lVert\mathbf{u}^{n+1}-\mathbf{u}^{n}\right\rVert_{\infty}
, is below the threshold value
10^{-3}
.
A logarithmic grid search minimizing the
\mathcal{V}_{h}
error has been conducted for all methods to determine the optimal hyperparameter values in the range between
10^{-15}
and
1
.
Although the total variation-based methods allow for different spatial and temporal regularization penalization schemes, the applications discussed in this paper induce equal space and time weighting in a logarithmic grid search for the unrelated physical dimensions, implying correlation.

Computing the reconstruction of the simulated body surface potential without noise perturbations induces relatively small optimal regularization parameters for all approaches.
To investigate the robustness and ensure numerical stability, we compute reconstructions with component-wise additive white Gaussian noise
\mathbf{n}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}_{\mathbf{n}})
on the vector of nodal values of the body surface measurements
\mathbf{z}
.
The noise is applied for different
Signal to Noise Ratio
levels in
{\mathrm{dB}}
as it is common in literature
[
16
]
.
For the simulated body surface potential
z_{h}^{\mathrm{g}}\coloneqq A[u_{h}^{g}]
with vector of nodal values
\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{g}}
, we compute the noisy measurement function by
\mathbf{z}=\mathbf{z}^{\mathrm{g}}+\mathbf{n}
with noise level

\mathrm{SNR}_{\mathrm{dB}}=20\log_{10}\frac{\left\lVert\mathbf{z}\right\rVert_{2}}{\left\lVert\mathbf{n}\right\rVert_{2}}.

6
Conclusions

This work introduced novel total variation-based regularization methods in a finite element setting to reconstruct the epicardial potential from body surface measurements.
After stating the mathematical model of the spatiotemporal inverse problem in ECGI, we introduced different discretizations to improve total variation-based regularization.
The resulting methods enhance reconstructions compared to state-of-the-art Tikhonov regularization since the regularizer captures the characteristics of the heart potential function more effectively.
We tested our approaches in a two- and three-dimensional database with simulated epicardial potential and showed their benefits of joint spatiotemporal regularization.

The non-linear optimization problem for total variation-based regularization is computationally more demanding due to the non-differentiability of the regularizer near zero and the high-dimensional dual variable in the iterative minimization algorithm proposed in this paper.
However, the improvements in accuracy achieved by total variation-based reconstruction and dependence on the resolution of the problem justify the computational complexity.

Still, the clinical application demands much more improvement regarding reconstructing the inverse problem in electrocardiographic imaging.
In conclusion, the spatiotemporal total variation-based approach is limited by the computational complexity of high-dimensional datasets.
Future work will further improve the regularization of the inverse problem and focus on real human heart geometry and non-synthetic models.
