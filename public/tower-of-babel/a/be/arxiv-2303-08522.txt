Quiver moduli spaces of a given dimension Thanks: Partially supported by NKFIH K 138828 and K 132002.
Mátyás Domokos

Abstract
. It is shown that certain transformations on quiver-dimension vector pairs induce isomorphisms on the corresponding moduli spaces of quiver representations and map a stable dimension vector to a stable dimension vector. This result combined with a combinatorial analysis of dimension vectors in the fundamental set of a wild quiver is applied to prove that in each dimension there are only finitely many projective algebraic varieties occurring as a moduli space of representations of a quiver with a dimension vector that satisfies some simple constraints.

Primary 14L24; Secondary 13A50, 16G20

1.
Introduction

The moduli spaces introduced by King
[
29
]
provide a link between
algebraic geometry and representation theory, by which quiver representations are
involved in several areas of mathematics. Quiver Grassmannians give another construction associating projective algebraic varieties to quiver representations.
Every projective algebraic variety can be realized as a
fine moduli space of representations of a quiver with relations (see
[
24
]
,

[
26
]
) and also as a quiver Grassmannian
(see
[
27
]
,
[
33
]
).
As it is remarked in
[
34
]
, these results can be traced back to
[
4
]
,
and have several variants and refinements, see

[
8
]
,
[
10
]
,
[
13
]
,
[
25
]
,

[
34
]
,
[
35
]
,
[
36
]
for a sample.

On the other hand, the class of moduli spaces of representations of quivers (with no relations)
is more exclusive in this respect. For example, as it is observed in

[
22
, Page 4]
, the only
1
-dimensional projective variety that occurs as a moduli space of representations of some quiver is the projective line
\mathbb{P}^{1}
.
Developing further some earlier works in
[
2
]
and
[
3
]
, it was pointed out in
[
17
]
that in each dimension there are only finitely many toric varieties that can be realized as the moduli space of thin representations of quivers, and a procedure for their classification is outlined.
Here ”thin” means that the dimension vector is constant
1
, in which case the resulting moduli spaces have a canonical structure of a toric variety.
Note also that the number of isomorphism classes of algebraic varieties that occur as a moduli space of representations of a quiver is countable. Indeed, the number of
possible quiver-dimension vector pairs is countable, and for a fixed quiver and a fixed dimension vector there are finitely many GIT-equivalence classes of weights, hence there are finitely many possible moduli spaces.

The present paper is motivated by the following questions:
how many
d
-dimensional projective algebraic varieties occur as
a moduli space of representations of a quiver for a given
d
, and how large quivers and dimension vectors are needed to realize them?

Recall that the moduli spaces
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,\theta)
defined in
[
29
]
are quasi-projective varieties depending on a quiver
Q
, a dimension vector
\alpha
, and a weight
\theta
. Under certain conditions on
(Q,\alpha)
, we shall define

(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
and a weight
\theta^{\prime}
such that

\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,\theta)\cong\mathcal{M}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime},\theta^{\prime})
; moreover, if
\alpha
is
\theta
-stable, then
\alpha^{\prime}
is
\theta^{\prime}
-stable (see Theorem
2.5
).
In good circumstances, with an iterated use of the above transformations we can get to
a quiver-dimension vector pair that is ”smaller” in an appropriate sense than
(Q,\alpha)

(see Corollary
2.6
, Corollary
2.7
).

These reduction steps are higher dimensional analogues of the transformations
from
[
17
]
that were sufficient to prove that in each dimension there are only finitely many toric varieties that can be realized as a moduli space of thin representations of quivers. The reduction steps in
[
17
]
were proved by establishing integral-affine equivalences between lattice polytopes. The higher dimensional analogues in the present paper are proved with the aid of
classical invariant theory (and in the case of the result about stability, some basic facts on the geometry of representation spaces of quivers).

This technique (combined with a combinatorial study of dimension vectors in the fundamental set, see Theorem
2.11
) suffices to prove
Theorem
2.10
, asserting that in each dimension only finitely many algebraic varieties can be realized as a moduli space of the form

\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,\theta)
, where
\alpha
is a
\theta
-stable dimension vector in the fundamental set for
Q
. Note that to classify moduli spaces of quiver representations it is sufficient to deal with stable dimension vectors (see Proposition
2.8
).
A
\theta
-stable dimension vector is necessarily a root.
Non-trivial moduli spaces can occur only for a non-real root, and every non-real root belongs to the Weyl group orbit of a root in the fundamental set (see
[
28
]
).
Therefore it seems natural to focus first on the dimension vectors in the fundamental set,
as we do in Theorem
2.10
.

As an application of this result, we show in Proposition
2.12
that under certain restrictions on the dimension vector all moduli spaces are isomorphic to products of the moduli spaces covered by Theorem
2.10
.
The assumption on the dimension vector here is very restrictive, but still this
gives Corollary
2.13
, which is
a direct generalization of the corresponding statement from
[
17
]
on the toric case.

In Section
2
we recall notation and terminology related to quivers, their representation spaces and the moduli spaces of quiver representations.
Our main results are stated in Section
2.5
, and the steps in their proofs are outlined. In Section
3
we develop our reduction steps that provide isomorphisms between algebras of relative invariants of different quiver-dimension vector pairs. In Section
4
we show that these reduction steps are compatible with stability.
In Section
5
we study dimension vectors in the fundamental set of a wild quiver.
The results proved in Section
3
, Section
4
, and
Section
5
have some independent interest, as they may be
useful in further studies of moduli spaces of representations of quivers.
We use them in Section
6
and Section
8

to prove our finiteness statements (Theorem
2.10
,
Corollary
2.13
).
For the latter we need to deduce from the literature
in Section
7
the proof
of Proposition
2.8
on the product decomposition of
a moduli space of quiver representations coming from the
\theta
-stable decomposition.
In Section
9
we provide an example
indicating that in order to prove that there are finitely many moduli spaces of quiver representations in each dimension, one probably needs to develop further techniques.
Finally, in Section
10
we comment on the affine counterpart of these moduli spaces, the varieties parameterizing semisimple representations of quivers.

2.
Preliminaries and main statements

2.1.
Quivers and the associated quadratic forms

A
quiver

Q
is a finite directed graph with (non-empty) vertex set
Q_{0}
and arrow set

Q_{1}
; for an arrow
a\in Q_{1}
denote by
\mathrm{s}a\in Q_{0}
its source and by
\mathrm{t}a\in Q_{0}
its target.
A
path
in
Q
is a sequence
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}
of arows such that
\mathrm{t}a_{i}=\mathrm{s}a_{i+1}

for
i=1,\dots,n-1
. A quiver
Q
is
acyclic
if there is no path in
Q
of positive length starting and ending at the same vertex.
By the
underlying graph
of
Q
we mean the graph (possibly with multiple edges) obtained by forgetting the orientation of the arrows,
and we shall keep the symbol
Q
to denote it. So the vertex set of this graph is

Q_{0}
and the edge set of this graph is
Q_{1}
, where we forget the orientation of
a\in Q_{1}
when we consider it as an edge of
Q
.
We say that the quiver
Q
is
connected
if its underlying graph
is connected. The
degree
of a vertex
v
of
Q
is

\deg_{Q}(v)=|\{a\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{s}a=v\}|+|\{a\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{t}a=v\}|.

A
dimension vector
is an element
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
(interpreted as a map from
Q_{0}
into the set
\mathbb{N}
of non-negative integers).
Given a dimension vector
\alpha
write
\mathrm{supp}(\alpha)
for the full subquiver of
Q
spanned by the vertices
v\in Q_{0}
with
\alpha(v)\neq 0
.
A dimension vector
\alpha
(or the pair
(Q,\alpha)
) is said to be
sincere
if
\alpha(v)>0
for all

v\in Q_{0}
(i.e.
\mathrm{supp}(\alpha)=Q
).
For
v\in Q_{0}
we shall denote by
\varepsilon_{v}\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
the dimension vector with

\varepsilon_{v}(w)=\begin{cases}1\text{ for }w=v\\
0\text{ for }w\neq v.\end{cases}

The
Ringel bilinear form
on
\mathbb{R}^{Q_{0}}
is given by

\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{Q}:=\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\alpha(v)\beta(v)-\sum_{a\in Q_{1}}\alpha(\mathrm{s}a)\beta(\mathrm{t}a)\ \text{ for }\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{R}^{Q_{0}}.

The specialization
\alpha=\beta
in the above formula gives the
Tits quadratic form

\alpha\mapsto\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q},\quad\mathbb{R}^{Q_{0}}\to\mathbb{R};

it depends only on the underlying graph of the quiver
Q
, and does not depend on the orientation.
Polarizing the Tits form we get the symmetric bilinear form

\displaystyle(\alpha,\beta)_{Q}

\displaystyle:=\langle\alpha+\beta,\alpha+\beta\rangle_{Q}-\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}-\langle\beta,\beta\rangle_{Q}=\langle\alpha,\beta\rangle_{Q}+\langle\beta,\alpha\rangle_{Q}

\displaystyle=2\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\alpha(v)\beta(v)-\sum_{a\in Q_{1}}(\alpha(\mathrm{s}a)\beta(\mathrm{t}a)+\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)\beta(\mathrm{s}a)).

We call it the
Cartan form
associated with
Q
,
because the matrix
C_{Q}
of this bilinear form with respect to the basis

\{\varepsilon_{v}\mid v\in Q_{0}\}
in
\mathbb{R}^{Q_{0}}
is the so-called

Cartan matrix
corresponding to
Q
(cf.
[
28
]
).

In this paper by
(extended) Dynkin graph
we mean a (extended) Dynkin graph of ADE type (listed for example in
[
30
, page 113]
).
A quiver is called (extended) Dynkin if its underlying graph is (extended) Dynkin.
Following
[
30
]
we call

F_{Q}:=\{\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}\setminus\{\underline{0}\}\mid\forall v\in Q_{0}:(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\leq 0,\ \mathrm{supp}(\alpha)\text{ is connected}\}

the
fundamental set
for
Q
(it depends only on the underlying graph of
Q
).
Note that
F_{Q}
is empty for a Dynkin graph
Q
,

F_{Q}
consists of the positive integer multiples of a non-zero dimension vector
\delta
satisfying
(\delta,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}=0
for all
v\in Q_{0}
for an extended Dynkin graph
Q
.
If
Q
is a connected quiver which is neither Dynkin nor extended
Dynkin (i.e.
Q
is a connected
wild quiver
), then
F_{Q}
contains an element
\alpha
such that
(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})<0
for some
v\in Q_{0}

by
[
40
, Theorem 3 and Lemma 13]

(see also
[
28
, Lemma 1.2]
or
[
30
, Lemma 3.1.1]
).

For dimension vectors
\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
we write

\beta\leq\alpha
if
\beta(v)\leq\alpha(v)
for all
v\in Q_{0}
, and set

|\alpha|:=\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\alpha(v)
.

2.2.
Quiver representations

Throughout this paper we fix an algebraically closed base field
\mathbb{F}
of arbitrary characteristic.
Recall that a
representation

R
of
Q
consists of a set of distinct
\mathbb{F}
-vector spaces

\{R_{v}\mid v\in Q_{0}\}
and an assignment
a\mapsto R_{a}

(a\in Q_{1})
, where

R_{a}
is an
\mathbb{F}
-linear map from
R_{\mathrm{s}a}
to
R_{\mathrm{t}a}
.
We call
\underline{\dim}(R):=(\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(R_{v})\mid v\in Q_{0})\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
the
dimension vector of

R
.
A representation
S
of
Q
is a
subrepresentation of

R
if
S_{v}
is a subspace of
R_{v}
for each
v\in Q_{0}
,

R_{a}
maps
S_{\mathrm{s}a}
into
S_{\mathrm{t}a}
for all
a\in Q_{1}
,
and
S_{a}
is the restriction of
R_{a}
to
S_{\mathrm{s}a}
.

By a
weight for

Q
we mean an integer valued function

\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
on the set of vertices of
Q
.
Following
[
29
]
, a representation
R
of
Q
is said to be

\theta
-semistable
for some weight
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
if
\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\theta(v)\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(R_{v})=0
and

\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\theta(v)\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{v})\geq 0
for all subrepresentations
S
of
R
.
A non-zero
\theta
-semistable representation
R
of
Q
is
\theta
-stable
if for any non-zero proper subrepresentation
S
of
R
we have
\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\theta(v)\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{v})>0
. The dimension vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
is
\theta
-semistable

(respectively
\theta
-stable
) if there exists a
\theta
-semistable (respectively

\theta
-stable) representation of
Q
of dimension vector
\alpha
.

The
space of representations
of the quiver
Q
with dimension vector
\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
is

\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha):=\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1}}\mathrm{Hom}_{\mathbb{F}}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(\mathrm{s}a)},\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)}).

This is a non-zero
\mathbb{F}
-vector space provided that
\mathrm{supp}(\alpha)
has at least one arrow.
The
base change group

\mathrm{GL}(\alpha):=\prod_{v\in Q_{0}}\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(v)})
acts linearly on
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
: for

g=(g_{v}\mid v\in Q_{0})
and
x=(x_{a}\mid a\in Q_{1})
we have

(g\cdot x)_{a}=g_{\mathrm{t}a}\circ x_{a}\circ g_{\mathrm{s}a}^{-1}.

Identify
x=(x_{a}\mid a\in Q_{1})\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
with the representation of
Q
which assigns a copy of
\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(v)}
with
v\in Q_{0}

and the linear map
x_{a}
from

x_{\mathrm{s}a}=\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(\mathrm{s}a)}\to x_{\mathrm{t}a}=\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)}
with
a\in Q_{1}
.
The points
x
and
y
in
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
belong to the same
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
-orbit if and only if the corresponding representations are isomorphic.

We shall denote by
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}
the set of those
x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
for which the corresponding representation
x
is
\theta
-semistable, and
denote by
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-st}}}
the set of those
x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
for which the corresponding representation
x
is
\theta
-stable.
Clearly these are
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
-invariant subsets of
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
.

The representation space
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
is naturally identified with

\mathcal{R}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
, where
Q^{\prime}=\mathrm{supp}(\alpha)
, and

\alpha^{\prime}=\alpha|_{Q^{\prime}_{0}}
. For a weight
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
denote by
\theta^{\prime}
the restriction of
\theta

to
Q^{\prime}_{0}
.
Then
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}
is identified with

\mathcal{R}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})^{\theta^{\prime}\tiny{\text{-sst}}}
and

\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-st}}}
is identified with

\mathcal{R}(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})^{\theta^{\prime}\tiny{\text{-st}}}
.
In particular,
\alpha
is
\theta
-semistable
(
\theta
-stable) if and only if
\alpha^{\prime}
is
\theta^{\prime}
-semistable
(
\theta^{\prime}
-stable). Thus frequently in our discussions it will cause no loss of generality
to assume that we are dealing with a sincere dimension vector for our quiver.
Moreover, in the sequel we shall identify quiver-dimension vector pairs that are isomorphic.
Here we say that
(Q,\alpha)

is isomorphic to

(Q^{\prime},\alpha^{\prime})
if there exists a bijection

\pi:Q_{0}\to Q^{\prime}_{0}
such that for all
(v,w)\in Q_{0}\times Q_{0}
we have

|\{a\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{s}a=v,\ \mathrm{t}a=w\}|=|\{a^{\prime}\in Q^{\prime}_{1}\mid\mathrm{s}a^{\prime}=\pi(v),\ \mathrm{t}a^{\prime}=\pi(w)\}|
and
\alpha^{\prime}(\pi(v))=\alpha(v)
.

2.3.
Semi-invariants and moduli spaces of quiver representations

Throughout this section
\alpha
is a sincere dimension vector for a quiver
Q
.
We associate with a weight
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
the character

\chi_{\theta}:\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)\to\mathbb{F}^{\times},\quad\chi_{\theta}(g)=\prod_{v\in Q_{0}}\det(g_{v})^{\theta(v)}

(as
\alpha
is sincere, the map
\theta\mapsto\chi_{\theta}
from
\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}

onto the character group of
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
is injective).
Denote by
\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]
the coordinate ring of
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
; this is a polynomial algebra in
\sum_{a\in Q_{1}}\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)\cdot\alpha(\mathrm{s}a)
variables.
The
space of relative invariants of weight
\theta
on
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
is

\displaystyle\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\alpha),\theta}:=\{p\in\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]\mid\forall g\in\mathrm{GL}(\alpha),\forall x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha):

\displaystyle p(g\cdot x)=\chi_{\theta}(g)p(x)\}.

This is contained in the algebra

\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\alpha)}
of
semi-invariants
of
Q
consisting of the polynomial functions
p
satisfying
p(g\cdot x)=p(x)
for all
g\in\mathrm{SL}(\alpha)
and

x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
, where
\mathrm{SL}(\alpha)=\prod_{v\in Q_{0}}\mathrm{SL}(\alpha(v))
is the commutator subgroup of
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
.
Note that spanning sets of
\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\alpha)}
were determined in

[
14
]
,
[
20
]
,
[
38
]
.
We set

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)_{n}=\begin{cases}\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\alpha),n\theta}&\text{ if }\theta\neq 0\text{ or }n=0\\
0&\text{ if }\theta=0\text{ and }n>0.\end{cases}

The algebra
\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\alpha)}
contains the subalgebra

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta):=\bigoplus_{n=0}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)_{n}.

We shall treat it as a graded algebra, with
\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)_{n}
as its degree
n
homogeneous component.
Note that

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)_{0}=\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,0)=\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)},

the algebra of polynomial
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
-invariants on
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
.
Its generators were described in
[
31
]
in characteristic zero and in
[
21
]
in arbitrary characteristic.

Mumford’s geometric invariant theory (GIT for short) was applied in
[
29
, Proposition 3.1]
to prove that

\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}
is the complement in
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
of the common zero locus of
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)_{n}
for a non-zero weight
\theta
(in particular, if the dimension vector
\alpha
is
\theta
-semistable, then there is a non-zero relative invariant on
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
whose weight is a positive integer multiple of
\theta
),
and
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{0\tiny{\text{-sst}}}=\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
. It is therefore a
Zariski open dense

\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
-invariant subset of
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
when
\alpha
is
\theta
-semistable, and there is a GIT-quotient

\pi:\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}\twoheadrightarrow\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}/\negmedspace/\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
.
Here
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,\theta):=\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}/\negmedspace/\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
is the quasi-projective variety obtained as the
projective spectrum of the graded algebra

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)
, and is called the
moduli space of
\theta
-semistable representations
of
Q
with dimension vector
\alpha
.
It is a coarse moduli space for families of
\theta
-semistable
\alpha
-dimensional representations of
Q
up to S-equivalence (cf.
[
29
, Proposition 5.2]
,
and see
[
32
]
for an introduction to GIT).
This means in particular that
x
and
y
in
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-sst}}}

belong to the same fiber of

\pi
if and only if the representations
x
and
y
have the same
composition factors in the abelian category of
\theta
-semistable
representations of
Q
(whose simple objects are the
\theta
-stable representations).
When
\alpha
is
\theta
-stable,
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-st}}}
is a
Zariski dense
open subset of
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
, and for
x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)^{\theta\tiny{\text{-st}}}
,
the fiber
\pi^{-1}(\pi(x))
is the

\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
-orbit of
x
. The stabilizer of
x
consists only of the scalar multiples of the identity element of
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
(by Schur’s Lemma).
It follows that whenever
\alpha
is
\theta
-stable, we have

(1)

\dim\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,\theta)=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)-\dim\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)+1=1-\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}.

Note also that when
Q
is acyclic, then
\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)_{0}=\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)}=\mathbb{F}
,
hence
\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,\theta)
is a projective algebraic variety over
\mathbb{F}
.

2.4.
The reductions
\tau
and
\sigma

Definition 2.1
.

Let
Q
be a quiver,
\alpha
a sincere dimension vector for
Q
, and
u\in Q_{0}
a vertex of
Q
such that
\deg_{Q}(u)>0
and
Q
has no loop at
u
(i.e. there is no arrow
a\in Q_{1}
with
\mathrm{s}a=u=\mathrm{t}a
).

(i)

We say that
u
is
large for
(Q,\alpha)
if

\alpha(u)\geq\max\{\sum_{b\in Q_{1}\colon\mathrm{t}b=u}\alpha(\mathrm{s}b),\ \sum_{c\in Q_{1}\colon\mathrm{s}c=u}\alpha(\mathrm{t}c)\}.

(ii)

Assume that
u
is large for
(Q,\alpha)
.
Then
denote by
\tau_{u}(Q,\alpha)
the quiver-dimension vector pair
(\tau_{u}Q,\tau_{u}\alpha)
obtained as follows:

–

remove from
Q
the vertex

u
and the arrows adjacent to
u
;

–

for each pair
(b,c)\in Q_{1}\times Q_{1}
with

\mathrm{t}b=u
and
\mathrm{s}c=u
, put a new arrow
d
such that
\mathrm{s}d=\mathrm{s}b
and
\mathrm{t}d=\mathrm{t}c
;

–

\tau_{u}\alpha:=\alpha|_{Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}}
.

Note that in the special case when the large vertex
u
is a
source
(i.e. there is no arrow
a\in Q_{1}
with
\mathrm{t}a=u
)
or a
sink
(i.e. there is no
a\in Q_{1}
with
\mathrm{s}a=u
), then

\tau_{u}(Q,\alpha)
is simply obtained from
(Q,\alpha)
by removing
u
and all arrows adjacent to
u
.

We need to recall the
reflection transformations
(in the sense of
[
5
]
).

Definition 2.2
.

Let
Q
be a quiver,
\alpha
a sincere dimension vector for
Q
,
and

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
(Q,\alpha)
be a sincere quiver-dimension vector pair,
u\in Q_{0}
a large vertex for
(Q,\alpha)
, and
let
\theta\in\mathbb{Z}^{Q_{0}}
be a weight such that
\alpha
is
\theta
-semistable. Then exactly one of the following holds for
the weight
\theta
and the dimension vector
\alpha
:

(a)

\theta(u)>0
and
\alpha(u)=\sum_{b\in Q_{1}\colon\mathrm{t}b=u}\alpha(\mathrm{s}b)
;

(b)

\theta(u)<0
and
\alpha(u)=\sum_{c\in Q_{1}\colon\mathrm{s}c=u}\alpha(\mathrm{t}c)
;

(c)

\theta(u)=0
.

Moreover, denote by
\tau_{u}\theta
the weight for
\tau_{u}Q
given by

•

(\tau_{u}\theta)(v)=\theta(v)+|\{b\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{t}b=u,\ \mathrm{s}b=v\}|\cdot\theta(u)

in case (a);

•

(\tau_{u}\theta)(v)=\theta(v)+|\{c\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{s}c=u,\ \mathrm{t}c=v\}|\cdot\theta(u)
in case (b);

•

(\tau_{u}\theta)(v)=\theta(v)
for all
v\in(\tau_{u}Q)_{0}=Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}
in case (c).

Then we have the isomorphism of graded algebras

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)\cong\mathcal{S}(\tau_{u}Q,\tau_{u}\alpha,\tau_{u}\theta)\text{ if }\tau_{u}\theta\neq 0\text{ or }\theta=0,

whereas

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)
is isomorphic as a graded algebra to the univariate polynomial algebra

\mathcal{S}(\tau_{u}Q,\tau_{u}\alpha,\tau_{u}\theta)[t]
(where the degree of
t
is
1
) if

\tau_{u}\theta=0
and
\theta\neq 0
.

Proof.

Since
u
is assumed to be large for
(Q,\alpha)
,
there is no loop adjacent to
u
.
Denote by
b_{1},\dots,b_{k}
the arrows of
Q
whose target is
u
and denote by
c_{1},\dots,c_{l}
the arows of
Q
whose source is
u
(so
\deg_{Q}(u)=k+l
). The cases when
u
is a source (i.e.
k=0
) or when
u
is a sink (i.e.
l=0
) are allowed.
Denote by
d_{i,j}
the arrow of
\tau_{u}Q
corresponding to the pair
(b_{i},c_{j})

for each pair
(i,j)\in\{1,\dots,k\}\times\{1,\dots,l\}
, so

\mathrm{s}d_{i,j}=\mathrm{s}b_{i}
and
\mathrm{t}d_{i,j}=\mathrm{t}c_{j}
. We have

(\tau_{u}Q)_{0}=Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}
and

(\tau_{u}Q)_{1}=Q_{1}\setminus\{b_{1},\dots,b_{k},c_{1},\dots,c_{l}\}\sqcup\{d_{i,j}\mid i=1,\dots,k;j=1,\dots,l\}
. Moreover,

\tau_{u}\alpha=\alpha|_{Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}}
.

For
x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
consider the
\alpha(u)\times\alpha(\mathrm{s}b_{i})
matrices

x_{b_{i}}

(i=1,\dots,k)
. Put them in a row as the blocks of a matrix

x_{b}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccc}x_{b_{1}}&\dots&x_{b_{k}}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)\times\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha(\mathrm{s}b_{i})}.

Similarly, put the
\alpha(\mathrm{t}c_{j})\times\alpha(u)
matrices
x_{c_{j}}
in a column as blocks of a
matrix

x_{c}:=\left(\begin{array}[]{c}x_{c_{1}}\\
\vdots\\
x_{c_{l}}\end{array}\right)\in\mathbb{F}^{(\sum_{j=1}^{l}\alpha(\mathrm{t}c_{j}))\times\alpha(u)}.

When
\alpha(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha(\mathrm{s}b_{i})
, we denote by
\det(b)
the polynomial function

\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)\to\mathbb{F}
,
x\mapsto\det(x_{b})
.
Similarly, when
\alpha(u)=\sum_{j=1}^{l}\alpha(\mathrm{t}c_{j})
, we denote by
\det(c)
the polynomial function

\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)\to\mathbb{F}
,
x\mapsto\det(x_{c})
.
For
g\in\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
and
x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
we have

(g\cdot x)_{b}=g_{u}x_{b}\mathrm{diag}(g_{\mathrm{s}b_{1}},\dots,g_{\mathrm{s}b_{k}})^{-1}\text{ and }(g\cdot x)_{c}=\mathrm{diag}(g_{\mathrm{t}c_{1}},\dots,g_{\mathrm{t}c_{l}})x_{c}g_{u}^{-1}.

It follows by multiplicativity of the determinant that
\det(b)
(when defined) is a relative invariant of weight

\beta:=\varepsilon_{u}-\sum_{i=1}^{k}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{s}b_{i}}
and

\det(c)
(when defined) is a relative invariant of weight

\gamma:=-\varepsilon_{u}+\sum_{j=1}^{l}\varepsilon_{\mathrm{t}c_{j}}
.

Identify
\mathrm{GL}(\tau_{u}\alpha)
with the subgroup

\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)\mid g_{u}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)}}\}
of
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)
and identify

\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})
with the subgroup

\{g\in\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)\mid\forall v\neq u:\ g_{v}=\mathrm{id}_{\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(v)}}\}
.
This way
\mathrm{GL}(\alpha)=\mathrm{GL}(\tau_{u}\alpha)\times\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})
.
We have

\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\alpha),n\theta}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)}),n\theta(u)}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})},

where
\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\alpha),n\theta(u)}
stands for the space of relative

\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})
-invariants on
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
with weight

.
Consequently,

\mathcal{S}(Q,\alpha,\theta)\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\alpha)}\subseteq\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})}.

By the First Fundamental Theorem of Classical Invariant Theory for the special linear group (see for example
[
41
]
,

[
12
]

that the algebra
\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{SL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})}

\mathbb{F}[\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)]^{\mathrm{GL}(\mathbb{F}^{\alpha(u)})}
and whichever of
\det(b)

Each function mapping

is surjective (such a representation exists by our assumption that
u
is a small sink). Write
K
for the kernel of
\bigoplus_{\mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a}
.
Denote by
R^{\prime}
the representation of
\sigma_{u}Q
such that
R^{\prime}_{u}=K
,

R^{\prime}_{v}=R_{v}
for
v\neq u
,
R^{\prime}_{a}=R_{a}
for the arrows
a
not adjacent to
u
,
whereas
\bigoplus_{a\in\sigma_{u}Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=u}R^{\prime}_{a}
is the embedding of
K=R^{\prime}_{u}
into

\bigoplus_{a\in\sigma_{u}Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=u}R^{\prime}_{\mathrm{t}a}=\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{\mathrm{s}a}
.
The surjectivity of
\bigoplus_{\mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a}:\bigoplus_{\mathrm{t}a=u}R_{\mathrm{s}a}\to R_{u}
implies
that
\underline{\dim}(R^{\prime})=\sigma_{u}\alpha
.
By choice of
\beta
,
R^{\prime}
has a subrepresentation

S^{\prime}
of dimension vector
\beta
.
Set
S_{v}:=S^{\prime}_{v}
for
u\neq v\in Q_{0}
and
set
S_{u}:=\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a}(S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}a})
.
Now
S
is a subrepresentation of
R
by construction.
Since
\theta(u)\neq 0
, (
6
) implies that there exists a
w\in Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}
with
\beta(w)\neq 0
and there exists a

w\in Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}
with
\beta(w)<\sigma_{u}\alpha(w)=\alpha(w)
.
So
S
is a proper non-zero subrepresentation of
R
.
We have

(\bigoplus_{a\in\sigma_{u}Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=u}R^{\prime}_{a})(S^{\prime}_{u})\subseteq\bigoplus_{a\in\sigma_{u}Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=u}S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{t}a}=\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}S_{\mathrm{s}a}

since
S^{\prime}
is a subrepresentation of
R^{\prime}
.
On the other hand,

(\bigoplus_{a\in\sigma_{u}Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=u}R^{\prime}_{a})(S^{\prime}_{u})\subseteq\ker(\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a})

by definition of
R^{\prime}
.
Consequently,

S^{\prime}_{u}\cong(\bigoplus_{a\in\sigma_{u}Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=u}R^{\prime}_{a})(S^{\prime}_{u})\subseteq\ker((\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a})\big|_{\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\mathrm{t}a=u}S_{\mathrm{s}a}}),

hence

\beta(u)\leq\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\ker((\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a})\big|_{\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\mathrm{t}a=u}S_{\mathrm{s}a}}))

It follows that

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{u})=\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}a})-\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\ker((\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a})\big|_{\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\mathrm{t}a=u}S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}a}}))

\displaystyle=\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}\beta(\mathrm{s}a)-\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(\ker((\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a})\big|_{\bigoplus_{a\in Q_{1},\mathrm{t}a=u}S_{\mathrm{s}a}}))

\displaystyle\leq-\beta(u)+\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}\beta(\mathrm{s}a).

By
\theta(u)>0
we conclude

\theta(u)\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{u})\leq-\theta(u)\beta(u)+\theta(u)\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}\beta(\mathrm{s}a).

The above inequality and
\underline{\dim}(S)(v)=\beta(v)=\underline{\dim}(S^{\prime})(v)
for

v\neq u
imply that

\displaystyle\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\theta(v)

\displaystyle\underline{\dim}(S)(v)=\theta(u)\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{u})+\sum_{v\in Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}}\theta(v)\underline{\dim}(S)(v)

\displaystyle\leq-\theta(u)\beta(u)+\theta(u)\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=u}\beta(\mathrm{s}a)+\sum_{v\in Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}}\theta(v)\beta(v)

\displaystyle=-\theta(u)\beta(u)+\sum_{v\in Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}}(\theta(v)+\theta(u)\cdot|\{a\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{t}a=u,\ \mathrm{s}a=v\}|)\beta(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\sigma_{u}Q_{0}}\sigma_{u}\theta(v)\beta(v)=0.

Thus
R
has a proper non-zero subrepresentation
S
with

\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}\theta(v)\underline{\dim}(S)(v)\leq 0
, and consequently,
R
is not
\theta
-stable.
This holds for any representation
R
of
Q
with dimension vector
\alpha
for which

\bigoplus_{\mathrm{t}a=u}R_{a}:\bigoplus_{\mathrm{t}a=u}R_{\mathrm{s}a}\to R_{u}
is surjective.
Note that this condition holds for the representations contained in an open Zariski dense subset of
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
, so the subset of non-
\theta
-stable points in
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
contains a Zariski open dense subset. This is a contradiction, since for the
\theta
-stable dimension vector
\alpha
the
\theta
-stable points in
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
constitute a Zariski dense open subset as well, and two open Zariski dense subsets of
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
have non-empty intersection.
The case when
\tau=\sigma_{u}
and
u
is a source vertex in
Q
can be dealt with by a
similar (dual) argument.

Case 2:
\tau=\tau_{u}
. We use the notation of the proof of Lemma
3.1
.
Suppose first that we are in case (a); that is,
\theta(u)>0
and
\alpha(u)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\alpha(\mathrm{s}b_{i})
.
The representation space
\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)
contains the Zariski open dense subset

U:=\{x\in\mathcal{R}(Q,\alpha)\mid\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}x_{b_{i}}:\bigoplus_{i=1}^{k}x_{\mathrm{s}b_{i}}\to x_{u}\mbox{ is an isomorphism}\}.

Denote by

the surjective map given in
the proof of Lemma

.
Pick some

.
By choice of
\beta
,
\mu(x)
has a subrepresentation

with
\underline{\dim}(S^{\prime})=\beta
.
Set

for

and
S_{u}:=\sum_{i=1}^{k}x_{b_{i}}(S_{\mathrm{s}b_{i}})

Then

x_{c_{j}}(x_{b_{i}}(S_{\mathrm{s}b_{i}}))=\mu(x)_{d_{i,j}}(S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{s}d_{i,j}})\subseteq S^{\prime}_{\mathrm{t}d_{i,j}}=S_{\mathrm{t}c_{j}}.

As

(7)

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{u})

whereas

\displaystyle\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S_{v})=\dim_{\mathbb{F}}(S^{\prime}_{v})=\beta(v).

We have

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in\tau_{u}Q_{0}}\tau_{u}\theta(v)\beta(v)

\displaystyle=\sum_{v\in Q_{0}\setminus\{u\}}(\theta(v)+\theta(u)\cdot|\{b\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{t}b=u,\ \mathrm{s}b=v\}|)\beta(v)

5.
Dimension vectors in the fundamental set

For this section we fix a wild connected quiver
Q
, and we fix a sincere dimension vector

\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{Q_{0}}
(i.e.
\alpha(v)>0
for all
v\in Q_{0}
). We shall assume that
\alpha
belongs to the
fundamental set

F_{Q}
.
Recall that by definition of the Cartan form we have

(8)

(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}=2\alpha(v)-\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=v}\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)-\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=v}\alpha(\mathrm{s}a),

so the assumption
\alpha\in F_{Q}
means that

2\alpha(v)\leq\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{s}a=v}\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)+\sum_{a\in Q_{1},\ \mathrm{t}a=v}\alpha(\mathrm{s}a)\quad\mbox{ for all }v\in Q_{0}.

Set

Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}:=\{v\in Q_{0}\mid(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}<0\}.

Denote by
Q^{\alpha,+}
the full subgraph of
Q
spanned by the vertices in

Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}:=Q_{0}\setminus Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}=\{v\in Q_{0}\mid(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}=0\};

that is, the vertex set of
Q^{\alpha,+}
is
Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}
, and the edge set
of
Q^{\alpha,+}
is

Q^{\alpha,+}_{1}:=\{a\in Q_{1}\mid\mathrm{s}a,\mathrm{t}a\in Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}\}.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
\Delta
be a subgraph of
Q
, and
v
a vertex of
\Delta
.
We have the inequality

(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\leq(\alpha|_{\Delta},\varepsilon_{v})_{\Delta}

with equality if and only if all edges of
Q
adjacent to
v
belong to
\Delta
.

Proof.

Denote by
E(v)
the set of edges in
Q
that are connected to
v
, and are not contained in
\Delta
.
We conclude from (
8
) that

(\alpha|_{\Delta},\varepsilon_{v})_{\Delta}-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}=\sum_{a\in E(v),\ \mathrm{s}a=v}\alpha(\mathrm{t}a)+\sum_{a\in E(v),\ \mathrm{t}a=v}\alpha(\mathrm{s}a).

This clearly implies the statement.
∎

Lemma 5.2
.

The graph
Q^{\alpha,+}
is the disjoint union of Dynkin graphs.

Proof.

Let
\Delta
be a connected component of
Q^{\alpha,+}
, and

\beta:=\alpha|_{\Delta}
. By Lemma
5.1
for each
v\in\Delta_{0}
we have

(9)

\displaystyle(\beta,\varepsilon_{v})_{\Delta}\geq(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\geq 0.

It follows that the Cartan matrix
C_{\Delta}
does not fall under case (N) in
[
28
, Lemma 1.2]
, hence

\Delta
is a Dynkin or extended Dynkin graph
(see for example
[
28
, Section 1.1]
or
[
30
, Section 1.3]
).
Moreover, since
Q
is connected and wild,
\Delta
must have a vertex
v
connected with some vertex in
Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
. For such a
v
the first inequality in (
9
) is strict, so we have
(\beta,\varepsilon_{v})_{\Delta}>0
. Consequently, the Cartan matrix
C_{\Delta}
does not fall under case (Z) in
[
28
, Lemma 1.2]
. Thus
\Delta
is a Dynkin graph.
∎

We introduce some ad hoc terminology.
A vertex of
Q^{\alpha,+}
is called
tied
if it is connected by an edge in
Q
to a vertex in
Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
. A vertex of
Q^{\alpha,+}
is called
free
if it is not connected by an edge in
Q
to a vertex in
Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
.

Lemma 5.3
.

(i)

We have
|Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}|\leq-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
.

(ii)

For each
v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
we have
\alpha(v)\leq-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
.

(iii)

For each
v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
we have
\deg_{Q}(v)\leq-6\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
.

(iv)

There are at most
12\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}^{2}
tied vertices in
Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}
.

(v)

For each tied vertex
w\in Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}
we have
\alpha(w)\leq-6\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
.

(vi)

If
w\in Q_{0}
is connected by an edge of
Q
to a vertex
v\in Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}
, then
\alpha(w)\leq 2\alpha(v)
.

(vii)

We have
|Q_{0}|\leq|Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}|-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
and

|Q_{1}|\leq|Q^{\alpha,+}_{1}|-6\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}|Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}|\leq|Q^{\alpha,+}_{1}|+12\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}^{2}.

Proof.

We have

(10)

-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}=-(\alpha,\alpha)_{Q}=\sum_{v\in Q_{0}}-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\cdot\alpha(v).

As
\alpha\in F_{Q}
, each summand on the right hand side of (
10
) is non-negative, and the summands
corresponding to
v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
are positive, implying that

\sum_{v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}}\alpha(v)\leq-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}.

Recall that
\alpha
is a sincere dimension vector, hence each summand on the left hand side above is at least
1
, and the number of summands is

|Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}|
, hence (i) holds.
Moreover, the summand corresponding to
v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
is at least

\alpha(v)
, so by (
10
) we get
\alpha(v)\leq-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
, thus (ii) is shown.
By (
10
) for each
v\in Q_{0}
we have

(11)

-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}\geq-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\cdot\alpha(v)\geq-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}.

By (
8
) for any
v\in Q_{0}
we have

(12)

-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\geq-2\alpha(v)+\deg_{Q}(v),

and if
w\in Q_{0}
is connected to
v
by an edge in
Q
, we also have

(13)

-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\geq-2\alpha(v)+\alpha(w).

Combining (ii), (
11
) and (
12
)
for
v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
we get that

-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}\geq 4\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}+\deg_{Q}(v),

so (iii) holds.
Similarly, combining (ii), (
11
) and (
13
)
for
v\in Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
and
w\in Q_{0}
connected to
v
by an edge in
Q

we get that

-2\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}\geq 4\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}+\alpha(w),

so (v) holds.
The number of tied vertices is obviously bounded by the number of edges adjacent to a vertex in
Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
, so
statement (iv) is an immediate consequence of (i) and (iii).
For
v\in Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}
we have
(\alpha,\epsilon_{v})_{Q}=0
,
so (
13
) yields

0=-(\alpha,\varepsilon_{v})_{Q}\geq-2\alpha(v)+\alpha(w),

thus (vi) holds. The bound for
|Q_{0}|
in (vii) follows from the obvious equality

|Q_{0}|=|Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}|+|Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}|
and (i).
To see the bound for
|Q_{1}|
in (vii) note that any edge in

Q_{1}\setminus Q^{\alpha,+}_{1}
is connected to a vertex of
Q^{\alpha,-}_{0}
, and the number of such arrows can be bounded by (i) and (iii).
∎

Remark 5.4
.

(i) Since
Q
is wild,
Lemma
5.3
(i) implies
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}<0
.

(ii) The numbers
|Q^{\alpha,+}_{0}|
and
|Q^{\alpha,+}_{1}|
can be arbitrarily large for a fixed value of
\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}
.

Note that any connected subgraph of a Dynkin graph is a Dynkin graph.
Every Dynkin graph is a tree. The graphs
E_{6}
,
E_{7}
,
E_{8}
, and
D_{n}
(
n\geq 4
)
have a vertex of degree
3
, three vertices of degree
1
, and their remaining vertices have degree
2
. The graphs
A_{n}
(
n\geq 2
) have two vertices of degree
1
and
n-2
vertices of degree
2
. Removing a degree
d\in\{2,3\}
vertex
v
from a Dynkin graph

\Delta
it splits into
d
connected components. Adding to a component the edge connecting it to
v
we obtain connected subgraphs
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{d}
of
\Delta
,
such that
\Delta
is their union, and for
i\neq j
,
\Delta_{i}
and
\Delta_{j}
have one common vertex, namely
v
. Note that
\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{d}
are Dynkin graphs, because any connected subgraph of a Dynkin graph is a Dynkin graph.

The above observations imply that the tied vertices decompose
Q^{\alpha,+}
as the union
of connected subgraphs
\Delta^{(i)}
(
i=1,\dots,\kappa_{Q,\alpha}
) satisfying that

(1)

each
\Delta^{(i)}
is a Dynkin graph;

(2)

each
\Delta^{(i)}
has a vertex
v^{(i)}
with
\deg_{\Delta^{(i)}}(v^{(i)})\leq 1

and
v^{(i)}
is a tied vertex;

(3)

all the vertices
v
of
\Delta^{(i)}
with
\deg_{\Delta^{(i)}}(v)\in\{2,3\}
are free in

Q^{\alpha,+}
;

(4)

for
i\neq j
, the subgraphs
\Delta^{(i)}
and
\Delta^{(j)}
are either disjoint or they have exactly one common vertex, and this common vertex is a tied vertex of

Q^{\alpha,+}
.

Lemma 5.5
.

The number
\kappa_{Q,\alpha}
of the subgraphs
\Delta^{(i)}
of
Q^{\alpha,+}

introduced above is at most

36\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}^{2}
.

Proof.

Each
\Delta^{(i)}
has a tied vertex, and one tied vertex belongs to at most three
subgraphs
\Delta^{(i)}
. Thus
\kappa_{Q,\alpha}
is at most three times the number of tied vertices. Consequently by Lemma
5.3
(iv) we get

\kappa_{Q,\alpha}\leq 3\cdot 12\cdot\langle\alpha,\alpha\rangle_{Q}^{2}
.
∎

For the weight

[

.

\mathcal{M}(Q,\alpha,0)
for some quiver

\alpha
.

by

A quiver

is

if for any pair

to

.

10.1

in

]
,
[

]
that any moduli space

is isomorphic to a product of such moduli spaces where the quiver is a strongly connected subquiver of

and the dimension vector is sincere. When
Q
is a one-lop quiver then

is the affine

.
