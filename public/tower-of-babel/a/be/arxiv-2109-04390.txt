Abstract
This paper tackles the problem of single-user multiple-input multiple-output communication with 1-bit digital-to-analog and analog-to-digital converters. With the information-theoretic capacity as benchmark, the complementary strategies of beamforming and equiprobable signaling are contrasted in the regimes of operational interest, and the ensuing spectral efficiencies are characterized. Various canonical channel types are considered, with emphasis on line-of-sight settings under both spherical and planar wavefronts, respectively representative of short and long transmission ranges at mmWave and terahertz frequencies. In all cases, a judicious combination of beamforming and equiprobable signaling is shown to operate within a modest gap from capacity.

From 1G to 5G, the spectrum devoted to wireless communication has surged from a handful of MHz to multiple GHz, roughly three orders of magnitude, and this growth is bound to continue as new mmWave bands open up and inroads are made into the terahertz realm

II
Signal and Channel Models

II-A

Signal Model

Consider a transmitter equipped with
{N_{\mathrm{t}}}
antennas and 1-bit DACs per complex dimension. The receiver, which features
{N_{\mathrm{r}}}
antennas and a 1-bit ADC per complex dimension, observes

{\bm{y}}=\text{sgn}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\,{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}+{\bm{z}}\right)

(2)

where the sign function is applied separately to the real and imaginary parts of each entry, such that
y_{n}\in\{\pm 1\pm\mathrm{j}\}
, while
{\bm{H}}
is the
{N_{\mathrm{r}}}\times{N_{\mathrm{t}}}
channel matrix,

{\bm{z}}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}({\bf 0},{\bm{I}})
is the noise, and
{\mathsf{SNR}}
is the signal-to-noise ratio per receive antenna. Each entry of the transmit vector
{\bm{x}}
also takes the values
\pm 1\pm\mathrm{j}
.

Each antenna in the foregoing formulation could actually correspond to a compact subarray, in which case the model subsumes array-of-subarrays structures for the transmitter and/or receiver
[
60
,
61
,
62
,
63
,
64
]
provided
{\mathsf{SNR}}
is appropriately scaled.

For each given
{\bm{H}}
, the relationship in (
2
) embodies a discrete memoryless channel with
4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}\times 4^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}}
transition probabilities determined by

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}}

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}p_{\Re\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}}\,p_{\Im\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}},

(3)

where the factorization follows from the noise independence per receive antenna and complex dimension.
Each such noise component has variance
1/2
, hence

\displaystyle p_{\Re\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}}(1|{\bm{\mathsf{x}}})

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\!\left[\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}+z_{n}\}>0\right]

(4)

\displaystyle=\text{Pr}\!\left[\Re\{z_{n}\}>-\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}\}\right]

(5)

\displaystyle=Q\!\left(\!-\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}\}\!\right)

(6)

where
{\bm{h}}_{n}
is the
n
th row of
{\bm{H}}
(for
n=0,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1
) and
Q(\cdot)
is the Gaussian Q-function. Similarly,

p_{\Re\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}}(-1|{\bm{\mathsf{x}}})=Q\!\left(\!\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}\}\!\right).

(7)

From (
6
) and (
7
),

p_{\Re\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}}(\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}|{\bm{\mathsf{x}}})=Q\!\left(\!-\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}\}\!\right)

(8)

and, mirroring it, finally

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}}|{\bm{\mathsf{x}}})

\displaystyle=\prod_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}Q\!\left(\!-\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot Q\!\left(\!-\Im\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Im\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}\}\!\right).

(9)

The transition probabilities correspond to (
9
) evaluated for the
4^{N_{\mathrm{r}}}
possible values of
{\bm{y}}
and the
4^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}
values of
{\bm{x}}
.
If
{\bm{H}}
is known, these transition probabilities can be readily computed. Conversely, if the transition probabilities are known,
{\bm{H}}
can be deduced.

The
4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}
transmit vectors
{\bm{x}}
can be partitioned into
4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}
quartets,
each containing four vectors and being invariant under a
90^{\circ}
phase rotation of all the entries: from any vector in the quartet, the other three are obtained by repeatedly multiplying by
\mathrm{j}
. Since a
90^{\circ}
phase rotation of
{\bm{x}}
propagates as a
90^{\circ}
phase rotation of
{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}
, and the added noise is circularly symmetric, the four vectors making up each transmit quartet are statistically equivalent and they should thus have the same transmission probability so as to convey the maximum amount of information.

Likewise, the set of
4^{N_{\mathrm{r}}}
possible vectors
{\bm{y}}
can be partitioned into
4^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}
quartets, and the four vectors
{\bm{y}}
within each received quartet are equiprobable.

II-B

Channel Model

If the channel is stable over each codeword, then every realization of
{\bm{H}}
has operational significance and
{\mathsf{SNR}}
is well defined under the normalization
{\rm tr}({\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}^{*})={N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{r}}}
. Conversely, if the coding takes place over a sufficiently broad range of channel fluctuations, that significance is acquired in an ergodic sense with
\mathbb{E}\big[{\rm tr}({\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}^{*})\big]={N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{r}}}

[
65
]
.
The following classes of channels are specifically considered.

Line-of-Sight (LOS) with Spherical Wavefronts

LOS is the chief propagation mechanism at mmWave and terahertz frequencies, and the spherical nature of the wavefronts is relevant for large arrays and short transmission ranges. For uniform linear arrays (ULAs)
[
66
]
,

{\bm{H}}={\bm{D}}_{\rm rx}\tilde{{\bm{H}}}{\bm{D}}_{\rm tx}

(10)

where
{\bm{D}}_{\rm rx}
and
{\bm{D}}_{\rm rx}
are diagonal matrices with entries

\displaystyle[{\bm{D}}_{\rm rx}]_{n,n}

\displaystyle=e^{-j\pi\left[\frac{2n}{\uplambda}{d_{\rm r}}\sin\!\theta_{\rm r}\cos\!\phi+\frac{n^{2}}{\uplambda D}d^{2}_{\rm r}\,(1-\sin^{2}\!\theta_{\rm r}\cos^{2}\!\phi)\right]}

\displaystyle[{\bm{D}}_{\rm tx}]_{m,m}

\displaystyle=e^{-j\pi\left[\frac{2m}{\uplambda}{d_{\rm t}}\sin\!\theta_{\rm t}+\frac{m^{2}}{\uplambda D}d^{2}_{\rm t}\right]}

(11)

and with
D
the range,
\uplambda
the wavelength,
d_{\rm t}
and
d_{\rm r}
the antenna spacings at transmitter and receiver,
\theta_{\rm t}
and
\theta_{\rm r}
the transmitter and receiver elevations, and
\phi
their relative azimuth angle.
In turn,
\tilde{{\bm{H}}}
is the Vandermonde matrix

\displaystyle\tilde{{\bm{H}}}=\begin{bmatrix}e^{j2\pi\eta\frac{0\times 0}{{N_{\mathsf{max}}}}}&\cdots&e^{j2\pi\eta\frac{(N_{\rm t}-1)\times 0}{{N_{\mathsf{max}}}}}\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
e^{j2\pi\eta\frac{0\times(N_{\rm r}-1)}{{N_{\mathsf{max}}}}}&\cdots&e^{j2\pi\eta\frac{(N_{\rm t}-1)\times(N_{\rm r}-1)}{{N_{\mathsf{max}}}}}\end{bmatrix}

(12)

where
{N_{\mathsf{max}}}=\max({N_{\mathrm{t}}},{N_{\mathrm{r}}})
while

\eta=\frac{({d_{\rm r}}\cos\theta_{\rm r})({d_{\rm t}}\cos\theta_{\rm t}){N_{\mathsf{max}}}}{\uplambda D}

(13)

is a parameter that concisely describes any LOS setting with ULAs.

Uniform rectangular arrays can be expressed as the Kronecker product of ULAs, and expressions deriving from (
10
) emerge
[
67
]
.
For more complex topologies, the entries of
{\bm{H}}
continue to be of unit magnitude, but the pattern of phase variations becomes more cumbersome.

LOS with Planar Wavefronts

For long enough transmission ranges, the planar wavefront counterpart to (
10
)
is obtained by letting
D\to\infty
, whereby the channel becomes rank-1 with

\displaystyle h_{n,m}=e^{-\mathrm{j}\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}(nd_{\rm r}\sin\theta_{\rm r}\cos\phi+md_{\rm t}\sin\theta_{\rm t})}.

(14)

IID Rayleigh Fading

In this model, representing situations of rich multipath propagation, the entries of
{\bm{H}}
are IID and
h_{n,m}\sim\mathcal{N}_{\mathbb{C}}(0,1)
.

We note that the frequency-flat representation embodied by
{\bm{H}}
is congruous for the two LOS channel models, but less so for the IID model, where the scattering would go hand in hand with frequency selectivity over the envisioned bandwidths. The analysis presented for this model intends to set the stage for more refined characterizations that account for the inevitable intersymbol interference.
In fact, even for the LOS channels, over a sufficiently broad bandwidth there is bound to be intersymbol interference because of spatial widening, i.e., because of the distinct propagation delays between the various transmit and receive antennas
[
68
,
69
]
.

III
1-Bit Capacity

Denote by
p_{1},\ldots,p_{4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}}
the activation probabilities of the transmit quartets, such that
\sum_{k}p_{k}=1

and
p_{\bm{x}}({\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})=p_{k}/4
with
{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}
any of the vectors in the
k
th quartet.
Letting
\mathcal{H}(\cdot)
indicate entropy, and with all the probabilities conditioned on
{\bm{H}}
, the mutual information is

\displaystyle\!\!\mathcal{I}({\mathsf{SNR}},{\bm{H}})

\displaystyle=\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}})-\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})

(15)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}}}p_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{\ell})\log_{2}\frac{1}{p_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{\ell})}-\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})

(16)

\displaystyle=4\!\sum_{\ell=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}}\!p_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{\ell})\log_{2}\frac{1}{p_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{\ell})}-\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})

(17)

where (
17
) follows from the equiprobability of the vectors in each received quartet and
{\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{\ell}
is any of the vectors in the
\ell
th such quartet while

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}}\!\frac{p_{k}}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{3}p_{{\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}}|\mathrm{j}^{i}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})

(18)

with

depending on
{\mathsf{SNR}}
and
{\bm{H}}
as per (
9
).
Elaborating on (
18
),

\displaystyle p_{{\bm{y}}}({\bm{\mathsf{y}}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}}\!\frac{p_{k}}{4}\left[\prod_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}Q\!\left(\!-\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\cdot Q\!\left(\!-\Im\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Im\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\prod_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}Q\!\left(\!\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Im\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot Q\!\left(\!-\Im\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\prod_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}Q\!\left(\!\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad\cdot Q\!\left(\!\Im\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Im\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\prod_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}Q\!\left(\!-\Re\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Im\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)

\displaystyle\quad\left.\cdot Q\!\left(\!\Im\{{\mathsf{y}}_{n}\}\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)\right].

(19)

In turn, because of the factorization of
p_{{\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}}}
in (
9
),

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}\big(\mathcal{H}(\Re\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}})+\mathcal{H}(\Im\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}})\big)

(20)

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{k=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}}\!\frac{p_{k}}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{3}\!\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}\big(\mathcal{H}(\Re\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}=\mathrm{j}^{i}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})

\displaystyle\quad+\mathcal{H}(\Im\{y_{n}\}|{\bm{x}}=\mathrm{j}^{i}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})\big)

(21)

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{k=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}}\!\frac{p_{k}}{4}\sum_{i=0}^{3}\!\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}\!\left[\mathcal{H}_{\rm b}\!\left(Q\!\left(\!-{\textstyle\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}\mathrm{j}^{i}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)\!\right)\right.

(22)

\mathcal{H}_{\rm b}(p)=-p\log_{2}p-(1-p)\log_{2}(1-p)
is the binary entropy function. Since changing

such that
\mathcal{H}_{\rm b}(Q(-\xi))=\mathcal{H}_{\rm b}(Q(\xi))

\displaystyle\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}}|{\bm{x}})

\displaystyle=\!\sum_{k=1}^{4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}}\!p_{k}\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}\!\left[\mathcal{H}_{\rm b}\!\left(Q\!\left(\!-\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Re\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)\!\right)\right.

\displaystyle\quad\left.+\mathcal{H}_{\rm b}\!\left(Q\!\left(\!-\sqrt{\frac{{\mathsf{SNR}}}{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}}\Im\{{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}\!\right)\!\right)\right].

(23)

17
), (

\mathcal{O}(4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}4^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1})

, hence the interest in analytical characterizations. From
\mathcal{I}({\mathsf{SNR}},{\bm{H}})
, the 1-bit capacity is

C({\mathsf{SNR}},{\bm{H}})=\max_{\{p_{k}\}:\sum_{k}p_{k}=1}\mathcal{I}({\mathsf{SNR}},{\bm{H}})

(24)

with maximization over

Since

In ergodic settings, what applies is the ergodic spectral efficiency

\mathcal{I}({\mathsf{SNR}})=\mathbb{E}_{\bm{H}}\big[\mathcal{I}({\mathsf{SNR}},{\bm{H}})\big]

(25)

and likewise for the ergodic capacity.
Alternatively, if the channel is stable over each codeword, then

and

themselves are meaningful for each

.

The 1-bit capacity cannot exceed
2\min({N_{\mathrm{t}}},{N_{\mathrm{r}}})
b/s/Hz, with three distinct regimes:

•

Low SNR. This is a key regime at mmWave and terahertz frequencies, given the difficulty in producing strong signals, the high propagation losses, and the noise bandwidth.

•

Intermediate SNR. Here, the spectral efficiency improves sustainedly with the SNR.

•

High SNR. This is a regime of diminishing returns, once the capacity nears
2\min({N_{\mathrm{t}}},{N_{\mathrm{r}}})
.

III-A

Low SNR

The low-SNR behavior is most conveniently examined with the mutual information expressed as function of the normalized energy per bit at the receiver,

Intermediate SNR

In 1-bit communication, the intermediate-SNR regime steals relevance from its high-SNR counterpart, which becomes unappealing.

IV
1-Bit vs Full-Resolution Capacity

A naïve comparison of the 1-bit and full-resolution capacities would indicate that the former always trails the latter.
In terms of power, their gap in dB is at least the difference between their

Such naïve comparison, however, only accounts for radiated power, disregarding any other differences in power consumption between the full-resolution and 1-bit alternatives. While appropriate when the radiated power dominates, this neglect becomes misleading when the digitalization consumes sizeable power and, since this is the chief motivation for 1-bit communication, by definition the comparison is somewhat deceptive.

Indeed, whenever the excess power of a full-resolution architecture, relative to 1-bit, exceeds a

V
Transmit Beamforming

Transmit beamforming corresponds to
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{x}}
being
rank-1
, i.e., to
{\bm{x}}
being drawn from a single quartet, with such quartet generally dependent on
{\bm{H}}
. We examine this strategy with an ergodic perspective; for nonergodic channels, the formulation stands without the expectations over
{\bm{H}}
.

V-A

Low SNR

For vanishing SNR, transmit beamforming is not only conceptually appealing, but information-theoretically optimum.
Indeed, (
30
) can be rewritten as

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\rm b}}{N_{0}}_{\rm\min}=\frac{\pi{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\mathbb{E}\big[\sum_{k}p_{k}\,\|{\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\|^{2}\big]\log_{2}e},

(36)

which is maximized by assigning probability
1
to the quartet
k^{\star}=\arg\max\|{\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\|^{2}
for each realization of
{\bm{H}}
.
Therefore, it is optimum to beamform, and the optimum beamforming quartet is the one maximizing the received power.
The task is then to determine
k^{\star}
from within the
4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}
possible quartets.

For
{N_{\mathrm{t}}}=1
, there is no need to optimize over
k
—only one quartet can be transmitted—and thus

\displaystyle\frac{E_{\rm b}}{N_{0}}_{\rm\min}

\displaystyle=\frac{\pi}{2{N_{\mathrm{r}}}\log_{2}e},

(37)

which amounts to
0.37
dB for
{N_{\mathrm{r}}}=1

[
73
]
and improves by
3
dB with every doubling of
{N_{\mathrm{r}}}
thereafter.

For
{N_{\mathrm{t}}}>1
, it is useful to recognize that
the choices for
{\bm{x}}
that are bound to yield high values for
\|{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}\|^{2}
are those that project maximally on the dimension of
{\bm{H}}
that offers the largest gain, namely the maximum-eigenvalue eigenvector of
{\bm{H}}^{*}{\bm{H}}
. This, in turn, requires that
{\bm{x}}
mimic, as best as possible, the structure of that eigenvector; since the magnitude of the entries of
{\bm{x}}
is fixed, this mimicking ought to be in terms of phases only.
Formalizing this intuition, it is possible to circumvent the need to exhaustively search the entire field of
4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}
possibilities and
conveniently identify a subset of only
{N_{\mathrm{t}}}
quartet candidates that is sure to contain the one best aligning with the maximum-eigenvalue eigenvector of
{\bm{H}}^{*}{\bm{H}}
, denoted henceforth by
{\bm{v}}_{0}
.
Precisely, as detailed in Appendix
A
, if we let
\varphi_{m}=\angle(v_{0,m})+\epsilon
for
m=0,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1
, the
{N_{\mathrm{t}}}
quartets in the subset can be determined as

{\bm{x}}_{k}=\text{sgn}\big(e^{\mathrm{j}\varphi_{k-1}}{\bm{v}}_{0}\big)\qquad\quad k=1,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{t}}}

(38)

where
\epsilon
is a small quantity, positive or negative. If the channel is rank-1, then this subset is sure to contain the optimum
{\bm{x}}_{k^{\star}}
; if the rank is higher, then optimality is not guaranteed, but the best value in the above subset is bound to yield excellent performance.

Turning to the
\frac{E_{\rm b}}{N_{0}}_{\rm min}
achieved by
{\bm{x}}_{k^{\star}}

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2\,\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{0}\big]\log_{2}e}\leq\frac{E_{\rm b}}{N_{0}}_{\rm\min}\leq\frac{\pi^{3}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}{16\,\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{0}\|{\bm{v}}_{0}\|^{2}_{1}\big]\log_{2}e}

where
\lambda_{0}
is the maximum eigenvalue of
{\bm{H}}^{*}{\bm{H}}

while
\|\cdot\|_{1}
denotes L1 norm.
For
{N_{\mathrm{r}}}=1

\displaystyle\frac{\pi}{2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\log_{2}e}\leq\frac{E_{\rm b}}{N_{0}}_{\rm\min}\leq\frac{\pi^{3}}{16\left(1+({N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1)\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\log_{2}e}.

Finally,
S_{0}
can be obtained by plugging
{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}_{k^{\star}}
and
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{x}}={\bm{x}}_{k^{\star}}{\bm{x}}^{*}_{k^{\star}}

V-B

Intermediate SNR

The low-SNR linearity of the mutual information in the received power is the root cause of the optimality of power-based beamforming in that regime. The orientation on the complex plane of the received signals is immaterial—a rotation shifts power from the real to the imaginary part, or vice versa, but the total power is preserved. Likewise, the power split among receive antennas is immaterial to the low-SNR mutual information.

At higher SNRs, the linearity breaks down and the mutual information becomes a more intricate function of
{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}
, such that proper signal orientations and power balances become important, to keep
{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{x}}
away from the ADC quantization boundaries for
n=0,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1
. This has a dual consequence:

•

Transmit beamforming ceases to be generally optimum, even if the channel is rank-1.

•

Even within the confines of beamforming, solutions not based on maximizing power are more satisfying.

As exemplified in Fig.
2
for
{N_{\mathrm{r}}}=1
, a beamforming quartet with a better complex-plane disposition at the receiver may be preferable to one yielding a larger magnitude.
This is because, after a 1-bit ADC, only

rotations and no scalings are possible (in contrast with full-resolution receivers, where
{\bm{h}}{\bm{x}}
can subsequently be rotated and scaled). The best beamforming quartet is the one that simultaneously ensures large real and imaginary parts for
{\bm{h}}_{n}{\bm{x}}
in a balanced fashion for
n=0,\ldots,{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1
, and the task of identifying this quartet is a fitting one for learning algorithms
[
74
,
58
]
.

VI
Equiprobable Signaling

The complementary strategy to beamforming is to activate multiple quartets, increasing the rank of
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{x}}
.
Ultimately, all quartets can be activated with equal probability, such that
\bm{\Sigma}_{\bm{x}}=2{\bm{I}}
.
This renders the signals IID across the transmit antennas, i.e., pure spatial multiplexing.
We examine this strategy with an ergodic perspective.

VI-A

Low SNR

With equiprobable signaling, (
30

\frac{E_{\rm b}}{N_{0}}_{\rm\min}=\frac{\pi}{2{N_{\mathrm{r}}}\log_{2}e}.

(40)

In addition

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\big[\|

\displaystyle\Re\{{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}\}\|^{4}_{4}+\|\Im\{{\bm{H}}{\bm{x}}\}\|^{4}_{4}\big]=6\,{\rm tr}\big((\text{diag}({\bm{H}}{\bm{H}}^{*}))^{2}\big)

\displaystyle-4\sum_{n=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}-1}\sum_{m=0}^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}\left(\Re\{h_{n,m}\}^{4}+\Im\{h_{n,m}\}^{4}\right),

Combining (

\frac{8\,\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{0}\|{\bm{v}}_{0}\|^{2}_{1}\big]}{\pi^{2}{N_{\mathrm{t}}}{N_{\mathrm{r}}}}\leq\Delta_{\sf BF}\leq\frac{\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{0}\big]}{{N_{\mathrm{r}}}}.

(42)

This enables some general considerations:

•

The low-SNR advantage of beamforming is essentially determined by the maximum eigenvalue of
{\bm{H}}^{*}{\bm{H}}

•

If all eigenvalues are equal, beamforming may still yield a lingering advantage for

, but not otherwise. Indeed, for
{N_{\mathrm{t}}}\leq{N_{\mathrm{r}}}
, if all eigenvalues all equal then
\mathbb{E}\big[\lambda_{0}\big]={N_{\mathrm{r}}}
and thus
\Delta_{\sf BF}\leq 1
.

VI-B

Intermediate SNR

While beamforming is optimum at low SNR, it is decidedly suboptimum beyond, and activating multiple quartets becomes instrumental to surpass the 2-b/s/Hz mark. This is the case even in rank-1 channels, where the activation of multiple quartets allows producing richer signals; this can be seen as the 1-bit counterpart to higher-order constellations. And, given how the curse of dimensionality afflicts the computation of the optimum quartet probabilities, equiprobable signaling is a very enticing way of going about this.
As will be seen, not only is it implementationally convenient, but highly effective.

VIII
Conclusion

A host of issues that are thoroughly understood for full-resolution settings must be tackled anew for 1-bit MIMO communication.
In particular, the computation of the capacity becomes unwieldy for even very modest dimensionalities and the derivation of general precoding solutions becomes a formidable task, itself power-consuming. Fortunately, in the single-user case such general precoding can be circumvented via a judicious switching between beamforming and equiprobable signaling, with the added benefits that these transmissions strategies are much more amenable to analytical characterizations and that their requirements in terms of channel-state information at the transmitter are minimal:
\log_{2}4^{{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1}=4\,({N_{\mathrm{t}}}-1)
bits for beamforming, none for equiprobable signaling.

The transition from beamforming to equiprobable signaling could be finessed by progressively activating quartets as the SNR grows, but the results in this paper suggest that there is a small margin of improvement: a direct switching at some appropriate point suffices to operate within a few dB of capacity at both low and intermediate SNRs.
It would be of interest to gauge this shortfall for more intricate channel models such as those in
[
81
,
82
,
83
]
.

Channel estimation at the receiver is an important aspect, with the need for procedures that avoid having to painstakingly gauge all the transition probabilities between
{\bm{x}}
and
{\bm{y}}
to deduce
{\bm{H}}
. Of much interest would be to extend existing results for channel estimation with full-resolution DACs and 1-bit ADCs
[
84
,
24
,
25
,
85
,
86
]

to the complete 1-bit realm. Equally pertinent would be to establish the bandwidths over which a frequency-flat representation suffices for each channel model, and to extend the respective analyses to account for intersymbol interference. This is acutely important given the impossibility of implementing OFDM with 1-bit converters.

In those multiuser settings where orthogonal (time/frequency) multiple access is effective, switching between beamforming and equiprobable signaling is also enticing. In other cases, chiefly if the antenna numbers are highly asymmetric, orthogonal multiple access is decidedly suboptimum, and there is room for more general schemes. We hope that the results in this paper can serve as a stepping stone to such schemes.

\displaystyle\;=-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{H}}|\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}}\!\Big[\log_{2}\mathbb{E}_{\bm{x}}\big[1\{\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{x}})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\}\big]\Big]

\displaystyle\;=-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{H}}|\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}}\!\!\left[\log_{2}\frac{1}{4^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}\sum_{k=1}^{4^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}1\{\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\}\!\right]

\displaystyle\;=2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{H}}|\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}}\!\!\left[\log_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{4^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}1\{\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\}\!\right]

where the last step follows from Jensen’s inequality. Since the expectation of an indicator function yields the probability of the underlying event,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{H}}\big[\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}})\big]\geq 2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}

(88)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad-\log_{2}\sum_{k=1}^{4^{N_{\mathrm{t}}}}{\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}|\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\big]

with

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}|\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\big]

(89)

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=\frac{{\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\,\cap\,\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\big]}{{\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\big]}

\displaystyle\qquad\quad=4^{N_{\mathrm{r}}}\,{\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\,\cap\,\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\big].

As the channel has IID entries, letting
{\bm{h}}
be an arbitrary row of
{\bm{H}}
,

{\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\,\cap\,\text{sgn}({\bm{H}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})={\bm{\mathsf{y}}}_{1}\big]=(P_{\cap})^{N_{\mathrm{r}}}

\displaystyle P_{\cap}

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}({\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k})=(1+\mathrm{j})\,\cap\,\text{sgn}({\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1})=(1+\mathrm{j})\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\big[\text{sgn}(\Re\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\})=1\,\cap\,\text{sgn}(\Im\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\})=1

\displaystyle\quad\,\cap\,\text{sgn}(\Re\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1}\})=1\,\cap\,\text{sgn}(\Im\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1}\})=1\big]

\displaystyle={\mathbb{P}}\big[\Re\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}>0\,\cap\,\Im\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}\}>0

\displaystyle\quad\,\cap\,\Re\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1}\}>0\,\cap\,\Im\{{\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{1}\}>0\big].

(92)

Defining
a_{k}={\bm{h}}{\bm{\mathsf{x}}}_{k}
and

\displaystyle P_{\cap}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}\!\!\!\!\int_{0}^{\infty}f_{\Re\{a_{k}\}\Im\{a_{k}\}\Re\{a_{1}\}\Im\{a_{1}\}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\xi)

(95)

with

and

the respective number of entries of
\Re\{a_{k}\}
and
\Im\{a_{k}\}
that are
-1
, the rest of their entries (along with all the entries of
\Re\{a_{1}\}
and
\Im\{a_{1}\}

+1
. Most importantly, because the entries of
{\bm{h}}
are IID, the position of those

\Re\{a_{k}\}
,

and

\text{erfc}(\cdot)

Returning to (

), and accounting for the number of indices

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\bm{H}}\big[\mathcal{H}({\bm{y}})\big]

\displaystyle\geq 2{N_{\mathrm{t}}}-2{N_{\mathrm{r}}}

\displaystyle f_{\Re\{a_{k}\}\Im\{a_{k}\}\Re\{a_{1}\}\Im\{a_{1}\}}(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\xi)=\frac{1}{8\pi^{2}}\frac{1}{i({N_{\mathrm{t}}}-i)+j({N_{\mathrm{t}}}-j)}\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{4}\frac{[\alpha\;\beta\;\gamma\;\xi]\,\bm{\Sigma}^{-1}_{k}\,[\alpha\;\beta\;\gamma\;\xi]^{\rm T}}{i({N_{\mathrm{t}}}-i)+j({N_{\mathrm{t}}}-j)}\right)

) reduces to

For

For fixed

For
