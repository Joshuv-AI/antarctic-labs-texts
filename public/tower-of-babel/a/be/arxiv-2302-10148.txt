Logical limit laws for Mallows random permutations
Tobias Müller, Fiona Skerman, Teun W. Verstraaten

Abstract
A random permutation Π n \Pi_{n} of { 1 , … , n } \{1,\dots,n\} follows the Mallows ⁡ ( n , q ) \Mallows(n,q) distribution with parameter q > 0 q>0 if ℙ ⁡ ( Π n = π ) {\mathbb{P}}\left(\Pi_{n}=\pi\right) is proportional to q inv ⁡ ( π ) q^{\inv(\pi)} for all π \pi . Here inv ⁡ ( π ) ≔ | { i < j : π ⁡ ( i ) > π ⁡ ( j ) } | \inv(\pi)\coloneqq|\{i<j:\pi(i)>\pi(j)\}| denotes the number of inversions of π \pi . We consider properties of permutations that can be expressed by the sentences of two different logical languages. Namely, the theory of one bijection ( 𝖳𝖮𝖮𝖡 \TOOB ), which describes permutations via a single binary relation, and the theory of two orders ( 𝖳𝖮𝖳𝖮 \TOTO ), where we describe permutations by two total orders. We say that the convergence law holds with respect to one of these languages if, for every sentence φ \varphi in the language, the probability ℙ ⁡ ( Π n ​ satisfies ​ φ ) {\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\text{ satisfies }\varphi) converges to a limit as n → ∞ n\to\infty . If moreover that limit is ∈ { 0 , 1 } \in\{0,1\} for all sentences, then the zero–one law holds. We will show that with respect to 𝖳𝖮𝖮𝖡 \TOOB the Mallows ⁡ ( n , q ) \Mallows(n,q) distribution satisfies the zero–one law when 0 < q < 1 0<q<1 is fixed, and for fixed q > 1 q>1 the convergence law fails. (In the case when q = 1 q=1 Compton [ 15 ] has shown the convergence law holds but not the zero–one law.) We will prove that with respect to 𝖳𝖮𝖳𝖮 \TOTO the Mallows ⁡ ( n , q ) \Mallows(n,q) distribution satisfies the convergence law but not the zero–one law for any fixed q ≠ 1 q\neq 1 , and that if q = q ⁡ ( n ) q=q(n) satisfies 1 − 1 / log ∗ ⁡ n < q < 1 + 1 / log ∗ ⁡ n 1-1/\log^{*}n<q<1+1/\log^{*}n then Mallows ⁡ ( n , q ) \Mallows(n,q) fails the convergence law. Here log ∗ \log^{*} denotes the discrete inverse of the tower function.

In the present paper we will study the Mallows distribution from the perspective of first order logic.
Given a sequence of random permutations
(\Pi_{n})_{n\geq 1}

with respect to some fixed logical language describing permutations
if the limit
\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Pi_{n}\models\varphi\right)
exists, for every sentence
\varphi
in the language.
Here and in the rest of the paper the notation

\varphi

\pi

If this limit is either
0
or

holds.
Following

2
]

\TOOB

\TOTO

Here we give an informal overview of them. More precise definitions follow in Section

In
\TOOB

using variables representing the elements of the domain of the permutation, the usual quantifiers
\exists,\forall
,
the usual logical connectives
\vee,\wedge,\neg,\dots
, parantheses and two binary relations

Here

holds if and only if
\pi(x)=y

We are for instance able to query in
\TOOB
if a permutation has a fixed point by

\exists x:R(x,x).

As is shown
[
2
]
(Proposition 3 and the comment following it), we cannot express by a
\TOOB

sentence whether or not a permutation contains the pattern
231
.
(A permutation

contains the pattern

\pi(k)<\pi(i)<\pi(j)

The logical language

Instead of the relation

That is,

if and only if
\pi(x)<\pi(y)

Proof Sketch of Theorem

The proof of Theorem

Firstly, given any sentence

Secondly, it follows from the Hanf-Locality Theorem for bounded degree structures (stated as Theorem

that for any fixed

For

by Jimmy He together with the first and last author of the current paper. They show that there are positive constants

Proof Sketch of Theorem

(i)

The proof of Theorem
1.2

(i)
is inspired by the approach taken by Lynch

[

to show a convergence law for random strings having certain letter distributions.
We consider some
\TOTO

\varphi
having quantifier depth

Writing
\pi\equiv_{d}\sigma

\pi
and
\sigma
agree on all
\TOTO

note that
\equiv_{d}

We now rely on a sampling procedure for generating a sequence
\Pi_{1},\Pi_{2},\ldots
of
\Mallows(n,q)

from a sequence

\Geo(1-q)

(The details of the construction will be given in Section

Taking a dynamic viewpoint, we can imagine exposing the values

sequence of permutations that arises.
Foregoing complicating details, we can define in this manner a Markov chain on a countably infinite state
space that follows the equivalence class under
\equiv_{d}
of this sequence of permutations.
We show that we can the state space have finitely many recurrent classes,
each of them irreducible, aperiodic and positive recurrent, and that the chain will hit such a
class a.s.
Standard results on the convergence of Markov chains will give the convergence of the quantity
{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\varphi)
as
n\to\infty
.

3.1.1
Markov chains

We give a brief overview of some of the concepts related to Markov chains which will be used in
Section
5
. The definitions and results that follow are widely known and
can be found in most books on Markov chains or more general stochastic processes, see for
instance
[
41
]
,
[
28
]
or
[
48
]
.

Let
X_{n}
be a Markov chain with state space
{\mathcal{S}}
. Given
i,j\in{\mathcal{S}}
, if

{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}=j\,|\,X_{0}=i\right)>0
for some
n\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
we say that

j
is reachable from
i
.
We denote this by
i\to j
. If
i\to j
and
j\to i
then we say that
the states
i
and
j
communicate

and we write
i\leftrightarrow j
. The relation
\leftrightarrow
is an equivalence relation, the equivalence classes under
\leftrightarrow
will be called
communicating classes
. For every state
i\in{\mathcal{S}}
we have
i\leftrightarrow i
. In the case that the entire state space is a single communicating class we call the Markov chain
irreducible
. The evolution of a Markov chain depends on the initial distribution over the states.
We use the notations
{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\cdot)
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{i}(\cdot)
for probabilities and expectations
with respect to the Markov chain started in state
X_{0}=i
. If
X_{0}
is distributed according to some distribution

\mu_{0}
over
{\mathcal{S}}
then we write
{\mathbb{P}}_{\mu_{0}}
and
{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu_{0}}
.

For any
i\in{\mathcal{S}}
we define the
hitting time

\tau_{i}:=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}=i\}

to be the first time that the Markov chain is in state
i
, and we
define the
first visit time

\tau^{+}_{i}:=\inf\{n\geq 1:X_{n}=i\}
to be the first time after zero that the Markov chain is in state
i
.
If
{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(\tau^{+}_{i}<\infty)=1
then we say that the state
i
is
recurrent
, otherwise we say that
it is
transient
.
If additionally
{\mathbb{E}}_{i}\tau^{+}_{i}<\infty
then we say that
i
is
positive recurrent
.
If
{\mathcal{S}}
is finite then recurrence implies positive recurrence. If
i
is transient, then all
j
in the communicating
class containing
i
are transient, and similarly if
i
is (positive) recurrent.
Thus we will talk about positive recurrent classes, etc.
If
X_{n}
is in a recurrent class for some
n
, then
X_{n+k}
is
contained in this class for all
k\geq 0
.
The
period
of a state is the greatest common divisor of the set
\{n\geq 1:{\mathbb{P}}_{i}(X_{n}=i)>0\}
.
A state is
aperiodic
if the period equals one. A communicating class or chain is called aperiodic if all of its states
are.
Proofs for the following two results are given in Theorem 4 and Lemma 5, respectively, in Section 6.3 of
[
28
]
.

Theorem 3.4
.

The state space
{\mathcal{S}}
of a Markov chain can be partitioned uniquely as

{\mathcal{S}}=T\cup C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\ldots

(12)

where
T
is the set of transient states, and the
C_{i}
are recurrent communicating classes.

Lemma 3.5
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is finite, then at least one state is recurrent and all recurrent states are positive recurrent.

Lemma 3.6
.

If
{\mathcal{S}}
is finite then with probability
1
the chain
X_{n}
will be in a recurrent state for some
n\geq 0
.

Proof.

Let
{\mathcal{S}}=T\cup C_{1}\cup C_{2}\cup\ldots
be as in Theorem
3.4
.
For each of the finitely many
i\in T
there is a positive probability that the chain moves from
i
to a
state in
{\mathcal{S}}\setminus T
in at most
|T|
steps. Then
{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in T\right)\leq Kc^{n}
for
some constants
K>0
and
0<c<1
.
By the Borel–Cantelli Lemma,
X_{n}
is in
T
for only finitely many
n
with probability one, so
that it must visit a recurrent state.
∎

The following theorem is the Markov chain convergence theorem, see e.g.
[
19
]
Theorem 7.6.4 for a proof (note that in
[
19
]
, what we call irreducibility is called strong irreducibility).

Theorem 3.7

(Markov chain convergence theorem)
.

Consider an irreducible, aperiodic and positive recurrent Markov chain
on a countable state space
{\mathcal{S}}
.
There exists a unique invariant probability measure
\pi
over
{\mathcal{S}}
such that for every probability measure
\mu_{0}
over

{\mathcal{S}}
:

d_{\operatorname{TV}}(X_{n},\pi)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0

(13)

where
X_{0}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\mu_{0}
and
(X_{n})_{n\geq 0}
denotes the evolving sequence of states of the Markov chain.

We will need the following result in Section
5
.
It is alluded to in many texts on Markov chains when discussing the convergence behavior
of a Markov chains that are not irreducible. We have not found an appropriate reference however.
So we present a short proof.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
X_{n}
be a Markov chain on a countable state space
{\mathcal{S}}
, and let
{\mathcal{M}}\subseteq{\mathcal{S}}
be an aperiodic and positively
recurrent communicating class.
Then the limit

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in A\right)

(14)

exists for all
A\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
.

Proof.

We define
\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}:=\inf\{n\geq 0:X_{n}\in{\mathcal{M}}\}
.
We have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in A\right)=\sum\limits_{j=0}^{\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in A\,|\,\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j\right){\mathbb{P}}\left(\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j\right).

(15)

(We use that
X_{n}\in A
can only happen if the chain enters
{\mathcal{M}}
at some point.)
Conditioning on
\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j
induces a probability distribution
\nu_{j}
over the states in
{\mathcal{M}}

where for
B\subseteq{\mathcal{M}}
we have
\nu_{j}(B)={\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{j}\in B\,|\,\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j\right)
.
Consider the Markov chain we get by discarding all states outside of
{\mathcal{M}}
and
leaving the transition probabilities inside
{\mathcal{M}}
as is. This is a valid chain, since
states in
{\mathcal{M}}
can only reach states inside
{\mathcal{M}}
, as
{\mathcal{M}}
is recurrent.
This “reduced chain” is clearly aperiodic, irreducible and positive recurrent.
So by Theorem
3.7
there exists
a unique distribution
\pi
over
{\mathcal{M}}
such that for all
j\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}

\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in A\,|\,\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j\right)=\lim_{n\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}}_{\nu_{j}}\left(X_{n-j}\in A\right)=\pi(A).

(16)

Now

\left|{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in A\right)-\pi(A)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}<\infty)\right|\leq\sum_{j=0}^{\infty}\left|{\mathbb{P}}\left(X_{n}\in A\,|\,\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j\right)-\pi(A)\right|\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j)\xrightarrow[n\to\infty]{}0,

where the limit follows by dominated convergence (each term of the summand tends to zero separately,
and the sum is bounded by
\sum_{j}{\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}=j)={\mathbb{P}}(\tau_{{\mathcal{M}}}<\infty)\leq 1
).
∎

3.3
First–order logic

Here we briefly cover some of the concepts from logic and model theory that we will need in our proofs.
For a more complete descriptions, see for instance Chapter 1 of
[

]
.

Given a set of relation symbols
R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}
with associated arities
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
, we define
{\mathcal{L}}_{R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}}
as the first–order language consisting of all
first–order formulas built from the usual Boolean connectives together
with
R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}
. As an example, for a single binary relation
R
, we may consider the formula
\varphi_{0}(x)\in{\mathcal{L}}_{R}
defined as

\varphi_{0}(x)\coloneqq R(x,x)\wedge\neg\exists y:(\neg(x=y)\wedge R(y,y)).

(32)

A
free variable
of a formula
\varphi
is a variable that does not have a quantifier.
A formula with no free variables is called a
sentence
.
Given
R_{1},\ldots,R_{k}
with arities
a_{1},\ldots,a_{k}
, a
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{k})
–structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
is a
tuple
(A,R_{1}^{\mathfrak{A}},\ldots,R_{k}^{\mathfrak{A}})
where
A
is a set, called the
domain of
{\mathfrak{A}}
, and each
R_{j}^{{\mathfrak{A}}}
is a relation of arity
a_{j}
over
A
.
We will routinely write simply
R_{j}
for
R_{j}^{\mathfrak{A}}
and talk about structures instead of
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{k})
–structures.
If a structure
{\mathfrak{A}}
satisfies a sentence
\varphi
then we write
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi
. Similarly, if
i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}

are elements in the domain of
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
\varphi(x_{1},\ldots,x_{k})
is a formula
whose free variables are
x_{1},\ldots,x_{k}
then we write
({\mathfrak{A}},i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\models\varphi
if
\varphi
is
satisfied by
{\mathfrak{A}}
under the assignment
x_{j}\mapsto i_{j}
.
We have for instance that
({\mathfrak{A}},i)\models\varphi_{0}
precisely when the element
x
is the unique
element satisfying
R(x,x)
in
{\mathfrak{A}}
.
An
atomic formula
is a formula without quantifiers and Boolean connectives.

Two
(R_{1},\ldots,R_{k})
–structures
{\mathfrak{A}},{\mathfrak{B}}
with domain

and
B
respectively are called

if there is a bijection

such that

(i_{1},\ldots,i_{k})\in R_{j}^{{\mathfrak{A}}}\iff(f(i_{1}),\ldots,f(i_{k}))\in R_{j}^{{\mathfrak{B}}}\qquad\text{ for all $i_{1},\ldots,i_{k}\in A$ and all $R_{j}$.}

(33)

The following is a well–known result on isomorphic structures, see e.g. Theorem W of
[
51
]
for a proof.

Lemma 3.16
.

If
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}
are isomorphic, then for all first–order sentences
\varphi
we have
{\mathfrak{A}}\models\varphi
if and only if
{\mathfrak{B}}\models\varphi
.

We recursively define the quantifier depth of a first–order sentence
\varphi
, denoted
D(\varphi)
, as follows:

•

For any atomic formula
\varphi
we set
D(\varphi)=0
;

•

For any formula
\varphi
we set
D(\neg\varphi)=D(\varphi)
;

•

For any two formulas
\varphi,\psi
we set
D(\varphi\wedge\psi)=D(\varphi\vee\psi)=\max\{D(\varphi),D(\psi)\}
;

•

For a formula
\psi(x)
with a free variable

we set
D(\exists x:\psi(x))=D(\forall x:\psi(x))=D(\psi(x))+1
.

We say that two structures
{\mathfrak{A}}
and
{\mathfrak{B}}
are

–equivalent
(notation :

)
if

We also briefly mention
second–order logic
. In second–order logic, formulas can now also take arguments that are relations, and we can quantify over relations. An example of such a formula is

\xi(R)\coloneqq\forall x\,(\neg R(x,x)\wedge(\exists!y:R(x,y)\wedge R(y,x))),

where we use the shorthand

to mean that there exists a unique such

{\exists!x:\psi(x)}\coloneqq\exists x:(\psi(x)\wedge\forall y\ (\psi(y)\rightarrow y=x))
. The formula

is satisfied by a binary relation

if and only if
R
encodes a perfect matching. Moreover, in second–order logic we can quantify over relations. So we may write the sentence
\varphi\coloneqq\exists R:\xi(R)
. A finite structure satisfies

if and only if its domain has even cardinality.

3.3.1
\mathsf{TOOB}
and
\mathsf{TOTO}

We define the

(40)

Lemma 3.19
.

Let
\varphi(\underline{x})\in\TOTO
be a formula with
k\geq 0
free variables, and
let
\psi(y)\in\TOTO
be a formula with one free variable. There exists a formula
\varphi^{\psi}(\underline{x})\in\TOTO

that for all

\pi\in S_{n}
and all

\underline{i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{k})in[n]^{k}

(\pi,\underline{i})\models\varphi^{\psi}(\underline{x})\quad\text{ if and only if }\quad\pi\models\psi(i_{1})\wedge\dots\wedge\psi(i_{k})\text{ and }(\pi^{*},\underline{i})\models\varphi(\underline{x}),

where
\pi^{*}=\rk(\pi(a_{1}),\ldots,\pi(a_{m}))
with

and

\{a_{1},\dots,a_{m}\}=\{a\in[n]:(\pi,a)\models\psi(y)\}

Proof.

The proof is by induction on the structure of the formula
\varphi
, starting with the case when
it is an atomic formula.
Every atomic formula is of the form

, for
R\in\{=,<_{1},<_{2}\}

In this case we define
\varphi^{\psi}(\underline{x})
simply as
\psi(x_{1})\wedge\psi(x_{2})\wedge R(x_{1},x_{2})
.
This clearly does the trick for atomic formulas.

For formulas constructed from smaller formulas using one of the logical connectives, we
define
(\neg\varphi)^{\psi}
as
\neg(\varphi^{\psi})
and

(\varphi\vee\psi)^{\psi}
as
\varphi^{\psi}\vee\psi^{\psi}
and similarly for
\wedge,\rightarrow,\leftrightarrow
.
And, if
\varphi
starts with a quantifier then we set

\left(\exists x:\varphi(x)\right)^{\psi}:=\exists x:\psi(x)\wedge\varphi^{\psi}(x),\quad\left(\forall x:\varphi(x)\right)^{\psi}:=\forall x:\psi(x)\rightarrow\varphi^{\psi}(x).

As the reader can readily check the obtained formula has the behaviour described by the lemma statement.
∎

Lemma 3.20
.

Let

Proof.

As in the proof Lemma

3.4
Two rapidly growing functions

The
tower function
is defined as

T(n)=2^{2^{\cdot^{\cdot^{2}}}}

(44)

where the tower of
2
’s has height
n
.
It may also be defined recursively by
T(0)=1
and
T(i)=2^{T(i-1)}
for
i\geq 1
.

Similarly, we define the
wowzer function

W(n)
by

W(0)=1,\qquad\text{ and }\qquad W(n)=T(W(n-1))\quad\text{ for $n\geq 1$}.

(45)

The function
W(\cdot)
escalates rapidly.
We have
W(1)=T(1)=2
,
W(2)=T(2)=4
,
W(3)=T(4)=65536
, and
W(4)=T(65536)
is a tower of
2
’s of height
65536
.

The previous two functions are part of a larger sequence of functions called the hyperoperation
sequence, see e.g.
[
27
]
. The tower function is sometimes known under the name tetration and the wowzer function under the name pentation. Knuth also describes these functions in
[
38
]
as part of a larger hierarchy in terms of arrow notation.

The
log–star
function
\log^{*}n
is the “discrete inverse” of the Tower function.
It can be defined by

\log^{*}n:=\min\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:T(k)\geq n\}.

(46)

We have
\log^{*}(T(n))=n
for all
n\geq 0
.
Also note that, phrased differently,
\log^{*}n
is the number of times we need to iterate the base two logarithm, starting from

n
to reach a number less than
1
.

We define the
\log^{**}
function as

\log^{**}n:=\min\{k\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}:W(k)\geq n\}.

(47)

The
\log^{**}n
function grows incredibly slowly.
Although the wowzer function is part of a larger hierarchy of functions as
mentioned, we have not found the function
\log^{**}
used anywhere in the literature.
We emphasize that the notation
\log^{**}n
is our own and may not be standard.

Although the
\log^{**}
function is not strictly increasing, we do have the following simple monotonicity principle.

(Here and in the rest of the paper, for
f
a function and
i
an integer,

f^{(i)}=\underbrace{f\circ\dots\circ f}_{i\times},

denotes the
i
-fold iteration of
f
.)

Lemma 3.21
.

For all
i\in{\mathbb{Z}}_{\geq 0}
and integers
x,y
, if
W^{(i)}(x)<y
then
x<(\log^{**})^{(i)}(y)
.

Proof.

We use induction. The base case
i=0
holds trivially. So suppose that
W^{(i+1)}(x)=W(W^{(i)}(x))<y
. From (
47
) we immediately obtain that
W^{(i)}(x)<\log^{**}y
, and the result follows by induction.
∎

Lemma 3.22
.

For all
m>2
we have

T^{(3)}\circ W^{(2)}(m)<W^{(2)}(m+1).

(48)

Proof.

By the definition of

we have
T^{(3)}(W(W(m)))=W(W(m)+3)

(49)

But

. As

∎

\alpha

\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}
the first visit time of
L_{i}
to
\alpha
.
That
\alpha
is positively recurrent for

means that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}<\infty
. We need to
deduce that
{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}<\infty
as well.

Let
\delta>0
be small but fixed, to be determined in the course of the proof.
We have

{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(\tau_{\alpha}^{+}>n)\leq{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}>\delta n)+{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(T_{\left\lfloor\delta n\right\rfloor}>n).

Note that the second probability in the RHS does not depend on the starting state.
For convenience we’ll write
m:=\left\lfloor\delta n\right\rfloor
.
Recall that

T_{m}=X_{1}+\dots+X_{m}
where
X_{1},\dots,X_{m}
are i.i.d. and distributed like
T_{1}
.
We see that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(T_{m}>n)&=&{\mathbb{P}}(T_{m}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{m}>n-{\mathbb{E}}T_{m})\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(T_{m}-m{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}>n-m{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})\\
&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}(T_{m}-m{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}>n/2)\\
&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}\left(\left(T_{m}-m{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}\right)^{4}>n^{4}/16\right)\\
&\leq&\frac{16{\mathbb{E}}\left(T_{m}-m{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}\right)^{4}}{n^{4}}.\end{array}

where the third line holds since
{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}<\infty
by Lemma
3.15
and
assuming the constant
\delta
is chosen sufficiently small. The last line uses Markov’s inequality.
Now note that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}\left(T_{m}-m{\mathbb{E}}T_{1}\right)^{4}&=&{\mathbb{E}}\left((X_{1}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})+\dots+(X_{m}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})\right)^{4}\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{m}\sum_{j=1}^{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}{\mathbb{E}}\left((X_{i}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})\cdot(X_{j}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})\cdot(X_{k}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})\cdot(X_{\ell}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&m{\mathbb{E}}(X_{1}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})^{4}+3m(m-1)\left({\mathbb{E}}(X_{1}-{\mathbb{E}}T_{1})^{2}\right)^{2}\\
&=&O(n^{2}),\end{array}

again using that all moments of
T_{1}
are finite (and that the
X_{i}
are i.i.d. distributed like
T_{1}
).
It follows that

{\mathbb{P}}(T_{m}>n)=O(n^{-2}).

This gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}\tau_{\alpha}^{+}&=&\sum_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(\tau_{\alpha}^{+}>n)\\
&\leq&\sum_{n}{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}>\delta n)+\sum_{n}O(n^{-2})\\
&\leq&\left\lceil 1/\delta\right\rceil\cdot\sum_{k}{\mathbb{P}}_{\alpha}(\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}>k)+O(1)\\
&=&\left\lceil 1/\delta\right\rceil\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\alpha}\tilde{\tau}_{\alpha}^{+}+O(1)\\
&<&\infty,\end{array}

using a standard formula for the expectation of non-negative integer valued random variables.
We’ve established that
\alpha
is positively recurrent. Therefore, so are all states in
the same communicating class.

For the fourth claim, consider a recurrent class containing
([\pi]_{d},s_{\text{empty}})
for some permutation
\pi
.
For all
n,t\geq 1
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(M_{n+t}=([\pi\oplus\operatorname{id}_{t}]_{d},s_{\text{empty}})\,|\,M_{n}=([\pi]_{d},s_{\text{empty}})\right)\geq(1-q)^{t}>0.

(54)

In particular the recurrent class contains both
([\pi\oplus\operatorname{id}_{2^{d}-1}]_{d},s_{\text{empty}})
and

([\pi\oplus\operatorname{id}_{2^{d}}]_{d},s_{\text{empty}})
, where
M_{n}
may transition from the former to the latter in one step
with positive probability. By Propositions
5.1
and
5.2
we have
[\pi\oplus\operatorname{id}_{2^{d}-1}]_{d}=[\pi\oplus\operatorname{id}_{2^{d}}]_{d}
so that
the class is indeed aperiodic.

For the fifth claim, note the probability that
M_{n}
is never equal to an element
({\mathcal{E}},s_{\text{empty}})
of
a recurrent class is exactly the probability that
L_{i}
is never equal to a recurrent state
({\mathcal{E}},s_{\text{empty}})
.
This probability is zero by Lemma
3.6
.
∎

6.1
Arithmetic on sets in second–order logic

We move away from permutations for the moment, and
consider a finite set
S
equipped with a total order
<
.
Our aim will be to determine a second–order sentence
\mathsf{Parity}
such that

S\models\mathsf{Parity}
if and only if
\log^{**}\log^{**}|S|
is even.

Given that there is a total order
<
, we can identity
S
with
[n]
in the obvious way, where of course

n:=|S|
.
Note that we can express that
i=1
in a (first order) logical formula via
\neg(\exists x:x<i)
.
Similarly we can express that
i=n
, and that
i=k
or
i=n-k
for any fixed
k
.
In Section
3.3.1
we defined the formulas
\mathsf{succ}_{1}^{(k)}
using the
<_{1}
relation in
\TOTO
.
We will use
\mathsf{succ}^{(j)}
to denote analogue of the formulas
\mathsf{succ}_{1}^{(j)}
with
<_{1}
replaced by
<
.
For clarity, we point out that
\mathsf{succ}^{(j)}(x,y)
expresses that
y=x+j
.

Our sentence
\mathsf{Parity}
will begin with the existential quantification over four binary relations
R_{D},R_{E},R_{T}
and

R_{W}
. Here the subscript
D
stands for “double”,
E
for (base two) “exponential”,

T
for “tower”, and
W
for “wowser”.
We want to demand that these relations are such that:

\begin{array}[]{rl}R_{D}(i,j)&\iff j=2i,\\
R_{E}(i,j)&\iff j=2^{i},\\
R_{T}(i,j)&\iff j=T(i),\\
R_{W}(i,j)&\iff j=W(i),\end{array}

(59)

where
T(i)
and
W(i)
denote the tower and wowser functions defined in Section
3.4
.
For
R_{D}
, the following two formulas axiomatize that
R_{D}
is as required:

\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\,i,j:\left(\neg\exists x:(x<i)\right)\leftrightarrow\left(R_{D}(i,j)\leftrightarrow\mathsf{succ}(i,j)\right)\\
\forall\,i,j:(\exists x:x<i)\leftrightarrow\left(R_{D}(i,j)\leftrightarrow\left(\exists{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}:\mathsf{succ}(i^{\prime},i)\wedge\mathsf{succ}^{(2)}(j^{\prime},j)\wedge R_{D}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\right)\right).\end{array}

(60)

(Translated into more intuitive/informal terms, the first line says that if
i
is the first element of
S
, then

R_{D}(i,j)
if and only if
j=i+1
.
The second line says that if
i
is not the first element of
S
then

R_{D}(i,j)
if and only if
R_{D}(i-1,j-2)
.)

For
R_{E}
, we can write:

\begin{array}[]{l}\forall\,i,j:\left(\neg\exists x:(x<i)\right)\leftrightarrow\left(R_{E}(i,j)\leftrightarrow R_{D}(i,j)\right)\\
\forall\,i,j:(\exists x:x<i)\leftrightarrow\left(R_{E}(i,j)\leftrightarrow\left(\exists{i^{\prime},j^{\prime}}:\mathsf{succ}(i^{\prime},i)\wedge R_{D}(j^{\prime},j)\wedge R_{E}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})\right)\right).\end{array}

(61)

(The first line says that if
i
is the first element of
S
then

R_{E}(i,j)
if and only if
j=2i
.
The second line says that if
i
is not the first element then

R_{E}(i,j)
if and only if there are
i^{\prime},j^{\prime}
such that
i=i^{\prime}+1
and

j=2j^{\prime}
and
R_{E}(i^{\prime},j^{\prime})
.)

It is easily seen that, to express that the relation
R_{T}
is as desired, we can substitute in (
61
)

R_{D}
and
R_{E}
by
R_{E}
and
R_{T}
, respectively.
Similarly, to express that
R_{W}
is as desired, we can substitute
R_{D}
and
R_{E}
by
R_{T}
and
R_{W}
, respectively.

The conjunction of all the axioms we’ve obtained
gives a formula
\mathsf{Arith}(R_{D},R_{E},R_{T},R_{W})
that is satisfied
if and only if the relations
R_{D},R_{E},R_{T},R_{W}
correctly encode the target arithmetic functions.

Given such relations we now want to express that
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is even, where
n:=|S|
.
Suppose that there is an element
x\in S
for which there exist

y,z\in S
such that

x=W(y)\quad\text{ and }\quad y=W(z).

(62)

Then
\log^{**}\log^{**}x=z
. Now let
x
be the largest element for which such
y
and
z
exist, certainly
x\leq n
.
Now, if
x=n
, which we can check using
<
, then
z=\log^{**}\log^{**}n
and we can query whether or not this is even by

\exists w:R_{D}(w,z)
. If instead
x<n
, then
\log^{**}(\log^{**}n)=z+1
, and we can again query whether
this is even by
\neg\exists w:R_{D}(w,z)
. All of this can be formalized in a second–order
formula
\mathsf{EvenSize}(R_{D},R_{E},R_{T},R_{W})
in a straightforward manner.
So a second–order sentence
\mathsf{Parity}
of the form

\mathsf{Parity}\coloneqq\exists R_{D},R_{E},R_{T},R_{W}:\mathsf{Arith}(R_{D},R_{E},R_{T},R_{W})\wedge\mathsf{EvenSize}(R_{D},R_{E},R_{T},R_{W})

(63)

exists such that
S\models\mathsf{Parity}
if and only if
\log^{**}\log^{**}|S|
is even.
Here
\mathsf{Arith}
expresses that the relations define the correct arithmetic operations, and
\mathsf{EvenSize}
uses
these relations to express that
\log^{**}\log^{**}|S|
is even.

In broad lines we will do the following in the next few sections: on
a random permutation
\Pi_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,1)
we will define directed graphs using
\TOTO
formulas,
whose vertices are intervals
\{i,i+1,\dots,i+j\}\subseteq[n]
.
These directed graphs will be so abundant that, with probability
1-o_{n}(1)
, we can find four such graphs,
all defined on the same set
S
of vertices/intervals of cardinality roughly
|S|\approx{\log\log n}
, that
encode the correct relations
R_{D},R_{E},R_{T}
and
R_{W}
on this set.
We will then be able to exploit the fact that
\log^{**}(\log^{**}(\log(\log n)))
oscillates
between being even and odd indefinitely, giving the desired non–convergence.

6.4
The probabilistic component

For
B\subseteq[n]
and

an injection, we will write

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}_{\tau}(.)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{P}}(.\,|\,\Pi_{n}(i)=\tau(i)\text{ for all $i\in B$}),

(75)

\displaystyle{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}(.)

\displaystyle\coloneqq{\mathbb{E}}(.\,|\,\Pi_{n}(i)=\tau(i)\text{ for all $i\in B$}).

(76)

Recall that we defined

\displaystyle w_{k}(A)

\displaystyle:=|\{i:\{\Pi_{n}(i),\Pi_{n}(i)+1,\dots,\Pi_{n}(i)+k-1\}\subseteq\Pi_{n}(A)\}|.

(77)

We define

Y_{i}(A)\coloneqq\mathbf{1}_{\{\{i,\ldots,i+k-1\}\,\subseteq\,\Pi_{n}(A)\}}.

(78)

We note that

Lemma 6.4
.

If
k
is fixed, and
A,B\subseteq[n]
are disjoint sets of cardinality
1\ll|A|\ll n
and
|B|\,\leq\,n/\log n
, and

\tau:B\to[n]
is an arbitrary injection, then

\begin{array}[]{l}{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A)=(1+o(1))n^{1-k}|A|^{k},\\
\\
{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A)^{2}\leq\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A)\right)^{2}+(2k-1)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A).\end{array}

(where the
o(.)
is uniform over all such
B
and
\tau
.)

Proof.

Let us denote by
J
the set of all
1\leq j\leq n-k+1
for which
\{j,\dots,j+k-1\}\cap\text{Im}(\tau)=\varnothing
.
For notational convenience, we will write
Y_{j}
instead of
Y_{j}(A)
.
We note that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}Y_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{|A|(|A|-1)\dots(|A|-k+1)}{(n-|B|)\dots(n-|B|-k+1)}&\text{ if $j\in J$, }\\
0&\text{ otherwise.}\end{array}\right..

Hence

{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A)=|J|\cdot\frac{|A|(|A|-1)\dots(|A|-k+1)}{(n-|B|)\dots(n-|B|-k+1)}=(1+o(1))\cdot n^{1-k}|A|^{k},

using that
|B|\,\leq\,n/\log n
and
(n-k(|B|+1))\leq|J|\leq n
. The
o(1)
term above is
uniform for all
B
by the uniform bound
B\,\leq\,n/\log n
.

For the second moment, we remark that

{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}Y_{i}Y_{j}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cl}\frac{|A|(|A|-1)\dots(|A|-2k+1)}{(n-|B|)\dots(n-|B|-2k+1)}&\text{ if $i,j\in J$ and $|i-j|>k$, }\\
0&\text{ if }i\not\in J\text{ or }j\not\in J,\\
\leq{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}Y_{i}&\text{ if }|i-j|\leq k.\end{array}\right.

(79)

and we also remark that

\frac{|A|(|A|-1)\dots(|A|-2k+1)}{(n-|B|)\dots(n-|B|-2k+1)}\leq\left(\frac{|A|(|A|-1)\dots(|A|-k+1)}{(n-|B|)\dots(n-|B|-k+1)}\right)^{2},

since
1\ll|A|,|B|\ll n
so that
(|A|-x)/(n-|B|-x)\leq(|A|-x-1)/(n-|B|-x-1)
for all
x=1,\dots,2k-1
.

We see that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A)^{2}&=&\sum_{i}\sum_{j}{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}Y_{i}Y_{j}\\[4.0pt]
&\leq&|J|^{2}\frac{|A|(|A|-1)\dots(|A|-2k+1)}{(n-|B|)\dots(n-|B|-2k+1)}+(2k-1)\sum_{i}{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}Y_{i}\\[4.0pt]
&\leq&\left({\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A)\right)^{2}+(2k-1)\cdot{\mathbb{E}}_{\tau}w_{k}(A).\end{array}

∎

1-n^{-4}<q<1
.
For
1\leq i\leq n
let
Z_{i}\sim\TGeo(n-i+1,1-q)
and
X_{i}\sim\Unif([n-i+1])
. Then

d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq 4\cdot n^{-3},

(101)

for
n
sufficiently large and all
1\leq i\leq n
.

Proof.

Let us write
m\coloneqq n-i+1
and
p_{j}:={\mathbb{P}}(Z_{i}=j)
. By (
9
) we may write

\displaystyle d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Z_{i},X_{i})=\sum_{j:p_{j}>1/m}\left(p_{j}-\frac{1}{m}\right).

(102)

Now note that

\frac{\max_{j}p_{j}}{\min_{j}p_{j}}=q^{-(m-1)}<\left(\frac{1}{1-n^{-4}}\right)^{m-1}<\left(\frac{1}{1-n^{-4}}\right)^{n}\leq\left(1+2n^{-4}\right)^{n}\leq e^{2n^{-3}}\leq 1+4n^{-3},

where the third inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large; the fourth inequality uses

1+x\leq e^{x}
, and; the last inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large, and uses the Taylor expansion for
e^{x}
.
It follows that

p_{j}\leq(1+4n^{-3})\cdot\frac{1}{m},

for all
j
. Filling this in to (
102
) gives
d_{\operatorname{TV}}(Z_{i},X_{i})\leq 4n^{-3}
as desired.
∎

Lemma 7.3
.

Let
q=q(n)
satisfy
|1-q|<n^{-4}
, and let
\Pi_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,q)
and
\Pi^{*}_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,1)
.
There is a coupling of
\Pi_{n}
and
\Pi^{*}_{n}

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Pi_{n}\neq\Pi_{n}^{*}\right)\leq 4n^{-2}
, for all sufficiently large
n

Proof.

If
q=1
, then the result is trivial.
We now assume that
1-n^{-4}<q<1
.

Let
\Pi_{n}
be generated by a sequence

of independent random variables
with

for
i\in[n]
(see Section
3.2
).
Let
\Pi_{n}^{*}
be generated in the same manner by a sequence

of independent

for
i\in[n]
.
By Lemma
7.2
and
3.3
we may couple
Z_{i}
and
X_{i}

{\mathbb{P}}\left(Z_{i}\neq X_{i}\right)\leq 4n^{-3},\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

(103)

This gives a coupling of

\Pi_{n}=\Pi^{*}_{n}

Finally we consider the case when

r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(n,1/q)

Finally we note that if

∎

7.2
Extending the

In this subsection we prove the following intermediate result.

Proposition 7.4
.

There is a sentence
\psi\in\TOTO
such that the following holds.
Suppose that
q=q(n)
satisfies
1-c/n<q<1+c/n
for some constant
c>0
.
For all
n
satisfying
W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)<\log\log\log n

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)=\begin{cases}1-O(n^{-.001})&\text{if }\log^{**}\log^{**}n\text{ is even},\\
O(n^{-.001})&\text{if }\log^{**}\log^{**}n\text{ is odd},\end{cases}

(106)

where the
O(n^{-.001})
terms can be taken uniform over all
sequences considered (but may depend on
c
).

(Again, the condition
W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)<\log\log\log n
is satisfied if

n
is of the form
n=W^{(2)}(m)
with
m>3
, by
Lemma
3.22
.)

For
\pi\in S_{n}
a permutation, we define

\Psi(\pi):=\min\{1\leq j\leq n:\text{ there exist $1\leq i_{1},i_{2}\leq j$ such that
$\pi(i_{2})=\pi(i_{1})+1$ }\}.

Letting
\Pi_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,q)
as usual, we define:

X_{n}:=\Psi(\Pi_{n}).

Before studying the behaviour of
X_{n}
, we first present a preparatory lemma.

Lemma 7.5
.

Suppose that
q=q(n)
satisfies
1-c/n<q<1
. There exist
c_{U}=c_{U}(c)>0
and
c_{L}=c_{L}(c)>0
such that
for
n
sufficiently large and all
1\leq m\leq n
and
A\subseteq[m]
we have

c_{L}\frac{|A|}{m}\leq{\mathbb{P}}\left(\TGeo(m,1-q)\in A\right)\leq c_{U}\frac{|A|}{m}.

(107)

Proof.

We fix
n
and some
m\in[n]
.
For convenience we again set
p_{j}:={\mathbb{P}}(Z_{i}=j)
.
We again have

\frac{\max_{j}p_{i}}{\min_{j}p_{j}}=q^{-(m-1)}\leq\left(\frac{1}{1-cn^{-1}}\right)^{n}\leq(1+2cn^{-1})^{n}\leq e^{2c},

where the penultimate inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large. Similarly

\frac{\min_{j}p_{j}}{\max_{j}p_{j}}=q^{m-1}\geq(1-cn^{-1})^{n}\geq\frac{1}{2}e^{-c},

where the last inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large.
We can conclude that

c_{L}\cdot\frac{1}{m}\leq p_{j}\leq c_{U}\cdot\frac{1}{m},

for all
j=1,\dots,m
, with
c_{L}:=\frac{1}{2}e^{-c},c_{U}:=e^{2c}
.
The result follows.
∎

Lemma 7.6
.

Let
q=q(n)
satisfy
1-cn^{-1}<q<1+cn^{-1}
for some
c>0
.
For every
\varepsilon>0
we have

{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{1/2-\varepsilon}\leq X_{n}\leq n^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right)>1-n^{-\varepsilon},

(108)

provided that
n\geq n_{0}
for some
n_{0}=n_{0}(c,\varepsilon)
depending on
\varepsilon
and
c
.

Proof.

We first assume
q\leq 1
.
For
i\geq 2
, let
E_{i}
denote the event that

\Pi_{n}(i)\not\in\{\Pi_{n}(1)-1,\Pi_{n}(1)+1,\dots,\Pi_{n}(i-1)-1,\Pi_{n}(i-1)+1\}=:A_{i}
.
We point out that if
E_{1},\dots,E_{i-1}
hold, then
i-1\leq|A_{i}|\leq 2i
.
Recalling the sampling procedure
24
for the Mallows model described in
Section
3.2
and using the
previous lemma, we find that for
i\geq 2
:

1-c_{U}\frac{2i}{n-i+1}\leq{\mathbb{P}}(E_{i}|E_{2}\cap\dots\cap E_{i-1})\leq 1-c_{L}\frac{i-1}{n-i+1}.

Writing
x:=\left\lfloor n^{1/2+\varepsilon}\right\rfloor
, we find that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(X>x)&=&{\mathbb{P}}(E_{2}\cap\dots\cap E_{x})\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(E_{2})\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(E_{3}|E_{2})\cdot\dots\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(E_{x}|E_{2}\cap\dots\cap E_{x-1})\\
&\leq&\prod_{i=2}^{x}\left(1-c_{L}\frac{i-1}{n-i+1}\right)\\
&\leq&\exp\left[-c_{L}\sum_{i=2}^{x}\frac{i-1}{n-i+1}\right]\\
&\leq&\exp\left[-c_{L}\cdot(2/n)\cdot{x\choose 2}\right]=\exp\left[-\Omega(n^{2\varepsilon})\right],\end{array}

where the third line holds assuming
n
is sufficiently large.

Writing
y:=\left\lceil n^{1/2-\varepsilon}\right\rceil
, we find that

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(X<y)&=&{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{E_{2}})+{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{E_{3}}|E_{2})+\dots+{\mathbb{P}}(\overline{E_{y}}|E_{2}\cap\dots\cap E_{y-1})\\
&\leq&\sum_{i=2}^{y}c_{U}\frac{2i}{n-i+1}\leq\frac{c_{U}}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{y}i\\
&=&\frac{c_{U}}{n}\cdot{y+1\choose 2}=O(n^{-2\varepsilon}).\end{array}

Combining these bounds completes the proof for the case when
q\leq 1
.
For the case when
1\leq q\leq 1+c/n
, we recall that

r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(n,1/q)
.
We also note that
1\geq 1/q\geq 1-cn^{-1}
and that

\Psi(\pi)=\Psi(r_{n}\circ\pi)
for all
\pi\in S_{n}
, by definition of
\Psi
.
So the result for
1\leq q\leq 1+c/n
follows immediately from the result for
1-cn^{-1}\leq q\leq 1
.
∎

We next define

Y_{n}:=\Psi\left(\rk(\Pi_{n}(1),\dots,\Pi_{n}(X_{n}))\right),

Z_{n}:=\Psi\left(\rk(\Pi_{n}(1),\dots,\Pi_{n}(Y_{n}))\right).

The next observation will allow us to make use of Lemma
7.6
also for
Y_{n}
and
Z_{n}
.

Lemma 7.7
.

For every
n\geq 3
,
2\leq k\leq n-1
and every
\pi\in S_{n}
we have

\Psi(\pi_{k})\leq\Psi(\pi_{k+1}),

(109)

where
\pi_{j}:=\rk(\pi(1),\ldots,\pi(j))
.

Proof.

This follows readily from the observation that, for all distinct
i_{1},i_{2}\leq k
,

\pi_{k+1}(i_{2})=\pi_{k+1}(i_{1})+1
implies that

\pi_{k}(i_{2})=\pi_{k}(i_{1})+1
.
∎

Lemma 7.8
.

Suppose that
q=q(n)
satisfies
1-c/n<q<1+c/n
for some constant
c>0
.
Then

{\mathbb{P}}\left(n^{.1}\leq Z_{n}\leq n^{.2}\right)>1-n^{-.001},

(110)

provided that
n\geq n_{0}
for some
n_{0}=n_{0}(c)
depending on
c
.

Proof.

We fix
\delta>0
, to be determined during the course of the proof.
For notational convenience, let us write

x:=\left\lceil n^{1/2-\delta}\right\rceil,y:=\left\lceil n^{1/4-\delta}\right\rceil,z:=\left\lceil n^{1/8-\delta}\right\rceil
.
Recall that by Lemma
3.9
, for each fixed
j
we have

\Pi_{j}^{*}:=\rk(\Pi_{n}(1),\dots,\Pi_{n}(j))\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(j,q)
.
Using also the previous two lemmas and the definitions of
X_{n},Y_{n},Z_{n}
, we find:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(Z<z)&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}<x)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Psi(\Pi_{x}^{*})<y)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Psi(\Pi_{y}^{*})<z)\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}<x)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Psi(\Pi_{x})<y)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Psi(\Pi_{y})<z)\\
&=&{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}<x)+{\mathbb{P}}(X_{x}<y)+{\mathbb{P}}(X_{y}<z)\\
&\leq&n^{-\delta}+x^{-\delta}+y^{-\delta}\\
&\leq&n^{-\delta/8},\end{array}

where the fourth line holds for
n
sufficiently large and we
use

\begin{array}[]{c}x^{1/2-\delta}=(1+o(1))n^{(1/2-\delta)^{2}}>y=(1+o(1))n^{1/4-\delta},\\
y^{1/2-\delta}=(1+o(1))n^{(1/2-\delta)^{3}}>z=(1+o(1))n^{1/8-\delta},\end{array}

and the last line holds for
n
sufficiently large.

Analogously we have,
writing
x^{\prime}:=\left\lfloor n^{1/2+\delta}\right\rfloor,y^{\prime}:=\left\lfloor n^{1/4+2\delta}\right\rfloor,z:=\left\lfloor n^{1/8+4\delta}\right\rfloor
:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(Z>z^{\prime})&\leq&{\mathbb{P}}(X_{n}>x^{\prime})+{\mathbb{P}}(X_{x^{\prime}}>y^{\prime})+{\mathbb{P}}(X_{y^{\prime}}>z^{\prime})\\
&\leq&n^{-\delta}+{x^{\prime}}^{-\delta}+{y^{\prime}}^{-\delta}\\
&\leq&n^{-\delta/8},\end{array}

for
n
sufficiently large.

Setting
\delta=.025
and combining the bounds gives the result, with some room to spare.
∎

We are almost ready to prove Proposition
7.4
.
But, we first point out that it is possible to express that
i\leq Z_{n}
in
\TOTO
.

Lemma 7.9
.

There is a formula
\varphi_{Z}(x)
in
\TOTO
with one free variable such that
for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and every

:

(\pi,i)\models\varphi_{Z}(x)\text{ if and only if }i\leq Z_{n}(\pi).

Proof.

We first show there is a formula
\varphi_{X}(x)
that expresses that
i\leq\Psi(\pi)

if
\pi\in S_{n},i\in[n]
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
.
This formula is given by

\forall y,z:\left((y<_{1}x)\wedge(z<_{1}x)\right)\implies\left(\neg\mathsf{succ}_{2}(y,z)\wedge\neg\mathsf{succ}_{2}(z,y)\right).

(“For every pair

we have
|\pi(y)-\pi(z)|\neq 1
”.)
Now let
\varphi_{Y}:=\varphi_{X}^{\varphi_{X}}
, the relativization of

\varphi_{X}
to
\varphi_{X}
, as provided by Lemma
3.19
.
Then, invoking that lemma,
(\pi,i)\models\varphi_{Y}(x)
if and only if
i\leq\Psi(\pi)
and

(\sigma,i)\models\varphi_{X}(x)
where
\sigma:=\rk(\pi(1),\dots,\pi(\Psi(\pi)))
.
In other words,
(\pi,i)\models\varphi_{Y}(x)
if and only if
i\leq Y_{n}(\pi)
.

Similarly, if we now set
\varphi_{Z}:=\varphi_{X}^{\varphi_{Y}}
then

(\pi,i)\models\varphi_{Z}(x)
if and only if

.
∎

Proof of Proposition
7.4
:

Let
\varphi
be the sentence provided by Proposition
7.1
and let
\varphi_{Z}
be the

.
Then the relativization

7.3
Lifting to

In this section we finalize the proof of Theorem

(ii)
.
Specifically, we will prove the following more detailed version of Theorem
1.2
Part
(ii)
.

Proposition 7.10
.

There exists a sentence
\xi\in\TOTO
such that, for all sequences
q=q(n)
satisfying

1-1/\log^{*}n<q<1+1/\log^{*}n
and all
n
satisfying
W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)<\log^{*}\log^{*}\log^{*}n

we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\xi)=\begin{cases}1-o(1)&\text{ if $\log^{**}\log^{**}n$ is even, }\\
o(1)&\text{ if $\log^{**}\log^{**}n$ is odd, }\end{cases},

where the error terms
o(1)
can be taken uniform over all sequences
q(n)
considered.

(Again, we note the condition
W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)<\log^{*}\log^{*}\log^{*}n
is for instance
satisfied for all
n
of the form

n=W^{(2)}(m)
with
m>3
, by Lemma
3.22
.)

The quantity
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)
will play an important role in the definition of the
\TOTO
property
\xi
.
We start with some preparatory lemmas on its behaviour.

Lemma 7.11
.

For every
c,\varepsilon>0
there exist constants
c_{1}=c_{1}(c,\varepsilon),c_{2}=c_{2}(c,\varepsilon)>0
and

n_{0}=n_{0}(c,\varepsilon)
such that for
n\geq n_{0}
and any sequence
q=q(n)
satisfying
1-1/\log^{*}n<q<1-c/n
,
the random permutation
\Pi_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,q)
satisfies

\displaystyle{\mathbb{P}}\left(\frac{c_{1}}{1-q}\leq\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\leq\frac{c_{2}}{1-q}\right)>1-\varepsilon.

(111)

Proof.

Recall from Section
3.2
that if
\Pi_{n}\sim\Mallows(n,q)
then also
\Pi_{n}^{-1}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Pi_{n}
.
In particular
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Pi_{n}(1)\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\TGeo(n,1-q)
,
where the last distributional equality follows from the sampling algorithm presented in
Section
3.2
.
Writing
x_{1}:=\left\lfloor c_{1}/(1-q)\right\rfloor
, we first note that
provided the constant
c_{1}>0
is chosen sufficiently small, we have

x_{1}<(c_{1}/c)\cdot n\leq n
.
We can thus write:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\leq\frac{c_{1}}{1-q}\right)=\sum_{i=1}^{x_{1}}\frac{q^{i-1}(1-q)}{1-q^{n}}=\frac{1-q^{x_{1}}}{1-q^{n}}<\frac{1-q^{x_{1}}}{1-(1-c/n)^{n}}<\frac{1-q^{x_{1}}}{1-e^{-c}}.

Now we note that

q^{x_{1}}=\left(1-(1-q)\right)^{x_{1}}\geq 1-x_{1}\cdot(1-q)\geq 1-c_{1}.

It follows that

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\leq\frac{c_{1}}{1-q}\right)\leq\frac{c_{1}}{1-e^{-c}}<\varepsilon/2,

provided we chose the constant
c_{1}>0
sufficiently small.

Similarly, writing
x_{2}:=c_{2}/(1-q)
we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)>x_{2})<\frac{1}{1-\left(1-c/n\right)^{n}}\cdot q^{x_{2}}\leq\frac{1}{1-e^{-c}}\cdot\left(1-(1-q)\right)^{c_{2}/(1-q)}\leq\frac{e^{-c_{2}}}{1-e^{-c}}<\varepsilon/2,

where the last inequality holds
provided we chose
c_{2}
sufficiently large. The result follows combining the two bounds.
∎

Lemma 7.12
.

For every
c,\varepsilon>0
there exist
\delta=\delta(c,\varepsilon),n_{0}=n_{0}(c,\varepsilon)
such that for all sequences
q=q(n)
with

1-c/n\leq q\leq 1+c/n
and all
n\geq n_{0}
:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\delta n\leq\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\leq(1-\delta)n\right)>1-\varepsilon.

Proof.

First note that in the special case when
q=1
, we have that
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)
is
chosen uniformly at random from
[n]
. So any
\delta<\varepsilon/2
would work in this case.
It therefore suffices to consider
q\neq 1
so long as we ensure the value of
\delta
we
end up selecting in the end is
<\varepsilon/2
. (Which is easily achieved by adjusting the choice of

\delta
in the end.)

Next, we consider the case when
1-c/n\leq q<1
.
Again we will use that
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\sim\TGeo(n,1-q)
.
With
c_{U}=c_{U}(c)
as provided by Lemma
7.5
, we can write:

{\mathbb{P}}\left(\delta n\leq\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\leq(1-\delta)n\right)\geq 1-c_{U}\cdot 2\delta>1-\varepsilon,

provided we chose
\delta
small enough.

It remains to consider
1<q<1+c/n
.
We recall that

\Pi_{n}^{-1}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Pi_{n}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(n,q)
and hence
r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}^{-1}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(n,1/q)
.
But then
1/(1+c/n)>1-c/n
, together with the previous case gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}\left(\delta n\leq\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)\leq(1-\delta)n\right)&=&{\mathbb{P}}\left(n+1-(1-\delta)n\leq(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}^{-1})(1)\leq n+1-\delta n\right)\\
&\geq&{\mathbb{P}}\left(2\delta\leq(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}^{-1})(1)\leq(1-2\delta)n\right)\\
&>&1-\varepsilon,\end{array}

where the first inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large, and the last inequality
by the previous case and adjusting the value of
\delta
.
∎

For the remainder of the section, we let
\Sigma_{n}
denote the
permutation on
\{1,\dots,\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)-1\}
induced by
\Pi_{n}
. (If
\Pi_{n}(1)=1
,
then
\Sigma_{n}
is undefined.)
In other words, we set

\Sigma_{n}:=\rk(\Pi_{n}(1),\dots,\Pi_{n}(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)-1)).

Lemma 7.13
.

For
0<q<1
,
n\geq 2
and
2\leq k\leq n
we have

\left(\Sigma_{n}\Big|\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k\right)\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(k-1,q).

Proof.

Fix an arbitrary
0<q<1,n\geq 2
and
2\leq k\leq n
.
Let
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
be the random variables used in the sampling algorithm from Section
3.2
.
In particular, they’re independent with
Z_{i}\sim\TGeo(n+1-i,1-q)
.
We first observe that

\{\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k\}=\{\Pi_{n}(k)=1\}=\{Z_{1}>1,\dots,Z_{k-1}>1,Z_{k}=1\},

(112)

We set
W_{i}:=Z_{i}-1
for
i<k
and
W_{i}=Z_{i+1}
for
k\leq i\leq n-1
.
Next, we claim that conditional on the event
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k
the random variables
W_{1},\dots,W_{n-1}
are independent
with
W_{i}\sim\TGeo(n-i,1-q)
. That they are independent (conditional on
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k
) should be obvious
from (
112
) and the independence of
Z_{1},\dots,Z_{n}
.
Their distribution is also obvious for
i\geq k
. For
i<k
the claim follows from

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(W_{i}=w|\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k)&=&{\mathbb{P}}(Z_{i}=w+1|Z_{i}>1)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\left(\frac{q^{w}(1-q)}{1-q^{n+1-i}}\right)/\left(\frac{q-q^{n+1-i}}{1-q^{n+1-i}}\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\frac{q^{w-1}(1-q)}{1-q^{n-i}}.\end{array}

Conditional on
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k
, we can define
\Sigma_{n}^{*}\in S_{n-1}
by

\Sigma_{n}^{*}(i):=\begin{cases}\Pi_{n}(i)-1&\text{ if $1\leq i\leq k-1$, }\\
\Pi_{n}(i+1)-1&\text{ if $k\leq i\leq n-1$. }\end{cases}

Now observe that, still conditional on
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k
, we have

\Sigma_{n}^{*}(i)=\begin{cases}W_{1}&\text{ if $i=1$, }\\
\text{ the $W_{i}$-th element of $[n-1]\setminus\{W_{1},\dots,W_{i-1}\}$}&\text{ otherwise.}\end{cases},

It follows that

\left(\Sigma_{n}^{*}\Big|\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k\right)\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(n-1,q).

This gives

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\left(\Sigma_{n}{\Big|}\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k\right)&=&\left(\rk(\Pi_{n}(1),\dots,\Pi_{n}(k-1)){\Big|}\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\left(\rk(\Sigma_{n}^{*}(1),\dots,\Sigma_{n}^{*}(k-1)){\Big|}\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k\right)\\[8.61108pt]
&\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}&\Mallows(k-1,q),\end{array}

applying Lemma
3.9
in the last line.
∎

We next establish that
i<\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)
is in fact expressible in
\TOTO
.
This is a seemingly innocuous statement.
As a side remark, let us mention that it is not possible to express
that
i<\Pi_{n}(1)
in
\TOTO

(since it is not relevant for the proofs in this paper, we do not offer a formal proof).
This is the reason we use
\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)
and not
\Pi_{n}(1)
to define
\Sigma_{n}
above.

Lemma 7.14
.

There is a
\TOTO
formula
\chi
with one free variable, such that
for ever
n\in\mathbb{N}
, every permutation
\pi\in S_{n}
and every
1\leq i\leq n
we have

(\pi,i)\models\chi(x)\text{ if and only if }i<\pi^{-1}(1).

Proof.

The formula
\chi
can be written as:

\exists y:(x<_{1}y)\wedge\neg\left(\exists z:z<_{2}y\right).

So, the formula
\chi
asks that there exists
y
with
x<y
such that
there is no
z
with
\pi(z)<\pi(y)
. This clearly does the trick.
∎

We now present a final preparatory
\TOTO
sentence whose probability does not convergence
on part of the target range for
q=q(n)
.

Corollary 7.15
.

There exists a sentence
\zeta\in\TOTO
such that for every fixed
c>0
, all
sequences
q=q(n)
satisfying
1-1/\log^{*}n<q<1+c/n

and all
n
satisfying
W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)<\log^{*}\log^{*}\log^{*}n
we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)=\begin{cases}1-o(1)&\text{ if $\log^{**}\log^{**}n$ is even, }\\
o(1)&\text{ if $\log^{**}\log^{**}n$ is odd }\end{cases},

where the error terms
o(1)
can be taken uniform over all sequences considered.

Proof.

Let
\psi
is as provided by Proposition
7.4
and let
\chi
be as provided by Lemma
7.14
.
We let
\zeta:=\psi^{\chi}
be the relativization as provided by Lemma
3.19
.
Then
\zeta
formalizes that
\Sigma_{n}
satisfies
\psi
.

Now let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary (but fixed) and let
n
be such that

W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)<\log^{*}\log^{*}\log^{*}n
.
Let
\delta=\delta(c,\varepsilon)
be as provided by Lemma
7.12
and let
c_{1}=c_{1}(c,\varepsilon),c_{2}=c_{2}(c,\varepsilon)

be as provided by Lemma
7.11
. We define

k_{1}:=\begin{cases}\delta n&\text{ if $q>1-c/n$, }\\
\frac{c_{1}}{1-q}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases},\quad k_{2}:=\begin{cases}n&\text{ if $q>1-c/n$, }\\
\frac{c_{2}}{1-q}&\text{otherwise.}\end{cases}.

A crucial step in the current proof is the observation that, provided
n
is sufficiently large,
for all
k_{1}\leq k\leq k_{2}
we have

1-\frac{C}{k-1}<q<1+\frac{C}{k-1},

(113)

where we can take
C:=2c/\delta
in the case when
q>1-c/n
and

C:=2/c_{1}
otherwise.

If
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is even then, using Lemma
7.13
, we can write

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)&=&{\mathbb{P}}(\Sigma_{n}\models\psi)\\[8.61108pt]
&\geq&\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}\leq k\leq k_{1}}{\mathbb{P}}(\Sigma_{n}\models\psi|\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k)\\[8.61108pt]
&=&\displaystyle\sum_{k_{1}\leq k\leq k_{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(\Mallows(k-1,q)\models\psi)\cdot{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)=k)\\[8.61108pt]
&\geq&\displaystyle\min_{k_{1}\leq k\leq k_{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(\Mallows(k-1,q)\models\psi)-{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)<k_{1}\text{ or }\Pi_{n}(1)>k_{2})\\[8.61108pt]
&>&1-O\left(\left(\log^{*}n\right)^{-.001}\right)-\varepsilon,\end{array}

using in the last line Proposition
7.4
together with (
113
)
and that the fact that for all
k\geq k_{1}
we have

\begin{array}[]{rcl}\log\log\log k&\geq&\log\log\log(c_{1}\cdot\log^{*}n)\geq\log^{*}\log^{*}\log^{*}n\\
&>&W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1)\geq W^{(2)}(\log^{**}\log^{**}k-1),\end{array}

for
n
sufficiently large.
If
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is odd then, analogously:

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)&\leq&\displaystyle\max_{k_{1}\leq k\leq k_{2}}{\mathbb{P}}(\Mallows(k-1,q)\models\psi)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)<k_{1}\text{ or }\Pi_{n}^{-1}(1)>k_{2})\\[8.61108pt]
&<&\displaystyle O\left(\left(\log^{*}n\right)^{-.001}\right)+\varepsilon.\end{array}

Sending
\varepsilon\searrow 0
finishes the proof.
∎

The sentence
\zeta
provided in the previous corollary works in an asymmetric range of
q
.
In order to remedy this, we will use a construction that allows us to distinguish (in
\TOTO
, with appropriately
high probability) between the case when
q<1-c/n
and
q>1+c/n
.
The following observation is the key to that construction.

Lemma 7.16
.

For every
\varepsilon>0
there are
c=c(\varepsilon),n_{0}=n_{0}(\varepsilon)
such the following holds
for all
n\geq n_{0}
;

(i)

If
0<q<1-c/n
then
{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n))>1-\varepsilon
, and;

(ii)

If
q>1+c/n
then
{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n))<\varepsilon
.

Proof.

We first consider the case
q<1-c/n
.
By Theorem
3.12
and Markov’s inequality:

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)>n/10)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(|\Pi_{n}(1)-1|>n/100)\leq\frac{100\cdot{\mathbb{E}}|\Pi_{n}(1)-1|}{n}\leq\frac{200q}{n(1-q)}\leq\frac{12}{c}<\varepsilon/2,

where the first inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large and
the last inequality holds provided the constant
c
is chosen sufficiently large.
Analogously

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(n)<9n/10)\leq\frac{100\cdot{\mathbb{E}}|\Pi_{n}(n)-n|}{n}<\varepsilon/2.

Part
(i)
follows.

Assume now
q>1+c/n
for some large constant
c
to be determined.
Recall that
r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\hskip 0.86108pt\raisebox{-0.43057pt}{$=$}\hskip-6.45831pt\raisebox{4.30554pt}{{$\scriptstyle d$}}\hskip 3.44444pt\Mallows(n,1/q)
.
We have
1/q<1/(1+c/n)<1-(c/2)/n
, where the last inequality holds for
n
sufficiently large.
Using the previous part:

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n))={\mathbb{P}}(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}(1)>r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}(n))<\varepsilon,

for
n
sufficiently large (and provided we chose
c
sufficiently large).
∎

Proof of Proposition
7.10
:

Let
\zeta
be as provided by the previous corollary, let
\zeta^{\text{reverse}}
be as provided by
Lemma
3.20
, and let

\theta:=\exists x\exists y:(x<_{2}y)\wedge\neg\left(\exists z:z<_{1}x\right)\wedge\neg\left(\exists z:y<_{1}z\right),

denote a
\TOTO
sentence that formalizes that
\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n)
.
The sentence
\xi
is now given by

\xi:=\left(\theta\wedge\zeta\right)\vee\left(\neg\theta\wedge\zeta^{\text{reverse}}\right).

That is,
\xi
demands that one of
a)

\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n)
and
\zeta
holds, or
b)

\Pi_{n}(1)>\Pi_{n}(n)
and
\zeta
holds for
r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}
.

Let
\varepsilon>0
be arbitrary (but fixed) and let
c>0
be as provided by Lemma
7.16
.
We consider an arbitrary sequence
q=q(n)
with
1-1/\log^{*}n<q<1+1/\log^{*}n
.
Let
n
be such that
W^{(2)}\left(\log^{**}\log^{**}n-1\right)<\log^{*}\log^{*}\log^{*}n
.
First we assume that
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is even and
1-c/n<q<1+c/n
. In this case

\begin{array}[]{rcl}{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)&\geq&{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta\text{ and }r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)\\
&\geq&1-{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\not\models\zeta)-{\mathbb{P}}(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\not\models\zeta)\\
&=&1-o(1),\end{array}

If
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is odd and
1-c/n<q<1+c/n
then similarly

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)+{\mathbb{P}}(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)=o(1).

If
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is even and
1-1/\log^{*}n<q\leq 1-c/n
then we have

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)-{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)>\Pi_{n}(n))>1-o(1)-\varepsilon.

If
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is odd and
1-1/\log^{*}n<q\leq 1-c/n
then

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)>\Pi_{n}(n))<o(1)+\varepsilon.

Now suppose that
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is even and
1+c/n\leq q<1+1/\log^{*}n
.
Observe that
1-1/\log^{*}n<1/q<1-(c/2)/n
for
n
sufficiently large.
Adjusting the value of
c
if needed, we can again apply Corollary
7.15
to obtain:

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)\geq{\mathbb{P}}(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)-{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n))>1-o(1)-\varepsilon.

Similarly, if
\log^{**}\log^{**}n
is odd and
1+c/n\leq q<1+1/\log^{*}n
then

{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}\models\psi)\leq{\mathbb{P}}(r_{n}\circ\Pi_{n}\models\zeta)+{\mathbb{P}}(\Pi_{n}(1)<\Pi_{n}(n))<o(1)+\varepsilon.

The result follows by sending
\varepsilon\searrow 0
.

\blacksquare

8
Discussion and suggestions for further work

Theorem
1.1
established that for
q<1
the zero-one law holds wrt.
\TOOB
, while for

q>1
there is some
\TOOB
sentence whose probability of holding does not converge with
n
.
(In the case when
q=1
Compton
[
15
]
has shown decades ago that the convergence law holds, but
not the zero-one law.)
The property exhibiting the non-convergence simply asks for at least a certain number of fixed points,
exploiting a result from
[
31
]
(repeated as Theorem
3.11
in the present paper).
Having another look at the result on the limiting distributions
for the cycle counts provided by that result, it should be relatively straightforward to show that
when
q>1
and we consider either the
sequence
\Mallows(2n,q)
of Mallows permutations restricted to
even domain sizes or the sequence
\Mallows(2n+1,q)
restricted to odd domain sizes, then
in fact the convergence law will hold.

Theorem
1.1
only considers the case of
q
fixed.
The case when
q=q(n)
depends on
n
is certainly worth investigating, in particular the
“phase transition” regime when
q\to 1
.
A natural line of inquiry would be to try and determine a
“critical window” inside which the behaviour is essentially the same as
q=1
(convergence law but not
zero-one law) while below we get the zero-one law and above the convergence law fails.
The behaviour of
\TOOB
sentences is essentially determined by the
limiting distribution of (short) cycles, so one might expect this to be relatively straightforward.
The behaviour of cycles in the regime when
q\to 1
is however far from well understood at the moment.
We point the reader to
[
31
]
for some more discussion of the relevant open questions
on cycles in the Mallows model.

In Theorem
1.2
, Part
(ii)
we did in fact consider a range
of
q
with
q\to 1
.
We constructed a sequence
\varphi\in\TOTO
such that
{\mathbb{P}}(\Mallows(n,q)\models\varphi)

does not converge for any sequence
q=q(n)
with
1-1/\log^{*}n<q<1+1/\log^{*}n
.
For the
\varphi
we’ve constructed, we believe it should be possible
to determine a sequence
q=q(n)
that converges to 1 (very slowly) while
{\mathbb{P}}(\Mallows(n,q)\models\varphi)

converges.

On the other hand, as mentioned earlier, the range
(1-1/\log^{*}n,1+1/\log^{*}n)
is certainly not best possible, as
should evident from the proof.
It should be possible to achieve a range for
q
that approaches
1
much more slowly, with a minor variation
on our proof. The only constraint on the range we can obtain seems to be what we can axiomatize using the
arithmetization construction in Section
6.1
.
That is, if we can axiomatize some increasing function
F(n)
using binary relations
as in Section
6.1
and
f(n)
is its
discrete inverse (i.e.
f(n):=\max\{m\leq n:F(m)\leq n\}
), then one should be able to construct
a
\varphi
such that
{\mathbb{P}}(\Mallows(n,q)\models\varphi)
does not converge for all
1-1/f(n)<q<1+1/f(n)
.
It is not quite clear to the authors at present, most likely just for want of a proper background in
the relevant subfields of Mathematics, exactly how wide of a range can be obtained in this manner.
But, perhaps a different proof technique can yield even wider ranges.
In particular, perhaps one can widen the range of
q
where there is non-convergence even more
if one does not insist on finding a single sentence that works in the entire range?
