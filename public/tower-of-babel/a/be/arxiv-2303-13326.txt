Decentralized Adversarial Training over Graphs
Ying Cao*, Elsa Rizk, Stefan Vlaski, Ali H. Sayed

Abstract
The vulnerability of machine learning models to adversarial attacks has been attracting considerable attention in recent years. Most existing studies focus on the behavior of stand-alone single-agent learners. In comparison, this work studies adversarial training over graphs, where individual agents are subjected to perturbations of varied strength levels across space. It is expected that interactions by linked agents, and the heterogeneity of the attack models that are possible over the graph, can help enhance robustness in view of the coordination power of the group. Using a min-max formulation of distributed learning, we develop a decentralized adversarial training framework for multi-agent systems. Specifically, we devise two decentralized adversarial training algorithms by relying on two popular decentralized learning strategies–diffusion and consensus. We analyze the convergence properties of the proposed framework for strongly-convex, convex, and non-convex environments, and illustrate the enhanced robustness to adversarial attacks.

for convex and non-convex environments, their approaches cannot be directly extended to the adversarial learning scenario. This limitation arises because traditional analyses rely heavily on the Lipshcitz continuity of the gradients of risk functions (or equivalently, the assumption of bounded Hessian matrices). However, the adversarial perturbations introduce changes to the properties of risk functions. As we will demonstrate, in adversarial learning, formulating the “real” loss function as a maximization problem over perturbations results in risk functions that do not necessarily satisfy the Lipschitz condition. Moreover, in the general case where the optimal perturbation of the maximization problem is not unique, the risk functions are non-differentiable. These factors make the analysis of the decentralized learning algorithms in the adversarial setting more challenging. To address these issues, we use analytical frameworks that are different from the literature on clean training data to establish the convergence of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms.

Our analytical framework in the strongly-convex and convex cases is inspired by
[
28

[
28
]

In contrast, we establish convergence results for both the strongly-convex and convex cases, remove the bounded initial distance assumption, and consider more general heterogeneous environments.
In the analysis for the non-convex case, existing works of single-agent adversarial training usually assume the loss function in the inner maximization step to be strongly-concave

Subsequently, all agents update their parameters using the aggregated gradient. Another line of work examines the adversarial robustness of neural networks in the context of federated learning

The contributions of this work are listed as follows:

(1) We formulate a sequential minimax optimization problem involving adversarial samples, where the perturbations are within some general
\ell_{p}
norm-bounded region. To solve the problem, we propose a general decentralized framework, where all agents are subjected to adversarial examples and work together through local interactions to defend the network globally. Specifically, to improve the robustness of multi-agent systems against adversarial examples, we develop two decentralized adversarial training algorithms based on two widely used decentralized strategies–diffusion and consensus. In our algorithms, if the closed-form solution of the inner maximization problem is accessible–which holds for some popular convex loss functions–it is directly added to the clean data. However, in the general case where the exact solution is not obtainable, an approximate one is used in the algorithms.

(2) In the performance analysis, we examine the convergence of the proposed framework for strongly-convex, convex, and non-convex optimization environments. Basically, we show that for strongly-convex loss functions, in the general case where the optimal perturbation of the inner maximization problem is not unique and analytically accessible, then the risk functions are not differentiable. In this case, the proposed algorithms are able to approach the global minimizer
within
O(\mu)+O(\epsilon)
after sufficient iterations at a linear rate, where
\mu
is the step-size parameter, and the error term
O(\epsilon)
arises due to the approximation error when solving the maximization problem over perturbations. Also,
\epsilon
is the maximum perturbation bound over the entire graph, which is small in the context of adversarial training. In particular, if the closed-form solution of the optimal perturbation is known, then the error term can be removed. In comparison, in the convex case, the proposed algorithms are verified to converge to a
O(\mu)
neighborhood of a global minimum after sufficient iterations at a sub-linear rate of
O(\frac{1}{\mu N})
, where
N
is the number of iterations. In addition, for non-convex losses, the algorithms are guaranteed to converge to a point that is
O(\mu)+O(\epsilon^{2})
close to an approximate stationary point of the global objective function at a sub-linear rate
O(\frac{1}{\mu N})
. Still, the error term
O(\epsilon^{2})
reflects the approximation error associated the inner maximization.

(3) In the simulations, we illustrate how the robustness of the multi-agent systems can be improved by the proposed algorithms. We simulate both the convex and non-convex environments. Furthermore, we simulate both homogeneous and heterogeneous networks. We illustrate how decentralized adversarial training algorithms enable more robust behavior than non-cooperative and even centralized methods in non-convex environments. This fact illustrates the role of the graph topology in resisting adversarial attacks.

II
Foundations and Approach

II-A

Problem formulation

We consider a collection of
K
agents over a graph, where each agent
k
observes independent realizations of some random data
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
, in which
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
plays the role of the feature vector and
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
plays the role of the label variable. Adversarial learning in the heterogeneous decentralized setting deals with the following stochastic minimax problem:

w^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{{w}\in\mathbbm{R}^{M}}\left\{J(w)\overset{\Delta}{=}\sum\limits_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}J_{k}(w)\right\}

(1)

where
M
is the dimension of the feature vectors,
\{\pi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{K}
are positive scaling weights adding up to one, and each individual risk function is defined by

\begin{split}J_{k}(w)&=\mathds{E}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\}}\left\{\max\limits_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\right\}\\
\end{split}

(2)

in terms of a loss function
Q_{k}(\cdot)
. In this formulation,
\boldsymbol{y}_{k}
is the true label of sample
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
, and the variable
\delta
represents a norm-bounded perturbation added to the clean sample. In this paper, we assume all agents observe independently and identically distributed (i.e., iid) data over time, but allow for non-iid samples over space. The type of the norm
p_{k}
and the upper bound
\epsilon_{k}
are allowed to vary across agents, thus leading to the possibility of heterogeneous models over the graph. In particular,
\ell_{2}
and
\ell_{\infty}
norms are two popular choices under which the results of this paper hold. We refer to
w^{\star}
as the
robust model
since it relies on the worst-case perturbation found from (
2
).

II-B

Algorithm development

One methodology for solving (
1
) is to first determine the inner maximizer in (
2
), thus reducing the minimax problem to a standard stochastic minimization formulation. Then, the traditional stochastic (sub)gradient method could be used to seek the robust minimizer.

We denote the true maximizer of the perturbed loss function in (
2
) by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}(w)\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

(3)

where the dependence of
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}
on
w
is shown explicitly. We use the boldface notation for
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}(\cdot)
because it is now a function of the random data
(\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
. To apply the stochastic gradient method, we would need to evaluate the gradient of
Q(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}(w),\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
relative to
w
, which can be challenging since
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}(w)
is also dependent on
w
. Fortunately, this difficulty can be resolved by appealing to Danskin’s theorem – see section 6.11 in
[
49
]
and section B.3 in
[
50
]
. Let

\displaystyle f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\overset{\Delta}{=}\max_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

(4)

which can be viewed as the “real” loss function of (
2
), so that the risk function of each agent in (
2
) is given by

\displaystyle J_{k}(w)

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}_{\{\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}\}}\left\{f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\right\}

(5)

Then, the theorem asserts that
f(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
remains (strongly) convex over
w
if
Q_{k}(w;\cdot,\cdot)
is (strongly) convex over
w
. However,
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
need not be differentiable over
w
even when
Q_{k}(w;\cdot,\cdot)
is differentiable. Nevertheless, and importantly for our purposes, we can determine subgradients for
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
by using the actual gradient of the loss evaluated at the worst perturbation, namely, it holds that

\displaystyle\partial_{w}f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})=\mathrm{conv}\bigg\{\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

\displaystyle\Big|\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\bigg\}

(6)

which means the set of subgradients
\partial_{w}f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is the convex hull of the gradients
\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
evaluated over all possible maximizers
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}
. This also implies that

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\in\partial_{w}f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

(7)

In (
II-B
) and (
7
), the gradient of
Q_{k}(\cdot)
relative to
w
at the perturbed data is computed by treating
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}
as a stand-alone vector and ignoring its dependence on
w
. When
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}
in (
3
) happens to be
unique
, then the gradient on the left in (
7
) will be equal to the right side, so that in that case the function
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
will be
differentiable
. In this scenario, the stochastic gradient methods can be employed to solve (
1
). However, in the more general case where
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}
is not unique, only the subdifferential property in (
II-B
) can be guaranteed. In this case, stochastic subgradient methods are applicable.

Moreover, while closed-from solutions to the maximization problem in (
4
) exist for some convex loss functions (as will be discussed later), obtaining such solutions is generally not possible, especially for non-convex loss functions. Instead, one is satisfied with some common approximations
[
16
,
7
,
14
]
. One popular strategy is to resort to a first-order Taylor expansion for each agent around
\boldsymbol{x}_{k}
as follows:

\displaystyle\small Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\approx Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})+\delta^{\sf T}\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

(8)

from which the following approximate maximizer can be derived:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\widehat{\delta}}_{k}=\mathop{\text{argmax}}\limits_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}\delta^{\sf T}\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

(9)

Note that different choices for the
p_{k}
norm lead to different expressions for the approximate maximizer. For instance, when
p_{k}=2
, we get:

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k}=\epsilon_{k}\frac{\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})}{\left\|\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\right\|}

(10)

which motivates the fast gradient method (FGM)
[
7
]
in single-agent learning, while for
p=\infty
we have

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}_{k}=\epsilon_{k}\mathop{\mathrm{sign}}\Big(\nabla_{x}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\Big)

(11)

which is the expression of the fast gradient sign method (FGSM)
[
15
]
. Moreover, depending on the number of iterations used when generating perturbations, the first-order gradient based methods include single-step attacks, such as FGM and FGSM, and multi-step ones, such as projected gradient descent (PGD)
[
14
]
, which can also be called multi-step FGM for
\ell_{2}-
bounded attacks and multi-step FGSM for
\ell_{\infty}-
bounded attacks. Although the latter multi-step perturbations have been empirically shown to be stronger than the former ones
[
14
]
and lead to more robust models, its computational complexity is also much higher
[
51
]
.

Motivated by the above discussion, and using (
7
), we can now devise algorithms to enhance the robustness of multi-agent systems to adversarial perturbations. To do so, we consider two popular decentralized strategies.

The first one is the adapt-then-combine (ATC) version of the
diffusion
strategy
[
4
,
19
]
, for which we can write down the mini-batch adversarial extension to solve (
1
)–(
2
). Basically, it consists of the following main three steps,

\displaystyle\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n}^{b}=\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}^{b}+\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{b}_{k,n}

(12a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{k,n}=\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1}-\frac{\mu}{B}\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n}^{b},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})

(12b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n}=\sum\limits_{\ell\mathcal{\in N}_{k}}a_{\ell k}\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\ell,n}

(12c)

where

\widehat{\boldsymbol{\delta}}^{b}_{k,n}\approx\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}^{b}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})

(13)

computes the approximate maximizer for each sample from the mini batch at iteration

is the intermediate state obtained by locally performing a stochastic gradient step at each agent. The intermediate states

Note that when the optimal perturbation (i.e., the exact maximizer) of (

III
Analysis in (strongly-)convex environments

This section analyzes the convergence of decentralized adversarial training algorithms, as listed in Algorithms
1
and
2
, in the context of (strongly) convex loss functions. To do so, it is critical to distinguish between the cases where the optimal solution of (
3
) is accessible and unique, and the case where it is not. As discussed in Section
II-B
, when the closed-form solution to (
3
) is accessible and unique, it is used in the algorithms directly. In this case, we are actually using stochastic gradient methods to solve (
1
). Analytical solutions for (
3
), corresponding to several popular (strongly) convex loss functions, are provided in Appendix
A
. Conversely, if the optimal perturbation of (
3
) is not obtainable, we approximate it using the Taylor expansion, as shown in (
8
). In this case, since the “real” loss function
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
, as defined in (
4
), may not be differentiable, we consider the general non-differentiable scenario. Consequently, Algorithms
1
and
2
operate as stochastic subgradient methods.

Moving on, considering
\epsilon=\max\limits_{k}\left\{\epsilon_{k}\right\}
, we first establish the following affine Lipschitz results. The proof can be found in Appendix
B
.

Lemma 1
.

(
Affine Lipschitz
) For each agent
k
, and any
w_{1},w_{2},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\|\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w_{2};\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta_{2},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})-\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w_{1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta_{1},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\|

\displaystyle\leq L\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|+O(\epsilon)

(19)

where the
\ell_{p_{k}}-
norms of
\delta_{1}
and
\delta_{2}
are bounded by
\epsilon_{k}
. Specifically, when the solution of (
3
) is unique,
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is differentiable. Then, the gradient of
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is affine Lipschitz, namely,

\|\nabla_{w}f_{k}(w_{2};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})-\nabla_{w}f_{k}(w_{1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})\|\leq L\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|+O(\epsilon)

(20)

which also implies

\|\nabla_{w}J_{k}(w_{2})-\nabla_{w}J_{k}(w_{1})\|\leq L\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|+O(\epsilon)

(21)

\hfill\square

Contrary to the traditional analysis of decentralized learning algorithms
[
24
,
26
,
25
]
, which relies heavily on the gradient-Lipschitz conditions of the risk functions
J_{k}(w)
, adversarial perturbations introduce a challenge. Specifically, the gradients of the risks in (
2
) are now affine Lipschitz, as demonstrated in (
20
), due to the additional term
O(\epsilon)
. Consequently, the traditional analysis methods are not directly applicable in this setting. This fact motivates adjustments to the convergence arguments. A similar situation arises, for example, when we study the convergence of decentralized learning under non-smooth losses and clean environments — see
[
28
,
4
]
.

To enable the convergence analysis of decentralized learning under the affine Lipschitz condition in the strongly-convex environment, the authors of
[
28
]
assume that the initial distance of all agents’ models, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{w}_{k,0}
, from the global minimizer are bounded. This assumption allows them to prove that the distance between the models of all agents and the global minimizer remains bounded throughout the training process.
However, their argument assumes a homogeneous setting, where the minimizers of all local risk functions are identical. In this work, we extend the analysis to heterogeneous settings, where the minimizers of local risk functions may differ. To do so, we introduce the following assumption, which requires the network heterogeneity is not extreme.

Assumption 3
.

(
Bounded gradient disagreement
) For any pair of agents
k
and
\ell
, the squared gradient disagreements are uniformly bounded on average, namely, for any
w
, it holds that

\mathds{E}_{\{\boldsymbol{x},\boldsymbol{y}\}}\|\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y})-\nabla_{w}Q_{\ell}(w;\boldsymbol{x}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y})\|^{2}\leq C^{2}

(22)

for any
\|\delta\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}
,
\|\delta\|_{p_{\ell}}\leq\epsilon_{\ell}
.

\hfill\square

This type of assumption is common in the literature, and is used for example in
[
52
,
53
]
. This condition is automatically satisfied when all agents use the same loss function, or in the centralized case where only a single agent is considered. More generally, when the agents are heterogeneous, the condition ensures that the level of the heterogeneity is not extreme. Moreover, as demonstrated later in the proof, this assumption facilitates the convergence of the distance between local agents and their centroid, which is crucial for verifying the convergence of decentralized adversarial training strategies in heterogeneous environments.

We now proceed with the network analysis. To begin with, we introduce the gradient noise process.
Recall first the maximization problem

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,n}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\left\|\delta\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

(23)

In the general non-differentiable case, where the optimal perturbation
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,n}^{\star}
is not necessarily unique, Danskin’s theorem guarantees

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,n}^{\star},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})\in\partial_{w}J_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1})

(24)

In this case, if the optimal perturbation of (
23
) is not accessible, we can only use an approximate one, as shown in (
13
). The stochastic gradient noise is defined by

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}\overset{\Delta}{=}

\displaystyle\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

\displaystyle-\mathds{E}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

(25)

This quantity measures the difference between the stochastic gradient at the approximate maximizer and the expected one over all samples at iteration
n
. Similarly, we define the gradient noise for the more general mini-batch version as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}^{B}\overset{\Delta}{=}

\displaystyle\frac{1}{B}\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n}^{b},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})

\displaystyle-\mathds{E}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

(26)

However, if the optimal perturbation
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,n}^{\star}
is accessible and unique, as is verified for some popular (strongly-)convex loss functions in Appendix
A
, then it will be used in the proposed algorithms in place of the approximate one. In this case, the functions
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
(as defined in (
4
)) and
J_{k}(w)
(as defined in (
5
)) are differentiable. Then, it follows from Danskin’s theorem that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k,n}^{\star},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

\displaystyle=\mathds{E}\nabla_{w}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{w}J_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1})

(27)

In this case, the stochastic mini-batch gradient noise is defined by the difference between the approximate gradient (represented by the average gradient of the loss over a mini batch of data) and the true
gradient (represented by the gradient of the risk), namely,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}^{B}

\displaystyle\overset{\Delta}{=}\frac{1}{B}\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\nabla_{w}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n}^{b},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})-\nabla_{w}J_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1})

(28)

Next, we exploit the eigen-structure of
A
by noting that the
K\times K
matrix
A
admits a Jordan canonical decomposition of the form
[
24
]
:

A=VJV^{-1}

(29)

where the matrices
\{V,J\}
are

V=\left[{\pi}\quad V_{R}\right],\quad J=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}1&0\\
0&J_{\alpha}\end{array}\right],\quad V^{-1}=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\mathbbm{1}^{\sf T}\\
V_{L}^{\sf T}\end{array}\right]

(30)

Here,
J_{\alpha}
is a block Jordan matrix with eigenvalues from the second largest eigenvalue
\lambda_{2}
to the smallest eigenvalue
\lambda_{K}
of
A
appearing on the diagonal and the positive scalar
\alpha
, which can be chosen arbitrarily small, appearing on the first lower sub-diagonal. Also, for later use, we consider the extended graph matrix:

\mathcal{A}\overset{\Delta}{=}A\otimes I_{M}

(31)

where
I_{M}
is the identity matrix of size
M
, while
\otimes
denotes the Kronecker product operation. Then, the extended matrix
\mathcal{A}
satisfies

\mathcal{A}=\mathcal{V}\mathcal{J}\mathcal{V}^{-1}

(32)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}=V\otimes I_{M},\quad\mathcal{J}=J\otimes I_{M},\quad\mathcal{V}^{-1}=V^{-1}\otimes I_{M}

(33)

Since

\displaystyle VV^{-1}=V^{-1}V=I_{K}

(34)

we have

\displaystyle{\pi}\mathbbm{1}^{\sf T}+V_{R}V_{L}^{\sf T}=\mathbbm{1}\pi^{\sf T}+V_{L}V_{R}^{\sf T}=I_{K}

\displaystyle V_{L}^{\sf T}V_{R}=I_{K-1}

\displaystyle V_{L}^{\sf T}\pi=V_{R}^{\sf T}\mathbbm{1}=0

(35)

Next, we collect the parameters from all agents into the
K\times 1
block column vector:

\displaystyle{{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}}_{n}\overset{\Delta}{=}\mbox{\rm col}_{k}\{{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k,n}\}

(36)

for
k=1,\ldots,K
. Similarly, we gather the stochastic gradients from all agents into:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\overset{\Delta}{=}\mbox{\rm col}_{k}\left\{\frac{1}{B}\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n}^{b},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})\right\}

(37)

Note that when the optimal perturbation of (

) is accessible and unique,
\boldsymbol{q}_{n}
becomes:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\overset{\Delta}{=}\mbox{\rm col}_{k}\left\{\frac{1}{B}\sum\limits_{b=1}^{B}\nabla_{w}f_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})\right\}

(38)

Using these variables, the recursions of the decentralized adversarial training algorithms listed in Algorithm

and

can be rewritten using a unified description as follows:

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\sf T}\left(\mathcal{A}_{1}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}_{n-1}-\mu\boldsymbol{q}_{n}\right)

(39)

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{2}=A_{2}\otimes I_{M},\quad\mathcal{A}_{1}=A_{1}\otimes I_{M},\quad A_{1}A_{2}=A

(40)

and the choices for the matrices
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
depend on the nature of the algorithm. Basically, for the consensus strategy, we set

\displaystyle A_{1}=A,\quad A_{2}=I_{K}

(41)

while for diffusion, it holds that

\displaystyle A_{1}=I_{K},\quad A_{2}=A

(42)

Recalling (
15
), and since
A_{1}
and
A_{2}
are either
A
or
I_{K}
, they are left-stochastic matrices. We defined
\pi
to be the right eigenvector corresponding to the eigenvalue 1, namely,

\displaystyle A_{1}\pi=A_{2}\pi=\pi,\quad A_{1}^{\sf T}\mathbbm{1}=A_{2}^{\sf T}\mathbbm{1}=1

(43)

Multiplying (

) from the left by
\mathcal{V}^{\sf T}
, and substituting (

) into it, we obtain the following relation:

\displaystyle\mathcal{V}^{\sf T}{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{c}(\pi^{\sf T}\otimes I_{M}){\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}\\
(V_{R}^{\sf T}\otimes I_{M}){\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}\end{array}\right]\overset{\Delta}{=}\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}\\
\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathcal{V}^{\sf T}\mathcal{A}^{\sf T}{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n-1}-\mu\mathcal{V}^{\sf T}\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathcal{J}^{\sf T}\mathcal{V}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}_{n-1}-\mu\mathcal{V}^{\sf T}\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{M}&\boldsymbol{0}\\
\boldsymbol{0}&J_{\alpha}^{\sf T}\otimes I_{M}\end{array}\right]\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n-1}\\
\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n-1}\end{array}\right]

\displaystyle\quad\ -\mu\left[\begin{array}[]{c}\pi^{\sf T}\otimes I_{M}\\
V_{R}^{\sf T}\otimes I_{M}\end{array}\right]\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

where
(a)
follows from (

). Then, it follows from (

) and (
III
) that we can split
(

) into the following two parts in terms of the transformed quantities
\{\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n},\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}\}
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{k}\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n}=\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n-1}-\mu(\pi^{\sf T}\otimes I_{M})\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

(54)

and

\displaystyle\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}=\mathcal{V}_{R}^{\sf T}{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}=\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}^{\sf T}\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n-1}-\mu\mathcal{V}_{R}^{\sf T}\mathcal{A}_{2}^{\sf T}\boldsymbol{q}_{n}

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{\alpha}=J_{\alpha}\otimes I_{M},\quad\mathcal{V}_{R}=V_{R}\otimes I_{M}

(56)

Note that
\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}
represents the network average (also called centroid) as it is the weighted mean of the parameters of all agents. In contrast,
\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}
is associated with the network disagreement, which quantifies the deviation of local agents from their average. Basically, by extending the centroid
\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}
to a block vector
{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{c,n}

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{c,n}\overset{\Delta}{=}(\mathbbm{1}\otimes I_{M})\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}

(57)

the relationship of the network disagreement and
\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}
is given by

\displaystyle{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{c,n}

\displaystyle=(I_{KM}-(\mathbbm{1}\pi^{\sf T})\otimes I_{M}){\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}

\displaystyle\overset{(a)}{=}\mathcal{V}_{L}\mathcal{V}_{R}^{\sf T}{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}\overset{(b)}{=}\mathcal{V}_{L}\ddot{w}_{n}

(58)

where
(a)
follows from (

), and
(b)
follows from (

). We further substitute the gradient noise term defined in (

) into (

), and obtain the following recursion in general:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}=

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n-1}-\mu\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\mathds{E}\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1};\widehat{\boldsymbol{x}}_{k,n}^{b},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n}^{b})

\displaystyle-\mu\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}^{B}

(59)

As for the case of the obtainable optimal perturbation, we substitute (

) into (

), and obtain

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n}=\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n-1}-\mu\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\nabla_{w}J_{k}(\boldsymbol{w}_{k,n-1})-\mu\sum_{k}\pi_{k}\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}^{B}

(60)

As discussed before, traditional analyses of decentralized learning algorithms rely heavily on the Lipschitz condition over the gradients of risk or loss functions. However, in our adversarial setting, where the gradient of risk functions is only guaranteed to be affine Lipschitz, the traditional analyses are no longer applicable. Motivated by
[
28
]
, we adopt a different framework to verify the convergence of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms. To this end, we first verify the convergence of the term
\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}
for later use, for which the proof can be found from Appendix
C
.

Theorem 1
.

(
Mean-square stability of network disagreement in (strongly)-convex environments.
)
Consider a network of
K
agents running the decentralized adversarial training algorithms covered by recursion (
39
). Under Assumptions
1
–
3
, for sufficiently small step-sizes
\mu
, and after enough iterations
n\geq\ddot{n}
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\|\ddot{\boldsymbol{w}}_{n}\|^{2}\leq O(\mu^{2})

(61)

with

\displaystyle\ddot{n}=O\left(\frac{\log\mu}{\log(\lambda_{2}+O(\mu^{2}))}\right)

(62)

Moreover, the average mean-square distance between the network centroid and local agents will be bounded by

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\|{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{n}-{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}_{c,n}\|^{2}\leq O(\mu^{2})

(63)

\hfill\square

The above theorem implies that, with sufficiently small step sizes, recursion (
55
) will be upper bounded by a small value after enough iterations. Moreover, all agents will converge to the network centroid in the long run. Importantly, the proof of Theorem
1
does not rely on convexity or strong convexity, making it applicable to both convex and strongly convex scenarios. Furthermore, as shown in Appendix
C
, Theorem
1
holds regardless of whether the optimal perturbations or approximate ones are used in the algorithms.

III-A

Convergence analysis for the strongly-convex case

In this section, we examine the convergence of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms in the strongly-convex environment.

Assumption 4
.

(
Strong convexity
) For each agent k, the loss function
Q_{k}(w;\cdot)
is strongly-convex over
w
, namely, for any
w_{1}
and
w_{2}
, it holds that

\displaystyle Q_{k}(w_{2};x,y)\geq\;

\displaystyle Q_{k}(w_{1};x,y)+\nabla_{w^{\sf T}}Q_{k}(w_{1};x,y)(w_{2}-w_{1})

\displaystyle+\frac{\nu}{2}\|w_{2}-w_{1}\|^{2}

(64)

where
\nu>0
.

\hfill\square

We remark that it follows from Danskin’s theorem
[
49
,
54
,
55
,
50
]
that the maximization over a strongly-convex function remains strongly-convex. One proof for this statement can be found in section 6.11 in
[
49
]
and section B.3 in
[
50
]
. Basically, when
Q_{k}(w;\cdot,\cdot)
is
\nu-
strongly convex over
w
, then its maximization function over the perturbation variable, i.e.,
f_{k}(w;\cdot,\cdot)
, is also
\nu-
strongly convex. As a result, the adversarial risk
J_{k}(w)
defined by (
2
) and the aggregate risk
J(w)
in (
1
) will be
\nu-
strongly convex as well.

Assume
w^{\star}
is the global minimum of
J(w)
, and introduce the error vector

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k,n-1}\overset{\Delta}{=}w^{\star}-{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k,n-1}

(65)

which measures the difference between the model of agent
k
and the global minimizer at iteration
n
. The following result establishes some useful properties for the gradient noise process defined in (
III
) and (
28
) in strongly-convex case, namely, it has zero mean and bounded second-order moment (conditioned on past history), for which proofs can be found in Appendix
D
.

Lemma 2
.

(
Moments of gradient noise in the strongly-convex environment
) For each agent k, the gradient noise defined in (
III
) and (
28
) have zero mean and their variance is bounded:

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left\{\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}^{B}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right\}=0

(66)

\displaystyle\mathds{E}\left\{\left\|\boldsymbol{s}_{k,n}^{B}\right\|^{2}|\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}\right\}\leq\beta_{k}^{2}\left\|\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k,n-1}\right\|^{2}+\sigma_{k}^{2}

(67)

for some non-negative scalars given by

\displaystyle\beta_{k}^{2}=\frac{16L^{2}}{B}

\displaystyle\sigma_{k}^{2}=\frac{8}{B}\mathds{E}\|\nabla_{w}Q_{k}(w^{\star};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}(w^{\star}),\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})\|^{2}+O(\epsilon^{2})

(68)

where

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{k}^{\star}(w^{\star})=\mathop{\rm{argmax}}\limits_{\left\|{\delta}\right\|_{p_{k}}\leq\epsilon_{k}}Q_{k}(w^{\star};\boldsymbol{x}_{k,n}+{\delta},\boldsymbol{y}_{k,n})

(69)

The quantity

\boldsymbol{\mathcal{F}}_{n-1}
is the filtration generated by the past history of the random process
\mbox{{\rm{col}}}{\{\boldsymbol{w}_{k,j}\}_{k=1}^{K}}
for

.

\hfill\square

To proceed, we collect the error vectors from all agents into the

\displaystyle{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\scriptstyle\mathcal{W}}}}_{n}\overset{\Delta}{=}{\mbox{\rm col}}\{\widetilde{\boldsymbol{w}}_{k,n}\}_{k=1}^{K}

with which we obtain a unified recursion for the decentralized adversarial training algorithms listed in Algorithm

\displaystyle=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}I_{M}&\boldsymbol{0}\\

The above corollary indicates that, if the true optimal perturbation in (

) is accessible and unique (a condition satisfied by several popular strongly-convex functions), then after a sufficient number of iterations, the proposed algorithms enable the network to converge in the mean-square-error sense to an
O(\mu)
-neighborhood of the robust minimizer
w^{\star}
.

III-B

Convergence analysis for the convex case

In this section, we analyze the convergence of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms in the convex environment. Specifically, we focus on the scenario where the optimal perturbation in (

Assumption 5
.

(

\hfill\square

Again, it follows from Danskin’s theorem that the maximization over a convex function remains convex. Therefore, the “real” loss function

in (

defined by (

We now present the following important Lemma, and the associated proofs can be found from Appendix

\hfill\square

IV
Analysis in non-convex environments

\hfill{\square}

One difficulty in the convergence analysis of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms, as listed in (
12a
)–(
12c
) and (
14a
)–(
14c
), is due to the non-smoothness of
J(w)
. In this case, verifying the stationarity of
J(w)
requires working with its subdifferential. However, since
J(w)
is both non-convex and non-smooth,
\partial_{w}J(w)
might not even be defined in general. Fortunately,
\partial_{w}J(w)
can be defined through
Fréchet subdifferntial
and
weak-convexity

[
50
,
33
]
. Basically, the Fréchet subdifferntial of a function
J(w)
is defined as the set:

\displaystyle\partial_{w}J(w)\overset{\Delta}{=}\left\{\zeta\Big|\mathop{\mathrm{lim}\ \mathrm{inf}}\limits_{w^{\prime}\to w}\ \frac{J(w^{\prime})-J(w)-\zeta^{\sf T}(w^{\prime}-w)}{\|w^{\prime}-w\|}\geq 0\right\}

(125)

Moreover, the smoothness condition implies weak convexity with the same parameter. Basically, the “real” loss function
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is
L-
weakly convex over
w
under the smooth condition of
Q_{k}
in (
16a
), namely, for any
w
and
w^{\prime}
, it holds that,

\displaystyle f_{k}(w^{\prime};\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})-f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})

\displaystyle\geq(\partial_{w}f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k}))^{\sf T}(w^{\prime}-w)-\frac{L}{2}\|w^{\prime}-w\|^{2}

(126)

where the subdifferential
\partial_{w}f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is defined as the set of vectors that satisfies this inequality. The proof of (
IV
) can be found in section B.3 in
[
50
]
. As a result, since each local risk function
J_{k}(w)
is an expectation of
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
over all samples, and the global risk function
J(w)
is a convex combination of
J_{k}(w)
for
k=1,\ldots,K
, it follows that
J_{k}(w)
and
J(w)
are
L
-weakly convex as well. Then, the subdifferential of
J(w)
is defined by the following set:

\displaystyle\partial_{w}J(w)\overset{\Delta}{=}\left\{\zeta|J(w^{\prime})-J(w)\geq\zeta^{\sf T}(w^{\prime}-w)-\frac{L}{2}\|w^{\prime}-w\|^{2}\right\}

(127)

where the notion of Fréchet subdifferential, as defined in (
125
), is generalized for non-smooth and
L
-weakly convex functions. If there exists a
w^{\star}
such that

\displaystyle 0\in\partial_{w}J(w^{\star})

(128)

then we say
w^{\star}
is a stationary point of
J(w)
.

However, it is still not tractable to carry out the convergence analysis with
\partial_{w}J(w)
directly. Thus, motivated by
[
32
,
55
,
33
,
54
,
50
]
, we resort to the Moreau envelope method to study near-stationarity for non-smooth functions. Consider a function
h(z;w)
defined by

\displaystyle h(z;w)\overset{\Delta}{=}J(z)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|w-z\right\|^{2}

(129)

since
J(z)
is
L
-weakly convex,
h(z;w)
is strongly convex over
z
if
\gamma<\frac{1}{L}
. In this case, the Moreau envelope of
J(w)
is defined by
[
4
]
:

J_{\gamma}(w)\overset{\Delta}{=}\min\limits_{z}h(z;w)=\min\limits_{z}J(z)+\frac{1}{2\gamma}\left\|w-z\right\|^{2}

(130)

It follows that the function
J(z)
and its Moreau envelope
J_{\gamma}(w)
have the same minimum, namely,

\displaystyle\min\limits_{w}J_{\gamma}(w)=\min\limits_{z}J(z)

(131)

Let

\widehat{z}=\mathop{\text{argmin}}\limits_{z}h(z;w)

(132)

then
\widehat{z}
is uniquely determined by
w
since
h(z;w)
is strongly convex over the variable
z
.

Even when
J(z)
is non-differentiable, the Moreau envelope will be differentiable relative to
w
. In particular, it holds that
[
4
,
54
]
:

\displaystyle\frac{1}{\gamma}(w-\widehat{z})=\nabla_{w}J_{\gamma}(w),\quad\;\nabla_{w}J_{\gamma}(w)\in\partial_{w}J(\widehat{z})

(133)

It follows that a stationary point of the Moreau envelope
J_{\gamma}(\cdot)
is also a stationary point of the primal function
J(\cdot)
. Moreover, if
J_{\gamma}(w)
arrives at an approximate stationary point
w^{o}
, i.e.,
if
\left\|\nabla_{w}J_{\gamma}(w^{o})\right\|^{2}
is upper bounded by a small value
\kappa^{2}
, then

\min_{\zeta\in\partial_{w}J(\widehat{z}^{o})}\|\zeta\|^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\gamma^{2}}\|w^{o}-\widehat{z}^{o}\|^{2}=\|\nabla_{w}J_{\gamma}(w^{o})\|^{2}\leq\kappa^{2}

(134)

where

\displaystyle\widehat{z}^{o}=\mathop{\text{argmin}}\limits_{z}h(z;w^{o})

(135)

which implies that
w^{o}
is
O(\kappa^{2})
near a
\kappa^{2}
-approximate stationary point of
J(w)
. Thus, it is reasonable to use the approximate stationarity of
\nabla_{w}J_{\gamma}(w)
as a surrogate for the approximate stationarity of of
\partial_{w}J(w)
[
32
,
55
,
33
,
54
,
50
]
.

We next show the convergence result of the proposed algorithms in the general non-smooth and non-convex environments, for which the proofs can be found in Appendix
M
.

Theorem 4
.

(
Network convergence in non-smooth and non-convex environments
.)
Consider a network of
K
agents running the decentralized adversarial training algorithms, as listed in (
12a
)–(
12c
) and (
14a
)–(
14c
). Under Assumptions
1
–
2
and
6
–
7
, and assuming
J(w)
is lower bounded by
\Delta
, after

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum\limits_{n}\mathds{E}||\nabla_{w}J_{\frac{1}{2L+1}}(\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n-1})||^{2}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2(J_{\frac{1}{2L+1}}(w_{c,n_{0}})-\Delta)}{\mu N}+O(\mu)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(136)

where the
O(\epsilon^{2})
term arises from the approximation error when solving the inner maximization problem in (
3
).

\hfill\square

Theorem
4
is based on properties of the Moreau envelope, and it guarantees that when
\mu
and
\epsilon
are small, the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms will converge asymptotically to a point that is
O(\mu)+O(\epsilon^{2})
close to an approximate stationary point of
J(w)
on average over iterations, and the convergence rate is
O(1/(\mu N))
. Basically, according to (
4
) and (
133
), the following inequality holds for large
N
with
N\geq O(1/\mu^{1+s})
for any

:

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathds{E}\|\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{N-1}-\boldsymbol{w}_{c,N-1}\|^{2}\leq\frac{\rho}{(2L+1)^{2}}

(137)

where
\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{N-1}
is an approximate stationary point for
J(w)
satisfying

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathds{E}\left\{\min_{\boldsymbol{\zeta}\in\partial_{w}J(\widehat{\boldsymbol{w}}_{N-1})}||\boldsymbol{\zeta}||^{2}\right\}\leq\rho\overset{\Delta}{=}O(\mu)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(138)

On the other hand, from the non-asymptotic perspective, for a fixed
N
, selecting the step size
\mu
such that
\mu\leq O(1/\sqrt{N})
ensures the proposed algorithms achieve a convergence rate of
O(1/\sqrt{N})
. This rate is similar to the results in the literature on non-convex optimization
[
57
]
and adversarial learning
[
29
]
. Note that the proof of Theorem
4
relies on the strong-convexity of the Moreau envelope function
J_{\gamma}(w)
. As a result, Theorem
4
can also be used to demonstrate the convergence of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms in non-smooth and convex environments. Basically, when
Q_{k}
is convex over
w
, Danskin’s theorem guarantees that the global risk function
J(w)
is also convex. Then, by selecting the same
\gamma
, the function
J_{\gamma}(w)
remains strongly convex. Furthermore, in the case of convex functions, any stationary point also admits a global minimum.

However, compared to the the traditional non-convex optimization in the clean case
[
57
]
, there is an extra error term, i.e.,
O(\epsilon^{2})
, which affects the final precision in the right-hand side of (
4
). The additional error term arises from the approximation error of the inner maximization; its presence is common in studies on adversarial learning, for example,
[
29
,
30
]
. When the maximizer can be found exactly, this term can be eliminated. As it is usually intractable to compute the accurate maximizer in the non-convex case, approximation methods are applied. Then, the smaller the
\epsilon
is, the better the exact maximizer can be approximated by the Taylor expansion.

Moreover, although both this paper and
[
29
]
study the convergence of the stochastic gradient algorithms in the context of non-convex optimization and adversarial training, and both obtain similar convergence rate of
O(1/(\mu N))
, our results differ as they are derived under different assumptions and conditions. Reference
[
29
]
focuses on the convergence of the adversarial extension of the stochastic gradient algorithm in the single-agent setting, and requires the loss function to be strongly-concave with respect to the perturbation variable
\delta
. This condition ensures some nice properties of the risk function
J(w)
. Basically, the strong-concavity guarantees that the optimal perturbation of (
3
) is unique and satisfies a Lipschitz condition over any two different model parameters.
As a result, functions
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
and
J(w)
remain differentiable, and their gradients satisfy the traditional Lipschitz condition. This allows the traditional analysis method of the stochastic gradient algorithm, developed for nonconvex optimization and standard (clean) training, to be directly extended to the adversarial setting. In contrast, we consider the general nonconvex and nonconcave minimax optimization for adversarial learning in the multi-agent setting. Consequently, functions
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
and
J(w)
may not be differentiable. Thus, we use different conditions, and our analysis follows a different framework from
[
29
]
.

Naturally, if we adopt the same conditions as those in
[
29
]
, their results can be extended to the multi-agent setting. Assume the function
Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is
\tau-
strongly concave over
\delta
, then the optimal perturbation of (
3
) is unique. As a result, the functions
f_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k},\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
,
J_{k}(w)
, and
J(w)
are differentiable. We next state the convergence of the proposed decentralized adversarial training algorithms under the strongly-concave condition, for which the proofs can be found from Appendix
O
.

Corollary 2
.

Consider a network of
K
agents running the decentralized adversarial training algorithms, as listed in (
12a
)–(
12c
) and (
14a
)–(
14c
). Under Assumptions
1
–
2
and
6
–
7
, and assuming
J(w)
is lower bounded, and the function
Q_{k}(w;\boldsymbol{x}_{k}+\delta,\boldsymbol{y}_{k})
is
\tau-
strongly concave over
\delta
, after
N
iterations, it holds that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n}\mathds{E}\|\nabla_{w}J(\boldsymbol{w}_{c,n-1})\|^{2}\leq O(\frac{1}{\mu N})+O(\mu)+O(\epsilon^{2})

(139)

where the
O(\epsilon^{2})
term arises from the approximation error when solving the inner maximization problem in (
3
).

\hfill\square

Corollary
2
extends the convergence result of
[
29
]
from the single-agent setting to the multi-agent case. Unlike Theorem
4
, which guarantees that the proposed algorithms converge to a point near an approximate stationary point of
J(w)
under the general nonconvex nonconcave condition, Corollary
2
means that the proposed algorithms can converge directly to an approximate stationary point of
J(w)
with the nonconvex strongly-concave condition.

Thus, due to cooperation across the networks, the adversarial agents help improve the robustness of clean agents in the clean+adv networks. Likewise, comparing the results between the homogeneous and clean+adv networks, we observe some improvement for the clean accuracy in the clean+adv networks while their robustness deteriorates since now only a half of the agents are trained adversarially. VI
Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed a general decentralized defense mechanism for adversarial attacks over graphs. We analyzed the different convergence properties of the proposed framework for strongly-convex, convex, and non-convex losses. We further illustrated the behavior of the trained networks to perturbations generated by different attack methods, and demonstrated the enhanced robust behavior. Although we motivate our framework by focusing on stochastic gradient implementations, we hasten to add that other more complex optimizers can be used as well. Future directions can be on the trade-off problems in heterogeneous networks, which add more flexibility to the system than traditional single-agent learning.

Appendix A
Analytical solutions for optimal perturbations for convex loss functions

In this section, we list the analytical solutions for the optimal perturbations corresponding to some popular (strongly)convex functions.

A.1. Logistic regression

Consider the logistic loss:

\displaystyle Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})=\mathrm{log}(1+e^{-yw^{\sf T}x})+\rho\|w\|^{2}

(144)

where
\rho\geq 0
, and
y=\left\{+1,-1\right\}
is the label of the data. We know that the function

\displaystyle f(d)=\mathrm{log}(1+e^{-d})

(145)

is decreasing since its gradient is always negative. Thus, we have

\displaystyle{\delta}^{\star}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}yw^{\sf T}x

(146)

according to which we get

\displaystyle\delta^{\star}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}-\epsilon y\frac{w}{\|w\|},&if\;p=2\\
-\epsilon y\mathrm{sign}(w),&if\;p=\infty\end{array}\right.

A.2. Exponential loss

Consider the exponential loss:

\displaystyle Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})=e^{-yw^{\sf T}x}+\rho\|w\|^{2}

(149)

Since the function

\displaystyle f(d)=e^{-d}

(150)

is also decreasing, the analytical solutions for
\delta^{\star}
are the same as the logistic loss.

A.3. Least-mean-squares loss

Consider the least-mean-squares loss:

\displaystyle Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})=\|w^{\sf T}({x}+\delta)-{y}\|^{2}+\rho\|w\|^{2}

(151)

We have

\displaystyle{\delta}^{\star}=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}\ (w^{\sf T}\delta)^{2}+2(w^{\sf T}\delta)\cdot(w^{\sf T}x-y)

(152)

Let
d=w^{\sf T}\delta
whose domain is closed and bounded since

\displaystyle-\epsilon\|w\|_{q}\leq-\|w\|_{q}\|\delta\|_{p}\leq d=w^{\sf T}\delta\leq\|w\|_{q}\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon\|w\|_{q}

(153)

Note that the function

\displaystyle f(d)=d^{2}+2d(w^{\sf T}x-y)

(154)

is strongly-convex over
d
with

\displaystyle f^{\prime}(d)=2d+2(w^{\sf T}x-y)

(155)

Thus, when
f^{\prime}(d)=0
, i.e.,
d=y-w^{\sf T}x
,
f(d)
achieves the minimum. Furthermore, to achieve the maximum of
f(d)
, it is easy to get

\displaystyle d^{\star}=\epsilon\|w\|_{q}\mathrm{sign}(w^{\sf T}x-y)

(156)

from which the analytical expression for
\delta^{\star}
can be obtained:

\displaystyle\delta^{\star}=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\epsilon\mathrm{sign}(w^{\sf T}x-y)\frac{w}{\|w\|},&if\;p=2\\
\epsilon\mathrm{sign}(w^{\sf T}x-y)\mathrm{sign}(w),&if\;p=\infty\end{array}\right.

A.4. Huber loss

Consider the Huber loss:

\displaystyle Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})

\displaystyle=\left\{\begin{array}[]{cc}\frac{1}{2}(w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y)^{2},&if\;|w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y|\leq\tau\\
\tau|w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y|-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2},&otherwise\end{array}\right.

Note that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}\ \tau|w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y|-\frac{\tau^{2}}{2}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}(w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y)^{2}

(161)

Thus, independent of whether
|w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y|
is larger than
\tau
or not, it always holds that

\displaystyle\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}Q(w;{x}+\delta,{y})=\mathop{\mathrm{argmax}}\limits_{\|\delta\|_{p}\leq\epsilon}(w^{\sf T}(x+\delta)-y)^{2}

(162)

Therefore, we conclude that the analytical solutions for the optimal perturbations corresponding to the Huber loss are the same with the least mean square loss.
