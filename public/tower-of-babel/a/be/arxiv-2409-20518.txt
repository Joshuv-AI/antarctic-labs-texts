Omission of Intervals: Deducing covering properties of subsets of the real line from their combinatorial structure
Boaz Tsaban

Abstract
. We develop a method that we call omission of intervals , for establishing topological properties of subsets of the real line based on their combinatorial structure. Using this method, we obtain conceptual proofs of the fundamental theorems in this realm, and new results that were hitherto inaccessible.

Preface

We develop
omission of intervals
,
a novel method for establishing that sets of real numbers with
appropriate combinatorial structures have strong topological covering properties.
The novelty is not only in the obtained results, but also in the uniform

This method is applied in the context of

.

2.
Sets of real numbers

While theorems about relations among properties are striving for minimal restrictions on the involved topological spaces,
examples of spaces with prescribed properties are best when the spaces are as simple and concrete as possible.
All of our examples will be subsets of the real line.
Since the properties considered here are preserved by continuous images,
we can work in topological spaces homeomorphic to subsets of the real line,
such as the Cantor space and the Baire space.

2.1.
The Cantor space

Our starting point is Cantor’s middle-thirds subset of the real line.
This classic set is constructed as follows.
Let
C_{0}:=[0,1]
, the closed unit interval.
For each natural number
n
, let

C_{n}
be the set obtained by removing the middle third (an open interval) from each interval in the set
C_{n-1}
.
Cantor’s set is the intersection
C:=\bigcap_{n=0}^{\infty}C_{n}
.

Definition 2.1
.

Let
\mathfrak{c}:=\left|\mathbb{R}\right|=2^{\aleph_{0}}
, the cardinality of the continuum.

The cardinality of Cantor’s set is
\mathfrak{c}
: For each
n
, enumerate the
2^{n}
intervals in the set
C_{n}
as

\{\,I_{(i_{1},\dotsc,i_{n})}:i_{1},\dotsc,i_{n}\in\{0,1\}\,\}.

Then the map

\displaystyle{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}

\displaystyle\longrightarrow C

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle\longmapsto x_{f}\in\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{(f(1),\dotsc,f(n))}

is bijective.

The construction of Cantor’s set leads naturally to the following classic fact.

Proposition 2.2
.

Every uncountable compact set of real numbers has cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
.
Moreover, if
K
is an uncountable compact set of real numbers and
D
is a countable set, then there is an uncountable compact set
J\subseteq K
that is disjoint from the set
D
.

Proof.

The key to the proof is the following lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X
be an uncountable subset of
\mathbb{R}
.
There are disjoint closed intervals
I_{1}
and
I_{2}
whose intersection with the set
X
is uncountable.

Proof.

Fix an interval
[a,b]
such that the set
[a,b]\cap X
is uncountable.
Let
a^{\prime}
be the supremum of the real numbers
x\geq a
with

X\cap[a,x]
countable, and
b^{\prime}
be the infimum of
the real numbers
x\leq b
with
X\cap[x,b]
countable.
Then
a^{\prime}<b^{\prime}
.
Take any intervals
[a^{\prime},c_{1}]
and
[c_{2},b^{\prime}]
with
a^{\prime}<c_{1}<c_{2}<b^{\prime}
.
∎

Repeat the construction of Cantor’s set, as follows. Let
n
be a natural number. On stage
n
, we have a set
C_{n}
comprising of
2^{n}
disjoint closed intervals, each having an uncountable intersection with the compact set
K
.
For each of these intervals
I
, the set
I\cap K
is an uncountable compact set, and the lemma applies to obtain two
disjoint closed subintervals whose intersection with the set
K
is uncountable. This gives us
2^{n+1}
disjoint intervals for the next stage. The injective map is defined as before,
taking intersections of the form
\bigcap_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{(f(1),\dotsc,f(n))}\cap K
.

To avoid a countable set
D=\{x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc\}
, notice that on
stage
n
, we can apply the lemma twice to obtain
three
disjoint intervals. Out of these, we pick two that do not contain the point
x_{n}
, and proceed as above.
∎

Cantor’s space
is the set
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, equipped with the metric

\dist(a,b):=\frac{1}{{\min\{\,n\in\mathbb{N}:a(n)\neq b(n)\,\}}}

for distinct points
a,b\in{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
Equivalently, as a topological space, the space
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is equipped with the Tychonoff
product topology.
Accordingly, we have
\dist(a,b)<1/n
if and only if

(a(1),\dotsc,a(n))=(b(1),\dotsc,b(n)).

It follows that the complement of an open ball in the Cantor space is a finite union of open balls (of the same radius). Thus, open balls are clopen in the Cantor space.
Moreover, the eventually zero sequences form a countable dense set in the Cantor space,
and thus this space has a countable basis consisting of clopen sets.

The standard bijection from the set
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
to the set
C
, introduced above, is a topological homeomorphism.
In particular, the Cantor space
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is homeomorphic to the Cantor set
C
and,
from a topological perspective,

every subset of the Cantor space is a set of real numbers
.

2.2.
The Cantor space a the power set

We identify, via characteristic functions, the set
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
with the set
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
of all sets of natural numbers. Since we view the space
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
as a set of real numbers, we denote its points by lowercase letters.
The correspondence between the sets
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
and
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
dictates that
the distance between distinct points
a,b\in{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
is

\dist(a,b)=\frac{1}{\min a\Delta b}.

Thus, we have
\dist(a,b)<1/n
if and only if

(1)

a\cap\{1,\dotsc,n\}=b\cap\{1,\dotsc,n\}.

There is a natural partition of the Cantor space,

{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}=[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}\cup\mathrm{Fin},

where
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is the family of infinite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
and
\mathrm{Fin}
is the family of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
.
By Equation (
1
), or by the correspondence with the set
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, the countable set
\mathrm{Fin}
is dense in the space
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
.

2.3.
The Baire space

The
Baire space

{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is equipped with the Tychonoff product topology or, equivalently, with the topology generated by the metric

\dist(a,b)=\frac{1}{\min\{\,n\in\mathbb{N}:a(n)\neq b(n)\,\}}.

Thus, the Cantor space
{\{0,1\}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is a metric subspace of the Baire space.
On the other hand, the Baire space is a
topological
subspace of the Cantor space.
To see this, we first notice that the Baire space
{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is isometric to its subspace consisting of
the increasing functions, by mapping every function

f
to the increasing function
g
defined by

g(n):=f(1)+\dotsb+f(n)

for all
n
.
The
increasing
functions
f\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
are in bijective correspondence
with their image sets
\operatorname{im}(f)
, an element of the Cantor space
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
.
In other words, we identify
every element of the set
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
with its increasing enumeration.
The topology of the space
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
, inherited from the Cantor space
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
, coincides with the topology induced by its identification with the increasing functions in the Baire space.
The identification described here is not uniformly continuous and,
in particular, not an isometry.
However, it is a homeomorphism and, since we deal here with topological properties, every subset of the Baire space may be viewed as a set of real numbers, too.

We view the elements of the set
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
both as infinite sets and as (increasing) functions,
and we refer to them according to their role in every context. Sometimes, we use
the set
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
, on occasions where the set
{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
would be equally good.
But, usually, we will use the set
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
when we wish to restrict attention to increasing functions.

3.
Menger’s Conjecture

3.1.
Menger’s property

Menger’s property is one of the oldest, and most important, selective covering properties.
Motivated by studies in dimension theory, Menger defined a certain metric property
[
25
]
.
Soon afterwards, Hurewicz realized that Menger’s property is equivalent to the topological covering property provided below
[
17
]
.

To avoid technical trivialities, we consider only nontrivial covers of nontrivial topological spaces.

Convention 3.1
.

We consider only infinite Hausdorff topological spaces.
Let
X
be a topological space. A
cover
of the space
X
is a family
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\operatorname{P}(X)
such that
X=\bigcup\mathcal{U}
but
X\notin\mathcal{U}
.
More generally, a
cover
of a subset
Y\subseteq X
is a family
\mathcal{U}\subseteq\operatorname{P}(X)
such that
Y\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{U}
, but the set
Y
is not contained in any single member of the cover.

Recall that a topological space
X
is
compact
if for every open cover
\mathcal{U}
of the space,
there is a finite family
\mathcal{F}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
that covers the space
X
. Compactness is a useful property,
but even very nice spaces, such as the real line
\mathbb{R}
, are not compact.
Menger’s property is a natural relaxation of compactness, by considering a
sequence
of open covers instead of just one.

Definition 3.2
.

For a topological space
X
, let
\mathrm{O}(X)
be the family of open covers of this space.

In the definition, the symbol
\mathrm{O}
stands for arbitrary open covers.
We will later consider restricted types of covers.

Definition 3.3
.

Let
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
be types of covers.
A topological space is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
if
for each sequence

\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{A}(X)
, there are finite sets
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{2}
, …,
such that
\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{F}_{n}\in\mathrm{B}(X)
.

Menger’s property
is the property
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

The selection principle
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}
stands for “select finite”.
We will consider additional selection principles later.

Every compact space is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.
A topological space is
\sigma
-compact
if it is a countable union of
compact sets. For a family of sets
\mathcal{F}
, we denote by
\bigcup\mathcal{F}

(a union without a subscript) the union of the members of
the family
\mathcal{F}
.

Proposition 3.4

(Menger)
.

Every
\sigma
-compact space is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

Proof.

Assume that
X=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}K_{n}
, a countable union of compact sets.
Given a sequence
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{O}(X)
, choose finite sets

\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{2}
, …, such that

K_{1}\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{F}_{1}
,
K_{2}\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{F}_{2}
, etc.
∎

Conjecture 3.5

(Menger)
.

A metric space is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
if and only if it is
\sigma
-compact.

We will address this conjecture soon.
The following assertion follows from Hurewicz’s formulation of Menger’s property.

Lemma 3.6
.

(1)

Menger’s property
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
is preserved by continuous images: If a topological space
X
is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
, then for each continuous surjective map
X\to Y
, the space
Y
is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

(2)

Every closed subset of a
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
space is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.
∎

3.2.
A combinatorial interpretation of Menger’s property

Definition 3.7
.

For functions
a,b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, we write
a\leq b
if
a(n)\leq b(n)
for all
n
.
We write
a\leq^{*}b
if
a(n)\leq b(n)
for
almost all

n
, that is,
if the set
\{\,n\in\mathbb{N}:a(n)\leq b(n)\,\}
is cofinite.
We write
a\leq^{\infty}b
if
a(n)\leq b(n)
for infinitely many
n
.
We define the relations
a<^{*}b
and
a<^{\infty}b
similarly.

Thus, we have
a\nleq^{*}b
if and only if
b<^{\infty}a
.
In practice, it will not matter whether strict or weak inequalities hold,
since we have
a+1\leq b
if and only if
a<b
, and similarly for the other relations.

Definition 3.8
.

A set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is
dominating
if
for each function
f\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
there is a function
s\in S
with
f\leq^{*}s
.

For example, the set
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is dominating.

Definition 3.9
.

Let
\prec
be a binary relation on
{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
For a set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
and an element

b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, we write
S\prec b
if
s\prec b
for all elements
s\in S
.

Corollary 3.10
.

Let
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
The following assertions are equivalent:

(1)

The set
S
is not dominating.

(2)

S<^{\infty}b
for some function
b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.

(3)

S\leq^{\infty}b
for some function
b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.∎

Lemma 3.11
.

Let
X
be an
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
space.
For each sequence
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{O}(X)
,
there are finite sets
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{2}
, …such that, for each point
x\in X
,
we have
x\in\bigcup\mathcal{F}_{n}
for infinitely many
n
.

Proof.

Split the sequence of open covers into infinitely many disjoint sequences, and apply the property

\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
to each of these sequences.
∎

Lemma 3.12
.

No
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
subset of the Baire space
{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is dominating.

Proof.

Fix a
n
. For each
m
, let

O^{n}_{m}:={\left\{\,f\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}:f(n)=m\,\right\}}.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{n}:=\{O^{n}_{1},O^{n}_{2},\dotsc\}
.

Assume that a set
S\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
is
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.
Let
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{2}
, …be finite sets as provided by
Lemma
3.11
. By adding finitely many elements to each set, we may
assume that, for an appropriate function
g\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
,
we have

\mathcal{F}_{1}=\{O^{1}_{1},\dotsc,O^{1}_{g(1)}\},\mathcal{F}_{2}=\{O^{2}_{1},\dotsc,O^{2}_{g(2)}\},\mathcal{F}_{3}=\{O^{3}_{1},\dotsc,O^{3}_{g(3)}\},\dotsc

Then
S\leq^{\infty}g

is not dominating.

A topological space is
Lindelöf
if every open cover of this space has a countable subcover.
Spaces with a countable basis, in particular sets of real numbers, are Lindelöf.
In particular, the Baire space is Lindel”of.
Thus, in contrast to compact spaces, Lindel”of spaces need not be
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

Definition 3.13
.

A cover
\mathcal{V}

refines
a cover
\mathcal{U}
if each element of
\mathcal{V}
is contained in some element of
\mathcal{U}
.

Lemma 3.14
.

Let
X
be a subset of the Cantor space. Every open cover of
X
is refined by a countable clopen cover whose members are pairwise disjoint.

Proof.

Recall that the Cantor space has a countable basis whose elements are clopen sets.
Let
\mathcal{U}\in\mathrm{O}(X)
. Replacing every open set
U\in\mathcal{U}
by all of its basic clopen subsets,
the family
\mathcal{U}
becomes a countable cover by clopen sets.
Enumerate this new family,
\mathcal{U}=\{C_{1},C_{2},\dotsc\}
.
Replacing each clopen set
C_{n}
by the clopen set
C_{n}\setminus(C_{1}\cup\dotsb\cup C_{n-1})
, we may
assume that the elements of the cover
\mathcal{U}
are also pairwise disjoint.
∎

By (2), the image of the map

3.3.
Addressing Menger’s Conjecture

Definition 3.19
.

Let
\kappa
be an uncountable cardinal.
A set
S\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is
\kappa
-unbounded
if
\left|S\right|\geq\kappa
, and for each function
b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
,
we have
\left|\{\,s\in S:s\leq b\,\}\right|<\kappa
.

Remark 3.20
.

A cardinal
\kappa
has
uncountable cofinality
if it is not the limit of a countable increasing sequence of cardinals.
We will consider
\kappa
-unbounded sets for cardinal numbers
\kappa
of uncountable cofinality.
For these cardinals, changing
\leq
to
\leq^{*}
will not alter our definition.

If a set
S\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is
\kappa
-unbounded, then this set is
\lambda
-unbounded for all cardinals
\lambda
with

\kappa\leq\lambda\leq\left|S\right|
.

Lemma 3.21
.

There are
\mathfrak{d}
-unbounded sets in
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
.

Proof.

Fix a dominating set
\{\,f_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{d}\,\}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
For each ordinal
\alpha<\mathfrak{d}
, take an increasing
function
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
with

\{\,f_{\beta}:\beta\leq\alpha\,\}<^{\infty}s
,
and let
s_{\alpha}
be an infinite subset of
s
that is not in the set

\{\,s_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\,\}
.
Since
s\leq s_{\alpha}
, we have

\{\,f_{\beta}:\beta\leq\alpha\,\}<^{\infty}s_{\alpha}\notin\{\,s_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\,\}.

The set
\{\,s_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{d}\,\}
is
\mathfrak{d}
-unbounded:

\left|S\right|=\mathfrak{d}
.
For each function
b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, there is an ordinal
\alpha
with
b\leq^{*}f_{\alpha}
.
For each ordinal

\beta\geq\alpha
, we have

b\leq^{*}f_{\alpha}<^{\infty}s_{\beta},

and thus
b<^{\infty}s_{\beta}
.
∎

Recall that
\mathrm{Fin}
is the set of finite subsets of
\mathbb{N}
, so that
{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}=[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}\cup\mathrm{Fin}

Definition 3.22
.

Let
\kappa
be an uncountable cardinal with

A set

Lemma 3.23

For each set

-concentrated on
\mathrm{Fin}

We can cover the countable set

\mathfrak{d}

Let
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{O}(X)

Take finite sets
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{1}

\mathcal{F}_{2}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{2}

Then

Lemma 3.24
.

(1)

For each compact set
K\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
, there is a function

b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
with
K\leq b
.

(2)

For each function
b\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, the set
{\left\{\,s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}:s\leq b\,\right\}}
is compact.

Proof.

(1) For each
n

\{\,f(n):f\in K\,\}
is a continuous image of the compact set

in
\mathbb{N}

(2) In the Baire space

\{\,s\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}:s\leq b\,\}=\prod_{n}\boldsymbol{\left[\right.}1,b(n)\boldsymbol{\left.\right]}

By Tychonoff’s Theorem, a product of finite sets is compact.
(Alternatively, the set

Since the set

provides a direct proof, that was slightly simplified recently

A set of real numbers is

4.
Interlude: Selecting one element from each family

4.1.
Rothberger’s property

Menger’s property is an instance of the selection principle
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}
.
We now introduce a related selection principle.

Definition 4.1
.

Let
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
be types of covers of topological spaces.
A topological space
X
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
if for each sequence of covers

\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{A}(X)
,
there are sets
U_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{1}
,
U_{2}\in\mathcal{U}_{2}
, …such that
\{U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\}\in\mathrm{B}(X)
.

Rothberger
[
33
]
introduced the property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
as a topological variation of
Borel’s
strong measure zero
property
[
7
]
:
A set
X
of real numbers has strong measure zero if for each sequence of positive real numbers
\epsilon_{1},\epsilon_{2},\dotsc
,
there is a cover by intervals
X\subseteq\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
such that
for each
n
, the length of the interval
I_{n}
is smaller than
\epsilon_{n}
.
Every
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
set of real numbers has strong measure zero.
These properties are restrictive:
Borel conjectured that only countable sets have strong measure zero,
and his conjecture is consistent
[
22
]
.
We have the following observation.

Lemma 4.2
.

For each set
X\subseteq{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
that is
\aleph_{1}
-concentrated on
\mathrm{Fin}
,
the set
X\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

Proof.

We modify the proof of Lemma
3.23
:
We first split the given sequence
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{O}(X)
to two disjoint subsequences.
We then can cover the countable set
\mathrm{Fin}
by elements from the first subsequence.
Since the set
X
is
\aleph_{1}
-concentrated on
\mathrm{Fin}
,
there remain only countably many uncovered elements.
We cover them with elements from the second subsequence.
∎

Since the property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
implies
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
, we have
\operatorname{non}(\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O}))\leq\operatorname{non}(\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O}))=\mathfrak{d}
.
Thus, for example, if
\mathfrak{d}=\aleph_{1}
then we have a nontrivial
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
of real numbers.
All sets that we consider are
\kappa
-concentrated for some cardinal
\kappa\leq\mathfrak{d}
, and thus provide
counterexamples for Borel’s conjecture if we assume an appropriate portion of the Continuum Hypothesis.

4.2.
A provably nontrivial variation of Rothberger’s property

There is another way to see that the sets that we consider are not
\sigma
-compact.

Definition 4.3
.

An cover
\mathcal{U}
of a topological space
X
is

point-cofinite
if it is infinite, and each point
x\in X
is in almost all sets
U\in\mathcal{U}
,
that is, for each point
x\in X
, the set
\{\,U\in\mathcal{U}:x\in U\,\}
is a cofinite subset of the cover
\mathcal{U}
.
Let
\Gamma(X)
be the family of all open point-cofinite covers of the space
X
.

Point-cofinite covers are those covers that are hereditary for infinite subsets.

Lemma 4.4
.

Every infinite subset of a point-cofinite cover of a topological space is a point-cofinite cover of that space.
Conversely, if all infinite subsets of an infinite cover are also covers, then the cover is point-cofinite.
∎

We have the following observation
[
20
, Theorem 1.2]
.

Lemma 4.5

(Just–Miller–Scheepers–Szeptycki)
.

For Lindelöf spaces,
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})=\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

Proof.

Let
X
be an
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})
space.
We need to prove
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{O}(X)
.
If any of these covers has a finite subcover, we are done.
Thus, assume that none has a finite subcover.
For each
n
, thin out the cover
\mathcal{U}_{n}
to obtain a countable cover

\mathcal{U}_{n}:=\{\,U^{n}_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\,\}.

Then, for each
m
, replace each set
U^{n}_{m}
with the set

V^{n}_{m}:=U^{n}_{1}\cup U^{n}_{2}\cup\dotsb\cup U^{n}_{m}.

The family
\{\,V^{n}_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\,\}
is a point-cofinite cover of the space
X
.
By the property
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})
, since the sets
V^{n}_{m}
are increasing with the index
m
,
there are natural numbers
m_{1},m_{2},\dotsc
with

\{\,V^{n}_{m_{n}}:n\in\mathbb{N}\,\}\in\mathrm{O}(X)
.
It follows that

\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\{U^{n}_{1},U^{n}_{2},\dotsc,U^{n}_{m_{n}}\}\in\mathrm{O}(X).\qed

By Lemma
4.5
, for Lindelöf spaces we have

\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})\longrightarrow\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})\longrightarrow\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O}).

We arrive at the following corollary
[
20
, Theorem 4.5]
.

Corollary 4.6

(Just–Miller–Scheepers–Szeptycki)
.

\operatorname{non}(\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O}))=\mathfrak{d}
.

Proof.

(\leq)
The property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})

(Corollary

(\geq)
Suppose that
\left|X\right|<\mathfrak{d}
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\Gamma(X)
.
By moving to infinite subsets, we may assume that the covers
\mathcal{U}_{n}
are countable, and enumerate

\mathcal{U}_{n}=\{\,U^{n}_{m}:m\in\mathbb{N}\,\}

for each
n
.
For each point
x\in X
, define a function
f_{x}\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
by

f_{x}(n):=\min\{\,m:x\in U^{n}_{m}\text{ for all }m\geq n\,\}.

Let
g\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
be a function with

Then every point

is in

.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

For each set
X\subseteq{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}

Since there are

-concentrated sets, we have the following observation

(Bartoszyński–Tsaban)

There are nontrivial
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})

There is a fundamental difference between the property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\mathrm{O})
and Menger’s property
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})

(Just–Miller–Scheepers–Szeptycki)

Assume otherwise.

In his study of Menger’s property
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
, Hurewicz
[

introduced the following, stronger property.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
be types of covers of topological spaces.
A topological space
X
is
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})
if for each sequence of covers

\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathrm{A}(X)

there are finite sets
\mathcal{F}_{1}\subseteq\mathcal{U}_{1}

Hurewicz’s property

\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)

In the notation
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{A},\mathrm{B})

\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}
stands for “union finite”; we
take the
unions
of the selected finite subfamilies.
Since
\sigma
-compact sets are also

3.4

establishes the following fact.

Proposition 5.2

(Hurewicz)
.

Every
\sigma
-compact space is
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
.∎

We thus have the following implications

\sigma\text{-compact}\longrightarrow\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)\longrightarrow\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O}).

Menger’s Conjecture asserted that these three properties are equivalent.
Motivated by that, Hurewicz formulated the following milder conjecture and natural problem.

Conjecture 5.3

(Hurewicz)
.

Every
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
metric space is
\sigma
-compact.

Problem 5.4

(Hurewicz)
.

Is there an
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
set of real numbers that is not
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
?

We will address the conjecture in the next section,
and the problem shortly.
The omitted proofs below are similar to those for
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
, and are left as an exercise.

Lemma 5.5
.

The property
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
is preserved by continuous images, and is hereditary for closed subsets.∎

5.3.
Comments for Section
5

At the correction stage of his paper
[
18
, page 196]
, Hurewicz added a proof of Sierpiński
that Luzin sets are not
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
. Thus, the Continuum Hypothesis settles his problem in the negative.

Within ZFC, the Hurewicz Problem was first solved by Chaber and Pol
[
10
]
, using topological methods developed by Michael.
Their proof is dichotomic:
It provides a nontrivial example if
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{d}
, and a trivial one if
\mathfrak{b}<\mathfrak{d}
.
Theorem
5.25
provides a uniform, nontrivial example.
The author and Zdomskyy used a topological argument for Lemma
5.22
.
Later
[
51
, Lemma 5.6]
, the author provided a combinatorial argument for this lemma,
based on one by Mildenberger
[
26
]
.
Mildenberger’s argument uses an ultrafilter;
the elementary proof provided here is more straightforward.
Lemma
5.22
is also essentially equivalent to the main result in the author’s work with Zdomskyy
[
54
, Theorem 4]
, whose proof was more elaborate.

The Chaber–Pol argument establishes the existence of a non-
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)

set of real numbers
X
such that all finite powers of
X
are
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.
The question whether there is a
nontrivial
example (of cardinality at least
\mathfrak{d}
)
of this kind is still open.
Chaber and Pol provide a nontrivial example when
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{d}
.
The cardinal number
\mathfrak{b}
is regular, that is, it is not the supremum of fewer than
\mathfrak{b}
ordinals that are smaller than
\mathfrak{b}
.
Zdomskyy and the author proved that the regularity of the cardinal

\mathfrak{d}
suffices for a positive answer to this question
[
55
, Theorem 4.6]
.

6.1.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let

The set

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
By Lemma

(\Rightarrow)
Assume that

For each set

\tilde{s}\leq\tilde{b}_{k}

Pick a function

with
\tilde{b}_{k}\leq^{*}c
for all

Then
\tilde{S}\leq^{*}c
.
∎

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
S\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}

(1)

The set

is unbounded.

(2)

-intervals, are nonempty.
Take a set

that omits infinitely many open
\tilde{a}
-intervals.
In particular, we have
s\neq\mathbb{N}
.
Then

(Corollary

).

(1)\Rightarrow(2)

such that for each set

, we have

s\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}a(n),\allowbreak a(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\neq\emptyset

for almost all
n
.
Let
k
be a natural number with
s\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}a(n),\allowbreak a(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\neq\emptyset
for all

Then

for all

Let

be a function with

7.

7.1.
Diagonalizing point-cofinite covers

For a topological space
X
, let
\operatorname{C}(X)
be the family of continuous real-valued functions on the space
X
,
endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence of nets, or equivalently, viewed as a subspace of the Tychonoff product space
\mathbb{R}^{X}
.
For functions
f,f_{1},f_{2},\dotsc\in\operatorname{C}(X)
we have
f_{n}\longrightarrow f
if and only if
f_{n}(x)\to f(x)
for every point
x\in X
.

The following notions are motivated by old studies in trigonometric series;
we refer to the introduction of Bukovský, Recław and Repický for details
[
9
]
.
A convergence of real-valued functions
f_{n}\longrightarrow f
is
quasinormal
if there is a sequence of positive real numbers
\epsilon_{n}\longrightarrow 0

such that for each point
x\in X
we have

\left|f_{n}(x)-f(x)\right|\leq\epsilon_{n}

for almost all
n
.
A topological space
X
is a
QN space
if every convergent sequence in the space
\operatorname{C}(X)
converges quasinormally.
It is a
wQN space
if every convergent sequence in
\operatorname{C}(X)
has a subsequence that converges quasinormally.

Scheepers
[
42
, Theorem 8]
proved that every
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
space is wQN.
Sakai
[
37
, Theorem 2.5]
and Bukovský–Haleš
[
8
, Theorem 9]
proved that a subset of the Cantor space is wQN if and only if it is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
for clopen covers.
Since
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\Gamma,\Gamma)=\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
, the property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
implies
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
.
By Proposition
4.9
, the property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
is strictly stronger than
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
.

Definition 7.14

The
Scheepers Conjecture

[
42
, Conjecture 1]
asserts that
for subsets of the Cantor space, the property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
restricted to clopen covers is equivalent to
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
(for open covers).
After two decades of study of this conjecture by various mathematicians, Peng
[
34
, Section 4]
proved that this conjecture is false:
Assuming the Continuum Hypothesis, there is a subset of the Cantor space that is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
for clopen covers but not for arbitrary open covers.
If we consider countable Borel covers instead of open covers, the properties corresponding to
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
, wQN and
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
are all equivalent,
and constitute a much stronger property
[
44
,
56
]
.

The property wQN is not the only manifestation of
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
(for clopen covers) in the realm of function spaces.
Arhangel’skiĭ
[
1
,
2
]
introduced four properties of topological space, that
help determining when a product of Fréchet–Urysohn spaces is Fréchet–Urysohn.
He named these properties
\alpha_{1}
to
\alpha_{4}
.
A topological space is
\alpha_{2}
if for every sequence of sequences converging to the same element,
we can choose one element from each sequence, and obtain a sequence that converges to the same element.
Scheepers
[
42
, Theorems 8 and 9]
, based on Fremlin
[
12
, Section 12]
, proved that for a topological space
X
,
the function space
\operatorname{C}(X)
is
\alpha_{2}
if and only if the space
X
is wQN.

The proof of Proposition
7.7
bridges a minor gap in the author’s original argument.

Miller and the author
[
28
, Theorem 2.8]
proved that if the union of fewer than
\mathfrak{b}
set that are
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
then for each unbounded
\mathfrak{b}
-tower
T
, all finite powers of the set
T\cup\mathrm{Fin}
are
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
.
Szewczak and Włudecka
[
47
, Theorem 3.1]
prove the same assertion
without any hypothesis
.
Moreover, they establish that every product of such sets is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
, and even of more general types of sets.

The original Bartoszyński–Shelah proof of Theorem
6.10
does not imply Theorem
7.16
.
Theorem
7.13
was first established for a slightly more general family than
\mathfrak{b}(\sqsubseteq^{*})
-scales
[

, Theorem 4.4]
,
but the relation used here is transitive, which is convenient.

8.
The Crown Theorem

Recall that for a topological space
X
, the space
\operatorname{C}(X)
consists of the continuous real-valued functions on
X
, with the topology of pointwise convergence.
The basic open neighborhoods of the zero function are the sets

[F,m]:={\bigl\{\,f\in\operatorname{C}(X):\left|f(x)\right|<\tfrac{1}{m}\text{ for }x\in F\,\bigr\}},

for
F\subseteq X
finite and
m\in\mathbb{N}
.

A topological space
X
is Tychonoff if for each point
a
and every closed set
C
with
a\notin C
,
there is a function
f\in\operatorname{C}(X)
that is
0
on the set
C
and
1
on the point
a
.
Every metric space, in particular every set of real numbers, is Tychonoff.
We have the following fact.

Proposition 8.1
.

For an infinite Tychonoff space
X
, the space
\operatorname{C}(X)
is metrizable if and only if the set
X
is countable.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
If the set
X
is countable, then the space
\mathbb{R}^{X}
is homeomorphic to the metrizable space
\mathbb{R}^{\mathbb{N}}
.
The space
\operatorname{C}(X)
is a subspace of
\mathbb{R}^{X}
.

(\Rightarrow)
Suppose that the space
\operatorname{C}(X)
is metrizable.
In particular, there is a countable local base at the zero function
0
.
We may assume that it is of the form

\{\,[F_{n},m_{n}]:n\in\mathbb{N}\,\}
.
Let
a\in X\setminus\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}{F_{n}}
.
For each
n
, we have
[F_{n},m_{n}]\nsubseteq[\{a\},1]
:
Let
U
be an open neighborhood of the point
a
that is disjoint from the finite set
F_{n}
.
Since the space
X
is Tychonoff, there is a function
f\in\operatorname{C}(X)
that is
0
on the set
U^{\text{\tt c}}
and
1
on the point
a
.
∎

A topological space
Y
is
Fréchet–Urysohn
if for each set
A\subseteq Y
,
every point in the closure of
A
is the limit of a convergent sequence of points from the set
A
.
Every metric space is Fréchet–Urysohn, and it is natural to ask whether, for uncountable sets of real numbers
X
,
the function space
\operatorname{C}(X)
may be Fréchet–Urysohn.

Definition 8.2
.

Let
\mathrm{A}
and
\mathrm{B}
be types of covers of topological spaces.
A topological space
X
is
\begin{pmatrix}\mathrm{A}\\
\mathrm{B}\end{pmatrix}
if every cover in
\mathrm{A}(X)
has a subcover in
\mathrm{B}(X)
.

For example,
compact
and
Lindelöf
are properties of this kind.
The following result
[
15
, Theorem 2]
translates the Fréchet–Urysohn property of the space
\operatorname{C}(X)
to a covering property of
X
.

Theorem 8.3

(Gerlits–Nagy)
.

Let
X
be a set of real numbers.
The space
\operatorname{C}(X)
is Fréchet–Urysohn if and only if the set
X
is
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
.

To carry out their proof, Gerlits and Nagy establish the following result.

Lemma 8.4
.

\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}=\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Gamma)
.

Proof.

(\Leftarrow)
Let
\mathcal{U}\in\Omega(X)
.
Apply
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Gamma)
to the constant sequence

\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{U},\dotsc
to obtain elements

U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\in\mathcal{U}
with
\{U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\}\in\Gamma(X)
.

(\Rightarrow)
Assume that
X
is
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
.
Let
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\Omega(X)
.
For each
n
, we have

\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime}:={\left\{\,U_{1}\cap\dotsb\cap U_{n}:U_{1}\in\mathcal{U}_{1},\dotsc,U_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}\,\right\}}\in\Omega(X),

the cover
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime}
refines the cover
\mathcal{U}_{n}
, and the covers
\mathcal{U}_{n}^{\prime}
get finer as
n
increases.
By Lemma
6.7
(2), if we enlarge the open sets in a point-cofinite cover the cover remains point-cofinite.
Thus, it suffices to consider these finer covers.
Thus, we may assume that for each
n
, the cover
\mathcal{U}_{n+1}
refines the cover
\mathcal{U}_{n}
.

Fix an injective sequence
x_{1},x_{2},\dotsc
in
X
.
For each
n
, let

\mathcal{V}_{n}:={\left\{\,U\setminus\{x_{n}\}:U\in\mathcal{U}_{n}\,\right\}}.

Then
\mathcal{V}:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathcal{V}_{n}\in\Omega(X)
, and thus there is a point-cofinite subfamily
\mathcal{W}\subseteq\mathcal{V}
.
It follows that for each
n
, there are only finitely many members of the family
\mathcal{V}_{n}
in
\mathcal{W}
.
By moving to a subset of
\mathcal{W}
, we may assume that there is a set
I\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
and for each
n\in I

U_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
with

\mathcal{W}={\left\{\,U_{n}\setminus\{x_{n}\}:n\in I\,\right\}}.

For
n\notin I
, let
k\in I
be greater than
n
, and choose a set
U_{n}\in\mathcal{U}_{n}
with
U_{k}\subseteq U_{n}
.
By Lemma
6.7
(2), we have
\{U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\}\in\Gamma(X)
.
∎

In particular, the property
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
implies
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
;
we will use this connection.

Definition 8.5
.

A set
S\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is
centered
if every finite subset of
S
has an infinite intersection.
An infinite set

is a

of a set

if for each set

we have
a\subseteq^{*}s
.
Let
\mathfrak{p}
be the minimal cardinality of a centered set that has no pseudointersection.

It is known that
\aleph_{1}\leq\mathfrak{p}\leq\mathfrak{b}
, and that strict inequalities are consistent
[
6
]
.

Lemma 8.6

(Arhangel’skiĭ)
.

Every open
\omega
-cover of a set of real numbers has a countable
\omega
-subcover of that set.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{U}\in\Omega(X)
.
For each natural number
k
, we have
\{\,U^{k}:U\in\mathcal{U}\,\}\in\mathrm{O}(X^{k})
.
Since
X
is a set of real numbers, its finite powers are Lindelöf, and there is a countable subcover

\mathcal{U}_{k}\in\mathrm{O}(X^{k})
.
It follows that for each
k
-element set
F\subseteq X
there is an element
U^{k}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}
with
F\subseteq U
.
Thus, we have

\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\{\,U:U^{k}\in\mathcal{U}_{k}\,\}\in\Omega(X).\qed

Proposition 8.7

(Gerlits–Nagy)
.

\operatorname{non}(\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix})=\mathfrak{p}
.

Proof.

Let
S\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
be a centered set of cardinality
\mathfrak{p}
that has no pseudointersection.
For each
n
, let

O_{n}:=\{\,a\in{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}:n\in a\,\}

Since the set

\{\,O_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\,\}\in\Omega(X)

If
\{\,O_{n}:n\in b\,\}\in\Gamma(X)

is a pseudointersection of

A contradiction.
This shows that
\operatorname{non}(\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix})\leq\mathfrak{p}
.

On the other hand, let
X

\left|X\right|<\mathfrak{p}
.
Suppose that
\mathcal{U}\in\Omega(X)

By Lemma

Enumerate

The sets

Then

.
∎

.

(\Rightarrow)

(\Leftarrow)
Assume that

Then there is an unbounded set
\{\,f_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{p}\,\}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}

We construct an unbounded tower

For

∎

Lemma 8.9
.

Let

\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)

For each sequence
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\Gamma(X)

Lemma 8.10

Let

There are infinite pairwise disjoint subsets

Proof.

Begin with the sets

Theorem 8.12

(Orenshtein–Tsaban)
.

For each unbounded
\mathfrak{p}
-tower
T\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
,
the set
T\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
.

Proof.

Let
T=\{\,t_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{p}\,\}
be an unbounded
\mathfrak{p}
-tower.
Let
X:=T\cup\mathrm{Fin}
.
For an ordinal
\alpha<\mathfrak{b}
, let

\displaystyle X_{<\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Fin}\cup\{\,t_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\,\};

\displaystyle X_{\geq\alpha}

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Fin}\cup\{\,t_{\beta}:\alpha\leq\beta<\mathfrak{b}\,\}.

We will use, repeatedly, the following simple variation of observations made in the earlier proofs.

Lemma 8.13
.

Let
\mathcal{U}\in\Omega(T\cup\mathrm{Fin})
.
For each ordinal
\alpha<\mathfrak{p}
, there are an ordinal
\beta<\mathfrak{p}
with
\alpha<\beta
and a set
\mathcal{V}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
with

\mathcal{V}\in\Gamma(X_{<\alpha}\cup X_{\geq\beta})
.

Proof.

If
\mathcal{V}\in\Gamma(X_{<\alpha}\cup X_{\geq\beta_{0}})
, then

\mathcal{V}\in\Gamma(X_{<\alpha}\cup X_{\geq\beta})
for all ordinals
\beta<\mathfrak{p}
with
\beta_{0}\leq\beta
.
Thus, it suffices to prove the lemma without the restriction
\alpha<\beta
.

Since
\left|X_{<\alpha}\right|<\mathfrak{p}
and
\mathcal{U}\in\Omega(X_{<\alpha})
,
there is a set
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
with
\mathcal{U}^{\prime}\in\Gamma(X_{<\alpha})
(Lemma
8.7
).
By Lemma
6.8
, there are a set
a\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
with
a(1)=1
and distinct sets
U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\in\mathcal{U}^{\prime}
such that for each
n
,
all points
x\in{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
that omit the interval
\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}a(n),\allowbreak a(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}
are in the set
U_{n}
.
We have
\{U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\}\in\Gamma(X_{<\alpha})
.

Since the set
T
is unbounded, there is an ordinal
\beta<\mathfrak{p}
such that the set

I:=\{\,n:t_{\beta}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}a(n),\allowbreak a(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}=\emptyset\,\}

is infinite (Lemma
6.2
).
Then
\mathcal{V}:=\{\,U_{n}:n\in I\,\}\in\Gamma(X_{<\alpha})
.

Let
\gamma<\mathfrak{p}
be an ordinal with
\beta\leq\gamma
.
For almost all
n\in I
, we have

t_{\gamma}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}a(n),\allowbreak a(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\subseteq t_{\beta}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}a(n),\allowbreak a(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}=\emptyset,

and thus
t_{\gamma}\in U_{n}
. Thus, we also have
\mathcal{V}\in\Gamma(X_{\geq\beta})
.
∎

Let
\alpha_{1}:=0
.
By Lemma
8.13
, for each natural number
n
there are a set
\mathcal{V}_{n}\subseteq\mathcal{U}
and an ordinal
\alpha_{n+1}<\mathfrak{p}

such that
\alpha_{n}<\alpha_{n+1}
and

\mathcal{V}_{n}\in\Gamma(X_{\alpha_{n}}\cup X_{\geq\alpha_{n+1}})
.
Let
\alpha:=\sup_{n}\alpha_{n}
.
For each
n
, let

Y_{n}:=X_{\alpha_{n}}\cup X_{\geq\alpha}.

Then
X=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}Y_{n}
, the union is increasing, and
\mathcal{V}_{n}\in\Gamma(X_{n})
for all
n
.

For each ordinal
\gamma\leq\alpha
, the set

\{\,\beta:\beta<\gamma\,\}\cup\{\,\beta:\alpha\leq\beta<\mathfrak{b}\,\}

is order-isomorphic to the set
\{\,\beta:\beta<\mathfrak{b}\,\}
, and thus
the set

\{\,t_{\beta}:\beta<\gamma\,\}\cup\{\,t_{\beta}:\alpha\leq\beta<\mathfrak{p}\,\}

is an unbounded
\mathfrak{b}
-tower.
By Theorem
7.6
, for each
n
, the set
Y_{n}
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
.

By Proposition
8.11
, there are sets

U_{1}\in\mathcal{V}_{1}
,

U_{2}\in\mathcal{V}_{2}
,

U_{3}\in\mathcal{V}_{3}
,
…,
such that

\{U_{1},U_{2},\dotsc\}\in\Gamma(X)
.
∎

8.1.
Comments for Section
8

The interest in the Crown Theorem (Theorem
8.12
) is as old as the Gerlits–Nagy seminal paper
[
15
]
.
The property
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
is very restrictive.
We have

\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}=\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Gamma)\longrightarrow\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\mathrm{O})=\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O}).

For the last equality
[
40
, Theorem 17]
, suppose that our space is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\mathrm{O})
and we are given a sequence of open covers.
We split the sequence into infinitely many disjoint subsequences, and produce from each subsequence a single
\omega
-cover,
consisting of all finite unions taking at most one set from each open cover.
We then apply our property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\mathrm{O})
to obtain a cover, and disassemble the unions to single sets.
This shows that our space is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
.
In particular,
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
sets of real numbers are strong measure zero.

Gerlits and Nagy noticed that uncountable
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
sets of real numbers exist
if
\aleph_{1}<\mathfrak{p}
(Proposition
8.7
), but did not know whether they may be nontrivial.
Galvin and Miller
[
14
, Theorem 1]
proved that nontrivial
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
sets exist if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{c}
.
Just, Miller, Scheepers and Szeptycki
[
20
, Theorem 5.1, Case 2]
proved that for each unbounded
\aleph_{1}
-tower

T
, the set
T\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Omega)
, and Scheepers
[
42
, Theorem 3]
proved that such a set must be
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.
They asked whether it must be
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}

[
20
, Problem 7]
.
Scheepers proved that for each unbounded
\mathfrak{p}
-tower
T
, the set
T\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)

[
41
, Theorem 6]
.
In Theorem 11 of the ArXiv version of his paper
[
27
]
,
Miller proves a theorem of Bartoszyński that a property strictly stronger than
\aleph_{1}=\mathfrak{b}
, denoted
\diamondsuit(\mathfrak{b})
, implies that there is a nontrivial
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
set,
and asked whether the hypothesis
\aleph_{1}=\mathfrak{b}
suffices.
In their study of a problem of Malykhin, Gruenhage and Szeptycki
[
16
, Corollary 10]
prove that if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}
then there is a set of real numbers with a property weaker than

\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
, and wonder whether
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}
implies the existence of a nontrivial
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
set.
The Crown Theorem improves upon all of these partial results, and answers these questions.

The author still does not fully understand his first proof of the Crown Theorem
[
30
, Theorem 3.6]
.
In 2016, while preparing a course on omission of intervals, he came up with a simpler proof, that he
presented in several conferences and included in a paper with Osipov and Szewczak
[
32
, Theorem 6]
.
That proof was still somewhat technical.
He arrived at the present, conceptual proof while working on the present paper,
motivated by the preparation of his 2023 course on omission of intervals.

The property
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
is preserved by finite powers.
It is not provably preserved by finite products:
Miller, Zdomskyy and the author
[
29
, Theorem 3.2]
proved that,
assuming the Continuum Hypothesis, there are two
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
(even for countable Borel covers)
sets of real numbers whose product is not even
\mathsf{S}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\mathrm{O})
. On the other hand
[
29
, Theorem 2.8]
they proved that
for each unbounded
\aleph_{1}
-tower
T
, the product of the set
T\cup\mathrm{Fin}

with every
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
set is
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
.
Szewczak and Włudecka
[
47
, Theorem 4.1]
prove that
this is, in fact, the case for every (generalized) unbounded
\mathfrak{p}
-tower
T
.

Refined scales and nonimplications

9.1.
Refinements

The proofs of our main theorems tell more than their statements.
For the following applications, we need to state some of these theorems more generally.

Definition 9.1
.

Let
R
be a binary relation on the set
[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
.
A set
B\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is an
R
-refinement
of a set
A\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
if
there is a surjection
\varphi\colon A\to B
with
aR\varphi(a)
for all
a\in A
.

Every
\supseteq^{*}
-refinement is a
\leq^{*}
-refinement and a
\sqsubseteq^{*}
-refinement.

Theorem 9.2
.

For each
\leq^{*}
-refinement
S^{\prime}
of a
\mathfrak{b}
-scale
S
, the set

S^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{U}_{\operatorname{id}}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
S=\{\,s_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{b}\,\}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
be a
\mathfrak{b}
-scale.
Using the surjection, we may write

S^{\prime}=\{\,s^{\prime}_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{b}\,\}
, where

s_{\alpha}\leq^{*}s^{\prime}_{\alpha}
for all
\alpha<\mathfrak{b}
.

We define a set
I
as in our proof of Theorem
6.10
.
In the remainder of the proof, we change everywhere
s
to
s^{\prime}
,
except for the case
\beta\geq\alpha
, where we use that

s_{\alpha}\leq^{*}s_{\beta}\leq^{*}s^{\prime}_{\beta}
, so that

s_{\alpha}\leq^{*}s^{\prime}_{\beta}
.

This establishes
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
.
The upgrade to
\mathsf{U}_{\operatorname{id}}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
is as in the proof of Theorem
7.16
, that is, using
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
instead of
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
in the first step.
∎

Similar modifications of the proofs of Theorem
7.13
, Theorem
7.6
and Theorem
8.12

establish the following results.
Here too, we use the transitivity of the relations
\sqsubseteq^{*}
and
\subseteq^{*}
.

Theorem 9.3
.

For each
\sqsubseteq^{*}
-refinement
S^{\prime}
of a
\mathfrak{b}(\sqsubseteq^{*})
-scale
S
, the set

S^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{U}_{2}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
. ∎

Theorem 9.4
.

For each
\supseteq^{*}
-refinement
T^{\prime}
of an unbounded
\mathfrak{b}
-tower
T
, the set

T^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
.
And if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}
, then this set is
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
.
∎

9.2.
Hitting sets

Definition 9.5
.

We fix a partition
\mathbb{N}=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
with

1=\min I_{1}<\min I_{2}<\min I_{3}<\dotsb.

Let

\mathcal{I}:={\left\{\,s\in{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}:s\cap I_{n}\text{ is finite for all }n\,\right\}}

For each
n
and each
m\in I_{n}
, let

O^{n}_{m}:=\{\,x\in{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}:n\notin x\,\}.

Then
\mathcal{O}_{n}:=\{\,O^{n}_{m}:m\in I_{n}\,\}\in\Gamma(\mathcal{I})
for all
n
.

For a set
A
and a natural number
k
, let
[A]^{k}:=\{\,F\subseteq A:\left|F\right|=k\,\}
.
Let
f\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
.
A set
S\subseteq\mathcal{I}
is
f
-hitting
if
for each function
g\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[I_{n}]^{f(n)}
there is an element
s\in S
with
g(n)\subseteq s
for infinitely many
n
.
It is
k
-hitting
if it is
f
-hitting for the constant function
f(n)=k
.
For
k=1
, we identify the sets
[I_{n}]^{1}
with the sets
I_{n}
.
A set
S\subseteq\mathcal{I}
is
*
-hitting
if for each function
g\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\mathrm{Fin}(I_{n})
there is an element
s\in S
with
g(n)\subseteq s
for infinitely many
n
.

Proposition 9.6
.

Let
X\subseteq\mathcal{I}
.

(1)

If the set
X
is
1
-hitting, then it is not
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
.

(2)

If the set
X
is
k
-hitting for
k\geq 2
, then it is not
\mathsf{U}_{k}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.

(3)

If the set
X
is
f
-hitting for an unbounded function
f\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
, then it is not
\mathsf{U}_{\operatorname{id}}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.

(4)

If the set
X
is
*
-hitting, then it is not
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that there are
m_{1}\in I_{1}
,
m_{2}\in I_{2}
, …such that

\{O^{1}_{m_{1}},O^{2}_{m_{2}},\dotsc\}\in\Gamma(X).

Let
g(n):=m_{n}
for all
n
.
For each set
x\in X
, for almost all
n
we have
x\in O^{n}_{m_{n}}
, and thus
g(n)\notin x
.
A contradiction.

(2) Suppose that there are sets
F_{1}\in[I_{1}]^{k}
,
F_{2}\in[I_{2}]^{k}
, …such that

{\bigl\{\,\bigcup_{m\in F_{n}}O^{n}_{m}:n\in\mathbb{N}\,\bigr\}}\in\Gamma(X).

Let
g(n):=F_{n}
for all
n
.
For each set
x\in X
, for almost all
n
,
there is
m\in F_{n}
with
x\in O^{n}_{m}
, that is,
m\notin x
, and thus
g(n)\nsubseteq x
.
A contradiction.

(3) By Lemma
7.15
, we have
\mathsf{U}_{f}(\Gamma,\Gamma)=\mathsf{U}_{\operatorname{id}}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.
The proof that the set
X
is not
\mathsf{U}_{f}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
is similar to the previous proofs.

The proof (4) is also similar.
∎

9.3.
Nonimplications

Theorem 9.7
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{c}
.
Every
\sqsubseteq^{*}
-refinement
S
of a
\mathfrak{b}(\sqsubseteq^{*})
-scale has a
\sqsubseteq^{*}
-refinement
S^{\prime}
such the set
S^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{U}_{2}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
but not
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
.

Moreover, if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{c}
then the set
S^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is also
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
S=\{\,s_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{b}\,\}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
be a
\sqsubseteq^{*}
-refinement of a
\mathfrak{b}(\sqsubseteq^{*})
-scale.
Enumerate

\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}=\{\,g_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\,\}
.
For each
\alpha<\mathfrak{b}
, pick an infinite subset
s^{\prime}_{\alpha}\subseteq g_{\alpha}[\mathbb{N}]
with
s_{\alpha}\sqsubseteq^{*}s^{\prime}_{\alpha}
.
Let

X:=\{\,s^{\prime}_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{b}\,\}\cup\mathrm{Fin}.

By Theorem
9.3
, the set
X
is
\mathsf{U}_{2}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.

The set
X
is 1-hitting: For each function
g\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}I_{n}
,
there is
\alpha<\mathfrak{c}
with
g=g_{\alpha}
.
Since
s^{\prime}_{\alpha}\subseteq g_{\alpha}[\mathbb{N}]
, we have
g(n)\in s^{\prime}_{\alpha}
for infinitely many
n
.
By Proposition
9.6
, the set
X
it is not
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{c}
.
The result we establish in this case is stronger than the asserted one.
It involves the Gerlits–Nagy
\delta
property
[
15
, Section 2]
,
a property stronger than
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Omega)

[
31
, Lemma 4.4]
.
S. Bardyla, J. Šupina and L. Zdomskyy proved that,
if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{c}
, then every set
A\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
of cardinality
\mathfrak{c}
has a
\supseteq^{*}
-refinement
A^{\prime}

such that the set

A^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\delta

[
3
, Theorem 3.1]
.

Let
S^{\prime\prime}
be a
\supseteq^{*}
-refinement of the set
S^{\prime}
such that the set
S^{\prime\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is a
\delta
-set (and thus
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Omega)
).
By Theorem
9.3
, the set
S^{\prime\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{U}_{2}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.
Since there are in the set infinite subsets of all possible images
g_{\alpha}[\mathbb{N}]
,
the set remains 1-hitting, and thus not
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
(Proposition
9.6
).
∎

Lemma 9.8
.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
,
g\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[I_{n}]^{k}
and
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
.
There is an infinite set
t\subseteq g[\mathbb{N}]
with
s\leq s^{\prime}:=\bigcup t
.

Proof.

Let
m_{1},m_{2},\dotsc
be an increasing sequence with

s(kn)<\min I_{m_{n}}
for all
n
.
Let
s^{\prime}:=\bigcup_{n=1}^{\infty}g(m_{n})
.
For each
n
, we have

\left|\boldsymbol{\left[\right.}1,s(kn)\boldsymbol{\left.\right]}\cap s^{\prime}\right|\leq\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{n-1}g(m_{i})\right|=k(n-1),

and thus
s(kn)<s^{\prime}(k(n-1)+1)
.
For each
i
, pick
n
with
i\in\boldsymbol{\left[\right.}k(n-1)+1,kn\boldsymbol{\left.\right]}
.
Then

s(i)\leq s(kn)\leq s^{\prime}(k(n-1)+1)\leq s^{\prime}(i).\qed

Theorem 9.9
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{c}
.
Every
\leq^{*}
-refinement
S
of a
\mathfrak{b}
-scale has a
\leq^{*}
-refinement
S^{\prime}
such that
the set
S^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is
\mathsf{U}_{\operatorname{id}}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
but not
\mathsf{U}_{k}(\Gamma,\Gamma)

.

Moreover, if
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{c}
then the set
S^{\prime}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
is also
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Omega,\Omega)
.

Proof.

Let
S=\{\,s_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{b}\,\}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
be a
\leq^{*}
-refinement of a
\mathfrak{b}
-scale.
Enumerate

\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[I_{n}]^{k}=\{\,g_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\,\}
.
For each
\alpha<\mathfrak{b}
, pick an infinite subset

with

).
Let

By Theorem

, the set
X
is

This settled an important problem from the Gerlits–Nagy seminal paper
[

.
Bardyla, Šupina and Zdomskyy proceed to establish an impressive array of results of this type,

The author has recently wrote a variation of the Bardyla–Šupina–Zdomskyy proof that gives a stronger result
[

]
.

The Crown Theorem asserts that the hypothesis
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}
suffices for the existence of a nontrivial
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
set of real numbers.
The examples considered in this paper are all based on unions of (variations of)
\mathfrak{b}
-scales with the set
\mathrm{Fin}
.
The hypothesis
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}
is consistent with the assertion that every union of a
\mathfrak{b}
-scale with
\mathrm{Fin}
is an
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
set
[

]
.
It remains open whether counterexamples such as the ones presented in this section can be proved to exist based on the hypothesis
\mathfrak{p}=\mathfrak{b}
.
A closely related question is whether it is provable, outright in ZFC, that the properties
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
and
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)

are nonequivalent for subsets of the real line.

By our results, the types of sets considered in the previous sections cannot provide examples of the kinds discussed below.
For that, we consider perturbations of scales.

Theorem 10.3
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{c}
.
For each
k
,
there are
\mathsf{U}_{k+1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
sets of real numbers that are not
\mathsf{U}_{k}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.

Proof.

Let
k\in\mathbb{N}
.
Enumerate
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[I_{n}]^{k}=\{\,g_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\,\}
.
Let
S=\{\,s_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\,\}
be a
\mathfrak{b}(\sqsubseteq^{*})
-scale.
For each
\alpha<\mathfrak{c}
, pick an infinite set
t_{\alpha}\subseteq g_{\alpha}[\mathbb{N}]

such that the set
s^{\prime}_{\alpha}:=\bigcup t_{\alpha}
has

\left|s^{\prime}_{\alpha}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}s_{\alpha}(n),s_{\alpha}(n+2)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq k

for all
n
(Lemma
10.2
).
Let
X:=\{\,s^{\prime}_{\alpha}:\alpha<\mathfrak{c}\,\}\cup\mathrm{Fin}
.

The set
X
is
k
-hitting, and thus not
\mathsf{U}_{k}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
.
To see that the set is
\mathsf{U}_{k+1}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
, we proceed as in the proof of Theorem
7.13
, using Lemma
10.1
.

Let
\mathcal{U}_{1},\mathcal{U}_{2},\dotsc\in\Gamma(S\cup\mathrm{Fin})

By Lemma
10.1
,
for each
n
there are a function
b_{n}\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
with
b_{n}(1)=1
and distinct sets
U^{n}_{1},U^{n}_{2},\dotsc\in\mathcal{U}_{n}

such that, for each

,

with

\left|x\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b_{n}(m),\allowbreak b_{n}(m\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq k,

are in the set

U^{n}_{(k+1)(m-1)+1}\cup\dotsb\cup U^{n}_{(k+1)m}.

By Lemma
7.5
, there is a set
b\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
with
\{\,b_{n}:n\in\mathbb{N}\,\}\sqsubseteq^{*}b
.
There is a function
c\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
such that, for each
n
, for all
m\geq c(n)
,
the interval
\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b(m),\allowbreak b(m\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}
contains an open
b_{n}
-interval,
say
\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b_{n}(i_{n,m}),\allowbreak b_{n}(i_{n,m}\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}
.
If
m\geq c(n)
and a set
x\in{\operatorname{P}(\mathbb{N})}
has

\left|x\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b(m),\allowbreak b(m\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq k,

then
\left|x\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b_{n}(i_{n,m}),\allowbreak b_{n}(i_{n,m}\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq k
,
and thus

x\in W^{n}_{m}:=U^{n}_{(k+1)(i_{n,m}-1)+1}\cup\dotsb\cup U^{n}_{(k+1)i_{n,m}}.

As the set
S
is unbounded, there is by Lemma
6.2
an ordinal
\alpha<\mathfrak{b}
,
such that the set

I:=\{\,m:s_{\alpha}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b(m),\allowbreak b(m\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}=\emptyset\,\}

is infinite.
Since the set
I
is infinite, for each
n
we have

{\left\{\,W^{n}_{m}:c(n)\leq m\in I\,\right\}}\in\Gamma(S\cup\mathrm{Fin}).

The set
\mathrm{Fin}\cup{\left\{\,s_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\,\right\}}
is
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
(Lemma
7.1
).
Thus, there is a function
g\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
with
c(n)\leq g(n)\in I
for all
n
, such that

{\left\{\,W^{n}_{g(n)}:n\in\mathbb{N}\,\right\}}\in\Gamma(\mathrm{Fin}\cup{\left\{\,s_{\beta}:\beta<\alpha\,\right\}}).

We have
n\leq c(n)\leq g(n)
for all
n
.

Let
\beta\geq\alpha
. Then
s_{\alpha}\sqsubseteq^{*}s_{\beta}
.
As in the proof of Theorem

,
for almost all

we have
\left|s_{\beta}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b(g(n)),\allowbreak b(g(n)\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq 1
.
Let
h\in{\mathbb{N}^{\mathbb{N}}}
be a function with

\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b(g(n)),\allowbreak b(g(n)\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\subseteq\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}s_{\beta}(h(n)),s_{\beta}(h(n)+2)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}

for almost all

.
Then for almost all

, we have

\left|s^{\prime}_{\beta}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}b(g(n)),\allowbreak b(g(n)\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq\left|s^{\prime}_{\beta}\cap\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}s_{\beta}(h(n)),s_{\beta}(h(n)+2)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq k

.

We will use bounded perturbations of

Lemma 10.4
.

Let
g\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[I_{n}]^{f(n)}
, where

For each set
a\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
there is a set
c\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
such that:
For each set
s\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
,
every
c
-interval omitted by the set
s
contains an
a
-interval omitted by the set

b:=\bigcup g[s]=\bigcup_{n\in s}g(n).

Proof.

By induction on
n
, choose a function
c\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
such that the interval
\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}c(n),\allowbreak c(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}

contains more than

d(n):=\sum_{i=1}^{c(n)}f(i)

open
a
-intervals.

Suppose that the set
s
omits a
c

\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}c(n),\allowbreak c(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}

For

c(n+1)\leq i\leq\min g(i).

It follows that

\left|b\cap\boldsymbol{\left[\right.}1,c(n+1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}\right|\leq\left|\bigcup_{i=1}^{c(n)-1}g(i)\right|=\sum_{i=1}^{c(n)-1}f(i)\leq d(n).

Since the interval
\boldsymbol{\left(\right.}c(n),\allowbreak c(n\!+\!1)\boldsymbol{\left.\right)}

the set
b

-intervals.

Proposition 10.5
.

Let
S\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
be a
\kappa
-unbounded set, for a cardinal
\kappa
of uncountable cofinality.
For each element
s\in S
let
g_{s}\in\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}[I_{n}]^{f(n)}

Then the set
S^{\prime}:=\{\,\bigcup g_{s}[s]:s\in S\,\}\subseteq[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
is
\kappa
-unbounded.

Proof.

Since the sets
I_{n}
are disjoint, the map
s\mapsto\bigcup g_{s}[s]
is bijective.
In particular, we have
\kappa\leq\left|S\right|=\left|S^{\prime}\right|

Let
a\in[\mathbb{N}]^{\mbox{\tiny$\infty$}}
.
Apply Lemma
10.4
to the set
\tilde{a}
to obtain a set
c
.
Let
d(n):=c(2n)
for all
n
.

Let
s\in S
.
For each
n
, if
c(2n)\leq s(n)
, then the set
s
omits at least
n

c
-intervals.
Assume that
d\leq^{\infty}s
.
Then
s
omits infinitely many
c
-intervals, and thus the set
\bigcup g_{s}[s]
omits infinitely many

-intervals.
It follows that

Thus,

It follows that only fewer than

are bounded by

(Remark

Theorem 10.6
.

Assume that
\mathfrak{b}=\mathfrak{c}

There is a
\mathfrak{b}
-unbounded set

Since
\mathfrak{b}

The proof that the set

Let

For each

For each

Pick an ordinal
\alpha<\mathfrak{b}
be such that the set

is infinite.
By Lemma

, the set

is

11.
Concluding remarks

In the author’s view,
omission of intervals
became a method when
he
realized that it is not only relevant to
\begin{pmatrix}\Omega\\
\Gamma\end{pmatrix}
sets
[
14
]
and the closely related property
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)

[
20
,
41
]
, but also to the Hurewicz property
\mathsf{U}_{\mathrm{fin}}(\mathrm{O},\Gamma)
and
to questions related to rate of growth
[
51
]
, such as the solution of the Hurewicz Problem presented here.
These were previously addressed by other, more topological means
[
4
,
10
,
55
]
.
The usage of omission of intervals led to the complete
analysis of the finer covering properties
\mathsf{U}_{f}(\Gamma,\Gamma)
,
as detailed here; we could not even dream about these achievements
before the development of this method.

The introduction of omission of intervals suggests a paradigm change:
Rather than asking about the
existence
of sets of real numbers with certain covering properties, we often
prove that
all sets
with an appropriate combinatorial structure have these properties.
The presented conceptual proof of the Crown Theorem, using that all intermediate sets are
\mathsf{S}_{1}(\Gamma,\allowbreak\Gamma)
, and the simplicity and strength of the proofs in the last two sections, demonstrate the importance of this paradigm change.

This paper is the first to provide a solid foundation for omission of intervals.
The readers are now equipped with the needed tools to read any paper that employs this method, and to
continue this journey on their own.
