Non-conjugate quantum subsystems
Adam Stokes

Abstract
We introduce an alternative way to understand the decomposition of a quantum system into interacting parts and show that it is natural in several physical models. This enables us to define a reduced density operator for a working system interacting with a thermal bath that is consistent with the inclusion of the interaction Hamiltonian within the working system’s energy. We subsequently provide a self-consistent formulation of quantum thermodynamics that incurs non-trivial physical corrections to thermodynamic relations and quantities previously defined within the literature.



I
Introduction

Physical processes are understood in terms of interactions between physical systems, so defining the notion of
interacting subsystem
can be considered a foremost task in theoretical physics. In a statistical theory, the probabilities
P(o_{A})
and
P(o_{B})
to obtain the outcomes
o_{A}
and
o_{B}
in measurements performed on statistically independent subsystems
A
and
B
should be those of independent events;
P(o_{A}\cap o_{B})=P(o_{A})P(o_{B})
, otherwise the subsystems are correlated. This requirement is met in quantum theory, because the inner-product on a tensor-product space
{\cal H}_{A}\otimes{\cal H}_{B}
is defined in terms of the constituent inner-products by
(\bra{\psi_{A}}\otimes\bra{\psi_{B}})(\ket{\phi_{A}}\otimes\ket{\phi_{B}})=\braket{\psi_{A}|\phi_{A}}\braket{\psi_{B}|\phi_{B}}
. More generally, subsystem density operators are defined using the partial trace by
\rho_{A}={\rm tr}_{B}\rho
and
\rho_{B}={\rm tr}_{A}\rho
, where
\rho
is the density operator representing the generally mixed state of the composite. By definition, the
state
of a systems must suffice to provide complete statistical predictions pertaining to any system
observable
. Observables of subsystems
A
and
B
must therefore be represented by operators of the form
O_{A}\equiv O_{A}\otimes I_{B}
and
O_{B}\equiv I_{A}\otimes O_{B}
respectively, where
I_{A}
and
I_{B}
are the identity operators on
{\cal H}_{A}
and
{\cal H}_{B}
. We then have that
{\rm tr}(O_{s}\rho)={\rm tr}(O_{s}\rho_{s}),~s=A,\,B
.

This framework is obviously sufficient whenever interactions are not present, but whether it is able to fully capture all of the properties expected of
interacting
subsystems is less clear. Specifically, according to these definitions, even when subsystems
A
and
B
are interacting, such that they cannot be spatially separated, their observables are trivially compatible, so an operation such as a measurement performed on subsystem
A
cannot disturb subsystem
B
. Quantum theory dictates however, that a measurement of
A
immediately disturbs
A
, and if by definition of an
interaction
, system
A
is able to immediately disturb
B
, then a measurement of
A
must be able to disturb
B
. A fundamental example is provided by quantum electrodynamics based on Maxwell’s equations; if system
A
is a charged particle and system
B
is the surrounding transverse electromagnetic field, then to obtain the correct Maxwell-Lorentz equations it must be the case that the transverse electric field is not compatible with the charged particle’s energy-momentum
Cohen-Tannoudji
et al. 1989
;
Craig and
Thirunamachandran 1998
;
Stokes and Nazir 2021
. Motivated by these observations, we introduce the concept of non-conjugate subsystems, defined as a pair of subsystems obtained using distinct Hilbert space frames rather than only one. Non-conjugate subsystems are allowed to possess incompatible observables and as a result they are able to exhibit fundamentally different properties to standard quantum subsystem pairs. This includes the possibility of immediately disturbing a subsystem by measuring another. We provide models in which physical subsystems are naturally non-conjugate.

We use the notion of non-conjugate subsystems to rectify an internal inconsistency occurring in formulations of quantum thermodynamics that include the system-bath interaction Hamiltonian within the definition of the working system’s internal energy
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. According to this definition, the working system’s standard reduced density operator does not suffice to determine the average system energy and so cannot be said to represent the
state
of the working system. It is possible to eliminate this inconsistency by treating the working system and the bath as non-conjugate subsystems. The resulting quantum thermodynamics differs significantly from other formulations within the literature, in that it modifies the naive definition of the working system’s state, rather than immediately modifying naive thermodynamic quantities
Seifert 2016
;
Jarzynski 2017
;
Miller 2018
;
Strasberg and
Esposito 2019
. However, as an emergent result, we obtain non-trivial and purely quantum corrections to previous results. The idea of non-conjugate subsystems should possess a number of further applications as a fundamental tool for understanding quantum interactions.

II
Conjugate and non-conjugate quantum subsystems

II.1
Conjugate quantum subsystems

A quantum system can be defined as a pair
({\cal D},{\cal A})
where
{\cal D}

{\cal H}
and
{\cal A}\supset{\cal D}

(

Ghirardi et al. 1980

i)

Spacelike separated systems,

An operation on system

may be defined as a completely positive trace preserving map

over
{\cal D}_{A}

{\rm tr}((\Phi_{A}\otimes I_{B}[\rho])I_{A}\otimes O_{B})={\rm tr}(\rho I_{A}\otimes O_{B})={\rm tr}(\rho_{B}O_{B})
. These equalities show that
\rho_{A}
(
\rho_{B}
) is sufficient to provide any statistical prediction pertaining to system

(
B

and
B
are themselves quantum systems
({\cal D}_{A},{\cal A}_{A})
and
({\cal D}_{B},{\cal A}_{B})
, and that it is indeed the case that an operation performed on a subsystem cannot affect the other subsystem. Note however, that these definitions do not make reference to whether or not the subsystems are interacting, and so they constitute a significantly stronger set of restrictions for defining physical subsystems than is necessitated by condition i).

II.3
Non-conjugate quantum subsystems

We have provided an example of a composite

and
Y
. Only the standard quantum formalism has been employed and all four subsystems are equally well defined. With respect to frame
X
, the mathematical decomposition
({\cal A}_{A}\otimes{\cal A}_{B},{\cal H}_{A}\otimes{\cal H}_{B})
yields the subsystems pair
(A,B)
whereas with respect to frame
Y
the same mathematical decomposition yields subsystem pair
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
. We refer to the pairs
(A,B)
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
as conjugate, because each requires the use of only a single Hilbert space frame. The physical dipole
A^{\prime}
and bath
B
, meanwhile, comprise the pair
(A^{\prime},B)
, which we refer to as
non-conjugate
because it requires the use of two distinct frames
X
and
Y
.

Energetic and entropic quantities can be defined for each quantum subsystem,
A,A^{\prime},B
and
B^{\prime}
. We show in Sec.
III
that physical laws can be provided to relate these quantities. The framework can therefore be understood as a generalisation in which the restriction of relating subsystem quantities defined using only a single Hilbert space frame is removed. We note finally that the approximation of the dipole-field example above to the case of a two-level dipole and a single mode is presented in appendix
Appendix: Material truncation
. We also note that it is straightforward to extend the example to multiple dipoles, multiple multi-mode baths, and to include arbitrary external potentials.

II.4
Significance

Figure 1:
Conventional subsystems are complementary with respect to information but not energy. The information contained in the system
A
conjugate to
B
is defined as the information not contained in the bath
B
as
\rho_{A}={\rm tr}_{B}\rho
(
b
), but as a result, the energy contained in the system
A
cannot be the energy not contained in the bath;
H-H_{B}\not\in{\cal A}_{A}
unless
V=0
(
a
). In contrast, it is possible for non-conjugate subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B
to be complementary with respect to energy;
E_{A^{\prime}}=H-H_{B}=H-E_{B}
(
c
), but the information contained in the physical system
A^{\prime}
is not the information not contained in the physical bath
B
;
\rho_{A^{\prime}}\neq{\rm tr}_{B}\rho
(
d
).

A non-conjugate decomposition provides different physical definitions of subsystems, resulting in different physical predictions and laws relating subsystem properties. For example, within the existing paradigm of open quantum systems theory, tracing out the bath
B
is supposed to yield a description of the measurable open quantum system, but this supposition is false if the measurable open quantum system is
A^{\prime}\neq A
. Complete statistical information pertaining to the physical system
A^{\prime}
is not simply obtained by tracing over the physical bath
B
, that is,
\rho_{A^{\prime}}\neq{\rm tr}_{B}\rho
(Fig
1
). One must instead trace over the conjugate subsystem
B^{\prime}
as
\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)={\rm tr}_{B}\rho^{\prime}
where
\rho^{\prime}(t)=R\rho(t)R^{\dagger}
.

Let us suppose that one is then interested in predicting measurement signals pertaining to
A^{\prime}
, such as its energy change. In the dipole-field example of Sec.
II.3
, solving the Heisenberg equation for
{\dot{a}}_{{\bf k}\lambda}(t)
yields a solution in the form
a_{{\bf k}\lambda}(t)=e^{-i\omega_{k}t}a_{{\bf k}\lambda}(0)+a_{{\bf k}\lambda,s}(t)
where
a_{{\bf k}\lambda,s}(t)=f_{A^{\prime}}(t)
and the operator
f_{A^{\prime}}
pertains to
A^{\prime}
. This allows one to compute averages such as
\langle a^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\lambda,s}(t)a_{{\bf k}\lambda,s}(t)\rangle={\rm tr}[\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)f_{A^{\prime}}(t)^{\dagger}f_{A^{\prime}}(t)]
using one’s reduced state
\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)
. But these averages are
not
correct predictions pertaining to
B
.

Since
H=E_{A^{\prime}}+E_{B}
and
{\dot{H}}=0
, we have;

\displaystyle\Delta E_{B}=-\Delta E_{A^{\prime}}.

(12)

This result can also be obtained by applying the divergence theorem to Poynting’s theorem for local energy conservation. Specifically, the rates of change of the subsystem energies are given in terms of corresponding energy densities by
{\dot{E}}_{S}=\int d^{3}x\,{\dot{\cal E}}_{S}
,
S=A^{\prime},B
where
{\dot{\cal E}}_{A^{\prime}}=({\bf J}_{\rm T}\cdot{\bf E}_{\rm T}+{\bf E}_{\rm T}\cdot{\bf J}_{\rm T})/2
, and
{\cal E}_{B}=({\bf E}_{\rm T}^{2}+{\bf B}^{2})/2
. Poynting’s theorem states that
{\dot{\cal E}}_{A^{\prime}}+{\dot{\cal E}}_{B}=-\nabla\cdot{\bf S}
where
{\bf S}=({\bf E}_{\rm T}\times{\bf B}-{\bf B}\times{\bf E}_{\rm T})/2
. With respect to frame
Y
the observables
{\cal E}_{B},\,E_{B}
, and
{\bf S}
do not belong in
{\cal A}_{B}
and so they cannot be expressed as functions of the photonic operators
a_{{\bf k}\lambda}
and
a^{\dagger}_{{\bf k}\lambda}
alone.

In Eq. (
12
) one can understand the bath
B
to be defined as the subsystem that, in the absence of external driving, is both necessary and sufficient to account for all energy changes in
A^{\prime}
. Indeed, Eq. (
12
) implies that

\displaystyle Q

\displaystyle:={\rm tr}[(\rho_{B}(0)-\rho_{B}(t))E_{B}]

\displaystyle={\rm tr}[(\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)-\rho_{A^{\prime}}(0))E_{A^{\prime}}]=:U,

(13)

which is an instance of the first law of thermodynamics relating the non-conjugate subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B
. Note that it is not possible to express the same law in terms of conjugate subsystem density operators and energy observables. If one is interested in determining the energy lost or gained by the measurable subsystem
A^{\prime}
then neither of the conjugate decompositions
(A,B)
or
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
will yield a strictly correct result. The former yields an incorrect reduced description of
A^{\prime}
, while the latter yields an incorrect description of its energy exchanges with
B
. The non-conjugate subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B
are complementary with respect to energy, but it follows that they are not complementary with respect to information. Essentially the reverse is true for the conjugate subsystems
A
and
B
. These underlying differences are depicted in Fig.
1
.

II.5
Further Examples

We provide further examples of physical models in which non-conjugate subsystems arise.

II.5.1
Quantum Brownian motion

The Caldeira-Leggett model of quantum Brownian motion describes a bound particle with bare Hamiltonian
H_{A}=p_{A}^{2}/(2m)+\theta(r_{A})
interacting with a bath of quantum harmonic oscillators;
H_{B}=\sum_{n}\left(p_{n}^{2}/(2m_{n})+m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}r_{n}^{2}/2\right)
. The Hamiltonian is given by
Caldeira and Leggett 1985
;
Breuer 2007

\displaystyle H_{\rm CL}

\displaystyle={p_{A}^{2}\over 2m}+\theta(r_{A})+\sum_{n}\left[{p_{n}^{2}\over 2m_{n}}+{m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}\over 2}\left(r_{n}-\kappa_{n}r_{A}\right)^{2}\right],

(14)

\displaystyle V_{\rm CL}

\displaystyle=-\sum_{n}m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}\kappa_{n}\left(r_{A}\otimes r_{n}-{\kappa_{n}\over 2}r_{A}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=H_{\rm CL}-H_{A}-H_{B}

(15)

where the
\kappa_{n}
are coupling parameters.

The system and bath particles share the interaction potential energy
V_{\rm CL}
in the sense that
m{\ddot{r}}_{A}=-\partial_{A}(\theta+V_{\rm CL})
and
m_{n}{\ddot{r}}_{n}=-m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}r_{n}-\partial_{n}V_{\rm CL}
. However, the position and momentum quadratures for each quantum oscillator can be relabelled by a local (unitary) Fourier transformation
S_{n}
defined by

\displaystyle S_{n}\psi(r_{n}):=\sqrt{{m_{n}\omega_{n}\over 2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dr_{n}^{\prime}\,e^{im_{n}\omega_{n}r_{n}r_{n}^{\prime}}\psi(r_{n}^{\prime}),

(16)

such that
S_{n}r_{n}S_{n}^{\dagger}=p_{n}/(m_{n}\omega_{n})
and
S_{n}p_{n}S_{n}^{\dagger}=-m_{n}\omega_{n}r_{n}
. This leaves the oscillator Hamiltonian
p_{n}^{2}/(2m_{n})+m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}r_{n}^{2}/2
unchanged, but it interchanges the kinetic and potential terms, such that
S_{n}(m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}r_{n}^{2}/2)S_{n}^{\dagger}
may be interpreted as kinetic energy. Letting
S_{B}=I_{A}\bigotimes_{n}S_{n}
, the Caldeira-Leggett Hamiltonian becomes
H^{\prime}=S_{B}H_{\rm CL}S_{B}^{\dagger}
and is given by

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=

\displaystyle{p_{A}^{2}\over 2m}+\theta(r_{A})

\displaystyle+\sum_{n}\left[{1\over 2m_{n}}\left(p_{n}-m_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n}r_{A}\right)^{2}+{m_{n}\omega_{n}^{2}\over 2}r_{n}^{2}\right],

(17)

\displaystyle V^{\prime}=

\displaystyle-\sum_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n}\left(r_{A}\otimes p_{n}-{m_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n}\over 2}r_{A}^{2}\right)

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\,H^{\prime}-H_{A}-H_{B}

(18)

in which each bath oscillator is minimally-coupled to the system. Via the equation of motion
m_{n}{\dot{r}}_{n}=-im_{n}[r_{n},H^{\prime}]=p_{n}-m_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n}r_{A}
, the Hamiltonian
H^{\prime}
can now be written as the sum of kinetic and potential energies for each particle;

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=

\displaystyle E_{A^{\prime}}+E_{B},

(19)

\displaystyle E_{A^{\prime}}=

\displaystyle{1\over 2}m{\dot{r}}_{A}^{2}+\theta(r_{A}),

(20)

\displaystyle E_{B}=

\displaystyle\sum_{n}{m_{n}\over 2}\left[{\dot{r}}_{n}^{2}+\omega_{n}^{2}r_{n}^{2}\right]=H_{B}+V^{\prime}.

(21)

The energy of the bath is defined as the sum of the total kinetic energy and total potential energy of the bath oscillators. This constitutes an expression of the theory in frame
Y
whereby physical system
A^{\prime}
has observables represented by operators of the form
O_{A}\otimes I_{B}
. In particular
E_{A^{\prime}}=H_{A}\otimes I_{B}
is the energy of the physical system oscillator defined as the sum of its kinetic and potential energies. The state of the system oscillator at time
t
is represented by
\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)={\rm tr}_{B}\rho^{\prime}(t)
where
\rho^{\prime}(t)
is the state of the composite at time
t
in frame
Y
. The canonical momenta
p_{n}=m{\dot{r}}_{n}+m_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n}r_{A}
of the conjugate subsystem
B^{\prime}
possess no straightforward physical interpretation and are not equal to the mechanical momenta
m_{n}{\dot{r}}_{n}
of the physical bath oscillators. Frame
X
, in which the physical bath oscillators comprise a subsystem
B
is obtained using the unitary transformation

\displaystyle R=\exp\left[i\sum_{n}m_{n}\omega_{n}\kappa_{n}r_{A}\otimes r_{n}\right].

(22)

In particular, in frame
X
we have
p_{n}=m_{n}{\dot{r}}_{n}
and the bath energy is represented by
E_{B}=R^{\dagger}(H_{B}+V^{\prime})R=I_{A}\otimes H_{B}
. The reduced state of the physical bath is given by
\rho_{B}(t)={\rm tr}_{A}\rho(t)
where
\rho^{\prime}(t)=R\rho(t)R^{\dagger}
. We see that in this model, only a non-conjugate decomposition
(A^{\prime},B)
defines
all
oscillators in a physically consistent way using their position and kinetic momentum observables. Unlike the non-conjugate decomposition
(A^{\prime},B)
which applies the same mechanical definition of “oscillator” to both system and bath, each of the conjugate pairs
(A,B)
and
(A^{\prime},B^{\prime})
applies two different definitions of “oscillator” in defining what is meant by “system oscillator” versus what is meant by “bath oscillator”.

Independent-boson and spin-boson models

The independent-boson model consists of a TLS with raising and lowering operators
\sigma^{\pm}

is

\displaystyle H^{\prime}=

\displaystyle\omega_{m}\sigma^{+}\sigma^{-}+\sigma^{+}\sigma^{-}\sum_{\bf k}{g_{\bf k}\over\sqrt{2}}(b^{\dagger}_{\bf k}+b_{\bf k})

\displaystyle+\sum_{\bf k}\omega_{\bf k}\left(b_{\bf k}^{\dagger}b_{\bf k}+{1\over 2}\right).

III
Thermodynamics of non-conjugate subsystems

Having provided motivating examples, we now discuss energies and entropies of non-conjugate subsystems in general. We prove first and second laws of thermodynamics for non-conjugate subsystems. We then derive non-trivial thermodynamic differences between conjugate and non-conjugate subsystems, and end with a simple illustrative example.

III.1
Information

Given three quantum systems
A
,
B
, and
C
, the difference between the information available via measurements of
C
and the combined information available via measurements of
A
and
B
, is
I(A,B,C)=S(\rho_{A})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{C})
where
S(\rho):=-{\rm tr}(\rho\ln\rho)
. If
C
is a composite system comprised of conjugate subsystems
A
and
B
, then the information contained in
A
(
B
) is the information not contained in
B
(
A
), as expressed by the definitions
{\rm tr}_{B}\rho=\rho_{A}
and
{\rm tr}_{A}\rho=\rho_{B}
where
\rho:=\rho_{C}
represents the state of
C
. In this case,
I(A,B,C)=I(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})
where

\displaystyle I(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})=S(\rho\|\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B})\geq 0

(31)

in which
S(\rho\|\sigma):={\rm tr}(\rho\ln\rho)-{\rm tr}(\rho\ln\sigma)

Nielsen and Chuang 2000
. For an initially uncorrelated state
\rho=\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}
the mutual information at a later time
t
is

\displaystyle 0\leq I(\rho_{A}(t),\rho_{B}(t))=\Delta S_{A}(t)+\Delta S_{B}(t),

(32)

which is an important identity for proving thermodynamic relations involving subsystem entropy changes, as will be seen in Sec.
III
. The non-negativity of
I(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})
tells us that the information associated with
C
includes that associated with
A
and
B
as well as that associated with their correlations. If the subsystems are statistically independent then
I=0
and if they are correlated then
I>0
. Viewed differently, learning about
A
can only ever
reveal
information about conjugate subsystem
B
. This is a consequence of the trivial compatibility of subsystem observables specified within condition i).

In contrast, there is generally an overlap in the information associated with non-conjugate subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B
. It is therefore possible for the total information associated with such non-conjugate subsystems to be larger than that associated with the composite. Viewed differently, this can be interpreted as demonstrating the capacity of one subsystem to
erase
information about the other. A measurement of
A^{\prime}
can immediately disturb non-conjugate subsystem
B
. Indeed, in Sec.
II
we identified this property as part of what might reasonably be taken to define interactions [condition ii)].

For non-conjugate subsystems
(A^{\prime},B)
the quantity most closely analogous to the mutual information of conjugate subsystems is
I(A^{\prime},B,C)=I(\rho_{A^{\prime}}.\rho_{B})
where

\displaystyle I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})

\displaystyle=S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho),

\displaystyle=S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})+S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho^{\prime}),

(33)

This quantity is not, however, a non-negative relative entropy. It is bounded instead by
I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})=S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})-S(\rho_{A})+I(\rho_{A},\rho_{B})\geq S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})-S(\rho_{A})
or similarly by
I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})=S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{B}^{\prime})+I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B^{\prime}})\geq S(\rho_{B})-S(\rho_{B^{\prime}})
. It follows that, even if
V(0)=0
such that initially
A^{\prime}=A
and even if the initial state is uncorrelated,
\rho=\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}
, the sum of non-conjugate subsystem entropy changes is not generally non-negative;

\displaystyle 0\not\leq I(\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t),\rho_{B}(t))=\Delta S_{A^{\prime}}(t)+\Delta S_{B}(t),

(34)

contrasting Eq. (
32
). This will be seen in Sec.
III.3
to imply non-trivial differences between the thermodynamics of conjugate and non-conjugate subsystems.

Assuming
\rho=\rho_{A}\otimes\rho_{B}
then
\rho_{A^{\prime}}=\Phi(\rho_{A})=\sum_{\lambda_{B},\mu_{B}}K_{\mu_{B}\lambda_{B}}\rho_{A}K_{\mu_{B}\lambda_{B}}^{\dagger}
where
\Phi
is a quantum operation in which
K_{\mu_{B}\lambda_{B}}=\sqrt{\lambda_{B}}\bra{\mu_{B}}R\ket{\lambda_{B}}
and where
\rho_{B}=\sum_{\lambda_{B}}\lambda_{B}\ket{\lambda_{B}}\bra{\lambda_{B}}
. The quantity
I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})=S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})-S(\rho_{A})
is negative if the operation
\Phi
is entropy reducing. Such
\Phi
certainly exist, an example is given in Appendix
IV.1
. Conversely, to a given a quantum operation
\Phi
acting on a system
A
it is possible to associate non-conjugate subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B
, because any such operation may be written
\Phi(\rho_{A})={\rm tr}_{B}(R\rho_{A}\otimes\ket{\psi_{B}}\bra{\psi_{B}}R^{\dagger})
where
R
is unitary
Nielsen and Chuang 2000
.

To show in a general way how a measurement of
A^{\prime}
can increase the uncertainty in the state of system
B
, let us suppose that in frame
X
the state of the composite is represented by
\rho=\rho_{A}\otimes\ket{\psi_{B}}\bra{\psi_{B}}
, such that the state of
B
is pure. In frame
Y
the state is entangled;
\rho^{\prime}=R\rho_{A}\otimes\ket{\psi_{B}}\bra{\psi_{B}}R^{\dagger}
and the system’s energy is represented by an operator that we denote
H^{\prime}_{A}\otimes I_{B}
. Let us assume for simplicity that
H_{A}^{\prime}
has non-degenerate spectrum;
H_{A}^{\prime}\ket{\epsilon_{i}}=\epsilon_{i}\ket{\epsilon_{i}}
. An ideal projective measurement of the system
A^{\prime}
’s energy yields the outcome
\epsilon_{i}
with probability
p_{i}^{\prime}={\rm tr}(P_{i}\otimes I_{B}\rho^{\prime})={\rm tr}(P_{i}\rho_{A^{\prime}})
where
P_{i}=\ket{\epsilon_{i}}\bra{\epsilon_{i}}
and
\rho_{A^{\prime}}={\rm tr}_{B}\rho^{\prime}
. The subensemble for which outcome
\epsilon_{i}
is found is represented in frame
Y
by

\displaystyle\sigma^{\prime}_{i}={1\over p_{i}^{\prime}}P_{i}\otimes I_{B}\rho^{\prime}P_{i}\otimes I_{B}=P_{i}\otimes\sigma_{iB^{\prime}}

(35)

where
\sigma_{iB^{\prime}}={\rm tr}_{A}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
. In frame
X
this state is represented by
\sigma_{i}=R^{\dagger}\sigma_{i}^{\prime}R
. The final state of
A^{\prime}
is represented by
\sigma_{iA^{\prime}}=P_{i}
. This state is pure (maximally informative), but since
\sigma_{i}
is entangled the physical bath state represented by
\sigma_{iB}={\rm tr}_{A}\sigma_{i}
is mixed. A gain in information (reduction in uncertainty) about
A^{\prime}
by

\displaystyle S(P_{i})-S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})=-S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})\leq 0,

(36)

has resulted in an increase in uncertainty about
B
by

\displaystyle S(\sigma_{iB})-S(\ket{\psi_{B}}\bra{\psi_{B}})=S(\sigma_{iB})\geq 0.

(37)

For conjugate subsystems this is impossible. Following a measurement of
A
the state of
B
continues to be represented by the vector
\ket{\psi_{B}}
.

For a non-selective measurement of system
A^{\prime}
’s energy, the final state of the composite is represented in frame
Y
by
\sigma^{\prime}=\sum_{i}p^{\prime}_{i}\sigma^{\prime}_{i}
and in frame
X
by
\sigma=\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\prime}\sigma_{i}
. The final state of the physical system
A^{\prime}
is represented by
\sigma_{A^{\prime}}=\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\prime}P_{i}
and its entropy change is

\displaystyle S(\sigma_{A^{\prime}})-S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})=S(\rho_{A^{\prime}}\|\sigma_{A^{\prime}})\geq 0.

(38)

Although subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B^{\prime}
are correlated, the measurement is non-selective, so the state of subsystem
B^{\prime}
remains unchanged;
\sigma_{B^{\prime}}={\rm tr}_{A}\sigma^{\prime}={\rm tr}_{A}\rho^{\prime}=\rho_{B^{\prime}}
. On the other hand, the final state of
B
is represented by
\sigma_{B}=\sum_{i}p_{i}^{\prime}\sigma_{iB}
, incurring an increase in entropy

\displaystyle S(\sigma_{B})-S(\ket{\psi_{B}}\bra{\psi_{B}})=S(\sigma_{B})\geq 0.

(39)

Such a change is impossible following a non-selective measurement of the conjugate system
A
. A simple example in which these general results can be illustrated is the Jaynes-Cummings model of quantum optics, which is discussed briefly in Appendix
IV.1
.

III.2
First and second laws

Let us now consider a working system, labelled
A^{\prime}
, interacting with a single thermal bath, labelled
B
. In a Hilbert space frame that we label
X
, we suppose that the total Hamiltonian is
H=H_{A}(t)+V(t)+H_{B}
where
H_{A}(t)\in{\cal A}_{A}
and
H_{B}\in{\cal A}_{B}
are bare Hamiltonians and
V(t)
is an interaction Hamiltonian. By assumption, the working system is accessible to measurement and control protocols whereas information about the bath is typically limited. We consider formulations of thermodynamics in which the energy of the working system is defined as
E_{A^{\prime}}(t):=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
, such that
H(t)=E_{A^{\prime}}(t)+E_{B}
where
E_{B}:=H_{B}
is the energy of the physical bath
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. With these definitions the first law of thermodynamics becomes essentially automatic
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. The second law is discussed and proven subsequently.

III.2.1
First law

The first law of thermodynamics for the internal energy change of the working system
A^{\prime}
reads

\displaystyle U=W+Q.

(40)

The total work
W
is typically defined as the change in total energy;
W={\rm tr}[H(t)\rho(t)-H(0)\rho(0)]
such that
{dW/dt}={\rm tr}[\{{\dot{H}}_{A}(t)+{\dot{V}}(t)\}\rho(t)]

Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. The heat
Q
is defined as minus the average change in energy of the bath, which is the part of the composite that is not directly controlled;
Q=-{\rm tr}[H_{B}\rho_{B}(t)-H_{B}\rho_{B}(0)]=-\Delta\langle E_{B}\rangle_{t}
and
{dQ/dt}=-{\rm tr}[H_{B}{\dot{\rho}}(t)]

Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
;
Reeb and Wolf 2014
;
Goold et al. 2015
. It then follows from Eq. (
40
) that

\displaystyle U

\displaystyle={\rm tr}[\{H_{A}(t)+V(t)\}\rho(t)-\{H_{A}(0)+V(0)\}\rho(0)]

\displaystyle=\langle E_{A^{\prime}}(t)\rangle-\langle E_{A^{\prime}}(0)\rangle.

(41)

This confirms our definition of the working system’s energy operator as
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
, which is also the definition arrived at in a number of works within the thermodynamics literature
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. Note however, that here we have started with the definition
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
, which does not fix
Q
and
W
beyond the requirement that Eq. (
40
) is satisfied. The definitions of
Q
and
W
above are particular examples, but they are not the only consistent definitions.

III.2.2
Temperature and equilibrium

The second law of thermodynamics in its most basic and fundamental form states that in any physical process the thermodynamic entropy
S_{\rm th}
(yet to be defined here) of a closed system does not decrease;

\displaystyle\Delta S_{\rm th}\geq 0

(42)

such that a closed system tends to a state of maximum entropy (equilibrium). A closed system is one that has no explicit interactions with anything else. Controlled processes like measurements and driving may be considered as external, i.e., without the devices and associated interactions being treated explicitly. Typically, the closed system is assumed to consist of a working system
A^{\prime}
, which is measurable, and at least one thermal bath. Here we are considering the case of a single thermal bath
B
. The definition of entropy
S_{\rm th}
should relate in some way to the information available to an experimenter who performs measurements. Once
\Delta S_{\rm th}
can be connected to physically measurable properties the second law becomes a meaningful and useful physical statement.

A suitable such definition of
S_{\rm th}
is given in Sec.
III.2.4
. We note first that in order to relate entropy and energy a quantity with dimensions inverse energy is required and the one employed almost universally is inverse temperature
\beta
. Temperature can be derived by relating information and energy via the notion of equilibrium
Jaynes 1957
. Since
\rho
is sufficient to provide complete statistical information regarding any observable, the Von-Neumann entropy
S(\rho)=-{\rm tr}(\rho\ln\rho)
can be interpreted as quantifying the information available to an observer who is able to perform any measurement. If the system possesses fixed average energy
\langle E\rangle={\rm tr}(\rho E)
and if one maximises
S(\rho)
subject to this constraint, i.e., if one minimises the Lagrangian
F(\rho,\beta)=\langle E\rangle-\beta^{-1}S(\rho)
where
\beta
is a Lagrange multiplier, then one obtains
\rho=\rho^{\rm eq}(\beta):=e^{-\beta E}/Z
where
Z={\rm tr}e^{-\beta E}
. The state
\rho^{\rm eq}(\beta)
is an equilibrium state of the system with temperature
\beta^{-1}
. The Lagrangian
F(\rho,\beta)
is called the free energy, which by construction satisfies
F(\rho^{\rm eq}(\beta),\beta)\leq F(\rho,\beta)
for all
\rho\in{\cal D}
. Note that the entropy
S(\rho)
comes from information theory and should not tacitly be equated with thermodynamic entropy. The above calculation indicates only that these entropies should coincide at thermal equilibrium.

III.2.3
Consistent identification of observables and states

In conjunction with the definition
E_{A^{\prime}}(t):=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
, previous works have assumed the standard definition of the reduced system state;
\rho_{A}(t):={\rm tr}_{B}\rho(t)
where
\rho(t)
represents the state of the composite in frame
X

Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. However, the two definitions
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)
and
\rho_{A}(t)
are incompatible, because the average of
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)
cannot be computed using
\rho_{A}(t)
alone, and yet in any physical theory, the
state
of a system must by definition suffice to provide complete statistical information for all system observables. In other words, we have that
\rho_{A}\in{\cal D}_{A}
whereas
{\cal A}\ni H_{A}(t)+V(t)\not\in{\cal A}_{A}
and yet unlike
({\cal D}_{A},{\cal A}_{A})
, the pair
({\cal D}_{A},{\cal A})
is not a quantum system. In particular, the Von Neumann entropy
S(\rho_{A}(t))
defined in terms of
\rho_{A}(t)
cannot be said to quantify the information available to an experimenter able to make measurements of the working system
A^{\prime}
.

If
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)
and
E_{B}
are assumed to be the energies of the physical working system
A^{\prime}
and its bath
B
respectively, and if
A^{\prime}
and
B
are also assumed to be quantum subsystems, then they must be non-conjugate. Specifically, there must exist a rotation
R(t)
mapping from frame
X
to a frame
Y
, such that
R(t)[H_{A}(t)+V(t)]R(t)^{\dagger}=H_{A}^{\prime}(t)\otimes I_{B}
where
H_{A}^{\prime}(t)
is an operator that belongs in
{\cal A}_{A}
and which represents the energy observable
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)
in frame
Y
. Entropy, as a measure of information available to an experimenter who measures
A^{\prime}
, is then correctly defined using
\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)
. As we will show, this fact implies non-trivial corrections to the basic thermodynamic quantities found within previous formulations
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
.

III.2.4
Second law

For a closed system with unitary dynamics, the Von Neumann entropy is invariant,
\Delta S=0
. It is not generally suitable as a definition of thermodynamic entropy, because it refers to the information available to an experimenter able to perform any measurement that is possible in principle rather than referring to the specific measurements that the experimenter does perform. A more suitable definition is provided by observational entropy, which is defined in terms of the measurements made. Here we briefly review the definition and apply it to prove the second law in its most basic form [inequality (
42
)]. We refer the reader to Refs.
Šafránek
et al. 2019a
;
Šafránek
et al. 2019b
;
Šafránek et al. 2020
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
for further details.

Consider an observable
{\cal O}
represented by a Hermitian operator
O=\sum_{i}o_{i}\Pi_{i}
such that
{\cal C}=\{\Pi_{i}\}
defines a coarse-graining of
{\cal H}
, that is complete;
\sum_{i}\Pi_{i}=I
. The probability that outcome
o_{i}
is obtained in a measurement of
{\cal O}
is
p_{i}={\rm tr}(\Pi_{i}\rho)
where
\rho
represents the (micro)state of the system. A measurement with outcome labelled by
i
corresponds to a portion of
{\cal H}
that represents states compatible with this measurement outcome. Thus,
i
labels a macrostate of the system with state-space “volume”
V_{i}={\rm tr}\Pi_{i}
. To a given microstate compatible with the macrostate
i
, Boltzmann associated an entropy equal to the log of the volume of the macrostate
i
. One therefore defines the Boltzmann entropy as
{\cal S}_{i}=\ln V_{i}
. The
observational entropy
is defined by

\displaystyle S_{\cal C}(\rho)=-\sum_{i}p_{i}\ln{p_{i}\over V_{i}}=S_{\rm Sh}\{p_{i}\}+\sum_{i}p_{i}{\cal S}_{i}

(43)

where
S_{\rm Sh}\{p_{i}\}:=-\sum_{i}p_{i}\ln p_{i}
is the Shannon entropy of the distribution
\{p_{i}\}
. If
\rho=\sum_{i}p_{i}\Pi_{i}
then this term coincides with the Von Neumann entropy
S(\rho)
. The first term in Eq. (
44
) therefore represents the expected information regarding the macrostate of the system obtained from a measurement with coarse-graining
{\cal C}=\{\Pi_{i}\}
. The second term represents the remaining average uncertainty regarding the state of the system once the measurement outcome is known
Šafránek
et al. 2019b
.

To describe a sequence of measurements a sequence of coarse-grainings
{\cal C}_{1},...,{\cal C}_{n}
must be specified. This defines a multimacrostate
{\bf i}=(i_{1},\dots,i_{n})
with volume
V_{i_{1}...i_{n}}={\rm tr}(\Pi_{i_{1}}\dots\Pi_{i_{n}}\dots\Pi_{i_{1}})
. The quantity
p_{i_{1}...i_{n}}={\rm tr}(\Pi_{i_{n}}\dots\Pi_{i_{1}}\rho\Pi_{i_{1}}\dots\Pi_{i_{n}})
is the joint probability that an outcome labelled by
i_{1}
is obtained in the first measurement, and outcome labelled by
i_{2}
is obtained in the second measurement, and so on, up to the
n^{\prime}th
measurement. Note that we do not require the measurements to be compatible, that is, in general
[\Pi_{i_{m}},\Pi_{i_{n}}]\neq 0
. The observational entropy can in this case be defined by
Šafránek
et al. 2019b

\displaystyle S_{{\cal C}_{1}...{\cal C}_{n}}(\rho)=-\sum_{\bf i}p_{i_{1}...i_{n}}\ln{p_{i_{1}...i_{n}}\over V_{i_{1}...i_{n}}}.

(44)

One expects intuitively that the least uncertainty possible is given by the Von Neumann entropy, which is indeed the case;

\displaystyle S_{{\cal C}_{1}...{\cal C}_{n}}(\rho)\geq S(\rho).

(45)

The proof involves a straightforward application of Jensen’s inequality to the concave function
f(x)=-x\ln x
. Details can be found in Ref.
Šafránek
et al. 2019b
.

Let us now suppose that an experimenter is able to perform measurements corresponding to some coarse-graining
{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}
, on a working system labelled
A^{\prime}
, as well as energy measurements corresponding to a coarse-graining
{\cal C}_{E_{B}}
, of the surrounding bath labelled
B
. This scenario was encountered in the dipole-field example of Sec.
II.3
. It was noted there that the relevant energy observables obey Poynting’s theorem [Eq. (
12
)], which is an operator form of the first law of thermodynamics [Eq. (
II.4
)], and that they are
incompatible
. We must therefore also allow this to be the case when considering the second law. In particular, we allow the subsystems
A^{\prime}
and
B
to be non-conjugate. However, following conventional open quantum systems theory
Breuer 2007
;
de Vega and Alonso 2017
, we consider an initial state of the form
\rho=\rho_{A}(0)\otimes\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta)
in which the bath is at thermal equilibrium with temperature
\beta^{-1}
. This is justified for arbitrary coupling strengths provided that at
t=0
the system and bath are non-interacting and subsequently brought into contact at some time
t>0
. It follows that at
t=0
the subsystems are conjugate with total energy
H(0)=E_{A^{\prime}}(0)+E_{B}=H_{A}\otimes I_{B}+I_{A}\otimes H_{B}
. At later times
H(t)=E_{A^{\prime}}(t)+E_{B}
but in general
[E_{A^{\prime}}(t),E_{B}]\neq 0
.

Since the experimenter is able to make measurements
{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}
we can suppose that
\rho_{A^{\prime}}(0)=\rho_{A}(0)
is of the form
\ket{s}\bra{s}\in{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}
, which represents a state in which the value of the measured observable is known. For simplicity we will assume that the spectrum
\{s\}
of this observable is non-degenerate. We also suppose that the available bath energy measurements are of sufficiently high resolution that
{\cal S}(\beta)=S(\rho^{\rm eq}_{B}(\beta))\approx S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho^{\rm eq}_{B}(\beta))

Strasberg and Winter 2021
. This means that the initial bath observational entropy is indeed that of a canonical ensemble at equilibrium. At time
t
the experimenter is able to perform measurements
{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}^{t}
on the working system
A^{\prime}
. The possible outcomes,
\{s_{t}\}
, depend on
t
in general.

Since at
t=0
we have
S_{C_{A^{\prime}}\otimes C_{E_{B}}}(\rho)=S(\rho)
and moreover
S(\rho)=S(\rho(t))
, it follows from inequality (
45
) that the change in observational entropy corresponding to the measurement sequence
{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}^{t},{\cal C}_{E_{B}}
satisfies

\displaystyle\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}:=S_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}^{t}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho(t))-S_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho)\geq 0

(46)

in which we identify the left-hand-side as a thermodynamic entropy change. Similarly the thermodynamic entropy change corresponding to the measurement sequence
{\cal C}_{E_{B}},{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}^{t}
is non-negative. This generalises the second law proved in Ref.
Strasberg and Winter 2021
.

The observational entropy is defined directly in terms of measurements performed, which makes it an immediately relevant thermodynamic entropy provided that a consistent identification of which observables the experimenter measures is made (cf. Sec.
III.2.3
). For example, if the energy
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)=H_{A}(t)+V(t)\not\in{\cal A}_{A}
is measured and
[E_{A^{\prime}}(t),E_{B}]\neq 0
, then the relevant observational entropy is evidently that associated with two incompatible observables. We remark before continuing that a non-negative thermodynamic entropy change as in inequality (
42
) is sometimes referred to as entropy production. Here however, we reserve the latter term for a different quantity that will be considered in the following section.

III.3
Further thermodynamic relations

The observational entropy provides a natural and operationally relevant second law. For system
A
and conjugate bath
B
some further relations can also be provided, which Ref.
Strasberg and Winter 2021
refers to as a hierarchy of second laws. We briefly review these results, which will allow us to understand the key differences obtained when we subsequently consider the
measurable
working system
A^{\prime}
and non-conjugate bath
B
.

III.3.1
Thermodynamic relations for the entropy of system
A

Since the system and bath are initially uncorrelated we have
S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{0}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho(0))=S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{0}}(\rho_{A}(0))+S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho_{B}(0))
, using which one immediately obtains an additive law analogous to Eq. (
32
) as
\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}}+\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}=I_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho_{A}(t),\rho_{B}(t))+\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}\geq\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}\geq 0
where the classical mutual information

\displaystyle I_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}(\rho_{A}(t),\rho_{B}(t))

\displaystyle:=\sum_{s_{t},\epsilon_{B}}p_{s_{t}\epsilon_{B}}(t)\ln\left[p_{s_{t}\epsilon_{B}}(t)\over p_{s_{t}}(t)p_{\epsilon_{B}}(t)\right]

\displaystyle\geq 0

(47)

quantifies the correlations within the measurement result
(s_{t},\epsilon_{B})
.

For incompatible measurements
{\cal C}_{A}^{\prime}
and
{\cal C}_{B}
the situation is different. In this case there is no obvious way to express the quantity
I_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}{\cal C}_{B}}(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B}):=S_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}}(\rho_{A^{\prime}})+S_{{\cal C}_{B}}(\rho_{B})-S_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}{\cal C}_{B}}(\rho)
as a non-negative Kullback-Leibler divergence. This is analogous to the case of
I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})
given in Eq. (
III.1
). Since we have already discussed
I(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})
in Sec.
III.1
, we omit a similar analysis of
I_{{\cal C}_{A^{\prime}}{\cal C}_{B}}(\rho_{A^{\prime}},\rho_{B})
. We note however that even when dealing with a macroscopic working system (“sys”) and heat bath (“
B
”), one expects to obtain an extensive entropy of the form
S_{\rm th}=S_{\rm th,sys}+S_{\rm th,B}
only as an approximation, albeit a very good one in many contexts. Yet, only when such a relation holds does the fundamental second law
\Delta S_{\rm th}\geq 0
become the additive law
\Delta S_{\rm th,sys}+\Delta S_{\rm th,B}\geq 0
and so the latter inequality is not fundamental in general.

Defining an effective inverse temperature
\beta^{*}(t)
by the equation
{\rm tr}[\rho_{B}(t)H_{B}]={\rm tr}[\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta^{*}(t))H_{B}]
, it is straightforward to show that
Strasberg and Winter 2021

\displaystyle\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}\leq\int dU_{B}(s)\beta^{*}(s)=-\int dQ(s)\beta^{*}(s)

(48)

where
U_{B}(t)=\Delta\langle E_{B}\rangle_{t}={\rm tr}[\rho_{B}(t)H_{B}]-{\rm tr}[\rho_{B}(0)H_{B}]
is the change in internal energy of the bath and
Q(t)=-U_{B}(t)
as in Sec.
III.2.1
. If the bath temperature hardly changes from its initial equilibrium value
\beta
, i.e.,
\beta^{*}(s)\approx\beta
for all
s\in[0,t]
, then inequality (
50
) becomes

\displaystyle\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}\leq-\beta Q.

(49)

For conjugate system and bath the additive law
\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}}+\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}\geq 0
and inequality (
48
) imply that

\displaystyle\Delta S_{{\cal C}^{t}_{A}}\geq\int dQ(s)\beta^{*}(s)

(50)

and for
\beta^{*}(s)\approx\beta

\displaystyle\Delta S_{{\cal C}^{t}_{A}}\geq\beta Q.

(51)

Inequalities (
50
) and (
51
) are nonequilibrium Clausius-type relations between the system’s thermodynamic entropy change and the heat
Q
, which hold for conjugate system and bath.

Rather than considering the observational entropy, Ref.
Esposito et al. 2010
provides elegant laws for Von Neumann entropy changes. It is assumed that the system energy
E_{A^{\prime}}
, heat
Q
, and work
W
are defined as in Sec.
III.2.1
. The difference
\Sigma:=\Delta S_{A}-\beta Q
between the Von Neumann entropy change of the subsystem
A\neq A^{\prime}
and the thermal entropy
\beta Q
, is then found to be
Esposito et al. 2010

\displaystyle\Sigma=S(\rho(t)\|\rho_{A}(t)\otimes\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta))\geq 0,

(52)

which quantifies the distance between the true state
\rho(t)
and the hypothetical uncorrelated state
\rho_{A}(t)\otimes\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta)
in which the bath
B
has remained at equilibrium. For an initial state
\rho_{A}(0)=\ket{s}\bra{s}
diagonal at
t=0
in some given measurement basis
{\cal C}_{A}
we have
\Delta S_{{\cal C}^{t}_{A}}\geq\Delta S_{A}=\Sigma+\beta Q\geq\beta Q
, which gives a tighter bound on
\beta Q
than the microscopic Clausius inequality (
51
).

Eq. (
52
) is mathematically exact, having been derived without approximation. Naturally,
\Sigma
is bounded from below by the non-negative mutual information, which equals the sum of the conjugate subsystem entropy changes for an initially uncorrelated state [Eq. (
32
)]. The difference
[\Sigma-I(\rho_{A}(t),\rho_{B}(t))]/\beta
is the bath free energy change, that is,
\beta\Delta F_{B}=S(\rho_{B}(t)\|\rho^{\rm eq}(\beta))\geq 0
where
F_{B}:=\langle E_{B}\rangle-\beta^{-1}S(\rho_{B})
. The inequality
\Delta F_{B}\geq 0
is equivalent to
\Delta S_{B}(t)\leq-\beta Q
, which is a looser lower bound on
-\beta Q
than inequality (
49
).

An important corollary specifying the maximum extractible work is
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Strasberg et al. 2017

\displaystyle W=\beta^{-1}\Sigma+\Delta F_{A}\geq\Delta F_{A},

(53)

where
F_{A}:=\langle E_{A^{\prime}}\rangle-\beta^{-1}S(\rho_{A})
. Note that
F_{A}
is neither the free energy of
A
nor
A^{\prime}
, but rather a hybrid quantity that combines the average energy of
A^{\prime}
with the entropy of
A
. It does not possess the defining properties of free-energy. For example, if
\rho
is such that the state of subsystem
A
is thermal,
\rho_{A}^{\rm eq}(\beta):=e^{-\beta H_{A}}/Z_{A}
, then
F_{A}={\rm tr}[V\rho]+\beta^{-1}\ln Z_{A}\neq\beta^{-1}\ln Z_{A}
, where the right-hand-side is the equilibrium free energy of
A
. However, by assuming that
V(t)=0
the subsystems
A
and
A^{\prime}
coincide at time
t
, such that
F_{A}(t)
is then the free energy of
A=A^{\prime}
, which is minimised at equilibrium. Under this assumption, using Eq. (
53
) one obtains

\displaystyle W-\Delta F_{A}^{\rm eq}=\beta^{-1}(\Sigma+\Delta I_{A})\geq\beta^{-1}\Delta I_{A}

(54)

where
\beta^{-1}I_{A}(t):=\beta^{-1}S(\rho_{A}(t)\|\rho_{A}^{\rm eq}(\beta,t))=F_{A}(t)-F_{A}^{\rm eq}(t)
can be interpreted as quantifying the information that must be processed in transforming from the equilibrium state, represented by
\rho_{A}^{\rm eq}(\beta,t)=e^{-\beta H_{A}(t)}/Z_{A}(t)
, to the actual state at time
t
, represented by
\rho_{A}(t)
. The authors of Ref.
Esposito and Broeck 2011
interpret Eq. (
54
) as a nonequilibrium Landauer principle. It states that the difference between the change in total energy
W
and the free energy change of corresponding initial and final equilibrium distributions of
A
, is bounded by the
information change

\Delta I_{A}
associated with
A
.

III.3.2
Thermodynamic relations for the entropy and the free energy of the system
A^{\prime}

Thermodynamic laws are useful only insofar as the quantities appearing therein are physically relevant.
For example, if
\rho_{A}(t)
were sufficient to provide statistical predictions for the measurable working system
A^{\prime}
, then Eq. (
52
) would tell us that the decrease in information available to the experimenter is at least as large as
\beta
times the heat gained from
B
, while inequality (
53
) would tell us that the maximum extractible work is given by the decrease in free energy of the measurable system. The results of Sec.
III.3.1

do not
constitute these useful physical statements, because
\rho_{A}(t)
does not suffice to provide the averages of the physically relevant observables that define the working system
A^{\prime}
, such as the energy
E_{A^{\prime}}
.

In order to provide
useful
thermodynamic laws for the working system
A^{\prime}
we must consider the entropy and free energy
of this system
, which are defined by

\displaystyle S(\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t))\neq S(\rho_{A}(t)),

(55)

\displaystyle F_{A^{\prime}}:=\langle E_{A^{\prime}}\rangle-\beta^{-1}S(\rho_{A^{\prime}})\neq F_{A}.

(56)

Since for non-conjugate system and bath one does not generally have an additive law
\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{A}^{t}}+\Delta S_{{\cal C}_{E_{B}}}\geq 0
, one cannot expect inequalities of the form in (
50
) and (
51
) to hold in general. Indeed, by considering
S_{A^{\prime}}
and
F_{A^{\prime}}
instead of
S(\rho_{A})
and
F_{A}
, we will obtain a novel correction, which can either be interpreted as a correction to the naively expected thermodynamic relations, or instead as a correction to the naively expected definitions of heat and work.

We begin by defining the naive entropy production
{\tilde{\Sigma}}:=\Delta S_{A^{\prime}}(t)-\beta Q
. A straightforward calculation yields

\displaystyle{\tilde{\Sigma}}=-S(\rho(t))-{\rm tr}[\rho^{\prime}(t)\ln\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)]-{\rm tr}[\rho(t)\ln\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta)].

(57)

If the Hilbert space frames
X
and
Y
are locally connected,
R(t)=R_{A}(t)\otimes R_{B}(t)
, then the subsystems are conjugate and we recover Eq. (
52
). Another sufficient condition in order that the right-hand-side of Eq. (
57
) is non-negative is that
[R(t),H_{B}]=0
, in which case using
\rho(t)=R(t)^{\dagger}\rho^{\prime}(t)R(t)
we find that
{\tilde{\Sigma}}=\Sigma^{\prime}
where

\displaystyle\Sigma^{\prime}:=S(\rho^{\prime}(t)\|\rho_{A^{\prime}}(t)\otimes\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta)).

(58)

In general
{\tilde{\Sigma}}
cannot be written as a relative entropy as in Eq. (
58
), but a straightforward calculation reveals that we can write
\Delta S_{A^{\prime}}(t)
as

\displaystyle\Delta S_{A^{\prime}}(t)=\Sigma^{\prime}+\beta Q^{\prime}

(59)

where

\displaystyle Q^{\prime}:=-{\rm tr}[H_{B}\rho^{\prime}(t)-H_{B}\rho^{\prime}_{B}(0)]

\displaystyle\hskip 11.09654pt=-{\rm tr}[H_{B}\rho^{\prime}(t)-H_{B}\rho_{B}^{\rm eq}(\beta)]=Q+\delta Q,

(60)

\displaystyle\delta Q:={\rm tr}[\rho(t)\delta H_{B}(t)],

(61)

\displaystyle\delta H_{B}(t):=H_{B}-R(t)^{\dagger}H_{B}R(t).

(62)

Furthermore, using Eqs. (
56
) and (
59
), we obtain analogously to Eq. (
53
)

\displaystyle W^{\prime}-\Delta F_{A^{\prime}}={\beta}^{-1}\Sigma^{\prime}\geq 0,

(63)

where
W^{\prime}:=W-\delta Q
.

Mathematically, Eq. (
59
) is nothing but the previous result
\Delta S_{A}(t)=\Sigma+\beta Q
with
\rho(t)
replaced everywhere by
\rho^{\prime}(t)
. Physically, this difference is important, because in frame
Y
, the operator
H_{B}
does not represent the energy
E_{B}
, but a different observable that we will denote
{\cal E}_{B}
. The energy
E_{B}
, meanwhile, is represented by the operator
R(t)H_{B}R^{\dagger}(t)
. The correction
\delta Q
is the average difference between these observables at time
t
;
\delta Q=Q^{\prime}-Q=\langle E_{B}-{\cal E}_{B}\rangle_{t}
. The operator
\delta H_{B}(t)
in Eq. (
61
) is of the same order as
V(t)
, therefore
\beta\delta Q
is expected to be small for weak-coupling, but it should become significant for sufficiently strong-coupling and low temperatures.

III.4
Meaning of
\delta Q
and its physical implications

Assuming an initially uncorrelated state, one obtains the (essentially trivial) additive relation (
32
) for conjugate subsystem Von Neumann entropy changes, but Eq. (
34
) shows that such an inequality does not generally hold for the sum
\Delta S_{A^{\prime}}+\Delta S_{B}
even if
A^{\prime}
and non-conjugate bath
B
are initially non-interacting and uncorrelated. This results in the correction
\delta Q
, which implies that either
a
) one no longer has a microscopic Clausius-type inequality of the form
\Delta S_{\rm sys}\geq\beta Q
and associated free energy bound of the form
W\geq\Delta F_{\rm sys}
, that is, the maximum extractable work is
not
the decrease in the measurable system’s free energy, or else,
b
) the naive definitions of heat and work,
Q
and
W
, fail, and must be replaced by
Q^{\prime}
and
W^{\prime}
respectively; the physical heat is no longer simply minus the change in bath energy and the physical work is no longer simply the average change in total energy.

Furthermore, from Eq. (
63
) we obtain

\displaystyle W^{\prime}-\Delta F_{A^{\prime}}^{\rm eq}=\beta^{-1}(\Sigma^{\prime}+\Delta I_{A^{\prime}})\geq\beta^{-1}\Delta I_{A^{\prime}}

(64)

of which Eq. (
54
) is a special case obtained by assuming that
V(t)=0
, such that
A=A^{\prime}
at time
t
(and so
F_{A^{\prime}}=F_{A}
,
I_{A^{\prime}}=I_{A}
and
W^{\prime}=W
). Thus, use of the genuine free energy
F_{A^{\prime}}
rather than the hybrid quantity
F_{A}
allows one to prove a general Landauer-type inequality (
64
) for non-conjugate subsystems, that cannot be proven for conjugate subsystems except in the specific case that the two definitions coincide. This bound involves the modified work
W^{\prime}
, such that
W
is instead bounded by
\beta^{-1}\Delta I_{A^{\prime}}+\delta Q
. Therefore, again we see that either
a
)
\delta Q
constitutes a non-trivial correction to the expected thermodynamic relation, that is, the difference
W-\Delta F_{A^{\prime}}^{\rm eq}
between the work and equilibrium free-energy change is not simply bounded by the information change
\Delta I_{A^{\prime}}
associated with the working system
A^{\prime}
, or else,
b
) heat and work must be non-trivially redefined as
Q^{\prime}=Q+\delta Q
and
W^{\prime}=W-\delta Q
.

The physical significance of these implications is especially evident when considering the work. The free-energy bounds (
63
) and (
64
) which hold for
W^{\prime}
, but not for
W
are physically important thermodynamic relations, and yet the definition of work as
W
is usually assumed to be unambiguous, not least because a redefinition leads to similarly unexpected thermodynamics. For example, by supposing that the work done is
W^{\prime}=W-\delta Q
then even if there is no net total energy change,
W=0
, an amount of work
\delta Q
would be extractible. Thus, we have shown that not all of the naively expected thermodynamic relations can be simultaneously satisfied by a
consistent
framework in which the working system’s energy is
E_{A^{\prime}}(t):=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
. All of the naively expected relations are simultaneously satisfied within the framework of Sec.
III.3.1
, only because that framework is internally
inconsistent
. Specifically, the definition of the working system’s density operator as being
\rho_{A}(t)
is incompatible with the definition of the working system’s energy as being
E_{A^{\prime}}(t)=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
(see Sec.
III.2.3
).

Of course, identifying the correct definition of the working system’s energy is a hotly debated topic in quantum thermodynamics. The only alternative to accepting at least one of the non-trivial physical implications
a
or
b
would be a universal rejection of the definition
E_{A^{\prime}}(t):=H_{A}(t)+V(t)
, despite that it has been independently derived in a number of studies in quantum thermodynamics
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
. Although different definitions may be appropriate in different contexts, we believe that the present results progress the discussion. It was found in the example of Sec.
II.3
that the definition
E_{A^{\prime}}:=H_{A}+V
is implied by physical equations of motion. It was noted in Sec.
II.4
that only this definition is the same one provided by Poynting’s theorem, which expresses the local conservation of energy, as implied by Noether’s theorem and time-translation invariance. In this example therefore, the definition appears to be essentially beyond dispute on both experimental and theoretical grounds, provided of course that one’s premise for defining what is meant by “working system” is
operational
, as it must obviously be. Moreover, beyond the example in Sec.
II.3
, we subsequently demonstrated that this definition is physically well-motivated in several other specific models.

The only additional non-trivial assumption we have made is that
V(0)=0
, which allows us to assume an initial thermal bath and thereby introduce the temperature
\beta^{-1}
. This is a common assumption, which does not restrict the coupling strength but does require that we can view the interaction as switchable. It is an important special case of the more general setting in which one allows completely arbitrary initial states and completely arbitrary
V(t)
. We leave for further work this extension, as well as the task of developing further thermodynamic relations.

IV
Conclusions

When the measurable energy of a subsystem includes the interaction Hamiltonian the standard definition of reduced subsystem state, as specified by a conjugate subsystem decomposition, cannot be employed. We have shown that this situation is indeed encountered in prevalent physical models and we have therefore provided an alternative subsystem decomposition, which we have termed non-conjugate.

Energy and information changes in non-conjugate quantum subsystems possess significantly different behaviour. In particular, since operations performed on a subsystem
A^{\prime}
can disturb a distinct non-conjugate subsystem
B
, they can increase uncertainty about system
B
. We have proven both first and second laws of thermodynamics for non-conjugate subsystems, which generalises previous results
Esposito et al. 2010
;
Esposito and Broeck 2011
;
Deffner and
Jarzynski 2013
;
Strasberg et al. 2017
;
Strasberg and Winter 2021
, while also ensuring internal consistency between the employed definitions of energy and entropy of the working system. Beyond these two basic laws, further additive laws for the Von Neumann and observational entropy changes of the measurable working system
A^{\prime}
and non-conjugate physical bath
B
do not generally hold. This leads either to a breakdown of Clausius and free-energy (Landauer-type) inequalities that relate entropy and free energy changes of the working system to heat and work, or else to a non-trivial redefinition of heat and, in particular, work. More broadly, the notion of non-conjugate subsystems should provide a useful tool in understanding quantum interactions, helping to provide fundamental insight into the physical mechanisms underpinning energy and information exchanges.
